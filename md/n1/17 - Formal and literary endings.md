# N1 - Formal and literary endings


---

# ～ざる

**Meaning** · not; un- - the classical negative <code>ず</code> in its noun-modifying shape, standing where modern Japanese would put `～ない`

<code>ざる</code> is the 連体形(れんたいけい) - the noun-modifying form - of the
Old Japanese negative auxiliary `ず`. It is not a living piece of
modern grammar. It survives in a small stock of frozen phrases -
<code>知(し)られざる</code>, <code>意図(いと)せざる</code>,
<code>絶(た)えざる</code>, やむを得(え)ざる - and in the deliberately weighty
register of documentary titles, editorials and formal notices. Recognise it and
read it as `ない`; write `ない` yourself.

## Formation

The base is the ない-stem (the classical 未然形(みぜんけい)), the same base `ない`
attaches to. Then `ざる`, then a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-stem + ざる + noun | 望(のぞ)まない → 望(のぞ)まざる結果(けっか) |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-stem + ざる + noun | 絶(た)えない → 絶(た)えざる努力(どりょく) |
| Verb (passive) | ～られない → ～られざる | 知(し)られない → 知(し)られざる一面(いちめん) |
| Verb (する) | <b>せざる</b> | 意図(いと)しない → 意図(いと)せざる結果(けっか) |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ざる</b> (very rare) | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ざる |
| Verb (ある) | あらざる (rare) | ある → あらざる |
| い-adjective | ～から + ざる | 少(すく)ない → 少(すく)なからざる影響(えいきょう) |
| Adverbial form of the same auxiliary | ～ず | 少(すく)なからず、思(おも)わず |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the auxiliary `ず` has **two** noun-modifying forms, and both are still
around. `ぬ` is the older one and is the commoner survivor:
<code>予期(よき)せぬ事態(じたい)</code>,
<code>見知(みし)らぬ人(ひと)</code>,
<code>思(おも)わぬ結果(けっか)</code>. `ざる` is the heavier, more bookish of
the pair. Note that `する` splits: せぬ and せざる - never ❌しざる, and never anything built on し.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
見(み)<b>ざる</b>聞(き)か<b>ざる</b>言(い)わ<b>ざる</b>。
See no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil.
</pre>

<pre>
知(し)られ<b>ざる</b>一面(いちめん)が、そこにあった。
There was a side of it that nobody had ever known.
</pre>

<pre>
絶(た)え<b>ざる</b>努力(どりょく)が、この技術(ぎじゅつ)を支(ささ)えてきた。
Unceasing effort is what has sustained this technology.
</pre>

<pre>
やむを得(え)<b>ざる</b>事情(じじょう)により、本日(ほんじつ)は休業(きゅうぎょう)いたします。
Owing to circumstances beyond our control, we are closed today.
</pre>

<pre>
規制(きせい)の緩和(かんわ)は、意図(いと)せ<b>ざる</b>結果(けっか)を招(まね)くおそれがある。
Deregulation carries the risk of producing unintended consequences.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)の閉鎖(へいさ)は、地域(ちいき)経済(けいざい)に少(すく)なから<b>ざる</b>影響(えいきょう)を与(あた)えた。
The closure of the plant had a considerable effect on the local economy.
</pre>

<pre>
遠(とお)から<b>ざる</b>将来(しょうらい)、この方式(ほうしき)が標準(ひょうじゅん)になるだろう。
In the not-too-distant future this method will surely become the standard.
</pre>

<pre>
望(のぞ)ま<b>ざる</b>形(かたち)で名前(なまえ)が世(よ)に出(で)てしまい、彼(かれ)は表(おもて)に立(た)つことをやめた。
His name became public in a way he had never wanted, and he stopped appearing in public.
</pre>

<pre>
戦争(せんそう)の記録(きろく)には、なお語(かた)られ<b>ざる</b>部分(ぶぶん)が多(おお)く残(のこ)されている。
Much of the record of the war remains untold to this day.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this.** <code>ざる</code> is classical Japanese embedded in
modern text. It appears in documentary and book titles
(<code>知(し)られざる～</code>), in shop notices and official announcements
(やむを得(え)ざる事情(じじょう)), in editorials and academic writing
(意図(いと)せざる結果(けっか)), and in proverbs. A learner who says
<code>知(し)らざる人(ひと)に会(あ)った</code> to a friend has produced a
sentence that is not wrong so much as *costumed*. The word you want is `ない`:
知(し)らない人(ひと).

Two things follow from `ざる` being a 連体形(れんたいけい). First, it **must** be followed by a
noun. It is not a way of ending a sentence: ❌<code>彼(かれ)は知(し)らざる。</code>
is broken Japanese, modern or classical. Second, because it modifies a noun, what
you meet in practice is a **compound**: the phrase
<code>知(し)られざる歴史(れきし)</code> behaves like a single lexical item
("the untold history"), not like a clause you assembled.

The one exception to "never produce this" is the handful of set phrases that have
been absorbed into ordinary formal Japanese. やむを得(え)ざる事情(じじょう)
is normal on a shop shutter; 意図(いと)せざる結果(けっか) is normal in a
report; <code>少(すく)なからざる</code> and <code>遠(とお)からざる</code>
appear in newspaper prose without anyone blinking. Learn those as vocabulary
rather than as a pattern you may extend.

The same auxiliary is behind the productive N2 pattern
<code>～ざるを得(え)ない</code> ("have no choice but to"), which *is* worth
producing in formal writing and speech. That expression is frozen; the free
noun-modifying `ざる` on this page is not.

## Contrast

### ～ざる vs ～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they mean the same thing. `ない` is what you write and say;
<code>ざる</code> is what you decode when a title or a notice reaches for weight.

</div>

<pre>
まだ知(し)られていない事実(じじつ)が出(で)てきた。
Facts that had not been known came to light.
</pre>

<pre>
番組(ばんぐみ)の題名(だいめい)は「知(し)られ<b>ざる</b>深海(しんかい)」だった。
The programme was called "The Unknown Deep".
</pre>

The first is how a person reports something. The second is how an editor names a
thing. If you are choosing between them for your own sentence, the answer is always
the first - unless you are writing a title, in which case `ざる` buys you brevity
and gravity in four syllables.

### ～ざる vs ～ぬ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are the noun-modifying negative. `ぬ` is shorter, older
and much more common in surviving vocabulary; `ざる` is heavier and more obviously
literary.

</div>

<pre>
思(おも)わ<b>ぬ</b>ところで昔(むかし)の友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)った。
I ran into an old friend somewhere I never expected to.
</pre>

<pre>
予期(よき)せ<b>ぬ</b>事態(じたい)に、現場(げんば)は混乱(こんらん)した。
An unforeseen situation threw the site into confusion.
</pre>

<pre>
許(ゆる)され<b>ざる</b>行為(こうい)として、厳(きび)しく処分(しょぶん)された。
It was treated as an unforgivable act and punished severely.
</pre>

There is no rule for choosing; the phrases are fixed and you learn which one each
takes. <code>思(おも)わぬ</code>, <code>見知(みし)らぬ</code>,
<code>予期(よき)せぬ</code> take `ぬ`. <code>知(し)られざる</code>,
<code>許(ゆる)されざる</code>, <code>絶(た)えざる</code> take `ざる`. Swapping
them does not produce a grammatical error so much as a phrase nobody uses.

### ～ざる vs ～べからざる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ざる</code> alone is a plain negative: "not X-ed".
<code>べからざる</code> adds prohibition: "must not be X-ed, may not be X-ed".

</div>

<pre>
許(ゆる)され<b>ざる</b>行為(こうい)。
An act that was not forgiven / is unforgivable.
</pre>

<pre>
許(ゆる)す<b>べからざる</b>行為(こうい)。
An act that must not be tolerated.
</pre>

The difference is the same one that separates 許(ゆる)されない from
許(ゆる)してはいけない. Note also the base each takes: <code>ざる</code> follows
the ない-stem (許(ゆる)され<b>ざる</b>), while <code>べからざる</code> follows the
dictionary form (許(ゆる)す<b>べからざる</b>). Getting those the wrong way round is
the standard slip.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 意図(いと)<b>しざる</b>結果(けっか) → ✅ 意図(いと)<b>せざる</b>結果(けっか)
  `する` takes the older negative stem `せ` in front of every classical ending.
  There is no ❌しざる and no ❌しぬ for する.

- ❌ 望(のぞ)む<b>ざる</b>結果(けっか) → ✅ 望(のぞ)ま<b>ざる</b>結果(けっか)
  The base is the ない-stem, not the dictionary form. Test it by producing the
  `ない` form first: 望(のぞ)まない → 望(のぞ)まざる.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はその事実(じじつ)を知(し)ら<b>ざる</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はその事実(じじつ)を知(し)ら<b>ない</b>。
  <code>ざる</code> modifies a noun and cannot close a sentence. If nothing follows
  it, you have written a fragment.

- ❌（to a friend）知(し)ら<b>ざる</b>人(ひと)に道(みち)を聞(き)いた。 → ✅ 知(し)ら<b>ない</b>人(ひと)に道(みち)を聞(き)いた。
  Grammatically assembled, socially impossible. In conversation this is costume
  drama, and it is also not one of the frozen phrases, so it sounds invented as
  well as archaic.

- ❌ 少(すく)ない<b>ざる</b>影響(えいきょう) → ✅ 少(すく)なから<b>ざる</b>影響(えいきょう)
  い-adjectives reach `ざる` through the classical `～から` base, exactly as they
  reach `ず` in 少(すく)なからず.

## Practice

**1.** 店(みせ)のシャッターに貼(は)られた紙(かみ): 「やむを得(え)____事情(じじょう)により、しばらく休業(きゅうぎょう)いたします」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

やむを得(え)<b>ざる</b>事情(じじょう)

This is one of the frozen phrases: "for unavoidable reasons". The base is the
ない-stem of 得(え)る, which is え, so え + ざる. In ordinary speech you would say
仕方(しかた)がない事情(じじょう), but nobody writes that on a shutter.

</details>

**2.** Where would you expect to meet this phrase, and what does it mean?
知(し)られ<b>ざる</b>素顔(すがお)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"The unknown face of X" - a television documentary title, a magazine feature, or
the subtitle of a biography. It is headline register: the whole point of
<code>ざる</code> here is that it is two syllables shorter and considerably more
solemn than 知(し)られていない. You would never say it about a colleague at
lunch.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 語(かた)られ<b>ざる</b>部分(ぶぶん)がまだ多(おお)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

語(かた)られていない部分(ぶぶん)がまだ多(おお)い。
"There is still a lot that has not been told."

Replace the classical 連体形(れんたいけい) with the ordinary negative. Because
<code>語(かた)られざる</code> describes a state that continues, the natural
modern form is ていない rather than a bare ない.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この問題(もんだい)は想像(そうぞう)しざる影響(えいきょう)を与(あた)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この問題(もんだい)は予想(よそう)せ<b>ざる</b>影響(えいきょう)を与(あた)えた。
Better still in modern Japanese: この問題(もんだい)は予想(よそう)できないほどの影響(えいきょう)を与(あた)えた。

Two things are wrong. ❌しざる is impossible - する-verbs take せ before classical
endings. And <code>想像(そうぞう)せざる</code> is not a phrase anyone uses; the
fixed collocations are <code>意図(いと)せざる</code> and
<code>予期(よき)せぬ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 絶(た)え<b>ざる</b>努力(どりょく)によって完成(かんせい)した。
(b) 努力(どりょく)を続(つづ)けて完成(かんせい)させた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a written formula. <code>絶(た)えざる努力(どりょく)</code> is a fixed
phrase of the kind that turns up in a commemorative address, a company history or a
museum caption. It presents the effort as an abstraction to be admired, and no
particular person is doing it.

(b) is what a person says. It names an agent, it can be spoken to anyone, and it
carries no ceremonial weight. If you are describing your own project to a colleague,
(b) is the only one of the two available to you.

</details>

# ～べからず

**Meaning** · do not; it is forbidden to - the classical prohibition, found on signs, mottoes and in a handful of set phrases, and essentially nowhere else

<code>べからず</code> is the negative of `べし`, the classical "ought to" that
modern Japanese still keeps as `べきだ`. Where <code>～べきだ</code> says "this is
the right thing to do", <code>～べからず</code> says "this may not be done", and it
says it with the flat authority of a notice board. Read it as
`～てはいけない`. Write `～てはいけない` yourself.

## Formation

`べし` is a classical auxiliary and its forms alternate: べから- / べく / べし /
べき / べけれ. <code>べからず</code> is べから (the negative base) + `ず`, and its
noun-modifying form is <code>べからざる</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + べからず | 入(はい)る → 入(はい)るべからず |
| Verb (する) | するべからず / すべからず | 落書(らくが)きする → 落書(らくが)きするべからず |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(く)るべからず | 来(く)る → 来(く)るべからず |
| Before a noun | ～べからざる + noun | 許(ゆる)す → 許(ゆる)すべからざる行為(こうい) |
| Fixed phrase, classical verb | 忘(わす)る + べからず | 初心(しょしん)忘(わす)るべからず |
| Fixed phrase, "indispensable" | 欠(か)く + べからざる | 欠(か)くべからざる存在(そんざい) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>べからず</code> is already a complete predicate: it ends the
sentence by itself. It takes no `だ` and no `です`, and there is no polite version
of it. ❌<code>入(はい)るべからずです</code> is the single most common learner
error with this form. If you need politeness, you have left
<code>べからず</code> behind and are writing
<code>入(はい)らないでください</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
芝生(しばふ)に入(はい)る<b>べからず</b>。
Keep off the grass.
</pre>

<pre>
働(はたら)かざる者(もの)食(く)う<b>べからず</b>。
He who does not work shall not eat.
</pre>

<pre>
初心(しょしん)忘(わす)る<b>べからず</b>。
Never forget how you felt when you first began.
</pre>

<pre>
門(もん)の札(ふだ)には「関係者(かんけいしゃ)以外(いがい)、ここより中(なか)へ入(はい)る<b>べからず</b>」とあった。
The sign on the gate read "No entry past this point except for authorised persons".
</pre>

<pre>
公園(こうえん)の古(ふる)い立(た)て札(ふだ)に、「ここでボール遊(あそ)びをする<b>べからず</b>」と書(か)かれていた。
An old post in the park said "No ball games here".
</pre>

<pre>
水(みず)と空気(くうき)は、人(ひと)の暮(く)らしに欠(か)く<b>べからざる</b>ものである。
Water and air are indispensable to human life.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)点検(てんけん)を軽(かる)く見(み)たことは、許(ゆる)す<b>べからざる</b>過(あやま)ちであった。
Treating the safety inspection lightly was an inexcusable failing.
</pre>

<pre>
社訓(しゃくん)の一番(いちばん)上(うえ)には、創業者(そうぎょうしゃ)の字(じ)で「客(きゃく)を待(ま)たせる<b>べからず</b>」と記(しる)されている。
At the top of the company creed, in the founder's own hand, stands "Never keep a customer waiting".
</pre>

<pre>
判決(はんけつ)は、この行為(こうい)を民主(みんしゅ)社会(しゃかい)において許(ゆる)す<b>べからざる</b>ものと断(だん)じた。
The judgment pronounced the act intolerable in a democratic society.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this.** <code>べからず</code> belongs to four places: old or
deliberately old-looking **signage** (芝生(しばふ)に入(はい)るべからず,
立(た)ち入(い)るべからず), **proverbs and mottoes**
(働(はたら)かざる者(もの)食(く)うべからず,
初心(しょしん)忘(わす)るべからず), **formal written judgement**
(許(ゆる)すべからざる, 欠(か)くべからざる), and **jokes** that borrow the
authority of the first three. Said out loud to a person, it is the register of a
period drama. A learner who tells a friend
<code>遅(おく)れるべからず</code> has said something closer to "thou shalt not
be late" than to "don't be late".

It is also worth knowing that <code>べからず</code> is losing even its home ground.
Real Japanese signage today overwhelmingly uses the noun
立入(たちいり)禁止(きんし) ("entry prohibited"), or
遊泳(ゆうえい)禁止(きんし), or the polite
<code>ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください</code>. When you *do* meet
<code>べからず</code> on a sign, it is usually an old one, a temple or park sign
kept for its look, or a shop being wry.

The <code>べからざる</code> half of the pattern is in better health. Two
collocations in particular are ordinary formal Japanese and turn up in newspapers
and reports without any archaic flavour: <code>欠(か)くべからざる</code>
("indispensable") and <code>許(ゆる)すべからざる</code> ("inexcusable"). Even so,
a writer with a choice will usually reach for
<code>欠(か)かせない</code> and <code>許(ゆる)されない</code>.

One point of morphology that saves confusion later: the べ- forms you meet across
N1 are all the same word. <code>べからず</code> (do not),
<code>べく</code> (in order to), <code>べくもない</code> (cannot possibly),
<code>べきだ</code> (ought to) and <code>べくして</code> (as was bound to happen)
are five inflections of `べし`, not five unrelated items to memorise.

## Contrast

### ～べからず vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different century. <code>てはいけない</code> is
what a person says to another person. <code>べからず</code> is what a board says to
the public.

</div>

<pre>
ここで写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っ<b>てはいけません</b>。
You may not take photographs here.
</pre>

<pre>
館内(かんない)にて写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る<b>べからず</b>。
No photography within the building.
</pre>

Note what changes besides the ending. The <code>べからず</code> version drops the
listener entirely - there is no `you`, no politeness, no softening particle - and
it pulls the rest of the sentence up with it: にて instead of で,
館内(かんない) instead of ここ. That is the real lesson. You cannot bolt
<code>べからず</code> onto an otherwise conversational sentence; the whole register
has to move, and once it has moved you are no longer writing anything you would
say.

### ～べからず vs ～べきではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>べきではない</code> is a **judgement** you are making and
someone could argue with. <code>べからず</code> is a **prohibition** already in
force, and there is nothing to discuss.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの前(まえ)でそんな話(はなし)をする<b>べきではない</b>。
You shouldn't talk like that in front of children.
</pre>

<pre>
関係者(かんけいしゃ)以外(いがい)立(た)ち入(い)る<b>べからず</b>。
Authorised personnel only.
</pre>

Both come from `べし`, so the family resemblance is real, but they sit at opposite
ends of it. <code>べきではない</code> is live modern Japanese that you should
absolutely learn to produce - in a meeting, in an essay, in an opinion. Its
politeness kit (<code>べきではないでしょうか</code>) exists precisely because a
listener may disagree. <code>べからず</code> has no such kit, because a notice board
does not negotiate.

### ～べからざる vs ～ない before a noun

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>べからざる</code> is the noun-modifying form of the
prohibition. Its plain modern counterparts are
<code>欠(か)かせない</code> and <code>許(ゆる)されない</code>.

</div>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)の医療(いりょう)に<b>欠(か)かせない</b>存在(そんざい)だ。
He is indispensable to healthcare in the area.
</pre>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)の医療(いりょう)に欠(か)く<b>べからざる</b>存在(そんざい)である。
He is an indispensable presence in the healthcare of the region.
</pre>

The content is identical; the second sentence has been dressed for a printed
citation, and it signals that by ending in である rather than だ. Watch the base
as well: <code>べからざる</code> follows the **dictionary form**
(許(ゆる)す<b>べからざる</b>), whereas the ordinary negative attaches to the
ない-stem (許(ゆる)され<b>ない</b>). Learners who start from
<code>欠(か)かせない</code> often produce ❌欠(か)かべからざる by analogy;
the form is 欠(か)くべからざる, built on the dictionary form
<code>欠(か)く</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ここでタバコを吸(す)う<b>べからずです</b>。 → ✅ ここでタバコを吸(す)う<b>べからず</b>。／ ✅ ここでタバコを吸(す)っては<b>いけません</b>。
  <code>べからず</code> is already the end of the sentence. Adding です to make it
  polite produces something that is neither archaic nor polite, only broken. If the
  situation calls for politeness, the pattern is the wrong tool.

- ❌ 入(はい)ら<b>べからず</b> → ✅ 入(はい)る<b>べからず</b>
  `べし` and everything built from it attach to the **dictionary form**, not to the
  ない-stem. The negation is inside <code>べからず</code> already.

- ❌ 入(はい)らない<b>べからず</b> → ✅ 入(はい)る<b>べからず</b>
  Same trap from the other side. Adding `ない` gives a double negative: "one must
  not not enter".

- ❌ 許(ゆる)す<b>べからず</b>行為(こうい) → ✅ 許(ゆる)す<b>べからざる</b>行為(こうい)
  In front of a noun the form is <code>べからざる</code>. <code>べからず</code>
  only ends sentences.

- ❌（友(とも)だちに）明日(あした)は遅(おく)れる<b>べからず</b>。 → ✅（友(とも)だちに）明日(あした)は遅(おく)れないでね。
  Correctly formed and completely unusable. To a friend this is a joke at best; if
  you did not intend a joke, you have told them off in the voice of a magistrate.

## Practice

**1.** 寺(てら)の門(もん)の横(よこ)に古(ふる)い札(ふだ)が立(た)っている: 「無断(むだん)にて中(なか)へ入(はい)る____」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

入(はい)る<b>べからず</b>

"No unauthorised entry." <code>べからず</code> attaches to the dictionary form
入(はい)る, and the にて earlier in the line already tells you the sentence is
written in the stiff register that <code>べからず</code> requires. A modern sign
would simply read 無断(むだん)立入(たちいり)禁止(きんし).

</details>

**2.** Where would you expect to see each of these, and which one could you write in an email?
(a) 許(ゆる)す<b>べからざる</b>行為(こうい)
(b) 遅刻(ちこく)する<b>べからず</b>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) belongs to newspaper editorials, court judgments and formal statements of
protest, and it is the one you could use in a stiff business email or a written
complaint. It has been absorbed into modern formal Japanese.

(b) belongs on a wall - a club room, a training hall, a poster written to look
old-fashioned. In an email it reads as a joke about being strict. Write
遅刻(ちこく)しないようにしてください instead.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: この部屋(へや)の物(もの)に手(て)を触(ふ)れる<b>べからず</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この部屋(へや)の物(もの)に触(さわ)らないでください。
Or, as a museum would actually label it: 展示品(てんじひん)にお手(て)を触(ふ)れないでください。

The prohibition becomes <code>ないでください</code>, and the archaic
手(て)を触(ふ)れる can relax into 触(さわ)る. Note the reading: in
触(ふ)れる the kanji is ふ, in 触(さわ)る it is さわ.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 工事中(こうじちゅう)のため、この道(みち)を通(とお)らないべからずです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 工事中(こうじちゅう)のため、この道(みち)を通(とお)る<b>べからず</b>。
More natural on a real sign: 工事中(こうじちゅう)につき通行止(つうこうど)め。

Two errors in one short line. `ない` is redundant because
<code>べからず</code> is itself negative, and です cannot follow it. If you find
yourself wanting です, the register you actually want is
通(とお)らないでください.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この制度(せいど)は見直(みなお)す<b>べきではない</b>。
(b) この制度(せいど)は見直(みなお)す<b>べからず</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an opinion in ordinary formal Japanese. The speaker thinks the system should
be left alone, and a colleague can reply
私(わたし)はそうは思(おも)いません. It fits a meeting or an essay.

(b) is not an opinion at all. It reads as an instruction carved somewhere - a rule
handed down, with no author present to argue with. Used in a live discussion it
would sound either theatrical or sarcastic, and the listener would hear the
theatre before they heard the content.

</details>

# ～まじき

**Meaning** · unbecoming; that must never be done - almost always in the frame "conduct unworthy of a [role]"

<code>まじき</code> is the noun-modifying form of `まじ`, the classical auxiliary
that negates `べし`: where `べし` said "ought to", `まじ` said "ought not to, surely
will not". Modern Japanese has kept exactly one construction alive -
<code>N + にあるまじき + noun</code>, "conduct unbecoming a doctor / a
teacher / a public servant". Recognise the frame; say
<code>教師(きょうし)として許(ゆる)されない行為(こうい)</code> yourself.

## Formation

In practice only one verb appears in front of <code>まじき</code>, and that verb is
`ある`. The pattern is a frame with a slot, not a productive ending.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The living frame | Noun + にあるまじき + noun | 教師(きょうし) → 教師(きょうし)にあるまじき行為(こうい) |
| Same frame, として | Noun + としてあるまじき + noun | 社会人(しゃかいじん) → 社会人(しゃかいじん)としてあるまじき発言(はつげん) |
| No preceding noun | あるまじき + こと / もの | あるまじきことが起(お)きた |
| Other verbs (rare, literary) | dictionary form + まじき + noun | 許(ゆる)す → 許(ゆる)すまじき罪(つみ) |
| Sentence-final classical form | ～まじ | 許(ゆる)すまじ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the nouns that fill the first slot are almost all **roles**:
教師(きょうし), 医師(いし), 警察官(けいさつかん), 議員(ぎいん),
政治家(せいじか), 社会人(しゃかいじん), 指導者(しどうしゃ), プロ. The nouns
that fill the second slot are a similarly short list:
行為(こうい), 発言(はつげん), 言動(げんどう), 態度(たいど),
振(ふ)る舞(ま)い, こと. Learning those two lists is more useful than learning
a rule.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
それは教師(きょうし)に<b>あるまじき</b>行為(こうい)だ。
That is conduct unbecoming a teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
警察官(けいさつかん)に<b>あるまじき</b>振(ふ)る舞(ま)いだった。
It was behaviour unworthy of a police officer.
</pre>

<pre>
社会人(しゃかいじん)として<b>あるまじき</b>発言(はつげん)だと批判(ひはん)された。
He was criticised for a remark unbecoming a working adult.
</pre>

<pre>
報告書(ほうこくしょ)は、これを医師(いし)に<b>あるまじき</b>判断(はんだん)であったと指摘(してき)している。
The report identifies this as a decision unworthy of a physician.
</pre>

<pre>
見出(みだ)しには「議員(ぎいん)に<b>あるまじき</b>言動(げんどう)」と大(おお)きく出(で)ていた。
The headline ran, in large type, "Conduct unbecoming a member of the assembly".
</pre>

<pre>
起(お)きてはならないこと、いや、<b>あるまじき</b>ことが起(お)きてしまった。
Something that should not have happened - that could not be allowed to happen - has happened.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)を侮(あなど)るような態度(たいど)は、勝負(しょうぶ)の場(ば)に立(た)つ者(もの)に<b>あるまじき</b>ものである。
An attitude that belittles one's opponent is unworthy of anyone who steps into a contest.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)は、顧客(こきゃく)の名簿(めいぼ)を持(も)ち出(だ)した社員(しゃいん)の行動(こうどう)を、「信用(しんよう)を預(あず)かる者(もの)に<b>あるまじき</b>もの」として処分(しょぶん)した。
The company disciplined the employee who took out the customer list, calling it conduct unworthy of someone entrusted with the public's confidence.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this.** <code>あるまじき</code> is the language of press
statements, disciplinary notices, newspaper headlines and formal apologies. It is
how an institution condemns one of its own in public. In conversation it does not
occur, and a learner who tries it will produce something that sounds like a
subtitle. The sentence you actually want is
<code>先生(せんせい)としてどうかと思(おも)います</code> or
<code>教師(きょうし)のすることではない</code>.

Two consequences of that register. First, the pattern is **heavy**: it accuses
someone of failing at their office, not merely of behaving badly. Applying it to a
small lapse is not a small overstatement, it is a category error -
<code>遅刻(ちこく)は学生(がくせい)にあるまじき行為(こうい)だ</code>
reads as a joke about a headmaster. Second, it is almost never used **to** the
person. It is used *about* them, to a third party or to the public.

<code>まじき</code> is a 連体形(れんたいけい), a noun-modifying form,
so a noun has to follow. There is no ❌<code>あるまじきだ</code> and no
❌<code>あるまじきです</code>; if you have nothing concrete to put after it, the
generic こと or もの fills the slot.

The bare classical ending <code>まじ</code> does still surface, but only in slogans
and titles - the shape of 許(ゆる)すまじ, "let this never be permitted", on a
banner or a memorial. Treat it as something you read, once, and understand.

## Contrast

### ～にあるまじき vs ～べきではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>べきではない</code> judges the **action**:
this should not be done. <code>にあるまじき</code> judges the action **against a
role**: someone in that position has disgraced it.

</div>

<pre>
生徒(せいと)の前(まえ)でそんな言(い)い方(かた)をする<b>べきではない</b>。
You shouldn't speak like that in front of the pupils.
</pre>

<pre>
生徒(せいと)の前(まえ)でのその発言(はつげん)は、教師(きょうし)に<b>あるまじき</b>ものだ。
That remark, made in front of pupils, is unbecoming a teacher.
</pre>

The first can be said privately, by a colleague, over coffee. The second is a
verdict; it names the office and finds the person unworthy of it, and it therefore
belongs in a written report or a public statement. If the person you are talking
about has no relevant role, <code>あるまじき</code> is simply unavailable -
❌<code>人(ひと)にあるまじき</code> does not work the way "inhuman" does in
English.

### ～にあるまじき vs ～らしくない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `らしくない` says the behaviour is **out of character**.
<code>にあるまじき</code> says it is **a disgrace to the office**. One is mild
surprise; the other is condemnation.

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)に遅(おく)れるなんて、彼(かれ)<b>らしくない</b>ね。
Turning up late - that's not like him at all.
</pre>

<pre>
記録(きろく)を書(か)き換(か)えたことは、研究者(けんきゅうしゃ)に<b>あるまじき</b>行為(こうい)である。
Altering the records is an act unworthy of a researcher.
</pre>

`らしくない` is everyday spoken Japanese and can even be affectionate. It also
attaches happily to a person's name, which <code>あるまじき</code> cannot:
❌<code>田中(たなか)さんにあるまじき</code> is wrong, because the slot wants a
**role**, not an individual. Say
教師(きょうし)にあるまじき or 上司(じょうし)にあるまじき.

### ～にあるまじき vs ～てはならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>てはならない</code> is a **prohibition** aimed forward:
do not do this. <code>にあるまじき</code> is a **judgement** aimed backward: this
has been done, and it was a disgrace.

</div>

<pre>
個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)を外部(がいぶ)に出(だ)し<b>てはならない</b>。
Personal data must not be released outside the organisation.
</pre>

<pre>
個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)を外部(がいぶ)に出(だ)したのは、担当者(たんとうしゃ)に<b>あるまじき</b>行為(こうい)だった。
Releasing personal data was an act unworthy of the officer responsible.
</pre>

`てはならない` is live formal Japanese: it appears in rules, contracts and
regulations, and you should be able to write it. <code>あるまじき</code> only ever
comments on what has already occurred. This is why the two so often appear in the
same document, one in the rulebook and the other in the statement announcing that
the rule was broken.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ その発言(はつげん)は教師(きょうし)に<b>あるまじきだ</b>。 → ✅ その発言(はつげん)は教師(きょうし)に<b>あるまじき</b>発言(はつげん)だ。／ ✅ ～に<b>あるまじき</b>ものだ。
  <code>まじき</code> modifies a noun and cannot end a sentence. When no specific
  noun is available, use こと or もの.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんに<b>あるまじき</b>行為(こうい) → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に<b>あるまじき</b>行為(こうい)
  The slot before に takes a role or a status, not a personal name. The whole force
  of the pattern comes from measuring a person against their office.

- ❌ 遅刻(ちこく)するのは学生(がくせい)に<b>あるまじき</b>行為(こうい)だ。 → ✅ 遅刻(ちこく)は学生(がくせい)としてよくない。
  Not ungrammatical, but wildly out of proportion. Reserve
  <code>あるまじき</code> for things that would be reported.

- ❌ 教師(きょうし)に<b>あるまじく</b>行動(こうどう)した → ✅ 教師(きょうし)に<b>あるまじき</b>行動(こうどう)をした
  `まじ` has no living adverbial form in modern Japanese. The only shape you will
  meet is <code>まじき</code> in front of a noun.

- ❌（本人(ほんにん)に向(む)かって）それは社会人(しゃかいじん)に<b>あるまじき</b>態度(たいど)ですよ。 → ✅ それは社会人(しゃかいじん)として恥(は)ずかしい態度(たいど)ですよ。
  Said to someone's face, the pattern is not stern but stagy. Japanese delivers
  this criticism in the third person, in writing.

## Practice

**1.** 新聞(しんぶん)の見出(みだ)し: 「警察官(けいさつかん)に____行為(こうい)、署長(しょちょう)が謝罪(しゃざい)」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

警察官(けいさつかん)に<b>あるまじき</b>行為(こうい)

The frame is Noun + にあるまじき + noun, and a headline is exactly where it lives.
Note that the verb in the frame is always ある - you are not choosing a verb, you
are filling two noun slots.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 今日(きょう)の田中(たなか)さん、全然(ぜんぜん)話(はな)さないね。（田中(たなか)さんにあるまじきね・田中(たなか)さんらしくないね）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

田中(たなか)さん<b>らしくないね</b>。

Two reasons. The slot before <code>にあるまじき</code> wants a role, not a
person's name; and the situation is a friend noticing something mildly unusual,
which is `らしくない` territory. <code>あるまじき</code> would turn a quiet
afternoon into a disciplinary hearing.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 指導者(しどうしゃ)に<b>あるまじき</b>発言(はつげん)である。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

指導者(しどうしゃ)として言(い)ってはいけないことを言(い)った。
Or, a little more formally: 指導者(しどうしゃ)として許(ゆる)されない発言(はつげん)だ。

<code>あるまじき</code> unpacks into "as a leader" + "must not". Note that the
modern version needs a verb, because the classical form was doing the work of a
whole clause in one word.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの判断(はんだん)は専門家(せんもんか)にあるまじきでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの判断(はんだん)は専門家(せんもんか)に<b>あるまじき</b>ものでした。
Or: あの判断(はんだん)は専門家(せんもんか)に<b>あるまじき</b>判断(はんだん)でした。

<code>まじき</code> must be followed by a noun; it cannot take です or でした
directly. The generic もの is the standard repair when no specific noun suggests
itself.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 上司(じょうし)として不適切(ふてきせつ)な発言(はつげん)だったと思(おも)います。
(b) 上司(じょうし)に<b>あるまじき</b>発言(はつげん)である。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a real sentence a real person could say, in a meeting or in a written
comment. It is critical but leaves room: 不適切(ふてきせつ) is a measured word,
and と思(おも)います marks it as the speaker's view.

(b) is a public verdict. である rather than です already tells you it is written
rather than spoken, and <code>あるまじき</code> removes the speaker altogether:
nobody is offering an opinion, the office itself is said to have been disgraced.
You would meet (b) in a newspaper or an official statement, and you would not hear
it in a room.

</details>

# ～べく

**Meaning** · in order to; with a view to - a purpose clause in written, formal Japanese, where speech would use `ために`

<code>べく</code> is the adverbial form of the classical `べし`, the same word that
gives modern Japanese `べきだ`. Attached to a verb it sets up a purpose: *Xべく、Y* means
"in order to X, Y was done". It belongs to newspapers, company
announcements, official reports and literary narration. Read it as `ために`; write
`ために`.

## Formation

`べし` inflects べから- / べく / べし / べき / べけれ, and <code>べく</code> is the
adverbial member of that set. Like all of them, it attaches to the **dictionary
form**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + べく | 勝(か)つ → 勝(か)つべく |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + べく | 求(もと)める → 求(もと)めるべく |
| Verb (する) | すべく / するべく | 実現(じつげん)する → 実現(じつげん)すべく |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(く)るべく (rare) | 来(く)る → 来(く)るべく |
| Negative purpose | ～ないように (not ❌べくない) | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れないように |
| Frozen adverb | なるべく | なるべく早(はや)く |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>すべく</code> and <code>するべく</code> are both correct;
<code>すべく</code> is older, shorter and much the commoner of the two in the kind
of writing where <code>べく</code> occurs at all. There is **no negative form**:
`べし` had one (<code>べからず</code>), but "in order not to" is expressed with
<code>ないように</code>, never with ❌<code>べくなく</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the everyday adverb `なるべく` ("as much as possible", なるべく早(はや)く,
なるべく安(やす)いもの) is this same <code>べく</code>, frozen solid. Nobody
analyses it any more, and it is completely ordinary in speech - so you already use
<code>べく</code> every day without producing the pattern on this page.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
勝(か)つ<b>べく</b>、チームは練習(れんしゅう)を重(かさ)ねた。
The team trained and trained in order to win.
</pre>

<pre>
真相(しんそう)を知(し)る<b>べく</b>、記者(きしゃ)は現地(げんち)へ向(む)かった。
The reporter set out for the scene in order to learn the truth.
</pre>

<pre>
事態(じたい)を収(おさ)める<b>べく</b>、社長(しゃちょう)が記者(きしゃ)会見(かいけん)を開(ひら)いた。
The president held a press conference in order to bring the situation under control.
</pre>

<pre>
資金(しきん)を調達(ちょうたつ)す<b>べく</b>、創業者(そうぎょうしゃ)は各地(かくち)の銀行(ぎんこう)を回(まわ)った。
The founder went round the banks of one region after another in order to raise capital.
</pre>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)の安全(あんぜん)を守(まも)る<b>べく</b>、住民(じゅうみん)自(みずか)らが夜(よる)の見回(みまわ)りを始(はじ)めた。
Residents themselves began night patrols in order to keep the neighbourhood safe.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい市場(しじょう)に参入(さんにゅう)す<b>べく</b>、同社(どうしゃ)は現地(げんち)の企業(きぎょう)と手(て)を結(むす)んだ。
The company tied up with a local firm in order to enter the new market.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は最後(さいご)の船(ふね)に間(ま)に合(あ)う<b>べく</b>、暗(くら)い坂道(さかみち)を駆(か)け下(お)りた。
He ran down the dark hill road in order to catch the last boat.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)も早(はや)い復旧(ふっきゅう)を実現(じつげん)す<b>べく</b>、全社(ぜんしゃ)を挙(あ)げて取(と)り組(く)んでまいります。
We will work as a whole company to restore service at the earliest possible date.
</pre>

<pre>
この条例(じょうれい)は、観光(かんこう)客(きゃく)の急増(きゅうぞう)に対応(たいおう)す<b>べく</b>、昨年(さくねん)改正(かいせい)された。
The ordinance was amended last year in order to cope with the sharp rise in visitors.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this.** <code>べく</code> is written Japanese, and narrowly so:
newspaper copy, corporate press releases, official reports, the more formal kind of
speech read from a script, and novels. In conversation it does not appear at all,
and a learner who says
<code>買(か)い物(もの)をす<b>べく</b>駅(えき)へ行(い)った</code> to a
friend has produced a sentence that would raise eyebrows in a boardroom, let alone
a kitchen. The word for "in order to" that you should be producing is `ために`, with
`ように` for the outcomes you cannot control.

Three restrictions worth knowing, because they are what makes
<code>べく</code> look "wrong" even in writing when a learner uses it:

- The verb before <code>べく</code> must be **volitional** - something someone
  decides to do. 分(わ)かるべく and できるべく are not usable.
- The **same subject** carries through both clauses. The one who intends the
  purpose is the one who acts.
- The clause after it reports an **action taken**. Requests, invitations and
  commands do not sit comfortably behind it: for
  <code>合格(ごうかく)すべく、頑張(がんば)ってください</code> a Japanese
  writer would say
  <code>合格(ごうかく)するために頑張(がんば)ってください</code>.

There is a mild literary flavour to <code>べく</code> that `ために` does not have:
it presents the purpose as a resolve rather than a plan, which is why novels use it
for characters setting out to do something difficult. That is a colour you should be
able to feel when reading, not one you need to reproduce.

Finally, keep the family straight. <code>べく</code> (in order to),
<code>べき</code> (ought to), <code>べからず</code> (must not),
<code>べくもない</code> (cannot possibly) and <code>べくして</code> (as was bound to
happen) are all the same classical word in different forms. Of the five, only
<code>べき</code> is worth using yourself.

## Contrast

### ～べく vs ～ために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different medium. `ために` is the purpose
clause of the language; <code>べく</code> is the purpose clause of the printed page.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)う<b>ために</b>、早(はや)めに家(いえ)を出(で)ました。
I left home early so as to be in time for the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)う<b>べく</b>、一行(いっこう)は夜明(よあ)け前(まえ)に宿(やど)を発(た)った。
The party left the inn before dawn in order to reach the meeting in time.
</pre>

Notice that the second sentence had to change everything else too -
一行(いっこう) rather than 私(わたし), 発(た)った rather than
出(で)ました. <code>べく</code> cannot be dropped into a conversational
sentence; it drags the surrounding vocabulary up with it. If the rest of your
sentence would not appear in a newspaper, neither should <code>べく</code>.

### ～べく vs ～ように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ように` is for outcomes you can only hope for; `ために` and
<code>べく</code> are for goals you act on directly. So <code>べく</code> lines up
with `ために`, not with `ように`.

</div>

<pre>
後(うし)ろの席(せき)にも聞(き)こえる<b>ように</b>、大(おお)きな声(こえ)で話(はな)した。
I spoke loudly so that people at the back could hear.
</pre>

<pre>
批判(ひはん)に応(こた)える<b>べく</b>、委員会(いいんかい)は報告書(ほうこくしょ)を公開(こうかい)した。
The committee published the report in order to answer the criticism.
</pre>

`ように` takes a potential or a non-volitional verb (聞(き)こえる,
忘(わす)れない, 間(ま)に合(あ)う) and often has a different subject in each
clause. <code>べく</code> takes neither. This is also where the negative goes: "in
order not to forget" is <code>忘(わす)れないように</code>, and there is no
<code>べく</code> version of it at all.

### ～べく vs ～べきだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one word, two forms. <code>べく</code> is adverbial and means
"in order to". <code>べき</code> modifies a noun or ends a sentence and means
"ought to".

</div>

<pre>
問題(もんだい)を解決(かいけつ)す<b>べく</b>、専門家(せんもんか)を招(まね)いた。
They brought in a specialist in order to solve the problem.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は早(はや)く解決(かいけつ)す<b>べきだ</b>。
This problem ought to be solved quickly.
</pre>

The first reports what was done and why; the second passes judgement on what should
be done. Learners who meet <code>べく</code> first sometimes read every
<code>べき</code> as a purpose clause, and vice versa. The test is position: if a
comma and a second clause follow, it is <code>べく</code> and means "in order to".
<code>べき</code> is the only member of the family in ordinary modern use, and it is
the one to practise.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 合格(ごうかく)<b>しべく</b> → ✅ 合格(ごうかく)<b>すべく</b>／合格(ごうかく)<b>するべく</b>
  `する` gives す or する in front of <code>べく</code>, never し. The short
  <code>すべく</code> is what you will meet in print.

- ❌ 勝(か)た<b>べく</b> → ✅ 勝(か)つ<b>べく</b>
  <code>べく</code> attaches to the dictionary form. The ない-stem belongs to a
  different classical ending altogether - that is where
  <code>んがため</code> attaches.

- ❌ 忘(わす)れない<b>べく</b>、メモを取(と)った。 → ✅ 忘(わす)れない<b>ように</b>、メモを取(と)った。
  There is no negative purpose form. "In order not to" is always
  <code>ないように</code>.

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)になる<b>べく</b>勉強(べんきょう)してください。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)になる<b>ように</b>勉強(べんきょう)してください。
  Two problems: 上手(じょうず)になる is not something you simply decide to do,
  and a request cannot follow <code>べく</code>.

- ❌（友(とも)だちに）お金(かね)を貯(た)める<b>べく</b>バイトを始(はじ)めたよ。 → ✅ お金(かね)を貯(た)める<b>ために</b>バイトを始(はじ)めたよ。
  Formally impeccable, socially absurd. Ending the sentence in よ while using a
  classical adverbial is exactly the mismatch to avoid.

## Practice

**1.** 新聞(しんぶん)記事(きじ): 市(し)は渋滞(じゅうたい)を解消(かいしょう)____、新(あたら)しいバス路線(ろせん)を設(もう)けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

解消(かいしょう)<b>すべく</b>

A news report is precisely where <code>べく</code> lives, and
解消(かいしょう)する is a する-verb, so the classical base is す.
<code>解消(かいしょう)するべく</code> is also correct but longer;
<code>すべく</code> is the newspaper's choice.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 子(こ)どもが安心(あんしん)して遊(あそ)べる（べく・ように）、公園(こうえん)に柵(さく)を付(つ)けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

遊(あそ)べる<b>ように</b>、公園(こうえん)に柵(さく)を付(つ)けた。

遊(あそ)べる is a potential form and the children are not the ones putting up
the fence, so both of `ように`'s conditions are met and both of
<code>べく</code>'s are broken. <code>べく</code> needs a volitional verb and a
single subject running through the sentence.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 技術(ぎじゅつ)を学(まな)ぶ<b>べく</b>、彼(かれ)は海外(かいがい)へ渡(わた)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

技術(ぎじゅつ)を学(まな)ぶ<b>ために</b>、彼(かれ)は海外(かいがい)へ行(い)った。

Swap <code>べく</code> for `ために` and the literary 渡(わた)った for the plain
行(い)った, and the sentence stops being narration and becomes something a person
could tell you.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 遅(おく)れないべく、早(はや)く家(いえ)を出(で)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 遅(おく)れない<b>ように</b>、早(はや)く家(いえ)を出(で)ました。

There is no negative <code>べく</code>. `べし` did have a negative -
<code>べからず</code> - but it means "must not", not "in order not to". The polite
ました ending is a second signal that the whole sentence belongs to the spoken
register, where <code>べく</code> has no place.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会社(かいしゃ)を立(た)て直(なお)す<b>ために</b>、新(あたら)しい社長(しゃちょう)を迎(むか)えた。
(b) 会社(かいしゃ)を立(た)て直(なお)す<b>べく</b>、新(あたら)しい社長(しゃちょう)を迎(むか)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The information is the same, and either could be true of the same event. What
differs is who is talking. (a) is what an employee tells a friend, or what a
textbook says; it is neutral and completely ordinary.

(b) has been written for print. It would sit in a business magazine or an annual
report, and it lends the decision a faint air of resolve - the company is presented
as having set out to do something. Reading it, you should register "this is written
Japanese"; writing it, you should choose (a) unless you are producing a document.

</details>

# ～べくもない

**Meaning** · there is no possibility of doing it; cannot possibly - the thing is ruled out by the situation, not merely difficult

<code>べくもない</code> is <code>べく</code> (the adverbial form of classical `べし`)
+ `も` + `ない`: "there is not even the *possibility* of doing it". It is a written,
literary way of closing a door, and it attaches to a short list of verbs about
knowing, hoping, matching and disputing. Read it as
<code>知(し)りようがない</code> or <code>望(のぞ)めない</code>; write those
instead.

## Formation

Like every member of the `べし` family, it follows the **dictionary form**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + べくもない | 知(し)る → 知(し)るべくもない |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + べくもない | 比(くら)べる → 比(くら)べるべくもない |
| Verb (する) | すべくもない / するべくもない | 想像(そうぞう)する → 想像(そうぞう)すべくもない |
| Past | ～べくもなかった | 望(のぞ)む → 望(のぞ)むべくもなかった |
| Polite | ～べくもありません | 及(およ)ぶ → 及(およ)ぶべくもありません |
| Classical variant of the verb | 比(くら)ぶ + べくもない | 比(くら)ぶべくもない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb slot is not free. In practice you will meet perhaps six verbs
there: 知(し)る, 望(のぞ)む, 及(およ)ぶ, 比(くら)べる,
疑(うたが)う, 争(あらそ)う. Anything else - ❌食(た)べるべくもない,
❌行(い)くべくもない - is not a stylistic slip but a phrase that does not exist.
Learn the collocations, not a rule.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
当時(とうじ)の事情(じじょう)は、もはや知(し)る<b>べくもない</b>。
The circumstances of the time are now beyond any possibility of knowing.
</pre>

<pre>
勝利(しょうり)など望(のぞ)む<b>べくもなかった</b>。
Victory had never been anything one could hope for.
</pre>

<pre>
素人(しろうと)の腕(うで)では、職人(しょくにん)の技(わざ)に及(およ)ぶ<b>べくもない</b>。
An amateur's hand cannot begin to approach a craftsman's skill.
</pre>

<pre>
両者(りょうしゃ)の規模(きぼ)は比(くら)べる<b>べくもない</b>。
The two are not remotely comparable in scale.
</pre>

<pre>
これだけの記録(きろく)が残(のこ)っている以上(いじょう)、事実(じじつ)は疑(うたが)う<b>べくもない</b>。
With this much on record, the facts are beyond any possibility of doubt.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)あいの小(ちい)さな工場(こうじょう)が、海外(かいがい)の大手(おおて)と価格(かかく)で争(あらそ)う<b>べくもない</b>。
A small workshop in the hills cannot possibly compete on price with the overseas giants.
</pre>

<pre>
焼(や)け落(お)ちた屋敷(やしき)の中(なか)から、母(はは)の手紙(てがみ)を見(み)つけ出(だ)す<b>べくもなかった</b>。
There was no hope of finding his mother's letters among the ruins of the burnt house.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)の技術(ぎじゅつ)では、これほどの精度(せいど)は実現(じつげん)す<b>べくもありませんでした</b>。
With the technology of the day, precision on this scale was simply out of the question.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this.** <code>べくもない</code> is a literary and formal
written form: essays, histories, novels, editorials, the occasional written
apology. In conversation it does not occur, and there is no casual version of it.
When you want to say the same thing out loud, Japanese gives you
<code>知(し)りようがない</code>, <code>知(し)るはずがない</code>,
<code>とても望(のぞ)めない</code>, <code>比(くら)べものにならない</code>.

The feeling it carries is **resignation**, not frustration. It says the possibility
was never on the table - because the evidence is gone, because the gap in scale is
too wide, because time has passed. That is why it so often has もはや or もう or
今(いま)となっては in front of it, and why it fits historical writing so
naturally. It does *not* express refusal or unwillingness; it is not the classical
version of ❌"I won't".

There is no affirmative <code>べくもある</code>, and no question form. The
expression exists only in this negative shape. It also cannot take a person as the
one being denied permission - it is about possibility, not permission, so
<code>入(はい)るべくもない</code> would mean "there is no way in", never "you may
not enter". That last sense is <code>入(はい)るべからず</code>, the other
negative of the same classical word.

## Contrast

### ～べくもない vs ～ようがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both say "there is no way to". `ようがない` is ordinary modern
Japanese and can be spoken; <code>べくもない</code> is its literary counterpart and
takes only a handful of verbs.

</div>

<pre>
連絡先(れんらくさき)を聞(き)いていないので、知(し)らせ<b>ようがない</b>。
I didn't get their contact details, so there's no way to let them know.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)の人(ひと)が何(なに)を思(おも)っていたかは、知(し)る<b>べくもない</b>。
What the people of that time were thinking is beyond our knowing.
</pre>

`ようがない` attaches to the ます-stem (知(し)らせ<b>ようがない</b>) and takes any
verb at all. <code>べくもない</code> attaches to the dictionary form and takes
almost none. If you are unsure whether the verb you want is allowed with
<code>べくもない</code>, that uncertainty is itself the answer: use `ようがない`.

### ～べくもない vs ～はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずがない` is a **judgement about likelihood**: given what I
know, that can't be so. <code>べくもない</code> is a statement that the
**possibility does not exist** in the world.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)がそんなうそをつく<b>はずがない</b>。
There's no way he would tell a lie like that.
</pre>

<pre>
記録(きろく)が失(うしな)われた今(いま)、正確(せいかく)な数字(すうじ)は知(し)る<b>べくもない</b>。
Now that the records are lost, the exact figures are beyond recovery.
</pre>

`はずがない` is about the speaker's reasoning and is used constantly in speech.
<code>べくもない</code> makes no claim about the speaker at all: the door is shut by
circumstances. This is also why `はずがない` can be wrong (the person did lie after
all) in a way that <code>べくもない</code> cannot.

### ～べくもない vs ～得(え)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal negatives of possibility.
<code>～得(え)ない</code> is **productive** - it attaches to almost any
ます-stem and you can use it in a report. <code>べくもない</code> is a closed set of
frozen phrases.

</div>

<pre>
そのような結論(けつろん)は受(う)け入(い)れ<b>得(え)ない</b>。
Such a conclusion is not one we can accept.
</pre>

<pre>
両者(りょうしゃ)の実力(じつりょく)は比(くら)べる<b>べくもない</b>。
There is no comparing the ability of the two.
</pre>

Note the different bases: <code>得(え)ない</code> follows the ます-stem
(受(う)け入(い)れ), <code>べくもない</code> the dictionary form. Of the two,
<code>得(え)ない</code> is the one to learn actively - it appears in modern
business and academic writing and nobody would call it archaic. Reserve
<code>べくもない</code> for reading.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 知(し)ら<b>べくもない</b> → ✅ 知(し)る<b>べくもない</b>
  Everything in the `べし` family attaches to the dictionary form. The ない-stem
  looks right because the meaning is negative, but the negation lives in the
  `ない` at the end.

- ❌ 想像(そうぞう)<b>しべくもない</b> → ✅ 想像(そうぞう)<b>すべくもない</b>
  `する` becomes す (or, less commonly, stays する) before <code>べく</code>. There
  is no し form here.

- ❌ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>べくもない</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>はずがない</b>。
  The pattern is not a way of predicting. It belongs to knowing, hoping, matching
  and disputing, and a weather forecast is none of those.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は解決(かいけつ)す<b>べくもある</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は解決(かいけつ)できる。
  There is no affirmative. The expression exists only as
  <code>べくもない</code> / <code>べくもなかった</code>.

- ❌（友人(ゆうじん)に）その店(みせ)の場所(ばしょ)は知(し)る<b>べくもないよ</b>。 → ✅ その店(みせ)、どこにあるか分(わ)からないよ。
  The よ at the end and the classical ending cannot occupy the same sentence. In
  speech, use 分(わ)からない or 知(し)りようがない.

## Practice

**1.** 随筆(ずいひつ)の一節(いっせつ): 祖父(そふ)が何(なに)を考(かんが)えてこの家(いえ)を建(た)てたのか、今(いま)となっては____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

知(し)る<b>べくもない</b>

今(いま)となっては ("now, at this remove") is the standard lead-in for this
pattern, and 知(し)る is the verb it most often takes. The whole sentence is
written in the reflective register of an essay - which is exactly where
<code>べくもない</code> lives.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会員(かいいん)カードを忘(わす)れたので、割引(わりびき)を受(う)け（べくもない・ようがない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

割引(わりびき)を受(う)け<b>ようがない</b>。

Two reasons. The base is wrong for <code>べくもない</code>, which needs the
dictionary form 受(う)ける; and more importantly this is an everyday situation at
a shop counter, where `ようがない` is the natural form and
<code>べくもない</code> would be absurd.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: あの二(ふた)つのチームの力(ちから)は比(くら)べる<b>べくもない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの二(ふた)つのチームの力(ちから)は比(くら)べものにならない。
Or, more plainly still: あの二(ふた)つのチームでは、力(ちから)が全然(ぜんぜん)違(ちが)う。

<code>比(くら)べものにならない</code> is the everyday idiom that carries exactly
this meaning and can be said out loud. Note the reading 二(ふた)つ - the native
numeral, not ❌につ.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昔(むかし)のことなので、正確(せいかく)な日付(ひづけ)は知(し)らべくもありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昔(むかし)のことなので、正確(せいかく)な日付(ひづけ)は知(し)る<b>べくもありません</b>。

The base must be the dictionary form 知(し)る. The ない-stem 知(し)ら is what
`ない` and `ず` take, not what <code>べく</code> takes. Note the reading
日付(ひづけ) - rendaku turns つけ into づけ after the noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 真相(しんそう)は知(し)る<b>べくもない</b>。
(b) 真相(しんそう)は知(し)りたくない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about possibility, and the speaker has no say in it. The evidence is gone,
the witnesses are dead, the door is shut - and the tone is one of resignation
rather than complaint. It reads as a line from an essay or a history.

(b) is about desire: the speaker could find out and is choosing not to. That is a
completely different sentence, and it is ordinary spoken Japanese. Learners
sometimes reach for (a) intending (b), because both come out as "I can't know" in a
rough translation.

</details>

# ～んがため

**Meaning** · for the sole purpose of; in order to - a heavy, archaic purpose clause, usually with a hint that the goal justified everything else

<code>んがため</code> is the classical volitional auxiliary `む` (written and
pronounced ん) + the old genitive particle `が` + ため: literally "for the sake of
[intending to do X]". It survives in speeches, editorials, court documents and
literary narration, and nowhere else. Read it as `ために`. Write `ために`.

## Formation

The base is the ない-stem (the classical 未然形(みぜんけい)) - the **opposite**
of <code>べく</code>, which takes the dictionary form. Mixing the two up is the
standard error.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-stem + んがため | 勝(か)たない → 勝(か)たんがため |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-stem + んがため | 生(い)きない → 生(い)きんがため |
| Verb (する) | <b>せんがため</b> | 維持(いじ)しない → 維持(いじ)せんがため |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)んがため</b> (rare) | 来(く)る → 来(こ)んがため |
| With に | ～んがために | 守(まも)らんがために |
| Before a noun | ～んがための + noun | 得(え)んがための行動(こうどう) |
| Sentence-final | ～んがためだ / ～んがためである | 勝(か)たんがためである |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the ん here is **not** the negative ん of
分(わ)からん or 知(し)らん. It is the classical `む`, the ancestor of modern
`よう` in 行(い)こう and しよう. So
<code>勝(か)たんがため</code> means "in order to win", never "in order not to
win". This is worth saying out loud once, because the shape is identical to the
dialect negative and learners regularly read the sentence backwards.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
生(い)き<b>んがため</b>に働(はたら)く。
One works in order to live.
</pre>

<pre>
勝(か)た<b>んがため</b>に手段(しゅだん)を選(えら)ばなかった。
He stopped at nothing in his determination to win.
</pre>

<pre>
己(おのれ)の利益(りえき)を得(え)<b>んがため</b>の行動(こうどう)にすぎない。
It is nothing more than conduct aimed at his own profit.
</pre>

<pre>
真実(しんじつ)を伝(つた)え<b>んがため</b>、記者(きしゃ)たちは危険(きけん)な地域(ちいき)に入(はい)った。
The reporters went into the dangerous districts in order to get the truth out.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)を守(まも)ら<b>んがため</b>に、事実(じじつ)を隠(かく)したのではないか。
Was the truth not concealed simply in order to protect the company?
</pre>

<pre>
政権(せいけん)を維持(いじ)せ<b>んがため</b>の政策(せいさく)だと、野党(やとう)は批判(ひはん)した。
The opposition attacked it as a policy designed purely to keep the government in office.
</pre>

<pre>
次(つぎ)の世代(せだい)に豊(ゆた)かな自然(しぜん)を残(のこ)さ<b>んがため</b>、われわれは今(いま)決断(けつだん)しなければならない。
It is in order to leave a rich natural world to the next generation that we must decide now.
</pre>

<pre>
一族(いちぞく)の名(な)を守(まも)ら<b>んがため</b>に、彼(かれ)は生涯(しょうがい)沈黙(ちんもく)を通(とお)した。
To protect the name of his family he kept silent all his life.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this.** <code>んがため</code> is the most archaic-sounding
purpose expression that still appears in modern text. It lives in political
speeches, newspaper editorials, court judgments, company statements of the gravest
kind, and historical fiction. Nobody says it. If you say
<code>昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べんがために出(で)かけた</code>, you
have said something no Japanese speaker would produce outside a joke. The word you
want is `ために`.

Beyond register, <code>んがため</code> has a **colour** that `ために` does not.
It presents the purpose as consuming - the only thing that mattered, everything
else subordinated to it. That is why it collocates so often with
手段(しゅだん)を選(えら)ばない ("stopping at nothing"), and why in journalism
it usually arrives with an accusation attached:
<code>保身(ほしん)を図(はか)らんがための説明(せつめい)</code> is not a
neutral description. It can also be used admiringly - a doctor working
人(ひと)を救(すく)わんがため - but the intensity is always there.

Two structural points. The verb must be **volitional**: you cannot form
❌<code>分(わ)からんがため</code>, because nobody decides to understand. And the
subject of the purpose is the subject of the main clause, as with
<code>べく</code>.

The family it belongs to is worth naming. The same classical `む` is behind
<code>んばかり</code> ("as if about to"), behind
<code>言(い)わんとする</code> ("to be on the point of saying"), and behind the
everyday volitional 行(い)こう. What makes *this* pattern archaic is not the ん
but the `が` - a genitive particle modern Japanese has otherwise lost, surviving
only in fossils such as 我(わ)が国(くに) and 我(わ)が家(や).

## Contrast

### ～んがため vs ～ために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning; `ために` is the living form and
<code>んがため</code> is its ceremonial ancestor, with an added sense of "and
nothing else mattered".

</div>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)を養(やしな)う<b>ために</b>、二(ふた)つの仕事(しごと)をしている。
He works two jobs in order to support his family.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)を養(やしな)わ<b>んがため</b>、男(おとこ)は夜(よる)も働(はたら)き続(つづ)けた。
It was to feed his family that the man went on working through the nights as well.
</pre>

Note the base as well as the register: `ために` follows the **dictionary form**
(養(やしな)う<b>ために</b>), <code>んがため</code> the **ない-stem**
(養(やしな)わ<b>んがため</b>). The first sentence is something a colleague could
tell you at lunch. The second has been written for a page, and it asks you to feel
the weight of the sacrifice.

### ～んがため vs ～べく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are literary purpose clauses.
<code>べく</code> is neutral and businesslike; <code>んがため</code> is emotional,
older, and often carries a judgement about the motive.

</div>

<pre>
信頼(しんらい)を回復(かいふく)す<b>べく</b>、再発(さいはつ)防止(ぼうし)策(さく)をまとめた。
They drew up measures to prevent a recurrence, in order to restore confidence.
</pre>

<pre>
信頼(しんらい)を回復(かいふく)せ<b>んがため</b>の、見(み)せかけの対策(たいさく)にすぎない。
It is no more than a token measure got up in order to win back confidence.
</pre>

Swap them and something is lost each way. <code>べく</code> in the second sentence
would drain the sarcasm; <code>んがため</code> in the first would suggest the
company was desperate rather than orderly. Note also the bases -
回復(かいふく)<b>す</b>べく but 回復(かいふく)<b>せ</b>んがため - which is the same
alternation as する → す / せ that runs through classical grammar.

### ～んがため vs ～ように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ように` is for results you can only hope for, and it takes
potential and negative verbs. <code>んがため</code> is for goals you drive at, and
it takes neither.

</div>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)し<b>ないように</b>、何度(なんど)も確(たし)かめた。
I checked over and over so as not to make a mistake.
</pre>

<pre>
真相(しんそう)を明(あき)らかにせ<b>んがため</b>、市民(しみん)は裁判(さいばん)を起(お)こした。
The citizens brought the case in order to bring the truth to light.
</pre>

"In order not to" has no <code>んがため</code> form at all: the ん is a volitional,
and you cannot intend not to do something with it. Any negative purpose goes to
<code>ないように</code>, whatever the register - even a court judgment writes
<code>生(しょう)じないように</code> rather than reaching for the classical.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 勝(か)つ<b>んがため</b> → ✅ 勝(か)た<b>んがため</b>
  The base is the ない-stem, not the dictionary form. Produce the `ない` form first
  (勝(か)たない) and drop the ない.

- ❌ 維持(いじ)<b>しんがため</b> → ✅ 維持(いじ)<b>せんがため</b>
  `する` takes the classical negative base せ in front of `ん`, exactly as it does
  in <code>せず</code> and <code>せざるを得(え)ない</code>.

- ❌ 忘(わす)れ<b>んがため</b>にメモした → ✅ 忘(わす)れ<b>ないように</b>メモした
  Two errors at once. There is no negative purpose form, and this is a homely
  everyday sentence that the classical ending has no business in.

- ❌ 来(き)<b>んがため</b> → ✅ 来(こ)<b>んがため</b>
  来(く)る is irregular; its ない-stem is こ, as in 来(こ)ない. The same
  こ appears in 来(こ)させる and 来(こ)られる.

- ❌（同僚(どうりょう)に）会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)わ<b>んがため</b>に走(はし)りました。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)う<b>ように</b>走(はし)りました。
  Correctly formed, unusable. Said to a colleague this is not formality, it is
  costume.

## Practice

**1.** 社説(しゃせつ)の一文(いちぶん): 自(みずか)らの立場(たちば)を守(まも)____の発言(はつげん)であったと言(い)わざるを得(え)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

守(まも)ら<b>んがため</b>の発言(はつげん)

An editorial passing judgement on a politician's motive - the natural home of this
pattern. The base is the ない-stem 守(まも)ら, and the の is required because
what follows is a noun. Note the sarcastic edge:
<code>んがための</code> here means "purely in order to".

</details>

**2.** Where would you meet the following, and what is wrong with using it in an email to a colleague?
情報(じょうほう)を集(あつ)め<b>んがため</b>、現地(げんち)に人(ひと)を送(おく)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

You would meet it in a newspaper article, a company history or a novel - written
narration reporting a deliberate act. In an email it fails twice over: the register
is a century too old for correspondence, and <code>んがため</code> hints that
gathering the information mattered more than anything else, which is not usually
what you mean. Write
情報(じょうほう)を集(あつ)めるために現地(げんち)に人(ひと)を送(おく)りました.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 会社(かいしゃ)を立(た)て直(なお)さ<b>んがため</b>、全員(ぜんいん)が力(ちから)を合(あ)わせた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会社(かいしゃ)を立(た)て直(なお)す<b>ために</b>、全員(ぜんいん)が力(ちから)を合(あ)わせた。

<code>んがため</code> → `ために`, and with it the base changes from the ない-stem
立(た)て直(なお)さ back to the dictionary form
立(た)て直(なお)す. Nothing else in the sentence needs touching, which is a
good illustration of how little the classical form actually adds.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)しんがために、毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)する<b>ために</b>、毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しています。

The form ❌しんがため does not exist - する gives せんがため. But the deeper
problem is the sentence itself: ています tells you this is ordinary polite speech
about the speaker's own study routine, and no such sentence would ever carry
<code>んがため</code>. Fix the register, not the classical ending.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 票(ひょう)を集(あつ)める<b>ために</b>地方(ちほう)を回(まわ)った。
(b) 票(ひょう)を集(あつ)め<b>んがため</b>地方(ちほう)を回(まわ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a fact. Candidates tour the regions to gather votes; that is what
campaigning is, and the sentence carries no comment.

(b) is written, and it editorialises. <code>んがため</code> presents the vote as
the *only* thing the person cared about, so the reader hears an accusation - the
tour was cynical, the interest in the regions was not real. This is the standard
journalistic use of the pattern, and it is why you should not reach for it when you
simply mean "in order to".

</details>

# ～んばかりに

**Meaning** · as if about to; all but - it looked exactly as though X were going to happen, and it did not

<code>んばかり</code> is the classical `む` ("would, is about to") + ばかり ("just
about"). It describes an **appearance**: the branches looked as though they would
snap, the hall looked as though it would burst. The crucial point is that the thing
does not actually happen. This is descriptive written Japanese - narration,
reportage, scene-setting. Read it as <code>今(いま)にも～そう</code>; write that
instead.

## Formation

The base is the ない-stem (the classical 未然形(みぜんけい)), the same base
<code>んがため</code> takes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-stem + んばかり | 泣(な)かない → 泣(な)かんばかり |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-stem + んばかり | あふれない → あふれんばかり |
| Verb (する) | <b>せんばかり</b> | 爆発(ばくはつ)しない → 爆発(ばくはつ)せんばかり |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)んばかり</b> (rare) | 来(く)る → 来(こ)んばかり |
| Adverbial, before a verb | ～んばかりに | 泣(な)かんばかりに頼(たの)んだ |
| Before a noun | ～んばかりの | 割(わ)れんばかりの拍手(はくしゅ) |
| Sentence-final | ～んばかりだ / ～んばかりであった | あふれんばかりだった |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the collocations are half the pattern. Four are so common they are worth
learning as vocabulary: <code>割(わ)れんばかりの拍手(はくしゅ)</code>
(thunderous applause), <code>あふれんばかりの笑顔(えがお)</code> (a beaming
smile), <code>泣(な)かんばかりに</code> (almost in tears), and
<code>言(い)わんばかりに</code> (as if to say).

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
割(わ)れ<b>んばかりの</b>拍手(はくしゅ)が起(お)こった。
Applause broke out loud enough to bring the roof down.
</pre>

<pre>
あふれ<b>んばかりの</b>笑顔(えがお)で迎(むか)えてくれた。
She greeted us with a smile that was almost spilling over.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は泣(な)か<b>んばかりに</b>頭(あたま)を下(さ)げた。
She bowed her head, on the very edge of tears.
</pre>

<pre>
枝(えだ)は折(お)れ<b>んばかりに</b>実(み)をつけていた。
The branches were so laden with fruit that they looked ready to snap.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は人(ひと)であふれ、壁(かべ)が押(お)し出(だ)され<b>んばかり</b>であった。
The hall was so packed that the walls themselves seemed about to give way.
</pre>

<pre>
少年(しょうねん)は飛(と)び上(あ)がら<b>んばかりに</b>喜(よろこ)んだ。
The boy was so delighted he all but leapt into the air.
</pre>

<pre>
老人(ろうじん)は何(なに)か言(い)わ<b>んばかりに</b>口(くち)を開(ひら)きかけたが、結局(けっきょく)黙(だま)ったままだった。
The old man opened his mouth as though about to say something, but in the end kept his silence.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)の知(し)らせに、町(まち)じゅうが沸(わ)き返(かえ)ら<b>んばかりの</b>騒(さわ)ぎとなった。
The news of the championship set off an uproar that seemed to lift the whole town off the ground.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this.** <code>んばかり</code> is descriptive prose: novels,
essays, feature journalism, match reports, the caption under a photograph. Nobody
uses it in conversation. The spoken equivalent is
<code>今(いま)にも泣(な)きそうだった</code>,
<code>落(お)ちそうなくらい</code>, <code>すごい拍手(はくしゅ)だった</code> -
and those are what you should be producing.

The single most important thing about the pattern is that **the event does not
occur**. <code>泣(な)かんばかりに頼(たの)んだ</code> means she begged
almost in tears - she did not cry. If she cried, the sentence is
<code>泣(な)きながら頼(たの)んだ</code>. Learners regularly use
<code>んばかり</code> for things that did happen, which is not a matter of degree
but a factual error.

It is also **visual**. <code>んばかり</code> reports what an observer could see or
hear at that moment: a face, a crowd, a gesture, a sound. It does not work for a
forecast (❌明日(あした)雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)らんばかりだ), for an internal
state nobody could observe, or for a general tendency. If you cannot point to the
scene, the pattern does not apply.

<code>言(い)わんばかりに</code> deserves separate mention. It is by far the
commonest use, and it means "as if to say" - the person did not speak, but their
manner said it for them:
<code>早(はや)く帰(かえ)れと言(い)わんばかりに時計(とけい)を見(み)た</code>.
Note that it needs a quoted phrase and と in front of it, which is where it meets -
and gets confused with - <code>とばかりに</code>.

## Contrast

### ～んばかりに vs 今(いま)にも～そう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different medium.
<code>今(いま)にも～そうだ</code> is what you say; <code>んばかり</code> is what
a writer prints.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもは<b>今(いま)にも泣(な)きそう</b>な顔(かお)をしていた。
The child looked as though she was about to burst into tears.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもは泣(な)き出(だ)さ<b>んばかりの</b>顔(かお)で立(た)っていた。
The child stood there with a face on the very brink of tears.
</pre>

Note the bases. `そう` follows the ます-stem (泣(な)き<b>そう</b>);
<code>んばかり</code> follows the ない-stem (泣(な)か<b>んばかり</b>). If you are
writing rather than reading, use the first: it is shorter, universally understood,
and carries no risk of sounding like a nineteenth-century novel.

### ～んばかりに vs ～とばかりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>んばかり</code> describes an **appearance**: it looked as
though it would happen. <code>とばかりに</code> reports an **unspoken message**: his
manner said "now!".

</div>

<pre>
皿(さら)は落(お)ち<b>んばかりに</b>棚(たな)のふちに乗(の)っていた。
The plate sat on the edge of the shelf, on the point of falling.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)だ<b>とばかりに</b>、犬(いぬ)が皿(さら)に飛(と)びついた。
Seizing the moment, the dog lunged at the plate.
</pre>

The forms look alike because both end in ばかりに, but the pieces in front differ.
<code>んばかり</code> takes a **verb stem** (落(お)ち + んばかり);
<code>とばかりに</code> takes a **quotation** with と (今(いま)だ + とばかりに).
The bridge between them is <code>と言(い)わんばかりに</code>, which is literally
"as if about to say ~" and is close in meaning to <code>とばかりに</code>.

### ～んばかりに vs ～ばかりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a completely different word. `ばかりに` (N3) means "just
because ~, and it turned out badly". <code>んばかりに</code> means "as if about
to". Only the ん distinguishes them.

</div>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)って来(こ)なかった<b>ばかりに</b>、ずぶぬれになった。
Just because I hadn't brought an umbrella, I got soaked through.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は嵐(あらし)の中(なか)を、飛(と)ばされ<b>んばかりに</b>歩(ある)いていた。
He walked on through the storm, looking as if he might be blown off his feet.
</pre>

Watch the base, which resolves it every time: `ばかりに` follows a **plain past or
plain form** (来(こ)なかった<b>ばかりに</b>), while <code>んばかりに</code> follows
a **bare ない-stem** with no ない (飛(と)ばされ<b>んばかりに</b>). If you can see a
complete verb form in front of it, it is the N3 pattern; if the verb looks
unfinished, it is this one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 泣(な)く<b>んばかりに</b> → ✅ 泣(な)か<b>んばかりに</b>
  The base is the ない-stem. Produce 泣(な)かない first and drop the ない.

- ❌ 感謝(かんしゃ)<b>しんばかり</b> → ✅ 感謝(かんしゃ)<b>せんばかり</b>
  `する` gives せ before every classical ending of this kind - the same せ as in
  <code>せず</code> and <code>せんがため</code>.

- ❌ 涙(なみだ)があふれ<b>んばかりに</b>あふれた。 → ✅ 涙(なみだ)があふれ<b>んばかり</b>だった。
  The pattern says the thing *nearly* happened. Reporting that it then happened
  contradicts it.

- ❌ もう帰(かえ)れ<b>んばかりに</b>、店員(てんいん)が片付(かたづ)け始(はじ)めた。 → ✅ もう帰(かえ)れ<b>と言(い)わんばかりに</b>、店員(てんいん)が片付(かたづ)け始(はじ)めた。
  A quoted phrase needs と言(い)わんばかりに (or <code>とばかりに</code>).
  <code>んばかり</code> alone attaches to a verb stem, not to a quotation.

- ❌（友(とも)だちに）お腹(なか)が割(わ)れ<b>んばかりに</b>笑(わら)ったよ。 → ✅ お腹(なか)が痛(いた)くなるくらい笑(わら)ったよ。
  Correctly built, but no one talks like this. In speech, ～くらい and
  今(いま)にも do all the work.

## Practice

**1.** 試合(しあい)後(ご)の記事(きじ): 逆転(ぎゃくてん)勝利(しょうり)の瞬間(しゅんかん)、スタジアムは割(わ)れ____歓声(かんせい)に包(つつ)まれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

割(わ)れ<b>んばかりの</b>歓声(かんせい)

Before a noun the form is <code>んばかりの</code>, not
<code>んばかりに</code>. <code>割(わ)れんばかりの</code> is one of the fixed
collocations, normally with 拍手(はくしゅ) or 歓声(かんせい) - and a match
report is exactly where you meet it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部長(ぶちょう)は「もう帰(かえ)れ」（とばかりに・んばかりに）時計(とけい)を見(み)た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「もう帰(かえ)れ」<b>とばかりに</b>時計(とけい)を見(み)た。

What precedes is a quoted phrase, so it needs と. <code>んばかり</code> attaches to
a verb stem instead: the version with ん would have to be
「もう帰(かえ)れ」と言(い)わんばかりに, with 言(い)わ supplying the stem.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 彼(かれ)は泣(な)か<b>んばかりの</b>声(こえ)で事情(じじょう)を話(はな)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は今(いま)にも泣(な)きそうな声(こえ)で事情(じじょう)を話(はな)した。

<code>今(いま)にも～そうだ</code> is the everyday equivalent, and note that the
base changes with it: `そう` takes the ます-stem 泣(な)き, while
<code>んばかり</code> took the ない-stem 泣(な)か.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 喜(よろこ)ぶんばかりに、彼(かれ)は何度(なんど)も手(て)を振(ふ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 喜(よろこ)ば<b>んばかりに</b>、彼(かれ)は何度(なんど)も手(て)を振(ふ)った。
More natural still: 飛(と)び上(あ)がら<b>んばかりに</b>喜(よろこ)んで、何度(なんど)も手(て)を振(ふ)った。

The base must be the ない-stem 喜(よろこ)ば, not the dictionary form. The second
version is better Japanese because <code>んばかり</code> wants a **visible**
near-event - leaping into the air - rather than the emotion itself.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 母(はは)は泣(な)か<b>んばかりに</b>喜(よろこ)んだ。
(b) 母(はは)は泣(な)きながら喜(よろこ)んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says she did **not** cry. She was visibly on the edge of it, and the writer is
describing what an onlooker would have seen. The sentence belongs to written
narration.

(b) says the tears came. It is plain modern Japanese and could be said out loud to
anyone. The two are not stronger and weaker versions of the same statement - they
report different facts, and choosing (a) when you mean (b) tells the reader
something untrue.

</details>

# ～とばかりに

**Meaning** · as if to say ~; with an air that said ~ - the words were never spoken, but the person's manner delivered them

<code>とばかりに</code> is the quotation particle `と` + ばかり + `に`. It puts words
into someone's behaviour: they did not say "now's my chance", but everything about
how they moved said it. This is the language of narration and reportage - novels,
match reports, feature articles. In speech, use
<code>～と言(い)うように</code> or simply describe what happened.

## Formation

What comes before と is a **quotation**: a sentence, a phrase, a command, a single
word. Because it is quoted, no politeness or agreement is required with the rest of
the sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Quoted sentence | plain clause + とばかりに | 今(いま)だ → 今(いま)だとばかりに |
| Quoted command | imperative + とばかりに | 帰(かえ)れ → 帰(かえ)れとばかりに |
| Quoted noun + だ | Noun + だ + とばかりに | チャンスだ → チャンスだとばかりに |
| Quoted negative intent | ～まい + とばかりに | 逃(のが)すまい → 逃(のが)すまいとばかりに |
| Fuller variant | ～と言(い)わんばかりに | 帰(かえ)れと言(い)わんばかりに |
| Fixed adverbials | ここぞとばかりに / 待(ま)っていましたとばかりに | ここぞとばかりに攻(せ)めた |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ここぞとばかりに</code> ("seizing the moment", "making the most of
it") has broken free of the pattern and become an ordinary adverb. Adults do say it
in conversation, and you will meet it in blog posts and everyday writing. It is the
one piece of this page you could reasonably produce yourself.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今(いま)だ<b>とばかりに</b>、選手(せんしゅ)が前(まえ)に出(で)た。
Now was the moment, the player's move said, and he pushed forward.
</pre>

<pre>
待(ま)っていました<b>とばかりに</b>、子(こ)どもたちが外(そと)へ飛(と)び出(だ)した。
The children shot outside as if they had been waiting all along for the word.
</pre>

<pre>
ここぞ<b>とばかりに</b>、記者(きしゃ)が質問(しつもん)を浴(あ)びせた。
The reporters seized their moment and fired off question after question.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)く帰(かえ)れ<b>とばかりに</b>、店員(てんいん)が椅子(いす)を片付(かたづ)け始(はじ)めた。
The staff started stacking the chairs, as good as telling us to leave.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)には関係(かんけい)ない<b>とばかりに</b>、彼(かれ)は窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ていた。
He looked out of the window with an air of its having nothing to do with him.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)は許(ゆる)さない<b>とばかりに</b>、監督(かんとく)は腕(うで)を組(く)んだまま動(うご)かなかった。
The manager stood there with folded arms, as if to say that failure would not be forgiven.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)はこれで終(お)わりだ<b>とばかりに</b>、老人(ろうじん)は湯(ゆ)のみを置(お)いて立(た)ち上(あ)がった。
The old man set down his teacup and rose, as though the conversation were now closed.
</pre>

<pre>
各社(かくしゃ)はこの好機(こうき)を逃(のが)すまい<b>とばかりに</b>、一斉(いっせい)に新製品(しんせいひん)を発表(はっぴょう)した。
Determined not to let the opening pass, the makers announced new products all at once.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this** - with the one exception of
<code>ここぞとばかりに</code>. The pattern belongs to **written description**: a
novelist telling you what a character's body language meant, a journalist
characterising a politician's silence, a match report reading a player's dash.
Spoken Japanese does the same work with
<code>～と言(い)っているような顔(かお)で</code> or by simply reporting the
action.

The pattern always involves a **reading of someone else's behaviour**, which has two
consequences. The subject is normally a third party, and the words in the quotation
are the *writer's* interpretation, not a transcript. If the person actually said the
words, you do not need <code>とばかりに</code> at all - you need と言(い)って.
This is the error to watch for.

There is often a faint edge of criticism or amusement in it. The writer is
attributing an attitude the person did not admit to: opportunism
(<code>ここぞとばかりに</code>), indifference
(<code>関係(かんけい)ないとばかりに</code>), impatience
(<code>帰(かえ)れとばかりに</code>). Neutral behaviour rarely gets described this
way, because there would be nothing to read into it.

<code>と言(い)わんばかりに</code> is the fuller form of the same idea and is
slightly commoner in careful writing. The two are close to interchangeable; if
anything, <code>と言(い)わんばかりに</code> foregrounds that the words were *not*
said, while <code>とばかりに</code> puts more weight on the vigour of the action.

## Contrast

### ～とばかりに vs ～と言(い)わんばかりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nearly the same. <code>と言(い)わんばかりに</code> says
"as if about to say it"; <code>とばかりに</code> says "as if that were the whole
point". The second is punchier and suits sudden action.

</div>

<pre>
まだ足(た)りない<b>と言(い)わんばかりに</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は資料(しりょう)を見(み)直(なお)した。
She went back over the documents as if to say it still was not enough.
</pre>

<pre>
行(い)くぞ<b>とばかりに</b>、彼(かれ)はかばんをつかんで部屋(へや)を出(で)た。
He grabbed his bag and left the room as though to announce that they were going.
</pre>

Where the sentence describes a **stance held** over some seconds,
<code>と言(い)わんばかりに</code> reads better; where it describes a **single
sharp movement**, <code>とばかりに</code> does. This is a tendency rather than a
rule, and plenty of writers use them interchangeably.

### ～とばかりに vs ～かのように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かのように` compares the whole situation to something imagined.
<code>とばかりに</code> puts specific **words** into someone's manner.

</div>

<pre>
何事(なにごと)もなかった<b>かのように</b>、会議(かいぎ)は続(つづ)いた。
The meeting went on as if nothing had happened.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)も聞(き)いていない<b>とばかりに</b>、部長(ぶちょう)は書類(しょるい)に目(め)を落(お)とした。
The director dropped his eyes to the papers, as if to say he had heard nothing.
</pre>

`かのように` needs no speaker and no attitude - the meeting is not claiming
anything. <code>とばかりに</code> requires a person whose behaviour can be read as a
statement, which is why it always has an animate subject. In modern conversation,
the way to say either of these is
<code>何(なに)も聞(き)こえなかったみたいな顔(かお)で</code>.

### ～とばかりに vs ～んばかりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とばかりに</code> needs a **quotation and と**.
<code>んばかりに</code> needs a **verb stem**. They are different patterns that
happen to share an ending.

</div>

<pre>
勝(か)った<b>とばかりに</b>、両手(りょうて)を上(あ)げた。
He threw up both arms as though declaring the thing won.
</pre>

<pre>
両手(りょうて)が天井(てんじょう)に届(とど)か<b>んばかりに</b>上(あ)がった。
His arms went up as though they would touch the ceiling.
</pre>

The first reports what the gesture *said*; the second reports what the gesture
*looked like*. The two meet in
<code>と言(い)わんばかりに</code>, which is <code>んばかり</code> attached to
言(い)う and therefore takes a quotation like <code>とばかりに</code>. If you can
hear words in front of it, you need と.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今(いま)だ<b>ばかりに</b>前(まえ)に出(で)た。 → ✅ 今(いま)だ<b>とばかりに</b>前(まえ)に出(で)た。
  The `と` is not optional; without it you have written the N3 pattern
  `ばかりに`, which means "just because", and the sentence stops making sense.

- ❌「帰(かえ)ってください」<b>とばかりに</b>言(い)った。 → ✅「帰(かえ)ってください」と言(い)った。
  If the words were actually spoken, the pattern is wrong by definition.
  <code>とばかりに</code> exists to report what was *not* said.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>とばかりに</b>空(そら)が暗(くら)くなった。 → ✅ 今(いま)にも雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)しそうに空(そら)が暗(くら)くなった。
  Only a person can be read as saying something. The sky has no manner to
  interpret, so the pattern has no subject to work on.

- ❌ 逃(のが)す<b>とばかりに</b>追(お)いかけた → ✅ 逃(のが)すまい<b>とばかりに</b>追(お)いかけた
  The quotation has to be a complete thought. <code>まい</code> ("I shall not let
  it") supplies one; a bare dictionary form does not.

- ❌（同僚(どうりょう)に）部長(ぶちょう)が早(はや)く帰(かえ)れ<b>とばかりに</b>時計(とけい)を見(み)ていましたよ。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、「早(はや)く帰(かえ)れ」って感(かん)じで時計(とけい)を見(み)てましたよ。
  In conversation, って感(かん)じで does precisely this job, and nobody will
  wonder whether you have been reading too many novels.

## Practice

**1.** 試合(しあい)の記事(きじ): 相手(あいて)のミスを見(み)た瞬間(しゅんかん)、____とばかりに一気(いっき)に攻(せ)め込(こ)んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ここぞ<b>とばかりに</b>（or 今(いま)だとばかりに）

Both are fixed and both fit a match report: a mistake by the other side is the
opening, and the team pounces on it. <code>ここぞとばかりに</code> is the phrase to
remember, since it is the only part of this pattern that also lives in ordinary
speech.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)は天井(てんじょう)に届(とど)か（とばかりに・んばかりに）高(たか)くジャンプした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

届(とど)か<b>んばかりに</b>高(たか)くジャンプした。

届(とど)か is a bare ない-stem, so it takes <code>んばかりに</code>. For
<code>とばかりに</code> you would need something quotable in front of it -
「見(み)ていろ」とばかりに, for instance. The test is whether you can hear words
there.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary spoken Japanese: 部長(ぶちょう)は、その話(はなし)はもう終(お)わりだ<b>とばかりに</b>席(せき)を立(た)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)、その話(はなし)はもう終(お)わりって感(かん)じで席(せき)を立(た)っちゃったんだよ。

The quotation survives; what changes is how it is attached.
って感(かん)じで is the everyday way of saying "with an air of ~", and the
casual ちゃった matches the register. A slightly more formal spoken version would be
終(お)わりだと言(い)わんばかりの様子(ようす)で.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)は「もう知(し)らない」とばかりに大(おお)きな声(こえ)で言(い)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は「もう知(し)らない」と大(おお)きな声(こえ)で言(い)った。
Or, keeping the pattern: 彼女(かのじょ)はもう知(し)らない<b>とばかりに</b>部屋(へや)を出(で)て行(い)った。

The sentence says both that she said the words and that her manner said them for
her. Choose one. <code>とばかりに</code> requires that the words stay unspoken, so
if you keep it, the verb must describe an action rather than an utterance.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は「もう帰(かえ)る」と言(い)って立(た)ち上(あ)がった。
(b) 彼(かれ)はもう帰(かえ)る<b>とばかりに</b>立(た)ち上(あ)がった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a report. He said the words, then he stood up; anyone in the room heard
them, and the sentence commits to nothing beyond that.

(b) says he did **not** speak. The writer is interpreting the way he got to his feet
and telling you what it meant, which means the sentence carries the writer's
judgement as well as the facts - often with a hint that the man was being abrupt.
(b) is also unmistakably written narration; (a) can be said to anybody.

</details>

# ～ごとき / ～ごとく

**Meaning** · like; as if - the classical `～のような / ～のように`. Separately, attached straight to a person, `ごとき` means "someone as insignificant as ~"

<code>如(ごと)し</code> is the classical auxiliary of comparison. Its adverbial
form is <code>ごとく</code> ("like, as"), its noun-modifying form
<code>ごとき</code> ("a ~ like this"), and its sentence-final form
<code>ごとし</code>. That half of the page is literary and you should read it, not
write it. But `ごとき` has a second life as a **dismissive suffix** -
私(わたし)ごとき, お前(まえ)ごとき - and that one is alive in speech.

## Formation

The comparative use needs `の` after a noun; the dismissive use must **not** have
it. That single particle is the whole difference.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (comparison) | Noun + の + ごとく / ごとき / ごとし | 矢(や) → 矢(や)の如(ごと)し |
| Verb | plain form + (が) + ごとく | 流(なが)れる → 流(なが)れるが如(ごと)く |
| With かの | ～かの + ごとく / ごとき | 知(し)っていた → 知(し)っていたかの如(ごと)く |
| Adverbial ("like ~") | ～の如(ごと)く + verb | 風(かぜ)の如(ごと)く去(さ)る |
| Noun-modifying | ～の如(ごと)き + noun | 氷(こおり)の如(ごと)き冷(つめ)たさ |
| Sentence-final | ～の如(ごと)し | 光陰(こういん)矢(や)の如(ごと)し |
| **Dismissive suffix** | Person + ごとき (no の) | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)ごとき |
| Set phrase in documents | 上記(じょうき)／下記(かき)の如(ごと)く | 上記(じょうき)の如(ごと)く |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kanji 如(ごと) is optional and both spellings are standard.
Legal documents, older prose and set phrases tend to write it; modern journalism
often uses kana. Learn to recognise 如(ごと)く, 如(ごと)き and 如(ごと)し on sight; the
reading is not one you could guess from the kanji's other uses (如何(いかが),
欠如(けつじょ)).

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
光陰(こういん)矢(や)の<b>如(ごと)し</b>。
Time flies like an arrow.
</pre>

<pre>
上記(じょうき)の<b>如(ごと)く</b>報告(ほうこく)いたします。
We report as stated above.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は風(かぜ)の<b>如(ごと)く</b>去(さ)っていった。
He was gone like the wind.
</pre>

<pre>
氷(こおり)の<b>如(ごと)き</b>冷(つめ)たさが、その一言(ひとこと)にはあった。
There was an icy coldness in that single remark.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>ごとき</b>が意見(いけん)を申(もう)し上(あ)げるのは恐(おそ)れ多(おお)いのですが。
It is presumptuous of someone like me to offer an opinion, but -
</pre>

<pre>
お前(まえ)<b>ごとき</b>に負(ま)けるものか。
As if I would lose to the likes of you.
</pre>

<pre>
動(うご)かざること山(やま)の<b>如(ごと)し</b>。
Immovable as a mountain.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)の人(ひと)の波(なみ)が、川(かわ)の<b>如(ごと)く</b>駅(えき)へ流(なが)れ込(こ)んでいく。
The morning crowds pour towards the station like a river.
</pre>

<pre>
新聞(しんぶん)は、事故(じこ)を予告(よこく)していたかの<b>如(ごと)き</b>記述(きじゅつ)に注目(ちゅうもく)した。
The papers seized on a passage that read as though it had foretold the accident.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The two uses have to be separated, because they live in different worlds.

**The comparison (ごとく / ごとき / ごとし) is literary and you should not produce
it.** It belongs to proverbs (光陰(こういん)矢(や)の如(ごと)し), formal
documents (上記(じょうき)の如(ごと)く, 下記(かき)の如(ごと)く), speeches,
and novels reaching for grandeur. Said in conversation it is not "very formal", it
is theatrical - somewhere between a sermon and a period drama. The modern forms are
`のように` and `のような`, and they say everything <code>ごとく</code> says.

**The dismissive suffix (ごとき straight after a person) is alive.** It has two
directions:

- **Humble**, about yourself: 私(わたし)ごとき, 僕(ぼく)ごとき - "someone as
  unimportant as me". This is genuinely used, in formal speech and in polite
  written Japanese, as a way of lowering yourself before saying something.
- **Contemptuous**, about someone else: お前(まえ)ごとき,
  素人(しろうと)ごとき, あいつごとき - "the likes of you". This is rude, and
  deliberately so. It is common in fiction, sport and argument, and you will
  certainly hear it; using it about a real person is picking a fight.

Note that the dismissive use takes **no の** - 私(わたし)ごとき, never
❌私(わたし)のごとき - and that the noun in front of it is almost always a person
or a category of person. This is the one part of the page you might reasonably use
yourself, and even then only the humble direction.

A practical reading note: because <code>ごとき</code> also serves the comparison,
context decides. <code>子供(こども)のごとき字(じ)</code> (with の) is
"handwriting like a child's". <code>子供(こども)ごときに</code> (without の) is
"to a mere child". The particle is doing all the work.

## Contrast

### ～の如(ごと)く vs ～のように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. `のように` is the living form;
<code>の如(ごと)く</code> is what a proverb, a legal document or a novel uses in
its place.

</div>

<pre>
水(みず)が流(なが)れる<b>ように</b>話(はなし)が進(すす)んだ。
The discussion flowed along like water.
</pre>

<pre>
時(とき)は水(みず)の<b>如(ごと)く</b>流(なが)れ、村(むら)の姿(すがた)も変(か)わった。
Time flowed like water, and the village changed with it.
</pre>

Everything the classical form adds is register. Notice that the second sentence has
also picked up 姿(すがた) and a literary rhythm - as with the rest of this group,
<code>ごとく</code> is not something you can drop into an ordinary sentence. Also
watch the attachment: after a verb the classical takes が
(流(なが)れる<b>が</b>如(ごと)く) where the modern takes nothing at all.

### 私(わたし)ごとき vs 私(わたし)なんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same move, two registers. `なんか` / `など` is how you
belittle something in speech; <code>ごとき</code> is how you do it in formal
Japanese or in writing.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>なんか</b>には無理(むり)ですよ。
Someone like me could never manage it.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>ごとき</b>にそのような大役(たいやく)が務(つと)まりましょうか。
How could a person as slight as myself carry out so great a duty?
</pre>

Both lower the speaker, and Japanese does this constantly as a politeness move.
`なんか` is neutral and everyday; <code>ごとき</code> is ceremonial, and is what you
would meet in a formal letter of acceptance or a speech of thanks. Turned outward,
both become rude - お前(まえ)なんか and お前(まえ)ごとき are both insults,
the second the colder of the two.

### ～如(ごと)し vs ～かのようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かのようだ` marks the comparison as **counterfactual**: it
looks that way but is not. <code>如(ごと)し</code> is a plain resemblance, with
no such claim - unless you add かの to it.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は何(なに)も知(し)らない<b>かのように</b>ふるまった。
He behaved as though he knew nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)は眠(ねむ)れる巨人(きょじん)の<b>如(ごと)く</b>横(よこ)たわっていた。
The mountain lay there like a sleeping giant.
</pre>

The first tells you he did know. The second makes no claim about giants at all; it
is a straight simile. When a writer does want the counterfactual reading in
classical dress, the form is
<code>かの如(ごと)く</code> - and that combination is common in editorials
describing someone pretending:
<code>何(なに)も起(お)きなかったかの如(ごと)く</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 山(やま)<b>如(ごと)し</b> → ✅ 山(やま)の<b>如(ごと)し</b>
  The comparison needs の after a noun. Dropping it turns the phrase into the
  dismissive suffix, which is a different meaning entirely.

- ❌ 私(わたし)の<b>ごとき</b>者(もの)には無理(むり)です → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>ごとき</b>者(もの)には無理(むり)です
  And the reverse: the dismissive use takes no の. With の, the sentence turns into
  "a person resembling me", which is not what you meant.

- ❌ 流(なが)れる<b>のごとく</b> → ✅ 流(なが)れる<b>が如(ごと)く</b>／流(なが)れる<b>如(ごと)く</b>
  After a verb the classical linker is が, not の. Modern `ように` takes neither,
  which is where the confusion starts.

- ❌ お前(まえ)<b>ごとき</b>に教(おし)えていただきたいです。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>ごとき</b>にご指導(しどう)いただければ幸(さいわ)いです。
  <code>ごとき</code> aimed at the listener is an insult. Aimed at yourself it is
  humility. Getting the direction wrong is not a grammar error but a serious social
  one.

- ❌（友(とも)だちに）昨日(きのう)のケーキ、雲(くも)の<b>如(ごと)く</b>やわらかかったよ。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)のケーキ、雲(くも)<b>みたいに</b>やわらかかったよ。
  In conversation the simile word is `みたい`. <code>如(ごと)く</code> over a
  slice of cake is a joke whether you meant one or not.

## Practice

**1.** 報告書(ほうこくしょ)の書(か)き出(だ)し: 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)は下記(かき)の____報告(ほうこく)いたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

下記(かき)の<b>如(ごと)く</b>

<code>上記(じょうき)の如(ごと)く</code> and
<code>下記(かき)の如(ごと)く</code> are frozen phrases of documentary
Japanese, and they are among the very few places a modern writer still produces
<code>ごとく</code>. The everyday equivalent, 下記(かき)のように, is also correct
and slightly less starched.

</details>

**2.** Choose, and say why: 新人(しんじん)の私(わたし)（ごとき・のごとき）が司会(しかい)を務(つと)めてよいのでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)<b>ごとき</b>が司会(しかい)を務(つと)めてよいのでしょうか。

The dismissive-humble use takes no の. With の the phrase would mean "someone
resembling me", which is not the point - the speaker is lowering *themselves*
before accepting a role, which is a standard and genuinely useful politeness move.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 彼女(かのじょ)は何事(なにごと)もなかったかの<b>如(ごと)く</b>席(せき)に戻(もど)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)は何事(なにごと)もなかった<b>かのように</b>席(せき)に戻(もど)った。

Only one word changes: <code>かの如(ごと)く</code> → `かのように`. The かの
carries the "but it was not so" meaning in both versions, so the classical form is
adding nothing here except a century.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 川(かわ)が流(なが)れるのごとく、時間(じかん)が過(す)ぎていった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 川(かわ)が流(なが)れる<b>が如(ごと)く</b>、時間(じかん)が過(す)ぎていった。
In modern Japanese: 川(かわ)の流(なが)れのように、時間(じかん)が過(す)ぎていった。

After a verb, classical <code>如(ごと)し</code> is linked with が, not の. The
の form is only for nouns: 川(かわ)の如(ごと)く.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 素人(しろうと)のような意見(いけん)で申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。
(b) 素人(しろうと)ごときの意見(いけん)で申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain, mild apology: the opinion is an amateur's. It is neutral and could
be said by anyone in a meeting.

(b) puts the speaker down harder. <code>ごとき</code> attached to
素人(しろうと) says "a mere amateur, of no account", and coming from the speaker
about themselves it reads as marked humility - appropriate in a formal setting,
faintly overdone among colleagues. Said of *someone else*
(素人(しろうと)ごときの意見(いけん)は聞(き)く必要(ひつよう)がない) the
same phrase is contemptuous.

</details>

# ～べくして

**Meaning** · it happened exactly as it was bound to - the same verb twice, with everything in between saying "this was no accident"

<code>～べくして～た</code> repeats one verb around <code>べくして</code>:
起(お)こる<b>べくして</b>起(お)こった, "it happened as it was always going to
happen". The speaker is not reporting the event but delivering a verdict on it -
the conditions were there, and anyone looking could have seen it coming. It is
written and spoken commentary, not conversation.

## Formation

<code>べくして</code> is <code>べく</code>, the adverbial form of classical `べし`,
plus `して`. Like the rest of the family it follows the **dictionary form**, and the
second verb - the same verb - goes into the **past**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary + べくして + same verb, past | 勝(か)つ → 勝(か)つべくして勝(か)った |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary + べくして + same verb, past | 負(ま)ける → 負(ま)けるべくして負(ま)けた |
| Verb (する) | するべくして / すべくして | 失敗(しっぱい)する → 失敗(しっぱい)するべくして失敗(しっぱい)した |
| Passive | ～れるべくして～れた | 選(えら)ばれる → 選(えら)ばれるべくして選(えら)ばれた |
| Before a noun | ～べくして～た + noun | 起(お)こるべくして起(お)こった事故(じこ) |
| Sentence-final, formal | ～べくして～たのである | 起(お)こるべくして起(お)こったのである |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the two verbs must be **the same verb**. This is what makes the pattern
recognisable at a glance, and it is also its limit: you cannot say "it failed as it
was bound to be cancelled". If you find yourself wanting two different verbs, the
pattern you want is 当然(とうぜん)の結果(けっか)として or ～のは当然(とうぜん)だ.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの事故(じこ)は起(お)こる<b>べくして</b>起(お)こった。
That accident happened exactly as it was bound to.
</pre>

<pre>
チームは勝(か)つ<b>べくして</b>勝(か)った。
The team won as it was always going to.
</pre>

<pre>
点検(てんけん)を怠(おこた)っていたのだから、故障(こしょう)は起(お)きる<b>べくして</b>起(お)きたと言(い)える。
Inspections had been neglected, so it is fair to say the breakdown was waiting to happen.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は選(えら)ばれる<b>べくして</b>選(えら)ばれた代表(だいひょう)だ。
She is a representative who was always going to be chosen.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)不足(ぶそく)のまま進(すす)めた以上(いじょう)、この計画(けいかく)は失敗(しっぱい)する<b>べくして</b>失敗(しっぱい)した。
Since it was pushed ahead without proper preparation, the project failed as it could only have failed.
</pre>

<pre>
監督(かんとく)は会見(かいけん)で、「負(ま)ける<b>べくして</b>負(ま)けた試合(しあい)です」と語(かた)った。
At the press conference the manager said, "It was a match we lost the way we deserved to lose."
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)基準(きじゅん)が長年(ながねん)守(まも)られていなかったことを考(かんが)えれば、今回(こんかい)の事故(じこ)は起(お)こる<b>べくして</b>起(お)こったものである。
Given that the safety standards had gone unobserved for years, this accident was one that was bound to occur.
</pre>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)は出会(であ)う<b>べくして</b>出会(であ)ったのだと、後(のち)に彼(かれ)は書(か)いている。
The two of them were always going to meet, he wrote later.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is not a phrase you build; it is a phrase you quote.** The one instance you
will meet again and again is
<code>起(お)こるべくして起(お)こった</code>, said of an accident, a scandal or
a collapse. After that come
<code>勝(か)つべくして勝(か)った</code> and
<code>負(ま)けるべくして負(ま)けた</code> in sport, and
<code>失敗(しっぱい)するべくして失敗(しっぱい)した</code> in business
commentary. Outside that short list the pattern is rare.

Register is worth being exact about. Unlike the other べ- forms in this group, this
one **is** heard - from commentators, coaches, analysts and journalists speaking on
air, and from educated adults discussing a news story. What it is not is
conversational: you would not say it to a friend about a spilled drink. Think of it
as the register of a considered public remark.

The judgement it carries is **backward-looking and slightly accusatory**. Saying
起(お)こるべくして起(お)こった about an accident is not neutral - it means
someone failed to prevent something foreseeable, and the sentence usually arrives
with the negligence named in the clause before it. The positive uses
(勝(か)つべくして勝(か)った, 出会(であ)うべくして出会(であ)った) carry
the same logic of inevitability without the blame, and the second of those has a
faintly romantic ring.

Note that <code>べくして</code> is not <code>べく</code> with an extra syllable.
<code>べく</code> looks **forward** to a purpose;
<code>べくして</code> looks **backward** at a result. The shared word `べし` is the
same, but reading one as the other will turn a sentence inside out.

## Contrast

### ～べくして～た vs ～のは当然(とうぜん)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical judgement, different packaging.
<code>～のは当然(とうぜん)だ</code> states it plainly and can be said to anyone;
<code>べくして</code> is the polished commentator's version.

</div>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)不足(ぶそく)だったのだから、負(ま)けたのは<b>当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
They hadn't trained enough, so of course they lost.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)不足(ぶそく)のチームは、負(ま)ける<b>べくして</b>負(ま)けた。
An under-trained team lost the way it was bound to lose.
</pre>

The first is what you say to a friend on the way home. The second is what the
newspaper prints the next morning. Two other plain equivalents are worth having:
<code>負(ま)けても無理(むり)はない</code> ("no wonder they lost") and
<code>当然(とうぜん)の結果(けっか)だ</code> ("it was the natural outcome").
All three are usable in speech; <code>べくして</code> is the one to leave for
reading and for a considered remark.

### ～べくして vs ～べく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>べく</code> is a **purpose** and points forward: "in
order to". <code>べくして</code> is a **result** and points backward: "as it was
bound to".

</div>

<pre>
事故(じこ)を防(ふせ)ぐ<b>べく</b>、会社(かいしゃ)は点検(てんけん)を強化(きょうか)した。
The company stepped up inspections in order to prevent accidents.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)は起(お)こる<b>べくして</b>起(お)こったと報告書(ほうこくしょ)は結論(けつろん)づけた。
The report concluded that the accident had been waiting to happen.
</pre>

The shapes give them away. <code>べく</code> is followed by a comma and a second
clause with a **different** verb; <code>べくして</code> is followed immediately by
the **same** verb in the past. If you see the verb repeated, you are looking at this
page's pattern, and "in order to" is the wrong reading.

### ～べくして～た vs ～はずだった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>べくして～た</code> says it **did** happen, and
inevitably. `はずだった` says it was **supposed** to happen - and usually implies it
did not.

</div>

<pre>
本来(ほんらい)なら今日(きょう)届(とど)く<b>はずだった</b>荷物(にもつ)が、まだ来(こ)ない。
The parcel that was supposed to arrive today still hasn't come.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は世(よ)に出(で)る<b>べくして</b>出(で)た作家(さっか)だ。
He is a writer who was always going to be discovered.
</pre>

`はずだった` is about an expectation, and the interest of the sentence is usually
that the expectation failed. <code>べくして</code> is about an outcome that arrived,
and it is the *reasons* that interest the speaker. Confusing them reverses the
facts, which is the worst kind of error to make with a pattern you only half
recognise.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 起(お)こった<b>べくして</b>起(お)こった → ✅ 起(お)こる<b>べくして</b>起(お)こった
  The first verb is the dictionary form. Only the second one is past. Note the
  okurigana as well: 起(お)こる has こ outside the kanji.

- ❌ 失敗(しっぱい)<b>しべくして</b>失敗(しっぱい)した → ✅ 失敗(しっぱい)<b>するべくして</b>失敗(しっぱい)した
  `する` gives するべくして or すべくして, never しべくして. In this particular
  frame するべくして is the commoner of the two.

- ❌ 練習(れんしゅう)する<b>べくして</b>勝(か)った → ✅ 勝(か)つ<b>べくして</b>勝(か)った
  The two verbs must match. With different verbs the sentence loses its shape and a
  Japanese reader will simply stop.

- ❌ 明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は勝(か)つ<b>べくして</b>勝(か)つだろう。 → ✅ 明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は勝(か)てるはずだ。
  The pattern is a verdict on something that has already happened. It cannot be used
  to predict.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)を防(ふせ)ぐ<b>べくして</b>、点検(てんけん)を増(ふ)やした。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)を防(ふせ)ぐ<b>べく</b>、点検(てんけん)を増(ふ)やした。
  "In order to" is <code>べく</code>, with no して. Adding して turns a purpose into
  a claim of inevitability, and the sentence stops meaning anything.

## Practice

**1.** 事故(じこ)の検証(けんしょう)記事(きじ): 警告(けいこく)が何度(なんど)も無視(むし)されていた。この事故(じこ)は起(お)こる____起(お)こったのである。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

起(お)こる<b>べくして</b>起(お)こった

The classic instance of the pattern, and the sentence before it does the standard
job of naming the negligence. Note the two forms: dictionary
起(お)こる first, past 起(お)こった second, and the same verb both times.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいているので、明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)（するべくして中止(ちゅうし)した・される可能性(かのうせい)が高(たか)い）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中止(ちゅうし)<b>される可能性(かのうせい)が高(たか)い</b>。

The sentence is about tomorrow. <code>べくして</code> passes judgement on something
that has already happened and cannot look forward, so it is not merely stiff here -
it is impossible.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary spoken Japanese: あのチームは負(ま)ける<b>べくして</b>負(ま)けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あのチーム、負(ま)けても仕方(しかた)なかったよね。
Or: あのチームが負(ま)けたのは当然(とうぜん)だと思(おも)う。

Both keep the judgement - the defeat was deserved and predictable - while dropping
the commentator's voice. In conversation, 当然(とうぜん)だ and
仕方(しかた)がない carry this idea without any classical grammar at all.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 安全(あんぜん)管理(かんり)がずさんだったので、事故(じこ)が起(お)こったべくして起(お)こりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 安全(あんぜん)管理(かんり)がずさんだったので、事故(じこ)は起(お)こる<b>べくして</b>起(お)こった。

Two repairs. The first verb must be the dictionary form
起(お)こる - <code>べく</code> and everything built on it attaches there. And the
polite ました does not belong with a classical ending; this pattern lives in plain
form, in writing or in a considered spoken remark.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)の失敗(しっぱい)は残念(ざんねん)な結果(けっか)だった。
(b) 今回(こんかい)の失敗(しっぱい)は失敗(しっぱい)する<b>べくして</b>失敗(しっぱい)したものだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) expresses regret and assigns no cause. It is the sort of line a company puts in
a short statement when it does not want to say more.

(b) is an accusation with the sympathy removed. It says the failure was foreseeable
and that nobody prevented it, and the reader will expect the next sentence to name
what went wrong. This is why journalists like the pattern: it delivers blame in a
form that sounds like calm analysis.

</details>
