# N1 - No matter what: concession

# ～(よ)うが / ～(よ)うと

**Meaning** · no matter what, no matter how - the main clause is settled in advance and nothing in the first clause can move it

The first half names something that might happen, or a whole range of things that
might happen. The second half then says: it makes no difference. Unlike `ても`,
which merely concedes one awkward case, <code>(よ)うが</code> and
<code>(よ)うと</code> sweep every case off the table at once, which is why they so
often stand next to 誰(だれ)が, 何(なに)を, どんなに or いくら.

## Formation

Both particles attach to the **volitional** form - the same `う`/`よう` you know
from <code>行(い)こう</code> and <code>食(た)べよう</code>. `が` and `と`
are interchangeable here.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + が／と | 言(い)う → 言(い)おうが／言(い)おうと |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + が／と | 負(ま)ける → 負(ま)けようが／負(ま)けようと |
| Verb (する) | しようが／しようと | 失敗(しっぱい)する → 失敗(しっぱい)しようと |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)ようが／来(こ)ようと | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようと |
| Verb (passive) | ～れようが／られようと | 笑(わら)われる → 笑(わら)われようと |
| い-adjective | ～かろうが／かろうと | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)かろうが |
| な-adjective | ～であろうが／であろうと | 下手(へた)だ → 下手(へた)であろうと |
| Noun | ～であろうが／であろうと | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)であろうと |
| Emphatic | ～(よ)うとも | 言(い)われようとも |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>高(たか)かろう</code> is the volitional of an い-adjective, and
outside this pattern you will hardly ever meet it. Learn it as part of the shape
rather than as a form you conjugate freely. <code>いい</code> gives
<code>よかろうが</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
誰(だれ)が何(なに)と<b>言(い)おうと</b>、私(わたし)の気持(きも)ちは変(か)わらない。
No matter what anyone says, my feelings will not change.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)ろうが</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われます。
Rain or no rain, the match goes ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>高(たか)かろうと</b>、この絵(え)を手放(てばな)すつもりはない。
However much it is worth, I have no intention of parting with this painting.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りに<b>笑(わら)われようが</b>、自分(じぶん)で決(き)めた道(みち)を行(い)く。
Let them laugh - I am taking the road I chose for myself.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)が社長(しゃちょう)<b>であろうと</b>、間違(まちが)っていることは間違(まちが)っている。
Even if the other party is the company president, what is wrong is wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
結果(けっか)がどう<b>なろうが</b>、挑戦(ちょうせん)したこと自体(じたい)に意味(いみ)がある。
Whatever the outcome turns out to be, there is value in having tried.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が<b>近(ちか)づこうと</b>、工事(こうじ)の期限(きげん)が延(の)びることはないらしい。
Typhoon or no typhoon, the construction deadline apparently is not going to move.
</pre>

<pre>
社内(しゃない)の規則(きそく)である以上(いじょう)、どんな事情(じじょう)が<b>あろうとも</b>例外(れいがい)は認(みと)められません。
As this is a company rule, no exception can be made whatever the circumstances.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The tone is **firm**. A sentence built on <code>(よ)うが</code> or
<code>(よ)うと</code> announces that the speaker has already finished deciding, and
it is at home in a declaration, an editorial, a company notice, a courtroom speech
or a character's moment of resolve in a novel. In conversation it is perfectly
usable, but it raises the temperature: 「何(なん)と言(い)われようと行(い)く」
is a good deal more immovable than 「何(なん)と言(い)われても行(い)く」.

`が` and `と` mean the same thing here. `と` is the lighter and slightly more
frequent of the two in speech; `が` has a faintly more literary, more combative
edge, and turns up more in writing. Adding `も` - <code>(よ)うとも</code> - makes
the sentence more emphatic and more formal again; there is no
❌<code>(よ)うがも</code>.

Two things the pattern likes. First, an **adverb of scale or a question word** in
front: たとえ, どんなに, いくら, 誰(だれ)が, 何(なに)を, どこで. Second, a main
clause that is a **standing position** - a decision, a rule, an assessment, a
refusal. It can report someone else's steadfastness in the past
(「止(と)められようと彼(かれ)は行(い)った」), but it does not sit well in
front of a hesitant guess, because a guess is exactly the thing the pattern says
has already been settled.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うと vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` concedes **one** inconvenient case and carries on.
<code>(よ)うと</code> sweeps **all** cases aside at once, and is emphatic where
`ても` is neutral.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>行(い)きます。
I will go even if it rains.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)ろうと</b>行(い)きます。
Rain or no rain, I am going.
</pre>

`ても` is also the far more flexible form. It can carry permission
(<code>入(はい)ってもいいですか</code>), it can be softened, and it fits any
register. <code>(よ)うと</code> can do none of that:
❌<code>入(はい)ろうといいですか</code> is not a sentence. If you only want to
grant one exception politely, `ても` is the form you actually need.

### ～(よ)うが vs ～(よ)うと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical in meaning. Pick one and **keep it** across a pair:
泣(な)こうが騒(さわ)こうが, not a `が` followed by a `と`.

</div>

<pre>
泣(な)こう<b>が</b>騒(さわ)こう<b>が</b>、決定(けってい)は覆(くつがえ)らない。
Cry, shout, do as you like - the decision will not be overturned.
</pre>

<pre>
暑(あつ)かろう<b>と</b>寒(さむ)かろう<b>と</b>、彼(かれ)は毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っている。
Hot or cold, he runs every morning.
</pre>

The doubled shape is a favourite of the pattern and reads as "whatever the case
may be". Mixing the particles inside one pair is not exactly ungrammatical, but it
sounds careless, in the way that "neither this nor also that" does in English.

### ～(よ)うと vs ～としても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `としても` **supposes one case** and reasons about it.
<code>(よ)うと</code> **refuses to care** which case it is.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)る<b>としても</b>、たいした量(りょう)にはならないだろう。
Even supposing it snows tomorrow, it will not amount to much.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雪(ゆき)が<b>降(ふ)ろうと</b>、予定(よてい)は変(か)えません。
Even if it snows tomorrow, I am not changing the plan.
</pre>

The first sentence is a piece of reasoning: grant the snow, and here is what
follows about the snow. The second refuses to reason at all - the plan was fixed
before the weather was mentioned. That is why the main clause after
<code>(よ)うと</code> so rarely contains かもしれない or だろう: a hedge undoes the
whole point.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 何(なに)を言(い)う<b>と</b>、気(き)にしない。 → ✅ 何(なに)を<b>言(い)おうと</b>、気(き)にしない。
  The particle attaches to the volitional form, not the dictionary form. A plain
  <code>言(い)うと</code> is the ordinary "when/if" conditional and means
  something else entirely.

- ❌ どんなに<b>高(たか)いだろうが</b> → ✅ どんなに<b>高(たか)かろうが</b>
  い-adjectives have their own volitional in `かろう`.
  <code>高(たか)いだろう</code> is a guess about the price, and pasting `が` on
  the end just gives you "it's probably expensive, but".

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>ようと</b>責任(せきにん)はある。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>であろうと</b>責任(せきにん)はある。
  Nouns and な-adjectives need <code>である</code> before the volitional ending.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が<b>来(き)ようと</b> → ✅ 彼(かれ)が<b>来(こ)ようと</b>
  来(く)る is irregular: its volitional is <b>こよう</b>, the same stem as
  来(こ)ない.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)ろうと</b>、中止(ちゅうし)になるかもしれない。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>としても</b>、中止(ちゅうし)にはならないだろう。
  The pattern promises that the main clause is immovable. Following it with a
  guess about that very case contradicts the promise.

## Practice

**1.** 周(まわ)りに何(なに)と____、この研究(けんきゅう)は続(つづ)けるつもりだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)と<b>言(い)われようと</b>（or 言(い)われようが）

The speaker's plan was fixed before anyone opened their mouth, which is the
signature of this pattern. The passive <code>言(い)われる</code> takes the
volitional <code>言(い)われよう</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 試験(しけん)に落(お)ちる（としても・ようと）、もう一度(いちど)受(う)ければいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

試験(しけん)に落(お)ちる<b>としても</b>、もう一度(いちど)受(う)ければいい。

Here the speaker is reasoning about one supposed case - failing - and drawing a
consequence from it. That is `としても`. <code>落(お)ちようと</code> would claim
the outcome is irrelevant, but the sentence is entirely about what to do if that
particular outcome happens.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Whether the customer is a child or an adult, the service we give is the same."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お客様(きゃくさま)が子供(こども)<b>であろうと</b>大人(おとな)<b>であろうと</b>、提供(ていきょう)するサービスは同(おな)じです。

Nouns take <code>であろうと</code>, and the doubled shape is the natural way to
line up two alternatives. Keeping `と` in both halves rather than switching to `が`
is the tidier choice.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: どんなに忙(いそが)しいだろうが、健康(けんこう)診断(しんだん)だけは受(う)けてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ どんなに<b>忙(いそが)しかろうと</b>、健康(けんこう)診断(しんだん)だけは受(う)けてください。

<code>忙(いそが)しい</code> is an い-adjective, so its volitional is
<code>忙(いそが)しかろう</code>. In everyday speech most people would simply say
<code>どんなに忙(いそが)しくても</code>; the かろう form belongs to the firmer,
more written register.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 誰(だれ)に反対(はんたい)され<b>ても</b>、私(わたし)はこの案(あん)を出(だ)します。
(b) 誰(だれ)に反対(はんたい)され<b>ようとも</b>、私(わたし)はこの案(あん)を出(だ)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the ordinary way to say it. The speaker expects opposition and is saying
that it will not stop them. It could be said mildly, over coffee.

(b) is a declaration. The volitional plus `とも` raises the register and the
temperature together: this is the line you deliver standing up in a meeting, or
that a character says in a novel just before walking out. The propositional
content is the same; what changes is that (b) presents the decision as already
beyond discussion.

</details>

# ～(よ)うが○○まいが / ～(よ)うと○○まいと

**Meaning** · whether one does it or not - both branches lead to the same place, so the choice is irrelevant

The same verb appears twice: once in the volitional, once in `まい`, which is the
**negative volitional** - "won't / shall not". Put the two side by side and you
have covered the entire field, yes and no, with nothing left over. The main clause
then holds no matter which branch turns out to be true.

## Formation

`が` and `と` are interchangeable, but the two halves must **match**: `が…が` or
`と…と`, never one of each.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + が／と ＋ dictionary + まい | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)ろうが降(ふ)るまいが |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional ＋ dictionary or stem + まい | 信(しん)じる → 信(しん)じようと信(しん)じまいと |
| Verb (する) | しよう ＋ しまい／すまい／するまい | 参加(さんか)する → 参加(さんか)しようが参加(さんか)しまいが |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)よう ＋ 来(こ)まい／来(く)るまい | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようと来(こ)まいと |
| ある | あろう ＋ あるまい | ある → あろうがあるまいが |
| Noun / な-adjective | ～であろうと ＋ ～ではなかろうと | 会員(かいいん) → 会員(かいいん)であろうとなかろうと |

<div class="note">

**Note** - る-verbs allow two shapes of `まい`. <code>信(しん)じまい</code>
(stem) is the older and more literary one; <code>信(しん)じるまい</code>
(dictionary form) is what most people write today. Both are accepted, and the pair
reads naturally either way.

</div>

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb has to be the **same verb** on both sides. This is a
yes-or-no fork, not a list of two different activities. ❌<code>行(い)こうが休(やす)むまいが</code>
is not a possible sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)ろうが降(ふ)るまいが</b>、明日(あした)は出(で)かけます。
Whether it rains or not, I am going out tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が<b>来(こ)ようと来(こ)まいと</b>、会議(かいぎ)は時間(じかん)どおりに始(はじ)めます。
Whether he turns up or not, the meeting starts on time.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>信(しん)じようが信(しん)じまいが</b>、これは実際(じっさい)にあったことだ。
Believe it or not, this actually happened.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)が<b>望(のぞ)もうと望(のぞ)むまいと</b>、うわさは広(ひろ)がっていくものだ。
Whether the person wants it or not, a rumour spreads all the same.
</pre>

<pre>
当日(とうじつ)<b>参加(さんか)しようが参加(さんか)しまいが</b>、会費(かいひ)は払(はら)う決(き)まりになっている。
Whether you show up on the day or not, the rules say you pay the fee.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>売(う)れようが売(う)れまいが</b>、自分(じぶん)が作(つく)りたいものを作(つく)り続(つづ)けたい。
Sell or not sell, I want to go on making what I want to make.
</pre>

<pre>
周囲(しゅうい)が<b>認(みと)めようと認(みと)めまいと</b>、彼女(かのじょ)が残(のこ)した功績(こうせき)は消(き)えない。
Whether the people around her acknowledge it or not, what she achieved does not vanish.
</pre>

<pre>
景気(けいき)が<b>回復(かいふく)しようがしまいが</b>、来年度(らいねんど)の予算(よさん)は削(けず)られることになりそうだ。
Whether or not the economy picks up, next year's budget looks set to be cut.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **formal, emphatic** construction, and it belongs to writing, speeches,
editorials and firm statements in meetings. The everyday version of the same idea
is <code>降(ふ)っても降(ふ)らなくても</code>, and that is what you say to a
friend.

The `まい` half is worth pausing on. On its own, `まい` is the classical negative
volitional - <code>もう言(い)うまい</code>, "I shall say no more" - and in
modern speech that use is genuinely archaic: it survives in novels, in older
characters' dialogue, and in the set phrase
<code>あるまいし</code>. Inside this doubled frame, though, `まい` is alive and
well, because there is no other way to build the shape. Recognise the two uses as
the same word wearing different clothes.

The attitude the pattern carries is **indifference bordering on dismissal**. It
says the other side's decision has no purchase on what happens next, which can be
brave (「売(う)れようが売(う)れまいが」) or cold, depending on whose choice
you are brushing aside. Used about a person standing in front of you - 「君(きみ)が来(こ)ようと来(こ)まいと」 -
it is blunt, and will be heard that way.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うが○○まいが vs ～ても～なくても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register.
<code>～ても～なくても</code> is ordinary Japanese; the volitional-plus-`まい`
version is formal and considerably more emphatic.

</div>

<pre>
行(い)っ<b>ても行(い)かなくても</b>、結果(けっか)は同(おな)じだよ。
Whether you go or not, the result's the same.
</pre>

<pre>
行(い)こ<b>うが行(い)くまいが</b>、結果(けっか)は変(か)わらない。
Go or do not go - the result will not change.
</pre>

The first could be said across a kitchen table. The second sounds like a verdict.
When you are not sure which to use in speech, the `ても～なくても` version is never
wrong.

### ～(よ)うが○○まいが vs ～かどうかにかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>かどうかにかかわらず</code> is **neutral and
administrative**: it states that a condition is not a factor.
<code>(よ)うが○○まいが</code> is **personal and emphatic**: it brushes the
condition aside.

</div>

<pre>
経験(けいけん)が<b>あるかどうかにかかわらず</b>、応募(おうぼ)できます。
You may apply regardless of whether you have experience.
</pre>

<pre>
経験(けいけん)が<b>あろうがあるまいが</b>、やる気(き)がなければ続(つづ)かない。
Experience or no experience, without motivation nobody lasts.
</pre>

Swap them and you can hear the problem. In a job advertisement the volitional
version sounds as though the company is arguing with the reader; in the second
sentence <code>かかわらず</code> would drain out the speaker's opinion, which was
the whole point.

### ～(よ)うが○○まいが vs ～(よ)うが on its own

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the doubled form covers exactly **two** branches, yes and no.
The single form covers an **open range**, and normally has a question word or a
scale adverb in front of it.

</div>

<pre>
何(なに)を<b>言(い)おうと</b>、彼(かれ)は耳(みみ)を貸(か)さないだろう。
Whatever you say, he will not listen.
</pre>

<pre>
意見(いけん)を<b>言(い)おうと言(い)うまいと</b>、彼(かれ)は耳(みみ)を貸(か)さないだろう。
Whether you voice an opinion or keep quiet, he will not listen.
</pre>

The first sentence is about the content of what you say and admits infinitely many
fillings. The second is about a single binary decision - speak, or do not speak.
If you can complete the sentence with a question word, use the single form.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)こうが<b>行(い)かないが</b> → ✅ 行(い)こうが<b>行(い)くまいが</b>
  The negative half is built with `まい`, not with the plain negative. A
  <code>ない</code> here leaves the `が` with nothing to do.

- ❌ 行(い)こう<b>が</b>行(い)くまい<b>と</b> → ✅ 行(い)こう<b>が</b>行(い)くまい<b>が</b>
  Choose `が` or `と` and use the same one twice.

- ❌ <b>来(き)ようが来(き)まいが</b> → ✅ <b>来(こ)ようが来(こ)まいが</b>
  来(く)る is irregular. Both halves take the こ stem here; the alternative
  negative is <code>来(く)るまい</code>, with the dictionary form intact.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>だろうが学生(がくせい)じゃあるまいが</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>であろうとなかろうと</b>
  Nouns do not take `まい`. They use
  <code>であろうとなかろうと</code>, built on <code>である</code> and its negative.

- ❌ 来(こ)ようが来(こ)まいが、ぜひ来(き)てください。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)があれば、ぜひ来(き)てください。
  The pattern declares the branch irrelevant, so it cannot be followed by an
  invitation that depends on that very branch.

## Practice

**1.** 試合(しあい)に____、この一年(いちねん)の練習(れんしゅう)は無駄(むだ)にならない。（勝(か)つ）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

試合(しあい)に<b>勝(か)とうが勝(か)つまいが</b>（or 勝(か)とうと勝(か)つまいと）

<code>勝(か)つ</code> is an う-verb, so the volitional is
<code>勝(か)とう</code> and the `まい` half keeps the dictionary form. The main
clause holds on both branches, which is what the pattern requires.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会員(かいいん)（であろうとなかろうと・であろうがあるまいが）、入場(にゅうじょう)料(りょう)は同(おな)じです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会員(かいいん)<b>であろうとなかろうと</b>、入場(にゅうじょう)料(りょう)は同(おな)じです。

<code>会員(かいいん)</code> is a noun, and nouns pair
<code>であろう</code> with <code>なかろう</code>. The `まい` form belongs to verbs.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Whether the client agrees or not, we cannot change the delivery date."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先方(せんぽう)が<b>納得(なっとく)しようがしまいが</b>、納期(のうき)を変(か)えることはできません。

<code>納得(なっとく)する</code> is a する-verb, so the pair is
<code>しよう</code> plus <code>しまい</code>. The formal register of the pattern
suits a sentence about contractual terms.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)が謝(あやま)ろうが許(ゆる)すまいが、私(わたし)の気持(きも)ちは変(か)わらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)が<b>謝(あやま)ろうが謝(あやま)るまいが</b>、私(わたし)の気持(きも)ちは変(か)わらない。

The two halves must be the same verb; as written, the sentence forks between "he
apologises" and "someone does not forgive", which is not a fork at all. If you
really want two different actions, use
<code>謝(あやま)ろうと黙(だま)っていようと</code> - the doubled
<code>(よ)うと</code> without `まい`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 参加(さんか)し<b>てもしなくても</b>、連絡(れんらく)だけはください。
(b) 参加(さんか)し<b>ようがしまいが</b>、連絡(れんらく)だけはください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a normal, friendly request. It simply removes the condition: come or don't
come, just let me know either way.

(b) says the same thing with a hard edge. The formality of the volitional pair
makes the sentence sound like a rule being laid down, and the dismissive flavour of
the construction - your decision does not matter to me, only the message does - can
read as irritation. To a colleague, (a) is almost always the right choice.

</details>

# ～であれ / ～であろうと

**Meaning** · whoever it is, whatever it is, wherever it is - the identity of the thing makes no difference to what follows

<code>であれ</code> is the imperative of <code>である</code>, pressed into service
as a concession: "be it what it may". <code>であろうと</code> is the same idea
built on the volitional instead. Both attach to nouns, both are formal, and both
say that the category named in front of them buys you no exemption.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | ～であれ | 誰(だれ) → 誰(だれ)であれ |
| Noun | ～であろうと／であろうとも | 理由(りゆう) → どんな理由(りゆう)であろうと |
| な-adjective | stem + であれ／であろうと | 困難(こんなん)だ → どれほど困難(こんなん)であれ |
| Question word | どこ／いつ／何(なん)／どちら + であれ | 何(なん)であれ、どこであれ |
| With たとえ | たとえ + noun + であれ | たとえ相手(あいて)が子供(こども)であれ |
| い-adjective | **not available** - use ～かろうと | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)かろうと |
| Verb | **not available** - use ～(よ)うと | 行(い)く → 行(い)こうと |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun in front is almost always **indefinite**: a question
word, どんな＋noun, どれほど＋noun, or a noun introduced by たとえ. Plugging in a
plain specific noun with nothing to set up the concession
(❌<code>田中(たなか)さんであれ、…</code>) leaves the sentence hanging.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
誰(だれ)<b>であれ</b>、この部屋(へや)に入(はい)ることはできません。
No one may enter this room, whoever they are.
</pre>

<pre>
理由(りゆう)が何(なん)<b>であれ</b>、暴力(ぼうりょく)は許(ゆる)されない。
Whatever the reason, violence cannot be excused.
</pre>

<pre>
どんな仕事(しごと)<b>であろうと</b>、最初(さいしょ)の一年(いちねん)はつらいものだ。
Whatever the job, the first year is hard.
</pre>

<pre>
場所(ばしょ)はどこ<b>であれ</b>、みんなで集(あつ)まって話(はな)せればそれでいい。
The venue can be anywhere - what matters is that we can all meet and talk.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ相手(あいて)が子供(こども)<b>であれ</b>、一度(いちど)した約束(やくそく)は守(まも)るべきだ。
Even if the other person is a child, a promise once made should be kept.
</pre>

<pre>
どれほど有名(ゆうめい)な学者(がくしゃ)<b>であろうとも</b>、誤(あやま)りを指摘(してき)されたら認(みと)めるほかない。
However eminent the scholar, when an error is pointed out there is nothing to do but admit it.
</pre>

<pre>
一時(いちじ)の感情(かんじょう)<b>であれ</b>、口(くち)に出(だ)してしまった言葉(ことば)は取(と)り消(け)せない。
Even if it was a passing mood, words once spoken cannot be taken back.
</pre>

<pre>
例外(れいがい)を一(ひと)つ認(みと)めれば、それがどんなに小(ちい)さなもの<b>であろうと</b>、制度(せいど)全体(ぜんたい)が揺(ゆ)らぐ。
Allow one exception, however small, and the whole system starts to give way.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal, and mostly **written**: notices, contracts, rules, editorials, the summing
up of an argument. It is not archaic - a manager will say
「理由(りゆう)が何(なん)であれ、報告(ほうこく)は必要(ひつよう)です」
without sounding odd - but it is the register of someone stating policy, not of
someone chatting. Among friends the same thought comes out as
<code>誰(だれ)でも</code> or <code>何(なん)でも</code>, or in the blunter
spoken <code>誰(だれ)だろうと</code>.

Between the two forms, <code>であれ</code> is short, crisp and slightly clipped -
it reads as a principle stated flatly. <code>であろうと</code> is heavier and more
argumentative, and adding `も` (<code>であろうとも</code>) pushes it further still,
towards a speech. Neither is more polite than the other; both take their politeness
from the main clause.

The pattern also has a strong preference about what the main clause contains. It
wants something that holds **without exception** - a rule, a prohibition, a
judgement, a refusal. It sits badly in front of a hesitant guess, because the whole
force of <code>であれ</code> is that the question of identity has already been
closed.

## Contrast

### ～であれ vs ～でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>誰(だれ)でも</code> means "anyone at all" and is
usually **permissive**. <code>誰(だれ)であれ</code> means "whoever it might be"
and is **concessive**: it names an expectation and refuses it.

</div>

<pre>
この図書館(としょかん)は誰(だれ)<b>でも</b>利用(りよう)できます。
Anyone can use this library.
</pre>

<pre>
利用者(りようしゃ)が誰(だれ)<b>であれ</b>、規則(きそく)は同(おな)じように適用(てきよう)されます。
Whoever the user is, the rules apply in the same way.
</pre>

The first sentence opens a door. The second closes off an escape route: you may
have thought that some particular person would be treated differently, and they
will not be. `でも` is also ordinary spoken Japanese and fits any register, while
<code>であれ</code> raises the sentence to the level of a stated principle.

### ～であろうと vs ～だろうと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical in meaning; <code>であろうと</code> is the written
form and <code>だろうと</code> is its everyday spoken counterpart.

</div>

<pre>
相手(あいて)が誰(だれ)<b>だろうと</b>、言(い)うことは言(い)うよ。
Whoever it is, I'll say what I have to say.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)が誰(だれ)<b>であろうと</b>、申(もう)し上(あ)げるべきことは申(もう)し上(あ)げます。
Whoever the other party may be, I shall say what needs to be said.
</pre>

<code>だろうと</code> is what you will actually hear. It is casual and a little
combative, and it goes happily with contracted, plain-form speech.
<code>であろうと</code> in the same conversation would sound as if the speaker had
started reading from a document.

### ～であれ vs ～にしろ／にせよ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>であれ</code> says the identity is **irrelevant**.
<code>にしろ／にせよ</code> grants a case and then usually adds **advice or a
qualification** about it.

</div>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)<b>にせよ</b>、言(い)っていいことと悪(わる)いことがある。
Even as a joke, there are things you should and should not say.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)<b>であれ</b>本気(ほんき)<b>であれ</b>、一度(いちど)傷(きず)つけた相手(あいて)は戻(もど)らない。
Joke or earnest, someone you have hurt does not come back.
</pre>

The two overlap and often either will pass. Where they part company is in what the
sentence is doing: <code>にしろ／にせよ</code> concedes one reading of a situation
and then pushes back on it, and it commonly stands alone with a single item.
<code>であれ</code> is happiest when it is sweeping a whole range - which is why it
so often comes doubled.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 誰(だれ)<b>であれば</b>、規則(きそく)は同(おな)じだ。 → ✅ 誰(だれ)<b>であれ</b>、規則(きそく)は同(おな)じだ。
  <code>であれば</code> is the conditional of <code>である</code> - "if it is" -
  and it points the sentence the opposite way. The concessive form has no `ば`.

- ❌ どんなに高(たか)い<b>であれ</b>、買(か)います。 → ✅ どんなに<b>高(たか)かろうと</b>、買(か)います。
  <code>であれ</code> attaches to nouns and な-adjectives only. い-adjectives use
  their own volitional in `かろう`.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が行(い)く<b>であれ</b> → ✅ 彼(かれ)が<b>行(い)こうと</b>
  Verbs cannot take <code>であれ</code> either. The verb equivalent is the
  volitional plus `と` or `が`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>であれ</b>、中止(ちゅうし)かもしれない。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>であれ</b>、中止(ちゅうし)にはしません。
  The main clause has to hold regardless. Following the pattern with かもしれない
  reopens the question that <code>であれ</code> just closed.

## Practice

**1.** 事情(じじょう)が何(なん)____、無断(むだん)欠勤(けっきん)は認(みと)められません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

事情(じじょう)が何(なん)<b>であれ</b>（or 何(なん)であろうと）

A question word plus <code>であれ</code>, followed by a flat refusal, is the
standard shape. Note the reading: <code>何</code> is <b>なん</b> before で, not なに.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この施設(しせつ)は市民(しみん)（であれ・でも）無料(むりょう)で使(つか)えます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この施設(しせつ)は市民(しみん)<b>でも</b>無料(むりょう)で使(つか)えます。

The sentence grants something rather than refusing an exemption, and
<code>市民(しみん)</code> is a specific category with no question word to build
a concession on. <code>であれ</code> would need something like
<code>市民(しみん)であれ観光客(かんこうきゃく)であれ</code> - a sweep across
alternatives.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "However urgent the matter, decisions of this size are not made by one person."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

どれほど急(きゅう)を要(よう)する案件(あんけん)<b>であろうと</b>、これほどの決定(けってい)を一人(ひとり)で下(くだ)すことはありません。

<code>どれほど…であろうと</code> handles "however urgent" when the predicate is a
noun phrase, and the formal register matches a sentence about how decisions are
made. Note 一人(ひとり), the irregular reading of <code>一人</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: どんな理由(りゆう)であれば、締(し)め切(き)りを過(す)ぎた書類(しょるい)は受(う)け取(と)れません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ どんな理由(りゆう)<b>であれ</b>、締(し)め切(き)りを過(す)ぎた書類(しょるい)は受(う)け取(と)れません。

<code>であれば</code> would mean "if the reason is…", which contradicts
<code>どんな</code>. Dropping the `ば` gives the concessive form the sentence
needs. The single most common slip with this pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 相手(あいて)が誰(だれ)<b>でも</b>、同(おな)じ説明(せつめい)をします。
(b) 相手(あいて)が誰(だれ)<b>であれ</b>、同(おな)じ説明(せつめい)をします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain description of how the speaker works: everyone gets the same
explanation. It is neutral and could be said to anyone.

(b) has an edge of principle to it. The formal <code>であれ</code> implies that
someone might have expected special treatment - a senior person, an important
client - and that it will not be forthcoming. It is the sentence you use when you
are quietly refusing to make an exception, and it belongs to a more formal moment
than (a).

</details>

# ～であれ○○であれ

**Meaning** · whether A or B - the two alternatives between them cover the ground, and the result is the same for both

The doubled form takes two nouns, usually opposite ends of a range, and lines them
up so that nothing falls between them. Having covered the field, the sentence then
says the main clause holds throughout it. It is the noun counterpart of
<code>降(ふ)ろうが降(ふ)るまいが</code>.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun + noun | A であれ B であれ | 晴(は)れ → 晴(は)れであれ雨(あめ)であれ |
| Noun + noun | A であろうと B であろうと | 賛成(さんせい) → 賛成(さんせい)であろうと反対(はんたい)であろうと |
| な-adjective | stem + であれ | 派手(はで)だ → 派手(はで)であれ地味(じみ)であれ |
| Open-ended tail | A であれ B であれ、何(なん)であれ | 仕事(しごと)であれ趣味(しゅみ)であれ何(なん)であれ |
| Verbs | **not available** - use ～(よ)うと～(よ)うと | 行(い)こうと行(い)くまいと |
| い-adjectives | **not available** - use ～かろうと～かろうと | 安(やす)かろうと高(たか)かろうと |

<div class="note">

**Note** - one い-adjective pair survives as a frozen idiom:
<code>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</code>, "to a greater or lesser degree".
That `かれ` is the same imperative ending as the `あれ` of
<code>であれ</code>, but the pattern is no longer productive; you cannot build
❌<code>寒(さむ)かれ暑(あつ)かれ</code> from it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
晴(は)れ<b>であれ</b>雨(あめ)<b>であれ</b>、市場(いちば)は毎朝(まいあさ)開(ひら)きます。
Rain or shine, the market opens every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
大人(おとな)<b>であれ</b>子供(こども)<b>であれ</b>、ここでのルールは同(おな)じです。
Adult or child, the rules here are the same.
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)<b>であれ</b>出張(しゅっちょう)<b>であれ</b>、荷物(にもつ)は少(すく)ないほうがいい。
Holiday or business trip, the less luggage the better.
</pre>

<pre>
和食(わしょく)<b>であれ</b>洋食(ようしょく)<b>であれ</b>、素材(そざい)がよければおいしくなる。
Japanese or Western cooking, good ingredients make it taste good.
</pre>

<pre>
賛成(さんせい)<b>であろうと</b>反対(はんたい)<b>であろうと</b>、一度(いちど)は意見(いけん)を出(だ)してほしい。
For or against, I would like everyone to speak once.
</pre>

<pre>
正社員(せいしゃいん)<b>であれ</b>アルバイト<b>であれ</b>、職場(しょくば)での責任(せきにん)に違(ちが)いはない。
Permanent staff or part-timer, responsibility in the workplace is no different.
</pre>

<pre>
口頭(こうとう)<b>であれ</b>文書(ぶんしょ)<b>であれ</b>、一度(いちど)交(か)わした約束(やくそく)は約束(やくそく)である。
Spoken or written, a promise once exchanged is a promise.
</pre>

<pre>
理由(りゆう)が健康(けんこう)<b>であれ</b>家庭(かてい)の事情(じじょう)<b>であれ</b>、退職(たいしょく)の届(とど)けは一(いっ)か月(げつ)前(まえ)までに出(だ)す決(き)まりです。
Whether the reason is health or family circumstances, notice of resignation must be given a month in advance.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and mainly **written**. Its natural homes are rules, guidelines, editorials
and the summing-up sentence of an argument, and in speech it belongs to
presentations and prepared remarks rather than to conversation. The everyday
version is <code>A でも B でも</code>, and nobody will think twice if you use that.

What the pattern buys you over `でも…でも` is a sense of **exhaustiveness**. The
two nouns are chosen so that between them they leave no third option -
晴(は)れ／雨(あめ), 大人(おとな)／子供(こども),
賛成(さんせい)／反対(はんたい) - and the sentence then rules out every escape
at once. This is why it reads as principled rather than merely informative.

When the pair is not exhaustive, Japanese adds a third item to close the list:
<code>仕事(しごと)であれ趣味(しゅみ)であれ何(なん)であれ</code>, "work,
hobby, whatever it may be". That trailing <code>何(なん)であれ</code> is a very
common move and worth recognising.

<code>であろうと…であろうと</code> is the heavier variant: longer, more
argumentative, and slightly more likely in a speech than in a printed rule.

## Contrast

### ～であれ○○であれ vs ～でも○○でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning; <code>でも…でも</code> is everyday speech,
<code>であれ…であれ</code> is formal writing and sounds like a stated principle.

</div>

<pre>
肉(にく)<b>でも</b>魚(さかな)<b>でも</b>、何(なん)でも食(た)べるよ。
Meat, fish - I'll eat anything.
</pre>

<pre>
肉(にく)<b>であれ</b>魚(さかな)<b>であれ</b>、鮮度(せんど)が味(あじ)を決(き)める。
Meat or fish, freshness decides the flavour.
</pre>

Note what happens to the sentence around them. The `でも` version fits a relaxed
main clause with よ on the end; the <code>であれ</code> version pulls the whole
sentence up into the register of a general truth. Putting
<code>であれ</code> in front of a casual main clause produces an audible mismatch.

### ～であれ○○であれ vs ～にしろ○○にしろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>であれ…であれ</code> says the difference between A and B
is **irrelevant**. <code>にしろ…にしろ</code> lists two cases and usually goes on
to say what should be **done** about them.

</div>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>にしろ</b>しない<b>にしろ</b>、早(はや)めに返事(へんじ)をしてください。
Whether you're joining or not, please reply soon.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)者(しゃ)<b>であれ</b>見学(けんがく)者(しゃ)<b>であれ</b>、受付(うけつけ)での記名(きめい)が必要(ひつよう)です。
Participant or observer, everyone signs in at reception.
</pre>

The practical difference is what each can attach to.
<code>にしろ／にせよ</code> takes verbs and adjectives as happily as nouns, which
makes it far more flexible; <code>であれ</code> is restricted to nouns and
な-adjectives. If your two alternatives are actions,
<code>にしろ…にしろ</code> is usually the form you want.

### ～であれ○○であれ vs ～か○○か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>A か B か</code> says one of the two **is** the case and
asks which. <code>A であれ B であれ</code> says it **does not matter** which.

</div>

<pre>
原因(げんいん)は設計(せっけい)不良(ふりょう)<b>か</b>操作(そうさ)ミス<b>か</b>、まだ分(わ)かっていない。
Whether the cause was a design fault or an operating error is not yet known.
</pre>

<pre>
原因(げんいん)が設計(せっけい)不良(ふりょう)<b>であれ</b>操作(そうさ)ミス<b>であれ</b>、責任(せきにん)は会社(かいしゃ)にある。
Whether the cause was a design fault or an operating error, the responsibility lies with the company.
</pre>

The first sentence still has an open question in it; the investigation continues.
The second has closed the question by declaring it beside the point. Learners
sometimes reach for <code>であれ</code> when translating "whether A or B" in the
*indirect question* sense - ❌<code>来(く)るであれ来(こ)ないであれ知(し)りたい</code>
- but that sense needs <code>かどうか</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>であれ</b>行(い)かない<b>であれ</b> → ✅ <b>行(い)こうと行(い)くまいと</b>
  <code>であれ</code> attaches to nouns. Verbs make the same shape with the
  volitional and `まい`.

- ❌ 安(やす)い<b>であれ</b>高(たか)い<b>であれ</b> → ✅ <b>安(やす)かろうと高(たか)かろうと</b>
  い-adjectives use their own volitional in `かろう`. Only the frozen
  <code>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</code> keeps the old adjectival form.

- ❌ 大人(おとな)<b>であれ</b>子供(こども)<b>でも</b> → ✅ 大人(おとな)<b>であれ</b>子供(こども)<b>であれ</b>
  Keep the two halves parallel. Mixing registers mid-sentence is the written
  equivalent of changing your voice halfway through.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)<b>であれ</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われる。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>であれ</b>雪(ゆき)<b>であれ</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われる。
  A single <code>であれ</code> needs a question word or どんな in front of it. With
  a bare specific noun, the sentence reads as though a second half has gone
  missing.

## Practice

**1.** 新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)____管理職(かんりしょく)____、研修(けんしゅう)は全員(ぜんいん)受(う)けていただきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)<b>であれ</b>管理職(かんりしょく)<b>であれ</b>

Two nouns at opposite ends of the company, one rule covering both - the standard
shape. The polite <code>いただきます</code> in the main clause sits comfortably
with the formal register of the pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 犯人(はんにん)が男(おとこ)（であれ・か）女(おんな)（であれ・か）、まだ特定(とくてい)できていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

犯人(はんにん)が男(おとこ)<b>か</b>女(おんな)<b>か</b>、まだ特定(とくてい)できていない。

The main clause says the question is still open, so the sentence needs the
either-or of `か…か`. <code>であれ…であれ</code> would declare the question
irrelevant, which contradicts <code>まだ特定(とくてい)できていない</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Success or failure, the experience will not be wasted."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

成功(せいこう)<b>であれ</b>失敗(しっぱい)<b>であれ</b>、この経験(けいけん)が無駄(むだ)になることはない。

Two nouns that between them cover every outcome, followed by a main clause that
holds in both cases. <code>成功(せいこう)しようが失敗(しっぱい)しようが</code>
is equally good if you prefer to keep them as verbs.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 勝(か)つであれ負(ま)けるであれ、最後(さいご)まで全力(ぜんりょく)で戦(たたか)いたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ <b>勝(か)とうと負(ま)けようと</b>、最後(さいご)まで全力(ぜんりょく)で戦(たたか)いたい。

<code>勝(か)つ</code> and <code>負(ま)ける</code> are verbs, and verbs take
the volitional plus `と` or `が`. If you want to keep <code>であれ</code>, turn
them into nouns first: <code>勝(か)ちであれ負(ま)けであれ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 電話(でんわ)<b>でも</b>メール<b>でも</b>、連絡(れんらく)をください。
(b) 電話(でんわ)<b>であれ</b>メール<b>であれ</b>、連絡(れんらく)は必(かなら)ず入(い)れてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) offers a free choice of channel. It is friendly and slightly casual, and the
listener hears "either is fine by me".

(b) states a requirement. The formal <code>であれ</code> pair, reinforced by
必(かなら)ず, turns the sentence from an offer into a rule: the channel is up to
you, but making contact is not optional. In a team briefing (b) is what a manager
would say; to a friend it would sound heavy-handed.

</details>

# いかなる

**Meaning** · any kind of; whatever sort of - the formal, written equivalent of どんな, and almost always part of a sentence that admits no exceptions

`いかなる` sits in front of a noun and empties it of particulars: not this reason or
that reason, but reasons of every kind. It rarely appears in a neutral sentence.
Its natural habitat is the clause that says **no exception will be made**, which is
why contracts, notices and official statements are full of it.

## Usage

`いかなる` is a 連体詞(れんたいし): a word whose entire job is to stand in front of a noun.
It does not conjugate, takes neither `な` nor `の`, and cannot modify a verb or an
adjective - that job belongs to `いかに`.

- いかなる + noun → いかなる理由(りゆう), いかなる場合(ばあい), いかなる事情(じじょう)

Three frames account for nearly every occurrence:

- **いかなる + noun + でも／であれ／であろうと** - "whatever the …":
  <code>いかなる事情(じじょう)であれ</code>
- **いかなる + noun + も + negative** - "no … whatsoever":
  <code>いかなる変更(へんこう)も認(みと)められません</code>
- **いかなる + noun + があろうと／があっても** - "whatever … there may be":
  <code>いかなる反対(はんたい)があろうと</code>

It is written 如何(いか)なる in older text and in some legal documents; modern
practice is kana.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>いかなる</b>理由(りゆう)があろうと、遅刻(ちこく)は遅刻(ちこく)です。
Whatever the reason may be, late is late.
</pre>

<pre>
当社(とうしゃ)は<b>いかなる</b>責任(せきにん)も負(お)いかねます。
Our company accepts no liability of any kind.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いかなる</b>場合(ばあい)でも、非常口(ひじょうぐち)の前(まえ)に荷物(にもつ)を置(お)かないでください。
Under no circumstances place luggage in front of the emergency exit.
</pre>

<pre>
この協定(きょうてい)は、<b>いかなる</b>加盟国(かめいこく)に対(たい)しても同(おな)じ条件(じょうけん)を求(もと)めている。
This agreement imposes the same conditions on every member state without distinction.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Strictly **written and institutional**. Terms of service, insurance policies,
station notices, court judgements, government statements, the closing paragraph of
a formal letter - that is the whole range. Spoken aloud in ordinary conversation it
sounds exactly like what it is: someone reading from a document.
「いかなる理由(りゆう)があっても嫌(いや)だ」 said to a friend is a joke, and
will be heard as one.

The spoken equivalent is `どんな`, and it is the right choice virtually every time
you open your mouth. In a formal meeting or a prepared presentation
<code>いかなる</code> is fine, and it lends a sentence weight - but it is heavy
weight, so use it where you actually want the sound of a rule being stated.

Two things it likes to have around it. First, a **negative or a concession**: `も`
plus a negative for "none whatsoever", or `でも／であれ／があろうと` for "whatever
the". A wholly neutral <code>いかなる</code> sentence is rare and reads as
unfinished. Second, an **abstract noun** - 理由(りゆう), 場合(ばあい),
事情(じじょう), 状況(じょうきょう), 条件(じょうけん),
手段(しゅだん), 責任(せきにん). It goes oddly with concrete everyday objects;
<code>いかなるパン</code> is not a phrase.

## Contrast

### いかなる vs どんな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different world. `どんな` is spoken Japanese and
can ask a genuine question. <code>いかなる</code> is written Japanese and, in
practice, only makes concessions.

</div>

<pre>
<b>どんな</b>音楽(おんがく)が好(す)きですか。
What kind of music do you like?
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)の同意(どうい)なしに、<b>いかなる</b>情報(じょうほう)も第三者(だいさんしゃ)に提供(ていきょう)することはありません。
No information of any kind will be provided to third parties without the person's consent.
</pre>

The first sentence cannot take <code>いかなる</code>: asking a friend
「いかなる音楽(おんがく)が好(す)きですか」 is like asking "of what nature is
your musical preference". The second could take `どんな`, and would simply drop a
register - which in a privacy policy is not what you want.

### いかなる vs あらゆる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あらゆる` gathers everything **in** ("all of them, every
one"). <code>いかなる</code> shuts everything **out** ("any at all, and none of
them counts").

</div>

<pre>
救助(きゅうじょ)のために<b>あらゆる</b>手段(しゅだん)を尽(つ)くした。
They exhausted every possible means to carry out the rescue.
</pre>

<pre>
救助(きゅうじょ)を妨(さまた)げる<b>いかなる</b>行為(こうい)も許(ゆる)されない。
No act of any kind that obstructs the rescue is permissible.
</pre>

Both are 連体詞(れんたいし) and both are formal, so the shapes look identical. The test is
the main clause: `あらゆる` normally ends in a positive verb, because it is
collecting things together, while <code>いかなる</code> normally ends in a negative
or a refusal, because it is denying them one by one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>いかなるな</b>理由(りゆう) → ✅ <b>いかなる</b>理由(りゆう)
  It is already in noun-modifying shape. It takes neither `な` nor `の`, exactly
  like `どんな` before a noun.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は<b>いかなる</b>難(むずか)しい。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)が<b>いかに</b>難(むずか)しいか。
  <code>いかなる</code> modifies nouns only. To modify an adjective you want
  `いかに`, its adverbial partner.

- ❌ 週末(しゅうまつ)は<b>いかなる</b>予定(よてい)ですか。 → ✅ 週末(しゅうまつ)は<b>どんな</b>予定(よてい)ですか。
  As a real question to a real person, <code>いかなる</code> is unusable. It
  survives as a question only in rhetorical writing.

- ❌ <b>いかなる</b>事情(じじょう)が、規則(きそく)は規則(きそく)だ。 → ✅ <b>いかなる</b>事情(じじょう)<b>であれ</b>、規則(きそく)は規則(きそく)だ。
  The word needs a frame to work inside. On its own with a bare `が` it leaves the
  concession unbuilt.

## Practice

**1.** 契約(けいやく)期間(きかん)中(ちゅう)は、____変更(へんこう)も認(みと)められません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>いかなる</b>変更(へんこう)も認(みと)められません

The `も` plus a negative predicate is the frame that
<code>いかなる</code> was made for: not "some changes are refused" but "no change
of any kind". The contractual context also fits the register exactly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)の映画(えいが)は（いかなる・どんな）話(はなし)だったの。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)の映画(えいが)は<b>どんな</b>話(はなし)だったの。

A genuine question, asked casually, with の on the end.
<code>いかなる</code> belongs to written and institutional Japanese and would
clash with every other word in the sentence. Note the reading of <code>話</code> here:
<b>はなし</b>, the noun.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 当館(とうかん)では<b>あらゆる</b>資料(しりょう)を公開(こうかい)しています。
(b) 当館(とうかん)では<b>いかなる</b>資料(しりょう)も持(も)ち出(だ)せません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) gathers the collection up and offers it: every document we hold is open to the
public. `あらゆる` is doing inclusive work, and the verb is positive.

(b) closes the door on all of it: no document whatsoever may be removed from the
building. <code>いかなる</code> plus `も` plus a negative is an absolute
prohibition, and the sentence would be at home on a sign by the reading-room desk.
Swapping the two words would make (a) sound like a ban and (b) sound like an offer.

</details>

# いかに

**Meaning** · how; how much; in what way - the literary counterpart of どんなに and どのように

`いかに` measures a degree or asks after a manner, and it does so in a register a
notch or two above ordinary speech. Two shapes carry almost all of its work:
<code>いかに…ても</code>, "no matter how", and
<code>いかに…か</code>, "how very …" - the second being one of the most useful
sentence patterns in written Japanese.

## Usage

`いかに` is an adverb. It modifies verbs, adjectives and other adverbs, never a
noun directly - noun-modifying is `いかなる`.

- **Concessive**: いかに + adjective / verb + ても・とも・(よ)うと →
  <code>いかに急(いそ)いでも</code>, <code>いかに優(すぐ)れていようと</code>
- **Degree, embedded**: いかに + clause + か →
  <code>いかに大切(たいせつ)かが分(わ)かる</code>
- **Manner**: いかにして + clause + か →
  <code>いかにして防(ふせ)ぐかが問題(もんだい)だ</code>
- **Deliberative**: いかに + ～べきか →
  <code>いかに対応(たいおう)すべきか</code>

The concessive use is often reinforced by `とも`, giving the very literary
<code>いかに…(よ)うとも</code>. The degree use nearly always ends in a verb of
understanding or showing - 分(わ)かる, 示(しめ)す, 物語(ものがた)る,
痛感(つうかん)する - because the point of the clause is to hand the reader a
measurement.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>いかに</b>急(いそ)いでも、終電(しゅうでん)には間(ま)に合(あ)わない。
No matter how much we hurry, we will not make the last train.
</pre>

<pre>
この数字(すうじ)は、問題(もんだい)が<b>いかに</b>深刻(しんこく)かを示(しめ)している。
These figures show how serious the problem is.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いかに</b>優(すぐ)れた制度(せいど)であろうと、動(うご)かすのは人(ひと)である。
However fine a system may be, it is people who make it work.
</pre>

<pre>
限(かぎ)られた予算(よさん)で<b>いかにして</b>成果(せいか)を出(だ)すかが、今回(こんかい)の課題(かだい)だ。
How to produce results on a limited budget is the problem this time.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Written and formal.** Essays, editorials, lectures, speeches, book titles and
headlines - <code>いかに生(い)きるか</code> is practically a genre. In speech it
belongs to prepared remarks; dropped into casual conversation it sounds theatrical,
and 「いかに疲(つか)れていても行(い)くよ」 to a friend will get you a look.
The everyday forms are `どんなに` and `どれだけ`.

Of the two main uses, <code>いかに…か</code> is much the more current. A Japanese
newspaper will use it several times a page, and it does not feel old-fashioned at
all - it is simply the compact way to write "how badly", "how far", "how much".
The concessive <code>いかに…ても</code> is the heavier of the two and leans more
literary; in a business email most writers would choose
<code>どんなに…ても</code>.

`いかに` also survives in a handful of fixed shapes worth recognising:
<code>いかにも</code> (a separate word - see below), <code>いかがですか</code>
(the polite "how about it?", historically the same 如何(いか)),
and the rhetorical <code>いかんともしがたい</code>, "there is nothing to be done
about it".

## Contrast

### いかに vs どんなに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `どんなに` is what you say;
<code>いかに</code> is what you write. Only <code>いかに</code> takes the
<code>いかにして</code> manner form.

</div>

<pre>
<b>どんなに</b>練習(れんしゅう)しても、兄(あに)には勝(か)てない。
No matter how much I practise, I can't beat my older brother.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いかに</b>技術(ぎじゅつ)が進(すす)もうと、判断(はんだん)する責任(せきにん)は人間(にんげん)に残(のこ)る。
However far technology advances, the responsibility for judgement remains with people.
</pre>

Notice how the rest of each sentence follows the choice. `どんなに` sits with a
personal, everyday main clause; <code>いかに</code> pulls the sentence towards
abstraction and general truths. Mixing them - <code>いかに</code> in front of a
sentence ending in よ or ね - produces a clash you can hear.

### いかに vs いかにも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are **different words**. `いかにも` does not mean "how";
it means "every inch", "just as you would expect", and it usually leads to `らしい`
or `そうだ`.

</div>

<pre>
<b>いかに</b>寒(さむ)くても、朝(あさ)の散歩(さんぽ)は欠(か)かさない。
However cold it is, he never misses his morning walk.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いかにも</b>彼(かれ)らしい答(こた)えだ。
That is an answer just like him.
</pre>

The `も` changes everything. <code>いかにも高(たか)そうな時計(とけい)</code>
is "a watch that looks every bit as expensive as it is", not "no matter how
expensive". Because the two look nearly identical on the page, learners
occasionally write <code>いかにも</code> into a concessive sentence, where it makes
no sense at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>いかに</b>理由(りゆう)があっても → ✅ <b>いかなる</b>理由(りゆう)があっても
  <code>いかに</code> is an adverb and cannot modify the noun
  理由(りゆう). Its noun-modifying partner is `いかなる`.

- ❌ <b>いかに</b>難(むずか)しい、あきらめない。 → ✅ <b>いかに</b>難(むずか)しく<b>ても</b>、あきらめない。
  In the concessive use, <code>いかに</code> is only the first half of a frame. It
  needs `ても`, `とも` or a volitional plus `と` further down the clause.

- ❌ この本(ほん)は<b>いかに</b>面白(おもしろ)いですか。 → ✅ この本(ほん)が<b>いかに</b>面白(おもしろ)いか、読(よ)めば分(わ)かります。
  As a direct question to a listener it does not work. The degree use is embedded:
  it ends in `か` inside a larger sentence.

- ❌ <b>いかにも</b>急(いそ)いでも間(ま)に合(あ)わない。 → ✅ <b>いかに</b>急(いそ)いでも間(ま)に合(あ)わない。
  `いかにも` is the other word. Adding `も` here turns "no matter how" into
  something the sentence cannot use.

## Practice

**1.** この事故(じこ)は、点検(てんけん)が____大切(たいせつ)かを教(おし)えている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

点検(てんけん)が<b>いかに</b>大切(たいせつ)かを教(おし)えている

The embedded degree use: <code>いかに…か</code> hands the reader a measurement,
and the main verb is one of showing or teaching. This is the shape you will meet
most often in written Japanese.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （いかに・いかにも）職人(しょくにん)らしい、丁寧(ていねい)な仕事(しごと)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>いかにも</b>職人(しょくにん)らしい、丁寧(ていねい)な仕事(しごと)だ。

`らしい` is the giveaway. <code>いかにも…らしい</code> means "every inch a …",
a completely different word from the <code>いかに</code> of degree, which would
leave the sentence without anything to measure.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この制度(せいど)が<b>どんなに</b>複雑(ふくざつ)か、使(つか)ってみれば分(わ)かる。
(b) この制度(せいど)が<b>いかに</b>複雑(ふくざつ)か、使(つか)ってみれば分(わ)かる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The two say the same thing; what differs is the voice. (a) is how a person would
say it out loud - to a colleague, with a sigh. It could appear in writing too, but
it keeps a conversational warmth.

(b) reads as written argument. <code>いかに</code> lifts the sentence into the
register of an article or a report, and a reader will expect the sentences around
it to be equally formal - plain <code>である</code> style rather than
<code>ですます</code>, abstract nouns, no sentence-final particles.

</details>

# ～といえども

**Meaning** · even (though it is) X - names something you would expect to be an exception, and refuses it the exception

Literally "even though one says it is X". The `ども` is a classical concessive
ending, the same one in <code>言(い)えども</code> and <code>されども</code>, and
the whole expression belongs to written argument. It takes a category that carries
a presumption - a specialist, a king, a single day - and says the presumption does
not hold here.

**You will meet this in reading and almost never say it.** The modern equivalent is
<code>たとえ…でも</code> or <code>…であっても</code>.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | ～といえども | 専門家(せんもんか) → 専門家(せんもんか)といえども |
| Number + counter | ～といえども | 一日(いちにち) → 一日(いちにち)といえども |
| Verb (plain) | plain form + といえども | 知(し)っている → 知(し)っているといえども |
| い-adjective | plain form + といえども | 少(すく)ない → 少(すく)ないといえども |
| な-adjective | stem + である + といえども | 不便(ふべん)だ → 不便(ふべん)であるといえども |
| Reinforced | たとえ／どんなに + … + といえども | たとえ王(おう)といえども |
| Modern equivalent | ～であっても／たとえ～でも | 専門家(せんもんか)であっても |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a noun attaches **bare**. There is no
❌<code>専門家(せんもんか)だといえども</code>; the `だ` has no place in front of
the quotative `と` here.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>といえども</b>、法(ほう)の前(まえ)では大人(おとな)と同(おな)じである。
Even a child stands equal with an adult before the law.
</pre>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>といえども</b>、地震(じしん)の予知(よち)は容易(ようい)ではない。
Even for specialists, predicting earthquakes is far from easy.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)<b>といえども</b>、練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)むわけにはいかない。
Not even for a single day can practice be skipped.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに親(した)しい友人(ゆうじん)<b>といえども</b>、言(い)っていいことと悪(わる)いことがある。
However close the friend, there are things one may say and things one may not.
</pre>

<pre>
王(おう)<b>といえども</b>、民(たみ)の声(こえ)を無視(むし)することはできなかった。
Even the king could not ignore the voice of the people.
</pre>

<pre>
少額(しょうがく)<b>といえども</b>、公金(こうきん)であることに変(か)わりはない。
Small though the sum may be, it is public money all the same.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)を起(お)こしたのが新人(しんじん)だった<b>といえども</b>、管理(かんり)責任(せきにん)は会社(かいしゃ)が負(お)うべきである。
Even granted that it was a new employee who caused the accident, the company should bear responsibility for supervision.
</pre>

<pre>
緊急時(きんきゅうじ)<b>といえども</b>、定(さだ)められた手続(てつづ)きを省(はぶ)くことは認(みと)められない。
Even in an emergency, the prescribed procedures may not be skipped.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Start here: **this is written Japanese, and using it in speech will sound wrong.**
Its actual habitats are newspaper editorials and columns, legal and administrative
prose, formal speeches, academic writing, and fiction - especially historical
fiction, where a narrator says
<code>王(おう)といえども</code> and nobody blinks. A learner who says
「忙(いそが)しいといえども行(い)きます」 to a colleague has said something
grammatical and something no colleague would say. Use
<code>たとえ忙(いそが)しくても</code>.

That does not make it obscure. Japanese readers meet it regularly, and its force is
precise enough to be worth knowing: it sets up an **expectation of exemption and
then denies it**. <code>専門家(せんもんか)といえども</code> works because we
assume specialists know; <code>王(おう)といえども</code> works because we assume
kings do as they like. Attach it to a category that carries no such presumption and
the sentence falls flat - which is why
<code>会社員(かいしゃいん)といえども</code> would puzzle a reader.

Two shapes are especially worth recognising. With a **number and counter**
- <code>一日(いちにち)といえども</code>,
<code>一円(いちえん)といえども</code>,
<code>一瞬(いっしゅん)といえども</code> - it means "not even one", and the main
clause is always negative. And in the frame
<code>たとえ…といえども</code>, たとえ signals the concession in advance, which is
how a formal speech usually builds it.

The main clause is normally a **judgement, rule or refusal** stated in the
<code>である</code> style. Following <code>といえども</code> with
<code>…ですよね</code> is a register collision.

## Contrast

### ～といえども vs たとえ～でも／～であっても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning; <code>であっても</code> is the version you should
actually produce. <code>といえども</code> is the version you should recognise on
the page.

</div>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>であっても</b>、間違(まちが)えることはあります。
Even specialists make mistakes.
</pre>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>といえども</b>、判断(はんだん)を誤(あやま)ることがある。
Even the specialist can misjudge.
</pre>

The first sentence can be said aloud to anyone, in ます form, in any setting. The
second is a line from an essay: notice how the vocabulary around it climbs to meet
it - <code>判断(はんだん)を誤(あやま)る</code> rather than
<code>間違(まちが)える</code>, and a bare plain-form ending rather than
<code>あります</code>. That climb is the real cost of the pattern. If the rest of your sentence is ordinary, the
<code>といえども</code> will stick out of it.

### ～といえども vs ～とはいえ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>といえども</code> concedes a **category**: even a
person/thing of this kind. <code>とはいえ</code> concedes a **fact already
stated**: that may be so, but.

</div>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が上(あ)がった。<b>とはいえ</b>、生活(せいかつ)が楽(らく)になったわけではない。
My salary went up. That said, life has not become any easier.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>といえども</b>、給料(きゅうりょう)をもらう以上(いじょう)は責任(せきにん)がある。
Newcomer or not, once you draw a salary you carry responsibility.
</pre>

<code>とはいえ</code> looks backwards at something on the table already, and often
begins a new sentence. <code>といえども</code> looks at a noun and denies it its
privilege. <code>とはいえ</code> is also markedly more usable: it is formal, but it
is alive in speech and in email in a way that <code>といえども</code> is not.

### ～といえども vs ～といっても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>といっても</code> **corrects an impression** the word
itself might give: "when I say X, it isn't much of one".
<code>といえども</code> **grants the word its full force** and overrides it anyway.

</div>

<pre>
庭(にわ)がある<b>といっても</b>、猫(ねこ)の額(ひたい)ほどの広(ひろ)さだ。
We have a garden, though it's barely the size of a postage stamp.
</pre>

<pre>
狭(せま)い庭(にわ)<b>といえども</b>、手入(てい)れを怠(おこた)れば草(くさ)が生(は)える。
Small though the garden is, neglect it and the weeds come.
</pre>

These two are genuinely easy to confuse because both English translations start
"though". The test: in the <code>といっても</code> sentence the garden turns out to
be *less* than the word suggests. In the <code>といえども</code> sentence the
garden really is small - that is granted - and the main clause holds regardless.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 専門家(せんもんか)<b>だといえども</b> → ✅ 専門家(せんもんか)<b>といえども</b>
  A noun attaches directly. な-adjectives need
  <code>である</code>, not <code>だ</code>:
  <code>重要(じゅうよう)であるといえども</code>.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は寒(さむ)い<b>といえども</b>、コートはいらないよ。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は寒(さむ)い<b>けど</b>、コートはいらないよ。
  A casual main clause with よ on the end cannot carry this pattern. The mismatch
  is the most common learner error with it, and it is a register error rather than
  a grammar one.

- ❌ 駅(えき)に近(ちか)い<b>といえども</b>、歩(ある)いて20分(ぷん)かかる。 → ✅ 駅(えき)に近(ちか)い<b>といっても</b>、歩(ある)いて20分(ぷん)かかる。
  The sentence is walking back the word "near", which is
  <code>といっても</code>. <code>といえども</code> would insist the place really is
  near and then say something that holds anyway.

- ❌ 会社員(かいしゃいん)<b>といえども</b>、毎日(まいにち)働(はたら)いている。 → ✅ 社長(しゃちょう)<b>といえども</b>、休(やす)む暇(ひま)はない。
  The noun must carry a presumption worth overturning. Office workers are not
  expected to be exempt from working, so there is nothing for the concession to
  push against.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence - a text message, a newspaper
editorial, or a shop assistant's speech?
一円(いちえん)<b>といえども</b>、税金(ぜいきん)の使(つか)い道(みち)は明(あき)らかにされねばならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A newspaper editorial, or a speech in the same register.

Three signals point that way: <code>といえども</code> itself, the number-plus-counter
frame <code>一円(いちえん)といえども</code> meaning "not even one yen", and the
classical negative obligation <code>されねばならない</code>. None of the three
belongs to a text message.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
新人(しんじん)<b>といえども</b>、同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)を繰(く)り返(かえ)すことは許(ゆる)されない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新人(しんじん)<b>でも</b>、同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)を何度(なんど)もするのはよくない。（or たとえ新人(しんじん)であっても）

The concession is carried perfectly well by `でも` or
<code>であっても</code>. Notice that once the pattern goes, the rest of the
sentence naturally comes down a register too - <code>許(ゆる)されない</code>
softens to <code>よくない</code>. Keeping one and not the other is what produces
the mismatched sentences learners write.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 社長(しゃちょう)（といえども・といっても）、社員(しゃいん)三人(さんにん)の小(ちい)さな会社(かいしゃ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

社長(しゃちょう)<b>といっても</b>、社員(しゃいん)三人(さんにん)の小(ちい)さな会社(かいしゃ)だ。

The sentence is deflating the word <code>社長</code> - the title sounds grander than the
reality. That is <code>といっても</code>. <code>といえども</code> would take the
presidency at full value and then override it, which needs a main clause like
<code>自分(じぶん)で掃除(そうじ)をする</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 明日(あした)試験(しけん)といえども、今夜(こんや)は早(はや)く寝(ね)ようと思(おも)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)は試験(しけん)だ<b>けれど</b>、今夜(こんや)は早(はや)く寝(ね)ようと思(おも)う。

Two problems, and both are about fit rather than grammar. The main clause is a
private, casual intention - <code>寝(ね)ようと思(おも)う</code> - which no
formal concessive can carry. And "there is an exam tomorrow" is a plain fact, not a
category expected to be exempt from anything. A simple
<code>けれど</code> is the honest choice.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 大企業(だいきぎょう)<b>といえども</b>、安泰(あんたい)ではない。
(b) 大企業(だいきぎょう)だ。<b>とはいえ</b>、安泰(あんたい)ではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats "large corporation" as a category that carries a presumption of safety,
and denies the presumption. It says something general: size is no guarantee, for
any such company. It reads as a line of argument.

(b) has already put one particular company on the table as a fact, and now
qualifies that fact: yes, it is large - and even so, it is not secure. The scope is
narrower and the movement is different, a step back from something just said rather
than a rule about a class. <code>とはいえ</code> is also the one of the two you
could use in a meeting without sounding as though you were reading aloud.

</details>

# ～とて

**Meaning** · even X; even if - a literary "too / even", used either of a noun ("even I") or after a past verb ("even if one had")

<code>とて</code> is the quotative `と` plus `て`, worn down over centuries into a
single particle. Two jobs survive. After a **noun** it means "X too, X as well,
even X", very often used by a speaker about themselves:
<code>私(わたし)とて</code>. After a **past verb** it means "even if", and the
sentence goes on to say that it would make no difference.

**This is a form you read, not a form you say.** In modern speech the noun use is
`も` or `だって`, and the verb use is <code>ても</code> or
<code>たところで</code>.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | ～とて | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)とて |
| Noun (negative main clause) | ～とて…ない | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)とて例外(れいがい)ではない |
| Verb | た-form + とて | 謝(あやま)る → 謝(あやま)ったとて |
| Verb | た-form + とて…ない | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)いだとて間(ま)に合(あ)わない |
| Reason | ～からとて | 子供(こども)だからとて |
| Fixed conjunction | さりとて | - → さりとて名案(めいあん)があるわけでもない |
| Modern equivalents | ～も／だって、～ても／～たところで | 私(わたし)も、謝(あやま)っても |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>さりとて</code> ("but even so") is the one member of the family
still in ordinary written use. It is a whole conjunction, not a particle, and it
opens a sentence: <code>さりとて、放(ほう)っておくわけにもいかない</code>. If
you learn one thing from this page, learn that one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>とて</b>、同(おな)じ気持(きも)ちだ。
I feel the same way myself.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>とて</b>、例外(れいがい)ではない。
Children are no exception either.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)さら謝(あやま)った<b>とて</b>、失(うしな)った信用(しんよう)は戻(もど)らない。
Apologising now would not bring back the trust that was lost.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)<b>とて</b>、好(この)んでこの道(みち)を選(えら)んだわけではあるまい。
He too can hardly have chosen this road because he wanted it.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)いだ<b>とて</b>、間(ま)に合(あ)うものではない。
Hurrying would get us nowhere in time.
</pre>

<pre>
勝(か)った<b>とて</b>、失(うしな)ったものが返(かえ)ってくるわけではなかった。
Even in victory, what had been lost did not come back.
</pre>

<pre>
老(お)いた母(はは)<b>とて</b>、息子(むすこ)の嘘(うそ)に気(き)づかぬはずはなかった。
His aged mother, too, could hardly have failed to notice her son's lie.
</pre>

<pre>
都会(とかい)の暮(く)らしには疲(つか)れた。<b>さりとて</b>、故郷(こきょう)に戻(もど)る勇気(ゆうき)もない。
City life has worn him out. And yet he has not the courage to go home either.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Literary, and in speech genuinely old-fashioned.** Where you will meet it: novels
and short stories, especially in narration and in the inner voice of a character;
period drama and historical fiction; essays and columns written in a deliberately
weighty style; the occasional line of a speech reaching for gravity. Where you will
not meet it: conversation, email, business Japanese, anything spoken by a person
under any circumstances you are likely to be in. Saying
「私(わたし)とて忙(いそが)しい」 to a colleague would land somewhere between
quaint and theatrical.

What it adds, when a writer uses it, is a note of **shared condition or quiet
resignation**. <code>私(わたし)とて</code> is not simply
<code>私(わたし)も</code>; it carries "I am no different from you in this, whatever
you may assume", and it very often introduces a hardship the speaker is claiming a
share of. Applied to another person -
<code>彼(かれ)とて</code> - it extends the same sympathy outwards.

The <code>たとて</code> use is bleaker. It nearly always ends in a negative, or in
<code>わけではない</code>, or in a rhetorical question, because its business is
futility: the action would change nothing. That is exactly the meaning modern
Japanese expresses with <code>たところで</code>, and
<code>たところで</code> is what you should write.

Do not confuse this <code>とて</code> with the colloquial contraction of
<code>という</code> and <code>といって</code>. In speech
<code>行(い)くって</code> is `って`, an entirely modern shortening, and it has
nothing to do with the literary particle here beyond a distant shared ancestor.

## Contrast

### ～とて vs ～も／～だって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical function after a noun.
`も` is neutral, `だって` is casual and slightly complaining, and
<code>とて</code> is literary. Say the first two; read the third.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>だって</b>、好(す)きでやっているわけじゃない。
It's not as if I'm doing this because I enjoy it either, you know.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>とて</b>、望(のぞ)んでこうしているのではない。
Nor am I doing this because I wish to.
</pre>

The two sentences make the same move - claiming a share of a difficulty someone
else assumed was theirs alone - and they belong to different centuries. `だって`
puts it in the voice of a real person mid-argument.
<code>とて</code> puts it in the voice of a narrator, or of a character in a novel
speaking with dignity.

### ～たとて vs ～たところで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning and the same futility.
<code>たところで</code> is current Japanese; <code>たとて</code> is its literary
ancestor.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)から走(はし)っ<b>たところで</b>、開演(かいえん)には間(ま)に合(あ)わない。
Even if we run now, we won't make the curtain.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)から走(はし)っ<b>たとて</b>、開演(かいえん)には間(ま)に合(あ)うまい。
Even were we to run now, we should hardly make the curtain.
</pre>

Note how <code>たとて</code> pulls the ending with it: `まい` rather than
<code>ない</code>. The two archaic forms belong together, and a sentence that
starts literary and finishes casual sounds broken. If you find yourself writing
<code>たとて</code>, check the end of your sentence as well.

### ～とて vs ～といえども

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>といえども</code> takes a category that is **expected to
be exempt** and refuses the exemption. <code>とて</code> simply says the subject is
**included**, and is often sympathetic rather than severe.

</div>

<pre>
大臣(だいじん)<b>といえども</b>、法(ほう)を超(こ)えることはできない。
Not even a minister stands above the law.
</pre>

<pre>
大臣(だいじん)<b>とて</b>、一人(ひとり)の人間(にんげん)である。
A minister, too, is only a human being.
</pre>

The first denies a privilege; the second grants a shared frailty. Both are
literary, but their emotional direction is opposite, and swapping them changes an
accusation into an excuse.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 謝(あやま)る<b>とて</b>遅(おそ)い → ✅ 謝(あやま)った<b>とて</b>遅(おそ)い
  In the "even if" use the verb goes into the た-form. The `た` here is not past
  tense; it is part of the frame, exactly as in
  <code>謝(あやま)ったところで</code>.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>だとて</b> → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>とて</b>
  A noun attaches bare. There is no `だ` in front of it.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>とて</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>なので</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。
  <code>とて</code> is concessive, never causal. Announcing a cancellation with it
  says the opposite of what you mean, and in a ですます sentence it is also badly
  out of register.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)<b>とて</b>喜(よろこ)んでいるよ。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>も</b>喜(よろこ)んでいるよ。
  Two problems at once: the casual ending clashes with the literary particle, and
  <code>とて</code> leans towards hardship rather than good news.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this line - a group chat, a novel, or a
train announcement?
老(お)いた父(ちち)<b>とて</b>、それを知(し)らぬわけではなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A novel - specifically, narration.

<code>とて</code> plus the classical negative <code>知(し)らぬ</code> plus the
past <code>なかった</code> is the voice of a narrator telling us what a character
privately understood. A train announcement would use plain polite Japanese, and in
a group chat the sentence would read as a joke.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
今(いま)から文句(もんく)を言(い)っ<b>たとて</b>、決(き)まったことは変(か)わらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今(いま)から文句(もんく)を言(い)っ<b>たところで</b>、決(き)まったことは変(か)わらない。（or 言(い)っても）

<code>たところで</code> carries the same futility and is fully current. A plain
<code>言(い)っても</code> also works and is the most neutral option; it just
loses the "and it would achieve nothing" colouring that both of the others have.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 社長(しゃちょう)（とて・といえども）、部下(ぶか)に頭(あたま)を下(さ)げねばならぬ時(とき)がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Either is possible, and they say different things. <b>といえども</b> is the sharper
reading: not even the president is above bowing to a subordinate - the privilege of
rank is denied. <b>とて</b> is the gentler one: the president is a person like
anyone else, and has his moments of bowing too. Given <code>ねばならぬ</code>, which
implies an obligation imposed from outside, <b>といえども</b> is the better fit.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しかったとて、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れてすみません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しかった<b>もので</b>、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れてすみません。

The sentence is giving a reason, and <code>とて</code> cannot give reasons - it
concedes. The apology also places this firmly in everyday polite Japanese, where a
literary particle has no business. <code>もので</code> or
<code>ので</code> is what the sentence wants.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)<b>も</b>不安(ふあん)です。
(b) 私(わたし)<b>とて</b>不安(ふあん)なのだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain, polite statement of fellow feeling: I am worried as well. It could
be said by anyone to anyone.

(b) is the same content delivered in a literary voice, and it carries a note of
protest with it - the speaker is answering an assumption that they are calm, or
unaffected, or somehow above it. The plain <code>のだ</code> ending reinforces
that. In a novel it would read as a moment of a character letting their guard down;
said aloud in an office, it would sound as if the speaker were quoting a book.

</details>

# ～たところで

**Meaning** · even if you do it, it will not help - a concession that comes with the verdict already attached

<code>たところで</code> is not neutral about the outcome. It grants the action and
dismisses it in the same breath: go ahead, do it, and nothing will change. The main
clause is therefore always negative in force - a denial, a limit, or a shrug.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | た-form + ところで | 謝(あやま)る → 謝(あやま)ったところで |
| Verb (る-verb) | た-form + ところで | 調(しら)べる → 調(しら)べたところで |
| Verb (する) | した + ところで | 説明(せつめい)する → 説明(せつめい)したところで |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き)た + ところで | 来(く)る → 来(き)たところで |
| Negative | ～なかった + ところで | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かなかったところで |
| Reinforced | いくら／どんなに／今(いま)さら／たとえ + … + たところで | 今(いま)さら走(はし)ったところで |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `た` is **not past tense**. The whole expression looks
forward: <code>謝(あやま)ったところで</code> is "even if I apologise (now or
later)", never "even though I apologised". The tense of the sentence sits in the
main clause.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the main clause has to be **negative or limiting**. Endings that fit:
<code>ない</code>, <code>わけではない</code>, <code>だけだ</code>,
<code>にすぎない</code>, <code>あるまい</code>, <code>せいぜい…だ</code>. Endings
that do not fit: requests, invitations, commands, and any cheerful prediction.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
謝(あやま)っ<b>たところで</b>、許(ゆる)してはもらえないだろう。
Even if I apologise, I doubt I'll be forgiven.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)さら後悔(こうかい)し<b>たところで</b>、どうにもならない。
Regretting it now will get you nowhere.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら説明(せつめい)し<b>たところで</b>、彼(かれ)は聞(き)く耳(みみ)を持(も)たないよ。
However much you explain, he isn't going to listen.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)めに着(つ)い<b>たところで</b>、受付(うけつけ)が始(はじ)まるまでは中(なか)に入(はい)れない。
Even if you get there early, you can't go in until reception opens.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)い<b>だところで</b>、節約(せつやく)できるのはせいぜい5分(ふん)だ。
Even at a rush, you'd save five minutes at the outside.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)を下(さ)げ<b>たところで</b>、品質(ひんしつ)が悪(わる)ければ売(う)れはしない。
Cutting the price will not sell it if the quality is poor.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)が反対(はんたい)し<b>たところで</b>、この方針(ほうしん)が変(か)わることはあるまい。
Even if every one of us objects, this policy is not going to change.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに準備(じゅんび)し<b>たところで</b>、本番(ほんばん)で緊張(きんちょう)してしまえば同(おな)じことだ。
However much you prepare, it comes to the same thing if you freeze on the day.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The tone is **dry and slightly resigned**, sometimes dismissive. The speaker is not
weighing up a possibility; they have already decided it is a dead end, and the
sentence exists to say so. That makes it a natural fit for arguments, columns,
considered advice and adult conversation, and a poor fit for encouraging anybody.

It is neither literary nor archaic. You will hear it in speech - 「今(いま)さら言(い)ったところで遅(おそ)いよ」
is entirely ordinary - though it is a shade more considered than
<code>言(い)っても</code>, and it is more frequent in writing than in chat.
Politeness lives in the main clause: <code>…変(か)わりません</code> makes the
whole sentence polite without touching the pattern.

Watch the company it keeps. 今(いま)さら, いくら, どんなに, たとえ and
どれだけ appear in front of it constantly, and せいぜい, たかが and
<code>…だけだ</code> after it. Together they build the shape the pattern wants: an
effort at one end, a small or empty result at the other.

One caution about the string itself. <code>～たところで</code> is also the ordinary
temporal use of the noun <code>ところ</code> - "at the point where …" - and the two
are spelled identically. Context separates them without difficulty once you know
both exist; see the third contrast below.

## Contrast

### ～たところで vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` is neutral and takes any main clause.
<code>たところで</code> has already judged the action **futile**, and its main
clause must agree.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、傘(かさ)があるから大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だ。
Even if it rains, I have an umbrella, so it's fine.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)っていっ<b>たところで</b>、この風(かぜ)では役(やく)に立(た)たない。
Taking an umbrella is no use in wind like this.
</pre>

Swap the patterns and the first sentence breaks:
❌<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったところで大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だ</code> promises
that things will be fine, which contradicts the pessimism built into the form. As a
test: if you could follow the clause with <code>無駄(むだ)だ</code>,
<code>たところで</code> is available. If you could follow it with
<code>いいですよ</code>, only `ても` will do.

### ～たところで vs ～たって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たって` is simply the **casual spoken** form of `ても`.
<code>たところで</code> is not casual, and it is not just "even if" - it adds the
futility.

</div>

<pre>
走(はし)っ<b>たって</b>、もう間(ま)に合(あ)わないよ。
No point running - we won't make it now.
</pre>

<pre>
走(はし)っ<b>たところで</b>、もう間(ま)に合(あ)わないだろう。
Running now would not get us there in time.
</pre>

These two sentences mean much the same, which is why the pair is worth having.
`たって` belongs to friends, contractions and sentence-final よ.
<code>たところで</code> is what the same thought looks like in a written report or
in a colder voice. Note also that `たって` is happy with a positive main clause -
<code>見(み)たっていいよ</code>, "go ahead and look" - and
<code>たところで</code> never is.

### ～たところで (concessive) vs ～たところ(で) (temporal)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the main clause is a **past event**, the `ところ` is the
ordinary noun meaning "the point at which". If the main clause is a **non-past
denial**, it is the concessive pattern.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)に着(つ)い<b>たところで</b>、忘(わす)れ物(もの)に気(き)づいた。
Just as I got to the station, I realised I'd left something behind.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで走(はし)っ<b>たところで</b>、終電(しゅうでん)には間(ま)に合(あ)わない。
Even if I run to the station, I won't catch the last train.
</pre>

The first sentence is a moment in a story and the `で` is doing its normal work on
a location in time. The second is a concession about something that has not
happened. In practice the disambiguating clues are strong: the temporal use has a
past-tense main clause and no scale adverb, while the concessive use is almost
always accompanied by いくら, 今(いま)さら or どんなに and ends in a negative.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>ところで</b>、会(あ)えない。 → ✅ 行(い)っ<b>たところで</b>、会(あ)えない。
  The pattern is built on the た-form. The dictionary form gives you the ordinary
  noun <code>ところ</code> and a different sentence.

- ❌ 練習(れんしゅう)し<b>たところで</b>、うまくなるはずだ。 → ✅ 練習(れんしゅう)し<b>たところで</b>、すぐにうまくなるわけではない。
  The main clause has to be a denial or a limit. A hopeful prediction contradicts
  the pattern outright.

- ❌ 早(はや)く来(き)<b>たところで</b>、手伝(てつだ)ってください。 → ✅ 早(はや)く来(き)<b>ても</b>、手伝(てつだ)わなくていいですよ。
  No requests, invitations or commands after
  <code>たところで</code>. It has already declared the action pointless, so asking
  for it makes no sense.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)謝(あやま)っ<b>たところで</b>、許(ゆる)してもらえなかった。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)謝(あやま)っ<b>たが</b>、許(ゆる)してもらえなかった。
  The `た` in the pattern is not a past tense, so it cannot report what actually
  happened yesterday. For a real past event use `が` or `のに`.

## Practice

**1.** 今(いま)さら文句(もんく)を言(い)っ____、決(き)まったことは変(か)わらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

言(い)っ<b>たところで</b>

今(いま)さら in front and a flat denial behind it is the pattern's home ground.
<code>言(い)っても</code> would also be grammatical, but it loses the
"and it would achieve nothing" that 今(いま)さら has already set up.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 窓(まど)を開(あ)け（たところで・ても）いいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>ても</b>いいですか。

This is a request for permission, and <code>たところで</code> cannot carry one -
it says an action would be useless, which is not a promising way to ask for
anything. Permission is `ても` territory, always.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if we hire two more people, the deadline won't move."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あと二人(ふたり)採用(さいよう)し<b>たところで</b>、締(し)め切(き)りが動(うご)くことはない。

The English "even if … won't" is exactly the futility this pattern encodes, so
<code>たところで</code> is a better fit than a plain
<code>採用(さいよう)しても</code>. Note 二人(ふたり), the irregular reading of
<code>二人</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: どんなに急(いそ)ぐところで、開演(かいえん)には間(ま)に合(あ)わないだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ どんなに<b>急(いそ)いだところで</b>、開演(かいえん)には間(ま)に合(あ)わないだろう。

The verb must be in the た-form. <code>急(いそ)ぐ</code> is an う-verb ending in
ぐ, so the た-form is <code>急(いそ)いだ</code> with the voiced だ - the same
sound change as in <code>泳(およ)いだ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 相談(そうだん)し<b>ても</b>、答(こた)えは同(おな)じだと思(おも)う。
(b) 相談(そうだん)し<b>たところで</b>、答(こた)えは同(おな)じだと思(おも)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a mild prediction. The speaker is guessing that consulting someone would not
change the answer, and the sentence leaves room for the listener to disagree and go
ahead anyway.

(b) closes the door. <code>たところで</code> presents the consultation as already
written off, and the sentence reads as advice not to bother. It is also a touch
drier and more formal, the sort of thing said by someone who has watched this
happen before. To a person who is anxious and asking for help, (a) is much the
kinder choice.

</details>

# ～としたって / ～としたところで

**Meaning** · even supposing; even in the case of - grants a hypothesis, or grants someone's standpoint, and then shows it changes nothing

Take <code>とする</code>, "suppose that", and put it into the
<code>たところで</code> frame you already know. The result concedes a supposition
and immediately limits it: suppose it is so - even then, this much is still true.
<code>としたって</code> is the same thing with the casual `たって` in place of
<code>たところで</code>.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (standpoint) | ～としたって／としたところで | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)としたって |
| Noun (hypothesis) | ～だとしたって／だとしたところで | 本当(ほんとう) → 本当(ほんとう)だとしたところで |
| Verb | plain form + としたって | 行(い)く → 行(い)くとしたって |
| Verb (past hypothesis) | ～たとしたところで | 失敗(しっぱい)した → 失敗(しっぱい)したとしたところで |
| い-adjective | plain form + としたって | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いとしたって |
| な-adjective | ～だとしたところで | 無理(むり)だ → 無理(むり)だとしたところで |
| Formal equivalent | ～としても | 行(い)くとしても |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the presence of `だ` decides the reading. A bare
<code>私(わたし)としたって</code> means "even from where I stand";
<code>私(わたし)だとしたって</code> means "even supposing it were me". Both are
correct Japanese and they say different things.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としたって</b>、急(きゅう)に言(い)われても困(こま)る。
Speaking for myself, being told at the last minute puts me in a difficult position too.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)行(い)く<b>としたって</b>、着(つ)くのは夜(よる)になるよ。
Even if we do go tomorrow, we won't arrive until the evening.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)い<b>としたって</b>、これ以上(いじょう)は買(か)えないな。
Cheap or not, I can't buy any more than this.
</pre>

<pre>
噂(うわさ)が本当(ほんとう)だ<b>としたところで</b>、今(いま)の計画(けいかく)を変(か)えるわけにはいかない。
Even supposing the rumour is true, we cannot go changing the plan now.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)<b>としたところで</b>、子(こ)の進路(しんろ)を勝手(かって)に決(き)める権利(けんり)はない。
Even a parent has no right to decide a child's future for them.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)が集(あつ)まった<b>としたところで</b>、この部屋(へや)には入(はい)りきらない。
Even if everyone did turn up, they would not all fit in this room.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げが避(さ)けられない<b>としたって</b>、事前(じぜん)の説明(せつめい)くらいはあってしかるべきだ。
Even granting that the price rise is unavoidable, we might at least have been told in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の話(はなし)が事実(じじつ)だ<b>としたところで</b>、会社(かいしゃ)の責任(せきにん)が消(き)えるわけではない。
Even if what he says is true, it does not make the company's responsibility go away.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Both forms are **conversational**. <code>としたって</code> is the more casual of
the two - `たって` is a spoken contraction - and it has a grumbling edge to it: it
turns up in complaints, in pushing back, in the sentence where someone defends
their own corner. <code>としたところで</code> is a little more measured and a
little longer, but it is still speech and informal writing rather than a report.
The neutral, formal member of the family is <code>としても</code>, and that is what
belongs in a document.

Both inherit the **futility** of <code>たところで</code>. The main clause is
normally a denial, a limit or a complaint - <code>夜(よる)になる</code>,
<code>入(はい)りきらない</code>, <code>わけではない</code>. A cheerful main
clause sits badly with them.

The two uses are worth keeping apart in your head. In the **standpoint** use, the
noun in front is a person or a group, and the sentence claims that they are no
better placed than anyone else: 「会社(かいしゃ)としたって、損(そん)をしたいわけではない」
- the company is not enjoying this either. In the **hypothesis** use, what precedes
is a proposition, and the sentence grants it for the sake of argument. Fluent
speakers move between the two without noticing; learners should notice, because
only one of them takes `だ`.

## Contrast

### ～としたって vs ～としても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register.
<code>としても</code> is neutral and writable; <code>としたって</code> is casual
and carries a complaint.

</div>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>としても</b>、午後(ごご)からになります。
Even if I attend, it will not be until the afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>としたって</b>、午後(ごご)からだよ。
Even if I go, it won't be till the afternoon.
</pre>

The propositional content is identical. What changes is who is speaking and to
whom: the first would be fine in an email to a client, the second only to a
colleague you know well. Writing <code>としたって</code> into a business message is
the commonest misuse of the pattern, and it reads as offhand.

### ～としたところで vs ～たところで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `と`, you are conceding a **supposition or a standpoint**.
Without it, you are conceding the **action itself**.

</div>

<pre>
調(しら)べ<b>たところで</b>、もう記録(きろく)は残(のこ)っていない。
Even if you look into it, the records are no longer there.
</pre>

<pre>
調(しら)べる<b>としたところで</b>、どこから手(て)をつければいいのか分(わ)からない。
Even supposing we investigate, I have no idea where we would start.
</pre>

In the first, the investigating is treated as done and dismissed. In the second, it
is still a proposal on the table which the speaker is entertaining and then finding
impractical. Note the verb forms: <code>たところで</code> demands the た-form,
while <code>としたところで</code> takes the plain form because
<code>とする</code> supplies the ending.

### ～としたって vs ～にしたって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` supposes; `に` points. <code>としたって</code> grants a
**case for argument**; <code>にしたって</code> picks out an **actual example**,
often one you would have expected to be exempt.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)<b>にしたって</b>、知(し)らないことはある。
Even teachers have things they don't know.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)だ<b>としたって</b>、全部(ぜんぶ)答(こた)えられるとは限(かぎ)らない。
Even supposing they are a teacher, that doesn't mean they can answer everything.
</pre>

The first sentence is about teachers who exist. The second is about a hypothetical
person whose being a teacher we are granting for the moment. The overlap is real -
in the standpoint use, <code>私(わたし)としたって</code> and
<code>私(わたし)にしたって</code> are close to interchangeable - but as soon as
a `だ` appears, only `と` can follow.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>だとしたって</b>困(こま)ります。（meaning "I'm in trouble too"） → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>としたって</b>困(こま)ります。
  With `だ` the sentence becomes "even supposing it were me", a hypothesis about
  identity. For the standpoint reading, drop the `だ`.

- ❌ 行(い)った<b>としたって</b>、楽(たの)しいだろう。 → ✅ 行(い)った<b>としたって</b>、楽(たの)しくはないだろう。
  Like <code>たところで</code>, these forms lead to a denial or a limit. A positive
  expectation contradicts the shape.

- ❌ ご出席(しゅっせき)いただける<b>としたって</b>、席(せき)をご用意(ようい)いたします。 → ✅ ご出席(しゅっせき)いただける<b>としましても</b>、席(せき)のご用意(ようい)が必要(ひつよう)です。
  A casual contraction inside honorific Japanese is a jarring mix. In polite
  writing use <code>としても</code> or <code>としましても</code>.

- ❌ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)<b>としたところで</b>、中止(ちゅうし)にはしない。 → ✅ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)<b>だとしたところで</b>、中止(ちゅうし)にはしない。
  A noun being supposed as a proposition needs `だ` before
  <code>としたところで</code>. Without it, the sentence tries to read
  雨(あめ) as a standpoint, and rain does not have one.

## Practice

**1.** 今(いま)から全力(ぜんりょく)で走(はし)る____、開始(かいし)には間(ま)に合(あ)わない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

走(はし)る<b>としたって</b>（or 走(はし)るとしたところで）

A supposition is being entertained and then limited, and the main clause is a flat
negative - the shape these forms want. <code>とする</code> takes the plain form
<code>走(はし)る</code>, not the た-form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 報告書(ほうこくしょ)に書(か)くなら、「遅(おく)れる（としたって・としても）、一日(いちにち)程度(ていど)です」。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

遅(おく)れる<b>としても</b>、一日(いちにち)程度(ていど)です。

The sentence is going into a written report, and
<code>としたって</code> is a spoken contraction. Content is identical; only the
register disqualifies it. Note the reading 一日(いちにち) - "one day" as a
duration, not ついたち.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even supposing the data is wrong, the conclusion doesn't change."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

データが間違(まちが)っている<b>としたところで</b>、結論(けつろん)は変(か)わらない。

A proposition granted for argument, followed by a denial. In a written paper you
would raise the register to
<code>間違(まちが)っているとしても</code>; <code>としたところで</code> suits
someone talking the problem through out loud.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)としたって、喜(よろこ)んで引(ひ)き受(う)けてくれるはずだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)<b>なら</b>、喜(よろこ)んで引(ひ)き受(う)けてくれるはずだ。

The main clause is a positive expectation, and these forms exist to say that
something will *not* help. If you want to keep the concession, the main clause has
to turn: <code>彼(かれ)としたって、断(ことわ)りにくいだろう</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 上司(じょうし)<b>としても</b>、判断(はんだん)に迷(まよ)っているのだろう。
(b) 上司(じょうし)<b>としたって</b>、判断(はんだん)に迷(まよ)ってるんだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is neutral and could be written down. It observes, fairly dispassionately, that
the boss is probably struggling with the decision as well.

(b) says the same thing in spoken shorthand, and adds an attitude. The casual
contraction, along with <code>迷(まよ)ってる</code> and
<code>んだろう</code>, puts the sentence in the middle of a grumble - either
defending the boss against a complaint, or conceding a point grudgingly before
making one's own. It is fine among colleagues and out of place in a meeting.

</details>

# ～にしたって

**Meaning** · even X; even in X's case - takes one item or one person's side of things and says the point holds there too

<code>にしたって</code> is the spoken, slightly grumbling relative of
<code>にしても</code>. It picks something out - often the very thing you would
expect to be an exception, or the speaker's own position - and folds it back into
the general case: even them, even that, even from where I sit.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | ～にしたって | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)にしたって |
| Question word | ～にしたって | 何(なに) → 何(なに)にしたって |
| Verb | plain form + にしたって | 行(い)く → 行(い)くにしたって |
| い-adjective | plain form + にしたって | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいにしたって |
| な-adjective | stem + にしたって | 下手(へた)だ → 下手(へた)にしたって |
| Doubled | A にしたって B にしたって | 味(あじ)にしたって値段(ねだん)にしたって |
| Neutral equivalent | ～にしても | 子供(こども)にしても |
| Formal equivalent | ～にしろ／にせよ | 子供(こども)にせよ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>たって</code> here is the casual contraction of
<code>ても</code>, the same one in <code>見(み)たっていい</code>. That is the
whole of the difference between <code>にしたって</code> and
<code>にしても</code>: one sound change, and the register that comes with it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>にしたって</b>、それくらいのことは分(わ)かるよ。
Even a child understands that much.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)<b>にしたって</b>、去年(きょねん)よりずいぶん高(たか)くなっている。
The price, too, has gone up a lot since last year.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)<b>にしたって</b>、まずは本人(ほんにん)に聞(き)いてみないと始(はじ)まらない。
Whatever it is, nothing starts until you ask the person themselves.
</pre>

<pre>
行(い)く<b>にしたって</b>、一人(ひとり)では危(あぶ)ないだろう。
Even if you do go, going alone would be dangerous.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)<b>にしたって</b>、わざとやったわけではないと思(おも)う。
I don't think he did it on purpose either.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は、味(あじ)<b>にしたって</b>値段(ねだん)<b>にしたって</b>、文句(もんく)のつけようがない。
That place - taste, price, you name it - there's nothing to complain about.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しい<b>にしたって</b>、一言(ひとこと)連絡(れんらく)するくらいはできたはずだ。
Busy or not, he could at least have sent a word.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)を導入(どうにゅう)する<b>にしたって</b>、現場(げんば)の意見(いけん)を聞(き)いてからにしてほしい。
Even if the new system is going in, I'd like them to hear from the people doing the work first.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Casual spoken Japanese**, and often a little blunt. It belongs to conversation
among friends, family and familiar colleagues, and to writing that imitates speech
- dialogue, blog posts, comment threads. In an email to a client or in a report it
is out of place; there you want <code>にしても</code>, or
<code>にしろ／にせよ</code> if the register is formal.

The attitude it carries is worth learning with it. A large share of real
occurrences are **complaints or defences**:
「忙(いそが)しいにしたって、ひどいよ」 pushes back on someone's excuse, and
「彼(かれ)にしたって好(す)きでやっているわけじゃない」 defends someone from
one. The pattern lets you concede a point and then immediately do something with
the concession, which is exactly what an argument needs.

Two neighbours are easy to mix up with it. <code>としたって</code> supposes a case
- "even supposing" - whereas <code>にしたって</code> points at a real one. And
<code>にしては</code>, which looks almost identical, means something else entirely;
see below, because that confusion is the expensive one.

When the noun in front is a person, the sentence usually means either "even they,
who you would think were exempt" or "from their side of it too". Which one you get
comes from context, and Japanese speakers do not find the ambiguity troubling.

## Contrast

### ～にしたって vs ～にしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different register.
<code>にしても</code> is neutral and can be written; <code>にしたって</code> is
spoken and carries an edge.

</div>

<pre>
急(いそ)ぐ<b>にしても</b>、安全(あんぜん)確認(かくにん)は省(はぶ)けません。
Even if we are in a hurry, the safety check cannot be skipped.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)ぐ<b>にしたって</b>、確認(かくにん)くらいはするでしょ。
Rushing or not, you'd at least check, wouldn't you.
</pre>

Notice that the choice pulls the rest of the sentence with it: the polite
<code>省(はぶ)けません</code> in the first, the casual
<code>でしょ</code> in the second. A sentence that mixes
<code>にしたって</code> with ます form sounds like two people talking at once.

### ～にしたって vs ～にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にしたって</code> means "**even** X".
<code>にしては</code> means "**for** an X" - measured against what an X would
normally be, this one is surprising.

</div>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて作(つく)った<b>にしては</b>、よくできている。
For a first attempt, it's very well made.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて作(つく)った<b>にしたって</b>、説明書(せつめいしょ)くらいは読(よ)むべきだった。
First attempt or not, he should at least have read the instructions.
</pre>

One `は` and one `って` apart, and the sentences do opposite things. The first
lowers the standard and then praises; the second grants an excuse and then refuses
to let it excuse anything. If your English translation is "for a …",
<code>にしては</code> is the form you want, and no amount of context will make
<code>にしたって</code> mean that.

### ～にしたって vs ～だって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are casual "even". `だって` attaches only to nouns and is
the simpler, more frequent word. <code>にしたって</code> also takes verbs and
adjectives, and sounds a shade more argued.

</div>

<pre>
大人(おとな)<b>だって</b>間違(まちが)えることはある。
Grown-ups make mistakes too.
</pre>

<pre>
大人(おとな)<b>にしたって</b>、全部(ぜんぶ)分(わ)かっているわけじゃない。
It's not as if grown-ups understand everything either.
</pre>

With a noun the two are close enough to swap. The difference appears as soon as you
need to concede a whole clause: <code>行(い)くにしたって</code> is fine, and
❌<code>行(い)くだって</code> is not Japanese. `だって` also has a second life as
a sentence-opening "but", which <code>にしたって</code> does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 新人(しんじん)<b>にしては</b>、ミスが多(おお)すぎる。（meaning "even for a newcomer"） → ✅ 新人(しんじん)<b>にしたって</b>、ミスが多(おお)すぎる。
  <code>にしては</code> would mean "for a newcomer, he makes a lot of mistakes" -
  which, oddly, is close to praise-shaped criticism. If the point is that being new
  is no excuse, you need <code>にしたって</code>.

- ❌ 便利(べんり)<b>だにしたって</b> → ✅ 便利(べんり)<b>にしたって</b>
  な-adjectives attach by their stem. The `だ` has nothing to attach to.

- ❌ ご多忙(たぼう)<b>にしたって</b>、ご返信(へんしん)をお待(ま)ちしております。 → ✅ ご多忙(たぼう)<b>とは存(ぞん)じますが</b>、ご返信(へんしん)をお待(ま)ちしております。
  A casual contraction inside honorific Japanese is a serious mismatch, and the
  complaint flavour makes it worse. Business writing needs a different construction
  altogether.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>にしたって</b>行(い)きたいです。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>も</b>行(い)きたいです。
  The pattern concedes ground in an argument; it is not a plain "me too". Used for
  simple agreement it sounds as though you are objecting to something.

## Practice

**1.** 疲(つか)れていた____、約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れるのはよくない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

疲(つか)れていた<b>にしたって</b>

An excuse is granted and then denied any force - the pattern's most typical job.
<code>疲(つか)れていたにしても</code> is the neutral equivalent; the
<code>たって</code> version fits a spoken complaint.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 中学生(ちゅうがくせい)（にしては・にしたって）、ずいぶんしっかりした文章(ぶんしょう)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中学生(ちゅうがくせい)<b>にしては</b>、ずいぶんしっかりした文章(ぶんしょう)だ。

The sentence is praising the writing by measuring it against what you would expect
of a middle-school student. That "for a …" reading is
<code>にしては</code>. <code>にしたって</code> would set up a complaint, and
nothing in the sentence complains.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if we cancel, we'll have to tell everyone today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中止(ちゅうし)する<b>にしたって</b>、今日(きょう)中(じゅう)にみんなに知(し)らせないといけない。

A clause rather than a noun, so the plain form attaches directly. In a written
notice you would raise this to <code>中止(ちゅうし)するにしても</code>. Note
今日(きょう)中(じゅう) - <code>中</code> is じゅう in this compound.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 静(しず)かだにしたって、図書館(としょかん)ほどではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 静(しず)か<b>にしたって</b>、図書館(としょかん)ほどではない。

な-adjectives attach by their stem, with no `だ`. The same rule holds for
<code>にしても</code> and <code>にせよ</code>:
<code>静(しず)かにせよ</code>, never ❌<code>静(しず)かだにせよ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)<b>にしても</b>、困(こま)っているのだと思(おも)います。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)<b>にしたって</b>、困(こま)ってるんだと思(おも)うよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both defend the department head by pointing out that he is in a difficult position
too, and both are perfectly natural.

(a) is neutral and polite enough to say in a meeting, or to write. (b) is the same
observation between colleagues over lunch: the contraction
<code>困(こま)ってる</code>, the <code>んだ</code> and the final よ all belong
with <code>にしたって</code>, and together they make it sound like one turn in an
ongoing grumble about the situation. Said upward to the department head himself,
(b) would be too familiar.

</details>

# ～ないまでも

**Meaning** · even if not all the way to X, at least Y - concedes the high bar, then names a lower one that still stands

The sentence sets up a scale. In front of <code>ないまでも</code> is the top of it,
declined; after it is a point further down that the speaker is not willing to give
up. "Not every day, but at least once a week." "Not first place, but a place on the
podium." Without that step down in the main clause, the pattern has nothing to do.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ない-form + までも | 完成(かんせい)する → 完成(かんせい)しないまでも |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)ない + までも | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないまでも |
| Verb (potential) | ～できないまでも | 優勝(ゆうしょう)できる → 優勝(ゆうしょう)できないまでも |
| い-adjective (uncommon) | ～くないまでも | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くないまでも |
| な-adjective | stem + ではないまでも | 完璧(かんぺき)だ → 完璧(かんぺき)ではないまでも |
| Noun | ～ではないまでも | 病気(びょうき) → 病気(びょうき)ではないまでも |
| Set frame | ～とは言(い)わないまでも | 毎日(まいにち) → 毎日(まいにち)とは言(い)わないまでも |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the main clause must sit **lower on the same scale**, and it is
usually flagged as such by せめて, 少(すく)なくとも, ～くらいは or ～だけでも.
If what follows is not a smaller version of what precedes, the sentence does not
work.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)とは言(い)わ<b>ないまでも</b>、半分(はんぶん)は今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に終(お)わらせたい。
Not all of it, perhaps, but I want at least half done today.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)とは言(い)わ<b>ないまでも</b>、週(しゅう)に一度(いちど)は連絡(れんらく)がほしい。
I'm not asking for every day, but I would like to hear from you once a week.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)はでき<b>ないまでも</b>、入賞(にゅうしょう)は狙(ねら)えるはずだ。
We may not win it, but we ought to be able to place.
</pre>

<pre>
完璧(かんぺき)で<b>ないまでも</b>、人(ひと)に見(み)せられる程度(ていど)には仕上(しあ)げたい。
Not perfect, maybe, but I want it finished to a standard I can show people.
</pre>

<pre>
謝罪(しゃざい)とは言(い)わ<b>ないまでも</b>、一言(ひとこと)説明(せつめい)くらいはあってよかったはずだ。
An apology may be too much to ask, but a word of explanation would not have gone amiss.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)が賛成(さんせい)し<b>ないまでも</b>、強(つよ)く反対(はんたい)する人(ひと)はいないだろう。
Not everyone will be in favour, but I doubt anyone will object strongly.
</pre>

<pre>
病気(びょうき)と言(い)える状態(じょうたい)では<b>ないまでも</b>、数日(すうじつ)休(やす)んだほうがいい。
It may not amount to being ill, but you should take a few days off.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きな利益(りえき)は生(う)ま<b>ないまでも</b>、この事業(じぎょう)は会社(かいしゃ)の信用(しんよう)を支(ささ)えてきた。
It may not generate large profits, but this line of business has underpinned the company's reputation.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal-leaning but entirely usable in speech. It is at home in considered
conversation, in meetings, in written requests and in evaluations - anywhere a
speaker wants to **lower a demand without abandoning it**. That makes it a
politeness device as much as a grammar point:
「毎日(まいにち)とは言(い)わないまでも」 concedes in advance that the full
request would be unreasonable, which makes the smaller one much harder to refuse.

The frame <code>～とは言(い)わないまでも</code> is worth memorising whole. It is
by far the most common shape the pattern takes, and it lets you name the maximal
version of a demand explicitly, disclaim it, and then ask for the realistic one.

The pattern is **not** a general "even if not". It carries a specific commitment to
a scale, and it fails when there is no scale: ❌<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らないまでも傘(かさ)を持(も)っていく</code>
is not a step down from anything, so the sentence has no shape. For plain "even if
it doesn't", <code>なくても</code> is what you want.

One further note: the main clause is usually an expectation, a request or an
assessment - <code>ほしい</code>, <code>べきだ</code>, <code>はずだ</code>,
<code>たい</code>, <code>だろう</code>. It is unusual to see the pattern report a
plain past event.

## Contrast

### ～ないまでも vs ～なくても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくても` concedes a negative and stops there.
<code>ないまでも</code> concedes a negative and **offers something smaller in
exchange**.

</div>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)ま<b>なくても</b>、レポートは書(か)けます。
You can write the report without reading all of it.
</pre>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)ま<b>ないまでも</b>、最初(さいしょ)の章(しょう)くらいは読(よ)んでおくべきだ。
You needn't read all of it, but you should at least get through the first chapter.
</pre>

The first sentence removes a requirement entirely. The second reduces it, and the
reduction is the point - notice the <code>くらいは</code> that marks the new,
lower bar. If you delete the second half of the second sentence, nothing is left
standing; delete it from the first and it still means something.

### ～ないまでも vs ～ないにしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ないにしても</code> grants a case: "even if it turns out
not to be so". <code>ないまでも</code> grants a **degree** and asks for less.

</div>

<pre>
参加(さんか)でき<b>ないにしても</b>、一度(いちど)返事(へんじ)をしてください。
Even if you can't take part, please reply once.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)とは言(い)わ<b>ないまでも</b>、見学(けんがく)くらいはしてほしい。
I'm not asking you to take part, but I would like you at least to come and watch.
</pre>

The two overlap in practice and either would pass in many sentences. Where they
differ: <code>にしても</code> is neutral about whether a scale exists, so it is at
home when the two clauses are simply different things - not attending, and
replying. <code>までも</code> insists that the second thing is a lesser version of
the first, which is why 見学(けんがく) rather than
返事(へんじ) appears in the second sentence.

### ～ないまでも vs ～までもない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the word order flips the meaning.
<code>ないまでも</code> = "even if not, at least …".
<code>までもない</code> = "there is **no need** to go that far".

</div>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)する<b>までもない</b>が、締(し)め切(き)りは来週(らいしゅう)だ。
It hardly needs saying, but the deadline is next week.
</pre>

<pre>
詳(くわ)しく説明(せつめい)し<b>ないまでも</b>、概要(がいよう)は伝(つた)えておくべきだ。
You needn't explain it in detail, but you should give them the outline.
</pre>

<code>までもない</code> attaches to the **dictionary form** and dismisses the
action as unnecessary; <code>ないまでも</code> attaches to the **ない-form** and
keeps a reduced version of it alive. <code>言(い)うまでもない</code>, "needless
to say", is the phrase you will meet most often, and it is a useful anchor: it does
not mean "even if I don't say it".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)む<b>までも</b>、要点(ようてん)は分(わ)かる。 → ✅ 全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)ま<b>ないまでも</b>、要点(ようてん)は分(わ)かる。
  The pattern is built on the ない-form. Without the negative you have the opening
  of <code>までもない</code>, which goes somewhere else.

- ❌ 週(しゅう)に一度(いちど)とは言(い)わ<b>ないまでも</b>、毎日(まいにち)連絡(れんらく)してほしい。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)とは言(い)わ<b>ないまでも</b>、週(しゅう)に一度(いちど)は連絡(れんらく)してほしい。
  The scale runs downwards. Declining the small request and then demanding the
  large one reverses the logic and reads as a joke.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が<b>来(き)ないまでも</b> → ✅ 彼(かれ)が<b>来(こ)ないまでも</b>
  来(く)る is irregular; its ない-form is <b>こない</b>.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が<b>ないまでも</b>、急(いそ)ぎましょう。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)が<b>なくても</b>、急(いそ)ぎましょう。
  There is no scale here - hurrying is not a smaller version of having no time. A
  plain <code>なくても</code> is the honest form.

## Practice

**1.** 完成(かんせい)とは____、来週(らいしゅう)までに形(かたち)にはしたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

完成(かんせい)とは<b>言(い)わないまでも</b>

The set frame <code>とは言(い)わないまでも</code> names the ideal, declines it,
and clears the way for the realistic goal. <code>形(かたち)にする</code> in the
main clause is exactly the lower rung the pattern needs.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 全員(ぜんいん)に知(し)らせる（までもない・ないまでも）が、関係者(かんけいしゃ)には伝(つた)えておこう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

全員(ぜんいん)に知(し)らせる<b>までもない</b>が、関係者(かんけいしゃ)には伝(つた)えておこう。

The verb is in the dictionary form, which only
<code>までもない</code> can take, and the meaning fits: telling everybody is
unnecessary. To use the other pattern you would write
<code>全員(ぜんいん)には知(し)らせないまでも</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It may not solve the problem completely, but this change will make things a good deal easier."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

問題(もんだい)が完全(かんぜん)に解決(かいけつ)し<b>ないまでも</b>、この変更(へんこう)でずいぶん楽(らく)になるはずだ。

"May not … completely, but …" is the scale the pattern encodes: full solution
declined, substantial improvement retained. The <code>はずだ</code> ending is the
kind of assessment the main clause usually carries.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 返事(へんじ)を書(か)くないまでも、読(よ)んだことだけは知(し)らせたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 返事(へんじ)を<b>書(か)かないまでも</b>、読(よ)んだことだけは知(し)らせたい。

The base is the ない-form, so <code>書(か)く</code> becomes
<code>書(か)かない</code>. ❌<code>書(か)くない</code> is not a form of the
verb at all - a slip that usually comes from thinking of `ない` as a separate word
rather than as a conjugation.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 手伝(てつだ)え<b>なくても</b>、様子(ようす)を見(み)に行(い)きます。
(b) 手伝(てつだ)えとは言(い)わ<b>ないまでも</b>、様子(ようす)くらいは見(み)に行(い)ってあげたら。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about the speaker. It removes a condition - being able to help - and states
what they will do regardless. Neutral, and slightly warm.

(b) is aimed at the listener, and it is doing negotiation. It names the big ask
(helping), waives it out loud, and then presents the small one as almost too modest
to refuse. That waiving move is what <code>ないまでも</code> adds, and it is why
the pattern turns up so often in requests and in gentle pressure.

</details>

# ～ずとも

**Meaning** · even without doing it; even if one does not - the literary ancestor of なくても

`ず` is the classical negative and `とも` the classical concessive, so
<code>ずとも</code> is "even not-doing". The sentence that follows says the thing
happens, or is known, or is fine, anyway - without the action ever being needed.

**This belongs to written Japanese and to set phrases.** Say
<code>なくても</code>. Recognising <code>ずとも</code> matters because it turns up
in maxims, in narration, in formal notices and, oddly enough, in product copy.

## Formation

Take the ない-form, drop the `ない`, and add <code>ずとも</code>. Two verbs are
irregular.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ずとも | 言(い)わない → 言(い)わずとも |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ずとも | 見(み)ない → 見(み)ずとも |
| Verb (する) | **せずとも** | 説明(せつめい)しない → 説明(せつめい)せずとも |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ずとも</b> | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ずとも |
| Permission frame | ～ずともよい／構(かま)わない | 来(こ)ずともよい |
| Sufficiency frame | ～ずとも済(す)む | 買(か)わずとも済(す)む |
| Modern equivalent | ～なくても | 言(い)わなくても |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `ず` itself is not archaic at all. It is alive in
<code>行(い)かずに</code>, <code>知(し)らずに</code>,
<code>言(い)わずにはいられない</code> and
<code>少(すく)なからず</code>, all of which are ordinary modern Japanese. It is
the combination with `とも` that is literary. Do not conclude from this page that
`ず` is a dead form.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
言(い)わ<b>ずとも</b>分(わ)かるだろう。
You will understand without my saying it.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)が<b>ずとも</b>、時間(じかん)は十分(じゅうぶん)にある。
There is no need to hurry; there is time enough.
</pre>

<pre>
設定(せってい)を変(か)え<b>ずとも</b>、そのままお使(つか)いいただけます。
It can be used as it is, with no need to change any settings.
</pre>

<pre>
見(み)<b>ずとも</b>、結果(けっか)はおよそ想像(そうぞう)がついた。
Without looking, he could more or less picture the result.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)せ<b>ずとも</b>、彼女(かのじょ)にはすべて伝(つた)わっていた。
Without a word of explanation, she had understood everything.
</pre>

<pre>
多(おお)くを語(かた)ら<b>ずとも</b>、その表情(ひょうじょう)がすべてを物語(ものがた)っていた。
He said little, but his face told the whole story.
</pre>

<pre>
わざわざ来(こ)<b>ずとも</b>構(かま)わない、と父(ちち)は書(か)いてよこした。
There was no need to make the journey, my father wrote back.
</pre>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)を示(しめ)さ<b>ずとも</b>、誰(だれ)の仕業(しわざ)かは明(あき)らかであった。
Even without evidence being produced, whose doing it was had become plain.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Written, and in conversation genuinely old-fashioned.** Where you will find it:
literary narration and fiction; aphorisms and proverb-like statements; formal
written notices and instructions; and - the one place a modern learner is most
likely to meet it - marketing and interface copy, where
<code>登録(とうろく)せずともご利用(りよう)いただけます</code> sounds compact
and dignified in a way that
<code>登録(とうろく)しなくても</code> does not. Saying
「急(いそ)がずともいいよ」 to a friend would land as a deliberate flourish, the
Japanese equivalent of "thou needst not hurry".

The modern equivalent is <code>なくても</code>, and it is right in essentially
every spoken situation. In writing you also have <code>なくとも</code>, which is a
useful middle rung: formal, current, and not literary.

Two frames account for a good share of real occurrences.
<code>～ずともよい</code> and <code>～ずとも構(かま)わない</code> mean "there is
no need to", and in older texts and period drama they are how permission is
granted from above. <code>～ずとも済(す)む</code> means "one gets by without
having to". Both are worth recognising as units.

Finally, a relative worth knowing:
<code>言(い)わずもがな</code>. It contains the same `ず` and looks as though it
should mean "even without saying", but it has settled into two set meanings -
"needless to say" and "better left unsaid". It is not the same construction, and
you cannot build new phrases on its pattern.

## Contrast

### ～ずとも vs ～なくても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. <code>なくても</code> is the form to
produce; <code>ずとも</code> is the form to recognise.

</div>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)ま<b>なくても</b>、内容(ないよう)は分(わ)かりますよ。
You can get the gist without reading all of it.
</pre>

<pre>
全(すべ)てを読(よ)ま<b>ずとも</b>、その意(い)は通(つう)じよう。
Even without reading the whole, the meaning will carry.
</pre>

Look at what else changes in the second sentence: 全(すべ)て for
全部(ぜんぶ), <code>意(い)</code> for
<code>内容(ないよう)</code>, the volitional <code>よう</code> for
<code>ますよ</code>. A literary particle drags the whole sentence up with it, and a
<code>ずとも</code> sitting in an otherwise everyday sentence is the tell-tale sign
of a learner who has met the form on a list.

### ～ずとも vs ～ずに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ずに` means simply "**without** doing", and describes how
something was done. <code>ずとも</code> is **concessive**: "even without doing", and
claims the result holds anyway.

</div>

<pre>
朝食(ちょうしょく)を取(と)ら<b>ずに</b>家(いえ)を出(で)た。
He left the house without eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
朝食(ちょうしょく)を取(と)ら<b>ずとも</b>昼(ひる)までは持(も)つ。
Even without breakfast, he can last until midday.
</pre>

This is the more useful of the two contrasts, because `ずに` is fully current and
you will use it often. The test is whether you can insert "even": if you can, it is
<code>ずとも</code> and you should probably be writing
<code>なくても</code> instead.

### ～ずとも vs ～なくとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three rungs of the same ladder.
<code>なくても</code> everyday, <code>なくとも</code> formal but current,
<code>ずとも</code> literary.

</div>

<pre>
少(すく)な<b>くとも</b>三日(みっか)は必要(ひつよう)です。
It will take at least three days.
</pre>

<pre>
確(たし)かめ<b>なくとも</b>、答(こた)えは見当(けんとう)がついている。
Even without checking, I have a fair idea of the answer.
</pre>

<code>少(すく)なくとも</code> is frozen and extremely common - most learners use
it long before they notice it contains this `とも` - and it is a good anchor for
the middle rung. In a report or a formal email, <code>なくとも</code> gives you the
weight of <code>ずとも</code> without the century.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 説明(せつめい)<b>しずとも</b> → ✅ 説明(せつめい)<b>せずとも</b>
  `する` uses the older negative stem `せ`, exactly as in
  <code>せずに</code> and <code>せざるを得(え)ない</code>. This is the most
  frequent error with the form.

- ❌ <b>来(き)ずとも</b> → ✅ <b>来(こ)ずとも</b>
  来(く)る is irregular: the negative stem is こ, as in 来(こ)ない.

- ❌ 買(か)わ<b>ないずとも</b> → ✅ 買(か)わ<b>ずとも</b>
  The base is the ない **stem**. The `ない` itself is replaced, not kept.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は来(こ)ずともいいよ、無理(むり)しないでね。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は来(こ)なくてもいいよ、無理(むり)しないでね。
  A classical concessive inside a friendly text message is a register collision.
  Nothing about the grammar is wrong; the sentence simply is not one a person
  would send.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence - a chat message, a product
page, or a shouted instruction?
工具(こうぐ)を使(つか)わ<b>ずとも</b>、簡単(かんたん)に組(く)み立(た)てられます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A product page, or the instruction leaflet inside the box.

<code>ずとも</code> paired with the polite potential
<code>組(く)み立(た)てられます</code> is exactly the compact, faintly
dignified register that marketing and instruction copy reaches for. In chat it
would be affected, and nobody shouts in the classical negative.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
確認(かくにん)せ<b>ずとも</b>、問題(もんだい)はあるまい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

確認(かくにん)し<b>なくても</b>、問題(もんだい)はないだろう。

Two things move together: <code>せずとも</code> becomes
<code>しなくても</code>, and the archaic <code>あるまい</code> becomes
<code>ないだろう</code>. Changing only one of them leaves a sentence with one foot
in each century, which is the usual result when a learner swaps a single word.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 傘(かさ)を持(も)た（ずに・ずとも）出(で)かけて、ずぶぬれになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傘(かさ)を持(も)た<b>ずに</b>出(で)かけて、ずぶぬれになった。

The sentence describes *how* he went out - without an umbrella - and then reports
the consequence. That is `ずに`. <code>ずとも</code> would claim that the lack of
an umbrella made no difference, which the soaking rather contradicts.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 返事(へんじ)をしずとも、意味(いみ)は通(つう)じるはずだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 返事(へんじ)を<b>せずとも</b>、意味(いみ)は通(つう)じるはずだ。

<code>する</code> takes the negative stem <code>せ</code> in this construction, not
<code>し</code>. The same irregularity shows up wherever the classical negative
appears, so <code>せずに</code>, <code>せず</code> and
<code>せざるを得(え)ない</code> all pattern together.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 言(い)わ<b>なくても</b>分(わ)かるよ。
(b) 言(い)わ<b>ずとも</b>分(わ)かる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is what a person says. It is warm, casual and addressed to someone in the room:
you don't have to spell it out, I get it.

(b) is a written line, or a line of narration, or a deliberate aphorism. The
classical form and the bare plain ending remove the listener from the sentence
altogether, so it reads as a general observation rather than a reassurance. If you
meet it in a novel it will usually be the narrator's voice; if you meet it in
speech, the speaker is quoting or performing.

</details>
