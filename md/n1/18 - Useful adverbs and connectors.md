# N1 - Useful adverbs and connectors

# 極めて

**Meaning** · extremely; in the highest degree

Read <code>極(きわ)めて</code>. It is the strongest of the ordinary "very"
words and also the stiffest: it lives in reports, editorials and official
statements, not in conversation.

## Usage

<code>極(きわ)めて</code> is an adverb. It stands directly in front of the word
it intensifies, and that word describes a **quality**:

- い-adjective: <code>極(きわ)めて高(たか)い確率(かくりつ)</code>
- な-adjective: <code>極(きわ)めて困難(こんなん)な状況(じょうきょう)</code> - the `な` still appears before a noun
- adverbial phrase: <code>極(きわ)めて順調(じゅんちょう)に進(すす)んでいる</code>
- a stative predicate: <code>極(きわ)めて限(かぎ)られている</code>

What it does **not** do is intensify an ordinary action verb. ❌<code>極(きわ)めて疲(つか)れた</code>
is not Japanese; that job belongs to <code>非常(ひじょう)に</code>, とても or すごく.
Nor is it a noun: there is no ❌<code>極(きわ)めての問題(もんだい)</code> and no
❌<code>問題(もんだい)は極(きわ)めてだ</code>.

The word is historically the て-form of <code>極(きわ)める</code>, "to take
something to its limit", and that is where the force comes from: not "very" but
"at the far end of the scale". Its habitual partners are evaluative -
<code>重要(じゅうよう)</code>, <code>困難(こんなん)</code>,
<code>深刻(しんこく)</code>, <code>危険(きけん)</code>, まれ,
<code>少(すく)ない</code>, <code>遺憾(いかん)</code>.

## Examples

<pre>
このやり方(かた)は<b>極(きわ)めて</b>危険(きけん)だ。
This approach is extremely dangerous.
</pre>

<pre>
成功(せいこう)の可能性(かのうせい)は<b>極(きわ)めて</b>低(ひく)いと言(い)わざるを得(え)ない。
We have to say that the chances of success are extremely low.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の対応(たいおう)は<b>極(きわ)めて</b>遺憾(いかん)であると、大臣(だいじん)は述(の)べた。
The minister said that the handling of the matter was extremely regrettable.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)が現場(げんば)に与(あた)える影響(えいきょう)は<b>極(きわ)めて</b>大(おお)きいと考(かんが)えられる。
The new rules are likely to have an extremely large impact on the people doing the work.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and formal, with almost no life in casual speech. You will meet it in
newspapers, academic papers, company announcements, court documents and prepared
remarks. Said out loud over dinner, <code>今日(きょう)は極(きわ)めて暑(あつ)いね</code>
sounds like a weather bureau reading its own bulletin; the natural sentence is
<code>今日(きょう)はすごく暑(あつ)いね</code>.

If you need the idea in speech, the ladder runs roughly すごく（casual）→ とても
（neutral polite）→ <code>非常(ひじょう)に</code>（formal but sayable）→
<code>極(きわ)めて</code>（written）. These are tendencies rather than hard
boundaries: <code>極(きわ)めて</code> does turn up in speech, but the speech in
question is a press conference or a lecture.

One collocation is worth learning whole. <code>極(きわ)めて遺憾(いかん)である</code>
is the standard formula of official displeasure - a government protesting, a
company apologising. It is stronger than it looks in translation, and it is the
sentence you will hear on the evening news.

## Contrast

### 極(きわ)めて vs 非常(ひじょう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal intensifiers, but
<code>非常(ひじょう)に</code> can be spoken and can intensify verbs of feeling,
while <code>極(きわ)めて</code> is a writer's word that only takes quality
words.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)のあと、<b>非常(ひじょう)に</b>疲(つか)れました。
I was very tired after yesterday's meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)を特定(とくてい)するのは<b>極(きわ)めて</b>難(むずか)しい。
Identifying the cause of the accident is extremely difficult.
</pre>

A speaker thanking you can say <code>非常(ひじょう)にありがたいです</code>; nobody
says ❌<code>極(きわ)めてありがたいです</code>. Going the other way, a written
report can use either, and <code>極(きわ)めて</code> will read as the graver of
the two.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は極(きわ)めて歩(ある)いた。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は<b>非常(ひじょう)に</b>たくさん歩(ある)いた。
  <code>極(きわ)めて</code> intensifies a quality, not an action. Put an
  adjective or a quantity word in front of it and the sentence works again.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は極(きわ)めての重要(じゅうよう)だ。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は<b>極(きわ)めて重要(じゅうよう)</b>だ。
  No `の`. It is an adverb attaching straight onto the adjective, exactly like
  とても.

- ❌ このケーキ、極(きわ)めておいしい！ → ✅ このケーキ、<b>すごく</b>おいしい！
  Grammatical, but the register is wrong by a wide margin. Nobody praises a cake
  in the language of a ministry statement.

## Practice

**1.** 報告書(ほうこくしょ)より：この数値(すうち)は____信頼性(しんらいせい)が低(ひく)く、再調査(さいちょうさ)が必要(ひつよう)である。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この数値(すうち)は<b>極(きわ)めて</b>信頼性(しんらいせい)が低(ひく)く、再調査(さいちょうさ)が必要(ひつよう)である。

A written report with a plain <code>である</code> ending is exactly the habitat of
this word, and <code>低(ひく)い</code> is the kind of evaluative adjective it
takes. すごく would clash with the surrounding style.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: この夏(なつ)は極(きわ)めて汗(あせ)をかいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この夏(なつ)は<b>非常(ひじょう)に</b>汗(あせ)をかいた。

<code>汗(あせ)をかく</code> is an action, and <code>極(きわ)めて</code> cannot
intensify one. Either swap in <code>非常(ひじょう)に</code>, or rebuild the
sentence around an adjective, as in <code>汗(あせ)の量(りょう)が極(きわ)めて多(おお)かった</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この案(あん)は非常(ひじょう)に難(むずか)しいです。
(b) この案(あん)は極(きわ)めて困難(こんなん)である。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say the plan is very hard, and neither is more "true" than the other. What
differs is the room they are spoken in. Sentence (a) is polite spoken Japanese and
could be said to a colleague; sentence (b), with the written adverb, the Sino-Japanese
adjective and the <code>である</code> ending, is a line from a document, and saying it
aloud would sound like reading a verdict.

</details>

# とりわけ

**Meaning** · especially; above all - singling out the one member of a group that
has the quality most

`とりわけ` does not just say "this one is X". It says: the whole set is X already,
and this one is X even more than the rest. Without a set in view, the word has
nothing to do.

## Usage

`とりわけ` is an adverb, and it can sit in two places:

- **In front of the clause**, picking out a whole situation:
  とりわけ今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)は厳(きび)しかった。
- **Directly in front of the element it selects**, including a bare noun phrase:
  果物(くだもの)では、とりわけ桃(もも)が好(す)きだ。

The group it selects from is normally spelled out in the sentence before it, or in
the clause before the comma. Very often it teams up with
<code>中(なか)でも</code> - <code>中(なか)でもとりわけ</code> - which makes
the set-and-member logic explicit and is a good frame to memorise.

Two things it is not. It is not a noun, so there is no ❌とりわけの;
and it does not head the "not particularly" frame that
<code>特(とく)に</code> owns. Asked whether you have any requests, you answer
<code>特(とく)にありません</code>, never ❌とりわけありません.

## Examples

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)の北海道(ほっかいどう)は寒(さむ)いが、<b>とりわけ</b>二月(にがつ)が厳(きび)しい。
Hokkaido is cold in winter, and February in particular is brutal.
</pre>

<pre>
果物(くだもの)の中(なか)でも、<b>とりわけ</b>桃(もも)が好(す)きです。
Of all the fruit there is, peaches are the ones I like best.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の新人(しんじん)はよく働(はたら)くが、<b>とりわけ</b>田中(たなか)さんの伸(の)びが著(いちじる)しい。
This year's new hires all work hard, but Tanaka's progress in particular is striking.
</pre>

<pre>
物価(ぶっか)の上昇(じょうしょう)はどの家庭(かてい)にも影響(えいきょう)するが、<b>とりわけ</b>年金(ねんきん)で暮(く)らす人(ひと)たちには重(おも)い負担(ふたん)となっている。
Rising prices affect every household, but for people living on a pension they are a particularly heavy burden.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Bookish but not stiff. `とりわけ` belongs to newspapers, essays, presentations and
careful spoken Japanese; used in a chat with friends it lands roughly the way
"above all" does in English - understood, slightly formal, faintly literary. It is
not marked for age or gender.

The everyday word is <code>特(とく)に</code>, and it will cover almost every
occasion on which a learner reaches for `とりわけ`. Where `とりわけ` earns its keep
is in writing, because it makes the comparison audible: a reader who meets it knows
immediately that a group has just been narrowed down. In a paragraph that already
listed the group, `とりわけ` is the cleaner choice.

There is a rarer written variant <code>とりわけて</code>, which means the same
thing and sounds older. You need only recognise it.

## Contrast

### とりわけ vs 特(とく)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>特(とく)に</code> is the all-purpose word and needs no
group. `とりわけ` is its written cousin and only works when a set is in play; it
also has no negative "not particularly" use.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は<b>特(とく)に</b>予定(よてい)がありません。
I've got nothing particular on today.
</pre>

<pre>
どの作品(さくひん)もよかったが、<b>とりわけ</b>最後(さいご)の一枚(いちまい)が印象(いんしょう)に残(のこ)った。
Every piece was good, but the last one especially stayed with me.
</pre>

Swap them in the first sentence and you get ❌とりわけ予定(よてい)がありません,
which is broken: there is no set of plans being ranked. Swap them in the second and
nothing breaks - <code>特(とく)に</code> works there too, just with less of the
"of all of them" flavour.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ とりわけ問題(もんだい)はありません。 → ✅ <b>特(とく)に</b>問題(もんだい)はありません。
  The "nothing in particular" answer is fixed to <code>特(とく)に</code>.
  `とりわけ` cannot take that job because there is no group to select from.

- ❌ とりわけの好(す)きな季節(きせつ)は秋(あき)です。 → ✅ 季節(きせつ)の中(なか)では、<b>とりわけ</b>秋(あき)が好(す)きです。
  No `の` after an adverb - and notice the repair also supplies the set, which is
  what the word was waiting for.

- ❌ （out of nowhere）とりわけ今日(きょう)は忙(いそが)しい。 → ✅ 今週(こんしゅう)はずっと忙(いそが)しいが、<b>とりわけ</b>今日(きょう)はひどい。
  Not ungrammatical, but it leaves the listener waiting for the comparison. State
  the group first; `とりわけ` is the second half of a two-part idea.

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)の小説(しょうせつ)はどれもおもしろいが、____初期(しょき)の作品(さくひん)がいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)の小説(しょうせつ)はどれもおもしろいが、<b>とりわけ</b>初期(しょき)の作品(さくひん)がいい。

The first clause sets up the group - all of his novels - and the second narrows it
to one part of that group, which is exactly the shape this adverb was built for.

</details>

**2.** Choose とりわけ or 特(とく)に, and say why:
「何(なに)か希望(きぼう)はありますか。」「いえ、____ございません。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>特(とく)に</b>ございません。

The negative "nothing in particular" reply is a fixed frame belonging to
<code>特(とく)に</code>. Here nothing is being ranked or compared, so `とりわけ`
has no work to do and the sentence would simply sound wrong.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, using とりわけ:
"The whole team played well, but the goalkeeper was outstanding."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

チーム全体(ぜんたい)がよく戦(たたか)ったが、<b>とりわけ</b>キーパーの活躍(かつやく)が目立(めだ)った。

The English "but ... outstanding" hides the same two-part structure: a group that
was all good, then one member above the rest. Note that `とりわけ` goes in front of
the member being singled out, not in front of the verb.

</details>

# あくまでも

**Meaning** · (1) purely; merely; nothing more than - and (2) to the very end,
without giving an inch

Two senses that look unrelated share one idea: a line that does not move. In sense
1 the line is around a claim - this is only that, do not read more into it. In
sense 2 it is around a position - I will not shift from it.

## Usage

`あくまで` and `あくまでも` are the same word; the `も` adds emphasis and is very
common in sense 1. It is written in kana in practice, though the kanji
<code>飽(あ)くまで</code> exists.

**Sense 1 - limiting a claim.** The frame is `あくまでも` + a noun + `だ／です`, or
`あくまでも` + a clause + <code>だけだ／にすぎない</code>. The nouns that turn up
here are worth learning as a set: <code>一例(いちれい)</code>,
<code>目安(めやす)</code>, <code>参考(さんこう)</code>,
<code>予定(よてい)</code>, <code>個人(こじん)の意見(いけん)</code>. This is
the sense you meet daily in meetings, emails and small print.

**Sense 2 - holding out.** Here `あくまで` modifies a verb of persistence or
resistance: <code>争(あらそ)う</code>, <code>否定(ひてい)する</code>,
<code>反対(はんたい)する</code>, <code>貫(つらぬ)く</code>,
<code>認(みと)めない</code>. The meaning is "right through to the end, whatever
happened", so a sentence that goes on to say the person gave in contradicts itself.

What it never means is "very". The dictionary gloss "to the utmost degree" tempts
learners into ❌あくまでも疲(つか)れた, which is not Japanese.

## Examples

<pre>
これは<b>あくまでも</b>目安(めやす)です。
This is no more than a rough guide.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>あくまで</b>私(わたし)個人(こじん)の意見(いけん)ですが、この案(あん)には無理(むり)があると思(おも)います。
Speaking purely for myself, I think this plan is a stretch.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)は<b>あくまでも</b>現時点(げんじてん)のもので、来月(らいげつ)には変(か)わる可能性(かのうせい)があります。
The figures are only as of today, and they may well change next month.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)側(がわ)は<b>あくまで</b>責任(せきにん)を認(みと)めず、裁判(さいばん)は五年(ごねん)に及(およ)んだ。
The company refused to admit responsibility to the very end, and the trial dragged on for five years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Sense 1 is formal but entirely sayable, and it is one of the most useful pieces of
working Japanese in this group. A consultant presenting a forecast, a colleague
offering an opinion without wanting to be quoted, a website disclaiming its own
figures - all of them reach for `あくまでも`. It protects the speaker, and Japanese
professional speech does a great deal of that.

Sense 2 is more declamatory. It suits reporting on disputes, trials and
negotiations, and in the first person it sounds firm to the point of stubborn:
<code>私(わたし)はあくまで反対(はんたい)します</code> is not a soft objection.
Choose it deliberately.

There is a mild politeness effect worth noticing. Prefacing an opinion with
<code>あくまで個人(こじん)の意見(いけん)ですが</code> lowers the temperature of
what follows, rather like "just my two cents". Dropping it makes the same sentence
sound like a ruling.

## Contrast

### あくまでも vs 一応(いちおう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both hedge, but they hedge different things.
<code>一応(いちおう)</code> lowers the *thoroughness* of what you did;
`あくまでも` limits the *status* of what you are claiming.

</div>

<pre>
<b>一応(いちおう)</b>確認(かくにん)しましたが、見落(みお)としがあるかもしれません。
I've had a look, for what it's worth, though I may have missed something.
</pre>

<pre>
これは<b>あくまでも</b>確認(かくにん)のための連絡(れんらく)で、決定(けってい)ではありません。
This message is purely to confirm; it is not a decision.
</pre>

The first speaker admits the check may have been shallow. The second makes no
admission at all about the quality of anything - they are drawing a boundary
around what the message *is*. Learners reach for
<code>一応(いちおう)</code> when they mean the boundary, and end up sounding
unsure of their own work.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今日(きょう)はあくまでも疲(つか)れた。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は<b>とても</b>疲(つか)れた。
  `あくまでも` is not an intensifier. The "to the utmost" in the dictionary gloss
  is about persistence, not about degree.

- ❌ あくまでの予想(よそう)です。 → ✅ <b>あくまでも</b>予想(よそう)です。
  No `の` between the adverb and the noun predicate. Say `あくまで` or `あくまでも`
  and continue straight into the noun.

- ❌ あくまで反対(はんたい)しましたが、最後(さいご)は賛成(さんせい)しました。 → ✅ 最初(さいしょ)は反対(はんたい)しましたが、最後(さいご)は賛成(さんせい)しました。
  Sense 2 means the position never moved. Following it with a change of mind
  cancels the word you just used.

## Practice

**1.** ____例(れい)ですので、実際(じっさい)の金額(きんがく)とは異(こと)なります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>あくまでも</b>例(れい)ですので、実際(じっさい)の金額(きんがく)とは異(こと)なります。

Classic sense 1: the speaker is fencing off the example so that nobody treats it as
a quotation. Note the shape - adverb, noun, copula - which is the frame to
memorise.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)はあくまでも上手(じょうず)にピアノを弾(ひ)く。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は<b>とても</b>上手(じょうず)にピアノを弾(ひ)く。

The sentence is trying to use `あくまでも` as "extremely", which it cannot do. If
you did want `あくまで` here it would have to mean persistence, as in
<code>あくまで自分(じぶん)のやり方(かた)で弾(ひ)く</code>, "he insists on
playing it his own way" - a completely different claim.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)はあくまで自分(じぶん)の説(せつ)を曲(ま)げなかった。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)はしばらく自分(じぶん)の説(せつ)を曲(ま)げなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) says she never gave way at all, and the adverb adds a note of
admiration or exasperation depending on who is speaking - it is the sense-2 use,
about a position held to the end. Sentence (b) merely reports a stretch of time and
quietly implies that she gave way afterwards.

</details>

# ことごとく

**Meaning** · every last one of them; all, without a single exception

`ことごとく` counts nothing and quantifies nothing. It says that a whole set went
the same way and not one member escaped - and, nine times out of ten, that the way
they went was bad.

## Usage

`ことごとく` is used adverbially in modern Japanese: it modifies the predicate, not
the noun. The subject or object is a **plural set** - proposals, predictions,
windows, plans - because a word meaning "without exception" needs exceptions to be
possible.

- ✅ <code>提案(ていあん)は<b>ことごとく</b>退(しりぞ)けられた</code>
- ❌ <code>ことごとくの提案(ていあん)</code> - for that you need
  <code>すべての提案(ていあん)</code>

Its habitual company is adversity, and very often the passive:
<code>失敗(しっぱい)する</code>, <code>外(はず)れる</code>,
<code>無視(むし)される</code>, <code>否定(ひてい)される</code>,
<code>崩(くず)れる</code>, <code>裏切(うらぎ)られる</code>. Favourable uses
exist - <code>予想(よそう)がことごとく的中(てきちゅう)した</code> is fine - but
the default colour is defeat, one item after another.

In kanji it is <code>悉(ことごと)く</code>, which is outside the standard
list and appears mainly in literary writing. Write the kana.

## Examples

<pre>
予想(よそう)は<b>ことごとく</b>外(はず)れた。
Every one of my predictions turned out wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
用意(ようい)していた質問(しつもん)は<b>ことごとく</b>無視(むし)された。
Every question I had prepared was ignored.
</pre>

<pre>
委員会(いいんかい)に出(だ)した案(あん)は、<b>ことごとく</b>反対(はんたい)にあって流(なが)れた。
The proposals we put to the committee all ran into opposition and came to nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)で、海岸(かいがん)沿(ぞ)いの建物(たてもの)は<b>ことごとく</b>屋根(やね)を飛(と)ばされた。
The typhoon tore the roofs off every one of the buildings along the coast.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written, and rather grand. `ことごとく` belongs to journalism, history writing,
fiction and speeches; in ordinary conversation it sounds as though you have stepped
into a narration booth. Reporting that everything in the shop was cheap, you say
<code>全部(ぜんぶ)安(やす)かった</code>, not
<code>ことごとく安(やす)かった</code>.

The spoken equivalents are <code>全部(ぜんぶ)</code>, <code>すべて</code>,
<code>何(なに)もかも</code> and, with a similar flavour of exhaustiveness,
<code>一(ひと)つ残(のこ)らず</code>. Of those,
<code>一(ひと)つ残(のこ)らず</code> is closest in feel, and it is safe in
speech.

Because the word is dramatic, it carries emotion. A writer choosing `ことごとく`
over <code>すべて</code> is usually registering dismay, or admiration for a
relentless run of something. Used of trivia it sounds comic - which is a real
effect writers use on purpose, but not one to stumble into.

## Contrast

### ことごとく vs すべて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>すべて</code> is a neutral quantifier and can be a noun
（<code>すべてが</code>, <code>すべての人(ひと)</code>）. `ことごとく` is
adverb-only, written, and adds "not one of them got through".

</div>

<pre>
書類(しょるい)は<b>すべて</b>確認(かくにん)しました。
I've checked all of the documents.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)は現場(げんば)の事情(じじょう)を<b>ことごとく</b>無視(むし)している。
The new rules ignore every last one of the realities on the ground.
</pre>

The first sentence is a report of coverage; nothing is felt about it. The second is
a complaint. Swapping in <code>すべて</code> there would still be grammatical, but
the sting goes out of it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ことごとくの窓(まど)が割(わ)れた。 → ✅ 窓(まど)が<b>ことごとく</b>割(わ)れた。
  It cannot modify a noun. Move it in front of the verb, or use
  <code>すべての窓(まど)</code>.

- ❌ この本(ほん)をことごとく読(よ)んだ。 → ✅ この本(ほん)を<b>全部(ぜんぶ)</b>読(よ)んだ。
  One book is not a set. `ことごとく` needs several things that could each have
  gone differently - <code>棚(たな)の本(ほん)をことごとく読(よ)んだ</code>
  would be fine.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)の買(か)い物(もの)、ことごとく安(やす)かったよ。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)の買(か)い物(もの)、<b>全部(ぜんぶ)</b>安(やす)かったよ。
  Casual chat about shopping is the wrong room for this word. The register clash is
  loud enough to sound like a joke.

## Practice

**1.** 事前(じぜん)に立(た)てた計画(けいかく)は、現地(げんち)に着(つ)いたとたん____崩(くず)れた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

事前(じぜん)に立(た)てた計画(けいかく)は、現地(げんち)に着(つ)いたとたん<b>ことごとく</b>崩(くず)れた。

The plural set - the plans made in advance - is already in the sentence, and the
outcome is a uniform collapse, which is exactly this adverb's territory. The
written narrative style of the sentence also fits it.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in natural spoken Japanese:
彼(かれ)の言(い)うことは、ことごとく正(ただ)しかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)の言(い)うこと、全部(ぜんぶ)当(あ)たってたよ。／彼(かれ)の言(い)ったことは一(ひと)つ残(のこ)らず正(ただ)しかった。

Speech drops the literary adverb for <code>全部(ぜんぶ)</code>, or keeps the
"without exception" feel with <code>一(ひと)つ残(のこ)らず</code>, which is at
home in conversation in a way that `ことごとく` is not.

</details>

**3.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)でことごとくの意見(いけん)が出(で)た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)で<b>さまざまな</b>意見(いけん)が出(で)た。

Two problems, and the second is the interesting one. `ことごとく` cannot take
`の` before a noun; and "various opinions came out" is not a claim about a set
being exhausted at all, so no repair using this adverb will work. Choose a
different word entirely.

</details>

# まるっきり

**Meaning** · totally; not in the slightest - a blunt spoken intensifier that
almost always lands on a negative

`まるっきり` is the sound of someone throwing up their hands. It is emphatic,
colloquial, and it needs something negative to attach to: a `ない`, or a word whose
content is already "not that".

## Usage

The partner is the lesson.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まるっきり` must be followed by a negative predicate, or by a
word of negative content: <code>違(ちが)う</code>, <code>別(べつ)</code>,
<code>反対(はんたい)</code>, <code>逆(ぎゃく)</code>, ダメ.

</div>

- With a negative: <code>まるっきり知(し)らない</code>,
  <code>まるっきり通(つう)じなかった</code>
- With a negative-content word: <code>まるっきり別(べつ)の話(はなし)</code>,
  <code>まるっきり反対(はんたい)だ</code>

There is no affirmative use. ❌<code>まるっきり上手(じょうず)だ</code> and
❌<code>まるっきりおいしい</code> are simply wrong, in the same way that "not in the
slightest good" cannot be turned into "in the slightest good".

`まるきり` is the same word without the small つ, and you will hear both. You may
also meet the very casual <code>まるっきし</code>. None of the three is a writing
word.

## Examples

<pre>
その話(はなし)は<b>まるっきり</b>知(し)らなかった。
I had absolutely no idea about that.
</pre>

<pre>
兄弟(きょうだい)なのに、性格(せいかく)は<b>まるっきり</b>違(ちが)う。
They're brothers, but their personalities are nothing alike.
</pre>

<pre>
習(なら)ったはずなのに、<b>まるっきり</b>覚(おぼ)えていないんだよね。
I'm supposed to have learned it, and yet I don't remember a thing.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)の職場(しょくば)とは<b>まるっきり</b>別(べつ)の世界(せかい)で、最初(さいしょ)の一年(いちねん)は苦労(くろう)した。
It was a completely different world from my old workplace, and the first year was hard going.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Spoken, casual, and faintly of an older generation. `まるっきり` turns up in
grumbling, in storytelling, in dubbed dialogue and in the speech of people who are
not choosing their words carefully. It is not rude, but it is not neat either, and
it has no place in a report, an email to a client, or an exam essay.

The neutral replacements are <code>全然(ぜんぜん)</code> in speech and
<code>まったく</code> in writing; both do the same work with none of the flavour.
If you want the flavour in writing, put it in a character's mouth.

Younger speakers use it less than older ones - a tendency, not a rule, and you will
still hear it from anyone recounting a total failure. What learners should take
from that is simply this: understanding it matters more than producing it, and if
you do produce it, do so in conversation and nowhere else.

## Contrast

### まるっきり vs まるで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they overlap on "not at all", and only `まるで` can build a
simile. `まるっきり` is the more colloquial of the two and never means "as if".

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は<b>まるっきり</b>分(わ)からなかった。
I couldn't follow his explanation at all.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は<b>まるで</b>暗号(あんごう)のようだった。
His explanation was like something in code.
</pre>

Put `まるっきり` into the second sentence and it breaks: there is no
❌<code>まるっきり暗号(あんごう)のようだ</code>. Put `まるで` into the first and
nothing breaks, but the sentence rises a step in register, toward writing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この映画(えいが)はまるっきりおもしろい。 → ✅ この映画(えいが)は<b>すごく</b>おもしろい。
  There is no positive use. To keep the word, negate the predicate:
  <code>まるっきりおもしろくない</code>.

- ❌ 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)は、両者(りょうしゃ)でまるっきり一致(いっち)しなかった。 → ✅ 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)は、両者(りょうしゃ)で<b>まったく</b>一致(いっち)しなかった。
  Grammatically fine, stylistically wrong. A written report takes
  <code>まったく</code>.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)はまるっきり子供(こども)のように泣(な)いた。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>まるで</b>子供(こども)のように泣(な)いた。
  Similes belong to `まるで`. `まるっきり` has no comparison use at all.

## Practice

**1.** 習(なら)ったばかりの言葉(ことば)なのに、テストでは____出(で)てこなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

習(なら)ったばかりの言葉(ことば)なのに、テストでは<b>まるっきり</b>出(で)てこなかった。

The predicate is negative, the tone is exasperated and the sentence is spoken
Japanese - all three point to this adverb. In a written account you would put
<code>まったく</code> here instead.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: この辞書(じしょ)はまるっきり役(やく)に立(た)つ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この辞書(じしょ)はまるっきり役(やく)に<b>立(た)たない</b>。

Reverse the predicate. As written, the sentence pairs a negative-only intensifier
with a positive statement, which no amount of context can rescue.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)のやり方(かた)とは、まるっきり逆(ぎゃく)です。
(b) 彼(かれ)のやり方(かた)とは、まったく逆(ぎゃく)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The information is identical: both say the two methods are opposites. The
difference is the speaker. Sentence (a) sounds like conversation, a little heated,
and would be at home in a complaint over coffee; sentence (b) is neutral and could
appear in a written comparison without anyone noticing the adverb at all.

</details>

# およそ

**Meaning** · roughly, approximately - and, in the N1 use, "not by any measure at
all", teamed with a negative

One word, two jobs, and the reader tells them apart by what comes next. A number
follows, and `およそ` means "about". A negative follows, and it means "utterly
not - this does not even belong in the category".

## Usage

**Sense 1 - approximately.** `およそ` goes in front of a quantity:
<code>およそ十分(じゅっぷん)</code>, <code>およそ二万人(にまんにん)</code>,
<code>およそ半分(はんぶん)</code>. Neutral, and usable in speech and writing
alike, though written Japanese often prefers <code>約(やく)</code> in front of
figures. Note that it can also mean "a rough idea of" in
<code>およその見当(けんとう)</code> and <code>およその数(かず)</code>.

**Sense 2 - the N1 one.** The frame is <code>およそ～ない</code>. The predicate is
negative, or a word whose content is negative:

- <code>およそ現実的(げんじつてき)ではない</code>
- <code>およそ考(かんが)えられない</code>
- <code>およそふさわしくない</code>
- <code>およそ縁(えん)のない</code>

The force is not "very much not". It is "not by any standard one might apply" - a
verdict rather than a measurement. There is no affirmative version of this sense:
❌<code>およそ現実的(げんじつてき)だ</code>.

**A third use** worth recognising: <code>およそ～というものは</code>, "any X at all,
X considered generally", as in
<code>およそ研究(けんきゅう)というものは時間(じかん)がかかる</code>. Essayistic
and formal.

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)まで<b>およそ</b>十分(じゅっぷん)かかります。
It takes about ten minutes to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
この計画(けいかく)は<b>およそ</b>現実的(げんじつてき)ではない。
This plan is not remotely realistic.
</pre>

<pre>
責任(せきにん)ある立場(たちば)の人間(にんげん)の発言(はつげん)としては、<b>およそ</b>ふさわしくない内容(ないよう)だった。
For someone in a position of responsibility, the remarks were wholly inappropriate.
</pre>

<pre>
流行(りゅうこう)とは<b>およそ</b>縁(えん)のない服装(ふくそう)で、彼女(かのじょ)は平気(へいき)な顔(かお)をして街(まち)を歩(ある)いていた。
Dressed in a way that had nothing whatever to do with fashion, she walked through town without a care.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Sense 1 is register-neutral and extremely common; nobody will notice you using it.
In documents and news copy <code>約(やく)</code> is the house style in front of
figures, and <code>およそ</code> sounds a touch softer and more spoken by
comparison.

Sense 2 is the opposite: written, formal and judgemental. It belongs to editorials,
reviews, criticism and prepared speech. Said in conversation about a bowl of ramen
it is absurd - ❌<code>このラーメン、およそおいしくない</code> - where you want
<code>全然(ぜんぜん)おいしくない</code>. Reserve it for the moment when you want
to say that something falls outside the bounds of what could be called reasonable,
realistic or appropriate.

The kanji <code>凡(およ)そ</code> exists and turns up in older writing. The kana
spelling is standard.

Because the two senses share a word, the surrounding sentence has to disambiguate,
and in practice it always does: numbers on one side, `ない` on the other. Learners
who trip up are usually reading sense 1 into a sentence that has no number in it.

## Contrast

### およそ～ない vs まったく～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>まったく～ない</code> measures: none at all.
<code>およそ～ない</code> judges: not by any standard, not even close to counting as
that.

</div>

<pre>
その説明(せつめい)は<b>まったく</b>理解(りかい)できなかった。
I couldn't understand that explanation at all.
</pre>

<pre>
その説明(せつめい)は、専門家(せんもんか)のものとしては<b>およそ</b>説明(せつめい)になっていない。
As the words of a specialist, that does not begin to count as an explanation.
</pre>

The first reports the speaker's own failure to follow. The second delivers a
verdict on the explanation itself, and needs the standard - "as the words of a
specialist" - to measure against. That comparison clause is very often present when
`およそ` is used this way.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ その案(あん)はおよそ現実的(げんじつてき)だ。 → ✅ その案(あん)はおよそ現実的(げんじつてき)では<b>ない</b>。
  Sense 2 exists only in the negative. Without the `ない` the sentence says nothing
  the speaker intended.

- ❌ 準備(じゅんび)はおよそ終(お)わりました。 → ✅ 準備(じゅんび)は<b>ほぼ</b>終(お)わりました。
  "Roughly" in the sense of "nearly complete" is <code>ほぼ</code> or
  <code>だいたい</code>. `およそ` in sense 1 quantifies numbers, not degrees of
  completion.

- ❌ この店(みせ)のコーヒーはおよそおいしくない。 → ✅ この店(みせ)のコーヒーは<b>全然(ぜんぜん)</b>おいしくない。
  Grammatical, and comically over-dressed. Everyday complaints take
  <code>全然(ぜんぜん)</code>.

## Practice

**1.** Which sense is this, and how do you know?
会場(かいじょう)にはおよそ三百人(さんびゃくにん)が集(あつ)まった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sense 1, "approximately". The giveaway is the number immediately after the adverb
and the entirely neutral, affirmative predicate. Sense 2 would require a negative
ending, and there is none here.

</details>

**2.** 子供(こども)を一人(ひとり)で留守番(るすばん)させるなど、安全(あんぜん)という点(てん)から見(み)て____感心(かんしん)できない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

安全(あんぜん)という点(てん)から見(み)て<b>およそ</b>感心(かんしん)できない。

The negative predicate and the explicit standard - "from the point of view of
safety" - are the two signals for sense 2. The sentence is passing judgement, not
reporting a quantity.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この提案(ていあん)はまったく新(あたら)しくない。
(b) この提案(ていあん)はおよそ新(あたら)しさに欠(か)けている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) is a flat statement: there is nothing new in it, and the tone could be
spoken. Sentence (b) reads as written criticism - the adverb says the proposal does
not come near the standard of novelty at all, and the whole sentence sounds like a
line from a review or a referee's report.

</details>

# さぞ

**Meaning** · you must be ...; it must have been ... - the speaker imagining how
something felt for somebody else

`さぞ` is a word of sympathy. You did not see the thing, you cannot feel it, and
you say so anyway: after a journey like that, you must be worn out. Everything on
this page follows from that one fact.

## Usage

The partner is not optional.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さぞ` must be completed by a conjecture ending:
<code>だろう／でしょう</code>, <code>～ことだろう／～ことでしょう</code>, or
<code>～にちがいない</code>. A `さぞ` sentence that ends in
<code>です／ます</code> is broken.

</div>

The commonest shape is polite and slightly formulaic:

- <code>さぞお疲(つか)れでしょう</code>
- <code>さぞご心配(しんぱい)だったでしょう</code>
- <code>さぞ驚(おどろ)かれたことでしょう</code>

Two restrictions matter as much as the ending. First, the feeling belongs to
**somebody else** - you know your own feelings, so ❌<code>さぞ私(わたし)は嬉(うれ)しいでしょう</code>
is nonsense. Second, `さぞ` imagines an experience, not a fact: it does not do
weather forecasts or train times, which take <code>きっと</code> or
<code>たぶん</code>.

<code>さぞかし</code> is the emphatic variant and is common;
<code>さぞや</code> is literary and old.

## Examples

<pre>
長(なが)い旅(たび)で、<b>さぞ</b>お疲(つか)れでしょう。
You must be exhausted after such a long journey.
</pre>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)の知(し)らせに、ご両親(りょうしん)も<b>さぞ</b>喜(よろこ)ばれたことでしょう。
Your parents must have been overjoyed at the news that you passed.
</pre>

<pre>
あんな大(おお)きな地震(じしん)のあとでは、<b>さぞ</b>不安(ふあん)な夜(よる)を過(す)ごされたにちがいありません。
After an earthquake that size, you must have spent some very anxious nights.
</pre>

<pre>
三十年(さんじゅうねん)会(あ)っていなかったのだから、再会(さいかい)の夜(よる)は<b>さぞかし</b>話(はなし)が尽(つ)きなかっただろう。
They hadn't seen each other in thirty years, so I'm sure they talked the whole night through.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Polite, formal-leaning, and close to a set phrase. `さぞ` lives in letters, in
condolences, in hospital corridors and in the careful speech of someone
acknowledging another person's hardship. It is the language of
<code>お疲(つか)れでしょう</code> and <code>おつらかったでしょう</code>, and it
carries warmth without claiming to know.

Between friends it sounds ceremonious. A friend who has just moved house wants
<code>引(ひ)っ越(こ)し大変(たいへん)だったでしょ</code> or
<code>つかれたよね</code>, not <code>さぞお疲(つか)れでしょう</code>, which would
sound like a hotel greeting. It is a tendency rather than a rule - people do use it
playfully among friends, precisely because it is so formal.

The default colour is sympathy: fatigue, worry, grief, hardship. It also does
admiring imagination, as in
<code>さぞ立派(りっぱ)なお宅(たく)なのでしょうね</code>, said of a house you
have never seen. What it will not do is malice; there is a sarcastic use
（<code>さぞご満足(まんぞく)でしょうね</code>）and it is unmistakably barbed, so
avoid it unless you mean it.

## Contrast

### さぞ vs きっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>きっと</code> is general-purpose certainty about anything
at all. `さぞ` only imagines another person's feelings or circumstances, and always
leans sympathetic.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は<b>きっと</b>晴(は)れますよ。
I'm sure it will clear up tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で全部(ぜんぶ)準備(じゅんび)したのなら、<b>さぞ</b>大変(たいへん)だったでしょう。
If you got it all ready on your own, it must have been quite something.
</pre>

Swap them and only one direction survives. <code>きっと大変(たいへん)だったでしょう</code>
is perfectly good Japanese, a little plainer. ❌<code>さぞ明日(あした)は晴(は)れるでしょう</code>
is not: the weather has no feelings for the speaker to imagine.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ さぞお疲(つか)れです。 → ✅ さぞお疲(つか)れ<b>でしょう</b>。
  The conjecture ending is obligatory. `さぞ` announces that you are guessing, so a
  flat assertion contradicts it.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)に受(う)かって、さぞ嬉(うれ)しいです。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)に受(う)かって、<b>とても</b>嬉(うれ)しいです。
  Your own joy is not something you infer. Use a plain intensifier for your own
  feelings.

- ❌ さぞ電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れるでしょう。 → ✅ <b>きっと</b>電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れるでしょう。
  A prediction about a fact takes <code>きっと</code> or <code>たぶん</code>. `さぞ`
  needs a person and a feeling.

## Practice

**1.** 夜中(よなか)まで看病(かんびょう)されていたそうですね。____お疲(つか)れになったことでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>さぞ</b>お疲(つか)れになったことでしょう。

Someone else's hardship, a polite honorific verb and a
<code>ことでしょう</code> ending: this is the sentence pattern `さぞ` was made for.
The speaker was not there and is imagining it, which is precisely what the adverb
signals.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: さぞ明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)くなるでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ <b>きっと</b>明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)くなるでしょう。

The conjecture ending is there, but the content is a scheduling prediction, not
somebody's inner state. `さぞ` would only work if the sentence turned to a person -
for instance <code>さぞお疲(つか)れになるでしょう</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, using さぞ:
"You must have been terribly worried while you were waiting for the results."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

結果(けっか)を待(ま)っている間(あいだ)、<b>さぞ</b>ご心配(しんぱい)だったことでしょう。

The English "must have been" maps onto the whole frame, not onto a single word: the
adverb opens the guess and <code>だったことでしょう</code> closes it. The honorific
<code>ご心配(しんぱい)</code> is what makes it sound like genuine sympathy rather
than commentary.

</details>

# さも

**Meaning** · with every appearance of; just as though - describing how somebody
looked while doing something, often with a hint that it was a performance

`さも` never tells you what a person felt. It tells you what they looked like they
felt, as seen by someone watching them - and the watcher is usually not entirely
convinced.

## Usage

Like まるで, `さも` cannot stand on its own.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さも` must be answered by an appearance marker:
<code>さも～そうに／そうな</code>, <code>さも～ように／ような</code>, or
<code>さも～かのように</code>.

</div>

The three frames in order of frequency:

- <code>さも楽(たの)しそうに笑(わら)う</code> - the commonest, "laughing as if
  he were having a wonderful time"
- <code>さも当然(とうぜん)のように言(い)う</code> - "saying it as though it went
  without saying"
- <code>さも見(み)てきたかのように話(はな)す</code> - "talking as though he had
  been there to see it"

Almost always it modifies a **manner**: how the person laughed, said it, nodded,
handed something over. That is why the ending is so often
<code>そうに</code> or <code>ように</code> rather than <code>そうだ</code>.

One trap. <code>さもないと</code> and <code>さもなければ</code>, meaning "otherwise,
or else", are a different word - <code>急(いそ)ごう。さもないと遅(おく)れる</code>.
Same three sounds, no connection.

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>さも</b>おいしそうにビールを飲(の)んだ。
He drank his beer with every appearance of enjoying it.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)は<b>さも</b>得意(とくい)そうな顔(かお)で絵(え)を見(み)せに来(き)た。
The child brought the picture over to show me, looking terribly pleased with himself.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>さも</b>当然(とうぜん)のように、私(わたし)の机(つくえ)から資料(しりょう)を持(も)っていった。
She took the documents off my desk as though it were the most natural thing in the world.
</pre>

<pre>
その男(おとこ)は<b>さも</b>現場(げんば)を見(み)てきたかのように、事故(じこ)の様子(ようす)を語(かた)り続(つづ)けた。
The man went on describing the accident as if he had been standing there watching it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

A writer's word. `さも` belongs to fiction, essays and reported description; it is
the narrator standing back and reading a face. In conversation it is rare enough
that using it will make you sound like a novel, and the everyday alternatives are
<code>いかにも～そうに</code>, <code>本当(ほんとう)に～そうに</code>, or simply
<code>～そうに</code> with no adverb at all.

The scepticism is the part learners miss. <code>さも</code> reports a display, and
in most contexts the display is being put on: the speaker is telling you that the
person *acted* delighted, *acted* as though they knew. It is not a rule -
<code>さも嬉(うれ)しそうに</code> can describe genuine delight - but the suspicion
is close enough to the surface that describing your own boss this way is not
neutral.

Because of that, `さも` is a good tool for characterisation and a bad one for
compliments. If you mean somebody really did look happy, and you want no
implication of theatre, drop the adverb:
<code>嬉(うれ)しそうに笑(わら)った</code> says it cleanly.

## Contrast

### さも vs いかにも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>いかにも</code> endorses an appearance: that is exactly
what such a thing looks like. `さも` reports a display and often doubts it: he made
a great show of it.

</div>

<pre>
<b>いかにも</b>高(たか)そうな時計(とけい)をしていた。
He was wearing a watch that looked expensive, just as you'd imagine.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>さも</b>高(たか)そうに時計(とけい)を見(み)せびらかしていた。
He was showing off the watch as though to say how expensive it was.
</pre>

The first is about the watch and how well it fits the type. The second is about the
man and the performance he is giving. Note too that
<code>いかにも</code> is comfortable in speech, while `さも` is not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はさも嬉(うれ)しかった。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はさも嬉(うれ)し<b>そうだった</b>。
  The appearance marker is compulsory. `さも` describes the look on a face, so it
  cannot attach to a plain statement about the feeling itself.

- ❌ さも雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうだ。 → ✅ <b>今(いま)にも</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうだ。
  `さも` reads people, not skies. For "it looks about to rain", use
  <code>今(いま)にも</code>.

- ❌ 急(いそ)ごう、さも遅(おく)れる。 → ✅ 急(いそ)ごう、<b>さもないと</b>遅(おく)れる。
  "Or else" is <code>さもないと</code> or <code>さもなければ</code>, a separate
  expression. Bare `さも` cannot carry that meaning.

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)は一言(ひとこと)も謝(あやま)らず、____悪(わる)いのはこちらだと____顔(かお)をしていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は一言(ひとこと)も謝(あやま)らず、<b>さも</b>悪(わる)いのはこちらだと<b>いう</b>顔(かお)をしていた。

The frame here is <code>さも～という顔(かお)をする</code>, a very common way of
reporting an expression. The adverb sets up the appearance and
<code>という顔(かお)</code> delivers it; without the second half the sentence has
nothing to land on.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)はさも自信(じしん)がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)はさも自信(じしん)が<b>ありそうに話(はな)した</b>。

Two repairs, both required by the same rule. The appearance marker
<code>そうに</code> has to appear, and because `さも` describes a manner, the
sentence wants a verb of doing - speaking, laughing, nodding - for the manner to
attach to.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)はさも感動(かんどう)したように話(はな)した。
(b) 彼(かれ)は感動(かんどう)して話(はな)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (b) states as fact that he was moved, and the speaker takes that at face
value. Sentence (a) reports only the outward show, and the adverb leans toward
disbelief: he spoke in the manner of a man who had been moved, and the writer is
inviting you to wonder whether he really was.

</details>

# いかにも

**Meaning** · exactly as you would expect; the very picture of

`いかにも` says that something matches the mental image of its type so closely that
it is almost a caricature. A shop that is *so* Showa. An excuse that is *so* him.
The word carries that "so" - and the small smile that comes with it.

## Usage

`いかにも` is an adverb and comes first in the phrase it colours. It is nearly
always followed by a marker of resemblance or appearance:

- <code>いかにも～らしい</code>: <code>いかにも彼(かれ)らしい答(こた)え</code>
- <code>いかにも～そうだ／そうな</code>: <code>いかにも高(たか)そうな車(くるま)</code>
- <code>いかにも～という／といった</code>: <code>いかにも職人(しょくにん)という手(て)</code>
- directly before a descriptive noun phrase:
  <code>いかにも日本(にほん)的(てき)な風景(ふうけい)</code>

The judgement is about **type membership**, not about intensity. That is why
❌<code>いかにもおいしい</code> is wrong where
<code>いかにもおいしそうだ</code> is right: the word comments on how something
presents itself, not on how it actually is.

There is a second, archaic use in which `いかにも` stands alone meaning "indeed,
quite so" - the reply of a retainer in a period drama. Recognise it; do not deploy
it. In a meeting the modern equivalent is
<code>おっしゃるとおりです</code>.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>いかにも</b>高(たか)そうなかばんですね。
That bag certainly looks expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)をすっかり忘(わす)れていたなんて、<b>いかにも</b>彼(かれ)らしい。
Completely forgetting the appointment - that's him all over.
</pre>

<pre>
古(ふる)い木(き)の看板(かんばん)が出(で)ていて、<b>いかにも</b>昭和(しょうわ)という感(かん)じの喫茶店(きっさてん)だった。
With its old wooden sign, it was a coffee shop that felt exactly like something out of the Showa era.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)は<b>いかにも</b>緊張(きんちょう)した様子(ようす)で、名刺(めいし)を逆(さか)さまに差(さ)し出(だ)した。
Looking every bit as nervous as you would expect, the new employee held out his business card upside down.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Usable in speech, unlike most of the adverbs around it in this chapter. It is
mildly evaluative and a shade bookish, but nothing about
<code>いかにも高(たか)そう</code> or <code>いかにも彼(かれ)らしい</code> would
raise an eyebrow in conversation. You will meet it constantly in reviews, travel
writing and character description.

The edge to watch is the caricature. Saying
<code>いかにも先生(せんせい)らしい人(ひと)</code> can be affectionate - "every
inch the teacher" - or dismissive - "a schoolteacher type" - and which one lands
depends on tone and context. Applied to a person's clothes, accent or manner it can
easily read as mockery, so it is safer aimed at things and scenes than at people
you have just met.

Note also that `いかにも` frequently means the appearance is *only* an appearance,
especially with <code>そうな</code>: <code>いかにも強(つよ)そうな犬(いぬ)</code>
is a dog that looks tough, with no promise that it is. That reading is one small
step from the doubting <code>さも</code>, and the two words meet there.

## Contrast

### いかにも vs さも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いかにも` says the thing is a perfect specimen of its type.
<code>さも</code> says the person put on a display. `いかにも` describes; `さも`
suspects.

</div>

<pre>
<b>いかにも</b>真面目(まじめ)そうな学生(がくせい)が前(まえ)の席(せき)に座(すわ)った。
A student who looked the very picture of diligence sat down in the front row.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>さも</b>真面目(まじめ)そうに話(はな)を聞(き)いていた。
He listened with every show of taking it seriously.
</pre>

The first sentence is about a type and how well the student fits it; the second is
about a performance being given to an audience. `いかにも` also modifies nouns
freely, while <code>さも</code> wants a manner and a verb -
<code>さも真面目(まじめ)そうな学生(がくせい)</code> is possible but reads as an
accusation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この料理(りょうり)はいかにもおいしい。 → ✅ この料理(りょうり)はいかにもおいし<b>そうだ</b>。
  The adverb comments on how something looks or seems. To say the food really is
  delicious, use <code>本当(ほんとう)に</code>.

- ❌ 「いかにも。」（agreeing in a meeting）→ ✅ <b>おっしゃるとおりです。</b>
  The standalone "indeed" is period-drama Japanese. In a modern office it will get
  a laugh, which may not be the one you wanted.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はいかにも平気(へいき)なふりをして嘘(うそ)をついた。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は<b>さも</b>平気(へいき)なように嘘(うそ)をついた。
  When the point is that somebody is faking a manner, the word is
  <code>さも</code>. `いかにも` would put the emphasis on how convincingly typical
  he looked, which is not the accusation intended.

## Practice

**1.** 窓(まど)から海(うみ)が見(み)えて、____夏(なつ)の旅行(りょこう)____部屋(へや)だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

窓(まど)から海(うみ)が見(み)えて、<b>いかにも</b>夏(なつ)の旅行(りょこう)<b>らしい</b>部屋(へや)だった。

The two blanks are one frame: the adverb opens the "perfect specimen" judgement and
<code>らしい</code> attaches it to the noun. Without the ending the sentence has no
resemblance marker for `いかにも` to strengthen.

</details>

**2.** Choose いかにも or さも, and say why:
彼(かれ)は____自分(じぶん)が考(かんが)えたかのように、私(わたし)の案(あん)を説明(せつめい)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>さも</b>自分(じぶん)が考(かんが)えたかのように説明(せつめい)した。

The sentence accuses him of passing off someone else's idea, and that reading of a
put-on display is <code>さも</code>'s job. The
<code>かのように</code> ending is another signal: it is the frame
<code>さも</code> keeps company with.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) いかにも元気(げんき)そうな子(こ)だ。
(b) 元気(げんき)な子(こ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (b) simply states that the child is lively. Sentence (a) reports the
impression the child gives and measures it against a type - this is what a healthy,
lively child looks like - and it leaves a small gap between the look and the fact,
which is why it can be said of a child you have only just glimpsed.

</details>

# てっきり

**Meaning** · I was sure that ... - and I was wrong

`てっきり` is a confession. It reports a belief the speaker held with complete
confidence, and it only exists because that belief turned out to be false. There is
no version of this word in which you were right.

## Usage

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てっきり` needs a **past-tense verb of believing** -
<code>と思(おも)っていた</code>, <code>と思(おも)い込(こ)んでいた</code>,
<code>とばかり思(おも)っていた</code> - and the sentence or the context must
reveal the correction.

</div>

The standard shapes:

- <code>てっきり X だと思(おも)っていた</code>
- <code>てっきり X だと思(おも)い込(こ)んでいた</code> - stronger; you had
  talked yourself into it
- <code>てっきり X だとばかり思(おも)っていた</code> - the most emphatic of the
  three
- <code>てっきり X だと思(おも)っていたが、実(じつ)は Y だった</code> - the full
  two-part sentence, with the correction spelled out

Because it lives in hindsight, the present and the future are closed to it:
❌<code>てっきり明日(あした)だと思(おも)う</code>. And because the belief has to
have been wrong, it cannot report an expectation that came true - that is
<code>やっぱり</code> in speech, <code>案(あん)の定(じょう)</code> in writing.

The belief is normally your own. You can report someone else's
（<code>彼(かれ)はてっきり中止(ちゅうし)になったと思(おも)っていたらしい</code>）,
but the first person is the home use.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>てっきり</b>今日(きょう)が締(し)め切(き)りだと思(おも)っていました。
I was quite sure the deadline was today.
</pre>

<pre>
電気(でんき)が消(き)えていたので、<b>てっきり</b>留守(るす)だと思(おも)った。
The lights were off, so I assumed nobody was home.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が来(こ)ないから、<b>てっきり</b>断(ことわ)られたものだと思(おも)い込(こ)んでいた。
There was no reply, so I'd convinced myself I had been turned down.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、会議(かいぎ)は<b>てっきり</b>来週(らいしゅう)に延(の)びたものとばかり思(おも)っておりました。
I'm terribly sorry - I was quite certain the meeting had been pushed back to next week.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Ordinary spoken Japanese, and one of the genuinely conversational words in this
group. It is neutral for politeness: it sits happily in casual speech
（<code>てっきり明日(あした)だと思(おも)ってた</code>）and in humble business
Japanese （<code>てっきり…と思(おも)っておりました</code>）, because the adverb
itself carries no formality - the verb ending does all that work.

Its most useful home is the apology. Explaining a mix-up, `てっきり` frames what
happened as an honest misunderstanding rather than carelessness, which is exactly
what you want when you have turned up on the wrong day. Note how naturally it pairs
with <code>すみません</code>.

There is a faint self-deprecating warmth to it. You are admitting you were
confidently wrong, and Japanese speakers use it lightly, often with a laugh. It
does not work as an accusation: `てっきり` about someone else's mistake sounds odd
unless you are reporting their thinking sympathetically.

## Contrast

### てっきり vs やっぱり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both start from an expectation you already had.
<code>やっぱり</code> says it was borne out; `てっきり` says it was not.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうだと思(おも)っていたら、<b>やっぱり</b>降(ふ)ってきた。
I thought it looked like rain, and sure enough it started.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>てっきり</b>雨(あめ)になると思(おも)っていたのに、一日(いちにち)晴(は)れだった。
I was sure it was going to rain, and it stayed sunny all day.
</pre>

The two are mirror images, and the second half of the sentence is what tells them
apart: <code>やっぱり</code> is followed by the thing happening, `てっきり` by the
thing not happening. In writing, the confirming half is often
<code>案(あん)の定(じょう)</code> rather than
<code>やっぱり</code>; there is no written cousin for `てっきり`, which stays
conversational.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ てっきり明日(あした)が試験(しけん)だと思(おも)います。 → ✅ てっきり明日(あした)が試験(しけん)だと<b>思(おも)っていました</b>。
  The verb has to be in the past, and in the continuous
  <code>思(おも)っていた</code> at that: the belief was held over a stretch of
  time and has since been corrected.

- ❌ 天気予報(てんきよほう)のとおり、てっきり雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)った。 → ✅ 天気予報(てんきよほう)のとおり、<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)った。
  Here the expectation came true, so `てっきり` is the wrong half of the pair.

- ❌ てっきり彼(かれ)は来(こ)ないだろう。 → ✅ <b>たぶん</b>彼(かれ)は来(こ)ないだろう。
  A fresh guess about the future is <code>たぶん</code> or <code>きっと</code>.
  `てっきり` can only look backwards at a belief you have already abandoned.

## Practice

**1.** 田中(たなか)さんが白衣(はくい)を着(き)ていたので、____医者(いしゃ)だと思(おも)っていました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>てっきり</b>医者(いしゃ)だと思(おも)っていました。

The sentence gives the evidence that misled the speaker - the white coat - and the
past continuous <code>思(おも)っていました</code> signals that the belief has
since been overturned. Both are the standard scaffolding for this adverb.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: てっきり彼女(かのじょ)が来(く)ると思(おも)っていて、本当(ほんとう)に来(き)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ <b>やっぱり</b>彼女(かのじょ)は来(き)ました。／思(おも)ったとおり彼女(かのじょ)は来(き)ました。

`てっきり` requires the belief to have been mistaken, so a sentence in which she did
turn up contradicts the adverb. When the expectation holds, the word you want is
<code>やっぱり</code> or <code>思(おも)ったとおり</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) てっきり中止(ちゅうし)になったと思(おも)っていました。
(b) 中止(ちゅうし)になったと思(おも)っていました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both report the same past belief, but (a) adds two things. It says the speaker held
the belief with no doubt at all, and it announces in advance that the belief was
wrong - so the listener already knows an apology or a correction is coming.
Sentence (b) is neutral and could still turn out to be right.

</details>

# 案の定

**Meaning** · sure enough; just as I was afraid it would be

案(あん)の定(じょう) reports that a private forecast has come true - and in
almost every sentence a Japanese speaker actually produces, the forecast was a
**bad** one. It is "I knew it", said with a sigh rather than with satisfaction.

## Usage

An adverb. It sits at the head of the clause, or straight after the topic, and the
clause it introduces reports a **finished** outcome, so the verb after it is normally
past tense.

The word is only half of the picture. <code>案(あん)の定(じょう)</code> is empty
unless the listener already knows what you expected, so it needs an expectation
sitting somewhere in front of it. In practice it arrives in one of three shapes:

- The expectation is spelled out first:
  <code>降(ふ)りそうだと思(おも)っていたら、案(あん)の定(じょう)降(ふ)ってきた。</code>
- Someone ignored a warning:
  <code>やめておけと言(い)ったのに、案(あん)の定(じょう)失敗(しっぱい)した。</code>
- The expectation is obvious to everyone present, so it is left unsaid:
  <code>案(あん)の定(じょう)、会場(かいじょう)はがらがらだった。</code>

The kanji spelling is the normal one and far commoner than kana. The `の` in the
middle belongs to the word and never drops, and nothing is ever added after
定(じょう) - no `に`, no `で`, no `だ`.

## Examples

<pre>
嫌(いや)な予感(よかん)がしたが、<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>電車(でんしゃ)は止(と)まっていた。
I had a bad feeling about it, and sure enough the trains had stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)い傘(かさ)を買(か)ったら、<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>一日(いちにち)で壊(こわ)れた。
I bought a cheap umbrella and, just as I thought, it broke within a day.
</pre>

<pre>
無理(むり)な日程(にってい)だと何度(なんど)も言(い)ったのに、<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>納期(のうき)に間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。
I told them over and over that the schedule was impossible, and sure enough we missed the deadline.
</pre>

<pre>
下調(したしら)べもせずに飛(と)び込(こ)みで訪問(ほうもん)した結果(けっか)、<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>担当者(たんとうしゃ)は席(せき)を外(はず)していた。
Having gone in cold with no preparation, we found - predictably enough - that the person in charge was away from her desk.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It is at home in conversation between adults, in a work email, in
a newspaper column and in narration, and it marks nothing about age or gender. It
has a faintly bookish ring next to やっぱり, which is why a spoken complaint often
uses やっぱり and the written-up version of the same complaint uses
案(あん)の定(じょう).

What is *not* neutral is the polarity. The outcome reported is nearly always
unwelcome: the train stopped, the umbrella broke, the deadline slipped. Native
speakers do occasionally stretch it to a good outcome, but it reads as a joke or as
boasting, and a learner is better off treating the negative bias as fixed. About
your own success it is especially unwise -
案(あん)の定(じょう)合格(ごうかく)した sounds smug in a way
思(おも)った通(とお)り合格(ごうかく)した does not.

The expectation being confirmed is normally the **speaker's own**, or one the speaker
and listener formed together. Reporting that a third party's prediction came true is
the job of <code>予想(よそう)通(どお)り</code>, not of this word.

It is worth keeping apart from てっきり, which a learner meets at the same level and
which does the opposite job: てっきり introduces an assumption that turned out to be
**wrong**. 案(あん)の定(じょう) is for the forecast that landed, てっきり for the
one that missed.

There is also a mild social edge to it. Aimed at another person's failure -
案(あん)の定(じょう)遅(おく)れてきたね - it says "you did exactly what I
expected of you", which is a reproach. Aimed at the weather, the trains or your own
bad luck it is harmless.

## Contrast

### 案(あん)の定(じょう) vs やはり・やっぱり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - やはり confirms *any* expectation, good or bad, and does several
other jobs besides. 案(あん)の定(じょう) does one job only: a bad forecast that
came true.

</div>

<pre>
<b>やっぱり</b>この店(みせ)が一番(いちばん)おいしいね。
This place really is the best after all.
</pre>

<pre>
混(こ)むと思(おも)っていたら、<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)たされた。
I thought it would be busy, and sure enough we were kept waiting an hour.
</pre>

The first sentence cannot take 案(あん)の定(じょう): nothing has gone wrong.
The second could take やっぱり with no loss of meaning, only a drop in formality -
whenever 案(あん)の定(じょう) works, やっぱり works too, and not the reverse.
やはり also covers "still, nevertheless" (高(たか)いが、やはり買(か)う) and the
change of mind of やっぱりやめる, neither of which
案(あん)の定(じょう) can touch.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>案(あん)の定(じょう)に</b>遅(おく)れてきた。 → ✅ <b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>遅(おく)れてきた。
  The word is already adverbial. Adding `に`, `で` or `な` to it is like writing
  "certainlyly".

- ❌ <b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>明日(あした)は混(こ)むだろう。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は案(あん)の定(じょう)混(こ)んでいた。
  It reports a result you can already see. A prediction about tomorrow has not come
  true yet, so there is nothing for it to confirm.

- ❌ 宝(たから)くじが<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>当(あ)たった。 → ✅ 宝(たから)くじが思(おも)った通(とお)り当(あ)たった。
  Good news breaks the negative bias. Use
  <code>思(おも)った通(とお)り</code> or やっぱり instead.

- ❌ いきなり<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>失敗(しっぱい)しました。 → ✅ 危(あぶ)ないと思(おも)っていたのですが、<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>失敗(しっぱい)しました。
  Dropped into a conversation with no expectation behind it, the word has nothing to
  point back at and the listener is left asking "expected by whom?".

## Practice

**1.** 準備(じゅんび)が足(た)りないと感(かん)じていたが、____本番(ほんばん)で失敗(しっぱい)してしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>本番(ほんばん)で失敗(しっぱい)してしまった

The first clause supplies the misgiving, the second reports the bad outcome in the
past tense. That is exactly the frame the word needs, and nothing attaches to
定(じょう).

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 案(あん)の定(じょう)に今度(こんど)の試合(しあい)も負(ま)けるでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 案(あん)の定(じょう)、今度(こんど)の試合(しあい)も負(ま)けてしまった。

Two problems. The `に` after 定(じょう) does not belong there, and でしょう makes
this a forecast about a match not yet played. The word can only confirm a result
that has already arrived.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) <b>やっぱり</b>彼(かれ)が選(えら)ばれたね。
(b) <b>案(あん)の定(じょう)</b>彼(かれ)が選(えら)ばれたね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is neutral. It says the outcome matched what everyone assumed, and it can be
warm - he deserved it, and we all saw it coming.

(b) sours it. Because 案(あん)の定(じょう) carries an expectation of something
going wrong, using it here implies the selection was a foregone conclusion for the
wrong reasons - connections, favouritism, laziness on the committee's part. Said out
loud in front of him, (b) is an insult and (a) is not.

</details>

# あらかじめ

**Meaning** · beforehand; in advance; ahead of time

`あらかじめ` marks an action as done **ahead of a known later point** - before the
meeting, before the deadline, before the customer arrives. It is the formal, written
member of a small family of words that all mean "in advance", and it is the one you
will meet on notices and in contracts rather than in conversation.

## Usage

An adverb, and only an adverb. It stands in front of the verb it modifies, usually at
the head of the clause, and nothing attaches to it - no `に`, no `の`, no `で`.

The verb has to be something **someone does on purpose**, in preparation. A thing
that merely happens early cannot be done `あらかじめ`.

Two collocations carry most of its real use, and both are worth learning whole.

- <code>あらかじめ ～ておく</code>. The adverb announces the preparation and
  <code>～ておく</code> supplies it: あらかじめ調(しら)べておく,
  あらかじめ伝(つた)えておく, あらかじめ買(か)っておく. If you are unsure whether
  a sentence needs `ておく`, it almost always does.
- <code>あらかじめ ～られた/～ている</code> before a noun, meaning "pre-set,
  pre-arranged": あらかじめ決(き)められた時間(じかん),
  あらかじめ用意(ようい)された資料(しりょう),
  あらかじめ定(さだ)められた手順(てじゅん). This shape is everywhere in written
  procedure.

One fixed phrase accounts for a large share of the sightings:
<code>あらかじめご了承(りょうしょう)ください</code>, "we ask for your understanding
in advance", the standard closing of an announcement about something inconvenient.

The kanji spelling <code>予(あらかじ)め</code> exists, but modern print and public
notices overwhelmingly use kana, and you should too.

## Examples

<pre>
参加(さんか)する人(ひと)の数(かず)を<b>あらかじめ</b>教(おし)えてください。
Please tell me the number of people attending in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)の場所(ばしょ)は<b>あらかじめ</b>調(しら)べておきました。
I looked up where the venue is beforehand.
</pre>

<pre>
当日(とうじつ)は混雑(こんざつ)が予想(よそう)されますので、<b>あらかじめ</b>ご了承(りょうしょう)ください。
Heavy crowds are expected on the day, and we ask for your understanding in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)に不備(ふび)があると受(う)け付(つ)けられませんので、記入例(きにゅうれい)を<b>あらかじめ</b>ご確認(かくにん)のうえ、ご提出(ていしゅつ)ください。
Incomplete application forms cannot be accepted, so please check the sample entry beforehand and then submit yours.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written and formal** word. The places you will genuinely meet it are
notices in stations and shops, terms and conditions, user manuals, application
guidance, and business email. In those places it is completely ordinary and
extremely frequent.

In speech it is not wrong, but it is heavy. Between friends, "I'll tell you in
advance" is 先(さき)に言(い)っておくね, not
あらかじめ言(い)っておくね; the second sounds like you are quoting the terms of
service at someone. Where a colleague might use it out loud is in a slightly formal
setting - explaining a procedure at a meeting, briefing a client - and even there
事前(じぜん)に is commoner.

It carries no politeness of its own. あらかじめ言(い)っとけよ is perfectly
possible and perfectly rude; the politeness in
あらかじめご了承(りょうしょう)ください comes from ご～ください, not from the adverb.

One quiet implication is worth knowing. Because `あらかじめ` frames an action as
prudent preparation, putting it in a request makes the request sound like a
procedural requirement rather than a favour. あらかじめご連絡(れんらく)ください
reads as a rule of the establishment; 先(さき)にご連絡(れんらく)いただけますか
reads as a person asking.

## Contrast

### あらかじめ vs 前(まえ)もって vs 事前(じぜん)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three mean "in advance". `あらかじめ` is the written one,
<code>前(まえ)もって</code> is the spoken one, and
<code>事前(じぜん)に</code> is the only one that is really a noun underneath, so
it is the only one that modifies a noun comfortably.

</div>

<pre>
遅(おく)れそうなときは<b>前(まえ)もって</b>連絡(れんらく)してね。
If you think you'll be late, let me know ahead of time.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)の日程(にってい)は<b>あらかじめ</b>掲示(けいじ)いたします。
The construction schedule will be posted in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
見学(けんがく)には<b>事前(じぜん)の</b>予約(よやく)が必要(ひつよう)です。
A prior booking is required in order to visit.
</pre>

Swap the adverbs in the first two sentences and nothing changes in the meaning -
only the temperature. The third sentence is the one that cannot be swapped:
事前(じぜん) is a noun, so <code>事前(じぜん)の予約(よやく)</code>,
<code>事前(じぜん)審査(しんさ)</code> and
<code>事前(じぜん)に</code> are all available, while
あらかじめの予約(よやく) is at best stiff and usually simply avoided.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>あらかじめの</b>連絡(れんらく)をお願(ねが)いします。 → ✅ <b>事前(じぜん)の</b>連絡(れんらく)をお願(ねが)いします。
  `あらかじめ` modifies verbs, not nouns. When you need a noun modifier, reach for
  <code>事前(じぜん)の</code>.

- ❌ 電車(でんしゃ)が<b>あらかじめ</b>着(つ)きました。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)が予定(よてい)より早(はや)く着(つ)きました。
  The train did not arrive as an act of preparation; it simply arrived early. `あらかじめ`
  needs a deliberate act aimed at something later.

- ❌ ちょっと<b>あらかじめ</b>言(い)っとくけど、明日(あした)遅(おく)れるわ。 → ✅ ちょっと<b>先(さき)に</b>言(い)っとくけど、明日(あした)遅(おく)れるわ。
  The clash is one of register, not grammar: ちょっと and 言(い)っとく are relaxed
  speech, and `あらかじめ` belongs to printed notices.

- ❌ <b>あらかじめ</b>質問(しつもん)を考(かんが)えます。 → ✅ <b>あらかじめ</b>質問(しつもん)を考(かんが)えておきます。
  Not ungrammatical, but incomplete-sounding. Without <code>～ておく</code> the
  sentence states an intention and leaves out the preparation the adverb has just
  promised.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)に備(そな)えて、水(みず)と食料(しょくりょう)を____買(か)っておいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>あらかじめ</b>買(か)っておいた

The typhoon is the later point the shopping is aimed at, and
<code>～ておく</code> is already in the sentence waiting for it. Note that
前(まえ)もって would also be correct here and would sound more conversational.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 工場(こうじょう)の見学(けんがく)には（あらかじめ・事前(じぜん)の）申(もう)し込(こ)みが必要(ひつよう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>事前(じぜん)の</b>申(もう)し込(こ)み

申(もう)し込(こ)み is a noun, and only 事前(じぜん) can modify it, because
only 事前(じぜん) is itself a noun. To use `あらかじめ` you would have to rebuild
the sentence around a verb: あらかじめ申(もう)し込(こ)んでおく必要(ひつよう)が
あります。

</details>

**3.** Where would you expect to read this sentence, and how would a friend say the same thing to you?
一部(いちぶ)の商品(しょうひん)は品切(しなぎ)れとなる場合(ばあい)がございます。<b>あらかじめ</b>ご了承(りょうしょう)ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

On a printed notice or a shop's website - a sign by the door, a line at the bottom of
a leaflet, the small print of an online order page. The combination of ございます,
ご了承(りょうしょう)ください and `あらかじめ` is the standard formula for warning
customers about an inconvenience.

A friend would not use any of it. Spoken, it collapses to something like
「売(う)り切(き)れてるかもしれないから、先(さき)に言(い)っとくね」 - same
information, none of the notice-board register.

</details>

# かつて

**Meaning** · once; formerly - and, with a negative, never yet

`かつて` places something in a past that is **finished**. It does not simply say
"earlier"; it says "there was a time when this was so, and that time is over". In the
negative it flips into "not once in all the time up to now", which is where the fixed
phrase かつてない, "unprecedented", comes from.

## Usage

An adverb of the written language. It normally stands early in the clause, and the
clause it governs is in the **past**. Three shapes cover nearly everything.

- **Plain past.** <code>かつて ～た / ～ていた</code>: この町(まち)はかつて
  城下町(じょうかまち)だった. The contrast with the present is built in, and it is
  often made explicit with `は`: <code>かつては～が、今(いま)は～</code>.
- **Before a noun, with `の`.** <code>かつての</code> = "former, one-time":
  かつての同僚(どうりょう), かつての栄光(えいこう), かつての自分(じぶん).
- **With a negative.** <code>かつて～ない</code> = "never yet". The commonest form by
  far is the compressed <code>かつてない</code> before a noun -
  かつてない規模(きぼ), かつてない経験(けいけん) - and the emphatic
  <code>未(いま)だかつて～ない</code> in longer sentences.

The past it points at is an **indefinite era**, not a dated occasion. That is why a
precise time word beside it jars: かつて三年前(さんねんまえ)に is wrong in the way
that "once, three years ago" is odd in English. It is also happiest with states and
long situations - 住(す)んでいた, 栄(さか)えていた, ～たことがある - rather than
with a single instant.

The kanji spellings <code>嘗(かつ)て</code> and <code>曽(かつ)て</code> exist and
you may meet them in older books, but modern text uses kana.

## Examples

<pre>
この辺(あた)りは<b>かつて</b>田(た)んぼだった。
This area was once rice paddies.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>かつて</b>の同僚(どうりょう)で、今(いま)は大学(だいがく)で教(おし)えている。
She is a former colleague of mine, and now she teaches at a university.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は<b>かつて</b>ない暑(あつ)さになるおそれがある。
There is a risk that this summer will be hotter than any before it.
</pre>

<pre>
港町(みなとまち)として<b>かつて</b>は栄(さか)えていたが、鉄道(てつどう)が通(とお)ってからは人(ひと)の流(なが)れがすっかり変(か)わってしまった。
It flourished in its day as a port town, but once the railway came through the flow of people changed completely.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`かつて` is a **written** word with a slightly elevated, retrospective tone. Where you
will meet it: history writing and museum captions, newspaper features, company
histories, documentary narration, novels, and the prepared part of a speech
("私(わたし)もかつてこの教室(きょうしつ)で学(まな)びました"). It suits
subjects with some weight - a town, an industry, a person's earlier life - and sounds
inflated over trivia.

Spoken Japanese does the same job with 昔(むかし) and 以前(いぜん), or with
nothing at all: 前(まえ)はよく行(い)ってたんだけどね. A learner who says
かつてラーメンが好(す)きでした to a friend has produced a grammatical sentence that
sounds like a voice-over.

The one part of the word that *is* alive in speech is the negative. かつてない has
escaped into ordinary use - sports commentary, advertising copy,
かつてない規模(きぼ)の災害(さいがい) in a news bulletin - and no one hears it as
literary. The full 未(いま)だかつて form stays formal.

There is also a faint note of loss about it. Because it frames the past as closed,
`かつて` tends to attach to things that have declined, ended or changed beyond
recognition. It is not a rule, but it is a strong tendency, and it is why
かつての自分(じぶん) usually introduces someone the speaker no longer is.

## Contrast

### かつて vs 昔(むかし) vs 以前(いぜん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>以前(いぜん)</code> = at some earlier point, possibly last
week. <code>昔(むかし)</code> = long ago, spoken, warm. `かつて` = in a former era
that is now over, written.

</div>

<pre>
<b>以前(いぜん)</b>この店(みせ)に来(き)たことがあります。
I've been to this shop before.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>昔(むかし)</b>はこの川(かわ)でよく泳(およ)いだものだ。
We used to swim in this river a lot in the old days.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>かつて</b>この島(しま)には三千人(さんぜんにん)が暮(く)らしていた。
Three thousand people once lived on this island.
</pre>

The first sentence cannot take `かつて`: a previous visit to a shop is not an era.
The second could take it, but the loss of 昔(むかし) would take the warmth with it -
昔(むかし) invites the listener into a shared memory, `かつて` reports from a
distance. The third is `かつて` territory precisely because the island's populated
period is over, and swapping in 昔(むかし) would make a documentary sentence sound
like chat.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>かつて</b>先週(せんしゅう)田中(たなか)さんに会(あ)いました。 → ✅ <b>以前(いぜん)</b>田中(たなか)さんに会(あ)いました。
  A dated, recent occasion is exactly what `かつて` cannot describe. It needs an
  indefinite former period.

- ❌ 父(ちち)は<b>かつて</b>銀行(ぎんこう)に勤(つと)めています。 → ✅ 父(ちち)は<b>かつて</b>銀行(ぎんこう)に勤(つと)めていた。
  The clause must be past. `かつて` says the situation has ended, so a present-tense
  verb contradicts it in the same sentence.

- ❌ 今(いま)まで<b>かつて</b>こんなに驚(おどろ)いた。 → ✅ 今(いま)まで<b>かつて</b>こんなに驚(おどろ)いたことはない。
  In this frame `かつて` is half of a negative pair and cannot be left hanging. Finish
  it with ない.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)のライブ、<b>かつて</b>楽(たの)しかったよ。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)のライブ、今(いま)までで一番(いちばん)楽(たの)しかったよ。
  Two faults at once: casual register, and `かつて` being read as "extremely" rather
  than "formerly". It is never an intensifier.

## Practice

**1.** この工場(こうじょう)は____、町(まち)で最(もっと)も大(おお)きな職場(しょくば)だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この工場(こうじょう)は<b>かつて</b>、町(まち)で最(もっと)も大(おお)きな職場(しょくば)だった。

An indefinite former period, a past-tense clause, and an implied "not any more" - the
three things the word wants. Adding `は` after it,
<code>かつては</code>, would sharpen the contrast with the present still further.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 去年(きょねん)（かつて・以前(いぜん)）お送(おく)りした資料(しりょう)をご覧(らん)ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

去年(きょねん)<b>以前(いぜん)</b>お送(おく)りした資料(しりょう)

Only 以前(いぜん) can sit beside a dated, recent occasion such as
去年(きょねん). `かつて` would frame last year as a closed historical age, which is
absurd for a document you emailed, and it would clash with the business register of
お送(おく)りした and ご覧(らん)ください as well.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は<b>かつて</b>の名(めい)選手(せんしゅ)だ。
(b) 彼(かれ)は昔(むかし)の名(めい)選手(せんしゅ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the natural sentence, and it says two things at once: he was a great player,
and that is over. Depending on the tone it can be respectful (a legend) or unkind (he
is finished), but the "no longer" is always audible.

(b) is odd in a way worth noticing. <code>昔(むかし)の</code> before a noun tends to
mean "of the old days" as a type - 昔(むかし)の建物(たてもの), an old-style
building - so (b) drifts towards "a great player of a bygone era" rather than "a man
who used to be a great player". For a person's own former status, `かつての` is the
phrase.

</details>

# もはや

**Meaning** · by now; at this point; (with a negative) no longer

`もはや` marks a **threshold that has been crossed**. Something has changed so far
that the old description has stopped fitting: it is too late, it cannot be undone, or
the thing has simply become something else. English reaches for "by now", "at this
point" or "no longer" depending on which side of the change is in view.

## Usage

An adverb. It stands in front of the predicate, and it works in two frames.

**Negative - "no longer, not any more."** This is the commoner one, and the collocations
are fixed enough to learn as units:
もはや手(て)遅(おく)れだ,
もはや疑(うたが)いの余地(よち)はない,
もはや後戻(あともど)りはできない,
もはや議論(ぎろん)の余地(よち)はない,
もはや<code>～とは言(い)えない</code>. The negative may be a plain ない, a
できない, or a noun of impossibility.

**Affirmative - "at this point it is simply X."** Here `もはや` sits in front of a noun
or in front of a claim about what category the thing now belongs to: もはや別人(べつじん)だ, もはや芸術(げいじゅつ)だ,
もはや趣味(しゅみ)の域(いき)を超(こ)えている,
もはや常識(じょうしき)と言(い)っていい. The claim is always that the thing has
outgrown its previous label.

What `もはや` is *not* is a word for elapsed time. It never means "soon", it never
means "another one", and it does not ask whether a task is finished. Those are all
`もう`, and the two are not interchangeable in either direction.

The kanji spelling <code>最早(もはや)</code> exists and turns up in older prose; kana
is standard now.

## Examples

<pre>
ここまで来(き)たら、<b>もはや</b>引(ひ)き返(かえ)せない。
Having come this far, we can no longer turn back.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の料理(りょうり)は<b>もはや</b>プロの腕前(うでまえ)だ。
His cooking is at this point the work of a professional.
</pre>

<pre>
失(うしな)われた時間(じかん)は、<b>もはや</b>取(と)り戻(もど)しようがない。
The time that has been lost can no longer be recovered.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は一(いち)企業(きぎょう)の努力(どりょく)で解決(かいけつ)できる段階(だんかい)を超(こ)えており、<b>もはや</b>社会(しゃかい)全体(ぜんたい)で取(と)り組(く)むほかない。
This has passed the stage where one company's efforts could solve it; by now there is no choice but to tackle it as a whole society.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`もはや` belongs to **written and formal Japanese**: editorials, essays, reports,
speeches, novels and news copy. It also carries emphasis and a certain amount of
feeling - resignation in the negative frame, mild astonishment in the affirmative one -
which is why it is the word an editorial reaches for when `もう` would be too flat.

In ordinary conversation the neutral choice is `もう`. もう間(ま)に合(あ)わない
is what a colleague says; もはや間(ま)に合(あ)わない is what the write-up of the
incident says. Using `もはや` in relaxed speech is not an error, but it is audibly
dramatic, and Japanese speakers know it.

That drama is exactly what has made the affirmative frame popular in casual writing
online and among younger speakers: 「これもはやスープじゃなくてソースだよ」. The
overblown register is the joke, and the speaker means it to be heard. It is a real,
current usage - but it depends on the listener recognising the formality being played
with, so it is not a safe first move for a learner.

`もはや` has no politeness of its own and combines happily with ます/です:
もはや説明(せつめい)の必要(ひつよう)はございません. It marks nothing about the
speaker's age or gender.

## Contrast

### もはや vs もう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もう` is the everyday word and covers four jobs: already, no
longer, soon, and one more. `もはや` covers exactly one of them - **no longer / at
this point** - in a formal register, with a note of finality.

</div>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)は<b>もう</b>食(た)べましたか。
Have you had lunch already?
</pre>

<pre>
コーヒーを<b>もう</b>一杯(いっぱい)いただけますか。
Could I have one more coffee?
</pre>

<pre>
状況(じょうきょう)はここまで悪化(あっか)しており、<b>もはや</b>楽観(らっかん)はできない。
The situation has deteriorated to this point, and optimism is no longer possible.
</pre>

The first two sentences cannot take `もはや` at all - it has no "already?" and no
"one more". The third could take `もう`, and the sentence would still be correct; what
it would lose is the sense that a line has been crossed and the previous stance has
to be abandoned. That sense is the whole of what `もはや` adds.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ビールを<b>もはや</b>一本(いっぽん)ください。 → ✅ ビールを<b>もう</b>一本(いっぽん)ください。
  `もはや` has no "another, one more" sense. Only `もう` counts.

- ❌ 宿題(しゅくだい)は<b>もはや</b>終(お)わりましたか。 → ✅ 宿題(しゅくだい)は<b>もう</b>終(お)わりましたか。
  Asking whether something is done is `もう`. `もはや` asserts a state of affairs; it
  does not sit comfortably in a yes-no question about completion.

- ❌ <b>もはや</b>着(つ)きますから、待(ま)っていてください。 → ✅ <b>もうすぐ</b>着(つ)きますから、待(ま)っていてください。
  "Soon" is <code>もうすぐ</code>. `もはや` points backwards at a change already
  complete, never forwards.

- ❌ お皿(さら)、<b>もはや</b>洗(あら)っといたよ。 → ✅ お皿(さら)、<b>もう</b>洗(あら)っといたよ。
  Grammar aside, the register collapses: 洗(あら)っといた is relaxed speech and
  `もはや` is leader-column formality.

## Practice

**1.** 証拠(しょうこ)がこれだけそろった以上(いじょう)、____言(い)い逃(のが)れはできない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もはや</b>言(い)い逃(のが)れはできない

The first clause states that a point has been reached; the second says the old option
is closed. That threshold-crossed structure is exactly what `もはや` is for, and the
formal 以上(いじょう) clause matches its register.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この部屋(へや)、（もう・もはや）一人(ひとり)入(はい)れそうだね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もう</b>一人(ひとり)入(はい)れそうだね

<code>もう一人(ひとり)</code> means "one more person". `もはや` cannot express
"one more" at all, and the casual ～そうだね ending would clash with it in any case.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は<b>もう</b>子供(こども)ではない。
(b) 彼(かれ)は<b>もはや</b>子供(こども)ではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both mean "he is not a child any more", and both are correct.

(a) is an ordinary observation - he has grown up, so treat him accordingly. A parent
could say it at the dinner table.

(b) is a pronouncement. `もはや` frames the change as decisive and irreversible, and
its written register makes the sentence sound like a line from a novel or the turning
point of an argument: whatever we were assuming about him no longer holds. Said aloud
in a family conversation it would sound theatrical.

</details>

# とっさに

**Meaning** · in a split second; on reflex; without stopping to think

`とっさに` is not simply "quickly". It describes an action taken in the instant
something unexpected happens, **before there is time to decide** - swerving, grabbing,
blurting out an excuse. The absence of deliberation is the whole content of the word.

## Usage

An adverb, standing in front of the verb. The kanji spelling
<code>咄嗟(とっさ)に</code> exists but neither character is in everyday use, so kana
is normal.

Three things it needs:

- **A sudden trigger.** Something has to happen to you. A child runs into the road, a
  glass tips over, an interviewer asks a question you had not prepared for. Without
  that jolt, `とっさに` has nothing to be a reaction to.
- **Past tense.** It reports what someone did in a moment that has already passed. A
  plan to act quickly tomorrow is not `とっさに`.
- **An action, or the failure to produce one.** The negative is just as common as the
  positive: <code>とっさに返事(へんじ)ができなかった</code>,
  <code>とっさに言葉(ことば)が出(で)てこなかった</code> - the mind went blank in
  the second available.

Before a noun the `に` becomes `の`: <code>とっさの判断(はんだん)</code> (a
split-second decision), <code>とっさの出来事(できごと)</code>,
<code>とっさのこと</code>. It is a noun plus a particle, never a な-adjective.

Verbs that collocate with it are almost all bodily or verbal reflexes:
手(て)が出(で)る, 身(み)をかわす, 飛(と)びのく,
受(う)け止(と)める, 嘘(うそ)をつく, 判断(はんだん)する.

## Examples

<pre>
子供(こども)が飛(と)び出(だ)してきて、<b>とっさに</b>ブレーキを踏(ふ)んだ。
A child ran out in front of me and I hit the brakes without thinking.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に意見(いけん)を求(もと)められ、<b>とっさに</b>答(こた)えられなかった。
I was asked for my opinion out of the blue and couldn't come up with anything on the spot.
</pre>

<pre>
落(お)ちてきた荷物(にもつ)を、店員(てんいん)さんが<b>とっさに</b>手(て)で受(う)け止(と)めてくれた。
The shop assistant caught the falling bag with her hands on pure reflex.
</pre>

<pre>
地震(じしん)のとき机(つくえ)の下(した)に潜(もぐ)ったのは<b>とっさに</b>取(と)った行動(こうどう)だったが、結果(けっか)として命(いのち)を守(まも)ることになった。
Getting under the desk during the earthquake was a split-second reaction, but it turned out to be what saved his life.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It is equally at home in a friend's anecdote, a driver's statement
to the police, a news report and a novel, and it marks nothing about the speaker.

Its social work is worth noticing. Because `とっさに` says there was no time to think,
it functions as a built-in **explanation or excuse**.
とっさに嘘(うそ)をついてしまった admits to a lie while asking the listener to
understand that no better answer was available in the second the speaker had.
Take the adverb out - 嘘(うそ)をついてしまった - and the same sentence becomes a
plain confession. Learners often reach for it to soften an account of something they
did badly, and that instinct is correct.

Applied to someone else's action it usually reads as **admiration**:
とっさの判断(はんだん)がよかった praises presence of mind. It very rarely blames,
because it has already granted that no thought was possible.

Two neighbours to keep straight. <code>思(おも)わず</code> is about intention - you
did it without meaning to - and can apply to a slow situation, as in
思(おも)わず笑(わら)ってしまった. <code>反射的(はんしゃてき)に</code> is the
clinical version, "reflexively", and is the natural choice in technical or medical
writing. `とっさに` sits between them, and is the ordinary word.

## Contrast

### とっさに vs すぐに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すぐに` is about **delay**: nothing came between
the cue and the action. `とっさに` is about **deliberation**: there was no time for
any. Only `とっさに` needs the situation to be sudden.

</div>

<pre>
連絡(れんらく)をもらったら、<b>すぐに</b>向(む)かいます。
I'll head over as soon as I hear from you.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)の車(くるま)が急(きゅう)に止(と)まったので、<b>とっさに</b>ハンドルを切(き)った。
The car in front stopped dead, and I yanked the wheel over on instinct.
</pre>

The first sentence is about the future and about an expected cue, so `とっさに` is
impossible there. The second could not take `すぐに` without loss:
すぐにハンドルを切(き)った would suggest the driver saw the problem, decided, and
acted promptly - a sequence, however fast. `とっさに` denies that there was a sequence
at all.

### とっさに vs 思(おも)わず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とっさに` = no time. <code>思(おも)わず</code> = no intention.
An action can be both, but the two words point at different things.

</div>

<pre>
懐(なつ)かしい写真(しゃしん)を見(み)て、<b>思(おも)わず</b>笑(わら)ってしまった。
Seeing the old photo, I found myself smiling.
</pre>

<pre>
後(うし)ろから声(こえ)をかけられ、<b>とっさに</b>振(ふ)り返(かえ)った。
Someone called out behind me and I spun round on reflex.
</pre>

Nothing sudden happens in the first sentence - the speaker is looking at a photograph
at leisure - so `とっさに` would be wrong; what is missing is intention, not time. In
the second, 思(おも)わず would also be acceptable, and the sentence would shift very
slightly towards "I turned round before I could stop myself" rather than "I turned
round in the instant".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)、着(つ)いたら<b>とっさに</b>連絡(れんらく)します。 → ✅ 明日(あした)、着(つ)いたら<b>すぐに</b>連絡(れんらく)します。
  A promise about tomorrow cannot be a reflex. `とっさに` only reports what already
  happened.

- ❌ 宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>とっさに</b>終(お)わらせた。 → ✅ 宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>一気(いっき)に</b>終(お)わらせた。
  Homework is a planned task with no sudden trigger, and finishing it takes longer
  than an instant. Two conditions of the word fail at once.

- ❌ <b>とっさな</b>判断(はんだん)で助(たす)かった。 → ✅ <b>とっさの</b>判断(はんだん)で助(たす)かった。
  咄嗟(とっさ) is a noun, so the noun-modifying particle is `の`, not `な`.

- ❌ <b>とっさに</b>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)考(かんが)えてから答(こた)えた。 → ✅ <b>じっくり</b>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)考(かんが)えてから答(こた)えた。
  Thirty minutes of thought is the exact opposite of what the word describes.

## Practice

**1.** 棚(たな)から花瓶(かびん)が落(お)ちてきたので、____受(う)け止(と)めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>とっさに</b>受(う)け止(と)めた

The vase falling is the sudden trigger, catching it is a bodily reflex, and the
sentence is in the past. All three conditions are met. Note that
思(おも)わず would also work here, shifting the emphasis from "no time" to "before I
could think about it".

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)の面接(めんせつ)では、質問(しつもん)にとっさに答(こた)えるつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)の面接(めんせつ)では、質問(しつもん)に<b>すぐに</b>答(こた)えるつもりです。

You cannot intend to react reflexively - つもり and `とっさに` contradict each other,
and the event is in the future besides. If you want to say that you hope to cope well
with the unexpected, rebuild it around the noun:
とっさの質問(しつもん)にも落(お)ち着(つ)いて答(こた)えたいです。

</details>

**3.** Explain what `とっさに` contributes:
遅(おく)れた理由(りゆう)を聞(き)かれて、<b>とっさに</b>「電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっていて」と言(い)ってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Factually it adds only that the excuse came out in the instant the question was
asked. Socially it does much more: it tells the listener that the speaker had not
planned to lie, that the lie was a panic response rather than a scheme, and that the
speaker is not proud of it.

Remove it and the sentence reports a deliberate falsehood. That difference between a
prepared lie and one that escaped in the moment is exactly the work this adverb is
doing, and it is why it appears so often in apologies and confessions.

</details>

# あえて

**Meaning** · deliberately, even though there is reason not to; to go so far as to; if I must

`あえて` marks a choice made **against resistance**. Something makes the action
difficult, risky, unpopular or simply unnecessary - and the person does it anyway,
knowingly. Turned negative it is just as useful: あえて言(い)わなかった means the
speaker had something to say and chose to keep it back.

## Usage

An adverb, standing before the verb. Both <code>敢(あ)えて</code> and the kana
spelling are normal in print.

The one thing it always needs is **something arguing against the action**. That
counter-reason is usually right there in the sentence - 危険(きけん)だが,
嫌(きら)われるのを承知(しょうち)で, 分(わ)かっていながら - or it is obvious
from the situation. Without it, the word has nothing to push against and the sentence
reads as though a word is missing.

Four frames account for most of its use.

- **Against an obstacle.** <code>あえて～する</code>:
  あえて反対(はんたい)する, あえて厳(きび)しいことを言(い)う,
  あえて険(けわ)しい道(みち)を選(えら)ぶ.
- **Deliberate restraint.** <code>あえて～ない</code>:
  あえて触(ふ)れない, あえて何(なに)も言(い)わなかった,
  あえて助(たす)けなかった. The action was available and was declined on purpose.
- **Reluctant choice.** <code>あえて言(い)えば</code>,
  <code>あえて言(い)うなら</code>, <code>あえて挙(あ)げるなら</code> - "if I
  really have to name one". This frame is a hedge, and what follows it is a mild
  preference, not a strong claim.
- **Denying the need.** <code>あえて～する必要(ひつよう)はない</code>,
  <code>あえて～するまでもない</code>: there is no call to go out of your way.

## Examples

<pre>
嫌(きら)われるのを承知(しょうち)で、<b>あえて</b>反対(はんたい)した。
Knowing it would make me unpopular, I went ahead and objected.
</pre>

<pre>
理由(りゆう)は分(わ)かっていたが、<b>あえて</b>何(なに)も聞(き)かなかった。
I knew why, but I made a point of not asking.
</pre>

<pre>
どちらか<b>あえて</b>選(えら)ぶなら、静(しず)かなほうの部屋(へや)がいいです。
If I had to pick one, I'd rather have the quieter room.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)に育(そだ)ってほしいので、手(て)を貸(か)したい気持(きも)ちを抑(おさ)えて<b>あえて</b>最後(さいご)まで任(まか)せることにした。
I want the new staff to grow, so I held back the urge to step in and made a point of leaving it entirely to them.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral to slightly formal. It is standard in written argument - opinion columns,
reports, essays - and equally normal in careful adult speech: a manager explaining a
decision, a reviewer justifying an unpopular verdict. It is not stiff and not
old-fashioned, but it is not chatty either, and you will hear it more in explanation
than in small talk.

The word flatters whatever it modifies. Because it asserts that the actor saw the
cost and accepted it, `あえて` frames a choice as **principled rather than careless**.
That is why あえて言(い)わせていただきますが is such a common preface to criticism:
it announces in advance that the speaker knows the remark is unwelcome and considers
it necessary anyway. Used of your own behaviour it is a small claim to courage or
judgement, so it is worth being sure the claim is warranted.

The negative frame is the one learners most often lack, and it is very common in
Japanese. あえて言(い)わなかった, あえて触(ふ)れなかった and
あえて見(み)ないふりをした all describe silence as an active, considered decision.
Without `あえて`, 言(い)わなかった is merely a fact; with it, the silence becomes a
choice the speaker will defend.

One caution about <code>あえて言(い)えば</code>. It signals reluctance - the speaker
is being pressed for an answer and would rather not commit - so it does not sit in
front of a strong opinion. Follow it with a hedged one.

## Contrast

### あえて vs わざわざ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both describe doing something you did not have to do. わざわざ is
about **trouble taken**; `あえて` is about **an objection overridden**. Only わざわざ
appears in thanks.

</div>

<pre>
遠(とお)いところを<b>わざわざ</b>来(き)てくださって、ありがとうございました。
Thank you for coming all this way specially.
</pre>

<pre>
近道(ちかみち)もあったが、景色(けしき)を見(み)たくて<b>あえて</b>遠回(とおまわ)りをした。
There was a shortcut, but I wanted to see the view, so I took the long way round on purpose.
</pre>

Swapping them changes the sentences badly. あえて来(き)てくださって would suggest
the visitor came despite having a reason not to - which is not a thank-you, it is an
accusation. And わざわざ遠回(とおまわ)りをした would put the emphasis on the
nuisance of the detour rather than on the reason for choosing it. わざわざ also has a
sour second life in complaints - わざわざ言(い)わなくてもいいのに, "you didn't have
to say that" - which `あえて` does not share.

### あえて vs わざと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>わざと</code> contrasts with *by accident*. `あえて`
contrasts with *taking the easy option*. わざと often implies the act was underhand;
`あえて` implies it was defensible.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は<b>わざと</b>負(ま)けて、妹(いもうと)を喜(よろこ)ばせた。
My brother lost on purpose to make our little sister happy.
</pre>

<pre>
勝(か)てる相手(あいて)だったが、<b>あえて</b>苦手(にがて)な戦(たたか)い方(かた)を試(ため)した。
I could have beaten him easily, but I deliberately tried a style I'm bad at.
</pre>

わざと answers "did you mean to?"; `あえて` answers "why take the hard road?".
Replace わざと with `あえて` in the first sentence and it starts to sound as though
losing were a principled stand. Replace `あえて` with わざと in the second and the
speaker sounds like they were being difficult on purpose.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>あえて</b>来(き)てくれてありがとう。 → ✅ <b>わざわざ</b>来(き)てくれてありがとう。
  Thanks for someone's trouble is わざわざ. `あえて` would imply the person came
  against their better judgement.

- ❌ <b>あえて</b>コップを割(わ)ってしまった。 → ✅ <b>うっかり</b>コップを割(わ)ってしまった。
  `あえて` describes a reasoned decision. An accident cannot be one - and if it was
  intentional and unjustified, the word is わざと.

- ❌ のどが渇(かわ)いたので<b>あえて</b>水(みず)を飲(の)んだ。 → ✅ のどが渇(かわ)いたので水(みず)を飲(の)んだ。
  Nothing argues against drinking when you are thirsty, so there is no resistance for
  the word to override. Delete it.

- ❌ <b>あえて言(い)えば</b>、この案(あん)こそが唯一(ゆいいつ)の正解(せいかい)だ。 → ✅ <b>あえて言(い)えば</b>、この案(あん)のほうが少(すこ)しましだろうか。
  The <code>あえて言(い)えば</code> frame promises a reluctant, hedged answer.
  Following it with an absolute claim contradicts the hedge you just made.

## Practice

**1.** 失敗(しっぱい)させたくなかったが、本人(ほんにん)のために____口(くち)を出(だ)さなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本人(ほんにん)のために<b>あえて</b>口(くち)を出(だ)さなかった

The first clause supplies the resistance - the speaker wanted to intervene - and the
second reports the deliberate restraint. This <code>あえて～ない</code> shape turns
saying nothing into an active decision made for the other person's sake.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部長(ぶちょう)が（あえて・わざわざ）空港(くうこう)まで迎(むか)えに来(き)てくださった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>わざわざ</b>空港(くうこう)まで迎(むか)えに来(き)てくださった

The sentence is an expression of gratitude for trouble taken, which is わざわざ.
`あえて` would suggest the department head had grounds for not coming and came
regardless - not a compliment.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)の失敗(しっぱい)について何(なに)も言(い)わなかった。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)の失敗(しっぱい)について<b>あえて</b>何(なに)も言(い)わなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a bare report. The speaker said nothing, and we are told nothing about why -
they may not have noticed, or may not have cared.

(b) makes the silence intentional and defends it. The speaker saw the mistake, had
words ready, and decided that saying them would not help. It invites the listener to
read the silence as consideration rather than indifference, and it usually implies
that the speaker would explain the reasoning if asked.

</details>

# ひとつ

**Meaning** · go on, give it a try; do me this one favour - an adverbial ひとつ that has nothing to do with counting

Dropped in front of a proposal or a request, `ひとつ` does not mean "one". It warms
the sentence up: ひとつやってみるか is "right, let's have a go at it", and
ひとつよろしくお願(ねが)いします is "I'd be grateful for your help". Think of it as
the Japanese of a genial "go on then".

## Usage

An adverb. Nothing attaches to it - no `を`, no `の`, no `が` - and in writing it is
usually followed by a comma. Kana is the safer spelling, because
<code>一(ひと)つ</code> invites the reader to look for something being counted.

It cannot stand on its own. `ひとつ` needs a **proposal, an attempt or a request**
after it, and the shape of that clause is where the meaning lives.

- <code>ひとつ～てみる / ～てみよう / ～てみるか</code> - trying something, either as a
  suggestion to a group or as a resolution to yourself:
  ひとつ試(ため)してみよう, ひとつ作(つく)ってみるか.
- <code>ここはひとつ～</code> - by far the strongest collocation. It means "in this
  particular situation, let us…", and it introduces a compromise, an appeal or a plea:
  ここはひとつ穏便(おんびん)に, ここはひとつ私(わたし)に任(まか)せてください.
- <code>ひとつよろしくお願(ねが)いします</code> and its relatives -
  ひとつご検討(けんとう)ください, ひとつお力(ちから)をお貸(か)しください. Here
  it is pure lubrication on a request.
- <code>ひとつ～てくれないか / ～ていただけませんか</code> - a favour asked as a
  favour, not as a right.

Put it in front of a plain statement and it reverts to the numeral. ひとつ食(た)べた
is "I ate one", not "I went ahead and ate".

## Examples

<pre>
せっかくだから、<b>ひとつ</b>挑戦(ちょうせん)してみるか。
Since we're here, shall we have a go at it?
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から同(おな)じ部署(ぶしょ)です。<b>ひとつ</b>よろしくお願(ねが)いします。
We'll be in the same section from next month. I look forward to working with you.
</pre>

<pre>
ここは<b>ひとつ</b>、私(わたし)の顔(かお)に免(めん)じて許(ゆる)してやってくれないか。
Look, do me a favour and let this one go, would you?
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい企画(きかく)をお持(も)ちしましたので、<b>ひとつ</b>ご検討(けんとう)いただけますと幸(さいわ)いです。
I've brought along a new proposal, and I'd be most grateful if you would consider it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is an idiomatic, slightly **old-fashioned and hearty** word. It tends to belong
to middle-aged speakers and, as a tendency rather than a rule, to men - a section
head raising a glass, a shopkeeper coaxing a customer, someone talking a dispute down.
Younger speakers use it, but often with a faint sense of putting on a voice.

The parts of it are not equally marked, and it is worth separating them.

<code>ひとつよろしくお願(ねが)いします</code> is ordinary, current business
Japanese. Anyone can say it on being introduced, at the start of a project, or when
handing work over. It is a shade more familiar and more personal than a bare
よろしくお願(ねが)いします - it says "I'm asking you as a person, not filing a
request" - and that is exactly why it is useful.

<code>ここはひとつ</code> is the marked one. It is the language of smoothing things
over, and it carries an audible appeal to the listener's generosity. It is common in
negotiation and in fiction, especially where someone is asking to be let off, and used
by a learner in a neutral situation it will sound theatrical.

`ひとつ` adds **no politeness of its own**. It supplies warmth and ingratiation, and
it takes its politeness from the ending: ひとつやってみるか is blokey, and
ひとつご検討(けんとう)ください is deferential. What it will not do is soften an
order. In front of an imperative the friendliness and the command work against each
other and the result sounds sarcastic.

One practical warning: ひとつ食(た)べてみてください is genuinely ambiguous between
"try one" and "go on, try it". Japanese speakers resolve it by context and intonation.
If you need to be sure, count with 一(ひと)つだけ or use どうぞ instead.

## Contrast

### ひとつ vs ちょっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both soften a request. <code>ちょっと</code> shrinks it ("it's only
a small thing"). `ひとつ` does not shrink anything - it appeals to the listener's
goodwill and asks them to grant a favour.

</div>

<pre>
すみません、<b>ちょっと</b>手伝(てつだ)ってもらえますか。
Sorry - could you give me a hand for a second?
</pre>

<pre>
ここは<b>ひとつ</b>、力(ちから)を貸(か)してもらえないだろうか。
Come on - couldn't you help me out here?
</pre>

The first is an everyday, low-stakes ask, and `ちょっと` is available to any speaker in
any register. The second concedes that the favour is a real one and leans on the
relationship to get it. Swap them and both sentences go wrong:
ひとつ手伝(てつだ)ってもらえますか makes a two-minute job sound like a matter of
honour, and ちょっと力(ちから)を貸(か)してもらえないだろうか trivialises
something the speaker has just made a plea about.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、<b>ひとつ</b>映画(えいが)を見(み)ました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)、映画(えいが)を見(み)ました。
  In front of a plain past statement, `ひとつ` can only be the numeral - and films are
  not usually counted with つ. The adverbial use needs a proposal or a request after
  it.

- ❌ <b>ひとつ</b>窓(まど)を閉(し)めろ。 → ✅ ここは<b>ひとつ</b>、窓(まど)を閉(し)めてもらえないか。
  The word asks for goodwill; an imperative does not. Together they sound sarcastic.

- ❌ <b>ひとつの</b>お願(ねが)いがあります。 → ✅ <b>ひとつ</b>お願(ねが)いできませんか。
  With `の` it becomes the numeral again, and the sentence turns into "I have one
  request". As an adverb it takes no particle at all.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、<b>ひとつ</b>説明(せつめい)しろとおっしゃいましたよね。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、説明(せつめい)するようにとおっしゃいましたよね。
  `ひとつ` belongs to the person doing the asking, not to a report of someone else's
  instruction. It cannot be inserted into a quotation of an order.

## Practice

**1.** 久(ひさ)しぶりに集(あつ)まったんだから、____みんなで写真(しゃしん)でも撮(と)ってみようか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ひとつ</b>みんなで写真(しゃしん)でも撮(と)ってみようか

The sentence already has the shape `ひとつ` needs: a suggestion in
<code>～てみようか</code>. The adverb adds the genial "go on then" that makes it an
invitation rather than a plan, and it fits the warm, reunion tone of the first clause.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 課長(かちょう)、ひとつの相談(そうだん)がありますので、後(あと)でお時間(じかん)をください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 課長(かちょう)、<b>ひとつ</b>ご相談(そうだん)したいことがありますので、後(あと)でお時間(じかん)をいただけませんか。

Two things. The `の` turns the word back into the numeral, and the adverbial `ひとつ`
takes no particle. And since `ひとつ` is asking a favour, the request itself should be
phrased as one - ください is a shade blunt to a superior here.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) よろしくお願(ねが)いします。
(b) <b>ひとつ</b>よろしくお願(ねが)いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the standard formula. It is correct everywhere, and it is what you write at the
end of an email or say on being introduced.

(b) is warmer and more personal. The `ひとつ` frames the request as something the
speaker is asking of *you* specifically, with a slight tilt of the head - "do look
after me, won't you". It is common when someone is about to depend on the listener,
and it has a faintly hearty, older-generation flavour, so it suits a greeting between
adults at work better than an email to a customer you have never met.

</details>

# いずれにせよ

**Meaning** · whichever it turns out to be; at any rate; in any case

`いずれにせよ` is a connective that does one precise job: it takes a set of
possibilities that has just been laid out, declares that the choice between them does
not matter, and states the thing that holds **on every branch**. It does not resolve
the question - it retires it.

## Usage

A sentence connective. It stands at the head of the sentence, or of the second clause,
and is normally followed by a comma.

It is built out of いずれ ("which of them") + `に` + せよ, the literary imperative of
する - the same せよ as in なんにせよ and 何(なに)をするにせよ. That imperative is
concessive: "let it be whichever it likes".

**It needs alternatives in front of it.** This is the part learners miss. `いずれにせよ`
is not a general-purpose "anyway"; without two or more possibilities already in the
listener's mind it has nothing to sweep aside, and the sentence sounds like the answer
to a question nobody asked. Those possibilities can be supplied in three ways:

- Spelled out as a disjunction: <code>～か～か</code>, <code>～にせよ～にせよ</code>,
  <code>～であれ～であれ</code>.
- Left open as an uncertainty:
  間(ま)に合(あ)うかどうかは分(わ)からないが、いずれにせよ…
- Left implicit because two people have just been disagreeing about them.

What follows is a **conclusion, an obligation or a decision** - the invariant. Typical
endings are 必要(ひつよう)がある, ～なければならない,
<code>ことに変(か)わりはない</code>, ～だろう.

The same expression comes in a register ladder, and they are otherwise
interchangeable: <code>いずれにせよ</code> and <code>いずれにしろ</code> are the formal
written pair, <code>いずれにしても</code> is neutral and usable aloud, and
<code>どちらにしても</code>, <code>どっちにしても</code> and <code>どっちみち</code>
are casual. Note that the どちら forms allow only two options; the いずれ forms allow
any number.

## Examples

<pre>
行(い)くか行(い)かないか、<b>いずれにせよ</b>今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に返事(へんじ)が必要(ひつよう)だ。
Go or don't go - either way, an answer is needed today.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)でも晴(は)れでも、<b>いずれにせよ</b>大会(たいかい)は行(おこな)われます。
Rain or shine, the tournament will go ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
原因(げんいん)はまだ特定(とくてい)できていないが、<b>いずれにせよ</b>再発(さいはつ)防止(ぼうし)策(さく)を示(しめ)す必要(ひつよう)がある。
The cause has not yet been identified, but in any case we will have to set out measures against a recurrence.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げに踏(ふ)み切(き)るか、品質(ひんしつ)を落(お)として価格(かかく)を据(す)え置(お)くか、<b>いずれにせよ</b>顧客(こきゃく)への説明(せつめい)は避(さ)けられない。
Raise prices, or cut quality and hold them where they are - whichever we do, we cannot avoid explaining ourselves to customers.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written and formal** connective. Its home is reports, minutes, editorials,
business email and prepared remarks - anywhere an argument is being tidied up. In
speech, the same move is made with いずれにしても, or more casually with
どっちにしても, どっちみち or とにかく. A learner who says いずれにせよ over coffee
will be understood perfectly and will sound like a memo.

The せよ ending is what makes it formal, and it is worth knowing that this is a fossil
rather than a live imperative. Nobody is being told to do anything.

`いずれにせよ` also carries a mild **closing-down** force, and that is a social fact
worth watching. Because it announces that the preceding distinctions are irrelevant,
using it while someone senior is still setting out those distinctions can read as
impatience - "whatever the case, the fact remains…". In writing this is efficient; in
a meeting it can be sharp. A softer alternative when you do not want to cut anyone off
is どちらにしても or a simple ただ.

One trap has nothing to do with register. **いずれ on its own is a different word.** It
means "sometime, in due course" - いずれまたお会(あ)いしましょう,
いずれ分(わ)かることだ - and has no connective function at all. Dropping the
にせよ does not shorten the phrase; it replaces it.

## Contrast

### いずれにせよ vs とにかく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いずれにせよ` says the conclusion **holds on every branch**, so
the branches can be set aside. とにかく says "never mind that for now" and pushes the
question away without claiming anything about it.

</div>

<pre>
理由(りゆう)はあとで聞(き)く。<b>とにかく</b>病院(びょういん)へ行(い)こう。
I'll hear the reasons later. Right now, let's just get to the hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)か故障(こしょう)か、<b>いずれにせよ</b>運行(うんこう)の再開(さいかい)には時間(じかん)がかかる。
Accident or breakdown - either way, it will be a while before services resume.
</pre>

The first is a decision to postpone a question; the reasons still matter, just not
yet. The second is a claim about logic: whichever explanation turns out to be right,
the delay follows. とにかく is spoken, urgent and often a little impatient;
`いずれにせよ` is written, calm and argumentative. Only `いずれにせよ` requires
alternatives to have been raised, which is why とにかく can open a conversation and
`いずれにせよ` cannot.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ （会話(かいわ)の冒頭(ぼうとう)で）<b>いずれにせよ</b>、明日(あした)は九時(くじ)集合(しゅうごう)です。 → ✅ <b>とにかく</b>明日(あした)は九時(くじ)集合(しゅうごう)です。
  Opened cold, the phrase points back at alternatives that were never mentioned. It
  can only follow something.

- ❌ 中止(ちゅうし)か延期(えんき)か、<b>いずれにせよ</b>まだ決(き)まっていない。 → ✅ 中止(ちゅうし)か延期(えんき)か、<b>いずれにせよ</b>参加者(さんかしゃ)への連絡(れんらく)は必要(ひつよう)だ。
  What follows must be the thing that is true on both branches. Restating the
  uncertainty undoes the whole point of the connective.

- ❌ <b>いずれ</b>、この案(あん)は見直(みなお)すべきだ。 → ✅ <b>いずれにせよ</b>、この案(あん)は見直(みなお)すべきだ。
  Bare いずれ means "sometime", so the first sentence says the plan should be revised
  at some point in the future - a different claim entirely.

- ❌ （報告書(ほうこくしょ)に）<b>どっちみち</b>追加(ついか)の調査(ちょうさ)が必要(ひつよう)である。 → ✅ （報告書(ほうこくしょ)に）<b>いずれにせよ</b>追加(ついか)の調査(ちょうさ)が必要(ひつよう)である。
  The meaning is identical; the register is not. どっちみち in a written report is as
  jarring as "either way, whatever" in a formal English one.

## Practice

**1.** 自分(じぶん)で直(なお)すか業者(ぎょうしゃ)に頼(たの)むか、____費用(ひよう)はかかる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>いずれにせよ</b>費用(ひよう)はかかる

The two options are laid out in front, and what follows is the thing that is true of
both of them. That is the shape the connective requires: alternatives, then the
invariant.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 参加者(さんかしゃ)が何人(なんにん)になるか分(わ)かりませんが、いずれ会場(かいじょう)は広(ひろ)めに押(お)さえておきましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 参加者(さんかしゃ)が何人(なんにん)になるか分(わ)かりませんが、<b>いずれにせよ</b>会場(かいじょう)は広(ひろ)めに押(お)さえておきましょう。

Bare いずれ means "at some point in the future", which turns the sentence into a plan
to book a room eventually. The intended meaning - "however many people come" - needs
the full connective. In speech, いずれにしても would be the more comfortable choice.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) <b>とにかく</b>一度(いちど)会(あ)って話(はな)しましょう。
(b) <b>いずれにせよ</b>一度(いちど)会(あ)って話(はな)す必要(ひつよう)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) brushes the discussion aside and proposes action. It is spoken, warm and slightly
impatient: whatever we have been arguing about, let's just meet. It works even if no
alternatives were on the table.

(b) reasons. It assumes the listener has raised two or more possible courses, and it
argues that a meeting is required under all of them. The register is that of an email
or a meeting summary, and the effect is to close the branch of the discussion rather
than to postpone it. Said to a superior mid-explanation, (b) can sound like an
interruption in a way (a) does not.

</details>

# 言わずもがな

**Meaning** · needless to say - and, in its older sense, better left unsaid. One fixed phrase with two nearly opposite readings.

言(い)わずもがな is a fossil: 言(い)わ (the imperfective stem of
言(い)う) + ず (negative) + もがな, an Old Japanese particle of wishing. Literally
"would that it were not said". From that one root the phrase has grown two senses that
pull in opposite directions - **"so obvious that saying it is unnecessary"** and **"a
remark that should not have been made"** - and modern Japanese keeps both.

## Formation

The phrase does not conjugate and it does not attach productively to other verbs. What
varies is the frame it sits in, and the frame is what tells you which sense is meant.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (listing sense) | Noun + は + 言(い)わずもがな（、Noun + も…） | 英語(えいご) → 英語(えいご)は言(い)わずもがな |
| Before a noun | 言(い)わずもがな + の + Noun | こと → 言(い)わずもがなのこと |
| As predicate | 言(い)わずもがなだ／である／であろう | - → 言(い)わずもがなである |
| Concessive lead-in | 言(い)わずもがなだが／のことだが、～ | - → 言(い)わずもがなだが、確認(かくにん)は必要(ひつよう)だ |
| Historical parse (not productive) | Verb imperfective stem + ず + もがな | 言(い)う → 言(い)わずもがな |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>言(い)わずもがなの＋noun</code> is almost always the
critical sense, "an uncalled-for ~". <code>Noun+は言(い)わずもがな</code> followed
by a second listed item is almost always the "needless to say" sense. The last row of
the table is history, not a pattern: あらずもがな survives in classical texts, but you
cannot build 行(い)かずもがな or 見(み)ずもがな in modern Japanese.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
基本(きほん)的(てき)な礼儀(れいぎ)は<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>、時間(じかん)を守(まも)ることも信用(しんよう)のうちである。
Basic courtesy goes without saying, and keeping to time is part of being trusted as well.
</pre>

<pre>
あの一言(ひとこと)は<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>だった。
That remark was one he would have done better not to make.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)の確保(かくほ)が最優先(さいゆうせん)であることは<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>であろう。
That securing safety comes first is presumably too obvious to need stating.
</pre>

<pre>
英語(えいご)は<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>、中国語(ちゅうごくご)まで求(もと)められる時代(じだい)になった。
We have reached an age in which English is taken as read and Chinese is expected on top of it.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>の一言(ひとこと)で、場(ば)の空気(くうき)が凍(こお)りついた。
One remark that was better left unsaid, and the room froze.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)というものは、つい<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>のことまで口(くち)にしてしまう。
Parents being what they are, they end up saying even the things that would be better left unsaid.
</pre>

<pre>
費用(ひよう)の問題(もんだい)は<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>、人手(ひとで)の確保(かくほ)も大(おお)きな課題(かだい)として残(のこ)っている。
The question of cost needs no stating, and securing enough staff remains a serious problem too.
</pre>

<pre>
忠告(ちゅうこく)のつもりであったが、相手(あいて)にとっては<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>の説教(せっきょう)にすぎなかった。
He had meant it as advice, but to the man on the receiving end it was a sermon nobody had asked for.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not deploy it.** 言(い)わずもがな belongs to written
Japanese of a fairly literary cast: essays and newspaper columns, book reviews and
commentary, the prepared portion of a formal address, and narration in fiction. It
also survives in the set noun phrase <code>言(い)わずもがなのこと</code>. It is not
archaic in the way <code>～べからず</code> is - an educated reader meets it without
blinking - but it is not spoken. Say it in conversation and you will sound as though
you are reading aloud from a column.

The modern equivalents are ordinary and entirely adequate. For "needless to say", use
<code>言(い)うまでもなく</code>, <code>言(い)うまでもない</code> or もちろん. For
"better left unsaid", use <code>余計(よけい)な一言(ひとこと)</code>,
<code>言(い)わなくてもいいこと</code> or <code>言(い)わなければよかった</code>.

There is a second and stronger reason to keep it in the reading pile. Because the two
senses coexist, a sentence built around it can be **misread as its own opposite**. If
you tell a colleague それは言(い)わずもがなですね intending "that goes without
saying", there is a real chance of being heard as "you should not have said that" -
which is a rebuke. Writers avoid the ambiguity by choosing the frame carefully; a
learner has no reason to take the risk when
言(い)うまでもありません is sitting right there.

When you meet it in text, the frame disambiguates almost perfectly. A の and a
following noun means the critical sense. A は and a second listed item means the
"needless to say" sense. A bare predicate - 言(い)わずもがなである - is usually the
"needless to say" sense in an essay, and the critical sense when the subject is a
remark someone made.

One last observation about tone. In the listing sense the phrase does a little more
than <code>～はもちろん</code> would: it flatters the reader slightly, treating the
first item as beneath discussion between people like us. That faint air of shared
sophistication is part of why it is a columnist's word.

## Contrast

### 言(い)わずもがな vs 言(い)うまでもなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning in the "needless to say" sense, two registers
apart. <code>言(い)うまでもなく</code> is the living form and is safe everywhere,
including speech.

</div>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)が大切(たいせつ)であることは<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>である。
That health matters is, of course, beyond stating.
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)が大切(たいせつ)であることは<b>言(い)うまでもない</b>。
It goes without saying that health matters.
</pre>

The content is identical. The first belongs in a printed essay; the second belongs
anywhere, from a report to a conversation. Note also that
<code>言(い)うまでもなく</code> has only the one sense, so it can never be
misheard as a criticism - which is the practical argument for preferring it.

### 言(い)わずもがな vs ～はもちろん・～はもとより

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three list a taken-for-granted item and then add a further one.
<code>～はもちろん</code> is neutral, <code>～はもとより</code> is formal, and
言(い)わずもがな is formal **and** literary, with a note of "we need hardly discuss
this one".

</div>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)<b>はもちろん</b>、職場(しょくば)の雰囲気(ふんいき)も大事(だいじ)だ。
Salary matters, obviously, but so does the atmosphere of the workplace.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>、職場(しょくば)の雰囲気(ふんいき)こそが定着(ていちゃく)を左右(さゆう)する。
Pay is beyond discussion; it is the atmosphere of the workplace that decides whether people stay.
</pre>

Structurally the two are the same move. What changes is who is speaking: the first
sentence could be said over lunch, the second is the voice of an article. If you find
yourself wanting 言(い)わずもがな in this frame, write <code>～はもちろん</code> in
speech and <code>～はもとより</code> in a formal document, and you will never be wrong.

### 言(い)わずもがな vs 余計(よけい)な一言(ひとこと)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for the *critical* sense, the modern phrase is
<code>余計(よけい)な一言(ひとこと)</code>. It is blunt, spoken, and carries no
risk of being taken the other way round.

</div>

<pre>
それは<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>の一言(ひとこと)だった。
That was a remark better left unsaid.
</pre>

<pre>
それは<b>余計(よけい)な一言(ひとこと)</b>だったね。
That was one word too many, wasn't it.
</pre>

The first is how a novelist reports it; the second is how a friend tells you that you
went too far. There is a difference of stance as well:
言(い)わずもがな judges the remark from a distance, almost with regret, while
余計(よけい)な一言(ひとこと) is a direct reproach. If you are actually telling
someone off, you want the second.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)か<b>ずもがな</b>の旅(たび)だった。 → ✅ 行(い)かなくてもよかった旅(たび)だった。
  もがな is not a productive suffix in modern Japanese. Outside the fixed
  言(い)わずもがな and the classical あらずもがな, it does not attach to verbs at
  all.

- ❌ 言(い)わずもがな<b>に</b>、準備(じゅんび)が必要(ひつよう)だ。 → ✅ <b>言(い)うまでもなく</b>、準備(じゅんび)が必要(ひつよう)だ。
  The phrase takes no `に`. As a sentence-opening adverbial, the modern
  <code>言(い)うまでもなく</code> is what you want.

- ❌ （会議(かいぎ)で上司(じょうし)に）「それは言(い)わずもがなですね。」 → ✅ 「それはもちろんですね。」
  Even setting the register aside, this is risky. The listener may hear the critical
  sense - "that was something you should not have said" - and you will have rebuked
  your manager by accident.

- ❌ 明日(あした)休(やす)みだよ。言(い)わずもがなだけどね。 → ✅ 明日(あした)休(やす)みだよ。言(い)うまでもないけどね。
  Casual speech and a literary fossil in the same breath. The phrase has no casual
  form; it simply does not belong in the sentence.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence, and who is speaking?
技術(ぎじゅつ)の進歩(しんぽ)は<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>、制度(せいど)の設計(せっけい)こそが問(と)われている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In print: a newspaper column, a magazine essay, or the opening of an argumentative
report. The voice is a commentator addressing a general but educated reader.

Two things mark it. The listing frame
<code>～は言(い)わずもがな、～こそ</code> is a writer's construction, and the
sentence assumes agreement about the first item instead of arguing for it. Nobody
speaks this way in a meeting.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
準備(じゅんび)の大切(たいせつ)さは<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>である。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

準備(じゅんび)が大切(たいせつ)なことは<b>言(い)うまでもない</b>。

<code>言(い)うまでもない</code> carries exactly the same claim and is usable in
speech, in email and in writing alike. もちろん準備(じゅんび)は大切(たいせつ)だ
would also do, at a more conversational level.

</details>

**3.** Which sense is each one, and how can you tell?
(a) 彼(かれ)の<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>の一言(ひとこと)が事態(じたい)をこじらせた。
(b) 費用(ひよう)の問題(もんだい)は<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>、時間(じかん)も足(た)りない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the critical sense: a remark that would have been better not made. The giveaway
is the frame - <code>言(い)わずもがなの</code> in front of a noun - reinforced by a
consequence, こじらせた, that only makes sense if the remark did harm.

(b) is the listing sense: cost goes without saying, and time is short as well. The
giveaway is `は` followed by a second item that is being *added*. When you see a second
item, read "needless to say"; when you see の plus a noun, read "better left unsaid".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the register error:
「ごめん、昨日(きのう)のあれ、言(い)わずもがなだったよね。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 「ごめん、昨日(きのう)のあれ、余計(よけい)な一言(ひとこと)だったよね。」

The sense is right - the speaker is apologising for something they should not have
said - but the phrase is literary and ごめん and よね are not. In a spoken apology, use
余計(よけい)なことを言(い)った or 言(い)わなきゃよかった.

</details>

**5.** Explain what 言(い)わずもがな contributes here, and why a learner should not
produce this sentence:
基礎(きそ)研究(けんきゅう)の重要性(じゅうようせい)は<b>言(い)わずもがな</b>だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It asserts that the importance of basic research is beyond argument, and it does so
with a small flourish: the phrase treats the point as settled among readers of the
writer's sort. <code>言(い)うまでもない</code> would make the same claim without the
flourish.

A learner should leave it alone for two reasons. It is written register, so it will
sound wrong out loud. And it is ambiguous - stripped of the essayistic context, a
listener could take 言(い)わずもがなだ as "that was a thing better not said", which
turns a statement of agreement into a complaint about the previous speaker.

</details>

# ぞ・ぜ・わ

**Meaning** · three emphatic sentence-final particles - and three of the most heavily register-marked words in the language

`ぞ`, `ぜ` and `わ` all add force or feeling to the end of a plain-form sentence, but
none of them is a neutral option. Each one tells the listener something about who the
speaker is and what kind of scene this is, and the picture a learner picks up from
subtitles is not the picture of how they are actually used.

## Usage

All three are 終助詞(しゅうじょし), sentence-final particles. They attach to the **plain form** of
the predicate and close the sentence.

- After a verb or an い-adjective, directly: 行(い)くぞ, やろうぜ,
  寒(さむ)いわ.
- After a noun or a な-adjective, `だ` comes first: 本気(ほんき)だぞ,
  最高(さいこう)だぜ, きれいだわ.
- `ぞ` and `ぜ` do not form questions and do not combine with か. They assert.

**ぞ** is a strong assertion, and it points in one of two directions. Aimed at
yourself it is a spur - よし、やるぞ, 負(ま)けないぞ - and in that use it is
essentially 独(ひと)り言(ごと), available to any speaker. Aimed at another person it is a warning
or a push delivered from above: 危(あぶ)ないぞ,
置(お)いていくぞ, 遅(おく)れるぞ. That second use leans strongly male and
strongly downward - to children, to close juniors, to a team you are leading.

**ぜ** is casual, chummy and addressed to a peer. It is the most male-marked of the
three, and it is liveliest in the volitional: 行(い)こうぜ, やろうぜ,
飲(の)もうぜ. Outside that, in real conversation, it is much rarer than fiction
would suggest.

**わ** is two different particles wearing one spelling, and **pitch tells them apart**.

- **Rising わ** is the soft, so-called feminine わ of standard Japanese:
  そうだと思(おも)うわ, きれいだわ. It softens an assertion rather than
  strengthening it.
- **Falling わ** is gender-neutral. It came into the standard language from Kansai and
  is now everywhere in casual speech: これは無理(むり)だわ, もう帰(かえ)るわ,
  知(し)らんわ. It marks something the speaker has just realised or decided, more or
  less to themselves.

## Examples

<pre>
よし、今日(きょう)こそ終(お)わらせる<b>ぞ</b>。
Right - today I'm getting this finished.
</pre>

<pre>
そろそろ行(い)こう<b>ぜ</b>、時間(じかん)がないんだから。
Come on, let's get moving - we haven't got time.
</pre>

<pre>
このパスワード、全然(ぜんぜん)思(おも)い出(だ)せない<b>わ</b>。
I genuinely cannot remember this password.
</pre>

<pre>
「わたくしも同(おな)じことを考(かんが)えておりました<b>わ</b>」と夫人(ふじん)は静(しず)かに言(い)った。
"I had been thinking precisely the same thing," the lady said quietly.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Treat these as things to **recognise in other people's mouths** before you put them in
your own. Nothing here is rude in itself, but each one places the speaker, and placing
yourself wrongly is the risk.

**ぞ and ぜ have no polite form and no upward use.** There is no ですぞ or ますぜ that
you can say to a manager. `ぞ` addressed to a superior is not merely casual, it is
insubordinate, because the particle carries the authority to warn. Between friends of
similar age `ぜ` is warm; from a learner to a teacher it is a small social accident.

You will nevertheless meet ですぞ and ますぞ, and they are worth knowing as what they
are: 役割語(やくわりご), character speech. In fiction, ～ますぞ is the voice of the
elderly professor, the old retainer, the wizard. Real people over seventy do not
generally talk like this. Read it, enjoy it, and do not borrow it.

`ぜ` has a similar problem in reverse. Anime, dubbing, song lyrics and manga are full
of it because it instantly signals "confident young man", and that is exactly why it
is thinner on the ground in real conversation than a learner expects. As a tendency,
men use it and women do not; as a fact about frequency, most men use it far less often
than the fiction they watch.

The two わ are the sharpest trap on this page, because the one learners copy is the one
they should avoid and the one they overlook is the one they could safely use.

Rising わ - the そうだと思(おも)うわ pattern - is strongly associated with women's
speech in fiction, with a genteel Tokyo register, and with older speakers. Real young
women use it much less than the subtitles imply, and ですわ／ますわ is the お嬢様(じょうさま) voice of drama and comedy. A learner who ends sentences this way is not
speaking politely; they are speaking in costume.

Falling わ is the useful one. It is not gendered, it is current, and it is heard from
men and women of all ages: 疲(つか)れたわ, これ無理(むり)だわ,
やっぱりそうだわ. It reports a realisation or a decision that the speaker is mostly
announcing to themselves, and it is soft rather than assertive. If you want one of
these three particles in your active vocabulary, this is the one - and the whole
difference from the other わ is the direction of the pitch, so listen for it.

None of the three attaches to keigo, and none of them is used in writing except to
quote speech or to sound deliberately informal.

## Contrast

### ぞ・ぜ・わ vs よ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よ` is the neutral emphatic and says nothing about the speaker.
The other three all say something: `ぞ` claims the standing to warn, `ぜ` claims peer
familiarity, rising `わ` claims a soft feminine register, falling `わ` marks a private
realisation.

</div>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)、もう来(き)ます<b>よ</b>。
The train's coming.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)、もう来(く)る<b>ぞ</b>。
Train's coming - move.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)、もう来(く)る<b>わ</b>。
Oh, the train's here already.
</pre>

The information is the same in all three. The first is available to anybody talking to
anybody, and it is the safe default; it survives being made polite as
来(き)ますよ. The second pushes the listener into action and would be strange from a
junior member of staff to a client. The third, with falling pitch, is the speaker
noticing something almost to themselves. Where you are unsure, `よ` is always the
correct answer.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まります<b>ぞ</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まります。
  There is no polite `ぞ`. ～ますぞ exists only as the fiction voice of an elderly
  gentleman, and to a manager it lands as either impertinent or absurd.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)、一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)こう<b>ぜ</b>。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)きませんか。
  `ぜ` is strictly peer-to-peer and downward. Addressed to a teacher it is not casual
  friendliness, it is a status error.

- ❌ 寒(さむ)い<b>だわ</b>。 → ✅ 寒(さむ)い<b>わ</b>。
  い-adjectives take the particle directly. The `だ` appears only after a noun or a
  な-adjective: しずかだわ, 休(やす)みだわ.

- ❌ （自己(じこ)紹介(しょうかい)で）はじめまして。台湾(たいわん)から来(き)ました<b>わ</b>。 → ✅ はじめまして。台湾(たいわん)から来(き)ました。
  Rising わ on a polite form is the register of a drama heroine, not of an ordinary
  introduction. In です／ます speech, end the sentence and stop.

## Practice

**1.** （現場(げんば)で後輩(こうはい)に）「その箱(はこ)、けっこう重(おも)い____。気(き)をつけて。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

けっこう重(おも)い<b>ぞ</b>

`ぞ` is the particle of a warning issued from a position of seniority, which is exactly
the scene described, and it attaches straight to the い-adjective. `よ` would also be
correct and a shade gentler; `ぜ` would be wrong, because `ぜ` treats the listener as an
equal rather than as someone you are looking out for.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: （会社(かいしゃ)の受付(うけつけ)で）「三時(さんじ)に約束(やくそく)がありますわ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ （会社(かいしゃ)の受付(うけつけ)で）「三時(さんじ)にお約束(やくそく)をいただいております。」

Rising わ on top of a polite sentence is the speech of a wealthy lady in a television
drama, and a reception desk is the wrong stage for it. Business Japanese takes no
sentence-final particle here at all.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう帰(かえ)る<b>わ</b>。（falling pitch）
(b) もう帰(かえ)る<b>ぞ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker announcing their own decision, half to themselves. It is soft, it
is not gendered, and it does not ask the listener to do anything - "right, I'm off
then". Any speaker can use it in casual company.

(b) is aimed outward. It tells the people present that the speaker is leaving and that
they had better move too, and the authority to say that is built into the particle. It
leans male and downward, so it fits a parent to a child or a group leader to the group,
and it would be out of place said to a superior. Swapping (a) for (b) turns a private
decision into an instruction.

</details>
