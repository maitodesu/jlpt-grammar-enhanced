# N1 - Picking out a topic, and putting it another way


---

# とは

**Meaning** · "the term X means…" - marks the word or phrase in front of it as the item being defined

<code>とは</code> is the quotative `と` plus the topic `は`. What it puts on the
table is not a thing but a **word**: it quotes the expression itself and promises
that the rest of the sentence will say what that expression means. A reader who
meets <code>とは</code> is already waiting for のことだ, という意味(いみ)だ or
を指(さ)す at the far end of the sentence.

## Formation

The "Attaches to" column here is really "what is being quoted" - a term, not a
thing out in the world.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a term) | ～とは | 過疎(かそ) → 過疎(かそ)とは |
| Loanword or abbreviation | ～とは | エコ → エコとは |
| Term in quotation marks | 「～」とは | 速(すみ)やかに → 「速(すみ)やかに」とは |
| Verb or clause quoted as a concept | plain form + とは | 働(はたら)く → 働(はたら)くとは |
| Asking for a definition | ～とは何(なに)か / ～とは何(なん)ですか | 自由(じゆう) → 自由(じゆう)とは何(なに)か |
| Usual closers | ～とは…のことだ / …という意味(いみ)だ / …を指(さ)す | 過疎(かそ)とは…状態(じょうたい)を指(さ)す |
| Spoken equivalent | ～というのは / ～って | 過疎(かそ)というのは |

<div class="note">

**Note** - no `だ` before <code>とは</code> in a definition. The term is quoted
bare: <code>過疎(かそ)とは</code>, never ❌<code>過疎(かそ)だとは</code>.
That `だとは` shape belongs to the separate exclamatory <code>とは</code> treated
in Contrast below.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
エコ<b>とは</b>、環境(かんきょう)にやさしいという意味(いみ)だ。
"Eco" means environmentally friendly.
</pre>

<pre>
時差(じさ)ぼけ<b>とは</b>、海外(かいがい)旅行(りょこう)のあとで眠(ねむ)れなくなることだ。
Jet lag is when you can't sleep properly after travelling abroad.
</pre>

<pre>
過疎(かそ)<b>とは</b>、地域(ちいき)の人口(じんこう)が大(おお)きく減(へ)った状態(じょうたい)を指(さ)す。
Depopulation refers to a state in which a region's population has fallen sharply.
</pre>

<pre>
幸(しあわ)せ<b>とは</b>何(なに)か、一言(ひとこと)では答(こた)えられない。
What happiness is cannot be answered in a single word.
</pre>

<pre>
働(はたら)く<b>とは</b>ただお金(かね)をもらうことではないと、父(ちち)はよく言(い)っていた。
My father often said that working is not simply a matter of being paid.
</pre>

<pre>
本書(ほんしょ)で言(い)う「市民(しみん)」<b>とは</b>、この市(し)に住民票(じゅうみんひょう)のある人(ひと)を指(さ)す。
"Citizen" in this book means a person registered as a resident of this city.
</pre>

<pre>
契約書(けいやくしょ)の「速(すみ)やかに」<b>とは</b>、具体(ぐたい)的(てき)に何日(なんにち)以内(いない)を意味(いみ)しますか。
In the contract, what number of days does "promptly" actually mean?
</pre>

<pre>
人工(じんこう)知能(ちのう)<b>とは</b>、人間(にんげん)の判断(はんだん)を機械(きかい)に置(お)き換(か)えようとする技術(ぎじゅつ)の総称(そうしょう)である。
Artificial intelligence is a blanket term for technologies that attempt to replace human judgement with machines.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Definitional <code>とは</code> is **written and expository**. Its home is
dictionaries, textbooks, contracts, glossaries, opening paragraphs of reports and
the first slide of a presentation - anywhere a term has to be pinned down before
the discussion can start. It pairs naturally with である and と定義(ていぎ)する,
and a whole title can consist of nothing else: 「教育(きょういく)とは」.

In speech the same job is done by <code>というのは</code>, and casually by
`って`: 「エコってどういう意味(いみ)？」 Using <code>とは</code> in that
sentence would sound like reading aloud from a manual. Conversely,
<code>というのは</code> in a written definition is not wrong, only looser and
wordier; academic prose prefers the tighter <code>とは</code>.

One habit worth copying: Japanese writers very often close a
<code>とは</code> definition with のことだ or を指(さ)す rather than a plain
です. のことだ says "this expression points at this"; a bare です risks reading as
an ordinary statement about the world rather than a statement about a word.

## Contrast

### とは vs というのは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different register. <code>とは</code> is the
compressed written form; <code>というのは</code> is how you say it out loud, and
`って` is its casual reduction.

</div>

<pre>
内需(ないじゅ)<b>とは</b>、国内(こくない)での需要(じゅよう)のことである。
Domestic demand means demand inside the country.
</pre>

<pre>
内需(ないじゅ)<b>というのは</b>、国内(こくない)での需要(じゅよう)という意味(いみ)ですよ。
"Domestic demand" - that just means demand within the country.
</pre>

The first would sit in a textbook; the second is a colleague explaining it to you
at a desk. There is a second difference besides register:
<code>というのは</code> also does ordinary "the reason is" work
（遅(おく)れたというのは、事故(じこ)があったからです）, and
<code>とは</code> cannot do that at all.

### とは vs は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` raises the **thing**. <code>とは</code> raises the
**word for the thing**, and commits you to explaining it.

</div>

<pre>
クジラ<b>は</b>海(うみ)にすむ哺乳類(ほにゅうるい)だ。
Whales are mammals that live in the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
クジラ<b>とは</b>、海(うみ)にすむ大型(おおがた)の哺乳類(ほにゅうるい)の総称(そうしょう)である。
"Whale" is a general term for large mammals that live in the sea.
</pre>

Both sentences are true and both are natural, but they are aimed at different
readers. The first tells you about whales; the second tells you how the word is
used, and belongs in an encyclopedia entry. Where a sentence is plainly about the
world rather than about vocabulary - 「今日(きょう)は寒(さむ)い」 -
<code>とは</code> is simply not available.

### とは (defining) vs とは (surprise)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same two characters do a second, unrelated job. If what
precedes <code>とは</code> is a **term**, it is a definition. If it is a whole
**event**, it is the exclamatory <code>とは</code>: "to think that…".

</div>

<pre>
まさか彼(かれ)が優勝(ゆうしょう)する<b>とは</b>思(おも)わなかった。
I never imagined he would actually win it.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)<b>とは</b>、その大会(たいかい)で一位(いちい)になることだ。
"Winning the championship" means finishing first in that tournament.
</pre>

Three signals separate them. The exclamatory one takes a full clause (often with
まさか in front), frequently keeps `だ` before it
（本当(ほんとう)だとは）, and either ends in
驚(おどろ)いた・思(おも)わなかった or trails off unfinished with nothing after
it at all. The defining one takes a bare term and always has a predicate waiting
at the end.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)<b>とは</b>難(むずか)しいです。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)<b>は</b>難(むずか)しいです。
  <code>とは</code> announces a definition, so an adjective predicate leaves the
  reader waiting for the rest. （The aphoristic
  <code>～とは…ものだ</code> - 人生(じんせい)とは不思議(ふしぎ)なものだ -
  is a separate, deliberately weighty use; it is not the everyday です sentence.）

- ❌ 過疎(かそ)<b>だとは</b>、人口(じんこう)が減(へ)ることだ。 → ✅ 過疎(かそ)<b>とは</b>、人口(じんこう)が減(へ)ることだ。
  The term is quoted bare. Inserting `だ` switches the sentence to the exclamatory
  reading, which then makes no sense with のことだ after it.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)<b>とは</b>とても親切(しんせつ)な人(ひと)です。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>は</b>とても親切(しんせつ)な人(ひと)です。
  A person is not a term. Left as written, the と reads as the "with" particle -
  「彼(かれ)とは去年(きょねん)会(あ)いました」 means "him I met last
  year", which is a different sentence entirely.

- ❌ 「エコ」<b>というとは</b>、環境(かんきょう)のことです。 → ✅ 「エコ」<b>とは</b> / 「エコ」<b>というのは</b>、環境(かんきょう)のことです。
  The two frames are alternatives, not building blocks. Pick one; ❌というとは does
  not exist.

## Practice

**1.** 再生(さいせい)可能(かのう)エネルギー____、太陽光(たいようこう)や風力(ふうりょく)などの自然(しぜん)の力(ちから)を使(つか)った電気(でんき)のことだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

再生(さいせい)可能(かのう)エネルギー<b>とは</b>

The sentence closes with のことだ, which is the tell: a term is being defined, and
the frame that opens such a sentence is <code>とは</code> with no `だ` in front of
it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この町(まち)（は・とは）昔(むかし)、港町(みなとまち)でした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この町(まち)<b>は</b>昔(むかし)、港町(みなとまち)でした。

This is a statement about a place, not about the meaning of an expression, so the
plain topic marker is the only option. <code>とは</code> would promise a
definition of the phrase "this town", which is not something a language defines.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "In this report, 'young people' means those aged from fifteen to twenty-nine."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この報告書(ほうこくしょ)で言(い)う「若者(わかもの)」<b>とは</b>、15歳(さい)から29歳(さい)までの人(ひと)を指(さ)す。

The quotation marks around the term and the closing を指(さ)す are both
standard furniture for a written definition, and this "as used in this document"
framing is exactly where <code>とは</code> earns its keep.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 留学(りゅうがく)というとは、外国(がいこく)の学校(がっこう)で学(まな)ぶことです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 留学(りゅうがく)<b>とは</b>、外国(がいこく)の学校(がっこう)で学(まな)ぶことです。
（or 留学(りゅうがく)<b>というのは</b>、…）

<code>とは</code> and <code>というのは</code> are two ways of doing the same job,
and they cannot be stacked. In a written definition the shorter one is the better
fit anyway.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 合格(ごうかく)した<b>とは</b>知(し)りませんでした。
(b) 「合格(ごうかく)」<b>とは</b>、基準(きじゅん)を満(み)たしたという意味(いみ)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the exclamatory <code>とは</code>. A whole event - somebody passing - is
quoted, and the speaker reports being surprised or uninformed about it. Nothing is
being defined.

(b) is the definitional <code>とは</code>. A single word is quoted, in brackets,
and the predicate explains what that word covers. The giveaway in each case is
what sits in front of とは: a clause in (a), a bare term in (b).

</details>

# ～ときたら

**Meaning** · "as for X - honestly!" - drags a topic into the open in order to complain about it

<code>ときたら</code> is a topic marker with a mood attached. It picks out a
person, a thing or a stretch of time and hands it straight to a grumble: the
listener knows before the comment arrives that it is going to be a criticism. It
is not a neutral "speaking of"; the exasperation is part of the grammar, not the
tone of voice.

**This is a recognise-only pattern.** Read on for where it turns up and what to
say instead.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person) | Noun + ときたら | 弟(おとうと) → 弟(おとうと)ときたら |
| Noun (thing or organisation) | Noun + ときたら | この会社(かいしゃ) → この会社(かいしゃ)ときたら |
| Noun (period or weather) | Noun + ときたら | 今年(ことし)の夏(なつ) → 今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)ときたら |
| Stiffer written variant | Noun + ときては | あの値段(ねだん) → あの値段(ねだん)ときては |
| Casual living equivalent | Noun + ったら | 弟(おとうと) → 弟(おとうと)ったら |
| Plain modern equivalent | Noun + は … 本当(ほんとう)に／まったく | 弟(おとうと) → 弟(おとうと)は本当(ほんとう)に… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - it attaches to a **noun** only. There is no
❌<code>来(こ)ないときたら</code>; a clause has to be nominalised first, or the
sentence rebuilt around は.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
うちの犬(いぬ)<b>ときたら</b>、お客(きゃく)さんが来(く)るたびに吠(ほ)える。
That dog of ours - it barks every single time we have a visitor.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>ときたら</b>、部屋(へや)の片(かた)づけ一(ひと)つしない。
My brother won't so much as tidy his own room.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)<b>ときたら</b>、夜(よる)になっても気温(きおん)が下(さ)がらない。
This summer - the temperature doesn't even drop at night.
</pre>

<pre>
あの課長(かちょう)<b>ときたら</b>、自分(じぶん)のミスまで人(ひと)のせいにする。
That section chief of his - he blames other people even for his own mistakes.
</pre>

<pre>
「うちの亭主(ていしゅ)<b>ときたら</b>、休(やす)みの日(ひ)は一日中(いちにちじゅう)寝(ね)てばかりでね」
"My husband, honestly - on his days off he does nothing but sleep."
</pre>

<pre>
昭和(しょうわ)の映画(えいが)に出(で)てくる大家(おおや)<b>ときたら</b>、たいてい家賃(やちん)の話(はなし)しかしない。
The landlords who turn up in Showa-era films never seem to talk about anything but the rent.
</pre>

<pre>
近頃(ちかごろ)の若(わか)い者(もの)<b>ときたら</b>、という書(か)き出(だ)しの文章(ぶんしょう)は千年(せんねん)前(まえ)からある。
Essays opening with "young people these days, honestly" have been around for a thousand years.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)のあの店(みせ)<b>ときたら</b>、値段(ねだん)は高(たか)いし待(ま)たされるしで、もう二度(にど)と行(い)かない。
That place by the station - expensive and slow, and I'm never setting foot in it again.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Where you will actually meet this.** <code>ときたら</code> lives in written
grumbling and in fiction: newspaper column complaints, essays, comic monologue,
manga and drama dialogue, and the speech of older characters. Real speakers do use
it, but it carries an audible "here comes a rant" flavour that makes it sound
staged. A learner who deploys it in conversation will sound as though they are
reading a line rather than talking, and used about a colleague or a client it is
openly rude. **Recognise it; say <code>は</code> with
本当(ほんとう)に or まったく, or the casual <code>ったら</code>, instead.**

The complaint is not optional. Whatever follows <code>ときたら</code> is
negative, or is praise so exaggerated that it reads as a joke - the "listen to
this" frame is doing the work. Because of that, the second half of the sentence
usually ends on a grievance shape: ～ばかりだ, ～しかしない, ～てばかりいる,
～のだから困(こま)る.

There is a mild self-deprecating use - 「私(わたし)ときたら、また傘(かさ)を
忘(わす)れた」 - where the speaker complains about themselves. That is friendly
rather than aggressive, but the register is unchanged: it still sounds like a line
of dialogue.

The variant <code>ときては</code> is stiffer and more written; it also shades
towards "given that it is X", which is not quite the same complaint.

## Contrast

### ときたら vs は (the modern equivalent)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - everything <code>ときたら</code> does can be done with plain
`は` plus an adverb of exasperation. That is the version to produce.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>ときたら</b>、約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)を守(まも)ったことがない。
My brother - he has never once been on time.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>は</b>まったく、約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)を守(まも)ったことがない。
My brother really has never once been on time.
</pre>

The content is identical. The first announces the complaint in the grammar and
sounds like narration; the second lets まったく carry the feeling and is what a
person says at a table. In writing where you want the topic and nothing more, plain
`は` is also the only safe choice - a report cannot grumble.

### ときたら vs ったら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ったら</code> is the living colloquial version of the
same move: still exasperated, but warm, and it does not sound like a stage line.

</div>

<pre>
お母(かあ)さん<b>ったら</b>、また同(おな)じ話(はなし)をしている。
Mum, honestly - she's telling that same story again.
</pre>

<pre>
お母(かあ)さん<b>ときたら</b>、また同(おな)じ話(はなし)をしている。
My mother - there she goes with that story again.
</pre>

<code>ったら</code> is affectionate as often as it is annoyed, and it is normal
between family and friends. <code>ときたら</code> tilts further towards genuine
criticism and towards performance. If you want one of the two in your own speech,
take <code>ったら</code>.

### ときたら vs に至(いた)っては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both single out one item and say something worse about it.
<code>ときたら</code> is spoken grumbling; <code>に至(いた)っては</code> is
written escalation after a list.

</div>

<pre>
各店(かくてん)とも売(う)り上(あ)げが落(お)ちたが、地方(ちほう)店(てん)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>前年(ぜんねん)の半分(はんぶん)である。
Sales fell at every branch; in the regional stores they were half of the previous year.
</pre>

<pre>
地方(ちほう)の店(みせ)<b>ときたら</b>、客(きゃく)より店員(てんいん)のほうが多(おお)い。
The regional shops - there are more staff in them than customers.
</pre>

<code>に至(いた)っては</code> needs a comparison set already on the table and
belongs to reports and news copy; it makes no emotional claim beyond "this is the
extreme case". <code>ときたら</code> needs no list, and its whole point is the
speaker's irritation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 旅行(りょこう)<b>ときたら</b>、どこへ行(い)きたい？ → ✅ 旅行(りょこう)<b>といえば</b>、どこへ行(い)きたい？
  <code>ときたら</code> is not a neutral "speaking of". Used to open a friendly
  topic it lands as an accusation about the topic itself.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ない<b>ときたら</b>、困(こま)る。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>ときたら</b>、いつも遅(おく)れてくる。
  It attaches to a noun, not to a clause. There is no conditional "if" reading
  here, despite the たら.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)<b>ときたら</b>、いつも的確(てきかく)な指示(しじ)をくださいます。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)<b>は</b>いつも的確(てきかく)な指示(しじ)をくださいます。
  The frame promises a complaint, so following it with praise and honorifics
  reads as sarcasm. It is also not a form to use about a
  superior at all.

- ❌ お客様(きゃくさま)のご注文(ちゅうもん)<b>ときたら</b>、来週(らいしゅう)到着(とうちゃく)の予定(よてい)です。 → ✅ お客様(きゃくさま)のご注文(ちゅうもん)<b>は</b>、来週(らいしゅう)到着(とうちゃく)の予定(よてい)です。
  In business writing the pattern is simply unusable. Anything you put after it
  becomes a complaint about the customer.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence: 「うちの上司(じょうし)ときたら、会議(かいぎ)のたびに同(おな)じ話(はなし)を繰(く)り返(かえ)す」? (a) an internal company report (b) a comic essay or a manga panel (c) a job interview

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) a comic essay or a manga panel.

<code>ときたら</code> announces a grumble, so it fits writing that is meant to be
entertaining. In a 報告書(ほうこくしょ) it would be unprofessional, and in an
interview it would be self-destructive.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 最近(さいきん)の電車(でんしゃ)ときたら、朝(あさ)も夜(よる)も混(こ)んでいる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

最近(さいきん)の電車(でんしゃ)<b>は</b>本当(ほんとう)に、朝(あさ)も夜(よる)も混(こ)んでいる。
（casual: 最近(さいきん)の電車(でんしゃ)<b>って</b>、朝(あさ)も夜(よる)も混(こ)んでるよね）

Move the exasperation out of the grammar and into an adverb such as
本当(ほんとう)に or まったく. The meaning survives intact and the sentence stops
sounding like narration.

</details>

**3.** Choose: この夏(なつ)（ときたら・といえば）花火(はなび)大会(たいかい)に行(い)きたいね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この夏(なつ)<b>といえば</b>花火(はなび)大会(たいかい)に行(い)きたいね。

The sentence is a cheerful suggestion, and <code>ときたら</code> can only
introduce a complaint. Put it in and the listener waits for you to say what is
wrong with the summer.

</details>

**4.** Find the register error: メールの本文(ほんぶん)「貴社(きしゃ)のご担当(たんとう)者(しゃ)ときたら、ご連絡(れんらく)が遅(おそ)いようです。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、ご担当(たんとう)者(しゃ)<b>から</b>のご連絡(れんらく)をお待(ま)ちしております。

<code>ときたら</code> is grumbling aimed at a third party, so addressing a client's
staff with it is close to an insult, and wrapping it in honorific language makes it
worse rather than softer. Business complaints are made
indirectly, without naming a culprit.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この町(まち)の冬(ふゆ)<b>は</b>風(かぜ)が強(つよ)い。
(b) この町(まち)の冬(ふゆ)<b>ときたら</b>、風(かぜ)が強(つよ)くて外(そと)を歩(ある)けない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of fact about the local climate. It could open a guidebook
or a weather report, and the speaker's feelings about the wind are not part of it.

(b) is a complaint dressed as a topic. The frame tells the listener in advance that
the speaker is fed up, and the sentence belongs to spoken grumbling or to a
first-person essay - not to anything factual or formal.

</details>

# ～ってば

**Meaning** · "I *said*…" - repeats something the speaker has already said, with visible impatience

<code>ってば</code> is the casual quotative `って` with an emphatic `ば` stuck on
the end. It re-delivers a statement the listener has failed to accept, act on or
hear, and the frustration is grammatical rather than a matter of tone. A second use
hangs the same impatience on a person's name - お母(かあ)さんってば - and means
"honestly, that woman".

**This is a recognise-only pattern.** You will meet it constantly in dialogue; it
is not something to reach for yourself.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | plain form + ってば | 行(い)く → 行(い)くってば |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ってば | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないってば |
| Request or command | て-form + ってば | 待(ま)って → 待(ま)ってってば |
| い-adjective | plain form + ってば | いい → いいってば |
| な-adjective / Noun | ～だってば | 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ) → 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だってば |
| Name or noun as exasperated topic | Noun + ってば | お母(かあ)さん → お母(かあ)さんってば |
| Neutral spoken equivalent | ～と言(い)ってるでしょ / だから～だよ | 行(い)く → だから行(い)くって言(い)ってるでしょ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - after a noun or な-adjective the `だ` is often dropped in fast speech
（大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ってば）. Both are heard; neither is more correct than the
other.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
違(ちが)う<b>ってば</b>。
No - I *said* that's not it.
</pre>

<pre>
もう大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だ<b>ってば</b>。心配(しんぱい)しないで。
I told you, I'm fine. Stop worrying.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)く起(お)きて<b>ってば</b>。遅刻(ちこく)するよ。
Get up, I said. You're going to be late.
</pre>

<pre>
待(ま)って<b>ってば</b>、置(お)いていかないで。
Wait - I said wait. Don't leave me behind.
</pre>

<pre>
お兄(にい)ちゃん<b>ってば</b>、また私(わたし)のプリンを食(た)べたでしょ。
My brother - honestly. He's eaten my pudding again, hasn't he.
</pre>

<pre>
だから、その話(はなし)は聞(き)いてない<b>ってば</b>。誰(だれ)にも言(い)われてないよ。
Look, I've told you, I haven't heard about it. Nobody said a word to me.
</pre>

<pre>
少年(しょうねん)は「僕(ぼく)がやったんじゃない<b>ってば</b>」と何度(なんど)も繰(く)り返(かえ)した。
The boy kept repeating, "I keep telling you, it wasn't me."
</pre>

<pre>
「いい<b>ってば</b>、自分(じぶん)で持(も)てるから」と彼女(かのじょ)は荷物(にもつ)を抱(かか)え直(なお)した。
"It's fine, I said - I can carry it," she said, shifting the bags in her arms.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Where you will actually meet this.** <code>ってば</code> is spoken Japanese at
its most informal, and in print it appears almost exclusively as dialogue - manga,
drama scripts, novels, subtitles. It is used between family members, children,
close friends and couples, and it presumes that kind of closeness. Two things make
it unsafe to produce. It is **petulant** by design: the speaker is saying "you are
not listening to me". And it tends to be heard as **childish or sulky**, which is
exactly why writers give it to young characters. **Recognise it; in your own
Japanese say だから～って言(い)ってるでしょ in casual company, or
～と申(もう)し上(あ)げたはずですが in a formal one.**

The pattern needs a prior statement. Said out of nowhere it is incoherent, because
the whole of its meaning is "again, and I am annoyed about it". This is why it so
often follows だから or もう, which mark the repetition explicitly.

There is an affectionate side to it - a friend saying
「いいってば、気(き)にしないで」 is brushing off thanks, not snapping. But the
line between playful and rude is drawn by the relationship, not by the words, and a
learner does not usually have enough of the relationship in the bank to risk it.
Aimed upwards at a teacher, a manager or a customer it is plainly rude, and adding
ます to the verb in front does not rescue it.

## Contrast

### ってば vs だから～って言(い)ってるでしょ (the modern equivalent)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the neutral way to repeat yourself is to *say* that you are
repeating yourself, rather than to encode it in a particle.

</div>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)はかけた<b>ってば</b>。
I *told* you I locked it.
</pre>

<pre>
だから、鍵(かぎ)はかけたって<b>言(い)ってるでしょ</b>。
Look, I'm telling you, I locked it.
</pre>

Both are casual, and the second is still not something to say to your boss - but it
is ordinary adult speech rather than a sulk. In a polite setting the whole strategy
changes: 「先(さき)ほどお伝(つた)えしたとおり、施錠(せじょう)は済(す)んでおります」.
Japanese handles "I already said this" upwards by re-stating politely, never by
adding force.

### ってば vs ったら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two overlap heavily. <code>ってば</code> leans on **what
was said**; <code>ったら</code> leans on **the person** who will not listen.

</div>

<pre>
待(ま)って<b>ってば</b>。
I said wait.
</pre>

<pre>
健太(けんた)<b>ったら</b>、人(ひと)の話(はなし)を全然(ぜんぜん)聞(き)かない。
Kenta, honestly - he never listens to a word anyone says.
</pre>

In the shared "exasperated topic" use（お母(かあ)さんってば / お母(かあ)さんったら）
the difference is thin enough that either will pass. Where they part company is the
repeated-statement use, which is <code>ってば</code>'s core and only a sideline for
<code>ったら</code>.

### ってば vs って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `って` is a neutral casual quotative and topic marker.
Adding `ば` adds nothing to the content and everything to the mood.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みだ<b>って</b>。
Apparently tomorrow's a day off.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みだ<b>ってば</b>。
Tomorrow is a day off - I've told you.
</pre>

The first passes on information, possibly heard from someone else, and is
completely unremarkable in casual speech. The second can only be a repetition
aimed at someone who did not take it in the first time. `って` is worth using;
`ってば` is worth recognising.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、資料(しりょう)は送(おく)りました<b>ってば</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、資料(しりょう)は先(さき)ほどお送(おく)りいたしました。
  Politeness on the verb does not neutralise the particle. To a superior this reads
  as "how many times do I have to tell you".

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)だ<b>ってば</b>。（opening a conversation） → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)だよ。
  The pattern requires something already said. With no prior statement the listener
  is left wondering what they missed.

- ❌ 日本(にほん)<b>ってば</b>島国(しまぐに)です。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)<b>は</b>島国(しまぐに)です。
  It is not a general topic marker. Attached to a neutral topic it turns a fact
  into a complaint about that topic.

- ❌ ご不明(ふめい)な点(てん)はお問(と)い合(あ)わせください<b>ってば</b>。 → ✅ ご不明(ふめい)な点(てん)はお問(と)い合(あ)わせください。
  Written Japanese has no use for it outside quoted dialogue. In a notice or an
  email it is simply not a possible ending.

## Practice

**1.** In which of these would 「いいってば」 be natural? (a) a friend keeps trying to pay for your coffee (b) a customer asks whether a refund is possible (c) a written apology to a client

(a) a friend keeps trying to pay for your coffee.

<code>ってば</code> needs an earlier statement being repeated - you have already
said no once - and it needs a close relationship. (b) and (c) are both aimed at
someone outside your circle, where the form is rude.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in neutral casual Japanese: 宿題(しゅくだい)はもう終(お)わったってば。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

だから、宿題(しゅくだい)はもう終(お)わったって言(い)ってるでしょ。
（or simply 宿題(しゅくだい)はもう終(お)わったよ）

Spell the repetition out with だから and って言(い)ってる instead of loading it

</details>

田中(たなか)さんは来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)だ<b>って</b>。

</details>

**4.** Find the error: 先生(せんせい)に「宿題(しゅくだい)は出(だ)しましたってば」と言(い)った。

# ～に至っては

**Meaning** · "and as for X…" - after a general statement, singles out the one case that is furthest gone

<code>に至(いた)っては</code> does not simply change the topic. It assumes a
situation has already been described - prices are up, attendance is down, several
branches are struggling - and then names the member of that set where the problem
reaches its extreme. The sentence that follows is the punchline: worse, later,
fewer, more absurd.

## Formation

The clause in front is a noun, so what goes there is the item being singled out,
not a whole event.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person, place, group) | Noun + に至(いた)っては | 新入生(しんにゅうせい) → 新入生(しんにゅうせい)に至(いた)っては |
| Noun (time period) | Noun + に至(いた)っては | 今年(ことし) → 今年(ことし)に至(いた)っては |
| Modified noun | …Noun + に至(いた)っては | 被害(ひがい)の大(おお)きい地区(ちく) → 被害(ひがい)の大(おお)きい地区(ちく)に至(いた)っては |
| Clause, via もの / 人(ひと) / 場合(ばあい) | …もの + に至(いた)っては | 届(とど)かなかった → 届(とど)かなかったものに至(いた)っては |
| Polite sentence | ～に至(いた)っては…です／ました | 地方(ちほう)店(てん)に至(いた)っては半分(はんぶん)です |
| Set phrase | 事(こと)ここに至(いた)っては | - → 事(こと)ここに至(いた)っては仕方(しかた)がない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading is <b>いたって</b>, from 至(いた)る "to reach". Related
but different: <code>に至(いた)るまで</code> "all the way down to", and
<code>に至(いた)っても</code> "even after it has come to this".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
野菜(やさい)が高(たか)い。トマト<b>に至(いた)っては</b>去年(きょねん)の2倍(ばい)だ。
Vegetables are expensive. Tomatoes are twice what they cost last year.
</pre>

<pre>
どの店(みせ)も客(きゃく)が少(すく)ない。平日(へいじつ)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>数人(すうにん)しか来(こ)ない。
None of the shops are busy. On weekdays only a handful of people come in at all.
</pre>

<pre>
残業(ざんぎょう)が増(ふ)え、営業(えいぎょう)部(ぶ)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>毎晩(まいばん)十時(じゅうじ)過(す)ぎまで働(はたら)いている。
Overtime is up, and in sales they are working past ten every night.
</pre>

<pre>
回答(かいとう)率(りつ)は全体(ぜんたい)で三割(さんわり)、20代(だい)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>一割(いちわり)を下回(したまわ)った。
The response rate was thirty percent overall, and among people in their twenties it fell below ten.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)は不十分(ふじゅうぶん)で、費用(ひよう)の内訳(うちわけ)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>一切(いっさい)示(しめ)されなかった。
The explanation was inadequate, and as for a breakdown of the costs, none was given at all.
</pre>

<pre>
会員(かいいん)の多(おお)くが規則(きそく)を知(し)らず、新入生(しんにゅうせい)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>そんな規則(きそく)があること自体(じたい)を知(し)らなかった。
Most members were unaware of the rule, and the first-years did not even know such a rule existed.
</pre>

<pre>
各(かく)路線(ろせん)で遅(おく)れが出(で)ており、山間(さんかん)部(ぶ)を走(はし)る区間(くかん)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>終日(しゅうじつ)運転(うんてん)見合(みあ)わせとなった。
Delays hit every line, and on the mountain sections services were suspended for the whole day.
</pre>

<pre>
市(し)の対応(たいおう)は遅(おそ)く、被害(ひがい)の大(おお)きかった地区(ちく)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>、一(いっ)か月(げつ)以上(いじょう)連絡(れんらく)がなかったという。
The city was slow to respond, and in the worst-hit districts there was reportedly no contact for over a month.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **written and formal**. It belongs to news copy, reports, editorials,
academic prose and the sort of speech that is delivered from notes - a board
meeting, a council session, a documentary voice-over. In everyday conversation it
sounds like reading a newspaper aloud; there, 特(とく)に or plain `は` does the
job.

Two conditions have to be met before it works. First, a **general statement must
already be on the table**: the pattern is the second half of a two-step move, and
put first it has nothing to be extreme relative to. Second, the comment is
**negative or startling**. Reports use it for the worst branch, the lowest figure,
the longest delay; a mild compliment after it feels like a joke that did not land.
When it does introduce something good, the effect is astonishment rather than
praise - the case that broke the pattern.

Two neighbours worth keeping apart. <code>に至(いた)っても</code> means "even
after things had come to that point"
（締(し)め切(き)り当日(とうじつ)に至(いた)っても返事(へんじ)がない）,
and <code>に至(いた)るまで</code> means "all the way down to"
（文字(もじ)の大(おお)きさに至(いた)るまで指定(してい)されている）.

Finally, the frozen phrase 事(こと)ここに至(いた)っては - "now that it has
come to this" - is a different, older use, meaning the situation has reached a
stage where only one course is left. It is common in fiction and in solemn speech,
and it does not single out one member of a set.

## Contrast

### に至(いた)っては vs は / はというと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` shifts the topic. <code>に至(いた)っては</code> shifts
the topic **and claims this one is the extreme case**.

</div>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)の店(みせ)<b>は</b>売(う)り上(あ)げが伸(の)びた。大阪(おおさか)の店(みせ)<b>は</b>横(よこ)ばいだった。
The Tokyo shop's sales grew. The Osaka shop's were flat.
</pre>

<pre>
どの店(みせ)も売(う)り上(あ)げを落(お)としたが、大阪(おおさか)の店(みせ)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>前年(ぜんねん)の半分(はんぶん)だった。
Every shop lost sales, and the Osaka shop's were half those of the year before.
</pre>

The `は` version merely lists. The second version ranks: the reader is told that
Osaka is where the trend goes furthest, and would be misled if a worse branch were
mentioned afterwards. This is also why a report can contain many `は` topics but
usually only one <code>に至(いた)っては</code> per point.

### に至(いた)っては vs 特(とく)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 特(とく)に is an adverb meaning "especially" and fits any
register. <code>に至(いた)っては</code> is a formal topic frame that also
implies a **scale running from bad to worse**.

</div>

<pre>
今週(こんしゅう)は忙(いそが)しく、<b>特(とく)に</b>水曜日(すいようび)は会議(かいぎ)が三(みっ)つもあった。
This week was busy - Wednesday especially, with three meetings.
</pre>

<pre>
今週(こんしゅう)は会議(かいぎ)が続(つづ)き、水曜日(すいようび)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>昼食(ちゅうしょく)をとる時間(じかん)もなかった。
Meetings ran all week, and by Wednesday there was not even time to eat lunch.
</pre>

特(とく)に can be said to a friend and can precede good news as easily as bad.
Swap it into the second sentence and you lose nothing but the sense of a
progression reaching its limit; swap <code>に至(いた)っては</code> into the
first and the sentence becomes oddly grave about a Wednesday.

### に至(いた)っては vs に至(いた)っても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same verb, one particle apart. `ては` picks out the
**extreme member of a set**; `ても` says that **even at that late stage** nothing
had changed.

</div>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)い部署(ぶしょ)が多(おお)く、総務(そうむ)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>二週間(にしゅうかん)音沙汰(おとさた)がない。
Several departments are slow to reply, and from general affairs there has been nothing for a fortnight.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)り当日(とうじつ)<b>に至(いた)っても</b>、原稿(げんこう)は届(とど)かなかった。
Even when the day of the deadline arrived, the manuscript had still not come.
</pre>

The first compares departments; the second compares points in time and expresses
frustration that the situation survived them all. If you can insert "even by then"
into the English, you want `ても`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さん<b>に至(いた)っては</b>親切(しんせつ)です。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは<b>特(とく)に</b>親切(しんせつ)です。
  Two faults at once: nothing has been said for Tanaka to be an extreme case of,
  and the comment is a plain compliment. The pattern needs a series and a sting.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れる<b>に至(いた)っては</b>困(こま)る。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れた人(ひと)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>連絡(れんらく)もしてこなかった。
  It attaches to a noun. A clause has to be turned into one with 人(ひと),
  もの, 場合(ばあい) or similar first.

- ❌ 地方(ちほう)店(てん)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>半分(はんぶん)だ。どの店(みせ)も売(う)り上(あ)げが落(お)ちた。 → ✅ どの店(みせ)も売(う)り上(あ)げが落(お)ちた。地方(ちほう)店(てん)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>半分(はんぶん)だ。
  Order matters. The general statement comes first; the extreme case is the
  follow-up, never the opening.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)の飲(の)み会(かい)、田中(たなか)さん<b>に至(いた)っては</b>寝(ね)ちゃってさ。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)の飲(の)み会(かい)、田中(たなか)さんなんて寝(ね)ちゃってさ。
  The content is fine; the register is not. Among friends the formal frame sounds
  like a news bulletin about a drinking party.

## Practice

**1.** 今年(ことし)はどの学部(がくぶ)も志願(しがん)者(しゃ)が減(へ)った。文学部(ぶんがくぶ)____、定員(ていいん)を割(わ)り込(こ)んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

文学部(ぶんがくぶ)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>

The first sentence establishes a general decline, and the second names the faculty
where it went furthest - below the intake quota. That two-step shape is precisely
what the pattern is for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 週末(しゅうまつ)は楽(たの)しかった。（特(とく)に・に至(いた)っては）日曜日(にちようび)の花火(はなび)がよかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>特(とく)に</b>日曜日(にちようび)の花火(はなび)がよかった。

The comment is positive and the register is conversational, so the adverb is the
fit. <code>に至(いた)っては</code> would imply a scale of increasing badness and
would sound absurdly solemn about fireworks.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"Across the board" supplies the general statement, and the evening session is the
extreme case, so the frame slots straight in. 下回(したまわ)る keeps the

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)が長引(ながび)くに至(いた)っては、終電(しゅうでん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)が長引(ながび)き、終電(しゅうでん)の時刻(じこく)<b>に至(いた)っても</b>議論(ぎろん)は続(つづ)いていた。

Two repairs. The pattern cannot take a plain verb clause, and the intended meaning
here is "even by that point", which is `ても`, not `ては`. As written, the original
promises to single out an extreme member of a set that was never introduced.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 地方(ちほう)の支店(してん)<b>は</b>人手(ひとで)が足(た)りない。
(b) 地方(ちほう)の支店(してん)<b>に至(いた)っては</b>人手(ひとで)が足(た)りない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a free-standing statement about staffing at regional branches. It can open a
paragraph and makes no claim about anywhere else.

(b) cannot stand alone. It says that staffing is a problem generally and that the
regional branches are the worst of it, so the reader will feel something is missing
unless the wider situation has already been described. It is also distinctly more
formal.

</details>

# ～に言わせれば

**Meaning** · "if you ask X"; "in X's book" - hands the floor to someone and reports the verdict they would give

The shape is the causative 言(い)わせる, "let someone speak", in its
conditional form: *if we let X have their say*. What follows is therefore
presented as **X's own way of putting it**, not as neutral fact. With
私(わたし) in front it is the Japanese for "if you ask me", complete with the
implication that the people who have already spoken got it wrong.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Person (noun or pronoun) | Noun + に言(い)わせれば | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)に言(い)わせれば |
| Role or group | Noun + に言(い)わせれば | 専門家(せんもんか) → 専門家(せんもんか)に言(い)わせれば |
| Variants | ～に言(い)わせると / ～に言(い)わせたら | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)に言(い)わせると |
| Humble, formal | ～に言(い)わせていただければ | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)に言(い)わせていただければ |
| Underlying form | 言(い)う → 使役(しえき) 言(い)わせる → 条件(じょうけん) 言(い)わせれば | 言(い)う → 言(い)わせれば |
| Usual closers (third person) | …そうだ / …という / …らしい | 彼(かれ)に言(い)わせれば…なのだそうだ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the person takes `に`, because in a causative sentence the one made to
act is marked with `に`. ❌<code>私(わたし)が言(い)わせれば</code> is the
standard slip and means nothing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、それはただの言(い)い訳(わけ)だ。
If you ask me, that's just an excuse.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、私(わたし)はいつまでも子供(こども)だ。
In my mother's book I will be a child forever.
</pre>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、この数字(すうじ)はまだ安全(あんぜん)な範囲(はんい)らしい。
The specialists would say these figures are still within a safe range.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)<b>に言(い)わせると</b>、父親(ちちおや)の服(ふく)はどれも古(ふる)くさいのだそうだ。
According to my son, everything his father wears is hopelessly out of date.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、定時(ていじ)で帰(かえ)るのは仕事(しごと)への情熱(じょうねつ)がない証拠(しょうこ)なのだという。
As he tells it, leaving on time is proof that you have no passion for the work.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、問題(もんだい)は予算(よさん)ではなく優先(ゆうせん)順位(じゅんい)の付(つ)け方(かた)にある。
The way I see it, the problem isn't the budget but how the priorities are set.
</pre>

<pre>
現場(げんば)の職人(しょくにん)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、図面(ずめん)どおりに作(つく)ると決(き)まって使(つか)いにくい物(もの)ができるらしい。
Ask the people on site and they'll tell you that building strictly to the drawings always produces something awkward to use.
</pre>

<pre>
失礼(しつれい)ながら私(わたし)<b>に言(い)わせていただければ</b>、この計画(けいかく)は準備(じゅんび)不足(ぶそく)のまま走(はし)り出(だ)しております。
If I may be permitted to say so, this project has been launched without adequate preparation.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **assertive**, and that is its whole point. With 私(わたし) it
signals that the speaker is departing from what has just been said or from what is
generally assumed: an opinion column, a debate, a discussion that has got heated, a
friend disagreeing with the received view. It is not rude in itself, but it is not
neutral either - a listener hears a correction coming.

With a third person it works as a small piece of reported perspective, and it
usually carries a light distance: the speaker is showing you how that person
frames things without signing up to it. That is why the sentence so often closes
with そうだ, という or らしい. Said about someone present, this distance turns into
something close to talking over their head, so it is a poor choice for
「部長(ぶちょう)に言(い)わせれば…」 spoken to 部長(ぶちょう).

Register is otherwise flexible. It is at home in essays and in ordinary
conversation alike, and it can be made formal - and much softer - by borrowing the
humble causative: <code>私(わたし)に言(い)わせていただければ</code>, or the
even more cautious <code>言(い)わせていただくなら</code>. That version asks
permission before disagreeing, which is why it survives in meetings where the bare
form would be too blunt.

The subject is normally a person or a group of people. Applying it to a thing -
「数字(すうじ)に言(い)わせれば」 - is a deliberate figure of speech, effective
once and odd twice.

## Contrast

### に言(い)わせれば vs によれば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>によれば</code> cites a **source** for information.
<code>に言(い)わせれば</code> attributes a **verdict**, and the person need
never have said it out loud.

</div>

<pre>
天気(てんき)予報(よほう)<b>によれば</b>、明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)になるそうだ。
According to the forecast, it will snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、今(いま)の冬(ふゆ)は冬(ふゆ)とは呼(よ)べないらしい。
In my grandfather's view, what we get now doesn't deserve to be called winter.
</pre>

The forecast made an actual statement, and the sentence passes it on. The
grandfather may never have said this exact thing; the speaker is characterising the
position he holds. Notice too that <code>によれば</code> takes non-human sources -
新聞(しんぶん), 調査(ちょうさ), 統計(とうけい) - which the causative
pattern normally cannot.

### に言(い)わせれば vs にとっては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にとっては</code> asks what something **is worth** to
someone. <code>に言(い)わせれば</code> asks what they would **say** about it.

</div>

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>にとっては</b>、一時間(いちじかん)の待(ま)ち時間(じかん)は長(なが)すぎる。
For a child, an hour's wait is far too long.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、大人(おとな)はいつも「あとで」としか言(い)わないそうだ。
Ask a child and they'll tell you grown-ups never say anything but "later".
</pre>

The first measures the wait against a child's tolerance and is a statement the
speaker themselves is making. The second quotes the child's own line of complaint.
Where the sentence is an evaluation of significance rather than a report of a
position, <code>にとっては</code> is the one you want.

### 私(わたし)に言(い)わせれば vs 私(わたし)は…と思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と思(おも)う</code> simply offers an opinion.
<code>私(わたし)に言(い)わせれば</code> offers it **against** a view already
in the room.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)はこの案(あん)のほうがいい<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I think this proposal is the better one.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、どちらの案(あん)も問題(もんだい)の本質(ほんしつ)を外(はず)している。
If you ask me, neither proposal touches the heart of the problem.
</pre>

The first can be the first opinion voiced in a meeting. The second cannot: it needs
something to push back against, and it claims a slightly superior vantage point.
Used where nobody has disagreed with you, it sounds combative for no reason.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>が言(い)わせれば</b>、この値段(ねだん)は高(たか)すぎる。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、この値段(ねだん)は高(たか)すぎる。
  The speaker is the one being *let* speak, and a causee is marked with `に`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>に言(い)えば</b>、それは無理(むり)だ。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、それは無理(むり)だ。
  Without the causative the phrase means "if you tell me", which is a different
  sentence and does not introduce an opinion.

- ❌ 統計(とうけい)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、人口(じんこう)は減(へ)り続(つづ)けている。 → ✅ 統計(とうけい)<b>によれば</b>、人口(じんこう)は減(へ)り続(つづ)けている。
  Statistics do not hold opinions. Non-human sources take <code>によれば</code> or
  <code>による</code>.

- ❌ （部長(ぶちょう)本人(ほんにん)に）部長(ぶちょう)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、納期(のうき)は動(うご)かせないのですね。 → ✅ （部長(ぶちょう)本人(ほんにん)に）部長(ぶちょう)のお考(かんが)えでは、納期(のうき)は動(うご)かせないということでしょうか。
  Characterising someone's position to their face puts them at arm's length. Ask
  them directly instead.

**1.** 兄(あに)____、野球(やきゅう)は見(み)るものではなく、やるものらしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

兄(あに)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>

The sentence reports a characteristic opinion of the brother's, closed off with
らしい to keep the speaker at a distance from it. That combination is the standard
third-person use.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この報告書(ほうこくしょ)（に言(い)わせれば・によれば）、事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)は整備(せいび)不足(ぶそく)だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この報告書(ほうこくしょ)<b>によれば</b>、事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)は整備(せいび)不足(ぶそく)だった。

A report is a source of information, not a person with a view. Only living
speakers can be "let to speak", which is what the causative in the other pattern
literally does.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If you ask the students, the exam wasn't difficult - it was just too long."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"If you ask the students" is the pattern almost word for word, and そうだ at the

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)が言(い)わせれば、この町(まち)は静(しず)かすぎるらしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、この町(まち)は静(しず)かすぎるらしい。

The particle is the only fault. 言(い)わせる is causative, so the person whose
words are being elicited is marked `に`; `が` would make her the one doing the

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)はこのやり方(かた)に反対(はんたい)です。
(b) 私(わたし)<b>に言(い)わせれば</b>、このやり方(かた)は時間(じかん)の無駄(むだ)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states a position. It is direct but unremarkable, and it could be the first
contribution anyone makes to the discussion.

(b) sets the speaker's judgement against whatever has already been said. It implies
that other people have looked at this and missed something, and it is noticeably
sharper - fine among peers in an argument, risky when addressed upward unless
softened to
<code>言(い)わせていただければ</code>.

</details>

# ～ならいざしらず

**Meaning** · "if it were A, I couldn't say - but it isn't" - grants one case as excusable, then denies the excuse to the real one

The literal parts are いざ ("well then") and 知(し)らず, a classical negative of
知(し)る: *as for that, I would not know*. The speaker waves the first case
through without ruling on it, only to make the second case stand out by contrast.
Whatever follows is nearly always a complaint, a refusal or an expression of
disbelief.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ならいざ知(し)らず | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)ならいざ知(し)らず |
| Noun, contrastive | Noun + はいざ知(し)らず | 昔(むかし) → 昔(むかし)はいざ知(し)らず |
| Verb clause | plain form + のなら + いざ知(し)らず | 知(し)らなかった → 知(し)らなかったのならいざ知(し)らず |
| い-adjective | plain form + のなら + いざ知(し)らず | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いのならいざ知(し)らず |
| な-adjective | ～なら + いざ知(し)らず | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)ならいざ知(し)らず |
| Second clause | criticism, refusal or surprise | …のは困(こま)る／…とは驚(おどろ)きだ／…はできない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - written both ならいざしらず and ならいざ知(し)らず; neither spelling
is more correct, and the fuller one is common in print. No `だ` comes between a
noun and なら: ❌<code>子供(こども)だならいざ知(し)らず</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、大人(おとな)がそんなことを言(い)うものではない。
Coming from a child it would be one thing, but an adult should not say such things.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)<b>はいざ知(し)らず</b>、今(いま)はだれでもスマホを持(も)っている。
How it was in the past I couldn't say, but these days everyone has a smartphone.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、片道(かたみち)3時間(じかん)では毎週(まいしゅう)は通(かよ)えない。
If it were nearby that would be different, but I can't go every week when it's a three-hour trip each way.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、10年目(ねんめ)の社員(しゃいん)が同(おな)じミスをするのは困(こま)る。
From a new hire it would be understandable, but a ten-year veteran making the same mistake is a problem.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)や二日(ふつか)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、一(いっ)か月(げつ)も連絡(れんらく)がないのは心配(しんぱい)だ。
A day or two would be nothing, but a whole month without a word is worrying.
</pre>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、素人(しろうと)の私(わたし)にこの数式(すうしき)の意味(いみ)が分(わ)かるはずもない。
A specialist might be another matter, but there is no way an amateur like me can make sense of this formula.
</pre>

<pre>
知(し)らなかったの<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、説明(せつめい)を受(う)けた上(うえ)での違反(いはん)は見過(みす)ごせない。
Had they not known, that would be one thing; a breach committed after being briefed cannot be overlooked.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が来(き)ているの<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、この程度(ていど)の雨(あめ)で中止(ちゅうし)にするのは早(はや)すぎる。
If a typhoon were on the way it would be different, but calling it off over rain like this is premature.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **formal and largely written**, though educated speech uses it
freely in argument - a complaint at a meeting, a letter, an editorial, a parent
telling off a teenager who is old enough to know better. It is not archaic and a
learner may produce it, but it lands with weight: it sets up a standard and then
finds the real case falling short of it.

The reproach is structural. Because the first clause is the case the speaker would
*forgive*, the second is by construction the one they will not, and a neutral or
cheerful comment there sounds like the sentence changed its mind halfway. Watch for
this in the very common shape
<code>N一人(ひとり)や二人(ふたり)ならいざ知(し)らず</code> or
<code>一日(いちにち)や二日(ふつか)ならいざ知(し)らず</code>, where a small
quantity is waved through so that a large one can be objected to.

<code>昔(むかし)はいざ知(し)らず</code> is close to a set phrase and is
worth learning whole: "whatever may have been true then, now…". Here the
"I wouldn't know" is nearly literal - the speaker really is disclaiming knowledge
of the earlier period.

In relaxed conversation the same move is made with <code>ならともかく</code> or
<code>なら別(べつ)だけど</code>, both of which are shorter and carry no
literary weight.

## Contrast

### ならいざ知(し)らず vs はともかく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>はともかく</code> **sets a matter aside** as not the
issue right now. <code>ならいざ知(し)らず</code> **grants it as an excuse** and
then points out that it does not apply.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)<b>はともかく</b>、デザインは気(き)に入(い)っている。
Price aside, I do like the design.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が安(やす)いの<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、この質(しつ)でこの金額(きんがく)は高(たか)すぎる。
If it were cheap that would be another matter, but this much money for this quality is too dear.
</pre>

<code>はともかく</code> is neutral; it postpones a topic and says nothing about
whether it counts for or against. <code>ならいざ知(し)らず</code> is always
evaluating, and the second clause is the verdict. They are also different shapes:
ともかく attaches to a bare noun, while いざ知(し)らず needs the conditional
なら in front of it.

### ならいざ知(し)らず vs ならまだしも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two overlap almost completely.
<code>ならまだしも</code> says the first case would be **tolerable**;
<code>ならいざ知(し)らず</code> says the speaker **would not pass judgement**
on it. The second is the more formal of the pair.

</div>

<pre>
一度(いちど)<b>ならまだしも</b>、三度(さんど)も遅刻(ちこく)するのはひどい。
Once would be bad enough, but turning up late three times is appalling.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、三度(さんど)も遅刻(ちこく)するとは信(しん)じられない。
Once I could let pass, but I can't believe someone turning up late three times.
</pre>

In most sentences either is possible and the difference is one of shading:
まだしも measures the first case on a scale of acceptability and finds it barely
acceptable, while いざ知(し)らず declines to place it on the scale at all. Where
the first case is genuinely unknown to the speaker -
<code>外国(がいこく)はいざ知(し)らず</code> - only いざ知(し)らず works,
because まだしも would claim to have weighed it.

### ならいざ知(し)らず vs ～ならわかるが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same argument, plain clothes. <code>～ならわかるが</code> is
what the pattern looks like in ordinary Japanese.

</div>

<pre>
急(きゅう)な話(はなし)<b>ならわかるが</b>、一(いっ)か月(げつ)前(まえ)から決(き)まっていたのに準備(じゅんび)していないのか。
If it had come up suddenly I'd understand, but this was settled a month ago and you haven't prepared?
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)な話(はなし)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、一(いっ)か月(げつ)前(まえ)から決(き)まっていた件(けん)である。
Had it come up suddenly it would be one thing, but this is a matter settled a month ago.
</pre>

The content is the same and only the register moves. <code>ならわかるが</code>
belongs in speech and in an ordinary email; <code>ならいざ知(し)らず</code> in a
written complaint, a speech or an argument the speaker wants to sound weighty. If
you are unsure which fits, the plain one is never wrong.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 子供(こども)<b>だならいざ知(し)らず</b>、大人(おとな)がそれを言(い)うのか。 → ✅ 子供(こども)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、大人(おとな)がそれを言(い)うのか。
  なら follows a bare noun. The `だ` has nowhere to stand.

- ❌ 子供(こども)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、大人(おとな)もこの遊(あそ)びが好(す)きだ。 → ✅ 子供(こども)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、大人(おとな)がこんな遊(あそ)びに夢中(むちゅう)になるとは驚(おどろ)きだ。
  The frame promises that the second case is the objectionable one. A comment that
  simply adds adults to the same group contradicts the structure.

- ❌ 一(いっ)か月(げつ)も返事(へんじ)がないのは困(こま)る、一日(いちにち)や二日(ふつか)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>。 → ✅ 一日(いちにち)や二日(ふつか)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、一(いっ)か月(げつ)も返事(へんじ)がないのは困(こま)る。
  The excusable case comes first. Reversed, the sentence reads as an afterthought
  and loses the contrast entirely.

- ❌ （友人(ゆうじん)との雑談(ざつだん)で）ラーメン<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、そばはちょっと苦手(にがて)。 → ✅ （友人(ゆうじん)との雑談(ざつだん)で）ラーメン<b>ならともかく</b>、そばはちょっと苦手(にがて)。
  Nothing is ungrammatical here, but the register jumps from a chat about lunch to
  a written argument. Casual speech takes ともかく or 別(べつ)だけど.

## Practice

**1.** 徒歩(とほ)五分(ごふん)____、駅(えき)から1時間(じかん)もかかる物件(ぶっけん)は考(かんが)えられない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

徒歩(とほ)五分(ごふん)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>

A five-minute walk is the case the speaker would accept without argument; the
hour-long commute is the one being rejected. The excusable case always occupies the
first slot.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 味(あじ)（はともかく・ならいざ知(し)らず）、値段(ねだん)は安(やす)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

味(あじ)<b>はともかく</b>、値段(ねだん)は安(やす)い。

The speaker is shelving the question of taste, not offering it as an excuse that
turns out not to apply. There is also no criticism in the second clause, which the
other pattern requires.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "For a beginner it would be understandable, but someone who has been driving for twenty years should not be making that mistake."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、20年(ねん)運転(うんてん)している人(ひと)がそんなミスをするべきではない。

"For a beginner it would be understandable" is exactly the concession the pattern
makes, and the reproach that follows is what it exists to set up.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 学生(がくせい)だならいざ知(し)らず、社会人(しゃかいじん)がこの時間(じかん)まで寝(ね)ているとは。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>ならいざ知(し)らず</b>、社会人(しゃかいじん)がこの時間(じかん)まで寝(ね)ているとは。

Delete the `だ`. なら attaches straight to the noun, and the trailing とは -
another N1 shape, the one that trails off in disbelief - is a natural partner for
this frame.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 昔(むかし)<b>はいざ知(し)らず</b>、今(いま)のこの町(まち)は夜(よる)も明(あか)るい。
(b) 昔(むかし)<b>ならまだしも</b>、今(いま)のこの町(まち)は夜(よる)も明(あか)るい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is natural. The speaker disclaims knowledge of what the town was like in the
past - they may not have been there - and reports only what is true now.

(b) is odd. まだしも weighs the first case and finds it just about acceptable, so
the sentence would have to be complaining about the town being bright, which the
second clause plainly is not. Where the first case is unknown rather than merely
tolerable, only いざ知(し)らず fits.

</details>

# というか

**Meaning** · "or rather"; "well, I mean" - takes back the word just used and reaches for a better one, mid-sentence

`というか` is the quotative <code>という</code> plus the question particle `か`:
*shall we call it that?* The speaker offers a label, immediately admits it is not
quite right, and supplies another. It also works at the head of a sentence, where
it redirects the whole conversation - "actually, hold on".

## Usage

Nothing conjugates. `というか` sits between the first attempt and the second, and
takes whatever came before it in plain form: a noun
（趣味(しゅみ)というか）, an adjective（安(やす)いというか、静(しず)かというか）,
a verb（忘(わす)れたというか）or a whole clause. No `だ` goes in front of a noun -
❌<code>趣味(しゅみ)だというか</code>.

Three positions, three jobs:

- **Mid-sentence repair** - the commonest use. The first label stands, imperfectly,
  and the second one refines it.
  疲(つか)れたというか、やる気(き)が出(で)ない。
- **Sentence-initial redirect** - "actually", "or rather", changing the angle of
  the whole exchange.
  というか、その話(はなし)、本人(ほんにん)に聞(き)いた方(ほう)が早(はや)くない?
- **Softening a blunt word** - the repair frame lets the speaker say something
  harsh and immediately dress it down.
  厳(きび)しいというか、正直(しょうじき)な人(ひと)なんです。

In speech it almost always contracts: ていうか, っていうか, and the very casual
つーか. All three are the same word.

## Examples

<pre>
疲(つか)れた<b>というか</b>、なんだかやる気(き)が出(で)ない。
I'm tired - or rather, I just can't get going somehow.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は先生(せんせい)<b>というか</b>、友(とも)だちのような存在(そんざい)だ。
He's a teacher - well, more like a friend really.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>というか</b>、そもそもこの会議(かいぎ)は必要(ひつよう)なんですか。
Actually - does this meeting need to happen at all?
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)い<b>というか</b>、安(やす)すぎて逆(ぎゃく)に不安(ふあん)になる値段(ねだん)だった。
Cheap, or rather so cheap it started to worry me.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`というか` is **casual and spoken**. It belongs to conversation, chat messages,
blogs and interview transcripts, and it is one of the most frequent fillers in
everyday Japanese - which is exactly why it is worth controlling rather than
sprinkling. In a report or a formal email the same repair is made with
正確(せいかく)には ("more precisely"), <code>むしろ</code> or
<code>言(い)い換(か)えれば</code>.

The contracted forms carry their own flavour. ていうか and っていうか are neutral
casual; つーか is blunter and reads as young or offhand, and in writing it looks
distinctly rough. Any of them at the head of a turn - 「っていうか、それ昨日(きのう)
聞(き)いたよ」 - is a mild interruption: the speaker is redirecting rather than
responding, which is friendly among equals and presumptuous upward.

The softening use is worth noticing. Because the frame admits that the first word
was not quite right, a speaker can put an unkind description on the table and pull
it back in the same breath: 「頑固(がんこ)というか、信念(しんねん)がある人(ひと)」.
The listener hears both, which is the point - it is politer than the bare
criticism and more honest than the compliment alone.

## Contrast

### というか vs というより

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>というより</code> **rejects** the first description and
replaces it. `というか` leaves it standing as an imperfect approximation.

</div>

<pre>
これは趣味(しゅみ)<b>というより</b>仕事(しごと)だ。
This isn't so much a hobby as a job.
</pre>

<pre>
これは趣味(しゅみ)<b>というか</b>、生活(せいかつ)の一部(いちぶ)になっている。
It's a hobby - well, it's become part of my life, really.
</pre>

In the first sentence "hobby" is ruled out. In the second it is still true, only
inadequate, and the speaker is groping for something better. That is why
<code>というより</code> takes a clean pair of alternatives and `というか` so often
trails into a longer, vaguer clause. <code>というより</code> is also the one that
survives into writing.

### というか vs つまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つまり` says the second version is the **same content, made
clearer**. `というか` says the first version was **the wrong word**.

</div>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)が出(で)てしまった。<b>つまり</b>、今夜(こんや)は帰(かえ)れない。
The last train has gone. Which means I can't get home tonight.
</pre>

<pre>
帰(かえ)れない<b>というか</b>、帰(かえ)る気(き)がなくなってきた。
I can't get home - or rather, I've stopped wanting to.
</pre>

`つまり` is a summary and is at home in explanation and in formal writing;
`というか` is a self-correction and is not. Note also that `つまり` opens the
clause it introduces, while `というか` normally sits between the two versions.

### というか vs ～というか～というか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **one** `というか` corrects; **two** of them decline to choose,
holding both labels up at once.

</div>

<pre>
静(しず)か<b>というか</b>、人(ひと)の話(はなし)をよく聞(き)く人(ひと)だ。
Quiet - well, she's someone who listens carefully.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)か<b>というか</b>、暗(くら)い<b>というか</b>、不思議(ふしぎ)な雰囲気(ふんいき)の店(みせ)だった。
Quiet, or gloomy, or something - the place had a strange atmosphere.
</pre>

The paired version leaves both words in play and adds a shrug; nothing is
retracted. The single version is a repair: the first word is on its way out as you
say the second.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ これは趣味(しゅみ)<b>だというか</b>仕事(しごと)だ。 → ✅ これは趣味(しゅみ)<b>というか</b>仕事(しごと)だ。
  A noun connects to <code>という</code> bare. The `だ` has no place before it.

- ❌ 昨年度(さくねんど)の不振(ふしん)は市場(しじょう)の問題(もんだい)<b>というか</b>、計画(けいかく)に無理(むり)があった。（in a written report） → ✅ 昨年度(さくねんど)の不振(ふしん)は市場(しじょう)の問題(もんだい)<b>というより</b>、計画(けいかく)自体(じたい)に無理(むり)があった。
  A report should not sound as though it is revising its wording as it goes.
  Formal prose takes <code>というより</code> or 正確(せいかく)には.

- ❌ お客様(きゃくさま)、<b>っていうか</b>、そちらの商品(しょうひん)は在庫(ざいこ)がございません。 → ✅ お客様(きゃくさま)、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございませんが、そちらの商品(しょうひん)は在庫(ざいこ)がございません。
  The contracted forms are for friends. Opening a turn with them to a customer
  sounds like an interruption as well as a familiarity.

- ❌ 三年(さんねん)住(す)んでいた。<b>というか</b>、ここは第二(だいに)の故郷(こきょう)だ。 → ✅ 三年(さんねん)住(す)んでいた。<b>つまり</b>、ここは第二(だいに)の故郷(こきょう)だ。
  Nothing is being corrected here; the second sentence is the upshot of the first.
  That is `つまり`'s job.

## Practice

**1.** Choose: 彼(かれ)の作品(さくひん)は絵(え)（というか・というより）設計図(せっけいず)に近(ちか)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)の作品(さくひん)は絵(え)<b>というより</b>設計図(せっけいず)に近(ちか)い。

The sentence sets two labels against each other and picks the second, which is what
<code>というより</code> does. `というか` would leave "picture" half-standing and
would want a vaguer follow-up rather than a clean alternative.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite for a written report: 準備(じゅんび)不足(ぶそく)っていうか、そもそも人手(ひとで)が足(た)りませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

準備(じゅんび)不足(ぶそく)<b>というより</b>、根本(こんぽん)的(てき)に人員(じんいん)が不足(ふそく)しておりました。

Two things have to change: the contraction っていうか cannot appear in a report,
and the self-correcting frame is replaced by the cleaner
<code>というより</code>. そもそも also gives way to a plainer adverb.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 正直(しょうじき)な人(ひと)です。
(b) 厳(きび)しい<b>というか</b>、正直(しょうじき)な人(ひと)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a straightforward compliment and leaves nothing hanging.

(b) says the harsh thing first and then softens it. The listener hears that the
person is hard work and that the speaker prefers to frame this as honesty - a
common, deliberately two-sided way of describing a difficult colleague without
committing to an insult.

</details>

# ～というわけだ

**Meaning** · "so that's how it works out" - takes everything just said and delivers the conclusion it adds up to

<code>わけ</code> is the noun 訳(わけ), "the way a thing works out", and
<code>という</code> is the quotative that scoops up **the whole of the preceding
explanation** and hands it over as the premise. That is the difference from bare
<code>わけだ</code>: this is the shape you reach for at the *end* of a story, when
the pieces are all on the table and you are stating the total.

## Formation

<code>わけ</code> is a noun, and <code>という</code> lets a whole clause - or a
bare noun - sit in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + というわけだ | 入(はい)れなかった → 入(はい)れなかったというわけだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + というわけだ | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いというわけだ |
| な-adjective | ～だ + というわけだ | 無理(むり)だ → 無理(むり)だというわけだ |
| Noun | Noun (+ だ) + というわけだ | 三年目(さんねんめ) → 三年目(さんねんめ)というわけだ |
| Polite | ～というわけです | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かないというわけです |
| Confirming what you heard | ～というわけですね / ～というわけか | 来(こ)られない → 来(こ)られないというわけですね |
| Connector to the next sentence | というわけで / そういうわけで | - → というわけで、終(お)わりにします |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern looks backwards, never forwards. Something has to have been
explained already; used to open a topic it leaves the listener hunting for the
missing first half.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)を忘(わす)れた。だから中(なか)に入(はい)れなかった<b>というわけだ</b>。
I forgot my key. So that's why I couldn't get in.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まった。全員(ぜんいん)遅(おく)れた<b>というわけです</b>。
The rain stopped the trains. Which is why everyone was late.
</pre>

<pre>
では、会場(かいじょう)は変(か)わらない<b>というわけですね</b>。
So what you're telling me is that the venue stays as it was.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)から値上(ねあ)げが続(つづ)いている。同(おな)じ一万円(いちまんえん)でも買(か)えるものが減(へ)った<b>というわけだ</b>。
Prices have been rising since last year. So the same ten thousand yen buys less than it did.
</pre>

<pre>
三人(さんにん)で分(わ)けると一人(ひとり)六個(ろっこ)、全部(ぜんぶ)で18個(こ)必要(ひつよう)だった<b>というわけです</b>。
Split three ways that's six each, so eighteen were needed altogether.
</pre>

<pre>
担当(たんとう)者(しゃ)が急(きゅう)に辞(や)めてしまい、引(ひ)き継(つ)ぎのないまま私(わたし)がやることになった<b>というわけです</b>。
The person in charge left suddenly, and with no handover it ended up falling to me.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)がドイツ人(じん)、母(はは)がフランス人(じん)で、育(そだ)ったのは日本(にほん)。だから三(さん)か国語(こくご)話(はな)せる<b>というわけだ</b>。
German father, French mother, raised in Japan - which is how he ends up with three languages.
</pre>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)の工場(こうじょう)が止(と)まり、国内(こくない)の在庫(ざいこ)も尽(つ)きました。来月(らいげつ)まで入荷(にゅうか)の見込(みこ)みはない<b>というわけです</b>。
The overseas plant has halted and domestic stock has run out, so there is no prospect of a delivery before next month.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The form is register-neutral; <code>というわけです</code> is at home in briefings,
customer explanations and classroom summaries, and <code>というわけだ</code> in
casual speech and in writing alike. Where it is *not* at home is the opening of a
paragraph, because it has nothing to conclude from.

Its natural habitat is the last sentence of an explanation. Speakers signal it in
advance with だから, つまり or ということで, and the listener is already expecting a
wrap-up when it arrives. In conversation the confirming version
<code>～というわけですね</code> is the standard way to check that you have followed
a complicated answer - and it is genuinely useful in meetings, where restating the
other side's position accurately is half the work.

<code>というわけで</code> deserves its own line. As a sentence opener it means
"and so", and it does two jobs: closing off reasoning and moving on
（というわけで、来週(らいしゅう)再開(さいかい)します）, and - in casual speech
- skipping the reasoning entirely with a wave of the hand
（というわけで、よろしく）. That second use is common and mildly comic; everyone
knows no reasons were actually given.

Two things it cannot do. It cannot report hearsay: for "I hear that", the form is
<code>ということだ</code>. And it cannot announce a decision of your own, because a
decision does not *follow* from anything - that is
<code>ことにしました</code> or <code>つもりです</code>.

## Contrast

### というわけだ vs わけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare <code>わけだ</code> concludes from the clause it is
attached to. <code>というわけだ</code> concludes from **everything said so far**,
which is why it closes explanations.

</div>

<pre>
北(きた)向(む)きの部屋(へや)だから、昼間(ひるま)でも暗(くら)い<b>わけだ</b>。
It faces north, so of course it's dark even during the day.
</pre>

<pre>
北(きた)向(む)きで、前(まえ)にビルが建(た)って、窓(まど)も小(ちい)さい。昼間(ひるま)でも暗(くら)い<b>というわけだ</b>。
North-facing, a block put up in front of it, and small windows. So it's dark even in the daytime.
</pre>

Both are correct. The first hangs one conclusion on one stated premise, in a single
sentence. The second gathers three sentences and totals them, and swapping in the
bare form there would sound as though only the last item mattered.
<code>という</code> is doing the gathering.

### というわけだ vs ということだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>というわけだ</code> says the content **follows** from
what is known. <code>ということだ</code> either restates the content flatly or
marks it as **something you heard**.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)によると、来月(らいげつ)から勤務(きんむ)時間(じかん)が変(か)わる<b>ということだ</b>。
According to the manager, working hours change from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)が通(とお)った。来月(らいげつ)から勤務(きんむ)時間(じかん)が変(か)わる<b>というわけだ</b>。
The new rule went through. So working hours change from next month.
</pre>

The moment a source is named - 部長(ぶちょう)によると, 新聞(しんぶん)では -
only <code>ということだ</code> is possible, because
<code>というわけだ</code> never carries hearsay. In the "in other words" sense the
two do overlap, and there the difference is only that
<code>というわけだ</code> insists the conclusion was forced by the facts.

### というわけだ vs ということになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>というわけだ</code> is the speaker **working it out**.
<code>ということになる</code> states the **official upshot**, the position things
now stand in.

</div>

<pre>
計算(けいさん)すると、一人(ひとり)あたり3,000円(えん)払(はら)う<b>というわけだ</b>。
Do the sums and it comes to three thousand yen each.
</pre>

<pre>
規定(きてい)では、三年(さんねん)勤(つと)めた人(ひと)は全員(ぜんいん)対象(たいしょう)<b>ということになります</b>。
Under the rules, anyone with three years' service counts as eligible.
</pre>

<code>ということになる</code> is the colder of the two and the one that appears in
contracts, minutes and announcements: it reports how a rule or a decision classifies
the situation, with no speaker standing behind it.
<code>というわけだ</code> always has someone doing the reasoning.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ （話(はなし)の冒頭(ぼうとう)で）明日(あした)は休(やす)みだ<b>というわけです</b>。 → ✅ （話(はなし)の冒頭(ぼうとう)で）明日(あした)は休(やす)みです。
  Nothing has been established yet, so there is nothing for the sentence to follow
  from. The listener will wait for a first half that never comes.

- ❌ 天気(てんき)予報(よほう)によると、明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)になる<b>というわけです</b>。 → ✅ 天気(てんき)予報(よほう)によると、明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)になる<b>ということです</b>。
  Reported information takes <code>ということだ</code>. The other form claims the
  snow was deduced, not heard.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来月(らいげつ)会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)める<b>というわけです</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来月(らいげつ)会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めることにしました。
  Your own decision is not a conclusion drawn from evidence. As written it suggests
  the resignation somehow emerged from the circumstances.

- ❌ <b>というわけで</b>、昨日(きのう)は熱(ねつ)が出(で)ました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は熱(ねつ)が出(で)ました。<b>というわけで</b>、会(かい)を欠席(けっせき)いたしました。
  The connector introduces the *consequence*, not the reason. Put the reason in
  front of it.

The roadworks are the premise and the morning jams are what falls out of them. The

</details>

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会員(かいいん)は全員(ぜんいん)参加(さんか)できる<b>というわけです</b>。
(b) 会員(かいいん)は全員(ぜんいん)参加(さんか)できる<b>ということになります</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is someone reasoning out loud from what has just been explained - the fee was
paid, the list was updated, therefore all members are in. There is a speaker behind
the conclusion.

(b) reports how the rules classify the situation. It is cooler and more official,
the sentence you would find in minutes or hear from a desk clerk, and it makes no
claim that anyone worked anything out.

</details>

# ～というところだ / ～といったところだ

**Meaning** · "about that much, and no more" - gives a rough figure or level, with a quiet ceiling on it

The noun is ところ, "the point things have reached", and <code>という</code> quotes
the estimate rather than asserting it. The result is a measured, slightly
deflationary judgement: *that is roughly where it stands*. English "about" is only
half of it - the pattern also implies you should not expect more.

## Formation

The two variants are interchangeable in almost every sentence;
<code>といったところだ</code> is the commoner of the pair.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Number or quantity | Number + といったところだ | 20人(にん) → 20人(にん)といったところだ |
| Noun (a level or stage) | Noun + といったところだ | 半分(はんぶん) → 半分(はんぶん)といったところだ |
| Clause (an outcome) | plain form + といったところだ | 少(すこ)し上(あ)がる → 少(すこ)し上(あ)がるといったところだ |
| Polite | ～といったところです | 半分(はんぶん) → 半分(はんぶん)といったところです |
| Hedged / musing | ～といったところか / ～といったところだろう | 来月(らいげつ) → 来月(らいげつ)といったところだろう |
| Casual contraction | ～ってところだ | 20人(にん) → 20人(にん)ってところだ |
| Frequent partners | まあ / せいぜい / どんなに多(おお)くても | - → せいぜい20人(にん)といったところだ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the ceiling is built in, so ❌<code>ぐらいといったところだ</code> is a
double hedge. Pick one: 20人(にん)ぐらいだ, or 20人(にん)といったところだ.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)の客(きゃく)は20人(にん)<b>といったところだ</b>。
Today's customers came to about twenty, no more.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)は半分(はんぶん)<b>といったところです</b>。
Preparations are about half done.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで歩(ある)いて十分(じゅっぷん)<b>といったところでしょうか</b>。
It's what, ten minutes on foot to the station?
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)の昇給(しょうきゅう)は、せいぜい一割(いちわり)上(あ)がる<b>といったところだ</b>。
Next year's raise will be ten percent at the very best.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)の売(う)り上(あ)げは、去年(きょねん)並(な)みか少(すこ)し落(お)ちる<b>といったところでしょう</b>。
Summer sales will be level with last year's, or a shade below.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の腕前(うでまえ)は、そろそろ一人(ひとり)で任(まか)せられる手前(てまえ)<b>といったところだ</b>。
The new recruit is just short of the point where you could leave him to it.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)の感想(かんそう)を聞(き)かれ、監督(かんとく)は「まあ、及第(きゅうだい)点(てん)<b>といったところですね</b>」と答(こた)えた。
Asked how the match had gone, the manager said, "A pass mark, I suppose, and not much more."
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)が終(お)わるのは、どんなに早(はや)くても来月(らいげつ)の中(なか)ごろ<b>といったところだろう</b>。
At the very earliest the survey will be finished around the middle of next month.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **adult, measured speech and writing**. It turns up in business
conversation, sports and market commentary, interviews and columns - anywhere
somebody is asked to size something up and wants to sound careful rather than
confident. It is not stiff, and it is not casual either; the contraction
<code>ってところ</code> is the version friends use
（「準備(じゅんび)? まあ半分(はんぶん)ってところ」）.

The tone is **modest, and often faintly disappointing**. Behind the estimate sits
"don't expect more than this", which is why the pattern pairs so naturally with
せいぜい and まあ, and why it fits a middling result better than a triumphant one.
Announcing a record profit with it would undercut the news. The
<code>といったところか</code> and <code>といったところだろう</code> endings push it
further towards thinking aloud, and are common when the speaker is guessing on the
spot.

Because the estimate is quoted rather than asserted, the speaker keeps a little
distance from it: they are offering a reasonable reading, not a measurement. That
makes it a safe answer to a question you cannot answer exactly - and a poor choice
when someone needs a real number.

As for the two variants, <code>といった</code> is the one you will hear most, and
no difference of meaning is reliable enough to be worth memorising.

## Contrast

### といったところだ vs ぐらいだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ぐらい` is a plain approximation and can sit anywhere in a
sentence. <code>といったところだ</code> is a **sentence-final verdict** that also
caps the figure.

</div>

<pre>
20人(にん)<b>ぐらい</b>の参加者(さんかしゃ)に資料(しりょう)を配(くば)った。
I handed out materials to about twenty participants.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は20人(にん)<b>といったところだ</b>。
Participants came to about twenty, and that was that.
</pre>

`ぐらい` modifies a noun and carries on - ぐらいの人(ひと), ぐらいから,
ぐらいしか - while the other pattern ends the sentence. `ぐらい` is also neutral
about whether twenty is a lot; <code>といったところだ</code> hints that it is not.

### といったところだ vs せいぜい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - せいぜい is an **adverb** that states the ceiling outright.
<code>といったところだ</code> implies it. The two combine happily.

</div>

<pre>
参加(さんか)できるのは<b>せいぜい</b>三人(さんにん)だ。
Three of us can go at the very most.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)できるのは<b>せいぜい</b>三人(さんにん)<b>といったところだ</b>。
Realistically, three of us at most can go.
</pre>

せいぜい alone is blunter and can sound dismissive; the pattern softens it into an
assessment. Where you want the cap stated plainly - a hard budget limit, a
capacity - use せいぜい on its own.

### といったところだ vs にすぎない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にすぎない</code> **belittles**: it is *only* that.
<code>といったところだ</code> merely **estimates**, with a modest ceiling.

</div>

<pre>
回答(かいとう)したのは全体(ぜんたい)の一割(いちわり)<b>にすぎない</b>。
Only ten percent of them responded.
</pre>

<pre>
回答(かいとう)率(りつ)は一割(いちわり)<b>といったところだ</b>。
The response rate is around ten percent.
</pre>

<code>にすぎない</code> is an argument - the writer is about to say the figure is
too small to rely on. The other sentence just reports where the number sits, and
could be followed by anything. Note also that
<code>にすぎない</code> needs a figure the writer regards as definite, while
<code>といったところだ</code> is by nature an estimate.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 参加者(さんかしゃ)は30人(にん)ぐらい<b>といったところだ</b>。 → ✅ 参加者(さんかしゃ)は30人(にん)<b>といったところだ</b>。
  The hedging is already in the pattern. Stacking ぐらい on top of it is like
  saying "roughly about approximately thirty".

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)<b>といったところだ</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)だ。
  It estimates a position on a scale - a quantity, a level, a stage. It cannot be
  used to identify what something simply is.

- ❌ 今期(こんき)の利益(りえき)は過去(かこ)最高(さいこう)の10億円(おくえん)<b>といったところです</b>。 → ✅ 今期(こんき)の利益(りえき)は過去(かこ)最高(さいこう)の10億円(おくえん)に達(たっ)しました。
  The built-in ceiling fights with the good news. Announcing a record and then
  capping it reads as though the speaker is unimpressed by their own company.

- ❌ （報告書(ほうこくしょ)に）進捗(しんちょく)率(りつ)は半分(はんぶん)<b>ってところです</b>。 → ✅ （報告書(ほうこくしょ)に）進捗(しんちょく)率(りつ)は半分(はんぶん)程度(ていど)です。
  The contraction is speech only. Written reports take
  程度(ていど) or 約(やく).

## Practice

**1.** A: 会場(かいじょう)にはどのくらい入(はい)りますか。 B: そうですね、満席(まんせき)で150人(にん)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

150人(にん)<b>といったところでしょうか</b>

The speaker is sizing up a capacity on the spot rather than quoting a measured
figure, and the でしょうか ending keeps the guess tentative. This is the pattern's
most natural home: an estimate given in answer to a question.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 投票(とうひょう)したのは有権者(ゆうけんしゃ)の三割(さんわり)（といったところだ・にすぎない）。民意(みんい)を示(しめ)したとは言(い)えない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三割(さんわり)<b>にすぎない</b>。

The second sentence is an argument that the turnout was too low to count, so the
first has to belittle the figure. <code>といったところだ</code> would be neutral
and would leave the criticism unsupported.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Repairs will take a week, or ten days at the outside."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

修理(しゅうり)にかかるのは一週間(いっしゅうかん)、長(なが)くても十日(とおか)<b>といったところです</b>。

"At the outside" is precisely the ceiling the pattern supplies, and
長(なが)くても reinforces it. 十日(とおか) is the native reading for ten
days, not ❌じゅうにち.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 今月(こんげつ)の残業(ざんぎょう)は20時間(じかん)ぐらいといったところってところです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 今月(こんげつ)の残業(ざんぎょう)は20時間(じかん)<b>といったところです</b>。

Three hedges have been piled on one figure. Keep the pattern once, drop ぐらい, and
do not mix the full form with its casual contraction in the same sentence.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新製品(しんせいひん)の評判(ひょうばん)は上々(じょうじょう)です。
(b) 新製品(しんせいひん)の評判(ひょうばん)は、まあ悪(わる)くない<b>といったところです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a clear positive report: the product is being well received, and the speaker
is happy to say so.

(b) puts a lid on it. The reception is acceptable and the speaker is declining to
claim more than that, with まあ adding a shrug. In a meeting, (b) would prepare
everyone for a modest sales figure rather than a good one.

</details>

# ～との

**Meaning** · "a report *that*…"; "word *that*…" - hooks quoted content onto a following noun

The quotative `と` normally hangs off a verb: 中止(ちゅうし)すると
聞(き)いた. Add `の` and it can hang off a **noun** instead:
中止(ちゅうし)するとの連絡(れんらく), "notice that it is being cancelled".
The noun on the right is almost always a piece of communication - a report, an
instruction, a complaint, a rumour - and the clause on the left is what that
communication said.

## Formation

What matters here is the noun on the **right**. It has to be a word for a message,
a statement or a view.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb clause | plain form + との + Noun | 遅(おく)れている → 遅(おく)れているとの報告(ほうこく) |
| い-adjective clause | plain form + との + Noun | 厳(きび)しい → 厳(きび)しいとの意見(いけん) |
| な-adjective / Noun clause | ～だ + との + Noun | 不要(ふよう)だ → 不要(ふよう)だとの回答(かいとう) |
| Request or command | ～てほしい / ～せよ + との + Noun | 対応(たいおう)してほしい → 対応(たいおう)してほしいとの依頼(いらい) |
| Question | ～か + との + Noun | いつ届(とど)くか → いつ届(とど)くかとの問(と)い合(あ)わせ |
| Honorific frame | ～とのご連絡(れんらく) / ～とのお話(はなし) | 欠席(けっせき)する → 欠席(けっせき)するとのご連絡(れんらく) |
| Relaying formula | ～とのことだ / ～とのことです | 不在(ふざい)だ → 不在(ふざい)だとのことです |
| Spoken equivalent | ～という + Noun / ～って | 遅(おく)れている → 遅(おく)れているという話(はなし) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the nouns that take <code>との</code> are message nouns:
報告(ほうこく), 連絡(れんらく), 通知(つうち), 指示(しじ),
指摘(してき), 意見(いけん), 声(こえ), 話(はなし), 噂(うわさ),
情報(じょうほう), 依頼(いらい), 要望(ようぼう), 回答(かいとう),
見方(みかた), 批判(ひはん). For any other noun the connector is
<code>という</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みだ<b>との</b>連絡(れんらく)があった。
Word came through that tomorrow is off.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)が遅(おく)れている<b>との</b>報告(ほうこく)を受(う)けた。
We received a report that the works are running late.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げはやむを得(え)ない<b>との</b>見方(みかた)が強(つよ)い。
The prevailing view is that a price rise is unavoidable.
</pre>

<pre>
至急(しきゅう)対応(たいおう)してほしい<b>との</b>依頼(いらい)が入(はい)っています。
A request has come in asking us to deal with it urgently.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)が不十分(ふじゅうぶん)だ<b>との</b>指摘(してき)を受(う)け、資料(しりょう)を作(つく)り直(なお)した。
Told that the explanation was inadequate, we rewrote the materials.
</pre>

<pre>
住民(じゅうみん)からは、夜間(やかん)の工事(こうじ)をやめてほしい<b>との</b>声(こえ)が上(あ)がっている。
Residents are raising calls for the night-time work to stop.
</pre>

<pre>
担当(たんとう)者(しゃ)は本日(ほんじつ)不在(ふざい)<b>とのこと</b>ですので、後日(ごじつ)あらためてご連絡(れんらく)いたします。
I am told the person in charge is out today, so I will contact you again another day.
</pre>

<pre>
会長(かいちょう)が今期(こんき)限(かぎ)りで退(しりぞ)く<b>との</b>情報(じょうほう)が流(なが)れ、社内(しゃない)は朝(あさ)から落(お)ち着(つ)かない。
Word that the chairman will step down at the end of this term has gone round, and the office has been unsettled since morning.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>との</code> is **written and formal**. It is the ordinary connector of news
copy, business email, minutes, official notices and internal reports - anywhere
somebody is passing on what was said without taking responsibility for it. In
casual speech it barely appears; there the same thing comes out as
<code>という話(はなし)</code> or simply
「遅(おく)れてるって言(い)ってたよ」.

Its value in business writing is precisely that it is **compact and neutral**.
「納期(のうき)を延(の)ばしてほしいとのご要望(ようぼう)をいただきました」
attributes the request cleanly, without a single word about whether the writer
agrees. That neutrality is why journalists lean on it so heavily: the sentence
reports the existence of a claim, not its truth.

<code>とのことです</code> is worth learning as a fixed unit. It is the standard way
to relay information you received from someone else in polite writing - "I am told
that…" - and it is noticeably softer than reporting the same fact flatly, because
it makes clear that the information is second-hand and that you are not its author.

One reading trap: <code>との</code> after a **person** is usually not this pattern
at all. 「取引先(とりひきさき)との連絡(れんらく)」 means contact *with* the
client - `と` there is the "with" particle. Content takes a clause on the left; a
partner takes a person.

## Contrast

### との vs という

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both put a clause in front of a noun. <code>という</code> works
with **any** noun and any register; <code>との</code> works only with **message
nouns**, and belongs to formal writing.

</div>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)に工場(こうじょう)を建(た)てる<b>という</b>計画(けいかく)が進(すす)んでいる。
The plan to build a plant overseas is moving forward.
</pre>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)に工場(こうじょう)を建(た)てる<b>との</b>発表(はっぴょう)があった。
An announcement was made that a plant will be built overseas.
</pre>

計画(けいかく) is a plan, not a statement, so
<code>という計画(けいかく)</code> is the natural pairing;
発表(はっぴょう) is an act of speech and takes either. Where both are
possible, <code>との</code> is the tighter, more official-sounding choice, and
<code>という</code> is the one that also works when you are talking.

### との vs と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare `と` feeds a **verb**; `との` feeds a **noun**. The `の` is
what makes the quote able to modify.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は延期(えんき)になった<b>と</b>聞(き)きました。
I heard that the meeting has been postponed.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は延期(えんき)になった<b>との</b>連絡(れんらく)を受(う)けました。
I received notice that the meeting has been postponed.
</pre>

Drop the `の` from the second sentence and the quote is left with nothing to attach
to. The pair also shows the register difference: the first is what you say, the
second what you write in a report of the same event.

### との vs からの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `からの` marks **who it came from**; `との` marks **what it
said**. They answer different questions and often appear in the same sentence.

</div>

<pre>
本社(ほんしゃ)<b>からの</b>連絡(れんらく)をお待(ま)ちください。
Please wait for word from head office.
</pre>

<pre>
本社(ほんしゃ)から、来週(らいしゅう)再開(さいかい)する<b>との</b>連絡(れんらく)がありました。
Head office have been in touch to say that operations resume next week.
</pre>

Confusing the two produces a real mistranslation:
部長(ぶちょう)との連絡(れんらく) is contact *with* the manager, while
部長(ぶちょう)からの連絡(れんらく) is a message *from* him. If the thing on
the left is a person, `の` is not doing quotative work.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 田中(たなか)<b>との</b>人(ひと)から電話(でんわ)がありました。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)<b>という</b>人(ひと)から電話(でんわ)がありました。
  Naming takes <code>という</code>. As written, the sentence says a call came from
  a person "with Tanaka".

- ❌ 参加(さんか)できない<b>との</b>人(ひと)が多(おお)い。 → ✅ 参加(さんか)できない<b>という</b>人(ひと)が多(おお)い。
  A person is not a message. The noun after <code>との</code> has to be the
  communication itself - 回答(かいとう), 声(こえ), 連絡(れんらく).

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)<b>との</b>連絡(れんらく)によると、会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)だそうだ。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)<b>からの</b>連絡(れんらく)によると、会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)だそうだ。
  With a person on the left, `と` reads as "with". The source of a message is
  marked with から.

- ❌ （友人(ゆうじん)に）来週(らいしゅう)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>との</b>話(はなし)だよ。 → ✅ （友人(ゆうじん)に）来週(らいしゅう)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>って</b>話(はなし)だよ。
  Nothing is ungrammatical, but the register is a newsreader's. Among friends the
  casual quotative `って` is the natural connector.

## Practice

**1.** 市(し)から、来月(らいげつ)から料金(りょうきん)が変(か)わる____通知(つうち)が届(とど)いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

変(か)わる<b>との</b>通知(つうち)

通知(つうち) is a message noun and the clause in front of it is what the notice
said, so the quotative `と` plus modifying `の` is what joins them. Note that the
sender is marked separately, with から.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 山田(やまだ)（という・との）方(かた)がお見(み)えです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)<b>という</b>方(かた)がお見(み)えです。

Giving a name is <code>という</code>'s job, not <code>との</code>'s. The other
version would read as "a person with Yamada", which is a different sentence
altogether.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese for a business email: "We have received a request from the client asking us to bring the deadline forward."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先方(せんぽう)より、納期(のうき)を早(はや)めてほしい<b>との</b>ご要望(ようぼう)をいただきました。

The sender takes より or から, and the content of the request goes in front of
<code>との</code>. Wrapping a request in this frame reports it without any hint of
whether the writer thinks it reasonable.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 取引先(とりひきさき)との連絡(れんらく)によると、納品(のうひん)は来週(らいしゅう)になるそうだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 取引先(とりひきさき)<b>からの</b>連絡(れんらく)によると、納品(のうひん)は来週(らいしゅう)になるそうだ。
（or 納品(のうひん)は来週(らいしゅう)になる<b>との</b>連絡(れんらく)が取引先(とりひきさき)からあった）

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)は明日(あした)出社(しゅっしゃ)しません。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)は明日(あした)出社(しゅっしゃ)しない<b>とのこと</b>です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the fact on the speaker's own authority. If it turns out to be wrong,
the speaker owns the error.

(b) relays it. The information came from somewhere else - the manager himself, a
colleague, a calendar entry - and the speaker is passing it on without vouching for
it. In business writing this is both politer and safer, which is why
<code>とのことです</code> is so common in email.

</details>
