# ～とあって

**Meaning** · because it is (a special occasion); given a circumstance this out of the ordinary, naturally ~

`とあって` names a cause and then shows what visibly came of it. The cause is never
ordinary - a public holiday, a rare visitor, a first in ten years - and the clause
after it reports the scene that resulted. It is a reporter's expression: you are
describing what happened and telling the reader what produced it.

## Formation

The clause in front is in plain form; nouns and な-adjectives attach bare, with no `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | noun + とあって | 連休(れんきゅう) → 連休(れんきゅう)とあって |
| Noun (stiff written) | noun + である + とあって | 新人(しんじん) → 新人(しんじん)であるとあって |
| な-adjective | stem + とあって | 有名(ゆうめい) → 有名(ゆうめい)とあって |
| い-adjective | plain form + とあって | 珍(めずら)しい → 珍(めずら)しいとあって |
| Verb | plain form + とあって | 出(で)る → 出(で)るとあって |
| Verb (past) | ～た + とあって | 優勝(ゆうしょう)した → 優勝(ゆうしょう)したとあって |
| Conditional relatives | ～とあっては / ～とあれば | 頼(たの)み → 頼(たの)みとあっては |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the second clause has to be a **statement of fact**, usually in
the past or in the present-progressive of a scene being observed. A request, an
invitation, a command or the speaker's own plan cannot follow `とあって`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
三連休(さんれんきゅう)<b>とあって</b>、駅(えき)は朝(あさ)から人(ひと)でいっぱいだった。
With a three-day weekend on, the station was packed from early morning.
</pre>

<pre>
初日(しょにち)<b>とあって</b>、会場(かいじょう)の前(まえ)には長(なが)い列(れつ)ができていた。
Being opening day, there was a long queue in front of the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
人気(にんき)歌手(かしゅ)が来(く)る<b>とあって</b>、チケットは一日(いちにち)で売(う)り切(き)れた。
Since a hugely popular singer was coming, the tickets sold out in a day.
</pre>

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)<b>とあって</b>、今日(きょう)の練習(れんしゅう)は昼(ひる)すぎに中止(ちゅうし)になった。
With rain that heavy, today's practice was called off in the early afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
珍(めずら)しい鳥(とり)が姿(すがた)を見(み)せた<b>とあって</b>、公園(こうえん)にはカメラを持(も)った人(ひと)が集(あつ)まった。
A rare bird had shown itself, and the park filled up with people carrying cameras.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)ぶりの優勝(ゆうしょう)<b>とあって</b>、商店街(しょうてんがい)は夜(よる)遅(おそ)くまで賑(にぎ)わっていた。
It was their first title in ten years, so the shopping street stayed lively until late.
</pre>

<pre>
首相(しゅしょう)自(みずか)らが出席(しゅっせき)する<b>とあって</b>、会場(かいじょう)の警備(けいび)はいつになく厳(きび)しかった。
With the prime minister attending in person, security at the venue was unusually tight.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい駅(えき)ができて初(はじ)めての夏祭(なつまつ)り<b>とあって</b>、町(まち)は例年(れいねん)にない人出(ひとで)を記録(きろく)した。
As it was the first summer festival since the new station opened, the town drew a bigger crowd than in any normal year.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`とあって` belongs to **written and reported Japanese**: newspaper copy, television
news, magazine features, event write-ups. It is not rude or archaic, and an
announcer says it without a second thought, but you will rarely hear it across a
dinner table. Among friends the same thought comes out as `連休だから`.

Two things it always assumes. First, that the circumstance is **worth remarking on**
- something special, unusual or newsworthy. Nobody writes
<code>水曜日(すいようび)とあって</code>. Second, that the result is a **natural,
almost predictable consequence** the reader will accept without argument: a holiday
means crowds, a famous guest means a queue, heavy rain means cancellation. If the
reader has to be talked into the connection, `とあって` is the wrong tool.

Because it is descriptive, the pattern sits badly with the speaker's own small
affairs. <code>頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いとあって</code> reads as though your headache
had been reported in the paper. Third-person subjects, public events and observed
scenes are its natural home; a first-person subject is possible only when you are
narrating yourself from the outside, as in a memoir.

`とあって` is also fixed in tense in a way learners miss: it does not itself carry
tense. <code>優勝(ゆうしょう)したとあって</code> and
<code>優勝(ゆうしょう)するとあって</code> differ, but the `とあって` never becomes
❌<code>とあった</code>. The final verb of the sentence carries the tense.

## Contrast

### ～とあって vs ～ので・～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ので` and `から` join any cause to any result, including your
own plans. `とあって` needs a **notable circumstance** and reports an **observed
outcome**.

</div>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)<b>だから</b>、明日(あした)は海(うみ)へ行(い)こう。
It's a long weekend, so let's go to the sea tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)<b>とあって</b>、海沿(うみぞ)いの道(みち)は昼前(ひるまえ)から渋滞(じゅうたい)していた。
With the long weekend on, the coast road was jammed from before noon.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence collapses: ❌<code>連休(れんきゅう)とあって、海(うみ)へ行(い)こう</code>
puts a proposal where the pattern demands a report. The second sentence works with
`だから` too - it simply loses the flavour of a scene being written up for a reader.

### ～とあって vs ～だけあって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけあって` **praises**: the result lives up to what the cause
promised. `とあって` only explains, and passes no judgement at all.

</div>

<pre>
一流(いちりゅう)のホテル<b>だけあって</b>、サービスが行(い)き届(とど)いている。
It's a first-class hotel, and the service shows it.
</pre>

<pre>
一流(いちりゅう)のホテルが会場(かいじょう)<b>とあって</b>、申(もう)し込(こ)みは定員(ていいん)の三倍(さんばい)に達(たっ)した。
With a first-class hotel as the venue, applications ran to three times the number of places.
</pre>

`だけあって` measures a result against a standard and finds it worthy, so it needs
something praiseworthy on both sides. `とあって` is indifferent: heavy rain, a
scandal and a royal visit are all equally good causes for it.

### ～とあって vs ～とあっては・～とあれば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とあって` reports a fact that **has** produced a result.
`とあっては` and `とあれば` are conditionals: **if** it is a case of ~, then this
follows - and what follows is usually the speaker's own decision.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)の頼(たの)み<b>とあっては</b>、断(ことわ)るわけにはいかない。
If it's my teacher asking, I can hardly refuse.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)が来(こ)られる<b>とあって</b>、部屋(へや)は前日(ぜんじつ)から片付(かたづ)けてあった。
Because the teacher was coming, the room had been tidied since the day before.
</pre>

This is where the ban on volitional endings dissolves: `とあっては` and `とあれば`
exist precisely to introduce what the speaker will therefore do. If your sentence
ends in <code>～しかない</code>, <code>～わけにはいかない</code> or a decision, you want
the conditional form, not bare `とあって`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 熱(ねつ)がある<b>とあって</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)んだ。 → ✅ 熱(ねつ)があった<b>ので</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)んだ。
  A private, everyday reason is too small for `とあって`. The pattern wants a
  circumstance a stranger would find worth reporting.

- ❌ 連休(れんきゅう)<b>とあって</b>、温泉(おんせん)にでも行(い)きませんか。 → ✅ 連休(れんきゅう)<b>だし</b>、温泉(おんせん)にでも行(い)きませんか。
  The second clause must state what happened, not propose what to do.

- ❌ 有名(ゆうめい)な店(みせ)だ<b>とあって</b>、行列(ぎょうれつ)ができていた。 → ✅ 有名(ゆうめい)な店(みせ)<b>とあって</b>、行列(ぎょうれつ)ができていた。
  Nouns and な-adjectives attach with nothing between them and `とあって`. The stiff
  written alternative is <code>である</code>, never `だ`.

- ❌ 開店(かいてん)初日(しょにち)<b>とあった</b>ので、店内(てんない)は混(こ)んでいた。 → ✅ 開店(かいてん)初日(しょにち)<b>とあって</b>、店内(てんない)は混(こ)んでいた。
  `とあって` is already a connective. It does not conjugate and does not take another
  reason word after it.

## Practice

**1.** 大雪(おおゆき)の予報(よほう)が出(で)た____、スーパーの棚(たな)は夕方(ゆうがた)にはほとんど空(から)になっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

予報(よほう)が出(で)た<b>とあって</b>

A snow warning is a public, newsworthy circumstance, and empty shelves are the scene
it produced - exactly the report shape `とあって` is built for. The verb in front
stays plain past.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 世界(せかい)的(てき)な建築家(けんちくか)が設計(せっけい)した（とあって・だけあって）、建物(たてもの)のどこを見(み)ても美(うつく)しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

設計(せっけい)した<b>だけあって</b>

The sentence is admiring the building, and praise that lives up to a reputation is
`だけあって`. `とあって` would be possible only with a factual outcome instead, such
as crowds of visitors.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It was the first game of the season, and the stadium was full an hour before kick-off."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今季(こんき)最初(さいしょ)の試合(しあい)<b>とあって</b>、スタジアムは開始(かいし)一時間(いちじかん)前(まえ)には満員(まんいん)になっていた。

The English is a piece of sports reporting, which is `とあって` at its most natural.
The noun attaches directly, and the outcome clause is a plain observed fact.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 社長(しゃちょう)のご指名(しめい)とあって、お受(う)けするしかありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 社長(しゃちょう)のご指名(しめい)<b>とあっては</b>、お受(う)けするしかありません。

The ending is the speaker's own decision, so the conditional `とあっては` is required.
Bare `とあって` can only be followed by a report of what happened.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した<b>ので</b>、花火大会(はなびたいかい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
(b) 台風(たいふう)の接近(せっきん)中(ちゅう)<b>とあって</b>、花火大会(はなびたいかい)は早々(そうそう)に中止(ちゅうし)が決(き)まった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is neutral information. It could be said to a friend, written in a diary, or
posted in a group chat, and it makes no claim that the cause was remarkable.

(b) reads like a news item. The typhoon is presented as a circumstance of public
significance, and the cancellation as the outcome any reader would expect from it.
Said casually to a friend, (b) would sound as though you were narrating your own
evening for television.

</details>

# ～ときている

**Meaning** · and on top of that it's ~; and since it's ~ (of all things) - adds the fact that clinches the case

`ときている` throws one more fact onto a pile and lets it land. The speaker has
already listed one or two things; this is the last item, delivered with a small
thump, and it is the one that settles the matter. Depending on what is being piled
up, the effect is exasperation ("and the boss repeats himself, on top of it all") or
delight ("cheap, fast, *and* delicious"). It is spoken Japanese with an attitude in
it, and it is written both in kana and, in print, as
<code>と来(き)ている</code>.

## Formation

The clause in front is plain. Nouns and な-adjectives attach bare.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | noun + ときている | 夜勤(やきん) → 夜勤(やきん)ときている |
| な-adjective | stem + ときている | 下手(へた) → 下手(へた)ときている |
| い-adjective | plain form + ときている | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いときている |
| Verb | plain form + ときている | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れるときている |
| Verb (negative) | ～ない + ときている | 払(はら)わない → 払(はら)わないときている |
| Contracted (speech) | ～ときてる | 狭(せま)い → 狭(せま)いときてる |
| Followed by a reason | ～ときているから | 遠(とお)い → 遠(とお)いときているから |
| Conditional variant | ～ときては | 留守(るす) → 留守(るす)ときては |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the 来(く)る inside it is the ordinary verb "come", frozen in the
て-form. That is why the whole thing is <code>と来(き)ている</code> and never
❌<code>と来(こ)ている</code>. Nothing in the sentence is actually arriving; think
of it as "and then along comes the fact that…".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
安(やす)くて早(はや)い。しかもうまい<b>ときている</b>。
Cheap and quick - and it tastes good into the bargain.
</pre>

<pre>
隣(となり)の犬(いぬ)は夜中(よなか)に吠(ほ)える。おまけに人(ひと)を見(み)ると飛(と)びつく<b>ときている</b>。
The dog next door barks in the middle of the night - and it jumps at anyone it sees, too.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は仕事(しごと)が速(はや)い上(うえ)に、字(じ)まできれい<b>ときている</b>。
He works fast, and on top of that he has beautiful handwriting.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は駅(えき)から遠(とお)い<b>ときているから</b>、客(きゃく)が入(はい)らないのも無理(むり)はない。
The place is miles from the station on top of everything, so no wonder it gets no customers.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い、日当(ひあ)たりは悪(わる)い、家賃(やちん)は高(たか)い<b>ときている</b>。よく借(か)り手(て)がついたものだ。
Small, dark, and expensive to boot. I'm amazed they found a tenant.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの課長(かちょう)はゴルフの話(はなし)しかしない。それも同(おな)じ話(はなし)を三度(さんど)する<b>ときている</b>。
Our section chief talks about nothing but golf - and tells you the same story three times, at that.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りは明日(あした)、資料(しりょう)はまだ届(とど)かない、担当者(たんとうしゃ)は出張中(しゅっちょうちゅう)<b>ときては</b>、間(ま)に合(あ)わせようがない。
Deadline tomorrow, materials still not here, and the person in charge away on a trip - there's no way to make it.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は据(す)え置(お)き、量(りょう)はそのまま、それでいて味(あじ)は以前(いぜん)より良(よ)くなった<b>ときている</b>んですから、売(う)れないはずがありません。
Same price, same portion, and better-tasting than before on top of that - of course it sells.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **spoken** pattern, and a colourful one. It lives in conversation, in
column-writing that imitates conversation, in comedy routines and in the grumbling
half of a phone call. It is not rude, but it is never neutral: using `ときている`
means you have taken a position on the facts you are listing.

The characteristic shape is a **list with a punchline**. One or two facts are laid
out with `し`, `上に`, `おまけに` or just a full stop, and then the last one arrives
with `ときている`. Because it is the closer, it usually carries the most extreme
item. Put it on the mildest fact in the list and the sentence deflates.

It also normally **stays plain** even when the surrounding conversation is polite.
Speakers reach for <code>～ときているんです</code> or
<code>～ときているものですから</code> rather than ❌<code>ときています</code>, which
sounds like a joke about being formal. In a written report it is out of place
altogether; there you would write `～上に` or `しかも`.

One more feature worth knowing: the subject of a `ときている` sentence is almost
always **someone or something other than the speaker**. Turning it on yourself -
<code>私(わたし)は不器用(ぶきよう)ときている</code> - is possible, but it comes
out as deliberate self-mockery, the voice of an essayist making fun of himself.

## Contrast

### ～ときている vs ～とあって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とあって` is a **reporter's** cause: neutral, written, no
judgement. `ときている` is a **talker's** cause: colloquial, evaluative, and always
piling on.

</div>

<pre>
人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)<b>とあって</b>、店(みせ)は営業(えいぎょう)時間(じかん)を短縮(たんしゅく)した。
Short of staff, the shop cut its opening hours.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)は足(た)りない、機械(きかい)は古(ふる)い<b>ときている</b>。もう回(まわ)らないよ。
Not enough people, and clapped-out machines on top of that. It just doesn't work any more.
</pre>

You could not put the second sentence in a news bulletin, and the first would sound
oddly stiff in a break room. Note too that `ときている` almost demands company: it
wants at least one other fact beside it, while `とあって` is perfectly happy alone.

### ～ときている vs ～ときたら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ときたら` puts a **topic** up front, ready to be complained
about. `ときている` puts a **fact** at the end, as the last straw.

</div>

<pre>
うちの息子(むすこ)<b>ときたら</b>、休(やす)みの日(ひ)は昼(ひる)まで寝(ね)ている。
My son, honestly - on days off he sleeps until noon.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)は昼(ひる)まで寝(ね)ている、起(お)きても手伝(てつだ)わない<b>ときている</b>。
He sleeps until noon on his days off, and when he does get up he doesn't lift a finger.
</pre>

They share a register and often appear in the same breath, but they occupy different
slots in the sentence. `ときたら` replaces `は`; `ときている` replaces the final
`だ`. Learners who mix them produce ❌<code>うちの息子(むすこ)ときている、…</code>,
which leaves the sentence with a predicate and no subject.

### ～ときている vs ～上(うえ)に・しかも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `上に` and `しかも` add another item **neutrally**. `ときている`
adds it and simultaneously says *and that settles it*.

</div>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い上(うえ)に、窓(まど)が一(ひと)つもない。
This room is cramped, and it has no windows.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い。窓(まど)が一(ひと)つもない<b>ときている</b>。
Cramped - and not a single window, at that.
</pre>

The information is identical; the second sentence has a speaker in it. Note that
`上に` can add a good thing to a good thing quite blandly, whereas `ときている` will
colour even praise with a kind of "would you believe it" astonishment.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いだ<b>ときている</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>ときている</b>。
  い-adjectives attach in plain form. There is never a `だ` before `ときている`, and
  nouns attach bare as well: <code>夜勤(やきん)ときている</code>.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)<b>ときている</b>、約束(やくそく)を守(まも)ったことがない。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>ときたら</b>、約束(やくそく)を守(まも)ったことがない。
  A topic at the head of the sentence is `ときたら`. `ときている` can only close a
  clause.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>ときている</b>ので、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)きます。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>ので</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)きます。
  A single plain reason has nothing to be piled on top of. `ときている` needs a list
  behind it and an attitude in it.

- ❌ 交通(こうつう)の便(べん)も悪(わる)い<b>ときています</b>。 → ✅ 交通(こうつう)の便(べん)も悪(わる)い<b>ときているんです</b>。
  The pattern resists ます. If you need politeness, wrap it in `んです` and leave the
  `ときている` itself plain.

## Practice

**1.** 給料(きゅうりょう)は安(やす)い、残業(ざんぎょう)は多(おお)い、おまけに上司(じょうし)は話(はなし)を聞(き)いてくれない____。よく続(つづ)けているものだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

聞(き)いてくれない<b>ときている</b>

Three complaints in a row, with the last one delivered as the clincher, is the exact
shape this pattern was made for. The verb stays in plain negative form in front of
it.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの人(ひと)<b>ときたら</b>

The phrase sits at the head of the sentence marking the topic to be complained
about, which is `ときたら`. `ときている` would have to come at the end, after the
predicate.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The hotel was old, the food was cold, and the staff were rude on top of it all."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"On top of it all" attached to the last item of a grumbling list is `ときている`
almost word for word. 失礼(しつれい) is a な-adjective, so it attaches with no `だ`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 息子(むすこ)は勉強(べんきょう)もしないし、部屋(へや)も片付(かたづ)けないときていますから、困(こま)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 息子(むすこ)は勉強(べんきょう)もしないし、部屋(へや)も片付(かたづ)けない<b>ときているんです</b>から、困(こま)っています。

The content is right; only the politeness is misplaced. `ときています` sounds like a
parody of formal speech, so keep the pattern plain and let `んです` carry the
politeness.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この地域(ちいき)はバスの本数(ほんすう)が少(すく)ない上(うえ)に、終電(しゅうでん)も早(はや)い。
(b) この地域(ちいき)はバスの本数(ほんすう)が少(すく)ない。終電(しゅうでん)も早(はや)い<b>ときている</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral description. It would be at home in a guidebook, a survey, or an
estate agent's honest summary, and the speaker's feelings are not on display.

(b) is someone talking. The early last train is offered as the final blow, and the
listener is expected to react - to sympathise, or to agree that living there would
be impossible. The facts are the same; only (b) has a complaint inside it.

</details>

# ～ところから

**Meaning** · from the fact that; on the strength of the observation that ~ - the noticed detail that a name, a nickname or a conclusion came out of

`ところから` points at one **observable feature** of a thing and says that this
feature is where something else came from. Overwhelmingly, what came from it is a
**name**: a mountain is called such-and-such because it looks like Fuji, a district
kept its old name because smiths once worked there. Its second job is to give the
evidence behind an inference - the light is on, *from which* we know he is home. In
both uses the speaker is explaining an origin to someone who did not know it, which
is why the pattern lives in captions, guidebooks and reference works.

## Formation

<code>ところ</code> is a noun, so what precedes it is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ところから | 似(に)ている → 似(に)ているところから |
| Verb (past) | ～た + ところから | 集(あつ)まった → 集(あつ)まったところから |
| い-adjective | plain form + ところから | 赤(あか)い → 赤(あか)いところから |
| な-adjective | ～な + ところから | 静(しず)かな → 静(しず)かなところから |
| Noun | ～である + ところから | 名産地(めいさんち) → 名産地(めいさんち)であるところから |
| Noun (past) | ～だった + ところから | 沼地(ぬまち)だった → 沼地(ぬまち)だったところから |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the naming use has a fixed second half. Look for
<code>～と呼(よ)ばれる</code>, <code>～と呼(よ)ばれるようになった</code>,
<code>～という名(な)がついた</code> or <code>～と名付(なづ)けられた</code>. If you
learn the whole frame together, the pattern becomes almost automatic to recognise.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
山(やま)の形(かたち)が富士山(ふじさん)に似(に)ている<b>ところから</b>、地元(じもと)では「裏(うら)富士(ふじ)」と呼(よ)ばれている。
Because the mountain looks like Mt Fuji, locals call it "the back Fuji".
</pre>

<pre>
葉(は)が猫(ねこ)の耳(みみ)の形(かたち)をしている<b>ところから</b>、この花(はな)にはネコミミという名(な)がついた。
The plant got the name "cat's ear" from the shape of its leaves.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)の明(あ)かりがついている<b>ところから</b>、彼(かれ)はまだ帰(かえ)っていないと分(わ)かる。
From the fact that the light is on in his room, we can tell he hasn't left yet.
</pre>

<pre>
足跡(あしあと)が二人分(ふたりぶん)残(のこ)っていた<b>ところから</b>、警察(けいさつ)は協力者(きょうりょくしゃ)がいたと見(み)ている。
Two sets of footprints were left behind, from which the police infer that someone helped.
</pre>

<pre>
江戸時代(えどじだい)にこのあたりに鍛冶屋(かじや)が多(おお)かった<b>ところから</b>、今(いま)も鍛冶町(かじまち)という地名(ちめい)が残(のこ)っている。
The place is still called Kajimachi because so many smiths worked here in the Edo period.
</pre>

<pre>
味(あじ)が栗(くり)によく似(に)ている<b>ところから</b>、この芋(いも)は市場(しじょう)では栗芋(くりいも)の名(な)で売(う)られている。
Its taste is so close to chestnut that the market sells this tuber as "chestnut yam".
</pre>

<pre>
手紙(てがみ)の字(じ)が震(ふる)えている<b>ところから</b>、書(か)いた人(ひと)の体調(たいちょう)がよくなかったことがうかがえる。
The shakiness of the handwriting suggests that whoever wrote the letter was unwell.
</pre>

<pre>
かつてこの一帯(いったい)が一面(いちめん)の沼地(ぬまち)だった<b>ところから</b>、駅(えき)の名前(なまえ)には今(いま)でも「沼(ぬま)」の字(じ)が使(つか)われている。
Because this whole area was once marshland, the station name still contains the character for "marsh".
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ところから` is **written and explanatory**. Its home is the caption under a museum
exhibit, the etymology paragraph of a dictionary entry, a guidebook, a company
history, or a news report summarising what investigators have concluded. Nobody says
it while chatting; in speech the same idea comes out as `～から` or
<code>～ので、それで</code>.

The tone is neutral and slightly scholarly. There is no emotion in it at all, which
is part of why it fits reference writing so well: the writer is not persuading, only
recording where something came from.

Two structural habits are worth memorising. First, the second clause is almost
always **passive or descriptive** - 呼(よ)ばれる, つけられた, 残(のこ)っている, 見(み)られている.
The pattern explains how things came to be called or regarded as they are, not what
anyone decided to do. Second, the thing in front of `ところから` is a **noticeable
feature**, something you could point at: a shape, a colour, a sound, a number, a
mark on the ground. An abstract state works badly.

`ことから` does the same work and is considerably more common; treat `ところから` as
its slightly more literary variant, with the extra suggestion that the speaker is
pointing at one particular *aspect* of the thing.

## Contrast

### ～ところから vs ～ことから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two are interchangeable in most naming sentences. `ことから`
is the **ordinary** choice and takes any kind of grounds; `ところから` is more
**literary** and prefers a concrete, observable feature.

</div>

<pre>
白(しろ)い花(はな)を咲(さ)かせる<b>ことから</b>、この木(き)は雪柳(ゆきやなぎ)と呼(よ)ばれる。
It bears white blossom, which is how the shrub came to be called "snow willow".
</pre>

<pre>
石(いし)の表面(ひょうめん)に波(なみ)のような模様(もよう)がある<b>ところから</b>、この岩(いわ)は波岩(なみいわ)と名付(なづ)けられた。
The wave-like pattern on its surface is what gave this rock the name "wave rock".
</pre>

Where they part company is breadth. `ことから` happily takes an abstract or negative
reason - <code>前例(ぜんれい)がないことから、判断(はんだん)は見送(みおく)られた</code>
- while `ところから` on the same sentence sounds as though you were pointing at a
non-existent precedent. If there is nothing to point at, use `ことから`.

### ～ところから vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` and `ので` explain **why something was done**.
`ところから` explains **what an observation gave rise to**: a name, a nickname, or a
conclusion.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた<b>から</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)って出(で)た。
It was raining, so I took an umbrella out with me.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)が濡(ぬ)れている<b>ところから</b>、外(そと)は雨(あめ)らしいと判断(はんだん)した。
From the wetness of the umbrella I judged that it must be raining outside.
</pre>

Notice the direction. In the first sentence the rain causes an action; in the second
the wet umbrella is evidence, and what follows is not an action but a **conclusion**.
❌<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていたところから、傘(かさ)を持(も)って出(で)た</code>
is the standard error - reasons for your own behaviour are `から` territory.

### ～ところから (grounds) vs ～ところから (starting point)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if `ところ` means a **stage or moment**, then `から` is the
ordinary "from": the story starts *from* that point. If `ところ` is a feature you
could point at, it is the grounds pattern.

</div>

<pre>
物語(ものがたり)は主人公(しゅじんこう)が島(しま)に着(つ)いた<b>ところから</b>始(はじ)まる。
The story begins from the point where the hero arrives on the island.
</pre>

<pre>
主人公(しゅじんこう)が島(しま)の言葉(ことば)を知(し)っていた<b>ところから</b>、読者(どくしゃ)は彼(かれ)の過去(かこ)を察(さっ)する。
Because the hero already knows the island's language, the reader guesses at his past.
</pre>

The two are spelled identically and are told apart only by what follows. A verb of
starting, continuing or resuming - 始(はじ)まる, 再開(さいかい)する,
話(はな)し直(なお)す - means you are looking at a point on a timeline. A verb of
naming, judging or inferring means you are looking at grounds. If neither fits, read
the sentence again before deciding.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>ところから</b>、この町(まち)は「眠(ねむ)る町(まち)」と呼(よ)ばれる。 → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>なところから</b>、この町(まち)は「眠(ねむ)る町(まち)」と呼(よ)ばれる。
  <code>ところ</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective needs its `な`, and a noun needs
  <code>である</code>.

- ❌ 寒(さむ)かった<b>ところから</b>、暖房(だんぼう)をつけた。 → ✅ 寒(さむ)かった<b>ので</b>、暖房(だんぼう)をつけた。
  The second clause has to be something concluded or named, not something the
  speaker decided to do.

- ❌ この島(しま)は形(かたち)が靴(くつ)に似(に)ている<b>ところから</b>、多(おお)くの観光客(かんこうきゃく)を集(あつ)めている。 → ✅ この島(しま)は形(かたち)が靴(くつ)に似(に)ている<b>ことから</b>「靴島(くつじま)」と呼(よ)ばれている。
  Attracting tourists is a consequence, not an origin. `ところから` needs the second
  clause to be the name or judgement that grew out of the observation.

- ❌ 明(あ)かりがついている<b>ところで</b>、中(なか)に誰(だれ)かいるようだ。 → ✅ 明(あ)かりがついている<b>ところから</b>、中(なか)に誰(だれ)かいるようだ。
  Only `から` gives the "grounds" reading. <code>ところで</code> and
  <code>ところに</code> belong to the temporal ところ family and change the meaning
  completely.

# ～こととて

**Meaning** · because it is a case of ~; it being ~ - an old causal connective, now used almost only to excuse a shortcoming

`こととて` gives a reason, and the reason is nearly always an **apology in
advance**: we are out in the country, so there is nothing to offer you; it was all
so sudden, so nothing was ready; the child did not know any better. The form is
several centuries old and has survived into modern Japanese only inside that
apologetic frame. Recognise it in a formal letter or an older novel; when you need
to say the same thing, say `ので` or `ものですから`.

## Formation

<code>こと</code> is a noun, so what precedes it modifies a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | ～の + こととて | 田舎(いなか) → 田舎(いなか)のこととて |
| な-adjective | ～な + こととて | 不慣(ふな)れな → 不慣(ふな)れなこととて |
| い-adjective | plain form + こととて | 慌(あわ)ただしい → 慌(あわ)ただしいこととて |
| Verb | plain form + こととて | 伺(うかが)う → 初(はじ)めて伺(うかが)うこととて |
| Verb (negative, literary) | ～ぬ + こととて | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らぬこととて |
| Past | ～た + こととて | 急(きゅう)に決(き)まった → 急(きゅう)に決(き)まったこととて |
| Fixed opener | なにぶん + ～こととて | - → なにぶん急(きゅう)なこととて |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the literary negative <code>～ぬ</code> is the giveaway.
<code>知(し)らぬこととて</code> and <code>慣(な)れぬこととて</code> are far more
frequent than the modern <code>知(し)らないこととて</code>, because the whole
expression belongs to an older layer of the language and pulls the negative back
with it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
なにぶん不慣(ふな)れな<b>こととて</b>、行(い)き届(とど)かぬ点(てん)はお許(ゆる)しください。
As we are new to this, please forgive anything we have overlooked.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)な<b>こととて</b>、粗末(そまつ)なものしかご用意(ようい)できませんでした。
It was all so sudden that we could only put out something very plain.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)のした<b>こととて</b>、どうかお許(ゆる)しいただきたく存(ぞん)じます。
It was a child's doing, and I would ask you to forgive it.
</pre>

<pre>
田舎(いなか)の<b>こととて</b>、これといったもてなしもできませんが、どうぞごゆっくり。
This being the countryside, we have no real hospitality to offer, but do make yourself at home.
</pre>

<pre>
知(し)らぬ<b>こととて</b>、とんだご無礼(ぶれい)を申(もう)し上(あ)げました。
Not knowing, I said something quite unpardonable.
</pre>

<pre>
夜分(やぶん)の<b>こととて</b>、お電話(でんわ)は明朝(みょうちょう)に改(あらた)めさせていただきます。
As it is late at night, I shall telephone again tomorrow morning instead.
</pre>

<pre>
戦後(せんご)間(ま)もない頃(ころ)の<b>こととて</b>、食(た)べるものにも事欠(ことか)く暮(く)らしであった。
Those being the years just after the war, it was a life short even of food.
</pre>

<pre>
なにぶんにも古(ふる)い建物(たてもの)の<b>こととて</b>、床(ゆか)のきしむ音(おと)ばかりはご容赦(ようしゃ)願(ねが)います。
The building being an old one, we must ask you to bear with the creaking floors.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Read this one; do not use it.** `こととて` is archaic. Where you will actually meet
it is a narrow set of places: formal written apologies and letters of thanks, the
speech of an innkeeper or a host in fiction, historical novels and period drama, and
occasionally the prepared remarks of a much older speaker at a ceremony. Used by a
learner in ordinary conversation it does not sound polite - it sounds like costume
drama. The modern equivalents are `ので`, `ものですから`, `ため` and, in a formal
letter, <code>～につき</code>.

Almost every real example is an **excuse for a deficiency**. The second clause asks
for indulgence: お許(ゆる)しください, ご容赦(ようしゃ)ください,
失礼(しつれい)申(もう)し上(あ)げました, できませんでした. A `こととて`
clause followed by a neutral statement of fact - "it being Tuesday, the shop was
busy" - is not how the pattern behaves.

The surrounding language is old too, and it tends to arrive as a package: なにぶん
or なにぶんにも in front, the literary negative `ぬ` inside, humble verbs
(存(ぞん)じます, 伺(うかが)う, 申(もう)し上(あ)げる) after. That whole
register is the real content of the page. If you can see the frame coming, you will
never misread `こととて` as anything but "because".

One practical warning: it looks like `ことで` and `ことだから`, and it is neither.
Do not let the surface similarity persuade you that it is available for everyday
reasons.

## Contrast

### ～こととて vs ～ので・～ものですから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, three centuries apart. Write
`ものですから` for an apologetic reason and `ので` for a neutral one; keep
`こととて` for reading.

</div>

<pre>
急(きゅう)な<b>こととて</b>、ご挨拶(あいさつ)もできませんでした。
It being so sudden, I was unable even to pay my respects.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に決(き)まった<b>ものですから</b>、ご挨拶(あいさつ)もできませんでした。
Because it was decided at short notice, I wasn't able to come and greet you properly.
</pre>

The two sentences carry exactly the same apology. The second is what a person says
at a leaving party in 2026; the first is what appears in a printed letter, or in the
mouth of a character in a novel set eighty years ago. Note that `ものですから` is
already apologetic in itself, which is why it is the closest modern match -
`ので` alone is flatter.

### ～こととて vs ～ことだから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `こととて` = **because it is** the case that ~ (excuse).
`ことだから` = **knowing what ~ is like**, we can predict what follows.

</div>

<pre>
田舎(いなか)の<b>こととて</b>、バスは一時間(いちじかん)に一本(いっぽん)しかありません。
This being the countryside, there is only one bus an hour.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)に正確(せいかく)な彼(かれ)の<b>ことだから</b>、もう着(つ)いているはずだ。
Knowing how punctual he is, he must be there already.
</pre>

`ことだから` takes a person or thing whose character is already known to both
speakers, and leads to a **prediction**. `こととて` takes a circumstance and leads to
an **apology**. They are not variants of one pattern, and the resemblance is a trap
of spelling only.

### ～こととて vs ～とて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `こととて` is causal, "because". Bare `とて` is concessive,
"even; even if". The `こと` is doing all the work.

</div>

<pre>
子供(こども)の<b>こととて</b>、大目(おおめ)に見(み)てやってください。
It was only a child, so please overlook it.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)の言(い)うこと<b>とて</b>、聞(き)き流(なが)してよいものではない。
Even a child's words are not to be brushed aside.
</pre>

Both are literary, both are old, and they differ by one noun. Misread the first as
"even though it was a child" and the sentence turns from forgiveness into reproach.
When you meet `とて` in a text, look first for a `こと` in front of it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 田舎(いなか)<b>こととて</b>、不便(ふべん)です。 → ✅ 田舎(いなか)<b>のこととて</b>、不便(ふべん)でございます。
  A noun needs `の` before <code>こと</code>, and a な-adjective needs `な`. The
  pattern is a noun phrase, not a particle.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)の<b>こととて</b>、遠足(えんそく)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)の<b>ため</b>、遠足(えんそく)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
  A neutral announcement has nothing to apologise for. `こととて` in a school notice
  reads as parody.

- ❌ すみません、寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>こととて</b>遅刻(ちこく)しました。 → ✅ すみません、寝坊(ねぼう)してしまったものですから遅刻(ちこく)しました。
  Spoken to a colleague, `こととて` sounds theatrical. Everyday spoken excuses take
  `ものですから` or `もので`.

- ❌ 慣(な)れない<b>こととて</b>、自信(じしん)があります。 → ✅ 慣(な)れぬ<b>こととて</b>、至(いた)らぬ点(てん)も多(おお)いかと存(ぞん)じます。
  The second clause has to admit a shortcoming and ask for indulgence. A confident
  statement after `こととて` contradicts the whole expression.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence?
なにぶん山中(さんちゅう)の<b>こととて</b>、ご不便(ふべん)をおかけいたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In writing rather than speech: a notice pinned up in a mountain inn, a line in a
guest information sheet, or a letter from the proprietor. The なにぶん opener, the
humble おかけいたします and `こととて` together mark it as a prepared written
apology, not something a receptionist would say aloud.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
急(きゅう)な<b>こととて</b>、十分(じゅうぶん)な準備(じゅんび)ができませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

急(きゅう)に決(き)まった<b>ものですから</b>、十分(じゅうぶん)な準備(じゅんび)ができませんでした。

`ものですから` carries the same note of apology in living Japanese. Plain
<code>ので</code> would also be correct, though it sounds a shade more matter-of-fact
than the original.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 時間(じかん)に厳(きび)しい部長(ぶちょう)の（こととて・ことだから）、もう会議室(かいぎしつ)で待(ま)っているだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)の<b>ことだから</b>

The sentence predicts what a known person will have done, which is `ことだから`.
`こととて` would need an apology after it, and there is nothing here to apologise
for.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the register error: 昨日(きのう)忙(いそが)しいこととて、メールの返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れてごめん。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)忙(いそが)しかった<b>もので</b>、メールの返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れてごめん。

Two things clash. `こととて` cannot sit next to ごめん - one is period-drama
formality and the other is friend-to-friend speech - and a casual apology takes
<code>もので</code> or <code>ものだから</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain what the `こととて` contributes:
初(はじ)めて伺(うかが)う<b>こととて</b>、勝手(かって)が分(わ)からず失礼(しつれい)をいたしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It supplies the reason for the failing admitted in the second half: this was my first
visit, so I did not know how things are done here. Structurally it is doing no more
than `ので` would.

What it adds is register. The speaker is presenting the apology as a formal, composed
one - the kind written down or delivered in a prepared greeting - and the literary
伺(うかが)う and いたしました around it belong to the same layer of language.

</details>

# ゆえに

**Meaning** · therefore; hence; it follows that - the "therefore" of proofs, judgements and formal argument

故(ゆえ)に stands at the head of a sentence and presents what follows as the
**necessary consequence** of what has just been stated. It is not "so" in the
conversational sense; it is the word a mathematician writes at the end of a proof
and a court writes before its ruling. Reach for it only when the step really is a
deduction, and only in writing that can carry the weight.

## Usage

Two shapes, and it is worth keeping them apart.

As a **conjunction**, ゆえに opens a sentence or follows a comma, with the premise in
front of it: <code>前提(ぜんてい)が正(ただ)しくない。ゆえに、結論(けつろん)も正(ただ)しくない。</code>
This is the sense the word is usually taught for, and it is the one you will meet in
philosophy, mathematics, logic textbooks, legal judgements and formal essays.
そのゆえに is not used; the plain ゆえに, or それゆえ / それゆえに, is the form.

As a **postposition**, it attaches to what caused something and means "because of".
A noun takes `の` or joins directly: <code>貧(まず)しさゆえに</code>,
<code>病気(びょうき)のゆえに</code>. A verb or adjective takes `が`:
<code>若(わか)いがゆえに</code>, <code>知(し)らなかったがゆえに</code>. The `が`
is the old genitive, the same one inside <code>わがくに</code>; without it a verb in
front of ゆえに sounds unfinished to a Japanese ear, so learn
<code>～がゆえに</code> as one unit.

The bare noun 故(ゆえ) - "reason, grounds" - survives in a handful of set phrases:
<code>故(ゆえ)あって</code> ("for reasons of my own"),
<code>何(なに)故(ゆえ)</code> ("why", literary). The character is read **ゆえ**
here; the こ of 故人(こじん) and 故障(こしょう) is a different word entirely.

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)れば地面(じめん)は濡(ぬ)れる。地面(じめん)は濡(ぬ)れていない。<b>ゆえに</b>、雨(あめ)は降(ふ)らなかった。
If it rains the ground gets wet. The ground is not wet. Therefore it did not rain.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)が不足(ふそく)している。<b>ゆえに</b>、結論(けつろん)は保留(ほりゅう)とする。
The data are insufficient; the conclusion is therefore reserved.
</pre>

<pre>
若(わか)さ<b>ゆえに</b>犯(おか)した過(あやま)ちであり、本人(ほんにん)も深(ふか)く反省(はんせい)している。
It was a mistake born of his youth, and he himself deeply regrets it.
</pre>

<pre>
本件(ほんけん)は当事者間(とうじしゃかん)ですでに和解(わかい)が成立(せいりつ)している。<b>ゆえに</b>、訴(うった)えの利益(りえき)は失(うしな)われたものと認(みと)められる。
The parties to this case have already reached a settlement. The action has therefore lost its purpose.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

ゆえに is **written, formal and faintly philosophical**. Spoken aloud in ordinary
conversation it sounds either pompous or like a joke - a student announcing
<code>ゆえに、今日(きょう)は飲(の)みに行(い)く</code> is being funny on
purpose, and the humour depends on everyone recognising the register. In speech the
word for "therefore" is `だから`; in a business email it is `そのため`; in a report or
a paper it is `したがって`.

Among those neighbours the ordering is worth having. `だから` is spoken and neutral.
`そのため` states a practical cause and is the everyday written choice. `したがって`
is the standard connective of academic and business writing, and is what most
learners actually want when they reach for ゆえに. `よって` is a step stiffer and
belongs to formal documents, resolutions and mathematical proofs. ゆえに sits beside
`よって`, with an added flavour of pure reasoning: it is the word chosen for the
Japanese rendering of Descartes' cogito, and that association clings to it. These are
tendencies rather than boundaries, and a single essay may use several of them.

One consequence of that flavour: ゆえに wants a conclusion that **follows**, not one
that merely happened next. <code>ゆえに明日(あした)は晴(は)れるだろう</code> is
odd, because a guess about the weather is not deduced from anything. Where the link
is real-world cause and effect rather than inference, `そのため` is the honest word.

The `がゆえに` form is common in a slightly different place: writing about people and
society, where it introduces a quality that both explains and slightly excuses -
<code>真面目(まじめ)であるがゆえに苦(くる)しむ</code>. That use is still formal,
but it appears in newspaper columns and novels as well as in academic prose.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 疲(つか)れた。<b>ゆえに</b>、今日(きょう)はもう寝(ね)る。 → ✅ 疲(つか)れた。<b>だから</b>今日(きょう)はもう寝(ね)る。
  Nothing is being deduced and nobody is being addressed formally. ゆえに in a diary
  entry or a text message reads as a parody of scholarship.

- ❌ 経験(けいけん)が少(すく)ない<b>ゆえに</b>、失敗(しっぱい)も多(おお)い。 → ✅ 経験(けいけん)が少(すく)ない<b>がゆえに</b>、失敗(しっぱい)も多(おお)い。
  After a verb or an adjective the `が` is expected. Nouns are the exception:
  <code>経験(けいけん)不足(ぶそく)ゆえに</code> is fine as it stands.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)が多(おお)い。<b>そのゆえに</b>、信号(しんごう)が設置(せっち)された。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)が多(おお)い。<b>そのため</b>、信号(しんごう)が設置(せっち)された。
  ❌<code>そのゆえに</code> is not Japanese. The demonstrative form is
  <code>それゆえ</code> or <code>それゆえに</code>, and for a practical cause like this
  one `そのため` is the natural choice anyway.

- ❌ この漢字(かんじ)は「こに」と読(よ)む。 → ✅ この漢字(かんじ)は「ゆえに」と読(よ)む。
  The character <code>故</code> has two lives. In 故障(こしょう) and 事故(じこ)
  it is こ; standing alone before に it is **ゆえ**.

## Practice

**1.** Choose the natural connective: 台風(たいふう)が接近(せっきん)しています。（ゆえに・そのため）、明日(あした)の見学(けんがく)は中止(ちゅうし)といたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>そのため</b>、明日(あした)の見学(けんがく)は中止(ちゅうし)といたします。

This is an announcement about a practical arrangement, not a deduction, and the
audience is being addressed politely rather than argued with. ゆえに would make a
cancellation notice sound like the closing line of a proof.

</details>

**2.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a written argument: "All the witnesses gave the same account. It follows that the defendant's statement is untrue."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

証人(しょうにん)は全員(ぜんいん)が同(おな)じ証言(しょうげん)をしている。<b>ゆえに</b>、被告(ひこく)の供述(きょうじゅつ)は事実(じじつ)ではないと考(かんが)えられる。

"It follows that" is a deduction from stated premises, which is exactly what ゆえに
marks, and the legal setting supports the formality. <code>したがって</code> would
also be correct and slightly less stiff.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference:
(a) 彼(かれ)は優(やさ)しい<b>ので</b>、人(ひと)に頼(たよ)られる。
(b) 彼(かれ)は優(やさ)しい<b>がゆえに</b>、損(そん)をすることが多(おお)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain spoken statement of cause: he is kind, so people lean on him. It could
be said about a colleague over lunch and carries no particular attitude.

(b) is written, and the `がゆえに` frames his kindness as the very quality that works
against him. There is a note of "precisely because" in it, and often a hint of
sympathy or irony - the sentence is being composed rather than said.

</details>

# ～ばこそ

**Meaning** · precisely because ~, and for no other reason - a literary emphatic causal, used to insist that the real motive is a good one

`ばこそ` singles out one reason and shuts every other reason out. Its natural work is
**defending a hard action by naming the kind feeling behind it**: I am strict with
you *because* I care; I resigned *because* I love the company. The shape is old -
a 仮定形(かていけい) with the emphatic particle `こそ` welded on - and it survives today in
speeches, formal letters, editorials and the dialogue of fiction. In speech, say
`からこそ`.

## Formation

Take the conditional ば-form and add `こそ`. The sentence then usually closes with
のだ / のです / ものだ.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ば-form + こそ | 思(おも)う → 思(おも)えばこそ |
| Verb (る-verb) | ば-form + こそ | 信(しん)じる → 信(しん)じればこそ |
| Verb (ある) | あればこそ | 支(ささ)えがある → 支(ささ)えがあればこそ |
| い-adjective | ～ければ + こそ | 苦(くる)しい → 苦(くる)しければこそ |
| な-adjective | ～であれば + こそ | 真剣(しんけん) → 真剣(しんけん)であればこそ |
| Noun | ～であれば + こそ | 親(おや) → 親(おや)であればこそ |
| Typical ending | ～ばこそ…のだ / のです | - → 思(おも)えばこそ言(い)うのです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in older literature you may also meet `ばこそ` doing the opposite job:
attached to a 已然形(いぜんけい) it formed a classical emphatic **negation**, so that a line like
返事(へんじ)をすればこそ means "he would not answer at all". That use is dead in
modern Japanese and you will only meet it in old texts, but it is worth knowing the
possibility exists before you assume every `ばこそ` is causal.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あなたの将来(しょうらい)を思(おも)え<b>ばこそ</b>、厳(きび)しいことを言(い)うのです。
It is because I have your future at heart that I speak harshly to you.
</pre>

<pre>
命(いのち)は限(かぎ)りがあれ<b>ばこそ</b>尊(とうと)いのだ。
Life is precious precisely because it does not last.
</pre>

<pre>
苦(くる)しい時期(じき)を知(し)っていれ<b>ばこそ</b>、今(いま)の喜(よろこ)びは大(おお)きい。
Having known the hard years is exactly why the present joy is so great.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)であれ<b>ばこそ</b>、子(こ)の失敗(しっぱい)を黙(だま)って見(み)ていられないのだ。
It is because he is the boy's father that he cannot watch the failure in silence.
</pre>

<pre>
皆様(みなさま)のご支援(しえん)があれ<b>ばこそ</b>、本日(ほんじつ)この日(ひ)を迎(むか)えることができました。
It is thanks to your support, and to nothing else, that we are able to greet this day.
</pre>

<pre>
職人(しょくにん)の技(わざ)は、毎日(まいにち)同(おな)じ動(うご)きを繰(く)り返(かえ)せ<b>ばこそ</b>身(み)につくものだ。
A craftsman's skill is acquired only through repeating the same movements day after day.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の将来(しょうらい)を考(かんが)えれ<b>ばこそ</b>、あえて反対(はんたい)の立場(たちば)を取(と)らせていただきます。
It is out of concern for the company's future that I take, deliberately, the opposing position.
</pre>

<pre>
恩師(おんし)の言葉(ことば)が胸(むね)にあれ<b>ばこそ</b>、どんなに苦(くる)しい時(とき)も筆(ふで)を置(お)かずにこられたのだと思(おも)う。
It is because my old teacher's words stayed with me that I was able, however hard things became, to keep writing.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ばこそ` is **literary and emphatic, and it is not spoken casually**. Where you will
actually find it: a speech at a retirement or an anniversary, a letter of thanks, an
editorial, a company president's message in an annual report, an aphorism, and above
all the dialogue of a character in a novel or a drama who is defending themselves. A
learner who uses it in conversation will sound as if they are quoting from a script.
The modern equivalent - and the form you should produce - is `からこそ`.

Two things follow from that. First, the **content is almost always positive**: love,
gratitude, responsibility, respect, a belief in someone's ability. `ばこそ` is the
form you use to insist that an action which looked cold or unwelcome came from a
good place, so a sentence like ❌<code>嫌(きら)いであればこそ無視(むし)した</code>
misuses the register even though the grammar is intact.

Second, it wants a **closing のだ or ものだ**. The `こそ` has raised the volume, and
the explanatory ending resolves it. Ending flat -
<code>…思(おも)えばこそ言(い)った</code> - leaves the emphasis hanging.

`あればこそ` deserves separate mention: it is by a distance the most frequent form,
because gratitude is what the pattern is most often used for.
<code>皆様(みなさま)のおかげがあればこそ</code> is close to a fixed phrase in
speeches, and if you learn nothing else from this page, learn to hear that one.

## Contrast

### ～ばこそ vs ～からこそ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different century. `からこそ` is the living form
and works in speech and writing alike; `ばこそ` is its literary predecessor.

</div>

<pre>
大切(たいせつ)に思(おも)っている<b>からこそ</b>、厳(きび)しく言(い)うんだよ。
I'm hard on you exactly because you matter to me.
</pre>

<pre>
大切(たいせつ)に思(おも)え<b>ばこそ</b>、あえて厳(きび)しく申(もう)し上(あ)げる次第(しだい)です。
It is because you matter to me that I venture to speak so plainly.
</pre>

The thought is identical; the first could be said to a child at the kitchen table
and the second could not. Note the company each keeps: `からこそ` sits with `んだ`
and casual endings, `ばこそ` with あえて, 申(もう)し上(あ)げる and
次第(しだい)です. When you rewrite one as the other, the whole sentence has to
move register, not just the connective.

### ～ばこそ vs plain ～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - without `こそ` you have a **condition**: if. With `こそ` it is no
longer a condition at all but a **reason**: because, and only because.

</div>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)すれ<b>ば</b>、上手(じょうず)になります。
If you practise, you will get better.
</pre>

<pre>
地道(じみち)に練習(れんしゅう)すれ<b>ばこそ</b>、あの舞台(ぶたい)に立(た)てたのだ。
It was only through steady practice that she was able to stand on that stage.
</pre>

This is the single most common misreading. Seeing 思(おも)えば, a learner translates "if I
think", and the sentence dissolves. The `こそ` is the whole signal: once it is there,
the clause states an established fact and gives it as the reason.

### ～ばこそ vs ～てこそ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばこそ` looks **back** at a reason that already holds.
`てこそ` looks **forward**: only by doing this does the result become possible.

</div>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で経験(けいけん)し<b>てこそ</b>、人(ひと)の苦労(くろう)が分(わ)かる。
It is only when you have been through it yourself that you understand what others suffer.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)が経験(けいけん)していれ<b>ばこそ</b>、人(ひと)の苦労(くろう)が分(わ)かるのだ。
It is because he has been through it himself that he understands what others suffer.
</pre>

`てこそ` states a general condition for something to be achieved, and is at home in
maxims and advice. `ばこそ` explains a particular case that is already true. The
second sentence is about one man; the first is about everybody.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 信(しん)じる<b>ばこそ</b>、任(まか)せたのです。 → ✅ 信(しん)じれ<b>ばこそ</b>、任(まか)せたのです。
  The base is the ば-form, not the dictionary form. 信(しん)じる is a る-verb, so
  the conditional is 信(しん)じれば.

- ❌ 親(おや)<b>ばこそ</b>、心配(しんぱい)するのだ。 → ✅ 親(おや)であれ<b>ばこそ</b>、心配(しんぱい)するのだ。
  Nouns and な-adjectives need <code>であれば</code>. There is no way to attach
  `ばこそ` directly to a noun.

- ❌ 面倒(めんどう)であれ<b>ばこそ</b>、仕事(しごと)を断(ことわ)った。 → ✅ 面倒(めんどう)だった<b>ので</b>、仕事(しごと)を断(ことわ)った。
  A grubby motive cannot be dressed in this form. `ばこそ` presents the reason as one
  the speaker is proud to name.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)があれ<b>ばこそ</b>、手伝(てつだ)いに行(い)きます。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)があれ<b>ば</b>、手伝(てつだ)いに行(い)きます。
  Here the speaker really does mean "if". Adding `こそ` turns an unmet condition into
  a stated fact, which is not what the sentence is about.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to hear or read this sentence, and what does the `ばこそ` add?
社員(しゃいん)一人(ひとり)一人(ひとり)の努力(どりょく)があれ<b>ばこそ</b>、今日(きょう)の当社(とうしゃ)があるのです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a prepared speech - a president's new year address, an anniversary greeting, or
the opening page of a company report. Nobody produces this sentence spontaneously in
conversation.

The `ばこそ` insists that the staff's effort is the sole and sufficient reason for
the company's present position. It is a way of giving credit emphatically, which is
why it belongs to occasions where credit is being given in public.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
君(きみ)のことを思(おも)え<b>ばこそ</b>、厳(きび)しいことも言(い)うのだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

君(きみ)のことを思(おも)っている<b>からこそ</b>、厳(きび)しいことも言(い)うんだ。

`からこそ` keeps the "precisely because" force while dropping the literary tone, and
the plain <code>んだ</code> ending matches it. This is the version to say out loud.

</details>

**3.** Which reading is correct, and why?
急(いそ)げばこそ、間(ま)に合(あ)ったのだ。
(a) "If we had hurried, we would have made it." (b) "It was only because we hurried that we made it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b). The `こそ` removes the conditional reading altogether: the hurrying is presented
as something that actually happened, and the clause states the reason for the result.

Reading (a) is the trap this pattern sets, because 急(いそ)げば on its own is a plain
conditional. A past-tense main clause plus のだ is a further clue that the first
clause is fact, not hypothesis.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 健康(けんこう)ばこそ、何(なに)でもできる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 健康(けんこう)であれ<b>ばこそ</b>、何(なに)でもできるのだ。

A な-adjective cannot take `ばこそ` directly; it needs <code>であれば</code> in
between. Adding のだ at the end also gives the sentence the explanatory close the
pattern expects.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 失敗(しっぱい)を重(かさ)ね<b>てこそ</b>、人(ひと)は強(つよ)くなる。
(b) 失敗(しっぱい)を重(かさ)ねてきたからこそ、彼(かれ)は強(つよ)くなったのだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a general maxim. It states the condition under which anyone becomes stronger,
and is looking forward: only by accumulating failures does the result follow. It
would be at home on a poster or in a coach's pep talk.

(b) is about one person and one history. The failures are established fact, and the
sentence explains his present strength by them. The literary equivalent of (b) would
be <code>失敗(しっぱい)を重(かさ)ねていればこそ</code>; (a) has no `ばこそ`
version, because a maxim has no particular case to point back at.

</details>

# ～にかこつけて

**Meaning** · using ~ as a pretext; on the excuse of ~ - the stated reason is a fig leaf for what the person wanted to do anyway

`にかこつけて` names the excuse, not the motive. Someone had already decided to open
the good wine, take the afternoon off, or cut the department; the birthday, the
business trip or the bad quarter simply came along and gave them cover. The pattern
carries the speaker's judgement with it: **you can see through the excuse**. Turned
on yourself it is wry rather than harsh, which is how it is most often used.

## Formation

The verb is かこつける, a る-verb, almost always met in this て-form. It is written in
kana in practice; the kanji spelling 託(かこ)つける exists but is rare.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | noun + にかこつけて | 出張(しゅっちょう) → 出張(しゅっちょう)にかこつけて |
| Verb | plain form + の / こと + にかこつけて | 引(ひ)っ越(こ)す → 引(ひ)っ越(こ)すのにかこつけて |
| Written (clause-linking) | ～にかこつけ | 不況(ふきょう) → 不況(ふきょう)にかこつけ、… |
| Modifying a noun | ～にかこつけた + noun | 視察(しさつ) → 視察(しさつ)にかこつけた旅行(りょこう) |
| Progressive habit | ～にかこつけては | 残業(ざんぎょう) → 残業(ざんぎょう)にかこつけては |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the excuse goes before `にかこつけて`; the thing actually wanted
goes after it. Get them the wrong way round and the sentence says the opposite of
what you mean.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
誕生日(たんじょうび)<b>にかこつけて</b>、いいワインを開(あ)けた。
I used my birthday as an excuse to open a good bottle of wine.
</pre>

<pre>
出張(しゅっちょう)<b>にかこつけて</b>、実家(じっか)に寄(よ)ってきた。
I made the business trip an excuse to drop in on my parents.
</pre>

<pre>
研修(けんしゅう)<b>にかこつけて</b>温泉(おんせん)に一泊(いっぱく)してきたらしい。
Apparently he used the training course as cover for a night at a hot spring.
</pre>

<pre>
歓迎会(かんげいかい)<b>にかこつけて</b>、課長(かちょう)はまた飲(の)みに行(い)きたいだけだろう。
The welcome party is a pretext; the section chief just wants another night out.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの受験(じゅけん)<b>にかこつけて</b>、夫(おっと)は書斎(しょさい)にこもる時間(じかん)を増(ふ)やしている。
On the pretext of the children's exams, my husband has been spending more and more time shut in his study.
</pre>

<pre>
取材(しゅざい)<b>にかこつけて</b>好(す)きな作家(さっか)に会(あ)いに行(い)った、というのが本当(ほんとう)のところだ。
The truth is that he used the interview as an excuse to go and meet his favourite novelist.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)は業績(ぎょうせき)不振(ふしん)<b>にかこつけて</b>、以前(いぜん)から畳(たた)みたかった部門(ぶもん)を閉(と)じた。
The company used the poor results as a pretext to close a division it had wanted rid of for years.
</pre>

<pre>
「片付(かたづ)けを手伝(てつだ)う」と言(い)っていたが、引(ひ)っ越(こ)し<b>にかこつけて</b>昔(むかし)の写真(しゃしん)を見(み)たかっただけらしい。
He said he was coming to help with the packing, but the move was really just an excuse to look at old photographs.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にかこつけて` is **neutral in politeness but never neutral in attitude**. It works
in conversation among adults, in essays and columns, and in news writing about
governments and companies; it is not stiff, and it is not slang. What it always
supplies is the speaker's verdict that the stated reason is not the real one.

The strength of the criticism depends entirely on who the subject is. About
**yourself** it is light and self-deprecating - admitting cheerfully that you wanted
the wine anyway. About a **friend or family member** it is teasing. About a
**company, a government or an opponent** it is a genuine accusation, and in that use
it appears in editorials and investigative reporting with real force.

Two boundaries are worth marking. The excuse has to be **plausible on its face**:
`にかこつけて` describes a cover story that would pass, not a transparent lie. And
the thing done afterwards is normally **mildly indulgent or self-serving**, not
criminal. Serious wrongdoing takes stronger words -
<code>～を口実(こうじつ)に</code>, <code>～を装(よそお)って</code>,
<code>～と偽(いつわ)って</code>.

The bare verb かこつける does exist outside this pattern, but you will rarely need
it. Learn the て-form as a unit, together with the written variant `にかこつけ`,
which is how newspapers write it.

## Contrast

### ～にかこつけて vs ～を口実(こうじつ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same manoeuvre at two temperatures. `にかこつけて` is
colloquial and often affectionate; <code>を口実(こうじつ)に</code> is colder and
suits a deliberate, calculated excuse.

</div>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)診断(しんだん)<b>にかこつけて</b>、午後(ごご)は休(やす)ませてもらった。
I used the medical check-up as an excuse to take the afternoon off.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)対策(たいさく)<b>を口実(こうじつ)に</b>、反対(はんたい)派(は)の集会(しゅうかい)が禁止(きんし)された。
Under the pretext of safety measures, the opposition's rallies were banned.
</pre>

口実(こうじつ) is a noun meaning "pretext", so it can also be used on its own -
<code>口実(こうじつ)を作(つく)る</code>, <code>ただの口実(こうじつ)だ</code> -
whereas `にかこつけて` only ever works as an adverbial phrase attached to a verb. For
the small indulgences of daily life, `にかこつけて` is the more idiomatic choice.

### ～にかこつけて vs ～を理由(りゆう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を理由(りゆう)に</code> is **neutral**: it states the
grounds given, with no comment on whether they were sincere. `にかこつけて` says they
were not.

</div>

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)<b>を理由(りゆう)に</b>、試合(しあい)は延期(えんき)された。
The match was postponed on account of the heavy snow.
</pre>

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)<b>にかこつけて</b>、練習(れんしゅう)を一日(いちにち)サボった。
I used the heavy snow as an excuse to skip a day's training.
</pre>

Notice that the first sentence takes no position at all - the snow may well have been
the whole story. Swapping in `にかこつけて` accuses somebody. This is why
<code>を理由(りゆう)に</code> is the safe form in a report and `にかこつけて`
is not.

### ～にかこつけて vs ～にかまけて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they sound almost alike and mean quite different things.
`にかこつけて` = X is my **excuse** for doing what I wanted. `にかまけて` = X has
**swallowed** me, and I have neglected everything else.

</div>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)<b>にかこつけて</b>、親戚(しんせき)の集(あつ)まりを欠席(けっせき)した。
I used work as an excuse to get out of the family gathering.
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)<b>にかまけて</b>、親戚(しんせき)づき合(あ)いをおろそかにしてきた。
I let work take me over, and neglected my relatives for years.
</pre>

In the first the speaker did not want to go and used work as cover; the work may not
even have been urgent. In the second the work was real and consuming, and the fault
is neglect rather than dishonesty. One sentence admits a small deception, the other
admits a long-running failure.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ワインを開(あ)けること<b>にかこつけて</b>、誕生日(たんじょうび)を祝(いわ)った。 → ✅ 誕生日(たんじょうび)<b>にかこつけて</b>、ワインを開(あ)けた。
  The order is fixed: excuse first, real object of desire second. Reversed, the
  sentence claims you invented a birthday to justify a bottle.

- ❌ 病気(びょうき)<b>にかこつけて</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)まなければならなかった。 → ✅ 病気(びょうき)の<b>ため</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)まなければならなかった。
  A genuine reason does not take this pattern. `にかこつけて` always implies the
  reason was a cover.

- ❌ 出張(しゅっちょう)する<b>にかこつけて</b>、観光(かんこう)もしてきた。 → ✅ 出張(しゅっちょう)する<b>のにかこつけて</b>、観光(かんこう)もしてきた。
  A verb clause needs `の` or `こと` to turn it into a noun before `に`. Nouns attach
  directly.

- ❌ 震災(しんさい)<b>にかこつけて</b>、被害者(ひがいしゃ)から金(かね)をだまし取(と)った。 → ✅ 震災(しんさい)を<b>口実(こうじつ)に</b>、被害者(ひがいしゃ)から金(かね)をだまし取(と)った。
  The pattern is too light for outright fraud. Serious deception wants
  口実(こうじつ), 装(よそお)う or 偽(いつわ)る.

# ～にかまけて

**Meaning** · so taken up with ~ that everything else was let slide - names the thing that swallowed your attention, and blames it

`にかまけて` is an admission of neglect. One thing - work, a hobby, the daily
firefighting - absorbed the person completely, and the sentence goes on to say what
suffered for it: the family, the study, the promise, the friend who was never called
back. There is always a casualty, and the tone is reproachful. Said about oneself,
it is regret; said about someone else, it is criticism.

## Formation

The verb is かまける, a る-verb. In practice it appears only in this て-form or as
かまけている, and it is written in kana.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | noun + にかまけて | 仕事(しごと) → 仕事(しごと)にかまけて |
| Verb | plain form + の + にかまけて | 稼(かせ)ぐ → 稼(かせ)ぐのにかまけて |
| Ongoing state | ～にかまけている | 遊(あそ)び → 遊(あそ)びにかまけている |
| While it lasts | ～にかまけているうちに | 準備(じゅんび) → 準備(じゅんび)にかまけているうちに |
| Written (clause-linking) | ～にかまけ | 日々(ひび)の業務(ぎょうむ) → 日々(ひび)の業務(ぎょうむ)にかまけ、… |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the sentence is not finished until you say **what was
neglected**. A `にかまけて` clause with nothing lost after it leaves the listener
waiting; that missing half is the point of the pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
仕事(しごと)<b>にかまけて</b>、家族(かぞく)のことを後回(あとまわ)しにしてきた。
I let work take me over and put my family second.
</pre>

<pre>
遊(あそ)び<b>にかまけて</b>勉強(べんきょう)を怠(おこた)った結果(けっか)がこれだ。
This is what comes of letting yourself be swallowed by play and neglecting your studies.
</pre>

<pre>
部活(ぶかつ)<b>にかまけて</b>、宿題(しゅくだい)を一(ひと)つも出(だ)していないらしい。
Apparently the club has taken him over and he hasn't handed in a single piece of homework.
</pre>

<pre>
目先(めさき)の利益(りえき)<b>にかまけていると</b>、いつか信用(しんよう)を失(うしな)うことになる。
Let yourself be consumed by short-term profit and you will lose people's trust sooner or later.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいゲーム<b>にかまけて</b>、試験(しけん)が来週(らいしゅう)だということをすっかり忘(わす)れていた。
The new game had taken me over so completely that I forgot the exam was next week.
</pre>

<pre>
日々(ひび)の業務(ぎょうむ)<b>にかまけて</b>、部下(ぶか)の変化(へんか)に気(き)づいてやれなかった。
Caught up in the day-to-day work, I failed to notice the change in my staff.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)を追(お)うこと<b>にかまけている</b>うちに、会社(かいしゃ)は長年(ながねん)の取引先(とりひきさき)を一社(いっしゃ)失(うしな)っていた。
While it was busy chasing numbers, the company lost a client it had had for decades.
</pre>

<pre>
若(わか)い頃(ころ)は自分(じぶん)の研究(けんきゅう)<b>にかまけて</b>親(おや)と話(はな)す時間(じかん)を作(つく)らなかったことを、今(いま)になって悔(く)やんでいる。
Now, late in the day, I regret how my research consumed me and left no time to talk with my parents.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にかまけて` is **plain, adult and slightly literary**, at home in spoken
self-reflection, in essays, and in the reflective passages of memoirs and speeches.
Nobody would call it stiff, but it is not a word children use, and it is rare in
casual chat among young speakers, who would say
<code>仕事(しごと)ばかりしていて</code>.

The pattern is **always evaluative and always negative**. Even when the absorbing
activity is respectable - work, research, caring for a business - `にかまけて` frames
it as an indulgence that was allowed to crowd out something more important. This is
why it is so common in apologies and in the "what I got wrong" half of a memoir.

A note on responsibility: `にかまけて` puts the fault on the **person**, not on the
circumstances. That is precisely what distinguishes it from
<code>～に追(お)われて</code>, which says the work chased you and you had no
choice. Using `にかまけて` about someone else is therefore a real reproach - it says
they let it happen.

The verb かまける is almost fossilised in this construction. You will occasionally
see <code>忙(いそが)しさにかまけて</code> and, more rarely, かまけていた as a full
predicate, but there is no useful dictionary-form use to learn. Take the て-form as
the unit.

## Contrast

### ～にかまけて vs ～に夢中(むちゅう)になって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>夢中(むちゅう)</code> describes **absorption**, and can
be entirely positive. `にかまけて` adds the bill: something else was neglected, and
the speaker is passing judgement.

</div>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は昆虫(こんちゅう)の採集(さいしゅう)<b>に夢中(むちゅう)になって</b>、夏休(なつやす)みを過(す)ごした。
My son spent the summer holiday completely absorbed in collecting insects.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は昆虫(こんちゅう)採集(さいしゅう)<b>にかまけて</b>、夏休(なつやす)みの課題(かだい)に手(て)をつけなかった。
My son was so wrapped up in collecting insects that he never touched his holiday assignment.
</pre>

The first sentence could be told fondly to a neighbour; the second is what the parent
says on the last day of August. Note also that
<code>夢中(むちゅう)になる</code> is a complete predicate in its own right -
<code>ゲームに夢中(むちゅう)だ</code> - while `にかまけて` cannot end a sentence.

### ～にかまけて vs ～に追(お)われて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に追(お)われて</code> = the work **chased** you; you
had no choice. `にかまけて` = you **let** it absorb you; the neglect is your fault.

</div>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)り<b>に追(お)われて</b>、食事(しょくじ)をとる時間(じかん)もなかった。
Chased by deadlines, I had no time even to eat.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)り<b>にかまけて</b>、子(こ)どもの運動会(うんどうかい)にも顔(かお)を出(だ)さなかった。
I let the deadlines take me over and did not even show my face at my child's sports day.
</pre>

The difference is where the blame lands. <code>追(お)われて</code> invites
sympathy and often precedes an excuse; `にかまけて` accepts, or assigns, the fault. A
manager writing an apology chooses `にかまけ`; a manager explaining why a report is
late chooses <code>追(お)われて</code>.

### ～にかまけて vs ～にかこつけて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one letter apart, and opposite in honesty. `にかまけて` = X
really did absorb me, and other things suffered. `にかこつけて` = X was never the
real reason; it was my cover story.

</div>

<pre>
介護(かいご)<b>にかまけて</b>、自分(じぶん)の健康(けんこう)をすっかり後回(あとまわ)しにしていた。
Taken up with caring for my mother, I neglected my own health entirely.
</pre>

<pre>
介護(かいご)<b>にかこつけて</b>、苦手(にがて)な集(あつ)まりを全部(ぜんぶ)断(ことわ)っている。
He uses caring for his mother as an excuse to turn down every gathering he dislikes.
</pre>

The first speaker really was busy; the failing is one of balance. The second is being
accused of dishonesty. If you cannot remember which is which, note the meanings of
the underlying verbs: かまける is to be taken up with something, かこつける is to
attribute your action to something. The excuse is in かこつける.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 仕事(しごと)<b>にかまけている</b>。 → ✅ 仕事(しごと)<b>にかまけて</b>、家庭(かてい)を顧(かえり)みなかった。
  The pattern needs a second clause naming what was neglected. On its own it is not a
  statement, only half of one.

- ❌ 勉強(べんきょう)<b>にかまけて</b>、成績(せいせき)が上(あ)がった。 → ✅ 勉強(べんきょう)に集中(しゅうちゅう)して、成績(せいせき)が上(あ)がった。
  A good outcome cannot follow `にかまけて`. The pattern exists to report a loss.

- ❌ 遊(あそ)ぶ<b>にかまけて</b>、課題(かだい)を忘(わす)れていた。 → ✅ 遊(あそ)び<b>にかまけて</b>、課題(かだい)を忘(わす)れていた。
  A verb must be nominalised first - either use the noun form 遊(あそ)び, or add
  `の`: <code>遊(あそ)ぶのにかまけて</code>.

- ❌ 残業(ざんぎょう)<b>にかまけて</b>、飲(の)み会(かい)を断(ことわ)った。 → ✅ 残業(ざんぎょう)<b>にかこつけて</b>、飲(の)み会(かい)を断(ことわ)った。
  If the overtime was your excuse rather than your undoing, the word you want is
  かこつける.

(b) 新(あたら)しい事業(じぎょう)の準備(じゅんび)<b>にかまけて</b>、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the speaker as swept along by circumstances. It reads as an explanation,
and the listener is expected to understand: there was simply no time.

(b) accepts the blame. The speaker is saying that they allowed the new project to
absorb them and that the delay was their own doing. In an apology (b) sounds more
sincere for exactly that reason, while (a) can come across as an excuse.
