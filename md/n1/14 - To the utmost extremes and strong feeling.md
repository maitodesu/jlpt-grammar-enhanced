# N1 - To the utmost: extremes and strong feeling


---

# ～極まる / ～極まりない

**Meaning** · extremely; utterly; beyond any limit - a verdict that some quality has gone as far as it can go, almost always a bad one

Both halves of this pair mean the same thing, which is the first surprise:
<code>危険(きけん)極(きわ)まる</code> and
<code>危険(きけん)極(きわ)まりない</code> are both "dangerous in the extreme".
<code>極(きわ)まる</code> says the quality *reaches* its limit;
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> says there *is no* limit to it. Japanese arrives at
the same place from opposite directions. What matters more than the logic is the
tone: this is the language of a written complaint, an editorial or a formal
protest, and it is a **judgement passed on something**, not a report of how the
speaker feels.

## Formation

The base is a な-adjective **stem** - no な, no い-adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| な-adjective (stem) | stem + 極(きわ)まりない | 失礼(しつれい)な → 失礼(しつれい)極(きわ)まりない |
| な-adjective (stem) | stem + 極(きわ)まる | 危険(きけん)な → 危険(きけん)極(きわ)まる |
| Noun of quality | noun + 極(きわ)まりない | 迷惑(めいわく) → 迷惑(めいわく)極(きわ)まりない |
| Before a noun | ～極(きわ)まる／極(きわ)まりない + noun | 危険(きけん)極(きわ)まりない行為(こうい) |
| Past | ～極(きわ)まりなかった | 退屈(たいくつ)極(きわ)まりなかった |
| Polite | ～極(きわ)まりないです／～極(きわ)まりありません | 失礼(しつれい)極(きわ)まりありません |
| Lexical verb | 感(かん)極(きわ)まる = be overcome with emotion | 感(かん)極(きわ)まって泣(な)き出(だ)す |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> is treated as an い-adjective, so it
ends a written sentence on its own with no だ: ❌<code>危険(きけん)極(きわ)まりないだ</code>.
The polite forms exist but are uncommon, because the pattern lives in writing,
where a plain ending is normal. Of the two,
<code>～極(きわ)まりありません</code> is the stiffer and fits a formal spoken
protest.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの運転(うんてん)は危険(きけん)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
That driving is dangerous in the extreme.
</pre>

<pre>
店員(てんいん)の態度(たいど)は失礼(しつれい)<b>極(きわ)まる</b>ものだった。
The clerk's attitude was utterly rude.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)の日(ひ)に予定(よてい)を変(か)えられるのは迷惑(めいわく)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
Having the schedule changed on us the day before is a thorough nuisance.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ説明(せつめい)が三(さん)時間(じかん)続(つづ)き、退屈(たいくつ)<b>極(きわ)まりなかった</b>。
The same explanation went on for three hours; it was crushingly dull.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)帯(たい)もつけずに屋根(やね)に登(のぼ)るとは、危険(きけん)<b>極(きわ)まる</b>行為(こうい)だ。
Climbing onto the roof without even a harness is an act of sheer recklessness.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)が決(き)まった瞬間(しゅんかん)、キャプテンは<b>感(かん)極(きわ)まって</b>声(こえ)を詰(つ)まらせた。
The moment the win was confirmed, the captain was overcome and his voice caught.
</pre>

<pre>
点検(てんけん)の記録(きろく)を残(のこ)さずに運行(うんこう)を続(つづ)けていたのなら、ずさん<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>管理(かんり)だ。
If they kept the service running without recording any inspections, the management was slapdash beyond words.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の席(せき)で部下(ぶか)を名指(なざ)しで笑(わら)い者(もの)にするなど、無礼(ぶれい)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>振(ふ)る舞(ま)いである。
To single out a subordinate for ridicule in a meeting is conduct of the utmost discourtesy.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **formal written Japanese**: newspaper columns, letters of complaint,
company statements, courtroom argument, the narrator's voice in a novel. In
conversation it lands as theatrical, and speakers who use it in speech are usually
doing it for effect - imitating an editorial to be funny, or being deliberately
severe.

The important restriction is not grammatical but lexical: **the adjective is nearly
always a critical one.** The stems that genuinely occur are a short list -
危険(きけん), 失礼(しつれい), 無礼(ぶれい), 非常識(ひじょうしき),
不愉快(ふゆかい), 不謹慎(ふきんしん), 迷惑(めいわく), 退屈(たいくつ),
卑劣(ひれつ), 滑稽(こっけい), 身勝手(みがって), 無責任(むせきにん),
面倒(めんどう), ずさん. 贅沢(ぜいたく)極(きわ)まりない is the one
common near-positive case, and even there the speaker is half-scolding the
extravagance. Attaching it to a happy word does not work: nobody says
❌幸(しあわ)せ極(きわ)まりない.

Watch the direction of the sentence. <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> **judges a
thing** - an act, a piece of work, someone's manner. It does not report what is
happening inside the speaker. That job belongs to
<code>～限(かぎ)りだ</code>, <code>～極(きわ)み</code> and
<code>～を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</code>, and the difference is invisible in an
English translation with "extremely" in it.

<code>感(かん)極(きわ)まる</code> is a separate word that happens to contain
the same verb, and it is worth learning on its own. It is a plain intransitive
verb meaning "be overwhelmed by emotion", it is not critical at all, and it is
mostly used in the て-form: 感(かん)極(きわ)まって泣(な)く,
感(かん)極(きわ)まって言葉(ことば)が出(で)ない. It appears in ordinary
news reporting about weddings, retirements and medal ceremonies.

## Contrast

### ～極(きわ)まる vs ～極(きわ)まりない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning. <code>～極(きわ)まる</code> is the older
and slightly more clipped of the two, and is most at home directly before a noun;
<code>～極(きわ)まりない</code> is the commoner choice and the safer default.

</div>

<pre>
不謹慎(ふきんしん)<b>極(きわ)まる</b>発言(はつげん)に抗議(こうぎ)が集(あつ)まった。
The grossly insensitive remark drew a wave of protest.
</pre>

<pre>
その言(い)い訳(わけ)は身勝手(みがって)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
That excuse is selfish in the extreme.
</pre>

Do not read the negative form as a negative meaning. ❌"is not extreme" is never
what <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> says; the 極(きわ)まり that is missing is
the *limit*, not the quality.

### ～極(きわ)まりない vs ～この上(うえ)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>この上(うえ)ない</code> is the neutral scale-topper and
works for good things. <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> carries a scowl.

</div>

<pre>
皆様(みなさま)にお越(こ)しいただき、この<b>上(うえ)ない</b>喜(よろこ)びです。
Having you all here is a joy beyond measure.
</pre>

<pre>
手続(てつづ)きが三(さん)度(ど)もやり直(なお)しになり、面倒(めんどう)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
The paperwork was sent back three times - it is an utter pain.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence becomes a complaint about your own guests. With
い-adjectives, <code>この上(うえ)ない</code> also has the escape hatch
<code>～ことこの上(うえ)ない</code>
（嬉(うれ)しいことこの上(うえ)ない）, which
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> does not.

### ～極(きわ)まりない vs ～の極(きわ)み

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - grammar first: <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> follows an
**adjective stem** and ends the sentence like an adjective;
<code>の極(きわ)み</code> follows a **noun** and *is* a noun. Then register:
<code>の極(きわ)み</code> is ceremonial and often describes the speaker's own
state.

</div>

<pre>
警備(けいび)の甘(あま)さは無責任(むせきにん)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
The laxness of the security is the height of irresponsibility.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)の仲間(なかま)を失(うしな)い、痛恨(つうこん)<b>の極(きわ)み</b>です。
Losing a colleague of many years is a matter of the deepest regret.
</pre>

The second sentence is a speaker reporting their own grief in public, and
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> could not replace it. The first is a verdict on
somebody else's work, and <code>の極(きわ)み</code> would sound like a eulogy for
the security guards.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 危険(きけん)な<b>極(きわ)まりない</b> → ✅ 危険(きけん)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>
  The base is the bare stem. The な that a な-adjective normally needs before a
  noun is not used here.

- ❌ 嬉(うれ)しい<b>極(きわ)まりない</b> → ✅ 嬉(うれ)しい限(かぎ)りだ／嬉(うれ)しいことこの上(うえ)ない
  い-adjectives do not take this pattern at all, and a positive feeling wants a
  different construction anyway.

- ❌ この状況(じょうきょう)は危険(きけん)極(きわ)まりない<b>だ</b>。 → ✅ この状況(じょうきょう)は危険(きけん)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
  <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> is already the predicate; adding だ is like
  writing ❌高(たか)いだ.

- ❌ 合格(ごうかく)して<b>感(かん)極(きわ)まりない</b>。 → ✅ 合格(ごうかく)して<b>感(かん)極(きわ)まった</b>。
  <code>感(かん)極(きわ)まる</code> is a fixed verb and has no
  <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> counterpart.

- ❌ ホテルの部屋(へや)は快適(かいてき)<b>極(きわ)まりなかった</b>。 → ✅ ホテルの部屋(へや)は快適(かいてき)この上(うえ)なかった。
  Grammatically well formed, and still wrong: the pattern is a criticism, so
  praising a hotel room with it reads as sarcasm.

## Practice

**1.** 説明(せつめい)も謝罪(しゃざい)もないまま工事(こうじ)を始(はじ)めるとは、非常識(ひじょうしき)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

非常識(ひじょうしき)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>（or 非常識(ひじょうしき)極(きわ)まる行為(こうい)だ）

非常識(ひじょうしき) is a な-adjective, so the stem attaches directly with no な,
and the sentence is a verdict on someone else's behaviour - the pattern's home
ground.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 孫(まご)の成長(せいちょう)を見(み)られて、嬉(うれ)しい（限(かぎ)りです・極(きわ)まりないです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

嬉(うれ)しい<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。

Two reasons. <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> cannot follow an い-adjective, and
even if the adjective were swapped for a な-adjective the pattern would be wrong
here: this is the speaker's own happy feeling, which is
<code>～限(かぎ)りだ</code> territory.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Cancelling on the morning of the event with no word of apology is thoroughly discourteous."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

謝罪(しゃざい)の言葉(ことば)もなく当日(とうじつ)の朝(あさ)にキャンセルするのは、失礼(しつれい)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。

"Thoroughly discourteous" is exactly what the pattern is for: a critical
な-adjective stem plus <code>極(きわ)まりない</code>, with no だ at the end.
無礼(ぶれい)極(きわ)まりない is also fine and is harsher again.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: その対応(たいおう)は不誠実(ふせいじつ)な極(きわ)まりないだと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ その対応(たいおう)は不誠実(ふせいじつ)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>と思(おも)います。
"I think that response was utterly insincere."

Two repairs: drop the な before the pattern, and drop the だ after it, since
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> already behaves as an い-adjective. Note that the
と思(おも)います frame is what softens an otherwise very sharp line.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の返事(へんじ)は失礼(しつれい)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)の返事(へんじ)は失礼(しつれい)この上(うえ)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say the reply was as rude as a reply can be, and either could appear in a
written complaint. (a) is the angrier and the more formal: it reads as a
judgement being placed on record, and it is the version you would expect in a
newspaper column or a letter from a lawyer.

(b) tops out the same scale with less heat. <code>この上(うえ)ない</code> is a
neutral superlative that also works with pleasant words
（この上(うえ)ない光栄(こうえい)）, so in (b) the severity comes from
失礼(しつれい) alone rather than from the construction as well.

</details>

# ～の極み

**Meaning** · the height of; the very peak of - a state that has gone as far as it is possible to go, said in formal, ceremonial Japanese

極(きわ)み is a noun meaning "the furthest point", so
<code>～の極(きわ)み</code> is literally "the summit of ～". In practice it is
not a free construction at all: it attaches to a small set of abstract nouns and
almost always appears in prepared, public language - a statement to the press, a
speech of thanks, a message of condolence, or a magazine paragraph about an
extraordinarily good meal.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (feeling or state) | noun + の極(きわ)み | 遺憾(いかん) → 遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)み |
| Ending a sentence | ～の極(きわ)みだ／です／である | 痛恨(つうこん)の極(きわ)みです |
| Reaching that point | ～の極(きわ)みに達(たっ)する | 疲労(ひろう)の極(きわ)みに達(たっ)する |
| Passing a verdict | ～の極(きわ)みと言(い)える | 贅沢(ぜいたく)の極(きわ)みと言(い)える |
| Never after an adjective | ❌嬉(うれ)しいの極(きわ)み | ✅喜(よろこ)びの極(きわ)み |

<div class="note">

**Note - the collocations that are actually live.** The noun slot is not open.
What you will really meet is 遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)み (regret, in an official
statement), 痛恨(つうこん)の極(きわ)み (bitter regret over a loss),
感激(かんげき)の極(きわ)み, 光栄(こうえい)の極(きわ)み,
贅沢(ぜいたく)の極(きわ)み, 疲労(ひろう)の極(きわ)み,
混乱(こんらん)の極(きわ)み, 悲(かな)しみの極(きわ)み and
無礼(ぶれい)の極(きわ)み. Outside that neighbourhood the phrase stops sounding
like Japanese: ❌仕事(しごと)の極(きわ)み, ❌天気(てんき)の極(きわ)み.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
このような事態(じたい)を招(まね)き、<b>遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)み</b>です。
That matters should have come to this is a source of the deepest regret.
</pre>

<pre>
この賞(しょう)をいただけるとは、<b>光栄(こうえい)の極(きわ)み</b>です。
To receive this award is the greatest of honours.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)を重(かさ)ねてきただけに、この敗戦(はいせん)は<b>痛恨(つうこん)の極(きわ)み</b>だ。
After all that preparation, this defeat is a bitter blow indeed.
</pre>

<pre>
恩師(おんし)から直筆(じきひつ)の手紙(てがみ)をいただき、<b>感激(かんげき)の極(きわ)み</b>でございます。
To have received a handwritten letter from my old teacher moves me beyond words.
</pre>

<pre>
炭火(すみび)で焼(や)きたての一切(ひとき)れを雪景色(ゆきげしき)を眺(なが)めながら味(あじ)わう。まさに<b>贅沢(ぜいたく)の極(きわ)み</b>である。
A slice straight off the charcoal, savoured while you watch the snow: luxury of the highest order.
</pre>

<pre>
三日三晩(みっかみばん)の捜索(そうさく)を終(お)え、隊員(たいいん)たちは<b>疲労(ひろう)の極(きわ)み</b>に達(たっ)していた。
Three days and nights of searching left the team members utterly spent.
</pre>

<pre>
情報(じょうほう)が二転三転(にてんさんてん)し、現場(げんば)は<b>混乱(こんらん)の極(きわ)み</b>にあった。
The information kept changing, and the site was in a state of total confusion.
</pre>

<pre>
突然(とつぜん)の訃報(ふほう)に接(せっ)し、<b>悲(かな)しみの極(きわ)み</b>であります。心(こころ)よりお悔(く)やみ申(もう)し上(あ)げます。
Learning of this sudden loss, I am overcome with grief. My deepest condolences.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not deploy it.** Its natural habitats are a company
apology read from a sheet of paper, a politician's comment to reporters, an
acceptance speech, a funeral address, and the copy in a travel or food magazine.
If you say <code>疲労(ひろう)の極(きわ)みです</code> to a friend who asks
whether you are tired, you have quoted a press release at them. The ordinary
modern way to say the same thing is
<code>本当(ほんとう)に残念(ざんねん)です</code>,
<code>この上(うえ)なく嬉(うれ)しいです</code>,
<code>最高(さいこう)の贅沢(ぜいたく)です</code> or simply
<code>とても疲(つか)れました</code> - and that is what you should produce.

Two of these collocations carry more freight than the dictionary suggests.
<code>遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)み</code> is the formula an organisation uses when
it wants to sound gravely displeased without conceding anything; Japanese readers
hear it as institutional, sometimes evasive, and it is often aimed at *someone
else's* conduct rather than at the speaker's own fault.
<code>痛恨(つうこん)の極(きわ)み</code>, by contrast, is genuinely personal
regret - a mistake you will not forgive yourself for - and it is the standard line
from a coach after a loss or an official who missed something.

Grammatically the phrase is a **noun**, which is why it takes だ, です, である or
でございます at the end, and why it can be followed by に達(たっ)する or
にある. The very formal ending
<code>悲(かな)しみの極(きわ)みであります</code> belongs to spoken addresses;
であります is a podium form and would be strange anywhere else.

## Contrast

### ～の極(きわ)み vs ～極(きわ)まりない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>の極(きわ)み</code> follows a **noun** and is itself a
noun; <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> follows a な-adjective **stem** and ends the
sentence like an adjective. In meaning, <code>の極(きわ)み</code> usually
describes a state the speaker is in, while
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> passes judgement on something outside.

</div>

<pre>
司会者(しかいしゃ)の言(い)い方(かた)は無礼(ぶれい)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
The way the host put it was insufferably rude.
</pre>

<pre>
招(まね)いた客(きゃく)を立(た)たせたままにするとは<b>無礼(ぶれい)の極(きわ)み</b>だ。
To leave an invited guest standing is the height of discourtesy.
</pre>

無礼(ぶれい) is one of the few words that takes both, so the pair is a clean
minimal one. The first sentence is a live reaction; the second sounds like a line
from an essay or a period drama.

### ～の極(きわ)み vs ～の至(いた)り

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are ceremonial, and they are not interchangeable.
<code>の至(いた)り</code> reports a **feeling welling up in the speaker** and is
tied to a handful of humble set phrases; <code>の極(きわ)み</code> can describe
a **situation** - fatigue, confusion, extravagance - with no speaker inside it.

</div>

<pre>
皆様(みなさま)の前(まえ)でお話(はなし)できますことは、<b>光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り</b>です。
To be able to speak before you all is an honour indeed.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は混乱(こんらん)<b>の極(きわ)み</b>で、案内(あんない)の声(こえ)も届(とど)かなかった。
The venue was in utter chaos, and the announcements could not be heard.
</pre>

❌<code>混乱(こんらん)の至(いた)り</code> is not Japanese: nobody *feels*
confusion as a swelling emotion in the way they feel honour or embarrassment.
光栄(こうえい) is the one noun that takes both, and
光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り is the more old-fashioned and more deferential of
the two.

### ～の極(きわ)み vs the modern equivalent

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in anything you actually write or say, use
<code>この上(うえ)なく</code>, <code>非常(ひじょう)に</code>,
<code>本当(ほんとう)に</code> or <code>最高(さいこう)の</code>. Keep
<code>の極(きわ)み</code> for reading.

</div>

<pre>
温泉(おんせん)に入(はい)ってから冷(つめ)たいビールを飲(の)む。<b>贅沢(ぜいたく)の極(きわ)み</b>だ。
A hot spring and then a cold beer: the last word in indulgence.
</pre>

<pre>
温泉(おんせん)のあとのビールって、<b>最高(さいこう)の贅沢(ぜいたく)</b>だよね。
A beer after the hot spring is just the best kind of luxury, isn't it.
</pre>

The content is identical; the first is written for a page and the second is said
to a person. Note that the casual version also drops the noun-heavy shape - real
speech prefers 最高(さいこう), すごく and めちゃくちゃ to any of this.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 嬉(うれ)しい<b>の極(きわ)み</b> → ✅ <b>喜(よろこ)びの極(きわ)み</b>／嬉(うれ)しい限(かぎ)りだ
  The slot takes a noun. An い-adjective has to be nominalised first, and in
  everyday Japanese <code>～限(かぎ)りだ</code> is the better fit anyway.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)の試験(しけん)は<b>疲労(ひろう)の極(きわ)み</b>でした。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)の試験(しけん)は本当(ほんとう)に疲(つか)れました。
  Grammatical, but it puts podium language into a chat about a test. This is the
  characteristic learner error with the pattern.

- ❌ この<b>極(きわ)み</b>の料理(りょうり) → ✅ <b>贅沢(ぜいたく)の極(きわ)み</b>のような料理(りょうり)
  極(きわ)み is not an adjective and cannot modify a noun on its own.

- ❌ 遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)み<b>する</b> → ✅ 遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)み<b>です</b>
  It is a noun phrase, so it takes the copula. The only verb that regularly
  follows it is 達(たっ)する, in ～の極(きわ)みに達(たっ)する.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence? 多(おお)くの皆様(みなさま)にご迷惑(めいわく)をおかけしたことは、遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)みであります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a prepared public statement - a press conference, an apology posted on a
company website, or a speech read from notes. The giveaways are
遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)み itself, the humble ご迷惑(めいわく)をおかけした,
and the podium copula であります. Nobody speaks like this to a colleague.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 久(ひさ)しぶりに友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)えて、感激(かんげき)の極(きわ)みでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

久(ひさ)しぶりに友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)えて、<b>本当(ほんとう)に嬉(うれ)しかったです</b>。（or 感動(かんどう)しました）

Meeting a friend is an everyday event, and the ceremonial noun phrase is far too
heavy for it. <code>感激(かんげき)の極(きわ)み</code> would fit a reunion
described in a speech at a formal gathering, not a report to a friend.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 担当者(たんとうしゃ)の説明(せつめい)は無責任(むせきにん)（の極(きわ)み・極(きわ)まりない）だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are possible, and they differ. <b>無責任(むせきにん)の極(きわ)み</b>だった
treats the explanation as reaching the summit of irresponsibility - the noun
phrase makes it sound like a written verdict. <b>無責任(むせきにん)極(きわ)まりない</b>
would need the だった removed, because
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> is already adjectival:
無責任(むせきにん)極(きわ)まりなかった.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山頂(さんちょう)からの景色(けしき)は美(うつく)しいの極(きわ)みでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山頂(さんちょう)からの景色(けしき)は<b>美(うつく)しさの極(きわ)み</b>でした。（more natural: この上(うえ)なく美(うつく)しかったです）

<code>の極(きわ)み</code> needs a noun in front of it, so the adjective has to
become 美(うつく)しさ. Even then the sentence reads like guidebook copy; in
speech, この上(うえ)なく美(うつく)しかった or ものすごくきれいでした is what a
person would say.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)の判断(はんだん)ミスは痛恨(つうこん)の極(きわ)みです。
(b) 今回(こんかい)の判断(はんだん)ミスは遺憾(いかん)の極(きわ)みです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker owning the error. 痛恨(つうこん) is personal, burning regret,
so the sentence reads as "this was my mistake and it will stay with me" - the line
a manager or a coach gives after a loss.

(b) distances the speaker from it. 遺憾(いかん) is institutional displeasure,
and Japanese listeners routinely hear it as the language of an organisation
registering disapproval rather than accepting blame. Used about one's own error it
can sound evasive, which is exactly why it is so common in official statements.

</details>

# ～の至り

**Meaning** · nothing short of; I could not be more ～ - a ceremonial way of reporting the speaker's own overwhelming feeling

至(いた)り is the noun of 至(いた)る, "to reach", so
<code>～の至(いた)り</code> is "the point that ～ arrives at". Unlike
<code>～の極(きわ)み</code>, which can describe a situation, this one is almost
always about **the person speaking**: their honour, their gratitude, their
embarrassment. It survives in a handful of frozen greetings and in one everyday
idiom, 若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (emotion) | noun + の至(いた)り | 光栄(こうえい) → 光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り |
| Ending a sentence | ～の至(いた)りです／でございます | 恐縮(きょうしゅく)の至(いた)りでございます |
| Humble report | ～の至(いた)りに存(ぞん)じます | 慶賀(けいが)の至(いた)りに存(ぞん)じます |
| Fixed idiom | 若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り = youthful folly | あれは若気(わかげ)の至(いた)りだった |
| Never a mere state | ❌混乱(こんらん)の至(いた)り | ✅混乱(こんらん)の極(きわ)み |

<div class="note">

**Note - the collocations that are actually live.** This is a closed list, and a
very short one: 光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り (honoured),
感激(かんげき)の至(いた)り (deeply moved),
恐縮(きょうしゅく)の至(いた)り (mortified at the trouble caused),
赤面(せきめん)の至(いた)り and 汗顔(かんがん)の至(いた)り (both
"I blush to say it"), 慶賀(けいが)の至(いた)り (formal congratulation), and
the idiom 若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り. Anything you assemble yourself will be
wrong: ❌疲労(ひろう)の至(いた)り, ❌贅沢(ぜいたく)の至(いた)り,
❌驚(おどろ)きの至(いた)り.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ご紹介(しょうかい)にあずかり、<b>光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り</b>です。
Thank you for that introduction; I am deeply honoured.
</pre>

<pre>
あのときの失敗(しっぱい)は<b>若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り</b>でした。
That blunder was the folly of youth.
</pre>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しい中(なか)をお運(はこ)びいただき、<b>恐縮(きょうしゅく)の至(いた)り</b>でございます。
That you should come all this way when you are so busy leaves me quite overwhelmed.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)の皆様(みなさま)から温(あたた)かい拍手(はくしゅ)をいただき、<b>感激(かんげき)の至(いた)り</b>です。
To be met with such warm applause from everyone here moves me more than I can say.
</pre>

<pre>
お名前(なまえ)を間違(まちが)えたまま挨拶(あいさつ)をしてしまい、<b>赤面(せきめん)の至(いた)り</b>です。
I gave the whole greeting with your name wrong; I could sink through the floor.
</pre>

<pre>
貴社(きしゃ)が創立(そうりつ)百周年(ひゃくしゅうねん)を迎(むか)えられましたこと、<b>慶賀(けいが)の至(いた)り</b>に存(ぞん)じます。
On your company's hundredth anniversary, please accept my warmest congratulations.
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)時代(じだい)に先輩(せんぱい)に食(く)ってかかったのは、今(いま)思(おも)えば<b>若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り</b>としか言(い)いようがない。
Squaring up to my seniors as a student was, looking back, nothing but youthful hot-headedness.
</pre>

<pre>
このたびは拙著(せっちょ)に過分(かぶん)のお言葉(ことば)を賜(たまわ)り、<b>光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り</b>に存(ぞん)じます。
To receive such generous words about my modest book is an honour indeed.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; produce the plain version instead.** Outside
若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り, every use of this pattern belongs to ceremonial
Japanese: an acceptance speech, the opening of a formal address, a letter of
congratulation or thanks, a message read aloud at a company anniversary. It sits
alongside でございます, 賜(たまわ)る and 存(ぞん)じます, and it sounds absurd
without them. If you are moved or honoured in real life, say
<code>大変(たいへん)光栄(こうえい)です</code>,
<code>本当(ほんとう)に嬉(うれ)しく思(おも)います</code> or
<code>お恥(は)ずかしい限(かぎ)りです</code>.

Two restrictions decide whether a sentence works. First, **the feeling must be the
speaker's own.** ❌先生(せんせい)は感激(かんげき)の至(いた)りです describes
someone else's inner state and is not said; you would write
先生(せんせい)は大変(たいへん)感激(かんげき)しておられました. Second,
**the noun must name an emotion, not a condition.** Fatigue, chaos and
extravagance are conditions, and they take
<code>の極(きわ)み</code> instead.

<code>若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り</code> is the exception you can actually use.
It is a fixed noun phrase meaning "something rash I did because I was young", it
appears in ordinary adult conversation, and it usually comes with a rueful smile.
Note that it does not mean "the height of youth" - the 至(いた)り has become
part of a word, and the phrase names the *deed*, not the feeling.

Younger speakers today are more likely to have heard
光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り in a costume drama or a stiff speech than to have
used it, and some of the other collocations -
汗顔(かんがん)の至(いた)り especially - already read as distinctly
old-fashioned even in formal writing.

## Contrast

### ～の至(いた)り vs the modern equivalent

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - what you should say is
<code>大変(たいへん)光栄(こうえい)です</code>,
<code>本当(ほんとう)に嬉(うれ)しいです</code>,
<code>恐縮(きょうしゅく)です</code>. They cover every situation
<code>の至(いた)り</code> covers, in language that does not need a lectern.

</div>

<pre>
このような席(せき)にお招(まね)きいただき、<b>光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り</b>に存(ぞん)じます。
To be invited to an occasion such as this is the greatest of honours.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな会(かい)に呼(よ)んでいただいて、<b>本当(ほんとう)に光栄(こうえい)です</b>。
It's a real honour to have been invited to something like this.
</pre>

Same event, same gratitude. The first is read from a card at a formal dinner; the
second is what you say when the host comes over to your table.
恐縮(きょうしゅく)です on its own is likewise the everyday descendant of
恐縮(きょうしゅく)の至(いた)りでございます.

### ～の至(いた)り vs ～の極(きわ)み

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>の至(いた)り</code> = a feeling rising in **me**.
<code>の極(きわ)み</code> = a state at its **maximum**, mine or anyone's or
nobody's.

</div>

<pre>
ご指名(しめい)をいただき、<b>感激(かんげき)の至(いた)り</b>でございます。
To have been chosen leaves me profoundly moved.
</pre>

<pre>
連日(れんじつ)の残業(ざんぎょう)で、職場(しょくば)は<b>疲労(ひろう)の極(きわ)み</b>にあった。
Day after day of overtime had left the office completely exhausted.
</pre>

The second cannot be rebuilt with <code>の至(いた)り</code>: an office is not
feeling anything, it is in a condition. In the small overlap where both are
possible - 光栄(こうえい) takes either - the
至(いた)り version is the humbler and the older.

### ～の至(いた)り vs ～限(かぎ)りだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both report the speaker's own feeling.
<code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> takes an **adjective** and is merely formal;
<code>の至(いた)り</code> takes a **noun** and is ceremonial. Of the two, only
<code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> is worth producing.

</div>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりに皆(みな)さんに会(あ)えて、<b>嬉(うれ)しい限(かぎ)りです</b>。
I could not be happier to see everyone again after so long.
</pre>

<pre>
身(み)に余(あま)るお言葉(ことば)をいただき、<b>恐縮(きょうしゅく)の至(いた)り</b>です。
Such undeserved praise leaves me quite abashed.
</pre>

You could say the first at a reunion, in a work email, or in a speech. The second
would be strange anywhere except in front of an audience or on paper - and even
there, 恐縮(きょうしゅく)です would do the job.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 疲労(ひろう)<b>の至(いた)り</b>です → ✅ 疲労(ひろう)<b>の極(きわ)み</b>です（or とても疲(つか)れました）
  Exhaustion is a condition, not a welling-up feeling, so it takes
  <code>の極(きわ)み</code>. This is the single commonest error with the pair.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)は<b>感激(かんげき)の至(いた)り</b>です。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)は大変(たいへん)感激(かんげき)しておられました。
  The pattern reports the speaker's own emotion. Used of a third person it reads
  as though you were speaking their lines for them.

- ❌ お会(あ)いできて光栄(こうえい)<b>の至(いた)ります</b>。 → ✅ お会(あ)いできて光栄(こうえい)<b>の至(いた)りです</b>。
  至(いた)り is a noun here, not the verb 至(いた)る. It takes です,
  でございます or に存(ぞん)じます.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は<b>若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り</b>をしてしまった。 → ✅ あれは<b>若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り</b>でした。
  The idiom is a label you put on a past deed, not something you do. It also needs
  distance in time: a person in their twenties saying it about yesterday sounds
  like a joke.

- ❌ （to a friend）ケーキありがとう。光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)りだよ。 → ✅ ケーキありがとう。すごく嬉(うれ)しい。
  Register, not grammar. Ceremonial vocabulary aimed at a friend is heard as
  sarcasm.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to hear this sentence, and who is speaking? 本日(ほんじつ)このような賞(しょう)をいただき、光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)りに存(ぞん)じます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

At an award ceremony, spoken by the recipient into a microphone. Three signals
stack up: 本日(ほんじつ) rather than 今日(きょう), the ceremonial
光栄(こうえい)の至(いた)り, and the humble に存(ぞん)じます. Written down
it would suit a letter of thanks; it fits no ordinary conversation.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: このたびはお招(まね)きいただき、感激(かんげき)の至(いた)りでございます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は招(まね)いてくださって、<b>本当(ほんとう)に嬉(うれ)しいです</b>。（more polite: お招(まね)きいただき、大変(たいへん)嬉(うれ)しく思(おも)います）

The ceremonial noun phrase and でございます both come out.
嬉(うれ)しく思(おも)います keeps the sentence polite enough for a work
setting without sounding like a speech.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 三日(みっか)連続(れんぞく)の徹夜(てつや)で、チームは（疲労(ひろう)の至(いた)り・疲労(ひろう)の極(きわ)み）だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

疲労(ひろう)<b>の極(きわ)み</b>だった。

The subject is a team and the noun names a physical condition, so
<code>の至(いた)り</code> is doubly wrong: it wants an emotion, and it wants
that emotion to be the speaker's. Note that a plain
疲(つか)れ切(き)っていた would be the natural sentence outside a written
report.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 若(わか)いころ会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めて世界(せかい)を回(まわ)ったのは、若気(わかげ)の極(きわ)みだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 若(わか)いころ会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めて世界(せかい)を回(まわ)ったのは、<b>若気(わかげ)の至(いた)り</b>だった。
"Quitting my job to travel the world when I was young was youthful recklessness."

若気(わかげ) only ever pairs with 至(いた)り. The phrase is frozen, which is
also why it is the one member of this family a learner can safely use.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ご指導(しどう)いただき、恐縮(きょうしゅく)の至(いた)りです。
(b) ご指導(しどう)いただき、ありがとうございます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is ceremonial and self-lowering. 恐縮(きょうしゅく) says the speaker feels
they have imposed and are barely worthy of the trouble taken, and
<code>の至(いた)り</code> pushes that to the ceiling. It fits a written note to
a senior figure, or remarks at a formal gathering.

(b) simply thanks the person. It is polite, warm and usable anywhere, including to
the same senior figure in the corridor. A learner should reach for (b) every time
and recognise (a) when it appears in a letter.

</details>

# ～限りだ

**Meaning** · I could not be more ～; nothing could be more ～ than this - the speaker's own feeling, at its ceiling

限(かぎ)り is "limit", so <code>嬉(うれ)しい限(かぎ)りだ</code> is "happy to
the limit". Two things make this pattern narrower than the English "extremely".
The adjective has to name **a feeling**, and the feeling has to be **the
speaker's own**. It is a report from inside, not a measurement of the world: a
price cannot be 高(たか)い限(かぎ)りだ, and your colleague cannot be
嬉(うれ)しい限(かぎ)りだ.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | plain form + 限(かぎ)りだ | 嬉(うれ)しい → 嬉(うれ)しい限(かぎ)りだ |
| い-adjective (humble お) | お + adjective + 限(かぎ)りです | 恥(は)ずかしい → お恥(は)ずかしい限(かぎ)りです |
| な-adjective (rare) | ～な + 限(かぎ)りだ | 残念(ざんねん)な → 残念(ざんねん)な限(かぎ)りだ |
| Polite | ～限(かぎ)りです | 心強(こころづよ)い限(かぎ)りです |
| Past | ～限(かぎ)りだった | 情(なさ)けない限(かぎ)りだった |
| Not a feeling | ❌高(たか)い限(かぎ)りだ | ✅高(たか)すぎる |

<div class="note">

**Note - which adjectives really occur.** The list is short enough to learn whole:
嬉(うれ)しい, 羨(うらや)ましい, 寂(さび)しい, 心細(こころぼそ)い,
心強(こころづよ)い, 頼(たの)もしい, 恥(は)ずかしい, 悔(くや)しい,
情(なさ)けない, ありがたい, 恐(おそ)ろしい. Grammar books also allow a
な-adjective with な in front of it, but in real text almost everything you meet is
an い-adjective; if you are unsure, use one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
後輩(こうはい)が活躍(かつやく)してくれて、頼(たの)もしい<b>限(かぎ)りだ</b>。
It is tremendously reassuring to see the younger staff doing so well.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)での夜勤(やきん)は心細(こころぼそ)い<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。
Working the night shift alone leaves me feeling utterly exposed.
</pre>

<pre>
毎年(まいとし)海外(かいがい)で休暇(きゅうか)を過(す)ごせるなんて、羨(うらや)ましい<b>限(かぎ)りだ</b>。
Spending your holidays abroad every single year - I could not be more envious.
</pre>

<pre>
見苦(みぐる)しいところをお見(み)せしてしまい、お恥(は)ずかしい<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。
I am thoroughly embarrassed that you had to see me like that.
</pre>

<pre>
経験(けいけん)豊富(ほうふ)な方(かた)に加(くわ)わっていただき、心強(こころづよ)い<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。
Having someone of your experience join us is a real weight off our minds.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)の準備(じゅんび)不足(ぶそく)で負(ま)けたのだから、情(なさ)けない<b>限(かぎ)りだった</b>。
I lost through my own lack of preparation, and I felt about as small as it is possible to feel.
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)のころから通(かよ)った喫茶店(きっさてん)が来月(らいげつ)閉(し)まると聞(き)き、寂(さび)しい<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。
Hearing that the coffee shop I have been going to since I was a student closes next month, I am terribly sad.
</pre>

<pre>
皆様(みなさま)の支(ささ)えがあってこそここまで続(つづ)けてこられたわけで、ありがたい<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。
It is only through everyone's support that we have kept going this long, and I am more grateful than I can say.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>～限(かぎ)りだ</code> is **formal but usable**, which sets it apart from the
rest of this family. It belongs to written Japanese, work email, speeches and
carefully spoken conversation; with a friend over coffee you would simply say
<code>すごく嬉(うれ)しい</code>. But in a thank-you message, a report or a
retirement speech, <code>嬉(うれ)しい限(かぎ)りです</code> is exactly right
and not at all stuffy.

Several of its uses have hardened into social formulas.
<code>お恥(は)ずかしい限(かぎ)りです</code> is the standard humble response to
praise or to having been caught out; <code>心強(こころづよ)い限(かぎ)りです</code>
is what you say when someone reliable joins the effort;
<code>ありがたい限(かぎ)りです</code> closes a message of thanks.

<code>羨(うらや)ましい限(かぎ)りです</code> deserves a warning. Said warmly it
is a compliment, but the same words delivered flatly are a well-known way of
needling someone whose good fortune you think is undeserved - the English "must be
nice". Tone and context carry all of it, so use it only where the goodwill is
obvious.

Because the feeling must be the speaker's own, third-person sentences need
something else: 彼女(かのじょ)は嬉(うれ)しそうだった,
彼(かれ)は悔(くや)しがっていた. A question to the listener is possible when
you are inviting them to voice their own feeling, but it is rare and reads as
literary.

## Contrast

### ～限(かぎ)りだ vs ～極(きわ)まりない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> reports **how I feel**.
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> passes a **verdict on something**, and it is
nearly always a hostile one.

</div>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)だけ説明(せつめい)を受(う)けていなかったとは、不愉快(ふゆかい)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
To find that I alone was never briefed is thoroughly offensive.
</pre>

<pre>
事情(じじょう)も知(し)らされないまま待(ま)たされて、情(なさ)けない<b>限(かぎ)りだ</b>。
Left waiting with nothing explained to me, I felt utterly wretched.
</pre>

Both speakers are unhappy about the same treatment. The first is condemning the
handling; the second is describing what it did to them. That is the whole
difference, and English "extremely" hides it.

### ～限(かぎ)りだ vs ～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>てたまらない</code> is a feeling you cannot bear, often
physical, and it is ordinary spoken Japanese.
<code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> is a feeling at its maximum, stated for the record, and
it is formal.

</div>

<pre>
エアコンが壊(こわ)れていて、暑(あつ)く<b>てたまらない</b>。
The air conditioning is broken and it is unbearably hot.
</pre>

<pre>
地元(じもと)のチームが優勝(ゆうしょう)し、嬉(うれ)しい<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。
Our local team took the title, and I could not be more delighted.
</pre>

<code>てたまらない</code> covers heat, itching, sleepiness and craving, none of
which <code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> can touch - ❌眠(ねむ)い限(かぎ)りだ.
Conversely <code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> handles social feelings such as gratitude
and embarrassment that <code>てたまらない</code> would make sound like a physical
symptom.

### ～限(かぎ)りだ vs the other 限(かぎ)り

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if 限(かぎ)り follows an **adjective of feeling** and ends
the sentence, it is this pattern. If it follows a **verb or a noun**, it is the
unrelated "as far as / as long as / unless" 限(かぎ)り.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)の知(し)る<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、変更(へんこう)の連絡(れんらく)は来(き)ていません。
As far as I know, no notice of any change has come through.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)が夢(ゆめ)を諦(あきら)めずにいてくれるのは、嬉(うれ)しい<b>限(かぎ)りだ</b>。
That my son has not given up on his dream makes me happier than I can say.
</pre>

The same noun is doing quite different work: scope in the first
（見(み)る限(かぎ)り, できる限(かぎ)り, 行(い)かない限(かぎ)り）,
degree of feeling in the second. Only the second takes だ or です directly.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>限(かぎ)りだ</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)すぎる。
  The adjective must name an emotion. Size, price, speed and temperature are out.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)は嬉(うれ)しい<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は嬉(うれ)しそうでした。
  The pattern states the speaker's own feeling. For someone else you need
  そうだ, がる or a reported form.

- ❌ 嬉(うれ)しい<b>の限(かぎ)りです</b> → ✅ 嬉(うれ)しい<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>
  No の. The adjective attaches directly, exactly as it would to こと.

- ❌ ご心配(しんぱい)をおかけして、恥(は)ずかしい限(かぎ)り<b>に思(おも)います</b>。 → ✅ ご心配(しんぱい)をおかけして、お恥(は)ずかしい<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>。
  <code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> is already the predicate and does not need a verb of
  feeling after it. In this apology the humble お is close to obligatory.

- ❌ 眠(ねむ)い<b>限(かぎ)りだ</b>。 → ✅ 眠(ねむ)くてたまらない。
  Sleepiness is a bodily state rather than an emotion about a situation, so it
  takes てたまらない or てしかたがない.

**1.** 新(あたら)しいチームに信頼(しんらい)できる先輩(せんぱい)がいてくれて、心強(こころづよ)い____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

心強(こころづよ)い<b>限(かぎ)りです</b>（or 限(かぎ)りだ）

心強(こころづよ)い is one of the pattern's core adjectives, and the feeling is
the speaker's own. The polite です suits the workplace setting the sentence
implies.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

失礼(しつれい)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。

The sentence condemns someone else's conduct, which is

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "To have been able to work with all of you for ten years is something I am deeply grateful for."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> ends the sentence by itself. Adding

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

own envy on the table, and <code>限(かぎ)りです</code> maximises it. Said warmly
it is a generous piece of praise; said flatly, or by someone with reason to be
sour, it is the Japanese "must be nice for some". Use it where your goodwill is
already established, and prefer (b) when it is not.

</details>

# ～といったらない

**Meaning** · there are no words for how ～ it is; unbelievably ～ - an exclamation, usually a complaint

Literally "if you were to try saying it, there is nothing (to say)": the speaker
claims that language runs out. In practice it is the everyday spoken way of
maxing out an adjective, and about four times out of five what it maxes out is an
irritation - heat, noise, hassle, someone's rudeness. It can express admiration
too, most naturally in the past form when recalling something remarkable.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | plain form + といったらない | うるさい → うるさいといったらない |
| な-adjective (stem) | stem + といったらない | 不親切(ふしんせつ) → 不親切(ふしんせつ)といったらない |
| Noun of quality | noun + といったらない | 暑(あつ)さ → 暑(あつ)さといったらない |
| Recalling (past) | ～といったらなかった | 緊張(きんちょう) → 緊張(きんちょう)といったらなかった |
| Casual contraction | ～ったらない | 面倒(めんどう)くさい → 面倒(めんどう)くさいったらない |
| Emphatic complaint | ～といったらありゃしない／ありはしない | 情(なさ)けないといったらありゃしない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun in the third row is nearly always a quality noun: an
adjective turned into a noun with ～さ (暑(あつ)さ, 汚(きたな)さ,
美(うつく)しさ), a feeling (緊張(きんちょう), 焦(あせ)り), or a ～ぶり /
～よう "manner of doing" noun (喜(よろこ)びよう, 慌(あわ)てよう). A plain
concrete noun does not work: ❌この机(つくえ)といったらない.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)の暑(あつ)さ<b>といったらない</b>。
The heat this summer is beyond anything.
</pre>

<pre>
夜中(よなか)まで続(つづ)く工事(こうじ)の音(おと)はうるさい<b>といったらない</b>。
The noise from the roadworks going on until the middle of the night is unbelievable.
</pre>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)、面倒(めんどう)くさい<b>ったらない</b>よ。
This paperwork is an absolute nightmare.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて一人(ひとり)で発表(はっぴょう)したときの緊張(きんちょう)<b>といったらなかった</b>。
I cannot describe how nervous I was the first time I presented on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)から見(み)た朝日(あさひ)の美(うつく)しさ<b>といったらなかった</b>。
The beauty of the sunrise seen from up on the mountain was something else entirely.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じミスを三度(さんど)も繰(く)り返(かえ)すなんて、情(なさ)けない<b>といったらありゃしない</b>。
Making the same mistake three times over - I have never felt so pathetic.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの荷造(にづく)りが終(お)わらないまま当日(とうじつ)の朝(あさ)を迎(むか)えたときの焦(あせ)り<b>といったらなかった</b>。
The panic when moving day arrived and the packing still was not finished - there are no words.
</pre>

<pre>
窓口(まどぐち)を三(みっ)つも回(まわ)らされたあげく、また同(おな)じ書類(しょるい)を出(だ)せと言(い)われて、手続(てつづ)きの煩雑(はんざつ)さ<b>といったらありはしない</b>。
After being sent to three different counters and then told to submit the same form again, the sheer tangle of the procedure defies belief.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **spoken** pattern, and a lively one. It fits conversation, blogs,
essays and dialogue in fiction; it is out of place in a report, a formal letter or
anything read from a podium, where
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> or a plain
<code>非常(ひじょう)に</code> would take over. Nothing about it is rude, but it
is emphatic, and stacking several in a row makes the speaker sound like a
complainer.

The four shapes are not interchangeable in tone.
<code>といったらない</code> is the neutral base.
<code>～ったらない</code> is its casual squeeze - friends, family, muttering to
yourself - and it usually ends with よ or ね.
<code>といったらありゃしない</code> is louder still, is **only** used for
complaints, and carries a slightly old-fashioned, downtown flavour; it is the
voice of a neighbour grumbling over the fence.
<code>といったらありはしない</code> is the same thing written out properly and
appears mostly in print.

The positive use is real but narrower. It works best in the past, looking back at
something extraordinary, and with a ～さ noun:
<code>美(うつく)しさといったらなかった</code>,
<code>嬉(うれ)しさといったらなかった</code>. In the present tense with a plain
adjective, listeners default to hearing a complaint, so
美味(おい)しいといったらない needs a happy face and a good context to land.

One structural point worth holding on to: this is a **degree exclamation about a
thing**, not a report of the speaker's inner state. It can therefore describe
someone else's behaviour freely - 弟(おとうと)の喜(よろこ)びようといったらなかった -
where <code>～限(かぎ)りだ</code> could not.

## Contrast

### ～といったらない vs ～極(きわ)まりない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, opposite registers.
<code>といったらない</code> is what you say out loud;
<code>極(きわ)まりない</code> is what you write in a complaint letter about the
same thing.

</div>

<pre>
あの店員(てんいん)の態度(たいど)、失礼(しつれい)<b>ったらない</b>。
That assistant's attitude - unbelievably rude.
</pre>

<pre>
当該(とうがい)職員(しょくいん)の対応(たいおう)は失礼(しつれい)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>ものであった。
The conduct of the member of staff in question was discourteous in the extreme.
</pre>

The content is the same complaint. The first would be odd in writing addressed to
a company; the second would be comical said to a friend on the pavement outside
the shop.

### ～といったらない vs ～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>といったらない</code> rates **the thing**: it is
off the scale. <code>てたまらない</code> reports **the speaker**: I cannot stand
it.

</div>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)の寒(さむ)さ<b>といったらない</b>。
The cold in this room is something else.
</pre>

<pre>
上着(うわぎ)を忘(わす)れてきて、寒(さむ)く<b>てたまらない</b>。
I left my jacket behind and I am freezing.
</pre>

Because <code>といったらない</code> is a rating, it works on other people's
experiences and on scenery; <code>てたまらない</code> is tied to the person
speaking, so ❌彼(かれ)は寒(さむ)くてたまらない needs らしい or そうだ to be
said at all.

### ～といったらない vs ～といったら (topic)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if a **negative word** follows, it is this pattern. If the
sentence **continues** with what comes to mind, it is the ordinary topic-raising
<code>といったら</code>, "speaking of".

</div>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の京都(きょうと)<b>といったら</b>、やはり祇園祭(ぎおんまつり)だろう。
Speak of Kyoto in summer and it has to be the Gion Festival.
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の京都(きょうと)の蒸(む)し暑(あつ)さ<b>といったらない</b>。
The humidity of Kyoto in summer has to be experienced to be believed.
</pre>

Same opening five syllables, completely different sentences. The clue is what
lands at the end: ない / ありゃしない closes the exclamation, whereas the topic use
hands over to a comment.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 不便(ふべん)な<b>といったらない</b> → ✅ 不便(ふべん)<b>といったらない</b>
  A な-adjective attaches by its bare stem, with no な.

- ❌ この暑(あつ)さといったらない<b>だ</b>。 → ✅ この暑(あつ)さ<b>といったらない</b>。
  The ending is already ない, an い-adjective form. Adding だ is the same error as
  ❌寒(さむ)いだ.

- ❌ うるさい<b>といったらありません</b> → ✅ うるさい<b>といったらありゃしない</b>／<b>といったらありはしない</b>
  This variant is frozen. There is no polite ありません version of it, because the
  whole expression is colloquial.

- ❌ （in a written complaint to a company）対応(たいおう)が遅(おそ)い<b>ったらない</b>。 → ✅ 対応(たいおう)の遅(おそ)さは遺憾(いかん)である。
  Grammatical, and the wrong register: ったらない in a formal letter reads as
  though the writer had lost their temper mid-sentence.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)に受(う)かって<b>嬉(うれ)しいといったらない</b>けど、次(つぎ)が心配(しんぱい)だ。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)に受(う)かって嬉(うれ)しい<b>限(かぎ)りだ</b>けど、次(つぎ)が心配(しんぱい)だ。
  Not impossible, but the present-tense positive use is weak, and a listener may
  hear the first half as sarcasm. For your own happiness,
  <code>限(かぎ)りだ</code> or plain とても嬉(うれ)しい is safer.

## Practice

**1.** 満員電車(まんいんでんしゃ)に一時間(いちじかん)立(た)ちっぱなしで、疲(つか)れる____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

疲(つか)れる<b>といったらない</b>（casual: 疲(つか)れるったらない）

A complaint about an everyday ordeal is the pattern's core use, and
疲(つか)れる attaches in its plain form just as an い-adjective would. In the
past, 疲(つか)れるといったらなかった.

</details>

**2.** Choose: (会議(かいぎ)の報告書(ほうこくしょ)に書(か)く) この事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)となった管理(かんり)体制(たいせい)は、ずさん（といったらありゃしない・極(きわ)まりない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ずさん<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。

The setting decides it. といったらありゃしない is spoken grumbling and would
undermine a written report; <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> is the formal written
verdict for exactly this kind of criticism.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You cannot imagine how happy my daughter was when she saw the puppy."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Two points make this idiomatic: the ～よう noun 喜(よろこ)びよう, "the way she
was delighted", and the past form for recalling a scene. Note that the feeling
belongs to the daughter, which is fine here -
<code>～限(かぎ)りだ</code> could not be used about someone else.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 説明書(せつめいしょ)が分(わ)かりにくいなといったらないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 説明書(せつめいしょ)が分(わ)かりにくい<b>といったらない</b>。
"The manual is impossibly hard to follow."

Two repairs. The stray な does not belong - 分(わ)かりにくい is an
い-adjective and attaches directly. And the tacked-on です clashes with a
thoroughly colloquial pattern; if you need to be polite, say
大変(たいへん)分(わ)かりにくいです instead.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 隣(となり)の部屋(へや)の音(おと)がうるさい<b>といったらない</b>。
(b) 隣(となり)の部屋(へや)の音(おと)がうるさく<b>てたまらない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) rates the noise itself: it is off the scale, more than words can cover. The
speaker sounds like someone reporting an outrage, and the sentence would sit
happily in a story told to a friend.

(b) reports the speaker's endurance: I cannot put up with it any longer. The focus
moves from the noise to the person suffering it, and it is the natural line just
before you go and knock on the neighbour's door.

</details>

# ～のなんのって

**Meaning** · you would not believe how ～; ～ is putting it mildly - spoken storytelling emphasis, thrown in mid-anecdote

The shape is a quotation that gives up on itself: "whether you call it ～ or call
it anything else…". A speaker uses it in the middle of telling a story, to signal
that the ordinary adjective they just reached for is not going to be enough, and
then usually goes straight on to what happened as a result.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | plain form + のなんのって | 痛(いた)い → 痛(いた)いのなんのって |
| Verb | plain form + のなんのって | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)くのなんのって |
| な-adjective | ～な + のなんのって | 大変(たいへん)な → 大変(たいへん)なのなんのって |
| Shorter variant | ～のなんの | 辛(から)い → 辛(から)いのなんの |
| Louder variant | ～のなんのったらない | 寒(さむ)いのなんのったらない |
| Different pattern | ～のなんのと + 言(い)う／文句(もんく) | 高(たか)いのなんのと文句(もんく)を言(い)う |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the last row is a genuinely different construction and catches learners
out. <code>～のなんのと</code> followed by a verb of speaking means "going on about
X and this and that", and it is dismissive of the *speaker being quoted*:
狭(せま)いのなんのと文句(もんく)ばかり言(い)っている, "he does nothing but
complain that it's cramped and so on". No emphasis of degree is involved.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
痛(いた)い<b>のなんのって</b>、しばらく動(うご)けなかったよ。
It hurt like you would not believe - I could not move for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
寒(さむ)い<b>のなんのって</b>、指(ゆび)の感覚(かんかく)がなくなった。
Cold does not begin to cover it; I lost all feeling in my fingers.
</pre>

<pre>
あの日(ひ)の人(ひと)の多(おお)さがすごい<b>のなんのって</b>、駅(えき)を出(で)るのに三十分(さんじゅっぷん)かかった。
The crowds that day were something else - it took half an hour just to get out of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
辛(から)い<b>のなんの</b>、一口(ひとくち)食(た)べただけで水(みず)を三杯(さんばい)飲(の)んだ。
Spicy is an understatement: one mouthful and I got through three glasses of water.
</pre>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)を伝(つた)えたら喜(よろこ)ぶ<b>のなんのって</b>、祖母(そぼ)は電話口(でんわぐち)で泣(な)き出(だ)した。
When I told her I had passed, she was so overjoyed that she started crying down the phone.
</pre>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)の渋滞(じゅうたい)がひどい<b>のなんのって</b>、三時間(さんじかん)走(はし)って十(じゅっ)キロも進(すす)まなかった。
The holiday traffic was beyond belief - three hours of driving and we had not covered ten kilometres.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが泣(な)く<b>のなんのって</b>、近所(きんじょ)中(じゅう)に聞(き)こえていたと思(おも)う。
The kid cried and cried; I think the whole neighbourhood could hear it.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりに会(あ)った同級生(どうきゅうせい)が変(か)わっていた<b>のなんのって</b>、最初(さいしょ)は誰(だれ)だか分(わ)からなかったほどだ。
My old classmate had changed so much that at first I genuinely could not tell who he was.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is spoken Japanese, and it is marked.** You will hear it in anecdotes over
dinner, on variety television, in comedy routines and in the dialogue of novels
and manga; it turns up in casual written storytelling, blogs and essays, precisely
because those imitate speech. It leans older and chatty - the voice of someone
warming to a good story - and a learner who drops it into a work conversation will
sound as though they are performing. **Recognise it; say something else.**

The ordinary equivalents are simple.
<code>ものすごく痛(いた)くて、しばらく動(うご)けなかった</code> or
<code>痛(いた)くてたまらなかった</code> carry the same content with no register
risk. In writing, <code>非常(ひじょう)に</code> or a plain
<code>とても</code> does the work.

Structurally, notice that the pattern is a **parenthesis, not a predicate**. It
does not end a proper sentence so much as break one off: the speaker states the
quality, waves it away as inadequate, and then delivers the evidence. That is why
almost every real example has a second clause after it. It can be left hanging -
「もう、暑(あつ)いのなんのって。」 - but then the listener is expected to fill in
the rest, which only works face to face.

The って is the casual quotative, so the whole thing is casual by construction.
There is no polite version: ❌痛(いた)いのなんのってです does not exist. If the
sentence needs です or ます, this pattern is the wrong choice.

## Contrast

### ～のなんのって vs the modern equivalent

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - produce <code>ものすごく～て</code>,
<code>～てたまらない</code> or <code>本当(ほんとう)に～</code>. Keep
<code>のなんのって</code> for understanding what you hear.

</div>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)で転(ころ)んで、恥(は)ずかしい<b>のなんのって</b>。
I fell over on the stairs - I have never been so embarrassed.
</pre>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)で転(ころ)んでしまって、<b>本当(ほんとう)に恥(は)ずかしかった</b>です。
I fell over on the stairs and I was really embarrassed.
</pre>

The first is a friend telling a story; the second can be said to anyone, including
your boss. Both are natural Japanese, but only one of them is safe to produce
before you have a native ear for register.

### ～のなんのって vs ～といったらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are colloquial exclamations of degree.
<code>といったらない</code> can stand as a complete sentence and is often a
verdict; <code>のなんのって</code> is a mid-story aside that expects a follow-up
clause.

</div>

<pre>
この季節(きせつ)の花粉(かふん)のひどさ<b>といったらない</b>。
The pollen at this time of year is beyond anything.
</pre>

<pre>
花粉(かふん)がひどい<b>のなんのって</b>、一日中(いちにちじゅう)くしゃみが止(と)まらなかった。
The pollen was so bad that I sneezed all day long.
</pre>

<code>といったらない</code> also attaches happily to a ～さ noun and appears in
print; <code>のなんのって</code> is tied to the speaking voice and to the anecdote
it is embedded in.

### ～のなんのって vs ～のなんのと言(い)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a verb of **speaking or complaining** after のなんのと changes
the meaning completely: not "extremely", but "going on about X and everything
else".

</div>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は狭(せま)いのなんの<b>と文句(もんく)を言(い)って</b>、部屋(へや)を替(か)えろとうるさい。
My son keeps complaining that it's cramped and so on, and pesters me to change rooms.
</pre>

<pre>
その部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>のなんのって</b>、ベッドを置(お)いたら何(なに)も入(はい)らなかった。
The room was so small that once the bed was in, nothing else would fit.
</pre>

In the first, someone else is doing the talking and the speaker is unimpressed by
it. In the second, the speaker is doing the talking and the room really was tiny.
The signal is the と plus a speech verb.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 暑(あつ)い<b>のなんのってです</b>。 → ✅ 暑(あつ)い<b>のなんのって</b>。／本当(ほんとう)に暑(あつ)かったです。
  The って is casual quotation; the pattern has no polite form at all.

- ❌ 大変(たいへん)<b>のなんのって</b> → ✅ 大変(たいへん)<b>なのなんのって</b>
  A な-adjective keeps its な before の, exactly as it does before のだ.

- ❌ (会議(かいぎ)で) 今回(こんかい)の遅(おく)れは深刻(しんこく)なのなんのって。 → ✅ (会議(かいぎ)で) 今回(こんかい)の遅(おく)れは極(きわ)めて深刻(しんこく)です。
  Register. In a meeting this sounds like a comedian's aside; the formal register
  wants 極(きわ)めて or 非常(ひじょう)に.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は給料(きゅうりょう)が安(やす)い<b>のなんのって</b>文句(もんく)を言(い)っている。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は給料(きゅうりょう)が安(やす)い<b>のなんのと</b>文句(もんく)を言(い)っている。
  With a speech verb the particle is と, not って, and the meaning shifts to "going
  on about the pay and everything else".

**1.** Where would you expect to hear this, and from whom? 「もう、待(ま)たされたのなんのって、二時間(にじかん)だよ、二時間(にじかん)。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In casual conversation - a friend or family member telling you about their day,

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)の会場(かいじょう)は<b>本当(ほんとう)に暑(あつ)くて</b>、途中(とちゅう)で外(そと)に出(で)てしまいました。（or 暑(あつ)くてたまらず）

The information does not change; only the performance does.
暑(あつ)くてたまらず is the slightly more formal written option, and both can be

</details>

**3.** Which of these is the "complaining" pattern rather than the emphatic one?
(a) 部屋(へや)が寒(さむ)いのなんのって、毛布(もうふ)を二枚(にまい)使(つか)った。
(b) 部屋(へや)が寒(さむ)いのなんのと言(い)って、息子(むすこ)は帰(かえ)ってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b). The と plus 言(い)って marks it as a report of someone else's grumbling:

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの映画(えいが)の最後(さいご)の場面(ばめん)は<b>本当(ほんとう)に感動的(かんどうてき)でした</b>。（casual: 感動的(かんどうてき)<b>なのなんのって</b>、涙(なみだ)が止(と)まらなかった）

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) is a complete exclamation and could close a paragraph. It is also the one you

speaking voice.

</details>

# ～を禁じ得ない

**Meaning** · cannot suppress; cannot help feeling - a feeling forces its way out despite the writer's restraint

禁(きん)じる is "to forbid" and 得(え)ない is "cannot", so the phrase is
literally "I cannot forbid it (in myself)". That image is the whole pattern: a
person who is trying to stay composed, and a feeling that will not be held down.
It takes a **noun** naming that feeling, it belongs to formal written Japanese,
and it reports the writer's own reaction, not a judgement about how bad the thing
is.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (emotion) | noun + を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない | 怒(いか)り → 怒(いか)りを禁(きん)じ得(え)ない |
| Noun + の念(ねん) | ～の念(ねん)を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない | 尊敬(そんけい) → 尊敬(そんけい)の念(ねん)を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない |
| Past | ～を禁(きん)じ得(え)なかった | 涙(なみだ)を禁(きん)じ得(え)なかった |
| Polite | ～を禁(きん)じ得(え)ません | 憤(いきどお)りを禁(きん)じ得(え)ません |
| Never a verb | ❌泣(な)くを禁(きん)じ得(え)ない | ✅涙(なみだ)を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない |
| No affirmative | ❌涙(なみだ)を禁(きん)じ得(え)る | the expression exists only in the negative |

<div class="note">

**Note on the reading and the spelling** - 得(え)ない here is the "cannot"
suffix, so it is <b>えない</b>, never うない: きんじえない. The phrase is written
both as 禁(きん)じ得(え)ない and as <code>禁(きん)じえない</code>, and both
are standard; newspapers tend to use the kana form.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note - the nouns that are actually live.** The slot is not open to any emotion.
What genuinely occurs is 怒(いか)り, 憤(いきどお)り, 涙(なみだ),
驚(おどろ)き, 疑問(ぎもん), 失望(しつぼう), 落胆(らくたん),
同情(どうじょう), 違和感(いわかん), 苦笑(くしょう), 感動(かんどう), and
the ～の念(ねん) phrases (尊敬(そんけい)の念(ねん),
畏敬(いけい)の念(ねん)). Notice the shape of that list: these are feelings a
composed adult would rather not show. Ordinary pleasure does not fit -
❌喜(よろこ)びを禁(きん)じ得(え)ない is not how the pattern is used.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この判決(はんけつ)には驚(おどろ)き<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b>。
One cannot but be astonished at this verdict.
</pre>

<pre>
被害者(ひがいしゃ)の話(はなし)を聞(き)き、涙(なみだ)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)なかった</b>。
Listening to the victim speak, I could not hold back my tears.
</pre>

<pre>
事前(じぜん)の説明(せつめい)と食(く)い違(ちが)う点(てん)が多(おお)く、疑問(ぎもん)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b>。
There is much here that contradicts the earlier account, and doubts inevitably arise.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)職場(しょくば)を支(ささ)えてきた人(ひと)たちの扱(あつか)いには、憤(いきどお)り<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ません</b>。
At the treatment of people who have held this workplace together for years, I cannot suppress my indignation.
</pre>

<pre>
被災地(ひさいち)から届(とど)く映像(えいぞう)を前(まえ)に、同情(どうじょう)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)なかった</b>。
Faced with the footage coming out of the disaster area, I could not help but feel for them.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)よりも日程(にってい)を優先(ゆうせん)したかのような説明(せつめい)には、違和感(いわかん)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b>。
There is something one cannot help finding wrong in an explanation that appears to put the schedule before safety.
</pre>

<pre>
逃(に)げ回(まわ)る大人(おとな)たちの慌(あわ)てぶりには、苦笑(くしょう)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)なかった</b>。
The sight of grown adults running about in a panic drew a wry smile I could not suppress.
</pre>

<pre>
生涯(しょうがい)を一(ひと)つの研究(けんきゅう)に捧(ささ)げたその姿勢(しせい)には、尊敬(そんけい)の念(ねん)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b>。
For a life given wholly to a single field of research, one can feel nothing but respect.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; write the plain version.** Its home is print and prepared text:
newspaper editorials, opinion columns, book and film reviews, official statements,
letters of condolence, the closing paragraph of a formal report. Spoken aloud it
belongs to a podium. Said across a table -
<code>この料理(りょうり)には感動(かんどう)を禁(きん)じ得(え)ません</code> -
it is comic. The everyday equivalents are
<code>泣(な)かずにはいられなかった</code>,
<code>どうしても腹(はら)が立(た)ってしまう</code>,
<code>本当(ほんとう)に驚(おどろ)きました</code>.

**It reports a feeling, not a verdict.** This is the distinction English
translations destroy. <code>怒(いか)りを禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</code> says
*anger rose in me and I could not stop it*;
<code>不誠実(ふせいじつ)極(きわ)まりない</code> says *this conduct is as
dishonest as conduct gets*. A columnist often writes both in the same paragraph,
one as the reaction and one as the ruling, and they are not substitutes for each
other.

The subject is the writer, or an unnamed "any decent person" that the writer
invites you to join - which is part of why the pattern is so useful in editorials:
it presents an opinion as an involuntary human reaction rather than as an
argument. In literary narration it can be used of a character whose mind the
narrator can see, but in ordinary writing about a third party you would use
怒(おこ)っていた or 涙(なみだ)を流(なが)していた instead.

Finally, the emotion has to be worth the register. Fatigue, hunger and mild
annoyance do not take it, and neither does everyday delight; the pattern implies
something serious enough that a composed adult was trying not to react.

## Contrast

### ～を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない vs ～ずにはいられない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same feeling, different plumbing and different register.
<code>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</code> takes a **noun** and is written formal;
<code>ずにはいられない</code> takes a **verb** and is ordinary Japanese you can
actually say.

</div>

<pre>
遺族(いぞく)の言葉(ことば)に、涙(なみだ)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)なかった</b>。
The words of the bereaved family brought tears I could not hold back.
</pre>

<pre>
遺族(いぞく)の言葉(ことば)を聞(き)いて、泣(な)か<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>。
Hearing the family speak, I could not help crying.
</pre>

Notice the swap: the noun 涙(なみだ) becomes the verb 泣(な)く. If you find
yourself wanting ❌泣(な)くを禁(きん)じ得(え)ない, that is the sign you
should be using <code>ずにはいられない</code> instead.

### ～を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない vs ～極(きわ)まりない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</code> is **emotional**: this
is what rose up in me. <code>極(きわ)まりない</code> is **evaluative**: this is
what the thing is. English "extremely" covers both and tells you nothing.

</div>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の報告書(ほうこくしょ)を読(よ)み、失望(しつぼう)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b>。
Reading the accident report, I cannot suppress a sense of dismay.
</pre>

<pre>
点検(てんけん)を怠(おこた)ったまま運行(うんこう)を続(つづ)けたのは無責任(むせきにん)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。
To have kept the service running with the inspections neglected was utterly irresponsible.
</pre>

The first sentence is about the writer; the second is about the operator. Only the
second is an accusation, which is why a careful writer chooses between them
deliberately.

### ～を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない vs ～てならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both say a feeling cannot be held down.
<code>てならない</code> attaches to an **adjective or verb** and describes a
feeling that keeps welling up over time; <code>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</code>
takes a **noun** and describes the reaction to one particular thing.

</div>

<pre>
試合(しあい)に出(で)られなかったことが残念(ざんねん)<b>でならない</b>。
I cannot stop feeling sorry that I was not able to play.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)の判断(はんだん)には疑問(ぎもん)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b>。
I cannot help questioning the decision taken at the venue.
</pre>

<code>てならない</code> is the softer and the more personal of the two, and it can
be said aloud without sounding like a leader column;
<code>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</code> always sounds like one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 泣(な)くこと<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b> → ✅ 涙(なみだ)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b>
  The slot wants the noun for the feeling itself, not a nominalised verb.

- ❌ このケーキの美味(おい)しさには感動(かんどう)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ません</b>。 → ✅ このケーキ、本当(ほんとう)に美味(おい)しくて感動(かんどう)しました。
  The pattern needs a subject weighty enough to justify editorial register.
  Applied to dessert it reads as parody.

- ❌ 喜(よろこ)び<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ない</b> → ✅ 嬉(うれ)しい限(かぎ)りです
  The feelings that fit are ones you were trying to hold in. Straightforward
  happiness is not one of them.

- ❌ 怒(いか)りを禁(きん)じ<b>得(え)た</b> → ✅ 怒(いか)りを禁(きん)じ<b>得(え)ない</b>
  There is no affirmative form; the expression lives only in the negative. Watch
  the reading too - it is きんじえない, and ❌きんじうない is a pure invention.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)は驚(おどろ)き<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)ませんでした</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は大変(たいへん)驚(おどろ)いていました。
  Outside literary narration the pattern reports the writer's own reaction.
  Attributing it to your boss reads as though you were inside his head.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a newspaper editorial or an opinion column - possibly in the closing paragraph

reaction of any reasonable reader, which is exactly what editorial writing wants.
It would not be said aloud in a meeting.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

危険(きけん)<b>極(きわ)まりない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの場面(ばめん)では<b>笑(わら)わずにはいられませんでした</b>。（written: 苦笑(くしょう)<b>を禁(きん)じ得(え)なかった</b>）

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)の対応(たいおう)には怒(いか)りを禁(きん)じ得(え)ない。
(b) 今回(こんかい)の対応(たいおう)は不誠実(ふせいじつ)極(きわ)まりない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the writer in the frame. It says the handling produced anger that the
writer could not suppress, and it invites the reader to share a human reaction
rather than to accept a claim. Strictly speaking it asserts nothing about the
handling at all.

(b) is a verdict on the handling itself: dishonest to the maximum degree. There is
no writer visible in it, and it can be argued with, whereas (a) cannot. Editorials
often run the two together - the ruling, then the feeling - because each does
something the other cannot.

</details>

# ～思いをする

**Meaning** · to go through something that leaves you feeling ～ - 嫌(いや)な思(おも)いをする is to have a thoroughly unpleasant time of it

The 思(おも)い here is not a thought but a **felt experience**. The pattern
describes what an episode was like from the inside for the person who lived
through it: not "I was embarrassed" as a state, but "I went through something
embarrassing". Its causative twin,
<code>思(おも)いをさせる</code>, is the standard way of apologising for having
put someone through it.

## Usage

An adjective describing the feeling goes in front, and 思(おも)い takes を with
する. い-adjectives attach in their plain form -
悔(くや)しい思(おも)いをする, 心細(こころぼそ)い思(おも)いをする -
and な-adjectives keep their な: 嫌(いや)な思(おも)いをする,
不安(ふあん)な思(おも)いをする. A short descriptive phrase can fill the same
slot, which is where the set expression
肩身(かたみ)の狭(せま)い思(おも)いをする, "to feel small in front of
others", comes from.

The past 思(おも)いをした is by far the commonest form, since the pattern
narrates something that has already happened. The causative
思(おも)いをさせる shifts the experience onto someone else and is almost
obligatory in apologies:
嫌(いや)な思(おも)いをさせてしまい、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません.
Passive and potential forms are not used with it.

The adjectives that occur are ones that describe how a situation felt to a person:
嫌(いや)な, つらい, 悲(かな)しい, 寂(さび)しい, 恥(は)ずかしい,
悔(くや)しい, 心細(こころぼそ)い, こわい, 痛(いた)い, 不安(ふあん)な,
不快(ふかい)な. Most are negative. The positive members are
楽(たの)しい思(おも)いをする and the idiom
いい思(おも)いをする, which does not mean "have a nice time" - see below.

## Examples

<pre>
初(はじ)めての海外(かいがい)出張(しゅっちょう)で、心細(こころぼそ)い<b>思(おも)いをした</b>。
On my first business trip abroad I felt completely out of my depth.
</pre>

<pre>
みんなの前(まえ)で大声(おおごえ)で注意(ちゅうい)され、恥(は)ずかしい<b>思(おも)いをした</b>。
Being told off loudly in front of everyone was mortifying.
</pre>

<pre>
このたびは嫌(いや)な<b>思(おも)いをさせて</b>しまい、誠(まこと)に申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません。
I am truly sorry to have put you through such an unpleasant experience.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りが経験者(けいけんしゃ)ばかりで、自分(じぶん)だけ肩身(かたみ)の狭(せま)い<b>思(おも)いをした</b>。
Everyone around me had done it before, and I was the only one feeling like an intruder.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The expression is **register-neutral** and extremely common. It works in
conversation, in essays, in customer emails and in formal apologies, and it does
not sound stiff in any of them. What it does carry is a viewpoint: the subject is
the person who *underwent* the experience, so it is a natural fit for stories about
being on the receiving end - of rudeness, of a mistake, of bad luck.

<code>思(おも)いをさせる</code> is worth drilling separately, because Japanese
apologies almost always take that shape. You do not apologise for the fact that
the customer was upset; you apologise for having caused them to go through
something unpleasant:
ご不便(ふべん)な思(おも)いをおかけしました is a stiffer variant of the same
move, and both are heard daily in service settings.

Keep <code>いい思(おも)いをする</code> at arm's length. It does not mean "have a
good time" - it means to do rather well out of something, usually at others'
expense, and it is said with a raised eyebrow.

<pre>
苦労(くろう)したのは現場(げんば)で、上(うえ)の人(ひと)たちだけが<b>いい思(おも)いをした</b>。
The people on the ground did the suffering, and only those at the top did well out of it.
</pre>

Finally, do not confuse the 思(おも)い of this pattern with the verb
思(おも)う. ～と思(おも)う reports an opinion;
～思(おも)いをする reports an experience. They share a kanji and nothing else.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 嫌(いや)<b>思(おも)いをした</b> → ✅ 嫌(いや)<b>な思(おも)いをした</b>
  嫌(いや) and 不安(ふあん) are な-adjectives and keep their な before
  思(おも)い.

- ❌ 悔(くや)しい思(おも)いを<b>思(おも)った</b>。 → ✅ 悔(くや)しい思(おも)いを<b>した</b>。
  The verb is する. 思(おも)いを思(おも)う is a double of the same word and is
  not Japanese.

- ❌ お客様(きゃくさま)に嫌(いや)な思(おも)いを<b>しました</b>。 → ✅ お客様(きゃくさま)に嫌(いや)な思(おも)いを<b>させてしまいました</b>。
  If the person who suffered is somebody else, the verb has to be causative.
  Without it the sentence says that you were the one having a bad time.

- ❌ 旅行(りょこう)で美味(おい)しい<b>思(おも)いをした</b>。 → ✅ 旅行(りょこう)で美味(おい)しいものをたくさん食(た)べた。
  The adjective must describe how an experience felt emotionally, not a property
  of a thing. Taste, price and size do not go in the slot.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)はみんなで<b>いい思(おも)いをしましょう</b>。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)はみんなで楽(たの)しみましょう。
  いい思(おも)いをする implies pocketing an advantage, so as an invitation it
  sounds like a proposal to profit at someone's expense.

## Practice

**1.** 終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)して知(し)らない町(まち)で一人(ひとり)になり、こわい____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

こわい<b>思(おも)いをした</b>

The sentence narrates an episode the speaker lived through, which is exactly what
<code>思(おも)いをする</code> is for, and the past form is the one that
almost always appears. こわかった alone would state the feeling;
こわい思(おも)いをした frames the whole night as the experience.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 説明(せつめい)が足(た)りず、参加者(さんかしゃ)の皆様(みなさま)に不安(ふあん)思(おも)いをしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 説明(せつめい)が足(た)りず、参加者(さんかしゃ)の皆様(みなさま)に<b>不安(ふあん)な思(おも)いをさせてしまいました</b>。
"Our explanation was inadequate, and we caused the participants anxiety."

Two repairs. 不安(ふあん) is a な-adjective and needs its な. And the people who
felt the anxiety were the participants, not the speaker, so the verb must be the
causative させる - the standard shape of a Japanese apology.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 去年(きょねん)は寂(さび)しかった。
(b) 去年(きょねん)は寂(さび)しい思(おも)いをした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a state: last year, the speaker felt lonely. It says nothing about why
or about what happened.

(b) frames last year as something the speaker was *put through*. The phrase
implies circumstances - a move, an absence, being left out - and invites the
listener to ask about them. It also leans slightly towards the speaker having been
on the receiving end of events rather than simply having a mood, which is why
思(おも)いをさせる works so naturally when someone else is responsible.

</details>

# ～でなくてなんだろう

**Meaning** · if this is not ～, then what is it? - a rhetorical question used to assert, with feeling, that something is exactly that

The sentence is a question in form only. By daring the reader to supply another
label, the writer asserts the label they have just given:
<code>奇跡(きせき)でなくてなんだろう</code> means "this is nothing other than a
miracle." It is a **written**
device - the closing line of a paragraph in an essay, a column, or a passage of
narration - and it needs an abstract, evaluative noun to work on.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (abstract) | noun + でなくてなんだろう | 奇跡(きせき) → 奇跡(きせき)でなくてなんだろう |
| With か | ～でなくてなんだろうか | 差別(さべつ)でなくてなんだろうか |
| More literary | ～でなくてなんであろう（か） | 幸(しあわ)せでなくてなんであろうか |
| Usual frame | これが～でなくてなんだろう | これが愛(あい)でなくてなんだろう |
| Never an adjective | ❌美(うつく)しいでなくてなんだろう | ✅美(うつく)しさでなくてなんだろう |

<div class="note">

**Note - which nouns work.** The slot takes a **label a reader could argue
about**: 愛(あい), 奇跡(きせき), 運命(うんめい), 勇気(ゆうき),
幸(しあわ)せ, 才能(さいのう), 差別(さべつ), 矛盾(むじゅん),
偽善(ぎぜん), 暴力(ぼうりょく), 無責任(むせきにん). The whole force of the
pattern is "you would struggle to call this anything else", so a noun with nothing
to dispute makes it collapse: ❌これが机(つくえ)でなくてなんだろう is a joke, not
a sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これが愛(あい)<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>。
If this is not love, what is?
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)ぶりに同(おな)じ町(まち)で再会(さいかい)した。運命(うんめい)<b>でなくてなんだろうか</b>。
They met again in the same town after ten years. What is that if not fate?
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ仕事(しごと)をして給料(きゅうりょう)が半分(はんぶん)なら、それは差別(さべつ)<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>。
If you do the same work for half the pay, what is that but discrimination?
</pre>

<pre>
助(たす)からないと言(い)われた人(ひと)が、自分(じぶん)の足(あし)で歩(ある)いて退院(たいいん)した。奇跡(きせき)<b>でなくてなんであろう</b>。
A man they had given up on walked out of the hospital on his own two feet. Call it anything but a miracle.
</pre>

<pre>
燃(も)える建物(たてもの)に飛(と)び込(こ)んで子(こ)どもを抱(だ)き上(あ)げた。これが勇気(ゆうき)<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>。
He ran into a burning building and carried a child out. If that is not courage, nothing is.
</pre>

<pre>
環境(かんきょう)を守(まも)ると言(い)いながら森(もり)を削(けず)り続(つづ)けるのは、矛盾(むじゅん)<b>でなくてなんだろうか</b>。
To go on clearing the forest while claiming to protect the environment - what is that if not a contradiction?
</pre>

<pre>
寄付(きふ)の額(がく)を自(みずか)ら大(おお)きく宣伝(せんでん)してみせるのは、偽善(ぎぜん)<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>。
Advertising the size of one's own donation is hypocrisy and nothing else.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)全員(ぜんいん)がそろって同(おな)じ食卓(しょくたく)を囲(かこ)む。平凡(へいぼん)だが、これが幸(しあわ)せ<b>でなくてなんであろうか</b>。
The whole family around one table. It is an ordinary thing, and if it is not happiness, what is?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; do not say it.** It lives in print: newspaper columns, personal
essays, literary criticism, the narrator's voice in a novel, occasionally a
speech written to be moving. Spoken in conversation it sounds as though the
speaker had begun reciting. The plain modern ways to make the same point are
<code>まさに奇跡(きせき)だ</code>,
<code>奇跡(きせき)としか言(い)いようがない</code> and
<code>本当(ほんとう)に奇跡(きせき)だと思(おも)う</code>, and those are what
you should produce.

Two forces are stacked in it. The rhetorical question shifts the burden onto the
reader - *you* try naming it something else - and the sentence-final だろう makes
the claim sound arrived at rather than declared. That combination is why it feels
emotional rather than argumentative, and why it works equally well for admiration
(奇跡(きせき), 勇気(ゆうき)) and for indignation
(差別(さべつ), 偽善(ぎぜん)).

<code>であろう</code> is the more literary of the two endings and dates the prose
slightly; it suits an essayist writing in a considered, old-fashioned voice.
Adding か -
<code>でなくてなんだろうか</code> - softens the sentence a little and makes it feel
more like a question genuinely put to the reader, though it is still not one that
expects an answer.

The pattern almost always comes **after** the evidence, as the last sentence of a
short paragraph. Placed first it has nothing to land on.

## Contrast

### ～でなくてなんだろう vs まさに～だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>まさに～だ</code> is the modern, sayable equivalent, and
it asserts directly. <code>でなくてなんだろう</code> stages the same assertion as a
challenge, and belongs on a page.

</div>

<pre>
三十年(さんじゅうねん)一度(いちど)も休(やす)まなかったという。これが<b>努力(どりょく)でなくてなんだろう</b>。
Thirty years without missing a single day. If that is not dedication, what is?
</pre>

<pre>
三十年(さんじゅうねん)休(やす)まずに続(つづ)けたなんて、<b>まさに努力(どりょく)の人(ひと)だ</b>。
Keeping it up for thirty years without a break - he really is a model of hard work.
</pre>

Same admiration; only the second can be said aloud to a colleague without
sounding like a broadcast.

### ～でなくてなんだろう vs ～としか言(い)いようがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "there is no other word for it".
<code>としか言(い)いようがない</code> is neutral, usable in speech and in
business writing; <code>でなくてなんだろう</code> is emotional and literary.

</div>

<pre>
この時期(じき)に値上(ねあ)げをするとは、無神経(むしんけい)<b>としか言(い)いようがない</b>。
Raising prices at a time like this can only be called insensitive.
</pre>

<pre>
この時期(じき)に値上(ねあ)げをするのは、無神経(むしんけい)<b>でなくてなんだろうか</b>。
To raise prices at a time like this - what is that if not insensitivity?
</pre>

The first would pass in a report or a meeting. The second reads as a columnist
inviting readers to be angry with them.

### ～でなくてなんだろう vs ～ではないだろうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these look alike and pull in opposite directions.
<code>ではないだろうか</code> is a **hedge**: "might it not be ～?"
<code>でなくてなんだろう</code> is the **strongest** assertion in the family.

</div>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の決定(けってい)は拙速(せっそく)<b>ではないだろうか</b>。
Was this decision not perhaps taken rather too hastily?
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)もなく決(き)めてしまうのは、独断(どくだん)<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>。
Deciding without a word of explanation - what is that if not high-handedness?
</pre>

The first offers the reader room to disagree and is common in careful writing and
in polite spoken argument. The second closes the door. Choosing the wrong one
turns a mild suggestion into an accusation, or an accusation into a shrug.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 美(うつく)しい<b>でなくてなんだろう</b> → ✅ 美(うつく)しさ<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>
  The slot needs a noun. Turn the adjective into a ～さ noun, or use
  <code>としか言(い)いようがない</code>, which takes adjectives freely.

- ❌ これが自転車(じてんしゃ)<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>。 → ✅ これが奇跡(きせき)<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>。
  The noun must be a judgement someone could contest. With a concrete object there
  is nothing to assert, and the sentence reads as a riddle.

- ❌ これが愛(あい)<b>でなくてなんですか</b>。 → ✅ これが愛(あい)<b>でなくてなんだろう</b>。
  The ending is fixed: だろう or であろう, optionally with か. The polite ですか
  turns it into a real question, which is precisely what it is not.

- ❌ （in a meeting）その進(すす)め方(かた)は独断(どくだん)でなくてなんだろう。 → ✅ （in a meeting）その進(すす)め方(かた)は独断的(どくだんてき)ではないでしょうか。
  Register. In a room full of people this lands as a speech; the hedged question
  is how the same objection is raised in Japanese business talk.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

At the end of a paragraph in an essay, a newspaper column or a piece of

speech. Read aloud in conversation it would sound like a recitation.

</details>

真面目(まじめ) is a な-adjective, and although it can be used as a noun the

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is written rhetoric. It asserts that the result was ability and dares the
reader to name anything else, and the だろう makes it sound like a conclusion the
writer has been driven to rather than a claim they are advancing. Put (b) into a
conversation and it will sound theatrical; put (a) into a stirring essay and it
will sound flat.

</details>

# ～てやまない

**Meaning** · never stop feeling it - a wish, a hope or an affection presented as unending. Formal and literary; it is written on the page far more often than it is said out loud.

The やまない is the negative of 止(や)む, "to cease", so the shape is literally
"and it does not stop". What stops being a plain statement of fact is the
register: <code>～てやまない</code> is the language of speeches, prefaces,
congratulatory letters and obituaries. It also attaches to a **very small set of
verbs** - wishing, praying, loving, respecting, hoping - and not to actions in
general.

## Formation

The verb goes into the て-form and やまない follows it directly. It is normally
written in kana; the kanji spelling 止(や)まない is possible but uncommon.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (emotion, う-verb) | て-form + やまない | 願(ねが)う → 願(ねが)ってやまない |
| Verb (emotion, する) | して + やまない | 尊敬(そんけい)する → 尊敬(そんけい)してやまない |
| Polite | ～てやみません | 祈(いの)る → 祈(いの)ってやみません |
| Past | ～てやまなかった | 愛(あい)する → 愛(あい)してやまなかった |
| Before a noun | ～てやまない + noun | 愛(あい)してやまない故郷(こきょう) |
| Not possible | ordinary action verb | ❌食(た)べてやまない |
| Not possible | negative before it | ❌願(ねが)わなくてやまない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verbs that really occur here can almost be listed:
願(ねが)う, 祈(いの)る, 愛(あい)する, 尊敬(そんけい)する,
期待(きたい)する, 支持(しじ)する, 求(もと)める. Anything outside that
band sounds like a mistake rather than a stylistic choice.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
両国(りょうこく)の友好(ゆうこう)を願(ねが)っ<b>てやまない</b>。
We wish, and will not stop wishing, for friendship between our two countries.
</pre>

<pre>
皆様(みなさま)のご健康(けんこう)を祈(いの)っ<b>てやみません</b>。
I hope sincerely that you will all stay well.
</pre>

<pre>
恩師(おんし)が愛(あい)し<b>てやまなかった</b>この町(まち)に、記念碑(きねんひ)が建(た)てられた。
A monument has been raised in this town, which my old teacher loved so deeply.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい世代(せだい)の活躍(かつやく)を期待(きたい)し<b>てやみません</b>。
We look to the new generation with the highest of hopes.
</pre>

<pre>
作者(さくしゃ)が愛(あい)し<b>てやまない</b>海辺(うみべ)の風景(ふうけい)が、全編(ぜんぺん)にわたって描(えが)かれている。
The seaside landscape the author loves so much runs through the whole book.
</pre>

<pre>
生涯(しょうがい)にわたって師(し)を尊敬(そんけい)し<b>てやまなかった</b>と、弟子(でし)は静(しず)かに語(かた)った。
His student said quietly that he had revered his master all his life.
</pre>

<pre>
貴社(きしゃ)のますますのご発展(はってん)を願(ねが)っ<b>てやみません</b>。
We wish your company continued and growing success.
</pre>

<pre>
多(おお)くの読者(どくしゃ)が支持(しじ)し<b>てやまない</b>この作品(さくひん)が、ついに映画(えいが)になる。
This much-loved book, which its readers champion without reserve, is finally being filmed.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not reach for it.** <code>～てやまない</code> belongs to
written and ceremonial Japanese: the closing line of a formal letter, a speech at
a retirement party, a eulogy, the blurb on a book jacket, a newspaper column. If
you say it to a friend about a film you like, you have quoted a book jacket at
them. The everyday way to say the same thing is
<code>心(こころ)から願(ねが)っています</code>,
<code>ずっと好(す)きです</code> or simply
<code>本当(ほんとう)に大好(だいす)きだ</code> - use one of those and reserve
<code>～てやまない</code> for reading.

Two live contexts are worth knowing. The first is the **fixed closing formula**:
<code>ご健康(けんこう)をお祈(いの)りしております</code> is the ordinary
version, and <code>～を祈(いの)ってやみません</code> is the raised, more
emotional one, used when the speaker wants the sentiment to sound heartfelt rather
than routine. The second is the phrase
<code>愛(あい)してやまない</code> before a noun, which has spread well beyond
formal prose into food writing, travel writing and advertising -
<code>地元(じもと)の人(ひと)が愛(あい)してやまない一杯(いっぱい)</code>.
That single collocation is common in light journalism; the rest of the pattern
is not.

The polite form is <code>～てやみません</code>, and in this pattern the polite
form is if anything **more** frequent than the plain one, because the situations
that call for it are formal ones. There is no casual contraction, because there is
no casual use.

## Contrast

### ～てやまない vs 心(こころ)から～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these say the same thing. <code>心(こころ)から</code> +
ordinary verb is what you should actually produce;
<code>～てやまない</code> is the ceremonial edition of it.

</div>

<pre>
ご成功(せいこう)を心(こころ)から<b>願(ねが)っています</b>。
I really hope it goes well for you.
</pre>

<pre>
ご成功(せいこう)を願(ねが)っ<b>てやみません</b>。
It is our sincere and lasting wish that you succeed.
</pre>

The first can be said to a colleague at the door. The second is read from a sheet
of paper at a podium, or printed at the bottom of a letter. Nothing in the meaning
separates them - only the setting, and the setting is the whole point.

### ～てやまない vs ～てならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～てやまない</code> is a feeling that **continues**, and
the speaker is content to have it. <code>～てならない</code> is a feeling that
**overwhelms**, and the speaker cannot push it away.

</div>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)の将来(しょうらい)が心配(しんぱい)<b>でならない</b>。
I can't stop worrying about my son's future.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)の幸(しあわ)せを願(ねが)っ<b>てやまない</b>。
I wish for my son's happiness, and always will.
</pre>

<code>ならない</code> takes anxiety, irritation, sadness, suspicion - things that
press on you. <code>やまない</code> takes hopes and affections. Swap them and both
sentences go strange: a wish is not something you struggle to suppress, and worry
is not something you would announce in a congratulatory speech.

### ～てやまない vs ～続(つづ)ける

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～続(つづ)ける</code> is neutral continuation and
works with any verb. <code>～てやまない</code> is not really about duration at
all; it is a way of saying the feeling is deep.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は三十年(さんじゅうねん)同(おな)じ絵(え)を描(か)き<b>続(つづ)けた</b>。
He went on painting the same picture for thirty years.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が愛(あい)し<b>てやまなかった</b>のは、その一枚(いちまい)の絵(え)だった。
The thing he loved above all else was that one painting.
</pre>

Only <code>続(つづ)ける</code> can carry a stretch of time
(三十年(さんじゅうねん)) and only it works on a physical action like
描(か)く. If you want to say someone kept doing something,
<code>～続(つづ)ける</code> is the pattern; <code>～てやまない</code> would be
both ungrammatical and pompous.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っ<b>てやまない</b>。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)り<b>続(つづ)けている</b>。
  Running is an action, not an emotion. The pattern only takes verbs of wishing,
  praying, loving, respecting and hoping.

- ❌ 願(ねが)い<b>てやまない</b> → ✅ 願(ねが)っ<b>てやまない</b>
  The base is the て-form, not the ます-stem.

- ❌ 平和(へいわ)を願(ねが)って止(と)まらない → ✅ 平和(へいわ)を願(ねが)っ<b>てやまない</b>
  The verb inside is 止(や)む, not 止(と)まる. Reading it as とまらない
  produces a different sentence ("cannot stop wishing", which is
  <code>願(ねが)わずにいられない</code>).

- ❌ このケーキ、愛(あい)し<b>てやまない</b>んだよね。 → ✅ このケーキ、本当(ほんとう)に大好(だいす)きなんだよね。
  Attaching a casual <code>んだよね</code> to a ceremonial phrase is the mismatch
  learners produce most. Say the plain thing in conversation.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this sentence? 故人(こじん)が愛(あい)し<b>てやまなかった</b>庭(にわ)は、今(いま)も手(て)入(い)れが続(つづ)いている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In writing - a memorial notice, a newspaper feature, a printed programme, or a
line of narration in a novel. The vocabulary
(故人(こじん)) and the pattern together mark it as prepared, respectful prose.
Nobody produces this sentence in conversation.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 皆様(みなさま)のご活躍(かつやく)を期待(きたい)し<b>てやみません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

皆様(みなさま)の活躍(かつやく)を心(こころ)から期待(きたい)しています。

<code>心(こころ)から</code> plus a plain polite verb carries the same warmth
without the podium. This is the substitution to reach for whenever you feel
tempted by the pattern.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 最近(さいきん)、夜(よる)の音(おと)が気(き)になっ（てやまない・てならない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

気(き)になっ<b>てならない</b>。

The sentence describes something that bothers the speaker and will not go away.
<code>ならない</code> takes that kind of pressing, unwanted feeling;
<code>やまない</code> takes hopes and affections, and would make the noise sound
like something cherished.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)は毎晩(まいばん)資料(しりょう)を読(よ)んでやまない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は毎晩(まいばん)資料(しりょう)を読(よ)み<b>続(つづ)けている</b>。
"The department head goes on reading documents every night."

Reading files is an action, so it needs
<code>～続(つづ)ける</code>. The pattern in the question also clashes with
毎晩(まいばん): <code>～てやまない</code> does not take an adverb of frequency,
because it is not describing repetition.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in register:
(a) ご家族(かぞく)の幸(しあわ)せを祈(いの)っています。
(b) ご家族(かぞく)の幸(しあわ)せを祈(いの)っ<b>てやみません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is ordinary polite Japanese. You could say it at the end of a phone call, write
it in a message, or add it to a card. It sounds warm and unremarkable.

(b) raises the same sentiment to formal written style. It fits the closing line of
a letter of condolence or congratulation, or a speech; delivered in casual
conversation it sounds theatrical, as though the speaker were reading from a
prepared text. The propositional content is identical - only the occasion differs.

</details>

# ～てみせる

**Meaning** · I will do it, and you will see me do it - a declaration of resolve made in front of an audience. The same shape also has a plainer sense: to do something in order to demonstrate it to someone.

Both senses come from the same idea: the action is performed **for someone to
see**. When the verb is in the past and the subject is a third person, that is
usually literal demonstration - the teacher wrote the character out so the class
could watch. When the verb is non-past and the subject is *I*, the seeing becomes
figurative and the sentence turns into a vow: doubt me if you like, I am going to
do this.

## Formation

<code>みせる</code> attaches to the て-form. It is a る-verb and conjugates
normally. In this construction it is almost always written in kana.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | て-form + みせる | 勝(か)つ → 勝(か)ってみせる |
| Verb (る-verb) | て-form + みせる | 続(つづ)ける → 続(つづ)けてみせる |
| Verb (する) | して + みせる | 成功(せいこう)する → 成功(せいこう)してみせる |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き)て + みせる | 戻(もど)ってくる → 戻(もど)ってきてみせる |
| Polite | ～てみせます | 合格(ごうかく)してみせます |
| Past (demonstration) | ～てみせた | 書(か)いてみせた |
| Common companions | 必(かなら)ず、絶対(ぜったい)に、今度(こんど)こそ | 必(かなら)ず勝(か)ってみせる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the resolve sense does not take a negative verb. To vow *not* to do
something, Japanese uses a different frame:
<code>二度(にど)とタバコは吸(す)わない</code>, or
<code>禁煙(きんえん)してみせる</code> stated positively. ❌<code>吸(す)わないでみせる</code>
is not Japanese.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今度(こんど)こそ勝(か)っ<b>てみせる</b>。
This time I'm going to win. Watch me.
</pre>

<pre>
必(かなら)ず合格(ごうかく)し<b>てみせます</b>。
I will pass. I promise you that.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)は正(ただ)しい発音(はつおん)をゆっくり言(い)っ<b>てみせた</b>。
The teacher said the correct pronunciation slowly for us to hear.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は折(お)り紙(がみ)で鶴(つる)を折(お)っ<b>てみせて</b>くれた。
My grandmother folded a paper crane to show me how.
</pre>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)で借金(しゃっきん)を返(かえ)し<b>てみせる</b>と、父(ちち)は言(い)い切(き)った。
My father declared flatly that he would clear the debt within a year.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもに泳(およ)ぎ方(かた)を教(おし)えるため、まず自分(じぶん)が浮(う)い<b>てみせた</b>。
To teach the children to swim, he floated in front of them first.
</pre>

<pre>
監督(かんとく)は記者(きしゃ)に、来季(らいき)こそ優勝(ゆうしょう)し<b>てみせます</b>と答(こた)えた。
The manager told reporters that next season they would take the title, no question.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)に笑(わら)われようと、祖父(そふ)が残(のこ)したこの店(みせ)は立(た)て直(なお)し<b>てみせる</b>。
Let people laugh - I am going to put my grandfather's shop back on its feet.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The resolve sense is **loud**. It says the speaker expects to be doubted and is
answering the doubt in advance, so it always implies an obstacle, a sceptical
audience, or a previous failure. That is why it lives where it does: sports
interviews, election speeches, curtain lines in drama, comic-book declarations,
and the sort of thing a person says to themselves at three in the morning. Between
friends it is fine, but it is not casual small talk - saying
<code>今夜(こんや)はカレーを作(つく)ってみせる</code> about dinner is a joke,
because there is nothing to prove.

Politeness works normally: <code>～てみせます</code> is perfectly usable in a press
conference or a formal pledge, and does not soften the force at all - if anything
the polite form in front of an audience sounds more like a commitment on the
record. The plain <code>～てみせる</code> in speech leans a little male and a
little dramatic, but it is not restricted, and in narration and quoted thought it
is neutral. A woman saying <code>絶対(ぜったい)に見返(みかえ)してみせる</code>
sounds exactly as determined as a man saying it.

The demonstration sense is register-neutral and very common, especially with
<code>～てみせてくれる</code> ("showed me how by doing it"). Keep the two apart by
looking at tense and person: past plus third person is nearly always
demonstration, non-past plus first person is nearly always resolve.

## Contrast

### ～てみせる vs ～てみる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～てみる</code> is *I'll try it and see what happens*.
<code>～てみせる</code> is *I'll do it and you will see*. One is tentative; the
other is the opposite of tentative.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいアプリを使(つか)っ<b>てみます</b>。
I'll give the new app a go.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいアプリで結果(けっか)を出(だ)し<b>てみせます</b>。
I'll get results with the new app - just wait.
</pre>

The single kana difference reverses the confidence level. This is the error that
matters most: a learner who means "let me try" and produces
<code>～てみせる</code> has announced a challenge to the room.

### ～てみせる vs ～つもりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>つもりだ</code> reports a plan.
<code>～てみせる</code> makes a promise **to someone**, with pride staked on it.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、留学(りゅうがく)する<b>つもりです</b>。
I'm planning to study abroad next year.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)こそ留学(りゅうがく)し<b>てみせます</b>。
Next year I'm getting on that plane, whatever it takes.
</pre>

<code>つもり</code> is information: you could say it to an immigration officer.
<code>～てみせる</code> is a stance, and it needs a reason to be defiant - a
rejection last year, a family that said no. Used with no such background it sounds
overheated.

### ～てみせる vs ～てやる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both can express defiance. <code>～てみせる</code> aims at
**proving something**; the defiant <code>～てやる</code> aims at **getting
even**, and is rougher.

</div>

<pre>
いつか見返(みかえ)し<b>てやる</b>。
One day I'll show them. (with an edge of spite)
</pre>

<pre>
いつか見返(みかえ)し<b>てみせる</b>。
One day I'll prove them wrong.
</pre>

<code>やる</code> in this use is blunt and can sound aggressive, and it has an
entirely separate everyday meaning of doing something for someone below you
(犬(いぬ)にえさをやる). <code>みせる</code> keeps the focus on the achievement
rather than on the other person's comeuppance, so it is the safer of the two in
mixed company.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 勝(か)る<b>てみせる</b> → ✅ 勝(か)っ<b>てみせる</b>
  <code>みせる</code> attaches to the て-form, never the dictionary form. (The verb
  is 勝(か)つ, so the て-form is 勝(か)って.)

- ❌ この料理(りょうり)を食(た)べ<b>てみせます</b>か。 → ✅ この料理(りょうり)を食(た)べ<b>てみます</b>。
  If you mean "I'll try it and see", the pattern is <code>～てみる</code>.
  <code>てみせる</code> would mean you were eating it to prove a point, and it does
  not form ordinary questions in the resolve sense.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は傘(かさ)を持(も)っ<b>てみせる</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は傘(かさ)を持(も)っていく。
  There is nothing to prove about carrying an umbrella. The pattern needs an
  obstacle or a doubter.

- ❌ タバコを吸(す)わない<b>でみせる</b>。 → ✅ 絶対(ぜったい)に禁煙(きんえん)し<b>てみせる</b>。
  The vow has to be stated as something you will do, not as something you will
  refrain from; the negative form does not attach.

## Practice

**1.** 今回(こんかい)は失敗(しっぱい)したが、次(つぎ)は必(かなら)ず成功(せいこう)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

成功(せいこう)<b>してみせる</b>（or してみせます）

The previous failure supplies exactly the background the pattern needs: the speaker
is answering an implied doubt. Note the する verb gives して, not ❌しって.

</details>

**2.** Choose: すみません、この靴(くつ)を履(は)い（てみて・てみせて）もいいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

履(は)い<b>てみて</b>もいいですか。

You are asking to try the shoes on. <code>てみせて</code> would be asking the shop
assistant to put them on and model them for you, which is a different request -
and with もいいですか attached it comes out as nonsense.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Everyone says it's impossible, but I'm going to finish it in a month."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

みんな無理(むり)だと言(い)うが、一(いっ)か月(げつ)で終(お)わらせ<b>てみせる</b>。

The English "I'm going to ... " against everyone's doubt is precisely the resolve
sense. Keep 終(お)わらせる (transitive, "bring to an end") rather than
終(お)わる, because the speaker is the one doing it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 母(はは)はケーキの作(つく)り方(かた)を説明(せつめい)しながら、卵(たまご)を割(わ)ってみせるくれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 母(はは)はケーキの作(つく)り方(かた)を説明(せつめい)しながら、卵(たまご)を割(わ)っ<b>てみせて</b>くれた。
"My mother explained how to make the cake, cracking an egg to show me."

To add くれる you need the て-form of みせる, which is みせて. This is the
demonstration sense, and <code>～てみせてくれる</code> is its most common shape.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 来年(らいねん)までにこの資格(しかく)を取(と)ろうと思(おも)っています。
(b) 来年(らいねん)までにこの資格(しかく)を取(と)っ<b>てみせます</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a stated intention, and a mild one. The speaker is telling you what is on
their plan; if it slips a year, nothing much has been broken.

(b) is a pledge with the speaker's credibility attached, made to a listener who is
assumed to have doubts - a boss who said the exam was too hard, a family that
suggested giving up. It sounds strong and slightly combative, and if the speaker
misses the deadline they will feel it. Use (a) unless you actually mean to stake
something.

</details>

# ～ともあろうものが

**Meaning** · someone who is supposed to be a X, of all people - said with indignation about a person or body whose position should have prevented exactly this. The criticism is never optional; it is built into the pattern.

The literal parts are <code>と</code> (quotative) + <code>も</code> +
<code>あろう</code> (the old volitional of ある) + <code>もの</code> - roughly
"a person who would even be called a X". What the phrase does in practice is set
up a gap: the title carries a standard, the behaviour fell below it, and the
speaker is disappointed. It is the language of editorials and of formal
reprimands, not of conversation.

## Formation

A noun naming a **position of standing or responsibility** goes in front. What
follows the が is always a negative judgement.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a role) | Noun + ともあろうものが | 大臣(だいじん) → 大臣(だいじん)ともあろうものが |
| Noun (respectful) | Noun + ともあろう方(かた)が | 専門家(せんもんか) → 専門家(せんもんか)ともあろう方(かた)が |
| Noun (kanji spelling) | Noun + ともあろう者(もの)が | 指導者(しどうしゃ)ともあろう者(もの)が |
| Noun (an organisation) | Noun + ともあろう + institution word + が | 大学(だいがく)ともあろう機関(きかん)が |
| Second half | a criticism, very often ～とは / ～なんて | ～とは情(なさ)けない、～とは何事(なにごと)か |
| Not possible | a noun with no status attached | ❌子(こ)どもともあろうものが |
| Not possible | praise after it | ❌～ともあろうものが、見事(みごと)な対応(たいおう)だった |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the が is the ordinary subject marker, not the "but" が. Replacing it
with は breaks the phrase: ❌<code>大臣(だいじん)ともあろうものは</code>. The
whole string <code>ともあろうものが</code> behaves like one frozen unit, and the
only part that really varies is the noun after ともあろう -
もの / 者(もの) / 人(ひと) / 方(かた) / 機関(きかん) / 会社(かいしゃ) / 国(くに).

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
警察官(けいさつかん)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、法(ほう)を破(やぶ)るとは。
A police officer, of all people, breaking the law.
</pre>

<pre>
大臣(だいじん)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、そんな発言(はつげん)をするとは情(なさ)けない。
For a cabinet minister to say a thing like that is simply pitiful.
</pre>

<pre>
新聞社(しんぶんしゃ)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、裏付(うらづ)けも取(と)らずに記事(きじ)を出(だ)してしまった。
A newspaper, of all organisations, ran the story without checking it.
</pre>

<pre>
師範(しはん)<b>ともあろう者(もの)が</b>、弟子(でし)の前(まえ)で取(と)り乱(みだ)すとは何事(なにごと)か。
A master of the art, losing his composure in front of his own students - what is he thinking?
</pre>

<pre>
一流(いちりゅう)メーカー<b>ともあろう会社(かいしゃ)が</b>、検査(けんさ)の数値(すうち)を書(か)き換(か)えていた。
A first-rank manufacturer had been altering its inspection figures.
</pre>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>ともあろう方(かた)が</b>、こんな初歩的(しょほてき)な見落(みお)としをされるとは驚(おどろ)きです。
That an expert of your standing should miss something this elementary is astonishing.
</pre>

<pre>
国(くに)を代表(だいひょう)する選手(せんしゅ)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、試合(しあい)の前日(ぜんじつ)に遅(おそ)くまで遊(あそ)んでいたという。
An athlete representing the country was reportedly out late the night before the match.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)を守(まも)るべき行政(ぎょうせい)機関(きかん)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、住民(じゅうみん)の通報(つうほう)を半年(はんとし)も放置(ほうち)していた。
A public body whose job is safety left residents' reports sitting for six months.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise it; write around it.** This is the voice of a newspaper editorial, a
formal complaint, a courtroom summing-up, or an angry senior person in a period
drama. It is not impossible in speech - an older speaker can produce
<code>先生(せんせい)ともあろう人(ひと)が</code> when genuinely appalled - but
in ordinary conversation it lands as pompous or theatrical. The everyday ways to
say the same thing are <code>大臣(だいじん)なのに</code>,
<code>大臣(だいじん)ほどの人(ひと)が</code>, or, in a franker register,
<code>仮(かり)にも大臣(だいじん)だろう</code>. Use one of those and keep
<code>ともあろうものが</code> for reading.

Three things it always carries. It **must** be followed by criticism; there is no
admiring use. The noun in front **must** carry status or responsibility - a
minister, a teacher, a doctor, a police force, a broadcaster, a national team. And
the speaker is positioned as an outsider passing judgement, which is why it is
awkward about oneself: for a blunder of your own, Japanese uses
<code>私(わたし)としたことが</code> instead.

The respectful variant <code>ともあろう方(かた)が</code> is worth noticing,
because it is not a softening. Wrapping a rebuke in honorific vocabulary makes it
colder and more formal, not gentler - it is the tone of a letter of protest to
someone senior.

## Contrast

### ～ともあろうものが vs ～なのに / ～ほどの人(ひと)が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>なのに</code> and <code>ほどの人(ひと)が</code> are
the modern, speakable versions. They say the same thing without the editorial page.

</div>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)<b>なのに</b>、自分(じぶん)の健康(けんこう)には無関心(むかんしん)なんだね。
He's a doctor, and yet he takes no interest in his own health.
</pre>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、患者(かんじゃ)の記録(きろく)を放置(ほうち)していたという。
A doctor - a doctor of all people - is said to have left patient records unattended.
</pre>

Both point at the same mismatch. The first is something you say to a friend; the
second is something a committee says in public. If you are speaking rather than
writing, use the first.

### ～ともあろうものが vs ～ともなると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ともなると</code> says *once you reach that position,
naturally this follows*. <code>ともあろうものが</code> says *given that position,
this should never have happened*.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)<b>ともなると</b>、会議(かいぎ)の数(かず)も一気(いっき)に増(ふ)える。
Once you make department head, the number of meetings shoots up.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、部下(ぶか)の名前(なまえ)を間違(まちが)えるとは。
For a department head to get his own staff's names wrong...
</pre>

<code>ともなると</code> is neutral, even admiring, and is followed by a
consequence. <code>ともあろうものが</code> is always followed by a failing. They
share a shape and share nothing else.

### ～ともあろうものが vs ～としたことが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ともあろうものが</code> points **outward** at someone
whose office they have disgraced. <code>としたことが</code> points **inward** at
the speaker's own uncharacteristic slip.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、大事(だいじ)な書類(しょるい)を忘(わす)れてしまいました。
How careless of me - I of all people have left the important papers behind.
</pre>

<pre>
責任者(せきにんしゃ)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、報告(ほうこく)を怠(おこた)っていた。
The person in charge, of all people, had been neglecting to report.
</pre>

The second difference is the weight of the fault.
<code>としたことが</code> covers a lapse - a forgotten umbrella, a slip of the
tongue - and is half an apology. <code>ともあろうものが</code> covers a breach of
the duty attached to a position, and is an accusation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さん<b>ともあろうものが</b>、すばらしい発表(はっぴょう)でした。 → ✅ さすが山田(やまだ)さんですね、すばらしい発表(はっぴょう)でした。
  The pattern cannot introduce praise. For "as one would expect of X", the word is
  さすが.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、宿題(しゅくだい)を忘(わす)れた。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)<b>ときたら</b>、また宿題(しゅくだい)を忘(わす)れたんだ。
  A younger brother carries no office to fall short of. The noun in front has to
  denote status or responsibility.

- ❌ 大臣(だいじん)ともあろうもの<b>は</b>、説明(せつめい)を避(さ)けた。 → ✅ 大臣(だいじん)ともあろうもの<b>が</b>、説明(せつめい)を避(さ)けた。
  The が is fixed. Swapping in は turns the phrase into a general statement about a
  class of people and the indignation disappears.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、寝坊(ねぼう)しました。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、寝坊(ねぼう)しました。
  Applied to yourself, the pattern claims your own eminence, which is either
  self-important or a joke. Use としたことが for your own mistakes.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence, and what tone does it carry? 公共(こうきょう)放送(ほうそう)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、事実(じじつ)の確認(かくにん)を怠(おこた)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In print - a newspaper editorial, a magazine column, a formal letter of complaint,
or the written findings of a review panel. The tone is cold public indignation: the
writer is holding an institution to the standard its own status implies. Spoken
aloud to a colleague it would sound like a performance.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 校長(こうちょう)先生(せんせい)<b>ともあろう方(かた)が</b>、約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れるとは。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

校長(こうちょう)先生(せんせい)<b>ほどの方(かた)が</b>約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れるなんて、意外(いがい)ですね。

<code>ほどの方(かた)が</code> keeps the respect and the surprise while dropping
the editorial register, and なんて carries the "of all people" flavour in
conversation. Adding 意外(いがい)ですね softens an accusation into a remark.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 社長(しゃちょう)（ともなると・ともあろうものが）、海外(かいがい)出張(しゅっちょう)も月(つき)に何度(なんど)もある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

社長(しゃちょう)<b>ともなると</b>

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 裁判官(さいばんかん)ともあろうものが、立派(りっぱ)な判断(はんだん)をした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ さすが裁判官(さいばんかん)だけあって、立派(りっぱ)な判断(はんだん)をした。
"As you would expect of a judge, it was a fine decision."

The original praises, and this pattern only ever introduces a fault. If you want
to say the behaviour matched the position, the frames are
<code>さすが</code> and <code>だけあって</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)としたことが、数字(すうじ)を間違(まちが)えてしまいました。
(b) 担当者(たんとうしゃ)ともあろうものが、数字(すうじ)を間違(まちが)えるとは。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker about the speaker. It admits a slip while implying it is out of
character, and it functions as part of an apology - the listener is expected to
say never mind.

(b) is one person judging another. It treats the same mistake as a failure of
professional duty and invites no forgiveness; it belongs in a written review or a
formal dressing-down. Note also that (b) needs a role noun, so
担当者(たんとうしゃ) rather than a personal name works best.

</details>

# ～とは比べものにならない

**Meaning** · there is no comparison - the two things are so far apart that putting them side by side is pointless. The 比(くら)べ is read くら.

Japanese has plenty of ordinary comparatives; this is not one of them. Saying
<code>AはBとは比(くら)べものにならない</code> refuses the comparison rather than
making it. The gap is not "bigger" or "better" but a different order of magnitude,
and the speaker is usually a little impressed - or, in the modest use, a little
embarrassed.

## Formation

A noun or noun phrase goes in front, followed by とは. The は is often dropped in
writing, giving <code>と比(くら)べものにならない</code>; the meaning does not
change.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + とは比(くら)べものにならない | 去年(きょねん) → 去年(きょねん)とは比(くら)べものにならない |
| Noun (は dropped) | Noun + と比(くら)べものにならない | 前(まえ)のモデルと比(くら)べものにならない |
| With a degree | ～とは比(くら)べものにならないほど + adjective | ～ならないほど静(しず)かだ |
| With a degree | ～とは比(くら)べものにならないくらい + adjective | ～ならないくらい安(やす)い |
| Polite | ～とは比(くら)べものになりません | プロとは比(くら)べものになりません |
| Past | ～とは比(くら)べものにならなかった | 想定(そうてい)とは比(くら)べものにならなかった |
| Before a noun | ～とは比(くら)べものにならない + noun | 比(くら)べものにならない差(さ) |
| Not possible | affirmative | ❌比(くら)べものになる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the もの is normally written in kana, though
比(くら)べ物(もの) appears too. There is no affirmative counterpart: the
expression exists only in the negative, so
<code>比(くら)べものになる</code> is not available for "they are comparable".
For that, say <code>同(おな)じくらいだ</code> or
<code>比(くら)べられる</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今年(ことし)の暑(あつ)さは去年(きょねん)<b>とは比(くら)べものにならない</b>。
This year's heat is nothing like last year's.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は前(まえ)のもの<b>とは比(くら)べものにならないほど</b>便利(べんり)だ。
This dictionary is more useful than the old one by a mile.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)の腕前(うでまえ)なんて、プロ<b>とは比(くら)べものになりません</b>。
My own ability is nowhere near a professional's.
</pre>

<pre>
田舎(いなか)の夜(よる)の静(しず)けさは、都会(とかい)<b>とは比(くら)べものにならない</b>。
The quiet of a country night is on another level from the city.
</pre>

<pre>
新型(しんがた)の処理(しょり)速度(そくど)は、旧型(きゅうがた)<b>と比(くら)べものにならない</b>。
The new model's processing speed leaves the old one standing.
</pre>

<pre>
地震(じしん)の被害(ひがい)は、想定(そうてい)していた規模(きぼ)<b>とは比(くら)べものにならなかった</b>。
The damage from the earthquake was on a scale no one had allowed for.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)から戻(もど)った妹(いもうと)の発音(はつおん)は、出(で)かける前(まえ)<b>とは比(くら)べものにならないほど</b>自然(しぜん)になっていた。
My sister came back from her year abroad pronouncing things incomparably more naturally than when she left.
</pre>

<pre>
人手(ひとで)が足(た)りない今(いま)の職場(しょくば)の忙(いそが)しさは、数年前(すうねんまえ)<b>とは比(くら)べものになりません</b>。
Short-staffed as we are, the workload here is not remotely what it was a few years ago.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This one is safe to use. It is **register-neutral to mildly formal**: it turns up
in conversation, in product reviews, in news copy and in company reports without
sounding out of place anywhere. The polite form
<code>比(くら)べものになりません</code> is what you want in a meeting or with
a customer; the plain form is ordinary among friends. There is no casual
contraction and no age or gender marking.

**Which side is bigger?** The pattern itself does not say, and learners are right
to find this uncomfortable. In practice the topic - the A of
<code>AはBとは…</code> - is the one being singled out, and context decides whether
it stands out upwards or downwards. Two reliable signals: if a
<code>ほど</code> or <code>くらい</code> phrase follows, the adjective there fixes
the direction outright
(比(くら)べものにならないほど速(はや)い); and if the topic is marked with a
modest なんて or the speaker is talking about their own ability, the sentence is
self-deprecating and A is the smaller one. When you produce it yourself, add the
<code>ほど</code> clause and the ambiguity disappears.

One thing it is not for: **small differences**. Because it denies that comparison
is meaningful at all, using it for a modest gap sounds like exaggeration.
<code>今日(きょう)は昨日(きのう)とは比(くら)べものにならないほど暖(あたた)かい</code>
is a claim about a jump of ten degrees, not two.

## Contrast

### ～とは比(くら)べものにならない vs ～より

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>より</code> places two things on one scale.
<code>比(くら)べものにならない</code> says they are not on the same scale.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいパソコンは前(まえ)の<b>より</b>速(はや)い。
The new computer is faster than the old one.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいパソコンは前(まえ)の<b>とは比(くら)べものにならないほど</b>速(はや)い。
The new computer is faster than the old one beyond all comparison.
</pre>

<code>より</code> is neutral and works for any size of gap, including a tiny one.
The longer phrase commits you to a large one, and it also carries a note of
surprise or admiration that <code>より</code> does not.

### ～とは比(くら)べものにならない vs ～にはかなわない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にはかなわない</code> always means *I lose*: the subject
is the weaker party. <code>比(くら)べものにならない</code> is neutral about
which side is which.

</div>

<pre>
将棋(しょうぎ)では祖父(そふ)<b>にはかなわない</b>。
I'm no match for my grandfather at shogi.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)の将棋(しょうぎ)の実力(じつりょく)は、私(わたし)<b>とは比(くら)べものにならない</b>。
My grandfather's shogi is in a different league from mine.
</pre>

<code>かなわない</code> needs a contest and a loser, so it is mostly used of people
and abilities. <code>比(くら)べものにならない</code> happily takes weather,
prices, damage, scenery - anything with a measurable scale.

### ～とは比(くら)べものにならない vs ～とは大違(おおちが)いだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>大違(おおちが)いだ</code> is about the difference
being **unexpected**, and often disappointing. <code>比(くら)べものにならない</code>
is about the difference being **enormous** on a single measure.

</div>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)で見(み)た部屋(へや)とは<b>大違(おおちが)いだ</b>。
It's nothing like the room in the photos.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)の広(ひろ)さは前(まえ)のアパート<b>とは比(くら)べものにならない</b>。
This room is vastly bigger than the old flat.
</pre>

<code>大違(おおちが)い</code> often carries a complaint - reality did not match
the description - and the difference can be qualitative, a different colour or a
different atmosphere. <code>比(くら)べものにならない</code> needs a dimension
you could put a number on.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)<b>を</b>比(くら)べものにならない → ✅ 去年(きょねん)<b>とは</b>比(くら)べものにならない
  The standard of comparison takes と, the same particle as
  <code>比(くら)べる</code> itself. を is never possible here.

- ❌ この二(ふた)つは比(くら)べものに<b>なる</b>。 → ✅ この二(ふた)つは同(おな)じくらいだ。
  The expression only exists in the negative. There is no affirmative version
  meaning "these are comparable".

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)より<b>比(くら)べものにならない</b>暖(あたた)かい。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)<b>とは比(くら)べものにならないほど</b>暖(あたた)かい。
  Two repairs: the particle is とは, not より, and an adjective needs
  <code>ほど</code> or <code>くらい</code> to hang off.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)の気温(きおん)は昨日(きのう)とは比(くら)べものにならない。（差(さ)は一度(いちど)） → ✅ 今日(きょう)は昨日(きのう)より少(すこ)し暖(あたた)かい。
  For a one-degree difference the phrase is absurdly oversized. Keep it for gaps
  that genuinely make comparison pointless.

## Practice

**1.** 今(いま)のスマートフォンのカメラは、十年前(じゅうねんまえ)のもの____ほど画質(がしつ)がいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

十年前(じゅうねんまえ)のもの<b>とは比(くら)べものにならない</b>ほど画質(がしつ)がいい

The gap between a phone camera now and one from ten years ago is exactly the
order-of-magnitude difference the pattern is for, and the
<code>ほど</code> clause pins the direction: the new one is better.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 料理(りょうり)の腕(うで)では、母(はは)（とは比(くら)べものになりません・にはかないません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

母(はは)<b>にはかないません</b>。

The speaker is naming themselves as the losing side in a contest of skill, which is
exactly what <code>かなわない</code> does. The other option cannot slot in as the
sentence stands: <code>とは比(くら)べものになりません</code> needs the two
things being set side by side to be named, so it would have to be rebuilt as
私(わたし)の腕(うで)は母(はは)とは比(くら)べものになりません.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The damage from this typhoon was on a completely different scale from last year's."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今回(こんかい)の台風(たいふう)の被害(ひがい)は、去年(きょねん)のもの<b>とは比(くら)べものにならなかった</b>。

"On a completely different scale" is the core sense of the pattern, so no extra
adverb is needed. Past tense, because the typhoon has been and gone; the polite
version would be <code>比(くら)べものになりませんでした</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しい工場(こうじょう)の生産量(せいさんりょう)は、古(ふる)い工場(こうじょう)と比(くら)べものになります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しい工場(こうじょう)の生産量(せいさんりょう)は、古(ふる)い工場(こうじょう)<b>とは比(くら)べものになりません</b>。
"The new plant's output is nothing like the old plant's."

The affirmative does not exist. If the intended meaning really was "the two are
similar", the sentence has to be rebuilt as
生産量(せいさんりょう)はどちらも同(おな)じくらいです.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 弟(おとうと)は私(わたし)より背(せ)が高(たか)い。
(b) 弟(おとうと)の背(せ)は私(わたし)<b>とは比(くら)べものにならない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat fact and says nothing about the size of the gap; it holds for one
centimetre and for thirty.

(b) insists the gap is so wide that comparing is silly, and it carries a tone -
amazement, or a joke at the speaker's own expense. It would be odd for a small
difference. Note also that (b) needs the topic to be the height
(背(せ)), because the two things being set side by side have to be the same
kind of thing.

</details>

# ～尽くす

**Meaning** · do it exhaustively - keep going until there is none of it left. Read 尽(つ)くす.

Attached to the stem of another verb, <code>尽(つ)くす</code> says the action
covered **all of it**: every snack, every square metre, every avenue, every last
scrap of strength. The focus is not on how long the doer kept going but on the
object being used up. This is the dignified, written-leaning end of the
"to the limit" family - the shouty colloquial end is
<code>～まくる</code>, and the two are almost never interchangeable.

## Formation

The stem is the ます-stem. <code>尽(つ)くす</code> is a う-verb and conjugates
as one.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + 尽(つ)くす | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)い尽(つ)くす |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + 尽(つ)くす | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べ尽(つ)くす |
| Past | ～尽(つ)くした | 語(かた)り尽(つ)くした |
| Passive | ～尽(つ)くされる | 埋(う)め尽(つ)くされる |
| Potential (usually negative) | ～尽(つ)くせない | 言(い)い尽(つ)くせない |
| Intransitive partner | 尽(つ)きる (not 尽(つ)くす) | 力(ちから)が尽(つ)きる |
| As a verb on its own | 尽(つ)くす = devote oneself | 家族(かぞく)に尽(つ)くす |
| Not possible | a verb with nothing to use up | ❌眠(ねむ)り尽(つ)くす |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is **semi-productive**, not free. It wants a verb whose object or
territory can be covered completely, and the compounds that really occur are a
recognisable set: 食(た)べ尽(つ)くす, 飲(の)み尽(つ)くす,
使(つか)い尽(つ)くす, 出(だ)し尽(つ)くす, やり尽(つ)くす,
語(かた)り尽(つ)くす, 言(い)い尽(つ)くす, 知(し)り尽(つ)くす,
調(しら)べ尽(つ)くす, 探(さが)し尽(つ)くす, 埋(う)め尽(つ)くす,
焼(や)き尽(つ)くす, 売(う)り尽(つ)くす, 味(あじ)わい尽(つ)くす.
Invent a new one and it will usually sound odd.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - three set phrases use the bare verb rather than the suffix, and they are
worth learning as units: 手(て)を尽(つ)くす ("try everything"),
最善(さいぜん)を尽(つ)くす ("do one's very best"),
力(ちから)を尽(つ)くす ("give it everything"). All three are common in formal
speech and in news writing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
お菓子(かし)は子(こ)どもたちが食(た)べ<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。
The children ate every last one of the sweets.
</pre>

<pre>
今月(こんげつ)の予算(よさん)を使(つか)い<b>尽(つ)くして</b>しまった。
We've gone through this month's budget down to the last yen.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は開演(かいえん)前(まえ)から観客(かんきゃく)で埋(う)め<b>尽(つ)くされて</b>いた。
The hall was packed solid with the audience before the curtain even went up.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)はこのあたりの山道(やまみち)を知(し)り<b>尽(つ)くしている</b>。
He knows every path in these hills.
</pre>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)は夜(よる)が明(あ)けるまで語(かた)り<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。
The two of them talked everything out until dawn.
</pre>

<pre>
一晩(ひとばん)の火事(かじ)が倉庫(そうこ)を焼(や)き<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。
A single night's fire burned the warehouse to the ground.
</pre>

<pre>
感謝(かんしゃ)の気持(きも)ちは、言葉(ことば)では言(い)い<b>尽(つ)くせません</b>。
Words cannot express everything I feel by way of thanks.
</pre>

<pre>
考(かんが)えられる手段(しゅだん)をすべて試(ため)し<b>尽(つ)くした</b>上(うえ)での判断(はんだん)だと、社長(しゃちょう)は説明(せつめい)した。
The president explained that the decision came only after every possible measure had been tried.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>尽(つ)くす</code> sounds **weighty and slightly literary**. It is at home
in news copy, formal statements, essays and narration, and it is what a speaker
reaches for when they want the exhaustiveness to sound serious - a search that left
nothing unchecked, a fire that left nothing standing, a conversation that left
nothing unsaid. Said about small everyday things it is faintly grand:
<code>冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)のプリンを食(た)べ尽(つ)くした</code> is funny
precisely because the register is too big for the pudding.

That said, several individual compounds have settled into ordinary use and carry no
special weight. <code>使(つか)い尽(つ)くす</code> and
<code>食(た)べ尽(つ)くす</code> are unremarkable in conversation, and
<code>売(う)り尽(つ)くし</code> is written on shop banners across the
country. Treat the *suffix* as formal and the *frozen compounds* as neutral.

There is no casual contraction, and no gender or age marking. What there is instead
is a register partner: when you want to say someone did something wildly and
repeatedly, in casual speech, the word is <code>まくる</code>, not
<code>尽(つ)くす</code>. Getting these two the wrong way round is the single
most noticeable error with either of them.

Finally, keep the intransitive apart. <code>尽(つ)きる</code> is what happens to
the thing that runs out - 力(ちから)が尽(つ)きる, 話(はなし)が尽(つ)きない,
燃(も)え尽(つ)きる. <code>尽(つ)くす</code> always needs someone doing the
using up.

## Contrast

### ～尽(つ)くす vs ～切(き)る

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～切(き)る</code> means the action was **carried
through to the end**. <code>～尽(つ)くす</code> means **nothing was left over**.
Often both are true, but they emphasise different halves.

</div>

<pre>
大(おお)きいケーキを一人(ひとり)で食(た)べ<b>切(き)った</b>。
I got through a whole big cake on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
棚(たな)のお菓子(かし)を食(た)べ<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。
They ate every last snack on the shelf.
</pre>

<code>切(き)る</code> looks at one task and reports that it was completed - it
fits a portion, a distance, a book. <code>尽(つ)くす</code> looks at a supply
and reports that it is gone. This is why
<code>走(はし)り切(き)る</code> ("run the whole course") is normal and
❌<code>走(はし)り尽(つ)くす</code> is not: a course is finished, not used up.

### ～尽(つ)くす vs ～抜(ぬ)く

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～抜(ぬ)く</code> is about the **doer's endurance**
through difficulty. <code>～尽(つ)くす</code> is about the **object being
exhausted**, and says nothing about how hard it was.

</div>

<pre>
苦(くる)しかったが、最後(さいご)までやり<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。
It was hard going, but I saw it through to the end.
</pre>

<pre>
試(ため)せる方法(ほうほう)はやり<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。
We have tried everything there was to try.
</pre>

Swap them and the sentences change subject. <code>やり抜(ぬ)いた</code> praises
the person; <code>やり尽(つ)くした</code> reports that the options are gone -
which is often said just before admitting defeat.

### ～尽(つ)くす vs ～まくる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same family, opposite ends of the register scale.
<code>尽(つ)くす</code> is written and dignified and counts what was used up;
<code>まくる</code> is loudly colloquial and counts how wildly the doer went at it.

</div>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)は寝(ね)<b>まくった</b>。
I slept like a log the whole long weekend.
</pre>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)の間(あいだ)に資料(しりょう)を読(よ)み<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。
Over the long weekend I got through every one of the documents.
</pre>

Sleeping uses nothing up, so <code>尽(つ)くす</code> cannot take it. Documents
can be finished off, so both would be grammatical with
読(よ)む - but <code>読(よ)みまくった</code> means "I read like mad" and
belongs in a message to a friend, while
<code>読(よ)み尽(つ)くした</code> means "there was nothing left unread" and
belongs in a report.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>尽(つ)くす</b> → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>尽(つ)くす</b>
  The base is the ます-stem, not the dictionary form - the same join as
  食(た)べ始(はじ)める or 食(た)べ終(お)わる.

- ❌ 力(ちから)が<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。 → ✅ 力(ちから)が<b>尽(つ)きた</b>。
  When the thing runs out by itself, the verb is the intransitive
  尽(つ)きる. <code>尽(つ)くす</code> needs an agent doing the exhausting.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)はカラオケで歌(うた)い<b>尽(つ)くした</b>よ。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)はカラオケで歌(うた)い<b>まくった</b>よ。
  A casual sentence with よ on the end wants the casual word. Singing a lot at
  karaoke is まくる territory; 尽(つ)くす would suggest the songbook was
  exhausted.

- ❌ 昼(ひる)まで眠(ねむ)り<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。 → ✅ 昼(ひる)まで寝(ね)<b>まくった</b>。
  Sleep has nothing to use up, so no exhaustive reading is available. The suffix is
  not free to attach to any verb you like.

## Practice

**1.** 警察(けいさつ)はあらゆる可能性(かのうせい)を調(しら)べ____が、手(て)がかりは見(み)つからなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

調(しら)べ<b>尽(つ)くした</b>

"Every possibility" is a supply that can be worked through completely, which is
what <code>尽(つ)くす</code> reports. It also matches the formal register of a
police investigation being described in writing.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 土曜日(どようび)はゲームをやり（尽(つ)くした・まくった）。楽(たの)しかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ゲームをやり<b>まくった</b>。

The sentence is chatty - short, plain, with a bare 楽(たの)しかった after it -
so the colloquial member of the pair fits. <code>やり尽(つ)くした</code> would
mean the games themselves had been finished off, every level and every mode, and
would sound oddly formal next to 楽(たの)しかった.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The fire burned the whole forest, leaving nothing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

火(ひ)は森(もり)を焼(や)き<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。

"Leaving nothing" is carried by <code>尽(つ)くす</code> itself, so there is no
need to translate it separately. 焼(や)き尽(つ)くす is one of the most
established compounds in the set and is standard in news reporting.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 三時間(さんじかん)話(はな)したので、もう話題(わだい)を尽(つ)くしてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 三時間(さんじかん)話(はな)したので、もう話題(わだい)が<b>尽(つ)きて</b>しまった。
"We talked for three hours, so we've run out of things to talk about."

Topics run out on their own; nobody is exhausting them deliberately. That calls for
the intransitive 尽(つ)きる with が, not 尽(つ)くす with を. The alternative
repair is 話題(わだい)を話(はな)し尽(つ)くしてしまった, with a real verb
stem in front.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 最後(さいご)まで走(はし)り<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。
(b) 持(も)てる力(ちから)を出(だ)し<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) praises the runner's persistence. The race was hard, and the point of the
sentence is that they did not stop - <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> always implies
difficulty pushed through.

(b) reports the state of the runner's reserves afterwards: there is nothing left in
the tank. It says nothing about whether the effort was difficult, only that it was
total. The two are often true of the same race, and a sports report may well use
both in consecutive sentences.

</details>

# ～まくる

**Meaning** · do it like crazy - over and over, in huge quantity, with no restraint at all. Loudly colloquial.

<code>まくる</code> is a live verb (捲(まく)る, "to roll up, to turn over") that
has become a suffix meaning *and then some*. Attached to a verb stem it reports
that the action was done to excess: bought and bought, ate and ate, talked and
would not stop. It carries energy and a hint of no self-control, and it belongs to
casual speech and casual writing only. In this use it is written in kana.

## Formation

The stem is the ます-stem. <code>まくる</code> is a **う-verb** - まくった,
まくって, まくらない - and learners regularly conjugate it as though it were a
る-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + まくる | 買(か)う → 買(か)いまくる |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + まくる | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べまくる |
| Verb (する) | し + まくる | 失敗(しっぱい)する → 失敗(しっぱい)しまくる |
| Past | ～まくった | 歌(うた)いまくった |
| て-form | ～まくって | 遊(あそ)びまくって |
| Negative | ～まくらない | 買(か)いまくらない |
| As a noun / predicate | ～まくり | 残業(ざんぎょう)する → 残業(ざんぎょう)しまくり |
| Not possible | uncontrollable states | ❌分(わ)かりまくる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb in front has to be a **deliberate action**. You can
走(はし)りまくる, 書(か)きまくる or 笑(わら)いまくる, but not
❌似(に)まくる or ❌見(み)えまくる, because nobody controls resembling or
being visible. Jokey exceptions circulate online; do not build a rule on them.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みは毎日(まいにち)泳(およ)ぎ<b>まくった</b>。
I swam my head off every day of the summer holiday.
</pre>

<pre>
セールで服(ふく)を買(か)い<b>まくって</b>しまった。
I went completely overboard buying clothes in the sale.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)前(まえ)は過去問(かこもん)を解(と)き<b>まくる</b>しかない。
Before the exam there's nothing for it but to hammer through past papers.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)は会議(かいぎ)でしゃべり<b>まくる</b>から、いつも時間(じかん)が足(た)りなくなる。
He talks non-stop in meetings, so we always run out of time.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)のころは失敗(しっぱい)し<b>まくって</b>、毎日(まいにち)謝(あやま)っていた。
When I was new I messed up constantly and spent every day apologising.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は好(す)きなバンドの曲(きょく)を一日中(いちにちじゅう)聞(き)き<b>まくって</b>いる。
My son has that band's songs on repeat all day long.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の夜(よる)はみんなでカラオケに行(い)って、終電(しゅうでん)まで歌(うた)い<b>まくった</b>。
Last night we all went to karaoke and sang our lungs out until the last train.
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)中(ちゅう)、妹(いもうと)は写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)り<b>まくって</b>いて、気(き)づいたら千枚(せんまい)を超(こ)えていた。
My sister snapped away non-stop on the trip, and before we knew it she was past a thousand photos.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **casual word and it sounds like one**. Its habitat is conversation
between friends, messages, social media, blogs, comedy, manga and variety
television. The polite form <code>～まくりました</code> exists and is grammatical,
but putting it in front of a superior is like saying "I smashed through the
paperwork" in a formal report - the politeness of ます does not cancel the
roughness of the word. In a work e-mail write
<code>何度(なんど)も確認(かくにん)しました</code> or
<code>集中的(しゅうちゅうてき)に進(すす)めました</code> instead.

The feeling it adds is **excess plus energy**. Very often the speaker is amused at
themselves - <code>買(か)いまくった</code> is half a confession - or is
complaining with a grin about someone else who would not stop. It is not an insult,
and it is not vulgar; it is simply loud. It is used freely across ages and genders
in casual settings, though it leans younger and more urban, and it can sound
try-hard from a speaker who is otherwise being formal.

Two smaller points. <code>まくる</code> and
<code>尽(つ)くす</code> both push an action to its limit, and they sit at
opposite ends of the register scale: if you want the dignified, written version of
"did all of it", the word is <code>尽(つ)くす</code>. And
<code>～まくり</code>, the stem form, is used on its own as a predicate or a
label - <code>今週(こんしゅう)は残業(ざんぎょう)しまくりだ</code>,
<code>勝(か)ちまくりのチーム</code> - which is even more colloquial than the
full verb.

## Contrast

### ～まくる vs ～尽(つ)くす

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>まくる</code> counts how **wildly the doer went at it**
and is casual. <code>尽(つ)くす</code> counts what was **used up** and is
formal and written.

</div>

<pre>
棚(たな)のマンガを読(よ)み<b>まくった</b>。
I plowed through the manga on that shelf.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)は一週間(いっしゅうかん)で調(しら)べ<b>尽(つ)くした</b>。
The materials were examined exhaustively within the week.
</pre>

The first could be said to a friend; the second could be written in a report.
Reversing them is jarring in both directions - a report does not say
調(しら)べまくりました, and a chat about comics does not say
読(よ)み尽(つ)くした unless the speaker is being grand on purpose.

### ～まくる vs ～続(つづ)ける

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～続(つづ)ける</code> is neutral: the action went on.
<code>まくる</code> adds volume, disorder and an opinion about it.

</div>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)歩(ある)き<b>続(つづ)けた</b>。
I kept walking for three hours.
</pre>

<pre>
旅先(たびさき)で歩(ある)き<b>まくった</b>ので、足(あし)が痛(いた)い。
I walked myself into the ground on that trip and now my feet hurt.
</pre>

<code>続(つづ)ける</code> takes a measured duration comfortably;
<code>まくる</code> resists it, because it is not about a span of time but about
going at something without restraint. <code>続(つづ)ける</code> is also
register-neutral and safe at work, which <code>まくる</code> is not.

### ～まくる vs ～てばかりいる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both say "does nothing but". <code>てばかりいる</code>
**disapproves**; <code>まくる</code> is usually **impressed or amused**.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はゲームをし<b>てばかりいる</b>。
My brother does nothing but play games. (and he should stop)
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はゲームをやり<b>まくって</b>いる。
My brother is gaming like a maniac.
</pre>

<code>てばかりいる</code> carries the sense that something else is being neglected,
so it is the natural choice for a parent's complaint. <code>まくる</code> just
reports the intensity; a speaker can use it about themselves with pleasure, which
<code>てばかりいる</code> cannot do.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>まくる</b> → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>まくる</b>
  <code>まくる</code> joins to the ます-stem, like
  食(た)べ始(はじ)める.

- ❌ 遊(あそ)び<b>まくない</b> → ✅ 遊(あそ)び<b>まくらない</b>
  <code>まくる</code> is a う-verb. Its negative is まくらない, its past is
  まくった, its て-form is まくって.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、資料(しりょう)を読(よ)み<b>まくりました</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、資料(しりょう)にはひととおり目(め)を通(とお)しました。
  The ます ending does not make the word polite. Reporting to a superior needs
  neutral vocabulary.

- ❌ この説明(せつめい)は分(わ)かり<b>まくる</b>。 → ✅ この説明(せつめい)はとても分(わ)かりやすい。
  Understanding is not an action you perform repeatedly at will, so the suffix has
  nothing to attach to.

## Practice

**1.** 給料日(きゅうりょうび)のあと、ついネットで買(か)い物(もの)を____しまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

買(か)い物(もの)を<b>しまくって</b>しまった

<code>する</code> becomes し before まくる, giving しまくる, and the て-form
しまくって joins to しまう for the note of regret. The whole sentence is casual,
which suits the word.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来週(らいしゅう)の報告書(ほうこくしょ)には、「各社(かくしゃ)の事例(じれい)を（調(しら)べまくりました・調(しら)べ尽(つ)くしました）」と書(か)く。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

調(しら)べ<b>尽(つ)くしました</b>

A written report needs the formal member of the pair.
<code>調(しら)べまくりました</code> is grammatical but reads as if the writer
forgot which document they were in - the ます ending does not rescue a colloquial
verb.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She ate non-stop at the party and regretted it the next day."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)はパーティーで食(た)べ<b>まくって</b>、次(つぎ)の日(ひ)に後悔(こうかい)した。

"Non-stop" with a note of excess and a rueful punchline is exactly what
<code>まくる</code> is for. The て-form links the two clauses; the register stays
casual throughout, which matches the situation.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)のライブで、ファンは叫(さけ)ぶまくっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)のライブで、ファンは叫(さけ)び<b>まくって</b>いた。
"At last night's gig the fans were screaming their heads off."

The join is to the ます-stem 叫(さけ)び, not the dictionary form
叫(さけ)ぶ. This is the most common error with the pattern, because the
suffix looks like an independent verb.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)は本(ほん)を読(よ)み<b>まくった</b>。
(b) 去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)は本(ほん)を読(よ)み<b>続(つづ)けた</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is casual and enthusiastic: a big, disorderly pile of books, read at speed, and
the speaker is rather pleased about it. You would say this to a friend.

(b) is a flat statement that the reading went on. It could describe a calm, steady
habit, and it fits a written account or a conversation with someone you are not
close to. It also allows a stated duration
(三時間(さんじかん)読(よ)み続(つづ)けた) in a way that (a) does not.

</details>

# ことこの上ない・この上ない

**Meaning** · there is nothing above this - utterly, supremely, in the extreme. Read この上(うえ)ない. Literary and ceremonial; you will meet it in print far more often than in speech.

The phrase says literally "there is nothing on top of this". Two shapes come out of
it. <code>この上(うえ)ない</code> in front of a noun, and its adverb
<code>この上(うえ)なく</code>, are the language of formal greetings and award
speeches: 光栄(こうえい), 喜(よろこ)び, 幸(しあわ)せ.
<code>～ことこの上(うえ)ない</code> after an adjective is a written predicate,
and it usually complains: 面倒(めんどう), 迷惑(めいわく),
危険(きけん).

## Formation

The predicate form takes an adjective, never a plain verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | plain form + ことこの上(うえ)ない | 嬉(うれ)しい → 嬉(うれ)しいことこの上(うえ)ない |
| な-adjective | ～な + ことこの上(うえ)ない | 面倒(めんどう)だ → 面倒(めんどう)なことこの上(うえ)ない |
| Noun (evaluative) | Noun + この上(うえ)ない | 失礼(しつれい) → 失礼(しつれい)この上(うえ)ない |
| Before a noun | この上(うえ)ない + noun | この上(うえ)ない光栄(こうえい) |
| Adverbially | この上(うえ)なく + adjective | この上(うえ)なく幸(しあわ)せだ |
| Polite | ～この上(うえ)ありません / ございません | 光栄(こうえい)この上(うえ)ございません |
| Not possible | plain verb + ことこの上(うえ)ない | ❌走(はし)ることこの上(うえ)ない |
| Not possible | この上(うえ)なく + noun | ❌この上(うえ)なく喜(よろこ)び |

<div class="note">

**Note** - keep the two endings apart. <code>ない</code> is the adjective form and
goes before a noun (この上(うえ)ない喜(よろこ)び);
<code>なく</code> is the adverb and goes before another adjective or a verb
(この上(うえ)なく嬉(うれ)しい). Its nearest relative is
極(きわ)まりない, treated in its own entry; the two overlap heavily in the
complaining use.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この<b>上(うえ)ない</b>喜(よろこ)びです。
It is a joy beyond anything.
</pre>

<pre>
手続(てつづ)きが面倒(めんどう)な<b>ことこの上(うえ)ない</b>。
The paperwork is a nuisance in the extreme.
</pre>

<pre>
皆様(みなさま)にお会(あ)いできて、<b>この上(うえ)なく</b>嬉(うれ)しく思(おも)います。
It gives me the greatest possible pleasure to meet you all.
</pre>

<pre>
受賞(じゅしょう)の知(し)らせをいただき、光栄(こうえい)<b>この上(うえ)ございません</b>。
Receiving word of the award is an honour beyond words.
</pre>

<pre>
案内(あんない)表示(ひょうじ)が一(ひと)つもない駅(えき)は、不便(ふべん)な<b>ことこの上(うえ)ない</b>。
A station without a single sign anywhere is inconvenient beyond belief.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)もなく予定(よてい)を変(か)えられては、迷惑(めいわく)な<b>ことこの上(うえ)ない</b>。
Having the schedule changed on us with no explanation is a thorough nuisance.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かな湖(みずうみ)のほとりで過(す)ごした一週間(いっしゅうかん)は、<b>この上(うえ)なく</b>穏(おだ)やかな時間(じかん)だった。
The week beside that quiet lake was as peaceful a stretch of time as I have known.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)の研究(けんきゅう)が認(みと)められたことは、研究者(けんきゅうしゃ)にとって<b>この上(うえ)ない</b>名誉(めいよ)である。
For a researcher, having a lifetime's work recognised is the highest honour there is.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; use something plainer.** The natural homes of the phrase are a
printed greeting, an acceptance speech, a company announcement, a piece of literary
narration, and - for the ことこの上(うえ)ない half - a written column grumbling
about something. It is not impossible in speech, but only in prepared speech. In
conversation the plain equivalents are <code>最高(さいこう)に嬉(うれ)しい</code>,
<code>本当(ほんとう)に面倒(めんどう)だ</code>, and, if you want a fixed polite
formula, <code>大変(たいへん)光栄(こうえい)です</code>. Say one of those.

The two halves lean in **opposite emotional directions**, and this is the most
useful thing to notice about the phrase. <code>この上(うえ)ない</code> before a
noun overwhelmingly attracts good things - 光栄(こうえい),
喜(よろこ)び, 名誉(めいよ), 幸(しあわ)せ - and sounds gracious.
<code>～ことこの上(うえ)ない</code> after an adjective attracts bad ones -
面倒(めんどう), 迷惑(めいわく), 危険(きけん), 不便(ふべん) - and
sounds exasperated, often with a dry, half-amused edge, which is why it turns up in
essays and opinion columns. Neither is a hard rule
(嬉(うれ)しいことこの上(うえ)ない is perfectly good), but the tendency is
strong enough to rely on when you are reading.

The polite forms are worth recognising because the fixed greetings use them:
<code>光栄(こうえい)この上(うえ)ございません</code> and
<code>この上(うえ)ない光栄(こうえい)に存(ぞん)じます</code> are stock
lines at ceremonies. Treat them as phrases to identify, not as building blocks.

## Contrast

### ことこの上(うえ)ない vs 本当(ほんとう)に・最高(さいこう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the ordinary intensifiers say the same thing and can be spoken
aloud. Use them; keep this pattern for reading.

</div>

<pre>
この手続(てつづ)き、<b>本当(ほんとう)に</b>面倒(めんどう)だね。
This paperwork is a real pain, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
この手続(てつづ)きは面倒(めんどう)な<b>ことこの上(うえ)ない</b>。
This procedure is a nuisance in the extreme.
</pre>

The first is a remark to a colleague at the next desk. The second is a sentence in
a column about bureaucracy. Nothing in the content separates them - only the
occasion, and using the second aloud makes the speaker sound as though they are
reading from an editorial.

### この上(うえ)ない vs これ以上(いじょう)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>これ以上(いじょう)ない</code> is the everyday cousin.
Same logic ("nothing beyond this"), ordinary register, and it can be spoken.

</div>

<pre>
これ<b>以上(いじょう)ない</b>ほどいい天気(てんき)だった。
The weather could not have been better.
</pre>

<pre>
旅(たび)の最終日(さいしゅうび)は<b>この上(うえ)なく</b>晴(は)れやかだった。
The last day of the journey was as bright as any could be.
</pre>

<code>これ以上(いじょう)ない</code> normally takes ほど or くらい and behaves
like a normal comparative; <code>この上(うえ)ない</code> attaches straight to a
noun and lifts the register. If you are speaking, the first is the safe one.

### ことこの上(うえ)ない vs ～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>てたまらない</code> reports a feeling the **speaker**
cannot bear. <code>ことこの上(うえ)ない</code> is a **judgement about a
situation**, delivered from a step back.

</div>

<pre>
待(ま)たされて、腹(はら)が立(た)っ<b>てたまらない</b>。
Being kept waiting, I'm furious.
</pre>

<pre>
一時間(いちじかん)も待(ま)たされるとは、不愉快(ふゆかい)な<b>ことこの上(うえ)ない</b>。
To be kept waiting a full hour is disagreeable in the extreme.
</pre>

<code>てたまらない</code> is first-person and immediate, and it is ordinary spoken
Japanese. <code>ことこの上(うえ)ない</code> stands outside the feeling and rates
the situation, which is why it can describe a train station or a set of rules -
things that have no feelings of their own.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 待(ま)つことこの上(うえ)ない。 → ✅ 待(ま)ち時間(じかん)が長(なが)いことこの上(うえ)ない。
  The predicate form needs an adjective in front, not a plain verb. Rebuild the
  clause so that an adjective is doing the work.

- ❌ 嬉(うれ)しいなことこの上(うえ)ない → ✅ 嬉(うれ)しいことこの上(うえ)ない
  い-adjectives join directly. The な belongs only to な-adjectives and nouns
  (面倒(めんどう)なことこの上(うえ)ない).

- ❌ この上(うえ)なく喜(よろこ)びです。 → ✅ この上(うえ)ない喜(よろこ)びです。
  喜(よろこ)び is a noun, so it needs the adjectival <code>ない</code>.
  <code>なく</code> is the adverb and requires an adjective or a verb after it.

- ❌ このラーメン、この上(うえ)なくおいしい! → ✅ このラーメン、めちゃくちゃおいしい!
  A ceremonial phrase in a casual sentence with an exclamation mark is comic. Match
  the register: casual food talk takes casual intensifiers.

In writing - an opinion column, a letter of complaint, an essay, a blog post

</details>

# ～としたことが

**Meaning** · I, of all people - dismay that someone who normally would never make this mistake has just made it. In practice the phrase is frozen into 私(わたし)としたことが.

The parts do not add up to anything useful; treat the whole string as one word. What
it does is set up a contrast between a person's usual competence and the blunder
they have just committed, and it is almost always the speaker talking about
themselves. Because it quietly claims *I am normally better than this*, it is a
phrase with a distinct flavour - theatrical, a little old-fashioned, and slightly
self-regarding.

## Formation

A person goes in front, followed directly by としたことが, and then the blunder.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 私(わたし) (by far the commonest) | 私(わたし)としたことが | 私(わたし)としたことが、うっかりしていました |
| Other first-person words | 僕(ぼく) / おれ / わたくし + としたことが | 僕(ぼく)としたことが |
| Second person (reproach) | 君(きみ) / あなた + としたことが | 君(きみ)としたことが |
| Third person (narration) | 彼(かれ) / 彼女(かのじょ) / a name + としたことが | 彼女(かのじょ)としたことが |
| What follows | the mistake, usually with ～てしまう / ～とは / ～なんて | 忘(わす)れてしまいました、間違(まちが)えるとは |
| Not possible | a non-human subject | ❌この機械(きかい)としたことが |
| Not possible | a good outcome after it | ❌私(わたし)としたことが、うまくいきました |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is a **set phrase, not a productive pattern**. You cannot swap in
other tenses or particles: ❌<code>私(わたし)とすることが</code>,
❌<code>私(わたし)としたことは</code>, ❌<code>私(わたし)としたことも</code>
are all wrong. The only slot that varies is the person in front, and even that slot
is filled by 私(わたし) the great majority of the time.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、うっかりしていました。
How careless of me - I really should have known better.
</pre>

<pre>
僕(ぼく)<b>としたことが</b>、大事(だいじ)な約束(やくそく)をすっかり忘(わす)れていた。
Me of all people, and I'd completely forgotten an important promise.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、お名前(なまえ)を間違(まちが)えてしまい、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません。
I of all people got your name wrong. I am very sorry.
</pre>

<pre>
君(きみ)<b>としたことが</b>、こんな単純(たんじゅん)な計算(けいさん)を間違(まちが)えるとはね。
You of all people, slipping up on a simple sum like that.
</pre>

<pre>
わたくし<b>としたことが</b>、傘(かさ)を店(みせ)に置(お)き忘(わす)れてしまいました。
I am ashamed to say I left my umbrella behind in the shop.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)<b>としたことが</b>、締(し)め切(き)りを一日(いちにち)勘違(かんちが)いしていたらしい。
She, of all people, seems to have had the deadline a day out.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)を二度(にど)も繰(く)り返(かえ)すとは、恥(は)ずかしくてなりません。
For me to make the same mistake twice - I can hardly bear the embarrassment.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)この道(みち)でやってきた私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、基本(きほん)の確認(かくにん)を怠(おこた)ってしまいました。
After all my years in this trade, I of all people let the basic checks slide.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise it before you use it.** In modern Japanese this phrase lives mainly in
fiction: novels, television drama, manga, the line a capable character delivers when
they have been caught out. In real speech it does occur - from older speakers, and
in formal spoken apologies where the speaker wants to signal that the lapse is out
of character - but from a young speaker in a casual setting it sounds like a
performance. The plain ways to say the same thing are
<code>うっかりしていました</code>,
<code>私(わたし)らしくないミスをしてしまいました</code>, and
<code>いつもならこんなことはないのですが</code>. Reach for one of those.

Two things to keep in mind if you ever do use it. First, it **claims competence**.
The whole force of the phrase is "this is not like me", so it fits a small,
uncharacteristic slip - a forgotten umbrella, a misread date, a name got wrong. Put
it in front of a serious failure and it sounds evasive, as though the speaker were
more concerned with their reputation than with the harm done. Second, in the second
person it is not really scolding: <code>君(きみ)としたことが</code> expresses
surprise, and often something close to sympathy, from someone who thinks well of
the listener. A genuine reprimand uses different words.

The phrase is normally followed by <code>～てしまう</code>, or by a
<code>～とは</code> / <code>～なんて</code> clause that trails off without a main
verb - <code>私(わたし)としたことが、なんという失敗(しっぱい)を</code>. That
unfinished shape is part of its dramatic feel and is one of the easiest ways to
spot it on the page.

## Contrast

### ～としたことが vs うっかり～てしまいました

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>うっかり</code> plus an ordinary apology is the modern,
speakable version. It admits the slip without also announcing how competent you
usually are.

</div>

<pre>
すみません、<b>うっかり</b>資料(しりょう)を持(も)ってくるのを忘(わす)れました。
Sorry - I completely forgot to bring the handout.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、資料(しりょう)を持(も)ってくるのを忘(わす)れました。
I of all people forgot to bring the handout.
</pre>

Both apologise. The first is what a colleague says; the second adds a layer of
self-characterisation that, from a junior person, can read as pompous. If you want
the "not like me" note without the theatre, say
<code>私(わたし)らしくないミスをしました</code>.

### ～としたことが vs ～ともあろうものが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>としたことが</code> points **inward** at a lapse of your
own. <code>ともあろうものが</code> points **outward** at someone who has disgraced
a position of responsibility.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、時間(じかん)を勘違(かんちが)いしておりました。
I of all people had the time wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
責任者(せきにんしゃ)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、時間(じかん)を守(まも)らないとは。
For the person in charge to be late, of all people...
</pre>

The second difference is the weight of the fault.
<code>としたことが</code> covers a slip and is half an apology;
<code>ともあろうものが</code> covers a breach of duty and is an accusation. The
noun in front differs too - a personal pronoun for the first, a role or an
institution for the second.

### ～としたことが vs ～としては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they share として and nothing else.
<code>としては</code> means *in my capacity as* or *for my part*, and is everyday
Japanese. <code>としたことが</code> is the frozen self-reproach.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としては</b>、この案(あん)に賛成(さんせい)です。
Speaking for myself, I'm in favour of this proposal.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、この案(あん)の数字(すうじ)を見落(みお)としていました。
I of all people missed the figures in this proposal.
</pre>

A third member of the family is worth adding, because learners mix it in:
<code>私(わたし)にしては</code> means "for me", *by my standards*, and it works
in both directions -
<code>私(わたし)にしてはうまく書(か)けた</code>, "that came out well, for me".
Only <code>としたことが</code> is restricted to failure.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>としては</b>、鍵(かぎ)を忘(わす)れてしまいました。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、鍵(かぎ)を忘(わす)れてしまいました。
  <code>としては</code> means "for my part" and cannot express the "of all people"
  sense. The two look alike and mean completely different things.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、今回(こんかい)はうまくいきました。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>にしては</b>、うまくいきました。
  The phrase only introduces a failure. For "that went well, considering it was me",
  the frame is <code>にしては</code>.

- ❌ この機械(きかい)<b>としたことが</b>、止(と)まってしまった。 → ✅ この機械(きかい)が止(と)まるなんて珍(めずら)しい。
  Only a person can be held to a standard of character. A machine takes a plain
  expression of surprise.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>としたことは</b>、遅刻(ちこく)しました。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>としたことが</b>、遅刻(ちこく)してしまいました。
  The が is fixed; the phrase does not take は or も. It is a set expression and
  cannot be taken apart.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this line, and who is likely to be speaking? 「私(わたし)としたことが、なんという失態(しったい)を。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In fiction - a novel, a drama script, a manga panel - and most likely from a
character established as capable and rather proud of it. The unfinished ending
(なんという失態(しったい)を, with the verb left off) and the formal word
失態(しったい) both mark it as written dialogue rather than everyday speech.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 私(わたし)としたことが、会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)を間違(まちが)えておりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません、うっかり会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)を間違(まちが)えていました。

<code>うっかり</code> carries the "careless slip" part, and a plain すみません does
the apologising. This is what you would actually say to a colleague; the original
would sound as if you were on stage.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 今回(こんかい)のテストは（私(わたし)としたことが・私(わたし)にしては）よくできたほうだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>私(わたし)にしては</b>よくできたほうだ。

The result was good, and <code>としたことが</code> can only introduce a failure.
<code>にしては</code> means "by my standards", and it happily reports a pleasant
surprise as well as a disappointing one.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)としたことが、会社(かいしゃ)の金(かね)を使(つか)い込(こ)んでいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)<b>ともあろうものが</b>、会社(かいしゃ)の金(かね)を使(つか)い込(こ)んでいた。
"The department head, of all people, had been embezzling company money."

Two problems with the original. The subject is a role rather than a person the
speaker identifies with, and the offence is serious wrongdoing rather than a
careless slip - both of which point to <code>ともあろうものが</code>. Applying
<code>としたことが</code> to embezzlement makes it sound like a forgotten umbrella.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 君(きみ)としたことが、数字(すうじ)を見間違(みまちが)えるとはね。
(b) どうして数字(すうじ)を見(み)間違(まちが)えたんだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is surprise rather than anger, and it contains a compliment: the speaker thinks
highly enough of the listener to find the mistake out of character. It is also
markedly literary, and in real life would come from an older speaker or a scripted
one.

(b) is a plain demand for an explanation, and depending on tone it can be a genuine
question or a telling-off. It carries no implication about the listener's usual
standard. If you want to soften a reprimand in real speech, the natural move is
珍(めずら)しいね or いつもと違(ちが)うね, not the pattern in (a).

</details>
