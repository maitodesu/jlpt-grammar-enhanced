# N1 - How it ends up, and what has to follow

# ～を余儀なくされる

**Meaning** · to be forced into something; to be left with no alternative - read <code>余儀(よぎ)なくされる</code>

余儀(よぎ) is an old noun meaning "another way", so 余儀(よぎ)ない is "there is
no other way". <code>を余儀(よぎ)なくされる</code> is the passive of that: the
subject is the person or organisation that has been left without an option, and the
noun in front of を is the unwelcome thing they now have to accept. It is the
sentence a news bulletin uses when weather, money or an accident has decided
something for somebody.

## Formation

The pattern takes a **noun**, almost always an action noun - the する of a
する-verb is simply dropped.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (action) | Noun + を余儀(よぎ)なくされる | 中止(ちゅうし) → 中止(ちゅうし)を余儀(よぎ)なくされる |
| する-verb | drop する | 休業(きゅうぎょう)する → 休業(きゅうぎょう)を余儀(よぎ)なくされる |
| Noun with a modifier | ～の + Noun + を… | 計画(けいかく)の変更(へんこう)を余儀(よぎ)なくされる |
| Past | ～を余儀(よぎ)なくされた | 撤退(てったい)を余儀(よぎ)なくされた |
| Ongoing state | ～を余儀(よぎ)なくされている | 避難(ひなん)生活(せいかつ)を余儀(よぎ)なくされている |
| Polite | ～を余儀(よぎ)なくされました | 延期(えんき)を余儀(よぎ)なくされました |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the grammatical subject is the **victim**, not the cause. The cause goes
in a separate phrase with で, のため, により or a preceding clause:
<code>大雨(おおあめ)で、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)を余儀(よぎ)なくされた</code>.
Putting the cause in the subject slot needs the causative
<code>を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる</code> instead.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雪(ゆき)のため、登山(とざん)は中止(ちゅうし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
The snow forced the climb to be called off.
</pre>

<pre>
けがで、その選手(せんしゅ)は引退(いんたい)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
An injury forced the player into retirement.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の都合(つごう)で、彼女(かのじょ)は転勤(てんきん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされました</b>。
Circumstances at the company left her no choice but to transfer.
</pre>

<pre>
地震(じしん)の後(あと)、数千人(すうせんにん)が車(くるま)での生活(せいかつ)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされている</b>。
Since the earthquake, several thousand people have been living in their cars with nowhere else to go.
</pre>

<pre>
燃料(ねんりょう)価格(かかく)の高騰(こうとう)により、航空(こうくう)会社(かいしゃ)は路線(ろせん)の縮小(しゅくしょう)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
Soaring fuel prices forced the airline to cut back its network.
</pre>

<pre>
水道管(すいどうかん)の工事(こうじ)で、近(ちか)くの店(みせ)は一週間(いっしゅうかん)の休業(きゅうぎょう)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされました</b>。
Work on the water main forced the nearby shops to close for a week.
</pre>

<pre>
資金(しきん)が集(あつ)まらず、劇団(げきだん)は公演(こうえん)の延期(えんき)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
The funding never came together, and the theatre company had to postpone the run.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)の供給(きょうきゅう)が止(と)まったため、多(おお)くの工場(こうじょう)が生産(せいさん)計画(けいかく)の見直(みなお)しを<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされている</b>。
With the supply of parts cut off, many plants are having to rewrite their production plans.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **formal written Japanese**, and its natural home is news copy, company
announcements, annual reports and official statements. In speech it appears in
meetings and interviews, never over lunch. Among friends the same idea comes out as
<code>中止(ちゅうし)するしかなかった</code> or
<code>中止(ちゅうし)になっちゃった</code>.

Three things travel with it. The outcome is **bad for the subject** - nobody is
余儀(よぎ)なくされる into a promotion they wanted. The cause is **external and
serious**: weather, illness, money, an accident, a regulation. And the tone is
**neutral and impersonal**: the form reports the situation rather than complaining
about it, which is why a company can use it about itself without sounding as though
it is making excuses.

Because of that seriousness, the pattern is badly out of scale for small
inconveniences. <code>昼食(ちゅうしょく)はコンビニの弁当(べんとう)を余儀(よぎ)なくされた</code>
is not wrong so much as comic - it dresses a sandwich up as a disaster. Some writers
use exactly that mismatch for humour, but recognise the joke rather than make it.

The expression is frozen. You will practically never meet the active
<code>余儀(よぎ)なくする</code> on its own; what exists in real text is this
passive and the causative <code>を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる</code>. The adjective
survives separately in the set phrase
<code>余儀(よぎ)ない事情(じじょう)</code>, "unavoidable circumstances".

## Contrast

### ～を余儀(よぎ)なくされる vs ～ざるを得(え)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を余儀(よぎ)なくされる</code> takes a **noun** and
reports the outcome from outside, like a bulletin.
<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> takes a **verb** and speaks from inside the
decision, with the speaker's reluctance audible.

</div>

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)で、大会(たいかい)は延期(えんき)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
Heavy snow forced the tournament to be postponed.
</pre>

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)なので、大会(たいかい)を延期(えんき)せ<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。
With snow like this we have no choice but to postpone the tournament.
</pre>

The first is what the newspaper prints; the second is what the organiser says at the
meeting. Note the grammar difference too: を余儀(よぎ)なくされる cannot take a
verb, and ざるを得(え)ない cannot take a bare noun.

### ～を余儀(よぎ)なくされる vs ～を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **される** puts the sufferer in the subject slot; **させる**
puts the cause there. Same event, opposite ends of the sentence.

</div>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)で、工事(こうじ)は中断(ちゅうだん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
The typhoon meant the works had to be suspended.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が工事(こうじ)の中断(ちゅうだん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
The typhoon forced a suspension of the works.
</pre>

Getting these the wrong way round is the single most common error with the pair.
Ask who is being pushed: if it is the subject of your sentence, use された.

### ～を余儀(よぎ)なくされる vs ～させられる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the causative-passive <code>させられる</code> means **a person
made me**, and it resents them. <code>を余儀(よぎ)なくされる</code> means
**circumstances left no opening**, and blames nobody.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に残業(ざんぎょう)を<b>させられた</b>。
The section head made me work late.
</pre>

<pre>
システム障害(しょうがい)で、全員(ぜんいん)が残業(ざんぎょう)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
A system failure left everyone with no option but to work late.
</pre>

<code>させられる</code> needs someone to point at, and it carries a grudge. The
余儀(よぎ)なく form deliberately has no villain, which is why organisations
reach for it when they would rather not name one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 帰(かえ)るを<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b> → ✅ 帰国(きこく)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>（or 帰(かえ)らざるを得(え)なかった）
  を needs a noun in front of it. A dictionary-form verb cannot sit there; either
  find the matching action noun or switch pattern.

- ❌ 中止(ちゅうし)するを<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b> → ✅ 中止(ちゅうし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>
  With a する-verb, drop the する. This is the most frequent slip in exam answers.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)が試合(しあい)の中止(ちゅうし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b> → ✅ 台風(たいふう)が試合(しあい)の中止(ちゅうし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>
  The cause cannot be the subject of the passive. Either flip to the causative, or
  demote the typhoon: 台風(たいふう)で試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)を余儀(よぎ)なくされた.

- ❌ 希望(きぼう)の大学(だいがく)への進学(しんがく)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b> → ✅ 希望(きぼう)の大学(だいがく)に進(すす)むことができた
  The pattern reports damage. Applied to something the subject wanted, it says the
  opposite of what you mean.

## Practice

**1.** 工場(こうじょう)の火事(かじ)により、会社(かいしゃ)は生産(せいさん)の停止(ていし)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

生産(せいさん)の停止(ていし)<b>を余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>

The fire is an external disaster and the company is the party left without options,
so the passive is right. Note that the cause sits in the により phrase, not in the
subject slot.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 急(きゅう)な雨(あめ)で、運動会(うんどうかい)は中止(ちゅうし)を（余儀(よぎ)なくされた・余儀(よぎ)なくさせた）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中止(ちゅうし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。

The subject of the sentence is 運動会(うんどうかい) - the thing that suffered
the cancellation. The causative would need the rain itself as subject:
急(きゅう)な雨(あめ)が運動会(うんどうかい)の中止(ちゅうし)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせた.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Because of the shortage of staff, the shop was forced to shorten its opening hours."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)のため、店(みせ)は営業(えいぎょう)時間(じかん)の短縮(たんしゅく)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。

"Was forced to shorten" becomes the action noun 短縮(たんしゅく) plus the
passive. Note the reading 人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく): 不足(ふそく) takes
rendaku and becomes ぶそく in this compound.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 大雨(おおあめ)が多(おお)くの住民(じゅうみん)に避難(ひなん)を余儀(よぎ)なくされた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 大雨(おおあめ)が多(おお)くの住民(じゅうみん)に避難(ひなん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
（or 大雨(おおあめ)で、多(おお)くの住民(じゅうみん)が避難(ひなん)を余儀(よぎ)なくされた。）

The rain is the cause and sits in the subject slot, so the verb has to be the
causative. Keeping された would mean the rain itself was the one left with no
choice.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 上司(じょうし)に休日(きゅうじつ)出勤(しゅっきん)を<b>させられた</b>。
(b) 納期(のうき)の関係(かんけい)で、休日(きゅうじつ)出勤(しゅっきん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) names a culprit. The boss made the speaker come in, and the causative-passive
carries the resentment openly; you would say it to a friend, not in a report.

(b) has no culprit at all. The delivery date is what closed off the alternatives,
and the sentence is written in the neutral, formal register a company would use
about itself. It also sounds more serious - a genuine constraint rather than an
unreasonable manager.

</details>

# ～羽目になる

**Meaning** · to end up having to do something you would rather not - read <code>羽目(はめ)になる</code>

羽目(はめ) began as the boarding on a wall and now means a fix, a spot you
cannot back out of. <code>羽目(はめ)になる</code> says that a chain of events -
usually one you set off yourself - has landed you with a job nobody wanted. There is
almost always a note of "and it serves me right" in it.

## Formation

The verb in front stays in the **dictionary form**, however far in the past the
event is. Tense is carried by なる alone.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary form) | Verb + 羽目(はめ)になる | 払(はら)う → 払(はら)う羽目(はめ)になる |
| Past result | Verb + 羽目(はめ)になった | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)く羽目(はめ)になった |
| Polite | Verb + 羽目(はめ)になりました | 謝(あやま)る → 謝(あやま)る羽目(はめ)になりました |
| Pointing back at a situation | こんな／そんな + 羽目(はめ)になる | どうしてこんな羽目(はめ)になったのか |
| Stiffer variant | Verb + 羽目(はめ)に陥(おちい)る | 返済(へんさい)に追(お)われる羽目(はめ)に陥(おちい)る |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>羽目(はめ)を外(はず)す</code>, "to let loose, to go
overboard", uses the same noun but is a completely separate idiom. The two turn up
together often enough to be worth keeping apart:
羽目(はめ)を外(はず)して羽目(はめ)になる is a real sequence of events, not a
pun.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れて、駅(えき)まで歩(ある)く<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
I left my wallet behind and ended up walking to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
じゃんけんに負(ま)けて、全部(ぜんぶ)片付(かたづ)ける<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
I lost at rock-paper-scissors and got landed with all the tidying up.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)のつもりだったのに、上司(じょうし)に謝(あやま)る<b>羽目(はめ)になりました</b>。
I meant it as a joke and ended up having to apologise to my boss.
</pre>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)して、友人(ゆうじん)の家(いえ)に泊(と)まる<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
I missed the last train and wound up staying over at a friend's place.
</pre>

<pre>
軽(かる)い気持(きも)ちで引(ひ)き受(う)けたら、一人(ひとり)で会場(かいじょう)の準備(じゅんび)をする<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
I took it on without thinking, and then found myself setting the whole venue up alone.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)いからと中古(ちゅうこ)の車(くるま)を買(か)ったが、修理(しゅうり)に新車(しんしゃ)並(なみ)の金(かね)を払(はら)う<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
I bought a second-hand car because it was cheap, and ended up spending as much on repairs as a new one would have cost.
</pre>

<pre>
幹事(かんじ)が急(きゅう)に来(こ)られなくなり、私(わたし)が二次会(にじかい)の店(みせ)を探(さが)す<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
The organiser suddenly couldn't make it, and I was the one left hunting for somewhere to go afterwards.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)まずに組(く)み立(た)てたせいで、全部(ぜんぶ)ばらして最初(さいしょ)からやり直(なお)す<b>羽目(はめ)になりました</b>。
I put it together without reading the instructions, so I had to take the whole thing apart and start over.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>羽目(はめ)になる</code> is **conversational**. It belongs to talking about
your day, to blog posts and essays, to the story you tell at the office on Monday
morning. Nothing about it is stiff, and
<code>羽目(はめ)になりました</code> is perfectly usable in a polite work email
describing a mishap.

The tone is **rueful rather than bitter**. Used about yourself it is
self-deprecating: the sentence in front of it names the small mistake that caused
the trouble - 忘(わす)れて, 逃(のが)して, 負(ま)けて, ～たら, ～せいで.
Used about someone else it sounds sympathetic, not accusing; you are on their side.
This is the opposite of <code>始末(しまつ)だ</code>, which passes judgement.

Two limits are worth remembering. First, the outcome must be **unwelcome** - the
pattern cannot report good luck, and a happy ending in front of
羽目(はめ)になる reads as sarcasm. Second, the outcome is normally a **task you
are stuck performing**, not merely a bad state. Something you *do* fits
(歩(ある)く, 払(はら)う, 謝(あやま)る); something that merely *happens to*
you sits badly, so ❌風邪(かぜ)を引(ひ)く羽目(はめ)になった is odd where
風邪(かぜ)を引(ひ)いてしまった is natural.

The scale is domestic. Missing a train, redoing the furniture, chairing a meeting
you did not volunteer for - these are 羽目(はめ) events. For an evacuation or a
factory closure, written Japanese reaches for
<code>を余儀(よぎ)なくされる</code> instead.

## Contrast

### ～羽目(はめ)になる vs ～ことになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことになる</code> is **neutral**: it reports that
something has been arranged or has turned out that way.
<code>羽目(はめ)になる</code> adds that it was a **nuisance** and that the
speaker walked into it.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)の支店(してん)で働(はたら)く<b>ことになりました</b>。
It's been arranged that I'll work at the Osaka branch from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)も手(て)を挙(あ)げず、私(わたし)が司会(しかい)をする<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
Nobody put their hand up, so I got stuck chairing it.
</pre>

Swap them and the meaning shifts audibly. ことになった about the chairing would
sound like a normal appointment; 羽目(はめ)になった about the transfer would
tell your colleagues you are unhappy about it, which may be more than you wanted to
say.

### ～羽目(はめ)になる vs ～を余儀(よぎ)なくされる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same idea, two different worlds.
<code>羽目(はめ)になる</code> is spoken, personal and small-scale.
<code>を余儀(よぎ)なくされる</code> is written, impersonal and serious.

</div>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を忘(わす)れて、雨(あめ)の中(なか)を走(はし)る<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
I forgot my umbrella and ended up running through the rain.
</pre>

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)のため、工事(こうじ)は中断(ちゅうだん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
Heavy rain forced the works to be suspended.
</pre>

The grammar differs too: 羽目(はめ)になる takes a **verb**,
を余儀(よぎ)なくされる takes a **noun**.

### ～羽目(はめ)になる vs ～始末(しまつ)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>羽目(はめ)になる</code> is about being **landed with
a job**. <code>始末(しまつ)だ</code> is about the **disgraceful state** a whole
run of events finally reached, and it passes judgement.

</div>

<pre>
後輩(こうはい)のミスで、休日(きゅうじつ)に出勤(しゅっきん)する<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
A junior colleague's mistake meant I had to come in on my day off.
</pre>

<pre>
後輩(こうはい)は遅刻(ちこく)を繰(く)り返(かえ)し、とうとう連絡(れんらく)もしない<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
He kept turning up late, and in the end stopped even letting anyone know.
</pre>

The first sentence is about the speaker's inconvenience; the second is a verdict on
somebody else's behaviour. 始末(しまつ)だ also needs a downhill run behind it,
where 羽目(はめ)になる is happy with a single blunder.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 歩(ある)いた<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b> → ✅ 歩(ある)く<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>
  The verb stays in the dictionary form. Only なる takes the past tense.

- ❌ 宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たって、旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b> → ✅ …旅行(りょこう)に行(い)くことになった
  Winning a holiday is not a fix you are stuck in. Good news cannot take
  羽目(はめ).

- ❌ 風邪(かぜ)を引(ひ)く<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b> → ✅ 風邪(かぜ)を引(ひ)いてしまった
  Catching a cold is something that happens to you, not a task you were forced to
  carry out.

- ❌ 自分(じぶん)で決(き)めて留学(りゅうがく)する<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b> → ✅ 自分(じぶん)で決(き)めて留学(りゅうがく)することにした
  A choice you made and wanted is the exact opposite of 羽目(はめ).

## Practice

**1.** 寝坊(ねぼう)して、会議(かいぎ)にタクシーで向(む)かう____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

タクシーで向(む)かう<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>

Oversleeping is the blunder, and the expensive taxi is the unwanted job it landed
the speaker with. Note the dictionary form 向(む)かう, not 向(む)かった.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来年(らいねん)から本社(ほんしゃ)で働(はたら)く（羽目(はめ)になりました・ことになりました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本社(ほんしゃ)で働(はたら)く<b>ことになりました</b>。

Announcing a transfer to colleagues calls for the neutral ことになる.
羽目(はめ)になる would tell everyone you resent the move - fine among close
friends, awkward in an announcement.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I put off writing the report, and ended up staying at the office until midnight."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

報告書(ほうこくしょ)を後回(あとまわ)しにして、夜中(よなか)まで会社(かいしゃ)に残(のこ)る<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。

"Ended up staying" is exactly 残(のこ)る羽目(はめ)になった - the speaker's
own procrastination caused it, which is the classic shape for this pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 道(みち)に迷(まよ)って、一時間(いちじかん)も歩(ある)いた羽目(はめ)になりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The verb before 羽目(はめ) must be the plain non-past. The past tense belongs on

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 友人(ゆうじん)の引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを手伝(てつだ)う<b>ことになった</b>。
(b) 友人(ゆうじん)の引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを手伝(てつだ)う<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

in the sentence says how they feel about it.

(b) says they were manoeuvred into it - they mentioned they were free, or nobody
else answered the message - and they are not thrilled. The listener will expect the
story of how it happened, and will probably laugh.

</details>

# ～始末だ

**Meaning** · and in the end it came to this - the final, deplorable stage of a downhill run, delivered as a complaint. Read <code>始末(しまつ)だ</code>

始末(しまつ) on its own means "how a thing turned out; the state of affairs" -
the same noun as in 後始末(あとしまつ), "clearing up after". As a sentence
ending, <code>始末(しまつ)だ</code> takes a series of events that has been going
wrong and reports the point it finally reached. The speaker is never neutral about
it: the form is a verdict.

## Formation

The clause in front is a **plain non-past** verb. The tense of the whole sentence
sits on だ.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain non-past) | Verb + 始末(しまつ)だ | 怒鳴(どな)り出(だ)す → 怒鳴(どな)り出(だ)す始末(しまつ)だ |
| Verb (negative) | ～ない + 始末(しまつ)だ | 返事(へんじ)もしない始末(しまつ)だ |
| Pointing back at the outcome | この／その + 始末(しまつ)だ | 結局(けっきょく)この始末(しまつ)だ |
| Past | ～始末(しまつ)だった | 泣(な)き出(だ)す始末(しまつ)だった |
| Polite | ～始末(しまつ)です | 席(せき)を立(た)つ始末(しまつ)です |
| Formal written | ～始末(しまつ)である | 放置(ほうち)される始末(しまつ)である |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern is nearly always announced in advance by an escalation
marker: 挙(あ)げ句(く)(に), しまいには, とうとう, ついには,
最後(さいご)には. If you can hear one of those in the sentence,
始末(しまつ)だ is what the listener is already waiting for at the end.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
文句(もんく)ばかり言(い)って、しまいには帰(かえ)ってしまう<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
He does nothing but complain, and in the end he simply walks out.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)に遅(おく)れ、とうとう来(こ)なくなる<b>始末(しまつ)です</b>。
He started turning up late for practice and has now stopped coming at all.
</pre>

<pre>
注意(ちゅうい)しても直(なお)らず、挙(あ)げ句(く)に人(ひと)のせいにする<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
Nothing changes however often he is warned, and on top of that he blames other people.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は遅(おく)れに遅(おく)れ、説明会(せつめいかい)すら開(ひら)かれない<b>始末(しまつ)である</b>。
The works fell further and further behind, and in the end not even a briefing was held.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三時間(さんじかん)続(つづ)き、最後(さいご)には議題(ぎだい)が何(なん)だったのかも分(わ)からなくなる<b>始末(しまつ)だった</b>。
The meeting ran three hours, and by the end nobody could even remember what the agenda had been.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は夜中(よなか)まで遊(あそ)び歩(ある)き、朝(あさ)は起(お)きられずに学校(がっこう)を休(やす)む<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
My son is out until all hours, then can't get up in the morning and skips school.
</pre>

<pre>
予算(よさん)は何度(なんど)も削(けず)られ、ついに部署(ぶしょ)そのものが廃止(はいし)される<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
The budget was cut again and again, until finally the department itself was abolished.
</pre>

<pre>
散々(さんざん)口(くち)を出(だ)しておきながら、いざ問題(もんだい)が起(お)きると知(し)らないと言(い)い出(だ)す<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
After interfering at every turn, the moment it went wrong he started claiming he knew nothing about it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>始末(しまつ)だ</code> is a **judgement**, not a report, and the
exasperation is the whole point of using it. It turns up in complaint columns, in
essays and novels, in a critical passage of a news feature, and in spoken grumbling
about a colleague or a relative. 始末(しまつ)です softens the delivery but not
the criticism; the polite form is used to be civil about someone while still saying
they were hopeless.

It is normally aimed at **somebody else**. Used about oneself it becomes
self-mocking, which works but is a deliberate effect. Used about a company or an
institution, it is the shape of a formal complaint.

The pattern needs a **run-up**. It sums up a sequence, so a single isolated event
cannot support it: ❌財布(さいふ)を落(お)とす始末(しまつ)だ has nothing to
be the end of. It also cannot describe a good ending - there is no neutral or
positive 始末(しまつ)だ, and where the outcome is simply the point a process
reached, formal Japanese uses <code>に至(いた)った</code> instead.

One more thing worth knowing: because it editorialises, it does not appear in the
neutral body of a report or a set of minutes. A company writing about its own
results uses plain statements; a journalist writing *about* that company may well
use 始末(しまつ)だ.

## Contrast

### ～始末(しまつ)だ vs ～羽目(はめ)になる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>羽目(はめ)になる</code> is about **you** being landed
with a job. <code>始末(しまつ)だ</code> is about **the state things ended up
in**, usually because of someone else, and it condemns.

</div>

<pre>
同僚(どうりょう)が休(やす)んで、一人(ひとり)で受付(うけつけ)をする<b>羽目(はめ)になった</b>。
My colleague was off, so I got stuck manning reception on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
同僚(どうりょう)は連絡(れんらく)もせずに休(やす)み、今(いま)では電話(でんわ)にも出(で)ない<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
My colleague took time off without a word, and now doesn't even answer the phone.
</pre>

The first reports an inconvenience the speaker had to absorb; the second is a case
being built against somebody.

### ～始末(しまつ)だ vs ～挙(あ)げ句(く)(に)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>挙(あ)げ句(く)に</code> is a **connective** in the
middle of a sentence, "and after all that…". <code>始末(しまつ)だ</code>
**ends** the sentence with the verdict. They are natural partners, not rivals.

</div>

<pre>
散々(さんざん)待(ま)たせた<b>挙(あ)げ句(く)に</b>、約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れていたと言(い)った。
After keeping me waiting for ages, he said he'd forgotten the arrangement.
</pre>

<pre>
散々(さんざん)待(ま)たせた挙(あ)げ句(く)に、約束(やくそく)そのものを忘(わす)れている<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
After keeping me waiting for ages, he had forgotten the arrangement altogether.
</pre>

挙(あ)げ句(く)に takes a past-tense clause in front of it and can be followed
by any ending at all; 始末(しまつ)だ takes a non-past clause and can only be the
ending. Used together, as in the second example, they make the classic complaint
shape.

### ～始末(しまつ)だ vs ～有様(ありさま)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>有様(ありさま)だ</code> shows you the **wretched
picture**. <code>始末(しまつ)だ</code> insists it is the **end point of a
process**, and assigns blame for it.

</div>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の後(あと)、畑(はたけ)は足(あし)の踏(ふ)み場(ば)もない<b>有様(ありさま)だった</b>。
After the typhoon there was nowhere to put your foot in the fields.
</pre>

<pre>
手入(てい)れを怠(おこた)り続(つづ)け、今(いま)では草(くさ)に埋(う)もれる<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>。
Maintenance was neglected year after year, and now the place is buried in weeds.
</pre>

A storm-hit field is simply a state, and 有様(ありさま)だ can describe something
you are looking at with no story behind it. 始末(しまつ)だ needs the story, and
it holds someone responsible for it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 失敗(しっぱい)した<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b> → ✅ 失敗(しっぱい)する<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>
  The clause before 始末(しまつ) is plain non-past. If the whole sentence needs
  the past, write 始末(しまつ)だった.

- ❌ 優勝(ゆうしょう)する<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b> → ✅ 優勝(ゆうしょう)するに<b>至(いた)った</b>
  始末(しまつ)だ only reports endings the speaker deplores. For a neutral or
  good outcome reached after a long process, use に至(いた)る.

- ❌ 遅刻(ちこく)の<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b> → ✅ 遅刻(ちこく)する<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b>
  A bare noun plus の cannot stand in front of it. The only things that can appear
  there directly are この and その.

- ❌ 今朝(けさ)バスに乗(の)り遅(おく)れる<b>始末(しまつ)だ</b> → ✅ 今朝(けさ)はバスに乗(の)り遅(おく)れてしまった
  One isolated mishap has no downhill run behind it, and the heavy verdict makes the
  sentence sound absurd.

</details>

# ～ではすまない

**Meaning** · it will not end there; just that is not going to settle it - read <code>では済(す)まない</code>

The verb is 済(す)む, "to be settled, to be got through". Its affirmative
<code>～で済(す)む</code> is a small everyday pleasure -
千円(せんえん)で済(す)んだ, "it only came to a thousand yen". Negate it and you get a warning:
the thing named in front of では is not enough, and something heavier is on its way.
The heavier thing is usually left unsaid, which is most of the force.

## Formation

だけ is present or implied almost every time, because the whole job of the pattern
is to say "just that is not enough".

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + では済(す)まない | 口頭(こうとう)の注意(ちゅうい)では済(す)まない |
| Noun + だけ (the usual shape) | ～だけでは済(す)まない | 謝罪(しゃざい)だけでは済(す)まない |
| Verb / い-adjective clause | plain form + のでは済(す)まない | 知(し)らなかったのでは済(す)まない |
| な-adjective | ～なのでは済(す)まない | 不注意(ふちゅうい)なのでは済(す)まない |
| Casual contraction | ～じゃ済(す)まない | 冗談(じょうだん)じゃ済(す)まない |
| Polite | ～では済(す)みません | 注意(ちゅうい)では済(す)みません |
| Heavier, passive | ～では済(す)まされない | 知(し)らなかったでは済(す)まされない |
| Set phrase | ただでは済(す)まない | 今回(こんかい)はただでは済(す)まない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the は is not optional. The affirmative is
<code>これで済(す)む</code> with plain で; the negative takes the contrastive
は, <code>これでは済(す)まない</code>. ❌これでは済(す)む is not idiomatic.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
謝(あやま)るだけ<b>では済(す)まない</b>よ。
Just apologising isn't going to be the end of it.
</pre>

<pre>
これは冗談(じょうだん)<b>では済(す)まない</b>。
This isn't something you can pass off as a joke.
</pre>

<pre>
皿(さら)を全部(ぜんぶ)割(わ)ったのだから、「ごめん」<b>では済(す)まない</b>だろう。
You broke every one of the plates, so "sorry" is hardly going to cover it.
</pre>

<pre>
期限(きげん)を過(す)ぎれば、注意(ちゅうい)<b>では済(す)みません</b>。罰金(ばっきん)が科(か)されます。
Past the deadline it doesn't stop at a warning - there is a fine.
</pre>

<pre>
個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)が流出(りゅうしゅつ)した以上(いじょう)、社内(しゃない)の処分(しょぶん)だけ<b>では済(す)まない</b>。
Now that personal data has leaked, internal discipline is not going to be the end of it.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)は本気(ほんき)で怒(おこ)っている。今回(こんかい)はただ<b>では済(す)まない</b>と思(おも)ったほうがいい。
He is genuinely angry; you had better assume you won't get off lightly this time.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)を知(し)らなかった<b>では済(す)まされない</b>立場(たちば)にある。
He is in a position where "I didn't know the rules" simply will not be accepted.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)信用(しんよう)を失(うしな)えば、担当者(たんとうしゃ)を替(か)えるだけ<b>では済(す)まない</b>。
Once trust has gone, swapping the account manager is not going to fix it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The form is a **warning or a verdict**, and its natural settings are a telling-off,
a news commentary, a formal complaint and a legal argument. The consequence it
threatens is deliberately left vague or pushed into the following sentence; that
vagueness is why <code>ただでは済(す)まない</code> reads as close to a threat.

<code>じゃ済(す)まない</code> is the ordinary spoken contraction and is common
among adults. It is not childish, but it is not polite either - it is what you say
when you are annoyed. In writing, both 済(す)まない and the kana すまない are
standard; print tends to prefer the kanji.

Two things learners should keep straight. First, the apology すみません comes from
the same verb - "this is not settled on my side yet" - but
<code>では済(す)まない</code> is never itself an apology. Saying
遅刻(ちこく)では済(す)みません to a client means "being late will not be the
end of this", which is not what you were reaching for. Second, the pattern says the
named thing is **insufficient**, not that it is **forbidden**; the action in front
of では is usually one that ought to happen, just not on its own.

## Contrast

### ～では済(す)まない vs ～で済(す)む

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>で済(す)む</code> is relief: it turned out to be
**only** that much. <code>では済(す)まない</code> is the warning that it will
**not** stop there.

</div>

<pre>
修理(しゅうり)は五千円(ごせんえん)<b>で済(す)んだ</b>。
The repair only came to five thousand yen.
</pre>

<pre>
この傷(きず)だと、五千円(ごせんえん)<b>では済(す)まない</b>だろう。
With damage like this it won't stop at five thousand.
</pre>

Learning the affirmative first makes the negative obvious. Notice again that the
affirmative uses plain で and the negative adds は.

### ～では済(す)まない vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>てはいけない</code> says the action is **not allowed**.
<code>では済(す)まない</code> says the action is fine, but **not enough**.

</div>

<pre>
黙(だま)っていて<b>はいけない</b>。
You mustn't keep quiet about it.
</pre>

<pre>
黙(だま)って頭(あたま)を下(さ)げるだけ<b>では済(す)まない</b>。
Bowing your head in silence is not going to settle this.
</pre>

The first forbids; the second accepts the bow and demands more on top of it. If your
English is "you can't just…", the Japanese you want is
では済(す)まない, not てはいけない.

### ～では済(す)まない vs ～では済(す)まされない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the passive raises it from "it will not end there" to **"the
world will not let it end there"**. Same event, more social pressure and a heavier
register.

</div>

<pre>
うっかりしていた<b>では済(す)まない</b>。
"I wasn't paying attention" is not going to be the end of it.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)を預(あず)かる立場(たちば)で、うっかりしていた<b>では済(す)まされない</b>。
For someone responsible for safety, "I wasn't paying attention" cannot be allowed to stand.
</pre>

済(す)まされない is the form editorials and courtrooms use, and it appears
especially after quoted excuses - 知(し)らなかった, 忘(わす)れていた,
聞(き)いていなかった.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 謝(あやま)る<b>では済(す)まない</b> → ✅ 謝(あやま)るだけ<b>では済(す)まない</b>（or 謝(あやま)ったの<b>では済(す)まない</b>）
  A plain verb cannot sit directly in front of では. Insert だけ, or nominalise the
  clause with の.

- ❌ この金額(きんがく)<b>では済(す)む</b> → ✅ この金額(きんがく)<b>で済(す)む</b>
  The は belongs to the negative. Affirmative 済(す)む takes bare で.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れて申(もう)し訳(わけ)ない、本当(ほんとう)に<b>では済(す)みません</b> → ✅ 遅(おく)れて本当(ほんとう)に<b>すみません</b>
  The apology is すみません on its own. <code>では済(す)みません</code> is a
  warning aimed at someone else.

- ❌ 寄付(きふ)だけ<b>では済(す)まない</b>ので、喜(よろこ)んで参加(さんか)した → ✅ 寄付(きふ)だけ<b>では済(す)まない</b>ので、会場(かいじょう)の準備(じゅんび)まで頼(たの)まれた
  The pattern always looks toward a consequence the speaker would rather avoid.
  Pairing it with a happy result contradicts the form.

## Practice

**1.** 会社(かいしゃ)の車(くるま)をぶつけたのだから、口頭(こうとう)の注意(ちゅうい)____。

口頭(こうとう)の注意(ちゅうい)<b>では済(す)まないだろう</b>

A verbal warning is the light option, and the sentence says it will not be the end
of the matter - a written report or a deduction is coming. だろう is natural here
because the speaker is predicting the consequence rather than announcing it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)を知(し)っていながら黙(だま)っていたのなら、「忘(わす)れていた」（では済(す)まされない・で済(す)む）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「忘(わす)れていた」<b>では済(す)まされない</b>。

The excuse is being rejected on behalf of everyone affected, not just by the
speaker, and the quoted excuse in front of it is the classic environment for the
passive form.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If the same fault happens a third time, a refund alone won't settle it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

同(おな)じ不具合(ふぐあい)が三度(さんど)起(お)きれば、返金(へんきん)だけ<b>では済(す)まない</b>。

"Alone won't settle it" is だけでは済(す)まない almost word for word. Note the
noun 返金(へんきん) before だけ - a verb would need の.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 規則(きそく)を破(やぶ)ったのだから、注意(ちゅうい)するでは済(す)まない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 規則(きそく)を破(やぶ)ったのだから、注意(ちゅうい)<b>だけでは済(す)まない</b>。

注意(ちゅうい)する is a verb, and a plain verb cannot sit in front of では.
Dropping する turns it into the noun 注意(ちゅうい), and だけ supplies the "just"
that the pattern needs.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)の件(けん)は、部長(ぶちょう)への報告(ほうこく)<b>で済(す)んだ</b>。
(b) 今回(こんかい)の件(けん)は、部長(ぶちょう)への報告(ほうこく)<b>では済(す)まない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is relief. Telling the section head turned out to be the whole of it - no
customer letter, no formal report, nothing further to do.

(b) is a warning. The report to the section head has been made or will be made, but
something bigger is coming: a written apology, a client visit, a disciplinary
process. The sentence deliberately leaves the listener to imagine what.

</details>

# ～ないではすまない / ～ずにはすまない

**Meaning** · you are not going to get out of doing it - the situation stays open until you do. Read <code>ないでは済(す)まない</code> / <code>ずには済(す)まない</code>

Literally "it will not be settled without doing it". The pressure comes from
**outside**: somebody is owed an apology, a payment or an explanation, and the
matter will simply not close until they get it. That is what separates this from an
ordinary statement of obligation - nothing in a rulebook forces the speaker, but
everybody around them is waiting.

## Formation

Both endings sit on the ない-stem. する has two shapes: the regular しないでは and
the older せずには.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ないでは／ずには済(す)まない | 払(はら)わない → 払(はら)わないでは済(す)まない・払(はら)わずには済(す)まない |
| Verb (る-verb) | same | 届(とど)けない → 届(とど)けずには済(す)まない |
| Verb (する) | **せずには済(す)まない**／しないでは済(す)まない | 説明(せつめい)する → 説明(せつめい)せずには済(す)まない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ずには済(す)まない</b> | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ずには済(す)まない |
| Past | ～ずには済(す)まなかった | 謝(あやま)らずには済(す)まなかった |
| Polite | ～ずには済(す)みません | 報告(ほうこく)せずには済(す)みません |
| Heavier, passive | ～ないでは済(す)まされない | 謝(あやま)らないでは済(す)まされない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>説明(せつめい)しずには済(す)まない</code> is the
standard error. With ずに, する takes the old negative stem せ:
<code>せずには済(す)まない</code>. If you want し, you need the other ending -
<code>しないでは済(す)まない</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これだけ待(ま)たせたのだから、謝(あやま)ら<b>ないでは済(す)まない</b>。
After keeping them waiting this long, I can hardly get out of apologising.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を割(わ)ったのなら、弁償(べんしょう)せ<b>ずには済(す)まない</b>。
If you broke the window, you are going to have to pay for it.
</pre>

<pre>
事情(じじょう)を知(し)っている以上(いじょう)、上司(じょうし)に報告(ほうこく)し<b>ないでは済(す)まない</b>。
Now that I know what happened, there is no way I can avoid reporting it.
</pre>

<pre>
式(しき)に呼(よ)ばれたのだから、何(なに)か一言(ひとこと)述(の)べ<b>ずには済(す)まない</b>だろう。
Having been invited to the ceremony, I don't suppose I'll get away without saying a few words.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)に迷惑(めいわく)をかけたので、挨拶(あいさつ)に行(い)か<b>ないでは済(す)みません</b>。
We caused the neighbours trouble, so we can't very well not go round and apologise.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)に不具合(ふぐあい)が見(み)つかった以上(いじょう)、全(ぜん)製品(せいひん)を回収(かいしゅう)せ<b>ずには済(す)まない</b>。
With a defect found in the component, a full recall cannot be avoided.
</pre>

<pre>
記者(きしゃ)会見(かいけん)で同(おな)じ質問(しつもん)が続(つづ)き、社長(しゃちょう)は経緯(けいい)を説明(せつめい)せ<b>ずには済(す)まなかった</b>。
With the same question coming back at him again and again, the president had no way of avoiding an account of what had happened.
</pre>

<pre>
預(あず)かった鍵(かぎ)をなくしてしまい、大家(おおや)さんに正直(しょうじき)に話(はな)さ<b>ないでは済(す)まなかった</b>。
I lost the key I had been entrusted with, and there was no getting round telling the landlord the truth.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Both endings are **formal**, and ずには is the stiffer, more written of the two.
ないでは is a shade lighter but still belongs to careful speech - a meeting, a
serious conversation with a client, a formal letter - not to chat. Among friends the
same thought comes out as <code>謝(あやま)るしかない</code> or
<code>謝(あやま)らないわけにはいかない</code>.

What the form always adds is the **social eye**. Somebody is watching, somebody is
owed something, and the speaker knows perfectly well how it would look if they did
nothing. That is why it collocates so heavily with 謝(あやま)る,
説明(せつめい)する, 弁償(べんしょう)する, 報告(ほうこく)する,
挨拶(あいさつ)する - the verbs of settling accounts with other people. It sits
badly on a purely private matter, because there is nobody there for the situation
to remain unsettled with.

The passive <code>ずには済(す)まされない</code> exists and is heavier still: it
moves the pressure from the speaker's own judgement to society's. Reserve it for
writing.

## Contrast

### ～ずには済(す)まない vs ～ずにはおかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>済(す)まない</code>: **the world will not let me
off** until I do it. <code>おかない</code>: **it will not stop** until it has had
its effect on somebody else. The pressure runs in opposite directions.

</div>

<pre>
これだけ騒(さわ)ぎになった以上(いじょう)、会社(かいしゃ)は説明(せつめい)せ<b>ずには済(す)まない</b>。
With a fuss on this scale, the company will not be allowed to say nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の発表(はっぴょう)は、利用者(りようしゃ)の反発(はんぱつ)を招(まね)か<b>ずにはおかない</b>。
This announcement is bound to provoke a backlash from users.
</pre>

In the first the company is under pressure; in the second the announcement is the
thing applying pressure. A quick test: if the subject of your sentence is the one
being squeezed, use 済(す)まない.

### ～ずには済(す)まない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>なければならない</code> is a **rule or a duty**, and it
would hold even if nobody were looking. <code>ずには済(す)まない</code> is
**social**: it holds because people are looking.

</div>

<pre>
税金(ぜいきん)は期限(きげん)までに払(はら)わ<b>なければなりません</b>。
Tax has to be paid by the deadline.
</pre>

<pre>
これだけ世話(せわ)になったのだから、お礼(れい)を言(い)わ<b>ずには済(す)まない</b>。
After all the help they gave me, I can't simply not thank them.
</pre>

The first is true of everyone and appears on a government leaflet. The second is
true of this speaker in this situation, and would be strange on a leaflet.

### ～ずには済(す)まない vs ～ずにはいられない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>済(す)まない</code> = **circumstances** will not let
the matter rest. <code>いられない</code> = **my own feelings** will not let me sit
still. Outside versus inside.

</div>

<pre>
あの知(し)らせを聞(き)いて、涙(なみだ)を流(なが)さ<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>。
Hearing that news, I couldn't hold back the tears.
</pre>

<pre>
部下(ぶか)のミスである以上(いじょう)、私(わたし)が頭(あたま)を下(さ)げ<b>ずには済(す)まなかった</b>。
Since the mistake was my subordinate's, there was no way I could avoid apologising myself.
</pre>

Swap them and both fail. Tears are not demanded by the situation, and a formal
apology is not an urge welling up from inside.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 説明(せつめい)<b>しずには済(す)まない</b> → ✅ 説明(せつめい)<b>せずには済(す)まない</b>（or 説明(せつめい)しないでは済(す)まない）
  With ずに, する uses the older stem せ. This is by far the most common slip.

- ❌ 払(はら)う<b>ないでは済(す)まない</b> → ✅ 払(はら)わ<b>ないでは済(す)まない</b>
  The base is the ない-form stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 嬉(うれ)しくて笑(わら)わ<b>ずには済(す)まなかった</b> → ✅ 嬉(うれ)しくて笑(わら)わ<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>
  Laughter is an inner reaction, and no situation was demanding it of the speaker.

- ❌ 毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)ら<b>ずには済(す)まない</b> → ✅ 毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)らないと気(き)が済(す)まない
  A private habit has nobody on the other side of it. The idiom for "I'm not
  satisfied unless I do it" is 気(き)が済(す)まない, with 気(き).

- ❌ 来(き)<b>ずには済(す)まない</b> → ✅ 来(こ)<b>ずには済(す)まない</b>
  来(く)る is irregular; its ない stem is こ, as in 来(こ)ない.

The pressure is entirely social - the press and the public are waiting, and the
matter will not close until the company faces them. Note the ない stem
開(ひら)か, not the dictionary form.

</details>

Reminiscing with an old teacher is an impulse, not an obligation, so いられない is

# ～ないではおかない / ～ずにはおかない

**Meaning** · it is bound to have that effect; I will not let it rest until I do it - read <code>ないではおかない</code> / <code>ずにはおかない</code>

おく here is "to leave a thing as it is". The double negative therefore says "will
not leave it undone", and two quite different sentences come out of that one shape.
With a verb of **effect** - usually a causative - it is a statement of inevitability:
the film *will* move you, and there is no leaving the audience alone. With a verb of
the speaker's **own action** it is a vow: I am not letting this go.

## Formation

Both endings sit on the ない-stem, exactly as with ずには済(す)まない.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ないでは／ずにはおかない | 招(まね)かない → 招(まね)かずにはおかない |
| Verb (る-verb) | same | 与(あた)えない → 与(あた)えないではおかない |
| Verb (する) | **せずにはおかない**／しないではおかない | 究明(きゅうめい)する → 究明(きゅうめい)せずにはおかない |
| Causative (the commonest shape) | ～させずにはおかない | 考(かんが)えさせる → 考(かんが)えさせずにはおかない |
| Past | ～ずにはおかなかった | 驚(おどろ)かせずにはおかなかった |
| Modifying a noun | ～ずにはおかない + Noun | 追及(ついきゅう)せずにはおかない構(かま)え |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the two senses take different subjects. **Inevitability** needs a
non-human subject and an effect on somebody else, which is why the verb is nearly
always a causative (感動(かんどう)させる, 考(かんが)えさせる) or a verb of
consequence (招(まね)く, 与(あた)える, 引(ひ)き起(お)こす). The **vow**
needs a first-person subject and a verb of deliberate action. A polite form exists,
<code>ずにはおきません</code>, but it is rare - this pattern lives in plain-form
written Japanese.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この映画(えいが)は見(み)る者(もの)を泣(な)かせ<b>ずにはおかない</b>。
This film cannot fail to move its audience to tears.
</pre>

<pre>
その一言(ひとこと)は人(ひと)を傷(きず)つけ<b>ないではおかない</b>。
A remark like that is bound to wound.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げの発表(はっぴょう)は、利用者(りようしゃ)の不満(ふまん)を招(まね)か<b>ずにはおかない</b>。
The announcement of the price rise is certain to provoke discontent among users.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の文章(ぶんしょう)は、読(よ)む者(もの)に自分(じぶん)の生(い)き方(かた)を考(かんが)えさせ<b>ずにはおかない</b>。
His writing makes the reader think about how they are living, whether they wish to or not.
</pre>

<pre>
真相(しんそう)は必(かなら)ず明(あき)らかにし<b>ないではおかない</b>。
I will get to the bottom of this, whatever it takes.
</pre>

<pre>
遺族(いぞく)は、事故(じこ)の責任(せきにん)を追及(ついきゅう)せ<b>ずにはおかない</b>構(かま)えだ。
The bereaved families are in no mood to let the question of responsibility drop.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の判決(はんけつ)は、同(おな)じような裁判(さいばん)に影響(えいきょう)を与(あた)え<b>ずにはおかない</b>だろう。
This ruling is bound to have an effect on similar cases.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かな語(かた)り口(くち)でありながら、その証言(しょうげん)は聞(き)く者(もの)に強(つよ)い衝撃(しょうげき)を与(あた)え<b>ずにはおかなかった</b>。
Quietly told though it was, the testimony could not but leave its hearers shaken.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **written, rhetorical Japanese**. Book reviews, film write-ups, editorials,
criticism, courtroom reporting and speeches are where it lives. Spoken aloud in
ordinary conversation it sounds theatrical, and the vow sense in particular has the
ring of a period drama or a politician at a microphone.

Of the two senses, **inevitability is far and away the commoner** in modern text.
It is the reviewer's phrase: the writer wants to say that the work has an effect on
you which you do not get to opt out of, and the double negative delivers exactly
that. The vow sense survives in headlines about investigations
(徹底(てってい)究明(きゅうめい)せずにはおかない) and in fiction, but a learner
should treat it as something to recognise. In speech you would say
必(かなら)ず～する or きっと～させる.

ないでは and ずには are interchangeable here, with ずには a shade more literary. The
form is stronger than 必(かなら)ず～する: it does not merely predict the effect,
it says the thing is incapable of *not* producing it.

## Contrast

### ～ずにはおかない vs ～ずには済(す)まない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **おかない**: the subject will not stop until it has had its
effect on **someone else**. **済(す)まない**: the subject is the one under
pressure, and the world will not let **them** off.

</div>

<pre>
この報告書(ほうこくしょ)は、関係者(かんけいしゃ)に見直(みなお)しを迫(せま)ら<b>ずにはおかない</b>。
This report is bound to force those involved into a rethink.
</pre>

<pre>
これだけの指摘(してき)を受(う)けた以上(いじょう)、関係者(かんけいしゃ)は見直(みなお)しをせ<b>ずには済(す)まない</b>。
Faced with criticism on this scale, those involved will not be allowed to leave things as they are.
</pre>

Same event, opposite grammar. In the first the report is doing the pushing; in the
second the people are being pushed. Identify who is squeezed and the choice makes
itself.

### ～ずにはおかない vs ～させる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the plain causative <code>させる</code> reports that the effect
**happens**. <code>させずにはおかない</code> adds that it **cannot fail to** happen,
and lifts the sentence into literary register.

</div>

<pre>
この小説(しょうせつ)は読者(どくしゃ)を感動(かんどう)<b>させる</b>。
This novel moves its readers.
</pre>

<pre>
この小説(しょうせつ)は読者(どくしゃ)を感動(かんどう)<b>させずにはおかない</b>。
This novel cannot leave a reader unmoved.
</pre>

The first is a plain statement and could appear anywhere. The second is a review
sentence: it claims the effect is inescapable, and the rhetoric is doing as much
work as the meaning. Note that the causative is not optional - the effect has to
land on somebody.

### ～ずにはおかない vs ～ずにはいられない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **おかない**: something out there will not leave **people**
alone. **いられない**: *I* cannot leave *myself* alone - my own feelings force the
action.

</div>

<pre>
その光景(こうけい)を見(み)て、声(こえ)をかけ<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>。
Seeing that, I couldn't stop myself saying something.
</pre>

<pre>
その光景(こうけい)は、見(み)る者(もの)に言葉(ことば)を失(うしな)わせ<b>ずにはおかない</b>。
A sight like that leaves anyone who sees it lost for words.
</pre>

The two look almost identical and share a stem. The test is the subject: a person
with feelings takes いられない, and a thing with an effect takes おかない.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 感動(かんどう)<b>しずにはおかない</b> → ✅ 感動(かんどう)<b>させずにはおかない</b>
  Two repairs at once. する takes the old stem せ before ずに, and the sense
  requires the causative because the emotion belongs to somebody else.

- ❌ 私(わたし)はこの映画(えいが)に感動(かんどう)せ<b>ずにはおかなかった</b> → ✅ 私(わたし)はこの映画(えいが)に感動(かんどう)せ<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>
  Your own reaction takes いられない. おかない would mean you were determined to
  force yourself to be moved.

- ❌ 明日(あした)までに宿題(しゅくだい)をやら<b>ずにはおかない</b> → ✅ 明日(あした)までに宿題(しゅくだい)をやらないわけにはいかない
  Homework is an obligation, not a vow to make the world answer for something. The
  mismatch of scale makes the sentence comic.

- ❌ 招(まね)く<b>ずにはおかない</b> → ✅ 招(まね)か<b>ずにはおかない</b>
  The base is the ない-form stem, not the dictionary form.

## Practice

**1.** 今回(こんかい)の決定(けってい)は、現場(げんば)に大(おお)きな混乱(こんらん)を招(まね)か____。

混乱(こんらん)を招(まね)か<b>ずにはおかない</b>

The decision is the subject, the confusion lands on somebody else, and the writer is
claiming the consequence is unavoidable - the inevitability sense. Note the ない stem
招(まね)か.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)の演奏(えんそう)は、聞(き)く人(ひと)を驚(おどろ)かせ（ずにはおかない・ずにはいられない）。

聞(き)く人(ひと)を驚(おどろ)かせ<b>ずにはおかない</b>。

The subject is the performance and the effect falls on the listeners, so おかない is
right. いられない would need a person as subject and their own feeling as the verb -
驚(おどろ)かずにはいられない, "I couldn't help being astonished".

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "A photograph like this is bound to make people think about the war."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このような写真(しゃしん)は、人々(ひとびと)に戦争(せんそう)について考(かんが)えさせ<b>ずにはおかない</b>。

"Is bound to make people think" is the causative 考(かんが)えさせる plus the
pattern. This is the review-and-editorial register the form belongs to, so the plain
form is correct even in polite writing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この事件(じけん)の真相(しんそう)を究明(きゅうめい)しずにはおかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この事件(じけん)の真相(しんそう)を究明(きゅうめい)<b>せずにはおかない</b>。
（or 究明(きゅうめい)<b>しないではおかない</b>。）

する takes the classical stem せ before ずに. The modern し only works with the
other ending, しないでは. This sentence is the vow sense, and it reads like a
headline or a line of dialogue rather than something said at a desk.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この報道(ほうどう)は世論(よろん)に影響(えいきょう)を与(あた)えるだろう。
(b) この報道(ほうどう)は世論(よろん)に影響(えいきょう)を与(あた)え<b>ずにはおかない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a prediction, and a hedged one. The reporting will probably have an effect;
the writer allows that it might not.

(b) removes the hedge and adds rhetoric. It says the reporting is *incapable* of
leaving opinion untouched, and the form itself signals that the sentence comes from
an editorial or a piece of commentary rather than a news report.

</details>

# ～てしかるべきだ

**Meaning** · it is only right that ~; there ought properly to be ~ - read <code>てしかるべきだ</code>

しかるべき is the old 然(しか)るべき, "as it should be" - 然(しか)り, "so, thus",
plus the classical べし. <code>てしかるべきだ</code> therefore does not ask for
anything and does not order anything. It asserts that something is **no more than
proper**, and it is nearly always written because the proper thing has *not*
happened.

## Formation

The passive is by far the commonest verb shape, because the sentence is usually
about what ought to be done *to* something rather than by anybody in particular.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (て-form) | ～てしかるべきだ | 考慮(こうりょ)する → 考慮(こうりょ)してしかるべきだ |
| Verb (passive) - the usual shape | ～されてしかるべきだ | 評価(ひょうか)する → 評価(ひょうか)されてしかるべきだ |
| ある | あってしかるべきだ | 説明(せつめい)があってしかるべきだ |
| Adjective or noun, via である | ～であってしかるべきだ | 慎重(しんちょう)だ → 慎重(しんちょう)であってしかるべきだ |
| Written ending | ～てしかるべきである | 見直(みなお)されてしかるべきである |
| Rhetorical question | ～てしかるべきではないか | 配慮(はいりょ)があってしかるべきではないか |
| Before a noun (adjectival use) | しかるべき + Noun | しかるべき処置(しょち)／しかるべき立場(たちば)の人(ひと) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the word has nothing to do with 叱(しか)る, "to scold". It is
然(しか)る, an old verb meaning "to be so". The accidental resemblance sends a
lot of learners looking for a telling-off that is not there.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
被害者(ひがいしゃ)への説明(せつめい)が<b>あってしかるべきだ</b>。
There ought, at the very least, to have been an explanation for the victims.
</pre>

<pre>
この決定(けってい)は見直(みなお)され<b>てしかるべきである</b>。
This decision ought properly to be reconsidered.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)の功績(こうせき)は、もっと評価(ひょうか)され<b>てしかるべきだ</b>。
Years of service like his deserve rather more recognition than they have had.
</pre>

<pre>
税金(ぜいきん)の使(つか)い道(みち)については、住民(じゅうみん)に十分(じゅうぶん)な情報(じょうほう)が示(しめ)され<b>てしかるべきだ</b>。
On the use of public money, residents ought to be given full information.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)に関(かか)わる判断(はんだん)である以上(いじょう)、現場(げんば)の意見(いけん)は尊重(そんちょう)され<b>てしかるべきだ</b>ろう。
Since it is a judgement that bears on safety, the view from the site ought surely to be respected.
</pre>

<pre>
「知(し)らなかった」で片付(かたづ)けるのではなく、経緯(けいい)が公(おおやけ)に説明(せつめい)され<b>てしかるべきだ</b>。
Rather than disposing of it with "we didn't know", the sequence of events ought to be set out publicly.
</pre>

<pre>
遺族(いぞく)の心情(しんじょう)に対(たい)する配慮(はいりょ)が、もう少(すこ)し<b>あってしかるべきではないか</b>。
Should there not have been rather more consideration for the feelings of the bereaved?
</pre>

<pre>
制度(せいど)が時代(じだい)に合(あ)わなくなったのであれば、速(すみ)やかに改(あらた)められ<b>てしかるべきである</b>。
If the system no longer fits the times, then it ought to be amended without delay.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; do not say it.** <code>てしかるべきだ</code> is written
Japanese and effectively nothing else. It belongs to newspaper editorials and
opinion columns, to formal letters of complaint, to committee minutes, to
administrative and legal documents, and to the more solemn kind of speech. Said
out loud to a colleague it sounds as though you are reading aloud from a leader
column - not rude, but distinctly odd. When you want to say the same thing, use
<code>～べきだ</code>, or <code>～て当然(とうぜん)だ</code>, or
<code>～なければならない</code>.

Two features are worth carrying away from the page. First, it is an **assertion
about propriety, not a request**: the writer is not asking anyone to do anything,
they are declaring what the right state of affairs would be. Second, it almost
always carries an **implied complaint** - the propriety is being asserted precisely
because reality has fallen short. <code>説明(せつめい)があってしかるべきだ</code>
means, in practice, "and there wasn't one".

The one part of the word a learner really will use is the **adjectival**
しかるべき, which is alive and well in formal speech and email:
<code>しかるべき処置(しょち)を取(と)る</code> ("take appropriate measures"),
<code>しかるべき部署(ぶしょ)にお伝(つた)えします</code> ("I'll pass this to
the relevant department"). That much is ordinary business Japanese. The sentence
ending is not.

## Contrast

### ～てしかるべきだ vs ～べきだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>べきだ</code> is what you **say**;
<code>てしかるべきだ</code> is what a leader column **writes**. The meaning is
close; the register is not, and the longer form adds "and it is only proper".

</div>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)はきちんと説明(せつめい)<b>するべきだ</b>。
The company ought to give a proper explanation.
</pre>

<pre>
利用者(りようしゃ)に対(たい)し、十分(じゅうぶん)な説明(せつめい)がなされ<b>てしかるべきである</b>。
A full explanation ought properly to be given to users.
</pre>

The first can be said at a meeting or written in an email. The second cannot be said
at all without sounding like a quotation. If you are choosing which to produce, the
answer is always べきだ.

### ～てしかるべきだ vs ～て当然(とうぜん)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>て当然(とうぜん)だ</code> is about what is
**naturally to be expected**; <code>てしかるべきだ</code> is about what is
**right**. Expectation versus propriety.

</div>

<pre>
あれだけ待(ま)たされたのだから、怒(おこ)っ<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
After being kept waiting that long, of course he's angry.
</pre>

<pre>
これほどの不手際(ふてぎわ)である以上(いじょう)、責任者(せきにんしゃ)は処分(しょぶん)され<b>てしかるべきだ</b>。
Given a failure on this scale, those responsible ought properly to face sanction.
</pre>

当然(とうぜん)だ predicts and excuses - anyone would react that way. しかるべきだ
judges - this is what justice requires. Note also that
当然(とうぜん)だ is at home in speech, where しかるべきだ is not.

### ～てしかるべきだ vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>なければならない</code> is **necessity**: it must
happen, or something breaks. <code>てしかるべきだ</code> is **propriety**: it ought
to happen, because that is what is right. Weaker in force, heavier in tone.

</div>

<pre>
申請書(しんせいしょ)は今週(こんしゅう)中(ちゅう)に出(だ)さ<b>なければなりません</b>。
The application has to be submitted this week.
</pre>

<pre>
申請(しんせい)の理由(りゆう)は、本人(ほんにん)に説明(せつめい)され<b>てしかるべきだ</b>。
The reasons for the decision on the application ought to be explained to the applicant.
</pre>

The first states a hard requirement with a consequence behind it. The second states
what would be decent, and quietly notes that it did not happen.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 考慮(こうりょ)する<b>しかるべきだ</b> → ✅ 考慮(こうりょ)し<b>てしかるべきだ</b>
  The base is the て-form. A dictionary-form verb in front of しかるべき is the
  commonest structural error.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>でしかるべきだ</b> → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>であってしかるべきだ</b>
  Adjectives and nouns connect through である, not through the plain で.

- ❌ 明日(あした)までに出(だ)し<b>てしかるべきだ</b>（said to a colleague at the next desk） → ✅ 明日(あした)までに出(だ)す<b>べきだ</b>
  Not a grammar error, a register error. The long form makes an office remark sound
  like a published opinion.

- ❌ Reading しかるべき as a form of 叱(しか)る → the word is 然(しか)るべき.
  There is no scolding in it; the sense is "as things ought to be".

Everything about the sentence points there: the passive

unspoken complaint that no explanation was given. It belongs on an opinion page.

# ～ばそれまでだ

**Meaning** · if that happens, that is the end of it - nothing you have built or planned survives it

それまで is "up to that point, and no further". Put a conditional in front and the
sentence says: whatever else may be true, once *this* happens the story stops.
Nothing follows, because there is nothing left to say. It is the shape of a resigned
aphorism, and it is usually a warning about how easily something can be lost.

## Formation

The commonest shape by a wide margin is てしまう plus ば - the loss is presented as
final and complete.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ば-form) | ～ばそれまでだ | 壊(こわ)れる → 壊(こわ)れればそれまでだ |
| Verb (たら-form) | ～たらそれまでだ | 落(お)とす → 落(お)としたらそれまでだ |
| てしまう + ば (the usual shape) | ～てしまえばそれまでだ | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れてしまえばそれまでだ |
| Set phrase | ～と言(い)ってしまえばそれまでだ | 運(うん)だと言(い)ってしまえばそれまでだ |
| Concessive continuation | ～ばそれまでだが、… | 仕方(しかた)がないと言(い)えばそれまでだが |
| Written ending | ～ばそれまでである | 失(うしな)えばそれまでである |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the sentence in front is almost always **hypothetical and unwelcome**:
dropping it, forgetting it, dying, burning, being found out. The pattern does not
report what happened; it warns about what one slip would cost.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どんなに高(たか)いカメラも、落(お)としてしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>。
However expensive the camera, one drop and that is the end of it.
</pre>

<pre>
命(いのち)を落(お)としてしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>。
Lose your life and there is nothing more to be said.
</pre>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)を捨(す)てられてしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>。
Once the evidence has been thrown away, that is the end of the matter.
</pre>

<pre>
どれほど準備(じゅんび)をしても、当日(とうじつ)に熱(ねつ)を出(だ)せ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>。
However much you prepare, if you run a fever on the day it is all over.
</pre>

<pre>
紙(かみ)の記録(きろく)は火事(かじ)で燃(も)えてしまえ<b>ばそれまでで</b>、だからこそ複製(ふくせい)が要(い)る。
Paper records are gone for good in a fire, which is exactly why copies are needed.
</pre>

<pre>
才能(さいのう)の問題(もんだい)だと言(い)ってしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>が、続(つづ)けた人(ひと)だけが残(のこ)っているのも事実(じじつ)だ。
You can put it down to talent and end the discussion there, but it is also a fact that the ones still standing are the ones who kept going.
</pre>

<pre>
運(うん)が悪(わる)かったと片付(かたづ)けてしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>が、同(おな)じ事故(じこ)が三度(さんど)も起(お)きている。
One could write it off as bad luck and leave it there, but the same accident has now happened three times.
</pre>

<pre>
どれだけ美(うつく)しい建物(たてもの)でも、住(す)む人(ひと)がいなくなれ<b>ばそれまでである</b>。
However beautiful a building may be, once there is no one living in it, that is the end.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this rather than reach for it.** <code>ばそれまでだ</code> is the
language of **aphorism and written argument** - essays, opinion columns, novels and
narration, the sententious paragraph of a speech. Produced in casual conversation it
sounds like a line from a proverb collection. In speech, say
<code>落(お)としたら終(お)わりだ</code> or
<code>バレたらおしまいだ</code>; those carry the same meaning in the register a
person actually talks in.

One shape does survive into adult speech, and it is worth knowing well:
<code>～と言(い)ってしまえばそれまでだが</code>. It is a discourse move, not a
statement about disaster - "you could put it that way and end the discussion, but…"
The が is almost obligatory, because the speaker is about to disagree with the easy
answer they have just quoted. Someone saying it is conceding a point in order to go
past it. That much you can use.

The tone elsewhere is **resigned and slightly grand**. It presents the loss as
absolute and not worth arguing with, which is why it attaches so readily to
命(いのち), 信用(しんよう), records, evidence and irreplaceable things. It
does not fit small reversible setbacks: missing one bus is not それまで.

## Contrast

### ～ばそれまでだ vs ～たら終(お)わりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same idea in two registers.
<code>たら終(お)わりだ</code> / <code>たらおしまいだ</code> is what people
**say**; <code>ばそれまでだ</code> is what essays **write**.

</div>

<pre>
パスワードを忘(わす)れ<b>たら終(お)わりだ</b>よ。
If you forget the password, that's it - you're done for.
</pre>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)となる記録(きろく)を失(うしな)ってしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>。
Should the key records be lost, that is the end of the matter.
</pre>

If you are choosing which to produce, choose 終(お)わりだ. Reserve それまでだ for
reading, and for the 言(い)ってしまえば set phrase.

### ～ばそれまでだ vs ～までだ・～までのことだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>それまでだ</code> = **the situation ends** and nothing
can be done. <code>までだ</code> = **I will simply do this instead**, and the
speaker is quite calm about it.

</div>

<pre>
断(ことわ)られたら、自分(じぶん)でやる<b>までだ</b>。
If they turn me down, I'll simply do it myself.
</pre>

<pre>
頼(たの)める人(ひと)がいなくなってしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>。
Once there is no one left to ask, that is the end of it.
</pre>

They look alike and mean opposite things. までだ announces the speaker's fallback
and is faintly defiant; それまでだ says there is no fallback at all. The giveaway is
what precedes までだ: a **verb of intended action**, not a disaster.

### ～ばそれまでだ vs ～たが最後(さいご)・～たら最後(さいご)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たが最後(さいご)</code> marks the **trigger**, and the
sentence carries on to say what dreadful thing follows.
<code>ばそれまでだ</code> marks the **end**, and nothing follows because there is
nothing more to report.

</div>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)は話(はな)し始(はじ)め<b>たが最後(さいご)</b>、一時間(いちじかん)は止(と)まらない。
Once he starts talking, he won't stop for an hour.
</pre>

<pre>
信用(しんよう)を失(うしな)ってしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>。
Once trust is gone, that is the end of it.
</pre>

Both are literary and both take an irreversible trigger. The structural test is
simple: if your sentence wants to go on and describe the consequences, you need
たが最後(さいご); if the whole point is that there is nothing left to describe,
you want それまでだ.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 落(お)とす<b>ばそれまでだ</b> → ✅ 落(お)とせ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>
  ば attaches to the conditional stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)すれ<b>ばそれまでだ</b> → ✅ 試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)すれば安心(あんしん)だ
  The pattern only reports losses and endings the speaker regrets. Passing an exam
  is not the end of anything.

- ❌ バスに乗(の)り遅(おく)れれ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>（to a friend） → ✅ バスに乗(の)り遅(おく)れたら終(お)わりだよ
  Both the scale and the register are wrong. A missed bus is recoverable, and the
  friend will hear a proverb where a warning was meant.

- ❌ 運(うん)が悪(わる)かったと言(い)ってしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>。（full stop, nothing after） → ✅ …言(い)ってしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>が、…
  In the 言(い)ってしまえば phrase the が is effectively part of the idiom.
  Stopping there leaves the listener waiting for the objection that never comes.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In writing - a magazine feature on traditional crafts, an essay, the closing

It warns that skill is only as durable as the chain of people who pass it on: one
broken link and the whole thing is lost, with no way back.

</details>

The speaker is naming their fallback - "then I'll just do it alone" - which is

continues into action.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 大切(たいせつ)な書類(しょるい)をなくすばそれまでだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 大切(たいせつ)な書類(しょるい)を<b>なくしてしまえばそれまでだ</b>。

ば cannot attach to the dictionary form; なくす becomes なくせば, and the idiomatic
shape adds てしまう to mark the loss as complete -
なくしてしまえば. Both repairs are needed for the sentence to read naturally.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 時代(じだい)のせいだと言(い)ってしまえ<b>ばそれまでだ</b>が、我々(われわれ)にできることもあったはずだ。
(b) 時代(じだい)のせいだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) quotes the easy explanation in order to refuse it. The speaker concedes that
blaming the times would close the discussion, and then reopens it - the が is doing
the real work, and the whole move is a mark of careful, considered argument.

(b) simply asserts the easy explanation, and closes the discussion for good. It is
the position (a) is arguing against.

</details>

# ～ことのないよう(に)

**Meaning** · so that ~ does not happen; taking care that ~ is avoided - the formal, written version of ～ないように

The shape is transparent once you take it apart. ことがない, "there is no such
occurrence", turns its が into の inside a noun-modifying clause, giving
ことのない; ように then makes it a purpose. The whole thing means "in such a way
that there is no occurrence of X". That extra machinery is exactly what gives it its
register: this is the Japanese of notices, announcements and official email, not of
telling your flatmate to be careful.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary form) | Verb + ことのないように | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れることのないように |
| Verb (passive) | ～されることのないよう | 盗(ぬす)まれる → 盗(ぬす)まれることのないよう |
| Plainer variant | ～ことがないように | 遅(おく)れることがないように |
| Very formal (notices) | ～ことのなきよう | 遅(おく)れることのなきようお願(ねが)いいたします |
| With a noun instead of a clause | Noun + のないよう | 事故(じこ)のないようご注意(ちゅうい)ください |
| Completed by a request | ～ことのないよう + ご注意(ちゅうい)ください／お願(ねが)いします | - |
| Completed by an action | ～ことのないように + する／気(き)をつける | 忘(わす)れることのないようにします |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the に is often dropped in written notices:
紛失(ふんしつ)することのないようご注意(ちゅうい)ください. Dropping it makes
the sentence stiffer, not less correct. Keep the に in speech and in ordinary email;
drop it if you are drafting a sign.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
忘(わす)れ物(もの)をする<b>ことのないよう</b>ご注意(ちゅうい)ください。
Please take care not to leave any belongings behind.
</pre>

<pre>
受験票(じゅけんひょう)をなくす<b>ことのないように</b>してください。
Please make sure you do not lose your examination slip.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じミスを繰(く)り返(かえ)す<b>ことのないよう</b>、手順(てじゅん)を見直(みなお)しました。
We have revised the procedure so that the same mistake is not repeated.
</pre>

<pre>
通路(つうろ)に荷物(にもつ)を置(お)き、他(ほか)のお客様(きゃくさま)のご迷惑(めいわく)となる<b>ことのないよう</b>お願(ねが)いいたします。
Please do not place luggage in the aisle where it may inconvenience other passengers.
</pre>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)期限(きげん)を過(す)ぎる<b>ことのないよう</b>、各自(かくじ)管理(かんり)してください。
Please manage your own schedule so that the submission deadline is not missed.
</pre>

<pre>
個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)が外部(がいぶ)に漏(も)れる<b>ことのないよう</b>、厳重(げんじゅう)に管理(かんり)しております。
Personal data is kept under strict control so that it cannot leak outside the company.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)の皆様(みなさま)には、当日(とうじつ)受付(うけつけ)で混乱(こんらん)が生(しょう)じる<b>ことのないよう</b>、事前(じぜん)の登録(とうろく)をお願(ねが)いしております。
To avoid any confusion at the reception desk on the day, we ask all participants to register in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
強(つよ)い風(かぜ)で看板(かんばん)が倒(たお)れ、通行人(つうこうにん)にけがをさせる<b>ことのないよう</b>、固定(こてい)を確認(かくにん)してください。
Please check that the signs are secured, so that they cannot blow over and injure a passer-by.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **formal but entirely usable**. Unlike some N1 material, it is not archaic:
you will meet it every day on station notices, in HR announcements, in safety
briefings and in the polite email that asks the whole department to do something.
And a learner writing a careful business email can produce it correctly and sound
exactly right.

What it adds over plain ないように is **distance and impersonality**. ないように
names an action the listener should avoid; ことのないよう names an *event* that
should not occur, which quietly removes the accusation. That is why an announcement
prefers it - the station is not telling *you* off, it is describing a state of
affairs to be avoided. The same instinct makes the passive so common here:
盗(ぬす)まれることのないよう rather than 盗(ぬす)まれないように.

The sentence usually finishes in one of two ways. With a **request** to somebody
else - ご注意(ちゅうい)ください, お願(ねが)いいたします,
お気(き)をつけください - which is the notice pattern. Or with an **action the speaker has taken**
- 見直(みなお)しました, 管理(かんり)しております - which is the report or
apology pattern.

<code>ことのなきよう</code>, with the classical なき, is a further step up and
belongs to formal written notices and set business phrases. Recognise it; you will
almost never need to write it.

## Contrast

### ～ことのないように vs ～ないように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning, one register apart.
<code>ないように</code> is everyday; <code>ことのないよう</code> is the notice,
the announcement and the formal email.

</div>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を忘(わす)れ<b>ないように</b>ね。
Don't forget your umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
お忘(わす)れ物(もの)のない<b>よう</b>、足(あし)もとをご確認(かくにん)ください。
Please check around you so that nothing is left behind.
</pre>

Said to a friend, the long form would be comic. Printed on a train door, the short
form would sound abrupt. Neither is more correct; they simply live in different
places.

### ～ことのないように vs ～ないでください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ないでください</code> tells the listener **not to do**
something. <code>ことのないよう</code> asks them to **arrange things so that it
does not happen**, which is softer and less direct.

</div>

<pre>
ここに自転車(じてんしゃ)を止(と)め<b>ないでください</b>。
Please do not park bicycles here.
</pre>

<pre>
通行(つうこう)の妨(さまた)げとなる<b>ことのないよう</b>、駐輪場(ちゅうりんじょう)をご利用(りよう)ください。
Please use the bicycle park, so as not to obstruct the pavement.
</pre>

The first prohibits and points at the reader. The second names the outcome to be
avoided and then makes a positive request, which is why organisations reach for it
when they want to be firm without sounding cross.

### ～ことのないよう vs ～ことのなきよう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>なき</code> is the classical form of ない. Same meaning,
one more notch of formality, and effectively confined to **written** notices and set
business phrases.

</div>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りに遅(おく)れる<b>ことのないよう</b>お願(ねが)いします。
Please make sure the deadline is not missed.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りに遅(おく)れる<b>ことのなきよう</b>お願(ねが)い申(もう)し上(あ)げます。
We would ask that the deadline not be missed.
</pre>

The second belongs in a printed circular or a formal letter, alongside
お願(ねが)い申(もう)し上(あ)げます. In an ordinary work email it would read
as over-dressed.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 忘(わす)れない<b>ことのないように</b> → ✅ 忘(わす)れる<b>ことのないように</b>
  The negative is already inside ことのない. Adding another one reverses the
  meaning: the sentence now asks people to make sure they *do* forget.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れた<b>ことのないよう</b> → ✅ 遅(おく)れる<b>ことのないよう</b>
  The verb stays in the dictionary form - the event has not happened yet, which is
  the whole point.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)な<b>ことのないよう</b> → ✅ 事故(じこ)の<b>ないよう</b>
  With a plain noun, drop こと and use の directly:
  事故(じこ)のないよう, 間違(まちが)いのないよう.

- ❌ 遅刻(ちこく)する<b>ことのないよう</b>ね。（to a friend） → ✅ 遅刻(ちこく)し<b>ないように</b>ね。
  Not a grammar error. The formal frame with a casual ね on the end is a register
  collision, and it sounds sarcastic.

## Practice

**1.** 貴重品(きちょうひん)を席(せき)に置(お)いたまま離(はな)れる____、ご注意(ちゅうい)ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

離(はな)れる<b>ことのないよう</b>、ご注意(ちゅうい)ください。

The sentence ends in ご注意(ちゅうい)ください, which is the announcement shape,
so the formal ことのないよう is the right register. Note the dictionary form
離(はな)れる and the dropped に, both normal in printed notices.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （宿題(しゅくだい)を忘(わす)れることのないように・宿題(しゅくだい)を忘(わす)れないように）ね、また明日(あした)。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>忘(わす)れないように</b>ね、また明日(あした)。

Said in parting to a child or a friend, the plain ないように is the only natural
choice. ことのないよう belongs to notices, and paired with ね it would sound as
though you were reading from a memo.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a line for a printed notice: "Please keep your ticket safe so that it is not lost."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

紛失(ふんしつ)する<b>ことのないよう</b>、大切(たいせつ)に保管(ほかん)してください。

The purpose clause comes first and the request second - that order is standard for
notices. 紛失(ふんしつ)する is the register-appropriate verb; なくす would be
correct but conversational.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)を繰(く)り返(かえ)さないことのないよう、記録(きろく)を残(のこ)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)を<b>繰(く)り返(かえ)すことのないよう</b>、記録(きろく)を残(のこ)します。

ことのない already supplies the negative. With 繰(く)り返(かえ)さない in front
of it the sentence promises to make sure the mistake is *not not* repeated - the
opposite of what was meant.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 入(い)り口(ぐち)を塞(ふさ)が<b>ないでください</b>。
(b) 入(い)り口(ぐち)を塞(ふさ)ぐ<b>ことのないよう</b>ご協力(きょうりょく)ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a direct prohibition aimed at the reader. It is clear, and on a small sign it
is perfectly normal, but it does point a finger.

(b) names the situation to be avoided rather than the reader's behaviour, and then
asks for cooperation instead of compliance. It is longer, more impersonal and
distinctly more polite - the register a station, a hospital or a company uses when
it wants to be firm without being sharp.

</details>

# ～に至る・～に至った

**Meaning** · to reach the point of; to culminate in - read <code>に至(いた)る</code>

至(いた)る is a formal verb meaning "to arrive at, to reach". As a grammar point
it takes the end point of a long or difficult process - an agreement, a break-up, a
resignation, a war - and reports that things finally got that far. The road behind
matters as much as the destination: <code>に至(いた)る</code> always implies
that something has been building. It is also the base form of a small family, since
に至(いた)って, に至(いた)っては and に至(いた)っても are all built on it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に至(いた)る | 合意(ごうい) → 合意(ごうい)に至(いた)る |
| Noun (achieved) | Noun + に至(いた)った | 離婚(りこん) → 離婚(りこん)に至(いた)った |
| Verb (dictionary form) | Verb + に至(いた)る | 辞任(じにん)する → 辞任(じにん)するに至(いた)った |
| Continuing to the present | ～に至(いた)っている | 今日(こんにち)に至(いた)っている |
| Negative - "it did not go that far" | ～には至(いた)らない／至(いた)らなかった | 大(おお)きな被害(ひがい)には至(いた)らなかった |
| Modifying a noun | ～に至(いた)った + Noun | 事故(じこ)に至(いた)った経緯(けいい) |
| Spatial range | ～から～に至(いた)る | 北(きた)から南(みなみ)に至(いた)る |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negative <code>には至(いた)らなかった</code> is at least as
common in news copy as the affirmative. It is the standard way of saying that
something serious was narrowly avoided: けがには至(いた)らなかった,
中止(ちゅうし)には至(いた)らなかった.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
長(なが)い話(はな)し合(あ)いの末(すえ)、ようやく合意(ごうい)に<b>至(いた)った</b>。
After long discussions they finally reached an agreement.
</pre>

<pre>
火(ひ)は早(はや)く消(け)し止(と)められ、大(おお)きな被害(ひがい)には<b>至(いた)らなかった</b>。
The fire was put out quickly and did not develop into serious damage.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も話(はな)し合(あ)ったが、結局(けっきょく)離婚(りこん)に<b>至(いた)った</b>。
They talked it over many times, but in the end it came to divorce.
</pre>

<pre>
小(ちい)さな工房(こうぼう)から始(はじ)まり、世界(せかい)に店(みせ)を持(も)つ今日(こんにち)に<b>至(いた)っている</b>。
It began as a small workshop and has grown into the company it is today, with shops around the world.
</pre>

<pre>
不正(ふせい)が明(あか)るみに出(で)て、社長(しゃちょう)が辞任(じにん)するに<b>至(いた)った</b>。
The fraud came to light, and it went as far as the president's resignation.
</pre>

<pre>
検査(けんさ)で異常(いじょう)が見(み)つかったが、手術(しゅじゅつ)を受(う)けるには<b>至(いた)らなかった</b>。
The tests turned up an abnormality, but it did not come to needing surgery.
</pre>

<pre>
両国(りょうこく)の対立(たいりつ)は次第(しだい)に深(ふか)まり、ついに国交(こっこう)の断絶(だんぜつ)に<b>至(いた)った</b>。
Relations between the two countries steadily worsened, until they were finally broken off altogether.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)に<b>至(いた)った</b>経緯(けいい)については、報告書(ほうこくしょ)に詳(くわ)しく記(しる)されている。
The sequence of events that led to the accident is set out in detail in the report.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Formal and written.** News reporting, company histories, court documents,
committee reports and academic prose are its home. In speech it appears in a
prepared statement or a briefing, not in conversation; a friend telling you about
their divorce says 別(わか)れることになった, not
離婚(りこん)に至(いた)った.

The verb is **narrative**. It wants a process in front of it, so it collocates with
～た末(すえ), ついに, ようやく, 次第(しだい)に, 何度(なんど)も and with
clauses that describe a build-up. Drop it onto a single quick event and the sentence
becomes pompous.

Unlike 始末(しまつ)だ or 羽目(はめ)になる, <code>に至(いた)る</code> is
**not inherently negative**. 合意(ごうい)に至(いた)る and
完成(かんせい)に至(いた)る are perfectly good. What it does insist on is
**weight**: the end point has to be consequential enough to be worth a long road.
In practice most real examples are grave, because grave things are what get written
up this way.

Three readings are worth pinning down. 今日(こんにち)に至(いた)る is
**こんにち**, "the present day", not きょう. 至(いた)る所(ところ),
"everywhere", is a separate lexical item. And the adverb
至(いた)って, as in 至(いた)って健康(けんこう)だ ("in perfect health"),
means "extremely" and has nothing to do with this pattern despite the shared kanji.

## Contrast

### ～に至(いた)る vs ～になる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>になる</code> simply reports the change.
<code>に至(いた)る</code> insists that a **long road** preceded it, and lifts
the register several notches.

</div>

<pre>
話(はな)し合(あ)いの結果(けっか)、来月(らいげつ)再開(さいかい)すること<b>になりました</b>。
Following discussion, it has been decided to resume next month.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)にわたる交渉(こうしょう)の末(すえ)、ようやく再開(さいかい)に<b>至(いた)った</b>。
After three years of negotiation, it finally came to a resumption.
</pre>

になる can report a change that took an afternoon. に至(いた)る in the same
sentence would promise a history the sentence does not have, and the reader would
wonder what they had missed.

### ～に至(いた)る vs ～に及(およ)ぶ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>及(およ)ぶ</code> **spreads**: it reaches out over a
scale, a duration or a range. <code>至(いた)る</code> **arrives**: it reaches an
end point.

</div>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)は十(じゅう)億円(おくえん)に<b>及(およ)んだ</b>。
The damage ran to a billion yen.
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)の末(すえ)、工事(こうじ)の中止(ちゅうし)に<b>至(いた)った</b>。
The investigation ended in the works being called off.
</pre>

及(およ)ぶ takes amounts, areas and lengths of time -
10年(ねん)に及(およ)ぶ研究(けんきゅう), 全国(ぜんこく)に及(およ)ぶ影響(えいきょう).
It also has the separate use 犯行(はんこう)に及(およ)ぶ, "to go so far as to
commit the act", which is where the two verbs come closest.

### ～に至(いた)る vs ～に達(たっ)する

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>達(たっ)する</code> is for **numbers, levels and
targets**. <code>至(いた)る</code> is for **outcomes and states**.

</div>

<pre>
来場者(らいじょうしゃ)は一万人(いちまんにん)に<b>達(たっ)した</b>。
Attendance reached ten thousand.
</pre>

<pre>
交渉(こうしょう)は合意(ごうい)には<b>至(いた)らなかった</b>。
The talks did not reach agreement.
</pre>

Swapping them is one of the clearest register errors an N1 candidate can make.
Ask what is on the other side of に: a figure takes 達(たっ)する, a situation
takes 至(いた)る.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 駅(えき)に着(つ)く代(か)わりに「駅(えき)に<b>至(いた)りました</b>」 → ✅ 駅(えき)に<b>着(つ)きました</b>
  For physically arriving somewhere, 至(いた)る survives only in route
  descriptions (山頂(さんちょう)に至(いた)る道(みち)). Ordinary arrival is
  着(つ)く.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、パンを買(か)うに<b>至(いた)った</b> → ✅ 昨日(きのう)、パンを<b>買(か)った</b>
  No process, no weight. The verb makes a trivial event sound like the outcome of a
  parliamentary inquiry.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)に至(いた)るまで → ✅ <b>今日(こんにち)</b>に至(いた)るまで
  A reading error, not a grammar one. In this expression 今日(こんにち) means
  "the present day", and きょう would be wrong.

- ❌ 売上(うりあげ)は一億円(いちおくえん)に<b>至(いた)った</b> → ✅ 売上(うりあげ)は一億円(いちおくえん)に<b>達(たっ)した</b>
  A figure is a level, not an outcome, so the verb is 達(たっ)する.

## Practice

**1.** 数(すう)か月(げつ)にわたる交渉(こうしょう)の末(すえ)、ようやく両社(りょうしゃ)は合意(ごうい)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

両社(りょうしゃ)は合意(ごうい)<b>に至(いた)った</b>。

～た末(すえ) and ようやく have already established the long road, which is
exactly the environment 至(いた)る needs. Note the noun 合意(ごうい) directly
before に - no する.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 参加者(さんかしゃ)の数(かず)は五百人(ごひゃくにん)に（至(いた)った・達(たっ)した）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

五百人(ごひゃくにん)に<b>達(たっ)した</b>。

A head count is a figure, and figures take 達(たっ)する.
至(いた)る would suggest that five hundred people was some sort of grave outcome
arrived at after a struggle.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The investigation dragged on for two years and finally led to the closure of the factory."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both verbs appear in their proper places: 及(およ)ぶ for the two-year span, and
至(いた)る for the outcome the whole process ended in. ついに is the natural
adverb to mark the culmination.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 説明(せつめい)が不十分(ふじゅうぶん)で、担当者(たんとうしゃ)が辞(や)めたに至(いた)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 説明(せつめい)が不十分(ふじゅうぶん)で、担当者(たんとうしゃ)が<b>辞(や)めるに至(いた)った</b>。
（or 担当者(たんとうしゃ)の辞任(じにん)<b>に至(いた)った</b>。）

The verb in front of に至(いた)る stays in the dictionary form; the past tense
sits on 至(いた)った. Replacing the clause with the action noun
辞任(じにん) is the more usual choice in formal writing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 工場(こうじょう)は閉鎖(へいさ)すること<b>になった</b>。
(b) 工場(こうじょう)は閉鎖(へいさ)する<b>に至(いた)った</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a decision in neutral, ordinary Japanese. It could be an internal email,
and it says nothing about how long or how painful the road to it was.

(b) frames the closure as the end point of a process the reader is expected to know
about - falling orders, failed negotiations, a scandal. It is written register, it
carries weight, and it invites the question "how did it come to that?", which the
surrounding text usually answers.

</details>

# ～に至っても

**Meaning** · even after things had come to that; even at this late stage - and still nothing changed. Read <code>に至(いた)っても</code>

に至(いた)る gives you the point a process finally reached. Add ても and the
sentence becomes a concession: things had gone all the way to *that*, and the
response that should have come still did not. What follows is nearly always negative
or critical, and the writer's impatience is audible.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a stage or a time) | Noun + に至(いた)っても | 当日(とうじつ) → 当日(とうじつ)に至(いた)っても |
| Verb (dictionary form) | Verb + に至(いた)っても | 死者(ししゃ)が出(で)る → 死者(ししゃ)が出(で)るに至(いた)っても |
| Fixed phrase | 今(いま)に至(いた)っても | 今(いま)に至(いた)っても説明(せつめい)がない |
| Emphatic, written | ～に至(いた)ってもなお | …に至(いた)ってもなお変(か)わらない |
| Sibling: "only when it got that far" | ～に至(いた)って | 事故(じこ)が起(お)きるに至(いた)って、ようやく… |
| Sibling: "as for the worst case" | ～に至(いた)っては | 彼(かれ)に至(いた)っては返事(へんじ)もしない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three siblings differ only by the particle, and the particle
carries the whole meaning. <code>に至(いた)って</code> = *only when it finally
got that far* did something happen. <code>に至(いた)っては</code> = *as for that
one*, picking out the most extreme case. <code>に至(いた)っても</code> = *even
though it got that far*, and still nothing. Learn them as a set or you will pick the
wrong one under time pressure.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)り当日(とうじつ)に<b>至(いた)っても</b>、返事(へんじ)は来(こ)なかった。
Even when the day of the deadline came, no reply arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)に<b>至(いた)っても</b>、原因(げんいん)は分(わ)かっていない。
Even now, the cause remains unknown.
</pre>

<pre>
出発(しゅっぱつ)の前日(ぜんじつ)に<b>至(いた)っても</b>、彼(かれ)は荷造(にづく)りに手(て)をつけていなかった。
Even on the day before departure he had not started packing.
</pre>

<pre>
三度目(さんどめ)の事故(じこ)が起(お)きるに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、会社(かいしゃ)は作業(さぎょう)を止(と)めなかった。
Even after a third accident, the company did not halt the work.
</pre>

<pre>
裁判(さいばん)が始(はじ)まるに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、本人(ほんにん)は事実(じじつ)を認(みと)めようとしなかった。
Even once the trial had begun, he still refused to admit the facts.
</pre>

<pre>
住民(じゅうみん)の反対(はんたい)がこれほど大(おお)きくなるに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、計画(けいかく)は一切(いっさい)見直(みなお)されていない。
Even with local opposition on this scale, the plan has not been reviewed at all.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)の不足(ふそく)が生産(せいさん)停止(ていし)を招(まね)くに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、経営陣(けいえいじん)は問題(もんだい)を認(みと)めなかった。
Even when the shortage of parts brought production to a halt, management would not acknowledge the problem.
</pre>

<pre>
発売(はつばい)から十年(じゅうねん)が過(す)ぎ、部品(ぶひん)の供給(きょうきゅう)が終(お)わるに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、この機械(きかい)を使(つか)い続(つづ)けている工場(こうじょう)がある。
Ten years after release, and even now that spare parts are no longer supplied, there are factories still running these machines.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Formal written Japanese**: reporting, criticism, official complaint, the
discussion section of a report. It is not conversational, and it is not neutral -
the whole point of choosing ても over a plain ても clause is to say that the stage
reached was **extreme enough that a response was overdue**.

It therefore pairs naturally with なお, いまだに, 依然(いぜん)として, まだ,
一切(いっさい) and with a negative predicate. If your sentence has no frustrated
expectation in it, this is the wrong pattern.

The everyday equivalents are ordinary. In speech you would say
<code>こんなことになっても、まだ…</code> or simply
<code>ここまで来(き)ても</code>. There is also the spoken idiom
<code>この期(ご)に及(およ)んで</code>, "at this late stage, after all
this" - close in feeling and much more colloquial. Note its reading: 期(ご),
not き.

One more caution. The stage named in front of に至(いた)っても has to be
**serious**. A trivial time point makes the sentence pompous rather than pointed,
which is why real examples cluster around deadlines that have arrived, trials that
have opened, accidents that have repeated and damage that has already been done.

## Contrast

### ～に至(いた)っても vs ～に至(いた)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に至(いた)って</code>: the response finally **did**
come, but only at that late stage. <code>に至(いた)っても</code>: even at that
stage, it **still did not**.

</div>

<pre>
死者(ししゃ)が出(で)るに<b>至(いた)って</b>、ようやく対策(たいさく)が取(と)られた。
It took a death before any measures were finally taken.
</pre>

<pre>
死者(ししゃ)が出(で)るに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、対策(たいさく)は取(と)られなかった。
Even after a death, no measures were taken.
</pre>

Both are critical, and they criticise different things: the first the delay, the
second the total absence of a response. ようやく belongs with に至(いた)って;
まだ and いまだに belong with に至(いた)っても.

### ～に至(いた)っても vs ～に至(いた)っては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に至(いた)っては</code> picks out **the worst example
in a list** - "and as for X…". <code>に至(いた)っても</code> marks **a stage
reached in time** and concedes it.

</div>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)い人(ひと)は多(おお)いが、彼(かれ)に<b>至(いた)っては</b>一度(いちど)も返(かえ)してこない。
Plenty of people are slow to reply, but as for him, he has never replied once.
</pre>

<pre>
催促(さいそく)を三度(さんど)送(おく)るに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、返事(へんじ)はなかった。
Even after three reminders had been sent, there was no reply.
</pre>

は needs something to contrast against - a group of cases, of which one is the
extreme. も needs a point on a timeline that ought to have triggered a response.
Getting these two round the wrong way is the commonest error in the family.

### ～に至(いた)っても vs ～ても・にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three concede. The plain <code>ても</code> and
<code>にもかかわらず</code> are **neutral**; <code>に至(いた)っても</code> adds
that the point conceded is an **extreme one**, reached after a long build-up.

</div>

<pre>
何度(なんど)注意(ちゅうい)し<b>ても</b>、直(なお)らなかった。
However often he was warned, it made no difference.
</pre>

<pre>
処分(しょぶん)を受(う)けるに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、態度(たいど)は変(か)わらなかった。
Even when it came to a formal reprimand, his attitude did not change.
</pre>

If your English is simply "even though", ても is enough. Reach for
に至(いた)っても only when you want the reader to feel how far things had gone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 事故(じこ)が起(お)きたに<b>至(いた)っても</b> → ✅ 事故(じこ)が起(お)きるに<b>至(いた)っても</b>
  The verb before に至(いた)る stays in the dictionary form, however far in the
  past the event is.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)が起(お)きるに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、ようやく対策(たいさく)が取(と)られた → ✅ 事故(じこ)が起(お)きるに<b>至(いた)って</b>、ようやく対策(たいさく)が取(と)られた
  ても requires a **frustrated** expectation. If the response finally did arrive,
  the particle is plain に至(いた)って.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)に<b>至(いた)っても</b>挨拶(あいさつ)もしない → ✅ 彼(かれ)に<b>至(いた)っては</b>挨拶(あいさつ)もしない
  Singling out a person as the extreme case takes は.

- ❌ お昼(ひる)に<b>至(いた)っても</b>お腹(なか)がすかない → ✅ お昼(ひる)になってもお腹(なか)がすかない
  Lunchtime is not a grave stage in a deteriorating situation. The heavy form makes
  a small observation sound like a public inquiry.

## Practice

The completion date is the stage that should have forced progress, and the negative

noun attaches directly, with no する.

</details>

</details>

(b) 事故(じこ)から三年(さんねん)が過(す)ぎるに<b>至(いた)っても</b>、補償(ほしょう)の話(はなし)は進(すす)んでいない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain, neutral concession. Three years have passed and nothing has moved;
the sentence reports it and could appear in any register.

(b) frames those three years as a point that had been reached after a long and
visible process, and the heavier form carries the writer's judgement: by this stage
the delay is no longer explicable, it is a failure. It belongs in a critical article
or a formal complaint, and it would sound stilted in conversation.

</details>

# ～を余儀なくさせる

**Meaning** · to leave someone no alternative but ~; to force ~ upon them - read <code>余儀(よぎ)なくさせる</code>

This is the mirror image of を余儀(よぎ)なくされる. There the sufferer was the
subject; here the **cause** is the subject, and the sentence says that this
circumstance closed off every other option. The noun before を is the unwelcome
course of action that was forced, and the party it was forced on - if named at all -
takes に.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cause + forced action | Noun(cause) + が + Noun + を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる | 大雪(おおゆき)が中止(ちゅうし)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせた |
| With the party affected | …が + Noun(person) + に + Noun + を… | 洪水(こうずい)が住民(じゅうみん)に避難(ひなん)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせた |
| Modified action noun | ～の + Noun | 計画(けいかく)の変更(へんこう)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせた |
| する-verb | drop する | 縮小(しゅくしょう)する → 縮小(しゅくしょう)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる |
| Ongoing | ～を余儀(よぎ)なくさせている | 減産(げんさん)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせている |
| Polite | ～を余儀(よぎ)なくさせました | 中断(ちゅうだん)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせました |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the subject is normally **impersonal**: weather, prices, a shortage, a
regulation, a piece of news. A named human being as the subject reads oddly, because
a person with authority to make you do something takes the ordinary causative
instead - 部長(ぶちょう)が残業(ざんぎょう)させた, not
❌部長(ぶちょう)が残業(ざんぎょう)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせた.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)が試合(しあい)の中止(ちゅうし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
The heavy snow forced the match to be cancelled.
</pre>

<pre>
けがが彼(かれ)に引退(いんたい)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
An injury forced him into retirement.
</pre>

<pre>
円安(えんやす)が多(おお)くの企業(きぎょう)に値上(ねあ)げを<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせている</b>。
The weak yen is forcing many firms to raise their prices.
</pre>

<pre>
資材(しざい)の高騰(こうとう)が工事(こうじ)の中断(ちゅうだん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせました</b>。
The steep rise in materials costs forced the works to be suspended.
</pre>

<pre>
川(かわ)の氾濫(はんらん)は、流域(りゅういき)の住民(じゅうみん)に長期(ちょうき)の避難(ひなん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
The flooding of the river forced people living along it into a long evacuation.
</pre>

<pre>
人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)が、地方(ちほう)の路線(ろせん)バスに減便(げんびん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせている</b>。
A shortage of drivers is forcing rural bus routes to cut services.
</pre>

<pre>
想定(そうてい)を超(こ)える申(もう)し込(こ)みが、主催者(しゅさいしゃ)に会場(かいじょう)の変更(へんこう)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
Applications far beyond anything anticipated left the organisers no choice but to change the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あら)たな規制(きせい)は、各社(かくしゃ)に生産(せいさん)体制(たいせい)の見直(みなお)しを<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせる</b>ことになるだろう。
The new regulations will oblige every company to rethink the way it manufactures.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Even more **written** than the passive. It belongs to analytical prose - news
commentary, business reporting, economic and historical writing - where the sentence
wants an abstract cause standing in the subject slot and a consequence stretching out
from it. Nobody says it in conversation.

There is a stylistic point worth knowing. Japanese normally prefers the affected
party as the topic, so many writers, offered the choice, will write
<code>大雪(おおゆき)で試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)を余儀(よぎ)なくされた</code>
rather than the causative. Some editors regard the さ**せ**る version as faintly
translation-flavoured, an English "X forced Y to Z" wearing Japanese clothes. That is
a tendency and a matter of taste, not a rule: the causative is standard, appears
constantly in print, and is the right choice when the **cause itself is the topic of
the paragraph** - when you have been discussing the weak yen, or the shortage, or
the new law, and want it to keep the subject position.

Everything else carries over from the passive. The forced action is a **noun**, the
する of a する-verb is dropped, and the outcome is always unwelcome for the party it
lands on.

## Contrast

### ～を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる vs ～を余儀(よぎ)なくされる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same event, told from opposite ends. **させる**: the cause
is the subject. **される**: the sufferer is the subject.

</div>

<pre>
停電(ていでん)が工場(こうじょう)に生産(せいさん)の停止(ていし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
The power cut forced the plant to stop production.
</pre>

<pre>
停電(ていでん)により、工場(こうじょう)は生産(せいさん)の停止(ていし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。
Because of the power cut, the plant was forced to stop production.
</pre>

Both are correct and both are formal. Choose by asking what the paragraph is about:
if it is about the power cut, keep the power cut as subject; if it is about the
factory, use the passive. Mixing them up - putting the cause in front of された - is
the single most common error with this pair.

### ～を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる vs ～させる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the plain causative <code>させる</code> means somebody with
**authority made** you do it. <code>を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる</code> means
circumstances **removed the alternatives**, with nobody giving an order.

</div>

<pre>
監督(かんとく)が選手(せんしゅ)を交代(こうたい)<b>させた</b>。
The manager substituted the player.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)なけがが選手(せんしゅ)の交代(こうたい)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
A sudden injury forced a substitution.
</pre>

The causative needs an agent who decides; the 余儀(よぎ)なく form needs a
circumstance that decides for everybody. Note the grammar difference too:
させる takes a verb, を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる takes a noun.

### ～を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる vs ～をもたらす・～を引(ひ)き起(お)こす

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>もたらす</code> and
<code>引(ひ)き起(お)こす</code> say the cause **produced a result**.
<code>を余儀(よぎ)なくさせる</code> says it **took away someone's choices**.

</div>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)は各地(かくち)に大(おお)きな被害(ひがい)を<b>もたらした</b>。
The typhoon brought serious damage to many areas.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)は各地(かくち)に避難(ひなん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
The typhoon forced evacuations in many areas.
</pre>

Damage simply happens; evacuation is something people had to decide to do, with no
real alternative left. That is the test - if there is no human choice being closed
off, use もたらす or 引(ひ)き起(お)こす.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 大雪(おおゆき)が中止(ちゅうし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b> → ✅ 大雪(おおゆき)が中止(ちゅうし)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>
  With the cause in the subject slot the verb has to be the causative. As written,
  the sentence says the snow was the one left with no choice.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)が私(わたし)に残業(ざんぎょう)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b> → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)が私(わたし)に残業(ざんぎょう)<b>させた</b>
  A person issuing an instruction takes the plain causative. The
  余儀(よぎ)なく form wants an impersonal cause and sounds inflated with a human
  subject.

- ❌ 不況(ふきょう)が工場(こうじょう)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b> → ✅ 不況(ふきょう)が工場(こうじょう)の閉鎖(へいさ)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>
  The noun taking を must be the **action forced**, not the thing affected. The
  affected party belongs with に or inside a の phrase.

- ❌ 円安(えんやす)が値上(ねあ)げするを<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b> → ✅ 円安(えんやす)が値上(ねあ)げを<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>
  を needs a bare noun. Drop the する.

## Practice

**1.** 原油(げんゆ)価格(かかく)の上昇(じょうしょう)が、各社(かくしゃ)に運賃(うんちん)の値上(ねあ)げを____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

値上(ねあ)げを<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>

The oil price is the cause and holds the subject slot, so the causative is required.
The companies, as the party whose options were closed off, take に.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 地震(じしん)により、多(おお)くの世帯(せたい)が避難(ひなん)を（余儀(よぎ)なくされた・余儀(よぎ)なくさせた）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

多(おお)くの世帯(せたい)が避難(ひなん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。

The subject here is the households, and the cause has already been demoted into the
により phrase. With the sufferer as subject, the passive is the only option.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The shortage of parts forced the company to cut production by half."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部品(ぶひん)の不足(ふそく)が、会社(かいしゃ)に生産(せいさん)の半減(はんげん)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。

"The shortage forced the company to…" maps directly onto the causative frame: cause
as subject, company with に, and the forced action as a noun. Note the reading
不足(ふそく) here - it is ふそく after の, and only becomes ぶそく inside a
compound such as 人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく).

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 悪天候(あくてんこう)が登山隊(とざんたい)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 悪天候(あくてんこう)が登山隊(とざんたい)に<b>下山(げざん)を余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。

As written, the sentence forces the climbing party itself rather than any action.
The を slot has to hold the action that was forced - here 下山(げざん) - and the
party affected moves to に.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 感染(かんせん)の拡大(かくだい)が、多(おお)くの店(みせ)に休業(きゅうぎょう)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくさせた</b>。
(b) 感染(かんせん)の拡大(かくだい)により、多(おお)くの店(みせ)が休業(きゅうぎょう)を<b>余儀(よぎ)なくされた</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The event is identical; what differs is where the reader's attention sits.

(a) keeps the spread of infection in the subject position and presents the closures
as one of its effects. It suits a paragraph whose subject is the epidemic itself -
analysis, commentary, a chapter on causes.

(b) makes the shops the topic and the epidemic mere background. It suits a paragraph
about the businesses, and it is the more usual shape in ordinary news copy, since
Japanese generally prefers the affected party as topic.

</details>
