# N1 - No need, not worth, and going too far


---

# ～に足る / ～に足りる

**Meaning** · worth doing; enough to deserve it - the thing clears the bar that the action demands

足(た)る and 足(た)りる both mean "to suffice". Put after an action, they say
that something is **good enough to justify** that action: worth trusting, worth
respecting, worth examining. This is not a statement that you have enough of
something - it is a verdict that the thing has earned the action.

## Formation

足(た)る is the older う-verb form of 足(た)りる (a る-verb). Both are current
in this pattern; 足(た)る is the stiffer of the two.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + に足(た)る | 信頼(しんらい)する → 信頼(しんらい)するに足(た)る |
| Verb | dictionary form + に足(た)りる | 尊敬(そんけい)する → 尊敬(そんけい)するに足(た)りる |
| Noun (する-noun) | noun + に足(た)る | 信頼(しんらい) → 信頼(しんらい)に足(た)る |
| Modifying a noun | ～に足(た)る + noun | 尊敬(そんけい)に足(た)る人物(じんぶつ) |
| As a predicate | ～に足(た)るものだ | 評価(ひょうか)するに足(た)るものだ |
| Negative | ～に足(た)りない ／ ～に足(た)らない | 取(と)るに足(た)らない |
| Polite | ～に足(た)ります ／ ～に足(た)りません | 検討(けんとう)に足(た)ります |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the two verbs have separate negatives. 足(た)りる gives
<code>足(た)りない</code>; 足(た)る is an う-verb, so it gives
<code>足(た)らない</code>, never ❌<code>足(た)るない</code>. Both negatives
are used with this pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>信頼(しんらい)に足(た)る</b>友人(ゆうじん)だ。
He is a friend worth trusting.
</pre>

<pre>
この計画(けいかく)は検討(けんとう)する<b>に足(た)りる</b>。
This plan is worth looking at seriously.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の成果(せいか)は十分(じゅうぶん)に評価(ひょうか)する<b>に足(た)る</b>ものだ。
What was achieved this time thoroughly deserves to be recognised.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の証言(しょうげん)には、法廷(ほうてい)で採用(さいよう)する<b>に足(た)る</b>裏付(うらづ)けがない。
His testimony lacks the corroboration a court would need before accepting it.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は一課(いっか)を任(まか)せる<b>に足(た)りる</b>経験(けいけん)を積(つ)んできた。
She has built up enough experience to be entrusted with the section.
</pre>

<pre>
この報告書(ほうこくしょ)は、方針(ほうしん)を見直(みなお)す<b>に足(た)る</b>根拠(こんきょ)を示(しめ)している。
This report sets out grounds solid enough to justify rethinking the policy.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)現場(げんば)を支(ささ)えてきた彼(かれ)の判断(はんだん)は、全面的(ぜんめんてき)に信(しん)じる<b>に足(た)る</b>と私(わたし)は思(おも)っている。
After all his years holding the front line together, I consider his judgement entirely worthy of trust.
</pre>

<pre>
受賞(じゅしょう)の知(し)らせを聞(き)いて、あの地道(じみち)な努力(どりょく)はやはり<b>賞賛(しょうさん)に足(た)る</b>ものだったと改(あらた)めて感(かん)じた。
Hearing about the award, I felt all over again that such patient work really did deserve the praise.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and almost entirely **written**: reports, reviews, editorials, court
language, references and speeches of appreciation. In speech it appears only in
prepared remarks. The everyday equivalent is
<code>価値(かち)がある</code> or <code>十分(じゅうぶん)だ</code>.

The verbs that go in front are a narrow, evaluative set:
信頼(しんらい)する, 尊敬(そんけい)する, 評価(ひょうか)する,
検討(けんとう)する, 賞賛(しょうさん)する, 注目(ちゅうもく)する,
信(しん)じる, 語(かた)る. All of them are acts of judgement. An ordinary
preference does not fit the slot: ❌この店(みせ)は行(い)くに足(た)る is
not Japanese, it is a translation of "worth going to".

Two shapes carry most of the real usage. One is the noun-modifying frame
<code>信頼(しんらい)に足(た)る情報(じょうほう)</code>, "information one
can rely on", which is standard in journalism. The other is the negative -
<code>取(と)るに足(た)らない</code>, "trivial" - which is commoner than any
affirmative use and is treated as a pattern in its own right.

Between the two verbs, 足(た)る leans literary and fixed and 足(た)りる
leans slightly plainer; in the set phrases (信頼(しんらい)に足(た)る,
取(と)るに足(た)らない) only 足(た)る sounds right.

## Contrast

### ～に足(た)る vs ～にたえる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に足(た)る</code> says the thing has **earned** the
action. `にたえる` says the thing **survives** the action - it holds up when you
actually look, listen or read.

</div>

<pre>
この論文(ろんぶん)は引用(いんよう)する<b>に足(た)る</b>内容(ないよう)を備(そな)えている。
This paper has enough substance to be worth citing.
</pre>

<pre>
素人(しろうと)の作品(さくひん)だが、十分(じゅうぶん)鑑賞(かんしょう)<b>にたえる</b>。
It's an amateur piece, but it holds up perfectly well as something to look at.
</pre>

The first is a verdict on merit; the second is about withstanding scrutiny. Where
they overlap - a book worth reading - both are possible, but `にたえる` implies
the thing survives a demanding audience, while <code>に足(た)る</code> implies
it deserves that audience's time in the first place.

### ～に足(た)る vs ～の価値(かち)がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same idea, different room.
<code>価値(かち)がある</code> is what you say out loud;
<code>に足(た)る</code> is what you write in the report.

</div>

<pre>
この映画(えいが)は一度(いちど)見(み)る<b>価値(かち)がある</b>よ。
This film is worth seeing once.
</pre>

<pre>
当該(とうがい)事業(じぎょう)は継続(けいぞく)する<b>に足(た)る</b>と判断(はんだん)した。
We concluded that the project in question merits continuation.
</pre>

<code>価値(かち)がある</code> also takes anything at all as its subject - a
film, a trip, a coffee. <code>に足(た)る</code> stays with things that are
being formally assessed.

### ～に足(た)る vs ～だけの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけの` measures an **amount** that is sufficient.
<code>に足(た)る</code> passes a **judgement** that something is worthy.

</div>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)を養(やしな)う<b>だけの</b>収入(しゅうにゅう)はある。
I earn enough to support a family.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)を養(やしな)う<b>に足(た)る</b>覚悟(かくご)が君(きみ)にあるのか。
Do you have the resolve that supporting a family deserves?
</pre>

Money is counted, so `だけの` fits it. Resolve is assessed, so
<code>に足(た)る</code> fits that. Swapping them makes the income sound noble
and the resolve sound measurable.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 信頼(しんらい)に<b>足(た)るない</b> → ✅ 信頼(しんらい)に<b>足(た)らない</b>
  足(た)る is an う-verb, so its negative is 足(た)らない, exactly like
  取(と)る → 取(と)らない. (The る-verb 足(た)りる gives
  足(た)りない, which is also correct here.)

- ❌ 信頼(しんらい)に足(た)りる<b>の</b>人(ひと) → ✅ 信頼(しんらい)に<b>足(た)りる</b>人(ひと)
  This is a verb modifying a noun directly. No の comes between them.

- ❌ この店(みせ)のラーメンは食(た)べる<b>に足(た)る</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)のラーメンは食(た)べる<b>価値(かち)がある</b>。
  The pattern is for formal evaluation, not for recommending lunch. English
  "worth ～ing" covers both; Japanese does not.

- ❌ この案(あん)は検討(けんとう)する<b>に足(た)るです</b>。 → ✅ この案(あん)は検討(けんとう)する<b>に足(た)ります</b>。
  足(た)る is a verb, so です cannot follow it. Use 足(た)ります, or
  <code>に足(た)るものです</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 複数(ふくすう)の資料(しりょう)で裏付(うらづ)けられており、信頼(しんらい)____情報(じょうほう)だと言(い)える。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

信頼(しんらい)<b>に足(た)る</b>情報(じょうほう)

The frame <code>信頼(しんらい)に足(た)る + noun</code> is the standard way
to say "information one can rely on" in written Japanese, and the corroboration
mentioned first is exactly the ground such a judgement rests on.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 旅費(りょひ)を払(はら)う（に足(た)る・だけの）貯金(ちょきん)がやっとできた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

旅費(りょひ)を払(はら)う<b>だけの</b>貯金(ちょきん)

Savings are an amount, and the sentence is counting whether there is enough of
them. <code>に足(た)る</code> would turn a bank balance into a moral verdict
on the trip.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "There is not yet enough evidence to warrant reopening the investigation."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

捜査(そうさ)を再開(さいかい)する<b>に足(た)る</b>証拠(しょうこ)はまだない。

"Enough to warrant" is the core sense of the pattern, and an official judgement
about whether a threshold has been met is exactly the register it belongs to.
<code>に足(た)りる</code> would also be grammatical, but 足(た)る is the
form used in this kind of writing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)の話(はなし)は証拠(しょうこ)がなく、信(しん)じるに足(た)るないと思(おも)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)の話(はなし)は証拠(しょうこ)がなく、信(しん)じるに<b>足(た)りない</b>と思(おも)う。
"There's no evidence behind what he says, so I don't think it is worth believing."

❌足(た)るない does not exist. Use either the る-verb negative
足(た)りない or the う-verb negative 足(た)らない; both are current, and
足(た)りない is the safer default.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この作品(さくひん)は鑑賞(かんしょう)<b>にたえる</b>。
(b) この作品(さくひん)は鑑賞(かんしょう)する<b>に足(た)る</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the work **holds up** when you actually sit down in front of it. It
often carries a faint concession - it may be an amateur piece, or an old one, but
it does not embarrass itself.

(b) says the work has **earned** your attention: it is good enough that looking
at it is justified. This is the language of a formal review, and it praises more
directly than (a) does.

</details>

# ～に足りない

**Meaning** · not worth doing; too small to deserve the trouble - beneath notice, beneath fear, beneath argument

This is the negative side of <code>に足(た)る</code>, and it is used far more
often than the affirmative. It says the thing does **not clear the bar**: it is
not worth taking seriously, not worth being afraid of, not worth arguing about.
The most frequent form by a wide margin is
<code>取(と)るに足(た)りない</code>, "trivial, negligible", which behaves
like a ready-made adjective.

## Formation

The verb in front is in the **dictionary form**. 足(た)りない is the negative
of the る-verb 足(た)りる; 足(た)らない is the negative of the う-verb
足(た)る. Both appear, and the fixed phrases prefer 足(た)らない.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + に足(た)りない | 恐(おそ)れる → 恐(おそ)れるに足(た)りない |
| Verb (う-verb negative) | dictionary form + に足(た)らない | 取(と)る → 取(と)るに足(た)らない |
| Noun (する-noun) | noun + に足(た)りない | 信頼(しんらい) → 信頼(しんらい)に足(た)りない |
| Modifying a noun | ～に足(た)りない + noun | 取(と)るに足(た)りない問題(もんだい) |
| Past | ～に足(た)りなかった | 論(ろん)じるに足(た)りなかった |
| Polite | ～に足(た)りません | 恐(おそ)れるに足(た)りません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not confuse this with the ordinary 足(た)りない you learned at
N5. <code>お金(かね)が足(た)りない</code> means there is not enough money.
<code>恐(おそ)れるに足(た)りない</code> means the thing is not worth fearing.
The particle に and the dictionary-form verb in front are what mark the grammar
point.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そんな噂(うわさ)は恐(おそ)れる<b>に足(た)りない</b>。
Rumours like that are nothing to be afraid of.
</pre>

<pre>
これは<b>取(と)るに足(た)りない</b>問題(もんだい)だ。
This is a trivial issue.
</pre>

<pre>
根拠(こんきょ)のない批判(ひはん)なので、いちいち論(ろん)じる<b>に足(た)りない</b>。
The criticism has no basis, so it is not worth answering point by point.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)の実力(じつりょく)を考(かんが)えれば、今回(こんかい)の敗戦(はいせん)は驚(おどろ)く<b>に足(た)りない</b>。
Given how strong the opposition are, this defeat is hardly a surprise.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)の上(うえ)では<b>取(と)るに足(た)らない</b>差(さ)だが、現場(げんば)では大(おお)きな意味(いみ)を持(も)つ。
On paper the gap is negligible, but on the ground it matters a great deal.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)一人(ひとり)の力(ちから)など<b>取(と)るに足(た)りない</b>と思(おも)っていたが、集(あつ)まれば話(はなし)は別(べつ)だった。
I used to think one person's efforts counted for nothing, but together it turned out to be another matter.
</pre>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)と呼(よ)ぶ<b>に足(た)りない</b>断片(だんぺん)をいくら並(なら)べても、主張(しゅちょう)は通(とお)らないだろう。
No matter how many scraps too slight to be called evidence you line up, the argument will not carry.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)の私(わたし)には重大(じゅうだい)な悩(なや)みだったが、今(いま)思(おも)えば<b>取(と)るに足(た)らない</b>ことだった。
At the time it weighed on me terribly; looking back, it was nothing at all.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and formal, and above all **dismissive**. The speaker is not describing a
size, they are refusing to take something seriously - which is why the pattern is
at home in editorials, criticism, court judgements and confident prose, and why
it can sting when applied to a person's work.

<code>取(と)るに足(た)りない</code> and
<code>取(と)るに足(た)らない</code> are effectively one word meaning
"trivial", and they modify nouns freely:
取(と)るに足(た)らない意見(いけん), 取(と)るに足(た)りない
存在(そんざい). Outside that phrase the verb slot is small and evaluative:
恐(おそ)れる, 驚(おどろ)く, 論(ろん)じる, 数(かぞ)える,
信頼(しんらい)する, 呼(よ)ぶ.

There is a self-deprecating use worth recognising. Talking about one's own effort
or contribution as 取(と)るに足(た)りないものですが is modest and gracious;
saying the same of someone else's is a hard judgement. The words do not change -
the target does.

The everyday spoken equivalents are ささいな, たいしたことではない and
つまらない.

## Contrast

### ～に足(た)りない vs ～にはあたらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に足(た)りない</code> judges the **thing**: it is too
slight to deserve this. <code>にはあたらない</code> judges the **reaction**: given
the circumstances, there is no call for it.

</div>

<pre>
あの程度(ていど)の失敗(しっぱい)は騒(さわ)ぐ<b>に足(た)りない</b>。
A slip on that scale is not worth making a fuss about.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)不足(ぶそく)だったのだから、負(ま)けても驚(おどろ)く<b>にはあたらない</b>。
They were short of practice, so losing is nothing to be surprised at.
</pre>

The first weighs the failure and finds it small. The second accepts a reason -
they had not trained - and concludes that surprise is misplaced. This is why
<code>にはあたらない</code> so often follows からといって or のだから, while
<code>に足(た)りない</code> needs no such support.

### 取(と)るに足(た)りない vs ささいな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ささいな is a neutral "minor". <code>取(と)るに足(た)りない</code>
adds a verdict: not merely small, but **not worth picking up**.

</div>

<pre>
ささいなことで喧嘩(けんか)してしまった。
We ended up quarrelling over something minor.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の指摘(してき)は<b>取(と)るに足(た)らない</b>と一蹴(いっしゅう)された。
His point was brushed aside as not worth considering.
</pre>

ささいな can describe your own small worry warmly.
<code>取(と)るに足(た)らない</code> in the same slot sounds cold, because
it does not measure - it dismisses.

### ～に足(た)りない vs ～ほどのことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both say "not worth it".
<code>ほどのことはない</code> is ordinary spoken Japanese about a specific
occasion; <code>に足(た)りない</code> is written judgement about a thing's
worth in general.

</div>

<pre>
かすり傷(きず)だから、病院(びょういん)に行(い)く<b>ほどのことはない</b>。
It's only a scratch - not worth going to the hospital for.
</pre>

<pre>
その反論(はんろん)は一顧(いっこ)する<b>に足(た)りない</b>。
That objection does not merit a moment's consideration.
</pre>

Say the second to a friend about a scratch and you sound as though you are
delivering a ruling. Put the first in a written judgement and it sounds like
chat.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は取(と)るに<b>足(た)るない</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は取(と)るに<b>足(た)らない</b>。
  足(た)る is an う-verb: the negative is 足(た)らない.
  足(た)りない (from 足(た)りる) is the other correct option.

- ❌ 取(と)るに足(た)りない<b>だ</b>問題(もんだい) → ✅ <b>取(と)るに足(た)りない</b>問題(もんだい)
  足(た)りない is already a negative verb form, so it modifies a noun on its
  own. Neither だ nor な belongs there.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が見(み)るに足(た)りない。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)が足(た)りない。
  If you mean "there is not enough of it", use plain 足(た)りない with が. The
  grammar point needs a dictionary-form verb plus に.

- ❌ 驚(おどろ)くに足(た)りない<b>でした</b>。 → ✅ 驚(おどろ)くに<b>足(た)りませんでした</b>。
  This is a verb, not an い-adjective, so the polite past is
  足(た)りませんでした.

**1.** 敵(てき)の数(かず)は多(おお)いが、装備(そうび)を見(み)れば恐(おそ)れる____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

恐(おそ)れる<b>に足(た)りない</b>

The sentence weighs the enemy and finds them beneath fear, which is the core use
of the pattern; 恐(おそ)れる is one of the small set of evaluative verbs it
takes.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 前日(ぜんじつ)に熱(ねつ)を出(だ)していたのだから、欠場(けつじょう)は（驚(おどろ)くに足(た)りない・驚(おどろ)くにはあたらない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

欠場(けつじょう)は<b>驚(おどろ)くにはあたらない</b>。

The clause before it supplies a reason - a fever the day before - and the
sentence concludes that surprise is uncalled for. That is
<code>にはあたらない</code>. <code>驚(おどろ)くに足(た)りない</code> would
be judging the withdrawal itself as too trifling to notice, which is not what the
reason clause sets up.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Compared with the scale of the problem, our efforts so far are negligible."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

問題(もんだい)の大(おお)きさに比(くら)べれば、これまでの努力(どりょく)など<b>取(と)るに足(た)りない</b>。

<code>取(と)るに足(た)りない</code> is the standard rendering of
"negligible", and など in front of it strengthens the dismissal. Because the
speaker is talking about their own side's efforts, the sentence reads as modest
rather than harsh.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)の意見(いけん)は取(と)るに足(た)りないなことだと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)の意見(いけん)は<b>取(と)るに足(た)りない</b>ことだと思(おも)います。
"I think his opinion is not worth taking up."

足(た)りない is a verb in its negative form, so it attaches straight to こと.
The な comes from treating it as a な-adjective, which it is not.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)のしたことなど<b>取(と)るに足(た)りません</b>。
(b) あなたのしたことなど<b>取(と)るに足(た)りません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is modest. Applied to your own contribution, the phrase is a polite way of
waving away thanks - roughly "it was nothing" - and a Japanese listener will hear
courtesy, not self-hatred.

(b) is an insult. The same dismissal aimed outward tells the listener their work
did not merit picking up at all. The grammar is identical in both; only the
target has moved, and that is enough to change the sentence from graceful to
cutting.

</details>

# ～にたえる / ～にたえない

**Meaning** · can stand up to it / cannot bear it - and, with a feeling noun, cannot contain it

One shape, two jobs. After a perception verb or a noun like 鑑賞(かんしょう),
<code>にたえる</code> says the thing **holds up** under looking, listening or use,
and <code>にたえない</code> says it is so poor or so painful that you cannot go
on. After a feeling noun - 感謝(かんしゃ), 遺憾(いかん),
同情(どうじょう) - <code>にたえない</code> means the opposite of poor: the
feeling is more than the speaker can hold, "I am deeply grateful", "I regret it
profoundly".

## Formation

The verb is 堪(た)える, usually written in kana in this pattern.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (perception) | dictionary form + にたえる | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)むにたえる |
| Verb (perception) | dictionary form + にたえない | 見(み)る → 見(み)るにたえない |
| Noun (appreciation, use) | noun + にたえる | 鑑賞(かんしょう) → 鑑賞(かんしょう)にたえる |
| Noun (feeling) | noun + にたえない | 感謝(かんしゃ) → 感謝(かんしゃ)にたえない |
| Modifying a noun | ～にたえる + noun | 使用(しよう)にたえる部品(ぶひん) |
| Past | ～にたえなかった | 聞(き)くにたえなかった |
| Polite | ～にたえます ／ ～にたえません | 遺憾(いかん)にたえません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kanji is 堪(た)える. The similar-looking
<code>耐(た)える</code> is the endurance verb of
<code>暑(あつ)さに耐(た)える</code>; convention keeps 耐(た) for putting
up with hardship and 堪(た) for this pattern. 堪(た)える is also the kanji
of a second verb read こらえる ("hold back tears"), which is why kana is the safe
spelling here.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの映画(えいが)は見(み)る<b>にたえなかった</b>。
That film was unwatchable.
</pre>

<pre>
この机(つくえ)はまだ使用(しよう)<b>にたえる</b>。
This desk is still perfectly usable.
</pre>

<pre>
悪口(わるぐち)ばかりで、聞(き)く<b>にたえない</b>話(はなし)だった。
It was nothing but insults - the kind of talk you cannot sit through.
</pre>

<pre>
皆様(みなさま)の温(あたた)かいご支援(しえん)に、感謝(かんしゃ)<b>にたえません</b>。
For all your warm support I am more grateful than I can say.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の現場(げんば)は見(み)る<b>にたえない</b>状態(じょうたい)だった。
The scene of the accident was in a state you could hardly bear to look at.
</pre>

<pre>
素人(しろうと)の翻訳(ほんやく)だが、内容(ないよう)は十分(じゅうぶん)読(よ)む<b>にたえる</b>。
It's an amateur translation, but it reads well enough to be worth your time.
</pre>

<pre>
このような結果(けっか)を招(まね)いたことは、誠(まこと)に遺憾(いかん)<b>にたえません</b>。
That matters should have come to this is a source of the deepest regret.
</pre>

<pre>
三十年(さんじゅうねん)前(まえ)の記録(きろく)映像(えいぞう)だが、画質(がしつ)がよく、今(いま)なお鑑賞(かんしょう)<b>にたえる</b>。
It's documentary footage from thirty years ago, but the picture is good and it still stands up to viewing today.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and written, in both senses, though 見(み)るにたえない and
聞(き)くにたえない do surface in emphatic speech - they are strong words, close
to English "unwatchable" or "sickening", and they carry disgust or pity rather
than mild disapproval.

The verb slot in the perception sense is small: 見(み)る, 聞(き)く,
読(よ)む and the nouns 鑑賞(かんしょう), 観賞(かんしょう),
使用(しよう). It is not a general "can't stand it" - ❌この暑(あつ)さは
耐(た)えるにたえない is not a sentence.

The feeling-noun frame is a different animal: it belongs to speeches, condolence
letters, apologies and official statements, and it is almost always polite -
感謝(かんしゃ)にたえません, 遺憾(いかん)にたえません,
同情(どうじょう)にたえません, 喜(よろこ)びにたえません. Notice that
遺憾(いかん)にたえない is the language of a public apology; hearing it, a
Japanese listener expects a bow and a formal setting, not a conversation.

That frame only exists in the negative. There is no ❌感謝(かんしゃ)にたえる:
the whole point is that the feeling cannot be contained.

## Contrast

### ～にたえない vs ～に足(た)りない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にたえない</code> is too **painful or too bad** to
continue. <code>に足(た)りない</code> is too **slight to bother** starting.

</div>

<pre>
差別的(さべつてき)な発言(はつげん)が続(つづ)き、聞(き)く<b>にたえなかった</b>。
The remarks turned discriminatory and became impossible to listen to.
</pre>

<pre>
中身(なかみ)のない批評(ひひょう)で、読(よ)む<b>に足(た)りない</b>。
The review has nothing in it and is not worth reading.
</pre>

Strong feeling versus cool dismissal. <code>にたえない</code> says the
experience assaulted you; <code>に足(た)りない</code> says you did not think
it merited your attention in the first place.

### ～にたえる vs ～に耐(た)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>鑑賞(かんしょう)にたえる</code> is about **quality**:
it survives being judged. <code>寒(さむ)さに耐(た)える</code> is about
**endurance**: a person or a thing puts up with hardship.

</div>

<pre>
この素材(そざい)は高温(こうおん)に<b>耐(た)える</b>。
This material withstands high temperatures.
</pre>

<pre>
初期(しょき)の作品(さくひん)だが、今(いま)見(み)ても鑑賞(かんしょう)<b>にたえる</b>。
It's early work, but it still repays viewing today.
</pre>

The subject is the giveaway. In the endurance sense something suffers a force -
heat, cold, pressure, grief. In the pattern taught here nothing suffers: an
audience is doing the judging, and the work is passing.

### 感謝(かんしゃ)にたえない vs 感謝(かんしゃ)している

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>感謝(かんしゃ)している</code> reports the feeling.
<code>感謝(かんしゃ)にたえない</code> performs it, in public, in formal
Japanese.

</div>

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)ってくれて、本当(ほんとう)に<b>感謝(かんしゃ)しているよ</b>。
Thanks for helping - I really do appreciate it.
</pre>

<pre>
関係者(かんけいしゃ)各位(かくい)のご尽力(じんりょく)に、感謝(かんしゃ)<b>にたえない次第(しだい)です</b>。
For the efforts of everyone concerned I am more grateful than words allow.
</pre>

Use the second with a friend and it sounds like a press release. The frame wants
an audience, a podium or a letter.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ご支援(しえん)に感謝(かんしゃ)<b>にたえます</b>。 → ✅ ご支援(しえん)に感謝(かんしゃ)<b>にたえません</b>。
  With a feeling noun the frame is negative only. The affirmative does not exist.

- ❌ 見(み)るにたえない<b>の</b>光景(こうけい) → ✅ <b>見(み)るにたえない</b>光景(こうけい)
  たえない is a verb form and modifies the noun directly.

- ❌ この料理(りょうり)は食(た)べる<b>にたえない</b>。 → ✅ この料理(りょうり)は<b>食(た)べられたものではない</b>。
  The perception frame does not extend to eating. Japanese has its own idiom for
  inedible food.

- ❌ 遺憾(いかん)にたえない<b>です</b>。 → ✅ 遺憾(いかん)に<b>たえません</b>。
  たえない is a verb, so its polite form is たえません. ないです is far too
  casual for the ceremonial register this phrase lives in.

## Practice

**1.** 会場(かいじょう)の野次(やじ)がひどく、演説(えんぜつ)は最後(さいご)まで聞(き)く____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

聞(き)く<b>にたえなかった</b>

The heckling made listening unbearable, which is the perception sense; the past
form fits because the speech is over. 聞(き)く is one of the handful of verbs
this frame accepts.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 古(ふる)い建物(たてもの)だが、補強(ほきょう)したので地震(じしん)（にたえる・に耐(た)える）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

地震(じしん)<b>に耐(た)える</b>。

An earthquake is a force acting on the building, so this is endurance, spelled
耐(た). <code>にたえる</code> in the sense taught here would claim the
building holds up as an object of appreciation, which is not what reinforcement
work is about.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "We received warm messages from so many people, and I am more grateful than I can express."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

多(おお)くの方(かた)から温(あたた)かいお言葉(ことば)をいただき、感謝(かんしゃ)<b>にたえません</b>。

"More grateful than I can express" is exactly the feeling-noun frame, and the
polite ません form is obligatory in the letter-or-podium setting where it
belongs.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この論文(ろんぶん)は誤(ご)りが多(おお)く、読(よ)むにたえないの出来(でき)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この論文(ろんぶん)は誤(ご)りが多(おお)く、<b>読(よ)むにたえない</b>出来(でき)です。
"The paper is riddled with errors and is in no state to be read."

たえない modifies 出来(でき) directly; the の between them comes from
treating a verb as if it were a noun. The rest of the sentence is fine, and the
register - a written judgement on a paper - suits the pattern well.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この作品(さくひん)は鑑賞(かんしょう)<b>にたえない</b>。
(b) この作品(さくひん)を見(み)て、感動(かんどう)<b>にたえない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the perception sense and is harsh: the work is so poor that looking at it
is not something a viewer can be asked to do. It is a review you would not want
written about you.

(b) is the feeling-noun sense and is the opposite - the speaker is overwhelmed
with emotion by the very same work. Nothing in the grammar distinguishes them
except what sits in front of に: an act of viewing in (a), a feeling in (b).

</details>

# ～にかたくない

**Meaning** · it is not hard to imagine; one can readily infer it - a written formula for "anyone can see how they must have felt"

難(かた)い is the literary adjective "difficult", the same 難(かた) that
appears voiced in ～がたい. <code>想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない</code> is
therefore "not difficult to imagine", and it is one of a very small number of
frozen phrases in which this word still lives. The writer is inviting the reader
to supply the picture themselves.

## Formation

The noun slot is tiny in practice: 想像(そうぞう), 察(さっ)する,
推察(すいさつ). Both a noun and a dictionary-form verb are possible before に.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (想像(そうぞう) etc.) | noun + に難(かた)くない | 想像(そうぞう) → 想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない |
| Verb | dictionary form + に難(かた)くない | 察(さっ)する → 察(さっ)するに難(かた)くない |
| Verb (想像(そうぞう)する) | dictionary form + に難(かた)くない | 想像(そうぞう)する → 想像(そうぞう)するに難(かた)くない |
| Polite | ～に難(かた)くありません | 想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くありません |
| Kana spelling | ～にかたくない | 想像(そうぞう)にかたくない |
| Typical frame | ～ことは ～に難(かた)くない | 反発(はんぱつ)が出(で)ることは想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - 難(かた)くない, read **かたくない**, is not
難(むずか)しくない. They share a kanji and nothing else: the first is this
literary formula, the second is the everyday "is not difficult".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)の落胆(らくたん)は想像(そうぞう)<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。
Her disappointment is not hard to imagine.
</pre>

<pre>
ご遺族(いぞく)の心情(しんじょう)は察(さっ)する<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。
One can readily sense what the bereaved family must be feeling.
</pre>

<pre>
当日(とうじつ)の会場(かいじょう)が混乱(こんらん)したことは想像(そうぞう)<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。
It takes little imagination to picture the confusion at the venue that day.
</pre>

<pre>
この決定(けってい)が地域(ちいき)経済(けいざい)に打撃(だげき)を与(あた)えることは、想像(そうぞう)<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。
That this decision will deal a blow to the local economy is not hard to foresee.
</pre>

<pre>
制度(せいど)が変(か)われば現場(げんば)に負担(ふたん)が集中(しゅうちゅう)することは、専門家(せんもんか)でなくとも想像(そうぞう)<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。
You need not be an expert to see that a change in the system will pile the burden onto the front line.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)守(まも)ってきた店(みせ)を閉(と)じる決断(けつだん)がどれほど重(おも)かったか、想像(そうぞう)<b>に難(かた)くありません</b>。
How heavy the decision to close a shop kept for so many years must have been is not hard to imagine.
</pre>

<pre>
突然(とつぜん)の解任(かいにん)に社員(しゃいん)が動揺(どうよう)したことは想像(そうぞう)<b>に難(かた)くない</b>が、業績(ぎょうせき)を見(み)れば避(さ)けられない判断(はんだん)であった。
The upset among the staff at the sudden dismissal is easy enough to imagine, but the figures made the decision unavoidable.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)の書簡(しょかん)を読(よ)めば、彼(かれ)が学界(がっかい)で孤立(こりつ)していたことは推察(すいさつ)<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。
Reading the letters of the period, it is not difficult to infer how isolated he was within his field.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not use it.** It belongs to newspaper editorials,
opinion columns, formal reports, eulogies and speeches - written Japanese
addressed to an audience. Said out loud in ordinary conversation it lands like a
leader-column sentence dropped into a chat, and a listener will hear it as
posturing. What you should say instead is
<code>想像(そうぞう)がつく</code>, or
<code>簡単(かんたん)に想像(そうぞう)できる</code>.

Two things are worth noticing when you meet it. First, it is a **rhetorical
move**, not a report: the writer asserts that the conclusion is obvious and so
declines to argue for it. Editorials use it to slide past the weakest link in an
argument, and reading it that way is a useful habit.

Second, it is almost always about **someone else's feelings or reaction** -
distress, anger, relief, the fallout of a decision. It is not used for facts you
could simply check. ❌明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ることは
想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない is wrong not grammatically but in kind.

The one place it does show up in speech is prepared, ceremonial speech: at a
memorial or a retirement address, ご心痛(しんつう)は察(さっ)するに
難(かた)くありません is entirely at home. That register - podium, script,
audience - is the test.

## Contrast

### ～に難(かた)くない vs 想像(そうぞう)がつく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, opposite settings.
<code>想像(そうぞう)がつく</code> is what a person says;
<code>想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない</code> is what a column prints.

</div>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)が怒(おこ)ったのは<b>想像(そうぞう)がつく</b>よ。
I can imagine he was angry.
</pre>

<pre>
関係者(かんけいしゃ)の落胆(らくたん)は<b>想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない</b>。
The dismay among those involved is not hard to imagine.
</pre>

If you are speaking, use the first. <code>想像(そうぞう)がつく</code> also
takes the negative freely - 想像(そうぞう)がつかない, "I can't imagine" -
whereas the literary phrase has no natural affirmative counterpart:
❌想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)い is not said.

### ～に難(かた)くない vs ～がたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same kanji points in opposite directions.
<code>に難(かた)くない</code> = **easy** to imagine. ～がたい = **hard** to do,
attached to a ます-stem.

</div>

<pre>
あの温厚(おんこう)な人(ひと)が怒鳴(どな)ったとは、<b>信(しん)じがたい</b>。
That so mild a man should have shouted is hard to believe.
</pre>

<pre>
周囲(しゅうい)が驚(おどろ)いたことは<b>想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない</b>。
That those around him were startled is not hard to imagine.
</pre>

Learners meeting 難(かた) for the first time in both patterns often read
<code>に難(かた)くない</code> as "cannot", because ～がたい feels negative.
It is the reverse: the negative ending makes the sentence say the inference is
easy.

### ～に難(かた)くない vs 言(い)うまでもない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に難(かた)くない</code> says the reader can **work it
out**. <code>言(い)うまでもない</code> says it is so obvious that **saying it
is a waste of breath**.

</div>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)が最優先(さいゆうせん)であることは<b>言(い)うまでもない</b>。
It goes without saying that safety comes first.
</pre>

<pre>
現場(げんば)が混乱(こんらん)したことは<b>想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない</b>。
The confusion on the ground is not hard to imagine.
</pre>

The first states a shared premise. The second hands the reader a scene and asks
them to fill it in. Only <code>に難(かた)くない</code> can do that, which is
why it takes feelings and reactions rather than principles.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 想像(そうぞう)に<b>難(むずか)しくない</b> → ✅ 想像(そうぞう)に<b>難(かた)くない</b>
  The reading is かたくない. 難(むずか)しくない is a different, ordinary word
  and does not form this phrase.

- ❌ 想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない<b>だ</b>。 → ✅ 想像(そうぞう)に<b>難(かた)くない</b>。
  難(かた)くない is already the negative of an い-adjective, so だ cannot
  follow. The polite form is 難(かた)くありません.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)の答(こた)えは分(わ)かる<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)の答(こた)えは<b>すぐ分(わ)かる</b>。
  The slot in front takes 想像(そうぞう), 察(さっ)する,
  推察(すいさつ) and little else. It is not a general "is easy to".

- ❌ (to a friend, about a delayed train) 遅(おく)れて怒(おこ)ったのは想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くないね。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れて怒(おこ)ったのは<b>想像(そうぞう)がつくね</b>。
  Correct grammar, wrong room. In conversation the literary phrase sounds like
  reading aloud from a newspaper.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence - (a) a message to a friend, (b) a newspaper editorial, (c) a children's picture book?
被災地(ひさいち)の方々(かたがた)のご苦労(くろう)は察(さっ)する<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b), a newspaper editorial** - or a speech, a formal report, a letter of
condolence. The phrase is written, public Japanese about other people's feelings.
To a friend you would say 想像(そうぞう)がつく, and a children's book would
not use 察(さっ)する at all.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 彼(かれ)が悩(なや)んでいたことは想像(そうぞう)に難(かた)くない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)が悩(なや)んでいたのは<b>想像(そうぞう)がつく</b>。
（or 簡単(かんたん)に想像(そうぞう)できる）

<code>想像(そうぞう)がつく</code> carries the whole meaning and is what a
speaker would actually say; dropping ことは for のは also brings the sentence
down into the spoken register.

</details>

**3.** Choose: あの慎重(しんちょう)な彼(かれ)が辞表(じひょう)を出(だ)したとは（信(しん)じがたい・信(しん)じるに難(かた)くない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>信(しん)じがたい</b>。

The sentence is about something **hard** to believe, so it needs ～がたい.
<code>に難(かた)くない</code> would claim the opposite, that believing it is
easy - and in any case the pattern does not take 信(しん)じる.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 住民(じゅうみん)が不安(ふあん)を感(かん)じたことは想像(そうぞう)に難(むずか)しくないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 住民(じゅうみん)が不安(ふあん)を感(かん)じたことは想像(そうぞう)に<b>難(かた)くありません</b>。
"It is not hard to imagine that the residents felt uneasy."

Two repairs: the reading is かたく, not むずかしく, and a phrase this formal takes
ありません rather than ないです. If the setting is conversation, abandon the
pattern altogether and say 想像(そうぞう)がつきます.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の苦労(くろう)は想像(そうぞう)<b>に難(かた)くない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)の苦労(くろう)は想像(そうぞう)<b>もつかない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the reader can picture it easily - the writer is claiming common ground
and moving on. It is sympathetic but slightly distant, and it belongs on a
printed page.

(b) says the opposite: what he went through is **beyond** the speaker's power to
imagine. It is spoken Japanese, and it expresses awe or helplessness rather than
easy agreement. Note that the negative of the literary phrase is not the way to
express (b); the two use different verbs entirely.

</details>

# ～にあたらない

**Meaning** · there is no call for it; it does not warrant that - the reaction you were about to have is not justified by the facts

当(あ)たる here is "to hit the mark, to correspond to". The negative says the
case **does not amount to** the thing named: not enough to be surprised at, not
enough to blame anyone for, not enough to count as a breach. The writer is
heading off a reaction, usually one the reader has already begun to have.

## Formation

Written には当(あ)たらない or にはあたらない; the は is often dropped, and the
kana spelling is common in newspapers.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (reaction) | dictionary form + には当(あ)たらない | 驚(おどろ)く → 驚(おどろ)くには当(あ)たらない |
| Verb, は dropped | dictionary form + に当(あ)たらない | 感心(かんしん)する → 感心(かんしん)するに当(あ)たらない |
| Noun | noun + には当(あ)たらない | 違反(いはん) → 違反(いはん)には当(あ)たらない |
| Typical frame | ～からといって、～には当(あ)たらない | 負(ま)けたからといって落(お)ち込(こ)むには当(あ)たらない |
| Polite | ～には当(あ)たりません | 心配(しんぱい)するには当(あ)たりません |
| Literary | ～には当(あ)たるまい | 責(せ)めるには当(あ)たるまい |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verbs that fit in front are **reactions**: 驚(おどろ)く,
感心(かんしん)する, 非難(ひなん)する, 責(せ)める, 褒(ほ)める,
同情(どうじょう)する, 悲観(ひかん)する, 心配(しんぱい)する,
恥(は)じる. Actions that are merely unnecessary - coming, phoning, paying -
take <code>には及(およ)ばない</code> instead. 心配(しんぱい)する sits in
both camps: 心配(しんぱい)するには及(およ)びません deflects the listener's
concern, while 心配(しんぱい)するにはあたりません rules that the situation
does not justify it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
その程度(ていど)のことで驚(おどろ)く<b>にはあたらない</b>。
Something on that scale is nothing to be surprised at.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)失敗(しっぱい)したくらいで悲観(ひかん)する<b>にはあたらない</b>。
One failure is no reason for despair.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)を確認(かくにん)したが、今回(こんかい)の対応(たいおう)は違反(いはん)<b>にはあたらない</b>。
We checked the rules, and what was done this time does not constitute a breach.
</pre>

<pre>
事情(じじょう)を聞(き)けば、彼(かれ)一人(ひとり)を責(せ)める<b>にはあたらない</b>気(き)がしてくる。
Once you hear the circumstances, you start to feel there is no call to blame him alone.
</pre>

<pre>
新記録(しんきろく)といっても条件(じょうけん)が違(ちが)うのだから、大(おお)さわぎする<b>にはあたらない</b>。
It may be a record, but the conditions were different, so there is no cause for all the fuss.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)に三年(さんねん)かけたことを思(おも)えば、この結果(けっか)は驚(おどろ)く<b>にはあたりません</b>。
Given that they spent three years preparing, the outcome is hardly astonishing.
</pre>

<pre>
就任(しゅうにん)早々(そうそう)の失言(しつげん)ではあるが、辞任(じにん)を求(もと)める<b>にはあたらない</b>という声(こえ)も根強(ねづよ)い。
It was a gaffe within days of taking office, but there is a persistent view that it does not warrant calling for a resignation.
</pre>

<pre>
寄付(きふ)をしたのは当然(とうぜん)の務(つと)めを果(は)たしたまでで、特(とく)に感心(かんしん)する<b>にはあたらない</b>と本人(ほんにん)は語(かた)っている。
He says himself that in donating he only did what was expected, and that it is nothing to admire him for.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal, and above all **argumentative**. This is a writer's move: the reader is
supposed to be surprised, or indignant, and the sentence tells them to stand
down, usually giving the reason in the same breath with からといって, のだから or
を考(かんが)えれば. Editorials, commentary, analyst reports, official
statements and calm advice all run on it.

Because it overrules the reader's reaction, it can sound superior. Aimed at
someone's feelings in conversation - 泣(な)くにはあたりませんよ - it comes
across as lecturing; the warm version is
泣(な)かなくてもいいですよ or 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですよ.

The noun use is a separate habit and is worth learning as its own phrase.
<code>違反(いはん)にはあたらない</code>,
<code>差別(さべつ)にはあたらない</code>,
<code>非難(ひなん)にはあたらない</code> mean "does not constitute" and are
standard in legal, regulatory and corporate language. In that use there is no
reaction being suppressed at all - only a category the case fails to fall into.

Spoken, the pattern is largely confined to prepared or professional talk:
meetings, interviews, courtrooms, the news. The everyday equivalent is
<code>驚(おどろ)くことはない</code> or
<code>驚(おどろ)かなくてもいい</code>.

## Contrast

### ～にはあたらない vs ～ことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same message, different height.
<code>ことはない</code> is spoken reassurance to a person;
<code>にはあたらない</code> is a written judgement about a case.

</div>

<pre>
そんなに落(お)ち込(こ)む<b>ことはない</b>よ。
There's no need to get so down about it.
</pre>

<pre>
統計(とうけい)の範囲(はんい)内(ない)の変動(へんどう)であり、懸念(けねん)する<b>にはあたらない</b>。
The movement is within statistical bounds and gives no cause for concern.
</pre>

Notice who is being addressed. `ことはない` comforts the person in front of you;
<code>にはあたらない</code> corrects an inference. That is also why
<code>にはあたらない</code> nearly always carries its reason, while `ことはない`
can stand alone with よ.

### ～にはあたらない vs ～には及(およ)ばない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にはあたらない</code> = the **reaction** is unwarranted.
<code>には及(およ)ばない</code> = the **trouble** is unnecessary; please don't
bother.

</div>

<pre>
わざわざお越(こ)しいただく<b>には及(およ)びません</b>。
There's really no need for you to come all this way.
</pre>

<pre>
遅刻(ちこく)は一度(いちど)きりで、処分(しょぶん)する<b>にはあたらない</b>。
It was a single instance of lateness and does not warrant disciplinary action.
</pre>

Swap them and both go wrong. Coming to visit is a courtesy someone is offering,
not a reaction to be judged; a disciplinary decision is a judgement, not an
inconvenience to be waved away. <code>には及(およ)ばない</code> is polite;
<code>にはあたらない</code> is evaluative.

### 違反(いはん)にはあたらない vs 違反(いはん)ではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ではない</code> flatly denies.
<code>にはあたらない</code> concedes that the case is close and rules that it
still falls outside.

</div>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の行為(こうい)は規則(きそく)違反(いはん)<b>ではない</b>。
What was done was not a breach of the rules.
</pre>

<pre>
形式的(けいしきてき)には問題(もんだい)が残(のこ)るが、規則(きそく)違反(いはん)<b>にはあたらない</b>。
Formally some questions remain, but it does not amount to a breach of the rules.
</pre>

The second is what a committee writes when the answer was not obvious. It
implies deliberation, and it is why the phrase is so common in findings and
statements: it settles a borderline case without pretending the border was
never approached.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 驚(おどろ)いた<b>にはあたらない</b> → ✅ 驚(おどろ)く<b>にはあたらない</b>
  The verb stays in the dictionary form, even when the event is in the past.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は来(く)る<b>にはあたりません</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は<b>お越(こ)しには及(およ)びません</b>。
  Coming is an action someone is kindly offering, not a reaction to be judged.
  For "no need to", use <code>には及(およ)ばない</code>.

- ❌ 心配(しんぱい)するにはあたらない<b>です</b>。 → ✅ 心配(しんぱい)するには<b>あたりません</b>。
  This is a verb, so the polite form is あたりません. ないです clashes badly
  with the formality of the pattern.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)の努力(どりょく)は感心(かんしん)する<b>にあたる</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)の努力(どりょく)は<b>感心(かんしん)に値(あたい)する</b>。
  There is no affirmative use of this pattern in modern Japanese. "Deserves
  admiration" is <code>値(あたい)する</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいているのだから、飛行機(ひこうき)が欠航(けっこう)しても驚(おどろ)く____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

驚(おどろ)く<b>にはあたらない</b>

The first clause supplies the reason and the second cancels the reader's
surprise, which is exactly what this pattern is for. Keep 驚(おどろ)く in the
dictionary form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 荷物(にもつ)は自分(じぶん)で運(はこ)びますので、手伝(てつだ)っていただく（にはあたりません・には及(およ)びません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

手伝(てつだ)っていただく<b>には及(およ)びません</b>。

The speaker is politely declining an offer of help - a courtesy, not a reaction.
<code>にはあたりません</code> would sound as though the offer had been judged
inappropriate, which is close to rude.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Sales fell slightly, but seasonal factors explain it and there is no cause for alarm."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

売上(うりあげ)はやや減(へ)ったが、季節(きせつ)要因(よういん)によるもので、懸念(けねん)する<b>にはあたらない</b>。

"No cause for alarm" plus a reason given in the same sentence is the natural home
of this pattern, and the register - a business or market comment - is right for

less formal report.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 一度(いちど)注意(ちゅうい)されたくらいで、そんなに落(お)ち込(こ)んだにはあたらないよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 一度(いちど)注意(ちゅうい)されたくらいで、そんなに落(お)ち込(こ)む<b>ことはない</b>よ。
"You were told off once - there's no need to take it so hard."

Two problems. The verb must be in the dictionary form, not the past. More
importantly, よ and this comforting tone mark the sentence as speech to a friend,
where <code>にはあたらない</code> sounds like a ruling from a committee;
`ことはない` is the natural choice.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)の発言(はつげん)は差別(さべつ)<b>ではない</b>。
(b) 今回(こんかい)の発言(はつげん)は差別(さべつ)<b>にはあたらない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat denial: whatever was said, it simply was not discriminatory. It
sounds like a person defending themselves, and it leaves no room for the
question.

(b) is a finding. It concedes that the remark was close enough to be examined,
and rules that it falls outside the category. That is why it is the wording used
in committee reports and official statements - and why, in a heated argument, it
can sound evasive rather than exonerating.

</details>

# ～には及ばない

**Meaning** · there is no need to go to that trouble - and, with a noun, does not come up to it

及(およ)ぶ means "to reach, to extend as far as". The negative therefore says
the case **does not reach** the point where the action is required: no need to
come, no need to thank me, no need to worry. The same verb gives a second,
comparative use - 兄(あに)には及(およ)ばない, "I am not the equal of my
brother" - and the two share one form.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb ("no need to") | dictionary form + には及(およ)ばない | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)するには及(およ)ばない |
| Verb (humble request form) | ～いただくには及(およ)ばない | 来(き)ていただく → 来(き)ていただくには及(およ)びません |
| Noun ("no need for") | noun + には及(およ)ばない | お礼(れい) → お礼(れい)には及(およ)びません |
| Noun ("is no match for") | noun + には及(およ)ばない | 兄(あに) → 兄(あに)には及(およ)ばない |
| Noun, は dropped | noun + に及(およ)ばない | 先輩(せんぱい) → 先輩(せんぱい)に及(およ)ばない |
| Past | ～には及(およ)ばなかった | 予想(よそう)には及(およ)ばなかった |
| Polite | ～には及(およ)びません | ご返信(へんしん)には及(およ)びません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a third, frozen use of the same verb is
<code>～は言(い)うに及(およ)ばず</code>, "to say nothing of":
漢字(かんじ)は言(い)うに及(およ)ばず、敬語(けいご)も難(むずか)しい.
Learn it as a phrase; it is not built from the pattern on this page.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ご心配(しんぱい)には<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
There's no need for you to worry.
</pre>

<pre>
お礼(れい)には<b>及(およ)びません</b>よ。
No thanks are necessary.
</pre>

<pre>
家(いえ)はすぐそこですので、送(おく)っていただくには<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
My place is just there, so please don't trouble yourself to walk me back.
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)では先輩(せんぱい)に<b>及(およ)ばない</b>が、体力(たいりょく)なら負(ま)けない。
In skill I am no match for my senior, but on stamina I can hold my own.
</pre>

<pre>
確認(かくにん)のためのご連絡(れんらく)ですので、ご返信(へんしん)には<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
This message is just for confirmation; there is no need to reply.
</pre>

<pre>
今期(こんき)の売上(うりあげ)は予想(よそう)には<b>及(およ)ばなかった</b>ものの、赤字(あかじ)は避(さ)けられた。
This term's sales fell short of the forecast, but we avoided going into the red.
</pre>

<pre>
書類(しょるい)はこちらで用意(ようい)いたしますので、改(あらた)めてご準備(じゅんび)いただくには<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
We will prepare the paperwork at this end, so you need not put anything together yourself.
</pre>

<pre>
似(に)たような商品(しょうひん)は増(ふ)えたが、仕上(しあ)げの美(うつく)しさでは本家(ほんけ)の職人(しょくにん)の技(わざ)には<b>及(およ)ばない</b>。
Copies have multiplied, but for sheer finish none of them comes near the original craftsmen's work.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The "no need" use is **polite deflection**, and that is its whole character. It
releases the other person from an effort they were about to make - coming,
replying, paying, apologising, worrying - and it does so gracefully, which is why
it lives in service Japanese, business email and careful speech. It appears
overwhelmingly in the polite form
<code>には及(およ)びません</code>, usually after a reason.

Said plainly to a friend it is too stiff:
<code>来(こ)なくていいよ</code> is what you would actually say. Said to a
customer it is exactly right.

There is one trap of tone. ご心配(しんぱい)には及(およ)びません is
reassuring when the worry is about you and your own affairs. Used to someone who
is worried about damage **you** caused, it can sound as though you are brushing
their concern aside; there, an apology plus an explanation is safer.

The comparative use is different in register - plain written or spoken Japanese,
common in modest self-assessment (私(わたし)などまだまだ及(およ)びません)
and in sober comparisons of quality or figures. It is almost always negative:
Japanese does not use the affirmative 及(およ)ぶ to say "A equals B".

## Contrast

### ～には及(およ)ばない vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくてもいい` gives **permission** to skip it.
<code>には及(およ)ばない</code> **declines an offer** on the other person's
behalf, politely.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は早(はや)く来(こ)<b>なくてもいい</b>よ。
You don't have to come in early tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
当日(とうじつ)は受付(うけつけ)でお名前(なまえ)をおっしゃっていただくだけで、事前(じぜん)の登録(とうろく)には<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
On the day you need only give your name at reception; there is no need to register in advance.
</pre>

The first is a boss to a subordinate; the second is an organiser to a guest.
`なくてもいい` can be said downward or between equals, but it is a permission,
so saying it to a customer sounds presumptuous.
<code>には及(およ)びません</code> has no such problem.

### ～には及(およ)ばない vs ～にはかなわない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "no match for".
<code>には及(およ)ばない</code> measures a **gap**, calmly.
<code>にはかなわない</code> **gives up**, with feeling.

</div>

<pre>
将棋(しょうぎ)では祖父(そふ)に<b>かなわない</b>。
At shogi I just can't beat my grandfather.
</pre>

<pre>
完成度(かんせいど)という点(てん)では、前作(ぜんさく)に<b>及(およ)ばない</b>。
In terms of polish it does not match the previous work.
</pre>

<code>かなわない</code> is personal and often rueful, and it needs a contest -
a game, an argument, a person you cannot outdo.
<code>及(およ)ばない</code> takes figures, quality and reputation as well, and
sounds like an assessment rather than a surrender.

### ～には及(およ)ばない vs ～にはあたらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>には及(およ)ばない</code> tells you not to **take the
trouble**. <code>にはあたらない</code> tells you the **reaction is unwarranted**.

</div>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)は軽(かる)いので、手伝(てつだ)っていただくには<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
The bags are light, so there's no need for you to help.
</pre>

<pre>
事前(じぜん)に説明(せつめい)があったのだから、変更(へんこう)を非難(ひなん)する<b>にはあたらない</b>。
We were told in advance, so the change hardly deserves criticism.
</pre>

Helping is a kindness offered; criticism is a judgement passed. Put
<code>にはあたらない</code> on the first and you appear to be ruling that the
offer was out of order.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 来(き)た<b>には及(およ)ばない</b> → ✅ <b>来(く)るには及(およ)ばない</b>
  The verb stays in the dictionary form. Note the reading too:
  来(く)る, not こる.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)の実力(じつりょく)は兄(あに)に<b>及(およ)ぶ</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)の実力(じつりょく)は兄(あに)<b>と同(おな)じくらいだ</b>。
  The comparative use exists only in the negative. Affirmative
  及(およ)ぶ means "reaches, extends to" -
  被害(ひがい)は全域(ぜんいき)に及(およ)ぶ - which is a different
  sentence entirely.

- ❌ お礼(れい)には及(およ)びません<b>です</b>。 → ✅ お礼(れい)には<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
  及(およ)びません is already the polite negative; です cannot be stacked on
  top of it.

- ❌ (自分(じぶん)の仕事(しごと)について) 私(わたし)が出席(しゅっせき)するには及(およ)びません。 → ✅ 私(わたし)が出席(しゅっせき)する<b>必要(ひつよう)はありません</b>。
  The pattern normally excuses **the other person** from an effort. Turned on
  your own duties it sounds oddly self-important, as though you were graciously
  letting yourself off.

The speaker is releasing the listener from bringing anything, which is the polite

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ご心配(しんぱい)には<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
(b) 心配(しんぱい)する<b>にはあたりません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is addressed **to the person worrying**, and it is warm: thank you for your
concern, but please set it down. It is the standard line when someone asks after
your health or your family.

(b) is a judgement **about the situation**: given the facts, worry is not
justified. It looks past the listener to the case itself, so it fits a report or
an announcement, and in a personal exchange it can sound as if you were correcting
the other person for feeling anything at all.

</details>

# ～までもない

**Meaning** · there is no need to go that far - the matter is already clear, so the step you were about to take would add nothing

まで marks a limit you would have to travel to. <code>までもない</code> says the
situation does not extend that far: you need not check, need not ask, need not
say it out loud, because the answer is settled already. The unnecessary action is
not too much trouble - it is simply **pointless**.

## Formation

Verbs only, in the **dictionary form**. Nouns cannot take this pattern directly.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + までもない | 言(い)う → 言(い)うまでもない |
| Verb | dictionary form + までもありません | 確認(かくにん)する → 確認(かくにん)するまでもありません |
| Adverbial ("without even ～ing") | dictionary form + までもなく | 調(しら)べる → 調(しら)べるまでもなく |
| Modifying a noun | ～までもない + noun | 相談(そうだん)する → 相談(そうだん)するまでもない話(はなし) |
| Past | ～までもなかった | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)くまでもなかった |
| Set phrase | 言(い)うまでもない ／ 言(い)うまでもなく | - → 言(い)うまでもなく安全(あんぜん)が第一(だいいち)だ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>までもなく</code> is the adverbial form and connects to what
follows: 調(しら)べるまでもなく分(わ)かった, "I knew without needing to look
it up". It is not a separate pattern, and it is the shape you will meet most
often in writing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そんなことは言(い)う<b>までもない</b>。
That goes without saying.
</pre>

<pre>
すぐ近(ちか)くだから、地図(ちず)を見(み)る<b>までもない</b>。
It's just nearby - no need to look at a map.
</pre>

<pre>
この程度(ていど)の汚(よご)れなら、洗剤(せんざい)を使(つか)う<b>までもない</b>。
For a mark this light there's no call to reach for detergent.
</pre>

<pre>
結果(けっか)は明(あき)らかで、集計(しゅうけい)する<b>までもなかった</b>。
The result was obvious; there was no need to add up the votes.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>言(い)うまでもなく</b>、この現場(げんば)では安全(あんぜん)が第一(だいいち)です。
Needless to say, safety comes first on this site.
</pre>

<pre>
わざわざ会議(かいぎ)を開(ひら)く<b>までもない</b>案件(あんけん)ですので、メールでご報告(ほうこく)します。
This isn't a matter that warrants calling a meeting, so I'll report it by email.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)に入(はい)ってきたときの表情(ひょうじょう)を見(み)れば、結果(けっか)は聞(き)く<b>までもなかった</b>。
One look at her face as she came in and there was no need to ask how it had gone.
</pre>

<pre>
契約書(けいやくしょ)に目(め)を通(とお)す<b>までもなく</b>、条件(じょうけん)が前回(ぜんかい)のままであることは察(さっ)しがついていた。
Without even having to read the contract, I had already gathered that the terms were unchanged.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral to formal. It is at home in writing and in careful speech - meetings,
reports, explanations - and it is not stiff enough to be strange in ordinary
conversation, though friends more often say
<code>見(み)なくてもわかる</code> or
<code>その必要(ひつよう)はない</code>.

The reason matters. <code>までもない</code> is dismissive of the **step**, not of
the person or the thing: the information is already in hand, the conclusion is
already visible, so the extra move would be redundant. This is why it pairs so
naturally with a clause that shows how the answer was already obtained -
表情(ひょうじょう)を見(み)れば, 数字(すうじ)を見(み)れば,
前回(ぜんかい)の例(れい)からして.

<code>言(い)うまでもない</code> and <code>言(い)うまでもなく</code> have
drifted into being fixed expressions meaning "needless to say", and they are
extremely common in written argument. A whole clause can sit inside them:
<code>～は言(い)うまでもない</code>. Used of a listener's remark
- そんなことは言(い)うまでもない - it can sting, since it implies they have
stated the obvious.

The polite form is <code>までもありません</code>; ❌までもないです is not
wrong in casual speech but sits badly in the settings the pattern favours.

## Contrast

### ～までもない vs ～ことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことはない` says the action is **unnecessary**, often
kindly. <code>までもない</code> says it would be **redundant**, because the point
is already settled.

</div>

<pre>
謝(あやま)る<b>ことはない</b>よ。君(きみ)のせいじゃない。
You don't need to apologise - it isn't your fault.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)が賛成(さんせい)なのだから、採決(さいけつ)を取(と)る<b>までもない</b>。
Everyone is in favour, so there is no point in taking a vote.
</pre>

Swap them and the tone shifts. <code>謝(あやま)るまでもない</code> would say
the apology is superfluous rather than that the listener is blameless, and it
sounds cold. Conversely `ことはない` about the vote loses the sense that the
result is already known.

### ～までもない vs ～ほどのことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>までもない</code>: the answer is **already obvious**.
<code>ほどのことはない</code>: the matter is **too small** to justify the fuss.

</div>

<pre>
軽(かる)い捻挫(ねんざ)なので、大(おお)さわぎする<b>ほどのことはない</b>。
It's a mild sprain - nothing to make a fuss about.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)説明(せつめい)したばかりなので、もう一度(いちど)説明(せつめい)する<b>までもない</b>。
I explained it only yesterday, so there's no need to go through it again.
</pre>

The first weighs the injury and finds it minor; the second weighs the
information and finds it already delivered. Where the two overlap, ask which is
being judged - the size of the matter, or the pointlessness of the step.

### 言(い)うまでもない vs もちろん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - もちろん is a speaker's "of course".
<code>言(い)うまでもない</code> is a writer's, and it frames the point as
shared ground that needs no argument.

</div>

<pre>
「手伝(てつだ)ってくれる？」「<b>もちろん</b>。」
"Will you help?" "Of course."
</pre>

<pre>
新制度(しんせいど)の成否(せいひ)が現場(げんば)の理解(りかい)にかかっていることは<b>言(い)うまでもない</b>。
That the new system's success depends on being understood on the ground hardly needs stating.
</pre>

もちろん can answer a question by itself; <code>言(い)うまでもない</code>
cannot - it needs a proposition in front of it. In an essay, the second is how a
writer nods at a premise and moves on.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 言(い)った<b>までもない</b> → ✅ 言(い)う<b>までもない</b>
  The verb stays in the dictionary form even when the situation is in the past;
  the past goes on までもない itself - 言(い)うまでもなかった.

- ❌ 確認(かくにん)<b>までもない</b> → ✅ 確認(かくにん)する<b>までもない</b>
  The pattern attaches to a verb, not to a bare noun. A する-noun needs its
  する.

- ❌ 説明(せつめい)するまでもない<b>だ</b>。 → ✅ 説明(せつめい)する<b>までもない</b>。
  ない is already the predicate. In polite Japanese use
  <code>までもありません</code>.

- ❌ 謝(あやま)る<b>までもない</b>よ、君(きみ)のせいじゃないから。 → ✅ 謝(あやま)る<b>ことはない</b>よ、君(きみ)のせいじゃないから。
  Grammatically fine, humanly wrong: it tells the other person their apology
  would be pointless rather than unnecessary. For comforting, use `ことはない`.

## Practice

**1.** 全員(ぜんいん)の意見(いけん)が一致(いっち)しているので、投票(とうひょう)する____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

投票(とうひょう)する<b>までもない</b>

The outcome is already known from the first clause, so the extra step would add
nothing - which is precisely what <code>までもない</code> expresses. Keep
投票(とうひょう)する in the dictionary form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 指(ゆび)を少(すこ)し切(き)っただけだから、病院(びょういん)に行(い)く（までもない・ほどのことはない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

病院(びょういん)に行(い)く<b>ほどのことはない</b>。

The sentence is measuring the size of the injury against the size of the
response, which is <code>ほどのことはない</code>. <code>までもない</code> would
suggest you already know what the hospital would tell you, a slightly odd thing
to claim about a cut.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It hardly needs saying that this data has not yet been verified."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このデータがまだ検証(けんしょう)されていないことは<b>言(い)うまでもない</b>。

"Hardly needs saying" is the set phrase <code>言(い)うまでもない</code>, and
the whole clause goes in front of it with ことは. In a report you could equally
open with 言(い)うまでもなく and put the clause after it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)報告(ほうこく)しましたので、もう一度(いちど)報告(ほうこく)までもないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)報告(ほうこく)しましたので、もう一度(いちど)報告(ほうこく)<b>するまでもありません</b>。
"I reported it yesterday, so there is no need to report it again."

Two repairs: 報告(ほうこく) is a noun and needs する before までもない, and in
this polite, business-like sentence までもありません is the natural ending.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)に聞(き)く<b>までもない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)に聞(き)く<b>ことはない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says asking him would tell you nothing you do not already know - the answer
is obvious, or you have it from elsewhere. It is about the pointlessness of the
question.

(b) says you are under no obligation to ask him: perhaps it is not his business,
perhaps you may decide alone. Depending on the tone it can even be
encouragement - go ahead without consulting him. The two sentences differ in
what they claim about him, so choosing the wrong one changes who is being
dismissed.

</details>

# ～ほどのことはない

**Meaning** · it's not that big a deal; it doesn't rise to the level of doing that - the response would be out of proportion to the matter

ほど measures an extent, こと is the matter itself. Together they weigh one
against the other and find the matter **too small**: not worth the hospital trip,
not worth the meeting, not worth the fuss. Where までもない says the step would be
redundant, this pattern says the step would be **excessive**.

## Formation

The verb in front is in the **dictionary form**, and it names the response that
is out of proportion.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ほどのことはない | 騒(さわ)ぐ → 騒(さわ)ぐほどのことはない |
| Verb (passive) | passive dictionary form + ほどのことはない | 褒(ほ)められる → 褒(ほ)められるほどのことはない |
| Noun | noun + というほどのことはない | けんか → けんかというほどのことはない |
| Variant | ～ほどのこともない | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)するほどのこともない |
| Past | ～ほどのことはなかった | 慌(あわ)てる → 慌(あわ)てるほどのことはなかった |
| Polite | ～ほどのことはありません | 報告(ほうこく)する → 報告(ほうこく)するほどのことはありません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the は is fixed. ❌騒(さわ)ぐほどのこと**が**ない is not the
pattern; if you hear が there, the speaker is saying something else - that no
such matter exists at all.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
大(おお)げさに騒(さわ)ぐ<b>ほどのことはない</b>。
It's nothing to make a song and dance about.
</pre>

<pre>
傷(きず)は浅(あさ)く、縫(ぬ)う<b>ほどのことはない</b>と言(い)われた。
The cut was shallow, and they told me it didn't need stitching.
</pre>

<pre>
けんか<b>というほどのことはなく</b>、少(すこ)し言(い)い合(あ)っただけだ。
It wasn't what you'd call a fight - we just exchanged a few words.
</pre>

<pre>
遅刻(ちこく)といっても三分(さんぷん)なので、始末書(しまつしょ)を書(か)かせる<b>ほどのことはない</b>。
He was late by three minutes; that hardly calls for a written apology.
</pre>

<pre>
相談(そうだん)に乗(の)っただけで、感謝(かんしゃ)される<b>ほどのことはありません</b>。
I only listened to what he had to say - it's really nothing to thank me for.
</pre>

<pre>
わざわざ本社(ほんしゃ)まで報告(ほうこく)に行(い)く<b>ほどのことはない</b>と課長(かちょう)は判断(はんだん)した。
The section chief decided it was not serious enough to warrant a trip to head office to report it.
</pre>

<pre>
一晩(ひとばん)眠(ねむ)れなかっただけで、検査(けんさ)を受(う)ける<b>ほどのことでもない</b>だろう。
I lost one night's sleep - surely that's not enough to send me for tests.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)は人生(じんせい)の一大事(いちだいじ)のつもりでいたが、振(ふ)り返(かえ)れば悩(なや)み続(つづ)ける<b>ほどのことではなかった</b>。
At the time I took it for a crisis of my whole life; looking back, it was hardly worth agonising over.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Register-neutral and genuinely usable.** Unlike most of the "no need"
expressions at this level, this one belongs to ordinary conversation as much as
to writing, and it is not stiff. The polite form
<code>ほどのことはありません</code> fits an office; the plain form fits a
kitchen table.

Its most graceful use is **about yourself**. Waved at your own injury, your own
trouble or your own kindness -
感謝(かんしゃ)されるほどのことはしていません, "I did nothing worth
thanking me for" - it is modest and warm. Aimed at another person's distress it
becomes a judgement that they are overreacting, and it can wound. Same words,
opposite effect; the direction is everything.

Two variants are common and interchangeable in most contexts. The negative may
be <code>ではない</code> instead of <code>はない</code>, and the noun may take も:
騒(さわ)ぐほどのことでもない. Adding も softens further, as も usually does.

With a noun in front, <code>というほどのことはない</code> denies the label rather
than the response: けんかというほどのことはない says what happened does not
deserve the name "fight" at all.

## Contrast

### ～ほどのことはない vs ～までもない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ほどのことはない</code> weighs the **matter** and finds
it small. <code>までもない</code> weighs the **step** and finds it redundant,
because the answer is already known.

</div>

<pre>
微熱(びねつ)程度(ていど)なので、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)む<b>ほどのことはない</b>。
It's barely a temperature - not enough to take the day off for.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)同(おな)じ質問(しつもん)に答(こた)えたので、説明(せつめい)する<b>までもない</b>。
I answered the same question yesterday, so there's nothing left to explain.
</pre>

Ask what the first clause is doing. If it establishes that the matter is
**minor**, use <code>ほどのことはない</code>. If it establishes that the outcome
is **already settled**, use <code>までもない</code>.

### ～ほどのことはない vs ～ことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことはない` says the action is simply unnecessary.
<code>ほどのことはない</code> adds a scale: it would be **more than the case
deserves**.

</div>

<pre>
急(いそ)ぐ<b>ことはない</b>よ。ゆっくりでいい。
There's no need to rush - take your time.
</pre>

<pre>
数分(すうふん)の遅(おく)れなので、タクシーを使(つか)う<b>ほどのことはない</b>。
We're only a few minutes behind; it hardly justifies a taxi.
</pre>

`ことはない` is often reassurance offered to a person.
<code>ほどのことはない</code> is a proportion being calculated, which is why a
scale word - 数分(すうふん), 少(すこ)し, かすり傷(きず) - so often sits
in the same sentence.

### ～ほどのことはない vs ～ほど…ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ほどのことはない</code> is one fixed expression about
proportion. `ほど…ない` is the comparison you learned earlier: **A is not as ～ as
B**.

</div>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)は去年(きょねん)<b>ほど寒(さむ)くない</b>。
This winter is not as cold as last year's.
</pre>

<pre>
寒(さむ)いが、コートを買(か)い足(た)す<b>ほどのことはない</b>。
It's cold, but not so cold as to need another coat.
</pre>

In the first, ほど has a noun in front of it and the sentence compares two
winters. In the second, ほど follows a verb and is followed by のことはない, and
nothing is being compared - the sentence is deciding whether an action is
warranted.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 騒(さわ)ぐほどのこと<b>が</b>ない → ✅ 騒(さわ)ぐほどのこと<b>はない</b>
  The は is part of the expression. With が the sentence stops being about
  proportion.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は<b>難(むずか)しいほどのことはない</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は<b>悩(なや)むほどのことはない</b>。
  The slot takes a verb naming the response, not an adjective describing the
  thing.

- ❌ 報告(ほうこく)する<b>ほどのことはないだ</b>。 → ✅ 報告(ほうこく)する<b>ほどのことはない</b>。
  ない is the predicate already; だ cannot follow it. The polite form is
  ありません.

- ❌ (to a grieving friend) そんなに落(お)ち込(こ)む<b>ほどのことはない</b>よ。 → ✅ 無理(むり)に元気(げんき)を出(だ)さなくていいよ。
  Correct grammar, badly aimed. Telling someone their grief is out of proportion
  is not comfort; keep this pattern for your own troubles.

## Practice

**1.** ボタンが一(ひと)つ取(と)れただけなので、新(あたら)しいシャツを買(か)う____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

買(か)う<b>ほどのことはない</b>

One missing button is being weighed against buying a whole shirt, and the
response is out of proportion - the core use of the pattern. 買(か)う stays in
the dictionary form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 同(おな)じ説明(せつめい)を朝礼(ちょうれい)でしたばかりだから、もう一度(いちど)伝(つた)える（ほどのことはない・までもない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう一度(いちど)伝(つた)える<b>までもない</b>。

Everyone has already heard the explanation, so repeating it would add nothing -
that is redundancy, not disproportion. <code>ほどのことはない</code> would claim
the information is too trivial to pass on, which is not what the first clause
says.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's only a scratch - nothing worth going to the doctor about."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

かすり傷(きず)だから、医者(いしゃ)に行(い)く<b>ほどのことはない</b>。

The English weighs a small injury against a disproportionate response, which is
exactly this pattern; ほどのことでもない would work equally well and sound a
shade softer.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 少(すこ)し意見(いけん)が合(あ)わなかっただけで、けんかほどのことはなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 少(すこ)し意見(いけん)が合(あ)わなかっただけで、けんか<b>というほどのことはなかった</b>。
"We just disagreed a little; it was nothing you could call a fight."

A noun needs という before ほどのことはない. Without it, けんか sits directly on
ほど and the sentence reads as a comparison with a fight rather than a denial of
the label.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)のしたことは、感謝(かんしゃ)される<b>ほどのことはありません</b>。
(b) それくらいで感謝(かんしゃ)される<b>ほどのことはない</b>でしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is modesty about your own action, and it is one of the politest ways to
decline thanks: what I did was not on the scale that deserves this.

(b) turns the same measurement on someone else and finds **them** wanting - the
speaker is saying the other person is being thanked for too little. The pattern
is neutral; the choice of whose deed is being weighed decides whether it sounds
gracious or grudging.

</details>

# ～ても差し支えない

**Meaning** · it is fine to; there is no objection - permission given impersonally, as an absence of any obstacle

差(さ)し支(つか)える means "to get in the way, to cause a problem", so
差(さ)し支(つか)えない says **nothing stands in the way**. That is what
makes it different from てもいい: the speaker is not granting a favour, they are
reporting that no impediment exists. Hospitals, offices and forms talk this way.

## Formation

Attach も差(さ)し支(つか)えない to the て-form of whatever is being allowed.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + も差(さ)し支(つか)えない | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)っても差(さ)し支(つか)えない |
| Verb (negative) | ～なくても差(さ)し支(つか)えない | 出席(しゅっせき)する → 出席(しゅっせき)しなくても差(さ)し支(つか)えない |
| い-adjective | ～くても差(さ)し支(つか)えない | 遅(おそ)い → 遅(おそ)くても差(さ)し支(つか)えない |
| な-adjective | ～でも差(さ)し支(つか)えない | 簡単(かんたん) → 簡単(かんたん)でも差(さ)し支(つか)えない |
| Noun | ～でも差(さ)し支(つか)えない | 鉛筆(えんぴつ) → 鉛筆(えんぴつ)でも差(さ)し支(つか)えない |
| Polite | ～も差(さ)し支(つか)えありません | 撮(と)っても差(さ)し支(つか)えありません |
| Honorific | ～も差(さ)し支(つか)えございません | 平服(へいふく)でも差(さ)し支(つか)えございません |
| Conditional set phrase | 差(さ)し支(つか)えなければ | - → 差(さ)し支(つか)えなければ、お名前(なまえ)を… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading is **さしつかえない**. 支(つか)える here is the verb of
obstruction; it is not the 支(ささ)える of
<code>支(ささ)える</code>, "to support", although the two share a kanji.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こちらのペンを使(つか)っ<b>ても差(さ)し支(つか)えありません</b>。
You are welcome to use this pen.
</pre>

<pre>
館内(かんない)で写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っ<b>ても差(さ)し支(つか)えないでしょうか</b>。
Would there be any objection to taking photographs inside?
</pre>

<pre>
入浴(にゅうよく)は明日(あした)から<b>でも差(さ)し支(つか)えありません</b>。
You may start bathing again from tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
記入(きにゅう)は鉛筆(えんぴつ)<b>でも差(さ)し支(つか)えない</b>が、署名(しょめい)だけはペンでお願(ねが)いします。
The form may be filled in in pencil, but the signature must be in pen.
</pre>

<pre>

It is quite all right if you cannot attend on the day, so please don't push yourself.
</pre>

<pre>
服装(ふくそう)は平服(へいふく)<b>でも差(さ)し支(つか)えございません</b>が、会場(かいじょう)が冷(ひ)えますので上着(うわぎ)をお持(も)ちください。
Ordinary clothes are perfectly acceptable, but the hall gets cold, so do bring a jacket.
</pre>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)が来週(らいしゅう)になっ<b>ても差(さ)し支(つか)えございません</b>ので、ご都合(つごう)のよいときにお送(おく)りください。
It is no trouble at all if you submit it next week, so please send it when it suits you.
</pre>

<pre>
痛(いた)みが残(のこ)っていても、歩(ある)ける範囲(はんい)で動(うご)かし<b>ても差(さ)し支(つか)えありません</b>と医師(いし)に言(い)われた。
The doctor told me that moving it as much as walking allows would do no harm, even with some pain still there.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Formal, and institutional.** This is the Japanese of doctors, receptionists,
city offices, contracts, guidance notes and business email. Its politeness comes
from being impersonal: no one is being generous, there is simply no obstacle.
That distance is why it is safe to use upward, to a customer or a patient, where
plain てもいい can sound as if you were dispensing favours.

Between friends it is comic. ❌トイレ借(か)りても差(さ)し支(つか)え
ありませんか sounds like a form being read aloud; say
借(か)りてもいい？

Three things worth taking away. The polite form is
差(さ)し支(つか)えありません, and in service Japanese it climbs to
差(さ)し支(つか)えございません. As a **question** -
差(さ)し支(つか)えないでしょうか - it is a very courteous way to ask
permission. And the conditional
<code>差(さ)し支(つか)えなければ</code>, "if it is not inconvenient for
you", is the phrase you will meet most often of all: it is the standard opener
for asking someone something slightly personal.

There is also the affirmative verb, alive and well:
仕事(しごと)に差(さ)し支(つか)える, "it will interfere with work". Meet
that and you have the literal sense the pattern is built on.

## Contrast

### ～ても差(さ)し支(つか)えない vs ～てもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てもいい` is a **person** allowing something.
<code>ても差(さ)し支(つか)えない</code> is an **institution** reporting that
nothing prevents it.

</div>

<pre>
疲(つか)れたら休(やす)ん<b>でもいい</b>よ。
You can take a break if you're tired.
</pre>

<pre>
検査(けんさ)のあとは通常(つうじょう)どおり食事(しょくじ)をなさっ<b>ても差(さ)し支(つか)えありません</b>。
After the examination you may eat as normal.
</pre>

The first is warm and personal and can be said downward or between equals; said
to a customer it is presumptuous. The second is neutral in a way that suits
strangers, and it carries an implication `てもいい` does not: that the matter has
been **assessed** and found harmless.

### ～ても差(さ)し支(つか)えない vs ～ても構(かま)わない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>構(かま)わない</code> = *I* don't mind.
<code>差(さ)し支(つか)えない</code> = *it* causes no problem.

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がかかっ<b>ても構(かま)いません</b>。丁寧(ていねい)にお願(ねが)いします。
I don't mind if it takes a while - please do it carefully.
</pre>

<pre>
原本(げんぽん)でなくコピーを提出(ていしゅつ)し<b>ても差(さ)し支(つか)えありません</b>。
Submitting a copy rather than the original is acceptable.
</pre>

<code>構(かま)わない</code> reveals the speaker's own tolerance, so it is
personal and can sound faintly indifferent -
どちらでも構(かま)わない, "either is fine by me".
<code>差(さ)し支(つか)えない</code> never says how anyone feels; it states a
rule.

### 差(さ)し支(つか)えなければ vs よろしければ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - よろしければ = *if you would like to*.
<code>差(さ)し支(つか)えなければ</code> = *if it causes you no trouble to
tell me* - the form for asking something you have no right to.

</div>

<pre>
<b>よろしければ</b>、こちらのパンフレットもお持(も)ちください。
Do take one of these leaflets as well, if you'd like.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>差(さ)し支(つか)えなければ</b>、ご年齢(ねんれい)をお聞(き)かせいただけますか。
If you don't mind my asking, could you tell me your age?
</pre>

よろしければ offers; <code>差(さ)し支(つか)えなければ</code> intrudes
carefully. Use the second before questions about age, income, family or health,
and the listener is given a graceful way to refuse.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 使(つか)う<b>も差(さ)し支(つか)えない</b> → ✅ 使(つか)っ<b>ても差(さ)し支(つか)えない</b>
  The pattern needs the て-form plus も, exactly as てもいい does.

- ❌ 差(さ)し支(つか)えない<b>です</b>。 → ✅ 差(さ)し支(つか)え<b>ありません</b>。
  ないです is too casual for a phrase this formal, and it clashes with the
  register of everything around it.

- ❌ 差(さ)し支(ささ)えありません → ✅ 差(さ)し支(つか)えありません
  The verb is 差(さ)し支(つか)える. The reading ささ belongs to
  支(ささ)える, "to support", which is a different word.

- ❌ (to a close friend) この席(せき)に座(すわ)っても差(さ)し支(つか)えありませんか。 → ✅ ここ座(すわ)っ<b>てもいい</b>？
  Grammatically perfect, socially absurd. Between friends the institutional
  register turns the question into a joke.

</details>

speaker's goodwill. Said with a smile it is considerate; said flatly, it can be
heard as "we can manage without you".

</details>

# ～にもほどがある

**Meaning** · there is a limit to that; you have gone too far - a reproach thrown at behaviour that has passed the point of acceptability

ほど is 程(ほど), "degree, measure". The sentence says that even for the thing
named there is a **limit**, and the person in front of you has sailed past it.
This is not a description of excess - it is an accusation, and it is delivered
with feeling.

## Formation

Whatever is being criticised goes in front of にもほどがある.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | noun + にもほどがある | 冗談(じょうだん) → 冗談(じょうだん)にもほどがある |
| な-adjective (bare stem) | stem + にもほどがある | 失礼(しつれい) → 失礼(しつれい)にもほどがある |
| い-adjective | plain form + にもほどがある | 図々(ずうずう)しい → 図々(ずうずう)しいにもほどがある |
| Verb | plain form + にもほどがある | ふざける → ふざけるにもほどがある |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + にもほどがある | 考(かんが)えない → 考(かんが)えないにもほどがある |
| Polite | ～にもほどがあります | 勝手(かって) → 勝手(かって)にもほどがあります |

<div class="note">

**Note** - な-adjectives lose their な here.
✅失礼(しつれい)にもほどがある, ❌失礼(しつれい)なにもほどがある. What
precedes にも is treated as a bare noun.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)<b>にもほどがある</b>。
That joke has gone too far.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の日記(にっき)を勝手(かって)に読(よ)むなんて、失礼(しつれい)<b>にもほどがある</b>。
Reading somebody's diary without asking - there are limits to rudeness.
</pre>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)も待(ま)たせておいて連絡(れんらく)もないとは、勝手(かって)<b>にもほどがある</b>。
Keeping me waiting three hours without a word - that is beyond selfish.
</pre>

<pre>
借(か)りたお金(かね)を返(かえ)さないうちにまた貸(か)してくれとは、図々(ずうずう)しい<b>にもほどがある</b>。
Asking to borrow again before repaying the last lot is brass-necked past all reason.
</pre>

<pre>
下調(したしら)べもせずに本番(ほんばん)に臨(のぞ)むとは、甘(あま)く見(み)る<b>にもほどがある</b>。
Walking into the real thing without any preparation is taking it far too lightly.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)り当日(とうじつ)になって企画(きかく)を全部(ぜんぶ)書(か)き直(なお)せというのは、無茶(むちゃ)<b>にもほどがある</b>。
Telling us on the deadline day to rewrite the whole proposal is asking the impossible, and then some.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)の担当(たんとう)だけ終(お)わらせて先(さき)に帰(かえ)るなんて、身勝手(みがって)<b>にもほどがあるだろう</b>。
Finishing your own share and going home ahead of everyone else is about as self-centred as it gets.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)のあった日(ひ)に同(おな)じ道(みち)をあの速(はや)さで走(はし)るとは、懲(こ)りない<b>にもほどがあります</b>。
To drive the same road at that speed on the very day of the accident shows a quite extraordinary failure to learn.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Spoken, blunt and emotional.** It is a scolding, and it is usually said with
raised feeling: to a friend who has overstepped, to a family member, in a
complaint, in an angry post. Because it passes judgement on the listener's
character it is not something to say upward - to a boss or a client it is close
to unusable. In writing it appears in opinion pieces and letters of complaint,
where it lands hard.

The frame is often preceded by a なんて or とは clause naming the offence, and
that is the shape to learn:
<code>～なんて、～にもほどがある</code>. だろう or よ frequently follows, which
is another sign of how spoken it is.

The polite 失礼(しつれい)にもほどがあります exists and appears in written
complaints, but politeness cannot really soften the content - it is a formal
accusation rather than a mild one.

One modern extension is worth recognising. Younger speakers, and the internet
generally, apply the frame to **admirable** qualities as a joke:
かわいいにもほどがある, "too cute to be legal". The literal shape is unchanged -
"there's a limit" - and the humour comes from the mismatch. Recognise it, and
keep your own use to genuine complaints until you are sure of the tone.

## Contrast

### ～にもほどがある vs ～すぎる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すぎる` **describes** an excess. にもほどがある
**accuses** someone of one.

</div>

<pre>
この荷物(にもつ)は重(おも)<b>すぎる</b>。
This luggage is too heavy.
</pre>

<pre>
遅刻(ちこく)して謝(あやま)りもしないとは、いい加減(かげん)<b>にもほどがある</b>。
Turning up late and not even apologising is sloppiness beyond excuse.
</pre>

Luggage cannot be blamed, so only `すぎる` fits the first. In the second,
`すぎる` would report a fact - 遅(おそ)すぎる, "too late" - while
にもほどがある delivers the reproach. Note also that `すぎる` attaches to a stem
and conjugates as a verb; にもほどがある is a fixed phrase and does not.

### ～にもほどがある vs ～も大概(たいがい)にしろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are angry.
<code>にもほどがある</code> **states** that a limit has been passed;
<code>も大概(たいがい)にしろ</code> and いい加減(かげん)にしろ **order**
the person to stop.

</div>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)も<b>大概(たいがい)にしろ</b>。
That's enough of the jokes.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)のせいにするのは、甘(あま)え<b>にもほどがある</b>。
Blaming other people is a childishness that has gone past its limit.
</pre>

The imperative forms are rougher and male-leaning in tone, and they are aimed at
the person directly. <code>にもほどがある</code> can be muttered about someone
absent, and it can appear in writing, which the imperatives cannot.

### ～にもほどがある vs ～には限度(げんど)がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>には限度(げんど)がある</code> states the limit as a
general truth, often as a **warning**. <code>にもほどがある</code> says the limit
has **already** been crossed.

</div>

<pre>
我慢(がまん)<b>には限度(げんど)がある</b>。
There is a limit to what a person can put up with.
</pre>

<pre>
毎回(まいかい)同(おな)じ言(い)い訳(わけ)をするとは、人(ひと)を馬鹿(ばか)にする<b>にもほどがある</b>。
The same excuse every single time - that is taking people for fools, and going well past the limit in doing it.
</pre>

The first can be a calm warning issued before anything has happened; it takes
我慢(がまん), 予算(よさん), 体力(たいりょく) - things that run out. The
second is retrospective and personal, and it is always about behaviour.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 冗談(じょうだん)<b>にほどがある</b> → ✅ 冗談(じょうだん)<b>にもほどがある</b>
  The も is part of the expression - "even for a joke, there is a limit".

- ❌ 失礼(しつれい)<b>なにもほどがある</b> → ✅ 失礼(しつれい)<b>にもほどがある</b>
  な-adjectives appear in their bare stem form before にも.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、その指示(しじ)は<b>無茶(むちゃ)にもほどがあります</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、その指示(しじ)は<b>さすがに難(むずか)しいかと思(おも)います</b>。
  The polite ending does not make the accusation polite. Said upward, this ends
  conversations and occasionally careers.

- ❌ 荷物(にもつ)が重(おも)い<b>にもほどがある</b>。 → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)が<b>重(おも)すぎる</b>。
  A suitcase cannot be blamed for anything. The pattern needs a responsible
  agent behind the excess.

face. If you are angry but do not want to issue orders, (a) is the safer of the
two.

</details>

# ～に忍びない

**Meaning** · I cannot bring myself to do it - the act would be too cruel, too sad or too final, and something in the speaker refuses

忍(しの)ぶ is "to bear, to endure". <code>に忍(しの)びない</code> says the
speaker's feelings cannot endure the act named: throwing the thing away,
watching the suffering, telling the news, walking away. The obstacle is never
practical. It is pity, or attachment, or plain tenderness.

## Formation

The verb in front is in the **dictionary form**, and 忍(しの)びない never
changes shape apart from tense and politeness.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + に忍(しの)びない | 捨(す)てる → 捨(す)てるに忍(しの)びない |
| Verb (perception) | dictionary form + に忍(しの)びない | 見(み)る → 見(み)るに忍(しの)びない |
| Continuing the sentence | ～に忍(しの)びず | 断(ことわ)る → 断(ことわ)るに忍(しの)びず、引(ひ)き受(う)けた |
| Modifying a noun | ～に忍(しの)びない + noun | 見(み)るに忍(しの)びない光景(こうけい) |
| Past | ～に忍(しの)びなかった | 伝(つた)えるに忍(しの)びなかった |
| Polite | ～に忍(しの)びません | お断(ことわ)りするに忍(しの)びません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the fixed form is 忍(しの)びない. The regular negative of
忍(しの)ぶ, ❌忍(しの)ばない, does not appear in this pattern; and the
literary 忍(しの)びず is how the phrase joins onto a following clause in
written Japanese.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
思(おも)い出(で)の品(しな)なので、捨(す)てる<b>に忍(しの)びない</b>。
It's tied up with too many memories - I can't bring myself to throw it out.
</pre>

<pre>
痩(や)せた犬(いぬ)の姿(すがた)は見(み)る<b>に忍(しの)びなかった</b>。
The sight of the thin, starved dog was more than I could stand to look at.
</pre>

<pre>
二十年(にじゅうねん)使(つか)った机(つくえ)を処分(しょぶん)する<b>に忍(しの)びず</b>、実家(じっか)に運(はこ)んだ。
Unable to bring myself to get rid of a desk I'd used for twenty years, I carted it back to my parents' house.
</pre>

<pre>
泣(な)いている子(こ)どもを一人(ひとり)で置(お)いていく<b>に忍(しの)びない</b>。
I can't bear to leave a crying child there on her own.
</pre>

<pre>
落胆(らくたん)する顔(かお)が目(め)に浮(う)かび、結果(けっか)を伝(つた)える<b>に忍(しの)びなかった</b>。
I could picture his face falling, and I could not bring myself to tell him the result.
</pre>

<pre>
被災(ひさい)した町(まち)の写真(しゃしん)は、<b>見(み)るに忍(しの)びない</b>ものばかりだった。
The photographs of the stricken town were almost all of them unbearable to look at.
</pre>

<pre>
世話(せわ)になった人(ひと)の頼(たの)みを断(ことわ)る<b>に忍(しの)びず</b>、結局(けっきょく)引(ひ)き受(う)けてしまった。
I could not find it in me to refuse a request from someone I owe so much to, and ended up taking it on.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)連(つ)れ添(そ)った愛犬(あいけん)を手放(てばな)す<b>に忍(しの)びず</b>、ペットを飼(か)える部屋(へや)を探(さが)し直(なお)した。
Unable to face parting with the dog we'd had all those years, we started the flat hunt again, this time for somewhere that allowed pets.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Literary in origin and formal in tone, but not dead: educated adults use
捨(す)てるに忍(しの)びない and 見(み)るに忍(しの)びない in ordinary
careful speech, and both are common in essays, memoirs and news writing. Among
friends the plain equivalent is
<code>捨(す)てられない</code> or
<code>見(み)ていられない</code>.

The feeling behind it is specific: **pity or attachment**, never inconvenience
and never disgust. Old possessions, animals, elderly relatives, people in
trouble, a request from someone who has been good to you - these are its
subjects. It cannot be used for physical discomfort:
❌この暑(あつ)さは忍(しの)びない should be
耐(た)えられない.

The subject is always the **speaker's own heart**, which is why the pattern
appears in the first person or in narration about a character seen from inside.
You cannot report someone else's unwillingness with it unless you are telling
their story: ❌彼(かれ)は捨(す)てるに忍(しの)びないです is odd in
conversation; 捨(す)てるに忍(しの)びないようだ is how you would say it.

<code>忍(しの)びず</code> is worth learning as a written connector. It carries
the reason into the next clause -
断(ことわ)るに忍(しの)びず、引(ひ)き受(う)けた - and is more elegant
than ので in that position.

## Contrast

### ～に忍(しの)びない vs ～にたえない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both can follow 見(み)る, and they say opposite things
about why. <code>に忍(しの)びない</code> = too **pitiful** to watch.
<code>にたえない</code> = too **awful or too poor** to watch.

</div>

<pre>
痛(いた)みに耐(た)える祖母(そぼ)の様子(ようす)は見(み)る<b>に忍(しの)びなかった</b>。
Watching my grandmother bearing the pain was more than my heart could take.
</pre>

<pre>
殴(なぐ)り合(あ)いの映像(えいぞう)が流(なが)れ、見(み)る<b>にたえなかった</b>。
Footage of the brawl came on and it was sickening to watch.
</pre>

Sympathy versus revulsion. Use <code>に忍(しの)びない</code> when your heart
goes out to what you are seeing, <code>にたえない</code> when you want to look
away in disgust - or when the thing is simply too badly made to sit through.

### ～に忍(しの)びない vs ～かねる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>かねる</code> is a formal, public "I am not in a
position to". <code>に忍(しの)びない</code> is a private "my feelings will not
let me".

</div>

<pre>
その件(けん)につきましては、お答(こた)え<b>いたしかねます</b>。
On that matter I am afraid I am unable to comment.
</pre>

<pre>
恩人(おんじん)の頼(たの)みを断(ことわ)る<b>に忍(しの)びなかった</b>。
I couldn't bring myself to turn down a request from someone who had helped me so much.
</pre>

<code>かねる</code> hides the speaker's feelings behind a policy, which is
exactly why business Japanese likes it. <code>に忍(しの)びない</code> exposes
them. In a refusal letter you would use the first; in the diary entry about
writing it, the second.

### ～に忍(しの)びない vs ～たくない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たくない` is simply not wanting to.
<code>に忍(しの)びない</code> is being unable to, **although** you may well
have decided you should.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は雨(あめ)だから、出(で)かけ<b>たくない</b>。
It's going to rain tomorrow, so I don't want to go out.
</pre>

<pre>
使(つか)わないと分(わ)かっていても、母(はは)の手紙(てがみ)を処分(しょぶん)する<b>に忍(しの)びない</b>。
I know I'll never look at them again, and still I cannot bring myself to get rid of my mother's letters.
</pre>

The second sentence contains a conflict; the first does not. That conflict -
reason on one side, feeling on the other - is what the pattern is for, and it is
why a clause like と分(わ)かっていても sits so naturally in front of it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 捨(す)てる<b>のに忍(しの)びない</b> → ✅ 捨(す)てる<b>に忍(しの)びない</b>
  The dictionary form joins straight to に. There is no の.

- ❌ 見(み)るに<b>忍(しの)ばない</b> → ✅ 見(み)るに<b>忍(しの)びない</b>
  The expression is frozen in the 忍(しの)びない shape; the ordinary negative
  of 忍(しの)ぶ does not appear here.

- ❌ 見(み)るに忍(しの)びない<b>です</b>。 → ✅ 見(み)るに<b>忍(しの)びません</b>。
  A literary phrase with ないです attached sounds patched together; use
  忍(しの)びません.

- ❌ この暑(あつ)さは忍(しの)びない。 → ✅ この暑(あつ)さには<b>耐(た)えられない</b>。
  Heat is endured, not pitied. The pattern needs compassion or attachment behind
  it.

## Practice

**1.** 祖父(そふ)が大切(たいせつ)にしていた時計(とけい)なので、壊(こわ)れていても捨(す)てる____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

捨(す)てる<b>に忍(しの)びない</b>

The watch is useless and the speaker still cannot part with it - reason on one
side, attachment on the other, which is the situation the pattern exists for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 規則(きそく)ですので、その書類(しょるい)のコピーは（お渡(わた)しするに忍(しの)びません・お渡(わた)しいたしかねます）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

コピーは<b>お渡(わた)しいたしかねます</b>。

The refusal rests on a rule, not on the speaker's feelings, so the impersonal
<code>かねる</code> is right. <code>に忍(しの)びない</code> would imply the
clerk's heart was breaking over the photocopy.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The children's faces in the photographs were more than I could bear to look at."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

写真(しゃしん)に写(うつ)った子(こ)どもたちの顔(かお)は、見(み)る<b>に忍(しの)びなかった</b>。

"More than I could bear to look at", with pity as the feeling, is
<code>見(み)るに忍(しの)びない</code>; the past tense fits because the
speaker is recalling the moment. <code>見(み)るにたえない</code> would suggest
disgust instead of compassion.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 世話(せわ)になった先輩(せんぱい)の頼(たの)みだったので、断(ことわ)るのに忍(しの)ばなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 世話(せわ)になった先輩(せんぱい)の頼(たの)みだったので、断(ことわ)る<b>に忍(しの)びなかった</b>。
"It was a request from a senior colleague I owed a lot to, and I couldn't bring myself to refuse."

Two repairs: の does not come between the dictionary form and に, and the phrase
is fixed as 忍(しの)びない, so the past is 忍(しの)びなかった rather than
❌忍(しの)ばなかった.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この服(ふく)は捨(す)てられない。
(b) この服(ふく)は捨(す)てる<b>に忍(しの)びない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the plain potential negative and covers everything from sentiment to "the
bin day has passed". It is what you would actually say to a family member while
tidying.

(b) narrows the reason to feeling: the clothes carry something - a person, a
time - and disposing of them would be an act the speaker's heart will not
perform. It is also a register step up, and in the middle of a clear-out it can
sound faintly theatrical unless the attachment is real.

</details>

# ～そびれる

**Meaning** · to miss the chance to do something you meant to do - the moment came, and it went past

You intended to say it, ask it, eat it, go. Then the conversation moved on, the
shop shut, the person left. <code>そびれる</code> names that miss: not a failure
of ability and not forgetfulness, but an **opportunity that slipped by** while
you hesitated. A small regret comes with it as standard.

## Formation

Attach そびれる to the ます-stem. It conjugates as a る-verb, and in practice it
appears in the past far more often than anywhere else.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + そびれる | 言(い)う → 言(い)いそびれる |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + そびれる | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べそびれる |
| Verb (する-verb) | ～し + そびれる | 連絡(れんらく)する → 連絡(れんらく)しそびれる |
| Usual past | ～そびれた | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)きそびれた |
| With regret | ～そびれてしまう | 買(か)う → 買(か)いそびれてしまった |
| Polite | ～そびれました | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)しそびれました |
| Modifying a noun | ～そびれた + noun | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)しそびれた手紙(てがみ) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb in front must be something you **chose** to do. Volition is
built into the meaning, so ❌忘(わす)れそびれる and
❌分(わ)かりそびれる are impossible: you cannot miss your chance to forget or
to understand.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
大事(だいじ)なことを言(い)い<b>そびれた</b>。
I missed my chance to say the thing that mattered.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しくて昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べ<b>そびれた</b>。
I was so busy I never got round to lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)が盛(も)り上(あ)がって、すっかり帰(かえ)り<b>そびれて</b>しまった。
The conversation took off and I quite missed the moment to leave.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)を聞(き)き<b>そびれた</b>まま、連絡(れんらく)が取(と)れなくなった。
I never did get around to asking her name, and now I have no way to reach her.
</pre>

<pre>
話題(わだい)の展覧会(てんらんかい)は、結局(けっきょく)見(み)<b>そびれた</b>。
In the end I never made it to that exhibition everyone was talking about.
</pre>

<pre>
手(て)を挙(あ)げかけたところで司会(しかい)が話(はなし)を進(すす)めてしまい、質問(しつもん)し<b>そびれた</b>。
I had my hand half up when the chair moved things along, and my chance to ask was gone.
</pre>

<pre>
昨夜(さくや)は考(かんが)え事(ごと)をしていて寝(ね)<b>そびれ</b>、明(あ)け方(がた)まで本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいた。
Last night my mind was too busy for me to drop off, and I read until it started getting light.
</pre>

<pre>
退職(たいしょく)される日(ひ)に一言(ひとこと)お礼(れい)を申(もう)し上(あ)げたかったのに、人(ひと)が多(おお)くて声(こえ)をかけ<b>そびれて</b>しまった。
I had wanted to thank her on her last day, but there were so many people that I never found my moment.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Neutral and everyday.** It belongs to ordinary conversation and to ordinary
writing alike, carries no formality of its own, and takes ます politely without
any awkwardness. Nothing about it is literary, which makes it one of the more
immediately usable patterns at this level.

Three conditions have to hold. You **meant** to do the thing; there was a
**specific opportunity**; and that opportunity **passed**. Take away the
intention and the sentence stops working; take away the specific moment and
結局(けっきょく)しなかった is the better wording.

The feeling is mild - awkwardness, or a small regret - and it is often softened
further with てしまう. It is a very common way of explaining oneself:
言(い)いそびれてしまって, "I never got the chance to mention it", excuses a
silence without claiming you forgot.

Two idiomatic members of the family are worth knowing.
寝(ね)そびれる is failing to fall asleep when you meant to, and
帰(かえ)りそびれる is staying on past the point where leaving was easy - the
awkward guest's verb.

One boundary. <code>そびれる</code> is not
<code>～ようがない</code>: 言(い)いようがない means there is no way to put the
thing into words at all, while 言(い)いそびれた means the words existed and the
moment did not.

## Contrast

### ～そびれる vs ～損(そこ)ねる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>そびれる</code> is about the **chance**: you never got
to start. <code>損(そこ)ねる</code> is about the **attempt**: you had a go and
it went wrong.

</div>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)に乗(の)り<b>そびれて</b>、タクシーで帰(かえ)った。
I missed my chance to catch the last train and went home by taxi.
</pre>

<pre>
受(う)け取(と)り<b>損(そこ)ねた</b>グラスが床(ゆか)で割(わ)れた。
The glass I fumbled as it was handed over smashed on the floor.
</pre>

Nobody fumbles a train, and nobody misses their opportunity to catch a falling
glass - they reach for it and fail. Where both are possible, as with
食(た)べそびれる and 食(た)べ損(そこ)ねる, the first says the meal never
began and the second leans towards its going wrong.

### ～そびれる vs ～忘(わす)れる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>言(い)い忘(わす)れる</code>: it went out of your
head. <code>言(い)いそびれる</code>: it stayed in your head the whole time,
and the moment never came.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)の件(けん)、伝(つた)え<b>忘(わす)れて</b>いました。すみません。
Sorry - I forgot to pass on the message about yesterday's meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
言(い)おうとは思(おも)っていたのですが、話(はなし)の流(なが)れで伝(つた)え<b>そびれました</b>。
I did mean to tell you, but the way the conversation went I never got the chance.
</pre>

The difference is entirely about what was in your mind. Forgetting is an
apology for carelessness; そびれる is an explanation involving no fault at all -
which is precisely why it is the more diplomatic of the two.

### ～そびれる vs ～ずじまい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>そびれる</code> reports **one missed moment**, with the
story still running. `ずじまい` is the **verdict at the end**: it never happened
at all.

</div>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)が混(こ)んでいて、挨拶(あいさつ)し<b>そびれた</b>。
Reception was crowded and I didn't manage to say hello.
</pre>

<pre>
結局(けっきょく)、最後(さいご)まで挨拶(あいさつ)できず<b>じまい</b>だった。
In the end I never greeted him at all.
</pre>

You can miss your chance at four o'clock and take it at five;
そびれた does not close the door. `ずじまい` does, and it looks back over a
finished occasion, which is why 結局(けっきょく) and 最後(さいご)まで sit
so comfortably with it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 言(い)う<b>そびれた</b> → ✅ 言(い)い<b>そびれた</b>
  The base is the ます-stem, not the dictionary form:
  言(い)います → 言(い)い.

- ❌ 名前(なまえ)を忘(わす)れ<b>そびれた</b>。 → ✅ 名前(なまえ)を<b>忘(わす)れてしまった</b>。
  There is no chance to be missed here. The verb in front must name something
  you were trying to do.

- ❌ 映画(えいが)の大事(だいじ)な場面(ばめん)を<b>見(み)そびれた</b>。 → ✅ 映画(えいが)の大事(だいじ)な場面(ばめん)を<b>見逃(みのが)した</b>。
  見(み)そびれる is never getting to the film at all. A scene that went past
  while you were watching is 見逃(みのが)す.

- ❌ 昔(むかし)から留学(りゅうがく)し<b>そびれて</b>いる。 → ✅ 昔(むかし)から留学(りゅうがく)<b>したいと思(おも)いつつ、できずにいる</b>。
  The pattern needs one identifiable moment that passed, not a vague ambition
  never acted on.

## Practice

**1.** 課長(かちょう)に相談(そうだん)しようと思(おも)っていたが、ずっと席(せき)を外(はず)していて____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

相談(そうだん)し<b>そびれた</b>

The speaker meant to consult him and a specific opportunity slipped away because
he was never at his desk - intention plus a lost moment, which is exactly what
そびれる reports. Note that a する-verb keeps its し.

</details>

**2.** Choose: すみません、昨日(きのう)の変更(へんこう)をお伝(つた)え（そびれました・忘(わす)れていました）。完全(かんぜん)に頭(あたま)から抜(ぬ)けていました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お伝(つた)え<b>忘(わす)れていました</b>。

The second sentence says it went clean out of the speaker's head, so the mistake
is forgetfulness. そびれる would contradict it, since it claims the message was
in mind the whole time and only the moment was lacking.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I meant to ask for her contact details, but the taxi came and I missed my chance."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

連絡先(れんらくさき)を聞(き)こうと思(おも)っていたのに、タクシーが来(き)てしまって聞(き)き<b>そびれた</b>。

"Meant to, but missed my chance" is そびれる almost word for word, and the のに
clause supplies the intention the pattern requires. 来(き)て is the て-form of
来(く)る - note the reading changes.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨夜(さくや)は疲(つか)れすぎて、寝(ね)るそびれてしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨夜(さくや)は疲(つか)れすぎて、<b>寝(ね)そびれて</b>しまいました。
"I was overtired last night and never managed to get to sleep."

そびれる attaches to the ます-stem, and for 寝(ね)る that stem is simply
寝(ね). 寝(ね)そびれる is idiomatic for lying awake past the moment when
sleep was possible.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)にお礼(れい)を言(い)い<b>そびれた</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)にお礼(れい)を言(い)い<b>忘(わす)れた</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the thanks were ready and the moment never arrived - he was surrounded,
or he left early, or the conversation turned. No carelessness is being admitted,
and there is a hint that the speaker still feels the words are owed.

(b) admits that it went out of the speaker's mind altogether. As an apology it is
more honest and rather less flattering to the speaker, which is why (a) is what
people reach for when they can honestly claim it.

</details>

# ～損なう / ～損ねる

**Meaning** · to fail in the attempt; to do something amiss - and, with a noun, to damage or impair it

損(そこ)なう and 損(そこ)ねる are the same idea in two shapes. After a
ます-stem they say the attempt **went wrong or came to nothing**: you wrote it
badly, you reached for the train and did not catch it. After a noun with を they
say something valuable has been **spoiled** - health, trust, someone's mood.

## Formation

損(そこ)なう is an う-verb (損(そこ)なった, 損(そこ)ないます);
損(そこ)ねる is a る-verb (損(そこ)ねた, 損(そこ)ねます). The two are
interchangeable in most sentences.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (failed attempt) | ます-stem + 損(そこ)なう | 書(か)く → 書(か)き損(そこ)なう |
| Verb (failed attempt) | ます-stem + 損(そこ)ねる | 乗(の)る → 乗(の)り損(そこ)ねる |
| Noun (damage) | noun + を損(そこ)なう | 健康(けんこう) → 健康(けんこう)を損(そこ)なう |
| Noun (damage) | noun + を損(そこ)ねる | 機嫌(きげん) → 機嫌(きげん)を損(そこ)ねる |
| Past | ～損(そこ)なった ／ ～損(そこ)ねた | 取(と)り損(そこ)ねた |
| Polite | ～損(そこ)ないます ／ ～損(そこ)ねます | 言(い)い損(そこ)ねました |
| Risk of | ～を損(そこ)なうおそれがある | 信用(しんよう)を損(そこ)なうおそれがある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - which verb goes with which noun is largely fixed by habit. Feelings
take 損(そこ)ねる (機嫌(きげん)を損(そこ)ねる,
気分(きぶん)を損(そこ)ねる); health, trust, value and scenery take
損(そこ)なう (健康(けんこう)を損(そこ)なう,
信用(しんよう)を損(そこ)なう). Neither pairing is a rule, but going against
it sounds slightly off.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
宛名(あてな)を書(か)き<b>損(そこ)なって</b>、封筒(ふうとう)を一枚(いちまい)無駄(むだ)にした。
I wrote the address wrong and wasted an envelope.
</pre>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)で転(ころ)びかけて、電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)り<b>損(そこ)ねた</b>。
I nearly fell on the stairs and ended up not catching my train.
</pre>

<pre>
言(い)い方(かた)がきつかったせいで、相手(あいて)の機嫌(きげん)を<b>損(そこ)ねて</b>しまった。
I put it too harshly and put the other person's back up.
</pre>

<pre>
睡眠(すいみん)を削(けず)る生活(せいかつ)を続(つづ)ければ、いずれ健康(けんこう)を<b>損(そこ)なう</b>。
Go on living on short sleep and sooner or later your health will suffer for it.
</pre>

<pre>
大事(だいじ)な場面(ばめん)でボールを取(と)り<b>損(そこ)ね</b>、そこから逆転(ぎゃくてん)された。
He fumbled the ball at the crucial moment, and from there the game turned.
</pre>

<pre>
うっかり口(くち)を滑(すべ)らせて、彼女(かのじょ)の気分(きぶん)を<b>損(そこ)ねた</b>らしい。
I let something slip without thinking, and it seems to have upset her.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)を後回(あとまわ)しにしたことが、かえって会社(かいしゃ)の信用(しんよう)を<b>損(そこ)なう</b>結果(けっか)となった。
Putting off the explanation ended up doing the company's good name more harm than good.
</pre>

<pre>
発言(はつげん)の一部(いちぶ)だけを切(き)り取(と)って伝(つた)えると、話(はな)し手(て)の意図(いと)を<b>損(そこ)なう</b>おそれがある。
Quoting only part of what someone said risks doing violence to what they meant.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Both uses are **ordinary Japanese**, neither literary nor casual. Between the two
verbs there is a slight tilt: 損(そこ)なう leans written and formal - reports,
warnings, notices - while 損(そこ)ねる is a little more conversational. In the
ます-stem use they are near-perfect substitutes, and speakers pick whichever
comes out.

In the failed-attempt sense the key is that **an attempt was made**. You reached
for the ball, you wrote the character, you went for the train. That is what
separates it from そびれる, where the chance never came round at all, and from
<code>～ようがない</code>, where no means of doing it existed in the first place.

機嫌(きげん)を損(そこ)ねる deserves its own line: it is the standard way to
say you have annoyed or offended someone, and it is everywhere in accounts of
office life, family life and customer service. It is gentler than
怒(おこ)らせる - the other person has gone quiet and cool rather than shouted.

Two idioms to recognise. 死(し)に損(そこ)なう is "to fail to die", used of
narrow escapes and, harshly, of oneself; and
見(み)損(そこ)なう has a second life meaning "to misjudge a person" -
君(きみ)を見(み)損(そこ)なった, "I thought better of you", which is a
serious thing to say.

## Contrast

### ～損(そこ)ねる vs ～そびれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>損(そこ)ねる</code>: you **tried** and it went
wrong. <code>そびれる</code>: the **moment** passed and you never started.

</div>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の名前(なまえ)を聞(き)き<b>そびれた</b>まま、歓迎会(かんげいかい)が終(お)わった。
The welcome party ended without my ever finding the chance to ask the new starter's name.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りがうるさくて、集合(しゅうごう)時間(じかん)を聞(き)き<b>損(そこ)ねた</b>。
It was so noisy around us that I failed to catch what time we were meeting.
</pre>

In the first, the question was never asked. In the second, the speaker was
listening and the information did not get through. The test is whether an
attempt took place.

### 書(か)き損(そこ)なう vs 書(か)き間違(まちが)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間違(まちが)える</code> is about the **content**
being wrong. <code>損(そこ)なう</code> is about the **execution** failing -
the thing came out spoiled.

</div>

<pre>
日付(ひづけ)を書(か)き<b>間違(まちが)えて</b>、来月(らいげつ)と書(か)いてしまった。
I wrote the wrong date and put next month.
</pre>

<pre>
ペンがかすれて署名(しょめい)を書(か)き<b>損(そこ)ない</b>、用紙(ようし)をもらい直(なお)した。
The pen skipped and ruined my signature, so I asked for a fresh form.
</pre>

A wrong date is correct writing of incorrect information; a smeared signature is
the right information written badly. Where English says "I made a mistake" for
both, Japanese separates them.

### ～損(そこ)ねる vs ～逃(のが)す

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>損(そこ)ねる</code>: your attempt failed.
<code>逃(のが)す</code>: something slipped past you while you were there -
you overlooked it or let it go.

</div>

<pre>
一瞬(いっしゅん)よそ見(み)をして、決勝(けっしょう)ゴールを見(み)<b>逃(のが)した</b>。
I looked away for a second and missed the winning goal.
</pre>

<pre>
ボールを受(う)け止(と)め<b>損(そこ)ねて</b>、後(うし)ろに逸(そ)らしてしまった。
I failed to stop the ball and let it get past me.
</pre>

逃(のが)す takes a chance or a sight that goes by -
機会(きかい)を逃(のが)す, 見逃(みのが)す, 聞(き)き逃(のが)す -
and often implies inattention. 損(そこ)ねる implies effort that fell short.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 書(か)く<b>損(そこ)なう</b> → ✅ 書(か)き<b>損(そこ)なう</b>
  The base is the ます-stem, as with そびれる and かねる.

- ❌ 機嫌(きげん)を損(そこ)な<b>て</b>しまった → ✅ 機嫌(きげん)を損(そこ)な<b>って</b>しまった
  損(そこ)なう is an う-verb, so its て-form is 損(そこ)なって.
  損(そこ)ねる, a る-verb, gives 損(そこ)ねて - do not mix the two
  conjugations.

- ❌ 無理(むり)をして<b>健康(けんこう)を壊(こわ)した</b>。 → ✅ 無理(むり)をして<b>健康(けんこう)を損(そこ)なった</b>。
  健康(けんこう) collocates with 損(そこ)なう. If you want
  壊(こわ)す, the noun changes: 体(からだ)を壊(こわ)した.

- ❌ 映画(えいが)の最後(さいご)の場面(ばめん)を<b>見(み)損(そこ)なった</b>。 → ✅ 映画(えいが)の最後(さいご)の場面(ばめん)を<b>見逃(みのが)した</b>。
  A scene that went past while you were watching is
  見逃(みのが)す. 見(み)損(そこ)なう is heard mainly in its other
  sense, misjudging a person.

## Practice

**1.** バスの中(なか)が混(こ)んでいて、降(お)りる準備(じゅんび)が間(ま)に合(あ)わず、降(お)り____しまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

降(お)り<b>損(そこ)ねて</b>しまった

The speaker was trying to get off and did not manage it in time, so the attempt
failed - 損(そこ)ねる. 降(お)り損(そこ)なって would be equally correct
and sounds a shade more formal.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 言(い)い方(かた)が悪(わる)かったのか、部長(ぶちょう)の機嫌(きげん)を（損(そこ)ねて・そびれて）しまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)の機嫌(きげん)を<b>損(そこ)ねて</b>しまった。

Only 損(そこ)ねる takes a noun with を and means "spoil, damage"; そびれる
attaches to a ます-stem and reports a missed opportunity, so it cannot take an
object at all. 機嫌(きげん)を損(そこ)ねる is a fixed pairing worth
memorising.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Cutting corners on inspections risks damaging the trust we have built up."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

検査(けんさ)を省略(しょうりゃく)することは、これまで築(きず)いてきた信頼(しんらい)を<b>損(そこ)なう</b>おそれがある。

信頼(しんらい) or 信用(しんよう) plus 損(そこ)なう is the standard
collocation for damaged trust, and
<code>～おそれがある</code> carries the "risks" of the English. The written
損(そこ)なう suits this warning-notice register better than
損(そこ)ねる.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 字(じ)を書(か)く損(そこ)なてしまったので、はじめから書(か)き直(なお)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 字(じ)を<b>書(か)き損(そこ)なって</b>しまったので、はじめから書(か)き直(なお)します。
"I made a mess of the writing, so I'll start again from the top."

Two repairs: the base is the ます-stem 書(か)き, not the dictionary form, and
損(そこ)なう is an う-verb whose て-form is 損(そこ)なって.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 終電(しゅうでん)に乗(の)り<b>損(そこ)ねた</b>。
(b) 終電(しゅうでん)に乗(の)り<b>そびれた</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) pictures the attempt: the speaker was on the platform, or running for it,
and the doors closed. Something was tried and it failed by a margin.

(b) pictures the evening: the speaker meant to catch it, the drinks went on, and
the moment to leave never arrived. Nothing was attempted at all. Both sentences
end with the speaker stranded, but they explain how differently, and a listener
will read the story behind them accordingly.

</details>
