# N1 - Having said that, and in spite of it


---

# とはいえ

**Meaning** · that said; even so; granted, but - grants the point that has just been made, then reports that the thing it led you to expect did not happen

`とはいえ` is `と` + `は` + the old imperative-shaped form of 言(い)う, and it still
behaves like its literal sense: *although one says that*. Something has been
established - by you, by the other person, or by the world at large - and this word
concedes it in full before turning to the part of reality that failed to follow.

## Usage

`とはいえ` sits in three positions, and all three are common.

- **Straight after a noun**, with no `だ`. This is its most distinctive shape and
  the one that has no easy English equivalent:
  子(こ)どもとはいえ、約束(やくそく)は約束(やくそく)だ。
- **After a plain clause**, verb or adjective:
  値段(ねだん)が下(さ)がったとはいえ、まだ高(たか)い。
  い-adjectives take no `だ` before it - ❌高(たか)いだとはいえ. な-adjectives and
  nouns usually drop the `だ` too, though 便利(べんり)だとはいえ also occurs.
- **Opening a new sentence**, after a full stop, followed by a comma:
  作業(さぎょう)は終(お)わった。とはいえ、確認(かくにん)が残(のこ)っている。

What follows `とはいえ` is a **statement of how things actually are**. The clause in
front of it must be something the listener is already prepared to accept; `とはいえ`
does not introduce new information, it disarms information already on the table.

## Examples

<pre>
春(はる)<b>とはいえ</b>、朝(あさ)はまだ寒(さむ)い。
It may be spring, but the mornings are still cold.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)くなった。<b>とはいえ</b>、私(わたし)にはまだ手(て)が出(で)ない。
The price has come down. Even so, it's still beyond me.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)した<b>とはいえ</b>、すぐに仕事(しごと)が見(み)つかるとは限(かぎ)らない。
Passing the exam is one thing; it hardly guarantees you'll find work straight away.
</pre>

<pre>
景気(けいき)は回復(かいふく)傾向(けいこう)にある<b>とはいえ</b>、中小(ちゅうしょう)企業(きぎょう)を取(と)り巻(ま)く環境(かんきょう)は依然(いぜん)として厳(きび)しい。
The economy may be trending upwards, but conditions for smaller firms remain harsh.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`とはいえ` is **written Japanese first**. Editorials, columns, reports and business
email are its home, and in speech it belongs to the register where someone is
choosing their words: a meeting, a presentation, an interview. It is not stiff
enough to sound odd in careful conversation, but dropped into chat with friends it
sounds as though you are quoting yourself. The everyday versions are `でも`,
`そうはいっても` and `とはいっても`.

The tone is fair-minded rather than combative. By conceding first, the speaker
signals that they are not brushing the other side away - they have taken the point
and are adding the qualification anyway. That is why it is so common in the second
half of a written argument, and why a `とはいえ` sentence rarely sounds rude even
when it is disagreeing.

It is not marked for age or gender, and it has no contracted form. The one thing to
watch is that its noun-attaching use covers a lot of ground:
新人(しんじん)とはいえ、冗談(じょうだん)とはいえ、一度(いちど)とはいえ - "new though
he is", "joke though it was", "even if only once". This is the shape you will meet
most often in print.

## Contrast

### とはいえ vs とはいうものの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same move, at slightly different speeds. `とはいえ` is
compressed and can hang straight off a bare noun; `とはいうものの` spells the
concession out and reads a shade softer.

</div>

<pre>
素人(しろうと)<b>とはいえ</b>、これはひどい。
Amateur or not, this is bad.
</pre>

<pre>
素人(しろうと)だ<b>とはいうものの</b>、これはひどい。
He's an amateur, granted - but this is bad.
</pre>

The difference is genuinely thin, and in a long clause either will pass unnoticed.
Where they part company is at the extremes: a bare noun plus `とはいえ` is idiomatic
and tight, while `とはいうものの` prefers a full clause behind it and is the one you
would more readily say out loud.

### とはいえ vs にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にもかかわらず` reports a **contradiction between two facts**.
`とはいえ` adds the speaker's **hand on the brake**: I accept that, now hold on.

</div>

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、会場(かいじょう)は満員(まんいん)だった。
Despite the heavy rain, the venue was full.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は満員(まんいん)だった<b>とはいえ</b>、売上(うりあげ)は目標(もくひょう)に届(とど)かなかった。
The venue was full, true - but takings still fell short of target.
</pre>

`にもかかわらず` is neutral and can be written by someone with no stake in the
matter; it simply notes that A did not prevent B. `とはいえ` always has a speaker
behind it, weighing something up. That is also why `にもかかわらず` sits comfortably
in a statistical report and `とはいえ` sits in the commentary next to it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 高(たか)いだ<b>とはいえ</b>、質(しつ)はいい。 → ✅ 高(たか)い<b>とはいえ</b>、質(しつ)はいい。
  The `と` is the quotative particle, so it takes a plain form. い-adjectives never
  carry `だ`.

- ❌ 京都(きょうと)<b>とはいえ</b>、紅葉(こうよう)が有名(ゆうめい)ですね。 → ✅ 京都(きょうと)<b>といえば</b>、紅葉(こうよう)が有名(ゆうめい)ですね。
  `といえば` is "speaking of"; `とはいえ` is "although one says that". The single
  `は` flips the meaning completely, and this is the most common mix-up.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は月曜日(げつようび)だ。<b>とはいえ</b>、明日(あした)は火曜日(かようび)だ。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は月曜日(げつようび)だ。だから、明日(あした)は火曜日(かようび)だ。
  Nothing has been conceded and nothing has gone against expectation. `とはいえ`
  needs a defeated expectation to work on.

- ❌ この案(あん)はいい<b>とはいえ</b>、採用(さいよう)しましょう。 → ✅ この案(あん)はいい<b>とはいえ</b>、問題(もんだい)も残(のこ)っている。
  What comes after `とはいえ` is a report of the awkward reality, not a proposal
  that agrees with the first half.

## Practice

**1.** 新人(しんじん)____、客(きゃく)の前(まえ)であの態度(たいど)は許(ゆる)されない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新人(しんじん)<b>とはいえ</b>、客(きゃく)の前(まえ)であの態度(たいど)は許(ゆる)されない。

Being new is granted as a genuine excuse, and then refused anyway - the classic
noun-plus-`とはいえ` shape. Note that no `だ` appears between the noun and `と`.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word:
北海道(ほっかいどう)（といえば・とはいえ）、やはり海(うみ)の幸(さち)でしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>といえば</b>、やはり海(うみ)の幸(さち)でしょう。

The sentence picks a topic up and runs with it, which is `といえば`, "speaking of".
Nothing is being conceded, so `とはいえ` has nothing to work against.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 給料(きゅうりょう)は上(あ)がった<b>が</b>、生活(せいかつ)は楽(らく)にならない。
(b) 給料(きゅうりょう)は上(あ)がった<b>とはいえ</b>、生活(せいかつ)は楽(らく)にならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply puts two facts side by side with a plain "but". It is neutral, spoken,
and could open a conversation from nothing.

(b) treats the pay rise as something already claimed - by the company, by the news,
by whoever the speaker is answering - and grants it before pushing back. It reads as
a written argument, and it implies there is an opposing view in the room.

</details>

# とはいうものの

**Meaning** · having said that; be that as it may - the speaker repeats a claim already on the table, allows it, and then reports the part of reality that does not match it

Read it literally and it explains itself: `と` (that) + `は` (as for) +
言(い)う (say) + `ものの` (although). *Although one says that.* Something has been
asserted - by the speaker a moment ago, by an official notice, by common opinion -
and this word picks the assertion up whole before setting the awkward facts beside
it.

## Usage

Two positions, both very common.

- **Opening a sentence**, after your own preceding statement, followed by a comma.
  This is the shape you will meet most often:
  準備(じゅんび)は整(ととの)った。とはいうものの、不安(ふあん)は残(のこ)る。
- **After a plain clause**, joining the two halves inside one sentence:
  便利(べんり)だとはいうものの、値段(ねだん)が高(たか)すぎる。

い-adjectives and verbs go in plain, with no `だ` - ❌安(やす)いだとはいうものの.
Nouns and な-adjectives usually keep the `だ`, and a bare noun in front of it is
possible but is the slot where `とはいえ` is the idiomatic choice.

What follows is always a **statement of how things stand**, and it must undercut the
claim rather than agree with it. If nothing has been asserted, there is nothing for
`とはいうものの` to concede, and the sentence will read as though its first half has
gone missing.

## Examples

<pre>
夏(なつ)は終(お)わった。<b>とはいうものの</b>、暑(あつ)さは続(つづ)いている。
Summer is over. That said, the heat is still with us.
</pre>

<pre>
便利(べんり)だ<b>とはいうものの</b>、この機能(きのう)は誰(だれ)も使(つか)っていない。
Handy, they say - but nobody actually uses this feature.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)に入(はい)った<b>とはいうものの</b>、授業(じゅぎょう)にはほとんど出(で)ていないらしい。
He got into university, apparently, though he hardly ever goes to lectures.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)で禁止(きんし)されている<b>とはいうものの</b>、実際(じっさい)に守(まも)っている部署(ぶしょ)はほとんどない。
The rules forbid it, true enough, but there is barely a department that observes them.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This belongs to **considered language**: essays, columns, reports, the reflective
middle of a business email. It also survives in careful speech - a presentation, an
interview, a person thinking aloud on the radio - because it buys the speaker a
moment while they turn the argument round. Among friends the same move is made with
`そうはいっても`, `とはいっても` or a plain `でも`.

Roughly, and only roughly: `とはいえ` reads as the most written of the family,
`とはいうものの` a step less so, `とはいっても` a step less again, and
`そうはいっても` is at home in speech. Treat that as a tendency; all four turn up
in print and none of them is wrong out loud.

The tone is honest rather than adversarial. Because the speaker has just conceded
the point in full, the objection that follows lands as fairness, not as
contradiction - which is why writers reach for it when they are about to complicate
their own argument. Overused, it starts to sound as though the writer cannot commit
to anything.

## Contrast

### とはいうものの vs ～ものの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ものの` attaches to the fact itself. `とはいうものの` attaches
to the **claim about** the fact - somebody said it, and the speaker is quoting it
back before dissenting.

</div>

<pre>
申(もう)し込(こ)んだ<b>ものの</b>、返事(へんじ)は来(こ)なかった。
I applied, but no reply ever came.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)でも申(もう)し込(こ)める<b>とはいうものの</b>、条件(じょうけん)は細(こま)かい。
Anyone may apply, so they say - but the conditions are fiddly.
</pre>

In the first sentence the applying simply happened; the speaker reports it and then
reports the shortfall. In the second, "anyone may apply" is somebody's line - a
poster, a website, a recruiter - and the sentence sets it against the small print.
That quoting layer is what the extra <code>とは言(い)う</code> adds, and it is why
`とはいうものの` can stand alone at the head of a sentence while bare `ものの` cannot.

### とはいうものの vs そうはいっても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same sentence in two registers. `そうはいっても` is what you
say; `とはいうものの` is what you write.

</div>

<pre>
節約(せつやく)しないとね。<b>そうはいっても</b>、外食(がいしょく)はやめられないよ。
We ought to economise. Even so, I can't give up eating out.
</pre>

<pre>
節約(せつやく)が必要(ひつよう)である。<b>とはいうものの</b>、生活(せいかつ)の質(しつ)を落(お)とすわけにはいかない。
Economising is necessary. That said, we cannot let our standard of living slip.
</pre>

`そうはいっても` points back with そう, so it needs the previous remark to be
physically there in the conversation - it is a reply. `とはいうものの` can look back
at a whole paragraph, or at an opinion nobody in the room has actually voiced, which
is exactly what a written argument needs.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 安(やす)いだ<b>とはいうものの</b>、質(しつ)が悪(わる)い。 → ✅ 安(やす)い<b>とはいうものの</b>、質(しつ)が悪(わる)い。
  `と` is the quotative particle and takes a plain form. い-adjectives never carry
  `だ`.

- ❌ それは言(い)い<b>とはいうものの</b>だ。 → ✅ それは言(い)い過(す)ぎ<b>というものだ</b>。
  <code>というものだ</code> ("that is what you would call…") is a different
  expression with no concessive force. The `は` is what makes this one concessive.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>ものの</b>、責任(せきにん)はある。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)だ<b>とはいうものの</b>、責任(せきにん)はある。
  Bare `ものの` cannot sit straight on a noun; it needs `である`, or the whole
  `とはいうものの` frame.

- ❌ <b>とはいうものの</b>、今日(きょう)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうです。（as the first thing said） → ✅ 今日(きょう)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうです。
  With nothing asserted beforehand, there is nothing to concede. The word is a
  reply, even when the thing it replies to is the writer's own last sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 給料(きゅうりょう)は悪(わる)くない。____、残業(ざんぎょう)が多(おお)すぎて続(つづ)けられない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>とはいうものの</b>、残業(ざんぎょう)が多(おお)すぎて続(つづ)けられない。

The speaker has just granted that the pay is decent and now takes the job apart on
other grounds. A concession already made, then the reality that spoils it, is
exactly this word's shape.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural expression for a message to a close friend:
禁煙(きんえん)すると決(き)めたんだ。（とはいうものの・そうはいっても）、一本(いっぽん)くらいいいよね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>そうはいっても</b>、一本(いっぽん)くらいいいよね。

The register is casual - `んだ`, `よね`, a friend - and そう points straight back at
what was just said. `とはいうものの` would read as though the speaker were writing a
column about their own smoking.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 海(うみ)が近(ちか)い<b>ものの</b>、泳(およ)げる場所(ばしょ)はない。
(b) 海(うみ)が近(ちか)い<b>とはいうものの</b>、泳(およ)げる場所(ばしょ)はない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports two facts about the place from the speaker's own observation: the sea is
close by, and there is nowhere to swim. It is a flat, slightly deflated description.

(b) treats "the sea is close by" as somebody's selling point - an estate agent, a
brochure, a friend who recommended the town - and answers it. The listener hears a
claim being tested, and a faint note of the speaker having been oversold.

</details>

# ～かいもなく

**Meaning** · for all the effort, to no avail - work was put in, aimed squarely at one result, and that result did not come

The noun is 甲斐(かい), "worth, avail, something to show for it". `かいもなく`
therefore says *there was not even that much to show for it*. Three things have to
be true for the pattern to fit: somebody made a real effort, the effort was aimed at
a particular outcome, and the outcome failed. Take any one away and the sentence
sounds wrong.

## Formation

The effort goes in front, either as a past-tense clause or as a noun with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (past) | plain past + かいもなく | 看病(かんびょう)した → 看病(かんびょう)したかいもなく |
| Noun | Noun + のかいもなく | 努力(どりょく) → 努力(どりょく)のかいもなく |
| Shorter variant | ～かいなく | 説得(せっとく)のかいなく |
| Positive counterpart | ～かいあって / かいがあって | 練習(れんしゅう)した → 練習(れんしゅう)したかいあって |
| As a predicate | ～かいがある / かいがない | 待(ま)った → 待(ま)ったかいがあった |
| Kanji spelling | ～甲斐(かい)もなく | 治療(ちりょう)の甲斐(かい)もなく |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front is **past**. ❌<code>練習(れんしゅう)するかいもなく</code>
is wrong: the effort has to be finished before you can report that it bought you
nothing.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - both spellings are standard, and news copy tends to prefer the kanji
甲斐(かい). The same noun voices to `がい` when it is welded onto a verb stem:
やりがい (work worth doing), 生(い)きがい (what makes life worth living),
働(はたら)きがい. Those are ordinary everyday words and carry none of the gloom of
`かいもなく`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
努力(どりょく)の<b>かいもなく</b>、試合(しあい)に負(ま)けた。
For all our effort, we lost the match.
</pre>

<pre>
徹夜(てつや)した<b>かいもなく</b>、発表(はっぴょう)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
I stayed up all night for nothing - the presentation was called off.
</pre>

<pre>
必死(ひっし)に説得(せっとく)した<b>かいもなく</b>、彼(かれ)は会社(かいしゃ)を去(さ)っていった。
We pleaded with him and it changed nothing; he left the company.
</pre>

<pre>
三年間(さんねんかん)通(かよ)い続(つづ)けた<b>かいもなく</b>、一次(いちじ)試験(しけん)で落(お)ちてしまった。
Three years of turning up week after week, and I fell at the first paper.
</pre>

<pre>
半年(はんとし)かけて改良(かいりょう)した<b>かいもなく</b>、新型(しんがた)の売(う)れ行(ゆ)きは前(まえ)の型(かた)を下回(したまわ)った。
Six months of redesign came to nothing: the new model sold worse than the old one.
</pre>

<pre>
住民(じゅうみん)の反対(はんたい)運動(うんどう)の<b>甲斐(かい)もなく</b>、古(ふる)い商店街(しょうてんがい)は取(と)り壊(こわ)されることになった。
The residents campaigned to no avail, and the old shopping street is to be demolished.
</pre>

<pre>
懸命(けんめい)の治療(ちりょう)の<b>甲斐(かい)もなく</b>、祖父(そふ)は静(しず)かに息(いき)を引(ひ)き取(と)った。
Despite everything the doctors did, my grandfather slipped quietly away.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)めに家(いえ)を出(で)た<b>かいもなく</b>、事故(じこ)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まり、面接(めんせつ)に遅刻(ちこく)した。
Leaving early did me no good at all - an accident stopped the trains and I was late for the interview.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The adverbial `かいもなく` leans **written and formal**. It is standard in news
reports, obituaries, company announcements and the reflective register of an essay;
in conversation the same thought comes out as
頑張(がんば)ったのに無駄(むだ)だった or せっかく…したのに. The related predicate
甲斐(かい)がある / 甲斐(かい)がない, by contrast, is completely ordinary spoken
Japanese - 待(ま)った甲斐(かい)があった, "the wait was worth it".

<code>懸命(けんめい)の治療(ちりょう)の甲斐(かい)もなく</code> is close to a
fixed formula in Japanese news writing and in the announcement of a death, and it is
worth knowing simply because you will meet it. Because of that association the
pattern carries real weight, and using it about something trivial - a missed bus, a
cold coffee - sounds either childishly dramatic or deliberately comic.

The emotion is **disappointment, not blame**. The effort is granted as genuine; what
the sentence laments is that it bought nothing. If you want to accuse somebody, this
is the wrong pattern.

## Contrast

### ～かいもなく vs ～かいあって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one noun, two outcomes. `かいあって` = the effort paid off.
`かいもなく` = it did not. Learning them as a pair is the fastest way to keep the
direction straight.

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)走(はし)った<b>かいあって</b>、タイムが大(おお)きく縮(ちぢ)んだ。
All that daily running paid off - my time came down sharply.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)走(はし)った<b>かいもなく</b>、タイムはほとんど変(か)わらなかった。
All that daily running got me nowhere; my time barely moved.
</pre>

The `も` in `かいもなく` is doing real work: *not even* the worth. Drop it and you
get `かいなく`, which is the same meaning in a slightly tighter, more literary
package. There is no ❌`かいもあって`.

### ～かいもなく vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` covers any expectation that was let down. `かいもなく`
needs **invested effort** that was aimed at the very thing that failed.

</div>

<pre>
晴(は)れる予報(よほう)だった<b>のに</b>、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
The forecast said clear, and then it started raining.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も練習(れんしゅう)した<b>かいもなく</b>、本番(ほんばん)で間違(まちが)えた。
For all that rehearsing, I fluffed it on the night.
</pre>

Nobody worked at making the forecast come true, so ❌<code>予報(よほう)のかいもなく</code>
does not work. The other direction is easier: almost anything you can say with
`かいもなく` can also be said with `のに`, at a lower register and with the loss of
the "wasted effort" colouring.

### ～かいもなく vs ～にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にもかかわらず` concedes a **circumstance**, and the outcome may
be good, bad or neutral. `かいもなく` concedes an **effort**, and the outcome is
always the failure of what that effort was for.

</div>

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、催(もよお)しは予定(よてい)どおり行(おこな)われた。
In spite of the downpour, the event went ahead as planned.
</pre>

<pre>
入念(にゅうねん)な準備(じゅんび)の<b>かいもなく</b>、催(もよお)しは途中(とちゅう)で中止(ちゅうし)になった。
For all the careful preparation, the event was abandoned halfway through.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence stops making sense: rain is not an effort anybody
made. The second could take `にもかかわらず` and would still be grammatical, but the
sting - all that work, gone - drains out of it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 努力(どりょく)した<b>かいもなく</b>、合格(ごうかく)した。 → ✅ 努力(どりょく)した<b>かいあって</b>、合格(ごうかく)した。
  A good outcome takes `かいあって`. `かいもなく` announces that the effort bought
  nothing, so pairing it with success contradicts itself.

- ❌ 練習(れんしゅう)する<b>かいもなく</b>、負(ま)けた。 → ✅ 練習(れんしゅう)した<b>かいもなく</b>、負(ま)けた。
  The clause is past. The effort has to be over before you can report that it
  yielded nothing.

- ❌ 努力(どりょく)<b>かいもなく</b> → ✅ 努力(どりょく)の<b>かいもなく</b>
  <code>かい</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)の<b>かいもなく</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)のため、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  Rain is a cause, not an effort somebody made. Only something a person did on
  purpose, hoping for a result, can go in front of <code>かい</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 一年(いちねん)かけて準備(じゅんび)した____、大会(たいかい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一年(いちねん)かけて準備(じゅんび)した<b>かいもなく</b>、大会(たいかい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。

A year of preparation, aimed at one event, and the event never happened - effort,
target and failure are all present. The verb in front stays in the plain past.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 毎朝(まいあさ)単語(たんご)を覚(おぼ)えた（かいもなく・かいあって）、読(よ)む速(はや)さがかなり上(あ)がった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

毎朝(まいあさ)単語(たんご)を覚(おぼ)えた<b>かいあって</b>、読(よ)む速(はや)さがかなり上(あ)がった。

The reading speed went up, so the effort was repaid. `かいもなく` is only for the
case where the hoped-for result never arrived.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "In spite of a month of rehearsals, the play closed after three days."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一(いっ)か月(げつ)にわたる稽古(けいこ)の<b>甲斐(かい)もなく</b>、芝居(しばい)は三日(みっか)で打(う)ち切(き)られた。

The rehearsals are effort aimed at a run of performances, and the run collapsed -
`かいもなく` almost writes itself. A noun phrase in front takes `の`, and
三日(みっか) is the native reading of the counter.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 渋滞(じゅうたい)の甲斐(かい)もなく、開演(かいえん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 早(はや)めに出(で)た<b>甲斐(かい)もなく</b>、開演(かいえん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。
"For all that leaving early, I missed the curtain."

A traffic jam is what got in the way, not the effort that was wasted. Whatever sits
in front of <code>甲斐(かい)</code> has to be the thing the speaker did on
purpose. If you want the jam as the cause, write
<code>渋滞(じゅうたい)のため</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 手術(しゅじゅつ)をした<b>のに</b>、痛(いた)みは消(き)えなかった。
(b) 手術(しゅじゅつ)の<b>甲斐(かい)もなく</b>、痛(いた)みは消(き)えなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is what a patient says. `のに` registers a personal grievance - I went through
that, and here I still am - and it is ordinary spoken Japanese.

(b) is what a written account says. It presents the operation as an intervention
undertaken in order to remove the pain, and reports coolly that it achieved nothing.
The regret is there, but it is the regret of a narrator rather than a complaint, and
the register is a clear step higher.

</details>

# ～ものを

**Meaning** · if only; and yet - a better outcome was there for the taking, nobody took it, and the speaker is not letting it go

`ものを` is not really about grammar; it is about a feeling. The sentence names a
door that was standing open - *just apologise*, *just say something*, *just see a
doctor* - and then reports that the person walked past it. What comes with that is
reproach if the person is somebody else, and bitter regret if it is the speaker.
It is one kana away from `ものの`, and it does an entirely different job.

## Formation

<code>もの</code> is a noun, and the `を` behind it is an old adversative particle
that survives almost nowhere else in the modern language. The clause in front is in
noun-modifying shape, and in practice it is nearly always a conditional.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ば-conditional + いい) | ～ばいい + ものを | 謝(あやま)ればいい → 謝(あやま)ればいいものを |
| Verb (ば-conditional + よかった) | ～ばよかった + ものを | 言(い)ってくれればよかったものを |
| Verb (たら-conditional) | ～たらいい + ものを | 休(やす)んだらいいものを |
| Verb (plain, past or present) | plain form + ものを | 治(なお)った → 治(なお)ったものを |
| Sentence-final | …ものを。(the rest left unsaid) | 一言(ひとこと)言(い)ってくれればよかったものを。 |
| Noun / adjective | does not occur - use `のに` | - |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause before `ものを` must describe **the better outcome
that was available**, and the clause after it (when there is one) reports **what
actually happened instead**. Both halves are statements. A request or an
instruction cannot follow it: ❌<code>時間(じかん)があるものを、手伝(てつだ)ってください</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the second half is very often swallowed. Ending on `ものを` and trailing
off is the pattern's most characteristic move, and it leaves the listener to supply
the disaster: 「早(はや)めに相談(そうだん)してくれればよかったものを…」

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
素直(すなお)に謝(あやま)ればいい<b>ものを</b>。
He could simply have apologised.
</pre>

<pre>
一言(ひとこと)言(い)ってくれればよかった<b>ものを</b>。
If only you had said something.
</pre>

<pre>
黙(だま)っていればいい<b>ものを</b>、余計(よけい)なことを口(くち)に出(だ)してしまった。
He should have kept quiet - instead he had to go and say too much.
</pre>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)に診(み)てもらえば治(なお)った<b>ものを</b>、父(ちち)は最後(さいご)まで我慢(がまん)し続(つづ)けた。
A doctor could have cured it; my father put up with it to the end instead.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)を頼(たよ)れば誰(だれ)かが手(て)を貸(か)した<b>ものを</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は最後(さいご)まで一人(ひとり)で抱(かか)え込(こ)んだ。
Someone would have helped if she had asked; she carried it alone to the very end.
</pre>

<pre>
正直(しょうじき)に話(はな)せば命(いのち)だけは助(たす)けてやった<b>ものを</b>。
Had you told the truth, I would at least have spared your life.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)めに申(もう)し込(こ)んでいれば席(せき)が取(と)れた<b>ものを</b>、ぐずぐずしているうちに満席(まんせき)になってしまった。
Booking early would have got us seats; we dithered, and the place sold out.
</pre>

<pre>
無理(むり)さえしなければ体(からだ)を壊(こわ)さずに済(す)んだ<b>ものを</b>、あの人(ひと)は人(ひと)の言(い)うことを聞(き)かない。
He need never have wrecked his health if he had only eased off - but he won't be told.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; do not say it.** `ものを` belongs to written and performed
Japanese: novels and the dialogue inside them, period drama, film subtitles, song
lyrics, and the occasional written complaint that wants to sound wounded. It also
survives in the speech of some older speakers, usually while telling somebody off.
Said by a learner in ordinary conversation it does not sound merely formal - it
sounds like a line delivered from a stage, and the listener will hear the costume.
The modern equivalents are `～ばよかったのに` and `～ばいいのに`, and they are what
you should actually produce.

The feeling it carries is specific enough to be worth naming. There is always a
**better path that was open and was not taken**, and the speaker is aggrieved about
it. Pointed at somebody else, that comes out as reproach - *you could have just told
me* - and it is not a gentle reproach; it implies the person's stubbornness or pride
caused the mess. Pointed at oneself or at the dead, it comes out as lament.

Because the emotion is built in, `ものを` cannot be neutral. A newspaper reporting a
policy failure will use `にもかかわらず`; a novelist describing a character's ruin
will use `ものを`. The tell is that the second half is usually something the speaker
minds about - and very often the second half is not said at all, which is more
eloquent than saying it.

## Contrast

### ～ものを vs ～ばよかったのに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same complaint, one archaic and one current.
`～ばよかったのに` is what a modern speaker says; `ものを` is what a modern speaker
reads.

</div>

<pre>
言(い)ってくれれば手伝(てつだ)った<b>のに</b>。
You should have said - I'd have helped.
</pre>

<pre>
言(い)ってくれれば手伝(てつだ)った<b>ものを</b>。
Had you but said the word, I would have helped.
</pre>

The content is identical. The difference is entirely one of register and era: the
first is what you would text a friend, the second is what a character says in a
novel two pages before something goes badly wrong. If you are choosing a form to
produce, choose the first every time.

### ～ものを vs ～ものの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one kana apart, and nothing else in common. `ものの` concedes a
fact and reports a shortfall, with no emotion required. `ものを` reproaches somebody
for a chance they let go.

</div>

<pre>
申(もう)し込(こ)んだ<b>ものの</b>、抽選(ちゅうせん)に外(はず)れた。
I applied, but I lost out in the ballot.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し込(こ)めば当(あ)たった<b>ものを</b>、彼(かれ)は面倒(めんどう)がって出(だ)さなかった。
He would have got a place if he had applied; he couldn't be bothered to send the form.
</pre>

`ものの` takes what did happen and reports the disappointing sequel. `ものを` takes
what did **not** happen and grieves over it, which is why its first clause is a
conditional and `ものの`'s never is. If you can put "although" in front of the
English, you want `ものの`; if you can put "if only", you want `ものを`.

### ～ものを vs ～ばよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ばよかった` is regret with **no one in the dock**. `ものを`
puts somebody there, and usually it is not the speaker.

</div>

<pre>
もっと早(はや)く家(いえ)を出(で)れ<b>ばよかった</b>。
I should have left home earlier.
</pre>

<pre>
もっと早(はや)く出(で)ればよかった<b>ものを</b>、彼(かれ)はぎりぎりまで動(うご)かなかった。
He could easily have set off earlier; he sat there until the last minute.
</pre>

The first sentence is a person kicking themselves, and it is completely ordinary
Japanese. The second passes judgement on someone's behaviour. That is why `ものを`
addressed straight to the person in front of you is a genuinely sharp thing to say,
even before its old-fashioned flavour is taken into account.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>ものを</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われた。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>ものの</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われた。
  Plain concession is `ものの`'s job. `ものを` needs a missed opportunity, and rain
  is not one.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がある<b>ものを</b>、手伝(てつだ)ってください。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)があるなら、手伝(てつだ)ってください。
  Nothing that follows `ものを` may be a request or an order. Both halves are
  reports, and the second one is the bad news.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>ものを</b>、勉強(べんきょう)しない。 → ✅ 勉強(べんきょう)すればいい<b>ものを</b>、遊(あそ)んでばかりいる。
  A bare noun cannot sit in front of it. What goes there is a clause describing the
  better course of action.

- ❌ （to a friend, casually）連絡(れんらく)してくれればよかった<b>ものを</b>。 → ✅ 連絡(れんらく)してくれればよかった<b>のに</b>。
  Not a grammar error - a register error. Among friends the archaic form lands as
  theatre or sarcasm, and the friend will hear it as either.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence? 「逃(に)げれば助(たす)かった<b>ものを</b>。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In fiction or drama - a line of narration, or a character speaking, very often in a
historical setting. It is the voice of someone looking at a disaster and naming the
escape route that was not used.

You would not hear it in an office, a shop or a conversation between friends; there
the same thought is 「逃(に)げればよかったのに」.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 早(はや)く病院(びょういん)へ行(い)けばよかった<b>ものを</b>、彼(かれ)は放(ほう)っておいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

早(はや)く病院(びょういん)へ行(い)けばよかった<b>のに</b>、彼(かれ)は放(ほう)っておいた。

Swapping `ものを` for `のに` keeps the meaning and the reproach exactly, and drops
the literary costume. This is the substitution to reach for whenever you are
tempted to produce `ものを` yourself.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 注意(ちゅうい)はした（ものの・ものを）、誰(だれ)も聞(き)いていなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

注意(ちゅうい)はした<b>ものの</b>、誰(だれ)も聞(き)いていなかった。

The warning was actually given, and the sentence reports the disappointing sequel -
that is `ものの`. `ものを` would require the first clause to be something that never
happened, such as 「注意(ちゅうい)すればよかったものを」.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 安(やす)いものを、品質(ひんしつ)もいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 安(やす)い<b>のに</b>、品質(ひんしつ)もいい。（or 安(やす)い<b>うえに</b>、品質(ひんしつ)もいい）

Two things are wrong. `ものを` never carries a **good** outcome - it exists to
lament a bad one - and it does not attach to an い-adjective in this way. As
written, 安(やす)いものを would be read as "a cheap one", the ordinary noun
<code>もの</code> with an object marker.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 相談(そうだん)してくれればよかった<b>のに</b>。
(b) 相談(そうだん)してくれればよかった<b>ものを</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The propositional content is the same: you did not consult me, and you should have.

(a) is live modern Japanese and can be said warmly - a friend gently telling you off
for struggling alone. Tone of voice decides how hard it lands.

(b) cannot be said warmly. The archaic frame formalises the reproach and puts a
distance between the speaker and the person, so it reads as the summing-up of
someone who has already given up on being listened to. In modern speech it would
also sound staged; you will meet it on the page far more often than in a room.

</details>

# ～をよそに

**Meaning** · paying no heed to; unmoved by - other people are worried, angry or opposed, and the person in the sentence carries on as though none of it were happening

<code>よそ</code> is 余所(よそ), "somewhere else, another place". `をよそに`
therefore files something away as *not my business*. What gets filed away is almost
always **somebody else's reaction** - their worry, their objection, their outrage -
and the sentence watches the subject walk straight through it.

## Formation

A noun goes in front. A clause has to be nominalised with `の` first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + をよそに | 心配(しんぱい) → 心配(しんぱい)をよそに |
| Noun (with a possessor) | ～の + Noun + をよそに | 親(おや)の反対(はんたい)をよそに |
| Clause | plain form + のをよそに | 騒(さわ)いでいる → 騒(さわ)いでいるのをよそに |
| Longer variant | ～をよそにして | 批判(ひはん)をよそにして |
| Kanji spelling | ～を余所(よそ)に | 忠告(ちゅうこく)を余所(よそ)に |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun in the slot names a **reaction**, not a circumstance.
心配(しんぱい), 不安(ふあん), 反対(はんたい), 批判(ひはん),
期待(きたい), 混乱(こんらん), 忠告(ちゅうこく) all work. ❌<code>雨(あめ)をよそに</code>
does not: rain is nobody's opinion.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reaction belongs to **other people**. A sentence about ignoring your
own feelings takes something else -
<code>不安(ふあん)を抑(おさ)えて</code>, <code>迷(まよ)いを振(ふ)り切(き)って</code> -
and the kanji spelling 余所(よそ) is nowadays much rarer than the kana.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
親(おや)の反対(はんたい)<b>をよそに</b>、彼(かれ)は会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めた。
Ignoring his parents' objections, he quit the company.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りの心配(しんぱい)<b>をよそに</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は平然(へいぜん)としていた。
Everyone around her was anxious; she was perfectly unruffled.
</pre>

<pre>
住民(じゅうみん)の不安(ふあん)<b>をよそに</b>、工事(こうじ)は予定(よてい)どおり進(すす)められた。
The work went ahead on schedule, with no regard for local anxieties.
</pre>

<pre>
世間(せけん)の批判(ひはん)<b>をよそに</b>、首相(しゅしょう)は方針(ほうしん)を変(か)えようとしなかった。
Undeterred by public criticism, the prime minister showed no sign of changing course.
</pre>

<pre>
大人(おとな)たちが言(い)い争(あらそ)っているの<b>をよそに</b>、子(こ)どもたちは庭(にわ)を走(はし)り回(まわ)っていた。
While the adults argued, the children ran about the garden, oblivious.
</pre>

<pre>
株価(かぶか)の下落(げらく)に対(たい)する市場(しじょう)の動揺(どうよう)<b>をよそに</b>、経営陣(けいえいじん)は強気(つよき)の見通(みとお)しを示(しめ)した。
For all the alarm in the market at the falling share price, management presented a bullish outlook.
</pre>

<pre>
周囲(しゅうい)の期待(きたい)<b>をよそに</b>、その新人(しんじん)は開幕(かいまく)から一度(いちど)も打席(だせき)に立(た)てなかった。
Against all the expectation surrounding him, the rookie did not come to bat once after opening day.
</pre>

<pre>
避難(ひなん)勧告(かんこく)が出(で)ているの<b>をよそに</b>、波(なみ)を見物(けんぶつ)しに訪(おとず)れる人(ひと)が後(あと)を絶(た)たなかった。
An evacuation advisory was in force, and still a steady stream of people came to watch the waves.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; reach for something else when you speak.** `をよそに` is not
archaic - it is alive and productive - but it lives almost entirely in **written
Japanese**, and above all in news reporting and narrative prose. It is the phrase a
journalist uses to frame a decision as high-handed, and the phrase a novelist uses
to show one character absorbed while the room boils around them. Said in
conversation it does not sound rude or wrong; it sounds like someone reading a
newspaper out loud. In speech, use
<code>～を気(き)にせず</code>, <code>～を無視(むし)して</code> or
<code>～にもかかわらず</code>.

The default colouring is **mild criticism**. Because what is disregarded is other
people's worry or opposition, the subject comes off as indifferent to them, and in
reporting this is often the whole point of choosing the phrase - it lets the writer
pass judgement without saying anything the subject could object to. That is a
tendency rather than a rule: with 心配(しんぱい) it can be admiring (nerves of
steel), and with 期待(きたい) it is simply the neutral "contrary to what
everyone expected".

Two restrictions worth holding on to. The subject is normally somebody being
observed - a third party, or the writer narrating their own past - rather than the
speaker in the room. And the disregarded thing belongs to **others**: there is no
natural ❌<code>自分(じぶん)の不安(ふあん)をよそに</code>.

## Contrast

### ～をよそに vs ～にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にもかかわらず` is the neutral, register-safe way to say
"despite". `をよそに` adds a **person who is disregarding** something, and a hint
that they perhaps should not be.

</div>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)の声(こえ)が強(つよ)かった<b>にもかかわらず</b>、計画(けいかく)は承認(しょうにん)された。
Despite strong opposition, the plan was approved.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)の声(こえ)<b>をよそに</b>、市(し)は計画(けいかく)を承認(しょうにん)した。
Brushing the objections aside, the city approved the plan.
</pre>

The first sentence could be written by anybody; it simply records that the
opposition did not stop the approval. The second has the city as an actor turning
its back, and the reader hears the writer's eyebrow go up. If you want to be
neutral, or if you are speaking rather than writing, take the first.

### ～をよそに vs ～をものともせず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `をものともせず` is for **obstacles overcome**, and it praises.
`をよそに` is for **other people's feelings ignored**, and it usually does not.

</div>

<pre>
強風(きょうふう)<b>をものともせず</b>、救助隊(きゅうじょたい)は山(やま)を登(のぼ)り続(つづ)けた。
Undaunted by the gale, the rescue team kept climbing.
</pre>

<pre>
救助隊(きゅうじょたい)の制止(せいし)<b>をよそに</b>、男性(だんせい)は一人(ひとり)で山(やま)に入(はい)っていった。
Paying no attention to the rescue team's attempts to stop him, the man walked into the mountains alone.
</pre>

Wind is a hardship, and beating it is admirable. Being told to stop is somebody
else's judgement, and walking past it is not. Swap the two and the sentences change
sides: ❌<code>強風(きょうふう)をよそに</code> would oddly cast the wind as having
an opinion, and <code>制止(せいし)をものともせず</code> would turn the man into a
hero.

### ～をよそに vs ～を無視(むし)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>無視(むし)する</code> is an **act**: the person saw it
and deliberately ignored it. `をよそに` describes a **state of detachment** from the
outside, and the subject may simply be absorbed elsewhere.

</div>

<pre>
注意(ちゅうい)を<b>無視(むし)して</b>撮影(さつえい)を続(つづ)けた客(きゃく)が追(お)い出(だ)された。
A visitor who ignored the warnings and carried on filming was thrown out.
</pre>

<pre>
係員(かかりいん)の注意(ちゅうい)<b>をよそに</b>、客(きゃく)は撮影(さつえい)を続(つづ)けていた。
Heedless of the attendant's warnings, the visitor went on filming.
</pre>

<code>無視(むし)して</code> is blunt, accusatory and perfectly ordinary in
speech - it is what you say to a colleague about a driver who ran a red light.
`をよそに` is the same event seen from the narrator's chair, and it is what the
report of the incident will say.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 大雨(おおあめ)<b>をよそに</b>、試合(しあい)は続(つづ)けられた。 → ✅ 大雨(おおあめ)<b>をものともせず</b>、試合(しあい)は続(つづ)けられた。
  Weather has no opinion. Only a reaction - worry, opposition, criticism, an
  instruction - fits in front of `をよそに`.

- ❌ 騒(さわ)いでいる<b>をよそに</b>、彼(かれ)は眠(ねむ)っていた。 → ✅ 騒(さわ)いでいるの<b>をよそに</b>、彼(かれ)は眠(ねむ)っていた。
  `を` needs a noun, so a clause must be nominalised with `の` first.

- ❌ 自分(じぶん)の不安(ふあん)<b>をよそに</b>、舞台(ぶたい)に上(あ)がった。 → ✅ 不安(ふあん)を抑(おさ)えて、舞台(ぶたい)に上(あ)がった。
  What is set aside belongs to other people. Your own feelings are suppressed, not
  filed elsewhere.

- ❌ （to a friend）心配(しんぱい)<b>をよそに</b>遊(あそ)んでてごめん。 → ✅ 心配(しんぱい)かけたのに遊(あそ)んでてごめん。
  A register error rather than a grammar one. Applying newspaper narration to
  yourself, in an apology, to a friend, is three mismatches at once.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A blizzard is a physical hardship being overcome, which is `をものともせず` and its

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"With the hall in uproar, the host stayed calm."

outside them; the reader draws the conclusion themselves. It is the wording a
written account of the episode would use, and it is cooler on the surface while
being, if anything, more damning.

</details>

# ～をものともせず

**Meaning** · undaunted by; in defiance of - something that would stop most people simply fails to stop this one, and the sentence admires them for it

<code>ものともしない</code> is "not to treat it as a thing" - not to count it, not
to give it the status of an obstacle. `せず` is the literary negative of `する`
(the same old stem `せ` that gives <code>せざるを得(え)ない</code>), so the whole
phrase means *without making anything of it*. Storm, injury, age, hostile
opinion: whatever is named, the sentence says it did not register.

## Formation

A noun goes in front; a clause is nominalised with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + をものともせず | 荒波(あらなみ) → 荒波(あらなみ)をものともせず |
| With に | ～をものともせずに | 寒(さむ)さをものともせずに |
| Clause | plain form + のをものともせず | 足(あし)を痛(いた)めている → 足(あし)を痛(いた)めているのをものともせず |
| Modifying a noun | ～をものともしない + Noun | 坂(さか)をものともしない足取(あしど)り |
| Ending a sentence | ～をものともしなかった | 批判(ひはん)をものともしなかった |
| する irregular stem | **せず**, never ❌しず | ものともせず |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the slot takes **whatever should have stopped them**: weather,
a wound, an illness, a disability, danger, exhaustion, age, or other people's
opposition. The main clause must then say that they went on and, usually, got
somewhere. A sentence that ends in failure contradicts the phrase.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `せず` is the negative of `する` built on the old stem `せ`. ❌`しず` does
not exist. The `に` in <code>ものともせずに</code> is optional and changes nothing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
寒(さむ)さ<b>をものともせず</b>、選手(せんしゅ)たちは走(はし)り続(つづ)けた。
Undaunted by the cold, the runners kept going.
</pre>

<pre>
足(あし)のけが<b>をものともせず</b>、彼(かれ)は最後(さいご)まで投(な)げ切(き)った。
He pitched to the end of the game, injured leg and all.
</pre>

<pre>
荒波(あらなみ)<b>をものともせず</b>、小(ちい)さな漁船(ぎょせん)は港(みなと)へ戻(もど)ってきた。
The little fishing boat came back into harbour through heavy seas.
</pre>

<pre>
周囲(しゅうい)の冷(つめ)たい視線(しせん)<b>をものともせず</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は自分(じぶん)の研究(けんきゅう)を続(つづ)けた。
Unmoved by the coldness around her, she carried on with her own research.
</pre>

<pre>
高齢(こうれい)<b>をものともせず</b>、館長(かんちょう)は毎朝(まいあさ)自転車(じてんしゃ)で通(かよ)ってくる。
Age notwithstanding, the director cycles in every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
度重(たびかさ)なる失敗(しっぱい)<b>をものともせず</b>、技術者(ぎじゅつしゃ)たちは改良(かいりょう)を重(かさ)ねていった。
Failure after failure did nothing to stop the engineers refining the design.
</pre>

<pre>
厳(きび)しい批判(ひはん)<b>をものともせず</b>、監督(かんとく)は若手(わかて)を起用(きよう)し続(つづ)けた。
In the teeth of harsh criticism, the manager went on fielding his young players.
</pre>

<pre>
険(けわ)しい山道(やまみち)<b>をものともしない</b>足取(あしど)りで、老(お)いた案内人(あんないにん)は先(さき)を歩(ある)いていった。
The old guide walked ahead with a stride that made nothing of the steep path.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; you will almost never need to produce it.** `をものともせず` lives
in **written and broadcast Japanese of a particular, elevated kind**: sports
reporting, obituaries, biographies, award citations, documentary narration, the
paragraph in a company history about the founder. Its `せず` is a literary negative,
and in ordinary conversation the phrase sounds like a caption being read aloud.
Speaking, you would say <code>～に負(ま)けず</code> or simply
「けがをしていたのに頑張(がんば)った」.

The phrase is **praise**, and that is what makes it hard to place. There has to be a
real hardship - a broken foot, a blizzard, eighty-five years, a hostile press - and
there has to be an achievement that the hardship did not prevent. Applied to
something trivial it becomes comic: ❌<code>眠気(ねむけ)をものともせず、テレビを見(み)た</code>
reads as a joke about heroism.

Because it praises, it is **not used of oneself**. 「私(わたし)は困難(こんなん)をものともせず…」
is self-congratulation of a kind Japanese avoids fairly strictly; write
何(なん)とか乗(の)り越(こ)えました instead. The natural subject is somebody
being observed, and the observer is impressed.

## Contrast

### ～をものともせず vs ～にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にもかかわらず` reports that A did not prevent B, and takes no
view. `をものともせず` adds that the subject **did not even flinch**, and hands them
the credit.

</div>

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、列車(れっしゃ)は定刻(ていこく)に到着(とうちゃく)した。
Despite the heavy snow, the train arrived on time.
</pre>

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)<b>をものともせず</b>、運転士(うんてんし)は列車(れっしゃ)を定刻(ていこく)に走(はし)らせた。
Undaunted by the heavy snow, the driver brought the train in on time.
</pre>

`にもかかわらず` is happy with an inanimate subject - the train did it - because it
is only noting a mismatch between two facts. `をものともせず` needs somebody with a
will, since the whole point is that a person refused to be impressed by the
obstacle. This is also the substitution to make whenever you are unsure: it is
neutral, current, and safe in any register.

### ～をものともせず vs ～をよそに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both can take opposition or criticism in the slot, and they
frame it opposite ways. `をものともせず` treats it as an **obstacle overcome** and
approves; `をよそに` treats it as **other people's concern brushed off** and
disapproves.

</div>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)の反対(はんたい)<b>をものともせず</b>、彼(かれ)は単身(たんしん)で海(うみ)を渡(わた)った。
Undeterred by his family's opposition, he crossed the sea alone.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)の反対(はんたい)<b>をよそに</b>、彼(かれ)は単身(たんしん)で海(うみ)を渡(わた)った。
Paying no attention to his family's objections, he crossed the sea alone.
</pre>

The event is identical; the writer's verdict is not. In the first the family were an
obstacle and the man was brave. In the second the family had a point and the man
turned his back on them. Choosing between the two is choosing whose side the
sentence is on, which is precisely why journalists find the pair useful.

### ～をものともせず vs ～に負(ま)けず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same admiration, one written and one sayable.
<code>～に負(ま)けず</code> is ordinary Japanese you can use to a person's face;
`をものともせず` is what the write-up says about them afterwards.

</div>

<pre>
暑(あつ)さに<b>負(ま)けず</b>、毎日(まいにち)よく頑張(がんば)っているね。
You're not letting the heat beat you - you're doing really well.
</pre>

<pre>
記録的(きろくてき)な暑(あつ)さ<b>をものともせず</b>、生徒(せいと)たちは練習(れんしゅう)を続(つづ)けた。
Undeterred by record heat, the students kept up their training.
</pre>

<code>負(ま)けず</code> can be addressed to the person, used of yourself, and put
in a note on a get-well card. `をものともせず` cannot do any of those three. If the
sentence you want has a listener in it, you want <code>負(ま)けず</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 困難(こんなん)をものとも<b>しず</b> → ✅ 困難(こんなん)をものとも<b>せず</b>
  `する` uses the old stem `せ` before `ず`. ❌`しず` is not a form of the verb at
  all, and this is the most frequent slip with the pattern.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は病気(びょうき)<b>をものともせず</b>、仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)けました。 → ✅ 病気(びょうき)をしましたが、何(なん)とか仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)けました。
  The phrase awards a medal, so awarding it to yourself sounds like boasting. Keep
  it for other people.

- ❌ 大雨(おおあめ)<b>をものともせず</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 大雨(おおあめ)のため、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  The obstacle has to lose. If the rain stopped the match, nothing was made light
  of, and the sentence contradicts itself.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は困難(こんなん)<b>をものともせず</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は困難(こんなん)<b>をものともしなかった</b>。
  `せず` is a connective form and has to lead somewhere. To end a sentence, use
  <code>ものともしなかった</code>, or in the attributive slot
  <code>ものともしない</code>.

# ～も顧みず / ～を顧みず

**Meaning** · without a backward glance at; regardless of - the subject sets aside something that genuinely had a claim on their attention, and presses on

顧(かえり)みる is "to look back at, to give thought to". The negative
<code>顧(かえり)みず</code> therefore says the person did not turn their head at
all. What they did not turn their head towards is never trivial: their own safety,
their health, their reputation, their family. That is why the same phrase can read
as heroism in one sentence and as neglect in the next - it depends entirely on who
was going to be hurt.

## Formation

A noun goes in front, marked with `を` or `も`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (plain object) | Noun + を顧(かえり)みず | 危険(きけん) → 危険(きけん)を顧(かえり)みず |
| Noun (emphatic) | Noun + も顧(かえり)みず | 身(み)の危険(きけん) → 身(み)の危険(きけん)も顧(かえり)みず |
| With に | ～も顧(かえり)みずに | 採算(さいさん)を顧(かえり)みずに |
| Modifying a noun | ～を顧(かえり)みない + Noun | 家庭(かてい)を顧(かえり)みない父親(ちちおや) |
| Ending a sentence | ～を顧(かえり)みなかった | 忠告(ちゅうこく)を顧(かえり)みなかった |
| Longer variant | ～を顧(かえり)みることなく | 批判(ひはん)を顧(かえり)みることなく |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` is the ordinary object marker; `も` replaces it and adds
*not even this*. That is why the emphatic set phrases -
<code>身(み)の危険(きけん)も顧(かえり)みず</code>,
<code>世間体(せけんてい)も顧(かえり)みず</code> - take `も`. Both particles
occur with the same nouns, and the choice is one of weight, not of grammar.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - two verbs are read かえりみる. 顧(かえり)みる is *to look back at
something outside yourself*, and it is the kanji this pattern uses.
省(かえり)みる is *to examine your own conduct* -
我(わ)が身(み)を省(かえり)みる. They overlap in the bare sense of looking
back, but for self-reflection the standard spelling is 省(かえり)みる.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
危険(きけん)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、彼(かれ)は川(かわ)に飛(と)び込(こ)んだ。
Heedless of the danger, he dived into the river.
</pre>

<pre>

My father gave no thought to his family and did nothing but work.
</pre>

<pre>
身(み)の危険(きけん)も<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、消防士(しょうぼうし)たちは建物(たてもの)の中(なか)へ入(はい)っていった。
With no thought for their own safety, the firefighters went into the building.
</pre>

<pre>
世間体(せけんてい)も<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は裁判(さいばん)で証言(しょうげん)することを選(えら)んだ。
Careless of what people would say, she chose to testify at the trial.
</pre>

<pre>
自(みずか)らの健康(けんこう)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、その医師(いし)は三日(みっか)三晩(みばん)診療(しんりょう)を続(つづ)けた。
Sparing no thought for his own health, the doctor kept seeing patients for three days and nights.
</pre>

<pre>
採算(さいさん)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>に安売(やすう)りを続(つづ)けた結果(けっか)、店(みせ)は立(た)ち行(ゆ)かなくなった。
Discounting with no regard for the margins, the shop finally became unviable.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)を<b>顧(かえり)みない</b>生(い)き方(かた)を、息子(むすこ)は静(しず)かに責(せ)めていた。
His son quietly reproached him for a life lived with his back to the family.
</pre>

<pre>
周囲(しゅうい)の忠告(ちゅうこく)を<b>顧(かえり)みることなく</b>、社長(しゃちょう)は巨額(きょがく)の買収(ばいしゅう)に踏(ふ)み切(き)った。
Without a glance at the advice around him, the president went ahead with a huge acquisition.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **written, formal Japanese**. It belongs to news copy, eulogies, citations
for bravery, biography, court reporting and the more serious kind of criticism. It
is not archaic - the verb 顧(かえり)みる is current - but the bare `ず` negative
is a literary connective, and in conversation the same ideas come out as
<code>危(あぶ)ないのも気(き)にせず</code> or
<code>家族(かぞく)をほったらかしにして</code>.

Which way it reads depends on **who suffers**. When what is set aside is the
subject's own stake - their safety, their life, their good name - the sentence
salutes them, and 身(み)の危険(きけん)も顧(かえり)みず is close to a fixed
phrase in reports of rescues. When what is set aside is an obligation owed to
somebody else - a family, a workforce, a set of accounts - the same words become an
accusation. 家庭(かてい)を顧(かえり)みない is one of the standard Japanese
criticisms of a certain kind of working life.

Because of that weight, the phrase does not scale down. Nobody writes
❌<code>眠気(ねむけ)を顧(かえり)みず</code>: sleepiness is not something you owe
anything to. And, as with most Japanese praise formulas, applying it to yourself
sounds like a boast rather than a report.

## Contrast

### ～も顧(かえり)みず vs ～をものともせず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `をものともせず` is about an **obstacle in the way** that failed
to stop them. <code>顧(かえり)みず</code> is about **something of their own** they
left behind - and it may be something they should not have.

</div>

<pre>
吹雪(ふぶき)<b>をものともせず</b>、隊(たい)は尾根(おね)を越(こ)えた。
Undaunted by the blizzard, the party crossed the ridge.
</pre>

<pre>
身(み)の危険(きけん)も<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、隊(たい)は尾根(おね)を越(こ)えた。
With no thought for their own safety, the party crossed the ridge.
</pre>

The blizzard is out there, pushing back; their safety is theirs, and they put it
down. That is why `をものともせず` is unmixed admiration while
<code>顧(かえり)みず</code> can carry an undertone of *they should not have had
to* - or, with a different object, of *they had no business doing that*.
`をものともせず` has no critical use at all.

### ～も顧(かえり)みず vs ～もかまわず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もかまわず` is not minding something **in the immediate
surroundings**, and it is everyday language. <code>顧(かえり)みず</code> is not
minding a **duty or a danger**, and it is formal.

</div>

<pre>
服(ふく)が汚(よご)れるの<b>もかまわず</b>、彼(かれ)はしゃがみ込(こ)んで猫(ねこ)を抱(だ)き上(あ)げた。
Never mind his clothes, he crouched down and picked the cat up.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)の評判(ひょうばん)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、彼(かれ)は内部(ないぶ)の不正(ふせい)を告発(こくはつ)した。
Careless of his own reputation, he blew the whistle on the wrongdoing inside.
</pre>

Getting your trousers dirty is a small thing you decline to fuss about; your
reputation is a serious asset you decide to spend. Slotting a trivial noun into
<code>顧(かえり)みず</code> sounds pompous, and slotting a grave one into
`もかまわず` sounds flippant.

### ～も顧(かえり)みず vs ～をよそに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - who owns the thing being ignored. `をよそに` ignores **other
people's** worry or objection. <code>顧(かえり)みず</code> ignores something that
is the subject's **own** - their safety, their name, their household.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)の反対(はんたい)<b>をよそに</b>、兄(あに)は山(やま)へ向(む)かった。
Ignoring our mother's objections, my brother set off for the mountains.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)の体調(たいちょう)も<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、兄(あに)は山(やま)へ向(む)かった。
Giving no thought to how unwell he was, my brother set off for the mountains.
</pre>

Both sentences describe the same departure, and they blame different things. The
first has the brother overriding somebody; the second has him overriding himself.
A useful test: if you can put a possessor like 周囲(しゅうい)の or 親(おや)の
in front of the noun, `をよそに` is available; if the noun is
自分(じぶん)の something, it is <code>顧(かえり)みず</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 危険(きけん)に<b>顧(かえり)みず</b> → ✅ 危険(きけん)<b>を顧(かえり)みず</b>（or 危険(きけん)<b>も顧(かえり)みず</b>）
  顧(かえり)みる takes a direct object. The particle is `を`, or `も` for
  emphasis - never `に`.

- ❌ 大雨(おおあめ)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)した。 → ✅ 大雨(おおあめ)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)した。
  Rain is not something you owe consideration to. Only a duty, a risk or a stake of
  your own can go in front of the verb.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は家族(かぞく)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は家族(かぞく)を<b>顧(かえり)みなかった</b>。
  `ず` is a connective and cannot close a sentence. Finish with
  <code>顧(かえり)みない</code> or <code>顧(かえり)みなかった</code>.

- ❌ 自分(じぶん)の行動(こうどう)を<b>顧(かえり)みて</b>反省(はんせい)した。 → ✅ 自分(じぶん)の行動(こうどう)を<b>省(かえり)みて</b>反省(はんせい)した。
  For examining your **own conduct**, the standard kanji is 省(かえり)みる. Same
  reading, different character, and editors do correct it.

## Practice

**1.** 消防士(しょうぼうし)は身(み)の危険(きけん)____、煙(けむり)の中(なか)へ飛(と)び込(こ)んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

身(み)の危険(きけん)<b>も顧(かえり)みず</b>、煙(けむり)の中(なか)へ飛(と)び込(こ)んだ。

What is set aside is the firefighter's own safety, so the sentence reads as praise.
<code>身(み)の危険(きけん)も顧(かえり)みず</code> with `も` is close to
fixed in reports of this kind.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 人目(ひとめ)（もかまわず・も顧(かえり)みず）、彼女(かのじょ)はホームで泣(な)き続(つづ)けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人目(ひとめ)<b>もかまわず</b>、彼女(かのじょ)はホームで泣(な)き続(つづ)けた。

Being seen is a matter of the immediate surroundings, not a duty or a danger, and
the scene is everyday. <code>顧(かえり)みず</code> would make weeping on a
platform sound like an act of self-sacrifice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He worked on with no regard for his own health, and collapsed in the end."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自分(じぶん)の健康(けんこう)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>働(はたら)き続(つづ)け、ついに倒(たお)れてしまった。

The thing disregarded is his own body, which is exactly the noun class this pattern
wants, and the `を` marks it as a plain object. Note the double edge: the sentence
sounds devoted and reckless at the same time, and that ambiguity is native to the
phrase.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 社長(しゃちょう)は社員(しゃいん)の生活(せいかつ)を顧(かえり)みず。工場(こうじょう)を閉(と)じた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 社長(しゃちょう)は社員(しゃいん)の生活(せいかつ)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、工場(こうじょう)を閉(と)じた。
"With no thought for his employees' livelihoods, the president closed the plant."

The full stop is the error. `ず` links one clause to the next, so it takes a comma;
a full stop leaves the first half hanging. Note also that the object here is other
people's living, so the sentence is an accusation, not a tribute.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 危険(きけん)を<b>顧(かえり)みず</b>、彼(かれ)は海(うみ)に飛(と)び込(こ)んだ。
(b) 波(なみ)の高(たか)さ<b>をものともせず</b>、彼(かれ)は海(うみ)に飛(と)び込(こ)んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the risk on his side of the ledger. He had something to lose - his life -
and he chose not to weigh it. Readers hear courage, and a shadow of recklessness
behind it.

(b) puts the waves on the other side of the ledger, as an opponent that failed to
impress him. There is no shadow: `をものともせず` only ever admires. If the sentence
is going into a citation for bravery, (a) is the one that acknowledges what he
risked.

</details>

# ～にひきかえ

**Meaning** · in contrast to; unlike - two comparable things are set side by side, and the speaker makes it plain which one comes off better

The verb behind it is 引(ひ)き換(か)える, "to exchange one thing for another".
`にひきかえ` swaps one item for its opposite number and invites you to look at the
gap. It is never neutral. Somebody is being praised and somebody is being found
wanting, and the sentence does not pretend otherwise - which is why it so often
comes out of the mouth of a parent, a boss or a disappointed customer.

## Formation

A noun goes in front. Anything else has to be turned into a noun phrase with `の`
first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + にひきかえ | 兄(あに) → 兄(あに)にひきかえ |
| Noun (fuller) | Noun + であるのにひきかえ | 好調(こうちょう) → 好調(こうちょう)であるのにひきかえ |
| Verb | plain form + のにひきかえ | 売(う)れている → 売(う)れているのにひきかえ |
| い-adjective | plain form + のにひきかえ | 明(あか)るい → 明(あか)るいのにひきかえ |
| な-adjective | ～な + のにひきかえ | 静(しず)かな → 静(しず)かなのにひきかえ |
| Variant | ～にひきかえて | 去年(きょねん)にひきかえて |
| Kanji spelling | ～に引(ひ)き換(か)え | 以前(いぜん)に引(ひ)き換(か)え |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two things compared have to be **the same kind of thing**:
two people, two periods, two products, two departments. A sentence that compares the
weather with an exam has nothing to swap, and `にひきかえ` will sound broken.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - it is usually written in kana. Do not be thrown by
代金(だいきん)引換(ひきかえ) on a delivery slip - that is the same verb used
literally, "cash on delivery", and has nothing to do with this pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
兄(あに)<b>にひきかえ</b>、弟(おとうと)はよく勉強(べんきょう)する。
Unlike his brother, the younger boy actually studies.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)<b>にひきかえ</b>、今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は涼(すず)しい。
Compared with last year, this summer is cool.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)の職場(しょくば)<b>にひきかえ</b>、ここは人間(にんげん)関係(かんけい)がずいぶん楽(らく)だ。
After my last workplace, the atmosphere here is a great deal easier.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の晴天(せいてん)<b>にひきかえ</b>、今日(きょう)は朝(あさ)から冷(つめ)たい雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている。
After yesterday's clear skies, today has been cold rain since morning.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しく動(うご)き回(まわ)っている母(はは)<b>にひきかえ</b>、父(ちち)は一日中(いちにちじゅう)新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)んでいる。
My mother is rushing about all day; my father, by contrast, sits with the paper.
</pre>

<pre>
売上(うりあげ)が伸(の)びている海外(かいがい)部門(ぶもん)<b>にひきかえ</b>、国内(こくない)は三期(さんき)連続(れんぞく)の減収(げんしゅう)だ。
Set against the growing overseas division, the domestic side has now had three straight quarters of decline.
</pre>

<pre>
口(くち)ばかり達者(たっしゃ)な上司(じょうし)<b>にひきかえ</b>、現場(げんば)の人(ひと)たちは黙(だま)って手(て)を動(うご)かしている。
While the boss is all talk, the people on the floor just get on with it in silence.
</pre>

<pre>
新居(しんきょ)を喜(よろこ)んでいる妻(つま)<b>にひきかえ</b>、通勤(つうきん)時間(じかん)が倍(ばい)になった私(わたし)は気(き)が重(おも)い。
My wife is delighted with the new house; my commute has doubled, and my heart sinks.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にひきかえ` is **formal-leaning and a shade literary**. It is at home in essays,
columns, reviews and business commentary, and it does occur in speech - but in the
careful register, and with a faint air of pronouncement. Everyday conversation uses
<code>とは違(ちが)って</code> or simply でも.

The thing to internalise is that the pattern **takes sides**. Where
<code>に対(たい)して</code> can report a contrast with no opinion in it,
`にひきかえ` puts the speaker's verdict on the page: this one is good and that one
is not. Because the comparison is usually unflattering to one party, saying it about
somebody in their hearing is a genuinely unkind thing to do -
「お姉(ねえ)ちゃんにひきかえ、あなたは…」 is the classic parental line that children
remember for thirty years.

There is a milder use where nobody is hurt: comparing two summers, two flats, two
periods of one's own life. Even there the speaker is clearly preferring one over the
other. If you need a comparison with no preference in it, this is the wrong pattern.

## Contrast

### ～にひきかえ vs ～に対(たい)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に対(たい)して</code> reports a contrast and takes no
view; it is happy with figures. `にひきかえ` **evaluates**, and needs the speaker to
prefer one side.

</div>

<pre>
昨年(さくねん)の輸出(ゆしゅつ)が3%減(げん)だったの<b>に対(たい)し</b>、今年(ことし)は5%増(ぞう)となった。
Against a 3 per cent fall in exports last year, this year saw a 5 per cent rise.
</pre>

<pre>
不振(ふしん)だった昨年(さくねん)<b>にひきかえ</b>、今年(ことし)の営業(えいぎょう)部(ぶ)はよくやっている。
After last year's poor showing, the sales team have done well this year.
</pre>

The first sentence is a table read out loud, and swapping in `にひきかえ` would make
a statistical report sound as though it were grading the two years. The second has
a judgement in it - 不振(ふしん), よくやっている - and that is `にひきかえ`'s
natural habitat. <code>に対(たい)して</code> also handles contrasts between
things that are not really rivals; `にひきかえ` does not.

### ～にひきかえ vs ～とは違(ちが)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same comparison, different register and different manners.
<code>とは違(ちが)って</code> is ordinary spoken Japanese and can be perfectly
kind; `にひきかえ` is written, and carries a verdict.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)<b>とは違(ちが)って</b>、今年(ことし)は会場(かいじょう)が広(ひろ)くて助(たす)かる。
Unlike last year, the venue this time is nice and big - a relief.
</pre>

<pre>
手(て)ぜまだった去年(きょねん)<b>にひきかえ</b>、今年(ことし)の会場(かいじょう)は申(もう)し分(ぶん)がない。
Set against last year's cramped hall, this year's venue leaves nothing to be desired.
</pre>

Both work here, and the difference is one of tone: the first is a remark to a
colleague, the second is a line in the write-up. Where they really part company is
in politeness. <code>あなたとは違(ちが)って</code> is already blunt;
「あなたにひきかえ」 is worse, because it does not merely note a difference but ranks
the two of you.

### ～にひきかえ vs 一方(いっぽう)（で）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>一方(いっぽう)</code> puts two statements **side by
side as equals**. `にひきかえ` makes one of them the **background** against which the
other is measured.

</div>

<pre>
都市部(としぶ)では人口(じんこう)が増(ふ)えている。<b>一方(いっぽう)</b>、地方(ちほう)では減少(げんしょう)が続(つづ)く。
Population is rising in the cities. In rural areas, meanwhile, it continues to fall.
</pre>

<pre>
人口(じんこう)が増(ふ)え続(つづ)ける都市部(としぶ)<b>にひきかえ</b>、地方(ちほう)の過疎化(かそか)は深刻(しんこく)だ。
Against the cities and their growing populations, rural depopulation is severe.
</pre>

<code>一方(いっぽう)</code> is grammatically independent - two sentences, two
equal halves, no judgement - and it is the safe choice in a neutral report.
`にひきかえ` subordinates the first item to the second and colours it, which is what
深刻(しんこく)だ in the example is already doing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 兄(あに)はまじめだ<b>にひきかえ</b>、弟(おとうと)は遊(あそ)んでばかりだ。 → ✅ まじめな兄(あに)<b>にひきかえ</b>、弟(おとうと)は遊(あそ)んでばかりだ。
  It attaches to a noun, not to a finished predicate. Either put the noun in front,
  or nominalise the clause: <code>まじめであるのにひきかえ</code>.

- ❌ 天気(てんき)<b>にひきかえ</b>、試験(しけん)は難(むずか)しかった。 → ✅ 天気(てんき)はよかったが、試験(しけん)は難(むずか)しかった。
  The weather and an exam are not two of the same thing, so there is nothing to
  exchange. The pattern needs a genuine pair.

- ❌ 昨年(さくねん)の数値(すうち)<b>にひきかえ</b>、今年(ことし)は3%増(ふ)えた。 → ✅ 昨年(さくねん)の数値(すうち)<b>に対(たい)し</b>、今年(ことし)は3%増(ふ)えた。
  A bare percentage carries no verdict. `にひきかえ` would make the report sound as
  if it were disappointed in last year.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さん<b>にひきかえ</b>、あなたは遅(おそ)いですね。（said to the person） → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さんはもう終(お)わったそうですよ。
  Not ungrammatical - unkind. Ranking the listener below a named third party to
  their face is about as direct as Japanese criticism gets.

## Practice

**1.** 静(しず)かだった以前(いぜん)のこの町(まち)____、今(いま)は観光客(かんこうきゃく)であふれている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

静(しず)かだった以前(いぜん)のこの町(まち)<b>にひきかえ</b>、今(いま)は観光客(かんこうきゃく)であふれている。

Two states of one town, and the speaker plainly preferred the earlier one - a
comparable pair plus a verdict, which is what the pattern is for. The thing in front
is a noun phrase, so `の` is not needed.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 輸入(ゆにゅう)が前年(ぜんねん)並(な)みだったの（にひきかえ・に対(たい)し）、輸出(ゆしゅつ)は2%伸(の)びた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

輸入(ゆにゅう)が前年(ぜんねん)並(な)みだったの<b>に対(たい)し</b>、輸出(ゆしゅつ)は2%伸(の)びた。

The sentence is reporting numbers with no opinion attached, which is
<code>に対(たい)し</code>. `にひきかえ` would smuggle in a judgement about imports

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Unlike her cheerful sister, she hardly says a word."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明(あか)るい姉(あね)<b>にひきかえ</b>、彼女(かのじょ)はほとんど口(くち)をきかない。

Two sisters are exactly the comparable pair the pattern wants, and the English

directly - 明(あか)るい姉(あね) - rather than being nominalised.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 去年(きょねん)の大会(たいかい)は盛(さか)り上(あ)がったにひきかえ、今年(ことし)は客(きゃく)が少(すく)なかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 盛(さか)り上(あ)がった去年(きょねん)の大会(たいかい)<b>にひきかえ</b>、今年(ことし)は客(きゃく)が少(すく)なかった。
（or 去年(きょねん)の大会(たいかい)が盛(さか)り上(あ)がった<b>のにひきかえ</b>…）

`にひきかえ` needs a noun in front of it. Either make the verb clause into a
modifier of 大会(たいかい), or nominalise it with `の`. A plain past verb cannot
sit there on its own.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 去年(きょねん)<b>とは違(ちが)って</b>、今年(ことし)の新人(しんじん)はよく質問(しつもん)する。
(b) 去年(きょねん)<b>にひきかえ</b>、今年(ことし)の新人(しんじん)はよく質問(しつもん)する。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) notes a difference. It is neutral spoken Japanese, and last year's intake are
not necessarily being criticised - they were simply quieter.

(b) grades them. Last year's group becomes the poor benchmark against which this
year's are being praised, and anyone from last year's intake who overheard it would
be entitled to feel got at. The register is also a step more written, the kind of
line that turns up in a manager's report rather than in the break room.

</details>

# ～こそあれ

**Meaning** · there may be A, but…; there is A, if anything - grants that one thing exists, and uses that concession to deny or outweigh something else

`こそ` is the emphatic particle you already know from これこそ and
今(いま)こそ. In classical Japanese a clause marked with `こそ` had to end its
verb in the 已然形(いぜんけい), and ある's 已然形(いぜんけい) is あれ. The agreement rule died centuries ago;
this fossil did not. `こそあれ` is therefore *there certainly is A - and now here is
what follows from admitting that*.

## Formation

<code>あれ</code> is the verb ある, so what stands in front of it is a **noun** or
something wearing a noun's clothes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + こそあれ | 違(ちが)い → 違(ちが)いこそあれ |
| Noun (predicative) | Noun + でこそあれ | 挑戦(ちょうせん) → 挑戦(ちょうせん)でこそあれ |
| な-adjective | ～で + こそあれ | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)でこそあれ |
| い-adjective (rare) | ～く + こそあれ | 少(すく)ない → 少(すく)なくこそあれ |
| Variants | ～こそあれど / ～こそあるが | 差(さ)こそあれど |
| Modern equivalent | ～はあっても / ～はあるが | 差(さ)はあっても |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a **verb** cannot stand in front of `あれ`. An action takes the
sibling form <code>こそすれ</code>, built on the 已然形(いぜんけい) of `する`:
増(ふ)えこそすれ, not ❌増(ふ)えこそあれ. If what you are conceding *exists*,
use あれ; if it *happens*, use すれ.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - two shapes of sentence follow. Either the second clause simply carries on
in spite of the concession - 差(さ)こそあれ、本質(ほんしつ)は同(おな)じだ -
or it denies a rival item outright:
感謝(かんしゃ)こそあれ、恨(うら)みはない. The second is the more emphatic, and
it is the one that shades into <code>こそすれ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
程度(ていど)の差(さ)<b>こそあれ</b>、誰(だれ)にでもある悩(なや)みだ。
It varies in degree, certainly, but it is a worry everyone has.
</pre>

<pre>
遅(おく)れ<b>こそあれ</b>、計画(けいかく)が中止(ちゅうし)になったわけではない。
There has been a delay, yes; that does not mean the plan has been abandoned.
</pre>

<pre>
多少(たしょう)の違(ちが)い<b>こそあれ</b>、両者(りょうしゃ)の主張(しゅちょう)は本質的(ほんしつてき)に同(おな)じである。
Whatever minor differences there may be, the two positions are essentially the same.
</pre>

<pre>
感謝(かんしゃ)<b>こそあれ</b>、恨(うら)みなど少(すこ)しもございません。
If anything I am grateful; there is not the slightest resentment on my part.
</pre>

<pre>
規模(きぼ)の大小(だいしょう)<b>こそあれ</b>、どの自治体(じちたい)も同(おな)じ課題(かだい)を抱(かか)えている。
Large or small, every local authority is grappling with the same problem.
</pre>

<pre>
これは挑戦(ちょうせん)で<b>こそあれ</b>、無謀(むぼう)な冒険(ぼうけん)ではない。
This is a challenge, not a reckless adventure.
</pre>

<pre>
形(かたち)<b>こそあれ</b>、中身(なかみ)が伴(ともな)わなければ制度(せいど)は意味(いみ)をなさない。
The form may be there, but without substance behind it the system means nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
不便(ふべん)で<b>こそあれ</b>、この土地(とち)を離(はな)れる気(き)はないと老人(ろうじん)は静(しず)かに言(い)った。
Inconvenient it may be, the old man said quietly, but he had no intention of leaving this place.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; never say it.** `こそあれ` is fossilised classical grammar living
on in modern **written** Japanese of a formal kind: editorials, prefaces, prepared
speeches, official statements, the summing-up paragraph of a report, and literary
prose. Its 已然形(いぜんけい) あれ is not a form of ある that modern Japanese produces anywhere
else. Used in conversation it does not sound "rather formal" - it sounds like
somebody has begun reciting, and the listener will notice.

What you should produce instead is <code>～はあっても</code> or
<code>～はあるが</code>. 「多少(たしょう)の違(ちが)いはあっても、本質(ほんしつ)は同(おな)じだ」
says exactly the same thing in the language people speak.

Two things `こそあれ` is good at, and worth recognising it by. First, **conceding a
small thing to protect a big one**: yes there is a delay, no the project is not
dead. Second, **rejecting an accusation by naming the opposite feeling**:
感謝(かんしゃ)こそあれ、恨(うら)みはない is close to a set formula in formal
apology and formal denial, and it is considerably more emphatic than a plain
恨(うら)んでいません.

## Contrast

### ～こそあれ vs ～はあっても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same sentence, one in classical dress and one in modern.
<code>はあっても</code> is what you write in an email; `こそあれ` is what you read in
a leader column.

</div>

<pre>
多少(たしょう)の不満(ふまん)<b>はあっても</b>、やめるつもりはありません。
I have my complaints, but I have no intention of quitting.
</pre>

<pre>
多少(たしょう)の不満(ふまん)<b>こそあれ</b>、職(しょく)を辞(じ)する意思(いし)はない。
Grievances there may be; of any intention to resign there is none.
</pre>

Notice how the rest of the sentence has to move with it. `こそあれ` drags the whole
register upward - 辞(じ)する rather than やめる,
意思(いし)はない rather than つもりはありません - because a classical concessive
sitting in a colloquial sentence sounds like a costume half put on.

### ～こそあれ vs ～こそすれ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あれ` is ある and takes **a noun**: something *exists*. `すれ`
is する and takes **a verb stem**: something *happens*. Same fossil, two verbs.

</div>

<pre>
不安(ふあん)<b>こそあれ</b>、後悔(こうかい)はしていない。
Anxious I may be, but I have no regrets.
</pre>

<pre>
不安(ふあん)は増(ふ)え<b>こそすれ</b>、減(へ)ることはないだろう。
The anxiety will only grow; it certainly will not lessen.
</pre>

The first weighs one existing thing against another. The second predicts the
direction of a process and rules out the opposite direction. If you can put "there
is" in front of the English, you want あれ; if you can put "it will only", you want
すれ.

### ～こそあれ vs plain ～こそ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - it is the **same particle**, and `あれ` is the ending that the
old grammar demanded after it. Nothing about `こそ` has changed; only the verb ending
survives from the older language.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)<b>こそ</b>決断(けつだん)すべき時(とき)だ。
Now is precisely the moment to decide.
</pre>

<pre>
決断(けつだん)の遅(おそ)さ<b>こそあれ</b>、方向(ほうこう)自体(じたい)は間違(まちが)っていない。
Slow in deciding they may have been; the direction itself is not wrong.
</pre>

In modern Japanese `こそ` simply spotlights the word it follows and the sentence
carries on normally. In `こそあれ` the spotlight is still there, but the sentence
must then end in あれ and hand over to a second clause. Seeing them as one particle
with a fossilised tail makes the form much easier to parse at reading speed - which
is the only speed at which you will meet it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 違(ちが)い<b>こそある</b>、結論(けつろん)は同(おな)じだ。 → ✅ 違(ちが)い<b>こそあれ</b>、結論(けつろん)は同(おな)じだ。
  The whole point of the form is the 已然形(いぜんけい) `あれ`. With plain `ある` the clause
  simply stops and the sentence falls apart.

- ❌ 増(ふ)え<b>こそあれ</b>、減(へ)りはしない。 → ✅ 増(ふ)え<b>こそすれ</b>、減(へ)りはしない。
  `あれ` is ある and needs something that exists. A verb stem takes `すれ`.

- ❌ 問題(もんだい)<b>こそあれ</b>。 → ✅ 問題(もんだい)<b>こそあれ</b>、運営(うんえい)に支障(ししょう)はない。
  It is a connective, not an ending. A second clause always follows.

- ❌ （to a friend）ちょっと不満(ふまん)<b>こそあれ</b>、まあいいや。 → ✅ ちょっと不満(ふまん)<b>はあるけど</b>、まあいいや。
  A register collision. Classical concord inside a sentence that ends in まあいいや
  is the linguistic equivalent of a dinner jacket with shorts.

## Practice

**1.** 表現(ひょうげん)の違(ちが)い____、二人(ふたり)の言(い)っていることは同(おな)じだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

表現(ひょうげん)の違(ちが)い<b>こそあれ</b>、二人(ふたり)の言(い)っていることは同(おな)じだ。

A small difference is granted in order to protect the larger claim of sameness -
the pattern's commonest job. The word in front is a noun, which is what `あれ`
requires.

</details>

**2.** Where would you meet this sentence?
批判(ひはん)<b>こそあれ</b>、当社(とうしゃ)の姿勢(しせい)に変(か)わりはございません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a written corporate statement - a press release, a notice on a company website,
a formal reply to a complaint. The polite ございません and the classical `こそあれ`
belong to the same register.

You would not hear it in a meeting. Spoken, it would be
「批判(ひはん)はありますが、方針(ほうしん)は変(か)えません」.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 苦労(くろう)<b>こそあれ</b>、この仕事(しごと)を選(えら)んだことを後悔(こうかい)してはいない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

苦労(くろう)<b>はあっても</b>、この仕事(しごと)を選(えら)んだことを後悔(こうかい)していない。

<code>はあっても</code> is the direct modern equivalent, and once the classical form
goes the rest of the sentence can relax too - していない rather than してはいない.
This is the substitution to make whenever you are tempted to produce `こそあれ`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 参加者(さんかしゃ)は減(へ)り<b>こそあれ</b>、増(ふ)えることはないだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 参加者(さんかしゃ)は減(へ)り<b>こそすれ</b>、増(ふ)えることはないだろう。

減(へ)り is a verb stem - an action, not a thing that exists - so the form is
`こそすれ`, the 已然形(いぜんけい) of `する`. `あれ` is reserved for nouns.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 失敗(しっぱい)<b>はあったが</b>、全体(ぜんたい)としては成功(せいこう)だった。
(b) 失敗(しっぱい)<b>こそあれ</b>、全体(ぜんたい)としては成功(せいこう)であった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The propositional content is identical: there were failures, and the project
succeeded overall.

(a) is ordinary modern Japanese, usable in a meeting or an email, and it sounds like
somebody being straightforward.

(b) is the written verdict. `こそ` pushes hard on the concession - *failures there
certainly were* - which paradoxically strengthens the defence that follows, and the
classical あれ marks the sentence as a formal judgement rather than a remark. Expect
it in a report's conclusion or a chairman's statement, and expect であった rather
than だった to keep it company.

</details>

# ～こそすれ～ない

**Meaning** · it will only A - it certainly will not B - names the one thing that is happening, and uses that to shut the door on its opposite

This is the sister form of <code>こそあれ</code>, built on the same dead grammar.
`こそ` once forced the verb at the end of its clause into the
已然形(いぜんけい), and する's 已然形(いぜんけい) is すれ. What survives is a
two-part rhetorical move: *A, yes, absolutely - B, never*. The second half is always
a denial, and it is the denial the speaker actually cares about.

## Formation

Because `すれ` is `する`, what stands in front of it is an **action**: a verb stem,
or a noun that takes する.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | stem + こそすれ | 増(ふ)える → 増(ふ)えこそすれ |
| Verb (う-verb stem) | stem + こそすれ | 減(へ)る → 減(へ)りこそすれ |
| する-noun | Noun + こそすれ | 感謝(かんしゃ) → 感謝(かんしゃ)こそすれ |
| Second clause | must be a **negative** | …ない / …ことはない / …はしない |
| Variant | ～こそすれど | 増(ふ)えこそすれど |
| Modern equivalent | ～ことはあっても～ない | 増(ふ)えることはあっても |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two halves are **opposites**, and the second half is
**always denied**. ❌<code>増(ふ)えこそすれ、減(へ)るだろう</code> is not a
weaker version of the pattern; it is a broken one. If the second clause is not a
negation, you do not want this form.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a plain `ない` is the minimum, but the pattern usually reaches for a
heavier denial: <code>ことはない</code>, <code>はしない</code>,
<code>など考(かんが)えられない</code>, <code>者(もの)は一人(ひとり)もいない</code>.
The weight of the second half is part of the effect.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
負担(ふたん)は増(ふ)え<b>こそすれ</b>、減(へ)ることはないだろう。
The burden will only grow; it certainly will not shrink.
</pre>

<pre>
感謝(かんしゃ)<b>こそすれ</b>、文句(もんく)を言(い)う筋合(すじあ)いはない。
I owe them thanks, if anything; I am in no position to complain.
</pre>

<pre>
状況(じょうきょう)は悪化(あっか)し<b>こそすれ</b>、改善(かいぜん)する見込(みこ)みはない。
The situation can only get worse; there is no prospect of improvement.
</pre>

<pre>
尊敬(そんけい)<b>こそすれ</b>、あの人(ひと)を軽蔑(けいべつ)するなど考(かんが)えられない。
I respect him, if anything; the idea of looking down on him is unthinkable.
</pre>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は格差(かくさ)を広(ひろ)げ<b>こそすれ</b>、縮(ちぢ)めることはない。
This system will only widen the gap; it will do nothing to narrow it.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がたてば、思(おも)い出(で)は美(うつく)しくなり<b>こそすれ</b>、薄(うす)れはしない。
With time, memories only grow more beautiful; they do not fade.
</pre>

<pre>
批判(ひはん)<b>こそすれ</b>、彼(かれ)の功績(こうせき)そのものを否定(ひてい)する者(もの)は一人(ひとり)もいなかった。
Criticise him they did, but there was not one person who denied his achievements as such.
</pre>

<pre>
規制(きせい)を強(つよ)めれば取引(とりひき)は地下(ちか)に潜(もぐ)り<b>こそすれ</b>、なくなりはしないというのが専門家(せんもんか)の見方(みかた)だ。
Tighten the rules and the trade will simply go underground rather than disappear - that is the experts' view.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; do not deploy it.** Like <code>こそあれ</code>, `こそすれ` is a
classical form kept alive in **formal written Japanese**: editorials, policy
argument, prepared speeches, statements of position, formal letters of apology or
defence. Spoken aloud outside those settings it sounds declamatory - the register of
a person addressing a hall rather than a room. In conversation the same idea comes
out as 「増(ふ)えることはあっても、減(へ)ることはないよ」 or simply
「むしろ増(ふ)えると思(おも)う」.

What the form is really for is **emphatic denial**. The first half is a concession
you are happy to make - often one that sounds bad for you - and its whole function
is to make the refusal that follows unanswerable. That is why it is a standard move
in formal self-defence: 「感謝(かんしゃ)こそすれ、恨(うら)んでなどおりません」
denies resentment far more forcefully than any direct denial could, because it
volunteers the opposite emotion first.

There is a second, quieter use in argument and forecasting:
悪化(あっか)しこそすれ、改善(かいぜん)は望(のぞ)めない. Here the writer is
ruling out one direction of travel, and the classical form lends the prediction the
air of a settled judgement.

## Contrast

### ～こそすれ vs ～ことはあっても～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the modern rewrite. <code>ことはあっても</code> says the same
thing in the language people actually speak, and loses only the ceremony.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は上(あ)がる<b>ことはあっても</b>、下(さ)がることはないと思(おも)う。
I think prices may well go up, but they won't come down.
</pre>

<pre>
価格(かかく)は上昇(じょうしょう)し<b>こそすれ</b>、下落(げらく)することはあるまい。
Prices will only rise; they are hardly going to fall.
</pre>

The content is identical. Notice again that the classical form pulls the whole
sentence up with it - 価格(かかく) for 値段(ねだん),
上昇(じょうしょう)する for 上(あ)がる, あるまい for ないと思(おも)う. A
`こそすれ` in a casual sentence sounds mismatched in both directions.

### ～こそすれ vs ～こそあれ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すれ` is `する` and takes **an action**; `あれ` is `ある` and
takes **a thing that exists**. And `こそすれ` demands a negative second clause,
where `こそあれ` often merely concedes and carries on.

</div>

<pre>
多少(たしょう)の遅(おく)れ<b>こそあれ</b>、工事(こうじ)は順調(じゅんちょう)に進(すす)んでいる。
There is a slight delay, but the work is going smoothly.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は遅(おく)れ<b>こそすれ</b>、予定(よてい)より早(はや)く終(お)わることはない。
The work may run late; it is certainly not going to finish early.
</pre>

The first sentence concedes a delay and then reports something positive - no denial
required. The second uses the delay to rule out the opposite outcome. If you are
reading and cannot remember which is which, look at what follows the comma: a flat
negative means `こそすれ`.

### ～こそすれ vs ～どころか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - mirror images. `どころか` names the **false** thing first and
then supplies the truth. `こそすれ` names the **true** thing first and then denies
the false one.

</div>

<pre>
謝(あやま)る<b>どころか</b>、彼(かれ)は怒(おこ)り出(だ)した。
Far from apologising, he lost his temper.
</pre>

<pre>
怒(おこ)り<b>こそすれ</b>、彼(かれ)が謝(あやま)ることなどあり得(え)ない。
Angry he may get; apologise he never will.
</pre>

The two sentences report the same man. `どころか` is ordinary Japanese, usable in
speech, and it lands as surprise or indignation. `こそすれ` is written, and it lands
as a verdict. If you want to say this out loud, `どころか` is the form to reach for.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 増(ふ)え<b>こそすれ</b>、減(へ)るだろう。 → ✅ 増(ふ)え<b>こそすれ</b>、減(へ)ることはないだろう。
  The second half must be a denial. Without it the sentence has conceded something
  for no reason and never arrives anywhere.

- ❌ 増(ふ)える<b>こそすれ</b> → ✅ 増(ふ)え<b>こそすれ</b>
  What goes in front is the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. `こそ` is a particle
  and cannot follow a finished verb here.

- ❌ 感謝(かんしゃ)<b>こそする</b>、恨(うら)んでいない。 → ✅ 感謝(かんしゃ)<b>こそすれ</b>、恨(うら)んでいない。
  The 已然形(いぜんけい) `すれ` is the form the old concord requires and the thing
  that links the two clauses. Plain `する` leaves the first clause stranded.

- ❌ 不安(ふあん)<b>こそすれ</b>、自信(じしん)はない。 → ✅ 不安(ふあん)<b>こそあれ</b>、自信(じしん)がないわけではない。
  不安(ふあん) is a state you *have*, not an action you *do*, so it belongs with
  `あれ`. A quick test: if the noun cannot take する, it cannot take すれ.

## Practice

**1.** この方針(ほうしん)では、問題(もんだい)は大(おお)きくなり____、解決(かいけつ)することはないだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

問題(もんだい)は大(おお)きくなり<b>こそすれ</b>、解決(かいけつ)することはないだろう。

Growing and being solved are the two opposite directions, and the sentence concedes
the first in order to rule out the second. The verb in front is the ます-stem
大(おお)きくなり.

</details>

**2.** Where would you meet this sentence, and how would it be said in conversation?
当社(とうしゃ)は雇用(こよう)を守(まも)り<b>こそすれ</b>、削減(さくげん)する考(かんが)えはございません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a written or read-out corporate statement - a press release, a shareholders'
briefing, a reply to a union. The classical `こそすれ` and the humble ございません
belong to the same register.

Spoken, it would be
「雇用(こよう)は守(まも)ります。減(へ)らすつもりはありません」. Two plain
sentences do the same work.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 人口(じんこう)は減少(げんしょう)し（こそあれ・こそすれ）、増加(ぞうか)に転(てん)じることはない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人口(じんこう)は減少(げんしょう)し<b>こそすれ</b>、増加(ぞうか)に転(てん)じることはない。

減少(げんしょう)する is an action - a process the population undergoes - so the
form is `すれ`. `あれ` would need a noun standing for something that simply exists.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 批判(ひはん)こそすれ、彼(かれ)を支持(しじ)する人(ひと)も多(おお)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 批判(ひはん)<b>こそすれ</b>、彼(かれ)を見捨(みす)てる人(ひと)は一人(ひとり)もいない。
"Criticise him they may; not one of them will abandon him."

The original's second clause is not a denial, so the pattern has nothing to shut
down. Either supply a negative, as above, or drop the form entirely and write
「批判(ひはん)もあるが、支持(しじ)する人(ひと)も多(おお)い」.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 文句(もんく)を言(い)う<b>どころか</b>、彼(かれ)は礼(れい)まで言(い)ってくれた。
(b) 礼(れい)を言(い)い<b>こそすれ</b>、文句(もんく)など言(い)うはずがない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports what happened and is surprised by it. `どころか` sets up the expected
complaint, knocks it down, and replaces it with something better; the speaker is
recounting an event, in ordinary spoken Japanese.

(b) argues. It is not a report but a defence - probably against a suggestion that
somebody complained - and the concession of thanks is offered precisely to make the
denial airtight. The register is written and formal, and the sentence sounds like a
position being stated rather than a story being told.

</details>

# ～たはいいが

**Meaning** · did it, and that much was fine, but then… - the act itself went through and felt like the right move; what nobody thought about was what came next

Take it apart and it is transparent. `～た` is the completed act, `は` topicalises
it, `いい` grades it - *that part was fine* - and `が` turns the corner. The frame
exists to praise one step and then drop the reader straight into the trouble that
followed it, which is why the second half is never good news. It is the shape of
every story told against oneself.

## Formation

The verb is **past**, and `は` sits directly on it. That bare `V-た + は` is what
makes the pattern look wrong to learners; it is a contraction of the fuller
<code>～たのはいいが</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (past) | plain past + はいいが | 買(か)った → 買(か)ったはいいが |
| Fuller form | ～たのはいいが | 買(か)ったのはいいが |
| Emphatic | ～たまではいいが | 買(か)ったまではいいが |
| Softer endings | ～たはいいけれど / けど | 辞(や)めたはいいけど |
| Past-tense evaluation | ～たのはよかったが | 辞(や)めたのはよかったが |
| Written variant | ～たはいいものの | 辞(や)めたはいいものの |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the first clause has to be something that felt **welcome at the
time**, and the second has to be a **problem**. ❌<code>財布(さいふ)を落(お)としたはいいが</code>
fails the first test; ❌<code>買(か)ったはいいが、とても便利(べんり)だ</code>
fails the second.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the subject need not have done the thing on purpose. A welcome event
works just as well: バスが来(き)たはいいが、満員(まんいん)で乗(の)れなかった.
What matters is that it looked good for a moment.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
パソコンを買(か)った<b>はいいが</b>、使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)からない。
I bought the computer, fine - now I can't work out how to use it.
</pre>

<pre>
家(いえ)を出(で)た<b>はいいが</b>、財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れていた。
I got out of the house all right, and then realised I'd left my wallet behind.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めた<b>はいいが</b>、次(つぎ)の仕事(しごと)がまだ決(き)まらない。
Quitting was all very well; I still haven't lined up anything else.
</pre>

<pre>
犬(いぬ)を飼(か)い始(はじ)めた<b>はいいが</b>、散歩(さんぽ)に行(い)くのは結局(けっきょく)母(はは)だ。
We got a dog, which was lovely - and it's my mother who ends up walking it.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きな机(つくえ)を注文(ちゅうもん)した<b>はいいが</b>、部屋(へや)のドアを通(とお)らなかった。
I ordered a big desk, pleased with myself, and it wouldn't fit through the door.
</pre>

<pre>
勢(いきお)いで手(て)を挙(あ)げた<b>はいいが</b>、何(なに)を話(はな)すか全(まった)く考(かんが)えていなかった。
I put my hand up on impulse - and had given absolutely no thought to what I'd say.
</pre>

<pre>
頂上(ちょうじょう)まで登(のぼ)った<b>はいいが</b>、霧(きり)で何(なに)も見(み)えなかった。
We made it to the summit, for what it was worth: fog, and nothing to see.
</pre>

<pre>
補助金(ほじょきん)の申請(しんせい)を出(だ)した<b>はいいが</b>、添付(てんぷ)書類(しょるい)が足(た)りず差(さ)し戻(もど)されてしまった。
I got the grant application in, which was the easy part; it came straight back for missing attachments.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this before you try to produce it.** The problem with
`～たはいいが` is not age - it is not archaic, and you will hear it - but
**narrowness**. It belongs to one setting: the wry, self-deprecating anecdote.
Blogs, essays, columns, chat between friends, the paragraph in a work email where
somebody admits a plan half-worked. In a formal report, a proposal or a piece of
news copy it is out of place, and the phrase that goes there is
<code>～たものの</code>. In neutral conversation, <code>～たけど</code> does the same
job with none of the framing.

Two things learners get wrong when they reach for it. They attach it to a first
clause that was never welcome, which makes the いい nonsensical; and they follow it
with something positive, which makes the が pointless. Both errors come from
translating "but" rather than hearing the frame.

The tone, when it is used well, is **rueful and faintly comic**. The speaker is
admitting to a small failure of foresight and inviting you to enjoy it. That is
also why it does not sit well in anything that has to sound serious: applied to a
real disaster it reads as flippant.

## Contrast

### ～たはいいが vs ～たものの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same two events, two registers. <code>～たものの</code> is
the neutral, written form and takes no attitude. `～たはいいが` adds *and I was
pleased with myself at the time*, and expects a wry smile.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)を導入(どうにゅう)した<b>ものの</b>、現場(げんば)の混乱(こんらん)は収(おさ)まらなかった。
The new system was introduced, but the confusion on the ground did not settle.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいアプリを入(い)れた<b>はいいが</b>、通知(つうち)が多(おお)すぎてすぐ消(け)した。
I installed the new app, all pleased with it, and the notifications were so bad I deleted it again.
</pre>

Put `～たはいいが` into the first sentence and a policy review starts sounding like a
diary entry. Put <code>ものの</code> into the second and the joke evaporates. If
what you are writing has a letterhead, use <code>ものの</code>.

### ～たはいいが vs ～たらいいが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one kana apart, and opposite in time. `～たはいいが` reports
something that **has happened**. `～たらいいが` is a **wish** about something that
has not.

</div>

<pre>
間(ま)に合(あ)った<b>はいいが</b>、席(せき)はもう後(うし)ろしか空(あ)いていなかった。
I made it in time, for all the good it did - only back-row seats left.
</pre>

<pre>
間(ま)に合(あ)っ<b>たらいいが</b>、この渋滞(じゅうたい)では難(むずか)しそうだ。
I hope we make it in time, but with this traffic it looks unlikely.
</pre>

The first speaker is already inside the hall; the second is still in the car. The
`が` at the end looks the same in both, but in the first it introduces the snag and
in the second it introduces the doubt. Reading fast, the `は`/`ら` is the only thing
telling you which situation you are in.

### ～たはいいが vs ～たあげく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あげく` needs a **long, wearing process** behind it and delivers
a bad end. `～たはいいが` needs a **single decisive act** and delivers an
unforeseen snag.

</div>

<pre>
何時間(なんじかん)も迷(まよ)った<b>あげく</b>、結局(けっきょく)何(なに)も買(か)わなかった。
After hours of dithering, I ended up buying nothing at all.
</pre>

<pre>
思(おも)い切(き)って買(か)った<b>はいいが</b>、一度(いちど)も着(き)ていない。
I took the plunge and bought it - and I have never once worn it.
</pre>

`あげく` looks back over the whole miserable process and reports where it landed.
`～たはいいが` skips the process entirely: the decision was quick, it felt good, and
the trouble arrived afterwards. They also differ in blame -
`あげく` is often impatient with the person, while `～たはいいが` is forgiving,
because the speaker is usually that person.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 買(か)う<b>はいいが</b>、置(お)く場所(ばしょ)がない。 → ✅ 買(か)った<b>はいいが</b>、置(お)く場所(ばしょ)がない。
  The verb is past. The frame reports something already done; a dictionary form
  turns it into an unfinished thought.

- ❌ 財布(さいふ)を落(お)とした<b>はいいが</b>、中身(なかみ)は無事(ぶじ)だった。 → ✅ 財布(さいふ)を落(お)としたが、中身(なかみ)は無事(ぶじ)だった。
  Losing a wallet was never the good part, and the news that follows is good, so
  both halves of the frame are inverted. Use a plain `が`.

- ❌ 予約(よやく)した<b>はいいが</b>、当日(とうじつ)はとても楽(たの)しかった。 → ✅ 予約(よやく)した<b>はいいが</b>、当日(とうじつ)は大雨(おおあめ)だった。
  What follows has to be the snag. With a happy second clause the いい and the が
  are both left with nothing to do.

- ❌ （in a formal report）設備(せつび)を更新(こうしん)した<b>はいいが</b>、稼働率(かどうりつ)は上(あ)がらなかった。 → ✅ 設備(せつび)を更新(こうしん)した<b>ものの</b>、稼働率(かどうりつ)は上(あ)がらなかった。
  A register error. The frame carries a shrug, and a shrug does not belong in a
  document that has to be taken seriously.

## Practice

**1.** 自転車(じてんしゃ)を安(やす)く手(て)に入(い)れた____、ブレーキが効(き)かなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自転車(じてんしゃ)を安(やす)く手(て)に入(い)れた<b>はいいが</b>、ブレーキが効(き)かなかった。

A cheap bike is a small triumph, and the brakes are the snag nobody checked - the
frame's two halves exactly. Note the plain past directly in front of `は`.

</details>

**2.** Where would this sentence be at home, and where would it not be?
補助金(ほじょきん)を申請(しんせい)した<b>はいいが</b>、書類(しょるい)の山(やま)に埋(う)もれている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

At home in a blog post, an essay, or a message to a colleague you are friendly with -
anywhere you are telling a story slightly against yourself.

Not at home in the report that goes with the application. There you would write
申請(しんせい)した<b>ものの</b>, which states the same facts without the shrug.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 明日(あした)晴(は)れ（たはいいが・たらいいが）、予報(よほう)は雨(あめ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)晴(は)れ<b>たらいいが</b>、予報(よほう)は雨(あめ)だ。

Tomorrow has not happened yet, so this is a wish, not a report: `たら` + `いいが`.
`たはいいが` would claim the sky had already cleared, which the second half then
contradicts.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 事故(じこ)に遭(あ)ったはいいが、けがはなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 事故(じこ)に遭(あ)った<b>が</b>、けがはなかった。
（or 事故(じこ)に遭(あ)った<b>ものの</b>、けがはなかった）

Being in an accident was not the good part, and coming out unhurt is not a snag.
Both conditions of the frame are violated at once, and the sentence reads as though
the speaker were disappointed to be uninjured.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 引(ひ)っ越(こ)した<b>ものの</b>、近所(きんじょ)に店(みせ)が一(いっ)軒(けん)もない。
(b) 引(ひ)っ越(こ)した<b>はいいが</b>、近所(きんじょ)に店(みせ)が一(いっ)軒(けん)もない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat report. The move happened, the shops are absent, and the speaker
sounds mildly deflated. It would look perfectly natural in a written account and
gives away nothing about how the speaker felt on moving day.

(b) puts the speaker's earlier optimism into the sentence. The move felt like a good
decision at the time, and the absent shops are the thing they failed to check. It is
warmer, funnier and more personal - and it would be the wrong choice in anything
that needed to sound measured.

</details>
