# N1 - Occasions, periods, and doing two things at once


---

# ～というもの

**Meaning** · for a whole ~ now; for the entire stretch of - takes a period of time and presents it as one unbroken span the speaker has felt every day of

After a length of time, <code>というもの</code> adds no information at all. It adds
**weight**. <code>この一年(いちねん)</code> is a measurement;
<code>この一年(いちねん)というもの</code> is a year that dragged, and the speaker is
about to say what made it drag. The main clause is almost always a state that never
let up - ずっと, 一度(いちど)も…ない, ～ばかり.

## Formation

The slot in front is a **span of time**, not a clause. Spans are usually built with
この or ここ.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Span with この | この + span + というもの | この一年(いちねん) → この一年(いちねん)というもの |
| Span with ここ | ここ + span + というもの | ここ数(すう)か月(げつ) → ここ数(すう)か月(げつ)というもの |
| Bare span | span + というもの | 十年間(じゅうねんかん)というもの |
| Span modified by a clause | clause + span + というもの | 入院(にゅういん)していた半年(はんとし)というもの |
| Topic-marked | ～というものは | この三日(みっか)というものは |
| From an event onwards | ～てから + というもの | 退院(たいいん)してからというもの |
| Usual main clause | ずっと／一度(いちど)も～ない／～ばかり | ずっと雨(あめ)だ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the sentence would be just as true with a plain
<code>間(あいだ)</code>, then <code>というもの</code> is telling you something about the
**speaker**, not about the calendar: it was long, and they felt it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>というもの</b>、ずっと雨(あめ)だ。
It has done nothing but rain this entire week.
</pre>

<pre>
ここ三日(みっか)<b>というもの</b>、ろくに寝(ね)ていない。
I have barely slept for the last three days.
</pre>

<pre>
この一年(いちねん)<b>というもの</b>、彼(かれ)からは一度(いちど)も連絡(れんらく)がない。
For a whole year now there has not been a single word from him.
</pre>

<pre>
入院(にゅういん)していた一(いっ)か月(げつ)<b>というもの</b>、天井(てんじょう)ばかり眺(なが)めていた。
For the whole month I was in hospital, all I did was stare at the ceiling.
</pre>

<pre>
この半年(はんとし)<b>というもの</b>、息子(むすこ)は部屋(へや)から出(で)ようとしない。
For half a year now my son has refused to come out of his room.
</pre>

<pre>
震災(しんさい)のあとの数(すう)か月(げつ)<b>というもの</b>、住民(じゅうみん)は水(みず)を運(はこ)ぶことに追(お)われていた。
For the months that followed the earthquake, residents spent their days hauling water.
</pre>

<pre>
戦争(せんそう)が終(お)わるまでの四年間(よねんかん)<b>というもの</b>、この通(とお)りに明(あ)かりがともることはなかった。
For the four years until the war ended, no light was ever lit along this street.
</pre>

<pre>
ここ十年(じゅうねん)<b>というもの</b>、この商店街(しょうてんがい)に新(あたら)しい店(みせ)ができたためしがない。
In the last ten years not one new shop has opened in this shopping street.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is an **emphatic, faintly literary** device that is nonetheless perfectly at
home in adult conversation - said with a sigh, in a complaint, or at the start of a
piece of reminiscence. It turns up in essays, memoirs, letters and interviews far
more often than in a report, because a report has no reason to tell you how long a
year felt.

Two restrictions follow from that emotional core. The span has to be one the speaker
has lived through, so the pattern **looks backwards**:
❌<code>これから一年(いちねん)というもの</code> is not how you announce a plan. And the
span has to feel long. Days, weeks, months and years are the natural fillers; a few
minutes works only when the speaker means that the minutes crawled.

Watch the homograph. <code>というもの</code> also exists as the "X is the kind of thing
that…" construction - <code>親(おや)というものはありがたい</code> - and as the verdict
<code>というものではない</code>. Those attach to a **noun naming a category** and are
followed by は or だ. The time-span use attaches to a **length of time** and is
followed by a comma and a clause. The two rarely collide in practice, but a learner
meeting the string for the second time often assumes it is the same pattern.

The sibling <code>～てからというもの</code> does the same work with an **event** in the
slot instead of a span: it names the moment the long stretch began.

## Contrast

### ～というもの vs ～の間(あいだ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間(あいだ)</code> measures the period.
<code>というもの</code> complains about it.

</div>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)の間(あいだ)、この町(まち)に住(す)んでいました。
I lived in this town for three years.
</pre>

<pre>
この三年(さんねん)<b>というもの</b>、この町(まち)を一歩(いっぽ)も出(で)ていない。
For three whole years I have not set foot outside this town.
</pre>

The first is a neutral fact and could go on a form. The second carries frustration
or wonder, and the listener expects you to go on and say why. Note too that
<code>間(あいだ)</code> takes a future span quite happily -
<code>夏(なつ)の間(あいだ)働(はたら)きます</code> - where
<code>というもの</code> cannot.

### ～というもの vs ～てからというもの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a **span** of time takes <code>というもの</code>; an **event** that
started the span takes <code>てからというもの</code>.

</div>

<pre>
この二年(にねん)<b>というもの</b>、外食(がいしょく)をしていない。
I have not eaten out once in these two years.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが生(う)まれ<b>てからというもの</b>、外食(がいしょく)をしていない。
Ever since the baby was born we have not eaten out.
</pre>

Both describe the same drought. The first frames it by the clock, the second by its
cause. They do not mix: ❌<code>生(う)まれたというもの</code> and
❌<code>この二年(にねん)てからというもの</code> are both broken.

### ～というもの vs ～というものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a **time span**, "for that whole stretch". After a **noun
naming a category**, "that is what X is".

</div>

<pre>
この十年(じゅうねん)<b>というもの</b>、給料(きゅうりょう)は上(あ)がっていない。
Wages have not gone up once in ten years.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)<b>というものは</b>、なかなか上(あ)がらないものだ。
Wages are the sort of thing that stubbornly refuse to rise.
</pre>

The tell is what follows. A comma and a clause about that period means the time use;
は or だ and a general truth means the definition use.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)る<b>というもの</b>、足(あし)が痛(いた)い。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っているので、足(あし)が痛(いた)い。
  A verb clause cannot fill the slot. Only a length of time can, and if you want an
  event as the starting point you need <code>てからというもの</code>.

- ❌ これから一年(いちねん)<b>というもの</b>、頑張(がんば)ります。 → ✅ これから一年間(いちねんかん)、頑張(がんば)ります。
  The pattern reports a stretch already lived through. It cannot look forward.

- ❌ この一年(いちねん)<b>というもの</b>、北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)った。 → ✅ この一年(いちねん)<b>というもの</b>、旅行(りょこう)らしい旅行(りょこう)をしていない。
  One completed event does not fill a long stretch. The main clause needs a state
  that held, or a run of nothing happening.

- ❌ 親(おや)というもの、心配(しんぱい)ばかりしている。 → ✅ 親(おや)<b>というものは</b>、心配(しんぱい)ばかりしているものだ。
  In the definition use the noun phrase is a topic, so は is not optional.

## Practice

**1.** ここ一週間(いっしゅうかん)____、息子(むすこ)からの電話(でんわ)が一本(いっぽん)もない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ここ一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>というもの</b>、息子(むすこ)からの電話(でんわ)が一本(いっぽん)もない。

A span built with ここ, and a main clause that is a run of nothing at all - this is
the shape the pattern was made for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 退院(たいいん)し（てからというもの・というもの）、父(ちち)は酒(さけ)を一滴(いってき)も飲(の)まなくなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

退院(たいいん)し<b>てからというもの</b>

The slot is filled by an event, leaving hospital, not by a length of time. An event
needs the <code>てから</code> version; bare <code>というもの</code> would have to follow
something like <code>この三年(さんねん)</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "For the whole of last winter the shop stayed shut."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

去年(きょねん)の冬(ふゆ)中(じゅう)<b>というもの</b>、店(みせ)は閉(し)まったままだった。

The season supplies the span, and <code>閉(し)まったまま</code> supplies the
unbroken state the pattern wants. A neutral <code>去年(きょねん)の冬(ふゆ)は</code>
is also grammatical, but it loses the sense that the closure went on and on.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来月(らいげつ)から三(さん)か月(げつ)というもの、海外(かいがい)で研修(けんしゅう)を受(う)ける予定(よてい)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来月(らいげつ)から三(さん)か月間(げつかん)、海外(かいがい)で研修(けんしゅう)を受(う)ける予定(よてい)です。

<code>というもの</code> reports on time the speaker has already been through, so it
cannot sit in front of a plan. With a future span, use the plain length of time.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この五年(ごねん)<b>というもの</b>、彼(かれ)とは会(あ)っていない。
(b) 五年間(ごねんかん)、彼(かれ)とは会(あ)っていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a plain fact about the last five years; it would sit comfortably in a witness
statement or an email.

(a) puts the speaker inside those five years. It reads as regret, or as the opening
of a story about why the friendship lapsed, and the listener will expect a second
sentence explaining it.

</details>

# ～にあって

**Meaning** · in; at; placed in (a time, a place, or a position) - and everything the sentence goes on to say follows from being there

<code>にあって</code> is the て-form of <code>にある</code>, so the literal sense is "being
in X". What the pattern adds is that X is a **circumstance with force**: a crisis, an
era, a posting far from home, a job that carries responsibility. The rest of the
sentence is then read as a consequence of standing in that place. An ordinary setting
- a classroom, a kitchen - takes で and nothing else.

## Formation

A noun goes straight in front; there is no の and no clause form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (time, era) | Noun + にあって | 戦時下(せんじか) → 戦時下(せんじか)にあって |
| Noun (place, situation) | Noun + にあって | 異国(いこく)の地(ち) → 異国(いこく)の地(ち)にあって |
| Noun (position, role) | Noun + にあって | その立場(たちば) → その立場(たちば)にあって |
| Topic-marked | ～にあっては | 非常時(ひじょうじ)にあっては |
| Concessive, "even in" | ～にあっても | 逆境(ぎゃっきょう)にあっても |
| Modifying a following noun | ～にある + Noun | 困難(こんなん)な状況(じょうきょう)にある人(ひと)々 |
| ❌ Verb clause | ❌働(はたら)くにあって | ✅働(はたら)く上(うえ)で |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>にあって</code> is a connective form and cannot end a sentence. To
finish on the idea, you drop back to the plain verb:
<code>会社(かいしゃ)は苦(くる)しい状況(じょうきょう)にある</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
非常時(ひじょうじ)<b>にあって</b>は、冷静(れいせい)さが何(なに)より大切(たいせつ)だ。
In an emergency, keeping calm matters more than anything.
</pre>

<pre>
戦時下(せんじか)<b>にあって</b>も、学校(がっこう)の授業(じゅぎょう)は続(つづ)けられた。
Even under wartime conditions, classes at the school carried on.
</pre>

<pre>
この不況(ふきょう)<b>にあって</b>、新(あたら)しい工場(こうじょう)を建(た)てるのは勇気(ゆうき)のいる判断(はんだん)だ。
In a recession like this one, building a new plant takes nerve.
</pre>

<pre>
異国(いこく)の地(ち)<b>にあって</b>、母国(ぼこく)の言葉(ことば)を耳(みみ)にすると胸(むね)が熱(あつ)くなる。
Far from home in a foreign country, hearing your own language catches at your chest.
</pre>

<pre>
どんな逆境(ぎゃっきょう)<b>にあって</b>も、彼女(かのじょ)は笑顔(えがお)を絶(た)やさなかった。
However badly things went against her, she never stopped smiling.
</pre>

<pre>
管理職(かんりしょく)という立場(たちば)<b>にあって</b>は、部下(ぶか)の失敗(しっぱい)も自分(じぶん)の責任(せきにん)となる。
In a management position, a subordinate's mistake is your own responsibility too.
</pre>

<pre>
情報(じょうほう)があふれる現代(げんだい)<b>にあって</b>、何(なに)を信(しん)じるかは一人一人(ひとりひとり)の判断(はんだん)に委(ゆだ)ねられている。
In an age awash with information, what to believe is left to each of us to decide.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)百年(ひゃくねん)という節目(ふしめ)<b>にあって</b>、会社(かいしゃ)は事業(じぎょう)の見直(みなお)しを迫(せま)られている。
Standing at its hundredth year, the company is under pressure to rethink what it does.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **formal written Japanese**. Its home ground is editorials, company
statements, history writing, biography and prepared speeches; in conversation it
appears only when someone is speaking in a public, prepared register, and even then
it sounds like a line from a text. The everyday equivalents are
<code>～の中(なか)で</code> and a plain <code>～のときに</code>.

The noun in front is doing more than naming a place. It has to be a condition that
**bears on** what follows - hard times, a decisive era, a position of responsibility,
exile, isolation. That is why the pattern so often introduces a contrast: the
circumstances were against it, and yet. With も it becomes explicitly concessive
(<code>逆境(ぎゃっきょう)にあっても</code>), and with は it sets the circumstance up as
a topic the whole sentence is measured against.

Because the meaning is "being situated in", <code>にあって</code> never marks the mere
venue of an action. ❌<code>教室(きょうしつ)にあって勉強(べんきょう)した</code> is not
formal Japanese, it is wrong Japanese;
<code>教室(きょうしつ)で勉強(べんきょう)した</code> is what you want. A useful test:
if you could replace the noun with "in the middle of a difficult X" and the sentence
still makes sense, <code>にあって</code> fits.

## Contrast

### ～にあって vs ～において

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>において</code> is a formal **at / in**: it names the venue,
the field or the period neutrally. <code>にあって</code> says the speaker is
**subject to** those circumstances.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は来月(らいげつ)大阪(おおさか)<b>において</b>開(ひら)かれる。
The conference will be held in Osaka next month.
</pre>

<pre>
人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)の時代(じだい)<b>にあって</b>、人材(じんざい)の確保(かくほ)が急務(きゅうむ)となっている。
In an era of labour shortage, securing staff has become the urgent task.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence collapses: a conference is not "subject to" Osaka.
The second would survive with において but go flat, since the recession-like pressure
that motivates the main clause would no longer be marked.

### ～にあって vs ～の中(なか)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same idea, two registers. <code>中(なか)で</code> is what a
person says; <code>にあって</code> is what a person writes.

</div>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しい毎日(まいにち)の中(なか)で、好(す)きなことをする時間(じかん)をどう作(つく)るか。
How do you make time for what you love in the middle of a busy life?
</pre>

<pre>
激(はげ)しい競争(きょうそう)<b>にあって</b>、品質(ひんしつ)こそが生(い)き残(のこ)る条件(じょうけん)である。
Amid fierce competition, quality is the condition of survival.
</pre>

<code>中(なか)で</code> also happily takes concrete places and groups -
<code>クラスの中(なか)で</code>, <code>雨(あめ)の中(なか)で</code> - where
<code>にあって</code> would be strange or plain wrong.

### ～にあっても vs ～にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にもかかわらず</code> concedes a **fact**.
<code>にあっても</code> concedes a **situation you are standing in**.

</div>

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、試合(しあい)は予定(よてい)どおり行(おこな)われた。
Despite the heavy rain, the match went ahead as scheduled.
</pre>

<pre>
どのような状況(じょうきょう)<b>にあっても</b>、報道(ほうどう)の自由(じゆう)は守(まも)られねばならない。
Whatever the circumstances, freedom of the press must be protected.
</pre>

<code>にもかかわらず</code> points at one specific obstacle that failed to stop
something. <code>にあっても</code> sweeps across a whole condition and often a whole
period, which is why it pairs so naturally with どんな and どのような.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 台所(だいどころ)<b>にあって</b>料理(りょうり)をしていた。 → ✅ 台所(だいどころ)<b>で</b>料理(りょうり)をしていた。
  An ordinary location where an action happens takes で. <code>にあって</code> needs
  circumstances that press on the speaker.

- ❌ 海外(かいがい)勤務(きんむ)する<b>にあって</b>、家族(かぞく)と離(はな)れた。 → ✅ 海外(かいがい)勤務(きんむ)するに<b>あたって</b>、家族(かぞく)と離(はな)れた。
  A verb clause cannot come in front. <code>にあって</code> takes a bare noun; for a
  clause, use <code>にあたって</code> or <code>上(うえ)で</code>.

- ❌ 会社(かいしゃ)は苦(くる)しい状況(じょうきょう)<b>にあって</b>。 → ✅ 会社(かいしゃ)は苦(くる)しい状況(じょうきょう)<b>にある</b>。
  The て-form is a connective. If the sentence ends there, use the plain form.

- ❌ その立場(たちば)<b>にあっての</b>人(ひと) → ✅ その立場(たちば)<b>にある</b>人(ひと)
  To modify a following noun, go back to <code>にある</code>.

## Practice

**1.** この厳(きび)しい経済(けいざい)状況(じょうきょう)____、各社(かくしゃ)は人員(じんいん)の削減(さくげん)を進(すす)めている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この厳(きび)しい経済(けいざい)状況(じょうきょう)<b>にあって</b>

The economic situation is a condition that presses on every company, and the main
clause is the consequence of being in it. The register - a news sentence about
staffing cuts - is exactly where the pattern lives.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 開会式(かいかいしき)は市民(しみん)ホール（において・にあって）行(おこな)われます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

市民(しみん)ホール<b>において</b>行(おこな)われます。

This is a venue, not a set of circumstances weighing on anybody, so the neutral
formal marker <code>において</code> is the only option. <code>にあって</code> would
suggest the hall was some kind of adversity.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even in such a difficult position, he never complained."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そのような苦(くる)しい立場(たちば)<b>にあって</b>も、彼(かれ)は不平(ふへい)ひとつ言(い)わなかった。

"Even in" is the concessive <code>にあっても</code>, and a difficult position is
precisely the kind of noun the pattern wants. Keeping the plain past
<code>言(い)わなかった</code> matches the written register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しい職場(しょくば)にあって毎日(まいにち)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しい職場(しょくば)<b>で</b>毎日(まいにち)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べている。

Lunch at work is an everyday action in an everyday place, so the location particle
で is correct. <code>にあって</code> would frame the new workplace as a trial the
speaker is enduring, which is not what the sentence says.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 情報(じょうほう)社会(しゃかい)の中(なか)で、個人(こじん)の秘密(ひみつ)を守(まも)るのは難(むずか)しい。
(b) 情報(じょうほう)社会(しゃかい)<b>にあって</b>、個人(こじん)の秘密(ひみつ)を守(まも)るのは難(むずか)しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is ordinary careful Japanese and could be spoken aloud in a class or a meeting.
It simply locates the difficulty inside the information society.

(b) reads as written prose - an editorial, a textbook chapter opening. It also
carries a faint sense that the information society bears down on the individual,
making the difficulty a consequence of the era rather than a coincidence.

</details>

# ～折に

**Meaning** · when; on the occasion of - the courteous word for a chance that comes round, used above all in letters and polite speech

<code>折(おり)</code> (read おり) is an old noun meaning "moment, occasion, season".
As a connective it does the job of <code>とき</code>, but it is the version you use
when you are being careful with someone: inviting them, thanking them for a past
kindness, or promising to do something the next time an opportunity arises. It leans
towards occasions that **may or may not come**, which is why
<code>折(おり)があれば</code> and <code>折(おり)を見(み)て</code> are so common.

## Formation

<code>折(おり)</code> is a noun, so what comes in front is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary) | dictionary + 折(おり)に | 上京(じょうきょう)する → 上京(じょうきょう)する折(おり)に |
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + 折(おり)に | お会(あ)いした → お会(あ)いした折(おり)に |
| Noun | Noun + の + 折(おり)に | ご旅行(りょこう) → ご旅行(りょこう)の折(おり)に |
| い-adjective (letter idiom) | ～い + 折(おり) | お忙(いそが)しい折(おり)に |
| Topic-marked, invitations | ～折(おり)には | お近(ちか)くへお越(こ)しの折(おり)には |
| Looking back | その折(おり)は／その折(おり)には | その折(おり)はお世話(せわ)になりました |
| Waiting for a chance | 折(おり)を見(み)て／折(おり)があれば | 折(おり)を見(み)て話(はな)します |
| に dropped, written style | ～折(おり)、… | ご来社(らいしゃ)の折(おり)、… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading is おり, not せつ or とき. The same kanji gives the verbs
<code>折(お)る</code> "to fold, to break" and <code>折(お)れる</code>, where the
reading is お with り as okurigana. The occasion noun is
<code>折(おり)</code> with no okurigana at all.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
お時間(じかん)のある<b>折(おり)に</b>、ご覧(らん)ください。
Please have a look at it when you have a moment.
</pre>

<pre>
その<b>折(おり)は</b>、たいへんお世話(せわ)になりました。
Thank you for everything you did for me on that occasion.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)へ来(こ)られた<b>折(おり)には</b>、ぜひお寄(よ)りください。
Do drop in on us when you next come up to Tokyo.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)大阪(おおさか)へ出張(しゅっちょう)する<b>折(おり)に</b>、恩師(おんし)をお訪(たず)ねするつもりだ。
When I go to Osaka on business next week I intend to call on my old teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
帰省(きせい)の<b>折(おり)に</b>は、必(かなら)ず祖母(そぼ)の家(いえ)に顔(かお)を出(だ)すことにしている。
Whenever I go back to my home town I make a point of looking in on my grandmother.
</pre>

<pre>
またお目(め)にかかれる<b>折(おり)が</b>あれば、ゆっくりお話(はな)ししましょう。
If the chance to meet again should come, let us have a proper talk then.
</pre>

<pre>

Allow me to explain the new product when I next visit you.
</pre>

<pre>

In this bitter cold, please do take care of yourself.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>折(おり)</code> is **polite and warm**, with a slightly old-fashioned grain to
it. It belongs to letters, greeting cards, formal invitations and the kind of spoken
Japanese that comes with keigo around it - a customer visit, a speech at a farewell
party, a message to a former teacher. Said between friends it would sound as if you
were reading from a card, and in that setting <code>とき</code> is the natural word.

Two idioms are worth learning whole. <code>その折(おり)は</code> looks back at a
shared past occasion and is the standard opening for thanking someone for it. And
<code>寒(さむ)さの厳(きび)しい折(おり)</code>, along with its seasonal cousins,
is a letter formula: here <code>折(おり)</code> means "season, time of year" and the
phrase leads into a wish for the reader's health. A stiffer written variant keeps the
old attributive ending - 寒(さむ)さ厳(きび)しき折(おり) - which you should
recognise on a New Year card but need never write.

There is a tendency worth knowing: <code>折(おり)</code> prefers occasions that are
neutral or pleasant, and sits badly on disasters and emergencies. Safety notices say
<code>地震(じしん)の際(さい)には</code>, not
❌<code>地震(じしん)の折(おり)には</code>. Think of
<code>折(おり)</code> as an opportunity rather than a contingency.

## Contrast

### ～折(おり)に vs ～際(さい)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal "when". <code>際(さい)</code> is
**institutional**: manuals, notices, contracts, emergencies.
<code>折(おり)</code> is **personal**: letters, invitations, thanks.

</div>

<pre>
ご入場(にゅうじょう)の<b>際(さい)は</b>、チケットをご用意(ようい)ください。
Please have your ticket ready when entering.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)へお越(こ)しの<b>折(おり)は</b>、ご連絡(れんらく)ください。
Do get in touch when you come to Kyoto.
</pre>

A second, quieter difference: <code>際(さい)</code> insists on an event and refuses
adjectives - ❌<code>お忙(いそが)しい際(さい)に</code> - while
<code>折(おり)</code> takes a state quite happily, which is why
<code>お忙(いそが)しい折(おり)に恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが</code> is a stock
business opening.

### ～折(おり)に vs ～とき(に)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とき</code> covers every "when" there is.
<code>折(おり)</code> covers the polite ones, and only occasions - never states of
being.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの<b>とき</b>、この川(かわ)でよく泳(およ)いだ。
When I was a child I often swam in this river.
</pre>

<pre>
先日(せんじつ)お会(あ)いした<b>折(おり)に</b>、その話(はなし)を伺(うかが)いました。
I heard about that when we met the other day.
</pre>

❌<code>子(こ)どもの折(おり)</code> fails because childhood is a stretch of life,
not an occasion. And in the second sentence <code>とき</code> would be perfectly
grammatical - just less deferential.

### ～折(おり)に vs 機会(きかい)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same idea, different clothes. <code>機会(きかい)</code> is the
plain modern word for an opportunity; <code>折(おり)</code> is its courteous,
literary counterpart.

</div>

<pre>
機会(きかい)があれば、また一緒(いっしょ)に飲(の)みに行(い)きましょう。
If we get the chance, let's go for a drink again.
</pre>

<pre>
折(おり)を見(み)て、部長(ぶちょう)にお伝(つた)えしておきます。
I will find a suitable moment and pass it on to the department head.
</pre>

<code>機会(きかい)</code> is neutral about timing; <code>折(おり)</code> hints that
the moment has to be a **fitting** one, which is exactly the work
<code>折(おり)を見(み)て</code> does - waiting until the mood is right.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 火事(かじ)の<b>折(おり)</b>は、エレベーターを使(つか)わないでください。 → ✅ 火事(かじ)の<b>際(さい)</b>は、エレベーターを使(つか)わないでください。
  Emergency instructions take <code>際(さい)</code>.
  <code>折(おり)</code> frames a fire as a nice opportunity.

- ❌ 旅行(りょこう)<b>折(おり)に</b>買(か)いました。 → ✅ 旅行(りょこう)<b>の折(おり)に</b>買(か)いました。
  <code>折(おり)</code> is a noun, so another noun links to it with の.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)の<b>折(おり)</b>、アルバイトばかりしていた。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)の<b>とき</b>、アルバイトばかりしていた。
  A period of life is not an occasion. <code>折(おり)</code> needs a bounded moment.

- ❌ その折(おり)は<b>ありがとう</b>。 → ✅ その折(おり)は<b>お世話(せわ)になりました</b>。
  Not a grammar error but a register clash. If you have reached for
  <code>折(おり)</code>, the rest of the sentence has to be polite too.

# ～を経て

**Meaning** · after going through; by way of - names the years, the stages or the places that had to be passed through before the result arrived

<code>経(へ)る</code> is a る-verb meaning "to pass through, to elapse", and
<code>を経(へ)て</code> is its て-form doing connective work. The noun in front is
never a mere point in time: it is a **stretch or a sequence** that took effort,
patience or distance to get across - ten years, three rounds of screening, a change
of trains at Nagoya. The main clause is what came out the other side.

## Formation

A noun goes straight in front with を. There is no clause form; a verb has to be
nominalised first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Span of time | Noun + を経(へ)て | 十年(じゅうねん) → 十年(じゅうねん)を経(へ)て |
| Process, procedure | Noun + を経(へ)て | 審査(しんさ) → 審査(しんさ)を経(へ)て |
| Place on a route | Noun + を経(へ)て | 名古屋(なごや) → 名古屋(なごや)を経(へ)て |
| Nominalised clause | ～こと + を経(へ)て | 議論(ぎろん)を重(かさ)ねること → …を経(へ)て |
| Modifying a noun | ～を経(へ)た + Noun | 長(なが)い議論(ぎろん)を経(へ)た結論(けつろん) |
| As the main verb | ～を経(へ)る／経(へ)ている | 三年(さんねん)を経(へ)ている |
| Negative (stiff, written) | ～を経(へ)ずに | 正式(せいしき)な手続(てつづ)きを経(へ)ずに |

<div class="note">

**Note** - two different verbs share the kanji.
<code>経(へ)る</code> is transitive-ish and takes を: 三年(さんねん)を経(へ)て.
<code>経(た)つ</code> "time passes" takes が: 三年(さんねん)が経(た)つ.
❌<code>三年(さんねん)が経(へ)て</code> mixes the two and is the standard error.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
三年(さんねん)の準備(じゅんび)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、ようやく店(みせ)をオープンした。
After three years of preparation we finally opened the shop.
</pre>

<pre>

A conclusion was reached after long discussions.
</pre>

<pre>
この列車(れっしゃ)は名古屋(なごや)<b>を経(へ)て</b>大阪(おおさか)へ向(む)かう。
This train runs to Osaka by way of Nagoya.
</pre>

<pre>
厳(きび)しい審査(しんさ)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、作品(さくひん)が展示(てんじ)されることになった。
Having come through a strict selection process, the work is to be exhibited.
</pre>

<pre>
数社(すうしゃ)での勤務(きんむ)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、彼(かれ)は独立(どくりつ)した。
After stints at several companies, he set up on his own.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)の歳月(さいげつ)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、大聖堂(だいせいどう)の修復(しゅうふく)が完了(かんりょう)した。
Ten years on, the restoration of the cathedral was complete.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)もの試作(しさく)と失敗(しっぱい)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、ようやく製品(せいひん)の形(かたち)になった。
After any number of prototypes and failures, it finally took shape as a product.
</pre>

<pre>
国会(こっかい)の審議(しんぎ)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、この法案(ほうあん)は来年(らいねん)四月(しがつ)に施行(しこう)される見通(みとお)しだ。
Once it has been through the Diet, the bill is expected to take effect next April.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Firmly **written and formal**. Its natural habitats are news copy, company histories,
museum captions, academic prose and the career summary in a profile - "after posts at
A and B, joined C in 2019". In speech it appears in presentations and formal
introductions; between friends you would say
<code>三年(さんねん)かかって</code> or <code>そのあとで</code>.

The pattern carries a quiet sense of **due process or hard-won time**. That is why
<code>審査(しんさ)を経(へ)て</code>, <code>議論(ぎろん)を経(へ)て</code>
and <code>歳月(さいげつ)を経(へ)て</code> sound so natural: each names something
you cannot skip. Put a short, effortless event in the slot and the sentence goes
strange - ❌<code>昼食(ちゅうしょく)を経(へ)て</code> makes lunch sound like an
ordeal.

The route sense is a separate flavour of the same idea and is common in transport and
travel writing: <code>ロンドン経由(けいゆ)</code> for the ticket,
<code>ロンドンを経(へ)て</code> for the narrative. Both are formal; only
<code>経由(けいゆ)</code> appears on a departure board.

## Contrast

### ～を経(へ)て vs ～てから／～のあとで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>てから</code> and <code>のあとで</code> report **sequence**.
<code>を経(へ)て</code> reports that something was **gone through**.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わっ<b>てから</b>、昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べた。
We had lunch after the meeting finished.
</pre>

<pre>
二年(にねん)にわたる交渉(こうしょう)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、両社(りょうしゃ)は合意(ごうい)に達(たっ)した。
After two years of negotiation the two companies reached agreement.
</pre>

The first is just what happened next; nobody had to endure the meeting for lunch to
occur. The second says the agreement exists **because** the two years were survived.
Note also the grammar: <code>てから</code> takes a verb,
<code>を経(へ)て</code> takes a noun.

### ～を経(へ)て vs ～を通(つう)じて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を通(つう)じて</code> is the **medium** something happens
through. <code>を経(へ)て</code> is a **stage** on the way that had to be crossed.

</div>

<pre>
通訳(つうやく)<b>を通(つう)じて</b>、相手(あいて)の考(かんが)えを確(たし)かめた。
I confirmed what they thought through an interpreter.
</pre>

<pre>
予選(よせん)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、決勝(けっしょう)に進(すす)む六(ろく)チームが出(で)そろった。
Coming through the qualifiers, the six finalist teams are now settled.
</pre>

An interpreter is not a hurdle to be cleared, and a qualifying round is not a
channel of communication. Where both are possible - an intermediary organisation,
say - <code>を通(つう)じて</code> frames it as the route the message took and
<code>を経(へ)て</code> frames it as a step in a procedure.

### ～を経(へ)て vs ～を経由(けいゆ)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for **physical routes**, <code>経由(けいゆ)</code> is the
everyday word and <code>を経(へ)て</code> the literary one. For **processes and
years**, only <code>を経(へ)て</code> works.

</div>

<pre>
ソウル<b>経由(けいゆ)</b>のほうが運賃(うんちん)が安(やす)い。
Going via Seoul is cheaper.
</pre>

<pre>
一行(いっこう)は険(けわ)しい山道(やまみち)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、ようやく村(むら)にたどり着(つ)いた。
The party came at last to the village by way of a steep mountain path.
</pre>

You cannot say ❌<code>審査(しんさ)経由(けいゆ)</code>:
<code>経由(けいゆ)</code> is about geography and routing, not about procedure.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 五年(ごねん)が<b>経(へ)て</b>、町(まち)はすっかり変(か)わった。 → ✅ 五年(ごねん)<b>を経(へ)て</b>、町(まち)はすっかり変(か)わった。（or 五年(ごねん)が経(た)って）
  <code>経(へ)る</code> takes を; the が verb is <code>経(た)つ</code>. Same kanji,
  different verbs, and mixing them is the commonest error with this pattern.

- ❌ 昼食(ちゅうしょく)<b>を経(へ)て</b>会議(かいぎ)を再開(さいかい)した。 → ✅ 昼食(ちゅうしょく)<b>のあとで</b>会議(かいぎ)を再開(さいかい)した。
  Lunch is an interval, not something you come through. The slot wants a process or a
  long stretch.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)を<b>経(へ)る後(あと)で</b>、結果(けっか)が出(で)る。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)を<b>経(へ)て</b>、結果(けっか)が出(で)る。
  The て-form already does the connecting; adding
  <code>後(あと)で</code> is redundant and ungrammatical here.

- ❌ きのう映画(えいが)<b>を経(へ)て</b>帰(かえ)った。 → ✅ きのう映画(えいが)を見(み)てから帰(かえ)った。
  Beyond the meaning problem, the register is wrong: casual weekend plans do not take
  a formal written connective.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the screening as a required stage that the application went through, and
the payment as its proper outcome. It is the wording of an official document.

(b) merely puts the two events in order. It would still be true if the grant had been
paid for some unrelated reason after the screening happened to finish, and it sounds
like a person describing events rather than an institution describing procedure.

</details>

# ～に至るまで

**Meaning** · all the way to; right down to; right up until - marks the far end of a range and insists that the range really does reach that far

<code>至(いた)る</code> is a formal verb meaning "to reach, to arrive at", so
<code>に至(いた)るまで</code> is literally "as far as the point where it reaches X".
Plain <code>まで</code> would already give the endpoint; what this adds is the
speaker's astonishment or emphasis - the list goes on further than you would expect,
or the state has held for longer than you would think. It very often answers a から
earlier in the sentence.

## Formation

A noun goes in front. <code>至(いた)る</code> is a う-verb (五段(ごだん)), so the form here
is the plain <code>至(いた)る</code> plus <code>まで</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (thing, category) | Noun + に至(いた)るまで | 靴(くつ)の色(いろ) → 靴(くつ)の色(いろ)に至(いた)るまで |
| Paired with から | ～から～に至(いた)るまで | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもからお年寄(としよ)りに至(いた)るまで |
| Noun (point in time) | Time + に至(いた)るまで | 今日(こんにち) → 今日(こんにち)に至(いた)るまで |
| Verb clause (written) | dictionary + に至(いた)るまで | 完成(かんせい)する → 完成(かんせい)するに至(いた)るまで |
| Modifying a noun | ～に至(いた)るまでの + Noun | 現在(げんざい)に至(いた)るまでの歩(あゆ)み |
| Related pattern (reaching) | ～に至(いた)る／～に至(いた)って | 解決(かいけつ)に至(いた)る |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in <code>今日(こんにち)に至(いた)るまで</code> the word is read
**こんにち**, "the present day", not きょう. きょう would mean this particular
Tuesday, which is not what a sentence spanning a company's history is about.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
靴(くつ)から時計(とけい)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>、すべて黒(くろ)で統一(とういつ)されていた。
Everything, from the shoes right down to the watch, was matched in black.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもからお年寄(としよ)り<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>、町(まち)の誰(だれ)もが祭(まつ)りに加(くわ)わる。
Everyone in the town, from children to the very old, joins in the festival.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)から今日(こんにち)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>、味(あじ)は一度(いちど)も変(か)えていない。
From its founding right up to the present day, the recipe has never once been changed.
</pre>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)は机(つくえ)の上(うえ)のペンの向(む)き<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>指示(しじ)を出(だ)す。
The president gives instructions on everything down to which way the pens face on the desk.
</pre>

<pre>
開演(かいえん)の時刻(じこく)から客席(きゃくせき)の照明(しょうめい)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>、すべて演出家(えんしゅつか)が決(き)めている。
From the curtain-up time to the lighting over the seats, the director decides all of it.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の調査(ちょうさ)では、整備(せいび)不足(ぶそく)から運転手(うんてんしゅ)の勤務(きんむ)時間(じかん)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>細(こま)かく調(しら)べられた。
The inquiry examined everything from poor maintenance to the driver's working hours.
</pre>

<pre>
新入(しんにゅう)社員(しゃいん)の研修(けんしゅう)では、名刺(めいし)の渡(わた)し方(かた)からエレベーターの乗(の)り方(かた)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>教(おそ)わる。
New-employee training covers everything from how to hand over a business card to how to get into a lift.
</pre>

<pre>
戦後(せんご)の復興(ふっこう)から経済(けいざい)成長(せいちょう)、そして現在(げんざい)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>の歩(あゆ)みが写真(しゃしん)で紹介(しょうかい)されている。
The journey from postwar reconstruction through the boom years to the present is told in photographs.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal, and much more common in **writing** than in speech: reports, inquiries,
museum panels, company profiles, feature journalism. In conversation the same
emphasis comes out as plain <code>まで</code> with the intonation doing the work, or
as <code>～まで全部(ぜんぶ)</code>.

Two flavours share the form. The **exhaustive** one runs across a set of things and
says the set is complete to its farthest, smallest or most unexpected member; it
loves the company of すべて, 全(すべ)て, 細(こま)かく, どれも. The **temporal**
one runs from a starting point to now and says the state has held that whole time;
<code>今日(こんにち)に至(いた)るまで</code> and
<code>現在(げんざい)に至(いた)るまで</code> are close to fixed expressions.

There is a rhetorical edge to the exhaustive use. Picking a trivial endpoint - the
angle of the pens, the colour of the socks - is a way of showing that the control was
excessive or the attention obsessive. Used of a person's supervision it can be quietly
critical, so choose the endpoint with care.

## Contrast

### ～に至(いた)るまで vs ～まで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>まで</code> states the endpoint.
<code>に至(いた)るまで</code> states the endpoint **and marks it as remarkable**.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)<b>まで</b>歩(ある)いて十分(じゅっぷん)かかります。
It is a ten-minute walk to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)は髪(かみ)の長(なが)さ<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>細(こま)かく決(き)められている。
The rules are laid down in detail, down to the length of your hair.
</pre>

The first sentence has no rhetoric in it and
<code>に至(いた)るまで</code> would be absurd there - an ordinary destination of an
ordinary walk. The test: if you could insert "even" or "right down to" in the English
without changing the meaning, the longer form is available.

### ～に至(いた)るまで vs ～から～にかけて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にかけて</code> spreads something **loosely across** a range
of time or space. <code>に至(いた)るまで</code> runs to a **precise far edge** and
claims everything up to it is included.

</div>

<pre>
夕方(ゆうがた)から夜(よる)<b>にかけて</b>、強(つよ)い雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るでしょう。
Heavy rain is expected from evening into the night.
</pre>

<pre>
昭和(しょうわ)の初(はじ)めから戦後(せんご)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>、この工場(こうじょう)は動(うご)き続(つづ)けた。
From the early Showa years right through to the postwar period, this factory kept running.
</pre>

<code>にかけて</code> is vague on purpose - the rain will not start at a stated
minute. <code>に至(いた)るまで</code> is the opposite: it fixes the far end and
asserts continuity all the way there.

### ～に至(いた)るまで vs ～に至(いた)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に至(いた)るまで</code> is about a **range**.
<code>に至(いた)って</code> is about a **threshold finally crossed**, usually a bad
one, and usually with the sense "only when it got this far".

</div>

<pre>
退院(たいいん)から今日(こんにち)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>、薬(くすり)は飲(の)み続(つづ)けている。
From leaving hospital up to today, I have kept taking the medicine.
</pre>

<pre>
死者(ししゃ)が出(で)る<b>に至(いた)って</b>、会社(かいしゃ)はようやく操業(そうぎょう)を止(と)めた。
Only when there were deaths did the company finally halt operations.
</pre>

Same verb, opposite jobs. If a まで is present you are looking at the range use; if
the sentence has ようやく, はじめて or a note of blame, it is the threshold use.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 会社(かいしゃ)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>自転車(じてんしゃ)で行(い)く。 → ✅ 会社(かいしゃ)<b>まで</b>自転車(じてんしゃ)で行(い)く。
  An ordinary destination takes plain まで. The pattern needs a range whose far end
  is worth remarking on.

- ❌ 五時(ごじ)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>待(ま)った。 → ✅ 五時(ごじ)<b>まで</b>待(ま)った。
  Same problem with clock time. A stretch that runs to the present -
  <code>今日(こんにち)に至(いた)るまで</code> - is fine, because the point is how
  long it has lasted.

- ❌ 創業(そうぎょう)から今日(きょう)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b> → ✅ 創業(そうぎょう)から今日(こんにち)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>
  Not a grammar error but a reading error, and a common one. In this phrase 今日(こんにち) is
  read こんにち, "the present day".

- ❌ 細部(さいぶ)<b>を至(いた)るまで</b>作(つく)り込(こ)まれている。 → ✅ 細部(さいぶ)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>作(つく)り込(こ)まれている。
  <code>至(いた)る</code> is a verb of arrival, so the point reached is marked with
  に, never を.

## Practice

**1.** この美術館(びじゅつかん)では、絵(え)の並(なら)べ方(かた)から壁(かべ)の色(いろ)____、すべて作家(さっか)自身(じしん)が決(き)めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

壁(かべ)の色(いろ)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>

から earlier in the sentence sets up the near end of the range, and the wall colour is
the unexpectedly small far end - the classic exhaustive use. すべて confirms it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来週(らいしゅう)の月曜日(げつようび)（まで・に至(いた)るまで）に返事(へんじ)をください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A deadline is an ordinary endpoint with nothing remarkable about it, so plain までに

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He remembers everything, from the names of the guests down to what they ate."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"From … down to" is the から … <code>に至(いた)るまで</code> frame, and a detail as

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)は朝(あさ)から夜(よる)に至(いた)るまで中止(ちゅうし)になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)は朝(あさ)から夜(よる)<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>続(つづ)いた。

The frame promises a range that is filled all the way to its far end, so the main
clause has to describe something that continued or was covered. A one-off
cancellation cannot fill a range; with 中止(ちゅうし) you would simply say
<code>会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になった</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)は書類(しょるい)の字(じ)の大(おお)きさ<b>まで</b>注意(ちゅうい)する。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)は書類(しょるい)の字(じ)の大(おお)きさ<b>に至(いた)るまで</b>注意(ちゅうい)する。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) already says the boss goes as far as font size, and in speech that is enough; the
surprise is carried by まで and by how you say it.

(b) is heavier and more written. It presents the font size as the outermost item in a
long list of things he checks, and the extra weight tips the sentence towards
criticism - this is a man who cannot leave anything alone.

</details>

# ～を機に

**Meaning** · taking X as the opportunity to; now that X has happened - names the event a person decided to use as the moment for a change

<code>機(き)</code> is the noun "opportunity, opening, the right moment". A sentence
built on <code>を機(き)に</code> has two halves and a decision between them: the
event in front, and the deliberate change behind it. Marriage, a move, retirement, a
company's anniversary - the event does not force anything, it simply supplies a
convenient line to start from.

## Formation

A noun goes in front with を. A verb clause has to be nominalised with の or こと
first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Event noun | Noun + を機(き)に | 結婚(けっこん) → 結婚(けっこん)を機(き)に |
| Emphatic | Noun + を機(き)にして | 退職(たいしょく) → 退職(たいしょく)を機(き)にして |
| Formal written twin | Noun + を契機(けいき)に／として | 受賞(じゅしょう) → 受賞(じゅしょう)を契機(けいき)として |
| Verb clause | plain past + の／こと + を機(き)に | 転勤(てんきん)した → 転勤(てんきん)したのを機(き)に |
| Pointing at the present occasion | これを機(き)に | これを機(き)に禁煙(きんえん)する |
| Set greeting | これを機(き)に今後(こんご)とも… | これを機(き)に今後(こんご)ともよろしく |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the second half must be something **somebody chose to do**:
始(はじ)める, やめる, 見直(みなお)す, 引(ひ)っ越(こ)す, 改(あらた)める.
If the second half is an accident or a physical result, the pattern is wrong and で
or によって is what you want.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
結婚(けっこん)<b>を機(き)に</b>、たばこをやめた。
I gave up smoking when I got married.
</pre>

<pre>
入学(にゅうがく)<b>を機(き)に</b>、一人暮(ひとりぐ)らしを始(はじ)めた。
Starting university was my chance to begin living alone.
</pre>

<pre>
定年(ていねん)<b>を機(き)に</b>、夫婦(ふうふ)で田舎(いなか)に移(うつ)り住(す)んだ。
On retiring, the couple moved out to the countryside.
</pre>

<pre>
転勤(てんきん)したの<b>を機(き)に</b>、車(くるま)を手放(てばな)すことにした。
Since I was being transferred anyway, I decided to get rid of the car.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きな病気(びょうき)<b>を機(き)に</b>、食事(しょくじ)の内容(ないよう)を見直(みなお)した。
A serious illness was what prompted me to rethink what I eat.
</pre>

<pre>
創立(そうりつ)百(ひゃく)周年(しゅうねん)<b>を機(き)に</b>、社名(しゃめい)を改(あらた)めることになった。
Marking its hundredth year, the company is to change its name.
</pre>

<pre>
これ<b>を機(き)に</b>、部署(ぶしょ)の仕事(しごと)の進(すす)め方(かた)を一度(いちど)整理(せいり)してはどうでしょうか。
Why not take this as our chance to sort out how the team works?
</pre>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)赴任(ふにん)<b>を契機(けいき)として</b>、会社(かいしゃ)は語学(ごがく)研修(けんしゅう)の制度(せいど)を整(ととの)えた。
The posting abroad was the occasion for the company to put language training on a proper footing.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>を機(き)に</code> is **formal but ordinary** - noticeably more careful than
everyday speech, and completely at home in a polite conversation, a self-introduction,
a New Year card, a company notice or a magazine profile. Learners often assume it is
stiff written grammar; in fact a colleague announcing a life change will use it
happily, because it is the compact way of saying "I used the occasion to…".

The event in front is usually **positive or at least neutral**, and it is very often a
milestone: 結婚(けっこん), 出産(しゅっさん), 就職(しゅうしょく),
定年(ていねん), 創立(そうりつ)記念(きねん). Illness and injury do appear -
<code>入院(にゅういん)を機(き)に生活(せいかつ)を改(あらた)めた</code> - but
notice what makes them work: the speaker turned the bad event into a starting point.
That is the constant. The event is treated as an **opening the speaker took**, never
as a blow that landed on them.

<code>契機(けいき)</code> is the same word in a stiffer suit. It belongs to editorial
and academic writing and can describe impersonal social change -
<code>震災(しんさい)を契機(けいき)として防災(ぼうさい)の意識(いしき)が高(たか)まった</code>
- where <code>を機(き)に</code>, with its sense of somebody choosing, would be
slightly odd.

## Contrast

### ～を機(き)に vs ～をきっかけに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>きっかけ</code> is a **trigger**, often accidental, and it
may set off something the person never intended. <code>機(き)</code> is an
**opportunity**, and somebody deliberately took it.

</div>

<pre>
友人(ゆうじん)の一言(ひとこと)<b>をきっかけに</b>、絵(え)を描(か)き始(はじ)めた。
A remark from a friend was what started me painting.
</pre>

<pre>
退職(たいしょく)<b>を機(き)に</b>、長年(ながねん)の夢(ゆめ)だった絵(え)を習(なら)い始(はじ)めた。
Retiring gave me the chance to take up the painting lessons I had always wanted.
</pre>

The stray remark was nobody's plan; the retirement was a known date the speaker built
on. This is also why <code>きっかけ</code> can head a sentence about something that
simply happened to somebody - a fear that took hold, a habit that crept in - while
<code>機(き)に</code> always implies an act of will.

### ～を機(き)に vs ～を契機(けいき)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different register.
<code>契機(けいき)</code> is the written, analytical one, and it can take an
impersonal subject.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)し<b>を機(き)に</b>、家具(かぐ)をすべて買(か)い替(か)えた。
We replaced all the furniture when we moved.
</pre>

<pre>
この事件(じけん)<b>を契機(けいき)として</b>、制度(せいど)の見直(みなお)しが始(はじ)まった。
The incident became the occasion for a review of the system.
</pre>

Put <code>契機(けいき)</code> into the furniture sentence and it sounds like a
government white paper about a sofa. Put <code>機(き)に</code> into the second and
the sentence quietly claims that someone chose to use the incident, which a report
would avoid saying.

### ～を機(き)に vs ～に際(さい)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に際(さい)して</code> says you do the thing **appropriate
to** the occasion. <code>を機(き)に</code> says you use the occasion to change
something **else**.

</div>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)<b>に際(さい)して</b>、近所(きんじょ)の方(かた)に挨拶(あいさつ)に回(まわ)った。
On opening the shop, we went round to greet the neighbours.
</pre>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)<b>を機(き)に</b>、朝(あさ)型(がた)の生活(せいかつ)に変(か)えた。
Opening the shop was my chance to switch to getting up early.
</pre>

Greeting the neighbours is part of opening a shop; changing your sleeping hours is
not. That is the whole difference, and it decides which pattern a sentence needs.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 事故(じこ)<b>を機(き)に</b>足(あし)を骨折(こっせつ)した。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)<b>で</b>足(あし)を骨折(こっせつ)した。
  A broken leg is not a decision. The second half has to be something the person
  chose to do.

- ❌ 引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>を機(き)に</b>、荷物(にもつ)を減(へ)らした。 → ✅ 引(ひ)っ越(こ)したの<b>を機(き)に</b>、荷物(にもつ)を減(へ)らした。
  <code>機(き)</code> is a noun, so a verb clause needs の or こと, and the past
  form is normal because the event has happened.

- ❌ 毎朝(まいあさ)のジョギング<b>を機(き)に</b>、体(からだ)が軽(かる)くなった。 → ✅ ジョギングを始(はじ)めてから、体(からだ)が軽(かる)くなった。
  A daily habit is not an occasion. The slot wants a one-off event with a date on it.

- ❌ 来月(らいげつ)結婚(けっこん)する<b>を機(き)に</b>引(ひ)っ越(こ)します。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)の結婚(けっこん)<b>を機(き)に</b>引(ひ)っ越(こ)します。
  Future events are fine, but the grammar is not: use the event noun, or
  <code>結婚(けっこん)するのを機(き)に</code>.

A milestone in front, a deliberate decision behind it - reviewing the insurance is

main clause is a decision, which is what the pattern requires.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 台風(たいふう)を機(き)に、屋根(やね)のかわらが飛(と)んでしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 台風(たいふう)<b>で</b>、屋根(やね)のかわらが飛(と)んでしまった。

Tiles blowing off is damage, not a choice, so the sentence needs the plain cause
marker で. A correct <code>を機(き)に</code> version would have to continue with
something the owner decided, such as
<code>屋根(やね)を直(なお)すことにした</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 入院(にゅういん)<b>をきっかけに</b>、酒(さけ)を飲(の)まなくなった。
(b) 入院(にゅういん)<b>を機(き)に</b>、酒(さけ)をやめた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a change that settled in after the hospital stay. なくなった leaves it
open whether the speaker resolved on it or simply lost the habit.

(b) has the speaker making a decision and using the hospital stay as the line to draw
it at. やめた is deliberate, and the sentence sounds like something said with a
certain amount of pride.

</details>

# ～を皮切りに

**Meaning** · starting with X - X was the first of a run, and the run kept going and usually kept growing

<code>皮切(かわき)り</code> is the noun "the first of a series". The pattern names
that first item and promises the rest: a tour that begins in one city and takes in
twenty, a shop that opens in one station and multiplies, a single remark that lets
loose a flood of them. The main clause almost always says how far the series then
spread.

## Formation

A noun goes in front with を; a verb clause is nominalised with の or こと first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Event noun | Noun + を皮切(かわき)りに | 大阪(おおさか)公演(こうえん) → 大阪(おおさか)公演(こうえん)を皮切(かわき)りに |
| Place noun | Noun + を皮切(かわき)りに | 札幌(さっぽろ) → 札幌(さっぽろ)を皮切(かわき)りに |
| Emphatic | ～を皮切(かわき)りにして | 受賞(じゅしょう)を皮切(かわき)りにして |
| Formal written | ～を皮切(かわき)りとして | 初日(しょにち)を皮切(かわき)りとして |
| Verb clause | plain past + の／こと + を皮切(かわき)りに | 発言(はつげん)したのを皮切(かわき)りに |
| Typical main clause | 次々(つぎつぎ)と／相次(あいつ)いで／各地(かくち)で | 次々(つぎつぎ)と開店(かいてん)した |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading is かわきり: 皮(かわ) plus 切(き)り. It is traditionally
explained as coming from the first burn in a course of moxa treatment
（灸(きゅう)）, the one that stung as though it cut the skin - hence "the painful
first of many".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
大阪(おおさか)での公演(こうえん)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、全国(ぜんこく)を回(まわ)る。
Starting with the Osaka concert, they will tour the whole country.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)の一号店(いちごうてん)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、店(みせ)は次々(つぎつぎ)と増(ふ)えていった。
Starting with the first branch by the station, the shops multiplied one after another.
</pre>

<pre>
札幌(さっぽろ)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、新製品(しんせいひん)の販売(はんばい)が全国(ぜんこく)へ広(ひろ)がる。
Beginning in Sapporo, sales of the new product will spread nationwide.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の受賞(じゅしょう)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、彼女(かのじょ)の作品(さくひん)は海外(かいがい)でも注目(ちゅうもく)されるようになった。
Beginning with last year's prize, her work has drawn attention abroad as well.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)の社員(しゃいん)の提案(ていあん)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、職場(しょくば)の改善(かいぜん)案(あん)が次々(つぎつぎ)と出(で)てきた。
One employee's suggestion opened the way, and ideas for improving the workplace came thick and fast.
</pre>

<pre>
七月(しちがつ)の花火(はなび)大会(たいかい)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、この町(まち)では夏(なつ)の行事(ぎょうじ)が毎週(まいしゅう)続(つづ)く。
Starting with the fireworks in July, this town has a summer event every week.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)の閉鎖(へいさ)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、この地域(ちいき)では人口(じんこう)の流出(りゅうしゅつ)が止(と)まらなくなった。
The closure of the plant was the beginning, and the drain of people from the area has not stopped since.
</pre>

<pre>
初日(しょにち)の記者(きしゃ)会見(かいけん)<b>を皮切(かわき)りとして</b>、監督(かんとく)は一(いっ)か月(げつ)にわたり各地(かくち)のイベントに出席(しゅっせき)した。
Beginning with the opening-day press conference, the director spent a month at events around the country.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This belongs to **announcements and news copy**: tour schedules, product rollouts,
festival programmes, corporate histories, sports write-ups. It is not casual speech.
A friend telling you about their weekend says
<code>最初(さいしょ)に渋谷(しぶや)に行(い)って</code>, not
<code>渋谷(しぶや)を皮切(かわき)りに</code>, and the formal version would sound
like a parody of a press release.

The pattern has one hard requirement: **a series must follow**. One first event and
one second event is not enough; the main clause has to show a run, an expansion or a
spread, which is why 次々(つぎつぎ)と, 相次(あいつ)いで, 各地(かくち)で and
全国(ぜんこく)に are its constant companions. If nothing follows, the sentence
sounds as though it has been cut off.

The tendency is towards **momentum**, and most often that momentum is welcome - tours,
openings, awards. It is not restricted to good news, though: journalism uses it
readily for a spreading strike, a wave of protests, a run of closures. What it will
not do is describe a small routine first step. A first cup of coffee is not the
opening of a series in the sense this word means.

## Contrast

### ～を皮切(かわき)りに vs ～をはじめ(として)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>をはじめ</code> picks a **representative** member of a group
that already exists. <code>を皮切(かわき)りに</code> picks the **first** of a series
that then unfolds in time.

</div>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)<b>をはじめ</b>、多(おお)くの社員(しゃいん)が式(しき)に出席(しゅっせき)した。
Many staff attended the ceremony, the president among them.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)公演(こうえん)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、三(さん)か月(げつ)の旅(たび)が始(はじ)まる。
Starting with the Tokyo show, a three-month tour gets under way.
</pre>

The president is not the first attendee to arrive; he is the most important name on
the list. Swap the patterns and the first sentence claims the staff turned up in
sequence behind him, which is not what anyone means.

### ～を皮切(かわき)りに vs ～を機(き)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を機(き)に</code> leads to **one change**, usually in the
speaker's own life. <code>を皮切(かわき)りに</code> leads to **a series** of similar
events.

</div>

<pre>
結婚(けっこん)<b>を機(き)に</b>、郊外(こうがい)に家(いえ)を借(か)りた。
Getting married, we rented a place out in the suburbs.
</pre>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)での開店(かいてん)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、店舗(てんぽ)は三年(さんねん)で全国(ぜんこく)に広(ひろ)がった。
Beginning with the branch in Hokkaido, the chain spread nationwide within three years.
</pre>

The married couple rent one house, once. The chain keeps opening shops. If you cannot
name the second, third and tenth members of the series,
<code>を皮切(かわき)りに</code> is the wrong choice.

### ～を皮切(かわき)りに vs ～から始(はじ)まって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same idea, ordinary clothes.
<code>から始(はじ)まって</code> is what you can say out loud without sounding like a
news bulletin.

</div>

<pre>
自己(じこ)紹介(しょうかい)から始(はじ)まって、最後(さいご)はみんなで歌(うた)を歌(うた)った。
It started with self-introductions and ended with everyone singing.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の福岡(ふくおか)大会(たいかい)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、全国(ぜんこく)六(ろっ)か所(しょ)で予選(よせん)が行(おこな)われる。
Beginning with next month's Fukuoka meet, qualifiers will be held at six venues nationwide.
</pre>

The first sentence is a person describing an evening; the second is a schedule being
announced. Both name a first item, but only one of them is written to be printed.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 朝(あさ)のコーヒー<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、仕事(しごと)を始(はじ)める。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)はコーヒーを飲(の)んでから仕事(しごと)を始(はじ)める。
  A daily routine has no series in it and no expansion. The pattern needs a run of
  comparable events.

- ❌ 開会式(かいかいしき)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、大会(たいかい)は終(お)わった。 → ✅ 開会式(かいかいしき)を皮切(かわき)りに、熱(あつ)い戦(たたか)いが一週間(いっしゅうかん)続(つづ)いた。
  The main clause has to open outwards, not shut the door. Ending immediately
  contradicts the pattern.

- ❌ 発表(はっぴょう)する<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、問(と)い合(あ)わせが増(ふ)えた。 → ✅ 発表(はっぴょう)したの<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、問(と)い合(あ)わせが増(ふ)えた。
  <code>皮切(かわき)り</code> is a noun; a verb clause needs の or こと in between.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、パン屋(や)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>スーパーにも行(い)った。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)はまずパン屋(や)に行(い)って、スーパーにも寄(よ)った。
  Two errands are not a series, and an errand is not news. Both the meaning and the
  register are wrong here.

# ～に先駆けて

**Meaning** · ahead of everyone else; in advance of - does something before the rivals do, or before the main event opens to everybody

<code>先駆(さきが)ける</code> means "to go on ahead, to lead the way". The pattern has
two faces that come from the same idea. With **people or organisations** in front of
it, it says you got there first: before the other companies, before the rest of the
country, before the world. With an **event** in front of it, it says something was
done in advance of that event, usually for a limited group - a press preview, a
pre-launch trial.

## Formation

A noun goes in front with に. There is no clause form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Rivals, others | Noun (people, bodies) + に先駆(さきが)けて | 他社(たしゃ) → 他社(たしゃ)に先駆(さきが)けて |
| The world, the country | Noun (group) + に先駆(さきが)けて | 世界(せかい) → 世界(せかい)に先駆(さきが)けて |
| Coming event | Noun (event) + に先駆(さきが)けて | 発売(はつばい) → 発売(はつばい)に先駆(さきが)けて |
| Modifying a noun | ～に先駆(さきが)けた + Noun | 時代(じだい)に先駆(さきが)けた試(こころ)み |
| As the main verb | ～に先駆(さきが)ける | 他社(たしゃ)に先駆(さきが)ける |
| Noun form | ～の先駆(さきが)け | この分野(ぶんや)の先駆(さきが)け |
| Stiffer written twin | ～に先(さき)んじて | 各国(かっこく)に先(さき)んじて |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading here is **さきがけて**, the native verb. The same two kanji are
read せんく in <code>先駆者(せんくしゃ)</code> "pioneer" and
<code>先駆的(せんくてき)</code>. Reading it ❌せんくけて is a common slip.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
他社(たしゃ)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>、新型(しんがた)のバッテリーを発表(はっぴょう)した。
They announced a new type of battery ahead of the other manufacturers.
</pre>

<pre>
発売(はつばい)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>、一部(いちぶ)の店(みせ)で試(ため)すことができる。
Ahead of the launch, you can try it at selected shops.
</pre>

<pre>
この大学(だいがく)は全国(ぜんこく)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>秋(あき)入学(にゅうがく)を取(と)り入(い)れた。
This university introduced autumn entry before any other in the country.
</pre>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>、近所(きんじょ)の方(かた)を招(まね)いた内覧会(ないらんかい)が開(ひら)かれた。
Before the shop opened, a preview was held for people from the neighbourhood.
</pre>

<pre>
世界(せかい)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>この技術(ぎじゅつ)を実用(じつよう)化(か)したのは、小(ちい)さな町工場(まちこうば)だった。
The first place in the world to put this technology to practical use was a small backstreet factory.
</pre>

<pre>
首都圏(しゅとけん)での運行(うんこう)開始(かいし)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>、地方(ちほう)の路線(ろせん)で試験(しけん)走行(そうこう)が続(つづ)いている。
Ahead of the start of services around the capital, test runs continue on regional lines.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の研究(けんきゅう)は時代(じだい)<b>に先駆(さきが)けた</b>ものとして、のちに高(たか)く評価(ひょうか)された。
His research was later recognised as work that had been ahead of its time.
</pre>

<pre>
正式(せいしき)な発表(はっぴょう)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>、社内(しゃない)向(む)けの説明会(せつめいかい)が開(ひら)かれることになっている。
In advance of the formal announcement, a briefing is to be held for staff.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **press-release and news Japanese**. Product announcements, corporate
histories, city council statements, museum publicity, and any writing that wants to
claim a first. It carries a note of **prestige**: saying
<code>世界(せかい)に先駆(さきが)けて</code> about your own product is a boast,
and it is meant to be.

In the "before an event" use the prestige softens into privilege - the people at the
preview are getting in early, ahead of the general public. That flavour is worth
keeping in mind, because it is what separates this pattern from a neutral "before".
Ordinary preparation before a meeting is not "getting in ahead of" anybody, and takes
<code>に先立(さきだ)って</code> or plain <code>前(まえ)に</code>.

Spoken use is limited to formal settings - a presentation, a company announcement, a
guided tour. Among friends the idea comes out as
<code>人(ひと)より先(さき)に</code> or <code>いちばん早(はや)く</code>.

## Contrast

### ～に先駆(さきが)けて vs ～に先立(さきだ)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に先立(さきだ)って</code> is a neutral, formal **before**,
usually about preparation. <code>に先駆(さきが)けて</code> adds that you are
**ahead of others** and that this is worth noting.

</div>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)<b>に先立(さきだ)って</b>、住民(じゅうみん)への説明会(せつめいかい)が開(ひら)かれた。
Prior to the works, a briefing was held for local residents.
</pre>

<pre>
大手(おおて)各社(かくしゃ)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>、この会社(かいしゃ)は週(しゅう)休(きゅう)三日(みっか)制(せい)を導入(どうにゅう)した。
Ahead of the big firms, this company brought in a four-day week.
</pre>

The residents' briefing beats nobody; it is simply a step that came first.
<code>に先立(さきだ)って</code> is by far the more common of the two in ordinary
formal writing, and it is the safer choice whenever no competition is involved.

### ～に先駆(さきが)けて vs ～より前(まえ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>より前(まえ)に</code> only orders two things in time.
<code>に先駆(さきが)けて</code> claims a lead.

</div>

<pre>
兄(あに)より前(まえ)に家(いえ)を出(で)た。
I left the house before my brother did.
</pre>

<pre>
国(くに)の制度(せいど)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>、この市(し)は独自(どくじ)の支援(しえん)を始(はじ)めた。
Ahead of any national scheme, the city started support of its own.
</pre>

Leaving the house first is not an achievement, and
<code>に先駆(さきが)けて</code> there would sound absurdly grand. The pattern needs
a lead that means something - over an industry, a country, an era.

### ～に先駆(さきが)けて vs ～に先(さき)んじて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - near-identical in meaning.
<code>に先(さき)んじて</code> is the stiffer, more literary of the two and is at
home in editorial and historical writing.

</div>

<pre>
他国(たこく)<b>に先(さき)んじて</b>、その国(くに)は条約(じょうやく)に署名(しょめい)した。
Ahead of the other nations, the country signed the treaty.
</pre>

<pre>
業界(ぎょうかい)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>、この工場(こうじょう)は生産(せいさん)の自動(じどう)化(か)を進(すす)めた。
Ahead of the industry, this plant pushed on with automating production.
</pre>

Either would pass in either sentence. As a tendency,
<code>先(さき)んじて</code> reads as older and more formal, and it is the one you
are more likely to meet in a history book than in a product announcement.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>資料(しりょう)を配(くば)った。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)<b>に先立(さきだ)って</b>資料(しりょう)を配(くば)った。
  Handing out papers is preparation, not a race won. Use
  <code>に先立(さきだ)って</code> when nobody is being beaten to anything.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べた。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)より先(さき)に昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べた。
  Both the scale and the register are wrong. Everyday precedence between two people
  takes <code>より先(さき)に</code>.

- ❌ 新製品(しんせいひん)を発売(はつばい)する<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b> → ✅ 新製品(しんせいひん)の発売(はつばい)<b>に先駆(さきが)けて</b>
  The slot takes a noun. Turn the verb into an event noun, or use
  <code>発売(はつばい)する前(まえ)に</code>.

- ❌ 他社(たしゃ)に<b>先駆(せんく)けて</b> → ✅ 他社(たしゃ)に<b>先駆(さきが)けて</b>
  A reading error, not a grammar one: せんく belongs to
  <code>先駆者(せんくしゃ)</code>, while the verb is さきがける.

## Practice

</details>

Cleaning the venue is a preparatory step, and no one is being beaten to it.

as something worth mentioning. It is the wording a publicity department would choose.

</details>

# ～を限りに

**Meaning** · as of; with X as the last time - names the day, month or occasion after which something stops for good

Read <code>を限(かぎ)りに</code> as drawing a line and shutting the door on the far
side of it. <code>本日(ほんじつ)を限(かぎ)りに閉店(へいてん)</code> means today
is the last day of trading: the new state - not trading - runs from that boundary
onwards. The syllabus gloss "starting from" is only half the story, and the half it
leaves out is the important one. **Something ends here.**

A separate, frozen use of the same words is
<code>声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに</code>, "at the top of one's voice", which belongs to
narrative prose.

## Formation

A noun goes in front with を. There is no verb form and no clause form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Day, date | Noun + を限(かぎ)りに | 本日(ほんじつ) → 本日(ほんじつ)を限(かぎ)りに |
| Month, year, period | Noun + を限(かぎ)りに | 今年度(こんねんど) → 今年度(こんねんど)を限(かぎ)りに |
| Occasion, event | Noun + を限(かぎ)りに | 今大会(こんたいかい) → 今大会(こんたいかい)を限(かぎ)りに |
| Usual main clause | ～を限(かぎ)りに + 終了(しゅうりょう)／閉店(へいてん)／引退(いんたい) | 営業(えいぎょう)を終了(しゅうりょう)する |
| Frozen expression | 声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに + 叫(さけ)ぶ／呼(よ)ぶ | 声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに叫(さけ)んだ |
| Modern equivalent (spoken) | Noun + で | 今日(きょう)で終(お)わり |
| Modern equivalent (notices) | Noun + をもって | 本日(ほんじつ)をもって閉店(へいてん)いたします |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three lookalikes, three different jobs.
<code>を限(かぎ)りに</code> = this is the last time.
<code>に限(かぎ)り</code> = only in this case
（本日(ほんじつ)に限(かぎ)り半額(はんがく)）.
<code>限(かぎ)り</code> = as long as it holds
（体力(たいりょく)が続(つづ)く限(かぎ)り）.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
当店(とうてん)は本日(ほんじつ)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>閉店(へいてん)いたします。
This shop will close for the last time today.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>、たばこはやめる。
As of today, I am finished with cigarettes.
</pre>

<pre>
勝手(かって)ながら、今月(こんげつ)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>配達(はいたつ)サービスを終了(しゅうりょう)させていただきます。
We regret that our delivery service will end at the close of this month.
</pre>

<pre>
その選手(せんしゅ)は今(こん)シーズン<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>現役(げんえき)を退(しりぞ)くと発表(はっぴょう)した。
The player announced that he would retire at the end of this season.
</pre>

<pre>
この路線(ろせん)は三月(さんがつ)末(まつ)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>廃止(はいし)されることが決(き)まった。
It has been decided that this line will be discontinued after the end of March.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)以来(いらい)続(つづ)いた夜(よる)の営業(えいぎょう)も、今年(ことし)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>幕(まく)を閉(と)じる。
The evening opening hours kept up since the shop was founded will end with this year.
</pre>

<pre>
会長(かいちょう)は今年度(こんねんど)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>職(しょく)を辞(じ)する意向(いこう)を示(しめ)した。
The chairman indicated that he intends to leave his post at the end of this financial year.
</pre>

<pre>
少女(しょうじょ)は声(こえ)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>助(たす)けを求(もと)めた。
The girl cried out for help at the top of her voice.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not reach for it.** Where you will actually meet it is on
printed notices in shop windows, in the closing paragraph of a formal announcement, in
sports and business news reporting a retirement or a withdrawal, and - in the
<code>声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに</code> form - in novels. It is stiff, faintly
old-fashioned, and it belongs to the voice of an institution announcing a decision.
Said across a kitchen table it lands like a proclamation.

What a Japanese speaker says instead is simply **で**:
<code>この店(みせ)、今日(きょう)で終(お)わりなんだって</code>,
<code>部活(ぶかつ)は今日(きょう)で最後(さいご)だ</code>. In a written notice
today the usual choice is <code>をもって</code> -
<code>本日(ほんじつ)をもって営業(えいぎょう)を終了(しゅうりょう)いたします</code>
- which sounds formal without sounding antique. Those two, で and をもって, are the
forms to produce.

The one place a learner might genuinely write
<code>を限(かぎ)りに</code> is the theatrical personal vow:
<code>今日(きょう)を限(かぎ)りに…</code> announcing that you are giving something
up. Even there it is a deliberately dramatic choice, closer to "from this day forth"
than to ordinary speech, and it is normally said with a smile at its own grandeur.

<code>声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに</code> is a separate matter: it is a fixed
collocation with 叫(さけ)ぶ, 呼(よ)ぶ and a few other verbs of calling out, it is
purely literary, and no one uses it about themselves.

## Contrast

### ～を限(かぎ)りに vs ～で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, opposite registers. <code>で</code> is what
people say; <code>を限(かぎ)りに</code> is what notices print. **Use で.**

</div>

<pre>
このバイト、今月(こんげつ)<b>で</b>やめるつもり。
I'm planning to quit this part-time job at the end of the month.
</pre>

<pre>
本店(ほんてん)は今月(こんげつ)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>営業(えいぎょう)を終了(しゅうりょう)いたします。
The main branch will cease trading at the end of this month.
</pre>

Same month, same ending, two different worlds. The first is a conversation; the
second is a sign on a door. If you are speaking, で and
<code>最後(さいご)</code> will carry everything you need:
<code>今日(きょう)が最後(さいご)です</code>.

### ～を限(かぎ)りに vs ～をもって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal announcement wording.
<code>をもって</code> is the **current** one and is far more common today;
<code>を限(かぎ)りに</code> sounds older and more emphatic.

</div>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)は三月(さんがつ)三十一日(さんじゅういちにち)<b>をもって</b>締(し)め切(き)らせていただきます。
Applications will close on 31 March.
</pre>

<pre>
大会(たいかい)は今回(こんかい)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>終(お)わることになった。
This will be the last time the competition is held.
</pre>

<code>をもって</code> is also the wider word: it can mark a starting boundary
(<code>四月(しがつ)一日(ついたち)をもって発効(はっこう)する</code>) and a means
(<code>書面(しょめん)をもって通知(つうち)する</code>), neither of which
<code>を限(かぎ)りに</code> can do. If you are drafting a notice, reach for
<code>をもって</code>.

### 声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに vs 大声(おおごえ)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに</code> is narrative prose
meaning "with everything the voice had". In speech it is
<code>大声(おおごえ)で</code> or <code>力(ちから)いっぱい</code>.

</div>

<pre>
男(おとこ)は声(こえ)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>仲間(なかま)の名(な)を呼(よ)んだ。
The man called his companion's name at the top of his voice.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちが大声(おおごえ)で応援(おうえん)している。
The children are cheering at the top of their voices.
</pre>

The first sentence is a line of fiction and would be strange in conversation; the
second is what you would say watching a school sports day. Note too that this use has
nothing to do with endings - it is the same word
<code>限(かぎ)り</code> in its other sense, "limit".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>新(あたら)しい生活(せいかつ)を始(はじ)める。 → ✅ 明日(あした)<b>から</b>新(あたら)しい生活(せいかつ)を始(はじ)める。
  The pattern marks the **last** occasion of something, never the first. For a
  beginning, use から.

- ❌ この割引(わりびき)は本日(ほんじつ)<b>を限(かぎ)りに</b>ご利用(りよう)いただけます。 → ✅ この割引(わりびき)は本日(ほんじつ)<b>に限(かぎ)り</b>ご利用(りよう)いただけます。
  "Only today" is <code>に限(かぎ)り</code>. The two look almost the same and mean
  quite different things.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)を限(かぎ)りにこのゲームやめるわ。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)<b>で</b>このゲームやめるわ。
  Not ungrammatical, but the register is broken in two: an antique announcement phrase
  followed by casual わ. In speech, で.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに<b>話(はな)した</b>。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は声(こえ)を限(かぎ)りに<b>叫(さけ)んだ</b>。
  The frozen phrase goes with verbs of shouting and calling, not with ordinary
  speaking.

# ～かたわら

**Meaning** · alongside one's main work - carrying on a second, smaller activity for years beside the thing one mainly does

<code>かたわら</code> is the noun 傍(かたわ)ら, "the side of something". The clause
in front of it names the person's main occupation; the main clause names what they
also do, off to one side of it. Both stretch over a long period. This is the
sentence shape of an author biography, not of an ordinary day.

## Formation

<code>かたわら</code> is a noun, so what precedes it is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + かたわら | 働(はたら)く → 働(はたら)くかたわら |
| Verb (ている) | ている + かたわら | 勤(つと)めている → 勤(つと)めているかたわら |
| Noun | ～の + かたわら | 研究(けんきゅう) → 研究(けんきゅう)のかたわら |
| Written form | 傍(かたわ)ら | 農業(のうぎょう)を営(いとな)む傍(かたわ)ら |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one person, two long-running activities, and the **main** one
goes in the <code>かたわら</code> clause. If the two clauses have different subjects,
or if the second activity lasted an afternoon, this is the wrong pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)に勤(つと)める<b>かたわら</b>、夜(よる)は日本語(にほんご)を教(おし)えている。
He has a company job, and teaches Japanese in the evenings.
</pre>

<pre>
研究(けんきゅう)の<b>かたわら</b>、新聞(しんぶん)に書評(しょひょう)を書(か)いていた。
Alongside his research he wrote book reviews for a newspaper.
</pre>

<pre>
農業(のうぎょう)を営(いとな)む<b>かたわら</b>、村(むら)の歴史(れきし)を調(しら)べ続(つづ)けた。
He farmed for a living and went on researching the history of his village.
</pre>

<pre>
主婦業(しゅふぎょう)の<b>かたわら</b>、地域(ちいき)の子(こ)どもたちに書道(しょどう)を教(おし)えている。
Alongside running the household she teaches calligraphy to children in the neighbourhood.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)で教(おし)える<b>かたわら</b>、各地(かくち)の方言(ほうげん)を記録(きろく)して回(まわ)った。
While teaching at the university, he travelled the country recording regional dialects.
</pre>

<pre>
医師(いし)としての仕事(しごと)の<b>かたわら</b>、二十年(にじゅうねん)にわたって詩(し)を書(か)き続(つづ)けた。
Alongside his work as a doctor, he went on writing poetry for twenty years.
</pre>

<pre>
会社員(かいしゃいん)として働(はたら)く<b>かたわら</b>、週末(しゅうまつ)は山岳(さんがく)救助隊(きゅうじょたい)の一員(いちいん)として活動(かつどう)している。
Alongside his office job he serves at weekends as a member of a mountain rescue team.
</pre>

<pre>
店(みせ)を継(つ)いだあとも、彼女(かのじょ)は経営(けいえい)の<b>かたわら</b>絵(え)を描(か)くことをやめなかった。
Even after she took over the shop, she never stopped painting alongside running the business.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not reach for it.** <code>かたわら</code> belongs to written
Japanese of a particular kind: the author blurb inside a book jacket, a newspaper
profile, an awards citation, an obituary, a company history. It is the standard way
Japanese prose says "and on top of the day job, he also spent thirty years doing
this". Said out loud about your own week it sounds as though you were reading your
own biography aloud. In speech and in ordinary writing, use
<code>働(はたら)きながら</code>, <code>～一方(いっぽう)で</code>, or
<code>～と並行(へいこう)して</code>.

Two constraints follow from what the word is *for*. The activities must be
**durable** - an occupation, a study, a practice - so
❌<code>テレビを見(み)るかたわら</code> is not just stiff, it is wrong. And the
**subject must be one person**, the same in both clauses, because the sentence is a
description of that person's life.

There is a very mild note of admiration in it. The second activity is usually
something creative, civic or scholarly that the person kept up without being paid
for it, and the sentence quietly credits them for the effort. It is not used to say
that someone held down two jobs to make ends meet; that is
<code>掛(か)け持(も)ち</code>.

Separately, 傍(かたわ)ら survives as an ordinary noun meaning "the side of",
as in <code>道(みち)の傍(かたわ)らに石碑(せきひ)が立(た)っている</code>.
That use is alive and unremarkable, and reading it in a text tells you nothing about
the grammar point.

## Contrast

### ～かたわら vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` is the everyday word and covers any timescale.
<code>かたわら</code> is written, and only covers **years**.

</div>

<pre>
コーヒーを飲(の)み<b>ながら</b>資料(しりょう)に目(め)を通(とお)した。
I looked over the papers while drinking a coffee.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)で働(はたら)く<b>かたわら</b>、夜間(やかん)の専門(せんもん)学校(がっこう)に通(かよ)った。
He worked in a factory and attended a night-school course alongside it.
</pre>

Swap them and only one direction fails. <code>工場(こうじょう)で働(はたら)きながら夜間(やかん)の学校(がっこう)に通(かよ)った</code>
is perfectly good Japanese and is what a person would actually say. The reverse -
coffee and paperwork joined by <code>かたわら</code> - is not stiff but broken,
because the clause is supposed to name a livelihood. When in doubt, this is the
substitution to make: `ながら` is almost never wrong where <code>かたわら</code> is
right.

### ～かたわら vs ～かたがた・～がてら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>かたわら</code> is a **standing arrangement**;
<code>かたがた</code> and `がてら` are a **single occasion** that serves two purposes.

</div>

<pre>
散歩(さんぽ)<b>がてら</b>、郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)に寄(よ)ってきた。
I walked over to the post office and got a stroll out of it.
</pre>

<pre>
翻訳(ほんやく)の仕事(しごと)の<b>かたわら</b>、同人(どうじん)誌(し)を出(だ)し続(つづ)けている。
Alongside her translation work she keeps putting out a small literary magazine.
</pre>

The first sentence covers one afternoon; the second covers a decade. A useful check:
if you could put a date on it, it is `がてら` or <code>かたがた</code>. If you would
have to give a span of years, it is <code>かたわら</code>.

### ～かたわら vs ～一方(いっぽう)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～一方(いっぽう)で</code> is the neutral modern option
and can contrast **anything** with anything, including two subjects or two sides of a
situation. <code>かたわら</code> needs **one person and two activities**.

</div>

<pre>
都市部(としぶ)の人口(じんこう)が増(ふ)える<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、地方(ちほう)では過疎(かそ)が進(すす)んでいる。
While the cities grow, depopulation is advancing in the regions.
</pre>

<pre>
市役所(しやくしょ)に勤(つと)める<b>かたわら</b>、市民(しみん)楽団(がくだん)で指揮(しき)をとっている。
Alongside his job at city hall he conducts a community orchestra.
</pre>

The first cannot take <code>かたわら</code> at all: two different populations are
doing the contrasting, and no one is pursuing anything. The second could take
<code>一方(いっぽう)で</code>, and in a spoken report it probably would. That
asymmetry is the whole story - <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> is the wider,
safer word.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ テレビを見(み)る<b>かたわら</b>、ご飯(はん)を食(た)べた。 → ✅ テレビを見(み)<b>ながら</b>、ご飯(はん)を食(た)べた。
  Two things inside the same half-hour are `ながら`. <code>かたわら</code> needs
  activities measured in years.

- ❌ 仕事(しごと)<b>かたわら</b>絵(え)を習(なら)っている。 → ✅ 仕事(しごと)<b>のかたわら</b>絵(え)を習(なら)っている。
  <code>かたわら</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`.

- ❌ 働(はたら)い<b>たかたわら</b> → ✅ 働(はたら)<b>くかたわら</b>
  The clause takes the dictionary form or `ている`, never the past. The pattern
  describes an ongoing arrangement, and a past-tense clause contradicts that even when
  the whole sentence is about the past.

- ❌ 兄(あに)が働(はたら)く<b>かたわら</b>、私(わたし)は大学(だいがく)に通(かよ)った。 → ✅ 兄(あに)が働(はたら)いてくれる<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、私(わたし)は大学(だいがく)に通(かよ)った。
  The two clauses must share a subject. With two different people the sentence needs
  <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)は新聞社(しんぶんしゃ)に勤(つと)める____、長年(ながねん)にわたって児童(じどう)文学(ぶんがく)を書(か)き続(つづ)けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新聞社(しんぶんしゃ)に勤(つと)める<b>かたわら</b>

A career at a newspaper and a decades-long second calling as a children's writer:
one person, two durable activities, the livelihood in the first clause. This is the
profile-piece sentence <code>かたわら</code> exists for.

</details>

Washing up to music takes ten minutes. <code>かたわら</code> would claim that

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

reason to learn <code>かたわら</code> - so that when you meet it in print you know
you are being handed a summed-up life.

</details>

# ～かたがた

**Meaning** · combining two errands of courtesy - doing one formal, social thing which also serves as another

<code>かたがた</code> joins two *social duties*: a thank-you and a report, an apology
and an announcement, a greeting and a visit. The noun in front names the further
purpose the act also serves; the main clause names what you actually did or are
doing. It lives almost entirely in formal letters and in the language of a formal
call, and it attaches to a very short list of nouns.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (act of courtesy) | Noun + かたがた | お礼(れい) → お礼(れい)かたがた |
| Honorific noun | お／ご + Noun + かたがた | ご挨拶(あいさつ) → ご挨拶(あいさつ)かたがた |
| Followed by | a humble verb of writing or visiting | ご報告(ほうこく)申(もう)し上(あ)げます／伺(うかが)いました |
| Letter sign-off | まずは … かたがた … まで | まずはお礼(れい)かたがたご挨拶(あいさつ)まで |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in practice the noun is drawn from a handful:
お礼(れい)・ご挨拶(あいさつ)・ご報告(ほうこく)・お詫(わ)び・お見(み)舞(ま)い・ご連絡(れんらく).
Outside that set the word sounds invented. <code>かたがた</code> is a conjunctive, so
it can never end a sentence: ❌<code>お礼(れい)かたがたです</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
お礼(れい)<b>かたがた</b>ご報告(ほうこく)申(もう)し上(あ)げます。
I write to report on the matter, and to offer my thanks at the same time.
</pre>

<pre>
ご挨拶(あいさつ)<b>かたがた</b>、近(ちか)くまで参(まい)りましたのでお寄(よ)りしました。
I was in the neighbourhood, so I have called in to pay my respects.
</pre>

<pre>
まずは書面(しょめん)にてお詫(わ)び<b>かたがた</b>ご連絡(れんらく)申(もう)し上(あ)げます。
For the moment I write to you both by way of apology and to keep you informed.
</pre>

<pre>
新居(しんきょ)のご挨拶(あいさつ)<b>かたがた</b>、隣(となり)の家(いえ)に菓子折(かしお)りを持(も)って伺(うかが)った。
I called next door with a box of sweets, by way of introducing ourselves as new neighbours.
</pre>

<pre>
就任(しゅうにん)のご挨拶(あいさつ)<b>かたがた</b>、取引先(とりひきさき)を一社(いっしゃ)ずつ回(まわ)っている。
He is going round the client companies one by one, announcing his appointment as he goes.
</pre>

<pre>
お見(み)舞(ま)い<b>かたがた</b>、入院(にゅういん)中(ちゅう)の引(ひ)き継(つ)ぎについて相談(そうだん)してきた。
I went to see him in hospital, and while I was there we discussed the handover.
</pre>

<pre>
退院(たいいん)のご報告(ほうこく)<b>かたがた</b>、お世話(せわ)になった方々(かたがた)に手紙(てがみ)を書(か)いた。
I wrote to everyone who had looked after me, to report that I was out of hospital and to thank them.
</pre>

<pre>
まずはお礼(れい)<b>かたがた</b>ご挨拶(あいさつ)まで。
For now, my thanks and my regards.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise it, and write <code>を兼(か)ねて</code> instead.**
<code>かたがた</code> is letter Japanese - the 拝啓(はいけい)／敬具(けいぐ)
register, business correspondence on paper, formal cards, the sentence a person says
at the door when making a courtesy call. It is not archaic in the way
<code>抜(ぬ)きつ抜(ぬ)かれつ</code> is archaic; it is simply confined. Used
between friends it does not sound old-fashioned so much as absurd, like ending a text
message with "yours faithfully". The everyday equivalents are
<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> for a planned double purpose and `ついでに` for a casual
one.

Where you will genuinely meet it is the **last line of a formal letter**.
<code>まずはお礼(れい)かたがたご挨拶(あいさつ)まで</code> and
<code>まずはご報告(ほうこく)かたがたお礼(れい)申(もう)し上(あ)げます</code>
are close to fixed formulas, and the <code>まずは…まで</code> frame around them is part
of the same style: "this much for now". If you ever have to write a Japanese thank-you
letter on paper, this is the one place the pattern is worth producing.

Two traps in the spelling. <code>方々(かたがた)</code> is a different word
entirely - the honorific plural "people, ladies and gentlemen" - and it is far more
common than the grammar point, so a かたがた in a text is more likely to be that.
And <code>方々(ほうぼう)</code>, the same two characters again, means "here and
there". The grammar point is written in kana, which is how you tell.

Finally, note that the nouns it takes are all things you do *to* someone. That is why
❌<code>買(か)い物(もの)かたがた</code> fails: shopping is not a courtesy owed
to anybody.

## Contrast

### ～かたがた vs ～を兼(か)ねて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different centuries.
<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> is the ordinary modern word and takes **any** noun;
<code>かたがた</code> is letter style and takes only **courtesies**.

</div>

<pre>
新年度(しんねんど)の顔合(かおあ)わせを<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、来週(らいしゅう)食事会(しょくじかい)を開(ひら)きます。
We'll hold a dinner next week, which will also serve as the introductions for the new year.
</pre>

<pre>
着任(ちゃくにん)のご挨拶(あいさつ)<b>かたがた</b>、本社(ほんしゃ)に伺(うかが)う予定(よてい)です。
I plan to visit head office, and to introduce myself in my new post while I am there.
</pre>

Both sentences pair two purposes. The first is something you would say in a meeting;
the second is something you would write. Note also the grammar:
<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> takes `を` and behaves like the verb
兼(か)ねる that it is, while <code>かたがた</code> is bare and takes nothing.

### ～かたがた vs ～がてら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both put two purposes into one outing. `がてら` is **personal and
relaxed**; <code>かたがた</code> is **owed to somebody and formal**.

</div>

<pre>
運動(うんどう)がてら、駅(えき)まで歩(ある)いて新聞(しんぶん)を買(か)ってきた。
I walked to the station for the exercise and picked up a paper.
</pre>

<pre>
ご寄付(きふ)のお礼(れい)<b>かたがた</b>、会(かい)の活動(かつどう)をご報告(ほうこく)いたします。
Let me thank you for your donation and at the same time report on the society's activities.
</pre>

`がてら` can be an errand, a walk, a trip - anything you were doing anyway.
<code>かたがた</code> requires an obligation of courtesy on at least one side. This is
why ❌<code>散歩(さんぽ)かたがた</code> is wrong and
<code>散歩(さんぽ)がてら</code> is idiomatic: a stroll is nobody's duty.

### ～かたがた vs ～ついでに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ついでに` frames the second thing as an **afterthought**. That is
exactly what you must not say to a superior, and it is why
<code>かたがた</code> exists.

</div>

<pre>
銀行(ぎんこう)に行(い)った<b>ついでに</b>、郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)にも寄(よ)っておいた。
While I was at the bank I dropped in on the post office too.
</pre>

<pre>
出張(しゅっちょう)の報告(ほうこく)<b>かたがた</b>、部長(ぶちょう)にご挨拶(あいさつ)に伺(うかが)った。
I went to see the department head, to report on the trip and to pay my respects.
</pre>

Rewrite the second with `ついでに` -
❌<code>報告(ほうこく)のついでに部長(ぶちょう)に挨拶(あいさつ)した</code> - and you
have told your boss that greeting him was something you fitted in around the real
business. `ついでに` is fine downward and sideways, and rude upward.
<code>かたがた</code> presents both purposes as equally intended, which is the whole
point of using it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 散歩(さんぽ)<b>かたがた</b>本屋(ほんや)に寄(よ)った。 → ✅ 散歩(さんぽ)<b>がてら</b>本屋(ほんや)に寄(よ)った。
  <code>かたがた</code> pairs two social obligations. A walk and a bookshop are neither
  social nor obligatory.

- ❌ 買(か)い物(もの)を<b>するかたがた</b> → ✅ 買(か)い物(もの)<b>がてら</b>
  In modern use <code>かたがた</code> follows a bare noun. A verb clause in front of it
  is not something you will find in contemporary Japanese.

- ❌ お礼(れい)<b>かたがたです</b>。 → ✅ お礼(れい)<b>かたがたご報告(ほうこく)いたします</b>。
  It is a conjunctive: it must be followed by the verb that does the acting. On its own
  it leaves the sentence with no predicate.

- ❌ 友達(ともだち)にお礼(れい)<b>かたがた</b>LINEした。 → ✅ 友達(ともだち)にお礼(れい)<b>のついでに</b>LINEした。
  A message to a friend cannot carry letter-writing vocabulary. The register clash is
  the error, not the grammar.

## Practice

**1.** Fill the blank with the pattern taught here: この度(たび)は結構(けっこう)なお品(しな)をありがとうございました。まずはお礼(れい)____ご挨拶(あいさつ)まで。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

まずはお礼(れい)<b>かたがた</b>ご挨拶(あいさつ)まで。

This is the standard closing line of a thank-you note, and the
<code>まずは…まで</code> frame is what tells you the missing word is
<code>かたがた</code>. The whole sentence means "for now, my thanks and my regards" -
the letter is doing two jobs, and it says so.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 温泉(おんせん)に行(い)く（かたがた・がてら）、昔(むかし)の友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)ってきた。

A hot-spring trip is a pleasure, not a duty, so `がてら` is the fit. Note that the

</details>

**3.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence, and who is writing to whom? ご無沙汰(ぶさた)しております。転勤(てんきん)のご報告(ほうこく)<b>かたがた</b>、近況(きんきょう)をお知(し)らせ申(もう)し上(あ)げます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A letter or a formal email, from someone to a senior contact they have not written to
in a while - a former teacher, a previous manager, a client.
<code>ご無沙汰(ぶさた)しております</code> opens it,
<code>申(もう)し上(あ)げます</code> is humble, and
<code>かたがた</code> ties the two purposes of the letter together. The relationship
is respectful and slightly distant; between close colleagues none of this vocabulary
would appear.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)、ご相談(そうだん)かたがたです。少(すこ)しお時間(じかん)よろしいでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、ご相談(そうだん)したいことがございます。少(すこ)しお時間(じかん)よろしいでしょうか。

Two problems. <code>かたがた</code> cannot end a sentence - it needs a following verb -
and even repaired it does not belong in spoken Japanese at someone's desk. Say the
thing plainly in humble form. Save <code>かたがた</code> for the letter you send
afterwards.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in effect:
(a) お見(み)舞(ま)いのついでに、書類(しょるい)を届(とど)けてきた。
(b) お見(み)舞(ま)い<b>かたがた</b>、書類(しょるい)をお届(とど)けした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the visit was the errand and the documents were tacked on - or, read the
other way round, that the real trip was the delivery and the sick person got looked in
on while you were there. Either way something is being treated as incidental, and if
the listener is the patient, that stings.

(b) presents both as intended and both as courtesies, and the humble
<code>お届(とど)けした</code> matches. It is what you would write in a follow-up
note to the person's family. The information is the same; (b) declines to rank the two
errands, and that refusal to rank them is what politeness consists of here.

</details>

# ～がてら

**Meaning** · while you're out doing one thing, do the other too - one trip, two purposes, both of them real

`がてら` puts two errands into a single outing. Unlike `ついでに`, neither one is an
afterthought: you set off knowing you would do both, and the shape of the trip serves
them equally. The clause before `がてら` names the purpose you would give if asked why
you went out; the main clause names what you also got done.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + がてら | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)きがてら |
| Verb (する-verb) | ～し + がてら | 見学(けんがく)する → 見学(けんがく)しがてら |
| Noun (an activity) | Noun + がてら | 散歩(さんぽ) → 散歩(さんぽ)がてら |
| Followed by | a clause about going or being somewhere | 寄(よ)る／訪(たず)ねる／買(か)ってくる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がてら` needs an **outing**. The two things happen on one trip out
of the house, not in one room at one moment. If nobody goes anywhere, you want
`ながら`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
散歩(さんぽ)<b>がてら</b>、パン屋(や)に寄(よ)ってきた。
I walked over to the bakery - a bit of a stroll and the bread at the same time.
</pre>

<pre>
買(か)い物(もの)<b>がてら</b>、図書館(としょかん)に本(ほん)を返(かえ)してきた。
I took the library books back while I was out doing the shopping.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで送(おく)り<b>がてら</b>、話(はなし)の続(つづ)きを聞(き)いた。
I walked her to the station and heard the rest of the story on the way.
</pre>

<pre>
運動(うんどう)<b>がてら</b>、一駅(ひとえき)手前(てまえ)で降(お)りて歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)っている。
I get off a stop early and walk the rest of the way home, partly for the exercise.
</pre>

<pre>
腹(はら)ごなし<b>がてら</b>、近(ちか)くの神社(じんじゃ)まで足(あし)を延(の)ばした。
We walked as far as the shrine nearby, to work off the lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
出張(しゅっちょう)<b>がてら</b>、学生(がくせい)時代(じだい)の友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)う約束(やくそく)をした。
I've arranged to see an old university friend while I'm down there on business.
</pre>

<pre>
見物(けんぶつ)<b>がてら</b>城下町(じょうかまち)を歩(ある)いていたら、途中(とちゅう)で雨(あめ)に降(ふ)られてしまった。
I was walking through the old castle town, sightseeing as I went, and got caught in the rain.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいカメラを試(ため)し<b>がてら</b>、夜(よ)明(あ)け前(まえ)の海岸(かいがん)まで車(くるま)を走(はし)らせた。
I drove out to the coast before dawn, partly to give the new camera a run.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`がてら` is neutral in politeness and works in speech and writing alike, but it has an
**adult, slightly bookish** flavour. It is the word of someone describing their own
day in an unhurried way, and it turns up constantly in essays, travel writing and
casual blog posts. Younger speakers reach for `ついでに` far more often; a
twenty-year-old is more likely to say <code>散歩(さんぽ)のついでに</code>, and
nobody would think it odd.

Two collocations are all but fixed: <code>散歩(さんぽ)がてら</code> and
<code>買(か)い物(もの)がてら</code>. If you learn only those two you have most of
the pattern's real-world frequency, and they are safe to produce in any register short
of a formal letter.

The word itself never inflects. There is no ❌<code>がてらです</code>, no
❌<code>がてらだった</code>; it hands the sentence straight on to the main clause,
which carries all the tense and politeness. That main clause is normally in the past
or in the habitual present - `がてら` is at its most natural reporting something you
did or something you regularly do, and slightly less so announcing a plan, where
<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> tends to take over.

One social caution. Because `がてら` names the *other* purpose out loud, saying it
about a visit to someone senior tells them you had a second reason for coming. That is
fine among equals and warm among friends - it means "I thought of you while I was out"
- but with a client or a professor it can read as a demotion of their importance. The
formal alternative that avoids the problem is <code>を兼(か)ねて</code>, or, in a
letter, <code>かたがた</code>.

## Contrast

### ～がてら vs ～ついでに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `がてら` both purposes were **planned**. With `ついでに` the
second one is an **opportunity you noticed** once you were already out.

</div>

<pre>
郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)に行(い)った<b>ついでに</b>、隣(となり)のカフェでコーヒーを飲(の)んだ。
Since I was at the post office anyway, I had a coffee at the café next door.
</pre>

<pre>
気分転換(きぶんてんかん)<b>がてら</b>、川沿(かわぞ)いの道(みち)を自転車(じてんしゃ)で走(はし)ってきた。
I took the bike along the river path - a ride and a change of air together.
</pre>

The grammar differs too, and this is the reliable test: `ついでに` follows a full clause
(<code>行(い)った ついでに</code>, <code>買(か)い物(もの)のついでに</code>),
while `がてら` follows a bare ます-stem or noun. If you find yourself wanting a
past-tense verb in front, the word you want is `ついでに`.

There is also a difference in weight. `ついでに` can make the second errand sound
trivial, which is useful when you are minimising a favour
(<code>ついでですから</code>, "it's no trouble") and unhelpful when you are not.
`がてら` never trivialises either side.

### ～がてら vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` means **at the same instant**. `がてら` means **on the same
trip**.

</div>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>ながら</b>走(はし)るのが習慣(しゅうかん)になっている。
Running with music on has become a habit of mine.
</pre>

<pre>
花見(はなみ)<b>がてら</b>、川(かわ)の向(む)こうの喫茶店(きっさてん)まで歩(ある)いた。
We walked over to the coffee shop across the river, taking in the blossom on the way.
</pre>

The two forms even look alike after a ます-stem -
<code>歩(ある)きながら</code> against <code>歩(ある)きがてら</code> - so the
distinction has to be carried in your head. In the first, the walking and the other
action overlap second by second. In the second, the walking *is* the trip, and the
other thing happens somewhere along it. That is why
❌<code>テレビを見(み)がてら食(た)べた</code> fails: watching and eating happen in
the same chair, and nothing has been gone out and done.

### ～がてら vs ～を兼(か)ねて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がてら` is a **personal outing**.
<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> is **an event or arrangement** that officially serves two
functions, and it is the one you use in writing and at work.

</div>

<pre>
歓迎会(かんげいかい)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、来月(らいげつ)懇親会(こんしんかい)を開(ひら)くことになりました。
We've decided to hold a get-together next month, which will double as a welcome party.
</pre>

<pre>
昼寝(ひるね)から覚(さ)めた子(こ)を連(つ)れて、夕涼(ゆうすず)み<b>がてら</b>公園(こうえん)まで歩(ある)いた。
With the children up from their nap we walked to the park, enjoying the cool of the evening.
</pre>

<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> can take an abstract or institutional noun - a welcome
party, a market survey, an evacuation shelter - and it fits a meeting agenda.
`がてら` cannot: ❌<code>歓迎会(かんげいかい)がてら懇親会(こんしんかい)を開(ひら)く</code>
sounds as if the company outing were somebody's afternoon walk.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 散歩(さんぽ)<b>するがてら</b>本屋(ほんや)に寄(よ)った。 → ✅ 散歩(さんぽ)<b>がてら</b>本屋(ほんや)に寄(よ)った。
  `がてら` takes a bare noun or a ます-stem, never the dictionary form.
  <code>散歩(さんぽ)しがてら</code> is also possible, but the bare noun is what people
  say.

- ❌ ソファで休(やす)み<b>がてら</b>雑誌(ざっし)を読(よ)んだ。 → ✅ ソファで休(やす)み<b>ながら</b>雑誌(ざっし)を読(よ)んだ。
  Nobody went anywhere. Two things done in the same chair at the same moment are
  `ながら`.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は買(か)い物(もの)<b>がてらでした</b>。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は買(か)い物(もの)<b>がてら散歩(さんぽ)してきました</b>。
  `がてら` is a conjunctive with no forms of its own. It cannot carry the tense or the
  politeness; the main verb does that.

- ❌ 買(か)い物(もの)<b>がてら</b>御社(おんしゃ)に伺(うかが)います。 → ✅ ご報告(ほうこく)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>御社(おんしゃ)に伺(うかが)います。
  Grammatically fine, socially poor: you have just told a client that their office was
  the second stop after the shops.

## Practice

**1.** 天気(てんき)がいいので、散歩(さんぽ)____近所(きんじょ)の花屋(はなや)をのぞいてきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

散歩(さんぽ)<b>がてら</b>近所(きんじょ)の花屋(はなや)をのぞいてきた。

One outing serving two purposes that were both in mind when you put your shoes on -
the walk and the flower shop. <code>散歩(さんぽ)がてら</code> is the single most
common collocation of the pattern, and it attaches to the bare noun with nothing in
between.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)を読(よ)み（がてら・ながら）、電車(でんしゃ)に揺(ゆ)られていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

資料(しりょう)を読(よ)み<b>ながら</b>電車(でんしゃ)に揺(ゆ)られていた。

Reading and being carried along by the train happen in the same seconds, so `ながら`.
`がてら` would need the reading and the travelling to be two separable errands of one
outing, and reading is not an errand you go out to perform.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I went to see the exhibition, and had lunch with an old classmate while I was in town."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

展覧会(てんらんかい)を見(み)に行(い)き<b>がてら</b>、昔(むかし)の同級生(どうきゅうせい)と昼食(ちゅうしょく)をとってきた。

The exhibition is the stated reason for the trip, so it goes in the `がてら` clause as
a ます-stem; lunch is the other thing the same trip achieved.
<code>展覧会(てんらんかい)がてら</code> with the bare noun is also natural.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は運動(うんどう)するがてら、川(かわ)まで走(はし)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は運動(うんどう)<b>がてら</b>、川(かわ)まで走(はし)りました。

The dictionary form <code>する</code> cannot stand in front of `がてら`. Drop it and
attach the noun directly, or use the ます-stem
<code>運動(うんどう)しがてら</code>. Everything else in the sentence is fine, and the
politeness is carried by <code>走(はし)りました</code>, exactly as it should be.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 出張(しゅっちょう)の<b>ついでに</b>実家(じっか)に寄(よ)った。
(b) 出張(しゅっちょう)<b>がてら</b>実家(じっか)に寄(よ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the business trip was the reason for being in the area and the visit home was
an opportunity taken once you were there. Said to your parents it is a little cool -
you would not have come otherwise.

(b) puts the two on a level: you were going anyway, and seeing your family was part of
what the trip was for. Nothing is being minimised. This is why (b) is the safer thing
to say to the people you visited, and (a) the more honest thing to say at the office.

</details>

# ～を兼ねて

**Meaning** · doing it partly for that as well - one action deliberately made to serve a second purpose

兼(か)ねる means "to serve as two things at once", and
<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> puts that second function into the sentence. The noun in
front of it is the **extra** purpose; the main clause is what actually happens. Both
were intended from the start, which is what separates it from `ついでに`.

## Formation

The pattern takes a noun. A verb has to be turned into one first - usually the
する-noun already exists.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + を兼(か)ねて | 運動(うんどう) → 運動(うんどう)を兼(か)ねて |
| Noun (adding it on) | Noun + も兼(か)ねて | 防災(ぼうさい) → 防災(ぼうさい)も兼(か)ねて |
| Modifying a noun | ～を兼(か)ねた + Noun | 書斎(しょさい) → 書斎(しょさい)を兼(か)ねた部屋(へや) |
| As the predicate | ～を兼(か)ねている | この旅行(りょこう)は取材(しゅざい)を兼(か)ねている |
| する-verb | drop する | 見学(けんがく)する → 見学(けんがく)を兼(か)ねて |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun before <code>を兼(か)ねて</code> is the **secondary**
purpose. <code>運動(うんどう)を兼(か)ねて歩(ある)く</code> is walking that
doubles as exercise; it is not exercise that doubles as walking. Get the order
backwards and the sentence says the opposite of what you mean.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
運動(うんどう)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、毎朝(まいあさ)一駅(ひとえき)分(ぶん)歩(ある)いている。
I walk a stop's worth every morning, partly to get some exercise.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の歓迎(かんげい)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、金曜日(きんようび)に部(ぶ)の飲(の)み会(かい)をやります。
We're having a department drinks on Friday, which will double as a welcome for the new staff.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の視察(しさつ)は市場(しじょう)調査(ちょうさ)を<b>兼(か)ねている</b>。
This inspection trip doubles as market research.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの挨拶(あいさつ)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、大家(おおや)さんに菓子(かし)を届(とど)けた。
I took some sweets round to the landlord, which also served as our moving-in greeting.
</pre>

<pre>
この建物(たてもの)は災害時(さいがいじ)の避難所(ひなんじょ)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>設計(せっけい)されている。
The building was designed to double as an evacuation shelter in a disaster.
</pre>

<pre>
後輩(こうはい)の指導(しどう)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、今回(こんかい)の案件(あんけん)は二人(ふたり)で担当(たんとう)してもらう。
I'll have the two of you handle this one together, partly so the junior member gets some training out of it.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の帰省(きせい)は、祖父母(そふぼ)への顔(かお)見(み)せと墓(はか)参(まい)りを<b>兼(か)ねている</b>。
Next month's trip home is both to show my face to my grandparents and to visit the family grave.
</pre>

<pre>
社員(しゃいん)研修(けんしゅう)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、工場(こうじょう)のある地方(ちほう)都市(とし)で合宿(がっしゅく)を行(おこな)うことになった。
It's been decided that we'll hold the retreat in the regional city where the plant is, so that it can serve as staff training too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> is **register-neutral and productive** - the plain workhorse
of this group. It is at home in a meeting, in an email, in a company announcement and
in ordinary conversation, and unlike `がてら` it takes institutional nouns without
strain: 歓迎会(かんげいかい), 市場(しじょう)調査(ちょうさ),
避難所(ひなんじょ), 社員(しゃいん)研修(けんしゅう). If you learn only one
pattern from this group to actually use, learn this one.

What it always signals is **intention**. Announcing that the trip
<code>を兼(か)ねている</code> tells everyone the double purpose was planned, budgeted
and approved, which is precisely why it turns up in business writing: it makes a mixed
motive respectable. The same sentence with `ついでに` would sound as if someone had
quietly bolted a private errand onto a company trip.

兼(か)ねる is a perfectly ordinary る-verb outside this pattern, and meeting it
elsewhere will not confuse you: <code>総務部長(そうむぶちょう)が広報(こうほう)も兼(か)ねている</code>
(holds both roles), <code>兼務(けんむ)</code>,
<code>寝室(しんしつ)兼(けん)書斎(しょさい)</code> - that last one read
<b>けん</b>, as a compound joiner, "bedroom-cum-study".

One homophone worth knowing. <code>かねて</code> written 予(かね)て is a different
word meaning "for some time now, all along" -
<code>予(かね)てから考(かんが)えていた通(とお)り</code>. It is sometimes written
兼(か)ねて in older or careless text, so a bare かねて at the head of a clause with no
`を` in front of it is probably that word, not this pattern.

## Contrast

### ～を兼(か)ねて vs ～がてら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がてら` needs a **trip**; <code>を兼(か)ねて</code> needs only a
**purpose**, and works for arrangements, buildings and jobs that go nowhere at all.

</div>

<pre>
昼休(ひるやす)み、気分転換(きぶんてんかん)<b>がてら</b>屋上(おくじょう)まで上(あ)がってみた。
At lunch I went up to the roof, partly just for a change of scene.
</pre>

<pre>
このソファーは来客用(らいきゃくよう)のベッドを<b>兼(か)ねている</b>。
This sofa doubles as a guest bed.
</pre>

The sofa sentence cannot be rewritten with `がてら` at all - furniture does not go on
outings - and that gap is the clearest way to feel the difference. Where both work, as
in <code>散歩(さんぽ)がてら</code> versus
<code>散歩(さんぽ)を兼(か)ねて</code>, the second is a shade more deliberate and
fits a written report better.

Note the shapes too: `がてら` attaches bare or to a ます-stem, while this pattern
demands the particle `を`.

### ～を兼(か)ねて vs ～ついでに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を兼(か)ねて</code> = **both were the plan**. `ついでに` =
**one of them came up because you were there anyway**.

</div>

<pre>
市役所(しやくしょ)に行(い)った<b>ついでに</b>、証明書(しょうめいしょ)ももらってきた。
While I was at city hall I picked up the certificate too.
</pre>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)の見守(みまも)りを<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、夕方(ゆうがた)に町内(ちょうない)を回(まわ)っています。
We walk the neighbourhood in the evenings, which also serves as a community watch.
</pre>

Try swapping them. <code>見守(みまも)りのついでに回(まわ)っている</code> would
turn a civic duty into something done in passing, and a resident hearing it would take
the scheme less seriously. Conversely
<code>証明書(しょうめいしょ)を兼(か)ねて市役所(しやくしょ)に行(い)った</code>
overstates a five-minute errand as a second official objective.

`ついでに` also has an idiomatic softening use with no equivalent here: offering to do
something for someone as <code>ついでですから</code>, "no trouble at all, I'm going
that way".

### ～を兼(か)ねて vs ～ために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ために` gives the **whole** reason for doing something.
<code>を兼(か)ねて</code> gives a **second** reason, and implies there is a first one
you have not repeated.

</div>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)の<b>ために</b>、毎晩(まいばん)30分(ぷん)歩(ある)くことにした。
I've decided to walk for thirty minutes every evening for my health.
</pre>

<pre>
犬(いぬ)の散歩(さんぽ)に出(で)るとき、健康(けんこう)づくりを<b>兼(か)ねて</b>遠回(とおまわ)りしている。
When I take the dog out I go the long way round, so that it counts as exercise as well.
</pre>

In the first, health is the entire motive; take it away and the walking stops. In the
second, the dog would need walking regardless, and the health benefit is stacked on
top. That is the test: if removing the stated purpose would cancel the action, use
`ために`. If the action would happen anyway, use <code>を兼(か)ねて</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 運動(うんどう)<b>兼(か)ねて</b>歩(ある)いている。 → ✅ 運動(うんどう)<b>を兼(か)ねて</b>歩(ある)いている。
  The particle is not optional. 兼(か)ねる is a transitive verb and needs its object
  marked.

- ❌ 歩(ある)くを<b>兼(か)ねて</b> → ✅ 散歩(さんぽ)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>
  A dictionary-form verb cannot be marked with `を`. Use the noun that already exists;
  運動(うんどう), 見学(けんがく), 挨拶(あいさつ) and so on are what this pattern
  is built for.

- ❌ お祝(いわ)いを<b>兼(か)ねって</b> → ✅ お祝(いわ)いを<b>兼(か)ねて</b>
  兼(か)ねる is a る-verb, so the て-form is 兼(か)ねて with one `ね`. The doubled
  ❌ねって comes from treating it like an う-verb.

- ❌ 食事会(しょくじかい)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>歓迎(かんげい)します。 → ✅ 歓迎(かんげい)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>食事会(しょくじかい)を開(ひら)きます。
  The purposes are the wrong way round. The dinner is the event; welcoming people is the
  extra function it serves, so 歓迎(かんげい) is what goes in front.

## Practice

**1.** 日(ひ)ごろの感謝(かんしゃ)を伝(つた)えることを____、今年(ことし)は家族(かぞく)全員(ぜんいん)で旅行(りょこう)することにした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

感謝(かんしゃ)を伝(つた)えることを<b>兼(か)ねて</b>

The trip was going to happen; saying thank you is the additional purpose folded into
it. Note that the verb phrase has been nominalised with <code>こと</code> so that `を`
has something to mark - that is the standard repair when no ready-made
する-noun exists.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 銀行(ぎんこう)へ行(い)った（ついでに・を兼(か)ねて）、向(む)かいの店(みせ)でお茶(ちゃ)を買(か)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

銀行(ぎんこう)へ行(い)った<b>ついでに</b>、向(む)かいの店(みせ)でお茶(ちゃ)を買(か)った。

Buying tea across the road is an opportunity taken on the spot, not a planned second
objective. The grammar decides it too: `ついでに` follows a full past-tense clause,
whereas <code>を兼(か)ねて</code> needs a bare noun in front of `を`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The meeting will also serve as a farewell for Mr Sato, so please make time for it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今度(こんど)の集(あつ)まりは佐藤(さとう)さんの送別(そうべつ)を<b>兼(か)ねています</b>ので、ぜひご都合(つごう)をつけてください。

"Also serve as" is <code>を兼(か)ねている</code> almost exactly. Using it as the
predicate rather than as a mid-sentence conjunctive is the natural way to announce the
double purpose, and the polite
<code>兼(か)ねています</code> suits a message to colleagues.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部屋(へや)が狭(せま)いので、この机(つくえ)は食卓(しょくたく)を兼(か)ねっている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部屋(へや)が狭(せま)いので、この机(つくえ)は食卓(しょくたく)を<b>兼(か)ねている</b>。

兼(か)ねる is a る-verb: 兼(か)ね + て / ている. There is no small `っ`. The
sentence is otherwise a good example of the non-outing use - a desk that doubles as a
dining table, where `がてら` could never appear.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 勉強(べんきょう)の<b>ために</b>、海外(かいがい)の支社(ししゃ)に行(い)かせてもらった。
(b) 勉強(べんきょう)を<b>兼(か)ねて</b>、海外(かいがい)の支社(ししゃ)に行(い)かせてもらった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the whole point of the trip was for the speaker to learn something. The
company sent them to be trained, and there is no other agenda on the table.

(b) implies the trip had a job attached - a handover, an audit, a client visit - and
that learning was a second, acknowledged benefit. It is the more modest of the two, and
in a workplace it is also the safer thing to say, because it does not claim that the
company spent money purely on your development.

</details>

# ～つ～つ

**Meaning** · back and forth between two opposite actions - alternating, first one then the other, over and over

Two verbs in the ます-stem, each followed by `つ`, describe a pair of actions that keep
trading places: going and coming back, overtaking and being overtaken, giving and being
given. The `つ` is an Old Japanese auxiliary, and the construction stopped being
productive centuries ago. What survives is a short list of fixed expressions.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two opposite verbs | ます-stem + つ + ます-stem + つ | 行(い)く・戻(もど)る → 行(い)きつ戻(もど)りつ |
| Active + its passive | ます-stem + つ + ～れ + つ | 抜(ぬ)く・抜(ぬ)かれる → 抜(ぬ)きつ抜(ぬ)かれつ |
| Used adverbially | ～つ～つ + verb | 押(お)しつ押(お)されつ進(すす)む |
| Used as a predicate | ～つ～つ + だ／する | 持(も)ちつ持(も)たれつだ |
| Modifying a noun | ～つ～つ + の + Noun | 抜(ぬ)きつ抜(ぬ)かれつの展開(てんかい) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in practice you will meet perhaps six of these and no more:
持(も)ちつ持(も)たれつ、行(い)きつ戻(もど)りつ、抜(ぬ)きつ抜(ぬ)かれつ、追(お)いつ追(お)われつ、差(さ)しつ差(さ)されつ、浮(う)きつ沈(しず)みつ、押(お)しつ押(お)されつ.
A related shape with `ん` instead of `つ` on the first verb survives in one idiom,
組(く)んずほぐれつ, "grappling".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
世(よ)の中(なか)は<b>持(も)ちつ持(も)たれつ</b>だ。
The world runs on give and take.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は廊下(ろうか)を<b>行(い)きつ戻(もど)りつ</b>していた。
He was pacing up and down the corridor.
</pre>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)は<b>差(さ)しつ差(さ)されつ</b>、夜(よる)遅(おそ)くまで飲(の)んでいた。
The two of them filled each other's cups and drank late into the night.
</pre>

<pre>
最後(さいご)の一周(いっしゅう)は<b>抜(ぬ)きつ抜(ぬ)かれつ</b>の展開(てんかい)となった。
The last lap turned into a neck-and-neck contest.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)ごみの中(なか)を<b>押(お)しつ押(お)されつ</b>改札(かいさつ)まで進(すす)んだ。
Shoving and being shoved, I worked my way through the crowd to the ticket gate.
</pre>

<pre>
小(ちい)さな舟(ふね)が波間(なみま)に<b>浮(う)きつ沈(しず)みつ</b>見(み)え隠(かく)れしている。
A small boat rides up and down between the waves, appearing and vanishing again.
</pre>

<pre>
同業(どうぎょう)同士(どうし)、<b>持(も)ちつ持(も)たれつ</b>でやってきた間柄(あいだがら)です。
We're in the same line of work, and we've kept each other going all these years.
</pre>

<pre>
先頭(せんとう)集団(しゅうだん)は<b>追(お)いつ追(お)われつ</b>、最終(さいしゅう)コーナーに差(さ)し掛(か)かった。
The leading group, trading places at the front, came into the final corner.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is an archaic form, and it is not productive.** You cannot build new ones. There
is no ❌<code>食(た)べつ飲(の)みつ</code>, no ❌<code>書(か)きつ消(け)しつ</code>;
inventing a pair does not sound literary, it sounds like a mistake. Learn the handful
that exist, recognise them on the page, and say
<code>～たり～たり</code> when you need the meaning yourself.

Where the survivors actually live:

- **持(も)ちつ持(も)たれつ** is the one genuine exception. It is a live idiom,
  used in ordinary adult speech, and means "we help each other out" - said of business
  partners, neighbours, in-laws. If you use one expression from this page, use this one.
- **抜(ぬ)きつ抜(ぬ)かれつ** and **追(お)いつ追(お)われつ** belong to sports
  commentary and race reporting, where they are close to cliché but entirely current.
- **行(い)きつ戻(もど)りつ**, **浮(う)きつ沈(しず)みつ**,
  **押(お)しつ押(お)されつ** are narrative prose - a novel, a memoir, a piece of
  travel writing. They describe a body moving, and a reader hears them as slightly
  written-up.
- **差(さ)しつ差(さ)されつ** is a set description of a drinking scene, most often
  with sake and most often in writing about the past.

The `つ` here is unrelated to the counter or to the `つ` of
<code>一(ひと)つ</code>. It is the perfective auxiliary of classical Japanese, the
same one behind <code>果(は)てつ</code> in old texts, and the alternating
<code>～つ～つ</code> shape is one of the few places it survives in modern Japanese at
all. That history is worth knowing only because it explains why the pattern is closed:
there is no living rule left to apply.

## Contrast

### ～つ～つ vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～たり～たり</code> is the modern equivalent and is what you
should produce. It attaches to any verbs at all, in any register.

</div>

<pre>
部屋(へや)の中(なか)を<b>行(い)ったり来(き)たり</b>しながら、返事(へんじ)を考(かんが)えた。
I walked back and forth across the room while I thought about what to reply.
</pre>

<pre>
老人(ろうじん)は窓辺(まどべ)を<b>行(い)きつ戻(もど)りつ</b>していた。
The old man moved back and forth by the window.
</pre>

The content is identical; the second sentence has been written rather than said. Note
also that <code>～たり～たり</code> can list more than two actions and does not need them
to be opposites - <code>読(よ)んだり書(か)いたりする</code> is simply "read, write
and so on". <code>～つ～つ</code> is locked to exactly two, and they must be a pair.

If you are ever tempted to reach for <code>～つ～つ</code> in your own Japanese, this is
the substitution to make.

### ～つ～つ vs ～ては～、～ては～

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～ては</code> repeats a **cycle** and often carries frustration
or futility. <code>～つ～つ</code> just reports the alternation, with no attitude.

</div>

<pre>
書(か)い<b>ては</b>消(け)し、書(か)い<b>ては</b>消(け)しして、一晩(ひとばん)かかってしまった。
I wrote and deleted, wrote and deleted, and it took me the whole night.
</pre>

<pre>
波(なみ)にもまれて、木(き)の葉(は)が<b>浮(う)きつ沈(しず)みつ</b>流(なが)れていった。
Tossed by the waves, the leaf floated away, bobbing under and up again.
</pre>

<code>～ては</code> is fully modern, fully productive, and usable in speech; it is the
pattern to use when you want to say that something kept happening and got nowhere.
<code>～つ～つ</code> has no such colour. It is a camera, not a complaint.

### ～つ～つ vs ～つつ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they look alike and mean different things. <code>～つつ</code> after
**one** ます-stem is a formal "while". <code>～つ～つ</code> needs **two** verbs and means
"alternately".

</div>

<pre>
危険(きけん)だと知(し)り<b>つつ</b>、彼(かれ)は扉(とびら)を開(あ)けた。
Knowing it was dangerous, he opened the door all the same.
</pre>

<pre>
両者(りょうしゃ)は終盤(しゅうばん)まで<b>追(お)いつ追(お)われつ</b>の接戦(せっせん)を演(えん)じた。
The two of them fought out a close contest right to the end, each in turn ahead of the other.
</pre>

<code>～つつ</code> is also literary, but it is *productive* - you can attach it to any
verb and be understood, and <code>～つつある</code> ("is in the process of") is
completely standard in news writing. <code>～つ～つ</code> can do neither. Seeing a single
`つ` followed by another `つ` immediately means "while"; seeing `つ` after each of two
different verb stems means alternation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べつ飲(の)みつ話(はな)した。 → ✅ 食(た)べたり飲(の)んだりしながら話(はな)した。
  The pattern is closed. Coining a new pair does not produce literary Japanese; it
  produces something a reader will stop and puzzle over.

- ❌ 持(も)ちつ<b>持(も)ちつ</b>の関係(かんけい) → ✅ 持(も)ちつ<b>持(も)たれつ</b>の関係(かんけい)
  The second verb is the **passive**. The idiom means "holding up and being held up", so
  repeating the active form destroys the sense.

- ❌ 行(い)くつ戻(もど)るつ → ✅ 行(い)きつ戻(もど)りつ
  `つ` follows the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

- ❌ 歩(ある)きつ考(かんが)えつ答(こた)えを探(さが)した。 → ✅ 歩(ある)き<b>つつ</b>答(こた)えを探(さが)した。
  Walking and thinking are not opposites and do not alternate; they overlap. That is
  <code>～つつ</code>, with the doubled `つ` on a single verb.

## Practice

**1.** Complete the set phrase: 困(こま)ったときはお互(たが)いさま、持(も)ちつ______でいきましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

持(も)ちつ<b>持(も)たれつ</b>でいきましょう。

The second half of the idiom is the **passive** 持(も)たれ, giving "supporting and
being supported". Paired with
<code>お互(たが)いさま</code> it is the standard way of waving off a debt of gratitude
between people who expect to go on helping each other.

</details>

**2.** Where would you expect to hear or read this, and why? 両(りょう)チームは最終(さいしゅう)回(かい)まで<b>追(お)いつ追(お)われつ</b>の熱戦(ねっせん)を繰(く)り広(ひろ)げた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A sports report - a newspaper match summary, a broadcast wrap-up, a club newsletter.
<code>抜(ぬ)きつ抜(ぬ)かれつ</code> and
<code>追(お)いつ追(お)われつ</code> are near-fixed expressions in that genre, and
<code>熱戦(ねっせん)を繰(く)り広(ひろ)げる</code> is from the same stock of
phrases. Nobody describes their weekend five-a-side this way in conversation.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 少年(しょうねん)は門(もん)の前(まえ)を行(い)きつ戻(もど)りつしていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

少年(しょうねん)は門(もん)の前(まえ)を<b>行(い)ったり来(き)たりしていた</b>。

<code>～たり～たり</code> is the everyday equivalent, and
<code>行(い)ったり来(き)たり</code> is its own fixed pair for pacing. Note the
reading: 来(き)たり from 来(く)る, not こ-. You could also say
<code>門(もん)の前(まえ)をうろうろしていた</code>, which adds the restlessness the
original only implies.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は友達(ともだち)と飲(の)みつ食(た)べつ、楽(たの)しい夜(よる)だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は友達(ともだち)と<b>飲(の)んだり食(た)べたり</b>して、楽(たの)しい夜(よる)だった。

Eating and drinking are not an opposed pair, and in any case the pattern admits no new
members. The intended meaning is a list of things you did, which is
<code>～たり～たり</code>. If you wanted the drinking-party image specifically, the
existing idiom is <code>差(さ)しつ差(さ)されつ</code> - though that would put the
sentence into a novel.

</details>

**5.** Two sentences describe the same scene. What has changed?
(a) 舟(ふね)は波(なみ)に<b>浮(う)きつ沈(しず)みつ</b>沖(おき)へ離(はな)れていった。
(b) 舟(ふね)は波(なみ)で<b>浮(う)いたり沈(しず)んだり</b>しながら沖(おき)へ離(はな)れていった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Nothing in the facts. (a) is narration: the compressed
<code>浮(う)きつ沈(しず)みつ</code> and the omission of しながら make it read like a
line from a novel, and the reader takes it as the narrator's voice rather than a
speaker's.

(b) is how the same thing is described out loud, or in a diary, or in a news bulletin.
It is longer, looser and completely neutral. A learner should be able to read (a) and
write (b), and that division of labour is what a recognise-only pattern is for.

</details>

# ～と相まって

**Meaning** · combined with, and amplified by - two conditions working on each other to produce an effect neither would have produced alone

The verb behind it is 相(あい)まつ, "to act together with". <code>A と相(あい)まって B</code>
says that A and B reinforced one another and that the result was
therefore larger, stronger or more striking. The sentence always has to end in a
**result**; without one it stops halfway. This is analytical prose, and it is not
spoken.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + と相(あい)まって | 円安(えんやす) → 円安(えんやす)と相(あい)まって |
| Two named factors | AがBと相(あい)まって | 猛暑(もうしょ)が水不足(みずぶそく)と相(あい)まって |
| Both under one subject | AとBが相(あい)まって | 技術(ぎじゅつ)と経験(けいけん)が相(あい)まって |
| Followed by | a clause stating the **result** | …効果(こうか)を高(たか)めている |
| Written also as | 相俟(あいま)って | 好天(こうてん)と相俟(あいま)って |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both slots take **conditions**: weather, prices, a shortage, a
quality, a policy, a colour. Not people, and not deliberate acts.
❌<code>友人(ゆうじん)と相(あい)まって出(で)かけた</code> is not a stiff way of
saying "I went out with a friend"; it is not Japanese.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
円安(えんやす)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、訪日(ほうにち)客(きゃく)の消費(しょうひ)額(がく)は過去(かこ)最高(さいこう)となった。
Together with the weak yen, spending by visitors to Japan reached a record high.
</pre>

<pre>
猛暑(もうしょ)が水不足(みずぶそく)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、農作物(のうさくもつ)への影響(えいきょう)が広(ひろ)がっている。
The extreme heat, compounded by the water shortage, is doing spreading damage to crops.
</pre>

<pre>
古(ふる)い町(まち)並(な)みが川(かわ)の景色(けしき)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、独特(どくとく)の風情(ふぜい)を生(う)んでいる。
The old streets, set against the river, give the place a character all its own.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かな音色(ねいろ)が歌詞(かし)の切(せつ)なさと<b>相(あい)まって</b>、聴(き)く者(もの)の胸(むね)を打(う)つ。
The quiet tone, reinforced by the ache in the lyrics, goes straight to the listener.
</pre>

<pre>
原材料(げんざいりょう)の高騰(こうとう)が人手不足(ひとでぶそく)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、外食(がいしょく)産業(さんぎょう)の経営(けいえい)を圧迫(あっぱく)している。
Soaring raw-material costs, together with the labour shortage, are squeezing the restaurant trade.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)量(りょう)と本人(ほんにん)の冷静(れいせい)さが<b>相(あい)まって</b>、見事(みごと)な優勝(ゆうしょう)につながった。
The sheer volume of training and her own composure came together to produce a fine championship win.
</pre>

<pre>
交通(こうつう)の便(べん)のよさが周辺(しゅうへん)の再開発(さいかいはつ)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、この地区(ちく)の地価(ちか)は大(おお)きく上昇(じょうしょう)した。
Good transport links, along with the redevelopment nearby, have pushed land prices in the district sharply higher.
</pre>

<pre>
高(たか)い天井(てんじょう)が白(しろ)い壁(かべ)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、部屋(へや)を実際(じっさい)より広(ひろ)く見(み)せている。
The high ceiling, together with the white walls, makes the room look larger than it is.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This belongs to written Japanese, and nowhere else.** It is the vocabulary of the
newspaper economics page, the culture review, the tourist-board brochure, the estate
agent's listing, the academic article. Said aloud at a dinner table it would land as a
parody of a news reader. Recognise it, and when you want the meaning yourself, say
<code>～も重(かさ)なって</code>, <code>～も加(くわ)わって</code> or simply
<code>それに</code>.

The reason it survives so strongly in that narrow home is that it does a job the plain
additives cannot: it claims **interaction**. Two factors listed with
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> are simply both present.
<code>と相(あい)まって</code> asserts that they worked on each other, and that the
outcome is greater than the sum. That is a real analytical claim, which is why editors
like it and why it is so common in sentences explaining a record figure or an unusual
success.

It leans **positive or at least impressive**, though it is not restricted to good news -
the crop-damage and cost-squeeze examples above are perfectly standard. What it cannot
do is sound casual. There is no contracted form, no spoken variant, and no polite
adjustment: the register is fixed by the word itself, and the surrounding sentence has
to match it.

Two mechanical points. The particle is `と`, never `に`, because 相(あい)まつ takes a
partner marked with `と`. And the older spelling 相俟(あいま)って turns up in
literary and legal text; it is the same word, and reading it is all that is required.

## Contrast

### ～と相(あい)まって vs ～も重(かさ)なって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same idea, ordinary register.
<code>～も重(かさ)なって</code> is what a person says; <code>と相(あい)まって</code>
is what a paper prints.

</div>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)に好天(こうてん)<b>も重(かさ)なって</b>、どこも人(ひと)でいっぱいだった。
The long weekend plus the good weather meant everywhere was packed.
</pre>

<pre>
大型(おおがた)連休(れんきゅう)が好天(こうてん)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、各地(かくち)の観光地(かんこうち)は例年(れいねん)を上回(うわまわ)る人出(ひとで)となった。
The long holiday, combined with fine weather, brought more visitors to resorts around the country than in an average year.
</pre>

The content is the same. The second sentence has additionally chosen
<code>大型(おおがた)連休(れんきゅう)</code> over <code>連休(れんきゅう)</code>,
<code>人出(ひとで)</code> over <code>人(ひと)</code>, and a
<code>となった</code> ending - which is the point. Register is not carried by one word;
if you use <code>と相(あい)まって</code> the rest of the sentence has to rise to meet
it, and that is exactly the trap for a learner who drops it into casual Japanese.

### ～と相(あい)まって vs ～に加(くわ)えて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に加(くわ)えて</code> merely **adds**.
<code>と相(あい)まって</code> claims the two things **worked on each other**.

</div>

<pre>
会費(かいひ)に<b>加(くわ)えて</b>、資料(しりょう)代(だい)が2000円(えん)かかります。
On top of the membership fee there is a 2,000-yen charge for materials.
</pre>

<pre>
軽(かる)さが握(にぎ)りやすい形状(けいじょう)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、長時間(ちょうじかん)の作業(さぎょう)でも疲(つか)れにくい。
Its light weight, together with a shape that sits well in the hand, keeps fatigue down over long periods of use.
</pre>

You cannot swap them freely. Fees do not amplify each other, so
❌<code>会費(かいひ)と相(あい)まって資料(しりょう)代(だい)がかかる</code> is
odd. Conversely <code>に加(くわ)えて</code> in the second sentence would be
grammatical but would throw away the claim the copywriter is making - that the lightness
and the grip *combine* into comfort.

The practical test: could you replace the phrase with "and also"? Then it is
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code>. Does it need "and because of the two together"? Then it is
<code>と相(あい)まって</code>.

### ～と相(あい)まって vs ～も手伝(てつだ)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>も手伝(てつだ)って</code> names a factor that **helped
along** something already under way, and is often a small or incidental one.
<code>と相(あい)まって</code> treats the two factors as **equals**.

</div>

<pre>
寝不足(ねぶそく)<b>も手伝(てつだ)って</b>、会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)に集中(しゅうちゅう)できなかった。
Not helped by the lack of sleep, I couldn't concentrate in the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
職人(しょくにん)の技術(ぎじゅつ)が土地(とち)の気候(きこう)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、この地方(ちほう)独自(どくじ)の味(あじ)を作(つく)り出(だ)している。
The craftsmen's skill, working with the local climate, produces a flavour peculiar to this region.
</pre>

<code>も手伝(てつだ)って</code> is usable in speech, frequently about the speaker's own
state - tiredness, nerves, the drink - and it has a faintly excusing tone.
<code>と相(あい)まって</code> never excuses anything and never puts one factor below
the other; both are named as causes of equal standing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 友人(ゆうじん)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>旅行(りょこう)に行(い)った。 → ✅ 友人(ゆうじん)<b>と一緒(いっしょ)に</b>旅行(りょこう)に行(い)った。
  The pattern joins conditions, not companions. The English "together with" is what
  misleads people here.

- ❌ 円安(えんやす)が原油高(げんゆだか)と<b>相(あい)まっている</b>。 → ✅ 円安(えんやす)が原油高(げんゆだか)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、物価(ぶっか)を押(お)し上(あ)げている。
  It is a conjunctive: the sentence has to go on and say what the two factors produced.
  Stopping at the combination leaves the reader waiting.

- ❌ 好天(こうてん)<b>に相(あい)まって</b> → ✅ 好天(こうてん)<b>と相(あい)まって</b>
  相(あい)まつ takes its partner with `と`, the same `と` as
  <code>一緒(いっしょ)に</code> or <code>結(むす)ぶ</code>.

- ❌ このカレー、辛(から)さが甘(あま)みと<b>相(あい)まって</b>うまいね。 → ✅ このカレー、辛(から)さと甘(あま)みが<b>ちょうどいい</b>ね。
  Grammatically defensible, socially impossible. Analytical vocabulary in a casual remark
  about dinner makes the speaker sound as if they were filing copy.

## Practice

**1.** 技術(ぎじゅつ)の進歩(しんぽ)が価格(かかく)の低下(ていか)____、この製品(せいひん)は急速(きゅうそく)に普及(ふきゅう)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

価格(かかく)の低下(ていか)<b>と相(あい)まって</b>

Two conditions - better technology and falling prices - working on each other to
produce rapid adoption. Note the particle `と`, and note that the sentence goes on to
name the result; both are required.

</details>

**2.** Choose the one that fits the register: a news article about a record tourist season.
（a）円安(えんやす)と相(あい)まって、宿泊(しゅくはく)需要(じゅよう)が急増(きゅうぞう)した。
（b）円安(えんやす)もあって、宿泊(しゅくはく)需要(じゅよう)がめっちゃ増(ふ)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（a）.

Both say the same thing. （b）is spoken Japanese -
<code>もあって</code> and especially <code>めっちゃ</code> - and would never appear in a
news article. The lesson runs the other way too: put （a）into conversation and it is
just as wrong, which is why this pattern is one to read rather than to say.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite for a friend, in ordinary spoken Japanese: 連休(れんきゅう)が好天(こうてん)と相(あい)まって、海岸(かいがん)沿(ぞ)いの道(みち)は大変(たいへん)な渋滞(じゅうたい)となった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

連休(れんきゅう)だったし天気(てんき)もよかったから、海岸(かいがん)沿(ぞ)いの道(みち)、すごく混(こ)んでたよ。

The two factors become a plain <code>し</code> list,
<code>好天(こうてん)</code> becomes <code>天気(てんき)がいい</code>, and
<code>渋滞(じゅうたい)となった</code> becomes <code>混(こ)んでた</code>. Notice how
much else had to change: the pattern drags a whole register with it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)の努力(どりょく)は周(まわ)りの支(ささ)えに相(あい)まっている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)の努力(どりょく)<b>が</b>周(まわ)りの支(ささ)え<b>と相(あい)まって</b>、大(おお)きな成果(せいか)につながった。

Two repairs. The partner is marked with `と`, not `に`. And
<code>相(あい)まっている</code> cannot end the sentence - the pattern exists to
introduce a result, so a consequence clause has to follow.

</details>

**5.** Explain what is being claimed differently in each:
(a) 立地(りっち)のよさ<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、家賃(やちん)も手(て)ごろだ。
(b) 立地(りっち)のよさが手(て)ごろな家賃(やちん)と<b>相(あい)まって</b>、申(もう)し込(こ)みが殺到(さっとう)している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) lists two selling points side by side. Location is good; rent is also reasonable.
No claim is made about the two together, and the sentence could stop there.

(b) says the two features multiplied each other and that this is why applications are
flooding in. It commits to a causal account, and it therefore requires the result clause
that (a) does without. The second is also unmistakably written - a property listing or a
market report, not something a friend would say about a flat.

</details>

# ～ままに

**Meaning** · just as it tells you to - doing whatever someone's words, or your own impulse, happen to dictate, without putting any will of your own in the way

<code>まま</code> is the noun "the state of things as they are". With `に` in front of a
verb, it hands the steering over: the clause before it names what is doing the
directing - being told, being urged, an impulse, a whim - and the main clause is what
you then did. Nobody is deciding. That absence of decision is the whole meaning.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (passive), commonest | ～れる／られる + ままに | 言(い)われる → 言(い)われるままに |
| Verb (plain non-past) | dictionary form + ままに | 向(む)く → 気(き)の向(む)くままに |
| Noun | ～の + ままに | 意(い) → 意(い)のままに |
| `に` dropped | ～まま + clause | 勧(すす)められるまま買(か)ってしまった |
| Set phrases | - | 足(あし)の向(む)くままに、心(こころ)の赴(おもむ)くままに、思(おも)うままに |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front is **non-past**. A past-tense verb before
<code>まま</code> is the other, far commoner pattern - the one that means "leaving it as
it was", as in <code>靴(くつ)を履(は)いたまま</code>. Tense is what separates them,
so check it before anything else.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
言(い)われる<b>ままに</b>書類(しょるい)にサインしてしまった。
I signed the papers just as I was told to.
</pre>

<pre>
店員(てんいん)に勧(すす)められる<b>ままに</b>、高(たか)いコートを買(か)ってしまった。
The shop assistant kept pushing it and I ended up buying an expensive coat.
</pre>

<pre>
気(き)の向(む)く<b>ままに</b>歩(ある)いていたら、知(し)らない路地(ろじ)に出(で)た。
I was wandering wherever the mood took me and came out in an alley I didn't know.
</pre>

<pre>
定年(ていねん)のあと、父(ちち)は足(あし)の向(む)く<b>ままに</b>旅(たび)を続(つづ)けている。
Since retiring, my father has gone on travelling wherever his feet take him.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は長年(ながねん)、組織(そしき)を意(い)の<b>ままに</b>動(うご)かしてきた。
For years he ran the organisation exactly as he pleased.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)に指示(しじ)される<b>ままに</b>動(うご)いているだけでは、いつまでも力(ちから)がつかない。
If all you ever do is move when your boss tells you to, you'll never build any real ability.
</pre>

<pre>
求(もと)められる<b>ままに</b>個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)を渡(わた)してしまい、あとで大変(たいへん)なことになった。
I handed over my personal details whenever they were asked for, and it caused me serious trouble later.
</pre>

<pre>
心(こころ)の赴(おもむ)く<b>ままに</b>筆(ふで)を走(はし)らせた、飾(かざ)り気(け)のない一冊(いっさつ)である。
It is an unaffected book, written wherever the author's heart happened to lead him.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>ままに</code> is **written-leaning but current**. It appears in novels, essays,
newspaper features and consumer-advice columns, and it is unremarkable in careful
speech; a person warning a friend about a pushy salesman really would say
<code>勧(すす)められるまま買(か)わないほうがいいよ</code>, usually with the `に`
dropped. It is not archaic and it is not stiff. What it is, always, is
**judgemental about agency**.

Two quite different colours, decided by what is doing the directing.

**Another person telling you.** With a passive verb -
<code>言(い)われる</code>, <code>勧(すす)められる</code>,
<code>求(もと)められる</code>, <code>指示(しじ)される</code> - the sentence says the
subject had no will of their own, and it usually carries regret or mild criticism. Note
how often it appears with <code>てしまった</code>: the outcome was bad, and the reason
was that nobody was thinking. Applied to yourself it is a confession; applied to someone
else it is a complaint.

**Your own impulse.** With 気(き), 足(あし), 心(こころ) and a verb of turning or
going, the same absence of a plan becomes freedom.
<code>気(き)の向(む)くままに</code> is pleasant and slightly lyrical, at home in
travel writing and in the way people describe a good day off.

**意(い)のままに and 思(おも)うままに** are a third case, and they invert the
subject. Here the will belongs to the person doing the acting, and the pattern means
"at will, with nothing in the way". <code>思(おも)うままに生(い)きる</code> is
admiring; <code>意(い)のままに操(あやつ)る</code> is usually sinister. The
grammatical shape is identical, so read the noun to know which you have.

The `に` is optional and dropping it is slightly more conversational. Both
<code>言(い)われるままに</code> and <code>言(い)われるまま</code> are correct.

## Contrast

### ～ままに vs ～たまま

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **non-past** verb + ままに = "as it directs".
**Past** verb + まま = "left in that state". One word, two patterns, and the tense is
the whole signal.

</div>

<pre>
テレビを<b>つけたまま</b>眠(ねむ)ってしまった。
I fell asleep with the television left on.
</pre>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)に<b>言(い)われるままに</b>薬(くすり)を飲(の)み続(つづ)けている。
I just keep taking the medicine, however the doctor tells me to.
</pre>

In the first, an action finished and its result was left untouched. In the second,
nothing is being left anywhere - somebody is issuing instructions and the subject is
following them. Swap the tenses and both sentences change meaning rather than becoming
wrong, which is why this is a genuinely dangerous pair.
<code>言(い)われたまま</code> would mean something closer to "still in the state
described", not "as instructed".

### ～ままに vs ～とおりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とおりに</code> = **accurately, as specified**, and is neutral
or approving. <code>ままに</code> = **passively, without judgement of my own**, and is
usually not.

</div>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)に書(か)いてある<b>とおりに</b>組(く)み立(た)てれば大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です。
As long as you assemble it the way the instructions say, you'll be fine.
</pre>

<pre>
よく考(かんが)えずに、友人(ゆうじん)に言(い)われる<b>ままに</b>契約(けいやく)してしまった。
Without thinking it through, I signed the contract just because a friend told me to.
</pre>

Following a manual correctly is a skill, and <code>とおりに</code> credits you with it.
<code>ままに</code> credits you with nothing: it says the instruction went in and the
action came out with no processing in between. That is why an instruction manual takes
<code>とおりに</code> and a story about being talked into something takes
<code>ままに</code>, and why swapping them changes how the speaker looks.

<code>とおりに</code> also takes a much wider range of sources - a plan, a forecast, a
memory, a recipe - while <code>ままに</code> in this sense wants a person doing the
telling.

### ～ままに vs ～に任(まか)せて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に任(まか)せて</code> means you **handed control over**, and
often names a resource being spent freely - time, money, force.
<code>ままに</code> just reports that something else was steering.

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)に<b>任(まか)せて</b>、古(ふる)い写真(しゃしん)を一枚(いちまい)ずつ整理(せいり)していった。
With time to spare, I worked through the old photographs one at a time.
</pre>

<pre>
休日(きゅうじつ)は気(き)の向(む)く<b>ままに</b>本屋(ほんや)を回(まわ)るのが楽(たの)しみだ。
On days off I enjoy drifting round the bookshops as the mood takes me.
</pre>

The pair to be careful with is <code>力(ちから)に任(まか)せて</code>, "using brute
force", which has no <code>ままに</code> equivalent, and
<code>足(あし)の向(む)くままに</code>, which has no
<code>任(まか)せて</code> equivalent. Where both are possible -
<code>気分(きぶん)に任(まか)せて</code> against
<code>気(き)の向(む)くままに</code> - the first sounds more like a decision to stop
deciding, the second like never having decided at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 店員(てんいん)が言(い)う<b>ままに</b>買(か)った。 → ✅ 店員(てんいん)に言(い)われる<b>ままに</b>買(か)った。
  When someone else is doing the telling, the verb has to be **passive**. The active form
  would make the shop assistant the one being directed.

- ❌ 窓(まど)を開(あ)ける<b>ままに</b>寝(ね)てしまった。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けた<b>まま</b>寝(ね)てしまった。
  A window left open is the "unchanged state" pattern: past-tense verb, and no `に`.

- ❌ 意(い)<b>ままに</b>使(つか)う → ✅ 意(い)の<b>ままに</b>使(つか)う
  <code>まま</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`. The same applies to
  <code>思(おも)いのまま</code> and <code>心(こころ)のまま</code>.

- ❌ レシピに書(か)かれる<b>ままに</b>作(つく)ったら、うまくできた。 → ✅ レシピに書(か)いてある<b>とおりに</b>作(つく)ったら、うまくできた。
  Following a recipe on purpose and getting a good result is <code>とおりに</code>.
  <code>ままに</code> would imply the cook surrendered their judgement, which sits oddly
  with a success.

## Practice

**1.** 不動産屋(ふどうさんや)に勧(すす)められる____契約(けいやく)してしまい、あとで後悔(こうかい)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

勧(すす)められる<b>ままに</b>契約(けいやく)してしまい

The passive <code>勧(すす)められる</code> plus <code>ままに</code> plus
<code>てしまう</code> is the classic three-part shape for "I went along with it and it
was a mistake". Dropping the `に` - <code>勧(すす)められるまま</code> - is equally
correct and a shade more conversational.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部屋(へや)の電気(でんき)を（つけるままに・つけたまま）出(で)かけてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電気(でんき)を<b>つけたまま</b>出(で)かけてしまった。

Nothing is directing anybody here; a light was switched on and then left that way. That
is the past-tense <code>まま</code>, with no `に`.
<code>つけるままに</code> would try to say "as the light instructs", which is not a
meaning available to a light switch.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I handed over the money exactly as the man on the phone told me to."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電話(でんわ)の相手(あいて)に言(い)われる<b>ままに</b>、お金(かね)を渡(わた)してしまった。

<code>言(い)われるままに</code> catches "exactly as told" together with the fact that
the speaker was not thinking - which is the point of the sentence. Adding
<code>てしまった</code> supplies the regret that the English "exactly as" only hints at.
<code>言(い)われたとおりに</code> would be too neutral, as if the instruction had been
reasonable.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 課長(かちょう)が指示(しじ)するままに資料(しりょう)を作(つく)り直(なお)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 課長(かちょう)に指示(しじ)<b>される</b>ままに資料(しりょう)を作(つく)り直(なお)した。

The subject of the sentence is the person doing the redrafting, so the instruction has to
reach them in the passive, with the section chief marked by `に`. Written actively, the
sentence claims the section chief was the one following orders.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 地図(ちず)も見(み)ずに、気(き)の向(む)く<b>ままに</b>町(まち)を歩(ある)いた。
(b) 地図(ちず)も見(み)ずに、ガイドに案内(あんない)される<b>ままに</b>町(まち)を歩(ある)いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is pleasant. The thing doing the directing is the speaker's own mood, so having no
plan reads as freedom, and the sentence belongs to a good afternoon or a travel essay.

(b) is not. Here the direction comes from another person, and the passive
<code>案内(あんない)される</code> makes the speaker a parcel being carried around. The
listener will expect the next sentence to say that the tour was disappointing, or that
they never found out where they had been. Same grammar, opposite feeling, and the
difference lies entirely in whose impulse is in charge.

</details>

# ～ところを

**Meaning** · at a moment when you were in the middle of something else - and I am about to impose on it, or something is about to happen to it

<code>ところ</code> is a point in time, and `を` makes that point the **thing acted
upon**. Two very different sentences come out of that one idea: the set greeting that
apologises for interrupting someone (<code>お忙(いそが)しいところを</code>), and the
narrative line where somebody is caught, stopped or rescued at exactly the wrong or
right moment.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | ～い + ところを | お忙(いそが)しい → お忙(いそが)しいところを |
| Noun | ～の + ところを | お休(やす)み → お休(やす)みのところを |
| な-adjective | ～な + ところを | 大変(たいへん)な → 大変(たいへん)なところをお邪魔(じゃま)しました |
| Verb (ている) | ている + ところを | 寝(ね)ている → 寝(ね)ているところを |
| Verb (on the point of) | ～ようとした + ところを | 逃(に)げようとした → 逃(に)げようとしたところを |
| Concessive | ～べき + ところを | 伺(うかが)うべき → 伺(うかが)うべきところを |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - whatever follows has to be able to take that moment as its
**object**. Thanks, an apology or a request in the greeting use
(<code>ありがとうございます／申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません／恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが</code>);
a verb of catching, seeing, stopping or saving in the narrative use, usually in the
passive.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しい<b>ところを</b>、お時間(じかん)をいただきありがとうございます。
Thank you for making time for me when you're so busy.
</pre>

<pre>
お休(やす)みの<b>ところを</b>申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。
I'm sorry to trouble you on your day off.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)中(ちゅう)に居(い)眠(ねむ)りしている<b>ところを</b>先生(せんせい)に見(み)られた。
The teacher saw me dozing off in class.
</pre>

<pre>
塀(へい)を乗(の)り越(こ)えようとした<b>ところを</b>警備員(けいびいん)に取(と)り押(お)さえられた。
He was climbing over the wall when a security guard grabbed him.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あや)うく川(かわ)に落(お)ちる<b>ところを</b>、通(とお)りがかりの人(ひと)に助(たす)けられた。
I was on the point of falling into the river, and a passer-by pulled me back.
</pre>

<pre>
ご多用(たよう)の<b>ところを</b>恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、添付(てんぷ)の資料(しりょう)をご確認(かくにん)いただけますでしょうか。
I'm sorry to bother you when you have so much on, but could you look over the attached document?
</pre>

<pre>
本来(ほんらい)なら私(わたし)のほうから伺(うかが)うべき<b>ところを</b>、お越(こ)しいただき恐縮(きょうしゅく)です。
I should have come to you, and instead you have made the journey - I'm most grateful.
</pre>

<pre>
定価(ていか)は一万円(いちまんえん)の<b>ところを</b>、半額(はんがく)で譲(ゆず)ってもらった。
The list price is ten thousand yen, but they let me have it for half.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The greeting use is **formal, extremely common, and worth producing**. It is standard at
the top of a business email, at the start of a meeting, in a phone call to a client, and
in the sentence you say when you knock on someone's door. Learn
<code>お忙(いそが)しいところを恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが</code> and
<code>お休(やす)みのところを申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません</code> as units; they
will carry a lot of weight. Note that the `を` is often simply dropped -
<code>お忙(いそが)しいところありがとうございます</code> - with no change of meaning
and a slight drop in formality.

What the greeting does is acknowledge a **cost to the other person**. That is why it
does not work downward or sideways among friends: telling a classmate you are sorry to
disturb their busy schedule sounds sarcastic. Between equals, say
<code>今(いま)いい？</code>.

The narrative use is register-neutral and mostly appears in the **past tense**, very
often with a passive main verb. The reason is structural: the ところ is the object, so
the person to whom the moment belongs stays the subject of the sentence -
<code>見(み)られた</code>, <code>捕(つか)まえられた</code>,
<code>助(たす)けられた</code>, <code>呼(よ)び止(と)められた</code>. The events
themselves are nearly always unwelcome - being caught, being seen, being interrupted -
with rescue as the standing exception.

The third use, <code>～べきところを</code> and
<code>Noun のところを</code>, marks a **departure from what was proper or expected**. In
apologies it is a fixed humble move: you name the correct course of action and then
report that things went otherwise. In shops and advertising the same shape does price
comparisons - <code>通常(つうじょう)3,000円(えん)のところを</code> - which is the
one place you will meet it in casual reading.

## Contrast

### ～ところを vs ～ところに・～ところへ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the particle says what role the moment plays.
<code>ところに／へ</code> = something **arrives into** that moment.
<code>ところを</code> = someone **acts on** you in that moment.

</div>

<pre>
出(で)かけようとした<b>ところに</b>、宅配便(たくはいびん)が届(とど)いた。
Just as I was about to leave, a delivery arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
出(で)かけようとした<b>ところを</b>、母(はは)に呼(よ)び止(と)められた。
Just as I was about to leave, my mother called me back.
</pre>

In the first, the parcel is the newcomer and the moment is its destination. In the
second, the moment itself is what the mother acted upon, so it takes `を` and the main
verb is transitive or passive. A quick test: if the main verb is intransitive and
something *turns up*, use `に` or `へ`. If someone *does something to you*, use `を`.

Note also that <code>～たところで</code> is a different pattern altogether, meaning "even
if you do", and shares nothing with these but the word.

### ～ところを vs ～中(なか)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the greeting formulas the two are close.
<code>お忙(いそが)しい中(なか)</code> looks at a **stretch** of busy time;
<code>お忙(いそが)しいところを</code> looks at the **point** you are being interrupted
at.

</div>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しい<b>中(なか)</b>、お集(あつ)まりいただきありがとうございました。
Thank you all for coming despite your busy schedules.
</pre>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しい<b>ところを</b>恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、少(すこ)しだけお時間(じかん)をいただけますか。
I'm sorry to catch you at a busy time, but could you spare me a few minutes?
</pre>

There is a tendency rather than a rule here.
<code>中(なか)</code> is what you hear in a speech to a room of people who all made
the effort to attend; <code>ところを</code> is what you say to one person whose
particular afternoon you are about to eat into. In many sentences either passes, and
choosing the wrong one is not an error a listener would notice.

### ～べきところを vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mark a gap between what should have happened and what did.
`のに` **complains**; <code>べきところを</code> **acknowledges**, and is what you use
about your own failing.

</div>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)に確認(かくにん)すべき<b>なのに</b>、どうして連絡(れんらく)しなかったんですか。
You were supposed to check with everyone - why didn't you get in touch?
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)に確認(かくにん)すべき<b>ところを</b>、一部(いちぶ)の担当者(たんとうしゃ)にしか連絡(れんらく)しておりませんでした。
We should have checked with everyone, and in fact only contacted some of those responsible.
</pre>

`のに` puts the speaker outside the failure, looking at it; it is accusing, or at least
disappointed. <code>べきところを</code> puts the speaker inside it and states the lapse
flatly, which is exactly what a written apology needs - no excuses, no emotion, just the
correct standard and the deviation from it. Turn the second sentence into `のに` and you
have someone complaining about themselves, which reads as insincere.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 逃(に)げようとした<b>ところに</b>警官(けいかん)に捕(つか)まえられた。 → ✅ 逃(に)げようとした<b>ところを</b>警官(けいかん)に捕(つか)まえられた。
  The moment is what the police officer acted on, so it is the object and takes `を`.
  `に` would need something merely arriving.

- ❌ お忙(いそが)しい<b>のところを</b> → ✅ お忙(いそが)しい<b>ところを</b>
  An い-adjective modifies <code>ところ</code> directly. The `の` belongs only after a
  noun.

- ❌ お休(やす)み<b>ところを</b> → ✅ お休(やす)み<b>のところを</b>
  And the reverse: a noun does need the `の`. Both halves of this pair get written wrong
  about equally often.

- ❌ 暇(ひま)な<b>ところを</b>ごめんね、ちょっといい？ → ✅ 今(いま)ちょっといい？
  The formula is a piece of deference. Aimed at a friend, and aimed at their *free* time
  at that, it reads as a joke at their expense.

## Practice

**1.** ご多忙(たぼう)の____恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、来週(らいしゅう)までにご回答(かいとう)いただけますでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ご多忙(たぼう)の<b>ところを</b>恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが

<code>多忙(たぼう)</code> is a noun, so it links with `の`. The whole opening is a
standard business-email formula: acknowledge the cost to the reader, then make the
request. <code>ご多忙(たぼう)のところ</code> without the `を` is also correct and very
slightly less formal.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいた（ところに・ところを）、後(うし)ろから肩(かた)を叩(たた)かれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいた<b>ところを</b>、後(うし)ろから肩(かた)を叩(たた)かれた。

Somebody acted on the reader in the middle of their reading, so the moment is the
object: `を`. With `に` the sentence would need something turning up of its own accord,
such as <code>友人(ゆうじん)が訪(たず)ねてきた</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, for an email to a client: "I'm sorry to write to you so late in the evening, but I wanted to report on today's meeting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夜分(やぶん)遅(おそ)くに失礼(しつれい)いたします。お休(やす)みの<b>ところを</b>恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、本日(ほんじつ)の打(う)ち合(あ)わせについてご報告(ほうこく)いたします。

<code>お休(やす)みのところを</code> names the cost to the reader - their evening -
before the request or the message itself, which is the expected order in Japanese
business email. Note that <code>夜分(やぶん)</code> and
<code>本日(ほんじつ)</code> are part of the same register and would look odd next to a
casual ending.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)に居(い)眠(ねむ)りしていたところに部長(ぶちょう)に注意(ちゅうい)された。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)に居(い)眠(ねむ)りしていた<b>ところを</b>部長(ぶちょう)に注意(ちゅうい)された。

Being told off is something done *to* the sleeping moment, so it takes `を`. This is the
standard shape of the narrative use: an embarrassing activity, the moment marked with
`を`, and a passive main verb that keeps the embarrassed person as the subject.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 本来(ほんらい)なら直接(ちょくせつ)お詫(わ)びに伺(うかが)うべき<b>ところを</b>、書面(しょめん)にて失礼(しつれい)いたします。
(b) 本来(ほんらい)なら直接(ちょくせつ)お詫(わ)びに伺(うかが)うべき<b>なのに</b>、書面(しょめん)になってしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a formal written apology. It names the proper course - going in person - and
reports the departure from it without any emotional colouring, then closes with a humble
verb. The reader is left with the impression of someone who knows the standard and is
squarely admitting they have not met it.

(b) leaks feeling. `のに` plus <code>なってしまいました</code> makes it sound as though
circumstances got the better of the writer, and in an apology that reads as
self-justification. The facts are identical; (a) takes responsibility and (b) explains
itself, which is why letters use (a).

</details>

# ～以前

**Meaning** · before that point - and, at N1, before that even becomes the question: the matter fails at a more basic level than the one being discussed

以前(いぜん) is the formal counterpart of 前(まえ), and on its own it means simply
"earlier than". What makes it an N1 point is the second, argumentative use:
<code>A以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)だ</code> refuses the topic A altogether, on the
grounds that something more elementary has already gone wrong.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a time or event) | Noun + 以前(いぜん)（に） | 明治(めいじ) → 明治(めいじ)以前(いぜん) |
| Verb | dictionary form + 以前(いぜん)に | 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)える以前(いぜん)に |
| Dismissing the topic | Noun + 以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい) | マナー → マナー以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい) |
| Dismissing, mid-sentence | Noun／Verb + 以前(いぜん)に | 好(す)き嫌(きら)い → 好(す)き嫌(きら)い以前(いぜん)に |
| Standalone adverb | 以前(いぜん)、… | - → 以前(いぜん)、京都(きょうと)に住(す)んでいた |

<div class="note">

**Note** - 以前(いぜん) cannot count backwards from now.
"Three days ago" is 三日(みっか)前(まえ), never
❌三日(みっか)以前(いぜん). With a number in front, 以前(いぜん) means "earlier
than that point", which is a different statement entirely.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>以前(いぜん)</b>、この辺(あた)りに大(おお)きな市場(いちば)があった。
There used to be a big market around here.
</pre>

<pre>
明治(めいじ)<b>以前(いぜん)</b>、この村(むら)には寺(てら)が三(みっ)つあったという。
Before the Meiji period, they say, there were three temples in this village.
</pre>

<pre>
入社(にゅうしゃ)<b>以前(いぜん)に</b>取(と)った資格(しかく)も評価(ひょうか)の対象(たいしょう)になります。
Qualifications obtained before you joined the company also count towards your assessment.
</pre>

<pre>
申込(もうしこみ)締切(しめきり)<b>以前(いぜん)に</b>提出(ていしゅつ)された書類(しょるい)のみ受(う)け付(つ)けます。
Only documents submitted on or before the application deadline will be accepted.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)を考(かんが)える<b>以前(いぜん)に</b>、この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)さが足(た)りない。
Never mind the price - the room simply isn't big enough.
</pre>

<pre>
デザインを議論(ぎろん)する<b>以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</b>だ。そもそも動(うご)かないのだから。
We're not even at the point of discussing the design. The thing doesn't run.
</pre>

<pre>
礼儀(れいぎ)<b>以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</b>として、まずは時間(じかん)を守(まも)ることから始(はじ)めてほしい。
Forget manners for the moment - I'd like him to start by turning up on time.
</pre>

<pre>
好(す)き嫌(きら)い<b>以前(いぜん)に</b>、彼(かれ)の仕事(しごと)の進(すす)め方(かた)は信用(しんよう)できない。
It isn't a question of whether I like him; I can't trust the way he works.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Plain 以前(いぜん) is **formal but entirely ordinary**. As a bare adverb at the head
of a sentence - <code>以前(いぜん)、大阪(おおさか)で働(はたら)いていました</code>
- it means "previously, at some earlier time" and is safe in any polite conversation. It
is a shade more distant than <code>前(まえ)に</code> and much less specific than
<code>去年(きょねん)</code>; the vagueness is the point, and it often signals that the
speaker does not intend to say exactly when.

In a document, 以前(いぜん) carries a boundary convention worth knowing. Like
以上(いじょう) and 以下(いか), it **includes** the point named:
<code>3月(がつ)31日(にち)以前(いぜん)</code> covers the 31st itself, whereas
<code>3月(がつ)31日(にち)前(まえ)</code> does not. Regulations, deadlines and
contracts rely on that difference. In everyday speech nobody counts on it, so if it
matters, say <code>31日(にち)を含(ふく)めて</code> and remove the doubt.

The argumentative use is a different animal. <code>～以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</code>
is **blunt**. It tells the listener that the thing they raised is premature and that
something more fundamental has already failed, and it is heard as a firm criticism -
often of a person, not just of a plan. Between colleagues it lands hard; aimed upward it
is close to insubordination. Said about a situation rather than a person
(<code>安全(あんぜん)以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)だ</code>) it is fair game and
very common in journalism and in meetings where something has gone badly wrong.

One homophone to keep straight. 依然(いぜん), written with different characters, means
"still, unchanged", and appears almost always as
<code>依然(いぜん)として</code>: <code>原因(げんいん)は依然(いぜん)として不明(ふめい)だ</code>.
It is common in news writing, and because the two words sound identical, only the
characters or the surrounding grammar will tell you which you have.

## Contrast

### 以前(いぜん) vs 前(まえ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)</code> is the everyday word and counts backwards
from a reference point. <code>以前(いぜん)</code> is formal, includes the point named,
and cannot take a number of units.

</div>

<pre>
寝(ね)る<b>前(まえ)に</b>歯(は)を磨(みが)きなさい。
Brush your teeth before you go to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
契約(けいやく)締結(ていけつ)<b>以前(いぜん)に</b>発生(はっせい)した費用(ひよう)は対象外(たいしょうがい)となります。
Costs incurred prior to the conclusion of the contract fall outside the scope.
</pre>

The first sentence would sound absurd with 以前(いぜん) - a parent does not use
contract vocabulary at bedtime - and the second would lose its precision with
前(まえ). Where the sentence is about a routine, a habit or a short interval, use
前(まえ). Where it is about a formal cut-off, use 以前(いぜん).

The number rule is the reliable one: 三日(みっか)前(まえ) is "three days ago", and
❌三日(みっか)以前(いぜん) is not the same thing said politely - it is a different
and usually unintended claim.

### ～以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい) vs ～どころではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both refuse the topic. <code>以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</code>
says it is **too advanced a question**; `どころではない` says circumstances leave **no
room** for it.

</div>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りが明日(あした)なので、旅行(りょこう)の話(はなし)をしている<b>どころではない</b>。
The deadline is tomorrow - this is no time to be talking about holidays.
</pre>

<pre>
味(あじ)を評価(ひょうか)する<b>以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</b>で、そもそも注文(ちゅうもん)した料理(りょうり)が出(で)てこない。
We're a long way from judging the food; the dishes we ordered never arrived.
</pre>

The difference is where the obstacle sits. With `どころではない` the topic is a perfectly
good one and the *timing* is impossible - come back next week and the holiday
conversation can happen. With <code>以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</code> the topic is
still standing but something underneath it has collapsed, and no amount of waiting will
help until that is fixed.

### 以前(いぜん) vs 以来(いらい)・以降(いこう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three take a point in time and all three include it.
以前(いぜん) faces **backwards**; 以降(いこう) faces **forwards**; 以来(いらい)
faces forwards **and reaches the present**.

</div>

<pre>
震災(しんさい)<b>以降(いこう)</b>、この地区(ちく)の建物(たてもの)は基準(きじゅん)が厳(きび)しくなった。
Since the earthquake, building standards in this district have been tightened.
</pre>

<pre>
卒業(そつぎょう)<b>以来(いらい)</b>、彼(かれ)とは一度(いちど)も会(あ)っていない。
I haven't seen him once since we graduated.
</pre>

以降(いこう) simply marks the start of a period and says nothing about whether it
continues; 以来(いらい) insists that it runs unbroken up to now, which is why it pairs
so naturally with a negative such as
<code>会(あ)っていない</code>. 以前(いぜん) is the mirror of 以降(いこう), not of
以来(いらい) - there is no backward-facing form that reaches the present, because a
period ending at the reference point is simply over.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 三日(みっか)<b>以前(いぜん)</b>に電話(でんわ)しました。 → ✅ 三日(みっか)<b>前(まえ)</b>に電話(でんわ)しました。
  "Three days ago" is 前(まえ). 以前(いぜん) cannot follow a duration; put a number
  in front of it and you have said "earlier than three days [from the reference point]",
  which is not what you meant.

- ❌ 食(た)べた<b>以前(いぜん)に</b>手(て)を洗(あら)う。 → ✅ 食(た)べる<b>前(まえ)に</b>手(て)を洗(あら)う。
  The verb before 以前(いぜん) stays in the dictionary form, exactly as with
  前(まえ). And a routine like handwashing wants 前(まえ) anyway; 以前(いぜん)
  would make it sound like a clause in a food-hygiene regulation.

- ❌ <b>依然(いぜん)</b>、この会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)いていました。 → ✅ <b>以前(いぜん)</b>、この会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)いていました。
  Same sound, different word. 依然(いぜん) means "still, as before" and virtually always
  appears as <code>依然(いぜん)として</code>.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、それは<b>説明(せつめい)以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</b>です。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、その前(まえ)に確認(かくにん)したい点(てん)がございます。
  Grammatically fine, and a serious social error. The phrase tells the listener their
  question was not worth asking; directed at a superior it is close to a rebuke.

## Practice

**1.** この機械(きかい)は、使(つか)いやすさを議論(ぎろん)する____、安全(あんぜん)基準(きじゅん)を満(み)たしていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

議論(ぎろん)する<b>以前(いぜん)に</b>（or <b>以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)で</b>）

Usability is a reasonable thing to discuss, but the machine has failed a more basic
test, so the discussion is premature. The verb stays in the dictionary form
<code>議論(ぎろん)する</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: レポートは金曜日(きんようび)（以前(いぜん)・前(まえ)）に提出(ていしゅつ)してください。ただし金曜日(きんようび)当日(とうじつ)も受(う)け付(つ)けます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

金曜日(きんようび)<b>以前(いぜん)</b>に提出(ていしゅつ)してください。

The second sentence says Friday itself is acceptable, and that is exactly the boundary
convention of 以前(いぜん): it includes the day named.
<code>金曜日(きんようび)前(まえ)</code> would exclude Friday and contradict what
follows. In a real notice you would probably write
<code>金曜日(きんようび)までに</code> and avoid the question.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Whether the design is good or bad isn't the issue - the site doesn't even load."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

デザインの良(よ)し悪(あ)し<b>以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</b>で、そもそもサイトが表示(ひょうじ)されない。

<code>～以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</code> is the natural rendering of "isn't even
the issue", and <code>そもそも</code> is its usual companion - it introduces the more
basic failure. Keep in mind how blunt this is; it is a fair thing to say about a website
and a harsh thing to say about a person.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる五分(ごふん)以前(いぜん)に部屋(へや)に着(つ)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる五分(ごふん)<b>前(まえ)</b>に部屋(へや)に着(つ)きました。

A measured interval before an event is 前(まえ). 以前(いぜん) cannot take a
duration in front of it. Note the reading 五分(ごふん) - the counter is ふん here,
where 一分(いっぷん), 三分(さんぷん) and 十分(じゅっぷん) all take ぷん.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今(いま)は新(あたら)しい企画(きかく)を考(かんが)える<b>どころではない</b>。
(b) 新(あたら)しい企画(きかく)を考(かんが)える<b>以前(いぜん)の問題(もんだい)</b>だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the timing is wrong. Something urgent has taken over - a crisis, a deadline, a
staff shortage - and once it passes, thinking about new projects will be a perfectly
sensible thing to do. The complaint is about circumstances.

(b) says the question itself is out of order. Something more fundamental is broken -
the team, the budget, the last project - and no change in the schedule will make new
proposals appropriate until that is dealt with. (b) is by far the harder thing to hear,
because it does not blame the situation.

</details>
