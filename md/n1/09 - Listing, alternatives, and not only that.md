# N1 - Listing, alternatives, and not only that


---

# ～といい～といい

**Meaning** · take A, take B - whichever part of it you look at, the same verdict holds

`といい` comes in pairs. The speaker picks two features out of one thing - a
person, a dish, a room, a season - and offers them as samples: look at this, look
at that. What closes the sentence is never a neutral fact but the speaker's
appraisal, admiring or fed up. Two items are quoted; the whole thing is being
judged.

## Formation

Nouns only, and the two nouns must be features of the same thing.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + といい + Noun + といい | 色(いろ)といい形(かたち)といい |
| The thing being judged | ～は、A といい B といい、+ verdict | この皿(さら)は、色(いろ)といい形(かたち)といい、美(うつく)しい |
| An adjective idea | make it a noun first | 暑(あつ)い → 暑(あつ)さといい |
| Three items | A といい B といい C といい | 味(あじ)といい量(りょう)といい値段(ねだん)といい |
| Polite | … + です／ですね | 色(いろ)といい形(かたち)といい、見事(みごと)ですね |

<div class="note">

**Note** - whatever follows the pair has to be an evaluation. A plan, a request or
an instruction cannot close the pattern:
❌<code>色(いろ)といい形(かたち)といい、買(か)ってください</code>. The
sentence has to end in a judgement, because the two items exist only to support
one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この絵(え)は色(いろ)<b>といい</b>形(かたち)<b>といい</b>、子(こ)どもの作品(さくひん)とは思(おも)えない。
The colours, the shapes - you'd never think a child painted this.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は味(あじ)<b>といい</b>値段(ねだん)<b>といい</b>、文句(もんく)のつけようがない。
That place - the food, the prices - there is nothing to complain about.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は話(はな)し方(かた)<b>といい</b>歩(ある)き方(かた)<b>といい</b>、父親(ちちおや)にそっくりだ。
The way he talks, the way he walks - he is his father all over.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)さ<b>といい</b>日(ひ)当(あ)たり<b>といい</b>、申(もう)し分(ぶん)がありません。
This room - the size, the light - leaves nothing to be desired.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は暑(あつ)さ<b>といい</b>湿気(しっけ)<b>といい</b>、例年(れいねん)とは比(くら)べものにならない。
This summer, the heat and the humidity both, is in another league from a normal year.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい担当者(たんとうしゃ)は、言(い)うこと<b>といい</b>やり方(かた)<b>といい</b>、前(まえ)の人(ひと)とは正反対(せいはんたい)だ。
The new person in charge is the opposite of the last one, in what he says and in how he does it.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の試合(しあい)は、審判(しんぱん)の判定(はんてい)<b>といい</b>観客(かんきゃく)の態度(たいど)<b>といい</b>、後味(あとあじ)の悪(わる)いものだった。
Yesterday's match left a sour taste, between the refereeing decisions and the behaviour of the crowd.
</pre>

<pre>
泊(と)まった宿(やど)は、料理(りょうり)<b>といい</b>部屋(へや)からの眺(なが)め<b>といい</b>、払(はら)った金額(きんがく)を考(かんが)えれば驚(おどろ)くほどよかった。
The inn we stayed at was astonishing value for what we paid, both for the food and for the view from the room.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is considered language rather than quick language. It belongs to reviews,
appraisals, a complaint told properly, an essay, a report on how something turned
out - places where the speaker has stopped to weigh a thing up. It is not stiff,
though: it takes plain or polite endings equally and would not sound out of place
over dinner.

What it always carries is **attitude**. Roughly half of its uses are admiration
and half are exasperation, and the verdict at the end decides which. That is why a
flat, factual close does not work. <code>色(いろ)といい形(かたち)といい、中国(ちゅうごく)製(せい)だ</code>
leaves the listener waiting: two features have been produced as evidence, and no
judgement arrived.

One social point. Used about your own possessions, your own work or your own
family, it tends to sound like boasting, because the pattern builds to praise and
the praise is coming from you. 「うちの息子(むすこ)は成績(せいせき)といい性格(せいかく)といい…」
is the shape of a parent showing off. That is a tendency and not a prohibition -
it is perfectly usable about your own failures, where the self-criticism lands
rather well.

Finally, two items is the normal number and it is enough. The pattern does not
mean "these two and no others"; it means "any part you pick". Piling up four or
five makes it sound like a list rather than a verdict.

## Contrast

### ～といい～といい vs ～も～も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `も…も` **adds two facts together**. `といい…といい` offers
two features as **evidence for one judgement** about the thing they belong to.

</div>

<pre>
この宿(やど)は料理(りょうり)<b>も</b>温泉(おんせん)<b>も</b>よかった。
The food was good at this inn, and so were the baths.
</pre>

<pre>
この宿(やど)は料理(りょうり)<b>といい</b>温泉(おんせん)<b>といい</b>、なかなかのものだ。
This inn - the food, the baths - is really quite something.
</pre>

The first is a report and could be the answer to "how was it?". The second is a
verdict, and the two items are only samples of it: the listener understands that
the service and the rooms would come out the same way. Where `も…も` can name two
completely unrelated things - 雨(あめ)も風(かぜ)も強(つよ)い - the pair
under `といい` has to belong to one whole.

### ～といい～といい vs ～といわず～といわず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `といい…といい` names two **features** and judges the thing.
`といわず…といわず` names two **points** and says there was no gap between them -
everywhere, or all the time.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の絵(え)は色使(いろづか)い<b>といい</b>構図(こうず)<b>といい</b>、プロ並(な)みだ。
His painting is professional standard, in its use of colour and in its composition.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちは顔(かお)<b>といわず</b>服(ふく)<b>といわず</b>泥(どろ)だらけだった。
The children were covered in mud, faces, clothes and everything in between.
</pre>

The second sentence is not appraising faces and clothes; it is saying the mud
respected no boundaries. Swap the patterns and both fail: composition and colour
are not areas that mud spreads across, and no one delivers a considered verdict on
a child's face versus a child's shirt.

### ～といい～といい vs ～にしても～にしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にしても…にしても` grants two **possibilities** and says what
is true either way. `といい…といい` takes two **actual features** and says what is
true of the thing that has them.

</div>

<pre>
行(い)く<b>にしても</b>行(い)かない<b>にしても</b>、今日中(きょうじゅう)に返事(へんじ)が必要(ひつよう)だ。
Whether you go or not, an answer is needed today.
</pre>

<pre>
あのホテルは立地(りっち)<b>といい</b>設備(せつび)<b>といい</b>、値段(ねだん)が高(たか)いのも当然(とうぜん)だ。
That hotel, with its location and its facilities, is expensive for good reason.
</pre>

`にしても` looks forward at branches that have not been settled, which is why it
takes verbs freely. `といい` looks at what is already there, which is why it takes
nothing but nouns.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この服(ふく)は安(やす)い<b>といい</b>丈夫(じょうぶ)だ<b>といい</b>、いい買(か)い物(もの)だった。 → ✅ この服(ふく)は値段(ねだん)<b>といい</b>生地(きじ)<b>といい</b>、いい買(か)い物(もの)だった。
  Adjectives and verbs cannot sit in front of <code>といい</code>. Name the
  feature as a noun - 値段(ねだん), 生地(きじ), 安(やす)さ - and the
  sentence works.

- ❌ この町(まち)は気候(きこう)<b>といい</b>物価(ぶっか)<b>といい</b>、ぜひ一度(いちど)来(き)てください。 → ✅ この町(まち)は気候(きこう)<b>といい</b>物価(ぶっか)<b>といい</b>、住(す)みやすいところです。
  The pattern has to land on a judgement. An invitation or an instruction leaves
  the two samples with nothing to support.

- ❌ 兄(あに)は背(せ)が高(たか)い<b>といい</b>、姉(あね)は頭(あたま)がいい<b>といい</b>… → ✅ 兄(あに)も姉(あね)も自慢(じまん)の家族(かぞく)です。
  The two items have to be features of **one** thing. Two different people are two
  subjects, and that is what `も…も` is for.

- ❌ この車(くるま)は燃費(ねんぴ)<b>といい</b>、気(き)に入(い)っている。 → ✅ この車(くるま)は燃費(ねんぴ)<b>といい</b>乗(の)り心地(ごこち)<b>といい</b>、気(き)に入(い)っている。
  A single <code>といい</code> is not the pattern. It needs the pair to mean
  "wherever you look".

The original ends in a plain announcement of fact, and the two features are left

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)の演奏(えんそう)は技術(ぎじゅつ)も表現力(ひょうげんりょく)もすばらしかった。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)の演奏(えんそう)は技術(ぎじゅつ)といい表現力(ひょうげんりょく)といい、すばらしかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) praises two things and stops there: the technique was excellent and so was the
expression. It reads as a report, and a listener may reasonably ask about the
programme or the tone.

(b) uses the same two things as samples. The claim is about the performance as a
whole - pick any aspect you like and it was excellent - and the speaker's
admiration is audible in the construction itself.

</details>

# ～だの～だの

**Meaning** · A and B and that sort of thing - a list quoted with a curl of the lip

`だの` is the **disparaging** member of the listing family. Like とか and やら it
names two representative items and leaves the rest implied, but it adds something
they do not: the speaker is unimpressed. What gets listed is usually other
people's complaints, other people's demands, or a pile of stuff the speaker would
rather not deal with.

## Formation

Each item carries its own `だの`, and the pattern is almost always followed by a
noun, by と言(い)う, or by a predicate describing the mess.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だの | 洗濯物(せんたくもの)だのゴミ袋(ぶくろ)だの |
| Verb | plain form + だの | 行(い)くだの行(い)かないだの |
| い-adjective | plain form + だの | 暑(あつ)いだの寒(さむ)いだの |
| な-adjective | stem + だの | 嫌(いや)だの面倒(めんどう)だの |
| Quoted grumbling | ～だの～だのと言(い)う | 高(たか)いだの遠(とお)いだのと言(い)う |
| Closing the list | ～だの～だので／…だのなんだの | 会議(かいぎ)だの出張(しゅっちょう)だので |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>だのなんだの</code> ("and what have you") is a fixed way of
finishing the list off with a shrug:
<code>保険(ほけん)だのなんだので給料(きゅうりょう)が減(へ)る</code>. It is
very common and always dismissive.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は眠(ねむ)い<b>だの</b>疲(つか)れた<b>だの</b>と、いつも文句(もんく)ばかりだ。
My brother is forever grumbling - he's sleepy, he's tired, on and on.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)は書類(しょるい)<b>だの</b>空(あ)き缶(かん)<b>だの</b>でひどい有様(ありさま)だった。
The desk was a disgrace, papers and empty cans and I don't know what else.
</pre>

<pre>
行(い)く<b>だの</b>行(い)かない<b>だの</b>で、結局(けっきょく)予約(よやく)は取(と)れなかった。
It was going, then not going, and in the end we never got a booking.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は誕生日(たんじょうび)が近(ちか)づくと、ゲーム機(き)<b>だの</b>自転車(じてんしゃ)<b>だの</b>と言(い)い出(だ)す。
As his birthday gets close my son starts on about a games console, or a bike, or whatever else.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は資料(しりょう)が古(ふる)い<b>だの</b>字(じ)が小(ちい)さい<b>だの</b>と、中身(なかみ)には触(ふ)れなかった。
The department head said the material was out of date and the type was small, and never touched the actual content.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しは手続(てつづ)き<b>だの</b>挨拶(あいさつ)<b>だの</b>で、荷造(にづく)りに取(と)りかかるまでが大変(たいへん)だ。
With a move it's the paperwork and the visits to the neighbours - just getting as far as the packing is hard work.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いていたのに、後(あと)になって話(はなし)が違(ちが)う<b>だの</b>聞(き)いていない<b>だの</b>と言(い)い出(だ)した。
They sat through the same briefing, and now they claim the story has changed, or that they were never told.
</pre>

<pre>
実家(じっか)に帰(かえ)るたびに、太(ふと)った<b>だの</b>老(ふ)けた<b>だの</b>と言(い)われて、正直(しょうじき)うんざりする。
Every time I go back to my parents' I get told I've put on weight, or that I look older, and frankly I've had enough of it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`だの` is **spoken, plain and openly critical**. It is adult language rather than
rough language - you would hear it from a colleague over lunch or from a parent
complaining about a child - but there is nothing neutral about it. Putting
somebody's words under `だの` is a way of saying you did not think much of them.

That has an obvious social consequence: do not list a superior's requests, or a
customer's requirements, with `だの` in front of them. 「部長(ぶちょう)は資料(しりょう)だの日程(にってい)だの…」
said to the department head's face is rude in a way that とか is not. Behind their
back among equals it is exactly what people say.

It also sits at a certain age. Younger speakers reach for とか for nearly
everything, and `だの` can sound faintly like something an older relative says -
which is part of why it works so well in the mouth of a narrator or a grumbling
character in fiction. That is a tendency and no more; it is in ordinary use across
ages, especially in writing that wants a bit of bite.

In formal writing it is rare, and where it does appear it is because the writer
wants the reader to hear the sneer. A report that lists requirements with `だの`
has taken a side.

## Contrast

### ～だの～だの vs ～とか～とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different face. `とか` is **neutral** and belongs to
everyday speech; `だの` says the speaker finds the items **tiresome or silly**.

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は買(か)い物(もの)<b>とか</b>掃除(そうじ)<b>とか</b>で終(お)わってしまう。
Weekends just go on shopping and cleaning and things like that.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は買(か)い物(もの)<b>だの</b>掃除(そうじ)<b>だの</b>で終(お)わってしまう。
Weekends disappear into shopping, cleaning, all of that nonsense.
</pre>

The information is identical. The second speaker resents it. Because `とか` is
neutral it can list things you like - 旅行(りょこう)とか映画(えいが)とか -
and `だの` in that slot would be strange, since you are not supposed to be
belittling your own pleasures.

### ～だの～だの vs ～やら～やら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `やら…やら` says the speaker was **overwhelmed**; `だの…だの`
says the speaker is **unimpressed**. Confusion versus contempt.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの日(ひ)は荷物(にもつ)<b>やら</b>来客(らいきゃく)<b>やら</b>で目(め)が回(まわ)った。
On moving day, what with the boxes and the visitors, my head was spinning.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)は音(おと)が大(おお)きい<b>だの</b>車(くるま)が邪魔(じゃま)<b>だの</b>と言(い)ってくる。
The neighbour comes round to say we're noisy, or the car is in the way.
</pre>

`やら` sympathises with the person in the middle of it, usually the speaker.
`だの` looks at someone else's words or someone else's stuff and finds them
wanting. One more formal difference: `やら` does not readily take quoted
complaints, while <code>だの…だのと言(い)う</code> is `だの`'s home ground.

### ～だの～だの vs や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `や` is the plain written "and, among others". `だの` is `や`
plus an opinion, and cannot be used where the list must stay factual.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)には机(つくえ)<b>や</b>椅子(いす)が運(はこ)び込(こ)まれた。
Desks, chairs and so on were carried into the hall.
</pre>

<pre>
倉庫(そうこ)には壊(こわ)れた机(つくえ)<b>だの</b>古(ふる)い椅子(いす)<b>だの</b>が放(ほう)り込(こ)んであった。
The storeroom had broken desks and old chairs and the rest of it dumped in.
</pre>

A minutes-taker writes `や`. Someone describing a junk room writes `だの`. This is
why `だの` almost never appears in official notices, manuals or academic prose: it
editorialises, and those genres are not allowed to.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ お客様(きゃくさま)から、色(いろ)<b>だの</b>サイズ<b>だの</b>のご要望(ようぼう)をいただきました。 → ✅ お客様(きゃくさま)から、色(いろ)<b>や</b>サイズについてご要望(ようぼう)をいただきました。
  The polite frame and the sneer contradict each other. Keeping ご要望(ようぼう)
  while dismissing the requests with `だの` is worse than plain rudeness - it
  sounds sarcastic.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は映画(えいが)<b>だの</b>食事(しょくじ)<b>だの</b>、本当(ほんとう)に楽(たの)しかった。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は映画(えいが)<b>とか</b>食事(しょくじ)<b>とか</b>、本当(ほんとう)に楽(たの)しかった。
  `だの` cannot carry enjoyment. Listing a good day with it makes the speaker
  sound as if they are complaining about their own fun.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は忙(いそが)しいだ<b>だの</b>眠(ねむ)いだ<b>だの</b>と言(い)う。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は忙(いそが)しい<b>だの</b>眠(ねむ)い<b>だの</b>と言(い)う。
  An い-adjective attaches in its plain form. The extra `だ` comes from thinking of
  `だの` as `だ` + `の`, which is not how it behaves after
  <code>忙(いそが)しい</code>.

- ❌ 荷物(にもつ)<b>だの</b>で部屋(へや)が狭(せま)い。 → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)<b>だの</b>本(ほん)<b>だの</b>で部屋(へや)が狭(せま)い。
  One `だの` is not the pattern. It comes in pairs, or ends with
  <code>だのなんだの</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 母(はは)は部屋(へや)が汚(きたな)い____帰(かえ)りが遅(おそ)い____と、会(あ)うたびに小言(こごと)を言(い)う。

汚(きたな)い<b>だの</b>遅(おそ)い<b>だの</b>と

Two quoted complaints, and the speaker plainly finds them tedious. That combination
- other people's words, reported without respect - is `だの`'s core use.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 夏休(なつやす)みは海(うみ)（だの・とか）花火(はなび)（だの・とか）で忙(いそが)しかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

海(うみ)<b>とか</b>花火(はなび)<b>とか</b>

A summer of beaches and fireworks is being remembered fondly, and `とか` is the
neutral list. `だの` would imply the speaker had been dragged along and resented
every minute - possible as a joke, but not the plain reading.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He says the pay is low and the commute is long, but he never actually looks for another job."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

給料(きゅうりょう)が安(やす)い<b>だの</b>通勤(つうきん)が長(なが)い<b>だの</b>と言(い)うわりに、転職(てんしょく)活動(かつどう)は一度(いちど)もしていない。

Two grumbles quoted by a speaker who does not accept them, closed with
<code>と言(い)う</code>. English carries the scepticism in the second clause;
Japanese has already carried it in `だの`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しい企画(きかく)について、社長(しゃちょう)は予算(よさん)だの人員(じんいん)だのを検討(けんとう)してくださいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しい企画(きかく)について、社長(しゃちょう)は予算(よさん)<b>や</b>人員(じんいん)を検討(けんとう)してくださいました。

The honorific くださいました shows respect while `だの` sneers at what was
considered. Use `や` when the list itself has to stay respectful or neutral.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 台所(だいどころ)は鍋(なべ)やら皿(さら)やらで足(あし)の踏(ふ)み場(ば)もない。
(b) 台所(だいどころ)は鍋(なべ)だの皿(さら)だので足(あし)の踏(ふ)み場(ば)もない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the speaker inside the chaos: there was too much of it to sort out, and
the tone is closer to bewilderment than to blame. It fits a kitchen the speaker
themselves has just cooked in.

(b) is a judgement passed from outside. The pans and plates are being written off
as clutter, and someone - probably not the speaker - is being held responsible for
them.

</details>

# ～なり～なり

**Meaning** · A or B - or anything else along those lines; pick one and get on with it

`なり…なり` puts two options on the table and hands the choice to the other person.
The two are **samples**, not the whole menu: whatever else would do the same job is
included by implication. The sentence it lives in is nearly always a suggestion, a
request, an instruction or a permission - the speaker is telling someone that a
decision is theirs to make, and that any reasonable option will do.

## Formation

Each option carries its own `なり`, and the pair is normally rounded off with
`する`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + なり | 売(う)る → 売(う)るなり捨(す)てるなり |
| Noun | Noun + なり | メールなり電話(でんわ)なり |
| Noun + particle | Noun + なり + に／で／へ | 先輩(せんぱい)なり上司(じょうし)なりに |
| Closing the pair | ～なり～なり + する／してください／したらどうか | 読(よ)むなり聞(き)くなりしてください |
| Question word | 何(なん)なりと／どこなりと | 何(なん)なりとお申(もう)し付(つ)けください |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front of `なり` is always **plain non-past**.
❌<code>売(う)ったなり捨(す)てたなり</code> is impossible, because the pattern
looks at options still open, not at things already done.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a single `なり` after a dictionary-form verb is a completely different
pattern meaning "the moment that", as in
<code>座(すわ)るなり眠(ねむ)ってしまった</code>. The alternatives reading needs
the **pair**. If you see only one `なり`, read it as "the moment".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
電話(でんわ)する<b>なり</b>メールする<b>なり</b>して、必(かなら)ず連絡(れんらく)してください。
Phone, send a message, whatever - just make sure you get in touch.
</pre>

<pre>
わからないことは、先輩(せんぱい)<b>なり</b>上司(じょうし)<b>なり</b>に聞(き)いたほうがいい。
Anything you don't understand, ask a senior colleague or your manager.
</pre>

<pre>
着(き)ない服(ふく)は売(う)る<b>なり</b>捨(す)てる<b>なり</b>したらどうですか。
Why not sell the clothes you don't wear, or throw them out?
</pre>

<pre>
土曜(どよう)<b>なり</b>日曜(にちよう)<b>なり</b>、ご都合(つごう)のよい日(ひ)を教(おし)えてください。
Saturday, Sunday - let me know whichever day suits you.
</pre>

<pre>
使(つか)い方(かた)がわからないなら、説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)む<b>なり</b>店(みせ)で聞(き)く<b>なり</b>してほしい。
If you don't know how it works, read the manual or ask in the shop - do something.
</pre>

<pre>
報告書(ほうこくしょ)は、印刷(いんさつ)する<b>なり</b>データで送(おく)る<b>なり</b>、受(う)け取(と)る側(がわ)が楽(らく)な方法(ほうほう)で構(かま)いません。
Print the report or send it as a file - whichever is easier for the person receiving it.
</pre>

<pre>
留守(るす)にする間(あいだ)は、新聞(しんぶん)を止(と)める<b>なり</b>隣(となり)の人(ひと)に頼(たの)む<b>なり</b>しておいたほうが安全(あんぜん)です。
While you're away it's safer to stop the paper, or ask the neighbour, or something of that kind.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で抱(かか)え込(こ)んでいないで、病院(びょういん)に行(い)く<b>なり</b>家族(かぞく)に話(はな)す<b>なり</b>、できることから始(はじ)めてはどうだろう。
Rather than carrying it all alone, why not see a doctor or talk to your family - start with whatever you can manage.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The register is neutral: `なり…なり` appears in polite requests and in blunt
advice alike, and it is at home in both speech and writing. What it does have is a
**direction**. Because it hands over a decision, it fits speech aimed downwards or
sideways - to a junior, a friend, a family member, a customer being given options -
far more comfortably than speech aimed upwards. Laying out
<code>電車(でんしゃ)で行(い)くなりタクシーを使(つか)うなりしてください</code>
for your own manager sounds as if you were assigning them homework. To a superior,
<code>どちらでも構(かま)いません</code> or
<code>ご都合(つごう)に合(あ)わせます</code> is the safer shape.

There is often a note of mild impatience underneath, especially with
`したらどうですか` or `してほしい`: the speaker thinks the other person has been
sitting on a problem that any of several obvious moves would solve. Said warmly it
is encouragement; said flatly it is a nudge.

<code>何(なん)なりと</code> is worth learning as a unit. It is service language -
<code>ご用(よう)がありましたら何(なん)なりとお申(もう)し付(つ)けください</code>
- and belongs to hotels, shops and formal correspondence.

One frozen relative to recognise but not to build on:
<code>大(だい)なり小(しょう)なり</code>, "to a greater or lesser degree". It is
a set phrase, and its 大(だい)・小(しょう) slots cannot be filled with
anything else.

## Contrast

### ～なり～なり vs ～か～か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か` presents a **closed** choice: these are the options. `なり`
presents **samples**: these, or anything similar.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)まではバス<b>か</b>電車(でんしゃ)で来(き)てください。
Come to the venue by bus or by train.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)まではバス<b>なり</b>電車(でんしゃ)<b>なり</b>で来(き)てください。
Get to the venue by bus, or train, or however you like.
</pre>

The first has quietly ruled out driving. The second has not: it names two ways of
travelling and leaves the rest of the world open. That is also why `か` is fine in
a plain statement of fact - 彼(かれ)はバスか電車(でんしゃ)で来(き)た -
where `なり` cannot go.

### ～なり～なり vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たり…たり` samples things that **happen**, often several of
them. `なり…なり` samples options of which the listener picks **one**.

</div>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)は本(ほん)を読(よ)ん<b>だり</b>散歩(さんぽ)し<b>たり</b>している。
On days off I read, go for walks, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
気分転換(きぶんてんかん)に本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>なり</b>散歩(さんぽ)する<b>なり</b>したらどう。
Why not read something or go for a walk, to clear your head?
</pre>

The first describes a life; both activities are real and both recur. The second
proposes a remedy, and the listener is expected to do one of them, not both. This
is the pair learners mix up most, because the English "or" and "and" both come out
vague in translation - decide first whether anyone is choosing.

### ～なり～なり vs ～にしろ～にしろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なり…なり` says **pick either**. `にしろ…にしろ` says it makes
no difference which, because the same thing is true of both.

</div>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>にしろ</b>欠席(けっせき)する<b>にしろ</b>、金曜(きんよう)までに返事(へんじ)をください。
Whether you're coming or not, let me have an answer by Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)はメール<b>なり</b>電話(でんわ)<b>なり</b>でお願(ねが)いします。
Send your answer by email or phone, whichever you prefer.
</pre>

`にしろ` concedes the branches and then states what holds across them; the listener
is not being invited to choose between them at all. `なり` does nothing but invite
the choice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は映画(えいが)を見(み)る<b>なり</b>買(か)い物(もの)をする<b>なり</b>した。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>たり</b>買(か)い物(もの)をし<b>たり</b>した。
  Yesterday is over and nobody is choosing anything. `なり` needs an open decision;
  a report of what happened takes `たり`.

- ❌ 捨(す)てた<b>なり</b>売(う)った<b>なり</b>してください。 → ✅ 捨(す)てる<b>なり</b>売(う)る<b>なり</b>してください。
  The verb before `なり` stays in the dictionary form. Tense is carried by the
  `する` that closes the pattern.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、新幹線(しんかんせん)を使(つか)う<b>なり</b>飛行機(ひこうき)にする<b>なり</b>してください。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、新幹線(しんかんせん)でも飛行機(ひこうき)でも、ご都合(つごう)のよろしい方(ほう)をお選(えら)びください。
  Grammatically sound, socially wrong. Handing a superior a menu of actions has an
  instructing tone that the polite ending cannot cancel.

- ❌ 困(こま)ったら相談(そうだん)する<b>なり</b>してください。 → ✅ 困(こま)ったら相談(そうだん)する<b>なり</b>連絡(れんらく)をくれる<b>なり</b>してください。
  The alternatives reading needs two. A lone `なり` after a dictionary form is read
  as "the moment you consult someone", which is not what was meant.

# ～なりとも

**Meaning** · even a little of it; at least that much - an old "even" that survives in a handful of formal phrases

`なりとも` is the `なり` of <code>AなりBなり</code> stiffened with the concessive
`とも`. Attached to a small quantity it means "even that little would count":
多少(たしょう)なりとも, "to however slight a degree". You will meet it in
business letters, in speeches and in older narration. In ordinary modern Japanese
the same job is done by `でも`.

## Formation

It attaches to nouns, and in practice to a very short list of them - words for
small amounts.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Amount word | 多少(たしょう)／少(すこ)し／わずか + なりとも | 多少(たしょう)なりとも |
| Counted unit | number + counter + なりとも | 一言(ひとこと)なりとも、一度(いちど)なりとも |
| Question word (fixed) | 何(なん)なりと（も） | 何(なん)なりとお申(もう)し付(つ)けください |
| Shortened | ～なりと | 少(すこ)しなりと |
| Modern equivalent | ～でも／せめて～だけでも | 少(すこ)しでも |

<div class="note">

**Note** - it does not attach to verbs. ❌<code>食(た)べるなりとも</code> is not
a possible form; the only verb-like uses are frozen, as in
<code>何(なん)なりと</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
多少(たしょう)<b>なりとも</b>お役(やく)に立(た)てれば幸(さいわ)いです。
I hope this may be of some small help to you.
</pre>

<pre>
ご不明(ふめい)な点(てん)は、何(なん)<b>なりと</b>お申(もう)し付(つ)けください。
If anything is unclear, please do not hesitate to ask.
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>なりとも</b>不安(ふあん)を感(かん)じた場合(ばあい)は、ただちに作業(さぎょう)を中止(ちゅうし)すること。
If you feel the slightest unease, stop work immediately.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)について、多少(たしょう)<b>なりとも</b>説明(せつめい)があってしかるべきだ。
There ought to be at least some explanation of the cause of the accident.
</pre>

<pre>
わずか<b>なりとも</b>望(のぞ)みが残(のこ)っているうちは、捜索(そうさく)は続(つづ)けられた。
As long as even a faint hope remained, the search went on.
</pre>

<pre>
この計画(けいかく)に多少(たしょう)<b>なりとも</b>関(かか)わった者(もの)として、黙(だま)っているわけにはいかない。
As someone who had at least some part in this plan, I cannot keep quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
亡(な)くなった祖父(そふ)に、一度(いちど)<b>なりとも</b>感謝(かんしゃ)を伝(つた)えたことがあっただろうか。
Did I ever, even once, tell my grandfather I was grateful?
</pre>

<pre>
別(わか)れる前(まえ)に一言(ひとこと)<b>なりとも</b>礼(れい)が言(い)いたかったと、今(いま)になって思(おも)う。
I find myself thinking now that I wanted to say at least a word of thanks before we parted.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not reach for it.** It belongs to written and ceremonial
Japanese - business correspondence, prefaces, formal apologies, speeches, and the
narration of novels - and to the fixed service formula
<code>何(なん)なりと</code> that hotel and shop staff use. Said to a friend it
lands like a line from a period drama. The modern equivalent is `でも`:
<code>少(すこ)しでも</code>, <code>せめて一言(ひとこと)だけでも</code>,
<code>一度(いちど)でも</code>. Use those.

One exception is worth knowing. <code>多少(たしょう)なりとも</code> is genuinely
alive in adult formal speech - interviews, meetings, public statements - where it
means "to some degree, however small" and sounds measured rather than antique.
<code>多少(たしょう)なりとも責任(せきにん)がある</code> is a normal thing for a
manager to say. Learn that phrase as a unit; the productive pattern behind it is
what you are only recognising.

Two shades to notice when you meet it. In a wish or an offer it is **modest** -
the speaker is minimising what they can give, or asking for the smallest possible
amount. In a demand or a criticism it is **sharp** - a floor is being set, and the
complaint is that not even that much was met. The same words do both, and the
surrounding sentence decides.

<code>なりと</code> without the `とも` is the same word with the ending worn off. It
is commoner in the fixed phrases and slightly less heavy, but it is not a spoken
form either.

## Contrast

### ～なりとも vs ～でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning, four centuries apart. `でも` is what you say;
`なりとも` is what you read.

</div>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>でも</b>時間(じかん)があったら手伝(てつだ)って。
Help me out if you've got even a minute.
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>なりとも</b>お時間(じかん)をいただければと存(ぞん)じます。
I should be grateful for even a little of your time.
</pre>

Both mean "even a little". The first is a request between friends; the second is a
sentence from a letter, and everything around it - <code>いただければ</code>,
<code>存(ぞん)じます</code> - has had to rise to match. That is the practical
test: if the rest of your sentence is not formal, `なりとも` cannot be either.

### ～なりとも vs ～だけでも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけでも` marks a **reduced request**: if not the whole thing,
then at least this part. `なりとも` marks a **minimal quantity**: however small an
amount, it counts.

</div>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)は無理(むり)でも、半分(はんぶん)<b>だけでも</b>終(お)わらせたい。
Even if I can't do all of it, I want to finish at least half.
</pre>

<pre>
多少(たしょう)<b>なりとも</b>進(すす)んだのなら、報告(ほうこく)してください。
If it has moved on at all, however little, please report it.
</pre>

`だけでも` names a specific fallback that the listener can picture - half of it,
the first page, one night. `なりとも` names no amount at all; it lowers the bar to
anything above zero.

### ～なりとも vs ～たりとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one vowel apart and opposite in polarity. `なりとも` = "even a
little, and that is enough". `たりとも` = "not even one", and it demands a
**negative** predicate.

</div>

<pre>
多少(たしょう)<b>なりとも</b>興味(きょうみ)があれば、ぜひご参加(さんか)ください。
If you have even the slightest interest, please do join us.
</pre>

<pre>
一分(いっぷん)<b>たりとも</b>無駄(むだ)にはできない。
We cannot afford to waste even a minute.
</pre>

Swapping them breaks both sentences: `たりとも` with an affirmative ending is
ungrammatical, and `なりとも` cannot express "not one". If the sentence ends in a
negative and the point is total exclusion, you are looking at `たりとも`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ちょっと<b>なりとも</b>手伝(てつだ)ってよ。 → ✅ 少(すこ)し<b>でも</b>手伝(てつだ)ってよ。
  Casual ending, archaic middle. The two cannot share a sentence, and ちょっと in
  particular is far too colloquial to sit in front of `なりとも`.

- ❌ その日(ひ)は一人(ひとり)<b>なりとも</b>来(こ)なかった。 → ✅ その日(ひ)は一人(ひとり)<b>たりとも</b>来(こ)なかった。
  With a negative and the sense "not a single one", the word is `たりとも`.

- ❌ 何(なん)<b>なりと</b>言(い)ってね。 → ✅ 何(なん)<b>でも</b>言(い)ってね。
  <code>何(なん)なりと</code> is service language addressed to a customer.
  Turned on a friend it sounds like a waiter taking an order.

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>なりとも</b>飲(の)む<b>なりとも</b>してください。 → ✅ 食(た)べる<b>なり</b>飲(の)む<b>なり</b>してください。
  Offering alternatives is `なり…なり`, without the `とも`, and it is the only one
  of the two that takes verbs.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence - in conversation, or in

statement to a committee.

The giveaway is the pair <code>なりとも</code> and
<code>感(かん)じております</code>: humble verb plus archaic quantifier is the
register of an official apology. Between friends you would hear
<code>少(すこ)しは責任(せきにん)を感(かん)じている</code>.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 「少(すこ)しなりとも参考(さんこう)になれば幸(さいわ)いです。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

少(すこ)し<b>でも</b>参考(さんこう)になればうれしいです。

`でも` replaces `なりとも` one for one, and once it does, the stiff
<code>幸(さいわ)いです</code> can come down to うれしいです as well. Register
travels through a whole sentence, not just one word.

</details>

**3.** Choose: この会社(かいしゃ)では、一日(いちにち)（なりとも・たりとも）気(き)を抜(ぬ)くことは許(ゆる)されない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一日(いちにち)<b>たりとも</b>気(き)を抜(ぬ)くことは許(ゆる)されない

The ending is negative and the meaning is "not even one day". That is `たりとも`.
`なりとも` would be trying to say "even one day would be enough", which contradicts
許(ゆる)されない.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は、出(で)るなりとも欠席(けっせき)するなりとも、早(はや)めに連絡(れんらく)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は、出(で)る<b>なり</b>欠席(けっせき)する<b>なり</b>、早(はや)めに連絡(れんらく)してください。

Two courses of action are being offered, which is `なり…なり`. `なりとも` means
"even a little" and cannot attach to a verb at all.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 多少(たしょう)なりとも事情(じじょう)を知(し)っている者(もの)は申(もう)し出(で)てほしい。
(b) 多少(たしょう)なりとも説明(せつめい)があってもよかったのではないか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is modest and inviting. The bar is being lowered so that people who know only a
fragment will still come forward; the speaker is making it easy to volunteer.

(b) is a complaint. The same lowered bar is now being used against the listener -
even that minimum was not provided. The word has not changed; the sentence around
it has turned it from an invitation into a rebuke.

</details>

# もしくは

**Meaning** · or - the clerical "or" of forms, contracts and notices

`もしくは` joins two things either of which will do. It is the driest member of the
"or" family: no doubt, no hedging, no question being put to anybody, just two
acceptable alternatives set down side by side. Where a document says
<code>もしくは</code>, a person says か.

## Usage

It stands between the two alternatives and never changes shape.

**Between nouns.** This is the overwhelming majority of its uses:
印鑑(いんかん)もしくはサイン, 交換(こうかん)もしくは返金(へんきん).

**Between predicates or clauses.** The first half usually ends in か:
<code>お電話(でんわ)いただくか、もしくはフォームからご連絡(れんらく)ください</code>.

**In a list of three or more,** mark only the last boundary:
<code>現金(げんきん)、カード、もしくは電子(でんし)マネー</code>. Putting
`もしくは` between every pair reads as heavily in Japanese as "or" repeated in
English does.

Two things it cannot do. It cannot open a sentence as an adverb meaning "possibly"
- that sense belongs to あるいは. And it cannot ask the listener to choose; a
question with two options in it takes それとも.

The kanji spelling <code>若(も)しくは</code> is standard in statutes and in
older official prose. Everywhere else it is written in kana.

## Examples

<pre>
印鑑(いんかん)<b>もしくは</b>サインをお願(ねが)いします。
Please provide a seal or a signature.
</pre>

<pre>
ご変更(へんこう)のご連絡(れんらく)は、電話(でんわ)<b>もしくは</b>専用(せんよう)フォームからお願(ねが)いいたします。
Please notify us of any change by telephone or through the dedicated form.
</pre>

<pre>
保護者(ほごしゃ)、<b>もしくは</b>それに代(か)わる方(かた)の署名(しょめい)が必要(ひつよう)です。
The signature of a parent, or of someone acting in their place, is required.
</pre>

<pre>
不良品(ふりょうひん)につきましては、交換(こうかん)<b>もしくは</b>返金(へんきん)にて対応(たいおう)させていただきます。
In the case of a defective item we will either exchange it or issue a refund.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written, formal, and slightly institutional. Its natural habitat is application
forms, terms of service, manuals, contracts, notices on a wall and the sentences
of a public announcement. In careful business speech and in academic presentations
it turns up quite normally; between friends it does not, and a
<code>もしくは</code> in a text message reads as though the sender were quoting
company policy at you.

Compared with あるいは it is narrower. `あるいは` carries a second, reflective life
as an adverb of possibility - あるいは間(ま)に合(あ)うかもしれない - and
`もしくは` has nothing of the kind. It only ever connects. That narrowness is why
it feels so administrative: it does one job, and the job is listing what is
permitted.

<div class="note">

**Note** - in statutory drafting, when alternatives are nested, the larger division
takes <code>又(また)は</code> and the smaller one inside it takes
<code>若(も)しくは</code>. So
<code>父(ちち)若(も)しくは母(はは)又(また)は後見人(こうけんにん)</code>
means "(father or mother), or guardian". This is a drafting convention, not a rule
of the ordinary language: in everyday writing the two words are interchangeable.

</div>

## Contrast

### もしくは vs または

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in ordinary writing, interchangeable. In legal drafting they
are ranked: <code>又(また)は</code> splits the big alternatives,
`もしくは` splits inside one of them.

</div>

<pre>
学生証(がくせいしょう)<b>または</b>在学証明書(ざいがくしょうめいしょ)をご持参(じさん)ください。
Please bring your student card or a certificate of enrolment.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)<b>若(も)しくは</b>家族(かぞく)又(また)は代理人(だいりにん)が手続(てつづ)きを行(おこな)う。
The procedure is carried out by the person themselves or a family member, or else by a representative.
</pre>

In the first sentence nothing is nested and either word would be correct;
<code>または</code> is marginally the commoner on printed forms. In the second, the
ranking is doing real work: the person and their family form one group, and the
representative stands outside it. Read a legal sentence with this in mind and an
otherwise ambiguous list resolves itself.

### もしくは vs あるいは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - as connectors they overlap. Only `あるいは` can also mean
"possibly", and only `あるいは` is comfortable weighing two explanations rather
than offering two permitted options.

</div>

<pre>
申請(しんせい)は本人(ほんにん)<b>もしくは</b>代理人(だいりにん)が行(おこな)ってください。
The application should be made by the applicant or by a representative.
</pre>

<pre>
原因(げんいん)は操作(そうさ)ミス、<b>あるいは</b>機械(きかい)の不具合(ふぐあい)だろう。
The cause is probably operator error, or possibly a fault in the machine.
</pre>

The first lists what is allowed; the second weighs what might be true. Put
`もしくは` into the second sentence and it stops sounding like a writer thinking and
starts sounding like a form. In casual speech neither is right - there, か does the
work: 操作(そうさ)ミスか機械(きかい)の故障(こしょう)だろう.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)<b>もしくは</b>明後日(あさって)、飲(の)みに行(い)かない？ → ✅ 明日(あした)<b>か</b>明後日(あさって)、飲(の)みに行(い)かない？
  An invitation to a friend is spoken Japanese, and spoken Japanese joins options
  with か. `もしくは` here sounds like a scheduling email.

- ❌ <b>もしくは</b>彼(かれ)はもう知(し)っているかもしれない。 → ✅ <b>あるいは</b>彼(かれ)はもう知(し)っているかもしれない。
  `もしくは` connects two items; it cannot head a sentence as a hedge. That job
  belongs to あるいは or もしかしたら.

- ❌ お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)<b>もしくは</b>カード<b>もしくは</b>電子(でんし)マネーがご利用(りよう)いただけます。 → ✅ お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)、カード、<b>もしくは</b>電子(でんし)マネーがご利用(りよう)いただけます。
  Japanese marks the last boundary of a list and lets commas do the rest.

- ❌ お飲(の)み物(もの)はコーヒー<b>もしくは</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)、どちらになさいますか。 → ✅ お飲(の)み物(もの)はコーヒーになさいますか。<b>それとも</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)になさいますか。
  Even in polite service language, putting a choice **to** someone is それとも's
  job. `もしくは` states the options that exist; it does not ask.

## Practice

**1.** 受付(うけつけ)で身分証明書(みぶんしょうめいしょ)____会員(かいいん)カードをご提示(ていじ)ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

身分証明書(みぶんしょうめいしょ)<b>もしくは</b>会員(かいいん)カード

A formal instruction naming two documents either of which is accepted - the
standard slot for this word. <code>または</code> would be equally correct here,
since nothing is nested.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 今度(こんど)の休(やす)み、海(うみ)もしくは山(やま)、どっちがいい？

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 今度(こんど)の休(やす)み、海(うみ)<b>か</b>山(やま)、どっちがいい？

Two problems at once: the register is casual, and the speaker is asking the
listener to choose. Spoken Japanese uses か here, and if you want to keep the two
options in separate questions, それとも.

</details>

**3.** Explain what the ranking tells you:
「申込者(もうしこみしゃ)若(も)しくは配偶者(はいぐうしゃ)又(また)は同居(どうきょ)のご家族(かぞく)」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The list has two levels, not three items. <code>若(も)しくは</code> binds the
applicant and their spouse into one group; <code>又(また)は</code> then sets
that whole group against a family member living at the same address.

Read as a flat list of three, the sentence loses the grouping that the drafter
built into it - which is exactly why statutes keep the two words distinct.

</details>

# ～かれ～かれ

**Meaning** · be it A or B - it makes no difference, the same thing holds either way

`かれ` is an old imperative form of the adjective, and the paired construction it
sits in is dead. What survives is a small set of fossils -
遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ, 多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ,
善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ - which behave as single adverbs and are best learned
as vocabulary. The pattern itself cannot be used to build anything new.

## Formation

Historically: adjective stem + `かれ`, twice, with the two adjectives opposites.
In practice there are three phrases and no productive rule.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective stem (fossil only) | stem + かれ | 遅(おそ)い → 遅(おそ)かれ |
| Sooner or later | 遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ | 遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ分(わ)かることだ |
| To some degree, all of them | 多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ | 誰(だれ)でも多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ |
| For better or worse | 善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ | 善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ、時代(じだい)は変(か)わった |
| Position | sentence adverb, in front of the clause | 遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ、～ |
| Modern equivalent | ～であれ～であれ／いつかは／程度(ていど)の差(さ)はあれ | |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ</code> is built on the classical
adjective 悪(あ)し, not on modern 悪(わる)い, and the reading is
**あしかれ**. A phrase that has to borrow a dead adjective to exist is a phrase you
memorise rather than analyse. The same old `かれ` also survives in
<code>良(よ)かれと思(おも)って</code>, "meaning well".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こうなることは、<b>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</b>避(さ)けられなかっただろう。
Sooner or later this was going to happen whatever we did.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)は誰(だれ)でも、<b>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</b>人前(ひとまえ)では自分(じぶん)をよく見(み)せようとする。
To a greater or lesser degree, everyone tries to look good in front of others.
</pre>

<pre>
真実(しんじつ)は<b>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</b>明(あき)らかになるものだ。
The truth comes out in the end, sooner or later.
</pre>

<pre>
どの家庭(かてい)にも、<b>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</b>言(い)えない事情(じじょう)がある。
Every household has things it cannot talk about, some more than others.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ</b>、この技術(ぎじゅつ)は人(ひと)の働(はたら)き方(かた)を変(か)えてしまった。
For better or worse, this technology has changed the way people work.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)の生活(せいかつ)を続(つづ)けていれば、<b>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</b>体(からだ)を壊(こわ)すことになる。
Carry on living like this and you will wreck your health sooner or later.
</pre>

<pre>
この地域(ちいき)の企業(きぎょう)は、<b>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</b>円安(えんやす)の影響(えいきょう)を受(う)けている。
Firms in this region have all been affected by the weak yen, to varying degrees.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ</b>自分(じぶん)で選(えら)んだ道(みち)なのだから、人(ひと)のせいにはできない。
For better or worse it was a road I chose myself, so I can hardly blame anyone else.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise the shape; learn the three phrases; do not build new ones.**
❌<code>暑(あつ)かれ寒(さむ)かれ</code> and
❌<code>高(たか)かれ安(やす)かれ</code> are not Japanese, however neatly they
follow the pattern. If you need to say "whether A or B" about something the
language has not already fossilised, the modern tools are `であれ…であれ`,
`にしろ…にしろ` and `～ても…ても`.

The three that exist are not equally archaic, and this is worth being precise
about. <code>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</code> and
<code>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</code> are in ordinary adult use - a news
report, a column, a manager summing up a situation, a friend making a serious
point. Nobody would blink at them. <code>善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ</code> is
more bookish, and belongs to editorial and essay writing rather than to
conversation. All three are written language that has been allowed into careful
speech; none of them fits a chat about the weekend.

Each carries its own colour:

- <code>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</code> announces something as **inevitable**,
  and what follows is usually unwelcome - a collapse, an exposure, a breakdown.
  Timing is conceded; the outcome is not.
- <code>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</code> makes a claim about a **whole
  class**: everybody, every family, every firm has some of this. It cannot describe
  one person on one occasion.
- <code>善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ</code> **declines to judge**. The speaker
  accepts a fact as settled and sets the question of whether it is good aside,
  usually before saying what follows from it.

The order inside each phrase is fixed. <code>早(はや)かれ遅(おそ)かれ</code>
is as odd as "later or sooner" in English.

## Contrast

### ～かれ～かれ vs ～であれ～であれ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `であれ…であれ` is the **living** version of this idea and takes
any pair of nouns you like. `かれ…かれ` is three frozen phrases, and takes nothing.

</div>

<pre>
大人(おとな)<b>であれ</b>子(こ)ども<b>であれ</b>、ルールは同(おな)じだ。
Adult or child, the rules are the same.
</pre>

<pre>
遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ、結果(けっか)は同(おな)じだったろう。
Sooner or later, the result would have been the same.
</pre>

You can put anything into the `であれ` slots - 雨(あめ)であれ雪(ゆき)であれ,
社長(しゃちょう)であれ新人(しんじん)であれ - and the sentence stays natural.
Try the same with `かれ` and you produce something no Japanese speaker has said.

### 遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ vs いつかは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いつかは` says it **will happen** some day.
<code>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</code> adds that the timing is the only thing
still open, and that no one can prevent it.

</div>

<pre>
<b>いつかは</b>自分(じぶん)の店(みせ)を持(も)ちたい。
One day I'd like to have a shop of my own.
</pre>

<pre>
この会社(かいしゃ)のやり方(かた)では、<b>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</b>人(ひと)が辞(や)めていく。
The way this company operates, people are going to start leaving sooner or later.
</pre>

`いつかは` fits a hope; the fossil does not. Put
<code>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</code> in front of your own ambition and you
sound resigned to it, as though owning a shop were a fate closing in on you.

### 多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ vs 多少(たしょう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>多少(たしょう)</code> measures **one case**: a bit,
somewhat. <code>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</code> makes a generalisation
across **all** cases, and each of them may have a different amount.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)の説明(せつめい)は<b>多少(たしょう)</b>わかりにくかった。
Today's explanation was somewhat hard to follow.
</pre>

<pre>
外国(がいこく)で暮(く)らせば、<b>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</b>苦労(くろう)するものだ。
Anyone living abroad has a hard time of it, to one degree or another.
</pre>

The first is about a single lecture and a single listener. The second is a claim
about everyone who does the thing, and could not be replaced by
<code>多少(たしょう)</code> without losing that scope. The frozen phrase
<code>大(だい)なり小(しょう)なり</code> is a close synonym of the second and
behaves the same way.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 高(たか)かれ安(やす)かれ、必要(ひつよう)なものは買(か)う。 → ✅ 高(たか)くても安(やす)くても、必要(ひつよう)なものは買(か)う。
  The pattern is not productive. Only the three fossils exist, and new pairs are
  simply wrong rather than old-fashioned.

- ❌ 善(よ)かれ悪(わる)しかれ → ✅ 善(よ)かれ<b>悪(あ)しかれ</b>
  The reading is **あしかれ**, from classical 悪(あ)し. わるしかれ is a
  mispronunciation that gives the borrowed spelling away.

- ❌ 早(はや)かれ遅(おそ)かれ終(お)わる。 → ✅ 遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ終(お)わる。
  Fixed order. The slow one comes first.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)で多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ怒(おこ)っていた。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)で多少(たしょう)怒(おこ)っていた。
  One man, one meeting. <code>多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ</code> needs a whole
  class of cases to range over.

## Practice

**1.** Only one of these is a real Japanese phrase. Which, and why?
(a) 寒(さむ)かれ暑(あつ)かれ (b) 多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) 多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ.

Both are formed the same way, and that is precisely the trap: the construction is
extinct, so being well-formed proves nothing. Only the handful of phrases the
language happened to keep are usable, and (a) is not one of them - it would have to
be 寒(さむ)くても暑(あつ)くても.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
「善(よ)かれ悪(あ)しかれ、決定(けってい)はもう下(くだ)された。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いいか悪(わる)いかは別(べつ)として、決定(けってい)はもう下(くだ)されている。

The fossil's job is to accept a fact while refusing to judge it, and
<code>～かは別(べつ)として</code> does exactly that in plain modern Japanese.

</details>

**3.** Where would you expect to read this sentence?
「遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ、制度(せいど)の見直(みなお)しは避(さ)けられまい。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a newspaper editorial or a commentary piece.

<code>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</code> is at home in that register, and the
ending <code>まい</code> confirms it - a written negative conjecture that has no
place in conversation. Spoken, this would be
<code>いつかは見直(みなお)さないといけなくなるだろう</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新人(しんじん)のうちは早(はや)かれ遅(おそ)かれ失敗(しっぱい)するものだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新人(しんじん)のうちは<b>遅(おそ)かれ早(はや)かれ</b>失敗(しっぱい)するものだ。

The two halves cannot be swapped. Set phrases are stored whole, and the order is
part of what is stored.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)には多少(たしょう)問題(もんだい)がある。
(b) 新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)には多(おお)かれ少(すく)なかれ問題(もんだい)がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about this one system and measures the trouble: there are some problems,
not many. It is a mild criticism of a specific thing.

(b) generalises. It says that new systems as a class always bring problems, in
varying amounts, and it therefore excuses this one as much as it criticises it -
which is why (b) sounds like a line from a report and (a) like a remark in a
meeting.

</details>

# ～か否か

**Meaning** · whether or not - the written, formal counterpart of かどうか (read か**いな**か)

否(いな) is the classical "no", the word that answers a yes-or-no question in the
negative. <code>か否(いな)か</code> therefore spells out both branches - "whether
it is so or it is not" - and hands the pair to a verb that decides, examines,
disputes or sets them aside. The meaning is exactly かどうか's. The difference is
that this one belongs to documents.

## Formation

A plain, non-polite form goes in front, and nouns and な-adjectives attach bare or
with `である`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + か否(いな)か | 行(い)く → 行(い)くか否(いな)か |
| Verb (past) | ～た + か否(いな)か | 受(う)け取(と)ったか否(いな)か |
| い-adjective | plain form + か否(いな)か | 正(ただ)しいか否(いな)か |
| な-adjective | stem（+である）+ か否(いな)か | 適切(てきせつ)か否(いな)か |
| Noun | bare noun（+である）+ か否(いな)か | 事実(じじつ)であるか否(いな)か |
| Set frames | ～か否(いな)かは別(べつ)として／～か否(いな)かにかかわらず | 勝(か)てるか否(いな)かは別(べつ)として |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `だ` in front of it.
❌<code>必要(ひつよう)だか否(いな)か</code> → ✅
<code>必要(ひつよう)か否(いな)か</code>, or the more formal
<code>必要(ひつよう)であるか否(いな)か</code>. This is the same restriction
かどうか has, and it is broken for the same reason: learners hear a copula where the
noun is in fact standing bare.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
その情報(じょうほう)が正(ただ)しい<b>か否(いな)か</b>、まだ確認(かくにん)できていない。
Whether that information is correct has not yet been established.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>か否(いな)か</b>は、各自(かくじ)の判断(はんだん)に任(まか)せます。
Whether or not to take part is left to each person to decide.
</pre>

<pre>
成功(せいこう)する<b>か否(いな)か</b>は別(べつ)として、挑戦(ちょうせん)する価値(かち)はある。
Leaving aside whether it will succeed, it is worth attempting.
</pre>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)を満(み)たしている<b>か否(いな)か</b>にかかわらず、書類(しょるい)はすべて受(う)け付(つ)けます。
All documents will be accepted, regardless of whether they meet the conditions.
</pre>

<pre>
この規定(きてい)が今回(こんかい)の件(けん)に適用(てきよう)される<b>か否(いな)か</b>が争点(そうてん)となった。
Whether this provision applies to the present case became the point at issue.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)の同意(どうい)があった<b>か否(いな)か</b>が、裁判(さいばん)の焦点(しょうてん)である。
Whether or not the person consented is the focus of the trial.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい薬(くすり)が有効(ゆうこう)である<b>か否(いな)か</b>を、三年(さんねん)かけて検証(けんしょう)する。
Over three years we will test whether the new drug is effective.
</pre>

<pre>
提案(ていあん)を受(う)け入(い)れる<b>か否(いな)か</b>で、委員会(いいんかい)は夜(よる)まで議論(ぎろん)を続(つづ)けた。
The committee argued into the evening over whether to accept the proposal.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and largely written: judgments and contracts, academic papers, newspaper
copy, committee minutes, the summing-up in a formal speech. Unlike some N1 forms it
is not archaic - a lawyer, a chair of a meeting or a television reporter will say
it out loud without a trace of self-consciousness. What it will not do is fit a
casual sentence. 「明日(あした)行(い)くか否(いな)か、まだ決(き)めてない」
is the kind of joke a Japanese speaker might make about their own indecision, and
straight-faced it sounds absurd. In speech, かどうか.

It attracts a particular set of predicates: 判断(はんだん)する,
検討(けんとう)する, 問(と)う, 争(あらそ)う, 確(たし)かめる, and the
nominal frames ～が焦点(しょうてん)だ, ～が問題(もんだい)だ. That is because
its job is to name a **yes-or-no question as an object** - something to be settled
by a body that has the authority to settle it.

Two frames are worth memorising whole.
<code>～か否(いな)かは別(べつ)として</code> parks the question and moves on,
and <code>～か否(いな)かにかかわらず</code> declares the answer irrelevant.
Both are standard moves in formal argument, and both are commoner than the bare
pattern.

## Contrast

### ～か否(いな)か vs ～かどうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different clothes. `かどうか` goes anywhere;
<code>か否(いな)か</code> needs a formal sentence around it.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が来(く)る<b>かどうか</b>、まだわからない。
I don't know yet whether he's coming.
</pre>

<pre>
契約(けいやく)が有効(ゆうこう)である<b>か否(いな)か</b>を慎重(しんちょう)に判断(はんだん)する必要(ひつよう)がある。
It will be necessary to determine carefully whether the contract is valid.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence turns pompous while the second turns chatty. Note
also what surrounds each: `かどうか` sits happily with わからない and まだ, while
<code>か否(いな)か</code> pulls in 慎重(しんちょう)に,
判断(はんだん)する and 必要(ひつよう)がある. Register is a property of the
whole sentence.

### ～か否(いな)か vs ～か～ないか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>か否(いな)か</code> **abbreviates** the negative
branch. `～か～ないか` **spells it out**, and is the ordinary spoken way of putting
the same fork.

</div>

<pre>
行(い)く<b>か行(い)かないか</b>、はっきりさせてほしい。
Make up your mind whether you're going or not.
</pre>

<pre>
支援(しえん)を続(つづ)ける<b>か否(いな)か</b>、来月(らいげつ)の会議(かいぎ)で決定(けってい)される。
Whether support is to continue will be decided at next month's meeting.
</pre>

Repeating the verb is emphatic and concrete; it makes the two branches feel like
two real futures, and it is what you say when you are pressing somebody. The
compressed form is impersonal, and no one is being pressed - a body will decide in
due course.

### ～か否(いな)か vs ～や否(いな)や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same 否(いな) in two unrelated patterns.
<code>か否(いな)か</code> = "whether or not"; <code>や否(いな)や</code> = "the
moment that". Look at the particle in front, not the kanji.

</div>

<pre>
辞(や)める<b>か否(いな)か</b>で何日(なんにち)も迷(まよ)った。
I agonised for days over whether or not to quit.
</pre>

<pre>
ベルが鳴(な)る<b>や否(いな)や</b>、生徒(せいと)たちは教室(きょうしつ)を飛(と)び出(だ)した。
The instant the bell rang, the pupils shot out of the classroom.
</pre>

`か` marks a question, so <code>か否(いな)か</code> is a question with its
negative half attached. `や` is doing something else entirely, and the phrase means
"no sooner than". Both are written forms and both are read with いな, which is why
the pair is worth fixing in memory now rather than in an exam.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この方法(ほうほう)が安全(あんぜん)だ<b>か否(いな)か</b>を検討(けんとう)する。 → ✅ この方法(ほうほう)が安全(あんぜん)<b>か否(いな)か</b>を検討(けんとう)する。
  な-adjectives and nouns attach bare. If you want the extra weight, use
  <code>安全(あんぜん)であるか否(いな)か</code> - never `だ`.

- ❌ 参加(さんか)します<b>か否(いな)か</b>を教(おし)えてください。 → ✅ 参加(さんか)する<b>か否(いな)か</b>をお知(し)らせください。
  The embedded clause stays plain. Politeness is carried by the main verb at the
  end of the sentence, not inside the question.

- ❌ 何(なに)を注文(ちゅうもん)する<b>か否(いな)か</b>決(き)めましょう。 → ✅ 何(なに)を注文(ちゅうもん)する<b>か</b>決(き)めましょう。
  否(いな) answers a yes-or-no question. With a question word in the clause there
  is no negative branch to attach.

- ❌ 今夜(こんや)飲(の)みに行(い)く<b>か否(いな)か</b>、決(き)めた？ → ✅ 今夜(こんや)飲(の)みに行(い)く<b>かどうか</b>、決(き)めた？
  Not wrong so much as ridiculous: a courtroom phrase in a message about drinks.

# ～のみか

**Meaning** · not only that, but - and the second thing is worse, or bigger, or harder to believe

`のみ` is the written 限定(げんてい) particle, "only". `のみか` asks whether that
is all - and answers no. The clause it introduces goes further than the first: the
damage did not stop there, the man did not merely fail to thank anyone, he
complained. The escalation is not optional. Without it the sentence sounds as if
half of it had gone missing.

## Formation

Plain forms in front; nouns attach bare and な-adjectives take `である`. The second
clause almost always carries も, さえ or まで.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のみか | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れるのみか |
| Verb (past) | ～た + のみか | 遅刻(ちこく)したのみか |
| い-adjective | plain form + のみか | 高(たか)いのみか |
| な-adjective | ～である + のみか | 危険(きけん)であるのみか |
| Noun | Noun + のみか | 国内(こくない)のみか |
| Second clause | ～も／さえ／まで + predicate | …のみか、文句(もんく)まで言(い)った |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>のみならず</code> is the same word with a negative conjunctive
ending, and it means the same thing. It is several times commoner than
<code>のみか</code> in modern writing, and if you only ever produce one of the two,
produce that one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
その案(あん)は時間(じかん)がかかる<b>のみか</b>、費用(ひよう)も大(おお)きい。
That plan is not only slow but expensive as well.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は礼(れい)を言(い)わない<b>のみか</b>、文句(もんく)まで言(い)い出(だ)した。
Not only did he not thank anyone, he actually started complaining.
</pre>

<pre>
批判(ひはん)は野党(やとう)<b>のみか</b>、与党(よとう)内(ない)からも上(あ)がった。
The criticism came not only from the opposition but from within the governing party.
</pre>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は効果(こうか)がない<b>のみか</b>、かえって不公平(ふこうへい)を生(う)んでいる。
This system is not merely ineffective; it is actively creating unfairness.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は三(さん)か国語(こくご)を話(はな)す<b>のみか</b>、通訳(つうやく)の資格(しかく)まで持(も)っている。
She speaks three languages, and on top of that holds an interpreter's qualification.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)は農作物(のうさくもつ)に被害(ひがい)を与(あた)えた<b>のみか</b>、交通(こうつう)網(もう)をも寸断(すんだん)した。
The typhoon damaged crops and, beyond that, severed the transport network.
</pre>

<pre>
遅刻(ちこく)した<b>のみか</b>、配(くば)る資料(しりょう)まで忘(わす)れてきたのでは話(はなし)にならない。
Turning up late is one thing, but forgetting the handouts as well is beyond excuse.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)ができてからというもの、通勤(つうきん)が楽(らく)になった<b>のみか</b>、町(まち)の人口(じんこう)までが増(ふ)え始(はじ)めた。
Since the station opened, commuting has got easier and the town's population has even begun to grow.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is written Japanese, and old-fashioned written Japanese at that. It belongs to essays,
editorials, formal reports and the kind of prose that is composed rather than
spoken. Nobody says it over lunch. If you produce it in conversation the effect is
of somebody quoting a leader column at you, and the safe modern choices are
`ばかりか` in writing and `だけでなく` anywhere.

That said, it is not archaic in the way `～んがため` is. It is simply narrow: a
writer reaching for a slightly literary "not only" still has it available, and you
will meet it often enough in print to need to recognise it instantly.

Two properties matter more than the register.

**It escalates.** The second clause has to outdo the first - bigger, worse, more
surprising. <code>世界(せかい)的(てき)に有名(ゆうめい)であるのみか、日本(にほん)でも知(し)られている</code>
is back to front, because being known at home is the smaller claim. Reverse the two
and the sentence works.

**It cannot ask for anything.** Like `ばかりか`, it describes; it does not instruct.
❌<code>野菜(やさい)のみか果物(くだもの)も買(か)ってきて</code> is
impossible. Requests, invitations and intentions take `だけでなく`, which is
neutral about who is doing what.

There is a faint negative pull as well. `のみか` is used slightly more often for
piling up bad news than good, and the 遅刻(ちこく) and 台風(たいふう)
sentences above are its most typical shape. It is a tendency, not a rule - the
sentence about the languages is perfectly normal.

## Contrast

### ～のみか vs ～ばかりか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical in meaning. `ばかりか` is the one modern Japanese
actually uses; `のみか` is its bookish elder.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は道(みち)に迷(まよ)った<b>ばかりか</b>、財布(さいふ)まで落(お)としてしまった。
He not only got lost, he lost his wallet into the bargain.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)は工場(こうじょう)を止(と)めた<b>のみか</b>、地域(ちいき)の経済(けいざい)にも影響(えいきょう)を及(およ)ぼした。
The accident not only halted the plant but affected the local economy as well.
</pre>

Both sentences would survive the swap; the second would merely get lighter, and the
first slightly grander. Choose by the company the sentence keeps. Anything with
しまった, まで and a rueful tone wants `ばかりか`; anything with
及(およ)ぼす and 地域(ちいき)の経済(けいざい) can carry `のみか`.

### ～のみか vs ～のみならず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same word, one ending apart. `のみならず` is the standard
written form and is heard in formal speech; `のみか` is confined to writing.

</div>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の決定(けってい)は社員(しゃいん)<b>のみならず</b>、取引先(とりひきさき)にも影響(えいきょう)する。
This decision affects not only our staff but our business partners.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の決定(けってい)は社員(しゃいん)<b>のみか</b>、取引先(とりひきさき)にまで影響(えいきょう)する。
This decision affects our staff and reaches as far as our business partners.
</pre>

A director could say the first out loud in a meeting. The second reads as a written
line, and the まで in it is doing what `のみか` likes best - pushing the second item
further out. Where `のみならず` is content simply to add,
`のみか` prefers to exceed.

### ～のみか vs ～だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけでなく` is the everyday, register-neutral "not only", and
it can appear in requests and plans. `のみか` is formal, written, and describes
what is already so.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は資料(しりょう)<b>だけでなく</b>、パソコンも持(も)ってきてください。
Tomorrow please bring your laptop as well as the handouts.
</pre>

<pre>
報告書(ほうこくしょ)は数字(すうじ)が古(ふる)い<b>のみか</b>、出典(しゅってん)さえ示(しめ)されていない。
The report not only uses outdated figures; it does not even give its sources.
</pre>

The first is an instruction, and `のみか` cannot be an instruction. The second is a
verdict on a document, and `だけでなく` would be too mild for the さえ that follows.
When in doubt about which to produce, `だけでなく` is almost never wrong.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 飲(の)み物(もの)<b>のみか</b>お菓子(かし)も買(か)ってきてね。 → ✅ 飲(の)み物(もの)<b>だけでなく</b>お菓子(かし)も買(か)ってきてね。
  Two problems at once: `のみか` cannot carry a request, and the casual ending is
  far below its register.

- ❌ この機械(きかい)は操作(そうさ)が簡単(かんたん)だ<b>のみか</b>、価格(かかく)も安(やす)い。 → ✅ この機械(きかい)は操作(そうさ)が簡単(かんたん)である<b>のみか</b>、価格(かかく)も安(やす)い。
  な-adjectives take `である` in front of `のみ`. The plain `だ` cannot support it.

- ❌ 国内(こくない)で問題(もんだい)になっている<b>のみか</b>、この町(まち)でも議論(ぎろん)されている。 → ✅ この町(まち)で議論(ぎろん)されている<b>のみか</b>、国内(こくない)でも問題(もんだい)になっている。
  The second half has to go further than the first. Ordered the other way round the
  sentence deflates instead of building.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は楽(たの)しかった<b>のみか</b>ね。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は楽(たの)しかった<b>ばかりか</b>、思(おも)わぬ再会(さいかい)まであった。
  `のみか` is not a sentence ending and cannot take a final particle. It joins two
  clauses, and the second clause is the point of it.

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れたのみか、謝(あやま)りもしなかった。
(b) 彼(かれ)は約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れただけでなく、謝(あやま)りもしなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reads as written comment - a line from an essay or a formal complaint. The
`のみか` frames the missing apology as the greater offence, and the whole sentence
has the tone of a considered judgement.

(b) says the same two facts in ordinary Japanese and could be spoken to a friend.
It adds rather than escalates: both things happened, and the speaker is annoyed
about both.

</details>

# ～にとどまらず

**Meaning** · it does not stop at A - the thing spread past a boundary you would have expected to hold it

とどまる is "to stay within, to stop at", and `にとどまらず` is its written negative:
the influence, the damage, the popularity, the argument did not stay where it
started. A is not simply one item on a list. It is a **limit** - a place, a field,
a group, a period - and the sentence is about that limit being crossed.

## Formation

Nouns are the normal input; a dictionary-form verb is also possible. The second
clause almost always carries も, まで or 及(およ)ぶ.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + にとどまらず | 国内(こくない)にとどまらず |
| Verb | dictionary form + にとどまらず | 指摘(してき)する → 指摘(してき)するにとどまらず |
| な-adjective | ～である + にとどまらず | 便利(べんり)であるにとどまらず |
| Second clause | ～にも／～にまで + 及(およ)ぶ・広(ひろ)がる | …海外(かいがい)にまで及(およ)んだ |
| Sentence-final | ～にとどまらない | 影響(えいきょう)は一社(いっしゃ)にとどまらない |
| Affirmative counterpart | ～にとどまる | 被害(ひがい)は一部(いちぶ)にとどまった |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the affirmative <code>にとどまる</code> is worth learning alongside it,
because news writing uses the two as a pair.
<code>死者(ししゃ)は出(で)ず、けが人(にん)三名(さんめい)にとどまった</code>
means the damage was contained at that figure. Recognising the positive makes the
negative obvious.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
このアニメの人気(にんき)は若者(わかもの)<b>にとどまらず</b>、親(おや)の世代(せだい)にも広(ひろ)がっている。
The show's popularity is not confined to the young; it has spread to their parents' generation.
</pre>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)は一社(いっしゃ)<b>にとどまらず</b>、業界(ぎょうかい)全体(ぜんたい)に及(およ)んだ。
The damage did not stop at one company but reached the industry as a whole.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の活動(かつどう)は音楽(おんがく)<b>にとどまらず</b>、映画(えいが)や舞台(ぶたい)にも広(ひろ)がった。
His work has gone beyond music into film and the stage.
</pre>

<pre>
停電(ていでん)の影響(えいきょう)は工場(こうじょう)<b>にとどまらず</b>、周辺(しゅうへん)の住宅(じゅうたく)にまで及(およ)んだ。
The effects of the power cut were not limited to the plant; they extended to homes in the area.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げは電気(でんき)代(だい)<b>にとどまらず</b>、食品(しょくひん)や交通費(こうつうひ)にも広(ひろ)がりつつある。
The rises are no longer confined to electricity; they are spreading to food and fares.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)では問題(もんだい)を指摘(してき)する<b>にとどまらず</b>、具体的(ぐたいてき)な対策(たいさく)まで示(しめ)された。
The meeting did not merely identify the problem; concrete measures were put forward.
</pre>

<pre>
支援(しえん)は物資(ぶっし)を送(おく)る<b>にとどまらず</b>、現地(げんち)での住宅(じゅうたく)再建(さいけん)にまで及(およ)んでいる。
The aid goes beyond sending supplies as far as rebuilding housing on the ground.
</pre>

<pre>
この研究(けんきゅう)の意義(いぎ)は、学問(がくもん)の世界(せかい)<b>にとどまらない</b>だろう。
The significance of this research will not, I think, remain within the academic world.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and formal, and completely current: newspapers, company reports, academic
prose, television commentary, the body of a briefing. It is not literary in the way
`のみか` is - a manager giving a presentation says it without ceremony - but you
would not use it about your own weekend.

What it needs is **scale**. The A slot has to be something a reader could have
imagined as a boundary. 国内(こくない), 一部(いちぶ)の社員(しゃいん),
昨年(さくねん), 音楽(おんがく)の分野(ぶんや) all work. A single object does
not: 「私(わたし)は英語(えいご)にとどまらず、中国語(ちゅうごくご)も勉強(べんきょう)している」
sounds absurdly grand, because one person's language study is not a frontier that
anything overflows. That sentence wants `だけでなく`.

The tone is usually **serious and often unwelcome**. Damage spreading, criticism
spreading, price rises spreading - these are its commonest subjects, and the
sentence carries a faint alarm. It handles good news perfectly well
(人気(にんき) spreading, an artist's range widening), but even then the feeling
is of something growing past its container.

<code>単(たん)に…にとどまらず</code> is a common intensification, and marks the
first item explicitly as the smaller, more obvious one.

## Contrast

### ～にとどまらず vs ～だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけでなく` **adds** B to A. `にとどまらず` says A was a
**limit** and the thing went past it.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>だけでなく</b>、品(しな)ぞろえもいい。
This shop isn't just cheap; it has a good range too.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)の評判(ひょうばん)は近所(きんじょ)<b>にとどまらず</b>、市外(しがい)からも客(きゃく)を集(あつ)めている。
The shop's reputation has spread beyond the neighbourhood and is drawing customers from outside the city.
</pre>

Cheapness and range are two qualities side by side, so `だけでなく` fits and
`にとどまらず` would be nonsense - cheapness is not a boundary. The second sentence
has a real edge to cross, and `だけでなく` there would lose the sense of spreading
outward.

### ～にとどまらず vs ～に限(かぎ)らず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> says A was **never the only
case**. `にとどまらず` says it **started** at A and has since gone further.

</div>

<pre>
土日(どにち)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、平日(へいじつ)も混(こ)んでいます。
It's busy on weekdays too, not just at weekends.
</pre>

<pre>
混雑(こんざつ)は土日(どにち)<b>にとどまらず</b>、平日(へいじつ)にまで広(ひろ)がってきた。
The crowding is no longer just at weekends; it has spread into the working week.
</pre>

The first is a standing fact, and could have been true for years. The second
describes a change over time: it used to be weekends, and now it is not. That is
the whole difference, and it is a difference of **movement**.

### ～にとどまらず vs ～はもとより

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はもとより` treats A as **too obvious to argue about** and adds
B. `にとどまらず` treats A as a **boundary** and reports that it failed to hold.

</div>

<pre>
新型(しんがた)は性能(せいのう)<b>はもとより</b>、デザインも評価(ひょうか)されている。
The new model is praised for its design as well as, of course, its performance.
</pre>

<pre>
議論(ぎろん)は価格(かかく)<b>にとどまらず</b>、会社(かいしゃ)の方針(ほうしん)そのものに及(およ)んだ。
The discussion went beyond price to the company's policy itself.
</pre>

`はもとより` is about what the reader already grants; it takes A for granted so that
B can be the news. `にとどまらず` is about extent, and both halves are news. They
overlap in translation - both come out as "not only" - and pull apart the moment
you ask whether A is *obvious* or *a limit*.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)は英語(えいご)<b>にとどまらず</b>、中国語(ちゅうごくご)も習(なら)っています。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は英語(えいご)<b>だけでなく</b>、中国語(ちゅうごくご)も習(なら)っています。
  One learner's two languages are not a boundary being overflowed. The pattern
  needs a scope big enough to be crossed.

- ❌ 影響(えいきょう)は国内(こくない)に<b>とどまらないで</b>、海外(かいがい)にも及(およ)んだ。 → ✅ 影響(えいきょう)は国内(こくない)に<b>とどまらず</b>、海外(かいがい)にも及(およ)んだ。
  This is a written pattern and takes the written negative `ず`. `ないで` drops the
  sentence two registers and does not join clauses this way.

- ❌ この制度(せいど)は便利(べんり)な<b>にとどまらず</b>、費用(ひよう)も安(やす)い。 → ✅ この制度(せいど)は便利(べんり)である<b>にとどまらず</b>、費用(ひよう)も安(やす)い。
  A な-adjective needs `である` before に. In practice a plain
  <code>便利(べんり)なだけでなく</code> is the more natural sentence anyway.

- ❌ 世界(せかい)中(じゅう)<b>にとどまらず</b>、日本(にほん)でも話題(わだい)になっている。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)国内(こくない)<b>にとどまらず</b>、世界(せかい)中(じゅう)で話題(わだい)になっている。
  The movement is outward. Putting the wider scope first makes the second clause an
  anticlimax.

## Practice

# ～はおろか

**Meaning** · A is out of the question - and it turns out even B, which is far easier, is impossible too

`はおろか` sets a thing aside as not worth discussing and then knocks away the floor
underneath it. The item in front of it is the **harder, further, more ambitious**
one; the item after it is something basic that nobody would have thought to doubt -
and that is the one the sentence denies. English reverses the order, which is
exactly why learners get this backwards: 「漢字(かんじ)はおろか、ひらがなも書(か)けない」
is "he can't write hiragana, **let alone** kanji".

## Formation

A noun in front, and after it a second noun marked with も, さえ or すら. The
predicate is negative, or describes something bad.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (the greater thing) | Noun + はおろか | 留学(りゅうがく)はおろか |
| Second item (the floor) | Noun + も／さえ／すら + negative | ひらがなも書(か)けない |
| Verb idea | ～こと + はおろか | 歩(ある)くことはおろか |
| Whole shape | A はおろか B さえ …ない | 返事(へんじ)はおろか挨拶(あいさつ)さえない |
| Modern equivalent | A どころか B も …ない | 漢字(かんじ)どころかひらがなも書(か)けない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can put "let alone" in front of the **first** Japanese
noun in your English translation, the order is wrong. The first noun is the one
English puts last.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
その村(むら)には病院(びょういん)<b>はおろか</b>、店(みせ)が一軒(いっけん)もなかった。
There was not a single shop in the village, let alone a hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は返事(へんじ)<b>はおろか</b>、目(め)を合(あ)わせようともしなかった。
He wouldn't even meet my eye, never mind answer me.
</pre>

<pre>
退院(たいいん)したばかりの父(ちち)は、散歩(さんぽ)<b>はおろか</b>一人(ひとり)で起(お)き上(あ)がることさえできなかった。
Just out of hospital, my father could not so much as sit up on his own, let alone go for a walk.
</pre>

<pre>
三日(みっか)続(つづ)いた停電(ていでん)で、風呂(ふろ)<b>はおろか</b>料理(りょうり)もできなかった。
Three days without power meant we could not even cook, let alone bathe.
</pre>

<pre>
昇進(しょうしん)<b>はおろか</b>、契約(けいやく)の更新(こうしん)すら危(あや)ういと言(い)われた。
I was told that even the renewal of my contract was in doubt, to say nothing of promotion.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)のあと、会社(かいしゃ)は謝罪(しゃざい)<b>はおろか</b>、事実(じじつ)の説明(せつめい)すらしていない。
Since the accident the company has not even explained what happened, let alone apologised.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)<b>はおろか</b>、県(けん)の外(そと)へ出(で)たこともない少年(しょうねん)だった。
He was a boy who had never been outside the prefecture, let alone studied abroad.
</pre>

<pre>
入社(にゅうしゃ)した年(とし)は、敬語(けいご)<b>はおろか</b>電話(でんわ)の取(と)り次(つ)ぎさえ満足(まんぞく)にできなかった。
In my first year I could not even pass on a phone call properly, never mind handle honorifics.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is a form to read, not to say.** It lives in novels and memoirs, in essays,
in editorial columns and in written complaints - places where a sentence has been
composed. Spoken, it sounds like narration, and a Japanese speaker telling you
about their bad week will use `どころか` instead:
<code>旅行(りょこう)どころか、休(やす)みも取(と)れなかったよ</code>. Learn
`はおろか` to recognise on the page, and produce `どころか`.

Two hard restrictions come with it, and both are about **direction**.

First, the **order**. The greater thing goes first and is dismissed; the smaller
thing comes second and is denied. Reverse them and the sentence still parses, which
is the danger - 「ひらがなはおろか漢字(かんじ)も書(か)けない」 is grammatical
Japanese that says something faintly silly, since being unable to write kanji is
hardly a surprise once hiragana has gone.

Second, the **polarity**. What follows is negative, or at least unwelcome. The
pattern reports a shortfall - what is missing, what could not be done, what was not
provided - and it cannot be turned round to praise:
❌<code>英語(えいご)はおろか中国語(ちゅうごくご)も話(はな)せる</code> is not
possible. For an accumulation of good news, `はもちろん` or `はもとより`.

The tone is bleak or indignant. Poverty, illness, neglect, an organisation that has
failed to do the minimum: these are the subjects it keeps. Used lightly - about
having no time for a haircut - it sounds theatrical, which is occasionally the
joke.

## Contrast

### ～はおろか vs ～どころか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same order, same job, different register. `どころか` is what
people say; `はおろか` is what writers write.

</div>

<pre>
貯金(ちょきん)<b>どころか</b>、今月(こんげつ)の家賃(やちん)も払(はら)えない。
I can't even pay this month's rent, never mind save anything.
</pre>

<pre>
貯金(ちょきん)<b>はおろか</b>、明日(あす)の米(こめ)を買(か)う金(かね)もなかった。
There was no money even for tomorrow's rice, let alone anything to put by.
</pre>

The two sentences are built identically. What differs is everything around them -
今月(こんげつ)の家賃(やちん) and a plain ending against 明日(あす)の米(こめ)
and a narrative past. One more thing `どころか` can do and `はおろか` cannot: stand
alone as a contradiction, as in <code>平気(へいき)どころか、喜(よろこ)んでいた</code>
("far from being upset, he was delighted").

### ～はおろか vs ～はもちろん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - opposite polarity. `はもちろん` = "A of course, **and B as
well**", and is neutral or positive. `はおろか` = "A goes without saying, **and not
even B**", and is always a shortfall.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は地元(じもと)の人(ひと)<b>はもちろん</b>、観光客(かんこうきゃく)にも人気(にんき)がある。
The place is popular with tourists as well as, naturally, with locals.
</pre>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)当時(とうじ)は観光客(かんこうきゃく)<b>はおろか</b>、近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)さえ来(こ)なかった。
When it opened, not even the neighbours came in, let alone tourists.
</pre>

Both sentences pair locals with tourists, and the patterns pull them in opposite
directions: one accumulates, the other subtracts. The test is the predicate. If it
is affirmative and welcome, `はもちろん`; if it is negative,
`はおろか`.

### ～はおろか vs さえ・すら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they are two halves of one machine. `はおろか` **discards** the
upper item; さえ・すら **marks the floor** that then gives way. Neither does the
other's job.

</div>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しくて、昼食(ちゅうしょく)を取(と)る時間(じかん)<b>さえ</b>なかった。
I was so busy I didn't even have time for lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
残業(ざんぎょう)が続(つづ)き、休日(きゅうじつ)<b>はおろか</b>昼食(ちゅうしょく)の時間(じかん)<b>さえ</b>なかった。
With the overtime going on and on, there was no time even for lunch, let alone a day off.
</pre>

The first sentence is complete on its own: さえ alone can carry "not even". Add
`はおろか` and you gain a second, higher item to abandon first, and the fall is
longer. This is why the two turn up together so often - and why a sentence with
`はおろか` and no も, さえ or すら in the second half usually feels unfinished.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ひらがな<b>はおろか</b>漢字(かんじ)も読(よ)めない。 → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)<b>はおろか</b>ひらがなも読(よ)めない。
  The harder thing is dismissed first. Written the other way round, the sentence
  builds up to a non-surprise.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は英語(えいご)<b>はおろか</b>中国語(ちゅうごくご)も話(はな)せる。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は英語(えいご)<b>はもちろん</b>中国語(ちゅうごくご)も話(はな)せる。
  Two accomplishments are good news, and `はおろか` cannot report good news. Its
  second clause has to fail.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は飲(の)み会(かい)<b>はおろか</b>、晩(ばん)ご飯(はん)も食(た)べられなかったよ。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は飲(の)み会(かい)<b>どころか</b>、晩(ばん)ご飯(はん)も食(た)べられなかったよ。
  Structurally perfect, socially odd. The casual よ at the end gives away that this
  is speech, and speech uses `どころか`.

- ❌ 走(はし)る<b>はおろか</b>、歩(ある)くこともつらい。 → ✅ 走(はし)る<b>ことはおろか</b>、歩(ある)くこともつらい。
  A verb has to be nominalised with こと first. `はおろか` attaches to nouns.

him. The lower item is the one being denied, and the higher one has already been

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

高校(こうこう)<b>はおろか</b>中学校(ちゅうがっこう)も廃校(はいこう)になってしまった

The content is a loss, and the middle school is the more basic institution - the
floor. `はもちろん` would treat two closures as an accumulation of good news, which
is exactly wrong.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 地震(じしん)の後(あと)、この町(まち)では水(みず)はおろか電気(でんき)も通(とお)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 地震(じしん)の後(あと)、この町(まち)では電気(でんき)はおろか水(みず)<b>さえ通(とお)っていなかった</b>。

The predicate has to be negative. As written, the sentence uses a pattern of
shortfall to announce that the utilities are working, and the two halves fight each
other.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は礼(れい)を言(い)わなかった。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)は礼(れい)はおろか、会釈(えしゃく)すらしなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports one omission. She did not say thank you, and that is all the sentence
claims.

(b) reports a floor giving way. Thanks were never in prospect; what the writer is
recording is that she did not even nod - the smallest courtesy there is. The
sentence is a judgement on her, and it belongs to a written account rather than to
a conversation.

</details>

# ～もさることながら

**Meaning** · A is impressive enough in itself, but B is what really deserves the attention

さる is the classical 然(さ)る, "such, of that sort", and
<code>さることながら</code> means roughly "while being a thing that is indeed so".
The pattern therefore **grants** A - it is real, it is worth mentioning, nobody is
denying it - and then moves the spotlight on to B. Whatever the sentence is about,
B is the point.

## Formation

A noun, or a clause nominalised with こと. The `も` is part of the pattern and
cannot be dropped.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + もさることながら | 値段(ねだん)もさることながら |
| Clause | ～こと + もさることながら | 安(やす)いこともさることながら |
| な-adjective idea | ～な + こと + もさることながら | 便利(べんり)なこともさることながら |
| Second clause | B が／は + the real point | …品質(ひんしつ)のよさが評価(ひょうか)されている |
| Polite | unchanged; politeness sits at the end | …印象(いんしょう)的(てき)でした |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the weight always falls on the **second** half. If you find yourself
wanting to stress A, you have the wrong pattern; use
<code>特(とく)に</code> or put A in the second slot instead.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この宿(やど)は料理(りょうり)<b>もさることながら</b>、従業員(じゅうぎょういん)の気(き)配(くば)りがすばらしい。
The food at this inn is one thing, but it is the attentiveness of the staff that is remarkable.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)<b>もさることながら</b>、品質(ひんしつ)のよさでこの製品(せいひん)は選(えら)ばれている。
The price matters, certainly, but it is the quality that makes people choose this product.
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)<b>もさることながら</b>、彼(かれ)の集中力(しゅうちゅうりょく)には驚(おどろ)かされる。
His technique is impressive enough, but it is his concentration that astonishes me.
</pre>

<pre>
費用(ひよう)<b>もさることながら</b>、完成(かんせい)までにかかる時間(じかん)が問題(もんだい)である。
The cost is a real issue, but the bigger one is how long it will take to finish.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)の努力(どりょく)<b>もさることながら</b>、周囲(しゅうい)の支(ささ)えが大(おお)きかったと思(おも)う。
He worked hard, of course, but I think the support around him counted for even more.
</pre>

<pre>
この映画(えいが)は映像(えいぞう)の美(うつく)しさ<b>もさることながら</b>、音楽(おんがく)が忘(わす)れがたい。
The film is beautiful to look at, but it is the music that stays with you.
</pre>

<pre>
受賞(じゅしょう)した作品(さくひん)は、物語(ものがたり)の面白(おもしろ)さ<b>もさることながら</b>、文章(ぶんしょう)の正確(せいかく)さが高(たか)く評価(ひょうか)された。
The prize-winning work tells a good story, but what earned it the highest praise was the precision of its prose.
</pre>

<pre>
交通(こうつう)の便(べん)のよさ<b>もさることながら</b>、この物件(ぶっけん)は管理(かんり)が行(ゆ)き届(とど)いている点(てん)が魅力(みりょく)です。
The transport links are good, but the real attraction of this property is how well it is looked after.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal, and comfortable in both writing and careful speech. Reviews, introductions
of a speaker, awards citations, product copy, a manager's summing-up, a piece of
criticism - anywhere someone is weighing two merits and deciding which to put
first. It is a shade too dressed-up for chat: telling a friend that a ramen shop's
味(あじ)もさることながら its 量(りょう) is impressive is the register of a
magazine, not of a lunch break. There, 「味(あじ)もいいけど…」 does the job.

The commonest use by a long way is **praise**. Because it concedes A before
promoting B, it lets a speaker compliment two things without seeming to gush, and
it is the standard way of saying "yes, the obvious virtue - but look at this one".
It handles problems just as correctly (費用(ひよう)もさることながら…), and there
the effect is of a measured warning: A is bad enough, and B is worse.

One social use worth noticing. Praising a person's effort with
<code>本人(ほんにん)の努力(どりょく)もさることながら</code> and then crediting
someone else is a graceful move in Japanese: it avoids taking anything away from
them while giving the credit where the speaker thinks it belongs. Reversed - B
first, A conceded afterwards - the same content sounds grudging.

The pattern is fixed. <code>さることながら</code> without the `も` is not idiomatic,
and <code>さる</code> cannot be replaced with the modern 然(しか)り or anything
else.

## Contrast

### ～もさることながら vs ～はもちろん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はもちろん` says A **goes without saying**, and adds B as
another true thing. `もさることながら` says A is **genuinely notable**, and then
ranks B above it.

</div>

<pre>
この大学(だいがく)は研究(けんきゅう)実績(じっせき)<b>はもちろん</b>、就職(しゅうしょく)支援(しえん)にも力(ちから)を入(い)れている。
The university puts effort into careers support as well as, of course, its research record.
</pre>

<pre>
この大学(だいがく)は研究(けんきゅう)実績(じっせき)<b>もさることながら</b>、学生(がくせい)への支援(しえん)の手厚(てあつ)さで知(し)られている。
The university's research record speaks for itself, but what it is known for is how well it looks after its students.
</pre>

In the first, both facts sit level and A is simply assumed. In the second there is a
ranking, and B wins it. That ranking is the whole reason to choose this pattern;
if you do not want one, `はもちろん` is lighter and easier.

### ～もさることながら vs ～だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけでなく` **adds**. `もさることながら` **weighs**, and hands
the verdict to B.

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は語数(ごすう)が多(おお)い<b>だけでなく</b>、例文(れいぶん)も豊富(ほうふ)だ。
This dictionary has a large vocabulary and plenty of example sentences too.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は語数(ごすう)の多(おお)さ<b>もさることながら</b>、例文(れいぶん)の質(しつ)が群(ぐん)を抜(ぬ)いている。
The number of entries is impressive, but the quality of the examples is in another class.
</pre>

`だけでなく` leaves the two merits equal, and is register-neutral into the bargain.
`もさることながら` is formal and takes a position. Notice that it also prefers
**nouns of quality** - 多(おお)さ, 質(しつ), よさ - where `だけでなく` is
happy with a plain adjective clause.

### ～もさることながら vs ～にもまして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both promote the second item. `もさることながら` compares **two
different things**; `にもまして` compares the **same thing** against an earlier
state or another holder of it.

</div>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は去年(きょねん)<b>にもまして</b>暑(あつ)い。
This summer is even hotter than last year's.
</pre>

<pre>
暑(あつ)さ<b>もさることながら</b>、湿気(しっけ)のひどさが体(からだ)にこたえる。
The heat is bad enough, but it is the humidity that wears you down.
</pre>

`にもまして` needs a single scale - heat, this year against last. `もさることながら`
needs two candidates for one title - heat and humidity, competing to be the thing
worth complaining about. They are easy to keep apart once you ask what is being
compared with what.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この車(くるま)は安(やす)い<b>もさることながら</b>、燃費(ねんぴ)もいい。 → ✅ この車(くるま)は安(やす)い<b>こともさることながら</b>、燃費(ねんぴ)のよさが光(ひか)る。
  An adjective clause needs こと before the pattern, and the second half should be
  the stronger claim, not a casual addition.

- ❌ 味(あじ)<b>さることながら</b>、盛(も)り付(つ)けも美(うつく)しい。 → ✅ 味(あじ)<b>もさることながら</b>、盛(も)り付(つ)けの美(うつく)しさに驚(おどろ)いた。
  The `も` is not optional. Without it the phrase is not idiomatic Japanese.

- ❌ 給料(きゅうりょう)のよさ<b>もさることながら</b>、会社(かいしゃ)が近(ちか)いのもまあいい。 → ✅ 会社(かいしゃ)が近(ちか)いこと<b>もさることながら</b>、給料(きゅうりょう)のよさが決(き)め手(て)になった。
  The second half has to be the heavier one. Following the pattern with a shrug
  wastes it, and the sentence lands upside down.

- ❌ このケーキ、見(み)た目(め)<b>もさることながら</b>、めっちゃおいしいよ。 → ✅ このケーキ、見(み)た目(め)もいいけど、味(あじ)が本当(ほんとう)にいいよ。
  Formal pattern, slang ending. めっちゃ and <code>さることながら</code> cannot
  share a sentence.

# ～といわず～といわず

**Meaning** · not just A, not just B - the whole of it, with no distinction between the parts

`といわず` comes in pairs, and the pair names two edges of something: two parts of a
body, two ends of a day, two areas of a room. The sentence then reports a state
that ignores the boundary between them. What the pattern really says is **"and
everything in between"** - the two nouns are not a list but a way of covering a
whole surface or a whole stretch of time.

## Formation

Nouns only, and the two should belong to one continuous whole.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (place or part) | N といわず N といわず | 壁(かべ)といわず天井(てんじょう)といわず |
| Noun (time) | N といわず N といわず | 昼(ひる)といわず夜(よる)といわず |
| Predicate | a state, usually excessive | …泥(どろ)だらけだった |
| Position | before the clause it saturates | 腕(うで)といわず足(あし)といわず、傷(きず)がある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a **single** <code>といわず</code> after a quantity is a different
expression meaning "don't stop at A":
<code>一(ひと)つといわず、二(ふた)つでも三(みっ)つでもどうぞ</code>. The
saturation reading needs the pair.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
壁(かべ)<b>といわず</b>天井(てんじょう)<b>といわず</b>、落書(らくが)きだらけだった。
The walls, the ceiling - the whole place was covered in graffiti.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)<b>といわず</b>夜(よる)<b>といわず</b>、工事(こうじ)の音(おと)が続(つづ)いている。
Day and night alike, the noise of the building work goes on.
</pre>

<pre>
腕(うで)<b>といわず</b>背中(せなか)<b>といわず</b>、蚊(か)に刺(さ)された跡(あと)が残(のこ)っている。
My arms, my back, everywhere - I'm covered in mosquito bites.
</pre>

<pre>
平日(へいじつ)<b>といわず</b>休日(きゅうじつ)<b>といわず</b>、その店(みせ)の前(まえ)には列(れつ)ができている。
Weekday or weekend, there is always a queue outside that shop.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)<b>といわず</b>床(ゆか)<b>といわず</b>、読(よ)みかけの本(ほん)が積(つ)み上(あ)げてあった。
On the desk, on the floor, everywhere, there were piles of half-read books.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)<b>といわず</b>晩(ばん)<b>といわず</b>電話(でんわ)をかけてくるので、さすがに参(まい)っている。
He rings morning and night, and I have honestly had enough.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が過(す)ぎた後(あと)、道路(どうろ)<b>といわず</b>庭(にわ)<b>といわず</b>、折(お)れた枝(えだ)が散(ち)らばっていた。
After the typhoon there were broken branches strewn everywhere, over the road and the gardens alike.
</pre>

<pre>
閉(と)じられたままの工場(こうじょう)は、窓(まど)<b>といわず</b>機械(きかい)<b>といわず</b>、油(あぶら)とほこりで黒(くろ)ずんでいた。
In the long-closed factory, windows and machinery alike had gone black with oil and dust.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This belongs to **description**, and description is mostly written. Novels, essays,
travel writing, a vivid report - anywhere a writer is painting a scene. It is not
archaic and a speaker can use it, but it will sound composed rather than casual:
someone complaining about their neighbour's phone calls in this shape is telling a
story, not just grumbling.

What it nearly always describes is **too much of something**, and usually something
unwelcome: dirt, noise, damage, bites, clutter, interruptions. The construction
itself carries a note of "there was no escaping it". That is why a neutral fact
sounds odd in it - 「窓(まど)といわずドアといわず新(あたら)しい」 is strange,
because being new is not a condition that spreads.

The two nouns should be **comparable and part of one whole**. Hands and face; walls
and ceiling; morning and night. Two unrelated things -
❌「犬(いぬ)といわず天気(てんき)といわず」 - produce nothing at all, because
there is no space between them for the pattern to fill.

It shares its shape with the evaluative `といい…といい` and the two are worth
keeping apart deliberately: same slots, same rhythm, and completely different
work.

## Contrast

### ～といわず～といわず vs ～といい～といい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `といい…といい` names two **features** and passes a **verdict**.
`といわず…といわず` names two **points** and reports that a state covered
**everything between them**.

</div>

<pre>
この旅館(りょかん)は部屋(へや)<b>といい</b>風呂(ふろ)<b>といい</b>、手入(てい)れが行(ゆ)き届(とど)いている。
The rooms, the baths - this inn is beautifully kept.
</pre>

<pre>
その空(あ)き家(や)は部屋(へや)<b>といわず</b>廊下(ろうか)<b>といわず</b>、ほこりが積(つ)もっていた。
In that empty house the dust lay thick, in the rooms and along the corridor alike.
</pre>

The first sentence could be answered with "I agree" or "I don't"; it is an opinion.
The second could only be answered with "how awful"; it is a description. A quick
test: if the sentence ends in a judgement word - すばらしい, ひどい,
見事(みごと)だ - you want `といい`.

### ～といわず～といわず vs ～も～も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `も…も` says **both of these**. `といわず…といわず` says **both
of these and everything between**, and adds that the difference between them did
not matter.

</div>

<pre>
手(て)<b>も</b>顔(かお)<b>も</b>真(ま)っ黒(くろ)だった。
His hands and his face were both filthy.
</pre>

<pre>
手(て)<b>といわず</b>顔(かお)<b>といわず</b>真(ま)っ黒(くろ)だった。
He was filthy all over - hands, face, everything.
</pre>

The first counts two things. The second uses two things to stand for the whole
child. This is why `も…も` can take a precise list - 「右手(みぎて)も左手(ひだりて)も」
- and `といわず` cannot: precision is the opposite of what it is for.

### ～といわず～といわず vs ～を問(と)わず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を問(と)わず</code> declares a distinction
**irrelevant**, usually in a rule. `といわず…といわず` reports that a state **ignored
the distinction** in fact.

</div>

<pre>
このイベントは年齢(ねんれい)や経験(けいけん)<b>を問(と)わず</b>ご参加(さんか)いただけます。
Anyone may join this event, regardless of age or experience.
</pre>

<pre>
若(わか)い人(ひと)<b>といわず</b>年配(ねんぱい)の方(かた)<b>といわず</b>、会場(かいじょう)は人(ひと)で埋(う)まっていた。
Young and old alike, the hall was packed.
</pre>

<code>を問(と)わず</code> is administrative language: it sets a policy and belongs
to notices and application forms. `といわず` is descriptive language: it observes
what happened. Neither can take the other's sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 走(はし)る<b>といわず</b>歩(ある)く<b>といわず</b>、足(あし)が痛(いた)い。 → ✅ 走(はし)っても歩(ある)いても足(あし)が痛(いた)い。
  Only nouns go in front of `といわず`. For two actions, use `～ても…ても`.

- ❌ 昼(ひる)<b>といわず</b>夜(よる)<b>といわず</b>勉強(べんきょう)してください。 → ✅ 昼(ひる)<b>といわず</b>夜(よる)<b>といわず</b>勉強(べんきょう)していた。
  The pattern describes a state that came about; it cannot issue an instruction.
  Turned into a request it sounds like a curse.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は味(あじ)<b>といわず</b>接客(せっきゃく)<b>といわず</b>見事(みごと)だ。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は味(あじ)<b>といい</b>接客(せっきゃく)<b>といい</b>見事(みごと)だ。
  Flavour and service are features being appraised, and the sentence ends in a
  verdict. That is `といい`'s job.

- ❌ 窓(まど)<b>といわず</b>、部屋(へや)中(じゅう)が汚(よご)れていた。 → ✅ 窓(まど)<b>といわず</b>床(ゆか)<b>といわず</b>、部屋(へや)中(じゅう)が汚(よご)れていた。
  A lone `といわず` is read as "not just one window", which is not the meaning
  wanted. The saturation sense needs the pair.

Two parts of one flat, and boxes filling the space between them. The predicate

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "There was sand everywhere in the car, on the seats and in the boot."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

車(くるま)の中(なか)は、シート<b>といわず</b>トランク<b>といわず</b>砂(すな)だらけだった。

English does this with "everywhere … and …"; Japanese names two points and lets the
pattern fill in the rest. だらけ is a natural partner for it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この辺(あた)りは朝(あさ)といわず夜(よる)といわず静(しず)かで、とても住(す)みやすい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この辺(あた)りは朝(あさ)<b>も</b>夜(よる)<b>も</b>静(しず)かで、とても住(す)みやすい。

Quiet is not a condition that spreads and overwhelms. The pattern needs a state
there is too much of; for a pleasant fact that holds at both times, `も…も` is the
natural choice.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の服(ふく)は袖(そで)も襟(えり)も汚(よご)れていた。
(b) 彼(かれ)の服(ふく)は袖(そで)といわず襟(えり)といわず汚(よご)れていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) inspects two named parts and finds both dirty. Someone could reply that the
front was clean.

(b) uses the cuffs and the collar as evidence that the whole garment was filthy.
The claim covers everything in between, and the speaker sounds rather more appalled
than in (a).

</details>

# ～にもまして

**Meaning** · even more than A - and A was already a great deal

`まして` is the て-form of 増(ま)す, "to exceed". <code>にもまして</code> compares
the present against a standard that was **already high**: last year was hot and
this year is hotter, he was busy before and is busier now. That built-in
presupposition is the whole of the pattern. A plain より compares two things; this
compares against a benchmark and says the benchmark has been passed.

## Formation

A noun goes in front - most often a time, a period, or a question word.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (time or standard) | Noun + にもまして | 去年(きょねん)にもまして |
| Question word | 何(なに)にもまして／誰(だれ)にもまして | 何(なに)にもまして大切(たいせつ)だ |
| Clause idea | ～こと + にもまして | 勝(か)つことにもまして |
| Predicate | a comparative degree | …忙(いそが)しい／…多(おお)い |
| Kanji spelling | ～にも増(ま)して | 以前(いぜん)にも増(ま)して |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>何(なに)にもまして</code> is read **なに**にもまして.
何(なに) keeps its なに reading before に, and only becomes なん before で, だ
and の.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は例年(れいねん)<b>にもまして</b>暑(あつ)い。
This summer is even hotter than a normal year.
</pre>

<pre>
退院(たいいん)してから、父(ちち)は以前(いぜん)<b>にもまして</b>元気(げんき)だ。
Since leaving hospital my father has been even better than he was before.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)<b>にもまして</b>大切(たいせつ)なのは、家族(かぞく)の健康(けんこう)です。
What matters above everything else is that the family stays well.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の大会(たいかい)には、去年(きょねん)<b>にもまして</b>多(おお)くの参加者(さんかしゃ)が集(あつ)まった。
Even more people took part in this year's competition than last year's.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は誰(だれ)<b>にもまして</b>練習(れんしゅう)を欠(か)かさない選手(せんしゅ)だった。
He was a player who missed a training session less often than anyone.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げの後(あと)、客足(きゃくあし)は以前(いぜん)<b>にもまして</b>落(お)ち込(こ)んでいる。
Since the price rise, custom has fallen off even further than it had before.
</pre>

<pre>
震災(しんさい)を経(へ)て、この町(まち)の人(ひと)たちは以前(いぜん)<b>にもまして</b>助(たす)け合(あ)うようになった。
Since the earthquake the people of this town have come to help each other even more than they did.
</pre>

<pre>
今年度(こんねんど)は予算(よさん)が減(へ)った分(ぶん)、昨年度(さくねんど)<b>にもまして</b>効率(こうりつ)が求(もと)められる。
With the budget cut, efficiency will be demanded of us even more than it was last year.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal, and mostly written or carefully spoken: annual reports, speeches, news
copy, a new year's greeting, a manager opening a meeting. In casual conversation
people say <code>前(まえ)より</code> or
<code>前(まえ)よりもっと</code>. Nothing about `にもまして` is archaic, though -
it is simply dressed for the occasion, and
<code>以前(いぜん)にもまして</code> in particular turns up in ordinary adult
speech without ceremony.

The presupposition is what to hold on to. Saying
<code>去年(きょねん)にもまして忙(いそが)しい</code> asserts two things at once:
this year is busy, and last year was too. If A was not remarkable to begin with,
the sentence misfires - <code>昨日(きのう)にもまして暇(ひま)だ</code> is odd
unless yesterday was already notably empty.

A second restriction follows from that. A is normally a **standard** - a period, a
previous state, a benchmark, or a question word standing for "anything" or
"anyone". It is not comfortable with an arbitrary object: for "I like oranges more
than apples", Japanese wants より, because apples were never a benchmark for
anything.

<code>何(なに)にもまして</code> is worth learning as a unit. It means "above all
else" and it is how a formal sentence names its priority - in a speech, a policy
statement, or a letter of thanks.

## Contrast

### ～にもまして vs ～より（も）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `より` **compares two things**, neutrally. `にもまして`
**presupposes that A was already high** and says B goes beyond it.

</div>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)は去年(きょねん)<b>より</b>暖(あたた)かい。
This winter is warmer than last winter.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)は去年(きょねん)<b>にもまして</b>暖(あたた)かい。
This winter is milder still than last year's - and that was mild.
</pre>

The first is a measurement and says nothing about last winter in itself; last
winter could have been freezing. The second smuggles in a claim: last winter was
already warm. This is also why `より` handles any two nouns while `にもまして` wants
a standard - ❌<code>みかんにもましてりんごが好(す)きだ</code> is not idiomatic.

### ～にもまして vs ～以上(いじょう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - very close. <code>以上(いじょう)に</code> is the ordinary,
register-neutral "more than"; `にもまして` is formal and insists that A was already
a lot.

</div>

<pre>
試験(しけん)は思(おも)った<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>難(むずか)しかった。
The exam was harder than I expected.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)は昨年(さくねん)<b>にもまして</b>厳(きび)しい状況(じょうきょう)が続(つづ)いている。
Conditions this year have been even tougher than they were last year.
</pre>

<code>以上(いじょう)に</code> takes a clause in front of it
（思(おも)った、予想(よそう)した）which `にもまして` cannot do without こと,
and it works happily in speech. Where both are possible -
去年(きょねん)以上(いじょう)に／去年(きょねん)にもまして - the second is
the more formal, and slightly the more emphatic.

### 何(なに)にもまして vs 何(なに)よりも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning at two levels of formality.
<code>何(なに)よりも</code> is everyday; <code>何(なに)にもまして</code>
belongs to a speech or a document.

</div>

<pre>
皆(みな)さんが無事(ぶじ)で、<b>何(なに)より</b>です。
I'm just glad you're all safe.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)の確保(かくほ)が、<b>何(なに)にもまして</b>優先(ゆうせん)されるべきである。
Securing safety must take priority over everything else.
</pre>

Note that <code>何(なに)より</code> has a life of its own as a set phrase meaning
"that's the main thing", used in reply to good news;
<code>何(なに)にもまして</code> has no such use and only ever modifies a
predicate.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ケーキ<b>にもまして</b>アイスが好(す)きだ。 → ✅ ケーキ<b>より</b>アイスが好(す)きだ。
  A plain preference between two foods is a neutral comparison. `にもまして` would
  claim that cake was already a favourite of yours, which is not what was meant.

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)<b>にまして</b>忙(いそが)しい。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)<b>にもまして</b>忙(いそが)しい。
  The `も` is part of the pattern. Dropping it leaves a fragment of the verb
  増(ま)す hanging in the sentence.

- ❌ 走(はし)る<b>にもまして</b>泳(およ)ぐほうが得意(とくい)だ。 → ✅ 走(はし)る<b>より</b>泳(およ)ぐほうが得意(とくい)だ。
  A verb cannot attach directly. If you must, nominalise -
  <code>走(はし)ることにもまして</code> - but a plain より is what the sentence
  wants.

- ❌ <code>何(なに)にもまして</code> pronounced なんにもまして → ✅ <b>なに</b>にもまして
  A reading error rather than a grammar one, and a common one: 何(なに) is なん
  only before で, だ and の.

## Practice

**1.** 今年(ことし)の新入生(しんにゅうせい)は、____にもまして意欲的(いよくてき)だと感(かん)じます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

例年(れいねん)<b>にもまして</b>（or 昨年(さくねん)にもまして）

The blank has to be a standard the speaker can measure against - a normal year, or
last year. Note what the sentence quietly claims: previous intakes were keen too.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 弟(おとうと)は私(わたし)（にもまして・より）背(せ)が高(たか)くなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)<b>より</b>背(せ)が高(たか)くなった

A straightforward comparison of two people's heights. `にもまして` would presuppose
that the speaker was already remarkably tall, and would sound like a boast smuggled
into a sentence about someone else.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This year, more than ever, we are counting on your support."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本年(ほんねん)は例年(れいねん)<b>にもまして</b>、皆様(みなさま)のご支援(しえん)を頼(たよ)りにしております。

"More than ever" against an implied benchmark of previous years is the pattern's
natural home, and the formal frame - 本年(ほんねん), 皆様(みなさま),
おります - matches its register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)は誰(だれ)にましてこの計画(けいかく)に熱心(ねっしん)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は誰(だれ)<b>にもまして</b>この計画(けいかく)に熱心(ねっしん)だ。

The `も` cannot be dropped. <code>誰(だれ)にもまして</code> - "more than
anyone" - is a fixed shape, like <code>何(なに)にもまして</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)は去年(きょねん)より寒(さむ)い。
(b) 今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)は去年(きょねん)にもまして寒(さむ)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a difference in temperature and nothing more. Last winter might have
been perfectly mild.

(b) says last winter was cold - and this one is colder still. The speaker is not
measuring but complaining, and the sentence sounds a level more formal, which is
why you would expect it in a written report rather than at a bus stop.

</details>

# ～からある

**Meaning** · a good X of them; no less than X - a floor put under a figure the speaker finds striking

`から` here is the から of "from": the amount **starts** at that figure and may well
be higher. What makes the pattern more than arithmetic is the admiration built into
it. <code>30キロからある荷物(にもつ)</code> is not a measurement but a reaction -
that case must weigh thirty kilos, and look who is carrying it. It cannot be used
of a small amount, because there would be nothing to react to.

## Formation

A number and its counter come first. Which of the three endings you use depends on
what is being measured.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Weight, length, size, quantity | number + counter + からある | 30キロからある荷物(にもつ) |
| Price | number + counter + からする | 5万円(まんえん)からするワイン |
| Modifying a noun (esp. people) | number + counter + からの + Noun | 500人(にん)からの観客(かんきゃく) |
| As the predicate | …は + number + からある | その石(いし)は50キロからある |
| Past | ～からあった／からした | 100万円(まんえん)からした |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>からある</code> for how big, <code>からする</code> for how
much it cost, <code>からの</code> when the phrase is modifying a following noun.
Mixing the first two is the standard error:
❌<code>3万円(まんえん)からある時計(とけい)</code> →
✅<code>3万円(まんえん)からする時計(とけい)</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
30キロ<b>からある</b>荷物(にもつ)を一人(ひとり)で運(はこ)んだ。
He carried a case of a good thirty kilos on his own.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)には500人(にん)<b>からの</b>人(ひと)が集(あつ)まった。
No fewer than five hundred people gathered at the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に、5センチ<b>からある</b>書類(しょるい)の束(たば)が置(お)いてあった。
On the desk lay a stack of documents a good five centimetres thick.
</pre>

<pre>
あの腕時計(うでどけい)は100万円(まんえん)<b>からする</b>そうだ。
That watch apparently costs upwards of a million yen.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)現場(げんば)には、10メートル<b>からある</b>鉄(てつ)のパイプが並(なら)べてあった。
Steel pipes of ten metres and more were laid out on the site.
</pre>

<pre>
川(かわ)で2メートル<b>からある</b>大(おお)きな魚(さかな)が釣(つ)り上(あ)げられ、地元(じもと)で話題(わだい)になった。
A fish of over two metres was landed in the river, and the town has talked of nothing else.
</pre>

<pre>
被災地(ひさいち)には、1万人(まんにん)<b>からの</b>ボランティアが全国(ぜんこく)から駆(か)けつけたという。
Ten thousand and more volunteers are said to have come from all over the country to the disaster area.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は若(わか)いころ、50キロ<b>からある</b>米袋(こめぶくろ)を肩(かた)に担(かつ)いで運(はこ)んでいたそうだ。
When he was young, my grandfather is supposed to have carried fifty-kilo sacks of rice on his shoulder.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written, or spoken in a storytelling register: newspaper copy, reportage, a memoir,
an anecdote being told properly. It is not stiff - somebody describing a monstrous
fish or an unbelievable queue may well reach for it - but in ordinary conversation
the everyday emphatic is `も`: <code>30キロもある</code>.

Three things it insists on.

**The figure must be large.** Large for the thing, that is: two metres is large for
a fish, five centimetres is large for a pile of paper, and a million yen is large
for a watch. ❌<code>1キロからある砂糖(さとう)</code> fails not because a kilo is
small in the abstract but because nobody is impressed by it.

**There must be a number.** ❌<code>たくさんからある</code> and
❌<code>大勢(おおぜい)からの人(ひと)</code> are impossible. The pattern puts a
floor under a *figure*, so a figure has to be there.

**The speaker is reacting.** A specification sheet does not say
<code>からある</code>; it says 以上(いじょう). The moment the pattern appears,
somebody is impressed, appalled or showing off.

<code>からの</code> is the form to watch for, because it is the one that turns up in
headlines and opening paragraphs -
<code>数百人(すうひゃくにん)からの観客(かんきゃく)</code> - and because it
modifies a noun rather than standing as a predicate.

## Contrast

### ～からある vs ～も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `も` reacts to an **exact figure**: it weighs all of thirty
kilos. `からある` reacts to a **minimum**: thirty at least, possibly more.

</div>

<pre>
このスーツケース、20キロ<b>も</b>あるよ。
This suitcase weighs twenty kilos!
</pre>

<pre>
20キロ<b>からある</b>スーツケースを階段(かいだん)で運(はこ)び上(あ)げた。
He hauled a twenty-kilo-plus suitcase up the stairs.
</pre>

The first speaker has weighed it, or has just picked it up and is telling you. The
second is estimating from the outside and rounding downwards for safety. `も` is
also the one you can say to a friend; `からある` in that first sentence would sound
like a line of narration.

### ～からある vs ～以上(いじょう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>以上(いじょう)</code> is **neutral and precise**: X or
more, and X counts. `からある` is **subjective**: X at the very least, and the
speaker is impressed.

</div>

<pre>
体重(たいじゅう)が20キロ<b>以上(いじょう)</b>の犬(いぬ)は預(あず)かれません。
We cannot take dogs weighing twenty kilos or more.
</pre>

<pre>
20キロ<b>からある</b>大型犬(おおがたけん)を二頭(にとう)飼(か)っている。
He keeps two big dogs, twenty kilos each if they're an ounce.
</pre>

The first is a rule, and rules need a threshold that can be applied by anyone. The
second is a description with an attitude in it. Put `からある` into a set of
regulations and it reads as though the drafter were marvelling at the dogs.

### ～からある vs ～ほど・～くらい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほど` and `くらい` give an **approximation**: about that much,
maybe less. `からある` gives a **floor**: that much at minimum.

</div>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)は20キロ<b>くらい</b>あった。
The luggage weighed about twenty kilos.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)は20キロ<b>からあった</b>。
The luggage was twenty kilos if it was a gram.
</pre>

They point in opposite directions from the same number. `くらい` allows eighteen;
`からある` does not, and hints at twenty-five. If your English has "about", you want
`くらい`; if it has "no less than", "a good", or "and more", you want `からある`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この時計(とけい)は50万円(まんえん)<b>からある</b>。 → ✅ この時計(とけい)は50万円(まんえん)<b>からする</b>。
  Money takes `からする`. `からある` is for how much there is of something, not for
  what it costs.

- ❌ <b>たくさんからの</b>人(ひと)が集(あつ)まった。 → ✅ <b>3000人(にん)からの</b>人(ひと)が集(あつ)まった。
  The pattern needs an actual figure. With a vague quantifier there is no floor to
  put under anything.

- ❌ 砂糖(さとう)を1キロ<b>からある</b>買(か)ってきた。 → ✅ 砂糖(さとう)を1キロ<b>も</b>買(か)ってきた。
  Two faults: a kilo of sugar is not an impressive quantity, and `からある` modifies
  a noun or stands as a predicate - it cannot sit adverbially in front of a verb
  like this.

- ❌ 500人(にん)<b>からある</b>観客(かんきゃく)が詰(つ)めかけた。 → ✅ 500人(にん)<b>からの</b>観客(かんきゃく)が詰(つ)めかけた。
  People take いる, not ある, so the ある form jars when the noun counted is human.
  For crowds the idiom is `からの`.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)で、樹齢(じゅれい)百年(ひゃくねん)の木(き)が倒(たお)れ、太(ふと)さ1メートル____幹(みき)が道(みち)をふさいだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1メートル<b>からある</b>幹(みき)

A trunk a metre thick and more, described by someone who saw it. The figure is
large for the thing, and the phrase modifies a following noun - the two conditions
the pattern needs.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この絵(え)は画廊(がろう)で300万円(まんえん)（からある・からする）そうだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

300万円(まんえん)<b>からする</b>そうだ

A price, so `からする`. `からある` would be talking about how much painting there
is, which is not a thing anyone measures in yen.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Two hundred people and more were queuing outside the hall before dawn."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夜明(よあ)け前(まえ)から、200人(にん)<b>からの</b>人(ひと)が会場(かいじょう)の外(そと)に並(なら)んでいた。

"And more" after a round number is the giveaway, and because the phrase modifies
人(ひと) the form wanted is `からの` rather than `からある`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会員(かいいん)が10人(にん)からある場合(ばあい)は、部屋(へや)を無料(むりょう)でお使(つか)いいただけます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会員(かいいん)が10人(にん)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>の場合(ばあい)は、部屋(へや)を無料(むりょう)でお使(つか)いいただけます。

A condition in a set of terms needs a threshold anyone can apply, and ten is not a
figure to marvel at. 以上(いじょう) is neutral and precise, which is exactly what
a rule requires.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 荷物(にもつ)は30キロくらいありそうだ。
(b) 荷物(にもつ)は30キロからありそうだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an estimate around thirty: it might turn out to be twenty-six, and the
speaker would not be surprised.

(b) sets thirty as the lower bound and expects to be proved right on the high side.
It also carries a reaction the first sentence does not - the speaker is weighing up
whether anyone can lift the thing.

</details>

# ～わ～わで

**Meaning** · what with A happening and B happening - one thing after another, and all of it bad

This is the shape a Japanese speaker uses to tell the story of a disastrous day.
Two things are named - it rained, the trains stopped - each closed with an
exclamatory `わ`, and the で at the end hands over to the conclusion: it was a
catastrophe, we gave up, I wanted to go home. The `わ` here is **not** the
sentence-final `わ` of 「そうだわ」. It is a listing particle, used by everybody, and
it exists to pile troubles up.

## Formation

Plain forms, and - this is the part that surprises people - normally **non-past**,
even when the story is about yesterday.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain non-past + わ | 降(ふ)る → 雨(あめ)は降(ふ)るわ |
| い-adjective | plain non-past + わ | 暑(あつ)い → 部屋(へや)は暑(あつ)いわ |
| Verb (negative) | ～ない + わ | 届(とど)かないわ |
| Noun predicate | use する instead | ❌停電(ていでん)だわ → ✅停電(ていでん)はするわ |
| Closing | ～わ～わ + で + consequence | …で、さんざんだった |
| Subject marking | each clause takes は | 子(こ)どもは泣(な)くわ犬(いぬ)は吠(ほ)えるわ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the non-past forms are a narrative present, the same trick English uses
in "so I'm standing there and the phone rings". The tense of the whole story is
carried by the verb after で:
<code>…で、大変(たいへん)だった</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)は降(ふ)る<b>わ</b>電車(でんしゃ)は止(と)まる<b>わ</b>で、さんざんな一日(いちにち)だった。
What with the rain and the trains stopping, it was a wretched day.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもは泣(な)く<b>わ</b>犬(いぬ)は吠(ほ)える<b>わ</b>で、電話(でんわ)どころではなかった。
The baby was crying, the dog was barking - there was no question of taking a call.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)は落(お)とす<b>わ</b>終電(しゅうでん)は逃(のが)す<b>わ</b>で、最悪(さいあく)の夜(よる)だった。
I lost my wallet, I missed the last train - the worst night I can remember.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は暑(あつ)い<b>わ</b>蚊(か)は多(おお)い<b>わ</b>で、ろくに眠(ねむ)れなかった。
The room was hot and full of mosquitoes, and I barely slept.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)には怒(おこ)られる<b>わ</b>残業(ざんぎょう)は続(つづ)く<b>わ</b>で、いよいよ辞(や)めたくなった。
Told off by the boss, overtime with no end to it - I really did start wanting to quit.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)し当日(とうじつ)は、業者(ぎょうしゃ)は遅(おく)れる<b>わ</b>雨(あめ)は降(ふ)り出(だ)す<b>わ</b>で、予定(よてい)がすっかりくるった。
On moving day the removal men were late and it started to rain, and the whole schedule fell apart.
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)初日(しょにち)から荷物(にもつ)は届(とど)かない<b>わ</b>部屋(へや)は変(か)わる<b>わ</b>で、先(さき)が思(おも)いやられた。
From the first day of the trip the luggage went missing and our room was changed, and I began to fear for the rest of it.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりの休(やす)みだったのに、朝(あさ)から頭(あたま)は痛(いた)い<b>わ</b>宅配(たくはい)は来(く)る<b>わ</b>で、結局(けっきょく)どこにも行(い)けなかった。
It was my first day off in ages, and between the headache from the morning and the deliveries arriving, I never got out of the house.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise it; tell your own bad-day stories with `し` instead.** This is not an
archaic pattern - you will hear it from friends, and read it in blogs, essays and
manga - but it is a strongly stylised one. It has a rhythm, a fixed emotional
colour and a set of things it can and cannot contain, and a learner who deploys it
slightly off - with past-tense verbs, with good news, or in a written report -
produces something that sounds like a bad impression of a comedian. The safe
equivalent is <code>～し、～しで</code>:
<code>雨(あめ)は降(ふ)るし電車(でんしゃ)は止(と)まるしで、さんざんだった</code>
says the same thing and costs you nothing.

Three things define it.

**It is spoken, and casual.** It belongs to complaining to a friend, to
storytelling, and to writing that imitates speech. It does not appear in a report,
a formal email or a news article.

**It is for troubles.** Two or more things went wrong, they piled up, and the
speaker was on the receiving end. A run of good luck can be told this way as a
deliberate joke - 「給料(きゅうりょう)は上(あ)がるわ彼女(かのじょ)はできるわで」
- and the humour lies precisely in borrowing the complaint frame. Straight, it is
complaints.

**The `わ` is not the feminine one.** Learners meet 「そうだわ」「きれいだわ」 as a
softening sentence-final particle with a female tendency, and then assume this わ is
the same. It is not: it does not end the sentence, it does not soften anything, and
it is used by speakers of any gender. Confusing the two is the single most common
misreading of the pattern.

## Contrast

### ～わ～わで vs ～し～しで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same list of grievances. `し` is neutral and safe;
`わ` is theatrical, and turns the list into a performance.

</div>

<pre>
道(みち)は混(こ)む<b>し</b>子(こ)どもは騒(さわ)ぐ<b>しで</b>、運転(うんてん)どころではなかった。
The roads were jammed and the kids were shrieking, so driving was no fun at all.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)は混(こ)む<b>わ</b>子(こ)どもは騒(さわ)ぐ<b>わ</b>で、運転(うんてん)どころではなかった。
What with the traffic and the kids screaming, driving was out of the question.
</pre>

`し` simply gives reasons, and can be used for anything - good, bad or neutral -
in almost any register. `わ` announces that a story is being told and that the
teller has been badly used. Where `し` can stand alone at the end of a sentence
（「安(やす)いし」）, `わ` cannot: it must come in a pair.

### ～わ～わで vs ～やら～やら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `やら…やら` lists **nouns** and reports bewilderment.
`わ…わ` lists **whole events** and reports a run of misfortune.

</div>

<pre>
年末(ねんまつ)は大掃除(おおそうじ)<b>やら</b>年賀状(ねんがじょう)<b>やら</b>で落(お)ち着(つ)かない。
The end of the year is unsettled, what with the big clean and the new year cards.
</pre>

<pre>
年末(ねんまつ)は来客(らいきゃく)はある<b>わ</b>風邪(かぜ)はひく<b>わ</b>で、休(やす)んだ気(き)がしなかった。
The end of the year brought visitors and then a cold, and it never felt like a break.
</pre>

The grammatical difference is what goes in the slots: `やら` takes nouns, `わ` takes
predicates. The feeling differs too. `やら` is a person overwhelmed by a heap of
tasks; `わ` is a person to whom things keep happening.

### ～わ～わで vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たり…たり` samples actions **neutrally**, and the speaker is
often the one doing them. `わ…わ` reports events that **happened to** the speaker,
with feeling.

</div>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)は映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>たり</b>友(とも)だちに会(あ)っ<b>たり</b>していた。
Over the long weekend I watched films, met friends, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)は熱(ねつ)は出(で)る<b>わ</b>車(くるま)は故障(こしょう)する<b>わ</b>で、ひどい目(め)に遭(あ)った。
Over the long weekend I came down with a fever and the car broke down - a thoroughly miserable time.
</pre>

Note the difference in agency. In the first the speaker chose everything; in the
second nothing was chosen. That is also why `たり` takes the past form
（見(み)たり）and `わ` does not（出(で)るわ）.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)は降(ふ)った<b>わ</b>風(かぜ)は強(つよ)かった<b>わ</b>で、大変(たいへん)だった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)は降(ふ)る<b>わ</b>風(かぜ)は強(つよ)い<b>わ</b>で、大変(たいへん)だった。
  The listed clauses stay in the plain non-past however long ago the day was. The
  tense lives in the final verb.

- ❌ 停電(ていでん)だ<b>わ</b>断水(だんすい)だ<b>わ</b>で、何(なに)もできなかった。 → ✅ 停電(ていでん)はする<b>わ</b>断水(だんすい)はする<b>わ</b>で、何(なに)もできなかった。
  A noun plus `だ` does not take this `わ`. Turn the noun into an event with する
  and the pattern works.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)い<b>わ</b>で、疲(つか)れた。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)い<b>わ</b>資料(しりょう)は足(た)りない<b>わ</b>で、疲(つか)れた。
  One `わ` is not the pattern. Alone it will be read as the sentence-final particle
  and the sentence changes character completely.

- ❌ 本件(ほんけん)については、担当者(たんとうしゃ)は不在(ふざい)にする<b>わ</b>資料(しりょう)は届(とど)かない<b>わ</b>で、対応(たいおう)が遅(おく)れました。 → ✅ 本件(ほんけん)については、担当者(たんとうしゃ)の不在(ふざい)や資料(しりょう)の遅(おく)れが重(かさ)なり、対応(たいおう)が遅(おく)れました。
  An apology to a client is the one place this pattern must never go. It sounds as
  though the writer were performing their own misfortune instead of apologising.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to hear this, and who is speaking to whom?
「朝(あさ)から停電(ていでん)はするわ水(みず)は出(で)ないわで、もう散々(さんざん)だったよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In conversation, to a friend or a family member - never to a customer or a
superior.

Everything about it is casual: the narrative present in the listed clauses, the
final よ, and the frank self-pity of 散々(さんざん)だった. The pattern is a
storytelling device, and stories of this kind are told to equals.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in neutral Japanese that would be safe in any spoken situation:
「会場(かいじょう)は遠(とお)いわ受付(うけつけ)は混(こ)むわで、開演(かいえん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会場(かいじょう)は遠(とお)い<b>し</b>受付(うけつけ)も混(こ)んでいた<b>ので</b>、開演(かいえん)に間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。

`し` carries the same accumulation of reasons without the performance, and once the
theatrical frame is gone the sentence can take polite endings comfortably.

</details>

**3.** True or false: the `わ` in this pattern is the same feminine sentence-final
particle as in 「きれいだわ」. Explain.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

False.

The sentence-final `わ` softens an assertion and ends the sentence; it has a female
tendency in standard Japanese. The `わ` of this pattern is a listing particle in the
middle of a sentence, has no softening function, and is used by speakers of any
gender. They are homophones, nothing more.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は道(みち)に迷(まよ)ったわ傘(かさ)をなくしたわで、へとへとになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は道(みち)に迷(まよ)う<b>わ</b>傘(かさ)をなくす<b>わ</b>で、へとへとになった。

Past forms in the listed clauses. Japanese switches to the plain non-past inside
this pattern and lets the closing verb - here になった - carry the tense.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 昨日(きのう)は残業(ざんぎょう)やら接待(せったい)やらで遅(おそ)くなった。
(b) 昨日(きのう)は残業(ざんぎょう)はある<b>わ</b>接待(せったい)は入(はい)る<b>わ</b>で遅(おそ)くなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) names two things as nouns and sounds tired but even-tempered. It would pass in a
fairly polite conversation.

(b) turns them into a sequence of blows and invites sympathy. It is more vivid,
more casual, and more clearly a complaint - the speaker is not just explaining why
they were late but performing how unfair the day was.

</details>
