# N1 - The moment something happens


---

# ～なり

**Meaning** · the instant one thing happened, the next thing did too - and the second action is abrupt, unplanned, or out of character

<code>なり</code> hangs off a dictionary-form verb and reports that a second event
followed with no gap whatever. What the writer is actually interested in is the
**suddenness**: the person acted sooner than anyone watching would have expected.
This is not the <code>なり</code> of <code>見(み)るなり聞(き)くなり</code>
("either look or ask"); the two are unrelated words that happen to share a shape.

## Formation

The verb in front is always in the dictionary form, however far in the past the
event is.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + なり | 座(すわ)る → 座(すわ)るなり |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + なり | 開(あ)ける → 開(あ)けるなり |
| Verb (する) | dictionary form + なり | 到着(とうちゃく)する → 到着(とうちゃく)するなり |
| Verb (来(く)る) | dictionary form + なり | 来(く)る → 来(く)るなり |
| Second clause | a completed event, normally past tense | 顔(かお)を見(み)るなり泣(な)き出(だ)した |
| Not possible | past form + なり | ❌見(み)たなり |
| Not possible | negative + なり | ❌見(み)ないなり |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>～たなり</code> does exist, but it is a different pattern meaning
"left in that state": <code>靴(くつ)を履(は)いたなり上(あ)がってきた</code>,
"he came up still wearing his shoes". Do not read it as "as soon as".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は家(いえ)に帰(かえ)る<b>なり</b>、かばんを置(お)いて外(そと)へ出(で)て行(い)った。
My little brother got home and was straight back out the door, bag dumped on the floor.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)は電話(でんわ)を切(き)る<b>なり</b>、出(で)かける支度(したく)を始(はじ)めた。
The moment she put the phone down, my mother started getting ready to go out.
</pre>

<pre>
犬(いぬ)は飼(か)い主(ぬし)の声(こえ)を聞(き)く<b>なり</b>、尻尾(しっぽ)を振(ふ)って走(はし)り寄(よ)った。
The dog heard its owner's voice and came running over, tail going.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は席(せき)に着(つ)く<b>なり</b>、険(けわ)しい顔(かお)で書類(しょるい)をめくり始(はじ)めた。
The section chief sat down and immediately began leafing through the papers, grim-faced.
</pre>

<pre>
少年(しょうねん)は手紙(てがみ)を開(ひら)く<b>なり</b>、声(こえ)を上(あ)げて笑(わら)い出(だ)した。
The boy opened the letter and burst out laughing.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は病室(びょうしつ)のドアが開(あ)くのを見(み)る<b>なり</b>、ベッドの上(うえ)で体(からだ)を起(お)こそうとした。
The moment my grandfather saw the door of the hospital room open, he tried to sit up in bed.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は封筒(ふうとう)の中身(なかみ)を確(たし)かめる<b>なり</b>、顔色(かおいろ)を変(か)えて部屋(へや)を飛(と)び出(だ)して行(い)った。
He checked what was inside the envelope, then went white and bolted from the room.
</pre>

<pre>
新入(しんにゅう)社員(しゃいん)は名刺(めいし)を受(う)け取(と)る<b>なり</b>、慌(あわ)てて両手(りょうて)で持(も)ち直(なお)し、深(ふか)く頭(あたま)を下(さ)げた。
The new employee took the business card, hurriedly shifted it into both hands, and bowed deeply.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>なり</code> belongs to **narration**. It is at home in novels, memoirs,
newspaper features and any careful written account of what someone did; you will
also hear it in a storytelling voice, when a speaker is describing a scene rather
than chatting. It is not stiff or archaic, but it is not conversational filler
either - a friend telling you about their morning would say
<code>着(つ)いてすぐ</code> or <code>着(つ)いたらすぐ</code>.

Three restrictions come with it, and they are the reason most learner sentences go
wrong.

**The subject is normally someone else.** <code>なり</code> presents the action as
observed from outside and slightly surprising, and you cannot be surprised by your
own behaviour. ❌<code>私(わたし)は駅(えき)に着(つ)くなり電話(でんわ)をかけた</code>
is odd for that reason. There is a narrow exception in autobiographical writing,
where the narrator treats their past self as a character, but treat it as
recognition, not production.

**The second clause reports a real, finished event.** No requests, no invitations,
no volitional, no plain negatives: ❌<code>来(く)るなり座(すわ)ってください</code>.
It also cannot describe a habit. ❌<code>授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わるなり毎日(まいにち)図書館(としょかん)へ行(い)く</code>
needs <code>終(お)わるとすぐ</code> instead.

**Both clauses share one subject**, and the two actions happen in one continuous
movement of the same body. If the second event happens to somebody else, this is
the wrong pattern.

## Contrast

### ～なり vs ～たとたん（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たとたん</code> is neutral about who acts: the second
event may happen to anyone or to nobody in particular. <code>なり</code> requires
**the same person** to do both things, one straight after the other.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たとたん</b>、冷(つめ)たい風(かぜ)が吹(ふ)き込(こ)んできた。
The instant I opened the window, cold air blew in.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は窓(まど)を開(あ)ける<b>なり</b>、外(そと)に向(む)かって手(て)を振(ふ)った。
She threw the window open and waved out of it.
</pre>

In the first, the wind is not the opener's doing - <code>なり</code> would be
impossible there. In the second, one person performs both actions. This also
explains why <code>たとたん</code> is the natural choice for accidents and
discoveries, and <code>なり</code> for decisive human behaviour.

### ～なり vs ～や否(いな)や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the meanings almost coincide; the difference is **register**.
<code>や否(いな)や</code> is markedly literary and turns up in newspaper prose,
where <code>なり</code> is ordinary narrative Japanese.

</div>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)は会場(かいじょう)に入(はい)る<b>なり</b>、集(あつ)まった記者(きしゃ)に頭(あたま)を下(さ)げた。
The president walked into the hall and bowed to the assembled reporters.
</pre>

<pre>
会見(かいけん)が終(お)わる<b>や否(いな)や</b>、株価(かぶか)は大(おお)きく動(うご)き始(はじ)めた。
No sooner had the press conference ended than the share price began to move sharply.
</pre>

The second sentence also shows the other split: <code>や否(いな)や</code> tolerates
a change of subject - the price is not what the conference did. <code>なり</code>
does not. If you want one rule to carry away, it is that <code>なり</code> follows a
person and <code>や否(いな)や</code> follows a clock.

### ～なり vs なり～なり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one <code>なり</code> after a dictionary-form verb means "as soon
as". Two of them, one after each of two items, means "either … or, whichever".

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは荷物(にもつ)を受(う)け取(と)る<b>なり</b>、中身(なかみ)を調(しら)べ始(はじ)めた。
Tanaka took delivery of the parcel and immediately started checking the contents.
</pre>

<pre>
分(わ)からなければ、辞書(じしょ)を引(ひ)く<b>なり</b>先生(せんせい)に聞(き)く<b>なり</b>しなさい。
If you don't know, look it up or ask the teacher - one or the other.
</pre>

The giveaway is the second <code>なり</code> and the <code>する</code> that usually
closes the listing pattern. Nouns are also possible there
(<code>コーヒーなりお茶(ちゃ)なり</code>), which the "as soon as" pattern never
allows.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 家(いえ)に帰(かえ)った<b>なり</b>、寝(ね)てしまった。 → ✅ 家(いえ)に帰(かえ)る<b>なり</b>、寝(ね)てしまった。
  The verb in front stays in the dictionary form. Tense is carried entirely by the
  second clause.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は部屋(へや)に入(はい)る<b>なり</b>、窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)に入(はい)るとすぐ、窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。
  <code>なり</code> reports someone else's abrupt behaviour. Used of yourself it
  sounds as though you were watching a stranger.

- ❌ ベルが鳴(な)る<b>なり</b>、外(そと)へ出(で)ましょう。 → ✅ ベルが鳴(な)ったら、すぐ外(そと)へ出(で)ましょう。
  No invitations, requests or commands in the second half. The pattern reports what
  did happen, not what should.

- ❌ 息子(むすこ)は帰(かえ)る<b>なり</b>、母親(ははおや)が叱(しか)った。 → ✅ 息子(むすこ)が帰(かえ)ってくると、すぐに母親(ははおや)が叱(しか)った。
  Two different subjects. <code>なり</code> needs one actor doing both things.

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)はコーヒーを一口(ひとくち)飲(の)む____、カップを置(お)いて立(た)ち上(あ)がった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一口(ひとくち)飲(の)む<b>なり</b>

One person, two actions in a single unbroken movement, and the second one is
abrupt - textbook <code>なり</code>. The verb stays in the dictionary form even
though the sentence is in the past.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 地震(じしん)で棚(たな)が揺(ゆ)れ始(はじ)め（るなり・たとたん）、皿(さら)が次々(つぎつぎ)に落(お)ちた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

揺(ゆ)れ始(はじ)め<b>たとたん</b>、皿(さら)が次々(つぎつぎ)に落(お)ちた。

The shelf shakes and the plates fall: two different things, and nobody is acting on
purpose. <code>なり</code> demands one human subject performing both halves, so it
cannot be used here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She saw my face and burst into tears."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)は私(わたし)の顔(かお)を見(み)る<b>なり</b>、泣(な)き出(だ)した。

Same subject, no gap, and the reaction is startling to the observer. Note that the
English past "saw" still comes out as 見(み)る - the tense sits in
泣(な)き出(だ)した.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 課長(かちょう)は報告(ほうこく)を聞(き)くなり、毎回(まいかい)同(おな)じ質問(しつもん)をする。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 課長(かちょう)は報告(ほうこく)を聞(き)くと、毎回(まいかい)同(おな)じ質問(しつもん)をする。

毎回(まいかい) makes this a habit, and <code>なり</code> can only report a single
event that actually happened. The conditional と is the natural pattern for a
repeated sequence.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)む<b>なり</b>、席(せき)を立(た)った。
(b) 彼(かれ)は手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)ん<b>でから</b>、席(せき)を立(た)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the two actions on top of each other and makes the departure look like a
reaction: something in the letter drove him out of his chair. The reader is meant
to feel the abruptness.

(b) simply orders the events - first he read it, then he left. There may have been
several minutes in between, and nothing is implied about why he stood up.

</details>

# ～や否や

**Meaning** · no sooner had one thing happened than the next did - a written, newspaper-and-novel way of saying "the moment that"

Read <code>や否(いな)や</code> as やいなや. It is a survival from classical
Japanese: <code>や</code> is the old exclamatory particle and
<code>否(いな)や</code> is literally "or not", so the phrase once meant "hardly
had it, or had it not". Nothing of that literal sense is left. What remains is a
compressed way of welding two events together, and it belongs to the page, not to
the mouth.

## Formation

The verb in front is in the dictionary form, whatever the tense of the sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + や否(いな)や | 止(と)まる → 止(と)まるや否(いな)や |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + や否(いな)や | 届(とど)ける → 届(とど)けるや否(いな)や |
| Verb (する) | dictionary form + や否(いな)や | 発表(はっぴょう)する → 発表(はっぴょう)するや否(いな)や |
| Verb (passive) | dictionary form + や否(いな)や | 発売(はつばい)される → 発売(はつばい)されるや否(いな)や |
| Shortened variant | dictionary form + や | 結(むす)ばれる → 結(むす)ばれるや |
| Second clause | past tense, a completed event | 降(お)り始(はじ)めた |
| Not possible | past form in front | ❌止(と)まったや否(いな)や |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the bare <code>～や</code> is the same pattern with the tail cut off. It
is even more literary, and in modern text it appears mostly in historical writing
and in headlines. The <code>否(いな)や</code> half is also written in kana as
<code>やいなや</code>; both spellings are standard.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
列車(れっしゃ)が止(と)まる<b>や否(いな)や</b>、乗客(じょうきゃく)は一斉(いっせい)に降(お)り始(はじ)めた。
The train had barely stopped before the passengers all began to get off.
</pre>

<pre>
幕(まく)が上(あ)がる<b>や否(いな)や</b>、客席(きゃくせき)から大(おお)きな拍手(はくしゅ)がわき起(お)こった。
The curtain went up, and at once applause broke out from the audience.
</pre>

<pre>
その知(し)らせが村(むら)に届(とど)く<b>や否(いな)や</b>、人々(ひとびと)は広場(ひろば)に集(あつ)まり始(はじ)めた。
The news had no sooner reached the village than people began gathering in the square.
</pre>

<pre>
新製品(しんせいひん)は発売(はつばい)される<b>や否(いな)や</b>、全国(ぜんこく)の店頭(てんとう)で品切(しなぎ)れとなった。
The new product sold out in shops nationwide the moment it went on sale.
</pre>

<pre>
警報(けいほう)が鳴(な)り響(ひび)く<b>や否(いな)や</b>、作業員(さぎょういん)たちは一目散(いちもくさん)に工場(こうじょう)の外(そと)へ走(はし)った。
The alarm sounded and the workers ran flat out for the factory gates.
</pre>

<pre>
首相(しゅしょう)が辞任(じにん)の意向(いこう)を示(しめ)す<b>や否(いな)や</b>、各党(かくとう)は後継(こうけい)をめぐって動(うご)き出(だ)した。
The prime minister had scarcely signalled his intention to resign before the parties were manoeuvring over the succession.
</pre>

<pre>
条約(じょうやく)が結(むす)ばれる<b>や</b>、両国(りょうこく)の貿易(ぼうえき)は急速(きゅうそく)に拡大(かくだい)していった。
The treaty was signed, and trade between the two countries expanded rapidly from that point.
</pre>

<pre>
判決(はんけつ)が言(い)い渡(わた)される<b>や否(いな)や</b>、傍聴席(ぼうちょうせき)のあちこちからため息(いき)がもれた。
The verdict was no sooner delivered than sighs went up here and there in the public gallery.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is a form you read, not one you use.** Its home is newspaper copy, magazine
features, company histories, historical writing and literary fiction - text whose
job is to compress a sequence of events. In conversation it is genuinely strange:
a Japanese speaker who said <code>帰(かえ)るや否(いな)や</code> to a friend
would sound as though they were reading a headline aloud, and the listener would
probably laugh. Learn to recognise it, and say
<code>帰(かえ)ったとたん</code> or <code>帰(かえ)ってすぐ</code> yourself.

The one setting where a speaker might reach for it is a prepared, formal spoken
text - a speech, a memorial address, a documentary narration. Even there it is a
deliberately literary flourish rather than neutral choice.

Two grammatical consequences of that written character are worth noting. First,
the second clause reports a **fact that occurred**, so no requests, invitations,
volitional forms or plain negatives can follow: ❌<code>着(つ)くや否(いな)や電話(でんわ)してください</code>.
Second, unlike <code>なり</code>, the two clauses **may have different subjects** -
the train stops, the passengers move - which is exactly why it suits reportage.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the sentence would fit under a photograph in a newspaper,
<code>や否(いな)や</code> is possible. If it would fit in a text message, it is
not.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～や否(いな)や vs ～たとたん（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たとたん</code> is the **modern equivalent**, and the one
to actually use. It says the same thing in ordinary Japanese and works in speech,
email and writing alike.

</div>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれ<b>たとたん</b>、彼(かれ)は顔(かお)を赤(あか)くした。
The instant his name was called, he went red.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)が読(よ)み上(あ)げられる<b>や否(いな)や</b>、会場(かいじょう)は静(しず)まり返(かえ)った。
The name was no sooner read out than the hall fell completely silent.
</pre>

Both sentences are correct Japanese; they simply come from different worlds. Note
the form change as well: <code>たとたん</code> takes the **past** form in front,
<code>や否(いな)や</code> the dictionary form. Rewriting one as the other means
changing the verb, not just swapping the tail.

### ～や否(いな)や vs ～が早(はや)いか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these two are near-identical, and no reliable meaning
difference separates them. <code>が早(はや)いか</code> leans towards fiction and a
single vivid human action; <code>や否(いな)や</code> leans towards reportage and
tolerates impersonal events.

</div>

<pre>
少年(しょうねん)はベルが鳴(な)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、かばんをつかんで教室(きょうしつ)を出(で)て行(い)った。
The bell had barely rung before the boy grabbed his bag and was out of the classroom.
</pre>

<pre>
規制(きせい)が緩和(かんわ)される<b>や否(いな)や</b>、各社(かくしゃ)が市場(しじょう)に参入(さんにゅう)した。
No sooner were the rules relaxed than firms started entering the market.
</pre>

Swap them and neither sentence becomes wrong, only slightly less idiomatic. Do not
invent a distinction here; treat them as stylistic neighbours and recognise both.

### ～や否(いな)や vs ～なり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>なり</code> follows **one person** through two actions.
<code>や否(いな)や</code> links **two events**, whoever or whatever performs them.

</div>

<pre>
父(ちち)は新聞(しんぶん)を置(お)く<b>なり</b>、だまって外(そと)へ出(で)て行(い)った。
My father put the paper down and walked out without a word.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)が終(お)わる<b>や否(いな)や</b>、スタンドから人(ひと)があふれ出(だ)した。
The match had barely finished before people came pouring out of the stands.
</pre>

The second sentence is impossible with <code>なり</code>: the match does not empty
the stands, and the subject changes across the comma. <code>なり</code> is also the
milder of the two on the register scale - narrative but not journalistic.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わった<b>や否(いな)や</b> → ✅ 授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わる<b>や否(いな)や</b>
  The dictionary form is fixed. Learners reach for the past because the English is
  past, but the tense lives in the second clause.

- ❌ 駅(えき)に着(つ)く<b>や否(いな)や</b>連絡(れんらく)してください。 → ✅ 駅(えき)に着(つ)いたら、すぐ連絡(れんらく)してください。
  The pattern reports events that happened. A request cannot follow it.

- ❌ ただいま。家(いえ)に着(つ)く<b>や否(いな)や</b>、おなかがすいちゃった。 → ✅ 家(いえ)に着(つ)いたとたん、おなかがすいちゃった。
  Not a grammar error, a register error - and the more damaging kind. Newspaper
  vocabulary inside a casual sentence sounds like a joke.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>や否(いな)や</b>、毎回(まいかい)傘(かさ)を買(か)ってしまう。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ると、毎回(まいかい)傘(かさ)を買(か)ってしまう。
  A single past event only. Habits take と or たびに.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence: 「工場(こうじょう)の門(もん)が開(ひら)く<b>や否(いな)や</b>、人々(ひとびと)がなだれ込(こ)んだ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In writing - a news report, a magazine feature, a work of fiction or a company
history. The construction compresses two events into one line, which is what
reportage and narration want. Nobody would produce this sentence in conversation;
there they would say <code>門(もん)が開(ひら)いたとたん</code>.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)る<b>や否(いな)や</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は受話器(じゅわき)を取(と)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)っ<b>たとたん</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は受話器(じゅわき)を取(と)った。（or 鳴(な)るとすぐに）

The two forms mean the same thing; only the register changes. Notice that the verb
in front has to move from the dictionary form to the past form when you switch.

</details>

**3.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まったや否(いな)や、議論(ぎろん)が白熱(はくねつ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる<b>や否(いな)や</b>、議論(ぎろん)が白熱(はくねつ)した。

<code>や否(いな)や</code> takes the dictionary form, never the past. The rest of
the sentence is fine: two different subjects across the comma is normal here, and
白熱(はくねつ)する is written-register vocabulary that matches the connective.

</details>

**4.** Choose the sentence that could actually appear in speech, and say why the
other could not:
(a) 先生(せんせい)が教室(きょうしつ)に入(はい)る<b>や否(いな)や</b>、みんな静(しず)かになった。
(b) 先生(せんせい)が教室(きょうしつ)に入(はい)っ<b>たとたん</b>、みんな静(しず)かになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b). It is neutral Japanese and fits a conversation between students.

(a) is grammatically perfect but written-language only. Spoken aloud between
friends it lands as mock-formal, the way "no sooner had the teacher entered" would
in an English text message.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 判定(はんてい)が出(で)る<b>や否(いな)や</b>、観客(かんきゃく)は席(せき)を立(た)った。
(b) 判定(はんてい)が出(で)てから、観客(かんきゃく)は席(せき)を立(た)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presses the two events together and colours the standing-up as an immediate,
almost reflexive reaction to the decision. It also sounds like reportage - you can
hear the sports page.

(b) only orders them: the decision came, and afterwards people left. A minute may
have passed, and no reaction is implied. It is also plain, everyday Japanese.

</details>

# ～が早いか

**Meaning** · no sooner had one thing happened than someone was already doing the next - a literary connective that makes the second action look almost indecently quick

Literally "was it early, or …?" - the writer pretends to ask which of the two
events came first, and the implied answer is that you could not tell. The effect is
vivid and slightly comic: the person did not wait, could not wait, was on it before
the first event had finished. Note the kanji: 早(はや)い, "early", not
速(はや)い, "fast".

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + が早(はや)いか | 鳴(な)る → 鳴(な)るが早(はや)いか |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + が早(はや)いか | 置(お)ける → 置(お)けるが早(はや)いか |
| Verb (する) | dictionary form + が早(はや)いか | 到着(とうちゃく)する → 到着(とうちゃく)するが早(はや)いか |
| Verb (passive) | dictionary form + が早(はや)いか | 配(くば)られる → 配(くば)られるが早(はや)いか |
| Occasional variant | past form + が早(はや)いか | 見(み)た → 見(み)たが早(はや)いか |
| Second clause | past tense, a single completed action | 駆(か)け出(だ)した |
| Not possible | negative in front | ❌鳴(な)らないが早(はや)いか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the past-form variant <code>見(み)たが早(はや)いか</code> turns up in
older fiction and is not wrong, but the dictionary form is overwhelmingly the
standard shape today. Write the dictionary form; recognise the other.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちはチャイムが鳴(な)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、校庭(こうてい)へ駆(か)け出(だ)して行(い)った。
The bell had barely rung before the children were tearing out into the playground.
</pre>

<pre>
猫(ねこ)は戸(と)が開(あ)く<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、外(そと)へ飛(と)び出(だ)して行(い)った。
The door was hardly open when the cat shot outside.
</pre>

<pre>
老人(ろうじん)は縁側(えんがわ)に腰(こし)を下(お)ろす<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、いびきをかき始(はじ)めた。
The old man sat down on the veranda and was snoring almost before he had settled.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)はケーキが運(はこ)ばれてくる<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、フォークを突(つ)き立(た)てた。
The cake had scarcely reached the table before my sister had a fork in it.
</pre>

<pre>
答案(とうあん)が配(くば)られる<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、生徒(せいと)たちは点数(てんすう)のところに目(め)をやった。
The papers were no sooner handed back than the pupils' eyes went straight to the marks.
</pre>

<pre>
男(おとこ)は封筒(ふうとう)の中身(なかみ)を見(み)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、それを上着(うわぎ)の内側(うちがわ)に押(お)し込(こ)んだ。
The man glanced at what was in the envelope and had it inside his jacket in the same movement.
</pre>

<pre>
馬(うま)はゲートが開(ひら)く<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、先頭(せんとう)に立(た)って直線(ちょくせん)を駆(か)け抜(ぬ)けて行(い)った。
The gates had hardly opened before the horse was in front and away down the straight.
</pre>

<pre>
会長(かいちょう)は議題(ぎだい)が読(よ)み上(あ)げられる<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、反対(はんたい)の意見(いけん)を述(の)べ始(はじ)めた。
The chairman began objecting almost before the item on the agenda had finished being read out.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This belongs to written narrative.** You will meet it in novels, short stories,
essays, sports writing and the kind of magazine feature that wants to make a scene
move; you will effectively never hear it in conversation. A learner who deploys
<code>が早(はや)いか</code> at lunch will be understood and will sound like a
character in a book. The everyday way to say the same thing is
<code>～たとたん</code> or simply <code>～てすぐ</code>, and that is what you
should produce.

What the form adds over its neutral equivalents is a **judgement about the actor**.
There is always a faint editorial colour - eagerness, greed, impatience, animal
reflex - and it works best when a living creature does something you can picture.
That is why an impersonal pairing such as
<code>気温(きおん)が上(あ)がるが早(はや)いか</code> reads oddly: nothing is
being impatient.

Three restrictions follow from its narrative job. The sentence describes **one
past occurrence**, not a habit. The second clause is a **statement of fact**, so no
requests, invitations or volitional forms. And the whole thing sits in the past -
you cannot use it to predict.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you could film the sentence, <code>が早(はや)いか</code>
may fit. If it is a schedule, a rule or a plan, it does not.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～が早(はや)いか vs ～たとたん（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たとたん</code> is the **modern equivalent** and the form
to use. It is neutral in register, works in speech and writing, and carries no
comment on the actor.

</div>

<pre>
立(た)ち上(あ)がっ<b>たとたん</b>、めまいがした。
The moment I stood up, I felt dizzy.
</pre>

<pre>
少年(しょうねん)は立(た)ち上(あ)がる<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、部屋(へや)を出(で)て行(い)った。
The boy was up and out of the room in one movement.
</pre>

The first sentence cannot take <code>が早(はや)いか</code> at all: dizziness is
not something you rush to do, and the pattern needs a deliberate second action.
Also compare the forms - <code>たとたん</code> follows the **past**,
<code>が早(はや)いか</code> the **dictionary form**.

### ～が早(はや)いか vs ～か～ないかのうちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>か～ないかのうちに</code> measures the **overlap**: the first
event was not quite finished. <code>が早(はや)いか</code> comments on the
**actor's speed**.

</div>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わる<b>か終(お)わらないかのうちに</b>、教室(きょうしつ)は空(から)になった。
The class was barely over - if it was over - and the room had emptied.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わる<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、アルバイトへ向(む)かった。
The lesson had hardly ended before he was off to his part-time job.
</pre>

<code>か～ないかのうちに</code> is the more neutral of the two and can describe
events with no human agent at all. <code>が早(はや)いか</code> wants somebody
doing something eagerly, and it is the more literary form of the pair.

### ～が早(はや)いか vs ～や否(いな)や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are **near-identical**. Do not look for a meaning
difference; there is only a difference of habitat.
<code>が早(はや)いか</code> lives in fiction, <code>や否(いな)や</code> in
reportage.

</div>

<pre>
客(きゃく)は品物(しなもの)を受(う)け取(と)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、店(みせ)を出(で)て行(い)った。
The customer took the goods and was out of the shop before you could blink.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げが発表(はっぴょう)される<b>や否(いな)や</b>、問(と)い合(あ)わせが殺到(さっとう)した。
The price rise was no sooner announced than enquiries flooded in.
</pre>

Either connective would pass in either sentence. The mild tendency is that
<code>や否(いな)や</code> is comfortable with institutions and statistics, while
<code>が早(はや)いか</code> prefers a person or an animal in view. Treat that as a
tendency, not a rule, and above all do not tell yourself one means "faster".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ベルが鳴(な)った<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、走(はし)り出(だ)した。 → ✅ ベルが鳴(な)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、走(はし)り出(だ)した。
  The dictionary form is the modern shape, even though the sentence is past.

- ❌ 着(つ)く<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、電話(でんわ)しよう。 → ✅ 着(つ)いたらすぐ電話(でんわ)しよう。
  No volitional, no request, no future. The pattern narrates something that already
  happened.

- ❌ 父(ちち)は帰(かえ)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、いつもテレビをつける。 → ✅ 父(ちち)は帰(かえ)ると、いつもテレビをつける。
  いつも makes it a habit. This connective reports one occasion.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、気温(きおん)が下(さ)がった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)したとたん、気温(きおん)が下(さ)がった。
  Weather is not in a hurry. Without an agent whose speed can be remarked on, the
  pattern misfires.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this line, and what does the connective add?
「少女(しょうじょ)は手紙(てがみ)を受(う)け取(と)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、封(ふう)を切(き)った。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a novel or short story - this is narrative prose, not speech. The connective
adds impatience: it tells the reader the girl could not wait even the second it
would have taken to walk to a chair. A neutral <code>受(う)け取(と)るとすぐ</code>
would report the same events without that colour.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary spoken Japanese: 弟(おとうと)は皿(さら)が出(だ)される<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、食(た)べ始(はじ)めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弟(おとうと)は皿(さら)が出(で)てくるとすぐに食(た)べ始(はじ)めた。（or 出(で)てきたとたん）

Two things change: the literary connective becomes とすぐに, and the passive
出(だ)される softens to the plainer 出(で)てくる, since heavy passives belong
to the same written register as the connective itself.

</details>

**3.** Choose and justify: 信号(しんごう)が青(あお)に変(か)わる（が早(はや)いか・か変(か)わらないかのうちに）、後(うし)ろの車(くるま)がクラクションを鳴(な)らした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Either is possible, and they colour the sentence differently.
<b>が早(はや)いか</b> makes the driver behind sound impatient, which is probably
what you want here. <b>か変(か)わらないかのうちに</b> is the neutral report: the
horn came before the light had properly changed.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)、ドアが開(ひら)くが早(はや)いか、席(せき)を取(と)ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)は、ドアが開(ひら)いたらすぐに席(せき)を取(と)ってください。

Two independent problems: the sentence is about next week, and the pattern only
narrates the past; and it ends in a request, which this connective never permits.
Neither can be repaired by adjusting the form, so the whole connective has to go.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)はベルが鳴(な)る<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、部屋(へや)を出(で)た。
(b) 彼(かれ)はベルが鳴(な)る<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、部屋(へや)を出(で)た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a novelist's sentence. It says the two things overlapped and quietly judges
him for it - he had clearly been waiting, poised, for the bell.

(b) is neutral and almost technical: the two events coincided in time. It could
appear in a report or a witness statement, and it implies nothing about his state
of mind.

</details>

# ～そばから

**Meaning** · you do it, and it is undone again immediately - over and over, with nothing to show for it

The そば is 側(そば), "the side of", so the literal picture is "from right beside
the doing". Two things make this pattern what it is: the sequence **repeats**, and
the repetition is normally **futile**. You learn the words and they leak away; you
tidy up and the room is a mess again. It is the grammar of a losing battle.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + そばから | 拭(ふ)く → 拭(ふ)くそばから |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + そばから | 覚(おぼ)える → 覚(おぼ)えるそばから |
| Verb (する) | dictionary form + そばから | 掃除(そうじ)する → 掃除(そうじ)するそばから |
| Verb (past) | past form + そばから | 注意(ちゅうい)した → 注意(ちゅうい)したそばから |
| Second clause | present or past; the event recurs | また散(ち)らかる／散(ち)らかった |
| Not possible | noun + の + そばから | ❌雨(あめ)のそばから |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>そばから</code> also occurs in its plain literal sense, "from beside
(a person or thing)": <code>母(はは)のそばから離(はな)れようとしない</code>, "he
won't leave his mother's side". That is not this pattern. The grammar point always
has a **verb clause** in front of it, never a noun plus の.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
単語(たんご)を覚(おぼ)える<b>そばから</b>忘(わす)れてしまう。
I forget vocabulary as fast as I learn it.
</pre>

<pre>
片付(かたづ)ける<b>そばから</b>、子(こ)どもがおもちゃを散(ち)らかす。
No sooner do I tidy up than the children have the toys out again.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)の雪(ゆき)をどける<b>そばから</b>、新(あたら)しい雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もっていった。
The snow piled up again as fast as we cleared the path.
</pre>

<pre>
注意(ちゅうい)した<b>そばから</b>、同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)を繰(く)り返(かえ)している。
He makes the same mistake again the moment after he has been told off for it.
</pre>

<pre>
焼(や)き上(あ)がる<b>そばから</b>、棚(たな)のパンは売(う)れていった。
The bread sold as fast as it came out of the oven.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は入(はい)る<b>そばから</b>家賃(やちん)と光熱費(こうねつひ)に消(き)えていく。
My salary vanishes into rent and bills the moment it arrives.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を拭(ふ)く<b>そばから</b>白(しろ)く曇(くも)るので、運転(うんてん)しづらくてしかたがなかった。
The windscreen misted over again as fast as I wiped it, and driving was miserable.
</pre>

<pre>
不具合(ふぐあい)を直(なお)す<b>そばから</b>別(べつ)の問題(もんだい)が見(み)つかり、作業(さぎょう)は一向(いっこう)に終(お)わらなかった。
Every fault we repaired turned up another one, and the work never got anywhere.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one rather than reach for it.** The form is not archaic, but its
range is narrow: it lives in essays, columns, novels and written complaints, and in
speech it survives mainly inside a handful of stock pairings -
<code>覚(おぼ)えるそばから忘(わす)れる</code> above all, then
<code>片付(かたづ)けるそばから</code> and <code>入(はい)るそばから</code>. Outside
those, a spoken <code>そばから</code> sounds bookish. What a Japanese speaker
actually says is <code>覚(おぼ)えても覚(おぼ)えてもすぐ忘(わす)れる</code> or
<code>片付(かたづ)けてもきりがない</code>, and those are the forms to produce.

The characteristic feeling is **exasperation**. The speaker is not neutrally
reporting a cycle; they are fed up with it, and the sentence usually carries a
faint sigh. That is why it attracts しまう, ていく and くる - forms that show
something slipping away.

There is a genuine non-complaining use, seen in the bread example above: when goods
sell as fast as they appear, the same "undone immediately" shape reads as brisk
good news. It is a minority of cases, and it works because being sold out is a kind
of disappearing too.

One structural point: the pattern is about **repetition**, so a one-off event does
not fit. ❌<code>昨日(きのう)傘(かさ)を買(か)うそばからなくした</code> needs
<code>買(か)ったとたんに</code> instead.

## Contrast

### ～そばから vs ～ても～てもきりがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～ても～ても</code> plus すぐ or きりがない is the **modern
spoken equivalent**. Same futile cycle, no bookish edge, and it works in any
conversation.

</div>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)は書(か)いても書(か)いてもすぐ忘(わす)れちゃう。
However much I write kanji out, I forget it straight away.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)は書(か)く<b>そばから</b>忘(わす)れていく。
The kanji drain away as fast as I write them out.
</pre>

The content is identical. The second is tighter and more literary, and it is what
you would write in an essay about studying; the first is what you would say to a
classmate. Note also that <code>～ても～ても</code> is free to describe a single long
struggle, while <code>そばから</code> insists on discrete repeated events.

### ～そばから vs ～たびに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たびに</code> means "every time X, Y", and Y can be
anything at all. <code>そばから</code> adds two conditions: Y follows **instantly**,
and Y **cancels** X.

</div>

<pre>
この曲(きょく)を聞(き)く<b>たびに</b>、学生(がくせい)時代(じだい)を思(おも)い出(だ)す。
Every time I hear this song I think of my student days.
</pre>

<pre>
貯(た)める<b>そばから</b>使(つか)ってしまうので、いつまでも貯金(ちょきん)が増(ふ)えない。
I spend it as fast as I save it, so my savings never grow.
</pre>

Swap them and the first becomes nonsense - remembering does not undo hearing, and
nothing is being cancelled. Any <code>そばから</code> sentence, on the other hand,
can be downgraded to <code>たびに</code>, losing the immediacy and the futility but
staying grammatical.

### ～そばから vs ～が早(はや)いか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are literary and both mean "immediately after".
<code>が早(はや)いか</code> narrates **one occasion** in the past.
<code>そばから</code> describes a **recurring cycle**.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は皿(さら)が届(とど)く<b>が早(はや)いか</b>、手(て)をつけた。
The plate had barely arrived before my brother started on it.
</pre>

<pre>
作(つく)る<b>そばから</b>弟(おとうと)が食(た)べてしまうので、いつまでも皿(さら)がいっぱいにならない。
My brother eats it as fast as I make it, so the plate never fills up.
</pre>

The tense test is quick: <code>が早(はや)いか</code> almost always ends in a past
form, because it is telling a story. <code>そばから</code> very often ends in a
present form, because it is describing how things always go.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、買(か)った<b>そばから</b>傘(かさ)をなくした。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)、買(か)ったとたんに傘(かさ)をなくした。
  One umbrella, one occasion. <code>そばから</code> needs the cycle to repeat.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)の<b>そばから</b>道(みち)がぬれる。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>そばから</b>道(みち)がぬれていく。
  A verb clause has to come in front. Noun plus の gives the unrelated literal
  "beside" meaning.

- ❌ 勉強(べんきょう)する<b>そばから</b>成績(せいせき)が上(あ)がった。 → ✅ 勉強(べんきょう)した分(ぶん)だけ成績(せいせき)が上(あ)がった。
  The second clause has to undo the first. Studying and improving point the same
  way, so the pattern has nothing to bite on.

- ❌ 明日(あした)、片付(かたづ)ける<b>そばから</b>散(ち)らかるでしょう。 → ✅ 片付(かたづ)けてもすぐ散(ち)らかるでしょう。
  The pattern reports how things actually go, not a forecast about one future
  occasion.

## Practice

**1.** 新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)を決(き)める____、抜(ぬ)け道(みち)を見(み)つける人(ひと)が出(で)てくる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

決(き)める<b>そばから</b>

Each new rule is defeated as soon as it exists, and the sentence is about the
pattern rather than one occasion - the ending is present tense for that reason.
The tone of quiet exasperation is exactly what the form is for.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary spoken Japanese: 洗(あら)う<b>そばから</b>茶碗(ちゃわん)が流(なが)しにたまっていく。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

洗(あら)っても洗(あら)ってもすぐ茶碗(ちゃわん)がたまっちゃう。

<code>～ても～てもすぐ</code> is the everyday shape of the same complaint, and the
casual たまっちゃう matches the register. Nothing of the meaning is lost; only the
literary compression goes.

</details>

**3.** Choose and justify: 給料(きゅうりょう)が入(はい)る（そばから・が早(はや)いか）、支払(しはら)いに消(き)えていく。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

入(はい)る<b>そばから</b>支払(しはら)いに消(き)えていく。

The sentence describes what happens every month, and the ending is present tense.
<code>が早(はや)いか</code> narrates a single past scene and would clash with
both.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)は説明(せつめい)を聞(き)くそばから、すぐに理解(りかい)してくださいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は説明(せつめい)を聞(き)くとすぐに理解(りかい)してくださいました。

Two problems. Understanding does not cancel hearing, so there is no futile cycle
for <code>そばから</code> to describe; and the honorific
してくださいました sits oddly with a form whose default flavour is complaint.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 習(なら)った<b>そばから</b>忘(わす)れてしまう。
(b) 習(なら)ってもなかなか覚(おぼ)えられない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is vivid and rueful. It says the knowledge disappears at the moment of
acquisition, again and again, and the speaker is half laughing at themselves. It is
the sort of line you write in an essay or a study diary.

(b) is a flat, ordinary report of difficulty. There is no image of a losing cycle,
no immediacy, and no self-mockery - it would fit a conversation with a teacher
where the tone needs to stay plain.

</details>

# ～た瞬間に

**Meaning** · at the very instant that something happened - a pinpoint moment, with nothing on either side of it

瞬間(しゅんかん) is an ordinary noun meaning "instant, split second"
(瞬(またた)く間(ま), "the space of a blink"). Used as a connective it puts a pin
in the timeline: not "soon after", not "when", but *at that exact point*. Because
it is a real noun it is far more flexible than the connectives it competes with -
it can be pointed at, counted, made the subject of a sentence, or stood on its own
as <code>その瞬間(しゅんかん)</code>.

## Formation

The に is optional and is dropped freely, especially in speech and in headings.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (two past events) | た-form + 瞬間(しゅんかん)（に） | 触(さわ)る → 触(さわ)った瞬間(しゅんかん)に |
| Verb (the moment itself) | dictionary form + 瞬間(しゅんかん) | 押(お)す → シャッターを押(お)す瞬間(しゅんかん) |
| Verb (instruction, future) | た-form + 瞬間(しゅんかん)に | 鳴(な)る → 鳴(な)った瞬間(しゅんかん)に押(お)してください |
| Noun | noun + の + 瞬間(しゅんかん) | 勝利(しょうり) → 勝利(しょうり)の瞬間(しゅんかん) |
| Standing alone | その瞬間(しゅんかん) | その瞬間(しゅんかん)、時(とき)が止(と)まった |
| As a plain noun | 瞬間(しゅんかん)が／を／は | 決(き)まる瞬間(しゅんかん)を撮(と)った |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the two verb forms are not interchangeable. The た-form links **two
events** (見(み)た瞬間(しゅんかん)に逃(に)げた, "the instant I saw it I ran").
The dictionary form names **the moment itself** as a thing you can watch or
photograph (飛(と)び込(こ)む瞬間(しゅんかん), "the instant of diving in").

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
目(め)が合(あ)った<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、彼女(かのじょ)はにっこり笑(わら)った。
The moment our eyes met, she broke into a smile.
</pre>

<pre>
ボールが手(て)を離(はな)れた<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)に</b>、入(はい)ると分(わ)かった。
The instant the ball left my hand I knew it was going in.
</pre>

<pre>
ドアを開(あ)けた<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、線香(せんこう)のにおいが鼻(はな)をついた。
The moment I opened the door, the smell of incense hit me.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれた<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、頭(あたま)の中(なか)が真(ま)っ白(しろ)になった。
The instant my name was called, my mind went completely blank.
</pre>

<pre>
合図(あいず)が出(で)た<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)に</b>、ペダルを踏(ふ)み込(こ)んでください。
Push the pedal down the instant you get the signal.
</pre>

<pre>
地面(じめん)に足(あし)がついた<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、膝(ひざ)にするどい痛(いた)みが走(はし)った。
The moment my foot hit the ground, a sharp pain shot through my knee.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)が決(き)まった<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、スタンドの観客(かんきゃく)がいっせいに立(た)ち上(あ)がった。
The moment the title was decided, the whole stand rose to its feet.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)ぶりに実家(じっか)の門(もん)をくぐった<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、子(こ)どものころの記憶(きおく)が一気(いっき)によみがえってきた。
The moment I stepped through the gate of my parents' house for the first time in ten years, childhood memories came flooding back.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **register-neutral and thoroughly modern**. It is at home in conversation,
in email, in essays, in a job interview and in sports commentary, and it changes
nothing about the politeness of the sentence around it. Of the "the moment that"
patterns collected at this level, it is the one you should feel free to use without
thinking about style.

Its characteristic colour is **drama**. 瞬間(しゅんかん) is a slightly heightened
word - the instant a record falls, the instant you first see someone, the instant
a diagnosis is delivered - and it invites the listener to slow down and look at
that one point in time. For a mundane sequence it is faintly overblown:
<code>コンビニに入(はい)った瞬間(しゅんかん)、パンを買(か)った</code> reads as
though buying bread were a turning point in a life. Ordinary sequences want
<code>入(はい)ってすぐ</code>.

Because it is a plain noun, two things are possible that are impossible with
<code>とたん</code>. You can put an **instruction or a plan** after it -
<code>ランプが消(き)えた瞬間(しゅんかん)にボタンを押(お)してください</code> - and
you can use it about the **future**. Those two freedoms are the practical reason to
keep it in your active vocabulary.

The に is genuinely optional. Dropping it is very slightly more literary and very
common in narration and in captions; keeping it is neutral. Neither choice is
marked.

## Contrast

### ～た瞬間(しゅんかん)（に） vs ～たとたん（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とたん</code> is locked to the **past** and to something
**unexpected**. <code>瞬間(しゅんかん)</code> is a free noun: it takes commands,
plans and futures as well.

</div>

<pre>
席(せき)に座(すわ)っ<b>たとたん</b>、電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)り出(だ)した。
The instant I sat down, the phone started ringing.
</pre>

<pre>
席(せき)に座(すわ)った<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)に</b>、ボタンを押(お)してください。
Press the button the moment you sit down.
</pre>

The second sentence cannot be rewritten with <code>とたん</code>: ❌<code>座(すわ)ったとたん押(お)してください</code>
is ungrammatical. Going the other way, <code>とたん</code> can nearly always be
replaced by <code>瞬間(しゅんかん)</code>, losing only the sense of an unwelcome
surprise.

### ～た瞬間(しゅんかん)（に） vs ～た時(とき)（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>時(とき)</code> is a **stretch** of time.
<code>瞬間(しゅんかん)</code> is a **point** with no width.

</div>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)に行(い)っ<b>た時(とき)</b>、古(ふる)い寺(てら)をいくつも回(まわ)った。
When I went to Kyoto I visited a good many old temples.
</pre>

<pre>
門(もん)をくぐっ<b>た瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、空気(くうき)が変(か)わった。
The moment I passed through the gate, the air changed.
</pre>

The first sentence is impossible with 瞬間(しゅんかん): you cannot fit a
temple tour into a split second. The test is whether the second clause could
plausibly take 一瞬(いっしゅん), "for an instant". If it needs minutes, use
時(とき).

### ～た瞬間(しゅんかん)（に） vs ～た拍子(ひょうし)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>拍子(ひょうし)</code> needs a **physical jolt** that
**accidentally** causes the second event. <code>瞬間(しゅんかん)</code> just marks
the timing and says nothing about cause.

</div>

<pre>
振(ふ)り向(む)いた<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、棚(たな)のコップを落(お)としてしまった。
I turned round and knocked a glass off the shelf as I did so.
</pre>

<pre>
振(ふ)り向(む)いた<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、見(み)なれた顔(かお)が目(め)に入(はい)った。
The moment I turned round, a familiar face came into view.
</pre>

Only the first is about cause and mishap. Replace 拍子(ひょうし) with
瞬間(しゅんかん) there and the sentence still works but stops blaming the turn for
the broken glass; replace 瞬間(しゅんかん) with 拍子(ひょうし) in the second and
it fails, because turning round did not knock the face into existence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 電車(でんしゃ)が出(で)た<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)に</b>、ずっと本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいた。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)が出(で)てから、ずっと本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいた。
  A point in time cannot host a long activity. ずっと and 瞬間(しゅんかん)
  contradict each other.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)を見(み)る<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、驚(おどろ)いた。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)を見(み)た<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、驚(おどろ)いた。
  Linking two events that happened needs the た-form. The dictionary form would
  make it "the instant of looking at him" as a thing being described.

- ❌ 昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べた<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、散歩(さんぽ)に出(で)た。 → ✅ 昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べてすぐ、散歩(さんぽ)に出(で)た。
  Nothing dramatic is happening, and eating lunch is not instantaneous. The word
  oversells an ordinary sequence.

- ❌ 優勝(ゆうしょう)した<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)の時(とき)</b>は泣(な)いた。 → ✅ 優勝(ゆうしょう)した<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>は泣(な)いた。
  瞬間(しゅんかん) is already a time noun; adding 時(とき) doubles it.

## Practice

**1.** カーテンを開(あ)けた____、朝日(あさひ)がまぶしく差(さ)し込(こ)んできた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

開(あ)けた<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>（に）

Two events meeting at one point in time, with a vivid, sudden second event - the
core use. The た-form is required because both things actually happened; the に can
be left in or out.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 信号(しんごう)が青(あお)に変(か)わった（とたん・瞬間(しゅんかん)に）渡(わた)り始(はじ)めてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

変(か)わった<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)に</b>渡(わた)り始(はじ)めてください。

The sentence is an instruction about the future. <code>とたん</code> can only report
an unexpected past event, so it cannot carry てください;
瞬間(しゅんかん), an ordinary noun, can.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The moment I heard her voice, I knew something had happened."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)の声(こえ)を聞(き)いた<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、何(なに)かあったと分(わ)かった。

A single pinpoint moment plus a flash of realisation is exactly what
瞬間(しゅんかん) is for. Note that "something had happened" is naturally
何(なに)かあった - no need to build a past perfect.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 試合(しあい)が終(お)わった瞬間(しゅんかん)に、三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ほど拍手(はくしゅ)が続(つづ)いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 試合(しあい)が終(お)わったあと、三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ほど拍手(はくしゅ)が続(つづ)いた。（or 終(お)わった瞬間(しゅんかん)に拍手(はくしゅ)がわき起(お)こった）

Half an hour of applause cannot be squeezed into an instant. Either widen the time
word, or keep 瞬間(しゅんかん) and make the second clause punctual.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 指輪(ゆびわ)を見(み)た<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、母(はは)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)した。
(b) 指輪(ゆびわ)を見(み)た<b>時(とき)</b>、母(はは)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) collapses the two events into a single point: the recognition was instant and
involuntary, and the sentence asks the reader to feel that jolt. It is the more
emotionally loaded of the pair.

(b) simply gives the occasion. Looking at the ring is the setting in which the
memory arrived, and it may have surfaced after a few seconds of turning it over. It
is flatter, and perfectly natural in a plain account.

</details>

# ～た拍子に

**Meaning** · in the middle of one sudden movement, something else happened by accident - "as I turned round, I knocked it over"

拍子(ひょうし) is "beat, rhythm" (read **ひょうし**, not びょうし). Used as a
connective it names the split second of a physical action and blames it for an
unintended result. Three things are always true: the first clause is a **bodily
movement**, the second is **not what anyone wanted**, and the movement is what
**caused** it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + 拍子(ひょうし)に | 転(ころ)ぶ → 転(ころ)んだ拍子(ひょうし)に |
| Verb (する) | た-form + 拍子(ひょうし)に | くしゃみをする → くしゃみをした拍子(ひょうし)に |
| Set phrase | 何(なに)かの + 拍子(ひょうし)に | 何(なに)かの拍子(ひょうし)に外(はず)れたらしい |
| Second clause | past tense, an unintended result | 頭(あたま)をぶつけた／落(お)ちた |
| Not possible | dictionary form + 拍子(ひょうし)に | ❌転(ころ)ぶ拍子(ひょうし)に |
| Not possible | a deliberate second clause | ❌開(あ)けた拍子(ひょうし)に中(なか)に入(はい)った |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>何(なに)かの拍子(ひょうし)に</code> is worth learning as a unit. It
means "somehow, at some point, I don't know when" and is the standard way to
explain a small mystery: <code>何(なに)かの拍子(ひょうし)に設定(せってい)が変(か)わったようだ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
立(た)ち上(あ)がった<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、机(つくえ)に頭(あたま)をぶつけた。
I stood up and banged my head on the desk as I did.
</pre>

<pre>
くしゃみをした<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、眼鏡(めがね)が飛(と)んでいった。
I sneezed and my glasses went flying.
</pre>

<pre>
振(ふ)り返(かえ)った<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、かばんが棚(たな)の荷物(にもつ)に当(あ)たった。
As I turned to look behind me, my bag caught the luggage on the rack.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)かの<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、鍵(かぎ)がポケットから落(お)ちたらしい。
The key seems to have dropped out of my pocket somewhere along the way.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが急(きゅう)に止(と)まった<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、前(まえ)の人(ひと)に肩(かた)がぶつかった。
The bus stopped short and my shoulder went into the person in front.
</pre>

<pre>
重(おも)い荷物(にもつ)を持(も)ち上(あ)げた<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、腰(こし)を痛(いた)めてしまった。
I hurt my back in the act of lifting a heavy load.
</pre>

<pre>
転(ころ)んだ<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>手(て)をついて、手首(てくび)をひねってしまったらしい。
It seems he put a hand out as he fell and twisted his wrist doing it.
</pre>

<pre>
扉(とびら)を勢(いきお)いよく閉(し)めた<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、棚(たな)の上(うえ)の写真(しゃしん)立(た)てが倒(たお)れて割(わ)れた。
Slamming the door knocked the photo frame off the shelf and broke it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This one is **ordinary spoken Japanese**. It is not literary and not stiff; people
use it constantly to explain how a small accident happened, and it fits both
<code>ぶつけた</code> among friends and <code>ぶつけてしまいました</code> in a report to
a supervisor. Of the "the moment that" family it is the most everyday after
<code>とたん</code>.

Its real content is **exculpatory**. Saying <code>振(ふ)り返(かえ)った拍子(ひょうし)に落(お)としました</code>
rather than <code>落(お)としました</code> quietly enters a plea: the movement did
it, not my carelessness. This makes it the natural verb for apologies and insurance
claims, and it is also why it sounds strange attached to something you did on
purpose - you cannot disown an intention.

The first clause has to be a **movement of a body or an object**. Abstract or
purely temporal triggers do not work: ❌<code>連絡(れんらく)が来(き)た拍子(ひょうし)に</code>,
❌<code>春(はる)になった拍子(ひょうし)に</code>. If nothing physically lurched,
this is not the pattern.

Watch the reading. 拍子(ひょうし) keeps its plain **ひょうし**; there is no rendaku
here, and the same word is what you hear in 三(さん)拍子(びょうし) only because
of the counter in front of it.

## Contrast

### ～た拍子(ひょうし)に vs ～た弾(はず)みに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are **near-identical**, and no dependable meaning
separates them. <code>弾(はず)み</code> is a shade more physical, since the word is
"bounce, momentum"; <code>拍子(ひょうし)</code> is the commoner of the two overall.

</div>

<pre>
ぶつかった<b>弾(はず)みに</b>、手(て)に持(も)っていたカップを落(お)とした。
The collision jolted the cup out of my hand.
</pre>

<pre>
ぶつかった<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、ポケットのペンが飛(と)び出(だ)した。
The pen shot out of my pocket when we bumped into each other.
</pre>

Either word passes in either sentence. <code>弾(はず)み</code> also appears as
<code>弾(はず)みで</code> and in the fixed phrase
<code>ものの弾(はず)みで</code>, "in the heat of the moment", which
<code>拍子(ひょうし)</code> does not have. Learn them as a pair rather than trying
to keep them apart.

### ～た拍子(ひょうし)に vs ～た瞬間(しゅんかん)（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>瞬間(しゅんかん)</code> reports **when**.
<code>拍子(ひょうし)</code> reports **what caused it**, and the cause is a jolt.

</div>

<pre>
棚(たな)に手(て)を伸(の)ばした<b>瞬間(しゅんかん)</b>、後(うし)ろで物音(ものおと)がした。
The moment I reached up to the shelf, there was a noise behind me.
</pre>

<pre>
棚(たな)に手(て)を伸(の)ばした<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、隣(となり)の箱(はこ)を落(お)とした。
Reaching up to the shelf, I knocked the box next to it down.
</pre>

The first cannot take 拍子(ひょうし): reaching for a shelf does not produce a
noise behind you. The second can take 瞬間(しゅんかん), but it becomes a bare
statement of timing and stops explaining the accident.

### ～た拍子(ひょうし)に vs ～たとたん（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とたん</code> needs only **surprise**, and its first clause
can be anything at all. <code>拍子(ひょうし)</code> needs a **physical action** and
a **causal link**.

</div>

<pre>
外(そと)に出(で)<b>たとたん</b>、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
The instant I stepped outside, it started to rain.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を開(ひら)いた<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、骨(ほね)が一本(いっぽん)折(お)れた。
One of the ribs snapped as I opened the umbrella.
</pre>

Going outside did not cause the rain, so only <code>とたん</code> works there.
Opening the umbrella is exactly what broke the rib, so
<code>拍子(ひょうし)</code> is the pointed choice in the second - though
<code>とたん</code> would also pass, at the cost of the explanation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)った<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、驚(おどろ)いた。 → ✅ 電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)っ<b>たとたん</b>、驚(おどろ)いた。
  A ringing phone is a sound, not a jolt, and being startled is not an accident
  caused by a movement.

- ❌ ドアを開(あ)けた<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、部屋(へや)に入(はい)った。 → ✅ ドアを開(あ)けて、部屋(へや)に入(はい)った。
  Walking in is deliberate. The second clause must be something you did not mean to
  do.

- ❌ 転(ころ)ぶ<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>膝(ひざ)をすりむいた。 → ✅ 転(ころ)んだ<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>膝(ひざ)をすりむいた。
  The た-form is required. There is no dictionary-form version of this pattern.

- ❌ 次(つぎ)から立(た)ち上(あ)がった<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>気(き)をつけます。 → ✅ 次(つぎ)からは立(た)ち上(あ)がるときに気(き)をつけます。
  It narrates an accident that already happened; it cannot frame a resolution about
  the future.

## Practice

**1.** 窓(まど)を閉(し)めようとした____、カーテンレールを引(ひ)っかけてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

閉(し)めようとした<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>

A physical movement produces an unintended result - the defining shape. Note the
た-form and the てしまった at the end, which is the natural partner for an accident.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 春(はる)になった（拍子(ひょうし)に・とたん）、花粉症(かふんしょう)がひどくなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

春(はる)になった<b>とたん</b>、花粉症(かふんしょう)がひどくなった。

The arrival of spring is not a physical jolt, and hay fever is not knocked over by
it. With no bodily movement to blame, 拍子(ひょうし) has nothing to attach to.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He tripped, and as he did he dropped the papers he was carrying."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"and as he did" is doing precisely the work of 拍子(ひょうし)に - it ties the
mishap to the stumble. てしまった carries the note of regret that English leaves
implicit.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 荷物(にもつ)を下(お)ろした拍子(ひょうし)に、ゆっくり休(やす)むことにした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 荷物(にもつ)を下(お)ろしてから、ゆっくり休(やす)むことにした。

Deciding to rest is a decision, not an accident, and it is not caused by the

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 振(ふ)り向(む)いた<b>拍子(ひょうし)に</b>、花瓶(かびん)を倒(たお)してしまいました。
(b) 振(ふ)り向(む)いたとき、花瓶(かびん)を倒(たお)してしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the turn itself as the culprit: the speaker's sleeve or elbow swung
into the vase. It is a mild defence, and it is what you would say while
apologising.

(b) only fixes the timing. The vase went over at the moment of turning, but the
sentence does not say the turning did it - it could have been a second person, or
pure clumsiness.

</details>

# ～とみるや

**Meaning** · the moment someone judged that the situation was thus, they acted on it - sized it up and moved, usually fast and usually to their own advantage

The 見(み)る here is not eyesight; it is the 見(み)る of
<code>甘(あま)く見(み)る</code> and <code>様子(ようす)を見(み)る</code> -
to assess, to reckon, to conclude. The や is the same literary particle as in
<code>や否(いな)や</code>. Put together: *saw how things stood, and at once …*.
An act of judgement is built into the pattern, which is what separates it from
every other "the moment that" form at this level.

## Formation

The と in front is the quotative と, so whatever it follows takes its plain
sentence-final shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + とみるや | 逃(に)げ切(き)れない → 逃(に)げ切(き)れないとみるや |
| い-adjective | plain form + とみるや | 分(ぶ)が悪(わる)い → 分(ぶ)が悪(わる)いとみるや |
| な-adjective | ～だ + とみるや（or bare） | 不利(ふり) → 不利(ふり)だとみるや／不利(ふり)とみるや |
| Noun | ～だ + とみるや（or bare） | チャンス → チャンスだとみるや／チャンスとみるや |
| Kanji spelling | ～と見(み)るや | 危険(きけん)と見(み)るや |
| Longer variant | ～と見(み)るや否(いな)や | 勝(か)てないと見(み)るや否(いな)や |
| Second clause | past tense, an immediate decisive act | 話題(わだい)を変(か)えた |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the bare-noun version (<code>不利(ふり)とみるや</code>, with no だ) is the
newspaper shape and is very common in print. Both are correct; the だ version is
marginally the plainer of the two.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は分(ぶ)が悪(わる)い<b>とみるや</b>、さっさと話題(わだい)を変(か)えた。
The moment he saw he was losing the argument, he changed the subject.
</pre>

<pre>
店主(てんしゅ)は客(きゃく)が迷(まよ)っている<b>とみるや</b>、すぐに声(こえ)をかけた。
Seeing that the customer was undecided, the shopkeeper was over to her at once.
</pre>

<pre>
少年(しょうねん)は大人(おとな)が見(み)ていない<b>とみるや</b>、棚(たな)の菓子(かし)に手(て)を伸(の)ばした。
The boy judged that no adult was watching and reached for the sweets on the shelf.
</pre>

<pre>
犯人(はんにん)は逃(に)げ切(き)れない<b>とみるや</b>、その場(ば)に座(すわ)り込(こ)んだ。
Realising he could not get away, the man simply sat down where he was.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)チームは勝(か)てない<b>とみるや</b>、主力(しゅりょく)の選手(せんしゅ)を下(さ)げてきた。
Once the other side decided the game was gone, they took their key players off.
</pre>

<pre>
各社(かくしゃ)は需要(じゅよう)が伸(の)びる<b>とみるや</b>、いっせいに増産(ぞうさん)に踏(ふ)み切(き)った。
The moment the firms judged that demand would grow, they all moved to raise output.
</pre>

<pre>
政府(せいふ)は世論(よろん)が変(か)わった<b>とみるや</b>、これまでの方針(ほうしん)をあっさり撤回(てっかい)した。
As soon as the government saw which way opinion had turned, it dropped its policy without ceremony.
</pre>

<pre>
その男(おとこ)は警官(けいかん)の姿(すがた)が見(み)えない<b>とみるや</b>、路地(ろじ)の奥(おく)へ走(はし)り去(さ)った。
The instant the man saw the police were out of sight, he was away down the alley.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>とみるや</code> is **written Japanese**. It belongs to news copy, editorials,
sports reporting, history, and fiction with a narrator who is watching people
closely. It is not stiff in the way a legal formula is stiff, but you will not hear
it over dinner; in speech the same idea comes out as
<code>無理(むり)だと分(わ)かると、すぐに</code> or
<code>～のを見(み)て、すぐ</code>.

Two things travel with it almost every time.

**The subject is someone being observed** - a suspect, a company, a rival, a
politician, a child. The narrator is describing behaviour from outside, and the
reader is invited to draw a conclusion about the person's character. Using it about
yourself is possible but unusual, and it makes you sound as though you were
narrating your own biography.

**There is a faint critical edge.** The judgement is quick, self-interested and
often not very admirable: dropping a policy the moment it stops polling well,
changing the subject the moment you are losing. The pattern is not automatically
negative - the shopkeeper example above is simply attentive - but if you want a
neutral report of good timing, <code>～のを見(み)て</code> carries less baggage.

The second clause is a **completed act**, so past tense and no requests,
suggestions or volitional forms. And because the whole point is an assessment, the
first clause has to be something assessable: a state of affairs, a prospect, a
balance of advantage. A bare physical event -
❌<code>ドアが開(あ)いたとみるや</code> - has nothing to judge.

## Contrast

### ～とみるや vs ～や否(いな)や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>や否(いな)や</code> links **two events**.
<code>とみるや</code> inserts a **person's judgement** between them: they perceived
something, and that perception is why they moved.

</div>

<pre>
開場(かいじょう)のアナウンスが流(なが)れる<b>や否(いな)や</b>、列(れつ)が動(うご)き始(はじ)めた。
No sooner had the opening announcement gone out than the queue began to move.
</pre>

<pre>
係員(かかりいん)は列(れつ)が長(なが)すぎる<b>とみるや</b>、入口(いりぐち)をもう一(ひと)つ開(あ)けた。
Seeing that the queue was too long, the attendant opened a second entrance.
</pre>

The first is pure sequence; nobody in it decides anything. In the second, the
attendant's assessment is the hinge of the sentence, and
<code>や否(いな)や</code> would throw that information away.

### ～とみるや vs ～のを見(み)て

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のを見(み)て</code> is **literal seeing**, in neutral
everyday Japanese. <code>とみるや</code> is **concluding**, in literary Japanese,
with a note of calculation.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)は弟(おとうと)が泣(な)いている<b>のを見(み)て</b>、すぐにだき上(あ)げた。
Seeing my little brother crying, my mother picked him straight up.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は母(はは)が甘(あま)い<b>とみるや</b>、同(おな)じ手(て)を何度(なんど)も使(つか)った。
Once my brother worked out that our mother was a soft touch, he used the same trick over and over.
</pre>

Notice that only the second involves working something out. Notice too that
<code>のを見(み)て</code> requires an object you could photograph, whereas
<code>とみるや</code> takes an invisible judgement - "she is a soft touch" is not
something you see.

### ～とみるや vs ～とみえて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same verb, opposite jobs. <code>とみるや</code> = *the person in
the story judged, and acted*. <code>とみえて</code> = *the narrator infers*:
"apparently, by the look of it".

</div>

<pre>
道(みち)がぬれている。夜中(よなか)に雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>とみえて</b>、草(くさ)にも水(みず)がついている。
The road is wet. It seems to have rained in the night - there's water on the grass too.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は雨(あめ)になる<b>とみるや</b>、洗濯物(せんたくもの)を取(と)り込(こ)んだ。
The moment she judged it was going to rain, she brought the washing in.
</pre>

These are easy to confuse on the page because they differ by two kana. The test is
who is doing the judging: with <code>とみえて</code> it is the writer, and the
sentence is a guess; with <code>とみるや</code> it is the character, and the sentence
is an action.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)の顔(かお)<b>とみるや</b>、逃(に)げ出(だ)した。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)の顔(かお)を見(み)る<b>や</b>、逃(に)げ出(だ)した。
  Two different constructions. <code>とみるや</code> takes a quoted judgement;
  <code>～を見(み)るや</code> is the plain literary "as soon as (he) saw".

- ❌ ドアが開(あ)いた<b>とみるや</b>、中(なか)に入(はい)った。 → ✅ ドアが開(あ)く<b>や否(いな)や</b>、中(なか)に入(はい)った。
  An open door is an event, not a conclusion. The pattern needs something that can
  be assessed.

- ❌ 危(あぶ)ない<b>とみるや</b>、すぐに逃(に)げてください。 → ✅ 危(あぶ)ないと思(おも)ったら、すぐに逃(に)げてください。
  Instructions are impossible after it. The pattern narrates what somebody already
  did.

- ❌ この絵(え)は高(たか)い<b>とみるや</b>、買(か)う人(ひと)が少(すく)ない。 → ✅ この絵(え)は高(たか)い<b>とみえて</b>、買(か)う人(ひと)が少(すく)ない。
  This is the 見(み)えて／見(み)るや mix-up. Here the writer is inferring, so
  the form is <code>とみえて</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 警備員(けいびいん)は様子(ようす)がおかしい____、すぐに無線(むせん)で連絡(れんらく)した。

The guard assesses the situation and acts on the assessment immediately, and the

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

took one look at the numbers and dropped the vote. There is a hint that this was a
tactical retreat rather than a principled one, and the sentence is journalistic in
register.

(b) states a cause flatly. The opposition was large, so the vote was postponed. It
says nothing about how quickly he decided, nothing about his motives, and it is
ordinary neutral Japanese.

</details>

# ～が最後

**Meaning** · once that happens, that is that - the consequence is unavoidable, and it is almost always one you would rather avoid

The 最後(さいご) is "the end" in the sense of *the point of no return*. The
sentence sets up an action, and then says that performing it once slams a door: from
there on, the outcome is out of anyone's hands. English reaches for "once you …,
there's no …" or "if he ever …, he'll never …", and the fatalism is the same.

## Formation

The verb in front is fixed in the **た-form**, whatever the tense of the sentence
as a whole.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | た-form + が最後(さいご) | 言(い)う → 言(い)ったが最後(さいご) |
| Verb (る-verb) | た-form + が最後(さいご) | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れたが最後(さいご) |
| Verb (する) | した + が最後(さいご) | 署名(しょめい)する → 署名(しょめい)したが最後(さいご) |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き)た + が最後(さいご) | 来(く)る → 来(き)たが最後(さいご) |
| Colloquial variant | た-form + ら最後(さいご) | 寝(ね)る → 寝(ね)たら最後(さいご) |
| Second clause | an unavoidable, normally unwelcome result | 二度(にど)と戻(もど)れない |
| Not possible | dictionary form + が最後(さいご) | ❌言(い)うが最後(さいご) |
| Not possible | noun or adjective in front | ❌危険(きけん)だが最後(さいご) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the second clause has to be **beyond control**. Look for
二度(にど)と…ない, もう…ない, ずっと, 必(かなら)ず, ～てしまう, ～なければならない,
or a potential in the negative. If the outcome could be avoided by trying harder,
this is the wrong pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの人(ひと)は話(はな)し始(はじ)めた<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、一時間(いちじかん)は止(と)まらない。
Once that man starts talking, he won't stop for an hour.
</pre>

<pre>
この坂(さか)を下(くだ)り始(はじ)めた<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、途中(とちゅう)では止(と)まれない。
Once you start down this slope there's no stopping halfway.
</pre>

<pre>
パスワードを忘(わす)れた<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、中(なか)のデータは二度(にど)と取(と)り出(だ)せない。
Forget the password and the data inside is gone for good.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)にその話(はなし)をした<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、翌日(よくじつ)には部署(ぶしょ)全員(ぜんいん)が知(し)っていた。
Tell him something and by the next day the whole department knows.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)この沼(ぬま)にはまった<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、自力(じりき)ではもう上(あ)がれないそうだ。
They say that once you go into this bog you can't get yourself out again.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)で道(みち)を外(はず)れた<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、自分(じぶん)がどこにいるのかも分(わ)からなくなる。
Stray off the path in the mountains and you lose track even of where you are.
</pre>

<pre>
契約(けいやく)書(しょ)に署名(しょめい)した<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、あとから条件(じょうけん)を変(か)えることはできません。
Once you have signed the contract, the terms cannot be altered afterwards.
</pre>

<pre>
この漫画(まんが)は一巻(いっかん)読(よ)み始(はじ)めた<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、夜中(よなか)まで止(と)められなくなるので覚悟(かくご)したほうがいい。
Start volume one of this manga and you'll be up until the small hours - consider yourself warned.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>が最後(さいご)</code> is the **written and slightly dramatic** member of the
pair; <code>たら最後(さいご)</code> is its everyday spoken twin. You will meet
<code>が最後(さいご)</code> in essays, warnings, contracts explained in plain
language, blurbs and fiction, and it is perfectly usable in a formal spoken setting
such as a briefing. Between friends, <code>たら最後(さいご)</code> sounds more
natural.

The tone is **warning, exaggeration, or fatalism** - often all three at once, and
often affectionate. Half the real-world uses are hyperbole about a harmless habit:
the friend who cannot stop talking, the game you cannot put down, the sofa you fall
asleep on. Because of that, the pattern carries a slight raise of the eyebrow, and
in a genuinely serious context - a safety notice, a medical warning - a plainer
<code>一度(いちど)…すると</code> is usually better.

Two restrictions matter in practice. The consequence must be **bad or at least
unwanted**: ❌<code>合格(ごうかく)したが最後(さいご)、いい会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)れる</code>
is not a sentence, because good news does not need a point of no return. And the
second clause must be a **statement**, not a request or a plan; the pattern reports
how the world works, and there is nothing left for the listener to decide.

The 最後(さいご) is fossilised here. Do not read it as "last" - nothing in
<code>寝(ね)たが最後(さいご)</code> is about being final in a sequence.

## Contrast

### ～たが最後(さいご) vs ～たら最後(さいご)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register.
<code>が最後(さいご)</code> is the written form; <code>たら最後(さいご)</code> is
what people say.

</div>

<pre>
ソファーで寝(ね)<b>たら最後(さいご)</b>、朝(あさ)まで起(お)きないよ。
If you fall asleep on that sofa you'll be out till morning.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)口(くち)に出(だ)し<b>たが最後(さいご)</b>、その言葉(ことば)を取(と)り消(け)すことはできない。
Once a word has left your mouth, there is no taking it back.
</pre>

Read them aloud and the split is obvious: the first is a warning to a flatmate, the
second is a line from an essay. There is no difference of meaning to learn, so pick
by the company the sentence is keeping.

### ～たが最後(さいご) vs ～（よ）うものなら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>が最後(さいご)</code> says the action **shuts a door**:
after it, the outcome is fixed. <code>（よ）うものなら</code> says the action would be
**reckless to attempt**: if you dared, look what would happen.

</div>

<pre>
遅刻(ちこく)し<b>ようものなら</b>、部長(ぶちょう)に何(なに)を言(い)われるか分(わ)からない。
If I were ever late, there's no telling what the manager would say.
</pre>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)をなくし<b>たが最後(さいご)</b>、再発行(さいはっこう)はしてもらえない。
Lose this document and you will not get another one issued.
</pre>

The first is about the risk of trying; the second is about irreversibility once
done. They overlap in warnings, and where both fit,
<code>（よ）うものなら</code> puts the weight on the speaker's nerves and
<code>が最後(さいご)</code> on the machinery of consequences.

### ～たが最後(さいご) vs ～たとたん（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とたん</code> reports **one past moment** that surprised
somebody. <code>が最後(さいご)</code> states a **general rule** about what always
follows.

</div>

<pre>
その話(はなし)を始(はじ)め<b>たとたん</b>、部屋(へや)が静(しず)かになった。
The moment he started on that story, the room went quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)がその話(はなし)を始(はじ)め<b>たが最後(さいご)</b>、だれも口(くち)をはさめなくなる。
Once he gets going on that story, nobody can get a word in.
</pre>

The tense gives it away. <code>とたん</code> ends in the past because it is
narrating; <code>が最後(さいご)</code> usually ends in a present or potential form
because it is describing how things invariably go. That also means only the second
can be used as a warning about the future.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 言(い)う<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、後(あと)には戻(もど)れない。 → ✅ 言(い)った<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、後(あと)には戻(もど)れない。
  The た-form is fixed. The pattern is about an action already completed, even in a
  sentence about the future.

- ❌ 優勝(ゆうしょう)した<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、全国(ぜんこく)大会(たいかい)に出(で)られる。 → ✅ 優勝(ゆうしょう)すれば、全国(ぜんこく)大会(たいかい)に出(で)られる。
  The consequence must be unwelcome or at least uncontrollable. Good news wants a
  plain conditional.

- ❌ この道(みち)に入(はい)った<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、気(き)をつけてください。 → ✅ この道(みち)に入(はい)ったら、気(き)をつけてください。
  No requests or instructions after it. The pattern says the matter is already out
  of your hands.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は昨日(きのう)部屋(へや)を出(で)た<b>が最後(さいご)</b>、帰(かえ)ってこなかった。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は昨日(きのう)部屋(へや)を出(で)たきり、帰(かえ)ってこなかった。
  One particular occasion in the past is <code>～たきり</code>.
  <code>が最後(さいご)</code> generalises, so it does not sit with 昨日(きのう).

## Practice

**1.** この機械(きかい)は一度(いちど)止(と)めた____、再起動(さいきどう)に一時間(いちじかん)かかってしまう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

止(と)めた<b>が最後(さいご)</b>

Stopping the machine commits you to an hour of waiting, and nothing you do
afterwards changes that - an irreversible and unwelcome consequence. Note the
た-form and the てしまう at the end, which is the pattern's favourite partner.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 課長(かちょう)にそんなことを言(い)（ったが最後(さいご)・おうものなら）、会議(かいぎ)が一時間(いちじかん)延(の)びるだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

言(い)<b>おうものなら</b>、会議(かいぎ)が一時間(いちじかん)延(の)びるだろう。

だろう marks this as a hypothetical the speaker is imagining and hoping to avoid,
which is <code>（よ）うものなら</code> territory. <code>が最後(さいご)</code> would
work better with a flat statement of consequence such as 延(の)びる.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Once you lend him a book, you never get it back."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)に本(ほん)を貸(か)し<b>たが最後(さいご)</b>、二度(にど)と返(かえ)ってこない。

"Once you …, you never …" is almost a direct calque of the pattern.
二度(にど)と plus a negative is the standard way to spell out the finality, and
the present tense keeps it general rather than about one particular book.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだが最後(さいご)、痛(いた)みがすぐに消(き)えます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この薬(くすり)を飲(の)めば、痛(いた)みがすぐに消(き)えます。

The consequence here is exactly what the patient wants, and
<code>が最後(さいご)</code> only introduces outcomes you would escape if you
could. A plain ば conditional carries the good news without the note of doom.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)はゲームを始(はじ)め<b>たが最後(さいご)</b>、食事(しょくじ)にも来(こ)ない。
(b) 彼(かれ)はゲームを始(はじ)め<b>ると</b>、食事(しょくじ)にも来(こ)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) dramatises. It frames starting the game as a threshold he crosses, after which
nothing anyone does will bring him to the table - and the exaggeration carries a
touch of complaint or amused despair.

(b) is a flat statement of a regular pattern: when he plays, he misses meals. It
reports the same facts without the sense of a door closing behind him, and it would
suit a neutral description of his habits.

</details>

# ～と思いきや

**Meaning** · I fully expected one thing, and it turned out to be another - "and yet, as it happened …"

<code>思(おも)いきや</code> is a fossil. The き is the classical past and the や a
rhetorical question, so the original force was "did I think that? - I did not." All
that is left in modern Japanese is a compact way of setting up an expectation and
knocking it down in the same breath. The surprise is the whole content: if the
second half is not unexpected, the pattern has nothing to do.

## Formation

Everything in front is in its plain sentence-final shape. The unit
<code>思(おも)いきや</code> never conjugates.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + と思(おも)いきや | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)ると思(おも)いきや |
| い-adjective | plain form + と思(おも)いきや | 難(むずか)しい → 難(むずか)しいと思(おも)いきや |
| な-adjective | ～だ + と思(おも)いきや | 簡単(かんたん) → 簡単(かんたん)だと思(おも)いきや |
| Noun | ～だ + と思(おも)いきや | 新人(しんじん) → 新人(しんじん)だと思(おも)いきや |
| Very common variant | ～か + と思(おも)いきや | 留守(るす) → 留守(るす)かと思(おも)いきや |
| Second clause | the fact, contrary to what was expected | 意外(いがい)に早(はや)く終(お)わった |
| Not possible | polite or conjugated forms of the unit | ❌思(おも)いきやました |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the <code>か</code> variant is worth having. Adding か marks the
expectation as a **guess** rather than a firm belief, and it softens the sentence:
<code>楽勝(らくしょう)かと思(おも)いきや</code>, "I rather assumed it would be
easy". With a noun or a な-adjective, か is often the more natural of the two.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
簡単(かんたん)な仕事(しごと)だ<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、三日(みっか)もかかってしまった。
I took it for a simple job, and it ended up taking three days.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)になるか<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、昼(ひる)すぎから見事(みごと)に晴(は)れた。
I thought we were in for rain, but from midday it cleared up beautifully.
</pre>

<pre>
留守(るす)か<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、奥(おく)のほうから返事(へんじ)が聞(き)こえた。
I assumed nobody was in, and then an answer came from somewhere at the back.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)だ<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、十年(じゅうねん)の経験(けいけん)がある人(ひと)だった。
I had him down as a newcomer; he turned out to have ten years behind him.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)いから味(あじ)は期待(きたい)できない<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、驚(おどろ)くほどおいしかった。
Cheap, so I expected nothing of the taste - and it was astonishingly good.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かな住宅街(じゅうたくがい)だ<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、夜(よる)になると線路(せんろ)の音(おと)がよく響(ひび)いてくる。
It looks like a quiet residential street, but at night the noise from the railway carries.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)は楽勝(らくしょう)か<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、終盤(しゅうばん)まで一点(いってん)差(さ)のまま進(すす)んだ。
The match looked like a stroll, and instead it stayed within a point until the closing stages.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)が反対(はんたい)するか<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、その案(あん)は意外(いがい)にもすんなり通(とお)ってしまった。
I expected the whole room to object; in the event the proposal went through without a murmur.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Although the form is classical, this one is **alive in modern writing**. It is
everywhere in reviews, blogs, sports write-ups, travel pieces and magazine copy,
because its shape is exactly what a punchline needs - build the expectation, drop
it. You will also hear it in speech, where it lands as a small knowing flourish:
the speaker is telling a story and enjoying it.

The colour is **light**. It suits pleasant surprises, mild disappointments and
comic reversals, and it sits badly on genuine misfortune -
<code>軽(かる)いけがだと思(おも)いきや、骨(ほね)が折(お)れていた</code> is
fine, but a serious accident or a bereavement wants
<code>～と思(おも)っていたが</code> and no flourish at all. Delivered in a solemn
context it can read as flippant.

The expectation is normally **the speaker's own**, held before the facts came in.
That is why the second clause has to be a **discovery** rather than a decision:
❌<code>難(むずか)しいと思(おも)いきや、頑張(がんば)った</code> fails because
working hard is not something that surprises you. Anything you did on purpose
belongs in a different sentence.

One mechanical point: the unit is frozen. There is no
❌<code>思(おも)いきやです</code>, no ❌<code>思(おも)いきやました</code>, and no
negative. If the surrounding sentence is polite, the politeness goes on the final
verb - <code>晴(は)れました</code>, not on <code>思(おも)いきや</code>.

## Contrast

### ～と思(おも)いきや vs ～かと思(おも)うと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both contain 思(おも)う and neither is about thinking.
<code>と思(おも)いきや</code> is about a **wrong expectation**;
<code>かと思(おも)うと</code> is about **timing** - one thing had barely happened
before the next did.

</div>

<pre>
泣(な)いていた<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、もう笑(わら)っている。
One moment she's crying, the next she's laughing.
</pre>

<pre>
泣(な)き出(だ)すか<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、子(こ)どもはにっこり笑(わら)った。
I braced myself for tears, and instead the child smiled.
</pre>

The first is a stopwatch; the second is a mistaken forecast. The giveaway is that
<code>かと思(おも)うと</code> describes something the speaker actually observed
happening first, while the clause before
<code>と思(おも)いきや</code> never happened at all.

### ～と思(おも)いきや vs ～はずだったのに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>はずだったのに</code> is a **plan or arrangement that
failed**, and it carries regret. <code>と思(おも)いきや</code> is a **guess that
missed**, and it carries surprise, which may be delighted.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)届(とど)く<b>はずだったのに</b>、まだ荷物(にもつ)が来(き)ていない。
It was supposed to arrive today, and the parcel still isn't here.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)になるか<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、荷物(にもつ)は翌日(よくじつ)届(とど)いた。
I thought it would be next week; the parcel came the very next day.
</pre>

<code>はずだった</code> implies something was arranged - a schedule, a promise, a
rule - and someone has let it slip. <code>と思(おも)いきや</code> implies only that
the speaker had an idea in their head. Put a complaint after
<code>と思(おも)いきや</code> and it sounds oddly cheerful about the disaster.

### ～と思(おも)いきや vs ～に反(はん)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に反(はん)して</code> is the **neutral, formal** way to
report an outcome that went against expectations. <code>と思(おも)いきや</code> is
**personal and lively**, and it names the expectation as a whole clause.

</div>

<pre>
大方(おおかた)の予想(よそう)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、投票率(とうひょうりつ)は大(おお)きく伸(の)びた。
Contrary to most predictions, turnout rose sharply.
</pre>

<pre>
投票率(とうひょうりつ)は下(さ)がるか<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、前回(ぜんかい)を大(おお)きく上回(うわまわ)った。
I assumed turnout would fall - in fact it was well up on last time.
</pre>

<code>に反(はん)して</code> takes a **noun** (予想(よそう), 期待(きたい),
予測(よそく)) and belongs in a report; <code>と思(おも)いきや</code> takes a
clause and belongs in a voice. For an official document, choose the first.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 難(むずか)しい<b>と思(おも)いきやました</b>が、簡単(かんたん)でした。 → ✅ 難(むずか)しい<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、簡単(かんたん)でした。
  The unit never conjugates and never takes ます. Politeness lives on the final
  verb.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がかかる<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、早(はや)めに出発(しゅっぱつ)した。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がかかると思(おも)ったので、早(はや)めに出発(しゅっぱつ)した。
  Leaving early is a decision, not a surprise. The second clause has to be
  something that turned out to be so.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は晴(は)れる<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)っていこう。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は晴(は)れると思(おも)うが、傘(かさ)を持(も)っていこう。
  The pattern looks back at an expectation the facts have already overturned. It
  cannot be used about the future.

- ❌ 簡単(かんたん)<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、簡単(かんたん)だった。 → ✅ 簡単(かんたん)だと思(おも)っていたとおり、簡単(かんたん)だった。
  If the expectation was correct, there is no reversal, and the connective has
  nothing to overturn.

## Practice

**1.** 初日(しょにち)はすいている____、開店(かいてん)前(まえ)から長(なが)い列(れつ)ができていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すいているか<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>

An expectation and its flat contradiction, discovered on arrival. The か is natural
here because the speaker was guessing rather than certain, and the second clause is
a fact they found, not something they did.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 静(しず)かになった（かと思(おも)うと・と思(おも)いきや）、また工事(こうじ)の音(おと)が鳴(な)り始(はじ)めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

静(しず)かになった<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、また工事(こうじ)の音(おと)が鳴(な)り始(はじ)めた。

The quiet really did arrive - it just did not last. That is a statement about
timing, so <code>かと思(おも)うと</code> is the fit;
<code>と思(おも)いきや</code> would claim the quiet never happened at all.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I thought the shop had closed down, but it had simply moved next door."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

店(みせ)はつぶれたか<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、隣(となり)に移(うつ)っただけだった。

The expectation is a guess, so か fits; the outcome is a discovery, which is what
the pattern needs. だけだった carries the small deflation that the English "simply"
is doing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来年(らいねん)の試験(しけん)は難(むずか)しいと思(おも)いきや、今(いま)から準備(じゅんび)するつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来年(らいねん)の試験(しけん)は難(むずか)しいと思(おも)うので、今(いま)から準備(じゅんび)するつもりです。

Two problems: the sentence is about the future, and the second clause is the
speaker's own plan. <code>と思(おも)いきや</code> needs an expectation that reality
has already overturned.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)くなるか<b>と思(おも)いきや</b>、三十分(さんじゅっぷん)で終(お)わった。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)くなると思(おも)っていたが、三十分(さんじゅっぷん)で終(お)わった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) has a small performance in it. The connective sets up the expectation and drops
it, and the reader is meant to feel the relief - it is the sentence you would write
in a blog post or say while telling the story afterwards.

(b) reports the same two facts plainly. It is neutral, fits any register including a
written report, and draws no attention to the reversal.

</details>

# ～てからというもの

**Meaning** · ever since that happened, things have been different - and they still are

<code>～てから</code> on its own just orders two events. Adding
<code>というもの</code> turns the starting point into a **watershed**: the sentence
is no longer about sequence but about a lasting change that began there and has run
ever since. There is always a stretch of time behind it and always a difference
between before and after, and the speaker usually has feelings about that
difference.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | て-form + からというもの | 引(ひ)っ越(こ)す → 引(ひ)っ越(こ)してからというもの |
| Verb (る-verb) | て-form + からというもの | 辞(や)める → 辞(や)めてからというもの |
| Verb (する) | して + からというもの | 結婚(けっこん)する → 結婚(けっこん)してからというもの |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き)て + からというもの | 来(く)る → 来(き)てからというもの |
| With topic は | ～てからというものは | 越(こ)してからというものは |
| Time noun (literary) | noun + から + というもの | あの日(ひ) → あの日(ひ)からというもの |
| Second clause | a continuing state or changed habit | ずっと元気(げんき)だ／～ようになった |
| Not possible | dictionary form in front | ❌引(ひ)っ越(こ)すからというもの |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the second clause must describe **how things have been since**,
not a single thing that happened afterwards. ずっと, 毎日(まいにち),
～ようになった, ～なくなった, ～てばかりいる and ～ている are its natural
companions.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)し<b>てからというもの</b>、体調(たいちょう)がずっといい。
Ever since I moved, I've felt well.
</pre>

<pre>
禁煙(きんえん)し<b>てからというもの</b>、ご飯(はん)がおいしく感(かん)じられるようになった。
Since I gave up smoking, food has started tasting good again.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)が独立(どくりつ)し<b>てからというもの</b>、家(いえ)の中(なか)が急(きゅう)に広(ひろ)く感(かん)じる。
Ever since my son moved out, the house has felt suddenly large.
</pre>

<pre>
スマートフォンを持(も)っ<b>てからというもの</b>、本(ほん)を読(よ)む時間(じかん)がめっきり減(へ)った。
Since I got a smartphone, my reading time has dropped right off.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)が亡(な)くなっ<b>てからというもの</b>、祖父(そふ)はほとんど外(そと)に出(で)なくなった。
Since my grandmother died, my grandfather has hardly gone out at all.
</pre>

<pre>
あの映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>てからというもの</b>、彼(かれ)は毎週(まいしゅう)のように山(やま)へ通(かよ)っている。
Ever since he saw that film he's been going up into the mountains almost every week.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)が閉(と)じられ<b>てからというもの</b>、駅前(えきまえ)の商店街(しょうてんがい)はすっかりさびれてしまった。
Since the factory was shut, the shopping street by the station has gone completely quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が代(か)わっ<b>てからというもの</b>、残業(ざんぎょう)がほとんどなくなり、職場(しょくば)の空気(くうき)も明(あか)るくなった。
Since the manager changed, overtime has all but disappeared and the mood in the office has lifted.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **emphatic Japanese**, slightly weightier than everyday speech but not at
all literary in the archaic sense. It fits essays, letters, interviews, memoirs and
any spoken account where the speaker is looking back over a period and marking a
turning point. In a plain conversational report of a sequence it is far too heavy:
<code>朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べてからというもの、学校(がっこう)へ行(い)った</code>
is comic, because breakfast is not a watershed in anyone's life.

What <code>というもの</code> adds is **duration plus feeling**. It insists that the
interval is long enough to matter, and it invites the listener to notice that the
speaker is affected - relieved, nostalgic, resigned, worried. Because of that it
attaches naturally to losses and gains that reorganise a life: a death, a move, a
marriage, an illness, quitting something, a change of boss.

Two consequences follow. The second clause must be a **state or a habit**, not one
event: ❌<code>退院(たいいん)してからというもの、昨日(きのう)映画(えいが)を見(み)た</code>.
And the interval has to be **substantial** - months and years, not the twenty
minutes since lunch.

The は variant, <code>～てからというものは</code>, is very slightly more emphatic and
more common in older writing. Nothing else changes.

## Contrast

### ～てからというもの vs ～てから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>てから</code> puts two events in **order**.
<code>てからというもの</code> claims that everything **since** has been different.

</div>

<pre>
手(て)を洗(あら)っ<b>てから</b>食(た)べなさい。
Wash your hands before you eat.
</pre>

<pre>
一人暮(ひとりぐ)らしを始(はじ)め<b>てからというもの</b>、家事(かじ)の大変(たいへん)さが身(み)にしみている。
Since I started living on my own, I've really come to feel how much work a household is.
</pre>

The first cannot take <code>というもの</code>: it is an instruction about the next
sixty seconds, and there is no era being described. Turn the test round - if you can
insert "and ever since then" into the English, the longer form is available.

### ～てからというもの vs ～て以来(いらい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these overlap heavily and often swap freely.
<code>て以来(いらい)</code> is the **neutral, factual** marker of a starting point;
<code>てからというもの</code> adds **length and feeling**.

</div>

<pre>
卒業(そつぎょう)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、一度(いちど)も会(あ)っていない。
I haven't seen him once since we graduated.
</pre>

<pre>
卒業(そつぎょう)し<b>てからというもの</b>、あのころの仲間(なかま)のことばかり思(おも)い出(だ)す。
Ever since graduating, I find myself thinking of nothing but the friends I had then.
</pre>

Both sentences would survive the other form, so do not build a wall between them.
The differences that do hold: <code>以来(いらい)</code> attaches directly to a
**noun** as well (卒業(そつぎょう)以来(いらい),
去年(きょねん)以来(いらい)), which <code>てからというもの</code> cannot do; and
<code>以来(いらい)</code> is at home in a formal report, where
<code>てからというもの</code> would sound as though the writer had personal feelings
about the data.

### ～てからというもの vs ～を境(さかい)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を境(さかい)に</code> takes a **noun** and draws an
analytical **dividing line**: before it, one thing; after it, another.
<code>てからというもの</code> takes a **verb** and describes the era that followed.

</div>

<pre>
四十歳(よんじゅっさい)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、体力(たいりょく)が落(お)ち始(はじ)めた。
From forty onwards my stamina began to go.
</pre>

<pre>
四十歳(よんじゅっさい)になっ<b>てからというもの</b>、階段(かいだん)がつらくてしかたがない。
Ever since I turned forty, stairs have been a misery.
</pre>

<code>を境(さかい)に</code> is the sentence a report would use; it is cool, and it
often carries figures. <code>てからというもの</code> is the sentence a person would
use about their own life. Note the grammar too: 四十歳(よんじゅっさい) needs to
become a verb clause before the longer form can attach.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 退院(たいいん)し<b>てからというもの</b>、昨日(きのう)友(とも)だちに会(あ)った。 → ✅ 退院(たいいん)してから、昨日(きのう)友(とも)だちに会(あ)った。
  One event on one day. The pattern needs an ongoing state to describe.

- ❌ 昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べ<b>てからというもの</b>、眠(ねむ)い。 → ✅ 昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べてから、眠(ねむ)い。
  Two hours is not an era. The interval has to be long enough for a change of life
  to be visible.

- ❌ 東京(とうきょう)に来(く)る<b>からというもの</b>、忙(いそが)しい毎日(まいにち)が続(つづ)いている。 → ✅ 東京(とうきょう)に来(き)<b>てからというもの</b>、忙(いそが)しい毎日(まいにち)が続(つづ)いている。
  The base is the て-form. The dictionary form gives the unrelated "because" reading
  of から.

- ❌ 入社(にゅうしゃ)<b>してからというもの</b>、来月(らいげつ)三年(さんねん)になります。 → ✅ 入社(にゅうしゃ)して来月(らいげつ)で三年(さんねん)になります。
  Counting elapsed time is a different job. The pattern describes what life has been
  like, not how long it has been.

## Practice

**1.** 犬(いぬ)を飼(か)い始(はじ)め____、毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きるようになった。

飼(か)い始(はじ)め<b>てからというもの</b>

Getting the dog is the watershed, and the second clause is a changed habit that has
held ever since - marked by ようになった, one of the pattern's usual partners.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This is an instruction about the order of two actions in the next few minutes.

</details>

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 去年(きょねん)引(ひ)っ越(こ)してからというもの、一度(いちど)近所(きんじょ)の祭(まつ)りに行(い)きました。

(b) makes the job change a turning point in the speaker's life and lets the relief
show. It suits a conversation with a friend or a paragraph in a personal essay,
where the point is the contrast with how things used to be.

</details>

# ～を境に

**Meaning** · with that as the dividing line, things on one side are not like things on the other

境(さかい) is a border - the word in 国境(こっきょう) and
境目(さかいめ). <code>を境(さかい)に</code> draws that border across time (or,
less often, across a map) and asserts a contrast: before this point, one state of
affairs; after it, a different one. The pattern's whole job is the **before and
after**, so a sentence that does not change anything has no use for it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (event) | noun + を境(さかい)に | 卒業(そつぎょう) → 卒業(そつぎょう)を境(さかい)に |
| Noun (point in time) | noun + を境(さかい)に | 去年(きょねん)の春(はる) → 去年(きょねん)の春(はる)を境(さかい)に |
| Noun (place) | noun + を境(さかい)に | 川(かわ) → 川(かわ)を境(さかい)に |
| Longer variant | noun + を境(さかい)にして | 震災(しんさい)を境(さかい)にして |
| Formal variant | noun + を境(さかい)として | 1990年(ねん)を境(さかい)として |
| Verb clause | ～のを + を… → ～のを境(さかい)に | 入院(にゅういん)した → 入院(にゅういん)したのを境(さかい)に |
| Second clause | a change of state, usually past or ～ている | 減(へ)り続(つづ)けている |
| Not possible | verb directly + を境(さかい)に | ❌卒業(そつぎょう)するを境(さかい)に |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the spatial use is real and worth recognising:
<code>川(かわ)を境(さかい)に東西(とうざい)で文化(ぶんか)が違(ちが)う</code>,
"the culture differs east and west of the river". The logic is identical; only the
axis has changed from time to space.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
卒業(そつぎょう)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、二人(ふたり)は連絡(れんらく)を取(と)らなくなった。
After graduation the two of them stopped keeping in touch.
</pre>

<pre>
その事故(じこ)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、彼(かれ)の生活(せいかつ)は大(おお)きく変(か)わった。
That accident was the dividing line; his life was quite different afterwards.
</pre>

<pre>
川(かわ)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、北(きた)と南(みなみ)では方言(ほうげん)がずいぶん違(ちが)う。
The river marks the divide: the dialect north of it is quite unlike the one south of it.
</pre>

<pre>
入院(にゅういん)したの<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、父(ちち)はきっぱりと酒(さけ)をやめた。
Going into hospital was the turning point - my father gave up drinking outright.
</pre>

<pre>
七月(しちがつ)の大雨(おおあめ)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、この川(かわ)の流(なが)れはすっかり変(か)わってしまった。
Since the heavy rain in July, the course of this river has changed completely.
</pre>

<pre>
1990年(ねん)<b>を境(さかい)として</b>、この地域(ちいき)の人口(じんこう)は減(へ)り続(つづ)けている。
From 1990 onwards the population of this area has fallen year on year.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)の導入(どうにゅう)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、問(と)い合(あ)わせの件数(けんすう)は半分(はんぶん)以下(いか)に落(お)ちた。
Enquiries fell by more than half once the new system came in.
</pre>

<pre>
震災(しんさい)<b>を境(さかい)にして</b>、この町(まち)の人(ひと)たちの防災(ぼうさい)への意識(いしき)は大(おお)きく変(か)わった。
The earthquake was a watershed: attitudes to disaster preparedness in this town are not what they were.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>を境(さかい)に</code> is **neutral, mildly formal and completely modern**. It
belongs to news reports, company documents, academic writing and careful speech,
and it is exactly the phrase a presenter reaches for when a graph changes direction.
Nothing about it is literary or old-fashioned; it is simply a shade more considered
than <code>から</code>.

It is also **emotionally neutral**. Unlike
<code>てからというもの</code>, which lets the speaker's feelings show, this pattern
stands back and observes: the change may be a blessing, a disaster or a statistic,
and the sentence does not say which. That objectivity is why it goes so well with
numbers and dates.

Two things it needs. A **genuine contrast** - if the situation before and after is
the same, the border is imaginary and the sentence is odd. And a **point**, not a
period: 去年(きょねん)の春(はる) works, ここ十年(じゅうねん) does not,
because a decade has no edge to draw a line at.

It says nothing about anyone's intentions. When the subject deliberately used the
event as an occasion to act - "I took my fiftieth birthday as a chance to start
running" - the pattern you want is <code>を機(き)に</code>, not this one.

## Contrast

### ～を境(さかい)に vs ～を機(き)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を機(き)に</code> means somebody **seized the occasion**
and did something on purpose. <code>を境(さかい)に</code> only reports that things
became different from that point.

</div>

<pre>
定年(ていねん)<b>を機(き)に</b>、夫婦(ふうふ)で田舎(いなか)に移(うつ)り住(す)むことにした。
We decided to take my retirement as the moment to move to the countryside.
</pre>

<pre>
定年(ていねん)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、父(ちち)は急(きゅう)に老(ふ)け込(こ)んだ。
After he retired my father aged suddenly.
</pre>

Nobody chose to age, so the second sentence cannot take
<code>を機(き)に</code>. Going the other way, the first *could* take
<code>を境(さかい)に</code>, but it would stop presenting the move as a decision
and merely note that the couple's address changed. Volition is the whole difference.

### ～を境(さかい)に vs ～以来(いらい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>以来(いらい)</code> emphasises the **span** running from
then to now. <code>を境(さかい)に</code> emphasises the **contrast** between the
two sides of the line.

</div>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)<b>以来(いらい)</b>、この味(あじ)を守(まも)り続(つづ)けています。
We have kept this same flavour ever since the shop opened.
</pre>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、通(とお)りの人通(ひとどお)りが増(ふ)えた。
Since the shop opened, there have been noticeably more people on the street.
</pre>

The first sentence has no before-and-after at all - the point is continuity - so
<code>を境(さかい)に</code> would contradict it. That is the test: if what you are
stressing is that nothing has changed since, use 以来(いらい).

### ～を境(さかい)に vs ～を皮切(かわき)りに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を皮切(かわき)りに</code> means "starting with", and what
follows is a **series** of similar things. <code>を境(さかい)に</code> means
"from that point on", and what follows is a **different state**.

</div>

<pre>
大阪(おおさか)公演(こうえん)<b>を皮切(かわき)りに</b>、全国(ぜんこく)十(じゅっ)か所(しょ)を回(まわ)る。
Starting with the Osaka show, they will play ten venues nationwide.
</pre>

<pre>
大阪(おおさか)公演(こうえん)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、チケットの売(う)れ行(ゆ)きが変(か)わった。
The Osaka show was the turning point for ticket sales.
</pre>

In the first, the Osaka show is the first item on a list. In the second it is a
hinge, and what comes after is not more shows but a different situation. Only
<code>を皮切(かわき)りに</code> implies that similar events follow.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 卒業(そつぎょう)する<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、東京(とうきょう)を離(はな)れた。 → ✅ 卒業(そつぎょう)したの<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、東京(とうきょう)を離(はな)れた。
  A verb has to be nominalised with の first, or replaced by the noun
  卒業(そつぎょう) on its own.

- ❌ 明日(あした)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、毎日(まいにち)走(はし)ります。 → ✅ 明日(あした)から毎日(まいにち)走(はし)ります。（or これ<b>を機(き)に</b>）
  A resolution about your own behaviour is not an observed change. Either drop the
  pattern or switch to <code>を機(き)に</code>.

- ❌ この十年(じゅうねん)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、人口(じんこう)が減(へ)っている。 → ✅ この十年(じゅうねん)、人口(じんこう)が減(へ)り続(つづ)けている。
  A ten-year stretch is a period, not a boundary. The pattern needs a point you
  could mark on a line.

- ❌ 開業(かいぎょう)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、同(おな)じサービスを続(つづ)けている。 → ✅ 開業(かいぎょう)以来(いらい)、同(おな)じサービスを続(つづ)けている。
  Nothing changed, so there is no border. Continuity belongs to
  以来(いらい).

A single event acting as a hinge, with a clear difference before and after, and no

</details>

ことにした marks a deliberate decision, and taking an occasion as a reason to act is

</details>

A date acting as a turning point in a trend is the pattern's home ground, and the

**4.** Find and fix the error: 工場(こうじょう)が閉(し)まるを境(さかい)に、町(まち)の人口(じんこう)が減(へ)り始(はじ)めた。

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) 入学(にゅうがく)し<b>てからというもの</b>、息子(むすこ)は口数(くちかず)が減(へ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

notes that the boy on one side of it is not the boy on the other. It is cool, and it
would fit a teacher's report as easily as a parent's remark.

(b) is the parent's sentence. It stresses that the quietness has gone on ever since,
and it lets a note of worry or wistfulness through. The facts are the same; the
distance is not.

</details>

# ～を控えて

**Meaning** · with something big coming up shortly - and the present already shaped by it

控(ひか)える here means "to have waiting in the wings". The sentence names an
event that has not happened yet and then describes **how things are now because of
it**: the nerves, the preparations, the empty shelves, the quiet market. The event
casts a shadow backwards, and that shadow is what the sentence is actually about.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (event) | noun + を控(ひか)えて | 試験(しけん) → 試験(しけん)を控(ひか)えて |
| With an interval | noun + を + time + に控(ひか)えて | 出発(しゅっぱつ) → 出発(しゅっぱつ)を明日(あした)に控(ひか)えて |
| Emphatic | noun + を目前(もくぜん)に控(ひか)えて | 大会(たいかい)を目前(もくぜん)に控(ひか)えて |
| Modifying a noun | noun + を控(ひか)えた + noun | 試験(しけん)を控(ひか)えた受験生(じゅけんせい) |
| Written clause link | noun + を控(ひか)え、… | 選挙(せんきょ)を来月(らいげつ)に控(ひか)え、… |
| Spatial sense | noun + を控(ひか)えた + noun | 背後(はいご)に山(やま)を控(ひか)えた村(むら) |
| Not possible | verb directly + を控(ひか)えて | ❌出発(しゅっぱつ)するを控(ひか)えて |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>控(ひか)える</code> has two other everyday meanings that have
nothing to do with this pattern: "cut down on" (塩分(えんぶん)を控(ひか)える,
"go easy on salt") and "note down" (番号(ばんごう)を控(ひか)えておく). Context
separates them easily, but the first in particular catches learners out.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
試験(しけん)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、息子(むすこ)は毎晩(まいばん)遅(おそ)くまで机(つくえ)に向(む)かっている。
With the exam coming up, my son is at his desk late every night.
</pre>

<pre>
出発(しゅっぱつ)<b>を明日(あした)に控(ひか)えて</b>、荷物(にもつ)の準備(じゅんび)に追(お)われている。
With departure tomorrow, I'm rushed off my feet getting the luggage together.
</pre>

<pre>
結婚式(けっこんしき)<b>を控(ひか)えた</b>姉(あね)は、このところ落(お)ち着(つ)きがない。
My sister, with her wedding coming up, has been in a flap lately.
</pre>

<pre>
選挙(せんきょ)<b>を来月(らいげつ)に控(ひか)え</b>、各党(かくとう)は公約(こうやく)の発表(はっぴょう)を急(いそ)いでいる。
With the election a month away, the parties are hurrying out their manifestos.
</pre>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)<b>を一週間(いっしゅうかん)後(ご)に控(ひか)えた</b>店内(てんない)は、まだ棚(たな)も空(から)のままだった。
A week from opening, the shop was still all bare shelves inside.
</pre>

<pre>
大会(たいかい)<b>を目前(もくぜん)に控(ひか)えて</b>、選手(せんしゅ)たちの表情(ひょうじょう)は引(ひ)き締(し)まっていた。
With the tournament right in front of them, the players' faces had tightened.
</pre>

<pre>
背後(はいご)に山(やま)<b>を控(ひか)えた</b>この村(むら)は、冬(ふゆ)でも風(かぜ)がほとんど吹(ふ)き込(こ)まない。
With the mountains at its back, this village gets almost no wind even in winter.
</pre>

<pre>
決算(けっさん)発表(はっぴょう)<b>を週明(しゅうあ)けに控(ひか)え</b>、市場(しじょう)では様子見(ようすみ)の動(うご)きが続(つづ)いている。
Ahead of Monday's results announcement, the market is holding back and waiting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the phrasing of **newspaper leads and briefings**. Open a Japanese paper on
the morning of an election, a summit or a season opener and the first clause will
very often be <code>～を控(ひか)え、</code>. It is equally at home in a report, a
speech or a company notice, and it works in careful conversation too - a colleague
saying <code>決算(けっさん)を控(ひか)えて忙(いそが)しくて</code> sounds
entirely normal, just a little formal.

What the pattern supplies is **atmosphere**. The event is in the future, so nothing
has happened yet; the interest is in the tension, preparation or restraint visible
in the present. That is why the second clause is nearly always
<code>～ている</code>, a description of an ongoing condition, and why the pattern
suits nerves, deadlines, rehearsals and markets standing still.

Two requirements. The noun must name a **scheduled or firmly expected event** with a
place on the calendar: 試験(しけん), 開幕(かいまく), 出産(しゅっさん),
引(ひ)っ越(こ)し, 決算(けっさん). A mere possibility does not qualify -
❌<code>雨(あめ)を控(ひか)えて</code> should be
<code>雨(あめ)に備(そな)えて</code>. And the event must lie **ahead** of the
situation described; once it has happened, the pattern is unusable.

The clause-final <code>控(ひか)え、</code> without て is the newspaper's preferred
shape and is very slightly more formal. In speech, keep the て.

## Contrast

### ～を控(ひか)えて vs ～を前(まえ)に（して）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for a scheduled event these are **near-identical**.
<code>を前(まえ)に</code> is the wider word: it also covers standing physically in
front of something, and reacting emotionally to it.

</div>

<pre>
大自然(だいしぜん)<b>を前(まえ)に</b>、言葉(ことば)が出(で)なかった。
Face to face with all that nature, I had no words.
</pre>

<pre>
本番(ほんばん)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、楽屋(がくや)は静(しず)まり返(かえ)っていた。
With the performance about to start, the dressing room had gone silent.
</pre>

The first is a scene, not a schedule, so <code>を控(ひか)えて</code> is impossible.
In the second, <code>を前(まえ)に</code> would pass without any change of meaning.
The one thing <code>を控(ひか)えて</code> does better is take an interval:
<code>三日(みっか)後(ご)に控(ひか)えて</code> is idiomatic in a way that
<code>三日(みっか)後(ご)に前(まえ)に</code> is not even grammatical.

### ～を控(ひか)えて vs ～に向(む)けて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に向(む)けて</code> is **purposeful**: the action is aimed
at the coming event. <code>を控(ひか)えて</code> is **circumstantial**: this is
simply how things are with the event looming.

</div>

<pre>
開幕(かいまく)<b>に向(む)けて</b>、クラブは新(あたら)しい選手(せんしゅ)を三人(さんにん)補強(ほきょう)した。
Ahead of the season opener, the club brought in three new players.
</pre>

<pre>
開幕(かいまく)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、スタジアムの周(まわ)りは落(お)ち着(つ)かない雰囲気(ふんいき)だ。
With the season about to open, there's a restless feeling around the stadium.
</pre>

<code>に向(む)けて</code> answers "what are they doing about it?";
<code>を控(ひか)えて</code> answers "what is it like right now?". A tense dressing
room is not aimed at anything, and buying players is not merely a symptom - which is
why each sentence resists the other form.

### ～を控(ひか)えて vs ～を境(さかい)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - opposite directions in time.
<code>を控(ひか)えて</code> looks **forward** at an event that has not happened.
<code>を境(さかい)に</code> looks **back** at one that has, and marks the change it
caused.

</div>

<pre>
移転(いてん)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、事務所(じむしょ)は段(だん)ボールだらけだ。
With the move coming up, the office is buried in cardboard boxes.
</pre>

<pre>
移転(いてん)<b>を境(さかい)に</b>、来客(らいきゃく)の数(かず)が大(おお)きく増(ふ)えた。
Since the move, visitor numbers have risen sharply.
</pre>

The first describes the week before; the second compares before with after. Both
attach to the same noun, and swapping them changes not the register but the whole
timeline of the sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 出発(しゅっぱつ)する<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、準備(じゅんび)をしている。 → ✅ 出発(しゅっぱつ)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、準備(じゅんび)をしている。
  を needs a noun. Drop the する, or nominalise the clause some other way.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、無事(ぶじ)合格(ごうかく)した。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)が終(お)わり、無事(ぶじ)合格(ごうかく)した。
  The event has to still be ahead. Once it is over, there is nothing left waiting in
  the wings.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、窓(まど)に板(いた)を打(う)った。 → ✅ 台風(たいふう)に備(そな)えて、窓(まど)に板(いた)を打(う)った。
  A typhoon may or may not arrive. The pattern wants a scheduled event, not a risk;
  risks take <code>に備(そな)えて</code>.

- ❌ 医者(いしゃ)に塩分(えんぶん)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>と言(い)われ、三(さん)キロやせた。 → ✅ 医者(いしゃ)に塩分(えんぶん)を控(ひか)えるように言(い)われ、三(さん)キロやせた。
  Here 控(ひか)える is the ordinary verb "cut down on", not the pattern. Written
  as a bare て-form it reads as a garbled version of one or the other.

## Practice

**1.** 引(ひ)っ越(こ)し____、部屋(へや)は荷物(にもつ)でいっぱいになっている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

引(ひ)っ越(こ)し<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>

A dated event still ahead, and a present condition caused by it - the two halves the
pattern needs. The ～ている ending is typical: what is being described is the state
of the room right now.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 地震(じしん)（を控(ひか)えて・に備(そな)えて）、水(みず)と食料(しょくりょう)を用意(ようい)しておいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

地震(じしん)<b>に備(そな)えて</b>、水(みず)と食料(しょくりょう)を用意(ようい)しておいた。

An earthquake is a risk with no date, so it cannot be "waiting in the wings".
<code>を控(ひか)えて</code> requires an event that is actually scheduled to
happen.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in news style: "With the summit three days away, security around the venue has been stepped up."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

首脳(しゅのう)会議(かいぎ)<b>を三日(みっか)後(ご)に控(ひか)え</b>、会場(かいじょう)周辺(しゅうへん)の警備(けいび)が強化(きょうか)されている。

The interval slot (三日(みっか)後(ご)に) and the clause-final
控(ひか)え without て are both marks of newspaper style, and the passive
強化(きょうか)されている keeps the second half in the same register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 卒業(そつぎょう)式(しき)を控(ひか)えて、みんなで写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)ってから解散(かいさん)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 卒業(そつぎょう)式(しき)のあと、みんなで写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)ってから解散(かいさん)しました。

Taking photographs and going home are things that happened after the ceremony, so
the ceremony is no longer ahead. <code>を控(ひか)えて</code> can only describe the
period before an event.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 開幕(かいまく)<b>を控(ひか)えて</b>、チームは練習(れんしゅう)を重(かさ)ねている。
(b) 開幕(かいまく)<b>に向(む)けて</b>、チームは練習(れんしゅう)を重(かさ)ねている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) frames the training as part of the atmosphere of the weeks before the opening
day. The emphasis is on the situation - the season is nearly here, and this is what
things look like.

(b) frames the training as aimed at the opening day. The emphasis is on purpose: the
team is preparing *for* it, and every session is directed at that target. Both are
natural here, and the choice is one of angle rather than of fact.

</details>
