# N1 - Describing what something is like


---

# ～びる

**Meaning** · to take on the look of ～; to come to seem ～ - said of something that has *become* that way, not of someone pretending

`びる` is an old suffix that turns a noun into a verb meaning "come to look like
that noun". It is not a productive ending you can attach at will: modern Japanese
keeps a handful of these verbs, and <code>大人(おとな)びる</code> and
<code>古(ふる)びる</code> carry almost all the traffic.

## Formation

`びる` attaches to a **bare noun** - no particle, no `の` - and the result is a
**る-verb** (一段(いちだん) / ichidan / Group 2), conjugating exactly like 見(み)る.
The verb is **intransitive**: the thing described is the subject, and there is no
`を`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + びる (る-verb) | 大人(おとな) → 大人(おとな)びる |
| Modifying a noun | ～びた + Noun | 古(ふる)びる → 古(ふる)びた看板(かんばん) |
| Present state | ～びている | 大人(おとな)びている |
| Negative | ～びない | 古(ふる)びない |
| Polite | ～びます | 古(ふる)びます |
| Adverbially | ～びて + verb | 古(ふる)びて見(み)える |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the working list is short: <code>大人(おとな)びる</code> (look grown
up), <code>古(ふる)びる</code> (look old, worn), <code>ひなびる</code> (look
rustic, usually written in kana, nearly always as <code>ひなびた</code>), and the
much rarer <code>田舎(いなか)びる</code>. You cannot coin new ones -
❌<code>先生(せんせい)びる</code>, ❌<code>新(あたら)しびる</code>. For
anything outside the list, use っぽい, らしい or <code>～のようだ</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - because these verbs name a change that has already taken place, the
weight falls on ～びた and ～びている. The plain present is used mainly for
generalisations: <code>この素材(そざい)は古(ふる)びにくい</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)はすっかり<b>古(ふる)びて</b>しまった。
This dictionary has got thoroughly worn-looking.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は年(とし)のわりに<b>大人(おとな)びている</b>。
She seems grown up for her age.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)に<b>古(ふる)びた</b>喫茶店(きっさてん)が一軒(いっけん)だけ残(のこ)っている。
There is just one worn old coffee shop left in front of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の奥(おく)の<b>ひなびた</b>温泉(おんせん)に一泊(いっぱく)した。
We spent a night at a sleepy little hot spring deep in the mountains.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりに会(あ)った姪(めい)は、服(ふく)の好(この)みまで<b>大人(おとな)びて</b>いた。
My niece, whom I hadn't seen in ages, had grown up even in her taste in clothes.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)の万年筆(まんねんひつ)は使(つか)い込(こ)まれて<b>古(ふる)びて</b>いたが、書(か)き味(あじ)は少(すこ)しも落(お)ちていなかった。
My grandfather's fountain pen was worn with use, but it wrote as well as ever.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)から帰(かえ)ってきた息子(むすこ)は話(はな)し方(かた)まで<b>大人(おとな)びて</b>いて、うれしいような寂(さび)しいような気(き)がした。
My son came back from his year abroad grown up even in the way he talks, and I felt glad and wistful at once.
</pre>

<pre>
この商店街(しょうてんがい)は<b>古(ふる)びて</b>はいるが、夕方(ゆうがた)になると不思議(ふしぎ)なほど活気(かっき)が出(で)てくる。
The shopping street looks its age, but come evening it fills with an improbable amount of life.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`びる` is faintly literary but not difficult: <code>古(ふる)びる</code> in
particular is ordinary conversational Japanese when you are talking about objects
and buildings, and <code>大人(おとな)びる</code> is what a relative says about a
child they have not seen for a while. Neither sounds stiff.

The evaluation each word carries is fixed by the word, not by the suffix.
<code>大人(おとな)びる</code> is warm and mildly admiring, and is said almost
exclusively of children and young people - calling a colleague of forty
大人(おとな)びた would be odd, since being grown up is not news at that age.
<code>古(ふる)びる</code> is neutral to slightly affectionate; it describes the
patina of use, not the disgrace of neglect, and a shop can be described that way in
a guidebook without insult. <code>ひなびた</code> is travel-writing vocabulary and
is essentially always a compliment: a hot spring, an inn, a fishing village.

The one thing `びる` never carries is pretence. A 大人(おとな)びた
少年(しょうねん) really does come across as older than he is; nobody is putting
on an act. That distinction is the whole reason `ぶる` exists alongside it.

## Contrast

### ～びる vs ～ぶる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `びる` is what something has genuinely **become**. `ぶる` is
what someone is deliberately **putting on**, and it is a criticism.

</div>

<pre>
中学生(ちゅうがくせい)の息子(むすこ)は近頃(ちかごろ)すっかり<b>大人(おとな)びた</b>。
My son in middle school has become really quite grown up lately.
</pre>

<pre>
中学生(ちゅうがくせい)のくせに<b>大人(おとな)ぶって</b>コーヒーを飲(の)んでいる。
He's only in middle school and he's drinking coffee to look grown up.
</pre>

The two share a root but split on volition. The first is an observation about how
the boy now is; the second says he is performing, and the speaker is amused or
annoyed. Praise a child with `ぶる` and you have insulted them.

### ～びる vs ～めく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `びる` is about **age and appearance settling onto** a thing.
`めく` is about **signs of something starting to show**, classically the seasons.

</div>

<pre>
三月(さんがつ)に入(はい)って、ようやく<b>春(はる)めいて</b>きた。
Now that March is here, it is at last beginning to feel like spring.
</pre>

<pre>
柱(はしら)も畳(たたみ)もすっかり<b>古(ふる)びて</b>いた。
The pillars and the tatami had all gone thoroughly old and worn.
</pre>

They also differ mechanically, which is worth memorising alongside the meaning:
`びる` makes a る-verb (古(ふる)びた) and `めく` makes an う-verb
(春(はる)めいた, with the same く → いた change as 書(か)く). Mixing the two
up produces ❌<code>古(ふる)びいた</code> and ❌<code>春(はる)めた</code>.

### ～びる vs ～っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `びる` is a **verb** describing a change already completed.
`っぽい` is an **い-adjective** giving your casual impression, and it is far wider
and far more usable.

</div>

<pre>
その建物(たてもの)は<b>古(ふる)びて</b>いて、中(なか)に入(はい)るのがためらわれた。
The building was worn with age, and I hesitated to go in.
</pre>

<pre>
その建物(たてもの)はなんだか<b>安(やす)っぽくて</b>、中(なか)に入(はい)る気(き)になれなかった。
The building looked somehow cheap, and I couldn't bring myself to go in.
</pre>

`っぽい` attaches to almost anything and is squarely colloquial; `びる` attaches to
four or five nouns and is faintly bookish. Note also that `っぽい` about a person is
usually unkind (子供(こども)っぽい, 安(やす)っぽい), whereas
<code>大人(おとな)びた</code> is a compliment.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 大人(おとな)<b>びるな</b>顔(かお) → ✅ 大人(おとな)<b>びた</b>顔(かお)
  `びる` produces a verb, not a な-adjective. A verb modifies a noun in its plain
  form, and because the change is already complete the past ～びた is what you want.

- ❌ この家(いえ)を古(ふる)<b>びた</b>。 → ✅ この家(いえ)は古(ふる)<b>びた</b>。
  These verbs are intransitive. Nothing takes `を`; the thing that has aged is the
  subject.

- ❌ 子(こ)ども<b>びた</b>言(い)い方(かた) → ✅ 子(こ)ども<b>っぽい</b>言(い)い方(かた)
  `びる` is not productive. Outside the fixed handful of verbs, use `っぽい`.

- ❌ この看板(かんばん)は古(ふる)<b>びるだ</b>。 → ✅ この看板(かんばん)は古(ふる)<b>びている</b>。
  A verb does not take `だ`. The state resulting from the change is ～ている.

The handrail has darkened with years of use - a change that has already happened

second clause.

</details>

感心(かんしん)する means people were impressed, so the sentence has to be a compliment.
`ぶる` would accuse the child of putting on an act, which cannot be admired in the

</details>

"Worn old" describing a building is the natural home of <code>古(ふる)びた</code>.
Note the past form even though the shop is old right now: ～びた describes the state

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>大人(おとな)びる</code> is a verb, so it modifies 話(はな)し方(かた)
directly with no `な`. The `な` slipped in because the word feels adjective-like in
English.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の部屋(へや)の家具(かぐ)は<b>古(ふる)びて</b>いる。
(b) 彼(かれ)の部屋(へや)の家具(かぐ)は<b>古(ふる)い</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a plain fact about age: the furniture was made a long time ago. It could be
a well-kept antique in perfect condition.

(a) is about how it looks now - darkened, scuffed, softened by use. Something can
be 古(ふる)い without being 古(ふる)びている (a museum piece kept immaculate),
and cheap furniture can be 古(ふる)びる in five years without being
古(ふる)い at all.

</details>

# ～ぶる

**Meaning** · to act as though one were ～; to put on airs of being ～ - and the speaker is not impressed

`ぶる` attaches to a word naming a quality or a status and makes a verb meaning
"behave as if you had it". The pretence is the point. Unlike `びる`, which says
something has genuinely come to look a certain way, `ぶる` says a person is
performing, and the sentence containing it is almost always a criticism.

## Formation

`ぶる` produces an **う-verb** (五段(ごだん) / godan / Group 1), conjugating like
作(つく)る: 偉(えら)ぶる、偉(えら)ぶって、偉(えら)ぶった、偉(えら)ぶらない、偉(えら)ぶります.
The base is a bare noun or an adjective **stem** - never a full adjective, never a verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ぶる | 学者(がくしゃ) → 学者(がくしゃ)ぶる |
| な-adjective | stem + ぶる | 上品(じょうひん)だ → 上品(じょうひん)ぶる |
| い-adjective | drop い + ぶる | 偉(えら)い → 偉(えら)ぶる |
| Modifying a noun | ～ぶった + Noun | 学者(がくしゃ)ぶった口調(くちょう) |
| Modifying a verb | ～ぶって + Verb | 上品(じょうひん)ぶって笑(わら)う |
| Negative (often praise) | ～ぶらない | 偉(えら)ぶらない人(ひと) |
| Noun form | ～ぶり | 知(し)ったかぶり |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the members you will actually meet are
<code>偉(えら)ぶる</code>, <code>上品(じょうひん)ぶる</code>,
<code>大人(おとな)ぶる</code>, <code>学者(がくしゃ)ぶる</code>,
<code>いい子(こ)ぶる</code>, <code>悪(わる)ぶる</code>,
<code>通(つう)ぶる</code> (act the connoisseur) and <code>もったいぶる</code>
(make a great production of something). `ぶる` is more freely extendable than `びる`
- a fresh coinage like <code>先輩(せんぱい)ぶる</code> is understood at once -
but the base has to name a quality or a role, never an action.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの人(ひと)は少(すこ)しも<b>偉(えら)ぶらない</b>。
He doesn't put on airs in the slightest.
</pre>

<pre>
お客(きゃく)さんの前(まえ)だけ<b>上品(じょうひん)ぶる</b>のはやめてよ。
Don't go all refined just because we have guests.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もったいぶらないで</b>、早(はや)く結果(けっか)を教(おし)えてよ。
Stop drawing it out and just tell me the result.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は友達(ともだち)の前(まえ)で<b>大人(おとな)ぶって</b>、コーヒーをブラックで飲(の)んでいた。
My brother was acting grown up in front of his friends, drinking his coffee black.
</pre>

<pre>
知(し)らないことまで<b>知(し)ったかぶり</b>をするから、話(はなし)が信用(しんよう)されなくなる。
He claims to know things he doesn't, which is why people have stopped believing him.
</pre>

<pre>
入(はい)ったばかりの後輩(こうはい)に<b>先輩(せんぱい)ぶった</b>口(くち)をきくのはみっともない。
Talking down to a brand-new junior like some grand senior is unbecoming.
</pre>

<pre>
あの評論家(ひょうろんか)は専門(せんもん)外(がい)のことまで<b>学者(がくしゃ)ぶって</b>語(かた)るので、聞(き)いていて疲(つか)れる。
That critic holds forth like a scholar even on subjects outside his field, and it wears you out to listen.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は実力(じつりょく)があるのに<b>偉(えら)ぶった</b>ところが全(まった)くなく、部下(ぶか)によく慕(した)われている。
The section chief is genuinely good at the job yet has not a trace of self-importance about him, and his team is fond of him.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ぶる` is **evaluative before it is descriptive**. Saying someone
上品(じょうひん)ぶっている is not a neutral report that they behaved elegantly; it
says the elegance was a performance and you saw through it. There is no polite
register in which this becomes complimentary, so keep it away from anyone senior to
you. You cannot use it about yourself either, except as a joke against yourself -
<code>通(つう)ぶって注文(ちゅうもん)したら失敗(しっぱい)した</code>, "I
ordered like I knew what I was doing and got it wrong."

Colloquially it is very much alive. <code>いい子(こ)ぶる</code> is playground and
office-gossip vocabulary alike, and <code>もったいぶる</code> is what you say to a
friend who is enjoying withholding news. The derived noun
<code>知(し)ったかぶり</code> is far commoner than the verb it comes from, and
<code>ぶりっ子(こ)</code> - a girl or woman who performs cuteness - is a familiar,
somewhat dated colloquialism you should recognise but be careful about using.

The **negative** is where `ぶる` turns into praise, and it does so reliably:
<code>偉(えら)ぶらない</code>, <code>飾(かざ)らない</code> and
<code>気取(きど)らない</code> are the standard vocabulary for admiring someone
with status who does not trade on it. If you want one `ぶる` phrase you can safely
say out loud at work, that is the one.

## Contrast

### ～ぶる vs ～ふりをする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ふりをする</code> is a **one-off act of pretending**, and
it can attach to any verb. `ぶる` is a **standing habit of posturing** about a
quality or a role, and it always judges.

</div>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)が通(とお)ったので、忙(いそが)しい<b>ふりをした</b>。
The section chief came past, so I pretended to be busy.
</pre>

<pre>
あいつは新人(しんじん)の前(まえ)でいつも<b>偉(えら)ぶって</b>いる。
He's forever throwing his weight around in front of the new hires.
</pre>

The first is a single moment and carries no verdict on the speaker's character;
you can even say it about yourself with a shrug. The second describes what kind of
person he is. Note too that `ぶる` cannot take a verb: "pretend to be asleep" is
<code>寝(ね)たふりをする</code>, never ❌<code>寝(ね)ぶる</code>.

### ～ぶる vs ～がる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がる` reports the **outward signs of a feeling** somebody
really has. `ぶる` says the display is **manufactured**.

</div>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は新(あたら)しいゲームを<b>欲(ほ)しがって</b>いる。
My little sister has her heart set on the new game.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は興味(きょうみ)もないくせに<b>通(つう)ぶって</b>ワインの話(はなし)をする。
My sister talks about wine like a connoisseur when she isn't even interested.
</pre>

`がる` exists because Japanese does not let you state a third person's inner state
as fact, so it reports the visible evidence - it is a grammatical necessity, not a
criticism. `ぶる` is pure criticism. The two also attach differently: `がる` takes
adjectives of feeling (寒(さむ)がる, 怖(こわ)がる), `ぶる` takes qualities and
roles.

### ～ぶる vs ～びる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same shape, opposite verdict. `びる` = has genuinely **come to
look** that way. `ぶる` = is **pretending** to be that way.

</div>

<pre>
姉(あね)の服(ふく)を着(き)た娘(むすめ)は、いつもより<b>大人(おとな)びて</b>見(み)えた。
In her sister's clothes, my daughter looked older than usual.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)の服(ふく)を着(き)た娘(むすめ)は、鏡(かがみ)の前(まえ)で<b>大人(おとな)ぶって</b>いた。
In her sister's clothes, my daughter was posing at the mirror playing grown-up.
</pre>

They even conjugate differently, and the difference is easy to check:
<code>大人(おとな)びる</code> is a る-verb (大人(おとな)びない,
大人(おとな)びた) while <code>大人(おとな)ぶる</code> is an う-verb
(大人(おとな)ぶらない, 大人(おとな)ぶった). If the negative is ～ぶらない, the
sentence is about an act.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 偉(えら)<b>いぶる</b> → ✅ 偉(えら)<b>ぶる</b>
  An い-adjective loses its `い` before `ぶる`. The same goes for a な-adjective's
  `だ`: 上品(じょうひん)ぶる, not ❌上品(じょうひん)だぶる.

- ❌ 上品(じょうひん)<b>ぶるな</b>話(はな)し方(かた) → ✅ 上品(じょうひん)<b>ぶった</b>話(はな)し方(かた)
  `ぶる` gives a verb, so it modifies a noun with the plain past ～ぶった, not with
  `な`.

- ❌ 寝(ね)<b>ぶって</b>話(はなし)を聞(き)いていた。 → ✅ 寝(ね)た<b>ふりをして</b>話(はなし)を聞(き)いていた。
  `ぶる` never attaches to a verb. Faking an action is <code>ふりをする</code>.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)はいつも<b>立派(りっぱ)ぶって</b>いらっしゃいます。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)はいつも立派(りっぱ)になさっています。
  Wrapping `ぶる` in honorifics does not make it polite - it accuses your boss of
  faking. `ぶる` has no complimentary use except in the negative,
  <code>偉(えら)ぶらない</code>.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"I can't stand the way she plays the good girl."

`ぶる` is an う-verb. To modify 態度(たいど) it takes the plain past ～ぶった;
`な` belongs to な-adjectives and has nothing to attach to here.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は専門家(せんもんか)のように話(はな)す。
(b) 彼(かれ)は<b>専門家(せんもんか)ぶって</b>話(はな)す。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a comparison and is neutral, even complimentary: his explanations are as
clear and organised as a specialist's. Whether he is one is left open.

(b) says he is not one and is putting on the manner of one. The listener hears
irritation or mockery, and the sentence is not something you would say in front of
him.

</details>

# ～ぶり

**Meaning** · the way someone does something, as it looks to an onlooker; also, after an interval of ～

`ぶり` turns a verb or a noun into a **noun** naming the *manner* of the thing -
働(はたら)きぶり is not the work itself but the style and impression of the
working. A second, very common use attaches it to a length of time and means "for
the first time in that long": 三年(さんねん)ぶり.

## Formation

The result is always a **noun**. It takes が, を, に, and modifies with `の`; it is
never a な-adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | stem + ぶり | 働(はたら)く → 働(はたら)きぶり |
| Noun (an activity) | Noun + ぶり | 仕事(しごと) → 仕事(しごと)ぶり |
| Noun (an observed state) | Noun + ぶり | 混雑(こんざつ) → 混雑(こんざつ)ぶり |
| Whose manner it is | Person + の + ～ぶり | 彼(かれ)の話(はな)しぶり |
| Emphatic, spoken | ます-stem + っぷり | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)みっぷり |
| Time span, before a verb | Time + ぶりに | 三年(さんねん) → 三年(さんねん)ぶりに会(あ)う |
| Time span, before a noun | Time + ぶりの | 十年(じゅうねん) → 十年(じゅうねん)ぶりの再会(さいかい) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the manner sense is not fully productive. The stock members are
<code>仕事(しごと)ぶり</code>, <code>働(はたら)きぶり</code>,
<code>話(はな)しぶり</code>, <code>暮(く)らしぶり</code>,
<code>生活(せいかつ)ぶり</code>, plus a large journalistic family built on
nouns of state - <code>混雑(こんざつ)ぶり</code>,
<code>活躍(かつやく)ぶり</code>, <code>成長(せいちょう)ぶり</code>,
<code>徹底(てってい)ぶり</code>. If the base names something with no visible
manner to it, `ぶり` will not attach.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the time use needs an event that has **happened before and is happening
again**. <code>三年(さんねん)ぶりに優勝(ゆうしょう)した</code> works because
there was an earlier win. A first-ever or once-only event cannot take it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)の仕事(しごと)<b>ぶり</b>には本当(ほんとう)に感心(かんしん)した。
I was genuinely impressed by the way he works.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんの働(はたら)き<b>ぶり</b>は皆(みな)が認(みと)めている。
Everyone acknowledges how hard Tanaka works.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)し<b>ぶり</b>から、関西(かんさい)の人(ひと)だとすぐに分(わ)かった。
The way he spoke told me at once that he was from Kansai.
</pre>

<pre>
いい飲(の)み<b>っぷり</b>だね。見(み)ていて気持(きも)ちがいい。
That's a fine way you have of drinking - it's a pleasure to watch.
</pre>

<pre>
新聞(しんぶん)は会場(かいじょう)の混雑(こんざつ)<b>ぶり</b>を写真(しゃしん)入(い)りで伝(つた)えていた。
The paper reported just how packed the venue was, with photographs.
</pre>

<pre>
番組(ばんぐみ)は北欧(ほくおう)の人々(ひとびと)の暮(く)らし<b>ぶり</b>を丁寧(ていねい)に紹介(しょうかい)していた。
The programme gave a careful account of how people live in Scandinavia.
</pre>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)では、前(まえ)の職場(しょくば)での働(はたら)き<b>ぶり</b>を細(こま)かく聞(き)かれた。
At the interview I was asked in detail about how I had worked at my previous job.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)<b>ぶり</b>に開(ひら)かれた同窓会(どうそうかい)で、みんなの活躍(かつやく)<b>ぶり</b>に驚(おどろ)かされた。
At the reunion, held for the first time in ten years, I was struck by how well everyone was doing.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ぶり` is register-neutral and turns up everywhere from performance reviews to
travel documentaries. Its distinguishing feature is that it always implies an
**observer**: 仕事(しごと)ぶり is your work as it appears to someone watching it,
which is why the word lives in appraisal language
(<code>仕事(しごと)ぶりが評価(ひょうか)される</code>) and in reporting
(<code>徹底(てってい)ぶりが話題(わだい)になる</code>). You do not normally
describe your own manner with it, for the same reason you do not describe your own
charisma.

The suffix carries no evaluation of its own; the surrounding sentence supplies it.
見事(みごと)な働(はたら)きぶり is praise, ひどい慌(あわ)てぶり is not.

<code>っぷり</code> is the same suffix with a colloquial, appreciative kick to it.
<code>飲(の)みっぷり</code>, <code>食(た)べっぷり</code>,
<code>脱(ぬ)ぎっぷり</code>, <code>男(おとこ)っぷり</code> - all of them praise
someone for doing the thing wholeheartedly and without fuss. It belongs to speech
and to lively writing, and it would look out of place in a report.

The time use is the one you will hear most. <code>久(ひさ)しぶり</code> is its
frozen everyday form, and <code>お久(ひさ)しぶりです</code> is the standard
greeting to someone you have not seen for a while. Note that with an actual number
the phrase is quietly newsworthy - it is the shape headlines use for a drought
broken, a record equalled, a champion returning.

## Contrast

### ～ぶり vs ～方(かた)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～方(かた)</code> is the **method**, something that can
be taught and copied. `ぶり` is the **impression** the doing gives, which cannot.

</div>

<pre>
敬語(けいご)の使(つか)い<b>方(かた)</b>を教(おし)えてもらった。
I was taught how to use honorific language.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)の落(お)ち着(つ)いた話(はな)し<b>ぶり</b>に安心(あんしん)した。
Her calm way of speaking put me at ease.
</pre>

Swap them and both fail. ❌<code>話(はな)しぶりを教(おし)える</code> - you
cannot teach someone the impression they make. ❌<code>使(つか)い方(かた)に
感心(かんしん)する</code> is possible only if you mean the technique itself was
clever. The test: if you could write it in a manual, it is
<code>方(かた)</code>.

### ～ぶり - manner vs interval

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one suffix, two jobs, and the **base** tells you which. A verb
stem or an activity noun gives **manner**. A length of time gives **interval**.

</div>

<pre>
新聞(しんぶん)は市長(しちょう)の仕事(しごと)<b>ぶり</b>を厳(きび)しく批判(ひはん)した。
The paper was sharply critical of the mayor's performance in the job.
</pre>

<pre>
五年(ごねん)<b>ぶり</b>に富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)った。
I climbed Mt Fuji for the first time in five years.
</pre>

There is no ambiguity in practice, because the two senses take different bases and
different particles. Manner: 仕事(しごと)ぶり**が**評価(ひょうか)される,
話(はな)しぶり**を**見(み)る. Interval: 五年(ごねん)ぶり**に**
登(のぼ)る, 五年(ごねん)ぶり**の**優勝(ゆうしょう). The interval use also
requires the event to have happened before and be happening again, which is why
❌<code>卒業(そつぎょう)ぶり</code> fails - it names an event, not a span of time.

### ～ぶり vs ～ぶる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - two different words that happen to look alike. `ぶり` after a
verb stem or activity noun = **manner**, neutral. The `ぶり` that comes from the
verb `ぶる` = **posturing**, and it is an insult.

</div>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の働(はたら)き<b>ぶり</b>を部長(ぶちょう)が褒(ほ)めていた。
The department head was praising how the new hire works.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の知(し)ったか<b>ぶり</b>に部長(ぶちょう)があきれていた。
The department head was exasperated by the new hire pretending to know everything.
</pre>

You can tell them apart by the base. <code>働(はたら)き</code> is a verb stem, so
the noun means the manner of working. <code>知(し)ったか</code> is not a stem at
all - the word is built on the verb <code>知(し)ったかぶる</code>, and the whole
thing is a judgement about pretence. The same applies to
<code>上品(じょうひん)ぶり</code>, which means affected refinement, not
"refined manner".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 働(はたら)<b>くぶり</b> → ✅ 働(はたら)<b>きぶり</b>
  A verb joins `ぶり` at the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. 話(はな)す →
  話(はな)しぶり, 暮(く)らす → 暮(く)らしぶり.

- ❌ 久(ひさ)<b>しいぶり</b>ですね。 → ✅ お久(ひさ)<b>しぶり</b>ですね。
  The frozen greeting keeps the old stem 久(ひさ)し with no `い`, and takes the
  polite お in most situations.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)生(う)まれた子(こ)牛(うし)を一年(いちねん)<b>ぶり</b>に見(み)た。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)生(う)まれた子(こ)牛(うし)を一年(いちねん)後(ご)に見(み)た。
  `ぶり` needs a *repeat*. If there was no earlier occasion, the sentence has no gap
  to measure.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は<b>仕事(しごと)ぶりな</b>人(ひと)だ。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は<b>仕事(しごと)ぶり</b>のいい人(ひと)だ。
  `ぶり` gives a noun, not a な-adjective. Nouns modify with `の`, and the quality
  has to be stated separately.

# ～めく

**Meaning** · to begin to show signs of ～; to take on a hint of ～ - the quality is not fully there, it is showing through

`めく` attaches to a noun and makes a verb meaning "come to have something of that
about it". Two families use it constantly: the seasons
(春(はる)めく, 秋(あき)めく), where it marks the first signs of a change, and
abstract nouns (謎(なぞ)めく, 皮肉(ひにく)めく, 説教(せっきょう)めく), where
it says a remark or a situation has a **whiff** of that thing without quite being it.

## Formation

`めく` attaches to a **bare noun** and produces an **う-verb** (五段(ごだん) / godan /
Group 1). It conjugates exactly like 書(か)く, which means the past is ～めいた -
this is where most errors happen.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + めく (う-verb) | 春(はる) → 春(はる)めく |
| Modifying a noun | ～めいた + Noun | 謎(なぞ) → 謎(なぞ)めいた話(はなし) |
| Emerging change | ～めいてくる | 秋(あき)めいてくる |
| Present state | ～めいている | 皮肉(ひにく)めいている |
| Negative | ～めかない | 春(はる)めかない |
| Transitive partner | ～めかす / ～めかして | 冗談(じょうだん)めかして言(い)う |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `めく` is **intransitive**: the thing takes on the air by
itself. When *you* deliberately give your own words that colouring, use the
transitive partner `めかす`:
<code>冗談(じょうだん)めかして断(ことわ)る</code>, "turn it down in a
half-joking way".

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the productive bases are nouns, and only nouns with an atmosphere to
them: seasons, and abstract nouns like 謎(なぞ)・皮肉(ひにく)・冗談(じょうだん)・説教(せっきょう)・脅迫(きょうはく)・言(い)い訳(わけ).
Adjectives do not attach - ❌<code>楽(たの)しめく</code>. Verbs such as
きらめく, ざわめく and ときめく contain the same ending historically but are simply
ordinary vocabulary now; learn them as words.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
夕方(ゆうがた)の風(かぜ)が少(すこ)し<b>秋(あき)めいて</b>きた。
The evening wind has started to feel like autumn.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>謎(なぞ)めいた</b>微笑(ほほえ)みを浮(う)かべた。
She gave an enigmatic smile.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)の<b>皮肉(ひにく)めいた</b>一言(ひとこと)が耳(みみ)に残(のこ)っている。
That faintly sarcastic remark of my boss's is still ringing in my ears.
</pre>

<pre>
庭(にわ)の花(はな)が咲(さ)き始(はじ)め、急(きゅう)に<b>春(はる)めいて</b>きた。
The flowers in the garden have started to open, and it has suddenly turned springlike.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>冗談(じょうだん)めかして</b>言(い)ったが、目(め)は笑(わら)っていなかった。
He said it as though it were a joke, but his eyes weren't laughing.
</pre>

<pre>
忠告(ちゅうこく)のつもりだったのに、いつのまにか<b>説教(せっきょう)めいて</b>しまった。
I meant it as advice, but before I knew it, it had turned into a lecture.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)に<b>脅迫(きょうはく)めいた</b>電話(でんわ)がかかってきたため、警察(けいさつ)に届(とど)け出(で)た。
The company received a call verging on a threat, so we reported it to the police.
</pre>

<pre>
すっかり<b>春(はる)めいて</b>まいりましたが、いかがお過(す)ごしでしょうか。
Spring is well and truly in the air now - I hope this finds you well.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`めく` is **written and formal-literary**. Nobody says
<code>春(はる)めいてきたね</code> to a friend at the station; they say
<code>春(はる)らしくなってきたね</code>. Where the seasonal words genuinely live
is the opening line of a letter or an email -
<code>秋(あき)めいてまいりました</code> - and in weather reports and essays. Recognising
that greeting formula is worth more to most learners than being able to produce it.

The abstract-noun uses are a different matter and are genuinely useful. ～めいた
lets you attribute a quality to somebody's words while stopping short of accusing
them: <code>皮肉(ひにく)めいた言(い)い方(かた)</code> is "a remark with a
sarcastic edge", not "a sarcastic remark". That hedge is why the pattern appears so
often in careful writing and in news copy - <code>脅迫(きょうはく)めいた
手紙(てがみ)</code> reports what the letter was like without asserting the crime.

The evaluation usually leans **negative**, because the nouns that attach are mostly
things one would rather not be: 説教(せっきょう), 言(い)い訳(わけ),
脅迫(きょうはく), 皮肉(ひにく). <code>謎(なぞ)めいた</code> is the clear
exception and is often admiring, and the seasonal words are simply pleasant. That is
a tendency of the vocabulary, not a rule of the suffix.

## Contrast

### ～めく vs ～びる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `めく` = signs of a quality **starting to show**, especially
season and atmosphere. `びる` = something has **come to look** a certain age or
character, and it stays that way.

</div>

<pre>
山(やま)の木々(きぎ)が色(いろ)づき、一気(いっき)に<b>秋(あき)めいて</b>きた。
The trees on the hills have turned, and it has become autumnal all at once.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)の小屋(こや)は柱(はしら)まで<b>古(ふる)びて</b>いた。
The hut up the mountain was worn with age right down to its posts.
</pre>

The mechanical difference is worth learning with the meaning. `めく` gives an
**う-verb**: 春(はる)めく → 春(はる)めいた, 春(はる)めかない. `びる` gives a
**る-verb**: 古(ふる)びる → 古(ふる)びた, 古(ふる)びない. So
❌<code>春(はる)めた</code> and ❌<code>古(ふる)びいた</code> are both
impossible.

### ～めいた vs ～じみた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "smacking of". ～めいた is a **hint**, and can be
neutral or attractive. ～じみた is **heavy and hostile** - the thing reeks of it.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)はどこか<b>言(い)い訳(わけ)めいて</b>聞(き)こえた。
There was something of an excuse about the way he explained it.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は完全(かんぜん)に<b>言(い)い訳(わけ)じみて</b>いた。
His explanation was nothing but transparent excuse-making.
</pre>

The first leaves room for the explanation to be honest; the second does not. That
difference in weight is why ～めいた can attach to attractive nouns
(謎(なぞ)めいた) and ～じみた essentially cannot. They also conjugate differently:
めく is an う-verb, じみる a る-verb.

### ～めいた vs ～のような

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のような</code> compares one thing to **another thing**.
～めいた says the thing has **begun to carry the quality itself**.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の話(はなし)は<b>作(つく)り話(ばなし)のようだ</b>った。
His story was like something made up.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の話(はなし)はしだいに<b>説教(せっきょう)めいて</b>きた。
His talk gradually took on the tone of a lecture.
</pre>

<code>のよう</code> is a free comparison and attaches to anything, including whole
clauses. `めく` is a suffix on a short list of nouns, and it always implies a
**change in progress** - which is why it pairs so naturally with ～てくる.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 謎(なぞ)<b>めった</b>事件(じけん) → ✅ 謎(なぞ)<b>めいた</b>事件(じけん)
  `めく` is an う-verb ending in `く`, so the past follows 書(か)く → 書(か)いた:
  ～めいた. There is no small つ in it.

- ❌ 皮肉(ひにく)<b>めくな</b>口調(くちょう) → ✅ 皮肉(ひにく)<b>めいた</b>口調(くちょう)
  It is a verb, not a な-adjective, and the noun-modifying form of choice is the
  past ～めいた, because the quality has already emerged.

- ❌ 冗談(じょうだん)<b>めいて</b>断(ことわ)った。 → ✅ 冗談(じょうだん)<b>めかして</b>断(ことわ)った。
  `めく` is intransitive: the words take on the air by themselves. Deliberately
  giving your own words that colouring is the transitive `めかす`.

- ❌ 楽(たの)し<b>めいた</b>声(こえ) → ✅ 楽(たの)し<b>げな</b>声(こえ)
  `めく` attaches to nouns. For the visible surface of a feeling, `げ` or
  <code>そう</code> is the pattern you want.

## Practice

**1.** 同(おな)じ質問(しつもん)が三度目(さんどめ)になると、どうしても____口調(くちょう)になってしまう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>説教(せっきょう)めいた</b>口調(くちょう)

By the third time, the speaker's tone starts to carry a whiff of lecturing without
being an outright lecture - the hedge that ～めいた exists for. The noun-modifying
form is the past ～めいた.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)の返事(へんじ)には、どこか（皮肉(ひにく)めいた・皮肉(ひにく)じみた）ところがあった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

どこか<b>皮肉(ひにく)めいた</b>ところがあった。

どこか ("somehow, faintly") sets up a hint, and that is ～めいた. ～じみた is far
heavier and would clash with どこか - it asserts that the reply reeked of sarcasm.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The wind has turned cold, and it is beginning to feel like winter."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

風(かぜ)が冷(つめ)たくなり、すっかり<b>冬(ふゆ)めいて</b>きた。

The seasonal use, with ～てくる to carry "beginning to". Note that this belongs to
writing and to letters; in speech you would more likely say
<code>冬(ふゆ)らしくなってきた</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は冗談(じょうだん)めいて「辞(や)めようかな」と言(い)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は冗談(じょうだん)<b>めかして</b>「辞(や)めようかな」と言(い)った。
"Half-jokingly, he said, 'Maybe I'll just quit.'"

He is doing it on purpose, so the transitive `めかす` is required. `めく` would say
his words drifted into sounding like a joke of their own accord, which is not how
someone delivers a line.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は謎(なぞ)の多(おお)い人(ひと)だ。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>謎(なぞ)めいた</b>人(ひと)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a statement of fact about her: there are things about her nobody knows. It
could be said by someone who has checked and found gaps.

(b) is about the impression she gives - an air of mystery about the way she speaks
and carries herself. She may in fact have no secrets at all. ～めいた always reports
the surface, which is why it is compatible with being wrong about the substance.

</details>

# ～じみた

**Meaning** · reeking of ～; looking exactly like ～ - a hostile description, and literally, stained with ～

`じみた` is the past form of the verb `じみる`, which is 染(し)みる, "to soak in,
to stain", voiced after a noun. Both of its senses come straight out of that image.
Physically, <code>汗(あせ)じみたシャツ</code> is a shirt the sweat has soaked into
and discoloured. Figuratively, <code>子供(こども)じみた言(い)い訳(わけ)</code>
is an excuse with childishness soaked all the way through it - and the speaker is
not being kind.

## Formation

`じみる` attaches to a **bare noun** and produces a **る-verb** (一段(いちだん) / ichidan /
Group 2). In practice you will almost never meet the dictionary form: the quality
is already there, so the useful forms are ～じみた before a noun and ～じみている as a
predicate.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + じみる (る-verb) | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)じみる |
| Modifying a noun | ～じみた + Noun | 芝居(しばい) → 芝居(しばい)じみた態度(たいど) |
| As a predicate | ～じみている | その話(はなし)は狂気(きょうき)じみている |
| Negative | ～じみない | 所帯(しょたい)じみない |
| Becoming that way | ～じみてくる | だんだん所帯(しょたい)じみてくる |
| Literal stain | Noun (substance) + じみた | 油(あぶら) → 油(あぶら)じみた作業着(さぎょうぎ) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the figurative bases are nouns naming something undignified:
子供(こども)・芝居(しばい)・狂気(きょうき)・説教(せっきょう)・言(い)い訳(わけ)・年寄(としよ)り・所帯(しょたい).
The literal bases are substances that stain: 汗(あせ)・油(あぶら)・垢(あか).
It is usually written in kana; the kanji spelling 染(し)みた exists but is
uncommon in this suffixed use.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そんな<b>子供(こども)じみた</b>言(い)い訳(わけ)はやめなさい。
Stop making such childish excuses.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の怒(おこ)り方(かた)はどこか<b>芝居(しばい)じみて</b>いた。
There was something theatrical about the way he lost his temper.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)き出(だ)しの奥(おく)から、<b>汗(あせ)じみた</b>古(ふる)いシャツが出(で)てきた。
An old sweat-stained shirt turned up at the back of the drawer.
</pre>

<pre>
けんかの理由(りゆう)を聞(き)いてみると、あまりに<b>子供(こども)じみて</b>いて笑(わら)ってしまった。
When I asked what the fight had been about, it was so childish that I laughed.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は<b>油(あぶら)じみた</b>作業着(さぎょうぎ)のまま、台所(だいどころ)に入(はい)ってきた。
My father came into the kitchen still in his oil-stained work clothes.
</pre>

<pre>
善意(ぜんい)のつもりで始(はじ)めた助言(じょげん)が、いつのまにか<b>説教(せっきょう)じみた</b>ものになっていた。
Advice I had begun in good faith had turned at some point into something like a sermon.
</pre>

<pre>
その計画(けいかく)は<b>狂気(きょうき)じみて</b>いると誰(だれ)もが思(おも)っていたが、口(くち)に出(だ)す者(もの)はいなかった。
Everyone thought the plan verged on insane, but nobody said so out loud.
</pre>

<pre>
結婚(けっこん)して十年(じゅうねん)、話題(わだい)が<b>所帯(しょたい)じみて</b>きたと自分(じぶん)でも思(おも)う。
Ten years married, and I notice myself that our conversation has got domestic and drab.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`じみた` is **a judgement, not a description**, and the judgement is negative every
time. <code>子供(こども)じみた</code> does not mean "childlike" in any warm sense;
it means petty, immature, beneath the person. Because of that, it cannot be turned
into a compliment by choosing a flattering base noun -
❌<code>大人(おとな)じみた</code> is not how you praise a mature child; that is
<code>大人(おとな)びた</code>.

Register-wise it is ordinary Japanese, at home in both speech and writing.
<code>子供(こども)じみたことを言(い)うな</code> is something one adult really
says to another in an argument, and it stings. It is not literary in the way `めく`
is.

Two members need a warning. <code>狂気(きょうき)じみた</code> is strong language
about a plan or an act; applied to a person it is close to an accusation of
madness, so keep it for schemes and behaviour. <code>所帯(しょたい)じみる</code>
means "worn down and narrowed by domestic life", and it has a long history of being
aimed at married women in particular - it is unflattering enough that it is best
recognised rather than deployed, and safest of all used about yourself, as in the
last example above.

## Contrast

### ～じみた vs ～めいた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ～めいた = **a hint** of it, and can be neutral or even
attractive. ～じみた = **saturated** with it, and is always an attack.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の返事(へんじ)には<b>冗談(じょうだん)めいた</b>響(ひび)きがあった。
There was a half-joking note in his reply.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)での提案(ていあん)はほとんど<b>冗談(じょうだん)じみて</b>いた。
His proposal at the meeting was barely short of a joke.
</pre>

The first is a light observation; the second says the proposal could not be taken
seriously. Note also that <code>謎(なぞ)めいた女性(じょせい)</code> is a
compliment while ❌<code>謎(なぞ)じみた女性(じょせい)</code> does not work at
all - the heavy suffix will not take an attractive noun.

### ～じみた vs ～っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `っぽい` is a light, casual "-ish" that attaches to almost
anything. `じみた` attaches to a short list and delivers a verdict.

</div>

<pre>
その言(い)い方(かた)、ちょっと<b>子供(こども)っぽい</b>よ。
The way you put that is a bit childish.
</pre>

<pre>
大(だい)の大人(おとな)が順番(じゅんばん)で争(あらそ)うなんて<b>子供(こども)じみて</b>いる。
Grown adults squabbling over whose turn it is - it's infantile.
</pre>

`っぽい` is softenable (子供(こども)っぽくてかわいい is possible); `じみた` is
not. `っぽい` also has meanings `じみた` does not have at all: a tendency
(忘(わす)れっぽい, "forgetful") and a colour or material
(白(しろ)っぽい, "whitish").

### ～じみた vs ～まみれ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for the literal sense: `じみた` is a stain that has **soaked in
and dried**. `まみれ` is a substance **sitting on the surface right now**.

</div>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)が終(お)わり、全員(ぜんいん)が<b>汗(あせ)まみれ</b>でベンチに座(すわ)っていた。
Practice over, they were all sitting on the bench drenched in sweat.
</pre>

<pre>
ロッカーには去年(きょねん)の<b>汗(あせ)じみた</b>ユニフォームが残(のこ)っていた。
Last year's sweat-stained uniform was still in the locker.
</pre>

You wash `まみれ` off; `じみた` is what is left when you did not. This is also why
`まみれ` can be washed away in the next sentence, while a ～じみた garment is
usually described as being thrown out.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 子供(こども)<b>じみるな</b>態度(たいど) → ✅ 子供(こども)<b>じみた</b>態度(たいど)
  `じみる` is a verb. It modifies a noun in its plain past form, since the quality
  has already set in.

- ❌ 娘(むすめ)はすっかり大人(おとな)<b>じみて</b>きた。（as praise） → ✅ 娘(むすめ)はすっかり大人(おとな)<b>びて</b>きた。
  `じみた` cannot praise. To say a child is growing up nicely, use `びる`.

- ❌ 走(はし)ってきたので、シャツが汗(あせ)<b>じみて</b>いる。 → ✅ 走(はし)ってきたので、シャツが汗(あせ)<b>まみれだ</b>。
  Fresh sweat is on the surface, so it is `まみれ`. `じみた` is an old stain that has
  set into the cloth.

- ❌ 所帯(しょたい)<b>じみらない</b>暮(く)らし → ✅ 所帯(しょたい)<b>じみない</b>暮(く)らし
  `じみる` is a る-verb, not an う-verb: the negative simply drops `る`.

# ～まみれ

**Meaning** · smeared all over with ～; covered in ～ - a substance clinging to the whole surface

`まみれ` comes from the verb 塗(まみ)れる, "to be smeared with", and it keeps that
verb's physical picture: mud, sweat, blood, oil, dust - something that **sticks to
the surface** of a person or a thing and coats it. It is heavier and more vivid than
`だらけ`, and it takes only substances, never counted-up flaws.

## Formation

`まみれ` attaches to a **bare noun** - no particle in front of it - and the result is
itself a **noun**. It predicates with だ/です, modifies a following noun with `の`,
and pairs constantly with なる.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a substance) | Noun + まみれ | 泥(どろ) → 泥(どろ)まみれ |
| Predicate | ～まみれ + だ / です | 顔(かお)が汗(あせ)まみれだ |
| Before a noun | ～まみれ + の + Noun | 油(あぶら)まみれの手(て) |
| Becoming that way | ～まみれ + に + なる | ほこりまみれになる |
| Verb form | Noun + に + 塗(まみ)れる | 汗(あせ)にまみれて働(はたら)く |
| Figurative | Noun (engulfing) + まみれ | 借金(しゃっきん)まみれ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the base must be **stuff, not items**. 泥(どろ)・汗(あせ)・血(ち)・油(あぶら)・砂(すな)・ほこり are all natural.
Countable defects go to `だらけ` instead: 傷(きず)だらけ,
間違(まちが)いだらけ, 人(ひと)だらけ - never ❌間違(まちが)いまみれ.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kanji spelling is 塗(まみ)れ, but the suffix is written in kana in
almost all modern text. The verb 塗(まみ)れる takes `に` for the substance, not
`を`: <code>泥(どろ)にまみれる</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
転(ころ)んで、ズボンが<b>泥(どろ)まみれ</b>になった。
I fell over and my trousers ended up smeared with mud.
</pre>

<pre>
物置(ものおき)の箱(はこ)はどれも<b>ほこりまみれ</b>だった。
Every one of the boxes in the shed was thick with dust.
</pre>

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)を直(なお)していたら、手(て)が<b>油(あぶら)まみれ</b>になった。
I was fixing my bike and my hands got covered in oil.
</pre>

<pre>
強(つよ)い風(かぜ)の中(なか)を歩(ある)いてきたので、髪(かみ)も顔(かお)も<b>砂(すな)まみれ</b>だ。
I walked here through a gale, so my hair and face are covered in sand.
</pre>

<pre>
真夏(まなつ)の工事(こうじ)現場(げんば)で、作業員(さぎょういん)は皆(みな)<b>汗(あせ)まみれ</b>だった。
On the building site in high summer, every one of the workers was drenched in sweat.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)の最後(さいご)の場面(ばめん)で、主人公(しゅじんこう)は<b>血(ち)まみれ</b>のまま立(た)ち上(あ)がる。
In the final scene of the film, the hero gets to his feet still covered in blood.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は一生(いっしょう)、<b>泥(どろ)にまみれて</b>この田(た)を守(まも)ってきた。
My grandfather spent his whole life in the mud, keeping these rice fields going.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)はすでに<b>借金(しゃっきん)まみれ</b>で、再建(さいけん)はもう難(むずか)しいと言(い)われている。
The company is already drowning in debt, and people say it is past saving.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`まみれ` is vivid rather than formal, and it works in speech and writing alike -
though it is a shade more literary than `だらけ`, and a novel will reach for it where
a friend telling the same story might not. Its force comes from the picture: a whole
surface coated, unpleasantly and completely.

Usually that is a complaint, but not always, and the exception is worth knowing.
<code>汗(あせ)にまみれて働(はたら)く</code> - or
<code>泥(どろ)にまみれて</code> - is a set image of honest, unglamorous labour, and
it is said with respect. That admiring use belongs to the **verb** form with `に`
much more than to the bare noun; <code>汗(あせ)まみれだ</code> on its own is just a
description of being sweaty. `だらけ` has no equivalent warm use at all.

The figurative extension is limited and worth learning as vocabulary rather than
reasoning about: <code>借金(しゃっきん)まみれ</code> is the common one, with
<code>嘘(うそ)まみれ</code> and <code>欲(よく)まみれ</code> turning up in
writing. The image is still "coated in it, and it will not come off", which is why
these all describe a discreditable condition rather than a quantity.

## Contrast

### ～まみれ vs ～だらけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まみれ` is a **substance smeared over a surface**. `だらけ` is
**a great many of something**, and the things can be counted.

</div>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)された原稿(げんこう)は<b>間違(まちが)いだらけ</b>だった。
The manuscript that came in was riddled with mistakes.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)が終(お)わったとき、ユニフォームは<b>泥(どろ)まみれ</b>だった。
By the end of the match, the uniform was caked in mud.
</pre>

Where the base is a genuine substance, both are often possible -
泥(どろ)だらけ and 泥(どろ)まみれ are both good Japanese - and `まみれ` then
suggests a more even, thorough coating while `だらけ` suggests patches of it all
over. Where the base is countable, only `だらけ` works: ❌傷(きず)まみれ,
❌人(ひと)まみれ. And `だらけ` complains about quantity where `まみれ` complains
about the state of the surface.

### ～まみれ vs ～ずくめ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まみれ` = coated in a substance, and unwelcome. `ずくめ` =
**everything, without exception, is X** - and it is happy to be good news.

</div>

<pre>
今年(ことし)は昇進(しょうしん)に結婚(けっこん)に、<b>いいことずくめ</b>の一年(いちねん)だった。
A promotion and a wedding - this year has been nothing but good news.
</pre>

<pre>
草(くさ)を刈(か)り終(お)えたころには、腕(うで)も首(くび)も<b>汗(あせ)まみれ</b>だった。
By the time the grass was cut, my arms and neck were dripping with sweat.
</pre>

The bases do not overlap at all: `ずくめ` takes abstractions and colours
(いいこと, 黒(くろ), 規則(きそく)), `まみれ` takes physical stuff.
❌<code>いいことまみれ</code> and ❌<code>泥(どろ)ずくめ</code> are both
impossible.

### ～まみれ vs ～漬(づ)け

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まみれ` coats the **outside**. <code>漬(づ)け</code> is
**steeped in it**, immersed over a long stretch of time.

</div>

<pre>
受験生(じゅけんせい)の息子(むすこ)は毎日(まいにち)<b>勉強(べんきょう)漬(づ)け</b>だ。
My son is sitting exams, and his days are nothing but study.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)から戻(もど)ってきた父(ちち)の服(ふく)は<b>油(あぶら)まみれ</b>だった。
My father's clothes were covered in oil when he got back from the plant.
</pre>

<code>漬(づ)け</code> is the pickling verb 漬(つ)ける, and it keeps that sense
of being left to soak: <code>薬(くすり)漬(づ)け</code>,
<code>借金(しゃっきん)漬(づ)け</code>. Where both are possible - debt takes
either - `まみれ` pictures being covered in it and `漬け` pictures being submerged in
it for years.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 間違(まちが)い<b>まみれ</b>のレポート → ✅ 間違(まちが)い<b>だらけ</b>のレポート
  Mistakes are counted, not smeared. `まみれ` needs a substance.

- ❌ 泥(どろ)<b>まみれな</b>靴(くつ) → ✅ 泥(どろ)<b>まみれの</b>靴(くつ)
  `まみれ` produces a noun, and nouns modify with `の`. It is never a な-adjective.

- ❌ 汗(あせ)<b>をまみれて</b>走(はし)った。 → ✅ 汗(あせ)<b>にまみれて</b>走(はし)った。
  The verb 塗(まみ)れる is intransitive and marks the substance with `に`.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は<b>いいことまみれ</b>だった。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は<b>いいことずくめ</b>だった。
  `まみれ` cannot take an abstract noun, and it cannot report good news. For "nothing
  but good things", the word is `ずくめ`.

**1.** 雨(あめ)の中(なか)で試合(しあい)をしたので、ユニフォームが____になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>泥(どろ)まみれ</b>

Mud in the rain coats the whole kit, which is the core `まみれ` image, and the
change of state takes <code>になる</code>. 泥(どろ)だらけ would also pass here;
`まみれ` suggests the more thorough, evenly smeared version.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この中古(ちゅうこ)の机(つくえ)は安(やす)いが、表面(ひょうめん)が傷(きず)（だらけ・まみれ）だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

表面(ひょうめん)が傷(きず)<b>だらけ</b>だ。

Scratches are countable marks, not a substance coating the surface, so `だらけ` is
the only option. ❌傷(きず)まみれ is one of the commonest errors with this pair.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He came back from the garden with his hands covered in soil."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は手(て)を<b>土(つち)まみれ</b>にして庭(にわ)から戻(もど)ってきた。

Soil on the hands is a substance on a surface. Note the transitive framing
<code>～を…まみれにする</code> alongside the intransitive
<code>～が…まみれになる</code>; either is fine here.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 長(なが)い間(あいだ)使(つか)っていなかったので、棚(たな)の上(うえ)はほこりまみれな状態(じょうたい)だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 長(なが)い間(あいだ)使(つか)っていなかったので、棚(たな)の上(うえ)はほこりまみれ<b>の</b>状態(じょうたい)だった。

`まみれ` gives a noun, so it links to the following noun with `の`. The `な` comes
from treating it as a な-adjective, which it is not.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は<b>汗(あせ)まみれ</b>だった。
(b) 彼(かれ)は<b>汗(あせ)にまみれて</b>働(はたら)いていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply describes his condition: he was drenched, and depending on context that
could be unpleasant - someone you would rather not sit next to on the train.

(b) is admiring. The `に` verb form followed by a verb of doing is a set image of
honest physical labour, and the sentence praises his effort rather than reporting
his state. Almost all the warm uses of this word take that shape.

</details>

# ～ずくめ

**Meaning** · nothing but ～; ～ from top to bottom - everything in sight is that one thing

`ずくめ` says that a whole set - an outfit, a year, a schedule, a menu - consists of
one thing with nothing else in it. It is a **recognition item**. The suffix is
locked to a handful of fixed combinations, and a learner who invents a new one will
produce Japanese that no native speaker would write. Read it; say `ばかり`.

## Formation

`ずくめ` attaches to a **bare noun** and the result is a **noun**. It predicates
with だ/です and modifies a following noun with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ずくめ | 黒(くろ) → 黒(くろ)ずくめ |
| Predicate | ～ずくめ + だ / です | 今年(ことし)はいいことずくめだ |
| Before a noun | ～ずくめ + の + Noun | 黒(くろ)ずくめの男(おとこ) |
| Noun phrase as base | いいこと + ずくめ | いいことずくめ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the list is essentially closed. What you will meet is
<code>黒(くろ)ずくめ</code> (dressed head to foot in black),
<code>いいことずくめ</code> (nothing but good news),
<code>規則(きそく)ずくめ</code> (hedged about with rules),
<code>記録(きろく)ずくめ</code> (record after record),
<code>異例(いれい)ずくめ</code> (unprecedented in every respect) and
<code>ごちそうずくめ</code> (a table groaning with food). Outside these,
❌<code>雨(あめ)ずくめ</code> and ❌<code>問題(もんだい)ずくめ</code> are simply
not Japanese.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the base is a bare noun, so the colour word is 黒(くろ), not
黒(くろ)い: ❌<code>黒(くろ)いずくめ</code>. In kanji the suffix is written
尽(つ)くめ, but modern text spells it in kana almost without exception.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>黒(くろ)ずくめ</b>の男(おとこ)が門(もん)の前(まえ)に立(た)っていた。
A man dressed head to foot in black was standing at the gate.
</pre>

<pre>
昇進(しょうしん)に続(つづ)いて長男(ちょうなん)も生(う)まれ、去年(きょねん)は<b>いいことずくめ</b>だった。
A promotion, and then our first son - last year was nothing but good news.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい職場(しょくば)は<b>規則(きそく)ずくめ</b>で、息(いき)が詰(つ)まりそうだ。
The new workplace is hedged about with rules; it's suffocating.
</pre>

<pre>
事件(じけん)の夜(よる)、防犯(ぼうはん)カメラには<b>黒(くろ)ずくめ</b>の人物(じんぶつ)が写(うつ)っていた。
On the night of the incident, the security camera caught a figure dressed all in black.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)のチームは開幕(かいまく)から<b>記録(きろく)ずくめ</b>の快進撃(かいしんげき)を続(つづ)けている。
Since opening day the team has been on a tear, breaking record after record.
</pre>

<pre>
首相(しゅしょう)の訪問(ほうもん)は<b>異例(いれい)ずくめ</b>の日程(にってい)で行(おこな)われた。
The prime minister's visit was conducted on a schedule that broke with precedent at every turn.
</pre>

<pre>
叔母(おば)の家(いえ)の食卓(しょくたく)は<b>ごちそうずくめ</b>で、最後(さいご)まで食(た)べきれなかった。
My aunt's table was loaded with one feast dish after another, and I couldn't finish.
</pre>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は一見(いっけん)<b>いいことずくめ</b>に見(み)えるが、負担(ふたん)は将来(しょうらい)の世代(せだい)に回(まわ)されている。
On the face of it the scheme looks like nothing but good news, but the cost is being passed to future generations.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not build with it.** `ずくめ` is not archaic - you will meet
it in newspapers and sports writing (記録(きろく)ずくめ, 異例(いれい)ずくめ),
in novels and crime reporting (黒(くろ)ずくめの男(おとこ)), and in a couple of
everyday set phrases (いいことずくめ, 規則(きそく)ずくめ). What makes it a
recognition item is that it is welded to those combinations. The suffix looks
productive and is not, so the natural learner move - taking the meaning "nothing but"
and attaching it to a new noun - produces sentences that are wrong in a way no rule
will warn you about. ❌<code>雨(あめ)ずくめの一週間(いっしゅうかん)</code>,
❌<code>残業(ざんぎょう)ずくめ</code>, ❌<code>失敗(しっぱい)ずくめ</code>: all
of these are the sort of thing a reader stops at.

The ordinary modern equivalent is <code>ばかり</code>, and it will carry you through
every one of those cases: <code>今週(こんしゅう)は雨(あめ)ばかりだ</code>,
<code>残業(ざんぎょう)ばかりの毎日(まいにち)</code>. Where the complaint is
about quantity of defects, `だらけ` is the other everyday option.

Two phrases are safe to say out loud, because they are the fixed collocations
themselves. <code>いいことずくめ</code> is ordinary conversational Japanese, and it
is very often used sceptically - "it *looks* like nothing but good news" - as in the
last example above. <code>黒(くろ)ずくめ</code> is the standard way to describe
someone dressed entirely in black, and has no more literary flavour than the English.
Everything else on the list belongs to print.

Note finally that `ずくめ` is neutral about good and bad. That sets it apart from
most of its neighbours: 記録(きろく)ずくめ is triumphant,
規則(きそく)ずくめ is a complaint, and the suffix itself takes no side.

## Contrast

### ～ずくめ vs ～ばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this is the pair that matters. `ばかり` is the **modern,
productive, always-available** way to say "nothing but". `ずくめ` is the frozen
literary version, usable only in its set phrases.

</div>

<pre>
今週(こんしゅう)は雨(あめ)<b>ばかり</b>で、洗濯物(せんたくもの)が乾(かわ)かない。
It's been nothing but rain this week and the washing won't dry.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)は<b>いいことずくめ</b>の一年(いちねん)だった。
This year has been one of nothing but good news.
</pre>

Anything `ずくめ` can say, `ばかり` can also say, and `ばかり` attaches to verbs and
clauses as well as nouns (遊(あそ)んでばかりいる). The reverse is emphatically not
true. If you are composing rather than reading, use `ばかり` and reserve `ずくめ` for
the four or five phrases you have actually seen in print.

### ～ずくめ vs ～だらけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ずくめ` = the whole set **consists of** X, good or bad.
`だらけ` = there is **far too much** of X, and it is always a complaint.

</div>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)された書類(しょるい)は<b>間違(まちが)いだらけ</b>だった。
The documents submitted were riddled with errors.
</pre>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)初日(しょにち)は<b>記録(きろく)ずくめ</b>の一日(いちにち)となった。
Opening day turned into a day of records all round.
</pre>

`だらけ` cannot deliver good news - ❌<code>いいことだらけ</code> is odd where
<code>いいことずくめ</code> is idiomatic - and `ずくめ` cannot report scattered
defects. Note as well that `だらけ` is fully productive: if you want to complain
about a quantity of something, that is the pattern to reach for.

### ～ずくめ vs ～づくし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ずくめ` = everything is the **same one thing**.
<code>づくし</code> = a full **line-up of different kinds** of one category.

</div>

<pre>
夕食(ゆうしょく)は海(うみ)の幸(さち)<b>づくし</b>だった。
Dinner was a whole parade of seafood, one kind after another.
</pre>

<pre>
通夜(つや)の席(せき)は<b>黒(くろ)ずくめ</b>だった。
At the wake everyone was in black from head to foot.
</pre>

The two look and sound close and both come from 尽(つ)くす. The difference is
variety: <code>カニづくし</code> is a meal of crab prepared six different ways, while
黒(くろ)ずくめ is one colour repeated with no variation at all.
<code>づくし</code> is restaurant and menu vocabulary above all, and like `ずくめ` it
is best recognised rather than coined.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今週(こんしゅう)は雨(あめ)<b>ずくめ</b>だ。 → ✅ 今週(こんしゅう)は雨(あめ)<b>ばかり</b>だ。
  The suffix does not extend to new bases. This is the single most likely way to get
  `ずくめ` wrong, because the meaning fits perfectly and the Japanese does not.

- ❌ 黒(くろ)ずくめ<b>な</b>服装(ふくそう) → ✅ 黒(くろ)ずくめ<b>の</b>服装(ふくそう)
  `ずくめ` produces a noun, so it modifies with `の`.

- ❌ <b>黒(くろ)い</b>ずくめ → ✅ <b>黒(くろ)</b>ずくめ
  The base is a bare noun. The colour noun is 黒(くろ); the い-adjective
  黒(くろ)い cannot attach.

- ❌ 新(あたら)しい家(いえ)は問題(もんだい)<b>ずくめ</b>だ。 → ✅ 新(あたら)しい家(いえ)は問題(もんだい)<b>だらけ</b>だ。
  A pile of separate faults is `だらけ`. `ずくめ` would need everything about the
  house to be the same single thing.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet the phrase <code>黒(くろ)ずくめの男(おとこ)</code>, and what does it tell you about the man?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In narration - a novel, a news report of a crime, a witness statement. It says his
clothes were black from head to foot: coat, trousers, shoes, hat. It is a
description of an outfit, not of his character, though the phrase carries an
atmosphere of menace precisely because crime writing uses it so often.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 先月(せんげつ)は残業(ざんぎょう)ずくめだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先月(せんげつ)は残業(ざんぎょう)<b>ばかり</b>だった。

残業(ざんぎょう) is not one of the fixed bases, so `ずくめ` does not work here at
all. `ばかり` says the same thing and is available with any noun.

</details>

**3.** Choose: 引(ひ)っ越(こ)した部屋(へや)は壁(かべ)のしみや傷(きず)（ずくめ・だらけ）で、入居(にゅうきょ)前(まえ)に写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っておいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傷(きず)<b>だらけ</b>で

Stains and scratches are a quantity of separate defects, which is `だらけ`. `ずくめ`
would claim the room consisted of nothing but scratches, and 傷(きず) is not one
of its set bases in any case.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 開会式(かいかいしき)に現(あらわ)れた選手団(せんしゅだん)は白(しろ)いずくめな服装(ふくそう)だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 開会式(かいかいしき)に現(あらわ)れた選手団(せんしゅだん)は白(しろ)ずくめ<b>の</b>服装(ふくそう)だった。

Two repairs: the base is the bare colour noun 白(しろ), not the adjective
白(しろ)い, and the result is a noun, so it modifies 服装(ふくそう) with `の`.
Note that 白(しろ)ずくめ is far rarer than 黒(くろ)ずくめ - it is understood,
but in most sentences you would write
<code>全員(ぜんいん)白(しろ)い服(ふく)だった</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今年(ことし)は<b>いいことずくめ</b>だった。
(b) 今年(ことし)は<b>いいことばかり</b>だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The meaning is essentially the same, and both are natural Japanese - this is the one
context where `ずくめ` is genuinely idiomatic in speech.

(a) is the fixed phrase, and it has a slightly rounded, formulaic ring to it, the
sort of thing said in a year-end greeting. It also lends itself to scepticism:
<code>いいことずくめに見(み)えるが</code> is a standard opening for pointing out
the catch.

(b) is plainer and more directly personal. If you are unsure which to use anywhere
else, (b)'s pattern is the safe one; (a)'s is safe only in this particular phrase.

</details>

# ～ぐるみ

**Meaning** · the whole of ～ together; involving every member of ～ - a group acting as one body

`ぐるみ` attaches to a word naming a group of people and says that **all of it** is
in on the thing: not one member of the family but the family as a unit, not one
employee but the company. The image is of the whole lot being wrapped up together,
and the sentence usually goes on to say either how admirable that cooperation is or
how damning the complicity is.

## Formation

`ぐるみ` attaches to a **bare noun** and the result is a **noun**, which in practice
appears with `で` before a verb or `の` before a noun. The initial consonant is
always voiced: ぐるみ, never くるみ.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a group) | Noun + ぐるみ | 家族(かぞく) → 家族(かぞく)ぐるみ |
| Before a verb | ～ぐるみ + で | 町(まち)ぐるみで取(と)り組(く)む |
| Before a noun | ～ぐるみ + の + Noun | 組織(そしき)ぐるみの不正(ふせい) |
| Fixed expression | 身(み) + ぐるみ | 身(み)ぐるみはがされる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the base must name a **group**: 家族(かぞく)・町(まち)・村(むら)・地域(ちいき)・会社(かいしゃ)・組織(そしき)・業界(ぎょうかい)・国(くに).
Individuals do not attach - ❌<code>私(わたし)ぐるみ</code>,
❌<code>二人(ふたり)ぐるみ</code>. The one exception is the frozen
<code>身(み)ぐるみ</code>, "everything one has on", which survives almost only in
<code>身(み)ぐるみはがされる</code> (be stripped of everything).

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
隣(となり)の家(いえ)とは<b>家族(かぞく)ぐるみ</b>の付(つ)き合(あ)いだ。
We're friends with the family next door as families.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>町(まち)ぐるみ</b>で祭(まつ)りの準備(じゅんび)を進(すす)めている。
The whole town is working together on the preparations for the festival.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>地域(ちいき)ぐるみ</b>で子(こ)どもの登下校(とうげこう)を見守(みまも)っている。
The neighbourhood as a whole keeps an eye on the children going to and from school.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>会社(かいしゃ)ぐるみ</b>の不正(ふせい)だったことが、内部(ないぶ)告発(こくはつ)で明(あか)るみに出(で)た。
A whistleblower brought to light that the wrongdoing went right through the company.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>村(むら)ぐるみ</b>で、山(やま)の上(うえ)の古(ふる)い棚田(たなだ)を守(まも)り続(つづ)けている。
The village works as one to keep the old terraced rice fields on the hillside going.
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)、事故(じこ)の記録(きろく)を消(け)したのは<b>組織(そしき)ぐるみ</b>の隠蔽(いんぺい)だったと結論(けつろん)づけられた。
The inquiry concluded that the deletion of the accident records was a cover-up reaching through the whole organisation.
</pre>

<pre>
旅先(たびさき)で財布(さいふ)もかばんも盗(ぬす)まれ、<b>身(み)ぐるみ</b>はがされたようなものだった。
My wallet and my bag were both stolen while I was travelling - I was left with nothing but what I stood up in.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの頃(ころ)から<b>家族(かぞく)ぐるみ</b>で付(つ)き合(あ)ってきたので、彼(かれ)のご両親(りょうしん)のことも昔(むかし)からよく知(し)っている。
Our families have been friends since I was a child, so I've known his parents for years too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ぐるみ` is register-neutral and equally at home in conversation and in news copy.
What varies enormously is the **evaluation**, and it comes entirely from the noun in
front, not from the suffix.

The warm family: <code>家族(かぞく)ぐるみの付(つ)き合(あ)い</code>,
<code>地域(ちいき)ぐるみの見守(みまも)り</code>,
<code>町(まち)ぐるみの取(と)り組(く)み</code>. These praise a community for
acting together, and they are the standard vocabulary of local-government leaflets
and of ordinary friendly conversation.

The damning family: <code>会社(かいしゃ)ぐるみ</code>,
<code>組織(そしき)ぐるみ</code>, <code>業界(ぎょうかい)ぐるみ</code>. These are
so heavily associated with scandal reporting that they now carry the accusation on
their own. If you say <code>組織(そしき)ぐるみでした</code> in a meeting, everyone
in the room hears "and that is much worse than one rogue employee" - the phrase is
the difference between an individual's mistake and institutional guilt, which in
Japanese public life is exactly the line an investigation is trying to draw.

<code>身(み)ぐるみ</code> is a different animal: a frozen idiom, faintly
old-fashioned, and used almost only with はがす / はがされる, literally of robbery
and figuratively of losing everything in a business failure or a divorce.

## Contrast

### ～ぐるみ vs ～ごと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ぐるみ` is a **group of people** all taking part.
`ごと` is a **physical thing included**, eaten or carried along with what it is
attached to.

</div>

<pre>
りんごは皮(かわ)<b>ごと</b>食(た)べたほうが栄養(えいよう)がある。
Apples are more nutritious eaten skin and all.
</pre>

<pre>
その問題(もんだい)には地域(ちいき)<b>ぐるみ</b>で取(と)り組(く)む必要(ひつよう)がある。
That problem needs the whole area to tackle it together.
</pre>

Both translate as "and all", which is why they get swapped, but they are not
interchangeable in either direction. ❌<code>皮(かわ)ぐるみ食(た)べる</code> and
❌<code>地域(ちいき)ごと取(と)り組(く)む</code> are both wrong. The test: if
the "whole" is people who are choosing to act, it is `ぐるみ`; if it is a physical
object swallowed up along with something else, it is `ごと`.

### ～ぐるみ vs ～全体(ぜんたい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>全体(ぜんたい)</code> simply states the **extent** of
something. `ぐるみ` says every member is **actively involved**, and adds either
praise or blame.

</div>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)全体(ぜんたい)の売上(うりあげ)は去年(きょねん)より伸(の)びている。
Sales across the company as a whole are up on last year.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)<b>ぐるみ</b>で数字(すうじ)を操作(そうさ)していたことが分(わ)かった。
It emerged that the figures had been manipulated with the whole company in on it.
</pre>

The first is a measurement and is completely neutral. The second is an accusation.
This is why replacing 全体(ぜんたい) with `ぐるみ` in a report is never a purely
stylistic change - you have added complicity to the sentence.

### ～ぐるみ vs ～と一緒(いっしょ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と一緒(いっしょ)に</code> = X **accompanies** me on
this occasion. `ぐるみ` = the two **groups relate to each other as groups**, as a
standing arrangement.

</div>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は家族(かぞく)<b>と一緒(いっしょ)に</b>買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)った。
On Sunday I went shopping with my family.
</pre>

<pre>
あの夫婦(ふうふ)とは十年(じゅうねん)来(らい)、家族(かぞく)<b>ぐるみ</b>で付(つ)き合(あ)っている。
We've been friends with that couple as families for ten years now.
</pre>

The first is one outing; the family came along. The second describes a relationship
in which the children, the grandparents and everyone else are part of the connection.
`ぐるみ` is about the standing shape of things, which is why it so rarely appears in
a sentence about a single occasion.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 家族(かぞく)ぐるみ<b>に</b>旅行(りょこう)した。 → ✅ 家族(かぞく)ぐるみ<b>で</b>旅行(りょこう)した。
  Before a verb the particle is `で` - the group is the agent doing it together.

- ❌ みかんを皮(かわ)<b>ぐるみ</b>食(た)べる → ✅ みかんを皮(かわ)<b>ごと</b>食(た)べる
  `ぐるみ` needs a group of people. A skin included with the fruit is `ごと`.

- ❌ 町(まち)ぐるみ<b>な</b>運動(うんどう) → ✅ 町(まち)ぐるみ<b>の</b>運動(うんどう)
  The result is a noun and modifies with `の`, never `な`.

- ❌ 兄(あに)と二人(ふたり)<b>ぐるみ</b>で店(みせ)を始(はじ)めた。 → ✅ 兄(あに)と二人(ふたり)<b>で</b>店(みせ)を始(はじ)めた。
  Two named individuals are not a "whole group". `ぐるみ` needs a collective noun -
  a family, a town, an organisation.

## Practice

**1.** 空(あ)き家(や)の見回(みまわ)りは、自治会(じちかい)が中心(ちゅうしん)となって地域(ちいき)____行(おこな)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

地域(ちいき)<b>ぐるみで</b>

The neighbourhood association leads it but the whole area takes part, which is the
cooperative use of `ぐるみ`. Before a verb it takes `で`.

</details>

</details>

</details>

<code>村(むら)ぐるみ</code> is a noun, so it links to the following noun with

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 工場(こうじょう)<b>全体(ぜんたい)</b>で安全(あんぜん)対策(たいさく)を見直(みなお)した。
(b) 工場(こうじょう)<b>ぐるみ</b>で安全(あんぜん)対策(たいさく)を見直(みなお)した。

"across the whole plant", <code>全体(ぜんたい)</code> is the safer word.

</details>

# ～ながらに / ～ながらの

**Meaning** · in that same unchanged state; just as it was - keeping the condition named while something else happens

This is not the everyday `ながら` of 歩(ある)きながら話(はな)す. Here `ながら`
means "**staying in that state**": 涙(なみだ)ながらに is "with the tears still
running", 昔(むかし)ながらの is "the way it always was",
生(う)まれながらに is "from birth and unchanged since". The pattern survives in a
small set of fixed expressions, and `に` or `の` chooses whether it modifies a verb
or a noun.

## Formation

The base is a **bare noun** or a **verb ます-stem**. `～ながらに` is adverbial and
comes before a verb; `～ながらの` is attributive and comes before a noun. That single
choice is what learners get wrong.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, before a verb | Noun + ながらに | 涙(なみだ) → 涙(なみだ)ながらに語(かた)る |
| Noun, before a noun | Noun + ながらの | 昔(むかし) → 昔(むかし)ながらの味(あじ) |
| Verb (ます-stem), before a verb | stem + ながらに | 生(う)まれる → 生(う)まれながらに持(も)つ |
| Verb (ます-stem), before a noun | stem + ながらの | 生(う)まれる → 生(う)まれながらの才能(さいのう) |
| Emphatic | ～ながらにして | 居(い)る → 家(いえ)にいながらにして |
| Fixed adverbs | いつもながら / 我(われ)ながら | いつもながらの手際(てぎわ) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に` before a **verb**, `の` before a **noun**. So
<code>涙(なみだ)ながらに訴(うった)える</code> but
<code>涙(なみだ)ながらの訴(うった)え</code>; ❌<code>昔(むかし)ながらに
製法(せいほう)</code> is impossible.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the productive list is tiny: 涙(なみだ)ながら, 昔(むかし)ながら,
生(う)まれながら, いつもながら, 我(われ)ながら, and
<code>居(い)ながらにして</code>. This is not a pattern to coin with. Note also
that `ながら` after a noun of *status* - 子(こ)どもながらに,
素人(しろうと)ながら - is heard as the **concessive** "though only a child",
which is a different meaning; see the Contrast section.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>涙(なみだ)ながらに</b>当時(とうじ)のことを語(かた)った。
In tears, she told us about that time.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は<b>昔(むかし)ながらの</b>味(あじ)を守(まも)っている。
This shop has kept the same taste it always had.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)は<b>生(う)まれながらに</b>自由(じゆう)であり、平等(びょうどう)である。
People are free and equal from birth.
</pre>

<pre>
この蔵(くら)では、今(いま)も<b>昔(むかし)ながらの</b>製法(せいほう)で醤油(しょうゆ)を造(つく)っている。
At this brewery they still make soy sauce by the old methods.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いつもながらの</b>見事(みごと)な手際(てぎわ)に、今回(こんかい)も感心(かんしん)させられた。
His usual deft handling of it impressed me all over again.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)では家(いえ)に<b>いながらにして</b>世界中(せかいじゅう)の新聞(しんぶん)が読(よ)める。
These days you can read newspapers from all over the world without leaving your house.
</pre>

<pre>
被害者(ひがいしゃ)の母親(ははおや)は記者(きしゃ)会見(かいけん)で、<b>涙(なみだ)ながらに</b>再発(さいはつ)防止(ぼうし)を訴(うった)えた。
At the press conference the victim's mother appealed in tears for measures to stop it happening again.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)には<b>生(う)まれながらの</b>音楽(おんがく)の才能(さいのう)があると、指導(しどう)した誰(だれ)もが認(みと)めている。
Everyone who has taught him agrees that his musical gift was there from birth.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ながらに` and `ながらの` are **formulaic and literary**, and each of the surviving
expressions has a home register you should learn along with it.

<code>涙(なみだ)ながらに</code> belongs to reporting. It is what a newspaper or a
television report says about someone speaking through tears at a press conference or
in court, and it carries a certain formality; between friends you would say
<code>泣(な)きながら話(はな)してた</code>.

<code>昔(むかし)ながらの</code> is the language of **advertising and food
writing**: 昔(むかし)ながらの中華(ちゅうか)そば,
昔(むかし)ながらの製法(せいほう), 昔(むかし)ながらの町並(まちな)み. It is
always warm - unchanged means authentic, not out of date - and you will see it on
shop signs and packaging constantly.

<code>生(う)まれながら</code> is formal and slightly elevated, at home in writing
about rights, talent and temperament. <code>居(い)ながらにして</code> is a set
phrase meaning "without having to go anywhere", now used mostly about what technology
lets you do from your desk. And <code>我(われ)ながら</code> - "even I think so" -
is genuinely conversational: <code>我(われ)ながらうまくできた</code>.

None of this is old-fashioned in a way that would sound strange; it is simply
frozen. Recognise the members, use the ones whose register you have a feel for, and
do not extend the pattern to new nouns.

## Contrast

### ～ながらに vs ～ながら (two things at once)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the ordinary `ながら` joins **two simultaneous actions** and
takes no `に`. The `ながら` of this page describes an **unchanging state** and takes
`に` or `の`.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は音楽(おんがく)を<b>聞(き)きながら</b>宿題(しゅくだい)をしている。
He does his homework while listening to music.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>生(う)まれながらに</b>強(つよ)い意志(いし)を持(も)っていた。
She had a strong will in her from birth.
</pre>

Both attach to a ます-stem, which is why they get confused. The test is whether the
first element is an **action being performed at the same time**
(聞(き)きながら) or a **condition that simply holds**
(生(う)まれながら). Only the second takes `に`, and only the second can appear as
`の` before a noun.

### ～ながらに vs ～ながら(も) (although)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a word naming a **status or a quality**, `ながら` is read
as **"although"**. After 涙(なみだ), 昔(むかし) or a stem like
生(う)まれ, it is read as **"in that state"**.

</div>

<pre>
狭(せま)い部屋(へや)<b>ながらも</b>、日(ひ)がよく入(はい)って気持(きも)ちがいい。
It's a cramped room, but the light is good and it feels pleasant.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は<b>昔(むかし)ながらの</b>梅干(うめぼ)しを毎年(まいとし)漬(つ)けている。
My grandmother pickles plums the old way every year.
</pre>

The concessive reading is signalled by `も` and by the base being an adjective or a
status noun; it usually stands at a clause boundary with a comma after it. Watch out
for the middle ground: <code>子(こ)どもながらに気(き)を遣(つか)っていた</code>
means "young as I was, I was being careful", not "in an unchanged childlike state" -
the status noun pulls it towards "although".

### ～ながらの vs ～のまま

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のまま</code> is **fully productive** and means "left as
it is". <code>ながらの</code> is a **frozen phrase** with the same sense, available
only for its handful of nouns.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)して三年(さんねん)経(た)つが、荷物(にもつ)は箱(はこ)に入(はい)った<b>まま</b>だ。
Three years after moving, my things are still in their boxes.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)の裏(うら)には<b>昔(むかし)ながらの</b>商店街(しょうてんがい)が残(のこ)っている。
Behind the station there's an old-style shopping street still standing.
</pre>

<code>昔(むかし)のまま</code> and <code>昔(むかし)ながらの</code> are close, and
in many sentences either works. The difference is evaluative:
<code>のまま</code> is neutral and can be a complaint about something nobody has got
round to changing, while <code>昔(むかし)ながらの</code> nearly always compliments
the thing for having been preserved. Note too that <code>のまま</code> can end a
sentence (<code>昔(むかし)のままだ</code>) and `ながらの` cannot.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 涙(なみだ)ながら<b>の</b>語(かた)った → ✅ 涙(なみだ)ながら<b>に</b>語(かた)った
  `に` goes before a verb, `の` before a noun. The noun version would be
  <code>涙(なみだ)ながらの訴(うった)え</code>.

- ❌ 昔(むかし)ながら<b>に</b>製法(せいほう) → ✅ 昔(むかし)ながら<b>の</b>製法(せいほう)
  The same rule the other way round. 製法(せいほう) is a noun.

- ❌ コーヒーを飲(の)み<b>ながらに</b>新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)む。 → ✅ コーヒーを飲(の)み<b>ながら</b>新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)む。
  Two actions at the same time use the plain `ながら` with no `に`. The `に` belongs
  only to the fixed state expressions.

- ❌ この町(まち)の風景(ふうけい)は<b>昔(むかし)ながらだ</b>。 → ✅ この町(まち)の風景(ふうけい)は<b>昔(むかし)のままだ</b>。
  `ながらの` modifies a following noun; it does not work as a predicate. To end the
  sentence, use <code>のまま</code>.

# ～なりに / ～なりの

**Meaning** · in one's own way; in a way suited to what one is - not perfectly, but appropriately, given the limits

`なりに` grants credit inside a limit. <code>子(こ)どもなりに考(かんが)えた</code>
says the child thought about it as well as a child can - which is real thinking, and
also not an adult's. The concession and the credit come together and cannot be
separated, which is why this pattern is warm about children and beginners and
insulting about your boss.

## Formation

`なりに` is adverbial and comes before a verb; `なりの` is attributive and comes
before a noun. The base is a noun or a plain-form clause, and the classic shape
repeats the same word on both sides: X は X なりに.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, before a verb | Noun + なりに | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)なりに考(かんが)える |
| Noun, before a noun | Noun + なりの | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)なりのやり方(かた) |
| い-adjective | plain form + なりに | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいなりに |
| Verb | plain form + なりに | できない → できないなりに |
| The repeating frame | X は / なら + X + なりに | 下手(へた)なら下手(へた)なりに |
| Fixed | 自分(じぶん)なりに / それなりに | 自分(じぶん)なりに工夫(くふう)する |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun in front is nearly always a **person or a level**:
私(わたし), 自分(じぶん), 彼(かれ), 子(こ)ども, 新人(しんじん),
素人(しろうと), or a quality word standing for a level (下手(へた),
少(すく)ない, 忙(いそが)しい). It does not attach to things:
❌<code>この機械(きかい)なりに</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは<b>私(わたし)なりに</b>考(かんが)えて出(だ)した結論(けつろん)です。
This is the conclusion I reached, thinking it through in my own way.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもは<b>子(こ)どもなりに</b>親(おや)に気(き)を遣(つか)っている。
Children, in their own childlike way, do worry about their parents' feelings.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)には<b>彼(かれ)なりの</b>やり方(かた)があるので、口(くち)は出(だ)さないことにした。
He has his own way of doing things, so I decided to keep out of it.
</pre>

<pre>
ゴルフは下手(へた)だが、<b>下手(へた)なりに</b>毎週(まいしゅう)楽(たの)しんでいる。
I'm bad at golf, but bad as I am I enjoy it every week.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しいなら<b>忙(いそが)しいなりに</b>、時間(じかん)の使(つか)い方(かた)を工夫(くふう)すればいい。
If you're busy, then be busy - and find a way to use the time you have.
</pre>

<pre>
予算(よさん)が少(すく)ないなら、<b>少(すく)ないなりの</b>見(み)せ方(かた)があるはずだ。
If the budget is small, there must be a way of presenting it that suits a small budget.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の説明(せつめい)は完璧(かんぺき)ではなかったが、<b>彼(かれ)なりに</b>準備(じゅんび)してきたことはよく伝(つた)わった。
The new hire's presentation wasn't polished, but it was clear he had prepared as best he knew how.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)中(ちゅう)は言葉(ことば)で苦労(くろう)したが、<b>自分(じぶん)なりに</b>工夫(くふう)して友達(ともだち)を作(つく)っていった。
I struggled with the language while I was abroad, but I found my own ways of making friends.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`なりに` is register-neutral and very common in speech, but it is socially loaded,
and the loading is always the same: **it concedes a limitation while giving credit
inside it**.

That makes <code>私(わたし)なりに</code> and <code>自分(じぶん)なりに</code>
extremely useful, because applied to yourself the concession is modesty.
<code>自分(じぶん)なりに考(かんが)えてみました</code> is one of the standard
ways to present your own view in a Japanese workplace or a job interview: it offers
the idea while acknowledging you may be missing something, and it invites correction
without inviting contempt.

Applied to **other people** the same concession becomes a judgement about their
level. About a child, a beginner or a junior this is affectionate -
<code>新人(しんじん)なりによく頑張(がんば)っている</code> is praise. About
someone senior to you it is not. <code>部長(ぶちょう)なりに努力(どりょく)
なさったのでしょう</code> implies the department head did what little he could, and
it will land badly however politely it is packaged. The safe habit: use `なりに`
downwards and inwards, not upwards.

One structural point worth noticing. Because the pattern builds in "not perfect", it
cannot appear in a sentence claiming excellence. ❌<code>私(わたし)なりに
完璧(かんぺき)な仕事(しごと)をした</code> contradicts itself; drop `なりに` or
drop 完璧(かんぺき).

## Contrast

### ～なりに vs ～らしく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>らしく</code> = behaving as the **ideal** X would.
`なりに` = doing what an actual X, with an actual X's limits, **can**.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもは子(こ)ども<b>らしく</b>外(そと)で遊(あそ)んでいればいい。
Children should be out playing, the way children ought to.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもは子(こ)ども<b>なりに</b>将来(しょうらい)のことを心配(しんぱい)している。
Children worry about the future in their own way too.
</pre>

<code>らしく</code> appeals to a norm and can be used to tell someone off for
falling short of it - <code>学生(がくせい)らしくしなさい</code>. `なりに` makes no
appeal to a norm at all; it accepts the person as they are and measures the effort
against that. The two are close enough that the same X-は-X frame fits both, so the
question to ask is whether the sentence is about a **standard** or about **limits**.

### ～なりに vs ～ながらも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ながらも</code> = "although X, nevertheless Y" - the two
halves are **in tension**. `なりに` = "being X, Y follows in an X-shaped way" - the
two halves **fit together**.

</div>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しい<b>ながらも</b>、毎日(まいにち)三十分(さんじゅっぷん)は本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいる。
Busy as I am, I still read for half an hour every day.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しい<b>なりに</b>、通勤(つうきん)時間(じかん)を読書(どくしょ)に充(あ)てている。
Being busy, I've made the commute my reading time - it suits the life I've got.
</pre>

The first is defiance: the busyness works against the reading and the reading wins
anyway. The second is adaptation: the busyness shapes how the reading happens. The
two also take different bases where verbs are concerned:
<code>ながらも</code> takes the **ます-stem** (働(はたら)きながらも), while `なりに`
takes the **plain form** (働(はたら)けないなりに). With い-adjectives both keep the
full form, 忙(いそが)しい.

### ～なりに vs ～にとって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にとって</code> marks **whose point of view** something is
judged from. `なりに` marks **by whose means and within whose limits** something was
done.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>にとって</b>この決定(けってい)は受(う)け入(い)れがたいものだった。
For me, this decision was hard to accept.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>なりに</b>この決定(けってい)の理由(りゆう)を考(かんが)えてみた。
I've tried, in my own way, to work out the reasoning behind this decision.
</pre>

<code>にとって</code> attaches to an evaluation and cannot attach to a plain action:
❌<code>私(わたし)にとって考(かんが)えてみた</code>. `なりに` attaches to what
someone *did* and cannot introduce a judgement about how something feels to them:
❌<code>私(わたし)なりに難(むずか)しい</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>なりに</b>考(かんが)えがあります。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>なりの</b>考(かんが)えがあります。
  `の` before a noun, `に` before a verb. 考(かんが)え here is the noun "an idea".

- ❌ 忙(いそが)し<b>なりに</b>頑張(がんば)っている。 → ✅ 忙(いそが)し<b>いなりに</b>頑張(がんば)っている。
  An い-adjective keeps its `い` before `なり`, exactly as it does before `のに` or
  `ので`. There is no shortened stem in this pattern.

- ❌ 社長(しゃちょう)も<b>社長(しゃちょう)なりに</b>悩(なや)んでいらっしゃると思(おも)います。 → ✅ 社長(しゃちょう)も社長(しゃちょう)のお立場(たちば)で悩(なや)んでいらっしゃると思(おも)います。
  `なりに` concedes a limitation, so aiming it at someone senior sounds
  condescending no matter how polite the rest of the sentence is.

- ❌ 自分(じぶん)なりに<b>完璧(かんぺき)に</b>仕上(しあ)げました。 → ✅ 自分(じぶん)なりに丁寧(ていねい)に仕上(しあ)げました。
  "Perfectly" contradicts the built-in "within my limits". Pair `なりに` with an
  honest effort word, not with a claim of perfection.

## Practice

**1.** 専門(せんもん)外(がい)の分野(ぶんや)でしたが、____調(しら)べて報告(ほうこく)をまとめました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>自分(じぶん)なりに</b>（or <b>私(わたし)なりに</b>）

The speaker is admitting the subject was outside their field and claiming credit for
the effort anyway - the modest self-application that makes `なりに` so useful at
work. Before the verb 調(しら)べる it takes `に`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新人(しんじん)は新人(しんじん)（らしく・なりに）工夫(くふう)して、自分(じぶん)のやり方(かた)を見(み)つけた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新人(しんじん)は新人(しんじん)<b>なりに</b>工夫(くふう)して

The sentence credits him with finding a way that works at his level, which is
`なりに`. <code>新人(しんじん)らしく</code> would mean he behaved the way a new
employee is supposed to - deferential, willing - and would clash with
<code>自分(じぶん)のやり方(かた)</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The house is small, but we've furnished it in a way that suits a small house."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

家(いえ)は狭(せま)いが、<b>狭(せま)いなりの</b>家具(かぐ)の置(お)き方(かた)を工夫(くふう)した。

The X-なりの frame with a quality word standing for a level. Before the noun
置(お)き方(かた) the particle is `の`; had the sentence run
<code>狭(せま)いなりに工夫(くふう)した</code>, it would be `に`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)なりに一生(いっしょう)懸命(けんめい)やってくださって、感謝(かんしゃ)しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)が一生(いっしょう)懸命(けんめい)やってくださって、感謝(かんしゃ)しています。

Grammatically the original is fine, and that is the trap. `なりに` says "as far as
his abilities went", so thanking your manager with it manages to insult him in the
middle of the thanks. Simply drop it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 息子(むすこ)は<b>息子(むすこ)なりに</b>将来(しょうらい)を考(かんが)えているようだ。
(b) 息子(むすこ)は将来(しょうらい)を真剣(しんけん)に考(かんが)えているようだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a straight report: he is thinking about it seriously, full stop.

(a) adds a parent's double message. It grants that he is genuinely thinking - and at
the same time frames that thinking as a young person's, not yet the whole picture.
Said warmly it is affectionate; said flatly it can sound patronising, which is
exactly why the same sentence about an adult peer would be unwise.

</details>

# それなりに

**Meaning** · well enough, given what it is; in its own way - decent for the price, the effort or the circumstances

<code>それなりに</code> praises something while keeping the standard low on purpose.
It looks back at some condition already understood - the price, the time available,
the fact that it was a first attempt - and says the result was reasonable **measured
against that**. The reservation is always audible: nothing described this way is
outstanding.

## Usage

<code>それ</code> + `なり` + `に`. The `なり` is the same one as in
<code>自分(じぶん)なりに</code>, "in a way suited to what one is", and
<code>それ</code> points back at the circumstances rather than at a person. Three
shapes cover essentially all of its uses.

Before a verb or an adjective it is a plain adverb:
<code>それなりにおいしい</code>, <code>それなりに苦労(くろう)した</code>,
<code>それなりに役(やく)に立(た)つ</code>.

Before a noun the `に` becomes `の`: <code>それなりの理由(りゆう)</code> (a good
enough reason of its own), <code>それなりの覚悟(かくご)</code>,
<code>それなりの値段(ねだん)</code>. That last one is idiomatic and worth learning
whole - <code>いいものはそれなりの値段(ねだん)がする</code>, "good things cost
what good things cost".

It can also close a sentence as a predicate, <code>それなりだ</code>, and it can
stand alone as a complete answer: 「どうだった？」「まあ、それなりに。」

## Examples

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が安(やす)いので期待(きたい)していなかったが、<b>それなりに</b>おいしかった。
It was cheap so I wasn't expecting much, but it was decent enough.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に頼(たの)まれた仕事(しごと)だったが、<b>それなりに</b>形(かたち)にはなった。
It was a job I was landed with at short notice, but it came together well enough.
</pre>

<pre>
途中(とちゅう)で抜(ぬ)けるなら、<b>それなりの</b>理由(りゆう)を説明(せつめい)してください。
If you're leaving partway through, please explain a reason that justifies it.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ会社(かいしゃ)に十年(じゅうねん)もいれば、<b>それなりに</b>責任(せきにん)のある仕事(しごと)を任(まか)されるようになる。
Stay ten years at the same company and you do end up being given work with a certain amount of responsibility.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>それなりに</code> is ordinary spoken and written Japanese, neither formal nor
casual, and it is heard constantly. What it is *not* is neutral: the phrase is
**faint praise**, and Japanese listeners hear the reservation loudly.

That makes it a useful hedge about your own experiences -
<code>それなりに楽(たの)しかった</code> is an honest, unpretentious way to describe
a trip that was fine - and a slightly dangerous thing to say about other people's
work. 「先輩(せんぱい)の発表(はっぴょう)もそれなりによかったです」 is technically
a compliment and will not be received as one. About a person's abilities it is worse
still, for the same reason <code>なりに</code> is: it grants credit only within a
ceiling you have just imposed.

Used alone in reply - 「どうだった？」「それなりに」 - it is a mild, faintly evasive
"yeah, it was all right". Friends use it to close a topic they do not want to expand
on.

Two near neighbours are worth keeping straight. <code>まあまあ</code> is blunter and
more clearly lukewarm - it rates the thing on an absolute scale and finds it middling.
<code>ある程度(ていど)</code> measures a quantity ("to a certain extent") with no
evaluation attached at all: <code>ある程度(ていど)理解(りかい)できた</code>
reports how much you understood, while
<code>それなりに理解(りかい)できた</code> adds "which was reasonable, considering".
Only <code>それなりに</code> carries that "considering".

The <code>それなりの</code> forms are different in tone and quite safe. In
<code>それなりの理由(りゆう)</code> and
<code>それなりの覚悟(かくご)</code> the phrase means "of a level that the situation
demands", and it can be quite serious - a warning, even:
<code>続(つづ)けるならそれなりの覚悟(かくご)がいる</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>それなり</b>楽(たの)しかった。 → ✅ <b>それなりに</b>楽(たの)しかった。
  Before a verb or an adjective the `に` is required. Bare
  <code>それなり</code> only appears as a predicate,
  <code>味(あじ)はそれなりだ</code>.

- ❌ それなり<b>にの</b>準備(じゅんび)が必要(ひつよう)だ。 → ✅ それなり<b>の</b>準備(じゅんび)が必要(ひつよう)だ。
  `に` and `の` are alternatives, never stacked: `に` before a verb, `の` before a
  noun.

- ❌ この映画(えいが)は<b>それなりに</b>素晴(すば)らしかった。 → ✅ この映画(えいが)は本当(ほんとう)に素晴(すば)らしかった。
  <code>それなりに</code> caps the praise it modifies, so it cannot sit in front of a
  strong word of praise without contradicting it.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)のご説明(せつめい)で<b>それなりに</b>分(わ)かりました。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)のご説明(せつめい)でよく分(わ)かりました。
  Applied to what someone senior gave you, the built-in reservation reads as a
  complaint about their explanation.

## Practice

**1.** 初(はじ)めて作(つく)ったにしては、____おいしくできた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それなりに</b>おいしくできた

<code>にしては</code> sets up exactly the kind of lowered standard
<code>それなりに</code> measures against - good, for a first attempt. Before the
adjective phrase the particle is `に`.

</details>

**2.** Translate into Japanese: "If you're going to turn the offer down, you'll need a reason that stands up."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

断(ことわ)るのなら、<b>それなりの</b>理由(りゆう)が必要(ひつよう)だ。

Before the noun 理由(りゆう) the form is <code>それなりの</code>. Note that this
use is not faint praise at all - it means "of a standard the situation demands", and
here it is close to a warning.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 旅行(りょこう)は<b>それなりに</b>楽(たの)しかった。
(b) 旅行(りょこう)は楽(たの)しかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a simple, warm report: the trip was fun.

(a) builds in a comparison with something the listener is expected to know - the
weather was bad, it was cheap, half the group cancelled - and says that against
that, it was fine. It also quietly rules out enthusiasm: a listener who asks
「どうだった？」 and gets (a) will understand that it was not the trip of a lifetime,
and will usually not press further.

</details>

# ～嫌いがある

**Meaning** · has an unfortunate tendency to ~ - names a standing fault in a person, an organisation or a piece of work, and names it politely

Read <code>きらいがある</code>. The <code>嫌(きら)い</code> in it is not the everyday
"dislike": it is an older noun meaning a leaning or a bias, and the pattern reports
that leaning as a **defect**. This is the verdict of a book review, an editorial or a
performance appraisal - measured, indirect, and never a compliment.

## Formation

<code>きらい</code> is a noun, so whatever comes in front of it is in noun-modifying
shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain non-past) | ～る + きらいがある | 急(いそ)ぐ → 結論(けつろん)を急(いそ)ぐきらいがある |
| Verb (negative) | ～ない + きらいがある | 聞(き)かない → 人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)かないきらいがある |
| Verb + すぎる (the commonest shape) | ～すぎる + きらいがある | 頼(たよ)りすぎる → 機械(きかい)に頼(たよ)りすぎるきらいがある |
| い-adjective | ～い + きらいがある | 難(むずか)しい → 表現(ひょうげん)が難(むずか)しいきらいがある |
| な-adjective | ～な + きらいがある | 感情的(かんじょうてき)な → 感情的(かんじょうてき)なきらいがある |
| Noun | ～の + きらいがある | 独断(どくだん) → 独断(どくだん)のきらいがある |
| Polite | ～きらいがあります | 評価(ひょうか)が甘(あま)くなるきらいがあります |
| Softened, as it usually is | やや／少(すこ)し／どうも + … | やや慎重(しんちょう)すぎるきらいがある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - both spellings are current. Newspapers and formal prose often write it in
kana, <code>きらいがある</code>, precisely so that no reader trips over
<code>嫌(きら)い</code> and reads "dislike". The kanji
<code>嫌(きら)いがある</code> is equally correct.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は何事(なにごと)も一人(ひとり)で決(き)める<b>きらいがある</b>。
He has a tendency to decide everything on his own.
</pre>

<pre>
この説明(せつめい)は少(すこ)し長(なが)すぎる<b>きらいがある</b>。
This explanation has a tendency to run on too long.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の報告書(ほうこくしょ)は事実(じじつ)と意見(いけん)が混(ま)ざる<b>きらいがある</b>。
Reports from new staff have a tendency to mix fact up with opinion.
</pre>

<pre>
当社(とうしゃ)の会議(かいぎ)は結論(けつろん)を先(さき)に延(の)ばす<b>きらいがあります</b>。
Meetings at this company have a tendency to put off reaching a decision.
</pre>

<pre>
あの評論家(ひょうろんか)は新(あたら)しいものを何(なん)でも褒(ほ)める<b>嫌(きら)いがある</b>。
That critic has a tendency to praise anything so long as it is new.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)に自覚(じかく)はないが、部下(ぶか)の話(はなし)を最後(さいご)まで聞(き)かない<b>きらいがある</b>。
He is not aware of it himself, but he has a tendency not to hear his staff out.
</pre>

<pre>
この論文(ろんぶん)は都合(つごう)のよい例(れい)だけを選(えら)ぶ<b>きらいがあり</b>、結論(けつろん)を急(いそ)ぎすぎている。
This paper has a tendency to select only the convenient examples, and it rushes its conclusion.
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)に自信(じしん)のある人(ひと)ほど、基本(きほん)の点検(てんけん)を軽(かる)く見(み)る<b>嫌(きら)いがある</b>と言(い)われている。
It is said that the more confident an engineer is, the more they tend to make light of basic checks.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not reach for it.** It lives in written evaluation - book
and film reviews, newspaper editorials, academic criticism, personnel appraisals,
committee reports - and in the spoken register that borrows from them: a lecture, a
formal meeting, a broadcast commentary. In ordinary conversation it sounds like
someone reading out of a report. The everyday ways to say the same thing are
<code>～傾向(けいこう)がある</code>, `～がちだ`, <code>～すぎる</code> and
<code>～ところがある</code>, and a learner should be producing those.

Two hard restrictions come with it.

**The tendency must be a fault.** The pattern carries the criticism by itself, so
the content has to be something the speaker disapproves of. ❌
<code>彼(かれ)は人(ひと)を助(たす)けるきらいがある</code> is not a
compliment gone wrong - it is simply broken, because nothing about helping people
can be read as a defect. Where the English "tends to" is neutral,
<code>きらいがある</code> never is.

**The tendency must be habitual.** It describes a disposition that shows up again
and again, not one occasion. A single overlong meeting last Tuesday cannot have
<code>きらいがあった</code>; it was just long.

Almost every real example is hedged - やや, 少(すこ)し, どうも,
一部(いちぶ)に. That is not decoration. The pattern is how Japanese delivers
criticism without pointing: the fault is attributed to a leaning rather than to a
person's will, and the hedge lets the reader decide how hard to take it. Delivered
unhedged about a named individual it is quite cutting, in the way a flat sentence in
a written appraisal is cutting.

## Contrast

### ～きらいがある vs ～傾向(けいこう)がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>傾向(けいこう)がある</code> is the **neutral** word and
takes good tendencies, bad tendencies and statistics alike.
<code>きらいがある</code> is a **verdict** and only takes faults.

</div>

<pre>
近年(きんねん)、若者(わかもの)の読書(どくしょ)時間(じかん)は増(ふ)える<b>傾向(けいこう)にある</b>。
In recent years young people's reading time has been on an increasing trend.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の文章(ぶんしょう)は専門(せんもん)用語(ようご)に走(はし)る<b>きらいがある</b>。
His writing has a tendency to lapse into jargon.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence becomes an accusation against reading, while the
second loses its point: the writer is not reporting a trend, he is complaining. Note
also that <code>傾向(けいこう)</code> works for populations and numbers -
<code>増(ふ)える傾向(けいこう)にある</code>, <code>傾向(けいこう)が強(つよ)い</code> - where
<code>きらいがある</code> wants a person, an institution, or a piece of work with an
author behind it.

### ～きらいがある vs ～がち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～がち` says the thing **happens often**.
<code>きらいがある</code> says the subject **is inclined that way**, and adds that the
speaker thinks it is a flaw.

</div>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)、朝食(ちょうしょく)を抜(ぬ)き<b>がち</b>だ。
I've been skipping breakfast a lot lately.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの部署(ぶしょ)は前例(ぜんれい)を重(おも)んじすぎる<b>きらいがある</b>。
Our department has a tendency to put too much weight on precedent.
</pre>

`がち` is everyday speech, attaches to a ます-stem or a noun
(病気(びょうき)がち, 留守(るす)がち, 忘(わす)れがち), and describes frequency -
including your own, which is why 「遅(おく)れがちです」 is an ordinary thing to say
about yourself. <code>きらいがある</code> takes a whole clause, describes character
rather than frequency, and used about yourself sounds like a mock self-assessment.

### ～きらいがある vs ～おそれがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal <code>名詞(めいし)＋がある</code> frames, and
they are about different times. <code>きらいがある</code> = a **standing disposition**,
already visible. <code>おそれがある</code> = a **specific bad outcome** that might yet
happen.

</div>

<pre>
この台風(たいふう)は上陸(じょうりく)する<b>おそれがある</b>。
There is a risk that this typhoon will make landfall.
</pre>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は手続(てつづ)きが複雑(ふくざつ)になりすぎる<b>きらいがある</b>。
This system has a tendency to let its procedures get too complicated.
</pre>

Weather, accidents and side effects take <code>おそれがある</code>; they are events,
not character. Anything you could put in a written appraisal takes
<code>きらいがある</code>. The clearest test is the tense you could add: an
<code>おそれ</code> is about the future and can be averted, while a
<code>きらい</code> is already there in the record.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)になる<b>きらいがある</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)になる<b>おそれがある</b>。
  A weather forecast is a future event, not an ingrained failing. Nature has no
  disposition to be criticised.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は人(ひと)に優(やさ)しくする<b>きらいがある</b>。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は人(ひと)に優(やさ)しくする<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
  The content has to be a fault. With a virtue inside it, the sentence does not read
  as faint praise - it simply fails.

- ❌ 独断(どくだん)<b>きらいがある</b> → ✅ 独断(どくだん)<b>のきらいがある</b>
  <code>きらい</code> is a noun. A noun in front of it needs `の`, and a な-adjective
  needs `な`.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)すぎる<b>きらいがあった</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)すぎた。
  One meeting is one event. Use the pattern only where you could add
  <code>いつも</code> or <code>どの会議(かいぎ)も</code>.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)かない<b>きらいだ</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)かない<b>きらいがある</b>。
  The expression is fixed with <code>がある</code>. Ending in
  <code>きらいだ</code> reads as the ordinary adjective "dislikes", which turns the
  sentence into nonsense.

## Practice

# ～ところがある

**Meaning** · there is a ~ side to them; part of them is ~ - picks out one facet of a person's character without claiming it is the whole of them

<code>ところ</code> is "place", and that is exactly the image: somewhere in this
person there is a patch that behaves this way. The sentence describes a **streak**,
not a summary. That is why it works equally well for praise and for complaint, and
why it is the gentlest way in Japanese to say that someone is difficult.

## Usage

The clause in front is in plain, noun-modifying shape, because
<code>ところ</code> is a noun.

- Verb → plain form + <code>ところがある</code>: 思(おも)い込(こ)む → 思(おも)い込(こ)むところがある
- Verb (negative) → ～ない + <code>ところがある</code>: 謝(あやま)らない → なかなか謝(あやま)らないところがある
- い-adjective → ～い + <code>ところがある</code>: 厳(きび)しい → 厳(きび)しいところがある
- な-adjective → ～な + <code>ところがある</code>: 強引(ごういん)な → 強引(ごういん)なところがある
- Polite / past → <code>ところがあります</code>, <code>ところがあった</code>
- The person is usually marked with `には` or `は`: <code>彼(かれ)には～ところがある</code>

A **noun does not attach directly**. There is no ❌<code>子供(こども)のところがある</code>;
you turn the noun into a describing word first -
<code>子供(こども)っぽいところがある</code>,
<code>職人(しょくにん)のようなところがある</code>.

The pattern loves a hedge in front of it: どこか, 少(すこ)し, なんとなく,
妙(みょう)に. <code>どこか寂(さび)しそうなところがある</code> - "there is
something a bit lonely about him" - is a very typical shape. And because it isolates
one facet, it pairs naturally with a `が` clause that grants the opposite:
<code>厳(きび)しいが、優(やさ)しいところもある</code>. Swapping `が` for `も`
in <code>ところもある</code> makes that concession explicit and is extremely common.

## Examples

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は厳(きび)しいが、優(やさ)しい<b>ところがある</b>。
She is strict, but there is a kind side to her.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)には少(すこ)し強引(ごういん)な<b>ところがある</b>。
My older brother has a somewhat pushy streak.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は人前(ひとまえ)に出(で)ると急(きゅう)に無口(むくち)になる<b>ところがあります</b>。
My little sister has a way of going quiet the moment she is in front of people.
</pre>

<pre>
口(くち)は悪(わる)いが、後輩(こうはい)の失敗(しっぱい)を黙(だま)って引(ひ)き受(う)ける<b>ところがある</b>人(ひと)だ。
He has a sharp tongue, but there is a side to him that quietly takes the blame for his juniors.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very common in speech. It takes ます politely and needs no
adjustment for a formal setting, and it is as usable in a job interview as it is at
a dinner table.

Its real work is **limiting the claim**. Saying
<code>彼(かれ)は強引(ごういん)だ</code> declares the man pushy, full stop.
<code>彼(かれ)には強引(ごういん)なところがある</code> confines the pushiness to
one corner of him and leaves the rest untouched, which is why Japanese speakers reach
for it when they must say something unflattering about a colleague, a friend or a
family member in front of others. It is not a euphemism - the criticism lands - but
it is proportionate, and it invites the listener to hold the rest of the person in
reserve.

The same limiting works upward. <code>意外(いがい)と優(やさ)しいところがあるんですよ</code>
does not mean the person is kind; it means kindness is in there somewhere, which is a
warmer thing to say about a prickly person than a flat compliment would be.

Two habits worth copying. First, <code>ところもある</code> with `も` when you are
conceding a second side, and <code>ところがあって</code> when the streak explains
what follows: <code>人(ひと)に頼(たの)めないところがあって、いつも一人(ひとり)で残業(ざんぎょう)している</code>.
Second, self-description. <code>私(わたし)にもそういうところがあります</code> is
a natural, modest way to admit a fault about yourself - the same limiting move,
turned inward.

The subject is normally a person. Extending it to a book, a film or a company reads
as treating the thing as having a personality, which writers do deliberately
(<code>この映画(えいが)には説明(せつめい)しすぎるところがある</code>), but a
machine that malfunctions now and then takes
<code>～ことがある</code>, not <code>ところがある</code>.

## Contrast

### ～ところがある vs ～性格(せいかく)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>性格(せいかく)だ</code> defines the **whole person**.
<code>ところがある</code> reports **one patch** and says nothing about the rest.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は負(ま)けず嫌(ぎら)いな<b>性格(せいかく)だ</b>。
He is a competitive sort of person.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)には負(ま)けず嫌(ぎら)いな<b>ところがある</b>。
There is a competitive streak in him.
</pre>

The first is a character summary and would sit comfortably in an introduction. The
second is an observation, often prompted by one incident you have just witnessed. If
you have known someone a week, the second is the honest sentence.

### ～ところがある vs ～面(めん)がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>面(めん)がある</code> is the analytical word and applies to
**issues and situations** as readily as to people. <code>ところがある</code> is the
everyday word and is about **someone's character**.

</div>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)には法律(ほうりつ)だけでは解決(かいけつ)できない<b>面(めん)がある</b>。
There is an aspect of this problem that law alone cannot settle.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)には意外(いがい)と心配(しんぱい)性(しょう)な<b>ところがある</b>。
The section head has a surprisingly anxious side to him.
</pre>

Put <code>ところがある</code> on the first sentence and the problem starts sounding
like a person with moods. Put <code>面(めん)がある</code> on the second and it
turns into a report - fine in a written profile, oddly clinical over lunch.

### ～ところがある vs ～っぽい・～がち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `っぽい` and `がち` describe **how the person comes across or how
often something happens**. <code>ところがある</code> locates a trait **inside** them,
and only ever attaches to a full clause.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は怒(おこ)り<b>っぽい</b>。
He is quick to anger.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)には自分(じぶん)の失敗(しっぱい)を認(みと)めない<b>ところがある</b>。
There is a part of him that will not admit his own mistakes.
</pre>

`っぽい` is a suffix on a stem and gives you a blunt one-word label; `がち` counts
occasions. <code>ところがある</code> can carry a whole proposition - a condition, an
object, a negative - which is why the second sentence has no `っぽい` equivalent at
all. The trade-off is length: for "he's forgetful" nobody says
❌<code>忘(わす)れるところがある</code> when <code>忘(わす)れっぽい</code>
exists.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は正直(しょうじき)<b>ところがある</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は正直(しょうじき)<b>なところがある</b>。
  <code>ところ</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective keeps its `な`.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は学生(がくせい)の<b>ところがある</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はまだ学生(がくせい)っぽい<b>ところがある</b>。
  A bare noun cannot describe a facet of character. Make it an adjective first -
  `っぽい`, <code>のような</code>, `らしい` - and then attach.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)のいい<b>ところだ</b>。（meaning "she has a good side"） → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)にはいい<b>ところがある</b>。
  Without <code>がある</code> the sentence means "that is her good point" about
  something already mentioned - a different statement.

- ❌ このプリンターは急(きゅう)に止(と)まる<b>ところがある</b>。 → ✅ このプリンターは急(きゅう)に止(と)まる<b>ことがある</b>。
  Machines have behaviour, not character. <code>ところがある</code> here personifies
  the printer, which is comic rather than informative.

## Practice

**1.** 田中(たなか)さんは冗談(じょうだん)ばかり言(い)っているが、仕事(しごと)には驚(おどろ)くほど厳(きび)しい____がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

厳(きび)しい<b>ところ</b>がある

The sentence sets up a contrast with `が` and then grants one serious facet, which

change at all.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Either is grammatical, and they say different things.
<code>頑固(がんこ)な性格(せいかく)だ</code> makes stubbornness his defining trait,

<code>頑固(がんこ)なところがある</code> would limit the stubbornness to one corner

speaker would actually use in the office, because it criticises accurately without
writing anyone off, and it is easy to walk back if the listener disagrees.

</details>

# ともすれば

**Meaning** · apt to; prone to; all too easily - warns that people or things slide in a particular unwelcome direction unless something holds them back

<code>ともすれば</code> is frozen out of an old conditional - roughly "if it does even
that much" - and it has kept the conditional's flavour: given half a chance, this is
what happens. It announces a **drift**, usually a drift away from where the speaker
thinks we ought to be, and it is nearly always followed by `がちだ`, `やすい` or
`かねない` at the end of the sentence.

## Formation

<code>ともすれば</code> is an adverb. Nothing attaches to it; what it needs is a
**partner at the end of the clause**, and choosing that partner is the whole of the
grammar.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Clause ending in がちだ | ともすれば … ～がちだ | ともすれば感情的(かんじょうてき)になりがちだ |
| Clause ending in やすい | ともすれば … ～やすい | ともすれば見落(みお)とされやすい |
| Clause ending in かねない | ともすれば … ～かねない | ともすれば誤解(ごかい)を招(まね)きかねない |
| Clause ending in てしまう | ともすれば … ～てしまう | ともすれば後回(あとまわ)しにしてしまう |
| Placement | topic + は + ともすれば … | 専門家(せんもんか)はともすれば前提(ぜんてい)を疑(うたが)わなくなる |
| Variant, equally common | ともすると | ともすると忘(わす)れられがちだ |
| Variant, more literary | ややもすれば／ややもすると | ややもすれば独善(どくぜん)に陥(おちい)りやすい |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the adverb cannot carry the meaning on its own. A flat predicate after it
- <code>ともすれば忘(わす)れる</code> - leaves the sentence sounding unfinished,
because <code>ともすれば</code> has promised a tendency and only
`がちだ`, `やすい`, `かねない` or `てしまう` delivers one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
人(ひと)は<b>ともすれば</b>楽(らく)な道(みち)を選(えら)びがちだ。
People are all too apt to take the easy road.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しいと、<b>ともすれば</b>基本(きほん)を忘(わす)れやすい。
When we are busy, the basics are all too easily forgotten.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい技術(ぎじゅつ)をめぐる議論(ぎろん)は、<b>ともすれば</b>理想(りそう)論(ろん)に流(なが)れがちである。
Debate about new technology is apt to drift off into idealism.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)ばかり見(み)ていると、<b>ともすると</b>現場(げんば)の実情(じつじょう)を見落(みお)としてしまう。
Watch only the figures and you all too easily miss what is actually happening on the ground.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)対策(たいさく)は、事故(じこ)が起(お)きないかぎり<b>ともすれば</b>後回(あとまわ)しにされかねない。
As long as no accident occurs, safety measures are liable to keep being put off.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)の期待(きたい)は、<b>ともすれば</b>子(こ)への圧力(あつりょく)に変(か)わってしまう。
A parent's expectations turn all too easily into pressure on the child.
</pre>

<pre>
効率(こうりつ)を追(お)い求(もと)める組織(そしき)は、<b>ややもすれば</b>働(はたら)く人(ひと)の健康(けんこう)を軽(かる)く見(み)がちである。
An organisation in pursuit of efficiency is apt to make light of its workers' health.
</pre>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)どうしの議論(ぎろん)は、<b>ともすれば</b>一般(いっぱん)の人(ひと)に届(とど)かない言葉(ことば)になりやすい。
Discussion among specialists is apt to become language that never reaches the public.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise it; say something else.** <code>ともすれば</code> belongs to written
Japanese that lectures a little: newspaper editorials and columns, the preface to a
report, essays, ceremonial speeches, safety and quality guidance, textbook
commentary. Spoken, it turns up in a lecture or a formal address and almost nowhere
else. Dropped into conversation it sounds like someone quoting an editorial at you.
What a speaker actually says is `～がちだ`, `～やすい`,
<code>つい～してしまう</code>, or - if the point is a specific slip - うっかりすると.

Three properties are worth holding on to.

**The drift is undesirable.** Nobody writes
❌<code>ともすれば健康(けんこう)になりやすい</code>. The pattern exists to warn, so
the outcome must be one the writer would rather avoid.

**The subject is general.** 人(ひと)は, 我々(われわれ)は,
日本人(にほんじん)は, 組織(そしき)は, この種(しゅ)の議論(ぎろん)は - the
sentence is usually about a class, not about Yamada on Tuesday. Applied to one named
person, it becomes a rather grand way of complaining.

**It often includes the speaker.** Much of its work is moralising in the
first-person plural: *we* let this happen, and we should watch for it. That gentle
self-implication is a large part of why the pattern survives in speeches, where
saying "you people are careless" would be impossible.

Among the variants, <code>ともすると</code> is the least marked and reads most
naturally in modern prose; <code>ともすれば</code> is a shade more formal, and
<code>ややもすれば</code> and <code>ややもすると</code> are the most literary of the
four, with a slightly older ring. Treat that as a gradient, not a rule - all four are
alive, and writers use them interchangeably.

## Contrast

### ともすれば vs ～がちだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がちだ` is the **predicate** and can stand alone.
<code>ともすれば</code> is only an **adverb** and needs `がちだ` (or a relative) to
finish the job. If you want one of the two, want `がちだ`.

</div>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)、会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)くなり<b>がち</b>です。
Meetings have been running long lately.
</pre>

<pre>
議論(ぎろん)が白熱(はくねつ)すると、<b>ともすれば</b>本来(ほんらい)の目的(もくてき)を見失(みうしな)い<b>がち</b>だ。
Once a discussion gets heated, the original purpose is all too easily lost sight of.
</pre>

The first sentence is ordinary spoken Japanese; the second is written commentary,
and dropping <code>ともすれば</code> from it would cost nothing but the editorial
tone. That is the honest summary: <code>ともすれば</code> adds emphasis and register,
not information. Learners should produce the first shape and be able to read the
second.

### ともすれば vs ややもすれば・ともすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different weight.
<code>ともすると</code> ≦ <code>ともすれば</code> ≦ <code>ややもすれば</code> in
how bookish they sound.

</div>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は、<b>ともすると</b>形(かたち)だけのものになってしまう。
This system all too easily ends up being a formality and nothing more.
</pre>

<pre>
権力(けんりょく)を持(も)つ者(もの)は、<b>ややもすれば</b>批判(ひはん)を嫌(きら)うようになる。
Those who hold power become prone to disliking criticism.
</pre>

Nothing changes in the meaning if you swap them. The second sentence simply sounds
as though it comes from an older essay, and <code>ややもすれば</code> is where a
writer goes when the sentence is already moralising and wants to sound as if it has
history behind it.

### ともすれば vs うっかりすると・へたをすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are the **spoken** members of the family.
<code>うっかりすると</code> = if your attention slips, on a particular occasion.
<code>へたをすると</code> = if it goes badly, worst case.
<code>ともすれば</code> = as a general standing tendency.

</div>

<pre>
<b>うっかりすると</b>乗(の)り過(す)ごすから、寝(ね)ないでね。
If you're not careful you'll miss the stop, so don't fall asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ作業(さぎょう)が続(つづ)くと、<b>ともすれば</b>確認(かくにん)が甘(あま)くなりがちだ。
When the same task goes on and on, checks are all too apt to get sloppy.
</pre>

The first is advice to one person about one train; the second is a general claim
about repetitive work, of the kind that belongs in a safety briefing. This is the
pair to remember, because it is the swap a learner most needs: what you want in
conversation is almost always <code>うっかりすると</code> or
<code>へたをすると</code>, not <code>ともすれば</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>ともすれば</b>昨日(きのう)寝坊(ねぼう)した。 → ✅ 最近(さいきん)<b>ともすれば</b>寝坊(ねぼう)しがちだ。
  The pattern states a standing tendency, so it cannot report one completed event.

- ❌ <b>ともすれば</b>試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)しやすい。 → ✅ 準備(じゅんび)すれば試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)しやすい。
  The outcome has to be one the writer is warning against. With good news in it, the
  sentence reads as a warning about something desirable, which makes no sense.

- ❌ <b>ともすれば</b>人(ひと)は人(ひと)を見(み)た目(め)で判断(はんだん)する。 → ✅ <b>ともすれば</b>人(ひと)は人(ひと)を見(み)た目(め)で判断(はんだん)し<b>がちだ</b>。
  Finish the sentence with `がちだ`, `やすい` or `かねない`. Without one the
  adverb is left dangling.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が<b>ともすれば</b>、品質(ひんしつ)が落(お)ちる。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)に追(お)われると、<b>ともすれば</b>品質(ひんしつ)が落(お)ちる。
  Although it ends in `～ば`, it is a fixed adverb, not a live conditional. Nothing
  can be conditioned by it, and no clause attaches in front of it.

- ❌ ごめん、<b>ともすれば</b>返事(へんじ)を忘(わす)れちゃうんだ。 → ✅ ごめん、つい返事(へんじ)を忘(わす)れちゃうんだ。
  Apologising to a friend in editorial register is its own kind of comedy. In casual
  speech use <code>つい～してしまう</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 遠(とお)くの災害(さいがい)は、報道(ほうどう)が減(へ)ると<b>ともすれば</b>忘(わす)れられ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

忘(わす)れられ<b>がちだ</b>（or 忘(わす)れられ<b>やすい</b>）

<code>ともすれば</code> has to be answered by a tendency ending. Both
<code>がちだ</code> and <code>やすい</code> work here; the passive
<code>忘(わす)れられる</code> keeps the sentence impersonal, which suits the
editorial register the adverb brings with it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 夜道(よみち)は暗(くら)いから、（ともすれば・うっかりすると）段差(だんさ)に気(き)づかないよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（うっかりすると）段差(だんさ)に気(き)づかないよ。

This is one person being warned about one dark path, and the `よ` ending marks it as
speech. <code>うっかりすると</code> is the spoken fit.
<code>ともすれば</code> generalises about human nature and would sound like a public
safety notice delivered to a friend.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary spoken Japanese: 在宅(ざいたく)勤務(きんむ)では、<b>ともすれば</b>生活(せいかつ)と仕事(しごと)の区別(くべつ)が曖昧(あいまい)になりがちである。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

家(いえ)で働(はたら)いていると、仕事(しごと)とプライベートの区別(くべつ)がつかなくなりがちなんだよね。

Three things change together: the adverb goes, `である` becomes a spoken ending, and
the vocabulary drops a register. `がちだ` alone carries the whole meaning - which is
the point of the exercise, since it shows how little
<code>ともすれば</code> was contributing beyond tone.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新人(しんじん)の田中(たなか)さんはともすれば昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)で緊張(きんちょう)してしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新人(しんじん)の田中(たなか)さんは、昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)で緊張(きんちょう)してしまったようだ。
Or, if you want the tendency: ✅ 新人(しんじん)のうちは、ともすれば会議(かいぎ)で緊張(きんちょう)してしまいがちだ。

Two problems. The sentence describes one meeting yesterday, and it describes one
named person - both of which fight the adverb's job of stating a general drift. Cut
it, or generalise the sentence around it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)くなり<b>がちだ</b>。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)は<b>ともすれば</b>長(なが)くなり<b>がちだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain observation about how meetings have been going. Anyone could say it
to a colleague in the corridor.

(b) says the same thing, and frames it as a general truth about meetings that we
ought to be guarding against. The adverb adds a note of warning and lifts the
sentence into written or platform register, so it fits a report on how the company
runs its meetings and sounds heavy in the corridor.

</details>

# きりがない

**Meaning** · there is no end to it; you could go on forever - said of something that has no natural stopping point, usually as a reason to stop anyway

The <code>きり</code> is 切(き)り, a *cut* - the same word as in
<code>区切(くぎ)り</code>, the break you put between one stretch of work and the
next. <code>きりがない</code> says there is no such cut anywhere ahead: the list, the
complaints, the corrections, the wanting will keep going as long as you keep going.
In practice it is almost always the sentence that ends the activity.

## Formation

The usual shape is a **conditional clause plus** <code>きりがない</code>: start doing
this, and it will not end.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb + ば | ～ば + きりがない | 数(かぞ)える → 数(かぞ)えればきりがない |
| Verb + たら | ～たら + きりがない | 言(い)い出(だ)す → 言(い)い出(だ)したらきりがない |
| Verb + と | ～と + きりがない | 気(き)にする → 気(き)にするときりがない |
| Verb + ても | ～ても + きりがない | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ってもきりがない |
| Noun as topic | ～は + きりがない | 家事(かじ) → 家事(かじ)はきりがない |
| Polite | きりがありません | 挙(あ)げていけばきりがありません |
| Modifying a noun | きりのない + 名詞(めいし) | きりのない議論(ぎろん) |
| Standing alone | きりがない（ので／から） | もうきりがないから、やめよう |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kana spelling is standard. <code>切(き)りがない</code> is correct
and キリがない turns up in casual writing, but a book or a newspaper will normally
print きりがない.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
欲(よく)を言(い)えば<b>きりがない</b>。
There's no end to it once you start asking for more.
</pre>

<pre>
上(うえ)を見(み)たら<b>きりがない</b>よ。
If you keep comparing yourself with people above you, it never ends.
</pre>

<pre>
細(こま)かい直(なお)しを始(はじ)めると<b>きりがない</b>ので、この原稿(げんこう)はこのまま出(だ)します。
Once I start making small fixes there's no end to it, so I'm submitting this draft as it stands.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)ずつ意見(いけん)を聞(き)いていたら<b>きりがありません</b>。
If we hear everyone's opinion one by one we will never get through it.
</pre>

<pre>
文句(もんく)を言(い)い出(だ)したら<b>きりがない</b>から、この辺(へん)にしておこう。
Once we start complaining there's no end to it, so let's leave it there.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)の「なぜ」に付(つ)き合(あ)っていると<b>きりがない</b>が、悪(わる)い気(き)はしない。
Keeping up with a child's "why?" is endless, but I don't mind it.
</pre>

<pre>
過去(かこ)の失敗(しっぱい)を数(かぞ)え上(あ)げても<b>きりがない</b>。今(いま)できることを考(かんが)えよう。
There is no end to listing past failures. Let's think about what we can do now.
</pre>

<pre>
掃除(そうじ)というのは、やり始(はじ)めると<b>きりがない</b>仕事(しごと)の代表(だいひょう)のようなものだ。
Cleaning is the very model of a job with no natural end once you've started it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and thoroughly everyday. <code>きりがない</code> is what a colleague
says at half past six, what a parent says at the end of a game, and what a chair says
to close a discussion; <code>きりがありません</code> is entirely at home in a meeting
or a written report. Nothing about it is literary or stiff.

The tone is **resigned rather than annoyed**. It does not blame anyone - it points at
the structure of the task and observes that the task has no bottom. That is what
makes it such a graceful way to stop something: 「言(い)い出(だ)したらきりがないので」
lets you cut a list of grievances short without dismissing any of them, and
「数(かぞ)えればきりがない」 lets you decline to enumerate while conceding there is
plenty to enumerate.

Watch the second half of the sentence. <code>きりがない</code> very often comes with
ので, から or が and then a decision - やめよう, この辺(へん)で, このまま出(だ)す.
Standing entirely on its own it can sound like a complaint about the world; with the
decision attached it sounds practical.

One idiom to know: <code>欲(よく)を言(い)えばきりがない</code>, "if I started
listing what I'd have preferred, we'd be here all day". It is a set way of admitting
you are not fully satisfied while declining to make a fuss, and it comes up
constantly in reviews and in polite conversation about something adequate.

## Contrast

### きりがない vs 果(は)てしない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>きりがない</code> is about a **process that will not stop**
if you continue it. <code>果(は)てしない</code> is about **something vast** -
distance, sky, time - and it is a describing word, not a verdict on your evening.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)の外(そと)には<b>果(は)てしない</b>草原(そうげん)が広(ひろ)がっていた。
Outside the window an endless grassland stretched away.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)を集(あつ)め続(つづ)けても<b>きりがない</b>ので、そろそろ書(か)き始(はじ)めます。
There's no end to gathering material, so I'm going to start writing.
</pre>

<code>果(は)てしない</code> is descriptive and slightly literary; it admires or
overwhelms. <code>きりがない</code> is practical and often faintly weary. Where
they overlap - an endless argument - <code>果(は)てしない議論(ぎろん)</code>
makes the argument sound epic, while
<code>きりのない議論(ぎろん)</code> makes it sound like something to adjourn.

### きりがない vs ～ても仕方(しかた)がない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>きりがない</code> = doing it will **never finish**.
<code>～ても仕方(しかた)がない</code> = doing it **achieves nothing**. Endless
versus pointless.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)さら悔(く)やんでも<b>仕方(しかた)がない</b>。
There's no use regretting it now.
</pre>

<pre>
反省(はんせい)すべき点(てん)を挙(あ)げていけば<b>きりがない</b>。
If we start listing the things we should have done better, there'll be no end to it.
</pre>

Regret is useless whether it lasts a minute or a year, so the first sentence is about
value. The second concedes the list would be worth making and objects only to its
length. Many situations allow both, and they are not the same complaint: choose by
asking whether the trouble is that the activity is *futile* or merely *bottomless*.

### きりがない vs 限(かぎ)りがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>限(かぎ)りがない</code> states that something has **no
upper limit**, and is neutral enough to be a compliment.
<code>きりがない</code> reports a **stopping problem**, and is never praise.

</div>

<pre>
子供(こども)の可能性(かのうせい)には<b>限(かぎ)りがない</b>。
There is no limit to what a child may become.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)の質問(しつもん)に答(こた)えていると<b>きりがない</b>。
Answering a child's questions is a bottomless task.
</pre>

Swap them and the first turns a child's potential into a chore, while the second
starts to sound like a philosophical remark about the infinitude of questions.
<code>限(かぎ)りがない</code> is also the more formal of the two and is happy in
written abstractions - <code>欲望(よくぼう)には限(かぎ)りがない</code> - where
<code>きりがない</code> keeps its feet on the ground.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 数(かぞ)える<b>きりがない</b>。 → ✅ 数(かぞ)えれば<b>きりがない</b>。
  The clause in front is a conditional - `ば`, `たら`, `と` or `ても`. A plain
  dictionary form cannot sit directly in front of it.

- ❌ もう<b>きりがないだ</b>。 → ✅ もう<b>きりがない</b>。（or きりがありません）
  <code>ない</code> is already an adjective ending, so no `だ` follows. The polite
  form is <code>きりがありません</code>; <code>きりがないです</code> is heard in casual
  speech, but in a meeting or in writing the ありません form is the safer one.

- ❌ この道(みち)は<b>きりがない</b>。 → ✅ この道(みち)は<b>果(は)てしなく</b>続(つづ)いている。
  Physical extent is not a stopping point. <code>きりがない</code> needs an activity
  that someone is choosing to continue.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)なので、外(そと)で練習(れんしゅう)しても<b>きりがない</b>。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)なので、外(そと)で練習(れんしゅう)しても<b>意味(いみ)がない</b>。
  Rain makes the practice pointless, not endless. Reach for
  <code>仕方(しかた)がない</code> or <code>意味(いみ)がない</code> when the
  problem is value rather than length.

## Practice

**1.** いい写真(しゃしん)を選(えら)び始(はじ)めると____から、今日(きょう)は10枚(まい)で終(お)わりにしよう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

選(えら)び始(はじ)めると<b>きりがない</b>から

The <code>～と</code> clause sets up an activity that feeds on itself, and the
second half draws the practical conclusion - a decision to stop. That pairing is the
pattern's most typical use.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 終(お)わったことを今(いま)悔(く)やんでも（きりがない・仕方(しかた)がない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今(いま)悔(く)やんでも<b>仕方(しかた)がない</b>。

The objection is that regret changes nothing, which is value, not length.
<code>きりがない</code> would say the regretting could go on forever - possible as a
sentence, but it would be conceding that regret is worth doing and merely too
time-consuming.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If we start worrying about every detail, there'll be no end to it, so let's decide on the main points today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

細(こま)かい点(てん)まで気(き)にし出(だ)したら<b>きりがない</b>ので、今日(きょう)は大(おお)きな方針(ほうしん)だけ決(き)めましょう。

<code>気(き)にし出(だ)したら</code> catches "if we start worrying", and the
`ので` clause carries the decision that <code>きりがない</code> almost always
introduces. <code>気(き)にするときりがない</code> would also pass.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 質問(しつもん)を受(う)けるきりがないですから、あと三(みっ)つでやめます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 質問(しつもん)を受(う)け付(つ)けていたら<b>きりがありません</b>ので、あと三(みっ)つでやめます。
"There'd be no end to taking questions, so we'll stop after three more."

Two repairs. The verb must be in a conditional form - here `たら` - and the polite
form of the expression is <code>きりがありません</code>. Note the reading of
<code>三(みっ)つ</code>: the native numeral, みっつ, not さんつ.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この仕事(しごと)は<b>きりがない</b>。
(b) この仕事(しごと)は<b>終(お)わらない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a complaint about this particular job: for whatever reason, it is not getting
finished, and the speaker may well be frustrated or blaming someone.

(a) says something about the nature of the work - it has no built-in end, so
finishing is a decision rather than an event. Housework, proofreading and studying
are the classic cases. The tone is resigned and rather philosophical, and it usually
sets up "so I'm stopping here", where (b) sets up "so I'm still here at nine".

</details>

# ～ためしがない

**Meaning** · it has never once happened; not a single time - the speaker has watched for it repeatedly and it has never come off

<code>ためし</code> here is an old noun meaning a *precedent*, an instance already on
the record. <code>ためしがない</code> reports that the record is empty, and it reports
it with feeling: the chances came round again and again, and not one of them worked
out. English "never" is neutral; this is a complaint.

## Formation

The verb in front is normally in the **plain past**, because the sentence is looking
back over a history of attempts.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain past) - the standard | ～た + ためしがない | 守(まも)る → 時間(じかん)を守(まも)ったためしがない |
| い-adjective (past) | ～かった + ためしがない | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)かったためしがない |
| Verb (plain non-past) - less common | ～る + ためしがない | 続(つづ)く → 長(なが)く続(つづ)くためしがない |
| Emphasised | 一度(いちど)も／これまで + ～たためしがない | 一度(いちど)も勝(か)ったためしがない |
| Polite | ～たためしがありません | 当(あ)たったためしがありません |
| Joining on | ～たためしがなく、… | 届(とど)いたためしがなく、いつも当日(とうじつ)だ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - write it in kana. The <code>ためし</code> of this pattern is the one
written 例(ためし) in older texts, "an instance"; the everyday
<code>試(ため)し</code> is a different word meaning "a try", and spelling the
pattern with it is a common slip.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの人(ひと)が時間(じかん)どおりに来(き)た<b>ためしがない</b>。
That man has never once turned up on time.
</pre>

<pre>
ここの天気(てんき)予報(よほう)が当(あ)たった<b>ためしがない</b>。
The weather forecast here has never once been right.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)が自分(じぶん)から皿(さら)を洗(あら)った<b>ためしがない</b>。
My brother has never once washed up of his own accord.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は約束(やくそく)した時間(じかん)に現(あらわ)れた<b>ためしがありません</b>。
My younger brother has never yet shown up at the time he agreed to.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい運動(うんどう)を始(はじ)めても、三日(みっか)以上(いじょう)続(つづ)いた<b>ためしがない</b>。
Whenever I take up a new form of exercise, it has never once lasted more than three days.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の「すぐ終(お)わります」を信(しん)じて、本当(ほんとう)にすぐ終(お)わった<b>ためしがない</b>。
I keep believing his "it'll be done in a minute", and it has never once been done in a minute.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)に資料(しりょう)が届(とど)いた<b>ためしがなく</b>、みんな当日(とうじつ)に初(はじ)めて中身(なかみ)を見(み)ている。
The papers have never once arrived before the meeting; everyone sees them for the first time on the day.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で立(た)てた計画(けいかく)がそのとおりに進(すす)んだ<b>ためしがない</b>のに、また細(こま)かい予定(よてい)を作(つく)ってしまった。
No plan I have ever drawn up has gone the way it was written, and yet here I am making another detailed schedule.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **an accusation, however lightly delivered**. Its whole point is the
gap between what should have happened and what the record shows, so it comes with
exasperation, irony or resignation built in. It is at home in speech and in writing
alike - a grumble to a friend, a wry line in an essay, a pointed sentence in a
report - and nothing about it is archaic.

Two conditions have to be met. There must have been **repeated opportunities**: a
person who has never climbed Mount Fuji has simply never climbed it, and that is
<code>登(のぼ)ったことがない</code>, not
<code>登(のぼ)ったためしがない</code>. And the outcome must be one that
**was expected or promised** - punctuality, an accurate forecast, a plan that holds.
Without an expectation to fail, there is nothing for the empty record to indict.

Turned on yourself it is one of the most likeable things in the language:
<code>続(つづ)いたためしがない</code> about your own diets and study plans is
self-deprecating and slightly funny. Turned on someone else it is sharp. Said about a
colleague to their face - or about a superior at all - it lands as a serious
complaint, because it does not say "sometimes" or "often"; it says the count is
zero, and a count of zero leaves no room to argue. If you only mean "he is often
late", say <code>遅(おく)れがちだ</code> and keep the relationship.

There is no polite-neutral version. <code>ためしがありません</code> raises the
politeness of the sentence but not one degree of its bluntness.

## Contrast

### ～たためしがない vs ～たことがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことがない</code> reports **experience** and is neutral.
<code>ためしがない</code> reports a **failure rate of zero out of many** and is a
complaint.

</div>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)には行(い)った<b>ことがない</b>。
I've never been to Hokkaido.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の話(はなし)が予定(よてい)の時間(じかん)で終(お)わった<b>ためしがない</b>。
His talks have never once finished in the time allotted.
</pre>

Nobody has been failing to send you to Hokkaido; there was no expectation, so
<code>ためしがない</code> would be bizarre there. Conversely,
<code>終(お)わったことがない</code> in the second sentence would flatten it into a
piece of trivia about his talks, losing the irritation that is the reason for saying
it at all.

### ～たためしがない vs 一度(いちど)も～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>一度(いちど)も～ない</code> **counts**.
<code>ためしがない</code> counts **and passes judgement**.

</div>

<pre>
このチームは今年(ことし)、まだ<b>一度(いちど)も</b>負(ま)けていない。
This team has not lost once this season.
</pre>

<pre>
このチームが大事(だいじ)な試合(しあい)で勝(か)った<b>ためしがない</b>。
This team has never once won when it mattered.
</pre>

The first is a statistic and can be good news; a scoreboard can say it.
<code>ためしがない</code> cannot be good news about the subject - the emptiness of
the record is always held against someone. Note that the two combine happily:
<code>一度(いちど)も勝(か)ったためしがない</code> is emphatic, not redundant.

### ～たためしがない vs ～わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ためしがない</code> is about the **past record**.
<code>わけがない</code> is about **impossibility** and points at the future.

</div>

<pre>
あんな安(やす)い部品(ぶひん)で直(なお)る<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way it'll be fixed with a cheap part like that.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)の修理(しゅうり)で一度(いちど)に直(なお)った<b>ためしがない</b>。
That shop's repairs have never once fixed it in one go.
</pre>

<code>わけがない</code> is a judgement about what is possible and needs no history
behind it; <code>ためしがない</code> claims nothing about possibility at all - the
thing evidently could happen, it simply never has. The two often appear together in
the same complaint, the record supporting the prediction.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)った<b>ためしがない</b>。 → ✅ 富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)った<b>ことがない</b>。
  With no repeated opportunity and no expectation, the pattern has nothing to be
  indignant about. Plain lack of experience is <code>ことがない</code>.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が時間(じかん)を守(まも)る<b>ためしがないだ</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が時間(じかん)を守(まも)った<b>ためしがない</b>。
  Two repairs: the verb goes into the plain past, and <code>ない</code> takes no
  `だ`.

- ❌ 試(ため)しがない → ✅ ためしがない
  <code>試(ため)し</code> is "a trial, a go". The word in this pattern is the old
  noun for "an instance", and kana is how it is written.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)は締(し)め切(き)りを守(まも)った<b>ためしがありません</b>ね。（to the boss） → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は締(し)め切(き)りが厳(きび)しいときは大変(たいへん)そうですね。
  Polite endings do not soften the content. A zero count delivered upward is a
  charge, and ます-form makes it a politely worded charge.

## Practice

**1.** うちの犬(いぬ)は、呼(よ)んですぐに来(き)た____がない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来(き)た<b>ためし</b>がない

The dog has been called hundreds of times and has never once come promptly - many
opportunities, a clear expectation, an empty record. Note the reading:
<code>来(き)た</code>, the past of <code>来(く)る</code> is き, not こ.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 私(わたし)はまだ一度(いちど)も海外(かいがい)で働(はたら)いた（ことがない・ためしがない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

海外(かいがい)で働(はたら)いた<b>ことがない</b>。

This is a plain statement about the speaker's experience, and nobody promised them a
job abroad. <code>ためしがない</code> would imply they had been trying and failing to
get one, and that the failure is somebody's fault.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The train on this line has never once been on time in the morning."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この路線(ろせん)の電車(でんしゃ)が朝(あさ)に時間(じかん)どおりに来(き)た<b>ためしがない</b>。

Every weekday morning is another opportunity and another failure, which is exactly
what the pattern is for. Mind the irregular verb: the plain past of
<code>来(く)る</code> is <code>来(き)た</code>, and こ- belongs to
<code>来(こ)ない</code> and its relatives.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)の予想(よそう)は当(あ)たるためしがなかったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)の予想(よそう)が当(あ)たった<b>ためしがありません</b>。
"Her predictions have never once come true."

Three points: the plain past <code>当(あ)たった</code> is the normal base; the
polite form is <code>ためしがありません</code> rather than
<code>ためしがなかったです</code>; and the subject of the inner clause is marked with
`が`, since <code>予想(よそう)が当(あ)たる</code> is the natural collocation.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 息子(むすこ)は部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)け<b>ない</b>。
(b) 息子(むすこ)が自分(じぶん)から部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けた<b>ためしがない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states a habit. It is a complaint, but a mild and ordinary one, and it leaves
room for exceptions - he does tidy up when told.

(b) closes that room. It says that across every occasion the speaker can remember,
the count of times he tidied up unprompted is zero, and the emphasis on
<code>自分(じぶん)から</code> shows what was being hoped for. It is funnier and
sharper than (a), and it is the sentence a parent uses when they have given up
expecting.

</details>

# ～たる・～たるもの

**Meaning** · one who is a ~, with everything that position demands - names a station and then states the duty that comes with it

<code>たる</code> is the attributive form of <code>たり</code>, the classical copula
that modern Japanese replaced with <code>である</code>. So
<code>教師(きょうし)たる者(もの)</code> is literally "a person who is a
teacher" - but nobody uses it for mere identification. It survives because it carries
the weight of the old written language, and it is used to invoke an office and then
tell whoever holds it how to behave.

## Formation

<code>たる</code> attaches directly to a **noun naming a role, rank or office**, and
must be followed by a noun, because it is an attributive form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun + noun | ～たる + 名詞(めいし) | 指導者(しどうしゃ) → 指導者(しどうしゃ)たる責任(せきにん) |
| Noun, as the topic of a maxim | ～たる者(もの)（は）、… | 医師(いし) → 医師(いし)たる者(もの)、… |
| Followed by an obligation | ～たる者(もの)、…べきだ／なければならない | 紳士(しんし)たる者(もの)、約束(やくそく)は守(まも)るべきだ |
| Qualifying a standard | ～たるに恥(は)じない／ふさわしい | 代表(だいひょう)たるにふさわしい態度(たいど) |
| Fossilised adjectives | 堂々(どうどう)たる／確固(かっこ)たる + 名詞(めいし) | 確固(かっこ)たる信念(しんねん) |
| Modern equivalent | ～である以上(いじょう)／～として | 医師(いし)である以上(いじょう)、… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>者(もの)</code> after it is read もの, and is very often written in
kana: <code>たるもの</code>. Do not confuse the pattern with
<code>～たりとも</code>, which comes from the same old copula but means "not even
one": <code>一日(いちにち)たりとも休(やす)めない</code>. The two are unrelated
in meaning.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the <code>堂々(どうどう)たる</code> group is the one place this ending
is still fully alive. A closed set of two-kanji words - 確固(かっこ),
堂々(どうどう), 歴然(れきぜん), 燦然(さんぜん), 錚々(そうそう) - form
adjectives only in this shape, taking <code>たる</code> before a noun and
<code>と</code> before a verb: <code>堂々(どうどう)と歩(ある)く</code>. You cannot
coin new ones.

</div>

<pre>
教師(きょうし)<b>たる者(もの)</b>、生徒(せいと)の前(まえ)で嘘(うそ)をついてはならない。
One who is a teacher must not lie in front of his pupils.
</pre>

<pre>
王(おう)<b>たる者(もの)</b>は、まず民(たみ)の声(こえ)を聞(き)け。
Let him who is king listen first to the voice of the people.
</pre>

<pre>
紳士(しんし)<b>たる者(もの)</b>、一度(いちど)した約束(やくそく)は必(かなら)ず守(まも)るべきである。
A gentleman keeps a promise once he has made it.
</pre>

<pre>
指導者(しどうしゃ)<b>たる者(もの)</b>に求(もと)められるのは、才能(さいのう)よりも覚悟(かくご)である。
What is asked of one who leads is resolve rather than talent.
</pre>

<pre>
一国(いっこく)の代表(だいひょう)<b>たるにふさわしい</b>態度(たいど)で試合(しあい)に臨(のぞ)んでほしい。
I want them to take the field in a manner worthy of a national side.
</pre>

<pre>
「社会人(しゃかいじん)<b>たる者(もの)</b>、時間(じかん)だけは守(まも)れ」と父(ちち)によく言(い)われた。
"If you are going to be an adult in society, at least be punctual" - my father said it often.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)になった以上(いじょう)、管理職(かんりしょく)<b>たる自覚(じかく)</b>を持(も)って行動(こうどう)してもらいたい。
Now that you are a section head, I want you to act with a proper sense of what the post means.
</pre>

<pre>
創業者(そうぎょうしゃ)は、技術者(ぎじゅつしゃ)<b>たる者(もの)</b>自分(じぶん)の手(て)で直(なお)せ、という人(ひと)だったそうだ。
The founder, they say, was a man who held that an engineer should be able to fix things with his own hands.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is a form to recognise, not to deploy.** It belongs to the written language of
another era and to the modern registers that borrow from it: an inaugural address, a
company creed, a school motto, a newspaper column scolding the government, the
narration of a historical drama, a character in period fiction, a manga villain
explaining his code. Said in an ordinary conversation it does not sound formal - it
sounds theatrical, and Japanese speakers often use it *because* it does, as a joke:
<code>学生(がくせい)たるもの、朝(あさ)は起(お)きるべし</code> among friends
is a parody of a lecture, not a lecture.

The modern way to do this job is <code>～である以上(いじょう)</code> or plain
<code>～として</code>:
<code>医師(いし)である以上(いじょう)、患者(かんじゃ)に説明(せつめい)する義務(ぎむ)がある</code>.
That sentence says everything the <code>たるもの</code> version says and can be
spoken aloud in a hospital corridor.

Three things travel with the pattern. It **moralises**: what follows is nearly always
べきだ, なければならない, てはならない or an imperative. It **looks down**, or at
least across from a position of authority - it is the voice of a founder, a father, a
leader, an editorial. And it is **almost never used of oneself**:
<code>私(わたし)はリーダーたる者(もの)…</code> is self-important in a way that
is hard to recover from. Turned on a listener it is a rebuke, and a heavy one, since
it accuses them not of a mistake but of failing their station.

The one corner where a learner may safely produce it is the fossilised adjective
group - <code>確固(かっこ)たる証拠(しょうこ)</code>,
<code>堂々(どうどう)と話(はな)す</code> - which is ordinary formal vocabulary
and carries none of the moralising.

## Contrast

### ～たる者(もの) vs ～である以上(いじょう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same argument, two centuries apart.
<code>～である以上(いじょう)</code> is what a modern speaker says; use it, and
recognise <code>たる者(もの)</code> when you meet it.

</div>

<pre>
審判(しんぱん)<b>たる者(もの)</b>、どちらにも味方(みかた)してはならない。
He who is a referee must take neither side.
</pre>

<pre>
審判(しんぱん)<b>である以上(いじょう)</b>、どちらか一方(いっぽう)に味方(みかた)してはいけません。
Since you are the referee, you must not favour one side over the other.
</pre>

The content is identical. The first belongs on a wall or in a speech, the second in a
meeting, an email or a conversation with the referee. Note that the modern version
also lets you soften the ending - <code>ほうがいい</code>,
<code>べきではないでしょうか</code> - where <code>たる者(もの)</code> practically
requires an absolute.

### ～たる vs ～として

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>として</code> states the **capacity someone acts in**, with
no moral weight. <code>たる</code> invokes the **dignity of the office** and expects
something of the holder.

</div>

<pre>
通訳(つうやく)<b>として</b>会議(かいぎ)に参加(さんか)しました。
I took part in the conference as an interpreter.
</pre>

<pre>
通訳(つうやく)<b>たる者(もの)</b>、自分(じぶん)の意見(いけん)を混(ま)ぜてはならない。
An interpreter must not mix in opinions of his own.
</pre>

Only the second is a rule about how interpreters ought to behave, which is why
<code>として</code> would weaken it and <code>たる者(もの)</code> in the first
sentence would be absurd - the speaker is reporting where they sat, not proclaiming a
creed. Grammatically, too, <code>として</code> attaches to a phrase and can precede a
verb; <code>たる</code> must be followed by a noun.

### ～たる vs ～なる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - two classical copulas, two closed sets.
<code>たる</code> goes with the 堂々(どうどう) group;
<code>なる</code> survives in 単(たん)なる, 大(おお)いなる,
聖(せい)なる. Neither is productive, and you cannot choose between them - each
word takes one or the other.

</div>

<pre>
それは<b>単(たん)なる</b>偶然(ぐうぜん)にすぎない。
That is nothing more than a coincidence.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>確固(かっこ)たる</b>証拠(しょうこ)をつかんでいた。
He had firm evidence in hand.
</pre>

Both endings mean roughly "which is", and both are frozen. The practical advice is
lexical, not grammatical: learn <code>単(たん)なる</code>,
<code>大(おお)いなる</code>, <code>聖(せい)なる</code>,
<code>確固(かっこ)たる</code>, <code>堂々(どうどう)たる</code> as vocabulary
items, and never build a new one from a noun you like the look of.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>だたる者(もの)</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>たる者(もの)</b>
  <code>たる</code> already contains the copula. Nothing goes between it and the
  noun - no `だ`, no <code>である</code>, no `の`.

- ❌ 優(やさ)しい<b>たる者(もの)</b> → ✅ 優(やさ)しい人(ひと)
  Only a noun that names a status or office can precede it. Adjectives and verbs
  cannot, and neither can an ordinary noun with no rank attached to it -
  ❌<code>読者(どくしゃ)たる者(もの)</code> is odd because readers have no
  duties.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はこの会(かい)の代表(だいひょう)<b>たる</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はこの会(かい)の代表(だいひょう)<b>である</b>。
  <code>たる</code> is an attributive form and cannot end a sentence. A noun must
  follow it.

- ❌ 一日(いちにち)<b>たる者(もの)</b>休(やす)めない。 → ✅ 一日(いちにち)<b>たりとも</b>休(やす)めない。
  "Not even a single day" is <code>たりとも</code>. The similarity of shape catches
  learners often, and the two patterns share nothing but their ancestor.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は部長(ぶちょう)<b>たる者(もの)</b>、部下(ぶか)を守(まも)ります。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)<b>として</b>、部下(ぶか)は私(わたし)が守(まも)ります。
  Applied to yourself the pattern announces your own eminence. Use
  <code>として</code> when the person under discussion is you.

## Practice

**1.** 「武士(ぶし)____者(もの)、恥(はじ)を知(し)れ」というのは時代劇(じだいげき)によくある台詞(せりふ)だ。

A samurai drama is exactly where this form lives now. Note the shape of the whole

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>たる者(もの)</code> becomes <code>である以上(いじょう)</code>, and the

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"We cannot waste so much as a single yen."

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) プロの選手(せんしゅ)<b>たる者(もの)</b>、体調(たいちょう)管理(かんり)に気(き)をつけるべきだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

team-mate in the changing room it is either an insult or a joke, and the listener

# ～並み

**Meaning** · on a par with ~; of ~ grade - measures something against a named standard and says it reaches that level, no more and no less

Read <code>並(な)み</code>. It is a noun suffix, not a grammatical ending: you
attach it to a word that names a **recognised level** - プロ, 大人(おとな),
例年(れいねん), 人(ひと) - and the compound behaves like an ordinary noun.
The measurement is *equality*, which is why the same word can be a compliment to an
amateur and a slight to an expert.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun naming a standard | ～並(な)み | プロ → プロ並(な)み |
| Modifying a noun | ～並(な)みの + 名詞(めいし) | プロ並(な)みの腕前(うでまえ) |
| Modifying a verb or adjective | ～並(な)みに + … | 大人(おとな)並(な)みに働(はたら)く |
| As predicate | ～並(な)みだ／です | 気温(きおん)は例年(れいねん)並(な)みだ |
| Benchmark periods | 例年(れいねん)／平年(へいねん)／前年(ぜんねん) + 並(な)み | 前年(ぜんねん)並(な)みの売上(うりあげ) |
| Set compounds | 人(ひと)並(な)み, 世間(せけん)並(な)み | 人(ひと)並(な)みの生活(せいかつ) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun in front must name a **class or benchmark** that the
listener can already picture as a level: a profession, an age group, a typical year,
"most people", a named product. It is not a free suffix you can bolt onto any noun.
❌<code>机(つくえ)並(な)みの重(おも)さ</code> fails because a desk is not a
recognised unit of heaviness; <code>大人(おとな)並(な)みの重(おも)さ</code>
works because adults are.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a second <code>並(な)み</code> means "a row", and it is a different
word-formation: 町並(まちな)み (a row of houses), 山並(やまな)み (a mountain
range), 軒並(のきな)み (eaves in a row → *across the board, every one of them*).
And beware <code>月(つき)並(な)み</code>, which is frozen with the meaning
**trite, hackneyed** - <code>月(つき)並(な)みな表現(ひょうげん)</code> is a
criticism, and has nothing to do with months.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
弟(おとうと)の料理(りょうり)はプロ<b>並(な)み</b>だ。
My little brother's cooking is on a par with a professional's.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)の暑(あつ)さは例年(れいねん)<b>並(な)み</b>だそうだ。
This summer's heat is apparently about the same as an average year's.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は中学生(ちゅうがくせい)のころから大人(おとな)<b>並(な)みに</b>働(はたら)いていた。
From his middle-school days he was working as hard as a grown man.
</pre>

<pre>
この値段(ねだん)で専門店(せんもんてん)<b>並(な)みの</b>味(あじ)が楽(たの)しめる。
At this price you get flavour on a par with a specialist shop.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)<b>並(な)みの</b>暮(く)らしができれば、それで十分(じゅうぶん)だと思(おも)っている。
If I can live the way most people do, I think that is enough.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の影響(えいきょう)で、市内(しない)の店(みせ)は<b>軒並(のきな)み</b>休業(きゅうぎょう)となった。
Because of the typhoon, the shops in town closed one and all.
</pre>

<pre>
新型(しんがた)の電池(でんち)は、容量(ようりょう)こそ従来(じゅうらい)品(ひん)<b>並(な)み</b>だが、寿命(じゅみょう)が二倍(にばい)になったという。
The new battery only matches the old one for capacity, they say, but it lasts twice as long.
</pre>

<pre>
入社(にゅうしゃ)三年(さんねん)目(め)で、すでに課長(かちょう)<b>並(な)みの</b>仕事(しごと)を任(まか)されているらしい。
In his third year with the company he is apparently already handling work on a par with a manager's.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and much more common than its N1 label suggests. Weather forecasts
run on it (平年(へいねん)並(な)みの気温(きおん)), so do business reports
(前年(ぜんねん)並(な)みの売上(うりあげ)) and advertising
(専門店(せんもんてん)並(な)みの品(しな)ぞろえ). Nothing about it is
literary.

The thing to feel is the **ceiling**. <code>並(な)み</code> claims equality and
implicitly denies anything beyond it, so where the standard is above the subject it
flatters - an amateur cook who is プロ並(な)み has been paid a real compliment -
and where the standard is at or below the subject it damns.
<code>この店(みせ)の寿司(すし)はスーパー並(な)みだ</code> is a bad review, and
telling a professional their work is プロ並(な)み would be heard as saying they
have only just cleared the bar.

Two set compounds are worth knowing as vocabulary.
<code>人(ひと)並(な)み</code> means "like everybody else, ordinary", and is
usually neutral or quietly positive:
<code>人(ひと)並(な)みに幸(しあわ)せです</code> is a modest, contented thing
to say, and <code>人(ひと)並(な)み以上(いじょう)</code> is the standard way
to say "above average". <code>世間(せけん)並(な)み</code> means "in line with
what society does", and often carries a hint that the speaker finds the standard a
little constraining.

There is also a bare 並(なみ), an ordinary noun meaning "the standard grade" -
<code>並(なみ)の選手(せんしゅ)ではない</code>, "no ordinary player", and on old
menus 上(じょう)・中(ちゅう)・並(なみ) for the three grades of a dish. The derived verb
<code>並外(なみはず)れる</code> ("to be out of the ordinary") comes from the same
noun and is common in praise:
<code>並外(なみはず)れた記憶力(きおくりょく)</code>.

## Contrast

### ～並(な)み vs ～級(きゅう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>並(な)み</code> says the thing **measures up to** a
standard. <code>級(きゅう)</code> says it **belongs to** a rank, and it scales
upward without a ceiling.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の走(はし)りはオリンピック<b>級(きゅう)</b>だ。
His running is Olympic class.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の走(はし)りは実業団(じつぎょうだん)の選手(せんしゅ)<b>並(な)み</b>だ。
His running is on a par with a corporate-team athlete's.
</pre>

<code>級(きゅう)</code> assigns membership of a class and sounds like a
classification - <code>世界(せかい)級(きゅう)</code>,
<code>一流(いちりゅう)級(きゅう)</code> - so it fits records, storms and
talent. <code>並(な)み</code> lines two things up side by side and states that
they match. That is why <code>並(な)み</code> readily takes an everyday standard
(スーパー並(な)み, 去年(きょねん)並(な)み) where
<code>級(きゅう)</code> would sound absurd.

### ～並(な)み vs ～どおり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>並(な)み</code> matches a **level or amount**.
<code>どおり</code> matches a **plan, expectation or manner**. Compare
<code>例年(れいねん)並(な)み</code> with
<code>例年(れいねん)どおり</code>.

</div>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の桜(さくら)は<b>例年(れいねん)どおり</b>四月(しがつ)初(はじ)めに咲(さ)いた。
The cherries bloomed at the start of April, as they do every year.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の花見(はなみ)客(きゃく)の数(かず)は<b>例年(れいねん)並(な)み</b>だった。
The number of blossom-viewers was about the same as in a normal year.
</pre>

The first is about following the usual course; the second is about hitting the usual
figure. Where the sentence has a number, a temperature or a volume in it,
<code>並(な)み</code> is what you want; where it has a schedule, a procedure or a
promise, <code>どおり</code> is. Note the reading:
<code>どおり</code> with rendaku after a noun, never ❌とおり in this compound.

### ～並(な)み vs ～ほど・～くらい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ほど</code> and <code>くらい</code> compare inside a
**clause** and are flexible about what they compare. <code>並(な)み</code> builds
a **compound noun** and needs the standard to be a recognised class.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>ほど</b>速(はや)くは泳(およ)げません。
I can't swim as fast as Tanaka.
</pre>

<pre>
中学生(ちゅうがくせい)<b>並(な)みに</b>しか泳(およ)げません。
I can only swim about as well as a middle-schooler.
</pre>

Only <code>ほど</code> works with a named individual in an ordinary sentence, and only
<code>ほど</code> pairs with a negative to mean "not as ... as".
<code>並(な)み</code> can take a person's name if that person is famous enough to
function as a yardstick - <code>あの選手(せんしゅ)並(な)みの肩(かた)</code>
about a legendary throwing arm - but that is the exception, and it is doing
comparison-to-a-standard, not comparison-to-a-friend.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ プロ<b>並(な)みだの</b>腕前(うでまえ) → ✅ プロ<b>並(な)みの</b>腕前(うでまえ)
  The compound is a noun, so it modifies another noun with `の`, and modifies a verb
  or adjective with `に`.

- ❌ 速(はや)い<b>並(な)み</b>の車(くるま) → ✅ レーシングカー<b>並(な)み</b>の車(くるま)
  <code>並(な)み</code> attaches to a noun that names a standard, never to an
  adjective or a verb.

- ❌ 月(つき)並(な)みの支払(しはら)い（meaning "the monthly payment"） → ✅ 毎月(まいつき)の支払(しはら)い
  <code>月(つき)並(な)み</code> is a frozen word meaning *trite*. Asking about
  your <code>月(つき)並(な)みの支払(しはら)い</code> asks about your
  unimaginative payment.

- ❌ 世界(せかい)記録(きろく)保持者(ほじしゃ)の泳(およ)ぎは、さすがオリンピック選手(せんしゅ)<b>並(な)み</b>だ。 → ✅ …さすがオリンピック選手(せんしゅ)を<b>上回(うわまわ)る</b>泳(およ)ぎだ。
  Praising someone with a standard they already exceed puts a ceiling on them. The
  compliment lands as a demotion.

## Practice

**1.** この冬(ふゆ)の降雪(こうせつ)量(りょう)は平年(へいねん)____で、交通(こうつう)への影響(えいきょう)は少(すく)なかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

平年(へいねん)<b>並(な)み</b>

A quantity measured against a benchmark year is the forecaster's standard phrase.
<code>平年(へいねん)どおり</code> would be about timing or procedure rather than
about how much snow fell.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 開会式(かいかいしき)は（例年(れいねん)並(な)み・例年(れいねん)どおり）体育館(たいいくかん)で行(おこな)われた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（例年(れいねん)どおり）体育館(たいいくかん)で行(おこな)われた。

Holding the ceremony in the gym is a matter of following the usual practice, so
<code>どおり</code>. <code>例年(れいねん)並(な)み</code> would need something
countable - the number of guests, the length of the ceremony - to be level with.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "For a first-year student, her presentation was on a par with a graduate student's."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The standard - graduate students - is a recognised class, and the subject sits below
it, so the comparison reads as praise. <code>大学院生(だいがくいんせい)並(な)みの発表(はっぴょう)だった</code>
works equally well.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しいカメラは画質(がしつ)がいい並(な)みで、値段(ねだん)も安(やす)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しいカメラは一眼(いちがん)レフ<b>並(な)みの</b>画質(がしつ)で、値段(ねだん)も安(やす)い。
"The new camera has image quality on a par with an SLR, and it's cheap too."

<code>並(な)み</code> cannot attach to the adjective
<code>いい</code>. It needs a noun that names the level being matched, and then `の`
to modify the following noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の英語(えいご)は通訳(つうやく)<b>並(な)み</b>だ。
(b) 彼(かれ)の英語(えいご)は通訳(つうやく)に<b>負(ま)けない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

implication not beyond them. Said of a salesman it is high praise; said of a working
interpreter it would sting.

(b) frames the same comparison as a contest he does not lose, which allows for the
possibility that he is better. It is warmer and more admiring, and it carries none
of the ceiling that <code>並(な)み</code> quietly installs.

</details>

# ～も同然だ

**Meaning** · as good as ~; practically ~ - strictly speaking it is not, but for every purpose that matters it may as well be

Read <code>も同然(どうぜん)だ</code>. <code>同然(どうぜん)</code> is a noun
meaning "the same thing", and the `も` is the one that says "you might as well call
it that too". The sentence is always a **judgement**: the speaker has decided that
the remaining difference between the situation and the label is not worth counting.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | ～も同然(どうぜん)だ | 新品(しんぴん) → 新品(しんぴん)も同然(どうぜん)だ |
| Verb (plain past) | ～たも同然(どうぜん)だ | 終(お)わる → 終(お)わったも同然(どうぜん)だ |
| Verb (negative) | ～ないも同然(どうぜん)だ | ある → あってもないも同然(どうぜん)だ |
| Modifying a noun | ～も同然(どうぜん)の + 名詞(めいし) | ただ同然(どうぜん)の値段(ねだん) |
| Polite | ～も同然(どうぜん)です | 決(き)まったも同然(どうぜん)です |
| Past | ～も同然(どうぜん)だった | 他人(たにん)同然(どうぜん)だった |
| With `も` dropped (set compounds) | 名詞(めいし) + 同然(どうぜん) | 家族(かぞく)同然(どうぜん), ただ同然(どうぜん), 他人(たにん)同然(どうぜん) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a **past-tense verb** in front is the norm, because the pattern says the
result is effectively already in place:
<code>勝(か)ったも同然(どうぜん)だ</code>, "we've as good as won", said while
the match is still going. ❌<code>勝(か)つも同然(どうぜん)だ</code> is not
Japanese. When it modifies a following noun, use `の`:
<code>家族(かぞく)同然(どうぜん)の付(つ)き合(あ)い</code>, not
❌<code>同然(どうぜん)な</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この自転車(じてんしゃ)は新品(しんぴん)<b>も同然(どうぜん)だ</b>。
This bicycle is as good as new.
</pre>

<pre>
あと一問(いちもん)だから、もう終(お)わった<b>も同然(どうぜん)だ</b>。
There's only one question left, so I'm as good as finished.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)連絡(れんらく)を取(と)っていないので、いとことはもう他人(たにん)<b>同然(どうぜん)</b>です。
We haven't been in touch for ten years, so my cousin and I are practically strangers now.
</pre>

<pre>
その机(つくえ)は閉店(へいてん)前(まえ)の店(みせ)でただ<b>同然(どうぜん)</b>で手(て)に入(い)れた。
I got that desk for next to nothing from a shop that was closing down.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)がドイツ語(ご)だけでは、私(わたし)には無(な)い<b>も同然(どうぜん)だ</b>。
With the manual only in German, for me it might as well not exist.
</pre>

<pre>
留学中(りゅうがくちゅう)、ホストファミリーには家族(かぞく)<b>同然(どうぜん)の</b>扱(あつか)いをしてもらった。
While I was studying abroad my host family treated me as one of their own.
</pre>

<pre>
決勝(けっしょう)進出(しんしゅつ)は決(き)まった<b>も同然(どうぜん)だ</b>と言(い)われていたが、最終(さいしゅう)戦(せん)で敗(やぶ)れた。
A place in the final was said to be as good as settled, but they lost the last match.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)の半分(はんぶん)が家賃(やちん)に消(き)えるのだから、貯金(ちょきん)は無(な)い<b>も同然(どうぜん)</b>です。
Half my salary goes on rent, so my savings are as good as nonexistent.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and thoroughly usable. <code>も同然(どうぜん)です</code> is fine
in a meeting, <code>も同然(どうぜん)だ</code> is fine over dinner, and it appears
in newspapers without looking formal.

What it always adds is the speaker's **willingness to round off**. Nothing in the
world is measuring the difference for you: the bicycle really has been ridden, the
exam really is not over, and the cousin really is family. The sentence says those
remainders do not matter. That makes it a natural fit for reassurance
(<code>もう終(お)わったも同然(どうぜん)だよ</code>), for a bargain
(<code>ただ同然(どうぜん)</code>), and for warmth
(<code>家族(かぞく)同然(どうぜん)</code>) - and equally for dismissal, which is
where it can bite.

Be careful in that last direction. <code>死(し)んだも同然(どうぜん)</code>,
<code>クビも同然(どうぜん)</code>, <code>他人(たにん)同然(どうぜん)</code>
are heavy sentences, and applied to a person's work -
<code>やっていないも同然(どうぜん)だ</code> - the pattern says their effort
counts for nothing. Because the rounding-off is the speaker's own choice, the
listener hears a verdict rather than a fact, and a mild-sounding
<code>です</code> at the end does not soften it.

Two idioms are worth learning whole. <code>ただ同然(どうぜん)</code> means "for
practically nothing" and is the standard way to describe a bargain price.
<code>家族(かぞく)同然(どうぜん)</code> means "like family" and is warm; note
that both drop the `も` - after a familiar noun the particle very often disappears,
though putting it back is never wrong.

## Contrast

### ～も同然(どうぜん)だ vs ～ようなものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>も同然(どうぜん)だ</code> says the situation has
**effectively reached** that state. <code>ようなものだ</code> offers a
**comparison or explanation** - "it's basically like X".

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)残業(ざんぎょう)で、会社(かいしゃ)に住(す)んでいる<b>ようなものだ</b>。
I work late every day - it's as if I lived at the office.
</pre>

<pre>
残(のこ)りは署名(しょめい)だけなので、契約(けいやく)は成立(せいりつ)した<b>も同然(どうぜん)です</b>。
All that's left is the signature, so the contract is as good as concluded.
</pre>

The first is a figure of speech: nobody thinks the speaker lives at the office, and
the sentence is describing what it feels like. The second is a claim about the actual
state of the contract, and you could act on it. Where a metaphor is intended,
<code>も同然(どうぜん)</code> sounds oddly literal; where a real state is being
reported, <code>ようなものだ</code> sounds evasive.

### ～も同然(どうぜん)だ vs ～に等(ひと)しい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same idea in two registers.
<code>に等(ひと)しい</code> is **written and often severe**, common in argument and
criticism; <code>も同然(どうぜん)だ</code> is **everyday**.

</div>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)もなくそう断(だん)じるのは、中傷(ちゅうしょう)<b>に等(ひと)しい</b>。
To assert that without evidence amounts to defamation.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)、物置(ものおき)<b>も同然(どうぜん)</b>だね。
This room is practically a storeroom, isn't it.
</pre>

<code>に等(ひと)しい</code> is at home with abstractions - 不可能(ふかのう),
犯罪(はんざい), ゼロ - and in editorial or legal prose, where it delivers a
judgement in impersonal-sounding terms.
<code>も同然(どうぜん)</code> handles concrete situations and everyday
exaggeration. Put <code>に等(ひと)しい</code> on the storeroom and you sound as
though you are drafting a complaint.

### ～も同然(どうぜん)だ vs ～と同(おな)じだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と同(おな)じだ</code> claims things **really are** the
same. <code>も同然(どうぜん)だ</code> concedes they are **not**, and says the
difference does not matter.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)のモデルと中身(なかみ)は<b>同(おな)じ</b>です。
The internals are the same as last year's model.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)のモデル<b>も同然(どうぜん)</b>です。
It might as well be last year's model.
</pre>

The first is a statement of fact that an engineer could confirm. The second is an
opinion - the changes are so slight that the speaker refuses to count them - and it
is faintly disparaging, which the first is not. Whenever a sentence could be checked
against reality, <code>同(おな)じ</code> is the honest word;
<code>同然(どうぜん)</code> announces that someone has made a judgement call.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 試合(しあい)は勝(か)つ<b>も同然(どうぜん)だ</b>。 → ✅ 試合(しあい)は勝(か)った<b>も同然(どうぜん)だ</b>。
  The verb goes into the plain past, because the point is that the result is already
  in place for practical purposes.

- ❌ この二(ふた)つの数字(すうじ)は<b>同然(どうぜん)だ</b>。 → ✅ この二(ふた)つの数字(すうじ)は<b>同(おな)じだ</b>。
  For literal identity use <code>同(おな)じ</code>.
  <code>同然(どうぜん)</code> exists precisely for cases where the two things are
  *not* identical.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は姉(あね)<b>も同然(どうぜん)な人(ひと)</b>だ。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は姉(あね)<b>同然(どうぜん)の人(ひと)</b>だ。
  <code>同然(どうぜん)</code> modifies a following noun with `の`, not `な`.

- ❌ 先輩(せんぱい)の資料(しりょう)は役(やく)に立(た)たない<b>も同然(どうぜん)ですね</b>。 → ✅ 先輩(せんぱい)の資料(しりょう)は少(すこ)し情報(じょうほう)が足(た)りないようですね。
  The polite ending does not blunt the content. Saying someone's work may as well not
  exist is a dismissal however you conjugate it.

## Practice

**1.** 荷物(にもつ)はあと一箱(ひとはこ)だけだから、引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの準備(じゅんび)は終(お)わった____だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

終(お)わった<b>も同然(どうぜん)</b>だ

One box is not zero boxes, and that is exactly the gap the pattern waves away. Note
the past tense <code>終(お)わった</code>: the state is being treated as already
reached.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 毎日(まいにち)こんなに残業(ざんぎょう)していたら、会社(かいしゃ)で暮(く)らしている（も同然(どうぜん)だ・ようなものだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会社(かいしゃ)で暮(く)らしている<b>ようなものだ</b>。

This is a figure of speech about how the days feel, not a report on where the speaker
sleeps, so <code>ようなものだ</code> is the fit.
<code>も同然(どうぜん)</code> would push it towards a literal claim about the
speaker's living arrangements.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The shop sold me the display model for next to nothing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

店(みせ)は展示品(てんじひん)をただ<b>同然(どうぜん)</b>で売(う)ってくれた。

<code>ただ同然(どうぜん)で</code> is the set phrase for "for next to nothing" -
note that `も` drops out of this one, and that

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)とは十年(じゅうねん)会(あ)っていないので、もう他人(たにん)も同然(どうぜん)な関係(かんけい)になってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)とは十年(じゅうねん)会(あ)っていないので、もう他人(たにん)<b>同然(どうぜん)の関係(かんけい)</b>になってしまった。
"I haven't seen him in ten years, so we've ended up practically strangers."

Before a noun the form is <code>同然(どうぜん)の</code>, never
❌<code>同然(どうぜん)な</code>. With a familiar noun like
<code>他人(たにん)</code> the `も` is usually dropped as well.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この地区(ちく)のバスは一日(いちにち)二本(にほん)しかない。
(b) この地区(ちく)のバスは無(な)い<b>も同然(どうぜん)だ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the timetable. Two buses a day is a fact, and a listener can draw their
own conclusions from it.

(b) draws the conclusion for them: for anyone trying to get about, a service that
thin does not count as a service. It is stronger, it is clearly the speaker's
judgement rather than the timetable's, and it invites agreement - which is why it
belongs in a complaint, a letter to the council or a conversation about moving away.

</details>

# ～に恥じない

**Meaning** · worthy of the name; bringing no shame to ~ - measures something against the standard a name, title or tradition sets, and finds it lives up to it

Read <code>に恥(は)じない</code>. The verb is
<code>恥(は)じる</code>, "to feel shame before something", so the literal sense is
"need not feel ashamed in front of the name". Japanese pays this compliment through a
negative: not *magnificent*, but *nothing to be ashamed of before the standard it
carries* - which is why it sounds dignified rather than gushing.

## Formation

The noun in front of `に` names the standard: a title, a name, a tradition, an award,
or the speaker's own conscience.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun + noun | ～に恥(は)じない + 名詞(めいし) | 王者(おうじゃ) → 王者(おうじゃ)の名(な)に恥(は)じない試合(しあい) |
| As predicate | …は～に恥(は)じない | その働(はたら)きは代表(だいひょう)の名(な)に恥(は)じない |
| Polite | ～に恥(は)じません | 伝統(でんとう)に恥(は)じません |
| Conscience / oneself | 良心(りょうしん)／自分(じぶん) + に恥(は)じない | 良心(りょうしん)に恥(は)じない判断(はんだん) |
| Fuller form | ～に恥(は)じることのない + 名詞(めいし) | 名(な)に恥(は)じることのない仕事(しごと) |
| Literary | ～に恥(は)じぬ + 名詞(めいし) | 名(な)に恥(は)じぬ生(い)き方(かた) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun before `に` must be something that **confers an
obligation to live up to it**: 名(な), 名前(なまえ), 伝統(でんとう),
代表(だいひょう), 王者(おうじゃ), 社名(しゃめい), 看板(かんばん),
賞(しょう), 良心(りょうしん). An ordinary noun will not do -
❌<code>値段(ねだん)に恥(は)じない味(あじ)</code> fails, because a price
imposes no honour. Say
<code>値段(ねだん)にふさわしい味(あじ)</code> instead.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading. <code>恥(は)じる</code> puts only は on the kanji; the じ
is okurigana, so the negative is <code>恥(は)じない</code> - はじない, not
はじじない. The noun <code>恥(はじ)</code> ("shame") does take はじ on the kanji
alone, which is what causes the confusion.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
王者(おうじゃ)の名(な)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>戦(たたか)いぶりだった。
It was a performance worthy of the champion's name.
</pre>

<pre>
伝統(でんとう)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>演奏(えんそう)を聞(き)かせてくれた。
They gave us a performance worthy of the orchestra's tradition.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)代表(だいひょう)の名(な)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>態度(たいど)で大会(たいかい)に臨(のぞ)みます。
We will approach the tournament in a manner worthy of the national team.
</pre>

<pre>
これからも社名(しゃめい)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>製品(せいひん)をお届(とど)けしてまいります。
We will go on delivering products worthy of the name we bear.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)はできなかったが、全力(ぜんりょく)を尽(つ)くし、自分(じぶん)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>試合(しあい)ができた。
We didn't win, but we gave everything and played a match I need not be ashamed of.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)の口癖(くちぐせ)は、良心(りょうしん)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>仕事(しごと)をしろ、というものだった。
My grandfather's constant refrain was to do work you can square with your conscience.
</pre>

<pre>
受賞者(じゅしょうしゃ)は、この賞(しょう)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>研究(けんきゅう)を続(つづ)けていきたいと語(かた)った。
The recipient said he hoped to go on doing research worthy of the award.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)百年(ひゃくねん)の看板(かんばん)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>味(あじ)を、三代(さんだい)にわたって守(まも)り続(つづ)けてきた。
Three generations have kept up a flavour worthy of a hundred-year-old sign over the door.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Formal, positive and slightly ceremonial.** Its habitat is award citations, company
statements and slogans, sports commentary, speeches at openings and retirements,
school and team mottos, and the paragraph of a cover letter where you say what you
intend to be. It is producible - unlike the genuinely archaic patterns at this level,
a learner can and should use it in those settings - but it is out of place in
ordinary conversation. Said about a packed lunch or a tidy desk it is mock-heroic,
and Japanese speakers do use it that way as a joke.

The negative construction matters. Because the praise is delivered as "brought no
shame", the compliment is **measured**: it certifies that a standard was met, rather
than raving. That is exactly what makes it usable about an institution you belong to
or about yourself. <code>自分(じぶん)に恥(は)じない仕事(しごと)をしたい</code>
is a modest, serious thing to say, where a direct boast would be impossible.

Two directions of use are worth separating. Looking outward, it **praises**: someone
else's work lived up to the name. Looking inward, it **pledges**:
<code>～に恥(は)じない～をしてまいります</code> is standard in a formal
undertaking, and the person saying it is accepting the standard rather than claiming
to have met it already.

Take care with the affirmative. <code>恥(は)じる</code> on its own means "to feel
ashamed of", so <code>名(な)に恥(は)じる行為(こうい)</code> is an act that
*disgraces* the name - the exact opposite of what a learner reaching for a compliment
intends. The pattern taught here is negative and stays negative.

## Contrast

### ～に恥(は)じない vs ～にふさわしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ふさわしい</code> = **suitable, appropriate**, and takes
almost any noun. <code>に恥(は)じない</code> = **lives up to an honour**, and takes
only nouns that carry one.

</div>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)<b>にふさわしい</b>服装(ふくそう)で来(き)てください。
Please come dressed appropriately for an interview.
</pre>

<pre>
老舗(しにせ)の名(な)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>もてなしだった。
The hospitality was worthy of a long-established house.
</pre>

Swap them and the difference is audible. <code>面接(めんせつ)に恥(は)じない服装(ふくそう)</code>
would suggest the interview has a dignity your clothes might disgrace, which is
overblown; <code>老舗(しにせ)の名(な)にふさわしいもてなし</code> is perfectly
correct but flatter, a matter of fit rather than of honour.
<code>ふさわしい</code> is also ordinary spoken Japanese, where
<code>に恥(は)じない</code> keeps its ceremonial edge.

### ～に恥(は)じない vs ～に値(あたい)する

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に値(あたい)する</code> says something **deserves** a
response - praise, attention, examination. <code>に恥(は)じない</code> says it
**meets** a standard already attached to it.

</div>

<pre>
その提案(ていあん)は検討(けんとう)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。
That proposal deserves consideration.
</pre>

<pre>
その提案(ていあん)は会社(かいしゃ)の理念(りねん)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>ものだ。
That proposal is one that does credit to the company's principles.
</pre>

The nouns each takes tell them apart. <code>値(あたい)する</code> is followed by
what the thing has earned (賞賛(しょうさん), 注目(ちゅうもく),
一読(いちどく)); <code>に恥(は)じない</code> is preceded by the name it must
honour. They are also structurally different - <code>値(あたい)する</code> is a
verb ending the sentence, while <code>に恥(は)じない</code> most often sits in
front of a noun.

### ～に恥(は)じない vs ～に応(こた)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に応(こた)える</code> answers **what people expect of
you**. <code>に恥(は)じない</code> answers **what a name demands**, whether anyone
is watching or not.

</div>

<pre>
ファンの期待(きたい)<b>に応(こた)える</b>活躍(かつやく)を見(み)せた。
He performed in a way that answered the fans' expectations.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)チームの名(な)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>戦(たたか)い方(かた)だった。
They played in a way worthy of the champions' name.
</pre>

Expectations belong to people and can be disappointed;
<code>期待(きたい)に恥(は)じない</code> is not idiomatic, because expectation
is not an honour to be shamed before. Conversely a name can be disgraced in an empty
stadium. This is also why <code>良心(りょうしん)に恥(は)じない</code> works and
❌<code>良心(りょうしん)に応(こた)える</code> does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 名(な)に<b>恥(は)じる</b>仕事(しごと)をしたい。 → ✅ 名(な)に<b>恥(は)じない</b>仕事(しごと)をしたい。
  Without the negative the sentence says you want to do work that disgraces the name.
  The pattern is negative by construction.

- ❌ 遅刻(ちこく)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>態度(たいど) → ✅ 社会人(しゃかいじん)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>態度(たいど)
  What precedes `に` has to be a name or standard that confers honour, not an event
  or a failing.

- ❌ 期待(きたい)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>結果(けっか) → ✅ 期待(きたい)<b>に応(こた)える</b>結果(けっか)
  Expectations are met, not honoured. Reserve
  <code>恥(は)じない</code> for names, titles, traditions and conscience.

- ❌ この弁当(べんとう)は母(はは)の名(な)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>。 → ✅ さすが母(はは)の弁当(べんとう)だ。
  Grammatical, and unintentionally funny. The register calls for an institution, a
  title or an occasion, not a lunchbox.

## Practice

**1.** 創立(そうりつ)五十年(ごじゅうねん)の歴史(れきし)____恥(は)じない大会(たいかい)にしたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

歴史(れきし)<b>に</b>恥(は)じない大会(たいかい)

The standard being lived up to is marked with `に`. Fifty years of history is exactly
the kind of noun the pattern wants - something that imposes an obligation on the
people who inherit it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 今日(きょう)の会議(かいぎ)には、場(ば)（にふさわしい・に恥(は)じない）服装(ふくそう)で来(き)てください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

場(ば)<b>にふさわしい</b>服装(ふくそう)で来(き)てください。

This is about what suits the occasion, which is <code>ふさわしい</code>.
<code>に恥(は)じない</code> would treat the meeting room as an honour that your
clothes might disgrace - far too grand, and slightly comic.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "We intend to keep making bread worthy of the name our shop has built up."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

店(みせ)が築(きず)いてきた名前(なまえ)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>パンを作(つく)り続(つづ)けてまいります。

The name is the standard, so it takes `に`, and
<code>まいります</code> matches the formal pledging register the pattern belongs to.
<code>看板(かんばん)に恥(は)じない</code> would work equally well for a shop.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この時計(とけい)は値段(ねだん)に恥(は)じない品質(ひんしつ)だと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この時計(とけい)は値段(ねだん)<b>にふさわしい</b>品質(ひんしつ)だと思(おも)います。
"I think this watch is of a quality that matches its price."

A price sets no standard of honour, so there is nothing for the quality to be
ashamed of before it. Fit is <code>ふさわしい</code>. Had the sentence named the
maker - <code>この工房(こうぼう)の名(な)に恥(は)じない品質(ひんしつ)</code>
- the original pattern would have been right.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一生(いっしょう)懸命(けんめい)働(はたら)きます。
(b) 会社(かいしゃ)の名(な)<b>に恥(は)じない</b>仕事(しごと)をします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) promises effort. It is sincere, ordinary and says nothing about the standard the
effort is aimed at.

(b) accepts an external measure: there is a name, it has a reputation, and the
speaker undertakes not to fall below it. It is markedly more formal - the sentence of
a joining ceremony or a company statement rather than of a chat with a colleague -
and it commits the speaker to a result, not just to trying hard.

</details>

# ～がましい

**Meaning** · smacking of ~; with the unwelcome air of ~ - says that behaviour has the flavour of something people dislike being on the receiving end of

<code>がましい</code> makes い-adjectives out of a **short, closed list** of stems,
and almost every word it makes is an accusation. It does not say the person *is*
doing the thing; it says their manner *smells* of it - the help that feels forced on
you, the favour you are made to feel grateful for, the explanation that sounds like
an excuse. Getting this wrong insults people, so treat the list as vocabulary and do
not invent members.

## Formation

The stems are fixed. Learn these; there is no rule that generates new ones.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb stem: 押(お)しつけ | 押(お)しつけがましい | 押(お)しつけがましい親切(しんせつ) - pushy, forced on you |
| Verb stem: 恩(おん)着(き)せ | 恩(おん)着(き)せがましい | 恩(おん)着(き)せがましい態度(たいど) - making you owe them |
| Verb phrase: 言(い)い訳(わけ) | 言(い)い訳(わけ)がましい | 言(い)い訳(わけ)がましく聞(き)こえる - excuse-making |
| Verb stem: 差(さ)し出(で) | 差(さ)し出(で)がましい | 差(さ)し出(で)がましいようですが - presumptuous |
| Noun: 未練(みれん) | 未練(みれん)がましい | 未練(みれん)がましい態度(たいど) - unable to let go |
| する-noun: 説教(せっきょう), 催促(さいそく) | 説教(せっきょう)がましい | 説教(せっきょう)がましい口調(くちょう) - preachy |
| Noun: 恨(うら)み | 恨(うら)みがましい | 恨(うら)みがましい目(め) - reproachful |
| Positive exception: 晴(は)れ | 晴(は)れがましい | 晴(は)れがましい舞台(ぶたい) - proud, in the public eye |
| Conjugation (い-adjective) | ～がましく／～がましくない／～がましさ | 押(お)しつけがましくない言(い)い方(かた) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this is **not a productive suffix**. You cannot build
❌<code>優(やさ)しがましい</code> or
❌<code>親切(しんせつ)がましい</code> the way you can build
<code>子供(こども)っぽい</code> from any noun you like. If the word is not on the
list, the ending you want is `っぽい`, `めいた`, `じみた` or a plain adjective.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの人(ひと)の親切(しんせつ)は少(すこ)し<b>押(お)しつけがましい</b>。
There's something a bit overbearing about the way he is kind to people.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>恩(おん)着(き)せがましい</b>言(い)い方(かた)はやめてほしい。
I wish he wouldn't put it in a way that makes you owe him.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>言(い)い訳(わけ)がましく</b>聞(き)こえるかもしれませんが、電車(でんしゃ)が一時間(いちじかん)止(と)まっていました。
This may sound like an excuse, but the trains were stopped for an hour.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>差(さ)し出(で)がましい</b>ようですが、一言(ひとこと)よろしいでしょうか。
Forgive me for speaking out of turn, but might I say one thing?
</pre>

<pre>
別(わか)れた相手(あいて)の話(はなし)ばかりするのは<b>未練(みれん)がましい</b>と友人(ゆうじん)に言(い)われた。
A friend told me that going on about my ex was just clinging to the past.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)の話(はなし)は、長(なが)くなると<b>説教(せっきょう)がましく</b>なりがちだ。
When the boss talks for a while, it tends to turn preachy.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>催促(さいそく)がましく</b>ならないように、一言(ひとこと)だけ添(そ)えてメールを送(おく)った。
I sent the email with one line added, taking care not to sound as though I were chasing them.
</pre>

<pre>
このような<b>晴(は)れがましい</b>席(せき)にお招(まね)きいただき、光栄(こうえい)に存(ぞん)じます。
It is an honour to be invited to so proud an occasion.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise these words; produce almost none of them.** They live in novels and
essays, in criticism, in complaints about people, and in the careful phrases of
business email. What a learner must not do is treat
<code>がましい</code> as a suffix and build new words with it - the result is not
merely odd, it is unintelligible, because each of these is an established word with
its own history.

**Every member but one is disparaging.** That is the point of the page. Applied to a
person in front of you, <code>押(お)しつけがましい</code>,
<code>恩(おん)着(き)せがましい</code> and
<code>説教(せっきょう)がましい</code> are not descriptions but insults - they
accuse the listener of a small, mean way of behaving, and they are the sort of thing
said behind someone's back or during an argument. A learner who reaches for
<code>押(お)しつけがましい</code> thinking it means "helpful in a strong way" will
do real damage. If you mean someone is a little forceful, say
<code>少(すこ)し強引(ごういん)ですね</code>; if you mean they are talkative
about advice, say <code>アドバイスが多(おお)いですね</code>.

The exception is <code>晴(は)れがましい</code>, which is positive: a
<code>晴(は)れがましい舞台(ぶたい)</code> is a proud, public, ceremonial
occasion. Even here there is a trace of self-consciousness - of being looked at - and
the word belongs to speeches and formal writing rather than to conversation.

Two of the list *are* safe for a learner, and they are safe because they are aimed at
**yourself**. <code>差(さ)し出(で)がましいようですが…</code> is a standard
humble preface before offering an opinion or help where you might not have standing,
and it is genuinely useful in a Japanese office.
<code>言(い)い訳(わけ)がましいのですが…</code> disarms your own excuse before
you make it. Both work by accusing yourself first, which is why they soften rather
than sting. Turned outward -
<code>差(さ)し出(で)がましいですね</code> to a colleague - they become sharp.

## Contrast

### ～がましい vs ～っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `っぽい` is the **productive** ending: attach it to almost any
noun or stem and be understood. <code>がましい</code> is a **closed list of set
words**. When you want to coin something, `っぽい` is the answer.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は言(い)い訳(わけ)<b>っぽかった</b>。
His explanation sounded like an excuse.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は少(すこ)し<b>言(い)い訳(わけ)がましかった</b>。
His explanation was a little excuse-making.
</pre>

Here both exist and the difference is register: `っぽい` is casual and offhand,
<code>がましい</code> is written and more considered - and a shade more severe,
because it names a manner rather than a resemblance. But the symmetry stops at this
one pair. <code>子供(こども)っぽい</code>, <code>安(やす)っぽい</code>,
<code>水(みず)っぽい</code> have no <code>がましい</code> counterparts, and
inventing them is the mistake this page exists to prevent.

### ～がましい vs ～じみた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both disparage.
<code>じみた</code> says something **looks like** the thing named -
<code>子供(こども)じみた</code>, <code>狂気(きょうき)じみた</code>.
<code>がましい</code> says someone's **conduct towards you** has the flavour of it.

</div>

<pre>
大(おお)の大人(おとな)が<b>子供(こども)じみた</b>ことで争(あらそ)っている。
Grown adults, squabbling over something childish.
</pre>

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)ってくれるのはありがたいが、やり方(かた)が<b>押(お)しつけがましい</b>。
I'm grateful for the help, but the way it's offered is overbearing.
</pre>

<code>じみた</code> compares an appearance with a category, and the subject can be a
thing, an act or an atmosphere. <code>がましい</code> nearly always concerns how
someone treats other people, which is why its words cluster around favours, advice,
excuses and demands - the social acts that can be done badly.

### ～がましい vs ～めいた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>めいた</code> is **neutral**: it reports a hint of
something with no criticism attached. <code>がましい</code> criticises by
construction.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>冗談(じょうだん)めいた</b>口調(くちょう)で本音(ほんね)を言(い)った。
He said what he really thought in a joking sort of tone.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>説教(せっきょう)がましい</b>口調(くちょう)で同(おな)じ話(はなし)を繰(く)り返(かえ)した。
He repeated the same speech in a preachy tone.
</pre>

Only the second is a complaint. If what you want to say is "there was something X
about it", with no verdict, <code>めいた</code> or <code>のような</code> is the
ending - <code>謎(なぞ)めいた微笑(ほほえ)み</code>,
<code>皮肉(ひにく)めいた言(い)い方(かた)</code>. Reaching for
<code>がましい</code> instead turns an observation into an accusation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 優(やさ)し<b>がましい</b>人(ひと) → ✅ おせっかいな人(ひと)
  You cannot coin new members. Only the established stems -
  押(お)しつけ, 恩(おん)着(き)せ, 言(い)い訳(わけ),
  差(さ)し出(で), 未練(みれん), 説教(せっきょう), 催促(さいそく),
  恨(うら)み, 晴(は)れ - take this ending.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)は<b>押(お)しつけがましい</b>ですね。（to the person, or to a colleague about them） → ✅ 課長(かちょう)は少(すこ)し熱心(ねっしん)すぎるところがありますね。
  This is an insult, not a description. Even said behind someone's back it is a
  serious charge; said to their face it ends the conversation.

- ❌ <b>恩(おん)着(き)せがましいの</b>人(ひと) → ✅ <b>恩(おん)着(き)せがましい</b>人(ひと)
  These are い-adjectives. They modify a noun directly - no `の`, no `な` - and
  conjugate normally: ～がましく, ～がましかった, ～がましくない.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は<b>晴(は)れがましい</b>天気(てんき)ですね。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は晴(は)れやかな天気(てんき)ですね。
  <code>晴(は)れがましい</code> has nothing to do with weather. It describes a
  proud public occasion and the slight self-consciousness of standing in the light.

- ❌ 部下(ぶか)に「<b>差(さ)し出(で)がましい</b>よ」と言(い)う。 → ✅ 自分(じぶん)について「<b>差(さ)し出(で)がましい</b>ようですが」と言(い)う。
  This one is a humble word about your own presumption. Aimed at a subordinate it
  becomes a rebuke for overstepping.

## Practice

**1.** 手伝(てつだ)ってもらったのに、あとで何度(なんど)もその話(はなし)をされて、____がましいと感(かん)じた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>恩(おん)着(き)せ</b>がましいと感(かん)じた

Being reminded repeatedly of a favour is the definition of
<code>恩(おん)着(き)せがましい</code> - the help was real, but it is being
banked and drawn on. Note that the complaint is about the *manner*, not about whether
the help happened.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)の部屋(へや)は白(しろ)い家具(かぐ)ばかりで、病院(びょういん)（がましい・っぽい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

病院(びょういん)<b>っぽい</b>。

There is no word ❌<code>病院(びょういん)がましい</code>, and there never could
be: the ending does not attach freely, and it describes how someone treats people,
not what a room resembles. Free coining belongs to `っぽい`.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite so that it is usable in a Japanese office: 先輩(せんぱい)、その言(い)い方(かた)は<b>押(お)しつけがましい</b>です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先輩(せんぱい)、自分(じぶん)のペースでやってみたいのですが、よろしいでしょうか。

The original is a direct accusation aimed upward, and no amount of です will rescue
it. The repair is to drop the judgement entirely and state what you want instead -
which is how the same disagreement is normally handled.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)はいつも親切(しんせつ)がましく人(ひと)を助(たす)けている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)はいつも親切(しんせつ)に人(ひと)を助(たす)けている。
Or, if criticism was intended: ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)の親切(しんせつ)は少(すこ)し<b>押(お)しつけがましい</b>。

❌<code>親切(しんせつ)がましい</code> is not a word - the ending cannot be
attached at will. And note the trap in the original: because everything in this
family disparages, a coined <code>がましい</code> would read as an attack even
though <code>親切(しんせつ)</code> is a compliment.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 遅(おく)れた理由(りゆう)を説明(せつめい)した。
(b) <b>言(い)い訳(わけ)がましく</b>聞(き)こえるかもしれませんが、と前置(まえお)きして理由(りゆう)を説明(せつめい)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports that an explanation was given.

(b) shows the speaker managing how the explanation will land. By naming the
suspicion first - that this is an excuse dressed as a reason - they take the charge
out of the listener's mouth, which is the standard Japanese way of making an excuse
acceptable. It is also one of the very few contexts in which a learner should
produce a <code>がましい</code> word at all, because the accusation is aimed at
themselves.

</details>

# ～放題

**Meaning** · as much as you like; left to run wild - marks something as unrestrained, either because the restraint was paid off or because nobody bothered

Read <code>放題(ほうだい)</code>. The kanji is 放(ほう) "to let loose", and that
single idea splits into two very different halves. Attached to verbs of consuming or
using, it is the language of menus and price plans:
<code>食(た)べ放題(ほうだい)</code>, "all you can eat". Attached to verbs of
neglect or of doing as one pleases, it is a **complaint**:
<code>荒(あ)れ放題(ほうだい)</code>, "left to fall apart";
やりたい放題(ほうだい), "doing whatever they liked, and no one stopped them".

## Formation

The base is the ます-stem, or the たい-form when the point is that someone did as they
pleased. The result is always a **noun**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb ます-stem (paid service) | ～放題(ほうだい) | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べ放題(ほうだい) |
| Verb ます-stem (left unchecked) | ～放題(ほうだい) | 荒(あ)れる → 荒(あ)れ放題(ほうだい) |
| Verb たい-form | ～たい放題(ほうだい) | 言(い)う → 言(い)いたい放題(ほうだい) |
| する-verbs | し放題(ほうだい)／したい放題(ほうだい) | 使用(しよう) → 使用(しよう)し放題(ほうだい) |
| Modifying a noun | ～放題(ほうだい)の + 名詞(めいし) | 伸(の)び放題(ほうだい)の草(くさ) |
| As predicate | ～放題(ほうだい)だ／です | 部屋(へや)は散(ち)らかし放題(ほうだい)だ |
| Established nouns | 食(た)べ／飲(の)み／使(つか)い／乗(の)り + 放題(ほうだい) | 二時間(にじかん)飲(の)み放題(ほうだい) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading never voices: ほうだい, not ❌ぼうだい, in every compound. And
the result is a **noun**, so it takes だ, の and が like any other -
<code>食(た)べ放題(ほうだい)の店(みせ)</code>,
<code>食(た)べ放題(ほうだい)が付(つ)いている</code> - and can never be
conjugated as a verb.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この店(みせ)は二時間(にじかん)食(た)べ<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>で3000円(えん)です。
This restaurant is 3,000 yen for two hours of all you can eat.
</pre>

<pre>
お飲(の)み物(もの)は飲(の)み<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>となっております。
Drinks are included on an all-you-can-drink basis.
</pre>

<pre>
月(つき)980円(えん)で、雑誌(ざっし)が読(よ)み<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>。
980 yen a month, and you can read as many magazines as you like.
</pre>

<pre>
空(あ)き家(や)の庭(にわ)は、草(くさ)が伸(の)び<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>だった。
The garden of the empty house had been left to run to weeds.
</pre>

<pre>
何年(なんねん)も人(ひと)が住(す)まず、家(いえ)の中(なか)は荒(あ)れ<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>になっていた。
Nobody had lived there for years, and inside the house had been left to fall apart.
</pre>

<pre>
責任(せきにん)を負(お)わない人(ひと)ほど、会議(かいぎ)では言(い)いたい<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>言(い)う。
The less responsibility a person carries, the more freely they talk in meetings.
</pre>

<pre>
権力(けんりょく)を握(にぎ)った一族(いちぞく)は、十年(じゅうねん)にわたってやりたい<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>だったという。
The family that seized power did exactly as it pleased for ten years, by all accounts.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)たちが帰(かえ)ったあとの居間(いま)は、おもちゃが散(ち)らかし<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>だった。
After the children went home the living room was left strewn with toys.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise the suffix; produce only the fixed compounds.** Everything below sorts
into two piles, and only one of them is a learner's to use.

**The service compounds are ordinary vocabulary.** 食(た)べ放題(ほうだい),
飲(の)み放題(ほうだい), 乗(の)り放題(ほうだい),
使(つか)い放題(ほうだい), 読(よ)み放題(ほうだい),
かけ放題(ほうだい) are nouns you will meet on menus, rail passes and phone
contracts, and asking <code>飲(の)み放題(ほうだい)は付(つ)きますか</code>
is perfectly normal Japanese. Learn them as words.

**The suffix behind them is not yours to use freely.** In advertising copy it is
semi-productive inside one frame - a flat fee that buys unlimited use of something -
which is where 学(まな)び放題(ほうだい) and 見(み)放題(ほうだい) come
from. Outside that frame, coining is where learners go wrong. ❌
<code>週末(しゅうまつ)は寝(ね)放題(ほうだい)だ</code> for "I can sleep as
much as I like at the weekend" reads like a hotel advertisement. The ordinary ways to
say it are <code>好(す)きなだけ寝(ね)られる</code> and
<code>いくらでも寝(ね)ていい</code>, and those are what a learner should produce.

**The other pile is a judgement, and a hard one.**
荒(あ)れ放題(ほうだい), 伸(の)び放題(ほうだい),
散(ち)らかし放題(ほうだい) accuse someone of neglect;
やりたい放題(ほうだい) and 言(い)いたい放題(ほうだい) accuse someone of
acting without restraint because nobody could stop them. They are the vocabulary of
newspaper criticism, of complaints about landlords and governments and badly behaved
guests, and of narration describing an abandoned place. Said about a person to their
face, やりたい放題(ほうだい) is close to calling them a tyrant, and no politeness
ending will soften it - <code>やりたい放題(ほうだい)ですね</code> to a manager is
not a comment on their autonomy, it is an accusation. If you mean someone works
freely, say <code>自由(じゆう)に進(すす)めていらっしゃいますね</code>.

So: recognise the whole pattern, buy the 食(た)べ放題(ほうだい), and leave the
coining and the accusations to native speakers.

## Contrast

### ～放題(ほうだい) vs ～っぱなし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `っぱなし` = **one action was not undone** (the light left on,
the window left open). <code>放題(ほうだい)</code> = **a state was allowed to run
to its extreme** over time.

</div>

<pre>
エアコンを<b>つけっぱなし</b>で出(で)かけてしまった。
I went out and left the air conditioning on.
</pre>

<pre>
手入(てい)れをしないので、庭(にわ)は荒(あ)れ<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>だ。
Nobody looks after it, so the garden has gone completely wild.
</pre>

`っぱなし` points at a single unfinished action and is everyday spoken Japanese -
置(お)きっぱなし, 出(だ)しっぱなし, 立(た)ちっぱなし.
<code>放題(ほうだい)</code> points at the result of long neglect and sounds
stronger and more literary. A light left on all night is
<code>つけっぱなし</code>; a house left empty for a decade is
<code>荒(あ)れ放題(ほうだい)</code>.

### ～放題(ほうだい) vs 好(す)きなだけ・いくらでも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this is the swap to remember.
<code>好(す)きなだけ</code> and <code>いくらでも</code> are the **ordinary,
productive** ways to say "as much as you like". <code>放題(ほうだい)</code>
outside its fixed compounds is advertising language.

</div>

<pre>
遠慮(えんりょ)しないで、<b>好(す)きなだけ</b>取(と)ってください。
Don't hold back - take as much as you want.
</pre>

<pre>
このパスがあれば、市内(しない)のバスは乗(の)り<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>です。
With this pass you can ride the city buses as much as you like.
</pre>

The first is what you say to a guest at your table; the second is what the tourist
office prints. Try to say the first sentence with
<code>放題(ほうだい)</code> - ❌<code>取(と)り放題(ほうだい)です</code> - and
you have turned your dinner into a buffet promotion.

### ～たい放題(ほうだい) vs ～ままに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ままに</code> (思(おも)うままに, なすがままに) is
**neutral or literary**: as one wishes, as it happens.
<code>たい放題(ほうだい)</code> adds that **nobody restrained them**, and
condemns it.

</div>

<pre>
定年後(ていねんご)は思(おも)う<b>ままに</b>絵(え)を描(か)いて暮(く)らしている。
Since retiring he has lived painting as he pleases.
</pre>

<pre>
管理人(かんりにん)がいないのをいいことに、住民(じゅうみん)はやり<b>たい放題(ほうだい)</b>だった。
With no caretaker on site, the residents did whatever they liked.
</pre>

Swap them and the retiree becomes a menace while the residents become a picture of
quiet freedom. <code>思(おも)うままに</code> is admiring;
<code>やりたい放題(ほうだい)</code> is an indictment, and the giveaway is usually
in the clause before it - some absence of supervision that the sentence is blaming.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>放題(ほうだい)</b> → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>
  The base is the ます-stem: 食(た)べ, 飲(の)み, 使(つか)い,
  乗(の)り. Not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は焼肉(やきにく)を食(た)べ<b>放題(ほうだい)しました</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は食(た)べ<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>の焼肉屋(やきにくや)に行(い)きました。
  It is a noun, not a verb. You go to a
  <code>食(た)べ放題(ほうだい)の店(みせ)</code>, or the meal
  <code>は食(た)べ放題(ほうだい)でした</code>.

- ❌ 土曜日(どようび)は家(いえ)で寝(ね)<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>だ。 → ✅ 土曜日(どようび)は家(いえ)で好(す)きなだけ寝(ね)られる。
  Coining outside the paid-service frame produces advertising copy about your own
  Saturday. Use <code>好(す)きなだけ</code> or <code>いくらでも</code>.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)はやりたい<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>ですね。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は自由(じゆう)に進(すす)めていらっしゃいますね。
  やりたい放題(ほうだい) says a person acts unchecked because no one can stop
  them. Aimed at anyone present, it is an accusation.

- ❌ 髪(かみ)が伸(の)び<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>で、うらやましいです。 → ✅ 髪(かみ)が長(なが)くてうらやましいです。
  <code>伸(の)び放題(ほうだい)</code> means unkempt, not enviably long. The
  neglect branch never compliments.

## Practice

**1.** 一年(いちねん)以上(いじょう)誰(だれ)も手入(てい)れをしていないので、生(い)け垣(がき)は伸(の)び____になっている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

伸(の)び<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>になっている

Long neglect plus an unchecked result is the criticising branch of the pattern. Note
the clue in the first half: sentences with this branch almost always name the absence
of care that allowed it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 温泉(おんせん)に入(はい)った後(あと)、冷(つめ)たい水(みず)を（飲(の)み放題(ほうだい)・好(す)きなだけ）飲(の)んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

冷(つめ)たい水(みず)を<b>好(す)きなだけ</b>飲(の)んだ。

<code>飲(の)み放題(ほうだい)</code> is a noun naming a service you have paid
for; it cannot describe how much you personally drank.
<code>好(す)きなだけ</code> is the ordinary adverbial way to say "as much as I
liked".

</details>

**3.** Where would you expect to read each of these? (a) 一泊(いっぱく)二食(にしょく)付(つ)き、ドリンク<b>飲(の)み放題(ほうだい)</b>。 (b) 工場(こうじょう)は閉鎖(へいさ)され、敷地(しきち)は荒(あ)れ<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>のまま残(のこ)された。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is hotel or travel-agency copy - a plan with two meals and unlimited drinks. It
is commercial vocabulary and completely neutral.

(b) is journalism or narration: a closed factory, a site nobody maintains. The same
suffix here carries neglect and implied blame, and the sentence would sit in a news
report or a novel rather than in conversation.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この旅館(りょかん)では温泉(おんせん)に入(はい)る放題(ほうだい)できます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この旅館(りょかん)では温泉(おんせん)に何度(なんど)でも入(はい)れます。
Or, in advertising style: ✅ 温泉(おんせん)入(はい)り<b>放題(ほうだい)</b>。

Two problems: the base must be the ます-stem, not
<code>入(はい)る</code>, and <code>放題(ほうだい)</code> is a noun and cannot
be followed by <code>できます</code>. In ordinary speech the natural repair drops
the pattern altogether.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は自由(じゆう)に意見(いけん)を言(い)っている。
(b) 彼(かれ)は<b>言(い)いたい放題(ほうだい)</b>言(い)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is neutral and can be praise: he speaks his mind, and an organisation where that
is possible is a healthy one.

(b) says he says whatever he likes because nobody holds him to account - the
responsibility is missing, and the speaker resents it. It belongs to a complaint
about a colleague, a commentator or a politician, and it is not a sentence to use
about someone who can hear you.

</details>
