# N1 - Only, even, and not even one


---

# ～すら・～ですら

**Meaning** · even - names the one case you would least expect to qualify, and leaves you to draw the conclusion about everything else

`すら` picks out a minimum, an extreme, a floor. Say
<code>水(みず)すら飲(の)めなかった</code> and you have not merely reported the
absence of water: you have said that if even *water* was out of reach, nothing
else needs listing. It is the written, harder-edged relative of `さえ`, which the
reader already has from N3.

## Formation

`すら` is a focus particle and attaches to nouns. The `で` in `ですら` comes from
the copula, and shows up mainly when the noun is the one doing something.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (subject; が drops) | Noun + すら / ですら | 専門家(せんもんか)が → 専門家(せんもんか)ですら |
| Noun (object; を drops) | Noun + すら | 名前(なまえ)を → 名前(なまえ)すら |
| Noun + に | ～にすら | 親(おや)に → 親(おや)にすら |
| Noun + と・から | ～とすら / ～からすら | 家族(かぞく)と → 家族(かぞく)とすら |
| Verb (て-form) | ～てすら | 見(み)る → 見(み)てすらいない |
| Polite predicate | ～すら + ません | 挨拶(あいさつ)すらしません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>見(み)てすらいない</code> is possible but uncommon; with a verb
base, `さえ` is the ordinary choice (<code>見(み)てさえいない</code>). Treat
`すら` as a noun particle and you will not go wrong.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)は日本人(にほんじん)<b>ですら</b>間違(まちが)えることがある。
Even Japanese people get kanji wrong sometimes.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しくて、昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)<b>すら</b>食(た)べていない。
I have been so busy I have not even had lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は自分(じぶん)の名前(なまえ)<b>すら</b>書(か)けなかった。
He could not even write his own name.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)のことは、家族(かぞく)に<b>すら</b>話(はな)していません。
I have not told even my own family about the accident.
</pre>

<pre>
その村(むら)には当時(とうじ)、電気(でんき)<b>すら</b>通(とお)っていなかった。
Back then that village did not even have electricity.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)した選手(せんしゅ)<b>ですら</b>、本番(ほんばん)の前(まえ)は手(て)が震(ふる)えるそうだ。
Apparently even the athlete who won gets shaking hands before the real thing.
</pre>

<pre>
紙(かみ)が貴重(きちょう)だった時代(じだい)には、役所(やくしょ)<b>ですら</b>使(つか)い終(お)わった封筒(ふうとう)を裏返(うらがえ)して使(つか)っていた。
In the days when paper was precious, even government offices turned used envelopes inside out and used them again.
</pre>

<pre>
この機能(きのう)がなぜ動(うご)くのか、設計(せっけい)した本人(ほんにん)に<b>すら</b>説明(せつめい)できないというのだから、手(て)の付(つ)けようがない。
Not even the person who designed it can explain why this feature works, so there is nothing we can do with it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`すら` is **written and formal**. It belongs to editorials, reports, essays,
literary narration and to speech that is consciously careful - a lecture, a
statement to the press, a serious argument between adults. In relaxed conversation
the same job is done by `さえ` or by plain `も`, and a learner who reaches for
`すら` over coffee will sound as though they are reading aloud.

That formality is the main thing separating it from `さえ` in most sentences.
`すら` is also a shade more absolute: it does not soften, and it pairs very
naturally with a negative predicate, where the sentence becomes a flat statement of
how far short of the minimum something fell.

`ですら` and bare `すら` are not two patterns. With a subject noun both are
available, and `ですら` is the more emphatic and the more common of the two;
<code>大人(おとな)ですら難(むずか)しい</code> lands harder than
<code>大人(おとな)すら難(むずか)しい</code>. Where the noun is the object,
`ですら` is not an option - <code>名前(なまえ)すら</code>, not
❌<code>名前(なまえ)ですら</code>.

One structural point worth memorising: **`すら` has no conditional use.** `さえ`
famously does - <code>お金(かね)さえあれば</code>, "if only I had money" - and
`すら` cannot be substituted there under any circumstances.

## Contrast

### ～すら vs ～さえ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register: `さえ` is neutral and works
in speech, `すら` is bookish. And only `さえ` forms the conditional `さえ～ば`.

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)<b>さえ</b>あれば、もっといいものが作(つく)れたのに。
If only I had had the time, I could have made something better.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)の時間(じかん)<b>すら</b>与(あた)えられなかった。
We were not even given time to prepare.
</pre>

Swap them in the first sentence and it collapses: ❌<code>時間(じかん)すらあれば</code>
is not Japanese. In the second, `さえ` would be perfectly grammatical and simply
sound less severe. So the substitution runs one way only - `すら` can always be
replaced by `さえ`, but `さえ` cannot always be replaced by `すら`.

### ～すら vs ～も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `も` says "this one too". `すら` says "this one, of all
things", and expects you to extend the judgement to everything else.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>も</b>その話(はなし)を知(し)らなかった。
My younger brother did not know about it either.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)<b>ですら</b>その話(はなし)を知(し)らなかった。
Not even his family knew about it.
</pre>

The first adds one more name to a list. The second sets up a scale - if the people
closest to him were in the dark, obviously nobody else had heard. `すら` always
implies that scale, which is why it fails on an unremarkable item:
<code>コンビニですら傘(かさ)が買(か)える</code> is odd, because a convenience
store is exactly where you would expect to buy an umbrella.

### ～すら vs ～まで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すら` marks a **floor** that was not reached. `まで` marks a
**ceiling** that surprisingly was: things went as far as this.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)<b>まで</b>会場(かいじょう)に来(き)てくれた。
Even the department head came along to the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
担当者(たんとうしゃ)<b>ですら</b>会場(かいじょう)に来(こ)なかった。
Not even the person in charge came to the venue.
</pre>

Both translate as "even", and the two sentences point in opposite directions. `まで`
runs upward and suits good news and escalation; `すら` runs downward to the minimum
and sits most comfortably with a negative. That is also why
<code>部長(ぶちょう)ですら来(き)てくれた</code> sounds wrong-footed - it treats
the boss's attendance as a bare minimum rather than as a bonus.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 時間(じかん)<b>すらあれば</b>終(お)わったのに。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)<b>さえあれば</b>終(お)わったのに。
  There is no <code>すら～ば</code> conditional. This is the commonest error with the
  pattern, because `さえ` and `すら` get taught as plain synonyms.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が<b>すら</b>反対(はんたい)した。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>ですら</b>反対(はんたい)した。
  `が` cannot stand in front of `すら`. Drop it, and use `ですら` if you want the
  emphasis back.

- ❌ 水(みず)を<b>すら</b>飲(の)めなかった。 → ✅ 水(みず)<b>すら</b>飲(の)めなかった。
  `を` drops in the same way. `に`, `と` and `から` do not - they stay, and `すら`
  follows them.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)のパーティー、ケーキ<b>すら</b>おいしかったよ。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)のパーティー、ケーキ<b>も</b>おいしかったよ。
  Two faults at once: the item is not an extreme case, and the register is chatty.
  `すら` in a sentence ending in よ is a mismatch a listener will notice.

## Practice

**1.** 専門家(せんもんか)____この現象(げんしょう)を説明(せつめい)できていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

専門家(せんもんか)<b>ですら</b>この現象(げんしょう)を説明(せつめい)できていない。

Specialists are the one group you would expect to manage it, so they are the
extreme case, and a subject noun takes `ですら` for full emphasis. Bare
<code>専門家(せんもんか)すら</code> is also correct, just flatter.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この書類(しょるい)（すら・さえ）揃(そろ)えば、手続(てつづ)きは今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に終(お)わります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この書類(しょるい)<b>さえ</b>揃(そろ)えば

The sentence is a conditional, and `すら` has no conditional form at all. Whenever
there is a `ば` or a `たら` downstream, the particle has to be `さえ`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in a written register: "In the first month after the earthquake, the hospital did not even have enough bandages."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

地震(じしん)から1か月(げつ)の間(あいだ)、病院(びょういん)には包帯(ほうたい)<b>すら</b>足(た)りなかった。

Bandages are the most basic thing a hospital keeps, so they are the floor that was
not reached - textbook `すら`. The written register the question asks for is exactly
what `すら` supplies over `さえ`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新入(しんにゅう)社員(しゃいん)がすらその規則(きそく)を知(し)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新入(しんにゅう)社員(しゃいん)<b>ですら</b>その規則(きそく)を知(し)っている。

Two things. `が` has to go - it cannot precede `すら`. And with the `が` gone, the
subject noun wants `ですら`, which also brings out the sense: the newest hire is the
last person you would expect to know the rule, so everyone else certainly does.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 課長(かちょう)<b>まで</b>手伝(てつだ)いに来(き)てくれた。
(b) 課長(かちょう)<b>ですら</b>手伝(てつだ)いに来(き)た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is warm. `まで` runs up a scale and lands on a surprising high point: help came
from further up the office than anyone expected, and the speaker is grateful.

(b) reads as criticism of somebody else. `ですら` sets the section chief as the
minimum case - if even he turned up, the people who should have been there have no
excuse. The same fact, and a completely different thing to say out loud.

</details>

# ～だに

**Meaning** · even; so much as - "merely to do this is already X", or, with a negative, "did not do it even a little"

`だに` is an Old Japanese particle meaning "even, at least". In the modern language
it is not a live piece of grammar: it survives inside a short list of frozen
expressions - <code>想像(そうぞう)するだに</code>,
<code>微動(びどう)だにしない</code>, <code>予想(よそう)だにしなかった</code> -
and almost nowhere else. Learn to read it; write `さえ`, `だけでも` or
<code>少(すこ)しも～ない</code> instead.

## Formation

Two shapes, and each is limited to particular words.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary form) + emotive predicate | ～するだに + 恐(おそ)ろしい etc. | 想像(そうぞう)する → 想像(そうぞう)するだに恐(おそ)ろしい |
| Verb (dictionary form) | 聞(き)く・思(おも)い出(だ)す + だに | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)くだに腹立(はらだ)たしい |
| する-noun + だに + negative する | ～だにしない | 予想(よそう) → 予想(よそう)だにしなかった |
| Noun (fixed set) + だに + negative する | ～だにしない | 微動(びどう) → 微動(びどう)だにしない |
| Continuative | ～だにせず | 微動(びどう) → 微動(びどう)だにせず |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the whole productive inventory is roughly this:
<code>想像(そうぞう)するだに</code>, <code>聞(き)くだに</code>,
<code>考(かんが)えるだに</code>, <code>思(おも)い出(だ)すだに</code>,
<code>想像(そうぞう)だにしない</code>, <code>予想(よそう)だにしない</code>,
<code>微動(びどう)だにしない</code>, <code>一顧(いっこ)だにしない</code>.
Anything you build outside that list will read as a mistake rather than as
elevated style.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
想像(そうぞう)する<b>だに</b>恐(おそ)ろしい。
It is terrifying merely to imagine.
</pre>

<pre>
石(いし)の像(ぞう)は微動(びどう)<b>だに</b>しなかった。
The stone statue did not stir in the slightest.
</pre>

<pre>
聞(き)く<b>だに</b>腹立(はらだ)たしい話(はなし)である。
It is a story that angers you just to hear it.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな結末(けつまつ)になろうとは、予想(よそう)<b>だに</b>しなかった。
That it would end like this I had not foreseen for a moment.
</pre>

<pre>
大臣(だいじん)は記者(きしゃ)たちの抗議(こうぎ)を一顧(いっこ)<b>だに</b>せず、車(くるま)に乗(の)り込(こ)んだ。
The minister got into the car without so much as a glance at the reporters' protests.
</pre>

<pre>
あの夜(よる)の叫(さけ)び声(ごえ)は、思(おも)い出(だ)す<b>だに</b>胸(むね)が苦(くる)しくなる。
The screaming of that night is painful even to recall.
</pre>

<pre>
焼(や)け野原(のはら)に立(た)っていた人々(ひとびと)は、この街(まち)が半世紀(はんせいき)後(ご)にこうなるとは想像(そうぞう)<b>だに</b>しなかっただろう。
The people who stood in those burnt-out fields can hardly have imagined that the city would look like this half a century later.
</pre>

<pre>
証人(しょうにん)は裁判長(さいばんちょう)に何度(なんど)促(うなが)されても口(くち)を開(ひら)かず、微動(びどう)<b>だに</b>せずに前(まえ)を見(み)つめていた。
However often the presiding judge prompted him, the witness would not speak, and sat staring ahead without moving a muscle.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not produce it.** `だに` is a survival from classical
Japanese, and outside the fixed expressions listed above it is dead. Where you
will actually meet it: literary narration, memoirs and war writing, newspaper
commentary, courtroom and historical reporting, and the occasional deliberately
grand line in a speech. In ordinary modern Japanese, say
<code>想像(そうぞう)するだけでも恐(おそ)ろしい</code> and
<code>少(すこ)しも動(うご)かなかった</code>.

There is one real exception, and it is worth knowing. <b>微動(びどう)だにしない</b> **has escaped into everyday speech.** People say it about a jammed door, a frozen
screen or a stubborn opinion, and it does not sound literary at all - it sounds
like a set phrase, which is what it is.
<code>一顧(いっこ)だにしない</code> and
<code>予想(よそう)だにしなかった</code> stay firmly in writing.

The verb use leans towards **unpleasant** feeling. The predicates that follow it
are 恐(おそ)ろしい, 身(み)の毛(け)がよだつ, 腹立(はらだ)たしい and
胸(むね)が痛(いた)む - things you would rather not dwell on. That is a strong tendency rather than an absolute rule, but a cheerful
<code>想像(そうぞう)するだに楽(たの)しい</code> is rare enough that a reader
would notice it.

One more surface point: `だに` is almost never written in kanji, and there is no
polite form of it. The politeness of the sentence lives in the final predicate -
<code>微動(びどう)だにしませんでした</code> is perfectly possible.

## Contrast

### ～だに vs ～だけでも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけでも` is the modern equivalent of the verb use. Same
meaning, no archaic flavour, and it attaches to any verb you like.

</div>

<pre>
想像(そうぞう)する<b>だに</b>恐(おそ)ろしい光景(こうけい)だった。
It was a scene terrible merely to imagine.
</pre>

<pre>
想像(そうぞう)する<b>だけでも</b>恐(おそ)ろしい光景(こうけい)だった。
It was a scene that was terrible just to imagine.
</pre>

The second is what you should say. The first is what you should be able to read.
The gap between them is not meaning but distance: `だに` makes the sentence sound
like narration, and in the middle of a conversation that distance registers as
theatrical.

### ～だにしない vs 少(すこ)しも～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for the negative use, the modern replacement is
<code>少(すこ)しも</code>, <code>まったく</code> or
<code>夢(ゆめ)にも</code> plus a negative.

</div>

<pre>
ドアは微動(びどう)<b>だに</b>しなかった。
The door did not budge an inch.
</pre>

<pre>
ドアは<b>まったく</b>動(うご)かなかった。
The door did not move at all.
</pre>

For <code>予想(よそう)だにしなかった</code> the everyday version is
<code>夢(ゆめ)にも思(おも)わなかった</code>, and for
<code>一顧(いっこ)だにしない</code> it is
<code>見向(みむ)きもしない</code>. Learning those three pairs is most of what
this page is for.

### ～だに vs ～すら・～さえ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すら` and `さえ` attach freely to nouns and are alive. `だに`
attaches to a closed list and is not. They are not interchangeable in either
direction.

</div>

<pre>
水(みず)<b>すら</b>飲(の)まずに歩(ある)き続(つづ)けた。
He kept walking without so much as drinking water.
</pre>

<pre>
想像(そうぞう)する<b>だに</b>恐(おそ)ろしい道(みち)のりだった。
It was a journey terrible even to imagine.
</pre>

❌<code>水(みず)だに飲(の)まずに</code> is not Japanese, and
❌<code>想像(そうぞう)するすら</code> is not either. `すら` wants a noun; `だに`
wants one of its handful of licensed partners. Where both are possible - and there
is almost nowhere - `だに` is the older and heavier of the two.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 音(おと)<b>だに</b>聞(き)こえなかった。 → ✅ 音(おと)<b>すら</b>聞(き)こえなかった。
  `だに` does not attach to ordinary nouns. Only the frozen partners -
  微動(びどう), 一顧(いっこ), 予想(よそう), 想像(そうぞう) - take it.

- ❌ 想像(そうぞう)する<b>だに</b>しなかった。 → ✅ 想像(そうぞう)<b>だに</b>しなかった。
  The two shapes are separate. Before the negative <code>しない</code> the base is
  the bare noun, not the verb.

- ❌ 微動(びどう)だに<b>する</b>。 → ✅ 微動(びどう)だに<b>しない</b>。
  The negative use only exists as a negative. There is no affirmative
  <code>微動(びどう)だにする</code>.

- ❌ そのケーキ、想像(そうぞう)する<b>だに</b>おいしそうだね。 → ✅ そのケーキ、想像(そうぞう)する<b>だけでも</b>おいしそうだね。
  Wrong on two counts: the register is casual, and the feeling is a pleasant one.
  Both cut against what `だに` is for.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence, and what would the everyday
version be? 首相(しゅしょう)は野党(やとう)の要求(ようきゅう)を一顧(いっこ)<b>だに</b>しなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A newspaper political report, or a commentary column - it is written register, and
`だに` is doing the work of a cold, formal verdict.

Everyday: 首相(しゅしょう)は野党(やとう)の要求(ようきゅう)を<b>まったく相手(あいて)にしなかった</b>、
or 見向(みむ)きもしなかった.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 当時(とうじ)の人々(ひとびと)は、人(ひと)が月(つき)に立(た)つ日(ひ)が来(く)るとは想像(そうぞう)<b>だに</b>しなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

当時(とうじ)の人々(ひとびと)は、人(ひと)が月(つき)に立(た)つ日(ひ)が来(く)るとは<b>夢(ゆめ)にも思(おも)わなかった</b>。

<code>想像(そうぞう)だにしなかった</code> and
<code>夢(ゆめ)にも思(おも)わなかった</code> are the literary and the everyday
version of one idea. Note that <code>来(く)る</code> keeps its dictionary form
before とは - the archaism is in `だに` alone.

</details>

**3.** Choose, and say why: この机(つくえ)、重(おも)すぎて（微動(びどう)だにしない・一顧(いっこ)だにしない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

微動(びどう)<b>だにしない</b>

<code>微動(びどう)</code> is a tiny movement, so the phrase means "will not budge",
which is what a heavy desk does. <code>一顧(いっこ)</code> is a single glance, and
that phrase means to ignore a person or a request. This is also the one `だに`
expression that is fine in casual speech.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 戦争(せんそう)の話(はなし)は聞(き)くだに嬉(うれ)しくなる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 戦争(せんそう)の話(はなし)は聞(き)く<b>だに</b>恐(おそ)ろしい。

The construction is sound; the emotion is not. <code>聞(き)くだに</code> introduces
something you would rather not have heard, so it needs a predicate like
恐(おそ)ろしい or 腹立(はらだ)たしい. Pairing it with 嬉(うれ)しい reads as a
sentence written by someone matching a pattern rather than meaning it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in register between:
(a) 彼(かれ)の裏切(うらぎ)りは予想(よそう)<b>だに</b>しなかった。
(b) 彼(かれ)の裏切(うらぎ)りは予想(よそう)もしていなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The content is identical. (a) is written Japanese: memoir, novel, a formal
statement read from paper. Spoken aloud in conversation it sounds staged.

(b) is what a person says. Note that the modern version also lets you use
<code>ていない</code> for the continuing state, which the frozen `だに` phrase
cannot - <code>予想(よそう)だにしていなかった</code> is possible but rare, and the
plain past is the shape you will actually see.

</details>

# ～たりとも

**Meaning** · not even one; not a single - takes the smallest possible quantity and denies even that

<code>たりとも</code> is the classical copula <code>たり</code> plus the concessive
<code>とも</code>: "even if it be one". What is left in modern Japanese is a
declaration form. It sits on <code>一(いち)</code> plus a counter, and the
predicate that follows is always a refusal, a denial or a prohibition -
<code>一日(いちにち)たりとも忘(わす)れない</code>, "not for one single day".
Recognise it; in ordinary Japanese say <code>一日(いちにち)も忘(わす)れない</code>.

## Formation

The number is essentially always <code>一(いち)</code>. The predicate must be
negative or prohibitive.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 一(いち) + counter | ～たりとも + negative | 一日(いちにち) → 一日(いちにち)たりとも休(やす)まない |
| 一(いち) + counter | ～たりとも + potential negative | 一分(いっぷん) → 一分(いっぷん)たりとも待(ま)てない |
| 一(いち) + counter | ～たりとも + prohibition | 一歩(いっぽ) → 一歩(いっぽ)たりとも引(ひ)くな |
| 一(いち) + counter | ～たりとも + わけにはいかない | 一人(ひとり) → 一人(ひとり)たりとも失(うしな)うわけにはいかない |
| 何人(なんぴと) | ～たりとも + prohibition | 何人(なんぴと) → 何人(なんぴと)たりとも立(た)ち入(い)るべからず |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the counters that actually occur are a short list:
一日(いちにち), 一瞬(いっしゅん), 一分(いっぷん), 一度(いちど),
一人(ひとり), 一円(いちえん), 一滴(いってき), 一歩(いっぽ),
一言(ひとこと). Watch the readings: 一人(ひとり) not いちにん, and
一分(いっぷん) with gemination. <code>何人(なんぴと)たりとも</code> is a frozen
legal-sounding phrase meaning "no person whatsoever"; it is also read
なんびと, and older texts have なにびと.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
一日(いちにち)<b>たりとも</b>忘(わす)れたことはない。
Not for a single day have I forgotten.
</pre>

<pre>
一分(いっぷん)<b>たりとも</b>遅(おく)れられない。
We cannot be even one minute late.
</pre>

<pre>
一瞬(いっしゅん)<b>たりとも</b>気(き)を抜(ぬ)いてはならない。
You must not let your concentration slip for so much as an instant.
</pre>

<pre>
何人(なんぴと)<b>たりとも</b>、許可(きょか)なくこの区域(くいき)に立(た)ち入(い)ることを禁(きん)ずる。
No person whatsoever may enter this area without permission.
</pre>

<pre>
預(あず)かった命(いのち)である。一人(ひとり)<b>たりとも</b>欠(か)けさせるわけにはいかない。
These are lives entrusted to us. We cannot afford to lose a single one.
</pre>

<pre>
砂漠(さばく)を越(こ)える間(あいだ)、水(みず)は一滴(いってき)<b>たりとも</b>無駄(むだ)にできなかった。
Crossing the desert, we could not waste even one drop of water.
</pre>

<pre>
公金(こうきん)を預(あず)かる立場(たちば)として、一円(いちえん)<b>たりとも</b>使途(しと)を曖昧(あいまい)にすることは許(ゆる)されません。
Entrusted as we are with public money, we cannot leave the use of a single yen unaccounted for.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)から四十年(よんじゅうねん)、父(ちち)が店(みせ)の鍵(かぎ)を開(あ)けなかった日(ひ)は一日(いちにち)<b>たりとも</b>なかった。
In the forty years since he founded it, there was not one single day my father failed to unlock the shop.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is a form to recognise, not to deploy.** Its natural homes are notices and
signage, contracts and regulations, formal apologies and pledges, courtroom and
parliamentary speech, coaches and commanders addressing a team or a unit, and
fiction that imitates any of those. Say it across a dinner table and it will land
as a joke, because it sounds like a vow. The everyday equivalent is
<code>一日(いちにち)も</code>, <code>一度(いちど)も</code>,
<code>少(すこ)しも</code> or <code>まったく</code> plus a negative.

Two hard structural facts. First, **the number is 一(いち)**. Japanese does not build
❌<code>三日(みっか)たりとも</code> or ❌<code>五人(ごにん)たりとも</code>; the
whole point is the smallest conceivable unit, and a larger number destroys it.
Second, **the predicate must be negative or prohibitive.** A positive sentence with
`たりとも` in it is simply broken.

The feeling the pattern carries is *resolve*. It is not neutral counting - it is a
speaker committing to something absolutely, or forbidding something absolutely. That
is why <code>一円(いちえん)たりとも</code> turns up in speeches about public money
and <code>一歩(いっぽ)たりとも引(ひ)かない</code> in speeches about
negotiations. When you meet it in a text, the question to ask is not "what does
this quantity mean" but "what is this person swearing to".

## Contrast

### ～たりとも vs 一(いち)～も～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `も` is the everyday form of the same idea. `たりとも` adds
solemnity and nothing else to the meaning.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)は一日(いちにち)<b>も</b>休(やす)まなかった。
I did not take a single day off last year.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)は一日(いちにち)<b>たりとも</b>休(やす)まなかった。
Not for one day last year did I rest.
</pre>

Identical facts. The first is a person reporting; the second is a person declaring.
If you are unsure which you want, you want the first - `も` is never wrong here,
whereas `たりとも` in a casual context always is.

### ～たりとも vs ～すら・～さえ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すら` and `さえ` pick out an extreme **item**. `たりとも`
denies a minimum **quantity**, and needs a number and a counter in front of it.

</div>

<pre>
水(みず)<b>すら</b>残(のこ)っていなかった。
There was not even water left.
</pre>

<pre>
水(みず)は一滴(いってき)<b>たりとも</b>残(のこ)っていなかった。
There was not one drop of water left.
</pre>

The first says the category was empty; the second measures the emptiness. That is
why ❌<code>水(みず)たりとも</code> fails - there is no quantity in it - and why
<code>一滴(いってき)すら</code> is fine but sounds less like a vow.

### ～たりとも vs ～といえども

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both come from the same classical concessive, but
`といえども` means "even though it is X" and takes any noun or clause.
`たりとも` is restricted to <code>一(いち)</code> plus a counter.

</div>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>といえども</b>間違(まちが)えることはある。
Even a specialist can be wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)<b>たりとも</b>間違(まちが)えたことはない。
He has never been wrong even once.
</pre>

`といえども` concedes a category and then contradicts it; `たりとも` counts to one
and stops there. They are cousins in origin and are not substitutable in either
direction.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 三日(みっか)<b>たりとも</b>休(やす)めない。 → ✅ 一日(いちにち)<b>たりとも</b>休(やす)めない。
  The numeral has to be <code>一(いち)</code>. Any larger quantity contradicts
  the whole point of the expression.

- ❌ 一日(いちにち)<b>たりとも</b>休(やす)んだ。 → ✅ 一日(いちにち)<b>たりとも</b>休(やす)まなかった。
  The pattern only exists with a negative or a prohibition. There is no affirmative
  use.

- ❌ 一人(いちにん)<b>たりとも</b>逃(に)がすな。 → ✅ 一人(ひとり)<b>たりとも</b>逃(に)がすな。
  A reading error rather than a grammar one, but a conspicuous one:
  一人(ひとり) and 二人(ふたり) keep their native readings.

- ❌ ごめん、一分(いっぷん)<b>たりとも</b>待(ま)てないんだけど。 → ✅ ごめん、一分(いっぷん)<b>も</b>待(ま)てないんだけど。
  Register clash. An apology, a contraction and a sentence-final んだけど do not
  belong in the same sentence as a formal vow.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this, and what is it doing? 何人(なんぴと)<b>たりとも</b>この線(せん)を越(こ)えてはならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A notice or a barrier sign - a construction site, a restricted zone, a courtroom -
or fiction imitating one. <code>何人(なんぴと)たりとも</code> is the frozen legal
phrase for "no person whatsoever", and the sentence is a prohibition addressed to
the world at large rather than to anyone in particular.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary spoken Japanese: 彼女(かのじょ)は練習(れんしゅう)を一度(いちど)<b>たりとも</b>休(やす)まなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)は練習(れんしゅう)を一度(いちど)<b>も</b>休(やす)まなかった。

Swap `たりとも` for `も` and the sentence becomes something a person would say
about a teammate. The meaning does not move at all; only the solemnity goes.

</details>

**3.** Fill in a counter that fits: 交渉(こうしょう)では、こちらから____たりとも譲(ゆず)るつもりはない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一歩(いっぽ)たりとも (also 一(いち)ミリたりとも in looser writing)

<code>一歩(いっぽ)も譲(ゆず)らない</code>, "not give an inch", is the standard
image for negotiation, and `たりとも` raises it to a declaration. Note the
gemination: 一歩(いっぽ), not ❌いちほ.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 一円(いちえん)たりとも大切(たいせつ)に使(つか)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 一円(いちえん)<b>たりとも</b>無駄(むだ)に<b>しません</b>。

The sentence is positive, and `たりとも` cannot live in a positive sentence. Turn the
promise around into a denial - "we will not waste even one yen" - and the pattern
works. Alternatively drop the pattern: <code>一円(いちえん)一円(いちえん)を大切(たいせつ)に使(つか)います</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一瞬(いっしゅん)<b>たりとも</b>目(め)を離(はな)さなかった。
(b) 一瞬(いっしゅん)<b>も</b>目(め)を離(はな)さなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both mean the speaker never looked away. (b) is a report: it could describe watching
a film, a child, or a football match, in any register.

(a) sounds like testimony or narration - a nurse describing a night at a bedside, a
witness in court, a line in a novel. The archaic particle frames the act as a duty
discharged without fail, which is more weight than most situations want.

</details>

# という - all; every

**Meaning** · every single one of them, without exception - said by repeating the noun on both sides of `という`

This is not the quotative `という` the reader already knows. Here the **same noun
appears twice**, and the effect is a sweep of the eye across the whole set:
<code>窓(まど)という窓(まど)</code> means every window there was, all of them,
none left out. Where `すべて` counts, this pattern *looks*.

## Formation

The two nouns must be identical. Nothing may come between them but `という`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (repeated) | Noun + という + Noun | 窓(まど) → 窓(まど)という窓(まど) |
| As subject | ～という～ + が | 店(みせ)という店(みせ)が閉(し)まっていた |
| As object | ～という～ + を | 棚(たな)という棚(たな)を探(さが)した |
| With a location particle | ～という～ + に | 壁(かべ)という壁(かべ)に貼(は)る |
| Compound noun | Compound + という + compound | 引(ひ)き出(だ)し → 引(ひ)き出(だ)しという引(ひ)き出(だ)し |
| Modified on the left only | Modifier + ～という～ | 近所(きんじょ)の店(みせ)という店(みせ) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the modifier goes in **front of the first noun only**.
<code>近所(きんじょ)の店(みせ)という店(みせ)</code> is right;
❌<code>店(みせ)という近所(きんじょ)の店(みせ)</code> is not.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
棚(たな)の<b>本(ほん)という本(ほん)</b>が床(ゆか)に落(お)ちていた。
Every last book on the shelves was down on the floor.
</pre>

<pre>
校舎(こうしゃ)の<b>窓(まど)という窓(まど)</b>が割(わ)れていた。
Every window in the school building was broken.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)の<b>店(みせ)という店(みせ)</b>を回(まわ)ったが、どこにも売(う)っていなかった。
I went round every shop in the neighbourhood, and nowhere had it.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)で、川沿(かわぞ)いの<b>家(いえ)という家(いえ)</b>が水(みず)に浸(つ)かった。
The typhoon flooded every single house along the river.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の<b>引(ひ)き出(だ)しという引(ひ)き出(だ)し</b>を開(あ)けてみたが、鍵(かぎ)は見(み)つからなかった。
I tried every drawer in the desk, but the key was not there.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)の受賞(じゅしょう)は、翌朝(よくあさ)の<b>新聞(しんぶん)という新聞(しんぶん)</b>の一面(いちめん)を飾(かざ)った。
Her award was on the front page of every newspaper the following morning.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)の<b>壁(かべ)という壁(かべ)</b>にポスターが貼(は)られ、白(しろ)い部分(ぶぶん)はほとんど残(のこ)っていなかった。
Posters had gone up on every wall in the hall, and hardly any bare surface was left.
</pre>

<pre>
退職(たいしょく)してからの父(ちち)は、町(まち)の<b>祭(まつ)りという祭(まつ)り</b>に顔(かお)を出(だ)すようになった。
Since retiring, my father has taken to turning up at every festival the town holds.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This one is **vivid rather than formal**. It reads as naturally in a friend's
account of a bad day as in a news report of a flood, and what it adds in both is
scale: the speaker is not counting but gesturing at the whole set at once. Because
of that it belongs with dramatic content - damage, exhaustive searching, saturation
coverage, obsessive habits - and it falls flat on a neutral fact. Nobody says
<code>机(つくえ)という机(つくえ)に椅子(いす)がある</code>.

There is a light note of exaggeration in it, and speakers know that. Saying
<code>店(みせ)という店(みせ)を回(まわ)った</code> after visiting four shops
is entirely normal Japanese; the pattern reports the effort, not an audit.

Two restrictions matter. The set has to be **plural** - there must be many windows
for <code>窓(まど)という窓(まど)</code> to mean anything, and a unique referent
kills it, which is why ❌<code>母(はは)という母(はは)</code> is nonsense.
And the noun is normally **concrete and countable**. Abstractions do occur, but they
tip into a different, older idiom: <code>人(ひと)という人(ひと)</code> works,
while <code>愛(あい)という愛(あい)</code> would just puzzle a reader.

Do not confuse the shape with the naming `という` that the same characters spell.
The test is mechanical: **if the two nouns are identical, it is this pattern; if
they differ, it is the ordinary quotative.**

## Contrast

### ～という～ vs すべての・あらゆる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すべての` states the total. `あらゆる` covers every *kind*.
`～という～` sweeps the eye over the set and carries feeling with it.

</div>

<pre>
市内(しない)の<b>すべての</b>信号(しんごう)が止(と)まった。
Every traffic light in the city stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
市内(しない)の<b>信号(しんごう)という信号(しんごう)</b>が止(と)まった。
Every last traffic light in the city went dead.
</pre>

The first is what a report says; the second is what somebody who was driving through
it says. `あらゆる` is different again - <code>あらゆる可能性(かのうせい)</code> is
every variety of possibility, and it happily takes abstract nouns, where
`～という～` mostly will not.

### ～という～ vs という (naming or quoting)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical nouns on both sides means "every". Different nouns
means "called" or "the fact that".

</div>

<pre>
富士(ふじ)<b>という</b>山(やま)は日本(にほん)に一(ひと)つしかない。
There is only one mountain called Fuji in Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りの<b>山(やま)という山(やま)</b>が雪(ゆき)をかぶっていた。
Every mountain around us was capped with snow.
</pre>

Same three characters, two unrelated grammars. In the first, `という` links a name to
a category; in the second it links a noun to itself. There is no ambiguity in
practice, because the repetition is impossible to miss.

### ～という～ vs どの～も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どの～も` says every member qualifies, one by one.
`～という～` says the whole set was hit at once.

</div>

<pre>
<b>どの</b>部屋(へや)<b>も</b>片付(かたづ)いていた。
Every one of the rooms was tidy.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>部屋(へや)という部屋(へや)</b>が散(ち)らかっていた。
Every room in the place was a mess.
</pre>

`どの～も` is neutral and works in any register, including a calm positive statement
like the first sentence above. `～という～` needs something worth exclaiming about,
which is why it gravitates to the second.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 家(いえ)という<b>建物(たてもの)</b>が流(なが)された。 → ✅ <b>家(いえ)という家(いえ)</b>が流(なが)された。
  The two nouns must be the same word. Change one of them and the sentence becomes
  the naming `という`, which means something else entirely.

- ❌ 私(わたし)の母(はは)という母(はは)が心配(しんぱい)している。 → ✅ 私(わたし)の母(はは)が心配(しんぱい)している。
  You only have one. The pattern needs a set large enough to sweep across.

- ❌ 窓(まど)という窓(まど)が一(ひと)つ割(わ)れた。 → ✅ 窓(まど)という窓(まど)が割(わ)れた。
  A singular quantity contradicts the pattern. Anything counting a subset has to go.

- ❌ 店(みせ)という近所(きんじょ)の店(みせ)を回(まわ)った。 → ✅ 近所(きんじょ)の店(みせ)という店(みせ)を回(まわ)った。
  Modifiers attach to the left-hand noun. Sliding one into the middle breaks the
  repetition that carries the meaning.

## Practice

**1.** 地震(じしん)のあと、棚(たな)の皿(さら)____皿(さら)が割(わ)れていた。

The noun is repeated on both sides of `という`, and the result is "every single

皿(さら) and is not repeated.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会場(かいじょう)の（すべての席(せき)・席(せき)という席(せき)）が埋(う)まり、立(た)っている人(ひと)まで出(で)た。

Both are grammatical, but the sentence goes on to a dramatic consequence - people

</details>

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 通(とお)りの<b>桜(さくら)という桜(さくら)</b>が満開(まんかい)だった。

The nouns have to match. As written, <code>木(き)という桜(さくら)</code> reads

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 机(つくえ)の引(ひ)き出(だ)しを全部(ぜんぶ)開(あ)けた。
(b) 机(つくえ)の引(ひ)き出(だ)しという引(ひ)き出(だ)しを開(あ)けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain report of a completed action: all the drawers, done. It says nothing
about how the speaker felt.

(b) puts you in the room. The repetition drags the action out, and what comes across
is the searching itself - pulling one drawer after another, increasingly desperate.
That is why (b) almost always leads into a result, usually a disappointing one.

</details>

# ほかに○○ない

**Meaning** · there is no other one - the alternatives have been looked for and there are none

`ほかに` is "elsewhere, besides", and the negative at the end of the clause closes
the door on it. The pattern is a statement about the **search**: someone has
considered the other candidates, the other routes, the other shops, and reports
back that nothing else qualifies. Depending on what is being ruled out, the result
is praise ("nobody else could do it") or resignation ("there is nothing else we can
try").

## Formation

<code>ほか</code> is a noun - it is written 他(ほか) or 外(ほか) in kanji, but
kana is the usual choice. A noun in front of it therefore needs `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing (bare) | ほかに + Noun + は + ない | ほかに方法(ほうほう)はない |
| Noun | Noun + の + ほかに ～ ない | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)のほかに適任者(てきにんしゃ)はいない |
| Animate noun | ほかに ～ は + いない | ほかに頼(たよ)れる人(ひと)はいない |
| Verb (plain) | Verb + ほかに ～ ない | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つほかに手(て)はない |
| Noun-modifying clause | Clause + ことは + ほかにない | 言(い)いたいことはほかにない |
| Polite | ～ほかに ～ ありません / いません | ほかに質問(しつもん)はありません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - use <code>ない</code> for things and <code>いない</code> for people. The
mistake ❌<code>彼(かれ)のほかに適任者(てきにんしゃ)はない</code> is common and
sounds odd in the same way "there is no such person available" would if you said
"there is not".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
すみません、<b>ほかに方法(ほうほう)はありません</b>。
I'm sorry, but there is no other way.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)を解(と)ける人(ひと)は、彼(かれ)の<b>ほかにいない</b>。
There is nobody but him who can solve this problem.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ほかに言(い)いたいことはない</b>ので、これで終(お)わります。
I have nothing else I want to say, so I will stop there.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)に出(で)られる人(ひと)は、私(わたし)の<b>ほかに一人(ひとり)もいなかった</b>。
Apart from me there was not a single person free to attend the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)が行(い)ってしまった以上(いじょう)、歩(ある)く<b>ほかに手(て)はない</b>。
Now that the last train has gone, there is nothing for it but to walk.
</pre>

<pre>
この味(あじ)を出(だ)せる店(みせ)は、市内(しない)には<b>ほかにない</b>と思(おも)う。
I don't think there is another shop in the city that can produce this flavour.
</pre>

<pre>
予算(よさん)が限(かぎ)られている以上(いじょう)、来年度(らいねんど)に先送(さきおく)りする<b>ほかに選択肢(せんたくし)はない</b>。
Given that the budget is limited, we have no option other than to postpone it to next year.
</pre>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)の近(ちか)い物件(ぶっけん)はいくつも見(み)たが、駅(えき)まで徒歩(とほ)三分(さんぷん)という点(てん)でここに勝(まさ)るものは<b>ほかになかった</b>。
We looked at several flats on similar terms, but nothing else beat this one for being three minutes from the station.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **register-neutral**. <code>ほかに方法(ほうほう)はない</code> is at
home in a boardroom, in a shop and between friends; the politeness lives entirely in
the ending you choose, <code>ない</code> or <code>ありません</code>. In writing you
may see it in kanji as 他(ほか) or, more old-fashionedly, 外(ほか), but kana is
the modern default and nothing is lost by it.

What the pattern adds over a plain negative is the sense of a **completed search**.
<code>いい店(みせ)がない</code> just says there is no good shop.
<code>ほかにいい店(みせ)はない</code> says you have been round them all and this is
the verdict. That is why it is so common in recommendations and in resigned
decisions, and why it sounds strange applied to something nobody would have looked
for: <code>ほかに空気(くうき)はない</code> is not a sentence with a use.

Note the small emphatic variants. <code>ほかに一人(ひとり)もいない</code> and
<code>ほかに何(なに)もない</code> put a counter or a question word in the middle
and harden the denial. And <code>ほかでもない</code>, which you will meet as a fixed
phrase - <code>ほかでもない君(きみ)の頼(たの)みだから</code>, "because it is
none other than you asking" - is built from the same 他(ほか) and means "precisely
this one", the positive side of the same coin.

## Contrast

### ほかに○○ない vs しか○○ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しか～ない` attaches to the thing that **remains**.
`ほかに～ない` denies that anything **else** exists. Same fact from two sides.

</div>

<pre>
残(のこ)っているのは、この一(ひと)つ<b>しかない</b>。
This one is all that's left.
</pre>

<pre>
使(つか)えそうなものは、これの<b>ほかになかった</b>。
There was nothing else that looked usable.
</pre>

`しか` is the everyday form and needs a specific item in front of it, which is why
it cannot open a sentence. `ほかに` can stand alone -
<code>ほかにありませんか</code>, "is there nothing else?" - and is the natural choice
when the other candidates, not the survivor, are the point.

### ほかに○○ない vs ～ほかない・～よりほかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほかに○○ない` says **no other thing exists**.
`～ほかない` says **there is nothing to do but** - it is about a course of action,
and always follows a verb.

</div>

<pre>
待(ま)つ<b>よりほかない</b>。
There's nothing to do but wait.
</pre>

<pre>
待(ま)つ以外(いがい)に、できることは<b>ほかにない</b>。
Apart from waiting, there is nothing else we can do.
</pre>

The two overlap heavily, and in the second sentence the difference is mostly one of
weight. Where they part company is that `～ほかない` needs a verb and expresses
obligation, while `ほかに○○ない` can take a noun -
<code>候補(こうほ)はほかにない</code> - and expresses absence.

### ほかに○○ない vs ～以外(いがい)に○○ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all but interchangeable. `以外に` is tighter and more written;
watch the particle, because `ほか` takes `の` after a noun and `以外` does not.

</div>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さん<b>以外(いがい)に</b>経験者(けいけんしゃ)はいません。
There is nobody with experience other than Yamada.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんの<b>ほかに</b>経験者(けいけんしゃ)はいません。
There is nobody with experience apart from Yamada.
</pre>

❌<code>山田(やまだ)さんほかに</code> and ❌<code>山田(やまだ)さんの以外(いがい)に</code>
are both wrong, and swapping the particles is the usual way learners get caught.
Meaning-wise, `以外に` is slightly the drier of the two and dominates contracts,
notices and academic prose.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)<b>ほかに</b>適任者(てきにんしゃ)はいない。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>のほかに</b>適任者(てきにんしゃ)はいない。
  <code>ほか</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it links with `の`. This is the
  single commonest slip, caused by interference from `以外に`, which takes no
  particle.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)のほかに適任者(てきにんしゃ)は<b>ない</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)のほかに適任者(てきにんしゃ)は<b>いない</b>。
  People take <code>いない</code>. Things take <code>ない</code>.

- ❌ ほかに方法(ほうほう)が<b>ある</b>。 → ✅ ほかに方法(ほうほう)が<b>ありますか</b>。
  In a plain affirmative, `ほかに` is simply the adverb "besides" and this is no
  longer the pattern. The pattern lives on the negative, or on a question expecting
  one.

- ❌ これ以外(いがい)に<b>ほかに</b>案(あん)はない。 → ✅ これ以外(いがい)に案(あん)はない。
  <code>以外(いがい)に</code> and <code>ほかに</code> say the same thing. Using
  both is a doubled negative frame that native speakers hear as clumsy.

## Practice

**1.** この仕事(しごと)を任(まか)せられるのは、田中(たなか)さん____ほかに____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

田中(たなか)さん<b>のほかにいない</b>。

The noun before <code>ほか</code> needs `の`, and because the missing item is a
person, the negative existence verb is <code>いない</code>, not <code>ない</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)には卵(たまご)（しか・ほかに）なかったので、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

卵(たまご)<b>しか</b>なかったので

`しか～ない` attaches directly to what survived - the eggs - and that is what the
sentence is about. To use `ほかに` you would have to rewrite it so the missing things
are the subject: <code>卵(たまご)以外(いがい)は何(なに)もなかった</code>, or
<code>卵(たまご)のほかには何(なに)もなかった</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've eaten ramen all over the country, and there is no bowl anywhere to beat this one."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

全国(ぜんこく)のラーメンを食(た)べ歩(ある)いてきたが、これに勝(まさ)るものは<b>ほかにない</b>。

The first clause supplies the completed search that `ほかに○○ない` presupposes,
which is exactly why the pattern fits here and a bare negative would not.
<code>食(た)べ歩(ある)く</code> is the idiomatic verb for eating your way round
a lot of places.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 急(いそ)いでいるので、タクシーで行(い)くほかにしかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 急(いそ)いでいるので、タクシーで行(い)く<b>ほかない</b>。
（or 行(い)く<b>しかない</b>）

Two patterns have been welded together. Pick one: <code>行(い)くほかない</code> or
<code>行(い)くしかない</code>, both meaning "there's nothing for it but to go by
taxi". <code>ほかに</code> plus <code>しか</code> in the same clause is not a
possible shape.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ほかに質問(しつもん)はありませんか。
(b) 質問(しつもん)はありませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) opens the floor: it is asked once, and it invites any question at all.

(a) can only be asked after at least one question has already been taken. `ほかに`
looks back at what has been covered and asks what is left over, so as the opening
line of a meeting it would leave the room briefly confused about what they had
missed.

</details>

# ただ○○のみだ

**Meaning** · that alone, and nothing besides - everything else has been stripped away and only this is left to do or to matter

`のみ` is the formal `だけ`, which the reader already has from N2. What `ただ` adds
in front of it is a **bracket**: it announces in advance that the sentence is about
to narrow to a single thing, and the `のみだ` at the end shuts the door. The result
is declarative and slightly grand - the sentence of someone summing up, deciding,
or resigning themselves.

## Formation

<code>ただ</code> is an adverb and simply sits at the front of the clause;
<code>のみ</code> is a particle attaching to a noun or to a plain-form verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain non-past) | ただ + Verb + のみだ | 待(ま)つ → ただ待(ま)つのみだ |
| Verb (nominalised) | ただ + Verb + ことのみだ | 祈(いの)る → ただ祈(いの)ることのみだった |
| Noun | ただ + Noun + のみだ | 結果(けっか) → ただ結果(けっか)のみだ |
| Noun (slogan shape) | ただ + Noun + あるのみ | 前進(ぜんしん) → ただ前進(ぜんしん)あるのみ |
| Written / formal | ただ ～ のみである | ただ事実(じじつ)のみである |
| Polite | ただ ～ のみです | ただ感謝(かんしゃ)するのみです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - adjectives sit awkwardly here. ❌<code>ただ静(しず)かなのみだ</code> is
not idiomatic; for an adjective, use <code>だけ</code> -
<code>ただ静(しず)かなだけだ</code>. Treat `のみ` as taking nouns and verbs.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あとは<b>ただ待(ま)つのみだ</b>。
All that is left now is to wait.
</pre>

<pre>
ここまで来(き)たら、<b>ただ進(すす)むのみです</b>。
Having come this far, we can only press on.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)は整(ととの)った。<b>ただ本番(ほんばん)あるのみ</b>。
Preparations are complete. All that remains is the performance itself.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)にできたのは、<b>ただ頭(あたま)を下(さ)げることのみだった</b>。
All I could do was bow my head.
</pre>

<pre>
この職(しょく)に求(もと)められているのは、<b>ただ正確(せいかく)さのみである</b>。
What this job asks for is accuracy and nothing else.
</pre>

<pre>
言葉(ことば)を尽(つ)くしても届(とど)かないのなら、<b>ただ黙(だま)って見守(みまも)るのみだ</b>。
If words will not reach him however many I use, there is nothing to do but watch in silence.
</pre>

<pre>
洪水(こうずい)のあと、住民(じゅうみん)にできたのは、<b>ただ家族(かぞく)の無事(ぶじ)を祈(いの)ることのみだった</b>。
After the flood, all the residents could do was pray that their families were safe.
</pre>

<pre>
議論(ぎろん)は尽(つ)くした。残(のこ)るは<b>ただ実行(じっこう)あるのみである</b>。
The argument has been had. What remains is simply to carry it out.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Formal, written, and declamatory.** This is the register of speeches, mottoes,
editorials, formal reports and captains addressing a team. It also turns up in
sports writing and in the closing line of an essay, because it makes a good curtain.
In ordinary conversation it is out of place, and a sentence like
❌<code>今日(きょう)はただ寝(ね)るのみだよ</code> collides with itself: the
final よ is chatting while the `のみだ` is making a speech.

The pattern has **two temperatures**, and context decides which you hear.
<code>ただ前(まえ)に進(すす)むのみ</code> is resolve - everything else has been
settled and only the action remains. <code>ただ待(ま)つのみだ</code> is
resignation - everything else is out of the speaker's hands. The grammar is the
same; the difference is whether the remaining thing is something you do or something
you endure.

<code>ただ ～ あるのみ</code> is worth learning as a unit. It takes a bare noun,
drops the copula entirely and ends the sentence dead:
<code>努力(どりょく)あるのみ</code>, <code>前進(ぜんしん)あるのみ</code>. This is
slogan Japanese - banners, training rooms, the last line of a pep talk - and it is
the one shape here that a learner can safely quote, because everybody recognises it
as a set phrase rather than as ordinary speech.

Finally, `ただ` can be dropped: <code>待(ま)つのみだ</code> is grammatical.
Keeping it makes the narrowing explicit and is much the more common choice, which is
why the pattern is taught as a pair.

## Contrast

### ただ○○のみだ vs ～だけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different register. `だけだ` is what people
say; `ただ○○のみだ` is what people write and declare.

</div>

<pre>
もう待(ま)つ<b>だけだ</b>ね。
Nothing to do but wait now, is there.
</pre>

<pre>
我々(われわれ)に残(のこ)されているのは、<b>ただ待(ま)つのみである</b>。
What is left to us is simply to wait.
</pre>

Both sentences describe the same evening. The first is two people in a waiting room;
the second is a paragraph in a novel about them. Note that `だけ` accepts adjectives
and casual endings, and `のみ` accepts neither comfortably.

### ただ○○のみだ vs ～しかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しかない` is about having **no choice**. `ただ○○のみだ` is
about **nothing else being left**, which is often the same situation seen with more
composure.

</div>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)が出(で)たので、歩(ある)く<b>しかない</b>。
The last train's gone, so we've no choice but to walk.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)は一(ひと)つ。<b>ただ歩(ある)き続(つづ)けるのみだ</b>。
There is one road. Simply to keep walking is all there is.
</pre>

`しかない` is neutral and can sound annoyed. `ただ○○のみだ` is composed, even
dignified, and that composure is most of what the writer is buying. Where `しかない`
grumbles, `ただ○○のみだ` accepts.

### ただ○○のみだ vs ～のみならず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same particle, opposite jobs. `のみだ` **closes** the set at
one item; `のみならず` **opens** it - "not only this, but also".

</div>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)を受(う)けたのは、<b>ただ一階(いっかい)のみだった</b>。
The damage was confined to the ground floor alone.
</pre>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)は一階(いっかい)<b>のみならず</b>二階(にかい)にも及(およ)んだ。
The damage was not confined to the ground floor but reached the first floor as well.
</pre>

Because the two look alike on the page, it is easy to read one as the other and get
the sentence exactly backwards. The tell is what follows: if another item comes
after `のみ`, it is `のみならず`; if the sentence ends, it is `のみだ`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ただ結果(けっか)<b>だのみだ</b>。 → ✅ ただ結果(けっか)<b>のみだ</b>。
  A noun attaches to <code>のみ</code> directly. The copula belongs at the end of the
  sentence, not in the middle of it.

- ❌ ただ待(ま)つ<b>だけのみだ</b>。 → ✅ ただ待(ま)つ<b>のみだ</b>。（or 待(ま)つ<b>だけだ</b>）
  `だけ` and `のみ` are the same word in two registers. Stacking them is like writing
  "only merely".

- ❌ この部屋(へや)はただ静(しず)か<b>なのみだ</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)はただ静(しず)か<b>なだけだ</b>。
  Adjectives do not take `のみ` naturally. If the thing being narrowed is a quality
  rather than a noun or an action, use `だけ`.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)はただ寝(ね)る<b>のみだよ</b>。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)はもう寝(ね)る<b>だけだよ</b>。
  Register clash. Sentence-final よ, ね and the contracted forms of speech do not go
  with a pattern whose whole flavour is formal declaration.

## Practice

**1.** 全力(ぜんりょく)で準備(じゅんび)してきた。あとは____信(しん)じる____だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あとは<b>ただ信(しん)じるのみ</b>だ。

The first clause establishes that everything else is finished, which is exactly the
set-up `ただ○○のみだ` needs. The verb goes in plain non-past form directly before
<code>のみ</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)の被害(ひがい)は農地(のうち)（のみだ・のみならず）、住宅(じゅうたく)にも広(ひろ)がった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

農地(のうち)<b>のみならず</b>

The sentence continues after the particle with a second item, so the set is being
opened, not closed. <code>のみだ</code> would have to end the sentence there and
would state the opposite of what the writer means.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in a written register: "The report contains no interpretation; it records the facts and nothing more."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この報告書(ほうこくしょ)に解釈(かいしゃく)は含(ふく)まれておらず、記(しる)されているのは<b>ただ事実(じじつ)のみである</b>。

<code>のみである</code> rather than <code>のみだ</code> because the sentence is
written prose; <code>である</code> is the register the pattern is happiest in.
記(しる)す is the verb for setting something down in a document.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 練習(れんしゅう)は終(お)わった。ただ本番(ほんばん)だあるのみ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 練習(れんしゅう)は終(お)わった。<b>ただ本番(ほんばん)あるのみ</b>。

<code>ただ ～ あるのみ</code> takes a bare noun. The inserted `だ` breaks the set
phrase, which is exactly the shape people recognise from banners and pep talks -
<code>努力(どりょく)あるのみ</code>, <code>前進(ぜんしん)あるのみ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう祈(いの)る<b>しかない</b>。
(b) 我々(われわれ)にできるのは<b>ただ祈(いの)ることのみである</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a person at the end of their options, and it can carry frustration - the
tone of "well, what else is there".

(b) is composed and public. It is the sentence of a spokesperson, a narrator or a
letter, and the formality reframes the same helplessness as something accepted
rather than complained about. The choice between them is a choice about how much
dignity the speaker wants the situation to have.

</details>

# ～までだ

**Meaning** · that is as far as it goes - either "then I will simply do X, and that's that", or, after a past tense, "I merely did X, nothing more"

The `まで` is the ordinary "as far as", used about the extent of an action rather
than a distance. Two readings fall out of that, and **the tense tells you which**.
Non-past: everything else has failed, so the speaker names a fallback and shrugs -
<code>歩(ある)くまでだ</code>, "then I'll walk, fine". Past: the speaker limits
what they did, usually in self-defence - <code>言(い)ったまでだ</code>, "I only
said it".

## Formation

The base is always a verb. `までのことだ` is the longer and slightly heavier form of
each.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain non-past) - fallback sense | ～までだ | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)くまでだ |
| Verb (plain non-past), weightier | ～までのことだ | 断(ことわ)る → 断(ことわ)るまでのことだ |
| Verb (た-form) - "merely did" sense | ～たまでだ | 言(い)った → 言(い)ったまでだ |
| Verb (た-form), weightier | ～たまでのことだ | 確認(かくにん)した → 確認(かくにん)したまでのことだ |
| Polite (either sense) | ～までです | 従(したが)った → 従(したが)ったまでです |
| Mid-sentence (either sense) | ～までで、… | 伝(つた)えたまでで、判断(はんだん)はしていない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - non-past = a plan you are falling back on. Past = a limit you
are placing on what you did. Get the tense wrong and the sentence means something
else entirely.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)がなければ、歩(ある)く<b>までだ</b>。
If there's no train, I'll simply walk.
</pre>

<pre>
事実(じじつ)を言(い)った<b>までです</b>。
I merely stated the facts.
</pre>

<pre>
責(せ)めているのではありません。確認(かくにん)した<b>までです</b>。
I am not criticising you. I was only checking.
</pre>

<pre>
断(ことわ)られたら、別(べつ)の会社(かいしゃ)に頼(たの)む<b>までのことだ</b>。
If they turn us down, we'll just ask another firm and that will be that.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)したところで命(いのち)を取(と)られるわけではない。また一(いち)から始(はじ)める<b>までだ</b>。
Even if it fails, nobody's going to kill me. I'll start again from scratch, that's all.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は上司(じょうし)の指示(しじ)に従(したが)った<b>までで</b>、判断(はんだん)に関(かか)わってはおりません。
I merely followed my superior's instructions; I had no part in the decision.
</pre>

<pre>
ここまで説明(せつめい)しても理解(りかい)していただけないのであれば、静(しず)かに身(み)を引(ひ)く<b>までです</b>。
If I cannot make you understand even after explaining this far, I shall simply withdraw quietly.
</pre>

<pre>
お礼(れい)をいただくようなことではありません。当然(とうぜん)のことをした<b>までのことです</b>。
This is nothing to thank me for. I only did what anyone would have done.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The two senses have different temperatures.

The **non-past fallback** sense is calm and a little tough. It is the voice of
someone who has just been told no and is not going to make a fuss about it:
<code>だめなら自分(じぶん)でやるまでだ</code>. Because it downplays the setback, it
can sound admirably unruffled, and it can also sound stubborn - the same sentence
does both, and tone decides. It almost always follows a conditional clause with
`なら`, `たら`, `ば` or `ても`, because the fallback needs something to fall back
from.

The **past** sense is defensive or modest. Its two great uses are deflecting blame
(<code>指示(しじ)に従(したが)ったまでです</code>) and deflecting thanks
(<code>当然(とうぜん)のことをしたまでです</code>). Both are careful, adult
Japanese; the polite <code>までです</code> is by far the more common ending, and the
whole shape belongs to workplaces, formal apologies, interviews and testimony rather
than to casual talk.

`までのことだ` is the same pattern with more ceremony. It adds finality - "and there
is nothing more to be said about it" - and appears more in writing and in prepared
speech than in the middle of a conversation.

A pragmatic warning about the past sense. Because it minimises, using it about
something the listener regards as serious will read as arrogant.
<code>正直(しょうじき)に言(い)ったまでです</code> after upsetting someone can
easily land as "I refuse to take responsibility for that." Native speakers use it
carefully, and so should you.

## Contrast

### ～たまでだ vs ～ただけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "I only did X". `ただけだ` is a plain statement of
quantity; `たまでだ` adds "and therefore you should not read anything more into it".

</div>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)を言(い)っ<b>ただけです</b>。
I was only joking.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)を言(い)っ<b>たまでです</b>。
I was merely making a joke - nothing was meant by it.
</pre>

The second is arguing a case. `だけ` counts what happened; `まで` draws a boundary
around it and pushes back against an interpretation. That is also why `ただけだ`
works happily in cheerful contexts and `たまでだ` almost never does.

### ～たまでだ vs ～に過(す)ぎない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にすぎない` **evaluates**, usually somebody else's action or a
fact: it is no more than X. `たまでだ` **defends**, and the subject is nearly always
the speaker.

</div>

<pre>
それは一(ひと)つの例(れい)<b>に過(す)ぎない</b>。
That is no more than a single example.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は例(れい)を挙(あ)げ<b>たまでです</b>。
I was simply giving an example.
</pre>

`にすぎない` takes nouns as well as verbs and is comfortable in analysis and
argument. `たまでだ` takes verbs only and is a personal move in a conversation - you
would not write it about a third party's conduct without sounding as though you were
speaking for them.

### ～までだ vs ～しかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しかない` means there is **no other option**, and often
grumbles. `までだ` means the speaker has **chosen** the fallback and is not troubled
by it.

</div>

<pre>
バスが来(こ)ないから、歩(ある)く<b>しかない</b>。
The bus isn't coming, so we've got no choice but to walk.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが来(こ)ないなら、歩(ある)く<b>までだ</b>。
If the bus isn't coming, we'll just walk, then.
</pre>

The facts are identical and the attitude is not. `しかない` reports a constraint;
`までだ` waves it away. That is why `までだ` sits so well after a threat or a refusal
and `しかない` does not: <code>クビにするなら辞(や)めるまでだ</code> is defiant,
where <code>辞(や)めるしかない</code> would be defeated.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)やり直(なお)し<b>たまでだ</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)やり直(なお)す<b>までだ</b>。
  The tense carries the meaning. A future fallback needs the plain non-past; the
  past form would claim you had already done it and were playing it down.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>までだ</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>だけだ</b>。
  The pattern takes verbs. Adjectives and nouns use `だけ`.

- ❌ 残(のこ)っているのは三人(さんにん)<b>までだ</b>。 → ✅ 残(のこ)っているのは三人(さんにん)<b>だけだ</b>。
  A noun before `までだ` is read as the ordinary "up to, as far as" - a limit on a
  scale, not the meaning taught here.

- ❌ すみません、皿(さら)を割(わ)っ<b>たまでです</b>。 → ✅ すみません、皿(さら)を割(わ)ってしまいました。
  Grammatically fine, pragmatically a disaster. Minimising damage you caused while
  apologising for it reads as refusing responsibility.

## Practice

**1.** 補助金(ほじょきん)が出(で)ないのであれば、自分(じぶん)たちで費用(ひよう)を出(だ)す____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自分(じぶん)たちで費用(ひよう)を出(だ)す<b>までだ</b>。（or <b>までのことだ</b>）

The `のであれば` clause sets up a fallback, so the non-past sense is wanted: if the
subsidy fails to come, we will simply pay ourselves. Note the plain non-past
<code>出(だ)す</code> - a past form here would change the meaning completely.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 感謝(かんしゃ)などとんでもありません。私(わたし)は当然(とうぜん)のことを（する・した）までです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

当然(とうぜん)のことを<b>した</b>までです。

The speaker is deflecting thanks for something already done, which is the past
sense. This is one of the two set uses of `～たまでだ`, and the polite
<code>までです</code> is what makes it sound gracious rather than curt.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I wasn't accusing anyone. I was simply reporting what I saw."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

誰(だれ)かを責(せ)めていたわけではありません。見(み)たことを報告(ほうこく)した<b>までです</b>。

Classic defensive use: the speaker limits their own action to head off an
interpretation. <code>わけではありません</code> in the first sentence and
<code>までです</code> in the second are a very common pairing, because both are ways
of denying an inference.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 失敗(しっぱい)しても大(たい)したことはない。もう一度(いちど)挑戦(ちょうせん)したまでのことだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 失敗(しっぱい)しても大(たい)したことはない。もう一度(いちど)挑戦(ちょうせん)<b>するまでのことだ</b>。

The first sentence looks forward to a possible failure, so the second has to be a
fallback plan and needs the non-past. As written, it says the speaker has already
tried once and is playing that down - which contradicts the sentence before it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 思(おも)ったことを言(い)っ<b>ただけです</b>。
(b) 思(おも)ったことを言(い)っ<b>たまでです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a description. Depending on tone it can be an apology, an explanation, or
even cheerful - it simply says that speaking your mind was the whole of what
happened.

(b) is a rebuttal. `まで` puts a wall around the action and rejects whatever the
listener has read into it, so (b) presupposes that somebody has taken offence. It is
noticeably cooler, and in a tense conversation it will not calm anyone down.

</details>

# ～くらいのものだ

**Meaning** · that is about the only one - names the sole thing or person that qualifies, with a shrug built in

The `くらい` is the ordinary "about, roughly", and `のものだ` turns the estimate into
a verdict. The effect is *only*, but a casual, hand-waving *only*: not a precise
claim that nothing else exists, but a speaker looking round, failing to find
anything else, and saying so. Compared with `だけ`, which the reader has from N5,
this adds an attitude - usually mild disparagement, occasionally admiration.

## Formation

The pattern is a **predicate**: it closes the sentence. The normal frame puts the
thing being narrowed at the end, after a `のは` clause.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + くらいのものだ | 母(はは) → 母(はは)くらいのものだ |
| Noun (spoken variant) | Noun + ぐらいのものだ | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)ぐらいのものだ |
| Standard frame | ～のは + Noun + くらいのものだ | 反対(はんたい)したのは彼(かれ)くらいのものだ |
| Verb (nominalised) | ～こと + くらいのものだ | 聞(き)く → 話(はなし)を聞(き)くことくらいのものだ |
| Past | ～くらいのものだった | 私(わたし)くらいのものだった |
| Polite | ～くらいのものです | 一人(ひとり)くらいのものです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>くらい</code> and <code>ぐらい</code> are the same word. `ぐらい`
leans spoken, `くらい` leans written, and nobody will notice either way.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こんな時間(じかん)に電話(でんわ)してくるのは、母(はは)<b>くらいのものだ</b>。
My mother is about the only person who rings at this hour.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)にできるのは、話(はなし)を聞(き)くこと<b>ぐらいのものです</b>。
About all I can do is listen.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)で遅(おそ)くまで開(あ)いている店(みせ)は、あのラーメン屋(や)<b>くらいのものだ</b>。
That ramen place is about the only thing open late round here.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)で反対(はんたい)するのは、いつも山田(やまだ)さん<b>くらいのものだ</b>。
Yamada is generally the only one who ever objects in a meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の日(ひ)に出勤(しゅっきん)してきたのは、部長(ぶちょう)と私(わたし)<b>くらいのものだった</b>。
On the day of the typhoon, about the only people who came in were the manager and me.
</pre>

<pre>
残(のこ)った荷物(にもつ)といっても、古(ふる)いアルバムが数冊(すうさつ)<b>くらいのものだ</b>。
As for what was left, it amounted to a few old photo albums and not much else.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)通(かよ)っているが、店主(てんしゅ)と交(か)わす言葉(ことば)は「いつもの」と「ごちそうさま」<b>くらいのものだ</b>。
I have been going for ten years, but the only words I exchange with the owner are "the usual" and "thanks for the meal".
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)で得(とく)をするのは、もともと余裕(よゆう)のある家庭(かてい)<b>くらいのもの</b>ではないか。
Surely the only households that gain from the new system are the ones that were comfortable to begin with.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **conversational to neutral**. It is at home in speech, in essays, in
columns and in email; it is not formal enough for a contract or a technical report,
where <code>のみである</code> or <code>以外(いがい)にない</code> would be used
instead. There is no politeness problem with it - <code>くらいのものです</code> is
perfectly polite - but there is a tone problem, because the pattern is never neutral.

The usual tone is **dismissive**. When what you name is small, the sentence says "and
that is not much": <code>残(のこ)ったのは小銭(こぜに)くらいのものだ</code> is a
complaint about how little is left, not an inventory. This is why it so often follows
a `といっても` clause that plays something down in advance.

The other tone is **admiring**, and it appears when the thing named is a person who
alone measures up: <code>あの仕事(しごと)を任(まか)せられるのは彼女(かのじょ)くらいのものだ</code>
is praise. The grammar has not changed; the size of the compliment comes from the
size of the thing being narrowed to.

Two more things worth noticing. The pattern is **approximate by design**, which
protects the speaker: saying <code>来(き)たのは三人(さんにん)くらいのものだ</code>
is not a headcount and cannot be contradicted the way <code>三人(さんにん)だけ</code>
can. And it is a **predicate**, so it ends its clause. You cannot use it as a subject
in the middle of a sentence the way `だけ` allows.

## Contrast

### ～くらいのものだ vs ～だけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけだ` states an exact limit. `くらいのものだ` gives an
impression of a limit, and adds a judgement about it.

</div>

<pre>
来(き)たのは二人(ふたり)<b>だけです</b>。
Only two people came.
</pre>

<pre>
来(き)たのは二人(ふたり)<b>くらいのものです</b>。
About the only people who came were a couple of them.
</pre>

The first could be a register of attendance. The second could not - it is a comment
on the turnout. `だけ` also goes anywhere in a sentence
(<code>二人(ふたり)だけが来(き)た</code>), where `くらいのものだ` has to end the
clause.

### ～くらいのものだ vs ～しか○○ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しか～ない` measures the **shortfall** and sounds negative
about it. `くらいのものだ` sounds resigned, wry, or occasionally impressed.

</div>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)には卵(たまご)<b>しか</b>残(のこ)ってい<b>ない</b>。
There are only eggs left in the fridge.
</pre>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に残(のこ)っているのは、卵(たまご)が数(すう)個(こ)<b>くらいのものだ</b>。
What's left in the fridge amounts to a few eggs, if that.
</pre>

`しか～ない` needs its negative and is a straightforward complaint.
`くらいのものだ` is affirmative in form, hedged in content, and often funnier - the
approximation is doing the joke.

### ～くらいのものだ vs ～くらいだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same `くらい`, two patterns. `～くらいだ` after a verb or
adjective expresses **degree**: "to the point where". `～くらいのものだ` after a noun
expresses **exclusivity**.

</div>

<pre>
疲(つか)れて立(た)っていられない<b>くらいだ</b>。
I'm so tired I can hardly stay on my feet.
</pre>

<pre>
立(た)っていられたのは、若(わか)い選手(せんしゅ)<b>くらいのものだった</b>。
About the only ones still standing were the young players.
</pre>

The tell is what comes in front. A clause describing a state means degree; a noun
naming a candidate means exclusivity. Mixing them produces sentences that native
speakers can parse but would never write.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さん<b>くらいのもの</b>が反対(はんたい)した。 → ✅ 反対(はんたい)したのは山田(やまだ)さん<b>くらいのものだ</b>。
  The pattern is a predicate and belongs at the end. Rebuild the sentence with a
  `のは` clause in front of it.

- ❌ 参加者(さんかしゃ)は百人(ひゃくにん)<b>くらいのものだ</b>。（meaning "about a hundred attended"） → ✅ 参加者(さんかしゃ)は百人(ひゃくにん)<b>くらいだ</b>。
  Plain `くらい` estimates a quantity. Adding `のものだ` turns it into "a hundred and
  no more, which is disappointing" - a judgement you may not have meant to make.

- ❌ 疲(つか)れて歩(ある)けない<b>くらいのものだ</b>。 → ✅ 疲(つか)れて歩(ある)けない<b>くらいだ</b>。
  A clause of degree takes plain `くらいだ`. `のものだ` needs a noun in front of it.

- ❌ 優勝(ゆうしょう)できるのは彼(かれ)<b>くらいのものだけだ</b>。 → ✅ 優勝(ゆうしょう)できるのは彼(かれ)<b>くらいのものだ</b>。
  `くらいのものだ` already means "only". Adding `だけ` restates it and undoes the
  hedge that the pattern exists to provide.

## Practice

**1.** 日曜日(にちようび)の朝(あさ)にジョギングしているのは、この辺(へん)では田中(たなか)さん____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

田中(たなか)さん<b>くらいのものだ</b>。

The `のは` clause sets up the search, and the pattern closes the sentence with the
one candidate found. Note that the noun goes directly in front of
<code>くらいのものだ</code>, with no particle.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 冷凍庫(れいとうこ)に残(のこ)っているのは、氷(こおり)（くらいだ・くらいのものだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

氷(こおり)<b>くらいのものだ</b>

Both are heard, but the sentence is about what is left rather than about how much ice
there is, so the exclusive reading is wanted. <code>氷(こおり)くらいだ</code> would
sound like an estimate of a quantity of ice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The only person in the office who can read the old contracts is Ms Sasaki, and she retires next year."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

社内(しゃない)で古(ふる)い契約書(けいやくしょ)を読(よ)めるのは佐々木(ささき)さん<b>くらいのものだ</b>が、来年(らいねん)定年(ていねん)を迎(むか)える。

This is the admiring-but-worried use: the narrowing is a compliment to Sasaki and a
problem for the company at the same time. Because the pattern ends a clause, `が`
attaches to <code>ものだ</code> to carry on into the second half.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会場(かいじょう)に来(き)た人(ひと)は五十人(ごじゅうにん)くらいのものが集(あつ)まった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会場(かいじょう)に集(あつ)まったのは五十人(ごじゅうにん)<b>くらいのものだった</b>。

Two problems. The pattern cannot sit in the middle as a subject, and the original
sentence has two predicates fighting each other. Rewrite it with `のは` in front and
let <code>くらいのものだった</code> finish the sentence.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この仕事(しごと)ができるのは彼(かれ)<b>だけだ</b>。
(b) この仕事(しごと)ができるのは彼(かれ)<b>くらいのものだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat statement of fact, and it could appear in a staffing plan. It says
nothing about how the speaker feels.

(b) is a person talking. The hedge makes it an impression rather than an audit - "he's
about the only one" - which simultaneously softens the claim and makes it warmer. In
praise it sounds generous; said with a sigh about a team that has nobody else, it
sounds worried.

</details>

# ～にして

**Meaning** · at that point and not before; or, of a span, in no more than that - and by extension, only someone of that standing

`にして` is the classical `に` plus `して`, and modern Japanese keeps it as a marker
of a **striking point on a scale**. Two uses dominate. With an age or an attempt
number it means "only at that point": <code>三度目(さんどめ)にしてようやく</code>,
it took until the third try. With a very short span it means "in no more than that":
<code>一夜(いちや)にして</code>, overnight, just like that.

## Formation

`にして` always follows a noun. Which sense you get depends entirely on what kind of
noun it is.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Age or ordinal | Noun + にして + 初(はじ)めて／ようやく | 七十歳(ななじゅっさい) → 七十歳(ななじゅっさい)にして初(はじ)めて |
| Short time span | Noun + にして | 一瞬(いっしゅん) → 一瞬(いっしゅん)にして |
| Person or standing | Noun + にして + 初(はじ)めて | 職人(しょくにん) → 職人(しょくにん)にして初(はじ)めて可能(かのう)だ |
| Dual role | Noun + にして + Noun | 学者(がくしゃ) → 学者(がくしゃ)にして詩人(しじん) |
| Set phrase | 今(いま)にして思(おも)えば | - → 今(いま)にして思(おも)えば軽率(けいそつ)だった |
| Written variant | ～にしてはじめて | 苦労(くろう)を知(し)る人(ひと)にしてはじめて |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the time-span use is largely **collocational**. The nouns that occur are
a small set: 一瞬(いっしゅん), 一夜(いちや), 一日(いちにち),
一年(いちねん), and occasionally わずか plus a number.
<code>一瞬(いっしゅん)にして</code> and <code>一夜(いちや)にして</code> are the
two you will meet most.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
三度目(さんどめ)<b>にして</b>ようやく合格(ごうかく)した。
It was only on the third attempt that I finally passed.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)は火事(かじ)で一夜(いちや)<b>にして</b>焼(や)け落(お)ちた。
The factory burned to the ground in a single night.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は七十歳(ななじゅっさい)<b>にして</b>初(はじ)めてパソコンに触(さわ)った。
My grandmother touched a computer for the first time at the age of seventy.
</pre>

<pre>
噂(うわさ)は一瞬(いっしゅん)<b>にして</b>町中(まちじゅう)に広(ひろ)がった。
The rumour was all over town in an instant.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)<b>にして</b>思(おも)えば、あのとき断(ことわ)っておくべきだった。
Looking back on it now, I should have refused at the time.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)五年目(ごねんめ)<b>にして</b>、会社(かいしゃ)はようやく黒字(くろじ)に転(てん)じた。
It was in its fifth year that the company finally moved into profit.
</pre>

<pre>
この域(いき)に達(たっ)するのは、長年(ながねん)修業(しゅぎょう)を積(つ)んだ職人(しょくにん)<b>にして</b>初(はじ)めて可能(かのう)になる。
Reaching this level becomes possible only for a craftsman who has trained for many years.
</pre>

<pre>
震災(しんさい)の夜(よる)、見慣(みな)れた街並(まちな)みが一夜(いちや)<b>にして</b>消(き)えてしまったことを、今(いま)でも信(しん)じられずにいる。
On the night of the earthquake the familiar streets vanished overnight, and I still cannot quite believe it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にして` is **formal and written**, and it survives in speech mainly through set
phrases. <code>今(いま)にして思(おも)えば</code> is the most useful of them and
is entirely normal in conversation; <code>一瞬(いっしゅん)にして</code> is close
behind. Beyond those, the pattern belongs to news copy, biography, obituaries,
company histories and formal speeches. In everyday Japanese the ordinary particles
`で` and `に` do the same work with no ceremony.

What `にして` adds over `で` is **remark**. <code>一年(いちねん)で完成(かんせい)した</code>
reports a duration; <code>一年(いちねん)にして完成(かんせい)した</code> says a
year was astonishingly little. The same applies to ages:
<code>三十歳(さんじゅっさい)で独立(どくりつ)した</code> is a fact, while
<code>三十歳(さんじゅっさい)にして</code> tells you the writer finds the age worth
commenting on - and with an age it nearly always wants <code>初(はじ)めて</code>
or <code>ようやく</code> in support.

The "person or standing" use is worth flagging because it looks like the others and
is not about time at all. <code>彼(かれ)にして初(はじ)めてできることだ</code>
means only someone of his calibre could manage it - and a close relative of it,
<code>彼(かれ)にしてこの失敗(しっぱい)か</code>, means the opposite kind of
surprise: even *he* got it wrong. Both are literary and both depend heavily on
context, so read them carefully before deciding which way round the compliment runs.

Finally, do not confuse `にして` with `にしては`, which is a different pattern
altogether. The one extra `は` changes "at, in" into "for a ...", and the two are a
standard exam trap.

## Contrast

### ～にして vs ～で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` is the neutral particle for a span or a point.
`にして` is `で` plus a raised eyebrow.

</div>

<pre>
この工事(こうじ)は三年(さんねん)<b>で</b>終(お)わった。
The construction was finished in three years.
</pre>

<pre>
この大(だい)工事(こうじ)は、わずか三年(さんねん)<b>にして</b>完成(かんせい)を見(み)た。
This enormous project reached completion in a mere three years.
</pre>

The second sentence is doing something the first is not: it is asking you to be
impressed. Use `で` unless the span really is the point, because `にして` on an
unremarkable duration reads as writing that is trying too hard.

### ～にして vs ～になって初(はじ)めて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning for the age use.
`になって初めて` is the everyday form; `にして初めて` is the written one.

</div>

<pre>
四十歳(よんじゅっさい)<b>になって初(はじ)めて</b>泳(およ)げるようになった。
I only learned to swim when I got to forty.
</pre>

<pre>
四十歳(よんじゅっさい)<b>にして初(はじ)めて</b>泳(およ)ぎを覚(おぼ)えた。
It was at forty that he first learned to swim.
</pre>

Both are correct and mean the same thing. The first is what you say about yourself
over dinner; the second is what a profile in a magazine says about someone. If you
are speaking, take the first.

### ～にして vs ～にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にして` marks a point on a scale. `にしては` means "for a ～",
setting up a judgement that defies expectation. Different patterns, one letter
apart.

</div>

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>にしては</b>よく書(か)けている。
For a child, it's very well written.
</pre>

<pre>
十歳(じゅっさい)<b>にして</b>プロの舞台(ぶたい)に立(た)った。
At the age of ten he was already on a professional stage.
</pre>

`にしては` compares against a standard and usually softens praise or criticism;
`にして` simply plants a flag at a point and lets its earliness or lateness speak.
Swapping them produces sentences that are grammatical and mean the wrong thing,
which is exactly why the pair turns up in tests.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>にして</b>終(お)わった。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>で</b>終(お)わった。
  Half an hour is an ordinary length for a meeting, so there is nothing to remark on.
  Reserve `にして` for spans the reader is meant to find striking.

- ❌ 子供(こども)<b>にして</b>よく書(か)けている。 → ✅ 子供(こども)<b>にしては</b>よく書(か)けている。
  Dropping the `は` changes the pattern. Without it the sentence tries to place a
  child on a time scale and fails.

- ❌ 毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)<b>にして</b>起(お)きる。 → ✅ 毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)<b>に</b>起(お)きる。
  A clock time takes plain `に`. `にして` needs a duration, an age or an ordinal,
  not a point in the day.

- ❌ 三十歳(さんじゅっさい)<b>にして</b>会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)った。 → ✅ 三十歳(さんじゅっさい)<b>で</b>会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)った。
  With an age, `にして` leans on <code>初(はじ)めて</code> or
  <code>ようやく</code> and implies the age is remarkable. On a neutral fact it
  leaves the reader waiting for a point that never arrives.

## Practice

**1.** 四度目(よんどめ)の挑戦(ちょうせん)____、ついに頂上(ちょうじょう)に立(た)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

四度目(よんどめ)の挑戦(ちょうせん)<b>にして</b>、ついに頂上(ちょうじょう)に立(た)った。

An ordinal plus `にして` is the "only at that point" use, and ついに or ようやく
downstream is the usual companion. The sentence says the first three attempts
failed without having to mention them.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新人(しんじん)（にして・にしては）落(お)ち着(つ)いた受(う)け答(こた)えだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新人(しんじん)<b>にしては</b>落(お)ち着(つ)いた受(う)け答(こた)えだった。

The sentence measures the performance against what you would expect of a newcomer,
which is `にしては`. <code>新人(しんじん)にして</code> would have to mean
something like "it takes a newcomer to manage that", which is not what a listener
would understand here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The town's entire economy collapsed overnight when the mine closed."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

炭鉱(たんこう)が閉(と)じられ、町(まち)の経済(けいざい)は一夜(いちや)<b>にして</b>崩(くず)れ去(さ)った。

"Overnight" in the sense of a sudden collapse is exactly
<code>一夜(いちや)にして</code> - one of the fixed collocations, and one that
carries the drama the English has. A plain <code>一晩(ひとばん)で</code> would
sound like a literal measurement of hours.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 祖父(そふ)は八十歳(はちじゅっさい)にして新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)んでいる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 祖父(そふ)は八十歳(はちじゅっさい)<b>にして</b>なお新聞(しんぶん)を毎日(まいにち)読(よ)んでいる。
（or simply 八十歳(はちじゅっさい)<b>で</b>新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)んでいる）

Reading a newspaper at eighty is not in itself remarkable, so the pattern is left
without a point. Either add the word that supplies the surprise - なお, "still" -
or drop `にして` for the neutral `で`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は二十歳(はたち)<b>で</b>会社(かいしゃ)を立(た)ち上(あ)げた。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)は二十歳(はたち)<b>にして</b>会社(かいしゃ)を立(た)ち上(あ)げた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) records the age at which she founded the company. It is neutral, and it would
sit comfortably in a CV.

(b) comments on it. `にして` tells you the writer thinks twenty is startlingly
young, and the sentence now belongs in a profile or an introduction rather than in a
list of facts. Note the reading: 二十歳(はたち) is the special native reading for
the age, not ❌にじゅっさい.

</details>

# ～をおいて

**Meaning** · other than this one; setting this one aside there is nothing - said to nominate the only possible candidate

The verb is 置(お)く in its old sense of "set aside". Put something aside, look at
what is left, and find nothing: that is the shape of
<code>彼(かれ)をおいてほかにいない</code>. In practice the pattern is a **piece of
advocacy**. It is how you say that a person is the only one for the job, or that now
is the only moment to act, and it is nearly always a compliment or a push.

## Formation

The particle is always `を`, whatever role the noun would otherwise have played, and
the predicate is always negative.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person) | Noun + をおいて + ほかにいない | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)をおいてほかにいない |
| Noun (thing) | Noun + をおいて + ほかにない | この案(あん) → この案(あん)をおいてほかにない |
| Noun (time) | Noun + をおいて + ～ない | 今(いま) → 今(いま)をおいてない |
| Without ほかに | Noun + をおいて + ない/いない | 彼女(かのじょ) → 彼女(かのじょ)をおいていない |
| Polite | ～をおいてほかにありません | 山口(やまぐち)さんをおいてほかにありません |
| Kanji spelling | ～を置(お)いて | kana is the usual choice |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the sentence must end in a negative. There is no
<code>をおいて ～ ある</code>; the pattern exists only to say that nothing else
qualifies.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この役(やく)ができるのは、彼(かれ)<b>をおいて</b>ほかにいない。
There is nobody but him who can play this part.
</pre>

<pre>
決断(けつだん)するなら、今(いま)<b>をおいて</b>ない。
If you're going to decide, it's now or never.
</pre>

<pre>
相談(そうだん)できる相手(あいて)は、君(きみ)<b>をおいて</b>ほかにいなかった。
You were the only person I could have talked to.
</pre>

<pre>
会長(かいちょう)を任(まか)せられる人物(じんぶつ)は、山口(やまぐち)さん<b>をおいて</b>ほかにないでしょう。
Yamaguchi must surely be the only person we could entrust the chairmanship to.
</pre>

<pre>
失(うしな)われた記録(きろく)を復元(ふくげん)できる研究者(けんきゅうしゃ)は、世界(せかい)に彼女(かのじょ)<b>をおいて</b>いない。
She is the only researcher in the world who can reconstruct the lost records.
</pre>

<pre>
この技術(ぎじゅつ)を受(う)け継(つ)げるのは、工房(こうぼう)に残(のこ)った弟子(でし)一人(ひとり)<b>をおいて</b>ほかにいなかった。
The one apprentice left in the workshop was the only person who could carry the craft on.
</pre>

<pre>
市場(しじょう)が動(うご)き始(はじ)めた今(いま)<b>をおいて</b>、参入(さんにゅう)の好機(こうき)はない。
With the market finally moving, there will be no better moment to enter than this one.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)百年(ひゃくねん)の節目(ふしめ)に挨拶(あいさつ)を述(の)べていただくのは、先代(せんだい)を支(ささ)えてこられたこの方(かた)<b>をおいて</b>ほかにありません。
For the address at our centenary there is nobody more fitting than this gentleman, who supported my predecessor for so long.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Formal, emphatic, and public.** Its natural settings are introductions and
nominations, recommendation letters, speeches, editorials and advertising copy. In
conversation it appears when someone is being deliberately emphatic - pressing a
friend to act now, or paying a large compliment - and it always sounds like a
statement being made rather than a remark being dropped.

The pattern is **evaluative, not merely factual**. It does not just report that
alternatives are absent; it argues that they are, and it takes the speaker's side.
That is why it fits a nomination so well and why it grates on something small.
<code>昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)はこのコンビニをおいてほかにない</code> is not wrong so
much as absurd - the machinery is far too heavy for the load, and a listener will
hear it as a joke.

Two collocations account for most of what you will meet. With a **person**, the
frame is <code>～をおいてほかにいない</code>, and the point is fitness for a role.
With <code>今(いま)</code>, the frame is <code>今(いま)をおいてない</code> and
the point is urgency: this is the moment, do not wait. Both are worth learning whole.

The kanji spelling <code>を置(お)いて</code> is correct and you will see it, but
kana dominates in modern text - partly because the kanji invites confusion with
<code>を除(のぞ)いて</code>, which looks similar in meaning and behaves completely
differently.

## Contrast

### ～をおいて vs ～以外(いがい)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `以外に` is the neutral "apart from" and can be used about
anything. `をおいて` argues for a single candidate, and needs a negative.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>以外(いがい)に</b>、中国語(ちゅうごくご)ができる人(ひと)はいますか。
Is there anyone besides Tanaka who speaks Chinese?
</pre>

<pre>
通訳(つうやく)を頼(たの)めるのは田中(たなか)さん<b>をおいて</b>ほかにいない。
Tanaka is the only person we could ask to interpret.
</pre>

`以外に` can head a question, a positive sentence or a list. `をおいて` can do none
of those - it is committed to the negative and to a single nominee, which is exactly
what makes it emphatic.

### ～をおいて vs ～を除(のぞ)いて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they look like twins and are not.
<code>を除(のぞ)いて</code> means **excluding**, and the rest of the sentence
carries on positively. `をおいて` means **there is nothing else**.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>を除(のぞ)いて</b>、全員(ぜんいん)が出席(しゅっせき)した。
Everyone attended except Tanaka.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>をおいて</b>、適任者(てきにんしゃ)はいない。
There is no suitable candidate other than Tanaka.
</pre>

The first removes Tanaka from a group; the second says the group consists of Tanaka.
Written in kanji as <code>を置(お)いて</code> and <code>を除(のぞ)いて</code>
they are one character apart, and getting them the wrong way round reverses the
sentence.

### ～をおいて vs ほかに○○ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほかに○○ない` reports that a search found nothing.
`をおいて` names the exception first and then denies everything else. They combine
happily: <code>～をおいてほかにない</code>.

</div>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)に合(あ)う物件(ぶっけん)は<b>ほかになかった</b>。
There was nothing else that met the conditions.
</pre>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)に合(あ)うのは、この部屋(へや)<b>をおいてほかになかった</b>。
This flat was the only one that met the conditions.
</pre>

The difference is where the emphasis falls. `ほかに○○ない` leaves the surviving
candidate implicit; `をおいて` puts it in the spotlight with `を` and then clears the
stage. When you want to name someone, you want `をおいて`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この仕事(しごと)を頼(たの)めるのは彼(かれ)をおいてほかに<b>いる</b>。 → ✅ この仕事(しごと)を頼(たの)めるのは彼(かれ)をおいてほかに<b>いない</b>。
  The pattern only exists with a negative predicate. A positive ending leaves the
  sentence saying the opposite of anything anyone means.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さん<b>をおいて</b>、全員(ぜんいん)出席(しゅっせき)した。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さん<b>を除(のぞ)いて</b>、全員(ぜんいん)出席(しゅっせき)した。
  "Excluding" is 除(のぞ)く. Using `をおいて` here produces a sentence that
  promises there is nobody but Tanaka and then contradicts itself.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)に<b>をおいて</b>ほかにいない。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>をおいて</b>ほかにいない。
  The particle is `を` and it replaces whatever the noun would otherwise have taken.
  Stacking `に` in front of it is a common slip when the noun is the person a job is
  entrusted *to*.

- ❌ 傘(かさ)を買(か)うなら、この店(みせ)<b>をおいて</b>ほかにない。 → ✅ 傘(かさ)を買(か)うなら、この店(みせ)が一番(いちばん)安(やす)い。
  Grammatical, but the register is far too grand for the content. Save `をおいて`
  for nominations, appointments and genuinely decisive moments.

## Practice

**1.** この難(むずか)しい交渉(こうしょう)を任(まか)せられるのは、森(もり)さん____ほかに____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

森(もり)さん<b>をおいて</b>ほかに<b>いない</b>。

The noun takes `を` regardless of the role it plays in the sentence, and because the
candidate is a person the negative is <code>いない</code>. The whole frame
<code>～をおいてほかにいない</code> is best memorised as a unit.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新人(しんじん)三人(さんにん)（をおいて・を除(のぞ)いて）、全員(ぜんいん)が研修(けんしゅう)を終(お)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新人(しんじん)三人(さんにん)<b>を除(のぞ)いて</b>

The sentence carries on positively - everyone else finished - so what is needed is
"excluding", which is 除(のぞ)く. `をおいて` would require the sentence to end in a
negative and would claim that the three newcomers were the only people who existed.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If we are going to renegotiate the contract, there will never be a better moment than now."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

契約(けいやく)を見直(みなお)すのであれば、今(いま)<b>をおいて</b>ほかにありません。

<code>今(いま)をおいて</code> is the fixed shape for urgency, and the polite
<code>ほかにありません</code> matches the business setting. Note that the English
"never" turns into the plain negative at the end - Japanese does not need a separate
word for it here.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 審査員(しんさいん)を務(つと)めるのは、先生(せんせい)にをおいてほかにいらっしゃいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 審査員(しんさいん)を務(つと)めるのは、先生(せんせい)<b>をおいて</b>ほかに<b>いらっしゃいません</b>。

Two faults. The stray `に` in front of `をおいて` has to go, since the pattern takes
`を` alone. And the predicate must be negative - here the honorific negative
<code>いらっしゃいません</code>, which fits the respectful register the rest of the
sentence has set up.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)に向(む)いているのは佐藤(さとう)さん<b>だけです</b>。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)に向(む)いているのは佐藤(さとう)さん<b>をおいてほかにいません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states a fact about the shortlist. It is neutral, and depending on tone it could
even be a complaint that the field is thin.

(b) is a recommendation. `をおいて` puts Sato forward and dismisses everyone else in
one movement, and the formality makes it sound like something said in a meeting or
written in a letter. If you want the sentence to *do* something rather than merely
report, (b) is the one.

</details>

# ～ならでは

**Meaning** · only this one could produce it - a quality that belongs to this place, this maker or this situation and to nothing else

Historically `ならでは` is `なら` plus a negative `で(は)`: "if it were not this, then
not". Modern Japanese has kept the sense and thrown away the negative ending, and
what remains is a compliment. <code>京都(きょうと)ならではの味(あじ)</code> is a
flavour you would not find anywhere but Kyoto - and the sentence is telling you it is
worth having.

## Formation

The base is a **noun**, and the pattern nearly always continues with `の` into
another noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (modifying) | Noun + ならではの + Noun | 京都(きょうと) → 京都(きょうと)ならではの味(あじ) |
| Noun (as predicate) | Noun + ならではだ | 老舗(しにせ) → この気配(きくば)りは老舗(しにせ)ならではだ |
| Polite predicate | Noun + ならではです | 手作(てづく)り → 手作(てづく)りならではです |
| Compound noun phrase | Long noun phrase + ならではの | 長年(ながねん)続(つづ)く店(みせ)ならではの |
| Literary residue | Noun + ならでは ～ ない | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)ならでは書(か)けない文章(ぶんしょう) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `の` is not optional when a noun follows.
❌<code>京都(きょうと)ならでは味(あじ)</code> is one of the two errors learners
make with this pattern; the other is attaching it to a verb, which it does not
accept.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは京都(きょうと)<b>ならではの</b>味(あじ)だ。
This is a flavour you get only in Kyoto.
</pre>

<pre>
手作(てづく)り<b>ならではの</b>温(あたた)かみがある。
It has a warmth that only handmade things have.
</pre>

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)の北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>ならではの</b>景色(けしき)を楽(たの)しんだ。
We enjoyed scenery you can see only in Hokkaido in winter.
</pre>

<pre>
この細(こま)やかな気配(きくば)りは、老舗(しにせ)の旅館(りょかん)<b>ならでは</b>だ。
This kind of attentiveness is the mark of a long-established inn and of nothing else.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>ならではの</b>思(おも)い切(き)った提案(ていあん)が、会議(かいぎ)の空気(くうき)を変(か)えた。
A bold proposal of the kind only a newcomer would make changed the mood of the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
小(ちい)さな劇場(げきじょう)<b>ならではの</b>、役者(やくしゃ)の息遣(いきづか)いまで伝(つた)わる舞台(ぶたい)だった。
It was the sort of performance only a small theatre can give, where you can hear the actors breathe.
</pre>

<pre>
地元(じもと)の人(ひと)に案内(あんない)してもらったおかげで、観光(かんこう)では味(あじ)わえない、暮(く)らし<b>ならではの</b>時間(じかん)を過(す)ごせた。
Being shown round by a local let us have the kind of time only ordinary life there offers, which tourism never reaches.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)現場(げんば)に立(た)ってきた職人(しょくにん)<b>ならではの</b>判断(はんだん)で、事故(じこ)は未然(みぜん)に防(ふせ)がれた。
A judgement only a craftsman with years on site could have made stopped the accident before it happened.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ならでは` is **evaluative and positive by default**. It is the standard vocabulary
of travel writing, restaurant reviews, product copy, tourism posters and craft
programmes on television, and it is entirely usable in speech - complimenting a
friend's cooking with <code>手作(てづく)りならではですね</code> is natural and
warm. Register-wise it sits comfortably in the middle: not casual slang, not stiff.

The praise is not optional. Because the pattern says "this could come from nowhere
else" *approvingly*, it fights with a negative complement.
❌<code>東京(とうきょう)ならではの渋滞(じゅうたい)</code> is odd for that reason -
traffic jams are not a distinction. Speakers do sometimes use exactly that mismatch
as irony, and you will meet it, but a learner should read the irony rather than
attempt it.

Two shapes to keep apart. As a **modifier** it needs `の`:
<code>～ならではの ＋ noun</code>. As a **predicate** it drops the `の` and takes the
copula: <code>この味(あじ)は母(はは)ならではだ</code>. Learners tend to
generalise one of the two and produce the other wrongly.

Finally, note the neighbour `なり`. <code>私(わたし)なりの考(かんが)え</code>
means "my own way of thinking, such as it is" - modest. <code>ならでは</code> means
"a thing only I could have produced" - a boast. Applying `ならでは` to yourself is
therefore risky, and `なり` is the word you usually want.

## Contrast

### ～ならでは vs ～だけの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけの` states exclusivity as a fact. `ならではの` states it as
a merit.

</div>

<pre>
ここ<b>だけの</b>話(はなし)なんですが、来月(らいげつ)異動(いどう)になります。
This is between us, but I'm being transferred next month.
</pre>

<pre>
ここ<b>ならではの</b>料理(りょうり)をぜひ召(め)し上(あ)がってください。
Do try the dishes you can only get here.
</pre>

`だけの` covers any kind of restriction, including secrets, limits and quantities.
`ならではの` covers one kind only: a distinctive quality worth admiring. Where the
restriction is neutral or unwelcome, `だけの` is the word.

### ～ならでは vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `らしい` means **typical of** and can be a criticism.
`ならでは` means **possible only for**, and is always a compliment.

</div>

<pre>
遅刻(ちこく)するなんて、彼(かれ)<b>らしい</b>ね。
Turning up late - that's so like him.
</pre>

<pre>
この丁寧(ていねい)な仕上(しあ)げは、彼(かれ)<b>ならでは</b>だ。
This careful finish is something only he could produce.
</pre>

The first sentence is affectionate at best and pointed at worst; `らしい` merely
matches behaviour to a known character. The second could not be used that way, which
is a good test: if the sentence would work as a mild dig, you need `らしい`.

### ～ならでは vs ～特有(とくゆう)の・～独特(どくとく)の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `特有の` and `独特の` are descriptive and can take anything,
including unpleasant things. `ならではの` is appreciative and cannot.

</div>

<pre>
この病気(びょうき)<b>特有(とくゆう)の</b>症状(しょうじょう)が現(あらわ)れた。
Symptoms characteristic of this illness appeared.
</pre>

<pre>
この土地(とち)<b>ならではの</b>食材(しょくざい)が並(なら)んでいた。
Ingredients you find only in this region were laid out.
</pre>

Swap them and only one direction fails: ❌<code>この病気(びょうき)ならではの症状(しょうじょう)</code>
reads as praising a disease. The other way round,
<code>この土地(とち)特有(とくゆう)の食材(しょくざい)</code> is perfectly fine -
just cooler, the voice of a reference book rather than a menu.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 京都(きょうと)<b>ならでは</b>風情(ふぜい)がある。 → ✅ 京都(きょうと)<b>ならではの</b>風情(ふぜい)がある。
  A following noun needs `の`. The `の` is dropped only when `ならでは` ends the
  sentence with a copula.

- ❌ 手作(てづく)り<b>する</b>ならではの味(あじ)。 → ✅ 手作(てづく)り<b>ならでは</b>の味(あじ)。
  The base is a noun. Verbs do not attach, so nominalise -
  <code>手作(てづく)り</code> rather than <code>手作(てづく)りする</code>.

- ❌ 東京(とうきょう)<b>ならではの</b>人(ひと)の多(おお)さにうんざりした。 → ✅ 東京(とうきょう)<b>特有(とくゆう)の</b>人(ひと)の多(おお)さにうんざりした。
  The complement has to be something admirable. Pairing `ならでは` with a complaint
  produces a sentence that praises and criticises in the same breath.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>ならではの</b>意見(いけん)を述(の)べさせていただきます。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>なりの</b>意見(いけん)を述(の)べさせていただきます。
  Used about yourself, `ならでは` is self-congratulation, which sits badly with the
  humble ending. `なり` is the modest counterpart and is what the situation calls
  for.

## Practice

**1.** 冬(ふゆ)の漁港(ぎょこう)を訪(おとず)れれば、この季節(きせつ)____新鮮(しんせん)な魚(さかな)が味(あじ)わえる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この季節(きせつ)<b>ならではの</b>新鮮(しんせん)な魚(さかな)

A noun follows, so the `の` is required. The sentence is recommending something, which
is the appreciative register `ならでは` is built for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 梅雨(つゆ)の時期(じき)（ならではの・特有(とくゆう)の）湿気(しっけ)で、洗濯物(せんたくもの)が乾(かわ)かない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

梅雨(つゆ)の時期(じき)<b>特有(とくゆう)の</b>湿気(しっけ)

Damp that stops the washing drying is a complaint, and `ならではの` would be
congratulating the rainy season on it. `特有の` is neutral and takes unwelcome
things without difficulty.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The quiet you get in a mountain village at night is something no city can offer."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夜(よる)の山(やま)の集落(しゅうらく)<b>ならではの</b>静(しず)けさは、都会(とかい)では決(けっ)して味(あじ)わえない。

The English "something no city can offer" is doing exactly what `ならではの` does -
naming a quality as belonging to one place alone. Note that
<code>静(しず)けさ</code>, the noun, is what the pattern modifies.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この音(おと)の良(よ)さは、アナログレコードならでは魅力(みりょく)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この音(おと)の良(よ)さは、アナログレコード<b>ならではの</b>魅力(みりょく)です。
（or … アナログレコード<b>ならではです</b>）

Either put the `の` in and keep 魅力(みりょく) as the head noun, or drop
魅力(みりょく) and let `ならではです` end the sentence. What you cannot do is have a
noun following `ならでは` with nothing joining them.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子供(こども)<b>らしい</b>発想(はっそう)だ。
(b) 子供(こども)<b>ならではの</b>発想(はっそう)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the idea is what you would expect from a child. Warmly said it is charming;
said flatly about an adult's proposal it is a put-down.

(b) can only be praise. It says the idea is one an adult could not have arrived at,
so the childishness is being treated as an advantage. In a design meeting, (a) risks
offence and (b) is a compliment.

</details>

# ～きっての

**Meaning** · the foremost in the whole of - names a group or a place, then singles out its outstanding member

The verb behind it is 切(き)る in the sense of cutting clean across something:
`きっての` cuts across an entire organisation, region or era and picks out the top of
it. <code>社内(しゃない)きっての論客(ろんかく)</code> is the sharpest arguer in
the whole company. This is **journalist's Japanese** - the vocabulary of profiles,
blurbs and introductions - and the everyday way to say it is
<code>社内(しゃない)で一番(いちばん)の</code>.

## Formation

Two nouns and nothing else. The left one is a **group, place, field or period**; the
right one is a **person or thing that can be ranked**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Organisation | Noun + きっての + Noun | 社内(しゃない) → 社内(しゃない)きっての論客(ろんかく) |
| Place | Noun + きっての + Noun | 町(まち) → 町(まち)きっての金持(かねも)ち |
| Country or region | Noun + きっての + Noun | 日本(にほん) → 日本(にほん)きっての名門校(めいもんこう) |
| Field or industry | Noun + きっての + Noun | 業界(ぎょうかい) → 業界(ぎょうかい)きってのやり手(て) |
| Period | Noun + きっての + Noun | 当代(とうだい) → 当代(とうだい)きっての名優(めいゆう) |
| Kanji spelling | ～切(き)っての | kana is the usual choice |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `きっての` is **attributive only**. It must be followed by a
noun; there is no ❌<code>彼(かれ)は社内(しゃない)きってだ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は社内(しゃない)<b>きっての</b>論客(ろんかく)だ。
He is the company's foremost debater.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)、祖父(そふ)は町(まち)<b>きっての</b>金持(かねも)ちだった。
In those days my grandfather was the richest man in town.
</pre>

<pre>
創立(そうりつ)百年(ひゃくねん)を超(こ)え、日本(にほん)<b>きっての</b>名門校(めいもんこう)として知(し)られる。
Founded over a century ago, it is known as one of the most prestigious schools in Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
業界(ぎょうかい)<b>きっての</b>やり手(て)と評(ひょう)される人物(じんぶつ)である。
He is a man reputed to be the sharpest operator in the industry.
</pre>

<pre>
地方紙(ちほうし)は彼女(かのじょ)を、県(けん)<b>きっての</b>実力者(じつりょくしゃ)と呼(よ)んだ。
The local paper called her the most powerful figure in the prefecture.
</pre>

<pre>
学年(がくねん)<b>きっての</b>秀才(しゅうさい)が、卒業(そつぎょう)後(ご)に故郷(こきょう)へ戻(もど)って教壇(きょうだん)に立(た)った。
The brightest pupil in the year went home after graduating and became a teacher there.
</pre>

<pre>
明治期(めいじき)の文壇(ぶんだん)<b>きっての</b>論争家(ろんそうか)として、今(いま)なお名(な)が残(のこ)っている。
His name survives to this day as the great controversialist of the Meiji literary world.
</pre>

<pre>
創業家(そうぎょうけ)の三男(さんなん)は、一族(いちぞく)<b>きっての</b>変(か)わり者(もの)として知(し)られていた。
The third son of the founding family was known as the eccentric of the clan.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise it, and say it another way.** `きっての` belongs to written introductions:
newspaper and magazine profiles, book jackets, company histories, event programmes,
and the master of ceremonies reading from a card. It has a slightly grand,
old-fashioned ring, and a learner who deploys it in conversation - introducing a
colleague at a party as <code>社内(しゃない)きっての論客(ろんかく)</code> - will
sound like someone reading out a press release. In speech, say
<code>社内(しゃない)で一番(いちばん)の</code> or
<code>社内(しゃない)で一番(いちばん)～な</code>.

Two structural facts do the work of remembering it. The left-hand noun must be a
**bounded set** you could in principle walk around: a company, a town, a school year,
an industry, an era. ❌<code>私(わたし)きっての</code> is impossible, because one
person is not a field. And the right-hand noun must be **rankable** - 名人(めいじん),
実力者(じつりょくしゃ), 秀才(しゅうさい), 名門(めいもん),
変(か)わり者(もの). A bare <code>人(ひと)</code> leaves the sentence with
nothing to be foremost *at*.

Note that the ranking is not always flattering. <code>一族(いちぞく)きっての変(か)わり者(もの)</code>
is the family's most conspicuous oddball, and the pattern is perfectly comfortable
with that: what it requires is prominence, not merit. That said, it is used far more
often in praise than in criticism.

Kanji spelling <code>切(き)っての</code> exists and turns up in older text, but
modern writing keeps it in kana.

## Contrast

### ～きっての vs ～で一番(いちばん)の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning; `～で一番の` is the living form and
`きっての` is the printed one. Use the first when you speak.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)はクラス<b>で一番(いちばん)</b>足(あし)が速(はや)い。
He's the fastest runner in the class.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は学年(がくねん)<b>きっての</b>俊足(しゅんそく)だった。
He was the outstanding sprinter of his year.
</pre>

Both sentences rank one person against a defined group. The first is what a
classmate says; the second is what the school's centenary booklet says. Note also
that `で一番` happily takes an adjective, where `きっての` demands a noun.

### ～きっての vs 随一(ずいいち)の・屈指(くっし)の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three are written-register superlatives.
`きっての` and `随一の` mean **the single top one**; `屈指の` means **among the very
few best**, which is a slightly weaker claim.

</div>

<pre>
国内(こくない)<b>屈指(くっし)の</b>規模(きぼ)を誇(ほこ)る工場(こうじょう)だ。
It is a plant of a size that puts it among the largest in the country.
</pre>

<pre>
国内(こくない)<b>きっての</b>規模(きぼ)を誇(ほこ)る工場(こうじょう)だ。
It is the largest plant of its kind in the country.
</pre>

`屈指の` hedges and is therefore safer in copy that has to be defensible; `随一の`
and `きっての` commit to first place. Of the three, `随一の` is the most neutral in
tone and `きっての` the most flavoured.

### ～きっての vs ～ならではの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both attach a place or group to a following noun, and they say
different things. `きっての` ranks a member **within** the group. `ならではの` says a
quality belongs **to the group itself** and to no other.

</div>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)<b>ならではの</b>静(しず)けさがある。
It has a quietness you find only in Kyoto.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)<b>きっての</b>老舗(しにせ)だと言(い)われている。
It is said to be the oldest established shop in Kyoto.
</pre>

The first compares Kyoto with everywhere else; the second compares shops inside
Kyoto with one another. They look alike on the page and mean nothing like the same
thing, which makes them a standard trap.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は社内(しゃない)<b>きってだ</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は社内(しゃない)<b>きっての</b>実力者(じつりょくしゃ)だ。
  The pattern must modify a noun. It cannot stand as a predicate on its own.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>きっての</b>自信作(じしんさく)です。 → ✅ 自分(じぶん)でも一番(いちばん)の自信作(じしんさく)です。
  The left-hand noun has to name a set with members in it. A single person is not a
  field to be foremost in.

- ❌ 町(まち)<b>きっての</b>人(ひと)に会(あ)った。 → ✅ 町(まち)<b>きっての</b>名士(めいし)に会(あ)った。
  The right-hand noun has to be something one can be the best at being. A bare
  <code>人(ひと)</code> gives the superlative nothing to bite on.

- ❌ うちの部署(ぶしょ)きってのムードメーカーだよ、こいつ。 → ✅ うちの部署(ぶしょ)で一番(いちばん)の盛(も)り上(あ)げ役(やく)だよ、こいつ。
  Register clash. A printed superlative in a sentence that ends in こいつ reads as a
  deliberate joke; if you did not intend one, use the ordinary form.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this, and what does it claim? 大正(たいしょう)期(き)の画壇(がだん)<b>きっての</b>異端児(いたんじ)であった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

An exhibition catalogue, an art history text, or a newspaper obituary - the past
tense <code>であった</code> and the printed superlative both point at written prose.

The claim is that within the Taisho-era art world, this painter was its most
conspicuous maverick. Note that the "best" here is not praise for skill: `きっての`
ranks prominence, and 異端児(いたんじ) is a label for someone who did not fit.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary spoken Japanese: 彼女(かのじょ)は部内(ぶない)<b>きっての</b>情報通(じょうほうつう)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)は部(ぶ)の中(なか)で<b>一番(いちばん)事情(じじょう)に詳(くわ)しい</b>。

Two changes make it speakable: `きっての` becomes
<code>中(なか)で一番(いちばん)</code>, and the compact written noun
情報通(じょうほうつう) unpacks into an adjective phrase. The claim is identical;
only the printed voice has gone.

</details>

**3.** Choose, and say why: あの旅館(りょかん)は（温泉地(おんせんち)きっての・温泉地(おんせんち)ならではの）老舗(しにせ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

温泉地(おんせんち)<b>きっての</b>老舗(しにせ)だ

The sentence ranks one inn against the others in the spa town, which is what
`きっての` does. <code>ならではの</code> would claim that being long-established is a
quality found only in spa towns, which is not what anyone means.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この机(つくえ)は工房(こうぼう)きってのだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この机(つくえ)は工房(こうぼう)<b>きっての</b>傑作(けっさく)だ。

`きっての` needs a noun after it, and the noun has to be one that can be ranked -
傑作(けっさく), 自信作(じしんさく), 名品(めいひん). Left dangling in front of
the copula, the pattern has nothing to attach to.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in register between:
(a) 山田(やまだ)は社内(しゃない)<b>きっての</b>理論家(りろんか)である。
(b) 山田(やまだ)さんは社内(しゃない)で<b>一番(いちばん)</b>理屈(りくつ)っぽい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a printed sentence. The bare surname, the <code>である</code> ending and the
formal noun 理論家(りろんか) all belong to a company newsletter or a profile, and
the superlative is a compliment.

(b) is people talking. The same fact has become a mild complaint - 理屈(りくつ)っぽい
is "argumentative" with a sigh in it - and nothing about the sentence would look
right in print. The grammar difference is small; the social difference is not.

</details>

# ～に限ったことではない

**Meaning** · it is not confined to this case - the thing being discussed happens elsewhere too, or has happened before

<code>限(かぎ)る</code> is "to limit", and the past-attributive
<code>限(かぎ)ったこと</code> is "a matter limited to". Negate it and you get a
move a writer makes constantly: someone has just pointed at one country, one company
or one occasion, and the sentence **widens the frame**. With a time word in front of
it - <code>今(いま)に限(かぎ)ったことではない</code> - it widens backwards
instead, meaning "this is nothing new".

## Formation

The base is a noun, and the pattern is a **predicate** that closes the clause.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に限(かぎ)ったことではない | 日本(にほん) → 日本(にほん)に限(かぎ)ったことではない |
| Time noun | 今(いま)・今回(こんかい) + に限(かぎ)ったことではない | 今(いま) → 今(いま)に限(かぎ)ったことではない |
| Variant | Noun + に限(かぎ)った話(はなし)ではない | 若者(わかもの) → 若者(わかもの)に限(かぎ)った話(はなし)ではない |
| Standard frame | ～のは + Noun + に限(かぎ)ったことではない | 遅(おく)れるのは今(いま)に限(かぎ)ったことではない |
| Polite | ～に限(かぎ)ったことではありません | 当社(とうしゃ)に限(かぎ)ったことではありません |
| Continuing | ～に限(かぎ)ったことではなく、… | 今回(こんかい)に限(かぎ)ったことではなく、… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a verb cannot attach directly.
❌<code>遅(おく)れるに限(かぎ)ったことではない</code> has to be rebuilt with a
`のは` clause: <code>遅(おく)れるのは今(いま)に限(かぎ)ったことではない</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは日本(にほん)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
This is not something confined to Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が遅刻(ちこく)するのは、今(いま)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
It's hardly the first time he has turned up late.
</pre>

<pre>
人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)は、飲食(いんしょく)業界(ぎょうかい)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではありません</b>。
The labour shortage is not confined to the restaurant trade.
</pre>

<pre>
スマートフォンに頼(たよ)りすぎるのは、若者(わかもの)<b>に限(かぎ)った話(はなし)ではない</b>。
Leaning on a smartphone too heavily is not a young people's problem.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げに苦(くる)しんでいるのは、小(ちい)さな店(みせ)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
It is not only small shops that are struggling with the price rises.
</pre>

<pre>
地方(ちほう)の路線(ろせん)が廃止(はいし)されていくのは、この県(けん)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
Rural lines being closed is not something happening only in this prefecture.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)が前日(ぜんじつ)になるのは今回(こんかい)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではなく</b>、そろそろ進(すす)め方(かた)を見直(みなお)すべきだ。
Leaving the preparation to the day before is not a one-off, and it is about time we reviewed how we work.
</pre>

<pre>
気候(きこう)変動(へんどう)の影響(えいきょう)が農業(のうぎょう)に及(およ)んでいるのは一部(いちぶ)の地域(ちいき)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>と報告(ほうこく)されている。
It is reported that the effects of climate change on farming are not restricted to a few regions.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **neutral to formal**, and it is genuinely common. Newspaper commentary,
opinion columns, meeting minutes and reports use it constantly, and so do adults in
conversation; there is nothing bookish about it, and
<code>ことではありません</code> makes it as polite as you need.

It has two distinct rhetorical jobs, and the noun in front tells you which.

With a **category noun** - a country, an industry, an age group - it is *widening*.
The speaker is pushing back against an argument that has been drawn too narrowly:
<code>日本(にほん)に限(かぎ)ったことではない</code> answers someone who has just
blamed something on Japan. This use is analytical and often corrective, so it can
carry a faint note of "let's be fair about this".

With a **time noun** - <code>今(いま)</code>, <code>今回(こんかい)</code>,
<code>今日(きょう)</code> - it is *complaining*.
<code>今(いま)に限(かぎ)ったことではない</code> means the problem is chronic,
and the speaker has run out of patience with it. Said about a colleague it is a
criticism, and a fairly pointed one, because it converts a single lapse into a
pattern.

The variant <code>限(かぎ)った話(はなし)ではない</code> means the same thing and
leans slightly more spoken. Note also that `に限らず`, which the reader has from N2,
is the same verb in a different shape - and the two are not interchangeable, because
one ends a sentence and the other cannot.

## Contrast

### ～に限(かぎ)ったことではない vs ～に限(かぎ)らず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different position. `に限らず` is a **connector**
in the middle of a sentence; `に限ったことではない` is a **predicate** that ends one.

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、平日(へいじつ)も混(こ)んでいる。
It's busy on weekdays as well, not just at weekends.
</pre>

<pre>
混(こ)んでいるのは週末(しゅうまつ)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
It isn't only at weekends that it's busy.
</pre>

`に限らず` has to be followed by the wider case it opens onto - that is its whole
function. `に限ったことではない` states the widening and stops, leaving the reader to
supply the rest. If you want to name what else is included, `に限らず` is the more
economical choice.

### ～に限(かぎ)ったことではない vs ～とは限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に限ったことではない` denies that a case is **the only one**.
`とは限らない` denies that a generalisation is **always true**.

</div>

<pre>
高(たか)い物(もの)がいいとは<b>限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Expensive things aren't necessarily good.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)で判断(はんだん)してしまうのは、この店(みせ)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
Judging by price is not something that happens only in this shop.
</pre>

The two share the verb and nothing else. `とは限らない` attacks a rule
("all X are Y"); `に限ったことではない` attacks a boundary ("only X does Y"). The
grammatical tell is what precedes: a clause plus `とは` for the first, a bare noun
plus `に` for the second.

### ～に限(かぎ)ったことではない vs ～だけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけではない` is the everyday version.
`に限ったことではない` is longer, more deliberate, and sounds like a considered
correction rather than a passing remark.

</div>

<pre>
困(こま)っているのは私(わたし)<b>だけではない</b>。
I'm not the only one having trouble.
</pre>

<pre>
困(こま)っているのは私(わたし)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
It is by no means only me who is in difficulty.
</pre>

Both are correct. The second is what you would write in a letter of complaint on
behalf of a group, and the first is what you would say to a friend. The extra length
of `に限ったことではない` is doing work: it signals that the speaker has thought
about the scope of the claim.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 遅(おく)れる<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れるのは今(いま)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
  The pattern takes a noun. A verb has to be packaged into a `のは` clause first, and
  the noun slot then holds the case being widened.

- ❌ 安(やす)い物(もの)が悪(わる)い<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。 → ✅ 安(やす)い物(もの)が悪(わる)い<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
  The sentence denies a generalisation, not a boundary, so it needs `とは限らない`.
  The two patterns look similar and mean genuinely different things.

- ❌ 週末(しゅうまつ)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではなく</b>。 → ✅ 週末(しゅうまつ)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。（or …ことではなく、平日(へいじつ)も混(こ)む）
  The <code>なく</code> form is a connector and cannot end a sentence. Either finish
  with <code>ない</code> or carry on into the second half.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は当社(とうしゃ)に<b>限(かぎ)らないことではない</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は当社(とうしゃ)に<b>限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
  A blend of the two shapes, and a double negative into the bargain. The fixed form
  is <code>限(かぎ)ったこと</code>, with the past-attributive.

## Practice

**1.** 残業(ざんぎょう)が多(おお)いのは、この部署(ぶしょ)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この部署(ぶしょ)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。

The `のは` clause states the phenomenon and the noun names the case that has been
singled out; the pattern then denies that the case is the only one. Note that a
noun, not a verb, sits directly in front of `に`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 努力(どりょく)すれば報(むく)われる（に限(かぎ)ったことではない・とは限(かぎ)らない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

報(むく)われる<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

The sentence is denying a general rule - that effort is always rewarded - so it needs
`とは限らない`. `に限ったことではない` would have to follow a noun and would be
saying that effort is rewarded in other places too, which is not the point being
made.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Staff leaving after two years is not something peculiar to our company."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

社員(しゃいん)が二年(にねん)で辞(や)めていくのは、当社(とうしゃ)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではありません</b>。

The English "peculiar to" is precisely what the pattern denies. The polite
<code>ありません</code> suits the corporate setting, and <code>当社(とうしゃ)</code>
is the standard formal word for one's own company in writing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部品(ぶひん)の遅(おく)れが出(で)たのは今回(こんかい)に限(かぎ)らないことではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部品(ぶひん)の遅(おく)れが出(で)たのは今回(こんかい)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。

Two shapes have been crossed. <code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> and
<code>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</code> both exist; ❌<code>に限(かぎ)らないことではない</code>
does not, and as written it cancels itself into a claim that the delay *was* unique.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)が長引(ながび)くのは今日(きょう)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)が長引(ながび)くのは営業部(えいぎょうぶ)<b>に限(かぎ)ったことではない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) widens backwards in time, and it is a complaint: meetings always overrun, and the
speaker is tired of it. A time word in the noun slot nearly always produces that
exasperated reading.

(b) widens sideways across the organisation, and it is a defence. Somebody has
criticised the sales department, and this sentence points out that other departments
do the same. Same grammar, and one sentence is an accusation while the other is an
answer to one.

</details>

# ～うちに入らない

**Meaning** · doesn't count as; can't be called that - the thing falls short of the category it is being put in

<code>うち</code> here is a *range*, the set of things a word covers. To say
<code>料理(りょうり)のうちに入(はい)らない</code> is to say the thing does not
get inside the boundary of "cooking" at all. This is not a judgement about quality on
a scale; it is a refusal of membership, and that bluntness is what the pattern is
for.

## Formation

A noun naming the category goes in front, linked with `の`. The verb is
入(はい)る, and it is always negative.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (the category) | Noun + のうちに入(はい)らない | 料理(りょうり) → 料理(りょうり)のうちに入(はい)らない |
| With a concessive lead-in | ～くらいでは + Noun + のうちに入(はい)らない | 歩(ある)くくらいでは運動(うんどう)のうちに入(はい)らない |
| Emphatic | Noun + のうちにも入(はい)らない | 失敗(しっぱい)のうちにも入(はい)らない |
| Polite | ～のうちに入(はい)りません | 苦労(くろう)のうちに入(はい)りません |
| Past | ～のうちに入(はい)らなかった | 当時(とうじ)は贅沢(ぜいたく)のうちに入(はい)らなかった |
| Affirmative (rarer) | ～のうちに入(はい)る | これも節約(せつやく)のうちに入(はい)る |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading is <b>はいらない</b>. 入(い)る and 入(はい)る are the
same character and different verbs; ❌<code>うちに入(い)らない</code> is a reading
error, not a variant.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こんなの、料理(りょうり)の<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>よ。
This doesn't count as cooking.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで歩(ある)くくらいでは、運動(うんどう)の<b>うちに入(はい)りません</b>。
Walking as far as the station hardly counts as exercise.
</pre>

<pre>
一泊(いっぱく)しただけでは、旅行(りょこう)の<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>気(き)がする。
One night away doesn't really feel like it counts as a trip.
</pre>

<pre>
三十分(さんじゅっぷん)の遅(おく)れなど、この路線(ろせん)では遅延(ちえん)の<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>。
On this line a thirty-minute delay does not even register as a delay.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)のミスは、去年(きょねん)の一件(いっけん)に比(くら)べれば失敗(しっぱい)の<b>うちにも入(はい)らない</b>。
Compared with last year's business, his slip doesn't even begin to count as a failure.
</pre>

<pre>
一週間(いっしゅうかん)練習(れんしゅう)しただけで弾(ひ)けるようになったのなら、苦労(くろう)の<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>。
If you could play it after a week's practice, that hardly counts as hard work.
</pre>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)の冬(ふゆ)を知(し)っている人(ひと)にとっては、東京(とうきょう)の寒(さむ)さなど寒(さむ)さの<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>らしい。
For anyone who knows a Hokkaido winter, apparently Tokyo's cold does not count as cold at all.
</pre>

<pre>
挨拶(あいさつ)と天気(てんき)の話(はなし)をしただけで、込(こ)み入(い)った相談(そうだん)は一切(いっさい)していないのだから、面談(めんだん)の<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>だろう。
We exchanged greetings and talked about the weather and nothing more involved than that, so it can hardly count as a meeting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **spoken and unceremonious**. It lives in conversation, in blogs and
in dialogue; <code>入(はい)りません</code> keeps it polite enough for the office,
but even then it stays informal in flavour and would look out of place in a report.
There the equivalent would be <code>とは言(い)えない</code> or
<code>該当(がいとう)しない</code>.

Its tone is **dismissive**, and who is being dismissed decides how it lands. Applied
to **yourself** it is modest and very common:
<code>私(わたし)の英語(えいご)なんて、話(はな)せるうちに入(はい)りません</code>
is a standard piece of self-deprecation and will be heard as good manners. Applied
to **your own hardship** it is stoic - the flood, the sixteen-hour day, whatever it
was, does not count next to what somebody else went through.

Applied to **another person's effort**, it is rude, and there is no polite ending
that fixes it. Telling a colleague that their all-nighter
<code>苦労(くろう)のうちに入(はい)りません</code> is not modesty transferred; it
is a straightforward insult. Native speakers know this and use the pattern about
others mainly in teasing between equals, or about things rather than people.

Two smaller points. <code>うちにも入(はい)らない</code>, with the extra `も`,
hardens the refusal to "does not even begin to count". And the affirmative
<code>うちに入(はい)る</code> does exist -
<code>水(みず)やりも世話(せわ)のうちに入(はい)る</code> - but it is far less
common, and it is the negative that gets taught because the negative is what people
say.

## Contrast

### ～うちに入(はい)らない vs ～とは言(い)えない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both deny a label. `とは言えない` is measured and hedged;
`うちに入らない` is flat and colloquial.

</div>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の結果(けっか)は成功(せいこう)<b>とは言(い)えない</b>。
This result cannot be called a success.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなもの、成功(せいこう)の<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>。
That doesn't count as a success at all.
</pre>

The first could go in a written review; it leaves room for the result to have been
partly good. The second closes the door and comes with an audible shrug. When you
need to be careful, `とは言えない` is the one.

### ～うちに入(はい)らない vs ～に過(す)ぎない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に過ぎない` says the thing **is** X and no more.
`うちに入らない` says it is **not even** X.

</div>

<pre>
これは一(ひと)つの例(れい)<b>に過(す)ぎない</b>。
This is no more than one example.
</pre>

<pre>
例(れい)が一(ひと)つでは、証拠(しょうこ)の<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>。
A single example doesn't count as evidence.
</pre>

`に過ぎない` grants the category and limits the size of the claim inside it;
`うちに入らない` refuses the category outright. Choosing the wrong one flips how
generous the sentence sounds.

### ～うちに入(はい)らない vs ～ほどではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほどではない` compares **degree**: not to that extent.
`うちに入らない` denies **membership**: not in that class at all.

</div>

<pre>
痛(いた)いけれど、病院(びょういん)に行(い)く<b>ほどではない</b>。
It hurts, but not enough to go to hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
この程度(ていど)の痛(いた)みは、けがの<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>。
Pain at this level doesn't count as an injury.
</pre>

`ほどではない` keeps the thing on the scale and places it low; `うちに入らない` takes
it off the scale. Note also that `ほどではない` follows a verb or an adjective, where
`うちに入らない` wants a noun plus `の`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あんなのは料理(りょうり)<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>。 → ✅ あんなのは料理(りょうり)<b>のうちに入(はい)らない</b>。
  <code>うち</code> is a noun, so the category in front of it links with `の`.

- ❌ うちに入(い)らない → ✅ うちに<b>入(はい)らない</b>
  The verb is 入(はい)る, "to go in", not 入(い)る. The same character spells
  both, and the wrong reading is the most audible error a learner can make with this
  pattern.

- ❌ 運動(うんどう)のうちに入(はい)らない<b>だ</b>。 → ✅ 運動(うんどう)のうちに入(はい)ら<b>ない</b>。
  The ending is already a verb. There is no copula to add.

- ❌ 先輩(せんぱい)の徹夜(てつや)なんて、苦労(くろう)のうちに<b>入(はい)りませんね</b>。 → ✅ 徹夜(てつや)まで大変(たいへん)でしたね。
  Grammatically impeccable and socially disastrous. Used about somebody else's
  effort, the polite ending does not soften it - it makes the dismissal sound
  deliberate.

## Practice

**1.** 近所(きんじょ)を一周(いっしゅう)するだけでは、ジョギングの____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ジョギングの<b>うちに入(はい)らない</b>。

The <code>だけでは</code> clause sets up something that falls short, and the pattern
denies it entry to the category. Note the `の` linking the category noun to
<code>うち</code>, and the reading はいらない.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 熱(ねつ)は少(すこ)しあるが、仕事(しごと)を休(やす)む（ほどではない・うちに入(はい)らない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

仕事(しごと)を休(やす)む<b>ほどではない</b>。

The sentence measures a temperature against a threshold, which is a question of
degree. `うちに入らない` needs a category noun plus `の` in front of it and would have
nothing to attach to here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in casual speech: "Two people turning up doesn't count as a party."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

二人(ふたり)集(あつ)まったくらいじゃ、パーティーの<b>うちに入(はい)らないよ</b>。

<code>くらいじゃ</code> is the casual contraction of <code>くらいでは</code> and is
the natural lead-in for this pattern. Note the reading 二人(ふたり), not
❌にじん.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 一日(いちにち)三十分(さんじゅっぷん)の散歩(さんぽ)は運動(うんどう)うちに入(い)らないだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 一日(いちにち)三十分(さんじゅっぷん)の散歩(さんぽ)は運動(うんどう)<b>のうちに入(はい)らない</b>。

Three faults in one short sentence: the missing `の` after the category noun, the
wrong reading of 入(はい)る, and a stray copula after a verb that is already the
predicate.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)の中国語(ちゅうごくご)なんて、話(はな)せる<b>うちに入(はい)りません</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)の中国語(ちゅうごくご)なんて、話(はな)せる<b>うちに入(はい)りません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is modesty, and it is what a Japanese speaker would expect to hear after being
complimented on a language. The politeness and the self-deprecation reinforce each
other, and nobody takes it as a literal claim.

(b) is an insult. Turned on a third person, the same words become a verdict on
somebody else's ability, and the polite ending makes it worse rather than better -
it now sounds considered. If you want to say his Chinese is limited, use
<code>まだ勉強(べんきょう)中(ちゅう)だそうです</code> or something equally
indirect.

</details>
