# N1 - Guessing, assuming, and how it's regarded


---

# ～とされる

**Meaning** · is held to be ～; is taken to be ～; is laid down as ～ - what the world, or the rule book, treats as true, with nobody named as the one doing the treating

<code>される</code> is the passive of する, and the する behind it is the one in
<code>AをBとする</code>, "treat A as B". Delete whoever does the treating and you are
left with <code>AはBとされる</code>: A counts as B - by convention, by scholarly
consensus, or by regulation. The anonymity is the point. The sentence is not
reporting anybody's opinion; it is reporting the standing state of the world.

## Formation

The clause in front is a **quotation**, so it takes a plain form and the `と` of
quoted speech. A noun or な-adjective may appear bare or with `だ`; the bare version
is the more documentary of the two.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + とされる | 効(き)く → 効(き)くとされる |
| Verb (past) | plain past + とされる | 建(た)てられた → 建(た)てられたとされる |
| い-adjective | plain form + とされる | 望(のぞ)ましい → 望(のぞ)ましいとされる |
| な-adjective | stem or ～だ + とされる | 必要(ひつよう) → 必要(ひつよう)（だ）とされる |
| Noun | bare or ～だ + とされる | 名作(めいさく) → 名作(めいさく)（だ）とされる |
| Established view, rule in force | ～とされている | 禁止(きんし) → 禁止(きんし)とされている |
| Rule as it has stood | ～とされてきた | 定説(ていせつ) → 定説(ていせつ)とされてきた |
| Polite | ～とされています | 原則(げんそく) → 原則(げんそく)とされています |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>必要(ひつよう)とされる</code> has drifted into a fixed sense of its
own: not "is considered necessary" but simply **"is required"**. A job advert saying
<code>英語力(えいごりょく)が必要(ひつよう)とされます</code> is stating a
requirement, not summarising an opinion.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この花(はな)は幸運(こううん)のしるし<b>とされている</b>。
This flower is taken to be a sign of good luck.
</pre>

<pre>
緑茶(りょくちゃ)は体(からだ)にいい<b>とされている</b>。
Green tea is held to be good for you.
</pre>

<pre>
この寺(てら)は8世紀(せいき)に建(た)てられた<b>とされる</b>。
This temple is thought to have been built in the eighth century.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)では、申請(しんせい)は書面(しょめん)で行(おこな)う<b>とされている</b>。
Under the rules, applications are to be made in writing.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)最古(さいこ)の辞書(じしょ)<b>とされる</b>本(ほん)が展示(てんじ)されていた。
The book regarded as Japan's oldest dictionary was on display.
</pre>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)期限(きげん)を過(す)ぎた書類(しょるい)は無効(むこう)<b>とされます</b>。
Documents submitted after the deadline are treated as invalid.
</pre>

<pre>
この地方(ちほう)では、正月(しょうがつ)に刃物(はもの)を使(つか)うのは縁起(えんぎ)が悪(わる)い<b>とされてきた</b>。
In this region, using a blade at New Year has always been considered unlucky.
</pre>

<pre>
長(なが)らく定説(ていせつ)<b>とされてきた</b>この説明(せつめい)は、近年(きんねん)の調査(ちょうさ)によって見直(みなお)されつつある。
This explanation, long accepted as settled, is being reconsidered in the light of recent surveys.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Written, and institutional.** Encyclopedia entries, museum captions, textbooks,
company rules, contracts, statutes and news copy are where it lives. In speech it
turns up in lectures, guided tours and briefings - places where somebody is
speaking *for* an institution rather than for themselves.

Three flavours share the one form, and the surrounding words tell you which:

| Flavour | Typical company | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| Received wisdom | 昔(むかし)から、この地方(ちほう)では | ～は縁起(えんぎ)がいいとされている |
| Scholarly attribution | ～世紀(せいき)に、～によって | ～が書(か)いたとされる |
| Regulation in force | 規則(きそく)では、法律(ほうりつ)上(じょう) | ～は無効(むこう)とされる |

The `ている` shape is much the commoner of the two, because a convention or a rule
is a standing state rather than an event. Bare <code>とされる</code> mostly appears
attached to a noun - <code>国宝(こくほう)とされる仏像(ぶつぞう)</code> - or in
the compressed register of headlines and captions, where auxiliaries get trimmed.

What the pattern cannot do is carry your own view. <code>とされる</code> works by
removing the judge from the sentence, so
❌<code>私(わたし)は…とされます</code> has no reading at all. Among friends the
same content comes out as `らしい`, <code>と言(い)われている</code>, or just
`って`.

One consequence is worth noticing. Because the source of the judgement is hidden,
<code>とされている</code> is a comfortable place to put something the writer does
**not** personally endorse. A sentence that begins <code>…とされてきたが</code> is
very often about to argue that the received view is wrong.

## Contrast

### とされる vs と言(い)われている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と言(い)われている</code> reports what **people say**.
<code>とされている</code> reports what something **officially counts as**.

</div>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)のそばは町(まち)で一番(いちばん)うまい<b>と言(い)われている</b>。
That shop's soba is said to be the best in town.
</pre>

<pre>
この庭園(ていえん)は市(し)の文化財(ぶんかざい)<b>とされている</b>。
This garden is designated as a cultural property of the city.
</pre>

Talk in the town cannot be <code>とされている</code>: there is no body whose ruling
it is. A heritage designation can be <code>と言(い)われている</code> only at the
cost of demoting an official status to a rumour. Where the two genuinely overlap is
folklore and old scholarship, and there <code>とされている</code> is the more settled
of the two.

### とされる vs と考(かんが)えられる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> shows **reasoning**;
<code>とされる</code> shows **convention**. The first has grounds behind it, the
second has agreement.

</div>

<pre>
出土(しゅつど)した土器(どき)の形(かたち)からみて、この遺跡(いせき)はかなり古(ふる)い<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
Judging from the shape of the pottery found there, this site can be taken to be considerably old.
</pre>

<pre>
この遺跡(いせき)は弥生(やよい)時代(じだい)のもの<b>とされている</b>。
This site is taken to date from the Yayoi period.
</pre>

The first sentence shows its working and invites you to check it; it belongs in the
argument of a paper. The second states where the question currently rests and
invites you to accept it; it belongs in the background section, or on the label
beside the exhibit. Swapping them changes what the reader is being asked to do.

### とされる vs ことになっている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for rules, <code>とされている</code> is the **wording of the
rule**; `ことになっている` is **how things are arranged** in practice.

</div>

<pre>
うちの部(ぶ)では朝(あさ)の会議(かいぎ)に出(で)る<b>ことになっています</b>。
In our department we're supposed to go to the morning meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
就業(しゅうぎょう)規則(きそく)では、遅刻(ちこく)の届(とど)け出(で)は当日(とうじつ)のうちに行(おこな)う<b>とされている</b>。
The staff regulations provide that notice of lateness is to be given the same day.
</pre>

`ことになっている` is ordinary spoken Japanese and covers arrangements of every
size, down to taking your shoes off at the door. <code>とされている</code> quotes a
written provision, and using it for a household habit sounds as though you were
reading from a statute. The reverse substitution is milder: `ことになっている` for a
real regulation simply makes it sound less formal than it is.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)はこの方法(ほうほう)が安全(あんぜん)だ<b>とされます</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)はこの方法(ほうほう)が安全(あんぜん)だ<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
  The pattern works by deleting the person who judges. Put 私(わたし)は back in
  and there is nothing left for the sentence to mean.

- ❌ この習慣(しゅうかん)は江戸(えど)時代(じだい)に始(はじ)まる<b>とされる</b>。 → ✅ この習慣(しゅうかん)は江戸(えど)時代(じだい)に始(はじ)まった<b>とされる</b>。
  English says "is said to have begun" with a present-tense main verb, and learners
  copy that across. Japanese does not: `と` quotes the clause as it stands, so a past
  event needs the past form **inside** the quotation.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>とされています</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>見込(みこ)みです</b>。
  A one-off forecast is neither a convention nor a rule. <code>とされる</code> needs
  something that stands over time.

- ❌ その映画(えいが)、けっこう面白(おもしろ)い<b>とされてるよ</b>。 → ✅ その映画(えいが)、けっこう面白(おもしろ)い<b>らしいよ</b>。
  Grammatical and absurd. Recommending a film to a friend is not the issuing of a
  finding.

## Practice

**1.** この温泉(おんせん)は傷(きず)によく効(き)く____。（received wisdom, in a guidebook）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

効(き)く<b>とされている</b>。

A guidebook is reporting a long-standing local belief with no named believer, which
is the received-wisdom use exactly. The `ている` is there because a belief is a
standing state, not an event.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 隣(となり)の課(か)の山口(やまぐち)さん、来年(らいねん)転勤(てんきん)する（とされている・らしい）よ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

転勤(てんきん)する<b>らしい</b>よ。

Office gossip has no institution behind it, and `よ` puts the sentence firmly in
conversation. <code>とされている</code> would make a colleague's transfer sound like
a clause in the company rules.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a museum caption: "This scroll is held to be the work of a painter of the fifteenth century."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この巻物(まきもの)は15世紀(せいき)の画家(がか)の作(さく)<b>とされる</b>。

A caption is compressed, so the bare <code>とされる</code> after a noun is the
natural choice, though <code>とされている</code> would also pass. "Is held to be"
here attaches to a noun, so no `だ` is needed in front of `と`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 私(わたし)は、法律(ほうりつ)ではこの契約(けいやく)は無効(むこう)になるとされています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 法律(ほうりつ)上(じょう)、この契約(けいやく)は無効(むこう)<b>とされています</b>。
"In law, this contract is treated as void."

Two repairs. 私(わたし)は cannot appear in a sentence whose grammar exists to
delete the person judging. And <code>無効(むこう)になるとされる</code> doubles the
machinery, since <code>とされる</code> already means "is treated as"; the noun goes
straight in front of `と`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この絵(え)は本人(ほんにん)が描(か)いたもの<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
(b) この絵(え)は本人(ほんにん)が描(か)いたもの<b>とされている</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the writer's own conclusion, drawn from evidence the reader has just been
shown or is about to be. It can be argued with on that evidence, and the writer
expects it to be.

(b) reports the attribution that currently stands. The writer is not claiming to
have worked it out and takes no responsibility for it - which is why (b) so often
comes just before a `が` and a paragraph explaining why the attribution is doubtful.

</details>

# ～とみられる

**Meaning** · is believed to be ～; is thought to have ～; is expected to ～ - the estimate that stands until somebody confirms it

Written <code>と見(み)られる</code> or, in news copy, usually in kana. The
literal shape is "is seen as", and the seeing is done by nobody in particular -
investigators, analysts, the people whose business it is to know. That is what the
pattern is for: it lets a writer put a number, a cause or a forecast on the page
**without asserting it**, because the facts are not in yet.

## Formation

The clause in front is quoted, so it takes the plain form and `と`. Nouns and
な-adjectives go in bare or with `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + とみられる | 増(ふ)える → 増(ふ)えるとみられる |
| Verb (past) | plain past + とみられる | 逃(に)げた → 逃(に)げたとみられる |
| い-adjective | plain form + とみられる | 難(むずか)しい → 難(むずか)しいとみられる |
| な-adjective | stem or ～だ + とみられる | 深刻(しんこく) → 深刻(しんこく)（だ）とみられる |
| Noun | bare or ～だ + とみられる | 漏電(ろうでん) → 漏電(ろうでん)（だ）とみられる |
| The prevailing estimate | ～とみられている | 回復(かいふく)する → 回復(かいふく)するとみられている |
| Modifying a noun | ～とみられる + Noun | 犯人(はんにん) → 犯人(はんにん)とみられる男(おとこ) |
| Polite | ～とみられます | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れるとみられます |

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not confuse this with <code>～が見(み)られる</code>, which has no
`と` and means "is observed, can be seen":
<code>回復(かいふく)の兆(きざ)しが見(み)られる</code>, "signs of a recovery
are visible". The `と` is the whole difference. With it you have an estimate; without
it you have an observation.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
犯人(はんにん)はまだ近(ちか)くにいる<b>とみられる</b>。
The culprit is believed to be still in the area.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)には結論(けつろん)が出(で)る<b>とみられている</b>。
A conclusion is expected next week.
</pre>

<pre>
出火(しゅっか)の原因(げんいん)は漏電(ろうでん)<b>とみられています</b>。
The fire is believed to have been started by an electrical fault.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)にはおよそ3万人(まんにん)が集(あつ)まった<b>とみられる</b>。
Around thirty thousand people are thought to have gathered at the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)は来年度(らいねんど)から実施(じっし)される<b>とみられる</b>。
The new system is expected to come into force from next year.
</pre>

<pre>
長雨(ながあめ)の影響(えいきょう)で、ことしの収穫(しゅうかく)は例年(れいねん)を下回(したまわ)る<b>とみられています</b>。
Because of the long spell of rain, this year's harvest is expected to fall short of the usual level.
</pre>

<pre>
交渉(こうしょう)は難航(なんこう)しており、年内(ねんない)の決着(けっちゃく)は難(むずか)しい<b>とみられる</b>。
The talks are going badly, and a settlement within the year is thought unlikely.
</pre>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)市場(しじょう)の需要(じゅよう)が戻(もど)ったことが、今期(こんき)の増益(ぞうえき)につながった<b>とみられている</b>。
The return of demand in overseas markets is thought to be behind this term's rise in profits.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **newspaper Japanese**, and once you have noticed it you will hear it in
every bulletin. Its home is the sentence that reports a cause, a casualty figure,
an identification or a timetable before any of them are official. It is equally at
home in analysts' notes, company outlooks and government projections.

Two directions, one form:

| Looking at | Example shape | English |
|---|---|---|
| Something already over | ～した／～だったとみられる | is thought to have ～ |
| Something still to come | ～する／～になるとみられる | is expected to ～ |

The forward-looking use is the one learners tend to miss. <code>発表(はっぴょう)は
来月(らいげつ)になるとみられる</code> is not "it seems it will be next month" in
the sense of a personal hunch; it is the considered expectation of the people who
follow such things.

`ている` again marks the estimate as one that stands and is shared -
<code>とみられている</code> is "the current view is". Without `ている`, the sentence
reads as the writer's own assessment given the material, and in a news report the
two are used almost interchangeably.

The pattern is **impersonal to the point that you cannot get into it**:
❌<code>私(わたし)は…とみられる</code> is not a modest way to say "I think". If
you need to say that a report's conclusion is your own,
<code>と考(かんが)えています</code> or
<code>と判断(はんだん)しました</code> does the job. Conversationally, none of this
applies - a friend's late arrival is `らしい` or `みたい`, never
<code>とみられる</code>.

## Contrast

### とみられる vs とされる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とみられる</code> is a **provisional estimate** that later
facts may overturn. <code>とされる</code> is a **settled convention or ruling** that
would take an authority to change.

</div>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)は数億円(すうおくえん)に上(のぼ)る<b>とみられている</b>。
The damage is believed to run to several hundred million yen.
</pre>

<pre>
この品種(ひんしゅ)は寒(さむ)さに強(つよ)い<b>とされている</b>。
This variety is held to be resistant to cold.
</pre>

The damage figure is somebody's working estimate, and tomorrow's paper may carry a
different one. The variety's cold-hardiness is what growers and catalogues have
agreed on for years. Ask whether the statement is waiting to be confirmed or has
already been accepted.

### とみられる vs と考(かんが)えられる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> is **reasoned** and belongs
to argument. <code>とみられる</code> is **estimated** and belongs to reporting.

</div>

<pre>
この数値(すうち)の変化(へんか)は測定(そくてい)方法(ほうほう)の違(ちが)いによるもの<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
The change in these figures is likely to be down to a difference in how they were measured.
</pre>

<pre>
感染(かんせん)の拡大(かくだい)は来月(らいげつ)まで続(つづ)く<b>とみられる</b>。
The spread of infection is expected to continue into next month.
</pre>

The first sentence is doing intellectual work in front of you: it offers an
explanation and stands behind the inference. The second is passing on a projection
without showing any workings at all. That is why an academic paper is full of
<code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> and a news bulletin is full of
<code>とみられる</code>.

### とみられる vs ようだ・らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical job, different height. `ようだ` and `らしい` are one
person's read of the evidence; <code>とみられる</code> is the read of an unnamed
professional body.

</div>

<pre>
隣(となり)の家(いえ)、もう誰(だれ)も住(す)んでいない<b>みたい</b>だね。
Looks like nobody lives next door any more.
</pre>

<pre>
空(あ)き家(や)は今後(こんご)さらに増(ふ)える<b>とみられる</b>。
The number of empty houses is expected to rise further.
</pre>

Say the second one to a neighbour over the fence and you sound as though you were
reading the evening news aloud. Put the first into a government white paper and the
paper has no authority. The content is close; the voice is not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)景気(けいき)が良(よ)くなる<b>とみられます</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)景気(けいき)が良(よ)くなる<b>とみています</b>。
  The passive deletes the observer, so you cannot be the observer. The active
  <code>とみている</code> is exactly how an analyst states their own forecast.

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)より改善(かいぜん)<b>とみられる</b>。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)より改善(かいぜん)が<b>見(み)られる</b>。
  "An improvement can be seen" is the <code>が見(み)られる</code> construction, with no `と`
  at all. Adding `と` turns an observation into an estimate about something else.

- ❌ ゆうべの地震(じしん)で建物(たてもの)が倒(たお)れる<b>とみられている</b>。 → ✅ ゆうべの地震(じしん)で建物(たてもの)が倒(たお)れた<b>とみられている</b>。
  Tense sits **inside** the quotation. For something that has already happened the
  quoted clause must be past, however the English is phrased.

- ❌ 木村(きむら)さん、今日(きょう)は来(こ)ない<b>とみられるよ</b>。 → ✅ 木村(きむら)さん、今日(きょう)は来(こ)ない<b>みたいだよ</b>。
  Nothing is wrong with the grammar; it is the register that fails. Reporting on a
  colleague to a colleague is not a press briefing.

## Practice

**1.** 事故(じこ)を起(お)こした車(くるま)は盗(ぬす)まれたもの____。（news report）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

盗(ぬす)まれたもの<b>とみられています</b>。

Police have an early view and no confirmation yet, which is what
<code>とみられる</code> exists for. `もの` gives a noun for the `と` to attach to, so
`だ` is optional here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この地域(ちいき)のかつての主産業(しゅさんぎょう)は漁業(ぎょぎょう)だった（とされている・とみられている）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

漁業(ぎょぎょう)だった<b>とされている</b>。

Local history that has been settled for generations is a convention, not a working
estimate. <code>とみられている</code> would suggest the question was live and someone
was still investigating it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a news bulletin: "The number of visitors is expected to top last year's figure."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来場者数(らいじょうしゃすう)は昨年(さくねん)を上回(うわまわ)る<b>とみられます</b>。

"Is expected to" going forward in time is the second of the pattern's two
directions. Keep the verb in the plain non-past inside the quotation; the
expectation lives in <code>とみられる</code>, not in the verb.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 各社(かくしゃ)の決算(けっさん)を見(み)る限(かぎ)り、私(わたし)は回復(かいふく)は来年(らいねん)まで待(ま)たなければならないとみられます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 各社(かくしゃ)の決算(けっさん)を見(み)る限(かぎ)り、回復(かいふく)は来年(らいねん)まで待(ま)たなければならない<b>とみられます</b>。
"On the evidence of the companies' results, a recovery looks likely to have to wait until next year."

The only repair needed is deleting 私(わたし)は. The pattern's grammar has already
removed the person doing the judging; naming yourself as well leaves the sentence
with two incompatible viewpoints. If the assessment must be marked as your own,
switch to <code>とみています</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 停電(ていでん)の原因(げんいん)は落雷(らくらい)<b>とみられている</b>。
(b) 停電(ていでん)の原因(げんいん)は落雷(らくらい)<b>だった</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a report filed while the investigation is still open. It commits nobody: if
the cause turns out to be a failed transformer, the sentence was not wrong, it was
provisional. This is why bulletins are written this way.

(b) states the cause as established fact. A reporter writes it only after the power
company has said so, and a listener hearing it treats the matter as closed.

</details>

# ～ものと思われる

**Meaning** · it is presumed that ～; it must be assumed that ～ - read *ものとおもわれる*; the most formal way Japanese has of saying "we take it that"

Three things are stacked here. <code>思(おも)われる</code> is not a passive: it is
the **spontaneous** -れる, so the thought is presented as arising by itself rather
than as anybody's opinion. <code>もの</code> then packages that thought as a state
of affairs rather than a feeling. And the whole thing is left without a subject. What
comes out is the voice of an incident report, an official statement or a technical
notice: *this is how the matter must be taken to stand.*

## Formation

<code>もの</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it is in **noun-modifying**
shape - not the quoted shape that `と` demands. This is the single point learners get
wrong.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ものと思(おも)われる | 終(お)わる → 終(お)わるものと思(おも)われる |
| Verb (past) | plain past + ものと思(おも)われる | 失(うしな)われた → 失(うしな)われたものと思(おも)われる |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ものと思(おも)われる | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かないものと思(おも)われる |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものと思(おも)われる | 少(すく)ない → 少(すく)ないものと思(おも)われる |
| な-adjective | ～な + ものと思(おも)われる | 安全(あんぜん)な → 安全(あんぜん)なものと思(おも)われる |
| Noun | ～による / ～の + ものと思(おも)われる | 漏電(ろうでん) → 漏電(ろうでん)によるものと思(おも)われる |
| Polite | ～ものと思(おも)われます | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れるものと思(おも)われます |
| Same frame, other verbs | ～ものと考(かんが)えられる／みられる | 終(お)わる → 終(お)わるものとみられる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> takes a **quotation**, so a
な-adjective keeps its `だ`. <code>ものと思(おも)われる</code> takes a **noun
modifier**, so the same word takes `な`. ❌<code>安全(あんぜん)だものと思(おも)われる</code>,
✅<code>安全(あんぜん)なものと思(おも)われる</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
作業(さぎょう)は明日(あした)中(じゅう)に終(お)わる<b>ものと思(おも)われます</b>。
The work is expected to be finished by the end of tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
原因(げんいん)は操作(そうさ)ミスによる<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>。
The cause is presumed to be an error in operation.
</pre>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)はごく一部(いちぶ)にとどまる<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>。
The damage must be assumed to be confined to a small area.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)の記録(きろく)はすでに失(うしな)われた<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>。
The records from that period are presumed to have been lost.
</pre>

<pre>
降雪(こうせつ)のため、到着(とうちゃく)は30分(ぷん)ほど遅(おく)れる<b>ものと思(おも)われます</b>。
Because of the snowfall, arrival is expected to be about thirty minutes late.
</pre>

<pre>
ご指摘(してき)の点(てん)は、初期(しょき)設定(せってい)の誤(あやま)りによる<b>ものと思(おも)われます</b>。
The matter you raise appears to stem from a mistake in the initial settings.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)使(つか)われていなかった<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>農具(のうぐ)が、倉(くら)の奥(おく)から出(で)てきた。
Farm tools that appear to have gone unused for many years came out of the back of the storehouse.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の不具合(ふぐあい)は、特定(とくてい)の条件(じょうけん)が重(かさ)なったときにのみ起(お)きる<b>ものと思(おも)われます</b>。
The present fault is believed to occur only when a particular set of conditions coincides.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Written, and formal even by written standards.** Accident and audit reports,
official announcements, technical support replies, archaeological and historical
description. In speech it survives only where somebody is reading a position out
loud - a briefing, a press conference, a courtroom. There is no casual version. If
you find yourself reaching for it in conversation, the word you want is `みたい`,
`らしい` or <code>と思(おも)います</code>.

Notice what it is doing socially. A support engineer writing
<code>設定(せってい)の誤(あやま)りによるものと思(おも)われます</code> is
telling a customer their own mistake caused the problem - and the grammar contrives
to have nobody say so. No one accuses, no one asserts; the conclusion simply presents
itself. That deniability is why the pattern is so common in exactly the messages
people find hardest to write.

For the same reason it is a hedge, not a claim, and it can be **too** soft. A report
that says <code>問題(もんだい)はないものと思(おも)われます</code> has not
declared the system safe; it has declined to. Where you do want to commit,
<code>確認(かくにん)しました</code> or
<code>問題(もんだい)ありません</code> is the honest form.

One neighbouring expression is worth knowing because it appears in apologies.
<code>ものと思(おも)っておりました</code> - active, humble, past - means "I had
been working on the assumption that", and it is the standard way of explaining a
mistake without excusing it: <code>すでに手配(てはい)済(ず)みのものと思(おも)っておりました</code>.
Do not confuse it with the -れる form on this page; they belong to different halves
of the same letter.

## Contrast

### ものと思(おも)われる vs と思(おも)われる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と思(おも)われる</code> is an impression.
<code>ものと思(おも)われる</code> is a **finding**: <code>もの</code> turns the
impression into a state of affairs, and raises the register a step.

</div>

<pre>
この案(あん)には無理(むり)がある<b>と思(おも)われる</b>。
There does seem to be a difficulty with this proposal.
</pre>

<pre>
契約(けいやく)は来月(らいげつ)中(ちゅう)に成立(せいりつ)する<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>。
The contract is to be assumed to be concluded within next month.
</pre>

The grammar differs as well as the tone. The first takes `と` and therefore a
quotation, so a noun in front of it keeps `だ`. The second takes a noun modifier, so
the same noun needs `の` or による. Get this backwards and the sentence is not merely
stiff, it is ungrammatical.

### ものと思(おも)われる vs と考(かんが)えられる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>思(おも)われる</code> is where the conclusion **comes to
you**; <code>考(かんが)えられる</code> is where the evidence **permits** it. The
first reports an impression that formed; the second reports reasoning that holds.

</div>

<pre>
この傾向(けいこう)は人口(じんこう)構成(こうせい)の変化(へんか)によるもの<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
This trend can be attributed to the change in the make-up of the population.
</pre>

<pre>
回答(かいとう)が少(すく)ないのは質問(しつもん)が長(なが)すぎたため<b>と思(おも)われる</b>。
The low number of replies is presumably because the questions were too long.
</pre>

In a paper, the two divide the labour: <code>考(かんが)えられる</code> for the
argument you are prepared to defend, <code>思(おも)われる</code> for the softer
remark you are putting on the record without defending it. A supervisor who changes
your <code>思(おも)われる</code> to <code>考(かんが)えられる</code> is telling
you to show your working.

### ものと思(おも)われる vs ものとみられる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are impersonal, but <code>思(おも)われる</code> keeps a
trace of a **mind forming the impression**; <code>みられる</code> is pure **outside
estimate**, and is what a newspaper prints.

</div>

<pre>
流出(りゅうしゅつ)した情報(じょうほう)は数千件(すうせんけん)に及(およ)ぶ<b>ものとみられる</b>。
The leaked records are believed to number in the thousands.
</pre>

<pre>
流出(りゅうしゅつ)は外部(がいぶ)からの不正(ふせい)アクセスによる<b>ものと思(おも)われます</b>。
The leak appears to have been caused by unauthorised access from outside.
</pre>

The first is how the story is reported; the second is how the company writes to its
customers about it. In many sentences either would pass, and where they do, the
choice is a choice of speaker: a reporter counting, or an organisation explaining
itself.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この方法(ほうほう)は安全(あんぜん)だ<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>。 → ✅ この方法(ほうほう)は安全(あんぜん)な<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>。
  <code>もの</code> is a noun and takes a noun modifier. The `だ` belongs with `と`
  patterns such as <code>と考(かんが)えられる</code>, not with this one.

- ❌ 原因(げんいん)は故障(こしょう)<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>。 → ✅ 原因(げんいん)は故障(こしょう)による<b>ものと思(おも)われる</b>。
  A bare noun cannot modify <code>もの</code>. Bridge it with による, or turn it into
  a verb: <code>故障(こしょう)したものと思(おも)われる</code>.

- ❌ 私(わたし)はこの案(あん)でよい<b>ものと思(おも)われます</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)はこの案(あん)でよい<b>ものと思(おも)っております</b>。
  With 私(わたし)は in the sentence you need the active verb. The -れる form
  exists precisely to leave the thinker out.

- ❌ あの店(みせ)、もう閉(し)まっている<b>ものと思(おも)われるね</b>。 → ✅ あの店(みせ)、もう閉(し)まってる<b>みたいだね</b>。
  A closed shop-front does not need an incident report. The pattern has no casual
  register at all, and `ね` on the end of it sounds like a parody.

## Practice

**1.** 今回(こんかい)の停止(ていし)は部品(ぶひん)の劣化(れっか)による____思(おも)われます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

劣化(れっか)による<b>ものと</b>思(おも)われます。

<code>による</code> is already a noun modifier, so it is waiting for a noun -
<code>もの</code> - and the frame completes with `と`. This is the standard shape for
naming a cause in a report.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)から、この方式(ほうしき)は有効(ゆうこう)（だと考(かんが)えられる・ものと思(おも)われる）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

有効(ゆうこう)<b>だと考(かんが)えられる</b>。

Two reasons. The sentence names its grounds -
<code>調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)から</code> - which calls for the reasoning
verb; and <code>有効(ゆうこう)</code> is written here with `だ`, the quoted shape,
which <code>ものと思(おも)われる</code> cannot take. To use this page's pattern you
would have to write <code>有効(ゆうこう)なものと思(おも)われる</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a service notice: "The affected accounts are presumed to number about two hundred."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

影響(えいきょう)を受(う)けたアカウントは200件(けん)ほどある<b>ものと思(おも)われます</b>。

"Are presumed to" with a figure attached is this pattern's home ground. A verb -
<code>ある</code>, <code>及(およ)ぶ</code>, <code>のぼる</code> - gives
<code>もの</code> something to hang on, which is tidier than trying to attach the
number directly.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 前回(ぜんかい)の連絡(れんらく)で了承(りょうしょう)をいただいたものと思(おも)われましたので、作業(さぎょう)を進(すす)めました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 前回(ぜんかい)の連絡(れんらく)で了承(りょうしょう)をいただいたものと<b>思(おも)っておりました</b>ので、作業(さぎょう)を進(すす)めました。
"I had understood from our last exchange that we had your approval, so I went ahead."

This is an explanation of the writer's own mistaken assumption, and an assumption has
an owner. The -れる form removes the owner, which leaves the sentence unable to say
whose error it was - the one thing this apology has to say.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) システムに問題(もんだい)はない<b>ものと思(おも)われます</b>。
(b) システムに問題(もんだい)はありませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) hedges. Nobody has certified the system; the writer is putting on record that,
so far as anything visible goes, the assumption should be that it is sound. If a
fault turns up next week, this sentence was not a lie.

(b) certifies. The writer has checked and is standing behind the result. In a report
the two sentences carry very different amounts of responsibility, and choosing (a)
where (b) is expected reads as evasion rather than caution.

</details>

# ～ものとして

**Meaning** · on the assumption that ～; treating it as though ～ - fixes a premise so that the work can go ahead, whether or not the premise is true

<code>もの</code> here is the abstract "case, state of affairs", and
<code>として</code> is "as, in the capacity of". Together they say: *take it as being
so, and proceed*. The pattern is not interested in whether the premise is correct.
It is interested in what follows from adopting it - a plan, a calculation, a piece
of paperwork, a polite fiction.

## Formation

<code>もの</code> is a noun, so what comes in front is a **noun modifier**. Nouns and
な-adjectives reach it through である rather than `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ものとして | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るものとして |
| Verb (past) | plain past + ものとして | 受(う)かった → 受(う)かったものとして |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ものとして | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないものとして |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものとして | ない → ないものとして |
| な-adjective | ～である + ものとして | 有効(ゆうこう) → 有効(ゆうこう)であるものとして |
| Noun | ～である + ものとして | 事実(じじつ) → 事実(じじつ)であるものとして |
| Predicate form | ～ものとする | 成立(せいりつ)した → 成立(せいりつ)したものとする |
| With a treating verb | ～ものとして扱(あつか)う／考(かんが)える | ない → ないものとして扱(あつか)う |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ものとして</code> is **adverbial**: it sets up the premise
and then something has to happen on the strength of it. It cannot finish a sentence.
To end on the premise itself, switch to <code>ものとする</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ものとする</code> is the ordinary way a Japanese contract says
*shall*: <code>費用(ひよう)は甲(こう)が負担(ふたん)するものとする</code>,
"the costs shall be borne by Party A". Its close relative
<code>ものとみなす</code> is *shall be deemed*. Neither is unusual language in a
document, and neither belongs anywhere else.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>ものとして</b>準備(じゅんび)しよう。
Let's get ready on the assumption that it will rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)の話(はなし)は聞(き)かなかった<b>ものとして</b>ください。
Please treat what I just said as something you never heard.
</pre>

<pre>
空気(くうき)の抵抗(ていこう)はない<b>ものとして</b>計算(けいさん)しなさい。
Calculate on the assumption that there is no air resistance.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は全員(ぜんいん)初(はじ)めて来(く)る<b>ものとして</b>、案内(あんない)を作(つく)り直(なお)した。
Assuming that every participant would be coming for the first time, we rewrote the directions.
</pre>

<pre>
当面(とうめん)この条件(じょうけん)は変(か)わらない<b>ものとして</b>、見積(みつ)もりを出(だ)します。
I'll draw up the estimate on the basis that these terms will hold for the time being.
</pre>

<pre>
期日(きじつ)までにご連絡(れんらく)がない場合(ばあい)は、参加(さんか)されない<b>ものとして</b>扱(あつか)わせていただきます。
If we do not hear from you by the closing date, we will take it that you are not attending.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は自分(じぶん)の失敗(しっぱい)を、はじめから無(な)かった<b>ものとして</b>話(はな)している。
He talks about his own failure as though it had never happened in the first place.
</pre>

<pre>
本(ほん)契約(けいやく)は、双方(そうほう)の署名(しょめい)をもって成立(せいりつ)した<b>ものとする</b>。
This agreement shall be deemed to have been concluded upon signature by both parties.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal, but not confined to writing. Four settings account for nearly every use:

| Setting | What the premise is for |
|---|---|
| Planning and estimating | working from a figure nobody has confirmed yet |
| Exam and textbook problems | throwing away the complications on purpose |
| Contracts and notices | fixing what will count as having happened |
| Social smoothing | agreeing to treat something as unsaid or unseen |

The exam use is worth recognising on sight. A physics or maths question that says
<code>摩擦(まさつ)はないものとする</code> is not claiming there is no friction; it
is telling you to ignore it. Read it as "for the purposes of this question".

The social use is the one that carries feeling.
<code>聞(き)かなかったものとしてください</code> asks the listener to co-operate in
a fiction, and it is a real request, made when something has been said that would be
awkward to have on the record. It is polite and slightly heavy - the sentence
acknowledges that it is asking a favour. Delivered to a superior it is fine;
delivered to a friend it sounds unexpectedly stiff, and 「今(いま)のなし」 is what
you actually say.

Because the premise is adopted rather than believed, the pattern is neutral about
truth. <code>無(な)かったものとして</code> can describe a reasonable simplification
or a piece of self-deception, and only the surrounding sentence tells you which.

## Contrast

### ものとして vs としたら・とすれば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `としたら` **entertains** a supposition and wonders what would
follow. <code>ものとして</code> **adopts** one and gets on with the work.

</div>

<pre>
宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たった<b>としたら</b>、何(なに)に使(つか)う？
If you won the lottery, what would you spend it on?
</pre>

<pre>
予算(よさん)は去年(きょねん)と同(おな)じ<b>であるものとして</b>、計画(けいかく)を立(た)てた。
We drew up the plan taking the budget to be the same as last year's.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences go wrong. A daydream about a lottery win is not a
premise anybody is working from, so <code>ものとして</code> makes it sound like a
board meeting. The budget sentence with `としたら` stops being a plan and becomes
speculation. The test is whether real work happens in the second half.

### ものとして vs ことにして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことにする` is a **decision**, and with a past clause it means
*pretend*. <code>ものとして</code> is a **working premise**, and pretending is only
one of the things you can do with it.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)のことは見(み)なかった<b>ことにする</b>よ。
I'll pretend I didn't see anything today.
</pre>

<pre>
在庫(ざいこ)はゼロである<b>ものとして</b>発注(はっちゅう)をかけた。
We placed the order on the basis that stock was at zero.
</pre>

<code>見(み)なかったことにする</code> is warm, personal and a little conspiratorial -
one person choosing to let something go. The stock sentence is a business decision
about how to treat a number. Where the two do overlap, in the social use,
<code>ものとして</code> is the more formal and the more distancing of the two.

### ものとして vs つもりで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもりで` sets an assumption about **yourself and your attitude**.
<code>ものとして</code> sets an assumption about **the facts**.

</div>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)になった<b>つもりで</b>、一(いち)から勉強(べんきょう)し直(なお)すことにした。
I decided to start studying again from scratch, as though I were a new recruit.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から人手(ひとで)が増(ふ)える<b>ものとして</b>、作業(さぎょう)を分(わ)けた。
We split up the work on the assumption that we'd have more people from next month.
</pre>

`つもりで` is about the frame of mind you adopt, and the person adopting it is always
the subject of the main clause. <code>ものとして</code> is about a state of the world
that anybody could adopt, and it is happy in an impersonal sentence where `つもりで`
would be strange.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 事実(じじつ)<b>ものとして</b>扱(あつか)う。 → ✅ 事実(じじつ)である<b>ものとして</b>扱(あつか)う。
  A bare noun cannot modify <code>もの</code>. Bridge it with である - or, in a
  contract, with とみなす.

- ❌ 来(こ)られない方(かた)は欠席(けっせき)する<b>ものとして</b>。 → ✅ 来(こ)られない方(かた)は欠席(けっせき)する<b>ものとします</b>。
  <code>ものとして</code> is adverbial and leaves the sentence unfinished. If nothing
  follows, you want <code>ものとする</code>.

- ❌ 宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たった<b>ものとして</b>、世界(せかい)一周(いっしゅう)したいなあ。 → ✅ 宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たった<b>としたら</b>、世界(せかい)一周(いっしゅう)したいなあ。
  Nothing is being worked out here, so there is no premise to adopt - only a
  daydream, which is `としたら` territory.

- ❌ 新人(しんじん)である<b>ものとして</b>、一(いち)から学(まな)び直(なお)す。 → ✅ 新人(しんじん)になった<b>つもりで</b>、一(いち)から学(まな)び直(なお)す。
  Adopting an attitude towards yourself is `つもりで`. The original reads as though
  somebody's employment status were being fixed for administrative purposes.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まる____、前(まえ)の日(ひ)に移動(いどう)しておこう。

止(と)まる<b>ものとして</b>、前(まえ)の日(ひ)に移動(いどう)しておこう。

</details>

speaker feels. In a meeting (b) reassures; between friends it sounds alarmingly like
a ruling.

</details>

# 可能性がある

**Meaning** · there is a possibility that ～ - read *かのうせいがある*; the neutral, measurable way of saying "might", and the one that survives in writing

<code>可能性(かのうせい)</code> is a noun - *possibility* - so the whole expression
is literally "a possibility of ～ exists". That noun is what makes the pattern
useful. A possibility can be high or low, can be ruled out, can be admitted; none of
that is available to `かもしれない`, which is a bare ending with no handles on it.

## Usage

The clause in front modifies <code>可能性(かのうせい)</code> as a noun, so it is in
noun-modifying shape: a verb or い-adjective goes in plain, a な-adjective takes `な`,
and a noun takes `の`. <code>来週(らいしゅう)になる可能性(かのうせい)</code>,
<code>不十分(ふじゅうぶん)な可能性(かのうせい)</code>,
<code>故障(こしょう)の可能性(かのうせい)</code>. Because it is a noun, the verb
at the end is free to vary, and that is where most of the expressive work happens:

| Shape | Force |
|---|---|
| ～可能性(かのうせい)がある | a possibility exists - the plain statement |
| ～可能性(かのうせい)が高(たか)い／低(ひく)い | likely / unlikely |
| ～可能性(かのうせい)も十分(じゅうぶん)にある | quite conceivable |
| ～可能性(かのうせい)は否定(ひてい)できない | cannot be ruled out |
| ～可能性(かのうせい)はない／ゼロだ | flatly impossible |
| ～可能性(かのうせい)が出(で)てきた | a possibility has arisen |

The one collocation to memorise is <b>高(たか)い／低(ひく)い</b> for degree.
Japanese measures a possibility by height, never by amount, so
❌<code>可能性(かのうせい)が多(おお)い</code> is wrong however natural "a big
possibility" feels in English.

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)になる<b>可能性(かのうせい)があります</b>。
There is a chance it will turn to snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
その情報(じょうほう)は古(ふる)い<b>可能性(かのうせい)がある</b>。
That information may well be out of date.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が長引(ながび)く<b>可能性(かのうせい)が高(たか)い</b>ので、夕食(ゆうしょく)の予約(よやく)は遅(おそ)めにしておきます。
The meeting is quite likely to run over, so I'll book dinner for a bit later.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ不具合(ふぐあい)が他(ほか)の機種(きしゅ)でも起(お)きる<b>可能性(かのうせい)は否定(ひてい)できません</b>。
We cannot rule out the possibility that the same fault occurs on other models as well.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Formal, and neutral about whether the outcome is welcome.** It belongs to
weather bulletins, medical explanations, engineering reports, risk assessments and
careful business speech. In relaxed conversation the same idea comes out as
`かもしれない`, and reaching for
<code>可能性(かのうせい)があります</code> over lunch makes you sound as though you
were briefing a committee on your own afternoon.

Its neutrality is the thing to hold on to. A championship win, a promotion and a
train delay can all be a <code>可能性(かのうせい)</code>; only the last of the
three can be an <code>恐(おそ)れ</code>. Slotted onto the scale the book has been
building since N4, it sits at the low end, but with a different voice from its
neighbours:

| Expression | Confidence | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| ～かもしれない | might, could | a person weighing it up |
| ～可能性(かのうせい)がある | there is a possibility | an assessment, good news or bad |
| ～恐(おそ)れがある | there is a risk | an assessment, bad news only |
| ～可能性(かのうせい)が高(たか)い | probably | an assessment with the odds attached |
| ～だろう／でしょう | probably | a person's general guess |
| ～はずだ | should be | grounds you can point to |
| ～に違(ちが)いない | must be | evidence plus conviction |

Two turns of phrase are worth knowing because they are so common in careful speech.
<code>可能性(かのうせい)は否定(ひてい)できない</code> is how a spokesperson
concedes something without conceding it, and
<code>可能性(かのうせい)が出(で)てきた</code> reports that a possibility has
newly appeared - the sentence a doctor or a project manager says when the situation
has just changed.

Finally, keep the "potential" sense separate.
<code>可能性(かのうせい)を秘(ひ)めた若(わか)い選手(せんしゅ)</code> means a
player with promise in them, not a player who might exist. That
<code>可能性(かのうせい)</code> has no clause in front of it, which is how you tell
the two apart at a glance.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)の可能性(かのうせい)が<b>多(おお)い</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)の可能性(かのうせい)が<b>高(たか)い</b>。
  A possibility is measured by height in Japanese. <code>多(おお)い</code> would
  mean there were numerous separate possibilities.

- ❌ 地元(じもと)のチームが優勝(ゆうしょう)する<b>恐(おそ)れがある</b>。 → ✅ 地元(じもと)のチームが優勝(ゆうしょう)する<b>可能性(かのうせい)がある</b>。
  <code>恐(おそ)れ</code> only ever points at something unwelcome. Used of your own
  team winning, it is either a joke or a slip.

- ❌ 今晩(こんばん)飲(の)みに行(い)く<b>可能性(かのうせい)があります</b>。 → ✅ 今晩(こんばん)飲(の)みに行(い)く<b>かもしれません</b>。
  Grammatical, and oddly detached: you are forecasting your own evening from the
  outside, as though your movements were a weather system.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)が起(お)きる<b>可能性(かのうせい)がある</b>恐(おそ)れがあります。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)が起(お)きる<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
  Two hedges of the same kind stacked on one clause. Pick whichever fits the
  outcome and stop there.

## Practice

**1.** この薬(くすり)は眠気(ねむけ)が出(で)る____がありますので、運転(うんてん)は控(ひか)えてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

眠気(ねむけ)が出(で)る<b>可能性(かのうせい)</b>がありますので…

A pharmacist's warning is exactly the register, and the plain form
<code>出(で)る</code> goes straight in front of the noun.
<code>恐(おそ)れ</code> would also be possible here and would sound graver;
<code>可能性(かのうせい)</code> keeps it factual.

</details>

**2.** Choose: え、明日(あした)のライブ、中止(ちゅうし)になる（可能性(かのうせい)があるらしいよ・かもしれないらしいよ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`かもしれない` is a guess of the speaker's own and cannot be handed over to `らしい`,
which reports somebody else's words. <code>可能性(かのうせい)がある</code> is a
statement about the world, so it can be quoted and reported freely - one practical
advantage of a noun over an ending.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 再発(さいはつ)する<b>可能性(かのうせい)は低(ひく)いです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is reassurance with a number behind it. The speaker has weighed the odds and is
telling you they are in your favour, while stopping short of a promise.

(b) is the opposite move made with the same politeness. Nothing has been estimated;
the speaker is refusing to rule the thing out, which in Japanese as in English is
how a professional warns you without predicting. Hearing (b), a listener braces.

</details>

# ～ないとも限らない

**Meaning** · you never know; it isn't impossible that ～ - read *とも かぎらない*; the odds are low, but not low enough to ignore

限(かぎ)る is "to be limited to", so <code>とも限(かぎ)らない</code> means "things
are not limited to that". Put a **negative** inside it and the two cancel:
<code>降(ふ)らないとも限(かぎ)らない</code> is "it is not necessarily the case
that it won't rain" - in other words, it might. What the double negative adds is the
reason you are mentioning it at all. This is the grammar of taking an umbrella.

## Formation

The clause in front is **negative**, and `と` quotes it. Drop the ない and the meaning
flips over completely.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ない-form + とも限(かぎ)らない | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)らないとも限(かぎ)らない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + とも限(かぎ)らない | 起(お)きる → 起(お)きないとも限(かぎ)らない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ない</b> + とも限(かぎ)らない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないとも限(かぎ)らない |
| Verb (past) | ～なかった + とも限(かぎ)らない | 見(み)た → 見(み)なかったとも限(かぎ)らない |
| い-adjective | ～くない + とも限(かぎ)らない | 正(ただ)しい → 正(ただ)しくないとも限(かぎ)らない |
| な-adjective | ～ではない + とも限(かぎ)らない | 安全(あんぜん) → 安全(あんぜん)ではないとも限(かぎ)らない |
| Noun | ～ではない + とも限(かぎ)らない | 嘘(うそ) → 嘘(うそ)ではないとも限(かぎ)らない |
| Polite | ～とも限(かぎ)りません | 漏(も)れない → 漏(も)れないとも限(かぎ)りません |
| Common opener | 万(まん)が一(いち)／絶対(ぜったい)に | 万(まん)が一(いち)、～ないとも限(かぎ)らない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - count the negatives. <code>来(こ)ないとも限(かぎ)らない</code>
= *he might come*. <code>来(く)るとも限(かぎ)らない</code> = *he might not come*.
Both are grammatical, so nothing will warn you that you have said the opposite of what
you meant.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ら<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)こう。
It might rain, so let's take an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の気(き)が変(か)わら<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>。
He might yet change his mind.
</pre>

<pre>
地震(じしん)で停電(ていでん)が起(お)き<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>ので、水(みず)を備(そな)えてある。
There's always a chance an earthquake knocks the power out, so we keep water in the house.
</pre>

<pre>
万(まん)が一(いち)、個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)が漏(も)れ<b>ないとも限(かぎ)りません</b>。
On the remote chance of it, we cannot say that personal data will not leak.
</pre>

<pre>
古(ふる)い機械(きかい)なので、作業(さぎょう)中(ちゅう)に急(きゅう)に止(と)まら<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>。
It's an old machine, so it could well stop dead in the middle of a job.
</pre>

<pre>
近道(ちかみち)に見(み)えるが、こういう道(みち)が通行止(つうこうど)めになってい<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>。
It looks like a short cut, but a road like this may perfectly well be closed.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)は条件(じょうけん)が合(あ)わなかったが、来年(らいねん)なら話(はなし)がまとまら<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>。
The terms didn't suit us this time, but next year it isn't impossible that we come to an agreement.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)のところ被害(ひがい)は出(で)ていませんが、同(おな)じ手口(てぐち)が悪用(あくよう)され<b>ないとも限(かぎ)りません</b>。
No harm has come of it so far, but we cannot rule out the same method being turned to bad use.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The register is **careful adult Japanese**: safety notices, insurance and legal
writing, planning meetings, and the speech of somebody thinking ahead on your behalf.
It is not literary or archaic, and it is perfectly sayable - a parent, a site foreman
or a systems administrator will use it in conversation - but it is measured, and it
sits oddly in light chat.

What it contributes over `かもしれない` is not probability but **relevance**. A
speaker who says <code>停電(ていでん)が起(お)きないとも限(かぎ)らない</code>
is estimating the chance as small; the reason for raising it is that the consequences
would matter. That is why a precaution nearly always follows in the same breath - ので,
から, and then a torch, a backup, a second set of keys.

Two smaller points. The pattern is at home with unwelcome outcomes, but it is not
restricted to them: <code>まとまらないとも限(かぎ)らない</code> holds out a faint
hope in exactly the same shape, and in negotiation that faint hope is often the
politest thing that can be said. And the `も` in <code>とも</code> is doing work -
it is the "even" that turns <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code>, a flat denial of a
generalisation, into the more tentative "one cannot even say that".

## Contrast

### ないとも限(かぎ)らない vs かもしれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かもしれない` says the chance is **real**.
<code>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</code> says the chance is **small but worth acting
on**.

</div>

<pre>
午後(ごご)から天気(てんき)が崩(くず)れる<b>かもしれない</b>。
The weather may turn this afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の天気(てんき)は急(きゅう)に変(か)わら<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>。
You can never be sure the weather in the mountains won't turn on you.
</pre>

The first is a forecast; you might cancel your plans on it. The second is not really
a forecast at all - it is an argument for putting a jacket in the bag. Replacing the
second with `かもしれない` would be perfectly grammatical and would lose the reason
the sentence exists.

### ないとも限(かぎ)らない vs とは限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> **knocks down** a
generalisation somebody is relying on. <code>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</code>
**raises** a possibility nobody has considered.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が高(たか)ければうまい<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Expensive doesn't necessarily mean good.
</pre>

<pre>
この時間(じかん)なら、あの店(みせ)が閉(し)まってい<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>。電話(でんわ)してみよう。
At this hour that place may well have shut. Let's ring first.
</pre>

They face opposite ways. The first takes an assumption already in the room and puts
a hole in it; a `が` clause conceding the general rule usually follows. The second
introduces a contingency out of nowhere and then does something about it. Both hang
on 限(かぎ)る, and the difference is entirely in whether the negative sits inside
the quotation.

### ないとも限(かぎ)らない vs 恐(おそ)れがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>恐(おそ)れがある</code> states a risk **as a fact about
the world**. <code>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</code> states it as something **one
cannot honestly deny**.

</div>

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)により、土砂(どしゃ)災害(さいがい)の<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
Heavy rain brings a risk of landslides.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)の音(おと)で近所(きんじょ)から苦情(くじょう)が来(こ)<b>ないとも限(かぎ)りません</b>。
It's not out of the question that the neighbours complain about the noise from the work.
</pre>

An official warning uses <code>恐(おそ)れ</code>: the risk is being asserted, and
somebody's name is on the bulletin. The second sentence asserts nothing - it declines
to promise, which is a much smaller claim and correspondingly easier to make in a
meeting. If you can imagine the sentence on a public notice, use
<code>恐(おそ)れがある</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が来(く)る<b>とも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、席(せき)を空(あ)けておこう。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、席(せき)を空(あ)けておこう。
  The inner clause must be negative. Without the ない you have said "he won't
  necessarily come", which is no reason to save him a seat.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)<b>とも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、保険(ほけん)に入(はい)った。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)が起(お)こら<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、保険(ほけん)に入(はい)った。
  A bare noun has nothing to negate. Give the pattern a predicate -
  <code>起(お)こらない</code>, <code>ではない</code> - and the double negative can
  do its work.

- ❌ 失敗(しっぱい)し<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>ので、安心(あんしん)してください。 → ✅ 失敗(しっぱい)し<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>ので、念(ねん)のため確認(かくにん)をお願(ねが)いします。
  The pattern has just raised an alarm, so reassurance cannot follow it. Whatever
  comes after ので has to be a precaution.

- ❌ この店(みせ)のケーキ、おいしく<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らないよ</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)のケーキ、おいしいかもしれないよ。
  Far too much machinery for a cake. The pattern implies a contingency somebody ought
  to guard against, and nobody guards against dessert.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The forecast says no, the speaker is not willing to bet on it, and a precaution

attaches to the verb, giving <code>変(か)わらない</code> before `とも`.

The sentence is denying a generalisation that the listener is taking for granted,

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 上司(じょうし)が反対(はんたい)するかもしれません。
(b) 上司(じょうし)が反対(はんたい)し<b>ないとも限(かぎ)りません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a straight estimate. The speaker thinks there is a genuine chance of
opposition and is telling you so; you can reasonably ask how likely they think it is.

(b) puts the chance lower and the caution higher. It says: I am not predicting
opposition, but I am not prepared to guarantee its absence, and you should plan on
that basis. In a meeting (b) is the more diplomatic of the two, because it commits
the speaker to no opinion about the boss at all.

</details>

# ～ないものでもない

**Meaning** · it isn't entirely out of the question; I suppose it could be done - a grudging yes, offered without enthusiasm and usually with a condition attached

Two negatives around <code>もの</code>: "it is not a thing that cannot be". What
survives the cancellation is a very small positive. The speaker is not agreeing, not
refusing, and not committing - they are conceding that the door is not locked. In
practice the sentence almost always goes on to say what would have to change first.

## Formation

The clause in front is **negative** and modifies <code>もの</code>, so it is in
noun-modifying shape. The verb is very often a potential.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ない-form + ものでもない | 引(ひ)き受(う)ける → 引(ひ)き受(う)けないものでもない |
| Verb (potential) | ～ないものでもない | 行(い)ける → 行(い)けないものでもない |
| Verb (する) | しない + ものでもない | 検討(けんとう)する → 検討(けんとう)しないものでもない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)られない</b> + ものでもない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)られないものでもない |
| い-adjective | ～くない + ものでもない | ない → ないものでもない |
| Polite | ～ないものでもありません | できない → できないものでもありません |
| Lighter, spoken | ～ないでもない ／ ～なくもない | 分(わ)かる → 分(わ)からないでもない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern lives on **verbs**. Adjectives and nouns can be squeezed in,
but Japanese would rather use the lighter frames for them:
<code>おいしくなくもない</code>, <code>嫌(きら)いでもない</code>. If you are
reaching for an adjective, reach for those instead.

</div>

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the ない **inside** is not optional and not decorative.
<code>行(い)くものでもない</code> is a different pattern altogether, meaning "it
isn't the sort of thing one does".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
一口(ひとくち)なら食(た)べられ<b>ないものでもない</b>。
I could manage a mouthful, I suppose.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)げば間(ま)に合(あ)わ<b>ないものでもない</b>。
If we get a move on, it isn't impossible that we make it.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の言(い)い分(ぶん)も分(わ)から<b>ないものでもない</b>。
I can see his side of it, up to a point.
</pre>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)によっては、この仕事(しごと)を引(ひ)き受(う)け<b>ないものでもない</b>。
Depending on the terms, I wouldn't necessarily turn the job down.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)をかければ直(なお)せ<b>ないものでもない</b>が、新(あたら)しく買(か)ったほうが安(やす)い。
Given time it could probably be repaired, but buying a new one would come cheaper.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)さえ下(さ)げていただければ、検討(けんとう)でき<b>ないものでもありません</b>。
If you were able to come down on price, it is not something we could not look at.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)でやれ<b>ないものでもない</b>が、人手(ひとで)があるに越(こ)したことはない。
I could do it on my own, but more hands would hardly hurt.
</pre>

<pre>
先方(せんぽう)が条件(じょうけん)を見直(みなお)してくるなら、話(はな)し合(あ)いの席(せき)に着(つ)か<b>ないものでもない</b>。
If the other side revises its terms, we are not unwilling to sit down and talk.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Formal, adult, and deliberately unenthusiastic.** Its natural home is negotiation -
business meetings, letters between organisations, and the dialogue of a character in
a novel who is not going to say yes outright. It is not archaic and it is perfectly
sayable, but it is heavy, and among friends it sounds like a boardroom answer to a
simple question.

Three things it always carries:

| Feature | Consequence in the sentence |
|---|---|
| Reluctance | never used for something the speaker wants |
| A condition | ～なら, ～さえ～ば, 条件(じょうけん)によっては is usually present |
| A reservation to follow | a `が` clause very often comes next |

The reluctance is the part learners underestimate. Answering
<code>手伝(てつだ)ってくれない？</code> with
<code>手伝(てつだ)わないものでもないよ</code> is not a friendly yes. It says the
speaker will help if pressed and would rather not be - which is sometimes exactly
what you mean, and is a rude thing to say by accident.

Conversationally, the lighter cousins do the same job with a fraction of the weight.
<code>分(わ)からないでもない</code> and <code>分(わ)からなくもない</code> both
mean "I do sort of see it", and both are ordinary spoken Japanese. The `も` matters
in all of these: without it,
<code>できないものではない</code> is a genuine assertion that the thing *is*
possible, while <code>できないものでもない</code> merely declines to rule it out.

## Contrast

### ないものでもない vs ないことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both concede a small yes. `ないことはない` is **neutral and
conversational**; <code>ないものでもない</code> is **stiffer, more reluctant, and
expects a condition**.

</div>

<pre>
海(うみ)なら泳(およ)げ<b>ないことはない</b>よ。
I can swim well enough, if it's the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
海(うみ)なら泳(およ)げ<b>ないものでもない</b>が、正直(しょうじき)苦手(にがて)だ。
I could swim in the sea if I had to, though honestly I'd rather not.
</pre>

Said to a friend on a beach, the first is a normal answer and the second is a small
speech. The gap is register more than meaning, and it goes the other way in a
meeting: <code>ないことはありません</code> in a written reply to a client sounds
slightly light, where <code>ないものでもありません</code> sounds properly
non-committal.

### ないものでもない vs ないでもない・なくもない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the short forms are for **feelings and understanding**;
<code>ないものでもない</code> is for **actions and undertakings**.

</div>

<pre>
そう言(い)いたくなる気持(きも)ちは分(わ)から<b>なくもない</b>。
I can see how you'd want to say that.
</pre>

<pre>
ご事情(じじょう)が事情(じじょう)ですので、期限(きげん)を延(の)ばせ<b>ないものでもありません</b>。
Given the circumstances, extending the deadline is not out of the question.
</pre>

<code>分(わ)からなくもない</code> is a small sympathetic noise, and the heavier
pattern would be absurd in its place. Conversely, an extension of a deadline is
something an organisation grants or refuses, and there the weight is doing useful
work: it signals that the answer is not yet a yes.

### ないものでもない vs ないとも限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ないものでもない</code> concedes what **could be done**.
<code>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</code> warns of what **might happen**. Capacity
against contingency.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)なら都合(つごう)をつけられ<b>ないものでもない</b>。
I could probably manage to make time next week.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)は急(きゅう)な出張(しゅっちょう)が入(はい)ら<b>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</b>。
There's always a chance a trip comes up next week.
</pre>

Both are double negatives about next week, and they are doing opposite jobs. The
first is the speaker offering something, grudgingly. The second is the speaker
warning that the world may interfere. If the sentence is about what *you* will do,
it is this page's pattern; if it is about what may *befall* you, it is the other.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 手伝(てつだ)う<b>ものでもない</b>。 → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)わ<b>ないものでもない</b>。
  Without the inner ない you have written a different pattern - "helping isn't the
  sort of thing one does" - and told your colleague off instead of half-agreeing.

- ❌ ぜひ引(ひ)き受(う)け<b>ないものでもありません</b>。 → ✅ ぜひ引(ひ)き受(う)けさせてください。
  ぜひ is eagerness and the pattern is reluctance. The two cannot appear in the same
  sentence without sounding sarcastic.

- ❌ うん、行(い)か<b>ないものでもないよ</b>。 → ✅ うん、行(い)け<b>なくもないよ</b>。
  A friend asking whether you fancy coming along does not want a negotiating
  position. The light form carries the same hedge at the right weight.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しく<b>ないものでもない</b>から、心配(しんぱい)いらないよ。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しく<b>ない</b>から、心配(しんぱい)いらないよ。
  Cancel the negatives and the original says the problem **is** difficult after all,
  which is no basis for telling somebody not to worry. Read your own sentence back
  with the negatives resolved before you commit to it.

## Practice

**1.** 納期(のうき)を一週間(いっしゅうかん)延(の)ばしていただけるなら、お受(う)けし____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お受(う)けし<b>ないものでもありません</b>。

A condition is stated first and a grudging opening follows - the pattern's standard
shape in a business reply. The humble お受(う)けする takes ない in the ordinary way
before <code>もの</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: その気持(きも)ち、分(わ)から（なくもない・ないものでもない）。ぼくも同(おな)じ経験(けいけん)をしたから。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

分(わ)から<b>なくもない</b>。

Sympathy between friends wants the light form. <code>ないものでもない</code> would
turn an expression of fellow feeling into a concession made across a table, and the
casual ぼく in the next clause makes the mismatch obvious.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a business meeting: "If the schedule were reviewed, participation would not be out of the question for us."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

日程(にってい)を見直(みなお)していただけるのであれば、参加(さんか)でき<b>ないものでもありません</b>。

The condition comes first and the concession second, which is the order Japanese
prefers here. Note that "would not be out of the question" is the potential
<code>参加(さんか)できない</code> inside the frame, not
<code>参加(さんか)しない</code> - the speaker is talking about what is feasible,
not about what they intend.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)までにできるものでもないので、やってみます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"It isn't impossible by tomorrow, so I'll give it a go."

The inner ない has gone missing, and with it the meaning. As written, the sentence
says it is not the kind of thing one manages by tomorrow - and then volunteers to
manage it by tomorrow.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一人(ひとり)でもでき<b>ます</b>。
(b) 一人(ひとり)でもでき<b>ないものでもありません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) accepts the job. The speaker says the work is within their reach and is offering
to do it, and a listener will stop worrying about it.

(b) accepts nothing. It says the work is not beyond reach, while making clear that
the speaker is not volunteering and would like the difficulty noticed. In a meeting
this is often a request for help disguised as an answer, and it is heard as one.

</details>

# ～(よ)うか○○まいか

**Meaning** · whether to do it or not - a decision the speaker is standing in front of, unable to move either way

The same verb appears twice: once in the volitional, "shall I", and once with `まい`,
the negative volitional, "shall I not". Each half is a question, and the two questions
are asked at once. That is what makes this pattern different from every other
"whether or not" in Japanese - it is not weighing two outcomes, it is a mind stuck
between two courses of action, and the sentence nearly always ends with a verb of
being stuck.

## Formation

`まい` attaches to the dictionary form for う-verbs; る-verbs allow either the
dictionary form or the ます-stem. The two halves take `か` and `か`, never `が` or `と`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + か ＋ dictionary + まいか | 行(い)く → 行(い)こうか行(い)くまいか |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + か ＋ dictionary or stem + まいか | 辞(や)める → 辞(や)めようか辞(や)めまいか |
| Verb (する) | しようか ＋ しまいか／するまいか | 参加(さんか)する → 参加(さんか)しようか参加(さんか)するまいか |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)ようか ＋ 来(こ)まいか／来(く)るまいか | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようか来(こ)まいか |
| Second half shortened | full verb ＋ bare stem + まいか | 声(こえ)をかける → かけようかかけまいか |
| Usual continuation | ～迷(まよ)う／悩(なや)む／考(かんが)える | ～まいか、迷(まよ)っている |
| Quoted before the verb | ～まいか + と + 悩(なや)む | ～まいかと悩(なや)んだ |
| Everyday spoken version | ～(よ)うかどうしようか | 行(い)こうかどうしようか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - it must be the **same verb** on both sides, and the endings must
be `か` and `か`. ❌<code>行(い)こうか帰(かえ)るまいか</code> is not a fork, it is
two unrelated ideas; ❌<code>行(い)こうが行(い)くまいが迷(まよ)う</code> is a
different pattern, and it means the opposite - that the choice does not matter.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>行(い)こうか行(い)くまいか</b>、ずっと迷(まよ)っている。
I've been going back and forth over whether to go.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>買(か)おうか買(か)うまいか</b>、一時間(いちじかん)も考(かんが)えた。
I spent a whole hour deciding whether to buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>言(い)おうか言(い)うまいか</b>迷(まよ)ったが、結局(けっきょく)黙(だま)っていた。
I hesitated over whether to say anything, and in the end kept quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>辞(や)めようか辞(や)めまいか</b>と悩(なや)んだ末(すえ)、もう一年(いちねん)続(つづ)けることにした。
After agonising over whether to quit, I decided to give it another year.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>参加(さんか)しようか参加(さんか)するまいか</b>、締(し)め切(き)りの日(ひ)まで決(き)めかねていた。
I could not make up my mind whether to take part, right up to the closing date.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>声(こえ)をかけようかかけまいか</b>迷(まよ)っているうちに、彼女(かのじょ)は改札(かいさつ)を抜(ぬ)けて行(い)ってしまった。
While I was hesitating over whether to call out to her, she went through the ticket gates and was gone.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>知(し)らせようか知(し)らせまいか</b>と考(かんが)えているうちに、夜(よる)が明(あ)けた。
Turning over whether to tell them or not, I found the night had ended.
</pre>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)が不十分(ふじゅうぶん)なまま<b>公表(こうひょう)しようか公表(こうひょう)するまいか</b>、編集(へんしゅう)部(ぶ)は最後(さいご)まで判断(はんだん)に迷(まよ)った。
With the evidence still thin, the editorial desk wavered to the last over whether to publish.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Written, and inward.** `まい` is an old form, and it gives the pattern a
deliberate, slightly literary weight. Its home is narration - a novel reporting what
a character could not decide, a memoir, an essay looking back on a hard choice - and
formal accounts of how a decision was reached. It is not archaic: a careful speaker
will use it in a meeting or a speech, and it will not sound odd there.

In everyday conversation, though, almost nobody says it. The spoken form is
<code>行(い)こうかどうしようか</code>, and it means the same thing at a fraction
of the weight. Say <code>行(い)こうか行(い)くまいか</code> to a friend over
coffee and you sound as if you were narrating your own life.

Two structural habits worth noticing. First, the pattern is a **subordinate**
element: it almost never ends a sentence, because it is describing the state of a
decision rather than making one. What follows is
<code>迷(まよ)う</code>, <code>悩(なや)む</code>,
<code>考(かんが)える</code>, <code>決(き)めかねる</code>, or a
<code>末(すえ)に</code> clause saying how it was resolved. Second, `と` may be
inserted before that verb - <code>～まいかと悩(なや)んだ</code> - which quotes the
whole hesitation as the content of the thought, and is very common in writing.

The subject is nearly always the person doing the deciding, and usually the speaker.
You can use it of someone else if their state of mind is being narrated from inside,
which is exactly what fiction does; you cannot use it to ask what somebody else is
going to do.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うか○○まいか vs ～(よ)うが○○まいが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か…か` is a **fork you are standing at**. `が…が` (or `と…と`) is
a fork that **does not matter**, because both branches lead to the same place.

</div>

<pre>
<b>申(もう)し込(こ)もうか申(もう)し込(こ)むまいか</b>、まだ決(き)めていない。
I still haven't decided whether to apply.
</pre>

<pre>
君(きみ)が<b>申(もう)し込(こ)もうが申(もう)し込(こ)むまいが</b>、締(し)め切(き)りは変(か)わらない。
Whether you apply or not, the deadline is the same.
</pre>

One letter apart, and opposite in force. The first sentence is about an undecided
mind and needs a verb of hesitation after it. The second dismisses the choice as
irrelevant and needs a main clause that holds either way. A learner who reaches for
`が` here has accidentally said that their own dilemma does not matter.

### ～(よ)うか○○まいか vs ～かどうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かどうか` embeds a **question of fact** and works with any verb.
<code>(よ)うか○○まいか</code> embeds a **choice of action**, and only ever with a
verb the subject can choose.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が来(く)る<b>かどうか</b>、まだ分(わ)かりません。
We still don't know whether he's coming.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)が<b>行(い)こうか行(い)くまいか</b>、まだ決(き)まらない。
I still can't settle whether I myself should go.
</pre>

`かどうか` reports missing information about the world - somebody else's plans, a
result, a fact. This page's pattern reports an unmade decision inside the subject's
own head. That is why ❌<code>明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ろうか
降(ふ)るまいか</code> is impossible: rain does not deliberate.

### ～(よ)うか○○まいか vs ～(よ)うかどうしようか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different room. `どうしようか` is what people
**say**; <code>まいか</code> is what writers **write**.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)の飲(の)み会(かい)、<b>行(い)こうかどうしようか</b>迷(まよ)ってるんだよね。
I can't decide whether to go to the drinks thing tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
辞表(じひょう)を<b>出(だ)そうか出(だ)すまいか</b>、彼(かれ)は三日(みっか)考(かんが)え続(つづ)けた。
He spent three days turning over whether to hand in his resignation.
</pre>

The spoken version repeats no verb - <code>どうしようか</code> stands in for the
negative half - which is why it is so much lighter. It also has no register problem
in either direction: <code>どうしようか</code> is fine in a novel, whereas
<code>まいか</code> in a chat about after-work drinks is not fine at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>行(い)こうか行(い)こまいか</b> → ✅ <b>行(い)こうか行(い)くまいか</b>
  `まい` takes the **dictionary form** of an う-verb, not the volitional stem. The two
  halves therefore do not look alike, and that is correct.

- ❌ <code>きようかきまいか</code> → ✅ <b>来(こ)ようか来(こ)まいか</b>
  来(く)る is irregular. Both halves are built on the こ stem, the same one as
  来(こ)ない and 来(こ)よう; ❌きまい is not a form. The alternative second half
  is <code>来(く)るまいか</code>, on the dictionary form.

- ❌ <b>行(い)こうか帰(かえ)るまいか</b>迷(まよ)った。 → ✅ <b>行(い)こうか行(い)くまいか</b>迷(まよ)った。
  It is a yes-or-no fork on one action, so the verb must repeat. Two different verbs
  make two different questions and the frame collapses.

- ❌ <b>買(か)おうか買(か)うまいか</b>。 → ✅ <b>買(か)おうか買(か)うまいか</b>、今(いま)も迷(まよ)っている。
  The pattern describes a state of indecision and cannot be the whole sentence. It
  needs a verb of hesitating, or a <code>末(すえ)に</code> clause saying how the
  matter ended.

## Practice

**1.** この手紙(てがみ)を出(だ)そうか____、一晩(ひとばん)考(かんが)えた。

The second half repeats the same verb in the dictionary form with `まい`.
出(だ)す is an う-verb, so it is <code>出(だ)すまいか</code>, never
❌<code>出(だ)そまいか</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 君(きみ)が（来(こ)ようか来(こ)まいか・来(こ)ようと来(こ)まいと）、準備(じゅんび)は進(すす)めておく。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来(こ)よう<b>と</b>来(こ)まい<b>と</b>、準備(じゅんび)は進(すす)めておく。

The main clause holds whichever way the choice falls, so this is the concessive
`と…と` (or `が…が`), not the hesitating `か…か`. The `か` version would need a verb
of wavering after it, and would be about the listener's state of mind rather than
about the preparations.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in a narrative register: "She wavered for a long time over whether to tell him the truth."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Narration is where this pattern belongs, and a third-person subject is fine as long
as we are being shown the inside of her mind. <code>迷(まよ)っていた</code> is the
verb the frame is waiting for; without it the sentence would have no main clause.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 参加(さんか)しようか参加(さんか)しまいが、会費(かいひ)は払(はら)わなければならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 参加(さんか)<b>しようが参加(さんか)しまいが</b>、会費(かいひ)は払(はら)わなければならない。
"Whether you take part or not, the fee has to be paid."

The main clause holds either way, so both halves need `が`. As written, the sentence
mixes the hesitating `か` with the concessive `が` - and the two halves of this frame
must always match.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 留学(りゅうがく)し<b>ようかどうしようか</b>、迷(まよ)っています。
(b) 留学(りゅうがく)し<b>ようか留学(りゅうがく)するまいか</b>、迷(まよ)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is what you would say to a friend, a teacher or a counsellor. It is ordinary
spoken Japanese, light on its feet, and invites the listener to talk it over with
you.

(b) says the same thing in a written, weighted voice. In an essay or a personal
statement it reads as a serious decision being described; said aloud in a casual
conversation it sounds theatrical, as though the speaker were quoting a novel about
themselves.

</details>

# ～ではあるまいし

**Meaning** · it's not as if ～; it isn't as though you were ～ - dismisses a false premise, and then tells the listener off on the strength of it

`まい` is the classical negative conjecture: <code>あるまい</code> is "surely is
not". Add the reason-giving `し` and you get "since it surely is not the case that
～". Nothing that follows is neutral. The speaker has decided the listener is
behaving as though something absurd were true, and the second half of the sentence
says so - *you are not a child, so do it yourself*.

## Formation

A **noun** in front, bare, with no `だ`. A clause has to be made noun-like first, with
`の` or `わけ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ではあるまいし | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)ではあるまいし |
| Noun, contracted | Noun + じゃあるまいし | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)じゃあるまいし |
| な-adjective | stem + ではあるまいし | 苦手(にがて) → 苦手(にがて)ではあるまいし |
| Verb clause | plain form + の／わけ + ではあるまいし | 行(い)く → 行(い)くわけではあるまいし |
| い-adjective clause | plain form + の + ではあるまいし | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいのではあるまいし |
| Variant | ～でもあるまいし | 医者(いしゃ) → 医者(いしゃ)でもあるまいし |
| What must follow | a reproach, an order, or "so obviously ～" | ～し、自分(じぶん)でやりなさい |
| Modern equivalent | ～ではないのだから／～じゃないんだから | 子供(こども)じゃないんだから |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `し` at the end is the ordinary reason-giving `し`, so the
sentence is **unfinished on purpose**. Something has to come after it, and that
something is never a compliment.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、それくらい自分(じぶん)でやりなさい。
You're not a child - do that much for yourself.
</pre>

<pre>
神様(かみさま)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、そんなことが分(わ)かるものか。
I'm not God. How am I supposed to know a thing like that?
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、同(おな)じミスを三度(さんど)も繰(く)り返(かえ)すとは何事(なにごと)だ。
You're hardly new here - what do you mean by making the same mistake three times?
</pre>

<pre>
世界(せかい)が終(お)わるわけ<b>ではあるまいし</b>、そんなに落(お)ち込(こ)むな。
It isn't the end of the world. Don't take it so hard.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、そんな都合(つごう)のいい話(はなし)があるものか。
This isn't a film. Things don't fall into place like that.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で山(やま)に登(のぼ)るわけ<b>ではあるまいし</b>、そこまで装備(そうび)をそろえる必要(ひつよう)はない。
It's not as though you were climbing the thing alone; there's no call for all that kit.
</pre>

<pre>
遠(とお)い外国(がいこく)へ行(い)くの<b>ではあるまいし</b>、涙(なみだ)まで流(なが)すことはないだろう。
She isn't going to the far side of the world. There's no need for tears.
</pre>

<pre>
命(いのち)に関(かか)わること<b>ではあるまいし</b>、そう深刻(しんこく)に考(かんが)えることもない。
It isn't a matter of life and death, so there's no need to take it so gravely.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not deploy it.** <code>あるまい</code> is a classical form
that died out of ordinary Japanese long ago, and it survives here only as a fossil.
Where you will meet it is fiction and manga dialogue, film subtitles, scripted
scolding by an older character, and a handful of set complaints that everybody
knows - <code>子供(こども)じゃあるまいし</code>,
<code>神様(かみさま)じゃあるまいし</code>,
<code>馬鹿(ばか)じゃあるまいし</code>. Outside those fixed phrases, a learner who
produces it will sound as though they had learned Japanese from a period drama.

**Say <code>子供(こども)じゃないんだから</code> instead.** That is the living form,
it means exactly the same thing, and it is what a real Japanese speaker uses when
they are exasperated.

There is a second reason for caution, and it has nothing to do with the age of the
form. The pattern is **rude by construction**. It tells the listener that they have
been acting on a premise so silly it needs stating out loud, and then instructs them.
It is used downward and sideways - parent to child, senior to junior, one friend to
another - and never, in any register, upward. A junior employee who says
<code>部長(ぶちょう)、新人(しんじん)じゃあるまいし</code> has not made a
grammatical error; they have made a career one. Where the target is the speaker
themselves - <code>神様(かみさま)じゃあるまいし</code> - it is not rude at all,
just weary, which is why that particular phrase is the one you hear most.

## Contrast

### ではあるまいし vs ではないんだから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. <code>ではあるまいし</code> is the literary
fossil; <code>じゃないんだから</code> is what people actually say. Use the second and
recognise the first.

</div>

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>じゃないんだから</b>、いちいち言(い)われなくても分(わ)かるでしょう。
You're not a child - you shouldn't need telling every time.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、いちいち言(い)われなくても分(わ)かるだろう。
You're hardly a child; you should manage without being told each time.
</pre>

Both scold. The second sounds written, or performed - it belongs in a novel, or in
the mouth of a character whose speech is being marked as old-fashioned or theatrical.
Note that the modern version also lets you soften the ending
(<code>でしょう</code>, <code>よね</code>), which the fossil does not: `まい` pulls
the whole sentence into the plain, blunt register and keeps it there.

### ではあるまいし vs ではあるまいか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same `まい`, doing opposite jobs. With `し` it **gives a
reason** and scolds. With `か` it **asks a rhetorical question** and asserts, in the
careful voice of an essay.

</div>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、そこまで詳(くわ)しく説明(せつめい)する必要(ひつよう)はない。
We're not specialists - there's no need to go into that much detail.
</pre>

<pre>
この変化(へんか)こそが問題(もんだい)の根(ね)にあるの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Might this change not be the root of the problem?
</pre>

Learners meeting the two on the same day mix them up constantly, and the endings are
the only signal. `し` leans forward into a complaint; `か` leans back into a
proposition, and it is at home in serious writing in a way that
<code>あるまいし</code> never is.

### ではあるまいし vs ならともかく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both dismiss a hypothetical. <code>ではあるまいし</code> says the
premise is **plainly false**; `ならともかく` says it **would have made a difference
if true**.

</div>

<pre>
急(きゅう)な用事(ようじ)<b>ならともかく</b>、そんなことで会議(かいぎ)を抜(ぬ)けるのはよくない。
If it were something urgent that would be one thing, but walking out of a meeting for that is not on.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)な用事(ようじ)<b>ではあるまいし</b>、会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わるまで待(ま)てばいい。
It's hardly an emergency; you can wait until the meeting is over.
</pre>

`ならともかく` grants the hypothetical a hearing before setting it aside, which makes
it much the more usable of the two in ordinary life - it is neutral in register and
carries no reproach of its own. <code>ではあるまいし</code> refuses the hypothetical
outright and adds contempt for having entertained it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 子供(こども)だ<b>ではあるまいし</b> → ✅ 子供(こども)<b>ではあるまいし</b>
  The noun goes in bare. `だ` and <code>ではあるまい</code> are two attempts at the
  same copula, and they cannot both be there.

- ❌ 外国(がいこく)へ行(い)く<b>ではあるまいし</b> → ✅ 外国(がいこく)へ行(い)く<b>わけではあるまいし</b>
  The pattern needs a noun in front of it. A verb clause has to be nominalised first,
  with `わけ` or `の`.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)、新人(しんじん)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、もう少(すこ)し早(はや)くお願(ねが)いします。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、もう少(すこ)しお急(いそ)ぎいただけますでしょうか。
  The grammar is intact and the sentence is unusable. This pattern scolds downward
  only; aimed at a superior it is an insult, whatever politeness is bolted onto the
  end of it.

- ❌ 子供(こども)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、よくできたね。 → ✅ 子供(こども)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、これくらいできて当然(とうぜん)だ。
  What follows has to be a reproach or an "obviously, then" conclusion. Praise after
  it reads as sarcasm, because the first half has already denied the listener any
  credit.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence, and what does it tell you about the speaker? 探偵(たんてい)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、そんなことまで調(しら)べられるものか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Fiction, manga or a drama script - or a real speaker quoting that style for effect.
The sentence is a set-shape complaint aimed at the speaker's own situation ("I'm not
a detective"), so it is weary rather than rude, and `ものか` at the end confirms the
literary, performed register. In life the same line would come out as
<code>探偵(たんてい)じゃないんだから、そんなことまで調(しら)べられないよ</code>.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 学生(がくせい)<b>ではあるまいし</b>、時間(じかん)の管理(かんり)くらいできるだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

学生(がくせい)<b>じゃないんだから</b>、時間(じかん)の管理(かんり)くらいできるでしょう。

<code>ではあるまいし</code> maps directly onto
<code>じゃないんだから</code>: same premise, same reason-giving function, same
reproach. Softening <code>だろう</code> to <code>でしょう</code> is optional, but it
is available only in the modern version - which is one practical reason to prefer it.

</details>

**3.** Choose, and say why: 人手(ひとで)が足(た)りないの（ではあるまいか・ではあるまいし）、募集(ぼしゅう)を考(かんが)えたほうがよいのではないか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人手(ひとで)が足(た)りないの<b>ではあるまいか</b>、…

The sentence is a careful written suggestion, not a telling-off: it floats a
proposition and then recommends something. That is the `か` of the rhetorical
question. <code>ではあるまいし</code> would assert that the staffing is obviously
adequate, which contradicts the second half of the sentence.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山(やま)に登(のぼ)るではあるまいし、そんな靴(くつ)はいらないよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山(やま)に登(のぼ)る<b>わけではあるまいし</b>、そんな靴(くつ)はいらないよ。

The pattern attaches to a noun, and <code>登(のぼ)る</code> is a verb; `わけ` (or
`の`) has to be inserted to give <code>ではあるまいし</code> something to sit on. In
conversation you would drop the whole fossil and say
<code>山(やま)に登(のぼ)るわけじゃないんだから</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain who could say (b) to whom, and why (a) is the safer sentence:
(a) 新人(しんじん)<b>じゃないんですから</b>、そろそろ一人(ひとり)で大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですよね。
(b) 新人(しんじん)<b>じゃあるまいし</b>、そろそろ一人(ひとり)でやってくれ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) can only be said downward: a manager to a subordinate, a senior to a junior, or
a coach to a player - and even then it stings, because the first half denies the
listener the excuse of inexperience before the second half issues an order. The
classical <code>あるまい</code> adds a curt, stagey edge on top.

(a) carries the same content with the politeness and the tag question doing the
social work. It can be said sideways between colleagues, it leaves the listener room
to disagree, and no one will hear it as a rebuke unless they have earned one.

</details>

# 自発の(ら)れる - spontaneous, "it comes to be felt"

**Meaning** · a thought or feeling that arises of itself - read *じはつ*; the third use of れる／られる, alongside the passive and the potential

You already know this ending in two jobs: the passive
(<code>叱(しか)られる</code>, "I got told off") and the potential
(<code>食(た)べられる</code>, "I can eat it"). There is a third, and written
Japanese is full of it. In <code>昔(むかし)のことが思(おも)い出(だ)される</code>
nobody remembers anything - the memory **comes**. No one is doing it, no one is
having it done to them, and there is nothing the subject could have chosen. That is
自発(じはつ), the spontaneous.

## Formation

Morphologically it is the passive exactly: あ-stem + `れる` for う-verbs, `られる` for
る-verbs, `される` for する. Nothing is new. What is new is which verbs take it, and
what the sentence is built around.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | あ-stem + れる | 思(おも)う → 思(おも)われる |
| Verb (う-verb) | あ-stem + れる | 悔(く)やむ → 悔(く)やまれる |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + られる | 感(かん)じる → 感(かん)じられる |
| Verb (する-verb) | される | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)される |
| Compound verb | あ-stem + れる | 思(おも)い出(だ)す → 思(おも)い出(だ)される |
| What is felt | ～が + the verb | 昔(むかし)のこと<b>が</b>思(おも)い出(だ)される |
| Who feels it | ～には（not ～は） | 私(わたし)<b>には</b>そう思(おも)われる |
| Intensified | ～てならない／～てしかたがない | 案(あん)じられてならない |
| Polite | ～れます／～られます | 思(おも)われます |

The use is restricted to a small, closed group of verbs of thinking, feeling and
remembering. This is most of it:

| Verb | Spontaneous form | What the sentence says |
|---|---|---|
| 思(おも)う | 思(おも)われる | it comes to seem so |
| 感(かん)じる | 感(かん)じられる | it makes itself felt |
| 思(おも)い出(だ)す | 思(おも)い出(だ)される | it comes back to one |
| 案(あん)じる | 案(あん)じられる | one cannot help worrying about it |
| 悔(く)やむ | 悔(く)やまれる | one cannot help regretting it |
| 偲(しの)ぶ | 偲(しの)ばれる | it brings the past to mind |
| 心配(しんぱい)する | 心配(しんぱい)される | it is a cause for concern |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three tests separate the spontaneous from the passive.
**One:** is the verb about thinking or feeling? Only that handful can be spontaneous.
**Two:** could you name an agent with `に` or によって? If yes, it is a passive.
**Three:** what is the subject? In the spontaneous it is the **content** of the
thought, marked with `が`; a person in the subject slot forces the passive reading.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - れる／られる has a fourth job as well, the honorific:
<code>先生(せんせい)が話(はな)される</code>, "the professor speaks". That one
belongs with 敬語(けいご) and is told apart by the subject being a person you are respecting.
A few verbs sit on the border of the spontaneous group -
<code>待(ま)たれる</code> in
<code>早期(そうき)の実現(じつげん)が待(ま)たれる</code>,
<code>望(のぞ)まれる</code>, <code>惜(お)しまれる</code> - where grammarians
differ over whether an unnamed public is doing the awaiting. The practical point is
the same either way: no agent is named, and the thing itself is the subject.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
昔(むかし)のことが<b>思(おも)い出(だ)される</b>。
Memories of long ago come back to me.
</pre>

<pre>
秋(あき)の気配(けはい)が<b>感(かん)じられる</b>。
There is a feel of autumn in the air.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)には、それが唯一(ゆいいつ)の方法(ほうほう)だと<b>思(おも)われた</b>。
To me it seemed the only way there was.
</pre>

<pre>
この写真(しゃしん)からは、当時(とうじ)の暮(く)らしぶりが<b>偲(しの)ばれる</b>。
The photograph calls up how people lived in those days.
</pre>

<pre>
一言(ひとこと)謝(あやま)っておけばよかったと、今(いま)になって<b>悔(く)やまれる</b>。
Now, too late, I find myself wishing I had simply said sorry.
</pre>

<pre>
文章(ぶんしょう)の端々(はしばし)に、書(か)き手(て)の迷(まよ)いが<b>感(かん)じられる</b>。
Here and there in the prose one senses the writer's hesitation.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で旅(たび)に出(で)た息子(むすこ)のことが<b>案(あん)じられて</b>ならない。
I cannot stop worrying about my son, off travelling on his own.
</pre>

<pre>
筆者(ひっしゃ)の言(い)わんとするところは、結局(けっきょく)この一点(いってん)に尽(つ)きるように<b>思(おも)われる</b>。
What the author is driving at seems in the end to come down to this single point.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Written Japanese, and everywhere in it.** Essays, criticism, academic prose,
memoirs, editorials, museum and memorial texts. It is not old-fashioned and not a
special effect: <code>と思(おも)われる</code> and
<code>感(かん)じられる</code> are among the most ordinary things a Japanese writer
does. The heavier members of the set -
<code>偲(しの)ばれる</code>, <code>悔(く)やまれる</code>,
<code>案(あん)じられる</code> - are more literary, and cluster around memory,
regret and loss. They are the language of obituaries and of writing about the past.

Two things it buys the writer. The first is **hedging**.
<code>そのように思(おも)われます</code> in a meeting or an email is a softened "I
think": the opinion is presented as something that formed rather than something the
speaker is pushing. It is polite, and it is slightly evasive, and both of those are
often the point.

The second is **honesty about involuntariness**. A feeling reported with
<code>思(おも)う</code> is one you are having; the same feeling reported with
<code>思(おも)われる</code> is one that happened to you. That is why the pattern
pairs so naturally with <code>てならない</code> and
<code>てしかたがない</code>, and why <code>悔(く)やまれる</code> is so much sadder
than <code>後悔(こうかい)している</code> - the regret is not being managed, it is
simply there.

Because the experiencer has been removed from the subject slot, ❌
<code>私(わたし)はそう思(おも)われます</code> does not mean "I think so". If a
listener parses it at all, they will parse it as a passive - *I am thought of that
way*. Where you must name yourself, use `には`:
<code>私(わたし)にはそう思(おも)われます</code>. In speech, simply say
<code>そう思(おも)います</code>.

## Contrast

### 自発(じはつ)の(ら)れる vs 受身(うけみ)の(ら)れる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a passive has an agent you could name, even when it is left out.
A spontaneous has **no agent at all**, and adding one with `に` flips the sentence
into the passive on the spot.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は同僚(どうりょう)に冷(つめ)たい人(ひと)だと<b>思(おも)われている</b>。
His colleagues take him for a cold person.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の態度(たいど)には何(なに)か事情(じじょう)がありそうに<b>思(おも)われる</b>。
There seems to be something behind the way he is behaving.
</pre>

Same verb, same ending, different grammar underneath. In the first you can ask "by
whom?" and the sentence answers - 同僚(どうりょう)に. In the second the question
has no answer, because nobody is doing the thinking; that is what makes it
spontaneous. Notice also what the subject is: a **person** in the first, a **state of
affairs** in the second. If you put a person in the subject slot of a
<code>思(おも)われる</code> sentence, you have written a passive whether you meant
to or not.

### 自発(じはつ)の思(おも)われる vs 思(おも)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>思(おも)える</code> is built on the potential: *I am
able to see it that way*. <code>思(おも)われる</code> is spontaneous: *it comes to
look that way to me*. In speech, use the first; in writing, the second.

</div>

<pre>
どう考(かんが)えても、あれが偶然(ぐうぜん)だったとは<b>思(おも)えない</b>。
However I look at it, I can't believe that was a coincidence.
</pre>

<pre>
この結果(けっか)は偶然(ぐうぜん)によるものとは<b>思(おも)われない</b>。
This result does not appear to be attributable to chance.
</pre>

The negative is where the split shows most clearly.
<code>思(おも)えない</code> is everyday spoken Japanese and carries a note of the
speaker resisting a conclusion. <code>思(おも)われない</code> is stiff, written
and impersonal, and says only that the impression does not arise. Saying
<code>思(おも)われない</code> to a friend sounds like reading out a report;
writing <code>思(おも)えない</code> in a paper sounds like an opinion where an
argument was expected.

### 自発(じはつ)の(ら)れる vs ～気(き)がする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same involuntary impression, at opposite ends of the
register. <code>気(き)がする</code> keeps the speaker in the sentence; the
spontaneous -られる takes the speaker out.

</div>

<pre>
なんだか忘(わす)れ物(もの)をしたような<b>気(き)がする</b>。
I've got a feeling I've forgotten something.
</pre>

<pre>
この結論(けつろん)にはやや無理(むり)があるように<b>思(おも)われる</b>。
There does seem to be a certain strain in this conclusion.
</pre>

Both report something the speaker did not decide to feel. <code>気(き)がする</code>
is conversational and openly subjective - it invites the listener to take it as a
hunch and treat it lightly. The spontaneous form claims no more truth but sounds far
more considered, because the grammar has quietly deleted the person whose hunch it
is. That deletion is exactly why academic Japanese prefers it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昔(むかし)のこと<b>を</b>思(おも)い出(だ)される。 → ✅ 昔(むかし)のこと<b>が</b>思(おも)い出(だ)される。
  The thing remembered becomes the subject. There is no one left in the sentence to
  take an object, so `を` has nothing to attach to.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>は</b>そう思(おも)われます。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>には</b>そう思(おも)われます。（or そう思(おも)います）
  With `は` the sentence reads as a passive - "I am thought of that way". The person
  to whom the impression comes is marked `には`, never `は`.

- ❌ この本(ほん)は一気(いっき)に<b>読(よ)まれた</b>。（meaning "I found myself reading it straight through") → ✅ この本(ほん)は一気(いっき)に<b>読(よ)んでしまった</b>。
  読(よ)む is not one of the spontaneous verbs, so
  <code>読(よ)まれた</code> can only be a passive - the book was read by somebody.
  For an action that carried you along, use <code>てしまう</code>.

- ❌ 母(はは)<b>に</b>案(あん)じられる。（meaning "I worry about my mother") → ✅ 母(はは)のこと<b>が</b>案(あん)じられる。
  `に` names an agent, and naming an agent makes it a passive: as written, it is the
  mother doing the worrying. The one you are worried about takes `が`.

## Practice

**1.** この町(まち)を歩(ある)いていると、子供(こども)のころのこと____思(おも)い出(だ)される。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子供(こども)のころのこと<b>が</b>思(おも)い出(だ)される。

In a spontaneous sentence the content of the memory is the subject. Nobody is
performing the remembering, so there is no agent to take `は` and no object to take
`を`.

</details>

**2.** Choose, and say which use of られる each one is: (a) 彼(かれ)は近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)に変(か)わり者(もの)だと思(おも)われている。 (b) 彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)には無理(むり)があるように思(おも)われる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a **passive**: the agent is right there in the sentence,
近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)に, and the subject is a person being judged.

(b) is **spontaneous**: no agent can be added, and the subject is a state of
affairs - the strain in his explanation - rather than a person. Try inserting
<code>誰(だれ)かに</code> into (b) and it stops meaning what it meant.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of an essay: "Read in this light, the ending of the novel seems anything but a happy one."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そう読(よ)むと、この小説(しょうせつ)の結末(けつまつ)はとても幸福(こうふく)なものとは<b>思(おも)われない</b>。

"Seems" with no one seeming it is the spontaneous
<code>思(おも)われる</code>, and the negative
<code>思(おも)われない</code> is the standard shape for this move in criticism.
<code>思(おも)えない</code> would also be understood, but it would sound like the
critic's personal reaction rather than a reading of the text.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)の進(すす)め方(かた)について、私(わたし)は改善(かいぜん)の余地(よち)があると思(おも)われます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)の進(すす)め方(かた)については、改善(かいぜん)の余地(よち)があると<b>思(おも)われます</b>。
"There would seem to be room for improvement in how the meetings are run."

Delete 私(わたし)は. The whole point of the spontaneous form is that no one is
named as the thinker, and leaving yourself in produces a passive reading. If you want
your name on the opinion, write
<code>私(わたし)は…と思(おも)います</code> instead - and in a meeting that is
often the braver and better sentence.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の判断(はんだん)は正(ただ)しかったと思(おも)います。
(b) 彼(かれ)の判断(はんだん)は正(ただ)しかったように思(おも)われます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is your opinion, offered as yours. It can be agreed with or argued against, and
the person doing the arguing will be arguing with you.

(b) presents the same content as an impression that formed by itself. It is softer,
more written, and harder to push back on, because there is nobody in the sentence to
push back against. Used well it is careful; used to avoid taking a position it is
evasion, and Japanese readers notice the difference as readily as English ones do.

</details>

# ～とも～とも

**Meaning** · neither A nor B; won't say either way - offers the two labels a thing might take, and reports that neither of them will stick

Two opposites are named, each followed by the quoting `と` plus `も`, and then the
sentence declines: <code>言(い)えない</code>, <code>言(い)わなかった</code>,
<code>つかない</code>, <code>判断(はんだん)できない</code>. The pattern is not
about being unable to choose. It is about a thing that genuinely fits neither
description - an answer that was no answer, evidence that points nowhere, a face you
could not read.

## Formation

Both halves must be the **same part of speech**, and they must be genuine
alternatives - yes and no, good and bad, true and false. Nouns and な-adjectives go in
bare, without `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + とも | 行(い)く → 行(い)くとも行(い)かないとも |
| Verb (past) | plain past + とも | 見(み)た → 見(み)たとも見(み)なかったとも |
| い-adjective | plain form + とも | いい → いいとも悪(わる)いとも |
| な-adjective | stem + とも | 好(す)き → 好(す)きとも嫌(きら)いとも |
| Noun | bare + とも | 本当(ほんとう) → 本当(ほんとう)とも嘘(うそ)とも |
| Usual continuation | ～言(い)えない／言(い)わない／判断(はんだん)できない | ～とも～とも言(い)えない |
| Neither label fits | ～とも～ともつかない | 夢(ゆめ)とも現実(げんじつ)ともつかない |
| Reads either way | ～とも～ともとれる／読(よ)める | 賛成(さんせい)とも反対(はんたい)ともとれる |
| Set phrase | うんともすんとも言(い)わない | - |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - what follows is always **negative or inconclusive**. There is no
affirmative version: ❌<code>いいとも悪(わる)いとも言(い)った</code> is not a
sentence. The whole construction exists to say that no label was applied.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>つかない</code> here is the 付(つ)く of
<code>区別(くべつ)がつかない</code>, "cannot be told apart". So
<code>夢(ゆめ)とも現実(げんじつ)ともつかない</code> is "of which one could not
say whether it was dream or waking" - a slightly literary turn, and a very common one
in fiction.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>行(い)くとも行(い)かないとも</b>言(い)わなかった。
He said neither that he would go nor that he wouldn't.
</pre>

<pre>
その案(あん)については、<b>いいとも悪(わる)いとも</b>言(い)えない。
On that proposal I can't say it's good and I can't say it's bad.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)は<b>賛成(さんせい)とも反対(はんたい)とも</b>とれる顔(かお)をしていた。
My mother's expression could have been taken for approval or for objection.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>本当(ほんとう)とも嘘(うそ)とも</b>つかない話(はなし)が、社内(しゃない)に広(ひろ)まっている。
A story that may or may not be true is going round the office.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)呼(よ)んでも、弟(おとうと)は<b>うんともすんとも</b>言(い)わなかった。
However often I called him, my brother didn't make a sound.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)の検査(けんさ)だけでは、<b>良性(りょうせい)とも悪性(あくせい)とも</b>判断(はんだん)できない段階(だんかい)です。
On these tests alone we are not yet at a stage where we can say whether it is benign or malignant.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>夢(ゆめ)とも現実(げんじつ)とも</b>つかない時間(じかん)が、しばらく続(つづ)いた。
For a while there was a stretch of time that was neither dream nor waking.
</pre>

<pre>
先方(せんぽう)からの返事(へんじ)は、<b>受(う)けるとも断(ことわ)るとも</b>読(よ)める曖昧(あいまい)なものだった。
The reply from the other side was vague enough to read either as acceptance or as refusal.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Middling formality, and usable in both speech and writing, though the writing is
where it belongs most comfortably: reports, minutes, medical explanation, journalism
and fiction. Spoken, it turns up whenever somebody is describing a **non-answer** -
which is a thing Japanese conversation produces a great deal of, and which this
pattern describes with unusual precision.

Three flavours are worth keeping apart:

| Ending | What it says |
|---|---|
| ～とも～とも言(い)わなかった | the person declined to commit |
| ～とも～とも言(い)えない | the speaker cannot honestly apply either label |
| ～とも～ともつかない | the thing itself falls between the two |

The first is a report about somebody's behaviour, and it usually carries a note of
complaint. The second is the speaker being careful. The third describes the object,
not anybody's knowledge of it, and is the most literary of the three.

<code>うんともすんとも言(い)わない</code> deserves its own line. うん and すん are
not words - すん is there only to rhyme - and the phrase means "not a peep". It is
used of a person who is refusing to respond and, just as often, of a machine that
will not start: <code>パソコンがうんともすんとも言(い)わない</code>. It is
thoroughly colloquial, and it is the one part of this page you will hear every week.

## Contrast

### ～とも～とも vs どちらとも言(い)えない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>どちらとも言(い)えない</code> is the everyday phrase and
needs the two options to be **already on the table**. <code>とも～とも</code>
**names** them, and can therefore start from nothing.

</div>

<pre>
「面白(おもしろ)かった？」「うーん、どちらとも言(い)えないな。」
"Was it any good?" "Mm - I couldn't say either way."
</pre>

<pre>
この時点(じてん)では、成功(せいこう)とも失敗(しっぱい)とも言(い)えません。
At this point we cannot call it either a success or a failure.
</pre>

In conversation the short form wins nearly every time, because the alternatives are
obvious from the question. Spell them out and the sentence becomes an assessment
rather than a shrug - which is why the second one belongs in a briefing and the first
one does not.

### ～とも～とも vs ～か～か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か…か` frames an **open question** and expects it to be
answerable. <code>とも…とも</code> reports that **neither label has been applied**,
and often that it cannot be.

</div>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)が来(く)るか来(こ)ないか、直接(ちょくせつ)聞(き)いてみよう。
Let's just ask him directly whether he's coming or not.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)は来(く)るとも来(こ)ないとも言(い)っていない。
He hasn't said either that he's coming or that he isn't.
</pre>

The first sentence assumes there is a fact to be found. The second is about the
absence of a statement - and note that it says nothing at all about what he intends.
That is the pattern's speciality: separating what somebody has committed to from what
they may actually do.

### ～とも～とも vs ～のやら～のやら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とも…とも</code> is **analytic**: the labels do not fit.
<code>のやら…のやら</code> is **emotional**: the speaker has given up trying to work
it out.

</div>

<pre>
喜(よろこ)んでいるのやら怒(おこ)っているのやら、さっぱり分(わ)からない。
Pleased or furious - I honestly can't tell.
</pre>

<pre>
喜(よろこ)んでいるとも怒(おこ)っているとも言(い)えない表情(ひょうじょう)だった。
His expression was one you could call neither pleased nor angry.
</pre>

The first has a sigh in it and is at home in speech. The second is a description
somebody might write, and is cooler: it does not report the speaker's bewilderment,
it reports a fact about the face. Both are common; they are not interchangeable in
tone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は<b>行(い)くとも行(い)くまいとも</b>言(い)わなかった。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は<b>行(い)くとも行(い)かないとも</b>言(い)わなかった。
  The second half is the ordinary plain negative. `まい` belongs to the
  <code>(よ)うが～まいが</code> family and is not used inside this frame.

- ❌ その案(あん)については、いい<b>とも悪(わる)いとも</b>言(い)った。 → ✅ その案(あん)については、いい<b>とも悪(わる)いとも</b>言(い)わなかった。
  The frame only ever ends in a negative or an inconclusive verb. With an affirmative
  it says the speaker made both opposite claims at once.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は行(い)く<b>とも</b>言(い)わなかった。（meaning "he wouldn't say either way") → ✅ 彼(かれ)は行(い)く<b>とも行(い)かないとも</b>言(い)わなかった。
  With only one `とも` the sentence is grammatical but says something narrower - he
  never said he *would* go, which leaves open that he said he wouldn't. The pattern
  needs both halves to close off both options.

- ❌ りんご<b>ともみかんとも</b>買(か)った。 → ✅ りんご<b>も</b>みかん<b>も</b>買(か)った。
  This is not a listing pattern. The two items are competing descriptions of one
  thing, not two things that both happened.

## Practice

**1.** 課長(かちょう)は、今回(こんかい)の企画(きかく)について進(すす)める____中止(ちゅうし)する____言(い)わないまま席(せき)を立(た)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

進(すす)める<b>とも</b>中止(ちゅうし)する<b>とも</b>言(い)わないまま…

Two opposite courses of action, both left unsaid - the classic non-answer, and the
classic use of this frame. Both halves take the plain form plus `とも`, and the verb
of saying is negative.

</details>

**2.** Choose: あの人(ひと)が犯人(はんにん)（か違(ちが)うか・とも違(ちが)うとも）、まだ捜査(そうさ)中(ちゅう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの人(ひと)が犯人(はんにん)<b>か違(ちが)うか</b>、まだ捜査(そうさ)中(ちゅう)です。

The sentence frames an open question that the investigation is expected to settle,
which is `か…か`. The other version would need a verb of saying or judging after it -
<code>犯人(はんにん)とも違(ちが)うとも言(い)えない段階(だんかい)です</code> -
and would be about the absence of a verdict rather than about the inquiry.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It was a smile you could read as agreement or as pity."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

同意(どうい)<b>とも同情(どうじょう)とも</b>とれる笑(わら)みだった。

"You could read it as either" is the <code>とも～ともとれる</code> shape, and both
nouns go in bare, with no `だ`. <code>読(よ)める</code> would work equally well in
place of <code>とれる</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 結果(けっか)を見(み)ても、成功(せいこう)だとも失敗(しっぱい)だとも思(おも)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 結果(けっか)を見(み)ても、<b>成功(せいこう)とも失敗(しっぱい)とも</b>言(い)えない。
"Even looking at the results, you can't call it a success or a failure."

Two repairs. The frame closes with a negative or inconclusive verb, so
<code>思(おも)う</code> cannot stand there; and nouns go in bare, so the two `だ`
come out.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)は<b>承知(しょうち)したとも断(ことわ)ったとも</b>言(い)わなかった。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)の返事(へんじ)は<b>承知(しょうち)とも断(ことわ)りとも</b>つかないものだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about the director's behaviour. He had the chance to commit and did not take
it, and the sentence quietly complains about that - the listener is being told that
no answer was given.

(b) is about the reply itself. It is not a complaint about evasion; it says the words
were genuinely between the two, so that nobody could classify them. (a) suspects a
deliberate dodge; (b) may simply be describing an ambiguity.

</details>

# ～のやら～のやら

**Meaning** · whether A or B - who knows; goodness knows which - two possibilities are set side by side and the speaker gives up on telling them apart

`の` turns each clause into an embedded question and `やら` leaves it hanging. Line up
two of them and you have a sentence that lists the possibilities and then refuses to
choose - not because the speaker is being careful, but because they have run out of
patience or of evidence. There is a sigh built into it, and the sigh is most of the
meaning.

## Formation

The clause in front is in **noun-modifying** shape, because `の` is the nominaliser.
な-adjectives and nouns therefore take `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のやら | 来(く)る → 来(く)るのやら来(こ)ないのやら |
| Verb (ている) | ～ているのやら | 寝(ね)ている → 寝(ね)ているのやら起(お)きているのやら |
| い-adjective | plain form + のやら | 嬉(うれ)しい → 嬉(うれ)しいのやら悲(かな)しいのやら |
| な-adjective | ～なのやら | 本気(ほんき) → 本気(ほんき)なのやら冗談(じょうだん)なのやら |
| Noun | ～なのやら | 夢(ゆめ) → 夢(ゆめ)なのやら現実(げんじつ)なのやら |
| Usual continuation | ～分(わ)からない／見当(けんとう)もつかない | ～のやら、さっぱり分(わ)からない |
| Single, with an interrogative | いつ／どう／何(なに) + ～のやら | いつ終(お)わるのやら |
| Set variant | ～ことやら | どうなることやら |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `の` is not optional. Drop it and you have
<code>やら…やら</code>, the N2 listing pattern, which means the **opposite**:
<code>泣(な)くやら笑(わら)うやら</code> says both things happened, while
<code>泣(な)いているのやら笑(わら)っているのやら</code> says the speaker cannot
tell which is happening.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>本気(ほんき)なのやら冗談(じょうだん)なのやら</b>、さっぱり分(わ)からない。
Serious or joking - I honestly have no idea.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>嬉(うれ)しいのやら悲(かな)しいのやら</b>、自分(じぶん)でもよく分(わ)からなかった。
Glad or sad, I couldn't have said myself.
</pre>

<pre>
あの子(こ)は<b>勉強(べんきょう)しているのやら遊(あそ)んでいるのやら</b>、部屋(へや)から出(で)てこない。
Studying or playing about, who knows - the boy won't come out of his room.
</pre>

<pre>
この工事(こうじ)、<b>いつ終(お)わることやら</b>。
Heaven knows when these roadworks will ever be finished.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>喜(よろこ)んでいるのやら困(こま)っているのやら</b>、電話(でんわ)の声(こえ)だけでは見当(けんとう)もつかない。
Pleased or put out, I couldn't begin to tell from her voice on the phone.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)からは、<b>元気(げんき)なのやらそうでないのやら</b>、便(たよ)りひとつ来(こ)ない。
Not a word from my son - whether he's well or not, I've no way of knowing.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>褒(ほ)められているのやらけなされているのやら</b>、返事(へんじ)に困(こま)ってしまった。
Being praised or being got at, I couldn't tell, and I had no idea what to say back.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)の指示(しじ)は毎回(まいかい)変(か)わり、<b>進(すす)めていいのやら待(ま)つべきなのやら</b>、現場(げんば)は混乱(こんらん)している。
The boss changes his instructions every time, and the team is in a muddle over whether to press on or to wait.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Ordinary Japanese, spoken and written, with a distinctly **adult, slightly worn**
colouring. You hear it from parents about their children, from long-suffering
colleagues, and from narrators looking back; it is common in the speech of older
speakers and rare in the speech of teenagers. It is not formal, and it is not
literary in the sense of being obsolete - `やら` is simply an old particle that has
kept one job and does it well.

The feeling it adds is **resignation**, and it comes in two temperatures:

| Warm | Cold |
|---|---|
| affection under the exasperation - a parent, a friend | genuine irritation - a bad manager, a delay with no end |
| 元気(げんき)なのやらどうなのやら、便(たよ)りもない | いつになったら決(き)まるのやら |

Either way the speaker has stopped investigating. That is what separates it from the
neutral <code>のか…のか</code>, which is compatible with going and finding out. If a
sentence ends <code>のやら…のやら</code> and then somebody picks up the phone to check,
the sentence has been contradicted.

Two smaller shapes. A **single** <code>のやら</code> with an interrogative -
<code>いつ帰(かえ)ってくるのやら</code> - is a complete sigh in itself and needs no
second half; it very often ends the sentence with nothing after it. And
<code>どうなることやら</code> is a fixed phrase, "goodness knows how this will turn
out", used constantly and usually with a wry face.

Because of that sigh, the pattern is **wrong in an official document**. A report that
says <code>原因(げんいん)は漏電(ろうでん)なのやら放火(ほうか)なのやら</code>
has editorialised: it now sounds as though the investigators had thrown up their
hands. Written Japanese of that kind uses `か…か` instead.

## Contrast

### ～のやら～のやら vs ～やら～やら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `の`, two possibilities and **only one is true**, and the
speaker cannot tell which. Without `の`, two items and **both are true**, among
others.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの片付(かたづ)け<b>やら</b>手続(てつづ)き<b>やら</b>で、今週(こんしゅう)は休(やす)む暇(ひま)もなかった。
What with the packing and the paperwork for the move, I've not had a moment this week.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しが<b>決(き)まったのやら決(き)まっていないのやら</b>、本人(ほんにん)に聞(き)いても要領(ようりょう)を得(え)ない。
Whether the move is settled or not, you get no sense out of him even when you ask.
</pre>

One syllable apart and they are not related in meaning at all. The first is a
cluttered list - packing *and* paperwork *and* other things. The second is a fork
where exactly one branch is true and nobody can see which. When you meet `やら` in
the wild, look for the `の` first.

### ～のやら～のやら vs ～のか～のか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のか…のか</code> is **neutral** and can be answered.
<code>のやら…のやら</code> has **given up**, and cannot be followed by "so let's find
out".

</div>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>のかしないのか</b>、今日中(きょうじゅう)に決(き)めてください。
Please decide by the end of today whether you are taking part or not.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>のやらしないのやら</b>、あの人(ひと)はいつもはっきりしない。
Coming or not coming, that man never gives a straight answer.
</pre>

The first can head an instruction, a question or an investigation. Try putting an
instruction after the second and the sentence collapses: you cannot tell someone to
decide and simultaneously wash your hands of the matter. `のやら` closes the door that
`のか` leaves open.

### ～のやら～のやら vs ～とも～とも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とも…とも</code> reports that **no label was applied** -
cool, analytic, often about somebody's words. <code>のやら…のやら</code> reports that
the **speaker cannot tell**, with feeling.

</div>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)は事業(じぎょう)を続(つづ)ける<b>ともやめるとも</b>発表(はっぴょう)していない。
The company has announced neither that it will continue the business nor that it will stop.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)が事業(じぎょう)を続(つづ)ける<b>のやらやめるのやら</b>、社員(しゃいん)にも知(し)らされていない。
Whether the company will carry on or pull out, not even the staff have been told.
</pre>

The first is the sentence a journalist writes: it states precisely what has and has
not been said. The second is the sentence an employee says over a drink. The facts
are the same; the second one has a person in it who is fed up.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 泣(な)く<b>やら笑(わら)うやら</b>、どちらか分(わ)からない。 → ✅ 泣(な)いている<b>のやら笑(わら)っているのやら</b>、どちらか分(わ)からない。
  Without `の` this is the listing pattern, which asserts that both were going on.
  With <code>どちらか分(わ)からない</code> after it, the sentence contradicts
  itself.

- ❌ <b>本気(ほんき)のやら冗談(じょうだん)のやら</b> → ✅ <b>本気(ほんき)なのやら冗談(じょうだん)なのやら</b>
  `の` here is the nominaliser, so な-adjectives and nouns need their `な`. This is the
  same `な` as in <code>本気(ほんき)なので</code>.

- ❌ 来(く)る<b>のやら来(こ)ないのやら</b>、電話(でんわ)で確(たし)かめてください。 → ✅ 来(く)る<b>のか来(こ)ないのか</b>、電話(でんわ)で確(たし)かめてください。
  The pattern says the speaker has stopped trying to find out, so an instruction to
  find out cannot follow it. Neutral <code>のか…のか</code> is what an instruction
  needs.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)は整備(せいび)不良(ふりょう)<b>なのやら操作(そうさ)ミスなのやら</b>、現在(げんざい)調査(ちょうさ)中(ちゅう)です。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)が整備(せいび)不良(ふりょう)<b>か操作(そうさ)ミスか</b>、現在(げんざい)調査(ちょうさ)中(ちゅう)です。
  In an official statement the sigh is out of place - it would suggest the
  investigators had given up. Keep `やら` for the register it belongs to.

## Practice

**1.** 父(ちち)は新聞(しんぶん)を広(ひろ)げたまま、読(よ)んでいる____寝(ね)ている____、動(うご)かない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

読(よ)んでいる<b>のやら</b>寝(ね)ている<b>のやら</b>、動(うご)かない。

Two possibilities, exactly one of them true, and a speaker with no intention of
going over to check - complete with the affectionate exasperation the pattern
carries. Both clauses are in ている form and take `の` directly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 準備(じゅんび)（やら練習(れんしゅう)やら・なのやら練習(れんしゅう)なのやら）で、先月(せんげつ)は週末(しゅうまつ)がつぶれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

準備(じゅんび)<b>やら練習(れんしゅう)やら</b>で、先月(せんげつ)は週末(しゅうまつ)がつぶれた。

Both the preparation and the practice really happened, and the sentence is
complaining about the pile of them. That is the listing `やら`, with no `の`. The
other version would claim the speaker could not tell which of the two they had been
doing, which is nonsense about one's own weekends.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Goodness knows how much longer this is going to take."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あと<b>どれくらいかかることやら</b>。

An interrogative plus a single <code>ことやら</code> is the standard shape for this
kind of sigh, and it needs nothing after it - the sentence simply stops.
<code>どれくらいかかるのやら</code> would do the same job.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しい担当者(たんとうしゃ)が経験者(けいけんしゃ)のやら未経験(みけいけん)のやら、人事(じんじ)に確認(かくにん)しておきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しい担当者(たんとうしゃ)が経験者(けいけんしゃ)<b>なのか未経験(みけいけん)なのか</b>、人事(じんじ)に確認(かくにん)しておきます。
"I'll check with HR whether the new person is experienced or not."

Two problems. Nouns need `な` before `のやら`; and more importantly, the speaker is
about to go and find out, which rules `やら` out altogether. The neutral
<code>のか…のか</code> is the form that can be followed by an inquiry.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)が怒(おこ)っている<b>のか</b>笑(わら)っている<b>のか</b>、分(わ)からなかった。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)が怒(おこ)っている<b>のやら</b>笑(わら)っている<b>のやら</b>、分(わ)からなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement that the speaker could not tell. It is neutral, it could
appear in a report, and it leaves open that the speaker went on trying.

(b) adds the speaker's own weariness. It says the situation was one of those in which
you stop trying to read the other person, and it carries a faint smile or a faint
complaint depending on context. In writing, (b) puts a voice in the sentence that (a)
does not have.

</details>

# ～とみえる・～とみえて

**Meaning** · it seems, judging from what one can see - an inference drawn on the spot from something in front of the speaker

Written <code>と見(み)える</code> or in kana. The verb is the ordinary
<code>見(み)える</code>, "to appear", and that is exactly what the pattern does: it
reports how a scene looks to the person standing in it. What makes it distinctive is
that the evidence is nearly always right there in the same sentence -
<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったとみえて、道(みち)が濡(ぬ)れている</code>. The
guess comes first, the thing that prompted it comes second.

## Formation

The clause in front is quoted, so it takes the plain form and `と`. Nouns and
な-adjectives may keep `だ` or drop it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + とみえる | 降(ふ)った → 降(ふ)ったとみえる |
| Verb (ている) | ～ているとみえる | 疲(つか)れている → 疲(つか)れているとみえる |
| い-adjective | plain form + とみえる | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いとみえる |
| な-adjective | stem or ～だ + とみえる | 留守(るす) → 留守(るす)（だ）とみえる |
| Noun | bare or ～だ + とみえる | 初心者(しょしんしゃ) → 初心者(しょしんしゃ)（だ）とみえる |
| Linking to the evidence | ～とみえて + observed fact | 留守(るす)とみえて、明(あ)かりがない |
| Ending the sentence | ～とみえる／とみえます | 仲(なか)がいいとみえる |
| Past | ～とみえた | 驚(おどろ)いたとみえた |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in a <code>とみえて</code> sentence the halves are the other way
round from `ので`. The **guess** goes in front of <code>とみえて</code>; the **fact
you can see** goes in the main clause. Read it as "…, judging by the fact that …".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの家(いえ)は留守(るす)<b>とみえて</b>、窓(まど)に明(あ)かりがない。
Nobody's home by the look of it - there's no light in the windows.
</pre>

<pre>
あの二人(ふたり)は仲(なか)がいい<b>とみえる</b>。
Those two appear to get on rather well.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>とみえて</b>、道(みち)が濡(ぬ)れている。
It must have rained around here - the road is wet.
</pre>

<pre>
よほど疲(つか)れていた<b>とみえて</b>、彼(かれ)は席(せき)に着(つ)くなり眠(ねむ)ってしまった。
He must have been shattered; he was asleep the moment he sat down.
</pre>

<pre>
よほど気(き)に入(い)った<b>とみえて</b>、娘(むすめ)はその絵本(えほん)を何度(なんど)も開(ひら)いていた。
She'd taken to it, evidently - my daughter kept opening that picture book again and again.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)いで出(で)て行(い)った<b>とみえて</b>、机(つくえ)の上(うえ)は開(ひら)いたままの資料(しりょう)で散(ち)らかっていた。
He had left in a hurry by the look of it: the desk was strewn with papers left open.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めての土地(とち)だ<b>とみえて</b>、男(おとこ)は地図(ちず)を片手(かたて)に何度(なんど)も立(た)ち止(と)まっていた。
A stranger to the place, apparently - the man kept stopping with a map in his hand.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の冬(ふゆ)はよほど寒(さむ)かった<b>とみえて</b>、庭(にわ)の木(き)が何本(なんぼん)も枯(か)れてしまった。
Last winter must have been a hard one - several of the trees in the garden died.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the voice of an **onlooker**, and it belongs to narrative. Novels and essays
are full of it, because it is how a writer reports what a scene suggests without
claiming to know. It is also perfectly sayable, with a slightly considered,
observational colour - the sort of thing said by someone looking out of a window
rather than someone chatting. Younger speakers reach for `みたい` or `らしい` instead;
`とみえる` is not old-fashioned exactly, but it does sound composed.

Two properties follow from the fact that it is an observer's form.

**It needs first-hand evidence.** The pattern rests on something the speaker can see,
hear or otherwise perceive right now, and that evidence normally appears in the
sentence. An ending that reports hearsay - <code>そうだ</code>,
<code>と聞(き)いた</code> - pulls in the opposite direction and does not sit
comfortably with it.

**It cannot be turned inward.** You do not have to infer your own tiredness, so
❌<code>私(わたし)は疲(つか)れているとみえる</code> is not how anyone reports
their own state. Where it does appear with a first-person subject, it means "I
apparently look ～ to other people", which is a different sentence.

<code>とみえて</code> is much the commoner of the two shapes, because the pattern
wants its evidence attached. The bare <code>とみえる</code> at the end of a sentence
usually has the evidence in the sentence just before it -
<code>話(はな)し声(ごえ)がする。まだ誰(だれ)かいるとみえる</code> - and often
reads as a small aside.

Do not confuse it with its near-homograph <code>とみられる</code>, which is built on
the same verb and lives in an entirely different world. That one is the impersonal
estimate of a news bulletin; this one is a person looking at something.

## Contrast

### とみえる vs ようだ・らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` and `らしい` accept **any** grounds, including a feeling
or something you were told. <code>とみえる</code> wants grounds you can **point at**,
and usually points at them in the same sentence.

</div>

<pre>
外(そと)は寒(さむ)い<b>ようだ</b>。みんなコートを着(き)ている。
It looks cold out. Everyone's in a coat.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)は冷(ひ)え込(こ)んでいる<b>とみえて</b>、窓(まど)が白(しろ)く曇(くも)っている。
It must have turned cold outside; the windows have misted over.
</pre>

The content is nearly the same and the packaging is not. `ようだ` states the
conclusion and may or may not go on to justify it. <code>とみえて</code> builds the
justification into the grammar, which is why it reads as more careful and more
literary. In casual speech `みたい` would replace both, and nothing would be lost
except the composure.

### とみえて vs ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - opposite directions. With `ので` the first clause is the **known
cause**. With <code>とみえて</code> the first clause is the **guessed** cause, and the
second clause is what you actually observed.

</div>

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>ので</b>、朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べずに家(いえ)を出(で)た。
I overslept, so I left the house without breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>とみえて</b>、彼(かれ)は髪(かみ)もとかさずに飛(と)び込(こ)んできた。
He'd overslept by the look of him - he came flying in without even having combed his hair.
</pre>

In the first sentence the speaker knows about the oversleeping because it happened to
them. In the second nobody has said anything about oversleeping; it is an inference,
and the hair is the evidence for it. Learners often write the two clauses the wrong
way round, producing "the road is wet, it seems, so it rained" - which reverses the
reasoning.

### とみえる vs とみられる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one verb, two worlds. <code>みえる</code> is **a person
looking**; <code>みられる</code> is **an unnamed authority estimating**.

</div>

<pre>
隣(となり)の部屋(へや)から話(はな)し声(ごえ)がする。まだ誰(だれ)かいる<b>とみえる</b>。
There are voices from the next room. Somebody must still be in there.
</pre>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)の全容(ぜんよう)が判明(はんめい)するには、なお時間(じかん)がかかる<b>とみられる</b>。
It is expected to be some time yet before the full extent of the damage is known.
</pre>

The first has a person in it - someone standing in a corridor, listening. The second
has nobody in it at all, and could not be improved by adding anyone. Swapping them is
an instant register failure in both directions: a news bulletin cannot peer through a
wall, and a person listening at a door is not issuing an estimate.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 道(みち)が濡(ぬ)れている<b>とみえて</b>、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったようだ。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>とみえて</b>、道(みち)が濡(ぬ)れている。
  The clauses are the wrong way round. The **guess** goes in front of
  <code>とみえて</code> and the **observed fact** comes after it.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は忙(いそが)しかった<b>とみえて</b>、私(わたし)はすっかり疲(つか)れた。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は忙(いそが)しかった<b>ので</b>、すっかり疲(つか)れた。
  Your own day is not something you have to deduce. The pattern needs an observer
  looking at somebody or something else.

- ❌ 人気(にんき)がある<b>とみえて</b>、あの店(みせ)は毎日(まいにち)行列(ぎょうれつ)だそうだ。 → ✅ 人気(にんき)がある<b>らしく</b>、あの店(みせ)は毎日(まいにち)行列(ぎょうれつ)だそうだ。
  <code>とみえる</code> rests on what the speaker has seen for themselves, so an
  ending that reports hearsay pulls against it. If the queue is something you were
  told about, use `らしい`.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)は漏電(ろうでん)だ<b>とみえる</b>。（in a news report） → ✅ 事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)は漏電(ろうでん)<b>とみられる</b>。
  Two letters, and the difference between a bystander's impression and an official
  estimate. A bulletin needs the passive form.

## Practice

**1.** 昨夜(さくや)は風(かぜ)が強(つよ)かった____、庭(にわ)に木(き)の葉(は)がたくさん落(お)ちている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

強(つよ)かった<b>とみえて</b>、庭(にわ)に木(き)の葉(は)がたくさん落(お)ちている。

The wind is the inference and the leaves are the evidence, so the inference takes
<code>とみえて</code> and the observation goes in the main clause. The speaker did not
hear the wind; they are looking at the garden this morning.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 捜査(そうさ)関係者(かんけいしゃ)によると、現場(げんば)から逃(に)げた人物(じんぶつ)は複数(ふくすう)いた（とみえる・とみられる）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

複数(ふくすう)いた<b>とみられる</b>。

<code>捜査(そうさ)関係者(かんけいしゃ)によると</code> announces a news report, and
a report's estimates are impersonal. <code>とみえる</code> would put the journalist
personally at the scene, forming an impression - which is not how bulletins are
written.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in a narrative register: "The cat had been out in the rain, evidently - it left wet prints all across the floor."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

雨(あめ)の中(なか)を歩(ある)いてきた<b>とみえて</b>、猫(ねこ)は床(ゆか)一面(いちめん)に濡(ぬ)れた足跡(あしあと)をつけていった。

"Evidently" attaches to the inference, which therefore goes in the
<code>とみえて</code> clause; the prints are what the narrator can see and belong in
the main clause. Note that the two clauses in English arrive in the same order.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部屋(へや)が片付(かたづ)いていない<b>とみえて</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は忙(いそが)しかったのだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 忙(いそが)しかった<b>とみえて</b>、彼女(かのじょ)の部屋(へや)は片付(かたづ)いていなかった。
"She must have been busy - her room hadn't been tidied."

The halves are reversed. The untidy room is the visible fact, so it belongs in the
main clause; the busyness is the guess and belongs in front of
<code>とみえて</code>. Note also that once <code>とみえて</code> is doing the guessing,
the trailing <code>のだろう</code> is redundant.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は何(なに)か隠(かく)している<b>ようだ</b>。
(b) 何(なに)か隠(かく)している<b>とみえて</b>、彼(かれ)は目(め)を合(あ)わせようとしなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states a conclusion and stops. The grounds may be a look on his face, something
a third person said, or simply the speaker's instinct - nothing in the sentence says
which, and the listener may reasonably ask.

(b) shows its working. The refusal of eye contact is offered as the reason for the
suspicion, and the sentence reads as a piece of observation rather than a verdict.
That is why (b) belongs in narration: it lets the reader watch the inference being
made.

</details>
