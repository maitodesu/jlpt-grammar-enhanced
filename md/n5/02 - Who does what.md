# N5 - Who does what


---

# が - subject marker

**Meaning** · marks the subject of a clause, and singles out *which one* it is

`が` names the thing that does the verb, or the thing an adjective is true of. Its
second job is the one learners underestimate: `が` **points**. It presents its noun
as the piece of news in the sentence - the answer to a "which one?" that may never
have been asked out loud.

## Formation

`が` is a particle. It attaches directly to the noun, with no change to the noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + が | 犬(いぬ) → 犬(いぬ)が好(す)きです |
| Question word | Question word + が (never は) | だれ → だれが来(き)ますか |
| Nominalised clause | Clause + の / こと + が | 走(はし)る → 走(はし)るのが速(はや)い |
| Subject of a noun-modifying clause | Noun + が + verb + Noun | 私(わたし)が作(つく)った → 私(わたし)が作(つく)ったケーキ |
| Object of 好(す)き・上手(じょうず)・ある・わかる・ほしい | Noun + が + predicate | 日本語(にほんご) → 日本語(にほんご)がわかります |
| Object of 見(み)える・聞(き)こえる・できる | Noun + が + predicate | 音(おと) → 音(おと)が聞(き)こえる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `が` never combines with `は`, `も` or `を`. When a subject becomes the
topic, `が` is *replaced*: 犬(いぬ)が → 犬(いぬ)は, 犬(いぬ)も. There is no
`がは` or `がも`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)<b>が</b>降(ふ)っています。
It's raining.
</pre>

<pre>
だれ<b>が</b>来(き)ますか。
Who's coming?
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は犬(いぬ)<b>が</b>好(す)きです。
I like dogs.
</pre>

<pre>
あ、バス<b>が</b>来(き)た。
Oh - here comes the bus.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は窓(まど)<b>が</b>大(おお)きくて、明(あか)るいですね。
This room has big windows - it's nice and bright.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)<b>が</b>ないから、また今度(こんど)話(はな)しましょう。
I don't have time, so let's talk another time.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)買(か)ったパソコン<b>が</b>急(きゅう)に動(うご)かなくなって、困(こま)っています。
The laptop I bought last year suddenly stopped working, and I'm stuck.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、あそこに立(た)っている人(ひと)<b>が</b>田中(たなか)さんですか。
Excuse me - is the person standing over there Tanaka-san?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`が` is completely register-neutral. It is identical in plain and polite speech,
in men's and women's speech, and in writing. Nothing about choosing `が` makes you
sound formal or casual.

What *does* vary is whether you say it at all. In relaxed conversation `が` is
routinely dropped when the sentence is short and the subject is obvious:

- 雨(あめ)、降(ふ)ってるよ。 - "It's raining."
- 時間(じかん)ある? - "Got a minute?"

Dropping `が` is casual, not wrong. Keep it in writing, in ます-form speech, and
any time the sentence is long enough that the listener needs the signpost.

Two things to keep separate. This `が` is a **particle** that follows a noun. There
is a completely different `が` - a **conjunction** meaning "but" - that follows a
whole clause: 高(たか)いですが、買(か)います。 Same syllable, unrelated word. If
what precedes `が` is a finished sentence, it is the "but" one.

Finally, `が` marks the subject **of its own clause**, which is why it turns up in
places English speakers do not expect: inside relative clauses, inside <code>と思(おも)う</code>
clauses, inside conditionals. 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったら、中止(ちゅうし)です。

## Contrast

### が vs は

This is the hardest thing at N5, and the reason is that `は` is not a subject
marker at all. `は` marks a **topic** - "about this thing, here's what I have to
say." `が` marks a **subject**. The two often sit in the same slot in the sentence,
which is why they look like alternatives, but they are answering different needs.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the new information goes **after** `は`, but **before** `が`.

</div>

That one line does most of the work. Look at where the news is:

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>は</b>京都(きょうと)へ行(い)きました。
Tanaka went to Kyoto.
</pre>

We already know who Tanaka is; the news is *Kyoto*, and it comes after `は`. This
answers "What did Tanaka do?"

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>が</b>京都(きょうと)へ行(い)きました。
It was Tanaka who went to Kyoto.
</pre>

Here we already know somebody went to Kyoto; the news is *Tanaka*, and it comes
before `が`. This answers "Who went to Kyoto?"

The same logic explains why **question words always take `が`, never `は`**. A
question word *is* the unknown, so it has to sit on the `が` side:

<pre>
だれ<b>が</b>この窓(まど)を割(わ)りましたか。
Who broke this window?
</pre>

And the answer keeps `が`, because the answer is still the news:

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>が</b>割(わ)りました。
My little brother broke it.
</pre>

A second, related use: `が` is how you report something you have just noticed - 
an event presented whole, with no topic yet established. This is why stories begin
with `が` and continue with `は`:

<pre>
むかしむかし、ある村(むら)におじいさん<b>が</b>住(す)んでいました。
Long ago, in a certain village, there lived an old man.
</pre>

<pre>
おじいさん<b>は</b>毎日(まいにち)山(やま)へ木(き)を切(き)りに行(い)きました。
Every day the old man went to the mountain to cut wood.
</pre>

Sentence one introduces him - brand new, so `が`. Sentence two talks *about* him - 
now known, so `は`.

Because they do different jobs, both can appear in one sentence, and the classic
example is worth memorising whole:

<pre>
象(ぞう)<b>は</b>鼻(はな)<b>が</b>長(なが)い。
Elephants have long trunks. (As for elephants - the nose is long.)
</pre>

<code>象(ぞう)</code> is the topic; <code>鼻(はな)</code> is the subject of <code>長(なが)い</code>. Neither particle could do the
other's job here.

One last thing `は` can do that `が` cannot: **contrast**. `は` implicitly sets its
noun against something else.

<pre>
コーヒー<b>は</b>飲(の)みますが、お酒(さけ)<b>は</b>飲(の)みません。
I drink coffee, but I don't drink alcohol.
</pre>

Swap those to `が` and the contrast evaporates.

### が vs は inside a noun-modifying clause

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - inside a clause that modifies a noun, the subject takes `が`.
`は` is not available there.

</div>

`は` scopes over a whole sentence, so it cannot be trapped inside a clause that is
only decorating a noun. This produces one of the most common N5 errors.

<pre>
これは私(わたし)<b>が</b>撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)です。
This is a photo I took.
</pre>

<code>私(わたし)が撮(と)った</code> modifies <code>写真(しゃしん)</code>. Saying
❌<code>私(わたし)は撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)</code> breaks the sentence, because `は`
would try to make <code>私(わたし)</code> the topic of the *outer* sentence.

The same holds inside conditionals and quoted thoughts:

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>が</b>来(く)ると思(おも)います。
I think Tanaka is coming.
</pre>

<div class="note">

**Note** - in a noun-modifying clause, `が` can be swapped for `の` with no change
in meaning: 私(わたし)**の**撮(と)った写真(しゃしん). This is common in
writing and sounds slightly compact. It only works inside such clauses - never in
a main sentence.

</div>

### が vs を

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a small set of predicates take their "object" with `が`, not
`を`, because grammatically that noun *is* the subject.

</div>

<code>好(す)き</code>, <code>嫌(きら)い</code>, <code>上手(じょうず)</code>, <code>下手(へた)</code>, `ほしい`, `わかる`,
`できる`, `ある`, <code>見(み)える</code>, <code>聞(き)こえる</code> all belong to this set. In English
these look transitive ("I understand Japanese"), so `を` is a strong pull - and it
is wrong.

<pre>
私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)<b>が</b>わかります。
I understand Japanese.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)から海(うみ)<b>が</b>見(み)えます。
You can see the sea from the top of the mountain.
</pre>

Compare <code>見(み)る</code>, an ordinary transitive verb, which does take `を`:
海(うみ)**を**見(み)ます - "I look at the sea." <code>見(み)える</code> is "is visible", so
the sea is what *is* - hence `が`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ だれ**は**来(き)ましたか。 → ✅ だれ**が**来(き)ましたか。
  A question word is the unknown, and unknowns cannot be topics. Question words
  take `が`.

- ❌ これは私(わたし)**は**撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)です。 → ✅ これは私(わたし)**が**撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)です。
  Inside a clause modifying a noun, the subject takes `が`. `は` cannot live there.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)**を**わかります。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)**が**わかります。
  English transitivity is not a guide. `わかる`, `できる`, <code>好(す)き</code>, `ほしい`,
  <code>見(み)える</code> all take `が`.

- ❌ 犬(いぬ)**がは**好(す)きです。 → ✅ 犬(いぬ)**は**好(す)きです。
  `が` is replaced by `は` and `も`, never stacked with them.

- ❌ ぼく**は**やります!（volunteering, "I'll do it!"）→ ✅ ぼく**が**やります!
  Volunteering means identifying *who* - you are the news. That is a `が` job.

## Practice

**1.** だれ____このケーキを作(つく)りましたか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

だれ**が**このケーキを作(つく)りましたか。

Question words are always the unknown information in the sentence, and the unknown
sits on the `が` side. `だれは` is never possible.

</details>

**2.** 「山田(やまだ)さんはどんな音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)きますか。」
「山田(やまだ)さん（は・が）ジャズをよく聞(き)きます。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)さん**は**ジャズをよく聞(き)きます。

The question already put 山田(やまだ)さん on the table, so he is old information - the
topic. The news is *ジャズ*, and news comes after `は`. Using `が` here would mean
"it's *Yamada* who listens to jazz", answering a question nobody asked.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Who made this? - I made it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「だれ**が**これを作(つく)りましたか。」「私(わたし)**が**作(つく)りました。」

Both halves take `が`. The question word is the unknown, and the answer replaces
that unknown - it is still the news, so it keeps `が`. Answering
「私(わたし)は作(つく)りました」 would sound like a contrast: "*I* made it (whatever
the others did)."

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 母(はは)は作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はとてもおいしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 母(はは)**が**作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はとてもおいしいです。
"The food my mother makes is delicious."

<code>母(はは)が作(つく)った</code> is a clause modifying <code>料理(りょうり)</code>, and a subject
inside such a clause must take `が`. The outer sentence keeps its own topic
particle on <code>料理(りょうり)は</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山田(やまだ)さん**は**来(き)ました。
(b) 山田(やまだ)さん**が**来(き)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats 山田(やまだ)さん as already-known and reports what he did - "Yamada came
(whatever the others did)." It fits a conversation that was already about him, and
with `は` it can carry a hint of contrast.

(b) presents 山田(やまだ)さん as the news - "*Yamada* is the one who came", or, said on
noticing him, simply "Yamada's here!" It answers "who came?", and it is what you
would say announcing his arrival to a room that did not know.

</details>

# が - but

**Meaning** · joins two clauses - "…, but …"

This `が` is a **conjunction**, not a particle: it follows a whole finished clause
and links it to the next one. Its core job is contrast, but a great deal of the time
it is doing something softer - simply setting the scene for what you are about to
say.

## Formation

`が` attaches to a complete clause. Whatever politeness that clause ends in stays
put, and `が` goes on after it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite clause | ～ます／～です + が | 高(たか)いです → 高(たか)いですが |
| Plain clause (verb) | dictionary / ～た / ～ない + が | 行(い)った → 行(い)ったが |
| Plain clause (い-adj) | ～い + が | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いが |
| Noun / な-adjective | + だ／です first | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)だが／学生(がくせい)ですが |
| Sentence-final | clause + が… (trailing off) | 失礼(しつれい)ですが… |
| Casual equivalent | replace が with けど | 高(たか)いけど |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun row is the one people get wrong. `が` cannot sit directly on a
noun the way the subject particle can. ❌<code>学生(がくせい)が、…</code> meaning "I'm a student,
but…" is not possible; the clause has to be finished with a copula first - 
<code>学生(がくせい)ですが</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)いです<b>が</b>、あまりおいしくないです。
This place is cheap, but the food isn't great.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)は寒(さむ)いです<b>が</b>、部屋(へや)の中(なか)は暖(あたた)かいです。
It's cold outside, but it's warm in the room.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません<b>が</b>、ちょっと静(しず)かにしてください。
Sorry to bother you, but could you keep it down a little?
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)です<b>が</b>、田中(たなか)さんはいらっしゃいますか。
This is Yamada - is Tanaka-san there?
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も電話(でんわ)しました<b>が</b>、だれも出(で)ませんでした。
I called several times, but nobody picked up.
</pre>

<pre>
行(い)きたい<b>が</b>、今週(こんしゅう)は時間(じかん)がない。
I'd like to go, but I've no time this week.
</pre>

<pre>
あの映画(えいが)は三時間(さんじかん)もあります<b>が</b>、全然(ぜんぜん)長(なが)く感(かん)じませんでした。
That film is a full three hours long, but it didn't feel long at all.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の予約(よやく)をお願(ねが)いしたいのです<b>が</b>…。
I'd like to make a booking for tomorrow, if I may…
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`が` is the **formal** member of a family. In descending order of stiffness:
`が` → `けれども` → `けれど` → `けども` → `けど`. All mean the same thing. `が` is at
home in writing, in speeches, in business email and in polite spoken Japanese
attached to `です` / `ます`. Attached to a plain form in casual conversation
(「行(い)きたいが」) it sounds bookish or old-fashioned; a friend would say
「行(い)きたいけど」.

Now the part that changes how you read Japanese. **`が` very often does not mean
"but".** A huge share of its uses are what you might call a preamble: the first
clause exists only to give the listener a place to stand, and there is no contrast
at all.

- 「失礼(しつれい)ですが、日本(にほん)の方(かた)ですか。」 - "Excuse me, are you
  Japanese?" Nothing is being contrasted; `が` is politely clearing its throat.
- 「田中(たなか)ですが、…」 - introducing yourself on the phone before the request.
- 「質問(しつもん)があるんですが、…」 - "I have a question…"

Translating those with "but" produces nonsense. Read `が` as a soft "and so" or a
comma whenever the first clause is not actually being denied by the second.

Related to this is the **trailing** `が`, where the sentence simply stops:
「予約(よやく)したいのですが…」. Leaving the request unsaid is a politeness
strategy - you set up the situation and let the listener supply the rest, which is
less imposing than stating your demand outright. This is extremely common in shops,
offices and phone calls, and a learner who finishes every sentence can sound blunt by
comparison.

One caution about mixing levels. Writing a plain clause before `が` and a polite one
after it (「安(やす)いが、おいしいです」) is normal in written Japanese but odd in
speech. When speaking, keep both halves polite.

## Contrast

### が vs けど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `が` for writing and polite
speech; `けど` for conversation.

</div>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)は読(よ)めます<b>が</b>、書(か)けません。
I can read this character, but I can't write it.
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)、読(よ)める<b>けど</b>、書(か)けない。
I can read this one, but I can't write it.
</pre>

`けど` also attaches happily to polite forms - 「読(よ)めますけど」 - which gives
you a middle register: polite, but relaxed. That combination is very common in spoken
Japanese at work. Going the other way, plain form + `が` in speech is the one
combination to avoid unless you are deliberately sounding formal.

### が vs でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` joins two clauses **inside one sentence**. `でも` starts a
**new** sentence.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は雨(あめ)でした<b>が</b>、試合(しあい)はありました。
It rained yesterday, but the match went ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は雨(あめ)でした。<b>でも</b>、試合(しあい)はありました。
It rained yesterday. But the match went ahead.
</pre>

The meaning is the same; the packaging differs. `でも` puts a full stop in first,
which makes the contrast land harder - it is the spoken equivalent of starting a new
paragraph. ❌<code>雨(あめ)でしたでも</code> is impossible: `でも` cannot glue two clauses
together the way `が` does. In formal writing, `しかし` plays the same sentence-initial
role as `でも`.

### が (but) vs が (subject marker)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what comes **before** the `が`. A bare noun means it is
the subject marker; a finished clause means it is the conjunction.

</div>

The two are unrelated words that happen to share a syllable, and the test is
mechanical.

<pre>
時間(じかん)<b>が</b>ありません。
I don't have time.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)はあります<b>が</b>、お金(かね)がありません。
I have the time, but not the money.
</pre>

In the first, <code>時間(じかん)</code> is just a noun, so `が` is marking it as the subject of
<code>ありません</code>. In the second, <code>時間(じかん)はあります</code> is a whole sentence already, so the
`が` after it can only be the conjunction. Sentences containing both are ordinary and
cause no confusion once you have the test:
「雨(あめ)<b>が</b>降(ふ)っていました<b>が</b>、傘(かさ)はありませんでした。」

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いですでも、おいしくないです。 → ✅ 安(やす)いです**が**、…／安(やす)いです。**でも**、…
  `でも` cannot join clauses. Either use `が` inside one sentence, or break the
  sentence and start the next one with `でも`.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)が、お金(かね)がありません。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)です**が**、お金(かね)がありません。
  The conjunction needs a finished clause in front of it, so a noun must first take
  the copula.

- ❌ Reading 「失礼(しつれい)ですが」 as "it's rude, but". → ✅ "Excuse me, but…"
  Preamble `が` is not adversative. If the second clause does not deny the first,
  do not translate it as "but".

- ❌ 友(とも)だちに「安(やす)いが、おいしくないよ」と言(い)いました。 → ✅ 「安(やす)いけど、おいしくないよ」
  Plain form + `が` in casual speech sounds stiff and literary. Between friends it
  is `けど`.

## Practice

**1.** 日本語(にほんご)は難(むずか)しいです____、面白(おもしろ)いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

難(むずか)しいです<b>が</b>、面白(おもしろ)いです。

The two clauses genuinely pull against each other, and both are polite, so the polite
conjunction `が` joins them inside a single sentence. In conversation `けど` would do
the same job more casually.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しかったです（が・でも）、今日(きょう)は暇(ひま)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

忙(いそが)しかったです<b>が</b>、今日(きょう)は暇(ひま)です。

`でも` only works at the start of a new sentence. Since the two halves are being kept
in one sentence with a comma, the connector has to be `が`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "Excuse me, but is this seat taken?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません<b>が</b>、この席(せき)は空(あ)いていますか。

This is preamble `が`, not contrast - the first clause is only softening the
approach. English uses "but" here too, which is a rare case of the two languages
lining up.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いですでも、暗(くら)いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いです<b>が</b>、暗(くら)いです。

`でも` cannot be attached to the end of a clause. Replace it with `が`, or split the
sentence: 「広(ひろ)いです。でも、暗(くら)いです。」

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 田中(たなか)ですが、山田(やまだ)さんをお願(ねが)いします。
(b) 田中(たなか)です。山田(やまだ)さんをお願(ねが)いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Neither sentence contains any contrast, which is the point of the pair.

(a) uses the preamble `が` to hand the two pieces over as one smooth move: the name
exists only to prepare the request, and the sentence flows into it. This is the
standard shape of a phone call.

(b) states the name and then makes the request as a separate, self-contained
sentence. It is not wrong, but it is more abrupt - two facts placed side by side
rather than one leading into the other.

</details>

# を

**Meaning** · marks the direct object - the thing the verb is done to

`を` points at what receives the action: the thing eaten, written, bought, opened.
It has two further jobs that surprise learners - it marks the **space you move
through** and the **place you leave**. It is written with its own kana but
**pronounced お**.

## Formation

`を` attaches straight to the noun. Which of its three jobs it is doing depends
entirely on the verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct object | Noun + を + transitive verb | ごはん → ごはんを食(た)べる |
| Space moved through | Place + を + 歩(ある)く・走(はし)る・飛(と)ぶ・渡(わた)る | 公園(こうえん) → 公園(こうえん)を歩(ある)く |
| Place left / exited | Place + を + 出(で)る・降(お)りる・卒業(そつぎょう)する | 家(いえ) → 家(いえ)を出(で)る |
| Under は or も | を is **replaced** | ごはんを → ごはんは／ごはんも |
| Casual speech | を is often dropped | ごはん食(た)べた? |
| Pronunciation | written を, said お | - |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a single clause normally carries only **one** `を`. If you find yourself
writing two, one of them is usually meant to be `に`, `で`, or a separate clause.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはん<b>を</b>食(た)べました。
I ate breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
手紙(てがみ)<b>を</b>書(か)きます。
I'm going to write a letter.
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)、公園(こうえん)<b>を</b>歩(ある)きます。
I walk through the park every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
七時(しちじ)に家(いえ)<b>を</b>出(で)ます。
I leave the house at seven.
</pre>

<pre>
次(つぎ)の駅(えき)で電車(でんしゃ)<b>を</b>降(お)ります。
I get off the train at the next station.
</pre>

<pre>
鳥(とり)が空(そら)<b>を</b>飛(と)んでいます。
A bird is flying across the sky.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、その角(かど)<b>を</b>右(みぎ)に曲(ま)がってください。
Excuse me - please turn right at that corner.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)<b>を</b>出(で)てから、三年間(さんねんかん)この会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)いています。
Since leaving university I've been working at this company for three years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`を` is register-neutral and carries no politeness of its own. It is written with a
kana used for nothing else, and it is pronounced **o**, exactly like お. Saying "wo"
marks you instantly as a learner; the only places you will hear it are singing and
extremely careful enunciation.

The thing to internalise is how freely `を` is **dropped** in casual speech. Short
sentences where the object is obvious lose it constantly:

- 「ごはん、食(た)べた?」 - "Did you eat?"
- 「これ、見(み)て。」 - "Look at this."
- 「コーヒー飲(の)む?」 - "Want coffee?"

That is casual, not wrong. Keep `を` in writing, in `ます`-form speech, and any time
the sentence is long enough that the listener needs the signpost. Also keep it when
the sentence would otherwise be ambiguous about who is doing what to whom.

Two smaller notes. `を` never stacks with `は`, `も` or `が` - when the object becomes
the topic it is *replaced*: ごはんは食(た)べました. And `を` is the marker used with
the causative later on, which is where the one-`を`-per-clause tendency starts to bite;
you do not need that yet, but it is why the rule is worth knowing early.

## Contrast

### を vs が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a small set of predicates take their "object" with `が`, not
`を`, because grammatically that noun is their subject.

</div>

English transitivity is a poor guide here, and this is one of the most persistent N5
errors.

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)、新聞(しんぶん)<b>を</b>読(よ)みます。
I read the newspaper every day.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)はスポーツ<b>が</b>好(す)きです。
I like sports.
</pre>

<code>読(よ)む</code> is an ordinary transitive verb and takes `を`. But <code>好(す)き</code>,
<code>嫌(きら)い</code>, <code>上手(じょうず)</code>, `ほしい`, `わかる`, `できる`, `ある`, <code>見(み)える</code> and
<code>聞(き)こえる</code> all take `が`, however transitive they look in English. Learn that set
as a list; there is no shortcut.

A useful pair is <code>見(み)る</code> and <code>見(み)える</code>. Looking at something is an action, so
「海(うみ)<b>を</b>見(み)ます」. Something being visible is a state, so
「海(うみ)<b>が</b>見(み)えます」.

### を vs で vs に for places

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` = through it, `で` = at it, `に` = to it.

</div>

The same place noun takes three different particles depending on what the verb is
doing with it, and this is where the "path" use of `を` earns its keep.

<pre>
公園(こうえん)<b>を</b>走(はし)ります。
I run through the park.
</pre>

<pre>
公園(こうえん)<b>で</b>走(はし)ります。
I run at the park.
</pre>

<pre>
公園(こうえん)<b>に</b>行(い)きます。
I go to the park.
</pre>

With `を`, the park is the ground your route crosses - you are moving along and
through it. With `で`, the park is merely the location where the running takes place.
`に` marks the destination and cannot be used for either of the others.

Verbs that describe covering ground - <code>歩(ある)く</code>, <code>走(はし)る</code>, <code>飛(と)ぶ</code>,
<code>渡(わた)る</code>, <code>通(とお)る</code>, <code>曲(ま)がる</code> - take `を` for the space they cover, even though
none of them is transitive in English.

### を出(で)る vs から出(で)る

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` treats the place as something you **leave behind**; `から`
treats it as the **point you started from**.

</div>

<pre>
八時(はちじ)に会社(かいしゃ)<b>を</b>出(で)ました。
I left the office at eight.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)<b>から</b>駅(えき)まで歩(ある)きます。
I walk from the office to the station.
</pre>

With `を`, the focus is on the act of departure itself - you are done with that place.
With `から`, the place is one end of a route, and the sentence is really about the
journey. This is why <code>大学(だいがく)を出(で)る</code> can mean "graduate" while
<code>大学(だいがく)から出(で)る</code> just means physically walking out of the building.

The same split shows up with vehicles: <code>電車(でんしゃ)を降(お)りる</code> is the normal way
to say "get off the train", because you are leaving the train behind.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)**を**わかります。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)**が**わかります。
  `わかる`, `できる`, <code>好(す)き</code>, `ほしい` and friends take `が` even though English
  makes them look transitive.

- ❌ 電車(でんしゃ)**を**乗(の)ります。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)**に**乗(の)ります。
  Getting on takes `に` (you end up inside it); getting off takes `を`
  (<code>電車(でんしゃ)を降(お)りる</code>). The pair has to be learned together.

- ❌ 道(みち)**で**渡(わた)ってください。 → ✅ 道(みち)**を**渡(わた)ってください。
  Crossing covers ground, so the road is the space traversed. `で` would make it the
  location where some other activity happens.

- ❌ ごはん**をも**食(た)べました。 → ✅ ごはん**も**食(た)べました。
  `を` is replaced by `は` and `も`, never stacked with them.

- ❌ Pronouncing を as *wo*. → ✅ It is **o**, identical to お.

## Practice

**1.** 毎晩(まいばん)、寝(ね)る前(まえ)に少(すこ)し本(ほん)____読(よ)みます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本(ほん)<b>を</b>読(よ)みます。

<code>読(よ)む</code> is a plain transitive verb and the book is what gets read, so it takes the
direct-object marker. In casual speech the particle would often simply be dropped.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 橋(はし)（を・で）渡(わた)ると、右(みぎ)に郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

橋(はし)<b>を</b>渡(わた)ると…

<code>渡(わた)る</code> is a verb of covering ground, so the bridge is the space you move across
and takes `を`. `で` would present the bridge as a location where something else was
going on.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I get off the bus in front of the station."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)の前(まえ)でバス<b>を</b>降(お)ります。

<code>降(お)りる</code> takes `を` for the thing you are leaving - the bus - while the place
where this happens is marked with `で`. Note that getting *on* would flip to `に`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 私(わたし)は新(あたら)しいパソコンをほしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 私(わたし)は新(あたら)しいパソコン<b>が</b>ほしいです。

`ほしい` is an adjective, not a verb - it describes a state of wanting, and the thing
wanted is its subject. That puts it in the `が` group along with `わかる`,
<code>好(す)き</code> and <code>見(み)える</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 公園(こうえん)<b>を</b>歩(ある)きました。
(b) 公園(こうえん)<b>で</b>歩(ある)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the "path" `を`: the park is the ground you covered, so the picture is of
walking along its paths, moving through and across it. This is the natural way to
describe a walk in the park.

(b) makes the park merely the location of the activity. It is not ungrammatical, but
it sounds like you went to the park specifically in order to do some walking there - 
as exercise, say - rather than strolling through it. For ordinary "I walked in the
park", (a) is what a Japanese speaker will produce.

</details>

# ね

**Meaning** · invites the listener to agree - "…, isn't it?", "…, right?"

`ね` at the end of a sentence hands the sentence to the other person. It says: *you
know this too, don't you.* That is why it warms a conversation up - and why it sounds
wrong the moment you attach it to something only you could know.

## Formation

`ね` goes at the very end, after everything else, including `よ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite sentence | ～です／～ます + ね | いいです → いいですね |
| Plain sentence (verb / い-adj) | plain + ね | おいしい → おいしいね |
| Noun / な-adjective (plain) | + だ + ね | 元気(げんき) → 元気(げんき)だね |
| Request | ～てください + ね | 待(ま)ってください → 待(ま)ってくださいね |
| Mid-sentence | phrase + ね (filler) | あのね、昨日(きのう)ね… |
| Combined with よ | よ + ね (never ねよ) | そうだ → そうだよね |
| Lengthened | ね → ねえ／ねー (casual) | そうだねえ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - after a plain noun or な-adjective, men typically keep the `だ`
(「元気(げんき)だね」) while women often drop it (「元気(げんき)ね」). Both are
normal; the version without `だ` sounds softer and is more marked as feminine.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
いい天気(てんき)です<b>ね</b>。
Lovely weather, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
この料理(りょうり)、おいしい<b>ね</b>。
This food is good, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)三時(さんじ)です<b>ね</b>。
Three o'clock tomorrow, right?
</pre>

<pre>
ちょっと待(ま)っていて<b>ね</b>。
Hang on here a moment, okay?
</pre>

<pre>
それは大変(たいへん)でした<b>ね</b>。
That must have been rough.
</pre>

<pre>
あの<b>ね</b>、話(はなし)があるんだけど。
Listen - there's something I want to talk to you about.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の試験(しけん)、思(おも)ったより難(むずか)しかったです<b>ね</b>。
Yesterday's exam was harder than we expected, wasn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、この書類(しょるい)は金曜日(きんようび)までに出(だ)せばいいんです<b>ね</b>。
Sorry - so I just need to hand this in by Friday, is that right?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ね` works at every politeness level: 「そうですね」 to a client, 「そうだね」 to a
friend. What it is sensitive to is not formality but **shared ground**. `ね` claims
that the listener already knows or feels what you are saying, so it belongs on
weather, on food you are both eating, on a fact you are checking. It does *not*
belong on information only you have. 「わたしは田中(たなか)ですね」 is strange for
the same reason English 「I'm Tanaka, isn't it?」 is strange.

Intonation splits `ね` into two uses that learners should keep apart:

- **Falling** `ね` shares a feeling. 「暑(あつ)いですね。」 = "Hot, isn't it." You
  are not asking; you are including the other person.
- **Rising** `ね?` checks a fact. 「明日(あした)三時(さんじ)ですね?」 = "Three
  o'clock tomorrow, right?" You are fairly sure and want confirmation.

`ね` also softens. Attached to a request it takes the edge off
(「気(き)をつけてね」), and it is the standard way adults speak to children. That
same softness is what makes **overuse** a real risk. A speaker who ends every
sentence with `ね` sounds as though they cannot commit to anything without approval - 
needy, or oddly ingratiating. Native speakers use it a lot, but interleaved with
plain endings and with `よ`; a solid wall of `ね` is a learner tell.

Two register cautions. In business, 「そうですね…」 with a long vowel is a thinking
noise ("well, let me see…") and often precedes a polite disagreement - do not hear it
as agreement. And with a superior, `ね` on something that is *their* business can
sound presumptuous: telling your boss 「忙(いそが)しいですね」 assumes you know how
their week is going.

Finally, the feminine variants. 「～わね」「～のね」 are marked as women's speech and
now sound somewhat traditional; 「～だね」「～だよね」 are neutral and used by
everybody. `ねえ` lengthened at the start of a sentence is a casual "hey".

## Contrast

### ね vs よ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ね` = "you know this too". `よ` = "you don't know this, so I'm
telling you".

</div>

This is the central axis of Japanese sentence-final particles, and getting it wrong
is what makes learners sound blunt.

<pre>
この店(みせ)、おいしいです<b>ね</b>。
This place is good, isn't it. (we're both eating here)
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)、おいしいです<b>よ</b>。
This place is good - you should try it. (you haven't been)
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences misfire. `ね` to someone who has never been to the
restaurant assumes knowledge they do not have; `よ` to the person sitting opposite you
eating the same meal sounds like you are correcting them.

`よね` combines the two, in that order and never ❌`ねよ`. It means "I'm telling you
this, but check me": you supply the information and simultaneously ask for
confirmation.

<pre>
明日(あした)の集合(しゅうごう)は九時(くじ)だ<b>よね</b>。
The meeting time tomorrow is nine, right?
</pre>

Use `よね` when you believe something but want the other person to confirm it - it is
softer than a bare `よ` and more committed than a bare `ね`. It is extremely common in
everyday speech.

### ね vs か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ね` checks something you already believe. `か` asks about
something you do not know.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は二時(にじ)からです<b>ね</b>。
The meeting starts at two, doesn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)からです<b>か</b>。
What time does the meeting start?
</pre>

The `ね` version says you have the information and want it confirmed; you are
expecting "はい". The `か` version says you have nothing and are asking to be told.
Using `か` when you already know the answer sounds like a test; using `ね` when you
genuinely have no idea puts the listener in the odd position of confirming something
you never knew.

### ね vs でしょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ね` invites agreement gently. `でしょう?` presses for it.

</div>

<pre>
きれいな景色(けしき)です<b>ね</b>。
Beautiful view, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
きれいな景色(けしき)<b>でしょう</b>?
Beautiful view, right? - told you.
</pre>

Rising `でしょう?` is used when you brought the listener here, recommended the film,
or predicted the outcome: it asks them to confirm that *you were right*. That makes
it warm among friends and slightly triumphant. `ね` claims no such credit. Note also
that falling `でしょう` is a different thing entirely - a guess, "probably" - so
intonation matters as much as it does with `ね` itself.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ はじめまして。田中(たなか)ですね。 → ✅ はじめまして。田中(たなか)です。
  Your own name is not shared knowledge. `ね` on information only you have sounds as
  though you are asking the listener to confirm who you are.

- ❌ ちょっと頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いですね。 → ✅ ちょっと頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いです。
  Nobody else can feel your headache. Use `ね` for the shared world - weather, food,
  the room you are both in.

- ❌ 「学生(がくせい)ですか。」「はい、そうですね。」 → ✅ 「はい、そうです。」
  「そうですね」 is agreement with someone else's opinion, or a thinking noise. As an
  answer about yourself it sounds evasive.

- ❌ そうだ**ねよ**。 → ✅ そうだ**よね**。
  The order is fixed: `よ` first, `ね` last. `ね` is always the final particle.

## Practice

**1.** （on the phone, confirming）明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は十時(じゅうじ)から____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

十時(じゅうじ)からです<b>ね</b>。

You already believe the meeting is at ten and want it confirmed, which is exactly
`ね`'s job. Rising intonation makes the checking function clear.

</details>

**2.** Choose: your friend has never been to this café, and you have.
このケーキ、おいしいです（ね・よ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このケーキ、おいしいです<b>よ</b>。

The friend has no experience of the cake, so there is nothing to agree with yet - 
you are supplying new information, which is `よ`. `ね` would wrongly assume they
already know.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "It's Monday tomorrow, right?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)、月曜日(げつようび)だ<b>よね</b>。

You believe it is Monday but want a check, which is precisely `よね` - the `よ`
supplies your belief and the `ね` asks for confirmation. A bare `ね` would work too,
but sounds more like idle small talk than a real check.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)、わたしは京都(きょうと)へ行(い)きましたね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)、わたしは京都(きょうと)へ行(い)きましたよ。

Where you went yesterday is your own information, so there is nothing for the
listener to agree with. `よ` presents it as news. `ね` would only work if the listener
had been there with you.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 寒(さむ)いですね。
(b) 寒(さむ)いですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) assumes you are both in the same cold - standing at the same bus stop, say. It
is a piece of social glue, and the expected reply is 「そうですね」.

(b) informs the listener of something they have not felt yet: you have just come in
from outside, or they are about to leave without a coat. Said to someone standing
next to you in the same wind, it sounds like a correction.

</details>

# よ

**Meaning** · tells the listener something they don't yet know

`よ` at the end of a sentence marks the content as **news**. It says: *this is
information you didn't have, and I'm giving it to you.* Used well it is helpful and
warm; used on something the listener already knows, it turns into a lecture.

## Formation

`よ` comes after the finished sentence, and before `ね` if both are present.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite sentence | ～です／～ます + よ | あります → ありますよ |
| Plain sentence (verb / い-adj) | plain + よ | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いよ |
| Noun / な-adjective (plain) | + だ + よ | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みだよ |
| Negative | ～ない／～ません + よ | 違(ちが)います → 違(ちが)いますよ |
| Request | ～てください + よ (pushy) | 待(ま)ってください → 待(ま)ってくださいよ |
| Combined with ね | よ + ね (never ねよ) | そうだ → そうだよね |
| Feminine variant | Noun／な-adj + よ, no だ | そう → そうよ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `だ` row is where register lives. 「そうだよ」 is neutral to
masculine; 「そうよ」 without the `だ` is marked as women's speech and sounds somewhat
traditional today. In polite speech the question does not arise, because `です` is
always there: 「そうですよ」.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あ、財布(さいふ)が落(お)ちました<b>よ</b>。
Excuse me - you've dropped your wallet.
</pre>

<pre>
その店(みせ)、もう閉(し)まってる<b>よ</b>。
That shop's already closed.
</pre>

<pre>
大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です<b>よ</b>。心配(しんぱい)しないでください。
It's fine, really. Don't worry.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あぶ)ない<b>よ</b>!
Careful!
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は来週(らいしゅう)の火曜日(かようび)です<b>よ</b>。
The meeting is next Tuesday, actually.
</pre>

<pre>
本当(ほんとう)に見(み)た<b>よ</b>。うそじゃない。
I really did see it. I'm not making it up.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)より地下鉄(ちかてつ)のほうが早(はや)く着(つ)きます<b>よ</b>。
The subway will get you there faster than the train.
</pre>

<pre>
このお店(みせ)のケーキ、とてもおいしいから、一度(いちど)食(た)べてみるといいです<b>よ</b>。
The cake here is really good, so you ought to try it sometime.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`よ` attaches at any politeness level, and by itself it is neither casual nor formal:
「ありますよ」 is perfectly polite. What `よ` does carry is an attitude, and that is
where it gets dangerous.

Because `よ` claims the listener does not know something, using it on information
they obviously do have sounds condescending - the Japanese equivalent of "just so you
know". 「今日(きょう)は月曜日(げつようび)ですよ」 said to a colleague who has
been at work all morning is not helpful; it is a small correction. The higher the
listener's status, the worse this lands. Telling your boss 「その資料(しりょう)は
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にありますよ」 is fine if they were looking for it, and
faintly impertinent if they were not.

`よ` also sharpens under stress. On a negative or a correction it can sting:
「違(ちが)いますよ」 is a good deal harder than 「違(ちが)います」. On a request
it turns into impatience: 「早(はや)くしてくださいよ」 is a complaint, not a
polite ask. And with the plain imperative - 「早(はや)くしろよ」 - it is
straightforwardly rough male speech.

The friendly uses are just as real. `よ` reassures (「大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですよ」),
warns (「危(あぶ)ないよ」), offers (「あちらですよ」) and recommends
(「おいしいですよ」). In all of these you genuinely have information the other person
lacks, which is exactly the condition `よ` wants.

Intonation matters. Rising `よ` is light and helpful; a flat or falling `よ` is firm
and can be read as insistent. A learner unsure of the intonation is safer leaving
`よ` off entirely - a bare `です` never offends.

Finally, `よね`: `よ` and `ね` in that order, never ❌`ねよ`. It offers information
*and* asks for confirmation - "it's this, isn't it?" It is softer than `よ` alone,
because you are leaving the listener room to correct you, and it is one of the most
common endings in real conversation.

## Contrast

### よ vs ね

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よ` gives information the listener lacks. `ね` assumes the
listener already has it.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんはもう帰(かえ)りました<b>よ</b>。
Tanaka's already gone home. (you didn't know)
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんはもう帰(かえ)りました<b>ね</b>。
Tanaka's gone home, hasn't he. (we both saw him leave)
</pre>

The information is identical; only the assumption about the listener changes. Get it
backwards and the effect is immediate: `よ` on shared knowledge sounds like a
correction, and `ね` on private knowledge leaves the listener unable to answer.

A quick test before you speak: *does this person already know?* If yes, `ね` or
nothing. If no, `よ`.

### よ vs よね

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よ` asserts. `よね` asserts **and** asks you to confirm.

</div>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)、「みち」と読(よ)みます<b>よ</b>。
This character is read "michi".
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)、「みち」と読(よ)みます<b>よね</b>。
This character is read "michi", isn't it?
</pre>

The first is confident: you know, they don't, end of story. The second hands the
listener a way out - you are fairly sure, but you would like a second opinion. `よね`
is therefore much safer when talking to someone who might know better than you, and
it is what you should reach for when correcting a superior.

Note that `よね` is not the same as a bare `ね`. `ね` alone would assume the listener
already had the reading in mind; `よね` says you are supplying it and checking at the
same time.

### よ vs saying nothing

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a sentence with no final particle is neutral. Adding `よ` pushes
it at the listener.

</div>

Learners tend to treat `よ` as decoration and sprinkle it everywhere. It is not
neutral, and the plain sentence is very often the better choice.

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みです。
Tomorrow is a day off.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みです<b>よ</b>。
Tomorrow's a day off, you know.
</pre>

The first states a fact - appropriate in a report, an announcement, or an answer to a
direct question. The second addresses the listener personally and implies they were
unaware, which is friendly if true and slightly pointed if not.

In writing, `よ` almost never appears outside dialogue and messages between friends;
it is a spoken-language particle. And when answering a question you were just asked,
the bare form is usually right - the questioner already knows they didn't know.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そうです**ねよ**。 → ✅ そうです**よね**。
  The order is fixed. `よ` comes first, `ね` is always last.

- ❌ 「今日(きょう)、金曜日(きんようび)ですよ。」 said to someone who plainly knows. → ✅ 「今日(きょう)、金曜日(きんようび)ですね。」
  `よ` claims the listener is missing the information. On shared knowledge it reads
  as a correction.

- ❌ （to your manager）その仕事(しごと)はもう終(お)わりましたよ。 → ✅ もう終(お)わりました。
  Answering a superior's question with `よ` can sound flippant, as though the answer
  should have been obvious. The plain polite form is safer.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)よ。（male speaker） → ✅ 学生(がくせい)だよ。
  After a plain noun, `よ` needs `だ` in front of it. Dropping the `だ` is a marked,
  feminine-sounding pattern, not a neutral one.

## Practice

**1.** （your friend is about to leave without their umbrella）あ、傘(かさ)、忘(わす)れています____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傘(かさ)、忘(わす)れています<b>よ</b>。

They have not noticed, so you are supplying genuinely new information - the
prototypical use of `よ`. Rising intonation keeps it helpful rather than scolding.

</details>

**2.** Choose: you and a colleague both watched it rain all morning.
よく降(ふ)ります（よ・ね）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

よく降(ふ)ります<b>ね</b>。

The rain is shared experience, so there is nothing to inform anyone of. `よ` here
would sound as though you were correcting a colleague who had somehow failed to
notice the weather.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Don't worry - there's still plenty of time."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

心配(しんぱい)しないでください。まだ時間(じかん)があります<b>よ</b>。

The listener believes time is short, so you are correcting their picture with
information they lack - a reassuring `よ`. Without it, 「まだ時間(じかん)があります」
is a flat statement and does much less comforting.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)よね、と先生(せんせい)が言(い)いました。（male speaker, plain style）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>だよね</b>、と先生(せんせい)が言(い)いました。

A plain noun predicate needs `だ` before `よ`. Leaving it out gives the feminine
pattern 「雨(あめ)よね」, which does not match the speaker described.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) こちらのほうが安(やす)いですよ。
(b) こちらのほうが安(やす)いですよね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is you informing the listener: you know this one is cheaper and they do not. Said
by a shop assistant it is helpful; said to someone who has already compared the
prices, it sounds like a correction.

(b) softens the same claim into a check. You still believe this one is cheaper, but
the `ね` invites the listener to confirm or contradict you. That makes it the safer
choice when the other person may know the prices better than you do.

</details>
