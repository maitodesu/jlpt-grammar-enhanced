# N5 - Where, when, how


---

# に

**Meaning** · marks a point - where something ends up, where it sits, when it happens, or who receives it

`に` names a **point**, never a stretch or an area. The place a movement finishes, the
spot a thing occupies, the moment on the clock, the person a thing lands with: all of
those are single points, and `に` is how Japanese pins them down. Nearly every use
below is a version of that one idea.

## Formation

`に` is a particle. It attaches straight onto a noun, a time word, or a ます-stem, and
nothing changes shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - destination | Noun + に + motion verb | 学校(がっこう) → 学校(がっこう)に行(い)く |
| Noun - where something exists | Noun + に + ある／いる | 台所(だいどころ) → 台所(だいどころ)にある |
| Noun - person who receives | Person + に + giving or telling verb | 母(はは) → 母(はは)に話(はな)す |
| Time word with a number or a name | Time + に | 六時(ろくじ) → 六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きる |
| Verb ます-stem - purpose | stem + に + 行(い)く／来(く)る／帰(かえ)る | 買(か)います → 買(か)いに行(い)く |
| Noun - thing entered, boarded, sat on | Noun + に + 入(はい)る／乗(の)る／座(すわ)る | いす → いすに座(すわ)る |
| Period - rate or frequency | Period + に + number | 週(しゅう) → 週(しゅう)に三回(さんかい) |
| Noun or な-adjective - result of a change | Noun + に + なる | 先生(せんせい) → 先生(せんせい)になる |

<div class="note">

**Note - which time words take `に`?** If the time word is a number or a fixed name on
the calendar, it takes `に`: 七時(しちじ)に, 三月(さんがつ)に, 月曜日(げつようび)に,
二十日(はつか)に, 誕生日(たんじょうび)に. If it is positioned relative to *now*, or
if it is a frequency word, it takes **nothing at all**: 今日(きょう), 明日(あした),
昨日(きのう), 今朝(けさ), 今晩(こんばん), 先週(せんしゅう), 来年(らいねん),
毎日(まいにち), いつ. Adding `に` to those is one of the most persistent N5 errors.
朝(あさ), 夜(よる) and 夏(なつ) sit in between - both forms are heard, and the
bare one is more common in speech.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

There's a cat under the desk.
</pre>

<pre>
ゆうべ母(はは)<b>に</b>電話(でんわ)しました。
I phoned my mother last night.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょっとパンを買(か)い<b>に</b>行(い)ってきます。
I'm just popping out to buy some bread.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の水曜日(すいようび)<b>に</b>大阪(おおさか)で会議(かいぎ)があります。
There's a meeting in Osaka next Wednesday.
</pre>

<pre>
週(しゅう)<b>に</b>三回(さんかい)ジムで泳(およ)いでいます。
I swim at the gym three times a week.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころ、この公園(こうえん)<b>に</b>よく遊(あそ)び<b>に</b>来(き)ました。
When I was a kid I often came to this park to play.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、この書類(しょるい)を田中(たなか)さん<b>に</b>渡(わた)してもらえますか。
Sorry - could you pass this document to Tanaka?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`に` is completely register-neutral. It is the same in plain speech, in ます-form, in
business email and in newspapers. Choosing `に` never makes you sound stiff or casual.

What varies is whether you say it. In quick casual speech the **destination** `に` is
routinely dropped:

- どこ行(い)くの? - "Where are you off to?"
- ちょっとコンビニ行(い)ってくる。 - "Just nipping to the convenience store."

That dropping is limited to destinations. The `に` of **existence**, the `に` of a
**recipient**, and the `に` of a **clock time** are not dropped - remove them and the
sentence stops parsing rather than sounding casual.

`に` is phonologically weightless. It never carries stress, and it attaches so tightly
to the preceding noun that Japanese speakers hear <code>六時(ろくじ)に</code> as one
word. Do not pause before it.

One more use worth knowing early, because it turns up constantly: `に` marks the
**result of a change**, with なる and する. 姉(あね)は医者(いしゃ)になりました。
部屋(へや)をきれい<b>に</b>してください。 It is the same "landing point" idea - the
thing ends up *at* that state.

## Contrast

### に vs で

This is the single most common particle error at N5, and it is worth over-learning.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に` marks where something **is**; `で` marks where something
**happens**.

</div>

If the verb is ある, いる, 住(す)む, 座(すわ)る, 置(お)く, 泊(と)まる - verbs about
a thing occupying a spot - use `に`. If the verb is an activity that could happen
anywhere, use `で`.

<pre>
公園(こうえん)<b>に</b>ベンチがあります。
There's a bench in the park.
</pre>

<pre>
公園(こうえん)<b>で</b>お弁当(べんとう)を食(た)べました。
We ate our packed lunch in the park.
</pre>

Same park; different job. The bench *is located* there, so `に`. The eating *took
place* there, so `で`.

The trap that catches nearly everyone is 住(す)む. In English "live in Tokyo" feels
like an activity, but Japanese treats it as being located somewhere:

<pre>
兄(あに)は東京(とうきょう)<b>に</b>住(す)んでいます。
My older brother lives in Tokyo.
</pre>

❌<code>東京(とうきょう)で住(す)んでいます</code> is wrong. Learn <code>住(す)む</code> as
a に-verb the way you learn a verb's conjugation.

### に vs へ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for a destination they are interchangeable. Everywhere else, only
`に` works.

</div>

<pre>
空港(くうこう)<b>に</b>着(つ)きました。
I've arrived at the airport.
</pre>

Swap that `に` for `へ` and the sentence is odd, because 着(つ)く is about the arrival
*point*, which is `に` territory. Where the verb is plain movement - 行(い)く,
来(く)る, 帰(かえ)る - either particle is fine and the difference is a shade of
emphasis: `に` on the endpoint, `へ` on the heading.

The clean division is what `へ` **cannot** do. It cannot mark a time
(❌<code>六時(ろくじ)へ</code>), it cannot mark existence
(❌<code>部屋(へや)へいます</code>), and it cannot mark the person you tell or ask
something (❌<code>先生(せんせい)へ聞(き)きました</code>). All three are `に`.

### に vs を with verbs of movement

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - go **in**, get **on**, sit **down**: `に`. Go **out**, get **off**,
pass **through**: `を`.

</div>

English uses a preposition for both directions, so learners reach for one particle for
all of them. Japanese splits them. Entering, boarding and attaching take `に`, because
they end at a point. Leaving and traversing take `を`, because the noun is the thing
being *departed* or *crossed*.

<pre>
次(つぎ)の駅(えき)で急行(きゅうこう)<b>に</b>乗(の)ります。
I'll get on the express at the next station.
</pre>

<pre>
次(つぎ)の駅(えき)で電車(でんしゃ)<b>を</b>降(お)ります。
I'll get off the train at the next station.
</pre>

The same pairing runs through 入(はい)る／出(で)る and
大学(だいがく)に入(はい)る／大学(だいがく)を出(で)る.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)<b>に</b>東京(とうきょう)へ行(い)きます。 → ✅ 明日(あした)東京(とうきょう)へ行(い)きます。
  Time words that float relative to now - 今日(きょう), 明日(あした), 毎日(まいにち),
  来週(らいしゅう) - take no particle at all.

- ❌ 公園(こうえん)<b>で</b>ベンチがあります。 → ✅ 公園(こうえん)<b>に</b>ベンチがあります。
  ある and いる report where a thing *exists*, and existence is always `に`.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)<b>を</b>聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)<b>に</b>聞(き)きました。
  The person you ask is a recipient, not an object. <code>聞(き)く</code> takes `を` for
  the *question* and `に` for the *person*.

- ❌ パンを買(か)う<b>に</b>行(い)きます。 → ✅ パンを買(か)い<b>に</b>行(い)きます。
  Purpose `に` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. With a する-noun you
  can drop the verb entirely: 買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)く.

- ❌ 東京(とうきょう)<b>で</b>住(す)んでいます。 → ✅ 東京(とうきょう)<b>に</b>住(す)んでいます。
  <code>住(す)む</code> is a location-of-existence verb in Japanese, however active
  "live" feels in English.

## Practice

**1.** 来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)____友達(ともだち)に会(あ)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)<b>に</b>友達(ともだち)に会(あ)います。

金曜日(きんようび) is a named day on the calendar, so it takes `に`. Note that
来週(らいしゅう) on its own would take nothing - it is the 金曜日(きんようび) inside the
phrase that licenses the particle.

</details>

**2.** 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)（に・で）辞書(じしょ)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

机(つくえ)の上(うえ)<b>に</b>辞書(じしょ)があります。

The verb is ある, which reports where a thing exists, and existence takes `に`. `で`
would need an action happening on the desk, which a dictionary is not doing.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I go to the gym twice a week."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

週(しゅう)<b>に</b>二回(にかい)ジム<b>に</b>行(い)きます。

Two different jobs for the same particle. The first `に` is rate - a number of times
per period. The second is destination. Saying 週(しゅう)二回(にかい) without `に` is
possible in casual speech but the full form is safer.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 毎朝(まいあさ)に六時(ろくじ)起(お)きて、会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)<b>に</b>起(お)きて、会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きます。
"I get up at six every morning and go to work."

The two particles are swapped. 毎朝(まいあさ) is a frequency word and takes nothing;
六時(ろくじ) is a clock time and needs `に`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) パンを買(か)い<b>に</b>行(い)きます。
(b) パンを買(か)って行(い)きます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is purpose: buying bread is the *reason* for going. You set off, and the errand is
what the trip is for. Nothing is said about where you go afterwards.

(b) is sequence: you buy bread and then continue on your way. The going is already
planned, and the bread is picked up en route - this is what you would say about
bringing something to a friend's house.

</details>

# へ - direction, toward

**Meaning** · points movement in a direction - toward, off in the direction of

`へ` orients. It says which way something is heading, and unlike `に` it says nothing
about arriving. That is why `へ` works for compass directions and for the address on an
envelope, and why it is useless for times, for existence, and for people you talk to.

<div class="note">

**Note - `へ` is read *e*.** When it is a particle, the hiragana へ is pronounced **え**,
not *he*. 東京(とうきょう)へ is *Tōkyō e*. It is written へ for historical reasons only,
exactly like the topic particle は being read *wa*.

</div>

## Formation

`へ` is a particle and attaches directly to a noun. The noun does not change.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - place you head for | Place + へ + motion verb | 京都(きょうと) → 京都(きょうと)へ行(い)く |
| Noun - compass or relative direction | Direction + へ | 右(みぎ) → 右(みぎ)へ曲(ま)がる |
| Noun - addressee of a letter or gift | Person + へ | 母(はは) → 母(はは)へ |
| Noun modifying another noun | Noun + への + Noun | 東京(とうきょう) → 東京(とうきょう)への電車(でんしゃ) |
| Fixed greeting | ようこそ + Place + へ | 日本(にほん) → ようこそ日本(にほん)へ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the fourth row is the one grammatical thing `へ` can do that `に` cannot.
`へ` takes `の` and modifies a following noun; `に` does not.
友達(ともだち)へのプレゼント is natural, ❌<code>友達(ともだち)にのプレゼント</code> is
not Japanese.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)<b>へ</b>行(い)きます。
I'm going to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
どこ<b>へ</b>行(い)きますか。
Where are you going?
</pre>

<pre>
あの角(かど)を右(みぎ)<b>へ</b>曲(ま)がってください。
Please turn right at that corner.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、国(くに)<b>へ</b>帰(かえ)ります。
I'm going back to my home country next month.
</pre>

<pre>
ようこそ日本(にほん)<b>へ</b>。
Welcome to Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)<b>へ</b>手紙(てがみ)を書(か)きました。
I wrote a letter to my grandmother.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)<b>への</b>電車(でんしゃ)はもう出(で)てしまいました。
The train to Tokyo has already left.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が北(きた)<b>へ</b>進(すす)んでいるので、明日(あした)の飛行機(ひこうき)は飛(と)ばないかもしれません。
The typhoon is moving north, so tomorrow's flight may not take off.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`へ` is neutral in politeness - it is not more formal than `に` in the way learners are
sometimes told. What is true is that `へ` is **more common in writing** and in slightly
composed speech, and `に` dominates ordinary conversation. Announcements, timetables,
travel writing and envelopes lean `へ`.

Two places where `へ` is essentially fixed and swapping in `に` would sound wrong:

- **Envelopes and dedications.** 田中(たなか)様(さま)へ on the front of a card,
  両親(りょうしん)へ at the top of a letter. The `へ` here is "to, in the direction of
  you" rather than "handed to you".
- **ようこそ～へ.** 大阪(おおさか)へようこそ, ようこそわが社(しゃ)へ. A set frame.

In fast casual speech `へ` is dropped just as `に` is - どこ行(い)くの? - so the choice
disappears entirely at the most informal level.

One last register point: because `へ` emphasises the heading rather than the arrival, it
carries a faint flavour of a journey being undertaken. 海(うみ)へ is a touch more
evocative than 海(うみ)に. Song titles and book titles exploit this constantly. You do
not need to reproduce the effect, but recognise it when you meet it.

## Contrast

### へ vs に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `へ` is the **heading**; `に` is the **landing point**. For plain
行(い)く／来(く)る／帰(かえ)る they overlap almost completely, so pick either.
Outside destinations, only `に` exists.

</div>

<pre>
山(やま)の方(ほう)<b>へ</b>歩(ある)いていきました。
He walked off in the direction of the mountains.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)<b>に</b>着(つ)きました。
We reached the top of the mountain.
</pre>

The first says nothing about whether he got anywhere; `へ` only gives the bearing. The
second is entirely about arrival, and that is a point, so `に`.

Because of this, verbs that focus on the endpoint prefer `に`: 着(つ)く,
入(はい)る, 乗(の)る, 届(とど)く. Verbs of open-ended motion happily take `へ`:
向(む)かう, 進(すす)む, 飛(と)ぶ, 歩(ある)く.

And the hard boundary is worth restating. `へ` cannot mark a **time**
(❌<code>九時(くじ)へ</code>), cannot mark **existence**
(❌<code>部屋(へや)へ犬(いぬ)がいます</code>), and cannot mark the **person you tell or
ask** (❌<code>先生(せんせい)へ質問(しつもん)しました</code>). Those are `に` jobs, all
three.

### へ vs まで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `へ` gives the direction of travel; `まで` gives the far end of the
stretch you cover.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)<b>へ</b>走(はし)りました。
I ran to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)<b>まで</b>走(はし)りました。
I ran as far as the station.
</pre>

The first is about where you were headed. The second measures the running: the station
is the point where it stopped, and the sentence implies the whole distance was covered
on foot. That is why `まで` pairs with `から` and `へ` does not:
家(いえ)から駅(えき)まで - a span with two ends.

### へ vs を

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `へ` marks the place you are heading for; `を` marks the space you
move **through**.

</div>

English "go along the river" and "go to the river" both look like one preposition slot,
so learners over-use `へ`. When the noun is the route rather than the goal, Japanese
uses `を` - with 通(とお)る, 渡(わた)る, 歩(ある)く, 散歩(さんぽ)する, 曲(ま)がる.

<pre>
この橋(はし)<b>を</b>渡(わた)ると、郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)があります。
Cross this bridge and there's a post office.
</pre>

<pre>
あの橋(はし)<b>へ</b>行(い)きましょう。
Let's go over to that bridge.
</pre>

Note that 曲(ま)がる takes both, in different slots: 角(かど)<b>を</b>右(みぎ)<b>へ</b>曲(ま)がる - you turn *at* the corner, *toward* the right.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ pronouncing 学校(がっこう)へ as "gakkō **he**" → ✅ "gakkō **e**"
  The particle へ is read え. Learners who first meet it in writing often carry the
  wrong pronunciation for years.

- ❌ 七時(しちじ)<b>へ</b>駅(えき)へ来(き)てください。 → ✅ 七時(しちじ)<b>に</b>駅(えき)へ来(き)てください。
  `へ` cannot mark a time. Only the destination slot is available to it.

- ❌ 教室(きょうしつ)<b>へ</b>先生(せんせい)がいます。 → ✅ 教室(きょうしつ)<b>に</b>先生(せんせい)がいます。
  Existence with ある／いる is always `に`. `へ` needs movement.

- ❌ 東京(とうきょう)<b>にの</b>荷物(にもつ) → ✅ 東京(とうきょう)<b>への</b>荷物(にもつ)
  Only `へ` combines with `の` to modify a following noun.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)<b>へ</b>聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)<b>に</b>聞(き)きました。
  A person you question is a recipient, not a direction. `へ` marks a person only when
  the thing sent is physical - a letter, a parcel, a present.

## Practice

**1.** 海外(かいがい)____の荷物(にもつ)は三階(さんがい)で受(う)け付(つ)けています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

海外(かいがい)<b>へ</b>の荷物(にもつ)は三階(さんがい)で受(う)け付(つ)けています。
"Parcels going overseas are handled on the third floor."

Only `へ` can take `の` and modify a noun. ❌<code>海外(かいがい)にの</code> is impossible,
so this blank has exactly one answer.

</details>

**2.** 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)（へ・に）花(はな)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

机(つくえ)の上(うえ)<b>に</b>花(はな)があります。

The verb is ある - nothing is moving, so there is no direction for `へ` to point in.
Location of existence is `に` without exception.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I want to go to Hokkaido next year."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来年(らいねん)、北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>へ</b>行(い)きたいです。

A plain destination with 行(い)く, so `へ` and `に` are both correct here; `へ` suits the
"setting off somewhere" feel of a trip. 来年(らいねん) takes no particle at all.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わってから、部長(ぶちょう)へ報告(ほうこく)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わってから、部長(ぶちょう)<b>に</b>報告(ほうこく)します。
"I'll report to the manager after the meeting."

Reporting, telling, asking and teaching all mark the person with `に`. `へ` would only
work if something physical were being sent to him.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 南(みなみ)<b>へ</b>向(む)かっています。
(b) 南(みなみ)の島(しま)<b>に</b>着(つ)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a bearing. 向(む)かう is open-ended motion, and `へ` gives the direction
without committing to a destination - you are somewhere on the way, pointing south.

(b) reports an arrival. 着(つ)く is entirely about the endpoint, which is a point, so
`に` is required; ❌<code>島(しま)へ着(つ)きました</code> sounds wrong to a native ear.

</details>

# で - location of an action

**Meaning** · at, in - the place where something is done

`で` marks the **stage the action is played out on**. Not where a thing sits, not where
a movement ends: where the doing happens. If you can put "at" or "in" in front of an
English verb phrase and it still describes an activity, Japanese almost certainly wants
`で`.

## Formation

`で` is a particle and attaches straight onto a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - place of an action | Place + で + action verb | 図書館(としょかん) → 図書館(としょかん)で勉強(べんきょう)する |
| Noun - organisation or institution | Org + で + verb | 会社(かいしゃ) → 会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)く |
| Noun - event, as its venue | Event は + Place + である | パーティー → パーティーはホールである |
| Noun - the field a statement ranges over | Scope + で（いちばん…） | 日本(にほん) → 日本(にほん)でいちばん高(たか)い |
| Question word | どこ + で | どこ → どこで会(あ)いますか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - one apparent exception that is really the rule. An **event** is something
that *takes place*, so it uses `で` even with ある:
試験(しけん)は大学(だいがく)であります。 Compare a *thing*, which uses `に`:
大学(だいがく)に図書館(としょかん)があります。 The particle tells you whether ある means
"is held" or "is located".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
図書館(としょかん)<b>で</b>勉強(べんきょう)します。
I study at the library.
</pre>

<pre>
どこ<b>で</b>買(か)いましたか。
Where did you buy it?
</pre>

<pre>
公園(こうえん)<b>で</b>サッカーをしました。
We played soccer in the park.
</pre>

<pre>
台所(だいどころ)<b>で</b>母(はは)がご飯(はん)を作(つく)っています。
My mother is making dinner in the kitchen.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)<b>で</b>いちばん高(たか)い山(やま)は富士山(ふじさん)です。
The highest mountain in Japan is Mt Fuji.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)のパーティーはこのホール<b>で</b>あります。
Next week's party is being held in this hall.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の近(ちか)くのカフェ<b>で</b>昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べることが多(おお)いです。
I usually have lunch at a café near the office.
</pre>

<pre>
先週(せんしゅう)の日曜日(にちようび)、駅前(えきまえ)の映画館(えいがかん)<b>で</b>話題(わだい)の映画(えいが)を見(み)ました。
Last Sunday I saw that much-talked-about film at the cinema by the station.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`で` is register-neutral. It behaves identically in casual speech, in ます-form and in
writing, and it is never dropped the way a destination `に` can be - the sentence would
become ambiguous, so speakers keep it.

Two habits of real speech are worth noting. First, `で` is very often followed by `は`
for contrast: 家(いえ)<b>では</b>静(しず)かです - "at home (as opposed to elsewhere) he's
quiet." Second, in a topic-first sentence the place can be lifted to the front with
`では`: 日本(にほん)では靴(くつ)を脱(ぬ)ぎます。 That `では` is this same `で`, just
wearing a topic marker.

The **scope** use - <code>世界(せかい)でいちばん</code>, <code>クラスで一番(いちばん)</code>
 - feels like a different word to learners, but it is the same idea stretched: the
comparison is *carried out* within that field. It appears constantly in N5 superlative
sentences, so learn it as part of this particle rather than as a separate item.

Finally, note that `で` is homophonous with the て-form of です／だ and with the て-form of
verbs like 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んで. Context separates them instantly, but a beginner
reading aloud can be thrown. If a noun of place sits immediately before it, it is this
particle.

## Contrast

### で vs に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` is where something **happens**; `に` is where something
**is**.

</div>

Ask what the verb is doing. An activity - 食(た)べる, 働(はたら)く, 遊(あそ)ぶ,
会(あ)う, 買(か)う, 泳(およ)ぐ - takes `で`. A verb of existence or position - 
ある, いる, 住(す)む, 座(すわ)る, 置(お)く - takes `に`.

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)のカフェ<b>で</b>友達(ともだち)を待(ま)っています。
I'm waiting for my friend at the café by the station.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)<b>に</b>新(あたら)しいカフェができました。
A new café has opened up by the station.
</pre>

Waiting is an activity; the café is its stage, so `で`. Coming into existence is not an
activity in this sense - the café now *is* there - so `に`.

The event/thing split under ある is where this rule earns its keep:

<pre>
三階(さんがい)<b>に</b>会議室(かいぎしつ)があります。
There's a meeting room on the third floor.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三階(さんがい)<b>で</b>あります。
The meeting is on the third floor.
</pre>

Same building, same verb, opposite particles - because a room *is located* and a meeting
*is held*.

### で vs を

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` is the place an action is set in; `を` is the space the action
moves **across**.

</div>

A handful of motion verbs - 歩(ある)く, 走(はし)る, 飛(と)ぶ, 渡(わた)る,
通(とお)る, 散歩(さんぽ)する - can take the place as `を`, and the meaning shifts.

<pre>
公園(こうえん)<b>で</b>走(はし)っています。
He's out running in the park.
</pre>

<pre>
公園(こうえん)<b>を</b>走(はし)っています。
He's running through the park.
</pre>

The first frames the park as the venue for a jog. The second traces a path across it - 
he entered one side and is heading for the other. With any verb that is not motion, `を`
is simply unavailable for a place.

### で as place vs で as means

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask what kind of noun it is. A place gives you "at"; a tool,
vehicle, language or material gives you "by / with".

</div>

The two `で` are the same particle historically, and Japanese speakers do not experience
them as ambiguous, because the noun settles it instantly.

<pre>
この部屋(へや)<b>で</b>作(つく)りました。
I made it in this room.
</pre>

<pre>
この機械(きかい)<b>で</b>作(つく)りました。
I made it with this machine.
</pre>

Identical grammar, and nobody is confused: a room is somewhere you can stand, a machine
is something you can use. When both appear at once they simply stack, place first:
台所(だいどころ)でナイフ<b>で</b>切(き)る is grammatical though clumsy - a native speaker
would rephrase.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)<b>で</b>ビールがあります。 → ✅ 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)<b>に</b>ビールがあります。
  ある reporting where a **thing** sits is `に`. Only an *event* takes であります.

- ❌ 学校(がっこう)<b>に</b>勉強(べんきょう)します。 → ✅ 学校(がっこう)<b>で</b>勉強(べんきょう)します。
  Studying is an activity, and activities are staged with `で`. `に` here would try to
  make the school a destination or a location of existence.

- ❌ 日本(にほん)<b>に</b>いちばん大(おお)きい町(まち) → ✅ 日本(にほん)<b>で</b>いちばん大(おお)きい町(まち)
  Superlatives set a field of comparison, and that field is marked `で`.

- ❌ コンサートはこのホール<b>に</b>あります。 → ✅ コンサートはこのホール<b>で</b>あります。
  A concert is held, not located. This is the one place ある takes `で`.

- ❌ この川(かわ)<b>を</b>泳(およ)いではいけません。 → ✅ この川(かわ)<b>で</b>泳(およ)いではいけません。
  泳(およ)ぐ in the sense "go swimming" treats the river as a venue. `を` would mean
  swimming *across* it, which is not what a warning sign is saying.

## Practice

**1.** きのう、海(うみ)____泳(およ)ぎました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

きのう、海(うみ)<b>で</b>泳(およ)ぎました。

Swimming is an activity and the sea is the place it was carried out in, so `で`. `に`
would need a verb of existence or arrival.

</details>

**2.** この町(まち)（に・で）大(おお)きい病院(びょういん)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この町(まち)<b>に</b>大(おお)きい病院(びょういん)があります。

A hospital is a thing, and things exist at a `に`. If the sentence were about an event
held there - 説明会(せつめいかい)はこの病院(びょういん)であります - it would flip to `で`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Let's talk about it at tomorrow's meeting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)<b>で</b>話(はな)しましょう。

A meeting is an occasion, and an occasion works exactly like a place: it is the setting
the talking happens in. Note 明日(あした) takes no particle of its own.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 教室(きょうしつ)に昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べてもいいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 教室(きょうしつ)<b>で</b>昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べてもいいですか。
"Is it all right to eat lunch in the classroom?"

Eating is an action, so the classroom is its stage: `で`. The `に` version would need
something to exist or arrive there.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 銀行(ぎんこう)の前(まえ)<b>に</b>人(ひと)がたくさんいます。
(b) 銀行(ぎんこう)の前(まえ)<b>で</b>人(ひと)がたくさん待(ま)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a photograph. いる simply reports that a lot of people occupy the space in front
of the bank; the sentence says nothing about what they are doing.

(b) is a scene. 待(ま)つ is an activity, so the space in front of the bank becomes the
stage it is played out on, and `で` is required. Swapping the particles in either
sentence produces something a native speaker would reject.

</details>

# で - means, instrument, cause

**Meaning** · with, by, in, out of - the tool, vehicle, language, material or cause that
makes the action possible

`で` names **what you used**. A knife, a train, English, wood, a hundred yen, ten
minutes, a typhoon - anything the action was carried out *by means of*, or *because of*.
It is the same particle as the one that marks the place of an action, and it works the
same way: it supplies the circumstance the verb depends on.

## Formation

`で` attaches directly to a noun. Nothing changes shape, and it never follows a verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - tool or instrument | Tool + で + verb | はし → はしで食(た)べる |
| Noun - vehicle | Vehicle + で + motion verb | 電車(でんしゃ) → 電車(でんしゃ)で行(い)く |
| Noun - language or medium | Language + で + verb | 英語(えいご) → 英語(えいご)で話(はな)す |
| Noun - material you can still see | Material + で + 作(つく)る | 木(き) → 木(き)で作(つく)る |
| Noun - cause or reason | Cause + で + verb | 風邪(かぜ) → 風邪(かぜ)で休(やす)む |
| Noun - amount, price or elapsed time | Quantity + で | 千円(せんえん) → 千円(せんえん)で買(か)える |
| Number of people | Number + で | 一人(ひとり) → 一人(ひとり)で行(い)く |

<div class="note">

**Note** - "on foot" breaks the pattern. 歩(ある)く is a verb, not a tool, so you say
歩(ある)いて行(い)きます, never ❌<code>歩(ある)いてで</code> and never
❌<code>足(あし)で行(い)きます</code>. Learn <code>歩(ある)いて</code> as the one member
of the transport list that is not a noun + で.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
はし<b>で</b>食(た)べます。
I eat with chopsticks.
</pre>

<pre>
バス<b>で</b>会社(かいしゃ)へ行(い)きます。
I go to work by bus.
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)は鉛筆(えんぴつ)<b>で</b>書(か)いてください。
Please write this kanji in pencil.
</pre>

<pre>
英語(えいご)<b>で</b>説明(せつめい)してもらえますか。
Could you explain it in English?
</pre>

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)<b>で</b>学校(がっこう)を休(やす)みました。
I stayed off school with a cold.
</pre>

<pre>
この椅子(いす)は竹(たけ)<b>で</b>できています。
This chair is made of bamboo.
</pre>

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)<b>で</b>新幹線(しんかんせん)が止(と)まってしまいました。
The bullet trains have stopped because of the heavy snow.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)はメール<b>で</b>送(おく)りますので、後(あと)で確認(かくにん)してください。
I'll send the meeting materials by email, so please check them afterwards.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`で` is register-neutral and is not dropped in casual speech - unlike a destination
particle, it carries information nothing else supplies. はし食(た)べる is not casual
Japanese, it is broken Japanese.

A few things worth knowing about how it behaves in real sentences:

- <b>一人(ひとり)で, 二人(ふたり)で, みんなで.</b> This is the standard way to say "alone",
  "the two of us", "all together". It is very frequent and learners reach for `と`
  instead, which does not work with a number.
- **Cause `で` is neutral and slightly detached.** 事故(じこ)で遅(おく)れました states
  the cause flatly and is perfect for an apology at work, because it does not argue.
  Compare the reason particle `から`, which sounds like you are making a case.
- **Amounts.** 三日(みっか)で終(お)わります - "it'll be done in three days" - and
  五百円(ごひゃくえん)で買(か)いました - "I got it for five hundred yen." Here `で`
  marks the quantity the action was completed *within* or *for*.
- **`で` is often followed by contrastive `は`.** 電車(でんしゃ)<b>では</b>行(い)けません
 - "you can't get there by train (whatever else you might manage)."

Because the tool `で` and the place `で` look identical, Japanese avoids stacking two of
them in one clause. If you need both, most speakers restructure the sentence rather than
write two `で` in a row.

## Contrast

### で vs に with vehicles

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>乗(の)る</code> takes `に`; every other verb takes `で`.

</div>

This is the highest-frequency `で` error at N5, because English uses "on" for both.

<pre>
東京駅(とうきょうえき)で新幹線(しんかんせん)<b>に</b>乗(の)ります。
I'll board the shinkansen at Tokyo Station.
</pre>

<pre>
新幹線(しんかんせん)<b>で</b>大阪(おおさか)まで行(い)きます。
I'll go to Osaka by shinkansen.
</pre>

<code>乗(の)る</code> is "get on", a movement that lands on a point, and points are `に`.
行(い)く, 来(く)る, 帰(かえ)る, 通(かよ)う all describe travel *using* the vehicle,
so they take `で`. The opposite of <code>乗(の)る</code>, <code>降(お)りる</code>, takes `を`.

### で vs から for material

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can still see what it was made of, use `で`. If the material
was chemically transformed and has disappeared, use `から`.

</div>

<pre>
このかばんは紙(かみ)<b>で</b>作(つく)りました。
I made this bag out of paper.
</pre>

<pre>
ワインはぶどう<b>から</b>作(つく)ります。
Wine is made from grapes.
</pre>

The bag is still visibly paper. The wine is not visibly grapes - the raw material went
in and something else came out. The same split gives 木(き)で作(つく)った机(つくえ)
(you can see the wood) but 米(こめ)から作(つく)る日本酒(にほんしゅ) (you cannot see the
rice). In practice `で` is far more common; `から` is reserved for genuine
transformations, and native speakers will not object to `で` in borderline cases.

### で as means vs で as place

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun decides. Somewhere you could stand is a place; something
you could pick up, speak, or blame is a means.

</div>

Japanese does not experience these as two words, and neither should you after a little
practice - the noun disambiguates before the verb arrives.

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)<b>で</b>来(き)ました。
I came by bicycle.
</pre>

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)置(お)き場(ば)<b>で</b>待(ま)っています。
I'm waiting at the bike park.
</pre>

The tricky residue is nouns that could be either. 電話(でんわ) is a means
(電話(でんわ)で話(はな)す, "talk on the phone"); 会社(かいしゃ) is a place. When a
noun is genuinely ambiguous, Japanese relies on the verb: 新聞(しんぶん)で知(し)る is
"find out from the newspaper" (means), because 知(し)る is not something you do *at* a
newspaper.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 電車(でんしゃ)<b>で</b>乗(の)ります。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)<b>に</b>乗(の)ります。
  <code>乗(の)る</code> is the one transport verb that takes `に`. Everything else in the
  travel vocabulary takes `で`.

- ❌ 歩(ある)いて<b>で</b>帰(かえ)りました。 → ✅ 歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)りました。
  `で` attaches to nouns only. The て-form already carries the "by doing" sense.

- ❌ この本(ほん)は英語(えいご)<b>を</b>書(か)いてあります。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は英語(えいご)<b>で</b>書(か)いてあります。
  A language used as a medium is a means. `を` would try to make English the thing that
  was written.

- ❌ 木(き)<b>から</b>作(つく)った机(つくえ) → ✅ 木(き)<b>で</b>作(つく)った机(つくえ)
  The wood is still visible in the desk, so it is `で`. Save `から` for materials that
  have been transformed out of sight.

- ❌ 友達(ともだち)と三人(さんにん)<b>と</b>行(い)きました。 → ✅ 友達(ともだち)と三人(さんにん)<b>で</b>行(い)きました。
  A number of people is a circumstance, not a companion. Counted groups take `で`.

## Practice

**1.** この手紙(てがみ)はボールペン____書(か)いてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この手紙(てがみ)はボールペン<b>で</b>書(か)いてください。

A pen is the instrument the writing is done with, which is the core use of this
particle. `に` would make no sense - you are not writing *onto* the pen.

</details>

**2.** 東京駅(とうきょうえき)で新幹線(しんかんせん)（で・に）乗(の)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

東京駅(とうきょうえき)で新幹線(しんかんせん)<b>に</b>乗(の)ります。

<code>乗(の)る</code> means "get on", and the vehicle is the point you land on, so it
takes `に`. The first `で` in the sentence is the place the boarding happens - both
particles are doing their own job.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This bridge is made of stone."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この橋(はし)は石(いし)<b>で</b>できています。

You can see the stone in the finished bridge, so the material takes `で`, not `から`.
できている is the standard way to say "is made of"; 作(つく)られています is possible but
sounds like a report.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 地震(じしん)に電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 地震(じしん)<b>で</b>電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まりました。
"The trains stopped because of the earthquake."

An external cause is marked `で`. `に` cannot mark a cause; it would be trying to make
the earthquake a destination or a recipient.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一人(ひとり)<b>で</b>旅行(りょこう)しました。
(b) 友達(ともだち)と旅行(りょこう)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the circumstance the trip was made under: alone. `で` here counts the party,
which is why ❌<code>一人(ひとり)と</code> is impossible - there is no companion to name.

(b) names a companion, which is the job of `と`. The two particles are not competing for
one slot: you can say 友達(ともだち)と二人(ふたり)で旅行(りょこう)しました and use both
at once, "I travelled with a friend, just the two of us."

</details>

# から - because, since

**Meaning** · because - gives the reason for what is said in the other half of the
sentence

`から` follows a **whole clause** and turns it into a reason. The reason comes first, the
consequence second: 「reason + から、result」. It is the reason particle a speaker uses
when they are willing to stand behind the reason - which is why it sits so naturally in
front of a suggestion, an invitation or a command.

## Formation

`から` attaches to a finished clause. Whatever ends that clause has to be a complete
predicate, so nouns and な-adjectives need `だ` or `です` first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb, plain | plain form + から | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るから |
| Verb, polite | ます-form + から | 降(ふ)ります → 降(ふ)りますから |
| い-adjective | plain form + から | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いから |
| い-adjective, polite | ～いです + から | 寒(さむ)いです → 寒(さむ)いですから |
| な-adjective | ～だ + から | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かだから |
| な-adjective, polite | ～です + から | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かですから |
| Noun | ～だ + から | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みだから |
| Noun, polite | ～です + から | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みですから |
| Past | past form + から | 遅(おく)れた → 遅(おく)れたから |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the politeness of the clause before `から` is free. Both
寒(さむ)いから閉(し)めます and 寒(さむ)いですから閉(し)めます are correct; the plain
form before `から` is more common even in polite speech, and the ですから version sounds a
little more careful. What matters for politeness is the **end** of the sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
寒(さむ)い<b>から</b>、窓(まど)を閉(し)めます。
It's cold, so I'll shut the window.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あぶ)ない<b>から</b>、触(さわ)らないでください。
It's dangerous, so please don't touch it.
</pre>

<pre>
頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い<b>から</b>、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)ります。
My head hurts, so I'm going home early today.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>から</b>、よく来(き)ます。
This shop is cheap, so I come here a lot.
</pre>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し待(ま)ってください。すぐ終(お)わります<b>から</b>。
Please wait a little longer - I'll be finished in a moment.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうです<b>から</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)っていきましょう。
It looks like rain, so let's take an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は道(みち)が込(こ)む<b>から</b>、朝(あさ)の早(はや)いうちに出発(しゅっぱつ)したほうがいいですよ。
The roads get busy on Sundays, so you'd do well to set off early in the morning.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は給料日(きゅうりょうび)だ<b>から</b>、みんなで飲(の)みに行(い)こうよ。
It's payday, so let's all go out for a drink.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`から` is neutral in politeness by itself - it appears in the most casual speech and in
formal writing - but it is **not** neutral in attitude. `から` presents the reason as
*yours*: something you assert, and are prepared to defend. That has three practical
consequences.

**It is the natural reason particle in front of a push.** Invitations, suggestions,
requests and commands almost always take `から` rather than `ので`:
時間(じかん)がないから急(いそ)いで。 The reason exists to justify what you are asking
for.

**It can sound blunt in an apology.** If you are explaining to a customer or a boss why
something went wrong, `から` can read as defensive - "because X, that's why." `ので` is
softer and is the safe default there.

**It stands alone at the end of a sentence.** ～から。 with nothing after it is extremely
common in speech and leaves the consequence implied:
大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)、私(わたし)がやるから。 - "It's fine, I'll do it." This has a warm,
reassuring feel, and is worth copying.

Two more forms you will meet immediately. **～からです** answers a どうして question as a
complete sentence: 「どうして休(やす)んだんですか。」「熱(ねつ)があったからです。」 And
**だから** at the head of a sentence is this same から promoted to a conjunction:
明日(あした)は試験(しけん)だ。だから、今夜(こんや)は勉強(べんきょう)する。 In polite
speech that becomes ですから.

## Contrast

### から vs ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` is *my* reason and pushes; `ので` is *the* reason and
explains. When in doubt with a superior, use `ので`.

</div>

Both mean "because". The difference is stance. `から` presents a judgement; `ので`
presents a circumstance and lets the listener draw their own conclusion, which makes it
sound more deferential.

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みだ<b>から</b>、ゆっくりしよう。
Tomorrow's a day off, so let's take it easy.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れました<b>ので</b>、少(すこ)し遅刻(ちこく)します。
My train was delayed, so I'll be a little late.
</pre>

There is a hard grammatical difference too, and it is the one exams test. After a noun
or a な-adjective, `から` takes **だ** and `ので` takes **な**:

- 病気(びょうき)<b>だ</b>から ／ 病気(びょうき)<b>な</b>ので
- 静(しず)か<b>だ</b>から ／ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>ので

Finally, a command, a request or a volitional in the second half strongly prefers `から`.
危(あぶ)ないから下(さ)がって! is natural; the `ので` version sounds oddly formal for a
shout.

### から vs the causal て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て-form can give a cause, but only when the result is something
that *happened to you*, never something you decide.

</div>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)が忙(いそが)しく<b>て</b>、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れました。
Work was hectic, and so my reply was late.
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)が忙(いそが)しい<b>から</b>、今日(きょう)は断(ことわ)ります。
Work is hectic, so I'm going to turn it down today.
</pre>

The first result is involuntary - the lateness simply followed. The second is a
decision, and a decision needs `から`. This is why
❌<code>忙(いそが)しくて、断(ことわ)りましょう</code> is wrong: you cannot hang a volitional,
a request or a command off a causal て-form.

### から as reason vs から as "from"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what is in front of it. A **clause** before `から` means
"because"; a bare **noun** before `から` means "from".

</div>

The two are the same word historically - "starting from this fact" - and Japanese
speakers never confuse them, because the test is mechanical.

<pre>
日本(にほん)<b>から</b>来(き)ました。
I came from Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)が好(す)きだ<b>から</b>来(き)ました。
I came because I love Japan.
</pre>

<code>日本(にほん)</code> alone is a noun, so the first is a starting point. Add a
predicate and the same から flips to a reason. The one place beginners genuinely trip is
answering どうして with a bare noun - see the mistakes below.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)から、安(やす)いです。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>だ</b>から、安(やす)いです。
  A noun cannot sit directly in front of reason `から`; it needs だ or です. Without it
  the sentence reads "from a student".

- ❌ 便利(べんり)<b>な</b>から、よく使(つか)います。 → ✅ 便利(べんり)<b>だ</b>から、よく使(つか)います。
  な belongs to `ので`. `から` takes だ.

- ❌ 「どうして休(やす)んだの?」「風邪(かぜ)から。」 → ✅ 「風邪(かぜ)<b>だ</b>から。」
  The bare noun version says "from a cold" and sounds like a starting point. The same
  fix in polite speech: 風邪(かぜ)だからです／風邪(かぜ)でしたから.

- ❌ (to a client) 担当(たんとう)者(しゃ)が休(やす)んでいますから、明日(あした)ご連絡(れんらく)します。 → ✅ 担当(たんとう)者(しゃ)が休(やす)んでおりますので、明日(あした)ご連絡(れんらく)します。
  Grammatically fine, socially blunt. In an apology or an explanation upward, `ので`
  removes the note of "and that's your answer".

- ❌ 寒(さむ)くて、窓(まど)を閉(し)めてください。 → ✅ 寒(さむ)いから、窓(まど)を閉(し)めてください。
  A request cannot follow a causal て-form. Use `から` whenever the second half is
  something you want the listener to do.

## Practice

**1.** 今日(きょう)は日曜日(にちようび)____、銀行(ぎんこう)は休(やす)みです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は日曜日(にちようび)<b>だから</b>、銀行(ぎんこう)は休(やす)みです。

A noun in front of reason `から` must be propped up with だ (or です). Leaving it out
would turn the phrase into "from Sunday", which is a starting point, not a reason.

</details>

**2.** すみません、用事(ようじ)がある（から・ので）、お先(さき)に失礼(しつれい)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません、用事(ようじ)がある<b>ので</b>、お先(さき)に失礼(しつれい)します。

You are excusing yourself to people who are still working, so you want the deferential
particle. `から` would be understood, but it presents your errand as a justification and
sounds a touch self-assured.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's hot, so let's turn the air conditioner on."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

暑(あつ)い<b>から</b>、エアコンをつけましょう。

The second half is a volitional - a proposal - and proposals take `から`, not a causal
て-form. ❌<code>暑(あつ)くて、エアコンをつけましょう</code> is ungrammatical.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 静(しず)かなから、この部屋(へや)が好(す)きです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 静(しず)か<b>だ</b>から、この部屋(へや)が好(す)きです。
"I like this room because it's quiet."

な-adjectives take だ before `から`. The な form belongs to `ので`:
静(しず)かなので would also be correct here.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>から</b>、中止(ちゅうし)にしよう。
(b) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っています<b>ので</b>、中止(ちゅうし)といたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a speaker making a call. `から` puts the reason forward as the speaker's own
judgement, and the volitional 中止(ちゅうし)にしよう follows naturally from it - this is
what you say to teammates.

(b) is an announcement. `ので` presents the rain as a plain circumstance rather than an
argument, and the humble いたします completes the picture: the decision is being reported
to people it affects, not debated with them.

</details>

# から - from

**Meaning** · from - the starting point of a distance, a stretch of time, or a flow of
something

`から` marks **where something begins**. A place a journey starts from, a clock time an
event starts at, the person a letter came from, the raw material a product started life
as. Its natural partner is `まで`, which marks the other end.

## Formation

`から` attaches directly to a noun, and it is the one place particle that also attaches
to a て-form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - place of origin | Place + から | 駅(えき) → 駅(えき)から歩(ある)く |
| Noun - starting time | Time + から | 九時(くじ) → 九時(くじ)から始(はじ)まる |
| Noun - person or source | Source + から + もらう／借(か)りる／聞(き)く | 先生(せんせい) → 先生(せんせい)から借(か)りる |
| Noun - raw material | Material + から + 作(つく)る | 米(こめ) → 米(こめ)から作(つく)る |
| Verb て-form - "after doing" | て-form + から | 食(た)べて → 食(た)べてから |
| Paired with まで | A から B まで | 東京(とうきょう) → 東京(とうきょう)から大阪(おおさか)まで |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>～てから</code> is a fixed pattern meaning "after doing X". The verb must
be in the て-form, and the tense of the whole sentence is carried by the **final** verb
only: 帰(かえ)ってから電話(でんわ)しました. Never conjugate the て-form for past.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
九時(くじ)<b>から</b>始(はじ)まります。
It starts at nine.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)<b>から</b>歩(ある)いて五分(ごふん)です。
It's a five-minute walk from the station.
</pre>

<pre>
友達(ともだち)<b>から</b>メールが来(き)ました。
I got an email from a friend.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は先生(せんせい)<b>から</b>借(か)りました。
I borrowed this dictionary from my teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
手(て)を洗(あら)って<b>から</b>ご飯(はん)を食(た)べなさい。
Wash your hands before you eat.
</pre>

<pre>
日本酒(にほんしゅ)は米(こめ)<b>から</b>作(つく)ります。
Sake is made from rice.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は二時(にじ)<b>から</b>ですが、少(すこ)し早(はや)めに会場(かいじょう)に入(はい)ってください。
The meeting is from two, but please come into the hall a little early.
</pre>

<pre>
北(きた)の窓(まど)<b>から</b>冷(つめ)たい風(かぜ)が入(はい)ってくるので、カーテンを閉(し)めました。
A cold draught was coming in through the north window, so I closed the curtain.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`から` is register-neutral and is never dropped - it carries information no other part of
the sentence supplies.

A few behaviours worth having ready:

- **から alone can be the predicate.** 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)からです。 - 
  "The meeting is from three." No verb is needed, and the question form works the same
  way: 何時(なんじ)からですか。
- **からの modifies a noun**, just as `への` does. 田中(たなか)さんからの手紙(てがみ) - 
  "a letter from Tanaka." This is one of the very few particles that takes `の` directly.
- **Passage through, not just origin.** 窓(まど)から入(はい)る,
  裏口(うらぐち)から出(で)る. `から` marks the opening something passes through, and
  English usually picks "through" rather than "from" here.
- **Range expressions.** 一時(いちじ)から三時(さんじ)まで, 東京(とうきょう)から
  大阪(おおさか)まで. The pair is so tightly bound that hearing `から` makes a Japanese
  listener expect `まで`.

`から` also has an entirely separate life as the reason particle meaning "because". The
test is what precedes it: a bare noun gives you "from", a finished clause gives you
"because". There is no ambiguity in practice.

## Contrast

### から vs まで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` is the near end, `まで` is the far end. They are a pair, and
either can appear alone.

</div>

<pre>
銀行(ぎんこう)は九時(くじ)<b>から</b>三時(さんじ)まで開(あ)いています。
The bank is open from nine until three.
</pre>

Use `から` alone when only the start matters (来月(らいげつ)から働(はたら)きます), and
`まで` alone when only the end does. What you cannot do is use one where the other
belongs:

<pre>
家(いえ)<b>から</b>駅(えき)まで自転車(じてんしゃ)で十五分(じゅうごふん)かかります。
It takes fifteen minutes from home to the station by bike.
</pre>

Note that `まで`, not `へ`, is what pairs with `から`. A `から～へ` sentence is not idiomatic
Japanese.

### から vs に for receiving

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with もらう, 借(か)りる, 聞(き)く and 習(なら)う, both work for a
person, but only `から` works for an organisation - and neither works with あげる, which
is always `に`.

</div>

<pre>
姉(あね)<b>から</b>まんがを借(か)りました。
I borrowed some manga from my sister.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)<b>に</b>まんがを貸(か)しました。
I lent some manga to my sister.
</pre>

When the verb is one of *receiving*, `に` and `から` are both possible for a person, with
`から` making the source a little more explicit and `に` a little more personal. As soon
as the source is a company, a school or a shop, `から` is the only natural choice:
会社(かいしゃ)から連絡(れんらく)がありました. When the verb is one of *giving* - 
あげる, 貸(か)す, 送(おく)る, 教(おし)える - the other party is a recipient and takes
`に` only.

### ～てから vs ～たあとで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～てから</code> says the first action is a **precondition** for
the second. <code>～たあとで</code> just puts them in order.

</div>

<pre>
くすりを飲(の)んで<b>から</b>寝(ね)ます。
I take my medicine and then go to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)を見(み)たあとで、ご飯(はん)を食(た)べました。
After we saw the film, we had dinner.
</pre>

The medicine has to come first for the sentence to make sense - that is `てから`. The
film and the dinner are simply two events in sequence, which is `あとで`. Because `てから`
implies dependency, it is what instructions and rules use:
手(て)を洗(あら)ってから, 確認(かくにん)してから. Also note `てから` cannot take past
tense on the first verb, while `あとで` requires it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)<b>から</b>プレゼントをあげました。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)<b>に</b>プレゼントをあげました。
  あげる is giving, so the other party is a recipient. Only *receiving* verbs allow
  `から`.

- ❌ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べる<b>から</b>、くすりを飲(の)みます。 → ✅ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べて<b>から</b>、くすりを飲(の)みます。
  The wrong version is grammatical but means "because I'm going to eat". "After eating"
  needs the て-form.

- ❌ 九時(くじ)から<b>に</b>始(はじ)まります。 → ✅ 九時(くじ)から始(はじ)まります。
  `から` already marks the time; stacking `に` on top of it is not possible. Choose one:
  九時(くじ)に始(はじ)まります means "starts at nine" with no sense of a span.

- ❌ 「どうして遅(おく)れたの?」「電車(でんしゃ)から。」 → ✅ 「電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れた<b>から</b>。」
  A bare noun before `から` gives the "from" reading - "from the train". A reason needs a
  full clause in front.

- ❌ 三時(さんじ)から五時(ごじ)<b>へ</b>勉強(べんきょう)します。 → ✅ 三時(さんじ)から五時(ごじ)<b>まで</b>勉強(べんきょう)します。
  The far end of a span is `まで`. `から` never pairs with `へ`.

## Practice

**1.** 銀行(ぎんこう)は九時(くじ)____三時(さんじ)までです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

銀行(ぎんこう)は九時(くじ)<b>から</b>三時(さんじ)までです。

`まで` at the far end makes `から` almost obligatory at the near end - the two form a
fixed pair. Note that the sentence ends in です - a `から～まで` span needs no verb.

</details>

**2.** この本(ほん)は図書館(としょかん)（から・に）借(か)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この本(ほん)は図書館(としょかん)<b>から</b>借(か)りました。

With a person, both particles are possible. With an institution - a library, a company,
a school - only `から` is natural, because there is no individual to hand it to you.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll call you after the meeting ends."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わって<b>から</b>電話(でんわ)します。

<code>～てから</code> because the call depends on the meeting being over. The て-form stays
uninflected; the tense of the sentence sits on 電話(でんわ)します at the end.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けるから、部屋(へや)が涼(すず)しくなりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けて<b>から</b>、部屋(へや)が涼(すず)しくなりました。
"The room got cooler after I opened the window."

With the dictionary form, `から` reads as "because I'm going to open the window", which
clashes with the past tense at the end. The て-form gives the intended "after".

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 田中(たなか)さん<b>に</b>聞(き)きました。
(b) 田中(たなか)さん<b>から</b>聞(き)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) most naturally means "I asked Tanaka" - with `に`, Tanaka is the person you directed
the question at, and the information may or may not have arrived.

(b) means "I heard it from Tanaka". `から` marks him as the source the information flowed
out of, so the sentence is about what you now know, not about the asking. Both are
possible with 聞(き)く, which is exactly why the particle carries the whole difference.

</details>

# まで

**Meaning** · until, up to, as far as - and, in one special use, "even"

`まで` marks the **far end**. Something continues, and `まで` says where it stops: an hour
on the clock, a station, the moment a condition changes. Whatever it attaches to is
*included* - 五時(ごじ)まで covers five o'clock itself. Its partner is `から`, which
marks the near end.

## Formation

`まで` attaches directly to a noun or to a plain non-past verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - place | Place + まで | 駅(えき) → 駅(えき)まで歩(ある)く |
| Noun - time | Time + まで | 五時(ごじ) → 五時(ごじ)まで働(はたら)く |
| Verb - plain non-past | dictionary form + まで | 終(お)わる → 終(お)わるまで待(ま)つ |
| Paired with から | A から B まで | 月曜日(げつようび) → 月曜日(げつようび)から金曜日(きんようび)まで |
| Deadline | Noun／Verb + までに | 金曜日(きんようび) → 金曜日(きんようび)までに出(だ)す |
| Noun - "even" | Noun + まで | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもまで知(し)っている |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb before `まで` is always **plain and non-past**, no matter how the
sentence ends. 終(お)わるまで待(ま)ちました is correct;
❌<code>終(お)わったまで</code> and ❌<code>終(お)わりますまで</code> are not. The final verb
carries all the tense and all the politeness.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)<b>まで</b>歩(ある)きます。
I'll walk as far as the station.
</pre>

<pre>
五時(ごじ)<b>まで</b>働(はたら)きます。
I work until five.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)がやむ<b>まで</b>ここで待(ま)ちましょう。
Let's wait here until the rain stops.
</pre>

<pre>
この美術館(びじゅつかん)は月曜日(げつようび)<b>まで</b>休(やす)みです。
This museum is closed until Monday.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)から大阪(おおさか)<b>まで</b>新幹線(しんかんせん)で行(い)きました。
I took the shinkansen from Tokyo to Osaka.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなことは子(こ)ども<b>まで</b>知(し)っていますよ。
Even children know that.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)が終(お)わる<b>まで</b>だれも席(せき)を立(た)たなかった。
Nobody left their seat until the match was over.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は仕事(しごと)が終(お)わらなくて、夜中(よなか)の二時(にじ)<b>まで</b>会社(かいしゃ)に残(のこ)っていました。
I couldn't get finished yesterday and stayed at the office until two in the morning.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`まで` is register-neutral. It is identical in plain and polite speech and in writing,
and it is never dropped.

Three things about how it feels in use:

- **It is inclusive.** 三日(みっか)まで means the third is still covered. If you need
  "before, not including", Japanese uses 前(まえ)に rather than `まで`.
- **The "even" use is emphatic and slightly loaded.** 先生(せんせい)まで笑(わら)った - 
  "even the teacher laughed" - presents the teacher as the least likely member of the
  set. It always carries a note of surprise, and it can sound accusatory if the sentence
  is a complaint: 君(きみ)まで反対(はんたい)するのか。 Use it deliberately.
- **までも and までは.** 明日(あした)までには終(お)わります adds "by tomorrow at the
  latest"; そこまでしなくてもいい means "you don't have to go that far". These build on the
  same core idea of a limit and are worth recognising early.

`まで` also survives on its own as a sentence-final marker in signs and notices:
受付(うけつけ)は五時(ごじ)まで。 Nothing is missing - the reader supplies です.

## Contrast

### まで vs までに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まで` = "**until**", for something that continues. `までに` = "**by**",
for something that happens once, at any point before the deadline.

</div>

This is the highest-value distinction on the page, and it is tested at every level.

<pre>
五時(ごじ)<b>まで</b>会社(かいしゃ)にいます。
I'll be at the office until five.
</pre>

<pre>
五時(ごじ)<b>までに</b>会社(かいしゃ)に戻(もど)ります。
I'll be back at the office by five.
</pre>

The first stretches across the whole period; the second is a single event that lands
somewhere inside it. So the test is the verb. **Durative** verbs - いる, 待(ま)つ,
働(はたら)く, 続(つづ)ける, 寝(ね)ている - take `まで`. **Punctual** verbs - 
着(つ)く, 出(だ)す, 帰(かえ)る, 終(お)わらせる - take `までに`.

<pre>
明日(あした)の朝(あさ)<b>までに</b>この書類(しょるい)を出(だ)してください。
Please hand this document in by tomorrow morning.
</pre>

Say まで there and you are asking someone to keep submitting the document all night.

### まで vs から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` opens the span, `まで` closes it. Either can stand alone; when
both appear, `から` always comes first.

</div>

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みは七月(しちがつ)から八月(はちがつ)<b>まで</b>です。
The summer holiday runs from July to August.
</pre>

Alone, each simply leaves the other end open. 来週(らいしゅう)から忙(いそが)しいです
says nothing about when the busy period ends; 水曜日(すいようび)まで忙(いそが)しいです
says nothing about when it started. The pairing is so strong that a `から` early in a
sentence sets up an expectation of `まで` - leaving it unfilled is a stylistic choice, not
an error.

### まで vs に and へ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に` and `へ` give the goal of a movement. `まで` measures the stretch
covered and says where it ended.

</div>

<pre>
空港(くうこう)<b>まで</b>送(おく)ります。
I'll take you as far as the airport.
</pre>

<pre>
空港(くうこう)<b>に</b>着(つ)きました。
I've arrived at the airport.
</pre>

The first is about the journey - the whole distance is covered and the airport is where
your involvement stops; it quietly implies you are not going further. The second is about
the endpoint alone. This is why 送(おく)る, 歩(ある)く and 走(はし)る take `まで` so
naturally, and why 着(つ)く cannot: arriving has no stretch to measure.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 金曜日(きんようび)<b>まで</b>レポートを出(だ)してください。 → ✅ 金曜日(きんようび)<b>までに</b>レポートを出(だ)してください。
  Handing something in happens once, so it needs the deadline form. The まで version
  asks for continuous submission.

- ❌ 六時(ろくじ)<b>までに</b>勉強(べんきょう)しました。 → ✅ 六時(ろくじ)<b>まで</b>勉強(べんきょう)しました。
  Studying is durative - it filled the period - so it takes plain `まで`.

- ❌ 映画(えいが)が終(お)わり<b>ますまで</b>席(せき)を立(た)たないでください。 → ✅ 映画(えいが)が終(お)わる<b>まで</b>席(せき)を立(た)たないでください。
  The verb in front of `まで` is always plain non-past. Politeness lives at the end of
  the sentence.

- ❌ 駅(えき)<b>までに</b>送(おく)ります。 → ✅ 駅(えき)<b>まで</b>送(おく)ります。
  With a place, `までに` would mean "by the time we reach the station". If you mean "as
  far as", it is plain `まで`.

- ❌ 三日(みっか)までに出(だ)してください（meaning "before the 3rd"） → ✅ 二日(ふつか)までに出(だ)してください
  `まで` and `までに` **include** the point they name, so the first sentence allows the
  3rd itself. To exclude it, name the day before, or say 三日(みっか)の前(まえ)に.

## Practice

**1.** 宿題(しゅくだい)は水曜日(すいようび)____出(だ)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

宿題(しゅくだい)は水曜日(すいようび)<b>までに</b>出(だ)してください。

Handing in homework is a one-off act, so Wednesday is a deadline, not a stretch. Plain
`まで` would ask the student to keep handing it in continuously until Wednesday.

</details>

**2.** 父(ちち)は毎晩(まいばん)遅(おそ)く（まで・までに）仕事(しごと)をしています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

父(ちち)は毎晩(まいばん)遅(おそ)く<b>まで</b>仕事(しごと)をしています。

仕事(しごと)をする here is durative - it fills the evening - so it takes `まで`. `までに`
needs a verb that happens at a single moment.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll wait here until you get back."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

帰(かえ)ってくる<b>まで</b>ここで待(ま)っています。

待(ま)つ is the classic durative verb, so `まで`. The verb in front of `まで` stays in
the plain non-past - 帰(かえ)ってくる, not 帰(かえ)ってきた - even though the return is
in the future.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まりますまで、ロビーでお待(ま)ちください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる<b>まで</b>、ロビーでお待(ま)ちください。
"Please wait in the lobby until the meeting begins."

`まで` takes the plain dictionary form. The sentence is still perfectly polite:
お待(ま)ちください at the end carries that, and it is the only place politeness is marked.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 五時(ごじ)<b>までに</b>レポートを書(か)きます。
(b) 五時(ごじ)<b>まで</b>レポートを書(か)きます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a promise about a deadline: the report will be finished at some point before five.
Nothing is said about when you actually work on it - you might write it in the last ten
minutes.

(b) is a description of how you will spend the time: you will be writing the report right
up to five o'clock. It says nothing about whether it gets finished. Native speakers hear
these as completely different commitments, which is why the extra `に` matters so much.

</details>

# と - and, joining a complete list

**Meaning** · and - joins nouns into a list, and claims the list is complete

`と` glues nouns together: A と B is "A and B". The part learners are not told is that
`と` is **exhaustive**. A と B と C says those three are all of them. If you only want to
name a couple of examples out of many, Japanese has a different particle, `や`, and using
`と` where you meant `や` makes a claim you did not intend.

## Formation

`と` sits between the nouns. The last noun takes whatever particle the sentence needs;
the `と`-group behaves as a single noun phrase.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two nouns | Noun と Noun | パン → パンと卵(たまご) |
| Three or more | Noun と Noun と Noun | 犬(いぬ) → 犬(いぬ)と猫(ねこ)と鳥(とり) |
| The group takes a particle | (N と N) + が／を／は | 父(ちち)・母(はは) → 父(ちち)と母(はは)が来(き)ます |
| Choosing between two | N と N、どちらが… | コーヒー → コーヒーと紅茶(こうちゃ)、どちらが |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `と` joins **nouns only**. It cannot join verbs, adjectives or clauses. For
those, Japanese uses the て-form (安(やす)くて便利(べんり)) or そして
(買(か)い物(もの)をしました。そして、帰(かえ)りました). The `と` you meet at the end of a
clause - 春(はる)になると暖(あたた)かくなる - is a different word entirely, a conditional.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
パン<b>と</b>牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を買(か)いました。
I bought bread and milk.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)<b>と</b>母(はは)は大阪(おおさか)にいます。
My father and mother are in Osaka.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)は卵(たまご)<b>と</b>ごはんです。
Breakfast is eggs and rice.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、コーヒー<b>と</b>ケーキをください。
Excuse me - a coffee and a cake, please.
</pre>

<pre>
英語(えいご)<b>と</b>中国語(ちゅうごくご)<b>と</b>フランス語(ご)が話(はな)せます。
I can speak English, Chinese and French.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)に出(で)るのは部長(ぶちょう)<b>と</b>私(わたし)の二人(ふたり)だけです。
The only two attending the meeting are the manager and me.
</pre>

<pre>
この料理(りょうり)には塩(しお)<b>と</b>こしょう<b>と</b>オリーブオイルしか使(つか)っていません。
This dish uses nothing but salt, pepper and olive oil.
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)に持(も)っていくのはパスポート<b>と</b>カメラ<b>と</b>薬(くすり)ぐらいです。
About all I'm taking on the trip is my passport, a camera and some medicine.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`と` is register-neutral and is never dropped. In a list of three or more it is repeated
between every pair - Japanese has no equivalent of the English "A, B and C" where the
first joins are commas. A comma-only list (犬(いぬ)、猫(ねこ)、鳥(とり)) does exist,
but it reads as a written enumeration, like items on a form, rather than as speech.

A trailing `と` after the last item - 本(ほん)と、ノートと。 - is heard when someone is
still counting things off out loud, and it signals "…and there may be more coming". Once
the list is finished, drop it. In a written sentence a trailing `と` before a particle is
simply wrong: ❌<code>本(ほん)とノートとが</code>.

The exhaustiveness of `と` is what makes it useful in shops, orders and instructions,
where you want to be precise. 材料(ざいりょう)は小麦粉(こむぎこ)と水(みず)と塩(しお)です
means those are the ingredients, full stop. In relaxed conversation about vaguer things,
`や` is often the more natural choice, and over-using `と` can make you sound like you are
reading from a list.

One more thing `と` does that looks unrelated but is the same idea: it sets up a
**two-way comparison**. コーヒーと紅茶(こうちゃ)、どちらがいいですか。 The two items are
being presented as a closed set to choose between - exhaustive again.

## Contrast

### と vs や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` says "these and no others". `や` says "these among others".

</div>

<pre>
かばんの中(なか)には財布(さいふ)<b>と</b>鍵(かぎ)が入(はい)っています。
There's a wallet and keys in my bag.
</pre>

<pre>
かばんの中(なか)には財布(さいふ)<b>や</b>鍵(かぎ)が入(はい)っています。
There's a wallet, keys and so on in my bag.
</pre>

The first is a complete inventory - that bag holds two things. The second offers a
sample; there is clearly other stuff in there. Because `や` is a sample, it very often
finishes with など: 財布(さいふ)や鍵(かぎ)など. That など is only available to `や`;
❌<code>財布(さいふ)と鍵(かぎ)など</code> is contradictory.

Practical consequence: if someone asks what is in a place and you are not sure you have
listed everything, `や` protects you. If you are giving an order or a specification, use
`と`.

### と vs the て-form and そして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` is for nouns. To link adjectives use the て-form; to link
actions use the て-form or そして.

</div>

This is the most frequent `と` error, because English "and" does all three jobs.

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)く<b>て</b>明(あか)るいです。
This room is spacious and bright.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べ<b>て</b>、会社(かいしゃ)へ行(い)きました。
I had breakfast and went to work.
</pre>

Both would be ungrammatical with `と`. The rule has no exceptions worth learning at N5:
if what you are joining ends in い, な or a verb, `と` is not the tool.

### と as "and" vs と as "with"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the と-phrase is one of the things the verb is *about*, it is
"and". If it names a person the subject acts *together with*, it is "with".

</div>

The same two words can go either way, and only the sentence shape decides.

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>と</b>山田(やまだ)さんが来(き)ました。
Tanaka and Yamada came.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんが山田(やまだ)さん<b>と</b>来(き)ました。
Tanaka came with Yamada.
</pre>

In the first, `が` follows the whole group, so the group is one subject: two people
arrived. In the second, `が` has already marked Tanaka as the subject, so 山田(やまだ)さん
can only be a companion. Watch where the subject particle lands and the ambiguity
disappears. Adding 一緒(いっしょ)に forces the "with" reading outright.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この町(まち)は静(しず)か<b>と</b>きれいです。 → ✅ この町(まち)は静(しず)かで、きれいです。
  `と` cannot join adjectives. な-adjectives link with で, い-adjectives with the
  て-form.

- ❌ 朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べた<b>と</b>、出(で)かけました。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べて、出(で)かけました。
  Two actions in sequence are linked with the て-form. `と` after a clause makes a
  conditional, not a list.

- ❌ りんご<b>と</b>バナナ<b>と</b>など好(す)きです。 → ✅ りんご<b>や</b>バナナなどが好(す)きです。
  など marks an open-ended sample, which contradicts the "complete list" meaning of `と`.
  など belongs with `や`.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんと山田(やまだ)さん<b>と</b>が来(き)ました。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんと山田(やまだ)さんが来(き)ました。
  No `と` after the final item in a written sentence. The group takes its particle
  directly.

## Practice

**1.** 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にノート____ペンがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にノート<b>と</b>ペンがあります。

Two nouns joined into one subject group, and the claim is that those are the two things
on the desk. If you were only naming examples of what was lying there, `や` would be the
choice.

</details>

**2.** 「冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に何(なに)がありますか。」「たまご（と・や）チーズなどがあります。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

たまご<b>や</b>チーズなどがあります。

The など at the end gives it away: it means "and things like that", which only works with
the sampling particle `や`. `と` would claim the fridge contains exactly two items, and
then など would contradict it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'd like a coffee and a sandwich, please."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

コーヒー<b>と</b>サンドイッチをお願(ねが)いします。

An order is exactly where `と` belongs - you are stating the complete list of what you
want. Using `や` here would leave the waiter unsure whether more was coming.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いときれいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)くてきれいです。
"This room is spacious and clean."

`と` joins nouns, never adjectives. An い-adjective drops its final い and takes くて.
As written, 広(ひろ)いと would be read as a conditional - "if it's spacious".

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) パーティーには田中(たなか)さん<b>と</b>山田(やまだ)さんが来(き)ました。
(b) パーティーには田中(たなか)さん<b>や</b>山田(やまだ)さんが来(き)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the full guest list: two people came, and naming a third would contradict the
sentence. This is what you say when the party was small or when you are being precise.

(b) picks two names out of a crowd. `や` implies other guests you have not bothered to
mention, so the party was bigger - and it protects you if you have forgotten someone.

</details>

# と - with, accompaniment

**Meaning** · with - names the person you do something together with, or the other party
in a two-sided action

`と` marks a **partner**. Sometimes the partner is optional - you could have gone to the
film alone - and sometimes the verb cannot exist without one: you cannot marry, argue or
shake hands by yourself. Both are the same `と`.

## Formation

`と` attaches directly to a noun, almost always a person or an animal, and the phrase
then modifies the verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - companion | Person + と + verb | 友達(ともだち) → 友達(ともだち)と行(い)く |
| Noun - companion, emphasised | Person + と一緒(いっしょ)に + verb | 家族(かぞく) → 家族(かぞく)と一緒(いっしょ)に食(た)べる |
| Noun - the other party in a mutual act | Person + と + 結婚(けっこん)する／けんかする／会(あ)う | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)と結婚(けっこん)する |
| Question word | だれ + と | だれ → だれと来(き)ましたか |
| Combined with a count | Person + と + number + で | 友達(ともだち) → 友達(ともだち)と二人(ふたり)で |

<div class="note">

**Note** - 一緒(いっしょ)に is optional but common. It adds nothing grammatically; it
just makes the togetherness explicit and slightly warmer. The `に` is not optional:
❌<code>友達(ともだち)と一緒(いっしょ)行(い)く</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
友達(ともだち)<b>と</b>映画(えいが)を見(み)ました。
I saw a film with a friend.
</pre>

<pre>
だれ<b>と</b>来(き)ましたか。
Who did you come with?
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)<b>と</b>一緒(いっしょ)に買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)きます。
I'm going shopping with my older sister.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)、大学(だいがく)の先輩(せんぱい)<b>と</b>結婚(けっこん)しました。
Last year I married a senior from my university.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)、先生(せんせい)<b>と</b>話(はな)す約束(やくそく)をしました。
I've arranged to speak with my teacher tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>と</b>けんかして、母(はは)に怒(おこ)られました。
I had a fight with my little brother and got told off by my mother.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は家族(かぞく)<b>と</b>川(かわ)へ行(い)って、バーベキューをする予定(よてい)です。
At the weekend I'm going to the river with my family for a barbecue.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)<b>と</b>相談(そうだん)してから、お返事(へんじ)します。
I'll get back to you once I've talked it over with my section chief.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`と` is register-neutral and is not dropped, even in fast speech - remove it and the
sentence loses the only thing marking the companion.

The set it belongs to is worth naming, because it decides the particle for a whole class
of verbs. **Mutual verbs** need two parties, and the second one is always `と`:
結婚(けっこん)する, 離婚(りこん)する, けんかする, 相談(そうだん)する, 約束(やくそく)する,
話(はな)す, 似(に)る - with the exception noted below. Learn these as と-verbs and a
large slice of N4 grammar comes for free.

Two frequency notes. **だれと** is the standard way to ask "who with?", and it comes up
constantly in small talk about the weekend. And <b>と一緒(いっしょ)に</b> is the polite, slightly
fuller version you use when the companionship is the point:
部長(ぶちょう)と一緒(いっしょ)に伺(うかが)います。

Finally, keep this `と` separate from the quotation `と`, which follows a whole clause
and precedes 言(い)う, 思(おも)う or 書(か)く:
明日(あした)行(い)くと言(い)いました。 If a full sentence sits in front of `と`, it
is quotation, not accompaniment. And if a bare noun sits in front and the group is what
the verb is about, it is the "and" `と` instead.

## Contrast

### と vs で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` needs a **named companion**. A **number of people** takes `で`.

</div>

This pair trips up almost everyone, because English says "with two people" and "with a
friend" the same way.

<pre>
友達(ともだち)<b>と</b>行(い)きました。
I went with a friend.
</pre>

<pre>
三人(さんにん)<b>で</b>行(い)きました。
The three of us went.
</pre>

You cannot say ❌<code>三人(さんにん)と行(い)きました</code>, because a bare number names
nobody. The same logic gives 一人(ひとり)で for "alone" and みんなで for "all together".

The two combine happily when you want both facts at once:

<pre>
同僚(どうりょう)<b>と</b>四人(よにん)<b>で</b>飲(の)みに行(い)きました。
I went out drinking with colleagues, four of us in all.
</pre>

### と vs に with mutual verbs

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` presents the action as **two-sided**; `に` presents it as
**one-directional**, aimed at the other person.

</div>

Several verbs take either particle, and the choice changes the picture.

<pre>
駅(えき)で先生(せんせい)<b>に</b>会(あ)いました。
I ran into my teacher at the station.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)で先生(せんせい)<b>と</b>会(あ)いました。
I met up with my teacher at the station.
</pre>

With `に`, you did the meeting - it can be an accident, and often is. With `と`, both
parties met, which implies it was arranged. The same split runs through
相談(そうだん)する (上司(じょうし)に = you take the problem to them;
上司(じょうし)と = you thrash it out together) and 話(はな)す.

Some verbs allow only one. 結婚(けっこん)する and けんかする are `と` only, because they
are unavoidably two-sided. 似(に)る is `に` only - 父(ちち)に似(に)ています,
"I take after my father" - even though resemblance sounds mutual in English.

### と as "with" vs と as "and"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - find the subject marker. If `が` or `は` follows the whole
と-group, it is "and". If the subject has already been named elsewhere, the と-phrase is
"with".

</div>

<pre>
兄(あに)<b>と</b>姉(あね)は東京(とうきょう)に住(す)んでいます。
My brother and sister live in Tokyo.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)は姉(あね)<b>と</b>東京(とうきょう)に住(す)んでいます。
My brother lives in Tokyo with my sister.
</pre>

In the first, `は` scopes over both people, so they are one subject: two residents. In the
second, `は` has already claimed 兄(あに), so 姉(あね)と can only be accompaniment.

When there is no subject particle at all - a bare 友達(ともだち)と行(い)きました - the
reading is "with", because a list of things needs a slot in the sentence and this one has
none. Adding 一緒(いっしょ)に removes any doubt.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 一人(ひとり)<b>と</b>行(い)きました。 → ✅ 一人(ひとり)<b>で</b>行(い)きました。
  A count of people is a circumstance, not a companion. Numbers take `で`.

- ❌ 友達(ともだち)と三人(さんにん)<b>と</b>行(い)きました。 → ✅ 友達(ともだち)と三人(さんにん)<b>で</b>行(い)きました。
  The first `と` names the companions; the number that follows still needs `で`.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さん<b>に</b>結婚(けっこん)しました。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さん<b>と</b>結婚(けっこん)しました。
  Marrying is unavoidably two-sided, so it is `と` only. The `に` version is a very
  common learner error and sounds strange to a native ear.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんと一緒(いっしょ)行(い)きます。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんと一緒(いっしょ)<b>に</b>行(い)きます。
  一緒(いっしょ)に is a fixed adverbial phrase and cannot lose its `に`.

- ❌ 父(ちち)<b>と</b>似(に)ています。 → ✅ 父(ちち)<b>に</b>似(に)ています。
  似(に)る is the mutual-looking verb that takes `に`. Resemblance in Japanese is
  modelled as one thing pointing at another.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)、同(おな)じクラスの人(ひと)____勉強(べんきょう)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)、同(おな)じクラスの人(ひと)<b>と</b>勉強(べんきょう)しました。

A named person doing the activity alongside you is a companion, which is `と`. `で` would
be wrong here because 人(ひと) is not a count.

</details>

**2.** 旅行(りょこう)には一人(ひとり)（と・で）行(い)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

旅行(りょこう)には一人(ひとり)<b>で</b>行(い)きました。

一人(ひとり) is a number of people, not a person, so it takes `で`. There is no companion
to mark - that is the whole point of the sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "There's something I'd like to discuss with the manager."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)<b>と</b>相談(そうだん)したいことがあります。

相談(そうだん)する with `と` frames it as a two-sided discussion, which is what "discuss
with" means. 部長(ぶちょう)に相談(そうだん)する is also correct Japanese, but it means
taking a problem *to* him for advice.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 去年(きょねん)、高校(こうこう)の友達(ともだち)に結婚(けっこん)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 去年(きょねん)、高校(こうこう)の友達(ともだち)<b>と</b>結婚(けっこん)しました。
"Last year I married a friend from high school."

結婚(けっこん)する belongs to the mutual-verb set, where the other party must be `と`.
`に` would try to make the friend a recipient, which makes no sense for a marriage.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)<b>に</b>話(はな)しました。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)<b>と</b>話(はな)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is one-directional: you told him something. The information went from you to him, and
the sentence says nothing about whether he replied - this is what you say after reporting
a problem upward.

(b) is two-sided: the two of you had a conversation. Both people spoke, and the sentence
implies some exchange took place. Choosing `と` with a superior can also sound a shade
more equal, so `に` is the safer default when you are simply reporting.

</details>
