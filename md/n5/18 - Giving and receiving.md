# N5 - Giving and receiving


---

# あげる・くれる・もらう

**Meaning** · give (away) / give (to me) / receive

English has one verb "give" and lets context sort out the direction. Japanese
does not: it has **two** verbs for giving, and which one you use depends on
whether the thing is moving toward you or away from you. Choosing wrongly is not
a stylistic slip - it makes the sentence unintelligible.

## Formation

Each verb decides who the subject is and which way the object travels. All three
are regular る-verbs (あげます, くれます - but note くださる → くださいます).

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| あげる（away from me） | Aが Bに Nを あげる | 私(わたし)は妹(いもうと)に本(ほん)をあげる |
| くれる（toward me） | Aが（私(わたし)に）Nを くれる | 友(とも)だちが私(わたし)に本(ほん)をくれる |
| もらう（I receive） | Bが Aに／から Nを もらう | 私(わたし)は友(とも)だちに本(ほん)をもらう |
| Polite | あげます／くれます／もらいます | 本(ほん)をもらいました |
| I give upward（humble） | さしあげる | 先生(せんせい)にさしあげる |
| A superior gives to me（honorific） | くださる → くださいます | 先生(せんせい)がくださる |
| I receive from above（humble） | いただく | 先生(せんせい)にいただく |
| Giving down the scale | やる | 花(はな)に水(みず)をやる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask where the thing ends up. If it ends up with **me or my
side**, the giving verb is `くれる`. If it ends up with **anyone else**, it is
`あげる`. `もらう` describes the same event from the receiver's chair.

</div>

The particles are worth memorising as a set: the **giver** is `が` (or `は`) with
あげる and くれる, and `に`／`から` with もらう. The **thing** is always `を`.

## Examples

<pre>
妹(いもうと)にプレゼントを<b>あげました</b>。
I gave my little sister a present.
</pre>

<pre>
友(とも)だちが私(わたし)にチョコレートを<b>くれました</b>。
A friend gave me some chocolate.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)に時計(とけい)を<b>もらいました</b>。
I got a watch from my father.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんは田中(たなか)さんに花(はな)を<b>あげました</b>。
Yamada gave Tanaka some flowers.
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)、犬(いぬ)にごはんを<b>やって</b>から会社(かいしゃ)へ行(い)きます。
Every morning I feed the dog before leaving for work.
</pre>

<pre>
となりの人(ひと)が子(こ)どもたちにおかしを<b>くれました</b>。
The neighbour gave my children some sweets.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)が弟(おとうと)に辞書(じしょ)を<b>くださいました</b>。
The teacher kindly gave my younger brother a dictionary.
</pre>

<pre>
誕生日(たんじょうび)に姉(あね)からきれいなカップを<b>もらいました</b>。
I got a lovely cup from my older sister for my birthday.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)をやめるとき、同(おな)じ部(ぶ)の人(ひと)たちが花束(はなたば)を<b>くれました</b>。
When I left the company, the people in my department gave me a bouquet.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The in-group is what really matters.** `くれる` is not only for gifts to *me*. It is for gifts to my **side** - what
Japanese calls うち, as opposed to そと, everyone else. My family, and depending on
the conversation my close friends, my colleagues, my company: if the thing lands
with any of them, `くれる` is the verb.

That is why 「先生(せんせい)が弟(おとうと)に本(ほん)をくださいました」 uses
a giving-toward-me verb even though the book went to my brother. My brother is
inside my circle, so the book came toward my side of the line. Say
❌先生(せんせい)が弟(おとうと)に本(ほん)をあげました and you have pushed
your own brother out of your family.

The line moves with the situation. Talking to a customer, everyone at my company
is うち; talking to a colleague, only my own team might be.

**The politeness axis.** Each verb has a version for when a status gap is in play:

- <code>さしあげる</code> - humble, for when *I* give to someone above me.
- <code>くださる</code> - honorific, for when someone above me gives to me or mine.
  The ます-form is irregular: <b>くださいます</b>, not ❌くださります. `ください`, the
  word you already know, is its imperative.
- <code>いただく</code> - humble, for when *I* receive from someone above me.

Of these, `くださる` and `いただく` are safe and extremely common. `さしあげる`
needs care: announcing that you have bestowed something on your superior can sound
self-important, so people often avoid it and say
先生(せんせい)にお渡(わた)ししました or simply
先生(せんせい)に渡(わた)しました instead.

<b>やる</b>**, and how rough it sounds.** `やる` is the downward version of `あげる`, used for plants, animals and - in
older or rougher speech - for children and younger siblings. It is standard for
花(はな)に水(みず)をやる and 犬(いぬ)にえさをやる. Used of people it now
sounds blunt and is male-marked; younger speakers say
犬(いぬ)にごはんをあげる without a second thought, and some older speakers find
that overly precious. Either is acceptable; know that both exist.

**Two smaller points.** `もらう` takes `に` or `から` for the source. `から` is preferred when the giver is
an organisation rather than a person:
会社(かいしゃ)から手紙(てがみ)をもらいました.

The plain command `くれ`（水(みず)をくれ）is very rough - men's speech,
brusque. Use ください.

Finally, these three verbs also work as helpers on the て-form - 
買(か)ってあげる, 教(おし)えてくれる, 作(つく)ってもらう - where the same
direction logic applies to *actions* rather than objects. That construction is
taught at N4, and it is unusable until the direction rule on this page is solid.

## Contrast

### あげる vs くれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "give", and both take the giver as subject. `あげる`
sends the thing away from the speaker; `くれる` brings it toward the speaker's
side.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)は田中(たなか)さんに傘(かさ)を<b>あげました</b>。
I gave Tanaka an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんが私(わたし)に傘(かさ)を<b>くれました</b>。
Tanaka gave me an umbrella.
</pre>

The two sentences describe opposite events, and the only substantive difference is
the verb. Note that `くれる` may **never** have the speaker as giver:
❌私(わたし)が田中(たなか)さんにくれました is impossible in any context.

In `くれる` sentences the 私(わたし)に is usually dropped, because the verb has
already said it: 田中(たなか)さんが傘(かさ)をくれました is the natural form.

### くれる vs もらう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one event, two camera angles. `くれる` puts the giver in the
subject slot; `もらう` puts the receiver there.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)が私(わたし)にセーターを<b>くれました</b>。
My mother gave me a jumper.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は母(はは)にセーターを<b>もらいました</b>。
I got a jumper from my mother.
</pre>

Both are correct and both are common. `くれる` feels slightly warmer - it presents
the other person as acting kindly toward you - while `もらう` is more neutral and
focuses on the fact that you now have the thing. Note that the particle on
母(はは) flips: `が` when she is the subject, `に` when she is the source.

### あげる・くれる・もらう vs さしあげる・くださる・いただく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the plain three are for equals and for people below you. The
polite three encode a status gap, and each one specifies which direction the gap
runs in.

</div>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)が資料(しりょう)を<b>くださいました</b>。
The section chief gave me the documents.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)に資料(しりょう)を<b>いただきました</b>。
I received the documents from the section chief.
</pre>

Both are polite and both are safe. What you must not do is use the plain verbs of
a superior's giving in a formal setting - 
課長(かちょう)が資料(しりょう)をくれました is grammatical but sounds
disrespectful in front of the section chief, though it is perfectly normal when
grumbling to a friend.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 友(とも)だちが私(わたし)にプレゼントを<b>あげました</b>。 → ✅ 友(とも)だちが私(わたし)にプレゼントを<b>くれました</b>。
  When the thing arrives at the speaker, the verb must be `くれる`. This is the
  single most frequent error learners make, because English uses "gave" for both
  directions.

- ❌ 私(わたし)が山田(やまだ)さんに本(ほん)を<b>くれました</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)が山田(やまだ)さんに本(ほん)を<b>あげました</b>。
  `くれる` can never have the speaker as the giver. Nothing can move toward you
  when you are the one letting go of it.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は兄(あに)<b>が</b>お金(かね)をもらいました。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は兄(あに)<b>に</b>お金(かね)をもらいました。
  With `もらう` the source takes `に` or `から`. `が` there would make your brother
  the one doing the receiving.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)が花(はな)を<b>くださります</b>。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)が花(はな)を<b>くださいます</b>。
  <code>くださる</code> is one of a small group of honorific verbs with an irregular
  ます-form - like おっしゃる → おっしゃいます.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)に辞書(じしょ)を<b>あげました</b>。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)に辞書(じしょ)を<b>さしあげました</b>。／先生(せんせい)に辞書(じしょ)をお渡(わた)ししました。
  Using plain `あげる` about a gift to a teacher sounds as though you were doing
  them a favour from above. Even the humble form is often sidestepped in favour of
  a neutral verb.

## Practice

**1.** 山田(やまだ)さんが私(わたし)に地図(ちず)を____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)さんが私(わたし)に地図(ちず)を<b>くれました</b>。

The map ends up with the speaker, so the verb has to be `くれる`. In real speech
the 私(わたし)に would usually be left out, since `くれる` already tells you
where the map went.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部長(ぶちょう)にお土産(みやげ)を（もらいました・いただきました）。 You are speaking at work, in front of colleagues.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)にお土産(みやげ)を<b>いただきました</b>。

<code>いただく</code> is the humble verb for receiving from someone above you, and a
department head qualifies. `もらいました` is not wrong grammatically, but in a
workplace setting it sounds too flat about a superior's kindness.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My grandmother gave my son a book."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

祖母(そぼ)が息子(むすこ)に本(ほん)を<b>くれました</b>。

Your son is inside your family, so the book has travelled toward your side and
`くれる` is required - even though the speaker is not the recipient. Using
あげました here would sound as though the boy were somebody else's child.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 私(わたし)は友(とも)だちにおかしをくれました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 私(わたし)は友(とも)だちにおかしを<b>あげました</b>。

The sweets are leaving the speaker, so the verb must be `あげる`. `くれる` with
私(わたし) as the giver is not merely unnatural - it is impossible, because the
verb's whole job is to mark movement toward the speaker.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 父(ちち)が自転車(じてんしゃ)をくれました。
(b) 父(ちち)に自転車(じてんしゃ)をもらいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both report the same bicycle changing hands, and both are perfectly natural.

(a) makes the father the subject and presents the act as something he did for the
speaker; there is a faint warmth of gratitude in it. (b) makes the speaker the
subject and simply records the acquisition - closer to "I got a bike from my
dad". The choice is about where you point the camera, not about the facts.

</details>

# お・ご

**Meaning** · the politeness prefix - attached to a noun to make it respectful,
humble, or simply more refined

`お` and `ご` are the same prefix wearing two coats. Which one a word takes is
decided by the word's **origin**, not by how polite you are feeling: native
Japanese vocabulary takes `お`, vocabulary borrowed from Chinese takes `ご`. The
choice is fixed per word and has to be learnt with the word.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Native noun (kun reading) | <b>お</b> + noun | 名前(なまえ) → お名前(なまえ) |
| Native noun (kun reading) | <b>お</b> + noun | 金(かね) → お金(かね) |
| Sino-Japanese noun (on reading) | <b>ご</b> + noun | 家族(かぞく) → ご家族(かぞく) |
| Sino-Japanese noun (on reading) | <b>ご</b> + noun | 住所(じゅうしょ) → ご住所(じゅうしょ) |
| Sino-Japanese exceptions | <b>お</b> + noun | 電話(でんわ) → お電話(でんわ) |
| な-adjective | お／ご + adjective | 元気(げんき) → お元気(げんき)／親切(しんせつ) → ご親切(しんせつ) |
| Verb, honorific | お + ます-stem + になる | 読(よ)む → お読(よ)みになる |
| Verb, humble | お／ご + stem + する | 電話(でんわ)する → お電話(でんわ)します |
| Fossilised | prefix is part of the word | ご飯(はん)、お茶(ちゃ)、おなか |
| Loanwords | normally nothing | コーヒー → ❌おコーヒー |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - read the word aloud. If it sounds like plain old Japanese
（名前(なまえ), 金(かね), 手紙(てがみ)）, use `お`. If it is a compact
two-character compound read with Chinese-derived sounds
（住所(じゅうしょ), 家族(かぞく), 注文(ちゅうもん)）, use `ご`. Then learn
the handful of exceptions by heart.

</div>

The common Sino-Japanese words that take `お` anyway are worth memorising as a
list: お電話(でんわ), お返事(へんじ), お食事(しょくじ),
お時間(じかん), お料理(りょうり), お約束(やくそく), お礼(れい),
お茶(ちゃ), お客(きゃく)さん. These have been in everyday Japanese so long
that they stopped feeling foreign.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>お名前(なまえ)</b>を教(おし)えてください。
Could you tell me your name?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ご家族(かぞく)</b>は何人(なんにん)ですか。
How many people are there in your family?
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)、<b>お元気(げんき)</b>ですか。
How are you, sensei?
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、<b>お手洗(てあら)い</b>はどこですか。
Excuse me, where is the toilet?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ご注文(ちゅうもん)</b>はお決(き)まりですか。
Are you ready to order?
</pre>

<pre>
あとで会社(かいしゃ)から<b>お電話(でんわ)します</b>。
I'll call you from the office later.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ご住所(じゅうしょ)</b>と電話(でんわ)番号(ばんごう)をこちらに書(か)いてください。
Please write your address and telephone number here.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は<b>ご親切(しんせつ)</b>にありがとうございました。
Thank you for your kindness yesterday.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The prefix is doing one of three quite different jobs, and telling them apart is
the whole skill.

**Respect.** Attached to something belonging to the listener - お名前(なまえ),
ご家族(かぞく), お仕事(しごと) - it raises *them*. This is the use that must
never be turned on yourself: ❌私(わたし)のお名前(なまえ)は… is the classic
beginner's error, and it sounds like bowing to your own reflection.

**Humility.** In お + stem + する（お電話(でんわ)します,
お待(ま)ちしています）the prefix belongs to *your* action, lowering you rather
than raising anyone. The `お` is attached to a verb here, not a noun.

**Beautification** - 美化語(びかご). お茶(ちゃ), ご飯(はん),
お金(かね), お菓子(かし), お水(みず) have nothing to do with respect at
all. Everyone says them about everyone's tea and everyone's money, and dropping
the prefix from some of these（茶(ちゃ), 金(かね)）sounds coarse. A few have
fused completely: おなか and おかし are simply the words, and no one hears a prefix
in them any more.

Register notes worth having:

- Heavy use of 美化語(びかご) is traditionally associated with women's speech and
  with a refined register. Layering it everywhere - おビール, おズボン - sounds
  affected, though shops and restaurants do exactly that.
- Katakana loanwords normally take nothing. The one you will genuinely hear is
  おトイレ, which has become ordinary polite speech.
- A small set of い-adjectives takes `お` in fixed courtesies:
  お忙(いそが)しいところすみません, お若(わか)いですね.
- Some words take either prefix - お返事(へんじ) and ご返事(へんじ) are both
  used, with お the more common today.

You cannot attach the prefix wherever you like. There is no ❌お机(つくえ), no
❌おテレビ, no ❌ご勉強(べんきょう)部屋(べや). The set of words that take it
is closed, and it is learnt word by word.

## Contrast

### お vs ご

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `お` for 和語(わご) (native words, kun readings), `ご` for
漢語(かんご) (Chinese-derived words, on readings).

</div>

<pre>
<b>お手紙(てがみ)</b>をありがとうございました。
Thank you for your letter.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ご意見(いけん)</b>をお聞(き)かせください。
Please let us hear your opinion.
</pre>

<code>手紙(てがみ)</code> is read with native Japanese sounds, so `お`.
<code>意見(いけん)</code> is a two-character on-reading compound, so `ご`. The test
is not infallible - 返事(へんじ) is an on-reading compound and still prefers
`お` - but it is right far more often than it is wrong.

### Respectful お vs beautifying お

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if removing the prefix would be rude to a *person*, it is
respect. If it would just sound rough, it is beautification.

</div>

<pre>
<b>お仕事(しごと)</b>は何(なん)ですか。
What do you do for a living?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>お茶(ちゃ)</b>でも飲(の)みませんか。
Shall we have some tea?
</pre>

The first `お` honours the listener, and you would drop it when talking about your
own job: 私(わたし)の仕事(しごと)は教師(きょうし)です. The second belongs
to the tea and stays put whoever the tea is for - you say お茶(ちゃ) about your
own cup without a second thought.

### お + stem + になる vs お + stem + する

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>になる</code> raises the other person; <code>する</code> lowers
you. Both are polite; they point in opposite directions.

</div>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)はもう<b>お帰(かえ)りになりました</b>。
The company president has already gone home.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)、こちらから<b>お届(とど)けします</b>。
We'll deliver it to you tomorrow.
</pre>

Use the first about somebody else's action, the second about your own. Applying
the honorific form to yourself - ❌私(わたし)がお帰(かえ)りになります - is a
serious error, and the mirror-image mistake of using
<code>私(わたし)のお名前(なまえ)</code>. Both are covered properly at N4; what
matters now is knowing that the prefix alone does not make a phrase polite in the
right direction.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)の<b>お</b>名前(なまえ)は田中(たなか)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)の名前(なまえ)は田中(たなか)です。
  The respectful prefix never goes on your own possessions. Ask the listener for
  お名前(なまえ), then answer with a bare 名前(なまえ).

- ❌ <b>ご</b>電話(でんわ)ありがとうございます。 → ✅ <b>お</b>電話(でんわ)ありがとうございます。
  <code>電話(でんわ)</code> looks like a `ご` word - two characters, on readings - 
  but it is one of the fixed exceptions that take `お`.

- ❌ <b>お</b>住所(じゅうしょ)を書(か)いてください。 → ✅ <b>ご</b>住所(じゅうしょ)を書(か)いてください。
  The mirror error. <code>住所(じゅうしょ)</code> is straightforwardly
  漢語(かんご) and takes `ご`, as do ご職業(しょくぎょう) and
  ご連絡(れんらく) on the same form.

- ❌ <b>お</b>コーヒーをどうぞ。 → ✅ コーヒーをどうぞ。
  Loanwords do not take the prefix. Shop staff sometimes say it anyway; a learner
  saying it sounds like a mistake rather than like service language.

- ❌ 朝(あさ)、飯(はん)を食(た)べました。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べました。
  In ご飯(はん) the prefix has fused into the word. Removing it does not make
  the sentence casual - it makes it wrong.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、____名前(なまえ)は何(なん)とおっしゃいますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません、<b>お</b>名前(なまえ)は何(なん)とおっしゃいますか。

<code>名前(なまえ)</code> is a native Japanese word, so the prefix is `お`. It is
required here because you are asking about the listener's name, and a bare
名前(なまえ) in a question to a stranger sounds abrupt.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （お・ご）家族(かぞく)によろしくお伝(つた)えください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ご</b>家族(かぞく)によろしくお伝(つた)えください。

<code>家族(かぞく)</code> is a two-character on-reading compound - 
漢語(かんご) - so it takes `ご`. Note that
お伝(つた)えください in the same sentence takes `お`, because
<code>伝(つた)える</code> is a native verb.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Thank you for your reply."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>お返事(へんじ)</b>ありがとうございます。

<code>返事(へんじ)</code> is one of the on-reading words that nevertheless prefers
`お`. You will also see ご返事(へんじ), especially in older writing, but
お返事(へんじ) is the safer choice today.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 私(わたし)のお仕事(しごと)は先生(せんせい)です。

✅ 私(わたし)の仕事(しごと)は教師(きょうし)です。

Two things. The honorific `お` cannot describe your own job, so it must go. And
<code>先生(せんせい)</code> is itself a respectful word - use
<code>教師(きょうし)</code> about yourself.

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 時間(じかん)がありますか。
(b) お時間(じかん)、よろしいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain question about availability. Between friends it is perfectly
normal; addressed to a customer or a superior it sounds blunt.

(b) treats the listener's time as something belonging to them and worth
honouring, which is why the `お` appears. Combined with よろしいですか it becomes
the standard way to ask a busy person for a moment - the prefix is carrying the
politeness that the grammar alone does not.

</details>
