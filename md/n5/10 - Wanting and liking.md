# N5 - Wanting and liking


---

# ～たい

**Meaning** · want to do something - the speaker's own desire

`～たい` attaches to a verb and reports a wish felt from the inside. That "from the
inside" is not decoration: it is a grammatical restriction. Plain `～たい` is for
**your own** desires, and for asking about the **listener's**. You cannot use it
to state flatly what a third person wants.

## Formation

Attach `たい` to the ます-stem, then conjugate it exactly like an い-adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + たい | 飲(の)みます → 飲(の)みたい |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + たい | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べたい |
| Verb (する) | し + たい | する → したい／勉強(べんきょう)したい |
| Verb (来(く)る) | き + たい | 来(く)る → 来(き)たい |

Once `たい` is attached, <code>飲(の)みたい</code> behaves like <code>高(たか)い</code> in every
respect:

| い-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | + です | 飲(の)みたいです |
| Negative | たい → たくない | 飲(の)みたくない |
| Past | たい → たかった | 飲(の)みたかった |
| Negative past | たい → たくなかった | 飲(の)みたくなかった |
| て-form | たい → たくて | 飲(の)みたくて |
| Adverbial | たい → たく | 飲(の)みたくなる |
| Before a noun | たい + Noun | 飲(の)みたいもの |
| Third person | ます-stem + たがる | 飲(の)みたがる／飲(の)みたがっている |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たい` is an い-adjective, not a verb. So it is
<code>行(い)きたくない</code>, never ❌<code>行(い)きたいじゃない</code>, and <code>行(い)きたかった</code>,
never ❌<code>行(い)きたいでした</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
水(みず)が<b>飲(の)みたい</b>。
I want some water.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)へ<b>行(い)きたい</b>です。
I want to go to Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)が<b>食(た)べたい</b>ですか。
What do you want to eat?
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)はどこにも<b>行(い)きたくない</b>。
I don't want to go anywhere today.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものとき、パイロットに<b>なりたかった</b>。
When I was a kid, I wanted to be a pilot.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は新(あたら)しいゲームを<b>買(か)いたがっている</b>。
My little brother wants to buy the new game.
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れたので今日(きょう)は早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)たい</b>んですが、まだ仕事(しごと)が終(お)わりません。
I'm exhausted and I want an early night, but I still haven't finished work.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)は沖縄(おきなわ)に行(い)って、海(うみ)で<b>泳(およ)ぎたい</b>と思(おも)っています。
Next year I'm hoping to go to Okinawa and swim in the sea.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～たい` itself is neutral; `～たいです` is polite in *form*. The trouble is that
politeness of form does not fix bluntness of content. Stating a raw want at
someone senior - 部長(ぶちょう)、早(はや)く帰(かえ)りたいです - is
grammatical and still lands badly, because you are pushing your preference at a
superior. Softeners exist for exactly this:

- **～たいと思(おも)います** - "I'm thinking I'd like to…". The standard business
  hedge. 来週(らいしゅう)お休(やす)みを取(と)りたいと思(おも)います。
- **～たいんですが** - sets up a request and leaves the ask unsaid.
  すみません、これを返(かえ)したいんですが…
- **～たいのですが／～たく存(ぞん)じます** - written and formal registers.

The same asymmetry runs the other way. Asking a superior
何(なに)が食(た)べたいですか is a bit bald; 何(なん)になさいますか or
何(なに)がよろしいですか is what you actually say. Among friends and to
subordinates, plain `～たい?` is perfectly normal.

In casual speech the negative `～たくない` is the everyday form; `～たくありません`
is correct but stiff, and mostly turns up in writing or careful speech.

`～たがる` is a real verb (a う-verb: `たがらない`, `たがります`, `たがって`) and it
carries a slight sense of *watching someone from outside* - you are describing
observable behaviour, not reading a mind. That distance can shade into criticism,
so `たがる` about a superior is rude:
❌社長(しゃちょう)は帰(かえ)りたがっています.

## Contrast

### ～たい vs ～たがる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～たい` for me and for you; `～たがる` for him, her and them.

</div>

A flat statement about a third person's desire in plain `～たい` sounds wrong to
native ears, because you are claiming direct access to someone else's inner state.

<pre>
妹(いもうと)はケーキを<b>食(た)べたがっています</b>。
My little sister wants cake.
</pre>

Note the particle: `たがる` is an ordinary verb, so its object takes `を`, never
the `が` that `たい` allows. Note the `～ている` too. For a desire someone is feeling *right now*,
`たがっている` is the normal form; bare `たがる` reads as a habit - 
弟(おとうと)はいつも新(あたら)しいものを買(か)いたがる - "my brother always
wants to buy the newest thing."

`たがる` is not the only way out. These are often more natural, and politer:

- 妹(いもうと)はケーキが食(た)べたいそうです。 (I hear that…)
- 妹(いもうと)はケーキが食(た)べたいと言(い)っています。 (she says…)
- 妹(いもうと)はケーキが食(た)べたいようです／みたいです。 (it seems…)

<div class="note">

**Note** - the third-person ban only applies to a **plain main-clause statement**.
Inside a subordinate clause, plain `たい` about someone else is fine:
日本(にほん)に行(い)きたい人(ひと)は手(て)を挙(あ)げてください。
「Anyone who wants to go to Japan, raise your hand.」 It is also fine under
<code>と思(おも)っている</code>: 彼(かれ)は行(い)きたいと思(おも)っています。

</div>

### が vs を with ～たい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are correct. `が` spotlights the *thing* you crave; `を`
keeps the focus on the *action*. When in doubt at N5, `が` is the safer answer.

</div>

Because `たい` turns the verb into an adjective-like word, the old object can take
`が` - and that option is genuinely alive in modern Japanese.

<pre>
ビール<b>が</b>飲(の)みたい。
I want a beer. (it's beer specifically that I'm after)
</pre>

<pre>
ビール<b>を</b>飲(の)みたい。
I want to drink a beer. (the drinking is what I'm picturing)
</pre>

Three places where `を` is the only option:

1. **The `を` is not a direct object.** Path and departure `を` never becomes `が`:
   空(そら)**を**飛(と)びたい, 家(いえ)**を**出(で)たい.
2. **The clause already has a `が`.** Two `が` in one clause collide:
   ❌私(わたし)がビールが飲(の)みたい.
3. **The object is far from the verb.** Once material intervenes, `が` degrades:
   ビール**を**冷(ひ)やして、あとでゆっくり飲(の)みたい.

`を` is also the safer default in writing and in longer sentences; `が` is at its
most natural in short, felt statements - the ones you say when you actually want
the thing.

### ～たい vs ほしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` wants a **noun**; `～たい` wants an **action**.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいパソコン<b>がほしい</b>です。
I want a new computer.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいパソコンが<b>買(か)いたい</b>です。
I want to buy a new computer.
</pre>

`ほしい` is also an い-adjective, and it carries the same first-person restriction
as `たい` - third person needs `ほしがっている`. Its verb-taking cousin,
`～てほしい`, means you want *someone else* to do something:
手伝(てつだ)ってほしい - "I want you to help."

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは日本(にほん)に行(い)きたいです。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは日本(にほん)に行(い)き**たがっています**。
  A flat statement about a third person's desire needs `たがる`, or a hedge like
  `たいそうです`／<code>たいと言(い)っています</code>.

- ❌ 行(い)きたい**じゃない**／行(い)きたい**でした** → ✅ 行(い)き**たくない**／行(い)き**たかった**
  `たい` is an い-adjective. It never takes `じゃない` or `でした`.

- ❌ 食(た)べる**たい** → ✅ 食(た)べ**たい**
  `たい` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.
  食(た)べます → 食(た)べ + たい.

- ❌ コーヒーが**ほしいたい**。 → ✅ コーヒーが<b>飲(の)みたい</b>。／コーヒーが**ほしい**。
  `ほしい` and `たい` do the same job for different word classes. Never stack them.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)、何(なに)が食(た)べたいですか。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、何(なに)がよろしいですか。
  Grammatical, but too direct upward. Asking a superior about their wants calls
  for `よろしいですか` or `なさいますか`.

## Practice

**1.** のどがかわきました。水(みず)が飲(の)____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

水(みず)が飲(の)**みたい**です。

`たい` attaches to the ます-stem: 飲(の)みます → 飲(の)み + たい. The polite
form is just `です` on the end, because <code>飲(の)みたい</code> is an い-adjective.

</details>

**2.** 妹(いもうと)は新(あたら)しい靴(くつ)を（買(か)いたいです・買(か)いたがっています）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

妹(いもうと)は新(あたら)しい靴(くつ)を<b>買(か)いたがっています</b>。

<code>妹(いもうと)</code> is a third person, so plain `たい` in a main clause is out - you cannot assert
someone else's inner state directly. `たがっている` describes the desire as
something you can observe from outside.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I didn't want to go to school yesterday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

きのうは学校(がっこう)に<b>行(い)きたくなかった</b>（です）。

Past negative of an い-adjective: たい → たく → たくない → たくなかった. There is no
❌<code>行(い)きたいじゃなかった</code>. Adding `です` makes it polite without changing the
stem.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 今日(きょう)は映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きたいじゃないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 今日(きょう)は映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)き**たくない**です。

`じゃない` is the negative for nouns and な-adjectives. `～たい` is an い-adjective,
so the negative is built by swapping the final `い` for `くない`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ラーメンが食(た)べたい。
(b) ラーメンを食(た)べたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are correct and a speaker would accept either. (a) puts the spotlight on
*ramen* as the object of the craving - "it's ramen I want", the thing itself. (b)
keeps the sentence built around the action of eating, so it reads a touch more
neutral and descriptive, and it's what you'd reach for in a longer or written
sentence.

The choice narrows once the sentence grows: with material between the object and
the verb, or with a `が`-marked subject already present, only `を` works.

</details>

# ほしい

**Meaning** · want a thing - the speaker's own desire for an object

`ほしい` (kanji 欲(ほ)しい, but usually written in kana at this level) is an
**い-adjective**, not a verb. It does not take an object with `を`; the thing you
want is marked with `が`. And like `～たい`, it reports a want felt from the inside,
so plain `ほしい` covers **your own** wants and questions about the **listener's** - 
never a flat statement about what a third person wants.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (the thing wanted) | Noun + が + ほしい | 車(くるま) → 車(くるま)がほしい |
| Verb (an action you want *from someone*) | て-form + ほしい | 手伝(てつだ)う → 手伝(てつだ)ってほしい |
| Verb (an action you want *to do*) | not possible - use `～たい` | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)みたい |
| Third person's want | Noun + を + ほしがる | 犬(いぬ) → 犬(いぬ)をほしがっている |

Once `ほしい` is in place it conjugates exactly like 高(たか)い or 安(やす)い:

| い-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | + です | ほしいです |
| Negative | ほしい → ほしくない | ほしくない |
| Polite negative | + です／ありません | ほしくないです／ほしくありません |
| Past | ほしい → ほしかった | ほしかった |
| Negative past | ほしい → ほしくなかった | ほしくなかった |
| て-form | ほしい → ほしくて | ほしくて |
| Adverbial | ほしい → ほしく | ほしくなる |
| Before a noun | ほしい + Noun | ほしいもの |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` is an い-adjective. So it is `ほしくない`, never
❌`ほしいじゃない`, and `ほしかった`, never ❌`ほしいでした`. And it never conjugates
like a verb: there is no ❌`ほしみます`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
お金(かね)が<b>ほしい</b>。
I want money.
</pre>

<pre>
もっと時間(じかん)が<b>ほしい</b>です。
I want more time.
</pre>

<pre>
誕生日(たんじょうび)に何(なに)が<b>ほしい</b>ですか。
What do you want for your birthday?
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)は何(なに)も<b>ほしくない</b>。
I don't want anything right now.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころ、自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)が<b>ほしかった</b>。
When I was little I wanted a room of my own.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は前(まえ)から犬(いぬ)を<b>ほしがっています</b>。
My little brother has wanted a dog for ages.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)は急(いそ)ぎませんが、一度(いちど)ゆっくり話(はな)す機会(きかい)が<b>ほしい</b>です。
There's no rush on an answer, but I'd like a chance to talk it through properly.
</pre>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)、明日(あした)の朝(あさ)までに目(め)を通(とお)して<b>ほしい</b>んですが、お願(ねが)いできますか。
I'd like you to look these documents over by tomorrow morning - could I ask you to?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ほしいです` is polite in *form* only. The content is still a bare statement of
appetite, and pointing it at someone above you lands like a child asking for a
present: 部長(ぶちょう)、新(あたら)しいパソコンがほしいです is grammatical and
socially awkward. Upward, you reframe the want as a request or a hedge:

- <b>～がほしいのですが／～がほしいんですが</b> - trails off and lets the listener
  offer. 資料(しりょう)のコピーがほしいんですが…
- <b>～をいただけますか／お願(ねが)いできますか</b> - asks instead of wanting.
- <b>～があると助(たす)かります</b> - "it would help if I had…", the standard
  workplace softener.

The same asymmetry runs upward in questions. 社長(しゃちょう)、何(なに)がほしいですか
is far too direct; offering with いかがですか is what you actually say.

In a shop or a café, `ほしい` is simply the wrong tool. You do not say
これがほしいです to a waiter - you say これをください or これをお願(ねが)いします.
`ほしい` describes your inner state; `ください` performs the request.

Casually, `ほしい` is very common and takes all the usual endings:
ほしいなあ (wistful), ほしい！, ほしいんだけど…. Men and women use it alike. In
writing the kanji 欲(ほ)しい appears. What `ほしい` does **not** have is a
humble register of its own - there is no polite conjugation that makes it suitable
upward, which is why formal Japanese switches word entirely, to
<code>頂(いただ)きたい</code> or <code>お願(ねが)いしたい</code>.

`ほしがる` is an ordinary う-verb (欲(ほ)しがらない, ほしがります, ほしがって).
It describes desire as something you can *see* in someone - the reaching, the
asking, the looking. That outside-observer flavour makes it faintly belittling, so
it is not used about superiors: ❌課長(かちょう)は新(あたら)しい車(くるま)をほしがっています.

## Contrast

### ほしい vs ～たい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` wants a **noun**; `～たい` wants an **action**. They
divide the work; they never stack.

</div>

<pre>
アイスクリーム<b>がほしい</b>。
I want ice cream.
</pre>

<pre>
アイスクリームが<b>食(た)べたい</b>。
I want to eat ice cream.
</pre>

The two are otherwise twins: both are い-adjectives, both mark their target with
`が`, both are barred from flat third-person statements, and both have a
`～がる` form for other people. What `ほしい` cannot do is attach to a verb - 
❌休(やす)むがほしい is not a sentence. If the thing you want is an action you
perform yourself, `～たい` is the only route.

### ほしい vs ～てほしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `Noun + がほしい` wants a **thing**; `Verb-て + ほしい` wants
**someone else to do something**. The person you want it from takes `に`.

</div>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)が<b>ほしい</b>。
I want the instruction manual.
</pre>

<pre>
使(つか)い方(かた)を<b>教(おし)えてほしい</b>。
I want you to show me how to use it.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)に来(き)て<b>ほしくない</b>。
I don't want my mother to come.
</pre>

`～てほしい` is a wish, not a command, so it is gentler than a direct request - but
it is also more personal, and to a superior it presumes. The polite ladder runs
待(ま)ってほしい → 待(ま)っていただきたい → 待(ま)っていただけないでしょうか.
The negative is `～ないでほしい`: 勝手(かって)に決(き)めないでほしい.

### ほしい vs ほしがる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` for me and you; `ほしがる` for him, her and them. The
particle flips too: `が` with `ほしい`, `を` with `ほしがる`.

</div>

<pre>
娘(むすめ)はずっとピアノを<b>ほしがっています</b>。
My daughter has been wanting a piano for a long time.
</pre>

Note the `～ている`. A desire someone is feeling *now* is `ほしがっている`; bare
`ほしがる` reads as a habit - あの子(こ)は友(とも)だちのものを何(なん)でもほしがる.

`ほしがる` is not the only escape. These are often smoother, and safer upward:

- 妹(いもうと)は新(あたら)しい辞書(じしょ)がほしいそうです。 (I hear that…)
- 妹(いもうと)は新(あたら)しい辞書(じしょ)がほしいと言(い)っています。 (she says…)
- 妹(いもうと)は新(あたら)しい辞書(じしょ)がほしいようです。 (it seems…)

<div class="note">

**Note** - the ban is on **plain main-clause statements** only. Inside a modifying
clause, plain `ほしい` about other people is perfectly normal:
資料(しりょう)がほしい人(ひと)は、前(まえ)の机(つくえ)から取(と)ってください。
「Anyone who wants a copy, take one from the desk at the front.」

</div>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)は車(くるま)<b>を</b>ほしいです。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は車(くるま)<b>が</b>ほしいです。
  `ほしい` is an adjective, so it has no direct object. `を` appears only with the
  verb `ほしがる`.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さんは新(あたら)しい車(くるま)がほしいです。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さんは新(あたら)しい車(くるま)を<b>ほしがっています</b>。
  A flat statement about a third person's want needs `ほしがっている`, or a hedge
  like `ほしいそうです`.

- ❌ <b>ほしいじゃない</b>／<b>ほしいでした</b> → ✅ <b>ほしくない</b>／<b>ほしかった</b>
  い-adjective endings, not な-adjective ones.

- ❌ 休(やす)む<b>がほしい</b>。 → ✅ 休(やす)み<b>たい</b>。
  `ほしい` cannot take a verb. Wanting to perform an action is `～たい` territory.

- ❌ （カフェで）これ<b>がほしいです</b>。 → ✅ これを<b>ください</b>。
  Naming your inner state at a server is odd. Ordering is done with `ください` or
  <code>お願(ねが)いします</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 新(あたら)しいカメラ____ほしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しいカメラ<b>が</b>ほしいです。

`ほしい` is an い-adjective, and what it wants is marked with `が`, not `を`. The
English "want a camera" has an object, but the Japanese does not.

</details>

**2.** 兄(あに)はバイクを（ほしいです・ほしがっています）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

兄(あに)はバイクを<b>ほしがっています</b>。

<code>兄(あに)</code> is a third person, so a plain `ほしい` statement would be claiming direct
access to someone else's inner state. `ほしがっている` reports the desire as
something you can observe - and note that it takes `を`, which is why the sentence
was written with `を` to begin with.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When I was a student, I wanted a motorbike."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

学生(がくせい)のとき、バイク<b>がほしかった</b>（です）。

The past of an い-adjective: ほしい → ほしかった. There is no ❌`ほしいでした`; adding
`です` after `ほしかった` is what makes it polite.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 辞書(じしょ)をほしいんですが、いい店(みせ)を知(し)りませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 辞書(じしょ)<b>が</b>ほしいんですが、いい店(みせ)を知(し)りませんか。

The `を` is the interference: English treats "want" as a transitive verb, Japanese
treats `ほしい` as an adjective whose target takes `が`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 静(しず)かにして<b>ほしい</b>。
(b) 静(しず)かにして<b>ください</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a direct request aimed at the listener - it tells them to do something, and
in the wrong context it can sound like an instruction. (a) reports the speaker's
wish instead of issuing the request, so it is indirect, and among friends it comes
across as softer, almost a complaint said out loud.

Indirectness is not the same as politeness upward, though. To a superior neither
is ideal: 静(しず)かにしていただけませんか is the form that actually works.

</details>

# ～のが好き

**Meaning** · like doing something - a standing preference, not a present urge

<code>好(す)き</code> is a **な-adjective** meaning "pleasing, to my taste", and what pleases you
is marked with `が`. English makes "like" a verb with an object; Japanese makes it
an adjective with a subject. To like an *action*, you first turn the verb into a
noun with the nominaliser `の`, then feed it to <code>好(す)き</code>.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary form) | Dictionary + のが + 好(す)き | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)ぐのが好(す)き |
| Verb (alternative nominaliser) | Dictionary + ことが + 好(す)き | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)むことが好(す)き |
| Noun | Noun + が + 好(す)き | 音楽(おんがく) → 音楽(おんがく)が好(す)き |
| Verb (する-noun) | Noun + が + 好(す)き | 料理(りょうり)する → 料理(りょうり)が好(す)き |

<code>好(す)き</code> then behaves like 静(しず)か or 元気(げんき) - a な-adjective in every respect:

| な-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plain | 好(す)きだ | 泳(およ)ぐのが好(す)きだ |
| Polite | 好(す)きです | 泳(およ)ぐのが好(す)きです |
| Negative | 好(す)きじゃない | 泳(およ)ぐのが好(す)きじゃない |
| Polite negative | 好(す)きじゃないです／ではありません | 好(す)きじゃありません |
| Past | 好(す)きだった／好(す)きでした | 好(す)きでした |
| Negative past | 好(す)きじゃなかった | 好(す)きじゃなかったです |
| Before a noun | 好(す)きな + Noun | 好(す)きな歌(うた) |
| Intensified | 大好(だいす)き | 歌(うた)うのが大好(だいす)き |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>好(す)き</code> is an adjective, so there is no ❌<code>好(す)きます</code>,
no ❌<code>好(す)きません</code> and no ❌<code>好(す)きくない</code>. And the verb needs `の`
to become a noun first: ❌<code>読(よ)むが好(す)き</code> → ✅<code>読(よ)むのが好(す)き</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
歌(うた)を<b>歌(うた)うのが好(す)き</b>です。
I like singing.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は<b>走(はし)るのが好(す)き</b>じゃない。
I don't like running.
</pre>

<pre>
どんな音楽(おんがく)を<b>聞(き)くのが好(す)き</b>ですか。
What kind of music do you like listening to?
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は朝(あさ)早(はや)く<b>起(お)きるのが好(す)き</b>です。
My father likes getting up early.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころは絵(え)を<b>かくのが大好(だいす)き</b>でした。
As a child I absolutely loved drawing.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)の日(ひ)に家(いえ)で映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)るのが好(す)き</b>です。
I like watching films at home on a rainy day.
</pre>

<pre>
妻(つま)は週末(しゅうまつ)に市場(いちば)まで歩(ある)いて野菜(やさい)を<b>選(えら)ぶのが好(す)き</b>です。
My wife likes walking to the market at the weekend and picking out the vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
地図(ちず)を見(み)ないで知(し)らない町(まち)を<b>歩(ある)き回(まわ)るのが好(す)き</b>なんです。
The thing is, I love wandering around a town I don't know without looking at a map.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral. <code>好(す)きです</code> is the everyday polite form,
<code>好(す)きだ</code> the plain one, and in casual speech the copula usually vanishes
altogether - <code>甘(あま)いものが好(す)き</code>, with a rising tone for a question.
Upward, お好(す)きですか is the polite version: 部長(ぶちょう)、コーヒーはお好(す)きですか.

One important difference from `～たい`: <code>好(す)き</code> may be used flatly about a third
person. A preference is a settled, observable trait, not a private impulse, so
姉(あね)は山(やま)に登(のぼ)るのが好(す)きです needs no `がる` and no hedge. This is
one of the few places where describing someone else is *easier* than describing
yourself.

Be careful with people as the object. <code>田中(たなか)さんが好(す)きです</code> is a
confession of romantic feeling, not "I get on with Tanaka". If you mean the latter,
say 田中(たなか)さんは、いい人(ひと)ですね or
田中(たなか)さんと話(はな)すのが好(す)きです - attaching the liking to an
activity defuses it.

Finally, negation carries weight. Japanese hedges dislike far more than English
does: あまり好(す)きじゃないです is the polite way to decline food, music or a
suggestion, and it is what a learner should reach for by default.

## Contrast

### ～のが好(す)き vs ～ことが好(す)き

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` for concrete, lived, experienced activity; `こと` for the
activity in the abstract. With <code>好(す)き</code> both are usually fine, and `の` is the
more conversational of the two.

</div>

<pre>
海(うみ)で<b>泳(およ)ぐのが好(す)き</b>です。
I like swimming in the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)に<b>教(おし)えることが好(す)き</b>です。
I like teaching people.
</pre>

The first is the feel of the water; the second is teaching as a category of thing.
Push the difference and you can hear it, but for a plain statement of taste nobody
will correct you either way.

Where the choice stops being free is when the nominalised clause is not the object
of an adjective but a noun in its own right - a `は…です` sentence, for instance.
There only `こと` works:

<pre>
趣味(しゅみ)は古(ふる)い本(ほん)を<b>集(あつ)めること</b>です。
My hobby is collecting old books.
</pre>

❌<code>集(あつ)めるのです</code> would be read as the explanatory `のです`, which means
something else entirely.

### ～のが好(す)き vs ～たい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>好(す)き</code> is what you like **in general**; `～たい` is what you
want **now**. Liking something does not mean you want it this minute.

</div>

<pre>
コーヒーを<b>飲(の)むのが好(す)き</b>です。
I like drinking coffee. (a habit, a taste)
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)、コーヒーが<b>飲(の)みたい</b>です。
I want a coffee right now. (an urge)
</pre>

The two also behave differently across persons. `～たい` cannot be stated flatly
about someone else, but <code>好(す)き</code> can - which is why a description of another
person's tastes almost always comes out in <code>好(す)き</code>:
夫(おっと)は料理(りょうり)するのが好(す)きです.

### 好(す)き vs 嫌(きら)い

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>嫌(きら)い</code> is much stronger than English "don't like". The
everyday negative is あまり好(す)きじゃない.

</div>

<pre>
運動(うんどう)するのは<b>あまり好(す)きじゃない</b>です。
I'm not that keen on exercising.
</pre>

<pre>
虫(むし)が<b>嫌(きら)い</b>です。
I hate insects.
</pre>

Two traps sit here. First, <code>嫌(きら)い</code> ends in `い` but is a **な-adjective**:
<code>嫌(きら)いな食(た)べ物(もの)</code>, <code>嫌(きら)いじゃない</code>, never
❌<code>嫌(きら)くない</code>. Second, notice the `は` in the first sentence. Swapping the
`が` for `は` when you negate is standard, and it softens further - you are marking
this one thing as the exception rather than making a sweeping declaration.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>が</b>好(す)きです。 → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>のが</b>好(す)きです。
  A verb cannot be the subject of an adjective on its own. `の` nominalises it.

- ❌ 音楽(おんがく)<b>を</b>好(す)きです。 → ✅ 音楽(おんがく)<b>が</b>好(す)きです。
  Interference from English. <code>好(す)き</code> is not a verb and has no direct object.

- ❌ ピアノを<b>好(す)きます</b>。 → ✅ ピアノが<b>好(す)きです</b>。
  <code>好(す)き</code> takes adjective endings. A verb <code>好(す)く</code> does exist, but in
  everyday Japanese it survives mainly in the passive (<code>人(ひと)に好(す)かれる</code>).

- ❌ 泳(およ)ぐのが<b>好(す)きくない</b>です。 → ✅ 泳(およ)ぐのが<b>好(す)きじゃない</b>です。
  な-adjective negation, despite the final `き` looking harmless.

- ❌ 好(す)き<b>の</b>食(た)べ物(もの) → ✅ 好(す)き<b>な</b>食(た)べ物(もの)
  Before a noun, a な-adjective takes `な`, not `の`.

## Practice

**1.** 私(わたし)は自転車(じてんしゃ)に乗(の)る____好(す)きです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自転車(じてんしゃ)に乗(の)る<b>のが</b>好(す)きです。

The verb <code>乗(の)る</code> has to become a noun before it can be the subject of
<code>好(す)き</code>, and that is what `の` does. The particle after it is `が`, never `を`.

</details>

**2.** 弟(おとうと)は甘(あま)いものを食(た)べるのが（好(す)きです・食(た)べたいです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弟(おとうと)は甘(あま)いものを食(た)べるのが<b>好(す)きです</b>。

Two reasons. The sentence describes a standing taste rather than a present urge,
and it is about a third person, where a flat `～たい` is not allowed anyway. The
second option would also leave the nominalised clause stranded.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My grandmother likes taking photographs of flowers."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

祖母(そぼ)は花(はな)の写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)るのが好(す)き</b>です。

Note that no hedge is needed even though the subject is a third person: unlike
`～たい`, a preference can be asserted about other people directly.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 兄(あに)はサッカーを好(す)きの人(ひと)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 兄(あに)はサッカーが<b>好(す)きな人(ひと)</b>です。

Two problems in one short sentence. The object of <code>好(す)き</code> takes `が`, not
`を`; and before a noun a な-adjective uses `な`, not `の`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) パクチーは<b>あまり好(す)きじゃない</b>です。
(b) パクチーが<b>嫌(きら)い</b>です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a flat declaration of dislike and sounds surprisingly strong in Japanese - 
closer to "I can't stand coriander" than to "I'm not a fan". (a) is the polite
default: the softener あまり plus a negative, with `は` marking coriander as the one
exception rather than condemning it outright.

If a host offers you something you would rather not eat, (a) is the sentence you
want. (b) risks sounding like a complaint about the food.

</details>

# ～のが上手

**Meaning** · to be good at something - skill you can see in the result

<code>上手(じょうず)</code> is a **な-adjective** describing an ability that has visibly been
trained. What someone is good at is marked with `が`, and to say they are good at
an *action* you nominalise the verb with `の` first. The important thing to learn
alongside the grammar is who you may say it about: <code>上手(じょうず)</code> is a judgement
handed down, so it is used about other people, and almost never about yourself.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary form) | Dictionary + のが + 上手(じょうず) | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)すのが上手(じょうず) |
| Noun | Noun + が + 上手(じょうず) | ピアノ → ピアノが上手(じょうず) |
| Person + thing | Person + は + Thing + が + 上手(じょうず) | 姉(あね)は絵(え)が上手(じょうず)です |
| Adverbially (modifying a verb) | 上手(じょうず)に + Verb | 上手(じょうず)に使(つか)う |

The な-adjective forms behave as expected:

| な-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plain | 上手(じょうず)だ | 歌(うた)うのが上手(じょうず)だ |
| Polite | 上手(じょうず)です | 歌(うた)うのが上手(じょうず)です |
| Negative | 上手(じょうず)じゃない | 上手(じょうず)じゃないです |
| Past | 上手(じょうず)だった／上手(じょうず)でした | 上手(じょうず)でした |
| Before a noun | 上手(じょうず)な + Noun | 上手(じょうず)な人(ひと) |
| Honorific | お上手(じょうず) | お上手(じょうず)ですね |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>上手(じょうず)</code> before a **noun** takes `な`; before a **verb** it
takes `に`. <code>上手(じょうず)な字(じ)</code> "neat handwriting" versus
<code>上手(じょうず)に書(か)く</code> "to write neatly".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
母(はは)は<b>料理(りょうり)を作(つく)るのが上手(じょうず)</b>です。
My mother is good at cooking.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>字(じ)を書(か)くのが上手(じょうず)</b>ですね。
He has lovely handwriting, doesn't he.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)は人(ひと)の名前(なまえ)を<b>覚(おぼ)えるのが上手(じょうず)</b>じゃないです。
My older brother isn't good at remembering people's names.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは人(ひと)の話(はなし)を<b>聞(き)くのが上手(じょうず)</b>です。
Tanaka is a good listener.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)はまだ五(ご)さいですが、自転車(じてんしゃ)に<b>乗(の)るのが上手(じょうず)</b>です。
My son is only five, but he's good at riding a bicycle.
</pre>

<pre>
娘(むすめ)は小(ちい)さいころから絵(え)を<b>上手(じょうず)に</b>かきました。
My daughter drew well from the time she was small.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんは会議(かいぎ)で話(はなし)を<b>まとめるのが上手(じょうず)</b>なので、いつも司会(しかい)をお願(ねが)いしています。
Yamada is good at pulling a discussion together, so we always ask her to chair.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は昔(むかし)から着物(きもの)を<b>着(き)るのが上手(じょうず)</b>で、近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)にもよく教(おし)えています。
My grandmother has always been good at putting on a kimono, and she often teaches the neighbours too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>上手(じょうず)</code> is a **verdict**. You are standing outside someone's performance and
rating it, and that is exactly why the word behaves the way it does socially.

First, you do not normally use it about yourself. ❌私(わたし)は英語(えいご)が上手(じょうず)です
is grammatical and sounds boastful; the natural equivalents are
英語(えいご)が得意(とくい)です, 英語(えいご)が少(すこ)し話(はな)せます, or the
modest classic まだまだです.

Second - and this catches learners going the other way - praising a **superior**
with <code>上手(じょうず)</code> is also awkward, because rating someone above you is not your
place. 部長(ぶちょう)、英語(えいご)がお上手(じょうず)ですね is softened by the
honorific `お`, and is heard, but さすがですね or
勉強(べんきょう)になります is safer. Between equals and downward it is completely
normal.

Third, when someone says
日本語(にほんご)がお上手(じょうず)ですね to you, the expected reply is not ありがとうございます
but a deflection: いいえ、まだまだです or そんなことないです. Accepting the compliment
flatly is not wrong, just noticeably un-Japanese.

<code>お上手(じょうず)ですね</code> said with the wrong timing means "you flatterer" - 
お上手(じょうず)を言(い)って is a fixed phrase for exactly that. Context carries it,
but be aware the word has that second life.

## Contrast

### ～のが上手(じょうず) vs ～のが得意(とくい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>上手(じょうず)</code> rates someone **else**; <code>得意(とくい)</code> is how
you talk about **yourself**. <code>得意(とくい)</code> also carries confidence, not just skill.

</div>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんはギターを<b>弾(ひ)くのが上手(じょうず)</b>です。
Hayashi is good at playing the guitar.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は人(ひと)の前(まえ)で<b>話(はな)すのが得意(とくい)</b>です。
I'm good at speaking in front of people.
</pre>

The difference is where the judgement comes from. <code>上手(じょうず)</code> is an outside
assessment of quality, so turning it on yourself is self-congratulation.
<code>得意(とくい)</code> describes your own sense of what you are strong at and can be
asserted without immodesty - it is what you write on a job application.

Note that <code>得意(とくい)</code> is not a full escape either: said too readily it still
sounds confident, so many speakers reach for
少(すこ)しできます or a plain potential form instead.

<code>得意(とくい)</code> about a third person is fine too, but it then reports *their*
confidence: 弟(おとうと)は数学(すうがく)が得意(とくい)です suggests he would say so
himself, whereas 弟(おとうと)は数学(すうがく)が上手(じょうず)です is your opinion of
him - and for school subjects it sounds odd, because <code>上手(じょうず)</code> wants a
performed skill.

### 上手(じょうず) vs できる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>上手(じょうず)</code> is *how well*; できる is *whether at all*.

</div>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)はスペイン語(ご)が<b>できます</b>。
My little sister can speak Spanish.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)はスペイン語(ご)を話(はな)すのが<b>上手(じょうず)</b>です。
My little sister speaks Spanish well.
</pre>

The first says the ability exists; the second grades it. This is why できる and the
potential form are the safe way to describe **your own** abilities - 
少(すこ)し泳(およ)げます reports a fact, while
泳(およ)ぐのが上手(じょうず)です awards yourself a prize.

They also differ in what they accept. できる takes nouns and する-nouns
(運転(うんてん)ができる) and, with `ことが`, verbs. <code>上手(じょうず)</code> takes nouns
and `のが` clauses, and refuses anything that is not a trainable skill:
❌背(せ)が高(たか)いのが上手(じょうず)です is nonsense.

### 上手(じょうず) vs うまい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, lower register. うまい is casual, slightly
male-coded, and ambiguous with "tasty".

</div>

<pre>
あいつ、歌(うた)<b>うまい</b>な。
Wow, he's a good singer.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)はテニスが<b>お上手(じょうず)</b>ですね。
You're good at tennis, aren't you, sensei.
</pre>

うまい is an い-adjective (上手(うま)い in kanji, though usually kana), so it takes
うまくない, うまかった - a different paradigm from <code>上手(じょうず)</code> entirely. It
belongs to friends, sport and the internet, never to a business email.

Its other meaning is "delicious", which is where the ambiguity bites: この店(みせ)、うまい
in a restaurant is about the food, not the chef's technique. And the negative
下手(へた) has no equivalent slot here - the casual opposite of うまい is
へた or へたくそ, which is blunter still.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)が<b>上手(じょうず)です</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)が<b>得意(とくい)です</b>。／少(すこ)し話(はな)せます。
  Grammatical but boastful. <code>上手(じょうず)</code> is a verdict on someone else.

- ❌ ギター<b>を</b>上手(じょうず)です。 → ✅ ギター<b>が</b>上手(じょうず)です。
  <code>上手(じょうず)</code> is an adjective and has no direct object.

- ❌ 泳(およ)ぐ<b>が</b>上手(じょうず)です。 → ✅ 泳(およ)ぐ<b>のが</b>上手(じょうず)です。
  A verb needs the nominaliser `の` before `が`.

- ❌ 上手(じょうず)<b>に</b>人(ひと) → ✅ 上手(じょうず)<b>な</b>人(ひと)
  `に` modifies verbs, `な` modifies nouns.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は上手(じょうず)<b>します</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は上手(じょうず)<b>です</b>。／上手(じょうず)に<b>します</b>。
  <code>上手(じょうず)</code> is not a verb; it needs the copula, or `に` if it is describing how
  something is done.

## Practice

**1.** 姉(あね)はケーキを作(つく)る____上手(じょうず)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ケーキを作(つく)る<b>のが</b>上手(じょうず)です。

The verb <code>作(つく)る</code> must be nominalised by `の` before it can take `が` and be
graded by <code>上手(じょうず)</code>.

</details>

**2.** 面接(めんせつ)で：「私(わたし)はコンピューターが（上手(じょうず)です・得意(とくい)です）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)はコンピューターが<b>得意(とくい)です</b>。

Talking about your own strengths calls for <code>得意(とくい)</code>. <code>上手(じょうず)</code> is an
evaluation you hand to someone else, so applying it to yourself in an interview
would sound like bragging rather than reporting.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My grandfather is good at growing vegetables."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

祖父(そふ)は野菜(やさい)を<b>育(そだ)てるのが上手(じょうず)</b>です。

A third person, a trainable skill, and a verb that needs `のが` - the textbook shape
for this pattern. <code>野菜(やさい)が上手(じょうず)です</code> on its own would not work,
because it is the growing, not the vegetables, that is skilled.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)はピアノを上手(じょうず)に人(ひと)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)はピアノ<b>が</b>上手(じょうず)<b>な</b>人(ひと)です。

Two errors. The thing you are good at takes `が`, not `を`; and before the noun
<code>人(ひと)</code> the な-adjective must be <code>上手(じょうず)な</code>, since `に` is the form
that attaches to verbs.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山田(やまだ)さんは英語(えいご)が<b>上手(じょうず)</b>です。
(b) 山田(やまだ)さんは英語(えいご)が<b>できます</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) reports a plain fact: Yamada has English at her disposal. It says nothing about
how good she is, and it is the neutral thing to write in an email about who can
handle a foreign client.

(a) grades her, and grading is a social act. It is warm and complimentary between
equals or from above, but if Yamada outranks you it can read as presumptuous - 
さすがですね avoids the problem.

</details>

# ～のが下手

**Meaning** · to be bad at something - a lack of skill, plainly stated

<code>下手(へた)</code> is a **な-adjective**, the mirror image of <code>上手(じょうず)</code>. What you
are bad at is marked with `が`, and an *action* has to be nominalised with `の`
first. Because it is a blunt verdict, it is comfortable about yourself - where it
reads as modesty - and uncomfortable about anyone else.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary form) | Dictionary + のが + 下手(へた) | 踊(おど)る → 踊(おど)るのが下手(へた) |
| Noun | Noun + が + 下手(へた) | テニス → テニスが下手(へた) |
| Person + thing | Person + は + Thing + が + 下手(へた) | 私(わたし)は絵(え)が下手(へた)です |
| Adverbially (modifying a verb) | 下手(へた)に + Verb | 下手(へた)に動(うご)く |

| な-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plain | 下手(へた)だ | 踊(おど)るのが下手(へた)だ |
| Polite | 下手(へた)です | 踊(おど)るのが下手(へた)です |
| Negative | 下手(へた)じゃない | 下手(へた)じゃないです |
| Past | 下手(へた)だった／下手(へた)でした | 下手(へた)でした |
| Before a noun | 下手(へた)な + Noun | 下手(へた)な絵(え) |
| Softened | あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない | あまり上手(じょうず)じゃないです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>下手(へた)</code> ends in `た`, not `い`, so nothing about it is an
い-adjective: ❌<code>下手(へた)くない</code>, ❌<code>下手(へた)かった</code>. It is
<code>下手(へた)じゃない</code> and <code>下手(へた)だった</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)は<b>泳(およ)ぐのが下手(へた)</b>です。
I'm a bad swimmer.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの父(ちち)は<b>運転(うんてん)が下手(へた)</b>だ。
My dad is a bad driver.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)を<b>片付(かたづ)けるのが下手(へた)</b>です。
I'm bad at tidying my room.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)は人(ひと)の前(まえ)で<b>話(はな)すのが下手(へた)</b>でした。
I used to be bad at speaking in front of people.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はまだはしを<b>使(つか)うのが下手(へた)</b>です。
My little brother still isn't good with chopsticks.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)はサッカーは上手(じょうず)ですが、テニスは<b>下手(へた)</b>です。
My older brother is good at football but bad at tennis.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は<b>断(ことわ)るのが下手(へた)</b>なので、いつも仕事(しごと)が多(おお)すぎます。
I'm bad at saying no, so I always end up with too much work.
</pre>

<pre>
字(じ)が<b>下手(へた)</b>ですから、メールで送(おく)ってもいいですか。
My handwriting is terrible, so would it be all right if I sent it by email?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Pointed at yourself, <code>下手(へた)</code> is safe and even likeable. Japanese conversation
runs on modesty, and 料理(りょうり)が下手(へた)なんです is a normal, friendly thing
to say about your own cooking. There is no need to soften it.

Pointed at anyone else it is a judgement, and a harsh one. Telling a colleague
英語(えいご)が下手(へた)ですね is close to an insult in English terms. The two
standard repairs are あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない (a hedged negative) and
苦手(にがて) (a weakness rather than a failing) - and about a superior you simply do
not go there at all.

<code>下手(へた)くそ</code> exists and is much cruder; it is what one friend shouts at
another over a video game, not something to try on strangers. Note also that unlike
<code>上手(じょうず)</code>, <code>下手(へた)</code> has no polite お-form in ordinary use - there is
nothing to be honorific about.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>下手(へた)に</code> before a verb has drifted away from "badly". It means
*carelessly, without thinking, rashly*:
下手(へた)に動(うご)くと危(あぶ)ないです「Move carelessly and you'll get hurt」.
The literal "does it badly" sense is usually carried by
下手(へた)な絵(え) or あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない instead.

</div>

## Contrast

### 下手(へた) vs 苦手(にがて)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>下手(へた)</code> is about **skill**; <code>苦手(にがて)</code> is about how
you **feel** about it. <code>苦手(にがて)</code> covers things you have no skill in at all - 
food, insects, particular people.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)は歌(うた)を<b>歌(うた)うのが下手(へた)</b>です。
I'm a bad singer.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)はカラオケが<b>苦手(にがて)</b>です。
I'm not keen on karaoke.
</pre>

The first is a claim about your voice. The second says karaoke makes you
uncomfortable - perhaps because you cannot sing, perhaps because you hate being
looked at. That extra emotional layer is why <code>苦手(にがて)</code> reaches places
<code>下手(へた)</code> cannot: なっとうが苦手(にがて)です and
あの人(ひと)は少(すこ)し苦手(にがて)です are both natural, while
❌なっとうが下手(へた)です is nonsense - you do not perform natto.

<code>苦手(にがて)</code> is also the kinder word about other people, because "he finds it
hard" is gentler than "he is bad at it": 山田(やまだ)さんは数字(すうじ)が苦手(にがて)みたいです.

### 下手(へた) vs あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - about **yourself**, <code>下手(へた)</code>. About **other people**, the
hedged negative あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)は<b>下手(へた)</b>ですが、やってみます。
I'm bad at this, but I'll give it a go.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は料理(りょうり)が<b>あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない</b>です。
He isn't a very good cook.
</pre>

The hedge does two things at once. あまり plus a negative is the standard Japanese
way of saying "not very", and phrasing the criticism as a *shortfall of skill*
rather than *presence of badness* takes the sting out. Add みたいです or
ようです and it softens again, because you are now reporting an impression rather
than passing sentence.

The asymmetry is the same one that governs <code>上手(じょうず)</code>, run in reverse: you
praise other people and criticise yourself, never the other way round.

### Noun が下手(へた) vs Verb のが下手(へた)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if a single noun names the skill, use it bare. Reach for `のが`
when the skill only exists as an action.

</div>

<pre>
姉(あね)はピアノが<b>下手(へた)</b>です。
My older sister is bad at the piano.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)はピアノを<b>人(ひと)の前(まえ)で弾(ひ)くのが下手(へた)</b>です。
My older sister is bad at playing the piano in front of an audience.
</pre>

Nouns that already name an activity - ピアノ, テニス, 料理(りょうり),
運転(うんてん) - go straight in with `が`. Adding <code>を弾(ひ)くのが</code> is not wrong,
it just spells the activity out, which is what you want as soon as the description
gets more specific than the noun can carry.

And some skills have no noun at all. There is no single word for "remembering
people's names" or "saying no", so the verb clause is the only route:
人(ひと)の名前(なまえ)を覚(おぼ)えるのが下手(へた)です. What you cannot do is
drop the `の` and hand the raw verb to the adjective.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ なっとうが<b>下手(へた)</b>です。 → ✅ なっとうが<b>苦手(にがて)</b>です。
  Food is not a skill. <code>苦手(にがて)</code> is the word for things you would rather avoid.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは英語(えいご)が<b>下手(へた)</b>ですね。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは英語(えいご)が<b>あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない</b>ですね。
  Direct <code>下手(へた)</code> about another person is rude. Hedge it, or use
  <code>苦手(にがて)</code>.

- ❌ 踊(おど)る<b>が</b>下手(へた)です。 → ✅ 踊(おど)る<b>のが</b>下手(へた)です。
  The verb has to be nominalised by `の` before it can take `が`.

- ❌ 下手(へた)<b>くない</b>です。 → ✅ 下手(へた)<b>じゃない</b>です。
  <code>下手(へた)</code> is a な-adjective. The final `た` is part of the reading, not an
  adjective ending.

- ❌ 下手(へた)<b>の</b>絵(え) → ✅ 下手(へた)<b>な</b>絵(え)
  な-adjectives take `な` before a noun.

## Practice

**1.** 私(わたし)は人(ひと)に道(みち)を説明(せつめい)する____下手(へた)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

道(みち)を説明(せつめい)する<b>のが</b>下手(へた)です。

<code>説明(せつめい)する</code> is a verb, so `の` turns it into something `が` can mark. You
could also say <code>説明(せつめい)が下手(へた)です</code>, using the noun on its own.

</details>

**2.** 課長(かちょう)に：「部長(ぶちょう)はゴルフが（下手(へた)です・あまり上手(じょうず)じゃないです）ね。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)はゴルフがあまり<b>上手(じょうず)じゃないです</b>ね。

Even this is risky about a superior, but of the two it is the survivable one. A
flat <code>下手(へた)</code> about someone above you is a straight insult; the hedged
negative reads as an observation rather than a verdict.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm bad at getting up early."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)は朝(あさ)早(はや)く<b>起(お)きるのが下手(へた)</b>です。

About yourself, <code>下手(へた)</code> is the modest, natural choice. Many speakers would
also say <code>苦手(にがて)です</code> here, which adds "and I dislike it" to the meaning.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 私(わたし)は歌(うた)が下手(へた)くなかったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 私(わたし)は歌(うた)が<b>下手(へた)じゃなかった</b>です。

<code>下手(へた)</code> only looks like an い-adjective. It is a な-adjective, so the
negative past is <code>じゃなかった</code>, exactly as with <code>静(しず)かじゃなかった</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)は漢字(かんじ)を書(か)くのが<b>下手(へた)</b>です。
(b) 私(わたし)は漢字(かんじ)が<b>苦手(にがて)</b>です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a statement about the output: your kanji come out badly formed. It says
nothing about how you feel while writing them.

(b) is about your relationship with kanji - they are your weak point, you avoid
them, tests involving them make you nervous. It may or may not imply that your
handwriting is poor, and it is the sentence a learner is usually reaching for when
they want to say "kanji are my nemesis".

</details>
