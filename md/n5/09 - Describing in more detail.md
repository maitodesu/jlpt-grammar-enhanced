# N5 - Describing in more detail

# の - of; possessive

**Meaning** · links two nouns, with the first one describing or owning the second

`の` is the glue between nouns. 私(わたし)の本(ほん) is "my book",
日本語(にほんご)の本(ほん) is "a Japanese-language book", 木(き)の机(つくえ) is
"a wooden desk" - ownership is only the most obvious of a much wider set of
relationships.

## Formation

The rule is one-directional and never varies: **the modifier comes first, the
head noun comes last.** Whatever `の` points at, the thing being described is
always on the right.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun + Noun | N1 の N2 | 私(わたし) + かばん → 私(わたし)のかばん |
| Chained | N1 の N2 の N3 | 友(とも)だちの車(くるま)のかぎ |
| Apposition ("N1, who is N2") | N1 の N2 | 友(とも)だちのミラーさん |
| Material or origin | N1 の N2 | 木(き)の机(つくえ)／イタリアのくつ |
| Standing in for the noun | N1 の (nothing after) | この傘(かさ)は姉(あね)の**です** |
| After another particle | N + particle + の + N | 友人(ゆうじん)**から**の手紙(てがみ) |
| ❌ な-adjective | takes `な`, not `の` | ❌きれいの花(はな) → ✅きれい**な**花(はな) |
| ❌ い-adjective | attaches bare | ❌大(おお)きいの家(いえ) → ✅大(おお)きい家(いえ) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` joins a **noun** to a noun. If the word in front of it is
an adjective, `の` is wrong: い-adjectives attach directly, な-adjectives take
`な`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは私(わたし)<b>の</b>自転車(じてんしゃ)です。
This is my bicycle.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>の</b>会社(かいしゃ)は東京(とうきょう)にあります。
Mr Tanaka's company is in Tokyo.
</pre>

<pre>
この傘(かさ)は姉(あね)<b>の</b>です。
This umbrella is my sister's.
</pre>

<pre>
イタリア語(ご)<b>の</b>本(ほん)を三(さん)冊(さつ)借(か)りました。
I borrowed three Italian-language books.
</pre>

<pre>
友(とも)だち<b>の</b>ミラーさんが来週(らいしゅう)遊(あそ)びに来(き)ます。
My friend Miller is coming to visit next week.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)<b>の</b>近(ちか)くにおいしいラーメン屋(や)があります。
There's a good ramen place near the office.
</pre>

<pre>
これは母(はは)が焼(や)いたパンで、あちらは店(みせ)<b>の</b>です。
This is bread my mother baked; that one over there is from the shop.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)時代(じだい)<b>の</b>友人(ゆうじん)から<b>の</b>手紙(てがみ)が、久(ひさ)しぶりに届(とど)きました。
A letter arrived from a university friend for the first time in ages.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`の` is completely register-neutral - the single most common word in Japanese
text, and invisible in the way that English "of" and "'s" are invisible.

Three things worth knowing beyond the basic rule:

**`の` is vague on purpose.** 山田(やまだ)さんの本(ほん) can mean the book Yamada
owns, the book Yamada wrote, the book Yamada is holding, or the book about
Yamada. Japanese does not distinguish these, and context does all the work. Do
not go looking for a more precise particle; there isn't one.

**`の` can stand in for a noun already understood.**
これは私(わたし)のです - "this is mine." The noun has been deleted, not the `の`.
This is why 赤(あか)いの ("the red one") looks similar but is a different `の`:
there, `の` is standing in for a whole noun after an adjective rather than
marking a possessor.

**Chains get heavy fast.** 会社(かいしゃ)の近(ちか)くの駅(えき)の前(まえ)の店(みせ) is grammatical and no Japanese speaker would say it. Two `の` in a row
is ordinary; three starts to strain; four means you should rebuild the sentence.

**Contracted `ん` in speech.** In fast casual speech `の` sometimes reduces:
俺(おれ)んち ("my place", from 俺(おれ)の家(うち)), 君(きみ)んとこ. These are
fixed, informal, and mostly limited to places and homes; do not generalise the
pattern.

## Contrast

### の vs な

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` after a **noun**, `な` after a **な-adjective**. The
words look alike in the dictionary, which is the whole problem.

</div>

Many な-adjectives - きれい, 有名(ゆうめい), 元気(げんき), 便利(べんり) - feel like
nouns to a learner, and some of them genuinely can be nouns in other contexts.
When they are describing the following noun, they take `な`.

<pre>
有名(ゆうめい)<b>な</b>店(みせ)に行(い)きました。
I went to a famous shop.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)<b>の</b>店(みせ)に行(い)きました。
I went to a shop in Tokyo.
</pre>

<code>東京(とうきょう)</code> is a noun, so it takes `の`; <code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is
a な-adjective, so it takes `な`. ❌有名(ゆうめい)の店(みせ) and
❌東京(とうきょう)な店(みせ) are both wrong, and the second one is instantly
noticeable to a native ear.

The awkward middle case is <code>病気(びょうき)</code> and <code>元気(げんき)</code>, which
go both ways depending on what you mean: 元気(げんき)な子(こ) = "a lively
child"; 病気(びょうき)の子(こ) = "a child who is ill" (the noun "illness"
describing them).

### の (linking nouns) vs の (the nominaliser)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the word in front of `の` is a **noun**, it is this `の`.
If it is a **verb or a clause**, it is the nominaliser - a different job
entirely.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>の</b>趣味(しゅみ)は写真(しゃしん)です。
My hobby is photography. (noun + の - possession)
</pre>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)をとる<b>の</b>が好(す)きです。
I like taking photographs. (verb + の - the verb has been made into a noun)
</pre>

The two never compete for the same slot, so once you can see whether the word in
front is a noun or a verb, the ambiguity disappears. Both are extremely common
and you will use them in the same conversation.

### AのB vs BのA

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the **head noun goes last**, always. English puts it first
("a map *of* Tokyo") and second ("Tokyo*'s* map"), which is exactly why learners
get the order backwards.

</div>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)<b>の</b>地図(ちず)を買(か)いました。
I bought a map of Tokyo.
</pre>

<pre>
地図(ちず)<b>の</b>東京(とうきょう)
the Tokyo that belongs to the map - not a thing anyone would say
</pre>

The test that always works: **what am I actually talking about?** In "a map of
Tokyo" you are talking about a map, so 地図(ちず) goes at the end. In "the
teacher's car" you are talking about a car, so 車(くるま) goes at the end:
先生(せんせい)の車(くるま).

## Common mistakes

- ❌ きれい**の**花(はな)をもらいました。 → ✅ きれい**な**花(はな)をもらいました。
  きれい is a な-adjective despite ending in い. It takes `な`, never `の`.

- ❌ 大(おお)きい**の**家(いえ)に住(す)んでいます。 → ✅ 大(おお)きい家(いえ)に住(す)んでいます。
  い-adjectives attach straight to the noun. Nothing goes in between.

- ❌ 車(くるま)**の**私(わたし)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)**の**車(くるま)です。
  The owner comes first and the thing owned comes last. Reversing them reverses
  the meaning, or produces nonsense.

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)先生(せんせい)に聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)**の**先生(せんせい)に聞(き)きました。
  Two nouns in a row need `の` to link them. (Some pairs have fused into single
  compound words over time, but you cannot invent new ones.)

- ❌ これは私(わたし)のかばん**の**です。 → ✅ これは私(わたし)のかばんです。
  `の` stands in for a missing noun. When the noun is right there, a second `の`
  has nothing to do.

## Practice

**1.** これは田中(たなか)さん____傘(かさ)ですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

これは田中(たなか)さん**の**傘(かさ)ですか。

Two nouns side by side always need `の` between them. Note that the answer could
also drop the second noun entirely - これは田中(たなか)さん**の**ですか - because
`の` can stand in for a noun both speakers already have in mind.

</details>

**2.** 静(しず)か（の・な）部屋(へや)をお願(ねが)いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

静(しず)か**な**部屋(へや)をお願(ねが)いします。

<code>静(しず)か</code> is a な-adjective, so it links to the following noun with
`な`. `の` is for nouns only, and the difference is audible - ❌静(しず)かの部屋(へや) marks a
speaker as a beginner faster than almost any other error.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "That's my mother's friend's dog."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あれは母(はは)**の**友(とも)だち**の**犬(いぬ)です。

Chained `の` runs in the same order as the English possessives - mother, then
friend, then dog - with the thing you are actually talking about at the very end.
Two `の` in a row like this is perfectly ordinary.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 有名(ゆうめい)の人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 有名(ゆうめい)**な**人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。

<code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is a な-adjective. The `の` here comes from thinking of it
as a noun meaning "fame", which it is not in this sentence - it is describing the
person, so it needs `な`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日本(にほん)の車(くるま)を買(か)いました。
(b) 車(くるま)の日本(にほん)を買(か)いました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a normal sentence: 日本(にほん) describes 車(くるま), so this is "I
bought a Japanese car." The head noun - the thing that was bought - sits at the
end.

(b) reverses the roles, making 車(くるま) the description and 日本(にほん) the
thing bought: "I bought the Japan that belongs to a car." It is not ambiguous or
poetic, just broken. The lesson is that in Japanese the order carries all the
meaning; there is no second particle to fall back on.

</details>

# の - nominaliser

**Meaning** · turns a verb or a whole clause into a noun, so it can be a subject or an object

Japanese has no infinitive and no "-ing" noun. To say "swimming is fun" or "I
forgot to lock the door", you take the clause you want and stick `の` on the end
of it. What comes out behaves like an ordinary noun and can take `が`, `を` or
`は`.

## Formation

`の` attaches to the **plain form** of whatever is in front of it. Nouns and
な-adjectives need `な` first - the same shape as `ので` and `のに`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | dictionary form + の | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)ぐの |
| Verb (past) | た-form + の | 会(あ)った → 会(あ)ったの |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + の | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないの |
| い-adjective | plain form + の | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いの |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + の | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)か**な**の |
| Noun | Noun + **な** + の | 学生(がくせい)だ → 学生(がくせい)**な**の |
| A whole clause | clause + の | 彼(かれ)が来(こ)なかったの |
| Before です／だ | の → **ん** | 行(い)くのです → 行(い)く**ん**です |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - never ❌`だの`. A noun or な-adjective takes `な` before the
nominalising `の`, exactly as it does before `ので`.

</div>

Once nominalised, the phrase takes a case particle like any noun:
泳(およ)ぐの**が**好(す)きです、閉(し)めるの**を**忘(わす)れた、買(か)うの**は**やめた。

## Examples

<pre>
泳(およ)ぐ<b>の</b>が好(す)きです。
I like swimming.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)える<b>の</b>は難(むずか)しい。
Memorising kanji is hard.
</pre>

<pre>
電気(でんき)を消(け)す<b>の</b>を忘(わす)れました。
I forgot to turn off the light.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが公園(こうえん)で遊(あそ)んでいる<b>の</b>を見(み)ました。
I saw children playing in the park.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)早(はや)く起(お)きる<b>の</b>は苦手(にがて)です。
I'm no good at getting up early.
</pre>

<pre>
隣(となり)の部屋(へや)で誰(だれ)かが歌(うた)っている<b>の</b>が聞(き)こえた。
I could hear someone singing in the next room.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)が会社(かいしゃ)をやめた<b>の</b>を知(し)っていますか。
Did you know she'd quit the company?
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)の日(ひ)に自転車(じてんしゃ)で通勤(つうきん)する<b>の</b>は、思(おも)っていたより大変(たいへん)でした。
Commuting by bike on rainy days turned out to be harder than I'd expected.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The nominaliser `の` is register-neutral, but it leans **spoken and concrete**.
Its rival `こと` leans written and abstract, and the two are treated in the
Contrast section below.

The thing worth real attention here is the `ん` contraction, because it is
everywhere and it changes the feel of a sentence completely.

**`の` becomes `ん` before `です` and `だ`.** 行(い)くのです → 行(い)くんです.
The `のです` form is the written or careful-speech version; `んです` is what you
actually hear.

**`んです` is not decoration.** It frames the sentence as an *explanation* - the
speaker is accounting for something, or asking the listener to account for
something.

- 頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い**です**。 - "I have a headache." A fact, offered flat.
- 頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い**んです**。 - "The thing is, I have a headache." Now it
  explains why you are leaving early.
- どうした**んですか**。 - "What's the matter?" Asking for an explanation. The bare
  ❌どうしましたか is possible but noticeably colder.

A learner who never uses `んです` sounds like they are reading a list; one who
uses it in every sentence sounds like they are constantly justifying themselves.
It belongs where there is a *reason* in the air.

The plain-form version is `んだ`: 明日(あした)、試験(しけん)なんだ。 Note the `な`
survives the contraction - noun + `な` + `んだ`.

Finally, a sentence-final `の` with rising intonation is a soft question, and in
Tokyo speech it is marked as feminine or as child-directed:
どうしたの? 食(た)べないの? Men use it too, but usually with a following `か` or
in a gentler register.

## Contrast

### の vs こと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` for things you directly see, hear and experience; `こと`
for abstractions, facts and reported information. Where either works, `の` is the
spoken choice.

</div>

Three cases where the choice is **not** free:

**Verbs of direct perception require `の`.** 見(み)る、聞(き)く、感(かん)じる and friends are watching an event happen, not contemplating a fact.

<pre>
猫(ねこ)が走(はし)っている<b>の</b>を見(み)ました。
I saw the cat running. (I watched it happen - の)
</pre>

**Fixed expressions require `こと`.** <code>ことができる</code>,
<code>ことがある</code>, <code>ことにする</code>, <code>ことになる</code> are set
phrases and admit no substitution: ❌泳(およ)ぐのができる.

**`X は Y です` equations require `こと`.** 趣味(しゅみ)は本(ほん)を読(よ)むことです。 With `の` this sounds like the hobby *is* the act happening in front of
you.

Everywhere else - 泳(およ)ぐの／ことが好(す)きです - both are correct, and `の`
is what you will hear.

### の (nominaliser) vs の ("the one")

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a **verb or clause**, `の` makes an action into a noun.
After an **adjective**, `の` usually stands in for a concrete thing: "the ~ one".

</div>

<pre>
走(はし)る<b>の</b>が速(はや)いですね。
You're fast at running. (の = the act of running)
</pre>

<pre>
赤(あか)い<b>の</b>をください。
I'll take the red one. (の = the object itself)
</pre>

In practice the difference is decided by what makes sense, and a clause can go
either way: 昨日(きのう)買(か)ったの can mean "the one I bought yesterday" or
"the fact that I bought it yesterday". Context settles it, and native speakers
do not experience this as ambiguous.

### ～のです／んです vs the plain statement

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `んです` says "here is the explanation". A plain `です`
sentence just states the fact.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)、東京(とうきょう)へ行(い)き<b>ます</b>。
I'm going to Tokyo tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)、東京(とうきょう)へ行(い)く<b>んです</b>。
I'm going to Tokyo tomorrow, you see. (…which is why I can't come / why I'm packing)
</pre>

Get this wrong in either direction and you sound odd. Answering
何(なに)をしていますか with a bare fact is fine; answering どうして来(こ)なかったんですか without `んです` in your own reply sounds evasive, because the question
explicitly asked for a reason.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)を話(はな)す**のができます**。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)を話(はな)す**ことができます**。
  <code>ことができる</code> is a fixed expression. `の` cannot be substituted into
  it, however natural the rest of the sentence sounds.

- ❌ 静(しず)か**の**が好(す)きです。 → ✅ 静(しず)か**なの**が好(す)きです。
  な-adjectives take `な` before the nominaliser, exactly as they do before `ので`
  and `のに`.

- ❌ 猫(ねこ)が鳴(な)いている**こと**を聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 猫(ねこ)が鳴(な)いている**の**を聞(き)きました。
  With `こと` this becomes "I heard *that* the cat was crying" - secondhand news.
  Direct perception needs `の`.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)**の**んです。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)**な**んです。
  A noun keeps its `な` when `の` contracts to `ん`. ❌<code>学生(がくせい)のんです</code> and
  ❌<code>学生(がくせい)だんです</code> are both wrong.

- ❌ 窓(まど)を閉(し)める**の**忘(わす)れた。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を閉(し)める**のを**忘(わす)れた。
  Once you have made a noun, it needs a particle like any other noun. (`を` does
  drop in fast speech, but learn the full form first.)

## Practice

**1.** 毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)る____は気持(きも)ちがいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)る**の**は気持(きも)ちがいいです。

<code>走(はし)る</code> is a verb, and the sentence needs a subject for
<code>気持(きも)ちがいい</code> - so the verb has to become a noun first. `こと`
would also be grammatical here; `の` is the more spoken choice.

</details>

**2.** 私(わたし)はギターを弾(ひ)く（の・こと）ができます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)はギターを弾(ひ)く**こと**ができます。

<code>ことができる</code> is a set phrase for ability, and its `こと` is frozen in
place. This is one of the small number of contexts where `の` and `こと` are not
interchangeable at all.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I saw him get on the train."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)が電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)る**の**を見(み)ました。

Direct perception - you watched it with your own eyes - so this must be `の`, not
`こと`. Note that the subject inside the nominalised clause takes `が`, never `は`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)、試験(しけん)だのです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、試験(しけん)**な**のです。 (spoken: 試験(しけん)**なんです**)

A noun takes `な` before the nominaliser, never `だ`. The same `な` is what
survives into the contracted `んです`, which is why you hear
<code>試験(しけん)なんです</code> and never ❌試験(しけん)だんです.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 田中(たなか)さんは来(き)ません。
(b) 田中(たなか)さんは来(こ)ないんです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports that Tanaka is not coming. It is the right sentence if someone
asked you for the guest list.

(b) presents the same fact as the explanation for something - perhaps why there
is a spare chair, or why the meeting is being moved. `んです` always points at a
surrounding situation the fact is accounting for, and using it with no such
situation in view makes the listener hunt for one.

</details>

# Noun-modifying clauses

**Meaning** · put a whole clause in front of a noun to describe it - no relative pronoun, no word order change

English says "the book **that** I bought yesterday", moving the noun to the front
and inserting a relative pronoun. Japanese says 昨日(きのう)買(か)った本(ほん) - 
clause first, noun last, nothing inserted. This one structure is the key to
every sentence above N5.

## Formation

The clause goes **in front of** the noun, in the **plain form**. That is the
whole rule.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + Noun | 買(か)った本(ほん)／買(か)う本(ほん) |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + Noun | 読(よ)まない人(ひと) |
| Verb (continuous) | ～ている + Noun | 走(はし)っている人(ひと) |
| い-adjective | plain form + Noun | 高(たか)い店(みせ)／高(たか)かった店(みせ) |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + Noun | 有名(ゆうめい)**な**店(みせ) |
| Noun (describing) | Noun + **の** + Noun | 木(き)**の**机(つくえ) |
| Noun (as a predicate inside the clause) | Noun + **だった** + Noun | 先生(せんせい)**だった**人(ひと) |
| Subject inside the clause | `が` - or `の` | 父(ちち)**が**作(つく)った／父(ちち)**の**作(つく)った |
| ❌ Polite form inside | never | ❌買(か)いました本(ほん) |
| ❌ Topic は inside | never | ❌私(わたし)**は**買(か)った本(ほん) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three things Japanese does *not* do: it does not use a
relative pronoun, it does not move the noun, and it does not use polite forms
inside the clause. Everything you would express with "who / which / that" is
expressed by position alone.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the clause can be any length at all, and it keeps its own particles
inside. 去年(きょねん)京都(きょうと)で買(か)った小(ちい)さい茶(ちゃ)わん - every
word before 茶(ちゃ)わん is describing it. When reading, look for the noun at
the end first, then work backwards.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは<b>母(はは)が作(つく)った</b>ケーキです。

</pre>

<pre>
<b>眼鏡(めがね)をかけている</b>人(ひと)が中村(なかむら)さんです。

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

The hotel we stayed at last year was a long way from the station.
</pre>

<pre>

A photograph my father took appeared in the newspaper.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

The construction itself carries no register - it is simply how Japanese
describes things, and it appears in every sentence type at every politeness
level. What *is* register-marked is the fact that the clause is always plain:
買(か)った本(ほん)をお送(おく)りします is entirely polite, because politeness
lives on the final verb of the sentence and nowhere else.

Three habits that separate fluent-sounding Japanese from translated English:

**Long modifiers are normal.** English resists front-loading and reaches for
"which…" clauses instead. Japanese does not: a modifier of fifteen syllables
sitting in front of a two-syllable noun is unremarkable in a newspaper. Learn to
read to the end of the phrase before deciding what it means.

**`が` inside the clause often becomes `の`.** 父(ちち)が撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)
and 父(ちち)の撮(と)った写真(しゃしん) mean the same thing. The `の` version
sounds slightly more compact and slightly more written; it is very common in
headlines and titles. This substitution works **only** inside a modifying clause
 - never in a main sentence.

**Ambiguity is tolerated.** 私(わたし)が好(す)きな人(ひと) is "the person I
like" in most contexts, but it can also be read as "the person who likes me".
Japanese does not disambiguate this, and speakers rely on context rather than
rebuilding the sentence.

## Contrast

### が vs の inside the clause

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - inside a noun-modifying clause, the subject may be marked
`が` **or** `の`, with no change of meaning. Outside such a clause, only `が`
works.

</div>

<pre>
<b>友(とも)だちが貸(か)してくれた</b>本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいます。
I'm reading a book a friend lent me.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>友(とも)だちの貸(か)してくれた</b>本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいます。
I'm reading a book a friend lent me. (same meaning, slightly tighter)
</pre>

Two guard rails. The `の` substitution fails as soon as the clause contains
another `の`, because the string becomes unreadable. And it never applies to a
main clause: ❌友(とも)だちの来(き)ました is simply wrong, where
友(とも)だちが来(き)ました is right.

### clause + Noun vs clause + の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a clause in front of a **noun** describes that noun. A clause
in front of **`の`** turns the clause itself into a noun.

</div>

The two look identical until you notice what is at the end.

<pre>
<b>毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っている</b>人(ひと)を見(み)ました。
I saw a person who runs every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っている<b>の</b>を見(み)ました。
I saw them running every morning.
</pre>

In the first, the thing seen is a **person**. In the second, the thing seen is
the **event** of running. Learners often produce the second when they meant the
first, because `の` feels like a safe filler - it is not; it changes what the
sentence is about.

### modifying clause vs ～という + Noun

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the noun could fill a slot inside the clause, modify it
directly. If the clause is the *content* of the noun, you need `という`.

</div>

<pre>
<b>田中(たなか)さんが書(か)いた</b>手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)みました。
I read a letter Tanaka wrote. (the letter is what he wrote - direct modification)
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんが来(こ)ない<b>という</b>話(はなし)を聞(き)きました。
I heard that Tanaka isn't coming. (the news *is* the clause - という)
</pre>

The test: can you put the noun back into the clause? 田中(たなか)さんが手紙(てがみ)を書(か)いた works, so 手紙(てがみ) modifies directly.
❌田中(たなか)さんが話(はなし)を来(こ)ない does not work, so 話(はなし)
needs `という`. Content nouns like 話(はなし)、ニュース、約束(やくそく) and
予定(よてい) behave this way; you will meet the rule properly at N4.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)買(か)い**ました**本(ほん)はこれです。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)買(か)っ**た**本(ほん)はこれです。
  The clause is always plain. Politeness goes on the last verb of the sentence,
  which here is `です`.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)見(み)た**の**映画(えいが)はよかった。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)見(み)た映画(えいが)はよかった。
  Nothing goes between the clause and the noun. The `の` is imported from
  私(わたし)の本(ほん), where it links two *nouns* - a verb needs no linker.

- ❌ 人(ひと)が走(はし)っている、田中(たなか)さんです。 → ✅ 走(はし)っている人(ひと)が田中(たなか)さんです。
  The description goes **before** the noun. Japanese has no way to hang a clause
  off the back of a noun.

- ❌ 私(わたし)**は**買(か)った本(ほん)をなくしました。 → ✅ 私(わたし)**が**買(か)った本(ほん)をなくしました。
  `は` marks the topic of a whole sentence and cannot appear inside a modifying
  clause. Inside, the subject takes `が` (or `の`).

- ❌ 有名(ゆうめい)人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。 → ✅ 有名(ゆうめい)**な**人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。
  な-adjectives keep their `な` in front of the noun, just as they do outside a
  clause.

## Practice

**1.** ____人(ひと)は山田(やまだ)さんです。（あそこで新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)んでいます）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>あそこで新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)んでいる</b>人(ひと)は山田(やまだ)さんです。

Two changes: the clause moves in front of 人(ひと), and the polite
<code>読(よ)んでいます</code> becomes plain <code>読(よ)んでいる</code>. Nothing is inserted between the clause
and the noun.

</details>

**2.** 母(はは)（が・は）作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はおいしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

母(はは)**が**作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はおいしいです。

`は` belongs to the main sentence - here it is already marking
<code>料理(りょうり)</code> as the topic. Inside a modifying clause the subject
takes `が`, and in this position `の` would also be acceptable:
母(はは)の作(つく)った料理(りょうり).

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The city where I was born is small."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)が生(う)まれた町(まち)は小(ちい)さいです。

English needs "where"; Japanese needs nothing - the relationship between
町(まち) and the clause is carried by position alone. Note again that the
clause verb is plain (<code>生(う)まれた</code>) while the sentence ends politely.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: きのう会(あ)いましたの人(ひと)は先生(せんせい)でした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ きのう会(あ)った人(ひと)は先生(せんせい)でした。

Two errors in one phrase: the clause must be plain (<code>会(あ)った</code>, not <code>会(あ)いました</code>),
and there is no `の` between a clause and the noun it describes. The `の` is only
for linking one noun to another.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 昨日(きのう)買(か)った本(ほん)を読(よ)みました。
(b) 昨日(きのう)買(か)ったのを読(よ)みました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) names the thing explicitly: a book, bought yesterday, was read. It works
whether or not the listener already knows about any book.

(b) leaves the noun out and lets `の` stand in for it, so it only works when both
speakers already know what is being talked about - "I read the one I bought
yesterday." Grammatically the difference is whether there is a noun at the end of
the clause at all; conversationally, (b) assumes shared context and (a) does not.

</details>

# Counters

**Meaning** · you cannot count a Japanese noun with a bare number - every noun needs a counter word

English says "three apples". Japanese says りんごを三(みっ)つ - number plus a
counter chosen to fit the *kind* of thing being counted. The counters themselves
are easy; the readings are not, because the number and the counter fuse and
change sound. Those changes are the whole content of this page.

## Formation

The counter attaches directly to the number, and the resulting phrase usually
sits **after** the noun and its particle, behaving like an adverb:
りんご**を**三(みっ)つ買(か)いました。

| People | Number + 人(にん) | <b>一人(ひとり)</b>、<b>二人(ふたり)</b>、三人(さんにん)、<b>四人(よにん)</b>、五人(ごにん) |
| Long thin things | Number + 本(ほん) | <b>一本(いっぽん)</b>、二本(にほん)、<b>三本(さんぼん)</b>、<b>六本(ろっぽん)</b>、<b>八本(はっぽん)</b> |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the sound changes are not random. A counter starting with
**h** (本(ほん)、杯(はい)、匹(ひき)、分(ふん)) shifts to **p** after 1, 6, 8
and 10, and to **b** after 三(さん) (三本(さんぼん)、三杯(さんばい)、三匹(さんびき) - though 三分(さんぷん) keeps the p). A counter starting
with **k** or **s** (回(かい)、個(こ)、冊(さつ)、歳(さい)) doubles its
consonant after 1, 6, 8 and 10. Learn the pattern once and most counters fall
out of it.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a few readings have accepted alternatives. 七人(しちにん) is also
said ななにん; 九人(きゅうにん) is also くにん; 十本(じゅっぽん) is also
じっぽん (the older prescribed form); 三階(さんがい) is also さんかい. All are
heard. The ones with no alternative - ひとり, ふたり, よにん, はたち - are the ones
to drill.

</div>

## Nuance & register

Counters are not optional and not stylistic - they are part of the grammar, and
a bare number in front of a noun is simply ungrammatical.

**The ～つ series is your safety net.** It is native Japanese vocabulary, it runs
only from one to ten, and it counts almost any concrete object whose proper
counter you have forgotten. Nobody will misunderstand りんごを四(よっ)つ. Above
ten, ～つ stops existing and you fall back on 個(こ) or the specific counter.

**People are the one place you cannot improvise.** ❌いちにん and ❌ににん do not
exist. 一人(ひとり) and 二人(ふたり) are irregular native forms, and from
three on the counter switches to the Sino-Japanese にん. This is worth over-
learning: 一人(ひとり)で ("alone") and 二人(ふたり)で ("the two of us") are
everyday words.

**Register.** In shops and restaurants you will hear the deferential
<code>名(めい)様(さま)</code> for people - 二名様(にめいさま) - and
<code>お一人(ひとり)様(さま)</code>. You are not expected to produce these at
N5, only to recognise them. In casual speech the counter can drop when context is
obvious: コーヒー、二(ふた)つ。

**Where the phrase sits.** The neutral position is after the noun-plus-particle,
as an adverb. Putting it in front with `の` (三(みっ)つのりんご) is also correct
but feels more written and slightly more emphatic - it is what you use when the
quantity is the point.

## Contrast

### ～つ vs ～個(こ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both count generic objects. ～つ is native, softer, and stops
at ten; <code>個(こ)</code> is Sino-Japanese, has no upper limit, and leans
towards small, discrete, roundish things.

</div>

<pre>
たまごを<b>六(むっ)つ</b>使(つか)います。
I'll use six eggs.
</pre>

<pre>
たまごを<b>十二個(じゅうにこ)</b>買(か)いました。
I bought twelve eggs.
</pre>

The second sentence cannot use ～つ, because the series has no word for twelve.
Below ten the two are largely interchangeable for objects like eggs, apples and
boxes, with ～つ sounding a shade more everyday.

<code>個(こ)</code> is also what a shop assistant will say, because it works for any
quantity and does not need a decision about shape.

### ～回(かい) vs ～階(かい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>回(かい)</code> counts **times**;
<code>階(かい)</code> counts **floors**. They sound almost identical, and the
one place they diverge is at three: 三回(さんかい) but 三階(さんがい).

</div>

<pre>
エレベーターで<b>三階(さんがい)</b>まで上(あ)がってください。
Please take the lift up to the third floor.
</pre>

<pre>
このボタンを<b>三回(さんかい)</b>押(お)してください。
Please press this button three times.
</pre>

Both 何回(なんかい) ("how many times") and 何階(なんがい) ("which floor")
exist, and the 階(かい) one is voiced. Getting 三階(さんがい) right is a
small thing that immediately marks a careful learner.

### りんごを三(みっ)つ vs 三(みっ)つのりんご

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the counter phrase normally goes **after** the noun and its
particle. Moving it in front with `の` is grammatical but shifts the emphasis
onto the quantity.

</div>

<pre>
テーブルにりんごを<b>三(みっ)つ</b>置(お)きました。
I put three apples on the table.
</pre>

<pre>
テーブルに<b>三(みっ)つの</b>りんごを置(お)きました。
I put three apples on the table. (with the "three" foregrounded)
</pre>

The first is what people say. The second reads like a description in a
catalogue or a maths problem, and a learner who defaults to it - because it
matches English word order - will sound stilted without ever making an outright
error.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ りんごを三(さん)買(か)いました。 → ✅ りんごを三(みっ)つ買(か)いました。
  A bare number cannot count a noun. You must choose a counter, and ～つ is the
  safe default for objects up to ten.

- ❌ いちにん、ににん → ✅ 一人(ひとり)、二人(ふたり)
  The first two people are native readings and do not follow the pattern. From
  三人(さんにん) onwards the Sino-Japanese にん takes over.

- ❌ よんにん → ✅ よにん
  <code>四人(よにん)</code> drops the ん. (よんにん is widely heard, but よにん is
  what you should say and what the JLPT expects.)

- ❌ さんほん、いちほん → ✅ さんぼん、いっぽん
  <code>本(ほん)</code> voices to ぼん after 三(さん) and geminates to ぽん
  after 1, 6, 8 and 10. The same shift hits 杯(はい)、匹(ひき) and
  分(ふん).

- ❌ 二(に)つ、三(さん)つ → ✅ 二(ふた)つ、三(みっ)つ
  The ～つ series takes **native** numerals (ひと、ふた、みっ…), never the
  Sino-Japanese ones. Mixing the two systems is the most audible counter error
  there is.

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "There were two cats and one dog in the garden."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

庭(にわ)に猫(ねこ)が二匹(にひき)と犬(いぬ)が一匹(いっぴき)いました。

Small animals take <code>匹(ひき)</code>, which geminates to ぴき after one:
いっぴき, not ❌いちひき. Note also that animals are counted with `いる`, not
`ある`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部屋(へや)に人(ひと)が三(みっ)ついます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部屋(へや)に人(ひと)が三人(さんにん)います。

The ～つ series counts objects and never people. People always take
<code>人(にん)</code>, which is irregular at one and two (一人(ひとり),
二人(ふたり)) but perfectly regular from three: さんにん.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) みかんを四(よっ)つ食(た)べました。
(b) 四(よっ)つのみかんを食(た)べました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the ordinary sentence. The counter phrase sits after the object and its
particle and works adverbially - this is how quantities are normally expressed,
and it is what you should default to.

(b) attaches the quantity to the noun with `の`, which makes "the four
mandarins" a single described thing. It is correct, but it foregrounds the number
in a way that suits a written description or a context where the count itself is
the news. Learners produce (b) too often because it mirrors English word order.

</details>

# ずつ

**Meaning** · ~ each; ~ at a time - the same amount, distributed or repeated

`ずつ` takes a quantity and spreads it evenly. Either across people
(一(ひと)つずつ = one each) or across time (少(すこ)しずつ = a little at a
time). Both readings come from the same idea: the same portion, again and again.

## Formation

`ずつ` attaches to the **end of a quantity expression** - a number with its
counter, or a word of amount. It never attaches to a bare noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Number + counter | Number+Counter + ずつ | 一(ひと)つ → 一(ひと)つずつ |
| Number + counter (people) | 二人(ふたり) + ずつ | 二人(ふたり)ずつ |
| Bare number | Number + ずつ | 三(さん)ずつ |
| Amount word | 少(すこ)し + ずつ | 少(すこ)しずつ |
| Casual amount word | ちょっと + ずつ | ちょっとずつ |
| With an object | Noun を Number+Counter ずつ | あめを二(ふた)つずつ |
| Per unit | ～に + Number+Counter ずつ | 一日(いちにち)に三(さん)ページずつ |
| ❌ Bare noun | not possible | ❌りんごずつ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - count first, `ずつ` second. The quantity always comes in
front: 一(ひと)つずつ, never ❌ずつ一(ひと)つ. And there must *be* a quantity - 
`ずつ` cannot hang off a noun on its own.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `ずつ` is written in hiragana. You will occasionally meet the old
spelling `づつ` in older books and on shop signs; it is the same word and it is
not the modern standard.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あめを一(ひと)つ<b>ずつ</b>とってください。
Take one sweet each.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)漢字(かんじ)を五(いつ)つ<b>ずつ</b>覚(おぼ)えます。
I learn five kanji a day.
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>ずつ</b>上手(じょうず)になっていますよ。
You're getting better bit by bit.
</pre>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)<b>ずつ</b>グループを作(つく)ってください。
Please form groups of two.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)に三(さん)ページ<b>ずつ</b>読(よ)めば、一(いっ)か月(げつ)で終(お)わります。
If you read three pages a day, you'll finish it in a month.
</pre>

<pre>
貯金(ちょきん)は毎月(まいつき)少(すこ)し<b>ずつ</b>増(ふ)えている。
My savings are growing a little at a time.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)にも、少(すこ)し<b>ずつ</b>慣(な)れてきました。
I'm gradually getting used to the new job too.
</pre>

<pre>
お土産(みやげ)のクッキーは、一人(ひとり)二(に)枚(まい)<b>ずつ</b>配(くば)ってください。
Please hand out the souvenir cookies, two per person.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ずつ` is register-neutral and unremarkable - it appears in classroom
instructions, in recipes, in business email and in conversation without changing
shape.

Its two senses are worth separating in your head, because they behave
differently:

**Distributive - "each".** 一人(ひとり)二(に)枚(まい)ずつ. There is a set
of recipients and everyone gets the same amount. This is the sense you need when
handing things out, forming groups, or dividing anything up. Japanese uses
`ずつ` here where English often uses nothing at all: "take one" is ambiguous in
English, 一(ひと)つずつとって is not.

**Incremental - "at a time".** 少(すこ)しずつ. There is no set of recipients;
the repetition is over time. This is by far the most common use of the word, and
少(すこ)しずつ is close to being a fixed adverb meaning "gradually".

The casual variant is `ちょっとずつ`, which is warmer and softer than
少(すこ)しずつ and belongs to speech only. You will also hear the emphatic
一(ひと)つ一(ひと)つ ("one by one, individually"), which is not the same
thing: that stresses handling each item separately rather than distributing
equally.

One small pitfall: `ずつ` needs the sharing to be *even*. If two people get
different amounts, `ずつ` is wrong and you should just name the amounts.

## Contrast

### 一(ひと)つ vs 一(ひと)つずつ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - without `ずつ`, the number is the total. With `ずつ`, the
number is the share **per person** or **per occasion**.

</div>

<pre>
クッキーを<b>二(ふた)つ</b>買(か)いました。
I bought two cookies. (two in total)
</pre>

<pre>
クッキーを<b>二(ふた)つずつ</b>買(か)いました。
I bought two cookies each. (two per person, so more than two altogether)
</pre>

The difference is not decoration - it changes the arithmetic. Leaving `ずつ` out
of a sentence about handing things round is one of the errors most likely to
produce an actual misunderstanding.

### 少(すこ)しずつ vs だんだん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 少(すこ)しずつ describes the **size of the steps**;
だんだん describes the **direction of the change**. They overlap in English as
"gradually".

</div>

<pre>
スープに水(みず)を少(すこ)し<b>ずつ</b>足(た)してください。
Add the water to the soup a little at a time.
</pre>

<pre>
だんだん暖(あたた)かくなってきました。
It's been getting warmer and warmer.
</pre>

You cannot swap them here: ❌だんだん水(みず)を足(た)してください gives no
instruction about portions at all.

少(すこ)しずつ implies a series of small increments you could in principle
count; だんだん implies a smooth trend and carries no notion of portions. They
combine happily - 少(すこ)しずつだんだん良(よ)くなる is redundant, but
少(すこ)しずつ覚(おぼ)える and だんだん覚(おぼ)える differ: the first is
about the batches, the second about the trajectory.

### ずつ vs それぞれ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ずつ` needs a **quantity** and says everyone gets the same
amount. `それぞれ` needs no quantity and says only that each person has their
own - possibly different - one.

</div>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)に本(ほん)を一冊(いっさつ)<b>ずつ</b>渡(わた)しました。
I handed each student one book. (same number for everybody)
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)はそれぞれ違(ちが)う意見(いけん)を持(も)っています。
The students each have different opinions.
</pre>

Because `ずつ` insists on equality, ❌それぞれ違(ちが)う数(かず)ずつ is
self-contradictory. And because it needs a quantity, `ずつ` cannot replace
`それぞれ` in the second sentence at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ずつ一(ひと)つとってください。 → ✅ 一(ひと)つずつとってください。
  `ずつ` follows the quantity; it never leads. Word order here is fixed.

- ❌ りんごずつ買(か)いました。 → ✅ りんごを一(ひと)つずつ買(か)いました。
  `ずつ` attaches to an amount, not to a noun. Without a number and counter in
  front of it there is nothing for it to distribute.

- ❌ 私(わたし)にケーキを一(ひと)つずつください。 → ✅ 私(わたし)にケーキを一(ひと)つください。
  With a single recipient there is nothing to distribute across, so `ずつ` has no
  work to do and sounds strange.

- ❌ 少(すこ)しずつずつ良(よ)くなる → ✅ 少(すこ)しずつ良(よ)くなる
  One `ずつ` per quantity. Doubling it for emphasis is not a thing; if you want
  emphasis, use 少(すこ)しずつではあるが or simply repeat the verb.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは三(さん)枚(まい)、山田(やまだ)さんは二(に)枚(まい)ずつもらいました。 → ✅ …山田(やまだ)さんは二(に)枚(まい)もらいました。
  `ずつ` promises that the shares are equal. If they are not, drop it and state
  the amounts.

## Practice

**1.** クラスを四人(よにん)____のグループに分(わ)けます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

クラスを四人(よにん)**ずつ**のグループに分(わ)けます。

The point is that every group has the same size, which is exactly what `ずつ`
encodes. Without it the sentence would just describe one group of four rather
than a whole class divided evenly.

</details>

**2.** 学生(がくせい)に紙(かみ)を一枚(いちまい)（ずつ・それぞれ）渡(わた)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

学生(がくせい)に紙(かみ)を一枚(いちまい)**ずつ**渡(わた)してください。

There is a quantity here - 一枚(いちまい) - and the instruction is that
everybody gets that same quantity. `それぞれ` cannot follow a counter phrase; it
would have to go earlier and would lose the "one apiece" meaning.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please add the sugar a little at a time."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

砂糖(さとう)を少(すこ)し**ずつ**入(い)れてください。

This is the incremental sense: no recipients, just repeated small portions over
time. <code>少(すこ)しずつ</code> is close to a fixed adverb and is the standard
phrase in recipes.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: みなさんにペンをずつ一本(いっぽん)配(くば)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ みなさんにペンを一本(いっぽん)ずつ配(くば)ります。

`ずつ` goes **after** the quantity, not before it. The counter phrase
一本(いっぽん) has to be complete before `ずつ` can attach - and note the
reading いっぽん, not ❌いちほん.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子(こ)どもたちにあめを三(みっ)つあげました。
(b) 子(こ)どもたちにあめを三(みっ)つずつあげました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) means three sweets were given to the children as a group - they will have to
work out among themselves who gets what, and if there are four children someone
is going without.

(b) means every child got three, so the total depends on how many children there
were. The single word `ずつ` is doing all of that work, and English needs a whole
phrase ("three each") to match it. When you are dividing anything up, leaving
`ずつ` out changes the facts.

</details>
