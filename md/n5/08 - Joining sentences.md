# N5 - Joining sentences

# や

**Meaning** · and - but only naming *some* of the things, not all of them

`や` joins nouns into a list the speaker is not finishing. You name two or three
representative items and leave the rest implied, the way English does with "eggs,
cheese, that sort of thing". If you want the list to be complete, `や` is the
wrong particle.

## Formation

`や` sits **between** nouns. It never attaches to a verb, an adjective or a
clause, and the particle that governs the whole list (`が`, `を`, `に`…) goes
after the **last** noun only.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (two items) | Noun や Noun | 卵(たまご) + チーズ → 卵(たまご)やチーズ |
| Noun (three or more) | N や N や N | ペンやノートや消(け)しゴム |
| Rounded off with など | N や N など | 野菜(やさい)や果物(くだもの)など |
| Followed by a case particle | N や N + particle | 机(つくえ)や椅子(いす)**を**運(はこ)ぶ |
| ❌ Verbs | not possible - use ～たり～たり | ❌読(よ)むや書(か)く |
| ❌ い-adjectives | not possible - use ～くて | ❌安(やす)いやおいしい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `や` is a *sampler*. Two items on the page, more items in the
world. `など` at the end makes that explicit and is very common with `や`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に卵(たまご)<b>や</b>チーズがあります。
There are eggs, cheese and so on in the fridge.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は洗濯(せんたく)<b>や</b>掃除(そうじ)をします。
On weekends I do laundry, cleaning, that kind of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)き出(だ)しの中(なか)にはさみ<b>や</b>のりが入(はい)っています。
There are scissors, glue and suchlike in the drawer.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)<b>や</b>奈良(なら)へ行(い)ってみたいです。
I'd like to visit Kyoto, Nara and places like that.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)では野菜(やさい)<b>や</b>果物(くだもの)が安(やす)く買(か)えます。
At this shop you can buy vegetables, fruit and the like cheaply.
</pre>

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みには海(うみ)<b>や</b>山(やま)へ出(で)かける人(ひと)が多(おお)いです。
Over the summer holidays a lot of people head to the sea, the mountains and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんはおにぎり<b>や</b>サンドイッチで済(す)ませることが多(おお)い。
I usually make do with a rice ball or a sandwich or something for lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しのとき、本棚(ほんだな)<b>や</b>古(ふる)いソファなどの大(おお)きい家具(かぐ)は全部(ぜんぶ)捨(す)てました。
When I moved I threw out all the big furniture - the bookshelf, the old sofa and so on.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`や` is register-neutral. It appears in newspapers, in textbooks and in ordinary
conversation, and it does not sound stiff or casual either way.

What it *does* carry is a small amount of politeness-by-vagueness. Because `や`
declines to close the list, it sounds less like a bureaucratic inventory. Asked
what you did on holiday, 海(うみ)や山(やま)に行(い)きました sounds relaxed;
the same sentence with `と` sounds like you are filing a report.

**In speech, `とか` does the same job and is much more common.**
野菜(やさい)とか果物(くだもの)とか買(か)ってきて - that is what you would
actually text a housemate. `とか` is casual, can repeat after the final item, and
can (unlike `や`) also hang off verbs. In writing, prefer `や`.

**`など` is the natural finisher.** <code>野菜(やさい)や果物(くだもの)など</code>
makes the open-endedness explicit and is standard in written Japanese. In formal
documents the same word is often written in kanji as <code>等(など)</code>; write it in
kana at N5.

## Contrast

### や vs と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` closes the list; `や` leaves it open. `と` = "A and B,
that's everything". `や` = "A and B, among other things".

</div>

This is the only difference, and it is a real one - a listener hears it.

<pre>
かばんの中(なか)には、さいふ<b>と</b>かぎがあります。
In my bag there's a wallet and a key. (Those two things. Nothing else.)
</pre>

<pre>
かばんの中(なか)には、さいふ<b>や</b>かぎがあります。
In my bag there's a wallet, a key, stuff like that. (And other things besides.)
</pre>

Two consequences worth remembering. First, when the set really is fixed, `や` is
odd: 日本(にほん)の大(おお)きい島(しま)は北海道(ほっかいどう)と本州(ほんしゅう)と… - you are
enumerating, so use `と`. Second, `や` needs at least two items; ❌水(みず)やください
is not a sentence.

### や vs とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `や` is neutral-to-written;
`とか` is spoken.

</div>

`とか` also repeats after the last item, which `や` cannot do.

<pre>
コーヒー<b>とか</b>お茶(ちゃ)<b>とか</b>、何(なに)か飲(の)む?
Coffee, tea, something - want a drink?
</pre>

And `とか` will attach to verbs, where `や` is simply ungrammatical:
走(はし)るとか泳(およ)ぐとか、運動(うんどう)をしたほうがいいよ。

### や vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `や` lists **nouns**. To give a non-exhaustive list of
**actions**, you need `～たり～たり`.

</div>

Learners reach for `や` here because English uses "and" for both. Japanese does
not.

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は音楽(おんがく)<b>や</b>映画(えいが)が好(す)きです。
On Sundays I like music, films, that sort of thing. (nouns - や is right)
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)い<b>たり</b>映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>たり</b>します。
On Sundays I listen to music, watch films, and so on. (actions - たり is required)
</pre>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む**や**音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)きます。 → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)ん**だり**音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)い**たり**します。
  `や` joins nouns only. A list of activities needs `～たり～たり`.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は明(あか)るい**や**広(ひろ)いです。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は明(あか)る**くて**広(ひろ)いです。
  Adjectives chain with `くて`／`で`, never with `や`.

- ❌ ペン**や**ノート**や**を買(か)いました。 → ✅ ペン**や**ノート**を**買(か)いました。
  `や` goes *between* nouns. Nothing follows the last item except the case
  particle (or `など`).

- ❌ 父(ちち)**や**母(はは)は二人(ふたり)とも先生(せんせい)です。 → ✅ 父(ちち)**と**母(はは)は二人(ふたり)とも先生(せんせい)です。
  <code>二人(ふたり)とも</code> says the list is exactly two people, which contradicts the
  open-endedness of `や`.

- ❌ 野菜(やさい)**など**や果物(くだもの)を買(か)います。 → ✅ 野菜(やさい)**や**果物(くだもの)**など**を買(か)います。
  `など` closes the whole list, so it comes at the end - not in the middle.

## Practice

**1.** テーブルの上(うえ)にコップ____おさらがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

テーブルの上(うえ)にコップ**や**おさらがあります。

Either `や` or `と` is grammatical here, and they say different things. With `や`
the cups and plates are examples of what is on the table; with `と` they are the
complete contents. If you can imagine a fork sitting there too, use `や`.

</details>

**2.** 私(わたし)の家族(かぞく)は父(ちち)（や・と）母(はは)と私(わたし)の三人(さんにん)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)の家族(かぞく)は父(ちち)**と**母(はは)と私(わたし)の三人(さんにん)です。

<code>三人(さんにん)です</code> states the exact size of the set, so the list has to be
exhaustive. `や` would promise that there are other family members you have not
mentioned, which flatly contradicts the rest of the sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I bought stamps, envelopes and things like that at the post office."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)で切手(きって)**や**封筒(ふうとう)（**など**）を買(か)いました。

"and things like that" is exactly what `や` encodes, so no extra word is needed - 
though `など` after the final noun makes it unmistakable. Note that `を` attaches
once, to the end of the whole list.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 朝(あさ)はパンを食(た)べるや新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)みます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 朝(あさ)はパンを食(た)べ**たり**新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)ん**だり**します。
"In the morning I eat toast, read the paper, and so on."

The two items being listed are *actions*, not nouns, so `や` cannot join them.
The non-exhaustive list of actions is `～たり～たり`, and the sentence then ends in
`します`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 土曜日(どようび)はそうじと買(か)い物(もの)をしました。
(b) 土曜日(どようび)はそうじや買(か)い物(もの)をしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents cleaning and shopping as the whole of Saturday - those two jobs, and
that is the account. It sounds like an answer to "what exactly did you do?"

(b) offers them as samples of a busier day: cleaning, shopping, and other errands
you are not bothering to list. Japanese speakers reach for (b) far more often in
small talk, because pinning the day down to precisely two activities sounds
strangely precise.

</details>

# ので

**Meaning** · because - presenting the reason as a plain fact, not as your opinion

`ので` links a cause to its result. The half before `ので` is the reason; the half
after it is what follows from that reason. It frames the connection as something
anyone would accept, which is why it is the polite way to explain yourself when
you are asking for indulgence.

## Formation

`ので` attaches to a **clause**, not to a word. The trap is nouns and
な-adjectives, which need `な` in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | plain form + ので | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るので |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ので | 遅(おく)れた → 遅(おく)れたので |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ので | わからない → わからないので |
| い-adjective | plain form + ので | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いので |
| い-adjective (past) | かった + ので | 寒(さむ)かったので |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + ので | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)か**な**ので |
| Noun | Noun + **な** + ので | 休(やす)みだ → 休(やす)み**な**ので |
| Noun (past) | ～だった + ので | 休(やす)みだったので |
| Polite (formal) | ます-form + ので | 参(まい)ります → 参(まい)りますので |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌`だので` does not exist. A noun or な-adjective takes `な`
before `ので`, and `な` only. The past forms `だったので` are fine, because there
the `だ` has already become `だった`.

</div>

Politeness lives on the **final** verb of the sentence, not on the `ので` clause:
寒(さむ)いので、まどを閉(し)め**ます**. The ます-form before `ので` shown in the last
row is a separate, extra-formal register - announcements and business email.

## Examples

<pre>
寒(さむ)い<b>ので</b>、コートを着(き)ます。
It's cold, so I'll put a coat on.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)み<b>なので</b>、ゆっくり寝(ね)ます。
Tomorrow's a day off, so I'm going to sleep in.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れた<b>ので</b>、会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。
The train was delayed, so I didn't make the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
図書館(としょかん)は静(しず)か<b>なので</b>、よく勉強(べんきょう)しに行(い)きます。
The library is quiet, so I often go there to study.
</pre>

<pre>
頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い<b>ので</b>、先(さき)に失礼(しつれい)します。
I have a headache, so I'll head off before the rest of you.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)じゃない<b>ので</b>、ゆっくり話(はな)していただけませんか。
My Japanese still isn't very good, so could you speak slowly?
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもがまだ小(ちい)さい<b>ので</b>、夜(よる)の集(あつ)まりには参加(さんか)できません。
My children are still small, so I can't come to evening gatherings.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいております<b>ので</b>、本日(ほんじつ)は早(はや)めに閉店(へいてん)させていただきます。
A typhoon is approaching, so we will be closing early today.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ので` is polite-leaning but not itself a polite form - you will hear plain
眠(ねむ)いので寝(ね)る among friends. What it does is present the reason as
**objective**: this is how things are, and the conclusion follows by itself.

That objectivity is why `ので` is the excuse-making particle. Cancelling on
someone, arriving late, asking to leave early - 急用(きゅうよう)ができました**ので**
sounds like circumstances have decided, not like you have. Saying the same thing
with `から` puts your own will in the frame and can land as "because I say so".

**The contraction is `んで`.** 眠(ねむ)いんで先(さき)に帰(かえ)るね。
This is very common in casual speech and completely normal among friends, but it
is markedly informal - it sounds rough in the mouth of a shop assistant and you
should not write it. Learners often meet `んで` in dramas long before anyone
tells them it is just `ので`.

At the other end, `ますので` (as in the last example above) is service and
business register. It is not more grammatical, just more deferential, and it
belongs to announcements, apologies and formal email.

One more habit worth copying: `ので` frequently ends a sentence on its own, with
the consequence left unsaid. 今(いま)手(て)が離(はな)せないので…。 The listener
supplies "…so I can't right now", and nobody has had to refuse anything out loud.

## Contrast

### ので vs から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` is *my* reason; `ので` is *the* reason. `から` pushes,
`ので` explains.

</div>

Both mean "because" and both are correct in most sentences. The difference is
whose judgement the reason belongs to.

<pre>
熱(ねつ)がある<b>ので</b>、今日(きょう)は休(やす)ませていただきます。
I have a fever, so I'll be taking today off. (Circumstances decide; I'm reporting them.)
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)がある<b>から</b>、今日(きょう)は休(やす)む。
I've got a fever, so I'm taking today off. (My call, stated plainly.)
</pre>

Practical consequences:

- Apologising or asking a favour of someone senior → `ので`. `から` can sound like
  you are pressing your case.
- Strong personal statements, warnings, invitations, imperatives → `から`.
  危(あぶ)ない**から**、来(く)るな! An imperative with `ので` sounds mismatched, though
  polite requests with `ので` are completely normal and extremely common:
  危(あぶ)ないので、下(さ)がってください。
- Answering the question どうして? → `からです`. ❌`のでです` is not a form.

### ので vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical attachment, opposite meaning. `ので` = the result
follows; `のに` = the result *fails* to follow, and the speaker is put out about
it.

</div>

They are one syllable apart and both take `な` after a noun, so they are easy to
mix up in production.

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>ので</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。
It's raining, so the match is cancelled.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>のに</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりませんでした。
It's raining, and yet the match wasn't cancelled.
</pre>

`のに` always carries feeling - surprise, complaint, disappointment. `ので` is
emotionally flat.

### ので vs the plain て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て-form can imply cause, but only when the result is
something outside your control. If the second clause is a decision, a request or
an invitation, you need `ので`.

</div>

<pre>
道(みち)が込(こ)んでいた<b>ので</b>、タクシーで行(い)きました。
The roads were busy, so I went by taxi. (a choice - ので)
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が込(こ)んで<b>いて</b>、なかなか進(すす)めませんでした。
The roads were busy and we could hardly move. (no choice involved - て is fine)
</pre>

So ❌寒(さむ)くて、まどを閉(し)めてください is wrong; closing a window is a request.
✅寒(さむ)いので、まどを閉(し)めてください。

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)**だ**ので、出(で)かけません。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)**な**ので、出(で)かけません。
  A noun takes `な` before `ので`. The same goes for な-adjectives:
  ❌便利(べんり)だので → ✅便利(べんり)なので.

- ❌ どうして来(こ)なかったんですか。 -  - 忙(いそが)しかった**のでです**。 → ✅ 忙(いそが)しかった**からです**。
  `ので` connects two clauses; it cannot stand as a predicate. The stand-alone
  answer form is `からです`.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がありません**ので**急(いそ)げ! → ✅ 時間(じかん)がない**から**急(いそ)げ!
  A blunt imperative wants the subjective `から`. `ので` with a barked order
  sounds like two different people talking.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした**ので**電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れました。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れました**ので**、会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。
  The reason comes **first**. Japanese never puts `ので` in front of the cause the
  way English puts "because" in front of it.

- ❌ 安(やす)かった**なので**買(か)いました。 → ✅ 安(やす)かった**ので**買(か)いました。
  The `な` belongs only to nouns and な-adjectives in their non-past form. Once a
  predicate is already `かった` or `だった`, `ので` attaches bare.

## Practice

**1.** 今日(きょう)は日曜日(にちようび)____、銀行(ぎんこう)は開(あ)いていません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は日曜日(にちようび)**なので**、銀行(ぎんこう)は開(あ)いていません。

<code>日曜日(にちようび)</code> is a noun, so `ので` needs `な` in front of it. ❌<code>日曜日(にちようび)だので</code>
is the single most common `ので` error, and it comes from thinking of `ので` as
attaching to a finished sentence.

</details>

**2.** 山田(やまだ)さん、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。急(きゅう)に用事(ようじ)ができました（ので・から）、今日(きょう)のパーティーは欠席(けっせき)させていただきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

…用事(ようじ)ができました**ので**、今日(きょう)のパーティーは欠席(けっせき)させていただきます。

You are apologising to someone and asking to be excused, so you want the reason
to look like circumstance rather than preference. `から` here would foreground
your own decision and sound faintly self-justifying.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The room was noisy, so I couldn't sleep well."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部屋(へや)がうるさかった**ので**、よく寝(ね)られませんでした。

うるさい is an い-adjective, so its past form `うるさかった` takes `ので` directly - 
no `な`. Note also that the whole sentence's tense sits on the final verb; you do
not need to mark past twice in any special way.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 字(じ)が小(ちい)さいので、もっと大(おお)きく書(か)け。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 字(じ)が小(ちい)さい**から**、もっと大(おお)きく書(か)け。
Or, keeping `ので`: 字(じ)が小(ちい)さいので、もっと大(おお)きく書(か)いてください。

The grammar of the `ので` clause is fine; the mismatch is in register. A bare
imperative is maximally subjective, and it pairs with the subjective `から`. Swap
either half and the sentence settles.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 時間(じかん)がないから、また今度(こんど)にしましょう。
(b) 時間(じかん)がないので、また今度(こんど)にしましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both propose postponing, and both are perfectly natural. (a) puts the shortage of
time forward as the speaker's own read on the situation - it is direct, it is
what you say to a friend, and among strangers it can sound slightly brusque.

(b) presents the lack of time as an external fact that has made the decision for
both of you. It is the softer, more deferential version, and it is what you would
say to a client or a teacher. Nothing about the facts differs; only who is seen
to be deciding.

</details>

# けど / けれども

**Meaning** · but; although - and, very often, just a soft way to start talking

`けど` joins two clauses where the second is not what the first led you to
expect. It is also, in everyday Japanese, the ordinary way to preface a request
or a piece of news without landing on it too hard.

## Formation

`けど` clips onto the **end of a finished clause**, plain or polite. Nouns and
な-adjectives need `だ` (plain) or `です` (polite) first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | plain form + けど | 行(い)く → 行(い)くけど |
| Verb (polite) | ます-form + けど | 行(い)きます → 行(い)きますけど |
| Verb (past) | た-form + けど | 行(い)った → 行(い)ったけど |
| い-adjective | plain form + けど | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いけど |
| な-adjective | ～**だ** + けど | 便利(べんり)だ → 便利(べんり)**だ**けど／便利(べんり)**です**けど |
| Noun | Noun + **だ** + けど | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)**だ**けど／学生(がくせい)**です**けど |
| Negative | ない-form + けど | 知(し)らないけど |

The four spellings are one word at four levels of formality:

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Casual speech | けど | 安(やす)いけど |
| Slightly softer | けども | 安(やす)いですけども |
| Neutral / written | けれど | 安(やす)いけれど |
| Formal / written | けれども | 安(やす)いけれども |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - longer means more formal. `けど` in conversation,
`けれども` in a written report. `が` is one step more formal again.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
このケーキは甘(あま)い<b>けど</b>、おいしくない。
This cake is sweet but it isn't good.
</pre>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)は難(むずか)しい<b>けど</b>、楽(たの)しいです。
Japanese is hard, but it's fun.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)電話(でんわ)した<b>けど</b>、出(で)なかったね。
I called you yesterday, but you didn't pick up.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、中村(なかむら)<b>ですけど</b>、部長(ぶちょう)はいらっしゃいますか。
Excuse me, this is Nakamura - is the department head in?
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の海(うみ)は好(す)き<b>だけど</b>、人(ひと)が多(おお)すぎるのはちょっとね。
I love the sea in summer, but the crowds are a bit much.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)買(か)った自転車(じてんしゃ)は安(やす)かった<b>けれども</b>、ひと月(つき)で壊(こわ)れてしまった。
The bike I bought last year was cheap, but it broke within a month.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)メールを送(おく)りました<b>けど</b>、まだお返事(へんじ)をいただいていません。
I did send an email once, but I haven't had a reply yet.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません<b>けれども</b>、明日(あした)の会(かい)は欠席(けっせき)させていただきます。
I'm very sorry, but I'll have to miss tomorrow's meeting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`けど` is one of the most-used words in spoken Japanese, and most of the time it
is not really contrastive at all.

**The trailing `けど`.** A sentence that stops on `けど` is a complete, polite
utterance. 少(すこ)し伺(うかが)いたいことがあるんですけど…。
Nothing has been left out by accident; the `けど` hands the floor to the listener
instead of stating your business flatly. Requests, complaints, refusals and
first approaches to a stranger nearly all arrive this way, and a learner who
never uses trailing `けど` sounds blunt without knowing why.

**The preface `けど`.** 映画(えいが)の話(はなし)なんですけど、今週(こんしゅう)見(み)に行(い)きませんか。 Here `けど` marks the first clause as background - 
"about the film" - with no contrast whatsoever. Translating it as "but" gives
nonsense; it is closer to English "so,".

**Register.** `けど` is casual but not rude, and it is fine after `です`／`ます`:
高(たか)いですけど、いいですか。 is perfectly polite. `けれども` in speech sounds
careful and slightly formal; in writing it is unremarkable. `けども` is a spoken
middle ground you will hear a lot from shop staff.

`けど` is not gender- or age-marked. What *is* marked is stacking: two `けど`
clauses in one sentence sounds rambling, and three sounds like you have lost the
thread.

## Contrast

### けど vs が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same grammar, different formality. `が` is the written and
formal member of the pair; `けど` is the spoken one.

</div>

They are interchangeable in structure - both attach to a finished clause, both
can be contrastive or merely prefatory, both can trail off at the end.

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は高(たか)いです<b>が</b>、品質(ひんしつ)はたしかです。
It is expensive, but the quality is dependable. (a written product description)
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は高(たか)い<b>けど</b>、ものはいいよ。
Pricey, but it's good stuff. (the same claim, said to a friend)
</pre>

Two practical notes. `が` after a plain form (高(たか)いが) reads as literary,
not casual - plain form plus `が` belongs to essays, not chat. And in a speech or
a formal email, `が` is the safe default.

### けど vs でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `けど` goes at the **end** of the first clause. `でも` goes at
the **start** of the second sentence. They are never interchangeable in position.

</div>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>けど</b>、明(あか)るいです。
This room is small, but it's bright. (one sentence)
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)いです。<b>でも</b>、明(あか)るいです。
This room is small. But it's bright. (two sentences)
</pre>

This is the mistake English speakers make most, because English "but" sits
between the two halves and so *looks* like `でも`. It is not: ❌狭(せま)いでも
is ungrammatical. If the word comes after the first clause, it must be `けど`.

### けど vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` is `けど` plus a grievance. Use `のに` only when the
outcome genuinely disappoints or surprises the speaker.

</div>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)待(ま)った<b>けど</b>、来(こ)なかった。
I waited three hours, but he didn't come.
</pre>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)も待(ま)った<b>のに</b>、来(こ)なかった。
I waited three whole hours and he still didn't come! (and I'm annoyed)
</pre>

Because `のに` carries emotion, it is wrong where you are simply setting two facts
side by side: ❌弟(おとうと)は背(せ)が低(ひく)いのに、兄(あに)は高(たか)いです。
Nobody's expectation has been thwarted by how tall someone's brother is, so this
needs `けど`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)けど、働(はたら)いています。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)**だ**けど、働(はたら)いています。
  A noun cannot sit bare in front of `けど`. Insert `だ` (plain) or `です`
  (polite). Same for な-adjectives: ❌静(しず)かけど → ✅静(しず)か**だ**けど.

- ❌ 高(たか)いでも、買(か)います。 → ✅ 高(たか)い**けど**、買(か)います。
  `でも` cannot follow a clause. It opens a new sentence:
  高(たか)いです。**でも**、買(か)います。

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っているけど、傘(かさ)を持(も)っているけど、ぬれました。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていて傘(かさ)も持(も)っていたけど、ぬれました。
  One `けど` per sentence. Chaining them reads as unfinished thinking.

- ❌ 安(やす)いけれども。 as a full written sentence → ✅ 安(やす)いけれども、買(か)いませんでした。
  Trailing off is a *spoken* move. In writing, finish the sentence - a dangling
  `けれども` on the page just looks truncated.

- ❌ けど、明日(あした)は行(い)けません。 (at the start of a sentence) → ✅ でも、明日(あした)は行(い)けません。
  `けど` is a clause-final connective. To begin a sentence with "but", use `でも`,
  `しかし`, or `ですが`.

## Practice

**1.** この辞書(じしょ)は便利(べんり)____、重(おも)いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この辞書(じしょ)は便利(べんり)**だけど**（polite: **ですけど**）、重(おも)いです。

<code>便利(べんり)</code> is a な-adjective, so it cannot touch `けど` directly - you need
`だ` or `です` between them. ❌<code>便利(べんり)けど</code> and ❌<code>便利(べんり)なけど</code> are both wrong.

</details>

**2.** 時間(じかん)がなかったです。（けど・でも）、全部(ぜんぶ)終(お)わりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

時間(じかん)がなかったです。**でも**、全部(ぜんぶ)終(お)わりました。

The first clause has already been closed with `です` and a full stop, so the
connector is starting a new sentence - that is `でも`'s job. `けど` would have had
to attach inside the first sentence: 時間(じかん)がなかったけど、全部(ぜんぶ)終(お)わりました。

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've read the book, but I don't really understand it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

その本(ほん)は読(よ)みました**けど**、あまりわかりません。

Polite `ました` plus `けど` is a completely normal combination - `けど` does not
drag the register down. In casual speech: 読(よ)んだ**けど**、あまりわからない。

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: すみません、道(みち)を聞(き)きたいでも、いいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ すみません、道(みち)を聞(き)きたいん**ですけど**、いいですか。

`でも` cannot follow a clause, and here the speaker is not contrasting anything
anyway - this is the prefatory `けど` that softens an approach to a stranger. The
`んですけど` shape is the standard opening for asking a favour.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)したけど、試合(しあい)に負(ま)けました。
(b) 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)したのに、試合(しあい)に負(ま)けました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states two facts and marks the second as unexpected. It is neutral - a
reasonable thing to write in a diary or say to a coach without making a scene.

(b) adds the speaker's feeling: after all that practice, losing is unfair or
maddening. `のに` is never emotionally blank. Use it when you want the listener to
hear the disappointment, and `けど` when you just want the facts joined.

</details>

# ～てから

**Meaning** · after doing X - and X has to happen first for Y to be possible

`～てから` puts two actions in order and says the first one *clears the way* for
the second. It is not a neutral timestamp: the reason you mention washing your
hands before eating is that eating comes after washing, and should.

## Formation

`から` clips onto the plain **て-form**. It never conjugates and never carries
tense - the tense of the whole sentence sits on the final verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + から | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べてから |
| Verb (voiced て-form) | で-form + から | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)ん**で**から |
| する | して + から | 勉強(べんきょう)する → 勉強(べんきょう)してから |
| 来(く)る | 来(き)て + から | 来(く)る → 来(き)てから |
| Tense of the sentence | on the **final** verb only | 洗(あら)ってから食(た)べ**ました** |
| Politeness | on the **final** verb only | 洗(あら)ってから食(た)べ**ます** |
| ❌ Noun | not possible - use Noun + のあとで | ❌仕事(しごと)のてから |
| ❌ Negative | ❌ないでから does not exist - rephrase | ❌見(み)ないでから |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front of `から` is *always* bare て-form.
❌帰(かえ)りますてから, ❌帰(かえ)ったから and ❌帰(かえ)るから all mean something
else or nothing at all.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `～てから` has a second, closely related job: measuring elapsed time
from a starting point. 日本(にほん)に来(き)てから３年(ねん)になります - "it's been
three years since I came to Japan." Same form, and the logic is the same
(everything after that point rests on it), but here the second clause is a
duration rather than an action.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
手(て)を洗(あら)っ<b>てから</b>、ごはんを食(た)べます。
I'll wash my hands and then eat.
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)をし<b>てから</b>、テレビを見(み)なさい。
Do your homework first, then watch television.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)を入(い)れ<b>てから</b>、ボタンを押(お)してください。
Put the money in first, then press the button.
</pre>

<pre>
靴(くつ)を脱(ぬ)い<b>でから</b>、上(あ)がってください。
Please take your shoes off before you come up.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に来(き)<b>てから</b>、３年(ねん)が経(た)ちました。
Three years have passed since I came to Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)をよく読(よ)ん<b>でから</b>、組(く)み立(た)ててください。
Read the instructions carefully before you assemble it.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)を卒業(そつぎょう)し<b>てから</b>、ずっと同(おな)じ会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)いています。
I've worked at the same company ever since I graduated.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)は、部長(ぶちょう)に確認(かくにん)していただい<b>てから</b>、皆(みな)さんにお送(おく)りします。
I'll send the meeting materials round once the department head has checked them.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～てから` is register-neutral. It has no casual contraction and no formal
variant; the politeness of the sentence is decided entirely by the verb at the
end.

What it does carry is a faint sense of **order being observed**. That makes it
the natural form for instructions, rules and procedures - machine displays,
recipes, safety notices, and parents talking to children all use it. ボタンを押(お)してから、レバーを下(さ)げてください reads as a step-by-step procedure in a
way that the plain て-form does not.

The flip side: because `てから` insists on the ordering, using it for two things
that merely happened to follow one another sounds fussy.
起(お)きてから窓(まど)を開(あ)けて、顔(かお)を洗(あら)ってから服(ふく)を着(き)て… - nobody
narrates a morning like that. **One `てから` per sentence** is the practical
limit; when you need a longer chain, use plain て-forms and reserve `てから` for
the one step that genuinely gates the rest.

For the elapsed-time use (来(き)てから３年(ねん)), the second half is very often
<code>なります</code>, <code>経(た)ちます</code> or a state - not an action.

## Contrast

### ～てから vs ～たあとで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てから` says the first action is a **precondition**;
`たあとで` just says it came **earlier**. Both translate as "after", and only
`てから` claims a connection between the two.

</div>

<pre>
くすりを飲(の)ん<b>でから</b>、寝(ね)ました。
I took the medicine and then went to bed. (I went to bed on the strength of having taken it.)
</pre>

<pre>
くすりを飲(の)ん<b>だあとで</b>、寝(ね)ました。
I went to bed after taking the medicine. (Simple sequence; the two are not necessarily linked.)
</pre>

Three consequences you can act on:

- If the second action **depends** on the first - permission, readiness,
  eligibility - `てから` is the right one:
  十八歳(じゅうはっさい)になっ<b>てから</b>運転(うんてん)できます。
- If you want a bare timeline, or the two events have nothing to do with each
  other, `たあとで` is safer.
- Only `てから` measures elapsed time. ❌来(き)たあとで３年(ねん)になります is wrong;
  来(き)てから３年(ねん)になります is right.

Note that the forms differ too: `てから` takes the **て-form**, `たあとで` takes the
**た-form**.

### ～てから vs the plain て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `～て` says "and then", loosely. `～てから` says "and
not until then".

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで歩(ある)い<b>て</b>、電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)りました。
I walked to the station and got on the train. (ordinary narration)
</pre>

<pre>
きっぷを買(か)っ<b>てから</b>、電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)ってください。
Buy a ticket before boarding the train. (you may not board until you have)
</pre>

Both are correct Japanese in most contexts, so the choice is about emphasis.
Where the て-form is genuinely wrong is when the gating matters and the listener
might miss it - a rule, a warning, an instruction.

### ～てから vs ～前(まえ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same two events, opposite anchor. `AてからB` puts A first and
looks forward; `BまえにA` puts B first and looks back. Watch the form: `てから`
wants the **て-form**, <code>前(まえ)に</code> wants the **dictionary form**.

</div>

<pre>
手(て)を洗(あら)っ<b>てから</b>、料理(りょうり)を始(はじ)めます。
I'll wash my hands, and then start cooking.
</pre>

<pre>
料理(りょうり)を始(はじ)める<b>前(まえ)に</b>、手(て)を洗(あら)います。
Before I start cooking, I'll wash my hands.
</pre>

The facts are identical. What differs is which action is the topic of the
sentence: with `てから` you are talking about the washing, with
<code>前(まえ)に</code> you are talking about the cooking.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる**から**、歯(は)をみがきます。 → ✅ 食(た)べ**てから**、歯(は)をみがきます。
  Dictionary form + `から` is "because", not "after". The sequence meaning needs
  the て-form, and dropping the `て` changes the sentence entirely.

- ❌ うちへ帰(かえ)り**ますてから**、晩(ばん)ごはんを作(つく)ります。 → ✅ うちへ帰(かえ)っ**てから**、晩(ばん)ごはんを作(つく)ります。
  Politeness belongs to the last verb only. The `てから` clause is always plain.

- ❌ 仕事(しごと)**のてから**、飲(の)みに行(い)きます。 → ✅ 仕事(しごと)**のあとで**、飲(の)みに行(い)きます。
  `てから` attaches to verbs, never to nouns. For a noun you need
  <code>のあとで</code>.

- ❌ 見(み)**ないでから**買(か)いました。 → ✅ 見(み)**ないで**買(か)いました。
  There is no negative `てから`. "Without doing X" is `～ないで`; "not until X" is
  handled by rephrasing in the positive.

- ❌ 起(お)きてから顔(かお)を洗(あら)ってから服(ふく)を着(き)てから出(で)かけました。 → ✅ 起(お)きて顔(かお)を洗(あら)って、服(ふく)を着(き)てから出(で)かけました。
  Chained `てから` sounds mechanical. Use plain て-forms for the run of events and
  keep `てから` for the one step that actually gates what follows.

## Practice

**1.** よく____から、はんこを押(お)してください。（読(よ)む）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

よく読(よ)ん**でから**、はんこを押(お)してください。

<code>読(よ)む</code> is a む-verb, so its て-form is <code>読(よ)んで</code> - voiced. And since the
signing is not supposed to happen until the reading is done, `てから` (rather
than a plain て-form) is exactly right for an instruction like this.

</details>

**2.** 日本(にほん)に来(き)（てから・たあとで）、五年(ごねん)になります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

日本(にほん)に来(き)**てから**、五年(ごねん)になります。

Measuring elapsed time from a starting point is a job only `てから` can do.
<code>来(き)たあとで</code> would set up a sequence of two events, and "five years
have become" is not an event - so the sentence would have nowhere to land.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please wait until the light turns green and then cross."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

信号(しんごう)が青(あお)になっ**てから**、渡(わた)ってください。

The crossing is conditional on the light changing, which is the core of `てから`.
Japanese calls a green light 青(あお) ("blue"), and <code>信号(しんごう)が青(あお)になる</code> is the fixed phrase.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: シャワーを浴(あ)びるてから、寝(ね)ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ シャワーを浴(あ)び**てから**、寝(ね)ます。

<code>浴(あ)びる</code> is a る-verb, so drop the `る` and add `て`: 浴(あ)びて. The
dictionary form can never sit in front of `てから` - ❌<code>浴(あ)びるてから</code> mixes two
different attachment rules.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) お風呂(ふろ)に入(はい)ってから、ビールを飲(の)みます。
(b) お風呂(ふろ)に入(はい)ったあとで、ビールを飲(の)みます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both describe a bath followed by a beer, and in casual conversation either would
pass unremarked.

(a) frames the bath as the thing that earns the beer - the beer is the reward,
and drinking it beforehand would be doing it wrong. (b) simply reports the order
of the evening: bath, then beer, no particular logic joining them. If someone
asked "so when do you drink it?", (b) is the answer; if the point is that you
never drink before bathing, (a) says that.

</details>

# ～前に

**Meaning** · before doing X - X hasn't happened yet at the moment Y takes place

<code>前(まえ)に</code> puts one event in front of another on the timeline. The whole
sentence can be past, present or future; the clause in front of
<code>前(まえ)に</code> is always in the **dictionary form**, because from the
viewpoint of the main clause that event still lies ahead.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **dictionary form** + 前(まえ)に | 寝(ね)る → 寝(ね)る前(まえ)に |
| Verb (in a past sentence) | still **dictionary form** | 来(く)る前(まえ)に習(なら)い**ました** |
| Verb (in a future sentence) | still **dictionary form** | 来(く)る前(まえ)に習(なら)い**ます** |
| Noun | Noun + **の** + 前(まえ)に | 食事(しょくじ) → 食事(しょくじ)**の**前(まえ)に |
| Duration ("ago") | Quantity + 前(まえ)に - **no** の | 三日(みっか)前(まえ)に、一週間(いっしゅうかん)前(まえ)に |
| Politeness | on the **final** verb only | 寝(ね)る前(まえ)に歯(は)をみがき**ます** |
| ❌ Negative | ❌ない前(まえ)に does not exist - use ～ないうちに | ❌忘(わす)れない前(まえ)に |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)に</code> takes the **dictionary form**, and its
mirror image <code>～たあとで</code> takes the **past form**. That asymmetry is not a
quirk of memorisation: "before eating" is a moment when the eating has not
happened yet, "after eating" is a moment when it has. Japanese marks that
literally. ❌食(た)べた前(まえ)に and ❌食(た)べるあとで are both wrong, and for
the same reason.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `に` can drop when the phrase is doing adverbial duty at the head
of a sentence: 寝(ね)る前(まえ)、少(すこ)し本(ほん)を読(よ)みます。 Keep the `に` while
you are learning; it is never wrong.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
寝(ね)る<b>前(まえ)に</b>、歯(は)をみがきます。
I brush my teeth before going to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
出(で)かける<b>前(まえ)に</b>、かぎをかけてください。
Please lock up before you go out.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)が始(はじ)まる<b>前(まえ)に</b>、飲(の)み物(もの)を買(か)っておきましょう。
Let's buy drinks before the film starts.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の<b>前(まえ)に</b>、トイレに行(い)っておいたほうがいいですよ。
You'd be wise to go to the toilet before the exam.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に来(く)る<b>前(まえ)に</b>、２年(ねん)ほど日本語(にほんご)を習(なら)いました。
I studied Japanese for about two years before coming to Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
暗(くら)くなる<b>前(まえ)に</b>、家(いえ)に帰(かえ)りなさい。
Get home before it gets dark.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる<b>前(まえ)に</b>、コピーを人数(にんずう)分(ぶん)用意(ようい)しておきました。
I got enough copies for everyone ready before the meeting started.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>前(まえ)に</b>、市役所(しやくしょ)で手続(てつづ)きをしなければなりません。
You have to do the paperwork at the city office before you move.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>前(まえ)に</code> is register-neutral and has no contracted form. It appears in
signs, in manuals and in ordinary chat without changing its shape.

The one thing to internalise is that **the dictionary form here carries no
tense**. Look at the fifth example again: the studying is finished and gone, the
main verb is <code>習(なら)いました</code>, and yet the clause still reads 来(く)る前(まえ)に. A
learner who translates "before I came" word for word produces
❌来(き)た前(まえ)に every time. The dictionary form is not "present tense" - it
is simply the unmarked form the construction demands.

Two related patterns worth recognising when you meet them:

- <b>A bare 前(まえ) after a quantity means "ago".</b> 三日(みっか)前(まえ)に電話(でんわ)しました - "I called three days ago." No `の`, and the sentence is
  about the past, not about an ordering.
- <b>前(まえ) also means "in front of" in space</b>, and then it takes `で` or `に`
  according to the usual place rules: 駅(えき)の前(まえ)で待(ま)っています。 Same
  kanji, entirely different job.

In more formal writing you will meet <code>以前(いぜん)に</code> and
<code>～に先立(さきだ)ち</code> doing similar work. Neither is expected at N5.

## Contrast

### ～前(まえ)に vs ～たあとで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)に</code> demands the **dictionary form**;
<code>あとで</code> demands the **た-form**. Neither one ever changes to match the
tense of the sentence.

</div>

This is the single most useful table on the page, so here it is as a pair:

<pre>
ごはんを食(た)べる<b>前(まえ)に</b>、薬(くすり)を飲(の)みました。
I took the medicine before eating.
</pre>

<pre>
ごはんを食(た)べ<b>たあとで</b>、薬(くすり)を飲(の)みました。
I took the medicine after eating.
</pre>

Both main clauses are past. Only the first clause differs, and it differs in
form, not in tense: <code>食(た)べる前(まえ)に</code> versus <code>食(た)べたあとで</code>. Learners routinely write
❌<code>食(た)べた前(まえ)に</code> by copying the English past tense across. Japanese does not.

### 前(まえ)に vs 前(まえ)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)に</code> is **time**; <code>前(まえ)で</code> is
**place**, and it means physically in front of something.

</div>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)の<b>前(まえ)に</b>質問(しつもん)しました。
I asked a question before the lesson.
</pre>

<pre>
教室(きょうしつ)の<b>前(まえ)で</b>質問(しつもん)しました。
I asked a question at the front of the classroom.
</pre>

The two are told apart by the noun as much as by the particle: a *time* noun
(授業(じゅぎょう)、食事(しょくじ)、試合(しあい)) makes it temporal, a *place* noun
(教室(きょうしつ)、駅(えき)、店(みせ)) makes it spatial. With a place noun and a
verb of existence you get <code>前(まえ)に</code> again, but locationally:
駅(えき)の前(まえ)に銀行(ぎんこう)があります。

### ～前(まえ)に vs ～までに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)に</code> orders two events;
<code>までに</code> sets a **deadline**. If English would say "by", you want
`までに`.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる<b>前(まえ)に</b>、資料(しりょう)を配(くば)ります。
I'll hand out the documents before the meeting starts.
</pre>

<pre>
金曜日(きんようび)<b>までに</b>、資料(しりょう)を出(だ)してください。
Please submit the documents by Friday.
</pre>

The difference bites with a fixed time. 五時(ごじ)の前(まえ)に帰(かえ)ります
sounds like you are arranging your afternoon around five o'clock;
五時(ごじ)までに帰(かえ)ります is a promise to be back no later than five. For
appointments and obligations, `までに`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 寝(ね)**た**前(まえ)に、電気(でんき)を消(け)しました。 → ✅ 寝(ね)**る**前(まえ)に、電気(でんき)を消(け)しました。
  Always the dictionary form, no matter how far in the past the sentence is. The
  English "before I went to bed" is what leads you astray.

- ❌ 三日(みっか)**の**前(まえ)に着(つ)きました。 → ✅ 三日(みっか)前(まえ)に着(つ)きました。
  A duration takes no `の`. The `の` appears only when
  <code>前(まえ)に</code> follows an ordinary noun: 食事(しょくじ)の前(まえ)に.

- ❌ 食事(しょくじ)前(まえ)に手(て)を洗(あら)います。 → ✅ 食事(しょくじ)**の**前(まえ)に手(て)を洗(あら)います。
  The mirror error: an ordinary noun does need `の`. (Some fixed compounds such as
  食前(しょくぜん) exist, but they are separate words with their own readings.)

- ❌ 駅(えき)の前(まえ)**に**待(ま)っています。 → ✅ 駅(えき)の前(まえ)**で**待(ま)っています。
  Waiting is an action, and actions happen `で`. `に` in front of
  <code>前(まえ)</code> marks location only with existence verbs.

- ❌ 忘(わす)れない前(まえ)に、メモしておきます。 → ✅ 忘(わす)れない**うちに**、メモしておきます。
  There is no negative <code>前(まえ)に</code>. "While it hasn't yet happened" is
  `～ないうちに`.

## Practice

**1.** 旅行(りょこう)に____前(まえ)に、パスポートを確認(かくにん)してください。（行(い)く）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>前(まえ)に</b>、パスポートを確認(かくにん)してください。

Dictionary form, unchanged. Even though the trip is in the future and the check
comes first, the verb in front of <code>前(まえ)に</code> is never conjugated - 
neither for tense nor for politeness.

</details>

**2.** 日本(にほん)に来(く)る前(まえ)に、中国(ちゅうごく)に（住(す)む・住(す)んでいました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

日本(にほん)に来(く)る前(まえ)に、中国(ちゅうごく)に<b>住(す)んでいました</b>。

The dictionary form belongs to the <code>前(まえ)に</code> clause and nowhere
else. All the tense in the sentence is carried by the **main** verb at the end,
which here has to be past - hence <code>住(す)んでいました</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Wash the vegetables before you cut them."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

野菜(やさい)は切(き)る<b>前(まえ)に</b>洗(あら)ってください。

<code>切(き)る</code> stays in the dictionary form; the instruction ("please wash")
sits at the end. Note the Japanese order is the reverse of the English one - the
<code>前(まえ)に</code> clause comes first, and the main verb last.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 試験(しけん)の前(まえ)で、よく勉強(べんきょう)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 試験(しけん)の前(まえ)**に**、よく勉強(べんきょう)しました。

<code>試験(しけん)</code> is an event in time, not a place, so this is the temporal
<code>前(まえ)に</code>. <code>前(まえ)で</code> would have to mean "physically in
front of the exam", which is not a thing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 六時(ろくじ)の前(まえ)に駅(えき)に着(つ)きます。
(b) 六時(ろくじ)までに駅(えき)に着(つ)きます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply places your arrival earlier on the clock than six - it describes the
order of things and sounds a little odd on its own, because a bare clock time is
not much of an event to be "before".

(b) treats six o'clock as a deadline and commits you to arriving at some point
between now and then. That is what you say when someone is waiting for you or a
train is leaving. With fixed times and obligations, Japanese overwhelmingly
prefers `までに`.

</details>

# ～たあとで

**Meaning** · after X - plain chronological order, with no claim that X caused Y

<code>～たあとで</code> places one event later than another on the timeline and says
nothing else about them. It is the neutral "after": useful precisely because it
does not imply that the first thing had to happen for the second to be possible.

## Formation

The verb in front of `あとで` is in the **た-form**, and it stays in the た-form
whether the sentence is about the past, the present or the future.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **た-form** + あとで | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べたあとで |
| Verb (voiced た-form) | だ-form + あとで | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)ん**だ**あとで |
| Verb (in a future sentence) | still **た-form** | 終(お)わったあとで行(い)き**ます** |
| Noun | Noun + **の** + あとで | 仕事(しごと) → 仕事(しごと)**の**あとで |
| Without で | た-form + あと | 食(た)べたあと、コーヒーを飲(の)む |
| In kanji | あと = 後(あと) | 食(た)べた後(あと)で |
| Politeness | on the **final** verb only | 食(た)べたあとで飲(の)み**ます** |
| ❌ Negative | ❌なかったあとで does not exist | ❌見(み)なかったあとで |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>あとで</code> takes the **た-form**; its mirror image
<code>前(まえ)に</code> takes the **dictionary form**. ❌食(た)べるあとで and
❌食(た)べた前(まえ)に are the two halves of the same mistake. The logic: after
eating, the eating is done (た); before eating, it is not (dictionary).

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `で` is optional and drops freely, especially in writing.
授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わったあと、質問(しつもん)しました is completely natural. There is
also <code>あとに</code>, but at N5 treat it as a different word: it is about a thing
being left *in the place of* another, not about time.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ごはんを食(た)べ<b>たあとで</b>、コーヒーを飲(の)みます。
I have coffee after eating.
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)<b>のあとで</b>、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)きます。
I'm going shopping after work.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>たあとで</b>、みんなで感想(かんそう)を話(はな)し合(あ)いました。
After the film we all talked about what we thought of it.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わっ<b>たあと</b>、先生(せんせい)に質問(しつもん)しました。
I asked the teacher a question after the lesson finished.
</pre>

<pre>
シャワーを浴(あ)び<b>たあとで</b>、すぐに寝(ね)てしまった。
I fell straight asleep after taking a shower.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)がやん<b>だあとで</b>、空(そら)に虹(にじ)が出(で)ました。
A rainbow appeared in the sky after the rain stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
夕(ゆう)ごはんを作(つく)っ<b>たあとで</b>、台所(だいどころ)をきれいに片付(かたづ)けた。
After making dinner I tidied the kitchen up properly.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わっ<b>たあとで</b>、少(すこ)しお時間(じかん)をいただけませんか。
Could I have a few minutes of your time once the meeting is over?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>たあとで</code> is register-neutral and does not contract. Whether the sentence
is polite is settled entirely by the final verb, exactly as with
<code>前(まえ)に</code>.

Its defining quality is **neutrality**. It reports a sequence and stops there. If
you want to say that the first event made the second possible, or that you were
waiting for it, <code>たあとで</code> is too flat and you want `てから` instead. This
is why <code>たあとで</code> feels slightly odd in instructions: a sign that read
手(て)を洗(あら)ったあとで食(た)べてください is grammatical but sounds like it is
describing your habits rather than telling you what to do.

Two smaller habits:

- <b>後(あと) written in kanji is common in adult writing</b>, and the reading is
  あと, not うしろ. Same character, two words: 後(あと)で = "later, afterwards";
  後(うし)ろ = "behind". A learner who reads them the wrong way round produces
  sentences about standing behind a meeting.
- **<code>あとで</code> also stands alone as an adverb meaning "later"**, with no
  clause in front of it at all: あとで電話(でんわ)するね。 That usage is extremely
  common in speech and is not the same grammar point, though it is obviously the
  same word.

## Contrast

### ～たあとで vs ～てから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たあとで</code> reports the order; `てから` claims the
first event was a **precondition** for the second. If you could insert "and only
then" in English, use `てから`.

</div>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)をし<b>たあとで</b>、友(とも)だちに電話(でんわ)した。
I phoned my friend after doing my homework. (What I did, in order.)
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)をし<b>てから</b>、友(とも)だちに電話(でんわ)した。
I did my homework first, and then phoned my friend. (The homework had to come first.)
</pre>

Three practical splits:

- Rules, instructions and permissions want `てから`, because the whole point is
  the gate: お金(かね)を払(はら)ってから入(はい)ってください。
- Elapsed time takes `てから` only: 結婚(けっこん)してから十年(じゅうねん)になります。
  ❌結婚(けっこん)したあとで十年(じゅうねん)になります is wrong.
- A noun can only take <code>のあとで</code>. There is no ❌仕事(しごと)のてから.

And do not miss the form difference: `てから` needs the **て-form**,
<code>たあとで</code> the **た-form**.

### ～たあとで vs ～前(まえ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - mirror images, and the mirror includes the verb form.
<code>たあとで</code> = **た-form**; <code>前(まえ)に</code> = **dictionary form**.

</div>

Put the two side by side on the same pair of events and the pattern is obvious.

<pre>
会(かい)が終(お)わっ<b>たあとで</b>、写真(しゃしん)をとりました。
We took photos after the party ended.
</pre>

<pre>
会(かい)が終(お)わる<b>前(まえ)に</b>、写真(しゃしん)をとりました。
We took photos before the party ended.
</pre>

Both main clauses are past, and neither first clause is marked for tense at all - 
<code>終(お)わった</code> and <code>終(お)わる</code> are chosen by the *construction*, not by when things
happened. English speakers reliably write ❌終(お)わった前(まえ)に because English
says "before it ended". Japanese never does.

There is one more asymmetry worth noticing: a noun takes `の` with both
(<code>会(かい)のあとで</code>, <code>会(かい)の前(まえ)に</code>), so the noun
pattern gives you no clue about which verb form to use. Learn the verb forms as a
pair.

### <code>あとで</code> on its own vs ～たあとで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with a clause in front, <code>あとで</code> means "after
[that event]". With nothing in front, it means simply "later on".

</div>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)が終(お)わっ<b>たあとで</b>、返事(へんじ)します。
I'll reply after I finish the phone call.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>あとで</b>返事(へんじ)します。
I'll reply later.
</pre>

The second sentence names no reference point at all - it just pushes the action
into the unspecified future, which is why it is a favourite for putting things
off. Note that 後(あと)でね。 on its own ("see you later", "in a bit") is a
complete utterance in casual speech.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ごはんを食(た)べ**る**あとで、薬(くすり)を飲(の)みます。 → ✅ ごはんを食(た)べ**た**あとで、薬(くすり)を飲(の)みます。
  <code>あとで</code> always takes the た-form, even when the whole sentence is about
  the future. Nothing here is being said about *when* - only about order.

- ❌ 授業(じゅぎょう)あとで質問(しつもん)します。 → ✅ 授業(じゅぎょう)**の**あとで質問(しつもん)します。
  A noun needs `の` before <code>あとで</code>. Only a verb can attach directly, and
  only in the た-form.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)の後(うし)ろで話(はな)しましょう。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)の後(あと)で話(はな)しましょう。
  <code>後(うし)ろ</code> is a place word meaning "behind". The time word is
  <code>後(あと)</code>, and it is written without the ろ.

- ❌ 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだあとで、運転(うんてん)してもいいです。 → ✅ 薬(くすり)を飲(の)ん**でから**、運転(うんてん)してもいいです。
  Not a grammar error, a nuance error. Permission depends on the first event, so
  the sentence wants the precondition marker `てから`.

- ❌ 日本(にほん)に来(き)たあとで、３年(ねん)になります。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)に来(き)**てから**、３年(ねん)になります。
  Only `てから` can measure elapsed time from a starting point.
  <code>たあとで</code> needs a second *event*, not a duration.

## Practice

**1.** 昼(ひる)ごはんを____あとで、散歩(さんぽ)しませんか。（食(た)べる）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べ**た**あとで、散歩(さんぽ)しませんか。

The た-form is required even though the lunch has not happened yet - the
invitation is about the future. <code>あとで</code> fixes the form of the verb in
front of it and leaves all the tense to the final verb.

</details>

**2.** 大学(だいがく)を出(で)（たあとで・てから）、もう十年(じゅうねん)になります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大学(だいがく)を出(で)**てから**、もう十年(じゅうねん)になります。

Counting years from a starting point is a job `てから` does and
<code>たあとで</code> cannot. <code>たあとで</code> needs a second event to place on
the timeline, and "ten years have become" is a duration, not an event.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll do the dishes after the guests go home."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お客(きゃく)さんが帰(かえ)っ**たあとで**、皿(さら)を洗(あら)います。

The washing-up is not conditional on the guests leaving in any interesting way - 
it is just the next thing that happens - so the neutral <code>たあとで</code> fits
better than `てから`. Note again the た-form on a future event.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 仕事(しごと)たあとで、飲(の)みに行(い)きましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 仕事(しごと)**の**あとで、飲(の)みに行(い)きましょう。

<code>仕事(しごと)</code> is a noun, and a noun links to <code>あとで</code> with `の`.
The `た` in <code>たあとで</code> is not part of the phrase - it is the past ending
of a verb, and there is no verb here.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 手(て)を洗(あら)ったあとで、パンを食(た)べた。
(b) 手(て)を洗(あら)ってから、パンを食(た)べた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a report: two things happened, in that order. It would be a fine sentence
in a diary, and it carries no suggestion that the hand-washing mattered.

(b) says the washing came first *because it should* - the bread was not going to
be touched until the hands were clean. Parents, food-safety notices and anyone
explaining a rule use (b). Same events, but (b) adds a reason for the order that
(a) leaves out entirely.

</details>
