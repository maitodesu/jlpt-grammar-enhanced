# N5 - Existence


---

# ～がある

**Meaning** · there is / there are - something inanimate exists, or is available

`ある` is the existence verb for things that do not move under their own will:
objects, places, plants, and abstractions like time, money and plans. The same
verb covers "I have" (時間(じかん)がある) and "there's a scheduled event"
(会議(かいぎ)がある) - in Japanese those are all just *something existing*.

## Formation

The thing that exists takes `が`. The place it exists in takes `に`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + が + ある | 本(ほん) → 本(ほん)がある |
| Noun + place | Place + に + Noun + が + ある | 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に本(ほん)がある |
| Polite | ある → あります | 本(ほん)があります |
| Negative | ある → **ない** | 本(ほん)がない / 本(ほん)がありません |
| Past | ある → あった | 本(ほん)があった / 本(ほん)がありました |
| Negative past | ある → なかった | 本(ほん)がなかった / 本(ほん)がありませんでした |
| て-form | ある → あって | 時間(じかん)があって… |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ある` has one irregularity, and it is the negative. The plain
negative is `ない`, not ❌`あらない`. Everything else conjugates like a normal
う-verb.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に本(ほん)<b>があります</b>。
There's a book on the desk.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)<b>がありません</b>。
I don't have time.
</pre>

<pre>
この近(ちか)くにコンビニ<b>がある</b>?
Is there a convenience store around here?
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の午後(ごご)、会議(かいぎ)<b>があります</b>。
There's a meeting tomorrow afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
かばんの中(なか)に何(なに)<b>があります</b>か。
What's in the bag?
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)まで、ここに大(おお)きい木(き)<b>がありました</b>。
Until last year there was a big tree here.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、ちょっとお話(はなし)<b>があるん</b>ですが、今(いま)よろしいですか。
Sorry - there's something I'd like to talk to you about. Do you have a moment?
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)にはスーパー<b>がない</b>ので、買(か)い物(もの)のときは駅(えき)まで歩(ある)きます。
There's no supermarket around here, so when I shop I walk all the way to the station.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ある` is register-neutral. `あります` is the ordinary polite form; `ある` is plain.
In formal service or business Japanese it climbs to `ございます`:
何(なに)か質問(しつもん)はございますか - "Are there any questions?" You will hear
that from shop staff and in speeches, and you are not expected to produce it at N5.

Two things learners get blindsided by:

**`が` drops in casual speech.**
時間(じかん)ある?／お金(かね)ない／コンビニあるかな。
This is extremely common - much more common than the full form in
conversation. Keep `が` in writing and in ます-speech.

**`ある` covers far more than physical objects.** Events (試験(しけん)がある),
appointments (約束(やくそく)がある), experiences (行(い)ったことがある),
abilities and resources (自信(じしん)がある). If you find yourself reaching for
"to have" in English, `ある` is usually the right verb.

One historical note: <code>子(こ)どもがある</code> for "to have children" does exist, and you
will meet it in older or formal writing, where it frames children as a fact about
someone's life rather than as people in the room. In modern speech use
<code>子(こ)どもがいる</code>.

## Contrast

### ～がある vs ～がいる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いる` is for things that move under their own will; `ある` is
for everything else. The line is *animacy*, not *life*.

</div>

People and animals take `いる`. Plants - alive, but rooted - take `ある`. So do
cars, buildings, books, and ideas.

<pre>
庭(にわ)に大(おお)きい木(き)<b>があります</b>。
There's a big tree in the garden.
</pre>

<pre>
庭(にわ)に猫(ねこ)が<b>います</b>。
There's a cat in the garden.
</pre>

The animacy test is about how the speaker frames the thing, which is why the same
noun can go either way. A fish swimming in a tank is
魚(さかな)が**いる**; a fish laid out at the shop is
魚(さかな)が**ある**. Ghosts, being willful, take `いる`.

<div class="note">

**Note** - vehicles are the classic grey area. `タクシーがある` means taxis are
*available* here; `タクシーがいる` pictures one sitting there with a driver in it.
Both are heard. When in doubt with a vehicle, `ある` is safe.

</div>

### ～がある vs ～を持(も)っている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ある` says a thing exists and is at your disposal.
<code>持(も)っている</code> says you own it or are physically carrying it.

</div>

Both translate as "have", and English gives you no help choosing.

<pre>
今(いま)、お金(かね)<b>がありません</b>。
I don't have any money right now.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)、お金(かね)を持(も)っていません。
I'm not carrying any money on me right now.
</pre>

The useful hard rule: **<code>持(も)っている</code> needs something you can hold or own.**
Abstract nouns cannot take it, so time, plans, meetings and experience are always
`ある`.

- ✅ 時間(じかん)があります　❌ 時間(じかん)を持(も)っています
- ✅ 会議(かいぎ)があります　❌ 会議(かいぎ)を持(も)っています
- ✅ 車(くるま)があります／車(くるま)を持(も)っています（both fine - "there's a car I can use" vs "I own a car"）

### ～に～がある vs ～は～にある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - put the **new** information late. If the *thing* is new, lead
with the place. If the *place* is new, lead with the thing and mark it `は`.

</div>

Both sentences below are correct, and they answer different questions.

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に本(ほん)<b>があります</b>。
There's a book on the desk.
</pre>

That answers "what's on the desk?" - or describes a scene from scratch. The book
is the news, so it stays on the `が` side.

<pre>
本(ほん)<b>は</b>机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にあります。
The book is on the desk.
</pre>

That answers "where's the book?" We both already know which book; the news is the
location, and news comes after `は`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が**あらない**。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)が**ない**。
  `ある` is the one verb whose plain negative is suppletive. `ない` - and past
  `なかった`, polite `ありません`.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)に猫(ねこ)が**あります**。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)に猫(ねこ)が**います**。
  Animals move under their own will, so they take `いる`.

- ❌ ペン**を**あります。 → ✅ ペン**が**あります。
  `ある` is intransitive. Nothing is being acted on, so `を` has no job here.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は時間(じかん)を持(も)っていません。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は時間(じかん)がありません。
  <code>持(も)っている</code> needs a concrete possession. Time is not one.

- ❌ ここ**で**銀行(ぎんこう)があります。 → ✅ ここ**に**銀行(ぎんこう)があります。
  `で` marks where an action happens. Existence is not an action - it takes `に`.

## Practice

**1.** 急(いそ)ぎましょう。もう時間(じかん)が____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう時間(じかん)が**ありません**（casual: **ない**）。
"Let's hurry - we're out of time."

The negative of `ある` is suppletive: `ない` in plain form, `ありません` in polite
form. ❌`あらない` and ❌`あらありません` do not exist.

</details>

**2.** 公園(こうえん)に古(ふる)い木(き)が（あります・います）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

公園(こうえん)に古(ふる)い木(き)が**あります**。

Trees are alive, but the `ある`／`いる` split is not about life - it's about moving
under your own will. Plants stay put, so they pattern with objects and take `ある`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Is there a post office near the station?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)の近(ちか)くに郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)が**ありますか**。

The location takes `に` (not `で` - nothing is happening there), the thing that
exists takes `が`, and a post office is inanimate, so `ある`. In casual speech you
would drop the particle: 駅(えき)の近(ちか)く、郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)ある?

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)、試験(しけん)を持(も)っていますから、勉強(べんきょう)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、試験(しけん)が**あります**から、勉強(べんきょう)します。
"I have an exam next week, so I'm going to study."

English "have an exam" pulls you toward <code>持(も)つ</code>, but <code>持(も)っている</code>
requires something you can physically hold or legally own. Scheduled events exist
 - they use `ある`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) つくえの上(うえ)に辞書(じしょ)があります。
(b) 辞書(じしょ)はつくえの上(うえ)にあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) introduces the dictionary as new information - "there's a dictionary on the
desk." You would say it describing a room, or answering "what's on the desk?"

(b) treats the dictionary as already under discussion (`は` marks it as the topic)
and delivers the *location* as the news - "the dictionary is on the desk." That's
the answer to "where's the dictionary?" Same facts, opposite information flow.

</details>

# ～がいる

**Meaning** · there is / there are - a living thing that moves under its own will exists, or someone has such a thing

`いる` is the existence verb Japanese reserves for beings that can decide to go
somewhere: people, animals, insects, fish in the water. It also does the work of
English "have" when what you have is a person or an animal - 
<code>兄(あに)がいる</code> is "I have an older brother", and
<code>猫(ねこ)がいる</code> can be either "there's a cat" or "I have a cat",
decided entirely by context.

## Formation

The being that exists takes `が`. The place it is in takes `に`. `いる` is a
regular る-verb, so every form is predictable - which makes it the easy half of the
`ある`／`いる` pair.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + が + いる | 犬(いぬ) → 犬(いぬ)がいる |
| Noun + place | Place + に + Noun + が + いる | 庭(にわ)に犬(いぬ)がいる |
| Polite | いる → います | 犬(いぬ)がいます |
| Negative | いる → いない | 犬(いぬ)がいない／いません |
| Past | いる → いた | 犬(いぬ)がいた／いました |
| Negative past | いる → いなかった | いなかった／いませんでした |
| て-form | いる → いて | 友(とも)だちがいて… |
| Honorific / humble | いる → いらっしゃる／おる | 先生(せんせい)がいらっしゃいます |
| Counting | Place + に + Noun + が + counter + いる | 部屋(へや)に三人(さんにん)います |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いる` conjugates with no surprises at all. Its partner `ある`
is the irregular one: the plain negative of `ある` is `ない`, whereas `いる` gives a
perfectly ordinary `いない`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
池(いけ)に鳥(とり)が<b>います</b>。
There are birds on the pond.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)には兄(あに)が二人(ふたり)<b>います</b>。
I have two older brothers.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)の前(まえ)に大(おお)きい犬(いぬ)が<b>いました</b>。
There was a big dog in front of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は今(いま)大阪(おおさか)に<b>いません</b>。
He isn't in Osaka at the moment.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、田中(たなか)さんは<b>いらっしゃいますか</b>。
Excuse me - is Mr Tanaka in?
</pre>

<pre>
さっきまでここに子(こ)どもが三人(さんにん)<b>いた</b>のに、急(きゅう)に静(しず)かになった。
There were three children here a moment ago, and then it suddenly went quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)に住(す)んでいる友(とも)だちが<b>いる</b>ので、時々(ときどき)英語(えいご)で話(はな)します。
I have a friend living abroad, so I sometimes speak English.
</pre>

<pre>
この会社(かいしゃ)には日本語(にほんご)が話(はな)せる社員(しゃいん)が何人(なんにん)<b>いますか</b>。
How many employees who can speak Japanese are there at this company?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Plain `いる` and polite `います` are the ordinary pair, and neither carries any
special colour. What does need care is the honorific layer and the casual
shortening.

**Honorific and humble forms are compulsory at work.** Talking *about* someone you
respect, `いる` climbs to `いらっしゃる`:
<code>社長(しゃちょう)は会議室(かいぎしつ)にいらっしゃいます</code>. Talking about
yourself or your own company to an outsider, it drops to `おる`:
<code>明日(あした)は一日(いちにち)会社(かいしゃ)におります</code>. Using `います`
about a customer is not rude exactly, but it marks you as untrained.

**`が` drops in casual speech**, exactly as it does with `ある`:
「誰(だれ)かいる?」「いないよ」. Keep `が` in writing and in ます-speech.

**`いる` is also how you say you have a partner.** <code>彼女(かのじょ)がいる</code>
and <code>彼氏(かれし)がいる</code> are the standard phrasings, and by extension
<code>結婚(けっこん)している人(ひと)がいる</code>. There is no verb "to have"
involved anywhere.

**Do not confuse the existence verb with the auxiliary.** The `いる` in
<code>食(た)べている</code> is the same word historically, but grammatically it is a
suffix on a て-form, not a predicate of its own. That is why
<code>待(ま)っています</code> means "I am waiting", not "there is a waiting".

**One homophone worth flagging early**: <code>要(い)る</code>, "to need", is also
pronounced いる, is also used with `が`, and appears at exactly the same stage of
study. It is a completely different verb - see the contrast below.

## Contrast

### ～がいる vs ～がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いる` for anything that can move itself; `ある` for everything
else. The dividing line is **animacy as the speaker frames it**, not biological
life.

</div>

People, animals, birds, insects and fish take `いる`. Plants, buildings, tools,
books and abstractions take `ある`, even though a plant is unambiguously alive.

<pre>
水槽(すいそう)に金魚(きんぎょ)が五(ご)匹(ひき)<b>います</b>。
There are five goldfish in the tank.
</pre>

<pre>
台所(だいどころ)に新(あたら)しい冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)があります。
There's a new fridge in the kitchen.
</pre>

The framing matters more than the noun. A goldfish swimming in the tank is
<code>いる</code>; the same fish sold frozen in a bag is <code>ある</code>. A robot
that walks and answers you may well get <code>いる</code>, and so does a character in
a story. The `ある` page treats this boundary from the other side and gives the
vehicle cases; the two accounts agree.

### ～がいる vs ～が要(い)る

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same pronunciation, same particle, unrelated meanings.
`いる` = exists. <code>要(い)る</code> = is needed. They are told apart by their
conjugation: negative `いない` versus <code>要(い)らない</code>.

</div>

This trips people up because both are N5, both take `が`, and the second is very
often written in kana.

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)に人(ひと)が<b>いません</b>。
There's nobody at the reception desk.
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)では予約(よやく)が要(い)りません。
You don't need a reservation at reception.
</pre>

The conjugation is decisive. <code>要(い)る</code> is a う-verb, so it goes
<code>要(い)らない</code>, <code>要(い)ります</code>,
<code>要(い)った</code>. The existence verb is a る-verb: `いない`, `います`,
`いた`. If you meet `いらない` it is never the existence verb, and if you meet
`いない` it never means "unnecessary".

### ～にいる vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～にいる` states *where someone is*. `～ている` states *what
someone is doing*, or the state that resulted from doing it. Only the first is the
existence verb.

</div>

Because both end in `いる`, learners routinely produce one when they mean the other.

<pre>
姉(あね)は今(いま)京都(きょうと)に<b>います</b>。
My older sister is in Kyoto right now.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)は京都(きょうと)に住(す)んでいます。
My older sister lives in Kyoto.
</pre>

The first is about location at this moment and answers 「どこにいますか」. The
second is about an ongoing situation and answers 「どこに住(す)んでいますか」.
Saying <code>京都(きょうと)にいます</code> when you mean you live there sounds as
though you are on a trip, and it is one of the most common small errors in
self-introductions.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この町(まち)に大(おお)きい病院(びょういん)が<b>います</b>。 → ✅ この町(まち)に大(おお)きい病院(びょういん)が<b>あります</b>。
  A hospital is a building. Nothing that cannot walk away takes `いる`.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)を<b>います</b>。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)が<b>います</b>。
  `いる` is intransitive: nothing is being acted upon, so `を` has no role. English
  "I have a brother" makes 弟(おとうと) look like an object; in Japanese it is the subject.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)<b>で</b>人(ひと)がいます。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)<b>に</b>人(ひと)がいます。
  `で` marks the place an action happens. Existing is not an action.

- ❌ 犬(いぬ)が<b>いらない</b>。（meaning "there is no dog"）→ ✅ 犬(いぬ)が<b>いない</b>。
  `いらない` is the negative of <code>要(い)る</code>, "to need". As written, the
  sentence says you do not want a dog.

- ❌ お客(きゃく)さまは会議室(かいぎしつ)に<b>います</b>。 → ✅ お客(きゃく)さまは会議室(かいぎしつ)に<b>いらっしゃいます</b>。
  Grammatical, but in front of a customer or a superior the plain `います` is
  flat-footed. Use `いらっしゃる` for them and `おる` for yourself.

## Practice

**1.** 木(き)の上(うえ)に猫(ねこ)が____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

木(き)の上(うえ)に猫(ねこ)が<b>います</b>。（casual: <b>いる</b>）

A cat can climb down whenever it likes, so it is animate and takes `いる`. Note the
tree in the same sentence would take `ある` - the choice follows the noun marked by
`が`, not the scenery.

</details>

**2.** 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に野菜(やさい)が（います・あります）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に野菜(やさい)が<b>あります</b>。

Vegetables are alive in a botanical sense and completely inert in a grammatical
one. The test is whether the thing moves of its own accord, and a carrot does not.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Is anyone from the sales team still in the office?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

営業(えいぎょう)の人(ひと)はまだ会社(かいしゃ)に<b>いますか</b>。

People take `いる`, and the location takes `に`. In a call to another company you
would raise it to <code>いらっしゃいますか</code>, and refer to your own colleagues
with <code>おります</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: すみません、この書類(しょるい)にはんこがいますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ すみません、この書類(しょるい)にはんこが<b>要(い)りますか</b>。
"Excuse me, does this document need a seal?"

A はんこ is an object, so the existence verb would have to be `ある` - but the
intended meaning is "is it needed", which is the separate verb
<code>要(い)る</code>. Its ます-form is <code>要(い)ります</code>, never `います`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 父(ちち)は東京(とうきょう)に<b>います</b>。
(b) 父(ちち)は東京(とうきょう)で働(はたら)いています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the existence verb and reports where your father physically is - possibly
for the next hour, possibly permanently. It answers "where is he?"

(b) uses <code>働(はたら)く</code> plus the `～ている` auxiliary, which happens to
contain the same syllables but is a suffix, not a predicate. It describes an ongoing
activity rather than a position, and the place therefore takes `で` instead of `に`.

</details>
