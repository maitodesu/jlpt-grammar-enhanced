# N5 - Change and choice


---

# ～なる

**Meaning** · to become; to get; to turn into - a change that simply happens

`なる` describes a change of state with **nobody responsible for it**. Water gets
cold, a child grows up, a town gets quiet at night. If a person deliberately
brings the change about, Japanese uses `する` instead - that difference runs
through the whole language.

## Formation

The word class decides the connector: い-adjectives take `く`, everything else
takes `に`, and verbs need `ように` first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | drop い + <b>く</b> + なる | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)くなる |
| いい／よい | よ + くなる（irregular） | いい → よくなる |
| ない | な + くなる | ない → なくなる |
| な-adjective | stem + <b>に</b> + なる | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かになる |
| Noun | Noun + <b>に</b> + なる | 先生(せんせい) → 先生(せんせい)になる |
| Verb | plain form + <b>ように</b> + なる | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)うようになる |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form → な<b>く</b>なる | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かなくなる |
| Polite | なる → なります | 寒(さむ)くなります |
| Past | なる → なった／なりました | 寒(さむ)くなりました |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `く` for い-adjectives, `に` for nouns and な-adjectives. It is
the same split you already use for adverbs（早(はや)く / 静(しず)かに）, and
learning it once serves you for `なる`, `する` and every other pattern that needs
a word turned into a modifier.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
夜(よる)は<b>寒(さむ)くなります</b>。
It gets cold at night.
</pre>

<pre>
四月(しがつ)から<b>大学生(だいがくせい)になります</b>。
I'll be a university student from April.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が<b>きれいになりました</b>ね。
The room's got tidy, hasn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだので、気分(きぶん)が<b>よくなりました</b>。
I took some medicine, so I feel better now.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べるようになりました</b>。
My son has come to eat vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)から甘(あま)い物(もの)を<b>食(た)べなくなりました</b>。
I stopped eating sweet things last year.
</pre>

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)になると、この道(みち)は人(ひと)が<b>少(すく)なくなります</b>。
When winter comes, this street gets much emptier.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)したので、ピアノが<b>上手(じょうず)になりました</b>。
I practised every day, and I've got good at the piano.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、姉(あね)は東京(とうきょう)の病院(びょういん)で<b>看護師(かんごし)になります</b>。
Next year my older sister is going to be a nurse at a hospital in Tokyo.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and one of the most-used verbs in the language.

The core feeling is **spontaneity**. Even when a person clearly did the work - 
上手(じょうず)になりました after months of practice - `なる` presents the result
as something that came about, not as something achieved. Japanese prefers this
framing far more often than English does, which is why
「日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)になりましたね」 is a compliment and not a
comment on your effort.

<code>～ようになる</code> is the verb version, and it always means a **change in what
is habitual or possible** - you did not use to do this, and now you do.
朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べるようになりました. The negative counterpart is
<code>～なくなる</code>: 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べなくなりました.

Two things that look like `なる` but are set phrases:

- お世話(せわ)になります - "thank you for looking after me", said constantly in
  business and on meeting someone's family. Never analysed as a change of state.
- Shop staff say 「こちらメニューになります」 for "here's the menu". This is
  much-criticised service-industry Japanese; it is very common, and you should not
  imitate it.

`なります` also does duty for scheduling and totals:
会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)になりました, and 全部(ぜんぶ)で千円(せんえん)になります.

## Contrast

### ～くなる vs ～になる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the connector follows the word class, not the meaning.
い-adjective → `く`. な-adjective or noun → `に`.

</div>

<pre>
外(そと)が<b>暗(くら)くなりました</b>。
It's got dark outside.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)は<b>有名(ゆうめい)になりました</b>。
This town has become famous.
</pre>

<code>暗(くら)い</code> ends in い and is an い-adjective, so it takes `く`.
<code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is a な-adjective and takes `に`. Beware the words that
end in い but are な-adjectives all the same: きれい, 嫌(きら)い and
元気(げんき) give きれいになる, 嫌(きら)いになる, 元気(げんき)になる - 
never ❌きれくなる.

### ～なる vs ～する

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なる` = it changed. `する` = somebody changed it.

</div>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が<b>暖(あたた)かくなりました</b>。
The room has got warm.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)を暖(あたた)かくしました。
I warmed the room up.
</pre>

Notice the particle. `なる` takes `が` on the thing that changes - it is
intransitive, and nothing is being acted on. `する` takes `を`, because there is
now an object and an agent. This `が`／`を` pairing is your quickest test for which
verb a sentence needs.

### ～ようになる vs plain ～なる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nouns and adjectives attach straight to `なる`. Verbs cannot;
they need `ように` in between.

</div>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)る<b>ようになりました</b>。
I've started running every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)が早(はや)く<b>なりました</b>。
The mornings have got earlier.
</pre>

The first describes a new habit, the second a change in a quality. ❌
走(はし)るなりました is simply not a possible shape - a plain verb cannot sit
directly in front of `なる`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 寒(さむ)い<b>なります</b>。 → ✅ <b>寒(さむ)くなります</b>。
  い-adjectives must lose their い and take `く`. Leaving the い in place is the
  most common error with this pattern.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>く</b>なる → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>に</b>なる
  な-adjectives take `に`. The `く` belongs to い-adjectives only.

- ❌ 医者(いしゃ)<b>を</b>なりたいです。 → ✅ 医者(いしゃ)<b>に</b>なりたいです。
  `なる` is intransitive and never takes `を`. The result of the change is marked
  with `に`, whatever that result is.

- ❌ <b>いくなりました</b>。 → ✅ <b>よくなりました</b>。
  `いい` switches to `よ-` in every form it takes - よくない, よかった, よくなる.

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)を話(はな)す<b>なりました</b>。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)を話(はな)す<b>ようになりました</b>。
  A verb needs `ように` before `なる`. Without it the sentence has two verbs
  colliding.

## Practice

**1.** 春(はる)になって、花(はな)が多(おお)____なりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

春(はる)になって、花(はな)が多(おお)<b>く</b>なりました。

<code>多(おお)い</code> is an い-adjective, so it drops い and takes `く`. Note that
春(はる) at the start of the sentence, being a noun, took `に` instead - both
connectors in one sentence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 髪(かみ)を短(みじか)く（なりました・しました）。 You had it cut.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

髪(かみ)を短(みじか)く<b>しました</b>。

You did it on purpose, and the `を` on 髪(かみ) already tells you a transitive
verb is needed. 髪(かみ)が短(みじか)くなりました would describe hair that
somehow became short by itself.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My little brother has become able to swim." (that is, he now swims, which he did not before)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弟(おとうと)は泳(およ)げる<b>ようになりました</b>。

Verbs take <code>ようになる</code>, and the verb here is the potential form
<code>泳(およ)げる</code> - "can swim". The pattern is the standard way to say that an
ability or a habit is newly acquired.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この店(みせ)は有名(ゆうめい)くなりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この店(みせ)は<b>有名(ゆうめい)になりました</b>。

<code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is a な-adjective - you can tell because it needs `な` in
front of a noun（有名(ゆうめい)な店(みせ)） - so the connector is `に`. The
final い of <code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is part of the reading, not an adjective
ending.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子(こ)どもが静(しず)かになりました。
(b) 子(こ)どもを静(しず)かにしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports what happened: the children went quiet. Maybe they fell asleep, maybe
they got bored. The speaker takes no credit and assigns no cause.

(b) says somebody quietened them - the speaker made it happen, and the `を` marks
the children as the thing acted upon. Japanese chooses between these two verbs
every time a change is described, so the choice is never optional.

</details>

# ～にする

**Meaning** · to decide on ～; to go with ～ - a choice the speaker makes

`にする` is the verb of **choosing**. Faced with a menu, a date, a colour or a
plan, this is how Japanese picks one and says so. The `に` points at the option
selected; the `する` supplies the deciding.

It has a second, closely related job: **making something into** a particular state
 - 静(しず)かにする, "make it quiet".

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + にする | コーヒー → コーヒーにします |
| Question word | 何(なに)／どれ + にする | どれにしますか |
| Verb | plain form + ことにする | 行(い)く → 行(い)くことにする |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことにする | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないことにする |
| な-adjective | stem + に + する | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かにする |
| い-adjective | drop い + く + する | 短(みじか)い → 短(みじか)くする |
| Habit / policy | ことにしている | 歩(ある)くことにしている |
| Polite | する → します | コーヒーにします |
| Past (decision made) | する → した／しました | 水曜日(すいようび)にしました |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nouns attach straight to `にする`, but a **verb cannot**. Turn
it into a noun first with `こと`: 行(い)く → 行(い)くこと →
行(い)くことにする. ❌行(い)くにする is impossible.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)はコーヒー<b>にします</b>。
I'll have the coffee.
</pre>

<pre>
飲(の)み物(もの)は何(なに)<b>にしますか</b>。
What would you like to drink?
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんはカレー<b>にしませんか</b>。
Shall we go with curry for lunch?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は来週(らいしゅう)の水曜日(すいようび)<b>にしました</b>。
We've settled on next Wednesday for the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は海(うみ)へ行(い)く<b>ことにしました</b>。
I've decided to go to the seaside this summer.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが寝(ね)ていますから、テレビの音(おと)を<b>小(ちい)さくして</b>ください。
The children are asleep, so please turn the television down.
</pre>

<pre>
体(からだ)のために、エレベーターを使(つか)わない<b>ことにしています</b>。
For my health, I make a point of not using the lift.
</pre>

<pre>
いろいろ考(かんが)えましたが、やっぱりこの赤(あか)いかばん<b>にします</b>。
I've thought about it a lot, and I'm going to go with this red bag after all.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and unavoidable in daily life - it is the verb you use to order
in a restaurant, agree a meeting time, or pick a seat.

The tense matters more than usual here. <code>にします</code> announces a decision
**being made now**, in front of the listener; <code>にしました</code> reports one
already taken. In a café you say コーヒーにします as you point at the menu, and it
would sound odd to say コーヒーにしました unless you had chosen earlier and are
now telling somebody about it.

<code>～ことにしている</code> is not a decision but a **standing policy**: something you
decided once and now keep to.
毎朝(まいあさ)歩(ある)くことにしています means "I make a point of walking
every morning." Learners often reach for
毎朝(まいあさ)歩(ある)くことにしました, which says the decision was taken
today.

In restaurants, <code>にします</code> is one of several ways to order and the most
neutral. 「コーヒーをください」 is a request; 「コーヒーをお願(ねが)いします」
is politer and very common; 「コーヒーで」 is clipped and casual. What you should
not say is ❌コーヒーをします.

<code>静(しず)かにする</code> and its relatives are ordinary transitive verbs meaning
"make X be Y", and they show up as instructions:
静(しず)かにしてください, きれいにしてください,
大(おお)きくしてください.

## Contrast

### ～にする vs ～になる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にする` is somebody's choice. `になる` is what happens.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)を金曜日(きんようび)<b>にしました</b>。
We moved the meeting to Friday. (we decided it)
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が金曜日(きんようび)になりました。
The meeting has ended up on Friday. (it turned out that way)
</pre>

Both may describe the same Friday. The difference is who is standing behind the
sentence. `する` names a decider - even if that decider is left unmentioned, the
listener knows there was one. `なる` presents the outcome as though it arrived by
itself, which is why Japanese so often prefers it in announcements: it avoids
pointing at anybody.

### ～ことにする vs ～ことになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことにする</code> = I decided. <code>ことになる</code> = it was
decided (by circumstances, or by someone above me).

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、会社(かいしゃ)をやめる<b>ことにしました</b>。
I've decided to leave the company next month.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、大阪(おおさか)へ転勤(てんきん)することになりました。
It's been decided that I'm transferring to Osaka next month.
</pre>

The second is the standard way to announce news about yourself that you did not
choose - a transfer, a wedding date, a company policy. Even when the speaker
*did* choose, <code>ことになりました</code> is often preferred because it sounds less
self-assertive. That modesty is a real feature of the language, not an accident.

### ～にする vs ～をください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にする</code> announces your choice; <code>ください</code> asks
someone to hand it over.

</div>

<pre>
じゃあ、この定食(ていしょく)<b>にします</b>。
Right, I'll go with this set meal.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、お水(みず)をください。
Excuse me, could I have some water.
</pre>

Use <code>にする</code> when you are selecting from options - a menu, a list of
dates, a range of colours. Use `ください` when there are no options to weigh and
you simply want the thing. Both are polite; they are answering different
questions.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ コーヒー<b>を</b>します。 → ✅ コーヒー<b>に</b>します。
  The chosen option is marked with `に`. `を` would make coffee the thing you are
  *doing*, which is not a sentence Japanese can use here.

- ❌ 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>にしました</b>。 → ✅ 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>ことにしました</b>。
  A verb needs `こと` to become a noun before `にする` can take it.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)を静(しず)か<b>な</b>にしてください。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)を静(しず)か<b>に</b>してください。
  な-adjectives connect with `に`. The `な` is only for standing in front of a
  noun.

- ❌ 髪(かみ)を短(みじか)い<b>に</b>してください。 → ✅ 髪(かみ)を<b>短(みじか)くして</b>ください。
  い-adjectives take `く`, not `に`. Same split as with `なる`.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)野菜(やさい)を食(た)べる<b>ことにしました</b>。（as a lasting habit）→ ✅ 毎日(まいにち)野菜(やさい)を食(た)べる<b>ことにしています</b>。
  The plain past reports the moment of deciding. To describe a rule you have been
  keeping for a while, you need the ている form.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、私(わたし)はおすし____します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません、私(わたし)はおすし<b>に</b>します。

`に` marks the item chosen. Think of it as pointing: the particle lands on the
option you have picked out of everything on the menu.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来年(らいねん)、日本(にほん)へ留学(りゅうがく)する（ことにしました・ことになりました）。 You applied and chose this yourself.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来年(らいねん)、日本(にほん)へ留学(りゅうがく)する<b>ことにしました</b>。

<code>ことにする</code> credits the decision to the speaker.
<code>ことになりました</code> would present it as something arranged for you - which is
what you would actually hear from many Japanese speakers, out of modesty, even in
this situation.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've decided not to drink coffee at night."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夜(よる)はコーヒーを飲(の)まない<b>ことにしました</b>。

The negative goes on the verb, in its ない-form, and `こと` follows it unchanged.
If this is a rule you have been keeping for some time, say
飲(の)まないことにしています instead.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)試験(しけん)があるので、今日(きょう)から勉強(べんきょう)するにします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)試験(しけん)があるので、今日(きょう)から<b>勉強(べんきょう)することにします</b>。

`こと` is missing. `にする` only ever attaches to a noun, so a verb must be
nominalised first - even a する-verb like <code>勉強(べんきょう)する</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 次(つぎ)の会議(かいぎ)は月曜日(げつようび)にします。
(b) 次(つぎ)の会議(かいぎ)は月曜日(げつようび)になります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker deciding, out loud, right now - a chairperson closing a
discussion. Someone is taking responsibility for Monday.

(b) reports Monday as the outcome, with no decider named. It might be the result
of everyone's schedules, or the speaker might simply be being tactful. Japanese
uses this softening constantly in the workplace.

</details>

# ～にいく

**Meaning** · to go in order to ～ - movement with a purpose attached

This is how Japanese says "go and do something". The verb naming the purpose is
cut back to its ます-stem, `に` marks it as the goal of the trip, and a verb of
movement finishes the sentence. The same `に` that points at a destination is
here pointing at an errand.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + に + 行(い)く | 買(か)う → 買(か)い → 買(か)いに行(い)く |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + に + 行(い)く | 見(み)る → 見(み) → 見(み)に行(い)く |
| する-verb (as noun) | Noun + に + 行(い)く | 買(か)い物(もの) → 買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)く |
| する-verb (as verb) | し + に + 行(い)く | 勉強(べんきょう)する → 勉強(べんきょう)しに行(い)く |
| Other movement verbs | stem + に + 来(く)る／帰(かえ)る／出(で)かける／戻(もど)る | 習(なら)いに来(く)る |
| With a destination | Place へ／に … stem + に行(い)く | 駅(えき)へ迎(むか)えに行(い)く |
| Polite | 行(い)く → 行(い)きます | 買(か)いに行(い)きます |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the purpose verb takes the shape it has in front of `ます`.
買(か)います → 買(か)い. Never the dictionary form:
❌買(か)うに行(い)く.

</div>

The object of the purpose verb keeps its own `を`, and the destination keeps its
own `へ` or `に`, so a full sentence can carry three particles at once:
本屋(ほんや)へ雑誌(ざっし)を買(か)いに行(い)きます。

## Examples

<pre>
映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)に行(い)きます</b>。
I'm going to see a film.
</pre>

<pre>
友(とも)だちに<b>会(あ)いに行(い)きました</b>。
I went to see a friend.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで母(はは)を<b>迎(むか)えに行(い)きます</b>。
I'm going to the station to pick up my mother.
</pre>

<pre>
コンビニへパンを<b>買(か)いに行(い)って</b>きます。
I'm just popping to the convenience store for some bread.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんを<b>食(た)べに出(で)かけました</b>。
He went out to get lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)に公園(こうえん)へ写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)りに行(い)きませんか</b>。
Shall we go to the park on Sunday to take some photos?
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)、いとこが日本語(にほんご)を<b>習(なら)いに来(き)ます</b>。
My cousin is coming to learn Japanese next week.
</pre>

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みには北海道(ほっかいどう)へ自転車(じてんしゃ)に<b>乗(の)りに行(い)きたい</b>です。
Over the summer holidays I want to go to Hokkaido to ride my bike.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. Everything about politeness is decided by the final movement
verb: 行(い)きます, 行(い)った, 行(い)きましょう.

The pattern only works with **verbs of movement**. 行(い)く, 来(く)る,
帰(かえ)る, 出(で)かける, 戻(もど)る, 入(はい)る are the usual set. You
cannot append it to a verb like 待(ま)つ or 作(つく)る; for purposes attached
to a non-movement verb Japanese uses <code>ために</code> instead.

Several very common expressions are the noun version of the pattern and are best
learnt whole: 買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)く (go shopping),
散歩(さんぽ)に行(い)く (go for a walk), 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く (go
travelling), 遊(あそ)びに行(い)く (go round to see someone, go out and
enjoy yourself). That last one has no tidy English equivalent and is used
constantly - 今度(こんど)遊(あそ)びに来(き)てください is the standard "come
and visit sometime".

Adding `てくる` - 買(か)いに行(い)ってきます - signals that you will be back,
and it is what you say on your way out of the house.

## Contrast

### ～に行(い)く vs ～ために行(い)く

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に行(い)く</code> is the everyday form for going to do
something. <code>ために</code> is heavier and states a purpose formally.

</div>

<pre>
銀行(ぎんこう)へお金(かね)を<b>下(お)ろしに行(い)きます</b>。
I'm going to the bank to take out some money.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)のために日本(にほん)へ行(い)きます。
I'm going to Japan in order to study abroad.
</pre>

<code>ために</code> takes a <b>dictionary-form verb or a noun + の</b>, not a stem, and it
suits large purposes - study, work, treatment. Using it for a trip to the shop
（買(か)うために行(い)きます）is not wrong but sounds oddly weighty, like
saying "I am proceeding to the shop for the purpose of purchasing bread."

### ～に行(い)く vs ～て行(い)く

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - stem + <code>に行(い)く</code> means "go *in order to* do it".
て-form + <code>行(い)く</code> means "do it *and then* go".

</div>

<pre>
お弁当(べんとう)を<b>買(か)いに行(い)きます</b>。
I'm going out to buy a packed lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
お弁当(べんとう)を買(か)って行(い)きます。
I'll buy a packed lunch and take it with me.
</pre>

In the first, the buying happens at the destination - that is the whole reason for
going. In the second the buying happens first, on the way. One て-form instead of
a stem reverses the order of events.

### Noun + に行(い)く vs stem + に行(い)く

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a する-noun goes in directly. Only a real verb gets cut back
to its stem.

</div>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)に<b>買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)きます</b>。
I'm going shopping on Saturday.
</pre>

<pre>
デパートへくつを<b>買(か)いに行(い)きます</b>。
I'm going to the department store to buy some shoes.
</pre>

Both are built on 買(か)う, but the first uses the noun
買(か)い物(もの) and names a general activity, while the second uses the
verb stem and names a specific thing you intend to buy. For する-verbs both routes
exist - 勉強(べんきょう)に行(い)く and
勉強(べんきょう)しに行(い)く are both said, the second slightly more
concrete.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ パンを<b>買(か)うに</b>行(い)きます。 → ✅ パンを<b>買(か)いに</b>行(い)きます。
  The dictionary form cannot carry this `に`. Cut the verb back to the shape it
  has before `ます`. This is by far the most common error with the pattern, because
  English "go to buy" tempts you toward the infinitive.

- ❌ 図書館(としょかん)へ勉強(べんきょう)<b>するに</b>行(い)きます。 → ✅ 図書館(としょかん)へ勉強(べんきょう)<b>しに</b>行(い)きます。
  Same rule for する-verbs: する → し. Or drop to the bare noun,
  勉強(べんきょう)に行(い)きます.

- ❌ 映画(えいが)<b>に</b>見(み)に行(い)きます。 → ✅ 映画(えいが)<b>を</b>見(み)に行(い)きます。
  The object of the purpose verb keeps `を`. Only the purpose itself takes `に`,
  and one sentence does not need two.

- ❌ プールへ<b>泳(およ)ぎ</b>行(い)きます。 → ✅ プールへ<b>泳(およ)ぎに</b>行(い)きます。
  The `に` is not optional. Without it the two verbs simply sit next to each other
  with nothing linking them.

- ❌ 友(とも)だちを待(ま)ちに駅(えき)に<b>立(た)っています</b>。 → ✅ 友(とも)だちを待(ま)つために駅(えき)に立(た)っています。
  <code>に + verb</code> for purpose only works when the second verb is one of
  movement. 立(た)つ is not, so the purpose must be expressed with
  <code>ために</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)へ切手(きって)を買(か)____行(い)きます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)へ切手(きって)を買(か)<b>いに</b>行(い)きます。

<code>買(か)う</code> becomes 買(か)い before `に` - the same form as in
買(か)います. Note that 切手(きって) keeps its `を` and the post office keeps
its `へ`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: おかしを（買(か)いに・買(か)って）行(い)きます。 You mean you'll buy them here first and bring them along.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

おかしを<b>買(か)って</b>行(い)きます。

The て-form puts the buying **before** the going.
買(か)いに行(い)きます would mean you are setting out in order to buy them,
with the buying still ahead of you.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I went to the library to borrow a book."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

図書館(としょかん)へ本(ほん)を<b>借(か)りに行(い)きました</b>。

<code>借(か)りる</code> is a る-verb, so the stem is 借(か)り. The destination
takes `へ` (or `に`), and the thing borrowed keeps `を`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)、友(とも)だちが遊(あそ)ぶに来(き)ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、友(とも)だちが<b>遊(あそ)びに来(き)ます</b>。

The dictionary form 遊(あそ)ぶ has to become the stem 遊(あそ)び. Note that
the pattern works just as well with <code>来(く)る</code> as with
<code>行(い)く</code> - the direction changes, the grammar does not.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べに帰(かえ)ります。
(b) 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べて帰(かえ)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says you are going home *in order to* eat lunch - the meal is waiting at home
and is the reason for the trip.

(b) says you will eat lunch first, somewhere else, and go home afterwards. The
choice between a stem and a て-form decides whether the eating happens at the
destination or before you set off.

</details>
