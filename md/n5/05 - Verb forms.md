# N5 - Verb forms

# ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした

**Meaning** · the polite verb endings - non-past and past, affirmative and negative

These four are one system, not four separate words. `ます` is a **politeness**
marker that happens to carry tense as well: it tells your listener you are
speaking to them carefully. Every Japanese verb has a plain form and a `ます`
form, and the choice between them is a choice about the relationship, not about
the meaning.

<div class="note">

**Note** - Japanese verbs fall into three classes, and every textbook names them
differently. **う-verbs** are also called 五段(ごだん) (godan) verbs, or Group 1;
**る-verbs** are also called 一段(いちだん) (ichidan) verbs, or Group 2; する and
来(く)る are the two irregulars, sometimes called Group 3. This page says
**う-verb** and **る-verb** throughout.

</div>

## Formation

All four endings attach to the same place - the **ます-stem**. Get the stem
right and the rest is just picking one of four endings.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final -u → -i, + ます | 書(か)く → 書(か)きます |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + ます | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べます |
| Verb (する) | し + ます | する → します |
| Verb (来(く)る) | き + ます | 来(く)る → 来(き)ます |

Then the four endings, which never change shape:

| Tense and polarity | Ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Non-past, affirmative | ます | 飲(の)みます |
| Non-past, negative | ません | 飲(の)みません |
| Past, affirmative | ました | 飲(の)みました |
| Past, negative | ませんでした | 飲(の)みませんでした |

For う-verbs, the final vowel moves from the う row to the い row. Every ending
behaves the same way:

| Dictionary ending | ます-stem ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う | い | 買(か)う → 買(か)います |
| つ | ち | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ちます |
| る | り | 帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)ります |
| む | み | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)みます |
| ぶ | び | 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)びます |
| ぬ | に | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)にます |
| く | き | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)きます |
| ぐ | ぎ | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)ぎます |
| す | し | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)します |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a verb ending in る is only a る-verb if the vowel before the
る is **i** or **e**. But plenty of verbs that pass that test are still う-verbs:
帰(かえ)る, 入(はい)る, 走(はし)る, 切(き)る, 知(し)る,
要(い)る. They give 帰(かえ)ります, 入(はい)ります, 走(はし)ります,
切(き)ります, 知(し)ります, 要(い)ります - never ❌帰(かえ)ます.
着(き)る "wear" is a genuine る-verb (着(き)ます); 切(き)る "cut" is not
(切(き)ります). Same sound, different class.

</div>

The ます-stem is not only for `ます`. It is the hub the rest of the language
hangs off: 飲(の)み**たい**, 飲(の)み**ながら**, 飲(の)み**ましょう**,
飲(の)み**やすい**, 飲(の)み**に行(い)く**. Learning to find it once pays
off many times.

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

Tomorrow's meeting starts at nine, so please be at the office by eight thirty.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ます` is 丁寧語(ていねいご) - the polite, or "distal", style. It is the default with anyone
you are not close to: shop staff, colleagues, teachers, strangers, and anyone
you have just met. The plain forms (飲(の)む, 飲(の)んだ,
飲(の)まない) are the "close" style, used with family, friends, and people
clearly junior to you.

Three things learners get wrong about that split:

- **`ます` is not honorific.** It says nothing about respecting the *subject* of
  the sentence. Honorific and humble language (召(め)し上(あ)がります,
  いたします) is a separate layer stacked on top.
- **Plain is not sloppy.** Newspapers, essays, academic writing and most novels
  are written in plain form throughout. `ます` in an essay looks like a letter to
  the reader.
- **Plain form is grammatically required inside sentences.** Before と思(おも)う,
  つもり, とき, ので, から and inside a clause modifying a noun, the verb goes
  plain regardless of how polite the sentence ends up:
  明日(あした)行(い)くと思(おも)います, not ❌行(い)きますと思(おも)います.
  `ます` lives at the **end** of the sentence.

The non-past `ます` covers habits and the future alike - 
毎日(まいにち)走(はし)ります "I run every day" and
明日(あした)走(はし)ります "I'll run tomorrow" - but never an action
happening at this moment; that needs `～ています`.

Within a single conversation you normally hold one register. Dropping one plain
sentence into polite speech is a real and natural move - it reads as thinking
aloud, or as a flash of warmth - but a whole reply in plain form to a stranger
is rude. Going the other way, switching from plain back to `ます` with a friend
signals distance, and Japanese speakers use it deliberately when they are
annoyed.

In speech, `ます` is very often followed by softeners: `～ますね`, `～ますよ`,
`～ますけど`. Bare `～ます` sentence after sentence sounds like a public
announcement.

## Contrast

### ～ます vs the dictionary form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the ます-form is about **who you are talking to**. The plain
form is what the **grammar around the verb** demands. They answer different
questions, so they are not really alternatives.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、国(くに)に<b>帰(かえ)ります</b>。
I'm going home to my country next year.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、国(くに)に<b>帰(かえ)る</b>と思(おも)います。
I think I'll go home to my country next year.
</pre>

The second sentence is exactly as polite as the first - the politeness is
carried by 思(おも)います at the end. The embedded verb has to be plain.

### ～ません vs ～ないです

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the meaning is identical. `～ません` is the tidier, more
formal negative; `～ないです` is the plain negative with a polite `です` bolted
on, and belongs to relaxed speech.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は時間(じかん)が<b>ありません</b>。
I don't have time today. (careful, businesslike)
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)はちょっと時間(じかん)が<b>ないです</b>。
I haven't really got time today. (softer, conversational)
</pre>

The same split runs through the past: `～ませんでした` is the formal past
negative, `～なかったです` the colloquial one. In writing, an email to a client,
or any situation with a script, use the `ません` column. With a colleague you
get on with, `ないです` is friendlier.

### ～ませんか vs ～ましょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ませんか` **asks** whether you'd like to; `～ましょう`
**assumes** you would. Both are built on the same ます-stem.

</div>

<pre>
一緒(いっしょ)にお昼(ひる)を<b>食(た)べませんか</b>。
Would you like to have lunch together?
</pre>

<pre>
じゃ、そろそろ<b>行(い)きましょう</b>。
Right, let's get going then.
</pre>

`～ませんか` leaves the other person free to say no, so it is the polite way to
open an invitation. `～ましょう` is for when the plan is already agreed, or when
you are the one leading - a tour guide, a teacher, the person organising the
evening. Using `～ましょう` to make the first invitation to someone senior is
pushy.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べるます → ✅ <b>食(た)べます</b>
  `ます` attaches to the ます-stem, never to the dictionary form. Drop the る
  first.

- ❌ 飲(の)みませんかった → ✅ <b>飲(の)みませんでした</b>
  `ません` has no past form of its own. You put the past tense on the copula
  behind it: ません + でした.

- ❌ 帰(かえ)ます → ✅ <b>帰(かえ)ります</b>
  帰(かえ)る ends in る but is an う-verb, so the stem is 帰(かえ)り, not
  帰(かえ).

- ❌ この本(ほん)は高(たか)いますか。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は<b>高(たか)いですか</b>。
  `ます` is for verbs only. Adjectives and nouns take `です`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)行(い)きますと思(おも)います。 → ✅ 明日(あした)<b>行(い)く</b>と思(おも)います。
  Inside a clause the verb is plain. `ます` only appears at the very end of the
  sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 山田(やまだ)さんは毎日(まいにち)電車(でんしゃ)で会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

行(い)<b>きます</b>

行(い)く is an う-verb, so the final -ku becomes -ki before `ます`:
行(い)く → 行(い)き + ます. The non-past covers habits, which is what
毎日(まいにち) calls for.

</details>

**2.** 昨日(きのう)は雪(ゆき)が（降(ふ)りません・降(ふ)りませんでした）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)は雪(ゆき)が<b>降(ふ)りませんでした</b>。

昨日(きのう) fixes the sentence in the past, so the negative has to be past
too. Japanese marks tense on the verb even when the time word already says it - 
you cannot leave the ending non-past the way English can.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I didn't watch television last night."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)の夜(よる)はテレビを<b>見(み)ませんでした</b>。

見(み)る is a る-verb, so the stem is just 見(み): drop る, add
ませんでした. The plain-form answer 見(み)なかった is equally correct if you
are talking to a friend.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 今朝(けさ)は朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べるませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 今朝(けさ)は朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を<b>食(た)べませんでした</b>。

The dictionary form and the polite ending cannot both be there. 食(た)べる is
a る-verb: remove る to get the stem 食(た)べ, then attach ませんでした.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今(いま)、時間(じかん)がありますか。
(b) 今(いま)、時間(じかん)ある？

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

They ask exactly the same question; only the relationship differs. (a) is the
polite style - what you say to a colleague, a teacher, or anyone you are not
close to. (b) is plain form with the question mark carried by rising
intonation alone, which is what you use with a friend or a family member.

Say (b) to your manager and it sounds startlingly familiar; say (a) to a close
friend and it sounds like you are about to deliver bad news.

</details>

# する

**Meaning** · to do - and the machine that turns nouns into verbs

`する` is the busiest verb in Japanese. On its own it means "do", but its real
job is bigger than that: stick `する` on the back of almost any noun and you have
made a verb. 勉強(べんきょう) "study (n.)" → 勉強(べんきょう)する "to
study". A very large share of the verbs you will ever meet are built this way.

<div class="note">

**Note** - Japanese has exactly two irregular verbs: `する` and 来(く)る. All
other verbs are う-verbs (五段(ごだん) / godan / Group 1) or る-verbs (一段(いちだん) / ichidan /
Group 2). This page uses **う-verb** and **る-verb**. `する` is irregular because
its stem changes vowel from form to form - し, さ, せ - so it cannot be predicted
from the dictionary form.

</div>

## Formation

`する` attaches to nouns. What it attaches to determines whether `を` can appear
between them.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (漢語(かんご) compound) | Noun + する | 掃除(そうじ) → 掃除(そうじ)する |
| Noun (with the object marker) | Noun + を + する | 掃除(そうじ) → 掃除(そうじ)をする |
| Noun (loanword) | Word + する | コピー → コピーする |
| Noun (activity, no する-verb) | Noun + を + する | テニス → テニスをする |
| な-adjective / Noun | ～に + する | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かにする |
| い-adjective | ～く + する | 短(みじか)い → 短(みじか)くする |

The verb itself conjugates like this. Nothing here follows the う-verb or る-verb
rules, which is what "irregular" means - these have to be memorised:

| Form | する | With a noun |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | する | 運動(うんどう)する |
| Polite | します | 運動(うんどう)します |
| Plain negative | しない | 運動(うんどう)しない |
| Polite negative | しません | 運動(うんどう)しません |
| Plain past | した | 運動(うんどう)した |
| Polite past | しました | 運動(うんどう)しました |
| Plain past negative | しなかった | 運動(うんどう)しなかった |
| て-form | して | 運動(うんどう)して |
| Volitional | しよう／しましょう | 運動(うんどう)しよう |
| Potential | できる | 運動(うんどう)できる |
| Conditional | したら／すれば | 運動(うんどう)したら |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the potential of `する` is **できる**, a completely separate
word. There is no ❌されます, no ❌しられます. And because `できる` is
intransitive, its object takes `が`:
料理(りょうり)<b>が</b>できます, never ❌料理(りょうり)をできます.

</div>

`する` also carries several meanings that have nothing to do with "doing":

- **Sensations**, with `が` - 音(おと)がする "there's a sound",
  においがする "there's a smell", 味(あじ)がする "it tastes of…".
- **Deciding**, with `に` - わたしはカレーにします "I'll have the curry."
- **Wearing** accessories - ネクタイをする, マスクをする, 時計(とけい)をする.
- **Costing** - この傘(かさ)は5000円(えん)しました "this umbrella cost
  5,000 yen."

My son is studying economics at a university in Tokyo.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Plain `する` is register-neutral: `します` is polite, `する` is plain, and
nothing about the word itself is stiff or casual. What is worth knowing is that
`する` sits at the bottom of a three-storey politeness building, and the other
two storeys are irregular too:

- <b>致(いた)す</b> - humble. `わたしがいたします` "I'll do it", offering your own
  action deferentially. Extremely common in service and business Japanese:
  説明(せつめい)いたします, ご案内(あんない)いたします.
- **なさる** - honorific, for someone else's action. Note the irregular polite
  form **なさいます** (not ❌なさります). ゴルフをなさいますか。

Because noun + する is so productive, it is the standard way Japanese absorbs
foreign words. Anything that arrives in katakana can become a verb overnight:
チェックする, キャンセルする, ダウンロードする, シェアする. If you know the
noun, you know the verb - this is the single fastest way to expand your active
vocabulary.

One register caution: the 漢語(かんご) compounds (確認(かくにん)する,
購入(こうにゅう)する) are a shade more formal than their native equivalents
(確(たし)かめる, 買(か)う). In an email they sound crisp and professional;
in casual chat with a friend they sound like you are reading from a manual.

`する` also underlies fixed expressions that never translate word for word:
気(き)にする "worry about it", 大切(たいせつ)にする "treasure something",
無理(むり)をする "overdo it". Meet these as vocabulary, not as grammar.

## Contrast

### 勉強(べんきょう)する vs 勉強(べんきょう)をする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are correct and mean the same thing. Insert `を` and
the noun becomes a genuine object, which has two consequences: it can now be
modified, and it now blocks a second `を` in the same clause.

</div>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)を<b>勉強(べんきょう)します</b>。
I study Japanese.
</pre>

<pre>
文法(ぶんぽう)の<b>勉強(べんきょう)をします</b>。
I do grammar study.
</pre>

The first sentence already has a `を` on 日本語(にほんご), so
❌日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)をします is out - one clause, one `を`.
The second sentence needs the `を` version, because 文法(ぶんぽう)の has to
attach to a noun; ❌文法(ぶんぽう)の勉強(べんきょう)します does not work.

How to choose: bare `する` when the compound is behaving as a plain verb,
`をする` when you want to describe or measure the activity itself - 
軽(かる)い運動(うんどう)をする, 1時間(じかん)練習(れんしゅう)をする.
Not every noun takes `を` equally happily: activity nouns (洗濯(せんたく), 散歩(さんぽ), 買(か)い物(もの))
accept it readily, while nouns naming a change of state (成功(せいこう)する,
完成(かんせい)する) essentially never appear with it.

### ～にする vs ～になる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `する` means *someone makes it so*; `なる` means *it comes to
be so*. Choosing from a menu is `する`, because you are the one deciding.

</div>

<pre>
テレビの音(おと)を小(ちい)さく<b>して</b>ください。
Please turn the television down.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)が急(きゅう)に暗(くら)く<b>なった</b>。
It suddenly got dark outside.
</pre>

The same pair runs through nouns: 先生(せんせい)にする "make someone a
teacher / decide on the teacher" against 先生(せんせい)になる "become a
teacher". If a person is deliberately causing the change, it is `する`; if the
change simply happens, it is `なる`. Japanese leans on `なる` far more than
English leans on "become", so when in doubt about a change of state, `なる` is
usually the more natural choice.

### する vs やる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `やる` is the casual,
slightly more physical word: it is what friends say to each other.

</div>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)、もう<b>やった</b>？
Have you done your homework yet?
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)はもう<b>しました</b>。
I have already done my homework.
</pre>

`やる` cannot fuse directly onto a noun the way `する` does. You can say
勉強(べんきょう)する, and you can say 勉強(べんきょう)をやる in relaxed
speech, but ❌勉強(べんきょう)やる is not a word. `やる` also means "give (to
an animal, a plant, or someone clearly junior)" - 花(はな)に水(みず)をやる
 - and it can sound rough or blunt from the wrong mouth, so it is a poor default
in polite company.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ するます → ✅ <b>します</b>
  `する` is irregular: the ます-stem is `し`, not ❌`すり` or ❌`する`.

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)をします。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)を<b>勉強(べんきょう)します</b>。
  Two `を` in one clause. Once 日本語(にほんご) has taken the object marker,
  勉強(べんきょう) must fuse onto `する` directly.

- ❌ テニスを遊(あそ)びます。 → ✅ テニスを<b>します</b>。
  Interference from English "play tennis". 遊(あそ)ぶ means to play in the
  sense of having fun; sports and games take `する`.

- ❌ わたしは料理(りょうり)をできます。 → ✅ わたしは料理(りょうり)が<b>できます</b>。
  `できる` is the potential of `する` and it is intransitive, so what you can do
  is marked with `が`.

- ❌ 外(そと)で大(おお)きい音(おと)をしました。 → ✅ 外(そと)で大(おお)きい音(おと)が<b>しました</b>。
  Sensations - sounds, smells, tastes - take `が` with `する`. Nobody is doing
  anything here; the sound simply occurred.

## Practice

**1.** 明日(あした)までにこの書類(しょるい)を確認(かくにん)____おきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

確認(かくにん)<b>して</b>おきます

`おく` attaches to the て-form, and the て-form of `する` is `して` - irregular,
not built from a stem. The whole compound 確認(かくにん)する conjugates
exactly as `する` does.

</details>

**2.** 来週(らいしゅう)から新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を（します・なります）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来週(らいしゅう)から新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を<b>します</b>。

The speaker is the one taking the job, so it is a deliberate action: `する`.
`なる` would need a different frame entirely, such as
来週(らいしゅう)から新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)になります, which would
mean the job itself is what changes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My daughter practises the piano for an hour every day."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

娘(むすめ)は毎日(まいにち)1時間(じかん)ピアノの<b>練習(れんしゅう)をします</b>。

Both 練習(れんしゅう)します and 練習(れんしゅう)をします are possible, but
here ピアノの has to modify a noun, so the `を` version is the natural one.
Alternatively: 娘(むすめ)は毎日(まいにち)1時間(じかん)ピアノを練習(れんしゅう)します。

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この部屋(へや)は静(しず)かなしてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この部屋(へや)では<b>静(しず)かにして</b>ください。

To make something take on a quality you use the adverbial form, and for a
な-adjective that is `に`, not `な`. `な` only appears directly before a noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部屋(へや)を きれいに しました。
(b) 部屋(へや)が きれいに なりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says somebody tidied the room - `する` puts an agent behind the change, and
the room is marked with `を` as the thing acted on. (b) simply reports that the
room ended up clean, with `が` marking it as the subject that changed.

Japanese speakers often prefer (b) even when they did the work themselves,
because `なる` sounds less like claiming credit. That preference for describing
outcomes rather than agents runs right through the language.

</details>

# て-form

**Meaning** · the connective form - the shape a verb takes when the sentence
isn't finished with it yet

The て-form is not a tense and not a politeness level. It is the form a word
wears when something else is going to attach to it, or when the sentence carries
on past it. On its own it says roughly "…and then", but its real importance is
structural: `～ている`, `～てください`, `～てもいい`, `～てから`, `～てしまう`,
`～てあげる` and dozens more all plug into it. Learn the て-form and a large slice
of the grammar you meet later costs you nothing extra.

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** (五段(ごだん) / godan / Group 1),
**る-verbs** (一段(いちだん) / ichidan / Group 2), and the two irregulars する and
来(く)る (Group 3). This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**. Only う-verbs
have complicated て-forms; the other two classes are trivial.

</div>

## Formation

る-verbs and the irregulars are one-liners. う-verbs change according to their
final syllable, and that table is the thing to memorise.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb, う・つ・る) | → って | 買(か)う → 買(か)って |
| Verb (う-verb, む・ぶ・ぬ) | → んで | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んで |
| Verb (う-verb, く) | → いて | 書(か)く → 書(か)いて |
| Verb (う-verb, ぐ) | → いで | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)いで |
| Verb (う-verb, す) | → して | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)して |
| Verb (行(い)く, exception) | → って | 行(い)く → 行(い)って |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + て | 開(あ)ける → 開(あ)けて |
| Verb (する) | して | 掃除(そうじ)する → 掃除(そうじ)して |
| Verb (来(く)る) | きて | 来(く)る → 来(き)て |
| Verb (negative) | ない → なくて／ないで | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かなくて／行(い)かないで |
| い-adjective | い → くて | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)くて |
| な-adjective | + で | 親切(しんせつ) → 親切(しんせつ)で |
| Noun | + で | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)で |

The う-verb rows again, one verb at a time, because this is the table you will
come back to:

| Dictionary ending | て-form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う | って | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)って |
| つ | って | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)って |
| る | って | 切(き)る → 切(き)って |
| む | んで | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んで |
| ぶ | んで | 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)んで |
| ぬ | んで | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)んで |
| く | いて | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)いて |
| ぐ | いで | 脱(ぬ)ぐ → 脱(ぬ)いで |
| す | して | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)して |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the **た-form uses exactly these sound changes**, with た/だ
in place of て/で. 読(よ)んで → 読(よ)んだ, 書(か)いて →
書(か)いた, 話(はな)して → 話(はな)した, 行(い)って →
行(い)った. Learn one table and you have learned both. This is the single
best return on memorisation effort in beginner Japanese.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - 行(い)く is the exception every learner trips over. By the く rule
it ought to be ❌行(い)いて; it is 行(い)って. Nothing else in ordinary
Japanese behaves this way. Watch out too for う-verbs that *look* like る-verbs:
帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)って (not ❌帰(かえ)て), 走(はし)る →
走(はし)って, 入(はい)る → 入(はい)って.

</div>

What the て-form is for, in rough order of how soon you will need it:

- **Linking events in sequence** - 起(お)きて、顔(かお)を洗(あら)って、出(で)かけます。
- **A continuing action or a state** - `～ている`.
- **Requests** - `～てください`.
- **Permission and prohibition** - `～てもいい`, `～てはいけない`.
- **Cause**, when the second clause is a feeling or a result you did not choose.
- **Manner or means** - 歩(ある)いて行(い)く "go on foot".

## Examples

<pre>
ちょっと<b>待(ま)って</b>。
Hang on a second.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を<b>食(た)べて</b>、会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きます。
I have breakfast and then go to work.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は今(いま)シャワーを<b>浴(あ)びて</b>います。
My brother is in the shower right now.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで<b>歩(ある)いて</b>10分(ぷん)ぐらいかかります。
It's about a ten-minute walk to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
電気(でんき)を<b>消(け)して</b>、部屋(へや)を出(で)ました。
I turned the light off and left the room.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは背(せ)が<b>高(たか)くて</b>、髪(かみ)の長(なが)い人(ひと)です。
Tanaka is the tall one with long hair.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は朝(あさ)から雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)って</b>、試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になりました。

</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)は夜(よる)になると店(みせ)が全部(ぜんぶ)<b>閉(し)まって</b>、人(ひと)も歩(ある)いていません。
Around here everything shuts at night and there's nobody about.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The て-form carries no politeness of its own - politeness is decided by whatever
ends the sentence. 食(た)べて行(い)く and 食(た)べて行(い)きます
differ only at the tail. That is exactly why it is so useful: a long chain of
て-forms lets you build a complicated sentence and only worry about register at
the very end.

A bare て-form left at the end of a sentence is a **casual request**:
ちょっと待(ま)って, 見(み)せて, 手伝(てつだ)って. It is `～てください`
with the polite part bitten off, so it is friendly among equals and rude
upwards. Softening it with `ね` - 待(ま)ってね - makes it gentler; adding
`よ` makes it more insistent.

Two spoken contractions to expect early, because they are everywhere:

- `～ている` → `～てる`: 何(なに)してるの？ , 待(ま)ってるよ.
- `～ておく` → `～とく`, `～てしまう` → `～ちゃう`: 買(か)っとく,
  食(た)べちゃった.

In formal writing, long chains of て-forms look chatty. Written Japanese often
replaces the linking て with the bare ます-stem instead - 朝(あさ)6時(じ)に
起(お)き、新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)む - which is compact and noticeably
literary. You do not need to produce it at N5, but you will see it.

One real limitation: the て-form does not mark *why* two events are next to each
other. It only says they belong together, and the listener infers sequence,
cause, or simple accompaniment from context. When you need the reason to be
unmistakable, use `から` or `ので` instead.

## Contrast

### ～て vs ～てから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `～て` says the events belong together; `～てから` says
the first one **finished** before the second started, and puts weight on that
order.

</div>

<pre>
手(て)を<b>洗(あら)って</b>、ご飯(はん)を食(た)べます。
I'll wash my hands and eat.
</pre>

<pre>
手(て)を<b>洗(あら)ってから</b>、ご飯(はん)を食(た)べます。
I'll eat after washing my hands.
</pre>

The difference is emphasis. With plain `て` the two actions are simply a
routine; with `てから` you are insisting on the order - first this, *then* that.
So `てから` is what you want in instructions and in warnings, and it sounds
fussy when the order was never in doubt.

### ～なくて vs ～ないで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～なくて` gives a **reason**; `～ないで` describes **how**
something was done, or asks someone **not** to do it.

</div>

<pre>
バスが<b>来(こ)なくて</b>、会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れました。
The bus didn't come, so I was late for the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を<b>食(た)べないで</b>学校(がっこう)に行(い)きました。
I went to school without eating breakfast.
</pre>

Swap them and you get nonsense: ❌バスが来(こ)ないで、会議(かいぎ)に
遅(おく)れました. A useful test - if you could put "so" or "because" in the
English, use `なくて`; if you could put "without", use `ないで`. Requests are
always `ないで`: ここに車(くるま)を止(と)めないでください.

Adjectives and nouns only have the `なくて` option:
安(やす)くなくて, 学生(がくせい)じゃなくて.

### ～て (cause) vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～て` can only introduce a cause when the second clause is
something **outside your control**: a feeling, a result, an inability. If the
second clause is a request, an invitation, or a decision, you must use `から` or
`ので`.

</div>

<pre>
ニュースを聞(き)い<b>て</b>、びっくりしました。
I was startled when I heard the news.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は忙(いそが)しい<b>から</b>、早(はや)く帰(かえ)ってください。
I'm busy today, so please go home early.
</pre>

The second sentence cannot use `て`: ❌忙(いそが)しくて、早(はや)く
帰(かえ)ってください is ungrammatical, because a `て` cause clause refuses to
be followed by a command, a request, an invitation or a volitional. This is the
most common way learners misuse the て-form, and it is invisible in translation - 
English "so" covers both.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)いて → ✅ <b>行(い)って</b>
  行(い)く is the one exception to the く → いて rule. Everything else with く
  behaves normally: 書(か)いて, 聞(き)いて, 歩(ある)いて.

- ❌ 飲(の)んて → ✅ <b>飲(の)んで</b>
  む・ぶ・ぬ take んで with a **voiced** で, never んて. Same for 遊(あそ)んで
  and 死(し)んで.

- ❌ 切(き)て → ✅ <b>切(き)って</b>
  切(き)る is an う-verb even though it ends in る, so it takes って.
  Contrast 着(き)る "wear", which really is a る-verb: 着(き)て.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いで明(あか)るいです。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は<b>広(ひろ)くて</b>明(あか)るいです。
  `で` is the connective for nouns and な-adjectives. い-adjectives drop the い
  and take くて.

- ❌ 食(た)べるてください。 → ✅ <b>食(た)べて</b>ください。
  `ください` attaches to the て-form, not to the dictionary form. Drop the る
  before adding て.

## Practice

**1.** 窓(まど)を開(あ)け____、部屋(へや)の空気(くうき)を入(い)れかえた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

開(あ)<b>けて</b>

開(あ)ける is a る-verb: drop る and add て. Two actions in sequence, so the
first verb goes into the て-form and only the last one carries the tense.

</details>

**2.** 時間(じかん)が（なくて・ないで）、昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)が食(た)べられませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

時間(じかん)が<b>なくて</b>、昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)が食(た)べられませんでした。

This is a cause - "because there was no time" - and causes take `なくて`.
`ないで` would describe the manner of doing something, which makes no sense with
an inability in the second clause.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I got up at six, read the newspaper, and left the house at seven."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

6時(じ)に<b>起(お)きて</b>、新聞(しんぶん)を<b>読(よ)んで</b>、7時(じ)に家(いえ)を出(で)ました。

Only the final verb takes tense and politeness; the て-forms in front of it are
neutral. 起(お)きる is a る-verb (起(お)きて) and 読(よ)む is an う-verb
ending in む, so it takes んで.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)って、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)っているから</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)ってください。

A `て` clause used as a cause cannot be followed by a request. Swap it for
`から` or `ので`. The second 持(も)って is fine - that one is manner, not
cause.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んで、寝(ね)ます。
(b) 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んでから、寝(ね)ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both describe the same two actions in the same order. (a) simply chains them - 
it is how you would describe your evening routine, with no particular stress on
sequence. (b) foregrounds the order, so it reads as "not before the medicine - 
medicine first, then bed."

That is why (b) is what a pharmacist or a parent would say: `てから` is the
form you reach for when getting the order right actually matters.

</details>

# た-form / plain past

**Meaning** · the plain past - "did", "was" - and the base that a whole family of
patterns attaches to

The た-form is the plain-style counterpart of `～ました`. It says an event
happened, or that something is now finished. It is also structural: `～たら`,
`～たり`, `～たことがある`, `～たあとで` and `～たほうがいい` are all built on it,
and none of them will make sense until this form is automatic.

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** (五段(ごだん) / godan / Group 1),
**る-verbs** (一段(いちだん) / ichidan / Group 2), and the irregulars する and 来(く)る
(Group 3). This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**.

</div>

## Formation

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **the た-form is the て-form with た instead of て.** Every
sound change is identical; only the last letter differs, and で becomes だ.
飲(の)んで → 飲(の)んだ. 書(か)いて → 書(か)いた. If you know one,
you already know the other.

</div>

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb, う・つ・る) | → った | 買(か)う → 買(か)った |
| Verb (う-verb, む・ぶ・ぬ) | → んだ | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んだ |
| Verb (う-verb, く) | → いた | 書(か)く → 書(か)いた |
| Verb (う-verb, ぐ) | → いだ | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)いだ |
| Verb (う-verb, す) | → した | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)した |
| Verb (行(い)く, exception) | → った | 行(い)く → 行(い)った |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + た | 寝(ね)る → 寝(ね)た |
| Verb (する) | した | 運動(うんどう)する → 運動(うんどう)した |
| Verb (来(く)る) | きた | 来(く)る → 来(き)た |
| Verb (negative) | ない → なかった | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かなかった |
| い-adjective | い → かった | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)かった |
| な-adjective | + だった | 元気(げんき) → 元気(げんき)だった |
| Noun | + だった | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みだった |

The う-verbs again, one ending at a time:

| Dictionary ending | た-form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う | った | 習(なら)う → 習(なら)った |
| つ | った | 勝(か)つ → 勝(か)った |
| る | った | 取(と)る → 取(と)った |
| む | んだ | 住(す)む → 住(す)んだ |
| ぶ | んだ | 呼(よ)ぶ → 呼(よ)んだ |
| ぬ | んだ | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)んだ |
| く | いた | 着(つ)く → 着(つ)いた |
| ぐ | いだ | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)いだ |
| す | した | 返(かえ)す → 返(かえ)した |

Patterns that need the た-form to exist at all:

- `～たら` - if / when. 着(つ)いたら電話(でんわ)して。
- `～たり～たりする` - doing this and that. 寝(ね)たり起(お)きたり。
- `～たことがある` - have ever done. 登(のぼ)ったことがある。
- `～たあとで` - after. 食(た)べたあとで。
- `～たほうがいい` - had better. 休(やす)んだほうがいい。

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

The た-form is plain style. Ending a sentence with it to a stranger or a superior
is too familiar - 食(た)べた to your boss should be 食(た)べました. Inside
a sentence, though, plain is compulsory regardless of politeness: the verb
before とき, ので, から, あとで, and before any noun it modifies, goes into the
た-form even when the sentence ends politely.
買(か)った本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいます is correct;
❌買(か)いました本(ほん) is not.

The た-form does more than past tense. Three uses that surprise learners:

- **Completion, not past time.** バスが来(き)た! - the bus is arriving right
  now; you are reporting that the awaited event has landed. Likewise
  あ、あった "there it is", said while still looking at the thing.
- **A sudden recollection.** 明日(あした)、休(やす)みだったね - "oh, tomorrow's
  a day off, isn't it." Nothing is past; the `た` marks the fact newly clicking
  into place.
- **A sharp command in casual speech.** 帰(かえ)った、帰(かえ)った! - 
  "go on, off you go!", typical of a shopkeeper closing up. Rough, and worth
  recognising rather than using.

In writing, plain past is the default: novels, newspapers and reports are
written in `た`, not `ました`. So the た-form is not "informal Japanese", it is
*plain-style* Japanese - a different axis from politeness.

In casual speech the た-form very often turns up inside the contraction
`～ちゃった` (from `～てしまった`), and `～じゃった` after ん-forms:
忘(わす)れちゃった "oh no, I forgot", 飲(の)んじゃった "I went and drank
it." Learners hear these long before they are taught them.

## Contrast

### ～た vs ～ました

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same tense, different register. `ました` is polite style;
`た` is plain style, and is also the only option inside a sentence.

</div>

<pre>
先週(せんしゅう)、新(あたら)しい自転車(じてんしゃ)を<b>買(か)いました</b>。
I bought a new bicycle last week.
</pre>

<pre>
先週(せんしゅう)<b>買(か)った</b>自転車(じてんしゃ)が、もう壊(こわ)れました。
The bicycle I bought last week has already broken.
</pre>

In the second sentence the politeness sits on 壊(こわ)れました at the end. The
relative clause modifying 自転車(じてんしゃ) has to be plain - this is a hard
grammatical rule, not a stylistic preference.

### ～た vs ～ていた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～た` reports an event as a single point; `～ていた` looks
inside it and describes it as ongoing, repeated, or a state that held for a
while.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)、本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)んだ</b>。
I read a book yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の夜(よる)はずっと本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)んでいた</b>。
I was reading all last night.
</pre>

The trap is verbs of change. 結婚(けっこん)した means the wedding happened;
結婚(けっこん)している means the person is married *now*. 知(し)る behaves
the same way: "I know" is 知(し)っています, never a plain
知(し)ります, while the negative flips back - 
知(し)りません "I don't know", 知(し)りませんでした "I didn't know."

### ～た vs ～たことがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～た` reports a **particular occasion**; `～たことがある`
reports that the thing is somewhere in your **experience**, with no occasion
attached.

</div>

<pre>
2019年(ねん)に北海道(ほっかいどう)へ<b>行(い)った</b>。
I went to Hokkaido in 2019.
</pre>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)へ<b>行(い)ったことがある</b>。
I've been to Hokkaido before.
</pre>

Because `たことがある` is about experience rather than occasion, it clashes with
a specific time word: ❌昨日(きのう)行(い)ったことがあります. It also
implies the experience is unusual enough to be worth mentioning, so it sounds
odd about everyday things - 今日(きょう)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べたことが
あります is grammatical and absurd.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 寒(さむ)いでした → ✅ <b>寒(さむ)かったです</b>
  い-adjectives carry their own past tense. The い becomes かった, and `です` is
  added afterwards for politeness without changing anything.

- ❌ 行(い)いた → ✅ <b>行(い)った</b>
  行(い)く breaks the く → いた rule. Compare 聞(き)いた, 着(つ)いた,
  which are regular.

- ❌ 遊(あそ)んた → ✅ <b>遊(あそ)んだ</b>
  む・ぶ・ぬ verbs take the voiced んだ. So do ぐ verbs: 泳(およ)いだ,
  never ❌泳(およ)いた.

- ❌ 食(た)べるた → ✅ <b>食(た)べた</b>
  For a る-verb you replace る with た; you never keep both.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、映画(えいが)を見(み)ます。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)、映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)ました</b>。
  A past time word does not conjugate the verb for you. Japanese marks tense on
  the verb every time.

## Practice

**1.** 先週(せんしゅう)、友達(ともだち)と山(やま)に登(のぼ)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

登(のぼ)<b>った</b>（or 登(のぼ)りました）

登(のぼ)る is an う-verb ending in る, so it takes った - the same sound change
as the て-form 登(のぼ)って. Note that る-looking う-verbs are common;
登(のぼ)る is one of them.

</details>

**2.** 富士山(ふじさん)に（登(のぼ)った・登(のぼ)ったことがある）？ - うん、2回(かい)あるよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

富士山(ふじさん)に<b>登(のぼ)ったことがある</b>？

The reply counts occasions, so the question is about experience in general, not
about one particular climb. Plain 登(のぼ)った would be asking whether you
climbed it on some specific occasion both speakers already have in mind.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The film I watched last night wasn't very interesting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)の夜(よる)<b>見(み)た</b>映画(えいが)は、あまり面白(おもしろ)くなかった。

The verb inside the relative clause modifying 映画(えいが) must be plain
past: 見(み)た, not ❌見(み)ました. The final adjective carries its own
past negative, 面白(おもしろ)くなかった.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 去年(きょねん)の冬(ふゆ)はあまり雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)りませんだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 去年(きょねん)の冬(ふゆ)はあまり雪(ゆき)が<b>降(ふ)りませんでした</b>。

`ません` takes `でした`, not ❌`んだった`. The plain-form equivalent is
降(ふ)らなかった - note that the plain past negative is built on `ない`, which
inflects like an い-adjective.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 財布(さいふ)を落(お)とした。
(b) 財布(さいふ)を落(お)としてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral report: the wallet got dropped, and that is the fact. (b) adds
`～てしまう`, which colours the same event with regret or with the sense that it
is now irreversible - closer to "I've gone and dropped my wallet."

Both are plain past. The tense is identical; what changes is how the speaker
feels about it, which is a job Japanese hands to an auxiliary rather than to
intonation.

</details>

# ない-form

**Meaning** · the plain negative - "doesn't", "won't", "isn't" - and the base a
whole set of later patterns attaches to

The ない-form is the plain-style negative, the partner of `～ません`. It is also
the foundation of `～ないでください`, `～なくてもいい`, `～なければならない`,
`～ないほうがいい` and `～ないで`. Get the stem wrong here and every one of those
patterns comes out wrong, which is why it repays care.

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** (五段(ごだん) / godan / Group 1),
**る-verbs** (一段(いちだん) / ichidan / Group 2), and the irregulars する and 来(く)る
(Group 3). This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**.

</div>

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final -u → -a, + ない | 書(か)く → 書(か)かない |
| Verb (う-verb ending in う) | う → **わ**, + ない | 買(か)う → 買(か)わない |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + ない | 見(み)る → 見(み)ない |
| Verb (する) | しない | 練習(れんしゅう)する → 練習(れんしゅう)しない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | こない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ない |
| Verb (ある, irregular) | ない | ある → ない |
| い-adjective | い → くない | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くない |
| な-adjective | + じゃない／ではない | 暇(ひま) → 暇(ひま)じゃない |
| Noun | + じゃない／ではない | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)じゃない |
| Past negative (all of the above) | ない → なかった | 書(か)かない → 書(か)かなかった |

For う-verbs the final vowel drops to the あ row:

| Dictionary ending | ない-form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う | わない | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)わない |
| つ | たない | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)たない |
| る | らない | 分(わ)かる → 分(わ)からない |
| む | まない | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)まない |
| ぶ | ばない | 呼(よ)ぶ → 呼(よ)ばない |
| ぬ | なない | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)なない |
| く | かない | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)かない |
| ぐ | がない | 脱(ぬ)ぐ → 脱(ぬ)がない |
| す | さない | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)さない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - verbs ending in う take **わない**, not ❌あない.
買(か)う → 買(か)わない, 言(い)う → 言(い)わない,
会(あ)う → 会(あ)わない, 歌(うた)う → 歌(うた)わない. The reason is
historical - these verbs used to end in a -wu sound, and the w only survives
before あ - but for learning purposes it is simply the rule.

</div>

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **ある has no ない-form**. Its negative is the word `ない`
itself. There is no ❌あらない. Polite: ありません.
時間(じかん)がない "there's no time", 時間(じかん)がなかった "there was no
time". This one irregularity catches almost everybody once.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - 来(く)る is read three different ways depending on the form:
来(く)る, 来(き)ます／来(き)た／来(き)て, and 来(こ)ない. The
kanji does not change, so the negative is read **こない**, never ❌きない.

</div>

Once `ない` is attached, the whole thing behaves as an **い-adjective**, and
inflects like one:

| Form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plain negative | ない | 行(い)かない |
| Past negative | ない → なかった | 行(い)かなかった |
| て-form (cause) | ない → なくて | 行(い)かなくて |
| Manner / request | ない + で | 行(い)かないで |
| Before a noun | ない + Noun | 行(い)かない人(ひと) |
| Polite | ない + です | 行(い)かないです |

## Examples

<pre>
わたしは肉(にく)を<b>食(た)べない</b>。
I don't eat meat.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は授業(じゅぎょう)が<b>ない</b>。
There's no class today.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん、まだ<b>来(こ)ない</b>ね。
Tanaka still isn't here, is he.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)が<b>足(た)りない</b>から、また今度(こんど)にします。
I haven't got enough money, so I'll leave it for another time.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を<b>持(も)って行(い)かない</b>と、ぬれるよ。
If you don't take an umbrella you'll get soaked.
</pre>

<pre>
ここでは写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)らないで</b>ください。
Please don't take photographs here.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)が来週(らいしゅう)なのに、弟(おとうと)は全然(ぜんぜん)<b>勉強(べんきょう)しない</b>。
The exam is next week and my brother isn't studying at all.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が何年(なんねん)も<b>上(あ)がらない</b>ので、新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を探(さが)している。
My pay hasn't gone up in years, so I'm looking for a new job.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ない` is plain style, so as a sentence ending it belongs with friends and
family. To a customer or a superior you want `～ません`. The middle option,
`～ないです`, is polite but relaxed, and is what colleagues say to each other all
day.

Bare `ない` at the end of a question - 行(い)かない？ - is a casual
invitation, not a plain question: "want to come?" It is the plain-style twin of
`～ませんか`, and it is extremely common. 今晩(こんばん)ご飯(はん)
食(た)べない？

The negative also softens requests, exactly as it does in English. Compare
窓(まど)を開(あ)けてくれますか with
窓(まど)を開(あ)けてくれませんか - the negative version is more polite,
because a negative question makes it easier to refuse.

Two things to keep separate. `ない` is the plain **negative** of a verb; the
adjective `ない` meaning "there isn't" is a different word that happens to look
the same. And in speech, ん is a very common contraction of ない in the Kansai
area and in men's rough speech (分(わ)からん, 知(し)らん) - recognise it,
but stick to `ない` yourself until your speech is firmly anchored somewhere.

Written formal Japanese prefers `ず` and `ぬ` in fixed phrases - 
知(し)らずに, やむを得(え)ず - and uses `ではない` where speech uses
`じゃない`. `じゃない` is a contraction of `ではない`, and it is casual: never
write it in a report.

## Contrast

### ～ない vs ～ません vs ～ないです

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one meaning, three registers. `ない` plain, `ないです`
polite-casual, `ません` polite-formal.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は学校(がっこう)に<b>行(い)かない</b>。
I'm not going to school tomorrow. (to a friend)
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は学校(がっこう)に<b>行(い)きません</b>。
I will not be going to school tomorrow. (to a teacher)
</pre>

Note the different bases: `ません` is built on the **ます-stem**
(行(い)き), while `ない` is built on the **あ-row stem** (行(い)か). They
are not two endings on the same stem, which is why you cannot convert one to the
other by ear.

### The verb ない vs the adjective ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three different negatives all end in `ない`, and each is
formed from a different base: verbs take あ-row + ない, い-adjectives swap い for
くない, and nouns and な-adjectives take じゃない.

</div>

<pre>
このカメラは<b>動(うご)かない</b>。
This camera doesn't work.
</pre>

<pre>
このカメラは<b>新(あたら)しくない</b>。
This camera isn't new.
</pre>

<pre>
このカメラは<b>防水(ぼうすい)じゃない</b>。
This camera isn't waterproof.
</pre>

All three then inflect identically, because `ない` is an い-adjective:
動(うご)かなかった, 新(あたら)しくなかった,
防水(ぼうすい)じゃなかった. That shared tail is why
❌動(うご)かないでした and ❌新(あたら)しくないでした are wrong - an
い-adjective never takes `でした`.

### ある → ない vs いる → いない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "there isn't", but they split the world the same
way their positives do: `ある`/`ない` for things, `いる`/`いない` for people and
animals.

</div>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に何(なに)も<b>ない</b>。
There's nothing in the fridge.
</pre>

<pre>
教室(きょうしつ)にはだれも<b>いない</b>。
There's nobody in the classroom.
</pre>

`いる` is a perfectly regular る-verb, so its negative is formed the ordinary
way: drop る, add ない. `ある` is the odd one out - it simply becomes `ない`.
The polite forms are ありません and いません respectively, and both keep the
thing-versus-creature split.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 買(か)あない → ✅ <b>買(か)わない</b>
  う-verbs ending in う insert わ, not あ. Same for 言(い)わない and
  会(あ)わない.

- ❌ お金(かね)があらない → ✅ お金(かね)が<b>ない</b>
  `ある` has no regular negative. Its negative is the word `ない`, and the polite
  form is ありません.

- ❌ 食(た)べるない → ✅ <b>食(た)べない</b>
  For a る-verb you replace る with ない. Keeping both is the commonest
  beginner slip of all.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いない → ✅ この部屋(へや)は<b>広(ひろ)くない</b>
  い-adjectives do not take the verb negative. Swap the final い for くない.

- ❌ 来(く)る → 来(き)ない → ✅ 来(く)る → <b>来(こ)ない</b>
  The kanji stays the same but the reading changes to こ in the negative. Say it
  aloud when you practise, or the mistake will be invisible on paper.

## Practice

**1.** 明日(あした)は日曜日(にちようび)だから、会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

行(い)<b>かない</b>

行(い)く is an う-verb ending in く, so the final -ku drops to -ka before
`ない`. The polite version is 行(い)きません, built on a different stem
altogether.

</details>

**2.** 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)が（ない・いない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)が<b>ない</b>。

Milk is not alive, so the existence verb is `ある`, whose negative is the
irregular `ない`. `いない` is the negative of `いる`, reserved for people and
animals.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I don't drink coffee at night, so I sleep well."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夜(よる)はコーヒーを<b>飲(の)まない</b>ので、よく寝(ね)られます。

飲(の)む is an う-verb ending in む, so the negative stem is 飲(の)ま.
`ので` requires the plain form in front of it even when the sentence ends
politely.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は友達(ともだち)に会(あ)あなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は友達(ともだち)に<b>会(あ)わなかった</b>。

会(あ)う ends in う, so the negative stem is 会(あ)わ, not
❌会(あ)あ. The past negative is then formed the い-adjective way:
ない → なかった.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べないで出(で)かけた。
(b) 朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べなくて、おなかがすいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses `ないで`, which describes the **manner** of the second action: he went
out, and the not-eating is how he did it - "without eating breakfast."

(b) uses `なくて`, which gives the **reason** for the second clause: because he
didn't eat, he got hungry. Substituting one for the other produces something
that a native speaker will hear as broken, even though English uses the same
word "not" in both.

</details>
