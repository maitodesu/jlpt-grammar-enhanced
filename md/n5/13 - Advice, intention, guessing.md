# N5 - Advice, intention, guessing


---

# ～ほうがいい - it'd be better to

**Meaning** · you'd better do it - doing this is the better of the two options

`ほう` means "side" or "alternative", so `～ほうがいい` literally weighs one choice
against another: *the doing-it side is better*. That comparison never fully goes
away, and it is what gives the pattern its bite. Saying it means you have judged
the listener's situation and found their current course wanting.

## Formation

For advice, the verb goes in the **た-form**. This is the form you will use ninety
percent of the time, and it is not a past tense.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (advice) | た-form + ほうがいい | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだほうがいい |
| Verb (negative advice) | ない-form + ほうがいい | 行(い)く → 行(い)かないほうがいい |
| Verb (general comparison) | dictionary form + ほうがいい | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)くほうがいい |
| い-adjective | plain form + ほうがいい | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いほうがいい |
| な-adjective | な + ほうがいい | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かなほうがいい |
| Noun | の + ほうがいい | バス → バスのほうがいい |
| Polite | いい → いいです | 休(やす)んだほうがいいです |
| Past ("should have") | いい → よかった | 休(やす)んだほうがよかった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - affirmative advice takes `た`; negative advice takes `ない`.
There is no ❌`～なかったほうがいい`. The two halves are not symmetrical, and this
is the single most common error with the pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)たほうがいい</b>よ。
You should get to bed early.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は<b>行(い)かないほうがいい</b>です。
It'd be better not to go today.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)んだほうがいい</b>ですよ。
You ought to take some medicine.
</pre>

<pre>
かさを<b>持(も)っていったほうがいい</b>よ。午後(ごご)から雨(あめ)だって。
Take an umbrella - apparently it's going to rain this afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は高(たか)いから、こっちで<b>買(か)ったほうがいい</b>と思(おも)う。
That shop's expensive, so I think you're better off buying it here.
</pre>

<pre>
夜(よる)遅(おそ)くにこの道(みち)は<b>通(とお)らないほうがいい</b>です。暗(くら)くて危(あぶ)ないですから。
You'd better not take this road late at night - it's dark and it's dangerous.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるなら、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)んで病院(びょういん)に<b>行(い)ったほうがいい</b>と思(おも)いますよ。
If you've got a fever, I really think you should take the day off and see a doctor.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)に相談(そうだん)してから<b>決(き)めたほうがいい</b>んじゃないでしょうか。
Wouldn't it be better to decide after you've talked it over with your manager?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ほうがいい` is often taught as a gentle "should", and that is the source of a lot
of accidental rudeness. It is a **recommendation with weight behind it**. The
comparison built into `ほう` means that when you recommend option A, you are
simultaneously saying option B - the one the listener is currently on - is worse.
English "you'd better" carries the same faint edge, and for the same reason.

Some practical consequences:

**Do not aim it upward.**
部長(ぶちょう)、早(はや)く帰(かえ)ったほうがいいですよ
is grammatical, polite in form, and still presumptuous, because you are
telling a superior what to do. To a superior, drop the directive framing entirely:
`～てはいかがでしょうか`, <code>～てもよろしいかと思(おも)います</code>, or just describe the
situation and let them decide.

**`よ` is doing real work.** In speech, `～たほうがいいよ` reads as friendly and
concerned; bare `～たほうがいい` can land flat, like a verdict. `～たほうがいいですよ`
is the everyday polite advice form you will actually hear.

**Hedges are normal, not weakness.** <code>～と思(おも)います</code>,
`～んじゃないですか`, `～かもしれません` all take the edge off without changing the
advice. Native speakers use them constantly.

**`～ないほうがいい` is blunter than the affirmative.** Telling someone *not* to do
something presupposes they were about to. Reserve it for cases where you mean it - 
warnings, danger, genuine bad ideas.

Two spelling and shape notes. `ほう` is very often written <code>方(ほう)</code>, and you
should expect <code>方(ほう)がいい</code> in print. And `いい` swaps freely for its politer
relatives: <code>～たほうがよろしいかと思(おも)います</code> is standard business Japanese,
while `～たほうがよかった` shifts the whole thing into regret - "I should have."

## Contrast

### ～たほうがいい vs ～るほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - た-form gives advice about *this* situation. Dictionary form
compares two options in the abstract.

</div>

This is the distinction the pattern is built on, and it is why the "past tense"
form is the normal one for advice.

<pre>
薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)んだほうがいい</b>です。
You should take some medicine.
</pre>

The た-form presents the action as a completed package - "having taken it" - and
holds that up against the listener's actual, present situation. It points at *you*,
*now*.

<pre>
薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)むほうがいい</b>です。
Taking medicine is the better approach.
</pre>

The dictionary form floats free of anyone in particular. It reads as a general
policy statement, which is why using it on a sick friend sounds oddly detached, as
though you were reading from a leaflet.

Dictionary form comes into its own when the two options are actually spelled out:

<pre>
バスで行(い)くより、電車(でんしゃ)で<b>行(い)くほうがいい</b>ですよ。
Going by train is better than going by bus.
</pre>

There the sentence really is a comparison, and `より` makes the other side
explicit. That is the same `ほうがいい` you meet as the comparison pattern.

### ～ないほうがいい vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ないほうがいい` = *don't*. `～なくてもいい` = *you don't have
to*. One forbids, the other releases.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は<b>来(こ)ないほうがいい</b>です。
You'd better not come tomorrow. (something is wrong with coming)
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は<b>来(こ)なくてもいい</b>です。
You don't have to come tomorrow. (it's optional; come if you like)
</pre>

Mixing these up produces one of the more embarrassing N5 errors, because the two
sentences are almost opposite in social effect. `～なくてもいい` grants permission;
`～ないほうがいい` withdraws it.

### ～ほうがいい vs ～たらどうですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ほうがいい` says the alternative is worse.
`～たらどうですか` merely puts an idea on the table.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)に<b>聞(き)いたほうがいい</b>ですよ。
You should ask the teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)に聞(き)いたらどうですか。
How about asking the teacher?
</pre>

The second leaves the decision visibly with the listener, which makes it the safer
choice when you are not sure the advice is wanted, or when the person outranks you.
It is not always softer - said sharply, `～たらどうですか` can sound impatient - but
its default is more open.

Further up the scale sit `～なければなりません` ("must") and `～べきです` ("ought
to", moral weight). `ほうがいい` sits between "how about" and "must": stronger than
a suggestion, weaker than an obligation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)か**なかった**ほうがいいです。 → ✅ 行(い)か**ない**ほうがいいです。
  Negative advice uses the plain `ない`-form. The た of `～たほうがいい` has no
  negative counterpart in this pattern.

- ❌ 風邪(かぜ)ですか。休(やす)**むほうがいい**ですよ。 → ✅ 休(やす)**んだほうがいい**ですよ。
  For advice aimed at the person in front of you, use the た-form. The dictionary
  form turns it into a detached general statement.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、もっと早(はや)く帰(かえ)ったほうがいいですよ。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、そろそろお帰(かえ)りになってはいかがでしょうか。
  The grammar is fine; the direction is not. `ほうがいい` implies the listener is
  currently choosing wrong, which you do not say upward.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は来(こ)ないほうがいいです。（intending "you don't have to come"）→ ✅ 明日(あした)は来(こ)なくてもいいです。
  `～ないほうがいい` tells someone not to do it. Optionality is `～なくてもいい`.

- ❌ この本(ほん)を読(よ)んだほうが**いいでした**。 → ✅ この本(ほん)を読(よ)んだほうが**よかった**です。
  `いい` is an い-adjective. Its past is `よかった`, never ❌`いいでした`.

## Practice

**1.** 風邪(かぜ)ですね。今日(きょう)は休(やす)____ほうがいいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は休(やす)**んだ**ほうがいいですよ。

Affirmative advice takes the た-form. <code>休(やす)む</code> is a う-verb ending in む, so
む → んだ. The た-form here is not past tense - it is what makes the advice land on
this person, in this situation.

</details>

**2.** 危(あぶ)ないですから、あの道(みち)は（通(とお)らない・通(とお)らなかった）ほうがいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの道(みち)は<b>通(とお)らない</b>ほうがいいです。

Affirmative and negative are not symmetrical here. Affirmative advice uses た, but
negative advice uses the plain `ない`-form. ❌<code>通(とお)らなかったほうがいい</code> is
not a form of this pattern at all.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You'd better take an umbrella."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

かさを<b>持(も)っていったほうがいい</b>ですよ。
（also fine: かさを持(も)ったほうがいいですよ。）

た-form because this is advice for the person in front of you. The `ですよ` is worth
adding - `よ` frames it as helpful information rather than a verdict, which is how
this advice is normally delivered in speech.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 部長(ぶちょう)、その資料(しりょう)はもう一度(いちど)確認(かくにん)したほうがいいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The grammar is correct - the problem is the register.

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、その資料(しりょう)はもう一度(いちど)確認(かくにん)なさってはいかがでしょうか。
（or: …確認(かくにん)していただいたほうがよろしいかと思(おも)います。）

`ほうがいい` carries the implication that the listener's current course is the
worse option, so aiming it at a superior is presumptuous however polite the ending
is. Reframe as a question or a hedged opinion.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)べたほうがいいです。
(b) もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるほうがいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is advice directed at the listener: "you should eat more vegetables." The
た-form ties the recommendation to this particular person's situation, and implies
the speaker has looked at how they currently eat.

(b) is a general comparison: "eating more vegetables is the better option." It
floats free of any individual and reads like a statement of policy - fine in a
health article, oddly impersonal said to a friend over dinner.

</details>

# ～つもり - plan to; intend to

**Meaning** · I intend to; I'm planning to - a decision already made in the
speaker's head

`つもり` is a noun meaning something like "intention", so a sentence with it
literally says *my intention is to do X*. That is why it feels more settled than
"I think I'll" and less fixed than a diary entry: the plan exists, it belongs to
the speaker, and it could still change.

## Formation

`つもり` is a noun, so it needs `だ` or `です` after it, and things attach to it the
way they attach to any noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + つもりだ | 買(か)う → 買(か)うつもりだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + つもりだ | 買(か)う → 買(か)わないつもりだ |
| Polite | つもりだ → つもりです | 買(か)うつもりです |
| Denying the intention | つもりはない | 買(か)うつもりはない |
| Intention that changed | つもりだった | 買(か)うつもりだった |
| Question | つもりですか | 買(か)うつもりですか |
| Noun | noun + の + つもり | 冗談(じょうだん) → 冗談(じょうだん)のつもり |
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + つもり = "under the impression" | 言(い)った → 言(い)ったつもり |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb before `つもり` stays **plain**.
❌<code>行(い)きますつもりです</code> is one of the most recognisable learner errors in
the language.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)は早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)るつもりです</b>。
I'm planning to go to bed early tonight.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、日本(にほん)へ<b>行(い)くつもりです</b>。
I intend to go to Japan next year.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)こそたばこを<b>やめるつもりだ</b>。
This year I really am going to quit smoking.
</pre>

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みはどう<b>するつもり</b>ですか。
What are you thinking of doing over the summer?
</pre>

<pre>
甘(あま)いものは、しばらく<b>食(た)べないつもりです</b>。
I'm planning to stay off sweet things for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいパソコンを<b>買(か)うつもりだった</b>が、お金(かね)が足(た)りなかった。
I'd meant to buy a new computer, but I didn't have enough money.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)を出(で)たら国(くに)に帰(かえ)って、日本語(にほんご)を<b>教(おし)えるつもりです</b>。
After I finish university I intend to go home and teach Japanese.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)にはもう<b>会(あ)うつもりはありません</b>。連絡(れんらく)もしないでください。
I have no intention of seeing him again. Please don't contact me either.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`つもり` is **volitional and personal**. It reports a decision the speaker has
taken about their own behaviour, which puts two hard limits on it.

**No intending what you cannot control.** ❌<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るつもりです</code> is
impossible - the rain has no plans. The same rule blocks `つもり` with verbs like
<code>できる</code>, <code>分(わ)かる</code> or <code>ある</code>. For predictions, `でしょう` or
<code>と思(おも)います</code> does the job.

**Careful with other people's intentions.** <code>田中(たなか)さんは来(く)るつもりです</code>
claims direct access to Tanaka's mind. Add evidence:
<code>来(く)るつもりらしいです</code>, <code>来(く)るつもりだと言(い)っていました</code>.

**`どうするつもりですか` can be an accusation.** Asked flatly, "what do you intend to
do?" carries the tone of a parent facing a broken window. Aimed at a superior it
is worse - <code>部長(ぶちょう)はいつ帰(かえ)るつもりですか</code> sounds like a
challenge. Upward, ask about their <code>ご予定(よてい)</code> instead:
<code>部長(ぶちょう)のご予定(よてい)はいかがでしょうか</code>.

**`つもりはない` is a door slamming.** It denies that the intention exists at all,
and in an argument it is very cold. `～ないつもりです` is the mild version.

**`つもりだった` almost always implies the plan failed or changed.** Nobody says
<code>行(い)くつもりだった</code> about a trip they took; it sets up a "but".

**A second life as a plain noun.** <code>冗談(じょうだん)のつもりだった</code> ("I meant
it as a joke"), <code>言(い)ったつもりだったんだけど</code> ("I was sure I'd told you").
Here `つもり` is about what you *believed*, not what you planned, and the belief is
usually wrong. Register-wise `つもり` itself is neutral; `つもりです` is fine in a job
interview, and `つもり` bare is fine with friends.

## Contrast

### ～つもりです vs ～予定(よてい)です

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもり` is what you have decided. <code>予定(よてい)</code> is what
is on the calendar. Intention lives in your head; a schedule exists outside it.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、引(ひ)っ<b>越(こ)すつもりです</b>。
I'm planning to move next month. (I've decided; nothing is booked yet)
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)す予定(よてい)です。
I'm due to move next month. (it's arranged - date set, movers booked)
</pre>

Because <code>予定(よてい)</code> points at an arrangement rather than a state of mind,
it is also the polite way to ask about someone senior, where `つもり` would be
intrusive.

### ～つもりです vs ～(よ)うと思(おも)います

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもり` reports a decision already taken.
<code>～(よ)うと思(おも)います</code> presents the thought as forming now, which makes it
softer.

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は海(うみ)へ<b>行(い)くつもりです</b>。
I'm planning to go to the sea at the weekend.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は海(うみ)へ行(い)こうと思(おも)います。
I think I'll head to the sea at the weekend.
</pre>

The second is the safer answer to a casual question, because it does not commit
you. The first can sound slightly firm, even stubborn, if the listener was hoping
to change your mind.

### ～ないつもりです vs ～つもりはありません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ないつもり` = I plan not to. `～つもりはない` = I have no such
plan, and I resent the suggestion.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)はお酒(さけ)を<b>飲(の)まないつもりです</b>。
I'm planning not to drink today.
</pre>

<pre>
お酒(さけ)を<b>飲(の)むつもりはありません</b>。
I have no intention of drinking.
</pre>

The first describes a resolution you have made for yourself. The second rejects
the whole idea, and depending on the tone it can be a refusal, a defence, or a
rebuke. It is a useful sentence to understand and a risky one to use.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)、行(い)き**ます**つもりです。 → ✅ 明日(あした)、行(い)**く**つもりです。
  `つもり` follows a plain form. The politeness goes on `です` at the end of the
  sentence, once.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るつもりです。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るでしょう。
  Only a person with a choice can have an intention. Weather takes `でしょう`.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さんは来(こ)ないつもりです。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さんは来(こ)ないつもりらしいです。
  Asserting someone else's intentions flatly claims to read their mind. Mark the
  evidence.

- ❌ これは冗談(じょうだん)つもりでした。 → ✅ これは冗談(じょうだん)のつもりでした。
  `つもり` is a noun, so another noun joins it with `の`.

- ❌ 社長(しゃちょう)、いつ海外(かいがい)へ行(い)くつもりですか。 → ✅ 社長(しゃちょう)、海外(かいがい)へはいつ行(い)かれるご予定(よてい)ですか。
  The grammar is sound; asking a superior about their `つもり` sounds like an
  interrogation.

## Practice

**1.** 来週(らいしゅう)から毎日(まいにち)３０分(ぷん)走(はし)____つもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

毎日(まいにち)３０分(ぷん)走(はし)<b>る</b>つもりです。

`つもり` takes the plain dictionary form, so <code>走(はし)る</code> stays as it is. The
politeness is carried by `です` at the end - never by the verb inside.

</details>

**2.** 来年(らいねん)、結婚(けっこん)する（つもり・予定(よてい)）です。式場(しきじょう)も、もう予約(よやく)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来年(らいねん)、結婚(けっこん)する<b>予定(よてい)</b>です。

The venue is already booked, so this is an arrangement in the world, not a plan in
the speaker's head. <code>予定(よてい)</code> is what fits. `つもり` would oddly suggest
nothing had been settled yet.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I was going to call you yesterday, but I forgot."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

きのう電話(でんわ)を<b>するつもりだった</b>んですが、忘(わす)れてしまいました。

`つもりだった` is the form for an intention that did not survive contact with
reality, and it practically demands a `が` or `けど` clause explaining what went
wrong.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 部長(ぶちょう)は明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)に出(で)るつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)に出(で)られるご予定(よてい)です。
（or, if you were told so: …出(で)るつもりだとおっしゃっていました。）

Two problems at once: you cannot assert another person's intention as fact, and
`つもり` is too familiar to use of a superior. Switch to
<code>ご予定(よてい)</code>, or quote your source.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)には出(で)ないつもりです。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)に出(で)るつもりはありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plan: the speaker has decided to skip the meeting, and the sentence
invites a follow-up reason without any heat in it.

(b) denies that the intention exists at all. It is flat and final, and in a
workplace it would read as a refusal - the kind of thing said when someone has
just pressed you to attend. Same facts, very different temperature.

</details>

# だろう・でしょう - probably; … right?

**Meaning** · probably - or, said with a rising tone, "…don't you think?"

`だろう` and `でしょう` are the same word in two politeness levels, and that word
does one job: it stops a sentence being a flat assertion. Whether it comes out as
a guess or as an appeal for agreement is decided almost entirely by **intonation**
 - falling for "probably", rising for "right?". Learners are usually taught only
the first half.

## Formation

`でしょう` attaches to **plain forms**, and the `だ` of nouns and な-adjectives
disappears in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + でしょう | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るでしょう |
| Verb (past) | た-form + でしょう | 降(ふ)った → 降(ふ)ったでしょう |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + でしょう | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないでしょう |
| い-adjective | plain form + でしょう | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いでしょう |
| な-adjective | drop だ + でしょう | 便利(べんり)だ → 便利(べんり)でしょう |
| Noun | noun + でしょう | 雨(あめ) → 雨(あめ)でしょう |
| Plain / written style | でしょう → だろう | 降(ふ)るだろう |
| Casual spoken | でしょう → でしょ／だろ | 降(ふ)るでしょ |
| Common partners | たぶん, きっと, もしかしたら | たぶん降(ふ)るでしょう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `だ` and no `ます` in front. ❌<code>学生(がくせい)だでしょう</code>,
❌<code>行(い)きますでしょう</code>. The politeness of the sentence is already carried by
`でしょう` itself.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>でしょう</b>。
It will probably rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
たぶん田中(たなか)さんは<b>来(こ)ないでしょう</b>。
Tanaka probably won't come.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)、<b>難(むずか)しいでしょう</b>？
This question's hard, isn't it?
</pre>

<pre>
今夜(こんや)はかなり<b>冷(ひ)えるだろう</b>。
It'll get pretty cold tonight.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)はもう空港(くうこう)に<b>着(つ)いているでしょう</b>。
He's probably already arrived at the airport.
</pre>

<pre>
ほら、言(い)った<b>でしょう</b>？だから傘(かさ)を持(も)っていけばよかったのに。
See? I told you. You should have taken an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
午後(ごご)から風(かぜ)が<b>強(つよ)くなるでしょう</b>。おでかけの方(かた)はご注意(ちゅうい)ください。
The wind will strengthen from the afternoon. Those going out should take care.
</pre>

<pre>
その映画(えいが)、もう<b>見(み)たでしょう</b>？どうだった？
You've already seen that film, haven't you? What did you think?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The two senses are one form.** Falling intonation gives conjecture: the speaker
is guessing and knows they are guessing. Rising intonation turns the sentence
outward and asks the listener to agree. In writing there is no intonation, so a
`？` marks the second sense - and a `でしょう` at the end of a written forecast is
always the first.

**`だろう` is plain, and in speech it leans male.** In writing - essays, novels,
news commentary - `だろう` is simply the plain-style equivalent of `でしょう` and
carries no gender at all. In conversation, bare <code>来(く)るだろう</code> and the
clipped `だろ？` sound distinctly masculine and rather rough; women's casual speech
prefers `でしょ？` or `～よね？`. `でしょ` is the everyday spoken shortening for
everyone.

**Weather forecasts live on `でしょう`.** Japanese meteorologists do not assert;
they say <code>晴(は)れるでしょう</code>, <code>雪(ゆき)になるでしょう</code>. Hearing that
register helps fix the falling, conjectural sense in your ear.

**The rising `でしょう？` has an edge.** It presses the listener toward your view.
<code>だから言(い)ったでしょう</code> is a genuine "I told you so", and used upward it
is bad news: <code>部長(ぶちょう)、これでいいでしょう？</code> is telling your boss what to
think. The upward version is a question - <code>これでよろしいでしょうか</code> - where
`でしょう` has become deference rather than pressure.

**Do not guess about your own choices.** ❌<code>私(わたし)は明日(あした)行(い)くでしょう</code>
is strange for the same reason "I will probably go, I suppose" is strange about a
decision that is yours to make. Use <code>行(い)きます</code> or
<code>行(い)くつもりです</code>. `でしょう` about yourself is only natural for things
outside your control: <code>私(わたし)も太(ふと)るでしょうね</code>.

## Contrast

### だろう vs でしょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different level. `でしょう` is the ます／です-style
form; `だろう` is the plain-style form and, in speech, noticeably blunt.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)には終(お)わる<b>でしょう</b>。
It'll probably be finished by next week.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)には終(お)わる<b>だろう</b>。
It'll probably be finished by next week.
</pre>

The English is identical; the Japanese is not interchangeable. Use `でしょう` with
anyone you would use `です` with. Use `だろう` in writing, in a diary, or with close
friends - and be aware that a man and a woman saying `だろう` out loud will be heard
differently, fairly or not.

### Falling でしょう vs rising でしょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - falling = *I think so, but I'm not sure*. Rising = *you know
this too, don't you?* One looks inward at the speaker's uncertainty; the other
reaches out to the listener.

</div>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんはこのニュースを<b>知(し)らないでしょう</b>。
Yamada probably doesn't know about this news. (falling - a guess)
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんもこのニュース、<b>知(し)っているでしょう</b>？
Yamada knows about this news too, right? (rising - checking with you)
</pre>

The rising version assumes the listener is in a position to confirm, so it is used
about things they can be expected to know - shared experience, obvious facts, or
something you told them earlier. Using it about something they could not possibly
know sounds like an interrogation.

### でしょう vs かもしれません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a rough scale of confidence:
`かもしれません` (maybe, 50/50) → `でしょう` (probably) → `はずです` (it should be, by
reasoning) → <code>に違(ちが)いありません</code> (it must be).

</div>

<pre>
午後(ごご)から雪(ゆき)になる<b>でしょう</b>。
It'll probably turn to snow this afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
午後(ごご)から雪(ゆき)になるかもしれません。
It might turn to snow this afternoon.
</pre>

<code>と思(おも)います</code> sits alongside these but does something different: it
labels the statement as the speaker's personal opinion rather than measuring
likelihood. <code>来(こ)ないでしょう</code> is a forecast about the world;
<code>来(こ)ないと思(おも)います</code> is what *I* think - which is why the second is
often the politer choice when you might be wrong.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は学生(がくせい)**だ**でしょう。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は学生(がくせい)でしょう。
  Nouns and な-adjectives lose their `だ` before `でしょう`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り**ます**でしょう。 → ✅ 明日(あした)、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るでしょう。
  `でしょう` follows plain forms. Doubling the politeness sounds like a bad
  announcement voice.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)大学(だいがく)に入(はい)るでしょう。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)大学(だいがく)に入(はい)るつもりです。
  You do not speculate about your own decisions.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、この案(あん)でいいでしょう？ → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、この案(あん)でよろしいでしょうか。
  Rising `でしょう？` pushes the listener to agree. Upward, ask.

- ❌ 静(しず)か**だろう**でしょう。 → ✅ 静(しず)かでしょう。
  `だろう` and `でしょう` are the same word; they never stack.

## Practice

**1.** 天気(てんき)予報(よほう)によると、明日(あした)は寒(さむ)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)は寒(さむ)<b>いでしょう</b>。

い-adjectives keep their `い` and simply take `でしょう`. A forecast is the textbook
home of falling-tone `でしょう`: the speaker is predicting, not asserting.

</details>

**2.** 一緒(いっしょ)に習(なら)ったんだから、この漢字(かんじ)は読(よ)める（でしょう？・かもしれません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この漢字(かんじ)は読(よ)める<b>でしょう？</b>

You studied it together, so you are appealing to shared knowledge and expecting
agreement - that is rising `でしょう？`. `かもしれません` would mean you have no idea
whether they can read it, which contradicts the first half of the sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He's probably still at work."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)はまだ会社(かいしゃ)に<b>いるでしょう</b>。
（plain: 彼(かれ)はまだ会社(かいしゃ)にいるだろう。）

`でしょう` attaches straight to the plain <code>いる</code>. Choose `だろう` only if the
surrounding sentences are also plain style.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: あの人(ひと)はたぶん先生(せんせい)だでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの人(ひと)はたぶん先生(せんせい)でしょう。

The `だ` has to go. `でしょう` is itself the conjectural form of `だ`／`です`, so
keeping both is like saying "is probably is". Note how naturally `たぶん` pairs with
the falling sense.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この店(みせ)、高(たか)いでしょう。
(b) この店(みせ)、高(たか)いでしょう？

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a), said with a falling tone, is a guess about a shop the speaker does not know
first-hand: "this place is probably expensive." It might be a reason for not going
in.

(b), rising, assumes the listener already knows - they have been there, or the
prices are on the board in front of you - and asks them to agree: "pricey, isn't
it?" Same eight characters; the tone decides who is supposed to know the answer.

</details>

# んです・のです - explaining, or asking for an explanation

**Meaning** · here's the background - or, as a question, so what's the story?

`んです` does not change what a sentence says. It changes what the sentence is
*for*. Adding it tells the listener that this statement is tied to the situation
you are both in: it explains something, or asks the other person to explain.
`んです` is the spoken form, `のです` the written one, and the `ん` is simply `の`
worn down by speech.

## Formation

`の` is a nominaliser, so everything attaches to it the way it attaches to a noun - 
which is why nouns and な-adjectives need `な` in between.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + んです | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れるんです |
| Verb (past) | た-form + んです | 遅(おく)れた → 遅(おく)れたんです |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + んです | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないんです |
| い-adjective | plain form + んです | 痛(いた)い → 痛(いた)いんです |
| な-adjective | drop だ + **な** + んです | 大変(たいへん)だ → 大変(たいへん)なんです |
| Noun | noun + **な** + んです | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)なんです |
| Plain style | んです → んだ | 痛(いた)いんだ |
| Written / formal | んです → のです | 痛(いた)いのです |
| Question | んですか | どうした → どうしたんですか |
| Preface, softener | んですが／んですけど | 聞(き)きたいんですが |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `な` after nouns and な-adjectives, nothing after verbs and
い-adjectives. ❌<code>学生(がくせい)だんです</code> is the classic slip;
<code>学生(がくせい)なんです</code> is what you want.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どうした<b>んです</b>か。
What's the matter?
</pre>

<pre>
頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い<b>んです</b>。
I've got a headache. (that's why I'm asking to go home)
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、道(みち)が分(わ)からない<b>んですが</b>…
Excuse me - I'm a bit lost…
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)はフランス人(じん)<b>なんです</b>。だからワインにくわしいんですよ。
She's French. That's why she knows so much about wine.
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)、なんて読(よ)む<b>んです</b>か。
How do you read this kanji?
</pre>

<pre>
「どうして遅(おく)れたんですか。」「事故(じこ)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まった<b>んです</b>。」
"Why were you late?" "The trains were stopped because of an accident."
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)、休(やす)ませていただけませんか。国(くに)から母(はは)が来(く)る<b>んです</b>。
Could I take tomorrow off? My mother is coming over from home.
</pre>

<pre>
実(じつ)は来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>のです</b>。長(なが)い間(あいだ)お世話(せわ)になりました。
The fact is, I'm moving next month. Thank you for everything over the years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**What `んです` adds is *connection*.** <code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っています</code> reports
the weather. <code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っているんです</code> offers it as the reason for
something - why I'm wet, why the match is off, why I'd rather not go out. Nothing
about the rain has changed; the sentence has been plugged into the conversation.

**That is also its danger.** A learner who ends every sentence with `んです` sounds
as if they are permanently explaining themselves, or making excuses. In neutral
self-introduction and plain description, use plain `です`／`ます`.
<code>東京(とうきょう)に住(す)んでいます</code> is a fact about you;
<code>東京(とうきょう)に住(す)んでいるんです</code> answers a question that was asked, out
loud or in the air.

**`んですか` can accuse.** Because the question demands an explanation, tone decides
everything. <code>何(なに)をしているんですか</code> asked warmly is "oh, what are you up
to?"; asked flatly it is "what do you think you're doing?" Similarly
<code>どうして来(こ)なかったんですか</code> presses for a justification in a way that
<code>来(こ)られませんでしたか</code> does not. When you only want information and not a
defence, drop the `ん`.

**`～んですが` is one of the most useful shapes in the language.** It sets up a
request by explaining the situation first and then trailing off, leaving the
listener to offer:
<code>ちょっとお願(ねが)いがあるんですが…</code>,
<code>すみません、この席(せき)、空(あ)いてるんですけど…</code>. The unfinished ending is
the politeness.

**Register.** `んです` is spoken and polite; `のです` is written and formal, at home
in essays, reports and public announcements, and it sounds stiff in conversation.
Plain style gives `んだ`／`のだ` (<code>忙(いそが)しいんだ</code>) and, very commonly,
bare `の` for questions and soft statements: <code>どうしたの？</code> is neutral for
everyone, while `～のよ` and `～のね` are female-leaning and `～んだよ` male-leaning.
The same `の` turns up as the casual reason connector `んで` (a worn-down `ので`):
<code>ちょっと急(いそ)いでるんで</code>.

## Contrast

### んです vs です

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `です` states. `んです` explains. Ask yourself whether the
sentence is answering a question, spoken or unspoken. If it is not, you probably
do not need `ん`.

</div>

<pre>
かぜを引(ひ)きました。
I've caught a cold. (a report - new information)
</pre>

<pre>
かぜを引(ひ)いた<b>んです</b>。
I've caught a cold. (that's why I look terrible / why I'm not coming)
</pre>

The second sentence needs a context to lean on. Said out of nowhere with nothing
to explain, it makes the listener hunt for the missing connection.

### ～んですか vs ～ですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ですか` asks for a fact. `～んですか` asks for the story behind
something you have just noticed.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)、大阪(おおさか)へ行(い)きますか。
Are you going to Osaka tomorrow? (simple question)
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)、大阪(おおさか)へ行(い)く<b>んです</b>か。
Oh, you're going to Osaka tomorrow? (I've seen the ticket / heard something)
</pre>

The `ん` version presupposes evidence. That makes it warm and interested with a
friend, and prickly with a stranger or a subordinate, because you are asking them
to account for something. Learners over-produce `んですか` in exactly the situations
where it stings.

### のです vs んです vs んだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one grammar point, three registers: `のです` on paper, `んです`
in polite speech, `んだ`／`の` among friends.

</div>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は一人(ひとり)では解決(かいけつ)できない<b>のです</b>。
This problem cannot be solved alone. (written, formal)
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)じゃ無理(むり)な<b>んだ</b>。手伝(てつだ)ってくれない？
I can't manage on my own. Could you give me a hand?
</pre>

Note that the plain form of the noun／な-adjective pattern is `なんだ`, not
❌`だんだ` - the `な` survives at every register.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)**だんです**。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)**なんです**。
  Nouns join `の` with `な`. The same goes for な-adjectives:
  ❌<code>静(しず)かだんです</code> → ✅<code>静(しず)かなんです</code>.

- ❌ 明日(あした)行(い)き**ます**んです。 → ✅ 明日(あした)行(い)くんです。
  `んです` follows plain forms; the politeness is already in the `です` at the end.

- ❌ はじめまして。田中(たなか)なんです。 → ✅ はじめまして。田中(たなか)です。
  An introduction explains nothing, so there is no `ん`. Using it here sounds like
  you are correcting a misunderstanding.

- ❌ (to a colleague, neutrally) どうして会議(かいぎ)に来(こ)なかったんですか。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)、来(こ)られませんでしたか。
  `んですか` demands an explanation. If you do not want to sound like you are
  taking someone to task, ask the plain question.

## Practice

**1.** どうしたんですか。 -  - 財布(さいふ)をなくした____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

財布(さいふ)をなくした<b>んです</b>。

The question asked for the story, so the answer supplies it. `んです` after the
た-form marks this as the explanation the listener was after, rather than a bare
announcement.

</details>

**2.** はじめまして、リンです。中国(ちゅうごく)から来(き)ました。（学生(がくせい)です・学生(がくせい)なんです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

学生(がくせい)<b>です</b>。

A self-introduction is new information, not an explanation of anything, so plain
`です` is right. <code>学生(がくせい)なんです</code> would imply you were clearing up a
misunderstanding - as if someone had just assumed you worked here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Excuse me, I'd like to ask you something…"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません、ちょっと<b>聞(き)きたいんですが</b>…

The `んですが` preface explains your situation and then stops, leaving the listener
room to respond. Finishing the sentence would make it heavier and slightly more
demanding.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 来週(らいしゅう)は休(やす)みます。旅行(りょこう)だんです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)は休(やす)みます。旅行(りょこう)なんです。

<code>旅行(りょこう)</code> is a noun, so it joins `んです` with `な`, never with `だ`.
The `んです` itself is well chosen here - the second sentence explains the first.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っていますか。
(b) 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っているんですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral question - do you run every day? It could come out of nowhere,
on a questionnaire or in small talk.

(b) is triggered by something: you have seen their running shoes by the door, or
they just mentioned a marathon. It reads as "oh, so you run every day?" - engaged
and curious with a friend, but slightly probing with someone you have just met,
because it asks them to account for what you noticed.

</details>
