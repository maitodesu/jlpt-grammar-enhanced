# N5 - Ongoing and completed


---

# ～ている - is doing; is in a state

**Meaning** · the action is going on now - or its result is still with us

`～ている` is not the English present progressive, and treating it as one is the
single most expensive mistake in beginner Japanese. It says that something
connected to the verb is **true right now**. Whether that something is the action
still running, or the state left behind after the action finished, is decided by
the verb itself - not by `ている`.

## Formation

`いる` is a る-verb, so it conjugates normally after the て-form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb (て) | て-form + いる | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)っている |
| う-verb (で) | で-form + いる | 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)んでいる |
| る-verb | て-form + いる | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べている |
| する | している | 勉強(べんきょう)する → 勉強(べんきょう)している |
| 来(く)る | きている | 来(く)る → 来(き)ている |
| Polite | ています | 食(た)べています |
| Negative | ていない／ていません | 食(た)べていません |
| Past | ていた／ていました | 食(た)べていました |
| Casual contraction | ている → てる | 食(た)べてる |
| Honorific / humble | ていらっしゃる／ております | 待(ま)っていらっしゃる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - verbs that take time (<code>食(た)べる</code>,
<code>読(よ)む</code>, <code>走(はし)る</code>) give **ongoing action**. Verbs that
happen in an instant and change something (<code>結婚(けっこん)する</code>,
<code>死(し)ぬ</code>, <code>開(あ)く</code>, <code>知(し)る</code>) give **resulting
state**. Same `ている`, two readings, and the verb decides.

</div>

There is a third, quieter use - **habitual action**
(<code>毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っています</code>) - and a fourth for accumulated record
(<code>三回(さんかい)優勝(ゆうしょう)している</code>, "has won three times"). Both are
common; the first is essential at N5.

## Examples

<pre>
今(いま)、ごはんを<b>食(た)べています</b>。
I'm eating right now.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちが公園(こうえん)で<b>遊(あそ)んでいる</b>。
The children are playing in the park.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)は<b>結婚(けっこん)しています</b>。
My older sister is married.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)の名前(なまえ)を<b>知(し)っています</b>か。
Do you know that person's name?
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんはめがねを<b>かけています</b>。
Tanaka wears glasses.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)が<b>開(あ)いています</b>よ。閉(し)めましょうか。
The window's open. Shall I shut it?
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きて、近(ちか)くの川(かわ)まで<b>走(はし)っています</b>。
Every morning I get up at six and run down to the river nearby.
</pre>

<pre>
庭(にわ)で虫(むし)が<b>死(し)んでいた</b>ので、土(つち)にうめてやった。
There was a dead insect in the garden, so I buried it.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が<b>混(こ)んでいる</b>から、着(つ)くのが少(すこ)し遅(おそ)くなりそうです。
The roads are busy, so I'll probably arrive a little late.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**`ている` is where Japanese talks about the present.** The dictionary form does
*not* mean "now": <code>食(た)べる</code> is "will eat" or "eats (habitually)", never
"is eating". If something is happening as you speak, `ている` is not optional.

**`てる` is the normal spoken shape.** <code>何(なに)してるの？</code>,
<code>待(ま)ってるよ</code>, <code>知(し)ってた？</code> - the `い` drops in casual speech,
including in `ていた` → `てた`. It is informal but extremely common; keep the full
form in writing, in `ます` speech and in the exam.

**States you would never think of as verbs.** Japanese uses `ている` for a large
class of descriptions that English handles with adjectives or the simple present:
<code>住(す)んでいます</code> (live), <code>持(も)っています</code> (have),
<code>勤(つと)めています</code> (work for), <code>似(に)ている</code> (resemble),
<code>太(ふと)っている</code> (be heavy-set), <code>曲(ま)がっている</code> (be bent).
Several of these are *only* natural in `ている` form - you cannot describe a
present resemblance with ❌<code>似(に)る</code>.

**Politeness.** Honorific speech gives <code>～ていらっしゃる</code> and
<code>～ておられる</code>; humble speech gives <code>～ております</code>
(<code>承知(しょうち)しております</code>). These are N4 and beyond, but you will hear
`ております` on the phone from day one.

**The negative is where the special cases hide.** <code>食(た)べていません</code> means
"hasn't eaten (yet)" - the action has not started or is not underway. But some
state verbs refuse the negative `ている` altogether, and <code>知(し)る</code> is the famous
one: see below.

## Contrast

### Ongoing action vs resulting state

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask how long the verb takes. If the action itself has
duration, `ている` catches it mid-flow. If the action is over in an instant and
leaves a changed world, `ている` describes the change that is still there.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は今(いま)、手紙(てがみ)を<b>書(か)いています</b>。
My little brother is writing a letter right now. (still writing)
</pre>

<pre>
おじいさんは去年(きょねん)<b>亡(な)くなっています</b>。
My grandfather passed away last year. (and is dead now)
</pre>

The second is not "is dying". <code>死(し)ぬ</code> and
<code>亡(な)くなる</code> take no time at all, so there is no flow for `ている` to catch
 - only the state afterwards. The same logic explains
<code>結婚(けっこん)している</code> (is married, not "is getting married"),
<code>来(き)ている</code> (is here, having come), <code>ドアが閉(し)まっている</code> (the
door is shut) and <code>覚(おぼ)えている</code> (remembers).

<pre>
電気(でんき)が<b>ついています</b>。誰(だれ)かいるのかもしれません。
The light is on. Someone might be in.
</pre>

A useful test: if the English translation is an adjective or "is/has been", you are
looking at a resulting state.

### ～ている vs the dictionary form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` = now. Dictionary form = will, or generally does. The
overlap English speakers expect between "I eat" and "I'm eating" does not exist
here.

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)みます。
I read the newspaper every day. (habit - plain present is fine)
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)、新聞(しんぶん)を<b>読(よ)んでいます</b>。
I'm reading the newspaper right now.
</pre>

Habitual actions can take either form, with a difference of framing: `ています`
presents the habit as a current, ongoing arrangement of your life - 
<code>銀行(ぎんこう)で働(はたら)いています</code> is how you say what you do for a
living, and <code>銀行(ぎんこう)で働(はたら)きます</code> would sound like a plan for
tomorrow.

### 知(し)っている vs 知(し)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - "I know" is <code>知(し)っています</code>. "I don't know" is
<code>知(し)りません</code>. There is no ❌<code>知(し)っていません</code> in ordinary use.

</div>

<pre>
その話(はなし)なら<b>知(し)っています</b>。
Yes, I know about that.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、知(し)りません。
Sorry, I don't know.
</pre>

<code>知(し)る</code> means "to come to know", so <code>知(し)っている</code> is the state of having
come to know - and its opposite is simply never having done so, which is
<code>知(し)らない</code>. Answering a question with ❌<code>知(し)っていません</code> marks you
immediately as a learner; answering with a flat <code>知(し)りません</code> can sound curt, so
soften it: <code>ちょっと分(わ)かりません</code>, <code>すみません、知(し)らないんです</code>.

<code>分(わ)かる</code> behaves differently again: it is already a state, so
<code>分(わ)かります</code>／<code>分(わ)かりません</code> need no `ている` at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ その人(ひと)は知(し)っていません。 → ✅ その人(ひと)は知(し)りません。
  The negative of <code>知(し)っている</code> is <code>知(し)らない</code>. This one is worth memorising
  as a pair rather than as a rule.

- ❌ 今(いま)、昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べます。（intending "I'm eating lunch now"）→ ✅ 今(いま)、昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べています。
  The plain present is future or habitual. It cannot describe what is happening as
  you speak.

- ❌ 兄(あに)は結婚(けっこん)します。（intending "my brother is married"）→ ✅ 兄(あに)は結婚(けっこん)しています。
  Without `ている` you have announced a wedding that has not happened yet.

- ❌ 東京(とうきょう)に住(す)みます。 → ✅ 東京(とうきょう)に住(す)んでいます。
  Living somewhere is a state. The dictionary form would mean you are about to
  move there.

- ❌ 駅(えき)で待(ま)ちています。 → ✅ 駅(えき)で待(ま)っています。
  <code>待(ま)つ</code> is a `つ` verb, so its て-form is <code>待(ま)って</code>. `ている`
  never attaches to the ます-stem.

## Practice

**1.** 母(はは)は台所(だいどころ)で料理(りょうり)を____います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

料理(りょうり)を<b>して</b>います。

<code>する</code> becomes <code>して</code>, and cooking takes time, so this is the ongoing
reading: she is in the middle of it right now.

</details>

**2.** 山田(やまだ)さんの電話番号(でんわばんごう)を（知(し)っていません・知(し)りません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)さんの電話番号(でんわばんごう)を<b>知(し)りません</b>。

<code>知(し)っている</code> has no negative form of its own - not knowing is expressed by
the plain negative <code>知(し)らない</code>／<code>知(し)りません</code>. The affirmative and the
negative simply do not match in shape.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The door is open."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ドアが<b>開(あ)いています</b>。

<code>開(あ)く</code> is an instantaneous change of state, so `ている` gives the
resulting state - the door is *currently open*, not "is opening". English speakers
often reach for an adjective here; Japanese uses the verb.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 今(いま)、田中(たなか)さんは電話(でんわ)を使(つか)います。あとでかけ直(なお)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 今(いま)、田中(たなか)さんは電話(でんわ)を使(つか)っています。あとでかけ直(なお)してください。

The second sentence makes it clear the phone is in use *at this moment*, so the
first needs `ています`. As written, it says Tanaka will use the phone - which gives
the listener no reason to call back later.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けています。
(b) 窓(まど)が開(あ)いています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the transitive <code>開(あ)ける</code> with `を`: somebody is in the act of
opening the window, and the sentence is about their action.

(b) uses the intransitive <code>開(あ)く</code> with `が`: the window is open. Nobody
is mentioned, nobody is doing anything, and the sentence simply describes the
state of the room. The particle and the verb pair together - change one and you
must change the other.

</details>

# まだ - still; not yet

**Meaning** · still (it hasn't changed) - or, with a negative, not yet (it hasn't
happened)

`まだ` marks a situation as **unchanged**. If the sentence is positive, what has
not changed is a state that continues: *still*. If the sentence is negative, what
has not changed is an event everyone is waiting for: *not yet*. One word, one
idea, two English translations.

## Usage

`まだ` is an adverb. It does not conjugate and it attaches to nothing - you simply
put it before the part of the sentence it applies to, usually near the front.

With an **affirmative** predicate it means *still*:
<code>まだ降(ふ)っています</code> (still raining),
<code>まだ子(こ)どもです</code> (still a child),
<code>まだ新(あたら)しい</code> (still new). Verbs, い-adjectives, な-adjectives and nouns
all take it equally.

With a **negative** predicate it means *not yet*:
<code>まだ決(き)まっていません</code> (not decided yet),
<code>まだ来(き)ません</code> (hasn't come yet). The most important pattern of this
kind is <code>まだ～ていません</code>, which has a page of its own.

`まだ` also stands alone as a complete answer. To
<code>もう食(た)べましたか</code> you can reply simply `まだです`, or casually `まだ`.
And doubled, `まだまだ` means "nowhere near yet":
<code>日本語(にほんご)はまだまだです</code>.

One extra use worth knowing: before a quantity or a time, `まだ` means *only* - 
<code>まだ三時(さんじ)ですよ</code> ("it's only three"), <code>まだ半分(はんぶん)残(のこ)っている</code>
("there's still half left"). The nuance is that there is more of it than the
listener assumed.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>まだ</b>時間(じかん)がありますよ。
There's still time.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は<b>まだ</b>寝(ね)ています。
My father is still asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが<b>まだ</b>来(き)ません。
The bus still hasn't come.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)のパン屋(や)、<b>まだ</b>やっているかな。もう九時(くじ)だけど。
I wonder if the bakery by the station is still open - it's gone nine, though.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`まだ` is register-neutral. It sits comfortably in a business email and in a text
message to a friend, and it carries no politeness of its own - the ending of the
sentence does that work.

What it does carry is a faint sense of **expectation not yet met**, and that is
where tone creeps in. A bare <code>まだ？</code> to someone who is running late is
impatient in any language. The polite answer `まだです` is neutral, but as a
*question* - <code>まだですか</code> - it presses. Aimed upward, replace it with
something that does not ask the person to account for the delay:
<code>恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、いかがでしょうか</code>.

In the other direction, `まだまだ` is a standard humility formula. Told that your
Japanese is good, <code>いえいえ、まだまだです</code> is the expected reply - literally
"not by a long way yet", and understood as modesty rather than self-criticism.

## Contrast

### まだ vs もう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まだ` says nothing has changed; `もう` says the change has
already happened. They divide the timeline at the moment of change, and they are
each other's natural opposite in both positive and negative sentences.

</div>

<pre>
外(そと)は<b>まだ</b>明(あか)るいですね。
It's still light outside.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)はもう暗(くら)いですね。
It's already dark outside.
</pre>

The negative pairing is the one to watch, because the English flips too:

<pre>
兄(あに)は<b>まだ</b>働(はたら)いていません。
My brother isn't working yet. (he will - he's still studying)
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)はもう働(はたら)いていません。
My brother isn't working any more. (he has retired or quit)
</pre>

`まだ` + negative looks forward to something expected; `もう` + negative looks back
at something finished. Choosing the wrong one does not produce a slightly odd
sentence - it produces the opposite fact.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>まだ</b>昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べませんでした。 → ✅ <b>まだ</b>昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べていません。
  `ませんでした` closes the door on the event: it did not happen and that is that.
  `まだ` means it is still coming, so it needs the `ていません` form.

- ❌ レポートは<b>まだ</b>終(お)わりました。 → ✅ レポートは<b>もう</b>終(お)わりました。
  `まだ` cannot introduce a completed event. Once something is done, the adverb you
  want is `もう`.

- ❌ もう<b>まだ</b>着(つ)いていません。 → ✅ <b>まだ</b>着(つ)いていません。
  `もう` and `まだ` are opposites and do not stack. Pick one.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、あの件(けん)、<b>まだ</b>ですか。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、あの件(けん)はいかがでしょうか。
  Grammatical, but it asks a superior why they have not finished. Ask about the
  status instead of the delay.

## Practice

**1.** 「もう帰(かえ)りますか。」「いいえ、____仕事(しごと)が残(のこ)っているので、もう少(すこ)しいます。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いいえ、<b>まだ</b>仕事(しごと)が残(のこ)っているので…

The work is unfinished and the situation is unchanged, which is exactly `まだ` with
an affirmative verb: *still* there is work left. `もう` would claim the opposite.

</details>

**2.** 子(こ)どもたちは（まだ・もう）起(お)きています。寝(ね)かせないと。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)どもたちは<b>まだ</b>起(お)きています。

The second sentence - "I need to get them to bed" - tells you the children ought
to be asleep already and are not. That is a continuing state contrary to
expectation, so `まだ`. <code>もう起(お)きています</code> would mean they are already
awake, which fits a morning, not a bedtime.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) まだ三時(さんじ)ですよ。
(b) もう三時(さんじ)ですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both state the same time and disagree entirely about what it means.

(a) presents three o'clock as early: there is more of the day left than the
listener thought - sit down, finish your coffee. (b) presents it as late: time has
run away and something should have happened by now. `まだ` measures what remains;
`もう` measures what has already gone.

</details>

# まだ～ていません - haven't ~ yet

**Meaning** · I haven't done it yet - and it is still on the list

This is the standard answer to <code>もう～ましたか</code>. The two halves each do a
job: `まだ` says the situation is unchanged, and `～ていません` says the action has
not reached completion. Together they describe something that has not happened
**and is still expected to** - which is why "yet" is such a good translation and
"didn't" is such a bad one.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | まだ + て-form + いません | 食(た)べる → まだ食(た)べていません |
| Plain style | まだ + て-form + いない | 食(た)べる → まだ食(た)べていない |
| Casual contraction | ていない → てない | 食(た)べる → まだ食(た)べてない |
| する verbs | まだ + していません | 予約(よやく)する → まだ予約(よやく)していません |
| 来(く)る | まだ + きていません | 来(く)る → まだ来(き)ていません |
| Short answer | まだです | 「もう読(よ)みましたか。」「いいえ、まだです。」 |
| The question it answers | もう～ましたか | もう読(よ)みましたか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative goes on `いる`, not on the main verb. It is
<code>食(た)べて + いません</code>, never ❌<code>食(た)べない + います</code> and never
❌<code>食(た)べませんでした</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
いいえ、<b>まだ読(よ)んでいません</b>。
No, I haven't read it yet.
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)は<b>まだ終(お)わっていません</b>。
I haven't finished my homework yet.
</pre>

<pre>
ホテルは<b>まだ予約(よやく)していません</b>。
I haven't booked the hotel yet.
</pre>

<pre>
「もう返事(へんじ)が来(き)ましたか。」「いいえ、<b>まだ来(き)ていません</b>。」
"Has the reply arrived?" "No, not yet."
</pre>

<pre>
そのドラマ、<b>まだ見(み)てない</b>から言(い)わないで。
I haven't watched that show yet, so don't tell me.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい住所(じゅうしょ)は<b>まだ誰(だれ)にも教(おし)えていません</b>。
I haven't given anyone my new address yet.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の旅行(りょこう)のことは、両親(りょうしん)に<b>まだ話(はな)していない</b>んです。
I haven't told my parents about next month's trip yet.
</pre>

<pre>
注文(ちゅうもん)した品物(しなもの)が<b>まだ届(とど)いていない</b>のですが、確認(かくにん)していただけますか。
The item I ordered still hasn't arrived - could you look into it?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**It promises the action is coming.** <code>まだ払(はら)っていません</code> means the
bill is unpaid *and* that paying is still on the table. That is why it makes such a
good excuse and such a good apology: it admits the gap and implies you will close
it. If the thing genuinely will never happen, this pattern is the wrong choice.

**It is softer than it looks.** Compared with a flat `いいえ`, answering
<code>まだです</code> or <code>まだ決(き)めていません</code> leaves the door open and buys
time. In business Japanese this is a standard hedge:
<code>社内(しゃない)でまだ検討(けんとう)しております</code> - "we are still considering it
internally" - says no without saying no.

**Register is carried by the ending, not by `まだ`.** `まだ～ていません` polite,
`まだ～ていない` plain, `まだ～てない` casual and extremely common in speech.
<code>まだ？</code> on its own is blunt and impatient; <code>まだですか</code> presses; and
neither should be aimed at a superior.

**Watch the stative verbs.** A handful of verbs describe states already and do not
take `ている` in the negative: it is <code>まだ分(わ)かりません</code> ("I don't know
yet"), <code>まだ要(い)りません</code> ("I don't need it yet"), and
<code>まだありません</code>. The most notorious is <code>知(し)る</code> - "I don't know yet" is
<code>まだ知(し)りません</code>, never ❌<code>まだ知(し)っていません</code>.

## Contrast

### まだ～ていません vs ～ませんでした

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ませんでした` closes the book: it didn't happen, end of
story. `まだ～ていません` leaves it open: it hasn't happened *so far*.

</div>

<pre>
お昼(ひる)は<b>まだ食(た)べていません</b>。
I haven't had lunch yet. (and I intend to)
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)はお昼(ひる)を食(た)べませんでした。
I didn't have lunch yesterday. (I skipped it - that day is over)
</pre>

English uses "haven't" and "didn't" for this distinction and Japanese uses the
aspect of the verb, so the mapping is clean once you see it. If you can add "yet"
to the English, you need `まだ～ていません`.

### まだ～ていません vs もう～ました

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these two are the answer pair to the same question. `もう～ました`
= done. `まだ～ていません` = not done. Nothing else belongs in that slot.

</div>

<pre>
「もう薬(くすり)を飲(の)みましたか。」「はい、もう飲(の)みました。」
"Have you taken your medicine?" "Yes, I've taken it."
</pre>

<pre>
「もう薬(くすり)を飲(の)みましたか。」「いいえ、<b>まだ飲(の)んでいません</b>。」
"Have you taken your medicine?" "No, not yet."
</pre>

Note the asymmetry that trips everyone up: the affirmative answer uses the plain
past, but the negative answer cannot. ❌<code>いいえ、飲(の)みませんでした</code> would
mean you skipped it altogether - a different, and in a hospital a dangerous,
answer.

### まだ～ていません vs ～たことがありません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - "not yet" implies you expect to. "Never" makes no such
promise. `まだ～ていません` is about a pending task;
<code>～たことがありません</code> is about an empty life-experience column.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)は、<b>まだ作(つく)っていません</b>。
I haven't made the materials for next week's meeting yet.
</pre>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)へは行(い)ったことがありません。
I've never been to Hokkaido.
</pre>

Saying <code>北海道(ほっかいどう)へはまだ行(い)っていません</code> is not wrong, but it
means something narrower: a trip is planned, or expected of you, and it has not
happened *yet*. Use it when there is a plan; use
<code>～たことがありません</code> when there is only an absence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ まだレポートを出(だ)し**ませんでした**。 → ✅ まだレポートを出(だ)し**ていません**。
  `まだ` cannot sit with a plain past negative. The two contradict each other: one
  says "still pending", the other says "over and done with".

- ❌ **もう**荷物(にもつ)が届(とど)いていません。 → ✅ **まだ**荷物(にもつ)が届(とど)いていません。
  `もう～ていません` means "not any more". With a parcel that never came, you want
  `まだ`.

- ❌ まだ知(し)っていません。 → ✅ まだ知(し)りません。
  <code>知(し)る</code> has no negative `ている` form. This exception survives inside
  the `まだ` pattern too.

- ❌ まだ食(た)べ**ないです**。 → ✅ まだ食(た)べ**ていません**。
  <code>食(た)べない</code> means "won't eat" - a refusal, not a delay. The negative
  belongs on `いる`.

- ❌ お寿司(すし)はまだ食(た)べていません。（meaning "I've never eaten sushi"）→ ✅ お寿司(すし)は食(た)べたことがありません。
  `まだ` promises that it is going to happen. For a plain absence of experience,
  use <code>～たことがありません</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 「もうチケットを買(か)いましたか。」「いいえ、まだ買(か)____。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いいえ、まだ<b>買(か)っていません</b>。

The negative attaches to `いる`, giving <code>買(か)って + いません</code>. The answer
also implies you still mean to buy them, which is exactly what the questioner
wants to know.

</details>

**2.** 昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しくて、晩(ばん)ごはんを（食(た)べませんでした・まだ食(た)べていません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しくて、晩(ばん)ごはんを<b>食(た)べませんでした</b>。

Yesterday is finished, so the meal was skipped rather than postponed.
`まだ～ていません` would claim that yesterday's dinner is still coming, which time
no longer allows.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I haven't decided yet."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>まだ決(き)めていません</b>。
（casual: まだ決(き)めてない。）

<code>決(き)める</code> → <code>決(き)めて</code> → <code>決(き)めていません</code>. This is
one of the most useful sentences in the language, because it declines to answer
without refusing to answer.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: メールをまだ送(おく)りませんでした。すみません、これから送(おく)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ メールをまだ送(おく)っていません。すみません、これから送(おく)ります。

The second sentence says you are about to send it, so the first cannot use
`ませんでした` - that would mean the sending is off the table. <code>まだ送(おく)っていません</code>
keeps the action pending, which matches the apology.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) その本(ほん)はまだ読(よ)んでいません。
(b) その本(ほん)は読(よ)んだことがありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the book is on your list. It is sitting on the shelf, someone recommended
it, perhaps it was assigned - and you fully expect to get to it.

(b) simply reports that reading it has never been part of your life. There is no
implication of a plan, and none of guilt. If your professor asks whether you have
read the set text, (a) is a promise and (b) is a confession.

</details>

# もう - already; anymore

**Meaning** · the change has happened - already, by now, or (with a negative) not
any more

`もう` marks a threshold as crossed. Whatever the situation used to be, it is not
that any longer: the meal is eaten, the shop is shut, the pain has stopped. Point
it at a positive sentence and you get *already*; point it at a negative one and
you get *not any more*.

## Usage

`もう` is an adverb and never changes shape. It normally comes early in the
sentence, though it can sit right before the word it modifies.

With a **past or completed** predicate it means *already*:
<code>もう終(お)わりました</code>, <code>もう出(で)かけた</code>,
<code>もう大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です</code>. In a question it is the standard way to ask
whether something is done: <code>もう見(み)ましたか</code>.

With a **negative** predicate it means *no longer*:
<code>もう吸(す)いません</code> (I don't smoke any more),
<code>もう子(こ)どもじゃない</code> (not a child any more). The change has already
happened and there is no going back.

Before a **number or quantity** it means *another*, *more*:
<code>もう一杯(いっぱい)</code> (one more glass), <code>もう一度(いちど)</code> (once
more), <code>もう少(すこ)し</code> (a bit more), <code>もうちょっと</code>. This use has
nothing to do with time, and learners often meet it before they meet the other
two.

Before a **time expression** it means the moment has arrived:
<code>もうすぐ着(つ)きます</code> (arriving shortly),
<code>もう帰(かえ)る時間(じかん)だ</code> (time to go home). And on its own, `もう` is
an interjection of exasperation - see below.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>もう</b>昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べましたか。
Have you had lunch already?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もう</b>お酒(さけ)は飲(の)みません。
I don't drink any more.
</pre>

<pre>
コーヒーを<b>もう</b>一杯(いっぱい)いかがですか。
Would you like another cup of coffee?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もう</b>こんな時間(じかん)。そろそろ失礼(しつれい)します。
Look at the time already. I ought to be going.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`もう` itself is neutral - it belongs equally to a text message and to a meeting.
What varies is what surrounds it, and `もう` has a few spoken lives worth knowing.

**`もう？` is surprise.** Said with a rising tone in reply to news, it means "that
soon?" - <code>え、もう終(お)わったの？</code>. It is friendly, and it is one of the
easiest natural-sounding reactions to learn.

**`もう！` is exhaustion.** Alone, drawn out, it expresses irritation or fond
exasperation: <code>もう、遅(おそ)いよ！</code>. This interjection leans female and very
casual, and it is the `もう` you will hear most often in drama.

**`もういい` needs care.** It means "that's enough" and lands anywhere between
"I'm full, thank you" and "forget it, I'm done with this," depending entirely on
tone. <code>もういいです</code> said flatly to a shop assistant sounds like giving up on
them; <code>結構(けっこう)です</code> is the safe refusal.

**Formal writing prefers `すでに`.** Reports and notices use
<code>すでに完了(かんりょう)しております</code> rather than `もう`. `もう` is not rude, just
conversational.

## Contrast

### もう vs まだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もう` is on the far side of the change; `まだ` is on the near
side. Every `もう` sentence has a `まだ` mirror image, and the mirror flips in the
negative as well as the positive.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は<b>もう</b>始(はじ)まりました。
The meeting has already started.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)はまだ始(はじ)まっていません。
The meeting hasn't started yet.
</pre>

Now the negatives, which is where English gives you two completely different
words:

<pre>
足(あし)は<b>もう</b>痛(いた)くありません。
My leg doesn't hurt any more. (it did; it stopped)
</pre>

<pre>
足(あし)はまだ痛(いた)いです。
My leg still hurts. (nothing has changed)
</pre>

Keep hold of the direction rather than the English: `もう` + negative looks back at
something that has ended, `まだ` + negative looks forward to something that has not
begun. <code>もう働(はたら)いていません</code> is a retirement;
<code>まだ働(はたら)いていません</code> is a student.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 宿題(しゅくだい)は<b>もう</b>しています。（intending "I've already done it"）→ ✅ 宿題(しゅくだい)は<b>もう</b>しました。
  <code>もう～ている</code> says you are already in the middle of it. Completion in the
  past takes the plain past: `もう～ました`.

- ❌ バスが<b>もう</b>来(き)ていません。（intending "the bus hasn't come yet"）→ ✅ バスが<b>まだ</b>来(き)ていません。
  With a negative, `もう` means "not any more" - you would be saying the bus service
  has stopped running.

- ❌ コーヒーを<b>もう</b>一(いち)ください。 → ✅ コーヒーを<b>もう</b>一杯(いっぱい)ください。
  `もう` needs a full counter phrase after it: <code>一杯(いっぱい)</code>,
  <code>一(ひと)つ</code>, <code>一度(いちど)</code>, <code>少(すこ)し</code>.

- ❌ （in a written report）作業(さぎょう)は<b>もう</b>終(お)わりました。 → ✅ 作業(さぎょう)はすでに完了(かんりょう)しております。
  Not ungrammatical, just conversational. Formal writing reaches for `すでに`.

## Practice

**1.** 「映画(えいが)のチケット、____買(か)いましたか。」「はい、昨日(きのう)買(か)いました。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「映画(えいが)のチケット、<b>もう</b>買(か)いましたか。」

Asking whether something is done by now is the core use of `もう` with the past
tense. It is also the question that `まだ～ていません` exists to answer in the
negative.

</details>

**2.** 昔(むかし)はたばこを吸(す)っていましたが、（もう・まだ）吸(す)っていません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昔(むかし)はたばこを吸(す)っていましたが、<b>もう</b>吸(す)っていません。

The first half establishes that the speaker used to smoke, so the habit has ended:
"not any more." `まだ` would mean they had never started, which contradicts
<code>昔(むかし)は吸(す)っていました</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう少(すこ)し待(ま)ってください。
(b) もう待(ま)ちません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the quantity sense: <code>もう少(すこ)し</code> is "a little more", so the speaker is
asking for extra time. It is polite and cooperative.

(b) uses the negative sense: the waiting is over and will not continue. Depending
on the tone it is either a decision to give up or an outright threat. The same
adverb, doing two of its jobs, in sentences that could not be further apart.

</details>

# ～たことがある

**Meaning** · to have done something before - an experience you carry with you

`～たことがある` is not a past tense. It says that somewhere in your life there
exists an instance of doing this thing. Literally: "there is a *time-when-I-did-it*."
That is why it uses `ある` - the experience is treated as something you possess,
not as an event on a calendar.

## Formation

Take the **た-form**, then add `ことがある`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + ことがある | 行(い)く → 行(い)った → 行(い)ったことがある |
| Verb (る-verb) | た-form + ことがある | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べた → 食(た)べたことがある |
| Verb (する／来(く)る) | した／来(き)た + ことがある | する → したことがある |
| い-adjective | かった + ことがある | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しかったことがある |
| な-adjective | だった + ことがある | 大変(たいへん)だ → 大変(たいへん)だったことがある |
| Noun | だった + ことがある | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)だったことがある |
| Polite | ある → あります | 行(い)ったことがあります |
| Negative | ある → ない／ありません | 行(い)ったことがない |
| Past | ある → あった／ありました | 行(い)ったことがありました |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `た` is compulsory and is **not** the past tense. It is the
form the pattern is built on, the way `～ておく` is built on the て-form. A
dictionary form here gives you a different pattern entirely (see Contrast).

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
おすしを<b>食(た)べたことがあります</b>か。
Have you ever eaten sushi?
</pre>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)に<b>行(い)ったことがあります</b>。
I've been to Hokkaido.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は馬(うま)に<b>乗(の)ったことがない</b>。
I've never ridden a horse.
</pre>

<pre>
富士山(ふじさん)に<b>登(のぼ)ったことある</b>?
Have you ever climbed Mt. Fuji?
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)のとき、パン屋(や)で<b>働(はたら)いたことがあります</b>。
When I was a student I worked at a bakery for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)だけ、テレビで見(み)た人(ひと)に道(みち)で<b>会(あ)ったことがある</b>。
Just once, I've run into someone on the street that I'd seen on TV.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に三年(さんねん)住(す)んでいましたが、京都(きょうと)には一度(いちど)も<b>行(い)ったことがありません</b>。
I lived in Japan for three years, but I've never once been to Kyoto.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに美(うつく)しい景色(けしき)は今(いま)まで<b>見(み)たことがない</b>。
I have never seen scenery this beautiful in my life.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. `あります` polite, `ある` plain, and nothing about the pattern
marks age or gender.

Two contractions dominate spoken Japanese and will ambush you:

- **`が` drops.** 行(い)ったことある? - this is the ordinary spoken form of the
  question. So is 見(み)たことない, for the negative.
- **`が` becomes `は` for emphasis.** 見(み)たこと**は**ない - "I've certainly
  never *seen* it (whatever else I may have done)." The `は` adds the contrast that
  `は` always adds.

The pattern carries a built-in sense of **distance**. The experience has to feel
like part of your history, not like something from this morning. That is why a
specific recent time and `～たことがある` fight each other:
❌ きのう、おすしを食(た)べたことがあります. Say
食(た)べました. Vague time expressions are fine, and in fact common:
前(まえ)に, 昔(むかし), 一度(いちど), 子(こ)どものとき, 今(いま)まで.

It also implies the experience is **worth remarking on**. Everyday, everyone-does-it
actions sound strange: ❌ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べたことがあります is odd, because
of course you have. The exception is when you are genuinely drawing a line - 
納豆(なっとう)は食(た)べたことがありません is fine, because plenty of people
haven't.

<code>一度(いちど)も</code> + negative is the fixed way to say "not even once", and the two
must agree: 一度(いちど)も行(い)ったことが**ありません**.

## Contrast

### ～たことがある vs the plain past

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain past puts the event on a timeline. `～たことがある` puts
it on your résumé.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)、北海道(ほっかいどう)に行(い)きました。
I went to Hokkaido last year.
</pre>

That is a report of one occasion, anchored to a date. Which occasion matters.

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)に<b>行(い)ったことがあります</b>。
I've been to Hokkaido.
</pre>

That is a fact about *me*. When I went, or how often, is beside the point - what is
being claimed is that Hokkaido is inside my experience.

This is why a definite time expression forces the plain past. <code>去年(きょねん)</code>,
`きのう`, <code>三時(さんじ)に</code> all pin the event down, and pinning it down is exactly
what `～たことがある` refuses to do. English "I have been to Hokkaido" behaves the
same way - you cannot say "I have been there yesterday" either.

### ～たことがある vs ～たことがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negation lands on `ある`, never on the verb.

</div>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)に<b>行(い)ったことがありません</b>。
I've never been abroad.
</pre>

Learners reach for the verb and produce ❌<code>行(い)かなかったことがあります</code>. That
is not a negative - it means "there have been occasions when I didn't go", which is
a real but very different sentence.

The negative is used far more than the affirmative in practice, and it has its own
set of companions: <code>一度(いちど)も</code> (not once), `まだ` (not yet), <code>今(いま)まで</code>
(up to now).

<pre>
まだ新(あたら)しい店(みせ)に行(い)ったことがないんです。
I haven't been to the new place yet.
</pre>

Past tense on `ある` gives you "hadn't, at that point":
去年(きょねん)まで一度(いちど)も飛行機(ひこうき)に乗(の)ったことが**ありませんでした**
 - "until last year I'd never flown."

### ～たことがある vs ～ることがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - た-form = "I have done it". Dictionary form = "it sometimes
happens".

</div>

Same three words, and swapping the verb form changes the meaning completely.

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が<b>遅(おく)れたことがあります</b>。
The train has been late before. (I've experienced it)
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が<b>遅(おく)れることがあります</b>。
The train is sometimes late. (it happens from time to time)
</pre>

The dictionary-form version is about frequency in the present, so it works with
`ときどき`, `たまに`, and with negatives:
朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べないことがあります - "there are times when I skip
breakfast."

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 北海道(ほっかいどう)に<b>行(い)くことがあります</b>。（meaning "I've been"）→ ✅ 北海道(ほっかいどう)に<b>行(い)ったことがあります</b>。
  Dictionary form gives you "I sometimes go there", which is a different claim.
  Experience needs the た-form.

- ❌ きのう、おすしを食(た)べたことがあります。 → ✅ きのう、おすしを<b>食(た)べました</b>。
  A specific recent time pins the event to the calendar, and `～たことがある` is
  precisely the form that refuses to be pinned. Use the plain past.

- ❌ 日本(にほん)に行(い)ったこと**を**あります。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)に行(い)ったこと**が**あります。
  `ある` is intransitive and never takes `を`. The `が` here is doing its normal
  job: marking what exists.

- ❌ 一度(いちど)も見(み)たことが**あります**。 → ✅ 一度(いちど)も見(み)たことが**ありません**。
  <code>一度(いちど)も</code> requires a negative to complete it, the way English "not once"
  does.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)ことがあります。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)**だった**ことがあります。
  Nouns and な-adjectives need `だった` in front of `ことがある`. The pattern always
  wants a plain past form before it, whatever the word class.

## Practice

**1.** 馬(うま)に乗(の)____ことがありますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

馬(うま)に乗(の)**った**ことがありますか。

The pattern is built on the た-form. <code>乗(の)る</code> is a う-verb ending in る, so
る → った: 乗(の)る → 乗(の)った.

</details>

**2.** "Trains around here are sometimes delayed."
この辺(へん)の電車(でんしゃ)は（遅(おく)れる・遅(おく)れた）ことがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この辺(へん)の電車(でんしゃ)は<b>遅(おく)れる</b>ことがあります。

Dictionary form + `ことがある` means "it happens from time to time" - a statement
about how often something occurs. The た-form would have said "the trains have been
late before", which is about experience rather than frequency.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've never been to Kyoto."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

京都(きょうと)に<b>行(い)ったことがありません</b>。
（casual: 京都(きょうと)、行(い)ったことない。）

The negation goes on `ある`, not on the verb - the た-form stays fixed. Adding
<code>一度(いちど)も</code> gives "never once":
京都(きょうと)には一度(いちど)も行(い)ったことがありません。

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 先週(せんしゅう)、あの映画(えいが)を見(み)たことがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先週(せんしゅう)、あの映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)ました</b>。

<code>先週(せんしゅう)</code> locates the event on a specific occasion, and
`～たことがある` cannot take a definite recent time - it deliberately leaves *when*
open. Drop the time word and <code>見(み)たことがあります</code> becomes fine again.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 沖縄(おきなわ)に行(い)きました。
(b) 沖縄(おきなわ)に行(い)ったことがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a particular trip. It belongs in a narrative - the listener will expect
to hear when, or who with, or what happened there. On its own it can sound like the
opening of a story.

(b) makes no claim about any particular trip. It says only that Okinawa is inside
my experience, which is what you offer when someone asks "have you been?" or
mentions Okinawa in passing. It is a fact about the speaker, not about a day.

</details>
