# N5 - Asking, offering, inviting


---

# ～てください

**Meaning** · please do ~ - a request, and often an instruction

`～てください` attaches to the て-form and asks the listener to perform an action.
It is polite in *form* - `ください` is the imperative of the honorific verb
<code>下(くだ)さる</code> - but it is not a soft or deferential way to ask. Teachers, doctors,
station staff and forms all use it, and that is the flavour it carries: a clear
directive, delivered courteously.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | て-form + ください | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ってください |
| Verb (る-verb) | て-form + ください | 見(み)る → 見(み)てください |
| Verb (する) | して + ください | 電話(でんわ)する → 電話(でんわ)してください |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き)て + ください | 来(く)る → 来(き)てください |
| Negative request | ない-form + で + ください | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないでください |
| Casual | て-form on its own | 待(ま)って／待(ま)ってね |
| More polite | ～てくださいませんか／～ていただけませんか | 待(ま)っていただけませんか |

To ask for a **thing** rather than an action, `ください` follows a noun with `を`
instead - that pattern has its own page in this book.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ください` attaches to the て-form, never the dictionary form.
Get the て-form right and the rest is automatic:
話(はな)す → 話(はな)して, 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)いで, 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んで,
行(い)く → 行(い)って.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ちょっと<b>待(ま)ってください</b>。
Hang on a moment, please.
</pre>

<pre>
ここに名前(なまえ)を<b>書(か)いてください</b>。
Please write your name here.
</pre>

<pre>
もう一度(いちど)<b>言(い)ってください</b>。
Could you say that again, please.
</pre>

<pre>
どうぞ<b>座(すわ)ってください</b>。
Please, have a seat.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)に着(つ)いたら<b>電話(でんわ)してください</b>。
Give me a call when you get to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
寒(さむ)いので、ドアを<b>閉(し)めてください</b>。
It's cold, so please shut the door.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)を、金曜日(きんようび)までにメールで<b>送(おく)ってください</b>。
Please email me the material for next week's meeting by Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが、明日(あした)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ほど早(はや)く来(き)て<b>くださいませんか</b>。
I'm terribly sorry, but could you possibly come in half an hour early tomorrow?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The single most useful thing to know about `～てください` is that **it is not the
polite form of a request**. It is the *neutral* one. Because it tells rather than
asks, it belongs to situations where the speaker has a right to direct: a teacher
to a class, a doctor to a patient, an announcement to passengers, a form to whoever
is filling it in. Used sideways or upward it can land as bossy - 
部長(ぶちょう)、これをコピーしてください is grammatically impeccable and would
raise eyebrows.

The ladder, roughly from most casual to most deferential:

- <b>待(ま)って</b> - friends, family. て-form alone.
- <b>待(ま)ってくれる？／待(ま)ってくれない？</b> - casual, but genuinely asking.
- <b>待(ま)ってください</b> - the neutral polite directive on this page.
- <b>待(ま)ってくださいませんか</b> - a real question, so noticeably softer.
- <b>待(ま)っていただけませんか／待(ま)っていただけますか</b> - humble; the workplace default upward.
- <b>お待(ま)ちください</b> - honorific, used *to* customers by staff.

`くださる` and `いただく` describe the same event from opposite ends: `くださる` is
"you kindly give", `いただく` is "I humbly receive". Both are polite; the `いただく`
versions are the ones you reach for when you want to sound careful.

Small softeners matter more than learners expect. Front the request with
すみませんが or 恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが, add ちょっと, and finish with ね
(気(き)をつけてくださいね) and the same sentence stops sounding like an order.

Note one friendly use that runs against all of this: with どうぞ, `～てください` is an
*offer*, not a command. 食(た)べてください at a dinner table means "please, help
yourself".

In casual speech the bare て-form does all this work - 見(み)て！, 貸(か)して - 
and ちょうだい (貸(か)してちょうだい) is a softer, childish or motherly variant.

## Contrast

### ～てください vs ～ていただけませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` *tells*; `～ていただけませんか` *asks*. Upward, and
for anything that is a favour rather than a duty, ask.

</div>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)の読(よ)み方(かた)を<b>教(おし)えてください</b>。
Tell me how to read this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)の読(よ)み方(かた)を<b>教(おし)えていただけませんか</b>。
Would you mind telling me how to read this kanji?
</pre>

The second is longer for a reason. It is a negative question, which leaves the
listener room to say no, and it uses the humble `いただく`, which frames you as the
one receiving a kindness. The pair
`～てくださいませんか` works the same way with `くださる` and is a shade less humble.

A useful test: if a "no" would be perfectly reasonable, use the question form. If
you are describing a step in a procedure - 靴(くつ)をぬいでください at the door - 
`～てください` is exactly right and the long version would sound strange.

### ～てください vs the plain て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - drop `ください` and you have the casual request. Drop it with
the wrong person and you have an order.

</div>

<pre>
ちょっと<b>手伝(てつだ)って</b>。
Give me a hand a sec.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょっと<b>手伝(てつだ)ってください</b>。
Could you give me a hand, please.
</pre>

The bare て-form is everywhere in speech between friends, within a family, and from
older to younger. It is not rude in itself - it is simply unmarked for politeness,
which means the relationship has to carry it. Adding ね or よ softens it
(急(いそ)いでね); adding よ alone can sharpen it.

`～てくれる？` and `～てくれない？` sit slightly above the bare form: still casual, but
phrased as a question, so they ask rather than tell.

### ～てください vs ～をください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ください` after a **て-form** asks for an action; `ください`
after a **noun + を** asks for a thing.

</div>

<pre>
水(みず)を<b>ください</b>。
Some water, please.
</pre>

<pre>
水(みず)を<b>持(も)ってきてください</b>。
Please bring me some water.
</pre>

Both are natural; they differ in what you are asking for. The first hands over an
object, the second requests the act of fetching. The mistake to avoid is trying to
force a noun into the verb slot - ❌手伝(てつだ)いをしてください is possible but
stilted, and ✅手伝(てつだ)ってください is what a speaker actually says.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>てください</b> → ✅ 食(た)<b>べてください</b>
  `ください` attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 待(ま)つ<b>てください</b> → ✅ 待(ま)っ<b>てください</b>
  `つ`-verbs make their て-form with a small `っ`: 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)って.

- ❌ 行(い)かない<b>てください</b> → ✅ 行(い)かない<b>でください</b>
  The negative request uses `ないで`, never `ないて`.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、これを<b>コピーしてください</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、これを<b>コピーしていただけませんか</b>。
  Grammatical, but you are giving an instruction to someone senior. Ask instead.

- ❌ ペンを<b>してください</b>。 → ✅ ペンを<b>ください</b>。
  Asking for an object needs no verb at all.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この字(じ)が読(よ)めません。ゆっくり話(はな)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ゆっくり話(はな)<b>してください</b>。

<code>話(はな)す</code> is a `す`-verb, so its て-form is <code>話(はな)して</code>, and `ください`
attaches directly to that. Adding すみませんが at the front, as the question does,
already softens the request.

</details>

**2.** 社長(しゃちょう)に：「この書類(しょるい)を（見(み)てください・見(み)ていただけませんか）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この書類(しょるい)を<b>見(み)ていただけませんか</b>。

Looking over your document is a favour, not part of the president's duties, and he
outranks you. The humble question form leaves him room to decline, which is exactly
what politeness upward is made of.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please turn off the light when you leave the room."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部屋(へや)を出(で)るとき、電気(でんき)を<b>消(け)してください</b>。

<code>消(け)す</code> → <code>消(け)して</code> + ください. This is the instructional use of the
pattern - a house rule, not a personal favour - so plain `～てください` is right.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あぶないですから、そこに立(た)つてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あぶないですから、そこに<b>立(た)たないでください</b>。

Two things are wrong. The て-form of <code>立(た)つ</code> is <code>立(た)って</code>, not
❌<code>立(た)つて</code>; and the sense demands a negative - "it's dangerous, so please
*don't* stand there" - which is built with `ないで`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けてください</b>。
(b) 窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けてくださいませんか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain polite directive. In a classroom, or between people who know each
other well, it is entirely normal - but it assumes compliance and does not invite a
refusal.

(b) is phrased as a negative question, so it genuinely asks. It is longer, more
deferential, and the natural choice when you are imposing on someone, addressing a
stranger, or speaking to someone senior to you.

</details>

# ～をください

**Meaning** · please give me ~ - asking for an object

`～をください` names a **thing** and asks for it. It is the sentence you use in a
shop, at a ticket window, at a counter, in a restaurant - anywhere the other person
is there to hand you something. `ください` is the imperative of the honorific verb
<code>下(くだ)さる</code> "to give (to me)", which is why a form that is literally a command
still counts as polite.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + を + ください | コーヒー → コーヒーをください |
| Noun + quantity | Noun + を + counter + ください | りんご → りんごを三(みっ)つください |
| Quantity (spoken, `を` dropped) | Noun + counter + ください | コーヒー二(ふた)つください |
| Verb (an action, not a thing) | て-form + ください | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ってください |
| Politer | ～をお願(ねが)いします | コーヒーをお願(ねが)いします |
| Politer still | ～をいただけますか | メニューをいただけますか |
| Casual | ～ちょうだい | コーヒーちょうだい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the thing takes `を`, never `が`. `ください` is a verb of giving,
so what is given is its direct object - the opposite of `ほしい`, which is an
adjective and takes `が`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒーを<b>ください</b>。
A coffee, please.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、メニューを<b>ください</b>。
Excuse me, could I have a menu.
</pre>

<pre>
この赤(あか)いりんごを五(いつ)つ<b>ください</b>。
Five of these red apples, please.
</pre>

<pre>
切符(きっぷ)を大人(おとな)二(に)まい、子(こ)ども一(いち)まい<b>ください</b>。
Two adult tickets and one child's, please.
</pre>

<pre>
袋(ふくろ)は要(い)りませんので、レシートだけ<b>ください</b>。
I don't need a bag - just the receipt, please.
</pre>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し時間(じかん)を<b>ください</b>。
Please give me a little more time.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、温(あたた)かいお茶(ちゃ)と、砂糖(さとう)を少(すこ)し<b>ください</b>。
Excuse me - a hot tea, and a little sugar, please.
</pre>

<pre>
白(しろ)いシャツの大(おお)きいサイズがあれば、それを<b>ください</b>。
If you have that white shirt in a large, I'll take it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～をください` is the customer's sentence. Between a customer and staff the balance
of the situation makes it perfectly polite, and it is what Japanese speakers
actually say at a counter - no hedging required.

Turn it around and the picture changes. Because `ください` is an imperative, asking a
**superior** for something with it sounds like an instruction:
部長(ぶちょう)、ペンをください is the sort of thing that gets noticed. Upward you
switch to a question - ペンをお借(か)りできますか, 資料(しりょう)をいただけますか
 - or to お願(ねが)いします.

Quantities behave loosely in speech. コーヒーを二(ふた)つください and
コーヒー二(ふた)つください are both normal, and the second is more common at a
counter; what you should not do is mark the number with `を` as well
(❌コーヒーを二(ふた)つをください). Politeness particles cluster at the front and
back instead: すみません、～ください and ～ください<b>ね</b> are both very common.

`ちょうだい` is the casual replacement - それちょうだい - and reads as childish or
warmly familiar depending on the speaker; it is more common from women and from
adults talking to children. In writing, ください survives in fixed instructions
(ご記入(きにゅう)ください, 少々(しょうしょう)お待(ま)ちください), which are honorific
forms rather than this pattern.

`ください` is not limited to physical objects. Abstract nouns work fine, and this
is where the pattern turns up outside shops:
時間(じかん)をください, 返事(へんじ)をください, もう一度(いちど)チャンスをください.

## Contrast

### ～をください vs ～をお願(ねが)いします

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ください` asks for a **thing you can hand over**;
お願(ねが)いします asks for that *or* for a service, and sounds a touch more
deferential.

</div>

<pre>
生(なま)ビールを<b>ください</b>。
A draught beer, please.
</pre>

<pre>
生(なま)ビールを<b>お願(ねが)いします</b>。
A draught beer, please. (slightly more polite)
</pre>

Where they come apart is scope. お願(ねが)いします covers requests that have no
object at all - 会計(かいけい)をお願(ねが)いします "the bill, please",
山田(やまだ)さんをお願(ねが)いします "could I speak to Yamada" - where ください
would be wrong or comic. As a rule, if you are asking someone to *do* something on
your behalf, お願(ねが)いします; if you are asking them to *give* you something,
either works.

### ～をください vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - noun + `を` + ください for an **object**; verb て-form +
ください for an **action**.

</div>

<pre>
地図(ちず)を<b>ください</b>。
Could I have a map.
</pre>

<pre>
地図(ちず)を<b>書(か)いてください</b>。
Please draw me a map.
</pre>

They share the word `ください` and nothing else. English blurs them - "give me a
hand" looks like a noun request but is really an action - so the trap is real:
✅手伝(てつだ)ってください, not ❌手伝(てつだ)いをください.

When you want an object *brought* to you rather than simply handed over, the two
combine: 水(みず)を持(も)ってきてください.

### ～をください vs ～がほしいです

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` describes your inner state; `ください` performs the
request. To a shop assistant, only the second one is a sentence that does anything.

</div>

<pre>
この靴(くつ)の黒(くろ)を<b>ください</b>。
I'll take these shoes in black.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい靴(くつ)が<b>ほしい</b>です。
I want new shoes.
</pre>

The second is a fine thing to say to a friend and an odd thing to say to a
salesperson - you have announced a feeling and left them to guess what to do about
it. Note the particles as well: `ほしい` is an adjective and takes `が`, while
`ください` is a verb and takes `を`. Mixing them up (❌これがください) is the single
most common error with this pattern.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ これ<b>が</b>ください。 → ✅ これ<b>を</b>ください。
  `ください` is a verb of giving; the thing given is its object.

- ❌ （レストランで）お水(みず)が<b>ほしいです</b>。 → ✅ お水(みず)を<b>ください</b>。
  Naming your desire at a server does not place an order.

- ❌ 手伝(てつだ)いを<b>ください</b>。 → ✅ <b>手伝(てつだ)ってください</b>。
  Help is an action, not an object. Use the て-form.

- ❌ コーヒーを二(ふた)つ<b>を</b>ください。 → ✅ コーヒーを二(ふた)つ<b>ください</b>。
  One `を` per object. The counter does not take a particle of its own.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)、はんこを<b>ください</b>。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、はんこを<b>いただけますか</b>。
  `ください` is an imperative. Pointed upward it reads as an instruction.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この本(ほん)____ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この本(ほん)<b>を</b>ください。

`ください` comes from the verb <code>下(くだ)さる</code>, so the thing you are asking for is
its direct object and takes `を`. The `が` that goes with `ほしい` does not transfer.

</details>

**2.** 店(みせ)で：「そのかばんを（ください・ほしいです）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そのかばんを<b>ください</b>。

In a shop you are placing a request, and `ください` is what performs it.
<code>ほしいです</code> only reports how you feel, which leaves the assistant to work out
what you actually want.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Two coffees and one orange juice, please."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

コーヒーを二(ふた)つと、オレンジジュースを一(ひと)つ<b>ください</b>。

Counters go between the noun and `ください`, and only one `を` per item. In speech
the `を` is often dropped altogether: コーヒー二(ふた)つとオレンジジュース一(ひと)つください.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: すみません、ちょっと手伝(てつだ)いをください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ すみません、ちょっと<b>手伝(てつだ)ってください</b>。

What you want is an action, not an object, so it belongs in the て-form pattern.
English "give me a hand" hides a verb inside a noun, and that is where the mistake
comes from.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) タクシーを<b>ください</b>。
(b) タクシーを<b>お願(ねが)いします</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) asks for a taxi as though it were an object to be handed across a counter, and
it sounds odd for that reason - nobody gives you a taxi.

(b) is what a hotel guest actually says. お願(ねが)いします asks the listener to
*arrange* something on your behalf, which covers services, phone calls and errands.
That wider scope is the main practical reason to learn it alongside `ください`.

</details>

# ～ないでください

**Meaning** · please don't do ~ - the negative request

`～ないでください` is the negative counterpart of `～てください`. Take the ない-form,
add `で`, add `ください`. The `で` is not decoration: `ないで` is the **negative
て-form** of a verb, and it does the same joining work elsewhere in the language - 
傘(かさ)を持(も)たないで出(で)かけた「I went out without an umbrella」.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form + で + ください | 吸(す)う → 吸(す)わないでください |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + で + ください | 捨(す)てる → 捨(す)てないでください |
| Verb (する) | しないで + ください | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)しないでください |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)ないで + ください | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないでください |
| Casual | ない-form + で | 見(み)ないで／見(み)ないでね |
| More polite | ～ないでいただけませんか | 入(はい)らないでいただけませんか |
| Notices and signs | ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください／おやめください | 喫煙(きつえん)はご遠慮(えんりょ)ください |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - build the ない-form first, then swap `ない` for `ないで`. The
ない-form is the hard part: 吸(す)う → 吸(す)<b>わ</b>ない (not ❌吸(す)あない),
する → しない, 来(く)る → 来(こ)ない (read <b>こ</b>ない, never ❌きない).

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `ないで` on its own is a full form of the verb, not just a piece of this
pattern. It joins clauses in the sense *without doing*
(何(なに)も言(い)わないで出(で)て行(い)った), and it feeds other endings:
忘(わす)れないでね, 来(こ)ないでほしい, 見(み)ないでくれる？

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ここでたばこを<b>吸(す)わないでください</b>。
Please don't smoke here.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ<b>帰(かえ)らないでください</b>。
Please don't leave yet.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>心配(しんぱい)しないでください</b>。
Please don't worry.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)中(ちゅう)はスマホを<b>見(み)ないでください</b>。
Please don't look at your phone during class.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)には<b>入(はい)らないでください</b>。
Please don't go into this room.
</pre>

<pre>
大切(たいせつ)な日(ひ)ですから、明日(あした)は<b>遅(おく)れないでください</b>ね。
Tomorrow's an important day, so please don't be late.
</pre>

<pre>
すみませんが、この話(はなし)はまだ誰(だれ)にも<b>言(い)わないでください</b>。
Sorry, but please don't tell anyone about this yet.
</pre>

<pre>
恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、館内(かんない)では写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)らないでいただけますか</b>。
I'm very sorry, but could I ask you not to take photographs inside the building?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everything true of `～てください` is true here: it is polite in form and directive in
function. A negative directive is inherently more confronting than a positive one,
though, because you are telling someone to stop or not to start - so the softeners
matter more.

The usual ones are すみませんが / 恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが at the front, ちょっと
in the middle, and ね at the end. 大声(おおごえ)を出(だ)さないでくださいね is
noticeably gentler than the same sentence without it.

Public notices avoid the bare form, because a sign that tells thousands of people
what not to do is at risk of sounding hectoring. Japanese signage prefers
ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください「we ask that you refrain」, おやめください「please stop」,
or a plain noun + 禁止(きんし)「prohibited」 on the sternest notices. If you learn
to read those, you have covered most stations and museums.

Casually, `ください` drops off and the ない-form does the whole job:
見(み)ないで！, 言(い)わないで. Add ね to soften, よ to press. `～ないでくれる？`
and `～ないでほしい` are common alternatives among friends - the first asks, the
second reports your wish.

One thing `～ないでください` is *not*: a statement of prohibition. It is a request. If
you need to say something is forbidden, `～てはいけません` is the pattern, and it
carries an authority that this one does not.

## Contrast

### ～ないでください vs ～ないでいただけませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative request, then the negative *question*. Upward, and
to strangers, ask rather than tell.

</div>

<pre>
ここに車(くるま)を<b>とめないでください</b>。
Please don't park here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここに車(くるま)を<b>とめないでいただけませんか</b>。
Would you mind not parking here?
</pre>

The second is what a neighbour says to a neighbour; the first is what the sign
says. `～ないでくださいませんか` sits between them, and the fully honorific
ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください is the institutional voice - a shop, a train company, a
museum.

The relationship decides. Telling a stranger not to do something is delicate in any
language, and Japanese solves it by turning the request into a question and letting
the listener seem to choose.

### ～ないで vs ～なくて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - Japanese has **two** negative て-forms. `ないで` means *without
doing* and is the one used in requests; `なくて` means *and so, because … not*.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはんを<b>食(た)べないで</b>学校(がっこう)へ行(い)きました。
I went to school without eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが<b>来(こ)なくて</b>、遅(おく)れました。
The bus didn't come, and so I was late.
</pre>

The first describes *how* the going happened; the second gives the *reason* for
being late. Swap them and the sentences stop making sense.

Two consequences worth memorising. First, requests always use `ないで` - there is no
❌行(い)かなくてください. Second, `なくて` is the form that adjectives and nouns
take when they are negated and joined
(高(たか)くなくて安心(あんしん)しました, 雨(あめ)じゃなくてよかった), so `ないで`
is verb-only territory.

### ～ないでください vs ～てはいけません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ないでください` **asks**; `～てはいけません` **forbids**.

</div>

<pre>
教室(きょうしつ)で走(はし)ら<b>ないでください</b>。
Please don't run in the classroom.
</pre>

<pre>
教室(きょうしつ)で走(はし)っ<b>てはいけません</b>。
You must not run in the classroom.
</pre>

The first is a request, however firmly meant; the second states a rule, and the
speaker has to be in a position to state it - a teacher, a parent, a notice. Used
by an equal it sounds like a scolding.

There is a third, gentler option that learners rarely meet early enough:
`～ないようにしてください`「please try not to…」, which frames the same content as
advice. 夜(よる)遅(おそ)く電話(でんわ)しないようにしてください is the version
that survives being said to almost anyone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 見(み)ない<b>てください</b> → ✅ 見(み)ない<b>でください</b>
  The negative て-form is `ないで`. There is no form ❌`ないて`.

- ❌ 行(い)か<b>なくてください</b> → ✅ 行(い)か<b>ないでください</b>
  `なくて` gives a reason; it cannot carry a request.

- ❌ <b>するないでください</b> → ✅ <b>しないでください</b>
  する is irregular: its ない-form is しない, so the request is しないでください.

- ❌ たばこを<b>吸(す)うないでください</b> → ✅ たばこを<b>吸(す)わないでください</b>
  `ないで` attaches to the ない-form, not the dictionary form. For う-verbs ending in
  `う`, the ない-form takes `わ`.

- ❌ 来(き)ないでください → ✅ 来(こ)ないでください
  Same kanji, different reading. The ない-form of 来(く)る is read <b>こ</b>ない.

## Practice

**1.** ここは図書館(としょかん)ですから、大(おお)きい声(こえ)で話(はな)____ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大(おお)きい声(こえ)で話(はな)<b>さないで</b>ください。

<code>話(はな)す</code> → ない-form <code>話(はな)さない</code> → swap `ない` for `ないで`. A
library is exactly the setting where the plain polite request is appropriate.

</details>

**2.** 窓(まど)を（開(あ)けなくてください・開(あ)けないでください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けないでください</b>。

Japanese has two negative て-forms and only one of them can carry a request.
`なくて` is for causes and consequences - <code>開(あ)けなくて寒(さむ)かった</code> - 
so it cannot be followed by `ください`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please don't come to the office tomorrow."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)は会社(かいしゃ)に<b>来(こ)ないでください</b>。

The ない-form of <code>来(く)る</code> is <code>来(こ)ない</code> - the kanji stays put and the
reading changes to こ. Writing きない is the classic slip.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 試験(しけん)中(ちゅう)は話(はなし)をするないでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 試験(しけん)中(ちゅう)は話(はなし)を<b>しないでください</b>。

する is one of the two irregular verbs. Its ない-form is しない, not ❌するない, so
the negative request is しないでください.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ここに荷物(にもつ)を置(お)か<b>ないでください</b>。
(b) ここに荷物(にもつ)を置(お)い<b>てはいけません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a request. It is firm and unambiguous, but it is still the speaker asking,
and a colleague could say it to another colleague without friction.

(b) states a rule and claims the authority to state it. It is what a member of
staff, a teacher or a posted notice would use, and coming from an equal it sounds
like being told off.

</details>

# ～ましょう

**Meaning** · let's - a proposal made on the assumption you already agree

`～ましょう` is the polite volitional. It turns a verb into "let's do it", and its
defining feature is **confidence**: the speaker takes agreement as read. That makes
it the right form for a plan already settled, for a leader moving a group along, and
for a public slogan - and the wrong form for a genuine, open invitation, where
`～ませんか` is what you want.

A second use hides inside the first. With <code>私(わたし)が</code>, `～ましょう` is not "let's"
at all but "I'll do it" - the speaker volunteering.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + ましょう | 行(い)きます → 行(い)きましょう |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + ましょう | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べましょう |
| Verb (する) | し + ましょう | します → しましょう |
| Verb (来(く)る) | き + ましょう | 来(き)ます → 来(き)ましょう |
| Softened into a question | ましょう + か | 行(い)きましょうか |
| Casual equivalent | plain volitional | 行(い)く → 行(い)こう／食(た)べる → 食(た)べよう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ましょう` replaces `ます`, so it builds off the same stem.
飲(の)み<b>ます</b> → 飲(の)み<b>ましょう</b>. It never attaches to the dictionary
form: ❌飲(の)むましょう.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no negative `ましょう`. ❌行(い)きませんしょう does not exist. To
propose *not* doing something, negate the content instead:
今日(きょう)は買(か)うのはやめましょう or 今日(きょう)は買(か)わないことにしましょう.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
じゃあ、<b>行(い)きましょう</b>。
Right - let's go.
</pre>

<pre>
一緒(いっしょ)に昼(ひる)ごはんを<b>食(た)べましょう</b>。
Let's have lunch together.
</pre>

<pre>
七(しち)時(じ)に駅(えき)の前(まえ)で<b>会(あ)いましょう</b>。
Let's meet in front of the station at seven.
</pre>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>始(はじ)めましょう</b>。
Let's get started.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)はここまでに<b>しましょう</b>。
Let's call it a day there.
</pre>

<pre>
はい、私(わたし)が<b>運転(うんてん)しましょう</b>。
All right - I'll drive.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうですから、傘(かさ)を持(も)って<b>行(い)きましょう</b>。
It looks like rain, so let's take umbrellas.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がありませんから、細(こま)かいことは後(あと)で<b>話(はな)しましょう</b>。
We're out of time, so let's go through the details afterwards.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ましょう` is polite in form but *assertive in function*. You are announcing what
the group is going to do. That is fine when the plan is agreed, when you are the one
running the meeting, or when the proposal is obviously welcome
(寒(さむ)いですね、中(なか)に入(はい)りましょう). It is presumptuous when the
other person has not yet agreed to anything, and doubly so upward:
部長(ぶちょう)、飲(の)みに行(い)きましょう helps itself to the boss's evening.
飲(の)みに行(い)きませんか or いかがですか leaves the decision where it belongs.

The same assertiveness is why `ましょう` is the voice of public exhortation.
Handwashing posters, road-safety signs and school announcements are full of it:
手(て)を洗(あら)いましょう, 気(き)をつけましょう,
時間(じかん)を守(まも)りましょう. Grammatically these are invitations; in
practice they are instructions with a smile.

As an answer, `ましょう` is the standard *yes*. To
一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)きませんか you reply ええ、行(い)きましょう - the
invitation was open, and `ましょう` closes it.

The <code>私(わたし)が</code> use is worth practising: 私(わたし)が持(も)ちましょう「I'll
carry that」. It is a shade more take-charge than
持(も)ちましょうか, which asks first, and noticeably more formal than
持(も)ちます. In service Japanese it is often replaced by the humble
お持(も)ちしましょう.

Casually, the whole thing collapses to the plain volitional: 行(い)こう,
食(た)べよう, しよう, 来(こ)よう. Between friends `ましょう` sounds oddly
ceremonious, like proposing a motion.

## Contrast

### ～ましょう vs ～ませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ましょう` **assumes** you'll agree; `ませんか` **asks** whether
you will. If the other person could reasonably say no, use `ませんか`.

</div>

<pre>
映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)に行(い)きましょう</b>。
Let's go and see a film.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)に行(い)きませんか</b>。
Would you like to go and see a film?
</pre>

The first works when you have already decided to spend the afternoon together and
are choosing what to do. The second works when you are asking someone out - it is a
question, it is negative, and both of those give the listener room.

This is why the pair so often appears in sequence: one person invites with
`ませんか`, the other accepts with `ましょう`. Reversing them is the classic learner
error, and it is the difference between "would you like to?" and "we're going".

### ～ましょう vs ～ましょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `か` turns a decision into a question. `ましょう` announces;
`ましょうか` checks, or offers to do something *for* the listener.

</div>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>出(で)ましょう</b>。
Let's head off.
</pre>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>出(で)ましょうか</b>。
Shall we head off?
</pre>

Adding `か` costs nothing and softens a great deal, so `ましょうか` is the safer
default whenever you are not certain the other person is ready. It also unlocks a
sense `ましょう` does not have: with the speaker as the doer, it becomes an offer - 
荷物(にもつ)を持(も)ちましょうか「shall I take your bag?」 where
荷物(にもつ)を持(も)ちましょう is you announcing that you will.

### ～ましょう vs the plain volitional

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different politeness. `ましょう` is the ます-level
form; 行(い)こう and 食(た)べよう are the plain-level ones.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は八(はち)時(じ)に<b>集(あつ)まりましょう</b>。
Let's gather at eight tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
おなかすいた。なんか<b>食(た)べよう</b>。
I'm hungry. Let's get something to eat.
</pre>

The plain forms are built like this: う-verbs change the final `-u` to `-ou`
(行(い)く → 行(い)こう, 飲(の)む → 飲(の)もう), る-verbs swap `る` for
`よう` (見(み)る → 見(み)よう), and the two irregulars are する → しよう,
来(く)る → 来(こ)よう.

Register maps straight across: use the plain volitional with friends, family and
anyone you would speak to in plain form, and `ましょう` everywhere you are using
`ます`. Note that the plain form can also take `か` - 行(い)こうか is the casual
equivalent of 行(い)きましょうか.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 飲(の)む<b>ましょう</b> → ✅ 飲(の)み<b>ましょう</b>
  `ましょう` replaces `ます` on the stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 行(い)き<b>ましょうですか</b> → ✅ 行(い)き<b>ましょうか</b>
  `ましょう` already contains the copula. Just add `か`.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)、一緒(いっしょ)に帰(かえ)り<b>ましょう</b>。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、一緒(いっしょ)に帰(かえ)り<b>ませんか</b>。
  Deciding a superior's movements for them. Ask instead of proposing.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>ましょう</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>でしょう</b>。
  `ましょう` is a proposal, not a prediction. Guessing about the weather is
  `でしょう`.

- ❌ 行(い)き<b>ませんしょう</b>。 → ✅ 行(い)くのは<b>やめましょう</b>。
  There is no negative `ましょう`; negate the content instead.

## Practice

**1.** つかれましたね。ちょっと休(やす)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ちょっと休(やす)<b>みましょう</b>。

<code>休(やす)む</code> → ます-stem <code>休(やす)み</code> → + ましょう. Both people are
already tired, so agreement is safe to assume and the assertive form is natural
here.

</details>

**2.** 初(はじ)めて話(はな)す人(ひと)に：「今度(こんど)うちに（来(き)ましょう・来(き)ませんか）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今度(こんど)うちに<b>来(き)ませんか</b>。

You are inviting someone you have only just met, so they must be free to decline.
`ましょう` would announce the visit as settled, which is presumptuous with a
stranger.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's cold, so let's go into the café."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

寒(さむ)いですから、喫茶店(きっさてん)に<b>入(はい)りましょう</b>。

Here the assumption of agreement is entirely safe - you are both cold - which is
exactly the situation `ましょう` is built for.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)の日曜日(にちようび)、テニスをするましょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)の日曜日(にちようび)、テニスを<b>しましょうか</b>。

`ましょう` builds on the ます-stem, and the ます-form of する is します, so the stem
is し. ❌するましょう doubles up the verb.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)め<b>ましょう</b>。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)め<b>ましょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) starts the meeting. The speaker has the floor, everyone is present, and the
sentence is really an announcement dressed as a proposal - the chair's line.

(b) checks first. It reads as "shall we make a start?", which suits someone who is
not chairing, or a chair who can see that two people are still on the phone. The
`か` costs one syllable and buys a lot of room.

</details>

# ～ましょうか

**Meaning** · shall I? / shall we? - offering to do something, or proposing tentatively

`～ましょうか` is `～ましょう` with a question mark built in, and that small `か`
does two different jobs.

- **Shall I?** - you offer to do something *for* the listener.
  窓(まど)を開(あ)けましょうか。
- **Shall we?** - you propose something for both of you, but check first instead of
  announcing. そろそろ帰(かえ)りましょうか。

Which one a listener hears is decided by the situation, not the grammar: if the
action benefits them and only you can do it, it is an offer; if it is something you
would do together, it is a proposal.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + ましょうか | 持(も)ちます → 持(も)ちましょうか |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + ましょうか | 開(あ)けます → 開(あ)けましょうか |
| Verb (する) | し + ましょうか | します → しましょうか |
| Verb (来(く)る) | き + ましょうか | 来(き)ます → 来(き)ましょうか |
| Humble offer (service Japanese) | お + ます-stem + しましょうか | お持(も)ちしましょうか |
| Casual | plain volitional + か | 開(あ)けようか／手伝(てつだ)おうか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - mark the doer and the ambiguity disappears.
私(わたし)が運(はこ)びましょうか is unmistakably an offer;
一緒(いっしょ)に運(はこ)びましょうか is unmistakably a proposal.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けましょうか</b>。
Shall I open the window?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>手伝(てつだ)いましょうか</b>。
Shall I give you a hand?
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)を<b>飲(の)みましょうか</b>。
What shall we have to drink?
</pre>

<pre>
暑(あつ)いですね。エアコンを<b>つけましょうか</b>。
It's hot, isn't it. Shall I put the air conditioning on?
</pre>

<pre>
どこで<b>会(あ)いましょうか</b>。
Where shall we meet?
</pre>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)りましょうか</b>。
Shall I take a photo for you?
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が混(こ)んでいますね。次(つぎ)の角(かど)を右(みぎ)に<b>曲(ま)がりましょうか</b>。
The traffic's heavy. Shall we turn right at the next corner?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)、私(わたし)が人数分(にんずうぶん)<b>コピーしておきましょうか</b>。
Shall I run off enough copies of the meeting handouts for everyone?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ましょうか` is the workhorse of everyday helpfulness. It is what you say when you
notice someone struggling with a door, a suitcase, a printer. It is polite without
being stiff, it commits you to nothing until they answer, and - crucially - it does
not put the listener under obligation, because you are volunteering rather than
asking.

Learn the replies as a set, because you will hear them constantly:

- <b>お願(ねが)いします</b> - yes, please.
- <b>すみません、ありがとうございます</b> - yes, with thanks and apology at once.
- <b>あ、大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です</b> - no, I'm fine. The everyday polite refusal.
- <b>いえ、結構(けっこう)です</b> - no thank you. Firmer, and cooler.

Upward, `ましょうか` is acceptable but plain. In service Japanese and towards
superiors the humble frame is standard: お持(も)ちしましょうか,
お調(しら)べしましょうか, ご案内(あんない)しましょうか. These raise the listener by
lowering the action, and they are what hotel staff and careful employees use.

Casually the whole thing shortens to the plain volitional plus `か`:
開(あ)けようか, 手伝(てつだ)おうか, 買(か)ってこようか. Note that in casual
speech the `か` is often dropped entirely and carried by intonation - 手伝(てつだ)おう？

One warning about intonation and reading: `ましょうか` is a question, and it takes a
rising tone. Written down, it is often followed by `。` rather than `？` in Japanese
prose, which does not make it any less of a question.

## Contrast

### ～ましょうか vs ～ましょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ましょう` **announces**; `ましょうか` **checks**. The `か` hands
the decision back to the listener.

</div>

<pre>
電気(でんき)を<b>つけましょう</b>。
Let's put the light on.
</pre>

<pre>
電気(でんき)を<b>つけましょうか</b>。
Shall I put the light on?
</pre>

The first version assumes; the second asks. That is why the `か` version doubles as
an offer and the bare version does not: an offer only makes sense if the other
person can decline it.

Between the two, `ましょうか` is almost always the safer choice with people you do
not know well. Use bare `ましょう` when the plan is already agreed and you are simply
moving it along - 行(い)きましょう as you both stand up.

### ～ましょうか vs ～ませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both ask, but `ませんか` **invites** the listener to join you;
`ましょうか` proposes a next step in something already underway, or offers to act on
their behalf.

</div>

<pre>
今度(こんど)の土曜日(どようび)、山(やま)に<b>登(のぼ)りませんか</b>。
How about climbing a mountain this Saturday?
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきましたね。下(お)り<b>ましょうか</b>。
It's started raining. Shall we head back down?
</pre>

`ませんか` opens something new and can be refused without awkwardness - it is the
form for asking someone out. `ましょうか` takes the shared activity as given and asks
about the *next move* within it.

There is also a distribution difference that catches learners. For an **offer**,
`ませんか` does not work: ❌荷物(にもつ)を持(も)ちませんか sounds like you are
inviting the other person to carry luggage, not offering to carry theirs. Offers are
`ましょうか` territory.

### ～ましょうか vs ～てあげましょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `ましょうか` for offers.
`～てあげましょうか` announces that you are doing the listener a kindness, and that
is not yours to announce.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで車(くるま)で<b>送(おく)りましょうか</b>。
Shall I drive you to the station?
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで車(くるま)で<b>送(おく)ってあげましょうか</b>。
Shall I do you the favour of driving you to the station?
</pre>

The second is grammatical and is used - among close friends, or lightly to a child - 
but `あげる` explicitly frames the act as a benefit conferred, and offering that to
an equal or a superior sounds self-congratulatory. The neutral offer leaves the
gratitude to the other person.

The polite move goes the other way, towards humility: お送(おく)りしましょうか
lowers your own action, and is the form used in service and business. `～てあげる` and
`お～する` are the two ends of the same axis, and the safe default at N5 is plain
`ましょうか` in the middle.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 開(あ)ける<b>ましょうか</b> → ✅ 開(あ)け<b>ましょうか</b>
  Built on the ます-stem: 開(あ)けます → 開(あ)け + ましょうか.

- ❌ 荷物(にもつ)を<b>持(も)ちませんか</b>。（as an offer） → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)を<b>持(も)ちましょうか</b>。
  `ませんか` invites the listener to do it. To offer your own help, use `ましょうか`.

- ❌ 窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けましょうか</b>。（meaning "please open the window"） → ✅ 窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けてください</b>。
  `ましょうか` puts the action on *you*, not on the listener. It can never be a
  request.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、コピーして<b>あげましょうか</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、コピー<b>しましょうか</b>。／お手伝(てつだ)い<b>しましょうか</b>。
  `てあげる` advertises your own kindness. Upward, drop it or go humble.

- ❌ 行(い)き<b>ましょうか、ですか</b>。 → ✅ 行(い)き<b>ましょうか</b>。
  `ましょうか` is already a complete question.

## Practice

**1.** 重(おも)そうですね。私(わたし)が持(も)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)が持(も)<b>ちましょうか</b>。

<code>持(も)つ</code> → ます-stem <code>持(も)ち</code> → + ましょうか. The <code>私(わたし)が</code>
makes it unambiguously an offer rather than a proposal that you both carry it.

</details>

**2.** 友(とも)だちが寒(さむ)そうです。（エアコンを消(け)しましょうか・エアコンを消(け)しませんか）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

エアコンを<b>消(け)しましょうか</b>。

You are offering to do something for your friend's benefit, and that is exactly what
`ましょうか` is for. <code>消(け)しませんか</code> would be inviting *them* to switch it
off, which is not what you mean.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Shall we decide the date now?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今(いま)、日(ひ)にちを<b>決(き)めましょうか</b>。

A joint action within a conversation already under way - the "shall we?" sense.
Plain <code>決(き)めましょう</code> would work too, but it announces the decision instead
of checking that the other person is ready.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: すみません、この字(じ)が読(よ)めません。読(よ)みましょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ すみません、この字(じ)が読(よ)めません。<b>読(よ)んでいただけませんか</b>。

The speaker wants *the listener* to read it, but `ましょうか` offers the speaker's own
action - as written, it says "shall I read it?", which makes no sense after "I
can't read it". A request needs the `ください` or `いただけませんか` family.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) お茶(ちゃ)を<b>入(い)れましょうか</b>。
(b) お茶(ちゃ)を<b>入(い)れてあげましょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain, warm offer to make tea. It is the version that works with a guest, a
colleague or a boss, because it says nothing about who is doing whom a favour.

(b) adds `あげる`, which explicitly marks the tea as a kindness you are bestowing.
Between close friends it can be playful; to anyone senior it sounds like you are
inviting thanks in advance. The humble お入(い)れしましょうか goes the opposite
way and is the safest of the three in a workplace.

</details>

# ～ませんか

**Meaning** · won't you…? how about it? - a genuine, open invitation

`～ませんか` invites. It is built as a **negative question**, and that is the whole
point: a negative question presupposes nothing, so it leaves the listener free to
decline without embarrassment. Where `～ましょう` announces a plan, `～ませんか` asks
whether you would like one - which is why it is the polite way to ask someone to do
something with you.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + ませんか | 行(い)きます → 行(い)きませんか |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + ませんか | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べませんか |
| Verb (する) | し + ませんか | します → しませんか |
| Verb (来(く)る) | き + ませんか | 来(き)ます → 来(き)ませんか |
| Casual | ない-form, rising tone | 行(い)かない？／食(た)べない？ |
| Politer | ～ませんでしょうか | いらっしゃいませんでしょうか |
| Accepting | ええ、～ましょう | ええ、行(い)きましょう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ませんか` is just the polite negative `ません` plus the question
particle `か`, so it is built on the ます-stem like everything else in that family:
話(はな)し<b>ます</b> → 話(はな)し<b>ません</b> → 話(はな)し<b>ませんか</b>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
一緒(いっしょ)にお茶(ちゃ)を<b>飲(の)みませんか</b>。
Would you like to have a cup of tea with me?
</pre>

<pre>
今度(こんど)の日曜日(にちようび)、買(か)い物(もの)に<b>行(い)きませんか</b>。
How about going shopping this Sunday?
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>休(やす)みませんか</b>。
Shall we take a short break?
</pre>

<pre>
よかったら、夕(ゆう)ごはんを一緒(いっしょ)に<b>食(た)べませんか</b>。
If you're free, would you like to have dinner together?
</pre>

<pre>
この本(ほん)、よかったら<b>読(よ)んでみませんか</b>。
Would you like to give this book a read?
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の土曜日(どようび)、うちでバーベキューをしますが、<b>来(き)ませんか</b>。
We're having a barbecue at ours next Saturday - do you want to come?
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から朝(あさ)の勉強(べんきょう)会(かい)を始(はじ)めます。田中(たなか)さんも<b>参加(さんか)しませんか</b>。
We're starting a morning study group next month. Would you like to join, Tanaka?
</pre>

<pre>
もしお時間(じかん)があれば、駅(えき)の近(ちか)くにできた美術館(びじゅつかん)に一緒(いっしょ)に<b>行(い)ってみませんか</b>。
If you have time, would you like to go with me to the new museum near the station?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ませんか` is the polite invitation, full stop. It works upward, sideways and to
strangers, and it is the form to reach for whenever the answer could genuinely be
no. The politeness comes from the shape of the question: by asking in the negative,
you build the refusal into the sentence and remove any pressure to accept.

Because it is a real question, it comes with a real conversational script.
**Accepting** is almost never a bare はい:

- ええ、行(い)きましょう。 - yes, let's.
- いいですね。ぜひ。 - sounds good, I'd love to.

**Declining** almost never uses いいえ either. The standard refusal trails off and
is never completed:

- すみません、明日(あした)はちょっと… - sorry, tomorrow is a bit…
- ありがとうございます。でも、その日(ひ)は用事(ようじ)があって… - thank you, but I have something on that day.

The unfinished ちょっと is doing real work. Saying the refusal out loud would be
blunt; leaving it hanging lets both sides treat it as understood.

Casually, `ませんか` becomes the plain negative with a rising tone: 行(い)かない？,
食(た)べない？, 飲(の)みに行(い)かない？ These are extremely common among
friends and carry exactly the same softness.

One caution: `～ませんか` is also, literally, an ordinary negative question. Out of
context, 食(た)べませんか could mean "aren't you eating?" Intonation and situation
sort it out, and the invitation reading dominates when the sentence names a shared
activity or includes 一緒(いっしょ)に.

Upward, `ませんか` is already good; いかがですか is the softer noun-based version, and
in formal invitations you will meet ～ませんでしょうか and
ご一緒(いっしょ)しませんか.

## Contrast

### ～ませんか vs ～ましょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ませんか` **asks**, `ましょう` **assumes**. Invite with the
first; accept with the second.

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)、テニスを<b>しませんか</b>。
Would you like to play tennis at the weekend?
</pre>

<pre>
いいですね、<b>しましょう</b>。
Sounds good - let's.
</pre>

That two-turn exchange is the natural home of both patterns, and it shows why they
are not interchangeable. Opening with `ましょう` treats the other person's weekend as
already spent; replying with `ませんか` would be asking them a question they have
just asked you.

`ましょう` is fine as an opener only when agreement is safe - when you are already
out together, or when you have the standing to direct the group.

### ～ませんか vs ～ましょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ませんか` invites the listener **to join you**; `ましょうか`
offers **your own** action, or checks the next step of something already agreed.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)、美術館(びじゅつかん)に<b>行(い)きませんか</b>。
How about going to the art museum tomorrow?
</pre>

<pre>
切符(きっぷ)は私(わたし)が<b>買(か)っておきましょうか</b>。
Shall I get the tickets in advance?
</pre>

The first opens a plan; the second handles a detail inside a plan that already
exists. And for offers of help the division is strict:
手伝(てつだ)いましょうか offers your hands, while
❌手伝(てつだ)いませんか asks the listener to come and help with something - 
possibly the opposite of what you meant.

### ～ませんか vs ～ますか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ますか` **asks a question** about what the listener will do;
`ませんか` **invites**. The negative is what turns information-gathering into an
invitation.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)のパーティーに<b>行(い)きますか</b>。
Are you going to the party tomorrow?
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)のパーティーに<b>行(い)きませんか</b>。
Would you like to come to the party tomorrow?
</pre>

The first is neutral enquiry - you want to know her plans, and you may not be going
yourself. The second includes you, and asks her to come along.

Using `ますか` where you meant to invite is a common learner slip. It is not rude, it
simply does not invite: 食(た)べますか offers information-gathering where
食(た)べませんか offers company. The affirmative question can also feel a touch
demanding, because it presupposes that going is on the table.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>ませんか</b> → ✅ 行(い)き<b>ませんか</b>
  Built on the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 行(い)き<b>ませんですか</b> → ✅ 行(い)き<b>ませんか</b>
  `ません` is already polite. Do not add another `です`.

- ❌ 荷物(にもつ)を<b>持(も)ちませんか</b>。（offering to carry it） → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)を<b>持(も)ちましょうか</b>。
  `ませんか` invites the other person to do the action. For your own offer, use
  `ましょうか`.

- ❌ 「コーヒーを飲(の)みませんか。」「はい、飲(の)みません。」 → ✅ 「ええ、いただきます。」／「ありがとうございます。」
  Do not answer the negative literally. Accepting an invitation is ええ plus a
  positive verb.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べに<b>行(い)きましょう</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べに<b>行(い)きませんか</b>。
  Upward, invite rather than announce.

## Practice

**1.** 来週(らいしゅう)、一緒(いっしょ)に花見(はなみ)に行(い)____か。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一緒(いっしょ)に花見(はなみ)に行(い)<b>きません</b>か。

<code>行(い)く</code> → ます-stem <code>行(い)き</code> → ません + か. The negative question
is what makes this an invitation rather than an announcement.

</details>

**2.** 重(おも)い箱(はこ)を運(はこ)んでいる同僚(どうりょう)に：「（手伝(てつだ)いませんか・手伝(てつだ)いましょうか）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>手伝(てつだ)いましょうか</b>。

You are offering your own help, and `ましょうか` is the form for that.
<code>手伝(てつだ)いませんか</code> would be inviting your colleague to come and help
someone else - the wrong direction entirely.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Would you like to go to the cinema with me on Friday?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

金曜日(きんようび)、一緒(いっしょ)に映画館(えいがかん)に<b>行(い)きませんか</b>。

An open invitation to someone who must be able to refuse - the core use of the
pattern. Adding よかったら at the front softens it further.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)、山(やま)に登(のぼ)るませんですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)、山(やま)に<b>登(のぼ)りませんか</b>。

Two errors stacked. `ません` attaches to the ます-stem <code>登(のぼ)り</code>, not the
dictionary form; and `ません` is already the polite negative, so the extra `です` has
nothing to do.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今晩(こんばん)、飲(の)みに<b>行(い)きますか</b>。
(b) 今晩(こんばん)、飲(の)みに<b>行(い)きませんか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) asks about the listener's plans. It is a perfectly normal question - you might
be checking whether she is joining a group that is already going - but it does not
in itself invite her, and it can sound as though you expect an answer either way.

(b) invites. The negative form builds in the possibility of "no", so it puts no
weight on the listener, and it implies that you are going too. For asking a
colleague out for a drink, (b) is the sentence you want.

</details>

# ～はどうですか

**Meaning** · how about ~? / how is ~? - putting an option on the table, or asking for an impression

`～はどうですか` does two related jobs, and Japanese does not distinguish them
grammatically:

- **Suggesting** - you offer something and ask what the listener makes of it.
  土曜日(どようび)はどうですか。「How about Saturday?」
- **Asking for an evaluation** - you ask how something is, or was.
  新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)はどうですか。「How's the new job?」

Underneath, both are the same question: *what is your assessment of this?* Tense and
context decide which one the listener hears.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + はどうですか | コーヒー → コーヒーはどうですか |
| Verb (proposing an action) | Dictionary + のは + どうですか | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)いて行(い)くのはどうですか |
| Verb (advising the listener) | た-form + らどうですか | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだらどうですか |
| Past (a finished event) | ～はどうでしたか | 旅行(りょこう) → 旅行(りょこう)はどうでしたか |
| Politer | ～はいかがですか | お茶(ちゃ)はいかがですか |
| Softer, more tentative | ～はどうでしょうか | 水曜日(すいようび)はどうでしょうか |
| Casual | ～はどう？ | これはどう？ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どう` asks *how / in what state*. It is not `どんな`, which asks
*what kind of* and must be followed by a noun.
✅映画(えいが)はどうでしたか。 ✅どんな映画(えいが)でしたか。
❌映画(えいが)はどんなでしたか。

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒー<b>はどうですか</b>。
How about a coffee?
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)<b>はどうですか</b>。
How's the new job?
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)の午後(ごご)<b>はどうですか</b>。
How about Saturday afternoon?
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)の旅行(りょこう)<b>はどうでしたか</b>。
How was the trip to Kyoto?
</pre>

<pre>
体(からだ)の調子(ちょうし)<b>はどうですか</b>。
How are you feeling?
</pre>

<pre>
この色(いろ)<b>はどうですか</b>。もう少(すこ)し明(あか)るいのもありますよ。
How about this colour? We have a slightly brighter one as well.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)の近(ちか)くに新(あたら)しいそば屋(や)ができたそうですが、今日(きょう)の昼(ひる)はそこ<b>はどうですか</b>。
Apparently a new soba place has opened near the station - how about there for lunch today?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)ですが、水曜日(すいようび)の午後(ごご)三(さん)時(じ)から<b>というのはどうでしょうか</b>。
About the time for the meeting - how would Wednesday from three in the afternoon be?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`どうですか` is polite and completely ordinary, but it is *plain* polite - the same
level as `です／ます` and no higher. Two situations expose that.

The first is **offering something to a superior or a customer**.
部長(ぶちょう)、コーヒーはどうですか is not rude, exactly, but it is the register
you would use with a colleague. お茶(ちゃ)はいかがですか is what waiters, hosts and
careful employees say, and it costs nothing to learn.

The second is **asking someone senior how something is going**.
論文(ろんぶん)はどうですか to your supervisor can sound like you are checking up on
them. いかがですか, or 進(すす)んでいますか, avoids the edge.

For proposals, `どうですか` is genuinely soft, because it asks for an opinion rather
than a commitment. It is the standard way to float a time, a place or an option in a
conversation, and 水曜日(すいようび)はどうでしょうか is softer still - `でしょうか`
adds a layer of tentativeness that is very common in business email.

Watch the tense. A finished event takes `どうでしたか`; using `どうですか` about last
night's party is a real mistake, not a stylistic one.

Casually the copula drops: これ、どう？, 味(あじ)はどう？ Among friends this is the
default, and it can be blunt in the sense of demanding a verdict - asking どう？
about someone's cooking while they watch you eat is as pointed in Japanese as in
English.

## Contrast

### ～はどうですか vs ～はいかがですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different altitude. `いかがですか` is the polite
form of `どうですか`, and it is the default when offering something to a guest,
customer or superior.

</div>

<pre>
デザート<b>はどうですか</b>。
How about dessert?
</pre>

<pre>
デザート<b>はいかがですか</b>。
Would you care for a dessert?
</pre>

`いかが` is not a different word class or a different construction - everything you
can do with `どう`, you can do with `いかが`: いかがでしたか, いかがでしょうか. What
changes is who you are speaking to.

The one place `どうですか` is preferred is when you genuinely want a *judgement*
between equals: この案(あん)はどうですか to a colleague asks for their honest read.
`いかがですか` in the same slot can sound as though you are serving them something.

### ～はどうですか vs ～ませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はどうですか` proposes an **option** and asks what you think of
it; `ませんか` invites the **person** to do something with you.

</div>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんは、そばとカレーとどちらがいいですか。ここ<b>はどうですか</b>。
Soba or curry for lunch? How about here?
</pre>

<pre>
一緒(いっしょ)に昼(ひる)ごはんを<b>食(た)べませんか</b>。
Would you like to have lunch together?
</pre>

The second commits you both to an activity; the first only puts a candidate forward
inside a plan you are already making. That is why `はどうですか` is so useful in
arranging things - dates, venues, options - while `ませんか` is what opens the
arrangement in the first place.

They stack naturally in one conversation: 食(た)べに行(い)きませんか opens it,
金曜日(きんようび)はどうですか settles the detail.

### ～はどうですか vs ～たらどうですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はどうですか` offers an option; `たらどうですか` tells the
listener what *they* should do. The second is advice, and it can sting.

</div>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)<b>はどうですか</b>。
How about this medicine?
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>休(やす)んだらどうですか</b>。
Why don't you take a bit of a rest?
</pre>

`～たらどうですか` is built on the conditional た-form and means "why don't you…".
Said warmly it is caring; said flatly it implies the listener has failed to think of
something obvious, which is why it carries a faint edge of impatience. Upward it is
best avoided altogether - ～てはいかがでしょうか is the safe version.

Casual speech shortens it to `～たら？` - 医者(いしゃ)に行(い)ったら？ - and the
trailing, unfinished shape is exactly what makes it sound offhand.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)のパーティー<b>はどうですか</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)のパーティー<b>はどうでしたか</b>。
  A finished event takes the past form.

- ❌ 映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)く<b>はどうですか</b>。 → ✅ 映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)く<b>のはどうですか</b>。
  A verb needs the nominaliser `の` before `は`.

- ❌ 新(あたら)しい先生(せんせい)は<b>どんなですか</b>。 → ✅ 新(あたら)しい先生(せんせい)は<b>どうですか</b>。／<b>どんな人(ひと)ですか</b>。
  `どんな` cannot stand alone; it must attach to a noun.

- ❌ 社長(しゃちょう)、お飲(の)み物(もの)は<b>どうですか</b>。 → ✅ 社長(しゃちょう)、お飲(の)み物(もの)は<b>いかがですか</b>。
  Offering to someone senior calls for `いかが`.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、もっと早(はや)く<b>帰(かえ)ったらどうですか</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、もっと早(はや)く<b>お帰(かえ)りになってはいかがでしょうか</b>。
  Advice aimed upward needs a much softer frame.

## Practice

**1.** つかれた顔(かお)をしていますね。少(すこ)し休(やす)む____どうですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

少(すこ)し休(やす)む<b>のは</b>どうですか。

A verb cannot be followed directly by `は`; the nominaliser `の` turns
<code>休(やす)む</code> into something the topic particle can mark. You could also say
<code>休(やす)んだらどうですか</code>, which is more directly advice.

</details>

**2.** お客(きゃく)さんに：「お茶(ちゃ)は（どうですか・いかがですか）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お茶(ちゃ)は<b>いかがですか</b>。

`いかが` is the polite counterpart of `どう`, and offering refreshment to a guest or
customer is exactly where it belongs. <code>どうですか</code> is not wrong, just level
with a colleague rather than with a customer.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "How was the exam yesterday?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)の試験(しけん)<b>はどうでしたか</b>。

The exam is over, so the question has to be in the past: <code>どうでしたか</code>.
<code>どうですか</code> would be asking about an exam that is still going on.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しいアパートはどんなですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しいアパートは<b>どうですか</b>。

`どんな` is an attributive - it has to sit in front of a noun, as in
<code>どんなアパートですか</code>. To ask how something is, on its own, the word is `どう`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)<b>はどうですか</b>。
(b) 明日(あした)会(あ)い<b>ませんか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts a date forward as a candidate. It assumes you are already arranging to meet
and asks the other person to evaluate the option - it is about the *day*, not about
whether to meet at all.

(b) is the invitation itself. It asks whether the listener wants to meet you, and it
is the sentence that opens the arrangement rather than filling in its details.

</details>
