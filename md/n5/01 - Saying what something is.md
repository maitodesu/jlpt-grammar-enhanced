# N5 - Saying what something is


---

# だ / です

**Meaning** · the copula - "X is Y"

`だ` and `です` are the word that glues a noun to what it *is*. They are the same
word at two politeness levels: `です` is polite, `だ` is plain. Japanese has no verb
"to be" in the English sense - this is what stands in for it.

## Formation

`だ` / `です` come at the very end of the sentence, after the noun or な-adjective
they attach to. Nothing changes inside the noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だ / です | 先生(せんせい) → 先生(せんせい)だ / 先生(せんせい)です |
| な-adjective | stem (no な) + だ / です | 静(しず)かな → 静(しず)かだ / 静(しず)かです |
| い-adjective | plain adjective + です (never だ) | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いです |
| の-phrase (possession) | Noun + の + だ / です | 兄(あに)の → 兄(あに)のです |
| Question (polite) | Noun + ですか | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)ですか |
| Question (plain) | Noun + ? - the だ drops | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)? |
| Verb | does **not** take です - use ます | 行(い)く → 行(い)きます |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `です` after an い-adjective is doing a different job. It is not linking
anything; the adjective already is the predicate, and `です` is bolted on purely to
make the sentence polite. That is why <code>高(たか)いです</code> is fine but
❌<code>高(たか)いだ</code> is not: `だ` has no politeness to add.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんは先生(せんせい)<b>です</b>。
Yamada-san is a teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は休(やす)み<b>だ</b>。
Today's a day off.
</pre>

<pre>
わたしの部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>です</b>。
My room is quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
その自転車(じてんしゃ)は兄(あに)の<b>です</b>。
That bicycle is my older brother's.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>ですか</b>。
Is it going to rain tomorrow?
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は新(あたら)しい<b>です</b>。
This dictionary is new.
</pre>

<pre>
待(ま)ち合(あ)わせは六時(ろくじ)に駅(えき)の前(まえ)<b>です</b>。
We're meeting at six in front of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
「ご出身(しゅっしん)はどちら<b>ですか</b>。」「大阪(おおさか)<b>です</b>。」
"Where are you from?" "Osaka."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`です` is the default polite ending and it is safe everywhere: with strangers, at
work, in class, in a shop. If you are unsure which form to use, use `です`. It is
neither stiff nor warm - it is simply neutral-polite, and nobody will read anything
into it.

`だ` is the plain form, used with friends, family, and in your own head. It is also
the form used in writing that has no listener - notes, diaries, newspapers, essays
 - where politeness would be meaningless. What makes `だ` tricky is that in actual
speech it is very often **dropped**:

- 「これ、だれのかさ?」「わたしの。」 - the `だ` is simply not said.
- 「あの人(ひと)、学生(がくせい)?」 - a plain question does not keep `だ`.

Spoken `だ` that is *kept* sounds assertive and a little blunt, and it is more
common in men's casual speech. Women's casual speech drops it more often, or
softens it with `よ` / `ね` (「そうよ」「そうだね」). A learner who ends every casual
sentence with a hard `だ` sounds like a cartoon character; leaving it off is safer.

Two further forms you will meet but do not need to produce yet. `である` is the
copula of formal written argument - reports, papers, legal text; it never appears in
ordinary conversation. `でございます` is the humble-polite copula of shop staff and
announcements: 「こちらが新(あたら)しいメニューでございます」. Recognising them is
enough for now.

## Contrast

### だ vs です

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same word, two registers. `です` for anyone you are being polite
to; `だ` for people you are close to, and for writing with no reader in mind.

</div>

The choice says nothing about meaning and everything about the relationship.

<pre>
こちらが私(わたし)の机(つくえ)<b>です</b>。
This is my desk.
</pre>

<pre>
そこ、ぼくの席(せき)<b>だ</b>よ。
Hey, that's my seat.
</pre>

The second sentence is not ruder in content - it is simply aimed at a friend. Said
to a stranger, it would land as abrupt.

Politeness in Japanese is decided at the **end** of the sentence, so this one word
sets the tone for everything that came before it. Do not mix levels inside a single
sentence without a reason.

### い-adjective + です vs な-adjective + だ / です

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - い-adjectives are already complete predicates: add `です` for
politeness, and never `だ`. な-adjectives and nouns need the copula to stand up at
all.

</div>

<pre>
この荷物(にもつ)は重(おも)い<b>です</b>。
This luggage is heavy.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>です</b>。
This room is quiet.
</pre>

Both end in `です`, but for different reasons - and the difference surfaces the
moment you go plain. <code>重(おも)い</code> stands alone as a whole sentence;
<code>静(しず)か</code> cannot, and needs `だ`:

<pre>
この荷物(にもつ)、<b>重(おも)い</b>。
This luggage is heavy.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)、静(しず)か<b>だ</b>。
This room is quiet.
</pre>

So the test is: strip the politeness away. If what is left needs propping up, it
takes `だ`.

### です vs ます

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `です` makes a **noun or adjective** sentence polite; `ます`
makes a **verb** sentence polite. You never need both.

</div>

English hides "is" inside every tense, so learners reach for `です` when a verb is
already doing the work. Japanese verbs carry their own politeness.

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは医者(いしゃ)<b>です</b>。
Tanaka-san is a doctor.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは毎日(まいにち)走(はし)り<b>ます</b>。
Tanaka-san runs every day.
</pre>

❌<code>走(はし)るです</code> and ❌<code>走(はし)りますです</code> are both broken. The one place a verb
does meet `です` is through a nominaliser - <code>走(はし)るのが好(す)きです</code> - and even there
`です` is attached to <code>好(す)き</code>, a な-adjective, not to the verb.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この本(ほん)は高(たか)いだ。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は高(たか)い。
  An い-adjective is already a predicate. It takes `です` for politeness, but never `だ`.

- ❌ 静(しず)かだです。 → ✅ 静(しず)かです。
  `だ` and `です` are one word at two levels. You choose one; you never stack them.

- ❌ 毎朝(まいあさ)コーヒーを飲(の)むです。 → ✅ 毎朝(まいあさ)コーヒーを飲(の)みます。
  Verbs are made polite with `ます`, not with `です`.

- ❌ あの人(ひと)は学生(がくせい)だですか。 → ✅ あの人(ひと)は学生(がくせい)ですか。
  In a polite question the whole copula is `です`, and `か` follows it directly.

- ❌ これは静(しず)かなです。 → ✅ これは静(しず)かです。
  A な-adjective keeps its `な` only in front of a noun (<code>静(しず)かな部屋(へや)</code>).
  Before the copula the `な` drops.

## Practice

**1.** あの建物(たてもの)は図書館(としょかん)____。（polite）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの建物(たてもの)は図書館(としょかん)<b>です</b>。

A noun predicate needs the copula, and the polite copula is `です`. Nothing changes
inside the noun itself.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しい（だ・です・nothing）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

難(むずか)しい<b>です</b> (polite), or 難(むずか)しい on its own (plain).

<code>難(むずか)しい</code> is an い-adjective, so it is already a complete sentence by itself.
Add `です` if you want to be polite, but ❌<code>難(むずか)しいだ</code> is never possible.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "That umbrella is my mother's."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そのかさは母(はは)の<b>です</b>。

The `の` carries the possession, and the noun it stands for ("umbrella") is simply
left out, so `です` attaches straight onto `の`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山田(やまだ)さんは明日(あした)来(く)るですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山田(やまだ)さんは明日(あした)来(き)ますか。

The predicate is a verb, and verbs take `ます` for politeness - `です` has no work to
do here. The question particle `か` then follows `ます`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in feel:
(a) 明日(あした)は休(やす)みです。
(b) 明日(あした)は休(やす)みだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The information is identical: tomorrow is a day off. Only the relationship differs.

(a) is aimed at someone you are being polite to - a colleague, a customer, a
teacher. (b) is aimed at a friend, or at nobody at all: it is also exactly how the
sentence would be written in a diary or a memo. Said out loud to a stranger, (b)
sounds abrupt.

</details>

# じゃない・ではない

**Meaning** · the negative copula - "X is not Y"

`じゃない` and `ではない` are the negative of `だ` / `です`. They deny that one thing
is the other. `ではない` is the full form; `じゃない` is its everyday contraction,
and it is what you will hear almost all the time.

## Formation

`では` contracts to `じゃ` in speech, which is the whole story behind the two shapes.
Everything below comes in pairs for that reason.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (plain) | Noun + じゃない / ではない | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)じゃない |
| Noun (polite) | Noun + じゃないです / じゃありません | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)じゃありません |
| Noun (formal) | Noun + ではありません | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)ではありません |
| な-adjective | stem (no な) + じゃない | 静(しず)かな → 静(しず)かじゃない |
| い-adjective | ～い → ～くない (never じゃない) | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)くない |
| Verb | ～ない form (never じゃない) | 行(い)く → 行(い)かない |
| Past | じゃなかった / じゃありませんでした | 学生(がくせい)じゃなかった |
| Joining on | じゃなくて (て-form) | Aじゃなくて、B |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the politeness ladder runs
`じゃない` → `じゃないです` → `じゃありません` → `ではありません` → `ではございません`,
from most casual to most formal. The middle three are all "polite"; they differ only
in how starched they sound.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは私(わたし)のかさ<b>じゃない</b>。
This isn't my umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は木曜日(もくようび)<b>じゃありません</b>。
Today isn't Thursday.
</pre>

<pre>
その話(はなし)は本当(ほんとう)<b>じゃないです</b>よ。
That story isn't true.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)は来週(らいしゅう)<b>じゃなくて</b>、再来週(さらいしゅう)です。
The exam isn't next week - it's the week after.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)<b>じゃない</b>よ。本当(ほんとう)の話(はなし)だ。
I'm not joking. It really happened.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)のとき、わたしは真面目(まじめ)な学生(がくせい)<b>じゃなかった</b>。
I wasn't a serious student back in university.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)は夜(よる)あまり静(しず)か<b>じゃない</b>ので、窓(まど)を閉(し)めて寝(ね)ています。
It isn't very quiet around here at night, so I sleep with the window shut.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが、これは私(わたし)の担当(たんとう)<b>ではありません</b>ので、係(かかり)の者(もの)を呼(よ)びます。
I'm very sorry, but this isn't my area - I'll fetch the person in charge.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The split between `じゃ` and `では` is one of pronunciation, not of meaning.
`ではない` is what you write; `じゃない` is what you say. Reading `ではありません`
aloud in a friendly conversation sounds like reading from a form.

Within the polite forms there is a further, smaller split. `じゃありません` is the
textbook polite negative and is perfectly normal in speech. `じゃないです` is a shade
more conversational - it is built by taking the plain `じゃない` and adding `です`,
the same trick い-adjectives use - and it is extremely common in modern spoken
Japanese. `ではありません` belongs to announcements, written notices and stiff
business speech, where nobody is trying to sound warm.

One more thing about `じゃない` catches learners badly. With **falling** intonation
it negates: 「山田(やまだ)さんじゃない。」 = "That's not Yamada." With **rising**
intonation it does the opposite - it becomes a tag question asserting that something
*is* so: 「山田(やまだ)さんじゃない?」 = "Isn't that Yamada?" This second use can
attach to things the negative copula normally cannot, including い-adjectives and
verbs (「高(たか)いじゃない!」 = "That's expensive!"). If you meet `じゃない`
attached to an い-adjective, it is this tag-question use, not negation.

In casual speech `じゃない` shortens further to `じゃん`: 「いいじゃん」 = "that's
fine, isn't it". It is friendly, young-sounding, and out of place at work.

## Contrast

### じゃない vs ～くない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `じゃない` negates **nouns and な-adjectives**. い-adjectives
negate themselves by changing `～い` to `～くない`.

</div>

The two look interchangeable in English ("is not X") but they are not.

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は簡単(かんたん)<b>じゃない</b>。
This problem isn't easy.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)し<b>くない</b>。
This problem isn't hard.
</pre>

<code>簡単(かんたん)</code> is a な-adjective, so it needs the copula and takes `じゃない`.
<code>難(むずか)しい</code> is an い-adjective, so it conjugates on its own. The same test as with
`だ`: if the word needs the copula to stand up, it negates with `じゃない`.

`いい` is the irregular one worth learning now: it negates as `よくない`, never
❌`いくない`.

### じゃありません vs じゃないです vs ではありません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three are polite. Pick by how formal the room is, not by
what you mean.

</div>

<pre>
それは私(わたし)のかばん<b>じゃないです</b>。
That's not my bag.
</pre>

<pre>
それは私(わたし)のかばん<b>じゃありません</b>。
That's not my bag.
</pre>

<pre>
当店(とうてん)は本日(ほんじつ)営業(えいぎょう)<b>ではありません</b>。
Our shop is not open for business today.
</pre>

The first is what you say to a coworker; the second is what a textbook or a careful
speaker says; the third is what a sign says. A learner who only ever produces
`じゃありません` will never sound wrong, which makes it the safe default.

### じゃない vs 違(ちが)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `じゃない` denies an equation ("A is not B"); <code>違(ちが)う</code> is a verb
meaning "to differ / to be mistaken", and it is what you say to correct someone.

</div>

<pre>
これは私(わたし)の傘(かさ)<b>じゃない</b>です。
This isn't my umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
「田中(たなか)さんですか。」「いいえ、<b>違(ちが)います</b>。」
"Are you Tanaka-san?" "No, I'm not."
</pre>

Both are heard as "no", but they work differently. <code>違(ちが)います</code> is a complete
answer on its own and needs no noun in front of it, which is exactly why it is the
standard way to say "that's not right". Notice that answering a question with a bare
「田中(たなか)じゃありません」 sounds blunter than 「違(ちが)います」 - the
verb is the softer, more idiomatic correction.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この本(ほん)は高(たか)いじゃない。（meaning "this book isn't expensive"） → ✅ この本(ほん)は高(たか)くない。
  い-adjectives negate with `～くない`. The string <code>高(たか)いじゃない</code> does exist, but only as
  the rising tag question "it's expensive, isn't it!" - the opposite of what you meant.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)かだじゃない。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)かじゃない。
  `じゃない` already contains the copula. Never leave `だ` in front of it.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は雨(あめ)じゃないでした。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は雨(あめ)じゃありませんでした。
  The polite negative past is built on `ありません`, not on `です`. In plain form:
  雨(あめ)じゃなかった。

- ❌ わたしはコーヒーを飲(の)むじゃない。 → ✅ わたしはコーヒーを飲(の)まない。
  Verbs have their own negative. `じゃない` never attaches to a verb to negate it.

## Practice

**1.** あの人(ひと)は先生(せんせい)____。（plain, negative）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの人(ひと)は先生(せんせい)<b>じゃない</b>。

<code>先生(せんせい)</code> is a noun, so it needs the copula, and the plain negative copula is
`じゃない`. The fuller `ではない` would be the written equivalent.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この店(みせ)は（安(やす)くない・安(やす)いじゃない）。 - "This shop isn't cheap."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)くない</b>。

<code>安(やす)い</code> is an い-adjective, so it negates by changing `い` to `くない`. The other
option would be heard as a tag question meaning "it's cheap, isn't it!", which is
the opposite claim.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "It isn't Monday today - it's Tuesday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は月曜日(げつようび)<b>じゃなくて</b>、火曜日(かようび)です。

The て-form `じゃなくて` links the correction to what follows, so you get one smooth
sentence instead of two. 「月曜日(げつようび)じゃありません。火曜日(かようび)です。」
also works and is a little more emphatic.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 去年(きょねん)のアパートは便利(べんり)だじゃありませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 去年(きょねん)のアパートは便利(べんり)じゃありませんでした。

<code>便利(べんり)</code> is a な-adjective, and the negative form already carries the copula, so
the extra `だ` has to go. Only the stem is left in front of `じゃ`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山田(やまだ)さんじゃない。
(b) 山田(やまだ)さんじゃない?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a), said with falling intonation, is straightforward negation: "that isn't Yamada."

(b), with a rising tail, flips into a tag question and actually claims the opposite:
"that's Yamada, isn't it?" The speaker is fairly sure and is inviting the listener to
confirm. The written form is identical, so intonation carries the whole meaning here.

</details>

# だった・でした

**Meaning** · the past copula - "X was Y"

`だった` and `でした` put the copula into the past. `だった` is plain, `でした` is
polite. They are how you say what something *was*, where it *was*, and how it *felt*
 - for nouns and な-adjectives.

## Formation

The past copula replaces `だ` / `です` entirely; nothing else in the sentence moves.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だった / でした | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)だった / 学生(がくせい)でした |
| な-adjective | stem (no な) + だった / でした | 元気(げんき)な → 元気(げんき)だった / 元気(げんき)でした |
| い-adjective | ～い → ～かった (+ です) | 楽(たの)しい → 楽(たの)しかった / 楽(たの)しかったです |
| Negative past | じゃなかった / じゃありませんでした | 学生(がくせい)じゃなかった |
| Question | Noun + でしたか / plain + ? | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)でしたか |
| Verb | ～た / ～ました (never でした) | 行(い)く → 行(い)った / 行(い)きました |
| Before a noun | だった + Noun (でした cannot) | 学生(がくせい)だった人(ひと) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the last row is worth pausing on. Inside a sentence, modifying a noun,
only the plain `だった` is available: <code>学生(がくせい)だった人(ひと)</code> = "a person who was a
student". The politeness of the whole sentence is still decided at the very end.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
昨日(きのう)のテストは簡単(かんたん)<b>だった</b>。
Yesterday's test was easy.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)まで学生(がくせい)<b>でした</b>。
I was a student until last year.
</pre>

<pre>
あの映画(えいが)、どう<b>だった</b>?
How was that movie?
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものとき、野球(やきゅう)が好(す)き<b>でした</b>。
I liked baseball when I was a child.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は水曜日(すいようび)<b>じゃなかった</b>。火曜日(かようび)だったよ。
The meeting wasn't Wednesday. It was Tuesday.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)のパーティーはにぎやか<b>でしたか</b>。
Was yesterday's party lively?
</pre>

<pre>
十年前(じゅうねんまえ)、ここは大(おお)きい工場(こうじょう)<b>でした</b>が、今(いま)は公園(こうえん)になっています。
Ten years ago this was a big factory, but now it's a park.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の旅行(りょこう)は雨(あめ)ばかりで大変(たいへん)<b>でした</b>が、とても楽(たの)しかったです。
Last year's trip was nothing but rain and pretty rough, but it was a lot of fun.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The register split is the same one `だ` and `です` already set up: `でした` for
people you are being polite to, `だった` for friends, family and writing with no
reader. Nothing about the past tense changes that calculation.

Unlike plain `だ`, plain `だった` is **not** dropped in casual speech. 「テスト、
簡単(かんたん)?」 works as a present-tense question with no copula at all, but to
put it in the past you must say 「簡単(かんたん)だった?」 - there is no other way
to carry the tense. This is the first place where beginners who have learned to drop
`だ` get stuck.

`でした` also has a quiet second life as part of the polite negative past for
adjectives and verbs: `～ませんでした`, `～くありませんでした`. In those it is not the
copula any more, just the polite past ending being reused. That is exactly why
❌<code>楽(たの)しいでした</code> feels tempting and is wrong: い-adjectives make their own past
with `～かった`, and `です` may only be added afterwards.

One nuance about `だった` in speech: with a falling, drawn-out delivery
(「そうだった～」) it often marks a sudden recollection - "oh, right, that's how it
was." Learners hear this a lot and mistake it for ordinary past tense.

## Contrast

### だった vs でした

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different politeness. `でした` in polite
speech, `だった` with friends and in plain-style writing.

</div>

<pre>
先週(せんしゅう)の試合(しあい)は残念(ざんねん)<b>でした</b>。
Last week's match was a disappointment.
</pre>

<pre>
先週(せんしゅう)の試合(しあい)、残念(ざんねん)<b>だった</b>な。
Shame about last week's match.
</pre>

There is one asymmetry worth knowing. `だった` can sit **inside** a sentence,
modifying a noun or leading into <code>と思(おも)う</code>; `でした` cannot, because polite
forms live only at the end.

<pre>
昔(むかし)、ここは海(うみ)<b>だった</b>と聞(き)きました。
I heard this used to be the sea.
</pre>

The sentence is polite - but the politeness is carried by <code>聞(き)きました</code> at the end,
and the embedded copula stays plain.

### い-adjective past vs でした

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - い-adjectives have their own past, `～かった`. They never take
`でした`.

</div>

This is the single most common past-tense error at N5, because English puts "was" in
front of both kinds of word.

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は寒(さむ)<b>かったです</b>。
It was cold yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は暇(ひま)<b>でした</b>。
I was free yesterday.
</pre>

<code>寒(さむ)い</code> is an い-adjective, so the past is built inside the word and `です` is
added only for politeness. <code>暇(ひま)</code> is a な-adjective, so it has no past of its own
and hands the job to the copula. ❌<code>寒(さむ)いでした</code> and ❌<code>寒(さむ)かったでした</code> are
both wrong; ❌<code>暇(ひま)かった</code> is wrong for the same reason in reverse.

`いい` is irregular here too: the past is `よかった`, never ❌`いかった`.

### でした vs ました

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `でした` is the past of a **noun / な-adjective** sentence;
`ました` is the past of a **verb** sentence.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)は北海道(ほっかいどう)に<b>いました</b>。
Last summer I was in Hokkaido.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)は北海道(ほっかいどう)に<b>いた</b>。
Last summer I was in Hokkaido.
</pre>

Note that English "was" here is not the copula at all - it is existence, which
Japanese handles with the verbs <code>いる</code> and <code>ある</code>, and therefore with `ました` / `た`.
Saying ❌<code>北海道(ほっかいどう)にでした</code> is not possible.

The negative past follows the same division: `～ませんでした` for verbs,
`じゃありませんでした` for nouns.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 映画(えいが)は面白(おもしろ)いでした。 → ✅ 映画(えいが)は面白(おもしろ)かったです。
  い-adjectives carry their own past in `～かった`. `でした` belongs to nouns and
  な-adjectives.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しかったでした。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しかったです。
  Once the adjective is already past, only `です` is added - the past is not marked twice.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)だでした。 → ✅ 元気(げんき)でした。
  `でした` already is the copula. There is no `だ` in front of it.

- ❌ 友(とも)だちと映画(えいが)を見(み)るでした。 → ✅ 友(とも)だちと映画(えいが)を見(み)ました。
  A verb sentence goes past with `ました`, never with `でした`.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)だったです。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)でした。
  <code>楽(たの)しかったです</code> works because `です` is attaching to an adjective. After the
  copula `だった` it is not standard - use `でした` instead.

## Practice

**1.** 先週(せんしゅう)の日曜日(にちようび)は雨(あめ)____。（polite）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先週(せんしゅう)の日曜日(にちようび)は雨(あめ)<b>でした</b>。

<code>雨(あめ)</code> is a noun, so the past tense has to be carried by the copula, and the
polite past copula is `でした`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 旅行(りょこう)は（楽(たの)しいでした・楽(たの)しかったです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

旅行(りょこう)は<b>楽(たの)しかったです</b>。

<code>楽(たの)しい</code> is an い-adjective, so it forms its own past by changing `い` to
`かった`; `です` is then added purely for politeness. The first option marks the past
in the wrong place.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, plain form: "When I was little, my father was a teacher."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

小(ちい)さいとき、父(ちち)は先生(せんせい)<b>だった</b>。

Both halves are past in English, but only the main clause needs marking: the
<code>とき</code> clause takes its tense from the adjective, and the noun predicate
<code>先生(せんせい)</code> takes the plain past copula.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)のコンサートはとてもよかったでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)のコンサートはとてもよかったです。

`よかった` is already the past of `いい`, so adding `でした` marks the past twice. Only
`です` may follow, and it adds politeness alone.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 田中(たなか)さんは先生(せんせい)でした。
(b) 田中(たなか)さんは先生(せんせい)だったんです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say Tanaka used to be a teacher, politely. (a) is a flat report of a fact - the
kind of thing you would write on a form or state in an introduction.

(b) wraps the same fact in the explanatory `んです`, so it comes across as
background offered for a reason: "the thing is, he used to be a teacher" - explaining
why he is so good with children, for instance. Notice the copula goes plain
(`だった`) before `んです`, with the politeness moved to the very end.

</details>

# は

**Meaning** · marks the topic - "as for X, …"

`は` does not mean anything by itself. It lifts a noun out of the sentence and hangs
it up as the thing being talked *about*; everything after it is the comment. It is
written with the kana は but **pronounced わ**.

## Formation

`は` attaches to a noun or a noun phrase with no change to the noun. What matters is
what it does to the particle that was already there.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + は | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)は |
| A subject marked が | が is **replaced** | 犬(いぬ)が好(す)き → 犬(いぬ)は好(す)き |
| An object marked を | を is **replaced** | ごはんを食(た)べる → ごはんは食(た)べる |
| に・で・と・から・まで | は is **added after** them | 東京(とうきょう)に → 東京(とうきょう)には |
| Time / adverb phrase | phrase + は | 今日(きょう) → 今日(きょう)は |
| Negative sentences | は is the default marker | 時間(じかん)はありません |
| Pronunciation | written は, said わ | - |

<div class="note">

**Note** - that third and fourth row are the whole mechanical rule, and it is worth
memorising as a pair. `が` and `を` **disappear** under `は`; every other particle
**survives** and `は` stacks on top. There is no ❌`がは` or ❌`をは`, but `には`,
`では`, `とは`, `からは` are all normal.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)<b>は</b>寒(さむ)いですね。
It's cold today, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
この本(ほん)<b>は</b>面白(おもしろ)いです。
This book is interesting.
</pre>

<pre>
野菜(やさい)<b>は</b>好(す)きですが、肉(にく)<b>は</b>あまり好(す)きじゃありません。
I like vegetables, but I'm not so keen on meat.
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)に<b>は</b>会議(かいぎ)がありません。
There are no meetings on Saturdays.
</pre>

<pre>
きのう<b>は</b>ありがとうございました。
Thank you for yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)のラーメン<b>は</b>本当(ほんとう)においしいですよ。
The ramen at that place is genuinely good.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の夏(なつ)<b>は</b>暑(あつ)くて、東京(とうきょう)で<b>は</b>夜(よる)も気温(きおん)が下(さ)がりません。
Summer in Japan is hot, and in Tokyo the temperature doesn't even drop at night.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>は</b>来週(らいしゅう)大阪(おおさか)へ行(い)きますが、妹(いもうと)<b>は</b>家(いえ)に残(のこ)ります。
I'm going to Osaka next week, but my younger sister is staying home.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`は` is register-neutral. It is the same in plain speech, polite speech, and
writing, and choosing it tells the listener nothing about how formal you are being.

Two habits will make you sound much more natural. The first is **leaving it out**.
Once a topic is established, Japanese simply keeps talking about it - you do not
restate it. A conversation that begins 「田中(たなか)さんは元気(げんき)ですか」
continues 「はい、元気(げんき)です」, with no topic at all. Beginners who start
every sentence with <code>私(わたし)は</code> sound like a textbook, and after a while
faintly self-absorbed; native speakers say it once, if at all, and then drop it.

The second is that in casual speech `は` is often just not pronounced - 
「これ、おいしい」 - or replaced by `って`, which is the conversational topic marker:
「田中(たなか)さん<b>って</b>、面白(おもしろ)いね」 = "Tanaka's funny, isn't he."
`って` is friendly and slightly gossipy, and it is out of place in a report.

Finally, `は` always carries a faint edge of **contrast**, even when you do not
intend one. Saying 「今日(きょう)は忙(いそが)しいです」 quietly implies "today
in particular - other days, not so much." That side effect is very useful for softening
refusals (「今日(きょう)はちょっと…」), and it is also why piling `は` onto
something can sound pointed: 「あなたはいいですね」 can land as "well, it's all
right for *you*."

## Contrast

### は vs が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the new information goes **after** `は`, but **before** `が`.

</div>

This is the single hardest thing at N5, and the confusion starts from a bad label:
`は` is not a subject marker. `が` marks the **subject** - the thing that does the
verb. `は` marks the **topic** - the thing under discussion. They often land in the
same slot in the sentence, which makes them look like alternatives, but they are
answering different needs.

Watch where the news sits:

<pre>
猫(ねこ)<b>は</b>外(そと)にいます。
The cat is outside.
</pre>

We both know which cat; the news is *where it is*, and it comes after `は`. This
answers "where's the cat?"

<pre>
猫(ねこ)<b>が</b>外(そと)にいます。
There's a cat outside.
</pre>

Here the news is *the cat itself* - presented fresh, before `が`. This answers
"what's out there?", or it is simply you noticing.

Two consequences follow directly. First, a question word is by definition the
unknown, so it must sit on the `が` side and can never take `は`:
「どれ<b>が</b>山田(やまだ)さんのかばんですか」. Second, a brand-new thing is
introduced with `が` and then talked about with `は` - which is why stories start one
way and continue the other.

`は` also reaches further than `が`. A topic scopes over everything to the end of the
sentence, while `が` only marks the subject of its own clause. That is why `は`
cannot appear inside a clause that is merely modifying a noun:

<pre>
これは姉(あね)<b>が</b>作(つく)ったケーキです。
This is a cake my older sister made.
</pre>

<code>姉(あね)が作(つく)った</code> is decorating <code>ケーキ</code>; the outer topic is <code>これ</code>. Putting `は`
on <code>姉(あね)</code> would try to make her the topic of the whole sentence, and it breaks.

And both can live in one sentence, doing their separate jobs:

<pre>
日本(にほん)<b>は</b>電車(でんしゃ)<b>が</b>便利(べんり)です。
Japan has convenient trains. (As for Japan - the trains are convenient.)
</pre>

If you take one thing from this section: ask yourself which word in your sentence is
the *news*. If the news is the noun in front of the particle, use `が`. If the news
is what comes after it, use `は`.

### は vs も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` says "as for this one"; `も` says "this one as well". Both
replace `が` and `を`, and only one can be there at a time.

</div>

They occupy the same slot, so choosing between them is choosing what you imply about
everything else.

<pre>
兄(あに)<b>は</b>大学生(だいがくせい)です。
My older brother is a university student.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)<b>も</b>大学生(だいがくせい)です。
My older brother is a university student too.
</pre>

The `も` version only makes sense if somebody else has already been named as a
student. The `は` version, by contrast, faintly suggests a comparison going the other
way - the brother, as opposed to whoever else we were discussing.

In negative sentences the pair splits cleanly: `は` gives "not this one (whatever
about the others)", `も` gives "not this one either".

<pre>
コーヒー<b>は</b>飲(の)みません。
I don't drink coffee. (tea might be another matter)
</pre>

<pre>
コーヒー<b>も</b>飲(の)みません。
I don't drink coffee either.
</pre>

### は with other particles: には・では・とは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` swallows `が` and `を`, but it rides on the back of every
other particle.

</div>

The particle that shows the *role* of the noun cannot be thrown away, or the sentence
stops making sense. So it stays, and `は` is added behind it.

<pre>
京都(きょうと)に<b>は</b>一度(いちど)行(い)きました。
I've been to Kyoto once. (Kyoto, at least - other places, who knows)
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)で<b>は</b>カードが使(つか)えません。
You can't use a card at this shop.
</pre>

Dropping the inner particle changes or destroys the meaning:
❌<code>京都(きょうと)は行(い)きました</code> loses the "to", and
❌<code>この店(みせ)は…</code> would make the shop itself the topic rather than the place of the
action. The order is always **particle first, `は` second** - never ❌`はに`, ❌`はで`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ だれ**は**この窓(まど)を開(あ)けましたか。 → ✅ だれ**が**この窓(まど)を開(あ)けましたか。
  A question word is the unknown, and an unknown cannot be a topic. Question words take `が`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)**がは**行(い)きます。 → ✅ 私(わたし)**は**行(い)きます。
  `は` replaces `が`; it never stacks with it. The same goes for `を`.

- ❌ 大阪(おおさか)**は**住(す)んでいます。 → ✅ 大阪(おおさか)**に**住(す)んでいます。（or 大阪(おおさか)**には**…）
  <code>住(す)む</code> needs `に` to show "in". `は` may be added on top of `に`, but it cannot
  take its place.

- ❌ これ**は**母(はは)**は**作(つく)った料理(りょうり)です。 → ✅ これ**は**母(はは)**が**作(つく)った料理(りょうり)です。
  Inside a clause that modifies a noun, the subject takes `が`. A topic cannot be
  trapped in there.

- ❌ Pronouncing は as *ha* when it is the particle. It is always **wa**. (The same
  kana is *ha* inside ordinary words, as in はな.)

## Practice

**1.** 「すみません、トイレ____どこですか。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

トイレ<b>は</b>どこですか。

The toilet is the thing you are asking *about* - old, shared information - so it is
the topic. The unknown is <code>どこ</code>, and in a `は` sentence the unknown comes after
the particle.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 「あ、あそこに人(ひと)（は・が）立(た)っていますよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人(ひと)<b>が</b>立(た)っています。

You are reporting something you have just noticed, and the person is brand-new
information. New information comes *before* `が`. `は` would imply the listener
already had that person in mind.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I don't eat meat, but I do eat fish."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

肉(にく)<b>は</b>食(た)べませんが、魚(さかな)<b>は</b>食(た)べます。

Both nouns are objects, so both would normally take `を` - but the sentence is built
on a contrast, and that is `は`'s speciality. `を` is replaced, not stacked, in both
halves.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来年(らいねん)、日本(にほん)は留学(りゅうがく)したいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来年(らいねん)、日本(にほん)<b>に</b>留学(りゅうがく)したいです。（or 日本(にほん)<b>には</b>…）

<code>留学(りゅうがく)する</code> needs `に` to mark the destination. `は` can be layered on top of
`に` for emphasis or contrast, but on its own it leaves the sentence with no
indication of where you are going.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今日(きょう)<b>は</b>忙(いそが)しいです。
(b) 今日(きょう)、忙(いそが)しいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) sets today up as the topic, and `は` drags a comparison in with it: "today I'm
busy" - with the unspoken implication "unlike other days", or "so not today,
please." This is why it is the polite way to turn an invitation down.

(b) has no topic marker and simply states the situation. It is flatter and more
factual, with none of the "as opposed to" flavour. In casual speech the particle is
often dropped for exactly this reason.

</details>

# も

**Meaning** · also; too; as well

`も` adds its noun to something already on the table. Whatever was just said about A
is now said about B as well. In negative sentences the same particle gives you "not
… either", and with a number it turns into an expression of surprise: "as many as".

## Formation

`も` sits exactly where `は` would sit, and follows the same replacement rule.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + も | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)も |
| A subject marked が | が is **replaced** | 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る → 雨(あめ)も降(ふ)る |
| An object marked を | を is **replaced** | ケーキを食(た)べる → ケーキも食(た)べる |
| A topic marked は | は is **replaced** | 私(わたし)は → 私(わたし)も |
| に・で・と・から・まで | も is **added after** them | 京都(きょうと)に → 京都(きょうと)にも |
| A も B も | both A and B (or, with a negative, neither) | 英語(えいご)も中国語(ちゅうごくご)も |
| Question word + も | + negative = not any | 何(なに)も／だれも／どこも |
| Number + counter + も | as many as … | 三杯(さんばい)も |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the replacement rule matters more here than anywhere else, because
English lets you tack "too" onto the end of a finished sentence. Japanese does not:
`も` goes on the noun that is being added, and it pushes `が` / `を` / `は` out of
the way.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
わたし<b>も</b>行(い)きます。
I'll go too.
</pre>

<pre>
これ<b>も</b>ください。
I'll take this one as well, please.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)は英語(えいご)<b>も</b>中国語(ちゅうごくご)<b>も</b>話(はな)します。
My older sister speaks both English and Chinese.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は何(なに)<b>も</b>買(か)いませんでした。
I didn't buy anything yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
「わたし、甘(あま)いものが苦手(にがて)なんだ。」「え、わたし<b>も</b>。」
"I'm not big on sweet things." "Oh - me neither."
</pre>

<pre>
きのうは水(みず)を二(に)リットル<b>も</b>飲(の)んだ。
I drank a whole two litres of water yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>も</b>同(おな)じ電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)っていて、駅(えき)でびっくりしました。
Tanaka was on the same train as well, and I got a surprise at the station.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は値段(ねだん)が安(やす)くて、店員(てんいん)さん<b>も</b>親切(しんせつ)なので、よく行(い)きます。
That shop is cheap and the staff are friendly too, so I go there often.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`も` is register-neutral: identical in plain and polite speech, in writing, and in
anyone's mouth. Nothing about it is casual or formal.

What it *is* sensitive to is context. `も` only works if the listener can supply the
"as well as what". 「わたしも」 as a one-word reply - "me too" - is perfectly natural
precisely because the other half was just said out loud. Dropped into a conversation
with nothing to attach to, `も` sounds like the beginning of a sentence you forgot to
finish.

Beyond plain addition, two extensions are worth having now.

With a **number**, `も` stops meaning "also" and starts meaning "as many as" - it
marks the amount as more than expected: 「三時間(さんじかん)も待(ま)った」 = "I
waited a full three hours." With a *small* number plus a negative it flips to "not
even": 「一度(いちど)も行(い)ったことがない」 = "I've never been, not even once."

With a **question word**, `も` plus a negative sweeps the whole category away:
だれも来(こ)ない = "nobody came", 何(なに)もない = "there's nothing",
どこにも行(い)かない = "I'm not going anywhere". This construction is fixed - the
negative is not optional, and this is where beginners most often build a sentence
that means the opposite of what they wanted.

`も` also has a softening, generous feel when offering things: 「コーヒーもいかがですか」
sounds like an addition rather than a demand, and shopkeepers use it constantly.

## Contrast

### も vs は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` sets its noun **apart** from the others; `も` puts it
**alongside** them.

</div>

They fight for the same slot in the sentence, so the choice is always meaningful.

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>は</b>ピアノを習(なら)っています。
My little brother takes piano lessons.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>も</b>ピアノを習(なら)っています。
My little brother takes piano lessons too.
</pre>

The second sentence only stands up if somebody else was already named as a piano
student. The first quietly does the reverse: it hints that the brother is the
exception among the people under discussion.

This distinction is sharpest in refusals and admissions. 「今日(きょう)は
無理(むり)です」 leaves other days open; 「今日(きょう)も無理(むり)です」
admits today is one more in a run of no's.

### も vs と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` glues nouns into one phrase ("A and B"); `も` marks each
noun separately as an addition ("A as well, B as well").

</div>

English "and" covers both, which is why this goes wrong so often.

<pre>
パン<b>と</b>たまごを買(か)いました。
I bought bread and eggs.
</pre>

<pre>
パン<b>も</b>たまご<b>も</b>買(か)いました。
I bought bread *and* eggs - both of them.
</pre>

The `と` version is a neutral shopping list. The `も…も` version stresses the
completeness of it, and it is the only one that can turn into "neither" simply by
negating the verb: 「パンもたまごも買(か)いませんでした」 = "I bought neither."

Note also that `と` joins **nouns only**. To link two whole sentences, `と` is not
available at all.

### だれも / 何(なに)も vs だれか / 何(なに)か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - question word + `も` needs a **negative** and means "none";
question word + `か` is **positive** and means "some".

</div>

The two look like near-twins and mean opposite things.

<pre>
部屋(へや)にだれ<b>も</b>いません。
There's nobody in the room.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)にだれ<b>か</b>いますか。
Is somebody in the room?
</pre>

Mixing them up produces sentences that are not just unnatural but ungrammatical:
❌<code>だれも来(き)ました</code> cannot mean "somebody came" - `だれも` demands a negative verb,
so the sentence simply breaks. Use <code>だれか来(き)ました</code> for "somebody came."

## Common mistakes

- ❌ わたし**はも**行(い)きます。 → ✅ わたし**も**行(い)きます。
  `も` replaces `は`, `が` and `を`. It never stacks with them.

- ❌ ケーキ**をも**食(た)べました。 → ✅ ケーキ**も**食(た)べました。
  Same rule for the object marker: `を` disappears under `も`.

- ❌ 友(とも)だち**もと**行(い)きました。 → ✅ 友(とも)だち**とも**行(い)きました。
  With the particles that survive (`に`, `で`, `と`, `から`), the order is always
  particle first, `も` second.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は何(なに)**も**食(た)べました。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は何(なに)**か**食(た)べました。
  <code>何(なに)も</code> can only be followed by a negative. For "I ate something", use <code>何(なに)か</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 「山田(やまだ)さんは来(き)ますか。」「はい。田中(たなか)さん____来(き)ますよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

田中(たなか)さん<b>も</b>来(き)ますよ。

Yamada has already been established as coming, so Tanaka is being added to that
group. `も` replaces the `が` that would otherwise mark the subject.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に何(なに)（も・か）ありませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に何(なに)<b>も</b>ありませんでした。

The verb is negative, and question word + `も` + negative is the fixed way to say
"nothing at all". <code>何(なに)か</code> would need a positive verb: <code>何(なに)かありました</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My brother can't swim, and I can't either."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

兄(あに)は泳(およ)げません。わたし<b>も</b>泳(およ)げません。

The `も` does the work of "either" - Japanese uses the same particle for "too" and
"either", and the negative verb supplies the difference. Note that `も` replaces the
`は` that marked the first speaker's topic.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 去年(きょねん)、北海道(ほっかいどう)もへ行(い)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 去年(きょねん)、北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>へも</b>行(い)きました。

`へ` shows the direction and cannot be dropped, so `も` goes behind it, not in front.
The rule is that `も` follows every particle except `が`, `を` and `は`, which it
replaces.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ケーキを三(みっ)つ食(た)べました。
(b) ケーキを三(みっ)つ<b>も</b>食(た)べました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain count: three cakes were eaten, no comment attached.

(b) adds `も` to the number, which turns it into an evaluation - "I ate *three whole
cakes*." The speaker is flagging the amount as more than expected, usually with a
touch of guilt or amazement. Nothing about the quantity itself has changed; only the
attitude toward it has.

</details>

# か - or

**Meaning** · joins alternatives - "A or B"

Placed **between** two nouns, `か` offers a choice: either one will do. This is the
everyday Japanese "or", and it is the same syllable as the question particle without
being the same word - position tells them apart.

## Formation

`か` goes between the items, not after the last one. Any case particle the phrase
needs (`を`, `に`, `で`…) is attached to the **whole group**, once, at the end.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two nouns | A か B | コーヒー か お茶(ちゃ) |
| Three or more | A か B か C | 月(げつ) か 火(か) か 水(すい) |
| With a case particle | (A か B) + を・に・で | コーヒーかお茶(ちゃ)を飲(の)む |
| Both items marked | A か B か（、）… | バスか電車(でんしゃ)か、どちらか |
| Numbers / dates | approximation: "…or…" | 三日(みっか)か四日(よっか) |
| Two clauses | plain form + か、plain form | 映画(えいが)を見(み)るか、本(ほん)を読(よ)む |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the case particle rule is the mechanical trap. ❌<code>コーヒーをかお茶(ちゃ)を</code>
is wrong; the `を` is not distributed over the alternatives. Build the choice first,
then mark it: <code>コーヒーかお茶(ちゃ)を飲(の)みます</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒー<b>か</b>お茶(ちゃ)を飲(の)みます。
I'll have coffee or tea.
</pre>

<pre>
ペン<b>か</b>えんぴつ、ありますか。
Do you have a pen or a pencil?
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)<b>か</b>日曜日(にちようび)に会(あ)いましょう。
Let's meet on Saturday or Sunday.
</pre>

<pre>
バス<b>か</b>電車(でんしゃ)<b>か</b>、どちらで行(い)きますか。
Bus or train - which will you take?
</pre>

<pre>
三日(みっか)<b>か</b>四日(よっか)で終(お)わると思(おも)います。
I think it'll be finished in three or four days.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雨(あめ)なら、映画(えいが)を見(み)る<b>か</b>、家(いえ)で本(ほん)を読(よ)みます。
If it rains tomorrow, I'll watch a film or read at home.
</pre>

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みは北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>か</b>沖縄(おきなわ)に行(い)きたいと思(おも)っています。
For the summer holiday I'm thinking of going to Hokkaido or Okinawa.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、受付(うけつけ)は一階(いっかい)<b>か</b>二階(にかい)か、どちらでしょうか。
Excuse me - is reception on the first floor or the second?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`か` is register-neutral. It is the same word in a text message and in a contract,
and nobody will hear it as either casual or stiff.

Marking **both** items - 「AかBか」 - changes the feel slightly. It slows the sentence
down and presents the choice as a genuine, deliberate fork: 「行(い)くか、
行(い)かないか」 = "whether to go or not". A single `か` between two nouns is the
lighter, more offhand version, and it is what you will say most of the time.

Two related shapes to recognise. In relaxed speech, `とか` does the same job with a
looser, "…or something like that" flavour: 「お茶(ちゃ)とかコーヒーとか」. And in
written or formal Japanese, `または` replaces `か` in instructions and forms:
「ペンまたはえんぴつでご記入(きにゅう)ください」.

Note one limit. `か` does not translate every English "or". English uses "or" to
threaten ("hurry, **or** we'll be late") and to correct ("Tuesday - **or** rather,
Wednesday"); Japanese uses other constructions for both, and reaching for `か` there
will not work.

Finally, do not read the choice as exclusive by default. 「コーヒーかお茶(ちゃ)」
offers two options; it does not insist you may not have both. If exclusivity
matters, say so - 「どちらか一(ひと)つ」.

## Contrast

### か vs と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` means **and** (both), `か` means **or** (either). They sit
in exactly the same place, so a single syllable flips the meaning.

</div>

<pre>
パン<b>と</b>牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を買(か)います。
I'll buy bread and milk.
</pre>

<pre>
パン<b>か</b>牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を買(か)います。
I'll buy bread or milk.
</pre>

The first commits you to two items; the second to one, not yet decided. Both take a
single `を` at the end, because in both cases the two nouns form one phrase together.

### か vs それとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か` joins **nouns inside one sentence**; `それとも` joins **two
complete questions**.

</div>

`それとも` is a conjunction, not a particle. It starts a new clause and cannot sit
between bare nouns.

<pre>
お茶(ちゃ)<b>か</b>コーヒーはいかがですか。
Would you like tea or coffee?
</pre>

<pre>
お茶(ちゃ)にしますか。<b>それとも</b>コーヒーにしますか。
Will you have tea? Or would you rather have coffee?
</pre>

The `それとも` version is more deliberate - it lays out two full alternatives and
asks the listener to pick between them, which makes it useful when you want to be
clearly polite about offering a real choice. ❌<code>お茶(ちゃ)それともコーヒー</code> is not
possible.

### か (or) vs か (question particle)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か` **between** nouns means "or"; `か` at the **end** of a
sentence makes a question. Position decides, every time.

</div>

Learners see the same kana and assume ambiguity. There is almost none, because the
two uses never occupy the same slot.

<pre>
紅茶(こうちゃ)<b>か</b>コーヒーを飲(の)みます。
I'll have black tea or coffee.
</pre>

<pre>
紅茶(こうちゃ)を飲(の)みます<b>か</b>。
Will you have some black tea?
</pre>

And both can appear in one sentence without any clash, each doing its own job:
「紅茶(こうちゃ)<b>か</b>コーヒーを飲(の)みます<b>か</b>。」 - "Will you have tea
or coffee?" The first `か` offers the choice; the last one turns the whole thing into
a question.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ コーヒーをかお茶(ちゃ)を飲(の)みます。 → ✅ コーヒーかお茶(ちゃ)を飲(の)みます。
  The case particle marks the whole choice once, at the end. It is not repeated on
  each alternative.

- ❌ パンかたまごを買(か)いました。（when you bought both） → ✅ パンとたまごを買(か)いました。
  `か` is "or". If you took both items home, the word you want is `と`.

- ❌ お茶(ちゃ)それともコーヒーがいいです。 → ✅ お茶(ちゃ)かコーヒーがいいです。
  `それとも` links two whole questions, not two nouns.

- ❌ 明日(あした)か行(い)きます。 → ✅ 明日(あした)か明後日(あさって)行(い)きます。
  `か` needs something on **both** sides. A single item with a trailing `か` at the
  end of a noun phrase is not an alternative - it will be read as a question particle
  or as "something like".

## Practice

**1.** 昼(ひる)ごはんはうどん____そばにします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

うどん<b>か</b>そばにします。

Two nouns offered as alternatives take `か` between them, and the following
particle - here `に` - attaches once, after the whole pair.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会議(かいぎ)は月曜日(げつようび)（と・か）火曜日(かようび)です。まだ決(き)まっていません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

月曜日(げつようび)<b>か</b>火曜日(かようび)です。

The second sentence says it hasn't been decided, so only one of the two days will
happen. That is "or", which is `か`. `と` would claim the meeting runs on both days.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please write your name in pen or pencil."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ペン<b>か</b>えんぴつでお名前(なまえ)を書(か)いてください。

The choice is built first, then marked once with `で` for the instrument. In a
printed form you would meet the written equivalent, `または`, in the same slot.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 飲(の)み物(もの)はビールをかワインをお願(ねが)いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 飲(の)み物(もの)はビールかワインをお願(ねが)いします。

`を` cannot be attached to each alternative. Join the nouns with `か` first, and let
the single `を` cover the resulting phrase.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) バスか電車(でんしゃ)で行(い)きます。
(b) バスか電車(でんしゃ)か、どちらかで行(い)きます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say you will travel by one of the two. (a) is light and offhand - you have not
thought about it much, and either is fine.

(b) marks both options and then names the choice explicitly with <code>どちらか</code>, which
slows the sentence down and presents the decision as a real, still-open fork. It is
what you would say when the choice actually matters, or when you are being careful
in front of someone.

</details>

# か - question particle

**Meaning** · turns a statement into a question

Put `か` at the end of a sentence and it becomes a question. Nothing else moves - 
no word order changes, no auxiliary appears. This is the same syllable as the "or"
particle, but at the end of the sentence rather than between two nouns.

## Formation

`か` is the last thing in the sentence, after the predicate is already complete.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ます-form verb | ～ます + か | 行(い)きます → 行(い)きますか |
| Noun + です | Noun + ですか | 学生(がくせい)です → 学生(がくせい)ですか |
| い-adjective + です | ～いです + か | 高(たか)いです → 高(たか)いですか |
| Past / negative | ～ましたか・～ませんか | 来(き)ました → 来(き)ましたか |
| Plain form | plain + か (blunt or written) | 行(い)く → 行(い)くか |
| Question word sentences | question word … + か | どこ → どこですか |
| Inside a sentence | clause + か + わかりません etc. | 何時(なんじ)に来(く)るか、わかりません |
| Punctuation | normally 。 - the か already asks | 行(い)きますか。 |

<div class="note">

**Note** - after a noun in the **plain** form, the `だ` drops before `か`:
<code>学生(がくせい)か</code>, never ❌<code>学生(がくせい)だか</code>. In the polite form there is no such
problem, because `です` stays: <code>学生(がくせい)ですか</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
お名前(なまえ)は何(なん)です<b>か</b>。
What's your name?
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)来(き)ます<b>か</b>。
Are you coming tomorrow?
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、コピー機(き)はどこです<b>か</b>。
Excuse me, where's the photocopier?
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の試合(しあい)を見(み)ました<b>か</b>。
Did you watch yesterday's match?
</pre>

<pre>
一緒(いっしょ)にお昼(ひる)を食(た)べません<b>か</b>。
Would you like to have lunch with me?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が何時(なんじ)に終(お)わる<b>か</b>、まだわかりません。
I still don't know what time the meeting ends.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、この席(せき)に座(すわ)ってもいいです<b>か</b>。
Excuse me, may I sit here?
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)っています<b>か</b>。午後(ごご)から降(ふ)るそうですよ。
Do you have an umbrella with you? Apparently it's going to rain from the afternoon.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`か` belongs with polite Japanese. Attached to `です` / `ます` it is simply the normal
way to ask anything, and it carries no attitude at all.

Attached to a **plain** form it is a different animal. 「行(い)くか」 sounds
blunt, brusque, and distinctly masculine - a boss barking at a subordinate, or a
character in a film. Ordinary casual questions do not use `か` at all: you take the
plain form and raise your pitch at the end.

- 「行(い)く?」 - "You going?"
- 「これ、食(た)べる?」 - "You want this?"

That is the single most useful register fact on this page. A learner who says
「食(た)べるか?」 to a friend sounds aggressive; 「食(た)べる?」 is warm and
normal. In writing, plain form + `か` is fine and neutral - it is the everyday shape
of a question in a book or a report, where nobody is being addressed.

Plain `か` also survives in a set of self-directed expressions that everybody uses
regardless of gender: 「そうか」("ah, I see"), 「本当(ほんとう)か」, and the polite
「そうですか」. Watch the intonation on that last one. Rising 「そうですか?」 is a real
question - "is that so?". Falling 「そうですか。」 is not a question at all; it is
"I see", an acknowledgement. Beginners produce the falling version by accident and
puzzle their listener.

Two more things worth knowing. `か` is normally followed by `。`, not `?`, in careful
writing - the particle already marks the question. And a **negative** question,
`～ませんか`, is not really a question about ability or fact: it is an invitation.
「見(み)ませんか」 = "won't you watch it with me?", not "do you not watch it?".

## Contrast

### か vs rising intonation

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - polite speech asks with `か`. Casual speech asks by raising the
pitch and adding nothing.

</div>

<pre>
コーヒーを飲(の)みます<b>か</b>。
Would you like some coffee?
</pre>

<pre>
コーヒー、飲(の)む<b>?</b>
Want some coffee?
</pre>

Both are questions and neither is wrong, but they are for different people. The
first is what you say to a client; the second to a friend. Fusing them - 
❌「コーヒーを飲(の)むか?」 - produces the rough, confrontational register mentioned
above, which is very rarely what a learner intends.

### ～ますか vs ～ませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ますか` asks for information. `～ませんか` offers an invitation.

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)、映画(えいが)を見(み)ます<b>か</b>。
Do you watch films at the weekend?
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)、映画(えいが)を見(み)ません<b>か</b>。
How about seeing a film at the weekend?
</pre>

The negative shape is doing politeness work, not negation: by phrasing the offer so
that "no" is the easy answer, you leave the listener room to refuse. English does the
same thing with "you wouldn't want to…, would you?" A step further along is
`～ましょうか`, which offers to do something *for* the listener:
「手伝(てつだ)いましょうか」 = "shall I give you a hand?"

### か vs の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か` is the neutral polite question. Sentence-final `の` is the
casual question that asks for an *explanation*.

</div>

<pre>
どうして来(き)ませんでした<b>か</b>。
Why didn't you come?
</pre>

<pre>
どうして来(こ)なかった<b>の</b>?
How come you didn't come?
</pre>

`の` builds on the explanatory `のだ` / `んです`, so it asks about the reason or the
situation behind something rather than the bare fact. It is soft and personal - used
a great deal with children and between friends, and slightly more by women than by
men in its bare form. Its polite counterpart is `んですか`:
「どうして来(こ)なかったんですか」, which is gentler than a flat `ませんでしたか` and
is often the better choice when a direct `か` question would feel like an
interrogation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あの人(ひと)は学生(がくせい)だか。 → ✅ あの人(ひと)は学生(がくせい)ですか。／学生(がくせい)か。
  In the polite form the copula is `です`; in the plain form the `だ` drops out
  entirely before `か`.

- ❌ 「学生(がくせい)ですか。」「はい、そうですか。」 → ✅ 「はい、そうです。」
  Falling 「そうですか」 means "I see" - it is what the *listener* says. Answering with
  it tells your questioner you have just learned your own occupation.

- ❌ どこか行(い)きますか。（meaning "where are you going?"） → ✅ どこへ行(い)きますか。
  <code>どこか</code> is one word meaning "somewhere". The question word needs its ordinary
  particle instead.

- ❌ 友(とも)だちに「食(た)べるか?」と聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 「食(た)べる?」と聞(き)きました。
  Plain form + `か` addressed at a person is rough. Casual questions drop the
  particle and rely on intonation.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、駅(えき)はどちら____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)はどちらです<b>か</b>。

The sentence ends in a noun predicate, so the polite copula `です` comes first and
`か` follows it to make the question. Asking a stranger, the polite form is the only
sensible choice.

</details>

**2.** Choose the more inviting version: 一緒(いっしょ)に（帰(かえ)りますか・帰(かえ)りませんか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一緒(いっしょ)に<b>帰(かえ)りませんか</b>。

The negative question is Japanese's standard invitation. It leaves refusing easy, so
it sounds considerate rather than pushy. <code>帰(かえ)りますか</code> would just be asking
whether the person intends to go home.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "Is this seat free?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この席(せき)は空(あ)いています<b>か</b>。

The predicate is a verb in polite form, so `か` simply attaches to `います`. No word
order changes and no extra word is needed to make it a question.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 友(とも)だちに「明日(あした)来(く)るか?」と聞(き)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 友(とも)だちに「明日(あした)来(く)る?」と聞(き)きました。

Plain form plus `か` aimed at a listener sounds harsh and confrontational. Between
friends the particle is dropped and the question is carried by rising intonation
alone.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) どうして会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みましたか。
(b) どうして会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)んだんですか。

Both are polite and both ask why the person missed work. (a) is a bare request for a
fact, and coming from a superior it can feel like being called in for questioning.

(b) uses the explanatory `んです`, which frames the question as "what's the story
behind it?" - it acknowledges there is a situation and invites the person to explain
it. That makes it warmer and more usual in real conversation, especially when the
answer might be personal.
