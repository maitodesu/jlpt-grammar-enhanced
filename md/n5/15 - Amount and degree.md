# N5 - Amount and degree


---

# くらい / ぐらい - about; approximately

**Meaning** · about, roughly - stuck onto an amount to say you are not being exact

`くらい` takes a number and loosens it. 三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ぐらい is "about half
an hour", 千円(せんえん)ぐらい is "a thousand yen or so", これくらい is "about this
much". Whatever it attaches to has to be a **quantity or an extent** - a length of
time, a price, a headcount, a size - never a point on the clock or the calendar.

## Formation

`くらい` and `ぐらい` are the same word, and nothing about what comes in front
decides which one you say. It is a suffix: it goes straight onto the amount, with
nothing in between.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Number + counter | Amount + くらい／ぐらい | 三十分(さんじゅっぷん) → 三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ぐらい |
| People | Number + 人(にん) + ぐらい | 十五人(じゅうごにん) → 十五人(じゅうごにん)ぐらい |
| Money | Price + ぐらい | 千円(せんえん) → 千円(せんえん)ぐらい |
| Amount word | Noun + くらい | 半分(はんぶん) → 半分(はんぶん)くらい |
| Demonstrative | これ／それ／あれ + くらい | これ → これくらい |
| Modifying a noun | ～くらいの + Noun | これ → これぐらいの大(おお)きさ |
| Asking an amount | どのくらい／どれくらい | どのくらいかかりますか |
| Followed by a particle | ～くらい + で／から／まで | 五分(ごふん) → 五分(ごふん)ぐらいで |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `くらい` answers **how much? how long? how many?** If the phrase
answers **when?**, you need `ごろ` instead: <code>三時間(さんじかん)ぐらい</code>
("about three hours") but <code>三時(さんじ)ごろ</code> ("around three o'clock").

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a quantity with `くらい` on it usually needs no particle at all. You say
<code>ビールを三本(さんぼん)ぐらい飲(の)みました</code>, not
❌<code>ビールを三本(さんぼん)ぐらいを飲(の)みました</code> - the amount sits in
front of the verb as an adverb, and `を` is already doing its work on ビール.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒーを三杯(さんばい)<b>ぐらい</b>飲(の)みました。
I drank about three cups of coffee.
</pre>

<pre>
この本(ほん)は千円(せんえん)<b>ぐらい</b>です。
This book is about a thousand yen.
</pre>

<pre>
教室(きょうしつ)に学生(がくせい)が十五人(じゅうごにん)<b>ぐらい</b>います。
There are about fifteen students in the classroom.
</pre>

<pre>
空港(くうこう)まで、どの<b>くらい</b>かかりますか。
How long does it take to get to the airport?
</pre>

<pre>
これ<b>ぐらい</b>の大(おお)きさの箱(はこ)はありますか。
Do you have a box about this big?
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)は毎日(まいにち)二時間(にじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>ピアノを練習(れんしゅう)します。
My older sister practises the piano for about two hours a day.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、あと十分(じゅっぷん)<b>ぐらい</b>で着(つ)きますので、先(さき)に注文(ちゅうもん)しておいてください。
Sorry - I'll be there in about ten minutes, so go ahead and order without me.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)のラーメンは、昼(ひる)に行(い)くと二十分(にじゅっぷん)<b>ぐらい</b>並(なら)ばなければなりません。
If you go to this ramen place at lunchtime you have to queue for twenty minutes or so.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`くらい` is completely register-neutral. It is the same in casual speech and in a
business email, it is not marked as male or female, and adding it to a number never
makes you sound less serious - if anything the opposite, because Japanese
conversation prefers a hedged number to a bare one. Answering
<code>どのくらいかかりますか</code> with a flat
<code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)です</code> claims a precision you probably do not
have; <code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ぐらいです</code> is what a native speaker says.

**`くらい` and `ぐらい` are the same word.** They mean the same thing, both are
correct everywhere, and no listener will notice which one you chose. The variation
is distributional rather than semantic: `ぐらい` is somewhat more common in speech
and especially after the demonstratives - これぐらい, どれぐらい - while `くらい`
turns up a little more often in careful writing. Those are tendencies, not rules.
Pick whichever comes out of your mouth, and stay consistent inside a sentence.

In writing, and in announcements read aloud, you will also meet
<code>約(やく)</code>, which does the same job from the other side of the number.
It is treated in the Contrast section below.

One thing to expect later: the same word has a second job, measuring a **degree**
rather than an amount - <code>泣(な)きたいくらいうれしい</code>, "so happy I could
cry" - along with a dismissive use that belittles a small task. That sense is a
separate grammar point at N3. If you meet a `くらい` that is not sitting on a number,
it is that other job, not this one.

## Contrast

### くらい／ぐらい vs ごろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ごろ` blurs a **point in time**; `くらい` blurs an **amount**.
One character in the counter decides it: <code>六時(ろくじ)</code> is a when,
<code>六時間(ろくじかん)</code> is a how-long.

</div>

<pre>
夕方(ゆうがた)六時(ろくじ)<b>ごろ</b>帰(かえ)ります。
I'll be home around six in the evening.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は六時間(ろくじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>寝(ね)ました。
I slept about six hours last night.
</pre>

Dates and days of the week are points in time too, so they take `ごろ`:
<code>来週(らいしゅう)ごろ</code>, <code>三月(さんがつ)ごろ</code>. Anything that
is counted rather than located - money, distance, weight, people, pages - can only
take `くらい`: ❌<code>五人(ごにん)ごろ</code> is not Japanese.

### くらい vs 約(やく)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, opposite position, different register.
<code>約(やく)</code> is a prefix and belongs to **written** Japanese; `ぐらい` is
a suffix and belongs to **speech**. Do not put both on one number.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)まで<b>約(やく)</b>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)かかります。
It is approximately thirty minutes to the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)まで三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>ぐらい</b>かかります。
It takes about half an hour to get to the venue.
</pre>

The first belongs on a printed leaflet or in a station announcement; the second is
what you say to a friend on the phone. <code>約(やく)</code> only ever attaches to
a number, so there is no ❌<code>約(やく)これ</code> to match これぐらい, and
stacking the two - ❌<code>約(やく)三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ぐらい</code> - hedges
the same number twice.

### Amount くらい vs degree くらい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if `くらい` sits on a **number or an amount**, it means "about".
If it sits on a **verb or an adjective**, it is measuring a degree, and "about" is
the wrong translation.

</div>

<pre>
三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>ぐらい</b>待(ま)ちました。
I waited about thirty minutes.
</pre>

<pre>
立(た)てない<b>ぐらい</b>疲(つか)れました。
I got so tired I couldn't stand up.
</pre>

The second sentence has no number in it at all: the clause
<code>立(た)てない</code> is being used as a yardstick for how tired the speaker
was. That degree use, together with a dismissive "it's only ～" reading, is a
separate grammar point at N3. Here you only need to recognise the shape and not
translate it as "approximately".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 二週間(にしゅうかん)<b>ごろ</b>休(やす)みます。 → ✅ 二週間(にしゅうかん)<b>ぐらい</b>休(やす)みます。
  <code>二週間(にしゅうかん)</code> is a length of time, not a date. Only a point
  in time takes `ごろ`.

- ❌ <b>ぐらい</b>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)かかります。 → ✅ 三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>ぐらい</b>かかります。
  English puts "about" in front of the number; Japanese puts `ぐらい` behind it.
  `くらい` is a suffix and can never lead.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は三時間(さんじかん)ぐらい<b>に</b>勉強(べんきょう)しました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は三時間(さんじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>勉強(べんきょう)しました。
  A duration goes in front of the verb with no particle. The `に` of
  <code>三時(さんじ)に</code> marks a point in time, and a span has no point to
  mark.

- ❌ 約(やく)五千円(ごせんえん)<b>ぐらい</b>でした。 → ✅ 約(やく)五千円(ごせんえん)でした。／五千円(ごせんえん)<b>ぐらい</b>でした。
  Both words already mean "about". Use one of them, and let the register decide
  which.

## Practice

**1.** 「駅(えき)から会社(かいしゃ)まで、どの____かかりますか。」「歩(ある)いて十五分(じゅうごふん)____です。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「どの<b>くらい</b>かかりますか。」「十五分(じゅうごふん)<b>ぐらい</b>です。」

Both blanks are about a **length** of time, so both take `くらい`／`ぐらい`. Note how
the hedged answer is the natural one: a bare
<code>十五分(じゅうごふん)です</code> would sound as though you had timed it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)は九時(くじ)（ごろ・ぐらい）会社(かいしゃ)に着(つ)いて、一時間(いちじかん)（ごろ・ぐらい）準備(じゅんび)をします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

九時(くじ)<b>ごろ</b>会社(かいしゃ)に着(つ)いて、一時間(いちじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>準備(じゅんび)をします。

<code>九時(くじ)</code> is a clock time - a point - so it takes `ごろ`.
<code>一時間(いちじかん)</code> is a span of time, so it takes `ぐらい`. The two
counters differ by one character and choose different words.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "About twenty people came to the party."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

パーティーに二十人(にじゅうにん)<b>ぐらい</b>来(き)ました。

The amount goes in front of the verb with `ぐらい` attached and no particle after
it. <code>二十人(にじゅうにん)くらい</code> is equally correct - the two forms are
the same word.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 土曜日(どようび)ぐらいに電話(でんわ)をします。話(はなし)は十分(じゅっぷん)ごろで終(お)わると思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 土曜日(どようび)<b>ごろ</b>電話(でんわ)をします。話(はなし)は十分(じゅっぷん)<b>ぐらい</b>で終(お)わると思(おも)います。

The two words have swapped places. A day of the week is a point in time and needs
`ごろ`; ten minutes is a span and needs `ぐらい`. Note that `に` also drops away once
`ごろ` is there.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference:
(a) この町(まち)に十年(じゅうねん)<b>ぐらい</b>住(す)んでいます。
(b) この町(まち)に十年前(じゅうねんまえ)<b>ごろ</b>引(ひ)っ越(こ)してきました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) measures **how long** the speaker has lived here: roughly ten years, and they
are still here. <code>十年(じゅうねん)</code> is a duration, so `ぐらい` is the
only choice.

(b) locates **when** they arrived: at some point around ten years ago, as a single
completed event. <code>十年前(じゅうねんまえ)</code> is a moment on the calendar,
which is `ごろ` territory. The same number, doing two different jobs.

</details>

# だけ

**Meaning** · only; just - this much and nothing more

`だけ` draws a line around something and says that everything outside the line is
excluded. It is a neutral limiter: it reports the boundary without complaining
about it and without apologising for it.

## Formation

`だけ` is a particle-like suffix. It goes **after** the word it limits, and the
word keeps its own plain form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だけ | 水(みず) → 水(みず)だけ |
| Number / counter | Counter + だけ | 三人(さんにん) → 三人(さんにん)だけ |
| Verb | plain form + だけ | 見(み)る → 見(み)るだけ |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけ | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いだけ |
| な-adjective | stem + な + だけ | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かなだけ |
| After a particle | に／で／と + だけ | 友(とも)だちにだけ／友(とも)だちだけに |
| Sentence-final | だけ + です／だ | 見(み)るだけです |

`が` and `を` are **dropped** after だけ, or moved behind it
（水(みず)だけ飲(の)む／水(みず)だけを飲(の)む）. `に`, `で`, `と`, `へ`
are kept, on either side of だけ.

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒーを一杯(いっぱい)<b>だけ</b>飲(の)みました。
I had just one cup of coffee.
</pre>

<pre>
うちには猫(ねこ)が一匹(いっぴき)<b>だけ</b>います。
We have only one cat.
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)<b>だけ</b>アルバイトをしています。
I only work my part-time job on Saturdays.
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>だけ</b>待(ま)ってください。
Please wait just a moment.
</pre>

<pre>
これは見(み)る<b>だけ</b>で、買(か)いません。
I'm only looking - I'm not going to buy anything.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)をここに書(か)く<b>だけ</b>でいいですよ。
All you have to do is write your name here.
</pre>

<pre>
その店(みせ)は高(たか)い<b>だけ</b>で、あまりおいしくありませんでした。
That place was just expensive - it wasn't even very good.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)に残(のこ)っていたのは私(わたし)<b>だけ</b>でしたから、電気(でんき)を消(け)して帰(かえ)りました。
I was the only one left in the room, so I turned off the lights and went home.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. `だけです` in polite speech, `だけだ` or a bare `だけ` in plain
speech, and nothing about it is marked for age or gender.

The important feeling is that `だけ` is **factual, not emotional**. It states the
limit and stops. If you want the limit to sound *insufficient* - "only three
people came, unfortunately" - Japanese reaches for <code>しか～ない</code> instead, which
is the same fact wrapped in disappointment.

Two very common frames are worth memorising whole:

- <code>～だけで</code> - "just with / merely by". 名前(なまえ)を書(か)くだけでいいです
  means the writing is all that is required.
- <code>これだけ</code>／<code>それだけ</code> - depending on the sentence these mean either
  "only this" or, with rising emphasis, "*this* much". これだけ勉強(べんきょう)したのに is
  "after studying *this* much…". Context decides.

In speech `だけ` often carries a small dismissive shrug when it follows an
adjective: 高(たか)いだけ, 大(おお)きいだけ - "expensive and that's all it has
going for it".

## Contrast

### だけ vs しか～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same fact, opposite framing. `だけ` is neutral; <code>しか～ない</code>
says the amount is *less than you'd want*, and forces the verb negative.

</div>

<pre>
千円(せんえん)<b>だけ</b>持(も)っています。
I have a thousand yen. (that's the amount, stated flatly)
</pre>

<pre>
千円(せんえん)しか持(も)っていません。
I've only got a thousand yen. (and it isn't going to be enough)
</pre>

Note the verb: `だけ` leaves it positive, `しか` requires the negative. Mixing them
up - ❌千円(せんえん)しか持(も)っています - is the single most common error with
this pair.

### だけ vs ばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけ` counts; `ばかり` complains. `ばかり` means "nothing but",
and usually implies the speaker thinks that is too much of one thing.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はゲーム<b>だけ</b>好(す)きです。
Games are the one thing my little brother likes.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はゲームばかりしています。
My little brother does nothing but play games.
</pre>

`ばかり` (an N4 pattern) does not really restrict the set - other things may
happen - it says one thing dominates, and it usually sounds critical.

### だけ vs のみ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `のみ` is the written,
formal twin of `だけ` and sounds stiff in conversation.

</div>

<pre>
このドアは関係者(かんけいしゃ)<b>だけ</b>使(つか)えます。
Only staff can use this door.
</pre>

<pre>
関係者(かんけいしゃ)のみ入場(にゅうじょう)できます。
Staff only. (as printed on a sign)
</pre>

You will meet `のみ` on notices, in contracts and in announcements. Say `だけ` when
you are speaking to a person.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ りんごを<b>だけ</b>買(か)いました。 → ✅ りんご<b>だけ</b>買(か)いました。／りんご<b>だけを</b>買(か)いました。
  `を` cannot sit in front of `だけ`. Either drop it or move it behind. The same
  goes for `が`.

- ❌ 水(みず)<b>だけ</b>飲(の)みません。（meaning "I only drink water"）→ ✅ 水(みず)<b>だけ</b>飲(の)みます。／水(みず)しか飲(の)みません。
  `だけ` plus a negative negates the limit itself: "it isn't only water that I
  drink." If you want the restriction to stand, keep the verb positive or switch
  to `しか`.

- ❌ 静(しず)かだ<b>だけ</b>の町(まち) → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>だけの町(まち)
  な-adjectives take `な` before `だけ`, exactly as they do before a noun.

- ❌ テレビを見(み)ます<b>だけ</b>です。 → ✅ テレビを見(み)る<b>だけ</b>です。
  Politeness is carried by the final `です`. The verb in front of `だけ` stays in
  its plain dictionary form.

## Practice

**1.** ケーキを一(ひと)つ____食(た)べました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ケーキを一(ひと)つ<b>だけ</b>食(た)べました。

`だけ` follows the counter directly, and the `を` on ケーキ is unaffected because
it belongs to a different word. "I ate just one piece of cake."

</details>

**2.** Choose: 時間(じかん)が十分(じゅっぷん)（だけ・しか）ありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

時間(じかん)が十分(じゅっぷん)<b>しか</b>ありません。

The verb is already negative (`ありません`), and only `しか` licenses that. With
`だけ` you would have to write 十分(じゅっぷん)だけあります - same ten minutes,
but reported as a plain fact rather than as "only ten minutes left".

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I only studied for thirty minutes yesterday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>だけ</b>勉強(べんきょう)しました。

The limiter goes on the amount of time, not on the verb. If you want it to sound
like an admission - *only* thirty minutes, which isn't enough - 
三十分(さんじゅっぷん)しか勉強(べんきょう)しませんでした does that job.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 私(わたし)は野菜(やさい)をだけ食(た)べます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 私(わたし)は野菜(やさい)<b>だけ</b>食(た)べます。

`を` may not precede `だけ`. The natural fix is to delete it;
野菜(やさい)だけを食(た)べます is also grammatical and puts a little extra
weight on the restriction.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 三人(さんにん)だけ来(き)ました。
(b) 三人(さんにん)しか来(き)ませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports the number: three people, no more. A host counting chairs
would say it. There is no attitude attached.

(b) says three was disappointing. The speaker had expected more, and the negative
verb carries that shortfall. Same three people, but (b) tells you how the speaker
feels about them.

</details>

# ～すぎる

**Meaning** · too much; excessively - past the point where it is still all right

`～すぎる` is **not** an intensifier. 高(たか)すぎる does not mean "very
expensive"; it means the price has crossed a line and become a problem. Whenever
you attach it, you are passing judgement: this is more than it should be.

## Formation

Cut the word back to its stem, then add `すぎる`. `すぎる` itself is a regular
る-verb: すぎます, すぎない, すぎた, すぎて.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + すぎる | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)み → 飲(の)みすぎる |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + すぎる | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べ → 食(た)べすぎる |
| Verb (する／来(く)る) | し／来(き) + すぎる | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)しすぎる |
| Verb (negative) | ない → なさ + すぎる | 食(た)べない → 食(た)べなさすぎる |
| い-adjective | drop い + すぎる | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)すぎる |
| な-adjective | stem + すぎる | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かすぎる |
| いい／よい | よ + すぎる（irregular） | いい → よすぎる |
| ない | な<b>さ</b> + すぎる | ない → なさすぎる |
| Noun form | すぎ | 飲(の)みすぎ, 働(はたら)きすぎ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - whatever goes in front of `すぎる` is the same shape you would
use in front of `ます` (for verbs) or in front of `～くない` (for adjectives, minus
the く). If you can still see an い or a だ, you have cut too little.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
このかばんは<b>高(たか)すぎます</b>。
This bag is too expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)のパーティーで<b>飲(の)みすぎました</b>。
I drank too much at the party yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
このスープは私(わたし)には<b>からすぎる</b>。
This soup is too spicy for me.
</pre>

<pre>
ごはんを<b>食(た)べすぎて</b>、おなかが痛(いた)いです。
I ate too much and now my stomach hurts.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は<b>静(しず)かすぎて</b>、なんだかおちつきません。
This room is too quiet - it makes me uneasy somehow.
</pre>

<pre>
このシャツは息子(むすこ)には少(すこ)し<b>大(おお)きすぎました</b>。
This shirt turned out a bit too big for my son.
</pre>

<pre>
テレビを<b>見(み)すぎる</b>と、目(め)が悪(わる)くなりますよ。
If you watch too much television, your eyes will suffer.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は一年生(いちねんせい)には<b>難(むずか)しすぎる</b>ので、もっとやさしいものに変(か)えましょう。
This question is too hard for first-years, so let's swap it for an easier one.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself - <code>高(たか)すぎます</code> is as polite as the `ます` on
the end makes it - but the *content* is always a criticism, so it can land harder
than you intend. Softening it with `ちょっと` or <code>少(すこ)し</code> is extremely common:
ちょっと高(たか)すぎますね sounds like a negotiation, while
高(たか)すぎます sounds like a verdict.

The `すぎ` noun form appears constantly in everyday health talk:
飲(の)みすぎ, 食(た)べすぎ, 働(はたら)きすぎ, 寝(ね)すぎ - often on its own
as a diagnosis. 昨日(きのう)は飲(の)みすぎだ。

One modern exception to the "always negative" rule: in casual speech, especially
among younger speakers, `すぎる` is used as pure hyperbole for praise - 
かわいすぎる, おいしすぎる, すごすぎる. This is slangy, it is written with a lot of
exclamation, and it does not belong in a polite conversation or in writing. Learn
to recognise it; do not build sentences on it yet.

Note also that `すぎる` is a real verb in its own right meaning "to pass by"
（時間(じかん)が過(す)ぎる, 駅(えき)を通(とお)り過(す)ぎる）. Compounds are
usually written in kana, the standalone verb in kanji.

## Contrast

### ～すぎる vs とても／すごく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とても` says the quality is high. `すぎる` says the quality has
gone wrong. Praise never uses `すぎる`.

</div>

<pre>
このコーヒーはとても熱(あつ)いです。
This coffee is very hot. (nice and hot)
</pre>

<pre>
このコーヒーは<b>熱(あつ)すぎます</b>。
This coffee is too hot. (I can't drink it)
</pre>

So a compliment must not take `すぎる` in careful speech: if you tell a cook
❌ この料理(りょうり)はおいしすぎます in a formal setting, the literal reading is
that the dish is unreasonably rich. Say とてもおいしいです.

### ～すぎる vs ～すぎ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すぎる` is a verb and conjugates; `すぎ` is a noun and takes
`だ／です`.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は<b>働(はたら)きすぎました</b>。
I worked too much yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
働(はたら)きすぎですよ。少(すこ)し休(やす)んでください。
You're overworking. Take a break.
</pre>

The noun form is what you use to label a situation rather than narrate an action,
and it is the form that appears in set warnings like
食(た)べすぎに注意(ちゅうい) - "don't overeat".

### ～すぎる vs the standalone verb すぎる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - attached to a stem, it means "too much". Standing alone with
its own particle, the same verb means "to go past".

</div>

<pre>
三時(さんじ)を過(す)ぎました。
It's gone three o'clock.
</pre>

<pre>
おかしを<b>食(た)べすぎました</b>。
I ate too many sweets.
</pre>

In the first sentence `を` marks the point being passed and the verb is the main
verb of the sentence. In the second, `すぎる` has no object of its own - it is
riding on 食(た)べ. If you can find a `を` in front of it, it is the "pass by"
verb.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べるすぎる → ✅ <b>食(た)べすぎる</b>
  Verbs attach at the ます-stem, never at the dictionary form. Ask yourself what
  goes before `ます`: 食(た)べます → 食(た)べ.

- ❌ 高(たか)いすぎる → ✅ <b>高(たか)すぎる</b>
  い-adjectives drop the final い first. The same cut you make for
  <code>高(たか)くない</code> or <code>高(たか)かった</code>.

- ❌ 静(しず)かなすぎる → ✅ <b>静(しず)かすぎる</b>
  な-adjectives use the bare stem. No `な`, and no `だ`.

- ❌ いいすぎる（meaning "too good"）→ ✅ <b>よすぎる</b>
  `いい` becomes `よ-` in every conjugation, and this one is no exception. Careful:
  言(い)いすぎる is a real and different word - "to say too much, to go too far".

- ❌ 野菜(やさい)を食(た)べないすぎる → ✅ 野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べなさすぎる</b>
  Negatives insert `さ` before `すぎる`, because they behave like adjectives - 
  the same `さ` you see in なさすぎる.

## Practice

**1.** この靴(くつ)は小(ちい)____すぎます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この靴(くつ)は小(ちい)<b>さ</b>すぎます。

<code>小(ちい)さい</code> is an い-adjective, so you remove only the final い and keep
the さ: 小(ちい)さい → 小(ちい)さ → 小(ちい)さすぎます. Learners often cut
back to 小(ちい) by mistake.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)の映画(えいが)は（とてもおもしろかった・おもしろすぎた）です。 You loved it and want to recommend it.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)の映画(えいが)は<b>とてもおもしろかった</b>です。

`すぎる` is a complaint. おもしろすぎた would suggest the film was somehow
excessive - overwhelming, more than you wanted. To recommend something, use
`とても` or `すごく`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I got up too late this morning."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今朝(けさ)は<b>起(お)きるのが遅(おそ)すぎました</b>。
（or simply: 今朝(けさ)は<b>寝(ね)すぎました</b>。）

Either the adjective takes `すぎる`（遅(おそ)い → 遅(おそ)すぎる）or you put it
on the verb that caused the problem. <code>寝(ね)すぎる</code> - "to oversleep" - is what
a Japanese speaker would actually say.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: このお茶(ちゃ)はあついいすぎます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ このお茶(ちゃ)は<b>あつすぎます</b>。

The writer has attached `すぎる` without cutting the adjective, and doubled the
い along the way. あつい → あつ → あつすぎます. Only the final い goes.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この町(まち)はとてもしずかです。
(b) この町(まち)はしずかすぎます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a compliment, or at worst a neutral description. Quiet is being presented
as a feature of the town.

(b) says the quiet has become a problem - nothing is open, nobody is about, the
speaker is unsettled by it. The `すぎる` is what converts a pleasant quality into
a complaint, and that conversion happens whatever the adjective is.

</details>

# ～と同じ

**Meaning** · the same as ～ - identical to something else, not merely similar

<code>同(おな)じ</code> is the word for sameness, and `と` is the particle that names
what the sameness is measured against. English says "the same **as**"; Japanese
says "<b>と</b> the same". Get that particle wrong and the sentence falls apart.

## Formation

<code>同(おな)じ</code> is a strange word: it behaves like a な-adjective everywhere
*except* directly in front of a noun, where the `な` disappears.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (predicate) | Noun + と同(おな)じ + です／だ | 私(わたし)と同(おな)じです |
| Noun (modifying) | Noun + と同(おな)じ + Noun | 私(わたし)と同(おな)じクラス |
| Negative | と同(おな)じ + じゃない／ではありません | 同(おな)じじゃありません |
| Past | と同(おな)じ + でした／だった | 同(おな)じでした |
| Degree | Noun + と同(おな)じくらい + adjective | 私(わたし)と同(おな)じくらい高(たか)い |
| Manner | Noun + と同(おな)じように + verb | 前(まえ)と同(おな)じように作(つく)る |
| Two things listed | AとBは同(おな)じ | このかばんとあのかばんは同(おな)じです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `な` before a noun. ✅同(おな)じ学校(がっこう) ／
❌同(おな)じな学校(がっこう). This one word is the exception that every
learner trips over, because 静(しず)かな学校(がっこう) and
有名(ゆうめい)な学校(がっこう) behave normally.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
このかばんは私(わたし)の<b>と同(おな)じ</b>です。
This bag is the same as mine.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは私(わたし)<b>と同(おな)じ</b>年(とし)です。
Tanaka is the same age as me.
</pre>

<pre>
この色(いろ)はあの色(いろ)<b>と同(おな)じ</b>じゃありません。
This colour isn't the same as that one.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は兄(あに)<b>と同(おな)じ</b>学校(がっこう)に通(かよ)っています。
My younger brother goes to the same school as my older brother.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は私(わたし)<b>と同(おな)じ</b>くらい背(せ)が高(たか)いです。
My little sister is about as tall as I am.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)<b>と同(おな)じ</b>ホテルに泊(と)まりました。
We stayed at the same hotel as last year.
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)、父(ちち)<b>と同(おな)じ</b>電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)って会社(かいしゃ)へ行(い)きます。
Every morning I take the same train as my father to the office.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)説明(せつめい)したこと<b>と同(おな)じ</b>ことを、もう一度(いちど)書(か)いておきます。
I'll write down the same thing I explained yesterday, one more time.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and just as common in speech as in writing.

The `と` may be dropped when the two items are listed together as a pair:
このペンとそのペンは同(おな)じです。 Here the `と` you can see is the "and"
particle joining the two pens, and <code>同(おな)じ</code> stands alone as the
predicate. Both patterns are correct - what is *not* correct is having neither.

Watch the `の` when you compare possessions. <code>私(わたし)と同(おな)じ</code>
compares the other thing to **me**, the person. <code>私(わたし)のと同(おな)じ</code>
compares it to **mine**, the object. English hides this distinction; Japanese does
not, and getting it wrong produces sentences like "this bag is the same as me."

Two fixed extensions are worth learning as units:

- <code>同(おな)じくらい</code> - "about the same amount / to the same degree". This is
  how you say "as … as" for adjectives.
- <code>同(おな)じように</code> - "in the same way". It modifies a verb, not a noun.

In writing you may also meet <code>同様(どうよう)に</code> and <code>同(おな)じく</code>, both
stiffer equivalents. Neither belongs in conversation at this level.

## Contrast

### 同(おな)じ + noun vs 同(おな)じ<b>な</b> + noun

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>同(おな)じ</code> is the one な-adjective that drops its `な`
in front of a noun.

</div>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんと<b>同(おな)じ</b>意見(いけん)です。
I'm of the same opinion as Yamada.
</pre>

❌ 同(おな)じな意見(いけん) is simply wrong. But `な` **does** come back before
`ので` and `のに`: 同(おな)じなので, 同(おな)じなのに. So the rule is narrower
than "no `な` ever" - it is "no `な` before a noun".

### ～と同(おな)じ vs ～に似(に)ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>同(おな)じ</code> means identical. <code>似(に)ている</code> means
resembling. And note the particle flips: `と` for same, `に` for similar.

</div>

<pre>
この時計(とけい)は兄(あに)の<b>と同(おな)じ</b>です。
This watch is the same one my brother has.
</pre>

<pre>
この時計(とけい)は兄(あに)のに似(に)ています。
This watch looks like my brother's.
</pre>

The first says you could swap them and nobody would notice. The second says they
merely look alike. For faces, <code>似(に)ている</code> is the only option - 
母(はは)に似(に)ています, never 母(はは)と同(おな)じです unless you mean
something very odd.

### ～と同(おな)じ vs ～と違(ちが)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same frame, negated. Both take `と`, and both put the
comparison target in front of it.

</div>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)はあの漢字(かんじ)<b>と同(おな)じ</b>読(よ)み方(かた)です。
This kanji has the same reading as that one.
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)はあの漢字(かんじ)と読(よ)み方(かた)が違(ちが)います。
This kanji is read differently from that one.
</pre>

Note that <code>違(ちが)う</code> is a **verb**, so it takes ます／ました and never
です: ❌違(ちが)うです → ✅違(ちが)います. <code>同(おな)じ</code>, being adjectival,
does the opposite and needs です.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>に</b>同(おな)じです。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>と</b>同(おな)じです。
  The comparison target is marked with `と`. `に` belongs to
  <code>似(に)ている</code>, not here.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さんと同(おな)じ<b>な</b>会社(かいしゃ)です。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さんと同(おな)じ会社(かいしゃ)です。
  No `な` in front of a noun. This is the single most frequent error with the word.

- ❌ このかばんは私(わたし)と同(おな)じです。（meaning "the same as mine"）→ ✅ このかばんは私(わたし)<b>の</b>と同(おな)じです。
  Without `の` you are comparing a bag to a person. `の` turns
  <code>私(わたし)</code> into "mine".

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)と同(おな)じ<b>に</b>作(つく)りました。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)と同(おな)じ<b>ように</b>作(つく)りました。
  To modify a verb you need <code>ように</code>. Bare
  <code>同(おな)じに</code> is not idiomatic here.

- ❌ 二人(ふたり)の背(せ)は同(おな)じ<b>くらいと</b>高(たか)いです。 → ✅ 二人(ふたり)は同(おな)じくらい背(せ)が高(たか)いです。
  <code>同(おな)じくらい</code> sits directly in front of the adjective, with no
  particle between them.

## Practice

**1.** 林(はやし)さんは私(わたし)____同(おな)じアパートに住(す)んでいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

林(はやし)さんは私(わたし)<b>と</b>同(おな)じアパートに住(す)んでいます。

The thing being matched always takes `と`. Note also that
<code>同(おな)じ</code> attaches straight to アパート with no `な` in between.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この写真(しゃしん)の人(ひと)は母(はは)（と同(おな)じ・に似(に)ている）です。 You mean the person resembles your mother.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この写真(しゃしん)の人(ひと)は母(はは)<b>に似(に)ています</b>。

Resemblance takes <code>に似(に)ている</code>, with `に`.
母(はは)と同(おな)じです would claim the two are the same person - or the
same *thing*, which is stranger still. Note the answer ends in ています, not です,
because <code>似(に)る</code> is a verb.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I ordered the same thing as you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あなた<b>と同(おな)じ</b>ものを注文(ちゅうもん)しました。

<code>同(おな)じ</code> modifies もの directly - no `な`. In real conversation the
`あなた` would usually be replaced by the person's name, or dropped entirely:
同(おな)じものをください。

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この傘(かさ)はあなたと同(おな)じなです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この傘(かさ)はあなた<b>の</b>と同(おな)じです。

Two problems. `な` cannot stand before `です` - <code>同(おな)じです</code> is the
predicate form. And the umbrella should be compared with *yours*, not with *you*,
which is what the added `の` does.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 去年(きょねん)と同(おな)じ店(みせ)です。
(b) 去年(きょねん)の店(みせ)に似(に)ている店(みせ)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) claims identity: it is literally that shop, the one you went to last year,
still in the same place under the same name.

(b) claims resemblance only: a different shop that reminds you of the old one - 
similar food, similar atmosphere. Japanese keeps these two claims strictly apart,
where English "like last year's place" can be read either way.

</details>
