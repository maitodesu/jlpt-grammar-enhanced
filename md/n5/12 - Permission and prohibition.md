# N5 - Permission and prohibition


---

# ～てもいい - it's okay to

**Meaning** · it's all right to do it; you may do it

`～てもいい` grants permission, or asks for it. Literally it says "even if you do
it, it's fine" - `も` is the "even" of concession and `いい` is the verdict. That
shape is worth holding on to: the pattern never pushes anyone to act. It only
opens a door and leaves it open.

## Formation

`いい` is an い-adjective, so everything you can do to `いい` you can do here:
`いいです`, `よかった`, `よろしいでしょうか`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + もいい | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)ってもいい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form → なくて + もいい | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)わなくてもいい |
| い-adjective | drop い + くて + もいい | 古(ふる)い → 古(ふる)くてもいい |
| な-adjective | drop だ + でもいい | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かでもいい |
| Noun | noun + でもいい | お茶(ちゃ) → お茶(ちゃ)でもいい |
| Polite | いい → いいです | 使(つか)ってもいいです |
| Asking permission | ～てもいいですか | 使(つか)ってもいいですか |
| Softer / politer | いい → かまいません, よろしいでしょうか | 使(つか)ってもよろしいでしょうか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - verbs join with `て`, everything else joins with `で`. The
`で` in <code>お茶(ちゃ)でもいい</code> is the て-form of `です`, not the particle `で`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ここに<b>座(すわ)ってもいい</b>ですか。
May I sit here?
</pre>

<pre>
その本(ほん)、<b>読(よ)んでもいい</b>よ。
Go ahead and read that book.
</pre>

<pre>
暑(あつ)いので、窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けてもいい</b>ですか。
It's hot, so is it all right if I open the window?
</pre>

<pre>
ペンを一本(いっぽん)<b>借(か)りてもいい</b>？
Can I borrow a pen?
</pre>

<pre>
支払(しはら)いはカード<b>でもいい</b>ですか。
Is it okay to pay by card?
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)が終(お)わったら、先(さき)に<b>帰(かえ)ってもいい</b>ですよ。
Once you've finished, you're welcome to head home ahead of me.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)には<b>来(こ)なくてもいい</b>ですよ。資料(しりょう)だけ送(おく)ってください。
You don't have to come to tomorrow's meeting - just send me the documents.
</pre>

<pre>
この写真(しゃしん)、ブログに<b>載(の)せてもいい</b>でしょうか。
Would it be all right if I put this photo up on my blog?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Permission has a direction, and `～てもいい` is sensitive to it. **Asking** goes
in any direction; **granting** normally goes downward, from the person with
authority to the person without it. That asymmetry causes more trouble for
learners than the grammar does.

**Asking upward.** <code>～てもいいですか</code> is perfectly polite to a classmate, a
colleague or a shop assistant. To a customer, a client or a senior manager it is
a little bare, and Japanese reaches for something longer:
<code>～てもよろしいでしょうか</code>, or the humble
<code>～させていただけますか</code>. The extra length *is* the politeness.

**Granting upward is the mistake.** <code>部長(ぶちょう)、もう帰(かえ)ってもいいですよ</code>
is flawless grammar and socially impossible - you have just given your boss
permission to leave. When someone senior asks *you*, agree instead of authorising:
`どうぞ`, `もちろんです`, <code>はい、大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です</code>.

**In casual speech `も` disappears.** <code>見(み)ていい？</code>,
<code>入(はい)っていい？</code> - the shortened form is what you will actually hear
between friends, and it is not sloppy, just spoken. `～たっていい` is another
casual variant (<code>食(た)べたっていいよ</code>), slightly more argumentative in tone:
"you *could* eat it, you know."

**Refusals almost never use `いけません`.** Answering
<code>写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)ってもいいですか</code> with `いいえ、いけません` sounds like a
teacher scolding a child. Adults say <code>すみません、ちょっと…</code>, or state the rule
impersonally: <code>館内(かんない)は撮影(さつえい)禁止(きんし)となっております</code>.

**A second, quieter use: offering.** <code>私(わたし)が行(い)ってもいいよ</code> is not
permission at all - it means "I'm willing to go" / "I don't mind going." Said
flatly it can sound grudging, as though you are doing everyone a favour, so it
usually comes with `よ` and a warm tone.

## Contrast

### ～てもいい vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもいい` opens the door; `～てはいけない` shuts it. They are
the two ends of the same axis, but they are not used with the same freedom:
permission is offered constantly, prohibition mostly by rules and by adults
talking to children.

</div>

<pre>
ここで写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)ってもいい</b>ですか。
May I take photographs here?
</pre>

<pre>
ここで写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)ってはいけません</b>。
You must not take photographs here.
</pre>

The second sentence is what a sign says, or what a guard says. Between equals it
lands hard, which is why a refused request usually gets softened into an
apology instead.

### ～てもいい vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもいい` permits the **doing**. `～なくてもいい` permits the
**not doing**. Both are permission; they release you from opposite things.

</div>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)<b>食(た)べてもいい</b>ですよ。
You can eat all of it. (help yourself)
</pre>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)<b>食(た)べなくてもいい</b>ですよ。
You don't have to eat all of it. (stop whenever you like)
</pre>

These two complete the square with obligation: `～なくてはいけない` says you must,
`～なくてもいい` says you needn't, `～てはいけない` says you mustn't, `～てもいい` says
you may.

### ～てもいいですか vs ～てくれませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもいいですか` asks about **my** action.
`～てくれませんか` asks **you** to act. English "can I / can you" blurs this; Japanese
does not.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>閉(し)めてもいい</b>ですか。
May I close the window? (I will close it)
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を閉(し)めてくれませんか。
Could you close the window? (you close it)
</pre>

Getting these backwards produces a genuinely confusing sentence, because the
listener cannot tell whose hand is meant to move. If you are reaching for the
handle yourself, it is always `～てもいいですか`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この席(せき)に座(すわ)**るもいい**ですか。 → ✅ この席(せき)に座(すわ)**ってもいい**ですか。
  `もいい` attaches to the て-form, never to the dictionary form.

- ❌ ペンはこれ**もいい**ですか。 → ✅ ペンはこれ**でもいい**ですか。
  Nouns and な-adjectives need `で` before `もいい`. Only verbs and い-adjectives
  supply their own て/くて.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、もう帰(かえ)ってもいいですよ。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、あとは私(わたし)がやりますので、どうぞお先(さき)に。
  The grammar is fine and the direction is not. You do not grant a superior
  permission; you offer to take over.

- ❌ ここで写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っても**よくないです**。 → ✅ ここで写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っては**いけません**。
  Negating `いい` does not produce prohibition. "May not" has its own pattern.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この席(せき)に座(すわ)____いいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この席(せき)に座(すわ)<b>ってもいい</b>ですか。

<code>座(すわ)る</code> is an う-verb in る, so its て-form is <code>座(すわ)って</code>, and
`もいい` attaches to that. Asking permission about your own action is exactly what
this pattern is for.

</details>

**2.** 明日(あした)は休(やす)みですから、（来(き)てもいい・来(こ)なくてもいい）ですよ。 - meaning "you don't need to come".

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)は休(やす)みですから、<b>来(こ)なくてもいい</b>ですよ。

`～てもいい` would give them permission to come, which is the opposite of the
intended message. Releasing someone from an obligation needs the negative stem:
<code>来(く)る → 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)なくてもいい</code>. Note the reading change - こ, not き.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Can I ask one more question?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう一(ひと)つ<b>質問(しつもん)してもいい</b>ですか。
（politer: もう一(ひと)つ質問(しつもん)してもよろしいでしょうか。）

The action is yours, so permission is the right frame rather than a request. In a
meeting or to a customer, stretch it to `よろしいでしょうか` - the longer ending is
what carries the deference.

</details>

**4.** A student says to their teacher: 先生(せんせい)、もう帰(かえ)ってもいいですよ。 They mean "may I go home now?" Fix it.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先生(せんせい)、そろそろ帰(かえ)ってもよろしいでしょうか。

Two problems. `よ` turns the sentence into a statement that *grants* permission,
so the student has dismissed the teacher. And a plain question needs to be a
question: `～てもいいですか`, or upward, `～てもよろしいでしょうか`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)は来(こ)なくてもいいです。
(b) 明日(あした)は来(こ)ないでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) releases the listener from an obligation: coming is now optional, and if they
turn up anyway nothing is wrong.

(b) asks them not to come. It is a request with a reason behind it - the office is
closed, the room is being used - and turning up anyway would go against what was
asked. (a) is generous; (b) is restrictive.

</details>

# ～てはいけない - must not

**Meaning** · you must not do it; doing it is not allowed

`～てはいけない` is prohibition. Broken apart it says "as for doing it - that
won't do": `は` puts the action up as a topic and `いけない` (literally "it cannot
go") passes judgement on it. Because the judgement comes from a rule or from
someone with authority, the pattern always sounds like it is being handed down
from above.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + はいけない | 入(はい)る → 入(はい)ってはいけない |
| い-adjective | drop い + くて + はいけない | 遅(おそ)い → 遅(おそ)くてはいけない |
| な-adjective | drop だ + で + はいけない | 不便(ふべん)だ → 不便(ふべん)ではいけない |
| Noun | noun + で + はいけない | うそ → うそではいけない |
| Polite | いけない → いけません | 入(はい)ってはいけません |
| Casual contraction | ては → ちゃ | 入(はい)っちゃいけない |
| Casual contraction (で-form) | では → じゃ | 読(よ)んじゃいけない |
| Written, rules and law | いけない → ならない | 入(はい)ってはならない |
| Blunt spoken | いけない → だめ | 入(はい)っちゃだめ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て/で of the verb decides the contraction.
<code>書(か)いて</code> → <code>書(か)いちゃ</code>, but <code>読(よ)んで</code> → <code>読(よ)んじゃ</code>. There is no
❌<code>読(よ)んでちゃ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ここでたばこを<b>吸(す)ってはいけません</b>。
You must not smoke here.
</pre>

<pre>
授業中(じゅぎょうちゅう)にスマホを<b>見(み)てはいけません</b>。
You mustn't look at your phone during class.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなことを<b>言(い)っちゃいけない</b>よ。
You shouldn't say things like that.
</pre>

<pre>
芝生(しばふ)に<b>入(はい)ってはいけません</b>。
Keep off the grass.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)では、二十歳(はたち)になるまでお酒(さけ)を<b>飲(の)んではいけません</b>。
In Japan you may not drink alcohol until you turn twenty.
</pre>

<pre>
試験中(しけんちゅう)は辞書(じしょ)を<b>使(つか)ってはいけません</b>。机(つくえ)の下(した)に置(お)いてください。
You may not use a dictionary during the exam - please put it under your desk.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)を<b>忘(わす)れてはいけない</b>ので、カレンダーに書(か)いておきました。
It wouldn't do to forget the appointment, so I wrote it on the calendar.
</pre>

<pre>
この建物(たてもの)の中(なか)で大(おお)きな声(こえ)を<b>出(だ)してはならない</b>。
No loud voices are permitted inside this building.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～てはいけない` is **strong and top-down**. It is the voice of a rule, a teacher, a
parent, a sign, a manual. That is not a matter of politeness level - putting it in
`ません` form makes it polite prohibition, not gentle prohibition.

**Who says it to whom.** Adults say it to children, teachers to students, staff to
visitors. Between colleagues and friends it is jarring, and upward it is
unthinkable. If you need to stop a superior or a customer doing something, the
language goes impersonal and passive-aggressive on purpose:
<code>申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございませんが、こちらは禁煙(きんえん)となっております</code>, or
<code>ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください</code> ("please refrain"). Nobody is *told* anything; a rule
simply exists.

**Contractions belong to speech.** <code>走(はし)っちゃだめ</code> is what a parent
actually shouts in a car park; `～てはいけません` is what the sign on the wall says.
`ちゃ` and `じゃ` are casual but not rude - they are the everyday shape of this
pattern among family and friends. `だめ` for `いけない` is blunter still and very
common with children.

**`～てはならない` is written.** You meet it in laws, contracts, school rules and
formal notices, rarely in conversation. `いけない` covers the individual case;
`ならない` states a standing prohibition.

**It can point at yourself.** <code>遅(おく)れてはいけないので、早(はや)めに出(で)ます</code>
 - "since it would be bad to be late, I'll leave early." Here nobody is forbidding
anything; the speaker is judging an outcome as unacceptable. This use is common
and often missed by learners who only learn the pattern as "must not".

## Contrast

### ～てはいけない vs ～ないでください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てはいけない` states a **rule**. `～ないでください` makes a
**request**. One says the thing is forbidden; the other says please don't.

</div>

<pre>
ここに車(くるま)を<b>止(と)めてはいけません</b>。
You may not park here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここに車(くるま)を止(と)めないでください。
Please don't park here.
</pre>

The first cites an authority the speaker did not invent; the second is the speaker
asking. In everyday interaction - a neighbour, a colleague, a stranger - 
`～ないでください` is almost always the right choice, and learners under-use it
badly because textbooks teach `～てはいけない` first.

### ～てはいけない vs ～な

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same content, different force. `～てはいけない` is a rule
delivered in words. `～な` is a bark.

</div>

<pre>
廊下(ろうか)を<b>走(はし)ってはいけません</b>。
You mustn't run in the corridor.
</pre>

<pre>
廊下(ろうか)を<b>走(はし)るな</b>。
Don't run in the corridor!
</pre>

The bare `な` prohibitive is abrupt and male-coded, at home in orders, warnings
and signs. `～てはいけません` is the neutral written and spoken standard, and the
form you should default to until you have a very good ear for register.

### ～てはいけない vs ～なくてはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ては` forbids; `～なくては` compels. One `なく` flips the
sentence to its opposite, and it is easy to lose in a long sentence.

</div>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)んではいけません</b>。
You must not take this medicine.
</pre>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)まなくてはいけません</b>。
You must take this medicine.
</pre>

Both end in `いけません`, so the ending tells you nothing. Read the middle: a
て-form means prohibition, a `なくて` means obligation. Together with `～てもいい`
(may) and `～なくてもいい` (needn't) these four cover the whole permission grid.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ここに入(はい)**るはいけません**。 → ✅ ここに入(はい)**ってはいけません**。
  `はいけない` attaches to the て-form. The dictionary form never takes it.

- ❌ 読(よ)ん**でちゃ**いけない。 → ✅ 読(よ)ん**じゃ**いけない。
  Verbs whose て-form is `で` contract to `じゃ`, not `ちゃ`.

- ❌ 薬(くすり)を飲(の)ん**では**いけませんよ。（intending "you have to take your medicine"）→ ✅ 薬(くすり)を飲(の)ま**なくては**いけませんよ。
  Prohibition and obligation differ by one `なく`. This mix-up can be dangerous, not
  merely wrong.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、ここでたばこを吸(す)ってはいけません。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、こちらは禁煙(きんえん)となっておりますので…
  Grammatically fine, socially impossible. Upward, you point at the rule rather
  than at the person.

## Practice

**1.** 美術館(びじゅつかん)の中(なか)で写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)____いけません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)<b>っては</b>いけません。

<code>撮(と)る</code> is an う-verb, so the て-form is <code>撮(と)って</code>, and prohibition
adds `は`. A museum notice is exactly the top-down context this pattern is built
for.

</details>

**2.** 明日(あした)までにレポートを出(だ)さ（なくては・ては）いけません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

レポートを出(だ)さ<b>なくては</b>いけません。

The sentence means you have to submit it, which is obligation, so it needs the
negative stem plus `なくては`. <code>出(だ)してはいけません</code> would forbid submitting
the report - the exact opposite of a deadline.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You mustn't open that door."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そのドアを<b>開(あ)けてはいけません</b>。
（casual: そのドア、開(あ)けちゃだめだよ。）

<code>開(あ)ける</code> is a る-verb, so the て-form is <code>開(あ)けて</code>. Choose the
polite form for a notice or a stranger, and the `ちゃだめ` contraction only with
people you would speak casually to anyway.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 課長(かちょう)、その書類(しょるい)をここに置(お)いてはいけませんよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The grammar is correct; the direction is wrong.

✅ 課長(かちょう)、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません、こちらの書類(しょるい)は棚(たな)に戻(もど)すことになっておりまして…

`～てはいけません` forbids, and you do not forbid your section chief anything.
Naming the rule impersonally - <code>～ことになっております</code> - lets the regulation do
the work instead of you.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ここで遊(あそ)んではいけません。
(b) ここで遊(あそ)ばないでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) announces a prohibition that exists independently of the speaker: this is a
place where playing is not permitted. It is what a park sign or a caretaker says.

(b) is the speaker's own request, and leaves room for reasons and for goodwill - 
a neighbour whose flowers keep getting trampled would say this. (a) invokes
authority; (b) asks a favour.

</details>

# な - don't (blunt prohibitive)

**Meaning** · don't do it - a flat, unsoftened order not to do something

`な` is the shortest prohibition in the language: one syllable stuck onto the
dictionary form. It carries no politeness, no reason and no room for discussion,
which is exactly why it survives in the places where speed matters - warnings,
orders, sports, signs, and the mouths of angry fathers in films.

## Formation

`な` attaches to the **dictionary form**, and to nothing else. It does not conjugate
and it cannot be made polite.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | dictionary form + な | 行(い)く → 行(い)くな |
| る-verb | dictionary form + な | 見(み)る → 見(み)るな |
| する | する + な | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)するな |
| 来(く)る | くる + な | 来(く)る → 来(く)るな |
| Softened among friends | + よ | 言(い)う → 言(い)うなよ |
| Shouted / on a sign | + ！ | 触(さわ)る → 触(さわ)るな！ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - dictionary form, always. There is no ❌<code>行(い)かないな</code>
and no ❌<code>行(い)きな</code> meaning "don't go" - the second is a *positive*
command, and the trap is covered below.

</div>

い-adjectives, な-adjectives and nouns cannot take prohibitive `な` at all. When you
see `な` after <code>寒(さむ)い</code> or <code>静(しず)かだ</code>, it is a different word entirely.

## Examples

<pre>
それに<b>触(さわ)るな</b>。
Don't touch that.
</pre>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)<b>するな</b>。
Don't worry.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなに<b>急(いそ)ぐな</b>よ。
Don't rush so much.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あぶ)ない。中(なか)に<b>入(はい)るな</b>。
Danger. Do not enter.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)だよ。そんなに<b>怒(おこ)るな</b>よ。
I'm joking. Don't get so angry.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)の最後(さいご)まで<b>あきらめるな</b>。
Don't give up until the match is over.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ<b>来(く)るな</b>。部屋(へや)の準備(じゅんび)ができていないんだ。
Don't come in yet - the room isn't ready.
</pre>

<pre>
この話(はなし)は誰(だれ)にも<b>言(い)うな</b>よ。来週(らいしゅう)発表(はっぴょう)するから。
Don't tell anyone about this - we're announcing it next week.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the one N5 pattern where register is not a footnote but the entire point.

**It is male-coded and harsh.** Prohibitive `な` belongs to men's speech,
especially older men, and to relationships with a clear hierarchy: a coach to a
player, a father to a child, a boss to a subordinate he is annoyed with, one
close male friend to another. A woman using it sounds deliberately rough, which
is sometimes exactly the intended effect and never a neutral choice. Learners
should be able to **recognise** `な` everywhere and should be very cautious about
**producing** it.

**Where you will actually meet it.** Signs and warnings
(<code>入(はい)るな</code>, <code>押(お)すな</code>), manga and film dialogue, song
lyrics, sports slogans (<code>負(ま)けるな</code>, `あきらめるな`), and advertising
copy that wants to sound urgent. In those written contexts the harshness reads as
brevity rather than rudeness - a sign has no register.

**`よ` changes it more than you would expect.** <code>泣(な)くな</code> is an order;
<code>泣(な)くなよ</code> is closer to "hey, don't cry" and can be affectionate. It
stays casual and still leans male, but the edge comes off.

**Never aim it upward, and rarely sideways.** To a superior, a customer, a stranger
or anyone you use です／ます with, the choices are `～ないでください`,
<code>ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください</code>, or `～てはいけません` if you are stating a rule. There
is no polite version of `な`; the archaic `～ますな`
(<code>お忘(わす)れなさいますな</code>) survives only in period drama.

**It is not the same `な` as `～なさい`.** Nor is it the sentence-final particle you
hear in <code>いい天気(てんき)だな</code>. Both look identical on the page. The next
section separates them.

## Contrast

### な vs ～ないで・～ないでください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same instruction, opposite social weight. `な` orders;
`～ないでください` asks. If you are not certain which you want, you want
`～ないでください`.

</div>

<pre>
ここに車(くるま)を<b>止(と)めるな</b>。
Don't park here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここに車(くるま)を止(と)めないでください。
Please don't park here.
</pre>

Between the two sits plain `～ないで` (<code>止(と)めないで</code>), which is casual but
not rough, and is the everyday negative request among friends and family - 
including for speakers who would never say `な`. Think of it as the neutral option
that `な` is the shouted version of.

### 行(い)くな vs 行(い)きな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - dictionary form + `な` = **don't**. ます-stem + `な` = **do**.
One syllable of difference reverses the sentence.

</div>

<pre>
先(さき)に<b>行(い)くな</b>。
Don't go on ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
先(さき)に<b>行(い)きな</b>。
Go on ahead.
</pre>

<code>行(い)きな</code> is `～なさい` with the tail bitten off, so it is a soft command - 
warm, casual, common from mothers and shopkeepers, and much less aggressive than
prohibitive `な`. <code>食(た)べるな</code> means don't eat; <code>食(た)べな</code> means go
ahead and eat. Reading the stem correctly is the whole game here.

### Prohibitive な vs the sentence-final particle な

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if it follows an い-adjective, a な-adjective or a noun, it can
**only** be the emotive particle. Only after a dictionary-form verb is prohibition
even possible.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は寒(さむ)い<b>な</b>。
It's cold today, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けるな</b>。
Don't open the window.
</pre>

After a plain verb both readings exist, and three things separate them: the
emotive particle is usually drawn out (`なあ`), it is said with a falling, musing
tone rather than a bark, and it attaches happily to non-volitional predicates - 
<code>よく降(ふ)るな</code> can only mean "it really is raining," because you cannot
order the sky. Prohibition needs an action the listener could choose to stop.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そこに行(い)か**ないな**。 → ✅ そこに行(い)**くな**。
  `な` goes on the dictionary form. Adding it to the ない-form produces something
  that is not a prohibition at all.

- ❌ 食(た)べ**な**。（intending "don't eat it"）→ ✅ 食(た)べ**るな**。
  Without `る` you have said the opposite: "go on, eat." Keep the dictionary form
  intact.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、そこに座(すわ)るな。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、そちらの席(せき)はご遠慮(えんりょ)ください。
  There is no polite `な`. Upward, switch pattern entirely.

- ❌ 静(しず)かだ**な**。（intending "don't be quiet"）→ ✅ 静(しず)かに**するな**。
  `な` after a な-adjective is the emotive particle - you have said "how quiet it
  is." Prohibition needs a verb.

- ❌ 走(はし)り**ますな**。 → ✅ 走(はし)**るな**。／走(はし)らないでください。
  `な` cannot be attached to a polite form. `～ますな` exists but is archaic and will
  sound like a samurai drama.

## Practice

**1.** 危(あぶ)ないから、そのボタンを押(お)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そのボタンを押(お)<b>すな</b>。

<code>押(お)す</code> is already the dictionary form, so `な` simply follows it. A
warning shouted in the moment is one of the few situations where the bluntness is
appropriate rather than rude.

</details>

**2.** 寒(さむ)いから、窓(まど)を（開(あ)けるな・開(あ)けな）。 - meaning "don't open the window".

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けるな</b>。

<code>開(あ)けな</code> is the ます-stem plus `な`, a shortened `～なさい`, and it would
tell them *to* open it. Prohibition keeps the whole dictionary form:
<code>開(あ)ける + な</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, twice - once as a shouted warning, once as something you could say to your manager: "Don't touch this switch."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Shouted: このスイッチに<b>触(さわ)るな</b>。
To a manager: このスイッチには触(さわ)らないでください。（or …お手(て)を触(ふ)れないようお願(ねが)いします。）

The content is identical; only the social distance changes. `な` is fine on a
machine label or yelled across a workshop, and impossible aimed at a superior.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 田中(たなか)さん、そんなに飲(の)まないな。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 田中(たなか)さん、そんなに飲(の)むな。／ more naturally: 田中(たなか)さん、そんなに飲(の)まないで。

Two things are wrong. Prohibitive `な` never attaches to a ない-form, so
<code>飲(の)まないな</code> is ungrammatical as an order. And addressing someone with
`さん` while barking `な` at them is a register clash - if you are polite enough to
say `さん`, use `～ないで` or `～ないでください`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 無理(むり)するな。
(b) 無理(むり)するなよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both mean "don't overdo it," and both are casual and male-leaning.

(a) is bare and can sound curt, even cold - an instruction delivered and closed.
(b) adds `よ`, which turns the order toward the listener as something said *for
their sake*; it comes out as concerned, almost affectionate, the sort of thing a
friend says as you leave the office at eleven at night. The particle carries the
warmth that `な` itself has none of.

</details>

# ～なくてはいけない・～なくてはならない - must; have to

**Meaning** · you have to do it - not doing it is not an option

Japanese builds obligation out of two negatives. `～なくては` means "if you don't",
`いけない`／`ならない` means "that won't do", so the whole thing is *not doing it
won't do*. Nothing in the pattern says the speaker wants you to act - the pressure
comes from a rule, a schedule or the situation itself, and the speaker is merely
reporting it.

## Formation

Everything hinges on the negative stem. Make the ない-form first, drop the `い`,
add `くては`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | ない-form → なくて + はいけない | 行(い)く → 行(い)かなくてはいけない |
| る-verb | ない-form → なくて + はいけない | 出(で)る → 出(で)なくてはいけない |
| する | しなくてはいけない | 掃除(そうじ)する → 掃除(そうじ)しなくてはいけない |
| 来(く)る | こなくてはいけない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)なくてはいけない |
| い-adjective | ～くない → ～くなくて + はいけない | 新(あたら)しい → 新(あたら)しくなくてはいけない |
| な-adjective | drop だ + でなくて + はいけない | 元気(げんき)だ → 元気(げんき)でなくてはいけない |
| Noun | noun + でなくて + はいけない | 日本語(にほんご) → 日本語(にほんご)でなくてはいけない |
| Polite | いけません／なりません | 行(い)かなくてはいけません |
| Variant | なくては → なければ | 行(い)かなければならない |
| Casual contraction | なくては → なくちゃ | 行(い)かなくちゃ |
| Casual contraction | なければ → なきゃ | 行(い)かなきゃ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - four endings, one meaning: `なくてはいけない`,
`なくてはならない`, `なければいけない`, `なければならない` all mean "must". Pick by
register, not by meaning.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう<b>行(い)かなくちゃ</b>。
I've got to go.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は早(はや)く<b>起(お)きなくてはいけません</b>。
I have to get up early tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
パスポートを<b>見(み)せなくてはいけません</b>か。
Do I have to show my passport?
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)を出(で)るとき、電気(でんき)を<b>消(け)さなくてはいけません</b>。
You have to turn the lights off when you leave the room.
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)この薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)まなければならない</b>。
I have to take this medicine every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)までに、この本(ほん)を図書館(としょかん)に<b>返(かえ)さなくてはいけない</b>。
I have to get this book back to the library by next week.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)で車(くるま)を運転(うんてん)するには、免許(めんきょ)を<b>取(と)らなければなりません</b>。
To drive a car in Japan you must obtain a licence.
</pre>

<pre>
今週(こんしゅう)は土曜日(どようび)も<b>働(はたら)かなくてはいけない</b>んです。ちょっと大変(たいへん)です。
I have to work on Saturday this week too. It's a bit rough.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The four endings are not interchangeable in feel, and the differences are the
kind a textbook table hides.

**`いけない` vs `ならない`.** `いけない` handles the case in front of you: *I* have to
do this, *today*. `ならない` states a standing requirement and sounds heavier and
more written - laws, regulations, company rules, formal writing.
<code>税金(ぜいきん)を払(はら)わなければならない</code> reads like a statute, while
<code>税金(ぜいきん)を払(はら)わなくちゃ</code> is a person groaning at a form.

**`なくては` vs `なければ`.** `なければ` is the tidier, more formal conditional and
dominates in writing and careful speech. `なくては` is a shade more conversational.
Paired up, `なければならない` is the most formal of the four and `なくてはいけない`
the most everyday.

**Contractions are everywhere in speech.** `なくちゃ`, `なきゃ`, and very often with
the ending simply dropped: <code>あ、電話(でんわ)しなきゃ</code>, <code>もう寝(ね)なくちゃ</code>.
Learners rarely produce these and hear them constantly. Note that they are casual,
not sloppy - but they do not belong in a report or a speech.

**`～ないと` is the most common spoken form of all.** <code>急(いそ)がないと</code>,
<code>買(か)わないといけない</code>. If you listen to two friends planning a weekend,
`ないと` will outnumber the textbook forms considerably.

**Direction of use.** Describing your own obligations is safe at any politeness
level. Telling someone else what they must do is strong - to a superior, replace
it with a question (<code>ご確認(かくにん)いただけますでしょうか</code>) or state the rule
without an addressee. Between friends it is fine, and often sympathetic: the
speaker is on your side against the requirement.

## Contrast

### ～なくてはいけない vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one syllable apart, opposite meanings. `～なくてはいけない`
= must; `～なくてもいい` = needn't. The **は** closes the option; the **も** opens it.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は七時(しちじ)に<b>来(こ)なくてはいけません</b>。
You have to be here at seven tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は七時(しちじ)に<b>来(こ)なくてもいいです</b>。
You don't have to be here at seven tomorrow.
</pre>

Because they differ by a single particle, they are easy to mishear and easy to
mistype. When you want to release someone from a duty, check that you wrote `も`.

### ～なくてはいけない vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～なくては` compels, `～ては` forbids. The `いけない` at the end
is shared and tells you nothing; the information is in the middle of the word.

</div>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)に<b>名前(なまえ)を書(か)かなくてはいけません</b>。
You have to write your name on this form.
</pre>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)に<b>名前(なまえ)を書(か)いてはいけません</b>。
You must not write your name on this form.
</pre>

Laid out, the four corners of the grid are: `～てもいい` may, `～てはいけない` must
not, `～なくてはいけない` must, `～なくてもいい` needn't. Permission and prohibition
sit on the て-form; obligation and exemption sit on the negative stem.

### いけない vs ならない vs ないと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ならない` for rules on paper, `いけない` for what you personally
have to do, `ないと` for what you are saying out loud to a friend.

</div>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)は三月(さんがつ)までに出(だ)さ<b>なければならない</b>。
Applications must be submitted by March.
</pre>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)、三月(さんがつ)までに出(だ)さ<b>ないと</b>。
I need to get the application in by March.
</pre>

The first is the wording of a notice; the second is a person remembering a
deadline in the kitchen. Same requirement, completely different voice - and the
trailing `ないと` with nothing after it is entirely normal Japanese, not an
unfinished sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く**なくては**いけません。 → ✅ 行(い)か**なくては**いけません。
  Build the ない-form first (<code>行(い)かない</code>), then swap `い` for `くては`. The
  dictionary form never takes it.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)**だなくては**いけません。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)**でなくては**いけません。
  Nouns and な-adjectives negate with `で(は)ない`, so the join is `でなくては`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は来(こ)な**くては**いいです。（intending "you don't have to come"）→ ✅ 明日(あした)は来(こ)な**くても**いいです。
  `は` and `も` are doing all the work. `なくてはいい` is not a pattern.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、この書類(しょるい)を確認(かくにん)しなくてはいけませんよ。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの書類(しょるい)をご確認(かくにん)いただけますでしょうか。
  Stating someone's obligations to them is strong even in polite form. Upward, ask
  rather than require.

- ❌ 急(いそ)が**なきゃなりません**。 → ✅ 急(いそ)が**なければなりません**。／急(いそ)が**なきゃ**。
  Mixing registers inside one word is jarring: `なきゃ` is the casual contraction and
  does not take the formal `なりません` tail.

## Practice

**1.** 明日(あした)は六時(ろくじ)に起(お)き____いけません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

六時(ろくじ)に起(お)き<b>なくては</b>いけません。

<code>起(お)きる</code> → <code>起(お)きない</code> → <code>起(お)きなくては</code>. The obligation
comes from outside the speaker - an early train, a meeting - which is exactly what
this pattern reports.

</details>

**2.** パーティーですから、ネクタイをし（なくては・なくても）いけません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ネクタイをし<b>なくては</b>いけません。

`なくても` plus `いけない` is not a pattern - `なくても` pairs with `いい`. If the tie
is required, use `なくてはいけません`; if it is optional, the whole ending changes to
<code>しなくてもいいです</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually, as if muttering to yourself: "I have to do the laundry today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)、洗濯(せんたく)しなきゃ。
（also fine: 今日(きょう)は洗濯(せんたく)しなくちゃ。）

Muttered self-obligation is the natural home of the contractions, and the
`いけない` tail is normally left off entirely. Writing out
<code>洗濯(せんたく)しなければなりません</code> here would sound like a regulation about
laundry.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: 来週(らいしゅう)までにレポートを出(だ)すなくてはならないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)までにレポートを出(だ)さなくてはなりません。

Two errors. `なくては` attaches to the negative stem, so <code>出(だ)す</code> becomes
<code>出(だ)さない</code> → <code>出(だ)さなくては</code>. And `ならない` is a verb, not an
adjective: its polite form is `なりません`, never ❌`ならないです`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会員(かいいん)は毎年(まいとし)会費(かいひ)を払(はら)わなければならない。
(b) 会費(かいひ)、払(はら)わなくちゃ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the wording of a rule. `なければならない` states a standing requirement that
applies to a class of people, and the sentence would sit comfortably in a
membership agreement.

(b) is one member remembering it. The contraction, the dropped ending and the
comma-pause all mark it as spoken and personal - the obligation has stopped being
a regulation and become a chore.

</details>
