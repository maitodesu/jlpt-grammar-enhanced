# N4 - Choosing は or が, and joining ideas


---

# は vs が

**Meaning** · which of the two to reach for once the "new information" test has done its work

At N5 the rule was: the news comes **after** `は` and **before** `が`. That is still
the first thing to check, and it settles most sentences. This page is about what the
rule does not cover - `は` used to set one thing against another, `が` used to say
"this one and no other", and what happens to both particles once a clause is buried
inside a larger sentence.

## Formation

Neither particle changes shape, and neither changes the noun. What you are choosing
between is a **job**, so the table sorts by job rather than by word class.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Known noun → topic | Noun + は | 祖母(そぼ) → 祖母(そぼ)は元気(げんき)です |
| Two nouns set against each other → contrast | A は …、B は … | 平日(へいじつ)・週末(しゅうまつ) → 平日(へいじつ)は働(はたら)き、週末(しゅうまつ)は休(やす)む |
| Non-subject noun, contrasted | Particle + は | 駅(えき)まで → 駅(えき)までは歩(ある)きます |
| Quantity word, contrasted | Quantity + は + negative | 全部(ぜんぶ) → 全部(ぜんぶ)は覚(おぼ)えられません |
| New noun in a reported event | Noun + が | 事故(じこ) → 事故(じこ)がありました |
| The one and only → exhaustive が | Noun + が + predicate | 山口(やまぐち)さん → 山口(やまぐち)さんが担当(たんとう)です |
| Subject inside a noun-modifying clause | Noun + が（or の） | 父(ちち) → 父(ちち)が建(た)てた家(いえ) |
| Subject inside a と・ば・たら・とき clause | Noun + が | 雪(ゆき) → 雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)ったら… |
| Contrast reaching into a けど・から clause | Noun + は | 値段(ねだん) → 値段(ねだん)は高(たか)いけど… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a sentence can carry only one **topic**, but it can carry several `は`.
The first is normally the topic; every later one is contrastive.
私(わたし)は刺身(さしみ)は食(た)べません。 - "Me, I don't eat sashimi
(whatever else I might eat)."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)<b>は</b>読(よ)めますが、書(か)けません。
I can read kanji, but I can't write it.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)<b>は</b>私(わたし)<b>が</b>持(も)ちます。
I'll take the bags.
</pre>

<pre>
さっき変(へん)な音(おと)<b>が</b>したけど、今(いま)<b>は</b>静(しず)かだね。
There was an odd noise a minute ago, but it's quiet now.
</pre>

<pre>
司会(しかい)は森(もり)さん<b>が</b>、受付(うけつけ)は林(はやし)さん<b>が</b>担当(たんとう)します。
Mori will run the meeting and Hayashi will look after reception.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)<b>が</b>昔(むかし)使(つか)っていたカメラ<b>は</b>、今(いま)でもちゃんと動(うご)きます。
The camera my father used years ago still works perfectly.
</pre>

<pre>
野菜(やさい)の値段(ねだん)<b>が</b>上(あ)がったので、今年(ことし)<b>は</b>旅行(りょこう)をやめました。
Vegetable prices went up, so we've given up on a trip this year.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)が変(か)わったこと、部長(ぶちょう)に<b>は</b>伝(つた)えましたが、山田(やまだ)さんに<b>は</b>まだ伝(つた)えていません。
I've told the manager the meeting time changed, but I haven't told Yamada yet.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)<b>は</b>私(わたし)<b>が</b>しますので、資料(しりょう)のコピー<b>は</b>田中(たなか)さんにお願(ねが)いできますか。
I'll do the explaining, so could I leave copying the handouts to you, Tanaka?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Both particles are register-neutral: nothing about choosing one makes you sound
formal or casual. What they do carry is **attitude**, and that is where politeness
gets involved.

`は` drags a comparison in behind it whether you want one or not, so an unnecessary
`は` can sound pointed. Volunteering with 私(わたし)<b>は</b>やります in a room
where a job is being handed out reads as "*I*, at any rate, will do it - never mind
the rest of you"; the cooperative version is 私(わたし)<b>が</b>やります, which
simply fills the empty slot of "who". The same edge is useful on purpose:
「今日(きょう)はちょっと…」 turns an invitation down gently precisely because `は`
implies "another day, gladly."

`が` in its selecting sense is the particle of taking responsibility, and business
Japanese leans on it: 私(わたし)<b>が</b>確認(かくにん)いたします,
こちら<b>が</b>新(あたら)しい資料(しりょう)でございます. Learners who default to
`は` in those slots sound oddly detached from what they are saying.

In casual speech both particles are routinely dropped - 
「あれ、鍵(かぎ)、どこ?」 - and the contrast `は` would have carried is done by
stress instead. Note that a **contrastive** `は` survives that dropping far more often
than a topic `は`: 「肉(にく)は食(た)べない」 keeps its particle because the
particle is the whole point of the sentence.

Writing gives `は` one job that speech uses less: definitions.
<code>敬語(けいご)とは、相手(あいて)を高(たか)める言葉(ことば)である</code> - "*keigo*
is language that elevates the other party." That <code>とは</code> frame opens dictionary
entries and academic articles, and it is always `は`, never `が`.

## Contrast

### Topic は vs contrastive は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can tack "…whatever about the others" onto the end and the
sentence still means what you meant, the `は` is contrastive. A topic `は` sits at the
head of the sentence; a contrastive `は` can sit anywhere, including on top of another
particle, and it takes the stress in speech.

</div>

The topic use simply announces what you are talking about:

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>は</b>大学(だいがく)で建築(けんちく)を勉強(べんきょう)しています。
My younger brother is studying architecture at university.
</pre>

The contrastive use is a different move. It lifts its noun out of a field of
alternatives and pointedly says nothing about the rest:

<pre>
夜(よる)<b>は</b>コーヒーを飲(の)みません。
I don't drink coffee at night. (daytime is another matter)
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで<b>は</b>走(はし)りましたが、間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。
I ran as far as the station, at least - but I didn't make it.
</pre>

The most useful case is a **quantity word plus a negative**. `は` there produces
partial negation, and leaving it out flips the meaning:

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)<b>は</b>覚(おぼ)えられませんでした。
I couldn't memorise all of it. (I did learn some)
</pre>

Without the `は`, 全部(ぜんぶ)覚(おぼ)えられませんでした is normally taken as "I
couldn't learn any of it." The same trick works on <code>全員(ぜんいん)は</code>,
<code>いつもは</code> and <code>毎日(まいにち)は</code>.

### Neutral-description が vs exhaustive が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` either **reports** a whole event as news, or **selects** one
member out of a set. The selecting kind answers a "which one?" and cannot be swapped
for `は`.

</div>

Reporting: nothing is being singled out. You are presenting a situation you have just
noticed, or announcing one the listener has not heard.

<pre>
駅(えき)の前(まえ)に新(あたら)しいパン屋(や)<b>が</b>できました。
A new bakery has opened in front of the station.
</pre>

Selecting: everyone already knows the predicate applies to somebody, and `が` names
who. This is why identifying yourself takes `が`:

<pre>
本日(ほんじつ)案内(あんない)いたします、木村(きむら)<b>が</b>担当(たんとう)でございます。
I'm Kimura, and I'll be looking after you today.
</pre>

The selecting `が` is what makes **exhaustive listing** possible - handing out roles
one by one, with the understanding that between them the list is complete:

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)は私(わたし)<b>が</b>、日曜日(にちようび)は妻(つま)<b>が</b>店(みせ)に出(で)ます。
I'll be at the shop on Saturday and my wife on Sunday.
</pre>

One practical consequence: with an adjective and an already-known subject, `が` forces
the selecting reading and sounds strange as a plain description.
この部屋(へや)<b>が</b>明(あか)るいです means "*this* is the bright one"; for "this
room is bright" you want <code>この部屋(へや)は明(あか)るいです</code>.

### Where は cannot go, and what it means when it can

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` scopes over a whole sentence, so it cannot sit inside a
clause that is only a *part* of one. Inside noun-modifying clauses, and inside
と・ば・たら・なら・とき clauses, the subject takes `が` - or `の` in a noun-modifying
clause.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)<b>が</b>送(おく)ってくれた野菜(やさい)を、近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)に分(わ)けました。
I shared the vegetables my mother sent me out among the neighbours.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)<b>が</b>止(と)まったら、タクシーで帰(かえ)ります。
If the trains stop, I'll go home by taxi.
</pre>

❌<code>電車(でんしゃ)は止(と)まったら</code> is not a stylistic slip - it breaks,
because the topic would be trying to scope over a sentence whose main clause is about
you, not about trains.

The exception is worth knowing, because it looks like a counterexample. Clauses joined
with けど・が・から・ので are not *embedded*; they are full clauses stuck together. `は`
can appear inside those - and when it does, it is always the contrastive `は`, never a
second topic:

<pre>
時間(じかん)<b>は</b>かかりますが、必(かなら)ず直(なお)します。
It will take time, granted - but I will definitely fix it.
</pre>

That is exactly the "…whatever about the rest" reading: time, yes; difficulty, cost
and outcome, not commented on. Test any `は` you meet inside a subordinate clause
against that, and it will almost always turn out to be contrast.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 外(そと)**は**暗(くら)くなったら、電気(でんき)をつけてください。 → ✅ 外(そと)**が**暗(くら)くなったら、電気(でんき)をつけてください。
  A たら clause is embedded inside the sentence, and a topic cannot live there. Its
  subject takes `が`.

- ❌ 「どちらが田中(たなか)さんですか。」「私(わたし)**は**田中(たなか)です。」 → ✅ 「私(わたし)**が**田中(たなか)です。」
  <code>私(わたし)は田中(たなか)です</code> is a fine self-introduction, but as an answer
  to "which of you is Tanaka?" it dodges the question. You were asked to select, and
  selecting is `が`.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)**が**静(しず)かです。（meaning "this room is quiet"） → ✅ この部屋(へや)**は**静(しず)かです。
  With a known subject and an adjective, `が` is heard as "this is the quiet *one*".
  For a plain description, use `は`.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)**は**雪(ゆき)**は**降(ふ)りました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)**は**雪(ゆき)**が**降(ふ)りました。
  The second `は` forces a contrast - "snow, at least, fell" - which is not what you
  meant. A reported event takes `が`.

## Practice

**1.** 「どうしたんですか。」「エアコン____動(うご)かないんです。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

エアコン**が**動(うご)かないんです。

You are reporting a problem the listener does not know about yet, so the air
conditioner is brand-new information - the reporting `が`. `は` would imply the two of
you were already discussing that particular machine.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 時間(じかん)（は・が）ないので、今日(きょう)（は・が）ここまでにしましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

時間(じかん)**が**ないので、今日(きょう)**は**ここまでにしましょう。

The first slot sits inside a `ので` clause and simply states a fact about time, so it
takes `が`. The second sets today against other days - we stop here today, and next
time we will go further - which is contrastive `は`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've read the first half, but I haven't read the second half yet."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

前半(ぜんはん)**は**読(よ)みましたが、後半(こうはん)**は**まだ読(よ)んでいません。

Both halves are grammatical objects and would normally take `を`, but the sentence
exists in order to set them against each other, so `を` is replaced by contrastive
`は` on both sides.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 雨(あめ)は降(ふ)ったら、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 雨(あめ)**が**降(ふ)ったら、試合(しあい)**は**中止(ちゅうし)です。

The first `は` is inside a たら clause and has to become `が`. The second one is in the
main clause and is correct as it stands - the match is the topic of the sentence.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山口(やまぐち)さん**は**部長(ぶちょう)です。
(b) 山口(やまぐち)さん**が**部長(ぶちょう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) takes 山口(やまぐち)さん as already under discussion and tells you something
about him: his job is manager. It is the answer to "what does he do?"

(b) starts from the other end. Everyone knows there is a manager; the sentence
identifies which person it is - "山口(やまぐち)さん is the one" - picking him out
of the group. It answers "which of them is the manager?", and it is what you say while
nodding across the room.

</details>

# ～し

**Meaning** · and what's more - piling up reasons or qualities, with the hint that there are others

`～し` joins clauses the way a person builds a case: one point, another point, and an
unstated "so, you see." It differs from a plain "and" in two ways. The items are
offered as **grounds** for something, and the list is **open** - you are naming a
couple of reasons out of several, not all of them.

## Formation

`し` attaches to the **plain form** of whatever ends the clause, and it can equally
follow a polite form when the sentence around it is polite.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + し | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るし |
| Verb (past) | plain past + し | 降(ふ)った → 降(ふ)ったし |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + し | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないし |
| Verb (polite) | ます-form + し | 降(ふ)ります → 降(ふ)りますし |
| い-adjective | plain form + し | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いし |
| い-adjective (polite) | です + し | 安(やす)いです → 安(やす)いですし |
| な-adjective | だ + し | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かだし |
| な-adjective (polite) | です + し | 静(しず)かです → 静(しず)かですし |
| Noun | だ + し | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みだし |
| Noun (polite) | です + し | 休(やす)みです → 休(やす)みですし |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `し` needs a *finished* clause in front of it. That is why nouns
and な-adjectives must keep their `だ`（❌<code>静(しず)かし</code>）while
い-adjectives must not add one（❌<code>安(やす)いだし</code>）.

</div>

A second habit worth building in from the start: the clause after `し` usually marks
one of its nouns with `も`. <code>雨(あめ)だし、風(かぜ)も強(つよ)い</code>,
<code>安(やす)いし、味(あじ)もいい</code>. That `も` is what makes the sentence feel like
an accumulation of points rather than a sequence of events.

## Examples

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>し</b>、おいしい。
This place is cheap, and the food's good.
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れた<b>し</b>、そろそろ帰(かえ)ろう。
I'm tired - let's head home soon.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みだ<b>し</b>、映画(えいが)でも見(み)に行(い)かない?
Tomorrow's a day off - how about going to see a film or something?
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)い<b>し</b>、駅(えき)にも近(ちか)い<b>し</b>、ここに決(き)めました。
The room's big and it's near the station too, so I've settled on this one.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は約束(やくそく)を守(まも)らない<b>し</b>、連絡(れんらく)もくれない<b>し</b>、もう信(しん)じられない。
He doesn't keep his promises, he never gets in touch - I just can't trust him any more.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ時間(じかん)もあります<b>し</b>、もう一軒(いっけん)寄(よ)っていきませんか。
We've still got time, so shall we stop off at one more place?
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は悪(わる)くない<b>し</b>、人間関係(にんげんかんけい)もいい<b>し</b>、今(いま)の会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)める理由(りゆう)がありません。
The pay isn't bad and I get on with everyone, so I've no reason to leave my current company.
</pre>

<pre>
「どうして引(ひ)っ越(こ)すんですか。」「家賃(やちん)が高(たか)いです<b>し</b>、会社(かいしゃ)からも遠(とお)いので。」
"Why are you moving?" "The rent's high, and it's a long way from the office."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`し` itself is neutral, but it belongs to **spoken** Japanese. It is at home in
conversation, in a chatty email, in a blog post; a formal report would reach for
<code>～上(うえ)に</code> or simply separate sentences instead.

Politeness is decided by the clause, not by `し`. In polite speech you attach it to
the polite form - <code>安(やす)いですし</code>, <code>あります**し**</code> - and mixing
the two inside one sentence sounds careless. Plain-form `し` inside an otherwise
polite sentence is heard sometimes in relaxed workplace speech, but it is not
something to imitate early.

The habit worth copying is **trailing し**: a sentence that ends on `し` and simply
stops.

- 「今日(きょう)はやめておきます。明日(あした)早(はや)いし。」 - "I'll pass
  today. Early start tomorrow and all."
- 「べつにいいよ。もう終(お)わったし。」 - "It's fine. It's over now anyway."

The unfinished shape is doing real work: it hands the conclusion to the listener
instead of stating it, which softens a refusal and lets you avoid saying the awkward
part out loud. The flip side is that a string of trailing `し` about someone else can
sound like grumbling or excuse-making - 「だって忙(いそが)しかったし」 is exactly the
tone of a child explaining why the homework is not done.

One more thing `し` does that `から` cannot: it can list reasons that point in
different directions, letting the speaker weigh them.
<code>給料(きゅうりょう)はいいし、でも残業(ざんぎょう)も多(おお)いし…</code> is a person
thinking out loud.

## Contrast

### ～し vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` gives **the** reason and pushes the conclusion at the
listener. `し` gives **a** reason among others and leaves room for more.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)がある<b>から</b>、今日(きょう)は休(やす)みます。
I've got a fever, so I'm taking today off.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)もある<b>し</b>、今日(きょう)は休(やす)みます。
I've got a bit of a temperature and so on, so I'm taking today off.
</pre>

The `から` version presents one clean cause. The `し` version implies a small pile of
reasons - tiredness, the weather, the fever - of which you have named one. That
vagueness is why `し` sounds softer and less accusatory, and why refusals reach for it.

The two combine, and the combination is extremely common:
<code>安(やす)いし、近(ちか)いし、いい店(みせ)ですから、ぜひ行(い)ってみてください</code>
 - the `し` clauses stack the evidence and the final `から` delivers the verdict.

### ～し vs the て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て-form strings things together in **order**; `し` adds them
up as **evidence**. If the second clause simply happened next, you want て.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)起(お)き<b>て</b>、顔(かお)を洗(あら)って、家(いえ)を出(で)ました。
I got up, washed my face and left the house.
</pre>

Putting `し` in there - ❌<code>朝(あさ)起(お)きたし、顔(かお)を洗(あら)いました</code>
 - makes it sound as though getting up were a reason for washing.

With adjectives the two overlap more, and both are correct:

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は軽(かる)く<b>て</b>、使(つか)いやすいです。
This dictionary is light and easy to use.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は軽(かる)い<b>し</b>、使(つか)いやすいです。
This dictionary is light, and easy to use into the bargain.
</pre>

The て version is a flat description. The `し` version sounds like a recommendation - 
you can hear "so you should buy it" behind it.

### ～し vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both give an incomplete list, but of different things. `し` lists
whole **clauses** as reasons; `たり` lists **activities** as samples of what went on.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は洗濯(せんたく)をし<b>たり</b>、部屋(へや)を片(かた)づけ<b>たり</b>しました。
Yesterday I did the washing, tidied my room and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は洗濯(せんたく)もした<b>し</b>、部屋(へや)も片(かた)づけた。
Yesterday I did the washing, and I tidied my room as well.
</pre>

The `たり` sentence answers "what did you get up to?" - it paints a picture of the day.
The `し` sentence answers "have you done anything useful?" - it is a defence, item by
item. Note also that `たり` must end in する, while `し` needs nothing after it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この町(まち)は静(しず)か**し**、空気(くうき)もきれいです。 → ✅ この町(まち)は静(しず)か**だし**、空気(くうき)もきれいです。
  A な-adjective needs its `だ` before `し`. The same goes for nouns: <code>学生(がくせい)だし</code>.

- ❌ この本(ほん)は安(やす)い**だし**、面白(おもしろ)いです。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は安(やす)い**し**、面白(おもしろ)いです。
  An い-adjective is already a complete predicate, so adding `だ` is one word too many.

- ❌ シャワーを浴(あ)びた**し**、寝(ね)ました。 → ✅ シャワーを浴(あ)び**て**、寝(ね)ました。
  `し` does not narrate a sequence. Two events that simply happened one after the
  other take the て-form.

- ❌ 近(ちか)いし、安(やす)い**です**し、いい店(みせ)です。 → ✅ 近(ちか)い**です**し、安(やす)い**です**し、いい店(みせ)です。
  Keep one politeness level across the whole sentence. Plain `し` followed by polite
  `し` sounds like a slip.

## Practice

**1.** この町(まち)は便利(べんり)____、家賃(やちん)も安(やす)いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この町(まち)は便利(べんり)<b>だし</b>、家賃(やちん)も安(やす)いです。

<code>便利(べんり)</code> is a な-adjective, so it needs `だ` to finish the clause before `し`
can attach. The polite alternative is <code>便利(べんり)ですし</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)は映画(えいが)を見(み)（し・て）、ごはんを食(た)べました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)は映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>て</b>、ごはんを食(た)べました。

Nothing here is a reason for anything - the two events simply happened in order, and
that is the て-form's job. `し` would suggest you were listing evidence for some
conclusion you have not stated.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This café is close to the station and it isn't expensive, so let's meet here."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この店(みせ)は駅(えき)から近(ちか)い<b>し</b>、値段(ねだん)も高(たか)くない<b>し</b>、ここで待(ま)ち合(あ)わせましょう。

Two `し` clauses stack the reasons, and the suggestion follows with no connective at
all - once `し` has done the work, `だから` would be redundant.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)は親切(しんせつ)し、頭(あたま)もいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は親切(しんせつ)<b>だし</b>、頭(あたま)もいいです。

<code>親切(しんせつ)</code> is a な-adjective. Without `だ` the clause never finishes, and `し`
has nothing to attach to. Polite version: <code>親切(しんせつ)ですし</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 雨(あめ)だ**から**、今日(きょう)は出(で)かけません。
(b) 雨(あめ)だ**し**、今日(きょう)は出(で)かけません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) names the rain as the reason, full stop. It is a clear, slightly firm statement of
cause and effect, and it invites the listener to accept it.

(b) offers the rain as *one* of the things making you stay in - you are tired, there
is nothing on, and besides, it's raining. It sounds more relaxed and less like an
argument, which is exactly why it is the friendlier way to decline an invitation.

</details>

# ～たり～たり

**Meaning** · doing things like A and B - naming a couple of examples out of a larger set

`～たり～たり` lists **samples**. Whatever you name, the listener understands that
other things happened too, and that the order they are given in means nothing. Its
second job is alternation: two states that keep swapping places, one day this, the
next day that.

## Formation

Take the plain past（た-form）of each item, add `り`, and close the whole list with
`する`. The `する` is not decoration - it is the verb of the sentence, and it carries
the tense and the politeness for everything in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + り | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んだり |
| Verb (する) | した + り | 掃除(そうじ)する → 掃除(そうじ)したり |
| Verb (negative) | なかった + り | 行(い)く → 行(い)かなかったり |
| い-adjective | かった + り | 暑(あつ)い → 暑(あつ)かったり |
| な-adjective | だった + り | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かだったり |
| Noun | だった + り | 雨(あめ) → 雨(あめ)だったり |
| Closing the list (verbs) | …たり…たり + する | 歩(ある)いたり走(はし)ったりする |
| Closing the list (past, polite) | …たり…たり + しました | 歌(うた)ったり踊(おど)ったりしました |
| Closing the list (adjectives / nouns) | …だったり…だったり + だ／です | 暑(あつ)かったり寒(さむ)かったりです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can make the た-form, you can make the たり-form: just add
`り`. The trap is the closing `する`, which learners drop. It is what conjugates:
<code>したり…したりしています</code>, <code>…したりしたい</code>, <code>…したりしないでください</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
夜(よる)は音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)い<b>たり</b>、ギターを弾(ひ)い<b>たり</b>します。
In the evenings I listen to music, play the guitar, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
教室(きょうしつ)で飲(の)ん<b>だり</b>食(た)べ<b>たり</b>しないでください。
Please don't eat or drink in the classroom.
</pre>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)は暑(あつ)かっ<b>たり</b>寒(さむ)かっ<b>たり</b>で、体調(たいちょう)を崩(くず)しやすい。
Lately it's hot one day and cold the next, so it's easy to get run down.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)はさっきから部屋(へや)の中(なか)を行(い)っ<b>たり</b>来(き)<b>たり</b>している。
Dad's been pacing up and down the room for a while now.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の場所(ばしょ)は本社(ほんしゃ)だっ<b>たり</b>支店(してん)だっ<b>たり</b>で、よく間違(まちが)えます。
The meeting is sometimes at head office, sometimes at the branch, so I often get it wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は野菜(やさい)を食(た)べ<b>たり</b>食(た)べなかっ<b>たり</b>で、心配(しんぱい)です。
My son eats his vegetables some days and not others, which worries me.
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は、近(ちか)くの公園(こうえん)を歩(ある)い<b>たり</b>、カフェで新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)ん<b>だり</b>して、ゆっくり過(す)ごしています。
On Sundays I walk in the park nearby, read the paper in a café, and generally take it slow.
</pre>

<pre>
一人暮(ひとりぐ)らしを始(はじ)めてから、自分(じぶん)で料理(りょうり)を作(つく)っ<b>たり</b>部屋(へや)を片(かた)づけ<b>たり</b>するのが当(あ)たり前(まえ)になりました。
Since I started living alone, cooking for myself and tidying up have just become normal.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～たり` is register-neutral. Everything about the level of the sentence is decided by
the closing verb: <code>見(み)たりします</code> is polite,
<code>見(み)たりする</code> is plain, <code>見(み)たりいたします</code> is humble. The
`たり` parts themselves never change.

Two conversational habits to recognise. First, a **single** `たり` is normal - it
still means "and things like that", and it often softens an accusation:
<code>泣(な)いたりしないで</code>, <code>無理(むり)をしたりしないでね</code>. Second,
`たりして` at the end of a sentence is a hedge, close to English "…or something":
「明日(あした)、急(きゅう)に雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)ったりして」 - "knowing our luck it'll
snow tomorrow." That use is casual and slightly playful; keep it out of a report.

In casual speech you will also hear `とか` doing the same job - 
<code>洗(あら)ったりとかして</code> - which is chattier still, and the closing `する` is
sometimes swallowed altogether: 「掃除(そうじ)したり洗濯(せんたく)したり、いろいろ」.
Understand it, but write the `する`.

The alternation use has one shape worth memorising because it appears everywhere:
**A + たり + A's negative + たり** - <code>降(ふ)ったり降(ふ)らなかったり</code>,
<code>来(き)たり来(こ)なかったり</code> - meaning "sometimes yes, sometimes no". It
carries a faint note of unreliability, so it is not a compliment.

## Contrast

### ～たり～たり vs the て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て-form reports what happened, **in order and in full**.
`たり` gives an unordered sample and implies there was more.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)、コーヒーを飲(の)ん<b>で</b>、メールをチェックして、家(いえ)を出(で)ました。
This morning I had a coffee, checked my email and left the house.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)は、コーヒーを飲(の)ん<b>だり</b>メールをチェックし<b>たり</b>しています。
In the mornings I have a coffee, check my email, and so on.
</pre>

The first is a report of one particular morning, in sequence. The second describes
what mornings are generally like, and rearranging the two items would not change it at
all. Because of that, `たり` is wrong when the order matters:
❌<code>歯(は)をみがいたり寝(ね)たりしました</code> for "I brushed my teeth and went to
bed."

### ～たり～たり vs ～や・～とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same "partial list" idea, different word class. `や` and `とか`
list **nouns**; `たり` lists **actions and states**.

</div>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にペン<b>や</b>ノートが置(お)いてあります。
There are pens, notebooks and so on lying on the desk.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)で書(か)い<b>たり</b>調(しら)べ<b>たり</b>しています。
I write and look things up at the desk.
</pre>

`とか` straddles the line - it lists nouns like `や`, but in casual speech it also
lists dictionary-form verbs: <code>洗(あら)うとか干(ほ)すとか</code>. That verb use is
markedly more casual than `たり`, and it does not need a closing `する`.

### ～たり～たり vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` is two actions happening **at the same moment**; `たり` is
two actions taking turns, or sampled from a stretch of time.

</div>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>ながら</b>走(はし)っています。
I run while listening to music. (both at once)
</pre>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)い<b>たり</b>走(はし)っ<b>たり</b>しています。
I listen to music, go running, that kind of thing. (at different times)
</pre>

Learners sometimes reach for `たり` when describing multitasking, and it quietly says
the opposite of what they mean. If the two things overlap in time, you want `ながら`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ テレビを見(み)る**たり**、本(ほん)を読(よ)む**たり**します。 → ✅ テレビを見(み)**たり**、本(ほん)を読(よ)ん**だり**します。
  `たり` is built on the た-form, never the dictionary form. Make the past tense first,
  then swap `た` for `たり` - including the voiced `だり`.

- ❌ 週末(しゅうまつ)は映画(えいが)を見(み)たり、友(とも)だちに会(あ)ったり。 → ✅ …会(あ)ったり**します**。
  The closing `する` is what carries the tense. Leaving it off is fine in casual
  conversation and wrong in writing or polite speech.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)か**たり**、うるさかったりします。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)か**だったり**、うるさかったりします。
  な-adjectives and nouns need `だった` before `り`; only verbs and い-adjectives have a
  た-form of their own.

- ❌ 起(お)きたり、顔(かお)を洗(あら)ったりして、会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きます。 → ✅ 起(お)き**て**、顔(かお)を洗(あら)っ**て**、会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きます。
  A fixed morning routine happens in a fixed order, and `たり` explicitly throws the
  order away. Use the て-form.

## Practice

**1.** 週末(しゅうまつ)は部屋(へや)を掃除(そうじ)し____、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)っ____します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

掃除(そうじ)し<b>たり</b>、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)っ<b>たり</b>します。

Both verbs go into the た-form first（<code>した</code>, <code>行(い)った</code>）and then take
`り`. The final `します` is the verb of the whole sentence and supplies the polite
present tense.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 手(て)を洗(あら)っ（たり・て）から、ごはんを食(た)べましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

手(て)を洗(あら)っ<b>て</b>から、ごはんを食(た)べましょう。

The whole point of the sentence is the order - hands first, then food - and
<code>てから</code> is what expresses that. `たり` would say the two happen in no
particular order, which is not the advice anyone gives about washing your hands.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Lately the weather has been rainy some days and clear on others."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

最近(さいきん)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>たり</b>晴(は)れ<b>たり</b>しています。

This is the alternation use: two states swapping back and forth rather than a list of
activities. <code>しています</code> presents it as an ongoing situation; <code>します</code>
would sound like a general truth about the climate.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は友(とも)だちと話(はな)するたり、ゲームをしたりしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は友(とも)だちと話(はな)し<b>たり</b>、ゲームをし<b>たり</b>しました。

<code>話(はな)す</code> is a う-verb, so its た-form is <code>話(はな)した</code> and the
たり-form is <code>話(はな)したり</code>. The dictionary form never appears before `たり`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 昨日(きのう)は洗濯(せんたく)をして、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)きました。
(b) 昨日(きのう)は洗濯(せんたく)をし**たり**、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)っ**たり**しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a report of the day in sequence: the washing first, then the shopping, and by
implication that is what you did.

(b) picks two things out of a fuller day and offers them as examples. The order is not
being claimed, and the listener understands there were other errands you have not
bothered to mention. It is the natural answer to a vague "what did you get up to
yesterday?"

</details>

# ～ながら

**Meaning** · while doing something else - two actions by one person at the same time

`～ながら` puts two activities on top of each other. The clause with `ながら` is the
**background**; the main clause at the end is what the sentence is really about. Both
actions must belong to the **same person**, and both must be things that last long
enough to overlap.

## Formation

`ながら` attaches to the ます-stem and never changes shape itself. All the tense and
politeness live in the final verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + ながら | 歩(ある)きます → 歩(ある)きながら |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + ながら | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べながら |
| Verb (する) | し + ながら | 運転(うんてん)する → 運転(うんてん)しながら |
| Verb (ている, concessive) | てい + ながら | 知(し)っている → 知(し)っていながら |
| い-adjective (concessive) | plain form + ながら（も） | 狭(せま)い → 狭(せま)いながらも |
| な-adjective (concessive) | stem + ながら（も） | 残念(ざんねん) → 残念(ざんねん)ながら |
| Noun (concessive) | Noun + ながら | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもながら |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` takes the same stem as `ます`, so
<code>食(た)べます → 食(た)べながら</code>, never ❌<code>食(た)べるながら</code>. The
verb before it also has to be one that *takes time*: you can walk while talking, but
not ❌<code>着(つ)きながら</code> ("while arriving").

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the last four rows are a **different** `ながら`, meaning "although". It
attaches to `ている` forms, い-adjectives, な-adjective stems and bare nouns - shapes
the "at the same time" `ながら` never takes - so the form itself usually tells you which
one you are looking at. See the third Contrast section.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒーを飲(の)み<b>ながら</b>話(はな)しましょう。
Let's talk over a coffee.
</pre>

<pre>
歩(ある)き<b>ながら</b>スマホを見(み)ないでください。
Please don't look at your phone while you're walking.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)は鼻歌(はなうた)を歌(うた)い<b>ながら</b>洗(あら)い物(もの)をしている。
My mother is doing the washing-up, humming to herself.
</pre>

<pre>
アルバイトをし<b>ながら</b>大学(だいがく)に通(かよ)っています。
I'm working part-time while going to university.
</pre>

<pre>
残念(ざんねん)<b>ながら</b>、今回(こんかい)の計画(けいかく)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
Regrettably, this time the plan has been called off.
</pre>

<pre>
悪(わる)いと知(し)ってい<b>ながら</b>、嘘(うそ)をついてしまいました。
I lied, even though I knew it was wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
地図(ちず)を見(み)<b>ながら</b>歩(ある)いていたのに、同(おな)じところに戻(もど)ってきてしまった。
I was walking along looking at the map, and still ended up back in the same place.
</pre>

<pre>
先輩(せんぱい)の説明(せつめい)をメモし<b>ながら</b>聞(き)いていたので、あとでゆっくり見直(みなお)すことができました。
I took notes while listening to my senior colleague's explanation, so I could go back over it properly later.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ながら` is neutral and belongs equally to speech and writing. What learners get wrong
is not the register but the **weight**: whichever verb ends the sentence is the one
being reported. テレビを見(み)<b>ながら</b>ごはんを食(た)べる is a sentence about
eating; ごはんを食(た)べ<b>ながら</b>テレビを見(み)る is a sentence about watching
television. Both are correct, and they answer different questions.

The pattern also stretches over long spans of time, not just a single moment.
働(はたら)きながら勉強(べんきょう)する describes years of your life, and this use
is completely standard in a self-introduction or a job interview.

Modern Japanese has turned `ながら` into a noun prefix for doing something *while*
distracted: <code>ながら運転(うんてん)</code> (using your phone at the wheel),
<code>ながら食(た)べ</code>, <code>ながらスマホ</code>. These carry disapproval - they are the
words on the warning poster.

The "although" `ながら` is a different animal in register: it is **written and
formal**, and in conversation it survives mainly in fixed expressions - 
<code>残念(ざんねん)ながら</code> (regrettably), <code>しかしながら</code> (however),
<code>いつもながら</code> (as always), <code>失礼(しつれい)ながら</code> (if I may be so bold).
Adding `も` - <code>狭(せま)いながらも</code> - makes the concession explicit and sounds
more literary still.

## Contrast

### ～ながら vs ～ている間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` needs **one** person doing both things. If the two clauses
have different subjects, you need <code>間(あいだ)に</code>.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが昼寝(ひるね)をしている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、仕事(しごと)を片(かた)づけます。
I'll get my work done while the children are having their nap.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの話(はなし)を聞(き)き<b>ながら</b>、仕事(しごと)をしていました。
I was working while listening to my child talk.
</pre>

In the first, the sleeper and the worker are different people, so `ながら` is
impossible. In the second, one person is doing both. There is a second difference
worth noticing: <code>間(あいだ)に</code> means "at some point during", so the main
action fits inside the other one, while `ながら` means the two run side by side for the
whole time.

### ～ながら vs the て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - て puts one action *after* another, or gives the **means**;
`ながら` puts them on top of each other.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで歩(ある)い<b>て</b>行(い)きます。
I'll walk to the station. (walking is *how* I go)
</pre>

<pre>
歩(ある)き<b>ながら</b>考(かんが)えましょう。
Let's think it over as we walk.
</pre>

The て version answers "how did you get there?"; the `ながら` version answers "what
were you doing at the same time?" Swapping them changes the sentence rather than just
its style: <code>歩(ある)いて考(かんが)える</code> would suggest you walked and then
thought.

### Concessive ～ながら（も）vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "although". `のに` is spoken and carries surprise or
complaint; `ながらも` is written, restrained, and often faintly admiring.

</div>

<pre>
小(ちい)さい店(みせ)<b>ながらも</b>、品(しな)ぞろえは立派(りっぱ)だ。
Small though the shop is, the range it stocks is impressive.
</pre>

<pre>
小(ちい)さい店(みせ)な<b>のに</b>、品(しな)ぞろえがすごいね。
The shop's tiny, but look at everything they've got!
</pre>

Two practical limits on the concessive `ながら`. It attaches only to a short list of
things - a ます-stem, a `ている` form, an い-adjective, a な-adjective stem or a plain
noun - so there is no ❌<code>行(い)ったながら</code>. And with verbs it is almost
always a `ている` form, meaning "knowing full well that…":
<code>知(し)っていながら</code>, <code>分(わ)かっていながら</code>,
<code>見(み)ていながら</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ごはんを食(た)べる**ながら**テレビを見(み)ます。 → ✅ ごはんを食(た)べ**ながら**テレビを見(み)ます。
  `ながら` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 妹(いもうと)がピアノを弾(ひ)き**ながら**、私(わたし)は勉強(べんきょう)しました。 → ✅ 妹(いもうと)がピアノを弾(ひ)いている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、私(わたし)は勉強(べんきょう)しました。
  Two different people, so `ながら` cannot join them. The sentence as written claims
  your sister was studying.

- ❌ 電気(でんき)をつけ**ながら**出(で)かけました。 → ✅ 電気(でんき)をつけた**まま**出(で)かけました。
  <code>つける</code> is instantaneous - the light goes on and that's that. For "leaving
  something in a state", the word is `まま`.

- ❌ 歩(ある)き**ながらでした**、話(はな)しました。 → ✅ 歩(ある)き**ながら**話(はな)しました。
  `ながら` never takes tense or politeness. Both live on the final verb.

## Practice

**1.** 音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き____、宿題(しゅくだい)をしています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>ながら</b>、宿題(しゅくだい)をしています。

One person, two overlapping activities, and the homework is the main event - exactly
what `ながら` is for. The stem is <code>聞(き)き</code>, from
<code>聞(き)きます</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 赤(あか)ちゃんが寝(ね)ている（ながら・間(あいだ)に）、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)ってきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

赤(あか)ちゃんが寝(ね)ている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)ってきます。

The baby sleeps and you shop - two different subjects, so `ながら` is out.
<code>間(あいだ)に</code> also gives the right meaning: the shopping happens somewhere
inside the nap, not for the whole of it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I studied Japanese while working at a hotel."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ホテルで働(はたら)き<b>ながら</b>日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)しました。

`ながら` happily covers a long stretch of life, not just a single moment. The past
tense goes on <code>勉強(べんきょう)しました</code> only - <code>働(はたら)きながら</code>
stays as it is.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けながら寝(ね)てしまいました。（intended: "I fell asleep with the window open."）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けた<b>まま</b>寝(ね)てしまいました。

<code>開(あ)ける</code> takes a moment and is then over, so it cannot run alongside
sleeping. What you mean is that the window stayed open, and that is `まま`. As written,
the sentence describes you opening the window and sleeping simultaneously.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) テレビを見(み)**ながら**ごはんを食(た)べます。
(b) ごはんを食(た)べ**ながら**テレビを見(み)ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both describe the same scene, but they report different things. (a) is a sentence
about a meal - television is the background noise, and it would answer "how do you
usually eat dinner?"

(b) is a sentence about watching television, with the meal as the accompaniment. You
would say it to explain how you spend your evenings, or to admit that dinner is not
getting your full attention.

</details>

# ～ても

**Meaning** · even if; even though - the second half happens anyway

`～ても` sets up a condition and then knocks it down. Whatever is in the `ても` clause
would normally be expected to change the outcome, and the sentence says it does not.
Depending on context it covers "even if it happens" (a hypothesis) and "even when it
happens" (a repeated fact).

## Formation

The mechanics are simple: build the て-form, add `も`. The only thing to watch is that
adjectives and nouns have their own て-forms.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + も | 行(い)く → 行(い)っても |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + も | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かなくても |
| い-adjective | くて + も | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)くても |
| い-adjective (negative) | くなくて + も | 高(たか)くない → 高(たか)くなくても |
| な-adjective | で + も | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)でも |
| Noun | で + も | 雨(あめ) → 雨(あめ)でも |
| Noun / な-adjective (negative) | じゃなくて + も | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)じゃなくても |
| Question word | question word + ても | どんなに → どんなに待(ま)っても |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no tense ever appears before `ても`. Past, future and habit are all
decided by the final verb: ❌<code>降(ふ)ったても</code>, ✅<code>降(ふ)っても</code>
for "even if it rains" *and* for "even though it rained".

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - you already know this pattern from N5 without knowing it. `～てもいい`
("you may") is literally "even if you do, it is fine", and `～なくてもいい` ("you
don't have to") is "even if you don't, it is fine". Same construction, with an
adjective supplied for the second half.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
寒(さむ)く<b>ても</b>、毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っています。
Even when it's cold, I run every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、山(やま)に登(のぼ)ります。
Even if it rains tomorrow, we're climbing the mountain.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)が<b>なくても</b>間(ま)に合(あ)いますよ。
You'll make it even without rushing.
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)<b>でも</b>開(あ)いている店(みせ)を探(さが)しています。
I'm looking for a shop that's open even on Sundays.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら説明(せつめい)し<b>ても</b>、彼(かれ)は分(わ)かってくれません。
No matter how much I explain, he just won't understand.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)を食(た)べ<b>ても</b>おいしく感(かん)じないので、少(すこ)し休(やす)むことにしました。
Nothing tastes good to me at the moment, so I've decided to take a short break.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに練習(れんしゅう)し<b>ても</b>、兄(あに)には一度(いちど)も勝(か)てませんでした。
However much I practised, I never once beat my older brother.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)の費用(ひよう)が高(たか)く<b>ても</b>、一度(いちど)は海外(かいがい)で生活(せいかつ)してみたいと思(おも)っています。
Even if studying abroad is expensive, I'd like to live overseas at least once.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ても` is register-neutral; the politeness of the sentence is set entirely by its final
verb. It is as much at home in a contract as in a conversation.

The casual spoken contraction is `～たって`, and it is everywhere:
<code>行(い)ったって</code>, <code>見(み)たって</code>, <code>高(たか)くたって</code>,
<code>子(こ)どもだって</code>. Verbs build it on the plain past - take
<code>行(い)った</code> and add `って`; い-adjectives use `くたって`
(<code>高(たか)くたって</code>), and nouns and な-adjectives use `だって`. It is
friendly, slightly emphatic, and out of place in writing or with a superior.
「そんなこと、今(いま)言(い)ったって遅(おそ)いよ」 - "It's too late to say that
now."

`ても` pairs up with a small family of adverbs that make the concession explicit, and
using one makes you sound noticeably more fluent:

- **たとえ～ても** - "even supposing", for hypotheticals: たとえ失敗(しっぱい)しても。
- **どんなに～ても／いくら～ても** - "no matter how much".
- **何(なに)を／だれが／どこへ～ても** - "no matter what/who/where".

One caution about the noun form. <code>雨(あめ)でも</code> can be this pattern ("even
in the rain"), but `でも` is *also* an independent word meaning "…or something"
（お茶(ちゃ)でも飲(の)みませんか）and the sentence-initial "but"
（でも、高(たか)いですよ）. Three unrelated things that look identical; the context
always sorts them out, but expect the double-take.

## Contrast

### ～ても vs ～たら・～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たら` and `ば` say the result **depends** on the condition; `ても`
says the result comes anyway. Same slot in the sentence, opposite claims.

</div>

<pre>
安(やす)くなっ<b>たら</b>、買(か)います。
If it gets cheaper, I'll buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)くなっ<b>ても</b>、買(か)いません。
Even if it gets cheaper, I won't buy it.
</pre>

This is the pair to burn into memory, because English "if" hides the difference. A
useful test: if you could insert "in that case" before the main clause, you want
`たら`; if you could insert "all the same", you want `ても`.

### ～ても vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` reports something that **actually happened** and adds
frustration or surprise. `ても` is neutral, and it covers hypotheses and repeated
situations that `のに` cannot.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われます。
The match will go ahead even if it rains.
</pre>

Swapping in `のに` there is impossible - nothing has rained yet, and `のに` cannot talk
about a hypothesis. Where both are available, the difference is attitude:

<pre>
三回(さんかい)メールを送(おく)っ<b>ても</b>、返事(へんじ)が来(き)ません。
I've sent three emails and I still get no reply.
</pre>

<pre>
三回(さんかい)メールを送(おく)った<b>のに</b>、返事(へんじ)が来(き)ません。
I sent three emails and there's *still* no reply.
</pre>

The `ても` version states the situation. The `のに` version complains about it - you can
hear the speaker's irritation, and it would be undiplomatic in front of the person
responsible.

### ～ても vs ～けど・～が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `けど` joins **two facts**; `ても` names a condition that fails to
have its usual effect. Only `ても` can be hypothetical.

</div>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)いです<b>が</b>、日(ひ)あたりがいいです。
This room is small, but it gets good light.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が狭(せま)く<b>ても</b>、日(ひ)あたりがよければ満足(まんぞく)です。
As long as it gets good light, I don't mind a small room.
</pre>

The `が` sentence describes one particular room you have both seen. The `ても` sentence
describes a policy that would hold for any room at all. Note also that `ても` attaches
to a bare stem while `けど・が` follow a complete clause with its own tense.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い**ても**、買(か)います。 → ✅ 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)く**ても**、買(か)います。
  An い-adjective has to become `くて` first. The `い` never survives in front of `も`.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)**ても**出(で)かけます。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)**でも**出(で)かけます。
  Nouns and な-adjectives take `で`, not `て` - the same shape they use for the
  て-form everywhere else.

- ❌ 明日(あした)行(い)く**ても**いいですか。 → ✅ 明日(あした)行(い)っ**ても**いいですか。
  `ても` is built on the て-form, never the dictionary form.

- ❌ 何(なに)**も**言(い)っても、彼(かれ)は怒(おこ)りません。 → ✅ 何(なに)**を**言(い)っても、彼(かれ)は怒(おこ)りません。
  The "no matter what" meaning comes from `ても` itself, so the question word keeps its
  ordinary particle. <code>何(なに)も</code> plus a negative is a different pattern
  entirely.

## Practice

**1.** 安(やす)く____、いらないものは買(か)いません。

Cheapness would normally be a reason to buy, and the sentence overrules it - that is
exactly the `ても` move. <code>安(やす)い</code> becomes <code>安(やす)くて</code> before
`も`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)晴(は)れ（ても・たら）、海(うみ)に行(い)きましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The trip to the sea *depends* on the weather, so this is a plain condition.
<code>晴(は)れても</code> would mean you are going regardless - and then mentioning the

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if you're busy, please eat breakfast."

<code>忙(いそが)しい</code> is an い-adjective, so it goes to <code>忙(いそが)しくて</code>

breakfast against everything else you might skip.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しいても、自分(じぶん)でやってみます。

い-adjectives form `ても` from their くて-form. A quick check: if you can say

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 薬(くすり)を飲(の)ん**でも**、熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がりません。
(b) 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだ**のに**、熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がりませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) describes an ongoing situation flatly: taking the medicine does not bring the
fever down, and it may have happened several times. It is what you would say to a
doctor when reporting symptoms.

(b) points at one occasion that is over and adds an emotional charge - you took the
medicine, so the fever *should* have gone down, and you are frustrated or puzzled that
it did not. `のに` always carries that "but it didn't work out" feeling; `ても` does
not.

</details>

# ～のに - although

**Meaning** · although; even though - and the speaker is not happy about it

`～のに` joins two facts that ought not to fit together, and it always carries the
speaker's reaction to the mismatch: surprise, disappointment, or a flat complaint.
That feeling is not optional. It is the difference between `のに` and the neutral
"but" you already know.

## Formation

`のに` follows a **plain form** - a complete clause, with its own tense. Only nouns and
な-adjectives need a linking `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のに | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つのに |
| Verb (past) | plain past + のに | 待(ま)った → 待(ま)ったのに |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + のに | 待(ま)たない → 待(ま)たないのに |
| い-adjective | plain form + のに | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いのに |
| い-adjective (past) | かった + のに | 安(やす)かった → 安(やす)かったのに |
| な-adjective | な + のに | 元気(げんき) → 元気(げんき)なのに |
| な-adjective (past) | だった + のに | 元気(げんき)だった → 元気(げんき)だったのに |
| Noun | な + のに | 日曜日(にちようび) → 日曜日(にちようび)なのに |
| Noun (past) | だった + のに | 子(こ)どもだった → 子(こ)どもだったのに |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - present-tense nouns and な-adjectives take `な`, never `だ`:
✅<code>静(しず)かなのに</code>, ❌<code>静(しず)かだのに</code>. In the **past** the
`だった` comes back, because `だった` is already a finished form:
<code>静(しず)かだったのに</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)な<b>のに</b>、会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きます。
It's Sunday, and I still have to go in to work.
</pre>

<pre>
たくさん寝(ね)た<b>のに</b>、まだ眠(ねむ)い。
I slept plenty and I'm still sleepy.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)した<b>のに</b>、彼(かれ)は来(こ)なかった。
He promised, and then he didn't come.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は駅(えき)から遠(とお)い<b>のに</b>、いつも混(こ)んでいる。
That shop is miles from the station, yet it's always packed.
</pre>

<pre>
春(はる)な<b>のに</b>、今日(きょう)は真冬(まふゆ)のように寒(さむ)い。
It's spring, and yet today feels like the middle of winter.
</pre>

<pre>
一生懸命(いっしょうけんめい)勉強(べんきょう)した<b>のに</b>、テストの点(てん)はあまりよくなかった。
I studied as hard as I could, and my test score still wasn't much good.
</pre>

<pre>
せっかく作(つく)った<b>のに</b>、誰(だれ)も食(た)べてくれなくて悲(かな)しかったです。
I went to the trouble of making it and nobody would eat any, which was upsetting.
</pre>

<pre>
「体調(たいちょう)が悪(わる)かったんですか。もう少(すこ)し早(はや)く言(い)ってくれればよかった<b>のに</b>。」
"You weren't feeling well? You really should have said something sooner."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`のに` is neither polite nor casual in itself - it attaches to plain forms, and the
politeness of the sentence is decided at the end. What it *is* is **emotionally
loaded**, and that is where it gets you into trouble.

Because `のに` always says "this should not have turned out this way", pointing it at
someone's behaviour is a reproach. お願(ねが)いした<b>のに</b>、まだ
終(お)わっていないんですか is an accusation, however politely the sentence ends. In
front of a customer or a superior, use `が` or `けど` and let the facts speak.

The **sentence-final** `のに` is very common in speech and drops the second clause
entirely, leaving the complaint hanging:

- 「来(き)てくれればよかったのに。」 - "You should have come."
- 「もっと早(はや)く言(い)えばいいのに。」 - "I wish he'd say so sooner."
- 「傘(かさ)、持(も)ってきたのに…」 - "But I brought an umbrella…"

This is the tone of mild reproach or wistfulness - warm between friends, sharp from a
stranger. It combines especially often with `せっかく` ("after going to all that
trouble"), which supplies the disappointment in advance.

`のに` attaches to plain forms. You will occasionally meet <code>～ますのに</code> in
older or very formal writing, but it sounds dated, and it is not a form to produce.

## Contrast

### ～のに vs ～が・～けど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` and `けど` just say "but". `のに` says "but" *and* adds
"which I find surprising, unfair or annoying".

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は古(ふる)いです<b>が</b>、まだ使(つか)えます。
This dictionary is old, but it's still usable.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は古(ふる)い<b>のに</b>、まだ使(つか)えます。
This dictionary is ancient and it still works - remarkable.
</pre>

There is also a hard grammatical limit. `のに` reports what **happened**, so its main
clause cannot be a request, an invitation, a command or a statement of intention. All
of these need `が` or `けど`:

- ❌疲(つか)れたのに、もう少(すこ)し頑張(がんば)ろう。 → ✅疲(つか)れたけど、もう少(すこ)し頑張(がんば)ろう。
- ❌雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っているのに、傘(かさ)を貸(か)してください。 → ✅雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っているので、傘(かさ)を貸(か)してください。

If the end of your sentence is something you are asking for or deciding, `のに` is the
wrong connective.

### ～のに vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` needs a **real** event; `ても` can be a hypothesis. And
`のに` complains, while `ても` merely states.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、明日(あした)は出(で)かけます。
I'm going out tomorrow even if it rains.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた<b>のに</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)たずに出(で)かけました。
It was raining and I went out without an umbrella anyway.
</pre>

There is no ❌<code>降(ふ)るのに、出(で)かけます</code> for "even if it rains" - 
nothing has happened yet, so there is nothing for `のに` to object to. The same reason
explains why `ても` and not `のに` follows question words: いくら待(ま)っても、
どんなに安(やす)くても.

### ～のに "although" vs ～のに "in order to"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - check the form in front. "Although" follows any complete plain
clause - past, negative, な. "In order to" follows a **dictionary-form verb only**, and
the sentence ends in a judgement about difficulty, cost or usefulness.

</div>

<pre>
この道具(どうぐ)は便利(べんり)な<b>のに</b>、あまり売(う)れていない。
This tool is handy, and yet it doesn't sell well. (although)
</pre>

<pre>
この道具(どうぐ)は野菜(やさい)を切(き)る<b>のに</b>便利(べんり)です。
This tool is handy for cutting vegetables. (in order to)
</pre>

Two more tests, if the form does not settle it. The purpose `のに` can be replaced by
<code>ために</code> with almost no change; the concessive one cannot. And the purpose
`のに` never takes a comma before it, while the concessive one usually does.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 元気(げんき)**だのに**、学校(がっこう)を休(やす)みました。 → ✅ 元気(げんき)**なのに**、学校(がっこう)を休(やす)みました。
  な-adjectives link with `な` in the present tense. The same applies to nouns:
  ❌<code>先生(せんせい)のに</code> → ✅<code>先生(せんせい)なのに</code>.

- ❌ 道(みち)が混(こ)んでいた**のに**、遅(おく)れてしまいました。 → ✅ 道(みち)が混(こ)んでいた**ので**、遅(おく)れてしまいました。
  Heavy traffic and arriving late fit together perfectly, so there is no mismatch for
  `のに` to react to. It needs two halves that clash.

- ❌ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る**のに**、試合(しあい)をします。 → ✅ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ**ても**、試合(しあい)をします。
  `のに` cannot describe a hypothetical. For "even if", use `ても`.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)な**のに**、よく働(はたら)きますね。（said to his manager as praise） → ✅ 彼(かれ)は新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)です**が**、よく働(はたら)きますね。
  Grammatical, but `のに` frames the situation as odd or unsatisfactory, which is not
  how you compliment somebody's new recruit.

## Practice

**1.** 夏(なつ)____、今年(ことし)はあまり暑(あつ)くならないですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夏(なつ)<b>なのに</b>、今年(ことし)はあまり暑(あつ)くならないですね。

<code>夏(なつ)</code> is a noun, so it needs `な` before `のに`. The sentence works
because summer and cool weather are supposed to be incompatible - without that
mismatch, `のに` has nothing to do.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)っ（ても・のに）、予定(よてい)どおり出(で)かけます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、予定(よてい)どおり出(で)かけます。

Tomorrow's snow has not happened, and the main clause states what you intend to do.
`のに` fails on both counts: it needs a real event, and it cannot be followed by a
statement of intention.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She studied that hard, and she still failed the exam."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あんなに勉強(べんきょう)した<b>のに</b>、試験(しけん)に落(お)ちてしまいました。

The past-tense plain form <code>勉強(べんきょう)した</code> goes straight in front of
`のに`. <code>あんなに</code> and <code>てしまう</code> both push the disappointment further - 
they are the natural company `のに` keeps.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この部屋(へや)は静(しず)かだのに、家賃(やちん)が安(やす)いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>なのに</b>、家賃(やちん)が安(やす)いです。

<code>静(しず)か</code> is a な-adjective, so the link is `な`. Note that the past tense
behaves differently - <code>静(しず)かだったのに</code> is correct, because `だった` is
a complete form on its own.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この店(みせ)は安(やす)い**けど**、おいしいです。
(b) この店(みせ)は安(やす)い**のに**、おいしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply links two facts with "but". It notes that cheapness and good food do not
usually go together, and leaves it there.

(b) adds the speaker's reaction. It is close to "it's cheap and yet the food is
genuinely good" - mild astonishment, and a recommendation hiding inside it. If you
want to sound impressed rather than merely informative, `のに` is the one.

</details>

# ～のに - in order to

**Meaning** · for doing something - what a thing is good for, and what it costs you

This `のに` is not the "although" one at all. It is the nominaliser `の` plus the
particle `に`: the verb is turned into a noun ("the doing of it") and `に` points at it
as a target. The sentence then judges something against that target - this tool is
handy **for** it, that job took two hours **to** do.

## Formation

Only the **dictionary form** goes in front of it, and the sentence has to end in an
evaluation: a cost, a length of time, or a judgement of usefulness.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + のに | 切(き)る → 切(き)るのに |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + のに | 開(あ)ける → 開(あ)けるのに |
| Verb (する) | する + のに | 予約(よやく)する → 予約(よやく)するのに |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(く)る + のに | 来(く)る → 来(く)るのに |
| With contrast marking | のに + は | 作(つく)る → 作(つく)るのには |

The predicates that finish these sentences come from a short list, and it is worth
learning them as a set:

| Second half | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 使(つか)う | is used for | 紙(かみ)を切(き)るのに使(つか)う |
| 便利(べんり)だ・いい | is handy for | 調(しら)べるのに便利(べんり)だ |
| 役(やく)に立(た)つ | is useful for | 覚(おぼ)えるのに役(やく)に立(た)つ |
| 必要(ひつよう)だ・要(い)る | is needed for | 申(もう)し込(こ)むのに必要(ひつよう)だ |
| （時間(じかん)・お金(かね)が）かかる | takes / costs | 直(なお)すのに時間(じかん)がかかる |
| 向(む)いている | is suited to | 歩(ある)くのに向(む)いている |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no past tense, no negative, no `な` ever appears before this
`のに`. If you are looking at ❌<code>買(か)ったのに使(つか)います</code> or
❌<code>静(しず)かなのに便利(べんり)です</code>, you have accidentally written the
"although" one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
このはさみは紙(かみ)を切(き)る<b>のに</b>使(つか)います。
These scissors are for cutting paper.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで行(い)く<b>のに</b>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)かかります。
It takes half an hour to get to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は漢字(かんじ)を調(しら)べる<b>のに</b>便利(べんり)です。
This dictionary is handy for looking up kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
ビザを取(と)る<b>のに</b>、時間(じかん)もお金(かね)もかかりました。
Getting the visa cost me both time and money.
</pre>

<pre>
この靴(くつ)は山(やま)を歩(ある)く<b>のに</b>向(む)いています。
These shoes are made for walking in the mountains.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)を片(かた)づける<b>のに</b>、一日中(いちにちじゅう)かかってしまった。
Tidying the room ended up taking me the whole day.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい言葉(ことば)を覚(おぼ)える<b>のに</b>、私(わたし)はカードを作(つく)って毎日(まいにち)見(み)るようにしています。
For learning new words, I make cards and try to look at them every day.
</pre>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)を全部(ぜんぶ)確認(かくにん)する<b>のに</b>二時間(にじかん)もかかったので、残業(ざんぎょう)になりました。
Checking all these documents took a full two hours, so I ended up working late.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and unusually even-handed about where it appears: it is the ordinary
way to say what a thing is for, so it turns up in conversation, in instruction
manuals, in product descriptions and in recipes alike.

Two shapes to have ready. The `のに` phrase can be **fronted**, with a comma, when the
purpose is what the sentence is about: 大人数(おおにんずう)で集(あつ)まるのに、
あの店(みせ)はちょうどいいですよ。 And because `の` is a genuine nominaliser, ordinary
particles can stack on it - <code>のには</code> for contrast,
<code>のにも</code> for "for that as well": 作(つく)るのには時間(じかん)が
かかりますが、食(た)べるのは一瞬(いっしゅん)です。

Watch what the sentence is actually judging, because that decides between `のに` and
`のが`. If the thing being described is a **tool, an amount of time or a cost**, you
want `のに`. If it is the **action itself** that is difficult, tiring or fun, you want
`のが`:

- この机(つくえ)は作業(さぎょう)する<b>のに</b>ちょうどいい。 - the desk is being judged.
- 作業(さぎょう)する<b>のが</b>大変(たいへん)だった。 - the work is being judged.

Learners produce ❌<code>覚(おぼ)えるのに難(むずか)しい</code> constantly. Say
<code>覚(おぼ)えるのが難(むずか)しい</code>, or keep `のに` and change the ending to
<code>時間(じかん)がかかる</code>.

## Contrast

### ～のに vs ～ために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ために` is followed by what you **do**; `のに` is followed by an
**assessment**. If the second half is an action you deliberately take, it is `ために`.

</div>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)のために、毎朝(まいあさ)野菜(やさい)ジュースを飲(の)んでいます。
I drink vegetable juice every morning for my health.
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)を保(たも)つ<b>のに</b>、野菜(やさい)ジュースはとてもいいそうです。
Apparently vegetable juice is very good for keeping healthy.
</pre>

The first sentence reports a decision you made and carried out. The second rates the
juice against a purpose. That is why
❌<code>日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)するのに日本(にほん)へ行(い)きます</code>
fails: going to Japan is a deliberate act, not a verdict - it needs `ために`.

There is also a subject rule that catches people out. `ために` normally requires the
same person to have the purpose and do the action; `のに` has no such restriction,
because nobody has to be doing anything at all - 
<code>この道具(どうぐ)は開(あ)けるのに使(つか)われます</code> is fine.

### ～のに vs ～ように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ように` follows a verb you **cannot simply decide to do**:
a potential form, a negative, or a change of state. `のに` follows a plain action.

</div>

<pre>
後(うし)ろの人(ひと)にも見(み)える<b>ように</b>、字(じ)を大(おお)きく書(か)きました。
I wrote the letters big so that the people at the back could see too.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きい字(じ)は遠(とお)くから読(よ)む<b>のに</b>いいですね。
Big letters are good for reading from a distance.
</pre>

`ように` states a desired outcome and is followed by the action taken to bring it
about; `のに` states a purpose and is followed by a judgement. A quick test:
<code>忘(わす)れないように</code> ("so as not to forget") could never become
❌<code>忘(わす)れないのに</code> in that sense, because `のに` takes no negatives.

### ～のに "in order to" vs ～のに "although"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at the verb form. Dictionary form and an evaluation at the
end means "in order to". Anything else - past, negative, `な`, an い-adjective - means
"although".

</div>

<pre>
この袋(ふくろ)は本(ほん)を運(はこ)ぶ<b>のに</b>丈夫(じょうぶ)です。
This bag is sturdy enough for carrying books. (in order to)
</pre>

<pre>
この袋(ふくろ)は丈夫(じょうぶ)な<b>のに</b>、軽(かる)いです。
This bag is sturdy and yet light. (although)
</pre>

The swap test settles the rest. Try `ために` in the gap: if the sentence survives, it is
the purpose one. Try `けど` instead: if *that* survives, it is the concessive one. No
sentence takes both, because the two `のに` want different verb forms in front of them
in the first place.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この机(つくえ)は勉強(べんきょう)した**のに**便利(べんり)です。 → ✅ この机(つくえ)は勉強(べんきょう)する**のに**便利(べんり)です。
  Only the dictionary form goes in front of the purpose `のに`. The past tense turns it
  into the "although" one, and the sentence stops making sense.

- ❌ 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)る**のに**、毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しています。 → ✅ 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)る**ために**、毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しています。
  Studying every day is something you have decided to do, not an assessment of how
  hard university entrance is. Deliberate action takes `ために`.

- ❌ この漢字(かんじ)は覚(おぼ)える**のに**難(むずか)しいです。 → ✅ この漢字(かんじ)は覚(おぼ)える**のが**難(むずか)しいです。
  <code>難(むずか)しい</code> describes the *act* of memorising, so it needs the
  nominaliser `の` as its subject. Keep `のに` only when the sentence is rating a tool,
  or counting time and money.

- ❌ 東京(とうきょう)まで行(い)く**に**三時間(さんじかん)かかります。 → ✅ 東京(とうきょう)まで行(い)く**のに**三時間(さんじかん)かかります。
  A verb cannot take `に` directly. The `の` is what turns it into something a particle
  can attach to.

## Practice

**1.** この袋(ふくろ)は野菜(やさい)を入(い)れる____使(つか)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

野菜(やさい)を入(い)れる<b>のに</b>使(つか)っています。

<code>使(つか)う</code> is the classic partner of this pattern: the bag is being rated
against a job. The verb in front stays in the dictionary form no matter what tense the
sentence ends in.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)る（のに・ために）、毎日(まいにち)夜(よる)遅(おそ)くまで勉強(べんきょう)しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大学(だいがく)に入(はい)る<b>ために</b>、毎日(まいにち)夜(よる)遅(おそ)くまで勉強(べんきょう)しています。

The second half is an action you have chosen and are carrying out, which is `ために`
territory. `のに` would need an evaluation at the end - something like
<code>時間(じかん)がかかります</code> or <code>お金(かね)が必要(ひつよう)です</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It took three hours to fix this computer."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>かかる</code> is the other predicate this pattern lives with. Note the English

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この地図(ちず)アプリは道(みち)を探(さが)したのに便利(べんり)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この地図(ちず)アプリは道(みち)を探(さが)す<b>のに</b>便利(べんり)です。

With the past tense in front, `のに` can only be read as "although", and "although I
looked for the road, it is convenient" is nonsense. The purpose `のに` takes the
dictionary form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 書類(しょるい)を作(つく)る**のに**時間(じかん)がかかりました。
(b) 書類(しょるい)を作(つく)る**のが**大変(たいへん)でした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) measures the job against a cost: the paperwork was the purpose, and time was what
it consumed. The sentence is really about the hours.

(b) makes the act of writing the paperwork the subject and passes judgement on it - it
was hard going. Nothing is being measured; you are describing the experience.

The general rule behind the pair: `のに` for what something costs or is good for,
`のが` for what the activity itself is like.

</details>

# それでも

**Meaning** · but still; and yet - everything just said pointed the other way, and it happened anyway

`それでも` opens a sentence by picking up the whole situation you have just described
and refusing to let it decide the outcome. It concedes the point first, then overrules
it. That two-step is why it sounds firmer, and often more thoughtful, than a bare
"but".

## Usage

`それでも` is a conjunction: it stands at the **head** of a sentence, usually followed
by a comma, and it needs a previous sentence to point back at. The `それ` is doing real
work - it means "that whole state of affairs" - so it cannot open a conversation.

<code>寒(さむ)かった。それでも、歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)った。</code>

It also lives inside a clause when the two halves are joined:
失敗(しっぱい)するかもしれない。それでも、やってみる価値(かち)はある。 The second
sentence is where the speaker's real position is; `それでも` marks everything before it
as acknowledged and set aside.

There is one fixed phrase built on it that behaves differently:
<code>それでもいい</code> / <code>それでもかまわない</code> - "even so, that's fine". Here
`それ` + `でも` is the ordinary "even if" construction, and the phrase is the standard
way to accept a condition you have just been warned about.
「時間(じかん)がかかりますよ。」「それでもいいです。」

Two neighbours to keep separate, because they differ by one syllable and mean the
opposite thing: <code>それで</code> and <code>そこで</code> both introduce a **consequence**
("and so"), while `それでも` introduces the **failure** of a consequence.

## Examples

<pre>
この薬(くすり)はとても苦(にが)いです。<b>それでも</b>、毎日(まいにち)飲(の)んでいます。
This medicine is really bitter. Even so, I take it every day.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は何度(なんど)も失敗(しっぱい)した。<b>それでも</b>、あきらめなかった。
He failed over and over. Still, he never gave up.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)っていた。<b>それでも</b>、子(こ)どもたちは公園(こうえん)へ走(はし)っていった。
It was snowing outside. The children ran off to the park regardless.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は安(やす)いし、残業(ざんぎょう)も多(おお)い。<b>それでも</b>、今(いま)の仕事(しごと)は続(つづ)けたいと思(おも)っています。
The pay is poor and there's a lot of overtime. All the same, I want to stay in this job.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`それでも` is register-neutral and equally at home in an essay and in conversation,
though everyday speech often just says `でも`. Choosing the longer word is not merely
emphasis: `でも` brushes the previous statement aside, while `それでも` picks it up,
weighs it, and then decides anyway. That makes it the polite way to disagree with
someone who has a point.

「おっしゃることはよく分(わ)かります。それでも、私(わたし)は反対(はんたい)です。」

Because of that acknowledging feel, `それでも` frequently follows a `し` clause or a
list of drawbacks - the speaker sets out the case against, and then states what they
are going to do. It also sits naturally in front of a resolution or a value judgement:
<code>それでもやってみます</code>, <code>それでも信(しん)じたい</code>.

Three near neighbours, worth keeping straight:

- **でも** - the everyday "but". Lighter, more casual, and does not necessarily
  concede anything.
- **それなのに** - "and yet", with a complaint attached. It says the outcome was
  *unfair*, where `それでも` merely says it was unexpected.
- **それにもかかわらず** - the same idea in formal written Japanese. Stiff in
  conversation; normal in a report.

And one that is not a neighbour at all: **それに** means "what's more", adding a second
point in the *same* direction. Learners reach for `それでも` when they want `それに`
surprisingly often.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いです。**それでも**、おいしいです。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いです。**それに**、おいしいです。
  Cheap and tasty are both good points, so nothing is being overruled. `それでも` needs
  the second sentence to go against the first.

- ❌ 電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まりました。**それでも**、会社(かいしゃ)に遅(おく)れました。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まりました。**それで**、会社(かいしゃ)に遅(おく)れました。
  Being late is exactly what you would expect after the trains stop. That is a
  consequence, so it takes `それで`.

- ❌ **それでも**、今日(きょう)は会議(かいぎ)があります。（as the opening line of an email） → ✅ 実(じつ)は、今日(きょう)は会議(かいぎ)があります。
  `それ` has to refer to something already said. With nothing in front of it, the
  sentence leaves the reader looking for a missing paragraph.

- ❌ 高(たか)かった**それでも**、買(か)いました。 → ✅ 高(たか)かったです。**それでも**、買(か)いました。／高(たか)かった**けど**、買(か)いました。
  `それでも` is not a clause connective. It starts a new sentence; joining two clauses
  inside one sentence is `けど` or `が`.

## Practice

**1.** 何度(なんど)も注意(ちゅうい)しました。____、彼(かれ)は同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)をくり返(かえ)しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それでも</b>、彼(かれ)は同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)をくり返(かえ)しています。

Being warned repeatedly ought to stop the mistakes, and it has not. `それでも` picks up
that whole first sentence and reports that it made no difference.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 朝(あさ)から熱(ねつ)がありました。（それでも・それで）、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

朝(あさ)から熱(ねつ)がありました。<b>それで</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みました。

Taking the day off is the natural result of having a fever, so this is a consequence - 
`それで`. `それでも` would only work if the second sentence went against expectation,
as in <code>それでも、会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きました</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた。**それでも**、彼女(かのじょ)は待(ま)っていてくれた。
(b) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた。**それなのに**、彼女(かのじょ)は傘(かさ)を持(も)っていなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) acknowledges the rain and then reports something admirable that happened in spite
of it. `それでも` is neutral about blame; here it carries a note of gratitude.

(b) uses `それなのに`, which adds the speaker's dissatisfaction - she *should* have had
an umbrella, and the fact that she did not is being criticised or worried over. Swap
the two connectives and each sentence loses its point.

</details>
