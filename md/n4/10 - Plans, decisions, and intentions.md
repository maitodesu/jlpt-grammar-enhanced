# N4 - Plans, decisions, and intentions


---

# Volitional form - ～(よ)う

**Meaning** · let's do it; I'll do it - the form a verb wears when it carries a
*will*

The volitional is the plain-form counterpart of `ましょう`. It does two jobs that
English keeps apart: proposing something to the people you are with ("let's go"),
and announcing a decision to yourself ("right - I'll go"). It is also the base
that a whole family of N4 patterns is built on, so the table below is worth more
than the page around it: <code>～(よ)うと思(おも)う</code>, <code>～(よ)うとする</code> and
<code>～(よ)うか</code> all need this form and nothing else will do.

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** (五段(ごだん) / godan /
Group 1), **る-verbs** (一段(いちだん) / ichidan / Group 2), and the two
irregulars する and 来(く)る. This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**
throughout.

</div>

## Formation

う-verbs swap the final `-u` vowel for `-o` and add `う`; る-verbs drop `る` and
add `よう`. That is the whole rule, and unlike the て-form there are no sound
changes to memorise.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb, く) | ～く → ～こう | 書(か)く → 書(か)こう |
| Verb (う-verb, ぐ) | ～ぐ → ～ごう | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)ごう |
| Verb (う-verb, す) | ～す → ～そう | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)そう |
| Verb (う-verb, つ) | ～つ → ～とう | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)とう |
| Verb (う-verb, ぬ) | ～ぬ → ～のう | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)のう |
| Verb (う-verb, ぶ) | ～ぶ → ～ぼう | 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)ぼう |
| Verb (う-verb, む) | ～む → ～もう | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)もう |
| Verb (う-verb, る) | ～る → ～ろう | 帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)ろう |
| Verb (う-verb, う) | ～う → ～<b>お</b>う | 買(か)う → 買(か)おう |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る + よう | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べよう |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る + よう | 見(み)る → 見(み)よう |
| Verb (する) | する → しよう | 散歩(さんぽ)する → 散歩(さんぽ)しよう |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(く)る → 来(こ)よう | 来(く)る → 来(こ)よう |
| Polite equivalent | ます-stem + ましょう | 行(い)きます → 行(い)きましょう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two traps are う-verbs whose dictionary form ends in `う`,
and る-verbs that look like う-verbs. <code>買(か)う</code> gives
<code>買(か)おう</code>, never ❌<code>買(か)うう</code>; <code>帰(かえ)る</code> is a
う-verb, so <code>帰(かえ)ろう</code>, not ❌<code>帰(かえ)よう</code>.

</div>

Things that plug straight into this form:

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Checking a proposal | ～(よ)う + か | 座(すわ)ろうか |
| Pressing a proposal | ～(よ)う + よ | 座(すわ)ろうよ |
| Stating an intention | ～(よ)う + と思(おも)う | 座(すわ)ろうと思(おも)う |
| Attempting / being about to | ～(よ)う + とする | 座(すわ)ろうとする |
| Talking to yourself | ～(よ)う + っと（casual） | 座(すわ)ろうっと |

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs that describe things nobody chooses (降(ふ)る, ある, できる,
分(わ)かる) have a volitional form on paper, but it is not used for prediction in
modern spoken Japanese. "It'll probably rain" is <code>降(ふ)るでしょう</code> or
<code>降(ふ)るだろう</code>.

</div>

## Nuance & register

The plain volitional is **casual**. Used as a proposal it belongs with friends,
family, classmates and people below you at work; said to a customer or a
部長(ぶちょう) it is startling. Upward, use `ましょう` - and even `ましょう` can
presume too much, so <code>～ましょうか</code> ("shall I / shall we?") and
<code>～ませんか</code> ("won't you?") are the genuinely safe openers.

Intonation splits the two main uses more reliably than grammar does. Flat or
falling, <code>行(い)こう</code> is a decision you are handing to the group. With
`か` on the end, <code>行(い)こうか</code> hands the decision back to them. Adding
`よ` - <code>行(い)こうよ</code> - pushes, and can sound like coaxing or whining
depending on who is speaking.

Two colloquial tails worth recognising:

- **～(よ)うっと** is a private mutter, not a proposal. Nobody is being invited:
  「あー、もう寝(ね)ようっと。」 It sounds relaxed and slightly childlike, and it
  is common on social media.
- **～(よ)うか迷(まよ)っている** ("I can't decide whether to…") is the standard
  way to say you are torn: 買(か)おうか迷(まよ)っています。

In writing, the volitional turns up as the essayist's "let us":
次(つぎ)に、この問題(もんだい)について考(かんが)えよう。 That is a register
away from the friendly spoken use, but it is the same form. The stiff literary
<code>～であろう</code> ("it may well be that…") is something to recognise in print
and never to say out loud.

`ましょう` has one job the plain form cannot do: it is polite. It cannot, however,
be embedded - <code>～ましょうと思(おも)う</code> is not Japanese. Whenever the
volitional is followed by something, it goes in plain.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)う vs ～ましょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different politeness. `ましょう` is the polite
sentence-final form; `～(よ)う` is the plain one, and it is the only one that can
have something attached after it.

</div>

<pre>
みんなで<b>歌(うた)おう</b>。
Let's all sing.
</pre>

<pre>
みんなで<b>歌(うた)いましょう</b>。
Let's all sing. (polite / addressed to a group you are leading)
</pre>

The moment another clause follows, politeness moves to the end of the sentence
and the volitional reverts to plain:

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)から<b>走(はし)ろう</b>と思(おも)っています。
I'm thinking of taking up running from next week.
</pre>

❌<code>走(はし)りましょうと思(おも)っています</code> is impossible. Politeness is
marked once, at the end.

### ～(よ)う vs ～ませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～(よ)う` and `ましょう` assume the answer is yes.
`～ませんか` genuinely asks, so it is the polite way to invite someone who is free
to refuse.

</div>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはん、いっしょに<b>食(た)べよう</b>。
Let's have lunch together. (we're already doing this)
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはん、いっしょに<b>食(た)べませんか</b>。
Would you like to have lunch together? (a real invitation)
</pre>

Inviting someone for the first time, `ませんか` is almost always the better choice;
`ましょう` after they have agreed is what settles the details. A useful ordering
from most to least presumptuous: <code>行(い)こう</code> →
<code>行(い)きましょう</code> → <code>行(い)きましょうか</code> →
<code>行(い)きませんか</code>. Treat that as a tendency, not a rulebook - tone of
voice moves any of them.

### ～(よ)う vs the plain non-past

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English "will" is usually **not** the volitional. A plain
future statement is just the dictionary form or `ます`; the volitional adds the
feeling of a will being exercised at this moment.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)、病院(びょういん)へ<b>行(い)きます</b>。
I'm going to the hospital tomorrow. (a fact about my schedule)
</pre>

<pre>
やっぱり明日(あした)、病院(びょういん)へ<b>行(い)こう</b>。
You know what - I'll go to the hospital tomorrow. (deciding as I speak)
</pre>

This is why the volitional cannot report the weather, someone else's plans, or
anything that happens without a decision. Those all take the plain form, with
`でしょう` or <code>と思(おも)います</code> if you need to hedge.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 手伝(てつだ)**うよう** → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)**おう**
  A う-verb ending in `う` becomes `おう`, not `うよう`. The `よう` ending belongs
  to る-verbs only.

- ❌ 食(た)べ**ろう** → ✅ 食(た)べ**よう**
  <code>食(た)べる</code> is a る-verb: drop `る`, add `よう`. `ろう` would be the
  う-verb pattern, which is why <code>帰(かえ)る</code> - a う-verb despite its
  looks - really is <code>帰(かえ)ろう</code>.

- ❌ 来(く)る → きよう → ✅ 来(く)る → **こよう**
  The irregular shifts to the `こ` stem, the same one you already use for
  <code>来(こ)ない</code>. Reading the written form <code>来(こ)よう</code> as *kiyou* is a very common
  slip.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)**ろう**。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)る**でしょう**。
  Snow has no intentions. The volitional needs a subject that can choose.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)、コーヒーを飲(の)**もう**。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、コーヒーでもいかがですか。
  Grammatically fine, socially wrong. Do not propose things to a superior in the
  plain volitional.

## Practice

**1.** 荷物(にもつ)が重(おも)いね。タクシーで帰(かえ)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

タクシーで帰(かえ)<b>ろう</b>。

<code>帰(かえ)る</code> is a う-verb, so the final `る` becomes `ろ` and takes `う`.
The `よう` ending would only be right if this were a る-verb.

</details>

**2.** 部長(ぶちょう)に向(む)かって：「そろそろ（出(で)かけよう・出(で)かけましょう）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そろそろ<b>出(で)かけましょう</b>。

The plain volitional is for friends and juniors. Speaking to a
部長(ぶちょう) you need `ましょう`, and if you want to leave him room to say no,
<code>出(で)かけましょうか</code> is softer still.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "It's cold - shall we go inside?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

寒(さむ)いね。中(なか)に<b>入(はい)ろうか</b>。

<code>入(はい)る</code> is a う-verb → <code>入(はい)ろう</code>, and the `か` turns
a flat proposal into a check with the other person. Without `か` it would be "come
on, inside" rather than "shall we?".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)映画(えいが)を見(み)ましょうと思(おも)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)よう</b>と思(おも)っています。

`ましょう` cannot be embedded in a larger sentence. When something follows the
volitional it must be plain, and the politeness is carried by
<code>思(おも)っています</code> at the end.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 映画(えいが)を見(み)よう。
(b) 映画(えいが)を見(み)よう**か**。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a decision presented as already taken - you are telling the group what is
happening, in a friendly way. It also works as a resolution said to yourself.

(b) hands the choice back: "shall we watch a film?" The `か` makes it a genuine
question, so the other person can say no without pushing back against you. In a
group where you are not the one who decides, (b) is the safer sentence.

</details>

# ～(よ)うと思う

**Meaning** · I think I'll ～; I'm thinking of ～ing - an intention, presented as a
thought rather than a fact

<code>～(よ)うと思(おも)う</code> takes the volitional - the "let's / I'll" form - 
and wraps it in "I think". The result is the most natural way in Japanese to
answer *what are you going to do?* It is softer than `つもり` because it frames
the plan as something going on in your head right now, which leaves the door
open. Note the shape: what you are thinking is <code>行(い)こう</code>, "I'll go";
`と` quotes that thought.

## Formation

The verb before `と` is the **plain volitional**, always. `ましょう` cannot be
quoted here.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + と思(おも)う | 習(なら)う → 習(なら)おうと思(おも)う |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + と思(おも)う | 始(はじ)める → 始(はじ)めようと思(おも)う |
| Verb (する) | しよう + と思(おも)う | 予約(よやく)する → 予約(よやく)しようと思(おも)う |
| Verb (来(く)る) | こよう + と思(おも)う | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようと思(おも)う |
| Polite | 思(おも)う → 思(おも)います | 習(なら)おうと思(おも)います |
| Intention already held | ～(よ)うと思(おも)っている | 習(なら)おうと思(おも)っています |
| Intention in the past | ～(よ)うと思(おも)った | 習(なら)おうと思(おも)った |
| Softer still | ～(よ)うかと思(おも)っている | 習(なら)おうかと思(おも)っています |
| Rejecting the idea | ～(よ)うとは思(おも)わない | 習(なら)おうとは思(おも)わない |
| As a reason | ～(よ)うと思(おも)って、… | 習(なら)おうと思(おも)って、申(もう)し込(こ)みました |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - there is no way to say "I'm thinking of **not** doing it" with
a negative volitional in ordinary speech. Use
<code>～のはやめようと思(おも)う</code> ("I'm thinking of giving up ～ing") or
<code>～ないことにしようと思(おも)う</code>. The literary negative volitional
<code>～まいと思(おも)う</code> exists but belongs in novels.

</div>

## Nuance & register

The whole point of this pattern is **softness**. `つもり` states a settled plan;
<code>～(よ)うと思(おも)います</code> reports a thought, and a thought can change
without anyone losing face. That makes it the default answer to a superior, a
customer, or anyone whose reaction you have not heard yet.

The `ています` layer does most of the fine-tuning:

- <code>～(よ)うと思(おも)います</code> - the decision is being taken **as you
  speak**. It fits a reply on the spot: 「じゃあ、そうしようと思(おも)います。」
- <code>～(よ)うと思(おも)っています</code> - you have been holding this intention
  for a while. It sounds more considered and, in practice, closer to a real plan.
  In a job interview or a meeting this is the form you want.

The same `ています` is what lets you talk about **other people**. Exactly as with
<code>思(おも)う</code> generally, a bare
<code>～(よ)うと思(おも)う</code> claims the thought is yours; someone else's
intention needs <code>思(おも)っている</code>, or evidence marking
（<code>思(おも)っているようです</code>,
<code>思(おも)っていると言(い)っていました</code>）.

One structural point that catches people: the person doing the volitional verb
must be the same person doing the thinking. You can say
私(わたし)が手伝(てつだ)おうと思(おも)います ("I'll help"), but you cannot
put someone else in there - ❌<code>彼(かれ)が来(こ)ようと思(おも)います</code>
is impossible. To talk about what you want *others* to do, you need a different
pattern altogether.

Register is otherwise neutral. Plain
<code>行(い)こうと思(おも)う</code> is everyday casual speech; in stiffer
written Japanese you will meet <code>～(よ)うと考(かんが)えております</code> and
<code>～たいと存(ぞん)じます</code>, which do the same softening job in keigo.
Adding `か` - <code>～(よ)うかと思(おも)っています</code> - softens further, to
roughly "I've half a mind to…", and is very useful when you genuinely have not
decided.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うと思(おも)う vs ～つもりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～(よ)うと思(おも)う</code> is a decision **being made
or reported now**; `つもり` is a decision **already settled**. Neither one is
English "will".

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、一人(ひとり)で住(す)もうと思(おも)っています。
I'm thinking of living on my own next year.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、一人(ひとり)で<b>住(す)むつもりです</b>。
I intend to live on my own next year.
</pre>

The first invites a response - the listener could still say "are you sure?" and
not be contradicting you. The second reports a fixed position, and pushing back
against it means arguing. This is why
<code>～(よ)うと思(おも)います</code> is the safer thing to say when your plan
touches someone else's, and why `つもり` is the right choice when you want to be
believed.

There is also a hard difference: `つもり` can be denied
（<code>行(い)くつもりはありません</code>）and questioned about others
（<code>どうするつもりですか</code>, which sounds like an interrogation). The
<code>思(おも)う</code> pattern has no equivalent edge to it.

### ～(よ)うと思(おも)う vs ～と思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **volitional** + <code>と思(おも)う</code> = my intention.
**Plain form** + <code>と思(おも)う</code> = my opinion or prediction about how
things will go.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)も<b>行(い)こうと思(おも)います</b>。
I think I'll go too. (I'm deciding to go)
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんも<b>行(い)くと思(おも)います</b>。
I think Tanaka will go too. (my guess about him)
</pre>

Get this pair wrong and the sentence does not become ungrammatical - it becomes a
different statement, which is worse. Saying
<code>私(わたし)も行(い)くと思(おも)います</code> about yourself sounds
oddly detached, as though you were predicting your own behaviour from the
outside. It is occasionally said, but it is not how you volunteer.

Because the plain form is for prediction, it is also what you use for events
nobody decides: 明日(あした)は晴(は)れると思(おも)います.

### ～(よ)うと思(おも)います vs ～(よ)うと思(おも)っています

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>思(おも)います</code> is the thought arriving now;
<code>思(おも)っています</code> is a thought you have been carrying.

</div>

<pre>
それはいい案(あん)ですね。じゃあ、そう<b>しようと思(おも)います</b>。
That's a good idea. All right, I'll do that.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)から会社(かいしゃ)を<b>辞(や)めようと思(おも)っていました</b>。
I'd been thinking about leaving the company for a while.
</pre>

In the first, the decision is a response to what was just said, so
<code>思(おも)います</code> is right and
<code>思(おも)っています</code> would sound like you had planned it in advance.
In the second, the `ていました` is doing real work: it says the intention predates
this conversation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)き**ましょう**と思(おも)います。 → ✅ 行(い)**こう**と思(おも)います。
  Only the plain volitional can be quoted with `と`. The politeness sits on
  <code>思(おも)います</code>.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めようと思(おも)います。 → ✅ …辞(や)めようと思(おも)って**います**。
  A bare <code>思(おも)う</code> asserts your own mind. For a third person use
  <code>思(おも)っている</code>, or hedge with
  <code>思(おも)っているようです</code>.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ろうと思(おも)います。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ると思(おも)います。
  Weather has no volition, so it cannot take the volitional. Plain form +
  <code>と思(おも)う</code> is prediction.

- ❌ 買(か)わないようと思(おも)います。 → ✅ 買(か)うのはやめようと思(おも)います。
  There is no everyday negative volitional. Negate the *plan*, not the verb.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)に手伝(てつだ)おうと思(おも)います。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)に手伝(てつだ)ってもらおうと思(おも)います。
  The volitional describes what **you** will do. If someone else does the acting,
  the sentence has to be rebuilt around that.

## Practice

**1.** 来年(らいねん)、中国語(ちゅうごくご)を習(なら)____と思(おも)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

習(なら)<b>おう</b>と思(おも)っています。

<code>習(なら)う</code> is a う-verb ending in `う`, so the volitional is
<code>習(なら)おう</code>. The `ています` says this is an intention you have been
holding, not one formed at this second.

</details>

**2.** 面接(めんせつ)で：「卒業(そつぎょう)したら、どうしますか。」
「（働(はたら)くつもりです・働(はたら)こうと思(おも)っています）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

働(はたら)<b>こうと思(おも)っています</b>。

Both are grammatical, but in an interview the softer form is the safer register:
it reports a considered intention without sounding as though your mind is closed.
`つもりです` is not rude, only firmer, and it invites the follow-up "and if that
doesn't work out?"

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm thinking of taking a day off next week."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来週(らいしゅう)、休(やす)みを<b>取(と)ろうと思(おも)っています</b>。

<code>取(と)る</code> is a う-verb → <code>取(と)ろう</code>. Because this is a
plan you are floating rather than announcing,
<code>思(おも)っています</code> is the natural choice; it is also the standard
way to raise leave with a manager.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 弟(おとうと)は大学(だいがく)で医学(いがく)を勉強(べんきょう)しようと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 弟(おとうと)は大学(だいがく)で医学(いがく)を勉強(べんきょう)しようと<b>思(おも)っています</b>。

You cannot state a third person's inner decision with a bare
<code>思(おも)う</code>. <code>思(おも)っている</code> presents it as a state
you have observed. <code>…と言(い)っていました</code> would work too, and marks
where the information came from.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)、銀行(ぎんこう)へ行(い)こうと思(おも)います。
(b) 明日(あした)、銀行(ぎんこう)へ行(い)くと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is your own intention: the volitional says the going is your decision, and
<code>と思(おも)います</code> presents it gently. This is what you say when
someone asks about your plans.

(b) predicts. With the plain form, <code>と思(おも)います</code> gives an
opinion about what will happen - most naturally about somebody else, and about
yourself it sounds like you are guessing at your own future rather than choosing
it. Learners produce (b) by translating "I think I'll go", which is exactly the
wrong route.

</details>

# ～(よ)うとする

**Meaning** · to try to do something; to be on the point of doing it

<code>～(よ)うとする</code> takes the volitional - the will to do something - and
puts it into a verb. It catches the moment *before* the action succeeds: the arm
reaching for the handle, the sun touching the horizon. That is why the pattern so
often ends in a "but": you tried, and something got in the way.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + とする | 立(た)つ → 立(た)とうとする |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + とする | 逃(に)げる → 逃(に)げようとする |
| Verb (する) | しよう + とする | 出発(しゅっぱつ)する → 出発(しゅっぱつ)しようとする |
| Verb (来(く)る) | こよう + とする | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようとする |
| Attempt in the past | ～(よ)うとした | 立(た)とうとした |
| Attempt that failed | ～(よ)うとしたが／したけれど | 立(た)とうとしたが、動(うご)けなかった |
| At the moment of | ～(よ)うとしたとき／したところ | 立(た)とうとしたとき |
| On the verge | ～(よ)うとしている | 沈(しず)もうとしている |
| Refusing to | ～(よ)うとしない | 立(た)とうとしない |
| Polite | とする → とします | 立(た)とうとしました |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `する` is the ordinary verb here, so it conjugates normally.
Everything before `と` is frozen in the plain volitional:
開(あ)けようと**しました**, never ❌開(あ)け**ましょう**とした.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
赤(あか)ちゃんが<b>立(た)とうとしている</b>。
The baby is trying to stand up.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べようとしない</b>。
My little brother won't even try to eat vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
ドアを<b>開(あ)けようとした</b>ら、かぎがかかっていました。
I went to open the door and found it locked.
</pre>

<pre>
日(ひ)が<b>沈(しず)もうとしている</b>。
The sun is about to set.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)に<b>乗(の)ろうとした</b>とき、後(うし)ろから名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれた。
Just as I was getting on the train, someone called my name from behind.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)説明(せつめい)しても、彼(かれ)は話(はなし)を<b>聞(き)こうとしなかった</b>。
No matter how many times I explained, he refused to listen.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)を棚(たな)に<b>上(あ)げようとしました</b>が、重(おも)すぎて一人(ひとり)では無理(むり)でした。
I tried to lift my bag onto the rack, but it was too heavy to manage on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が<b>始(はじ)まろうとしていた</b>ので、急(いそ)いで席(せき)に着(つ)きました。
The meeting was on the point of starting, so I hurried to my seat.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Three uses live under one form, and they behave differently.

**1. The interrupted attempt.** In the past tense,
<code>～(よ)うとした</code> almost always implies that the action did *not* come
off, or that something cut across it. If you tried and succeeded, Japanese just
reports the success: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けました. This is a real difference from
English, where "I tried to open the window" can be modest understatement for "I
opened it". Here it means the window did not open.

**2. On the verge.** <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> and
<code>～(よ)うとしたとき</code> mark the instant before something happens, and this
is the one use that works with subjects that have no will at all:
台風(たいふう)が上陸(じょうりく)しようとしている,
一年(いちねん)が終(お)わろうとしている. It has a slightly heightened,
narrative feel - common in news writing and novels, less so in casual chat, where
<code>もうすぐ</code> or `ところ` does the job.

**3. Refusal.** <code>～(よ)うとしない</code> says someone is unwilling, not merely
that they are not doing it. It carries a note of complaint, so it is comfortable
about children, animals and people you are annoyed with, and risky about a
superior: <code>部長(ぶちょう)は決(き)めようとしません</code> is close to
saying the boss is being obstructive.

A restriction worth internalising: for **your own ongoing effort**, the plain
non-past <code>～(よ)うとする</code> is unnatural.
❌<code>毎日(まいにち)運動(うんどう)しようとしています</code> should be
<code>運動(うんどう)するようにしています</code>. Reserve
<code>～(よ)うとする</code> for a single moment of trying, not a standing policy.
The plain non-past does appear in general statements about people - 
人(ひと)はだれでも楽(らく)をしようとする - where it is descriptive rather
than personal.

Register is neutral. `する` carries the politeness, so
<code>開(あ)けようとしました</code> is as polite as its ending, and nothing
about the pattern is formal or slangy in itself.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うとする vs ～てみる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～(よ)うとする</code> is the **attempt**, which may
never get off the ground. `～てみる` is **doing it to find out** what happens - 
the action really takes place.

</div>

This pair is the classic confusion, because English uses "try" for both.

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けようとした</b>が、固(かた)くて動(うご)かなかった。
I tried to open the window, but it was stiff and wouldn't budge.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けてみた</b>ら、涼(すず)しい風(かぜ)が入(はい)ってきた。
I opened the window to see, and a cool breeze came in.
</pre>

In the first, the window stayed shut. In the second it opened - the "trying" was
about the *result being unknown*, not about whether the act was possible. So
納豆(なっとう)を食(た)べてみました means you ate some and now you know what
it tastes like, while
納豆(なっとう)を食(た)べようとしました means you got as far as raising it to
your mouth.

<div class="note">

**Note** - `てみる` cannot express failure, and
<code>～(よ)うとする</code> cannot express curiosity. Neither can substitute for
the other, and the two can be stacked when both ideas are present:
食(た)べてみようとしました - "I made an attempt at tasting it."

</div>

### ～(よ)うとしている vs ～ところだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "about to". `ところだ` is the everyday, neutral one
and needs a subject with a plan. <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> is more vivid, and
it works for events nobody chose.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)、出(で)かける<b>ところです</b>。
I'm just about to head out.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が<b>出発(しゅっぱつ)しようとしている</b>。
The train is on the point of departing.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences get strange. <code>出(で)かけようとしています</code>
about yourself sounds like you are narrating your own life; a train has no
`ところ` of its own to be at.

### ～(よ)うとしない vs the plain negative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ない` reports that something is not happening.
<code>～(よ)うとしない</code> says the person is **refusing to even try**.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)まない</b>。
My child isn't taking the medicine.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)もうとしない</b>。
My child won't take the medicine. (clamps his mouth shut every time)
</pre>

The second is a complaint. Because it attributes stubbornness, it is common in
speech about kids and pets and best avoided about people you are supposed to
defer to.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ドアを開(あ)け**るとした** → ✅ ドアを開(あ)け**ようとした**
  `とする` attaches to the volitional, not the dictionary form. The dictionary
  form with `とする` is a different, much more advanced pattern meaning "supposing
  that".

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えようとしています。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えるようにしています。
  A standing effort of your own is <code>～ようにする</code>.
  <code>～(よ)うとする</code> is one moment of trying, not a habit.

- ❌ ケーキを作(つく)ろうとしました。おいしかったです。 → ✅ ケーキを作(つく)ってみました。おいしかったです。
  If the cake got made and eaten, the attempt succeeded, so
  <code>～(よ)うとした</code> is the wrong verb. Use `てみる`, or simply
  <code>作(つく)りました</code>.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)はまだ来(こ)ようとしません。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)はまだ来(き)ていません。
  <code>～(よ)うとしない</code> accuses the teacher of refusing to come. For a
  plain statement that someone has not arrived, use `ている` with a negative.

## Practice

**1.** バスに乗(の)____とした時(とき)、さいふがないことに気(き)がついた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

バスに乗(の)<b>ろう</b>とした時(とき)…

<code>乗(の)る</code> is a う-verb, so the volitional is
<code>乗(の)ろう</code>. <code>～(よ)うとしたとき</code> pins the sentence to the
instant before boarding - which is exactly when you would notice the wallet was
gone.

</details>

**2.** 新(あたら)しいカレーの店(みせ)へ行(い)って、（食(た)べようとしました・食(た)べてみました）。とてもおいしかったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

食(た)べ<b>てみました</b>。

The food was eaten and judged delicious, so the action succeeded. `てみる` is
"did it to find out what it was like". <code>食(た)べようとしました</code> would
say you never got it down.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I tried to phone her, but the line was busy."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)に<b>電話(でんわ)しようとした</b>が、話(はなし)中(ちゅう)でした。

The call never connected, so this is an attempt cut short - 
<code>～(よ)うとした</code>, not `てみた`. Note that `する` verbs give
<code>しよう</code>: 電話(でんわ)する → 電話(でんわ)しよう.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 健康(けんこう)のために、毎朝(まいあさ)野菜(やさい)ジュースを飲(の)もうとしています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 健康(けんこう)のために、毎朝(まいあさ)野菜(やさい)ジュースを<b>飲(の)むようにしています</b>。

<code>毎朝(まいあさ)</code> makes this a habit you are maintaining, and habits
of effort take <code>～ようにしている</code>.
<code>～(よ)うとしている</code> would describe a single moment - you, right now,
with the glass halfway to your lips.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は質問(しつもん)に答(こた)えなかった。
(b) 彼(かれ)は質問(しつもん)に答(こた)えようとしなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports that no answer came. He might have been asleep, absent, or
unable to hear the question; the sentence takes no position on why.

(b) puts his will in the frame: he was asked, he understood, and he would not
make the attempt. It reads as criticism, and a listener will hear the speaker's
irritation in it. That extra layer is the whole point of
<code>～(よ)うとしない</code>.

</details>

# ～予定だ

**Meaning** · to be scheduled to; to be due to - the plan is on a calendar, not
just in your head

予定(よてい) is an ordinary noun meaning *schedule* or *arrangement*, so
<code>行(い)く予定(よてい)です</code> says literally "the arrangement is that
I go". That is why it feels so different from `つもり`, which reports what you have
decided: <code>予定(よてい)</code> points at something outside you - a date, a
booking, a timetable other people can also see.

## Formation

The verb in front stays **plain**, exactly as it does before `つもり`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + 予定(よてい)だ | 着(つ)く → 着(つ)く予定(よてい)だ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 予定(よてい)だ | 出(で)ない → 出(で)ない予定(よてい)だ |
| Noun | Noun + の + 予定(よてい)だ | 出張(しゅっちょう) → 出張(しゅっちょう)の予定(よてい)だ |
| Polite | 予定(よてい)だ → 予定(よてい)です | 着(つ)く予定(よてい)です |
| Arrangement that changed | 予定(よてい)だった／でした | 着(つ)く予定(よてい)でした |
| No such arrangement | ～予定(よてい)はない／はありません | 行(い)く予定(よてい)はありません |
| Honorific (asking others) | ご予定(よてい) | ご予定(よてい)はいかがですか |
| As a plain noun | 予定(よてい)がある／を立(た)てる／が変(か)わる | 週末(しゅうまつ)は予定(よてい)があります |
| Fixed phrase | 予定(よてい)どおり | 予定(よてい)どおりに進(すす)む |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>行(い)きます予定(よてい)です</code> is the same
error as ❌<code>行(い)きますつもりです</code>. A noun is being modified, and a
noun-modifying clause is always plain.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に<b>終(お)わる予定(よてい)です</b>。
The meeting is scheduled to finish at three.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)、大阪(おおさか)へ<b>出張(しゅっちょう)する予定(よてい)です</b>。
I'm due to travel to Osaka on business next week.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の午後(ごご)は健康(けんこう)診断(しんだん)<b>の予定(よてい)です</b>。
Tomorrow afternoon is down for a health check.
</pre>

<pre>
今月(こんげつ)は忙(いそが)しいので、旅行(りょこう)に<b>行(い)く予定(よてい)はありません</b>。
I'm busy this month, so I have no trips scheduled.
</pre>

<pre>
飛行機(ひこうき)は十時(じゅうじ)に<b>着(つ)く予定(よてい)でした</b>が、一時間(いちじかん)遅(おく)れました。
The plane was due in at ten, but it was an hour late.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)、来週(らいしゅう)の<b>ご予定(よてい)</b>を教(おし)えていただけますか。
Could you let me know your schedule for next week, sir?
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)の工事(こうじ)は来年(らいねん)の春(はる)に<b>終(お)わる予定(よてい)だった</b>が、雨(あめ)が多(おお)くて遅(おく)れている。
The work in front of the station was supposed to finish next spring, but there's been a lot of rain and it's behind.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません、新(あたら)しい商品(しょうひん)は来月(らいげつ)に<b>入(はい)る予定(よてい)になっております</b>。
I'm sorry - the new stock is scheduled to arrive next month.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>予定(よてい)</code> is **impersonal by design**, and that is its whole
value. It reports an arrangement without saying whose will produced it, so it can
describe a plane, a shop's opening hours, a company's project or your own Tuesday
with the same words. `つもり`, by contrast, can only ever describe a person's
intention.

That neutrality makes it the polite register for other people's plans. Asking a
superior <code>ご予定(よてい)はいかがでしょうか</code> is safe; asking about
their `つもり` sounds like an interrogation. In business Japanese the noun turns up
constantly on its own - <code>予定(よてい)を立(た)てる</code> (draw up a
schedule), <code>予定(よてい)を変更(へんこう)する</code> (reschedule),
<code>予定(よてい)どおり</code> (as planned) - and
<code>～予定(よてい)となっております</code> is the standard humble phrasing for
telling a customer when something will happen.

Two limits to keep straight:

- **An arrangement is not a forecast.** Something has to have been *arranged* by
  someone. ❌<code>明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る予定(よてい)です</code>
  is wrong; weather takes <code>予報(よほう)</code>, `でしょう` or `そうです`. A
  train arriving at 10:04 is fine, because a timetable is an arrangement.
- **<code>予定(よてい)でした</code> implies it did not go that way.** Like
  <code>つもりだった</code>, the past tense sets up a "but". If the plan was kept,
  say <code>予定(よてい)どおり終(お)わりました</code>.

Because <code>予定(よてい)</code> keeps you out of the sentence, it can also be
used to duck. Answering
<code>参加(さんか)する予定(よてい)です</code> instead of
<code>参加(さんか)します</code> quietly leaves room for the schedule to change
 - useful, and occasionally heard as non-committal.

## Contrast

### ～予定(よてい)だ vs ～つもりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>予定(よてい)</code> is what is booked;
`つもり` is what you have decided. If nothing exists outside your own head, it is
not a <code>予定(よてい)</code>.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、京都(きょうと)で友(とも)だちに<b>会(あ)う予定(よてい)です</b>。
I'm meeting a friend in Kyoto next month. (arranged, date agreed)
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、京都(きょうと)で友(とも)だちに<b>会(あ)うつもりです</b>。
I mean to see a friend in Kyoto next month. (my plan; nothing fixed yet)
</pre>

The corollary is that <code>予定(よてい)</code> does not carry determination.
Pairing it with a word like <code>絶対(ぜったい)に</code> or
<code>今年(ことし)こそ</code> is odd, because those express resolve and a
schedule has none. Resolve is `つもり` territory.

### ～予定(よてい)だ vs ～(よ)うと思(おも)っている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three degrees of firmness:
<code>～(よ)うと思(おも)っています</code> (a thought) → `つもりです` (a decision)
→ <code>予定(よてい)です</code> (an entry in the diary).

</div>

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みは北海道(ほっかいどう)へ<b>行(い)こうと思(おも)っています</b>。
I'm thinking of going to Hokkaido in the summer holidays.
</pre>

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みは北海道(ほっかいどう)へ<b>行(い)く予定(よてい)です</b>。
I'm going to Hokkaido in the summer holidays. (tickets are booked)
</pre>

Answering a colleague's casual question with the second when nothing is booked
overstates your case; answering a travel agent with the first understates it.
Match the form to how real the plan is.

### ～予定(よてい)だ vs ～ことになっている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>予定(よてい)</code> is a **dated item**:
this happens, then. <code>ことになっている</code> is a **standing arrangement or
rule**: this is how it is done.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の月曜日(げつようび)に試験(しけん)を<b>受(う)ける予定(よてい)です</b>。
I'm scheduled to sit the exam next Monday.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)では靴(くつ)を<b>脱(ぬ)ぐことになっています</b>。
You're supposed to take your shoes off in this room.
</pre>

Both hide the person who decided, which is why they feel related.
<code>予定(よてい)</code> attaches to a point in time and can be missed;
<code>ことになっている</code> attaches to a practice and can be broken.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)、帰(かえ)り**ます**予定(よてい)です。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、帰(かえ)**る**予定(よてい)です。
  <code>予定(よてい)</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it is plain.
  The politeness goes once, on `です`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)る予定(よてい)です。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)るそうです。
  Nobody schedules snow. <code>予定(よてい)</code> needs an arrangement behind
  it; a forecast is <code>予報(よほう)</code>.

- ❌ 来月(らいげつ)は引(ひ)っ越(こ)し予定(よてい)です。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)は引(ひ)っ越(こ)し**の**予定(よてい)です。
  Two nouns are joined with `の`. This is the same rule that gives
  <code>冗談(じょうだん)のつもり</code>.

- ❌ 今年(ことし)こそ、たばこをやめる予定(よてい)です。 → ✅ 今年(ことし)こそ、たばこをやめるつもりです。
  <code>今年(ことし)こそ</code> is a declaration of will, and a schedule has no
  will. Determination belongs with `つもり`.

- ❌ 先週(せんしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)に出(で)る予定(よてい)でした。（and you did go） → ✅ 先週(せんしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)には予定(よてい)どおり出(で)ました。
  The past <code>予定(よてい)でした</code> almost always sets up a "but" - it
  says the plan was **not** kept. If it was, <code>予定(よてい)どおり</code> is
  the phrase you want.

## Practice

**1.** この仕事(しごと)は金曜日(きんようび)までに終(お)わ____予定(よてい)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

終(お)わ<b>る</b>予定(よてい)です。

<code>予定(よてい)</code> is a noun and takes a plain, dictionary-form clause in
front of it. ❌<code>終(お)わります予定(よてい)</code> is the standard learner
slip.

</details>

**2.** 旅行(りょこう)会社(がいしゃ)で：「ホテルはもう取(と)りました。来月(らいげつ)の五日(いつか)に（出発(しゅっぱつ)するつもりです・出発(しゅっぱつ)する予定(よてい)です）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来月(らいげつ)の五日(いつか)に<b>出発(しゅっぱつ)する予定(よてい)です</b>。

The hotel is booked and the date is fixed, so the plan exists outside your head - 
that is precisely what <code>予定(よてい)</code> reports. `つもり` would suggest
you had merely decided, which understates a booking.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The train was due to leave at 8:20, but it hasn't come yet."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)は八時(はちじ)二十分(にじゅっぷん)に<b>出(で)る予定(よてい)でした</b>が、まだ来(き)ません。

A timetable is an arrangement, so a train can have a
<code>予定(よてい)</code>. The past <code>予定(よてい)でした</code> is doing
its usual work here: it signals that the schedule was not kept.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)の木曜日(もくようび)は、会議(かいぎ)予定(よてい)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)の木曜日(もくようび)は、会議(かいぎ)<b>の</b>予定(よてい)です。

<code>会議(かいぎ)</code> is a noun, and one noun modifies another through `の`.
Only a **verb** clause attaches to <code>予定(よてい)</code> directly, and then
only in plain form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 来年(らいねん)、留学(りゅうがく)する予定(よてい)です。
(b) 来年(らいねん)、留学(りゅうがく)するつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents study abroad as a settled arrangement: an acceptance letter, a
programme, dates. The speaker is reporting a fact about the calendar, and the
sentence says nothing about how they feel about it.

(b) presents it as the speaker's own decision. It can be said long before anything
is arranged, and it carries a personal commitment that (a) does not. If someone
argues with (b) they are arguing with you; if they argue with (a) they are arguing
with the schedule.

</details>

# ～ことにする

**Meaning** · to decide to do something - and the person deciding is the speaker

N5 gave you `にする` for choosing a noun: コーヒーにします, "I'll have the coffee."
A verb cannot sit in that slot, so `こと` is added to turn the whole action into a
thing that can be chosen. <code>歩(ある)くことにします</code> is "I'll go with
walking." The pattern always carries an agent, and unless the sentence says
otherwise, that agent is you.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ことにする | 習(なら)う → 習(なら)うことにする |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことにする | 習(なら)わない → 習(なら)わないことにする |
| Decision already taken | ことにした／ことにしました | 習(なら)うことにしました |
| Decision taken now | ことにする／ことにします | 習(なら)うことにします |
| Standing policy | ことにしている | 習(なら)うことにしています |
| Treating it as true | た-form + ことにする | 聞(き)いた → 聞(き)かなかったことにする |
| Noun (no こと) | Noun + にする | 紅茶(こうちゃ) → 紅茶(こうちゃ)にします |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `こと` exists to nominalise. ❌<code>行(い)くにする</code> is
impossible and ❌<code>行(い)きますことにする</code> is impossible; the verb goes
in plain form and `こと` does the rest.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は山(やま)に<b>登(のぼ)ることにしました</b>。
We've decided to go climbing this summer.
</pre>

<pre>
しばらくゲームを<b>しないことにした</b>。
I've decided to lay off games for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)はゆっくり<b>休(やす)むことにします</b>。
I'm going to take Saturday off and rest properly.
</pre>

<pre>
寝(ね)る前(まえ)に日記(にっき)を<b>書(か)くことにしています</b>。
I make a point of writing in my diary before bed.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)のことは<b>見(み)なかったことにします</b>。
I'll pretend I didn't see anything today.
</pre>

<pre>
よく考(かんが)えて、大学院(だいがくいん)には<b>行(い)かないことにしました</b>。
After thinking it over carefully, I've decided against graduate school.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで歩(ある)くと三十分(さんじゅっぷん)かかるので、自転車(じてんしゃ)で<b>行(い)くことにした</b>。
It takes thirty minutes to walk to the station, so I decided to cycle.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)と話(はな)し合(あ)って、来年(らいねん)の春(はる)に<b>引(ひ)っ越(こ)すことにしました</b>。
We talked it over as a family and decided to move next spring.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The tense of `する` is doing more work than learners expect.

- <code>ことにしました</code> - the decision is **already made**, and you are
  reporting it. This is by far the commonest form, and it is what English "I've
  decided to…" corresponds to.
- <code>ことにします</code> - you are deciding **at this moment**, often out loud,
  in response to what has just been said: 「じゃあ、そうすることにします。」
- <code>ことにしている</code> - not a single decision but a **policy** you keep to.
  肉(にく)は食(た)べないことにしています is "I don't eat meat" said as a rule
  you have adopted, which is subtly different from
  食(た)べません (I don't) or 食(た)べられません (I can't).

<code>～たことにする</code> and <code>～なかったことにする</code> mean *treat it as
though*. They are enormously useful and slightly conspiratorial:
今(いま)のは聞(き)かなかったことにしてください ("please forget I said
that"), この話(はなし)はなかったことにしましょう ("let's call the whole thing
off"). Nothing about this is rude, but it does presuppose that both parties are
willing to bend the record a little.

One sociolinguistic point worth knowing. In Japanese workplaces, people
frequently report their own decisions with <code>ことになりました</code> - the
*became* version - even when they made the choice themselves:
来月(らいげつ)、退職(たいしょく)することになりました. Saying
<code>ことにしました</code> there is not wrong, but it puts your own will in the
foreground, and the tendency is to avoid that when announcing something that
affects other people. Treat this as a tendency, not a rule: among friends
<code>ことにした</code> is completely normal.

The pattern itself is register-neutral, and `する` carries the politeness.

## Contrast

### ～ことにする vs ～ことになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **who decided?** <code>ことにする</code>: I did.
<code>ことになる</code>: someone or something else did, or it simply worked out
that way.

</div>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)を<b>辞(や)めることにしました</b>。
I've decided to leave the company.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)で<b>働(はたら)くことになりました</b>。
It's been arranged that I'll be working in Osaka from next month.
</pre>

The first is a resignation you chose. The second is a transfer that landed on you
 - or that you did engineer, but are presenting as an outcome. This is the single
distinction that runs through both patterns, and it is the one exam questions
test.

### ～ことにする vs ～にする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にする` chooses a **noun**; <code>ことにする</code> chooses an
**action**. `こと` is the adapter between them.

</div>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんはそば<b>にします</b>。
I'll have soba for lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんは外(そと)で<b>食(た)べることにします</b>。
I'll eat out for lunch.
</pre>

Both are decisions and both use the same verb. What changes is only whether the
thing being decided on is a noun or a whole verb phrase.

### ～ことにしている vs ～ようにしている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことにしている</code> is a **rule you follow**;
<code>ようにしている</code> is an **effort you make**. The first is kept or broken;
the second is only ever attempted.

</div>

<pre>
エレベーターは<b>使(つか)わないことにしています</b>。
I make it a rule not to use the lift.
</pre>

<pre>
なるべく階段(かいだん)を<b>使(つか)うようにしています</b>。
I try to use the stairs where I can.
</pre>

The first sounds absolute - you have decided, and that is that. The second admits
the days you take the lift anyway. Adverbs give it away: なるべく,
できるだけ and 少(すこ)しずつ sit naturally with
<code>ようにしている</code> and awkwardly with <code>ことにしている</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)く**にする**。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)く**ことにする**。
  A verb cannot attach to `にする` on its own. `こと` turns it into a noun first.

- ❌ 行(い)き**ます**ことにしました。 → ✅ 行(い)**く**ことにしました。
  `こと` is a noun being modified, so the clause in front of it is plain. The
  politeness is on `しました`.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に始(はじ)まることにしました。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に始(はじ)める**ことにしました**。
  <code>始(はじ)まる</code> is the intransitive verb - things starting by
  themselves - and it cannot be the object of a decision. If you decided, use the
  transitive <code>始(はじ)める</code>; if the schedule decided, use
  <code>始(はじ)まることになりました</code>.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)から東京(とうきょう)へ転勤(てんきん)することにしました。（the company moved you） → ✅ …転勤(てんきん)する**ことになりました**。
  If the choice was not yours, <code>ことにする</code> claims credit you do not
  have and sounds odd to a listener who knows how transfers work.

- ❌ 明日(あした)晴(は)れることにします。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は晴(は)れるでしょう。
  You can only decide things you control. Weather, arriving trains and other
  people's feelings are outside the pattern.

## Practice

**1.** ダイエットのために、エレベーターを使(つか)わない____しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

使(つか)わない<b>ことに</b>しました。

The choice is the speaker's own, so <code>ことにする</code> is the right pattern,
and the past `しました` reports a decision already taken. Note that the negative
goes on the main verb, not on `する`.

</details>

**2.** 社長(しゃちょう)が決(き)めた：「来年(らいねん)、新(あたら)しい工場(こうじょう)を建(た)てる（ことにしました・ことになりました）。」と社員(しゃいん)が話(はな)している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しい工場(こうじょう)を建(た)てる<b>ことになりました</b>。

The speaker is an employee reporting a decision the president made. Since the
decision was not theirs, <code>ことになる</code> is the honest choice.
<code>ことにしました</code> would claim the decision as their own.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've decided not to drink coffee after eight o'clock."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

八時(はちじ)以降(いこう)はコーヒーを<b>飲(の)まないことにしました</b>。

The negative attaches to the verb - <code>飲(の)まない</code> + こと - and the
decision is reported in the past. If you meant it as a standing rule rather than a
one-off resolution, <code>飲(の)まないことにしています</code> is the form you
want.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 夏休(なつやす)みは国(くに)に帰(かえ)るにすることにしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 夏休(なつやす)みは国(くに)に<b>帰(かえ)ることにしました</b>。

Two patterns have been stacked. Either the object of the decision is a noun
(<code>国(くに)にします</code>) or it is a verb phrase
(<code>帰(かえ)ることにします</code>), never both. `こと` already supplies
everything `にする` needs.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べることにしました。
(b) 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べることにしています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a decision taken at one point in the past: at some moment you resolved to
start eating breakfast. It says nothing about whether you have kept it up.

(b) describes an ongoing practice governed by that decision - "I make a point of
eating breakfast." The `ている` turns the single act of deciding into a policy that
is still in force. If someone asks about your daily routine, (b) is the answer;
(a) is what you say the day you make the change.

</details>

# ～ことになる

**Meaning** · it has been decided that ～; it works out that ～ - an outcome, with
nobody named as having decided it

<code>ことになる</code> is the mirror image of <code>ことにする</code>. `なる` is the
verb of things happening by themselves, so the sentence reports a decision or an
arrangement as a **result**: something has been settled, and the sentence declines
to say by whom. English almost always has to supply an agent ("they decided",
"I've been transferred"); Japanese does not, and that gap is what makes this
pattern feel alien at first.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ことになる | 通(かよ)う → 通(かよ)うことになる |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことになる | 通(かよ)わない → 通(かよ)わないことになる |
| Decision reached | ことになった／ことになりました | 通(かよ)うことになりました |
| Rule or arrangement in force | ことになっている | 通(かよ)うことになっています |
| Humble / business | ことになっております | お届(とど)けすることになっております |
| "That amounts to" | plain clause + ということになる | 中止(ちゅうし)ということになる |
| Noun | Noun + ということになる | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みということになる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことになる</code> takes a **verb** clause. A change of
state in an adjective or noun uses plain `なる`:
寒(さむ)く**なる**, 先生(せんせい)に**なる** - never
❌<code>寒(さむ)いことになる</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)たち、来年(らいねん)<b>結婚(けっこん)することになりました</b>。
We're getting married next year.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)から新(あたら)しい先生(せんせい)が<b>来(く)ることになりました</b>。
A new teacher will be joining us from next week.
</pre>

<pre>
この寮(りょう)では十一時(じゅういちじ)までに<b>帰(かえ)ることになっています</b>。
In this dormitory you're expected to be back by eleven.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)のため、今日(きょう)の試合(しあい)は<b>しないことになりました</b>。
Because of the typhoon, today's match has been called off.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)の三月(さんがつ)で、この会社(かいしゃ)に十年(じゅうねん)<b>いることになります</b>。
Come March next year, I'll have been at this company ten years.
</pre>

<pre>
つまり、最初(さいしょ)から全部(ぜんぶ)やり直(なお)す<b>ということになります</b>ね。
So in other words, we start the whole thing over from scratch.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)の学校(がっこう)が遠(とお)いので、毎朝(まいあさ)駅(えき)まで<b>送(おく)っていくことになりました</b>。
My son's school is a long way off, so it's ended up with me driving him to the station every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
ご注文(ちゅうもん)の品(しな)は、来週(らいしゅう)の水曜日(すいようび)にお届(とど)け<b>することになっております</b>。
Your order is scheduled for delivery next Wednesday.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Three things live inside this one form.

**1. A decision made elsewhere.** The everyday use.
来月(らいげつ)、支店(してん)に移(うつ)ることになりました - the company
decided, and you are passing on the result. Because no agent is named, the
sentence works equally well when the decider is a committee, a rule, a
circumstance, or nobody at all.

**2. A rule in force.** <code>ことになっている</code> is how Japanese states
regulations, customs and standing arrangements:
会社(かいしゃ)では九時(くじ)までに来(く)ることになっています,
ここでは写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)らないことになっています. The `ている` is
essential - it says the decision is not news but the state of things. This is
also the polite way to refuse: rather than "you can't", you say "it isn't done",
and nobody has to be the one saying no.

**3. Logical consequence.** <code>ということになる</code> means *that amounts to*.
It is how you sum up: では、来週(らいしゅう)は休(やす)みということになりますね.
Here nothing has been decided at all; the sentence is doing arithmetic.

The social point is the interesting one. Japanese speakers very often report
their **own** decisions with <code>ことになりました</code>:
来月(らいげつ)退職(たいしょく)することになりました, said by someone who
resigned of their own accord. This is not deception. Putting the outcome forward
instead of your will is a way of not imposing, and it is close to standard for
announcements - marriages, job changes, moves - that affect the people listening.
The tendency is strong in workplaces and weaker among close friends, where
<code>ことにした</code> is perfectly normal. Read
<code>ことになりました</code> as "here is what has been settled", and do not assume
the speaker was passive.

Register is neutral; <code>ことになっております</code> is the humble business
version and belongs in customer-facing speech.

## Contrast

### ～ことになる vs ～ことにする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことにする</code> names the decider (you).
<code>ことになる</code> reports the outcome and leaves the decider out. That is the
whole distinction.

</div>

<pre>
部屋(へや)を変(か)えて<b>もらうことにしました</b>。
I decided to ask them to change my room.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)を変(か)えて<b>もらうことになりました</b>。
It's been arranged that they'll change my room for me.
</pre>

Same event, two framings. The first foregrounds your initiative; the second
foregrounds the arrangement. Neither is more truthful - Japanese simply offers a
choice about how much of your own will to put on the table, and takes the modest
option more often than English does.

### ～ことになった vs ～ことになっている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことになった</code> is the **moment** something was
settled; <code>ことになっている</code> is the **state** of its being settled. News
versus rule.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から制服(せいふく)を<b>着(き)ることになりました</b>。
From next month we'll be wearing uniforms. (this has just been decided)
</pre>

<pre>
この学校(がっこう)では制服(せいふく)を<b>着(き)ることになっています</b>。
At this school you wear a uniform. (that's how it is)
</pre>

Use the past form to announce a change, and the `ている` form to explain the way
things are. Saying <code>ことになりました</code> about a rule that has been in
place for decades makes it sound like this morning's memo.

### ～ことになる vs ～ようになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことになる</code> is a **decision** landing;
<code>ようになる</code> is a **change** arriving - a new ability, or a habit that
grew.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、海外(かいがい)で<b>働(はたら)くことになりました</b>。
It's been settled that I'll work abroad next year.
</pre>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)習(なら)って、英語(えいご)で<b>話(はな)せるようになりました</b>。
After a year of lessons, I've got to the point where I can hold a conversation in English.
</pre>

The first happened at a stroke, because someone decided. The second happened
gradually, because you changed. If you can point to a meeting where it was agreed,
it is <code>ことになる</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)き**ます**ことになりました。 → ✅ 行(い)**く**ことになりました。
  `こと` is a noun being modified, so the verb before it is plain.

- ❌ この会社(かいしゃ)では制服(せいふく)を着(き)ることに**なりました**。（describing a long-standing rule） → ✅ …着(き)ることに**なっています**。
  A rule already in force needs the `ている` form. The past tense announces a
  change that has just been made.

- ❌ 今晩(こんばん)はカレーを作(つく)ることになります。（your own choice） → ✅ 今晩(こんばん)はカレーを作(つく)ることにします。
  If you are the one deciding and there is no arrangement to report,
  <code>ことにする</code> is the honest form. <code>ことになる</code> here sounds
  as though the curry were imposed on you.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)から忙(いそが)しいことになります。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)から忙(いそが)しくなります。
  <code>ことになる</code> attaches to verbs. Adjectives change with plain `なる`.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)は中止(ちゅうし)ことになりました。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)は中止(ちゅうし)**という**ことになりました。
  A noun needs `という` before `こと`. Without it there is nothing joining the two
  nouns.

## Practice

**1.** 会社(かいしゃ)の都合(つごう)で、来月(らいげつ)から名古屋(なごや)で働(はたら)く____なりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

働(はたら)く<b>ことに</b>なりました。

The company's circumstances made the decision, so the outcome is reported with
<code>ことになる</code> and the agent is left unnamed. <code>ことにしました</code>
would claim the move was your idea.

</details>

**2.** 友(とも)だちに：「よく考(かんが)えて、留学(りゅうがく)する（ことにした・ことになった）んだ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

留学(りゅうがく)する<b>ことにした</b>んだ。

<code>よく考(かんが)えて</code> says the thinking was yours, so the decision is
yours too. Among friends there is no need to hide your own will - 
<code>ことになった</code> here would leave your friend wondering who decided for
you.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "At this hospital you're not allowed to use your phone."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この病院(びょういん)では電話(でんわ)を<b>使(つか)わないことになっています</b>。

A standing rule takes <code>ことになっている</code>, and the negative goes on the
main verb. This is softer than
<code>使(つか)ってはいけません</code>: it presents the ban as an established
arrangement rather than as you forbidding something.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)は休(やす)みことになりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)は休(やす)み<b>という</b>ことになりました。

<code>休(やす)み</code> is a noun, and a noun cannot modify `こと` directly.
`という` is the connector. Alternatively, rebuild it around a verb:
<code>休(やす)むことになりました</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 来月(らいげつ)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)すことにしました。
(b) 来月(らいげつ)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)すことになりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the move is your decision, taken by you, and it puts your will in the
foreground. It is what you say to a friend who asked what you had decided.

(b) reports the move as something that has been settled. It might mean your
company transferred you - or it might mean exactly the same situation as (a),
announced modestly. Japanese speakers frequently choose (b) for news that affects
other people, because it does not push the speaker's own choices at the listener.
Without further context, (b) does not actually tell you who decided; that is the
point of it.

</details>

# ～ようにする

**Meaning** · to make an effort to do something; to see to it that something is
the case

The `よう` here is the noun meaning *manner* or *way*, so
<code>歩(ある)くようにする</code> is literally "act in such a way that walking
happens." That gives the pattern its two faces: an ongoing **effort** you make
about your own behaviour, and an **arrangement** you put in place so that some
state holds. Both are about steering, not about a single act - which is exactly
what separates it from <code>ことにする</code>.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ようにする | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)くようにする |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ようにする | 遅(おく)れない → 遅(おく)れないようにする |
| Verb (potential) | potential + ようにする | 読(よ)める → 読(よ)めるようにする |
| Effort you keep up | ようにしている | 歩(ある)くようにしています |
| Effort starting now | ようにします | これから歩(ある)くようにします |
| Request / instruction | ようにしてください | 遅(おく)れないようにしてください |
| Arrangement made | ようにした | 見(み)えるようにした |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative goes on the **main verb**, never on `する`.
<code>食(た)べないようにする</code> = "try not to eat".
❌<code>食(た)べるようにしない</code> would mean something like "not make the
effort", which is not what anyone wants to say.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
毎晩(まいばん)十二時(じゅうにじ)までに<b>寝(ね)るようにしています</b>。
I try to be in bed by midnight every night.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)に<b>遅(おく)れないようにします</b>。
I'll make sure not to be late for class.
</pre>

<pre>
塩(しお)を<b>使(つか)いすぎないようにしています</b>。
I'm trying not to use too much salt.
</pre>

<pre>
パスワードは人(ひと)に<b>見(み)られないようにしてください</b>。
Please make sure nobody can see your password.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は八時(はちじ)までに<b>来(く)るようにしてください</b>。
Please see to it that you're here by eight tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)と話(はな)すときは、やさしい言葉(ことば)を<b>使(つか)うようにしています</b>。
When I talk with international students I make a point of using simple words.
</pre>

<pre>
夜(よる)遅(おそ)くコーヒーを<b>飲(の)まないようにした</b>ら、よく眠(ねむ)れるようになりました。
Once I made a point of not drinking coffee late at night, I started sleeping well.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は医者(いしゃ)に注意(ちゅうい)されてから、毎朝(まいあさ)三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>歩(ある)くようにしている</b>そうです。
Apparently my father has been making a point of walking for thirty minutes every morning since the doctor had a word with him.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The commonest form by far is <code>ようにしている</code>. It says: this is
something I aim at, and I mostly manage it. Crucially, it does **not** claim
success every time - which is why it is the honest way to describe a resolution
you keep about eighty percent of. It sits naturally with hedging adverbs:
なるべく, できるだけ, 少(すこ)しずつ,
毎日(まいにち). Compare the flat present tense
毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)きます, which simply asserts that you do.

<code>ようにします</code> - non-past, no `ている` - is a promise to start.
今度(こんど)から気(き)をつけるようにします is the standard thing to say
after being corrected: it accepts the point and commits to changing without
claiming you have already changed.

<code>ようにしてください</code> is worth its own paragraph. Beside plain
`てください` it is **less immediate and more supervisory**. `てください` asks for an
action now; <code>ようにしてください</code> asks you to arrange your behaviour so
that something is generally true. That makes it the natural form for rules,
instructions and reminders - and it can carry a faint note of "I shouldn't have to
say this", so among friends it is heavier than it looks. A teacher, a doctor or a
manual will use it constantly.

The second flavour of the pattern - **arranging conditions** - usually shows up
with potential or intransitive verbs:
子(こ)どもが触(さわ)れないようにする ("make it so children can't touch it"),
後(うし)ろからも聞(き)こえるようにする ("make it audible from the back").
Here nobody is making an effort; something is being set up.

Register is neutral, and `する` carries the politeness as usual. One small pitfall:
`よう` used this way is not the `よう` of
<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るようだ</code> ("it seems"). Same syllable, unrelated
job.

## Contrast

### ～ようにする vs ～ことにする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことにする</code> is a **decision**, taken once.
<code>ようにする</code> is an **effort**, made repeatedly. A decision is announced;
an effort is only ever aimed at.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)からジムに<b>通(かよ)うことにしました</b>。
I've decided to start going to the gym next month.
</pre>

<pre>
週(しゅう)に二回(にかい)はジムに<b>行(い)くようにしています</b>。
I try to get to the gym twice a week.
</pre>

The first is a single event: you decided. The second describes how you conduct
yourself over time, and it quietly admits the weeks you miss. This is why a one-off
plan cannot take <code>ようにする</code>:
❌<code>来週(らいしゅう)、京都(きょうと)へ行(い)くようにしました</code> is
wrong, because a single trip is not a policy.

### ～ようにする vs ～ようになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `する` is **you steering**; `なる` is **the change arriving**.
Effort versus result.

</div>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を毎日(まいにち)十個(じゅっこ)ずつ<b>覚(おぼ)えるようにしました</b>。
I made a point of learning ten kanji a day.
</pre>

<pre>
新聞(しんぶん)が少(すこ)し<b>読(よ)めるようになりました</b>。
I've got to the point where I can read a newspaper a little.
</pre>

The first is the work; the second is what the work produced. A very common error
is to use `する` where the sentence is really about a change you underwent:
❌<code>日本語(にほんご)が話(はな)せるようにしました</code> claims you
engineered your own fluency, and native ears hear it as strange. You *became* able
 - <code>なりました</code>.

### ～ようにしてください vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てください` asks for **one action**.
<code>ようにしてください</code> asks you to make it **generally so**, and often
carries an ongoing obligation.

</div>

<pre>
まどを<b>閉(し)めてください</b>。
Please close the window.
</pre>

<pre>
出(で)かけるときは、まどを<b>閉(し)めるようにしてください</b>。
Please make sure you close the windows when you go out.
</pre>

The first is about this window, now. The second is a standing instruction, and it
implies that on some past occasion the windows were left open. That implication is
why the form can sting: to a colleague,
<code>返事(へんじ)を早(はや)く出(だ)すようにしてください</code> lands
closer to a reprimand than a request.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 歩(ある)き**ます**ようにしています。 → ✅ 歩(ある)**く**ようにしています。
  `よう` is a noun, so the verb in front of it is plain.

- ❌ 甘(あま)いものを食(た)べる**ようにしていません**。 → ✅ 甘(あま)いものを食(た)べ**ないようにしています**。
  The negative belongs on the main verb. Negating `する` denies that you are making
  any effort at all.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)、映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)くようにしました。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)くことにしました。
  A single planned outing is a decision, not a policy. <code>ようにする</code>
  needs something repeated or maintained.

- ❌ 一年(いちねん)勉強(べんきょう)して、日本語(にほんご)が話(はな)せるようにしました。 → ✅ …話(はな)せるようになりました。
  You did not arrange your own fluency; you acquired it. Change that happens to
  you takes `なる`.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるようになっています。（meaning "I try to"） → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるようにしています。
  <code>ようになっている</code> would report a state that came about by itself. If
  the effort is yours, it is `して`.

## Practice

**1.** 健康(けんこう)のために、エレベーターを使(つか)わない____しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

使(つか)わない<b>ように</b>しています。

An ongoing effort about your own behaviour is <code>ようにする</code>, and the
negative sits on <code>使(つか)う</code>. The `ている` says the effort is still
running.

</details>

**2.** 来月(らいげつ)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)す（ようにしました・ことにしました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>ことにしました</b>。

Moving house happens once, so it is a decision, not a policy.
<code>ようにする</code> needs something you do repeatedly or keep true over time.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please make sure you don't forget your umbrella."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

かさを<b>忘(わす)れないようにしてください</b>。

<code>ようにしてください</code> asks the listener to arrange things so that
forgetting does not happen, which is what "make sure" means. Plain
<code>忘(わす)れないでください</code> is also possible and slightly more direct
 - it addresses this one umbrella rather than the habit.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 三年(さんねん)練習(れんしゅう)して、ピアノが上手(じょうず)に弾(ひ)けるようにしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 三年(さんねん)練習(れんしゅう)して、ピアノが上手(じょうず)に<b>弾(ひ)けるようになりました</b>。

The sentence is about a change the speaker underwent as a result of practice, so
it needs `なる`. <code>ようにしました</code> would claim you arranged your own
ability, which is not something a person can do to themselves.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きます。
(b) 毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きるようにしています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of fact: this is what happens every morning. It makes no
reference to effort and implies no exceptions.

(b) says you aim at six and generally get there. The `ようにしている` frames it as a
policy you maintain, which both credits you with the discipline and quietly leaves
room for the mornings it does not work out. If someone asks how you manage such an
early start, (b) is the modest, natural answer.

</details>

# ～ようになる

**Meaning** · to reach the point where ～; to come to do ～ - a change that has
taken place over time

N5 introduced `なる` with the note that verbs need `ように` in front of it. This is
that pattern in full. <code>ようになる</code> marks a **threshold crossed**: there
was a time when this was not so, and now it is. That is why it is the natural home
of the potential form - <code>話(はな)せるようになりました</code>, "I've got to
where I can speak" - and why it cannot describe a single event.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ようになる | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)ぐようになる |
| Verb (potential) | potential form + ようになる | 泳(およ)げる → 泳(およ)げるようになる |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form → な**く**なる | 泳(およ)がない → 泳(およ)がなくなる |
| Change completed | ようになった／なりました | 泳(およ)げるようになりました |
| State that has come about | ようになっている | 自動(じどう)で閉(し)まるようになっている |
| い-adjective (no よう) | drop い + くなる | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)くなる |
| な-adjective / Noun (no よう) | + に + なる | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かになる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ように` is the adapter that lets a **verb** plug into `なる`.
Adjectives and nouns already have their own connectors (`く` and `に`) and must
not take it: ❌<code>寒(さむ)いようになる</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negative is the exception you have to memorise. In principle
<code>飲(の)まないようになる</code> is formable, but ordinary Japanese contracts
it to <code>飲(の)まなくなる</code>, treating `ない` as the い-adjective it
grammatically is. Use `なくなる`; you will meet
<code>ないようになる</code> occasionally, usually where the speaker wants to stress
that the change was deliberate.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
やっと自転車(じてんしゃ)に<b>乗(の)れるようになりました</b>。
I can finally ride a bike.
</pre>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)、朝(あさ)ごはんを<b>食(た)べるようになった</b>。
Lately I've started eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)して、平仮名(ひらがな)が全部(ぜんぶ)<b>読(よ)めるようになりました</b>。
With practice I've got to the point where I can read all the hiragana.
</pre>

<pre>
春(はる)になって、公園(こうえん)で遊(あそ)ぶ子(こ)どもをよく<b>見(み)かけるようになった</b>。
Now that it's spring, I see children playing in the park all the time.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)のドアは、人(ひと)が近(ちか)づくと自動(じどう)で<b>開(あ)くようになっています</b>。
The door of this shop is set up to open automatically when someone approaches.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)はスマホでメールを<b>送(おく)れるようになって</b>、とても喜(よろこ)んでいます。
My grandmother has learnt to send messages on her phone and is delighted about it.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが生(う)まれてから、夜(よる)は早(はや)く家(いえ)に<b>帰(かえ)るようになりました</b>。
Since our child was born I've taken to coming home early in the evenings.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりのころは何(なに)も分(わ)かりませんでしたが、半年(はんとし)で買(か)い物(もの)ぐらいは<b>できるようになりました</b>。
When I'd just arrived in Japan I understood nothing, but within six months I could at least manage the shopping.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everything about this pattern depends on there being a **before**. The sentence
implicitly says "it wasn't like this previously", so it is odd wherever no change
is involved. This is why a one-off future event cannot use it:
❌<code>明日(あした)、会議(かいぎ)に出(で)るようになります</code> - 
attending one meeting is not a new state of affairs. That sentence wants
<code>ことになります</code>.

The two everyday uses are:

- **New ability.** Almost always with the potential form:
  運転(うんてん)できるようになった,
  漢字(かんじ)が書(か)けるようになった. Learners under-use this and reach
  for the bare potential instead, which reports the ability without the
  achievement in it.
- **New habit.** With a plain verb:
  最近(さいきん)、本(ほん)を読(よ)むようになりました. Nothing about the
  speaker's ability changed; their behaviour did.

<code>ようになっている</code> is a third, quieter use: a state that has come about,
or a mechanism set up so that something happens.
この機械(きかい)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)で止(と)まるようになっています is
how you describe the way a device behaves, and manuals are full of it. The close
relative <code>ようにできている</code> puts the stress on the design rather than on
the state.

Because the change is presented as something that happened *to* the subject, the
pattern is modest by nature. Saying
話(はな)せるようになりました about your own Japanese sounds more graceful than
話(はな)せます, and it is the standard answer when someone compliments your
progress. It is register-neutral otherwise; `なる` carries the politeness.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>分(わ)かる</code>, `できる` and <code>ある</code> already
contain the sense of a state, and they take <code>ようになる</code> directly:
分(わ)かるようになりました. There is no ❌<code>分(わ)かれるようになる</code>.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～ようになる vs ～なくなる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are the positive and negative halves of one idea.
Started doing it: <code>ようになった</code>. Stopped doing it:
<code>なくなった</code>.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)してから、バスを<b>使(つか)うようになりました</b>。
Since moving I've started using the bus.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)してから、電車(でんしゃ)に<b>乗(の)らなくなりました</b>。
Since moving I've stopped taking the train.
</pre>

The asymmetry catches people: the positive needs `ように`, the negative does not,
because `ない` behaves like an い-adjective and takes the `く` connector. The same
split gives you <code>読(よ)めるようになった</code> against
<code>読(よ)めなくなった</code>.

### ～ようになる vs ～ようにする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なる` is the **change**; `する` is the **effort** that
produces it. Do not use `する` for something that happened to you.

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ漢字(かんじ)を<b>覚(おぼ)えるようにしました</b>。
I made a point of learning a few kanji every day.
</pre>

<pre>
新聞(しんぶん)の見出(みだ)しが<b>読(よ)めるようになりました</b>。
I got to the point where I could read newspaper headlines.
</pre>

The first sentence is the practice; the second is the result, and the two are
often said one after the other. Where English says "I managed to learn to read",
Japanese splits the effort from the outcome and marks which one it means.

### ～ようになる vs ～くなる／～になる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ように` is only for **verbs**. い-adjectives take `く`, and
な-adjectives and nouns take `に`.

</div>

<pre>
日(ひ)が長(なが)<b>くなりました</b>。
The days have got longer.
</pre>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)で冗談(じょうだん)が言(い)<b>えるようになりました</b>。
I've reached the point where I can crack jokes in Japanese.
</pre>

Watch the potential form here. <code>言(い)える</code> is a verb, not an
adjective, so it takes `ように` even though its `る` ending makes it look like a
candidate for `くなる`. ❌<code>言(い)えくなる</code> is a fairly common slip.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 泳(およ)ぐ**になりました** → ✅ 泳(およ)ぐ**ようになりました**
  A verb needs `ように` to attach to `なる`. Only nouns join `なる` with a bare
  `に`.

- ❌ 話(はな)せ**くなりました** → ✅ 話(はな)せ**るようになりました**
  The potential form is a verb. `くなる` is the い-adjective connector and does not
  apply.

- ❌ たばこを吸(す)わない**ようになりました** → ✅ たばこを吸(す)わ**なくなりました**
  For a change in the negative direction, `ない` contracts with `なる` directly.
  The `ようになる` version is not impossible but sounds laboured.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かなようになりました。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)か**に**なりました。
  な-adjectives take `に`. `ように` here would be the "seems like" `よう`, which
  gives a completely different sentence.

- ❌ 来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)に住(す)むようになります。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)に住(す)むことになりました。
  A single arranged move is a decision landing, not a gradual change. Use
  <code>ことになる</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)したので、ギターが少(すこ)し弾(ひ)ける____なりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弾(ひ)ける<b>ように</b>なりました。

<code>弾(ひ)ける</code> is a potential verb, and verbs attach to `なる` through
`ように`. The sentence reports a threshold crossed - you could not before, and now
you can.

</details>

**2.** 大人(おとな)になってから、朝(あさ)のコーヒーを（飲(の)まないようになりました・飲(の)まなくなりました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

飲(の)ま<b>なくなりました</b>。

For a change *away* from doing something, `ない` joins `なる` directly as `なくなる`,
because `ない` inflects like an い-adjective. The `ないようになる` version is
grammatical but stilted, and no one would choose it here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My daughter has started reading books on her own."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

娘(むすめ)が一人(ひとり)で本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)むようになりました</b>。

This is a new habit rather than a new ability, so the plain dictionary form goes
in front of `ようになる`. Using the potential
<code>読(よ)めるようになりました</code> would change the meaning to "she has
learnt to read".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 運動(うんどう)を始(はじ)めてから、体(からだ)の調子(ちょうし)がいいようになりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 運動(うんどう)を始(はじ)めてから、体(からだ)の調子(ちょうし)が<b>よくなりました</b>。

<code>いい</code> is an い-adjective, so it changes with `くなる` - and irregularly,
through the `よ` stem: いい → よくなる. `ように` only ever attaches to verbs.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日本語(にほんご)のニュースが分(わ)かります。
(b) 日本語(にほんご)のニュースが分(わ)かるようになりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states a present ability flatly: you understand Japanese news. It says nothing
about where you started.

(b) puts a history behind it - there was a time when you did not, and now you do.
That makes it both more informative and more modest: the change is presented as
something that happened, rather than as a skill you are asserting. When a Japanese
speaker praises your progress, (b) is the graceful reply.

</details>
