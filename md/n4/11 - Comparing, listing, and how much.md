# より

**Meaning** · than - it marks the side of a comparison that comes off worse

`より` is a postposition: it goes **after** the noun that loses. Nothing else in
the sentence changes, because Japanese adjectives have no comparative ending - 
安(やす)い covers both "cheap" and "cheaper", and `より` is what tells you which
one is meant. It also has a second, unrelated life as a stiff written stand-in for
`から`, "from".

## Formation

`より` attaches to whatever names the standard you are measuring against. That
standard does not have to be another object: it can be a moment in time, an
expectation, or a number.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + より | 兄(あに) → 兄(あに)より背(せ)が高(たか)い |
| Verb (plain form) | V(plain) + より | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つより歩(ある)く方(ほう)が早(はや)い |
| An expectation | 思(おも)った／予想(よそう)した + より | 思(おも)ったより安(やす)い |
| A habitual standard | いつも／前(まえ)／去年(きょねん) + より | いつもより早(はや)く起(お)きた |
| A number | Number + より + 多(おお)い／少(すく)ない | 百人(ひゃくにん)より多(おお)い |
| With emphasis | ～よりも | 去年(きょねん)よりも寒(さむ)い |
| Written "from" (= から) | Time / place + より | 九時(くじ)より開始(かいし)します |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `より` compares exactly **two** things. The moment there are
three or more, Japanese switches to the superlative frame with
<code>中(なか)で</code> and `いちばん`; the two constructions never combine in one
clause.

</div>

The winner half of the comparison is optional. <code>去年(きょねん)より寒(さむ)いです</code>
is a complete sentence - the thing being measured is simply the topic, stated or
understood.

## Examples

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)はあの辞書(じしょ)<b>より</b>重(おも)いです。
This dictionary is heavier than that one.
</pre>

<pre>
思(おも)った<b>より</b>人(ひと)が少(すく)なかったです。
There were fewer people than I expected.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)はいつも<b>より</b>雪(ゆき)が多(おお)いです。
There's more snow than usual this winter.
</pre>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)する<b>より</b>、メールを送(おく)る方(ほう)が楽(らく)です。
Sending an email is easier than phoning.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は去年(きょねん)<b>より</b>五(ご)センチも背(せ)が高(たか)くなりました。
My little brother has grown a whole five centimetres since last year.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)<b>より</b>大(おお)ホールで行(おこな)います。
The meeting will be held from three o'clock in the main hall.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)の冬(ふゆ)は北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>より</b>寒(さむ)いと言(い)われています。
They say the winters in this town are colder than in Hokkaido.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいアパートは駅(えき)から遠(とお)いですが、前(まえ)のところ<b>より</b>家賃(やちん)が安(やす)くて静(しず)かです。
The new flat is far from the station, but the rent is lower than my old place and it's quiet.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The comparative `より` is register-neutral. It is the same word in
安(やす)いです and 安(やす)い, in conversation and in a report, and nothing
about it is marked for age or gender.

`よりも` is the emphatic form - "than that, *definitely*". It is common in both
speech and writing and costs nothing to use:
今日(きょう)は昨日(きのう)よりも風(かぜ)が強(つよ)い. There is also a
casual `よりか` heard in relaxed speech; recognise it, but keep it out of writing
and out of anything you are graded on.

Two expressions are worth learning whole, because native speakers reach for them
constantly:

- <code>思(おも)ったより</code> - "more/less … than I thought". Always the **past**
  form 思(おも)った, even when you are talking about right now.
- <code>何(なに)より</code> - "above all else".
  お元気(げんき)そうで何(なに)よりです is a set greeting meaning "I'm glad
  above all to see you well".

The "from" `より` is a different animal. It is written, formal and slightly
old-fashioned in feel: timetables, invitations, event notices, price tags
（千円(せんえん)より, "from ¥1,000"）, and the sign-off of a letter
（田中(たなか)より, "from Tanaka"）. Nobody says it to a friend. If you are
speaking, use `から`.

## Contrast

### より vs ～の方(ほう)が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are not rivals; they are the two ends of one comparison.
`より` goes on the loser, <code>の方(ほう)が</code> on the winner. Either half can
stand alone, but `より` can never mark the winner.

</div>

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)<b>より</b>地下鉄(ちかてつ)の方(ほう)が速(はや)いです。
The subway is faster than a bicycle.
</pre>

Drop either half and the sentence still works, because context supplies the rest:

<pre>
地下鉄(ちかてつ)の方(ほう)が速(はや)いです。
The subway is faster.
</pre>

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)<b>より</b>速(はや)いです。
It's faster than a bicycle.
</pre>

What you cannot do is put `より` on the thing that wins.
❌<code>地下鉄(ちかてつ)より速(はや)いのは自転車(じてんしゃ)です</code> reverses
the meaning: it now says the bicycle is the faster one.

### より（＝から）vs から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - where they overlap, `より` is the formal written form and
`から` the spoken one. But `から` covers ground `より` never touches.

</div>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)は午前(ごぜん)十時(じゅうじ)<b>より</b>始(はじ)まります。
Reception opens at 10 a.m. (printed on a notice)
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)、十時(じゅうじ)<b>から</b>だって。
Apparently reception starts at ten. (said to a friend)
</pre>

`から` also means "because" and marks the material something is made of;
寒(さむ)いから帰(かえ)ります cannot become
❌寒(さむ)いより帰(かえ)ります. The overlap is only the starting-point sense.

### より vs もっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `より` names what you are comparing against; `もっと` refuses to.

</div>

<pre>
もっと大(おお)きい声(こえ)で話(はな)してください。
Please speak louder.
</pre>

<pre>
さっき<b>より</b>大(おお)きい声(こえ)で話(はな)してください。
Please speak louder than you were a moment ago.
</pre>

`もっと` is a bare "more" - one step further up the scale from wherever we are now.
`より` demands a stated benchmark. That is why the two do not stack: with `より`
already in the sentence, `もっと` is redundant.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ より去年(きょねん)寒(さむ)いです。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)<b>より</b>寒(さむ)いです。
  English puts "than" in front of its noun; Japanese puts `より` behind it. Word
  order is the whole grammar here.

- ❌ 思(おも)う<b>より</b>難(むずか)しかったです。 → ✅ 思(おも)った<b>より</b>難(むずか)しかったです。
  The fixed expression takes the plain past 思(おも)った. The thought came first,
  then reality contradicted it.

- ❌ 犬(いぬ)より猫(ねこ)がもっと好(す)きです。 → ✅ 犬(いぬ)より猫(ねこ)が好(す)きです。
  `より` already supplies "more". Adding `もっと` says it twice.

- ❌ 三時(さんじ)より飲(の)みに行(い)こうよ。 → ✅ 三時(さんじ)から飲(の)みに行(い)こうよ。
  The "from" `より` belongs on printed notices. In casual speech it sounds like you
  are reading an announcement aloud.

## Practice

**1.** 去年(きょねん)____給料(きゅうりょう)が少(すこ)し上(あ)がりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

去年(きょねん)<b>より</b>給料(きゅうりょう)が少(すこ)し上(あ)がりました。

The standard being measured against is last year, and it is the side that comes
off lower, so it takes `より`. Note that the benchmark here is a point in time,
not another object - that is completely normal for `より`.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural particle, said to a friend over lunch:
コンサートは七時(しちじ)（より・から）始(はじ)まるよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

コンサートは七時(しちじ)<b>から</b>始(はじ)まるよ。

Both words can mark a starting time, but the `より` version belongs to a printed
programme. Saying it to a friend, with the casual ending `よ`, mixes two registers
that do not go together.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This bag is lighter than it looks."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このかばんは見(み)た目(め)<b>より</b>軽(かる)いです。

<code>見(み)た目(め)</code> ("appearance") is the benchmark, so it takes `より`,
and 軽(かる)い stays in its ordinary form - there is no separate comparative to
conjugate into. <code>思(おも)ったより軽(かる)いです</code> is also fine and shifts
the benchmark from how it looks to what you had assumed.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: ぼくは姉(あね)よりもっと背(せ)が高(たか)いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ ぼくは姉(あね)<b>より</b>背(せ)が高(たか)いです。

Delete `もっと`. The comparison is already carried by `より`; `もっと` is a
free-standing "more" for when no benchmark is named, so putting both in one clause
doubles the same idea. If you want extra force, use `よりも` instead.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もっと静(しず)かな部屋(へや)がいいです。
(b) この部屋(へや)より静(しず)かな部屋(へや)がいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) asks for a quieter room without saying quieter than what - it works anywhere,
including before you have seen a single room, and it is the softer request.

(b) pins the comparison to the room you are standing in. It is a complaint with a
measurement attached, and the desk clerk now knows exactly which room failed. The
benchmark is what `より` adds.

</details>

# ～の中で

**Meaning** · in; among - it fences off the set you are choosing or ranking within

<code>～の中(なか)で</code> names the field. Everything that follows it - a
superlative, a second place, an exception, a question word - is understood to
apply *inside that fence and nowhere else*. Literally "in the middle of ～", but
the set does not have to be a physical container: friends, seasons, films you have
watched all work.

## Formation

The pattern is a noun (中(なか)) plus the particle `で`, so anything that can
modify a noun can sit in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a set) | Noun + の中(なか)で | クラス → クラスの中(なか)で |
| A spelled-out set | AとBとCの中(なか)で | 米(こめ)とパンと麺(めん)の中(なか)で |
| Demonstrative | この／その／あの + 中(なか)で（no の） | この中(なか)で |
| Noun-modifying clause | Clause + Noun + の中(なか)で | 読(よ)んだ本(ほん)の中(なか)で |
| Topicalised | ～の中(なか)では | スポーツの中(なか)では |
| Place or time span | Place／span + で（**no** 中(なか)） | 日本(にほん)で／一年(いちねん)で |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>の中(なか)で</code> is only half a sentence. It has to be
followed by something that picks a member out: `いちばん`,
<code>二番目(にばんめ)に</code>, `だけ`, <code>特(とく)に</code>, or a question word
like `どれ` / `だれ` / `どこ`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a demonstrative already contains the "this set" idea, so it takes
中(なか) directly: この中(なか)で, never ❌このの中(なか)で.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この四(よっ)つ<b>の中(なか)で</b>、どれがいいですか。
Which of these four would you like?
</pre>

<pre>
野菜(やさい)<b>の中(なか)で</b>、トマトだけ苦手(にがて)です。
Tomatoes are the one vegetable I can't handle.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の都市(とし)<b>の中(なか)で</b>、京都(きょうと)が二番目(にばんめ)に好(す)きです。
Of Japan's cities, Kyoto is my second favourite.
</pre>

<pre>
友(とも)だち<b>の中(なか)で</b>、彼女(かのじょ)だけが本当(ほんとう)のことを知(し)っています。
She's the only one of my friends who knows the truth.
</pre>

<pre>
スポーツ<b>の中(なか)では</b>、特(とく)に水泳(すいえい)が得意(とくい)です。
Among sports, swimming is what I'm especially good at.
</pre>

<pre>
世界(せかい)の国(くに)<b>の中(なか)で</b>、一度(いちど)行(い)ってみたいのはアイスランドです。
Of all the countries in the world, the one I'd like to visit once is Iceland.
</pre>

<pre>
今学期(こんがっき)習(なら)った文法(ぶんぽう)<b>の中(なか)で</b>、いちばん難(むずか)しかったのは敬語(けいご)でした。
Of the grammar we studied this term, the hardest was the honorific language.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じチームのメンバー<b>の中(なか)で</b>、朝(あさ)いちばん早(はや)く来(く)るのは部長(ぶちょう)です。
Out of everyone on the team, the one who gets in earliest is the department head.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and equally at home in speech and writing. 中(なか) is often
written in kanji and often in kana; neither spelling changes the reading or the
formality.

Two things do vary. First, with a **group of members** the 中(なか) is
frequently dropped in speech: クラスでいちばん足(あし)が速(はや)い says the
same thing as クラスの中(なか)でいちばん足(あし)が速(はや)い, a little more
briskly. Keeping 中(なか) makes the boundary of the set explicit, which is why
it survives in careful speech and in writing.

Second, with a **place or a time span** the 中(なか) is not merely optional - 
it is out of place. 日本(にほん)で, 世界(せかい)で,
一年(いちねん)で, クラスで are locations and stretches of time, not bags of
items, so there is no "inside the set" to point at.
日本(にほん)の中(なか)で is not ungrammatical, but it reads as though you
meant physically within Japan's borders, and most speakers would just say
日本(にほん)で.

Adding `は` - <code>の中(なか)では</code> - topicalises the field and quietly
implies a contrast: "*within this set* it's true; elsewhere, who knows."
和食(わしょく)の中(なか)ではおすしがいちばん好(す)きです leaves room for
some non-Japanese dish to be the overall winner.

## Contrast

### ～の中(なか)で vs bare で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a set of members takes <code>の中(なか)で</code>; a place or a
period takes plain `で`.

</div>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)の中(なか)で父(ちち)がいちばんよく笑(わら)います。
My father laughs the most of anyone in the family.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)で一番(いちばん)古(ふる)いお寺(てら)はどこですか。
Which is the oldest temple in this town?
</pre>

A family is a collection of individuals you can line up and compare - the fence is
real, so 中(なか) earns its place. A town is where the comparing happens, not
what is being compared, and inserting 中(なか) there points at geography instead
of at a set.

### ～の中(なか)で vs ～の中(なか)から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` compares within the set; `から` takes something out of it.

</div>

<pre>
この中(なか)で、いちばん軽(かる)いのはどれですか。
Which of these is the lightest?
</pre>

<pre>
この中(なか)から、好(す)きなものを一(ひと)つ選(えら)んでください。
Please choose one thing you like from these.
</pre>

Verbs of choosing and extracting - 選(えら)ぶ, 取(と)り出(だ)す,
決(き)める - pull the item out, so they want `から`. Adjectival
judgements leave everything where it is, so they want `で`. Using
<code>中(なか)で選(えら)ぶ</code> is not impossible, but it shifts the meaning to
"do your choosing inside this room".

### ～の中(なか)で vs より

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three or more members: <code>の中(なか)で</code> plus a
superlative. Exactly two: `より`.

</div>

<pre>
この三(さん)種類(しゅるい)の中(なか)で、これがいちばん甘(あま)いです。
Of these three varieties, this one is the sweetest.
</pre>

<pre>
こちらのほうがあちら<b>より</b>甘(あま)いです。
This one is sweeter than that one.
</pre>

The mistake to avoid is running a two-item set through the superlative frame.
紅茶(こうちゃ)と緑茶(りょくちゃ)の中(なか)でどちらがいいですか is
overbuilt - with two candidates on the table, drop the fence and just ask
紅茶(こうちゃ)と緑茶(りょくちゃ)と、どちらがいいですか.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この<b>の</b>中(なか)で → ✅ この中(なか)で
  この, その, あの already attach to the following noun. Doubling the `の` is the
  single most frequent slip on this pattern.

- ❌ 果物(くだもの)の中(なか)<b>に</b>みかんがいちばん好(す)きです。 → ✅ 果物(くだもの)の中(なか)<b>で</b>みかんがいちばん好(す)きです。
  `に` would say the tangerines are physically located inside the fruit. `で` is
  the particle that turns a set into an arena for comparison.

- ❌ 犬(いぬ)と猫(ねこ)の中(なか)で、猫(ねこ)がいちばん好(す)きです。 → ✅ 犬(いぬ)と猫(ねこ)と、猫(ねこ)のほうが好(す)きです。
  Two candidates cannot have a "number one". A superlative needs a field of at
  least three.

- ❌ 世界(せかい)の中(なか)でこの湖(みずうみ)がいちばん深(ふか)いです。 → ✅ 世界(せかい)でこの湖(みずうみ)がいちばん深(ふか)いです。
  世界(せかい), 日本(にほん), 町(まち) and the like are places. Attaching
  中(なか) to them sounds like you are drawing a line on a map.

## Practice

**1.** 一年(いちねん)____二月(にがつ)がいちばん寒(さむ)いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一年(いちねん)<b>で</b>二月(にがつ)がいちばん寒(さむ)いです。

A year is a stretch of time, not a bag of members, so it takes plain `で`.
一年(いちねん)の中(なか)で is heard, but it makes the year sound like a
container you are rummaging in; the bare `で` is what a native speaker says.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
三(さん)つのプラン（の中(なか)で・の中(なか)から）一(ひと)つ選(えら)んでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三(さん)つのプラン<b>の中(なか)から</b>一(ひと)つ選(えら)んでください。

選(えら)ぶ physically removes one item from the group, and the source of a
movement is `から`. Use <code>の中(なか)で</code> when nothing is being taken out - 
when you are only judging which member wins.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Of the films I saw last year, this one was the most interesting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

去年(きょねん)見(み)た映画(えいが)<b>の中(なか)で</b>、これがいちばんおもしろかったです。

<code>去年(きょねん)見(み)た</code> is a clause modifying 映画(えいが), and
the whole noun phrase then takes <code>の中(なか)で</code>. Note that
映画(えいが) is plural here without any marking - Japanese leaves number to
context, and the fence does the work of "of the films".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あのの中(なか)にどれが一番(いちばん)便利(べんり)ですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの中(なか)でどれが一番(いちばん)便利(べんり)ですか。

Two fixes. First, あの attaches straight to 中(なか) - there is no `の` in
between, because あの is already the modifier. Second, the particle on
中(なか) must be `で`: the set is the arena in which the comparison is carried
out, and `に` would only say where something sits.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日本料理(にほんりょうり)の中(なか)で、てんぷらがいちばん好(す)きです。
(b) 日本料理(にほんりょうり)の中(なか)では、てんぷらがいちばん好(す)きです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply states the winner inside a stated field: among Japanese dishes,
tempura tops the list.

(b) adds `は`, which topicalises the field and hints at a boundary - "*as far as
Japanese food goes*, tempura." It leaves open that something outside that set,
maybe a curry or a steak, would beat it overall. The `は` is doing its usual
contrastive work.

</details>

# AとBとどちらが

**Meaning** · which is more ～, A or B? - the question form of a two-way comparison

You already know how to *state* a comparison. This is how to *ask* for one. Put
the two candidates side by side with `と`, then let `どちら` stand in for the
answer. The reply comes back with <code>の方(ほう)が</code>, which is why the two
patterns are really one conversation split in half.

## Formation

The frame is fixed: **A と B と、どちらが + adjective + か**. The second `と` is
usually there and usually followed by a comma when written.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two nouns | AとBと、どちらが | 春(はる)と秋(あき)と、どちらが好(す)きですか |
| Two verbs | Vるのと Vるのと、どちらが | 歩(ある)くのと走(はし)るのと、どちらが |
| Two い-adjective options | ～いのと～いのと | 安(やす)いのと軽(かる)いのと |
| With 方(ほう) spelled out | どちらの方(ほう)が | どちらの方(ほう)が近(ちか)いですか |
| Casual | どっち（の方(ほう)）が | どっちが速(はや)い? |
| The answer | Bの方(ほう)が + adjective + です | 秋(あき)の方(ほう)が好(す)きです |
| Answering "both" | どちらも + affirmative | どちらも好(す)きです |
| Answering "neither" | どちらも + negative | どちらも好(す)きではありません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with exactly **two** candidates the question word is
`どちら`, whatever they are. Not `どれ` for things, not `だれ` for people, not
`どこ` for places. Two means `どちら`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `どちら` takes `が`, never `は`. It is the unknown in the sentence, and
unknowns sit on the `が` side. The same applies to the answer:
<code>秋(あき)の方(ほう)が</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒーと紅茶(こうちゃ)と、<b>どちらが</b>いいですか。
Which would you like, coffee or tea?
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)と夜(よる)と、<b>どちらが</b>忙(いそが)しいですか。
Which is busier for you, mornings or evenings?
</pre>

<pre>
新幹線(しんかんせん)と飛行機(ひこうき)と、<b>どちらが</b>安(やす)いですか。
Which is cheaper, the bullet train or flying?
</pre>

<pre>
この二(ふた)つ、<b>どっちが</b>似合(にあ)うと思(おも)う?
Which of these two do you think suits me better?
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で作(つく)るのと店(みせ)で買(か)うのと、<b>どちらが</b>早(はや)いでしょうか。
Which would be quicker, making it myself or buying it in a shop?
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんと山田(やまだ)さんと、<b>どちらが</b>先(さき)に着(つ)きましたか。
Which of them arrived first, Tanaka or Yamada?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は水曜日(すいようび)と木曜日(もくようび)と、<b>どちらが</b>ご都合(つごう)がよろしいですか。
For the meeting, which suits you better - Wednesday or Thursday?
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)すなら、駅(えき)の近(ちか)くと公園(こうえん)の近(ちか)くと、<b>どちらが</b>いいと思(おも)いますか。
If you were moving, which do you think would be better - near the station or near the park?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`どちら` is the neutral-to-polite form and works everywhere: with strangers, with
customers, in writing. `どっち` is its casual contraction and is extremely common
in relaxed speech among friends and family. There is nothing rude about `どっち`
between equals, but it lands badly in a shop or an office.

Adding <code>の方(ほう)</code> - どちらの方(ほう)が - is slightly more deliberate
and a shade more formal. It is never wrong, and it is what you hear when someone
is choosing their words carefully.

`どちら` has a second job worth knowing: it is the **polite word for "where" and
"which one"** in general. どちらからいらっしゃいましたか means "where have you
come from", and どちらさまですか is a formal "who are you?". So when you hear
`どちら`, check whether two options were actually put on the table.

Politeness at the top end: いかがですか or よろしいですか replace いいですか,
and でしょうか softens the question further. To a customer, a shop assistant is
more likely to say どちらになさいますか than どちらがいいですか.

One thing English speakers forget: this is **not** a yes/no question. Answering
はい to どちらがいいですか leaves the other person waiting.

## Contrast

### どちらが vs どれが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - two candidates: `どちら`. Three or more: `どれ`.

</div>

<pre>
赤(あか)と青(あお)と、<b>どちらが</b>好(す)きですか。
Which do you prefer, red or blue?
</pre>

<pre>
この五(いつ)つの色(いろ)の中(なか)で、<b>どれが</b>好(す)きですか。
Of these five colours, which do you like?
</pre>

The split is about how many options exist, not about what kind of thing they are.
This is also why the two patterns come with different frames: `どれ` normally
needs a stated field with <code>の中(なか)で</code>, while `どちら` carries its
own field in the AとBと part.

### どちらが vs どっちが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same grammar, different clothes. `どっち` is the casual form;
`どちら` is the one to default to when you are unsure.

</div>

<pre>
ねえ、<b>どっちが</b>おいしそう?
Hey, which one looks better?
</pre>

<pre>
お飲(の)み物(もの)は、<b>どちらが</b>よろしいでしょうか。
Which would you prefer to drink?
</pre>

Notice that the register is carried by the *whole* sentence, not just the question
word. どっち sits with plain forms and a rising intonation; どちら sits with
です・ます, よろしい and でしょうか. Mixing them - ❌どっちがよろしいでしょうか - 
sounds like two people talking at once.

### どちらが vs どちらか・どちらも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どちらが` asks which one. `どちらか` means "one or the other,
I don't mind which". `どちらも` means "both" with an affirmative verb and
"neither" with a negative one.

</div>

<pre>
傘(かさ)は、<b>どちらが</b>軽(かる)いですか。
Which umbrella is lighter?
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)は<b>どちらか</b>一(ひと)つ持(も)っていってください。
Take one umbrella or the other with you.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)は<b>どちらも</b>古(ふる)いです。
Both umbrellas are old.
</pre>

`か` and `も` are doing exactly what they do after `だれ` and `なに`: `か`
makes the identity vague, `も` sweeps in everything. The negative flip on
`どちらも` is automatic - どちらも新(あたら)しくないです is "neither is new".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 紅茶(こうちゃ)と緑茶(りょくちゃ)と、どれが好(す)きですか。 → ✅ 紅茶(こうちゃ)と緑茶(りょくちゃ)と、<b>どちらが</b>好(す)きですか。
  Only two things were named. `どれ` needs a field of three or more.

- ❌ 姉(あね)と妹(いもうと)と、だれが背(せ)が高(たか)いですか。 → ✅ 姉(あね)と妹(いもうと)と、<b>どちらが</b>背(せ)が高(たか)いですか。
  Even for people, two options means `どちら`. `だれ` opens the field to everybody.

- ❌ どちら<b>は</b>安(やす)いですか。 → ✅ どちら<b>が</b>安(やす)いですか。
  A question word is the unknown, and it cannot be a topic. `どちらが`, always.

- ❌ 「どちらが好(す)きですか。」「はい、コーヒーです。」 → ✅ 「コーヒーの方(ほう)が好(す)きです。」
  This is a choice question, not a yes/no one. The answer names a winner, normally
  with <code>の方(ほう)が</code>.

## Practice

**1.** バスと電車(でんしゃ)と、____が便利(べんり)ですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

バスと電車(でんしゃ)と、<b>どちら</b>が便利(べんり)ですか。

Two candidates have been put on the table, so the question word is `どちら`, and
it takes `が` because it stands for the unknown. In casual speech the same
sentence becomes バスと電車(でんしゃ)、どっちが便利(べんり)?

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option, spoken to a client:
お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)とカードと、（どっち・どちら）がよろしいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)とカードと、<b>どちら</b>がよろしいですか。

The rest of the sentence is fully polite - お支払(しはら)い, よろしい - so the
casual `どっち` would clash with it. Register has to be consistent across the whole
sentence, not just at the end.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Which is harder, reading or listening?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

読(よ)むのと聞(き)くのと、<b>どちらが</b>難(むずか)しいですか。

Verbs cannot be joined by `と` directly, so each one is nominalised with `の`
first. <code>読(よ)むこと</code> and <code>聞(き)くこと</code> also work and sound
slightly more written.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 東京(とうきょう)と大阪(おおさか)と、どちらは人口(じんこう)が多(おお)いですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 東京(とうきょう)と大阪(おおさか)と、<b>どちらが</b>人口(じんこう)が多(おお)いですか。

`どちらは` is impossible: `は` presents old, settled information, and a question
word is by definition the thing nobody has settled yet. Two `が` in one sentence
looks odd to an English speaker but is entirely normal here - the first marks the
unknown, the second marks what is numerous.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 和食(わしょく)と洋食(ようしょく)と、どちらが好(す)きですか。
(b) 和食(わしょく)と洋食(ようしょく)と、どちらも好(す)きですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) assumes you have a preference and asks which way it falls. It cannot be
answered with はい - the reply names a winner, normally
和食(わしょく)の方(ほう)が好(す)きです.

(b) sweeps both options in at once and asks whether you like them equally. It
**is** a yes/no question, and はい、どちらも好(す)きです is a complete answer.
`が` singles one out; `も` refuses to.

</details>

# など

**Meaning** · such as; things like - it declares a list unfinished

`など` is a full stop that refuses to be one. You name one or two items, add
`など`, and the listener understands that more of the same kind exist but are not
worth spelling out. Its second use grows straight out of the first: if you present
something as a mere example, you are also saying it is nothing special - which is
how `など` becomes modest about yourself and dismissive about everything else.

## Formation

`など` follows the **last** item of the list. The case particle for the whole
phrase then follows `など`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + など | ペン → ペンなど |
| A や-list | AやB + など | パンや卵(たまご)など |
| A single example | Noun + など | お茶(ちゃ)など |
| Before a case particle | ～など + が／を／に／で | 野菜(やさい)などを買(か)う |
| Quoted words | ～などと言(い)う／思(おも)う | 無理(むり)だなどと言(い)う |
| Dismissive / humble | Noun + など + deprecating predicate | お礼(れい)など要(い)りません |
| Casual equivalent | なんか | ケーキなんか |
| Emphatic equivalent | なんて | 私(わたし)なんて |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `や` *joins* items; `など` *closes* the list. They are not
alternatives, and the two together - AやBなど - is the most common shape of all.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `など` attaches to nouns. To leave a list of **actions** open, use
`～とか～とか` or the `～たり～たり` frame instead; ❌走(はし)るなど泳(およ)ぐ
is not a sentence. In writing you will meet ～などする
（運動(うんどう)するなどして）, but that is a formal construction, not everyday
speech.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
かばんの中(なか)に財布(さいふ)や鍵(かぎ)<b>など</b>が入(はい)っています。
There's a wallet, keys and so on in the bag.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんはおにぎり<b>など</b>でいいです。
Rice balls or something will do for lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)にはコンビニや郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)<b>など</b>があって便利(べんり)です。
There's a convenience store, a post office and the like around here, so it's handy.
</pre>

<pre>
お礼(れい)<b>など</b>要(い)りませんよ。
There's really no need to thank me.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>など</b>にはとても無理(むり)です。
Someone like me could never manage that.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)では予算(よさん)や日程(にってい)<b>など</b>について話(はな)し合(あ)いました。
At the meeting we discussed the budget, the schedule and so forth.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は「もう続(つづ)けられない」<b>など</b>と言(い)っていました。
He was saying things like "I can't keep this up any more".
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)に行(い)くときは、パスポートや保険証(ほけんしょう)<b>など</b>を忘(わす)れないようにしてください。
When you travel, try not to forget your passport, insurance card and the like.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

In its plain listing sense `など` is neutral, and if it leans anywhere it leans
**written and careful**. It is the form you want in a report, an announcement or a
polite explanation to a customer.

Its two informal twins are worth keeping straight:

- **なんか** is the everyday spoken form. Same meaning, no formality.
  漫画(まんが)なんか読(よ)まないよ. Very frequent, completely ordinary in
  conversation, out of place in writing.
- **なんて** adds feeling - surprise, contempt, or dismissal.
  日本語(にほんご)なんて簡単(かんたん)だ is boastful; 私(わたし)なんて
  is self-deprecating. It is not a neutral option.

The **softening** use is one you will hear constantly and rarely see taught:
offering something as a mere example makes the offer less pushy.
コーヒーなどいかがですか is warmer and less presumptuous than
コーヒーはいかがですか, because it pretends not to have decided anything for you.

The dismissive use cuts both ways, and the direction depends on **who** the noun
refers to. Pointed at yourself it is humble and perfectly polite:
私(わたし)などが申(もう)し上(あ)げることではありませんが. Pointed at
someone else it belittles them, so
❌田中(たなか)さんなどに聞(き)いてみます, said in front of Tanaka, is an
insult you did not intend.

## Contrast

### など vs や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `や` sits **between** two nouns and links them. `など` sits
**after** the last noun and stops the list without finishing it.

</div>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にノート<b>や</b>はさみがあります。
There's a notebook, scissors and so on on the desk.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にノートやはさみ<b>など</b>があります。
There's a notebook, scissors and that sort of thing on the desk.
</pre>

`や` already implies the list is partial, so `など` is not strictly required - it
just makes the implication explicit and a little more polite. What you cannot do
is swap their positions: ❌ノートなどはさみ joins nothing.

### など vs なんか・なんて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one meaning, three registers: `など` written and neutral,
`なんか` casual and spoken, `なんて` emotional.

</div>

<pre>
報告書(ほうこくしょ)には人数(にんずう)や費用(ひよう)<b>など</b>を書(か)きます。
In the report we write the number of people, the cost and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなこと、私(わたし)<b>なんか</b>に聞(き)かないでよ。
Don't ask me about that, of all people.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で行(い)く<b>なんて</b>、あぶないよ。
Going alone - that's dangerous.
</pre>

Note the third one: `なんて` will attach to a whole verb phrase, which `など`
resists in speech. `なんて` also tends to run straight into the predicate without
a case particle behind it.

### など vs とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とか` is repeated after **each** item and is casual; `など`
appears **once** at the end and is the neutral or written choice.

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は買(か)い物(もの)<b>とか</b>散歩(さんぽ)<b>とか</b>しています。
At weekends I go shopping, take walks, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は買(か)い物(もの)や散歩(さんぽ)<b>など</b>をしています。
At weekends I do things like shopping and going for walks.
</pre>

Both say the same thing about the world. The difference is who is speaking: the
first is a friend over coffee, the second could go in a self-introduction. `とか`
also attaches happily to verbs and whole clauses, where `など` has to be helped
along by a noun.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ パン<b>など</b>卵(たまご)を買(か)いました。 → ✅ パン<b>や</b>卵(たまご)<b>など</b>を買(か)いました。
  `など` is not a conjunction. It cannot stand between two nouns; it can only
  close the list from behind.

- ❌ 野菜(やさい)<b>を</b>など買(か)います。 → ✅ 野菜(やさい)<b>など</b>を買(か)います。
  The case particle goes **outside** `など`, because `など` belongs to the noun
  phrase and `を` belongs to the sentence.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)<b>など</b>にご相談(そうだん)します。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)にご相談(そうだん)します。
  Attaching `など` to a person you are showing respect to reduces them to a random
  example. Keep the dismissive `など` for yourself.

- ❌ この資料(しりょう)には住所(じゅうしょ)や電話番号(でんわばんごう)<b>なんか</b>が書(か)いてあります。 → ✅ ～電話番号(でんわばんごう)<b>など</b>が書(か)いてあります。
  Not ungrammatical, but `なんか` is spoken register and jars against a written
  document. Match the word to the medium.

## Practice

**1.** 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)____チーズ____入(はい)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)<b>や</b>チーズ<b>など</b>が入(はい)っています。

`や` links the two nouns; `など` closes the list off as partial. This double
frame - AやBなど - is the default shape for an open-ended list of things, and
`が` then attaches after `など`.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option for a company email:
会場(かいじょう)や時間(じかん)（など・なんか）は後(あと)でお知(し)らせします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会場(かいじょう)や時間(じかん)<b>など</b>は後(あと)でお知(し)らせします。

`なんか` carries the same meaning but belongs to casual speech, and it would sit
badly next to the humble お知(し)らせします. In writing, `など` is the only
comfortable choice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Would you like some tea or something?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お茶(ちゃ)<b>など</b>いかがですか。

`など` is doing softening work here rather than listing work: by offering tea as
just one example of what might be available, you avoid pinning the other person
down. お茶(ちゃ)でもいかがですか is the more casual version of the same move.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山田(やまだ)さんなどが手伝(てつだ)ってくださいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山田(やまだ)さんが手伝(てつだ)ってくださいました。

The sentence uses くださる, an honorific, and then undercuts it with `など`, which
treats 山田(やまだ)さん as one interchangeable example among many. Delete the
`など`. If you genuinely mean "Yamada and others", say
山田(やまだ)さんたち.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)は料理(りょうり)が下手(へた)です。
(b) 私(わたし)など料理(りょうり)が下手(へた)で、お見(み)せできません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of fact about your cooking, with no attitude attached.

(b) uses `など` to put yourself forward as a nobody - "someone like me". It is a
humbling move, common when declining praise or an invitation to demonstrate
something, and it makes the sentence noticeably more modest and more formal. Aimed
at anyone other than yourself, the same word would be rude.

</details>

# ～とか～とか

**Meaning** · things like A and B - a casual, deliberately incomplete list

`とか` tags each item as *one example among others*. Two or three go on the
record, the rest stay implied, and the whole thing sounds relaxed rather than
official. Unlike the tidier `や`, it will happily list verbs, adjectives and whole
complaints, which is why spoken Japanese leans on it so heavily.

## Formation

`とか` follows **each** item, including - usually - the last one.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Nとか Nとか | すし → すしとかラーメンとか |
| Verb (plain form) | Vるとか Vるとか | 歩(ある)くとか走(はし)るとか |
| Verb (past) | Vたとか Vたとか | 忘(わす)れたとか落(お)としたとか |
| い-adjective | ～いとか | 暑(あつ)いとか寒(さむ)いとか |
| な-adjective / noun | ～だとか | 静(しず)かだとか便利(べんり)だとか |
| A single item | Nとか（one only） | 明日(あした)とか、どう? |
| Before a case particle | ～とか + が／を／に | パンとかを買(か)う（often dropped） |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the final `とか` is optional but normal. Leaving it on is what
makes the list feel open; cutting it off after the last item makes the sentence
sound as though you had planned it.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - with な-adjectives and nouns the standard written form inserts `だ`:
静(しず)かだとか. In fast speech you will hear the bare 静(しず)かとか as
well; treat `だとか` as the safe form.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
朝(あさ)はパン<b>とか</b>ヨーグルト<b>とか</b>を食(た)べます。
For breakfast I have bread, yoghurt, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)はジャズ<b>とか</b>クラシック<b>とか</b>が好(す)きです。
Music-wise I like jazz, classical and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)<b>とか</b>、時間(じかん)ある?
Are you free tomorrow or something?
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)は部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)ける<b>とか</b>、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)く<b>とか</b>しています。
On my days off I tidy the flat, go shopping, things like that.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は忙(いそが)しい<b>とか</b>疲(つか)れた<b>とか</b>言(い)って、集(あつ)まりに来(こ)ません。
He says he's busy, he's tired and so on, and never turns up to the get-togethers.
</pre>

<pre>
沖縄(おきなわ)<b>とか</b>北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>とか</b>、遠(とお)いところへ行(い)ってみたいなあ。
Okinawa, Hokkaido - I'd love to go somewhere far away like that.
</pre>

<pre>
アルバイトを探(さが)すなら、カフェ<b>とか</b>本屋(ほんや)<b>とか</b>がいいんじゃない?
If you're looking for part-time work, wouldn't a cafe or a bookshop or somewhere be good?
</pre>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)では志望(しぼう)の理由(りゆう)<b>とか</b>将来(しょうらい)の計画(けいかく)<b>とか</b>を聞(き)かれました。
In the interview they asked me about my reasons for applying, my future plans, that kind of thing.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`とか` is **casual and spoken**. It is not slang and no one will think less of you
for it in conversation, but it does not belong in a report, a formal email or an
exam essay - the neutral equivalents there are `や` and `など`.

Piled on too thickly it makes a speaker sound vague and non-committal, which is
exactly why it is useful when you *want* to be non-committal:

- **Softening a proposal.** 来週(らいしゅう)の火曜日(かようび)とか、どうですか
  floats a date instead of demanding one. This is one of the most common modern
  uses and it involves no listing at all.
- **Distancing yourself from what you are reporting.**
  中止(ちゅうし)になるとか聞(き)きました - "I heard something about it being
  cancelled." You pass on the information without vouching for it.
- **Quoting a complaint you find tiresome.** The
  <code>～とか～とか言(い)って</code> frame in the fifth example above carries an
  audible eye-roll.

`とかいう` deserves its own line: 田中(たなか)とかいう人(ひと)が来(き)ました
means "some person called Tanaka, or something like that, came". It signals that
you barely registered the name - polite in a self-effacing way when the name is
yours to remember, faintly rude when it is a person the listener cares about.

Written forms of the same word exist（`だとか`, `～とか～とかいう`）, but if you are
choosing between registers, the rule is simple: speech takes `とか`, paper takes
`や` and `など`.

## Contrast

### とか vs や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both leave the list open. `や` goes **between** nouns only and
is register-neutral; `とか` goes **after each** item, is casual, and takes verbs
and adjectives as well.

</div>

<pre>
棚(たな)に辞書(じしょ)<b>や</b>地図(ちず)が置(お)いてあります。
There are dictionaries, maps and so on on the shelf.
</pre>

<pre>
棚(たな)に辞書(じしょ)<b>とか</b>地図(ちず)<b>とか</b>置(お)いてあるよ。
There are dictionaries and maps and stuff on the shelf.
</pre>

The first could be a museum label; the second could not. And where `や` simply
stops working, `とか` carries on:

<pre>
泳(およ)ぐ<b>とか</b>走(はし)る<b>とか</b>、体(からだ)を動(うご)かすことが好(す)きです。
I like moving my body - swimming, running, that sort of thing.
</pre>

❌泳(およ)ぐや走(はし)る is impossible: `や` joins nouns and nothing else.

### とか vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～たり～たり` lists **actions** and has to be closed with
`する`. `とか` just lists, and needs no closing verb.

</div>

<pre>
夜(よる)は音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)い<b>たり</b>、本(ほん)を読(よ)ん<b>だり</b>します。
In the evening I listen to music, read, and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
夜(よる)は音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)く<b>とか</b>、本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>とか</b>かな。
Evenings are, like, listening to music or reading, I suppose.
</pre>

`～たり` is neutral in register and grammatically tighter - the `する` at the end
is not optional. `とか` is looser in both senses. Note also that `とか` can list
nouns, which `～たり` cannot.

### とか vs か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か` offers a genuine either/or. `とか` offers examples and
asks you to choose nothing.

</div>

<pre>
コーヒー<b>か</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)を持(も)ってきてください。
Please bring coffee or tea.
</pre>

<pre>
コーヒー<b>とか</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)<b>とか</b>を持(も)ってきてください。
Please bring coffee, tea, something along those lines.
</pre>

With `か` the listener picks one of exactly two. With `とか` the two named drinks
are only illustrations, and green tea would satisfy the request perfectly well.
This is also why a question offering a real choice cannot use `とか`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 泳(およ)ぐや走(はし)るが好(す)きです。 → ✅ 泳(およ)ぐ<b>とか</b>走(はし)る<b>とか</b>が好(す)きです。
  `や` links nouns and only nouns. When the items are actions, `とか` is the
  listing particle that works.

- ❌ この書類(しょるい)には名前(なまえ)とか住所(じゅうしょ)とかを書(か)いてください。 → ✅ ～名前(なまえ)や住所(じゅうしょ)などを書(か)いてください。
  Grammatically fine, socially wrong. A written instruction takes `や` and `など`;
  `とか` makes an official form sound like a chat.

- ❌ 肉(にく)とか魚(さかな)とか、どちらが好(す)きですか。 → ✅ 肉(にく)と魚(さかな)と、どちらが好(す)きですか。
  `どちら` asks for a choice between two fixed options, but `とか` says the two
  named items are merely examples. The two ideas cancel each other out.

- ❌ とかコーヒーとか紅茶(こうちゃ)が飲(の)みたい。 → ✅ コーヒー<b>とか</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)<b>とか</b>飲(の)みたい。
  `とか` is a postposition: it follows the item it tags. It never leads.

## Practice

**1.** 旅行(りょこう)のとき、カメラ____地図(ちず)____持(も)っていきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

旅行(りょこう)のとき、カメラ<b>とか</b>地図(ちず)<b>とか</b>持(も)っていきます。

One `とか` after each item, and the `を` is comfortably dropped in speech. Written
neutrally, the same sentence would be
カメラや地図(ちず)などを持(も)っていきます.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option for a job application form:
趣味(しゅみ)は読書(どくしょ)（とか・や）映画(えいが)（とか・など）です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

趣味(しゅみ)は読書(どくしょ)<b>や</b>映画(えいが)<b>など</b>です。

A form is written register. `とか` would read as if you had wandered in mid
conversation. Keep `とか` for the interview itself, where speaking casually about
your hobbies is perfectly appropriate.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "Shall we do something on Saturday or whenever?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

土曜日(どようび)<b>とか</b>、何(なに)かしない?

Here `とか` is not listing anything - a single `とか` after a date softens the
proposal into a suggestion the other person can decline without awkwardness. This
vagueness-as-politeness use is everywhere in casual Japanese.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)では予算(よさん)とかスケジュールとかについて報告(ほうこく)いたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)では予算(よさん)<b>や</b>スケジュール<b>など</b>について報告(ほうこく)いたします。

いたします is humble, formal Japanese, and `とか` is casual speech; the two cannot
share a sentence. Swap in `や` and `など` and the register lines up from beginning
to end.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 昼(ひる)ごはんはうどんかそばにします。
(b) 昼(ひる)ごはんはうどんとかそばとかにします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) narrows lunch down to two candidates and commits to picking one of them. The
decision is nearly made.

(b) names udon and soba only as illustrations of the sort of thing you feel like - 
noodles, something light, whatever the shop has. Ramen would not contradict it.
`か` closes a set; `とか` opens one.

</details>

# でも

**Meaning** · ～ or something; ～ or the like - names one thing as a token example

Attach `でも` to a noun in a suggestion and the noun stops being a demand. You put
one option on the table as a representative of a whole class of acceptable
options, and the listener is left free to counter with something else. It is the
single most useful politeness trick in casual invitations.

## Formation

`でも` follows the noun. It replaces `が` and `を`, and stacks on top of `に`,
`へ` and `と`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + でも | お茶(ちゃ) → お茶(ちゃ)でも |
| Noun taking を | を is **dropped** | ケーキでも食(た)べよう |
| Noun taking が | が is **dropped** | 音楽(おんがく)でもあるといいね |
| Noun taking に／へ／と | に／へ／と + でも | 海(うみ)にでも行(い)こう |
| Question word | いつ／どこ／だれ／なに + でも | いつでも／だれでも（＝any） |
| Typical predicates | ～でも + どう／いかが／～ませんか／～よう | お茶(ちゃ)でもいかがですか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this `でも` lives in sentences about **what might happen**:
invitations, suggestions, offers, plans, volitional forms. It cannot report a
completed fact. ❌昨日(きのう)コーヒーでも飲(の)みました is not a sentence you
can rescue with context.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - three different things share these two kana. This page is about the
one that follows a **noun** in a suggestion. There is also the concessive
`～ても／でも` that follows a verb or an adjective
（雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っても, "even if it rains"）, and the sentence-initial
`でも` meaning "but". Same shape, unrelated jobs.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
少(すこ)し休(やす)んで、お茶(ちゃ)<b>でも</b>飲(の)みませんか。
Let's take a break - shall we have some tea or something?
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)<b>でも</b>見(み)に行(い)こうか。
Shall we go and see a film or something?
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は公園(こうえん)に<b>でも</b>行(い)きましょうか。
Shall we go to the park or somewhere at the weekend?
</pre>

<pre>
暇(ひま)なら、本(ほん)<b>でも</b>読(よ)んだらどう?
If you're free, why not read a book or something?
</pre>

<pre>
ケーキ<b>でも</b>買(か)って帰(かえ)ろうかな。
Maybe I'll pick up a cake or something on the way home.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で悩(なや)まないで、先生(せんせい)に<b>でも</b>相談(そうだん)してみたらどうですか。
Don't sit with it on your own - why not talk to your teacher or somebody?
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れたでしょう。冷(つめ)たいもの<b>でも</b>飲(の)んで、少(すこ)し休(やす)んでください。
You must be tired. Have something cold to drink and take a rest.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の土曜日(どようび)、時間(じかん)があったら食事(しょくじ)に<b>でも</b>行(い)きませんか。
If you have time next Saturday, would you like to go out for a meal or something?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The whole point of this `でも` is **not committing the listener**. Compare the two
invitations:

- お茶(ちゃ)を飲(の)みませんか - "would you like to drink tea?" The listener
  has to accept tea or refuse the invitation.
- お茶(ちゃ)でも飲(の)みませんか - "shall we have tea or something?" The
  listener can say "sure, but I'd rather have juice" without refusing anything.

That softness makes it standard in casual and semi-polite invitations at every
level of formality: ～でも食(た)べようか between friends,
～でもいかがですか to a guest. It is register-neutral in itself; the politeness is
carried by the ending.

There is one situation where it backfires. If the other person has just told you
exactly what they want, offering it back with `でも` treats their request as
arbitrary. 「コーヒーが飲(の)みたい」「じゃあコーヒーでも買(か)ってくるね」
reads as mildly dismissive - they asked for coffee, not for coffee-or-whatever.
Drop the `でも` and use `を`.

The related **question word + でも** pattern - いつでも, どこでも, だれでも,
何(なん)でも - means "any at all", and it is a different construction with the
same particle: だれでもできる仕事(しごと)です, "it's a job anyone can do".
Worth knowing here because learners meet both in the same week and mix them.

Finally, `でも` is not the only softener of its kind. `とか` does a similar job
（明日(あした)とかどう?）but is more casual and attaches happily to times and
dates; `でも` sits more comfortably with the thing being consumed or done.

## Contrast

### ～でも vs ～を

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` names the thing you mean. `でも` names one thing you would
accept.

</div>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんにそば<b>を</b>食(た)べましょう。
Let's have soba for lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんにそば<b>でも</b>食(た)べましょう。
Let's have soba or something for lunch.
</pre>

The first has decided; the second is thinking aloud. Note that `を` disappears
entirely when `でも` moves in - ❌そばをでも is impossible, because both are
fighting for the same slot.

### Noun + でも vs question word + でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - noun + `でも` offers an example. Question word + `でも` sweeps
in the whole category: "any at all".

</div>

<pre>
飲(の)み物(もの)<b>でも</b>買(か)ってきます。
I'll go and buy some drinks or something.
</pre>

<pre>
飲(の)み物(もの)なら<b>何(なん)でも</b>いいです。
As for drinks, anything at all is fine.
</pre>

The first suggests one purchase among several possible ones; the second refuses to
narrow anything down. The same contrast runs through the family: どこでも
"anywhere", いつでも "any time", だれでも "anyone".

### ～でも vs ～ても・でも（even if）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - check what comes **before** it. A noun means "or something".
A verb or adjective stem means "even if".

</div>

<pre>
散歩(さんぽ)に<b>でも</b>行(い)きませんか。
Shall we go for a walk or something?
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>散歩(さんぽ)に行(い)きます。
I'm going for a walk even if it rains.
</pre>

They are not related in meaning at all, and the second one is not really `でも` - 
it is the て-form plus `も`, which turns into `でも` only after verbs whose
て-form ends in `で`（読(よ)んでも）. And a `でも` at the very front of a
sentence is the conjunction "but": でも、今日(きょう)は忙(いそが)しいです。

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ケーキ<b>を</b>でも食(た)べましょう。 → ✅ ケーキ<b>でも</b>食(た)べましょう。
  `でも` takes the place of `を` and `が`. Only `に`, `へ` and `と` survive
  alongside it.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)コーヒー<b>でも</b>飲(の)みました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)コーヒー<b>を</b>飲(の)みました。
  You cannot be vague about what you already did. This `でも` only works where the
  action is still open - suggestions, offers, plans.

- ❌ 「紅茶(こうちゃ)がほしいです。」「じゃ、紅茶(こうちゃ)<b>でも</b>入(い)れますね。」 → ✅ 「じゃ、紅茶(こうちゃ)<b>を</b>入(い)れますね。」
  When the other person has named exactly what they want, `でも` says you were not
  really listening.

- ❌ でも、音楽(おんがく)聞(き)きませんか。（meaning "music or something"） → ✅ 音楽(おんがく)<b>でも</b>聞(き)きませんか。
  At the head of a sentence `でも` is the conjunction "but". The softening `でも`
  has to sit on the noun.

## Practice

**1.** 雨(あめ)でどこにも行(い)けませんね。うちでDVD____見(み)ましょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

うちでDVD<b>でも</b>見(み)ましょうか。

The speaker is proposing a way to spend the afternoon, not insisting on DVDs. With
`でも` the other person can suggest a board game instead without turning anything
down, which is exactly what makes the invitation comfortable.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
「何(なに)が食(た)べたい?」「うーん、ラーメン（でも・が）いいかな。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「うーん、ラーメン<b>でも</b>いいかな。」

You have been asked to choose and you are staying deliberately loose - ramen is a
suggestion, not a decision. Answering ラーメン<b>が</b>いい would be a firm choice,
which is fine too, but it is a different reply: it commits you.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Shall we go to a cafe or somewhere?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

カフェに<b>でも</b>行(い)きませんか。

`に` marks the destination and survives, with `でも` stacking behind it. Note the
word order - ❌でもにカフェ and ❌カフェでもに are both impossible; the case
particle comes first and `でも` follows.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 先週(せんしゅう)の日曜日(にちようび)、友(とも)だちと買(か)い物(もの)でも行(い)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先週(せんしゅう)の日曜日(にちようび)、友(とも)だちと買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)きました。

Two problems, one cause. The sentence reports something that already happened, so
the vague `でも` has nothing to be vague about; and with 行(い)く the
destination needs `に`. If you want the softened version, move it into the future:
買(か)い物(もの)にでも行(い)きませんか.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山田(やまだ)さんに聞(き)いてみます。
(b) 山田(やまだ)さんに<b>でも</b>聞(き)いてみます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says you will ask Yamada, because Yamada is the person who would know.

(b) presents Yamada as one plausible person among several - "I'll ask Yamada or
somebody". It sounds less certain and less planned, which can be a relaxed, modest
way to talk about your own next step. Said in front of Yamada, though, it is a
little unflattering: it implies he was not the obvious choice.

</details>

# ばかり - only; nothing but

**Meaning** · nothing but ～; ～ and nothing else - usually with the feeling that
there is too much of it

`ばかり` draws the same line `だけ` does, then adds an opinion. It says one thing
fills the whole picture and, nine times out of ten, that this is too much of it.
It is also not a mathematician: someone who
肉(にく)ばかり食(た)べている probably eats a salad occasionally. `ばかり` is
about **proportion and attitude**, not arithmetic.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ばかり | マンガ → マンガばかり |
| Verb (habitual excess) | て-form + ばかりいる | 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)んでばかりいる |
| Verb (plain form) | V(plain) + ばかりだ | 文句(もんく)を言(い)うばかりだ |
| Modifying a noun | ～ばかり + の + Noun | 男(おとこ)の人(ひと)ばかりのクラス |
| After が／を | が and を are **dropped** | ❌水(みず)をばかり → ✅水(みず)ばかり |
| After に／で／と | particle is **kept** | 外(そと)でばかり食(た)べる |
| Casual | ばっかり／ばっか | マンガばっかり |
| Quantity (approximately) | Number + ばかり | 三日(みっか)ばかり（written, dated） |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can add "…and I'm getting tired of it" to the English,
`ばかり` is the right word. If you are just stating a boundary, you want `だけ`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はゲーム<b>ばかり</b>しています。
My little brother does nothing but play games.
</pre>

<pre>
甘(あま)いもの<b>ばかり</b>食(た)べてはいけませんよ。
You mustn't live on nothing but sweets.
</pre>

<pre>
文句(もんく)<b>ばかり</b>言(い)わないで、手伝(てつだ)ってよ。
Stop complaining all the time and give me a hand.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は寝(ね)て<b>ばかり</b>いて、勉強(べんきょう)しようとしません。
My son does nothing but sleep - he won't even try to study.
</pre>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)、雨(あめ)の日(ひ)<b>ばかり</b>で洗濯物(せんたくもの)が乾(かわ)きません。
Lately it's been rain, rain, rain, and the washing won't dry.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)は自分(じぶん)の話(はなし)<b>ばかり</b>して、人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)きません。
He only ever talks about himself and never listens to anyone else.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)では同(おな)じ話(はなし)<b>ばかり</b>繰(く)り返(かえ)していて、時間(じかん)だけが過(す)ぎていきました。
In the meeting they went over the same ground again and again, and the time just drained away.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)はいいこと<b>ばかり</b>で、本当(ほんとう)に幸(しあわ)せな一年(いちねん)でした。
Last year was nothing but good news - a genuinely happy year.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ばかり` itself is register-neutral: 遊(あそ)んでばかりいます and
遊(あそ)んでばかりいる differ only in politeness of ending. The contraction
`ばっかり`（and the clipped `ばっか`）is casual and adds emphasis and feeling - 
ゲームばっかりして! is what a parent shouts, not what a report says.

The attitude is the heart of the word. In the great majority of sentences it is
**criticism**, either of somebody else（文句(もんく)ばかり）or, turned inward,
an apology（ご迷惑(めいわく)ばかりかけてすみません, "I do nothing but cause you
trouble"）. That inward use is genuinely humble and very common in polite speech.

But the underlying idea is **excess**, not badness, and excess is occasionally
welcome. いいことばかりでした and
好(す)きな人(ひと)ばかり集(あつ)まったパーティー are both positive. What
you cannot do is use `ばかり` neutrally: it always comments.

Two smaller points:

- The counting `ばかり` - 一週間(いっしゅうかん)ばかり休(やす)みます, "I'll
  take about a week off" - is formal and slightly old-fashioned. Recognise it;
  in speech use `ぐらい`.
- <code>～たばかり</code> is a **different pattern** meaning "just did", and it is
  built on the past form: 着(つ)いたばかりです, "I've only just arrived". The
  contrast section below separates them.

## Contrast

### ばかり vs だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけ` reports a boundary and says nothing about how it feels.
`ばかり` says the boundary is *too tight*, and does not claim to be exact.

</div>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんはパン<b>だけ</b>でした。
Lunch was just bread.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんはパン<b>ばかり</b>です。
It's bread for lunch every single time.
</pre>

The first is a fact about one lunch: bread, nothing else, no comment. The second
complains about a pattern across many lunches - and would still be a fair thing to
say on a day when you also had an apple. That inexactness is why `ばかり` and a
precise number sit badly together: 三人(さんにん)だけ来(き)ました is a count,
三人(さんにん)ばかり来(き)ました means "about three people came" and belongs
to the other, formal `ばかり`.

<pre>
テレビを見(み)ている人(ひと)<b>ばかり</b>で、手伝(てつだ)ってくれる人(ひと)がいません。
Everyone's just sitting watching television and nobody will help.
</pre>

Swap `だけ` in there and the annoyance evaporates.

### ばかり vs ～しか～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばかり` complains that there is **too much of one thing**.
<code>しか～ない</code> complains that there is **not enough of everything else**.

</div>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)にビール<b>ばかり</b>入(はい)っています。
The fridge is full of nothing but beer.
</pre>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)にビール<b>しか</b>入(はい)っていません。
There's nothing in the fridge but beer.
</pre>

Both describe the same fridge and both are unhappy, but they are unhappy about
different things. The first is disgusted by the quantity of beer; the second is
disappointed that there is no food. Note the grammar too: `ばかり` takes a positive
verb, `しか` demands a negative one.

### Noun + ばかり vs ～たばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what is in front. A **noun** or a **て-form** gives
you "nothing but". A **past-tense verb** gives you "just did".

</div>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に来(き)た<b>ばかり</b>で、まだ漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)めません。
I've only just come to Japan, so I still can't read kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)へ来(き)てから、コンビニのごはん<b>ばかり</b>食(た)べています。
Since coming to Japan I've eaten nothing but convenience store food.
</pre>

The first is about time - the arrival was recent. The second is about proportion - 
the diet is monotonous. Learners produce
❌来(き)るばかりです for "I just arrived"; the pattern needs the plain past
来(き)た.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 水(みず)<b>を</b>ばかり飲(の)んでいます。 → ✅ 水(みず)<b>ばかり</b>飲(の)んでいます。
  `が` and `を` disappear in front of `ばかり`. If you want the object marked, it
  goes behind instead: 水(みず)ばかりを飲(の)む.

- ❌ 遊(あそ)ぶばかりいます。 → ✅ 遊(あそ)<b>んでばかり</b>います。
  With `いる`, the verb has to be in て-form. The plain form works only in the
  <code>V(plain) + ばかりだ</code> frame, which has no `いる`.

- ❌ きのうは一人(ひとり)<b>ばかり</b>来(き)ました。（meaning "only one person came"） → ✅ きのうは一人(ひとり)<b>しか</b>来(き)ませんでした。
  `ばかり` cannot mean "a mere N". With a number it either means "approximately"
  or nothing at all; the "disappointingly few" job belongs to
  <code>しか～ない</code>.

- ❌ 野菜(やさい)<b>ばかり</b>食(た)べて、健康(けんこう)にいいですね。 → ✅ 野菜(やさい)をたくさん食(た)べていて、健康(けんこう)にいいですね。
  `ばかり` on somebody's habits reads as a complaint, so pairing it with praise
  sounds sarcastic. Use it when you mean the criticism.

## Practice

**1.** 姉(あね)はスマホ____見(み)ていて、話(はなし)を聞(き)いてくれません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

姉(あね)はスマホ<b>ばかり</b>見(み)ていて、話(はなし)を聞(き)いてくれません。

The second half - she won't listen - tells you the speaker is annoyed, and that
annoyance is exactly what `ばかり` carries. `だけ` would report the same behaviour
with no complaint attached, which would clash with くれません.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
このクラスには留学生(りゅうがくせい)が二人(ふたり)（だけ・ばかり）います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このクラスには留学生(りゅうがくせい)が二人(ふたり)<b>だけ</b>います。

A precise count needs `だけ`. Putting `ばかり` after a number switches on its other
meaning - "about two" - and sounds like a formal announcement estimating a crowd.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He does nothing but grumble, and he never actually does anything."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は文句(もんく)を言(い)う<b>ばかり</b>で、何(なに)もしません。

The <code>V(plain) + ばかりで</code> frame links the excess to its consequence and
is very common in criticism. 文句(もんく)ばかり言(い)っていて、
何(なに)もしません says the same thing with the noun taking `ばかり` instead - 
both are natural.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 父(ちち)はお酒(さけ)をばかり飲(の)むばかりいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 父(ちち)はお酒(さけ)<b>ばかり</b>飲(の)んでいます。

Two errors. First, `を` cannot stand in front of `ばかり` - it is simply dropped.
Second, `ばかり` should appear once, not twice; and if you do use the
<code>～てばかりいる</code> frame, the verb must be in て-form:
お酒(さけ)を飲(の)んでばかりいます.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 休(やす)みの日(ひ)は家(いえ)にいる<b>だけ</b>です。
(b) 休(やす)みの日(ひ)は家(いえ)にいて<b>ばかり</b>います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states what your days off consist of and leaves the judgement to the listener.
It can even sound content - "I just stay in, that's all."

(b) hands down a verdict on yourself: you stay in far too much and you know it.
`ばかり` supplies the excess and the disapproval, which is why (b) fits naturally
before a phrase like <code>運動(うんどう)しないといけませんね</code> and (a) does
not.

</details>

# ～だけで

**Meaning** · just by doing ～; with only ～ - that small amount is all it takes

`だけ` draws the line; the `で` behind it says "and that line is where the cause
sits". So <code>～だけで</code> is not simply "only" - it links a minimal amount of
effort, time, money or evidence to a result that follows from it. Sometimes the
tone is admiring ("that's all it takes!") and sometimes dismissive ("that's all it
is").

## Formation

`だけ` attaches as it always does, and `で` follows it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | Vる + だけで | 見(み)る → 見(み)るだけで |
| Verb (past) | Vた + だけで | 聞(き)いた → 聞(き)いただけで |
| Noun | Noun + だけで | 水(みず) → 水(みず)だけで |
| Number / counter | Counter + だけで | 一人(ひとり)だけで |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけで | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いだけで |
| な-adjective | stem + な + だけで | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かなだけで |
| Common endings | ～だけで + いい／十分(じゅうぶん)／分(わ)かる | 見(み)るだけでいいです |
| Negative judgement | ～だけで**は** + negative | それだけでは足(た)りません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the sentence goes on to say what **results** from the small
amount, you want <code>だけで</code>. If it simply reports the amount and stops,
plain `だけ` is enough.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
顔(かお)を見(み)る<b>だけで</b>元気(げんき)になります。
Just seeing your face cheers me up.
</pre>

<pre>
ボタンを押(お)す<b>だけで</b>お湯(ゆ)が出(で)ます。
Hot water comes out just by pressing the button.
</pre>

<pre>
考(かんが)える<b>だけで</b>怖(こわ)くなります。
It frightens me just to think about it.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)<b>だけで</b>全部(ぜんぶ)運(はこ)ぶのは無理(むり)ですよ。
There's no way you can carry all of it with just one person.
</pre>

<pre>
スマホ<b>だけで</b>切符(きっぷ)が買(か)えます。
You can buy a ticket with nothing but your phone.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)を聞(き)いた<b>だけで</b>、何(なに)が起(お)きたかすぐに分(わ)かりました。
Just from hearing about it, I knew straight away what had happened.
</pre>

<pre>
この仕事(しごと)は給料(きゅうりょう)がいい<b>だけで</b>、やりがいがありません。
This job has nothing going for it but the pay - there's no satisfaction in it.
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)十分(じゅっぷん)歩(ある)く<b>だけで</b>、体(からだ)の調子(ちょうし)がずいぶんよくなりました。
Just walking for ten minutes every morning has made me feel a great deal better.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself; it takes its politeness from the ending. Two very
different tones live inside the same three kana, and which one you hear depends on
what the result is:

- **"That's all it takes."** The result is good and the effort is tiny.
  Instruction manuals, advertising and recipes are built out of this:
  混(ま)ぜるだけでできます. In speech it is warm and encouraging.
- **"That's all it is."** Here <code>だけで</code> follows an adjective and the
  second half of the sentence takes something away:
  新(あたら)しいだけで、使(つか)いにくい. This is faint praise turning into a
  complaint, and it is quite cutting.

The frame <code>～だけでいい</code> - "that's all you need to do" - is worth
memorising whole. It is how permission and reassurance are given in Japanese, and
it is much softer than an imperative: ここにサインするだけでいいです is far
friendlier than ここにサインしてください.

Adding `は` - <code>だけでは</code> - flips the sentence to "that alone is not
enough", and it almost always precedes a negative:
練習(れんしゅう)だけでは上手(じょうず)になりません. The `は` is the ordinary
contrastive `は`, and leaving it out sounds unfinished.

## Contrast

### ～だけで vs ～だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `だけ` reports an amount. `だけで` makes that amount the
**cause** of what comes next.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は水(みず)<b>だけ</b>飲(の)みました。
Yesterday I drank only water.
</pre>

<pre>
水(みず)<b>だけで</b>三日間(みっかかん)生(い)きられますか。
Can you survive for three days on water alone?
</pre>

In the first, water is what got drunk. In the second, water is the *means* by
which surviving happens. Slipping a `で` into the first sentence - 
❌水(みず)だけで飲(の)みました - would say you drank something using only
water, which is nonsense.

### ～だけで vs ～だけでは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけで` says the small amount **is** enough. `だけでは` says
it **is not**.

</div>

<pre>
この説明(せつめい)を読(よ)む<b>だけで</b>分(わ)かります。
You can understand it just by reading this explanation.
</pre>

<pre>
この説明(せつめい)を読(よ)む<b>だけでは</b>分(わ)かりません。
Just reading this explanation isn't enough to understand it.
</pre>

The `は` is contrastive: it sets "reading the explanation" against everything else
you would also have to do. Because of that, the predicate after `だけでは` is
nearly always negative - 足(た)りない, できない, いけない,
分(わ)からない.

### ～だけで vs ～ば・～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a conditional says *if* X, then Y. `だけで` says X **and
nothing more** is required for Y.

</div>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)め<b>ば</b>使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)かります。
If you read the manual you'll understand how to use it.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)む<b>だけで</b>使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)かります。
Just reading the manual is enough to understand how to use it.
</pre>

The conditional states a route to the result; <code>だけで</code> also insists the
route is short. That extra claim is what makes it the natural choice for anything
selling ease - and what makes it sound sarcastic if the task is in fact difficult.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 見(み)て<b>だけで</b>分(わ)かります。 → ✅ 見(み)る<b>だけで</b>分(わ)かります。
  `だけ` attaches to the plain form or the past form, never to the て-form. Both
  見(み)るだけで and 見(み)ただけで are fine; the past one presents the
  looking as already done.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>だ</b>だけで、不便(ふべん)です。 → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>だけで、不便(ふべん)です。
  な-adjectives take `な` before `だけ`, exactly as they do before a noun. `だ`
  only appears at the end of a sentence.

- ❌ これだけで足(た)りません。 → ✅ これだけ<b>では</b>足(た)りません。
  When the point is that the amount is **insufficient**, the contrastive `は` is
  required. Without it the sentence sounds like it was cut off halfway.

- ❌ サラダ<b>だけで</b>食(た)べました。（meaning "I only ate salad"） → ✅ サラダ<b>だけ</b>食(た)べました。
  The `で` adds "by means of", which the sentence does not want. Reserve
  <code>だけで</code> for when a result follows.

## Practice

**1.** このアプリは写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る____、花(はな)の名前(なまえ)を教(おし)えてくれます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このアプリは写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る<b>だけで</b>、花(はな)の名前(なまえ)を教(おし)えてくれます。

Taking a photo is the whole of the effort required, and the app naming the flower
is the result. That "minimum input, full result" shape is exactly what
<code>だけで</code> exists for, which is why product descriptions are full of it.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
辞書(じしょ)を買(か)う（だけで・だけでは）上手(じょうず)になりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

辞書(じしょ)を買(か)う<b>だけでは</b>上手(じょうず)になりません。

The predicate is negative - you will *not* improve - so the sentence is saying the
one action is insufficient, and that requires the contrastive `は`. With plain
`だけで` the sentence would be claiming the opposite and then contradicting itself.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You can register with just an email address."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

メールアドレス<b>だけで</b>登録(とうろく)できます。

A noun takes `だけ` directly, and the `で` then marks it as the means. Compare
メールアドレスだけを入力(にゅうりょく)してください, "enter only your email
address", where nothing follows as a result and plain `だけ` is right.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は親切(しんせつ)だだけで、仕事(しごと)はあまりできません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は親切(しんせつ)<b>な</b>だけで、仕事(しごと)はあまりできません。

親切(しんせつ) is a な-adjective, so it takes `な` in front of `だけ`. Notice
what the sentence does with the pattern: it grants one merit and then withdraws
everything else - the dismissive "that's all he is" flavour of
<code>だけで</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 話(はなし)を聞(き)く<b>だけ</b>でした。
(b) 話(はなし)を聞(き)く<b>だけで</b>涙(なみだ)が出(で)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a closed report: listening was the whole of what you did, and nothing is
said about any consequence. It often carries a hint of "I didn't get involved".

(b) makes the listening a trigger. The point is not the amount of listening but
how little of it was needed before you cried. The `で` is doing all of that work - 
it turns a boundary into a cause.

</details>

# ～しか～ない

**Meaning** · only ～; nothing but ～ - and it is less than you wanted

`しか` is a limiter that comes with a mood attached. It excludes everything outside
its noun, exactly as `だけ` does, but it also tells you the speaker finds the
remainder thin. The grammar is the strange part: the verb at the end **must** be
negative, even though the sentence is reporting something that does exist.
千円(せんえん)しかありません means "I have a thousand yen" - said by somebody
who wishes they had more.

## Formation

`しか` goes on the noun; the negative goes on the predicate. Anything at all can
sit between them.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + しか + negative | 水(みず)しかありません |
| Number / counter | Counter + しか + negative | 二(ふた)つしか残(のこ)っていない |
| After が／を／は | those particles are **dropped** | ❌お金(かね)をしか → ✅お金(かね)しか |
| After に／で／と／から | particle + しか | 駅(えき)でしか買(か)えない |
| Polite | ～しか + ません／ないです | 一人(ひとり)しか来(き)ませんでした |
| With material in between | ～しか … + negative | 少(すこ)ししか食(た)べませんでした |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しか` and the negative are one unit with a gap in the middle.
If you write `しか` you have committed yourself to a negative ending, however many
words later it arrives.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the sentence is negative in **form** only. To answer
「お金(かね)、ありますか」 with 「百円(ひゃくえん)しかありません」 is to say
that you *do* have a hundred yen. English speakers hear the negative and conclude
the opposite.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に卵(たまご)が一(ひと)つ<b>しか</b>残(のこ)ってい<b>ません</b>。
There's only one egg left in the fridge.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は水(みず)<b>しか</b>飲(の)んでい<b>ません</b>。
All I've had to drink today is water.
</pre>

<pre>
その話(はなし)は田中(たなか)さんに<b>しか</b>話(はな)してい<b>ません</b>。
Tanaka is the only person I've told about it.
</pre>

<pre>
この雑誌(ざっし)は大(おお)きい書店(しょてん)で<b>しか</b>売(う)ってい<b>ません</b>。
This magazine is only sold in the big bookshops.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)には十人(じゅうにん)<b>しか</b>来(き)<b>ませんでした</b>。
Only ten people turned up at the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
残(のこ)り時間(じかん)が五分(ごふん)<b>しか</b><b>ありません</b>。
There are only five minutes left.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)買(か)ったコートは高(たか)かったのに、まだ二回(にかい)<b>しか</b>着(き)てい<b>ません</b>。
The coat I bought last year was expensive, and I've still only worn it twice.
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)が終(お)わらなくて、昨日(きのう)は三時間(さんじかん)<b>しか</b>寝(ね)られ<b>ませんでした</b>。
I couldn't finish work, so I only got three hours' sleep last night.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral: 一人(ひとり)しかいない and 一人(ひとり)しかいません are the
same sentence at different politeness levels, and nothing about `しか` marks age or
gender. It is extremely common in ordinary speech.

The feeling it carries is **shortfall**. That can be regret
（三時間(さんじかん)しか寝(ね)ていない）, an apology
（少(すこ)ししかご用意(ようい)できませんでした, said while serving a
generous meal - a standard piece of Japanese modesty）, or a warning
（時間(じかん)が少(すこ)ししかありません）. What it cannot be is neutral. If
you have no feelings about the quantity, use `だけ`.

You will hear `だけしか` doubled up - これだけしかない. Strictly it says the same
thing twice, but it is standard colloquial Japanese and intensifies the
disappointment.

One more thing worth flagging early: <code>V(plain) + しかない</code>, as in
行(い)くしかない, is a **different pattern** meaning "there's no choice but to
go". It looks identical and it is not the same construction; the contrast section
separates them.

## Contrast

### ～しか～ない vs ～だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same fact, opposite mood. `だけ` states a limit flatly;
<code>しか～ない</code> states it with regret. And only `しか` forces the verb
negative.

</div>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)<b>だけ</b>営業(えいぎょう)しています。
We're open on Saturdays.
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)<b>しか</b>営業(えいぎょう)してい<b>ません</b>。
We're only open on Saturdays, I'm afraid.
</pre>

A shop advertising its hours writes the first. A shop apologising for them says
the second. Advertisers do sometimes use `しか` on purpose - 
当店(とうてん)でしか買(か)えません, "you can't get it anywhere else" - but
notice what they are doing: they are turning the shopper's inability to buy it
elsewhere into the shortfall, not the shop's own range.

The mechanical difference matters too. `だけ` leaves the verb alone and can take
`が`/`を` on either side; `しか` swallows `が`, `を` and `は` entirely and rewrites
the ending.

### ～しか～ない vs a plain negative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a plain negative denies. <code>しか～ない</code> denies
everything **except** the thing it marks - so it quietly asserts that thing.

</div>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)来(き)<b>ませんでした</b>。
One person didn't come.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)<b>しか</b>来(き)<b>ませんでした</b>。
Only one person came.
</pre>

The first sentence has one absentee and an unknown number of attendees. The second
has one attendee and everybody else absent. The single word `しか` reverses which
side of the count the number is on, and this is the mistake that costs learners
marks in reading comprehension.

### Noun + しかない vs verb + しかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a **noun**, `しかない` means "there is only ～". After a
**plain-form verb**, it means "there is nothing to do but ～".

</div>

<pre>
バスは一日(いちにち)に三本(さんぼん)<b>しかありません</b>。
There are only three buses a day.
</pre>

<pre>
バスがないので、歩(ある)く<b>しかありません</b>。
There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.
</pre>

The second is a decision made under pressure, not a count of anything, and it is
formally an N3 pattern. Keeping them apart is a matter of looking left: a noun or
a counter means quantity, a dictionary-form verb means resignation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 千円(せんえん)しか<b>あります</b>。 → ✅ 千円(せんえん)しか<b>ありません</b>。
  `しか` cannot live with a positive predicate. The negative ending is part of the
  pattern, not a separate decision.

- ❌ お金(かね)<b>を</b>しか持(も)っていません。 → ✅ お金(かね)<b>しか</b>持(も)っていません。
  `を` is dropped in front of `しか`, as are `が` and `は`. Only `に`, `で`, `と`
  and `から` survive, and they come first: 駅(えき)でしか.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>は</b>しか知(し)りません。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>しか</b>知(し)りません。
  Same rule, and this one is easy to miss because `は` feels like it belongs to the
  topic rather than to the noun. `しか` takes the slot.

- ❌ 二人(ふたり)しか来(こ)なくて、うれしかったです。 → ✅ 二人(ふたり)だけ来(き)て、うれしかったです。
  `しか` says the number disappointed you, so it fights with うれしい. When the
  small number is good news, `だけ` is the word.

## Practice

**1.** この町(まち)には病院(びょういん)が一(ひと)つ____あり____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この町(まち)には病院(びょういん)が一(ひと)つ<b>しか</b>あり<b>ません</b>。

`しか` needs the negative ending to complete it. Note that 一(ひと)つ, the
counter, is where `しか` attaches - the pattern is very often built on a number,
because that is where shortfall is easiest to feel.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
小(ちい)さいパーティーにしたかったので、友(とも)だちを五人(ごにん)（だけ・しか）呼(よ)び（ました・ませんでした）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

友(とも)だちを五人(ごにん)<b>だけ</b>呼(よ)びました。

The first half says the small guest list was deliberate and wanted, so there is no
shortfall to complain about. `しか` would say five friends was disappointingly few,
which contradicts the reason given - and it would also force the ending negative:
五人(ごにん)しか呼(よ)びませんでした.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've only been to Japan once."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

日本(にほん)へは一度(いちど)<b>しか</b>行(い)ったことが<b>ありません</b>。

`しか` sits on 一度(いちど) and the ending goes negative - ありません, not
あります. The English "only once" already contains the regret, which is why `しか`
fits better here than 一度(いちど)だけ行(い)ったことがあります, a flatter
statement of fact.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)をしか勉強(べんきょう)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>しか</b>勉強(べんきょう)し<b>ませんでした</b>。

Two fixes. The `を` in front of `しか` is deleted - `しか` occupies that slot
itself. And the ending has to go negative: `しか` is only half a pattern without
it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 土曜日(どようび)だけ休(やす)めます。
(b) 土曜日(どようび)しか休(やす)めません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both mean that Saturday is your one free day.

(a) reports it. It could be an answer to "which day are you free?", and it carries
no complaint - you might even be pleased to have the day.

(b) complains about it. The negative ending and `しか` together say one day is not
enough, and the sentence naturally continues with something like
疲(つか)れます. Choosing between them is choosing whether to sound content or
hard done by.

</details>

# あまり～ない

**Meaning** · not very; not much; not particularly

`あまり` sits in front of a negative and softens it. It converts a flat denial into
a measured one: not "I don't like it" but "I'm not all that keen". Because
Japanese conversation avoids blunt refusals, this is one of the most-used adverbs
in the language - and one learners under-use, because English rarely needs it.

## Formation

`あまり` is an adverb, but it comes with a condition attached, which is why it is
treated as a pattern: the predicate at the end of its clause **must be negative**.
The two halves belong together the way <code>しか～ない</code> does.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | あまり + ～くない | 高(たか)い → あまり高(たか)くない |
| な-adjective | あまり + ～じゃない | 静(しず)かだ → あまり静(しず)かじゃない |
| Noun predicate | あまり + ～じゃない | あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない |
| Verb | あまり + ない-form | 見(み)る → あまり見(み)ない |
| Polite | あまり + ～ません／～ないです | あまり飲(の)みません |
| Past | あまり + ～なかった／～ませんでした | あまり寒(さむ)くなかった |
| Casual | あんまり／あんま + negative | あんまりおいしくない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あまり` goes in front of whatever it is measuring, and the
negative comes at the end of that same clause. Anything may sit between them:
あまり日本(にほん)の映画(えいが)を見(み)ません.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `あまり` with a **positive** predicate is a different, more literary
construction meaning "excessively": あまりの寒(さむ)さに驚(おどろ)いた, "I
was startled by how bitterly cold it was". At N4 the safe rule is the one above - 
`あまり` takes a negative.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この料理(りょうり)は<b>あまり</b>辛(から)く<b>ありません</b>。
This dish isn't very spicy.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は<b>あまり</b>テレビを見(み)<b>ません</b>。
I don't watch much television.
</pre>

<pre>
きのうの夜(よる)は<b>あまり</b>眠(ねむ)れ<b>ませんでした</b>。
I didn't sleep very well last night.
</pre>

<pre>
その映画(えいが)は<b>あまり</b>おもしろく<b>なかった</b>ですね。
That film wasn't all that interesting, was it.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)は<b>あんまり</b>覚(おぼ)えられ<b>ない</b>んです。
I'm not very good at getting kanji to stick, to be honest.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)から遠(とお)いので、この店(みせ)は<b>あまり</b>込(こ)み<b>ません</b>。
It's a long way from the station, so this place doesn't get very crowded.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)は給料(きゅうりょう)はいいのですが、自分(じぶん)の時間(じかん)が<b>あまり</b>取(と)れ<b>ません</b>。
The new job pays well, but I don't get much time to myself.
</pre>

<pre>
甘(あま)いものは<b>あまり</b>好(す)きでは<b>ない</b>ので、飲(の)み物(もの)だけいただきます。
I'm not that fond of sweet things, so I'll just have a drink.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`あまり` itself is register-neutral. What varies is the contraction:

- **あまり** - the standard form. Safe everywhere, including writing.
- **あんまり** - very common in ordinary speech, slightly warmer and more
  emphatic. Fine in conversation, out of place in a report or an exam essay.
- **あんま** - clipped and quite casual. Recognise it; think twice before using it.

The reason to reach for `あまり` is almost always **politeness**. Japanese treats a
bare negative judgement as blunt, so the softened version is the default in
company:

- 嫌(きら)いです - "I dislike it." Strong, and can sound aggressive about
  someone's cooking.
- 好(す)きではありません - "I don't like it." Still flat.
- あまり好(す)きではありません - "I'm not particularly keen." This is the one
  people actually say.

The same softening lets `あまり` function as a **polite refusal**. Asked whether
you can come on Saturday, 土曜日(どようび)はあまり… - trailing off, with no
negative at all - is understood as no. The listener supplies the rest. Learning to
hear this saves a lot of confusion.

Do not confuse the degree with zero. あまり飲(の)みません means you drink a
little; 飲(の)みません means you do not drink; ぜんぜん飲(の)みません means
never, not a drop.

## Contrast

### あまり～ない vs ぜんぜん～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あまり` means a small amount. `ぜんぜん` means none at all.

</div>

<pre>
肉(にく)は<b>あまり</b>食(た)べ<b>ません</b>。
I don't eat much meat.
</pre>

<pre>
肉(にく)は<b>ぜんぜん</b>食(た)べ<b>ません</b>。
I don't eat meat at all.
</pre>

The first speaker will accept a slice of ham; the second will not. Because
`ぜんぜん` is absolute, it is also more forceful, and using it about someone's
work or cooking can sound harsh where `あまり` would be diplomatic. If you are
unsure how strong to be, `あまり` is the safer word.

### あまり～ない vs a plain negative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the plain negative states a fact. `あまり` states the same fact
politely, and leaves the door slightly open.

</div>

<pre>
この辺(へん)のことは知(し)り<b>ません</b>。
I don't know this area.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)のことは<b>あまり</b>知(し)り<b>ません</b>。
I don't know this area very well.
</pre>

The second is both more modest and more accurate - you know a little. In
conversation the softened version also invites the other person to keep talking,
where the flat one closes the topic. This is why Japanese speakers add `あまり` to
statements that are, strictly speaking, absolute.

### あまり～ない vs そんなに～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あまり` measures against a general expectation. `そんなに`
measures against something **the other person just said or did**.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は<b>あまり</b>高(たか)く<b>ない</b>ですよ。
This place isn't very expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は<b>そんなに</b>高(たか)く<b>ない</b>ですよ。
It isn't that expensive here - not as much as you're imagining.
</pre>

`そんなに` points backwards at a standard someone else raised: they winced at the
menu, or said they had heard the place was pricey. `あまり` needs no such
antecedent and works as an opening remark. In many contexts either is fine, and
`そんなに` simply sounds more conversational.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は<b>あまり</b>簡単(かんたん)です。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は<b>あまり</b>簡単(かんたん)じゃありません。
  `あまり` cannot sit with a positive predicate. Without the negative the sentence
  has no meaning to land on.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)はあまり暑(あつ)かったです。（meaning "it was very hot"） → ✅ 昨日(きのう)はとても暑(あつ)かったです。
  `あまり` is not a synonym for とても. It looks like an intensifier and behaves
  like the opposite of one.

- ❌ この報告書(ほうこくしょ)の数字(すうじ)は<b>あんまり</b>正(ただ)しくありません。 → ✅ ～は<b>あまり</b>正(ただ)しくありません。
  Grammatically fine, but `あんまり` is spoken register and clashes with a written
  document.

- ❌ 「納豆(なっとう)は好(す)きですか。」「ぜんぜん好(す)きじゃありません。」（meaning "not all that keen"） → ✅ 「あまり好(す)きじゃありません。」
  `ぜんぜん` is a total rejection and can sting. If you only mean "not
  especially", `あまり` is the word - and the polite one.

## Practice

**1.** 今朝(けさ)は忙(いそが)しくて、____食(た)べ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今朝(けさ)は忙(いそが)しくて、<b>あまり</b>食(た)べ<b>ませんでした</b>。

`あまり` and the negative ending are one unit: you cannot supply one without the
other. The meaning is that you ate a little, not that you skipped breakfast - for
that you would need ぜんぜん食(た)べませんでした.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
「新(あたら)しいカメラ、使(つか)ってみましたか。」「いいえ、まだ（あまり・とても）使(つか)っていません。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「まだ<b>あまり</b>使(つか)っていません。」

The reply ends in a negative, and only `あまり` can pair with one. `とても` needs a
positive predicate - とても使(つか)いやすいです - so it has nothing to attach to
here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "I'm not very good at singing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

歌(うた)は<b>あまり</b>上手(じょうず)じゃありません。

上手(じょうず) is a な-adjective, so its negative is じゃありません, and
`あまり` sits in front of the whole thing. This is the standard modest reply when
someone praises your singing; 下手(へた)です would be true but unnecessarily
harsh about yourself.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この町(まち)は静(しず)かあまりじゃないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この町(まち)は<b>あまり</b>静(しず)かじゃないです。

`あまり` is an adverb and has to come **before** the word it modifies, not after
it. Word order is what tells the listener how much of the sentence `あまり` is
measuring.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) その話(はなし)は知(し)りません。
(b) その話(はなし)は<b>あまり</b>知(し)りません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a clean denial: the topic is new to you, and the sentence closes the
subject.

(b) admits partial knowledge and hedges it. You have heard something, but not
enough to be useful - and by declining to sound certain, you invite the other
person to fill you in. In a workplace, (b) is also the safer answer, because it
does not commit you to knowing nothing.

</details>

# ぜんぜん

**Meaning** · (not) at all; not in the slightest

`ぜんぜん` is the zero end of the scale. Where `あまり` says "not much",
`ぜんぜん` says "none, nothing, no part of it". It normally pairs with a negative,
and in casual modern speech it has picked up a second, affirmative life that
textbooks tend not to mention and learners hear on their first day in Japan.

## Usage

`ぜんぜん` is an adverb. It sits in front of the predicate it is emptying out, and
anything may stand between it and the end of the sentence. In kanji it is
全然(ぜんぜん); both spellings are ordinary.

There are three patterns to recognise:

- **With a negative** - the core use.
  全然(ぜんぜん)分(わ)かりません, 全然(ぜんぜん)寒(さむ)くなかった,
  全然(ぜんぜん)好(す)きじゃない.
- **With a positive verb or adjective that already means "not"** - 
  全然(ぜんぜん)違(ちが)います ("completely wrong"),
  全然(ぜんぜん)だめです ("no good at all"). These are positive in form and
  negative in sense, and they are fully standard.
- **With an ordinary positive, in casual speech** - 全然(ぜんぜん)大丈夫(だいじょうぶ),
  全然(ぜんぜん)いいよ, meaning "totally fine, no problem at all". Extremely
  common in conversation; see the register note below before you write it down.

It also works as a one-word answer: 「疲(つか)れた?」「ぜんぜん。」 - "Tired?"
"Not a bit."

## Examples

<pre>
その話(はなし)は<b>ぜんぜん</b>知(し)りませんでした。
I had absolutely no idea about that.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)の意味(いみ)が<b>ぜんぜん</b>分(わ)かりません。
I don't understand what this question means at all.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の説明(せつめい)と<b>ぜんぜん</b>違(ちが)うじゃないですか。
That's completely different from what you told us yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
「待(ま)たせてごめん。」「ううん、<b>ぜんぜん</b>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だよ。」
"Sorry to keep you waiting." "No, no, it's totally fine."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

In its negative use `ぜんぜん` is register-neutral and works in speech and writing
alike. What it is not is **soft**. It is an absolute, and pointed at another
person's work it can be blunt: 全然(ぜんぜん)おもしろくなかったです about a
friend's film recommendation is a flat verdict. When you want to be diplomatic,
`あまり` is the word.

The affirmative use - 全然(ぜんぜん)平気(へいき), 全然(ぜんぜん)いける - 
needs a clear-eyed description, because you will meet it constantly and be told it
is wrong. It is casual spoken Japanese, especially common among younger speakers,
and in conversation nobody blinks. Style guides and many older speakers treat it as
incorrect, and it does not belong in an essay, a business email or a JLPT answer.
Interestingly the affirmative pattern is not new: it appears in Meiji-era
literature, and the "negative only" rule solidified later - so what is often called
a modern corruption is closer to a revival. Use it in speech if you like; keep it
off paper.

Two more points of feel:

- The intensity ladder runs 全然(ぜんぜん) → あまり → ちょっと. Only the first
  is a true zero.
- Because it is absolute, `ぜんぜん` cannot be qualified. There is no "almost not
  at all"; if the amount is small but real, you have moved to `あまり`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ その映画(えいが)はぜんぜん楽(たの)しかったです。（meaning "not fun at all"） → ✅ その映画(えいが)はぜんぜん楽(たの)しく<b>なかった</b>です。
  With an ordinary adjective the predicate has to go negative. Leaving it positive
  produces the casual "totally fun", which is the opposite of what you meant.

- ❌ ぜんぜん少(すこ)し分(わ)かります。 → ✅ 少(すこ)し分(わ)かります。／あまり分(わ)かりません。
  `ぜんぜん` is zero, so it cannot be attached to a small quantity. Choose one or
  the other.

- ❌ この作品(さくひん)は全然(ぜんぜん)すばらしいと思(おも)います。（in an essay） → ✅ この作品(さくひん)は非常(ひじょう)にすばらしいと思(おも)います。
  The affirmative `ぜんぜん` is spoken register. In writing it reads as an error
  even to readers who use it themselves every day.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が全然(ぜんぜん)ありましたから、ゆっくりできました。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がたくさんありましたから、ゆっくりできました。
  全然(ぜんぜん)ある is not the affirmative pattern; the casual use attaches to
  evaluations like 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ) and いい, not to plain statements of
  quantity.

## Practice

**1.** 三年(さんねん)習(なら)いましたが、まだ____話(はな)せ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三年(さんねん)習(なら)いましたが、まだ<b>ぜんぜん</b>話(はな)せ<b>ません</b>。

`ぜんぜん` demands the negative ending, and the contrast with
三年(さんねん)習(なら)いました is what makes it the right adverb here - 
three years of study and *nothing* to show for it. あまり話(はな)せません would
mean you can manage a little, which is a different complaint.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)のテストはぜんぜん難(むずか)しいでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)のテストはぜんぜん難(むずか)しく<b>ありませんでした</b>。

Two things. い-adjectives never take でした - the past goes on the adjective
itself. And `ぜんぜん` needs the negative, so the correct past negative is
難(むずか)しくありませんでした（or 難(むずか)しくなかったです）.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この料理(りょうり)は<b>あまり</b>辛(から)くないです。
(b) この料理(りょうり)は<b>ぜんぜん</b>辛(から)くないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says there is a little heat in it, but not much. Someone who cannot take spice
at all should still be careful.

(b) says there is no heat whatsoever - it is safe for anyone. `ぜんぜん` is an
absolute zero, `あまり` a small but real amount, and choosing the wrong one is a
genuinely useful thing to get right at a dinner table.

</details>

# そんなに

**Meaning** · that much; so much; to that extent

`そんなに` measures against something **the other person has just supplied** - 
their words, their reaction, the amount they poured, the price they winced at. It
is not a free-standing "very". Point it at a standard nobody has raised and it
sounds as though you are answering a question that was never asked.

## Usage

`そんなに` is an adverb built from `そんな` ("that kind of") plus `に`. It goes
directly in front of the adjective, adverb or verb whose degree it is measuring.

Most of the time it appears **with a negative**, playing down an amount the other
person has overestimated:

- そんなに高(たか)くないですよ - "it isn't that expensive."
- そんなに遠(とお)くありません - "it isn't that far."
- そんなに時間(じかん)はかかりません - "it won't take that long."

With a **positive** predicate it usually carries surprise or a mild protest:
そんなに食(た)べたの? ("you ate that much?"),
そんなに急(いそ)がないで ("don't rush like that").

It belongs to a set of four. `こんなに` measures against something in the
speaker's own hands, `そんなに` against the listener's, `あんなに` against
something remembered or far from both, and `どんなに` asks "how much?".

The crucial spelling point: **そんな** goes in front of a noun
（そんな人(ひと), そんなこと）and **そんなに** in front of an adjective or verb
（そんなに大(おお)きい, そんなに笑(わら)う）.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>そんなに</b>急(いそ)がなくてもいいですよ。
There's no need to rush like that.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は<b>そんなに</b>重(おも)くありません。
This dictionary isn't as heavy as all that.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>そんなに</b>大(おお)きい声(こえ)を出(だ)さないでください。赤(あか)ちゃんが起(お)きてしまいます。
Please don't talk that loudly - you'll wake the baby.
</pre>

<pre>
「三時間(さんじかん)も並(なら)んだんだ。」「えっ、<b>そんなに</b>待(ま)ったの?」
"I queued for three whole hours." "What - you waited that long?"
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very frequent in conversation. In formal writing the same job
is done by `それほど`, which is the stiffer equivalent:
それほど難(むずか)しくはありません in a manual,
そんなに難(むずか)しくないよ to a friend.

The reason it turns up so often is that it is a **polite way to disagree**. Rather
than contradicting someone flatly, you scale their estimate down:
「大変(たいへん)でしょう」「いえ、そんなに大変(たいへん)じゃありません」. The
trailing 「そんなに…」 on its own - with the rest left unsaid - is a very soft way
of saying "not really".

Two feelings to keep apart. With a negative, `そんなに` is reassuring: it tells the
listener to relax. With a positive it is often the opposite - surprise
（そんなにかかったの?）or restraint
（そんなに怒(おこ)らないで, "don't be so angry"）, and in that second use it can
sound like a mild telling-off.

Compared with `あまり`, `そんなに` is more conversational and more tied to context.
`あまり` measures against the world in general and can open a conversation;
`そんなに` needs something to point back at.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そんなに人(ひと)は信用(しんよう)できません。 → ✅ <b>そんな</b>人(ひと)は信用(しんよう)できません。
  Before a **noun** the form is `そんな`. `そんなに` is the adverb and needs an
  adjective or a verb to modify.

- ❌ そんな高(たか)くないですよ。 → ✅ <b>そんなに</b>高(たか)くないですよ。
  The mirror image of the mistake above. You will hear the reduced そんな高(たか)い
  in fast casual speech, but `そんなに` is the standard form and the only safe one
  in writing or on a test.

- ❌ （opening a conversation with no context）そんなに忙(いそが)しくないです。 → ✅ あまり忙(いそが)しくないです。
  `そんなに` answers an estimate someone else has made. With nothing to point back
  at, it leaves the listener hunting for what "that much" refers to.

- ❌ （holding up your own enormous suitcase）そんなに重(おも)いんです。 → ✅ <b>こんなに</b>重(おも)いんです。
  The thing is in your hands, so it belongs to the speaker's territory: `こんなに`.
  `そんなに` would put it on the listener's side of the conversation.

## Practice

**1.** 「駅(えき)まで歩(ある)くと一時間(いちじかん)ぐらいかかりますか。」「いいえ、____かかりません。十五分(じゅうごふん)ぐらいです。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「いいえ、<b>そんなに</b>かかりません。十五分(じゅうごふん)ぐらいです。」

The other person has just put an hour on the table, and `そんなに` scales that
specific estimate down without calling them wrong. This is the pattern's home
ground: a polite correction that points back at what was just said.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: そんなに話(はなし)は信(しん)じられません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ <b>そんな</b>話(はなし)は信(しん)じられません。
"I can't believe a story like that."

話(はなし) is a noun, so it takes `そんな`. If you wanted the adverb you would
need something for it to modify: そんなに簡単(かんたん)には信(しん)じられません,
"I can't believe it that easily."

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) <b>こんなに</b>降(ふ)るとは思(おも)いませんでした。
(b) <b>あんなに</b>降(ふ)るとは思(おも)いませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is said while the rain is coming down in front of you - the amount is right
here, in the speaker's own space, so `こんなに` is the form.

(b) is said afterwards, about a downpour that is over or elsewhere. `あんなに`
points at something distant from both speakers, in space or in memory, which is
why it so often shows up in stories about the past.

</details>
