# N4 - Can do, made to do, told to do

# ～ことができる

**Meaning** · can; be able to - the long, formal way of saying that something is possible

`～ことができる` turns a verb into a noun-like chunk with `こと` and then says that
that chunk "exists as a possibility". It means exactly what the short potential
form means, but it is longer, flatter and more official - the shape you meet on
signs, in manuals, in contracts and in written explanations.

## Formation

Attach `ことができる` to the **dictionary form**. Nothing about the verb changes,
which is what makes this pattern the safe fallback while the potential form is
still settling in.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + ことができる | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)ぐことができる |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + ことができる | 調(しら)べる → 調(しら)べることができる |
| Verb (する) | する + ことができる | 説明(せつめい)する → 説明(せつめい)することができる |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(く)る + ことができる | 来(く)る → 来(く)ることができる |
| Noun (an activity) | Noun + ができる - no こと | テニス → テニスができる |
| Negative | ことができない | 泳(およ)ぐことができない |
| Past | ことができた | 泳(およ)ぐことができた |
| Polite | ことができます／ことができません／ことができました | 泳(およ)ぐことができます |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `できる` is an ordinary る-verb, so everything after `こと` conjugates
normally: ことができて, ことができれば, ことができるように. The `こと` itself never
changes and is never replaced by `の`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)はピアノを<b>弾(ひ)くことができます</b>。
I can play the piano.
</pre>

<pre>
ここで写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)ることができません</b>。
You can't take photographs here.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは中国語(ちゅうごくご)を<b>話(はな)すことができる</b>。
Tanaka can speak Chinese.
</pre>

<pre>
このカードで電車(でんしゃ)にも<b>乗(の)ることができます</b>。
You can use this card on the trains as well.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころは自転車(じてんしゃ)に<b>乗(の)ることができなかった</b>。
As a child I couldn't ride a bicycle.
</pre>

<pre>
予約(よやく)は三日(みっか)前(まえ)まで<b>変更(へんこう)することができます</b>。
Reservations may be changed up to three days in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
インターネットさえあれば、家(いえ)からでも会議(かいぎ)に<b>参加(さんか)することができます</b>。
As long as you have internet, you can join the meeting from home too.
</pre>

<pre>
風(かぜ)が強(つよ)い日(ひ)は屋上(おくじょう)に<b>上(あ)がることができません</b>のでご注意(ちゅうい)ください。
On windy days the roof terrace cannot be accessed, so please be aware.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～ことができる` is **not** casual. It is grammatically fine at any politeness
level, but its flavour is written and institutional. A shop puts
<code>ご利用(りよう)いただくことができます</code> on a sign; a friend asking whether
you can come tonight says <code>来(こ)られる?</code>, never
❌<code>来(く)ることができる?</code>. Using it in chatty speech is the single
clearest way to sound like a textbook.

Three places where it is genuinely the better choice:

- **Rules and notices.** 館内(かんない)では飲食(いんしょく)することができません.
  The length reads as officialdom, which is the point.
- **When the potential form would be ambiguous.** For る-verbs,
  <code>食(た)べられる</code> could be "can eat" or "gets eaten"; 
  <code>食(た)べることができる</code> can only be "can eat".
- **In careful writing**, where the ら抜(ぬ)き potential
  (<code>食(た)べれる</code>) is unacceptable and the full
  <code>食(た)べられる</code> feels heavy.

With する-verbs the pattern is often padded out for no reason.
<code>運転(うんてん)することができます</code> and
<code>運転(うんてん)できます</code> mean the same thing, and the short one is
what people say. Reserve the long version for the register that wants it.

Finally, note that `できる` here is a stative verb, so it does not take `～ている`
for a present ability: ❌<code>泳(およ)ぐことができています</code>. Plain
<code>泳(およ)ぐことができます</code> already covers the present.

## Contrast

### ～ことができる vs the potential form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. The potential form is what
you **say**; `～ことができる` is what the notice on the wall **prints**.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)は漢字(かんじ)を書(か)くことが<b>できます</b>。
I can write kanji. (measured, explanatory)
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は漢字(かんじ)が<b>書(か)けます</b>。
I can write kanji. (ordinary spoken Japanese)
</pre>

Watch the particle. `～ことができる` keeps the original `を`, because the object
still belongs to the inner verb. The potential form pulls the object into `が`.
Both <code>漢字(かんじ)を書(か)ける</code> and
<code>漢字(かんじ)が書(か)ける</code> exist, but ❌<code>漢字(かんじ)が書(か)くことができる</code>
does not - the `が` has nothing to attach to.

### ～ことができる vs Noun + ができる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a **verb** needs `こと` in front of `が`. A **noun** takes `が`
directly and must not have `こと`.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はスキー<b>ができます</b>。
My little brother can ski.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はスキーを<b>することができます</b>。
My little brother is able to ski. (stiffer, but grammatical)
</pre>

Nouns of activity - スキー, テニス, 料理(りょうり), 運転(うんてん),
通訳(つうやく) - go straight into `が` + `できる`. Adding `こと` to a noun
(❌スキーのことができる) is meaningless.

### ～ることができる vs ～たことがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the tense before `こと` decides everything. **Dictionary form**
+ ことができる = *can*. <b>た-form</b> + ことがある = *have ever*.

</div>

These two look almost identical on the page and mean completely different things.

<pre>
富士山(ふじさん)に<b>登(のぼ)ることができます</b>。
You can climb Mt Fuji. (it is possible)
</pre>

<pre>
富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)った<b>ことがあります</b>。
I have climbed Mt Fuji. (experience)
</pre>

The verb at the end differs too - `できる` for ability, `ある` for experience.
Mixing them (❌登(のぼ)ったことができます) produces a sentence that means nothing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 泳(およ)ぐのができます。 → ✅ 泳(およ)ぐ<b>ことができます</b>。
  `の` nominalises fine before <code>好(す)き</code> and
  <code>上手(じょうず)</code>, but this pattern is fixed with `こと`.

- ❌ 食(た)べられることができる → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>ることができる</b>／食(た)べ<b>られる</b>
  Pick one. Stacking the potential form inside `ことができる` says "can" twice.

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)をことができます。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)<b>ができます</b>。
  A bare noun takes `が` + `できる` with no `こと` and no `を`.

- ❌ 来(く)ることができないでした。 → ✅ 来(く)ること<b>ができませんでした</b>。
  `できる` is a verb, not an adjective; the past negative is `できませんでした`.

- ❌ 漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)むことができます。 → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)<b>を</b>読(よ)むことができます。
  Inside the `こと` chunk the verb is still ordinary and transitive, so its object
  keeps `を`.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、ここでタバコを吸(す)う____ができますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

タバコを吸(す)う<b>こと</b>ができますか。

A verb has to be nominalised by `こと` before `が できる` can take it. The
dictionary form <code>吸(す)う</code> is what `こと` attaches to, and the object
keeps its `を`.

</details>

**2.** 姉(あね)はフランス語(ご)（ができます・のことができます）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

姉(あね)はフランス語(ご)<b>ができます</b>。

<code>フランス語(ご)</code> is a noun, so it goes straight into `が` + `できる`.
`こと` is only needed to turn a *verb* into something `できる` can accept.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a museum notice: "Photography is
not permitted inside the hall."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

館内(かんない)では写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る<b>ことができません</b>。

This is exactly the register `～ことができません` was made for - impersonal,
written, institutional. The spoken equivalent
<code>写真(しゃしん)、撮(と)れないよ</code> would be badly out of place on a sign.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 私(わたし)は一度(いちど)北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)ったことができます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 私(わたし)は一度(いちど)北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)った<b>ことがあります</b>。

The た-form before `こと` signals *experience*, and experience pairs with `ある`,
not `できる`. If the intended meaning were "I can go to Hokkaido", it would be
<code>行(い)くことができます</code> - dictionary form, and `できる`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この部屋(へや)を使(つか)うことができます。
(b) この部屋(へや)が使(つか)えます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say the room is available. (a) is the announcement version - a receptionist
explaining the rules, a sign on the door, a written policy. Its length and the
retained `を` make it sound like an official statement of what is permitted.

(b) is what a colleague says to you in the corridor. The potential form
<code>使(つか)える</code> with `が` is compact and unremarkable, and in ordinary
conversation (a) would sound oddly ceremonious for the same piece of information.

</details>

# ～られる - potential, "can do"

**Meaning** · be able to do it - the ability, or the circumstances, are there

The potential form says that an action is available to the subject. It covers both
halves of English *can*: the skill you have (<code>泳(およ)げる</code>, "I can
swim") and the opportunity the world gives you
(<code>今日(きょう)は休(やす)める</code>, "I can take today off"). Japanese does
not separate the two.

## Formation

Only る-verbs and 来(く)る actually use the shape `られる`. う-verbs use a
shorter ending, and する is replaced outright.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → え-row kana, then + る | 書(か)く → 書(か)ける |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + られる | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べられる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by できる - no られる at all | 運転(うんてん)する → 運転(うんてん)できる |
| Verb (来(く)る) | stem こ, + られる | 来(く)る → 来(こ)られる |

Every う-verb ending, spelled out. The last row is the one people get wrong:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → え + る | 買(か)う → 買(か)える |
| ～く | く → け + る | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)ける |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → げ + る | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)げる |
| ～す | す → せ + る | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)せる |
| ～つ | つ → て + る | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)てる |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → ね + る | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)ねる |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → べ + る | 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)べる |
| ～む | む → め + る | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)める |
| ～る | る → れ + る | 帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)れる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a potential verb is always a <b>る-verb</b>, whatever it started
as. So 書(か)ける → 書(か)けない, 書(か)けます, 書(か)けて,
書(か)けた; and 食(た)べられる → 食(た)べられない, 食(た)べられます.

</div>

| Further forms | Example |
|---|---|
| Negative | 書(か)けない／食(た)べられない／できない／来(こ)られない |
| Polite | 書(か)けます／食(た)べられます／できます／来(こ)られます |
| Past | 書(か)けた／食(た)べられた／できた／来(こ)られた |
| ら抜(ぬ)き (spoken, nonstandard) | - ／食(た)べれる／ - ／来(こ)れる |

The object of a potential verb normally shifts from `を` to `が`:
漢字(かんじ)<b>を</b>書(か)く → 漢字(かんじ)<b>が</b>書(か)ける.

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)は泳(およ)<b>げません</b>。
I can't swim.
</pre>

<pre>
辛(から)いものが<b>食(た)べられます</b>か。
Can you eat spicy food?
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)が<b>読(よ)めない</b>。
I can't read this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
予約(よやく)なしでも<b>入(はい)れます</b>か。
Can we get in without a reservation?
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるので、今日(きょう)は会社(かいしゃ)に<b>来(こ)られません</b>。
I have a fever, so I can't come in to work today.
</pre>

<pre>
チケットが取(と)れたから、来月(らいげつ)のコンサートに<b>行(い)ける</b>よ。
I got tickets, so I can go to the concert next month.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は現金(げんきん)でしか<b>払(はら)えない</b>ので、カードは使(つか)えません。
This place only takes cash, so you can't use a card.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころは牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)が<b>飲(の)めなかった</b>が、今(いま)は平気(へいき)になった。
I couldn't drink milk as a child, but I'm fine with it now.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The potential form is register-neutral and is the default way to say *can* in
speech. It has no built-in politeness; `～ます` on the end handles that.

**The particle.** `が` is the textbook answer and the safest one:
日本語(にほんご)が話(はな)せます. `を` is also alive and common, and it is
preferred when the object is long, when the clause already contains a `が`, or when
the `を` was never a direct object to begin with - 
道(みち)<b>を</b>歩(ある)ける, 家(いえ)<b>を</b>出(で)られる.

<b>ら抜(ぬ)き言葉(ことば).</b> In everyday speech many speakers drop the `ら` from the る-verb and
来(く)る potentials: 食(た)べれる, 見(み)れる, 起(お)きれる,
来(こ)れる. This is extremely widespread - in some regions it is simply the
normal form - and it has a real advantage: it separates potential from passive,
which the full form cannot do. It is nevertheless still classed as nonstandard.
Do not write it in an exam, an essay or a business email, and note that it never
touches う-verbs: 書(か)ける is not ら抜(ぬ)き, it is just the potential.

<b>れ足(た)す言葉(ことば)</b> is the opposite error and is unambiguously wrong: ❌行(い)けれる,
❌読(よ)めれる. An extra `れ` is inserted where nothing belongs.

**Verbs with no potential form.** Some verbs already contain the idea of ability
or spontaneous perception and refuse the potential: `わかる`, `できる`,
<code>見(み)える</code>, <code>聞(き)こえる</code>. `ある` has none either. And
verbs describing something involuntary - <code>疲(つか)れる</code>,
<code>降(ふ)る</code> - take one only in odd, marked contexts.

**Politeness by potential.** Turning a request into a potential question softens it
a great deal: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けてもらえますか is gentler than
開(あ)けてください, and <code>開(あ)けていただけませんか</code> gentler still.
This is one of the main jobs the form does in adult conversation.

## Contrast

### ～られる - potential vs passive

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for <b>う-verbs</b> the two forms are different words
(読(よ)める vs 読(よ)まれる). For <b>る-verbs and 来(く)る</b> they are
identical, and only the particles and the context tell you which one you are
looking at.

</div>

<code>食(た)べられる</code> is genuinely two words wearing one coat:

<pre>
私(わたし)は納豆(なっとう)が<b>食(た)べられる</b>。
I can eat nattō. (potential)
</pre>

<pre>
ケーキを弟(おとうと)に<b>食(た)べられた</b>。
My little brother ate my cake (on me). (passive)
</pre>

Three signals separate them. The potential marks its object with `が` and has no
agent; the passive marks its agent with `に`. The potential is about the subject's
own capacity; the passive is about something done to the subject. And the passive
very often turns up in the past, reporting a nuisance.

When the sentence would be genuinely ambiguous, Japanese avoids the form rather
than tolerating it: speakers reach for <code>食(た)べれる</code> in casual speech
or <code>食(た)べることができる</code> in writing, both of which can only mean
"can eat".

### the potential form vs ～ことができる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning; the potential form is spoken Japanese,
`～ことができる` is written and institutional Japanese.

</div>

<pre>
ここから富士山(ふじさん)が<b>見(み)られます</b>。
You can see Mt Fuji from here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここから富士山(ふじさん)を<b>見(み)ることができます</b>。
Mt Fuji can be viewed from here. (a sign, a guidebook)
</pre>

Note the particle again: the potential form pulls the object to `が`, while
`～ことができる` leaves the inner verb intact with its `を`.

### 見(み)られる vs 見(み)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>見(み)られる</code> is *able to see* - you have the
chance, the permission, the ticket. <code>見(み)える</code> is *is visible* - 
the image simply arrives at your eyes.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)から海(うみ)が<b>見(み)える</b>。
You can see the sea from the window. (it is simply there)
</pre>

<pre>
チケットがあれば、リハーサルも<b>見(み)られる</b>。
If you have a ticket, you can watch the rehearsal too. (access)
</pre>

The same split runs through <code>聞(き)こえる</code> (a sound reaches you) and
<code>聞(き)ける</code> (you get the opportunity to listen). Because
<code>見(み)える</code> and <code>聞(き)こえる</code> already carry the
meaning, they have no potential form of their own:
❌<code>見(み)えられる</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 話(はな)せられる → ✅ 話(はな)<b>せる</b>
  `られる` is only for る-verbs and 来(く)る. An う-verb changes its own ending
  and stops there.

- ❌ 行(い)けれる／読(よ)めれる → ✅ 行(い)<b>ける</b>／読(よ)<b>める</b>
  This is れ足(た)す言葉(ことば) - an inserted `れ`. Unlike ら抜(ぬ)き it is not accepted in any
  register.

- ❌ 明日(あした)来(き)られますか。 → ✅ 明日(あした)<b>来(こ)られますか</b>。
  The potential stem of 来(く)る is こ. `き` is the ます-stem
  (来(き)ます), and it does not belong here.

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)がわかれます。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)が<b>わかります</b>。
  `わかる` already means "is understandable to me". It has no potential form.

- ❌ レポートを食(た)べれます（in an essay） → ✅ …食(た)<b>べられます</b>
  ら抜(ぬ)き is fine among friends and wrong in writing. In anything marked or
  submitted, keep the `ら`.

## Practice

**1.** 田中(たなか)さんはギターが弾(ひ)____ますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ギターが弾(ひ)<b>け</b>ますか。

<code>弾(ひ)く</code> is an う-verb, so the final `く` becomes `け` and `る` is
added: 弾(ひ)ける → 弾(ひ)けます. The object takes `が` because the verb is
now potential.

</details>

**2.** 朝(あさ)早(はや)く（起(お)きられます・起(お)きることができます）か、と友(とも)だちに聞(き)かれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

朝(あさ)早(はや)く<b>起(お)きられます</b>か。

A friend asking a casual question uses the potential form. The
`～ことができる` version is not wrong, but its written, official flavour would sit
strangely in ordinary conversation.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I couldn't sleep at all last night."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨夜(ゆうべ)はぜんぜん<b>寝(ね)られなかった</b>。

<code>寝(ね)る</code> is a る-verb, so the potential is
<code>寝(ね)られる</code>, and the plain past negative is
<code>寝(ね)られなかった</code>. In speech you will also hear
<code>寝(ね)れなかった</code> - that is the ら抜(ぬ)き form, fine to say and not to
write.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: バスが来(こ)なくて、会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)わせられませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)に<b>間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした</b>／<b>間(ま)に合(あ)えませんでした</b>

<code>間(ま)に合(あ)う</code> is an う-verb, so its potential is
<code>間(ま)に合(あ)える</code> - the `せ` came from applying the causative
pattern by mistake. In practice speakers usually just say
<code>間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした</code>, since arriving on time is an event
rather than a skill.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この部屋(へや)からは花火(はなび)が見(み)えます。
(b) この部屋(へや)からは花火(はなび)が見(み)られます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a plain physical fact: point your eyes out of that window and the
fireworks are in your field of view. <code>見(み)える</code> is about the image
arriving, with no effort or permission involved.

(b) frames it as an opportunity - this room is one from which you *get to* watch
the fireworks. It fits a hotel describing what its guests are entitled to, and it
implies that not everyone has that access.

</details>

# ～られる - passive, "it was done to me"

**Meaning** · something is done to the subject - often, in Japanese, something the
subject would rather have avoided

The passive rebuilds a sentence around the person or thing **on the receiving
end**. English does this to move an object into the subject slot. Japanese does
that too, but it also does something English cannot: it can build a passive on an
*intransitive* verb, to say that an event happened and you were stuck with the
consequences.

## Formation

The passive is built on the あ-stem - the same stem the ない-form uses - plus
`れる`. る-verbs and 来(く)る instead take `られる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → あ-row kana, + れる | 書(か)く → 書(か)かれる |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + られる | 褒(ほ)める → 褒(ほ)められる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by される | 注意(ちゅうい)する → 注意(ちゅうい)される |
| Verb (来(く)る) | stem こ, + られる | 来(く)る → 来(こ)られる |

Every う-verb ending. The `～う` row is the trap - it goes to `わ`, not `あ`:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → わ + れる | 言(い)う → 言(い)われる |
| ～く | く → か + れる | 叩(たた)く → 叩(たた)かれる |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → が + れる | 騒(さわ)ぐ → 騒(さわ)がれる |
| ～す | す → さ + れる | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)される |
| ～つ | つ → た + れる | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)たれる |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → な + れる | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)なれる |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → ば + れる | 呼(よ)ぶ → 呼(よ)ばれる |
| ～む | む → ま + れる | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)まれる |
| ～る | る → ら + れる | 取(と)る → 取(と)られる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a passive verb is always a <b>る-verb</b>:
言(い)われる → 言(い)われない, 言(い)われます, 言(い)われて,
言(い)われた.

</div>

Three sentence shapes use it:

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plain passive | A は B に + passive | 私(わたし)は先生(せんせい)に褒(ほ)められた |
| Possession passive | A は B に + Noun を + passive | 私(わたし)は財布(さいふ)を盗(ぬす)まれた |
| Suffering passive | A は B に + passive (verb may be intransitive) | 私(わたし)は雨(あめ)に降(ふ)られた |

## Examples

<pre>
母(はは)に<b>叱(しか)られた</b>。
I got told off by my mother.
</pre>

<pre>
犬(いぬ)に手(て)を<b>噛(か)まれました</b>。
I was bitten on the hand by a dog.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は来週(らいしゅう)の月曜日(げつようび)に<b>行(おこな)われます</b>。
The meeting will be held next Monday.
</pre>

<pre>
満員(まんいん)電車(でんしゃ)の中(なか)で足(あし)を<b>踏(ふ)まれた</b>。
Someone stepped on my foot in the packed train.
</pre>

<pre>
帰(かえ)る途中(とちゅう)で雨(あめ)に<b>降(ふ)られて</b>、服(ふく)がびしょびしょになった。
I got caught in the rain on the way home and my clothes were soaked.
</pre>

<pre>
この寺(てら)は四百(よんひゃく)年(ねん)前(まえ)に<b>建(た)てられた</b>そうだ。
Apparently this temple was built four hundred years ago.
</pre>

<pre>
夜中(よなか)に赤(あか)ちゃんに<b>泣(な)かれて</b>、ほとんど眠(ねむ)れなかった。
The baby cried all night on me and I barely slept.
</pre>

<pre>
発表(はっぴょう)のあと学生(がくせい)たちにいろいろ<b>質問(しつもん)されて</b>、答(こた)えるのに困(こま)った。
After the talk the students fired all sorts of questions at me and I struggled to answer.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The passive itself is register-neutral; politeness comes from `～ます` as usual.
What is *not* neutral is how often you reach for it. English uses the passive
freely to avoid naming an agent; Japanese usually solves that problem by simply
leaving the subject out of an active sentence. Translating every English passive
into a Japanese one produces stiff, faintly self-pitying prose.

**Marking the agent.** `に` is the default. Two alternatives matter:

- <b>によって</b> for creation, discovery, invention and other one-off acts, especially
  with an inanimate subject: この絵(え)はピカソによって描(か)かれた.
  Saying ピカソに描(か)かれた makes the painting sound put-upon.
- <b>から</b> with verbs of giving, telling and teaching, where the thing flows out of
  the agent: 先生(せんせい)から注意(ちゅうい)された.

**The affected subject.** A Japanese passive normally wants a *person* as its
subject, because the form is fundamentally about who is affected. Inanimate
subjects are fine when the sentence is a neutral report - 
会議(かいぎ)が行(おこな)われる, 新(あたら)しい店(みせ)が開(ひら)かれる,
この本(ほん)はよく読(よ)まれている - and that use is at home in newspapers
and formal writing.

<b>られる as honorific.</b> The same form doubles as a light honorific:
社長(しゃちょう)はもう帰(かえ)られました means "the president has already gone
home", not that anyone went home on him. It is weaker than
<code>お帰(かえ)りになる</code> and very common in offices precisely because it
is easy. Context disambiguates: an honorific `られる` has no `に`-marked agent and
its subject is someone you are looking up to.

## Contrast

### ～られる - passive vs potential

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - う-verbs keep them apart (読(よ)まれる vs 読(よ)める).
る-verbs and 来(く)る do not - 食(た)べられる, 見(み)られる and
来(こ)られる are one shape doing two jobs.

</div>

<pre>
夜中(よなか)に友(とも)だちに<b>来(こ)られて</b>、寝(ね)られなかった。
A friend turned up in the middle of the night and I couldn't sleep.
</pre>

That sentence contains both: <code>来(こ)られて</code> is passive (a friend did
it *to* me, and it was a nuisance), <code>寝(ね)られなかった</code> is potential
(I lacked the ability). Nothing in the endings tells you apart - the particles do.
A passive has an agent in `に`; a potential has an object in `が` and no agent.

Because the collision is real, speakers avoid it. In casual speech the ら抜(ぬ)き
potential (<code>食(た)べれる</code>, <code>見(み)れる</code>,
<code>来(こ)れる</code>) is unambiguous, which is one honest reason it keeps
spreading despite being nonstandard. In writing, <code>～ことができる</code> does
the same job.

### the plain passive vs the suffering passive

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the active sentence had an object, the passive is the
ordinary kind. If it had **no object at all**, you are looking at the suffering
passive, and its whole job is to add a victim who was not in the original sentence.

</div>

Start from an intransitive verb with nobody affected:

<pre>
雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)った</b>。
It rained.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)に<b>降(ふ)られた</b>。
I got rained on. (and it ruined things for me)
</pre>

The second sentence has a silent 私(わたし)は in front. Nothing was done *to* the
rain; the passive exists purely to say that the rain's falling landed on me. The
same machinery gives 子(こ)どもに泣(な)かれる,
前(まえ)の人(ひと)に立(た)たれる, 客(きゃく)に来(こ)られる, and - for
one of the heaviest sentences in the language - 父(ちち)に死(し)なれる,
"my father died on me".

A closely related form takes a transitive verb but puts the *owner*, not the
object, in the subject slot:

<pre>
誰(だれ)かに自転車(じてんしゃ)を<b>盗(ぬす)まれた</b>。
Someone stole my bicycle.
</pre>

Note that `を` survives: 自転車(じてんしゃ) is still the object of the stealing.
The subject is me, because I am the one out a bicycle. Saying
自転車(じてんしゃ)が盗(ぬす)まれた is grammatical but sounds like a police
report about a bicycle, not a complaint about my morning.

### ～られる vs ～てもらう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same event, opposite feelings. `～てもらう` says you are
grateful; the passive typically says you are not.

</div>

<pre>
友(とも)だちに宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>手伝(てつだ)ってもらった</b>。
A friend helped me with my homework. (thank you)
</pre>

<pre>
友(とも)だちに日記(にっき)を<b>読(よ)まれた</b>。
A friend read my diary. (thanks for nothing)
</pre>

Both take the other person in `に` and both make me the subject, so the only thing
carrying the attitude is the choice of ending. Using the passive for something
kind - ❌先生(せんせい)に教(おし)えられた for "my teacher taught me" - reads
as a complaint; <code>教(おし)えていただきました</code> is what you want.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 買(か)あれる → ✅ 買(か)<b>われる</b>
  Verbs ending in `～う` go to `わ`, never `あ`. Same rule as the ない-form:
  買(か)わない.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)られた。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>に</b>降(ふ)られた。
  In a suffering passive the cause is the agent, and agents take `に`. The `が`
  slot belongs to the person who suffered.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は先生(せんせい)を褒(ほ)められた。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は先生(せんせい)<b>に</b>褒(ほ)められた。
  The praiser is the agent, marked `に`. `を` here would try to make the teacher
  the thing being praised.

- ❌ この小説(しょうせつ)は有名(ゆうめい)な作家(さっか)に書(か)かれた。 → ✅ …作家(さっか)<b>によって</b>書(か)かれた。
  With acts of creation and an inanimate subject, `によって` is the natural agent
  marker; plain `に` makes the novel sound like a victim.

- ❌ 夜中(よなか)に友(とも)だちに来(き)られた。 → ✅ …友(とも)だちに<b>来(こ)られた</b>。
  The passive stem of 来(く)る is こ. `き` is the ます-stem and has no business
  in a passive.

## Practice

**1.** 電車(でんしゃ)の中(なか)で、知(し)らない人(ひと)に話(はな)し____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

話(はな)し<b>かけられた</b>（or 話(はな)しかけられました）

<code>話(はな)しかける</code> is a る-verb, so the passive drops `る` and adds
`られる`. The stranger is the agent and takes `に`; the unstated subject is me, the
person it happened to.

</details>

**2.** 弟(おとうと)に私(わたし)のケーキを（食(た)べられた・食(た)べられる）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弟(おとうと)に私(わたし)のケーキを<b>食(た)べられた</b>。

Both are the same form, but the `に`-marked agent and the `を`-marked object make
this a passive, not a potential - and a passive about a completed nuisance goes in
the past. A potential reading would need `が` and no agent:
ケーキ<b>が</b>食(た)べられる, "I'm able to eat cake".

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I was invited to a party by a friend."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

友(とも)だちにパーティーに<b>誘(さそ)われた</b>（or 誘(さそ)われました）。

<code>誘(さそ)う</code> ends in `～う`, so the passive stem is
誘(さそ)<b>わ</b>, never ❌誘(さそ)あ. The inviter is the agent and takes `に`.
Note that this is a perfectly pleasant event - the passive is not obliged to be a
complaint, it just often is.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)、課長(かちょう)に新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を教(おし)えられて、とても感謝(かんしゃ)しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 課長(かちょう)に新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を<b>教(おし)えていただいて</b>、とても感謝(かんしゃ)しています。

The grammar of <code>教(おし)えられて</code> is sound, but the passive carries a
default note of nuisance, which clashes head-on with
<code>感謝(かんしゃ)しています</code>. For something done for your benefit, use
<code>～てもらう</code>, or <code>～ていただく</code> when the giver outranks you.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 隣(となり)の人(ひと)がタバコを吸(す)いました。
(b) 隣(となり)の人(ひと)にタバコを吸(す)われました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral report of what the person next to me did. It says nothing about
how I felt; it could just as easily be an observation about someone across the
room.

(b) rebuilds the sentence around me. The smoker becomes an agent in `に` and I
become the silent subject, which is the passive's way of saying *and I had to sit
in it*. There is no English structure that does this, which is why the natural
translation has to smuggle the complaint in somewhere else: "the guy next to me
went and lit up."

</details>

# ～させる - causative

**Meaning** · make someone do something, or let someone do something

One form, two meanings that feel like opposites in English. `～させる` says that the
subject **caused** the action - and Japanese leaves it to the particles and the
situation to say whether that causing was an order or a permission. Learning to
read that difference is most of the work on this page.

## Formation

The causative is built on the あ-stem - the same stem as the ない-form and the
passive - plus `せる`. る-verbs and 来(く)る take `させる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → あ-row kana, + せる | 行(い)く → 行(い)かせる |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + させる | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べさせる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by させる | 掃除(そうじ)する → 掃除(そうじ)させる |
| Verb (来(く)る) | stem こ, + させる | 来(く)る → 来(こ)させる |

Every う-verb ending. `～う` goes to `わ`, exactly as in the passive:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → わ + せる | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)わせる |
| ～く | く → か + せる | 働(はたら)く → 働(はたら)かせる |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → が + せる | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)がせる |
| ～す | す → さ + せる | 直(なお)す → 直(なお)させる |
| ～つ | つ → た + せる | 持(も)つ → 持(も)たせる |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → な + せる | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)なせる |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → ば + せる | 運(はこ)ぶ → 運(はこ)ばせる |
| ～む | む → ま + せる | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)ませる |
| ～る | る → ら + せる | 座(すわ)る → 座(すわ)らせる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - passive `れる`, causative `せる`. The stem is identical; one
consonant carries the whole difference. 読(よ)ま<b>れる</b> = it was read on me.
読(よ)ま<b>せる</b> = I made someone read it.

</div>

A causative verb is a る-verb: 行(い)かせる → 行(い)かせない,
行(い)かせます, 行(い)かせて, 行(い)かせた.

**Particles.** This is where the meaning lives:

| Verb in the causative | Causee | Object | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intransitive | を | - | 子(こ)どもを帰(かえ)らせる |
| Intransitive, causee willing | に | - | 子(こ)どもに帰(かえ)らせる |
| Transitive | に | を | 子(こ)どもに野菜(やさい)を食(た)べさせる |
| Verb of emotion | を only | - | 母(はは)を心配(しんぱい)させる |

## Examples

<pre>
母(はは)は妹(いもうと)に部屋(へや)を<b>掃除(そうじ)させた</b>。
My mother made my little sister clean her room.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)は学生(がくせい)に作文(さくぶん)を<b>書(か)かせました</b>。
The teacher had the students write an essay.
</pre>

<pre>
つまらないことを言(い)って妹(いもうと)を<b>泣(な)かせて</b>しまった。
I said something stupid and made my little sister cry.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもを一人(ひとり)で公園(こうえん)に<b>行(い)かせる</b>のは心配(しんぱい)だ。
I'm nervous about letting the kids go to the park on their own.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は私(わたし)に好(す)きな大学(だいがく)を<b>選(えら)ばせて</b>くれた。
My father let me choose whichever university I wanted.
</pre>

<pre>
娘(むすめ)が熱(ねつ)を出(だ)したので、学校(がっこう)を<b>休(やす)ませる</b>ことにした。
My daughter had a fever, so I decided to keep her home from school.
</pre>

<pre>
あの部長(ぶちょう)は部下(ぶか)を毎晩(まいばん)遅(おそ)くまで<b>残業(ざんぎょう)させている</b>。
That manager has his staff working overtime until late every night.
</pre>

<pre>
連絡(れんらく)もしないで三日(みっか)も帰(かえ)らないなんて、家族(かぞく)を<b>心配(しんぱい)させる</b>にもほどがある。
Not coming home for three days without a word - there's a limit to how much worry you can put your family through.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The form is register-neutral, but the **social geometry** is not. A causative
sentence says the subject has enough authority over the causee to bring the action
about. That means it is fundamentally a downward or lateral form: a parent over a
child, a teacher over a student, a manager over staff. Using it with a superior as
the causee is not merely impolite, it is close to nonsense - 
❌社長(しゃちょう)に資料(しりょう)を作(つく)らせました claims you gave the
president orders. What you want is
<code>作(つく)っていただきました</code>.

The one place a causative points **upward** is when you turn it on yourself.
<code>～させていただく</code> frames your own action as something the listener
graciously permits, and it is the backbone of business humility:
説明(せつめい)させていただきます, お先(さき)に失礼(しつれい)させていただきます.

**The short causative.** う-verbs have a contracted causative in `～す`:
行(い)かす, 飲(の)ます, 待(ま)たす, やらす. It is casual, common in
speech and especially associated with Kansai. It conjugates as an う-verb
(行(い)かした, 行(い)かして). Keep `～せる` in writing and in polite speech.

**Emotion causatives** are so common they are worth learning as vocabulary:
喜(よろこ)ばせる, 困(こま)らせる, 驚(おどろ)かせる,
安心(あんしん)させる, がっかりさせる. They take the person in `を`, and they are
the normal way to say "make someone feel X" - there is no separate construction.

## Contrast

### させる - "make" vs "let"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nothing in the verb distinguishes them. Look at three things:
the particle on the causee, whether the causee wanted it, and whether a
`～てあげる`／`～てくれる` is attached.

</div>

With an **intransitive** verb the particle is a live choice, and it carries weight:

<pre>
息子(むすこ)を塾(じゅく)に<b>通(かよ)わせている</b>。
I have my son attending cram school. (my decision)
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)に好(す)きなだけ<b>遊(あそ)ばせている</b>。
I let my son play as much as he likes. (his wish, I allow it)
</pre>

`を` presents the causee as something the subject moves around; `に` presents them
as a person with their own will who is being given room. With a **transitive**
verb the object has already claimed `を`, so the causee is stuck with `に` and the
particle stops helping - 弟(おとうと)に皿(さら)を洗(あら)わせた is "made"
or "let" depending entirely on whether 弟(おとうと) wanted to.

The clearest signal in real Japanese is the benefactive tail. A causative with
`～てくれる`, `～てあげる` or `～てやる` is always *let*, because those verbs mean
the action was a favour:

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は会議(かいぎ)に新人(しんじん)を<b>出(で)させてくれた</b>。
The section chief let the new hire sit in on the meeting.
</pre>

Strip the `くれた` and 新人(しんじん)を出(で)させた tilts straight back
towards "made him attend".

### ～させる vs ～てもらう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - who is holding the power? `～させる` puts it with the subject;
`～てもらう` puts it with the other person, whose cooperation you receive.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は山田(やまだ)さんに資料(しりょう)を<b>集(あつ)めさせた</b>。
The manager had Yamada gather the materials.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんに資料(しりょう)を<b>集(あつ)めてもらった</b>。
I got Yamada to gather the materials (and I'm grateful).
</pre>

Both English translations can be "had someone do it", which is why the pair is
confusing. The Japanese is not ambiguous at all: `させる` says you had the standing
to require it; `てもらう` says they did you a favour. Since you almost never have
standing over a customer or a senior, `～ていただく` is the only safe choice
upwards.

### ～させる vs a lexical transitive verb

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the language already owns a transitive verb for the idea,
use it. A causative built on the intransitive twin sounds laboured, and sometimes
means something different.

</div>

Japanese has hundreds of intransitive/transitive pairs, and the transitive member
already contains "cause":

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)七(しち)時(じ)に子(こ)どもを<b>起(お)こす</b>。
I wake the children at seven every morning.
</pre>

起(お)きさせる exists, but it means something narrower and stranger - forcing
someone to get themselves up. The same holds for
見(み)せる versus 見(み)させる: 写真(しゃしん)を見(み)せる is "show
someone a photo", while 写真(しゃしん)を見(み)させる is "make them look at
it". Other pairs to reach for first: 着(き)せる (dress someone),
止(と)める (stop something), 入(い)れる (put in),
決(き)める (decide).

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 使(つか)あせる → ✅ 使(つか)<b>わせる</b>
  Verbs in `～う` take `わ`, matching 使(つか)わない and 使(つか)われる.

- ❌ 食(た)べらせる → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>させる</b>
  る-verbs add the whole of `させる`. Only う-verbs get the bare `せる`.

- ❌ 子(こ)ども<b>に</b>泣(な)かせた。 → ✅ 子(こ)ども<b>を</b>泣(な)かせた。
  Nobody permits a child to cry. Verbs of emotion take the causee in `を`, because
  the "letting" reading is unavailable.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>を</b>作文(さくぶん)を書(か)かせる → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>に</b>作文(さくぶん)を書(か)かせる
  One `を` per clause. The object has it, so the causee falls to `に`.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)に説明(せつめい)させました。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に<b>説明(せつめい)していただきました</b>。
  A causative claims authority over the causee. Pointed at your boss it is worse
  than rude - it is a status error.

## Practice

**1.** 母(はは)は弟(おとうと)____薬(くすり)を飲(の)ませた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

母(はは)は弟(おとうと)<b>に</b>薬(くすり)を飲(の)ませた。

<code>飲(の)む</code> is transitive, so <code>薬(くすり)</code> already holds
the `を`. A clause cannot carry two `を`, so the causee takes `に`.

</details>

**2.** 娘(むすめ)がピアノを習(なら)いたいと言(い)うので、（習(なら)わせて・習(なら)われて）あげることにした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

習(なら)いたいと言(い)うので、<b>習(なら)わせて</b>あげることにした。

The daughter wants the lessons and the parent permits them - that is the "let"
side of the causative, and `～てあげる` confirms it is being done as a kindness.
<code>習(なら)われる</code> is the passive and would mean somebody took lessons
at my expense.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The teacher made the students stand for thirty minutes."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先生(せんせい)は学生(がくせい)を三十(さんじゅっ)分(ぷん)<b>立(た)たせた</b>。

<code>立(た)つ</code> is an う-verb ending in `つ`, so the causative is
立(た)た + せる. It is intransitive and this is plainly coercion, so the causee
takes `を`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 社長(しゃちょう)に新(あたら)しい計画(けいかく)を考(かんが)えさせました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 社長(しゃちょう)に新(あたら)しい計画(けいかく)を<b>考(かんが)えていただきました</b>。

The conjugation is right and the sentence is still unusable: a causative asserts
that you had the authority to set the president a task. Anything you receive from
someone above you goes through <code>～ていただく</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 息子(むすこ)を留学(りゅうがく)させた。
(b) 息子(むすこ)に留学(りゅうがく)させた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are the causative of an intransitive action, so the particle is doing all the
work. (a) with `を` treats the son as the thing being moved: the decision was the
parent's, and it can read as "I sent my son abroad", possibly over his objections.

(b) with `に` grants the son his own will and reads as permission - he wanted to
study abroad and I let him go. Neither is rude; the difference is simply whose
idea it was, and a listener will draw exactly that inference from the particle.

</details>

# ～させられる - causative-passive

**Meaning** · to be made to do something you did not want to do

This is the causative with the passive stacked on top of it, and the meaning is
exactly that sum: somebody made me do it, and I am the one it happened to. Almost
every sentence built with `～させられる` carries an audible *and I hated it*.

## Formation

Build it in two steps, in this order. Get the causative first; the passive is then
mechanical.

1. Make the **causative**: 飲(の)む → 飲(の)ませる.
2. Treat that as a る-verb and make it **passive**: drop `る`, add `られる` →
   飲(の)ませられる.

For う-verbs, step 2 then contracts: `～せられ` collapses to `～され`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | あ-stem + せられる, normally contracted to あ-stem + される | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)ませられる → 飲(の)まされる |
| Verb (う-verb ending in ～す) | あ-stem + せられる - **never contracts** | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)させられる |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + させられる | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べさせられる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by させられる | 掃除(そうじ)する → 掃除(そうじ)させられる |
| Verb (来(く)る) | stem こ, + させられる | 来(く)る → 来(こ)させられる |

Every う-verb ending, with the contracted form on the right:

| Ending | Full form | Contracted |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | 歌(うた)わせられる | 歌(うた)わされる |
| ～く | 書(か)かせられる | 書(か)かされる |
| ～ぐ | 脱(ぬ)がせられる | 脱(ぬ)がされる |
| ～す | 出(だ)させられる | - (no contraction) |
| ～つ | 待(ま)たせられる | 待(ま)たされる |
| ～ぬ | 死(し)なせられる | 死(し)なされる |
| ～ぶ | 運(はこ)ばせられる | 運(はこ)ばされる |
| ～む | 飲(の)ませられる | 飲(の)まされる |
| ～る | 座(すわ)らせられる | 座(すわ)らされる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `～す` row is the one that catches people. Contracting it
would give ❌話(はな)さされる, with two `さ` in a row, and Japanese refuses.
Verbs in `～す` keep the full 話(はな)させられる, 出(だ)させられる,
直(なお)させられる.

</div>

る-verbs, する and 来(く)る never contract either: 食(た)べさせられる,
させられる, 来(こ)させられる are the only forms.

The sentence shape is the passive's, with the person who gave the order in `に`:

<code>私(わたし)は 母(はは)に 野菜(やさい)を 食(た)べさせられた。</code>

## Examples

<pre>
子(こ)どものころ、母(はは)に野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べさせられた</b>。
As a child I was made to eat my vegetables by my mother.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)漢字(かんじ)を<b>書(か)かされる</b>。
We get made to write kanji every day.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に二(に)時間(じかん)も<b>待(ま)たされた</b>。
The manager kept me waiting a full two hours.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころ、ピアノを<b>習(なら)わされて</b>、あまり好(す)きになれなかった。
I was made to take piano lessons as a child, and I never really warmed to it.
</pre>

<pre>
飲(の)み会(かい)でカラオケを<b>歌(うた)わされる</b>のが苦手(にがて)だ。
I hate being made to sing karaoke at drinking parties.
</pre>

<pre>
新入(しんにゅう)社員(しゃいん)のときは、毎朝(まいあさ)早(はや)く来(き)て<b>掃除(そうじ)させられました</b>。
When I was a new employee I had to come in early every morning and do the cleaning.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)で急(きゅう)に意見(いけん)を<b>言(い)わされて</b>、頭(あたま)が真(ま)っ白(しろ)になった。
I was suddenly put on the spot for an opinion in the meeting and my mind went blank.
</pre>

<pre>
戦争(せんそう)を扱(あつか)ったあの映画(えいが)には、いろいろ<b>考(かんが)えさせられた</b>。
That film about the war really gave me a lot to think about.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The default colour of `～させられる` is **grievance**. Even without any explicit
complaint attached, the form implies the speaker had no say. That makes it the
natural way to describe unpaid overtime, childhood piano lessons, drinking-party
rituals and school rules, and an odd way to describe anything you enjoyed. If the
action was welcome, use `～させてもらう` or `～させていただく` instead:
好(す)きな仕事(しごと)をやらせてもらっている.

**Contracted or full?** For う-verbs, the contracted `～される` is what people
actually say: 待(ま)たされる, 飲(の)まされる, 買(か)わされる. The full
`～せられる` is not wrong, but it sounds heavy and slightly literary, and outside
the `～す` verbs you will rarely hear it in conversation.

**The one warm use.** A handful of verbs of thought and feeling take the
causative-passive to mean "it moved me", with no unwillingness at all - 
考(かんが)えさせられる, 驚(おどろ)かされる, 感動(かんどう)させられる. Here
the cause is usually a thing rather than a person, and the `に` marks that thing:
その言葉(ことば)に考(かんが)えさせられた. This is common in reviews and
essays.

**Register.** The form is neutral and works at any politeness level - 
残業(ざんぎょう)させられました is perfectly polite. What it is not is *neutral in
content*: describing your boss's instructions as `させられました` in front of that
boss is a small act of protest, and will be heard as one.

## Contrast

### ～させられる vs ～させる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same event seen from opposite ends. `～させる` puts the person
with the power in the subject slot; `～させられる` puts the person who obeyed there.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)は私(わたし)に教室(きょうしつ)を<b>片付(かたづ)けさせた</b>。
The teacher made me tidy the classroom.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は先生(せんせい)に教室(きょうしつ)を<b>片付(かたづ)けさせられた</b>。
I was made to tidy the classroom by the teacher.
</pre>

The facts are identical; the sympathy is not. The second sentence is the one you
say to a friend afterwards. Note the particle swap: the teacher moves from subject
to `に`-marked agent, exactly as in an ordinary passive.

### ～させられる vs the plain passive ～られる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the plain passive somebody did the action **to** you; in the
causative-passive **you** did the action, under orders.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)に作文(さくぶん)を<b>読(よ)まれた</b>。
The teacher read my essay. (the teacher's eyes, my writing)
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)に作文(さくぶん)を<b>読(よ)まされた</b>。
The teacher made me read my essay out. (my mouth, under duress)
</pre>

The two differ by a single `さ`, and they attribute the action to different people.
Check who physically performs the verb: if it is you, the sentence needs the extra
causative layer.

### ～させられる vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～させられる` needs a **person** applying pressure.
`～なければならない` is obligation with no named source - rules, circumstance,
common sense.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)までにこの書類(しょるい)を出(だ)<b>さなければならない</b>。
I have to submit this document by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)に明日(あした)までにこの書類(しょるい)を<b>出(だ)させられる</b>。
The section chief is making me submit this document by tomorrow.
</pre>

The first is a statement about a deadline; the second is a complaint about a man.
Because `～させられる` points at a person, it is far more loaded - using it where
plain obligation was meant sounds like you are picking a fight.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 話(はな)さされる → ✅ 話(はな)<b>させられる</b>
  Verbs ending in `～す` do not contract. The same goes for
  出(だ)させられる and 直(なお)させられる.

- ❌ 読(よ)まさせられる → ✅ 読(よ)<b>ませられる</b>／読(よ)<b>まされる</b>
  This is さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば) - an extra `さ` in an う-verb. Either the full form or the
  contraction, never a hybrid of the two.

- ❌ 食(た)べさされる → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>させられる</b>
  る-verbs never contract. Only う-verbs can lose the `せら`.

- ❌ 母(はは)<b>を</b>野菜(やさい)を食(た)べさせられた。 → ✅ 母(はは)<b>に</b>野菜(やさい)を食(た)べさせられた。
  The person giving the order is an agent, and agents in a passive take `に`.

- ❌ 休(やす)みの日(ひ)に会社(かいしゃ)に来(き)させられた。 → ✅ …<b>来(こ)させられた</b>。
  The causative stem of 来(く)る is こ. `き` belongs only to 来(き)ます.

## Practice

**1.** 部長(ぶちょう)に一(いち)時間(じかん)も立(た)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

立(た)<b>たされた</b>（full form: 立(た)たせられた）

<code>立(た)つ</code> → causative 立(た)たせる → causative-passive
立(た)たせられる, which contracts to 立(た)たされる. The contracted form is
what a speaker would use out loud.

</details>

**2.** 先生(せんせい)にみんなの前(まえ)で（話(はな)させられた・話(はな)さされた）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先生(せんせい)にみんなの前(まえ)で<b>話(はな)させられた</b>。

<code>話(はな)す</code> ends in `～す`, and that is the one う-verb ending whose
causative-passive refuses to contract - ❌話(はな)さされる would put two `さ`
together.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "As a child I was made to drink milk every morning."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)どものころ、毎朝(まいあさ)牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を<b>飲(の)まされた</b>。

<code>飲(の)む</code> → 飲(の)ませる → 飲(の)ませられる →
飲(の)まされる. The unwillingness is built into the form, so no extra word for
"had to" is needed.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新入(しんにゅう)社員(しゃいん)のとき、毎日(まいにち)コピーを取(と)らさせられた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 毎日(まいにち)コピーを<b>取(と)らされた</b>（or 取(と)らせられた）。

<code>取(と)る</code> is an う-verb, so the causative is 取(と)らせる and the
causative-passive is 取(と)らせられる → 取(と)らされる. The extra `さ` in
取(と)らさせられた is さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば), a very common slip in speech and still an error.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この本(ほん)を読(よ)んで、いろいろ考(かんが)えた。
(b) この本(ほん)には、いろいろ考(かんが)えさせられた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports that reading the book led to some thinking. The speaker is the
active party and the sentence is flat.

(b) hands the initiative to the book. The causative-passive says the thinking was
provoked - I did not set out to reflect, the book made me. Unusually for this form
there is no resentment in it at all; with verbs like
<code>考(かんが)える</code>, <code>驚(おどろ)く</code> and
<code>感動(かんどう)する</code> it is a compliment, and it is the standard way to
praise a book or a film in writing.

</details>

# ～させてください

**Meaning** · please let me do it - asking permission by asking the other person to
cause it

Literally this says "please cause me to do X". You are not asking whether you may;
you are asking the listener to grant it, which puts the decision squarely on their
side of the table. That framing is why `～させてください` is so much more deferential
than a flat "is it okay if I…".

## Formation

Make the causative, put it in the て-form, then add `ください`. The te-form step is
easy because every causative verb is a る-verb: drop `る`, add `て`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | あ-stem + せて + ください | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)わせてください |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + させて + ください | 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)えさせてください |
| Verb (する) | させて + ください | 参加(さんか)する → 参加(さんか)させてください |
| Verb (来(く)る) | stem こ, + させて + ください | 来(く)る → 来(こ)させてください |

う-verb endings, since this is where the slips happen:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → わ + せて | 手伝(てつだ)う → 手伝(てつだ)わせて |
| ～く | く → か + せて | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)かせて |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → が + せて | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)がせて |
| ～す | す → さ + せて | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)させて |
| ～つ | つ → た + せて | 持(も)つ → 持(も)たせて |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → な + せて | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)なせて |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → ば + せて | 運(はこ)ぶ → 運(はこ)ばせて |
| ～む | む → ま + せて | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)ませて |
| ～る | る → ら + せて | 帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)らせて |

The whole politeness ladder is built on that same causative て-form:

| Register | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Casual | ～させて | ちょっと使(つか)わせて |
| Standard polite | ～させてください | 使(つか)わせてください |
| Polite request | ～させていただけませんか | 使(つか)わせていただけませんか |
| Tentative | ～させていただきたいのですが | 使(つか)わせていただきたいのですが |
| Humble declaration | ～させていただきます | 使(つか)わせていただきます |

## Examples

<pre>
すみません、電話(でんわ)を<b>使(つか)わせてください</b>。
Excuse me, could you let me use the phone?
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>休(やす)ませてください</b>。
Please let me rest for a bit.
</pre>

<pre>
その仕事(しごと)、ぜひ私(わたし)に<b>やらせてください</b>。
Please let me take that job on.
</pre>

<pre>
大事(だいじ)なことなので、もう一度(いちど)<b>考(かんが)えさせてください</b>。
It's an important decision, so please let me think it over once more.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の午後(ごご)、少(すこ)し早(はや)く<b>帰(かえ)らせていただけませんか</b>。
Would it be possible for me to leave a little early tomorrow afternoon?
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)、休(やす)みを<b>取(と)らせていただきたいのですが</b>。
I'd like to take next Friday off, if that would be all right.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)を読(よ)んでから、来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)で<b>説明(せつめい)させてください</b>。
Let me read the materials and then explain at next week's meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
せっかくの機会(きかい)ですので、ぜひ私(わたし)にも<b>参加(さんか)させてください</b>。
It's such a good opportunity - please let me join in too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～させてください` is polite, but it is not humble in itself; it is a direct request
with `ください` on the end. In business the humble branch of the ladder does most of
the work:

- <b>～させていただきます</b> announces what you are about to do while crediting the
  other side with permitting it: 担当(たんとう)の田中(たなか)が
  説明(せつめい)させていただきます. It is everywhere in Japanese business and
  broadcasting, and it is also the form that gets criticised for overuse - used
  where nobody is granting anything (開店(かいてん)させていただきます on a shop
  notice), it sounds hollow.
- <b>～させていただけませんか</b> is a genuine question and the safest way to ask a
  superior for something inconvenient.
- <b>～させていただきたいのですが</b> trails off deliberately, leaving the listener to
  offer. Very common, very soft.

Several fixed phrases live here and are worth memorising whole:
お先(さき)に失礼(しつれい)させていただきます (leaving before your colleagues),
遠慮(えんりょ)させていただきます (a polite refusal - literally "let me hold
back"), 検討(けんとう)させていただきます (we'll consider it, which in practice
often means no).

In casual speech the bare て-form is the everyday version: ちょっと見(み)させて,
明日(あした)使(つか)わせて. Among friends `～させてよ` and `～させてくれ`
also occur, the latter distinctly masculine and blunt.

One structural point: because the *speaker* is the one performing the action,
`～させてください` almost always has an unstated 私(わたし)に. Adding it
explicitly (私(わたし)にやらせてください) is a way of pushing yourself forward - 
volunteering, or claiming the job over someone else.

## Contrast

### ～させてください vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` asks **you** to do it. `～させてください` asks you
to let **me** do it. The `させ` flips who acts.

</div>

This is the highest-frequency error at N4, because English "please help" hides the
distinction entirely.

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)っ<b>てください</b>。
Please help me. (you do the helping)
</pre>

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)わ<b>せてください</b>。
Please let me help. (I do the helping)
</pre>

Both are polite, and they are nearly opposite offers. Before you say either one,
check whose hands are going to move.

### ～させてください vs ～てもいいですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもいいですか` asks whether a rule allows it.
`～させてください` asks a person to allow it. The second is warmer and puts you
lower.

</div>

<pre>
この席(せき)に座(すわ)っ<b>てもいいですか</b>。
Is it okay if I sit here? (checking, neutral)
</pre>

<pre>
その仕事(しごと)を私(わたし)に<b>担当(たんとう)させてください</b>。
Please put me on that job. (asking to be granted it)
</pre>

Use `～てもいいですか` for small, impersonal permissions - a seat, a window, a
photograph. Use `～させてください` when the other person genuinely holds the
authority, and when you want to sound like you are asking a favour rather than
checking the rules. Asking a manager
❌明日(あした)休(やす)んでもいいですか is not rude exactly, but
休(やす)ませていただけませんか is what a careful employee says.

### ～させてください vs ～させていただきます

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～させてください` **asks**. `～させていただきます` **announces**,
having already assumed the permission.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)に、資料(しりょう)を<b>配(くば)らせてください</b>。
Please let me hand out the materials before the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
それでは、資料(しりょう)を<b>配(くば)らせていただきます</b>。
Right then - allow me to hand out the materials.
</pre>

The first is still open; the second is what you say as you start moving. Because
`いただきます` is a statement, using it for something the listener has not agreed
to can come across as presumptuous - that is the real risk with this very useful
phrase.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 手伝(てつだ)ってください（meaning "let me help"） → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)<b>わせてください</b>
  Without `させ` you are asking the other person to do the work.

- ❌ 使(つか)わさせてください → ✅ 使(つか)<b>わせてください</b>
  This is さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば). う-verbs take a bare `せる`, so the causative て-form is
  使(つか)わせて - no extra `さ`.

- ❌ 帰(かえ)らさせていただきます → ✅ 帰(かえ)<b>らせていただきます</b>
  Same error, in the phrase where it is heard most often. 帰(かえ)る is an
  う-verb.

- ❌ 考(かんが)えらせてください → ✅ 考(かんが)え<b>させてください</b>
  考(かんが)える is a る-verb, so it takes the full `させる`, not `せる`.

- ❌ 来(き)させてください → ✅ <b>来(こ)させてください</b>
  The causative stem of 来(く)る is こ.

**1.** その件(けん)については、もう少(すこ)し考(かんが)え____ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>考(かんが)える</code> is a る-verb: drop `る`, add `させる`, then take the
て-form, giving 考(かんが)えさせて. The speaker is the one who will do the
thinking, so the causative is essential.

</details>

**2.** 先生(せんせい)、明日(あした)のクラスを（休(やす)んでください・休(やす)ませてください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Without `させ`, you would be telling your teacher to take the day off. The `させ`
puts the action back on you and the permission with the teacher.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "Would you let me use this computer for
about an hour?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: すみません、その写真(しゃしん)をちょっと見(み)らせてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ その写真(しゃしん)をちょっと<b>見(み)せてください</b>（or <b>見(み)させてください</b>）。

<code>見(み)る</code> is a る-verb, so its causative is 見(み)させる - never
❌見(み)らせる. In practice, though, Japanese already has the transitive verb

<code>見(み)せてください</code> is what a speaker would actually say when asking

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)早(はや)く帰(かえ)ってもいいですか。
(b) 明日(あした)早(はや)く帰(かえ)らせていただけませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats leaving early as a matter of policy and asks whether the policy permits
it. It is grammatical and polite in form, but it puts the question on the same
level as asking whether you may open a window - which, addressed to a manager about
your working hours, can sound casual.

(b) hands the decision to the listener as a personal favour and lowers the speaker
with `いただく`. It is longer, softer, and the form an employee would normally use
for anything that inconveniences the team.

</details>

# 命令形 - the plain imperative

**Meaning** · a bare order - "do it" - with nothing softening it

The 命令形(めいれいけい) is the shortest possible way to tell someone to act.
It has no politeness of any kind built in, which makes it rude between adults and
exactly right in three places: emergencies, signs, and quoting an order that
someone else gave.

## Formation

う-verbs shift their final kana one row; る-verbs take `ろ`; the irregulars must be
learnt.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → え-row kana | 書(か)く → 書(か)け |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + ろ (spoken) or + よ (written) | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べろ／食(た)べよ |
| Verb (する) | しろ (spoken) or せよ (written) | 注意(ちゅうい)する → 注意(ちゅうい)しろ |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)い - nothing is added, the whole word changes | 来(く)る → 来(こ)い |

Every う-verb ending:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → え | 買(か)う → 買(か)え |
| ～く | く → け | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)け |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → げ | 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)げ |
| ～す | す → せ | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)せ |
| ～つ | つ → て | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)て |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → ね | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)ね |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → べ | 呼(よ)ぶ → 呼(よ)べ |
| ～む | む → め | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)め |
| ～る | る → れ | 走(はし)る → 走(はし)れ |

<div class="note">

**A word about the ぬ row** - 死(し)ぬ is used here because it is essentially the
only ぬ-verb in ordinary modern Japanese, so every conjugation table in this book
falls back on it. Be aware that its imperative, <b>死(し)ね</b>, is a serious
insult - roughly "drop dead" - and is genuinely shocking to say. Learn the
conjugation; never use the word.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a handful of verbs break the pattern. `くれる` gives <b>くれ</b>, not
❌くれろ. The four honorific う-verbs take `～い` instead of `～え`:
いらっしゃる → いらっしゃい, なさる → なさい, くださる → ください,
おっしゃる → おっしゃい. Those endings are the reason
<code>ください</code> and <code>なさい</code> look the way they do.

</div>

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for う-verbs the imperative is the potential stem without the
`る`: 書(か)<b>け</b>る is "can write", 書(か)<b>け</b> is "write!". One syllable
apart, and worlds apart in tone.

</div>

The negative counterpart is dictionary form + `な`: 行(い)くな, "don't go".

## Examples

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>しろ</b>。
Hurry up.
</pre>

<pre>
こっちに<b>来(こ)い</b>。
Get over here.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あぶ)ない、<b>逃(に)げろ</b>！
Look out - run!
</pre>

<pre>
<b>がんばれ</b>、あと少(すこ)しだ。
Come on, you're nearly there!
</pre>

<pre>
門(もん)のところに「<b>止(と)まれ</b>」と書(か)いてある。
There's a "STOP" painted at the gate.
</pre>

<pre>
監督(かんとく)は選手(せんしゅ)たちに「もっと<b>走(はし)れ</b>」と怒鳴(どな)った。
The coach yelled at the players to run harder.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が来(く)るぞ、白(しろ)い線(せん)の内側(うちがわ)に<b>下(さ)がれ</b>。
Train coming - get back behind the white line.
</pre>

<pre>
次(つぎ)の文章(ぶんしょう)を読(よ)んで、あとの問(とい)に<b>答(こた)えよ</b>。
Read the following passage and answer the questions below.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Treat the 命令形(めいれいけい) as a form you must **recognise** rather than one
you use. Pointed at a real person it is harsh - it is what you hear in an
argument, from an angry parent, or from a drill instructor. Most Japanese adults go
weeks without saying one to another adult.

Where it does live:

- **Emergencies.** Politeness costs syllables, and syllables cost lives:
  逃(に)げろ, 伏(ふ)せろ, 離(はな)れろ. Nobody hears these as rude.
- **Signs and instructions.** 止(と)まれ, 押(お)せ, 引(ひ)け. Impersonal
  and addressed to no one in particular. Exam papers use the `よ` variant:
  答(こた)えよ, 選(えら)べ, 述(の)べよ.
- **Cheering.** がんばれ, 勝(か)て, 行(い)け - a stadium is exempt.
- **Sport, the military, fiction.** Coaches to players, seniors to juniors in a
  club, and a very large share of what characters shout in manga and anime.
- **Quoting.** Reporting an order almost always keeps the imperative inside the
  quote: 「待(ま)て」と言(い)われた.

**Register colouring.** The plain imperative is strongly male-associated in
conversation. Women use it in emergencies, in anger and in cheering, but a woman
telling a friend 早(はや)く食(た)べろ would be marked. The gentler downward
orders - `～なさい` and the bare て-form - are unmarked for either speaker.

`ろ` versus `よ`: `ろ` is spoken, `よ` is written and formal. 見(み)ろ in
speech, 見(み)よ in a slogan or on a test. For する the split is
しろ／せよ, and せよ has a distinctly stiff, official ring.

Adding a particle does not rescue it. 早(はや)くしろ<b>よ</b> is nagging;
来(こ)い<b>よ</b> is a blunt invitation between close male friends. If you want to
be gentle, change the form, not the ending.

## Contrast

### 命令形(めいれいけい) vs ～なさい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are orders given downward, but `～なさい` comes with
authority and care, while the 命令形(めいれいけい) comes with force. A parent
uses `～なさい`; the same parent uses the 命令形(めいれいけい) when they have
run out of patience.

</div>

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)なさい</b>。
Go to bed now. (parent, firm but calm)
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)ろ</b>。
Get to bed. (sharp, angry, or male-casual)
</pre>

`～なさい` is also the only one of the two that appears in neutral written
instructions to a *person* - school worksheets, cookery books for children. The
命令形(めいれいけい) in writing is reserved for signage and for the terse `よ`
form on exams.

### 命令形(めいれいけい) vs ～てください and the bare ～て

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` is a request and can go upward or sideways. The
bare て-form is a casual request between equals. The
命令形(めいれいけい) is neither - it does not request anything.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>閉(し)めてください</b>。
Please close the window.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)、<b>閉(し)めて</b>。
Shut the window, would you.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>閉(し)めろ</b>。
Shut that window.
</pre>

The bare て-form is the one to reach for in casual speech. It is friendly, it is
used by everyone, and it is what learners usually *mean* when they produce a
命令形(めいれいけい) by accident.

### 命令形(めいれいけい) vs ～な - the negative order

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the positive order changes the verb's ending; the negative
order does not. Dictionary form + `な` is "don't", and it is just as blunt.

</div>

<pre>
そこに<b>入(はい)れ</b>。
Get in there.
</pre>

<pre>
そこに<b>入(はい)るな</b>。
Don't go in there.
</pre>

These two are a matched pair in tone - signs use both
(止(と)まれ, 入(はい)るな) and so do angry people. Beware the collision with
the *softened* command written the same way: ます-stem + `な` is a gentle order
contracted from `～なさい`. 食(た)べる<b>な</b> means "don't eat";
食(た)べ<b>な</b> means "eat up". The difference is a single `る`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 来(く)ろ／来(き)ろ → ✅ <b>来(こ)い</b>
  来(く)る has no `ろ` imperative at all; the whole word is replaced.

- ❌ するろ／しれ → ✅ <b>しろ</b>（written: <b>せよ</b>）
  する is irregular here too. Its imperative is not built from the dictionary
  form.

- ❌ くれろ → ✅ <b>くれ</b>
  `くれる` looks like an ordinary る-verb and is not. Compare
  見(み)せる → 見(み)せろ, which is regular.

- ❌ 食(た)べれ → ✅ <b>食(た)べろ</b>
  Applying the う-verb rule to a る-verb produces 食(た)べれ, which is not a
  word. る-verbs take `ろ`.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)、これを読(よ)め。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、これを<b>読(よ)んでください</b>。
  The conjugation is fine; the sentence is not. Never aim a
  命令形(めいれいけい) at anyone you owe respect to.

## Practice

**1.** 急(いそ)いでいるんだ、早(はや)く歩(ある)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>歩(ある)く</code> is an う-verb, so the final `く` moves to the え-row and
nothing is added: 歩(ある)け. Note how blunt this is - between friends
早(はや)く歩(ある)いて would be normal.

</details>

**2.** 試験(しけん)問題(もんだい): 正(ただ)しい答(こた)えを一(ひと)つ（選(えら)べ・選(えら)んでください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Exam papers address no particular person, so the impersonal
命令形(めいれいけい) is standard there. <code>～てください</code> would sound
oddly chatty on a test paper.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a shout: "Stop! Don't touch that!"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The positive order changes the verb ending (やめる → やめろ, a
る-verb) and the negative order adds `な` to the dictionary form
(触(さわ)る → 触(さわ)るな). Shouted in an emergency, neither is rude.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)、この書類(しょるい)にサインしろ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`しろ` is the correct imperative of する, so the grammar is not the problem - the
target is. A 命令形(めいれいけい) aimed at a superior is about as bad as
Japanese gets. Upwards, a request has to be a request.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 静(しず)かにしろ。
(b) 静(しず)かにしなさい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is raw force. It is what someone says when they have lost their temper, or a
line in a film; from a stranger it would start a fight, and in conversation it is
strongly male-coded.

(b) still comes from above - you could not say it to your boss - but it carries the
tone of a person with legitimate authority correcting someone in their care. A
teacher says it to a noisy class without any anger at all. Both are downward; only
(a) is aggressive.

</details>

# ～なさい

**Meaning** · a firm instruction given downward - "do it", said with authority
rather than anger

`～なさい` is the imperative of `なさる`, the honorific verb for "do". The
politeness in its ancestry has drained away, and what is left is a command that is
softer in tone than the bare 命令形(めいれいけい) but strictly one-directional:
parent to child, teacher to student, exam paper to candidate. You cannot aim it
upwards, and pointing it at an adult equal is condescending.

## Formation

Attach `なさい` to the <b>ます-stem</b>. If you can build 書(か)きます, you can build
書(か)きなさい.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem (final う-row kana → い-row kana) + なさい | 書(か)く → 書(か)きなさい |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + なさい | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べなさい |
| Verb (する) | し + なさい | 勉強(べんきょう)する → 勉強(べんきょう)しなさい |
| Verb (来(く)る) | stem き, + なさい | 来(く)る → 来(き)なさい |

Every う-verb ending:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → い | 買(か)う → 買(か)いなさい |
| ～く | く → き | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)きなさい |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → ぎ | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)ぎなさい |
| ～す | す → し | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)しなさい |
| ～つ | つ → ち | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ちなさい |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → に | 死(し)ぬ → 死(し)になさい |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → び | 呼(よ)ぶ → 呼(よ)びなさい |
| ～む | む → み | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)みなさい |
| ～る | る → り | 座(すわ)る → 座(すわ)りなさい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three different stems of 来(く)る turn up in this chapter and
they are easy to swap by accident. <b>来(き)なさい</b> (ます-stem),
<b>来(こ)い</b> (imperative), <b>来(こ)られる／来(こ)させる</b> (potential,
passive, causative). Only `なさい` uses き.

</div>

| Related forms | Example |
|---|---|
| Contracted, casual | ます-stem + な - 食(た)べな, 行(い)きな |
| Negative | none. Use ～ないようにしなさい or ～てはいけません |
| Fossilised in fixed phrases | お休(やす)みなさい, お帰(かえ)りなさい, ごめんなさい |

もう遅(おそ)いから早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)なさい</b>。
It's late - go to bed.
</pre>

<pre>

Wash your hands before dinner.
</pre>

<pre>

Answer the following questions in Japanese.
</pre>

<pre>
もっと野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べなさい</b>と母(はは)に言(い)われた。

</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)を終(お)わらせてから<b>遊(あそ)びなさい</b>。
Finish your homework before you go and play.
</pre>

<pre>
廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)らないように<b>しなさい</b>。

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

Before you start complaining, have a good think about what you did yourself.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The politeness of `～なさい` is a **historical** fact, not a usable one. Because it
descends from the honorific `なさる`, it sounds educated and controlled rather than
crude - but its social direction is fixed downward. A teacher, a parent, an older
sibling or a coach can use it. To a colleague it is patronising; to a customer or a
superior it is unthinkable.

Three settings where it is completely unmarked:

- **Adults to children.** This is its home. 早(はや)く起(お)きなさい,
  ちゃんと挨拶(あいさつ)しなさい.
- **Written instructions to a reader.** Textbook exercises and exam papers use it
  constantly: 正(ただ)しいものを選(えら)びなさい,
  理由(りゆう)を書(か)きなさい. Here it addresses nobody in particular and
  carries no attitude.
- **Set expressions.** お休(やす)みなさい and お帰(かえ)りなさい are frozen
  `～なさい` forms that stopped being commands centuries ago. Nobody hears them as
  orders.

<b>The contracted `～な`.</b> Dropping `さい` gives a much warmer, more casual order:
食(た)べな, 行(い)きな, 見(み)な, もう帰(かえ)りな. It is friendly - 
closer to English "go on, eat up" - and is common from older to younger and among
close friends. It leans masculine in some regions but is by no means exclusively
so. Never use it in writing.

**There is no negative.** ❌食(た)べないなさい does not exist. To forbid
something at the same level of authority you switch construction:
食(た)べてはいけません, 食(た)べないようにしなさい, or - much blunter - the
prohibitive 食(た)べるな.

<b>お～なさい</b> softens it a little further: お入(はい)りなさい,
お食(た)べなさい. This sounds distinctly old-fashioned and grandmotherly in
modern Japanese, and you will meet it more in fiction than in life.

## Contrast

### ～なさい vs 命令形(めいれいけい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same direction, different heat. `～なさい` is the order of
someone with legitimate authority; the 命令形(めいれいけい) is the order of
someone applying force.

</div>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)を<b>片付(かたづ)けなさい</b>。
Tidy your room.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)を<b>片付(かたづ)けろ</b>。
Tidy your damn room.
</pre>

A parent says the first every week and the second when they have snapped. There
is also a difference in who says them: the 命令形(めいれいけい) is strongly
male-coded in conversation, while `～なさい` is used freely by everyone.

Note too that only `～なさい` is at home in print. A worksheet says
計算(けいさん)しなさい; a road sign says 止(と)まれ. Neither would trade
places.

### ～なさい vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` **asks**, and can point in any direction.
`～なさい` **tells**, and only points down.

</div>

<pre>
すみません、こちらに<b>座(すわ)ってください</b>。
Please have a seat over here.
</pre>

<pre>
そこに<b>座(すわ)りなさい</b>。
Sit down there. (teacher to pupil)
</pre>

Learners often assume that because `～なさい` looks longer and comes from an
honorific verb, it must be politer than `～てください`. It is not. `～てください` is
the neutral all-purpose form; `～なさい` is what you use precisely when you are
*not* asking. Said to a guest, 座(すわ)りなさい would be startling - 
お座(すわ)りください is the polite version.

### 食(た)べな vs 食(た)べるな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same `な` after two different stems means opposite things.
<b>ます-stem + な</b> = do it. <b>Dictionary form + な</b> = don't do it.

</div>

<pre>
冷(さ)めないうちに<b>食(た)べな</b>。
Go on, eat it while it's hot.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ<b>食(た)べるな</b>。
Don't eat it yet.
</pre>

The only visible difference is the `る`. The first is `～なさい` with its tail
bitten off and is friendly; the second is the prohibitive and is as blunt as the
命令形(めいれいけい). Intonation helps in speech - the prohibitive `な` is
clipped and stressed - but the stem is the reliable signal.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 来(こ)なさい → ✅ <b>来(き)なさい</b>
  `なさい` attaches to the ます-stem, and the ます-stem of 来(く)る is
  来(き). The `こ` stem belongs to 来(こ)い, 来(こ)られる and
  来(こ)させる.

- ❌ 食(た)べるなさい → ✅ <b>食(た)べなさい</b>
  Never attach `なさい` to the dictionary form. 食(た)べます → 食(た)べ +
  なさい.

- ❌ するなさい → ✅ <b>しなさい</b>
  The ます-stem of する is し.

- ❌ 廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)らないなさい。 → ✅ 廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)っ<b>てはいけません</b>／走(はし)ら<b>ないようにしなさい</b>。
  `～なさい` has no negative form. You have to change construction.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、ここに名前(なまえ)を書(か)きなさい。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、ここにお名前(なまえ)を<b>お書(か)きください</b>。
  Correctly formed and socially impossible. `～なさい` only ever points downward.

## Practice

**1.** 時間(じかん)がありませんよ。早(はや)く準備(じゅんび)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

早(はや)く準備(じゅんび)<b>しなさい</b>。

<code>準備(じゅんび)する</code> is a する-verb, and the ます-stem of する is `し`,
so the form is 準備(じゅんび)しなさい. ❌準備(じゅんび)するなさい applies
`なさい` to the dictionary form, which never happens.

</details>

**2.** （先生(せんせい)が学生(がくせい)に）この問題(もんだい)をもう一度(いちど)（考(かんが)えなさい・考(かんが)えてください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この問題(もんだい)をもう一度(いちど)<b>考(かんが)えなさい</b>。

Teacher to student is exactly the downward relationship `～なさい` is for.
<code>考(かんが)えてください</code> is not wrong, but it frames the instruction
as a request between equals, which is a different classroom.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Get up. You'll be late for school."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>起(お)きなさい</b>。学校(がっこう)に遅(おく)れますよ。

<code>起(お)きる</code> is a る-verb, so drop `る` and add `なさい`. This is the
classic parent-to-child use, and `なさい` is warmer here than
起(お)きろ without being a request.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 危(あぶ)ないから、道(みち)で遊(あそ)ばないなさい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 危(あぶ)ないから、道(みち)で<b>遊(あそ)んではいけません</b>（or 遊(あそ)ば<b>ないようにしなさい</b>）。

`～なさい` has no negative. To forbid something you either use
<code>～てはいけません</code>, or keep `なさい` on a positive verb by routing the
negative through <code>～ないようにする</code>. The bluntest option is the
prohibitive 遊(あそ)ぶな.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう帰(かえ)りなさい。
(b) もう帰(かえ)りな。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an instruction from someone in charge - a parent sending a child home, a
teacher clearing the classroom. It is calm, but it is not negotiable, and the full
`なさい` keeps a certain formality.

(b) is the same order with the ending clipped, and clipping it makes it kind. It
sounds like an older friend saying "go on, head home" - casual, a bit warm, and
completely unusable in writing. Note that neither can be said upwards: even the
friendly (b) assumes you are the senior one.

</details>

# ～ように言う

**Meaning** · tell someone to do something - reporting an instruction without
quoting it

<code>～ように言(い)う</code> passes on the **content** of an order or request. The original
words are gone: you cannot tell from the report whether the speaker barked
帰(かえ)れ or murmured
お帰(かえ)りいただけますか. That flattening is the whole point of the pattern, and
it is why learners meet it most often in the passive - 
言(い)われた, "I was told to".

## Formation

`ように` takes the **plain form** of the verb, positive or negative. Nothing
changes about the verb itself, which is why the four verb classes all behave the
same way here.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + ように + 言(い)う | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つように言(い)う |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + ように + 言(い)う | 片付(かたづ)ける → 片付(かたづ)けるように言(い)う |
| Verb (する) | dictionary form + ように + 言(い)う | 準備(じゅんび)する → 準備(じゅんび)するように言(い)う |
| Verb (来(く)る) | dictionary form + ように + 言(い)う | 来(く)る → 来(く)るように言(い)う |
| Negative order | ない-form + ように + 言(い)う | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れないように言(い)う |
| な-adjective / noun | ～にする + ように | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かにするように言(い)う |
| Asking a favour | ～てくれる／～てもらう + ように | 手伝(てつだ)う → 手伝(てつだ)ってくれるように頼(たの)む |

The verb at the end is not restricted to <code>言(い)う</code>:

| Verb | Sense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 言(い)う | tell | 来(く)るように言(い)う |
| 伝(つた)える | pass on a message | 来(く)るように伝(つた)える |
| 頼(たの)む | ask a favour | 来(く)るように頼(たの)む |
| お願(ねが)いする | request politely | 来(く)るようにお願(ねが)いする |
| 注意(ちゅうい)する | warn | 来(こ)ないように注意(ちゅうい)する |
| 命(めい)じる | order (formal) | 来(く)るように命(めい)じる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the person receiving the instruction is marked with `に`, the
same as the listener of any speech act:
弟(おとうと)<b>に</b>片付(かたづ)けるように言(い)った. In the passive that
person becomes the subject and the speaker moves into `に`:
母(はは)<b>に</b>言(い)われた.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
先生(せんせい)は学生(がくせい)に静(しず)かに<b>するように言(い)った</b>。
The teacher told the students to be quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)に早(はや)く<b>帰(かえ)るように言(い)われた</b>。
My mother told me to come home early.
</pre>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)に、お酒(さけ)を<b>飲(の)まないように言(い)われました</b>。
The doctor told me not to drink.
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)で、ここに名前(なまえ)を<b>書(か)くように言(い)われた</b>。
At reception they told me to write my name here.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に、金曜日(きんようび)までに報告書(ほうこくしょ)を<b>出(だ)すように言(い)われている</b>。
The manager has told me to hand in the report by Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)に部屋(へや)を<b>片付(かたづ)けるように</b>何度(なんど)も<b>言(い)った</b>のに、まだそのままだ。
I've told my little brother to tidy his room over and over, and it's still exactly as it was.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんに、会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)が変(か)わったことをみんなに<b>伝(つた)えるように頼(たの)んだ</b>。
I asked Tanaka to let everyone know that the meeting time had changed.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちには、知(し)らない人(ひと)について<b>行(い)かないようにと注意(ちゅうい)して</b>あります。
We've warned the children not to go off with strangers.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral. It appears in family conversation and in business
email alike, and any politeness sits on the final verb - 
言(い)いました, 伝(つた)えていただけますか, お願(ねが)いしました.

**The lost politeness.** Because `ように` reports content rather than words, the
report is silent about the tone of the original. If that matters, you say so
outside the pattern: 強(つよ)い口調(くちょう)で言(い)われた,
丁寧(ていねい)に頼(たの)まれた. Alternatively, quote directly with `と`.

<b>ようにと.</b> Adding `と` - 来(く)るように<b>と</b>言(い)われた - is very common
and slightly more formal or written. It changes nothing semantically; think of it
as the quotative `と` reasserting itself.

**The passive is the everyday form.** In real speech you will hear
<code>～ように言(い)われた</code> far more than the active. Reporting what you were told to
do is simply a more frequent thing to say than reporting what you told someone
else.

**Business softening.** Upward, <code>言(い)う</code> is too blunt. Use
<code>ようにお願(ねが)いします</code> or <code>ようにお伝(つた)えください</code> - 
山田(やまだ)さんに折(お)り返(かえ)し電話(でんわ)をくださるように
お伝(つた)えください is the sort of sentence this pattern was made for.

<b>Dangling ように.</b> A written instruction sometimes stops after `ように`, with the
verb left off entirely: 受付(うけつけ)にお越(こ)しくださいますように. This is
formal and a bit old-fashioned, but you will see it on notices.

## Contrast

### ～ように言(い)う vs ～と言(い)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` quotes the **words**; `ように` reports the **instruction**.
If you can put quotation marks round it, use `と`.

</div>

<pre>
父(ちち)は「早(はや)く寝(ね)ろ」<b>と言(い)った</b>。
My father said, "Get to bed."
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は早(はや)く寝(ね)る<b>ように言(い)った</b>。
My father told me to go to bed early.
</pre>

The first preserves the imperative and with it the temper. The second could have
been said in any tone at all. Note what happens to the verb: inside a `と` quote
you keep whatever form was actually uttered (寝(ね)ろ, 寝(ね)なさい,
寝(ね)てください); with `ように` the verb goes into the plain dictionary form no
matter what.

`と` can also report a request in indirect speech - 
早(はや)く寝(ね)るように言(い)った and
「早(はや)く寝(ね)なさい」と言(い)った are both natural - but
❌「早(はや)く寝(ね)なさい」ように言(い)った mixes the two and is simply
ungrammatical.

### ～ように頼(たの)む vs ～てくださいと頼(たの)む

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same message, different packaging.
`～てくださいと` reproduces the polite words that were used;
`～ように` summarises what was asked for.

</div>

<pre>
友(とも)だちに空港(くうこう)まで送(おく)っ<b>てくれるように頼(たの)んだ</b>。
I asked my friend to drive me to the airport.
</pre>

<pre>
友(とも)だちに「空港(くうこう)まで送(おく)ってください」<b>と頼(たの)んだ</b>。
I asked my friend, "Please take me to the airport."
</pre>

When the request is a favour rather than an order, the `ように` version usually
keeps a benefactive verb - `～てくれるように`, `～てもらえるように` - because
without it the sentence sounds like an instruction rather than a request. Compare
bare 送(おく)るように頼(たの)んだ, which reads oddly high-handed for
something a friend is doing for you.

### ～ように言(い)う vs ～ようにする・～ようになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same `ように`, three unrelated jobs. <code>言(い)う</code> reports an
instruction, `する` describes an effort you make, `なる` describes a change that
happened.

</div>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)に毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)く<b>ように言(い)われた</b>。
The doctor told me to walk every day.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)く<b>ようにしている</b>。
I make a point of walking every day.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)く<b>ようになった</b>。
I've got into the habit of walking every day.
</pre>

Learners meet all three within a few weeks of each other and fold them together.
Keep the final verb in view: it, not `ように`, is carrying the meaning.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 母(はは)は「早(はや)く帰(かえ)りなさい」ように言(い)った。 → ✅ 母(はは)は早(はや)く<b>帰(かえ)るように言(い)った</b>。
  `ように` never follows a quotation. Either quote with `と` or summarise with
  `ように` - not both.

- ❌ 来(き)てように言(い)う → ✅ <b>来(く)るように言(い)う</b>
  `ように` takes the plain form, not the て-form.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れないで<b>ように</b>言(い)う → ✅ 遅(おく)れ<b>ないように言(い)う</b>
  The negative slot wants the plain ない-form. `～ないで` belongs with
  <code>～ないでください</code>, a different pattern.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)は私(わたし)<b>を</b>発表(はっぴょう)するように言(い)った。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)は私(わたし)<b>に</b>…
  The person told is the addressee of a speech act, so `に`.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>ように</b>言(い)った → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>にするように</b>言(い)った
  `ように` needs a verb in front of it. A な-adjective has to be turned into one
  with `～にする`.

## Practice

**1.** 受付(うけつけ)の人(ひと)に、ここで待(ま)____ように言(い)われた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ここで待(ま)<b>つ</b>ように言(い)われた。

`ように` takes the plain dictionary form, so <code>待(ま)つ</code> goes in
unchanged. The receptionist is the source of the instruction and takes `に` in the
passive.

</details>

**2.** 田中(たなか)さんは「明日(あした)は早(はや)く来(き)てください」（と・ように）言(い)いました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「明日(あした)は早(はや)く来(き)てください」<b>と</b>言(い)いました。

Once the words are inside quotation marks, only the quotative `と` can follow. The
`ように` version would drop the quotes and the politeness:
明日(あした)は早(はや)く来(く)るように言(い)いました.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The doctor told me not to eat anything before the
examination."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

医者(いしゃ)に、検査(けんさ)の前(まえ)は何(なに)も<b>食(た)べないように言(い)われた</b>。

A negative instruction goes in as the plain ない-form,
<code>食(た)べない</code>, directly before `ように`. The passive
言(い)われた is the natural way to report an instruction you received.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)に、この書類(しょるい)をコピーしてように頼(たの)まれました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に、この書類(しょるい)を<b>コピーするように</b>頼(たの)まれました。

The て-form cannot sit in front of `ように`; the slot takes the plain form, here
<code>コピーする</code>. If you wanted to keep the て-form you would have to quote:
「コピーしてください」と頼(たの)まれました.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 先生(せんせい)は毎日(まいにち)日記(にっき)を書(か)くように言(い)った。
(b) 先生(せんせい)は「毎日(まいにち)日記(にっき)を書(か)きなさい」と言(い)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports only the substance of the instruction. It tells you what the students
now have to do and says nothing about how the teacher put it - she may have asked
politely or issued an order.

(b) reproduces the teacher's actual words, and those words carry information: the
`なさい` marks her as speaking downward with authority, calmly rather than angrily.
Direct quotation is how Japanese keeps that texture, since `ように` deliberately
throws it away.

</details>
