# N4 - How things look, and what you hear


---

# ～ようだ

**Meaning** · it seems that; it appears that - a conclusion the speaker has reached themselves

`～ようだ` reports an **inference**. The speaker has some evidence - something seen,
heard, felt, or worked out - and offers a careful conclusion drawn from it. The
judgement belongs to the speaker, which is exactly what separates it from
`らしい`, where the judgement belongs to somebody else.

## Formation

`よう` is a noun meaning "appearance, manner", so everything attaches to it the way
things attach to any noun you learned to modify at N5: verbs and い-adjectives in
plain form, な-adjectives with `な`, nouns with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ようだ | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るようだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ようだ | 降(ふ)った → 降(ふ)ったようだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ようだ | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないようだ |
| Verb (ている) | ている + ようだ | 寝(ね)ている → 寝(ね)ているようだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + ようだ | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいようだ |
| な-adjective | ～な + ようだ | 大変(たいへん)だ → 大変(たいへん)なようだ |
| Noun | Noun + の + ようだ | 事故(じこ) → 事故(じこ)のようだ |
| Polite | ようだ → ようです | 降(ふ)るようです |
| Past | ようだ → ようだった／ようでした | 降(ふ)ったようでした |
| Before a verb | ようだ → ように | 降(ふ)るように見(み)える |
| Before a noun | ようだ → ような | 夢(ゆめ)のような話(はなし) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` inflects exactly like a な-adjective: よう**だ**, よう**な**,
よう**に**, よう**で**, よう**でした**. And nothing in front of it ever keeps its `だ`:
❌<code>事故(じこ)だようだ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
外(そと)は寒(さむ)い<b>ようです</b>。
It seems to be cold outside.
</pre>

<pre>
だれか<b>来(き)たようだ</b>。
Looks like somebody's arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は中村(なかむら)さん<b>のようです</b>。
This dictionary appears to be Nakamura's.
</pre>

<pre>
六月(ろくがつ)なのに、今日(きょう)は夏(なつ)<b>のようだ</b>。
It's only June, but today feels like summer.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)がある<b>ようなので</b>、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)ります。
I seem to have a temperature, so I'm going home early today.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)がぬれているから、夜(よる)のうちに雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)ったようだ</b>。
The road is wet, so it must have rained during the night.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の話(はな)し方(かた)を聞(き)くと、この町(まち)に長(なが)く住(す)んでいる<b>ようですね</b>。
From the way he talks, it sounds like he's lived in this town a long time.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は今(いま)会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)の<b>ようですので</b>、あとでもう一度(いちど)おかけします。
The section chief seems to be in a meeting just now, so I'll call again later.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ようです` is the neutral polite form and is safe everywhere - with strangers, with
customers, in a report. Plain `ようだ` is the written form and the one you meet in
newspapers and essays; spoken out loud between friends it sounds a little
buttoned-up, and casual speech reaches for `みたい` instead. Nothing about `ようだ`
is male- or female-marked.

The adverb `どうやら` is its natural partner and makes the hedge audible:
どうやら道(みち)を間違(まちが)えたようだ。 A speaker who wants to sound less
sure adds `かもしれない`; one who wants to sound sure drops the hedge entirely.

`ようだ` has a second job that has nothing to do with guessing: it is also the
**politeness** device you use to say an awkward thing without saying it flatly.
私(わたし)にはできないようです is softer than 私(わたし)にはできません,
because it presents your refusal as an observation rather than a decision. In
business Japanese そのようです ("that does appear to be the case") is the standard
way to confirm something you were not personally present for.

Finally, `ようだ` is fine about **your own body**, because the evidence there is
still indirect: 風邪(かぜ)をひいたようだ - you feel bad, you have not seen a
doctor. It is not fine about facts you simply know.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing, before
reasoning starts. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis altogether - it is a plain relay
of somebody's words.

</div>

### ～ようだ vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` is *my* conclusion from *my* evidence. `らしい` is
somebody else's information, which I am passing on and not vouching for.

</div>

<pre>
山本(やまもと)さんは会社(かいしゃ)をやめた<b>ようだ</b>。
It looks like Yamamoto has quit - his desk is cleared and nobody's seen him.
</pre>

<pre>
山本(やまもと)さんは会社(かいしゃ)をやめた<b>らしい</b>。
Apparently Yamamoto has quit - that's what people are saying.
</pre>

Because `ようだ` commits you to the reasoning, it is the wrong choice when you name
an outside source out loud. A sentence that opens with <code>ニュースによると</code>
wants `そうだ` or `らしい` to finish it.

### ～ようだ vs ～みたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register, different attachment.
`ようだ` is written and formal; `みたい` is spoken and casual. Nouns take `の` before
`ようだ` and **nothing** before `みたい`.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)の彼(かれ)は子(こ)ども<b>のようだ</b>。
He's behaving like a child today.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)の彼(かれ)、子(こ)ども<b>みたいだ</b>ね。
He's acting like such a kid today, isn't he.
</pre>

The second is what you actually say to a friend. Putting `みたい` into a written
report, or `ようだ` into a chat about a coworker, is not an error of grammar - it is
an error of tone, and it is heard as one.

### ～ようだ vs the two ～そうだ

The word `そうだ` covers **two unrelated grammars**, and they attach differently.
Line all three up on one adjective and the difference is impossible to miss.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain form + `そうだ` = "I hear that". Adjective **stem** +
`そうだ` = "looks". Plain form + `ようだ` = "seems, judging by the evidence".

</div>

<pre>
このケーキは甘(あま)い<b>そうだ</b>。
I hear this cake is sweet. (someone told me; I haven't tried it)
</pre>

<pre>
このケーキは<b>甘(あま)そうだ</b>。
This cake looks sweet. (I'm looking straight at it)
</pre>

<pre>
このケーキは甘(あま)い<b>ようだ</b>。
This cake seems to be sweet. (everyone is leaving half of it, so I conclude…)
</pre>

Note the shape: <code>甘(あま)い</code> keeps its `い` before hearsay `そうだ` and before
`ようだ`, and **loses** it before appearance `そうだ`. That single `い` is the whole
difference between reporting a rumour and describing a photograph.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 病気(びょうき)**だ**ようです → ✅ 病気(びょうき)**の**ようです
  A noun connects to `よう` with `の`, never with `だ`. The same rule you learned
  for noun-modifying clauses at N5 applies here.

- ❌ 静(しず)か**ようだ** → ✅ 静(しず)か**なようだ**
  A な-adjective keeps its `な` in front of `よう`, because `よう` is a noun.

- ❌ 降(ふ)るよう**だです** → ✅ 降(ふ)る**ようです**
  `ようだ` and `ようです` are one form at two politeness levels. You never stack them.

- ❌ 私(わたし)はきのう京都(きょうと)へ行(い)った**ようです**。 → ✅ 私(わたし)はきのう京都(きょうと)へ行(い)き**ました**。
  You were there. `ようだ` needs indirect evidence; using it about your own known
  actions sounds as though you have lost your memory.

## Practice

**1.** 受付(うけつけ)にだれもいない。今日(きょう)は休(やす)み____ようだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は休(やす)み**の**ようだ。

<code>休(やす)み</code> is a noun, and nouns attach to `よう` with `の`. Only な-adjectives
take `な`; verbs and い-adjectives attach in plain form with nothing added.

</details>

**2.** ニュースによると、来週(らいしゅう)は寒(さむ)く（なるようだ・なるそうだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来週(らいしゅう)は寒(さむ)く<b>なるそうだ</b>。

The sentence names its source out loud, so it is a relay, not a deduction. Once you
have said <code>ニュースによると</code>, the evidence is other people's, and hearsay
`そうだ` (or `らしい`) is what finishes the sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It seems the train has stopped."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)が<b>止(と)まっているようです</b>。

Use `ている` for the resulting state - the train stopped and is still stopped - then
attach `ようです` to that plain form. Nothing goes between the verb and `よう`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は元気(げんき)ようですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は元気(げんき)**な**ようですね。

<code>元気(げんき)</code> is a な-adjective, so it needs `な` to modify the noun `よう`.
Dropping it is the commonest slip learners make with this pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)はもう帰(かえ)った**ようだ**。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)はもう帰(かえ)った**らしい**。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In (a) the speaker worked it out: her coat is gone, the light is off, so the
conclusion is theirs and they would defend it. In (b) the speaker heard it from
somebody - a colleague mentioned it - and is repeating the information at a slight
distance, without taking responsibility for whether it is true.

</details>

# ～ように・～ような

**Meaning** · as; like; in the way that - the adverbial and adnominal faces of ようだ

`ようだ` is a な-adjective, so it has the two forms every な-adjective has. `～ように`
goes in front of a **verb or adjective** and says the action happens *in that
manner*. `～ような` goes in front of a **noun** and says the thing is *of that kind*.
This page is about attaching them to a clause; attaching them to a bare noun takes
`の` first, and that is the sibling pattern <code>のように／のような</code>.

## Formation

Attachment is identical to `ようだ` - only the ending changes according to what
comes next.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ように／ような | 言(い)った → 言(い)ったように |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ように／ような | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないような |
| Verb (ている) | ている + ように／ような | 動(うご)いている → 動(うご)いているように |
| い-adjective | plain form + ように／ような | 冷(つめ)たい → 冷(つめ)たいような |
| な-adjective | ～な + ように／ような | 好(す)きだ → 好(す)きなように |
| Noun | Noun + の + ように／ような | 夢(ゆめ) → 夢(ゆめ)のような |
| Before a verb / adjective | ように | ように見(み)える |
| Before a noun | ような | ような話(はなし) |
| End of sentence | ようだ／ようです | 動(うご)いているようです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at the **next** word, not the previous one. Noun next →
`ような`. Anything else next → `ように`. It is the same choice you already make
between <code>静(しず)かな部屋(へや)</code> and <code>静(しず)かに話(はな)す</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)が書(か)いた<b>ように</b>読(よ)んでください。
Please read it the way I wrote it.
</pre>

<pre>
思(おも)っていた<b>ような</b>店(みせ)ではなかった。
It wasn't the sort of shop I'd been picturing.
</pre>

<pre>
雲(くも)が動(うご)いている<b>ように</b>見(み)える。
The clouds look as though they're moving.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)が言(い)った<b>ように</b>、この漢字(かんじ)には読(よ)み方(かた)が二(ふた)つある。
As the teacher said, this kanji has two readings.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもでも分(わ)かる<b>ような</b>説明(せつめい)をしてください。
Please give an explanation that even a child could follow.
</pre>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)で見(み)た<b>ような</b>きれいな海(うみ)だった。
It was a beautiful stretch of sea, just like the one in the photo.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんがやっている<b>ように</b>やれば、きっとうまくいきますよ。
If you do it the way Yamada does, I'm sure it'll go fine.
</pre>

<pre>
思(おも)った<b>ように</b>は進(すす)まなかったが、なんとか時間(じかん)までに終(お)わらせた。
It didn't go the way I'd expected, but I got it finished in time somehow.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ように` and `ような` are register-neutral and belong equally to speech and writing.
Their casual twins are `みたいに` and `みたいな`, which say the same thing in a
looser voice; in a report or a presentation you want the `よう` forms.

Several fixed phrases are worth learning whole, because they open sentences in
written and semi-formal Japanese:

- ご存(ぞん)じのように - "as you know"
- 次(つぎ)のように - "as follows"
- 上(うえ)で述(の)べたように - "as stated above"

<div class="note">

**Note** - `ように` has a second, unrelated job: **purpose**. After a verb that is
not under your direct control - a potential form, a negative, or an intransitive - 
`ように` means "so that": 忘(わす)れないようにメモしておく,
字(じ)が見(み)えるように前(まえ)に座(すわ)る. That `ように` is not making a
comparison at all, and it is the root of the separate patterns
<code>ようにする</code>, <code>ようになる</code> and <code>ように言(い)う</code>.
Tell them apart by asking whether the clause describes *how* something is done
(comparison) or *why* (purpose).

</div>

The purpose sense also gives Japanese its wish formula, left hanging with no main
clause: 合格(ごうかく)できますように。 - "May I pass." You see it written on
絵馬(えま) at shrines and hear it muttered before exams.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - it is a plain relay of somebody's words. `ように` and
`ような` inherit their position from `ようだ`.

</div>

### ～ように vs ～ような

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ような` is an adjective and must be followed by a noun.
`ように` is an adverb and must be followed by a verb or an adjective. Nothing else
distinguishes them.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)が作(つく)る<b>ような</b>カレーが食(た)べたい。
I want to eat curry like the kind my mother makes.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)が作(つく)る<b>ように</b>カレーを作(つく)ってみた。
I tried making curry the way my mother makes it.
</pre>

The first compares two curries; the second compares two methods. Swap the endings
and both sentences break - <code>ような</code> with a verb after it and
<code>ように</code> with a noun after it are simply ungrammatical, not just odd.

### ～ように vs ～みたいに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different level. `ように` is neutral to
formal; `みたいに` is casual and slightly more emphatic. And the attachment to a
noun differs: `Noun + のように` but `Noun + みたいに`.

</div>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)に書(か)いてある<b>ように</b>組(く)み立(た)ててください。
Please assemble it as described in the instructions.
</pre>

<pre>
さっき言(い)った<b>みたいに</b>やってみてよ。
Try doing it like I said a minute ago.
</pre>

The first belongs in a manual; the second belongs in a kitchen. Say the first to a
friend and you sound like a manual.

### comparison ～ように vs purpose ～ように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can replace it with "in the same way as", it is
comparison. If you can replace it with "so that", it is purpose. Purpose `ように`
almost always follows a potential verb, a negative, or an intransitive one.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が走(はし)る<b>ように</b>走(はし)ってみたが、全然(ぜんぜん)速(はや)くならなかった。
I tried running the way he runs, but I didn't get any faster at all.
</pre>

<pre>
後(うし)ろの人(ひと)にも聞(き)こえる<b>ように</b>、大(おお)きな声(こえ)で話(はな)した。
I spoke loudly so that the people at the back could hear too.
</pre>

Same three syllables, two different grammars. In the first, `ように` answers *how*;
in the second, it answers *what for*.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 言(い)った**ような**やってください → ✅ 言(い)った**ように**やってください
  `ような` may only be followed by a noun. In front of a verb it has to be `ように`.

- ❌ 子(こ)ども**ような**字(じ) → ✅ 子(こ)ども**のような**字(じ)
  A bare noun needs `の` before `よう`. Only clauses attach directly.

- ❌ 好(す)き**ように**やっていいよ → ✅ 好(す)き**なように**やっていいよ
  な-adjectives keep their `な` in front of `よう`, because `よう` is a noun.

- ❌ 夢(ゆめ)の**ようだ**話(はなし) → ✅ 夢(ゆめ)の**ような**話(はなし)
  `ようだ` ends a sentence. To put it in front of a noun you need the `な` form,
  the same way <code>元気(げんき)だ</code> becomes <code>元気(げんき)な</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 矢印(やじるし)が示(しめ)している____進(すす)んでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

矢印(やじるし)が示(しめ)している<b>ように</b>進(すす)んでください。

The next word is a verb, so the adverbial form `ように` is required. "Proceed in the
manner the arrow indicates" - this is comparison of manner, not purpose.

</details>

**2.** 田中(たなか)さんが作(つく)った（ように・ような）レポートを書(か)きたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

田中(たなか)さんが作(つく)った<b>ような</b>レポートを書(か)きたい。

<code>レポート</code> is a noun, so the adnominal `ような` is the only option. The
sentence compares two reports; if you wanted to compare two ways of writing, you
would say <code>作(つく)ったように書(か)きたい</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It wasn't as difficult as I had thought."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

思(おも)っていた<b>ように</b>は難(むずか)しくなかった。

Adding `は` to `ように` is what produces the "not as … as" reading, because the `は`
marks a contrast: difficult in the way I imagined, no - difficult in some other
way, possibly. Without `は` the sentence sounds oddly flat.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 地図(ちず)で見(み)たように建物(たてもの)はここにはなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 地図(ちず)で見(み)た**ような**建物(たてもの)はここにはなかった。

The word after the pattern is <code>建物(たてもの)</code>, a noun, so it must be
`ような`. With `ように` the sentence would try to say "the building wasn't here in the
manner I saw on the map", which means nothing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 外国人(がいこくじん)にも**わかるように**、ゆっくり話(はな)した。
(b) 外国人(がいこくじん)が<b>話(はな)すように</b>、ゆっくり話(はな)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is purpose: the slow speech was aimed at making comprehension possible, so
`ように` means "so that". It sits on a potential verb, which is the usual signal.
(b) is comparison: it says the speaker imitated the manner of a foreigner speaking,
which is a strange thing to claim - and that strangeness is exactly how you feel
the two uses pulling apart.

</details>

# ～のように・～のような

**Meaning** · like; similar to; such as - comparing something to a **noun**

When the thing you are comparing to is a noun rather than a clause, `よう` needs
`の` to hold on to it. `Noun + のように` goes in front of a verb or adjective;
`Noun + のような` goes in front of another noun. The same pair has a second job that
looks unrelated but is not: introducing an **example** - "fruit such as apples".

## Formation

The `の` is not optional and it is not the possessive `の` you learned at N5 - it is
the connector that lets a noun modify another noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, before a verb or adjective | Noun + のように | 雪(ゆき) → 雪(ゆき)のように白(しろ)い |
| Noun, before a noun | Noun + のような + Noun | 夢(ゆめ) → 夢(ゆめ)のような話(はなし) |
| Noun, at the end of a sentence | Noun + のようだ／のようです | 夢(ゆめ)のようです |
| Giving an example | Noun や Noun + のような + Noun | りんごやみかんのような果物(くだもの) |
| この・その・あの | drop the の | そのような／そのように |
| Casual equivalent | Noun + みたいな／みたいに | 夢(ゆめ)みたいな話(はなし) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` before `よう`, nothing before `みたい`. And with
`この・その・あの` the `の` is already inside the word, so you write
<code>そのような</code>, never ❌<code>そののような</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雪(ゆき)<b>のように</b>白(しろ)い。
As white as snow.
</pre>

<pre>
人形(にんぎょう)<b>のような</b>顔(かお)ですね。
She has a face like a doll's, doesn't she.
</pre>

<pre>
鳥(とり)<b>のように</b>空(そら)を飛(と)んでみたい。
I'd love to fly through the sky like a bird.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に仕事(しごと)が山(やま)<b>のように</b>ある。
There's a mountain of work on my desk.
</pre>

<pre>
みかんやバナナ<b>のような</b>果物(くだもの)が好(す)きです。
I like fruit such as mandarins and bananas.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は毎日(まいにち)<b>のように</b>この店(みせ)に通(かよ)っている。
He turns up at this shop practically every day.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の痛(いた)みが、うそ<b>のように</b>なくなった。
Yesterday's pain vanished as if it had never been there.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて会(あ)ったのに、昔(むかし)からの友(とも)だち<b>のような</b>気(き)がした。
Even though we'd only just met, I felt as if we'd been friends for years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`のように` and `のような` are neutral: fine in an essay, fine in conversation, not
marked for age or gender. In relaxed speech `みたいに` and `みたいな` are commoner,
and they are what you will hear far more often between friends. Going the other
direction, formal writing has its own set-piece openers:
ご覧(らん)のように - "as you can see", 以下(いか)のように - "as below".

Two families of fixed expressions are worth memorising rather than analysing:

- **Exaggerated quantity or manner** - 山(やま)のように (heaps of),
  飛(と)ぶように売(う)れる (selling like hot cakes), うそのように (unbelievably).
- **"Practically every…"** - 毎日(まいにち)のように,
  毎週(まいしゅう)のように. These do not mean literally every day; they mean often
  enough that it feels like it.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>のように</code> compares; <code>と同(おな)じ</code>, which you met at
N5, equates. 兄(あに)のように背(せ)が高(たか)い says you are tall in the way
your brother is tall; 兄(あに)と同(おな)じくらい背(せ)が高(たか)い says the
two heights match. If a measurement is involved, you probably want
<code>同(おな)じ</code>.

</div>

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words. `のように` and
`のような` are the noun-comparing corner of the `ようだ` family.

</div>

### ～のように／のような vs ～みたいに／みたいな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning; `よう` needs `の` and `みたい` does not. `よう` is
neutral to formal, `みたい` is casual.

</div>

<pre>
父(ちち)<b>のような</b>技術者(ぎじゅつしゃ)になりたいと思(おも)っています。
I hope to become an engineer like my father.
</pre>

<pre>
おれもお父(とう)さん<b>みたいな</b>エンジニアになりたいな。
I wanna be an engineer like my dad too.
</pre>

Both sentences say the same thing about the same ambition. The first would go in a
university application; the second is a fifteen-year-old talking. Notice that the
`の` disappears the moment you switch to `みたい` - that is the mechanical half of
the difference, and it is where learners slip.

### Noun + のような vs Noun + らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `Xのような` means *resembling X, and probably not X*. `Xらしい`
means *a proper X, behaving as an X should*. They are close to opposites.

</div>

<pre>
男(おとこ)<b>のような</b>人(ひと)が立(た)っていた。
Someone who looked like a man was standing there. (I couldn't tell)
</pre>

<pre>
男(おとこ)<b>らしい</b>人(ひと)だと言(い)われている。
People say he's a very manly sort.
</pre>

The first says the resemblance is all you have; the second says the category fits
so well it is worth remarking on. Use `のような` about someone you are unsure of and
you are being cautious; use it about someone whose gender is obvious and you have
said something strange.

### ～のような "resembling" vs ～のような "such as"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if what precedes `のような` is a **member** of the noun that
follows, it is "such as". If it is outside that category, it is a comparison.

</div>

<pre>
バラ<b>のような</b>花(はな)を買(か)ってきてください。
Please buy some flowers - roses, say. (roses are flowers → "such as")
</pre>

<pre>
火(ひ)<b>のような</b>花(はな)が咲(さ)いていた。
Flowers like flames were in bloom. (fire is not a flower → comparison)
</pre>

Japanese does not mark the difference; the listener works it out from whether the
category contains the example. English does mark it, which is why translating in
either direction here needs a moment's thought.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雪(ゆき)**ように**白(しろ)い → ✅ 雪(ゆき)**のように**白(しろ)い
  A bare noun cannot attach to `よう` directly. Only a clause can.

- ❌ **そののような**話(はなし) → ✅ **そのような**話(はなし)
  `その` already ends in `の`. Adding another is the commonest slip in this whole
  pattern.

- ❌ 子(こ)どもの**ように**字(じ) → ✅ 子(こ)どもの**ような**字(じ)
  A noun follows, so the form must be `のような`. `のように` only goes in front of
  verbs and adjectives.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は男(おとこ)の**ような**人(ひと)だ。(meaning "he's manly") → ✅ 彼(かれ)は男(おとこ)**らしい**人(ひと)だ。
  `のような` distances the noun from the category. To praise someone for fitting the
  category, you need `らしい`.

## Practice

**1.** この部屋(へや)は冬(ふゆ)____さむいですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この部屋(へや)は冬(ふゆ)<b>のように</b>さむいですね。

<code>冬(ふゆ)</code> is a noun, so it needs `の` before `よう`, and the word that
follows is an adjective, so the adverbial `ように` is required rather than `ような`.

</details>

**2.** 彼女(かのじょ)はモデル（のような・みたいな）人(ひと)です。Which fits a job interview?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)はモデル<b>のような</b>人(ひと)です。

Both are grammatical and mean the same thing, but `みたいな` is casual and would
sound too loose in an interview. Note also that `みたい` takes no `の` - writing
❌<code>モデルのみたいな</code> mixes the two patterns.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I want to live in a quiet town like Kyoto."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

京都(きょうと)<b>のような</b>静(しず)かな町(まち)に住(す)みたいです。

Here Kyoto genuinely is a quiet town, so this is the "such as" reading: Kyoto is
offered as an example of the category. The pattern goes in front of the whole noun
phrase, adjective included.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あのような人(ひと)のように服(ふく)を着(き)たい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの人(ひと)<b>のような</b>服(ふく)を着(き)たい。

The original stacks the pattern twice. Decide what you are comparing: if it is the
clothes, the noun after the pattern is <code>服(ふく)</code>, so you want
<code>あの人(ひと)のような服(ふく)</code> with a single `のような`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 先生(せんせい)**のような**人(ひと)になりたい。
(b) 先生(せんせい)**らしい**人(ひと)になりたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) points at one particular teacher you admire and says you want to resemble that
person. (b) says you want to be the sort of person who is recognisably a teacher - 
to fit the role well. The first is about a model you have in mind; the second is
about living up to a category, and it would only make sense if you are a teacher
already or about to be one.

</details>

# ～みたい

**Meaning** · seems like; looks like; is like - the everyday spoken form of ようだ

`～みたい` says exactly what `ようだ` says: the speaker has drawn a conclusion from
evidence, or is comparing one thing to another. What changes is the register - this
is the form you actually hear in conversation - and the attachment, which is
simpler: `みたい` never wants the `の` or the `な` that `よう` insists on.

## Formation

Everything goes in front of `みたい` in its **bare** form. That is the whole rule.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + みたい | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るみたい |
| Verb (past) | た-form + みたい | 来(き)た → 来(き)たみたい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + みたい | 分(わ)からない → 分(わ)からないみたい |
| い-adjective | plain form + みたい | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いみたい |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + みたい | 元気(げんき)だ → 元気(げんき)みたい |
| Noun | Noun + みたい (no の) | 風邪(かぜ) → 風邪(かぜ)みたい |
| Polite | みたい + です | 降(ふ)るみたいです |
| Past | みたいだった／みたいでした | 風邪(かぜ)みたいでした |
| Before a noun | みたい + な | 夢(ゆめ)みたいな話(はなし) |
| Before a verb / adjective | みたい + に | 雪(ゆき)みたいに白(しろ)い |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `みたい` behaves like a な-adjective *after* it attaches
(みたい**だ**／みたい**な**／みたい**に**), but takes nothing *before* it. Compare
<code>子(こ)どものようだ</code> with <code>子(こ)どもみたいだ</code>: the `の`
belongs to `よう` only.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - this `みたい` is **always written in kana**. <code>見(み)たい</code> with
the kanji is a different word entirely - the ます-stem of <code>見(み)る</code> plus
`たい`, "want to see". <code>見(み)たい映画(えいが)</code> is a film you want to
watch; <code>映画(えいが)みたい</code> is "it's like a film". The two are commonly
explained as related - `みたい` is usually traced back to a contraction of
<code>見(み)たよう</code> - but in modern Japanese they are separate words, and
writing this one with kanji is simply an error.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
外(そと)、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってる<b>みたい</b>だよ。
It looks like it's raining out there.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)、先生(せんせい)<b>みたい</b>だね。
That person seems to be a teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)くんは今日(きょう)休(やす)み<b>みたいです</b>。
Tanaka seems to be off today.
</pre>

<pre>
家(いえ)のかぎをかけ忘(わす)れた<b>みたい</b>。
I think I forgot to lock up the house.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)は夜(よる)になると静(しず)か<b>みたい</b>だ。
This neighbourhood seems to go quiet once night falls.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はゲームをしていて、こっちの話(はなし)が聞(き)こえていない<b>みたい</b>だ。
My brother's playing a game and doesn't seem to hear a word I'm saying.
</pre>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんは新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)が楽(たの)しい<b>みたいです</b>ね。
Hayashi seems to be enjoying the new job.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)電話(でんわ)してもつながらないから、まだ会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わっていない<b>みたい</b>だ。
I can't get through however many times I call, so the meeting apparently isn't over yet.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`みたい` is **casual**. Between friends, family and close colleagues it is the
default; `ようだ` in the same conversation sounds like reading from a memo. Adding
`です` - みたいです - raises it to a polite-but-relaxed level that is perfectly
normal at work in speech, but it never becomes *formal*. In a written report, a
notice, a news article or a speech, switch to `ようです`.

Sentence-final `みたい` with **nothing after it** is extremely common in speech:
「終(お)わったみたい」「留守(るす)みたい」. The `だ` is optional, and dropping it
softens the sentence; keeping the `だ` makes it flatter and blunter, a tendency
associated more with male speech, though it is not a rule and plenty of speakers of
any gender use both.

`みたい` also does emotional work that `ようだ` is too stiff for. 「うそみたい!」 is
"I can't believe it!", not a claim about lying. 「夢(ゆめ)みたい」 is what you say
when something wonderful happens. These are frozen exclamations; learn them whole.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words.

</div>

### ～みたい vs ～ようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. `みたい` for speech, `ようだ` for writing and
formal speech. Attachment: `Noun + みたい` but `Noun + のようだ`; `な-adj + みたい`
but `な-adj + なようだ`.

</div>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さん、もう帰(かえ)っちゃった<b>みたい</b>。
Looks like Yamada's already gone home.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)は既(すで)に退社(たいしゃ)した<b>ようです</b>。
It appears that Yamada has already left for the day.
</pre>

Same fact, two worlds. The second is what you would write in a message to a client;
say it to a friend and you sound like an answering machine.

### ～みたい vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are common in casual speech, but they point in opposite
directions. `みたい` says *I* worked it out. `らしい` says *somebody told me* - and
quietly declines responsibility for it.

</div>

<pre>
加藤(かとう)さん、引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>みたい</b>だよ。箱(はこ)がたくさんあった。
Kato seems to be moving - there were boxes everywhere.
</pre>

<pre>
加藤(かとう)さん、引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>らしい</b>よ。課長(かちょう)が言(い)ってた。
Kato's apparently moving - the section chief said so.
</pre>

The second half of each sentence is doing the diagnostic work. `みたい` invites
"what makes you think so?"; `らしい` invites "who told you?".

### ～みたい vs appearance ～そう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そう` reports the **surface**: what the thing looks like right
now, with no reasoning. `みたい` reports a **conclusion**, however small the leap.
And they attach differently: <code>おいしいみたい</code> keeps the `い`;
<code>おいしそう</code> drops it.

</div>

<pre>
このスープ、<b>おいしそう</b>。
This soup looks delicious. (I'm looking at it; I haven't tasted it)
</pre>

<pre>
このスープ、<b>おいしいみたい</b>。
This soup is apparently good. (people are going back for more, so I conclude…)
</pre>

Say <b>おいしそう</b> in front of a bowl you are about to eat. Say
<b>おいしいみたい</b> about a bowl in front of somebody else. Getting this backwards
is the single commonest error in this corner of the grammar.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)**のみたい**です → ✅ 学生(がくせい)**みたい**です
  The `の` belongs to `よう`. `みたい` attaches to a bare noun.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)**なみたい** → ✅ 元気(げんき)**みたい**
  な-adjectives drop the `な` in front of `みたい`, unlike in front of `よう`.

- ❌ この映画(えいが)、面白(おもしろ)い見(み)たい。 → ✅ この映画(えいが)、面白(おもしろ)い**みたい**。
  Never write this pattern with kanji. <code>見(み)たい</code> means "want to see"
  and turns the sentence into nonsense.

- ❌ (in a written notice) 会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になった**みたいです**。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になった**ようです**。
  Grammatical but out of register. Written announcements do not use `みたい`.

## Practice

**1.** 部屋(へや)の電気(でんき)が消(き)えている。だれもいない____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

だれもいない<b>みたい</b>（だ）。

The verb goes in front of `みたい` in plain form with nothing added, and the
final `だ` is optional in speech. The speaker is reasoning from the dark room, which
is what makes this `みたい` rather than `らしい`.

</details>

**2.** となりの部屋(へや)から音(おと)がする。だれか（いるみたい・いるらしい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

だれか<b>いるみたい</b>。

You are hearing the noise yourself, so the evidence is first-hand and the
conclusion is yours. `らしい` would suggest somebody had told you there was a person
next door, which is not the situation the sentence describes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "This bag seems to be Suzuki's."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このかばん、鈴木(すずき)さん<b>みたい</b>だよ。

A bare noun takes `みたい` with nothing between them. The formal equivalent would be
<code>鈴木(すずき)さんのようです</code>, where the `の` is compulsory.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は料理(りょうり)が上手(じょうず)なみたいですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は料理(りょうり)が上手(じょうず)**みたい**ですね。

<code>上手(じょうず)</code> is a な-adjective, and な-adjectives shed their `な` before
`みたい`. The version with `な` is the one you would use before `よう`:
<code>上手(じょうず)なようですね</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) あの子(こ)、**さむそう**。
(b) あの子(こ)、**さむいみたい**。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a reading of the surface: the child is hunched up and shivering, and you say
so on sight, with no reasoning in between. (b) is a small deduction - perhaps the
child asked for a blanket, or keeps going back indoors - so you are concluding
rather than describing. Both are natural; (a) is what you say the instant you look
over.

</details>

# ～みたいに・～みたいな

**Meaning** · like; similar to - the adverbial and adnominal forms of みたい

`みたい` inflects like a な-adjective, so it has the same two faces as `ようだ`.
`～みたいに` goes in front of a **verb or adjective**: the action happens in that
manner. `～みたいな` goes in front of a **noun**: the thing is of that kind. These
are the casual counterparts of `のように` and `のような`, and the whole difference in
form is that no `の` appears anywhere.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, before a verb or adjective | Noun + みたいに | 春(はる) → 春(はる)みたいにあたたかい |
| Noun, before a noun | Noun + みたいな + Noun | 夢(ゆめ) → 夢(ゆめ)みたいな話(はなし) |
| Verb clause | plain form + みたいに／みたいな | 言(い)った → 言(い)ったみたいに |
| い-adjective | plain form + みたいに／みたいな | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いみたいな |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + みたいに／みたいな | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かみたいに |
| Before a verb / adjective | みたい + に | みたいに笑(わら)う |
| Before a noun | みたい + な | みたいな人(ひと) |
| Formal equivalent | のように／のような (with の) | 夢(ゆめ)のような話(はなし) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - choose by what comes **next**. Noun next → `みたいな`. Anything
else → `みたいに`. And never insert `の` or `な` before `みたい`, however strongly the
`よう` patterns have trained you to.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the casual counterparts of <code>このような・そのような・あのような</code>
are not ❌<code>これみたいな</code> and friends but the ordinary demonstratives
`こんな`, `そんな`, `あんな`, and for the adverbial, `こんなに`, `そんなに`,
`あんなに`. そんな話(はなし)は聞(き)いたことがない is what a speaker actually
says.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
猫(ねこ)<b>みたいに</b>静(しず)かに歩(ある)いてね。
Walk quietly, like a cat.
</pre>

<pre>
お父(とう)さん<b>みたいな</b>人(ひと)と結婚(けっこん)したい。
I want to marry someone like my dad.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は春(はる)<b>みたいに</b>あたたかいね。
It's as warm as spring today, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
水(みず)<b>みたいに</b>うすいコーヒーだった。
The coffee was as weak as water.
</pre>

<pre>
さっき説明(せつめい)した<b>みたいに</b>、ボタンを二回(にかい)押(お)してください。
Press the button twice, the way I explained a moment ago.
</pre>

<pre>
夢(ゆめ)を見(み)ている<b>みたいな</b>一日(いちにち)だった。
It was the kind of day that felt like a dream.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は子(こ)ども<b>みたいに</b>大声(おおごえ)で笑(わら)っていた。
He was laughing at the top of his voice like a child.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)していれば、兄(あに)<b>みたいに</b>速(はや)く泳(およ)げるようになるよ。
If you practise every day, you'll end up swimming as fast as my brother.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

These are casual forms and they carry that everywhere they go. In a school essay,
a company email or a formal presentation, use `のように` and `のような`. In speech
among friends, `みたいに` and `みたいな` are so much the default that the `よう` forms
sound self-consciously careful.

`みたいな` has a further, very modern life as a **hedge** in young people's speech,
tacked on after a quoted thought or a rough description: 「もうやめようかな、
みたいな」 - "sort of, like, 'maybe I should quit'". This use is heard constantly
among speakers in their teens and twenties, is disliked by some older listeners, and
should stay out of anything you write. It is worth recognising, not worth copying.

`みたいに` combines freely with a negative to mean "not like…":
人(ひと)みたいに上手(じょうず)にはできない - "I can't do it as well as other
people can." The `は` there is the same contrastive `は` you met at N5.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words.

</div>

### ～みたいに vs ～みたいな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `みたいな` must be followed by a noun; `みたいに` must be followed
by a verb or an adjective. That is the only difference, and it is absolute.

</div>

<pre>
山(やま)<b>みたいな</b>波(なみ)が来(き)た。
A wave like a mountain came in.
</pre>

<pre>
波(なみ)が山(やま)<b>みたいに</b>高(たか)くなった。
The waves rose as high as mountains.
</pre>

The first describes the wave as an object; the second describes how it rose. Put
`みたいな` in front of a verb and the sentence has no predicate to hang on.

### ～みたいに／みたいな vs ～のように／のような

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, opposite ends of the register scale. And the
form differs by exactly one syllable: `Noun + のような` versus `Noun + みたいな`.

</div>

<pre>
氷(こおり)<b>みたいに</b>冷(つめ)たい手(て)だね。
Your hands are freezing - like ice.
</pre>

<pre>
氷(こおり)<b>のように</b>冷(つめ)たい風(かぜ)が吹(ふ)いていた。
A wind as cold as ice was blowing.
</pre>

The first is something you say holding somebody's hand; the second is a line from a
novel. Neither is more correct - but a written description in `みたいに` reads as
sloppy, and a remark to a friend in `のように` reads as arch.

### ～みたいに vs ～そうに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `みたいに` compares the action to **something else**. `そうに`
describes how the doer **appears while doing it**, with no comparison at all.

</div>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は<b>うれしそうに</b>話(はな)していた。
My sister was talking happily. (she looked happy)
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は子(こ)ども<b>みたいに</b>話(はな)していた。
My sister was talking like a child. (she sounded childish)
</pre>

Learners mix these because both translate with "-ly" or "like". Ask yourself
whether a second thing is being brought into the sentence. If yes, `みたいに`. If
you are just reading somebody's face, `そうに`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 子(こ)ども**のみたいな**字(じ) → ✅ 子(こ)ども**みたいな**字(じ)
  The `の` belongs to `よう` alone. Attaching both is the classic crossover error.

- ❌ 猫(ねこ)**みたいな**歩(ある)く → ✅ 猫(ねこ)**みたいに**歩(ある)く
  A verb follows, so the adverbial form is required.

- ❌ **そんなみたいな**話(はなし) → ✅ **そんな**話(はなし)
  `こんな・そんな・あんな` already *are* the casual "like this/that" words. They
  never combine with `みたいな`.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)**なみたいに**見(み)える → ✅ 元気(げんき)**みたいに**見(み)える
  な-adjectives shed their `な` in front of `みたい`. Only `よう` keeps it.

## Practice

**1.** 弟(おとうと)は大人(おとな)____話(はな)し方(かた)をする。

The word after the blank, <code>話(はな)し方(かた)</code>, is a noun, so the
adnominal `みたいな` is required. Had the sentence been

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

One is a description of a face, the other is a simile.

</details>

# ～そうだ - hearsay

**Meaning** · I hear that; they say that - passing on information you got from somewhere else

This `～そうだ` is a **relay**. Somebody told you, you read it, you saw it on the
news, and now you are handing the same content on unchanged. You are not judging it
and not adding to it.

<div class="rule">

**Warning** - there are **two** grammars spelled `そうだ`, and they are unrelated.
This one attaches to the **plain form** and means "I hear that". The other attaches
to the **stem** and means "looks like". <code>高(たか)いそうだ</code> = "I hear it's
expensive". <code>高(たか)そうだ</code> = "it looks expensive". One `い` is the entire
difference.

</div>

## Formation

Attach `そうだ` to the plain form of anything - and unlike `ようだ`, nouns and
な-adjectives **keep their だ**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + そうだ | 来(く)る → 来(く)るそうだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + そうだ | 来(き)た → 来(き)たそうだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + そうだ | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないそうだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + そうだ | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いそうだ |
| い-adjective (past) | かった-form + そうだ | 高(たか)かった → 高(たか)かったそうだ |
| な-adjective | ～だ + そうだ | 便利(べんり)だ → 便利(べんり)だそうだ |
| Noun | Noun + だ + そうだ | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)だそうだ |
| Polite | そうだ → そうです | 来(く)るそうです |
| Naming the source | Source + によると／の話(はなし)では | 新聞(しんぶん)によると… |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - hearsay `そうだ` **never conjugates**. There is no
❌<code>そうだった</code>, no ❌<code>そうな</code>, no ❌<code>そうに</code>, no
❌<code>そうじゃない</code>. Tense and negation go on the clause *in front* of it:
<code>降(ふ)ったそうです</code>, not ❌<code>降(ふ)るそうでした</code>. This is the
sharpest formal difference from the other `そうだ`, which conjugates freely.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんは来(こ)ない<b>そうです</b>。
I hear Yamada isn't coming.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)のラーメンは高(たか)い<b>そうだ</b>。
They say the ramen at that place is expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)予報(よほう)によると、明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)だ<b>そうです</b>。
According to the forecast, tomorrow will be snowy.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい駅(えき)はとても便利(べんり)だ<b>そうだ</b>。
The new station is supposed to be very convenient.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)の話(はなし)では、おばあさんは若(わか)いころ東京(とうきょう)に住(す)んでいた<b>そうだ</b>。
According to my mother, my grandmother lived in Tokyo when she was young.
</pre>

<pre>
先輩(せんぱい)は大学(だいがく)のとき毎日(まいにち)アルバイトをしていた<b>そうです</b>。
Apparently my senior worked a part-time job every single day at university.
</pre>

<pre>
新聞(しんぶん)によると、その事故(じこ)でけがをした人(ひと)はいなかった<b>そうだ</b>。
According to the paper, nobody was injured in the accident.
</pre>

<pre>
山口(やまぐち)さんによると、来月(らいげつ)から会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)が変(か)わる<b>そうです</b>。
Yamaguchi says the meeting time changes from next month.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`そうです` is neutral polite and turns up everywhere: news reports, workplace
updates, ordinary conversation. Plain `そうだ` is the written form; spoken between
friends it sounds bookish, and casual Japanese reaches instead for the quotative
`って` - 「来(こ)ないって」 - or for `らしい`.

Two structural facts follow from the fact that this is a pure relay:

- **It cannot be questioned.** ❌<code>来(く)るそうですか</code> is not how you ask.
  If you want to check what somebody heard, you say
  <code>来(く)るそうですね</code> (seeking agreement) or
  <code>来(く)るんですか</code>.
- **It cannot be hedged.** You do not write
  ❌<code>たぶん来(く)るそうです</code>. The hedging belongs to whoever said it
  first, not to you.

Naming the source is optional but very common, and it is what makes the sentence
sound responsible rather than gossipy. `～によると` is the neutral formula;
<code>～の話(はなし)では</code> is slightly softer and more personal;
<code>～の話(はなし)によると</code> is the fullest and most formal of the three.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - it does not weigh evidence at all, it forwards a message.

</div>

### hearsay ～そうだ vs appearance ～そうだ

This is the distinction the whole page exists for.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **plain form** + `そうだ` = "I hear". **Stem** + `そうだ` =
"looks". Nouns can take the first and never the second.

</div>

<pre>
このかばんは高(たか)い<b>そうだ</b>。
I hear this bag is expensive. (someone told me the price)
</pre>

<pre>
このかばんは<b>高(たか)そうだ</b>。
This bag looks expensive. (I'm looking at it; I have no idea of the price)
</pre>

Three further tests separate them when the form alone is ambiguous:

1. **Conjugation.** Appearance `そうだ` becomes <code>高(たか)そうな</code>,
   <code>高(たか)そうに</code>, <code>高(たか)そうだった</code>. Hearsay `そうだ`
   does none of that.
2. **Nouns.** <code>学生(がくせい)だそうだ</code> ("I hear he's a student") is fine;
   ❌<code>学生(がくせい)そうだ</code> does not exist, because appearance `そうだ`
   cannot attach to a noun at all.
3. **Source phrases.** `～によると` can only introduce the hearsay one.

### ～そうだ vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` forwards a message with its source intact. `らしい` says
"this is going around" - the source is vaguer, and a little of the speaker's own
judgement leaks in.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)によると、その計画(けいかく)は中止(ちゅうし)になった<b>そうです</b>。
According to the department head, the plan has been cancelled.
</pre>

<pre>
その計画(けいかく)、中止(ちゅうし)になった<b>らしい</b>ですよ。
That plan's apparently been cancelled.
</pre>

The first is a report you would stand behind because you can name who said it. The
second is what you say when you are not quite sure where you picked it up. There is
also a formal difference: `らしい` conjugates like an い-adjective and can modify a
noun - <code>中止(ちゅうし)になったらしい計画(けいかく)</code> - while `そうだ`
can only end a sentence.

### ～そうだ vs ～と言(い)っていた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と言(い)っていた</code> quotes a **person** saying a
**thing**. `そうだ` reports the **content** and leaves the speech act behind.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)ると<b>言(い)っていました</b>。
Tanaka said he'd be leaving early today.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは今日(きょう)早(はや)く帰(かえ)る<b>そうです</b>。
Tanaka is apparently leaving early today.
</pre>

Use the first when the fact that he *said it* matters - you are reporting a
conversation. Use the second when only the information matters. In a workplace, the
first is the safer choice if somebody might later ask who told you.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)**そうです**。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)**だそうです**。
  Nouns keep their `だ` before hearsay `そうだ`. Dropping it produces the other
  `そうだ`, which cannot take a noun anyway - so the sentence is simply broken.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)**なそうです** → ✅ 元気(げんき)**だそうです**
  な-adjectives also keep `だ`. The `な` form belongs in front of `よう`, not `そう`.

- ❌ きのうは寒(さむ)い**そうでした**。 → ✅ きのうは寒(さむ)かった**そうです**。
  `そうだ` itself never carries tense. Put the past on the adjective in front of it.

- ❌ この料理(りょうり)は<b>辛(から)そうです</b>。(meaning "I hear it's spicy") → ✅ この料理(りょうり)は<b>辛(から)いそうです</b>。
  Without the `い` you have said it *looks* spicy, which is a claim about the photo
  rather than the taste.

## Practice

**1.** 林(はやし)さんの話(はなし)では、新(あたら)しい先生(せんせい)は親切(しんせつ)____そうです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>親切(しんせつ)</code> is a な-adjective, and な-adjectives keep `だ` in front of
hearsay `そうだ`. Writing ❌<code>親切(しんせつ)なそうです</code> confuses this
pattern with `ようだ`.

</details>

**2.** 窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ながら：「空(そら)が暗(くら)いね。（降(ふ)るそうだ・降(ふ)りそうだ）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The speaker is looking at the sky, so the evidence is a direct visual impression and
the appearance `そうだ` on the ます-stem is what fits. Hearsay `そうだ` would claim
somebody had told you it was going to rain, which the situation rules out.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "According to the news, that shop closed last month."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ニュースによると、あの店(みせ)は先月(せんげつ)しまった<b>そうです</b>。

The tense sits on the verb in front of `そうです` - <code>しまった</code> - because
`そうだ` never inflects. <code>によると</code> naming the source is the standard
partner of this pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 田中(たなか)さんの息子(むすこ)さんは大学生(だいがくせい)そうだったそうです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Two faults: a noun needs `だ` before `そうだ`, and `そうだ` cannot be put into the
past. If the point is that he *used to be* a student, move the tense to the clause:
<code>大学生(だいがくせい)だったそうです</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) この薬(くすり)は<b>効(き)きそうだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) forwards somebody else's claim: a pharmacist, a friend, a review said this
medicine works well, and the speaker is repeating it without having tried it. (b) is
the speaker's own impression from looking at the thing - the packaging, the size of
the tablet - with nobody else involved. The first is information; the second is a
hunch from the surface.

</details>

# ～そうだ - appearance

**Meaning** · looks like; seems about to - an immediate impression taken off the surface

This `～そうだ` reports what something **looks like right now**. There is no
reasoning in it and no information from anybody else: you are describing a surface
you can see. With an adjective it means "looks ～"; with a verb it means "looks
about to ～".

<div class="rule">

**Warning** - there are **two** grammars spelled `そうだ`, and this is the other
one. Hearsay `そうだ` attaches to the **plain form**:
<code>高(たか)いそうだ</code> = "I hear it's expensive". Appearance `そうだ`
attaches to the **stem**: <code>高(たか)そうだ</code> = "it looks expensive".
They are different sentences and they are not interchangeable.

</div>

## Formation

Everything loses its ending before `そう`. Verbs give up `ます`, い-adjectives give
up `い`, な-adjectives give up `な`. **Nouns cannot take this pattern at all.**

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + そうだ | 降(ふ)ります → 降(ふ)りそうだ |
| い-adjective | drop い + そうだ | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しそうだ |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + そうだ | 元気(げんき)だ → 元気(げんき)そうだ |
| Noun | - | not possible; use `のようだ`／`みたいだ` |
| いい (irregular) | よ + さそうだ | いい → よさそうだ |
| ない (irregular) | な + さそうだ | ない → なさそうだ |
| ～たい | ～た + そうだ | 行(い)きたい → 行(い)きたそうだ |
| い-adj negative | ～くな + さそうだ | 安(やす)くない → 安(やす)くなさそうだ |
| Verb negative | ます-stem + そうにない／そうもない | 降(ふ)りそうにない |
| Polite | そうだ → そうです | 降(ふ)りそうです |
| Past | そうだった／そうでした | 元気(げんき)そうでした |
| Before a noun / verb | そうな／そうに | 眠(ねむ)そうな顔(かお)／眠(ねむ)そうに見(み)る |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one-syllable adjectives need a cushion. `いい` and `ない` are too
short to survive losing their ending, so they insert `さ`: **よさそう**, **なさそう**.
❌<code>いそう</code> and ❌<code>なそう</code> are not words.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今(いま)にも雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)りそうだ</b>。
It looks like it could rain any moment.
</pre>

<pre>
この肉(にく)、<b>やわらかそう</b>ですね。
This meat looks tender.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりに会(あ)った彼(かれ)は<b>元気(げんき)そう</b>だった。
He looked well when I saw him after all that time.
</pre>

<pre>
ボタンが<b>取(と)れそう</b>だよ。
Your button's about to come off.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は<b>難(むずか)しそう</b>だが、やってみよう。
This problem looks hard, but let's have a go at it.
</pre>

<pre>
このアプリ、<b>よさそう</b>だから使(つか)ってみよう。
This app looks decent - let's give it a try.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は時間(じかん)が<b>なさそう</b>なので、また今度(こんど)にしましょう。
It doesn't look like there'll be time today, so let's do it another time.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)中(じゅう)には終(お)わり<b>そうにない</b>から、続(つづ)きは明日(あした)にします。
There's no sign of this being finished today, so I'll carry on tomorrow.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`そうだ` is register-neutral and enormously common in speech. As a one-word reaction
to food it is almost obligatory - 「おいしそう!」 said over somebody's plate is a
compliment, not a comment, and its absence is noticed.

Two restrictions are worth knowing, because breaking either sounds distinctly odd:

- **Not for properties you can simply see.** Colour, shape and
  <code>きれい</code> are already right in front of you, so `そう` has nothing to add:
  you say <code>きれいですね</code>, not ❌<code>きれいそうですね</code>. `そう` is for
  a property you are *inferring* from the surface - tenderness, expense, difficulty.
- **Not about yourself.** ❌<code>私(わたし)はねむそうです</code> is strange,
  because you do not need to read your own face. Say
  <code>私(わたし)はねむいです</code>. `そう` about other people's inner states - 
  <code>うれしそう</code>, <code>寂(さび)しそう</code> - is exactly right, because
  their feelings are only available to you through appearance.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>かわいい</code> has no `そう` form, because
<code>かわいそう</code> is already a separate word meaning "poor thing, pitiable".
To say something looks cute, use <code>かわいらしい</code> or
<code>かわいく見(み)える</code>.

</div>

The negative deserves a paragraph of its own. For **adjectives**, the negative goes
inside: <code>安(やす)くなさそう</code> ("doesn't look cheap"). For **verbs**, the
standard form is <code>ます-stem + そうにない</code> or
<code>そうもない</code>: <code>間(ま)に合(あ)いそうにない</code>. You will also
hear <code>降(ふ)らなさそう</code> in casual speech, formed on the ない-form
instead; it is widespread but not what a textbook or a careful writer will use.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing, before
reasoning starts. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of a message.

</div>

### appearance ～そうだ vs hearsay ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - count the `い`. <code>高(たか)い</code> **そうだ** is a
rumour about the price. <code>高(たか)</code>**そうだ** is a look at the object.

</div>

<pre>
この時計(とけい)は<b>高(たか)そうだ</b>。
This watch looks expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
この時計(とけい)は高(たか)い<b>そうだ</b>。
I hear this watch is expensive.
</pre>

Everything else follows from that. The appearance form **conjugates** - you can say
<code>高(たか)そうな時計(とけい)</code> and <code>高(たか)そうだった</code> - 
whereas the hearsay form is frozen: no ❌<code>高(たか)いそうな</code>, no
❌<code>高(たか)いそうだった</code>. And a noun can precede the hearsay one
(<code>本物(ほんもの)だそうだ</code>) but never the appearance one.

### appearance ～そうだ vs ～ようだ／～みたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` describes the **surface**. `ようだ` and `みたい` report
a **conclusion** you have reasoned your way to. `そうだ` is what you say at a
glance; `ようだ` is what you say after thinking.

</div>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>忙(いそが)しそう</b>だ。
She looks busy. (she's rushing around with her arms full)
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は忙(いそが)しい<b>ようだ</b>。
She seems to be busy. (she hasn't answered my messages for three days)
</pre>

The first is a photograph, the second is an argument. Notice also that `そうだ`
cannot look at things that have no surface: you can say
<code>忙(いそが)しそう</code> about a person in front of you, but for someone you
have not seen, only `ようだ` and `みたい` work.

### ～そうにない／そうもない vs ～なさそう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - verbs take `そうにない`; adjectives take `なさそう`. Mixing them
is the commonest error in the negative.

</div>

<pre>
この雨(あめ)は<b>やみそうにない</b>。
There's no sign of this rain letting up.
</pre>

<pre>
この傘(かさ)は<b>丈夫(じょうぶ)じゃなさそう</b>だ。
This umbrella doesn't look sturdy.
</pre>

`そうもない` is a slightly stronger, more emphatic variant of `そうにない` - "not the
faintest sign of it" - and `そうにもない` combines both for extra weight. All three
are natural; `そうにない` is the neutral one to default to.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は先生(せんせい)**そうです**。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は先生(せんせい)**のようです**。
  Appearance `そうだ` cannot attach to a noun. Use `ようだ` or `みたい` instead.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は**いいそう**だ。(meaning "looks good") → ✅ この店(みせ)は**よさそう**だ。
  `いい` inserts `さ`. Note that <code>いいそうだ</code> is not meaningless - it is the
  *hearsay* form, "I hear it's good".

- ❌ お金(かね)が**ないそう**だ。(meaning "looks like there's no money") → ✅ お金(かね)が**なさそう**だ。
  Same irregularity: `ない` also takes the `さ` cushion.

- ❌ この料理(りょうり)は**おいしいそう**だ。(meaning "looks delicious") → ✅ この料理(りょうり)は**おいしそう**だ。
  Keeping the `い` turns it into hearsay. The `い` must go.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は**うれしそうです**。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は**うれしいです**。
  You do not infer your own feelings from your own appearance. `そう` about the
  speaker's inner state sounds as if you were watching yourself on video.

## Practice

**1.** ケーキが落(お)ち____ですよ。気(き)をつけて。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ケーキが落(お)ち<b>そう</b>ですよ。

With a verb, `そう` goes on the ます-stem and means the event is on the point of
happening. <code>落(お)ちます</code> → <code>落(お)ち</code> + そう, with nothing
in between.

</details>

**2.** 山田(やまだ)さんから聞(き)いたんですが、あの映画(えいが)は（面白(おもしろ)そうです・面白(おもしろ)いそうです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの映画(えいが)は<b>面白(おもしろ)いそうです</b>。

The sentence names a person who told you, so it is a relay and needs the hearsay
form on the plain <code>面白(おもしろ)い</code>. The stem form would claim you had
judged the film from its poster.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This bag doesn't look heavy."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このかばんは<b>重(おも)くなさそうです</b>。

For an い-adjective, the negative is built first - <code>重(おも)くない</code> - 
and then `ない` takes its irregular `さ` cushion to become `なさそう`. The verb-style
negative `そうにない` is not used with adjectives.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この仕事(しごと)は今日(きょう)終(お)わりなさそうです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この仕事(しごと)は今日(きょう)終(お)わり**そうにありません**。

<code>終(お)わる</code> is a verb, and verbs form the negative appearance with
`そうにない` (polite: <code>そうにありません</code>) or `そうもない`. `なさそう` belongs
to adjectives and to the verb <code>ある</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山(やま)の上(うえ)は<b>寒(さむ)そうだ</b>。
(b) 山(やま)の上(うえ)は<b>寒(さむ)いようだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a look: you can see snow on the peak, or people coming down in thick coats,
and you say so on the spot. (b) is a conclusion: perhaps the forecast, or the fact
that the cable car has closed, has led you to work out that it is cold up there.
(a) needs something visible; (b) does not, and would still be usable in a room with
no window.

</details>

# ～そうに・～そうな

**Meaning** · looking ～; ～ly, by the look of it - the adverbial and adnominal forms of appearance そうだ

Appearance `そうだ` inflects like a な-adjective, so it can step inside a sentence
instead of ending one. `～そうな` goes in front of a **noun** and describes how that
thing looks. `～そうに` goes in front of a **verb or adjective** and describes the
manner the doer appears to be in. Hearsay `そうだ` has no such forms at all - so a
`そうな` or `そうに` you meet in the wild is always the appearance one.

## Formation

The attachment is the same as for sentence-final appearance `そうだ`: everything
loses its ending first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb, before a noun | ます-stem + そうな | 泣(な)きます → 泣(な)きそうな顔(かお) |
| Verb, before a verb | ます-stem + そうに | 落(お)ちます → 落(お)ちそうに見(み)える |
| い-adjective | drop い + そうな／そうに | 楽(たの)しい → 楽(たの)しそうに |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + そうな／そうに | 大変(たいへん)だ → 大変(たいへん)そうな仕事(しごと) |
| いい (irregular) | よ + さそうな／さそうに | いい → よさそうな席(せき) |
| ない (irregular) | な + さそうな／さそうに | ない → なさそうに |
| Negative (verb) | ます-stem + そうにない／そうもない | 届(とど)きます → 届(とど)きそうにない |
| Before a noun | そう + な | そうな人(ひと) |
| Before a verb / adjective | そう + に | そうに笑(わら)う |
| Noun | - | not possible; use `のような`／`みたいな` |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same choice as every な-adjective. Noun follows → `そうな`.
Verb or adjective follows → `そうに`. And `そうにない` is literally `そうに` + `ない`,
which is why the negative of a verb takes this shape rather than ❌`そうくない`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は<b>ねむそうな</b>顔(かお)をしている。
My little brother has a sleepy look on his face.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちが<b>楽(たの)しそうに</b>遊(あそ)んでいる。
The children are playing away happily.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>重(おも)そうな</b>かばんですね。持(も)ちましょうか。
That bag looks heavy. Shall I carry it?
</pre>

<pre>
犬(いぬ)が<b>うれしそうに</b>しっぽをふっている。
The dog is wagging its tail in delight.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>言(い)いにくそうに</b>話(はな)し始(はじ)めた。
She started speaking as though she found it hard to get the words out.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)にも<b>泣(な)きそうな</b>声(こえ)で電話(でんわ)をかけてきた。
He rang me in a voice that sounded ready to break.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>よさそうな</b>席(せき)が空(あ)いていたので、そこに座(すわ)った。
A decent-looking seat was free, so I sat there.
</pre>

<pre>
雪(ゆき)がひどいので、バスは<b>来(き)そうにない</b>ですね。
The snow's bad, so it doesn't look like the bus is coming.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

These forms are register-neutral and belong to speech and writing alike. Politeness
lives elsewhere in the sentence - <code>楽(たの)しそうに話(はな)しています</code>
is polite, <code>楽(たの)しそうに話(はな)している</code> is plain, and `そうに`
itself never changes.

`そうに` before a verb of action is worth dwelling on, because English does not
carve it up the same way. <code>おいしそうに食(た)べる</code> does **not** mean
"eat something that looks delicious" - it means "eat with evident enjoyment". The
`そう` describes the **eater's appearance**, not the food's. The same goes for
<code>眠(ねむ)そうに運転(うんてん)する</code> and
<code>つまらなそうに聞(き)く</code>: in every case you are reading the person.

Three combinations worth learning as units:

- <code>～そうに見(み)える</code> - "looks ～". Slightly more deliberate than plain
  `そうだ`, and useful when you want to contrast appearance with reality:
  元気(げんき)そうに見(み)えるが、本当(ほんとう)は疲(つか)れている。
- <code>～そうにする</code> - "act ～", put on the appearance:
  忙(いそが)しそうにしている。
- <code>今(いま)にも～そうな</code> - "on the very point of ～". `今にも` is the
  standard partner adverb for imminence.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ`, and with it `そうな` and `そうに`, is narrower than any of them: the
**immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis, and has no
adverbial or adnominal form to offer.

</div>

### ～そうな vs ～そうに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうな` describes a **thing**; `そうに` describes an **action**.
Look at what comes after the pattern, not before it.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>苦(くる)しそうな</b>顔(かお)をした。
He made a pained face.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>苦(くる)しそうに</b>息(いき)をしていた。
He was breathing with evident difficulty.
</pre>

The first attributes the look to the face; the second attributes it to the way he
was breathing. The difference is small in English and structural in Japanese: put
`そうな` in front of a verb and there is nothing for it to modify.

### ～そうに vs ～ように／みたいに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうに` reads the doer's own appearance. `ように` and `みたいに`
compare the action to **something else**. If a second thing enters the sentence, it
is not `そうに`.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)は<b>悲(かな)しそうに</b>手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)んでいた。
My mother was reading the letter with a sad look.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)は何度(なんど)も読(よ)んだことがある<b>ように</b>手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)んでいた。
My mother read the letter as though she had read it many times before.
</pre>

In the first, all the information is on her face. In the second, the sentence brings
in an imagined alternative situation and measures her against it.

### ～そうにない vs ～ないそうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうにない` is *my* reading of the situation. `ないそうだ` is
*somebody else's* information. They sound alike and they mean entirely different
things.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは<b>来(き)そうにない</b>。
It doesn't look like Tanaka is coming. (it's late and there's no sign of him)
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは<b>来(こ)ないそうだ</b>。
Tanaka isn't coming, apparently. (he told somebody, and it got to me)
</pre>

Note the verb shape as well as the ending: `そうにない` sits on the **ます-stem**
<code>来(き)</code>, while hearsay `そうだ` sits on the full negative
<code>来(こ)ない</code>. Two different readings of the same kanji, which makes this
pair worth saying out loud until it sticks.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ねむ**そうだ**顔(かお) → ✅ ねむ**そうな**顔(かお)
  `そうだ` ends a sentence. In front of a noun it takes the `な` form, exactly like
  <code>静(しず)かだ</code> becoming <code>静(しず)かな</code>.

- ❌ 楽(たの)し**そうな**遊(あそ)んでいる → ✅ 楽(たの)し**そうに**遊(あそ)んでいる
  A verb follows, so the adverbial form is the only option.

- ❌ **いいそうな**店(みせ) → ✅ **よさそうな**店(みせ)
  `いい` takes the irregular `さ` cushion. <code>いいそうな</code> is not a form at
  all - hearsay `そうだ` has no `な`.

- ❌ 間(ま)に<b>合(あ)うそうにない</b> → ✅ 間(ま)に<b>合(あ)いそうにない</b>
  `そうにない` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form
  before `そう` would be hearsay, which cannot be negated this way.

- ❌ 高(たか)い**そうな**店(みせ) → ✅ 高(たか)**そうな**店(みせ)
  Drop the `い`. With it, you have tried to build an adnominal form out of the
  hearsay pattern, which does not have one.

## Practice

**1.** 山(やま)のような宿題(しゅくだい)を見(み)て、妹(いもうと)は泣(な)き____顔(かお)になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

妹(いもうと)は泣(な)き<b>そうな</b>顔(かお)になった。

<code>顔(かお)</code> is a noun, so the adnominal `そうな` is required, and it sits
on the ます-stem <code>泣(な)き</code>. With a verb it means the crying is on the
point of starting.

</details>

**2.** 天気(てんき)予報(よほう)を見(み)て：「明日(あした)は（降(ふ)りそうにない・降(ふ)らないそうだ）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)は<b>降(ふ)らないそうだ</b>。

The information came from the forecast - somebody else's report - so this is
hearsay `そうだ` on the plain negative. `そうにない` would mean you had looked at the
sky and judged for yourself, with no forecast involved.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The children were eating the cake happily."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)どもたちはケーキを<b>うれしそうに</b>食(た)べていた。

`そうに` goes in front of the verb and describes how the children looked, not how
the cake looked. Putting `そうな` here would leave the pattern with no noun to
modify.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 重(おも)いそうな荷物(にもつ)を一人(ひとり)で運(はこ)んでいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 重(おも)**そうな**荷物(にもつ)を一人(ひとり)で運(はこ)んでいた。

The `い` has to go: appearance `そう` attaches to the adjective stem. Keeping it
produces the hearsay pattern, which has no `な` form and so cannot sit in front of
<code>荷物(にもつ)</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は<b>忙(いそが)しそうに</b>働(はたら)いている。
(b) 彼(かれ)は<b>忙(いそが)しそうにしている</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply describes how he looks while working: rushed, absorbed, no judgement
attached. (b) uses <code>そうにする</code>, "to act ～", and so hints that the busy
appearance is being **put on** - he is making a show of being busy. That extra layer
of suspicion is carried entirely by the verb <code>する</code>.

</details>

# ～らしい

**Meaning** · apparently, I gather - and, after a noun, "a proper ～, just as a ～ should be"

`～らしい` does two jobs. As an **evidential** it says the information came from
outside the speaker - heard, read, picked up somewhere - and quietly declines to
vouch for it. As a **suffix on a noun** it says the thing fits its category
admirably: 春(はる)らしい天気(てんき) is weather that is exactly what spring
ought to be.

## Formation

`らしい` attaches to plain forms, and nouns and な-adjectives attach **bare** - no
`だ`, no `な`, no `の`. Once attached, it conjugates like an い-adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + らしい | 来(く)る → 来(く)るらしい |
| Verb (past) | た-form + らしい | 来(き)た → 来(き)たらしい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + らしい | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないらしい |
| い-adjective | plain form + らしい | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いらしい |
| な-adjective | drop だ + らしい | 大変(たいへん)だ → 大変(たいへん)らしい |
| Noun (evidential) | Noun + らしい | 病気(びょうき) → 病気(びょうき)らしい |
| Noun (typical of) | Noun + らしい + Noun | 春(はる) → 春(はる)らしい天気(てんき) |
| Polite | らしい + です | 来(く)るらしいです |
| Past | らしかった | 来(き)たらしかった |
| Adverbial (suffix sense only) | らしく | 自分(じぶん)らしく生(い)きる |
| Negative (suffix sense only) | らしくない | 君(きみ)らしくない |
| Noun form (suffix sense only) | らしさ | 彼(かれ)らしさ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two senses split on the negative. The **suffix** sense has
`らしくない` ("not very ～, unlike them"). The **evidential** sense does not: to say
"apparently he isn't coming", negate the verb, not `らしい` - 
<code>来(こ)ないらしい</code>, never ❌<code>来(く)るらしくない</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
試験(しけん)は来週(らいしゅう)<b>らしい</b>よ。
The exam's apparently next week.
</pre>

<pre>
この道(みち)は夜(よる)あぶない<b>らしい</b>です。
I gather this road is dangerous at night.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は春(はる)<b>らしい</b>天気(てんき)ですね。
Proper spring weather today, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
となりの家(いえ)は犬(いぬ)を飼(か)い始(はじ)めた<b>らしい</b>。
The people next door have apparently got themselves a dog.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなことを言(い)うなんて、君(きみ)<b>らしくない</b>ね。
Saying a thing like that isn't like you at all.
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)<b>らしく</b>、まじめに勉強(べんきょう)しなさい。
Study properly, the way a student should.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は来年(らいねん)大阪(おおさか)に行(い)く<b>らしい</b>ですが、まだはっきりしません。
Word is the department head is moving to Osaka next year, but nothing's settled yet.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)の話(はなし)では、この辺(へん)に新(あたら)しいスーパーができる<b>らしい</b>です。
According to the neighbours, a new supermarket is going up around here.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`らしい` is at home in ordinary conversation, plain or polite. Its defining flavour
is **distance**: you are relaying something without putting your name to it. That
distance is useful when you genuinely do not know, and damaging when you are
expected to. A doctor saying <code>熱(ねつ)があるらしいです</code> about his own
patient sounds as though he has not checked; a shop assistant saying
<code>明日(あした)届(とど)くらしいです</code> about your order sounds as though
nobody is in charge. In those situations use `そうです` with a named source, or state
the fact plainly.

For the same reason, `らしい` is not the form for formal writing. A report uses
`とのことだ` or hearsay `そうだ`.

The **suffix** sense is a compliment machine. 男(おとこ)らしい,
女(おんな)らしい, 子(こ)どもらしい, 自分(じぶん)らしい,
先生(せんせい)らしい - each says the person or thing lives up to what the noun
implies. Be aware that the gendered pair carries traditional expectations and, in
present-day Japanese, has a somewhat conservative ring for many speakers; it is
still used, but it is not neutral in the way <code>自分(じぶん)らしい</code> is.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>自分(じぶん)らしく生(い)きる</code>, "live as yourself", and the
noun <code>自分(じぶん)らしさ</code>, "one's own character", are set phrases you
will meet constantly in advertising and self-help writing. The `らしさ` form belongs
to the suffix sense only.

</div>

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words.

</div>

### ～らしい vs hearsay ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both pass information on, but `そうだ` forwards it **intact and
sourced**, while `らしい` reports what is **in the air**. If you can say who told
you, `そうだ` is the honest choice.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)の話(はなし)によると、来月(らいげつ)テストがある<b>そうです</b>。
According to the teacher, there's a test next month.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)テストがある<b>らしい</b>よ。
There's a test next month, apparently.
</pre>

There is a formal difference too, and it is decisive in an exam. `らしい` conjugates
and can modify a noun - <code>中止(ちゅうし)になったらしい試合(しあい)</code> - 
while hearsay `そうだ` is frozen at the end of a sentence and can do neither.

### ～らしい vs ～ようだ／～みたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `らしい` points **outward** at a source. `ようだ` and `みたい`
point **inward** at the speaker's own reasoning. Ask "what makes you say that?": if
the answer is "someone said so", it is `らしい`.

</div>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんは来年(らいねん)結婚(けっこん)する<b>らしい</b>。
Hayashi is apparently getting married next year. (that's the word going round)
</pre>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんは来年(らいねん)結婚(けっこん)する<b>ようだ</b>。
It looks as though Hayashi is getting married next year. (the ring, the house-hunting)
</pre>

The two shade into each other in practice, and a speaker choosing between them is
often choosing how much responsibility to take. `ようだ` puts the speaker's judgement
behind the claim; `らしい` sets it down on the table and steps back.

### Noun + らしい vs Noun + っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `らしい` means "admirably like a ～". `っぽい` means "～-ish", and
usually with a shrug or a complaint attached. They are not interchangeable, and
learners reach for the wrong one constantly.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>らしい</b>まっすぐな質問(しつもん)だった。
It was a straightforward question, in the good way children ask them.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>っぽい</b>話(はな)し方(かた)はやめたほうがいい。
You should stop talking in that childish way.
</pre>

`っぽい` is casual and productive - 安(やす)っぽい, 水(みず)っぽい, 忘(わす)れっぽい - 
and it very often carries mild disapproval. `らしい` in the same slot is praise.
There is a third option, <code>Noun + のような</code>, which is neither: it merely
notes a resemblance without saying whether the thing is really in the category.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)**だらしい** → ✅ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)**らしい**
  Nouns attach bare. The `だ` is required before hearsay `そうだ` and forbidden here
 - a difference worth drilling, because the two patterns sit next to each other.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)**ならしい** → ✅ 元気(げんき)**らしい**
  な-adjectives also drop everything. `な` belongs in front of `よう`.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは来(く)る**らしくない**。(meaning "apparently isn't coming") → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは来(こ)ない**らしい**。
  The evidential sense has no negative. Negate the clause instead. `らしくない` only
  exists in the "not like a proper ～" sense.

- ❌ 子(こ)ども**っぽい**元気(げんき)な声(こえ)がいいですね。(intended as praise) → ✅ 子(こ)ども**らしい**元気(げんき)な声(こえ)がいいですね。
  `っぽい` would turn the compliment into a complaint about immaturity.

## Practice

**1.** ニュースで見(み)たんだけど、あの橋(はし)は来年(らいねん)まで使(つか)えない____よ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの橋(はし)は来年(らいねん)まで使(つか)えない<b>らしい</b>よ。

The information came from outside the speaker, which is what `らしい` marks, and it
attaches straight to the plain negative <code>使(つか)えない</code>. Note that the
negation lives on the verb, never on `らしい` itself.

</details>

**2.** 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)がきれいに片(かた)づいている。彼(かれ)は帰(かえ)った（らしい・ようだ）。Which fits better if nobody told you?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は帰(かえ)った<b>ようだ</b>。

You are reasoning from a tidy desk that you can see with your own eyes, so the
judgement is yours. `らしい` would imply somebody had mentioned it, which the
situation as described rules out.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "That was a very Japanese answer."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

とても日本人(にほんじん)<b>らしい</b>答(こた)えでしたね。

This is the suffix sense: the answer fits the category admirably. `らしい` attaches
to the bare noun and then modifies <code>答(こた)え</code> like an い-adjective.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)だらしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)**らしい**です。

Nouns take `らしい` bare. The `だ` you are remembering belongs to hearsay `そうだ`:
<code>中止(ちゅうし)だそうです</code> is the correct sentence with that pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>先生(せんせい)らしい</b>。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>先生(せんせい)のようだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is real: it can mean "apparently she's a
teacher" (evidential, someone told you) or "she is very much the teacher" (suffix,
she has the manner). Context and intonation decide. (b) has only one reading - she
resembles a teacher, and the implication is that she probably is not one. That is
the general rule of thumb: `らしい` puts the person inside the category, `のようだ`
keeps them outside it.

</details>
