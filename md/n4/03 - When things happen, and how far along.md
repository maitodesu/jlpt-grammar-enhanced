# N4 - When things happen, and how far along

# ～とき

**Meaning** · when; at the time that

`とき` is the ordinary noun for "time", pressed into service as a joint between two
clauses: *at the time when X, Y*. What lifts it above vocabulary is the tense
inside the clause. Japanese sets it by looking at the **main verb**, not at the
present moment - so <code>行(い)くとき</code> and <code>行(い)ったとき</code> can both sit in a
sentence about last year and mean quite different things.

## Formation

`とき` is a noun, so anything that can modify a noun can modify it - and it takes
that modifier in the **plain** form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary) | dictionary form + とき | 出(で)かける → 出(で)かけるとき |
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + とき | 出(で)かけた → 出(で)かけたとき |
| Verb (ない-form) | ない-form + とき | わからない → わからないとき |
| Verb (ている) | ている + とき | 運転(うんてん)している → 運転(うんてん)しているとき |
| い-adjective | plain い + とき | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいとき |
| な-adjective | な + とき | 暇(ひま)だ → 暇(ひま)なとき |
| Noun | Noun + <b>の</b> + とき | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)のとき |
| Noun (past state) | Noun + だった + とき | 子(こ)どもだったとき |
| Particles after it | とき + は／に／も | 寒(さむ)いときは、寒(さむ)いときに |
| In kanji | とき = 時(とき) | 子(こ)どもの時(とき) |
| Politeness | on the **final** verb only | 来(く)るとき買(か)い<b>ます</b> |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front of `とき` is plain. ❌来(き)ますとき is not
how ordinary Japanese sounds; politeness lives on the last verb of the sentence
and nowhere else.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `に` is optional. Adding it pins the clause to a definite point
(大学(だいがく)に入(はい)ったときに買(か)いました); leaving it off lets the clause read
as a general circumstance (寒(さむ)いとき、温(あたた)かいものが食(た)べたくなる). With
`は` on the end - `ときは` - the clause becomes the topic, which is how you state a
rule or a piece of advice.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
暇(ひま)な<b>とき</b>、何(なに)をしますか。
What do you do when you have free time?
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの<b>とき</b>、ピアノを習(なら)っていました。
I used to take piano lessons when I was little.
</pre>

<pre>
眠(ねむ)い<b>とき</b>は、冷(つめ)たい水(みず)で顔(かお)を洗(あら)います。
When I'm sleepy I wash my face with cold water.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)がわからない<b>とき</b>は、駅員(えきいん)さんに聞(き)いてください。
When you can't work out the way, ask a member of station staff.
</pre>

<pre>
家(いえ)を出(で)る<b>とき</b>、かぎをかけるのを忘(わす)れてしまいました。
I forgot to lock up as I was leaving the house.
</pre>

<pre>
大阪(おおさか)に着(つ)いた<b>とき</b>、もう夜(よる)の十時(じゅうじ)を過(す)ぎていました。
By the time I got to Osaka it was already gone ten at night.
</pre>

<pre>
運転(うんてん)している<b>とき</b>は、携帯(けいたい)を見(み)ないでください。危(あぶ)ないですから。
Please don't look at your phone while you're driving - it's dangerous.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)くなりそうな<b>とき</b>は、先(さき)に昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べておいたほうがいいですよ。
When it looks like the meeting is going to run long, you're better off eating lunch first.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`とき` is register-neutral and appears in every kind of Japanese, from a text
message to a contract. The politeness of the sentence is decided entirely by the
final verb, so <code>子(こ)どものとき、よく泣(な)いた</code> and
<code>子(こ)どものとき、よく泣(な)きました</code> differ only at the end.

One thing that *is* worth copying from careful writing: when `とき` is doing this
grammatical job, Japanese usually writes it **in kana**. The kanji
<code>時(とき)</code> is kept for cases where it means time as a real thing - 
<code>時(とき)が経(た)つ</code>, <code>時(とき)には</code>. Both spellings are read the
same and neither is wrong; kana simply looks more natural on the page.

Two step-ups you will meet as the register rises. <code>際(さい)に</code> is the
formal twin of `とき` and is everywhere in announcements and manuals:
<code>ご利用(りよう)の際(さい)は、係員(かかりいん)にお申(もう)し出(で)ください</code>. And
<code>場合(ばあい)</code> ("in the case that") takes over when the sentence is about a
**contingency** rather than a time: <code>地震(じしん)の場合(ばあい)は、エレベーターを使(つか)わないでください</code>.
Using `とき` there is not wrong, but `場合` is what the sign says.

Going the other way, casual speech shortens nothing about `とき` itself - it just
drops the `に` and often the comma, and adds `さ` or `ね`:
<code>高校(こうこう)のときさ、よくここで遊(あそ)んだよね</code>.

## Contrast

### 行(い)くとき vs 行(い)ったとき

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the tense before `とき` is measured against the **main clause**,
not against now. Dictionary form: at the moment of the main action, the とき-event
has **not finished**. た-form: it **has**.

</div>

This is the single most tested thing about `とき`, and English gives you no help,
because English says "when" for both.

<pre>
日本(にほん)へ行(い)く<b>とき</b>、スーツケースを買(か)いました。
I bought a suitcase for my trip to Japan. (bought before I got there)
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)へ行(い)った<b>とき</b>、スーツケースを買(か)いました。
I bought a suitcase when I was in Japan. (bought after I arrived)
</pre>

Both sentences are about a finished trip, and both end in `ました`. Only the form
in front of `とき` tells you which side of the arrival the shopping happened on.

The same logic makes some pairs obviously right and obviously wrong:

<pre>
お風呂(ふろ)に入(はい)る<b>とき</b>、服(ふく)を脱(ぬ)ぎます。
You take your clothes off when you get into the bath.
</pre>

You are not yet in the bath when you undress, so it has to be
<code>入(はい)るとき</code>. ❌<code>入(はい)ったとき服(ふく)を脱(ぬ)ぐ</code> describes
someone undressing in the water.

### ～とき vs ～たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とき` names a **time**; `たら` sets a **condition**. If you
could replace the English with "at the time when", use `とき`. If it is really
"once" or "if", use `たら`.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)に着(つ)い<b>たら</b>、電話(でんわ)してください。
Give me a ring once you get to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)に着(つ)いた<b>とき</b>、あの店(みせ)はもう閉(し)まっていました。
When I got to the station, that shop had already closed.
</pre>

`たら` looks forward and triggers something; `とき` looks at a point on the
timeline and reports what was true there. That is why an instruction about the
future almost always takes `たら`, and why a narrated memory almost always takes
`とき`.

`とき` also reaches places `たら` cannot go without extra machinery: it attaches
straight to a noun with `の` - <code>学生(がくせい)のとき</code> - where `たら` needs
<code>学生(がくせい)だったら</code>, and that already sounds hypothetical ("if you were a
student").

### ～ているとき vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` needs **one person doing two things at once**, and the
main action is the one at the end. `ているとき` has no such restriction: the two
clauses can have different subjects entirely.

</div>

<pre>
テレビを見(み)<b>ながら</b>ごはんを食(た)べます。
I eat while watching TV.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)がごはんを食(た)べ<b>ているとき</b>に、姉(あね)が帰(かえ)ってきました。
My older sister got home while I was eating.
</pre>

Swap them and the second sentence breaks: ❌<code>食(た)べながら姉(あね)が帰(かえ)ってきた</code>
would claim your sister was eating and coming home simultaneously. When the two
events belong to different people, `ているとき` is the only one of the two that
works.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 子(こ)ども<b>とき</b>、よく熱(ねつ)を出(だ)しました。 → ✅ 子(こ)ども<b>の</b>とき、よく熱(ねつ)を出(だ)しました。
  `とき` is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`, exactly as in
  <code>日本語(にほんご)の先生(せんせい)</code>.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>とき</b> → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>とき
  な-adjectives keep their `な` before any noun, and `とき` is a noun.

- ❌ 国(くに)へ帰(かえ)り<b>ますとき</b>、連絡(れんらく)します。 → ✅ 国(くに)へ帰(かえ)る<b>とき</b>、連絡(れんらく)します。
  The clause before `とき` is plain. Politeness goes on the final verb only.

- ❌ ガイドブックは京都(きょうと)へ行(い)っ<b>たとき</b>、東京(とうきょう)の本屋(ほんや)で買(か)いました。 → ✅ 京都(きょうと)へ行(い)く<b>とき</b>、東京(とうきょう)の本屋(ほんや)で買(か)いました。
  You bought it before you set off, so the trip had not happened yet: dictionary
  form. The た-form would put the Tokyo bookshop after your arrival in Kyoto.

## Practice

**1.** 大学生(だいがくせい)____とき、北海道(ほっかいどう)を旅行(りょこう)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大学生(だいがくせい)<b>の</b>とき、北海道(ほっかいどう)を旅行(りょこう)しました。

`とき` is a noun, and a noun modifying another noun needs `の` between them. Only
verbs and adjectives attach to `とき` directly.

</details>

**2.** ドアを（開(あ)ける・開(あ)けた）とき、このかぎを使(つか)ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ドアを<b>開(あ)ける</b>とき、このかぎを使(つか)ってください。

The key is what makes the opening possible, so at the moment you use it the door
is still shut - the とき-event has not finished. That is the dictionary form.
<code>開(あ)けたとき</code> would have you fiddling with the key after the door was
already open.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When I was a high school student, I worked part-time at a convenience store."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

高校生(こうこうせい)の<b>とき</b>、コンビニでアルバイトをしていました。

A noun takes `の` before `とき`. The main verb carries both the tense and the
politeness, and `ていました` is the natural choice because the job lasted a
stretch of time rather than happening once.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 忙(いそが)しいですとき、電話(でんわ)しないでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 忙(いそが)しい<b>とき</b>、電話(でんわ)しないでください。

An い-adjective goes in front of `とき` in its plain form. `です` is a sentence
ending and cannot sit in the middle of a clause that modifies a noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 家(いえ)を出(で)るとき、電気(でんき)を消(け)しました。
(b) 家(いえ)を出(で)たとき、となりの人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the dictionary form because the leaving was not finished yet: you were
still inside, on your way out, when you hit the light switch. (b) uses the た-form
because the leaving *was* finished: you were already through the door when you ran
into the neighbour.

Swapping them produces nonsense in both directions - turning off the lights from
the street, and bumping into the neighbour in your own hallway.

</details>

# ～後で

**Meaning** · after something; afterwards, later on

N5 taught this word in kana, as <code>～たあとで</code>. Here it is in kanji, plus the
two jobs the kana page left alone: `後で` standing on its own as the adverb
*later*, and the four different readings the character takes once it appears in
writing.

## Formation

The clause in front of `後で` is in the <b>た-form</b>, whatever the tense of the
sentence as a whole. A noun joins with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + 後(あと)で | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べた後(あと)で |
| Noun | Noun + <b>の</b> + 後(あと)で | 授業(じゅぎょう) → 授業(じゅぎょう)<b>の</b>後(あと)で |
| On its own (adverb) | 後(あと)で + clause | 後(あと)で連絡(れんらく)します |
| Demonstrative | その後(あと)で／この後(あと) | その後(あと)で決(き)めましょう |
| Without で | た-form + 後(あと) | 見(み)た後(あと)、考(かんが)えます |
| With に instead | た-form + 後(あと)に | 私(わたし)の後(あと)に発表(はっぴょう)します |
| Amount of time | Quantity + 後(ご)（に） | 三日(みっか)後(ご)に、一時間(いちじかん)後(ご)に |
| Sino-Japanese compound | Noun + 後(ご) | 卒業(そつぎょう)後(ご)、使用(しよう)後(ご) |
| Formal register | 後(のち)ほど | 後(のち)ほどご連絡(れんらく)いたします |
| Politeness | on the **final** verb only | 着(つ)いた後(あと)で電話(でんわ)し<b>ます</b> |
| ❌ | ❌ dictionary form + 後(あと)で | ❌食(た)べる後(あと)で |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one character, four readings, and they are not
interchangeable. <code>後(あと)</code> is *after* in time,
<code>後(うし)ろ</code> is *behind* in space, <code>後(ご)</code> is the suffix on
Sino-Japanese words and on amounts of time, and <code>後(のち)</code> is the formal,
written one. Reading <code>三日(みっか)後(ご)</code> as あと marks you out instantly.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `で` is optional and drops freely, and the whole phrase can take `は` or
`も`: <code>食(た)べた後(あと)は眠(ねむ)くなる</code>. What you cannot do is put a
verb in the dictionary form in front of it - that slot belongs to
<code>前(まえ)に</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>後(あと)で</b>メールしますね。
I'll email you later.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)は食事(しょくじ)の<b>後(あと)で</b>飲(の)んでください。
Please take the medicine after meals.
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)の<b>後(あと)で</b>、ジムで泳(およ)ぐつもりです。
I'm planning to swim at the gym after work.
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)を終(お)わらせた<b>後(あと)で</b>なら、ゲームをしてもいいよ。
You can play games once you've finished your homework.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)の<b>後(あと)で</b>、チームのみんなでラーメンを食(た)べに行(い)った。
After the match the whole team went out for ramen.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)した<b>後(あと)で</b>、住所(じゅうしょ)が変(か)わったことを会社(かいしゃ)に伝(つた)えました。
After I moved, I let the company know my address had changed.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いた<b>後(あと)で</b>質問(しつもん)がある方(かた)は、手(て)を挙(あ)げてください。
If you have a question after hearing the explanation, please raise your hand.
</pre>

<pre>
三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>後(ご)</b>に出発(しゅっぱつ)しますので、それまでに準備(じゅんび)をお願(ねが)いします。
We leave in thirty minutes, so please be ready by then.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

As a clause connector `後で` is plain and neutral. As a **standalone adverb** it
carries something extra, and that is worth knowing: `後で` is deliberately vague.
It means "at some point after now, probably today", and it names no time at all.

That vagueness is friendly among equals - <code>じゃあ、また後(あと)でね</code> is
how you end a conversation you expect to resume - and unhelpful upward. Told
<code>後(あと)でやります</code>, a manager has learned nothing; <code>三時(さんじ)までに出(だ)します</code>
is what they wanted. If you need the vagueness *and* the politeness, the business
word is <code>後(のち)ほど</code>:
<code>後(のち)ほど担当(たんとう)よりご連絡(れんらく)いたします</code>. It is one register
step above `後で` and never sounds casual.

Spelling is a genuine choice here. In the grammatical use, careful writing often
prefers kana - <code>食(た)べたあとで</code> - because the kanji pulls the reader
towards the "behind" meaning. Inside a compound the kanji is compulsory and the
reading switches: <code>卒業(そつぎょう)後(ご)</code>, <code>開店(かいてん)後(ご)</code>,
<code>二年(にねん)後(ご)</code>.

One last spoken habit: <code>後(あと)にする</code> means "leave it for later" - 
<code>その話(はなし)は後(あと)にしよう</code>. It is not the same construction as
`後で`, but it lives in the same corner of the language.

## Contrast

### ～た後(あと)で vs ～てから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てから` says the second thing does not happen **until** the
first is done. `後で` only says it happens later. When the order is a requirement,
use `てから`; when it is just the order things came in, `後で` is enough.

</div>

<pre>
お金(かね)を入(い)れ<b>てから</b>、ボタンを押(お)してください。
Put the money in first, then press the button.
</pre>

Here `てから` is doing real work: pressing the button before paying achieves
nothing. Swap in <code>入(い)れた後(あと)で</code> and the instruction goes soft - it
now merely reports a sequence.

<pre>
映画(えいが)の<b>後(あと)で</b>、喫茶店(きっさてん)で少(すこ)し話(はな)した。
After the film we chatted for a bit in a coffee shop.
</pre>

Nothing about the film enabled the chat. This is plain sequence, so `後で` fits and
`てから` would sound oddly purposeful.

`てから` has one job `後で` cannot take on at all: measuring elapsed time from a
starting point. <code>日本(にほん)に来(き)てから三年(さんねん)になります</code> - 
"it's been three years since I came to Japan." ❌<code>来(き)た後(あと)で三年(さんねん)になります</code>
is not Japanese. Going the other way, `後で` attaches straight to a noun with `の`,
which `てから` cannot do: <code>会議(かいぎ)の後(あと)で</code> has no `てから` equivalent
short of adding a verb.

### 後(あと)で vs 後(あと)に vs ～後(ご)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` is the everyday "afterwards". `に` locates a **point**:
use it when you are placing something in an order, and you must use the
<code>後(ご)</code> reading with a measured quantity of time.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんの<b>後(あと)に</b>発表(はっぴょう)します。
I'm presenting after Tanaka.
</pre>

<pre>
五分(ごふん)<b>後(ご)</b>にまたかけ直(なお)します。
I'll call back in five minutes.
</pre>

A quantity of time never takes `後で`: ❌<code>五分(ごふん)後(あと)で</code> is simply
wrong, both in the particle and in the reading. In the ordinary
"X happened, then Y" use, `後で` and `後に` overlap heavily and `後で` is the safer
spoken default; `後に` leans written, and it is what you want when the sentence is
about a slot in a sequence rather than about a time.

There is also <code>後(のち)に</code>, read のち, which means "later in life" or
"subsequently" and belongs to biography and history:
<code>彼(かれ)は後(のち)に市長(しちょう)になった</code>. `後で` cannot carry that sense.

### 後(あと)で vs 今度(こんど)・そのうち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `後で` is later **today**. <code>今度(こんど)</code> is **next
time**, on some other occasion. `そのうち` is **eventually**, with no occasion in
mind at all.

</div>

English collapses all three into "later", and picking the wrong one changes what
you have promised.

<pre>
その書類(しょるい)は<b>後(あと)で</b>見(み)ておきます。
I'll look over that document later on. (today, before I go home)
</pre>

<pre>
<b>今度(こんど)</b>いっしょに飲(の)みに行(い)きましょう。
Let's go for a drink sometime.
</pre>

That second sentence is an invitation, not an appointment, and everyone hears it
that way. If you say <code>今度(こんど)電話(でんわ)します</code> to someone waiting
for a call in the next hour, they will assume you mean next week. `そのうち` is
vaguer still - <code>そのうち片付(かたづ)けます</code> is close to admitting it may
never happen.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>後(あと)で</b>歯(は)をみがきます。 → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>た</b>後(あと)で歯(は)をみがきます。
  `後で` takes the た-form. The dictionary form in that slot belongs to
  <code>前(まえ)に</code>.

- ❌ 授業(じゅぎょう)<b>後(あと)で</b>質問(しつもん)しました。 → ✅ 授業(じゅぎょう)<b>の</b>後(あと)で質問(しつもん)しました。
  `後` is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`. (The compound
  <code>授業(じゅぎょう)後(ご)</code> exists and needs no `の` - but then it is read
  ご and belongs to written style.)

- ❌ 二時間(にじかん)<b>後(あと)で</b>戻(もど)ります。 → ✅ 二時間(にじかん)<b>後(ご)に</b>戻(もど)ります。
  With a measured amount of time the reading is ご and the particle is `に`.

- ❌ すぐ終(お)わるので、<b>今度(こんど)</b>電話(でんわ)します。 → ✅ すぐ終(お)わるので、<b>後(あと)で</b>電話(でんわ)します。
  <code>今度(こんど)</code> pushes the call to another occasion. For "later today",
  it has to be `後で`.

## Practice

**1.** シャワーを浴(あ)び____後(あと)で、ビールを飲(の)みます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

シャワーを浴(あ)び<b>た</b>後(あと)で、ビールを飲(の)みます。

The verb in front of `後で` is always in the た-form, even though the sentence
describes a habit and ends in the present `ます`. The た marks the shower as
complete relative to the beer, not relative to now.

</details>

**2.** 十分(じゅっぷん)（後(あと)で・後(ご)に）会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

十分(じゅっぷん)<b>後(ご)に</b>会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まります。

A measured quantity of time takes the ご reading and the particle `に`.
❌<code>十分(じゅっぷん)後(あと)で</code> is wrong on both counts, and native
listeners notice the reading before they notice the particle.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll explain the details later - let's eat first."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

詳(くわ)しいことは<b>後(あと)で</b>説明(せつめい)します。まず食(た)べましょう。

`後で` on its own is the everyday adverb "later", and it is exactly right for
pushing something to the end of the same afternoon. <code>今度(こんど)</code> would
mean the explanation comes on a different day.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べる後(あと)で、公園(こうえん)を散歩(さんぽ)しませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>た</b>後(あと)で、公園(こうえん)を散歩(さんぽ)しませんか。

Only the た-form goes in front of `後で`. The invitation is still about the future,
but the た is measuring the lunch against the walk, not against the present
moment.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 後(あと)でご連絡(れんらく)します。
(b) 後(のち)ほどご連絡(れんらく)いたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both promise a message with no time attached. (a) is ordinary polite Japanese and
fits a colleague, a friend, or a shop you deal with often. (b) is the same promise
in business register: `後ほど` is the formal word for "later", and `いたします`
lowers the speaker. You would hear (b) from a call centre or a receptionist, and
it would sound stiff between friends.

</details>

# ～までに

**Meaning** · by - at any point up to that limit, but not after it

N5 gave you `まで`, "until", which measures a stretch of time. Adding `に` turns
the stretch into an **edge**. `までに` names the last moment at which something may
happen, and says nothing about when inside the window it actually does. It is the
particle of deadlines.

## Formation

`までに` follows a time expression, or a clause in the **dictionary form** that
names the moment the window closes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Time noun | Noun + までに | 五時(ごじ) → 五時(ごじ)までに |
| Day or date | Noun + までに | 金曜日(きんようび) → 金曜日(きんようび)までに |
| Event noun | Noun + までに | 試験(しけん) → 試験(しけん)までに |
| Verb | **dictionary form** + までに | 出(で)かける → 出(で)かけるまでに |
| Demonstrative | それ／いつ + までに | それまでに、いつまでに |
| Emphasis | までに + は | 八時(はちじ)までには着(つ)きます |
| Very often with | までに + ～ておく | 買(か)っておく、片付(かたづ)けておく |
| ❌ | ❌ た-form + までに | ❌着(つ)いたまでに |
| ❌ | ❌ ない-form + までに | ❌来(こ)ないまでに |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the main verb of a `までに` sentence has to be something that
**finishes in one go**: 出(だ)す, 着(つ)く, 決(き)める, 終(お)わらせる,
帰(かえ)る. If the verb describes something that goes *on* - 待(ま)つ,
寝(ね)る, 働(はたら)く - you want plain `まで`, not `までに`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>までには</code> adds "at the latest, and I mean it":
<code>七時(しちじ)までには戻(もど)ります</code> promises seven as the outer bound,
whereas <code>七時(しちじ)までに戻(もど)ります</code> merely states the deadline.
The `は` is what turns a fact into a commitment.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
五時(ごじ)<b>までに</b>帰(かえ)ってきてね。
Be back by five, all right?
</pre>

<pre>
レポートは金曜日(きんようび)<b>までに</b>出(だ)してください。
Please hand the report in by Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が来(く)る<b>までに</b>、きっぷを買(か)っておきます。
I'll buy the ticket before the train gets here.
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)が終(お)わる<b>までに</b>、一度(いちど)は海(うみ)に行(い)きたいなあ。
I'd like to get to the sea at least once before the summer's over.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の月曜日(げつようび)<b>までに</b>お返事(へんじ)をいただけますか。
Could I have your reply by Monday next week?
</pre>

<pre>
お客(きゃく)さんが来(く)る<b>までに</b>、会議室(かいぎしつ)を片付(かたづ)けておきましょう。
Let's get the meeting room tidied up before the client arrives.
</pre>

<pre>
三十歳(さんじゅっさい)になる<b>までに</b>、自分(じぶん)の店(みせ)を持(も)ちたいと思(おも)っています。
I'm hoping to have my own shop by the time I turn thirty.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し込(こ)みは今月(こんげつ)の終(お)わりまで受(う)け付(つ)けていますが、できれば今週(こんしゅう)<b>までに</b>決(き)めてほしいそうです。
Applications are open to the end of the month, but apparently they'd like a decision by this week if possible.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`までに` is register-neutral and lives in the parts of life that have deadlines:
homework, forms, trains, work. Nothing about it is polite or blunt in itself - the
verb at the end does that. <code>金曜(きんよう)までに出(だ)せ</code> is an order,
<code>金曜(きんよう)までにご提出(ていしゅつ)ください</code> is a notice, and the
`までに` is identical in both.

What it *does* carry is pressure, because it draws a line. Softening usually
happens around it rather than to it. <code>できれば</code> ("if possible"),
<code>念(ねん)のため</code> ("just in case") and turning the request into a question - 
<code>明日(あした)までにいただけますか</code> - all take the edge off without moving the
deadline. Dropping `に` does not soften anything; it changes the meaning.

Two things you will hear rather than read. First, in speech the whole phrase is
often reduced to the bare time: <code>じゃ、六時(ろくじ)ね</code>. Second, the
normal way to *ask* about a deadline keeps the `に` in place and adds a verb - 
<code>いつまでに出(だ)せばいいですか</code>,
<code>いつまでにお返事(へんじ)すればよろしいでしょうか</code>. Asking
<code>いつまでですか</code> without the `に` is a different question: it asks how
long something lasts, not when it is due.

## Contrast

### までに vs まで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まで` fills the whole span; `までに` picks one moment inside
it. Ask whether the verb can go on for hours. If it can, `まで`. If it happens and
is over, `までに`.

</div>

This is the whole of the point, and the two are not interchangeable in either
direction.

<pre>
三時(さんじ)<b>まで</b>ここで待(ま)っています。
I'll be waiting here until three.
</pre>

Waiting occupies every minute up to three o'clock, so `まで`. There is no single
instant at which the waiting happens.

<pre>
三時(さんじ)<b>までに</b>ここへ来(き)てください。
Please get here by three.
</pre>

Arriving takes an instant, and any instant before three will do. Say
❌<code>三時(さんじ)まで来(き)てください</code> and you have asked someone to keep
arriving continuously until three.

The mirror error is just as common. ❌<code>三時(さんじ)までに待(ま)っていてください</code>
asks the listener to complete the act of waiting before three - which is not a
thing waiting can do.

### までに vs ～前(まえ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)に</code> reports **order**; `までに` imposes a
**limit**. Both often fit the same sentence, but only `までに` implies that being
late is a failure.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の<b>前(まえ)に</b>資料(しりょう)を読(よ)みました。
I read the material before the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)<b>までに</b>資料(しりょう)を読(よ)んでおいてください。
Please have read the material before the meeting.
</pre>

The first simply tells you which came first. The second sets a deadline and
implies a consequence for missing it, which is why notices, homework instructions
and workplace requests reach for `までに`.

Two mechanical differences. <code>前(まえ)に</code> attaches to a noun with `の` - 
<code>食事(しょくじ)の前(まえ)に</code> - while `までに` attaches to the bare noun:
<code>食事(しょくじ)までに</code>. And <code>前(まえ)に</code> can follow a duration to
mean "ago" - <code>三日(みっか)前(まえ)に</code> - which `までに` can never do.

### までに vs ～中(じゅう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `までに` draws a line and lets you act any time **before** it.
<code>～中(じゅう)に</code> names a period and asks you to act **inside** it.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)<b>中(じゅう)に</b>返事(へんじ)をします。
I'll get back to you before the day is out.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)<b>までに</b>返事(へんじ)をします。
I'll get back to you by tomorrow.
</pre>

<code>今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に</code> is said *on* the day in question and treats
today as a container. <code>明日(あした)までに</code> can be said days in advance and
treats tomorrow as a wall. When the two overlap - 
<code>今日(きょう)までに</code> versus <code>今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に</code> - the
first is what a form says to you in advance, the second is what a colleague says
to you at nine in the morning.

<div class="note">

**Note** - watch the reading of `中`. After a span you can live through it is
じゅう: <code>今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に</code>, <code>今週(こんしゅう)中(じゅう)に</code>,
<code>一日(いちにち)中(じゅう)</code>. After a word naming an activity or a portion
of the day it is ちゅう: <code>午前(ごぜん)中(ちゅう)に</code>,
<code>会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)</code>, <code>仕事(しごと)中(ちゅう)</code>. Same
character, and no rule will save you at the moment you have to say it aloud.

</div>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 五時(ごじ)<b>まで</b>宿題(しゅくだい)を出(だ)してください。 → ✅ 五時(ごじ)<b>までに</b>宿題(しゅくだい)を出(だ)してください。
  Handing something in is momentary, so it needs the deadline particle. Without
  `に` you have asked for continuous handing-in until five.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)がやむ<b>までに</b>、ここで休(やす)んでいましょう。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)がやむ<b>まで</b>、ここで休(やす)んでいましょう。
  Resting goes on throughout, so the span particle is the right one.

- ❌ 家(いえ)に着(つ)い<b>たまでに</b> → ✅ 家(いえ)に着(つ)く<b>までに</b>
  A clause before `までに` is in the dictionary form. It names the moment the
  window shuts, and that moment has not arrived yet.

- ❌ 夏休(なつやす)み<b>までに</b>毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)します。 → ✅ 夏休(なつやす)み<b>まで</b>毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)します。
  <code>毎日(まいにち)</code> spreads the action over the whole period, which is
  exactly what `までに` forbids.

## Practice

**1.** 九時(くじ)____空港(くうこう)に着(つ)かなければなりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

九時(くじ)<b>までに</b>空港(くうこう)に着(つ)かなければなりません。

Arriving is a one-off event, and nine o'clock is the limit it must fall inside.
<code>九時(くじ)まで着(つ)く</code> would ask you to go on arriving all morning.

</details>

**2.** 雨(あめ)がやむ（まで・までに）、この喫茶店(きっさてん)にいましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

雨(あめ)がやむ<b>まで</b>、この喫茶店(きっさてん)にいましょう。

Staying somewhere occupies the whole stretch of time, so it takes the span
particle. `までに` would need a verb that finishes in a moment - for instance
<code>雨(あめ)がやむまでに注文(ちゅうもん)しましょう</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Could you finish this by Wednesday?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

これを水曜日(すいようび)<b>までに</b>終(お)わらせてもらえますか。

`までに` marks Wednesday as the deadline, and any time before it counts as on
time. Turning the request into a question with `もらえますか` is what keeps it
polite; the deadline itself is not softened.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 家(いえ)に帰(かえ)ったまでに、スーパーで買(か)い物(もの)をします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 家(いえ)に帰(かえ)る<b>までに</b>、スーパーで買(か)い物(もの)をします。

The clause naming the deadline goes in the dictionary form. From the point of view
of the shopping, getting home has not happened yet, so た is impossible here.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) レポートは金曜日(きんようび)までに出(だ)します。
(b) レポートは金曜日(きんようび)まで書(か)きます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about a deadline: the report leaves your hands at some single moment no
later than Friday, and it might well be Wednesday. (b) is about a span: you will
be writing continuously up to Friday, and Friday is when you stop - with no
promise that it is finished then.

The particle and the verb move together. Momentary <code>出(だ)す</code> pulls
`までに`; ongoing <code>書(か)く</code> pulls `まで`.

</details>

# ～ごろ

**Meaning** · around, about - attached to a **point** in time and nothing else

`ごろ` blurs a moment on the clock or the calendar: <code>三時(さんじ)ごろ</code>,
<code>来週(らいしゅう)ごろ</code>. It is not a general-purpose "approximately". The
moment you are approximating an *amount* - three hours, five people, a thousand
yen - `ごろ` is the wrong word and `ぐらい` is the right one.

## Formation

`ごろ` is a suffix. It goes straight onto the time word, with nothing in between.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Clock time | Time + ごろ | 三時(さんじ) → 三時(さんじ)ごろ |
| Date, month, year | Noun + ごろ | 五月(ごがつ) → 五月(ごがつ)ごろ |
| Part of the day | Noun + ごろ | 夕方(ゆうがた) → 夕方(ゆうがた)ごろ |
| Question word | 何時(なんじ)／いつ + ごろ | 何時(なんじ)ごろ、いつごろ |
| With に | ごろ（に） - the に is optional | 七時(しちじ)ごろ（に）起(お)きます |
| With other particles | ごろから、ごろまで、ごろには | 九時(くじ)ごろから |
| As an independent noun | ころ | 子(こ)どものころ、あのころ |
| In kanji | 頃(ころ)／頃(ごろ) | 若(わか)い頃(ころ) |
| ❌ Length of time | ❌三時間(さんじかん)ごろ | ✅三時間(さんじかん)ぐらい |
| ❌ Number or amount | ❌五人(ごにん)ごろ | ✅五人(ごにん)ぐらい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask which question the phrase answers. If it answers
<code>いつ</code> ("when?"), use `ごろ`. If it answers <code>どのくらい</code> ("how
much? how long?"), use `ぐらい`. <code>三時(さんじ)</code> is a when;
<code>三時間(さんじかん)</code> is a how-long, and one character is the whole
difference.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `に` sits *after* `ごろ`, never before it:
<code>六時(ろくじ)ごろに</code>, ❌<code>六時(ろくじ)にごろ</code>. And once `ごろ` is
there, `に` is optional - it is dropped more often than not, because an
approximate time is already loose.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)七時(しちじ)<b>ごろ</b>起(お)きます。
I get up around seven every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)<b>ごろ</b>から雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうですよ。
Apparently it's going to rain from around midday.
</pre>

<pre>
何時(なんじ)<b>ごろ</b>そちらに着(つ)きますか。
Roughly what time will you get there?
</pre>

<pre>
桜(さくら)は四月(しがつ)の初(はじ)め<b>ごろ</b>に咲(さ)きます。
The cherry blossom comes out around the beginning of April.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの<b>ころ</b>、この川(かわ)でよく泳(およ)いだものだ。
When I was small I used to swim in this river all the time.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の今(いま)<b>ごろ</b>は、まだ大阪(おおさか)に住(す)んでいました。
This time last year I was still living in Osaka.
</pre>

<pre>
夕方(ゆうがた)<b>ごろ</b>から風(かぜ)が強(つよ)くなるので、洗濯物(せんたくもの)は早(はや)めに取(と)り込(こ)んでください。
The wind picks up from around evening, so bring the washing in early.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の中(なか)<b>ごろ</b>に引(ひ)っ越(こ)す予定(よてい)なので、それまでに荷物(にもつ)をまとめないといけません。
I'm due to move around the middle of next month, so I have to get my things packed before then.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ごろ` is register-neutral and extremely common in speech. It does, however, do
something socially useful: it declines to pin anyone down. Saying
<code>三時(さんじ)ごろ伺(うかが)います</code> promises an afternoon visit without
promising the minute, which is why it turns up in arrangements between friends and
in service Japanese alike. The flip side is that a business appointment that
genuinely starts at three is announced as <code>三時(さんじ)から</code> - putting
`ごろ` on a fixed start time reads as vagueness where precision was expected.

The `ご`/`こ` alternation is not two words. It is one word that voices when it is
stuck straight onto a time expression: <code>今(いま)ごろ</code>,
<code>近(ちか)ごろ</code>, <code>日(ひ)ごろ</code>, <code>六時(ろくじ)ごろ</code>. Left
standing on its own - after `の`, after an adjective, after この／その／あの - it
stays unvoiced: <code>学生(がくせい)のころ</code>, <code>若(わか)いころ</code>,
<code>あのころ</code>. Both are written <code>頃(ころ)</code> when written in kanji,
though modern text usually leaves them in kana.

<code>今(いま)ごろ</code> deserves a line of its own, because it has an idiomatic
second life. Literally "around now", it is also the word for *too late*:
<code>今(いま)ごろ来(き)ても、もう終(お)わったよ</code> - "turning up now, are
you? It's over." Tone carries the difference, and the sting is real.

Finally, `ごろ` has quietly built a small family of vocabulary meaning "the right
time for": <code>見(み)ごろ</code> (best time to see it),
<code>食(た)べごろ</code> (perfectly ripe), <code>手(て)ごろ</code> (a reasonable
price or size). <code>桜(さくら)は今(いま)が見(み)ごろです</code> is what the
sign in the park says.

## Contrast

### ごろ vs ぐらい・くらい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ごろ` approximates a **point**; `ぐらい` approximates an
**amount**. Clock times, dates and eras take `ごろ`. Durations, counts, prices,
distances and sizes take `ぐらい`.

</div>

<pre>
三時(さんじ)<b>ごろ</b>会社(かいしゃ)を出(で)ます。
I'll leave the office at about three.
</pre>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>かかりました。
It took about three hours.
</pre>

The pair differ by one character, and that character decides everything: `三時` is
a place on the clock, `三時間` is a length. Everything countable behaves like the
second one - <code>五人(ごにん)ぐらい</code>, <code>千円(せんえん)ぐらい</code>,
<code>十(じゅう)キロぐらい</code> - and none of them will take `ごろ`.

<div class="note">

**Note** - you will hear <code>三時(さんじ)ぐらいに</code> in casual speech, and
speakers do not treat it as an error any more. `ごろ` is still the standard choice
for a clock time and the one an exam expects. What is unambiguously clumsy is
stacking them: <code>三時(さんじ)ごろぐらい</code> says the same thing twice.

</div>

### ごろ vs 約(やく)・だいたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ごろ` is a **suffix** and goes after the time.
<code>約(やく)</code> is a **prefix** on a number and belongs to written and
formal Japanese. `だいたい` is an ordinary **adverb** and floats freely.

</div>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)は<b>約(やく)</b>十分(じゅっぷん)遅(おく)れています。
The train is running about ten minutes late.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は<b>だいたい</b>五時(ごじ)<b>ごろ</b>終(お)わります。
The meeting will finish around five-ish.
</pre>

<code>約(やく)</code> attaches to a quantity, so it patterns with `ぐらい`, not
with `ごろ`; you see it in timetables, reports and announcements, and it sounds
stiff in conversation. `だいたい` is happy in front of almost anything and can sit
in the same sentence as `ごろ`, as the second example shows - the two are not in
competition. In written notices you will also meet
<code>前後(ぜんご)</code>: <code>三時(さんじ)前後(ぜんご)に到着(とうちゃく)の予定(よてい)</code>,
"scheduled to arrive around three", which is `ごろ` in a suit.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 二時間(にじかん)<b>ごろ</b>待(ま)ちました。 → ✅ 二時間(にじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>待(ま)ちました。
  <code>二時間(にじかん)</code> is a length of time, not a point on the clock.
  Lengths take `ぐらい`.

- ❌ 六時(ろくじ)<b>にごろ</b>帰(かえ)ります。 → ✅ 六時(ろくじ)<b>ごろに</b>帰(かえ)ります。
  `ごろ` bonds to the time word first; any particle comes after it. The `に` is
  optional, but it can only stand in that order.

- ❌ 子(こ)どもの<b>ごろ</b>は野球(やきゅう)が好(す)きでした。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもの<b>ころ</b>は野球(やきゅう)が好(す)きでした。
  After `の` the word is standing on its own as a noun, so it does not voice.

- ❌ 十時(じゅうじ)<b>ごろぐらいに</b>寝(ね)ます。 → ✅ 十時(じゅうじ)<b>ごろ</b>寝(ね)ます。
  Both words mean "about". Using them together is not wrong so much as sloppy - 
  pick one.

## Practice

**1.** 映画(えいが)は八時(はちじ)____終(お)わると思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

映画(えいが)は八時(はちじ)<b>ごろ</b>終(お)わると思(おも)います。

Eight o'clock is a point on the clock, and the sentence is guessing at *when*, so
`ごろ` is the suffix that fits. Nothing here is being measured, so `ぐらい` has no
job to do.

</details>

**2.** 駅(えき)まで歩(ある)いて十五分(じゅうごふん)（ごろ・ぐらい）かかります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)まで歩(ある)いて十五分(じゅうごふん)<b>ぐらい</b>かかります。

<code>十五分(じゅうごふん)</code> here is a duration - how long the walk takes - and
durations take `ぐらい`. It would only be `ごろ` if it named a time on the clock,
as in <code>十時(じゅうじ)十五分(じゅうごふん)ごろ</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I think I'll get home around eleven tonight."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今夜(こんや)は十一時(じゅういちじ)<b>ごろ</b>家(いえ)に着(つ)くと思(おも)います。

Eleven is a clock time, so `ごろ` blurs it. The `に` after `ごろ` would not be
wrong, but with an approximate time it is usually left out.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 夏休(なつやす)みは一(いっ)か月(げつ)ごろあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 夏休(なつやす)みは一(いっ)か月(げつ)<b>ぐらい</b>あります。

<code>一(いっ)か月(げつ)</code> measures how long the holiday lasts, so it needs
`ぐらい`. Compare <code>八月(はちがつ)ごろ</code> - "around August" - where the same
kind of word names a position in the year instead.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 三時(さんじ)に伺(うかが)います。
(b) 三時(さんじ)ごろ伺(うかが)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) commits to three o'clock. It is what you say when the appointment is fixed and
someone is expecting you at that minute - and it means being late is a broken
promise.

(b) commits to the middle of the afternoon and leaves yourself ten or fifteen
minutes of room. It is friendlier and lower-risk, and it is the normal choice for
a casual visit. Said about a formal appointment, though, it can sound as if you
have not really committed.

</details>

# ～おきに

**Meaning** · at intervals of - leaving a gap of X between one occurrence and the next

`おきに` describes something that repeats with a fixed **gap** in between. The word
to hold on to is *gap*, not *every*, because that single idea explains why
<code>十分(じゅっぷん)おきに</code> means "every ten minutes" while
<code>一日(いちにち)おきに</code> means "every **other** day". English translates them
differently; Japanese is saying the same thing both times.

## Formation

`おきに` is a suffix and needs a **number** in front of it. It attaches straight to
a quantity - of time, of distance, or of countable things.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Minutes, hours | Quantity + おきに | 十分(じゅっぷん) → 十分(じゅっぷん)おきに |
| Days, weeks, months, years | Quantity + おきに | 一日(いちにち) → 一日(いちにち)おきに |
| Distance | Quantity + おきに | 五(ご)メートル → 五(ご)メートルおきに |
| Countable things | Counter + おきに | 一(ひと)つ → 一(ひと)つおきに |
| People, seats, rows | Counter + おきに | 一人(ひとり)おきに、一列(いちれつ)おきに |
| Before a noun | Quantity + おき + <b>の</b> | 一日(いちにち)おきの練習(れんしゅう) |
| Vague quantity | 数(すう)～ + おきに | 数分(すうふん)おきに、数(すう)メートルおきに |
| ❌ Bare unit | ❌日(ひ)おきに | ✅一日(いちにち)おきに |
| ❌ Before a noun with な | ❌一日(いちにち)おきな | ✅一日(いちにち)おきの |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `おきに` measures the **space between** two occurrences, never
the length of the cycle. <code>一日(いちにち)おきに</code> leaves one whole day
empty: Monday, Wednesday, Friday.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - why do the English translations diverge? Because of what the unit is.
With days, weeks and years, and with seats and people, the unit is a **container**
the event sits inside, so a gap of one means one whole container left empty:
<code>一(ひと)つおきに座(すわ)る</code> is one seat used, one seat free. With
minutes, hours and metres, the event takes no measurable time or space of its own,
so a gap of ten minutes simply puts the events ten minutes apart. One definition,
two arithmetics.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
バスは十分(じゅっぷん)<b>おきに</b>来(き)ます。
The buses come every ten minutes.
</pre>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)は一日(いちにち)<b>おきに</b>飲(の)んでください。
Take this medicine every other day.
</pre>

<pre>
一(ひと)つ<b>おきに</b>席(せき)に座(すわ)ってください。
Please sit in every other seat.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)には一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>おきに</b>電話(でんわ)しています。
I phone my grandmother every other week.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)の両側(りょうがわ)に、五(ご)メートル<b>おきに</b>木(き)が植(う)えてあります。
Trees are planted every five metres on both sides of the road.
</pre>

<pre>
この会(かい)は二年(にねん)<b>おきに</b>東京(とうきょう)で開(ひら)かれます。
This conference is held in Tokyo every third year.
</pre>

<pre>
係(かかり)の人(ひと)が三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>おきに</b>見回(みまわ)りに来(き)ますので、何(なに)かあったら声(こえ)をかけてください。
A member of staff comes round every thirty minutes, so give them a shout if anything's wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の春(はる)から一日(いちにち)<b>おきに</b>ジムに通(かよ)っていて、おかげで体重(たいじゅう)が五(ご)キロ減(へ)りました。
I've been going to the gym every other day since last spring, and I've lost five kilos as a result.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`おきに` is neutral and faintly precise-sounding - it belongs to timetables,
instructions on a packet, and descriptions of routines. Nothing about it is formal
or casual, and it does not change shape in polite speech.

It does have one real-world problem, and you should know about it rather than be
caught by it: with days, weeks and years, **speakers themselves disagree** about
what `おきに` means. By the rule above, <code>三日(みっか)おきに</code> leaves three
days empty and so happens once every four days - but a large minority of native
speakers use it to mean once every three days. Everyone agrees about
<code>一日(いちにち)おき</code> ("every other day"); past that, confidence drops.

The practical consequence is that when it genuinely matters, Japanese avoids the
word. A pharmacist writes <code>二日(ふつか)に一回(いっかい)</code>, a timetable
writes <code>十分(じゅっぷん)間隔(かんかく)</code>, and a coach says
<code>中(なか)一日(いちにち)あけて</code>. If you are giving instructions someone
has to follow, say how many times per period and skip `おきに` entirely. If you are
merely describing a rhythm, `おきに` is exactly right.

Before a noun, use `の`: <code>一日(いちにち)おきの練習(れんしゅう)</code>,
<code>十分(じゅっぷん)おきのアナウンス</code>. It is a noun-like word, not a
な-adjective.

## Contrast

### おきに vs ごとに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ごとに` names the **cycle**; `おきに` names the **gap**. So
<code>三日(みっか)ごとに</code> is day 1, day 4, day 7 - and
<code>三日(みっか)おきに</code> is day 1, day 5, day 9.

</div>

<pre>
二日(ふつか)<b>ごとに</b>水(みず)をやります。
I water them every two days.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)<b>おきに</b>水(みず)をやります。
I water them every other day.
</pre>

Those two sentences describe **the same schedule**, which is the neatest way to
feel the difference: a cycle of two days is a gap of one. Whenever you are unsure,
convert to `ごとに` in your head and count.

With small units of time the two land in the same place - 
<code>十分(じゅっぷん)ごとに</code> and <code>十分(じゅっぷん)おきに</code> both
describe buses ten minutes apart - which is why the distinction seems to vanish
and then reappear the moment you switch to days.

`ごとに` also does something `おきに` cannot: it attaches to ordinary nouns and to
verbs, meaning "each". <code>人(ひと)ごとに考(かんが)え方(かた)が違(ちが)う</code>
("everyone thinks differently"), <code>会(あ)うごとに背(せ)が高(たか)くなる</code>
("he's taller every time I see him"). `おきに` needs a number and always will.

### おきに vs 毎(まい)～

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>毎(まい)</code> means **every single one, no gap**.
`おきに` exists precisely to describe the gaps that <code>毎(まい)</code> denies.

</div>

<pre>
毎週(まいしゅう)日曜日(にちようび)にテニスをします。
I play tennis every Sunday.
</pre>

<pre>
一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>おきに</b>テニスをします。
I play tennis every other week.
</pre>

The mechanical difference is just as important: <code>毎(まい)</code> is a prefix
on a bare unit - <code>毎日(まいにち)</code>, <code>毎週(まいしゅう)</code>,
<code>毎月(まいつき)</code>, <code>毎年(まいとし)</code>,
<code>毎回(まいかい)</code> - and it will not take a number.
❌<code>毎(まい)三日(みっか)</code> does not exist; the Japanese for "every three
days" has to be <code>三日(みっか)ごとに</code> or
<code>二日(ふつか)おきに</code>.

### おきに vs ～に一回(いっかい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～に一回(いっかい)</code> counts occurrences inside a
period and is never ambiguous. When you need to be understood exactly, use it
instead of `おきに`.

</div>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)<b>おきに</b>走(はし)っています。
I go running every other day.
</pre>

<pre>
二日(ふつか)<b>に一回(いっかい)</b>走(はし)っています。
I go running once every two days.
</pre>

Again the same schedule, said two ways. The second phrasing has no gap-versus-cycle
trap in it at all, which is why hospitals, schools and instruction manuals prefer
it. `おきに` is more compact and sounds more like a rhythm you live by; `に一回` is
what you write when someone will be counting.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 毎(まい)三日(みっか)掃除(そうじ)します。 → ✅ 三日(みっか)<b>ごとに</b>掃除(そうじ)します。
  <code>毎(まい)</code> cannot take a number. Use `ごとに` for a cycle, or
  <code>二日(ふつか)おきに</code> for the same schedule expressed as a gap.

- ❌ 一日(いちにち)<b>おきに</b>学校(がっこう)へ行(い)っています。（meaning "every day"） → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)学校(がっこう)へ行(い)っています。
  `おきに` always inserts a gap. If nothing is skipped, the word you want is
  <code>毎日(まいにち)</code>.

- ❌ <b>おきに</b>十分(じゅっぷん)電車(でんしゃ)が来(き)ます。 → ✅ 十分(じゅっぷん)<b>おきに</b>電車(でんしゃ)が来(き)ます。
  `おきに` is a suffix. The quantity always comes first.

- ❌ 一日(いちにち)おき<b>な</b>練習(れんしゅう)はつらいです。 → ✅ 一日(いちにち)おき<b>の</b>練習(れんしゅう)はつらいです。
  It behaves like a noun, so it links to the next noun with `の`, not `な`.

## Practice

**1.** 花(はな)に水(みず)をやるのは____おきに、つまり二日(ふつか)に一回(いっかい)で十分(じゅうぶん)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

花(はな)に水(みず)をやるのは<b>一日(いちにち)</b>おきに…

Once every two days means one whole day is skipped between waterings, and a gap of
one day is <code>一日(いちにち)おきに</code>. The second half of the sentence is the
conversion rule in miniature: a gap of one equals a cycle of two.

</details>

**2.** バスは（十分(じゅっぷん)おきに・毎(まい)十分(じゅっぷん)）出(で)ています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

バスは<b>十分(じゅっぷん)おきに</b>出(で)ています。

<code>毎(まい)</code> attaches only to a bare unit - <code>毎日(まいにち)</code>,
<code>毎朝(まいあさ)</code> - and never to a number, so
❌<code>毎(まい)十分(じゅっぷん)</code> is not a possible word.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Benches are placed every hundred metres along the river."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

川(かわ)に沿(そ)って、百(ひゃく)メートル<b>おきに</b>ベンチが置(お)いてあります。

Distance is a measured quantity, so a gap of a hundred metres simply means the
benches are a hundred metres apart - no skipping involved. `おきに` attaches
straight to the quantity.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 毎(まい)二週間(にしゅうかん)、部長(ぶちょう)と面談(めんだん)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>おきに</b>、部長(ぶちょう)と面談(めんだん)があります。
（or 二週間(にしゅうかん)<b>ごとに</b>）

<code>毎(まい)</code> refuses numbers. Expressed as a gap it is
<code>一週間(いっしゅうかん)おきに</code>; expressed as a cycle it is
<code>二週間(にしゅうかん)ごとに</code>. Both describe the same fortnightly meeting.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 三日(みっか)おきに通(かよ)っています。
(b) 三日(みっか)ごとに通(かよ)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

By the rule, (a) leaves three days empty between visits, so you go once every four
days; (b) names a three-day cycle, so you go once every three days. They are not
the same schedule, and (a) is the sparser one.

In practice, be careful with (a). Speakers vary on how they count `おきに` with
days, and plenty of people would hear (a) as meaning (b). If the number has to be
right, say <code>四日(よっか)に一回(いっかい)</code> and remove the doubt.

</details>

# ～始める

**Meaning** · start doing something; begin to

<code>始(はじ)める</code> welded onto the stem of another verb marks the moment an
action gets under way. It is the plain, unremarkable "start": no surprise, no
drama, and equally usable for something you planned, something you decided on the
spot, and a slow change in the weather.

## Formation

Attach <code>始(はじ)める</code> to the <b>ます-stem</b>. The result is a single
compound verb, and it conjugates as an ordinary る-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + 始(はじ)める | 読(よ)みます → 読(よ)み始(はじ)める |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + 始(はじ)める | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べ始(はじ)める |
| Verb (する) | し + 始(はじ)める | 勉強(べんきょう)する → 勉強(べんきょう)し始(はじ)める |
| Verb (来(く)る) | き + 始(はじ)める | 来(く)る → 来(き)始(はじ)める |
| い-adjective (change) | くなり + 始(はじ)める | 暑(あつ)い → 暑(あつ)くなり始(はじ)める |
| な-adjective (change) | になり + 始(はじ)める | 元気(げんき)だ → 元気(げんき)になり始(はじ)める |
| Conjugation | る-verb | 降(ふ)り始(はじ)めた、降(ふ)り始(はじ)めている |
| Object particle | unchanged from the first verb | 本(ほん)<b>を</b>読(よ)み始(はじ)める |
| Event noun | Noun + <b>を</b> + 始(はじ)める | 会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)める |
| Intransitive twin | Noun + <b>が</b> + 始(はじ)まる | 会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる |
| ❌ | ❌ dictionary form + 始(はじ)める | ❌読(よ)む始(はじ)める |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the compound keeps the particles of the **first** verb, not of
<code>始(はじ)める</code>. <code>本(ほん)を読(よ)む</code> becomes
<code>本(ほん)を読(よ)み始(はじ)める</code>; nothing moves and nothing is added.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>始(はじ)める</code> wants a verb that **lasts**. An action over in
an instant resists it: ❌<code>着(つ)き始(はじ)める</code> of one person arriving
is odd, because there is no process to begin. It becomes fine the moment the
subject is plural, though, since many arrivals spread out over time:
<code>お客(きゃく)さんが着(つ)き始(はじ)めた</code> is perfectly natural.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あ、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>始(はじ)め</b>ましたね。
Oh, it's started raining.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)からギターを習(なら)い<b>始(はじ)め</b>ました。
I took up the guitar last year.
</pre>

<pre>
六時(ろくじ)になったので、夕(ゆう)ごはんを作(つく)り<b>始(はじ)め</b>ます。
It's six, so I'm going to start making dinner.
</pre>

<pre>
五月(ごがつ)に入(はい)って、朝(あさ)から暑(あつ)くなり<b>始(はじ)め</b>た。
Now that it's May, it's started being hot from the morning on.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちが一人(ひとり)ずつ教室(きょうしつ)に入(はい)り<b>始(はじ)め</b>た。
The children started filing into the classroom one by one.
</pre>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)し<b>始(はじ)め</b>てから、もう三年(さんねん)になります。
It's been three years now since I started studying Japanese.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)し<b>始(はじ)め</b>たら止(と)まらない人(ひと)なので、時間(じかん)に気(き)をつけてくださいね。
Once he starts talking he doesn't stop, so keep an eye on the clock.
</pre>

<pre>
退院(たいいん)した祖父(そふ)は、先週(せんしゅう)から少(すこ)しずつ歩(ある)き<b>始(はじ)め</b>ました。
My grandfather is out of hospital and started walking a little from last week.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Compound verbs of this shape are register-neutral. <code>書(か)き始(はじ)める</code>
is as ordinary in a text message as in a report, and the politeness of the
sentence is decided at the very end - <code>書(か)き始(はじ)めた</code> versus
<code>書(か)き始(はじ)めました</code>.

What makes <code>始(はじ)める</code> the workhorse is that it has no attitude. It
does not say the start was sudden, welcome, overdue or surprising; it just puts a
mark on the timeline. That neutrality is also why it can appear in forms its rival
<code>～出(だ)す</code> cannot: you can request one
(<code>書(か)き始(はじ)めてください</code>), propose one
(<code>食(た)べ始(はじ)めましょう</code>), or plan one
(<code>来月(らいげつ)から通(かよ)い始(はじ)めます</code>).

With する-nouns there are two routes and they are not quite the same.
<code>勉強(べんきょう)し始(はじ)める</code> treats the studying as an activity
that has now begun; <code>勉強(べんきょう)を始(はじ)める</code> treats it as a
thing you have taken up. For events with a fixed shape - a meeting, a lesson, a
ceremony - only the second works: you say
<code>会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)めます</code>, never ❌<code>会議(かいぎ)し始(はじ)めます</code>.

In speech you will also hear the whole idea carried by
<code>始(はじ)めた</code> on its own - <code>もう始(はじ)めた?</code> - where
context supplies the verb. That is ellipsis, not a different pattern.

## Contrast

### ～始(はじ)める vs ～出(だ)す

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>始(はじ)める</code> is a **neutral** start;
<code>出(だ)す</code> is a **sudden, unplanned** one. If you chose to do it, or
you are asking someone else to, it has to be <code>始(はじ)める</code>.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から水泳(すいえい)を習(なら)い<b>始(はじ)め</b>ます。
I'm starting swimming lessons next month.
</pre>

<pre>
後(うし)ろの犬(いぬ)が急(きゅう)に走(はし)り<b>出(だ)し</b>た。
The dog behind us suddenly took off running.
</pre>

The first is a decision announced in advance, and
❌<code>習(なら)い出(だ)します</code> would be strange - you cannot schedule a
surprise. The second is an event nobody controlled, which is exactly
<code>出(だ)す</code>'s territory.

The clearest test is the form of the verb. Requests, invitations and volitionals
are closed to <code>出(だ)す</code>:
❌<code>書(か)き出(だ)してください</code>, ❌<code>歌(うた)い出(だ)そう</code>.
Their <code>始(はじ)める</code> versions are unremarkable.

### ～始(はじ)める vs 始(はじ)まる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>始(はじ)める</code> needs somebody doing the starting;
<code>始(はじ)まる</code> is the thing starting by itself. The pair splits exactly
as <code>開(あ)ける</code>／<code>開(あ)く</code> does.

</div>

<pre>
九時(くじ)にミーティングを<b>始(はじ)め</b>ます。
We'll start the meeting at nine. (someone is starting it)
</pre>

<pre>
ミーティングは九時(くじ)に<b>始(はじ)まり</b>ます。
The meeting starts at nine. (that's the schedule)
</pre>

Note the particles: <code>を</code> with <code>始(はじ)める</code>,
<code>が</code>／<code>は</code> with <code>始(はじ)まる</code>. The compound suffix
built on the first of these is the only one that exists - there is **no**
❌<code>読(よ)み始(はじ)まる</code>. If you want to say a process got under way
on its own, either use <code>始(はじ)める</code> anyway
(<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り始(はじ)めた</code>) or reach for
<code>出(だ)す</code>.

### ～始(はじ)める vs ～てくる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>始(はじ)める</code> points at the **starting line**;
`～てくる` traces a change **moving towards now**. Both fit gradual change, and
they emphasise different halves of it.

</div>

<pre>
葉(は)が赤(あか)くなり<b>始(はじ)め</b>ました。
The leaves have started turning red.
</pre>

<pre>
葉(は)が赤(あか)くなって<b>きました</b>。
The leaves have been turning red.
</pre>

The first says the process has a beginning and we have just passed it. The second
says the change has been accumulating and here is where it has got to. In everyday
speech about weather and seasons, `～てくる` is the more common of the two, and the
two happily combine: <code>寒(さむ)くなり始(はじ)めてきた</code> is heard, though
it is doing the job twice over.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>始(はじ)める</b> → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)み<b>始(はじ)める</b>
  The suffix attaches to the ます-stem, never to the dictionary form:
  読(よ)みます → 読(よ)み + 始(はじ)める.

- ❌ 手紙(てがみ)を書(か)き<b>始(はじ)まり</b>ました。 → ✅ 手紙(てがみ)を書(か)き<b>始(はじ)め</b>ました。
  <code>始(はじ)まる</code> is intransitive and cannot take the `を` object.
  The compound suffix is always <code>始(はじ)める</code>.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)から運動(うんどう)<b>始(はじ)め</b>ます。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)から運動(うんどう)<b>を</b>始(はじ)めます。／運動(うんどう)<b>し</b>始(はじ)めます。
  A する-noun needs either `を` in front of the independent verb, or `し` to make
  the compound. It cannot sit bare against <code>始(はじ)める</code>.

- ❌ 寒(さむ)い<b>始(はじ)め</b>ました。 → ✅ 寒(さむ)く<b>なり</b>始(はじ)めました。
  Adjectives have no ます-stem. Turn the adjective into the verb of change first - 
  <code>寒(さむ)くなる</code> - and attach the suffix to that.

## Practice

**1.** 公園(こうえん)の桜(さくら)が咲(さ)き____ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

公園(こうえん)の桜(さくら)が咲(さ)き<b>始(はじ)め</b>ました。

<code>咲(さ)きます</code> gives the stem <code>咲(さ)き</code>, and
<code>始(はじ)める</code> bolts straight onto it. Blossom opening over several days
is a gradual process, which is exactly what this suffix wants.

</details>

**2.** 来年(らいねん)から新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を（始(はじ)めます・始(はじ)まります）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来年(らいねん)から新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を<b>始(はじ)め</b>ます。

The `を` tells you the job is being acted on by somebody, and only the transitive
<code>始(はじ)める</code> takes an object. With
<code>始(はじ)まります</code> the sentence would need `が`:
<code>新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)が始(はじ)まります</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I started studying Japanese three years ago."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三年(さんねん)前(まえ)に日本語(にほんご)を<b>勉強(べんきょう)し始(はじ)め</b>ました。

For a する-noun the stem is `し`, so the compound is
<code>勉強(べんきょう)し始(はじ)める</code>. The `を` still belongs to
<code>日本語(にほんご)</code> and does not move.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: みんなそろいましたから、昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べる始(はじ)めましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ みんなそろいましたから、昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)<b>べ始(はじ)め</b>ましょう。

The dictionary form cannot carry the suffix. Take the ます-stem
<code>食(た)べ</code> and attach <code>始(はじ)める</code> to it; the invitation
ending `ましょう` then goes on the compound as a whole.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) みんなが歩(ある)き始(はじ)めた。
(b) みんなが歩(ある)き出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply records that the walking started. It fits a group that had been waiting
for a signal, or a hike that begins at the planned hour - nothing about it is
remarkable.

(b) adds abruptness. Something set them off, or they moved before you expected it,
and the sentence carries a flicker of surprise. That extra colour is why
<code>出(だ)す</code> cannot be used to announce your own plans or to ask someone
to get going.

</details>

# ～出す

**Meaning** · suddenly start doing; burst out doing

<code>出(だ)す</code> on the stem of another verb says the action **broke out**.
Where <code>～始(はじ)める</code> merely marks a beginning,
<code>～出(だ)す</code> adds abruptness: nobody saw it coming, nobody arranged
it, and often nobody could have stopped it.

## Formation

Attach <code>出(だ)す</code> to the <b>ます-stem</b>. The compound is a う-verb, so
it conjugates like <code>話(はな)す</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + 出(だ)す | 降(ふ)ります → 降(ふ)り出(だ)す |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + 出(だ)す | 逃(に)げます → 逃(に)げ出(だ)す |
| Verb (する) | し + 出(だ)す | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)し出(だ)す |
| Conjugation | う-verb | 降(ふ)り出(だ)した、降(ふ)り出(だ)して、降(ふ)り出(だ)します |
| Object particle | unchanged from the first verb | 文句(もんく)<b>を</b>言(い)い出(だ)す |
| Often paired with | 急(きゅう)に、いきなり、突然(とつぜん) | 急(きゅう)に笑(わら)い出(だ)す |
| ❌ Request | ❌走(はし)り出(だ)してください | ✅走(はし)り始(はじ)めてください |
| ❌ Invitation / volitional | ❌歌(うた)い出(だ)そう | ✅歌(うた)い始(はじ)めよう |
| ❌ Instantaneous verb | ❌消(き)え出(だ)す | ✅急(きゅう)に消(き)える |
| ❌ | ❌ dictionary form + 出(だ)す | ❌読(よ)む出(だ)す |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>出(だ)す</code> removes the plan. Because of that, it
cannot appear in any form that assumes control: no ください, no ましょう, no
volitional, and no announcement of what you intend to do next week.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - like <code>始(はじ)める</code>, this suffix needs a verb with some
duration. Something that is over in an instant has no onset to mark:
❌<code>電気(でんき)が消(き)え出(だ)した</code>. Say
<code>急(きゅう)に消(き)えた</code> instead, and keep
<code>出(だ)す</code> for things that go on once they have started - rain,
crying, running, arguing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
赤(あか)ちゃんが急(きゅう)に<b>泣(な)き出(だ)し</b>た。
The baby suddenly burst into tears.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)を見(み)たら、雪(ゆき)が<b>降(ふ)り出(だ)し</b>ていた。
I looked outside and it had started snowing.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)の話(はなし)がおもしろくて、クラスのみんなが<b>笑(わら)い出(だ)し</b>た。
The teacher's story was so funny the whole class burst out laughing.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが<b>動(うご)き出(だ)し</b>てから、かばんを忘(わす)れたことに気(き)がついた。
It was only after the bus pulled away that I realised I'd left my bag behind.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)が何(なに)も言(い)わずに<b>走(はし)り出(だ)し</b>たので、みんな驚(おどろ)きました。
She broke into a run without a word, and everyone was taken aback.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かだった教室(きょうしつ)が、ベルの音(おと)で一度(いちど)に<b>騒(さわ)ぎ出(だ)し</b>た。
The classroom had been silent, and at the bell it erupted all at once.
</pre>

<pre>
夜中(よなか)に隣(となり)の犬(いぬ)が<b>鳴(な)き出(だ)し</b>て、朝(あさ)まで眠(ねむ)れませんでした。
The dog next door started barking in the night and I couldn't sleep till morning.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わりそうになったころ、田中(たなか)さんが急(きゅう)に反対(はんたい)だと<b>言(い)い出(だ)し</b>て、また一時間(いちじかん)かかりました。
Just as the meeting was winding down, Tanaka suddenly came out against it, and it took another hour.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The suffix is register-neutral - it is as at home in a novel as in a complaint to
a friend - but it is never *emotionally* neutral. Something about the event was
unexpected, and the speaker is registering that. This is why
<code>出(だ)す</code> collects the same adverbs over and over:
<code>急(きゅう)に</code>, <code>いきなり</code>, <code>突然(とつぜん)</code>.

The loss of control has one consequence learners rarely anticipate: used about
your own deliberate action, <code>出(だ)す</code> sounds as if you surprised
yourself. <code>気(き)がついたら書(か)き出(だ)していた</code> ("I looked up
and I was already writing") is a good sentence; ❌
<code>明日(あした)から書(か)き出(だ)します</code> is not, because you cannot
schedule your own outburst.

<code>言(い)い出(だ)す</code> deserves separate mention. It means "to come
out with", "to be the one who raises it", and it carries a whiff of blame:
<code>言(い)い出(だ)したのは君(きみ)だよ</code> - "you're the one who brought
it up." That is the suffix doing exactly its usual job, applied to speech.

Finally, be aware that a handful of very common verbs ending in
<code>出(だ)す</code> are fully lexicalised and have nothing to do with
suddenness: <code>思(おも)い出(だ)す</code> (remember),
<code>取(と)り出(だ)す</code> (take out),
<code>引(ひ)き出(だ)す</code> (draw out),
<code>貸(か)し出(だ)す</code> (lend out),
<code>見(み)つけ出(だ)す</code> (track down). You learn these as words, not as
this pattern.

## Contrast

### ～出(だ)す vs ～始(はじ)める

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same slot, different attitude.
<code>始(はじ)める</code> is available in every form of the verb;
<code>出(だ)す</code> is available only where nobody is in charge.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)から朝(あさ)のジョギングを<b>始(はじ)め</b>ます。
I'm taking up jogging in the mornings from next week.
</pre>

<pre>
信号(しんごう)が青(あお)になって、車(くるま)が一斉(いっせい)に<b>走(はし)り出(だ)し</b>た。
The lights went green and the cars all pulled away together.
</pre>

The first announces a plan; <code>出(だ)す</code> cannot go there. The second
describes something that simply happened, and
<code>走(はし)り始(はじ)めた</code> would drain the moment of its snap.

When the same verb takes either suffix, the difference is a threshold.
<code>泣(な)き始(はじ)めた</code> reports that crying commenced;
<code>泣(な)き出(だ)した</code> shows you the face crumpling.

### ～出(だ)す vs 急(きゅう)に・いきなり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>急(きゅう)に</code> is an **adverb** that can modify
any event. <code>出(だ)す</code> is a **suffix** that can only mark a beginning.
They stack, and neither replaces the other.

</div>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に電気(でんき)が消(き)えた。
The lights suddenly went out.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)り出(だ)し</b>た。
It suddenly started to rain.
</pre>

Going out is instantaneous, so there is no onset for
<code>出(だ)す</code> to mark and only the adverb is possible. Raining lasts, so
both work - and the second sentence uses both at once, which is completely normal
and not redundant: the adverb says it was sudden, the suffix says this was the
*start* of something that then went on.

### 出(だ)す "suddenly begin" vs 出(だ)す "outwards"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the first verb is about **handling or moving something**,
<code>出(だ)す</code> is probably the literal "out". If it is about an
**activity that can start**, it is the aspect marker.

</div>

<pre>
ポケットからかぎを<b>取(と)り出(だ)し</b>た。
I took the key out of my pocket.
</pre>

<pre>
犬(いぬ)が庭(にわ)から<b>逃(に)げ出(だ)し</b>た。
The dog got out of the garden and ran off.
</pre>

Nothing in the first sentence is sudden - the key came *out*, and that is all the
suffix is contributing. The second sits on the border and carries a little of
both: escaping is outward motion, and it is also something that happened without
warning.

The one to watch is <code>思(おも)い出(だ)す</code>. It means *remember*, not
"suddenly begin to think" - the memory comes out, it does not start. Treat it as a
single word.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ みなさん、そろそろ歌(うた)い<b>出(だ)し</b>てください。 → ✅ みなさん、そろそろ歌(うた)い<b>始(はじ)め</b>てください。
  A request assumes the listener is in control, which is precisely what
  <code>出(だ)す</code> denies.

- ❌ 来年(らいねん)からピアノを習(なら)い<b>出(だ)し</b>ます。 → ✅ 来年(らいねん)からピアノを習(なら)い<b>始(はじ)め</b>ます。
  You cannot schedule a surprise. Plans announced in advance take
  <code>始(はじ)める</code>.

- ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>出(だ)す</b> → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)み<b>出(だ)す</b>
  The suffix goes on the ます-stem, exactly as
  <code>始(はじ)める</code> does.

- ❌ 急(きゅう)に電気(でんき)が消(き)え<b>出(だ)し</b>た。 → ✅ 急(きゅう)に電気(でんき)が消(き)えた。
  <code>消(き)える</code> happens in an instant, so it has no beginning for the
  suffix to mark. The adverb alone carries the suddenness.

**1.** 話(はなし)を聞(き)いたとき、彼(かれ)は急(きゅう)に笑(わら)い____た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は急(きゅう)に笑(わら)い<b>出(だ)し</b>た。

Laughing is something that starts and then continues, and
<code>急(きゅう)に</code> tells you it was unplanned - both conditions for
<code>出(だ)す</code>. <code>笑(わら)い始(はじ)めた</code> is possible but
flatter; it reports a beginning without the burst.

</details>

**2.** 来月(らいげつ)から新(あたら)しい習(なら)い事(ごと)を（出(だ)します・始(はじ)めます）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来月(らいげつ)から新(あたら)しい習(なら)い事(ごと)を<b>始(はじ)め</b>ます。

This is a plan, stated in advance, about your own action.
<code>出(だ)す</code> is blocked in exactly that situation, because it means the
action broke out of its own accord.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "As soon as the film started, the person next to me began talking."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The talking was neither planned by the speaker nor welcome, and it went on - the
classic conditions for <code>出(だ)す</code>. Note that
<code>始(はじ)まる</code> earlier in the sentence is the ordinary intransitive

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 準備(じゅんび)ができましたので、そろそろ食(た)べ出(だ)しましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 準備(じゅんび)ができましたので、そろそろ食(た)べ<b>始(はじ)め</b>ましょう。

`ましょう` is an invitation, and an invitation cannot be built on a suffix that
means "it happened without anyone deciding". Swap in
<code>始(はじ)める</code>, which is free in every form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子(こ)どもが泣(な)き始(はじ)めた。
(b) 子(こ)どもが泣(な)き出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) records the fact: at some point the crying began. It fits a calm account - a
nurse writing notes, or a parent explaining what happened at what time.

(b) puts you in the room. It says the crying broke out, probably without warning
and probably to everyone's dismay. Both are correct Japanese about the same event;
(b) is the one with a reaction built into it, which is also why it cannot be used
to announce a plan.

</details>

# 急に

**Meaning** · suddenly, all at once - and, of an arrangement, at short notice

<code>急(きゅう)に</code> marks a change that arrived with no warning. Its second
job is quieter and just as common: applied to plans and appointments it means
*unexpectedly, at the last minute*, which is why it opens so many apologies.

## Usage

<code>急(きゅう)に</code> is the adverbial form of the な-adjective
<code>急(きゅう)だ</code>. That matters in practice, because in front of a noun it
has to change shape: <code>急(きゅう)な用事(ようじ)</code> ("something urgent
that has come up"), <code>急(きゅう)な話(はなし)</code> ("short notice"),
<code>急(きゅう)な坂(さか)</code> ("a steep hill" - the same word, applied to a
slope). Only the `に` form modifies a verb.

Its position in the sentence is free; most often it sits immediately before the
verb, or at the head of the clause when it colours the whole event. It clusters
around three kinds of predicate:

- **change** - <code>急(きゅう)に暗(くら)くなる</code>,
  <code>急(きゅう)に静(しず)かになる</code>,
  <code>急(きゅう)に変(か)わる</code>
- **starting and stopping** - <code>急(きゅう)に降(ふ)り出(だ)す</code>,
  <code>急(きゅう)に止(と)まる</code>, <code>急(きゅう)に消(き)える</code>
- **arrangements** - <code>急(きゅう)に決(き)まる</code>,
  <code>急(きゅう)に休(やす)む</code>,
  <code>急(きゅう)に呼(よ)ばれる</code>

It does not take degree adverbs. ❌<code>とても急(きゅう)に</code> is not said - 
suddenness is not a quantity you can have more of.

The word to keep separate is <code>急(いそ)いで</code>, the て-form of the verb
<code>急(いそ)ぐ</code>. Same kanji, different reading, opposite kind of meaning:
<code>急(きゅう)に</code> is about the event surprising you,
<code>急(いそ)いで</code> is about you hurrying.

## Examples

<pre>
空(そら)が<b>急(きゅう)に</b>暗(くら)くなった。
The sky went dark all of a sudden.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が<b>急(きゅう)に</b>止(と)まって、びっくりしました。
The train stopped suddenly and gave me a fright.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>急(きゅう)に</b>用事(ようじ)ができたので、今日(きょう)の約束(やくそく)はキャンセルさせてください。
Something's come up out of the blue, so please let me cancel today's plans.
</pre>

<pre>
先月(せんげつ)まで元気(げんき)だった祖父(そふ)が<b>急(きゅう)に</b>入院(にゅういん)することになって、家族(かぞく)みんなで病院(びょういん)に通(かよ)っています。
My grandfather was fine until last month and has suddenly ended up in hospital, so the whole family has been going in to see him.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>急(きゅう)に</code> is register-neutral and extremely frequent. It is
equally at home in a weather report and in a text to a friend, and it carries no
politeness of its own.

Its most useful social function is as the opening of an apology. When you have to
change something at the last minute, Japanese says so first and explains
afterwards: <code>急(きゅう)にすみません</code> among friends,
<code>急(きゅう)で申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが</code> at work,
<code>急(きゅう)なお願(ねが)いで恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが</code> in a formal
email. In all three the word is doing the same thing: acknowledging that the other
person has had no time to prepare. Leaving it out makes the same request sound
inconsiderate.

Watch the shape when a noun follows. <code>急(きゅう)だ</code> is a な-adjective,
so it is <code>急(きゅう)な仕事(しごと)</code>,
<code>急(きゅう)な変更(へんこう)</code>,
<code>急(きゅう)な出張(しゅっちょう)</code>. The related noun
<code>急(いそ)ぎ</code> exists too and behaves differently:
<code>急(いそ)ぎの用事(ようじ)</code> joins with `の`, and it means "a job that
must be done fast" rather than "a job that came up unexpectedly".

## Contrast

### 急(きゅう)に vs 突然(とつぜん)・いきなり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three mean "suddenly".
<code>急(きゅう)に</code> is the everyday default;
<code>突然(とつぜん)</code> is stronger and more written;
`いきなり` complains that there was no lead-up at all.

</div>

<pre>
<b>急(きゅう)に</b>会議(かいぎ)が入(はい)りました。
A meeting's been put in my diary at short notice.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>突然(とつぜん)</b>、部屋(へや)の電気(でんき)が消(き)えた。
All at once the lights in the room went out.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いきなり</b>大声(おおごえ)で話(はな)しかけられて、こわかった。
Someone started shouting at me out of nowhere, and it was frightening.
</pre>

<code>突然(とつぜん)</code> is dramatic. It suits accidents, disappearances and
news, and it reads as slightly literary in ordinary chat. `いきなり` is the most
colloquial and focuses on the missing preliminaries - no greeting, no warning, no
run-up - which is why it so often carries irritation.

<code>急(きゅう)に</code> is the only one of the three that comfortably covers
scheduling. <code>急(きゅう)に休(やす)みます</code> is the normal way to say you
are taking unplanned leave; ❌<code>突然(とつぜん)休(やす)みます</code> would
sound as though you had vanished.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 急(きゅう)に来(き)てください。（meaning "come quickly"） → ✅ 急(いそ)いで来(き)てください。／すぐに来(き)てください。
  <code>急(きゅう)に</code> describes an event that surprises people. Telling
  someone to hurry needs <code>急(いそ)いで</code> or `すぐに`.

- ❌ 急(きゅう)に用事(ようじ)があるので、失礼(しつれい)します。 → ✅ 急(きゅう)な用事(ようじ)があるので、失礼(しつれい)します。
  In front of a noun the な-adjective needs its `な`. The `に` form can only modify
  a verb.

- ❌ バスがとても急(きゅう)に止(と)まった。 → ✅ バスが急(きゅう)に止(と)まった。
  Suddenness has no degrees, so `とても` has nothing to intensify. If you want the
  event to sound more violent, change the verb, not the adverb.

## Practice

**1.** 山(やま)の天気(てんき)は____変(か)わるので、上着(うわぎ)を持(も)っていったほうがいいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山(やま)の天気(てんき)は<b>急(きゅう)に</b>変(か)わるので…

<code>変(か)わる</code> is a verb of change, which is where this adverb lives, and
the point of the sentence is that the change gives you no warning. The `に` form is
required because a verb follows.

</details>

**2.** Translate into Japanese: "My plans changed at the last minute, so I couldn't go."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>急(きゅう)に</b>予定(よてい)が変(か)わって、行(い)けなくなりました。

This is the scheduling sense: the change came without notice. `いきなり` would
sound like a complaint about somebody's manners, and
<code>突然(とつぜん)</code> would be dramatic for a diary clash.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 急(きゅう)に出(で)かけました。
(b) 急(いそ)いで出(で)かけました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Same kanji, two different words. (a) is
<code>急(きゅう)に</code> - the leaving was unplanned. Something came up and you
went out when nobody expected you to, and the sentence says nothing about how fast
you moved.

(b) is <code>急(いそ)いで</code>, the て-form of
<code>急(いそ)ぐ</code> - you left **in a hurry**. The trip may have been on your
calendar for weeks; what the sentence reports is that you rushed.

</details>

# ～続ける

**Meaning** · keep doing; go on doing without stopping

<code>続(つづ)ける</code> welded onto a verb stem is the mirror image of
<code>～始(はじ)める</code>. Where that one marks the moment something starts,
this one claims the stretch after it: the action ran on, and it did not break off.
Very often the sentence is also saying that this took some doing.

## Formation

Attach <code>続(つづ)ける</code> to the <b>ます-stem</b>. The compound is a る-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + 続(つづ)ける | 話(はな)します → 話(はな)し続(つづ)ける |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + 続(つづ)ける | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べ続(つづ)ける |
| Verb (する) | し + 続(つづ)ける | 練習(れんしゅう)する → 練習(れんしゅう)し続(つづ)ける |
| Conjugation | る-verb | 待(ま)ち続(つづ)けた、待(ま)ち続(つづ)けて |
| "has been doing" | ～続(つづ)けている | 十年(じゅうねん)働(はたら)き続(つづ)けている |
| Object particle | unchanged from the first verb | 本(ほん)<b>を</b>読(よ)み続(つづ)ける |
| Event noun | Noun + <b>を</b> + 続(つづ)ける | 話(はなし)を続(つづ)ける |
| Intransitive twin | Noun + <b>が</b> + 続(つづ)く | 会議(かいぎ)が続(つづ)く |
| Suffix twin | ます-stem + 続(つづ)く | 降(ふ)り続(つづ)く |
| As an adverb | 続(つづ)けて | 三日(みっか)続(つづ)けて休(やす)む |
| ❌ | ❌ dictionary form + 続(つづ)ける | ❌読(よ)む続(つづ)ける |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>続(つづ)ける</code> is transitive and needs someone
doing the continuing; <code>続(つづ)く</code> is intransitive and describes
something that goes on by itself. The suffix pair splits the same way.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the suffix covers two shapes of continuation, and both are normal. One
is an **unbroken** action: <code>二時間(にじかん)話(はな)し続(つづ)けた</code>.
The other is a habit **kept up** over a long period:
<code>十年(じゅうねん)通(かよ)い続(つづ)けている</code>. What it cannot do is
attach to something over in an instant - ❌<code>着(つ)き続(つづ)ける</code> of
one arrival has nothing to stretch.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は一時間(いちじかん)も<b>話(はな)し続(つづ)け</b>た。
He talked for a solid hour.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)の前(まえ)で三十分(さんじゅっぷん)も<b>待(ま)ち続(つづ)け</b>ました。
I stood waiting outside the station for a full half hour.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ<b>練習(れんしゅう)し続(つづ)け</b>れば、きっと上手(じょうず)になりますよ。
If you keep practising a little every day, you're bound to get good at it.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は六十年(ろくじゅうねん)同(おな)じ店(みせ)で<b>働(はたら)き続(つづ)け</b>ています。
My grandmother has been working in the same shop for sixty years.
</pre>

<pre>
足(あし)が痛(いた)くても、ゴールまで<b>走(はし)り続(つづ)け</b>た。
My legs hurt, but I kept running all the way to the finish.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)が暗(くら)くなっても、兄(あに)は机(つくえ)に向(む)かって<b>書(か)き続(つづ)け</b>ていた。
Even after it got dark outside, my brother stayed at his desk writing.
</pre>

<pre>
野菜(やさい)の値段(ねだん)が<b>上(あ)がり続(つづ)け</b>ているので、買(か)い物(もの)が大変(たいへん)です。
Vegetable prices just keep going up, so shopping is hard work.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が来(こ)なくても、彼女(かのじょ)は毎週(まいしゅう)友(とも)だちに手紙(てがみ)を<b>送(おく)り続(つづ)け</b>ました。
Even though no reply ever came, she went on sending her friend a letter every week.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The suffix is register-neutral, but it is rarely emotionally flat. Because it
insists that something *did not stop*, it tends to pick up either admiration or
exasperation from the surrounding sentence.
<code>夢(ゆめ)を追(お)い続(つづ)ける</code> is a compliment;
<code>文句(もんく)を言(い)い続(つづ)ける</code> is not. That is context, not
grammar - but it is why the word is so common in speeches and so common in
complaints.

The form you will use most is <code>～続(つづ)けている</code>, which is how
Japanese says "have been doing X for N years":
<code>二十年(にじゅうねん)住(す)み続(つづ)けています</code>. The plain past
<code>～続(つづ)けた</code> closes the stretch off, so it belongs to stories about
finished periods.

Two neighbours worth knowing. <code>続(つづ)けて</code> works as a bare adverb
meaning "in a row" or "carry on":
<code>三日(みっか)続(つづ)けて雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った</code>,
<code>どうぞ続(つづ)けてください</code>. And with a する-noun, spoken Japanese
often prefers the independent verb - <code>練習(れんしゅう)を続(つづ)ける</code>
rather than <code>練習(れんしゅう)し続(つづ)ける</code> - especially when the
activity has a name and a shape.

## Contrast

### ～続(つづ)ける vs ～続(つづ)く

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if a person is keeping it going, <code>続(つづ)ける</code>.
If it simply goes on, <code>続(つづ)く</code>. The particles follow: `を` with
the first, `が` with the second.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が三日(みっか)も<b>降(ふ)り続(つづ)い</b>ている。
It's been raining for three days straight.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は夜中(よなか)まで<b>ゲームをし続(つづ)け</b>ていた。
My son carried on playing games until the middle of the night.
</pre>

With weather and other things nobody controls, both suffixes are heard, and
<code>降(ふ)り続(つづ)く</code> is the more usual - it is what a forecast says.
With a human agent only <code>続(つづ)ける</code> is possible; there is no
❌<code>話(はな)し続(つづ)く</code>.

The independent verbs split the same way, and this is the pair to memorise:

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)を<b>続(つづ)け</b>ましょう。
Let's carry on with the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は六時(ろくじ)まで<b>続(つづ)き</b>ました。
The meeting went on until six.
</pre>

### ～続(つづ)ける vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` says the action is **under way**;
<code>続(つづ)ける</code> says it **has not stopped**. The first is a snapshot,
the second is a claim about the whole stretch.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)、レポートを書(か)い<b>ています</b>。
I'm writing the report right now.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)から同(おな)じレポートを<b>書(か)き続(つづ)け</b>ています。
I've been writing the same report since this morning.
</pre>

Both are true of the same moment, and only the second complains. Note also what
`ている` can do that this suffix cannot: it covers habits
(<code>毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っています</code>) and resulting states
(<code>結婚(けっこん)しています</code>). ❌<code>結婚(けっこん)し続(つづ)けています</code>
is not a way to say you are married - it would mean you keep getting married.

### ～続(つづ)ける vs ずっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ずっと` is an **adverb** you drop into any sentence;
<code>続(つづ)ける</code> is a **suffix** that changes the verb. They stack
happily, and `ずっと` reaches places the suffix cannot.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころから<b>ずっと</b>サッカーが好(す)きです。
I've loved football ever since I was a child.
</pre>

<code>好(す)き</code> is not a verb, so there is no stem for
<code>続(つづ)ける</code> to attach to; `ずっと` has no such problem and works with
adjectives, nouns and states alike. It also does duty as "a long way back" - 
<code>ずっと前(まえ)に会(あ)いました</code> - which has nothing to do with
continuing at all.

Where both are available, `ずっと` reports the span while
<code>続(つづ)ける</code> insists on the unbrokenness, and putting them together
is completely normal:
<code>ずっと待(ま)ち続(つづ)けていました</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>続(つづ)ける</b> → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)み<b>続(つづ)ける</b>
  The suffix takes the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)を続(つづ)きます。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)を<b>続(つづ)け</b>ます。／会議(かいぎ)が<b>続(つづ)き</b>ます。
  <code>続(つづ)く</code> is intransitive and cannot take a `を` object. Pick the
  verb that matches the particle.

- ❌ 夜中(よなか)まで勉強(べんきょう)<b>続(つづ)け</b>ました。 → ✅ 夜中(よなか)まで勉強(べんきょう)<b>し続(つづ)け</b>ました。
  A する-noun needs the `し` to make the compound - or use the independent verb
  with `を`: <code>勉強(べんきょう)を続(つづ)けました</code>.

- ❌ この町(まち)に十年(じゅうねん)住(す)み<b>続(つづ)け</b>ます。（meaning "I've lived here ten years"） → ✅ この町(まち)に十年(じゅうねん)住(す)み<b>続(つづ)け</b>ています。
  A stretch that reaches the present needs `ている`. The plain
  <code>続(つづ)けます</code> is a promise about the future.

## Practice

**1.** 三時間(さんじかん)以上(いじょう)歩(ある)き____て、足(あし)が痛(いた)くなりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三時間(さんじかん)以上(いじょう)歩(ある)き<b>続(つづ)け</b>て、足(あし)が痛(いた)くなりました。

The walking went on without a break for over three hours, which is exactly what
this suffix claims. <code>歩(ある)いて</code> alone would report the walking
without saying it never stopped, and the sore feet would lose their explanation.

</details>

**2.** すみません、話(はなし)を（続(つづ)けて・続(つづ)いて）ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

話(はなし)を<b>続(つづ)け</b>てください。

You are asking a person to keep going, and the `を` marks what they are keeping
going - both point to the transitive verb.
<code>続(つづ)く</code> would need <code>話(はなし)が続(つづ)く</code>, "the
conversation carries on by itself", which nobody can be asked to do.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She has been teaching at the same school for twenty years."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)は二十年(にじゅうねん)同(おな)じ学校(がっこう)で<b>教(おし)え続(つづ)け</b>ています。

For a stretch that runs up to the present, the suffix takes `ている`. The plain
past <code>教(おし)え続(つづ)けました</code> would close the period off and
imply she has stopped.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 去年(きょねん)から運動(うんどう)続(つづ)けていますが、なかなか痩(や)せません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 去年(きょねん)から運動(うんどう)<b>を続(つづ)け</b>ています…（or 運動(うんどう)<b>し続(つづ)け</b>ています）

A する-noun cannot sit bare against the suffix. Either insert `を` and use
<code>続(つづ)ける</code> as an independent verb, or insert `し` and build the
compound. Both are natural; the `を` version is the more common in speech.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)っていました。
(b) 一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)ち続(つづ)けました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports that waiting was what you were doing for an hour. It is
neutral, and it would be a perfectly ordinary answer to "what were you doing?"

(b) puts weight on the unbrokenness: you stayed there, you did not give up, and
the hour felt like an hour. It invites sympathy or an apology, which is why it is
so often what you say to the person who was late.

</details>

# ～終わる

**Meaning** · finish doing something; get to the end of it

<code>終(お)わる</code> on a verb stem closes the bracket that
<code>～始(はじ)める</code> opened. It says the action ran its course and reached
its natural end - the book was read to the last page, the meal was eaten, the
letter was written and signed.

## Formation

Attach <code>終(お)わる</code> to the <b>ます-stem</b>. The compound is a う-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + 終(お)わる | 読(よ)みます → 読(よ)み終(お)わる |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + 終(お)わる | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べ終(お)わる |
| Verb (する) | し + 終(お)わる | 掃除(そうじ)する → 掃除(そうじ)し終(お)わる |
| Conjugation | う-verb | 書(か)き終(お)わった、書(か)き終(お)わって |
| Object particle | unchanged from the first verb | 本(ほん)<b>を</b>読(よ)み終(お)わる |
| Deliberate / written twin | ます-stem + 終(お)える | 読(よ)み終(お)える |
| Independent verb | Noun + <b>が</b> + 終(お)わる | 授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わる |
| Independent transitive | Noun + <b>を</b> + 終(お)える／終(お)わらせる | 仕事(しごと)を終(お)わらせる |
| Very common with | ～終(お)わったら、～終(お)わってから | 食(た)べ終(お)わったら |
| ❌ Instantaneous verb | ❌着(つ)き終(お)わる | ✅駅(えき)に着(つ)いた |
| ❌ Uncontrolled event | ❌降(ふ)り終(お)わる | ✅雨(あめ)がやむ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>終(お)わる</code> wants a verb with a beginning, a
middle and a finish line. Reading a book qualifies. Arriving does not: it has no
middle. Raining does not either - it has no finish line, only a stop, and Japanese
uses <code>やむ</code> for that.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the compound looks intransitive but keeps the `を` of the first verb:
<code>本(ほん)を読(よ)み終(お)わりました</code>. Nothing about the object
changes when you bolt <code>終(お)わる</code> on, even though the independent
verb <code>終(お)わる</code> would never take `を` on its own.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ごはんを<b>食(た)べ終(お)わっ</b>たら、お皿(さら)を洗(あら)ってね。
When you've finished eating, wash your plate.
</pre>

<pre>
この本(ほん)はもう<b>読(よ)み終(お)わり</b>ました。
I've finished this book.
</pre>

<pre>
作文(さくぶん)を<b>書(か)き終(お)わっ</b>た人(ひと)から出(だ)してください。
Hand your composition in as soon as you've finished writing it.
</pre>

<pre>
洗濯(せんたく)を<b>し終(お)わっ</b>たら、ベランダに干(ほ)しに行(い)きます。
Once I've finished the washing I'll go and hang it out on the balcony.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)を<b>聞(き)き終(お)わっ</b>てから、ゆっくり考(かんが)えましょう。
Let's hear the whole story out and then think it over properly.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)を最後(さいご)まで<b>見(み)終(お)わっ</b>たときには、もう夜中(よなか)の一時(いちじ)だった。
By the time I'd watched the film to the end it was already one in the morning.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の問題(もんだい)を全部(ぜんぶ)<b>解(と)き終(お)わっ</b>たのに、名前(なまえ)を書(か)くのを忘(わす)れてしまいました。
I got through every question on the exam and then forgot to write my name.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)を<b>片付(かたづ)け終(お)わっ</b>たら、近(ちか)くの喫茶店(きっさてん)でコーヒーでも飲(の)みませんか。
When we've finished putting everything away, shall we get a coffee somewhere nearby?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The suffix is register-neutral and enormously frequent, above all in the form
<code>～終(お)わったら</code>, which is how Japanese sequences the tasks of an
ordinary day.

Its close relative <code>～終(お)える</code> means the same thing with a
different feel. <code>終(お)える</code> is genuinely transitive and sounds more
deliberate and slightly more written: <code>読(よ)み終(お)える</code> suggests
you set out to get through the thing and did.
<code>読(よ)み終(お)わる</code> just notes that the reading came to an end.
In conversation <code>終(お)わる</code> is far more common; in a report or an
essay <code>終(お)える</code> often reads better.

Around the independent verbs the picture is worth keeping straight.
<code>終(お)わる</code> is basically intransitive - 
<code>会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わる</code> - and its everyday transitive partners are
<code>終(お)わらせる</code> ("get it finished") and
<code>終(お)える</code> ("bring it to a close"). There is one place where
<code>終(お)わる</code> does take `を`, and you will hear it constantly: the fixed
announcement <code>これで授業(じゅぎょう)を終(お)わります</code>,
<code>本日(ほんじつ)はこれで終(お)わります</code>. Careful writers prefer
<code>終(お)わりにします</code> or <code>終了(しゅうりょう)します</code>, but the
`を` version is standard spoken Japanese in that formula.

## Contrast

### ～終(お)わる vs ～終(お)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different temperature.
<code>終(お)わる</code> is the neutral everyday form;
<code>終(お)える</code> is transitive, deliberate, and at home in writing.

</div>

<pre>
レポートを書(か)き<b>終(お)わり</b>ました。
I've finished writing the report.
</pre>

<pre>
レポートを書(か)き<b>終(お)え</b>ました。
I have completed the report.
</pre>

The second sounds like a person reporting an achievement, and it would be the
better choice in an email to a client. Between friends it is faintly formal.

The one thing <code>終(お)える</code> cannot do is describe something ending by
itself: ❌<code>授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)える</code> is impossible, because
<code>終(お)える</code> always needs someone bringing the thing to an end.

### ～終(お)わる vs ～てしまう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>終(お)わる</code> is about **reaching the end** of a
process. `てしまう` is about **completion with an attitude** - either "the whole
lot" or "and I wish I hadn't".

</div>

<pre>
ケーキを<b>食(た)べ終(お)わっ</b>てから、コーヒーを入(い)れた。
I made coffee after I'd finished the cake.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)でケーキを食(た)べ<b>てしまっ</b>た。
I went and ate the whole cake on my own.
</pre>

The first is neutral sequencing. The second reports a completed act with a
feeling attached - pride, or more often regret. `てしまう` also attaches happily to
verbs with no duration at all (<code>忘(わす)れてしまった</code>,
<code>落(お)としてしまった</code>), which is exactly where
<code>終(お)わる</code> is impossible.

### ～終(お)わる vs 終(お)わる・終(お)わらせる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - as a **suffix** it rides on another verb's stem. As an
**independent verb** it needs a noun and `が`; to say that *you* finished
something, the verb is <code>終(お)わらせる</code> or
<code>終(お)える</code>.

</div>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)は三時(さんじ)に<b>終(お)わり</b>ます。
Class finishes at three.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)中(じゅう)にこの仕事(しごと)を<b>終(お)わらせ</b>ます。
I'll get this job finished before the day is out.
</pre>

The first has no agent - three o'clock arrives and the class is over. The second
has you in it, pushing. That is why a manager says
<code>終(お)わらせてください</code> and never
❌<code>終(お)わってください</code> when they mean "get it done";
<code>終(お)わってください</code> would be asking the task to end of its own
accord.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>終(お)わる</b> → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)み<b>終(お)わる</b>
  The suffix goes on the ます-stem, like
  <code>始(はじ)める</code> and <code>続(つづ)ける</code>.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>終(お)わっ</b>た。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)がやんだ。
  Rain does not complete anything; it stops. Uncontrolled processes with no
  finish line take <code>やむ</code>, <code>止(と)まる</code> or
  <code>終(お)わる</code> as an independent verb.

- ❌ 掃除(そうじ)<b>終(お)わり</b>ました。 → ✅ 掃除(そうじ)<b>し終(お)わり</b>ました。／掃除(そうじ)<b>が終(お)わり</b>ました。
  A する-noun needs either `し` to build the compound, or `が` in front of the
  independent verb.

- ❌ 駅(えき)に着(つ)き<b>終(お)わっ</b>た。 → ✅ 駅(えき)に着(つ)いた。
  Arriving takes no time, so there is no process to finish. The plain past says
  everything.

## Practice

**1.** レポートを書(か)き____たら、先生(せんせい)に見(み)せてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

レポートを書(か)き<b>終(お)わっ</b>たら、先生(せんせい)に見(み)せてください。

Writing a report is a process with a clear end, so the suffix fits, and
<code>～終(お)わったら</code> is the standard way to sequence "once you've
finished X, do Y". <code>書(か)いたら</code> alone would not insist that the
report is complete.

</details>

**2.** 授業(じゅぎょう)は三時(さんじ)に（終(お)わります・終(お)えます）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

授業(じゅぎょう)は三時(さんじ)に<b>終(お)わり</b>ます。

Nobody is bringing the class to an end here - the sentence just states when it is
over, and that is the intransitive verb.
<code>終(お)える</code> would need an agent and a `を`:
<code>先生(せんせい)が授業(じゅぎょう)を終(お)えました</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Once I've finished reading this book, I'll lend it to you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)み終(お)わっ</b>たら、貸(か)してあげます。

The `を` belongs to <code>読(よ)む</code> and stays where it is when the suffix

sound a shade more formal.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り終(お)わってから、散歩(さんぽ)に行(い)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) お菓子(かし)を食(た)べ終(お)わった。
(b) お菓子(かし)を食(た)べてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the eating ran to its end. It is neutral and factual, and it usually sets

(b) adds an attitude. Depending on the context it means "I ate the lot" or "oh no,
I've eaten them" - the sweets were somebody else's, or you were supposed to be
saving them. The grammar is not reporting a finish line so much as a completed act
you have feelings about.

</details>

# ～ているところ

**Meaning** · right in the middle of doing it - at this exact stage

`ところ` is the ordinary noun for "place". Put after `ている`, the place it names is
on a **timeline** rather than a map: the point you have reached in an action that
is under way. N5 gave you `ている` for "is doing"; `ているところ` narrows that to
*this stage, right now, neither before nor after*.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ている + ところ | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べているところ |
| Verb (する) | している + ところ | 準備(じゅんび)する → 準備(じゅんび)しているところ |
| Plain sentence end | ているところだ | 作(つく)っているところだ |
| Polite sentence end | ているところです | 作(つく)っているところです |
| Past reference | ているところだった | 寝(ね)ているところだった |
| Interrupted by something | ているところ + に／へ | 話(はな)しているところに来(き)た |
| Seen or caught | ているところ + を | 寝(ね)ているところを見(み)られた |
| Typical companions | 今(いま)、ちょうど、ちょうど今(いま) | 今(いま)読(よ)んでいるところです |
| Casual contraction | てるとこ | 今(いま)行(い)ってるとこ |
| ❌ Habit | ❌毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っているところです | ✅毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っています |
| ❌ Resulting state | ❌窓(まど)が開(あ)いているところです | ✅窓(まど)が開(あ)いています |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` has three jobs: action in progress, resulting state,
and habit. `ているところ` takes **only the first**. If you could not put
<code>今(いま)</code> or `ちょうど` in front of the sentence, `ところ` does not
belong there.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - because `ところ` is really "place", the same string of characters can be
a literal noun phrase:
<code>私(わたし)が住(す)んでいるところ</code> is "where I live", not "the stage
I am at in living". Context sorts this out easily. The aspect reading almost
always ends the sentence with `だ`／`です`, or carries on with `に`, `へ` or `を`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今(いま)、ごはんを<b>作(つく)っているところ</b>です。
I'm making dinner right now.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょうど宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>やっているところ</b>だよ。
I'm doing my homework as we speak.
</pre>

<pre>
その件(けん)は今(いま)<b>調(しら)べているところ</b>です。もう少(すこ)しお待(ま)ちください。
We're looking into that at the moment - please bear with us a little longer.
</pre>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)を<b>かけているところ</b>に、お客(きゃく)さんが来(き)てしまった。
A customer turned up right as I was in the middle of a phone call.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)がケーキを<b>食(た)べているところ</b>を母(はは)に見(み)られた。
My little brother got caught by our mother in the act of eating the cake.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)を<b>片付(かたづ)けているところ</b>だったので、まだ散(ち)らかっています。
I was in the middle of tidying the room, so it's still a mess.
</pre>

<pre>
さっき駅(えき)に着(つ)いて、今(いま)タクシーを<b>探(さが)しているところ</b>です。
I got to the station a moment ago and I'm looking for a taxi now.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が新(あたら)しい計画(けいかく)を<b>説明(せつめい)しているところ</b>だったので、質問(しつもん)はあとにしました。
The manager was in the middle of explaining the new plan, so I saved my question for later.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>ているところです</code> is the standard polite way to report **status**, and
this is the single most useful thing to take from the page. On the phone, in an
email, at a counter, <code>今(いま)確認(かくにん)しているところです</code> ("I'm
checking on that now") says far more than <code>まだです</code>: it tells the other
person the work is actually under way, and it buys time without sounding evasive.
Business Japanese leans on it heavily.

In casual speech the whole thing contracts. `ている` becomes `てる` and `ところ`
becomes `とこ`: <code>今(いま)出(で)てるとこ</code>,
<code>向(む)かってるとこだよ</code>. The `だ` usually disappears too. This is
extremely common between friends and completely out of place in writing.

Two constructions grow out of the same phrase. With `に` or `へ`, `ところ` sets up
an **interruption** - something arrives at the stage you were at:
<code>出(で)かけようとしているところに電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)った</code>. With
`を`, it sets up being **caught**, usually by a verb of seeing or stopping:
<code>サボっているところを見(み)つかった</code>. Both are worth recognising even
before you produce them.

## Contrast

### ～ているところ vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` is broad; `ているところ` is narrow. Adding `ところ`
throws away the habit reading and the resulting-state reading and keeps only
"in progress at this moment".

</div>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に走(はし)っています。
I run at six every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)走(はし)っているところなので、あとでかけ直(なお)します。
I'm out running at the moment, so I'll call you back.
</pre>

The first is a habit, and ❌<code>毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っているところです</code>
is impossible - a habit is not a stage you are standing in. The same goes for
states: <code>窓(まど)が開(あ)いています</code> ("the window is open") describes
a result, and ❌<code>開(あ)いているところです</code> would be describing a window
in the middle of opening itself.

Where both are possible, `ところ` is the emphatic one. It answers "have you done it
yet?" with "I am doing it *this second*", and it is much harder to hear as an
excuse.

### 食(た)べるところ vs 食(た)べているところ vs 食(た)べたところ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one noun, three stages. Dictionary form = **about to**.
`ている` = **in the middle of**. た-form = **just finished**.

</div>

<pre>
これから昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べる<b>ところ</b>です。
I'm just about to have lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>ているところ</b>です。
I'm having lunch right now.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>たところ</b>です。
I've just finished lunch.
</pre>

Nothing changes but the form of the verb, and the three sentences point at three
different minutes. Learn them as a set: the same `ところ` is doing the same job in
all three, and only the tense of the verb moves you along the timeline.

### ～ているところ vs ～ている間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ているところ` describes **the subject's own stage**, and
usually ends the sentence. <code>間(あいだ)</code> opens a **window** during
which something else happens - and the `に` decides whether the other event fills
the window or falls inside it.

</div>

The `に` is the entire difference, and it is worth slowing down for:

<pre>
母(はは)が寝(ね)ている<b>間(あいだ)</b>、ずっとそばにいました。
I stayed beside my mother the whole time she was asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)が寝(ね)ている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)ってきました。
While my mother was asleep, I nipped out and did the shopping.
</pre>

Without `に`, the second clause must last as long as the window - hence
<code>ずっと</code>, and hence verbs like
<code>待(ま)っていた</code>, <code>読(よ)んでいた</code>. With `に`, the second
clause is a one-off event that fits somewhere inside the window, and it is
typically something finishable: going out, making a call, tidying up. Swap them
and both sentences go wrong - ❌<code>寝(ね)ている間(あいだ)、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)った</code>
claims you shopped for the entire nap.

`ているところ` is doing something different again. It is not a time clause at all;
it is a statement about where the subject has got to:

<pre>
母(はは)は今(いま)寝(ね)<b>ているところ</b>です。
My mother is asleep just at the moment.
</pre>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)運動(うんどう)しているところです。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)運動(うんどう)しています。
  A habit has no "stage". `ところ` only works for something happening at the
  moment of speaking.

- ❌ 窓(まど)が開(あ)いているところです。 → ✅ 窓(まど)が開(あ)いています。
  This `ている` is a resulting state, not an action in progress, and `ところ`
  cannot attach to it.

- ❌ 今(いま)食(た)べるているところです。 → ✅ 今(いま)食(た)べているところです。
  The `ている` is built on the て-form, not the dictionary form. The dictionary
  form goes straight onto `ところ` and means "about to".

- ❌ 父(ちち)は十年(じゅうねん)前(まえ)から同(おな)じ会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)いているところです。 → ✅ …働(はたら)いています。
  Ten years is not a stage you are standing in. Long-running situations take plain
  `ている`.

## Practice

**1.** 「もうレポートを出(だ)しましたか。」「いいえ、今(いま)書(か)い____ところです。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いいえ、今(いま)書(か)い<b>ている</b>ところです。

The writing is under way at this moment, which is exactly the stage `ところ` picks
out. <code>書(か)くところです</code> would mean you have not started yet, and
<code>書(か)いたところです</code> would mean it is already done - a very different
answer to give your boss.

</details>

**2.** 毎朝(まいあさ)新聞(しんぶん)を（読(よ)んでいます・読(よ)んでいるところです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

毎朝(まいあさ)新聞(しんぶん)を<b>読(よ)んでいます</b>。

<code>毎朝(まいあさ)</code> makes this a habit, and `ところ` cannot express a
habit - it names a single stage in a single action. Drop
<code>毎朝(まいあさ)</code> and put <code>今(いま)</code> there instead, and the
other option becomes the right one.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm just checking now, so could you wait a moment?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今(いま)<b>確認(かくにん)しているところ</b>ですので、少々(しょうしょう)お待(ま)ちいただけますか。

This is the standard status report. <code>ているところです</code> tells the other
person the work is actually in motion, which is why it is the polite alternative
to a bare <code>まだです</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 姉(あね)は三年(さんねん)前(まえ)から大阪(おおさか)に住(す)んでいるところです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 姉(あね)は三年(さんねん)前(まえ)から大阪(おおさか)に住(す)んでいます。

<code>住(す)んでいる</code> is a state, not an action in progress, and three
years is not a moment. As it stands the sentence would also be misread as "the
place where my sister lives", since `ところ` still means "place".

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 姉(あね)が料理(りょうり)をしている間(あいだ)、私(わたし)は皿(さら)を洗(あら)っていました。
(b) 姉(あね)が料理(りょうり)をしている間(あいだ)に、私(わたし)は買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) has no `に`, so the two activities are the same length: for the whole time
your sister was cooking, you were at the sink. Both clauses describe stretches,
and neither has a single point attached to it.

(b) has `に`, which turns the cooking into a **window** and puts a one-off event
inside it. The shopping trip started and finished somewhere in the middle of the
cooking; it did not last the whole time and nobody claims it did.

That one syllable is the whole distinction. A verb that goes on (<code>洗(あら)っていた</code>,
<code>待(ま)っていた</code>) wants <code>間(あいだ)</code>; a verb that finishes
(<code>行(い)った</code>, <code>電話(でんわ)した</code>) wants
<code>間(あいだ)に</code>.

</details>

# ～ところ

**Meaning** · about to do; on the point of doing

With a verb in the **dictionary form**, `ところ` names the stage just *before* the
action: you have not started, and you are about to. The same construction in the
past - <code>～ところだった</code> - becomes one of the most useful sentences in
the language: "I very nearly did."

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **dictionary form** + ところだ | 出(で)る → 出(で)るところだ |
| Polite | dictionary form + ところです | 帰(かえ)るところです |
| Past - "nearly" | dictionary form + ところだった | 遅(おく)れるところだった |
| Past polite | dictionary form + ところでした | 忘(わす)れるところでした |
| More imminent still | ようとしている + ところ | 出(で)かけようとしているところ |
| Interrupted | ところ + に／へ | 寝(ね)るところに電話(でんわ)が来(き)た |
| Typical companions | これから、今(いま)から、ちょうど、もう少(すこ)しで | ちょうど始(はじ)めるところ |
| Casual contraction | とこ | 今(いま)出(で)るとこ |
| ❌ ます-form | ❌行(い)きますところ | ✅行(い)くところ |
| ❌ Distant future | ❌来月(らいげつ)行(い)くところです | ✅来月(らいげつ)行(い)く予定(よてい)です |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the dictionary form in front of `ところ` means the action has
**not begun**. Not "I am doing it", not "I have done it" - the foot is still in
the air.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - in this sense `ところ` normally ends the sentence with `だ`／`です`, or
carries on with `に`／`へ` to introduce an interruption. It is not a free
subordinate clause: to say "the phone rang as I was about to go to bed" you need
<code>寝(ね)るところに</code>, not a bare <code>寝(ね)るところ</code> followed by
a comma.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今(いま)から出(で)かける<b>ところ</b>です。
I'm just heading out now.
</pre>

<pre>
これから会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる<b>ところ</b>です。
The meeting is about to start.
</pre>

<pre>
あぶない! もう少(すこ)しで転(ころ)ぶ<b>ところ</b>だった。
Careful! I nearly went over.
</pre>

<pre>
お風呂(ふろ)に入(はい)る<b>ところ</b>だったので、電話(でんわ)に出(で)られませんでした。
I was about to get in the bath, so I couldn't pick up.
</pre>

<pre>
寝(ね)る<b>ところ</b>に友(とも)だちから電話(でんわ)がかかってきた。
Just as I was going to bed, a friend rang.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あや)うく終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)す<b>ところ</b>でした。
I very nearly missed the last train.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょうど家(いえ)を出(で)る<b>ところ</b>なので、あと十分(じゅっぷん)くらいで着(つ)くと思(おも)います。
I'm walking out the door right now, so I should be there in about ten minutes.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)を聞(き)かずに帰(かえ)る<b>ところ</b>でしたが、同僚(どうりょう)が教(おし)えてくれて助(たす)かりました。
I was about to go home without hearing the explanation, but a colleague filled me in - a lucky escape.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

In its present-tense use, <code>ところです</code> is a status report about the very
near future, and it is the natural answer to
<code>今(いま)何(なに)してる?</code> or <code>もう出(で)た?</code>. In casual
speech it contracts to `とこ` and drops the copula:
<code>今(いま)出(で)るとこ</code>. Both registers are extremely common; what
you will not hear is this pattern in a formal written notice, where a schedule
would be stated with times instead.

The past form is where the real value is.
<code>～ところだった</code> means *it nearly happened and it didn't*, and it almost
always carries relief. It collects a fixed set of adverbs - 
<code>もう少(すこ)しで</code>, <code>危(あや)うく</code>,
<code>あぶなく</code> - and it is how Japanese says all of these:

- <code>忘(わす)れるところでした</code> - "I almost forgot."
- <code>遅刻(ちこく)するところだった</code> - "I nearly ended up late."
- <code>買(か)ってしまうところだった</code> - "I almost bought it."

Note that the verb stays in the **dictionary form** even though the sentence is
about the past. The past lives in `だった`／`でした` at the end; the verb has to stay
un-happened, because the whole point is that it never happened.

One thing this pattern does **not** do is announce plans. It has a horizon of a
few minutes. For anything further out, the words are
<code>つもり</code> and <code>予定(よてい)</code>.

## Contrast

### 出(で)るところ vs 出(で)たところ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the form of the verb walks you across the event. Dictionary
form: **not started**. た-form: **just finished**. The `ところ` is identical in
both.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)、家(いえ)を出(で)る<b>ところ</b>です。
I'm just about to leave the house.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)、家(いえ)を出(で)た<b>ところ</b>です。
I've just left the house.
</pre>

Said into a phone, these two put you in completely different places: still
indoors, or already on the street. The middle member of the set - 
<code>出(で)ているところ</code> - is built on `ている` and covers the stretch in
between.

This is why the form in front of `ところ` deserves more care than any other choice
on this page. The word never changes; the verb carries all the information.

### ～ところだ vs ～つもり・～予定(よてい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ところ` reaches a few **minutes** into the future.
<code>つもり</code> is an **intention** and <code>予定(よてい)</code> is a
**schedule**, and both stretch as far as you like.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、京都(きょうと)へ行(い)く<b>つもり</b>です。
I'm planning to go to Kyoto next month.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)駅(えき)へ行(い)く<b>ところ</b>です。
I'm on my way to the station this minute.
</pre>

❌<code>来月(らいげつ)京都(きょうと)へ行(い)くところです</code> is wrong, and
audibly so: `ところ` claims you are on the very brink of the action, and you cannot
be on the brink of something a month away.

The reverse substitution is not an error so much as a loss.
<code>今(いま)行(い)くつもりです</code> reports your intention and leaves open
whether you have moved; <code>今(いま)行(い)くところです</code> says you are
already on your feet.

### ～ところだった vs ～そうになった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "almost". <code>ところだった</code> is a whole
**situation** you narrowly avoided; <code>そうになった</code> is a **physical
event** that nearly happened to you.

</div>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)しで遅刻(ちこく)する<b>ところ</b>でした。
I very nearly ended up late.
</pre>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)で転(ころ)び<b>そうになり</b>ました。
I almost fell on the stairs.
</pre>

The mechanics differ too: <code>そうになる</code> attaches to the <b>ます-stem</b>
(<code>転(ころ)び</code>, <code>落(お)ち</code>, <code>泣(な)き</code>),
while `ところ` takes the dictionary form. And their coverage differs.
<code>そうになる</code> wants something on the verge of physically occurring - 
falling, dropping, crying, crashing. <code>ところだった</code> is happy with
outcomes you avoided by *deciding differently*:
<code>危(あや)うく高(たか)いほうを買(か)うところだった</code>, "I almost bought
the expensive one." <code>そうになる</code> cannot say that.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今(いま)出(で)かけ<b>ますところ</b>です。 → ✅ 今(いま)出(で)かけ<b>る</b>ところです。
  `ところ` is a noun, and a noun takes the plain form in front of it. The
  politeness is already there in `です`.

- ❌ 来年(らいねん)、アメリカに留学(りゅうがく)する<b>ところ</b>です。 → ✅ 来年(らいねん)、アメリカに留学(りゅうがく)する<b>予定(よてい)</b>です。
  A year is far outside the range of this pattern. Distant plans take
  <code>予定(よてい)</code> or `つもり`.

- ❌ もう少(すこ)しで財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れ<b>たところ</b>でした。 → ✅ もう少(すこ)しで財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れ<b>るところ</b>でした。
  The verb stays in the dictionary form: the forgetting never happened. Only
  <code>でした</code> moves into the past.

- ❌ 今(いま)食(た)べ<b>ているところ</b>だったので、後(あと)で行(い)きます。（meaning "I was about to eat"） → ✅ 今(いま)食(た)べ<b>るところ</b>だったので…
  `ている` puts you in the middle of the meal. If the food is untouched, the
  dictionary form is the one you want.

## Practice

**1.** あぶない! もう少(すこ)しで車(くるま)にぶつかる____だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう少(すこ)しで車(くるま)にぶつかる<b>ところ</b>だった。

<code>もう少(すこ)しで</code> is the giveaway: it introduces something that came
close to happening and then did not, and the pattern for that is
<code>～るところだった</code>. Note that the verb stays in the dictionary form
because the collision never took place.

</details>

**2.** ちょうど今(いま)、会社(かいしゃ)を（出(で)る・出(で)た）ところです。これから向(む)かいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ちょうど今(いま)、会社(かいしゃ)を<b>出(で)る</b>ところです。

The second sentence - "I'll head over now" - tells you the journey has not started
yet, so you are still on the office side of the door. That is the dictionary form.
<code>出(で)たところ</code> would put you outside already.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I was just about to phone you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ちょうど電話(でんわ)する<b>ところ</b>でした。

The call had not been made, so the verb is in the dictionary form, and the past
sits in <code>でした</code>. This is a very common thing to say when someone
contacts you first, and it is friendlier than
<code>電話(でんわ)しようと思(おも)っていました</code> because it claims you were
already in motion.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来月(らいげつ)、東京(とうきょう)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)すところです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来月(らいげつ)、東京(とうきょう)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>予定(よてい)</b>です。（or 引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>つもり</b>です）

`ところ` means you are on the brink of the action, within minutes. A move next
month is a plan, so it needs <code>予定(よてい)</code> for a fixed arrangement or
`つもり` for an intention.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れるところでした。
(b) 財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れてしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a near miss with a happy ending: the wallet is in your pocket. You noticed
in time, and the sentence is coloured by relief - this is what you say as you turn
back from the door with it in your hand.

(b) is the disaster itself. The wallet is on the kitchen table and you are on the
train. `てしまう` reports a completed action you regret, where
<code>ところでした</code> reports one that never happened at all.

</details>

# ～たところ

**Meaning** · have just done it - standing at the moment immediately after

With the verb in the <b>た-form</b>, `ところ` names the stage right after the action
finished. The emphasis is on *right*: this is a claim about the clock, not about
how fresh the event feels. Minutes ago, not last month.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | <b>た-form</b> + ところだ | 着(つ)く → 着(つ)いたところだ |
| Verb (voiced た-form) | だ-form + ところだ | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んだところだ |
| Polite | た-form + ところです | 終(お)わったところです |
| Past reference | た-form + ところだった | 出(で)たところだった |
| Followed by an event | たところ + に／へ／で | 出(で)たところに会(あ)った |
| Typical companions | 今(いま)、たった今(いま)、ちょうど | たった今(いま)帰(かえ)ったところ |
| Casual contraction | たとこ | 今(いま)着(つ)いたとこ |
| ❌ Modifying a noun | ❌買(か)ったところの車(くるま) | ✅買(か)ったばかりの車(くるま) |
| ❌ Distant past | ❌去年(きょねん)結婚(けっこん)したところです | ✅去年(きょねん)結婚(けっこん)したばかりです |
| ❌ Negative | ❌食(た)べなかったところ | ✅まだ食(た)べていません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たところ` pins you to the moment immediately after the action.
If you can put <code>たった今(いま)</code> in front of the sentence, it fits. If
you would have to put <code>先月(せんげつ)</code> there, it does not.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `～たところ` has a second, unrelated life as a **connective**, and you
will meet it in reading before you produce it:
<code>店(みせ)に電話(でんわ)したところ、もう閉(し)まっていました</code> - "when I
rang the shop, it turned out to be closed." That version has no `だ`, sits at a
comma, and belongs to formal writing. The last contrast section below separates
the two.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
たった今(いま)うちに<b>帰(かえ)ったところ</b>です。
I've literally just got home.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)<b>起(お)きたところ</b>なので、まだ顔(かお)も洗(あら)っていません。
I've only just woken up - I haven't even washed my face yet.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょうど会議(かいぎ)が<b>終(お)わったところ</b>です。
The meeting has just this minute finished.
</pre>

<pre>
メールは今(いま)<b>送(おく)ったところ</b>ですので、確認(かくにん)してみてください。
I've just sent the email, so please check for it.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)を<b>出(で)たところ</b>で、高校(こうこう)の友(とも)だちにばったり会(あ)った。
I'd just come out of the station when I ran straight into an old school friend.
</pre>

<pre>
お湯(ゆ)が<b>わいたところ</b>だから、お茶(ちゃ)を入(い)れましょうか。
The water's just boiled - shall I make some tea?
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの荷物(にもつ)を<b>運(はこ)び終(お)わったところ</b>に、大家(おおや)さんが来(き)ました。
The landlord turned up just as we'd finished carrying in the boxes.
</pre>

<pre>
さっき銀行(ぎんこう)から戻(もど)って、今(いま)やっと書類(しょるい)を全部(ぜんぶ)<b>出(だ)したところ</b>です。
I got back from the bank a while ago, and I've only just now handed in all the paperwork.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>たところです</code> is the polite way to report that something is done *this
instant*, and it is a workplace staple. Asked whether you have sent the file,
<code>今(いま)送(おく)ったところです</code> is better than
<code>送(おく)りました</code> because it tells the other person to look now. In
casual speech it shortens to `たとこ`:
<code>ごめん、今(いま)起(お)きたとこ</code>.

The construction is **objective**. It is measured against the clock, and it will
not stretch. That is its whole personality, and it is exactly what separates it
from `たばかり`, which is measured against how the speaker feels. Because of that,
`たところ` has no emotional colour of its own: it neither excuses nor boasts, it
just reports where on the timeline you are standing.

Two structural limits follow from the same fact. `たところ` cannot modify a noun - 
there is no ❌<code>作(つく)ったところの料理(りょうり)</code> - because it
describes a moment rather than a property of the thing. And it has no negative
form: "I haven't eaten yet" is <code>まだ食(た)べていません</code>, never
❌<code>食(た)べなかったところです</code>.

For narration in the past, use <code>たところだった</code>:
<code>部屋(へや)に入(はい)ったとき、彼(かれ)は電話(でんわ)を切(き)ったところだった</code>.

## Contrast

### ～たところ vs ～たばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たところ` measures with a **clock**; `たばかり` measures with a
**feeling**. Both translate as "just", and only one of them will stretch to last
year.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)駅(えき)に<b>着(つ)いたところ</b>です。
I've just arrived at the station.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)日本(にほん)に<b>来(き)たばかり</b>です。
I only came to Japan last year.
</pre>

The second sentence is impossible with `ところ`:
❌<code>去年(きょねん)来(き)たところです</code>. A year is not a moment. `ばかり`
survives it because the speaker is not reporting the clock - they are saying the
arrival is still recent *as far as they are concerned*, which is why it works as
an excuse for not knowing their way around.

The second difference is structural and easy to test. `ばかり` modifies nouns
freely; `ところ` does not:

<pre>
買(か)った<b>ばかりの</b>パソコンが壊(こわ)れてしまった。
The computer I'd only just bought has broken.
</pre>

❌<code>買(か)ったところのパソコン</code> is not Japanese. If a noun follows,
`ばかり` is your only option.

### ～たところ vs ～たあとで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たところ` is a **statement about now** and ends the sentence.
<code>～たあとで</code> is a **connector** and always has another clause after it.

</div>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)が<b>終(お)わったところ</b>です。
I've just finished work.
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)が終(お)わった<b>あとで</b>、飲(の)みに行(い)きます。
After work I'm going out for a drink.
</pre>

The first sentence is complete; the second would be unfinished without its second
half. And they carry different information: `あとで` says nothing at all about how
recent the event is, while `たところ` says nothing about what happens next.

When you do want to hang a following clause on `たところ`, it needs a particle - 
`に`, `へ` or `で` - and the meaning becomes "just as I had done X, Y happened":
<code>席(せき)に座(すわ)ったところに先生(せんせい)が入(はい)ってきた</code>.

### ～たところだ vs ～たところ、…

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `だ`／`です` it means **"just finished"**. Standing at a
comma with no copula, it is a formal connective meaning **"when I did X, it turned
out that Y"**.

</div>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)に<b>聞(き)いたところ</b>です。
I've just this minute asked the section chief.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)に<b>聞(き)いたところ</b>、会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になったそうです。
I asked the section chief, and apparently the meeting has been cancelled.
</pre>

The connective version has three tells: no `だ`, a comma, and a second clause that
reports something **discovered** as a result. It belongs to written and formal
Japanese - reports, business email, news - and in speech people say
<code>聞(き)いてみたら</code> instead. You need to recognise it so that a
sentence like the second one does not read to you as "I have just asked."

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)この会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)ったところです。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)この会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)った<b>ばかり</b>です。
  A year ago is not "just now" on any clock. Subjective recency is `ばかり`'s job.

- ❌ これは先週(せんしゅう)買(か)った<b>ところの</b>自転車(じてんしゃ)です。 → ✅ これは先週(せんしゅう)買(か)った<b>ばかりの</b>自転車(じてんしゃ)です。
  `ところ` cannot modify a noun. When a noun follows, only `ばかり` works.

- ❌ 今(いま)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べる<b>ところ</b>です。（meaning "I've just eaten"） → ✅ 今(いま)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>た</b>ところです。
  The dictionary form means you have not started. One character decides whether
  the food is gone.

- ❌ まだ返事(へんじ)が来(こ)なかったところです。 → ✅ まだ返事(へんじ)が来(き)ていません。
  There is no negative version of this pattern. "Not yet" is
  <code>まだ～ていません</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 「もう昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べましたか。」「ええ、今(いま)食(た)べ____ところです。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ええ、今(いま)食(た)べ<b>た</b>ところです。

The た-form places you immediately after the meal, which is what
<code>今(いま)</code> and the question both call for.
<code>食(た)べるところです</code> would mean the food is still untouched, and
would answer the question with a flat no.

</details>

**2.** 三年(さんねん)前(まえ)に大学(だいがく)を卒業(そつぎょう)した（ところ・ばかり）です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三年(さんねん)前(まえ)に大学(だいがく)を卒業(そつぎょう)した<b>ばかり</b>です。

Three years cannot be squeezed into `ところ`, which is measured on the clock.
`ばかり` reports how recent it feels to the speaker - here, that they still count
as a new graduate.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The train has just left, so the next one is in ten minutes."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)は今(いま)<b>出(で)たところ</b>なので、次(つぎ)は十分(じゅっぷん)後(ご)です。

The departure happened seconds ago and the sentence is about the clock, so
`たところ` is exactly right. <code>出(で)たばかり</code> would also be understood
but would sound like a complaint about your luck rather than a timetable fact.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: これは先月(せんげつ)直(なお)したところのテレビです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ これは先月(せんげつ)直(なお)した<b>ばかりの</b>テレビです。

Two problems, one fix. `ところ` cannot sit in front of a noun, and last month is
too far back for it anyway. `ばかり` handles both: it takes `の` before a noun and
it stretches to cover anything the speaker still thinks of as recent.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日本(にほん)に来(き)たところです。
(b) 日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) means you have this moment arrived - you are at the airport, or you have just
walked out of it, and the suitcase is still in your hand. It is a statement about
the clock and it will not stretch past a few minutes.

(b) means you are new here. It might be a week, it might be six months; what it
reports is that the arrival is still recent from where you stand. This is the one
you use to explain why your Japanese is shaky or why you got lost, and (a) would
be strange in that role.

</details>

# ～たばかり

**Meaning** · only just did it - recent as far as the speaker is concerned

`たばかり` also translates as "just", but it is measuring something quite different
from `たところ`. `ところ` reads the clock. `ばかり` reads the **speaker**: it says
the event is still recent *to them*, and that judgement stretches. Five minutes,
five weeks, sometimes five years - as long as the speaker still feels new to it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | <b>た-form</b> + ばかりだ | 来(く)る → 来(き)たばかりだ |
| Verb (voiced た-form) | だ-form + ばかりだ | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んだばかりだ |
| Polite | た-form + ばかりです | 着(つ)いたばかりです |
| **Before a noun** | た-form + ばかり + <b>の</b> + Noun | 焼(や)いたばかりのパン |
| Giving a reason | たばかりなので／たばかりで | 入(はい)ったばかりなので… |
| Past reference | たばかりだった | 始(はじ)めたばかりだった |
| Casual | ばっかり／ばっか | 来(き)たばっかり |
| Different sense: "only" | Noun + ばかり | ゲームばかりしている |
| Different sense: "nothing but" | て-form + ばかり + いる | 遊(あそ)んでばかりいる |
| ❌ Dictionary form | ❌来(く)るばかりです | ✅来(き)たばかりです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - recency here is **relative to the thing**. A bus that left five
minutes ago, a job started last month and a building put up five years ago can all
take `ばかり`, because "new" means something different for each. That flexibility
is exactly what `たところ` refuses.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun-modifying form is the practical reason to learn this pattern.
<code>生(う)まれたばかりの赤(あか)ちゃん</code>,
<code>習(なら)ったばかりの漢字(かんじ)</code>,
<code>買(か)ったばかりの靴(くつ)</code> - all completely ordinary, and none of
them can be built with `ところ`. Do not drop the `の`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
さっき昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べた<b>ばかり</b>です。
I only had lunch a little while ago.
</pre>

<pre>
この会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)った<b>ばかり</b>で、まだ何(なに)もわかりません。
I've only just joined this company, so I don't know anything yet.
</pre>

<pre>
焼(や)いた<b>ばかり</b>のパンはやっぱりおいしいね。
Bread straight out of the oven really is good, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
生(う)まれた<b>ばかり</b>の赤(あか)ちゃんは、一日(いちにち)中(じゅう)寝(ね)ています。
A newborn baby sleeps all day long.
</pre>

<pre>
習(なら)った<b>ばかり</b>の漢字(かんじ)を、もう忘(わす)れてしまいました。
I've already forgotten the kanji I only just learned.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)買(か)った<b>ばかり</b>のパソコンなのに、もう動(うご)かなくなった。
I only bought this computer last year and it's already stopped working.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)してきた<b>ばかり</b>なので、この辺(へん)のことはまだよく知(し)らないんです。
I've only just moved to this town, so I don't really know the area yet.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に来(き)た<b>ばかり</b>のころは、コンビニで買(か)い物(もの)をするだけでも緊張(きんちょう)しました。
When I'd only just arrived in Japan, even shopping in a convenience store made me nervous.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たばかり` is register-neutral, and its most characteristic job is **explaining
yourself**. <code>～たばかりなので</code> is how Japanese excuses inexperience,
disorder or ignorance without apologising outright:
<code>始(はじ)めたばかりなので、まだ下手(へた)です</code>,
<code>引(ひ)っ越(こ)したばかりで、部屋(へや)が片付(かたづ)いていません</code>.
It buys the speaker some room, and because the recency is subjective, nobody
argues with it.

In casual speech it lengthens rather than shortens:
<code>ばっかり</code> and <code>ばっか</code> are both extremely common - 
<code>今(いま)起(お)きたばっかりだよ</code> - and both are firmly spoken-only.

Be aware that the same word `ばかり` has a much more frequent meaning, "only,
nothing but": <code>肉(にく)ばかり食(た)べる</code>,
<code>文句(もんく)ばかり言(い)う</code>,
<code>寝(ね)てばかりいる</code>. The two are told apart by what comes in front.
A <b>た-form</b> gives you "just did"; a **noun** or a <b>て-form</b> gives you "nothing
but". Getting this wrong produces a sentence that means the opposite of what you
intended, so it is worth a moment's care.

## Contrast

### ～たばかり vs ～たところ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ところ` is measured on the **clock**; `ばかり` is measured by
the **speaker**. In the few minutes after an event both are available; outside
that window only `ばかり` survives.

</div>

<pre>
入学(にゅうがく)した<b>ばかり</b>なので、友(とも)だちがまだ少(すく)ないです。
I've only just started at the school, so I don't have many friends yet.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は今(いま)終(お)わった<b>ところ</b>です。
The meeting finished just now.
</pre>

Try swapping them. ❌<code>入学(にゅうがく)したところなので</code> would claim you
enrolled minutes ago, which makes nonsense of the excuse. And
<code>終(お)わったばかりです</code>, while perfectly grammatical, shifts the
emphasis from the timetable to how it feels - closer to "it's barely over" than to
"it finished at 3.02".

The second difference is the one that decides most sentences in practice: only
`ばかり` can sit in front of a noun. <code>届(とど)いたばかりの荷物(にもつ)</code>
is normal; ❌<code>届(とど)いたところの荷物(にもつ)</code> does not exist.

### ～たばかり vs ばかり ("only, nothing but")

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look left. <b>た-form</b> + ばかり = "just did". **Noun** + ばかり,
or <b>て-form</b> + ばかり + いる = "nothing but".

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は新(あたら)しいゲームを買(か)った<b>ばかり</b>だ。
My little brother has only just bought the new game.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はゲーム<b>ばかり</b>している。
My little brother does nothing but play games.
</pre>

Same four syllables, and the two sentences do not even share a topic: the first is
about when, the second about proportion. The `ている` version is the one to watch,
because it looks so close to the pattern on this page:
<code>泣(な)いてばかりいる</code> means "does nothing but cry", while
<code>泣(な)いたばかりだ</code> would mean "has only just cried".

### ～たばかり vs 最近(さいきん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>最近(さいきん)</code> is an adverb about a **period**;
`たばかり` attaches to a **single completed event**. They combine happily, but they
are not substitutes.

</div>

<pre>
<b>最近(さいきん)</b>、よく映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きます。
I've been going to the cinema a lot lately.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は先月(せんげつ)開(ひら)いた<b>ばかり</b>です。
This shop only opened last month.
</pre>

<code>最近(さいきん)</code> can cover a habit spread over weeks, which `ばかり`
cannot do at all - there is no single event for it to attach to. Conversely
`ばかり` pins the recency to one thing that happened, which is why it, and not
<code>最近(さいきん)</code>, can go in front of a noun.

Putting them together is completely normal and adds emphasis:
<code>最近(さいきん)始(はじ)めたばかりの趣味(しゅみ)です</code>, "it's a hobby
I've only recently taken up."

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 日本(にほん)に来(く)る<b>ばかり</b>です。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)に来(き)<b>た</b>ばかりです。
  This pattern is built on the た-form. The dictionary form gives you an entirely
  different, much rarer construction.

- ❌ 買(か)ったばかり車(くるま)を見(み)せてください。 → ✅ 買(か)ったばかり<b>の</b>車(くるま)を見(み)せてください。
  In front of a noun, `ばかり` needs `の`. Leaving it out is the single most common
  slip with this pattern.

- ❌ 兄(あに)は寝(ね)た<b>ばかり</b>います。 → ✅ 兄(あに)は寝(ね)<b>て</b>ばかりいます。
  "Does nothing but sleep" is built on the て-form plus `いる`. With the た-form the
  sentence would mean he had only just fallen asleep.

- ❌ 日本(にほん)に来(き)<b>て</b>ばかりです。（meaning "I've only just come to Japan"） → ✅ 日本(にほん)に来(き)<b>た</b>ばかりです。
  The て-form pushes you into the "nothing but" meaning, which here would be
  something like "all I do is come to Japan".

## Practice

**1.** この町(まち)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)して来(き)____ばかりなので、まだ道(みち)がよくわかりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

引(ひ)っ越(こ)して来(き)<b>た</b>ばかりなので…

The pattern needs the た-form. Note what the sentence is doing: the move might have
been a month ago, and `ばかり` is being used to excuse not knowing the streets yet
 - a job the clock-bound `たところ` could not do.

</details>

**2.** これは五年(ごねん)前(まえ)に建(た)った（ところ・ばかり）のビルです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

これは五年(ごねん)前(まえ)に建(た)った<b>ばかり</b>のビルです。

Two reasons, and either one settles it. Five years is far outside `ところ`'s range,
and `ところ` cannot modify a noun in the first place. For a building, five years
old genuinely counts as new - which is exactly the kind of judgement `ばかり` is
built to express.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "These are cakes I've just baked - please help yourself."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

焼(や)いた<b>ばかりの</b>ケーキです。どうぞ召(め)し上(あ)がってください。

A noun follows, so the form must be <code>たばかりの</code> with the `の` in place.
This is the everyday way to say "freshly made", and it is one of the commonest
uses of the pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 息子(むすこ)は勉強(べんきょう)しないで、遊(あそ)んだばかりいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 息子(むすこ)は勉強(べんきょう)しないで、遊(あそ)<b>んで</b>ばかりいます。

"Does nothing but play" is the *other* `ばかり`, and it is built on the て-form plus
`いる`. With the た-form the sentence would say he had only just started playing,
which is not a complaint at all.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を始(はじ)めたばかりです。
(b) 新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を始(はじ)めたところです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says you are new to the job. It could be your third day or your third month;
what the sentence claims is that you have not settled in yet, and it usually leads
into an explanation - you are still learning, you cannot answer that question
without asking someone.

(b) reads the clock. It says the starting happened moments ago, which for a job is
odd unless you are describing this morning: "I've just this minute started."
Native speakers would understand it as the literal instant, and would probably
have expected (a).

</details>
