# N4 - Adding another verb after て


---

# Transitive & intransitive verbs

**Meaning** · paired verbs built on one root - one for "somebody does it to
something", one for "it happens"

Japanese keeps two separate verbs where English is happy with one. 開(あ)ける
is *open something*; 開(あ)く is *(something) opens*. English uses the word
"open" for both and lets the sentence sort it out. Japanese does not: picking the
wrong half of the pair forces the wrong particle, and the sentence stops being
Japanese. This is vocabulary, but it behaves like grammar, which is why it gets a
page.

The vocabulary word for the transitive half is 他動詞(たどうし) and for the
intransitive half 自動詞(じどうし). You will see both in dictionaries.

## Formation

There is no single rule that derives one member of a pair from the other - there
are recurring shapes, and the shapes are worth knowing because they let you guess.
Each row below gives the intransitive first (the one that takes `が`), then the
transitive (the one that takes `を`).

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ending ～aru / ～eru | が ～aru ／ を ～eru | ドアが閉(し)まる → ドアを閉(し)める |
| Ending ～u / ～eru | が ～u ／ を ～eru | ドアが開(あ)く → ドアを開(あ)ける |
| Ending ～eru / ～u | が ～eru ／ を ～u | 枝(えだ)が折(お)れる → 枝(えだ)を折(お)る |
| Ending ～reru / ～su | が ～reru ／ を ～su | いすが壊(こわ)れる → いすを壊(こわ)す |
| Ending ～ru / ～su | が ～ru ／ を ～su | 時計(とけい)が直(なお)る → 時計(とけい)を直(なお)す |
| Ending ～iru / ～osu | が ～iru ／ を ～osu | 事故(じこ)が起(お)きる → 事故(じこ)を起(お)こす |
| Ending ～u / ～asu | が ～u ／ を ～asu | お湯(ゆ)が沸(わ)く → お湯(ゆ)を沸(わ)かす |
| Ending ～ku / ～kasu | が ～ku ／ を ～kasu | 車(くるま)が動(うご)く → 車(くるま)を動(うご)かす |
| Intransitive only (no partner) | が only | 事故(じこ)が起(お)きる、人(ひと)が来(く)る |
| Transitive only (no partner) | を only | ごはんを食(た)べる、本(ほん)を読(よ)む |
| One verb, both roles | が or を, same verb | 花(はな)が開(ひら)く ／ 本(ほん)を開(ひら)く |

The pairs below are the ones that actually turn up. Learn them two at a time - 
memorising one member alone is what produces the mistakes.

| Intransitive（が） | Transitive（を） | English |
|---|---|---|
| 開(あ)く | 開(あ)ける | open |
| 閉(し)まる | 閉(し)める | close |
| 付(つ)く | 付(つ)ける | come on / switch on |
| 消(き)える | 消(け)す | go out / put out |
| 入(はい)る | 入(い)れる | go in / put in |
| 出(で)る | 出(だ)す | come out / take out |
| 始(はじ)まる | 始(はじ)める | begin |
| 終(お)わる | 終(お)える | end |
| 止(と)まる | 止(と)める | stop |
| 落(お)ちる | 落(お)とす | fall / drop |
| 割(わ)れる | 割(わ)る | break（glass, plates） |
| 壊(こわ)れる | 壊(こわ)す | break（machines, things） |
| 汚(よご)れる | 汚(よご)す | get dirty / make dirty |
| 集(あつ)まる | 集(あつ)める | gather |
| 続(つづ)く | 続(つづ)ける | continue |
| 変(か)わる | 変(か)える | change |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb chooses the particle, not the other way round. An
intransitive verb can never take a `を` object, and a transitive verb always wants
one. If you can say "somebody did it **to** something", you need the transitive.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>

</pre>

My little brother turned the light off.
</pre>

<pre>

The bus stopped in front of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
コップが<b>割(わ)れて</b>、テーブルが水(みず)だらけになった。
The glass broke and the table was covered in water.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、花瓶(かびん)を<b>割(わ)って</b>しまいました。
I'm sorry - I've broken the vase.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に<b>始(はじ)まります</b>ので、十分(じゅっぷん)前(まえ)にお集(あつ)まりください。
The meeting starts at three, so please assemble ten minutes beforehand.
</pre>

<pre>
パソコンは自分(じぶん)で<b>直(なお)した</b>けれど、また同(おな)じところが壊(こわ)れそうだ。
I fixed the computer myself, but the same part looks like it's about to break again.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)あたりから村(むら)の人口(じんこう)が<b>減(へ)って</b>いて、バスの本数(ほんすう)も少(すく)なくなった。
The village's population has been shrinking since about last year, and there are fewer buses too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Transitivity carries no politeness of its own. Both halves of a pair are equally
usable in casual speech, in `ます` speech and in writing, and nothing about the
choice makes you sound formal or rough. What it does carry is **whether a person
is in the picture**, and that has social weight.

**Japanese reaches for the intransitive far more often than English does.** Where
English says "we've decided the date" or "they found the key", ordinary Japanese
says 日(ひ)にちが決(き)まりました and 鍵(かぎ)が見(み)つかりました. No one
is named, no one is credited, and the sentence sounds neutral and adult rather
than evasive. English speakers who default to the transitive end up sounding as
though they are claiming credit for everything.

**The same choice is how you take or dodge responsibility.** お皿(さら)が
割(わ)れました reports an event; お皿(さら)を割(わ)りました admits you did
it. If you broke it and you are apologising, the intransitive will read as
squirming. If you are simply telling someone the plate is in pieces, the
transitive drags in a culprit nobody asked about.

**Each half has its own resulting-state construction.** The intransitive takes
<code>～ている</code> (<code>閉(し)まっている</code>, "is shut"); the transitive takes
<code>～てある</code> (<code>閉(し)めてある</code>, "has been shut"). That pairing has a
page of its own, but it is worth knowing now that it exists - mixing the halves
there is the single commonest N4 error.

**A few verbs work both ways with no change of shape.** 開(ひら)く,
増(ま)す and 吹(ふ)く each take `が` or `を` depending on the sentence. They are
the exception; assume a pair until you find out otherwise.

## Contrast

### 開(あ)く vs 開(あ)ける - the particle follows the verb

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>開(あ)く</code> takes `が` and no object at all.
<code>開(あ)ける</code> takes `を` and needs somebody doing the opening. You cannot
mix and match.

</div>

<pre>
風(かぜ)で窓(まど)が<b>開(あ)いた</b>。
The window blew open in the wind.
</pre>

<pre>
暑(あつ)かったので窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けた</b>。
It was hot, so I opened the window.
</pre>

In the first sentence nothing has an agent - the wind is a cause, marked with
`で`, not a doer. In the second there is a person, and the window is what they
acted on. Writing ❌窓(まど)を開(あ)いた is the most frequent transitivity
error there is, and it happens because English lets "opened" cover both.

The same test works on every pair. Ask: does the sentence have somebody doing it
**to** something?

<pre>
話(はな)し合(あ)いは十時(じゅうじ)まで<b>続(つづ)いた</b>。
The discussion went on until ten.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)されても、彼(かれ)は研究(けんきゅう)を<b>続(つづ)けた</b>。
Even when people objected, he carried on with his research.
</pre>

### The intransitive vs the passive

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the intransitive says *it happened*. The passive says
*somebody did it, and it landed on me*.

</div>

English translates both with "was broken", which is exactly why the distinction is
invisible to learners.

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)が<b>壊(こわ)れた</b>。
My bike broke.
</pre>

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)を<b>壊(こわ)された</b>。
Somebody went and broke my bike.
</pre>

The first is bad luck: the bike gave out. The second has a culprit in it, unnamed
but definitely there, and the speaker is the injured party - which is why the
object keeps `を` even though the verb is passive. Choosing the passive where the
intransitive would do makes you sound aggrieved. Choosing the intransitive when
someone really did wreck your things makes you sound oddly forgiving.

<pre>
財布(さいふ)が見(み)つかりました。
My wallet turned up.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)を盗(ぬす)まれました。
My wallet was stolen.
</pre>

### Where English wants a person and Japanese does not

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - when no one needs naming, Japanese states the event with the
intransitive rather than reaching for a passive.

</div>

English has no intransitive verb for "decide", so it borrows the passive: "the date
has been decided". Japanese has 決(き)まる sitting there ready, so the passive
is unnecessary and would sound heavy.

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の予定(よてい)が<b>決(き)まりました</b>。
Next month's schedule has been settled.
</pre>

<pre>
なくした鍵(かぎ)が机(つくえ)の下(した)から<b>見(み)つかりました</b>。
The key I lost turned up under the desk.
</pre>

This is the reason so much everyday Japanese has no visible subject.
連絡(れんらく)が入(はい)りました ("word came in"), 準備(じゅんび)が
整(ととの)いました ("everything's ready"), 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わりました
("the meeting's over") - all intransitive, all agentless, all completely standard
in a workplace where naming who did what would be beside the point.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 窓(まど)を開(あ)きました。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けました</b>。／窓(まど)が<b>開(あ)きました</b>。
  <code>開(あ)く</code> is intransitive and cannot take a `を` object. Either switch
  the verb or switch the particle - but the two must agree.

- ❌ 電気(でんき)が消(け)しました。 → ✅ 電気(でんき)を<b>消(け)しました</b>。／電気(でんき)が<b>消(き)えました</b>。
  Same error in the other direction. Note the readings too:
  <code>消(け)す</code> and <code>消(き)える</code> share a kanji and not a stem.

- ❌ 授業(じゅぎょう)を始(はじ)まります。 → ✅ 授業(じゅぎょう)を<b>始(はじ)めます</b>。
  A teacher starting a lesson is doing something *to* the lesson.
  <code>始(はじ)まる</code> would need <code>授業(じゅぎょう)が</code> and no actor.

- ❌ かばんから財布(さいふ)を落(お)ちました。 → ✅ かばんから財布(さいふ)を<b>落(お)としました</b>。
  Dropping something is transitive. <code>落(お)ちる</code> is what the wallet does
  on its own: 財布(さいふ)が落(お)ちました.

- ❌ ドアが閉(し)めています。 → ✅ ドアが<b>閉(し)まっています</b>。
  With `が` you need the intransitive. <code>閉(し)めている</code> would mean somebody
  is in the middle of closing it, and that person would need `を`.

## Practice

**1.** ボタンを押(お)すと、ドアが____ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ドアが<b>開(あ)き</b>ます。

The subject is marked with `が` and there is no person acting on the door - the
button does it - so the intransitive <code>開(あ)く</code> is required.
<code>開(あ)ける</code> would need <code>ドアを</code> and somebody to open it.

</details>

**2.** 電車(でんしゃ)が駅(えき)に（止(と)まりました・止(と)めました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)が駅(えき)に<b>止(と)まりました</b>。

The train is the thing that came to a halt, and it is marked with `が`, so the
intransitive is the only fit. <code>止(と)めました</code> would describe a driver
bringing something to a stop: 車(くるま)を止(と)めました.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I dropped my wallet on the train."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)の中(なか)で財布(さいふ)を<b>落(お)としました</b>。

You are the one who lost hold of it, so the transitive
<code>落(お)とす</code> with `を` is right. Saying
財布(さいふ)が落(お)ちました would report that the wallet fell, with nobody at
fault - fine as a description, wrong as a confession.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)まる前(まえ)に、資料(しりょう)をコピーします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)が<b>始(はじ)まる</b>前(まえ)に、資料(しりょう)をコピーします。

The meeting starts by itself here - nobody is named as starting it - so it takes
`が` plus the intransitive. If you did want to name the chairperson, the other
half of the pair works with `を`:
会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)める前(まえ)に.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) パスワードを変(か)えました。
(b) パスワードが変(か)わりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the transitive: somebody - normally the speaker - deliberately changed
the password. It answers "what did you do?" and it puts a person on record.

(b) uses the intransitive: the password is now different, and the sentence says
nothing about who or why. It is what a system message says, and it is also what
you say when you want the change reported without anyone being credited or blamed.

</details>

# ～てある

**Meaning** · something has been done, on purpose, and the result is still there

`～てある` takes a **transitive** verb and reports the state it left behind.
Somebody did it - that is built into the meaning - but the sentence deliberately
declines to say who. What you are describing is the leftover: the note is written,
the seats are booked, the beer is in the fridge, and none of that happened by
itself.

## Formation

`ある` is the ordinary existence verb, so it conjugates exactly as you already
know it: 書(か)いてある → 書(か)いてあります → 書(か)いてあった →
書(か)いてない.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (transitive, て) | て-form + ある | 書(か)く → 書(か)いてある |
| Verb (transitive, で) | で-form + ある | 運(はこ)ぶ → 運(はこ)んである |
| Verb (する) | してある | 予約(よやく)する → 予約(よやく)してある |
| Polite | てあります | 書(か)いてあります |
| Past | てあった／てありました | 書(か)いてありました |
| Negative | てない／てありません | まだ書(か)いてありません |
| Particle (usual) | Noun + が + Verb てある | メモが置(お)いてある |
| Particle (also possible) | Noun + を + Verb てある | メモを置(お)いてある |
| Before a noun | Verb てある + Noun | 予約(よやく)してある店(みせ) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てある` only takes verbs that have an object. If the verb
takes `が` and describes something happening by itself, it wants
<code>～ている</code> instead, and there is no ❌<code>倒(たお)れてある</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb also has to leave a **result you can point at**, arranged by
someone for a reason. <code>貼(は)る</code>, <code>置(お)く</code>,
<code>書(か)く</code>, <code>入(い)れる</code>, <code>決(き)める</code> and
<code>予約(よやく)する</code> all qualify. Verbs whose result disappears into the
doer - <code>食(た)べる</code>, <code>見(み)る</code>, <code>聞(き)く</code> - 
sound odd with `てある` unless the situation genuinely makes them preparation.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
テーブルに花(はな)が<b>飾(かざ)ってある</b>。
There are flowers arranged on the table.
</pre>

<pre>
ホテルはもう<b>予約(よやく)してあります</b>。
The hotel is already booked.
</pre>

<pre>
かべに地図(ちず)が<b>はってある</b>。
There's a map up on the wall.
</pre>

<pre>
ビールは冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に<b>入(い)れてあります</b>よ。
The beer's in the fridge - I put it in there for you.
</pre>

<pre>
この箱(はこ)には何(なに)も<b>書(か)いてありません</b>でした。
There was nothing written on this box.
</pre>

<pre>
会議室(かいぎしつ)のいすは人数(にんずう)分(ぶん)<b>並(なら)べてある</b>ので、そのまま使(つか)ってください。
The chairs in the meeting room have been set out to match the headcount, so just use them as they are.
</pre>

<pre>

It said "under construction" at the entrance, so I went in by the back door.
</pre>

<pre>

The tickets and the hotel had all been booked before we left, so we could take it easy on the day.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～てある` is register-neutral. `てあります` is its polite form and there is no
casual contraction of the auxiliary itself; what does contract is the negative,
where <code>書(か)いてありません</code> becomes <code>書(か)いてない</code> in
everyday speech. <code>書(か)いてない</code> is extremely common and entirely
normal - do not read it as a different pattern.

**The invisible person is the whole point.** `～てある` is how Japanese says "this
was seen to" without naming an agent, so it turns up constantly in notices, in
handovers, and in the answer to "is everything ready?". Because the agent is
implied rather than stated, `てある` also carries a faint sense of *for you* or
*for later*: エアコンが付(つ)けてあります is not just a fact about the room, it
tells you someone thought of you.

**It quietly commits you to intention.** Saying
窓(まど)が開(あ)けてあります claims that somebody opened the window on
purpose. If the window is simply open and you have no idea why, that claim is
wrong and you want <code>開(あ)いています</code> instead.

**It reads as reporting, not as doing.** `てある` is stative, like `ある` itself,
so it does not take commands, invitations or intentions: there is no
❌置(お)いてあってください and no ❌置(お)いてあろう. When you need to ask
somebody to arrange something, the pattern you want is <code>～ておく</code>.

**On signs and in formal writing** you will also meet the passive - 
禁煙(きんえん)と書(か)かれています. It says almost the same thing but sounds
more official and more distant. `～てある` is the everyday version.

## Contrast

### ～てある vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the transitive verb takes `てある`; the intransitive verb
takes `ている`. Both describe the same room. `てある` adds that a person arranged
it that way.

</div>

This is the pair the whole transitivity system exists for. Take
<code>閉(し)まる</code> (intransitive) and <code>閉(し)める</code> (transitive):

<pre>
門(もん)が<b>閉(し)まっている</b>。
The gate is shut.
</pre>

<pre>
門(もん)が<b>閉(し)めてある</b>。
The gate has been shut. (somebody closed it, deliberately)
</pre>

You are looking at the same gate. The first sentence describes it and stops. The
second says a human being is behind the situation - which matters, because it
tells you the gate is shut *for a reason* and possibly that you should not open
it.

There is a third member of the set, and forgetting it is where learners come
unstuck. The **transitive** verb with `ている` is not a state at all - it is the
action in progress:

<pre>
係(かかり)の人(ひと)が門(もん)を<b>閉(し)めている</b>。
The attendant is closing the gate.
</pre>

So: <code>閉(し)まっている</code> = shut, nobody in view.
<code>閉(し)めてある</code> = shut, somebody saw to it.
<code>閉(し)めている</code> = somebody is closing it right now. Three sentences,
one gate, and the verb plus the auxiliary decide which you get.

### ～てある vs ～ておく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ておく` is the doing; `～てある` is what you find
afterwards. You do the `ておく`, and later somebody walks in and reports the
`てある`.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)飲(の)む分(ぶん)のコーヒーを<b>買(か)っておいた</b>。
I bought the coffee for tomorrow ahead of time.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)飲(の)む分(ぶん)のコーヒーは<b>買(か)ってある</b>。
Tomorrow's coffee is bought and waiting.
</pre>

The world is identical; the camera angle is not. The first is a report of your
action and answers "what did you do?". The second is a report of the state of the
kitchen and answers "are we out of coffee?".

The grammatical consequence is sharper than the feel. `ておく` is a live action
verb and behaves like one - <code>買(か)っておこう</code>,
<code>買(か)っておいてください</code>, <code>買(か)っておかなきゃ</code>. `てある`
can do none of that, because a state cannot be volunteered for or ordered.

### が vs を with ～てある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both occur. `が` presents the state; `を` keeps the speaker's
own action in view. `が` is much commoner, and it is the safe answer.

</div>

<pre>
ホワイトボードに予定(よてい)が<b>書(か)いてある</b>。
The schedule is up on the whiteboard.
</pre>

<pre>
ホワイトボードに予定(よてい)を<b>書(か)いてある</b>。
I've written the schedule up on the whiteboard.
</pre>

With `が`, the object has been promoted to subject and the sentence is pure
description - this is what you say when you notice something, or when you tell
somebody where to look. With `を`, the sentence keeps its object and therefore
keeps a doer hovering behind it, normally the speaker; the effect shades towards
"I've taken care of it", which is `ておく` territory.

If you are unsure, use `が`. The `を` version is fine but narrow, and it sounds
wrong when the speaker clearly did not do the deed:
❌誰(だれ)かが窓(まど)を開(あ)けてある.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 木(き)が倒(たお)れてある。 → ✅ 木(き)が<b>倒(たお)れている</b>。
  <code>倒(たお)れる</code> is intransitive, and nobody knocks a tree over on
  purpose. Events without an arranger take <code>ている</code>.

- ❌ 窓(まど)が開(あ)いてある。 → ✅ 窓(まど)が<b>開(あ)けてある</b>。
  `てある` needs the transitive half of the pair.
  <code>開(あ)く</code> + `てある` mixes the two systems and is simply not a form.

- ❌ ドアを閉(し)めてあってください。 → ✅ ドアを<b>閉(し)めておいて</b>ください。
  `てある` is a state and cannot be requested. Asking somebody to arrange
  something is <code>～ておく</code>.

- ❌ この本(ほん)はもう読(よ)んであります。（meaning "I've already read it"）→ ✅ この本(ほん)はもう<b>読(よ)みました</b>。
  `てある` reports a prepared state that someone can now make use of, not your
  personal experience of having done something. Reading a book leaves no arranged
  result for anyone to find.

## Practice

**1.** テーブルの上(うえ)にメモが置(お)いて____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

メモが置(お)いて<b>あります</b>。

<code>置(お)く</code> is transitive and somebody clearly left the note there on
purpose, so this is the `てある` state. <code>置(お)いています</code> would say
that a person is in the middle of putting it down.

</details>

**2.** 台風(たいふう)で庭(にわ)の木(き)が（倒(たお)れています・倒(たお)してあります）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

台風(たいふう)で庭(にわ)の木(き)が<b>倒(たお)れています</b>。

The typhoon knocked the tree down, so there is no arranger and no intention - 
which rules `てある` out entirely. <code>倒(たお)れる</code> is the intransitive
verb and it pairs with <code>ている</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The seats have already been reserved, so you don't need to queue."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

席(せき)はもう<b>取(と)ってある</b>ので、並(なら)ばなくてもいいですよ。

The booking is done and its result is still standing, and a person did it on
purpose - exactly what `てある` is for. <code>取(と)りました</code> would be a
plain report of the action rather than of the situation the listener is walking
into.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)を印刷(いんさつ)してあってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)を<b>印刷(いんさつ)しておいて</b>ください。

`てある` describes a state, and you cannot ask somebody to be in a state. A request
to get something done in advance uses <code>～ておく</code>. If you later want to
confirm it happened, that is when `てある` comes back:
資料(しりょう)は印刷(いんさつ)してありますか.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) かぎが掛(か)かっています。
(b) かぎが掛(か)けてあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the intransitive <code>掛(か)かる</code>: the door is locked, and that is
all the sentence claims. It might have locked itself; you are just describing what
you found.

(b) uses the transitive <code>掛(か)ける</code> with `てある`: somebody locked it,
on purpose, and the lock is still on. It implies a decision - the room was
secured, and probably for a reason you should respect.

</details>

# ～てみる

**Meaning** · do something and see what happens - try it out

`～てみる` does not mean "attempt". The action really is carried out; what is
uncertain is the **result**. You eat the thing to find out what it tastes like,
you press the button to find out what it does, you ask the question to find out
what the answer is. English "try" covers this and a second meaning that
`～てみる` cannot express, which is where most of the trouble comes from.

## Formation

`みる` here is a る-verb and conjugates exactly like the verb it comes from, so
every form you already know is available.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (て) | て-form + みる | 着(き)る → 着(き)てみる |
| Verb (で) | で-form + みる | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)んでみる |
| Verb (する) | してみる | 運転(うんてん)する → 運転(うんてん)してみる |
| Polite | てみます | 着(き)てみます |
| Past | てみた／てみました | 着(き)てみました |
| Negative | てみない | 着(き)てみない |
| Desire | てみたい | 着(き)てみたい |
| Volitional | てみよう | 着(き)てみよう |
| Request / recommendation | てみて（ください） | 着(き)てみてください |
| Conditional | てみたら／てみると | 着(き)てみたらどうですか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - write the auxiliary in **kana**. ❌<code>食(た)べて見(み)る</code>
is a spelling mistake, not a variant. The kanji is reserved for the ordinary verb
"look", which is why <code>見(み)てみる</code> - "take a look and see" - is
perfectly normal and needs both forms.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この服(ふく)を<b>着(き)てみて</b>もいいですか。
Can I try this on?
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)の新(あたら)しいラーメン屋(や)に<b>行(い)ってみた</b>。
I gave the new ramen place by the station a go.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)富士山(ふじさん)に<b>登(のぼ)ってみたい</b>です。
I'd like to climb Mt Fuji once.
</pre>

<pre>
むずかしそうだけど、とりあえず<b>やってみよう</b>。
It looks hard, but let's give it a shot anyway.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんに<b>聞(き)いてみます</b>ね。
I'll ask Tanaka and see what he says.
</pre>

<pre>
このアプリ、ためしに<b>使(つか)ってみて</b>ください。びっくりするほど便利(べんり)ですよ。
Give this app a go - it's astonishingly handy.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で<b>作(つく)ってみた</b>けれど、店(みせ)の味(あじ)にはぜんぜんかなわなかった。
I had a go at making it myself, but it was nowhere near as good as the shop's.
</pre>

<pre>
どこにもないと思(おも)っていたが、机(つくえ)の引(ひ)き出(だ)しを<b>開(あ)けてみたら</b>、なくした鍵(かぎ)はそこにあった。
I thought it was nowhere to be found, but when I opened the desk drawer to check, the lost key was sitting right there.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～てみる` is register-neutral in itself, but it is one of the most useful
politeness tools in the language, because it converts an instruction into an
invitation. <code>読(よ)んでください</code> tells somebody to read;
<code>読(よ)んでみてください</code> offers them the chance and leaves the verdict
to them. Upward, the same idea takes a longer shape:
<code>～てみていただけますか</code>, <code>～てみてはいかがでしょうか</code>.

**The past form invites a follow-up.** <code>～てみた</code> almost always wants a
result after it, because the whole point was to find something out:
食(た)べてみたけど、思(おも)ったより甘(あま)かった. A bare
<code>食(た)べてみました</code> with nothing after it sounds unfinished, like
telling half a story.

**It needs an action you control.** Weather, feelings and involuntary events
cannot be sampled: ❌雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってみる,
❌疲(つか)れてみる, ❌わかってみる. If the subject cannot decide to do it,
`てみる` is out.

**It can sound presumptuous in the wrong direction.** Suggesting that a superior
"give something a try" implies you know better and that they are the one
experimenting. To a customer or a boss, prefer
<code>一度(いちど)ご覧(らん)ください</code> or a plain polite request over
<code>～てみてください</code> for anything they are obliged to do.

**Do not confuse it with `～てみせる`.** That is a separate auxiliary meaning
"do it and show you" or, with a volitional, "I'll manage it come what may":
今度(こんど)こそ合格(ごうかく)してみせる.

## Contrast

### ～てみる vs ～ようとする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てみる` means the action **happened** and you are waiting on
the outcome. `ようとする` means you were **making the effort**, and it may never
have come off at all.

</div>

<pre>
その薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)んでみた</b>が、ぜんぜん効(き)かなかった。
I tried the medicine, but it didn't do a thing.
</pre>

<pre>
その薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)もうとした</b>が、苦(にが)くて飲(の)めなかった。
I tried to take the medicine, but it was too bitter to get down.
</pre>

In the first sentence the medicine went down; only its effect disappointed. In the
second it never got past the speaker's lips. English uses "tried" for both, so
learners reach for `てみる` in situations where the action failed to occur - and
that is a contradiction, because `てみる` presupposes it occurred.

A quick test: if you can add "…and here is what I found out", you want `てみる`.
If you have to add "…but I couldn't", you want `ようとする`.

### ～てみる vs ～たことがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てみる` is the occasion when you did it to find out.
`たことがある` is the bare fact that at some point in your life you have done it.

</div>

<pre>
なっとうを<b>食(た)べてみました</b>。
I gave natto a try.
</pre>

<pre>
なっとうを食(た)べたことがあります。
I've eaten natto before.
</pre>

English "I've tried natto" is the second of these, not the first, and that mismatch
sends learners the wrong way constantly. The first sentence is an event with a
time and a purpose behind it; the second is a line on your CV of experiences.

The two stack happily when you mean both at once:

<pre>
一度(いちど)だけ馬(うま)に乗(の)ってみたことがあります。
I've had a go at riding a horse exactly once.
</pre>

### ～てみてください vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てください` tells. `てみてください` offers. Same request,
completely different pressure on the listener.

</div>

<pre>
このボタンを<b>押(お)してください</b>。
Press this button.
</pre>

<pre>
うまく動(うご)かないときは、このボタンを<b>押(お)してみてください</b>。
If it isn't working properly, try pressing this button.
</pre>

The second version admits that the speaker is not certain it will help, which is
exactly what makes it feel considerate: the listener is being given an option, not
an order. That is also its limit. For something the listener genuinely has to do - 
a form to sign, a passport to hand over - `てみて` is wrong, because it suggests
the outcome is up for discussion:
❌パスポートを見(み)せてみてください.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べるてみる → ✅ <b>食(た)べてみる</b>
  `みる` attaches to the て-form, never to the dictionary form.

- ❌ 漢字(かんじ)を書(か)いて見(み)ました。 → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)を<b>書(か)いてみました</b>。
  The auxiliary is written in kana. Using the kanji turns it back into "looked at",
  which is not what you mean.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってみます。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るかもしれません。
  You can only sample something you choose to do. Weather does not experiment with
  itself.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、新(あたら)しいカメラを買(か)ってみました。（as a plain report）→ ✅ 昨日(きのう)、新(あたら)しいカメラを<b>買(か)いました</b>。
  `てみる` adds "to see how it would go", so on an ordinary purchase it sounds as
  though you bought the camera as an experiment and are still deciding.

## Practice

**1.** おいしいかどうかわからないので、少(すこ)し食(た)べ____ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

少(すこ)し食(た)べ<b>てみ</b>ます。

The reason given is that the outcome is unknown, which is precisely the job of
`てみる`: you carry out the action in order to find out. Plain
<code>食(た)べます</code> would lose the "and then I'll know" that the first
clause sets up.

</details>

**2.** 窓(まど)を（開(あ)けてみた・開(あ)けようとした）が、固(かた)くて動(うご)かなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けようとした</b>が、固(かた)くて動(うご)かなかった。

The window never moved, so the action did not take place - and `てみる` requires
that it did. `ようとする` is the pattern for an effort that came to nothing.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'd like to try living abroad someday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いつか外国(がいこく)に<b>住(す)んでみたい</b>です。

`てみたい` is `てみる` plus the desire ending, and it is the natural way to say you
want to sample an experience rather than simply wanting a thing.
<code>住(す)みたい</code> alone would state a settled wish to live there;
<code>住(す)んでみたい</code> keeps the sense of finding out what it is like.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: すみません、この書類(しょるい)にサインしてみてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ すみません、この書類(しょるい)に<b>サインしてください</b>。

Signing the document is not optional and there is nothing to find out, so the
invitation flavour of `てみて` is out of place - it makes a required step sound
like a suggestion the listener could decline.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 京都(きょうと)に行(い)ったことがあります。
(b) 京都(きょうと)に行(い)ってみました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a statement about your past experience: at some point you have been to
Kyoto. It carries no information about why you went or how it turned out, and it
is the right answer to "have you ever been?".

(b) is about one particular occasion, and it frames the trip as something you did
to see what Kyoto was like. It practically demands a follow-up - 
どうでしたか - because the whole point of `てみる` is the verdict that comes after.

</details>

# ～ておく / ～とく

**Meaning** · do it now so that later is taken care of - or leave it as it stands

`～ておく` frames an action as **setup**. The speaker is not merely doing the
thing; they are doing it *now* so that some later moment goes smoothly. Out of
that comes a second, quieter use: if something is already in a useful state,
`～ておく` means deliberately leaving it there instead of undoing it.

## Formation

`おく` is a う-verb and conjugates normally after the て-form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (て) | て-form + おく | 買(か)う → 買(か)っておく |
| Verb (で) | で-form + おく | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んでおく |
| Verb (する) | しておく | 用意(ようい)する → 用意(ようい)しておく |
| Polite | ておきます | 買(か)っておきます |
| Past | ておいた／ておきました | 買(か)っておきました |
| Negative | ておかない／ておきません | 買(か)っておかない |
| Deliberately not doing it | ない-form + でおく | 買(か)わないでおく |
| Volitional | ておこう | 買(か)っておこう |
| Request | ておいて（ください） | 買(か)っておいてください |

The contractions matter more than the table above, because they are what you will
actually hear. `ておく` collapses to `とく`, and `でおく` to `どく`, all the way
through the conjugation:

| Full form | Contracted |
|---|---|
| ておく | 買(か)っておく → 買(か)っとく |
| ておいた | 買(か)っておいた → 買(か)っといた |
| ておいて | 買(か)っておいて → 買(か)っといて |
| ておこう | 買(か)っておこう → 買(か)っとこう |
| でおく | 飲(の)んでおく → 飲(の)んどく |
| でおいた | 飲(の)んでおいた → 飲(の)んどいた |
| しておく | 用意(ようい)しておく → 用意(ようい)しとく |

<div class="note">

**Note** - write the auxiliary in kana. The source verb 置(お)く means "put
something down and leave it there", and that is exactly the image: you put the
situation into a state and walk away from it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ビールを<b>冷(ひ)やしておきます</b>。
I'll put the beer in to chill, ready for later.
</pre>

<pre>
出発(しゅっぱつ)前(まえ)にホテルを<b>調(しら)べておいた</b>。
I looked up hotels before we set off.
</pre>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)、コピー<b>しといて</b>くれる？
Could you get this photocopied?
</pre>

<pre>
そのままでいいですよ。エアコンは<b>つけておいて</b>ください。
Leave it as it is - please keep the air conditioning on.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)のうちに宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>やっとこう</b>。
Let's get the homework out of the way while we've got the chance.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)で配(くば)るので、資料(しりょう)を三十(さんじゅう)部(ぶ)<b>印刷(いんさつ)しておいて</b>ください。
We're handing these out at tomorrow's meeting, so please print thirty copies in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)がややこしくなるから、そのことは彼女(かのじょ)には<b>言(い)わないでおこう</b>。
It'll only complicate things, so let's keep that from her.
</pre>

<pre>
冷(さ)めてもおいしいので、お弁当(べんとう)は朝(あさ)のうちに<b>作(つく)っておきました</b>。
It tastes fine cold, so I made the lunchbox first thing this morning.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**`とく` is not slang; it is how people talk.** <code>やっとく</code>,
<code>見(み)とく</code>, <code>言(い)っとくけど</code>,
<code>覚(おぼ)えとけ</code> - the contraction runs across every age and both
genders, and refusing to use it will make your casual speech sound stiff. It stays
out of writing, out of `ます` speech and out of exams, but it belongs in your ear
from day one.

**As a request, `ておいて` carries an edge.** It hands somebody a task and closes
the topic - 「じゃ、よろしく。予約(よやく)しといて」 is what a manager says to a
junior. Aimed upward it is too brisk; use
<code>予約(よやく)しておいていただけますか</code> or
<code>予約(よやく)をお願(ねが)いできますでしょうか</code> instead.

**There is a self-interested tinge to it.** `ておく` says the action clears your
own path, so it can sound slightly calculating when the beneficiary is somebody
else: 「言(い)っておきますが…」 is a common preface meaning "just so we're
clear", and it is a mild warning shot, not a favour.

**The negative splits two ways.** <code>買(か)っておかない</code> is "I'm not
going to buy it in advance"; <code>買(か)わないでおく</code> is "I'm going to
deliberately not buy it (and leave things that way)". The second is the one people
mean when they choose to hold back - <code>聞(き)かないでおく</code>, "I'll not
ask".

**In polite speech it is completely ordinary**, and it appears constantly in
service Japanese: <code>お預(あず)かりしておきます</code>,
<code>確認(かくにん)しておきます</code>. Nothing about `ておく` is casual by
itself - only the contraction is.

## Contrast

### The two jobs of ～ておく - get it done ahead, or leave it as it is

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the て-verb *changes* something, `ておく` means do it now
for later. If the て-verb has *already produced* the state you want, `ておく`
means don't undo it.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)に席(せき)を<b>決(き)めておく</b>。
We'll settle the seating before the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
暑(あつ)いので、窓(まど)は<b>開(あ)けておいて</b>ください。
It's hot, so please leave the window open.
</pre>

There is only one grammar here. The "leave it" reading appears when the state is
already in place and closing it again would take an action - so telling somebody
<code>開(あ)けておいて</code> is telling them to *keep choosing* the open window. English needs
two different verbs, "set up" and "leave", where Japanese needs one.

The clearest case of the second reading is the fixed phrase
<code>そのままにしておく</code>:

<pre>
散(ち)らかっているけど、今日(きょう)は<b>そのままにしておこう</b>。
It's a mess, but let's just leave it for today.
</pre>

### ～ておく vs ～てある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ておく` is what you do; `てある` is what somebody finds
afterwards. `ておく` is a live action verb, `てある` is a state.

</div>

<pre>
飲(の)み物(もの)を<b>用意(ようい)しておきました</b>。
I got the drinks ready.
</pre>

<pre>
飲(の)み物(もの)は<b>用意(ようい)してあります</b>。
The drinks are ready - somebody's seen to it.
</pre>

The first answers "what did you do?"; the second answers "is everything sorted?".
The grammatical fallout is the sharper half of the distinction. Because `ておく`
is an action, it takes everything an action takes - <code>買(か)っておこう</code>,
<code>買(か)っておいてください</code>, <code>買(か)っておかなければ</code>.
`てある` takes none of them, so the moment you need a command, an invitation or an
intention, `ておく` is your only option.

Note also the particles. `ておく` keeps the original verb's frame, so the object
stays `を`: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けておく. `てある` normally promotes that object
to `が`: 窓(まど)が開(あ)けてある.

### ～ておく vs ～てしまう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ておく` looks **forward**: it's done so the future is easier.
`てしまう` looks at the **finish line**: it's done, over, off the list.

</div>

<pre>
夜(よる)のうちにレポートを<b>書(か)いておく</b>。
I'll write the report tonight so it's ready.
</pre>

<pre>
夜(よる)のうちにレポートを<b>書(か)いてしまう</b>。
I'll get the report finished off tonight.
</pre>

In practice the same evening produces both sentences, but the reason differs.
`ておく` is chosen because something comes *next* - the report is needed tomorrow.
`てしまう` is chosen because you want the thing *behind* you, complete, with
nothing left over.

Two further separations. `てしまう` can carry regret, and `ておく` never can:
there is no way to say `ておく` about something you wish had not happened. And
`てしまう` insists on completion - you finish the whole report - whereas `ておく`
is satisfied with whatever preparation is enough:
少(すこ)しだけ書(か)いておく is fine, while
❌少(すこ)しだけ書(か)いてしまう fights itself.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 買(か)うておく → ✅ <b>買(か)っておく</b>
  `おく` attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ まだホテルを予約(よやく)しておきません。 → ✅ まだホテルを<b>予約(よやく)していません</b>。
  "I haven't booked yet" is the plain <code>ていない</code>. The negative of
  `ておく` means you have *decided against* preparing, which is a different claim.

- ❌ 窓(まど)が開(あ)けておく。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けておく</b>。
  `ておく` does not change the verb's particles. The object of a transitive verb
  stays on `を`; promotion to `が` belongs to <code>てある</code>.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)、これを読(よ)んどいてください。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、こちらを<b>読(よ)んでおいて</b>いただけますか。
  Two problems at once: `どく` is a casual contraction, and `ておいてください`
  hands out tasks. Neither goes upward.

## Practice

**1.** 友(とも)だちが来(く)る前(まえ)に、部屋(へや)を片(かた)づけて____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部屋(へや)を片(かた)づけて<b>おきます</b>（casual: 片(かた)づけ<b>とく</b>）。

The tidying is being done *because* something is coming afterwards, which is
exactly the preparation reading of `ておく`. Plain
<code>片(かた)づけます</code> would report the action with no forward-looking
purpose attached.

</details>

**2.** ケーキは冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に（入(い)れておきました・入(い)れてあります）。自分(じぶん)の行動(こうどう)を報告(ほうこく)する場合(ばあい)は、どちらですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

入(い)れて<b>おきました</b>。

Reporting your own action calls for `ておく`, which is a normal action verb in the
past tense. <code>入(い)れてあります</code> would describe the state of the
fridge without saying who did it - the right choice if you were answering "is the
cake somewhere safe?".

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll charge my phone before I go out."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

出(で)かける前(まえ)に携帯(けいたい)を<b>充電(じゅうでん)しておきます</b>。

The charging exists only to serve the trip that follows, which is what `ておく`
marks. In speech this would very often come out as
充電(じゅうでん)<b>しとく</b>, and the meaning is identical.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 旅行(りょこう)のことはまだ家族(かぞく)に話(はな)しておきません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 旅行(りょこう)のことはまだ家族(かぞく)に<b>話(はな)していません</b>。／旅行(りょこう)のことは家族(かぞく)には<b>話(はな)さないでおきます</b>。

If you simply have not got round to it, the plain negative
<code>ていません</code> is what you want. If you are choosing not to tell them and
leaving it that way on purpose, the form is
<code>ない-form + でおく</code> - and those are genuinely different situations.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 電気(でんき)をつけておきます。
(b) 電気(でんき)をつけます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) simply reports switching the light on. It says nothing about why, and it could
be about the next second.

(a) says the light is being switched on *for what comes later* - so that the person
arriving home tonight is not fumbling in the dark, or so that the room is ready for
the guests. Depending on the situation it can also mean the light is already on and
you are leaving it that way rather than turning it off.

</details>

# ～てしまう / ～ちゃう

**Meaning** · finish something off completely - or do something you wish you
hadn't

`～てしまう` carries two loads at once, and learners usually notice only one of
them. The first is **completion**: the action is taken all the way to the end,
with nothing left over. The second is **regret**: it happened, it cannot be
undone, and you are not pleased about it. The same three syllables do both jobs,
and the situation tells you which.

## Formation

`しまう` is a う-verb and conjugates normally. The contracted forms are far more
common in speech than the full ones.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (て) | て-form + しまう | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べてしまう |
| Verb (で) | で-form + しまう | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んでしまう |
| Verb (する) | してしまう | 失敗(しっぱい)する → 失敗(しっぱい)してしまう |
| Polite | てしまいます | 食(た)べてしまいます |
| Past | てしまった／てしまいました | 食(た)べてしまいました |
| Volitional | てしまおう | 食(た)べてしまおう |
| て-form (for apologies) | てしまって | 遅(おく)れてしまって、すみません |

| Full form | Contracted | Example |
|---|---|---|
| てしまう | ちゃう | 食(た)べてしまう → 食(た)べちゃう |
| でしまう | じゃう | 読(よ)んでしまう → 読(よ)んじゃう |
| てしまった | ちゃった | 忘(わす)れてしまった → 忘(わす)れちゃった |
| でしまった | じゃった | 死(し)んでしまった → 死(し)んじゃった |
| てしまおう | ちゃおう | やってしまおう → やっちゃおう |
| てしまう（rough） | ちまう／じまう | 食(た)べちまう、飲(の)んじまう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the contraction follows the て/で split exactly. A verb whose
て-form ends in <b>で</b> gives <b>じゃう</b>, never ちゃう. So
<code>読(よ)んで</code> → <code>読(よ)んじゃう</code> but
<code>買(か)って</code> → <code>買(か)っちゃう</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ケーキを全部(ぜんぶ)<b>食(た)べてしまった</b>。
I ate the whole cake.
</pre>

<pre>
かさを電車(でんしゃ)に<b>忘(わす)れちゃった</b>。
I've gone and left my umbrella on the train.
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)を先(さき)に<b>やってしまいましょう</b>。
Let's get the homework done and out of the way first.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが<b>行(い)ってしまいました</b>。
The bus has gone.
</pre>

<pre>
その本(ほん)、おもしろくて一日(いちにち)で<b>読(よ)んじゃった</b>。
That book was so good I got through it in a day.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)いでいて、まだ送(おく)っていない大事(だいじ)なメールを<b>消(け)してしまいました</b>。
I was in a rush and deleted an important email I hadn't even sent yet.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)んでいるうちに、弾(ひ)き方(かた)を<b>忘(わす)れてしまった</b>。
I took a break from practising and ended up forgetting how to play.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)までにこの仕事(しごと)を<b>終(お)わらせてしまいたい</b>んですが、手伝(てつだ)っていただけませんか。
I'd like to get this job finished off before lunch - could you give me a hand?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**`ちゃった` is one of the most-heard endings in casual Japanese.** It is neutral
between men and women and across ages, and it is the default in conversation:
<code>寝坊(ねぼう)しちゃった</code>, <code>壊(こわ)しちゃった</code>,
<code>買(か)っちゃった</code>. Polite speech uses the full
<code>てしまいました</code>, and business apologies stretch it further:
<code>遅(おく)れてしまい、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません</code>.

**`ちまう` / `じまう` is a different register again** - rough, blunt, distinctly
masculine and slightly old-fashioned. You will meet it in films and manga and in
older men's speech. Recognise it; do not adopt it.

**Regret here is softer than "regret" sounds in English.** `てしまう` does not
mean you are wracked with guilt; it means *this is not how I wanted it*. That is
why it is the standard way to own up to something. 「壊(こわ)しちゃいました」
sounds honest and human, while a flat 「壊(こわ)しました」 can come across as
oddly cold, as though you broke it and do not much mind.

**The completion reading is the one that survives into plans.** With the
volitional, with `たい`, and in invitations, only the "get it finished" sense is
available: <code>やってしまおう</code>, <code>片(かた)づけてしまいたい</code>.
Nobody volunteers to regret something.

**Write `しまう` in kana.** The kanji 仕舞(しま)う belongs to the independent
verb "put away, stow" - which is the image the auxiliary comes from: you finish
with the thing and shut it away.

**In neutral writing it is an intrusion.** A news report or a lab record uses the
plain past, because `てしまう` editorialises. Seeing it in an otherwise flat text
tells you the writer has an opinion.

## Contrast

### Completion vs regret - telling them apart

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at the verb and at whether you chose it. A verb you
control, plus a quantity or a deadline, gives **completion**. An outcome that
happened *to* you gives **regret**.

</div>

<pre>
三日(みっか)でこのドラマを全部(ぜんぶ)<b>見(み)てしまった</b>。
I got through the whole drama in three days.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)にもらった大切(たいせつ)なお皿(さら)を<b>割(わ)ってしまった</b>。
I broke the plate my mother gave me.
</pre>

The first sentence has a time span and a total, and watching a drama is something
you decide to do - so `てしまう` measures the finish. The second has a valued
object and a bad outcome, so it measures the damage.

Certain verbs point one way almost automatically. <code>忘(わす)れる</code>,
<code>転(ころ)ぶ</code>, <code>落(お)とす</code>,
<code>遅(おく)れる</code>, <code>寝坊(ねぼう)する</code>,
<code>死(し)ぬ</code> - nobody plans these, so `てしまう` on them is regret.
Meanwhile <code>全部(ぜんぶ)</code>, <code>最後(さいご)まで</code>,
<code>一日(いちにち)で</code> and a volitional ending pull hard towards
completion.

And sometimes both readings sit in one sentence.
全部(ぜんぶ)食(た)べちゃった can be a satisfied "I polished off the lot" or a
sheepish "oh no, I've eaten all of it". Japanese speakers hear no ambiguity
because the tone of voice and the situation settle it - which is the honest answer
to "how do I tell?".

### ～てしまう vs ～終(お)わる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～終(お)わる</code> is the neutral fact that the
action reached its end. `～てしまう` adds an attitude: relief, thoroughness, or
dismay.

</div>

<pre>
この本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)み終(お)わった</b>ら、貸(か)してください。
Once you've finished reading this book, lend it to me.
</pre>

<pre>
貸(か)した本(ほん)を一晩(ひとばん)で<b>読(よ)んでしまった</b>そうだ。
Apparently he polished off the book I lent him in a single night.
</pre>

Three practical differences. <code>終(お)わる</code> attaches to the **ます-stem**
(読(よ)み終(お)わる), not the て-form. It is purely aspectual and can never
mean regret. And it needs a verb with a natural finishing point, so
❌忘(わす)れ終(お)わる and ❌死(し)に終(お)わる are impossible, while
`てしまう` takes both quite happily.

### ～てしまった vs the plain past

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the plain past **reports**. `てしまった` **comments**.

</div>

<pre>
さいふを<b>なくした</b>。
I lost my wallet.
</pre>

<pre>
さいふを<b>なくしてしまった</b>。
I've gone and lost my wallet.
</pre>

Both are true and both are grammatical; the second tells you how the speaker feels
about it. In apologies the auxiliary is close to obligatory, because leaving it
out makes the event sound like weather:

<pre>
待(ま)たせて<b>しまって</b>、すみませんでした。
I'm sorry to have kept you waiting.
</pre>

The reverse holds too. Where nothing is regrettable and nothing is being finished
off, adding `てしまう` is a mistake of tone rather than of grammar:
昨日(きのう)映画(えいが)を見(み)てしまいました would leave a listener
wondering what went wrong at the cinema.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べるてしまう → ✅ <b>食(た)べてしまう</b>
  `しまう` attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 読(よ)んでちゃった → ✅ <b>読(よ)んじゃった</b>
  A `で` て-form contracts to `じゃう`, not `ちゃう`. The rule follows the same
  voicing split you already learned for the て-form itself.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、遅(おく)れちゃってすみません。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、<b>遅(おく)れてしまい</b>、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。
  The contraction is casual. An apology to a superior needs the full form, and
  ideally the stiffer <code>申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません</code> as well.

- ❌ 宿題(しゅくだい)を忘(わす)れ終(お)わった。 → ✅ 宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>忘(わす)れてしまった</b>。
  <code>終(お)わる</code> needs an action with a finish line, and forgetting has
  none. This is also exactly the meaning `てしまう` exists for.

## Practice

**1.** 急(いそ)いでいたので、駅(えき)に傘(かさ)を置(お)いて____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傘(かさ)を置(お)いて<b>しまいました</b>（casual: 置(お)い<b>ちゃった</b>）。

Leaving the umbrella behind was an accident with a result the speaker regrets, so
`てしまう` is what carries that. The plain
<code>置(お)いてきました</code> would be a flat report with no sense that
anything went wrong.

</details>

**2.** 夕方(ゆうがた)までにこの仕事(しごと)を（やってしまおう・やり終(お)わろう）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夕方(ゆうがた)までにこの仕事(しごと)を<b>やってしまおう</b>。

<code>終(お)わる</code> describes an action coming to its end as a fact and is
odd in the volitional - you do not decide to reach an endpoint, you decide to get
something finished. `てしまおう` is the standard way to say "let's knock this
out".

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I overslept and missed the first train."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

寝坊(ねぼう)して、始発(しはつ)に<b>乗(の)り遅(おく)れてしまいました</b>。

Missing a train is not something anyone plans, so the regret reading is the one
that fires. In casual speech this becomes
乗(の)り遅(おく)れ<b>ちゃった</b>, which is what you would actually say to a
friend.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しいゲームをやったら、夜中(よなか)まで遊(あそ)んでちゃった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しいゲームをやったら、夜中(よなか)まで<b>遊(あそ)んじゃった</b>。

<code>遊(あそ)ぶ</code> is a `ぶ` verb, so its て-form is
<code>遊(あそ)んで</code> - a `で` form, which contracts to `じゃう`. Only verbs
whose て-form ends in `て` take `ちゃう`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この漫画(まんが)を全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)みました。
(b) この漫画(まんが)を全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)んでしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of fact: you have read the whole series, and the sentence
takes no position on it.

(b) adds the speaker's attitude. Depending on the situation it reads either as
"I got through the lot" - thoroughness, maybe a little pride at the speed - or as
"I've gone and read them all", the tone you would use if you had meant to ration
them or should have been studying instead.

</details>

# ～ていく

**Meaning** · do it and go - and, on the time axis, keep changing from now on

One idea sits behind everything `～ていく` does: **movement away from where the
speaker is standing**. Physically, that means the subject does the て-verb and
then leaves, or takes something along. On the time axis, "away from here" becomes
"on into the future", so a change that starts now and carries forward also takes
`～ていく`.

## Formation

`いく` is the ordinary motion verb and conjugates as it always does, including the
irregular て-form 行(い)って.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (て) | て-form + いく | 持(も)つ → 持(も)っていく |
| Verb (で) | で-form + いく | 運(はこ)ぶ → 運(はこ)んでいく |
| Verb (する) | していく | 注文(ちゅうもん)する → 注文(ちゅうもん)していく |
| Polite | ていきます | 持(も)っていきます |
| Past | ていった／ていきました | 持(も)っていきました |
| Negative | ていかない／ていきません | 持(も)っていかない |
| Volitional | ていこう | 持(も)っていこう |
| Change over time | い-adj + く + なっていく | 寒(さむ)くなっていく |
| Change over time | Noun／な-adj + に + なっていく | 便利(べんり)になっていく |
| Casual contraction | ていく → てく | 持(も)ってく |

<div class="note">

**Note** - as an auxiliary this is normally written in **kana**. The kanji
行(い)く is reserved for real travel, and you will see it in
持(も)って行(い)く too, but kana is the safer default and makes the
auxiliary reading obvious.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
かさを<b>持(も)っていきます</b>。
I'll take an umbrella with me.
</pre>

<pre>
鳥(とり)が山(やま)のほうへ<b>飛(と)んでいった</b>。
The bird flew off towards the mountains.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはんを<b>食(た)べていった</b>ほうがいいよ。
You'd be better off eating breakfast before you head out.
</pre>

<pre>
これから物価(ぶっか)はもっと<b>上(あ)がっていく</b>だろう。
Prices will probably keep going up from here.
</pre>

<pre>
三月(さんがつ)を過(す)ぎると、だんだん暖(あたた)かく<b>なっていきます</b>。
Once March is behind us, it gradually gets warmer and warmer.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)が重(おも)いから、車(くるま)で<b>運(はこ)んでいこう</b>。
The bags are heavy, so let's take them in the car.
</pre>

<pre>
うちは駅(えき)から遠(とお)いので、昼(ひる)ごはんは途中(とちゅう)で<b>買(か)っていく</b>ことにしている。
My place is a long way from the station, so I make a point of buying lunch on the way.
</pre>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)は時間(じかん)がかかるが、毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ<b>続(つづ)けていけば</b>、いつか話(はな)せるようになる。
Japanese takes time, but if you keep at it a little each day, one day you'll be able to speak it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～ていく` is register-neutral. `ていきます` is its polite form and nothing about
either sounds stiff or rough.

**The casual contraction is `てく`.** <code>持(も)ってく</code>,
<code>連(つ)れてく</code>, <code>やってく</code> - the `い` simply drops. The
past goes further in relaxed speech, where <code>持(も)っていった</code> becomes
<code>持(も)ってった</code>. Both belong to conversation only.

**Everything depends on where the speaker imagines standing.** If you are at home
and the party is elsewhere, you 持(も)っていく the wine. If you are already at
the party, the same bottle 持(も)ってくる - and the mistake of choosing by the
map rather than by the viewpoint is the classic one. The speaker's position can
also be a place they merely identify with: a receptionist says
<code>お持(も)ちします</code> about their own building.

**In speeches and public commitments `～ていきます` is close to a fixed formula.**
「これからも努力(どりょく)していきます」, 「安全(あんぜん)を守(まも)って
いきます」 - the auxiliary projects the promise forward, and leaving it out makes
the sentence sound like a one-off rather than a commitment.

**A past `ていった` in the change sense is perfectly normal.** It simply places
the viewpoint at a past moment and lets the change run forward from there:
人(ひと)が少(すこ)しずつ減(へ)っていった. The auxiliary is about direction,
not about tense.

## Contrast

### ～ていく vs ～てくる - which way from the speaker

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いく` = away from me. `くる` = towards me. The choice is not
about the map; it is about where the speaker is standing when they say it.

</div>

<pre>
おみやげを<b>持(も)っていく</b>。
I'll take a present with me. (to where they are)
</pre>

<pre>
おみやげを<b>持(も)ってくる</b>。
I'll bring a present back. (to where we are)
</pre>

The same split runs through every pair built this way:
<code>連(つ)れていく</code> / <code>連(つ)れてくる</code> for people,
<code>送(おく)っていく</code> / <code>送(おく)ってくる</code> for seeing
somebody off or home, <code>走(はし)っていく</code> /
<code>走(はし)ってくる</code> for a figure receding or approaching.

<pre>
犬(いぬ)が門(もん)のほうへ<b>走(はし)っていった</b>。
The dog ran off towards the gate.
</pre>

If you are on the phone to somebody in another city and you say you will visit,
Japanese takes *their* position as the destination, so the verb is
<code>行(い)く</code> where English says "I'll come" - the same instinct that
makes `ていく` the right auxiliary there.

### ～ていく vs ～に行(い)く

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～に行(い)く</code>: you go *in order to* do it, and
the doing happens at the far end. `～ていく`: you do it *first*, then go.

</div>

<pre>
パンを<b>買(か)いに行(い)く</b>。
I'm going out to buy bread.
</pre>

<pre>
パンを<b>買(か)っていく</b>。
I'll pick up bread on the way.
</pre>

The order of events is the whole difference. In the first, the going comes first
and the shop is the destination. In the second, the buying comes first - at a
shop you pass - and then you continue to wherever you were actually headed, bread
in hand.

Note the forms as well: <code>～に行(い)く</code> attaches to the **ます-stem**
(買(か)い + に), while `～ていく` attaches to the て-form. Mixing them up
produces ❌買(か)ってに行(い)く, which is not a form at all.

### "Do and go" vs "keep on" - telling the two ～ていく apart

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a concrete action somebody performs gives the **physical**
reading. A verb of change (なる, 増(ふ)える, 減(へ)る,
変(か)わる) or of continued activity (続(つづ)ける, がんばる) gives the
**time-axis** reading.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもを保育園(ほいくえん)に<b>送(おく)っていった</b>。
I dropped the kids off at nursery on my way.
</pre>

<pre>
町(まち)の様子(ようす)は少(すこ)しずつ<b>変(か)わっていった</b>。
The look of the town changed little by little.
</pre>

There is no journey anywhere in the second sentence. Nothing physically goes
anywhere; what moves is time, and the change is being followed forward from the
moment the speaker has in mind.

The adverbs are a reliable clue. <code>これから</code>, <code>今後(こんご)</code>,
<code>だんだん</code> and <code>少(すこ)しずつ</code> point forward and pull the
time reading; a destination marked with `に` or `へ` pulls the physical one.

<pre>
人口(じんこう)はこれからも<b>減(へ)っていく</b>と言(い)われている。
The population is said to be set on continuing to fall.
</pre>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 持(も)つていく → ✅ <b>持(も)っていく</b>
  `いく` attaches to the て-form. <code>持(も)つ</code> is a `つ` verb, so the
  て-form is <code>持(も)って</code>.

- ❌（arriving at the host's house）ケーキを持(も)っていきました。 → ✅ ケーキを<b>持(も)ってきました</b>。
  You are already at the destination, so the cake has come to where you both are.
  Deixis is judged from where you stand as you speak, not from where you set off.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)から寒(さむ)くなってきます。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)から寒(さむ)く<b>なっていきます</b>。
  A change running into the future takes `ていく`. <code>てくる</code> looks back
  at a change that has already reached the present.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていきました。（meaning "it started raining"）→ ✅ 雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)ってきました</b>。
  Something newly arriving at you is `てくる`. `ていく` would suggest the rain was
  moving off away from the speaker.

## Practice

**1.** 寒(さむ)いから、コートを____いきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

コートを<b>着(き)て</b>いきます。

The て-form of <code>着(き)る</code> plus `いく` gives "put it on and then go" - 
you dress first and leave afterwards. This is the everyday physical reading of the
pattern, and it is why the coat never has to be mentioned again.

</details>

**2.** 会社(かいしゃ)の近(ちか)くのパン屋(や)で昼(ひる)ごはんを（買(か)っていきます・買(か)いに行(い)きます）。通勤(つうきん)の途中(とちゅう)で買(か)う場合(ばあい)は？

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>買(か)っていきます</b>。

Buying on the way means the purchase happens first and the journey continues
afterwards, which is `ていく`. <code>買(か)いに行(い)きます</code> would mean
you make a separate trip to the bakery with buying lunch as its purpose.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "From now on, working conditions will slowly improve."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

これから、働(はたら)く環境(かんきょう)は少(すこ)しずつ<b>よくなっていく</b>でしょう。

<code>これから</code> puts the viewpoint at the present and looks forward, and the
verb is a change rather than a journey - the time-axis reading of `ていく`. Using
<code>よくなってくる</code> would describe an improvement that had already reached
us.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 友(とも)だちの家(いえ)に着(つ)いて、「おみやげを持(も)っていきました」と言(い)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 友(とも)だちの家(いえ)に着(つ)いて、「おみやげを<b>持(も)ってきました</b>」と言(い)った。

By the time you speak you are standing in your friend's house, so the souvenir has
travelled *towards* the place you now are. `ていく` would only work said before you
left, while you were still at home.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子(こ)どもの数(かず)が減(へ)ってきた。
(b) 子(こ)どもの数(かず)が減(へ)っていく。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) looks backwards from now: the number has been falling over some past stretch of
time and has arrived at today's figure. It is a report on what has already
happened.

(b) looks forwards from now: the fall is expected to continue past the present
moment into the future. It is a prediction rather than a report, which is why it
so often turns up with これから or でしょう.

</details>

# ～てくる

**Meaning** · do it and come back - and, on the time axis, a change that has
reached now

`～てくる` is the mirror image of `～ていく`: **movement towards where the speaker
is**. Three everyday uses fall out of that one idea. Someone does the て-verb
while approaching; or they go off, do it, and return; or - because "towards here"
on the time axis means "up to now" - a change has been building and has just
arrived at the present.

## Formation

`くる` is the irregular motion verb, so watch the readings: 来(き)て,
来(こ)ない, 来(こ)よう.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (て) | て-form + くる | 買(か)う → 買(か)ってくる |
| Verb (で) | で-form + くる | 呼(よ)ぶ → 呼(よ)んでくる |
| Verb (する) | してくる | 電話(でんわ)する → 電話(でんわ)してくる |
| Polite | てきます | 買(か)ってきます |
| Past | てきた／てきました | 買(か)ってきました |
| Negative | てこない／てきません | 買(か)ってこない |
| Volitional | てこよう | 買(か)ってこよう |
| Request | てきて（ください） | 買(か)ってきてください |
| Change up to now | い-adj + く + なってくる | 暑(あつ)くなってくる |
| Change up to now | Noun／な-adj + に + なってくる | 好(す)きになってくる |
| Humble | てまいります | 行(い)ってまいります |

<div class="note">

**Note** - as an auxiliary this is normally written in **kana**, which also spares
you the reading trap: 買(か)ってくる has no `く` in it to misread, whereas
買(か)って来(こ)ない invites the wrong reading of the kanji.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ちょっとコンビニに<b>行(い)ってきます</b>。
I'm just popping to the convenience store.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)ってきた</b>。
It's started raining.
</pre>

<pre>
牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を<b>買(か)ってきて</b>くれない？
Could you pick up some milk while you're out?
</pre>

<pre>
夜(よる)になって、風(かぜ)が強(つよ)く<b>なってきました</b>。
The wind has picked up now that it's evening.
</pre>

<pre>
むこうから大(おお)きな犬(いぬ)が<b>走(はし)ってきた</b>。
A big dog came running towards us.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)勉強(べんきょう)して、やっと新聞(しんぶん)が読(よ)めるように<b>なってきた</b>。
After three years of study I'm finally starting to be able to read the newspaper.
</pre>

<pre>
この地方(ちほう)では昔(むかし)から米(こめ)を<b>作(つく)ってきました</b>。
This region has grown rice since long ago.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)しているうちに、彼(かれ)が何(なに)を言(い)いたいのかだんだん<b>わかってきた</b>。
As we talked, it gradually became clear what he was getting at.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～てくる` is register-neutral, and `てきます` is its ordinary polite form. The
humble equivalent <code>てまいります</code> belongs to business and announcements - 
<code>行(い)ってまいります</code>, <code>確認(かくにん)してまいります</code> - 
and sounds noticeably formal.

**The round trip is why 「行(い)ってきます」 is the phrase you say leaving the
house.** Literally "I'll go and come back", it promises a return, which is exactly
what the household reply 「行(い)ってらっしゃい」 answers. Any errand can take the
same shape: <code>見(み)てきます</code> ("I'll go and have a look"),
<code>聞(き)いてきます</code> ("I'll go and ask").

**The change reading normally surfaces in the past.** <code>てきた</code> is far
commoner than <code>てくる</code> here, because you notice a change only once it has
arrived: 眠(ねむ)くなってきた, 見(み)えてきた, 慣(な)れてきた. The state it
announces is present even though the verb is past - this catches learners out
regularly.

**With verbs of feeling and perception it means a feeling emerging.**
<code>思(おも)えてくる</code>, <code>気(き)がしてくる</code>,
<code>腹(はら)が立(た)ってくる</code> - the sensation was not there, and now
it is welling up. This is one of the most natural-sounding uses and one learners
almost never reach for.

**There is no tidy contraction** to match `ていく` → `てく`, because `てくる` is
already short. What you will hear instead is `てくる` stacked with other
auxiliaries in casual speech - <code>買(か)ってきちゃった</code>,
<code>買(か)ってきといて</code> - and the request form clipped to
<code>買(か)ってきて</code> with the `ください` simply dropped.

## Contrast

### The three jobs of ～てくる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て-verb tells you which reading you have: a verb of
**motion** gives approach, a verb of **doing** gives a round trip, a verb of
**change or perception** gives the time axis.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが部屋(へや)に<b>入(はい)ってきた</b>。
A child came into the room.
</pre>

<pre>
切手(きって)を<b>買(か)ってきます</b>。
I'll go and buy some stamps. (and come back)
</pre>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)、日(ひ)が長(なが)く<b>なってきた</b>。
The days have been getting longer lately.
</pre>

Buying stamps involves no movement towards the speaker at all - the goods come
back with you, and the auxiliary marks the return leg of the errand. That is why
`てくる` on an action verb so often translates as "go and…", which looks backwards
until you notice that the sentence is spoken from the place you will return to.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the round-trip reading needs somewhere to come back to. Said on your
own doorstep, <code>走(はし)ってきます</code> means "I'm off for a run (and
back)". Said watching someone approach, <code>走(はし)ってきます</code> is plain
motion. Nothing in the grammar distinguishes them; the situation does.

</div>

### ～てくる vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` is the state **now**. `てくる` is the **arrival** at
that state - it was not so, and now it is.

</div>

<pre>
雪(ゆき)が<b>降(ふ)ってきた</b>。
It's started to snow.
</pre>

<pre>
雪(ゆき)が<b>降(ふ)っている</b>。
It's snowing.
</pre>

The second is what you say when somebody asks about the weather: you look out and
describe what is going on. The first is what you say the moment the first flakes
appear - the news is not the snow but the *change*, and you were there for it.

The same split runs through anything gradual:

<pre>
おなかがすいてきた。
I'm starting to get hungry.
</pre>

<pre>
おなかがすいている。
I'm hungry.
</pre>

Because `てくる` reports a change you have witnessed, it does not work for states
that have simply always been true. ❌背(せ)が高(たか)くなってきました said
about an adult's fixed height is odd; said about a growing child it is perfect.

### ～てくる vs ～ていく on the time axis

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - draw an arrow at **now**. `てきた` runs from the past into the
present; `ていく` runs from the present into the future.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)から会員(かいいん)が<b>増(ふ)えてきました</b>。
Membership has been growing since last year.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)からも会員(かいいん)は<b>増(ふ)えていく</b>でしょう。
Membership will probably go on growing next year as well.
</pre>

The time expressions give it away every time. <code>昔(むかし)から</code>,
<code>最近(さいきん)</code>, <code>これまで</code> and
<code>だんだん…てきた</code> pull `てくる`; <code>これから</code>,
<code>今後(こんご)</code> and a prediction ending pull `ていく`. A sentence with
both is a standard rhetorical move:

<pre>
これまで続(つづ)けてきたことを、これからも続(つづ)けていきたい。
I want to keep on doing what we have been doing up to now.
</pre>

Note that this contrast lives entirely on the time axis. In the *physical* uses,
`てくる` and `ていく` are about direction in space, and no amount of thinking about
tense will help you choose.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 買(か)うてくる → ✅ <b>買(か)ってくる</b>
  `くる` attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

- ❌（leaving the house）行(い)っていきます。 → ✅ <b>行(い)ってきます</b>。
  You intend to return, and the whole point of the phrase is the return, so it has
  to be `てくる`. This is a fixed expression - learn it whole.

- ❌ 昔(むかし)から日本人(にほんじん)は魚(さかな)を食(た)べていきました。 → ✅ 昔(むかし)から日本人(にほんじん)は魚(さかな)を<b>食(た)べてきました</b>。
  <code>昔(むかし)から</code> runs from the past up to the present, which is
  `てくる` territory. `ていく` would push the eating off into the future.

- ❌ さっき昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べていきました。（meaning "I went out and had lunch"）→ ✅ さっき昼(ひる)ごはんを<b>食(た)べてきました</b>。
  You left, ate, and came back to where you are speaking - a round trip, so
  `てくる`. `ていく` would mean you ate and then carried on somewhere else.

## Practice

**1.** 急(きゅう)に空(そら)が暗(くら)くなって、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>てきた</b>。

The sky darkening and then the rain arriving is a change that has just reached the
present moment, which is exactly the `てくる` reading. Note the past tense:
<code>てきた</code> announces a state that is true right now.

</details>

**2.** 田中(たなか)さんに資料(しりょう)を渡(わた)したいので、席(せき)まで（持(も)っていきます・持(も)ってきます）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

席(せき)まで<b>持(も)っていきます</b>。

You are moving the documents away from where you are towards Tanaka's desk, so the
direction is outward and `ていく` is right. `てくる` would be what Tanaka says if
he asked you to fetch them to *his* seat.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll go and ask the teacher, so wait here."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先生(せんせい)に<b>聞(き)いてきます</b>ので、ここで待(ま)っていてください。

The errand ends with you returning to the person you are speaking to, and that
return is what `てくる` marks. <code>聞(き)きます</code> alone would leave the
listener unsure whether you were coming back at all.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)ってから、だんだん数学(すうがく)がおもしろくなっていきました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)ってから、だんだん数学(すうがく)がおもしろく<b>なってきました</b>。

The change runs from a point in the past up to the speaker's present, so the arrow
points towards now - `てくる`. `ていく` would only fit if the sentence were looking
ahead: これからもっとおもしろくなっていくでしょう.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 息子(むすこ)は漢字(かんじ)が書(か)けるようになってきた。
(b) 息子(むすこ)は漢字(かんじ)が書(か)けるようになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) states the achievement as a completed fact: at some point he crossed the line
and now he can write kanji.

(a) presents the same development as a gradual approach to that line, watched from
the present. It implies the process took time, that the speaker has been observing
it, and often that it is not quite finished - he is getting there.

</details>
