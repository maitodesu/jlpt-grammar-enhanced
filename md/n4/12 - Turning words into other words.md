# N4 - Turning words into other words


---

# こと - nominaliser

**Meaning** · turns a verb, an adjective or a whole clause into a noun, so the sentence can talk *about* it

Japanese has no infinitive and no "-ing" noun. When you need an action to behave
like a thing - to be liked, to be important, to be decided at a meeting - you put
`こと` on the end of the clause. What comes out is an ordinary noun and takes `が`,
`を`, `は` or `です` like any other.

## Formation

`こと` attaches to the **plain form**. Only な-adjectives and nouns change shape
first, and they change in the way they always do before a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | dictionary form + こと | 集(あつ)める → 集(あつ)めること |
| Verb (past) | た-form + こと | 決(き)まった → 決(き)まったこと |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + こと | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないこと |
| い-adjective | plain form + こと | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいこと |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + こと | 大切(たいせつ)だ → 大切(たいせつ)なこと |
| Noun | Noun + **である** + こと | 学生(がくせい)だ → 学生(がくせい)であること |
| A whole clause | clause (plain) + こと | 兄(あに)が結婚(けっこん)する → 兄(あに)が結婚(けっこん)すること |
| As the predicate | ～ことだ／～ことです | 趣味(しゅみ)は走(はし)ることです |
| Fixed frames | ことができる／たことがある／ことにする | 泳(およ)ぐことができる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - never ❌<code>学生(がくせい)だこと</code>. A noun needs
<code>である</code> in front of `こと`, and in everyday Japanese most speakers avoid
the problem altogether by using <code>学生(がくせい)だということ</code>, which is
the clause-packaging pattern treated separately at this level.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `こと` is also an ordinary noun meaning "thing, matter", and it keeps that
job here: <code>日本(にほん)のことを知(し)りたい</code> ("I want to know about
Japan"), <code>田中(たなか)さんのこと</code> ("the Tanaka business", or simply
Tanaka as a topic). The nominaliser is the same word doing the same thing to a
clause instead of a noun.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
絵(え)をかく<b>こと</b>が好(す)きです。
I like drawing.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)を守(まも)る<b>こと</b>は大切(たいせつ)です。
Keeping to the time you agreed is important.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)の趣味(しゅみ)は古(ふる)い切手(きって)を集(あつ)める<b>こと</b>です。
My hobby is collecting old stamps.
</pre>

<pre>
外国(がいこく)で働(はたら)く<b>こと</b>を考(かんが)えています。
I'm thinking about working abroad.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)が来月(らいげつ)結婚(けっこん)する<b>こと</b>は、まだ誰(だれ)にも話(はな)していません。
I haven't told anyone yet that my brother is getting married next month.
</pre>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)で電話(でんわ)をかける<b>こと</b>が、まだ少(すこ)し怖(こわ)いです。
Making phone calls in Japanese still frightens me a little.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)で一番(いちばん)大変(たいへん)なのは、朝(あさ)五時(ごじ)に起(お)きる<b>こと</b>です。
The hardest part of the new job is getting up at five in the morning.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)する<b>こと</b>は、昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)で決(き)まりました。
It was settled at yesterday's meeting that the manager is going away on business next week.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The nominaliser itself carries no politeness. What it carries is a slight lean
towards the **written and the abstract**, which is the one thing that separates it
from its rival `の`. `こと` names a fact, a rule, a plan, a proposition - something
you can think about, decide, promise or announce. `の` names an event you are
watching, hearing or living through. Where both are grammatical, `の` is the more
spoken of the two, and a page of conversation will contain many more of them.

`こと` never contracts. `の` shrinks to `ん` before `です`, and the packaging form
`ということ` shrinks to `ってこと` in casual speech, but plain `こと` stays as it is
in every register.

<code>趣味(しゅみ)は…ことです</code> deserves a line of its own because it is the
first thing you will need it for. Self-introductions, job interviews and profile
forms are full of it, and there `の` is not an option at all - the reason is in the
Contrast section.

One more `こと` exists and it looks identical on the page. A verb plus a bare `こと`
and a full stop, with **nothing after it**, is not a noun phrase at all: it is a
written instruction, the `こと` of classroom notices and exam papers, and it is
treated as its own point at N3. The test is mechanical: if a particle or `です`
follows the `こと`, you are looking at the nominaliser on this page. If the sentence
stops dead, you are looking at a rule.

## Contrast

### こと vs の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` for what you see, hear and take part in; `こと` for facts,
plans and propositions. Where either would work, `の` is the spoken choice - but
three situations take the decision out of your hands.

</div>

**Verbs of direct perception require `の`.** 見(み)る, 聞(き)く,
聞(き)こえる, 見(み)える, 感(かん)じる are watching an event happen, and an
event is not a fact.

<pre>
外(そと)で雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した<b>の</b>に気(き)がつかなかった。
I didn't notice that it had started raining outside.
</pre>

**A nominalised clause standing in front of `だ`／`です` requires `こと`.**
<code>趣味(しゅみ)は走(はし)ることです</code> is right;
❌<code>走(はし)るのです</code> is not available, because that string already means
something else - the explanatory `んです`.

**A set of fixed frames requires `こと`.** <code>ことができる</code>,
<code>たことがある</code>, <code>ことにする</code>, <code>ことになる</code> are frozen
expressions and admit no substitution: ❌<code>泳(およ)ぐのができる</code>.

Everywhere else both are grammatical. There is one pair worth memorising because
the two versions genuinely mean different things:

<pre>
電話(でんわ)する<b>の</b>を忘(わす)れた。
I forgot to ring.
</pre>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)した<b>こと</b>を忘(わす)れた。
I forgot that I had rung.
</pre>

The first is an action that never happened; the second is a fact that slipped your
mind. That is the difference between the two words in miniature.

### ことができる vs the potential form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning at two lengths. The potential form
(話(はな)せる) is the default in speech; <code>ことができる</code> is longer,
flatter and more official, and it is what notices and manuals use.

</div>

<pre>
ここで切符(きっぷ)を買(か)う<b>ことができます</b>。
Tickets may be purchased here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここで切符(きっぷ)が<b>買(か)えます</b>。
You can buy tickets here.
</pre>

Both are correct and mean the same thing. The `こと` in <code>ことができる</code> is
frozen in place - it cannot become `の`, and it cannot be dropped - which is a good
reminder that `こと` is not always a free choice.

### Nominalising こと vs the instruction こと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what comes **after** the `こと`. A particle or `です`
means it is a noun. A full stop and nothing else means it is a rule.

</div>

<pre>
使(つか)った皿(さら)を洗(あら)う<b>こと</b>が、今週(こんしゅう)の係(かかり)の仕事(しごと)です。
Washing the dishes we've used is this week's duty.
</pre>

<pre>
使(つか)った皿(さら)は自分(じぶん)で洗(あら)う<b>こと</b>。
Wash your own dishes.
</pre>

The first sentence talks about the washing-up as a thing; the second orders it. The
second has no politeness level and no addressee - it is the voice of a notice on a
kitchen wall - and that impersonal instruction `こと` is a separate grammar point
you meet at N3. Nothing may follow it, so the moment you see `が`, `を`, `は` or
`です` after the `こと`, you are back on this page.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が学生(がくせい)<b>だこと</b>を知(し)らなかった。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が学生(がくせい)<b>だということ</b>を知(し)らなかった。
  A plain noun cannot sit in front of `こと` with `だ` on it. Everyday Japanese uses
  `だということ`; formal writing uses <code>であること</code>.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)か<b>こと</b>が一番(いちばん)大事(だいじ)です。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)か<b>なこと</b>が一番(いちばん)大事(だいじ)です。
  な-adjectives keep their `な` in front of `こと`, exactly as they do in front of
  any other noun.

- ❌ 隣(となり)の人(ひと)がピアノを弾(ひ)いている<b>こと</b>が聞(き)こえます。 → ✅ …弾(ひ)いている<b>の</b>が聞(き)こえます。
  You are hearing an event, not a fact. Verbs of direct perception take `の` and
  nothing else.

- ❌ 私(わたし)の仕事(しごと)は子(こ)どもに英語(えいご)を教(おし)える<b>のです</b>。 → ✅ …教(おし)える<b>ことです</b>。
  In front of `です`, only `こと` works. With `の` the sentence turns into the
  explanatory `んです` and stops saying what you meant.

- ❌ 朝(あさ)早(はや)く起(お)きること苦手(にがて)です。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)早(はや)く起(お)きること<b>が</b>苦手(にがて)です。
  Once the clause has become a noun it needs a particle like any other noun. The
  nominaliser does not replace `が` or `を`; it makes room for one.

## Practice

**1.** 私(わたし)の夢(ゆめ)は、いつか自分(じぶん)の店(みせ)を持(も)つ____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自分(じぶん)の店(みせ)を持(も)つ<b>こと</b>です。

The nominalised clause is standing directly in front of `です` as the predicate, and
that slot only accepts `こと`. <code>持(も)つのです</code> would be read as the
explanatory `んです` instead.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 子(こ)どもたちが公園(こうえん)で走(はし)り回(まわ)っている（の・こと）が窓(まど)から見(み)えます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

…走(はし)り回(まわ)っている<b>の</b>が窓(まど)から見(み)えます。

<code>見(み)える</code> is direct perception: the running is happening in front of
your eyes, so the nominaliser has to be `の`. `こと` would turn it into a fact you
had been told about, which is not something a window can show you.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I promised my mother that I would come home by nine."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

九時(くじ)までに家(いえ)に帰(かえ)る<b>こと</b>を母(はは)と約束(やくそく)しました。

A promise is made about a proposition, not about an event you are watching, so
`こと` is the natural choice. <code>帰(かえ)ると母(はは)に約束(やくそく)した</code>
with the quotative `と` is also perfectly good Japanese.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 毎朝(まいあさ)散歩(さんぽ)するのが私(わたし)の習慣(しゅうかん)で、健康(けんこう)にいいのは続(つづ)けるのです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 毎朝(まいあさ)散歩(さんぽ)するのが私(わたし)の習慣(しゅうかん)で、健康(けんこう)にいいのは続(つづ)ける<b>ことです</b>。

The first `の` is fine - it is followed by `が`, and either nominaliser would do
there. The second one is in front of `です`, where only `こと` is possible.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference:
(a) 鍵(かぎ)をかける<b>の</b>を忘(わす)れました。
(b) 鍵(かぎ)をかけた<b>こと</b>を忘(わす)れました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the locking never happened. `の` names the act itself, and
<code>忘(わす)れる</code> reports that you failed to carry it out - the door is
unlocked.

(b) says the locking did happen, and the memory of it is what went missing. `こと`
names the *fact* that you locked up, which is why the past form
<code>かけた</code> is possible here and impossible in (a). The door is locked; you
are just about to go back and check it.

</details>

# ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun

**Meaning** · takes a whole sentence and hands it on as a single noun - "the fact that ～", "that ～"

`こと` on its own nominalises a verb phrase. `ということ` nominalises a **finished
statement**: the quotative `という` wraps the clause up with its own ending intact,
`だ` and all, and `こと` then turns the parcel into a noun the rest of the sentence
can take as its subject or object.

## Formation

`という` attaches to a **plain-form clause** exactly as it does after
言(い)う and 思(おも)う. Nouns and な-adjectives keep their `だ`, which is the
whole reason the pattern exists.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ということ | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないということ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ということ | 決(き)まった → 決(き)まったということ |
| い-adjective | plain form + ということ | 暑(あつ)い → 暑(あつ)いということ |
| な-adjective | ～**だ** + ということ | 無理(むり)だ → 無理(むり)だということ |
| Noun | Noun + **だ** + ということ | 中止(ちゅうし)だ → 中止(ちゅうし)だということ |
| Embedded question | ～か + ということ | どこで会(あ)うか → どこで会(あ)うかということ |
| Taking a particle | ～ということ + が／を／は | ～ということを知(し)る |
| Casual speech | ってこと | 無理(むり)だ → 無理(むり)だってこと |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `だ` is what tells the two nominalisers apart. Plain `こと`
cannot follow `だ` (❌<code>中止(ちゅうし)だこと</code>), so a clause ending in a
noun or a な-adjective needs `ということ`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same `という` sits inside a family of expressions built the same way:
<code>という話(はなし)</code>, <code>という意味(いみ)</code>,
<code>という理由(りゆう)</code>. `こと` is simply the most general noun you can put
there, which is why this one turns up far more often than the others.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんが来(こ)ない<b>ということ</b>を知(し)っていましたか。
Did you know that Yamada isn't coming?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)だ<b>ということ</b>を聞(き)きました。
I heard that the meeting has been called off.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の夏(なつ)が暑(あつ)い<b>ということ</b>は、前(まえ)から知(し)っていました。
I already knew that summers in Japan are hot.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)しても続(つづ)ける<b>ということ</b>が大事(だいじ)だと思(おも)います。
I think what matters is carrying on even when you fail.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が本当(ほんとう)のことを話(はな)している<b>ということ</b>が、だんだん分(わ)かってきました。
It gradually became clear that he was telling the truth.
</pre>

<pre>
どこで待(ま)ち合(あ)わせるか<b>ということ</b>を、まだ決(き)めていません。
We still haven't settled the question of where to meet.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)が若(わか)いころ東京(とうきょう)に住(す)んでいた<b>ということ</b>は、この写真(しゃしん)を見(み)るまで知(し)りませんでした。
I had no idea my father lived in Tokyo when he was young until I saw this photograph.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に、来週(らいしゅう)までにお返事(へんじ)がほしい<b>ということ</b>を伝(つた)えておいてください。
Please pass on to the manager that we would like an answer by next week.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ということ` is register-neutral but noticeably **heavy**. It is four syllables of
scaffolding, and Japanese drops it whenever the sentence can stand without it:
<code>来(こ)ないと聞(き)きました</code> says the same thing as
<code>来(こ)ないということを聞(き)きました</code> in half the space. Reach for the
full form when the clause is long, when it is doing something a bare `と` cannot do
(taking `が`, taking `は`, sitting in front of `です`), or when you want the content
held up for inspection rather than slipped past.

In casual speech it contracts to `ってこと`, and this is extremely common:
<code>明日(あした)休(やす)みだってこと、聞(き)いてる?</code> The contraction is
conversational only - it would look wrong in a report - but it is what you will
actually hear, and learners who only know the long form miss it entirely.

Because the pattern begins with a quotative, it leans towards content that came
from **somewhere**: something said, written, decided, noticed or worked out. That
is not a rule - <code>暑(あつ)いということ</code> above is nobody's utterance - but
it explains why the pattern feels natural after 聞(き)く, 知(し)る,
伝(つた)える, 意味(いみ)する and awkward after a verb of direct perception.

One warning about the shape. Put `だ`／`です` on the end of this string and let the
sentence stop there, and you have a different pattern altogether: the hearsay
`ということだ`, "I hear that ～", which is a separate point at N3. On this page the
`ということ` is always followed by something - a particle, or a predicate that takes
it as an argument. The next section sets the two side by side.

## Contrast

### ということ vs こと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `こと` nominalises an **action**. `ということ` nominalises a
**statement**. If the clause ends in a noun or a な-adjective, only `ということ` is
available, because plain `こと` cannot follow `だ`.

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)走(はし)る<b>こと</b>は体(からだ)にいいです。
Running every day is good for you.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っている<b>ということ</b>は、誰(だれ)も知(し)りませんでした。
Nobody knew that he ran every day.
</pre>

The first names an activity; the second names a piece of information about
somebody. Where the clause is a complete statement with a subject in it, the longer
form is the natural one. Note the difference this makes with a noun in front:
<code>事故(じこ)のことを聞(き)きました</code> is "I heard about the accident" - a
topic - while <code>事故(じこ)だったということを聞(き)きました</code> is "I heard
that it was an accident", a proposition.

### ということ vs ということだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what comes **after** it. A particle means it is a noun
and the sentence is still going. `だ`／`です` closing the sentence means it is a
report: "I hear that ～".

</div>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は来月(らいげつ)終(お)わる<b>ということ</b>を、係(かかり)の人(ひと)に確認(かくにん)しました。
I checked with the person in charge that the work finishes next month.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は来月(らいげつ)終(お)わる<b>ということです</b>。
The work finishes next month, I gather.
</pre>

Same eleven characters, two different grammars. In the first the `ということ` is the
object of 確認(かくにん)する - it is a noun, and it could equally take `が` or `は`.
In the second nothing follows, `です` closes the sentence, and the whole frame has
become a sentence-final marker that puts the information at arm's length; that
hearsay use is treated at N3. The one place the test needs care is a cleft
frame - <code>問題(もんだい)なのは…ということです</code> - where the sentence does
end on です but the `のは` in front makes it plainly the nominaliser again.

### ということ vs the bare quotative と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` feeds a clause straight into 言(い)う, 思(おも)う,
聞(き)く. `ということ` makes it a noun first, so it can take a particle or be the
subject.

</div>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんは行(い)かない<b>と</b>言(い)っていました。
Hayashi said he wasn't going.
</pre>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんが行(い)かない<b>ということ</b>は、みんなに伝(つた)えました。
I've told everyone that Hayashi isn't going.
</pre>

With 言(い)う and 思(おも)う, `と` is shorter and better, and a learner who
writes `ということ` every time sounds laboured. But `と` cannot be marked with `は` or
`が`, and it cannot be the subject of an adjective, so the moment the clause needs
to move around the sentence, the full nominaliser is the only option.

## Common mistakes

- ❌（meaning "I heard that it was cancelled"）中止(ちゅうし)のことを聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 中止(ちゅうし)だ<b>ということ</b>を聞(き)きました。
  <code>中止(ちゅうし)のこと</code> is a topic - "about the cancellation". To report
  the statement itself you need the clause packaged with `という`.

- ❌ この町(まち)は静(しず)か<b>ということ</b>を知(し)りませんでした。 → ✅ この町(まち)は静(しず)か<b>だということ</b>を知(し)りませんでした。
  A な-adjective keeps its `だ` in front of `という`. This is the opposite of what
  happens before plain `こと`, where the same word takes `な`.

- ❌ 高(たか)い<b>だということ</b>が問題(もんだい)です。 → ✅ 高(たか)い<b>ということ</b>が問題(もんだい)です。
  い-adjectives never take `だ`. Only nouns and な-adjectives do.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ない<b>ということだ</b>を知(し)らなかった。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ない<b>ということ</b>を知(し)らなかった。
  The `だ` belongs to the sentence-final hearsay pattern. Inside a sentence, where a
  particle follows, the noun ends at `こと`.

- ❌ 子(こ)どもが泣(な)いている<b>ということ</b>を見(み)ました。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもが泣(な)いている<b>の</b>を見(み)ました。
  You cannot watch a proposition. Verbs of direct perception take `の`, never `こと`
  and never `ということ`.

## Practice

**1.** 田中(たなか)さんが来年(らいねん)アメリカに行(い)く____を、昨日(きのう)初(はじ)めて聞(き)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

…アメリカに行(い)く<b>ということ</b>を、昨日(きのう)初(はじ)めて聞(き)きました。

The clause is a complete statement with its own subject, and it has to become a
noun to take `を`. <code>行(い)くと聞(き)きました</code> with the bare quotative
is shorter and equally correct.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)（だということ・なこと）が今朝(けさ)決(き)まりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中止(ちゅうし)<b>だということ</b>が今朝(けさ)決(き)まりました。

<code>中止(ちゅうし)</code> is a noun, so the clause ends in `だ`, and plain `こと`
cannot follow `だ`. ❌<code>中止(ちゅうし)なこと</code> would treat it as a
な-adjective, which it is not.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I didn't know that this shop is closed on Mondays."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この店(みせ)が月曜日(げつようび)は休(やす)みだ<b>ということ</b>を知(し)りませんでした。

<code>休(やす)み</code> is a noun, so the clause keeps `だ` and needs the `という`
before `こと`. In speech you would very likely hear the contracted
<code>休(やす)みだってこと</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)が忙(いそが)しいだということは、顔(かお)を見(み)れば分(わ)かります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)が忙(いそが)しい<b>ということ</b>は、顔(かお)を見(み)れば分(わ)かります。

<code>忙(いそが)しい</code> is an い-adjective and takes no `だ`. The `だ` row in the
Formation table applies to nouns and な-adjectives only.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference:
(a) 台風(たいふう)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まった<b>ということ</b>を、駅(えき)で聞(き)きました。
(b) 台風(たいふう)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まった<b>ということです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the pattern on this page. The `ということ` is a noun, it is marked with `を`,
and the sentence carries on to say where you heard it. You are reporting your own
act of finding out.

(b) has nothing after it: `です` closes the sentence, and the frame has become the
hearsay ending taught at N3. You are not describing an event of hearing - you are
passing the information on while keeping it at arm's length, roughly "apparently
the trains have stopped."

</details>

# ～やすい

**Meaning** · easy to do - or, with some verbs, liable to happen

`～やすい` attaches to a verb and produces an い-adjective. What that adjective
describes is the **thing**, not the person: the pen is easy to write with, the
white shirt is quick to get dirty. You already met this machinery at N5 with
`～すぎる` - same ます-stem, same い-adjective coming out the other end.

## Formation

Cut the verb back to its ます-stem and add `やすい`. The result conjugates exactly
like <code>高(たか)い</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + やすい | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)いやすい |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + やすい | 覚(おぼ)える → 覚(おぼ)えやすい |
| Verb (する) | し + やすい | 説明(せつめい)する → 説明(せつめい)しやすい |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き) + やすい | 来(く)る → 来(き)やすい |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the labels used throughout this book; you will
also meet them as 五段(ごだん)/Group 1 and 一段(いちだん)/Group 2 elsewhere.

</div>

Once `やすい` is on, everything an い-adjective does is available:

| い-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | + です | 使(つか)いやすいです |
| Negative | やすい → やすくない | 使(つか)いやすくない |
| Past | やすい → やすかった | 使(つか)いやすかった |
| て-form | やすい → やすくて | 使(つか)いやすくて |
| Before a noun | やすい + Noun | 使(つか)いやすいアプリ |
| Adverbial | やすい → やすく | 使(つか)いやすくなる |
| Noun form | やすい → やすさ | 使(つか)いやすさ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the thing that is easy takes `は` or `が`, never `を`. Adding
`やすい` turns the verb into an adjective, and adjectives do not take objects:
この本(ほん)<b>は</b>読(よ)みやすい, not ❌この本(ほん)を読(よ)みやすい.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
このペンは<b>書(か)きやすい</b>。
This pen is easy to write with.
</pre>

<pre>
ひらがなは<b>覚(おぼ)えやすい</b>です。
Hiragana is easy to learn.
</pre>

<pre>
白(しろ)いシャツは<b>汚(よご)れやすい</b>。
White shirts get dirty easily.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)の説明(せつめい)は<b>分(わ)かりやすかった</b>です。
The teacher's explanation was easy to follow.
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れているときは<b>間違(まちが)えやすい</b>から、気(き)をつけてください。
You slip up easily when you're tired, so please be careful.
</pre>

<pre>
この説明書(せつめいしょ)は字(じ)が小(ちい)さくて、あまり<b>読(よ)みやすくない</b>。
The print in this manual is small, so it isn't very easy to read.
</pre>

<pre>
この靴(くつ)は軽(かる)くて<b>歩(ある)きやすい</b>ので、旅行(りょこう)のときはいつもこれをはいています。
These shoes are light and easy to walk in, so I always wear them when I travel.
</pre>

<pre>
春(はる)は天気(てんき)が<b>変(か)わりやすい</b>ですから、かさを持(も)って出(で)かけたほうがいいですよ。
The weather turns quickly in spring, so you'd do well to take an umbrella out with you.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The two meanings are not two words. Which one a listener hears is decided by the
verb underneath.

- A verb someone **chooses** to do - <code>書(か)く</code>, <code>使(つか)う</code>,
  <code>歩(ある)く</code>, <code>飲(の)む</code> - gives **ease**. The thing has a
  property that makes the action comfortable.
- A verb that **just happens** - <code>汚(よご)れる</code>, <code>割(わ)れる</code>,
  <code>忘(わす)れる</code>, <code>太(ふと)る</code>, <code>変(か)わる</code> - gives
  **propensity**. Nobody is finding anything easy; the event simply occurs
  readily.

The propensity reading is often, but not always, a complaint.
<code>壊(こわ)れやすい</code> is a defect; <code>覚(おぼ)えやすい</code> is a
compliment. Do not assume `やすい` is the positive one and `にくい` the negative
one - each takes its colour from the verb.

`～やすい` is register-neutral. It is equally at home in casual speech
（このアプリ、使(つか)いやすいね）, in polite conversation, and in written product
copy, where <code>持(も)ちやすい</code>・<code>洗(あら)いやすい</code>・<code>落(お)ちやすい</code>
are everyday advertising vocabulary.

Two derived forms are worth having now. <code>やすくなる</code> means "become easier"
or "start happening more readily":
漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えてから、新聞(しんぶん)が読(よ)みやすくなりました。
And <code>やすさ</code> is its noun - the `さ` nominaliser applied to the whole
compound: この車(くるま)は運転(うんてん)のしやすさで人気(にんき)があります。

## Contrast

### ～やすい vs ～にくい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they are opposites in meaning but not perfect mirrors in use.
Negating a `やすい` word by swapping in `にくい` is safe when the verb is a
happening; it can shift the sense when the verb is a choice.

</div>

<pre>
この皿(さら)は<b>割(わ)れやすい</b>から、気(き)をつけて。
These plates break easily, so be careful.
</pre>

<pre>
この皿(さら)は丈夫(じょうぶ)で<b>割(わ)れにくい</b>です。
These plates are sturdy and don't break easily.
</pre>

That pair is a clean mirror. Now compare <code>来(き)やすい</code>, an everyday word
meaning "easy to get to", with <code>来(き)にくい</code>, which a listener will
usually take socially - awkwardness about turning up - rather than as a statement
about distance. The lesson is to learn the useful compounds as words, not to
generate every possible one mechanically.

### ～やすい vs 簡単(かんたん)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>簡単(かんたん)だ</code> judges the **task**;
`～やすい` credits a **thing** with making the task go smoothly.

</div>

<pre>
図(ず)があるので、この問題(もんだい)は<b>考(かんが)えやすい</b>です。
There's a diagram, so this question is easy to think through.
</pre>

Compare この問題(もんだい)は簡単(かんたん)です, which says the question is not
difficult. The `やすい` sentence says nothing about its difficulty - it says the
diagram makes the thinking go smoothly. That gap matters when you write a review:
このアプリは簡単(かんたん)です sounds like the app barely does anything, while
<code>使(つか)いやすい</code> praises how it was built.

### ～やすい vs the potential form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the potential form asks *can you at all?*; `～やすい` asks
*how smoothly?*

</div>

<pre>
この字(じ)は小(ちい)さいですが、<b>読(よ)みやすい</b>です。
The characters are small, but they're easy to read.
</pre>

<code>読(よ)めます</code> is a yes-or-no ability. `やすい` is a comfort rating, and
a sentence can carry both at once:
眼鏡(めがね)をかければ読(よ)めますが、読(よ)みやすくはありません。
"I can read it with glasses on, but it isn't comfortable reading."

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 使(つか)う<b>やすい</b> → ✅ 使(つか)<b>いやすい</b>
  `やすい` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.
  使(つか)います → 使(つか)い + やすい.

- ❌ 読(よ)みやすい<b>でした</b> → ✅ 読(よ)みやす<b>かったです</b>
  The compound is an い-adjective, so the past tense is built on its `い`, and
  `です` simply rides on top without changing.

- ❌ この本(ほん)<b>を</b>読(よ)みやすいです。 → ✅ この本(ほん)<b>は</b>読(よ)みやすいです。
  Once `やすい` is attached there is no verb left to take an object, so the former
  object becomes the topic or the subject.

- ❌ うちは<b>来(こ)やすい</b>です。 → ✅ うちは<b>来(き)やすい</b>です。
  <code>来(く)る</code> contributes its ます-stem here, the same 来(き) you see
  in 来(き)ます - not the ない-form stem 来(こ).

## Practice

**1.** このスプーンは小(ちい)さいので、子(こ)どもでも使(つか)____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

使(つか)<b>いやすい</b>です

The stem is 使(つか)い, taken from 使(つか)います, and `やすい` goes straight
onto it. The spoon has a property that makes using it comfortable, which is the
ease reading.

</details>

**2.** この紙(かみ)はうすいので、（破(やぶ)れやすい・破(やぶ)れにくい）です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この紙(かみ)はうすいので、<b>破(やぶ)れやすい</b>です。

Thin paper tears readily, so you want the propensity reading. Note that
<code>破(やぶ)れる</code> is intransitive, so nobody is finding anything easy here
 - the tearing happens by itself.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This town is quiet and easy to live in."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この町(まち)は静(しず)かで<b>住(す)みやすい</b>です。

<code>住(す)む</code> gives the stem 住(す)み, and a な-adjective links onward
with `で`. <code>住(す)みやすい</code> is the ordinary way to praise a place as
liveable.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この辞書(じしょ)を引(ひ)きやすいじゃないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この辞書(じしょ)<b>は引(ひ)きやすくない</b>です。

There are two faults. The object particle `を` cannot survive, because the
compound is an adjective and adjectives take no object; and `じゃない` is the
negative for nouns and な-adjectives, while `やすい` needs `やすくない`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この皿(さら)は割(わ)りやすい。
(b) この皿(さら)は割(わ)れやすい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The difference lives in the verb, not in `やすい`. (a) uses transitive
<code>割(わ)る</code>, so somebody is deliberately breaking the plate and finding
it easy - the sort of remark you would make while smashing old crockery on
purpose.

(b) uses intransitive <code>割(わ)れる</code>, so nothing is deliberate: the plate
is fragile and breaks of its own accord. This is the sentence you actually want
when warning someone to handle it carefully.

</details>

# ～にくい

**Meaning** · hard to do - the action meets resistance, physical or social

`～にくい` is built the same way as `～やすい`, from the ます-stem, and it comes out
as an い-adjective describing the thing. But it is not simply "not easy". `にくい`
says there is something in the way: the meat is tough, the handle is the wrong
shape, the request is embarrassing to make.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + にくい | 言(い)う → 言(い)いにくい |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + にくい | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べにくい |
| Verb (する) | し + にくい | 運転(うんてん)する → 運転(うんてん)しにくい |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き) + にくい | 来(く)る → 来(き)にくい |

The compound then behaves as a plain い-adjective:

| い-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | + です | 言(い)いにくいです |
| Negative | にくい → にくくない | 言(い)いにくくない |
| Past | にくい → にくかった | 言(い)いにくかった |
| て-form | にくい → にくくて | 言(い)いにくくて |
| Before a noun | にくい + Noun | 言(い)いにくい話(はなし) |
| Adverbial | にくい → にくく | 言(い)いにくくなる |
| Noun form | にくい → にくさ | 言(い)いにくさ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `にくい` is occasionally written 難(にく)い, but kana is the normal
spelling for the suffix. Keep it apart from the unrelated adjective
憎(にく)い, "hateful", which is a full word in its own right and never attaches
to a stem.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この肉(にく)は固(かた)くて<b>食(た)べにくい</b>。
This meat is tough and hard to eat.
</pre>

<pre>
その漢字(かんじ)は<b>書(か)きにくい</b>です。
That kanji is awkward to write.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が暗(くら)くて、字(じ)が<b>読(よ)みにくい</b>。
The room is dark and the writing is hard to read.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、ちょっと<b>言(い)いにくい</b>ことなんですが…
Sorry - this is a little awkward to bring up, but…
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は説明(せつめい)が長(なが)くて、<b>分(わ)かりにくかった</b>です。
The write-up for this question was long, and it was hard to follow.
</pre>

<pre>
この傘(かさ)は風(かぜ)に強(つよ)くて、<b>壊(こわ)れにくい</b>そうです。
Apparently this umbrella stands up to wind and doesn't break easily.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいスマホは画面(がめん)が大(おお)きすぎて、片手(かたて)では<b>使(つか)いにくい</b>です。
The new phone's screen is too big, so it's awkward to use one-handed.
</pre>

<pre>
先輩(せんぱい)には少(すこ)し<b>頼(たの)みにくかった</b>ので、同(おな)じクラスの友(とも)だちに手伝(てつだ)ってもらいました。
It felt a bit awkward to ask my senior, so I got a classmate to help me instead.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にくい` covers three kinds of resistance, and it is worth naming them because
learners tend to notice only the first.

1. **Physical** - the shape, size or state of a thing fights the action:
   <code>切(き)りにくい</code>, <code>持(も)ちにくい</code>, <code>歩(ある)きにくい</code>.
2. **Social or emotional** - nothing physical is stopping you; it is simply
   uncomfortable: <code>言(い)いにくい</code>, <code>聞(き)きにくい</code>,
   <code>断(ことわ)りにくい</code>. This is one of the most useful things `にくい`
   does, and it has no neat English equivalent.
3. **Resistance to a spontaneous event** - with a verb that happens by itself,
   `にくい` means the thing holds out against it:
   <code>汚(よご)れにくい</code>, <code>冷(さ)めにくい</code>,
   <code>太(ふと)りにくい</code>. This sense is a selling point, and packaging
   copy is full of it.

The social sense earns its keep as a softener. Opening a difficult request with
言(い)いにくいんですが or お願(ねが)いしにくいのですが tells the listener you
know you are imposing, and it is standard in polite adult speech at any level of
formality.

`にくい` itself is register-neutral: no politeness, no gender or age marking. In
casual speech people often reach for `づらい` instead (see below), but
`にくい` is never wrong and is the safer choice in writing.

Two derived forms carry over from `やすい`. <code>にくくなる</code> - "become harder":
年(とし)を取(と)ると、新(あたら)しい言葉(ことば)が覚(おぼ)えにくくなります。
And the noun in `さ`: この道具(どうぐ)は使(つか)いにくさが問題(もんだい)です。

## Contrast

### ～にくい vs ～づらい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `づらい` is about **the speaker's own discomfort** while doing
something on purpose. `にくい` is about the situation, and it can also describe a
thing resisting an event nobody is doing.

</div>

`づらい` is <code>辛(つら)い</code>, "painful", in suffix form, and that origin
still shows: it reports how the action feels to the person performing it.

<pre>
足(あし)が痛(いた)くて、階段(かいだん)が<b>上(のぼ)りづらい</b>です。
My leg hurts, so the stairs are hard going.
</pre>

<pre>
このドアは重(おも)くて<b>開(あ)けにくい</b>です。
This door is heavy and hard to open.
</pre>

Both would be understood either way round, and for volitional actions
（言(い)いにくい／言(い)いづらい） the two are close to interchangeable, with
`づらい` a shade more subjective and more colloquial. The clear split is at sense
3 above: for a thing resisting a spontaneous event, `にくい` is the settled
choice. <code>壊(こわ)れづらい</code> is increasingly heard in casual speech, but
many speakers still prefer <code>壊(こわ)れにくい</code>, and it is what you will
see in print.

### ～にくい vs ～がたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がたい` means *almost impossible to bring oneself to*, it is
formal and written, and it attaches to a short fixed list of verbs.

</div>

<pre>
その話(はなし)は<b>信(しん)じがたい</b>。
That story is hard to credit.
</pre>

Compare <code>信(しん)じにくい</code>, which is the everyday spoken version and
sounds milder. `がたい` lives on a handful of verbs of thought and feeling - 
信(しん)じる, 想像(そうぞう)する, 理解(りかい)する, 忘(わす)れる - and
never on a physical action: ❌切(き)りがたい is not Japanese, while
<code>切(き)りにくい</code> is ordinary. Treat `がたい` as vocabulary to recognise
rather than a productive pattern.

### ～にくい vs the negative potential

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にくい` says the action is possible but unpleasant. The
negative potential says it cannot be done at all.

</div>

<pre>
この字(じ)は小(ちい)さくて<b>読(よ)みにくい</b>ですが、読(よ)めます。
This print is small and hard on the eyes, but I can read it.
</pre>

Swap in 読(よ)めません and the sentence contradicts itself. This distinction is
worth practising because English uses "can't read this" loosely for both. If a
magnifying glass would solve it, you want `にくい`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>にくい</b> → ✅ 食(た)<b>べにくい</b>
  Like `やすい` and `すぎる`, `にくい` attaches to the ます-stem, never the
  dictionary form.

- ❌ この本(ほん)は難(むずか)し<b>にくい</b>です。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は<b>読(よ)みにくい</b>です。
  `にくい` attaches to verbs only. There is no way to hang it on an adjective;
  if you want "hard", the adjective 難(むずか)しい already says it.

- ❌ 使(つか)いにくい<b>でした</b> → ✅ 使(つか)いにく<b>かったです</b>
  The whole compound is an い-adjective, so the past tense is formed on its `い`.

- ❌ この靴(くつ)<b>を</b>歩(ある)きにくいです。 → ✅ この靴(くつ)<b>は</b>歩(ある)きにくいです。
  There is no verb left to take an object once `にくい` is attached, so the thing
  being described becomes the topic.

## Practice

**1.** このコップは口(くち)が小(ちい)さいので、洗(あら)____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

洗(あら)<b>いにくい</b>です

The stem is 洗(あら)い, from 洗(あら)います. A narrow opening physically gets
in the way of washing, which is exactly the resistance `にくい` reports.

</details>

**2.** この服(ふく)は色(いろ)が濃(こ)いので、汚(よご)れが（目立(めだ)ちやすい・目立(めだ)ちにくい）です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

汚(よご)れが<b>目立(めだ)ちにくい</b>です。

Dark colours hide marks, so the stains resist standing out. This is the third
sense: a thing holding out against something that happens by itself, which is the
one advertising copy uses constantly.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "That's a hard thing to ask my boss."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

それは部長(ぶちょう)には<b>聞(き)きにくい</b>ことです。

Nothing physical prevents the question; the obstacle is social, and that is
precisely what `にくい` conveys. <code>聞(き)きづらい</code> would also work here
and sounds slightly more personal.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この机(つくえ)は重(おも)くて運(はこ)ぶにくいでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この机(つくえ)は重(おも)くて<b>運(はこ)びにくかった</b>です。

Two faults in one short sentence: `にくい` needs the ます-stem 運(はこ)び, not
the dictionary form, and the past of an い-adjective is `にくかった`, never
`にくいでした`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この質問(しつもん)は答(こた)えにくいです。
(b) この質問(しつもん)には答(こた)えられません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says an answer is available but uncomfortable to give - you know the answer
and would rather not say it, or the question is delicate. It leaves the door open
and often precedes an answer anyway.

(b) shuts the door: you cannot answer, whether from ignorance or from a rule that
forbids it. Choosing (b) when you mean (a) sounds far more final than you
probably intend, which is why (a) is the diplomatic option.

</details>

# ～さ

**Meaning** · turns an adjective into a noun - the height, the weight, the difficulty of it

At N5 you learned to nominalise a whole clause with `の`. `さ` does something
narrower and more mechanical: it takes a single adjective and gives you the noun
for the **dimension it measures**. <code>高(たか)い</code> is "high";
<code>高(たか)さ</code> is "height" - a scale that short things have too.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | drop い + さ | 長(なが)い → 長(なが)さ |
| な-adjective | stem (no な, no だ) + さ | 便利(べんり)だ → 便利(べんり)さ |
| いい／よい | よ + さ (irregular) | いい → よさ |
| ない | な + さ | ない → なさ |
| ～やすい／～にくい | drop い + さ | 読(よ)みやすい → 読(よ)みやすさ |
| ～たい | たい → たさ | 見(み)たい → 見(み)たさ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - whatever you would put in front of `くない` (for い-adjectives)
or in front of `です` (for な-adjectives) is what `さ` attaches to. If you can
still see an `い` or a `な`, you have cut too little.

</div>

A `さ`-noun is an ordinary noun from that moment on. It takes `が`, `を`, `の`,
`に` like any other: 重(おも)さ**を**測(はか)る、寒(さむ)さ**に**驚(おどろ)く、
広(ひろ)さ**が**足(た)りない。

<div class="note">

**Note** - `さ` is productive: given an adjective, you can generally build its
`さ`-noun and be understood. The exceptions are な-adjectives that are already
nouns in their own right - 健康(けんこう), 自由(じゆう), 安心(あんしん) - 
where the bare word normally does the job and the `さ`-form sounds laboured.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この山(やま)の<b>高(たか)さ</b>は三千(さんぜん)メートルです。
This mountain is three thousand metres high.
</pre>

<pre>
テーブルの<b>長(なが)さ</b>を測(はか)ってください。
Please measure the length of the table.
</pre>

<pre>
この荷物(にもつ)の<b>重(おも)さ</b>はどのくらいですか。
How much does this parcel weigh?
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)の<b>暑(あつ)さ</b>は特別(とくべつ)でした。
The heat this summer was something else.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)の<b>やさしさ</b>に感謝(かんしゃ)しています。
I'm grateful for my teacher's kindness.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)の<b>よさ</b>は、人(ひと)が親切(しんせつ)なところです。
What's good about this town is that the people are kind.
</pre>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)してみて、漢字(かんじ)の<b>難(むずか)しさ</b>がやっと分(わ)かりました。
Now that I've tried studying Japanese, I finally understand how hard kanji are.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は<b>広(ひろ)さ</b>が十分(じゅうぶん)ですが、窓(まど)が少(すく)なくて暗(くら)いです。
This room is big enough, but it has few windows and it's dark.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The single most useful thing to understand about `さ` is that it is **neutral**.
<code>高(たか)さ</code> does not mean "tallness" in the sense of being tall; it
names the scale, and a low thing has one too. That is why
この机(つくえ)の高(たか)さは七十(ななじゅう)センチです is perfectly
ordinary Japanese about an unremarkable desk. The same goes for
<code>長(なが)さ</code>, <code>重(おも)さ</code>, <code>広(ひろ)さ</code>,
<code>深(ふか)さ</code>, <code>速(はや)さ</code> - these are the words you use
to ask for a measurement.

With adjectives that judge rather than measure - 
<code>難(むずか)しさ</code>, <code>楽(たの)しさ</code>, <code>大切(たいせつ)さ</code>,
やさしさ - the noun names *how much* or *the quality of being so*, and it tends to
turn up after verbs of realising and feeling:
大切(たいせつ)さに気(き)づく、楽(たの)しさを知(し)る。

`さ` is register-neutral, and it is if anything commoner in writing than in
speech: product specifications, recipes, weather reports and essays all lean on
it. Nothing about it sounds stiff in conversation either.

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not confuse this suffix with the casual sentence-final particle
`さ`, as in そんなの関係(かんけい)ないさ. That one attaches to a finished
sentence, is markedly casual, and leans male in most speech. Two unrelated
words that happen to share a syllable.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～さ vs ～み

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さ` is productive and measures. `み` is **not productive**: it
belongs to a fixed set of established words, and it names something felt or
concrete rather than a quantity.

</div>

This is the pair learners most want to treat as a free choice, and it is not one.
You may build a `さ`-noun from any adjective; you may only *use* the `み`-nouns
that already exist.

<pre>
この石(いし)の<b>重(おも)さ</b>は五(ご)キロです。
This stone weighs five kilos.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)の言葉(ことば)には<b>重(おも)み</b>があります。
The manager's words carry weight.
</pre>

The established pairs are worth learning as vocabulary:
<code>深(ふか)さ</code> is a depth in metres while <code>深(ふか)み</code> is a
deep spot or a richness of character; <code>甘(あま)さ</code> is a degree of
sweetness while <code>甘(あま)み</code> is the taste itself;
<code>楽(たの)しさ</code> is how enjoyable something is while
<code>楽(たの)しみ</code> has become a plain noun meaning something you look
forward to. Some adjectives have only the `み` form in common use - the noun for
hurting is <code>痛(いた)み</code>, not <code>痛(いた)さ</code>, unless you
specifically mean the degree of the pain.

Anything outside that set is not available: ❌新(あたら)しみ,
❌難(むずか)しみ, ❌広(ひろ)み. When in doubt, use `さ`.

### ～さ vs こと／の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さ` nominalises **one adjective** and names its degree.
`こと` and `の` nominalise **a whole clause** and name the fact.

</div>

<pre>
川(かわ)の<b>深(ふか)さ</b>に驚(おどろ)きました。
I was taken aback by how deep the river was.
</pre>

Compare 川(かわ)が深(ふか)いことに驚(おどろ)きました, "I was surprised at
the fact that the river was deep." The `さ` version takes the depth for granted
and points at its degree; the `こと` version presents the depth as news. The `さ`
one is also the only option once you attach a number:
深(ふか)さは二(に)メートルです, never ❌深(ふか)いことは二(に)メートルです.

Because `さ` only takes adjectives, a verb has no `さ`-form at all: for
"swimming", you still need 泳(およ)ぐこと or 泳(およ)ぐの.

### ～さ vs leaving the adjective alone

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - use `さ` only when the sentence needs a **noun** in that slot.
If the adjective can simply be the predicate, leave it as an adjective.

</div>

Learners who have just met `さ` tend to sprinkle it everywhere, and the result is
a sentence that says the same thing twice:

<pre>
この山(やま)は<b>高(たか)い</b>です。
This mountain is high.
</pre>

❌この山(やま)の高(たか)さは高(たか)いです is not a stronger version of that
sentence; it is a broken one, roughly "the height of this mountain is high." Use
`さ` when a noun is genuinely required - as the object of
<code>測(はか)る</code>, after `に`, before `です` with a number, or as the topic
of a comparison: この山(やま)の高(たか)さは富士山(ふじさん)と同(おな)じくらいです。

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 高(たか)<b>いさ</b> → ✅ 高(たか)<b>さ</b>
  The final `い` of an い-adjective is replaced, not kept.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>なさ</b>／静(しず)か<b>ださ</b> → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>さ</b>
  な-adjectives contribute their bare stem. Neither `な` nor `だ` survives.

- ❌ この町(まち)の<b>いさ</b> → ✅ この町(まち)の<b>よさ</b>
  `いい` is irregular everywhere, and `さ` is no exception - it builds on the
  older stem `よ`, exactly as `よくない` and `よかった` do.

- ❌ 旅行(りょこう)の楽(たの)し<b>み</b>を友(とも)だちに話(はな)しました。（meaning "I told them how fun the trip was"）→ ✅ 旅行(りょこう)の楽(たの)し<b>さ</b>を友(とも)だちに話(はな)しました。
  <code>楽(たの)しみ</code> is a separate word meaning something you are looking
  forward to, so the sentence with `み` says you talked about your anticipation.

## Practice

**1.** このプールの深(ふか)____は二(に)メートルです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

深(ふか)<b>さ</b>は二(に)メートルです

A number of metres needs a noun in front of `です`, and that noun is the
measurement word. Drop the `い` of 深(ふか)い and add `さ`.

</details>

**2.** 来週(らいしゅう)の旅行(りょこう)が（楽(たの)しさ・楽(たの)しみ）です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来週(らいしゅう)の旅行(りょこう)が<b>楽(たの)しみ</b>です。

This is the fixed lexical `み` word, meaning something you look forward to, and it
is the only natural choice here. <code>楽(たの)しさ</code> would name how
enjoyable the trip was, which you cannot yet know about next week.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I was surprised at the size of this city."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この町(まち)の<b>大(おお)きさ</b>に驚(おどろ)きました。

<code>驚(おどろ)く</code> takes `に`, and `に` needs a noun in front of it, so the
adjective has to be nominalised. <code>大(おお)きい</code> drops its `い` and
takes `さ`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この荷物(にもつ)の重(おも)いさを教(おし)えてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この荷物(にもつ)の<b>重(おも)さ</b>を教(おし)えてください。

The `い` of 重(おも)い is replaced by `さ`, not followed by it. A useful check:
the finished noun should end in exactly one `さ` and contain no `い` from the
adjective ending.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この石(いし)の重(おも)さ
(b) この石(いし)の重(おも)み

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the measurable weight, the thing a scale reports in kilos. It is neutral
and works for a pebble as well as a boulder.

(b) is the felt heaviness - the sense of substance or gravity the stone gives you
when you pick it up, and by extension the impressiveness of something. It is one
of the established `み` words, and it is a judgement rather than a measurement.
You cannot generate new `み` nouns this way; where no fixed `み` word exists, `さ`
is what you use.

</details>

# ～方 - way of doing

**Meaning** · the way of doing something - how to do it, or the manner in which someone does it

Read `かた`. Attach it to a verb's ます-stem and you get a noun:
<code>作(つく)り方(かた)</code> "how to make it",
<code>話(はな)し方(かた)</code> "the way someone speaks". This is the single
most productive noun-building suffix at N4, and it turns any verb you already know
into something you can ask about, teach, learn and criticise.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + 方(かた) | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)い方(かた) |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + 方(かた) | 開(あ)ける → 開(あ)け方(かた) |
| Verb (する) | the noun is 仕方(しかた) | する → 仕方(しかた) |
| Noun + する | Noun + の + 仕方(しかた) | 予約(よやく)する → 予約(よやく)の仕方(しかた) |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き) + 方(かた) | 来(く)る → 来(き)方(かた)（rare） |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the object particle `を` becomes `の`.
漢字(かんじ)<b>を</b>書(か)く → 漢字(かんじ)<b>の</b>書(か)き方(かた).
Nothing in the phrase is a verb any more, so nothing can take an object.

</div>

A destination `に`／`へ` usually survives, with `の` added after it:
空港(くうこう)へ行(い)く → 空港(くうこう)への行(い)き方(かた). Some
fixed pairs simply drop the particle instead - バスに乗(の)る is normally
バスの乗(の)り方(かた) - so learn the common ones as phrases.

The finished word is an ordinary noun and behaves like one:
使(つか)い方(かた)<b>が</b>分(わ)からない、作(つく)り方(かた)<b>を</b>習(なら)う、話(はな)し方(かた)<b>で</b>分(わ)かる。

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>方</code> is only one of the things a ます-stem can do. The bare stem is
itself a noun-maker, and a good deal of everyday vocabulary is built that way:
動(うご)く → 動(うご)き, 始(はじ)まる → 始(はじ)まり,
帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)り, 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)え,
休(やす)む → 休(やす)み. Not every verb yields a usable one, so treat these
as words to collect rather than forms to generate - but when you meet a noun that
looks like a ます-stem, it usually is one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この機械(きかい)の<b>使(つか)い方(かた)</b>を教(おし)えてください。
Please show me how to use this machine.
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)の<b>読(よ)み方(かた)</b>が分(わ)かりません。
I don't know how to read this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
カレーの<b>作(つく)り方(かた)</b>は意外(いがい)と簡単(かんたん)です。
Curry is easier to make than you'd think.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)の<b>話(はな)し方(かた)</b>はとてもていねいです。
The way she speaks is very polite.
</pre>

<pre>
空港(くうこう)への<b>行(い)き方(かた)</b>をスマホで調(しら)べた。
I looked up how to get to the airport on my phone.
</pre>

<pre>
おはしの<b>持(も)ち方(かた)</b>は子(こ)どものときに習(なら)いました。
I learned how to hold chopsticks when I was a child.
</pre>

<pre>
パソコンの調子(ちょうし)が悪(わる)いので、修理(しゅうり)の<b>仕方(しかた)</b>をネットで探(さが)しています。
My computer is playing up, so I'm hunting online for how to repair it.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じことを伝(つた)えても、<b>言(い)い方(かた)</b>によって相手(あいて)の気持(きも)ちは変(か)わります。
Even when you convey the same thing, how you put it changes how the other person feels.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>～方</code> splits into two closely related senses, and it is worth knowing which one
you are producing.

- **Method** - <code>作(つく)り方(かた)</code>,
  <code>使(つか)い方(かた)</code>, <code>行(い)き方(かた)</code>,
  <code>直(なお)し方(かた)</code>. These answer "how do I…?" and are what you
  ask a shop assistant or a recipe.
- **Manner** - <code>話(はな)し方(かた)</code>,
  <code>歩(ある)き方(かた)</code>, <code>食(た)べ方(かた)</code>,
  <code>笑(わら)い方(かた)</code>. These describe someone's personal style and
  almost always come with an evaluation attached:
  食(た)べ方(かた)がきれいですね。

Because the manner sense evaluates a person, it can carry criticism.
言(い)い方(かた)が悪(わる)かった - "I put that badly" - is a standard
apology, and あの人(ひと)の話(はな)し方(かた)はちょっと… is a soft way of
saying you dislike how someone talks. Nothing about the suffix itself is rude; the judgement
comes from the adjective you pair it with.

The suffix is register-neutral and equally normal in speech and writing.
<code>使(つか)い方(かた)を教(おし)えていただけますか</code> is a polite
request; <code>使(つか)い方(かた)分(わ)かる?</code> is what you say to a
friend. Only the surrounding grammar changes.

One high-frequency idiom: <code>仕方(しかた)がない</code> - "it can't be
helped", "there's nothing for it". In casual speech this contracts to
<code>仕方(しかた)ない</code> or, very commonly, しょうがない. Learn it as a set
phrase; the <code>方</code> inside it is the same word, but the meaning of the whole is
fixed.

## Contrast

### ～方(かた) vs 方法(ほうほう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～方</code> is built out of a specific verb and is everyday.
<code>方法(ほうほう)</code> is a free-standing noun meaning "method", and it is
more abstract and more formal.

</div>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)の<b>解(と)き方(かた)</b>を教(おし)えてください。
Please show me how to solve this problem.
</pre>

Compare この問題(もんだい)を解決(かいけつ)する方法(ほうほう)を考(かんが)えましょう
 - "let's think of a way to resolve this issue." The <code>方</code> version is about the
concrete steps of one action; <code>方法(ほうほう)</code> takes a whole clause in
front of it and suits reports, meetings and instructions. In a manual you will
often see both: 支払(しはら)い方法(ほうほう) for the section heading and
払(はら)い方(かた) in the shop.

### ～方(かた) vs どうやって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - どうやって **asks** the question. <code>～方</code> packages the answer as
a noun you can then do things to.

</div>

<pre>
このお茶(ちゃ)の<b>いれ方(かた)</b>は少(すこ)し難(むずか)しいです。
The way you brew this tea is a little tricky.
</pre>

You could ask このお茶(ちゃ)はどうやっていれますか and get the same information,
but only the noun can be the subject of 難(むずか)しい, the object of
習(なら)う, or the thing you 忘(わす)れた. That is the whole point of
nominalising: どうやって is a question word and cannot sit in a noun slot.

### 方(かた) vs 方(ほう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a ます-stem, <code>方</code> is always **かた**. The `ほう` reading
belongs to the comparison word <code>～の方(ほう)が</code>, which follows a noun
or a plain-form clause, not a stem.

</div>

<pre>
バスの<b>乗(の)り方(かた)</b>が分(わ)からなくて、駅員(えきいん)さんに聞(き)きました。
I didn't know how to ride the bus, so I asked a station attendant.
</pre>

Set that against 電車(でんしゃ)の方(ほう)が速(はや)いです, "the train is
faster", where <code>方</code> means "side" and is read ほう. There is a third word here too:
<code>方</code> read かた as a free noun is the polite word for a person - 
あの方(かた)はどなたですか. Same character, three jobs; the stem test sorts out
the suffix instantly.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 読(よ)む<b>方(かた)</b> → ✅ 読(よ)<b>み方(かた)</b>
  <code>方</code> attaches to the ます-stem, like `やすい` and `すぎる`. The dictionary form
  before <code>方</code> produces the *ほう* comparison word instead, which means something
  else entirely.

- ❌ 漢字(かんじ)<b>を</b>書(か)き方(かた) → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)<b>の</b>書(か)き方(かた)
  The compound is a noun, so its former object is attached with `の`.

- ❌ 運転(うんてん)<b>し方(かた)</b> → ✅ 運転(うんてん)<b>の仕方(しかた)</b>
  A noun + する verb uses the lexicalised noun 仕方(しかた), linked with `の`.

- ❌ この言葉(ことば)の読(よ)み<b>方(ほう)</b>を教(おし)えてください。 → ✅ …読(よ)み<b>方(かた)</b>…
  A written mistake becomes a spoken one the moment you read it aloud. On a
  ます-stem the reading is かた, always.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この料理(りょうり)の作(つく)____を教(おし)えてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

作(つく)<b>り方(かた)</b>を教(おし)えてください

The stem of 作(つく)る is 作(つく)り, from 作(つく)ります, and <code>方</code> goes
straight onto it. Note the `の` linking it to the dish, where the plain verb would
have taken `を`.

</details>

**2.** ネットで（予約(よやく)の仕方(しかた)・予約(よやく)し方(かた)）が分(わ)かりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ネットで<b>予約(よやく)の仕方(しかた)</b>が分(わ)かりません。

<code>予約(よやく)する</code> is a noun + する verb, and that type takes the
lexicalised noun 仕方(しかた), joined on with `の`. The stem of する does not
appear bare in front of <code>方</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Could you tell me how to get to the station?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)への<b>行(い)き方(かた)</b>を教(おし)えていただけますか。

<code>駅(えき)へ行(い)く</code> keeps its destination particle, and `の` is
added so the phrase can modify the noun. <code>教(おし)えていただけますか</code>
is the polite way to ask a stranger.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 日本語(にほんご)を話(はな)す方(かた)を習(なら)いたいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 日本語(にほんご)<b>の話(はな)し方(かた)</b>を習(なら)いたいです。

Two corrections at once: the dictionary form 話(はな)す becomes the stem
話(はな)し, and the object particle `を` on 日本語(にほんご) becomes `の`,
because there is no longer a verb to take an object.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の字(じ)の書(か)き方(かた)はきれいです。
(b) 彼(かれ)は字(じ)を書(か)くのが上手(じょうず)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the manner itself under the microscope: his stroke order, his hand, the
shape of his writing. <code>方</code> names a style, so the compliment is about *how* the
action is carried out.

(b) uses the N5 nominaliser `の` on the whole action and rates his ability at it.
It says he is good at writing, without committing to anything about what the
result looks like. Use <code>方</code> when the way of doing it is the point, and `の` when
the doing is.

</details>

# ～がる

**Meaning** · to show signs of feeling something - how you report another person's inner state

Japanese does not let you simply announce what somebody else feels. `～がる`
solves that: it takes an adjective of emotion or sensation and turns it into a
**verb** meaning "behaves as though X". You are no longer claiming to know what is
in their head; you are describing what you can see them doing.

## Formation

`がる` attaches to the adjective stem and produces an ordinary う-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | drop い + がる | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)がる |
| な-adjective | stem + がる | 嫌(いや)だ → 嫌(いや)がる |
| ほしい | ほし + がる | ほしい → ほしがる |
| ～たい | たい → たがる | 行(い)きたい → 行(い)きたがる |

Once formed, it conjugates as a う-verb like <code>作(つく)る</code>:

| Verb form | Example |
|---|---|
| Polite | 怖(こわ)がります |
| Negative | 怖(こわ)がらない |
| Past | 怖(こわ)がった |
| て-form | 怖(こわ)がって |
| Progressive / current state | 怖(こわ)がっている |
| Person noun | 怖(こわ)がり、寒(さむ)がり、恥(は)ずかしがり屋(や) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がる` makes a transitive verb, so what the feeling is aimed at
takes `を`, not the `が` the original adjective wanted:
おもちゃ<b>が</b>ほしい → おもちゃ<b>を</b>ほしがる.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `がる` only attaches to adjectives describing **how someone feels**:
寒(さむ)い, 暑(あつ)い, 痛(いた)い, 怖(こわ)い, うれしい,
悲(かな)しい, さびしい, 恥(は)ずかしい, うらやましい, ほしい,
嫌(いや)だ, 残念(ざんねん)だ. It cannot attach to an adjective describing a
thing from outside: there is no ❌高(たか)がる or ❌赤(あか)がる.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子(こ)どもが<b>寒(さむ)がって</b>います。
The children are feeling the cold.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は犬(いぬ)を<b>怖(こわ)がります</b>。
My little sister is afraid of dogs.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は新(あたら)しい時計(とけい)を<b>ほしがって</b>いる。
My father's had his eye on a new watch.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は注射(ちゅうしゃ)のあとで腕(うで)を<b>痛(いた)がって</b>いました。
My son was making a fuss about his arm after the injection.
</pre>

<pre>
猫(ねこ)はおふろを<b>嫌(いや)がる</b>ので、洗(あら)うのが大変(たいへん)です。
Cats hate baths, so washing them is hard work.
</pre>

<pre>
みんなが<b>不思議(ふしぎ)がって</b>いたので、理由(りゆう)を説明(せつめい)しました。
Everyone seemed puzzled, so I explained the reason.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんは一人(ひとり)で住(す)んでいるので、ときどき<b>さびしがり</b>ます。
Yamada lives on his own, so he gets lonely from time to time.
</pre>

<pre>
先輩(せんぱい)は留学(りゅうがく)の話(はなし)を聞(き)いて、ずいぶん<b>うらやましがって</b>いました。
My senior sounded thoroughly envious when he heard about the exchange programme.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The heart of `がる` is **evidence**. <code>痛(いた)がっている</code> does not mean
"is in pain"; it means the person is behaving in a way that shows pain - wincing,
holding the spot, saying it hurts. That is why the pattern belongs to third
persons: you can see other people's behaviour, but you feel your own sensations
directly.

**Bare `がる` versus `がっている`.** The plain form describes a habit or a general
truth: 子(こ)どもは注射(ちゅうしゃ)を怖(こわ)がる is "children are afraid
of injections", a statement about children at large. For what someone is doing
right now, use `がっている`.

**First person.** You would not normally say
❌私(わたし)は今(いま)寒(さむ)がっています - for your own present feeling,
the plain adjective 寒(さむ)いです is what you want. But `がる` is fine about
yourself when you step outside your own experience: describing a habit
（私(わたし)は昔(むかし)から暗(くら)いところを怖(こわ)がります）or looking
back on your younger self.

**Tone.** Because it reports behaviour, `がる` can slide into "making a fuss
about", and with some verbs it tends to sound faintly critical or amused. That
tendency, plus the fact that it is a bald description of someone's inner life,
makes it a poor fit for talking about a superior. Prefer a hedge:
部長(ぶちょう)は残念(ざんねん)そうでした, or
残念(ざんねん)だとおっしゃっていました.

**The `がり` nouns.** Drop the verb ending and you get a noun for the kind of
person: 寒(さむ)がり (someone who feels the cold), 暑(あつ)がり,
怖(こわ)がり, and with `屋` attached, さびしがり屋(や) and
恥(は)ずかしがり屋(や). These are everyday self-descriptions - 
私(わたし)、寒(さむ)がりなんです is a perfectly normal thing to say in
January.

`がる` on `たい` gives `たがる`, which is common enough to get its own treatment;
everything above about evidence, third persons and politeness applies there too.

## Contrast

### ～がる vs ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` is a guess from **how something looks** at a glance.
`がる` reports **what someone is actually doing**.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は上着(うわぎ)をぬいで、ずっと<b>暑(あつ)がって</b>います。
He's taken his jacket off and has been hot the whole time.
</pre>

Compare 彼(かれ)は暑(あつ)そうです, "he looks hot" - one look, one inference,
no claim about behaviour over time. Two further differences follow from this.
`そうだ` works on things as well as people
（このケーキ、おいしそう）, while `がる` needs someone with feelings; and `そうだ`
is a much softer thing to say about a superior, which is why it is the polite way
out.

### ～がる vs the plain adjective

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in a plain main-clause statement, an emotion adjective about a
third person needs `がる` or a hedge. About yourself, use the bare adjective.

</div>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)のリーさんは国(くに)の料理(りょうり)が食(た)べられなくて、<b>さびしがって</b>います。
Lee, the exchange student, can't get food from home and is feeling homesick.
</pre>

A flat ❌リーさんはさびしいです claims direct access to Lee's feelings. The escapes
are `がる`, or one of the reporting frames you already have:
さびしいそうです, さびしいと言(い)っています, さびしいようです. Inside a
subordinate clause the restriction lifts entirely - 
さびしい人(ひと)は連絡(れんらく)してください is fine.

### ほしがる vs ほしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` is an い-adjective and takes `が`; `ほしがる` is a verb
and takes `を`. The particle change is the giveaway that the word class changed.

</div>

<pre>
娘(むすめ)は自分(じぶん)のパソコンを<b>ほしがって</b>います。
My daughter wants a computer of her own.
</pre>

Set that against 私(わたし)は自分(じぶん)のパソコンがほしいです for your own
want. The same asymmetry you met with `たい` at N5 is at work: the adjective is
for the inside view, the `がる` verb for the outside one. And as with `たい`,
`ほしがっている` is the normal shape for a want someone has right now, while bare
ほしがる suits a habit.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 寒(さむ)<b>いがる</b> → ✅ 寒(さむ)<b>がる</b>
  The final `い` is replaced, exactly as it is for `さ` and `すぎる`.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)はおもちゃ<b>が</b>ほしがっています。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)はおもちゃ<b>を</b>ほしがっています。
  `がる` builds a transitive verb, and transitive verbs take `を`. Leaving `が` in
  place is the single commonest slip with this pattern.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は<b>高(たか)がる</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は高(たか)いです。
  `がる` needs an adjective about someone's feelings. 高(たか)い describes a
  price, not an emotion, so there is nothing for `がる` to report.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は今(いま)寒(さむ)<b>がって</b>います。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は今(いま)寒(さむ)<b>いです</b>。
  You do not need evidence for your own present sensation. Reserve `がる` for
  other people, or for yourself viewed as a type of person.

## Practice

**1.** 子(こ)どもがアイスクリームを____います。（ほしい）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

アイスクリームを<b>ほしがって</b>います

Drop the `い` of ほしい and add `がる`, then put it in the て-form for a want that
is happening now. Notice that the particle on アイスクリーム is `を`, because
ほしがる is a verb.

</details>

**2.** 弟(おとうと)は新(あたら)しい自転車(じてんしゃ)を（ほしいです・ほしがっています）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弟(おとうと)は新(あたら)しい自転車(じてんしゃ)を<b>ほしがっています</b>。

Your brother is a third person, so a flat ほしいです would be claiming to feel his
desire yourself. The `を` already in the sentence is another clue that the verb
form is the one being asked for.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My daughter is afraid of dark places."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

娘(むすめ)は暗(くら)いところを<b>怖(こわ)がります</b>。

This is a standing trait rather than something happening at this moment, so the
plain form is right; 怖(こわ)がっています would describe her right now. The
thing feared takes `を`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 友(とも)だちは新(あたら)しいゲームがほしいがっています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 友(とも)だちは新(あたら)しいゲーム<b>をほしがって</b>います。

Two faults. The `い` of ほしい has to go before `がる` attaches, and the particle
must move from `が` to `を` because the result is a transitive verb.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は寒(さむ)そうです。
(b) 彼(かれ)は寒(さむ)がっています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is your inference from appearance in the moment - he is thinly dressed, his
shoulders are hunched, so he looks cold. You are guessing, and you are being
tentative about it.

(b) says he is behaving in a way that displays being cold: rubbing his hands,
saying it is freezing, edging towards the heater. It is a report of observable
conduct rather than a guess, and depending on tone it can carry a hint that he is
making rather a lot of it.

</details>

# ～たがる

**Meaning** · someone else wants to do something - as read off their behaviour

`～たがる` is `がる` applied to `たい`. At N5 you learned that plain `～たい` is
only for your own wishes and for asking about the listener's; `～たがる` is the
form that lets you talk about everybody else. It is a verb, not an adjective, and
it reports a want the way an observer sees it: from the outside, in what the
person does.

## Formation

Take the ます-stem, add `たがる`. Equivalently: build the `たい` form and swap
`たい` for `たがる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + たがる | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)みたがる |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + たがる | 出(で)かける → 出(で)かけたがる |
| Verb (する) | し + たがる | 運動(うんどう)する → 運動(うんどう)したがる |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き) + たがる | 来(く)る → 来(き)たがる |

The result is a plain う-verb and conjugates like one:

| Verb form | Example |
|---|---|
| Polite | 行(い)きたがります |
| Negative | 行(い)きたがらない |
| Past | 行(い)きたがった |
| て-form | 行(い)きたがって |
| Current state | 行(い)きたがっている |
| Past state | 行(い)きたがっていた |
| Person noun | 目立(めだ)ちたがり屋(や) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the object takes `を`, never the `が` that `たい` permits.
水(みず)<b>が</b>飲(の)みたい, but
水(みず)<b>を</b>飲(の)みたがっている. `たい` is an adjective; `たがる` is a
verb, and verbs take `を`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は外(そと)で<b>遊(あそ)びたがって</b>います。
My little brother wants to play outside.
</pre>

<pre>
猫(ねこ)が部屋(へや)に<b>入(はい)りたがって</b>いる。
The cat wants to get into the room.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べたがりません</b>。
My son won't eat his vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもはなんでも<b>知(し)りたがる</b>ものです。
Children want to know about everything - that's just how they are.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は自分(じぶん)のことをあまり<b>話(はな)したがらない</b>。
He isn't very forthcoming about himself.
</pre>

<pre>
友(とも)だちがその映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)たがって</b>いたので、一緒(いっしょ)に見(み)に行(い)きました。
A friend of mine had been wanting to see that film, so we went to see it together.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は病院(びょういん)に<b>行(い)きたがらない</b>ので、家族(かぞく)はみんな心配(しんぱい)しています。
My father won't go to the doctor, so the whole family is worried.
</pre>

<pre>
クラスのみんなが京都(きょうと)へ<b>行(い)きたがって</b>いたので、週末(しゅうまつ)に旅行(りょこう)を計画(けいかく)しました。
Everyone in the class had been wanting to go to Kyoto, so I planned a weekend trip.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The negative is the workhorse.** <code>～たがらない</code> is at least as common as
the positive, and it does a job English handles with "won't":
薬(くすり)を飲(の)みたがらない is not a neutral report of an absent desire - 
it says the person is visibly resisting. Doctors, parents and teachers use it
constantly.

**Bare form versus `～たがっている`.** As with `がる`, the plain form states a habit
or a general truth - 彼(かれ)はいつも新(あたら)しいことを試(ため)したがる - 
while `たがっている` reports what someone wants at this moment. If you are
describing what is going on in front of you, `ている` is almost always the right
choice, and leaving it off is a common learner slip.

**Tone.** Because it describes someone from outside, `たがる` can carry a faint
edge - "keeps wanting to", "is always trying to". That edge is what powers
<code>目立(めだ)ちたがり屋(や)</code>, "attention seeker", and it is why the
pattern is a bad idea about anyone you are being polite to. Do not say
❌部長(ぶちょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)りたがっています. Say instead
部長(ぶちょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)りたいとおっしゃっていました, or
お帰(かえ)りになりたいようです.

**Yourself.** `たがる` about your own present wish is wrong - you do not need to
observe yourself. It does become available when you describe your own character
from a distance:
私(わたし)は昔(むかし)から人前(ひとまえ)で歌(うた)いたがらない性格(せいかく)です。

**Register.** The pattern itself is neutral: no politeness marking, no gender or
age association, equally at home in speech and writing. What is marked is *who*
you point it at.

## Contrast

### ～たがる vs ～たい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たい` for me and for you; `たがる` for everyone else. Three
things change at once: the person, the particle, and the word class.

</div>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)はピアノを<b>習(なら)いたがって</b>います。
My little sister wants to take piano lessons.
</pre>

Say 私(わたし)はピアノが習(なら)いたいです about yourself and every piece of
the sentence differs: `が` is allowed, and the polite form is `です` on an
adjective rather than `ます` on a verb. Mixing the two produces
❌習(なら)いたがりたいです or ❌習(なら)いたがるです, both of which come from
forgetting that `たがる` is a verb.

The restriction is on **plain main-clause statements** only. Inside a modifying
clause, plain `たい` about a third person is fine:
参加(さんか)したい人(ひと)は受付(うけつけ)に来(き)てください。

### ～たがっている vs ～たがる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たがっている` for a want someone has **now**; bare `たがる` for
a **habit** or a general truth about a group.

</div>

<pre>
赤(あか)ちゃんが<b>だっこされたがって</b>います。
The baby wants to be picked up.
</pre>

Compare 赤(あか)ちゃんは夜(よる)になるとだっこされたがる, "babies want to be
held once evening comes" - a pattern of behaviour rather than a report on this
baby right now. The generic use is where you will most often meet the bare form,
frequently rounded off with `ものだ`.

### ～たがる vs ～たいそうです・～たいと言(い)っています

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たがる` says you **saw** it. The reporting frames say you
**heard** it, and they are the polite route upward.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは会議(かいぎ)に出(で)たがっていません。
Tanaka doesn't want to sit in on the meeting.
</pre>

That sentence claims to read Tanaka from his conduct, which is fine among peers
and rude about a superior. The alternatives shift the source of the information:
出(で)たくないそうです ("I hear he doesn't"),
出(で)たくないと言(い)っています ("he says he doesn't"),
出(で)たくないようです ("he seems not to"). All three are softer, and the last
two are what you use in a workplace when the person outranks you.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>たがる</b> → ✅ 行(い)<b>きたがる</b>
  `たがる` builds on the ます-stem, exactly like `たい` does.

- ❌ 子(こ)どもがジュース<b>が</b>飲(の)みたがっています。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもがジュース<b>を</b>飲(の)みたがっています。
  The `が` option belongs to `たい`. Once the word is a verb, the object must be
  marked with `を`.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)は行(い)きたがっ<b>たです</b>。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)は行(い)きたがっ<b>ていました</b>。
  Learners carry `たい`'s adjective endings across. `たがる` is a う-verb: the past
  polite is 行(い)きたがりました, and for a past state, 行(い)きたがっていました.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)は新(あたら)しい辞書(じしょ)を買(か)いたがっています。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)は新(あたら)しい辞書(じしょ)を買(か)いたいとおっしゃっていました。
  Grammatical, but it inspects a teacher's motives out loud. Report what they
  said instead.

## Practice

**1.** 妹(いもうと)は外国(がいこく)で働(はたら)____います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

働(はたら)<b>きたがって</b>います

The stem 働(はたら)き takes `たがる`, and the て-form plus います gives the
current-desire shape. Your sister is a third person, so plain
働(はたら)きたいです would be overstepping.

</details>

**2.** （私(わたし)・弟(おとうと)）は海(うみ)へ行(い)きたがっています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>弟(おとうと)</b>は海(うみ)へ行(い)きたがっています。

`たがる` describes a desire inferred from someone's behaviour, which rules out
yourself: you do not need evidence of your own wishes. For the first person the
sentence would be 私(わたし)は海(うみ)へ行(い)きたいです.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My grandmother doesn't want to use a smartphone."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

祖母(そぼ)はスマホを<b>使(つか)いたがりません</b>。

The negative of a う-verb, and the form that carries the "won't, resists it"
flavour English gets from "doesn't want to". 使(つか)いたがらない is the plain
equivalent.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 友(とも)だちは新(あたら)しいカメラがほしたがっています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 友(とも)だちは新(あたら)しいカメラ<b>をほしがって</b>います。

`たがる` attaches to a verb stem, and ほしい is an adjective, so there is no
ほしたがる - the form built from ほしい is ほしがる. The particle also has to move
from `が` to `を`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)はもっと話(はな)したがっています。
(b) 彼(かれ)はもっと話(はな)したいと言(い)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is your reading of his behaviour: he keeps leaning in, he has not finished,
you can tell he wants to keep going. Nobody has necessarily said anything, and
the judgement is yours - which is why it would be presumptuous about a superior.

(b) hands the claim back to him. He said it; you are relaying it. This version is
safe in any company, and it is what you would write in a message to a manager.

</details>

# ～がする

**Meaning** · a sensation reaches you - a smell, a sound, a taste, a feeling

`する` is not always "do". With a small set of sensation nouns it means something
closer to "there is" or "it comes to me": 音(おと)がする, "there's a sound";
においがする, "there's a smell". Nobody is performing anything. The sensation
arrives, and the noun naming it is the subject.

## Formation

Sensation noun + `が` + `する`. There is no conjugating of the noun; only `する`
moves.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sound | 音(おと)・声(こえ) + がする | 変(へん)な音(おと)がする |
| Smell | におい・香(かお)り + がする | いいにおいがする |
| Taste | 味(あじ) + がする | 甘(あま)い味(あじ)がする |
| Feeling, hunch | 気(き)・感(かん)じ + がする | 気(き)がする |
| Bodily sensation | 寒気(さむけ)・頭痛(ずつう)・めまい + がする | めまいがする |
| Sense of a presence | 気配(けはい) + がする | 人(ひと)の気配(けはい)がする |

`する` is irregular and carries the whole conjugation:

| Verb form | Example |
|---|---|
| Polite | 音(おと)がします |
| Negative | 音(おと)がしない |
| Past | 音(おと)がした |
| て-form | 音(おと)がして |
| Ongoing | 音(おと)がしている |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the sensation is the **subject**, so the particle is `が`.
Never ❌においをする. Nothing is being done to the smell; the smell is what is
there.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the list of nouns that work this way is short and fixed. Sight is not
on it: for something visible you use 見(み)える, never ❌山(やま)がする.
Pain is not on it either - it is 頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いです or
痛(いた)みがあります, though the noun 頭痛(ずつう) does take がする.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
だれかの<b>声(こえ)がする</b>。
I can hear someone's voice.
</pre>

<pre>
台所(だいどころ)からいい<b>においがします</b>。
There's a lovely smell coming from the kitchen.
</pre>

<pre>
このスープは少(すこ)し甘(あま)い<b>味(あじ)がします</b>。
This soup tastes slightly sweet.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)で変(へん)な<b>音(おと)がした</b>ので、窓(まど)を開(あ)けてみました。
There was an odd noise outside, so I opened the window to look.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)から<b>寒気(さむけ)がする</b>ので、今日(きょう)は会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みます。
I've had chills since this morning, so I'm taking the day off work.
</pre>

<pre>
どこかでお会(あ)いしたような<b>気(き)がします</b>。
I have a feeling we've met somewhere before.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は古(ふる)い本(ほん)の<b>においがして</b>、なんだか落(お)ち着(つ)きます。
This room smells of old books, and something about that is calming.
</pre>

<pre>
さっきから上(うえ)の階(かい)で人(ひと)が歩(ある)いている<b>音(おと)がして</b>、なかなか眠(ねむ)れません。
I've been hearing someone walking about upstairs for a while now, and I can't get to sleep.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The defining feature of `がする` is that the sensation is **unbidden and often
unidentified**. 音(おと)がする is what you say when something made a noise and
you do not yet know what: you noticed, you did not investigate. That is why it
pairs so naturally with 変(へん)な, 大(おお)きな and だれかの - words that
admit you have not pinned the source down.

The noun can be modified freely, and this is where most of the expressive work
happens: レモンの味(あじ)がする, 焦(こ)げたにおいがする,
子(こ)どもの声(こえ)がする. A `の` phrase in front means "the smell/taste/sound
*of* that thing", which is how you say something tastes *like* something else.

Tense matters more than usual here. `がした` reports one moment - a bang, a whiff.
`がする` in the plain present covers a sensation happening now or as a general
property （このお菓子(かし)はバターのにおいがする）. `がしている` is available for
something clearly ongoing, but Japanese reaches for the plain present far more
readily than English does with "is smelling".

**`気がする` deserves separate attention.** It is one of the most used expressions
in the spoken language: どこかで聞(き)いたことがあるような気(き)がする,
「忘(わす)れ物(もの)をしたような気(き)がする」. It reports a hunch you cannot
justify, and because it commits you to nothing it is very useful for softening - 
少(すこ)し高(たか)いような気(き)がします is a gentler way to say something
is overpriced.

The pattern is register-neutral: identical in casual and polite speech, and
completely standard in writing. The medical uses - 
寒気(さむけ)がする, 吐(は)き気(け)がする, めまいがする - are the phrases
you actually need at a clinic, and they are worth memorising as units.

## Contrast

### ～がする vs 聞(き)こえる・見(み)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>聞(き)こえる</code> says **you heard** a particular
thing. `がする` says **there was** a sound. And there is no visual `がする`: for
sight, only <code>見(み)える</code>.

</div>

<pre>
夜中(よなか)に猫(ねこ)の<b>声(こえ)がして</b>、目(め)が覚(さ)めました。
A cat's cry in the night woke me up.
</pre>

Compare 猫(ねこ)の声(こえ)が聞(き)こえました, "I could hear a cat." Both are
correct, and the difference is where the sentence puts you. `聞こえる` is about
your ears reaching the sound and implies you made it out; `がする` is about the
sound being there at all, and sits comfortably with not knowing what it was. This
is also why 気配(けはい)がする works while ❌気配(けはい)が聞(き)こえる does
not - a presence is not a thing you hear.

### 気(き)がする vs と思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>気(き)がする</code> is a hunch with no grounds behind
it. <code>と思(おも)う</code> is an opinion you would defend.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうな<b>気(き)がします</b>。
I've got a feeling it's going to rain today.
</pre>

Say 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ると思(おも)います instead and you sound as though you
checked the forecast. `気がする` explicitly declines to give a reason, which makes
it the safer form when you are half-remembering something or disagreeing gently
with someone senior.

### ～がする vs ～をしている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がする` is a sensation coming **to** you. `をしている`
describes a property a thing **has** - its colour, its shape, its expression.

</div>

<pre>
この石(いし)は変(へん)わった形(かたち)をしています。
This stone has an unusual shape.
</pre>

Same verb, opposite direction, opposite particle. 色(いろ)をしている,
形(かたち)をしている, 顔(かお)をしている are the three you will actually meet:
青(あお)い目(め)をした猫(ねこ), 悲(かな)しそうな顔(かお)をしていた.
Sensations never take `を`, and appearances never take `が` in this construction.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ いいにおい<b>を</b>します。 → ✅ いいにおい<b>が</b>します。
  The smell is the subject of the sentence, not an object someone acts on.

- ❌ この花(はな)はいいにおいが<b>あります</b>。 → ✅ この花(はな)はいいにおいが<b>します</b>。
  English "has a nice smell" pushes learners towards ある. Japanese treats a
  perceived smell as something that *does* - it `する`.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)から頭痛(ずつう)を<b>しています</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)から頭痛(ずつう)が<b>します</b>。
  Same particle error in a medical setting, where being understood matters most.

- ❌ あそこに大(おお)きい建物(たてもの)が<b>します</b>。 → ✅ あそこに大(おお)きい建物(たてもの)が<b>見(み)えます</b>。
  `がする` covers hearing, smell, taste and bodily feeling - never sight.

(b) 下(した)の階(かい)の音(おと)が聞(き)こえます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

who asked whether you can hear anything. Where (a) is about the event, (b) is
about your perception of it.

</details>

# ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences

**Meaning** · singles out one part of a sentence and puts it in the spotlight - "the one who did it was…", "it was last year that…"

Take a sentence, nominalise all of it except the piece you care about, and put
that piece after `は`…`だ`. 弟(おとうと)が窓(まど)を割(わ)った becomes
窓(まど)を割(わ)った<b>のは弟(おとうと)です</b> - "the one who broke the
window was my brother." Everything before `のは` is treated as already agreed;
everything after it is the answer.

## Formation

The clause takes the plain form and then `の`, exactly as with the N5 nominaliser.
The focused element follows `は`, with `だ` or `です` closing the sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | dictionary form + のは…だ | 作(つく)る → 作(つく)るのは母(はは)です |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のは…だ | 落(お)とした → 落(お)としたのは弟(おとうと)です |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + のは…だ | 来(こ)なかった → 来(こ)なかったのは田中(たなか)さんです |
| い-adjective | plain form + のは…だ | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いのはこちらです |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + のは…だ | 好(す)きだ → 好(す)き**な**のは秋(あき)です |
| Focus = a reason | …のは…**からです** | 休(やす)んだのは熱(ねつ)があったからです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the subject inside the `の`-clause takes `が`, never `は`. The
sentence already has its `は`, and it belongs to the `のは` frame:
私(わたし)<b>が</b>買(か)ったのはこの本(ほん)です.

</div>

When the focus is a time, a place or a person, the particle that element would
have carried is normally dropped: 会(あ)ったのは駅(えき)です. Keeping it - 
駅(えき)ででした - is possible in careful writing but sounds heavy, and
learners are safer without it. A reason is the exception: it keeps `から` and adds
です, as in the last row above.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `の` here is a light noun meaning "the one / the thing / the time".
You can often replace it with the specific noun for extra clarity:
割(わ)った<b>人(ひと)</b>は弟(おとうと)です,
日本(にほん)に来(き)た<b>とき</b>は去年(きょねん)でした. `の` is the
general-purpose version, and it is what you reach for when no particular noun
suggests itself.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この絵(え)を<b>かいたのは妹(いもうと)です</b>。
My little sister is the one who drew this picture.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が<b>好(す)きなのは秋(あき)です</b>。
The season I like is autumn.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に<b>来(き)たのは去年(きょねん)の春(はる)です</b>。
It was last spring that I came to Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>悪(わる)いのは私(わたし)です</b>。本当(ほんとう)にすみませんでした。
I'm the one at fault. I really am sorry.
</pre>

<pre>
最初(さいしょ)に<b>気(き)がついたのは田中(たなか)さんでした</b>。
Tanaka was the one who noticed first.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)<b>休(やす)んだのは熱(ねつ)があったからです</b>。
The reason I was off yesterday is that I had a temperature.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が毎朝(まいあさ)<b>飲(の)むのはコーヒーではなく紅茶(こうちゃ)です</b>。
What I drink every morning isn't coffee but tea.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)で<b>反対(はんたい)したのは山田(やまだ)さんだけ</b>で、ほかの人(ひと)はみんな賛成(さんせい)しました。
The only person who objected at the meeting was Yamada; everyone else was in favour.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

A cleft splits a sentence into two halves that do different jobs. The `の`-clause
is the part you and your listener are already taking for granted - somebody broke
the window, I came to Japan at some point. The part after `は` is the news, and
because the frame exists only to deliver it, the emphasis is much stronger than
in an ordinary sentence.

That makes clefts the natural answer to a question word. Ask
だれが割(わ)ったんですか and 割(わ)ったのは弟(おとうと)です answers it
exactly; ask いつ来(き)ましたか and 来(き)たのは先週(せんしゅう)です does
the same. It is equally the form for **correcting** someone, because the frame
lets you accept everything except the one piece you are disputing:
私(わたし)が言(い)ったのはそういう意味(いみ)ではありません。

The pattern is register-neutral. In polite speech the ending is です／でした; in
casual speech it becomes `だ`, or vanishes altogether - 
落(お)としたの、弟(おとうと)? is a perfectly ordinary question between
friends. It is if anything even commoner in writing, where
大切(たいせつ)なのは… and 問題(もんだい)なのは… open paragraphs constantly.

One shape worth learning whole: <code>大切(たいせつ)なのは…ことです</code>. When
the focus itself is an action rather than a thing, it has to be nominalised too,
and there the nominaliser must be `こと`, not `の` - see the next section.

## Contrast

### ～のは○○だ vs plain `が` focus

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` presents its noun as the news. The cleft does that *and*
states out loud what everyone is presupposing, which makes it more emphatic and
more exclusive.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)遅(おく)れたのは<b>私(わたし)です</b>。
The one who was late yesterday was me.
</pre>

Compare 昨日(きのう)私(わたし)が遅(おく)れました, which simply reports the
event with the subject highlighted. The cleft takes for granted that somebody was
late - it is the sentence you use when that is already on the table - and it
carries a clear implication that nobody else was. That exclusivity is why clefts
are so useful for owning up, for correcting, and for singling one item out of a
list.

### `の` vs `こと` as the nominaliser

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` when the focus is a **person, thing, time or place**.
`こと` when what you are naming is itself an **action or a proposition**, and
always when the nominalised clause sits before `だ`／`です` as the predicate.

</div>

For a cleft that identifies an entity, only `の` works:
落(お)としたのは弟(おとうと)です, never ❌落(お)としたことは弟(おとうと)です.
A person is not an event, so `こと` has nothing to name.

Turn the sentence round, though, and the requirement flips:

<pre>
私(わたし)の趣味(しゅみ)は<b>本(ほん)を読(よ)むことです</b>。
My hobby is reading.
</pre>

Here the nominalised clause is the predicate, and ❌読(よ)むのです is not
available - that string already means something else entirely, the explanatory
`んです`. The same rule governs
大切(たいせつ)なのは毎日(まいにち)続(つづ)けることです.

Two more places where the choice is fixed, both worth memorising. Before a verb
of direct perception - 見(み)る, 聞(き)く, 聞(き)こえる,
見(み)える - only `の` is possible, because you witness an event rather than a
fact: 子(こ)どもが泣(な)いているのを聞(き)きました. And a set of idioms
takes `こと` and nothing else: ことができる, たことがある.

### ～のは○○だ vs ～んです

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both contain `の`, and they do opposite things. `んです`
explains the **whole** situation. `のは…だ` isolates **one element** of it.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)する<b>のは大阪(おおさか)です</b>。
The place I'm travelling to next week is Osaka.
</pre>

Set that beside 来週(らいしゅう)大阪(おおさか)に出張(しゅっちょう)するんです,
"the thing is, I'm off to Osaka next week" - an explanation offered as background,
with no single element picked out. If you can point at one word and say "that is
the answer", you want the cleft; if the whole sentence is the answer, you want
`んです`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>は</b>一番(いちばん)好(す)きなのは夏(なつ)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>が</b>一番(いちばん)好(す)きなのは夏(なつ)です。
  The subject sits inside the nominalised clause, and a clause like that takes
  `が`. Two `は` in one sentence leaves the first one homeless.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>だ</b>のは朝(あさ)だけです。 → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>のは朝(あさ)だけです。
  な-adjectives take `な` before a nominalising `の`, the same shape they use
  before `ので` and `のに`.

- ❌ 大切(たいせつ)なのは練習(れんしゅう)する<b>のです</b>。 → ✅ 大切(たいせつ)なのは練習(れんしゅう)する<b>ことです</b>。
  A nominalised clause standing before `です` must use `こと`. With `の` the
  sentence turns into an explanatory `んです` and stops meaning what you wanted.

- ❌ 皿(さら)を割(わ)った<b>ことは</b>猫(ねこ)です。 → ✅ 皿(さら)を割(わ)った<b>のは</b>猫(ねこ)です。
  When the focus is a creature, a thing or a time, the nominaliser is `の`.
  `こと` would be naming an event, which cannot equal a cat.

**1.** この料理(りょうり)を作(つく)った____父(ちち)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

作(つく)った<b>のは</b>父(ちち)です

The cook is a person, so the light noun `の` is the one you want, followed by `は`
to mark everything before it as shared background. The past form
作(つく)った goes straight in front of `の`.

</details>

**2.** 私(わたし)の夢(ゆめ)は世界(せかい)を旅行(りょこう)する（の・こと）です。

私(わたし)の夢(ゆめ)は世界(せかい)を旅行(りょこう)する<b>こと</b>です。

A nominalised clause immediately in front of `です` has to use `こと`. Writing
するのです would produce the explanatory `んです` pattern, which says something
completely different.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It was in Osaka that we first met."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

初(はじ)めて会(あ)った<b>のは大阪(おおさか)でした</b>。

The meeting is presupposed and the place is the news, which is exactly what a
cleft is for. Note that the `で` you would use in
大阪(おおさか)で会(あ)いました is dropped once the place moves into the focus
slot.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)学校(がっこう)を休(やす)んだのは頭(あたま)が痛(いた)かったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)学校(がっこう)を休(やす)んだのは頭(あたま)が痛(いた)かった<b>からです</b>。

When the element in focus is a reason rather than a thing, the clause has to be
closed with `から` before `です`. Without it the sentence claims that the act of
staying home *was* a headache.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 弟(おとうと)が皿(さら)を落(お)としました。
(b) 皿(さら)を落(お)としたのは弟(おとうと)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the event as a whole, with the subject marked as the new piece of
information. It works as an answer to "what happened?" and does not assume the
listener knows anything yet.

(b) assumes the broken plate is already common knowledge and supplies the missing
name. It answers "who did it?", it excludes everyone else, and depending on the
situation it can sound like an accusation or like clearing someone's name. Use it
when the event is settled and only the identity is in question.

</details>
