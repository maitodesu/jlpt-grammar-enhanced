# N4 - Without doing, having to, and not having to


---

# ～ないで

**Meaning** · without doing something - and, in speech, a bare "don't"

`～ないで` is the <b>negative て-form of a verb</b>. Like the ordinary て-form it hooks
one clause onto the next, but it hooks on a *non-event*: the thing that did not
happen while the main action went ahead. You met it at N5 inside
`～ないでください`; that `で` was never decoration, and the form stands on its own.

## Formation

Build the ない-form, then add `で`. Nothing else changes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form + で | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)がないで |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + で | 閉(し)める → 閉(し)めないで |
| Verb (する) | しないで | 運動(うんどう)する → 運動(うんどう)しないで |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)ないで | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないで |
| Written / formal equivalent | ない-form − ない + ずに | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)がずに |
| With a following ending | ないで + ください／ほしい／くれる | 急(いそ)がないでください |
| い-adjective | not available - use くなくて | 高(たか)い → ❌高(たか)くないで |
| な-adjective / Noun | not available - use でなくて | 静(しず)かだ → ❌静(しず)かじゃないで |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないで` is **verb-only**. If the negated word is an adjective
or a noun, the joining form is `なくて`, and no `で` version exists.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same string does three jobs, and context separates them cleanly.
(1) *Without doing*: 地図(ちず)を見(み)ないで歩(ある)いた.
(2) *Instead of doing*: 勉強(べんきょう)しないでゲームをした.
(3) A **negative request**, with `ください` or with nothing at all:
開(あ)けないでください／開(あ)けないで.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
けさは<b>食(た)べないで</b>家(いえ)を出(で)た。
I left the house this morning without eating.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)も<b>買(か)わないで</b>帰(かえ)りました。
I came home without buying anything.
</pre>

<pre>
靴(くつ)を<b>脱(ぬ)がないで</b>入(はい)ってください。
Please come in without taking your shoes off.
</pre>

<pre>
辞書(じしょ)を<b>使(つか)わないで</b>読(よ)んでみましょう。
Let's try reading it without using a dictionary.
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)はどこにも<b>行(い)かないで</b>、一日中(いちにちじゅう)ゲームをしていた。
On Sunday I didn't go anywhere - I played games all day.
</pre>

<pre>
お願(ねが)い、この話(はなし)はまだ<b>言(い)わないで</b>ね。
Please - don't tell anyone about this yet.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は疲(つか)れていたので、晩(ばん)ごはんも<b>作(つく)らないで</b>そのまま寝(ね)てしまいました。
I was so tired yesterday that I fell asleep without even making dinner.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を<b>読(よ)まないで</b>組(く)み立(た)てたら、ねじが二本(にほん)余(あま)ってしまった。
I assembled it without reading the instructions and ended up with two screws left over.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The joining `ないで` is register-neutral. It appears in casual speech and in
ordinary polite writing alike, and the politeness of the sentence is decided at the
end, by the main verb: <code>傘(かさ)を持(も)たないで出(で)かけた</code> and
<code>傘(かさ)を持(も)たないで出(で)かけました</code> differ only there.

The **request** use is different, and it is the one to watch. Bare `ないで` with
nothing after it - 見(み)ないで, 行(い)かないで - is casual and emotionally
direct. It is what you say to a friend, a partner, a child or a pet, and it can
sound pleading rather than commanding. `ね` softens it further
(<code>忘(わす)れないでね</code>); `よ` presses. Said to a stranger or a superior it is far
too blunt, and you want `ないでください` at minimum, or
`ないでいただけますか` if you are genuinely asking a favour.

Two neighbouring endings live on the same stem and are worth having ready:
`～ないでほしい`「I'd rather you didn't」, which reports a wish instead of issuing an
order, and `～ないでくれる？／～ないでくれない？`「could you not…?」, casual but softer
than the bare form because it is a question.

In writing - reports, instructions, signage, anything with a formal voice - the
joining `ないで` is routinely replaced by `ずに`. That swap is not optional in a
stiff register; <code>確認(かくにん)しないで送(おく)りました</code> in a written apology
reads as spoken language.

## Contrast

### ～ないで vs ～ずに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `ないで` is spoken and
neutral; `ずに` is written and formal. Only `ずに` has an irregular form: する
becomes <b>せずに</b>, never ❌しずに.

</div>

`ずに` is a survival from classical Japanese, and it attaches to the same negative
stem: 書(か)かない → 書(か)かずに, 食(た)べない → 食(た)べずに,
来(く)る → 来(こ)ずに.

<pre>
彼(かれ)は何(なに)も<b>言(い)わずに</b>部屋(へや)を出(で)て行(い)った。
He left the room without saying a word.
</pre>

<pre>
無理(むり)を<b>せずに</b>ゆっくり休(やす)んでください。
Please rest properly and don't push yourself.
</pre>

Two limits on `ずに`. It cannot carry a request: there is no ❌<code>見(み)ずにください</code>
 - requests are `ないで` territory. And the *instead of* reading is weaker with `ずに`;
for that sense `ないで` is the natural choice in speech.

You will also meet bare `ず` with no `に`, joining clauses in written Japanese:
<code>誰(だれ)にも相談(そうだん)せず、一人(ひとり)で決(き)めた</code>. Treat it as the
same word wearing its most formal clothes.

### ～ないで vs ～なくて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないで` tells you *how* the next thing happened;
`なくて` tells you *why* it happened.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはんを<b>食(た)べないで</b>会社(かいしゃ)へ行(い)った。
I went to work without eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはんを<b>食(た)べなくて</b>、おなかがすいた。
I didn't eat breakfast, so I got hungry.
</pre>

The first sentence is about the manner of going; the second gives the cause of the
hunger. The practical consequence is that `なくて` cannot introduce a request or a
command - ❌<code>食(た)べなくてください</code> is not Japanese - and that a `ないで`
clause must be followed by something the same subject deliberately did. This
distinction has its own page at this level; it repays the time.

### ～ないで vs ～ないように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないで` is *without doing it*; `ないように` is *so that it does
not happen*. One reports, the other aims.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>閉(し)めないで</b>出(で)かけた。
I went out without closing the window.
</pre>

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)を<b>ひかないように</b>、上着(うわぎ)を持(も)って行(い)きます。
I'll take a jacket so I don't catch a cold.
</pre>

The difference matters most in requests. <code>遅(おく)れないでください</code> is a flat
"please don't be late"; <code>遅(おく)れないようにしてください</code> is "please make sure
you're not late", which frames the same content as advice and lands far more gently.
`ように` also survives being said upward, where the bare negative request does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 見(み)ない<b>て</b> → ✅ 見(み)ない<b>で</b>
  The negative て-form is `ないで`. There is no form ❌`ないて` anywhere in the
  language.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>じゃないで</b>集中(しゅうちゅう)できない。 → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>じゃなくて</b>集中(しゅうちゅう)できない。
  `ないで` attaches only to verbs. Adjectives and nouns join with `なくて` /
  `でなくて`.

- ❌ 準備(じゅんび)を<b>しずに</b>出(で)かけた。 → ✅ 準備(じゅんび)を<b>せずに</b>出(で)かけた。
  する is irregular before `ずに`: the stem is `せ`, not `し`.

- ❌ 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>なくて</b>出(で)かけた。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>ないで</b>出(で)かけた。
  Going out is not *caused* by not eating. When the negative describes the manner
  of the main action, it must be `ないで`.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、そこに座(すわ)ら<b>ないで</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、そちらには座(すわ)ら<b>ないでください</b>。
  Bare `ないで` is intimate and blunt. Upward it needs `ください`, or better, a
  question form.

## Practice

**1.** 傘(かさ)を持(も)た____出(で)かけたので、ぬれてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傘(かさ)を持(も)た<b>ないで</b>出(で)かけたので、ぬれてしまった。

Not taking an umbrella is the *manner* in which the going-out happened, so the
joining form is `ないで`. Writing <code>持(も)たなくて</code> would claim that going out
was caused by the lack of an umbrella.

</details>

**2.** 会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)を確認(かくにん)（しないで・しなくて）送(おく)ってしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

資料(しりょう)を確認(かくにん)<b>しないで</b>送(おく)ってしまいました。

The sending is the main event and the not-checking describes how it was done - 
carelessly, in one motion. `しなくて` would need a consequence after it, such as
<code>確認(かくにん)しなくて、あとで困(こま)った</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in a formal written register: "He signed the contract without reading it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は契約書(けいやくしょ)を<b>読(よ)まずに</b>サインした。

`ずに` is the written counterpart of `ないで`, and a sentence about contracts is
exactly where it belongs. <code>読(よ)まないで</code> is not wrong, but it sounds spoken.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は宿題(しゅくだい)をしずにねてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>せずに</b>寝(ね)てしまった。

The `ず` ending takes an irregular stem from する - `せ`. Every other verb uses its
ordinary ない-stem, which is why this one is so easy to get wrong.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 電気(でんき)を消(け)さ<b>ないで</b>。
(b) 電気(でんき)を消(け)さ<b>ないでください</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is bare and intimate. With no ending after it, it reads as an immediate,
emotional "don't!" - the kind of thing said to a family member or a close friend,
and depending on the tone it can sound closer to pleading than to ordering.

(b) adds the standard polite request ending. It is neutral, usable with a
colleague or a stranger, and it sounds like an instruction rather than a reaction.
Between the two sit <code>消(け)さないでね</code> (softer) and
<code>消(け)さないでくれる？</code> (casual but framed as a question).

</details>

# ～ないで vs ～なくて

**Meaning** · the two negative て-forms - *without doing* versus *because … not*

Japanese has one positive て-form and **two** negative ones. `～ないで` joins a
non-event to an action and says *how* that action happened. `～なくて` joins a
non-event to a consequence and says *why* it happened. They are not stylistic
variants, and swapping them usually produces a sentence that means nothing.

## Formation

Both start from the ない-form. The split is in the ending, and in what the word
being negated is.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb → *without doing* | ない-form + で | 言(い)う → 言(い)わないで |
| Verb → *because not* | ない-form − い + くて | 言(い)う → 言(い)わなくて |
| Verb (する) | しないで／しなくて | 練習(れんしゅう)する → 練習(れんしゅう)しないで／しなくて |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)ないで／来(こ)なくて | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないで／来(こ)なくて |
| い-adjective | ～くない → ～くなくて (no `ないで`) | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くなくて |
| な-adjective | じゃない → じゃなくて (no `ないで`) | 便利(べんり)だ → 便利(べんり)じゃなくて |
| Noun | じゃない → じゃなくて (no `ないで`) | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)じゃなくて |
| Written equivalent of `ないで` | ない-form − ない + ずに | 言(い)う → 言(い)わずに |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくて` can negate **anything**; `ないで` only ever negates a
**verb**. So if the word before the blank is an adjective or a noun, the answer is
`なくて` before you have thought about meaning at all.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
上着(うわぎ)を<b>着(き)ないで</b>外(そと)に出(で)た。
I went outside without putting a jacket on.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が<b>来(こ)なくて</b>、心配(しんぱい)です。
No reply has come, and I'm worried.
</pre>

<pre>
席(せき)を<b>立(た)たないで</b>待(ま)っていてください。
Please wait without leaving your seat.
</pre>

<pre>
意味(いみ)が<b>わからなくて</b>、先生(せんせい)に聞(き)きました。
I didn't understand what it meant, so I asked the teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べないで</b>肉(にく)ばかり食(た)べる。
My son leaves the vegetables and eats nothing but meat.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が<b>広(ひろ)くなくて</b>、ベッドが入(はい)りませんでした。
The room wasn't big enough, so the bed wouldn't fit.
</pre>

<pre>
原因(げんいん)は雨(あめ)<b>じゃなくて</b>、工事(こうじ)でした。
The cause wasn't the rain - it was the roadworks.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が<b>動(うご)かなくて</b>、面接(めんせつ)に三十分(さんじゅっぷん)も遅(おく)れてしまいました。
The trains weren't running, and I ended up half an hour late for my interview.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)にも<b>相談(そうだん)しないで</b>会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めたので、親(おや)にひどく怒(おこ)られました。
I quit my job without talking to anyone about it, and my parents were furious.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neither form is marked for politeness. Both sit in the middle of the sentence, and
the ending decides the register.

What `なくて` *is* marked for is **control**. Causal `なくて` is at its most natural
when the thing that failed to happen was outside the speaker's hands - the bus, the
weather, an email, your own memory or ability:
<code>見(み)つからなくて</code>, <code>間(ま)に合(あ)わなくて</code>,
<code>来(こ)なくて</code>, <code>思(おも)い出(だ)せなくて</code>. When the non-action was a
choice you made, native speakers reach for `なかったので／なかったから` instead:
<code>勉強(べんきょう)しなかったので、試験(しけん)に落(お)ちた</code> rather than
❌<code>勉強(べんきょう)しなくて、試験(しけん)に落(お)ちた</code>.

The other half of that restriction is what comes **after** `なくて`. It wants a
state, a feeling or an involuntary result - 困(こま)った, 寂(さび)しい,
安心(あんしん)した, よかった, 大変(たいへん)だった. It does not want a
deliberate next action, and it flatly cannot take a request, an invitation or an
intention. That single fact resolves most of the exam questions on this pair.

Two frozen expressions are worth memorising whole, because you will use them
constantly and both take `なくて`:
<code>来(こ)られなくてすみません</code>「sorry I couldn't come」 and
<code>大(おお)きい事故(じこ)にならなくてよかった</code>「thank goodness it wasn't a
serious accident」.

Finally, `ないで` has a written twin, `ずに`, and `なくて` does not. In a formal
report <code>確認(かくにん)せずに</code> replaces <code>確認(かくにん)しないで</code>, while a
causal `なくて` in the same document would normally be rewritten as `ないため` - 
<code>届(とど)かないため、再送(さいそう)いたします</code>.

## Contrast

### Manner vs cause

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask what the second clause *is*. An action performed → `ないで`.
A result suffered → `なくて`.

</div>

<pre>
めがねを<b>かけないで</b>運転(うんてん)した。
I drove without wearing my glasses.
</pre>

<pre>
めがねが<b>なくて</b>運転(うんてん)できなかった。
I didn't have my glasses, so I couldn't drive.
</pre>

In the first, the driving happened and the not-wearing describes the state I was in
while doing it - one event, described two ways. In the second there are two facts in
a chain: no glasses, therefore no driving. English blurs this because both come out
as "without" or "didn't … so"; Japanese does not.

A useful test: if you can insert 「そのまま」 or 「その状態(じょうたい)で」 before the
main verb and the sentence still works, you want `ないで`.

### What can follow each

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - requests, commands, invitations and intentions can only follow
`ないで`. `なくて` is followed by things that happen *to* you.

</div>

<pre>
砂糖(さとう)を<b>入(い)れないで</b>ください。
Please don't put sugar in it.
</pre>

<pre>
砂糖(さとう)が<b>入(はい)っていなくて</b>、安心(あんしん)しました。
There was no sugar in it, which was a relief.
</pre>

There is no ❌<code>入(い)れなくてください</code>, no ❌<code>行(い)かなくて、帰(かえ)ろう</code>,
no ❌<code>待(ま)たなくて、先(さき)に始(はじ)めます</code>. If the sentence ends in
ください, ましょう, つもり, たい or an imperative, the negative in front of it is
`ないで`.

The mirror of that rule: an emotion word after the negative almost forces `なくて`.
眠(ねむ)れなくて辛(つら)かった, 会(あ)えなくて残念(ざんねん)です,
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らなくてよかった.

### Adjectives and nouns: only なくて exists

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないで` is verb-only. Adjectives and nouns have exactly one
negative て-form, and it is `なくて` / `でなくて`.

</div>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>じゃなくて</b>、勉強(べんきょう)できません。
This room isn't quiet, so I can't study.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は高(たか)<b>くなくて</b>、味(あじ)もよかったです。
It wasn't expensive, and it tasted good too.
</pre>

Note the second sentence: with adjectives, `なくて` is not always causal. It can
simply be the *and* of a list - "not A, and B" - exactly as the positive て-form
links two descriptions. The same neutral linking shows up in corrections, where
`じゃなくて` means "not A but B":
<code>明日(あした)じゃなくて、あさってです</code>.

For nouns the formal written variant is `ではなくて`, and in the stiffest registers
`ではなく` with no `て` at all:
<code>対象(たいしょう)は学生(がくせい)ではなく、社会人(しゃかいじん)です</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ここに荷物(にもつ)を置(お)か<b>なくて</b>ください。 → ✅ ここに荷物(にもつ)を置(お)か<b>ないで</b>ください。
  A request can only follow `ないで`. This is the single most common error with the
  pair, and it survives well past N4 if it is not fixed early.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が明(あか)る<b>くないで</b>、本(ほん)が読(よ)めない。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が明(あか)る<b>くなくて</b>、本(ほん)が読(よ)めない。
  <code>明(あか)るい</code> is an adjective, and adjectives have no `ないで` form at all.

- ❌ 鍵(かぎ)が見(み)つから<b>ないで</b>、困(こま)りました。 → ✅ 鍵(かぎ)が見(み)つから<b>なくて</b>、困(こま)りました。
  Being stuck is a result, not an action performed while not finding the key. Causal
  chain → `なくて`.

- ❌ 練習(れんしゅう)し<b>なくて</b>、試合(しあい)に負(ま)けた。 → ✅ 練習(れんしゅう)し<b>なかったので</b>、試合(しあい)に負(ま)けた。
  The non-action was a choice. Causal `なくて` prefers things you could not help;
  for deliberate causes use `なかったので／なかったから`.

- ❌ 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>なくて</b>会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きます。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>ないで</b>会社(かいしゃ)に行(い)きます。
  Going to work is a deliberate act, and skipping breakfast is the manner of doing
  it, not its cause.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)は暑(あつ)くて、エアコンを消(け)さ____寝(ね)てしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

エアコンを消(け)さ<b>ないで</b>寝(ね)てしまった。

Sleeping is something the speaker did, and leaving the air conditioner on describes
the state they were in while doing it. That is manner, so `ないで`.

</details>

**2.** バスが来(こ)（ないで・なくて）、一時間(いちじかん)も待(ま)ちました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

バスが来(こ)<b>なくて</b>、一時間(いちじかん)も待(ま)ちました。

The waiting is a consequence the speaker suffered, and the bus not coming was
outside their control - the textbook profile for causal `なくて`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please don't decide anything without asking me."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)に聞(き)か<b>ないで</b>決(き)めないでください。

Two negatives, both `ないで`, and for different reasons: the first is *without
asking* (manner), the second is the negative request. Neither slot can take
`なくて`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この店(みせ)は安(やす)くないで、あまり人気(にんき)がない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)<b>くなくて</b>、あまり人気(にんき)がない。

<code>安(やす)い</code> is an い-adjective. Only verbs form `ないで`; every other word
class negates and joins with `なくて`, built from the `くない` form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 連絡(れんらく)し<b>ないで</b>帰(かえ)りました。
(b) 連絡(れんらく)が<b>なくて</b>心配(しんぱい)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about the speaker's own behaviour: they went home, and the going home was
done without getting in touch first. The focus is on the manner of one action, and
it carries a faint sense of something skipped that should have been done.

(b) is about what happened to the speaker as a result of someone else's silence.
There are two linked facts - no contact, therefore worry - and the second half is a
feeling rather than a deliberate act, which is exactly the environment causal
`なくて` wants.

</details>

# ～なければいけない・～なければならない - must; have to

**Meaning** · you have to do it; there is no option of not doing it

This is the same double-negative machine you met at N5 in `～なくてはいけない`:
*if you don't do it, that won't do*. What changes here is the conditional in front.
`～なければ` is the <b>ば-conditional of ない</b>, and it is the tidier, more formal of
the two ways in. `なければならない` is the wording of rules, contracts and careful
written Japanese.

## Formation

`ない` is an い-adjective, so it conditionalises exactly like one: 高(たか)い →
高(たか)ければ, therefore ない → なければ. Build the ない-form, drop the final `い`,
add `ければ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form − い + ければ | 払(はら)う → 払(はら)わなければならない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form − い + ければ | 続(つづ)ける → 続(つづ)けなければならない |
| Verb (する) | しなければならない | 提出(ていしゅつ)する → 提出(ていしゅつ)しなければならない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)なければならない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)なければならない |
| い-adjective | ～くない → ～くなければ | 新(あたら)しい → 新(あたら)しくなければならない |
| な-adjective | drop だ + でなければ | 安全(あんぜん)だ → 安全(あんぜん)でなければならない |
| Noun | noun + でなければ | 本人(ほんにん) → 本人(ほんにん)でなければならない |
| Polite | いけません／なりません | 払(はら)わなければいけません |
| Past | いけなかった／ならなかった | 払(はら)わなければなりませんでした |
| Casual contraction | なければ → なきゃ | 払(はら)わなきゃ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the ending `ならない` is a **verb**, so its polite form is
`なりません`, never ❌`ならないです`. Same for `いけない` → `いけません`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `なければ` on its own is just an ordinary conditional and has nothing to
do with obligation: <code>時間(じかん)がなければ、また今度(こんど)でいいですよ</code>
「if you haven't got time, another day is fine」. The obligation only appears when
`いけない` or `ならない` closes the sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう<b>出(で)なければなりません</b>。
I have to leave now.
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)を<b>しなければいけない</b>。
I have to do my homework.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)も会社(かいしゃ)に<b>来(こ)なければなりません</b>か。
Do I have to come in tomorrow as well?
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し込(こ)みは本人(ほんにん)<b>でなければなりません</b>。
The application must be made by the person themselves.
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)のために、もっと野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べなければいけない</b>と思(おも)っている。
I know I ought to eat more vegetables for my health.
</pre>

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)は決(き)められた場所(ばしょ)に<b>とめなければならない</b>。
Bicycles must be parked in the designated area.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)すので、今週中(こんしゅうちゅう)に新(あたら)しい部屋(へや)を<b>決(き)めなければいけません</b>。
I'm moving next month, so I have to settle on a new flat this week.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいているので、明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)に<b>しなければならない</b>かもしれません。
A typhoon is on the way, so we may have to call tomorrow's match off.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

There is only one meaning in this family, and five common ways to say it. Put them
on a single line and the choice becomes mechanical:

1. <b>～なければならない</b> - the most formal. Laws, contracts, manuals, academic
   writing, official announcements. Impersonal: it states a requirement that holds
   for anyone, not a chore facing one person.
2. <b>～なければいけません</b> - formal-polite speech and ordinary business writing.
   Still stiff, but it can be about you.
3. <b>～なくてはいけません</b> - the everyday polite form (this is the N5 branch). What
   you say to a colleague or in a classroom.
4. <b>～ないといけない</b> - the everyday **spoken** form, and the most frequent of all
   in conversation.
5. <b>～なきゃ／～なくちゃ</b> - casual, contracted, usually with the ending dropped
   entirely. What people actually say to themselves and to friends.

Two axes are hiding inside that list. <b>`なければ` versus `なくては`</b> is a matter of
tidiness: `なければ` is the written-feeling conditional, `なくては` the more
conversational one. <b>`ならない` versus `いけない`</b> is a matter of who the
requirement belongs to: `ならない` states a standing rule with no addressee, while
`いけない` points at a situation and often at a person.

Because `なければならない` is impersonal, it is a **safe** thing to say upward. You
cannot comfortably tell your boss <code>確認(かくにん)しなければいけませんよ</code>, but
you can report the rule - <code>規則(きそく)では部長(ぶちょう)のサインが必要(ひつよう)です</code> - and let the rule do the pushing.

Do not use it about a mild preference. `なければならない` is heavy; if what you mean
is "you'd be better off", the pattern is `～たほうがいい`, and reaching for the
obligation form instead sounds alarming.

Finally, the polite negative-past forms come out longer than learners expect:
<code>待(ま)たなければなりませんでした</code>「I had to wait」. There is no shortcut, and
in speech people simply say <code>待(ま)たなきゃいけなかった</code>.

## Contrast

### ～なければ vs ～なくては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. `なければ` is the neater, more written
conditional; `なくては` is a shade more conversational.

</div>

<pre>
会費(かいひ)は三月(さんがつ)までに<b>納(おさ)めなければなりません</b>。
Membership fees must be paid by March.
</pre>

<pre>
会費(かいひ)、三月(さんがつ)までに<b>納(おさ)めなくてはいけませんね</b>。
We've got to get the membership fee in by March, haven't we.
</pre>

The requirement is the same; the voice is not. The first could be printed on a
form. The second is two people discussing the form.

One structural difference worth knowing: `なくては` contracts to `なくちゃ` and
`なければ` contracts to `なきゃ`, and the contractions do not cross. There is no
❌`なくきゃ`.

### いけない vs ならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ならない` for a rule that stands on its own; `いけない` for
something a particular person has to do about a particular situation.

</div>

<pre>
受験生(じゅけんせい)は九時(くじ)までに入室(にゅうしつ)し<b>なければならない</b>。
Candidates must be seated by nine o'clock.
</pre>

<pre>
あ、九時(くじ)までに着(つ)か<b>なければいけない</b>んだった。
Oh - I have to be there by nine.
</pre>

`ならない` is faceless, which is why it dominates in writing and why it sounds odd
when someone uses it about their own small errands. `いけない` is the workhorse of
spoken obligation above the casual line. In a `～んです` explanation, in a question,
in an apology, `いけない` is almost always the one.

### ～なければならない vs ～たほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - obligation versus advice. `なければならない` says there is no
alternative; `たほうがいい` says one alternative is better.

</div>

<pre>
薬(くすり)は食後(しょくご)に<b>飲(の)まなければなりません</b>。
The medicine must be taken after meals.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)は食後(しょくご)に<b>飲(の)んだほうがいい</b>ですよ。
You're better off taking the medicine after meals.
</pre>

The first is the pharmacist reading the instruction; the second is a friend passing
on a tip. Learners routinely over-use the obligation form because English "you have
to" is so mild - but Japanese `なければならない` is not mild, and using it for a
suggestion makes a recommendation sound like a regulation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>なければ</b>なりません。 → ✅ 行(い)か<b>なければ</b>なりません。
  The pattern builds on the ない-form, never on the dictionary form:
  <code>行(い)かない</code> → <code>行(い)かなければ</code>.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>だなければ</b>いけません。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>でなければ</b>いけません。
  Nouns and な-adjectives negate through `で(は)ない`, so the conditional is
  `でなければ`.

- ❌ 急(いそ)がなければ<b>ならないです</b>。 → ✅ 急(いそ)がなければ<b>なりません</b>。
  `ならない` is the negative of the verb `なる`. Verbs do not take `です` to become
  polite.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が明(あか)る<b>いなければ</b>いけません。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が明(あか)る<b>くなければ</b>いけません。
  い-adjectives negate to `くない` first, and only then take `ければ`.

- ❌ 来(こ)なければならな<b>くないです</b>。（intending "you don't have to come"）→ ✅ 来(こ)<b>なくてもいいです</b>。
  You cannot negate the obligation form to release someone from it. The opposite of
  `なければならない` is a different pattern: `なくてもいい`.

## Practice

**1.** パスポートの写真(しゃしん)は六(ろく)か月(げつ)以内(いない)のもの____なりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

六(ろく)か月(げつ)以内(いない)のもの<b>でなければ</b>なりません。

The word being required is a noun (もの), so the negative conditional is `でなければ`.
`ならない` fits because this is a printed regulation rather than one person's
errand.

</details>

**2.** 寒(さむ)くなってきたから、そろそろコートを（出(だ)さなければなりません・出(だ)したほうがいいです）ね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そろそろコートを<b>出(だ)したほうがいい</b>ですね。

Nothing is forcing the speaker to dig the coat out - it is simply a good idea.
`なければなりません` would turn a remark about the weather into a rule, which is
much heavier than the English "you should" that prompted it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a line in a company handbook: "Employees must report any accident on the same day."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

社員(しゃいん)は、事故(じこ)があった場合(ばあい)、当日(とうじつ)中(ちゅう)に報告(ほうこく)し<b>なければならない</b>。

A handbook is exactly where `なければならない` belongs: plain form, no addressee, a
requirement stated as holding for a whole class of people. Using `なきゃいけない`
here would be jarringly conversational.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)までにこの仕事(しごと)を終(お)わらせなきゃなりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)までにこの仕事(しごと)を終(お)わらせ<b>なければなりません</b>。
（casual: 終(お)わらせ<b>なきゃ</b>。）

`なきゃ` is the contracted, casual form of `なければ`, and gluing the formal tail
`なりません` onto it mixes two registers inside one word. Pick one level and stay
there.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 三月(さんがつ)までに申(もう)し込(こ)ま<b>なければならない</b>。
(b) 三月(さんがつ)までに申(もう)し込(こ)ま<b>なければいけない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reads as the requirement itself, quoted. It has no addressee and no speaker
feeling in it, so it fits a notice, a set of guidelines, or someone reciting what
the rules say.

(b) attaches the same requirement to a situation and usually to a person: the
speaker, or whoever they are talking to, has this to deal with. Add `ね` or
`んです` and it becomes unmistakably conversational - which you cannot really do
to (a).

</details>

# ～ないといけない

**Meaning** · have to; must - the version people actually say out loud

Third conditional, same machine. `～なくては` and `～なければ` build obligation out of
*if you don't … that won't do*; `～ないと` does the same with the `と` conditional.
What makes it worth a page of its own is frequency: in conversation this form
outnumbers the textbook ones, and it has a trick the others do not - it can stop
in the middle and still be a complete sentence.

## Formation

Take the ない-form and add `と`. Then close with `いけない`, `だめだ`, or nothing at
all.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form + といけない | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)さないといけない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + といけない | 調(しら)べる → 調(しら)べないといけない |
| Verb (する) | しないといけない | 準備(じゅんび)する → 準備(じゅんび)しないといけない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)ないといけない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないといけない |
| い-adjective | ～くないと + いけない | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くないといけない |
| な-adjective / Noun | ～でないと + いけない | 会員(かいいん) → 会員(かいいん)でないといけない |
| Polite | いけません | 出(だ)さないといけません |
| Past | いけなかった／いけませんでした | 出(だ)さないといけませんでした |
| Blunter casual | といけない → とだめ | 出(だ)さないとだめ |
| Ending dropped | ～ないと (＋ ね／な) | 出(だ)さないと。／出(だ)さないとね。 |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front of `と` must be **negative**.
<code>行(い)かないといけない</code> is "I have to go"; <code>行(い)くといけない</code> is a
different sentence altogether - "it would be bad if I went."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そろそろ<b>帰(かえ)らないといけない</b>。
I have to head home soon.
</pre>

<pre>
銀行(ぎんこう)に<b>行(い)かないといけません</b>。
I have to go to the bank.
</pre>

<pre>
あ、ごみを<b>出(だ)さないと</b>。
Oh - I need to put the rubbish out.
</pre>

<pre>
靴(くつ)が小(ちい)さくなったので、新(あたら)しいのを<b>買(か)わないといけない</b>。
My shoes have got too small, so I have to buy new ones.
</pre>

<pre>
このプールは会員(かいいん)<b>でないといけない</b>んですか。
Do you have to be a member to use this pool?
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが熱(ねつ)を出(だ)したので、早(はや)く迎(むか)えに<b>行(い)かないといけなくなりました</b>。
My child has come down with a fever, so now I have to go and collect them early.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)までにこの本(ほん)を読(よ)み終(お)わら<b>ないといけない</b>んです。
The thing is, I have to finish this book by the weekend.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の出張(しゅっちょう)の前(まえ)に、ホテルもチケットも自分(じぶん)で予約(よやく)し<b>ないといけません</b>。
Before next week's business trip I have to book the hotel and the tickets myself.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～ないといけない` sits in the middle of the obligation ladder and leans casual. In
plain form it is what friends, family and colleagues use with each other all day.
In `いけません` it is perfectly usable at work, though it still sounds like speech
rather than writing - a report or a notice would say `なければならない`.

The ending falls off constantly. <code>急(いそ)がないと</code>, <code>買(か)わないとね</code>,
<code>そろそろ寝(ね)ないとな</code> are not fragments; the trailing `と` carries the
whole meaning, and adding `いけない` would sound oddly complete. Learners
consistently under-produce this and it is one of the quickest wins in sounding
natural. `ね` invites agreement, `な` is a masculine-leaning mutter to oneself.

`～ないとだめ` swaps a softer ending for a blunter one. `だめ` is flat and physical - 
"no good" - so <code>出(だ)さないとだめだよ</code> presses harder than
<code>出(だ)さないといけないよ</code>. Said to a child or a close friend it is normal; said
to a colleague it can sting.

Two things to file away. First, `ならない` almost never follows `と`: you will
occasionally hear <code>～ないとならない</code>, but it is uncommon and `いけない` is the
safe choice. Second, in Kansai the whole family is replaced by `～なあかん`
(<code>行(い)かなあかん</code>, <code>せなあかん</code>), which you will hear constantly in the
west and in comedy, and which no textbook prepares you for.

As with every obligation form, aiming it at someone above you is strong. Reporting
your own obligation is always safe; telling your boss
<code>確認(かくにん)しないといけませんよ</code> is not.

## Contrast

### ～ないといけない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same requirement, different room. `ないと` belongs in the
kitchen; `なければならない` belongs on the noticeboard.

</div>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)は窓口(まどぐち)に<b>提出(ていしゅつ)しなければならない</b>。
Application forms must be submitted at the counter.
</pre>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)、窓口(まどぐち)に<b>出(だ)さないといけないんだって</b>。
Apparently you have to hand the form in at the counter.
</pre>

The information is identical. The first is impersonal and could be printed; the
second is one person relaying it to another, complete with `んだ` and hearsay
`って`. Mixing them up is not a grammar error, it is a costume error - and it is
just as noticeable.

### ～ないと, and stopping there

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a bare `～ないと` at the end of an utterance is a finished
sentence, not a broken one. The unspoken `いけない` is understood.

</div>

<pre>
やばい、電車(でんしゃ)来(く)る。<b>走(はし)らないと</b>。
Uh-oh, the train's coming. We've got to run.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)試験(しけん)だから、今日(きょう)は<b>寝(ね)ないとね</b>。
I've got an exam tomorrow, so I'd better get some sleep.
</pre>

This works because Japanese lets a conditional trail off when the consequence is
obvious, and "that would be bad" is always obvious. The same trick is available to
`なきゃ` and `なくちゃ`, which is why the casual end of this family so often sounds
unfinished to a learner's ear. It is not.

### Obligation ～ないと vs the plain conditional ～ないと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if a consequence follows, `と` is just "if". Obligation only
appears when the consequence is `いけない`/`だめ`, or is left unsaid.

</div>

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>出(で)ないと</b>、電車(でんしゃ)に間(ま)に合(あ)わないよ。
If we don't leave soon we won't make the train.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>出(で)ないと</b>。
We've got to get going.
</pre>

Same five words, two different sentences. In the first, `と` behaves exactly as
it does in any conditional - the second half spells out what goes wrong. In the
second there is no second half, so the listener supplies the standard one and the
whole thing reads as obligation.

This is also why `ないと` cannot be used for a rule stated in the abstract. A
conditional needs a situation; a regulation with no situation attached wants
`なければならない`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は早(はや)く<b>起(お)きるといけない</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は早(はや)く<b>起(お)きないといけない</b>。
  The obligation lives in the negative. <code>起(お)きるといけない</code> means "it would
  be a problem if I got up", which is the opposite of the intended sentence.

- ❌ 三人以上(さんにんいじょう)<b>だといけません</b>。（intending "there must be three or more people"）→ ✅ 三人以上(さんにんいじょう)<b>でないといけません</b>。
  Nouns negate with `でない` before `と`. `だと` is the affirmative conditional and
  flips the meaning.

- ❌ 待(ま)たないといけない<b>でした</b>。 → ✅ 待(ま)たないといけません<b>でした</b>。／待(ま)たないといけなかった。
  `いけない` is a negative predicate in its own right; make it past by conjugating it
  (`いけなかった`) or polite-past (`いけませんでした`), not by hanging `でした` on the
  plain form.

- ❌ 急(いそ)がないと<b>ならないです</b>。 → ✅ 急(いそ)がないと<b>いけません</b>。／急(いそ)が<b>なければなりません</b>。
  `ならない` is at home after `なければ`/`なくては`, not after `と`, and it never takes
  `です`.

- ❌（in a written report）至急(しきゅう)対応(たいおう)し<b>ないといけない</b>。 → ✅ 至急(しきゅう)対応(たいおう)し<b>なければならない</b>。
  Not ungrammatical, just spoken. In documents the `と` branch of the family reads
  as if someone forgot they were writing.

## Practice

**1.** 明日(あした)は六時(ろくじ)の電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)る。だから五時(ごじ)に起(お)き____いけない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

五時(ごじ)に起(お)き<b>ないと</b>いけない。

The obligation form needs the negative in front of `と`: <code>起(お)きない</code> +
`と` + `いけない`. The literal logic is "if I don't get up at five, that's no good."

</details>

**2.** （走(はし)らないと・走(はし)ると）間(ま)に合(あ)わないよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>走(はし)らないと</b>間(ま)に合(あ)わないよ。

Here `と` is doing ordinary conditional work, and the two halves have to line up:
not running leads to not making it. <code>走(はし)ると間(ま)に合(あ)わない</code>
would claim that running is what makes you late.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually, as something you would mutter on remembering it: "Ah, I have to answer that email."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あ、メールの返事(へんじ)、<b>書(か)かないと</b>。

The trailing `ないと` with the ending left off is the natural shape for remembering
a task out loud. <code>書(か)かないといけない</code> is fine too, but the fuller form
sounds like reporting the obligation rather than reacting to it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: パスポートを見(み)せるといけませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ パスポートを見(み)せ<b>ないといけません</b>か。

Without the negative, the sentence asks whether showing your passport would cause a
problem. The obligation reading requires <code>見(み)せない</code> before `と`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)け<b>ないといけないよ</b>。
(b) 部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)け<b>ないとだめだよ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the requirement and lets `よ` add a nudge. It is the ordinary spoken way
to point something out, and it can be said sympathetically - the speaker may well be
on the listener's side about the chore.

(b) replaces the neutral ending with `だめ`, which is flat and unarguable. It is what
a parent says to a child or one close friend says to another, and it carries
disapproval that (a) does not. To a colleague it would sound like being told off.

</details>

# ～なくちゃ・～なきゃ

**Meaning** · gotta - the same obligation, squeezed down for speech

These are not new grammar. `なくちゃ` is `なくては` with the syllables worn off, and
`なきゃ` is `なければ` with the same treatment. What they add is register: they are
what obligation sounds like in an actual conversation, and they usually appear with
the `いけない`/`ならない` tail chopped off entirely. Learners are blindsided by
`なきゃ` because no textbook sentence ever contains it and every real one does.

## Formation

Japanese contracts `ては` to `ちゃ` everywhere (見(み)ては → 見(み)ちゃ), and
`ければ` to `きゃ`. Apply that to the two obligation conditionals and you get these.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form → なくちゃ／なきゃ | 行(い)く → 行(い)かなくちゃ／行(い)かなきゃ |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form → なくちゃ／なきゃ | 起(お)きる → 起(お)きなくちゃ／起(お)きなきゃ |
| Verb (する) | しなくちゃ／しなきゃ | 洗濯(せんたく)する → 洗濯(せんたく)しなきゃ |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)なくちゃ／来(こ)なきゃ | 来(く)る → 来(こ)なきゃ |
| い-adjective | ～くなくちゃ／～くなきゃ | 新(あたら)しい → 新(あたら)しくなきゃ |
| な-adjective / Noun | ～じゃなくちゃ／～じゃなきゃ | 無料(むりょう) → 無料(むりょう)じゃなきゃ |
| With the tail kept | ＋ いけない／だめ | 行(い)かなきゃいけない／行(い)かなきゃだめ |
| With the tail dropped | nothing after it | 行(い)かなきゃ。／行(い)かなくちゃ。 |
| Emphatic variant | なくちゃ → なくっちゃ | 行(い)かなくっちゃ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two contractions come from different parents and do not
mix. `なくては` → <b>なくちゃ</b>; `なければ` → <b>なきゃ</b>. There is no ❌`なくきゃ` and
no ❌`なけちゃ`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう<b>寝(ね)なきゃ</b>。
I've got to go to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
あ、電気(でんき)消(け)さ<b>なきゃ</b>。
Oh - I need to turn the light off.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)早(はや)いから、そろそろ<b>帰(かえ)らなくちゃ</b>。
I've got an early start, so I should be heading home.
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)、今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に<b>終(お)わらせなきゃいけない</b>んだ。
The thing is, I've got to get the homework finished today.
</pre>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)が空(から)っぽだ。買(か)い物(もの)に<b>行(い)かなきゃ</b>。
The fridge is empty. I need to go shopping.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)、持(も)って<b>行(い)かなくちゃだめだよ</b>。午後(ごご)から雨(あめ)だって。
You've got to take an umbrella - apparently it's raining from the afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)友(とも)だちの結婚式(けっこんしき)だから、新(あたら)しい服(ふく)を<b>買(か)わなきゃ</b>いけないなあ。
My friend's wedding is next week, so I'm going to have to buy something new to wear.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)がこんなに散(ち)らかっているのに、<b>片付(かたづ)けなくちゃ</b>と思(おも)うだけで何(なに)もしていない。
The room is in this state and all I do is think I ought to tidy it, without ever actually starting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Casual. That is the whole point, and it is also the whole restriction. `なくちゃ`
and `なきゃ` belong in speech among friends, family and close colleagues, and in
writing only where writing imitates speech - messages, chat, blogs, dialogue in
manga and novels. They do not appear in reports, emails to clients, exam answers
or announcements.

They are **not** sloppy or uneducated, and they are not gender-marked: adults of
every age and both sexes use them constantly. What they are is *unguarded*. Saying
<code>帰(かえ)らなきゃ</code> lets the listener see you making a decision, which is
exactly right with a friend and wrong in front of a customer.

`なきゃ` is the more frequent of the two in everyday Tokyo speech. `なくちゃ` is a
touch lighter and rounder, and it turns up often when someone is talking to a
child or being deliberately breezy; a few speakers hear it as slightly cuter than
`なきゃ`, but nobody would call it a different level of politeness. The stretched
`なくっちゃ` adds emphasis - <code>これは見(み)なくっちゃ</code>「this I've *got* to
see」.

The tail is normally dropped. When it is kept, it stays in plain form:
`なきゃいけない`, `なきゃだめ`, and in very casual Tokyo speech `なきゃなんない` (from
`ならない`). Attaching a polite tail is the classic learner error - you cannot
half-contract a sentence, and ❌<code>行(い)かなきゃいけません</code> mixes two
registers inside one predicate. If you need politeness, expand the whole thing back
out to `なければなりません`.

One more thing that catches people out in listening: because the ending is missing,
these forms often end an utterance on a rising or trailing intonation and sound
unfinished. <code>あ、洗濯(せんたく)しなきゃ…</code> is a complete thought. Do not wait
for the rest of it.

## Contrast

### ～なきゃ vs ～なくちゃ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same word twice over. Choose by ear, not by meaning;
`なきゃ` is the more common default.

</div>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>出(で)なきゃ</b>。
I'd better get going.
</pre>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>出(で)なくちゃ</b>。
I'd better get going.
</pre>

There is no difference of meaning, obligation strength or politeness to report, and
a page that invented one would be lying. The only observable tendencies are that
`なきゃ` is heard more often overall, `なくちゃ` sounds slightly softer and is common
in speech directed at children, and only `なくちゃ` has the emphatic stretched form
`なくっちゃ`.

### ～なきゃ vs ～ないと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are casual spoken obligation. `ないと` is neutral and
usable slightly further up the politeness scale; `なきゃ` is more informal and more
self-directed.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)、作(つく)ら<b>ないと</b>いけませんね。
We'll need to put the meeting materials together, won't we.
</pre>

<pre>
うわ、資料(しりょう)作(つく)ら<b>なきゃ</b>。
Ugh - I've got to make the handouts.
</pre>

`ないと` survives being made polite: <code>行(い)かないといけません</code> is fine at
work. `なきゃ` does not. And `なきゃ` leans towards things the speaker is telling
*themselves*, which is why it so often follows あ or うわ and precedes nothing at
all.

### ～なきゃ as obligation vs ～なきゃ as plain "unless"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なきゃ` is just contracted `なければ`. If a consequence follows
it, it is an ordinary conditional and has nothing to do with obligation.

</div>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で<b>見(み)なきゃ</b>わからないよ。
You won't know unless you look for yourself.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で<b>見(み)なきゃ</b>。
I've got to go and look for myself.
</pre>

The first is "if you don't look, you won't understand" - the `いけない` is not
implied and never was. The second has nothing after it, so the listener fills in
the missing "that won't do" and hears an obligation.

The same holds for `なくちゃ`, and it explains a shape you will meet often:
<code>安(やす)くなきゃ買(か)わない</code>「I won't buy it unless it's cheap」,
<code>君(きみ)じゃなきゃだめだ</code>「it has to be you」.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)か<b>なきゃです</b>。 → ✅ 行(い)か<b>なきゃ</b>。／行(い)か<b>なければなりません</b>。
  There is no polite version of a contraction. `です` cannot be bolted onto
  `なきゃ`; if you need politeness, expand the form.

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>なきゃ</b> → ✅ 行(い)か<b>なきゃ</b>
  The contraction still starts from the ない-form. Build <code>行(い)かない</code>
  first, then squeeze.

- ❌ 急(いそ)が<b>なくきゃ</b> → ✅ 急(いそ)が<b>なくちゃ</b>／急(いそ)が<b>なきゃ</b>
  The two contractions have different parents and cannot be blended.
  `なくては`→`なくちゃ`, `なければ`→`なきゃ`.

- ❌ 会員(かいいん)<b>だなきゃ</b>だめです。 → ✅ 会員(かいいん)<b>じゃなきゃ</b>だめだよ。
  Nouns negate to `じゃない`, so the contracted conditional is `じゃなきゃ`. Note the
  ending changed too - `だめです` after `じゃなきゃ` is a register mismatch.

- ❌（in an email to a client）明日(あした)までに送(おく)ら<b>なきゃ</b>いけません。 → ✅ 明日(あした)までに送(おく)ら<b>なければなりません</b>。
  Contractions do not belong in written business Japanese, however polite the tail
  you attach to them.

## Practice

**1.** やばい、電車(でんしゃ)行(い)っちゃう。走(はし)ら____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

走(はし)ら<b>なきゃ</b>。（also fine: 走(はし)ら<b>なくちゃ</b>。）

The situation is urgent, spoken and self-directed, which is the natural home of the
contracted forms with the ending dropped. Anything longer would sound like the
speaker had time to think.

</details>

**2.** 部長(ぶちょう)に（報告(ほうこく)しなきゃいけません・報告(ほうこく)しなければなりません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

報告(ほうこく)し<b>なければなりません</b>。

`なきゃ` is a casual contraction and `いけません` is a polite ending; putting them in
one predicate mixes registers inside a single word. Pick one level - fully
contracted for speech among friends, fully expanded when you are being polite.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as something you say to yourself on remembering it: "Oh no, I have to return the library books today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あ、今日(きょう)図書館(としょかん)の本(ほん)返(かえ)さ<b>なきゃ</b>。

Muttered self-obligation drops the `いけない` and drops the particles too. The
interjection at the front is part of the pattern: these forms almost always follow
a moment of realisation.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この映画(えいが)、絶対(ぜったい)見(み)るなきゃ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この映画(えいが)、絶対(ぜったい)見(み)<b>なきゃ</b>。（emphatic: 見(み)<b>なくっちゃ</b>。）

`なきゃ` replaces `なければ`, which is built on the ない-form - <code>見(み)ない</code> →
<code>見(み)なければ</code> → <code>見(み)なきゃ</code>. The dictionary form never appears in
front of it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 早(はや)く<b>予約(よやく)しなきゃ</b>席(せき)がなくなるよ。
(b) 早(はや)く<b>予約(よやく)しなきゃ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is not an obligation sentence at all. `なきゃ` here is simply contracted
`なければ` working as an ordinary conditional, and the second half states the
consequence: book early, or the seats will be gone.

(b) leaves the consequence out. Because the missing half is understood to be "that
won't do", the whole utterance is heard as the speaker realising they have to act.
The contraction is identical in both; what decides the reading is whether anything
follows it.

</details>

# ～なくてもいい

**Meaning** · you don't have to; it's fine not to

`～なくてもいい` is the exact negative counterpart of `～てもいい`. Same shape, same
`も` of concession, same verdict `いい` - only the thing being conceded is now a
*non*-action: "even if you don't do it, that's fine." It does not tell anyone to
stop. It removes a requirement and leaves the choice open.

## Formation

Negate first, swap the final `い` of `ない` for `くても`, then finish with `いい`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form − い + くてもいい | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)たなくてもいい |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form − い + くてもいい | 着(き)る → 着(き)なくてもいい |
| Verb (する) | しなくてもいい | 予約(よやく)する → 予約(よやく)しなくてもいい |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)なくてもいい | 来(く)る → 来(こ)なくてもいい |
| い-adjective | ～くない → ～くなくてもいい | 新(あたら)しい → 新(あたら)しくなくてもいい |
| な-adjective | drop だ + でなくてもいい | 完璧(かんぺき)だ → 完璧(かんぺき)でなくてもいい |
| Noun | noun + でなくてもいい | スーツ → スーツでなくてもいい |
| Polite | いい → いいです | 待(ま)たなくてもいいです |
| Asking | ～なくてもいいですか | 待(ま)たなくてもいいですか |
| Softer / politer | いい → かまいません, 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です | 待(ま)たなくてもかまいません |
| Casual (も dropped) | ～なくていい | 待(ま)たなくていいよ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the particle carries the whole meaning.
`～なくて<b>は</b>いけない` = must; `～なくて<b>も</b>いい` = needn't. Everything else in
the two patterns is shared scaffolding.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
靴(くつ)は<b>脱(ぬ)がなくてもいいです</b>。
You don't have to take your shoes off.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)が<b>なくてもいい</b>よ。
There's no need to rush.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)は<b>書(か)かなくてもいいです</b>か。
Is it all right if I don't write back?
</pre>

<pre>
服装(ふくそう)はスーツ<b>でなくてもいい</b>そうです。
I hear it doesn't have to be a suit.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は<b>広(ひろ)くなくてもいい</b>けど、駅(えき)に近(ちか)いほうがいい。
The flat doesn't have to be big, but I'd rather it were near a station.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなに<b>心配(しんぱい)しなくてもいい</b>んじゃない？
Don't you think you're worrying more than you need to?
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は仕事(しごと)が早(はや)く終(お)わったので、残業(ざんぎょう)は<b>しなくてもいい</b>ことになりました。
Work finished early today, so it turns out we don't have to stay late.
</pre>

<pre>
レポートは手書(てが)きでも印刷(いんさつ)でもよくて、表紙(ひょうし)は<b>つけなくてもかまいません</b>。
The report may be handwritten or printed, and you needn't add a cover page.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern itself is neutral; `いいです` makes it polite in form. What is not
neutral is the **social act**. Saying "you don't have to" means you are in a
position to lift the requirement, and that is a position of authority.
Between equals it is generous. Aimed upward it is presumptuous: telling your
manager <code>部長(ぶちょう)は来(こ)なくてもいいですよ</code> sounds like you are
giving them permission to skip work.

Upward, the same content goes through different wording:
<code>ご無理(むり)なさらないでください</code>, <code>お気(き)になさらないでください</code>,
<code>そのままで結構(けっこう)です</code>. Note that <code>結構(けっこう)です</code> is genuinely ambiguous - 
it can mean "that's fine as it is" or "no thank you" - and tone and context are the
only things separating them.

In speech the `も` disappears constantly: <code>行(い)かなくていいよ</code>,
<code>待(ま)たなくていいから</code>. This is not a different pattern and carries no
change in meaning; it is simply what fast Japanese does to the phrase. Learners who
have only seen `なくてもいい` in a textbook often fail to recognise `なくていい` in
conversation. The more emphatic casual variant is `なくたっていい`
(<code>謝(あやま)らなくたっていいよ</code>).

Three neighbours worth knowing, in rising order of formality: <code>なくても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)</code>
(warm, spoken), `なくてもかまいません`「it doesn't matter if you don't」(neutral,
business), <code>なくても結構(けっこう)です</code>「it is not required」(formal, institutional).
`かまわない` is the one to reach for when you want to sound like you truly do not
mind.

Finally, the pattern is not only about other people. `～なくてもいい` about yourself
reports relief or a decision:
<code>明日(あした)は早(はや)く起(お)きなくてもいいから、映画(えいが)でも見(み)よう</code>.

## Contrast

### ～なくてもいい vs ～てもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもいい` opens a door: you *may* do it.
`～なくてもいい` closes a duty: you *needn't* do it. Neither one pushes you in
either direction.

</div>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)ってもいい</b>ですか。
Is it okay if I take photos?
</pre>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)らなくてもいい</b>ですか。
Is it okay if I don't take photos?
</pre>

The first asks whether the action is permitted; the second asks whether the action
is required. In a museum you would ask the first; on a job where photographing is
part of your task, you would ask the second.

They pair up into a neat square with the obligation forms. `てもいい` may,
`てはいけない` must not, `なくてはいけない` must, `なくてもいい` needn't. Permission
and prohibition are built on the て-form; obligation and exemption on the negative
stem.

### ～なくてもいい vs ～ないでください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - "you don't have to" is not "please don't". `なくてもいい`
leaves the action available; `ないでください` takes it away.

</div>

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)わ<b>なくてもいいですよ</b>。
You don't have to help.
</pre>

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)わ<b>ないでください</b>。
Please don't help.
</pre>

English blurs these - "you don't have to help" is often *meant* as a polite
refusal - but Japanese does not. The first sentence releases someone who was
offering; the second turns them away, and would be startling from a colleague.
When you want to decline an offer kindly, the natural wording is
<code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です、ありがとうございます</code>, not either of these.

### ～なくてもいい vs ～なくてもかまいません・～なくても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same exemption, three temperatures. <code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)</code> is warm, `いい` is
neutral, `かまいません` is businesslike.

</div>

<pre>
無理(むり)に全部(ぜんぶ)<b>食(た)べなくても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だよ</b>。
Honestly, you don't have to finish it all.
</pre>

<pre>
当日(とうじつ)は印鑑(いんかん)を<b>お持(も)ちにならなくてもかまいません</b>。
You do not need to bring a personal seal on the day.
</pre>

<code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)</code> says *don't worry about it* and belongs in speech between people who know
each other. `かまいません` says *it makes no difference to us* and belongs in
counters, forms and customer service. Plain `いい` sits between them and is never
wrong, which is why it is the form to learn first.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 払(はら)わ<b>なくてはいい</b>です。 → ✅ 払(はら)わ<b>なくても</b>いいです。
  `なくては` pairs with `いけない`; `なくても` pairs with `いい`. Mixing them produces
  a pattern that does not exist.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>だなくても</b>いいです。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>でなくても</b>いいです。
  Nouns and な-adjectives negate through `で(は)ない`, so the join is `でなくても`.

- ❌ 新(あたら)しい<b>じゃなくても</b>いい。 → ✅ 新(あたら)し<b>くなくても</b>いい。
  <code>新(あたら)しい</code> is an い-adjective, so its negative is `くない` and the
  join is `くなくても`.

- ❌（meaning "you don't have to come tomorrow"）明日(あした)は来(こ)<b>ないでください</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は来(こ)<b>なくてもいいですよ</b>。
  Straight from English. The `ないでください` version instructs the person to stay
  away, which is a completely different message.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)は出席(しゅっせき)し<b>なくてもいいですよ</b>。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、ご都合(つごう)が悪(わる)ければ、ご欠席(けっせき)でも問題(もんだい)ございません。
  Releasing someone from a duty implies you had the authority to impose it. Upward,
  state the situation instead of granting an exemption.

## Practice

**1.** このレストランは予約(よやく)し____いいですよ。いつも空(す)いていますから。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

予約(よやく)し<b>なくても</b>いいですよ。

The restaurant is always empty, so booking is not required - the exemption reading.
Note the particle: `なくても` with `いい`, never `なくては`.

</details>

**2.** 会議(かいぎ)のとき、ネクタイを（しなくてもいいです・しないでください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Either is grammatical, but they say different things.
<b>しなくてもいいです</b> means a tie is optional; <b>しないでください</b> instructs
people not to wear one. For a normal dress-code announcement the first is what is
meant.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "You don't need to bring anything."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)も<b>持(も)って来(こ)なくてもいいですよ</b>。
（more formal: 何(なに)も<b>お持(も)ちいただかなくてもかまいません</b>。）

The `よ` matters: without it the sentence can read as a flat instruction, whereas
telling a guest not to trouble themselves should sound warm. <code>来(く)る</code> takes
the irregular negative stem <code>来(こ)</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)のパーティーは正式(せいしき)な服(ふく)だなくてもいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)のパーティーは正式(せいしき)な服(ふく)<b>でなくてもいいです</b>。

`だ` never survives in front of a negative join. A noun negates as
<code>服(ふく)ではない</code>, and the exemption form built on that is
<code>服(ふく)でなくてもいい</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)ま<b>なくてもいいです</b>。
(b) 全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)ま<b>なくてはいけません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) removes a requirement: reading all of it is optional, and the listener may read
as much or as little as they like. One particle - `も` - is doing that work.

(b) imposes one: all of it has to be read, and there is no choice in the matter.
The `は` closes off the alternative that `も` opens. Because the two sentences
differ by a single kana in the middle of a long predicate, they are easy to mishear
and worth saying aloud a few times.

</details>

# ～必要がある

**Meaning** · there is a need to do it; it is necessary to do it

Read 必要(ひつよう). <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> is a noun meaning *necessity*, and
`ある` is the ordinary verb of existence - so the pattern literally says *a need to
do X exists*. That is why it feels cooler than the obligation forms: it does not
push anybody, it states a fact about the situation.

## Formation

The verb in front stays in the **dictionary form**. Nouns join with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + 必要(ひつよう)がある | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つ必要(ひつよう)がある |
| Verb (する) | dictionary form + 必要(ひつよう)がある | 登録(とうろく)する → 登録(とうろく)する必要(ひつよう)がある |
| Noun | noun + の + 必要(ひつよう)がある | 修理(しゅうり) → 修理(しゅうり)の必要(ひつよう)がある |
| Negative | 必要(ひつよう)はない／必要(ひつよう)がない | 待(ま)つ必要(ひつよう)はない |
| Polite | 必要(ひつよう)があります／ありません | 待(ま)つ必要(ひつよう)があります |
| Past | 必要(ひつよう)があった／なかった | 待(ま)つ必要(ひつよう)はなかった |
| Question | 必要(ひつよう)がありますか | 待(ま)つ必要(ひつよう)がありますか |
| Formal negative | 必要(ひつよう)はございません | 待(ま)つ必要(ひつよう)はございません |
| い-adjective / な-adjective | not available | 静(しず)かだ → ❌静(しず)かな必要(ひつよう)がある |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative flips the particle. Affirmative
<code>必要(ひつよう)が<b>ある</b></code>, negative <code>必要(ひつよう)は<b>ない</b></code>. The
`は` is doing what `は` always does with a negative: marking the thing being denied.
<code>必要(ひつよう)がない</code> is possible, but `は` is the everyday choice.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negation goes on `ある`, never on the verb in front. "You don't need
to go" is <code>行(い)く必要(ひつよう)はない</code>, not
❌<code>行(い)かない必要(ひつよう)がある</code>, which would mean something like "there
is a need for you not to go."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
パスポートを見(み)せる<b>必要(ひつよう)があります</b>。
You need to show your passport.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)ぐ<b>必要(ひつよう)はありません</b>。
There's no need to hurry.
</pre>

<pre>
もう一度(いちど)確認(かくにん)する<b>必要(ひつよう)がある</b>。
We need to check it one more time.
</pre>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)は翻訳(ほんやく)の<b>必要(ひつよう)がありますか</b>。
Does this document need translating?
</pre>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)する<b>必要(ひつよう)はない</b>よ。全部(ぜんぶ)うまくいっているから。
You've no need to worry - everything's going fine.
</pre>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)で働(はたら)くには、ビザを申請(しんせい)する<b>必要(ひつよう)があります</b>。
To work abroad, you need to apply for a visa.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)作(つく)った資料(しりょう)なので、数字(すうじ)を新(あたら)しいものに直(なお)す<b>必要(ひつよう)があります</b>。
These materials are from last year, so the figures need updating.
</pre>

<pre>
会員(かいいん)カードをお持(も)ちの方(かた)は、受付(うけつけ)で住所(じゅうしょ)を書(か)く<b>必要(ひつよう)はございません</b>。
Members do not need to write their address at reception.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>～必要(ひつよう)がある</code> is the **objective** member of the family. It describes
a requirement as a property of the situation, with nobody's authority behind it,
which makes it the default in manuals, instructions, application guidance, business
email and academic writing. If you have ever read a Japanese form, you have read
this pattern.

That neutrality has a practical use: it lets you tell someone what has to happen
without telling *them* what to do. <code>ご確認(かくにん)いただく必要(ひつよう)がございます</code> is still direct, but
<code>三月(さんがつ)までに提出(ていしゅつ)する必要(ひつよう)があります</code> said
about a shared deadline points at the deadline, not at the listener.

The flip side is that it sounds stiff about small personal errands. Nobody says
<code>今日(きょう)は買(か)い物(もの)をする必要(ひつよう)がある</code> to a
friend - that is <code>買(か)い物(もの)行(い)かないと</code> territory. Using the
formal pattern for a trip to the supermarket sounds like reading from a report.

The negative is the half you will use most, and it is unusually warm for such a
formal pattern. <code>謝(あやま)る必要(ひつよう)はないよ</code>「you don't need to
apologise」 and <code>気(き)にする必要(ひつよう)はありません</code>「there's no need to
worry about it」 are everyday reassurances. In casual speech `が` drops out
altogether: <code>そんなに急(いそ)ぐ必要(ひつよう)ない</code>.

At the formal end, `ある` is replaced by its humble-polite equivalents:
<code>必要(ひつよう)がございます</code> and <code>必要(ひつよう)はございません</code>, which
is what shop staff and official notices use.

## Contrast

### ～必要(ひつよう)がある vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>必要(ひつよう)がある</code> reports that a need exists;
`なければならない` says the alternative is unacceptable. The first describes, the
second compels.

</div>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)は毎日(まいにち)飲(の)む<b>必要(ひつよう)があります</b>。
This medicine needs to be taken every day.
</pre>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)は毎日(まいにち)<b>飲(の)まなければなりません</b>。
You must take this medicine every day.
</pre>

Both are correct and a doctor might say either. The first sounds like an
explanation of how the treatment works; the second sounds like an instruction being
issued to you. When the requirement comes from a rule with force behind it - a law,
a contract, a school regulation - `なければならない` is the stronger fit. When it
comes from the nature of the task, <code>必要(ひつよう)がある</code> is.

There is also a grammatical difference worth noticing: `なければならない` attaches to
a *negative* stem, while <code>必要(ひつよう)がある</code> attaches to a plain dictionary
form. That makes the second much easier to build under time pressure.

### ～必要(ひつよう)はない vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくてもいい` grants an exemption; <code>必要(ひつよう)はない</code>
denies that a requirement ever existed.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は<b>来(こ)なくてもいいですよ</b>。
You don't have to come in tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は<b>来(く)る必要(ひつよう)はありません</b>。
There is no need for you to come in tomorrow.
</pre>

The first is a person releasing another person, which is why it needs `よ` or a
smile to sound kind. The second is a statement about the schedule, and because it
does not position the speaker as the one granting permission, it is often the safer
thing to say upward or to a stranger.

In their negative-reassurance use they come very close:
<code>心配(しんぱい)しなくてもいい</code> and
<code>心配(しんぱい)する必要(ひつよう)はない</code> are near-synonyms, the second
slightly firmer, as if arguing the worry away rather than waving it off.

### ～必要(ひつよう)がある vs ～が必要(ひつよう)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>する必要(ひつよう)がある</code> needs an **action**;
<code>が必要(ひつよう)だ</code> needs a **thing**.

</div>

<pre>
ここで靴(くつ)を<b>脱(ぬ)ぐ必要(ひつよう)があります</b>。
You need to take your shoes off here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここでは上(うわ)ばきが<b>必要(ひつよう)です</b>。
You need indoor shoes here.
</pre>

The two are often interchangeable in translation and never in grammar. A verb
before <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> requires the dictionary form and the `が…ある` frame; a
noun before it takes `が` and the plain な-adjective predicate
<code>必要(ひつよう)だ／です</code>. A noun can also be forced into the `ある` frame with
`の` - <code>修理(しゅうり)の必要(ひつよう)がある</code> - which sounds distinctly more
formal than <code>修理(しゅうり)が必要(ひつよう)だ</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)き<b>ます</b>必要(ひつよう)があります。 → ✅ 行(い)く必要(ひつよう)があります。
  The verb in front of <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> is plain and non-past. `ます` cannot appear
  inside the sentence; politeness goes on `あります` at the end.

- ❌ 行(い)か<b>ない</b>必要(ひつよう)があります。 → ✅ 行(い)く必要(ひつよう)は<b>ありません</b>。
  To say there is no need, negate `ある`, not the verb. Negating the verb creates a
  need *not* to go, which is a different and much rarer idea.

- ❌ ビザ必要(ひつよう)があります。 → ✅ ビザ<b>の</b>必要(ひつよう)があります。／ビザ<b>が</b>必要(ひつよう)です。
  A noun cannot sit straight against <code>必要(ひつよう)</code>. Either link it with `の` or
  switch to the <code>が必要(ひつよう)だ</code> pattern.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)か<b>な</b>必要(ひつよう)があります。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>でなければなりません</b>。
  Adjectives do not take this pattern at all. Requirements about qualities go
  through the obligation forms.

- ❌（to a friend）そろそろ帰(かえ)る必要(ひつよう)がある。 → ✅ そろそろ帰(かえ)らないと。
  Not wrong, just wrongly dressed. For your own small obligations in conversation,
  the `ないと` / `なきゃ` end of the family is what people actually use.

## Practice

**1.** この申込書(もうしこみしょ)には、はんこを押(お)す____ありますか。

The verb stays in the dictionary form, and the question is about whether a
requirement exists - exactly what this pattern is for. On a form, it is more
natural than asking <code>押(お)さなくてもいいですか</code>.

</details>

**2.** 課長(かちょう)に（報告(ほうこく)する必要(ひつよう)があります・報告(ほうこく)しなきゃ）と会議(かいぎ)で言(い)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A meeting is a formal setting and the sentence is stating what the situation
requires, not muttering a personal chore. `なきゃ` is a casual contraction and would
sound out of place in front of colleagues.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a line of written guidance: "Applicants need to submit two photographs."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Written guidance wants the impersonal pattern: it states what the process requires
without addressing anyone. <code>提出(ていしゅつ)しなければならない</code> would also fit
a rulebook, but it sounds more like a command than an instruction sheet.

</details>

(b) tells the listener they are released from writing one. It implies the speaker
is in a position to decide, so it sounds more personal and, depending on who is
speaking to whom, either kind or presumptuous.

</details>

# 必要

**Meaning** · necessary; needed - the word itself, not the pattern

Read 必要(ひつよう). It is a noun that behaves as a な-adjective: something
<code>が必要(ひつよう)だ</code>「is needed」, and a <code>必要(ひつよう)な</code> thing is a required
one. The verb pattern <code>～必要(ひつよう)がある</code> is built out of this word, but the
word does plenty of work on its own, and getting its particles right is what stops
learners writing English with Japanese vocabulary in it.

## Formation

The thing that is needed takes `が`, not `を` - <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> is not a verb and
has no object.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (predicate) | Noun + が + 必要(ひつよう)だ／です | ビザ → ビザが必要(ひつよう)です |
| Before a noun | 必要(ひつよう) + な + Noun | 道具(どうぐ) → 必要(ひつよう)な道具(どうぐ) |
| Purpose | Noun + に + 必要(ひつよう)な + Noun | 生活(せいかつ) → 生活(せいかつ)に必要(ひつよう)なもの |
| Negative (casual) | 必要(ひつよう)ない／必要(ひつよう)じゃない | それは必要(ひつよう)ない |
| Negative (polite) | 必要(ひつよう)ありません／必要(ひつよう)ではありません | それは必要(ひつよう)ありません |
| Past | 必要(ひつよう)だった／必要(ひつよう)なかった | 時間(じかん)が必要(ひつよう)だった |
| Becoming | 必要(ひつよう)になる | お金(かね)が必要(ひつよう)になった |
| Verb instead of noun | dictionary form + 必要(ひつよう)がある | 申(もう)し込(こ)む必要(ひつよう)がある |
| Formal verb | Noun + を + 必要(ひつよう)とする | 助(たす)けを必要(ひつよう)とする人(ひと) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> is a な-adjective, so it never takes `くない`
or `かった`. The negative is <code>必要(ひつよう)じゃない</code>／<code>必要(ひつよう)ない</code>, and
the past is <code>必要(ひつよう)だった</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the written antonym is <code>不要(ふよう)</code>, which you will meet on forms and
labels rather than in speech: <code>記入(きにゅう)不要(ふよう)</code>「no entry
required」, <code>返却(へんきゃく)不要(ふよう)</code>「no need to return this」.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
パスポートが<b>必要(ひつよう)です</b>。
You'll need your passport.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>必要(ひつよう)な</b>ものだけ持(も)って来(き)てください。
Please bring only what you need.
</pre>

<pre>
その説明(せつめい)は<b>必要(ひつよう)ない</b>よ。
That explanation isn't necessary.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し込(こ)みに<b>必要(ひつよう)な</b>書類(しょるい)は三(みっ)つです。
There are three documents required for the application.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に現金(げんきん)が<b>必要(ひつよう)になった</b>。
I suddenly found myself needing cash.
</pre>

<pre>
この仕事(しごと)に経験(けいけん)は<b>必要(ひつよう)ではありません</b>。
No experience is required for this job.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしを始(はじ)めるとき、思(おも)っていたより多(おお)くのものが<b>必要(ひつよう)だった</b>。
When I started living on my own, I needed more things than I had expected.
</pre>

<pre>
災害(さいがい)のときに本当(ほんとう)に<b>必要(ひつよう)な</b>のは、水(みず)と情報(じょうほう)だと言(い)われています。
What you really need in a disaster, they say, is water and information.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>必要(ひつよう)</code> is a Sino-Japanese word, and like most of them it leans formal
and slightly abstract. It is completely ordinary in polite speech, business email,
notices and writing. In casual conversation, though, Japanese usually reaches for
the plain verb `いる` instead: a friend asks <code>袋(ふくろ)いる？</code>, not
<code>袋(ふくろ)が必要(ひつよう)ですか</code>. At a convenience store counter you will
hear both, because staff speech is formal.

The negatives are worth separating. <code>必要(ひつよう)ない</code> is short, everyday and
can sound abrupt - <code>そんなの必要(ひつよう)ない</code> is close to "we don't need
that rubbish." <code>必要(ひつよう)ありません</code> is the neutral polite version and the one
to use with strangers. <code>必要(ひつよう)ではありません</code> is stiffer again and belongs
in writing. As a refusal at a shop counter, the standard phrase is not any of these
but <code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です</code> or <code>結構(けっこう)です</code>.

<code>必要(ひつよう)とする</code> is a genuine verb and a register marker: it appears in
journalism, official documents and formal description - 
<code>支援(しえん)を必要(ひつよう)としている地域(ちいき)</code>「areas that require
assistance」. Using it in conversation would sound like reading a press release.

One more thing to notice: <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> describes what a *purpose* demands. It
carries no warmth. That is why it is the right word for documents, tools, time and
money, and the wrong word for people - see the contrast below.

## Contrast

### 必要(ひつよう) vs いる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different word class and different register.
`いる` is a verb and casual; <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> is a な-adjective and formal.

</div>

<pre>
レシート、<b>いる</b>？
D'you want the receipt?
</pre>

<pre>
レシートは<b>必要(ひつよう)ですか</b>。
Would you like a receipt?
</pre>

Both take their object with `が` (or with no particle at all in speech), which is
the one thing they share structurally. `いる` - written 要(い)る, though usually
left in kana - is a う-verb: `いります`, `いらない`, `いった`. Beware that its past
and て-forms collide with <code>入(はい)る</code>, <code>行(い)く</code> and the existence verb
`いる`, which is why it is far more common in the non-past.

`いる` is also limited to things. For an *action* you need
<code>～必要(ひつよう)がある</code> or one of the obligation forms: there is no
❌<code>行(い)くがいる</code>.

### 必要(ひつよう) vs 大切(たいせつ)・大事(だいじ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> = required for a purpose.
<code>大切(たいせつ)</code>／<code>大事(だいじ)</code> = important, precious, valued. Do not use
<code>必要(ひつよう)</code> about people.

</div>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)はとても<b>大切(たいせつ)です</b>。
My family means a great deal to me.
</pre>

<pre>
この仕事(しごと)には集中力(しゅうちゅうりょく)が<b>必要(ひつよう)です</b>。
This job requires concentration.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences go wrong in interesting ways.
<code>家族(かぞく)が必要(ひつよう)です</code> sounds like you are shopping for one - 
the word treats its object as a resource. And
<code>集中力(しゅうちゅうりょく)が大切(たいせつ)です</code> is fine but says something
milder: concentration *matters*, rather than the job being impossible without it.

<code>大事(だいじ)</code> is the more conversational of the two importance words;
<code>大切(たいせつ)</code> is a shade warmer and more careful. Both are な-adjectives, like
<code>必要(ひつよう)</code> itself.

### 必要(ひつよう)だ vs 必要(ひつよう)がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a **noun** is needed → <code>が必要(ひつよう)だ</code>. An **action** is
needed → <code>～する必要(ひつよう)がある</code>.

</div>

<pre>
予約(よやく)が<b>必要(ひつよう)です</b>。
A reservation is required.
</pre>

<pre>
予約(よやく)する<b>必要(ひつよう)があります</b>。
You need to make a reservation.
</pre>

The information is the same and the framing is not. The first names a thing the
situation demands, and would suit a sign on a door. The second describes something
the listener has to do, and suits an explanation.

A noun can be pushed into the `ある` frame with `の`
(<code>予約(よやく)の必要(ひつよう)があります</code>), which sounds noticeably more
bureaucratic. A verb, on the other hand, cannot use the `だ` frame at all - there is
no ❌<code>予約(よやく)するが必要(ひつよう)です</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 必要(ひつよう)<b>の</b>書類(しょるい) → ✅ 必要(ひつよう)<b>な</b>書類(しょるい)
  <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> is a な-adjective when it modifies a noun. `の` looks tempting
  because the word is also a noun, but it is not how the phrase is built.

- ❌ パスポート<b>を</b>必要(ひつよう)です。 → ✅ パスポート<b>が</b>必要(ひつよう)です。
  There is no verb here, so there is no object. The needed thing is the subject and
  takes `が`.

- ❌ この靴(くつ)は必要(ひつよう)<b>くない</b>。 → ✅ この靴(くつ)は必要(ひつよう)<b>じゃない</b>。
  Only い-adjectives negate with `くない`. <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> takes `じゃない`,
  `ではない`, or bare `ない`.

- ❌ 病院(びょういん)に行(い)く<b>が</b>必要(ひつよう)です。 → ✅ 病院(びょういん)に行(い)く<b>必要(ひつよう)があります</b>。
  A verb cannot be marked with `が`. To say an action is needed, switch to the
  <code>必要(ひつよう)がある</code> frame.

- ❌（to a friend, about a plastic bag）袋(ふくろ)が必要(ひつよう)ですか。 → ✅ 袋(ふくろ)いる？
  Grammatical and oddly formal. Among friends the everyday word for needing a thing
  is `いる`.

## Practice

**1.** この手続(てつづ)きに____な物(もの)を全部(ぜんぶ)持(も)って来(き)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この手続(てつづ)きに<b>必要(ひつよう)</b>な物(もの)を全部(ぜんぶ)持(も)って来(き)ました。

Modifying a noun, <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> behaves as a な-adjective, so the connector is
`な`. The `に` marks the purpose the things are needed *for*.

</details>

**2.** ペン、（いる・必要(ひつよう)でいらっしゃいますか）？ - said to a close friend.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ペン、<b>いる</b>？

Between friends the plain verb is what people use; the honorific version is not
merely over-polite, it is comic. <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> itself is not rude here, but it
still sounds like a form rather than a question about a pen.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a line on a website: "A credit card is not required."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

クレジットカードは<b>必要(ひつよう)ありません</b>。

<code>必要(ひつよう)ありません</code> is the standard polite negative and the usual wording on
notices. <code>必要(ひつよう)ない</code> would be too blunt for a public-facing page, and
<code>必要(ひつよう)ではございません</code> would be stiffer than most sites go.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)は早(はや)く起(お)きるが必要(ひつよう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)は早(はや)く起(お)きる<b>必要(ひつよう)があります</b>。

`が` can only mark a noun. When what is needed is an action, the dictionary form
attaches straight to <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> and the sentence closes with `ある`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この会社(かいしゃ)には君(きみ)が<b>必要(ひつよう)だ</b>。
(b) この会社(かいしゃ)にとって君(きみ)は<b>大切(たいせつ)な人(ひと)だ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the company cannot function without you. <code>必要(ひつよう)</code> frames a person
as something the organisation requires, so the compliment is real but cool - it is
about what you produce. In a film this is the line a boss says to stop someone
resigning.

(b) says you are valued. <code>大切(たいせつ)</code> is about worth rather than utility, and
it is the word you would use about a person if you wanted the sentence to sound
warm. Both are natural Japanese; only one of them is affectionate.

</details>
