# N4 - If and when


---

# と - conditional

**Meaning** · whenever A happens, B follows - automatically, every time

This is not the `と` you already know from N5, which joins nouns ("A and B") or
marks a companion ("with"). This one joins two **clauses** and says the second
follows from the first as a matter of course. Not a plan, not a choice: this is
how the world works.

## Formation

The clause in front of `と` is always **plain non-past**, no matter when the
sentence as a whole happens. There is no past-tense form here at all.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + と | 押(お)す → 押(お)すと |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + と | 押(お)さない → 押(お)さないと |
| い-adjective | plain form + と | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いと |
| い-adjective (negative) | くない + と | 安(やす)くない → 安(やす)くないと |
| な-adjective | stem + **だ** + と | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かだと |
| Noun | Noun + **だ** + と | 冬(ふゆ) → 冬(ふゆ)だと |
| Polite (announcements) | ます-form + と | 押(お)します → 押(お)しますと |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nothing that expresses the **speaker's will** may stand in the
result clause. No ～ましょう, no ～てください, no ～たい, no ～たほうがいい, no
command, no invitation. `と` reports what *happens*, never what anyone decides
to do.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `だ` in front of `と` is not optional for nouns and な-adjectives.
学生(がくせい)**だ**と is "if you're a student"; bare 学生(がくせい)と is
"with a student" - the N5 particle, and a completely different sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ここを<b>押(お)すと</b>、音(おと)が出(で)ます。
Press here and it makes a sound.
</pre>

<pre>
春(はる)に<b>なると</b>、この山(やま)は緑(みどり)になります。
When spring comes, this mountain turns green.
</pre>

<pre>
3に5を<b>足(た)すと</b>、8になります。
Three plus five is eight.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>出(で)ないと</b>、電車(でんしゃ)に間(ま)に合(あ)いません。
If we don't leave soon, we won't make the train.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は酒(さけ)を<b>飲(の)むと</b>、いつも同(おな)じ話(はなし)を始(はじ)めます。
Whenever my father drinks, he starts telling the same story.
</pre>

<pre>
この角(かど)を右(みぎ)に<b>曲(ま)がると</b>、左側(ひだりがわ)に小(ちい)さな花屋(はなや)があります。
Turn right at this corner and there's a little flower shop on your left.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)に<b>入(はい)ると</b>、誰(だれ)もいませんでした。
When I went into the room, there was nobody there.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は週末(しゅうまつ)に<b>なると</b>急(きゅう)に込(こ)んで、一時間(いちじかん)ぐらい待(ま)つことになります。
This place suddenly fills up at weekends, and you end up waiting about an hour.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けると</b>、遠(とお)くで祭(まつ)りの太鼓(たいこ)の音(おと)が聞(き)こえた。
When I opened the window, I could hear festival drums somewhere far off.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`と` is register-neutral. It sounds the same in speech and in writing, and the
clause before it stays plain even in the politest sentence:
押(お)すと開(ひら)き**ます** - the politeness lives on the final verb only.
The one polite variant worth recognising is `ますと`, which turns up in
announcements, instruction manuals and careful business speech:
こちらのボタンを押(お)し**ますと**、扉(とびら)が開(ひら)きます。

Where `と` really lives is anywhere a fixed procedure is being described: recipes,
machine instructions, road directions, arithmetic, seasons, and habits. If you can
put "every time" in front of the English and it still makes sense, `と` is
probably right.

Two things learners are blindsided by:

- **`もし` and `と` do not go together.** `もし` announces a hypothesis; `と`
  announces a regularity. もし雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ると… is odd. Use
  たら or ば after `もし`.
- **`～ないと` on its own means "I have to".** The consequence is simply left
  unsaid, and everybody fills it in: もう行(い)かないと。 - "I've got to
  go." これ、今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に出(だ)さないと。 - "I have to hand
  this in today." Extremely common in casual speech.

Finally, `と` with a **past-tense main clause** is not a conditional at all. It is
the narrative "upon doing X, …", used for something the speaker then discovered or
witnessed. Novels and travel diaries are full of it, and it is the one place a
`と` sentence describes a single occasion rather than a rule.

## Contrast

### と vs たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` says *every time, automatically*. `たら` says *on the
occasion that this happens*. Only `たら` can be followed by a request, an
invitation, or a plan.

</div>

This is the difference that gets sentences rejected, because the ban on
will-expressing main clauses after `と` is absolute. Compare:

<pre>
駅(えき)に<b>着(つ)いたら</b>、電話(でんわ)してください。
Call me when you get to the station.
</pre>

❌駅(えき)に着(つ)くと、電話(でんわ)してください is wrong, and not
mildly so - it is asking the listener to do something as an automatic physical
consequence of arriving, which is not a thing you can ask.

<pre>
この自動販売機(じどうはんばいき)は、お金(かね)を<b>入(い)れると</b>ランプがつきます。
On this vending machine, a light comes on when you put money in.
</pre>

That is a property of the machine - invariable, no speaker in it. Switch to
`たら` and it becomes a report of one particular time you fed it a coin.

### と vs ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` states a link the speaker treats as **actual and
invariable**. `ば` puts a condition up as a **premise** and reasons from it.

</div>

Both handle general rules, so they overlap widely, and in many sentences either
is fine. Where they part company is attitude. `と` has no speaker in it; `ば` is
the form you use when you are weighing something up.

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が<b>安(やす)いと</b>、すぐ売(う)り切(き)れます。
When something is cheap, it sells out straight away.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が<b>安(やす)ければ</b>、買(か)います。
If it's cheap, I'll buy it.
</pre>

The first is an observation about how this shop behaves. The second is my
decision procedure - and a decision is exactly what `と` cannot host. Two more
consequences of this: `ば` is the form that takes `もし` comfortably, and `ば`
builds the regret pattern ～ばよかった, for which there is no ～とよかった.

### と vs なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なら` picks up a **premise someone has just supplied**,
usually the other person. `と` connects two events with nobody supplying
anything.

</div>

`なら` is a reply; `と` is a description. This shows up most clearly in
conversation, where `なら` can also put its main clause **earlier in time** than
its condition - something `と` can never do.

<pre>
「北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)くんです。」「北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>なら</b>、六月(ろくがつ)がいいですよ。」
"I'm going to Hokkaido." "Hokkaido? June's the time to go."
</pre>

❌北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)くと、六月(ろくがつ)がいいですよ is
impossible: going to Hokkaido does not cause June to be good, and the speaker is
giving advice, not reporting a mechanism.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 駅(えき)に着(つ)くと、電話(でんわ)してください。 → ✅ 駅(えき)に着(つ)い**たら**、電話(でんわ)してください。
  The clause after `と` is an automatic result, so no request, command,
  invitation or wish can stand there. This is the single most common `と` error.

- ❌ 天気(てんき)がいいと、散歩(さんぽ)しましょう。 → ✅ 天気(てんき)が**よかったら**、散歩(さんぽ)しましょう。
  Same rule. `～ましょう` is a proposal - the speaker's will - and `と` has no
  room for it.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったと、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る**と**／降(ふ)っ**たら**、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。
  Nothing past ever stands in front of `と`. The tense is carried by the main
  clause.

- ❌ もし時間(じかん)があると、手伝(てつだ)います。 → ✅ もし時間(じかん)が**あれば**／**あったら**、手伝(てつだ)います。
  Two problems at once: `もし` flags a hypothesis, and 手伝(てつだ)います is
  an offer. `と` wants neither.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)と安(やす)くなります。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)**だ**と安(やす)くなります。
  Without `だ`, this is the N5 particle: "it gets cheaper with a student."

## Practice

**1.** 秋(あき)になる____、この公園(こうえん)の木(き)が赤(あか)くなります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

秋(あき)になる<b>と</b>、この公園(こうえん)の木(き)が赤(あか)くなります。

Every autumn, without fail, with nobody deciding anything - that is the
definition of a `と` sentence. Note the plain non-past form <code>なる</code> in
front of it even though the main clause is polite.

</details>

**2.** 家(いえ)に（着(つ)くと・着(つ)いたら）、メールをください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

家(いえ)に<b>着(つ)いたら</b>、メールをください。

The main clause is a request. `と` forbids requests outright because its result
clause reports an automatic consequence, and you cannot ask someone to do
something automatically. `たら` has no such restriction.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When it gets cold, my grandmother's legs hurt."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

寒(さむ)く<b>なると</b>、祖母(そぼ)は足(あし)が痛(いた)くなります。

A recurring, involuntary consequence - nobody's choice, every time. `と` is the
natural form. `たら` would push it towards one specific cold day.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 時間(じかん)があると、映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 時間(じかん)が<b>あったら</b>（or <b>あれば</b>）、映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きませんか。

`～ませんか` is an invitation, so the sentence ends with the speaker's will. `と`
cannot carry that. Both `たら` and `ば` work here, because the condition is a
state (`ある`) rather than an action.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けると、雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)っていた。
(b) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けると、部屋(へや)が寒(さむ)くなる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) has a **past** main clause, so it is not a conditional at all. It narrates one
occasion: "I opened the window, and - snow." The speaker is reporting a
discovery, and this is the standard way to write that in Japanese.

(b) has a **non-past** main clause, so it is the ordinary `と`: a general rule
about this room. Open the window, and it gets cold - every time.

Same `と`, and the tense of the *main* clause is what decides which reading you
get.

</details>

# ～ば

**Meaning** · if A, then B - A offered as the condition that would bring B about

`～ば` is the conditional of **reasoning**. It puts a premise on the table and
follows it to its consequence, which is why it dominates written Japanese,
instructions, arguments - and regrets about what you should have done.

## Formation

Every ば-form ends in **-eba**. For verbs, change the final `-u` of the dictionary
form to `-e` and add `ば`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final -u → -e + ば | 書(か)く → 書(か)けば |
| Verb (う-verb) | final -u → -e + ば | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)げば |
| Verb (る-verb) | る → れば | 見(み)る → 見(み)れば |
| Verb (する) | する → すれば | 練習(れんしゅう)する → 練習(れんしゅう)すれば |
| Verb (来(く)る) | irregular | 来(く)る → 来(く)れば |
| Verb (negative) | ない → なければ | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かなければ |
| い-adjective | い → ければ | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しければ |
| いい | irregular | いい → よければ |
| い-adjective (negative) | くない → くなければ | 高(たか)くない → 高(たか)くなければ |
| な-adjective | stem + なら(ば) | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)なら(ば) |
| Noun | Noun + なら(ば) | 雨(あめ) → 雨(あめ)なら(ば) |
| Noun / な-adj (formal) | + であれば | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)であれば |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the bottom two rows are why `なら` looks like part of this table. It
is: `なら(ば)` genuinely is the ば-form of the copula. But the standalone `なら`
you meet as its own pattern does a different job, and the two should be kept
apart in your head.

</div>

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the ば-clause is an **action verb with the same subject**
as the main clause, you cannot end the sentence with a request, a command, an
invitation or a statement of intention. Switch to `たら`. The restriction lifts
when the ば-clause is a **state**: an adjective, `ある`／`いる`, a potential form,
or a negative.

</div>

That single rule explains why 分(わ)からなければ聞(き)いてください is
perfectly idiomatic while ❌東京(とうきょう)に着(つ)けば連絡(れんらく)してください
is not.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>急(いそ)げば</b>、間(ま)に合(あ)いますよ。
If we hurry, we'll make it.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)が<b>よければ</b>、山(やま)が見(み)えます。
If the weather's good, you can see the mountains.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)が<b>あれば</b>、手伝(てつだ)ってください。
If you have time, please give me a hand.
</pre>

<pre>
もっと<b>練習(れんしゅう)すれば</b>、うまくなりますよ。
You'll get better if you practise more.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を<b>読(よ)まなければ</b>、使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)かりません。
You won't know how to use it unless you read the manual.
</pre>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し早(はや)く家(いえ)を<b>出(で)れば</b>よかった。
I should have left home a bit earlier.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)らなければ</b>、川(かわ)でバーベキューをする予定(よてい)です。
If it doesn't rain tomorrow, we're planning to have a barbecue by the river.
</pre>

<pre>
この地図(ちず)を<b>見(み)れば</b>、どこに駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)があるかすぐ分(わ)かります。
One look at this map and you'll see straight away where the car parks are.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ば` itself is neither polite nor casual, but it leans **written and considered**.
Notices, manuals, instructions, mathematical and legal prose, and any sentence
that is doing a piece of reasoning all reach for it. In relaxed conversation
`たら` is more frequent - except in the fixed patterns below, which are everywhere
in speech.

Patterns built on `ば` that you will meet constantly:

- **～ばいい** - "you just have to…", "it'd be good if…":
  この紙(かみ)に名前(なまえ)を書(か)けばいいですか。
- **～ばよかった** - regret about the road not taken:
  もっと早(はや)く言(い)えばよかった。 There is no `～とよかった`.
- **～ば～ほど** - "the more…, the more…":
  考(かんが)えれば考(かんが)えるほど分(わ)からなくなる。
- **～なければならない／なければいけない** - the written sibling of the N5
  `～なくてはいけない`. Same meaning, stiffer feel.

The casual contractions are the part that catches learners out in real
conversation:

- `なければ` → **なきゃ**: 帰(かえ)ら**なきゃ**。 - "I've got to go home."
  Very common, all ages, both genders.
- `なければならない` → **なきゃ** on its own, with the rest swallowed.
- `～れば` → **～りゃ**: 見(み)りゃ分(わ)かる。 - blunt, older, and
  male-sounding. Recognise it; don't produce it.

## Contrast

### ～ば vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` reports an **actual, invariable** link. `ば` floats a
**premise** and reasons from it, which is why only `ば` can end in a wish, an
offer or a request.

</div>

<pre>
春(はる)になる<b>と</b>、この池(いけ)の氷(こおり)が溶(と)けます。
Every spring the ice on this pond melts.
</pre>

<pre>
春(はる)に<b>なれば</b>、この池(いけ)の氷(こおり)も溶(と)けるでしょう。
Once spring comes, the ice on this pond should melt too.
</pre>

The `と` version is a fact about how the pond behaves. The `ば` version is the
speaker working forward from a supposition about *this* year - note that it needs
`でしょう`, because a prediction is a judgement, and `と` has no room for
judgements.

Two more `ば`-only territories: `もし` sits comfortably in front of it
(もし間違(まちが)っていれば直(なお)してください), and counterfactual regret
belongs to it alone (～ばよかった, ～ばよかったのに).

### ～ば vs ～たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - where `ば` is blocked, `たら` steps in: before requests and
commands after an action verb, and for anything that actually happened once.

</div>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)に<b>着(つ)いたら</b>、すぐ報告(ほうこく)してください。
Report in as soon as you get to the office.
</pre>

❌会社(かいしゃ)に着(つ)けば、すぐ報告(ほうこく)してください is
rejected: 着(つ)く is an action the listener performs, and the main clause is a
command aimed at the same person. But make the condition a state and `ば`
returns:

<pre>
分(わ)から<b>なければ</b>、いつでも聞(き)いてください。
If you don't understand, ask me any time.
</pre>

The other gap is the past. `たら` can narrate a one-off sequence that really
happened - 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がった - and `ば`
cannot. A `ば` sentence ending in the past tense is read as counterfactual
instead: 聞(き)けばよかった means "I should have asked", i.e. I didn't.

### ～ば vs ～なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ば` conditions on the event **happening**; `なら` conditions
on a supposition **being true**. So `ば`'s main clause follows in time, while
`なら`'s can come first.

</div>

<pre>
沖縄(おきなわ)に<b>行(い)けば</b>、12月(がつ)でも泳(およ)げますよ。
If you go to Okinawa, you can swim even in December.
</pre>

<pre>
沖縄(おきなわ)に行(い)く<b>なら</b>、飛行機(ひこうき)を早(はや)めに予約(よやく)したほうがいいですよ。
If you're going to Okinawa, you should book your flight early.
</pre>

In the first, the swimming happens once you are there. In the second, the booking
happens *before* you go - the trip is a premise the listener has just announced,
and the speaker responds to it. `ば` cannot reverse the order like that.

The second difference is mechanical: `なら` attaches straight to a bare noun and
picks it up as a topic - コーヒー**なら**台所(だいどころ)にありますよ - a job
no ば-form can do.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 買(か)う**ば** → ✅ 買(か)**えば**
  The ば-form ends in **-eba**. For a う-verb the final vowel changes:
  ka-**u** → ka-**e**ba.

- ❌ 高(たか)**いければ** → ✅ 高(たか)**ければ**
  The final `い` of an い-adjective is *replaced* by `ければ`, not kept in front
  of it.

- ❌ **いければ**／**いいば** → ✅ **よければ**
  `いい` borrows every conjugated form from `よい`, here as elsewhere.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)**ければ** → ✅ 学生(がくせい)**なら(ば)**／学生(がくせい)**であれば**
  `ければ` belongs to い-adjectives only. Nouns and な-adjectives take
  `なら(ば)`, or `であれば` in formal writing.

- ❌ 国(くに)に帰(かえ)**れば**、写真(しゃしん)を送(おく)ってください。 → ✅ 国(くに)に帰(かえ)っ**たら**、写真(しゃしん)を送(おく)ってください。
  Action verb plus a request aimed at the same person - the one configuration
  `ば` refuses.

## Practice

**1.** もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)べ____、健康(けんこう)になりますよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)べ<b>れば</b>、健康(けんこう)になりますよ。

食(た)べる is a る-verb, so the `る` is replaced by `れば`. The sentence is a
piece of reasoning - eat more vegetables and health follows - which is exactly
what `ば` is for.

</details>

**2.** 空港(くうこう)に（着(つ)けば・着(つ)いたら）、メールしてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

空港(くうこう)に<b>着(つ)いたら</b>、メールしてください。

The condition is an action the listener performs, and the main clause is a
request to that same listener. `ば` is blocked in exactly that configuration.
Had the condition been a state - 時間(じかん)があれば - `ば` would have been
fine.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If it's cheap, I'll buy two."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

安(やす)<b>ければ</b>、2つ買(か)います。

い-adjective: 安(やす)い → 安(やす)ければ. The main clause is the speaker's
own decision, not a request to anyone, and the condition is a state - so `ば` is
comfortable here even though the sentence expresses an intention.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この本(ほん)が高(たか)いければ、図書館(としょかん)で借(か)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この本(ほん)が高(たか)<b>ければ</b>、図書館(としょかん)で借(か)ります。

い-adjectives lose their final `い` before `ければ`. Keeping it produces a form
that does not exist. The same trap catches 安(やす)ければ, 寒(さむ)ければ,
忙(いそが)しければ.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)を開(あ)ければ、涼(すず)しくなりますよ。
(b) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けると、涼(すず)しくなります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a statement about how this room works: open the window and it cools down,
every time. There is no speaker in it - it could be printed on a sign.

(a) presents opening the window as a **solution** the listener might adopt. The
speaker is reasoning from a premise and offering the result, which is why the
`よ` fits so naturally on the end. In practice you would say (a) to someone who
has just complained about the heat, and (b) to someone asking how the room
behaves.

</details>

# ～たら

**Meaning** · if / when / after - the all-purpose conditional

`～たら` is the one you reach for when you are not sure which conditional you
want, and you will usually be right. It takes any kind of main clause, it handles
both "if" and "when", and it is the everyday spoken default.

## Formation

Take the **plain past** of anything at all and add `ら`. That is the entire rule,
which is a large part of why `たら` is so easy to use.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain past + ら | 飲(の)む → 飲(の)んだ → 飲(の)んだら |
| Verb (る-verb) | plain past + ら | 届(とど)ける → 届(とど)けた → 届(とど)けたら |
| Verb (negative) | なかった + ら | 飲(の)まない → 飲(の)まなかったら |
| い-adjective | かった + ら | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)かったら |
| い-adjective (negative) | くなかった + ら | 寒(さむ)くない → 寒(さむ)くなかったら |
| な-adjective | だった + ら | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)だったら |
| Noun | だった + ら | 雨(あめ) → 雨(あめ)だったら |
| Polite (formal) | ました + ら | 届(とど)きます → 届(とど)きましたら |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the past form in front of `ら` says nothing about **past
time**. It marks the condition as *completed at the moment the main clause
happens*. The tense of the sentence is carried entirely by the final verb.

</div>

`たら` does three jobs, and it is worth naming them separately:

1. **If** - a genuine condition: 雨(あめ)だったら中止(ちゅうし)です。
2. **When / after** - for something certain to happen, where English would not
   say "if": 20歳(さい)になったら…
3. **Discovery** - with a **past** main clause, no conditional meaning survives at
   all. See the last two examples.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>寒(さむ)かったら</b>、エアコンを消(け)してください。
If you're cold, turn the air conditioning off.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)<b>晴(は)れたら</b>、海(うみ)に行(い)こう。
If it's clear tomorrow, let's go to the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)が<b>終(お)わったら</b>、飲(の)みに行(い)きませんか。
Shall we go for a drink after work?
</pre>

<pre>
20歳(さい)に<b>なったら</b>、一人(ひとり)で暮(く)らしたいです。
When I turn twenty, I want to live on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)んだら</b>、熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がりました。
I took the medicine and my fever went down.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けたら</b>、小(ちい)さな鳥(とり)が入(はい)ってきた。
I opened the window and a little bird flew in.
</pre>

<pre>
もし財布(さいふ)を<b>落(お)としたら</b>、すぐ交番(こうばん)に届(とど)けてください。
If you drop your wallet, take it straight to a police box.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)が<b>できたら</b>、部長(ぶちょう)に見(み)せてから、みんなに送(おく)ってください。
Once next week's meeting materials are ready, show them to the manager and then send them round.
</pre>

<pre>
もし道(みち)に<b>迷(まよ)ったら</b>、遠慮(えんりょ)しないで近(ちか)くの人(ひと)に聞(き)いてみてください。
If you get lost, don't hesitate - just ask someone nearby.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たら` is the spoken workhorse. In ordinary conversation it outnumbers `と`, `ば`
and `なら` put together; in formal writing it thins out and
<code>場合(ばあい)</code> and `ば` take over. It is not casual in itself - the
politeness sits on the final verb, and `ましたら` exists for genuinely formal
contexts: 荷物(にもつ)が届(とど)きましたら、ご連絡(れんらく)します。

`もし` pairs with `たら` more naturally than with anything else. `もし` is
optional and only reinforces the "this may not happen" reading; it never replaces
the `たら`.

Two constructions built on `たら` that you meet immediately:

- **～たらいい／～たらいいですか** - "it'd be good if…", "what should I…".
- **～たらどうですか／～たら?** - a suggestion aimed at the listener. Both have
  their own pages; note only that the suggestion reading comes from `どう`, not
  from `たら`.

The **discovery** use deserves its own paragraph, because it is the one that
makes learners think they have misread the sentence. With a past-tense main
clause, `たら` means "I did X, and then - as it turned out - Y". There is no
condition anywhere in it. The Y part has to be something outside the speaker's
control: a discovery, a surprise, an event that simply occurred. A *planned* next
action is not allowed there, and needs `～てから` instead.

## Contrast

### ～たら vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` needs the link to be automatic and repeatable, and bans
any main clause expressing the speaker's will. `たら` accepts single occasions
and every kind of ending.

</div>

<pre>
この窓(まど)を開(あ)ける<b>と</b>、海(うみ)が見(み)えます。
Open this window and you can see the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たら</b>、海(うみ)が見(み)えました。
I opened the window, and there was the sea.
</pre>

The first is a permanent property of the window; the second is one morning. And
where `と` would refuse outright - 
❌窓(まど)を開(あ)けると、写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)りましょう - `たら`
is fine: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けたら、写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)りましょう。

Note the shapes too: `と` takes the plain **non-past**, `たら` the plain
**past**. If you find yourself writing a past form in front of `と`, you have
picked the wrong one.

### ～たら vs ～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after an **action verb**, only `たら` can be followed by a
request or a command. And only `たら` narrates something that actually happened
once.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんが<b>来(き)たら</b>、教(おし)えてください。
Let me know when Tanaka gets here.
</pre>

❌田中(たなか)さんが来(く)れば、教(おし)えてください is wrong. Both
work, though, once the condition is a state rather than an action:
分(わ)からなかったら／分(わ)からなければ、聞(き)いてください are equally
good.

The second gap is the past. A `たら` clause with a past main clause reports a real
sequence; the `ば` equivalent flips into counterfactual regret
(聞(き)けばよかった = "I should have asked, and didn't"). If you want to say
what happened, `ば` is not available.

### ～たら vs ～なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a `たら` clause always happens **before** its main clause. A
`なら` clause need not: `なら` takes up a supposition, and the main clause can be
carried out first.

</div>

The clearest illustration is a road-safety slogan you see on posters all over
Japan, which uses both in one breath:

<pre>
<b>飲(の)んだら</b>乗(の)るな、乗(の)る<b>なら</b>飲(の)むな。
If you've drunk, don't drive; if you're going to drive, don't drink.
</pre>

<code>飲(の)んだら</code> = the drinking is finished, and the driving would come after it.
<code>乗(の)るなら</code> = driving is a plan you are entertaining, and the instruction
applies *beforehand*. Swap the two and the slogan collapses.

The same split in a plain sentence:

<pre>
日本(にほん)に行(い)っ<b>たら</b>、温泉(おんせん)に入(はい)りたい。
When I get to Japan, I want to go to a hot spring.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に行(い)く<b>なら</b>、春(はる)がいちばんいいですよ。
If you're going to Japan, spring is the best time.
</pre>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く**たら** → ✅ 行(い)っ**たら**
  `たら` is built on the **past** form, never the dictionary form.
  行(い)く → 行(い)った → 行(い)ったら.

- ❌ 高(たか)い**たら** → ✅ 高(たか)**かったら**
  い-adjectives go through their past form first: 高(たか)い →
  高(たか)かった → 高(たか)かったら.

- ❌ 日本(にほん)に行(い)ったら、ビザを取(と)ります。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)に行(い)く**なら**、ビザを取(と)ります。
  A `たら` clause must come first in time, and you get the visa before you fly.
  For a plan you are reacting to, `なら`.

- ❌ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けたら、部屋(へや)を掃除(そうじ)しました。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)け**てから**、部屋(へや)を掃除(そうじ)しました。
  With a past main clause, `たら` means the speaker *discovered* something. A
  deliberate next action needs `～てから`.

- ❌ このボタンを押(お)したら、お湯(ゆ)が出(で)ます。 → ✅ このボタンを押(お)す**と**、お湯(ゆ)が出(で)ます。
  Not ungrammatical, but wrong for a manual: `たら` frames it as one occasion,
  while a machine's fixed behaviour calls for `と`.

## Practice

**1.** 安(やす)____、もう一(ひと)つ買(か)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

安(やす)<b>かったら</b>、もう一(ひと)つ買(か)います。

An い-adjective forms `たら` through its past: 安(やす)い →
安(やす)かった → 安(やす)かったら. ❌安(やす)いたら does not exist.

</details>

**2.** 大学(だいがく)を（出(で)ると・出(で)たら）、国(くに)に帰(かえ)るつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大学(だいがく)を<b>出(で)たら</b>、国(くに)に帰(かえ)るつもりです。

`つもり` states the speaker's intention, and `と` cannot host anything that
expresses the speaker's will. `たら` has no such limit - and here it carries the
"when", not "if": graduating is going to happen.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When the rain stops, let's go for a walk."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

雨(あめ)が<b>やんだら</b>、散歩(さんぽ)に行(い)きましょう。

`～ましょう` is a proposal, so `と` is out. The event is expected rather than
doubtful, which is why English says "when" and Japanese still uses `たら` - the
same form covers both.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)京都(きょうと)に行(い)ったら、ホテルを予約(よやく)しておきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)京都(きょうと)に行(い)く<b>なら</b>、ホテルを予約(よやく)しておきます。

You book the hotel *before* leaving, and a `たら` clause is always the earlier
event. `なら` is the one conditional that lets the main clause come first in
time.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)に電話(でんわ)したら、留守(るす)でした。
(b) 彼(かれ)に電話(でんわ)したら、教(おし)えてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) has a **past** main clause, so there is no condition in it at all: "I phoned
him, and he was out." The `たら` clause just sets the scene, and what follows is
something the speaker found out.

(b) has a **request** as the main clause, so it is a genuine conditional: "if
(or when) you phone him, let me know." Nothing has happened yet.

The `たら` is identical in both. The tense of the main clause decides whether you
are reading a report or a condition - and this is the single most useful thing to
know about `たら`.

</details>

# ～なら

**Meaning** · if that's the case - taking up something just said, and replying to it

`～なら` is the conditional that **answers**. It picks up a premise - nearly always
one the other person has just handed you - and builds the speaker's judgement on
top of it: advice, an offer, an opinion, a decision. Nothing about `なら` reports
what will happen; it reports what the speaker thinks about it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + なら (**no** だ) | 明日(あした) → 明日(あした)なら |
| な-adjective | stem + なら (**no** だ) | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かなら |
| い-adjective | plain form + なら | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいなら |
| Verb | plain non-past + なら | 行(い)く → 行(い)くなら |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + なら | 要(い)らない → 要(い)らないなら |
| Verb (past) | plain past + なら | 終(お)わった → 終(お)わったなら |
| With の | plain form + のなら／んなら | 行(い)く → 行(い)くのなら／行(い)くんなら |
| Formal | + であれば／でしたら | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)であれば |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だ` never appears in front of `なら`.
❌静(しず)かだなら, ❌雨(あめ)だなら. Compare `と`, which **requires** it
(雨(あめ)だと). Getting these two the wrong way round is the commonest form
error at N4.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `だったら`, `でしたら` and `それなら` are the everyday cousins of this
pattern. `だったら` is the casual "in that case"; `でしたら` is the polite version
you hear across a counter: 明日(あした)でしたら、お席(せき)がございます。

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ビール<b>なら</b>冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)にありますよ。
Beer? There's some in the fridge.
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)は無理(むり)だけど、日曜日(にちようび)<b>なら</b>空(あ)いているよ。
Saturday's no good, but Sunday I'm free.
</pre>

<pre>
頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い<b>なら</b>、早(はや)く帰(かえ)ったほうがいいですよ。
If your head hurts, you should go home early.
</pre>

<pre>
お寿司(すし)<b>なら</b>、駅前(えきまえ)の店(みせ)がおいしいですよ。
For sushi, the place in front of the station is good.
</pre>

<pre>
君(きみ)が行(い)かない<b>なら</b>、私(わたし)も行(い)かない。
If you're not going, I'm not going either.
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)が終(お)わった<b>なら</b>、遊(あそ)びに行(い)ってもいいよ。
If you've finished your homework, you can go out and play.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)に登(のぼ)る<b>なら</b>、いい靴(くつ)を買(か)っておいたほうがいいですよ。
If you're going to climb a mountain, you'd better buy proper boots first.
</pre>

<pre>
中古(ちゅうこ)の車(くるま)を買(か)う<b>なら</b>、一度(いちど)専門(せんもん)の人(ひと)に見(み)てもらったほうが安心(あんしん)です。
If you're buying a used car, it's safer to have a specialist look at it first.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`なら` is conversational glue, and its natural habitat is the second turn of a
dialogue. Someone says something; you take their words and hand back a judgement.
That is why so many `なら` sentences begin by repeating a noun the other person
just used - ビール**なら**, 京都(きょうと)**なら**, その本(ほん)**なら**.
In that use it behaves almost like a topic marker, and it is the only conditional
that can attach to a bare noun this way.

The main clause is always the speaker: `～たほうがいい`, `～てあげる`,
`～てください`, `～つもりだ`, an opinion, an offer. It is never a natural
consequence - that is `と`'s territory.

`のなら`（casual `んなら`）adds a shade of "given that you're telling me this",
which can read as sympathetic or as slightly pointed depending on the tone:
そんなに嫌(きら)いなのなら、食(た)べなくていいよ。 Used that way, `なら`
carries a real edge - you are throwing the listener's own premise back at them.

In writing, `なら` is replaced by `であれば` or by
<code>場合(ばあい)は</code>; the stiff `ならば` survives in formal prose
(必要(ひつよう)ならば再度(さいど)ご連絡(れんらく)ください). In speech,
`ならば` sounds bookish.

## Contrast

### ～なら vs ～たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `たら`, the main clause comes **after** the condition in
time. With `なら`, it can come **before**. And `たら` reports events, while `なら`
responds to premises.

</div>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>なら</b>、カメラを貸(か)してあげるよ。
If you're going on a trip, I'll lend you my camera.
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)に行(い)っ<b>たら</b>、写真(しゃしん)をたくさん撮(と)りたい。
When I go on the trip, I want to take lots of photos.
</pre>

The camera is lent **before** the trip - the trip is only a plan the listener has
mentioned. The photos are taken **during** it. `たら` can never do what the first
sentence does; that inversion is `なら`'s alone.

The second half of the difference is where the condition comes from. `たら`
supposes something itself; `なら` accepts something already on the table. So
`なら` is at home in replies, and slightly odd as the opening line of a
conversation.

### ～なら vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` is a mechanism with no speaker in it. `なら` is nothing
*but* the speaker, reacting to a premise.

</div>

<pre>
このボタンを押(お)す<b>と</b>、お湯(ゆ)が出(で)ます。
Press this button and hot water comes out.
</pre>

❌このボタンを押(お)すなら、お湯(ゆ)が出(で)ます is wrong: the water
coming out is a fact about the machine, not the speaker's opinion about a
supposition. Conversely:

<pre>
このボタンを押(お)す<b>なら</b>、先(さき)に説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)んでください。
If you're going to press this button, read the manual first.
</pre>

Now there is a speaker with an instruction, and the instruction applies *before*
the button is pressed - both hallmarks of `なら`, both impossible for `と`.

`なら` also cannot express a certain future "when":
❌春(はる)になるなら, ❌夜(よる)になるなら. Something guaranteed to occur
is not a premise anyone needs to entertain; use `と` or `たら`.

### ～なら vs ～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なら(ば)` is literally the ば-form of the copula, which is
why they blur. Separate them by what the condition is *about*: `ば` conditions on
an event **occurring**, `なら` on a supposition **being true**.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に<b>聞(き)けば</b>、すぐ分(わ)かりますよ。
Ask the manager and you'll know right away.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に聞(き)く<b>なら</b>、午前中(ごぜんちゅう)がいいですよ。
If you're going to ask the manager, the morning is a good time.
</pre>

In the first, the asking produces the result. In the second, the asking is a plan
the listener has announced, and the advice is about *how* to carry it out. Note
again that the advice applies before the asking happens.

The other split is mechanical. `ば` needs a predicate to attach to; `なら` fastens
straight onto a bare noun and lifts it out as a topic - 
その辞書(じしょ)**なら**机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にあります - and no ば-form
can do that.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 静(しず)か**だなら** → ✅ 静(しず)か**なら**
  `だ` disappears before `なら`. The same for nouns: ❌雨(あめ)だなら → ✅
  雨(あめ)なら／雨(あめ)だったら.

- ❌ 家(いえ)に帰(かえ)る**なら**、シャワーを浴(あ)びます。 → ✅ 家(いえ)に帰(かえ)っ**たら**、シャワーを浴(あ)びます。
  A plain sequence of your own actions is not a premise you are responding to,
  and the shower comes after you get home. That is a `たら` sentence.

- ❌ 春(はる)になる**なら**、暖(あたた)かくなります。 → ✅ 春(はる)になる**と**、暖(あたた)かくなります。
  Spring is not a supposition, and warming up is not the speaker's judgement.
  Natural consequences belong to `と`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る**なら**、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。 → ✅ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ**たら**／降(ふ)れ**ば**…
  If *you* are the one supposing the rain, use `たら` or `ば`. `降るなら` becomes
  correct the moment somebody else says it is going to rain and you reply to
  them.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)**の**なら割引(わりびき)があります。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)なら割引(わりびき)があります。
  A noun takes `なら` directly. The `の` belongs with
  <code>場合(ばあい)</code>, not with `なら`.

## Practice

**1.** 「傘(かさ)を忘(わす)れました。」「傘(かさ)____、私(わたし)のを貸(か)しますよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「傘(かさ)<b>なら</b>、私(わたし)のを貸(か)しますよ。」

The second speaker picks up the exact noun the first one used and replies to it
with an offer. That is `なら` at its most typical - attached to a bare noun, no
`だ`, and a main clause that is purely the speaker's own decision.

</details>

**2.** 温泉(おんせん)に（行(い)くなら・行(い)ったら）、平日(へいじつ)のほうがすいていますよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

温泉(おんせん)に<b>行(い)くなら</b>、平日(へいじつ)のほうがすいていますよ。

The advice is about *which day to choose*, so it has to reach the listener before
the trip. `たら` would put the advice after arrival, which makes no sense - you
cannot pick a weekday once you are already there.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If you're not going to use it, could you lend it to me?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

使(つか)わない<b>なら</b>、貸(か)してくれませんか。

You are responding to something the other person has implied or said, and the
main clause is a request - both squarely `なら`. `たら` would be understood, but
it would frame not-using as an event that happens first, which is not the point.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 大学(だいがく)を卒業(そつぎょう)するなら、国(くに)に帰(かえ)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 大学(だいがく)を卒業(そつぎょう)し<b>たら</b>、国(くに)に帰(かえ)ります。

Graduating is a scheduled certainty and the return happens afterwards, so this is
"when", not "if it's the case that" - `たら`. `卒業するなら` would only work as a
reply to someone else raising the possibility, with advice attached:
卒業(そつぎょう)するなら、早(はや)めに仕事(しごと)を探(さが)したほうがいい。

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 引(ひ)っ越(こ)すなら、手伝(てつだ)うよ。
(b) 引(ひ)っ越(こ)したら、新(あたら)しい住所(じゅうしょ)を教(おし)えてね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) responds to news: you have just said you are moving, and the speaker offers
help - help that will be given **during** the move, not after it. That
before-or-during timing is only possible with `なら`.

(b) waits for the move to be over. `たら` locks the main clause into second
place, so the new address can only be requested once there is one.

Both sentences are about the same move. `なら` reacts to the plan; `たら` waits
for the event.

</details>

# 場合は

**Meaning** · in the event that X - one case picked out, with the rule that applies to it

<code>場合(ばあい)</code> (read ばあい) is an ordinary noun meaning "case,
occasion". Used as a conditional it sets out one possible situation among several
and states what applies in it. It is the language of notices, manuals and rules,
not of conversation.

## Formation

Everything in front of <code>場合(ばあい)</code> is a noun-modifying clause - 
exactly the N5 pattern - because <code>場合(ばあい)</code> is just a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + 場合(ばあい)は | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れる場合(ばあい)は |
| Verb (past) | た-form + 場合(ばあい)は | 忘(わす)れた → 忘(わす)れた場合(ばあい)は |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 場合(ばあい)は | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かない場合(ばあい)は |
| い-adjective | plain form + 場合(ばあい)は | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しい場合(ばあい)は |
| な-adjective | stem + **な** + 場合(ばあい)は | 必要(ひつよう) → 必要(ひつよう)な場合(ばあい)は |
| Noun | Noun + **の** + 場合(ばあい)は | 雨(あめ) → 雨(あめ)の場合(ばあい)は |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can put it in front of a noun, you can put it in front
of <code>場合(ばあい)</code>, using exactly the same shape. That is why
な-adjectives take `な` and nouns take `の` here, while `と` demands `だ` and
`なら` demands nothing at all.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `は` is the contrast particle doing its usual job: "in *this*
case (as opposed to the others)". Drop it - 
雨(あめ)の場合(ばあい)、中止(ちゅうし)します - and the sentence is
flatter and slightly more written. `には` makes the contrast heavier still. On
forms and in headings you often see the bare noun phrase alone:
「団体(だんたい)の場合(ばあい)」.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)の<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、体育館(たいいくかん)で行(おこな)います。
In the event of rain, it will be held in the gym.
</pre>

<pre>
遅(おく)れる<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、必(かなら)ず連絡(れんらく)してください。
If you are going to be late, be sure to get in touch.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がない<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、メールでも大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です。
If you don't have time, email is fine too.
</pre>

<pre>
パスワードを忘(わす)れた<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、こちらのボタンを押(お)してください。
If you have forgotten your password, press this button.
</pre>

<pre>
火事(かじ)の<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、この階段(かいだん)をお使(つか)いください。
In case of fire, please use these stairs.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)が必要(ひつよう)な<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、いつでもお呼(よ)びください。
If you need an explanation, call me any time.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)が1週間(しゅうかん)以内(いない)に届(とど)かない<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、こちらの番号(ばんごう)までお電話(でんわ)ください。
If your parcel has not arrived within a week, please call this number.
</pre>

<pre>
キャンセルは前日(ぜんじつ)まで無料(むりょう)ですが、当日(とうじつ)の<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>料金(りょうきん)の半分(はんぶん)をいただきます。
Cancellation is free up to the day before; on the day itself we charge half the fee.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the **formal, written** conditional. Signs at stations, safety notices,
insurance documents, school regulations, software error messages, business email
 - that is where <code>場合(ばあい)は</code> lives. It has a bureaucratic
steadiness to it: here is a contingency, here is the procedure.

Because <code>場合(ばあい)</code> is a noun, it behaves like one. It takes
demonstratives (その場合(ばあい)は - "in that case"), it appears in fixed
expressions (場合(ばあい)によって - "depending on the case";
場合(ばあい)によっては - "in some cases"), and it can head a column on a form.
None of the other conditionals can do any of that.

The flip side is that using it with a friend sounds like reading aloud from a
manual. ❌お腹(なか)がすいた場合(ばあい)は、何(なに)か作(つく)るよ
is grammatical and comic; say
お腹(なか)がすいたら、何(なに)か作(つく)るよ. A rough guide: if the
sentence could plausibly be printed and laminated, use
<code>場合(ばあい)は</code>; otherwise use `たら`.

One more consequence of its formality: the main clause is almost always an
instruction, a rule, or a consequence stated impersonally - 
`～てください`, `お～ください`, `～ことになっています`, `～ます`. You will rarely
see it with a casual ending.

## Contrast

### 場合(ばあい)は vs ときは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とき` locates an event **in time** and takes for granted
that it happens. <code>場合(ばあい)</code> presents it as **one possibility
among others**, and usually leads to an instruction.

</div>

This is the pair that matters, because both translate as "when".

<pre>
子(こ)どもの<b>ときは</b>、よく川(かわ)で泳(およ)ぎました。
When I was a child, I often swam in the river.
</pre>

❌子(こ)どもの場合(ばあい)は、よく川(かわ)で泳(およ)ぎました is not a
sentence about the speaker's childhood at all - it says "in the case of
children…", i.e. as opposed to adults. Childhood is a period of time, not a
contingency.

<pre>
地震(じしん)の<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、エレベーターを使(つか)わないでください。
In the event of an earthquake, do not use the lift.
</pre>

Here <code>場合(ばあい)</code> is right precisely because the earthquake may
never come, and because the sentence sits in a list beside
火事(かじ)の場合(ばあい)は… and 停電(ていでん)の場合(ばあい)は….

### 場合(ばあい)は vs ～たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different room. `たら` is what you say;
<code>場合(ばあい)は</code> is what the sign says.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらない<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、もう一度(いちど)ご来院(らいいん)ください。
If your temperature does not come down, please come in again.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらな<b>かったら</b>、もう一度(いちど)来(き)てくださいね。
If your fever doesn't go down, come back in again, all right?
</pre>

Identical content; the first is the printed instruction slip, the second is the
doctor speaking. Note also that <code>場合(ばあい)</code> being a noun lets a
notice enumerate cases in parallel, each on its own line - a structure `たら`
handles clumsily.

### 場合(ばあい)は vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in a manual the two divide the labour: `と` says what the
machine normally **does**; <code>場合(ばあい)は</code> introduces the
exception and tells you what **you** should do.

</div>

<pre>
ボタンを押(お)す<b>と</b>、ランプが光(ひか)ります。光(ひか)らない<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、電池(でんち)を確(たし)かめてください。
Press the button and the lamp lights up. If it does not light up, check the batteries.
</pre>

The first half is an automatic consequence with no speaker in it, which is `と`'s
whole job - and `と` cannot be followed by an instruction. The second half needs
one, so it switches to <code>場合(ばあい)は</code>. Almost every instruction
manual in Japanese alternates between exactly these two.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 子(こ)どもの場合(ばあい)、よく山(やま)に登(のぼ)りました。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもの**とき**、よく山(やま)に登(のぼ)りました。
  A stretch of past time is `とき`. <code>場合(ばあい)</code> would mean "as
  for children (as opposed to adults)".

- ❌ 必要(ひつよう)**だ**場合(ばあい) → ✅ 必要(ひつよう)**な**場合(ばあい)
  <code>場合(ばあい)</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective in front of it takes
  `な` - the ordinary noun-modifying rule from N5.

- ❌ 病気(びょうき)場合(ばあい)は → ✅ 病気(びょうき)**の**場合(ばあい)は
  A noun needs `の` to modify another noun. Compare `と`, which wants `だ`, and
  `なら`, which wants nothing.

- ❌ 友(とも)だちと会(あ)う場合(ばあい)は、いつも駅(えき)で待(ま)ちます。 → ✅ 友(とも)だちと会(あ)う**ときは**、いつも駅(えき)で待(ま)ちます。
  A regular habit is not a contingency. <code>場合(ばあい)</code> needs
  something that might or might not arise.

## Practice

**1.** 都合(つごう)が悪(わる)い____は、前(まえ)の日(ひ)までにお知(し)らせください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

都合(つごう)が悪(わる)い<b>場合(ばあい)</b>は、前(まえ)の日(ひ)までにお知(し)らせください。

An い-adjective attaches in its plain form, with nothing in between. The polite
`お～ください` ending confirms the register: this is a written notice, which is
where <code>場合(ばあい)は</code> belongs.

</details>

**2.** 学生(がくせい)の（場合(ばあい)は・ときは）、料金(りょうきん)が半額(はんがく)になります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

学生(がくせい)の<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、料金(りょうきん)が半額(はんがく)になります。

The sentence sorts customers into categories - students as against everybody else
 - which is what <code>場合(ばあい)</code> does.
学生(がくせい)のときは would mean "back when I was a student", a period of
the speaker's life.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If the machine does not start, please contact the office."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

機械(きかい)が動(うご)かない<b>場合(ばあい)は</b>、事務所(じむしょ)に連絡(れんらく)してください。

A negative verb attaches in its plain ない-form. This is a printed-notice
sentence, so <code>場合(ばあい)は</code> is the natural choice;
動(うご)かなかったら would be the spoken version.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 大学生(だいがくせい)だ場合(ばあい)は、この申(もう)し込(こ)み書(しょ)を使(つか)ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 大学生(だいがくせい)<b>の</b>場合(ばあい)は、この申(もう)し込(こ)み書(しょ)を使(つか)ってください。

A noun modifying another noun takes `の`, never `だ`. The `だ` version is
interference from `～だと`, where `だ` really is required.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 地震(じしん)のときは、エレベーターを使(つか)わないでください。
(b) 地震(じしん)の場合(ばあい)は、エレベーターを使(つか)わないでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are correct and you will see both on the same building's signage. (a) frames
the earthquake as a moment in time - *while* it is happening, do not use the
lift. (b) frames it as one entry in a list of emergencies, alongside fire and
power cuts, and reads more like a clause in a set of regulations.

(b) is the more bureaucratic of the two, and the one that invites a parallel
sentence beginning 火事(かじ)の場合(ばあい)は.

</details>

# ～たらどうですか

**Meaning** · why don't you…? - putting an action to the listener as a suggestion

Literally "if you did X, how would that be?" - the `たら` is the ordinary
conditional and `どうですか` is the N5 "how about it?". Put together they make a
suggestion aimed squarely at the other person, and that aim is what makes the
pattern touchier than it looks.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + らどうですか | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだらどうですか |
| Verb (negative) | なかった + らどうですか | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かなかったらどうですか |
| Casual | た-form + ら? | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだら? |
| Softer | た-form + らどうでしょうか | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだらどうでしょうか |
| Politer | た-form + らいかがですか | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだらいかがですか |
| Politest (upward) | て-form + はいかがでしょうか | 休(やす)む → お休(やす)みになってはいかがでしょうか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the suggestion lives in `どう`, not in `たら`. Change `どう`
and you change the sentence completely:
`～たらどうですか` = "why don't you…?", but
`～たらどうしますか` = "what will you do if…?" - a real question about a real
condition.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - inserting `～てみる` softens the whole thing, because it proposes a
*trial* rather than a course of action:
聞(き)いてみたらどうですか lands much more gently than
聞(き)いたらどうですか. This is the single easiest way to take the edge off.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し<b>待(ま)ったらどうですか</b>。
Why don't you wait a bit longer?
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)に<b>聞(き)いてみたらどう</b>?
Why not try asking the teacher?
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるなら、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く<b>帰(かえ)ったらどうですか</b>。
If you've got a fever, why don't you go home early today?
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がないなら、タクシーで<b>行(い)ったらどうでしょうか</b>。
If you're short of time, how about taking a taxi?
</pre>

<pre>
そんなに心配(しんぱい)なら、自分(じぶん)で<b>確(たし)かめたらどう</b>?
If you're that worried, why don't you go and check it yourself?
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの案(あん)も一度(いちど)ご覧(らん)に<b>なってはいかがでしょうか</b>。
Might I suggest you also take a look at this proposal?
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)コンビニのお弁当(べんとう)ばかりじゃなくて、たまには自分(じぶん)で<b>作(つく)ってみたらどうですか</b>。
Instead of convenience-store bentos every single day, why not cook for yourself once in a while?
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で悩(なや)んでいないで、一度(いちど)ご両親(りょうしん)に<b>相談(そうだん)してみたらいかがですか</b>。
Rather than worrying about it alone, why not talk it over with your parents?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **not** a neutral pattern, and the polite `ですか` does not make it safe.
`～たらどうですか` carries an unstated "you are not doing the obvious thing",
which is fine from a friend and grating from anyone else. Repeated, it is
nagging; delivered flatly, it is criticism.

The register scale, roughly:

- **休(やす)んだら?** - casual, friendly with rising intonation. With
  *falling* intonation it is openly annoyed: 自分(じぶん)でやったら? = "then
  do it yourself."
- **休(やす)んだらどうですか** - polite in form; use downward or sideways, not
  upward.
- **休(やす)んだらどうでしょうか** - hedged, noticeably softer.
- **休(やす)んだらいかがですか** - polite vocabulary, safe with customers and
  acquaintances.
- **お休(やす)みになってはいかがでしょうか** - the form to use with a superior.
  Notice it abandons `たら` entirely for `～ては`.

It is a spoken pattern. In writing you would use
`～てはいかがでしょうか` or `～ことをおすすめします`.

Two traps worth keeping apart, both hinging on what follows `たら`:

- **～たらどうですか** = a suggestion.
- **～たらどうしますか** = "what will you do if…?" - 
  終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)したらどうしますか。
- **～たらどうなりますか** = "what happens if…?" - about consequences, not
  people.

## Contrast

### ～たらどうですか vs ～ほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～たらどうですか` **asks**; `～ほうがいい` **tells**.

</div>

`～ほうがいい` (N5) weighs one option against the alternative and comes down on
one side, often with an unspoken warning attached. `～たらどうですか` floats an
action and hands the decision back to the listener - formally, at least. It ends
in a question, and an answer is expected.

<pre>
病院(びょういん)に<b>行(い)ったらどうですか</b>。
Why don't you go to the doctor?
</pre>

<pre>
病院(びょういん)に<b>行(い)ったほうがいいですよ</b>。
You'd better go to the doctor.
</pre>

The second is stronger and implies a consequence if you don't. The first is
softer on the surface - but because it points at something the listener has
visibly failed to do, it can end up sounding more pointed. Neither is safe to aim
at a superior.

### ～たらどうですか vs ～ませんか／～ましょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ませんか` and `～ましょう` include the speaker.
`～たらどうですか` is for the listener **alone**.

</div>

<pre>
一緒(いっしょ)に昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を<b>食(た)べませんか</b>。
Shall we have lunch together?
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)くらい<b>食(た)べたらどうですか</b>。
Why don't you at least eat some lunch?
</pre>

The second explicitly excludes the speaker: you are telling someone else to eat.
Using `～たらどうですか` when you mean to come along is one of the more
embarrassing N4 errors, because it turns an invitation into a criticism of the
listener's habits.

### ～たらどうですか vs ～たらいいですよ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～たらいいですよ` **answers** a request for advice.
`～たらどうですか` **offers** advice nobody asked for.

</div>

<pre>
「どこで買(か)えますか。」「駅前(えきまえ)の店(みせ)で<b>買(か)ったらいいですよ</b>。」
"Where can I buy one?" "You could buy one at the shop by the station."
</pre>

`～たらいい` states the good option as information - no question, no pressure. It
is the natural reply to どうしたらいいですか. Swapping in
買(か)ったらどうですか would be odd there, because the listener has already
asked; suggesting it back at them implies they should have thought of it
themselves.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 休(やす)む**たらどうですか** → ✅ 休(やす)**んだら**どうですか
  `たら` is built on the past form: 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだ →
  休(やす)んだら.

- ❌ 社長(しゃちょう)、少(すこ)し休(やす)んだらどうですか。 → ✅ 社長(しゃちょう)、少(すこ)しお休(やす)みに**なってはいかがでしょうか**。
  Grammatical, and rude upward. Telling a superior what they ought to be doing
  needs the `～てはいかがでしょうか` frame and an honorific verb.

- ❌ 一緒(いっしょ)にコンサートに行(い)ったらどうですか。（meaning "shall we go together?"）→ ✅ 一緒(いっしょ)にコンサートに行(い)き**ませんか**。
  `～たらどうですか` never includes the speaker.

- ❌ Reading 台風(たいふう)が来(き)たらどうしますか as a suggestion → it
  means "what will you do if a typhoon comes?" `どうですか` suggests;
  `どうしますか` asks.

## Practice

**1.** 眠(ねむ)そうですね。コーヒーでも飲(の)____どうですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

コーヒーでも飲(の)<b>んだら</b>どうですか。

The pattern is built on the plain past: 飲(の)む → 飲(の)んだ →
飲(の)んだら. The `でも` softens it further by making the coffee an example
rather than a demand.

</details>

**2.** 明日(あした)一緒(いっしょ)にテニスを（しませんか・したらどうですか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)一緒(いっしょ)にテニスを<b>しませんか</b>。

一緒(いっしょ)に puts the speaker in the picture, and `～たらどうですか` is
strictly about what the *listener* should do. With `したらどうですか` the
sentence would mean "why don't you play tennis with someone tomorrow" - and would
sound faintly critical of how they spend their time.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Why don't you try asking the station staff?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)の人(ひと)に<b>聞(き)いてみたらどうですか</b>。

`～てみる` inside the pattern turns it into "why not give it a try", which is the
friendliest version available. Without it,
聞(き)いたらどうですか sounds more like "you should have asked already".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 先生(せんせい)、この本(ほん)を読(よ)んだらどうですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先生(せんせい)、この本(ほん)をお読(よ)みに<b>なってはいかがでしょうか</b>。

The grammar of the original is fine; the problem is direction. Suggesting an
action to a teacher, boss or customer needs `～てはいかがでしょうか` with an
honorific verb. A slightly lighter option is
読(よ)まれてはいかがでしょうか.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 医者(いしゃ)に行(い)ったらどうですか。
(b) 医者(いしゃ)に行(い)ったほうがいいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is shaped as a question and formally leaves the choice open - but it also
points out that the listener has not gone yet, so depending on tone it can come
across as impatient: "why don't you just go?"

(b) is a straight recommendation with the speaker's judgement behind it, and the
`よ` adds a note of concern. It is stronger, but paradoxically often kinder,
because it reads as looking out for the listener rather than commenting on their
inaction.

Neither is appropriate upward; to a superior you would say
一度(いちど)病院(びょういん)へ行(い)かれてはいかがでしょうか.

</details>

# どうしたらいいですか

**Meaning** · what should I do? - asking someone to tell you the right course of action

This is a frame, not a single word: a **question word** + a **conditional** +
`いい`. "If I did what, would it be good?" Swap the question word and the verb and
you can build the whole family - どこに出(だ)せばいいですか,
何時(なんじ)に来(き)たらいいですか - which is why it is worth learning as a
shape rather than as a phrase.

## Formation

The `たら` and `ば` versions are interchangeable in almost every sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (たら) | た-form + らいいですか | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)したらいいですか |
| Verb (ば) | ば-form + いいですか | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)せばいいですか |
| する (fixed frame) | どう + したら／すれば + いいですか | する → どうしたらいいですか |
| Other question words | 何(なに)／どこ／いつ／どう + …たらいいですか | どこ → どこで買(か)えばいいですか |
| Softened | …らいい**でしょうか** | どうしたらいいでしょうか |
| Embedded (no ですか) | …らいい**か** + 分(わ)からない | どうしたらいいか分(わ)かりません |
| Formal / to a superior | どのように…らよろしいでしょうか | どのようにすればよろしいでしょうか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - when the question is embedded inside a larger sentence, the
`ですか` disappears and a bare `か` holds the question together:
どうしたらいい**か**教(おし)えてください. Never
❌どうしたらいいですかを教(おし)えてください.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `いい` here is an ordinary い-adjective, so it conjugates:
どうしたらよかったんですか ("what should I have done?"),
どうしたらいいのか分(わ)かりません, どうしたらよろしいでしょうか (formal,
`いい` → `よろしい`).

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
切符(きっぷ)をなくしました。<b>どうしたらいいですか</b>。
I've lost my ticket. What should I do?
</pre>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)はどこに<b>出(だ)したらいいですか</b>。
Where should I hand in these documents?
</pre>

<pre>
靴(くつ)はここで<b>脱(ぬ)げばいいですか</b>。
Should I take my shoes off here?
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)がなかなか覚(おぼ)えられません。<b>どうすればいいですか</b>。
I just can't seem to memorise kanji. What should I do?
</pre>

<pre>
次(つぎ)は<b>どうしたらいいか</b>、教(おし)えてください。
Please tell me what I should do next.
</pre>

<pre>
パソコンが急(きゅう)に動(うご)かなくなったんですが、<b>どうしたらいいでしょうか</b>。
My computer's suddenly stopped working - what would you suggest I do?
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めての出張(しゅっちょう)で、何(なに)を<b>準備(じゅんび)したらいいか</b>全(まった)く分(わ)かりません。
It's my first business trip and I have no idea what I should be getting ready.
</pre>

<pre>
保証書(ほしょうしょ)をなくしてしまった場合(ばあい)は、<b>どうしたらよろしいでしょうか</b>。
In the event that the warranty card has been lost, how should we proceed?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`どうしたらいいですか` is the standard polite way to ask for guidance, and it is
safe with strangers, shop staff, teachers and colleagues. It is neither stiff nor
casual on its own; the register is set by the ending you attach.

- **どうしよう** - muttered to yourself. "Oh no, what do I do."
- **どうしたらいい?／どうすればいいの?** - casual, to a friend.
- **どうしたらいいですか** - the neutral polite default.
- **どうしたらいいでしょうか** - softer, and slightly more deferential; it
  presents the question as something you are wondering rather than demanding an
  answer to.
- **どうしたらよろしいでしょうか／どのようにすればよろしいでしょうか** - business
  and customer-facing.

`たら` versus `ば` in this frame: they are close to interchangeable, and no native
speaker will correct you either way. `どうすればいいですか` leans a touch more
procedural and written - it fits "what is the correct procedure" - while
`どうしたらいいですか` is marginally more common in speech and a little softer.
With other question words the same holds:
どこに置(お)いたらいいですか and どこに置(お)けばいいですか are both fine.

One keigo distinction worth knowing early: if you are offering to act *for*
someone - a shop assistant, say - the humble
`いかがいたしましょうか` ("how would you like me to…?") replaces this pattern
entirely. `どうしたらいいですか` asks for instructions; `いかがいたしましょうか`
offers service.

## Contrast

### どうしたらいいですか vs どうしますか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どうしたらいいですか` asks for **advice**;
`どうしますか` asks about **intention**.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきました。<b>どうしますか</b>。
It's started raining. What do you want to do?
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきました。<b>どうしたらいいですか</b>。
It's started raining. What should we do?
</pre>

The first hands the decision to the listener and expects them to state their own
plan; a waiter or a travel agent would use it of you. The second admits you do
not know the right answer and asks to be told. Asking a superior
`どうしますか` when you meant "what should I do?" is a small but real workplace
mistake - it sounds like you are asking about *their* plans.

### どうしたらいいですか vs どうしたんですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these look alike and share nothing.
`どうしたんですか` = "what happened? / what's wrong?"

</div>

<pre>
顔色(かおいろ)が悪(わる)いですね。<b>どうしたんですか</b>。
You look pale. What's the matter?
</pre>

`どうしたんですか` is `どうした` (past of する) plus the N5 explanatory
`んです` - it looks backwards at a situation. `どうしたらいいですか` looks
forwards at an action. The same trap catches `どうしましたか`, which a doctor
uses to mean "what seems to be the problem?" - never "what should I do?"

### どうしたらいいですか vs ～たほうがいいですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どうしたらいいですか` is an **open** question.
`～たほうがいいですか` names one action and asks yes or no.

</div>

<pre>
この紙(かみ)は捨(す)て<b>たほうがいいですか</b>。
Should I throw this paper away?
</pre>

<pre>
この紙(かみ)は<b>どうしたらいいですか</b>。
What should I do with this paper?
</pre>

Use the first when you already have a candidate action in mind and want it
confirmed; use the second when you have nothing. The answer you get back will
often be `～たらいいですよ` or `～たらどうですか` - the mirror image of this
pattern, and the reason the two sit next to each other in this book.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ どう**する**たらいいですか → ✅ どう**した**らいいですか
  `たら` is built on the plain past. する → した → したら.

- ❌ どうしたらいい**ますか** → ✅ どうしたらいい**ですか**
  `いい` is an い-adjective, so it takes `です`, not `ます`.

- ❌ 財布(さいふ)をなくしました。**どうしましたか**。 → ✅ 財布(さいふ)をなくしました。**どうしたらいいですか**。
  `どうしましたか` asks what happened - which you have just said. It cannot mean
  "what should I do?"

- ❌ どうすればいいですか**を**教(おし)えてください。 → ✅ どうすればいい**か**教(おし)えてください。
  An embedded question drops `ですか` and ends in bare `か`. It is not a noun and
  cannot take `を`.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、この件(けん)はどうしますか。（meaning "what should I do?"）→ ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、この件(けん)はどうしたら**よろしいでしょうか**。
  Asking upward for instructions needs the advice frame, and `よろしいでしょうか`
  for the register.

## Practice

**1.** この申込書(もうしこみしょ)は、どこに出(だ)____いいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

どこに出(だ)<b>したら</b>いいですか。（or どこに出(だ)<b>せば</b>いいですか）

Either conditional works in this frame. `出したら` is the past form plus `ら`;
`出せば` is the ば-form. The question word `どこ` simply replaces `どう` - the
shape of the sentence is unchanged.

</details>

**2.** パスポートをなくしてしまいました。（どうしましたか・どうしたらいいですか）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

パスポートをなくしてしまいました。<b>どうしたらいいですか</b>。

You have already said what happened, so `どうしましたか` - "what happened?" - 
would be asking your listener to tell you your own news. The advice frame is the
only one that fits.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "What time should I come tomorrow?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)は何時(なんじ)に<b>来(き)たらいいですか</b>。（or 来(く)ればいいですか）

Question word + conditional + `いい`. Note the readings of the irregular verb:
来(く)る → 来(き)た → 来(き)たら, but the ば-form is
来(く)る → 来(く)れば.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: どうすればいいですかを教(おし)えてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ どうすればいい<b>か</b>教(おし)えてください。

Once the question is embedded in a bigger sentence it loses `ですか` and keeps
only `か`. The politeness moves to the outer verb, which is already
教(おし)えてください. Adding `を` treats the question as a noun, which it is
not.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) どうしたらいいですか。
(b) どうしますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says "I don't know the right thing to do - please tell me." The speaker is
asking to be given a course of action, and the answer is expected to be advice:
係(かかり)の人(ひと)に聞(き)いたらいいですよ.

(b) says "what are you going to do?" The decision belongs to the listener, and
the answer will be a plan, not advice. Said to a customer it is a polite "what
would you like to do?"; said upward to a boss when you meant (a), it lands as
though you were querying their intentions rather than asking for instructions.

</details>
