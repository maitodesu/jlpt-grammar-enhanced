# ～と思う

**Meaning** · I think (that) … - <code>思(おも)う</code> is "think", and `と` is the string that ties a whole sentence into a parcel for it to carry

`と` is a **quotation marker**. It takes an entire sentence, wraps it up, and hands
it to a verb of thinking or saying. <code>と思(おも)う</code> is therefore not
"think about" - it reports a complete thought, word for word, as your opinion. The
parcel is always tied in **plain form**, no matter how polite the sentence around
it is.

## Formation

`と` follows the plain form of the quoted clause. Nouns and な-adjectives keep
their `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + と思(おも)う | 来(く)る → 来(く)ると思(おも)う |
| Verb (past) | た-form + と思(おも)う | 終(お)わった → 終(お)わったと思(おも)う |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + と思(おも)う | 間(ま)に合(あ)わない → 間(ま)に合(あ)わないと思(おも)う |
| い-adjective | plain form + と思(おも)う | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いと思(おも)う |
| な-adjective | + だ + と思(おも)う | 無理(むり) → 無理(むり)だと思(おも)う |
| Noun | + だ + と思(おも)う | 冗談(じょうだん) → 冗談(じょうだん)だと思(おも)う |
| Volitional - "I'm thinking of…" | よう-form + と思(おも)う | 休(やす)もう → 休(やす)もうと思(おも)う |
| Somebody else's opinion | plain form + と思(おも)っている | 正(ただ)しい → 正(ただ)しいと思(おも)っている |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nothing polite goes in front of `と`.
❌<code>行(い)きますと思(おも)います</code> → ✅<code>行(い)くと思(おも)います</code>. The
politeness of the whole sentence is carried by the **final** verb and nowhere else.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `だ` that disappears before `でしょう` stays before `と`. It is
<code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)でしょう</code> but <code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だと思(おも)います</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの店(みせ)はもう閉(し)まっている<b>と思(おも)う</b>。
I think that shop has already closed.
</pre>

<pre>
この傘(かさ)は山田(やまだ)さんのだ<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I think this umbrella is Yamada's.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)はそんなに難(むずか)しくない<b>と思(おも)う</b>よ。
I don't think the test will be all that hard.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)は去年(きょねん)より暖(あたた)かい<b>と思(おも)いませんか</b>。
Don't you think this winter is milder than last year's?
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から自転車(じてんしゃ)で通(かよ)おう<b>と思(おも)っています</b>。
I'm thinking of cycling to work from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は自分(じぶん)の料理(りょうり)が世界(せかい)で一番(いちばん)おいしい<b>と思(おも)っている</b>。
My sister is convinced her own cooking is the best in the world.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に終(お)わる<b>と思(おも)います</b>が、念(ねん)のため確認(かくにん)しておきます。
I think the meeting finishes at three, but I'll check to be safe.
</pre>

<pre>
正直(しょうじき)に言(い)うと、あの計画(けいかく)はうまくいかない<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
To be honest, I don't think that plan is going to work.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>思(おも)う</code> is the plain form and <code>思(おも)います</code> the polite one;
everything before `と` is untouched by that choice. This is the single most useful
thing to know about the pattern, and the single most common place learners slip.

Beyond grammar, <code>と思(おも)います</code> is a **softener**. A bare assertion - 
その方法(ほうほう)は無理(むり)です - commits you to a fact. Adding
<code>と思(おも)います</code> reframes it as one person's reading of the situation, which
is why Japanese workplace speech is full of it. Overusing it is a real thing too:
answering a question about your own department with
<code>多分(たぶん)そうだと思(おも)います</code> can sound as if you have not bothered to
find out.

In casual speech you will hear `って` in place of `と` - 
無理(むり)だって思(おも)った - and the whole clause often ends in
<code>んだけど</code> to leave the door open:
明日(あした)でもいいと思(おも)うんだけど。 At the other end, formal writing prefers
the passive <code>と思(おも)われます</code> or <code>と考(かんが)えられます</code>, which
removes the "I" altogether.

One limit worth stating plainly: the plain-form rule is about **indirect** quotation.
If you put actual quotation marks round someone's words you are reproducing what
came out of their mouth, so anything can appear inside:
「明日(あした)は休(やす)みます」と言(い)っていました。 That is a direct quote, and
it is not an exception to the rule - it is a different operation.

## Contrast

### ～と思(おも)う vs ～と思(おも)っている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>思(おも)う</code> is a thought formed **now**, at the moment of
speaking. <code>思(おも)っている</code> is a view somebody **holds** - and it is the only
option for a flat statement about a third person.

</div>

<pre>
その色(いろ)のほうが似合(にあ)う<b>と思(おも)う</b>。
I think that colour suits you better.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は私(わたし)をまだ子(こ)どもだ<b>と思(おも)っている</b>。
My father still thinks of me as a child.
</pre>

You cannot see into someone else's head, so a bare
❌<code>父(ちち)は…と思(おも)う</code> claims access you do not have. The `ている` turns
it into an observation about a state you can infer from outside. About yourself both
work: <code>私(わたし)もそう思(おも)っています</code> just adds that you have thought so
for a while.

### ～と思(おも)う vs ～でしょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と思(おも)う</code> labels the claim as **mine**. `でしょう`
estimates how **likely** the claim is. One is about the speaker, the other about the
world.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)は野菜(やさい)の値段(ねだん)が上(あ)がる<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I think vegetable prices will go up next year.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)は野菜(やさい)の値段(ねだん)が上(あ)がる<b>でしょう</b>。
Vegetable prices will probably go up next year.
</pre>

The second is what a news presenter says; the first is what you say at dinner. Because
<code>と思(おも)います</code> keeps the responsibility on you, it is usually the safer
choice when you might be wrong, and the wrong choice when you are supposed to be the
authority.

### ～ないと思(おも)う vs ～と思(おも)わない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English negates "think"; Japanese negates the **thought**. "I don't
think it will rain" is <code>降(ふ)らないと思(おも)う</code>, not
<code>降(ふ)ると思(おも)わない</code>.

</div>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)はその話(はなし)を知(し)らない<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I don't think she knows about it.
</pre>

<code>と思(おも)わない</code> is not ungrammatical - it is simply much stronger. It
rejects the proposition rather than doubting it, so it lands as disagreement:

<pre>
私(わたし)はそうは<b>思(おも)いません</b>。
I don't see it that way.
</pre>

Note the `は` on `そう`, which is typical: negating <code>思(おも)う</code> almost always
comes with contrastive `は`, and that is part of why it feels like pushing back.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)りますと思(おも)います</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)ると思(おも)います</b>。
  Nothing in ます-form may sit in front of `と`. Politeness belongs to the last verb.

- ❌ この辞書(じしょ)は<b>便利(べんり)と思(おも)う</b>。 → ✅ この辞書(じしょ)は<b>便利(べんり)だと思(おも)う</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns keep `だ` before `と`. Only い-adjectives and verbs attach bare.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)はその映画(えいが)がつまらないと<b>思(おも)います</b>。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)はその映画(えいが)がつまらないと<b>思(おも)っています</b>。
  A plain statement about what someone else thinks needs `ている` - or a hedge such as
  <code>思(おも)っているようです</code>.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が<b>あると思(おも)いません</b>。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)が<b>ないと思(おも)います</b>。
  Put the negative inside the quoted thought. The outer negative is reserved for
  disagreeing.

## Practice

**1.** 田中(たなか)さんは来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)____思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

田中(たなか)さんは来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)<b>だと</b>思(おも)います。

<code>出張(しゅっちょう)</code> is a noun, and a noun predicate keeps its `だ` in front of
the quotative `と`. Dropping it is only correct before `でしょう`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 姉(あね)は自分(じぶん)が正(ただ)しい（と思(おも)います・と思(おも)っています）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

姉(あね)は自分(じぶん)が正(ただ)しい<b>と思(おも)っています</b>。

The subject is a third person, so a bare <code>と思(おも)います</code> would claim direct
knowledge of her mind. `ている` presents the belief as something you observe her
holding.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I don't think he will come today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は今日(きょう)<b>来(こ)ないと思(おも)います</b>。

The negative goes inside the quoted thought, not on
<code>思(おも)う</code>. <code>来(く)ると思(おも)いません</code> would read as flatly
rejecting the idea rather than doubting it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの人(ひと)は親切(しんせつ)ですと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの人(ひと)は<b>親切(しんせつ)だと思(おも)います</b>。

Two things go wrong at once: `です` cannot appear before `と`, and the な-adjective
still needs its plain copula `だ`. The polite ending survives on
<code>思(おも)います</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この案(あん)は失敗(しっぱい)すると思(おも)います。
(b) この案(あん)は失敗(しっぱい)するでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) marks the prediction as your own reading and keeps the responsibility with you - 
appropriate in a meeting where you are offering an opinion someone may argue with.

(b) states it as a likely outcome, the way a forecast or an analysis does. It sounds
more detached and more authoritative, which is fine from an expert and presumptuous
from a newcomer.

</details>

# って

**Meaning** · the spoken shortcut - "they said…", "you know…", "the thing called…"

`って` is what `と` (and often <code>という</code>, and sometimes `は`) becomes in
relaxed speech. It quotes, it reports hearsay, it introduces a name, and it puts a
topic on the table with a little more energy than `は` does. One syllable, several
jobs - but they all come from the same place: `って` **presents a chunk of language**
and asks the listener to take it as a unit.

## Formation

Like `と`, `って` follows the **plain form**. Nouns and な-adjectives keep `だ` when
`って` is quoting; they drop it when `って` is marking a topic.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - quoting | plain form + って（+ 言(い)う／聞(き)く／思(おも)う） | 辞(や)める → 辞(や)めるって言(い)ってた |
| Noun / な-adjective - quoting | + だ + って | 中止(ちゅうし) → 中止(ちゅうし)だって聞(き)いた |
| Sentence-final - hearsay | plain form + って（falling tone） | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないって |
| Direct quote | 「…」+ って | 「まだだ」 → 「まだだ」って言(い)われた |
| Naming - replaces <code>という</code> | Noun + って + Noun | 林(はやし) → 林(はやし)さんって人(ひと) |
| Topic - replaces `は` / <code>というのは</code> | Noun + って（no だ） | 敬語(けいご) → 敬語(けいご)って難(むずか)しい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `って` inherits the `と` rule exactly: the clause in front of it is
**plain**, however polite you are being. ❌<code>晴(は)れますって</code> →
✅<code>晴(は)れるって</code>. The one exception is a direct quote in 「」, where you are
copying someone's actual words.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん、明日(あした)来(こ)ない<b>って</b>。
Tanaka says he's not coming tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)、中止(ちゅうし)だ<b>って</b>。
Apparently the meeting's off.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)<b>って</b>覚(おぼ)えるのが大変(たいへん)だね。
Kanji really are a slog to memorise, aren't they.
</pre>

<pre>
さっき先生(せんせい)が今日(きょう)の宿題(しゅくだい)はない<b>って</b>言(い)ってたよ。
The teacher said a moment ago that there's no homework today.
</pre>

<pre>
林(はやし)さん<b>って</b>人(ひと)から電話(でんわ)がありましたよ。
There was a phone call from someone called Hayashi.
</pre>

<pre>
「もう帰(かえ)りたい」<b>って</b>顔(かお)をしているよ。
You've got an "I want to go home already" look on your face.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)に貸(か)した本(ほん)、まだ読(よ)んでない<b>って</b>言(い)うんだけど、もう一(いっ)か月(げつ)経(た)つよ。
My sister says she still hasn't read the book I lent her - and it's been a month.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)する<b>って</b>本当(ほんとう)？いつ決(き)めたの？
Is it true you're studying abroad next year? When did you decide?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`って` is **spoken casual and nothing else**. It belongs in conversation with friends,
family and people you are on plain-form terms with. It does not belong in an email to
a client, a report, an essay, or a sentence you are otherwise ending in `ます`. The
neutral replacements are `と` for quoting and <code>そうです</code>／
<code>とのことです</code> for hearsay.

Intonation does a lot of work. Falling `って` at the end of a sentence is hearsay - 
雨(あめ)になるって。 Rising `って`? is a request for confirmation, close to "wait,
did you say…?" - 明日(あした)休(やす)みだって？ Both are the same particle.

Two extensions you will hear constantly and will not find in a textbook:
`っていうか` at the head of a sentence, which retracts what you just said and
restarts ("or rather…"), and `ってば`, which repeats something impatiently - 
だから違(ちが)うってば。 The second is distinctly childish or intimate; it is not a
neutral emphasis marker.

There is a semi-polite hearsay form, `～んですって`, which keeps a `です` on the end:
となりの店(みせ)、閉(し)めるんですって。 It is polite in form but chatty in feel,
and in modern Tokyo speech it leans female. `～だってです` is not a form at all.

## Contrast

### って vs と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same grammar, different register: `って` is `と` in a T-shirt. But
`って` also covers jobs `と` cannot do, namely naming and topic-marking.

</div>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は出席(しゅっせき)できない<b>と</b>言(い)っていました。
The section chief said he can't attend.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)、出席(しゅっせき)できない<b>って</b>。
The boss says he can't make it.
</pre>

Both report the same fact. The first is what you write in a message to the team; the
second is what you say over your shoulder to the colleague next to you. Where the two
part company is self-introduction and any fixed formula:
田中(たなか)と申(もう)します is the set phrase, and `って` there is simply wrong.

### topic って vs は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` sets a topic quietly. `って` sets a topic and signals that
you are about to say something **about the category itself**, usually with a reaction
attached.

</div>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の夏(なつ)<b>は</b>暑(あつ)いです。
Summers in Japan are hot.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の夏(なつ)<b>って</b>暑(あつ)いね。
Japanese summers are hot, aren't they.
</pre>

The `って` version reads as a comment brought up fresh, and it pairs naturally with
`ね`, `よ` and evaluative adjectives. It is close to
<code>日本(にほん)の夏(なつ)というのは</code>, which is exactly what it is a
contraction of - and that is why it can also mean "what is…?":
「バイト」<b>って</b>何(なに)？

### hearsay って vs ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical content, opposite registers. `って` is what you say;
<code>そうです</code> is what you write.

</div>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さん、来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>って</b>。
Yamada's moving house next month, apparently.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんは来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>そうです</b>。
I hear that Yamada is moving house next month.
</pre>

One practical difference: `って` easily carries a named source in front of it - 
母(はは)が寒(さむ)くなるって言(い)ってた - while <code>そうだ</code> usually leaves
the source vague or marks it with <code>によると</code>. If you want to say who told
you, `って` is the more natural spoken frame.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)は田中(たなか)<b>って申(もう)します</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は田中(たなか)<b>と申(もう)します</b>。
  Self-introduction is a fixed humble formula. `って` cannot be dropped into a phrase
  that is formal by definition.

- ❌ 天気予報(てんきよほう)で明日(あした)は<b>晴(は)れますって</b>言(い)ってた。 → ✅ …<b>晴(は)れるって</b>言(い)ってた。
  An indirect quote takes the plain form, exactly as with `と`. `ます` only survives
  inside 「」.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)<b>だってです</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)<b>だそうです</b>。
  You cannot make `って` polite by bolting `です` on. Switch to
  <code>そうです</code> or <code>とのことです</code>.

- ❌ (in a written report) 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)、問題(もんだい)はなかった<b>って</b>。 → ✅ …問題(もんだい)はなかった<b>とのことです</b>。
  `って` is a spoken form. In writing it reads as though the report were a text
  message.

## Practice

**1.** 友達(ともだち)に：「山田(やまだ)さん、今日(きょう)来(こ)られない____。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)さん、今日(きょう)来(こ)られない<b>って</b>。

Sentence-final `って` after a plain form reports what you were told, with the source
left implicit. Said with a falling tone it means "apparently", not "did you say?".

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option for an email to a client: 納期(のうき)は来週(らいしゅう)（だって・だそうです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

納期(のうき)は来週(らいしゅう)<b>だそうです</b>。

`って` is casual spoken Japanese and never appears in business writing. The hearsay
meaning is unchanged; only the register is fixed.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "Someone called Kimura came looking for you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

木村(きむら)さん<b>って人(ひと)</b>が来(き)たよ。

`って` before a noun is the contraction of <code>という</code> and is the everyday way
of naming someone the listener may not know. <code>木村(きむら)さんという人(ひと)</code>
is the same thing in a neutral register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を探(さが)していますって言(い)ってた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を<b>探(さが)しているって</b>言(い)ってた。

The quoted clause must be plain. `って` is only a casual `と`, so it obeys the same
restriction: nothing in ます-form immediately in front of it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一人暮(ひとりぐ)らしはお金(かね)がかかります。
(b) 一人暮(ひとりぐ)らし<b>って</b>お金(かね)がかかるよね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral, polite statement of fact - the kind of line that could open a
paragraph in an article about living costs.

(b) raises "living alone" as a topic the two of you both know something about, and
adds a shared reaction with `よね`. `って` marks the category as newly put on the
table and invites agreement; it is conversation, not exposition.

</details>

# かどうか

**Meaning** · whether or not - packs a yes/no question inside a bigger sentence

`かどうか` takes a question that could be answered *yes* or *no*, ties it into a
single lump, and hands that lump to a verb like <code>分(わ)かる</code>,
<code>知(し)らない</code> or <code>調(しら)べる</code>. It is literally
"か（yes?）どう（how?）か" - is it so, or how is it? The English that maps onto it is
**whether**, and mapping it onto **if** instead is where nearly all the trouble starts.

## Formation

`か` attaches to plain forms. Nouns and な-adjectives **lose** their `だ` - the
opposite of what happens before the quotative `と`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + かどうか | 届(とど)く → 届(とど)くかどうか |
| Verb (past) | た-form + かどうか | 送(おく)った → 送(おく)ったかどうか |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + かどうか | 要(い)らない → 要(い)らないかどうか |
| い-adjective | plain form + かどうか | 新(あたら)しい → 新(あたら)しいかどうか |
| な-adjective | drop だ + かどうか | 安全(あんぜん)だ → 安全(あんぜん)かどうか |
| Noun | drop だ + かどうか | 本物(ほんもの)だ → 本物(ほんもの)かどうか |
| Both options spelled out | ～か～ないか | 行(い)く → 行(い)くか行(い)かないか |

Typical main verbs after it: <code>分(わ)かる</code>, <code>知(し)らない</code>,
<code>聞(き)く</code>, <code>調(しら)べる</code>, <code>確(たし)かめる</code>,
<code>迷(まよ)う</code>, <code>覚(おぼ)えていない</code>, <code>決(き)める</code>.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nothing polite and no `だ` in front of `か`.
❌<code>行(い)きますかどうか</code>, ❌<code>安全(あんぜん)だかどうか</code> →
✅<code>行(い)くかどうか</code>, ✅<code>安全(あんぜん)かどうか</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the particle that would follow the lump (`を`, `が`) is normally left out:
届(とど)いたかどうか確(たし)かめます. `は` does appear, and adds contrast:
届(とど)いたかどうかは、まだ分(わ)かりません。

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これでいい<b>かどうか</b>、見(み)てください。
Please have a look and tell me whether this is all right.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)る<b>かどうか</b>分(わ)かりません。
I don't know whether it will snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
この水(みず)が安全(あんぜん)<b>かどうか</b>、先(さき)に調(しら)べたほうがいいよ。
You should check first whether this water is safe.
</pre>

<pre>
受(う)かった<b>かどうか</b>は、来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)に分(わ)かります。
Whether I passed or not will be clear next Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
パーティーに出(で)る<b>かどうか</b>、まだ迷(まよ)っているんです。
I still can't make up my mind whether to go to the party.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が本当(ほんとう)のことを言(い)っている<b>かどうか</b>、私(わたし)には判断(はんだん)できません。
I'm in no position to judge whether he's telling the truth.
</pre>

<pre>
サイズが合(あ)う<b>かどうか</b>、試着(しちゃく)してから決(き)めようと思(おも)います。
I'll try it on before deciding, to see whether the size works.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し込(こ)みが締(し)め切(き)りに間(ま)に合(あ)った<b>かどうか</b>、事務所(じむしょ)に確認(かくにん)してみます。
I'll check with the office whether my application made the deadline.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`かどうか` is register-neutral. It appears unchanged in a chat with friends, in a
lecture and in a contract; as always, the politeness of the sentence lives on the
final verb. It is also one of the few N4 patterns with no casual contraction of its
own - although in relaxed speech people often drop `どうか` entirely and leave a bare
`か`: 間(ま)に合(あ)うか分(わ)かんない。 That is shorter, not ruder.

The one thing `かどうか` cannot do is finish a sentence. It is a lump that some other
verb has to pick up, so <code>来(く)るかどうか。</code> on its own is a fragment. If
you want to end there, you need the wondering forms instead - 
<code>来(く)るかな</code>, <code>来(く)るでしょうか</code>.

Two fixed expressions are worth having ready. <code>かどうかは別(べつ)として</code>
("setting aside whether…") concedes a point before moving on, and
<code>かどうか分(わ)かりませんが</code> is the standard hedge for offering something
you are not sure will help: お役(やく)に立(た)つかどうか分(わ)かりませんが、こちらの資料(しりょう)をどうぞ。

## Contrast

### かどうか vs か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the embedded question contains a **question word**
（何(なに), どこ, だれ, いつ, どう, どちら）, use bare `か`. `かどうか` is only for
questions whose answer is *yes* or *no*.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)何(なに)を注文(ちゅうもん)した<b>か</b>覚(おぼ)えていません。
I don't remember what I ordered yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)注文(ちゅうもん)した<b>かどうか</b>覚(おぼ)えていません。
I don't remember whether I ordered yesterday.
</pre>

The first asks about the *contents* of the order, the second about whether the
ordering happened at all. Sticking `どうか` onto the first - 
❌<code>何(なに)を注文(ちゅうもん)したかどうか</code> - is ungrammatical, because
何(なに) has already supplied the question and `どうか` has nothing left to ask.

Bare `か` is also available for yes/no questions, especially in speech:
空(あ)いているか見(み)てきます。 `かどうか` simply spells the alternative out and
is the clearer choice in writing.

### かどうか vs ～たら／～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English "if" is two unrelated words. "If" you could replace with
**whether** → `かどうか`. "If" meaning **on condition that** → `たら`／`ば`／`と`.

</div>

<pre>
木村(きむら)さんが参加(さんか)する<b>かどうか</b>、聞(き)いてみます。
I'll ask whether Kimura is taking part.
</pre>

<pre>
木村(きむら)さんが参加(さんか)し<b>たら</b>、席(せき)を一(ひと)つ増(ふ)やします。
If Kimura takes part, I'll add one more seat.
</pre>

These are not stylistic variants - they say different things. The first is about the
speaker's ignorance; the second is about a consequence. "I don't know if he's coming"
is ❌<code>来(き)たら分(わ)かりません</code> and
✅<code>来(く)るかどうか分(わ)かりません</code>. This is the most common single error
with the pattern, and it comes straight out of English.

### かどうか vs ～か～ないか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, more deliberate delivery. And when the two options
are not *yes* and *no* but two **different things**, only the spelled-out version
works.

</div>

<pre>
出席(しゅっせき)する<b>かどうか</b>、金曜日(きんようび)までにお返事(へんじ)ください。
Please reply by Friday as to whether you'll attend.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)で行(い)く<b>かバスで行(い)くか</b>、まだ決(き)めていません。
I haven't decided yet whether to go by train or by bus.
</pre>

The second could not be rewritten with `かどうか`: there is no *no* to negate, just
two alternatives. Keep `かどうか` for the yes/no case and lay both options out when
they are genuinely two options.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)が<b>静(しず)かだかどうか</b>確(たし)かめます。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)が<b>静(しず)かかどうか</b>確(たし)かめます。
  Nouns and な-adjectives drop `だ` before `か`. It is `だ` before `と`, no `だ` before
  `か` - the two quotative-ish particles behave in opposite ways.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんが<b>来(き)ますかどうか</b>知(し)りません。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんが<b>来(く)るかどうか</b>知(し)りません。
  The embedded clause is plain. `ます` belongs to the end of the sentence, not to the
  middle of it.

- ❌ 荷物(にもつ)が<b>届(とど)いたら</b>分(わ)かりません。 → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)が<b>届(とど)いたかどうか</b>分(わ)かりません。
  "I don't know if the parcel arrived" is *whether*, not a condition. `たら` here would
  mean "once it arrives, I won't understand", which is nonsense.

- ❌ <b>どこで買(か)えるかどうか</b>教(おし)えてください。 → ✅ <b>どこで買(か)えるか</b>教(おし)えてください。
  With a question word present, `どうか` has nothing to do. Drop it.

**1.** この情報(じょうほう)が正確(せいかく)____確(たし)かめる必要(ひつよう)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>正確(せいかく)</code> is a な-adjective, so its `だ` disappears before `か`.
Writing ❌<code>正確(せいかく)だかどうか</code> is the single most frequent form error
here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 誰(だれ)が担当(たんとう)する（か・かどうか）聞(き)いておきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

誰(だれ)が担当(たんとう)する<b>か</b>聞(き)いておきます。

The clause already contains the question word 誰(だれ), so the question has been
asked and `どうか` would be asking a second, different one. `かどうか` is reserved for

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I don't know whether this key fits that door."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"Whether" is the giveaway for `かどうか`. Note that a conditional
（❌<code>合(あ)ったら分(わ)かりません</code>）would say something quite different and

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 息子(むすこ)が朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べましたかどうか覚(おぼ)えていません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 息子(むすこ)が朝(あさ)ごはんを<b>食(た)べたかどうか</b>覚(おぼ)えていません。

<code>食(た)べました</code> becomes <code>食(た)べた</code>. The sentence stays polite

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 注文(ちゅうもん)した品物(しなもの)が届(とど)いたかどうか確(たし)かめます。
(b) 注文(ちゅうもん)した品物(しなもの)が届(とど)いたら確(たし)かめます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) makes the arrival itself the unknown: you do not yet know whether the goods came,
and checking is how you will find out.

(b) treats the arrival as a condition that has not been met yet: once the goods do
arrive, you will check them (presumably for damage). English "if" covers both, which
is exactly why the two get swapped.

</details>

# かもしれない

**Meaning** · might; maybe - the possibility is open, and the speaker refuses to say more than that

Take the pieces apart and the word explains itself: `か` (is it so?) + `も` (even) +
<code>知(し)れない</code> (cannot be known) - "it cannot even be known whether it is
so." `かもしれない` does **not** say something is likely. It says the door is open,
and leaves the door on the other side open too. That is the whole of its meaning, and
it is why it sits at the bottom of the confidence ladder.

## Formation

Attaches to plain forms. Nouns and な-adjectives drop `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + かもしれない | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れるかもしれない |
| Verb (past) | た-form + かもしれない | 忘(わす)れた → 忘(わす)れたかもしれない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + かもしれない | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かないかもしれない |
| い-adjective | plain form + かもしれない | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いかもしれない |
| な-adjective | drop だ + かもしれない | 不便(ふべん)だ → 不便(ふべん)かもしれない |
| Noun | drop だ + かもしれない | 風邪(かぜ)だ → 風邪(かぜ)かもしれない |
| Polite | かもしれません | 遅(おく)れるかもしれません |
| Casual | かも／かもね／かもよ | 遅(おく)れるかも |
| Common partners | もしかしたら／ひょっとすると + …かもしれない | もしかしたら遅(おく)れるかもしれない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - tense lives **inside**, politeness lives **outside**.
"It might have been wrong" is
<code>間違(まちが)っていたかもしれません</code>, never
❌<code>間違(まちが)っているかもしれませんでした</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れる<b>かもしれない</b>。
The train might be late.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)は田中(たなか)さんの弟(おとうと)さん<b>かもしれません</b>。
That might be Tanaka's younger brother.
</pre>

<pre>
かぎを家(いえ)に忘(わす)れてきた<b>かもしれない</b>。
I may have left my keys at home.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)は思(おも)ったより寒(さむ)い<b>かもしれない</b>から、上着(うわぎ)を持(も)っていこう。
It might be colder than we expect up the mountain, so let's take jackets.
</pre>

<pre>
もしかしたら、私(わたし)の勘違(かんちが)いだった<b>かもしれません</b>。
It's possible I got the wrong end of the stick.
</pre>

<pre>
この方法(ほうほう)は少(すこ)し面倒(めんどう)<b>かもしれない</b>けど、確実(かくじつ)だよ。
This way might be a bit of a faff, but it's reliable.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)がないので、メールが届(とど)いていない<b>かもしれません</b>ね。
There's been no reply, so perhaps the email never arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
言(い)いにくいのですが、この数字(すうじ)はどこかで間違(まちが)っている<b>かもしれません</b>。
This is awkward to raise, but there may be an error somewhere in these figures.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Plain `かもしれない`, polite `かもしれません`. In speech the tail is very often chopped
off to `かも` - そうかも。／行(い)けないかも。 - and `かも` on its own is a complete
reply, roughly "could be". `かもね` softens it further and `かもよ` warns. None of these
short forms belong in writing; `かもしれない` itself is perfectly at home in an essay.

Its social use is at least as important as its logical one. Because `かもしれません`
commits to nothing, it is the standard tool for **raising something delicate**: a risk
nobody has mentioned, an error in a superior's work, a disagreement you do not want to
turn into a confrontation. <code>私(わたし)の説明(せつめい)が足(た)りなかったかもしれません</code>
takes the blame without quite admitting it, which is exactly why it is said so often.

The flip side is that overusing it sounds evasive. If your manager asks when your own
work will be finished, <code>明日(あした)終(お)わるかもしれません</code> reads as "I
haven't really thought about it". About your own actions and your own knowledge,
commit: <code>終(お)わると思(おも)います</code>.

`もしかしたら` and `ひょっとすると` at the head of the sentence flag the guess in
advance and push it further towards "just possibly". They pair with `かもしれない`
almost automatically, and a sentence that opens with `もしかしたら` and then ends
flatly in `です` sounds unfinished.

## Contrast

### かもしれない vs でしょう・だろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `でしょう` is an **estimate**: the speaker thinks it probably *is*
so. `かもしれない` is an **admission**: the speaker will only say it *could* be so, and
that the opposite could be too.

</div>

<pre>
午後(ごご)は風(かぜ)が強(つよ)くなる<b>でしょう</b>。
The wind will probably get stronger this afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
午後(ごご)は風(かぜ)が強(つよ)くなる<b>かもしれません</b>。
The wind might get stronger this afternoon.
</pre>

The forecaster says the first; the person deciding whether to hang the washing out says
the second. There is a neat test: you can chain `かもしれない` with its own negation - 
強(つよ)くなるかもしれないし、ならないかもしれない - and it sounds entirely normal,
because both branches were open all along. Doing the same with `でしょう` sounds like
someone contradicting themselves.

### かもしれない vs はずだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the difference is **what justifies the claim**.
<code>はずだ</code> rests on a reason you could name if challenged. `かもしれない` rests
on nothing at all, and needs nothing.

</div>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)はもう着(つ)いている<b>はずです</b>。七時(しちじ)の電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)ると言(い)っていましたから。
The section chief should already be there - he said he'd take the seven o'clock train.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)はもう着(つ)いている<b>かもしれません</b>。
The section chief might already be there.
</pre>

Ask どうして？ after the first and you owe an answer; the timetable is the grounds. Ask
it after the second and "no particular reason, it's just possible" is a complete reply.
This is also why `はずです` can be **wrong** in a way `かもしれません` cannot: if he is
not there, your reasoning has failed, and Japanese has a form for exactly that
disappointment - <code>はずだったのに</code>.

### かもしれない vs ～と思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と思(おも)う</code> puts an opinion on the record.
`かもしれない` declines to have one.

</div>

<pre>
こちらの辞書(じしょ)のほうが使(つか)いやすい<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I think this dictionary is easier to use.
</pre>

<pre>
こちらの辞書(じしょ)のほうが使(つか)いやすい<b>かもしれません</b>。
This dictionary might be easier to use.
</pre>

Both are polite; only the second leaves the listener free to disagree without
contradicting you, which is why it is the gentler way to suggest a change to someone
senior. The cost is authority: if you are the person who is supposed to know,
`かもしれません` shifts the decision back onto the listener.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>元気(げんき)だかもしれません</b>。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>元気(げんき)かもしれません</b>。
  Nouns and な-adjectives lose `だ` in front of `か`. Compare
  <code>元気(げんき)だと思(おも)います</code>, where the `だ` stays.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は<b>行(い)きますかもしれません</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は<b>行(い)くかもしれません</b>。
  The clause in front is plain. The politeness is already carried by
  `かもしれません`.

- ❌ さっきの説明(せつめい)は<b>間違(まちが)っているかもしれませんでした</b>。 → ✅ さっきの説明(せつめい)は<b>間違(まちが)っていたかもしれません</b>。
  Past tense goes on the embedded clause, not on `かもしれない`. There is no
  ❌`かもしれませんでした`.

- ❌ 「レポートはいつ出(だ)せますか。」「来週(らいしゅう)出(だ)せる<b>かもしれません</b>。」 → ✅ 「来週(らいしゅう)出(だ)せる<b>と思(おも)います</b>。」
  Not a grammar error, a register one. About your own work, `かもしれません` sounds
  like you have not looked at the calendar.

## Practice

**1.** 空(そら)が暗(くら)くなってきましたね。夕方(ゆうがた)から雨(あめ)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夕方(ゆうがた)から雨(あめ)<b>かもしれません</b>。

<code>雨(あめ)</code> is a noun, so it attaches bare - no `だ`. You could equally say
<code>降(ふ)るかもしれません</code> with the verb; either way the `か` needs a plain
form or a bare noun in front of it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新幹線(しんかんせん)は時間(じかん)に正確(せいかく)だから、もう着(つ)いている（かもしれません・はずです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう着(つ)いている<b>はずです</b>。

The sentence supplies a reason - the bullet train runs on time - and a stated reason is
precisely what <code>はずです</code> requires. `かもしれません` would throw that reasoning
away and say only that it is possible.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I might have taken the wrong umbrella."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傘(かさ)を<b>間違(まちが)えたかもしれません</b>。

The past tense goes inside the clause, on
<code>間違(まちが)える</code>. `かもしれません` itself never conjugates for tense.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この問題(もんだい)は簡単(かんたん)だかもしれませんが、時間(じかん)がかかります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この問題(もんだい)は<b>簡単(かんたん)かもしれません</b>が、時間(じかん)がかかります。

<code>簡単(かんたん)</code> is a な-adjective and must drop `だ` before `か`. Learners
who have just learned <code>簡単(かんたん)だと思(おも)う</code> carry the `だ` across
by habit.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は来(こ)ないでしょう。
(b) 彼(かれ)は来(こ)ないかもしれません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a prediction the speaker is fairly confident in - "he probably won't come." If
you are organising the room, you would set out one chair fewer.

(b) only opens the possibility, and leaves his coming equally open. It is what you say
to warn people not to count on him, and it is also the more tactful of the two, since
it does not tell anyone what to expect.

</details>

# はずだ

**Meaning** · it should be; it must be; it's supposed to be - an expectation you could justify if asked

`はず` is a noun meaning something like *what follows*. Attach a clause to it and you
are saying: given what I know, this is how things ought to line up. The key word is
**given**. `はずだ` always rests on grounds - a timetable, a promise, a rule, a fact
you were told - and if somebody asks どうして？ you are expected to produce them.

## Formation

`はず` is a noun, so the clause in front takes the forms a noun-modifying clause takes:
な-adjectives get `な`, nouns get `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + はずだ | 届(とど)く → 届(とど)くはずだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + はずだ | 出(で)た → 出(で)たはずだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + はずだ | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないはずだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + はずだ | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いはずだ |
| な-adjective | + な + はずだ | 便利(べんり)だ → 便利(べんり)なはずだ |
| Noun | + の + はずだ | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みのはずだ |
| Polite | はずです | 届(とど)くはずです |
| Expectation that failed | はずだった（のに） | 届(とど)くはずだった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `な` for な-adjectives, `の` for nouns.
❌<code>便利(べんり)はずだ</code>, ❌<code>休(やす)みはずだ</code>. Compare
`かもしれない`, which takes both bare - the two patterns pull in opposite directions and
learners routinely swap them.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
銀行(ぎんこう)は三時(さんじ)まで開(あ)いている<b>はず</b>です。
The bank should be open until three.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は木曜日(もくようび)だから、あの店(みせ)は休(やす)み<b>のはず</b>だ。
It's Thursday, so that shop should be shut.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)送(おく)りましたから、もう届(とど)いている<b>はず</b>です。
I posted it yesterday, so it should have arrived by now.
</pre>

<pre>
この道(みち)のほうが近(ちか)い<b>はず</b>なんだけど、あまり自信(じしん)がない。
This road should be the shorter one, though I'm not too sure.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は運転(うんてん)が上手(じょうず)な<b>はず</b>です。10年(ねん)も毎日(まいにち)運転(うんてん)していますから。
He should be a good driver - he's driven every day for ten years.
</pre>

<pre>
おかしいな。ここに置(お)いた<b>はず</b>なのに、見(み)つからない。
That's odd. I'm sure I put it here, and yet I can't find it.
</pre>

<pre>
あの二人(ふたり)は同(おな)じ高校(こうこう)だから、知(し)り合(あ)い<b>のはず</b>ですよ。
Those two went to the same school, so they should know each other.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいカードは先週(せんしゅう)届(とど)く<b>はずだった</b>のに、まだ連絡(れんらく)もありません。
My new card was supposed to arrive last week, and I haven't even had word.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`はずだ` is plain, `はずです` polite, and the pattern is otherwise register-neutral: it
turns up in conversation, in business email and in written explanation without changing
shape. In casual speech people say `はずだよ`, `はずでしょ`, and - dropping the copula
entirely - 来(く)るはず、来(く)るはず。 as a mutter of reassurance.

The past form `はずだった` is worth learning as a unit. It does not mean "it should have
been" in the sense of blame; it means the expectation existed and reality did not
cooperate. It nearly always comes with `のに` or `が`, and it carries audible
disappointment.

There is also a small idiom that catches learners off guard: `はずだ` after a discovery
means "**no wonder**". You notice something, work out why, and say so.

<pre>
どうりで寒(さむ)い<b>はず</b>だ。窓(まど)が開(あ)いている。
No wonder it's cold - the window's open.
</pre>

Here the speaker is not predicting anything; they are confirming, after the fact, that
the world lines up. It is the same `はず` seen from the other end.

Finally, a limit: `はずだ` is poor at describing **your own intentions**, because you do
not need to deduce what you have decided. <code>私(わたし)は明日(あした)行(い)くはずです</code>
sounds as though your movements are arranged by someone else. That said, when they
genuinely are - a shift roster, a schedule you have been handed - `はず` about yourself
is fine: 明日(あした)は休(やす)みのはずなんですが、確(たし)かめてみます。

## Contrast

### はずだ vs べきだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English "should" is two words. "I expect it is so" → `はずだ`.
"It is the right thing to do" → `べきだ`.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)はその話(はなし)をもう知(し)っている<b>はず</b>です。
He should already know about it. (I expect he does)
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)にその話(はなし)を早(はや)く知(し)らせる<b>べき</b>です。
We should tell him about it soon. (it's the right thing to do)
</pre>

The test is substitution. If "should" can become "is supposed to" or "I expect", it is
`はずだ`, and the sentence is a prediction. If it can become "ought to", it is `べきだ`,
and the sentence is a judgement. Telling a friend
❌<code>早(はや)く寝(ね)るはずです</code> does not advise them to go to bed; it
predicts that they will.

### はずだ vs かもしれない・でしょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a ladder of confidence, `かもしれない` → `でしょう` → `はずだ` - but
`はずだ` is not merely the top rung. It is a **deduction from stated grounds**, where the
other two are estimates.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)<b>のはず</b>です。三時(さんじ)からだと聞(き)きました。
The director should be in a meeting - I was told it started at three.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)<b>でしょう</b>。
The director is probably in a meeting.
</pre>

Strip the second sentence of the first one and `はずです` starts to feel unsupported;
`でしょう` never needs support. The practical consequence is that `はずだ` can be
*refuted*: if the meeting was cancelled, your premise was wrong and you say
会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)のはずだったんですが. A `でしょう` that misses was only ever a
guess.

### はずだ vs つもり・予定(よてい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもり` is what **I** have decided, <code>予定(よてい)</code> is
what the **diary** says, `はず` is what **I work out** about someone or something else.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>つもり</b>です。
I'm planning to move next month.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>はず</b>です。
Tanaka should be moving next month, as far as I know.
</pre>

Swap them and the meaning goes badly wrong: `つもり` about a third person claims to know
their intentions, and `はず` about your own decision hands your life over to somebody
else's timetable. <code>予定(よてい)です</code> sits between them - it reports an
arrangement without saying whose idea it was, which is why it is the safest thing to say
at work.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この辞書(じしょ)は<b>便利(べんり)はず</b>です。 → ✅ この辞書(じしょ)は<b>便利(べんり)なはず</b>です。
  `はず` is a noun, so a な-adjective in front of it needs its `な`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は<b>祝日(しゅくじつ)はず</b>ですよ。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は<b>祝日(しゅくじつ)のはず</b>ですよ。
  A noun links to `はず` with `の`. Dropping it is the same error one step along.

- ❌ 体(からだ)に悪(わる)いから、たばこを<b>やめるはず</b>です。 → ✅ 体(からだ)に悪(わる)いから、たばこを<b>やめるべき</b>です。
  This is advice, not prediction. English "should" hides the difference; Japanese does
  not.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)大学(だいがく)を<b>受(う)けるはず</b>です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)大学(だいがく)を<b>受(う)けるつもり</b>です。
  Your own intention is not something you deduce. `はず` here sounds as if someone else
  had signed you up.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)メールで送(おく)りましたから、もう読(よ)んでいる____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう読(よ)んでいる<b>はず</b>です。

The first half supplies the grounds - you sent it yesterday - and grounds are exactly
what `はず` is built on. `かもしれません` would waste the reason you just gave.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 子(こ)どもはもっと外(そと)で遊(あそ)ぶ（はず・べき）だと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)どもはもっと外(そと)で遊(あそ)ぶ<b>べき</b>だと思(おも)います。

This is a value judgement about what is right, so it takes `べき`. `はず` would turn it
into a prediction that children are going to play outside more, which is not an opinion
anyone holds.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "That restaurant should be quiet on a weekday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あのレストランは平日(へいじつ)なら<b>静(しず)かなはず</b>です。

<code>静(しず)か</code> is a な-adjective, so it needs `な` before `はず`. Note that a
learner who has just met `かもしれない` will be tempted to write
❌<code>静(しず)かはず</code> by analogy - the two patterns take opposite forms.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 田中(たなか)さんは今日(きょう)、午後(ごご)から会議(かいぎ)はずですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 田中(たなか)さんは今日(きょう)、午後(ごご)から<b>会議(かいぎ)のはず</b>ですよ。

<code>会議(かいぎ)</code> and `はず` are both nouns, so they need `の` between them. Note
that the other repair a learner reaches for - ❌<code>会議(かいぎ)だはず</code> - is
equally wrong; `だ` is what the quotative `と` wants, not what `はず` wants.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山田(やまだ)さんは来(こ)ないはずです。
(b) 山田(やまだ)さんは来(こ)ないかもしれません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says you have a reason to expect his absence - he told you, or he is away, or the
rota says so. If someone challenges you, you are supposed to be able to name it, and if
he walks in you have been wrong about something.

(b) only concedes that his not coming is possible. No reason is implied and none is
owed; it is a warning not to count on him rather than a claim about what will happen.

</details>

# はずがない

**Meaning** · there's no way; it cannot possibly be - the negative twin of はずだ, and much stronger than it looks

`はず` is the noun "what follows from what I know". `はずがない` says there is **no such
noun to be had**: nothing in what I know leaves room for this. It is not "probably not".
It is a flat refusal to entertain the idea, and like `はずだ` it normally comes with a
reason attached.

## Formation

Identical to `はずだ`: `はず` is a noun, so な-adjectives take `な` and nouns take `の`.
Only the ending changes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + はずがない | 勝(か)つ → 勝(か)つはずがない |
| Verb (past) | た-form + はずがない | 知(し)っていた → 知(し)っていたはずがない |
| Verb (potential) | potential form + はずがない | 読(よ)める → 読(よ)めるはずがない |
| い-adjective | plain form + はずがない | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いはずがない |
| な-adjective | + な + はずがない | 簡単(かんたん)だ → 簡単(かんたん)なはずがない |
| Noun | + の + はずがない | 偶然(ぐうぜん) → 偶然(ぐうぜん)のはずがない |
| Polite | はずがありません／はずはありません | 勝(か)つはずがありません |
| Casual | はずない | 勝(か)つはずないよ |
| With contrastive `は` | はずはない | 勝(か)つはずはない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative goes on `ない`, not on the verb.
<code>来(く)るはずがない</code> = "there's no way he's coming."
<code>来(こ)ないはずがない</code> = "there's no way he *isn't* coming" - a double
negative, and almost never what a learner means.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こんな時間(じかん)に田中(たなか)さんが来(く)る<b>はずがない</b>。
There's no way Tanaka is coming at this hour.
</pre>

<pre>
その話(はなし)が本当(ほんとう)<b>のはずがありません</b>。
That story can't possibly be true.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ習(なら)っていないのだから、分(わ)かる<b>はずがない</b>よ。
They haven't been taught it yet, so of course they can't be expected to understand.
</pre>

<pre>
一週間(いっしゅうかん)で全部(ぜんぶ)覚(おぼ)えられる<b>はずがない</b>でしょう。
There's no way anyone could memorise the lot in a week.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに練習(れんしゅう)したのだから、失敗(しっぱい)する<b>はずがありません</b>。
She's practised that hard - there's no chance of her slipping up.
</pre>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)をかけて出(で)たのだから、誰(だれ)かが入(はい)れた<b>はずはない</b>。
I locked up when I left, so nobody can have got in.
</pre>

<pre>
先月(せんげつ)入(はい)ったばかりの人(ひと)がそんな事情(じじょう)を知(し)っていた<b>はずがありません</b>。
Someone who only joined last month couldn't possibly have known about that.
</pre>

<pre>
毎晩(まいばん)コーヒーを飲(の)んでいたら、よく眠(ねむ)れる<b>はずがない</b>よ。
If you drink coffee every night, no wonder you can't sleep properly.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`はずがない` is emphatic. On the scale that runs
<code>来(こ)ないかもしれません</code> → <code>来(こ)ないでしょう</code> →
<code>来(こ)ないはずです</code> → <code>来(く)るはずがありません</code>, it sits at
the far end: the last of those does not estimate a low probability, it rules the
possibility out.

That force gives it a temperature. `はずがない` is often said with feeling - indignation,
loyalty, disbelief - and <code>あの人(ひと)が犯人(はんにん)のはずがありません</code>
is a defence of someone, not a calculation. Used against another person's claim it is
combative: telling your manager
<code>そんなことがあるはずがありません</code> is close to "that's simply not the
case", and in a workplace you would usually reach for
<code>ないと思(おも)いますが</code> instead.

Register: `はずがない` is the neutral form and works in speech and writing.
`はずがありません` is its polite version. In casual speech the `が` drops - 
そんなはずないよ - which is a very common short reply. `はずはない` swaps `が` for
contrastive `は` and narrows the denial to this one point: whatever else may be true,
*that* cannot be.

## Contrast

### はずがない vs ないはずだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずがない` denies the **possibility**. `ないはずだ` expects the
**negative outcome**. The first says the idea is out of the question; the second says you
would be surprised.

</div>

<pre>
木村(きむら)さんがこの会(かい)に来(く)る<b>はずがない</b>。
There's no way Kimura would come to this.
</pre>

<pre>
木村(きむら)さんはこの会(かい)には来(こ)ない<b>はずだ</b>。
I don't think Kimura is coming to this one.
</pre>

Both end up predicting his absence, but only the first would make you argue with someone
who claimed to have seen him. `ないはずだ` is an ordinary expectation with ordinary
grounds - a message he sent, a scheduling clash - and it can be quietly wrong.
`はずがない` cannot be quietly wrong; if he walks in, something you believed was false.

### はずがない vs わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - very close in meaning. `はずがない` reasons from grounds;
`わけがない` tends to be blunter and more emotional, and belongs more to speech.

</div>

<pre>
この値段(ねだん)で本物(ほんもの)の<b>はずがありません</b>。
At this price it can't be genuine.
</pre>

<pre>
あんな話(はなし)、信(しん)じる<b>わけがない</b>でしょう。
As if anyone would believe a story like that.
</pre>

Swapping them rarely produces something wrong, but the flavour shifts: `わけがない`
carries a shrug or a snort with it, and is at home in an argument. `はずがない` keeps the
shape of an inference even when it is said with heat, which is why it is the safer of
the two in writing.

### はずがない vs ～ことができない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English "can't" is two things. "It is impossible that…" → `はずがない`.
"is unable to…" → the potential form or <code>ことができない</code>.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はまだ泳(およ)ぐことができません。
My little brother can't swim yet.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)が一人(ひとり)であそこまで泳(およ)いだ<b>はずがありません</b>。
There's no way my brother swam out that far on his own.
</pre>

The first is about his ability, the second about your belief. A sentence like
❌<code>弟(おとうと)は泳(およ)ぐはずがありません</code>, meant as "he can't swim",
comes out as "there is no possibility of him swimming", which sounds like you are
denying an accusation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そんな作業(さぎょう)が<b>簡単(かんたん)はずがない</b>。 → ✅ そんな作業(さぎょう)が<b>簡単(かんたん)なはずがない</b>。
  `はず` is a noun; a な-adjective in front of it keeps its `な`.

- ❌ これが彼(かれ)の<b>字(じ)はずがありません</b>。 → ✅ これが彼(かれ)の<b>字(じ)のはずがありません</b>。
  A noun links with `の`. The `の` is easy to lose when there is already another `の`
  earlier in the sentence.

- ❌ 祖母(そぼ)はパソコンを<b>使(つか)うはずがありません</b>。（meaning: she isn't able to）→ ✅ 祖母(そぼ)はパソコンを<b>使(つか)えません</b>。
  Inability is the potential form. `はずがない` is about what you refuse to believe, not
  about what someone is capable of.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が約束(やくそく)を<b>忘(わす)れないはずがない</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が約束(やくそく)を<b>忘(わす)れるはずがない</b>。
  Two negatives cancel. The first sentence means "there's no way he'd *fail* to forget",
  which is the opposite of the intended defence.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)自分(じぶん)で確認(かくにん)したのだから、間違(まちが)っている____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

間違(まちが)っている<b>はずがありません</b>。

The first clause supplies the grounds, and the conclusion is a flat denial rather than a
low estimate. <code>間違(まちが)っていないでしょう</code> would water down a reason you have just stated
outright.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 妹(いもうと)はまだ三(さん)さいなので、漢字(かんじ)が（読(よ)めません・読(よ)めるはずがありません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

妹(いもうと)はまだ三(さん)さいなので、漢字(かんじ)が<b>読(よ)めません</b>。

This is a plain statement about ability, so the potential negative is what you want.
<code>読(よ)めるはずがありません</code> would be arguing with someone who had suggested
she could - grammatical, but a different conversation.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "A room this small can't cost eighty thousand yen."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

こんなに狭(せま)い部屋(へや)が八万円(はちまんえん)も<b>するはずがありません</b>。

"Can't" here means "it is impossible that", so `はずがない` is right rather than a
potential form. <code>八万円(はちまんえん)のはずがありません</code> works too, with the
noun linked by `の`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの人(ひと)が嘘(うそ)はずがない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの人(ひと)が<b>嘘(うそ)をつくはずがない</b>。／あの人(ひと)の話(はなし)が<b>嘘(うそ)のはずがない</b>。

<code>嘘(うそ)</code> is a noun, so it cannot sit bare in front of `はず` - either link
it with `の`, or use the verb <code>嘘(うそ)をつく</code> and attach `はずがない` to
that instead.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)はその書類(しょるい)を見(み)ていないはずです。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)がその書類(しょるい)を見(み)たはずがありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an ordinary expectation: as far as you know the document never reached him. If it
turns out he did see it, you were simply working from incomplete information.

(b) rules it out. You are asserting that his having seen it is impossible - because the
file was locked, or because it was only printed this morning. Said in a meeting, (b)
contradicts whoever suggested otherwise, so it carries a good deal more weight and a
good deal more risk.

</details>

# かな

**Meaning** · I wonder - a question aimed at yourself, said out loud

`かな` is the question particle `か` plus the soft `な` you tack onto a thought you are
having rather than an enquiry you are making. Grammatically the sentence is a question;
socially it is not, because nobody has been asked. That gap is what makes `かな` so
useful: it lets you put a question into the room without putting anyone on the spot.

## Formation

Plain forms only. Nouns and な-adjectives drop `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + かな | 間(ま)に合(あ)う → 間(ま)に合(あ)うかな |
| Verb (past) | た-form + かな | 置(お)いた → どこに置(お)いたかな |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + かな | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないかな |
| い-adjective | plain form + かな | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いかな |
| な-adjective | drop だ + かな | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かかな |
| Noun | drop だ + かな | 明日(あした)だ → 明日(あした)かな |
| With a question word | どこ・何(なに)・いつ… + かな | いつ来(く)るかな |
| Drawn out | かなあ | 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)かなあ |
| Hoping | ～ないかな（あ） | 晴(は)れないかなあ |
| Softening an opinion | ～かなと思(おも)う | 早(はや)すぎるかなと思(おも)います |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かな` never follows `です`／`ます`, and never follows `だ`.
❌<code>静(しず)かだかな</code> → ✅<code>静(しず)かかな</code>. The polite way to wonder
aloud is `でしょうか`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
間(ま)に合(あ)う<b>かな</b>。
Will we make it in time, I wonder.
</pre>

<pre>
この服(ふく)、似合(にあ)う<b>かな</b>。
Does this outfit suit me?
</pre>

<pre>
あれ、かぎはどこに置(お)いた<b>かな</b>。
Hmm - where did I put the keys?
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の試合(しあい)、雨(あめ)で中止(ちゅうし)になる<b>かな</b>。
I wonder if tomorrow's match will be rained off.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょっと手伝(てつだ)ってもらえない<b>かな</b>。
Any chance you could give me a hand?
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)くバスが来(こ)ない<b>かなあ</b>。足(あし)が痛(いた)くなってきた。
I wish the bus would hurry up. My feet are starting to ache.
</pre>

<pre>
この色(いろ)、派手(はで)すぎる<b>かな</b>。地味(じみ)なほうがいいかもしれない。
Is this colour too loud? A plainer one might be better.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)に相談(そうだん)したほうがいい<b>かな</b>と思(おも)ったんですが、どう思(おも)いますか。
I was wondering whether I ought to raise it with my boss - what do you reckon?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`かな` is casual. It belongs with plain-form speech and with people you are relaxed
around; the polite way to wonder is `でしょうか`, and to a customer or a superior that is
what you should reach for. (In easy-going polite speech you will also hear `ですかね` and
`ますかね` - a blend of the two registers that works among colleagues but is too loose for
a formal setting.)

Its usefulness comes from the fact that it is *addressed to nobody*. Because you have
not asked, the listener is free to answer or not, and that makes `かな` the natural way
to float a request - <code>明日(あした)、車(くるま)貸(か)してもらえないかな</code> - or
to dissent gently: <code>それはちょっと違(ちが)うかな</code> is a good deal easier to
hear than <code>違(ちが)います</code>. The same trick works inside polite speech through
<code>かなと思(おも)います</code>, which is common even in careful ます-style
conversation.

`かなあ`, with the vowel stretched, is more wistful and more felt. On its own - 
どうかなあ。 - it can be a complete, non-committal answer meaning something like "hmm,
I'm not sure about that."

On who says it: `かな` is unmarked and used by speakers of every age and gender. Its
close relative `かしら` leans strongly female in modern speech, and `かね` leans older
and somewhat male. If you want one wondering form that never sounds odd coming out of
your mouth, `かな` is it.

## Contrast

### かな vs か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か` asks the **listener**. `かな` asks the **air**. Only one of them
obliges anybody to answer.

</div>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんは来(き)ます<b>か</b>。
Is Yamada coming?
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さん、来(く)る<b>かな</b>。
I wonder whether Yamada's coming.
</pre>

There is a second reason to prefer `かな` in casual speech: a plain-form question ending
in bare `か` - 来(く)るか。 - sounds abrupt and rather rough, more like a demand than a
question. Casual questions are normally marked by rising intonation alone
（来(く)る？）or softened with `かな`. Bare `か` after a plain form is not a neutral
casual question.

### かな vs かしら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different speaker profile. `かな` is the unmarked
choice; `かしら` leans distinctly female and slightly formal or old-fashioned.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は傘(かさ)、いる<b>かな</b>。
Will I need an umbrella today?
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は傘(かさ)、いる<b>かしら</b>。
Will I need an umbrella today?
</pre>

The sentences mean the same thing. What differs is what the listener infers about the
speaker - and these are tendencies, not rules: plenty of women use `かな` as their
default, and `かしら` is far more common in novels and drama than on the street.

### ～かな vs ～ないかな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - put a negative in front of `かな` and wondering turns into
**hoping**.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)、やむ<b>かな</b>。
I wonder if the rain will stop.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)、やまない<b>かなあ</b>。
I do wish the rain would stop.
</pre>

Literally the second asks "won't it stop?", but what it expresses is a wish. The
stretched `かなあ` and a falling, sighing delivery are the usual signals. Context still
matters: <code>来(こ)ないかな</code> can also be a straight negative wondering - "I
wonder if he isn't coming" - so listen for the tone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)、<b>静(しず)かだかな</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)、<b>静(しず)かかな</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns drop `だ` in front of `か`, just as they do before
  `かどうか` and `かもしれない`.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)、この漢字(かんじ)の読(よ)み方(かた)は<b>何(なに)かな</b>。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、この漢字(かんじ)の読(よ)み方(かた)は<b>何(なん)でしょうか</b>。
  `かな` is a casual, self-directed form. Pointed at a teacher it sounds as though you
  are talking past them.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、これ、<b>見(み)てもらえないかな</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、これ、<b>見(み)ていただけないでしょうか</b>。
  The indirect-request use of `かな` works downward and sideways, not upward.

- ❌ (in an email to a client) ご都合(つごう)はいかが<b>かな</b>。 → ✅ ご都合(つごう)はいかが<b>でしょうか</b>。
  `かな` has no place in writing that is not a message to a friend.

## Practice

**1.** 田中(たなか)さん、今日(きょう)は休(やす)み____。机(つくえ)に荷物(にもつ)がないね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は休(やす)み<b>かな</b>。

<code>休(やす)み</code> is a noun, so `かな` attaches straight on with no `だ`. The
second sentence supplies the observation that prompted the thought, which is exactly the
context `かな` lives in.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural form for speaking to a client: 来週(らいしゅう)の水曜日(すいようび)はご都合(つごう)が（いいかな・よろしいでしょうか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来週(らいしゅう)の水曜日(すいようび)はご都合(つごう)が<b>よろしいでしょうか</b>。

`かな` is plain-form casual and cannot be made polite. `でしょうか` is its polite
counterpart, and it does the same softening job while staying in ます／です style.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "I wonder if this shop is still open."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この店(みせ)、まだ<b>開(あ)いてるかな</b>。

Plain form in front of `かな`, and in speech
<code>開(あ)いている</code> usually contracts to <code>開(あ)いてる</code>. Nothing
polite may appear before the particle.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 課長(かちょう)、この書類(しょるい)、明日(あした)までに直(なお)してくれないかな。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 課長(かちょう)、この書類(しょるい)、明日(あした)までに<b>直(なお)していただけないでしょうか</b>。

The grammar is fine; the direction is not. <code>くれないかな</code> is a casual nudge
aimed at an equal or a junior, and pointing it at a section chief adds presumption to
informality.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 電話(でんわ)、かかってくるかな。
(b) 電話(でんわ)、かかってこないかなあ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is genuine uncertainty: you do not know whether the call will come, and you are
turning the question over. It would fit equally well if you were dreading the call.

(b) is a wish. The negative plus the drawn-out `かなあ` converts the wondering into
longing - you are waiting for that call and it is taking too long. The literal reading
"I wonder if it won't come" is available too, and only the tone tells them apart.

</details>

# かしら

**Meaning** · I wonder - the same wondering particle as かな, in a distinctly softer and more feminine key

`かしら` is a worn-down <code>か知(し)らぬ</code> - "I don't know whether…". Like `かな`
it turns a question inward, so that it hangs in the air rather than landing on anybody.
What separates the two is not meaning but **who is heard saying it**: in modern standard
Japanese `かしら` leans strongly female, and carries a slightly refined, slightly
old-fashioned air with it.

## Formation

Plain forms only, exactly as with `かな`. Nouns and な-adjectives drop `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + かしら | 届(とど)く → 届(とど)くかしら |
| Verb (past) | た-form + かしら | 閉(し)めた → 閉(し)めたかしら |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + かしら | 要(い)らない → 要(い)らないかしら |
| い-adjective | plain form + かしら | 重(おも)い → 重(おも)いかしら |
| な-adjective | drop だ + かしら | 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だ → 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)かしら |
| Noun | drop だ + かしら | どちら → どちらかしら |
| With a question word | どこ・どなた・いつ… + かしら | どなたかしら |
| Softened request | ～てくださらないかしら | 見(み)てくださらないかしら |
| With `ね` | かしらね | いらっしゃるかしらね |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かしら` sounds genteel but is **not polite**. It attaches to plain
forms, never to `です`／`ます`, and it does not belong in business speech or writing.
The polite way to wonder aloud is still `でしょうか`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの人(ひと)、どなた<b>かしら</b>。
Who is that, I wonder.
</pre>

<pre>
この花(はな)、また咲(さ)く<b>かしら</b>。
Will these flowers bloom again, do you think?
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を閉(し)めてきた<b>かしら</b>。心配(しんぱい)になってきたわ。
Did I shut the window? I'm starting to worry.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)はちゃんと食(た)べている<b>かしら</b>。
I do wonder whether my son is eating properly.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、この席(せき)、空(あ)いている<b>かしら</b>。
Excuse me - is this seat free?
</pre>

<pre>
どちらの色(いろ)がいい<b>かしら</b>。迷(まよ)ってしまうわ。
Which colour would be better? I can't make up my mind.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょっと手(て)を貸(か)してくださらない<b>かしら</b>。
Would you be so kind as to give me a hand?
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の集(あつ)まりには、あの方(かた)もいらっしゃる<b>かしら</b>ね。
I wonder whether she'll be at next week's gathering too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Two separate things are going on, and keeping them apart saves a lot of confusion.

**Register.** `かしら` is casual. It sits on plain forms and belongs to relaxed speech,
so despite its genteel ring it cannot be used where `です`／`ます` is required. What makes
it *sound* refined is the company it keeps: it pairs naturally with polite vocabulary - 
どなた, いらっしゃる, くださる, ご覧(らん)になる - and that combination produces the
soft, well-mannered effect people associate with it.

**Speaker profile.** In present-day standard Japanese `かしら` is heard mainly from women,
and more from older speakers than younger ones; many younger women use `かな` as their
default. In fiction and dubbing it is a stock signal for an elegant, mature or
old-fashioned female character. These are tendencies, not rules - men did use it freely
in earlier periods, and it survives in some regional and literary usage - but a learner
who adopts `かしら` as a neutral "I wonder" will be heard as marked. If you want a
wondering particle that raises no eyebrows, use `かな`.

`かしら` also does the two extra jobs `かな` does. With a negative it becomes a wish or a
hint - 早(はや)く春(はる)が来(こ)ないかしら - and in the form
<code>～てくださらないかしら</code> it is an unusually soft request. Note the softness has
a ceiling: it is gentle among equals, and still too informal to aim at a client.

## Contrast

### かしら vs かな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical grammar, identical meaning. The difference is entirely in
who is expected to say it: `かな` is unmarked, `かしら` leans female and a touch formal.

</div>

<pre>
この荷物(にもつ)、重(おも)すぎる<b>かな</b>。
Is this parcel too heavy, I wonder.
</pre>

<pre>
この荷物(にもつ)、重(おも)すぎる<b>かしら</b>。
Is this parcel too heavy, I wonder.
</pre>

Nothing in the content changes. `かな` would be unremarkable from anyone; `かしら` sketches
a speaker. Because the marking is one-directional - `かな` is safe for everybody,
`かしら` is not - the practical advice is to **recognise** `かしら` fluently and
**produce** `かな`, unless you are deliberately matching the speech style of the people
around you.

### かしら vs でしょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かしら` is *soft*; `でしょうか` is *polite*. Softness is not
politeness, and only one of them is safe upward.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)からだった<b>かしら</b>。
What time was the meeting again?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)からだった<b>でしょうか</b>。
Might I ask what time the meeting was again?
</pre>

The first is fine muttered to a colleague you know well and wrong addressed to a
director. `でしょうか` keeps you in ます／です style while still avoiding a blunt
question, which is why it is the workhorse of polite enquiry.

### ～かしら vs ～ないかしら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - as with `かな`, adding a negative turns the wondering into **hoping**
or into a **hint**.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん、気(き)がつく<b>かしら</b>。
I wonder whether Tanaka will notice.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)か気(き)がついてくれない<b>かしら</b>。
I do wish somebody would notice.
</pre>

The second is not really a question about anyone's likelihood of noticing; it is a wish,
and said in front of the right person it is a request that never had to be made. This
indirectness is the main reason `かしら` survives in polite female speech: it asks for
things without asking.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この説明(せつめい)で<b>十分(じゅうぶん)だかしら</b>。 → ✅ この説明(せつめい)で<b>十分(じゅうぶん)かしら</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns lose `だ` before `か`, in `かしら` exactly as in `かな` and
  `かどうか`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は<b>来(き)ますかしら</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は<b>来(く)るかしら</b>。／<b>いらっしゃるでしょうか</b>。
  `かしら` attaches to plain forms. Adding `ます` in front does not make it polite; it
  just breaks it.

- ❌ (in an email to a client) ご確認(かくにん)いただける<b>かしら</b>。 → ✅ ご確認(かくにん)いただけない<b>でしょうか</b>。
  However genteel it sounds, `かしら` is casual speech and reads as startlingly informal
  in business writing.

- ❌ A learner using `かしら` as their everyday "I wonder" in mixed company. → ✅ `かな`.
  Not an error of grammar but of self-presentation. `かしら` tells the listener something
  about you that you may not have meant to say.

## Practice

**1.** もう八時(はちじ)ね。あの店(みせ)、まだ開(あ)いている____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

まだ開(あ)いている<b>かしら</b>。

A plain-form verb takes `かしら` directly. The sentence is a thought said out loud rather
than a question put to anyone, which is precisely the slot this particle fills.

</details>

**2.** Choose the form suitable for asking your department head: 資料(しりょう)は明日(あした)までに（できるかしら・できるでしょうか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

資料(しりょう)は明日(あした)までに<b>できるでしょうか</b>。

`かしら` is soft but casual, and softness does not substitute for politeness when you are
speaking upward. `でしょうか` keeps the enquiry indirect while staying in polite style.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese using かしら: "I wonder whether this cake is sweet enough."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このケーキ、甘(あま)さは足(た)りている<b>かしら</b>。

An い-adjective or a plain verb attaches with nothing in between. Said while tasting
something you have made yourself, this is the classic setting for `かしら` - a question
addressed to nobody in particular.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 田中(たなか)さん、この辺(へん)は静(しず)かだかしら。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 田中(たなか)さん、この辺(へん)は<b>静(しず)かかしら</b>。

<code>静(しず)か</code> is a な-adjective, and its `だ` disappears before `か`. The habit
comes from <code>静(しず)かだと思(おも)う</code>, where the `だ` is required - `と`
and `か` behave in opposite ways.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 雪(ゆき)になるかな。
(b) 雪(ゆき)になるかしら。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The two sentences say the same thing: the speaker is wondering, out loud, whether it will
turn to snow. Neither is more or less certain than the other.

What differs is the picture the listener forms of the speaker. (a) is unmarked and could
come from anyone. (b) reads as female and slightly refined or old-fashioned - a tendency
rather than a rule, but a strong enough one that the choice carries information.

</details>

# かい

**Meaning** · turns a plain-form sentence into a yes/no question - friendly, familiar, and noticeably old-fashioned

`かい` is the question particle `か` with the edges rounded off. A plain-form sentence
ending in bare `か` sounds curt, almost like an interrogation; add the `い` and the same
question comes out warm - the tone of a parent asking a child how school went. That
warmth is the whole point of the particle, and it comes with a speaker profile attached.

## Formation

Plain forms only. Nouns and な-adjectives drop `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + かい | 行(い)く → 行(い)くかい |
| Verb (past) | た-form + かい | 終(お)わった → 終(お)わったかい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + かい | 要(い)らない → 要(い)らないかい |
| い-adjective | plain form + かい | 痛(いた)い → 痛(いた)いかい |
| な-adjective | drop だ + かい | 元気(げんき)だ → 元気(げんき)かい |
| Noun | drop だ + かい | 本当(ほんとう)だ → 本当(ほんとう)かい |
| Explanatory | ～のかい／～んかい | 来(き)たのかい |
| Question-word counterpart | ～んだい | どこへ行(い)くんだい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かい` is for **yes/no questions only**. With a question word in the
sentence you need `だい` instead: ❌<code>どこへ行(い)くかい</code> →
✅<code>どこへ行(い)くんだい</code>. The pair `かい`／`だい` splits the work that polite
`ですか` does on its own.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
元気(げんき)<b>かい</b>。
How are you keeping?
</pre>

<pre>
もう宿題(しゅくだい)は終(お)わった<b>かい</b>。
Have you finished your homework yet?
</pre>

<pre>
お茶(ちゃ)でも飲(の)む<b>かい</b>。
Fancy a cup of tea?
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)でここまで来(き)たの<b>かい</b>。
Did you come all this way on your own?
</pre>

<pre>
その荷物(にもつ)、重(おも)くない<b>かい</b>。持(も)とうか。
Isn't that bag heavy? Let me take it.
</pre>

<pre>
そう<b>かい</b>。それは大変(たいへん)だったね。
Is that right. That must have been rough.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりだね。仕事(しごと)のほうは順調(じゅんちょう)<b>かい</b>。
Long time no see. Is work going all right?
</pre>

<pre>
まだ諦(あきら)めていない<b>かい</b>。それなら、もう一度(いちど)やってみなさい。
So you haven't given up? In that case, have another go.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`かい` is plain-style and cannot be made polite: there is no ❌`かいです`, and `ますかい`
is not a form of standard Tokyo Japanese. If you need politeness, the question is
`ですか`／`ますか`.

Its social shape matters more than its grammar. In modern standard Japanese `かい` leans
**male and older**, and it leans **downward or sideways** - a parent to a child, a
grandparent to a grandchild, a teacher to a pupil, a senior colleague being kind to a
junior. Younger speakers use it rarely in earnest; where they do, it is often a
deliberate impression of that older voice. Translated fiction and dubbing are full of it
for exactly this reason: it is the standard way to make a character sound avuncular. As
always these are tendencies rather than rules, but they are strong enough that a learner
using `かい` with a superior will sound not merely informal but oddly patronising.

The default casual yes/no question in ordinary modern speech is not `かい` at all - it is
the plain form said with a rise: 行(い)く？／食(た)べた？ `かい` is the marked,
warmer, older-sounding alternative to that.

One expression worth knowing on its own: `そうかい`. Said with interest it means "oh
really?"; said flat it means "oh, is that so" and sounds dismissive, even sulky. The
words are identical, so listen to the delivery.

## Contrast

### かい vs だい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かい` answers *yes* or *no*. `だい` answers a **question word**. They
are a matched pair and cannot be swapped.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)も来(く)る<b>かい</b>。
Are you coming tomorrow as well?
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は何時(なんじ)に来(く)る<b>んだい</b>。
What time are you coming tomorrow?
</pre>

`だい` normally follows the explanatory `の`／`ん`, which is why you see `んだい` far more
often than bare `だい` after a verb; after a noun it stands alone - 
名前(なまえ)は何(なん)だい。 Both particles carry the same familiar, male-leaning,
somewhat dated flavour, so they turn up together in the mouth of the same character.

### かい vs か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare `か` on a plain form is **blunt**; `かい` is the same question
made **kind**.

</div>

<pre>
分(わ)かった<b>か</b>。
Got that?
</pre>

<pre>
分(わ)かった<b>かい</b>。
Did that make sense?
</pre>

The first is the tone of an instructor or a superior checking compliance, and it can land
as a challenge. The second checks the same fact with none of the pressure. This is worth
remembering in reverse too: bare plain-form `か` is not a neutral casual question, and a
learner reaching for informality often produces something much sharper than intended.

### かい vs rising intonation

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the everyday casual question is the plain form with a rise. `かい`
adds warmth **and** a generation.

</div>

<pre>
これ、食(た)べる？
Are you going to eat this?
</pre>

<pre>
これ、食(た)べる<b>かい</b>。
Would you like this?
</pre>

Both are casual and both are friendly. The difference is that the first is unmarked in
any age group, while the second sketches an older speaker addressing someone younger. If
you are a learner in your twenties talking to friends, the rise is what you want; keep
`かい` for recognising, and for the moments when you are consciously playing the older
role.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>どこで買(か)ったかい</b>。 → ✅ <b>どこで買(か)ったんだい</b>。
  A question word rules `かい` out. `かい` is strictly yes/no; `だい` handles the rest.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)は<b>心配(しんぱい)だかい</b>。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)は<b>心配(しんぱい)かい</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns lose `だ` in front of `か`, as they do before every other
  `か`-based ending.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、もう<b>お帰(かえ)りかい</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、もう<b>お帰(かえ)りですか</b>。
  `かい` is friendly, but it is friendly *downward*. Aimed upward it manages to be
  informal and condescending at the same time.

- ❌ 明日(あした)も<b>来(き)ますかい</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)も<b>来(く)るかい</b>。／<b>来(き)ますか</b>。
  `かい` attaches to plain forms. There is no polite version of it - you switch particles
  instead.

## Practice

**1.** （おじいさんが孫(まご)に）そんなに走(はし)って、疲(つか)れていない____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

疲(つか)れていない<b>かい</b>。

A yes/no question in plain style, asked kindly and downward - the exact situation `かい`
exists for. Bare <code>疲(つか)れていないか</code> would sound like a sergeant checking
on a recruit.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)は（どこへ行(い)ったかい・どこへ行(い)ったんだい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)はどこへ<b>行(い)ったんだい</b>。

The sentence contains どこ, so it is not a yes/no question and `かい` cannot be used.
`だい` is the question-word member of the pair, and it normally follows the explanatory
`ん`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese in the `かい` register: "Is that lunch enough for you?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

その昼(ひる)ごはんで足(た)りる<b>かい</b>。

A plain-form verb takes `かい` directly. The English "for you" needs no pronoun - the
particle itself makes clear you are asking the person in front of you.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 先生(せんせい)、この問題(もんだい)の答(こた)えは三番(さんばん)かい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先生(せんせい)、この問題(もんだい)の答(こた)えは三番(さんばん)<b>ですか</b>。

The form is well built, but the direction is wrong. `かい` is used by the senior party to
the junior one; a student asking a teacher needs plain `ですか`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう帰(かえ)るのか。
(b) もう帰(かえ)るのかい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is blunt. Depending on the delivery it can be a neutral checking of a fact, but it
easily reads as displeasure - "you're leaving *already*?" - and it leans distinctly
masculine and rough.

(b) asks the same thing gently. The `い` takes the sharpness out and adds a note of
concern, as though the speaker is older than the listener and mildly sorry to see them
go.

</details>

# じゃないか

**Meaning** · isn't it? - an assertion wearing the clothes of a negative question

`じゃないか` is not really a question. It states something and dares the listener to
disagree: *look, it's raining* / *I did tell you* / *hey, that's rather good*. The
negative-question shape is what makes it feel shared rather than imposed - you are
inviting the other person to notice what you have noticed. Which of its several flavours
you get depends almost entirely on tone and situation.

## Formation

Attaches to plain forms. Nouns and な-adjectives drop `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + じゃないか | 言(い)った → 言(い)ったじゃないか |
| い-adjective | plain form + じゃないか | いい → いいじゃないか |
| な-adjective | drop だ + じゃないか | 上手(じょうず)だ → 上手(じょうず)じゃないか |
| Noun | drop だ + じゃないか | 嘘(うそ)だ → 嘘(うそ)じゃないか |
| Polite | じゃないですか | 言(い)ったじゃないですか |
| Formal spoken | ではありませんか | 言(い)ったではありませんか |
| Written / rhetorical | ではないか | 言(い)ったではないか |
| Very casual | じゃん | いいじゃん |
| Softer, rising | じゃない？ | 上手(じょうず)じゃない？ |
| Floating a suspicion | ～んじゃないか | 間違(まちが)っているんじゃないか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `だ` in front. ❌<code>嘘(うそ)だじゃないか</code>. The `じゃ` is
already the contracted <code>では</code> of the copula, so the copula cannot appear twice.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ほら、やっぱり雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた<b>じゃないか</b>。
See - it's started raining after all.
</pre>

<pre>
いい<b>じゃないか</b>。一杯(いっぱい)くらい。
Oh, come on. One drink won't hurt.
</pre>

<pre>
あれ、財布(さいふ)、ここにあった<b>じゃない</b>。
Oh - the wallet was here all along.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)、遅(おく)れるなって言(い)った<b>じゃないか</b>。
I told you yesterday not to be late, didn't I.
</pre>

<pre>
なかなか上手(じょうず)<b>じゃないか</b>。いつ習(なら)ったんだ？
Hey, that's not bad at all. When did you learn?
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)の前(まえ)に大(おお)きな本屋(ほんや)があった<b>じゃないですか</b>。あそこが閉(し)まるそうです。
You know that big bookshop in front of the station? Apparently it's closing.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなに急(いそ)がなくてもいい<b>じゃないですか</b>。まだ時間(じかん)はありますよ。
There's really no need to rush, is there. We've still got time.
</pre>

<pre>
この説明(せつめい)だと、読(よ)む人(ひと)が混乱(こんらん)するん<b>じゃないでしょうか</b>。
Wouldn't this explanation leave the reader confused?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The forms make a ladder, and the rungs are far apart.

`じゃないか` with the `か` said out loud is blunt and, in casual speech, leans male.
`じゃない` with a rise - 安(やす)いじゃない？ - is the wider-used everyday version and
sounds softer. `じゃん` is very casual and youthful. Going the other way,
`じゃないですか` is the ordinary polite form, `ではありませんか` is formal and a little
theatrical, and `ではないか` belongs to written argument, where it works as a rhetorical
"is it not the case that…?" rather than as a question anybody answers.

Three things it does, all recognisable in context:

- **Pointing out** - you have spotted something the listener has not.
  ここに書(か)いてあるじゃないですか。
- **Reproach or reminder** - you said this before and were ignored.
  だから気(き)をつけろと言(い)ったじゃないか。
- **Discovery** - pleased surprise. 意外(いがい)とおいしいじゃないか。

A warning about the polite form. `じゃないですか` is often used to open a topic by
presenting something as shared knowledge - <code>去年(きょねん)、地震(じしん)があったじゃないですか</code>.
That is fine when the listener genuinely does know. Used about things they could not
possibly know - <code>私(わたし)、甘(あま)い物(もの)が苦手(にがて)じゃないですか</code> - 
it is widely criticised in Japan as pushy, because it assigns the listener knowledge they
never had. Say <code>んですが</code> instead.

## Contrast

### じゃないか vs plain じゃない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same syllables, two different sentences. Falling `じゃない` is a plain
**negative**. Rising `じゃない`, and anything with `か` attached, is an **assertion seeking
agreement**.

</div>

<pre>
これは私(わたし)のかばん<b>じゃない</b>。
This isn't my bag.
</pre>

<pre>
これ、私(わたし)のかばん<b>じゃない</b>？
Hang on - this is my bag, isn't it?
</pre>

Adding `か` removes the ambiguity entirely: <code>私(わたし)のかばんじゃないか</code>
can only be the second reading. In writing, where intonation is unavailable, the question
mark or the `か` is doing all the work, so leave it in.

### じゃないか vs でしょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both fish for agreement. `でしょう` assumes the listener **already
knows** and asks them to confirm. `じゃないか` asserts and **presses**, often implying they
had overlooked it.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)話(はな)した<b>でしょう</b>？
I mentioned it yesterday, remember?
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)話(はな)した<b>じゃないか</b>。
I did tell you yesterday!
</pre>

The first is a reminder; the second is close to an accusation. That extra push is why
`じゃないか` is risky upward: telling a manager
<code>間違(まちが)っているじゃないですか</code> is not "isn't this wrong?" but "this is
wrong and you missed it."

### じゃないか vs ～んじゃないか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare `じゃないか` asserts something you are **sure** of. `んじゃないか`
floats a **suspicion** you are not sure of at all.

</div>

<pre>
この数字(すうじ)、間違(まちが)っている<b>じゃないか</b>。
This figure is wrong - look.
</pre>

<pre>
この数字(すうじ)、間違(まちが)っている<b>んじゃないか</b>。
I've a feeling this figure might be wrong.
</pre>

The `ん` turns the whole thing tentative, and the polite
<code>んじゃないでしょうか</code> is one of the most useful phrases in a Japanese
workplace: it raises a doubt without accusing anyone of anything. When you want to
question a superior's work, this is the form to reach for.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この辺(へん)は<b>静(しず)かだじゃないですか</b>。 → ✅ この辺(へん)は<b>静(しず)かじゃないですか</b>。
  `じゃ` is already the copula. な-adjectives and nouns attach bare.

- ❌ もう<b>終(お)わりましたじゃないですか</b>。 → ✅ もう<b>終(お)わったじゃないですか</b>。
  The clause in front is plain. `です` on the end carries all the politeness the sentence
  needs.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)、この計算(けいさん)、<b>違(ちが)うじゃないですか</b>。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、この計算(けいさん)、<b>違(ちが)うんじゃないでしょうか</b>。
  Polite in form, confrontational in force. Adding `ん` and `でしょう` converts an
  accusation into a query.

- ❌ 私(わたし)、北海道(ほっかいどう)の出身(しゅっしん)<b>じゃないですか</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)、北海道(ほっかいどう)の出身(しゅっしん)<b>なんですが</b>。
  You cannot present your own biography as something the listener already knows. This
  misuse is a well-known irritant.

## Practice

**1.** ほら、傘(かさ)を持(も)っていけと言(い)った____。びしょぬれだよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傘(かさ)を持(も)っていけと言(い)った<b>じゃないか</b>。

The speaker warned the listener earlier and was ignored, which is the reproach use.
`でしょう` would soften it into a mere reminder, and the drenched listener makes clear
this is not a mere reminder.

</details>

**2.** Choose the safer form for pointing out a possible error in your boss's document:
この日付(ひづけ)、（違(ちが)うじゃないですか・違(ちが)うんじゃないでしょうか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この日付(ひづけ)、<b>違(ちが)うんじゃないでしょうか</b>。

`じゃないですか` asserts the error and implies it was overlooked. The `ん` plus
`でしょうか` presents the same observation as a doubt of your own, which leaves the other
person room to check it themselves.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "Look, there's a light on - someone's still in the office."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ほら、電気(でんき)がついている<b>じゃないか</b>。まだ誰(だれ)か残(のこ)っているよ。

The first clause is a discovery you are drawing the listener's attention to, which is
exactly what `じゃないか` marks. In a softer register you could say
<code>ついているじゃない？</code> with a rise.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの店(みせ)のラーメン、けっこう有名(ゆうめい)だじゃないですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの店(みせ)のラーメン、けっこう<b>有名(ゆうめい)じゃないですか</b>。

<code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is a な-adjective, and `じゃ` is the contracted
<code>では</code> of the copula, so putting `だ` in front doubles it up. Compare
<code>有名(ゆうめい)だと思(おも)います</code>, where the `だ` is required.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は知(し)らないじゃないですか。
(b) 彼(かれ)は知(し)らないんじゃないでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) asserts that he does not know and treats it as obvious - the sort of thing you say to
someone who has just proposed asking him. It carries a note of "you should have realised
that", so it can sting.

(b) offers his not knowing as a possibility you have thought of. It commits to nothing
and leaves the decision with the listener, which makes it the form to use when you are
speaking upward or when you might turn out to be wrong.

</details>
