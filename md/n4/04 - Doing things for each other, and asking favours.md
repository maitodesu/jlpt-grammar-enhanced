# N4 - Doing things for each other, and asking favours


---

# ～てあげる

**Meaning** · do something *for* someone - the action itself is the gift, and it travels away from the speaker

At N5 you learned that `あげる` hands an **object** to someone outside your circle.
`～てあげる` hands over an **action** instead. The direction rule is identical; what
is new is that naming an act as a favour puts it on the record, and that can be
generous or it can be smug, depending on who you say it to.

## Formation

Attach `あげる` to the て-form. `あげる` then conjugates as an ordinary る-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + あげる | 作(つく)る → 作(つく)ってあげる |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + あげます | 貸(か)す → 貸(か)してあげました |
| Verb (offering) | て-form + あげよう／あげましょうか | 運(はこ)ぶ → 運(はこ)んであげましょうか |
| To someone above you (humble) | て-form + さしあげる | 案内(あんない)する → 案内(あんない)してさしあげる |
| To someone below you / an animal | て-form + やる | 買(か)う → 買(か)ってやる |

The particle on the person who benefits is **not** decided by `あげる`. It is
decided by the verb underneath, exactly as it would be without `あげる` attached:

| Underlying verb frame | Particle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb already takes 人(ひと)に | に | 友(とも)だち<b>に</b>道(みち)を教(おし)えてあげる |
| Verb already takes 人(ひと)を | を | 子(こ)ども<b>を</b>迎(むか)えに行(い)ってあげる |
| Verb acts on the person's possession | の | 母(はは)<b>の</b>荷物(にもつ)を持(も)ってあげる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the benefit must land **outside** the speaker. There is no
❌<code>私(わたし)に教(おし)えてあげる</code>. If the favour comes toward you or your family,
the verb you need is <code>教(おし)えてくれる</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
妹(いもうと)に本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)んであげた</b>。
I read my little sister a book.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)が重(おも)そうだったので、<b>持(も)ってあげました</b>。
The bags looked heavy, so I carried them for her.
</pre>

<pre>
お母(かあ)さんに<b>電話(でんわ)してあげて</b>。心配(しんぱい)してるよ。
Give your mum a call. She's worried.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が分(わ)からないと言(い)うので、駅(えき)まで<b>案内(あんない)してあげた</b>。
He said he didn't know the way, so I walked him to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)の自転車(じてんしゃ)を<b>直(なお)してあげたら</b>、とても喜(よろこ)んだ。
When I fixed my son's bike for him, he was delighted.
</pre>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)に漢字(かんじ)の読(よ)み方(かた)を<b>教(おし)えてあげる</b>約束(やくそく)をしました。
I promised to teach the exchange student how to read kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきたから、隣(となり)のおばあさんの洗濯物(せんたくもの)を<b>取(と)り込(こ)んであげました</b>。
It started raining, so I brought in the laundry for the old lady next door.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で考(かんが)えさせたほうがいいので、答(こた)えは<b>教(おし)えてあげません</b>でした。
It's better to make him think for himself, so I didn't give him the answer.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**`～てあげる` announces the favour.** That is its entire function, and it is why
the pattern needs handling. <code>荷物(にもつ)を持(も)ちましょうか</code> is a
plain offer to carry a bag. <code>荷物(にもつ)を持(も)ってあげましょうか</code> adds
"…as a kindness to you", and the person hearing it now has a debt on the table
they did not ask for. Between friends this is warm. Aimed at a stranger, a
customer or anyone senior, it lands as condescending.

The safe habit: **do not say `～てあげる` to the person receiving the favour.**
Say it *about* the favour afterwards, or to a third party.

- To the person: 手伝(てつだ)いましょうか。／お手伝(てつだ)いしましょうか。
- About it later: 昨日(きのう)、田中(たなか)さんの引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを手伝(てつだ)ってあげた。

`さしあげる` does **not** rescue the situation. It is grammatically the humble
form, but announcing to a superior that you have bestowed something on them is
worse, not better - the humility is in the word and the presumption is in the
construction. Real Japanese reaches for the honorific-humble frame instead:
お持(も)ちします, ご案内(あんない)します, お貸(か)しします. Keep
`さしあげる` for narrating in the third person
（部長(ぶちょう)にお土産(みやげ)を買(か)ってさしあげた）and even there it
is slightly stiff.

`～てやる` is the downward member of the family - for animals, plants, small
children, one's own younger siblings - and it is blunter and more male-marked.

There is a second, sharper `～てあげる`: said with a flat tone about something the
listener does not want, it turns sarcastic or threatening.
本当(ほんとう)のことを言(い)ってあげようか - "shall I *do you the favour* of
telling you the truth?" The mechanism is the same: you are insisting that this is
a kindness when both of you know it is not.

In casual speech the contraction is `～たげる`:
買(か)ってあげる → 買(か)ったげる, 見(み)ておいてあげる →
見(み)といたげる. Common in the Kansai area and in relaxed speech generally;
never write it.

## Contrast

### ～てあげる vs ～てくれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same event, opposite direction. `～てあげる` when the favour
leaves your side; `～てくれる` when it arrives at your side.

</div>

The subject of both is the person **doing** the action. What changes is where the
benefit ends up relative to the うち／そと line you learned at N5.

<pre>
私(わたし)が山田(やまだ)さんの仕事(しごと)を<b>手伝(てつだ)ってあげた</b>。
I helped Yamada with his work.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんが私(わたし)の仕事(しごと)を<b>手伝(てつだ)ってくれた</b>。
Yamada helped me with my work.
</pre>

The line is your family, not just your body. If a teacher explains something to
your daughter, that favour has come toward your side, so it is
娘(むすめ)に説明(せつめい)してくださいました - never ❌してあげました, which
would push your own daughter into the そと group.

### ～てあげる vs the plain verb

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the plain verb reports *what happened*. `～てあげる` reports
*who it was for*. If the beneficiary is not the point, leave it off.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)、弟(おとうと)の部屋(へや)を<b>片(かた)づけました</b>。
I tidied my brother's room yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)、弟(おとうと)の部屋(へや)を<b>片(かた)づけてあげました</b>。
I tidied my brother's room for him yesterday.
</pre>

Both are correct. The second one is the version you say when you want credit, or
when you are complaining that you had to. This is not a subtlety learners can
skip: overusing `～てあげる` is one of the clearest markers of a foreign speaker,
because English "for him" is neutral and Japanese `～てあげる` is not.

### ～てあげる vs ～させてあげる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てあげる` means you *did* it for them. `～させてあげる`
means you *let them* do it, as a kindness.

</div>

English "let him" and "did it for him" are far apart; in Japanese they are one
suffix apart, and learners reach for the wrong one.

<pre>
子(こ)どもに絵(え)を<b>描(か)いてあげた</b>。
I drew a picture for my child.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもに自由(じゆう)に絵(え)を<b>描(か)かせてあげた</b>。
I let my child draw whatever they liked.
</pre>

The causative `～させてあげる` is extremely common with permission
（休(やす)ませてあげる, 遊(あそ)ばせてあげる）and carries the same warmth - 
and the same risk of sounding lofty about your own generosity.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんが私(わたし)に地図(ちず)を書(か)いて<b>あげました</b>。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんが私(わたし)に地図(ちず)を書(か)いて<b>くれました</b>。
  A favour arriving at the speaker can never be `～てあげる`. This is the N5
  あげる／くれる rule, unchanged.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、この資料(しりょう)をコピーして<b>さしあげましょうか</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、この資料(しりょう)をコピー<b>しましょうか</b>。／お持(も)ちしましょうか。
  Offering a superior a *labelled* favour is presumptuous even in the humble form.
  Drop the giving verb and use the plain offer or the お～します frame.

- ❌ 友(とも)だち<b>に</b>手伝(てつだ)ってあげました。 → ✅ 友(とも)だち<b>を</b>手伝(てつだ)ってあげました。
  `あげる` does not assign the particle. <code>手伝(てつだ)う</code> takes 人(ひと)を, so the
  person keeps `を`.

- ❌ 傘(かさ)を貸(か)し<b>あげます</b>。 → ✅ 傘(かさ)を貸(か)し<b>てあげます</b>。
  `あげる` attaches to the て-form, not the ます-stem.

- ❌ 母(はは)が妹(いもうと)にセーターを編(あ)んで<b>あげました</b>。（telling an outsider）→ ✅ 母(はは)が妹(いもうと)にセーターを編(あ)んで<b>くれました</b>。
  Both are inside your family, so from your chair the favour landed on your side.
  `あげる` here would sound as though neither of them were related to you.

## Practice

**1.** 荷物(にもつ)が多(おお)かったので、後輩(こうはい)のかばんを持(も)っ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

持(も)っ<b>てあげました</b>。

The favour goes from the speaker outward to a junior colleague, so `～てあげる` is
the right direction. Note the `の` on <code>後輩(こうはい)</code>: the verb acts on the
bag, and the owner of the bag is marked with `の`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 課長(かちょう)が新(あたら)しいやり方(かた)を教(おし)えて（あげました・くださいました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

課長(かちょう)が新(あたら)しいやり方(かた)を<b>教(おし)えてくださいました</b>。

The section chief is the one acting and the benefit reaches the speaker, so the
verb must be the toward-me one, in its honorific form. `あげました` would reverse
the direction and put the speaker above the boss.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll look after the dog for you while you're away."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

留守(るす)の間(あいだ)、犬(いぬ)の世話(せわ)を<b>してあげるよ</b>。

Said to a close friend this is warm and normal. To an acquaintance you would drop
the giving verb - 犬(いぬ)の世話(せわ)をしておきますよ - because announcing
the favour to its recipient is what makes `～てあげる` sound self-satisfied.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 先生(せんせい)、重(おも)そうですね。荷物(にもつ)を持(も)ってあげましょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先生(せんせい)、重(おも)そうですね。お持(も)ち<b>しましょうか</b>。

The grammar is fine and the effect is rude: you are informing your teacher that
you are about to be generous to them. Swapping in `さしあげましょうか` does not
help. The natural offer upward is the humble お + ます-stem + します frame.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けました。
(b) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けてあげました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral report of an action: the window got opened, and nothing is said
about who wanted it open.

(b) frames the same action as a service performed for somebody - you opened it
*for* them, because they were hot or could not reach. It implies a specific
beneficiary who is not you, and depending on tone it can carry either kindness or
a hint of "and you're welcome". If the beneficiary is the person you are speaking
to, (a) is almost always the better choice.

</details>

# ～てくれる

**Meaning** · someone does something for me, or for my side - and I am grateful for it

`～てくれる` is the action counterpart of the `くれる` you met at N5. Someone else
performs the verb, and the good of it arrives at the speaker's うち. Unlike
`～てあげる`, this one is not risky to say: marking a favour that came *to* you is
simply politeness, and leaving it off can sound cold.

## Formation

Attach `くれる` to the て-form. The doer is the subject; the beneficiary - usually
<code>私(わたし)</code> - is normally left unsaid, because the verb has already said it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + くれる | 手伝(てつだ)う → 手伝(てつだ)ってくれる |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + くれます | 送(おく)る → 送(おく)ってくれました |
| Verb (negative) | て-form + くれない | 返事(へんじ)する → 返事(へんじ)してくれない |
| From someone above you (honorific) | て-form + くださる → くださいます | 直(なお)す → 直(なお)してくださいました |
| Casual request | て-form + くれない？／くれる？ | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ってくれない? |

The sentence frame is <code>Aが（私(わたし)に）～てくれる</code>. As with plain
`くれる`, the particle on the beneficiary comes from the underlying verb - 
私(わたし)に教(おし)えてくれる but 私(わたし)を待(ま)ってくれる.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てくれる` can never have the speaker as the doer. There is
no ❌<code>私(わたし)が田中(たなか)さんに貸(か)してくれた</code>. Nothing can move
toward you while you are the one doing it.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the ます-form of `くださる` is irregular: <b>くださいます</b>, not
❌くださります. And the `ください` you have used since your first week is nothing
more than the imperative of this verb - <code>待(ま)ってください</code> is literally
"give me the favour of waiting".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
父(ちち)が駅(えき)まで<b>送(おく)ってくれました</b>。
My dad drove me to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
だれが窓(まど)を<b>閉(し)めてくれた</b>の?
Who closed the window for me?
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)が作文(さくぶん)を<b>直(なお)してくださいました</b>。
My teacher kindly corrected my essay.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ課(か)の人(ひと)が仕事(しごと)を<b>代(か)わってくれて</b>、助(たす)かりました。
Someone in my section covered my shift, which was a real help.
</pre>

<pre>
だれも<b>教(おし)えてくれなかった</b>から、会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)が変(か)わったのを知(し)らなかった。
Nobody told me, so I didn't know the meeting time had changed.
</pre>

<pre>
妻(つま)が毎朝(まいあさ)お弁当(べんとう)を<b>作(つく)ってくれる</b>ので、昼(ひる)ごはんに困(こま)りません。
My wife makes me a packed lunch every morning, so lunch is never a problem.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)が熱(ねつ)を出(だ)したとき、隣(となり)の人(ひと)が病院(びょういん)まで車(くるま)で<b>運(はこ)んでくださいました</b>。
When my son had a fever, our neighbour was kind enough to drive us to the hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しいのに最後(さいご)まで話(はなし)を<b>聞(き)いてくれて</b>、本当(ほんとう)にありがとう。
Thank you so much for hearing me out to the end even though you were busy.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～てくれる` is **gratitude built into the grammar**. Japanese has no polite way to
report a favour received without it. If a colleague fixes your spreadsheet and you
tell someone
田中(たなか)さんが直(なお)しました, you have described an event; add
`くれました` and you have acknowledged a kindness. Omitting it where it belongs
sounds ungrateful in a way that has no English equivalent, so learners should err
toward using it.

The single most useful frame built on it is
<code>～てくれて、ありがとう</code> - the standard way to thank someone for an
**action**（来(き)てくれてありがとう）, as opposed to
<code>～をありがとう</code> for a thing. Polite version:
<code>～てくださって、ありがとうございます</code>. Also common:
<code>～てくれて助(たす)かりました</code>, "you saved me."

The **negative** is where the emotion flips. `～てくれない` / `～てくれなかった`
is a complaint, not a neutral report: it says the favour was owed and did not
arrive. 誰(だれ)も手伝(てつだ)ってくれなかった means "nobody would help me",
with the resentment audible. Compare the bare
誰(だれ)も手伝(てつだ)いませんでした, which is a flat statement of fact.

In the **question** form `～てくれない?` the same negative becomes a soft casual
request - the first rung of the request ladder that runs on up through
`～てくれませんか`, `～てもらえませんか` and `～ていただけませんか`.

Register notes: `くれる` is plain, `くれます` polite, `くださる` honorific.
`くださる` is used for a superior's kindness toward you and is completely standard
in business speech. The plain imperative `くれ`（待(ま)ってくれ）is rough,
male-marked and abrupt - fine among close male friends, never to a stranger. In
fast speech `～てくれる` reduces to `～てくれ` + rising tone or, very casually,
見(み)せてくんない? Do not write that.

One extension worth knowing: `～てくれる` can be used with things and weather by
personification, and it is warm rather than childish - 
天気(てんき)が持(も)ってくれてよかった, "thankfully the weather held out for
us."

## Contrast

### ～てくれる vs ～てもらう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one event, two chairs. `～てくれる` seats the **doer** as
subject; `～てもらう` seats the **beneficiary** as subject.

</div>

<pre>
友(とも)だちが引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを<b>手伝(てつだ)ってくれました</b>。
My friend helped me move.
</pre>

<pre>
友(とも)だちに引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを<b>手伝(てつだ)ってもらいました</b>。
I got my friend to help me move.
</pre>

Both are natural and describe the same afternoon. The difference is initiative and
warmth. `くれる` presents the friend as acting of his own accord and reads as
thanks. `もらう` presents the speaker as having arranged it, which is why it is
the form you use when you asked, hired, or requested. Notice the particle flip on
the friend: `が` as subject, `に` as source.

### ～てくれる vs ～てくださる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `くださる` is `くれる` with a status gap. Use it when the
person doing you the favour is above you, or is a customer.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が空港(くうこう)まで<b>迎(むか)えに来(き)てくださいました</b>。
The department head came to meet me at the airport.
</pre>

Using plain `くれました` about a superior is grammatical but flat, and in front of
that superior or their colleagues it sounds disrespectful. In private grumbling to
a friend it is perfectly normal - the choice tracks the audience as much as the
person.

### ～てくれる vs ～てあげる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `くれる` is safe to say to anyone; `あげる` is not.

</div>

They are mirror images grammatically, but not socially. Thanking someone with
<code>手伝(てつだ)ってくれてありがとう</code> is warm. Telling that same person
<code>手伝(てつだ)ってあげたよ</code> is smug, because you are the one naming your
own act a favour.

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんが会場(かいじょう)まで地図(ちず)を<b>書(か)いてくれた</b>。
Tanaka drew me a map to the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんに会場(かいじょう)までの地図(ちず)を<b>書(か)いてあげた</b>。
I drew Tanaka a map to the venue.
</pre>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)が友(とも)だちに自転車(じてんしゃ)を貸(か)して<b>くれました</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)が友(とも)だちに自転車(じてんしゃ)を貸(か)して<b>あげました</b>。
  `くれる` cannot take the speaker as the doer. When the favour leaves you, it is
  `あげる`.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)が説明(せつめい)して<b>くださりました</b>。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)が説明(せつめい)して<b>くださいました</b>。
  <code>くださる</code> has an irregular ます-form, like おっしゃる → おっしゃいます.

- ❌ 来(き)てくれて<b>ありがとうを言(い)います</b>。 → ✅ 来(き)てくれて<b>ありがとう</b>。
  Thanks for an action is expressed with <code>～てくれてありがとう</code> directly;
  there is no need to build a sentence around the word ありがとう.

- ❌ 母(はは)が私(わたし)に作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はおいしいです。 → ✅ 母(はは)が作(つく)ってくれた料理(りょうり)はおいしいです。
  Not ungrammatical, but a Japanese speaker would hear the missing `くれる` as
  oddly detached about their own mother's cooking.

- ❌ 手伝(てつだ)って<b>くれ</b>、お願(ねが)いします。 → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)って<b>ください</b>。／手伝(てつだ)って<b>くれない</b>?
  Bare `くれ` is a rough male command and clashes with a polite frame.

## Practice

**1.** 友(とも)だちが空港(くうこう)まで迎(むか)えに来(き)て____ので、タクシーに乗(の)らずにすみました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

迎(むか)えに来(き)て<b>くれた</b>ので…

The friend acted and the benefit reached the speaker, so `くれる` is required. It
also does real work in the sentence: it is what tells the listener the trip to the
airport was a kindness rather than a coincidence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部長(ぶちょう)が推薦状(すいせんじょう)を書(か)いて（くれました・くださいました）。You are telling a colleague at work.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)が推薦状(すいせんじょう)を書(か)いて<b>くださいました</b>。

A department head is above you, and you are speaking in a workplace register, so
the honorific `くださる` is the expected choice. `くれました` is not wrong Japanese,
but about a superior at work it sounds careless.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Thank you for waiting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>待(ま)っていてくれて、ありがとう</b>。／ polite: <b>お待(ま)ちくださって、ありがとうございます</b>。

Thanks for an *action* is built on <code>～てくれて</code>, not on a noun. Note the
`ていて`: the person was in the state of waiting, so <code>待(ま)っていて</code> is
more natural than the bare <code>待(ま)って</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山田(やまだ)さんは私(わたし)に日本語(にほんご)を教(おし)えてあげました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山田(やまだ)さんは私(わたし)に日本語(にほんご)を教(おし)えて<b>くれました</b>。

The teaching arrives at the speaker, so the direction verb must be `くれる`. As
written, the sentence claims Yamada did the speaker a favour *away from* the
speaker, which is impossible.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 誰(だれ)も手伝(てつだ)いませんでした。
(b) 誰(だれ)も手伝(てつだ)ってくれませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral fact: no help occurred. It could describe a task nobody was
expected to join.

(b) adds the speaker's stake. Because `くれる` frames help as something that should
have come toward them, its negative says the help was owed and withheld - "nobody
would lift a finger for me." It is a complaint, and native listeners hear the
resentment clearly.

</details>

# ～てもらう

**Meaning** · get someone to do something for you; have something done for your benefit

`～てもらう` describes the same favour as `～てくれる`, but from the receiving
chair. The person who benefits becomes the **subject** of the sentence, and the
person who actually does the work is demoted to a `に` phrase. That
re-arrangement is the whole difficulty: English "I had my hair cut" hides the
hairdresser, and Japanese puts them back in with `に`.

## Formation

Attach `もらう` to the て-form. The frame is
<code>Bは Aに ～てもらう</code> - B receives, A does.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + もらう | 直(なお)す → 直(なお)してもらう |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + もらいます | 送(おく)る → 送(おく)ってもらいました |
| From someone above you (humble) | て-form + いただく | 見(み)る → 見(み)ていただきました |
| Potential (used for requests) | て-form + もらえる／いただける | 来(く)る → 来(き)てもらえますか |
| Negative potential (polite request) | て-form + もらえませんか | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ってもらえませんか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the doer takes `に`. Not `が`, which would make them the
receiver, and not `を`, which is reserved for the thing acted on.

</div>

`から` is possible instead of `に` when the favour is information or an object
moving from a source, and it is preferred when the giver is an organisation:
大学(だいがく)<b>から</b>説明(せつめい)してもらいました. With most action verbs,
`に` is the default.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the beneficiary is normally the speaker or the speaker's side, exactly
as with `くれる`. 田中(たなか)さんは山田(やまだ)さんに手伝(てつだ)ってもらった
is fine when you are narrating about a third party, but it quietly puts you in
Tanaka's camp for the length of the sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
友(とも)だちに写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)ってもらった</b>。
I got a friend to take a photo of me.
</pre>

<pre>
駅員(えきいん)さんに行(い)き方(かた)を<b>教(おし)えてもらいました</b>。
I had the station attendant tell me how to get there.
</pre>

<pre>
パソコンが動(うご)かなくなったので、弟(おとうと)に<b>見(み)てもらった</b>。
My laptop stopped working, so I got my brother to take a look at it.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)、美容院(びよういん)で髪(かみ)を<b>切(き)ってもらう</b>予定(よてい)です。
I'm getting my hair cut at the salon next week.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に書類(しょるい)にサインを<b>していただきました</b>。
I had the department head sign the documents.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの日(ひ)は後輩(こうはい)に車(くるま)を<b>出(だ)してもらう</b>つもりです。
I'm planning to get a junior colleague to lend me his car on moving day.
</pre>

<pre>
書(か)き方(かた)が分(わ)からなかったので、窓口(まどぐち)の人(ひと)にゆっくり<b>説明(せつめい)していただきました</b>。
I didn't know how to fill it in, so I had the clerk at the counter explain it slowly.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で決(き)められないなら、一度(いちど)専門家(せんもんか)に<b>相談(そうだん)に乗(の)ってもらった</b>ほうがいいですよ。
If you can't decide on your own, you'd do better to get a specialist to talk it through with you.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**`～てもらう` implies the receiver had a hand in it.** Not always a spoken
request - sometimes just consent, or having gone somewhere for the service - but
the event is not a bolt from the blue. That is why
髪(かみ)を切(き)ってもらった is the normal way to say you had a haircut: you
went to the salon and asked. Compare
急(きゅう)に雨(あめ)がやんでくれた, where nothing was arranged, and only
`くれる` works.

Because the receiver is the subject, `～てもらう` is also the natural frame for
**planning and arranging**: 明日(あした)、田中(たなか)さんに来(き)てもらいます
means "I'll have Tanaka come" - the speaker is organising it. Said about a person
present, this can sound managerial, so it belongs downward or sideways, not upward.

`～ていただく` is the humble form and is one of the workhorses of business
Japanese. Whole registers of office speech are built on it:
ご確認(かくにん)いただけますでしょうか, 少々(しょうしょう)お待(ま)ちいただきます.
Note the honorific frame <code>お／ご + stem + いただく</code>
（お待(ま)ちいただく, ご記入(きにゅう)いただく）, which is politer still than
<code>待(ま)っていただく</code> and very common on signs and announcements.

The potential forms are where this pattern earns its keep in daily life.
`もらえる` means "can receive the favour", which is exactly what a request is:

- <code>～てもらえる?</code> - casual, to a friend
- <code>～てもらえますか</code> - polite, everyday
- <code>～てもらえませんか</code> - politer; the negative leaves room to refuse
- <code>～ていただけませんか</code> - humble, business standard
- <code>～ていただけないでしょうか</code> - the top of the ladder

Register: `もらう` is neutral in plain and polite speech alike, with no
male/female marking. `いただく` belongs to formal and business situations; using
it with close friends sounds like you are being funny on purpose. Casually
`もらえる?` often contracts in speech to `もらえん?` in western dialects - regional,
not standard.

## Contrast

### ～てもらう vs ～てくれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもらう` says *I got them to*; `～てくれる` says *they
did it for me*. Initiative sits with the subject.

</div>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)に留守(るす)の間(あいだ)猫(ねこ)の世話(せわ)を<b>してもらいました</b>。
I got a neighbour to look after the cat while I was away.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)が留守(るす)の間(あいだ)猫(ねこ)の世話(せわ)を<b>してくれました</b>。
A neighbour looked after the cat for me while I was away.
</pre>

The first says you arranged it. The second says they stepped in - and it carries
more warmth, which is why thanks are almost always phrased with `くれる`:
世話(せわ)をしてくれてありがとう. してもらってありがとう is occasionally heard
in casual speech, but it is not the standard shape: gratitude points at what the
other person did, so the giving verb is the one that belongs there.

There is a hard limit on `もらう`. If the doer acted entirely on their own and the
event was not arranged, `もらう` is unnatural:
知(し)らない人(ひと)が財布(さいふ)を拾(ひろ)ってくれた - a stranger
picked up my dropped wallet - cannot become ❌拾(ひろ)ってもらった unless you
had asked them to.

### ～てもらう vs the causative ～させる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are English "have someone do it". `～てもらう` is
*asking* and the doer is willing; `～させる` is *making* and the speaker has
authority.

</div>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)に買(か)い物(もの)に<b>行(い)ってもらいました</b>。
I got my son to go and do the shopping (and thanks, son).
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)に買(か)い物(もの)に<b>行(い)かせました</b>。
I made my son go and do the shopping.
</pre>

Because `もらう` builds in gratitude, it softens an instruction; because `させる`
asserts power, it is unusable upward and sounds harsh even downward. Combining
both is how you ask for permission politely:
<code>説明(せつめい)させていただきます</code>, "allow me to explain."

### ～てもらう vs ～てほしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもらう` reports a favour that happened or is arranged;
`～てほしい` states a wish that has not.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)までに返事(へんじ)を<b>もらいたい</b>んですが。
I'd like to get an answer by next week.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)までに返事(へんじ)を<b>してほしい</b>んですが。
I'd like you to answer by next week.
</pre>

The desiderative <code>～てもらいたい</code> sits between them: it is a wish
expressed through the receiving frame, and it is a shade more indirect - and
therefore softer - than `～てほしい`, because it talks about what you would
receive rather than about what the other person should do.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 友(とも)だち<b>が</b>手伝(てつだ)ってもらいました。 → ✅ 友(とも)だち<b>に</b>手伝(てつだ)ってもらいました。
  With `もらう` the doer takes `に`. `が` would make the friend the one receiving
  the favour.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)に推薦状(すいせんじょう)を書(か)いて<b>もらいました</b>。（in a formal report）→ ✅ 先生(せんせい)に推薦状(すいせんじょう)を書(か)いて<b>いただきました</b>。
  Receiving a favour from someone above you takes the humble `いただく` in any
  formal setting.

- ❌ 助(たす)けてもらって<b>ありがとうございました</b>。 → ✅ 助(たす)けて<b>くださって</b>ありがとうございました。
  Thanks point at the *giver's* act, so the giving verb is the one that fits.

- ❌ 知(し)らない人(ひと)に道(みち)を教(おし)えてもらった。（when they volunteered）→ ✅ 知(し)らない人(ひと)が道(みち)を教(おし)えてくれた。
  Unrequested help is `くれる` territory. `もらう` implies you asked.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)に会議(かいぎ)に出(で)てもらいます。（said to the department head）→ ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に会議(かいぎ)にご出席(しゅっせき)いただけますでしょうか。
  Arranging a superior's actions out loud sounds managerial. Turn it into a
  request with the humble potential.

## Practice

**1.** カメラを持(も)っていなかったので、通(とお)りかかった人(ひと)____撮(と)ってもらいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

通(とお)りかかった人(ひと)<b>に</b>撮(と)ってもらいました。

The doer of the favour is marked with `に` in a `もらう` sentence. The subject slot
belongs to the speaker, who is the one receiving the service.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お店(みせ)に電話(でんわ)して調(しら)べて<b>もらいました</b>。

The speaker rang up and asked, so the favour was arranged, and the shop is marked

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Could I have you explain it once more?" (to a client)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう一度(いちど)ご説明(せつめい)<b>いただけますでしょうか</b>。／もう一度(いちど)説明(せつめい)して<b>いただけませんか</b>。

Upward, `もらう` becomes `いただく`, and the potential `いただける` in a negative or
でしょうか question is what turns the receiving frame into a polite request. The
お／ご + noun + いただく version is the more formal of the two.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)、友(とも)だち<b>に</b>宿題(しゅくだい)を手伝(てつだ)ってもらいました。
（or ✅ 昨日(きのう)、友(とも)だちが宿題(しゅくだい)を手伝(てつだ)って<b>くれました</b>。）

The sentence mixes the two frames. Either keep `もらう` and mark the friend with

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山田(やまだ)さんに空港(くうこう)まで送(おく)ってもらいました。
(b) 山田(やまだ)さんが空港(くうこう)まで送(おく)ってくれました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both describe Yamada driving the speaker to the airport, and both are natural.

(a) puts the speaker in the subject slot and implies the lift was arranged - you
asked, or it had been agreed in advance. (b) puts Yamada in the subject slot and
presents the lift as something he did for you, which foregrounds his kindness. If
you are thanking him, (b) is the frame you want; if you are explaining your travel
plans to someone else, (a) is more informative.

</details>

# ～てやる

**Meaning** · do something for someone below you - and, in a second life, *I'll show them*: a declaration of defiant intent

`～てやる` is the bottom rung of the `さしあげる／あげる／やる` ladder you met at
N5, now carrying an action instead of an object. It points **downward**: to
animals, plants, small children, your own younger family. Alongside that, it has a
completely separate use with no beneficiary at all, where it means "just watch me
do it" - and that second use is far more common in real speech than textbooks let
on.

## Formation

Attach `やる` to the て-form. `やる` is a regular う-verb: やります, やった,
やらない, やろう.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (favour, downward) | て-form + やる | 買(か)う → 買(か)ってやる |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + やります | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んでやります |
| Verb (offer / resolve) | て-form + やろう | 開(あ)ける → 開(あ)けてやろう |
| Verb (resolve, plain) | て-form + やる（no `に` phrase） | 勝(か)つ → 勝(か)ってやる |
| Contracted, very casual | て-form + たる | 買(か)ってやる → 買(か)ったる |

In the favour sense the particles come from the underlying verb, exactly as with
`～てあげる`: 弟(おとうと)<b>に</b>説明(せつめい)してやる,
犬(いぬ)<b>を</b>散歩(さんぽ)に連(つ)れて行(い)ってやる.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the sentence has a `に` phrase naming who benefits, it is
the favour `～てやる`. If it has no beneficiary and the verb is about the speaker's
own achievement, it is the resolve `～てやる`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
犬(いぬ)を散歩(さんぽ)に<b>連(つ)れて行(い)ってやった</b>。
I took the dog for a walk.
</pre>

<pre>
猫(ねこ)が外(そと)に出(で)たがっているから、窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けてやろう</b>。
The cat wants to go out, so I'll open the window for her.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)が泣(な)いていたので、アイスを<b>買(か)ってやった</b>。
My little brother was crying, so I bought him an ice cream.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちが寝(ね)る前(まえ)に、毎晩(まいばん)絵本(えほん)を<b>読(よ)んでやります</b>。
Every night I read the children a picture book before bed.
</pre>

<pre>
今度(こんど)こそ絶対(ぜったい)に<b>勝(か)ってやる</b>。
This time I'm definitely going to win.
</pre>

<pre>
見(み)ていろよ、いつか<b>見返(みかえ)してやる</b>。
Just you wait - one day I'll show them all.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな会社(かいしゃ)、いつか<b>やめてやる</b>と思(おも)いながら働(はたら)いている。
I go to work thinking that one day I'm going to walk out of this place.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)が大学(だいがく)に受(う)かったら、好(す)きな自転車(じてんしゃ)を<b>買(か)ってやろう</b>と思(おも)っています。
If my son gets into university, I'm thinking I'll buy him whatever bike he wants.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The favour sense is rough, and it is age- and gender-marked.** `やる` for people
was once the ordinary form; it now sounds blunt, and it is noticeably more common
in men's speech and in older speakers. A father saying
宿題(しゅくだい)を見(み)てやる about his own child is completely normal and
even affectionate. The same sentence to a colleague's child would be presumptuous.

Toward animals and plants it is entirely neutral - 
花(はな)に水(みず)をやる is the standard phrase, though younger speakers
increasingly say あげる there too, and some older speakers find *that* precious.
Both are heard; neither will get you into trouble.

Where you must not use it: **anyone senior, anyone outside your circle, and
anyone you are being polite to.** `～てやります` exists but does not fix the
problem, because the rudeness lives in `やる`, not in the politeness ending. There
is no upward version - the ladder goes やる → あげる → さしあげる, and the top rung
has its own difficulties (see `～てあげる`, which is presumptuous for a different
reason).

**The resolve sense** is the one you will hear in dramas, sports and complaints.
It attaches to a verb about the speaker's own action, has no beneficiary, and
means roughly "I'm going to do it, and let that be noted." The emotional colour
comes from the same source as the favour sense: you are casting your own act as
something imposed on the world.

- 絶対(ぜったい)に合格(ごうかく)してやる。 - I *will* pass.
- こんな仕事(しごと)、辞(や)めてやる。 - I quit. (defiant)
- 文句(もんく)を言(い)ってやった。 - I gave them a piece of my mind.

That last type shades into threat: 殴(なぐ)ってやる is "I'll hit you", not a
kindness. Tone and context do all the disambiguating, and it is almost always
obvious.

Casually - and especially in Kansai speech - `～てやる` contracts to `～たる`:
買(か)ってやる → 買(か)ったる, 貸(か)してやる → 貸(か)したる. You will
hear it constantly in comedy and in the west of the country; do not write it, and
do not use it in an exam.

<div class="note">

**Note** - `～てやる` is also how you tell someone to do something for a third
party who is below them: 犬(いぬ)にえさを<b>やってください</b>,
弟(おとうと)に教(おし)えて<b>やって</b>ください. The polite frame is on the
outside and the downward frame on the inside, and this is normal, not
contradictory.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～てやる vs ～てあげる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same direction, different altitude. `～てあげる` is for
equals and outsiders; `～てやる` is for those clearly below you, and for animals.

</div>

<pre>
娘(むすめ)の髪(かみ)を<b>結(むす)んでやった</b>。
I tied my daughter's hair for her.
</pre>

<pre>
同僚(どうりょう)の仕事(しごと)を<b>引(ひ)き受(う)けてあげた</b>。
I took on a colleague's work for them.
</pre>

Swap those and both sentences go wrong in opposite ways: `あげる` about your own
small child sounds oddly formal, as if she were a guest; `やる` about a colleague
sounds contemptuous. When you are not sure of the altitude, `あげる` is the safer
error - but remember that neither should be said *to* the beneficiary's face.

### ～てやる (resolve) vs ～てみせる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both declare determination. `～てやる` aims the declaration at
the world; `～てみせる` aims it at an audience: *I'll show you*.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)は一位(いちい)を<b>取(と)ってやる</b>。
Next year I'm taking first place.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)は一位(いちい)を<b>取(と)ってみせる</b>。
Next year I'll show you I can take first place.
</pre>

`～てみせる` promises a demonstration to someone who doubts you; `～てやる` is
closer to spite or grit and does not need a doubter. They combine freely - 
必(かなら)ず勝(か)ってみせてやる is heavy-handed but grammatical.

### ～てやる vs ～てさしあげる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are the two ends of one vertical scale, and in modern
speech **both ends are avoided**. The safe middle is `～てあげる`, or no giving
verb at all.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)の奥(おく)さまを駅(えき)まで<b>お送(おく)りしました</b>。
I saw the department head's wife to the station.
</pre>

`さしあげる` is grammatically the humble upward form, but announcing your own
kindness to a superior is presumptuous no matter how humble the verb, so speakers
use the お + stem + します frame instead. At the other end, `やる` is fine within
the family and rude outside it. The lesson is the same in both directions: the
higher the stakes, the less Japanese wants you to name the favour.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 先輩(せんぱい)のコピーを取(と)って<b>やりました</b>。 → ✅ 先輩(せんぱい)のコピーを取(と)って<b>おきました</b>。
  `やる` points downward. Used about a senior it is close to insulting, and the
  polite ending does not soften it.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、荷物(にもつ)を持(も)って<b>やりましょうか</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、お荷物(にもつ)を<b>お持(も)ちしましょうか</b>。
  Upward offers use the humble お + ます-stem + します frame, with no giving verb.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)を宿題(しゅくだい)を見(み)てやった。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)の宿題(しゅくだい)を見(み)てやった。
  `やる` does not assign particles. The verb acts on the homework, so the owner
  takes `の` and only one `を` survives.

- ❌ 絶対(ぜったい)に合格(ごうかく)して<b>やります</b>。（in a formal speech）→ ✅ 絶対(ぜったい)に合格(ごうかく)<b>したい</b>と思(おも)っています。
  The resolve sense is emotional and confrontational. It does not belong in
  formal or public register even with `ます` on the end.

- ❌ 母(はは)が私(わたし)にセーターを編(あ)んで<b>やりました</b>。 → ✅ 母(はは)が私(わたし)にセーターを編(あ)んで<b>くれました</b>。
  `やる` sends the favour away from the speaker, so it can never land on the
  speaker. Toward you it is always `くれる`, however junior the doer.

## Practice

**1.** 息子(むすこ)が自転車(じてんしゃ)に乗(の)れないので、後(うし)ろを持(も)っ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

後(うし)ろを持(も)っ<b>てやった</b>。（or 持(も)っ<b>てあげた</b>）

The beneficiary is the speaker's own young child, which is `やる` territory:
downward, inside the family, and affectionate rather than rude. `あげた` is also
perfectly acceptable and is what many younger parents say.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 課長(かちょう)に資料(しりょう)を届(とど)けて（やりました・差(さ)し上(あ)げました・きました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

課長(かちょう)に資料(しりょう)を届(とど)けて<b>きました</b>。

`やる` is rude upward, and `さしあげる`, though humble in form, announces your own
kindness to a superior. The natural sentence drops the giving verb altogether - 
here <code>届(とど)けてきました</code>, "I went and delivered it."

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll never give up. I'm going to finish this."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

絶対(ぜったい)にあきらめない。<b>やり遂(と)げてやる</b>。（or <b>最後(さいご)までやってやる</b>）

This is the resolve `～てやる`: no beneficiary, the speaker's own action, and a
defiant edge. In polite speech you would soften it to
<code>やり遂(と)げたいと思(おも)います</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 田中(たなか)さんが忙(いそが)しそうだったので、仕事(しごと)を手伝(てつだ)ってやりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 田中(たなか)さんが忙(いそが)しそうだったので、仕事(しごと)を手伝(てつだ)い<b>ました</b>。

Tanaka is a peer, not someone below you, so `やる` is out of place and sounds
scornful. <code>手伝(てつだ)ってあげました</code> is possible when narrating to a third party, but the
plainest version, with no giving verb, is what you would actually say.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 必(かなら)ず終(お)わらせます。
(b) 必(かなら)ず終(お)わらせてやる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain polite commitment: the work will be finished. It is what you say to
a manager, and it carries no emotion beyond reliability.

(b) is the resolve `～てやる`. It adds defiance - against a deadline, a doubter, or
the situation itself - and it is plain, emphatic and slightly aggressive. You mutter
it to yourself or say it among friends; saying it to your boss would sound like you
were picking a fight.

</details>

# ～てほしい

**Meaning** · I want *you* (or someone else) to do something

At N5 you learned two ways to want: `～たい` for an action you will perform
yourself, and `ほしい` for an object. `～てほしい` fills the remaining square - 
an action performed by **somebody else**. The wish is still yours, so the
first-person restriction you learned with `たい` carries over unchanged.

## Formation

Attach `ほしい` to the て-form. `ほしい` is an い-adjective and conjugates like one.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ほしい | 手伝(てつだ)う → 手伝(てつだ)ってほしい |
| Verb (negative wish) | ない-form + でほしい | 言(い)う → 言(い)わないでほしい |
| Polite | ～てほしいです | 来(く)る → 来(き)てほしいです |
| Past | ほしい → ほしかった | 分(わ)かる → 分(わ)かってほしかった |
| Negative | ほしい → ほしくない | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れてほしくない |
| Softened request | ～てほしいんですが／～てほしいんだけど | 見(み)る → 見(み)てほしいんですが |
| Third person's wish | ～てほしいと言(い)っている／～てほしがっている | 直(なお)す → 直(なお)してほしいと言(い)っています |

The person you want to act takes `に`. The wisher is the topic, and as usual is
dropped when it is you:
（私(わたし)は）部長(ぶちょう)<b>に</b>確認(かくにん)してほしい。

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - negatives split. "I want you *not* to do it" is
<code>～ないで<b>ほしい</b></code>; "I *don't* want you to do it" is
<code>～て<b>ほしくない</b></code>. The first is a request, the second a rejection,
and learners routinely swap them.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the subject of `～てほしい` does not have to be a person.
雨(あめ)がやんでほしい and 電車(でんしゃ)が時間(じかん)どおりに来(き)てほしい
are both natural: you can wish an outcome onto the world. With inanimate subjects
the particle is `が`, not `に`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ちょっと<b>待(ま)ってほしい</b>。
I want you to wait a moment.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は<b>晴(は)れてほしい</b>な。
I hope it clears up tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
これは誰(だれ)にも<b>言(い)わないでほしい</b>。
I don't want you telling anyone about this.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)にはもっと外(そと)で<b>遊(あそ)んでほしい</b>。
I'd like my son to play outside more.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かに<b>してほしい</b>んですが、下(した)の階(かい)の人(ひと)に言(い)いにくくて。
I'd like them to keep it down, but it's hard to say anything to the people downstairs.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)に<b>分(わ)かってほしかった</b>ことが、うまく伝(つた)えられなかった。
I couldn't get across the thing I wanted my boss to understand.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)は金曜日(きんようび)までに<b>出(だ)してほしい</b>んですが、間(ま)に合(あ)いますか。
I'd like the meeting materials in by Friday - will you manage it?
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに楽(たの)しい時間(じかん)が<b>終(お)わらないでほしい</b>と心(こころ)から思(おも)った。
I found myself wishing with all my heart that this happy time would never end.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**`～てほしい` is direct.** It states, out loud, what you want another person to
do. Between friends and family, and downward at work, that is ordinary. Aimed
upward it is too bald - you are placing a demand on someone senior - and the
polite ending does not help, because the directness is in the construction, not
in the ending. ❌部長(ぶちょう)、確認(かくにん)してほしいです is grammatical
and will make the room uncomfortable.

The standard softeners, from lightest to heaviest:

- <code>～てほしいんだけど</code> / <code>～てほしいんですが</code> - trails off and
  leaves the ask implied. Very common.
- <code>～てほしいなあ</code> - turns the demand into a wistful wish. Friendly.
- <code>～てもらいたい</code> - the receiving frame; a shade more indirect, because
  it talks about what you would receive rather than what they should do.
- <code>～ていただきたい</code> - the humble version, and the normal business form:
  ご確認(かくにん)いただきたいのですが。
- <code>～ていただけませんか</code> - a question rather than a statement, and the
  politest of the set.

The **negative wish** `～ないでほしい` is worth practising, because English "I
want you not to…" is rare and learners avoid the structure.
心配(しんぱい)しないでほしい, 無理(むり)をしないでほしい - both are warm,
not scolding.

**Third person.** As with `たい`, you cannot flatly assert someone else's wish in
a main clause: ❌妹(いもうと)は父(ちち)に来(き)てほしいです. Route it
through reported speech or evidence - 
来(き)てほしいと言(い)っています,
来(き)てほしいようです. The form `～てほしがっている` exists but is much less
natural than the simple `ほしがる` for objects; most speakers avoid it.

Register: `～てほしい` is plain, `～てほしいです` polite, and neither is
gender-marked. In writing - opinion essays, editorials - it is extremely common
as a way of stating what society or government ought to do:
国(くに)にはもっと説明(せつめい)してほしい。

## Contrast

### ～てほしい vs ～たい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～たい` is *I* do it; `～てほしい` is *you* do it. Same wish,
different actor.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)、京都(きょうと)へ<b>行(い)きたい</b>。
I want to go to Kyoto next week.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)、京都(きょうと)へ<b>行(い)ってほしい</b>。
I want you to go to Kyoto next week.
</pre>

Both are い-adjectives and both are restricted to the speaker's own wishes. The
difference is only who does the verb - but English "want" collapses the two, so
the error is common and the resulting sentence is badly wrong rather than merely
odd.

### ～てほしい vs ～てもらいたい・～ていただきたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, rising politeness. `てほしい` states the wish
bare; `てもらいたい` routes it through receiving; `ていただきたい` does that
humbly.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)までに<b>返事(へんじ)してほしい</b>。
I want an answer by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)までに<b>返事(へんじ)していただきたい</b>のですが。
I would like to receive your answer by tomorrow.
</pre>

The second is what you write in an email to a client. Note that `もらいたい` and
`いただきたい` are the desiderative of `もらう`／`いただく` - verbs, so their
subject is you, the receiver, while the other person keeps `に`. The particle
pattern is identical to `てほしい`, which makes them easy to substitute.

### ～てほしい vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` tells someone to do it; `～てほしい` says you
want it done. A wish is not an instruction, and the two are not
interchangeable upward.

</div>

<pre>
ここに名前(なまえ)を<b>書(か)いてください</b>。
Please write your name here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここに名前(なまえ)を<b>書(か)いてほしい</b>んです。
I'd like you to write your name here.
</pre>

`ください` is polite in form but is still an imperative, which is why it is fine
from staff to customer and awkward from junior to superior. `～てほしいんですが`
is softer because it stops short of telling anyone to do anything - it puts the
wish on the table and lets the listener pick it up.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 友(とも)だちに来(き)<b>たいです</b>。 → ✅ 友(とも)だちに<b>来(き)てほしいです</b>。
  `たい` can only describe an action *you* perform. When somebody else does the
  verb, the wish takes `～てほしい`.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは私(わたし)に手伝(てつだ)っ<b>てほしいです</b>。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは私(わたし)に手伝(てつだ)っ<b>てほしいと言(い)っています</b>。
  A third person's wish cannot be asserted flatly, exactly as with `たい`.

- ❌ 心配(しんぱい)し<b>てほしくない</b>。（meaning "please don't worry"）→ ✅ 心配(しんぱい)し<b>ないでほしい</b>。
  <code>～てほしくない</code> rejects the wish; <code>～ないでほしい</code> wishes for
  the non-action. Only the second one asks them to stop worrying.

- ❌ 社長(しゃちょう)、この書類(しょるい)を見(み)<b>てほしいです</b>。 → ✅ 社長(しゃちょう)、この書類(しょるい)を見(み)<b>ていただけませんか</b>。
  Grammatical, and far too direct upward. The humble request form is what the
  situation calls for.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)<b>に</b>やんでほしい。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>が</b>やんでほしい。
  `に` marks a person you are asking to act. An inanimate subject takes `が`.

## Practice

**1.** うるさいので、もう少(すこ)し静(しず)かに話(はな)し____ほしいです。

`ほしい` attaches to the て-form when the wish is about somebody else's action.
Note that the polite `です` sits on `ほしい`, because <code>ほしい</code> is an
い-adjective and carries the sentence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部長(ぶちょう)、明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)に（出(で)てほしいです・ご出席(しゅっせき)いただけませんか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Stating a wish about a superior's behaviour is too direct, however polite the

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)のことは<b>心配(しんぱい)しないでほしい</b>。

The wish is for a *non-action*, so it takes <code>～ないでほしい</code>.
<code>心配(しんぱい)してほしくない</code> would be understood, but it reads as

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 弟(おとうと)は母(はは)に新(あたら)しいゲームを買(か)ってほしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 弟(おとうと)は母(はは)に新(あたら)しいゲームを買(か)って<b>ほしがっています</b>。
（more natural: ✅ …買(か)って<b>ほしいと言(い)っています</b>。）

`ほしい` is an い-adjective of desire, and Japanese does not let you assert another
person's inner state directly in a plain main clause. Report it, or mark it as
observed.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)までに返事(へんじ)をください。
(b) 明日(あした)までに返事(へんじ)してほしいんですが。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an instruction. `ください` is polite in form but imperative in force, so it
suits a situation where you are entitled to ask - a shop to a customer, a teacher
to a class, a manager to a team.

(b) states a need and stops. Because it never tells the listener to act, it leaves
them room to negotiate the deadline, and the trailing `んですが` invites them to
respond. It is what you use sideways or upward, where issuing instructions would
be out of place.

</details>

# ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか

**Meaning** · could you do that for me? - the everyday polite request, built on the giving-and-receiving verbs

A Japanese request is not an imperative with "please" stuck on it. It is a
question about a **favour**: either "will you give me this action?"
（`～てくれませんか`）or "could I receive this action?"（`～てもらえませんか`）.
Once you see that, the whole ladder from
`～てくれない?` up to `～ていただけないでしょうか` is one structure with the
politeness dial turned.

## Formation

Both families take the て-form. `くれる` is used as it stands; `もらう` goes into
the **potential**, `もらえる`, because you are asking whether receiving the favour
is possible.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (giving frame) | て-form + くれる? | 開(あ)ける → 開(あ)けてくれる? |
| Verb (giving frame, negative) | て-form + くれない? | 貸(か)す → 貸(か)してくれない? |
| Verb (giving frame, polite) | て-form + くれませんか | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ってくれませんか |
| Verb (receiving frame) | て-form + もらえる? | 来(く)る → 来(き)てもらえる? |
| Verb (receiving frame, polite) | て-form + もらえますか | 手伝(てつだ)う → 手伝(てつだ)ってもらえますか |
| Verb (receiving frame, politest) | て-form + もらえませんか | 見(み)る → 見(み)てもらえませんか |
| Negative request | ない-form + でくれませんか／でもらえませんか | 入(はい)る → 入(はい)らないでもらえませんか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - two dials, and they turn independently.
**Dial 1: frame.** `くれ-` asks the listener to give; `もらえ-` asks whether you
may receive. Receiving is more indirect, so `もらえ-` is politer than `くれ-` at
every matching rung.
**Dial 2: negative.** A negative question - `くれない?`, `くれませんか`,
`もらえませんか` - is politer than the affirmative, because it builds in room to
say no.

</div>

Put the two dials together and you get the ladder learners need memorised as a
single sequence:

| Rung | Form | Who you say it to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 待(ま)って | close friends, family; blunt |
| 2 | 待(ま)ってくれる? | friends |
| 3 | 待(ま)ってくれない? | friends; softer than rung 2 |
| 4 | 待(ま)ってもらえる? | friends; softer still |
| 5 | 待(ま)ってくれませんか | polite, but the listener is the giver - slightly direct |
| 6 | 待(ま)ってもらえませんか | polite, standard with strangers and colleagues |
| 7 | 待(ま)っていただけませんか | humble; superiors, customers, business |
| 8 | 待(ま)っていただけないでしょうか | the top rung; a real imposition |

## Examples

<pre>
その本(ほん)を<b>取(と)ってくれない</b>?
Could you pass me that book?
</pre>

<pre>
ペンを<b>貸(か)してもらえませんか</b>。
Could you lend me a pen?
</pre>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し大(おお)きい声(こえ)で<b>話(はな)してくれませんか</b>。
Could you speak up a bit?
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けてもらえませんか</b>。暑(あつ)くて。
Excuse me, could you open the window? It's stifling.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に<b>置(お)いてくれませんか</b>。あとで自分(じぶん)で片(かた)づけます。
Could you just leave it on the desk? I'll sort it out later myself.
</pre>

<pre>
あさっての会議(かいぎ)、代(か)わりに<b>出(で)てもらえない</b>かな。
I don't suppose you could go to the meeting the day after tomorrow instead of me?
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に用事(ようじ)ができたので、約束(やくそく)を来週(らいしゅう)に<b>変(か)えてもらえませんか</b>。
Something's come up, so could we move our appointment to next week?
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ないんですが、夜(よる)遅(おそ)い時間(じかん)に洗濯機(せんたくき)を<b>使(つか)わないでもらえませんか</b>。
I'm sorry to ask, but could you not run the washing machine late at night?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Why negatives are politer.** `～てもらえますか` asks whether the favour is
possible; `～てもらえませんか` asks whether it is *not* possible. The second one
presents refusal as the expected answer, so agreeing is a generosity rather than a
duty. Japanese politeness runs on exactly this move, and it is why the longest
forms are the politest: every extra layer is another step away from a bare demand.

**The `くれ-`／`もらえ-` split is small but real.** Both are polite; `くれませんか`
puts the listener in the subject slot and so points at them a little more
directly, while `もらえませんか` talks about what you would receive. With strangers
and in service situations, `もらえませんか` is the safer default. Inside a team,
`くれませんか` is perfectly normal and sounds slightly warmer, because `くれる`
carries the same gratitude it always does.

**Softening prefaces** matter as much as the ending. Real requests almost never
start cold:

- すみませんが… - the general-purpose opener
- 悪(わる)いんだけど… - casual, "sorry to ask"
- 申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが… - formal
- お忙(いそが)しいところ恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが… - business, for a real imposition
- ちょっとお願(ねが)いがあるんですが… - announces that a request is coming

Trailing off is also standard: 「手伝(てつだ)ってもらえませんか…」 with the
sentence left hanging is softer than finishing it crisply.

**Register warnings.** The plain imperative `くれ`（待(ま)ってくれ）is rough and
male-marked; do not use it. `～てくれる?` and `～てもらえる?` with rising
intonation are casual and completely normal among friends of any gender.
The affirmative `～てくれますか` is fine but a touch businesslike - the
tone of a form or an announcement.

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not stack. ❌待(ま)ってくれてもらえませんか is a beginner error
produced by trying to be extra polite. One giving verb per request; climb the
ladder instead of doubling up.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～てくれませんか vs ～てもらえませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same politeness band, different camera. `くれませんか` asks
*you* to give; `もらえませんか` asks whether *I* may receive. The receiving frame
is the more distant, and therefore slightly politer.

</div>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)の読(よ)み方(かた)を<b>教(おし)えてくれませんか</b>。
Would you tell me how to read this kanji?
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)の読(よ)み方(かた)を<b>教(おし)えてもらえませんか</b>。
Could I get you to tell me how to read this kanji?
</pre>

Both are perfectly usable to a classmate or a colleague. The gap only becomes
important as the social distance grows: to a stranger on the street, choose the
`もらえ-` version, and to your section chief, climb one more rung to
`いただけませんか`.

### Affirmative vs negative: ～てもらえますか vs ～てもらえませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative question is the politer one. Adding `ません`
does not make it a refusal; it makes it an offer of one.

</div>

<pre>
すみません、この荷物(にもつ)を上(うえ)まで<b>運(はこ)んでもらえますか</b>。
Excuse me, can you carry this luggage upstairs?
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、この荷物(にもつ)を上(うえ)まで<b>運(はこ)んでもらえませんか</b>。
Excuse me, would you mind carrying this luggage upstairs?
</pre>

The first is brisk and appropriate when the favour is part of the person's job.
The second treats the favour as optional, which is what you want when you are
asking for something extra, or from someone who owes you nothing. English does the
same thing with "can you" versus "would you mind".

### ～てもらえませんか vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` is an instruction dressed politely. The
`もらえ-`／`くれ-` forms are questions, and questions can be declined.

</div>

<pre>
こちらに<b>並(なら)んでください</b>。
Please queue here.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、こちらに<b>並(なら)んでもらえませんか</b>。
Sorry - would you mind queueing over here?
</pre>

The first is what a member of staff says to the public: they have the standing to
direct people. The second is what one customer says to another, because no such
standing exists. Learners overuse `～てください` for exactly this reason - it is
the first request form taught, and it is the one that carries authority.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 手伝(てつだ)って<b>もらいませんか</b>。 → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)って<b>もらえませんか</b>。
  The request form uses the **potential** `もらえる`. `もらいませんか` would be an
  invitation - "shall we not receive?" - and does not work as a request.

- ❌ 待(ま)って<b>くれられませんか</b>。 → ✅ 待(ま)って<b>くれませんか</b>。
  `くれる` is not put into the potential in this construction. If you want the
  potential, switch to the receiving frame: <code>待(ま)ってもらえませんか</code>.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、この書類(しょるい)を見(み)て<b>くれませんか</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、この書類(しょるい)を見(み)て<b>いただけませんか</b>。
  Rungs 5 and 6 are polite but not humble. A superior needs the `いただく` rung.

- ❌ 静(しず)かに<b>してくれませんでしたか</b>。 → ✅ 静(しず)かに<b>してくれませんか</b>。
  The request is about now, not the past. The negative here is a politeness
  device, not a tense.

- ❌ ここでたばこを<b>吸(す)ってもらえませんか</b>。（meaning "please don't smoke"）→ ✅ ここでたばこを<b>吸(す)わないでもらえませんか</b>。
  To ask for a non-action, negate the **verb**, not the request ending.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この字(じ)が読(よ)めないので、読(よ)んで____か。

The potential of `もらう` plus a negative question is the standard polite request.
To a stranger the `もらえ-` frame is the safer of the two, because it asks about
your own receiving rather than pointing at what the listener should do.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 社長(しゃちょう)、こちらにサインを（してくれませんか・していただけませんか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`くれませんか` is polite but not humble, and a company president sits well above

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The opener `すみません` is not optional politeness padding - a bare request to a

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

(b) 手伝(てつだ)ってもらえませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

to a friend or a sibling, and it assumes a relationship where small favours pass
back and forth without ceremony.

(b) is rung 6: polite, negative, receiving frame. Every one of those three choices
adds distance - `ます` politeness, a negative that invites refusal, and a frame
that talks about your receiving rather than their giving. It is the version for a
colleague you do not know well, or for a favour large enough that you want to make
saying no easy.

</details>

# ～ていただけませんか

**Meaning** · could you please do this for me? - the humble, business-standard request

`～ていただけませんか` is the top of the request ladder that starts at
`～てくれない?`. Every piece of it is doing work: `いただく` is the humble verb for
receiving, `いただける` is its potential ("may I receive"), and `ませんか` is a
negative question that hands the listener an easy way out. Stack all three and you
have the sentence that runs Japanese offices.

## Formation

Attach `いただく` to the て-form, put it in the potential, and ask the question in
the negative.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + いただけませんか | 書(か)く → 書(か)いていただけませんか |
| Verb (lighter) | て-form + いただけますか | 来(く)る → 来(き)ていただけますか |
| Verb (heaviest) | て-form + いただけないでしょうか | 変(か)える → 変(か)えていただけないでしょうか |
| Verb (negative request) | ない-form + でいただけませんか | 入(はい)る → 入(はい)らないでいただけませんか |
| Verb (statement form) | て-form + いただきたいのですが | 直(なお)す → 直(なお)していただきたいのですが |
| Honorific-humble frame | お + ます-stem + いただけませんか | 待(ま)つ → お待(ま)ちいただけませんか |
| する-verbs in that frame | ご + noun + いただけませんか | 確認(かくにん)する → ご確認(かくにん)いただけませんか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the length *is* the politeness. Each of the four rungs below
is longer than the one before, and each is politer:
<code>いただけますか</code> → <code>いただけませんか</code> →
<code>いただけないでしょうか</code> → <code>いただけますと幸(さいわ)いです</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the person you are asking takes `に`, exactly as with `もらう`, and is
almost always dropped because it is obvious:
（先生(せんせい)に）推薦状(すいせんじょう)を書(か)いていただけませんか。

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう一度(いちど)<b>言(い)っていただけませんか</b>。
Could you say that once more, please?
</pre>

<pre>
ここに住所(じゅうしょ)を<b>書(か)いていただけますか</b>。
Could you write your address here?
</pre>

<pre>
すみませんが、少々(しょうしょう)<b>お待(ま)ちいただけますか</b>。
I'm sorry - could you wait just a moment?
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)を明日(あした)の朝(あさ)までに<b>送(おく)っていただけないでしょうか</b>。
Would it be possible for you to send the materials by tomorrow morning?
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが、日(ひ)にちを<b>変(か)えていただけませんか</b>。
I do apologise, but could we change the date?
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)の音(おと)がひどいので、夜(よる)八時(はちじ)以降(いこう)は<b>やめていただけませんか</b>。
The construction noise is terrible, so could you stop after eight in the evening?
</pre>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しいところ恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、この書類(しょるい)に目(め)を<b>通(とお)していただけないでしょうか</b>。
I'm sorry to trouble you when you're busy, but might I ask you to look over this document?
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の打(う)ち合(あ)わせですが、時間(じかん)を三時(さんじ)に<b>していただけると助(たす)かります</b>。
About next week's meeting - it would be a great help if you could make it three o'clock.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **business Japanese's default request**, and it is worth over-learning.
Emails, phone calls, service counters, and any conversation with a customer or a
superior run on it. Used with a friend it sounds like you are joking or being cold.

The pieces are separable, and knowing which piece you are turning lets you fine-tune:

- **`いただける` vs `いただけます`** - plain vs polite potential. In a request you
  almost always want the polite one.
- **negative vs affirmative** - `いただけませんか` is politer than
  `いただけますか` because refusal is written into the question.
- **`でしょうか` instead of `か`** - adds hesitancy. `いただけないでしょうか` is
  the form for a genuine imposition: a deadline change, a favour outside someone's
  job.
- **statement instead of question** - <code>していただきたいのですが</code> trails
  off and leaves the request unspoken. Softer again, and very common in speech.
- **conditional endings** - <code>いただけると助(たす)かります</code> ("it would
  help"), <code>いただけますと幸(さいわ)いです</code> ("I would be grateful"),
  <code>いただければと思(おも)います</code>. These are email register; the last two
  are stiff enough that you would rarely say them out loud.

**The お／ご frame is politer than the plain て-form version.**
お待(ま)ちいただけませんか outranks 待(ま)っていただけませんか, and
ご記入(きにゅう)いただけませんか outranks
記入(きにゅう)していただけませんか. Use `お` with native-Japanese verbs
（お書(か)き, お使(つか)い）and `ご` with Sino-Japanese nouns
（ご連絡(れんらく), ご確認(かくにん)）. There are exceptions - 
お電話(でんわ), お返事(へんじ) - that simply have to be learned.

**Two things to keep apart.** `～ていただけませんか` asks the *other* person to
act. `～させていただけませんか` asks for permission for *you* to act:
早(はや)く帰(かえ)らせていただけませんか, "would you allow me to leave
early?" The middle syllables are almost identical and the meanings are opposite.

Spoken register also compresses. In quick service speech you will hear
少々(しょうしょう)お待(ま)ちいただけますでしょうか - technically doubled politeness
（`ます` plus `でしょう`）that prescriptivists dislike and everyone uses.

## Contrast

### ～ていただけませんか vs ～てもらえませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical structure, one step apart on the humility scale.
`もらう` is neutral; `いただく` is humble and marks the other person as above you.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん、この数字(すうじ)を<b>確(たし)かめてもらえませんか</b>。
Tanaka, could you check these figures?
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)、この数字(すうじ)を<b>確(たし)かめていただけませんか</b>。
Sir, could you check these figures?
</pre>

Nothing changes but the verb, and the social reading changes completely. Using
`もらえませんか` upward is not rude in the way a bare imperative is - it is more
like calling your manager by their first name in a formal meeting: nothing is
broken, but you have misjudged the room.

### ～ていただけませんか vs ～ていただけないでしょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `でしょうか` adds doubt, and doubt reads as deference. Save it
for requests you would feel awkward making.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)を少(すこ)し遅(おく)らせて<b>いただけませんか</b>。
Could we push the meeting back a little?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)を少(すこ)し遅(おく)らせて<b>いただけないでしょうか</b>。
Might it be at all possible to push the meeting back a little?
</pre>

Both are correct and both are polite. The second sounds more apologetic and is
what you use when you are asking someone to rearrange their day. Overusing it for
small things - asking for a pen - sounds theatrical.

### ～ていただけませんか vs お～ください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `お～ください` is honorific but still an **instruction**;
`～ていただけませんか` is a **question**. Instructions suit signs and staff;
questions suit asking a favour.

</div>

<pre>
こちらで<b>お待(ま)ちください</b>。
Please wait here.
</pre>

<pre>
こちらで<b>お待(ま)ちいただけませんか</b>。
Would you mind waiting here?
</pre>

The first is what a receptionist says - perfectly polite, and it presumes
compliance because directing visitors is her job. The second asks. Learners
default to `ください` because it is the first request form they meet, and end up
issuing gentle orders to their boss.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 待(ま)って<b>いただきませんか</b>。 → ✅ 待(ま)って<b>いただけませんか</b>。
  The request needs the **potential** `いただける`. `いただきませんか` reads as a
  strange invitation, not a request.

- ❌ 書(か)いて<b>いただけましたか</b>。（as a request）→ ✅ 書(か)いて<b>いただけませんか</b>。
  The past tense turns it into a question about whether it has already been done.
  A request stays in the non-past.

- ❌ 明日(あした)、休(やす)んで<b>いただけませんか</b>。（meaning "may I take tomorrow off?"）→ ✅ 明日(あした)、休(やす)ま<b>せていただけませんか</b>。
  <code>～ていただく</code> asks the *other* person to act. Asking permission for
  your own action needs the causative <code>～させていただく</code>.

- ❌ ここで写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)っていただけませんか</b>。（meaning "please don't take photos"）→ ✅ ここで写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)らないでいただけませんか</b>。
  To ask someone to refrain, negate the verb. Negating only the ending still asks
  them to do it.

- ❌ お待(ま)ち<b>していただけませんか</b>。 → ✅ お待(ま)ち<b>いただけませんか</b>。
  <code>お + stem + する</code> is the humble form for *your own* actions. When the
  other person is the one waiting, the frame is <code>お + stem + いただく</code>,
  with no `する`.

potential-negative question turns it into a polite request. This is the exact
sentence you hear at a bank or a clinic counter.

</details>

`いただきません` is the plain negative of `いただく` and does not form a request. The

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 少々(しょうしょう)お待(ま)ちください。
(b) 少々(しょうしょう)お待(ま)ちいただけないでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an honorific instruction. It is entirely polite and is the standard line
from staff to customer, because a receptionist directing a visitor is doing her
job - compliance is assumed, and no answer is expected.

(b) is a question, and a heavily hedged one. It presents waiting as a favour the
listener may decline, which is what you want when you have no standing to direct
them: asking a client to hold on while you fix your own mistake, for instance.
Said by a receptionist to an ordinary visitor it would sound oddly abject.

</details>

# ～てすみません

**Meaning** · I'm sorry for doing (or not doing) that - an apology attached to the specific act

Bare `すみません` apologises for nothing in particular. `～てすみません` names the
offence: the て-form supplies the cause, `すみません` supplies the apology, and the
two are joined by the ordinary "reason" use of the て-form you already know from
遅(おく)れて困(こま)りました. What makes it worth its own page is that
Japanese reaches for this construction in places where English would say *thank
you*.

## Formation

The て-form of whatever caused the trouble, then the apology word.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + すみません | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れてすみません |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + すみません | 行(い)く → 行(い)けなくてすみません |
| Verb (past act) | て-form + すみませんでした | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んですみませんでした |
| い-adjective | くて + すみません | 遅(おそ)い → 遅(おそ)くてすみません |
| な-adjective | で + すみません | 下手(へた) → 下手(へた)ですみません |
| Noun | で + すみません | この字(じ) → 汚(きたな)い字(じ)ですみません |
| Casual | て + ごめん／ごめんね | 待(ま)たせる → 待(ま)たせてごめん |
| Formal | て + 申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れて申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すみません` for a thing that is still going on, and
`すみませんでした` for a thing that is finished. Arriving late, you say
遅(おく)れてすみません as you come in; the next day you say
昨日(きのう)は遅(おく)れてすみませんでした.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `すみません` is the standard written and careful-speech form.
`すいません` is the same word as it is actually pronounced in casual speech.
Say it, do not write it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>遅(おく)れてすみません</b>。
Sorry I'm late.
</pre>

<pre>
うるさく<b>してすみません</b>。
Sorry for the noise.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は急(きゅう)に<b>休(やす)んですみませんでした</b>。
I'm sorry for taking the day off at short notice yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)に<b>出(で)られなくてすみません</b>でした。会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)だったんです。
Sorry I couldn't pick up - I was in a meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も<b>聞(き)いてすみません</b>が、もう一度(いちど)住所(じゅうしょ)を教(おし)えてください。
Sorry to keep asking, but could you give me the address once more?
</pre>

<pre>
わざわざ来(き)て<b>いただいてすみません</b>。近(ちか)くまで送(おく)りますよ。
It's very good of you to have come all this way. Let me give you a lift part of the way back.
</pre>

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)っていただいたのに、お礼(れい)も<b>言(い)わなくてすみませんでした</b>。
I'm sorry I never even thanked you after you helped me out.
</pre>

<pre>
つまらないことで時間(じかん)を<b>取(と)らせてしまってすみません</b>でした。
I'm sorry to have taken up your time with something so trivial.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The apology ladder.** Four rungs, and they are not interchangeable:

- <code>～てごめん／ごめんね</code> - casual, friends and family. Warm.
- <code>～てすみません</code> - the everyday polite apology. Works almost everywhere.
- <code>～て申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません</code> - formal; business, customers,
  real mistakes.
- <code>～て申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません</code> - the most formal; service
  industry and written apologies.

`すみません` is the safe default. `ごめんなさい` sits between rungs one and two: it
is politer than `ごめん` but has a slightly childlike, personal quality, and it is
odd in a business setting. Note also that `ごめん` cannot take the past-tense trick
 - there is no ❌ごめんでした.

**Japanese apologises where English thanks.** This is the point that catches
learners hardest. When someone has gone out of their way for you, Japanese often
acknowledges the *trouble you caused them* rather than the *good they did you*:

- 重(おも)い荷物(にもつ)を持(も)っていただいてすみません。
- こんなに遅(おそ)くまで付(つ)き合(あ)わせてすみません。

Both would be "thank you" in English. `ありがとうございます` is also correct in
these situations and is increasingly common among younger speakers; the
`すみません` version sounds more considerate and slightly more formal. Using both
together - すみません、ありがとうございます - is entirely normal.

**Negatives.** For an apology about something you *failed* to do, the form is
`～なくてすみません`, built on the plain negative plus て:
返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)くなってすみません,
手伝(てつだ)えなくてすみません. `～ないですみません` is not used.

**Common set phrases** worth learning whole, because their internal grammar is
opaque until you have seen it:

- お待(ま)たせしてすみません - sorry to have kept you waiting (causative + humble)
- ご迷惑(めいわく)をおかけしてすみません - sorry for the trouble I've caused
- 無理(むり)を言(い)ってすみません - sorry to ask something unreasonable

Register: `すみません` itself is neutral, used by all speakers. `すまない` is its
plain form and is male-marked and gruff. In Kansai speech `すんません` and
`かんにん` turn up; both are regional and casual.

## Contrast

### ～てすみません vs ～てありがとう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ありがとう` looks at what the other person gave you;
`すみません` looks at what it cost them. Both can describe the same kindness.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで送(おく)って<b>くれてありがとう</b>。
Thanks for driving me to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで送(おく)って<b>いただいてすみません</b>。
I'm sorry to have made you drive me to the station.
</pre>

Both are natural and both are grateful. The first is warmer and more direct - the
normal choice between friends. The second acknowledges the imposition, which reads
as more considerate and fits an unequal relationship or a genuinely large favour.
Note the pairing that native speakers keep: `くれて` with thanks, because thanks
point at the giver's act, and `いただいて` with an apology, because the apology
points at your receiving.

### ～てすみません vs ～て申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません</code> is for real
damage. If nobody was actually harmed, `すみません` is enough, and the heavier form
sounds melodramatic.

</div>

<pre>
ペンをお借(か)りします。<b>使(つか)わせてもらってすみません</b>。
Let me borrow your pen - sorry to use your things.
</pre>

<pre>
こちらの確認(かくにん)不足(ぶそく)で<b>ご迷惑(めいわく)をおかけして申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません</b>でした。
I sincerely apologise for the trouble caused by our failure to check properly.
</pre>

In business the two also split by role: `すみません` is what you say about your own
small slips, while <code>申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません</code> is what you say on behalf of your company. A
customer-facing apology almost never uses `すみません`.

### ～てすみません vs ～てしまってすみません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - inserting `しまって` says the act was unintended or
regrettable. It makes the apology sound more sincere, and it is very common.

</div>

<pre>
大切(たいせつ)な本(ほん)を汚(よご)して<b>すみません</b>。
Sorry for getting your important book dirty.
</pre>

<pre>
大切(たいせつ)な本(ほん)を汚(よご)して<b>しまってすみません</b>。
I'm so sorry - I've gone and got your important book dirty.
</pre>

`～てしまう` adds the sense of "and now it's done, and I wish it weren't". Because
apologies are exactly the situation where that feeling belongs, the combination is
almost formulaic: 遅(おく)れてしまってすみません,
忘(わす)れてしまって申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 来(こ)ない<b>ですみません</b>。 → ✅ 来(こ)られ<b>なくてすみません</b>。
  A negative reason takes `なくて`, not `ないで`. `ないで` marks manner, not cause.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れる<b>すみません</b>。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れ<b>てすみません</b>。
  The apology attaches to the て-form. The dictionary form cannot sit in front of
  `すみません`.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は<b>ごめんでした</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は<b>ごめんね</b>。／昨日(きのう)は<b>すみませんでした</b>。
  `ごめん` has no past form. If you need the past, move up the ladder to
  `すみませんでした`.

- ❌ お客(きゃく)さま、大変(たいへん)お待(ま)たせして<b>すみません</b>。 → ✅ お客(きゃく)さま、大変(たいへん)お待(ま)たせして<b>申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません</b>。
  To a customer, `すみません` is too light. Service register uses
  <code>申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません</code>.

- ❌ 荷物(にもつ)を持(も)っていただいて<b>すみましたか</b>。 → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)を持(も)っていただいて<b>すみません</b>。
  `すみません` is a fixed apology expression here. Do not try to conjugate it like
  the verb <code>済(す)む</code> it came from.

## Practice

**1.** 返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)く____すみませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)く<b>なって</b>すみませんでした。

The cause is expressed with the て-form, and here the cause is a change of state:
<code>遅(おそ)くなる</code> → <code>遅(おそ)くなって</code>. The past
`でした` fits because the delay is over.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 傘(かさ)を貸(か)して（くれてありがとう・いただいてすみません）。You are talking to a close friend.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傘(かさ)を貸(か)して<b>くれてありがとう</b>。

Between friends, thanks are direct and the giving verb is `くれる`. The
`いただいてすみません` version is not wrong, but its combination of humble verb and
apology creates a formal distance that would sound strange with someone close.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm sorry I couldn't come to the party yesterday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)はパーティーに<b>行(い)けなくてすみませんでした</b>。

Two things to get right: the negative reason takes `なくて`, and the event is
finished, so the apology is `すみませんでした`. Japanese normally says
<code>行(い)けなくて</code> rather than <code>来(こ)られなくて</code> when
talking about a place you did not go to.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れないですみません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れて<b>すみません</b>。

Two problems. The negative was inserted by mistake - you are apologising *for*
being late, not for not being late - and even for a genuine negative the reason
form is `なくて`, never `ないで`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 手伝(てつだ)ってくれてありがとう。
(b) 手伝(てつだ)っていただいてすみません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both respond to the same help, and both are appropriate responses to it.

(a) is gratitude, pure and simple. It points at what the other person did for you,
and its plain form makes it friendly and equal.

(b) apologises instead: it points at the trouble your problem caused them. Combined
with the humble `いただく` it marks the helper as senior or as having gone
substantially out of their way. Japanese uses this move constantly where English
would only ever say thank you, and getting comfortable with it is one of the
clearer steps from correct Japanese to natural Japanese.

</details>

# ～てよかった

**Meaning** · I'm glad I did that; it's a good thing that happened - relief, looking back

`～てよかった` evaluates something that has **actually happened** and finds it
good. The feeling underneath is relief: it could have gone the other way, and it
didn't. That backward glance is what separates it from a plain "I'm happy", and it
is why the past tense `よかった` is fixed even when the good thing is still going
on.

## Formation

The て-form of the thing that happened, then `よかった` - the past of
<code>いい／よい</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + よかった | 来(く)る → 来(き)てよかった |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + よかった | 行(い)く → 行(い)かなくてよかった |
| い-adjective | くて + よかった | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くてよかった |
| な-adjective | で + よかった | 元気(げんき) → 元気(げんき)でよかった |
| Noun | で + よかった | いい天気(てんき) → いい天気(てんき)でよかった |
| Polite | ～てよかったです | 習(なら)う → 習(なら)ってよかったです |
| Reported | ～てよかったと思(おも)う | 買(か)う → 買(か)ってよかったと思(おも)う |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よかった` is always past, even about the present or the
future. 明日(あした)休(やす)みでよかった - "I'm glad tomorrow's a day off" - 
is about tomorrow, and `よかった` still cannot become `いい`. What is past is the
*establishing* of the fact, not the fact itself.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `～なくてよかった` and `～ないでよかった` are both heard.
`なくて` is the standard reason form and the safer answer on a test;
`ないで` turns up in speech, especially where a deliberate choice is meant:
行(い)かないでよかった, "good thing I decided not to go."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>来(き)てよかった</b>。
I'm glad I came.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)らなくてよかった</b>。
Good thing it didn't rain.
</pre>

<pre>
この映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)てよかった</b>です。
I'm glad I watched this film.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を<b>持(も)ってきてよかった</b>。急(きゅう)に降(ふ)りだしたね。
Good thing I brought an umbrella - it's suddenly started pouring.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)にあったと聞(き)きましたが、けがが<b>なくてよかった</b>ですね。
I heard you were in an accident - I'm so glad you weren't hurt.
</pre>

<pre>
あの日(ひ)、勇気(ゆうき)を出(だ)して<b>声(こえ)をかけてよかった</b>。
I'm glad I plucked up the courage to say something to her that day.
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)のうちに日本語(にほんご)を<b>習(なら)っておいてよかった</b>と今(いま)になって思(おも)う。
Now that I'm older I'm glad I learned Japanese while I was still a student.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)のときに留学(りゅうがく)<b>してよかった</b>と、働(はたら)き始(はじ)めてから強(つよ)く感(かん)じるようになった。
Since starting work I've come to feel strongly that studying abroad at university was the right call.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The relief is the point.** `～てよかった` implies an alternative that would have
been worse. That is why 生(う)まれてよかった sounds strange out of context - 
nothing was at stake - while 生(い)きていてよかった is powerful. If you are
simply reporting that something nice occurred, with no sense of a near miss, the
pattern is a poor fit.

**Uses split three ways**, and learners usually only meet the first:

1. **Relief about your own past choice.** 早(はや)めに出(で)てよかった.
2. **Relief about someone else's situation.** 間(ま)に合(あ)ってよかったですね
 - said *to* the person, with `ね`. This is how Japanese expresses "thank
   goodness" on someone else's behalf, and the `ね` is close to obligatory.
3. **Relief that something did *not* happen.** 大(おお)きい地震(じしん)じゃなくてよかった.

**Register.** Plain `よかった` is casual and extremely common as a one-word
reaction - a friend tells you their exam went well and you say ああ、よかった.
`よかったです` is polite and fine in ordinary conversation, though in careful or
business speech people often prefer
<code>よかったと思(おも)います</code> or
<code>何(なに)よりです</code>（ご無事(ぶじ)で何(なに)よりです）, because a
bare emotional `よかったです` can sound a little bald upward.

**Emphasis** is done with <code>本当(ほんとう)に</code>, `つくづく`, or by stretching the vowel in
speech: 来(き)てよかったあ. The written-out long vowel is casual.

`～てよかった` also lives inside larger structures:
<code>～てよかったと思(おも)う</code> (reporting your own judgement, common in
writing), <code>～てよかったですね</code> (to someone else), and
<code>～てよかったのに</code>, which flips the meaning to "you needn't have" - 
来(こ)なくてよかったのに, "you didn't have to come all this way." The `のに`
does that work, not the `よかった`.

## Contrast

### ～てよかった vs ～ばよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てよかった` looks at what you **did** and approves.
`～ばよかった` looks at what you **didn't** and regrets it.

</div>

This is the pair that matters most, because they sit one letter apart in a
learner's head and mean opposite things.

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>予約(よやく)してよかった</b>。
I'm glad I booked early.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)く<b>予約(よやく)すればよかった</b>。
I should have booked early.
</pre>

The first is a satisfied look back at a real action; the second is regret about an
action that never happened. `～たらよかった` and `～ておけばよかった` work the same
way. Note the negatives, which double the confusion:
行(い)かなくてよかった is relief that you stayed home,
行(い)かなければよかった is regret that you went.

### ～てよかった vs ～てうれしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `うれしい` reports the emotion; `よかった` delivers a verdict
on the event. `よかった` carries relief, `うれしい` carries joy.

</div>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)できて<b>うれしい</b>です。
I'm happy I passed.
</pre>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)できて<b>よかった</b>です。
I'm relieved I passed.
</pre>

Both are natural here, because passing an exam is both a pleasure and a narrow
escape. They come apart at the edges. Pure good news takes `うれしい` - 
プレゼントをもらってうれしい, where nothing bad was ever in prospect. Averted
disaster takes `よかった` - 財布(さいふ)が見(み)つかってよかった, where
"うれしい" would understate the panic that preceded it. And only `よかった` works
for a negative event: ❌雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らなくてうれしい is odd.

### ～てよかった vs ～て助(たす)かった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よかった` evaluates the situation; <code>助(たす)かった</code> says *you*
personally were rescued from trouble by it. <code>助(たす)かった</code> needs a helper, usually a
person.

</div>

<pre>
道(みち)が空(す)いていて<b>よかった</b>。
Good thing the roads were clear.
</pre>

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)ってくださって<b>助(たす)かりました</b>。
You really saved me by helping out.
</pre>

Because <code>助(たす)かる</code> points at a rescuer, it doubles as thanks and is one of the most
useful phrases in working Japanese - 
代(か)わってくれて助(たす)かった. `よかった` cannot do that job: saying
手伝(てつだ)ってくださってよかったです to the person who helped sounds like you
are grading their performance rather than thanking them.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 来(き)て<b>いいです</b>。（meaning "I'm glad I came"）→ ✅ 来(き)て<b>よかったです</b>。
  The pattern is fixed in the past. `いい` here would be read as permission - 
  "you may come."

- ❌ 勉強(べんきょう)<b>すればよかった</b>と思(おも)っています。（meaning "I'm glad I studied"）→ ✅ 勉強(べんきょう)<b>してよかった</b>と思(おも)っています。
  `～ばよかった` is regret about what you did *not* do. It reverses your meaning
  completely.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)<b>くてよかった</b>。 → ✅ 元気(げんき)<b>でよかった</b>。
  <code>元気(げんき)</code> is a な-adjective, so it connects with `で`. Only
  い-adjectives take `くて`.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れ<b>ないでよかったです</b>ね。（to someone whose train was on time）→ ✅ 遅(おく)れ<b>なくてよかったです</b>ね。
  For a fact rather than a decision, `なくて` is the natural reason form. `ないで`
  suggests they chose not to be late.

- ❌ プレゼントをもらって<b>よかった</b>。（just pleased）→ ✅ プレゼントをもらって<b>うれしい</b>。
  Not ungrammatical, but `よかった` implies you had feared not getting one, which
  is not usually what you mean.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)が来(こ)なく____、旅行(りょこう)に行(い)けました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

台風(たいふう)が来(こ)なく<b>てよかった</b>。

The negative reason form is <code>来(こ)なくて</code>, and `よかった` supplies the
relief: the trip could easily have been cancelled, and wasn't. That averted
alternative is exactly what the pattern is for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)、もっと早(はや)く寝(ね)（てよかった・ればよかった）。You were exhausted all day today.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)、もっと早(はや)く寝(ね)<b>ればよかった</b>。

You did *not* go to bed early, and you regret it, so the counterfactual
`～ばよかった` is required. <code>寝(ね)てよかった</code> would claim you went to bed early and are
pleased about it - the opposite of the situation described.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm glad I asked you before I bought it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

買(か)う前(まえ)に<b>聞(き)いてよかった</b>。（polite: <b>聞(き)いてよかったです</b>）

The action really happened and turned out well, so it is `～てよかった`, not
`～ばよかった`. The relief is implied: had you not asked, you would have bought the
wrong thing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: パスポートをなくさなくていいでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ パスポートをなくさ<b>なくてよかった</b>。

Two errors. `よかった` is fixed in the past - the construction has no
❌<code>いいでした</code> form, and `いい` never takes `でした` in any case. The
polite version is <code>なくてよかったです</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会(あ)えてうれしいです。
(b) 会(あ)えてよかったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports emotion: meeting you makes me happy. It is the standard line on being
introduced to someone, and it says nothing about whether the meeting was in doubt.

(b) delivers a verdict with relief behind it: I'm glad the meeting happened. It
fits when there was a real chance it wouldn't - you almost missed each other, the
train was late, you had been trying to arrange it for months. Said at a first
introduction with no such backstory, it can sound as though you had been dreading
the encounter.

</details>
