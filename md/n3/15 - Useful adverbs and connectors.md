# N3 - Useful adverbs and connectors

# だけど

**Meaning** · but; however - the casual sentence-opening "but"

`だけど` opens a **new sentence** that pushes back on the one just said. It is the
everyday spoken "however": friends use it constantly, and it is one of the few
words you should deliberately leave outside a job interview.

## Usage

`だけど` stands at the head of a sentence, with a comma after it in writing, and
the objection follows. It does not conjugate, take particles or attach to
anything - it is a fixed one-word opener.

- 「明日(あした)、海(うみ)に行(い)こうよ。」「だけど、台風(たいふう)が来(く)るらしいよ。」

Historically it is the copula `だ` plus the clause-final `けど` you met at N5, and
that history explains the one place learners reliably go wrong. After a **noun or
な-adjective** the same string turns up *inside* a sentence, because the `だ`
belongs to the first clause: 学生(がくせい)だけど、もう働(はたら)いている.
After a **verb or い-adjective** there is no `だ` available, so it is plain `けど`:
安(やす)いけど使(つか)いにくい - never ❌安(やす)いだけど.

Sentence-initially, then, `だけど` is always available. Mid-sentence it is only
available after a noun or な-adjective, and there it is really two words wearing
one coat.

One more very common shape: <code>～んだけど</code>, which prefaces a request or a piece
of news - ちょっと聞(き)きたいことがあるんだけど… - where the trailing `だけど`
softens the approach rather than contradicting anything.

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)は寒(さむ)い。<b>だけど</b>、雪(ゆき)は降(ふ)らないと思(おも)う。
It's cold today. But I don't think it'll snow.
</pre>

<pre>
「もう一杯(いっぱい)どう?」「<b>だけど</b>、明日(あした)早(はや)いんだよね。」
"Another drink?" "But I've got an early start tomorrow."
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)習(なら)った。<b>だけど</b>、まだ電話(でんわ)で話(はな)すのはこわい。
I studied it for three years. But phone calls still terrify me.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は狭(せま)いし、駅(えき)からも遠(とお)い。<b>だけど</b>、この家賃(やちん)なら文句(もんく)は言(い)えない。
The flat is cramped and far from the station. But at this rent I can't complain.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Casual. `だけど` belongs to conversation with friends and family, to messages, and
to relaxed writing such as blog posts. In a business email, an essay or anything
said to a customer, use `ですが` or `しかし` instead. Roughly, the ladder runs
`しかし` (formal, written) - `ですが` (polite) - `でも` (neutral casual) - `だけど`
(casual) - `けど` (very casual, often trailing off).

Against `でも`, `だけど` tends to be the firmer of the two. `でも` can register a
mild reservation; `だけど` picks up the previous statement and argues with it, so
opening several sentences in a row with it comes across as combative.

`だけれど` and `だけれども` are the unshortened forms: a shade more careful and
slightly bookish, and they lean older in speech. `だけどさ`, `だけどね` do the
opposite, softening the pushback with a final particle. None of these are marked
for gender.

Note also that `だけど` is not a subordinating conjunction. It cannot join two
clauses into one sentence the way `が` or `けど` do; it needs a full stop in front
of it, or at least the pause that a full stop would represent.

## Contrast

### だけど vs でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same slot, same register, different force. `でも` raises a
qualification; `だけど` contradicts.

</div>

<pre>
「これ、おいしいね。」「<b>でも</b>、ちょっと甘(あま)すぎない?」
"This is good." "Though - isn't it a bit too sweet?"
</pre>

<pre>
「これ、おいしいね。」「<b>だけど</b>、値段(ねだん)を見(み)た?」
"This is good." "But did you see the price?"
</pre>

Both are natural; the second sounds more like the start of an argument. When you
are not sure which you want, `でも` is the safer default.

### だけど vs けど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけど` begins a sentence; `けど` ends a clause. They are not
interchangeable positions.

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)はある<b>けど</b>、お金(かね)がない。
I have the time, but not the money.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)はある。<b>だけど</b>、お金(かね)がない。
I have the time. But I don't have the money.
</pre>

The content is identical; the second version breaks it into two beats and lands
harder. Sentence-initial `けど` alone does exist in very casual speech - 
「けど、無理(むり)でしょ」 - but `だけど` is the standard choice there.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がないだけど、行(い)きます。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がない<b>けど</b>、行(い)きます。
  A verb or い-adjective cannot be followed by `だ`. Only nouns and な-adjectives
  can carry the `だ` that makes `だけど`.

- ❌ 静(しず)かですだけど → ✅ 静(しず)かです<b>けど</b>
  `です` is already the copula; adding `だ` on top of it doubles up. In polite
  speech the clause-final connector is `ですけど` or `ですが`.

- ❌（取引先(とりひきさき)へのメールで）だけど、納期(のうき)は来週(らいしゅう)になります。 → ✅ <b>しかし</b>、納期(のうき)は来週(らいしゅう)になります。
  `だけど` in business writing reads as though you forgot who you were talking to.
  `しかし` or `ですが` carries the same meaning at the right register.

## Practice

**1.** 「明日(あした)のパーティー、来(く)る?」「行(い)きたい。____、バイトなんだ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「行(い)きたい。<b>だけど</b>、バイトなんだ。」

The speaker has finished one sentence and starts another that contradicts it, so
the connector has to be the sentence-initial kind. `でも` would also fit here and
would sound a little softer.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 高(たか)いだけど、けっきょく買(か)うことにした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 高(たか)い<b>けど</b>、けっきょく買(か)うことにした。

<code>高(たか)い</code> is an い-adjective, and い-adjectives are never followed by `だ`.
Splitting it into two sentences - 高(たか)い。だけど、買(か)うことにした。 - 
works too.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) <b>だけど</b>、私(わたし)はそうは思(おも)わない。
(b) <b>ですが</b>、私(わたし)はそうは思(おも)いません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The grammar is the same and the meaning is the same: both reject what was just
said. The difference is entirely register. Sentence (a) is what you say to a
friend over coffee, while (b) is what you say in a meeting or write in an essay.
Using (a) in a formal discussion would sound abrupt and a little rude, and using
(b) with a close friend would sound stiff and distant.

</details>

# ところで

**Meaning** · by the way - putting the current topic down and picking up a new one

`ところで` is a signal, not a link. It tells the listener that what follows has
nothing to do with what came before, and that the speaker is doing it on purpose.
Nine times out of ten, what follows is a question the speaker has been sitting on.

## Usage

`ところで` goes at the **head of a sentence**, followed by a comma or a pause. It
does not attach to a word, does not conjugate, and never appears in the middle of
a clause.

- ところで、来月(らいげつ)の旅行(りょこう)はどうなりましたか。
- ところで、あの話(はなし)、覚(おぼ)えてる?

Two things it is *not*. It is not "however" - it contradicts nothing, so
❌ところで、私(わたし)は反対(はんたい)です is wrong where `しかし` was wanted.
And it is not the `ところ` family of grammar points: the <code>～たところで</code> of
急(いそ)いだところで間(ま)に合(あ)わない ("even if you hurry…") and the
<code>～ているところ</code> of 今(いま)食(た)べているところです ("in the middle of
eating") are separate constructions that merely look alike. The `ところで` of this
page always begins a sentence and never follows a verb.

Because the whole point of the word is to close one subject and open another, it
usually appears once in a conversation, at a natural seam - after a topic has run
its course, not on top of someone else who is still talking.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>ところで</b>、山田(やまだ)さんは元気(げんき)?
By the way, how's Yamada doing?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ところで</b>、昼(ひる)ごはんはもう食(た)べましたか。
By the way, have you had lunch yet?
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)は以上(いじょう)です。<b>ところで</b>、来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)からでしたか。
That's everything in the handouts. By the way, what time was next week's meeting again?
</pre>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)、ありがとう。すごくよく撮(と)れてるね。<b>ところで</b>、あのカメラっていくらぐらいしたの?
Thanks for the photos - they came out really well. By the way, roughly how much was that camera?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ところで` is register-neutral in itself: it works in ます-form speech and in plain
speech, and it appears in writing wherever a writer changes tack. What is not
neutral is the act it performs. Announcing a change of subject is a small claim on
the floor, so it lands most comfortably when you have the standing to make it - 
among equals, or from whoever is hosting the conversation.

Speaking to a customer or someone well above you, the same move is usually padded:
話(はなし)は変(か)わりますが、… or それはそうと、… or simply
一(ひと)つお伺(うかが)いしてもよろしいでしょうか. `ところで` itself is not
rude - it is used in business meetings constantly - but it is brisk, and dropped
straight after someone has said something emotionally weighty it can sound as
though you were not listening.

There is no casual contraction of it, and it is not marked for age or gender.

## Contrast

### ところで vs ちなみに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ところで` drops the current topic. `ちなみに` stays on it and
adds a footnote.

</div>

<pre>
このカフェ、安(やす)くておいしいよ。<b>ちなみに</b>、夜(よる)は十時(じゅうじ)までやってる。
This café is cheap and good. Incidentally, it's open until ten at night.
</pre>

<pre>
このカフェ、安(やす)くておいしいよ。<b>ところで</b>、引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの準備(じゅんび)は進(すす)んでる?
This café is cheap and good. Anyway - how's the packing for your move coming along?
</pre>

Swapping them breaks both sentences: the opening hours are plainly still about the
café, and the packing plainly is not. A quick test - if the new sentence would
also work with そういえば in front of it, `ところで` is the right choice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ところで、この案(あん)には賛成(さんせい)できません。 → ✅ <b>しかし</b>、この案(あん)には賛成(さんせい)できません。
  `ところで` changes the subject; it does not disagree. Learners reach for it
  because it looks like a conjunction, but it carries no adversative meaning at all.

- ❌ 映画(えいが)を見(み)たところで、晩(ばん)ごはんは何(なに)にする?（meaning "by the way"）→ ✅ 映画(えいが)、面白(おもしろ)かったね。<b>ところで</b>、晩(ばん)ごはんは何(なに)にする?
  Placed after a verb, the same syllables become the pattern meaning "even if", and
  the sentence turns into "even if I watch the film…". Keep it at the front.

- ❌ ところでですが、… → ✅ ところで、…
  `ところで` already contains everything it needs, and `ですが` does not politen it.
  If you want a politer version, replace the whole word with
  話(はなし)は変(か)わりますが.

## Practice

**1.** ____、田中(たなか)さんの連絡先(れんらくさき)を知(し)っていますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ところで</b>、田中(たなか)さんの連絡先(れんらくさき)を知(し)っていますか。

A question with no connection to the previous topic needs a marker warning the
listener to switch tracks, and that is exactly the job this word does.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
明日(あした)の会場(かいじょう)は市民(しみん)ホールです。（ところで・ちなみに）、駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)は有料(ゆうりょう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ちなみに</b>、駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)は有料(ゆうりょう)です。

The parking charge is a further detail about the same venue, so the topic has not
changed at all. `ところで` would promise the listener something unrelated and then
fail to deliver it.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ところで、部長(ぶちょう)、そろそろ失礼(しつれい)してもよろしいでしょうか。
(b) 話(はなし)は変(か)わりますが、部長(ぶちょう)、そろそろ失礼(しつれい)してもよろしいでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both change the subject and both are grammatical. Sentence (a) is brisk: the
speaker announces the switch in one word and moves on, which is fine between
colleagues but a little bald when addressing a superior. Sentence (b) spends a few
extra syllables acknowledging that a switch is being made, and that small detour
is what makes it sound considerate.

</details>

# つまり

**Meaning** · in other words; which means - restating something more plainly, or
spelling out what it amounts to

`つまり` announces that the same content is coming round again in a clearer shape.
It does two jobs with one word: rewording something complicated, and stating the
consequence nobody has said out loud yet.

## Usage

`つまり` is an adverb and sits at the front of the clause it introduces. Nothing
attaches to it, and it never changes form.

The habit worth building is what comes at the **end** of that clause. A `つまり`
sentence very often closes with <code>ということだ</code>, <code>わけだ</code> or `のだ`,
because you are labelling a conclusion rather than reporting a fresh fact:

- つまり、来(こ)られないということですね。
- 三年(さんねん)住(す)んでいた。つまり、地元(じもと)のようなものだ。

The two jobs, one line each:

- **Rewording** - the second half says the same thing in easier words.
  検討(けんとう)します。つまり、今(いま)は決(き)められないということです。
- **Consequence** - the second half is what the first one comes to.
  終電(しゅうでん)はもう出(で)た。つまり、歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)るしかない。

As a standalone check on your understanding, `つまり` with a rising question is
extremely common: 「つまり、明日(あした)は休(やす)みってこと?」

## Examples

<pre>
父(ちち)の姉(あね)、<b>つまり</b>おばです。
My father's older sister - my aunt, in other words.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)がない。<b>つまり</b>、断(ことわ)られたということだ。
There's been no reply. Which means I've been turned down.
</pre>

<pre>
材料費(ざいりょうひ)も送料(そうりょう)も上(あ)がっている。<b>つまり</b>、値上(ねあ)げは避(さ)けられないわけです。
Materials and shipping have both gone up. In other words, a price rise can't be avoided.
</pre>

<pre>
「前向(まえむ)きに検討(けんとう)します」って言(い)われたんだけど、<b>つまり</b>断(ことわ)られたってこと?
They said they'd "consider it positively" - so does that mean I've been refused?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very frequent. It is at home in a lecture, a contract summary,
a news broadcast and a conversation between friends, with no change of form.

In speech it has a second life as a filler. Some speakers open every third sentence
with つまり、つまりですね… while they assemble a thought, and the word then carries
no summarising meaning at all. Worth recognising, not worth imitating.

One genuine risk: aiming `つまり` at what *somebody else* just said can sound like
a correction - "so what you actually meant was…". A softener fixes it:
つまり、こういうことでしょうか. Aimed at your own words it is completely safe, and
in a presentation it is a useful signpost that the summary has arrived.

`つまり` is not a cause-and-effect connector. `だから` and したがって say "therefore,
here is a new fact"; `つまり` says "the same fact, re-expressed". When the second
sentence contains information the first one did not, you want `だから`.

## Contrast

### つまり vs 要(よう)するに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つまり` re-expresses; <code>要(よう)するに</code> compresses. The
second one claims the right to decide what the point was.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)が変(か)わりました。<b>つまり</b>、駅(えき)の南口(みなみぐち)に集合(しゅうごう)です。
The venue has changed - that is, we meet at the south exit of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)は長(なが)かったが、<b>要(よう)するに</b>予算(よさん)が足(た)りないのだ。
The explanation went on a while, but the gist is that there isn't enough budget.
</pre>

<code>要(よう)するに</code> implies the original was long-winded, so pointing it at a
superior's remarks is risky. `つまり` carries no such judgement.

### つまり vs すなわち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different clothes. すなわち is the written, formal
register: it puts an equals sign between two terms.

</div>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の首都(しゅと)、<b>すなわち</b>東京(とうきょう)に本社(ほんしゃ)を置(お)いている。
Our head office is in the capital of Japan, namely Tokyo.
</pre>

In conversation すなわち sounds like a legal document being read aloud. Reach for
`つまり` when you are speaking, and keep すなわち for formal writing, where it is
usually left in kana.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた。つまり、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた。<b>だから</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  The cancellation is a new event, not a restatement of the rain. `つまり` needs
  the second sentence to be the *same* content in another form.

- ❌ つまり三人(さんにん)来(き)ました。 → ✅ 全部(ぜんぶ)で三人(さんにん)来(き)ました。
  `つまり` cannot introduce a plain new fact out of nowhere. Something has to have
  been said already for it to restate.

- ❌ 要(よう)するに、先生(せんせい)のおっしゃりたいことは…（said to your own teacher）→ ✅ <b>つまり</b>、先生(せんせい)のおっしゃりたいことは…
  Summarising a superior's words with <code>要(よう)するに</code> hints that they
  rambled. `つまり` is the neutral choice whenever the words being restated are not
  your own.

## Practice

**1.** 木曜(もくよう)も金曜(きんよう)も予定(よてい)が入(はい)っている。____、今週(こんしゅう)はもう時間(じかん)がない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>つまり</b>、今週(こんしゅう)はもう時間(じかん)がない。

The second sentence adds no new information - it is the first one seen from a step
back. That is exactly the restating job, and `だから` would also be possible if you
wanted to present it as a result rather than a rewording.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)のお話(はなし)は、要(よう)するに予算(よさん)を減(へ)らせということですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)のお話(はなし)は、<b>つまり</b>予算(よさん)を減(へ)らせということですね。

Grammatically the original is fine, but <code>要(よう)するに</code> tells your manager
that his explanation needed boiling down. `つまり` restates without passing
judgement on the length of the original.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会員(かいいん)は無料(むりょう)です。つまり、お金(かね)はかかりません。
(b) 会員(かいいん)は無料(むりょう)です。だから、みんな入(はい)りたがります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In (a) the two halves are the same fact twice, the second time in plainer words, so
`つまり` is doing a translation job for the listener. In (b) the second half is a
separate fact that follows from the first, which calls for a causal connector. The
test is simple: if deleting the second clause loses no information at all, `つまり`
is the word you want.

</details>

# たとえば

**Meaning** · for example; say - pulling one concrete case out of a general
statement

`たとえば` warns the listener that what follows is an illustration, not a new
claim. The example is offered as one of several possibilities, which is why it so
often arrives with `とか` or `など` trailing behind it.

## Usage

`たとえば` normally stands at the head of the sentence, but it can also sit
directly in front of the noun phrase it is introducing:

- 果物(くだもの)が好(す)きです。たとえば、みかんとかぶどうとか。
- 近(ちか)くの店(みせ)、たとえばコンビニでも買(か)えます。

Three shapes carry most of its use:

- **たとえば～とか／など** - a list left deliberately open.
  たとえば漢字(かんじ)のアプリなど、毎日(まいにち)使(つか)えるものがいいです。
- **たとえば～のような／みたいな + noun** - the example modifies a noun.
  たとえば京都(きょうと)のような古(ふる)い町(まち)
- **たとえば～なら** - set up a hypothetical case and then say what happens in it.
  たとえば雨(あめ)なら、中(なか)でやりましょう。

In kanji it is 例(たと)えば, and both spellings are completely normal: printed
prose and official documents tend to use the kanji, casual writing the kana. The
reading and the meaning are identical either way.

Do not confuse it with `たとえ`, which is a different word entirely - see the
contrast below.

## Examples

<pre>
甘(あま)いものが好(す)きです。<b>たとえば</b>、チョコレートやアイスです。
I like sweet things - chocolate and ice cream, for example.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>たとえば</b>、この漢字(かんじ)は読(よ)み方(かた)が三(みっ)つあります。
This kanji, for instance, has three different readings.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)にできること、<b>たとえば</b>散歩(さんぽ)とか料理(りょうり)とかを増(ふ)やしたい。
I want more things I can do on a day off - walks, cooking, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>たとえば</b>地震(じしん)が起(お)きたら、まず机(つくえ)の下(した)に入(はい)ってください。
Say an earthquake happens - the first thing to do is get under a desk.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. Nothing about `たとえば` marks formality, age or gender, and it
appears unchanged in academic writing and in chat with friends.

Two conversational uses are worth memorising whole.
「たとえばの話(はなし)だけど」 prefaces a hypothetical you are not committing to - 
"just supposing, though". And 「たとえば?」 said alone is a request for one: "such
as?" That one-word question is enormously useful and very common.

In writing, a `たとえば` sentence is usually short and ends with `など`, `といった`
or a plain noun phrase, and the paragraph then returns to the general point. If
you find yourself giving three sentences of example, consider 具体(ぐたい)的(てき)には
("specifically") instead, which promises a fuller account.

`たとえば` presents one member of a set. When you want to say that your example is
the *representative* one - the first of many, the flagship - Japanese has
<code>をはじめ</code> for that, and it is not interchangeable.

## Contrast

### たとえば vs たとえ○○ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たとえば` introduces an example. `たとえ` introduces a
concession, and it always has a `ても` waiting for it later in the sentence.

</div>

<pre>
<b>たとえば</b>明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったら、どうしますか。
If it rains tomorrow, say, what will you do?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>たとえ</b>明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、試合(しあい)はやります。
Even if it rains tomorrow, the match is going ahead.
</pre>

The first sentence asks about one possible case among many; the second brushes the
case aside. The `ば` is the whole difference in writing, and in speech the giveaway
is the `ても` at the far end: if you hear one, the word at the front was `たとえ`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ たとえ日本料理(にほんりょうり)が好(す)きです。 → ✅ <b>たとえば</b>日本料理(にほんりょうり)が好(す)きです。
  Without the `ば` the word means "even if", and the sentence is left dangling with
  nothing for it to concede to.

- ❌ たとえば、日本(にほん)の人口(じんこう)は約(やく)一億(いちおく)二千万人(にせんまんにん)です。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)の人口(じんこう)は約(やく)一億(いちおく)二千万人(にせんまんにん)です。
  A single hard fact is not an example of anything. `たとえば` needs a general
  statement in front of it for the example to illustrate.

- ❌ 野菜(やさい)、たとえばにんじんをはじめ買(か)いました。 → ✅ 野菜(やさい)、<b>たとえば</b>にんじんなどを買(か)いました。
  `たとえば` pairs with `など`, `とか` and `といった`. <code>をはじめ</code> is a
  different pattern meaning "starting with", and the two cannot be stacked.

## Practice

**1.** 運動(うんどう)不足(ぶそく)なので、____階段(かいだん)を使(つか)うようにしています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>たとえば</b>階段(かいだん)を使(つか)うようにしています。

The stairs are one concrete instance of the general problem stated first, which is
what this word is for. Note that the example still needs the general statement in
front of it to lean on.

</details>

**2.** Choose the right word: （たとえ・たとえば）失敗(しっぱい)しても、やってみる価値(かち)はある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>たとえ</b>失敗(しっぱい)しても、やってみる価値(かち)はある。

The `ても` later in the sentence is the tell: this is a concession, "even if I
fail", not an illustration. `たとえば` would leave the `ても` with no partner.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 静(しず)かな町(まち)、たとえば奈良(なら)のような町(まち)に住(す)みたい。
(b) 静(しず)かな町(まち)、奈良(なら)に住(す)みたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) uses Nara as an illustration of the kind of town the speaker wants;
other quiet towns would do just as well, and nobody would be surprised if they
ended up somewhere else. Sentence (b) names Nara as the actual destination and
describes it as quiet along the way. The example frame is what keeps the choice
open.

</details>

# 確かに

**Meaning** · certainly; admittedly; you're right - vouching for something, or
granting a point before you argue with it

確(たし)かに has two lives. It can mean "definitely, without doubt", said of
something you are sure of. Far more often at this level it means "granted" - you
concede what the other person said, and then the `が` or `けど` arrives.

## Usage

確(たし)かに is an adverb: it goes in front of the predicate, or at the head of
the sentence, and never changes shape. Three uses:

- **Conceding** - the big one. 確(たし)かに～が／けど、～. The first half agrees,
  the second half pushes back.
  確(たし)かに値段(ねだん)は高(たか)いが、質(しつ)はいい。
- **Vouching** - "for certain", of an event you witnessed or a fact you can stand
  behind.
  昨日(きのう)、確(たし)かに渡(わた)しました。
- **Agreeing, on its own** - 「確(たし)かに。」 is a complete turn in
  conversation, meaning "true" or "fair point". This is very common in modern
  casual speech.

The single most useful thing to remember is the concessive frame. Whenever an
essay or a discussion needs "it's true that…, but…", 確(たし)かに at the front
and `が` in the middle is the standard shape, and a listener hears the `が` coming
the moment you say the first word.

Kanji 確(たし)かに and kana たしかに are both normal; the kanji is the default in
print.

## Examples

<pre>
「この店(みせ)、安(やす)いね。」「<b>確(たし)かに</b>。」
"This place is cheap." "It is, isn't it."
</pre>

<pre>
<b>確(たし)かに</b>寒(さむ)いけど、雪(ゆき)は降(ふ)らないよ。
It's cold, admittedly, but it isn't going to snow.
</pre>

<pre>
書類(しょるい)は<b>確(たし)かに</b>お預(あず)かりしました。
I have received the documents - they are safely with me.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>確(たし)かに</b>彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は分(わ)かりやすかったが、肝心(かんじん)の費用(ひよう)の話(はなし)が抜(ぬ)けていた。
His explanation was certainly easy to follow, but it left out the crucial question of cost.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and equally at home in an academic paper and in a text message.
The concessive frame 確(たし)かに～が is a staple of written argument; the
one-word 「確(たし)かに。」 reply is casual but not slangy, and works in the office
as well as with friends.

The vouching use has a slightly formal flavour, because it is what you say when
responsibility is at stake: 確(たし)かに承(うけたまわ)りました, "I have
definitely taken note of that", is standard business phrasing. In everyday talk
about the future, きっと is the natural word instead.

Be aware of what conceding does to the shape of a conversation. Opening with
確(たし)かに sets up an expectation that a `が` is coming; if you concede and
then stop, the listener will wait for the rest of the sentence. That is often
exactly the effect you want when disagreeing politely - the agreement takes the
sting out of what follows - but it does mean 確(たし)かに is rarely the whole
of your position.

## Contrast

### 確(たし)かに vs 確(たし)か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one `に` reverses the meaning. 確(たし)かに is confidence;
確(たし)か is a memory you are not sure of.

</div>

<pre>
<b>確(たし)か</b>会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)からだったと思(おも)います。
The meeting was at three, if I remember rightly.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は<b>確(たし)かに</b>三時(さんじ)から始(はじ)まりました。
The meeting definitely started at three.
</pre>

確(たし)か is an adverb of *hedged recollection* and normally comes with
と思(おも)う, はず or だったよね. 確(たし)かに asserts. Mixing them up tells
your listener the opposite of what you meant, which is why this pair is worth
drilling.

### 確(たし)かに vs きっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 確(たし)かに is about what is already established. きっと is
a confident guess about what is not.

</div>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>きっと</b>来(き)ます。
She'll definitely come.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は<b>確(たし)かに</b>来(き)ました。
She did come - I can vouch for it.
</pre>

きっと looks forward and is a prediction, however confident; 確(たし)かに looks
back at evidence. Saying ❌確(たし)かに来(き)ます about a future event sounds
like you are promising on someone else's behalf.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 確(たし)かに明日(あした)は雨(あめ)でしょう。 → ✅ <b>きっと</b>明日(あした)は雨(あめ)でしょう。
  A forecast is a guess, not something you can vouch for. 確(たし)かに needs
  evidence already in hand.

- ❌ 確(たし)かに田中(たなか)さんの傘(かさ)だったと思(おも)います。 → ✅ <b>確(たし)か</b>田中(たなか)さんの傘(かさ)だったと思(おも)います。
  と思(おも)います signals that you are not sure, so the hedging
  確(たし)か is the form that fits. With `に` the sentence contradicts itself.

- ❌ 確(たし)かにです。 → ✅ <b>確(たし)かに</b>そうですね。
  確(たし)かに is an adverb and cannot take `です` directly. To agree politely,
  give it something to modify.

## Practice

**1.** ____不便(ふべん)な場所(ばしょ)だが、家賃(やちん)が安(やす)いのは魅力(みりょく)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>確(たし)かに</b>不便(ふべん)な場所(ばしょ)だが、家賃(やちん)が安(やす)いのは魅力(みりょく)だ。

The `が` in the middle is the giveaway: the writer grants the drawback first and
then argues past it, which is the standard concessive frame this word builds.

</details>

**2.** Choose the right form: （確(たし)か・確(たし)かに）去年(きょねん)も同(おな)じ問題(もんだい)が出(で)たはずです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>確(たし)か</b>去年(きょねん)も同(おな)じ問題(もんだい)が出(で)たはずです。

`はず` marks this as inference from a hazy memory rather than a fact the speaker
can guarantee, so the hedging form without `に` is the one that fits.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 確(たし)かにこの方法(ほうほう)は速(はや)い。
(b) この方法(ほうほう)は速(はや)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (b) is a flat statement of the speaker's own view. Sentence (a) sounds
like a reply: it accepts a point somebody else has made, and it leaves the door
open for a "but" - about cost, or safety, or whatever the speaker is really
driving at. Ending there without the "but" makes the agreement sound reluctant.

</details>

# おそらく

**Meaning** · in all likelihood; probably - a considered estimate, stated carefully

`おそらく` is the measured, slightly formal cousin of たぶん. It says the speaker
has weighed what they know and arrived at a probable answer. The weighing is the
point: `おそらく` sounds like a judgement, not a shrug.

## Usage

`おそらく` is an adverb and stands at the head of the sentence or immediately
before the predicate. It never conjugates and takes no particles.

What matters is the **end of the sentence**. `おそらく` almost always pairs with a
marker of conjecture:

- **おそらく～だろう／でしょう** - the default.
  おそらく明日(あした)には届(とど)くでしょう。
- **おそらく～と思(おも)う／と思(おも)われる** - reasoning out loud, or in
  writing.
  おそらく原因(げんいん)は配線(はいせん)だと思(おも)われます。
- **おそらく～はずだ／に違(ちが)いない** - when the estimate is close to a
  conclusion.
  おそらく彼(かれ)はもう家(いえ)に着(つ)いているはずだ。

Ending a plain assertion with nothing at all - ❌おそらく雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る - 
sounds unfinished to a native ear. The hedge at the end is not decoration; it is
what `おそらく` is agreeing with.

In kanji it is 恐(おそ)らく. The kanji spelling is common in print; the kana is
common in ordinary writing. It has nothing to do with fear despite the character.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>おそらく</b>彼(かれ)はもう知(し)っているでしょう。
He probably already knows.
</pre>

<pre>
この時間(じかん)なら、<b>おそらく</b>道(みち)は空(す)いているだろう。
At this hour the roads are most likely clear.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)がないところを見(み)ると、<b>おそらく</b>忙(いそが)しいのだろう。
Judging by the lack of a reply, he's probably swamped.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の不具合(ふぐあい)は、<b>おそらく</b>先月(せんげつ)の更新(こうしん)が原因(げんいん)だと考(かんが)えられます。
This fault is in all likelihood caused by last month's update.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`おそらく` leans formal. It is the natural choice in a report, a news article, a
presentation or a careful explanation to a client, and it sounds a little stiff
between friends deciding where to eat, where たぶん is what people actually say.

It also leans toward **inference about circumstances** rather than announcements
about your own plans. たぶん行(い)きます ("I'll probably go") is ordinary;
おそらく行(い)きます sounds oddly detached, as though you were forecasting the
behaviour of a stranger who happens to be you. Reserve `おそらく` for things
outside your control.

On the confidence scale, `おそらく` sits high - above もしかしたら, roughly level
with or a shade above たぶん - but it is still a guess. If you know, say so
plainly; hedging a fact with `おそらく` reads as evasive, and in business that
impression is hard to shake off.

## Contrast

### おそらく vs たぶん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same probability, different room. `たぶん` is spoken and
casual; `おそらく` is written and considered.

</div>

<pre>
<b>たぶん</b>間(ま)に合(あ)うと思(おも)うよ。
We'll probably make it, I reckon.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>おそらく</b>間(ま)に合(あ)うものと思(おも)われます。
We are likely to arrive in time.
</pre>

`たぶん` can be tossed off with no evidence at all; `おそらく` implies you looked at
something first. In casual conversation `おそらく` is not wrong, merely bookish.

### おそらく vs きっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `おそらく` estimates. `きっと` believes.

</div>

<pre>
<b>きっと</b>喜(よろこ)んでくれるよ。
She's going to love it.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>おそらく</b>喜(よろこ)んでくれるだろう。
She will probably be pleased.
</pre>

`きっと` carries the speaker's feeling - hope, conviction, encouragement - and is
what you say when reassuring someone. `おそらく` carries none of that warmth, which
makes it excellent for analysis and poor for comfort.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ おそらく明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ります。 → ✅ おそらく明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>でしょう</b>。
  A flat ます-ending states a fact, which contradicts the word at the front.
  `おそらく` needs `でしょう`, `だろう` or と思(おも)います to lean on.

- ❌ おそらく私(わたし)が作(つく)りました。 → ✅ <b>たぶん</b>私(わたし)が作(つく)ったと思(おも)います。
  You do not estimate your own past actions from the outside. `おそらく` is for
  inference about things you did not do.

- ❌ 明日(あした)のパーティー、おそらく行(い)くね。 → ✅ 明日(あした)のパーティー、<b>たぶん</b>行(い)くね。
  Announcing your own plans to a friend calls for the casual word. `おそらく` with
  the final particle `ね` mixes two registers in one short sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 電気(でんき)が消(き)えているから、____もう寝(ね)ているだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>おそらく</b>もう寝(ね)ているだろう。

The speaker reasons from visible evidence - the lights are off - to a probable
conclusion, and the `だろう` at the end is the partner this adverb expects.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: この結果(けっか)は、（きっと・おそらく）気温(きおん)の影響(えいきょう)によるものと思(おも)われる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>おそらく</b>気温(きおん)の影響(えいきょう)によるものと思(おも)われる。

The sentence is written analysis, complete with the formal
と思(おも)われる ending. `きっと` would import a note of personal conviction that
does not belong in a report.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) おそらく彼(かれ)は来(こ)ないでしょう。
(b) もしかしたら彼(かれ)は来(こ)ないかもしれません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) is a considered estimate: the speaker has reasons and expects him not
to turn up. Sentence (b) merely floats the possibility, and the speaker would not
be surprised either way. In practice (a) is what you say when planning around his
absence, and (b) is what you say when raising a risk nobody had mentioned yet.

</details>

# もしかしたら

**Meaning** · maybe; there's a chance that - raising a possibility the speaker has
just thought of

`もしかしたら` opens the door to something that has not been considered yet, often
something slightly unexpected or unwelcome. It is a low-confidence word: the
speaker is not predicting, only pointing at a possibility.

## Usage

`もしかしたら` sits at the head of the sentence. Like every adverb of conjecture,
its real lesson is the **partner at the other end of the sentence**:

- **もしかしたら～かもしれない／かも** - the default pairing, by a wide margin.
  もしかしたら明日(あした)は休(やす)みかもしれない。
- **もしかしたら～のではないか** - the same idea in careful or written style.
  もしかしたら何(なに)か誤解(ごかい)があるのではないでしょうか。
- **もしかしたら～かな／かしら** - thinking aloud.
  もしかしたら道(みち)に迷(まよ)ったのかな。

Leaving the sentence to end flatly - ❌もしかしたら雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ります - is
the single most common error. The adverb only sets up the possibility; something at
the end has to keep it open.

Three variants mean essentially the same thing and are interchangeable in most
sentences: もしかすると (a shade more formal), ひょっとしたら and ひょっとすると
(more colloquial, slightly more surprised). `もしかして` looks like a fourth but
behaves differently - see the contrast below.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>もしかしたら</b>明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)かもしれない。
There's a chance it'll snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もしかしたら</b>、鍵(かぎ)を会社(かいしゃ)に忘(わす)れてきたかも。
I might have left my keys at the office.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なん)の連絡(れんらく)もないので、<b>もしかしたら</b>日(ひ)にちを間違(まちが)えているのではないでしょうか。
There's been no word at all, so I wonder if she hasn't got the date wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もしかしたら</b>来年(らいねん)、大阪(おおさか)に転勤(てんきん)になるかもしれないと上司(じょうし)に言(い)われた。
My boss told me there's a chance I'll be transferred to Osaka next year.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and usable in ます-form or plain form without changing shape. In
careful business speech it usually comes with the fuller ending
のではないでしょうか rather than `かも`, which is where the register is actually
carried.

The word has a distinctly tentative colour. Because it presents an idea as one that
has only just occurred to the speaker, it is a soft way to raise something
uncomfortable: もしかしたら私(わたし)の勘違(かんちが)いかもしれませんが…
lets you question someone's claim while taking the blame for the doubt yourself.
That politeness trick is worth stealing.

Contracted `もしかしたら～かも` is very common in speech and messages, and the whole
sentence is often left trailing: 「もしかしたら無理(むり)かも…」 The trailing-off
is part of the effect - you have floated the bad news without stating it.

On the confidence ladder, `もしかしたら` sits at the bottom: below たぶん and well
below おそらく. If you are more than about half sure, it undersells you.

## Contrast

### もしかしたら vs もしかして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もしかしたら` floats a possibility to the world and ends in
`かもしれない`. `もしかして` aims a guess at the listener and ends in a question.

</div>

<pre>
<b>もしかして</b>、田中(たなか)さんですか。
Excuse me - are you Tanaka, by any chance?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もしかしたら</b>田中(たなか)さんも来(く)るかもしれません。
There's a chance Tanaka will come too.
</pre>

`もしかして` is what you say when you suspect something about the person in front
of you - that they are the one you were told to meet, that they are angry, that
they have not eaten. It expects an answer. Using `もしかしたら` there
（❌もしかしたら田中(たなか)さんですか）sounds like you are guessing at a stranger
rather than asking one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ もしかしたら彼女(かのじょ)は来(き)ます。 → ✅ もしかしたら彼女(かのじょ)は来(こ)ない<b>かもしれません</b>。
  A bare ます-ending asserts, which cancels the doubt the adverb just raised. The
  possibility has to stay open at the end of the sentence.

- ❌ もしかしたら明日(あした)会議(かいぎ)がありますか。 → ✅ <b>もしかして</b>明日(あした)会議(かいぎ)がありますか。
  A direct question to the listener takes `もしかして`. `もしかしたら` is for
  possibilities you are laying out, not for questions you are putting.

- ❌ もしかしたら絶対(ぜったい)遅(おく)れる。 → ✅ <b>きっと</b>遅(おく)れる。
  You cannot hedge and insist in the same breath. If you are sure, drop the adverb
  of possibility entirely.

## Practice

**1.** 電気(でんき)がついているから、____誰(だれ)か残(のこ)っている____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電気(でんき)がついているから、<b>もしかしたら</b>誰(だれ)か残(のこ)っている<b>かもしれない</b>。

Both blanks belong to one frame. The adverb opens the possibility and
<code>かもしれない</code> keeps it open; filling only one of them leaves the sentence
lopsided.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: （もしかして・もしかしたら）今(いま)、お時間(じかん)ありませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もしかして</b>今(いま)、お時間(じかん)ありませんか。

The speaker is putting a guess directly to the person in front of them and wants an
answer, which is the question-shaped use. `もしかしたら` would be floating an idea
into the air with nobody to answer it.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もしかしたら間(ま)に合(あ)わないかもしれません。
(b) たぶん間(ま)に合(あ)わないと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) raises a risk: the speaker still expects to make it, but wants the
listener warned in case. Sentence (b) is an actual expectation - the speaker
believes they will miss it. If you say (a) and then arrive late, nobody is
surprised; if you say (b), the listener will already have made other plans.

</details>

# まるで

**Meaning** · just like; as if - flagging a comparison that everyone knows is not
literally true

`まるで` is the word that turns a plain statement into a simile. It tells the
listener in advance: what I am about to say is an exaggeration, take it as a
picture rather than a fact.

## Usage

`まるで` is an adverb and normally opens the clause. The lesson of this page is the
ending, because `まるで` cannot stand alone - it must be answered by a
likeness-marker:

- **まるで～ようだ／ような／ように** - the standard, and the one to learn first.
  まるで夢(ゆめ)のようだ。
- **まるで～みたいだ／みたいな／みたいに** - the same thing in casual speech.
  まるで子供(こども)みたいに喜(よろこ)んでいた。
- **まるで～かのようだ** - literary and a shade more emphatic; common in writing.
  まるで何(なに)もなかったかのように話(はな)し続(つづ)けた。

A sentence like ❌まるで夢(ゆめ)だ is broken. Whatever comes after `まるで` has to
be marked as a comparison; without that, you have asserted the impossible thing
instead of comparing to it.

There is a second, quite different use. With a **negative** predicate, `まるで`
means "not at all": まるで話(はなし)にならない, まるで覚(おぼ)えていない. Here
it is an intensifier of the negation, and no `ようだ` appears. This use is
emphatic and a little literary; ぜんぜん and まったく are the everyday equivalents.

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)は<b>まるで</b>春(はる)のように暖(あたた)かい。
It's as warm as spring today.
</pre>

<pre>
その犬(いぬ)は<b>まるで</b>ぬいぐるみみたいだった。
That dog looked just like a stuffed toy.
</pre>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)ぶりに来(き)たのに、<b>まるで</b>昨日(きのう)のことのように覚(おぼ)えている。
I hadn't been back in a year, yet I remember it as though it were yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
質問(しつもん)には一(ひと)つも答(こた)えず、<b>まるで</b>聞(き)こえていないかのように窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ていた。
He answered not one question and stared out of the window as if he could not hear us.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and common in speech, novels and journalism alike. The choice of
ending carries the register instead: `みたい` is casual, `ようだ` is neutral,
`かのようだ` is written and rather literary.

`まるで` is an exaggeration marker, so the listener understands the comparison to be
false. That makes it a good tool for complaint and for praise, and a bad one for
description. If the resemblance is real - a child who genuinely looks like her
mother - the natural word is そっくり: 母親(ははおや)にそっくりだ. Saying
まるで母親(ははおや)のようだ suggests the likeness is uncanny rather than simply
present.

Also note that `まるで` is optional. 夢(ゆめ)のようだ is a complete and natural
sentence; adding `まるで` in front of it only turns the volume up. Overusing it in
writing quickly sounds breathless.

The negative use is a different register again. まるで分(わ)からない is
emphatic, slightly old-fashioned in flavour, and more common in writing and in
grumbling than in neutral conversation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はまるで王様(おうさま)だ。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はまるで王様(おうさま)<b>のようだ</b>。
  Without a likeness-marker the sentence claims he *is* a king. `まるで` sets up a
  comparison and needs `ようだ`, `みたいだ` or `かのようだ` to complete it.

- ❌ 姉(あね)はまるで母(はは)のようです。（meaning they really do look alike）→ ✅ 姉(あね)は母(はは)に<b>そっくり</b>です。
  `まるで` says the likeness is a figure of speech. For an actual family
  resemblance it undercuts what you meant.

- ❌ まるで漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)めます。 → ✅ まるで漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)め<b>ません</b>。
  In its intensifier use, `まるで` attaches only to a negative. There is no
  affirmative version of that sense.

## Practice

**1.** 雨(あめ)にぬれて、____ぬれた猫(ねこ)____姿(すがた)だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

雨(あめ)にぬれて、<b>まるで</b>ぬれた猫(ねこ)<b>のような</b>姿(すがた)だった。

The two blanks are one frame: the adverb opens the comparison and
<code>のような</code> attaches it to the noun. Because a noun follows,
<code>のように</code> would be wrong here.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)の部屋(へや)はまるでホテルだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)の部屋(へや)はまるでホテル<b>のようだ</b>。

As written, the sentence says her room *is* a hotel. Adding <code>のようだ</code> - or
`みたいだ` in casual speech - turns it back into the comparison the speaker
intended.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)はまるで分(わ)からなかった。
(b) 彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)はあまり分(わ)からなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) uses the intensifier sense: nothing at all got through, and the tone
is emphatic, close to a complaint. Sentence (b) is measured - some of it landed,
much of it did not. Note that (a) contains no comparison at all, which is why no
<code>ようだ</code> appears in it.

</details>

# なるべく

**Meaning** · as far as possible; if you can - a preference stated without making
it an order

`なるべく` softens whatever follows it into an aim rather than a requirement. Put
it in front of a request and the request becomes a wish; put it in front of your
own plan and the plan becomes an intention you will try to keep.

## Usage

`なるべく` is an adverb. It goes immediately in front of the part it qualifies - 
usually a verb, often an adverb of degree such as 早(はや)く or 多(おお)く - 
and it does not change form.

Three places it lives:

- **Requests.** なるべく早(はや)めにお返事(へんじ)をください。 The deadline is
  still a deadline, but you have signalled that you understand life happens.
- **Negative requests.** なるべく音(おと)を立(た)てないでください。 Very common
  on notices, where an outright prohibition would be too strong.
- **Resolutions and habits.** なるべく歩(ある)くようにしています。 Here it pairs
  naturally with <code>ようにする</code>, which is itself a "make an effort to" pattern.

`なるべく` belongs with things that are still open - requests, intentions, advice.
It sits badly on a finished fact: ❌なるべく早(はや)く来(き)ました reports
what you did, and there is nothing left to try for. Use できるだけ急(いそ)いで
来(き)ました, or simply 急(いそ)いで来(き)ました.

In kanji it is 成(な)るべく, but the kana spelling is overwhelmingly the norm and
the kanji looks archaic.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>なるべく</b>早(はや)く帰(かえ)ります。
I'll be home as early as I can.
</pre>

<pre>
夜(よる)は<b>なるべく</b>コーヒーを飲(の)まないようにしている。
I try not to drink coffee at night if I can help it.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)が狭(せま)いので、<b>なるべく</b>荷物(にもつ)は少(すく)なめにお願(ねが)いします。
The venue is small, so please keep your luggage to a minimum if you can.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)は急(いそ)ぎませんが、<b>なるべく</b>今週中(こんしゅうちゅう)にいただけると助(たす)かります。
There's no rush on the reply, but it would help if I could have it this week if at all possible.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`なるべく` is neutral to slightly informal, and works fine inside ます-form speech - 
なるべく早(はや)くお送(おく)りします is perfectly good business Japanese. It is
common in notices and instructions, where its softness is the whole point.

The softness cuts both ways. Attach `なるべく` to something that genuinely must
happen and you have accidentally made it optional: a hard deadline announced as
なるべく明日(あした)までに will be read as "tomorrow would be nice". When the
requirement is firm, drop the adverb, or use 必(かなら)ず.

Received rather than given, `なるべく` is a small courtesy: the speaker is telling
you they know what they are asking may be inconvenient. Answering
「はい、なるべく早(はや)く」 in return is a natural way to accept without
promising a time.

## Contrast

### なるべく vs できるだけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same request, measured differently. `なるべく` asks for
willingness; できるだけ asks for the maximum that is actually possible.

</div>

<pre>
<b>なるべく</b>静(しず)かにしてください。
Please keep it down if you can.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>できるだけ</b>静(しず)かにしてください。
Please be as quiet as you possibly can.
</pre>

Both are polite and both are common; できるだけ is the firmer of the two because it
names the limit of your ability and asks you to go to it. It is also the one that
suits objective, written contexts - できるだけ正確(せいかく)に記入(きにゅう)して
ください on a form. Stronger still, and distinctly formal, is
できる限(かぎ)り. Where a difference is felt, the ordering runs
`なるべく` - できるだけ - できる限(かぎ)り, but in most everyday sentences the
first two are interchangeable.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は九時(くじ)に来(き)てください。なるべく遅(おく)れないでください。 → ✅ …<b>絶対(ぜったい)に</b>遅(おく)れないでください。
  If lateness is genuinely not acceptable, `なるべく` gives permission you did not
  mean to give. Save it for things you would forgive.

- ❌ なるべく昨日(きのう)片付(かたづ)けました。 → ✅ <b>できるだけ</b>片付(かたづ)けました。
  A completed action leaves nothing to aim at. `なるべく` needs a request, a plan
  or a habit - something still in front of you.

- ❌ なるべくの早(はや)い時間(じかん)にお願(ねが)いします。 → ✅ <b>なるべく</b>早(はや)い時間(じかん)にお願(ねが)いします。
  `なるべく` is an adverb and takes no `の`. It attaches straight to what it
  modifies.

## Practice

**1.** 席(せき)が少(すく)ないので、____早(はや)めにお越(こ)しください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>なるべく</b>早(はや)めにお越(こ)しください。

The request is real but the host is not in a position to insist, so the softening
adverb fits exactly. できるだけ would also work and would push a little harder.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)はなるべく急(いそ)いで作(つく)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は<b>できるだけ</b>急(いそ)いで作(つく)りました。

The action is finished, so there is no remaining effort for `なるべく` to point at.
できるだけ works because it describes the extent of the effort that was actually
made.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) なるべく明日(あした)までに出(だ)してください。
(b) 必(かなら)ず明日(あした)までに出(だ)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) states a preferred deadline and quietly accepts that it might slip;
the listener will not feel they have broken a promise if they submit on the day
after. Sentence (b) is an instruction with no give in it. Choosing between them is
really a choice about what you will do if the deadline is missed.

</details>

# めったに

**Meaning** · hardly ever; rarely - and only ever with a negative verb

`めったに` says that something happens so seldom it is almost worth remarking on.
The frequency it describes is very low - lower than あまり, close to
ほとんど - and the word is grammatically incomplete without a negative to attach to.

## Usage

`めったに` is an adverb and goes in front of the predicate. The rule that matters:

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `めったに` **requires a negative predicate**. めったに～ない,
めったに～ません. There is no affirmative version.

</div>

❌めったに行(い)く and ❌めったに見(み)ます are not "rarely go" - they are not
Japanese at all. The negative carries the frequency; `めったに` only tells you how
far down the scale it goes.

- 外食(がいしょく)はめったにしません。
- 彼(かれ)はめったに怒(おこ)らない。
- 雪(ゆき)はめったに降(ふ)りませんでした。 - past tense works normally.

One fixed expression is worth learning as a unit: <code>めったにない</code> used before
a noun means "rare, exceptional" - めったにないチャンス, "a chance you don't get
twice". The same negative rule is still doing the work; it has simply become a
noun-modifier.

In kanji it is 滅多(めった)に, an ateji spelling that appears in print, but the
kana is more usual.

## Examples

<pre>
父(ちち)は<b>めったに</b>映画(えいが)を見(み)ません。
My father hardly ever watches films.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)では<b>めったに</b>雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)らない。
It almost never snows around here.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しくて、平日(へいじつ)に家族(かぞく)そろって夕食(ゆうしょく)をとることは<b>めったに</b>ない。
We're so busy that the whole family is hardly ever at dinner together on a weekday.
</pre>

<pre>
あの寡黙(かもく)な部長(ぶちょう)が<b>めったに</b>見(み)せない笑顔(えがお)を見(み)せた。
Our famously silent department head produced a smile of a kind he almost never shows.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral: it appears in ます-form and plain form, in speech and in writing,
with no change. It is not marked for age or gender.

What it does carry is a faint note of *comment*. Saying めったに来(こ)ない tends
to imply that the rarity is notable - surprising, disappointing, or precious.
When you simply want to report a low frequency with no colour at all, あまり～ない
is flatter and safer.

Because the frequency is so low, `めったに` and a stated number sit badly together.
月(つき)に一回(いっかい)めったに行(い)かない is contradictory: if you can
count them, they are not "hardly ever". Use たまに行(い)きます for a countable
occasional habit.

Note finally that `めったに` describes frequency, not degree. It cannot modify an
adjective: ❌めったに高(たか)くない is wrong for "not very expensive"; that is
あまり高(たか)くない.

## Contrast

### めったに vs あまり○○ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both need a negative, but `あまり` measures **degree or
moderate frequency**, and `めったに` measures **very low frequency only**.

</div>

<pre>
お酒(さけ)は<b>あまり</b>飲(の)みません。
I don't drink much.
</pre>

<pre>
お酒(さけ)は<b>めったに</b>飲(の)みません。
I almost never drink.
</pre>

The first speaker probably has a beer now and then; the second is close to
teetotal. And because `あまり` can also scale degree, it works with adjectives - 
あまり辛(から)くない - where `めったに` cannot. The everyday positive counterpart
of both is たまに, "occasionally", which takes an ordinary affirmative verb:
たまに飲(の)みます.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)はめったに外食(がいしょく)します。 → ✅ 私(わたし)はめったに外食(がいしょく)し<b>ません</b>。
  The adverb cannot appear with an affirmative predicate. If you mean "I eat out
  occasionally", the word you want is たまに.

- ❌ この店(みせ)はめったに高(たか)くない。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は<b>あまり</b>高(たか)くない。
  `めったに` counts occasions, not degrees, so it cannot modify an adjective.

- ❌ 年(ねん)に二回(にかい)ぐらいめったに会(あ)わない。 → ✅ 年(ねん)に二回(にかい)ぐらいしか会(あ)わない。
  Naming a frequency and then calling it "hardly ever" pulls in two directions.
  Pick one: either the number, or the adverb.

## Practice

**1.** 忙(いそが)しいので、旅行(りょこう)には____行(い)き____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

旅行(りょこう)には<b>めったに</b>行(い)き<b>ません</b>。

The two blanks are a single frame - the adverb is only half a statement until the
negative arrives at the end. Filling the first blank and leaving an affirmative
verb would be ungrammatical rather than merely odd.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 甘(あま)いものは（めったに・あまり）好(す)きじゃない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

甘(あま)いものは<b>あまり</b>好(す)きじゃない。

<code>好(す)き</code> is a state, not a repeated event, so there are no occasions to
count. `あまり` scales the degree of liking, which is what the sentence needs.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 田中(たなか)さんはめったに遅刻(ちこく)しない。
(b) 田中(たなか)さんは遅刻(ちこく)しない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (b) is a flat description of a reliable person. Sentence (a) admits that
it has happened once or twice and, by pointing at how seldom it happens, quietly
turns the remark into praise - or, depending on the conversation, into a way of
saying that today's lateness is out of character.

</details>

# 決して～ない

**Meaning** · never; not under any circumstances - a categorical denial or an
absolute commitment

決(けっ)して does not make a sentence negative; the verb does that. What
決(けっ)して adds is *finality*. It closes the door on exceptions, which is why
it belongs to promises, warnings, rules and firm denials rather than to ordinary
reporting.

## Formation

決(けっ)して is an adverb placed before the predicate, and the predicate must be
negative. Everything else about the sentence is unchanged.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | 決(けっ)して + ない-form | 忘(わす)れる → 決(けっ)して忘(わす)れない |
| Verb (polite) | 決(けっ)して + ません | 言(い)う → 決(けっ)して言(い)いません |
| Verb (past) | 決(けっ)して + なかった／ませんでした | 許(ゆる)す → 決(けっ)して許(ゆる)さなかった |
| Verb (potential) | 決(けっ)して + ～られない | 忘(わす)れる → 決(けっ)して忘(わす)れられない |
| い-adjective | 決(けっ)して + くない | 安(やす)い → 決(けっ)して安(やす)くない |
| な-adjective・Noun | 決(けっ)して + ではない／じゃない | 無駄(むだ) → 決(けっ)して無駄(むだ)ではない |
| Prohibition | 決(けっ)して + ないでください／てはいけない | 触(さわ)る → 決(けっ)して触(さわ)らないでください |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no negative, no 決(けっ)して. There is no affirmative use of
this word in modern Japanese, so ❌決(けっ)して行(い)きます is not a weaker
version of anything - it is simply broken.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negative does not have to sit immediately next to
決(けっ)して, but it does have to be in the same clause.
決(けっ)して人(ひと)の悪口(わるぐち)を言(い)わない is fine: the adverb
is at the front, the negation at the end, and everything between them is inside
the scope.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この約束(やくそく)は<b>決(けっ)して忘(わす)れません</b>。
I will never forget this promise.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>決(けっ)して</b>弱音(よわね)を吐(は)か<b>ない</b>。
He never lets on that he's struggling.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は<b>決(けっ)して</b>安(やす)く<b>ない</b>が、作(つく)りはしっかりしている。
It is by no means cheap, but it's solidly made.
</pre>

<pre>
中(なか)の水(みず)は<b>決(けっ)して</b>飲(の)ま<b>ないでください</b>。
Under no circumstances drink the water inside.
</pre>

<pre>
あのときいただいたご恩(おん)を、私(わたし)は<b>決(けっ)して</b>忘(わす)れ<b>ません</b>。
I will never forget the kindness you showed me then.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)したからといって、それが<b>決(けっ)して</b>無駄(むだ)だった<b>わけではありません</b>。
Just because it failed doesn't mean it was wasted effort.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)のため、走行中(そうこうちゅう)は<b>決(けっ)して</b>席(せき)をお立(た)ちに<b>ならないでください</b>。
For your safety, please do not leave your seat at any time while the vehicle is moving.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

決(けっ)して is emphatic and leans formal. It is the natural word in written
rules, safety notices, apologies, contracts and public statements, and it is what
you reach for in speech when you want to be heard as making a commitment rather
than a remark. Among friends, the everyday word for the same force is
絶対(ぜったい)に.

Because it is absolute, it is also a favourite of denial. A politician saying
決(けっ)してそのような事実(じじつ)はございません is not saying "probably
not" - and the very strength of the word is why it can sound defensive when
overused.

Two smaller points. The kana spelling けっして is common in casual writing, and the
kanji 決(けっ)して in print; the reading is the same. And in formal writing you
will meet 決(けっ)して～まい, an old-fashioned negative-volitional pairing
meaning "I shall never": 決(けっ)して忘(わす)れまい. Recognise it; you do not
need to produce it.

## Contrast

### 決(けっ)して vs 絶対(ぜったい)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 絶対(ぜったい)に works with both affirmatives and negatives.
決(けっ)して works only with negatives, and sounds more formal doing it.

</div>

<pre>
絶対(ぜったい)に<b>勝(か)ちます</b>。
We are absolutely going to win.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>決(けっ)して</b>あきらめ<b>ません</b>。
I will never give up.
</pre>

Note that ❌決(けっ)して勝(か)ちます is impossible while
絶対(ぜったい)に負(ま)けません is perfectly natural - so
絶対(ぜったい)に is the more flexible word, and the one you will hear far more
often in conversation. In writing, 決(けっ)して is the more dignified of the two.

### 決(けっ)して vs 全然(ぜんぜん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 全然(ぜんぜん) measures **degree**: not at all, right now.
決(けっ)して makes a claim about **every occasion**: not ever, no exceptions.

</div>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は<b>全然(ぜんぜん)</b>分(わ)かり<b>ません</b>。
I don't understand this question at all.
</pre>

<pre>
嘘(うそ)は<b>決(けっ)して</b>つき<b>ません</b>。
I never tell lies.
</pre>

Putting 全然(ぜんぜん) in the second sentence would say your lies are
unconvincing rather than absent. And 決(けっ)して in the first would turn a
statement about today's homework into a lifelong vow of incomprehension.

### 決(けっ)して vs めったに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both take a negative, but めったに leaves room for exceptions
and 決(けっ)して does not.

</div>

<pre>
父(ちち)は<b>めったに</b>怒(おこ)ら<b>ない</b>。
My father hardly ever gets angry.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は<b>決(けっ)して</b>怒(おこ)ら<b>ない</b>。
My father never gets angry - not once.
</pre>

めったに is a frequency report; 決(けっ)して is a claim about principle or
character. If you can imagine a counter-example, you want めったに.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 決(けっ)して行(い)きます。 → ✅ <b>絶対(ぜったい)に</b>行(い)きます。
  The word cannot appear with an affirmative predicate at all. For an emphatic
  positive promise, 絶対(ぜったい)に is the word.

- ❌ 決(けっ)して好(す)きです。 → ✅ 決(けっ)して嫌(きら)い<b>ではありません</b>。
  The same trap with an adjective. Note how the natural fix flips the sentence into
  a denial, which is the shape this adverb always wants.

- ❌ 週(しゅう)に一回(いっかい)ぐらいしか決(けっ)して行(い)きません。 → ✅ 週(しゅう)に一回(いっかい)ぐらいしか行(い)きません。
  A stated frequency contradicts an absolute denial. If exceptions exist, use
  めったに or あまり instead.

- ❌ 決(けっ)して忘(わす)れないでください、大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です。 → ✅ 忘(わす)れないでくださいね。
  In a light, friendly reminder 決(けっ)して is far too heavy - it sounds as
  though a serious consequence is waiting. Keep it for real warnings.

## Practice

**1.** 安全(あんぜん)のため、工事中(こうじちゅう)はこの線(せん)より中(なか)に____入(はい)ら____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この線(せん)より中(なか)に<b>決(けっ)して</b>入(はい)ら<b>ないでください</b>。

The adverb and the negative are one frame: the first blank supplies the absolute
force and the second supplies the negation it needs. A safety notice is precisely
the register this word belongs to.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 母(はは)は（決(けっ)して・めったに）映画館(えいがかん)に行(い)きません。年(ねん)に一度(いちど)ぐらいでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

母(はは)は<b>めったに</b>映画館(えいがかん)に行(い)きません。

The second sentence admits about one visit a year, so the denial cannot be
absolute. 決(けっ)して would contradict the very next sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I will never say anything like that again."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>決(けっ)して</b>二度(にど)とそんなことは言(い)い<b>ません</b>。
（or simply 決(けっ)してそんなことは言(い)いません。）

The English "never … again" is a promise about all future occasions, which is what
this adverb is for. 二度(にど)と reinforces it, and the sentence still needs the
negative ending to be grammatical.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)の作品(さくひん)は決(けっ)して素晴(すば)らしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)の作品(さくひん)は決(けっ)して悪(わる)く<b>ありません</b>。
（or, if praise was intended: 彼(かれ)の作品(さくひん)は本当(ほんとう)に素晴(すば)らしいです。）

The original pairs an absolute-denial adverb with a positive adjective, which is
ungrammatical. Either make the predicate negative, or drop the adverb and use an
ordinary intensifier.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この方法(ほうほう)は決(けっ)して難(むずか)しくありません。
(b) この方法(ほうほう)は全然(ぜんぜん)難(むずか)しくありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both deny that the method is hard. Sentence (b) is a plain, casual measurement of
difficulty - there is none. Sentence (a) sounds like a rebuttal: someone has
suggested the method is difficult, and the speaker is closing that idea down, in
the slightly formal register of a manual or a presentation.

</details>

# べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない

**Meaning** · not really; not particularly - denying that there is anything special
to report

`べつに` does not deny the fact so much as deny that the fact matters. It says
there is nothing worth singling out here: no strong feeling, no problem, no reason
worth going into. That is what makes it so useful for deflecting a question - and
so easy to sound sulky with.

## Formation

`べつに` is an adverb placed before the predicate, and the predicate is negative.
Nothing attaches to it and it never changes shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | べつに + ない-form | 困(こま)る → べつに困(こま)らない |
| Verb (polite) | べつに + ません | 急(いそ)ぐ → べつに急(いそ)ぎません |
| い-adjective | べつに + くない | 忙(いそが)しい → べつに忙(いそが)しくない |
| な-adjective・Noun | べつに + じゃない／ではない | 嫌(きら)い → べつに嫌(きら)いじゃない |
| Permission | べつに + なくてもいい | 来(く)る → べつに来(こ)なくてもいい |
| Standalone | べつに。 | 「どうしたの?」「べつに。」 |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - like 決(けっ)して and めったに, `べつに` needs a negative
predicate. The standalone reply 「べつに。」 is not an exception: the negative is
simply left unsaid, and both speakers hear it.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is a second, unrelated 別(べつ)に meaning "separately", and it
takes an ordinary affirmative verb:
別(べつ)に包(つつ)んでいただけますか, "could you wrap them separately?"
Same characters, different word. Context separates them without any effort: if the
predicate is negative, it is the "not particularly" one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
「怒(おこ)ってる?」「<b>べつに</b>怒(おこ)って<b>ない</b>よ。」
"Are you angry?" "Not particularly, no."
</pre>

<pre>
<b>べつに</b>急(いそ)ぎ<b>ません</b>から、ゆっくりどうぞ。
There's no rush at all - please take your time.
</pre>

<pre>
「どうしたの?」「……<b>べつに</b>。」
"What's wrong?" "…Nothing."
</pre>

<pre>
<b>べつに</b>嫌(きら)い<b>じゃない</b>けど、自分(じぶん)では買(か)わないかな。
It's not that I dislike it - I just wouldn't buy one myself.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>べつに</b>深(ふか)い意味(いみ)は<b>ありません</b>。ただ気(き)になっただけです。
There's no deeper meaning to it. I was just curious.
</pre>

<pre>
遅(おく)れた理由(りゆう)を聞(き)いただけで、<b>べつに</b>責(せ)めるつもりは<b>ありません</b>。
I only asked why you were late; I'm not blaming you or anything.
</pre>

<pre>
「手伝(てつだ)おうか?」「ありがとう。でも<b>べつに</b>一人(ひとり)でも平気(へいき)だから、休(やす)んでて。」
"Want a hand?" "Thanks, but I'm honestly fine on my own - go and rest."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`べつに` is casual to neutral. It is entirely normal inside ます-form speech - 
べつに問題(もんだい)ありません is ordinary office Japanese - but the flavour is
conversational, and in a formal document 特(とく)に is the better word.

Everything depends on delivery. Said warmly and followed by the rest of the
sentence, `べつに` reassures: べつに迷惑(めいわく)じゃありませんよ. Said flat, alone,
and clipped, 「べつに。」 is the classic teenage non-answer - "nothing", meaning
"something, and I'm not telling you". Learners sometimes pick up the standalone
form from television and use it with a teacher or a manager, where it lands as
rude. Keep the rest of the sentence, and the risk disappears.

The word also does real politeness work. Because it denies that anything special is
going on, it lowers the temperature: telling someone
べつに怒(おこ)っていませんよ is gentler than a bare
怒(おこ)っていません, which can sound like a protest.

Both spellings, 別(べつ)に and べつに, are common. The kana version looks
slightly softer and is usual in messages.

## Contrast

### べつに vs 特(とく)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 特(とく)に～ない is a neutral "nothing in particular".
`べつに` adds a shrug, and sometimes a hint of defensiveness.

</div>

<pre>
質問(しつもん)は<b>特(とく)に</b>ありません。
I have no questions in particular.
</pre>

<pre>
質問(しつもん)は<b>べつに</b>ないけど。
Nah, I haven't got any questions.
</pre>

In a meeting, on a form, or replying to a customer, 特(とく)に is the safe
choice. `べつに` in the same slot is not wrong, but it sounds like you could not be
bothered to think about it.

### べつに vs あまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - あまり～ない scales the **degree**: "not very". `べつに` denies
that the thing is **notable at all**.

</div>

<pre>
この映画(えいが)は<b>あまり</b>面白(おもしろ)く<b>なかった</b>。
That film wasn't very good.
</pre>

<pre>
この映画(えいが)は<b>べつに</b>面白(おもしろ)く<b>なかった</b>。
That film was nothing special.
</pre>

The first is a measured judgement; the second dismisses the film, and often
implies the speaker is contradicting someone who praised it.

### べつに vs 全然(ぜんぜん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 全然(ぜんぜん) is emphatic: not at all, none whatsoever.
`べつに` is deliberately unemphatic.

</div>

<pre>
<b>全然(ぜんぜん)</b>気(き)にして<b>ない</b>よ。
I don't mind in the slightest.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>べつに</b>気(き)にして<b>ない</b>よ。
I don't especially mind.
</pre>

Reassuring a friend who has apologised, 全然(ぜんぜん) is warmer, because it
sounds like you mean it. `べつに` can come out cooler than you intended - which is
exactly why it works when you *want* to sound unbothered.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ べつに好(す)きです。 → ✅ べつに嫌(きら)い<b>じゃない</b>です。
  The predicate must be negative. Learners who translate "not really" one word at a
  time often leave the verb affirmative and reverse the meaning.

- ❌（部長(ぶちょう)に）「何(なに)かありましたか。」「べつに。」 → ✅ 「いえ、<b>特(とく)に</b>ありません。」
  The one-word reply is fine among friends and abrupt to anyone above you. Give the
  full sentence, or switch to 特(とく)に.

- ❌ べつに包(つつ)まないでください。（meaning "please wrap them separately"）→ ✅ <b>別(べつ)に</b>包(つつ)んでください。
  With an affirmative verb the word means "separately", so a negative here says the
  opposite of what you wanted.

- ❌ べつに三人(さんにん)しか来(こ)なかった。 → ✅ 三人(さんにん)しか来(こ)なかった。
  `べつに` denies that anything is noteworthy, so it clashes with `しか`, which
  points out that the number is small.

## Practice

**1.** 「この色(いろ)、変(へん)かな?」「____変(へん)じゃないと思(おも)うよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「<b>べつに</b>変(へん)じゃないと思(おも)うよ。」

The speaker is playing the worry down - there is nothing odd worth remarking on - 
and the negative <code>じゃない</code> is the partner the adverb requires.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 会議(かいぎ)で「ご意見(いけん)は?」と聞(き)かれて → 「（べつに・特(とく)に）ございません。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「<b>特(とく)に</b>ございません。」

A meeting calls for the neutral, formal word. `べつに` alongside the very polite
ございません would mix registers and make the reply sound offhand.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's not that I don't want to go - I just have no money."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>べつに</b>行(い)きたくない<b>わけじゃない</b>。ただお金(かね)がないだけ。

`べつに` sets up the denial and <code>わけじゃない</code> completes it, which is a very
common pairing when you are correcting an impression somebody has formed.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: べつに問題(もんだい)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ べつに問題(もんだい)は<b>ありません</b>。

`べつに` cannot stand with an affirmative predicate in this sense. If you did mean
"there is a separate problem", the natural phrasing is
別(べつ)の問題(もんだい)があります.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 「どうかした?」「べつに。」
(b) 「どうかした?」「ううん、なんでもないよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both mean "nothing". Reply (a) is clipped, and to a Japanese ear it usually signals
the opposite of what it says: something is wrong and the speaker does not want to
discuss it. Reply (b) closes the subject warmly and invites no follow-up. If you
genuinely have nothing to report, (b) is much the safer choice.

</details>

# ふと - a thought that arrives on its own

**Meaning** · suddenly; out of nowhere - used of a thought, a memory or a feeling
that surfaces without being summoned

`ふと` is the word for the moment when something occurs to you. Nobody made it
happen, you were not trying to remember it, and there is no cause worth naming - 
it simply came.

## Usage

`ふと` is an adverb and stands directly in front of the verb. It does not
conjugate and takes no particles.

The lesson of the page is which verbs it goes with. In this sense `ふと` selects
**verbs of the mind**:

- 思(おも)う - ふと思(おも)ったんだけど…
- 思(おも)い出(だ)す - ふと昔(むかし)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)した。
- 気(き)づく - ふと気(き)づくと、外(そと)は暗(くら)くなっていた。
- 考(かんが)える, 不安(ふあん)になる, 心配(しんぱい)になる, 頭(あたま)に浮(う)かぶ

Two shapes are near-fixed and worth learning whole:

- <code>ふと思(おも)うことがある</code> - "the thought sometimes strikes me".
- <code>ふと気(き)づくと～ていた</code> - "before I knew it, …". The `と` here is the
  N4 conditional, and the second half is almost always in a past or `ていた` form.

`ふと` describes a mental event happening to you, so it is almost always narrated
in the **past**. ❌ふと思(おも)います, as a statement about the present, does not
work; a thought you are having on purpose is not a `ふと` thought.

It cannot be used of external events. Rain, noises and visitors do not arrive
`ふと` - that is 急(きゅう)に or 突然(とつぜん).

## Examples

<pre>
<b>ふと</b>母(はは)の声(こえ)を思(おも)い出(だ)した。
My mother's voice suddenly came back to me.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ふと</b>気(き)づくと、電車(でんしゃ)はもう終点(しゅうてん)だった。
Before I knew it, the train had reached the last stop.
</pre>

<pre>
洗(あら)い物(もの)をしているとき、<b>ふと</b>来年(らいねん)のことが心配(しんぱい)になった。
While I was doing the washing-up, a worry about next year came over me out of nowhere.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)は何(なに)のために働(はたら)いているのだろうと、<b>ふと</b>考(かんが)えてしまうことがある。
Now and then the thought creeps up on me: what exactly am I working for?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ふと` is neutral in politeness - it fits ます-form and plain form equally - but it
is not evenly distributed. It belongs to reflective narration: novels, essays,
diary entries, and the kind of conversation where somebody is telling you what went
through their head. You will hear it far less in brisk, practical talk.

Because of that, `ふと` colours a sentence. It signals that the speaker was not in
control of the thought, which makes it useful for softening: ふと思(おも)った
んだけど、… is a gentle way to introduce a suggestion, since you are presenting it
as something that merely occurred to you rather than a position you have taken.

The word is written in kana. It has no kanji in ordinary use, no contraction, and
no politeness variants.

## Contrast

### ふと vs 急(きゅう)に・突然(とつぜん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 急(きゅう)に and 突然(とつぜん) describe an event
changing suddenly. `ふと` describes something surfacing in your own mind.

</div>

<pre>
<b>急(きゅう)に</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
It suddenly started to rain.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ふと</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)ってきていないことに気(き)づいた。
It struck me that I hadn't brought an umbrella.
</pre>

The rain is out there in the world, so it takes 急(きゅう)に; the realisation is
inside the speaker's head, so it takes `ふと`. Note also the difference in force:
急(きゅう)に is abrupt and often unwelcome, while `ふと` is quiet - a thought
drifting in, not a door slamming.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ふと電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)った。 → ✅ <b>急(きゅう)に</b>電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)った。
  A ringing phone is an event in the world, not a thought in your mind. `ふと`
  cannot take an external subject.

- ❌ ふと思(おも)い出(だ)そうとした。 → ✅ <b>ふと</b>思(おも)い出(だ)した。
  Trying to remember is deliberate, and `ふと` means precisely that no effort was
  involved. The two cancel each other out.

- ❌ 毎朝(まいあさ)ふと母(はは)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)します。 → ✅ 毎朝(まいあさ)母(はは)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)します。
  Something that happens every morning is a habit, not a thought arriving
  unbidden. `ふと` needs the occasion to be one-off and unplanned.

## Practice

**1.** 散歩(さんぽ)をしていて、____学生時代(がくせいじだい)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

散歩(さんぽ)をしていて、<b>ふと</b>学生時代(がくせいじだい)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)した。

A memory surfacing during an unrelated activity, with no trigger the speaker cares
to name, is the core use of this adverb. The past tense is part of the pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: （ふと・急(きゅう)に）後(うし)ろから名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>急(きゅう)に</b>後(うし)ろから名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれた。

Someone else calling your name is an external event, so it needs the adverb for
sudden happenings. `ふと` would only work if the sentence described something
arising inside the speaker's own head.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ふと彼(かれ)の言葉(ことば)を思(おも)い出(だ)した。
(b) 彼(かれ)の言葉(ことば)を思(おも)い出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (b) is neutral and would also fit someone deliberately casting their mind
back. Sentence (a) says the memory arrived by itself, unprompted, in the middle of
something else - and because the speaker did not choose to have it, the remark
carries a hint that the words have been sitting with them.

</details>

# ふと - without meaning to

**Meaning** · unintentionally; by chance - of an action done with no purpose behind
it, and of what you happen to notice as a result

This is the same word as the `ふと` of thoughts arriving unbidden, pointed
outward. Here it modifies something you **did** - glancing up, stopping, saying a
thing - and its job is to strip the action of any intention.

## Usage

`ふと` stands directly in front of the verb. In this sense it selects small,
momentary, involuntary actions:

- 見(み)る・見(み)上(あ)げる - ふと空(そら)を見(み)上(あ)げた。
- 目(め)が合(あ)う - ふと目(め)が合(あ)って、気(き)まずかった。
- 立(た)ち止(ど)まる - ふと立(た)ち止(ど)まって振(ふ)り返(かえ)った。
- 口(くち)にする, 手(て)に取(と)る, 足(あし)を止(と)める

It also appears before a noun as `ふとした`, an adnominal form that means "chance,
trifling":

- ふとしたことから知(し)り合(あ)った。 - "we met through some small chance"
- ふとした瞬間(しゅんかん)に思(おも)い出(だ)す

Two limits are worth stating plainly. First, the action has to be **small and
momentary**: you can ふと glance at a shop window, but not ふと buy the shop.
❌ふと会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めた is wrong, because quitting a job takes
decisions. Second, `ふと` implies no carelessness and no damage. It is neutral - 
for an unintended action with a bad outcome, Japanese uses うっかり.

Like the other sense, `ふと` is almost always narrated in the past.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>ふと</b>空(そら)を見(み)上(あ)げると、星(ほし)が見(み)えた。
I happened to look up, and there were stars.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ふと</b>隣(となり)の人(ひと)と目(め)が合(あ)った。
My eyes met the person's next to me, quite by chance.
</pre>

<pre>
本屋(ほんや)で<b>ふと</b>手(て)に取(と)った本(ほん)が、今(いま)でも一番(いちばん)好(す)きな一冊(いっさつ)だ。
A book I picked up at random in a shop is still my favourite one.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の帰(かえ)りに<b>ふと</b>漏(も)らした一言(ひとこと)が、思(おも)わぬ誤解(ごかい)を招(まね)いてしまった。
An offhand remark I let slip on the way back from the meeting caused an unexpected misunderstanding.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in politeness, but stylistically marked: `ふと` belongs to
narration. It is common in novels, essays and anecdotes, and rarer in brisk
practical speech, where people simply say なんとなく ("for no particular reason")
or たまたま ("as it happened").

The word is quiet. It gives an action a drifting, unhurried feel, which is why
writers use it to open a scene - ふと窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ると… - and
why it sits oddly in a hurried context.

Note that `ふと` says nothing about the consequences. In the fourth example above
the remark caused trouble, but the trouble is carried by the rest of the sentence,
not by `ふと`. If you want the listener to hear that you were careless and are
sorry, you need うっかり.

`ふと` is written in kana, has no contraction, and is not marked for age or gender.

## Contrast

### ふと vs うっかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "without intending to", but うっかり adds
carelessness and a bad result. `ふと` is neutral and blames nobody.

</div>

<pre>
<b>うっかり</b>傘(かさ)を電車(でんしゃ)に忘(わす)れてしまった。
I carelessly left my umbrella on the train.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ふと</b>窓(まど)の外(そと)に目(め)をやった。
I let my eyes drift to the window.
</pre>

うっかり goes with mistakes - forgetting, mixing things up, pressing the wrong
button - and typically appears with `てしまう`, which is itself the marker of
regret. `ふと` goes with harmless small actions and does not take `てしまう` for
that reason. Swapping them is immediately audible:
❌うっかり空(そら)を見(み)上(あ)げた makes looking at the sky sound like an
error.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ふとパスワードを間違(まちが)えた。 → ✅ <b>うっかり</b>パスワードを間違(まちが)えた。
  A mistake with a consequence calls for the careless-action adverb. `ふと` says
  only that there was no intention, which is not what you want to convey when
  apologising.

- ❌ ふと三時間(さんじかん)散歩(さんぽ)した。 → ✅ <b>なんとなく</b>三時間(さんじかん)散歩(さんぽ)してしまった。
  `ふと` covers an instant, not a stretch of time. It marks the moment an action
  begins, so a three-hour activity is outside its range.

- ❌ ふとの一言(ひとこと)が問題(もんだい)になった。 → ✅ <b>ふとした</b>一言(ひとこと)が問題(もんだい)になった。
  Before a noun the form is `ふとした`, not ❌ふとの. As an adverb it modifies verbs
  only.

## Practice

**1.** 駅(えき)の前(まえ)で____立(た)ち止(ど)まって、来(き)た道(みち)を振(ふ)り返(かえ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)の前(まえ)で<b>ふと</b>立(た)ち止(ど)まって、来(き)た道(みち)を振(ふ)り返(かえ)った。

Stopping for no reason the speaker could name is exactly the unintended, momentary
action this adverb marks, and the past-tense narration is typical of it.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: （ふと・うっかり）大事(だいじ)なファイルを消(け)してしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>うっかり</b>大事(だいじ)なファイルを消(け)してしまった。

Deleting an important file is a mistake with a cost, and `てしまう` at the end is
already signalling regret. `ふと` would strangely report the deletion as an idle
gesture with no consequence attached.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ふとした質問(しつもん)から話(はなし)が広(ひろ)がった。
(b) ある質問(しつもん)から話(はなし)が広(ひろ)がった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (b) simply says a question started the conversation. Sentence (a) adds
that the question was a trivial one, asked with no plan behind it - and the
implication is that the interesting turn the conversation took was pure accident.
`ふとした` is doing that work in one word.

</details>
