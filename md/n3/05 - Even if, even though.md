# N3 - Even if, even though


---

# たとえ～ても

**Meaning** · even if - the speaker grants a supposition and says the outcome would not change

`たとえ` is an adverb parked at the head of the clause. It warns the listener in
advance: *what follows is a supposition, and I am about to throw it away.* The
grammar is carried by the `ても` you learned at N4; `たとえ` supplies the emphasis
and the sense of "however extreme this gets."

This is the calm end of the concession family. `のに` and `くせに` react to a gap
between expectation and reality that has already opened up, and they mind about it.
`たとえ～ても` looks forward at a gap that has not opened yet, and announces that the
speaker will not mind when it does.

## Formation

`たとえ` never changes shape and never attaches to anything. Everything conjugates
on the `ても` side, exactly as at N4.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | たとえ + て-form + も | 断(ことわ)られる → たとえ断(ことわ)られても |
| Verb (negative) | たとえ + なくて + も | 間(ま)に合(あ)わない → たとえ間(ま)に合(あ)わなくても |
| い-adjective | たとえ + くて + も | 厳(きび)しい → たとえ厳(きび)しくても |
| い-adjective (negative) | たとえ + くなくて + も | 安(やす)くない → たとえ安(やす)くなくても |
| な-adjective | たとえ + で + も | 無理(むり) → たとえ無理(むり)でも |
| Noun | たとえ + で + も | 冗談(じょうだん) → たとえ冗談(じょうだん)でも |
| Any plain clause (formal) | たとえ + plain form + としても | 失敗(しっぱい)する → たとえ失敗(しっぱい)するとしても |
| Verb (literary) | たとえ + volitional + と（も） | 言(い)われる → たとえ何(なに)を言(い)われようと |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たとえ` is decoration you can delete; `ても` is not. Take
`たとえ` out of たとえ寒(さむ)くても行(い)く and you still have a sentence. Take
`ても` out and you have nothing.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - write it in kana. The kanji spelling 例(たと)え collides with
<code>例(たと)えば</code> ("for example") and with <code>例(たと)える</code> ("to liken"),
which are different words entirely. The older form たとい still turns up in legal and
literary writing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
たとえ<b>安(やす)くても</b>、これは買(か)わない。
Even if it were cheap, I wouldn't buy this.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ<b>冗談(じょうだん)でも</b>、そんなことは言(い)わないで。
Don't say things like that, even as a joke.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ<b>一人(ひとり)になっても</b>、ぼくはこの案(あん)に賛成(さんせい)します。
Even if I end up the only one, I'm backing this proposal.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ<b>親(おや)に反対(はんたい)されても</b>、来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)するつもりだ。
Even if my parents are against it, I intend to go abroad to study next year.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ<b>忙(いそが)しくても</b>、返事(へんじ)だけは今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に出(だ)します。
However busy things get, I'll at least send a reply before the day is out.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ<b>失敗(しっぱい)したとしても</b>、挑戦(ちょうせん)したことは無駄(むだ)にならないよ。
Even supposing it doesn't work out, having tried won't have been for nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ<b>社長(しゃちょう)の指示(しじ)でも</b>、安全(あんぜん)を後回(あとまわ)しにするわけにはいきません。
Even if the order comes from the president, we can't put safety second.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ<b>何(なに)を言(い)われようと</b>、祖父(そふ)の店(みせ)は閉(し)めないと決(き)めている。
Whatever anyone says to me, I've made up my mind not to close my grandfather's shop.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たとえ` is not casual, but it is not stiff either. It is **emphatic**, and emphasis
has its own register: you hear it in speeches, in resolutions, in job interviews, and
in the moments when someone is stating what they will not compromise on. In an
ordinary exchange about the weather, people just use bare `ても` and leave `たとえ` out.

Because `たとえ` announces a supposition, it pulls the main clause toward **will and
judgement** rather than plain report. The natural endings are `つもりだ`, `～たい`,
`～なければならない`, `～わけにはいかない`, a negative, or a command. A flat factual
ending sounds like the sentence lost its point halfway through.

Three shapes, roughly by formality:

- たとえ～ても - the everyday one.
- たとえ～としても - a step more formal and more clearly hypothetical; common in
  writing and in careful speech.
- たとえ～（よ）うと（も） - literary and declamatory. Fine in a written pledge or a
  dramatic line, overblown in conversation.

`たとえ` also survives in fixed emotional phrasing:
たとえ一(いち)パーセントでも可能性(かのうせい)があるなら - "if there's even a one
percent chance." Notice the pattern: `たとえ` plus a deliberately tiny quantity plus
`でも` is how Japanese says "even so much as."

## Contrast

### たとえ～ても vs bare ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare `ても` covers both "even if" (a supposition) and "even when"
(a habit). `たとえ` selects the supposition reading and turns the volume up.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)が<b>あっても</b>、兄(あに)は練習(れんしゅう)に出(で)ている。
My brother turns up to practice even when he has a temperature.
</pre>

That is a report of what actually keeps happening. Adding `たとえ` there would be
odd, because there is nothing to suppose - it is already true. Now the other reading:

<pre>
たとえ熱(ねつ)が<b>あっても</b>、明日(あした)の練習(れんしゅう)は休(やす)まない。
Even if I'm running a temperature, I'm not missing practice tomorrow.
</pre>

Nothing has happened; the speaker is pre-committing. That is `たとえ`'s home ground.

### たとえ～ても vs いくら／どんなに～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たとえ` concedes **whether**; `いくら` and `どんなに` concede
**how much**. If there is no scale of degree or repetition, `いくら` has nothing to
measure.

</div>

<pre>
たとえ一円(いちえん)<b>でも</b>、人(ひと)のお金(かね)に手(て)をつけてはいけない。
Even one yen of someone else's money is not yours to touch.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら説明(せつめい)<b>しても</b>、彼(かれ)は納得(なっとく)してくれなかった。
No matter how much I explained, he wouldn't accept it.
</pre>

The first is a single yes-or-no supposition, so `たとえ`. The second is about repeated
effort piling up with no result, so `いくら`. Where a degree really is at stake both
can appear together: たとえどんなに時間(じかん)がかかっても。

### たとえ～ても vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たとえ～ても` is about a gap that has **not happened yet** and
the speaker does not mind. `のに` is about a gap that **has** happened and the
speaker does mind. They never swap.

</div>

<pre>
たとえ会社(かいしゃ)が遠(とお)<b>くても</b>、自転車(じてんしゃ)で通(かよ)います。
Even if the office turns out to be far, I'll cycle in.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)が遠(とお)い<b>のに</b>、毎日(まいにち)自転車(じてんしゃ)で通(かよ)っている。
The office is a long way off and yet he cycles in every day.
</pre>

The first speaker is deciding something in advance. The second is looking at a fact
and reacting to it - with surprise here, but `のに` can just as easily carry a
complaint. That is the axis this whole group of patterns is sorted on: how much the
speaker minds.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ たとえ雨(あめ)だても → ✅ たとえ雨(あめ)でも
  Nouns and な-adjectives take `で` + `も`. `だ` never survives in front of `ても`.

- ❌ たとえ降(ふ)ったても → ✅ たとえ降(ふ)っても
  No tense goes in front of `ても`. The final verb decides whether the sentence is
  about the future or the past.

- ❌ たとえば高(たか)くても買(か)います → ✅ たとえ高(たか)くても買(か)います
  `たとえば` is "for example" - a different word that happens to start the same way.
  There is no `ば` in this pattern.

- ❌ たとえ謝(あやま)っても、姉(あね)はまだ怒(おこ)っている → ✅ 謝(あやま)った<b>のに</b>、姉(あね)はまだ怒(おこ)っている
  If the apology actually happened, it is not a supposition any more. Reporting a
  real, disappointing outcome is `のに`'s job.

## Practice

**1.** たとえ子(こ)ども____、この約束(やくそく)は守(まも)らせます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

たとえ子(こ)ども<b>でも</b>、この約束(やくそく)は守(まも)らせます。

<code>子(こ)ども</code> is a noun, so the て-form it needs is `で`, giving `でも`.
The sentence says the speaker will hold even a child to the promise - a small,
extreme case granted and then dismissed, which is what `たとえ` is for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)が来(き)た（たとえ・のに）、試合(しあい)は予定(よてい)どおり行(おこな)われた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

台風(たいふう)が来(き)た<b>のに</b>、試合(しあい)は予定(よてい)どおり行(おこな)われた。

The typhoon actually arrived, so this is a real event, not a supposition - and
`たとえ` cannot attach to a past plain form anyway. The forward-looking version
would be たとえ台風(たいふう)が来(き)ても、試合(しあい)はやります。

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if nobody agrees with me, I'm going to say what I think."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

たとえ<b>だれも賛成(さんせい)してくれなくても</b>、思(おも)っていることは言(い)います。

The negative て-form is `なくて`, so the concessive is `なくても`. Note how naturally
the main clause states an intention: `たとえ` sets up a supposition precisely so the
speaker can declare that it changes nothing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: たとえ給料(きゅうりょう)が安(やす)いだても、この仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)けたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ たとえ給料(きゅうりょう)が安(やす)<b>くても</b>、この仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)けたい。

<code>安(やす)い</code> is an い-adjective, so its て-form is <code>安(やす)くて</code>
and the concessive is <code>安(やす)くても</code>. The `だ` is a noun-sentence ending
and has no business here; even with a noun the form would be `でも`, never `だても`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) たとえ一人(ひとり)でやることになっても、この企画(きかく)は進(すす)めます。
(b) 一人(ひとり)でやることになったのに、この企画(きかく)を進(すす)めています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a pledge made before the fact. The speaker imagines the worst version of the
situation - being left to do it alone - grants it, and says the plan goes ahead
regardless. There is no complaint in it; if anything it sounds determined.

(b) reports what did happen. The speaker was in fact left alone with the project and
is carrying on, and `のに` colours that with feeling: admiration if said about someone
else, and something close to a grumble if said about oneself.

</details>

# いくら～ても / いくら～でも

**Meaning** · no matter how much - turn the amount up as far as you like, the result still does not follow

`いくら` is the question word for a price: "how much?" Put it in front of a `ても`
clause and it stops asking and starts conceding. Whatever quantity of effort, time,
money, patience or intensity you want to imagine, the sentence says the outcome is
unaffected.

On this batch's axis - how much the speaker minds the gap between expectation and
outcome - `いくら～ても` sits in the middle. `たとえ～ても` grants one supposition and
shrugs. `いくら～ても` grants an unlimited amount of effort and usually reports that it
was wasted, so frustration leaks in even when no complaint is stated.

## Formation

`いくら` is an adverb. It stands at the head of the clause and the `ても` does the
conjugating, exactly as at N4. `でも` is not a separate word to learn: it is simply
what `ても` looks like after a noun or a な-adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | いくら + て-form + も | 呼(よ)ぶ → いくら呼(よ)んでも |
| Verb (negative) | いくら + なくて + も | 食(た)べない → いくら食(た)べなくても |
| い-adjective | いくら + くて + も | 寒(さむ)い → いくら寒(さむ)くても |
| い-adjective (negative) | いくら + くなくて + も | 甘(あま)くない → いくら甘(あま)くなくても |
| な-adjective | いくら + で + も | 好(す)き → いくら好(す)きでも |
| Noun | いくら + で + も | プロ → いくらプロでも |
| Fixed emphatic phrase | いくらなんでも + judgement | いくらなんでも安(やす)すぎる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `でも` here is the noun's て-form plus `も`, nothing else.
い-adjectives already have their own て-form, so they take `くても` and never `でも`:
✅<code>いくら寒(さむ)くても</code>, ❌<code>いくら寒(さむ)いでも</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `いくら` counts. It needs something that can be piled up: repetitions,
hours, yen, or a degree on a scale. That is why it fits
<code>いくら待(ま)っても</code> ("however long I wait") but not a one-shot yes-or-no
supposition, where `たとえ` is the word you want.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
いくら<b>呼(よ)んでも</b>、猫(ねこ)は出(で)てこなかった。
No matter how much I called, the cat wouldn't come out.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら<b>寒(さむ)くても</b>、弟(おとうと)は上着(うわぎ)を着(き)ない。
However cold it gets, my little brother won't put a coat on.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら<b>好(す)きでも</b>、毎日(まいにち)カレーだと飽(あ)きるよ。
However much you like it, curry every single day gets old.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら<b>考(かんが)えても</b>、いい題名(だいめい)が思(おも)いつかなかった。
However long I thought about it, no good title came to me.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら<b>練習(れんしゅう)しても</b>、発音(はつおん)だけはなかなか直(なお)らない。
No matter how much I practise, my pronunciation is the one thing that won't improve.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら<b>急(いそ)いでも</b>、この渋滞(じゅうたい)では終電(しゅうでん)に間(ま)に合(あ)いそうにない。
However much we hurry, in this traffic we're not going to make the last train.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら<b>部長(ぶちょう)に頼(たの)まれても</b>、土曜日(どようび)の出勤(しゅっきん)はお断(ことわ)りするつもりです。
However hard the manager presses me, I intend to say no to working Saturday.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いくらなんでも</b>、三日(みっか)で百(ひゃく)ページの翻訳(ほんやく)は無理(むり)ですよ。
A hundred pages of translation in three days is asking too much, surely.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`いくら～ても` is thoroughly colloquial and completely neutral as to politeness. It
appears in `ます` speech and in plain speech unchanged, and nothing about it is
male, female, young or old.

What it *is* marked for is **futility**. The commonest ending by far is a negative or
a disappointing result: 来(こ)なかった, 直(なお)らない,
分(わ)からない, ダメだった. A learner who uses `いくら～ても` with a happy outcome
will usually be misunderstood, because the pattern has been carrying resignation
since the first word. When the ending is positive it is nearly always a statement of
refusal or resolve - as in the Saturday example above - which is still the speaker
pushing back against pressure.

Two set expressions worth memorising whole:

- **いくらなんでも** - "surely, that's beyond reason." Not "no matter what": it is a
  protest against something excessive, and it needs a judgement after it
  (`ひどい`, `無理だ`, `～すぎる`).
- **いくら～からといって** - "just because ~". いくら安(やす)いからといって、三(さん)つも買(か)うことはない。

In writing, `どんなに` and the stiffer `いかに` often replace `いくら`; a formal report
is more likely to say いかに努力(どりょく)しても than いくら努力(どりょく)しても.
In speech the proportion reverses.

## Contrast

### いくら～ても vs どんなに～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いくら` counts an **amount** (money, time, tries, effort);
`どんなに` measures an **intensity** (how hard, how painful, how much someone cared).
They overlap in most sentences; where a literal quantity is in view, `いくら` is the
natural word, and where feeling is the point, `どんなに` is.

</div>

<pre>
いくら<b>お金(かね)を出(だ)しても</b>、あの絵(え)は手(て)に入(はい)らないだろう。
However much money you put up, that painting isn't going to be yours.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>悔(くや)しくても</b>、判定(はんてい)に文句(もんく)を言(い)ってはいけない。
However bitter you feel about it, you don't argue with the decision.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences still parse; the second just loses some of its warmth.
One thing only `どんなに` can do is head an exclamation:
どんなに心配(しんぱい)したことか - "you have no idea how worried I was."
`いくら` has no such use.

### いくら～ても vs たとえ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たとえ` concedes **whether** something is so; `いくら` concedes
**how much** of it there is. Ask whether the clause has a dial you can turn up.

</div>

<pre>
たとえ<b>中止(ちゅうし)になっても</b>、会場(かいじょう)の準備(じゅんび)はしておきます。
Even if it gets cancelled, I'll have the venue set up.
</pre>

Cancellation has no degrees - it either happens or it does not - so `いくら` would be
strange there. Compare a clause with a dial:

<pre>
いくら<b>準備(じゅんび)しても</b>、当日(とうじつ)は必(かなら)ず何(なに)か起(お)きる。
However much you prepare, something always goes wrong on the day.
</pre>

### いくら～ても vs いくらでも / いくらか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いくらでも` on its own, with no `ても` clause around it, is not
concessive at all. It means "as much as you like."

</div>

<pre>
おかわりは<b>いくらでも</b>どうぞ。
Help yourself to as many refills as you want.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら、<b>いくらか</b>楽(らく)になりました。
After the medicine I felt somewhat better.
</pre>

Three different words sharing a stem: `いくら～ても` concedes, `いくらでも` offers an
unlimited amount, and `いくらか` means "some, a bit." Only the first has a verb in
`ても` form after it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ いくら高(たか)いでも買(か)います → ✅ いくら高(たか)<b>くても</b>買(か)います
  `でも` is the *noun* form. い-adjectives make their own て-form in `くて`.

- ❌ いくら走(はし)ったても → ✅ いくら走(はし)っても
  Nothing is ever marked for tense in front of `ても`; the final verb carries the time.

- ❌ いくら練習(れんしゅう)しても、上手(じょうず)になりました → ✅ たくさん練習(れんしゅう)したので、上手(じょうず)になりました
  `いくら～ても` promises that the effort did **not** pay off. With a result that
  matches the effort, the pattern contradicts itself.

- ❌ いくらでも説明(せつめい)しても、伝(つた)わらなかった → ✅ いくら説明(せつめい)しても、伝(つた)わらなかった
  `でも` and `ても` are the same slot; you cannot have both. `いくらでも` belongs to a
  different construction entirely.

## Practice

**1.** いくら待(ま)っ____、バスは来(こ)なかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いくら待(ま)っ<b>ても</b>、バスは来(こ)なかった。

<code>待(ま)つ</code> has the て-form <code>待(ま)って</code>, so the concessive is
<code>待(ま)っても</code>. The negative ending is typical: `いくら～ても` almost always
reports that the effort came to nothing.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （いくら・たとえ）一日(いちにち)遅(おく)れても、品質(ひんしつ)は落(お)としたくない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>たとえ</b>一日(いちにち)遅(おく)れても、品質(ひんしつ)は落(お)としたくない。

"One day late" is a single fixed supposition, not an amount being turned up, so
`たとえ` fits. `いくら` would need something gradable - 
いくら遅(おく)れても would mean "however late it ends up being", which is a
different sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "No matter how many times I read it, I don't understand this contract."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いくら<b>読(よ)んでも</b>、この契約書(けいやくしょ)は理解(りかい)できない。

"No matter how many times" is a count, which is `いくら`'s natural territory - 
何回(なんかい)読(よ)んでも works too and is a shade more concrete. The negative
main clause is what the pattern expects.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: いくらなんでも、彼(かれ)は来(こ)なかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ いくら待(ま)っても、彼(かれ)は来(こ)なかった。

`いくらなんでも` is a fixed protest meaning "surely that's too much", and it has to be
followed by a judgement such as <code>ひどい</code> or <code>無理(むり)だ</code>. It
cannot serve as the concessive clause of an ordinary report.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) いくら説明(せつめい)しても、母(はは)は納得(なっとく)しなかった。
(b) 説明(せつめい)したのに、母(はは)は納得(なっとく)しなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) piles up the effort: the speaker explained again and again, at length, and none
of it landed. The frustration is aimed at the sheer waste of effort, and the sentence
implies several attempts.

(b) reports a single explanation and a disappointing response, and `のに` puts the
speaker's dissatisfaction on the mother rather than on the effort. One explanation
should have been enough - that expectation is exactly what `のに` says was betrayed.

</details>

# どんなに～ても

**Meaning** · no matter how - push the intensity as far as it will go, and the result still stands

`どんなに` asks "to what degree?" and then refuses to wait for an answer. Whatever
level of cold, pain, effort, skill or worry you care to imagine, the `ても` clause
grants it and the main clause carries on regardless.

Placed on this batch's axis - how much the speaker minds the gap between expectation
and outcome - `どんなに～ても` is the emotional member of the "even if" branch.
`たとえ～ても` grants a supposition coolly; `いくら～ても` counts up wasted effort;
`どんなに～ても` reaches for the feeling behind the effort, which is why it is the one
that shows up in pledges, song lyrics and speeches.

## Formation

`どんなに` is an adverb of degree standing at the head of the clause. All the
conjugation happens on the `ても`, as at N4.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | どんなに + て-form + も | 頑張(がんば)る → どんなに頑張(がんば)っても |
| Verb (negative) | どんなに + なくて + も | 眠(ねむ)れない → どんなに眠(ねむ)れなくても |
| い-adjective | どんなに + くて + も | 苦(くる)しい → どんなに苦(くる)しくても |
| い-adjective (negative) | どんなに + くなくて + も | 上手(うま)くない → どんなに上手(うま)くなくても |
| な-adjective | どんなに + で + も | 大変(たいへん) → どんなに大変(たいへん)でも |
| Noun phrase with a degree in it | どんなに + adjective + Noun + でも | 優秀(ゆうしゅう)な人(ひと) → どんなに優秀(ゆうしゅう)な人(ひと)でも |
| Exclamation (no main clause) | どんなに + clause + ことか／だろう | 嬉(うれ)しい → どんなに嬉(うれ)しかったことか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どんなに` modifies a **degree**, so it wants a verb or an
adjective after it, not a bare noun. ❌<code>どんなに医者(いしゃ)でも</code> →
✅<code>どんなに経験(けいけん)のある医者(いしゃ)でも</code>. When you really do want
"even a doctor", `いくら` or plain `でも` handles it more comfortably.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どんなに<b>眠(ねむ)くても</b>、歯(は)だけは磨(みが)く。
However sleepy I am, I at least brush my teeth.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>急(いそ)いでも</b>、ここから空港(くうこう)までは一時間(いちじかん)かかります。
However much we rush, it's an hour from here to the airport.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>説得(せっとく)しても</b>、祖母(そぼ)は病院(びょういん)に行(い)こうとしない。
No matter how hard we try to talk her round, my grandmother won't go to the hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>辛(つら)くても</b>、最後(さいご)まで走(はし)り切(き)ります。
However much it hurts, I'm running this to the finish.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>叱(しか)られても</b>、あの子(こ)は同(おな)じいたずらを繰(く)り返(かえ)す。
However often he's told off, that child repeats the same prank.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>高価(こうか)な道具(どうぐ)でも</b>、使(つか)わなければただの荷物(にもつ)だ。
However expensive the tools, unused they're just so much luggage.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>忙(いそが)しくても</b>、娘(むすめ)の話(はなし)を聞(き)く時間(じかん)だけは作(つく)るようにしている。
However busy I am, I try to keep aside time to listen to my daughter.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>条件(じょうけん)がよくても</b>、家族(かぞく)と離(はな)れて暮(く)らす仕事(しごと)は選(えら)べません。
However good the terms, I can't take a job that means living apart from my family.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`どんなに` is neutral for politeness - it sits happily in `ます` speech and in plain
speech - but it is not neutral for **temperature**. It reaches for feeling. A weather
forecast says いくら, a wedding speech says どんなに.

That warmth gives it a use `いくら` does not have: the bare exclamation, with no
concessive clause at all.

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)したと聞(き)いて、<b>どんなに</b>嬉(うれ)しかったことか。
You can't imagine how glad I was to hear you'd passed.
</pre>

`どんなに～ことか` and `どんなに～だろう` are both this exclamatory use. They are
distinctly written or heartfelt-spoken; nobody says them while ordering lunch.

Register neighbours, roughly from stiff to slangy - treat these as tendencies rather
than fixed rules:

- `いかに～ても` - formal writing, reports, editorials.
- `どんなに～ても` - the general-purpose word, speech and writing alike.
- `どれだけ～ても` - colloquial, and slightly more about quantity.
- `どんだけ～ても` - markedly casual, common in younger speech, out of place at work.

Finally, note the main clauses `どんなに` attracts. Roughly half are resigned
(<code>無理(むり)だ</code>, <code>変(か)わらない</code>) and half are defiant
(<code>やめない</code>, <code>続(つづ)ける</code>). What they have in common is that
the speaker has already taken a position on the outcome.

## Contrast

### どんなに～ても vs いくら～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いくら` counts; `どんなに` feels. Where the clause is about a
literal amount of money, time or repetitions, `いくら` is the plainer choice; where it
is about how intensely something is experienced, `どんなに` is.

</div>

<pre>
いくら<b>払(はら)っても</b>、この古(ふる)い車(くるま)は直(なお)らなかった。
However much I paid, the old car never got fixed.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>悲(かな)しくても</b>、人前(ひとまえ)では泣(な)かない人(ひと)だった。
However sad she was, she was not one to cry in front of others.
</pre>

Most sentences accept either word. The two places they part company are the literal
price tag, where `いくら` is idiomatic, and the exclamation
どんなに～ことか, which `いくら` cannot form at all.

### どんなに～ても vs どんな～でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どんなに` + degree + `ても` = "no matter **how** much."
`どんな` + noun + `でも` = "**any** ~ at all." One syllable apart, completely
different sentences.

</div>

<pre>
姉(あね)は<b>どんな料理(りょうり)でも</b>作(つく)れます。
My sister can cook anything you like.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>どんなに料理(りょうり)を習(なら)っても</b>、母(はは)の味(あじ)にはならない。
However many cooking lessons I take, it never tastes the way my mother's did.
</pre>

The first ranges over **kinds** of thing; the second ranges over **degrees** of
effort. Remember that `どんな` needs a noun immediately after it, so if there is no
noun there, the word you want is `どんなに`.

### どんなに～ても vs question word + ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どんなに` concedes degree only. To concede **who**, **what**,
**when** or **where**, put that question word in front of the `ても` clause instead.

</div>

<pre>
<b>だれが来(き)ても</b>、この部屋(へや)には入(い)れないでください。
Whoever turns up, don't let them into this room.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>何(なに)を言(い)われても</b>、決(き)めたことは変(か)えません。
Whatever I'm told, I'm not changing what I've decided.
</pre>

The frame is identical - question word, `ても`, unmoved main clause - and `どんなに`
is simply the member of the set that handles "how much." They stack, too:
だれが何(なに)を言(い)おうと。

## Common mistakes

- ❌ どんなに人(ひと)でも間違(まちが)える → ✅ どんなに優秀(ゆうしゅう)な人(ひと)でも間違(まちが)える
  `どんなに` needs something gradable to modify, and a bare noun gives it nothing to
  measure. Either supply an adjective, or drop to plain
  <code>どんな人(ひと)でも</code>, which means "anyone at all."

- ❌ どんなに大変(たいへん)くても → ✅ どんなに大変(たいへん)でも
  <code>大変(たいへん)</code> is a な-adjective, so its て-form is
  <code>大変(たいへん)で</code>. Only い-adjectives take `くても`.

- ❌ どんなに頑張(がんば)るても → ✅ どんなに頑張(がんば)っても
  `も` attaches to the て-form, never to the dictionary form.

- ❌ どんなに説明(せつめい)しても、やっと分(わ)かってくれた → ✅ 何度(なんど)も説明(せつめい)して、やっと分(わ)かってくれた
  The whole point of `どんなに～ても` is that the effort **fails**. If it finally
  worked, you need an ordinary て-form linking cause to result.

## Practice

**1.** どんなに練習(れんしゅう)し____、兄(あに)には勝(か)てない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

どんなに練習(れんしゅう)し<b>ても</b>、兄(あに)には勝(か)てない。

<code>する</code> makes the て-form <code>して</code>, so the concessive is `しても`. The
negative-potential ending is typical of this pattern: the effort is granted in full
and then declared not to matter.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 父(ちち)は（どんな・どんなに）質問(しつもん)でも丁寧(ていねい)に答(こた)えてくれる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

父(ちち)は<b>どんな</b>質問(しつもん)でも丁寧(ていねい)に答(こた)えてくれる。

A noun follows directly, so the word is `どんな` - "any question at all." With
`どんなに` you would need a degree to modify and a `ても` clause, for example
どんなに難(むずか)しい質問(しつもん)でも.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "However cold it gets, this flower doesn't die."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

どんなに<b>寒(さむ)くなっても</b>、この花(はな)は枯(か)れません。

<code>寒(さむ)くなる</code> ("get cold") gives the て-form
<code>寒(さむ)くなって</code>. You could also write
どんなに寒(さむ)くても, which grants a state rather than a change - both are
natural, and the `なる` version stresses the drop in temperature.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: どんなに不安(ふあん)くても、人(ひと)に相談(そうだん)しない人(ひと)もいる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ どんなに<b>不安(ふあん)でも</b>、人(ひと)に相談(そうだん)しない人(ひと)もいる。

<code>不安(ふあん)</code> is a な-adjective. Its て-form is
<code>不安(ふあん)で</code>, so the concessive is <code>不安(ふあん)でも</code>. The
`くても` ending belongs to い-adjectives only, and the final `い` of `不安` is part of
the reading, not an adjective ending.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) どんなに待(ま)っても、返事(へんじ)は来(こ)なかった。
(b) どんなに待(ま)ったことか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the ordinary concessive: the waiting is granted at any length you like, and
the sentence reports that the reply never arrived anyway. The focus is on the
outcome.

(b) has no main clause and is not conceding anything. It is an exclamation - "how
long I waited!" - inviting the listener to imagine the degree. That use is warm and
somewhat literary, and `いくら` cannot be substituted into it.

</details>

# ～にしても

**Meaning** · even if; even granting that - the speaker accepts the other side's point and objects anyway

`にしても` picks up a reason, an excuse or a possibility that is already on the table,
grants it, and then says it is not enough. Almost every `にしても` sentence has an
argument going on inside it: *fine, suppose that is true - even so, this still does
not follow.*

On this batch's axis of how much the speaker minds, `にしても` is the reasonable
complainer. `のに` blurts out its dissatisfaction; `くせに` sneers; `にしても` concedes
first and criticises second, which is why it is the concession you hear in meetings.

## Formation

`にしても` follows a **plain form** - a whole clause, with its own tense - and nouns
and な-adjectives attach bare, with no `だ` and no `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + にしても | 断(ことわ)る → 断(ことわ)るにしても |
| Verb (past) | plain past + にしても | 忘(わす)れた → 忘(わす)れたにしても |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + にしても | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないにしても |
| い-adjective | plain form + にしても | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいにしても |
| な-adjective | stem (+ である) + にしても | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)（である）にしても |
| Noun | Noun (+ である) + にしても | 冗談(じょうだん) → 冗談(じょうだん)にしても |
| Two alternatives | A にしても B にしても | 買(か)う・借(か)りる → 買(か)うにしても借(か)りるにしても |
| Fixed conjunction | それにしても + remark | - → それにしても暑(あつ)いね |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `だ` and no `な` before `にしても`:
✅<code>学生(がくせい)にしても</code>, ❌<code>学生(がくせい)だにしても</code>. If you
want the copula visible, the formal option is `である`:
<code>学生(がくせい)であるにしても</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - with a な-adjective the bare stem can be ambiguous, because
<code>静(しず)かにしても</code> also reads as "even if you make it quiet"
(<code>静(しず)かにする</code> + `ても`). In speech people dodge this by saying
<code>静(しず)かだとしても</code> instead. In writing, `である` does the same job.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)<b>にしても</b>、言(い)い過(す)ぎだよ。
Even as a joke, that's going too far.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しい<b>にしても</b>、一言(ひとこと)連絡(れんらく)ぐらいはできたでしょう。
Busy or not, you could have sent me a single word.
</pre>

<pre>
行(い)く<b>にしても</b>行(い)かない<b>にしても</b>、今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に決(き)めてください。
Whether you're going or not, please decide before the day is out.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>にしても</b>、同(おな)じ立場(たちば)なら黙(だま)っていたと思(おも)います。
I'd have kept quiet too, in the same position.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいパソコンを買(か)う<b>にしても</b>、来月(らいげつ)のボーナスまでは待(ま)つつもりだ。
Even if I do buy a new computer, I'm going to wait until next month's bonus.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>それにしても</b>、今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は長(なが)すぎませんか。
All the same - isn't this summer dragging on rather?
</pre>

<pre>
初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>にしても</b>、道具(どうぐ)の使(つか)い方(かた)ぐらいは覚(おぼ)えてきてほしかった。
Beginner or not, I did expect him to turn up knowing how the tools work.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)を優先(ゆうせん)する<b>にしても</b>、この規則(きそく)は厳(きび)しすぎるのではないでしょうか。
Granting that safety comes first, isn't this rule far too strict?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にしても` is the vocabulary of **argument conducted politely**. It concedes in order
to earn the right to object, which makes it a staple of meetings, negotiations, letters
of complaint and mild parental scolding. Because the concession comes first, the
objection that follows lands as reasonable rather than hostile - this is a genuinely
useful piece of adult Japanese.

The main clause is where the speaker's feeling lives, and it is usually one of:

- a criticism - <code>～すぎる</code>, <code>ひどい</code>, <code>おかしい</code>;
- a minimum demand - <code>ぐらいは～てほしい</code>, <code>～べきだ</code>;
- a limit on a plan - <code>それでも～は変(か)えない</code>.

A flat, neutral report after `にしても` sounds unfinished, because the listener is
waiting for the objection the concession promised.

Its family, roughly by register:

- `としても` - the most clearly hypothetical: "even supposing." Neutral.
- `にしても` - concedes something already raised, real or supposed. Neutral.
- `にしろ` - the same, a shade curt; common in spoken instructions.
- `にせよ` - formal and written; documents and speeches.

`それにしても` is worth learning as a single word. It re-opens a topic to comment on
its degree: 「遅(おそ)いね。」「うん、それにしても遅(おそ)すぎるよ。」 It is not
conceding anything specific - it just says "granting everything we have said, still."

## Contrast

### ～にしても vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` attaches to a **te-form** and states a bare concession.
`にしても` attaches to a **whole plain clause**, so it can carry past tense and nouns,
and it concedes a *claim someone has actually made*.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、畑(はたけ)の水(みず)やりは必要(ひつよう)です。
Even if it rains, the field still needs watering.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>にしても</b>、これだけしか降(ふ)らなければ同(おな)じことです。
Granted it rained - but this little makes no difference.
</pre>

Notice what the second one does that the first cannot: it keeps the past tense
(<code>降(ふ)った</code>) and it answers somebody. `ても` cannot take tense at all,
and it does not imply a conversation.

### ～にしても vs ～にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one syllable, two different sentences. `にしても` = "even granting
X, Y is still not acceptable." `にしては` = "measured against X, Y is surprising."
`にしても` complains; `にしては` evaluates.

</div>

<pre>
初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>にしては</b>、ずいぶん上手(じょうず)に削(けず)れていますね。
For a beginner, that's remarkably well planed.
</pre>

<pre>
初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>にしても</b>、安全(あんぜん)な持(も)ち方(かた)は習(なら)っておくべきだ。
Beginner or not, he ought to have learned how to hold it safely.
</pre>

The first is a compliment: the standard "beginner" predicts little, and the result
beat it. The second is a reproach: being a beginner is offered as an excuse, accepted,
and ruled insufficient.

### ～にしても vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` reports a **fact** that disappointed the speaker.
`にしても` concedes a **premise** - which may be a fact or merely a supposition - 
and then argues from it.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があった<b>のに</b>、最後(さいご)まで司会(しかい)を続(つづ)けた。
She had a temperature and still chaired the whole thing.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があった<b>にしても</b>、一言(ひとこと)言(い)ってくれればよかったのに。
Even with a temperature, she could have said something.
</pre>

The first admires a fact. The second takes the same fact as an excuse and pushes
back on it. If you find yourself simply reporting something surprising with no
argument attached, `のに` is the word.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)だにしても → ✅ 学生(がくせい)にしても
  Nouns and な-adjectives attach bare. `だ` never appears before `にしても`; the formal
  alternative is `である`.

- ❌ 忙(いそが)しいにしても、妹(いもうと)は手伝(てつだ)いに来(き)てくれた → ✅ 忙(いそが)しい<b>のに</b>、妹(いもうと)は手伝(てつだ)いに来(き)てくれた
  `にしても` sets up an objection. When you are grateful rather than dissatisfied,
  the fact-reporting `のに` is what you want.

- ❌ この字(じ)は子(こ)どもが書(か)いたにしても、きれいだ → ✅ この字(じ)は子(こ)どもが書(か)いた<b>にしては</b>、きれいだ
  You are measuring the handwriting against the standard "written by a child", so
  the particle is `は`, not `も`.

- ❌ 行(い)くにしても、行(い)かないにも、返事(へんじ)をください → ✅ 行(い)くにしても、行(い)かないにしても、返事(へんじ)をください
  In the paired "whether A or B" frame, both halves take the full `にしても`.

## Practice

**1.** 冗談(じょうだん)____、人(ひと)の見(み)た目(め)のことは言(い)わないほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

冗談(じょうだん)<b>にしても</b>、人(ひと)の見(み)た目(め)のことは言(い)わないほうがいい。

A noun attaches bare, with no `だ`. The sentence grants the excuse "it was only a
joke" and rules it insufficient, which is the core job of `にしても`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 中学生(ちゅうがくせい)（にしても・にしては）、作文(さくぶん)がずいぶん大人(おとな)っぽい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中学生(ちゅうがくせい)<b>にしては</b>、作文(さくぶん)がずいぶん大人(おとな)っぽい。

The sentence is an assessment: judged against what you expect from a junior-high
student, the essay is surprisingly grown-up. `にしても` would turn it into a
complaint - "even for a junior-high student, this is not good enough."

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if we do postpone it, we should tell the guests today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

延期(えんき)する<b>にしても</b>、お客(きゃく)さんには今日(きょう)知(し)らせるべきです。

`にしても` takes the plain form <code>延期(えんき)する</code> directly. The pattern
fits because the sentence grants a possibility that is under discussion and then
states a requirement that holds regardless.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 失敗(しっぱい)しただにしても、報告(ほうこく)は早(はや)いほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 失敗(しっぱい)した<b>にしても</b>、報告(ほうこく)は早(はや)いほうがいい。

<code>失敗(しっぱい)した</code> is already a complete plain past form, so nothing
goes between it and `にしても`. The stray `だ` comes from treating
<code>失敗(しっぱい)</code> as a bare noun; here it is a verb.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新人(しんじん)にしても、この資料(しりょう)は雑(ざつ)すぎる。
(b) 新人(しんじん)にしては、この資料(しりょう)はよくできている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) grants that the person is new - the excuse is heard and accepted - and then
rejects it: newness does not stretch far enough to cover work this sloppy. The
speaker is dissatisfied and is saying so in a controlled way.

(b) uses newness as a yardstick instead of an excuse. Expectations for a new hire
were low, the work cleared them, and the speaker is impressed. Same noun, opposite
attitude, decided entirely by `も` versus `は`.

</details>

# ～といっても

**Meaning** · although I say ~ - the word just used promises more than the reality delivers

`といっても` takes the label that has just been put on something - "I can cook", "it's
near the station", "he's a company president" - and immediately cuts it down to size.
The listener has drawn a conclusion from the label, and the speaker heads that
conclusion off before it settles.

On this batch's axis, `といっても` is not really about minding at all. `のに` and
`くせに` are unhappy about a gap between expectation and outcome. `といっても` is
**correcting** one, usually the speaker's own, and it is more often modest or wry
than annoyed.

## Formation

`といっても` follows a **plain form** or a bare noun. The `と` is the quotation
particle, so what precedes it is treated as a quoted claim.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + といっても | できる → できるといっても |
| Verb (past) | plain past + といっても | 優勝(ゆうしょう)した → 優勝(ゆうしょう)したといっても |
| い-adjective | plain form + といっても | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いといっても |
| な-adjective | stem (+ だ) + といっても | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)（だ）といっても |
| Noun | Noun (+ だ) + といっても | 旅行(りょこう) → 旅行(りょこう)（だ）といっても |
| Start of a new sentence | といっても + correction | - → といっても、まだ決(き)まっていません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause after `といっても` must **shrink** what came before it,
so it very often contains `だけ`, `ぐらい`, `ほんの`, `たった`, or
<code>たいしたことはない</code>. If your second clause adds new information instead of
scaling the first one down, `といっても` is the wrong join.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same shape appears as a stand-alone connector at the head of a
sentence. 「来週(らいしゅう)引(ひ)っ越(こ)します。といっても、同(おな)じ
町(まち)の中(なか)ですが。」 - "We're moving next week. Only within the same town,
mind."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
料理(りょうり)ができる<b>といっても</b>、たまごを焼(や)くぐらいです。
I say I can cook, but it's about as far as frying an egg.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)から近(ちか)い<b>といっても</b>、歩(ある)いて十五分(じゅうごふん)はかかるよ。
It's close to the station, but that still means a fifteen-minute walk.
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)<b>といっても</b>、一泊(いっぱく)の温泉(おんせん)だけどね。
When I say trip, I mean one night at a hot spring.
</pre>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)<b>といっても</b>、社員(しゃいん)が三人(さんにん)しかいない会社(かいしゃ)です。
President, yes - of a company with three employees.
</pre>

<pre>
寒(さむ)い<b>といっても</b>、北海道(ほっかいどう)に比(くら)べればずっと過(す)ごしやすいですよ。
It's cold, but compared with Hokkaido it's far easier to live with.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しい<b>といっても</b>、映画(えいが)を一本(いっぽん)見(み)る時間(じかん)ぐらいはあるでしょう。
You say you're busy, but you've surely got time for one film.
</pre>

<pre>
独立(どくりつ)した<b>といっても</b>、仕事(しごと)の半分(はんぶん)はまだ前(まえ)の会社(かいしゃ)からもらっている。
I've gone independent, though half my work still comes from my old employer.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)した<b>といっても</b>、参加(さんか)チームは四(よっ)つしかなかったんです。
We won it, but there were only four teams in the competition.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`といっても` is register-neutral and equally at home in speech and writing, but it is
strongly associated with one social move: **modest self-presentation**. Japanese
conversation makes a habit of naming an achievement and deflating it in the same
breath, and `といっても` is the hinge that deflation turns on. Learners who say
「日本語(にほんご)が話(はな)せます」 and stop can sound like they are boasting;
「話(はな)せるといっても、まだ簡単(かんたん)な会話(かいわ)だけですが」 lands
much better.

Turned outward, it becomes a gentle correction of somebody else's overstatement, as
in the "you say you're busy" example above. Said sharply it can needle, so keep the
tone light - a `ね` or `けど` at the end helps.

Casual contractions are everywhere in speech:

- `っていっても` - the everyday spoken shape after a vowel:
  「無理(むり)っていっても、やるしかないよ。」
- `つっても` - very casual, more common in men's relaxed speech.

Two neighbours to keep straight. `そうはいっても` ("even so") pushes back on what was
said instead of shrinking it. `とはいえ` does much the same work as `といっても` but
belongs to written and formal register - you will meet it in editorials, not in chat.

## Contrast

### ～といっても vs ～けど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `けど` simply joins two clauses that lean opposite ways.
`といっても` says something more specific: *the word I just used was too big.*

</div>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は上(あ)がった<b>けど</b>、残業(ざんぎょう)も増(ふ)えた。
My pay went up, but so did the overtime.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が上(あ)がった<b>といっても</b>、月(つき)に二千円(にせんえん)ほどです。
My pay went up - by about two thousand yen a month.
</pre>

The `けど` sentence adds a second, separate fact. The `といっても` sentence stays on the
first fact and shows you it is smaller than it sounded. Swap them and the `けど`
version loses nothing; the `といっても` version stops making sense, because "the
overtime increased" does not measure the pay rise.

### ～といっても vs ～というより

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `といっても` **keeps** the label and shrinks it. `というより`
**rejects** the label and offers a better one.

</div>

<pre>
趣味(しゅみ)<b>といっても</b>、月(つき)に一度(いちど)触(さわ)る程度(ていど)です。
It's a hobby, if you can call something I touch once a month a hobby.
</pre>

<pre>
あれは趣味(しゅみ)<b>というより</b>、もう仕事(しごと)ですね。
That's not so much a hobby as a job by now.
</pre>

In the first the thing is still a hobby, just a thin one. In the second the word
"hobby" is withdrawn and replaced.

### ～といっても vs そうはいっても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `といっても` deflates the claim. `そうはいっても` accepts the claim
and refuses the conclusion drawn from it.

</div>

<pre>
安(やす)い<b>といっても</b>、中古(ちゅうこ)なら当然(とうぜん)でしょう。
Cheap, sure - but it's second-hand, so of course it is.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しいのはわかります。<b>そうはいっても</b>、締(し)め切(き)りは動(うご)かせません。
I know you're busy. Even so, the deadline can't move.
</pre>

`そうはいっても` needs something to point back at, so it opens a sentence rather than
joining one. In formal writing the equivalent is `とはいえ`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っているといっても、傘(かさ)をささない → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>のに</b>、傘(かさ)をささない
  `といっても` needs a **claim or label** in front of it to cut down. A plain observed
  fact contradicted by someone's behaviour is `のに` territory.

- ❌ 英語(えいご)が話(はな)せるといっても、毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しています → ✅ 英語(えいご)が話(はな)せるといっても、挨拶(あいさつ)ぐらいです
  The second clause has to measure the first one downward. Adding an unrelated fact
  leaves the pattern with nothing to do.

- ❌ 高(たか)いといっても、思(おも)い切(き)って買(か)いました → ✅ 高(たか)かった<b>けど</b>、思(おも)い切(き)って買(か)いました
  "It was expensive but I bought it anyway" is a plain adversative join. `といっても`
  would mean "expensive, but not as expensive as you think."

- ❌ 忙(いそが)しいいっても → ✅ 忙(いそが)しい<b>と</b>いっても
  The `と` is the quotation particle and cannot be dropped. In fast speech it fuses
  into `っていっても`, but it is never simply absent.

A bare noun attaches directly. "Newly built" is the label; the second clause shows

</details>

The sentence throws the word "explanation" out and puts "boasting" in its place, so

</details>

(b) is dissatisfied. `のに` treats the small team as something that should not go with
the title - the speaker feels short-changed, or is criticising whoever made that
decision.

</details>

# ところが

**Meaning** · however; but then - what actually happened defeated what everyone was expecting

`ところが` starts a new sentence and drops the floor out from under the previous one.
The first sentence sets up an expectation; `ところが` announces that reality went the
other way. Crucially, the thing that went the other way has **already happened** - 
`ところが` reports outcomes, never plans.

On this batch's axis of how much the speaker minds, `ところが` is close to neutral.
It marks **surprise**, not dissatisfaction. The turn can be for the worse, as it
usually is in a complaint, but it can equally be for the better - a cheap hotel that
turned out to be lovely takes `ところが` just as happily.

## Formation

`ところが` is a conjunction, not a suffix, so there is nothing for it to attach to in
the usual sense. What matters is the shape of the sentences on either side of it.

| Position | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Between two sentences | Sentence。ところが、Sentence。 | 出(で)かけた。ところが、閉(し)まっていた。 |
| Head of a spoken turn | ところが、… | ところが、それが違(ちが)ったんです |
| Inside one sentence (written) | Verb plain past + ところが | 頼(たの)んだ → 頼(たの)んだところが |
| What may follow it | a **realized fact** only | ✅閉(し)まっていた ❌閉(し)めましょう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nothing forward-looking can follow `ところが`. No volitional, no
request, no invitation, no command. ❌<code>ところが、明日(あした)行(い)きましょう</code>
is simply not available; that sentence needs `しかし` or `でも`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the written <code>～たところが</code> packs the same reversal into a single
sentence: 問(と)い合(あ)わせたところが、担当者(たんとうしゃ)は
退職(たいしょく)していた。 It always follows a **past** verb, and it belongs to
reports and essays rather than conversation.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)って出(で)かけた。<b>ところが</b>、一滴(いってき)も降(ふ)らなかった。
I took an umbrella out with me. Not a drop fell.
</pre>

<pre>
楽(たの)しみにしていました。<b>ところが</b>、当日(とうじつ)熱(ねつ)を出(だ)してしまいました。
I'd been looking forward to it. Then on the day itself I came down with a fever.
</pre>

<pre>
簡単(かんたん)な仕事(しごと)だと聞(き)いていた。<b>ところが</b>、実際(じっさい)には三日(みっか)かかった。
I'd been told it was an easy job. In fact it took three days.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)いホテルを予約(よやく)した。<b>ところが</b>、部屋(へや)は想像(そうぞう)よりずっと広(ひろ)かった。
I booked a cheap hotel. The room turned out to be far bigger than I'd pictured.
</pre>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)前(まえ)から並(なら)んだ。<b>ところが</b>、目当(めあ)てのパンはもう売(う)り切(き)れていた。
I queued from before opening time. The bread I'd come for was already sold out.
</pre>

<pre>
洗濯機(せんたくき)を静(しず)かなものに買(か)い替(か)えました。<b>ところが</b>、音(おと)は前(まえ)のより大(おお)きいんです。
I replaced the washing machine with a quiet model. It's noisier than the old one.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんなら知(し)っているだろうと思(おも)って聞(き)いてみた。<b>ところが</b>、彼(かれ)も首(くび)をかしげるばかりだった。
I asked Yamada, thinking he was bound to know. All he did was tilt his head at me.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)に相談(そうだん)してみた<b>ところが</b>、その場(ば)ですぐに決(き)めてくださった。
When I took it to the section chief, he settled it on the spot.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ところが` is the storyteller's word. It carries no politeness of its own - it appears
unchanged before `ます` sentences and plain ones - but it does presuppose a
**narrative**: something was set up, and now it collapses. That is why it so often
turns up with `のだ／んです` after it. The speaker is not just reporting the reversal,
they are explaining it to you.

Because the reversal has to be real, `ところが` is overwhelmingly followed by past
tense or by `ている`. A present-tense fact is possible when it is a discovered state
(<code>音(おと)は前(まえ)のより大(おお)きいんです</code>), but a future or a
decision is not.

Neighbours in the "but" family:

- `しかし` - neutral, all-purpose, no surprise required; fine in writing and speech.
- `でも` - the casual everyday one, and the only one that comfortably opens a
  disagreement with the person you are talking to.
- `ところが` - narrative reversal, expectation defeated, outcome already in.
- `それが` - the colloquial cousin of `ところが`, used to answer a question whose
  premise has failed: 「来(き)ましたか。」「それが、来(こ)なかったんです。」

One more thing `ところが` does not do: it does not disagree with your listener. If
someone has just stated an opinion and you want to push back, `でも` or
`そうはいっても` is what you need. `ところが` argues with the world, not with people.

## Contrast

### ところが vs しかし・でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しかし` and `でも` can be followed by anything, including a plan
or a request. `ところが` can only be followed by a fact that has already turned out.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。<b>でも</b>、予定(よてい)どおり出発(しゅっぱつ)しましょう。
It's started raining. Let's set off as planned anyway.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。<b>ところが</b>、誰(だれ)も傘(かさ)を持(も)っていなかった。
It started raining. Nobody had an umbrella.
</pre>

Replace `でも` with `ところが` in the first and the sentence breaks: a suggestion is
not something that "turned out." The second works with either word, but `ところが`
adds the click of an expectation failing, which `でも` leaves out.

### ところが vs ところで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one syllable apart, and unrelated. `ところが` = "but then."
`ところで` = "by the way", a change of topic.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は終(お)わりました。<b>ところで</b>、この後(あと)時間(じかん)ありますか。
The meeting's over. By the way, are you free after this?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に終(お)わるはずだった。<b>ところが</b>、五時(ごじ)まで続(つづ)いた。
The meeting was supposed to finish at three. It ran until five.
</pre>

Note also the unrelated `～たところで` ("even if you were to…"), which is a concessive
and belongs to a later level. Three similar strings, three separate words: check the
final syllable and whether a full stop precedes.

### ところが vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` lives **inside** a sentence and carries the speaker's
dissatisfaction. `ところが` stands **between** sentences and carries surprise, which
may be pleasant.

</div>

<pre>
一時間(いちじかん)も待(ま)った<b>のに</b>、連絡(れんらく)ひとつなかった。
I waited a whole hour and didn't get so much as a message.
</pre>

<pre>
一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)つ覚悟(かくご)で店(みせ)に行(い)った。<b>ところが</b>、すぐに座(すわ)れた。
I went to the restaurant braced for an hour's wait. We were seated straight away.
</pre>

Try putting `のに` in the second one and it turns sour - `のに` would suggest the
speaker resents being seated quickly. That is the difference in one line: `のに`
minds, `ところが` merely did not see it coming.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 道(みち)が混(こ)んでいる。ところが、電車(でんしゃ)で行(い)きましょう。 → ✅ 道(みち)が混(こ)んでいる。<b>でも</b>、電車(でんしゃ)で行(い)きましょう。
  A suggestion cannot follow `ところが`, which reports only what has already turned
  out.

- ❌ ところが、コーヒーでも飲(の)みませんか。 → ✅ <b>ところで</b>、コーヒーでも飲(の)みませんか。
  Changing the subject is `ところで`. The `が` version announces a reversal, which is
  not what a change of topic is.

- ❌ 安(やす)いところが、買(か)わなかった。 → ✅ 安(やす)かった<b>けれど</b>、買(か)わなかった。
  As a reversal marker `ところが` needs a full stop in front of it. The only in-sentence
  form is <code>～たところが</code>, which requires a past verb and belongs to written
  style.

- ❌ 姉(あね)は朝型(あさがた)だ。ところが、妹(いもうと)は夜型(よるがた)だ。 → ✅ 姉(あね)は朝型(あさがた)だ。<b>一方(いっぽう)</b>、妹(いもうと)は夜型(よるがた)だ。
  Two things merely differing is a comparison, not a defeated expectation. `ところが`
  needs something to have gone wrong against a prediction.

## Practice

**1.** 三十分(さんじゅっぷん)前(まえ)に着(つ)いた。____、会場(かいじょう)はまだ開(あ)いていなかった。

三十分(さんじゅっぷん)前(まえ)に着(つ)いた。<b>ところが</b>、会場(かいじょう)はまだ開(あ)いていなかった。

Arriving early sets up the expectation of getting in early, and the second sentence
reports that reality defeated it. Both halves are finished facts in the past, which
is what `ところが` requires.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 資料(しりょう)はそろいました。（ところが・ところで）、印刷(いんさつ)は何部(なんぶ)必要(ひつよう)ですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The second sentence opens a new topic and asks a question - nothing has been
contradicted. A question about a plan also cannot follow `ところが`, which admits only
outcomes that have already happened.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I saved for a year. But then the price went up."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一年(いちねん)貯金(ちょきん)しました。<b>ところが</b>、値段(ねだん)が上(あ)がってしまいました。

Saving up sets an expectation of being able to buy; the price rise cancels it. The
`てしまいました` ending suits `ところが` well, because it adds the speaker's dismay at
an outcome they did not control.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 予約(よやく)が取(と)れなかった。ところが、来週(らいしゅう)また電話(でんわ)してみるつもりだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 予約(よやく)が取(と)れなかった。<b>でも</b>、来週(らいしゅう)また電話(でんわ)してみるつもりだ。

<code>つもりだ</code> states an intention, and intentions are forward-looking, so
`ところが` cannot introduce them. Swapping in `でも` or `しかし` fixes it; alternatively
keep `ところが` and follow it with what actually happened.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 説明書(せつめいしょ)のとおりに組(く)み立(た)てた。ところが、部品(ぶひん)が一(ひと)つ余(あま)った。
(b) 説明書(せつめいしょ)のとおりに組(く)み立(た)てたのに、部品(ぶひん)が一(ひと)つ余(あま)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) tells it as a small story: here is what I did, and here is the turn nobody saw
coming. The two halves stand as separate sentences, so the reversal gets its own
beat, and the tone can be amused as easily as annoyed.

(b) packs the same content into one sentence and adds feeling. `のに` says the speaker
followed the instructions and feels let down by the result - there is a hint of
blame, aimed at the instructions or at themselves.

</details>

# ～どころか

**Meaning** · far from ~; never mind ~ - the word just offered is not merely wrong, it points the wrong way

`どころか` rejects a description and replaces it with one further along the scale, or
on the other side of it altogether. Somebody expects an apology and gets laughter;
somebody expects a ten-minute wait and waits an hour. `どころか` says: that is not the
half of it.

On this batch's axis of how much the speaker minds, `どころか` is the emphatic end.
`のに` reports a gap between expectation and outcome; `どころか` says the gap runs in
the opposite direction from the one you assumed, and it usually says so with feeling.

## Formation

`どころか` attaches to a plain form or a bare noun. Nouns and な-adjectives take no
`だ` and no `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + どころか | 謝(あやま)る → 謝(あやま)るどころか |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + どころか | 減(へ)らない → 減(へ)らないどころか |
| い-adjective | plain form + どころか | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いどころか |
| な-adjective | stem + どころか | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)どころか |
| Noun | Noun + どころか | 休憩(きゅうけい) → 休憩(きゅうけい)どころか |
| "not even" frame | A どころか B + も／さえ + negative | 漢字(かんじ)どころかひらがなも読(よ)めない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - whatever follows `どころか` must **contradict or overshoot** what
precedes it. A second clause that simply agrees with the first leaves the pattern
with nothing to reject: ❌<code>下(さ)がるどころか少(すこ)し下(さ)がった</code>,
✅<code>下(さ)がるどころかかえって上(あ)がった</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - in the "not even" frame the order looks backwards to English eyes. The
**bigger** item goes before `どころか` and the **smaller** one after it, with a
negative at the end: 漢字(かんじ)どころか、ひらがなも読(よ)めない - "never mind
kanji, he can't even read hiragana."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は謝(あやま)る<b>どころか</b>、笑(わら)っていた。
Far from apologising, he was laughing.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)<b>どころか</b>、ひらがなもまだ覚(おぼ)えていません。
Never mind kanji - I haven't even learned hiragana yet.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)む<b>どころか</b>、昼(ひる)ごはんも食(た)べていないんです。
Rest? I haven't even had lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
十分(じゅっぷん)<b>どころか</b>、一時間(いちじかん)以上(いじょう)待(ま)たされました。
Ten minutes? I was kept waiting over an hour.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだのに、よくなる<b>どころか</b>熱(ねつ)が上(あ)がってしまった。
I took the medicine, and instead of getting better my temperature went up.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は不便(ふべん)<b>どころか</b>、駅(えき)の真(ま)ん前(まえ)にありますよ。
Inconvenient? That shop is right in front of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)のせいで、旅行(りょこう)<b>どころか</b>、外(そと)に出(で)ることもできなかった。
Because of the typhoon we couldn't go outside, let alone take our trip.
</pre>

<pre>
後輩(こうはい)に注意(ちゅうい)したら、反省(はんせい)する<b>どころか</b>、口(くち)を利(き)いてくれなくなった。
I had a word with my junior colleague, and rather than take it on board he stopped speaking to me.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`どころか` is neutral for politeness and appears in `です・ます` speech and in writing
without changing, but it is far from neutral in **temperature**. It is a correction
delivered with force, and it nearly always carries one of three feelings: indignation
("far from apologising…"), astonishment ("an hour, not ten minutes"), or self-deprecation
("never mind kanji…"). Said flatly it can sound like you are contradicting the
listener, so in careful conversation people soften it with `よ`, `ね`, or an
explanatory `んです`.

Both directions are available and it is worth noticing which one you are in:

- **Downward** - the reality falls short of the label:
  上達(じょうたつ)するどころか、下手(へた)になった気(き)がする。
- **Upward** - the label is too modest for the reality:
  上手(じょうず)どころか、プロと間違(まちが)えられるほどだ。

The related `どころではない` looks similar and does something else: it says the
situation rules the thing out entirely. 「今(いま)は旅行(りょこう)どころではない」
 - "this is no time for travelling." Notice that it ends the sentence, while `どころか`
is a join.

## Contrast

### ～どころか vs ～ばかりか・～だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばかりか` and `だけでなく` **accept** the first item and add a
second. `どころか` **rejects** the first item as an inadequate description.

</div>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は英語(えいご)<b>だけでなく</b>、ロシア語(ご)も話(はな)します。
My grandfather speaks not only English but Russian too.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は英語(えいご)<b>どころか</b>、自分(じぶん)の名前(なまえ)もローマ字(じ)で書(か)けません。
Never mind English - my grandfather can't even write his own name in roman letters.
</pre>

In the first, the English is real and the Russian is extra. In the second, the English
is thrown out as absurdly optimistic. That is why `どころか` in the "not even" frame
almost always ends in a negative, and `だけでなく` almost never does.

### ～どころか vs ところが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どころか` joins two **descriptions inside one sentence** and
denies the first. `ところが` joins two **sentences** and reports that events took an
unexpected turn.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい靴(くつ)を買(か)った。<b>ところが</b>、一日(いちにち)で靴(くつ)ずれができた。
I bought new shoes. Within a day they'd given me blisters.
</pre>

<pre>
その靴(くつ)は楽(らく)になる<b>どころか</b>、履(は)くたびに痛(いた)くなる。
Far from breaking in, those shoes hurt more every time I wear them.
</pre>

`ところが` needs a full stop before it and a finished event after it; `どころか` sits
mid-sentence and needs two competing descriptions of the same thing.

### ～どころか vs ～どころではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どころか` continues the sentence and offers the truth.
`どころではない` ends it and says the thing is out of the question.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの準備(じゅんび)で、映画(えいが)を見(み)る<b>どころではない</b>。
With the move to get ready, watching a film is out of the question.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの準備(じゅんび)で、映画(えいが)<b>どころか</b>、買(か)い物(もの)にも行(い)けていない。
What with the move, I haven't even managed the shopping, never mind a film.
</pre>

The first is a verdict on the situation. The second still describes what is and is not
happening, and it needs its second half.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 漢字(かんじ)どころか、ひらがなも読(よ)めます → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)どころか、ひらがなも読(よ)めません
  The "never mind A" frame is built on a negative. With an affirmative ending there is
  nothing being ruled out and the sentence collapses.

- ❌ 値段(ねだん)が下(さ)がるどころか、少(すこ)し下(さ)がった → ✅ 値段(ねだん)が下(さ)がるどころか、かえって上(あ)がった
  What follows `どころか` has to run counter to what precedes it. Repeating the same
  direction, even weakly, gives the pattern nothing to reject.

- ❌ 一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)った。どころか、彼(かれ)は来(こ)なかった。 → ✅ 一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)った。<b>ところが</b>、彼(かれ)は来(こ)なかった。
  `どころか` cannot start a sentence. A narrative reversal between two sentences is
  `ところが`.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は残業(ざんぎょう)で、飲(の)み会(かい)どころか。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は残業(ざんぎょう)で、飲(の)み会(かい)<b>どころではない</b>。
  `どころか` is a join and needs a second half. To end the sentence with "that's out of
  the question", use `どころではない`.

## Practice

**1.** 貯金(ちょきん)____、今月(こんげつ)の家賃(やちん)も払(はら)えない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

貯金(ちょきん)<b>どころか</b>、今月(こんげつ)の家賃(やちん)も払(はら)えない。

The bigger, more ambitious item - saving - goes first and is dismissed; the smaller
one follows with `も` and a negative. That ordering is the fixed shape of the "never
mind A" frame.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 弟(おとうと)はギター（どころか・だけでなく）ピアノも弾(ひ)けます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弟(おとうと)はギター<b>だけでなく</b>ピアノも弾(ひ)けます。

Both instruments are genuinely played, so the first item is being *accepted* and the
second added - that is `だけでなく`. With `どころか` the sentence would have to deny
the guitar, and it would need a negative ending.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Far from thanking me, she got angry."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)はお礼(れい)を言(い)う<b>どころか</b>、怒(おこ)り出(だ)した。

The plain form <code>言(い)う</code> takes `どころか` directly, and the second clause
runs in the opposite direction to the first. This is the indignant use, and it is the
commonest one in speech.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 試験(しけん)が近(ちか)いので、遊(あそ)びに行(い)くどころか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 試験(しけん)が近(ちか)いので、遊(あそ)びに行(い)く<b>どころではない</b>。

`どころか` is a connector: something has to follow it. When you want to end on "that
is out of the question", the form is `どころではない` (or the more casual
`どころじゃない`).

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部屋(へや)が片付(かたづ)くどころか、前(まえ)より散(ち)らかってしまった。
(b) 部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けたのに、また散(ち)らかってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) denies the expected direction outright: the tidying did not merely fail, it made
things worse. `どころか` is doing the work of "far from", and the sentence carries a
note of exasperation at how completely the expectation was inverted.

(b) reports two facts - the room was tidied, and it is messy again - and colours the
mismatch with the speaker's dissatisfaction. Nothing is being denied; `のに` simply
says the second half should not have followed the first.

</details>

# ～くせに

**Meaning** · even though; and yet - said about a person, with a curl of the lip

`くせに` marks the same expectation-versus-outcome gap that `のに` marks, and then adds
something `のに` does not have: a **judgement on the person**. Whoever the sentence is
about should have behaved otherwise, and the speaker is holding it against them.

Two restrictions come with that. The clause has to be about a **person**, and both
halves of the sentence have to be about the **same** person. Those two facts are most
of what there is to learn here, and they are what stops `くせに` from being a free
substitute for `のに`.

## Formation

`くせに` follows a plain form. It began life as the noun <code>くせ</code> ("a habit,
a trait"), which is why nouns link to it with `の` and な-adjectives with `な` - the
same links a noun takes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + くせに | 知(し)っている → 知(し)っているくせに |
| Verb (past) | plain past + くせに | 断(ことわ)った → 断(ことわ)ったくせに |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + くせに | できない → できないくせに |
| い-adjective | plain form + くせに | 若(わか)い → 若(わか)いくせに |
| な-adjective | な + くせに | 下手(へた) → 下手(へた)なくせに |
| Noun | の + くせに | 新人(しんじん) → 新人(しんじん)のくせに |
| Sentence-final jab | …くせに。 | 分(わ)かっているくせに。 |
| Blunter colloquial variant | …くせして | 知(し)らないくせして |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **one person, both clauses.** ✅
彼(かれ)は手伝(てつだ)ってもらったくせに、お礼(れい)も言(い)わない。
❌ 私(わたし)が手伝(てつだ)ったくせに、彼(かれ)はお礼(れい)も言(い)わない。
The second sentence has two different subjects, so it must use `のに`. `のに` never
cares who is doing what; `くせに` is passing judgement on one individual and cannot
split its attention.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the subject also has to be a **person**. Textbook Japanese does not allow
❌<code>この道(みち)は広(ひろ)いくせに暗(くら)い</code>. In casual grumbling you
will hear people stretch it to a shop, a phone or a piece of software they are cross
with, which works precisely because it treats the object as somebody misbehaving.
Keep it for people until you have a feel for that.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
知(し)っている<b>くせに</b>、何(なに)も教(おし)えてくれない。
He knows perfectly well and won't tell me a thing.
</pre>

<pre>
下手(へた)な<b>くせに</b>、文句(もんく)ばかり言(い)っている。
He's no good at it himself, and all he does is complain.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)も遅(おく)れてきた<b>くせに</b>、人(ひと)の遅刻(ちこく)には厳(きび)しい。
He turned up late himself, yet he's merciless about other people being late.
</pre>

<pre>
甘(あま)い物(もの)は嫌(きら)いだと言(い)っていた<b>くせに</b>、ケーキを三(みっ)つも食(た)べた。
He said he didn't like sweet things - then ate three slices of cake.
</pre>

<pre>
運転(うんてん)できない<b>くせに</b>、助手席(じょしゅせき)でうるさく指示(しじ)を出(だ)す。
He can't drive himself, but he barks directions from the passenger seat.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに反対(はんたい)していた<b>くせに</b>、今(いま)では誰(だれ)よりも楽(たの)しんでいる。
She was so against it, and now she's enjoying it more than any of us.
</pre>

<pre>
本当(ほんとう)は寂(さび)しい<b>くせに</b>、一人(ひとり)のほうが楽(らく)だなんて言(い)っている。
He's lonely really, and there he is saying he prefers being on his own.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)では一円(いちえん)も出(だ)していない<b>くせに</b>、店(みせ)の選(えら)び方(かた)にだけは口(くち)を出(だ)してくる。
He hasn't put in a single yen, and yet he has plenty to say about which restaurant we pick.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`くせに` is **never neutral**. Where `のに` ranges from mild surprise to real
irritation, `くせに` starts at criticism and goes down from there: blame, scorn,
resentment. Its politeness ceiling is low. You do not use it about a customer, a
teacher, a manager or anyone else you owe deference to, and it does not belong in
written Japanese outside dialogue.

Within that range it has two quite different lives:

- **Aimed at someone absent** - a complaint, and it can sound genuinely mean.
- **Aimed at a friend, face to face** - teasing, and it softens almost to affection.
  「泣(な)いてたくせに。」 said with a grin between friends is nothing worse than
  "you *were* crying, though."

The sentence-final version, with the main clause simply left off, is common in casual
speech: 「本当(ほんとう)は行(い)きたいくせに。」 It works like a raised eyebrow - 
the listener knows what was left unsaid.

`くせして` is the same word, blunter and more colloquial. `くせに` is already strong;
`くせして` is stronger, and rarer in mixed company.

A note about 子(こ)どものくせに and 新人(しんじん)のくせに. These fixed scoldings
are still heard, but they land as high-handed and are drifting out of acceptable
workplace speech. Recognise them; think twice before producing them.

Finally, `くせに` can be turned on yourself, and then it becomes self-mockery:
泳(およ)げないくせに、深(ふか)いところまで行(い)ってしまった。 The contempt is
still there - the speaker has simply aimed it at their own behaviour.

## Contrast

### ～くせに vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` reports a gap and expresses the speaker's dissatisfaction.
`くせに` blames a person for the gap, and needs the **same person** in both clauses.
Every `くせに` sentence could be `のに`; the reverse is not true.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)が何度(なんど)も誘(さそ)った<b>のに</b>、弟(おとうと)は一度(いちど)も来(こ)なかった。
I invited him again and again, and my brother never came once.
</pre>

Two subjects - I invited, he didn't come - so `くせに` is unavailable no matter how
annoyed the speaker is. Now put both halves on one person:

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は自分(じぶん)から誘(さそ)った<b>くせに</b>、当日(とうじつ)になって断(ことわ)ってきた。
My brother was the one who suggested it, and then cancelled on the day.
</pre>

Even where both are possible, the feeling differs. 遠(とお)いのに来(き)てくれた
is gratitude; ❌遠(とお)いくせに来(き)てくれた is not a sentence anyone would say,
because you cannot sneer and thank someone in the same breath.

### ～くせに vs ～にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same gap, opposite register. `にもかかわらず` is formal, written
and emotionally flat; `くせに` is spoken and hostile. `のに` sits between them.

</div>

`にもかかわらず` takes a plain form or a bare noun:
雨天(うてん)にもかかわらず、大勢(おおぜい)が集(あつ)まった。

<pre>
悪天候(あくてんこう)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、開会式(かいかいしき)は予定(よてい)どおり行(おこな)われた。
Despite the poor weather, the opening ceremony went ahead as scheduled.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)が悪(わる)いと文句(もんく)を言(い)っていた<b>くせに</b>、一番(いちばん)早(はや)く来(き)ていた。
For all his moaning about the weather, he was the first one there.
</pre>

Notice that the formal version happily takes a **non-human** subject - the weather - 
which `くせに` cannot. Reports, notices and speeches use `にもかかわらず` precisely
because it registers the contradiction without any attitude attached.

### ～くせに vs ～ながら（も）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながらも` concedes something about a person without attacking
them, and often about the speaker themselves. It is literary where `くせに` is
colloquial.

</div>

<pre>
狭(せま)い部屋(へや)<b>ながらも</b>、一人(ひとり)で暮(く)らせるのが嬉(うれ)しかった。
Cramped as the room was, I was glad to be living on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
狭(せま)い部屋(へや)に住(す)んでいる<b>くせに</b>、家具(かぐ)ばかり買(か)っている。
He lives in a cramped little room and still keeps buying furniture.
</pre>

`ながらも` accepts the drawback and moves on; `くせに` uses it as ammunition. `ながらも`
also has no same-subject-must-be-human requirement of the kind `くせに` imposes, which
is one reason it survives in formal writing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)くせに → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>の</b>くせに
  `くせ` is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`. な-adjectives need `な`:
  ✅<code>下手(へた)なくせに</code>, ❌<code>下手(へた)だくせに</code>.

- ❌ 母(はは)が作(つく)ったくせに、兄(あに)は食(た)べなかった → ✅ 母(はは)が作(つく)った<b>のに</b>、兄(あに)は食(た)べなかった
  Two different people, so `くせに` is out. This is the single commonest `くせに`
  error, and it produces a sentence native speakers cannot parse.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)はお忙(いそが)しいくせに、話(はなし)を聞(き)いてくださった → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)はお忙(いそが)しい<b>のに</b>、話(はなし)を聞(き)いてくださった
  `くせに` sneers, so it cannot coexist with honorific language or with gratitude.
  Aimed upward it is close to an insult.

- ❌ この電車(でんしゃ)は新(あたら)しいくせに遅(おそ)い → ✅ この電車(でんしゃ)は新(あたら)しい<b>のに</b>遅(おそ)い
  Standard `くせに` wants a person to blame. A train can be disappointing, but it
  cannot be shameless.

## Practice

**1.** 山田(やまだ)さんは料理(りょうり)ができない____、レストランの味(あじ)に厳(きび)しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)さんは料理(りょうり)ができない<b>くせに</b>、レストランの味(あじ)に厳(きび)しい。

Both clauses describe Yamada, and the speaker clearly thinks he has no right to be
fussy - a person, one subject, and a judgement. That is exactly the shape `くせに`
wants. The `ない`-form attaches directly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいていた（くせに・にもかかわらず）、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)にならなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいていた<b>にもかかわらず</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)にならなかった。

The subject of the first clause is a typhoon, and the two clauses have different
subjects besides. `にもかかわらず` accepts both, and its flat, formal tone suits a
report of what the organisers decided.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She said she wasn't interested, and yet she read the whole thing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

興味(きょうみ)がないと言(い)っていた<b>くせに</b>、最後(さいご)まで読(よ)んでいた。

One person does both things - claims disinterest, then reads it all - and the English
"and yet" carries a knowing dig. `のに` would also be grammatical; `くせに` is what
gives it the teasing edge.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 弟(おとうと)が皿(さら)を割(わ)ったくせに、私(わたし)が怒(おこ)られた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 弟(おとうと)が皿(さら)を割(わ)った<b>のに</b>、私(わたし)が怒(おこ)られた。

The breaking and the telling-off belong to different people, and `くせに` cannot span
two subjects. If you want to keep `くせに`, rewrite so that one person carries both
halves: 弟(おとうと)は自分(じぶん)で割(わ)ったくせに、黙(だま)っていた。

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)なのに、よく気(き)がつく。
(b) 新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)のくせに、口(くち)の利(き)き方(かた)を知(し)らない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is `のに` used for pleasant surprise: newness predicted less, and the speaker is
impressed. `くせに` could not replace it here, because you cannot sneer at somebody
while praising them.

(b) is `くせに` doing what it does - using the person's junior status as grounds for
looking down on them. It is grammatical and it is also the kind of remark that dates
the speaker; `のに` would report the same gap without the contempt.

</details>

# ～ながら / ～いながら / ～ながらも

**Meaning** · although; despite - a quality is granted, and something that does not fit it is asserted anyway

This is the **second** `ながら`. The one you met at N4 stacks two actions on top of each
other ("while eating"); this one concedes. It grants a fact - 
the room is small, he knew the risk, she is only a child - and then states something
that the fact should have ruled out.

The two are told apart by shape, not by guesswork. Concessive `ながら` attaches to
い-adjectives, な-adjective stems, nouns and `ている` forms, none of which the "while"
`ながら` can take. And `ながらも`, with the extra `も`, is always concessive.

On this batch's axis of how much the speaker minds, concessive `ながら` minds least of
all. It is dignified rather than annoyed, and it is often outright warm:
狭(せま)いながらも楽(たの)しい我(わ)が家(や) is a compliment to a small flat.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb of knowing／thinking | ます-stem + ながら | 知(し)る → 知(し)りながら |
| Verb in ている | てい + ながら（も） | 分(わ)かっている → 分(わ)かっていながら |
| い-adjective | plain form + ながら（も） | 狭(せま)い → 狭(せま)いながらも |
| な-adjective | stem + ながら（も） | 残念(ざんねん) → 残念(ざんねん)ながら |
| Noun | Noun + ながら（も） | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもながら |
| Noun + に (adverbial) | Noun + ながらに | 涙(なみだ) → 涙(なみだ)ながらに |
| Existence, fixed | いながらにして | 家(いえ)にいながらにして |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - な-adjectives and nouns attach **bare**: ✅
<code>残念(ざんねん)ながら</code>, ❌<code>残念(ざんねん)なながら</code>,
❌<code>子(こ)どもだながら</code>. There is no `な` and no `だ` in front of this
`ながら`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>いながら</code> is just <code>～ている</code> plus `ながら` with the
`て` swallowed in speech and writing alike: 知(し)っていながら. It is the standard
way to concede that somebody **was aware** and acted anyway, and it is noticeably
sharper than a plain `のに` because awareness is built into it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
狭(せま)い<b>ながらも</b>、居心地(いごこち)のいい部屋(へや)です。
It's a small room, but a comfortable one.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>残念(ざんねん)ながら</b>、今回(こんかい)は見送(みおく)らせていただきます。
Regrettably, we'll be passing on it this time.
</pre>

<pre>
危険(きけん)だと知(し)り<b>ながら</b>、誰(だれ)も注意(ちゅうい)しようとしなかった。
Everyone knew it was dangerous, and no one would say anything.
</pre>

<pre>
体(からだ)に悪(わる)いと分(わ)かってい<b>ながら</b>、なかなかやめられません。
I know full well it's bad for me, and still I can't quite stop.
</pre>

<pre>
小(ちい)さな店(みせ)<b>ながら</b>、常連(じょうれん)が全国(ぜんこく)にいるそうだ。
Small as it is, the shop apparently has regulars all over the country.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>ながらに</b>、母(はは)が無理(むり)をしていることは感(かん)じていた。
Young as I was, I could tell my mother was pushing herself too hard.
</pre>

<pre>
貧(まず)しい<b>ながらも</b>、家族(かぞく)全員(ぜんいん)がよく笑(わら)う家(いえ)だった。
Poor though we were, ours was a house where everybody laughed a lot.
</pre>

<pre>
家(いえ)に<b>いながらにして</b>、世界中(せかいじゅう)の授業(じゅぎょう)が受(う)けられる時代(じだい)になりました。
We've reached an age where you can take classes from anywhere in the world without leaving home.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Concessive `ながら` is **written and formal**. In an essay, a notice, a speech or a
carefully worded email it is entirely ordinary; in relaxed conversation it survives
mainly as a handful of set phrases, and using it freely in chat sounds like you have
swallowed a textbook.

The set phrases are worth learning as vocabulary, because they are extremely common:

- 残念(ざんねん)ながら - "regrettably", the standard soft refusal.
- 失礼(しつれい)ながら - "if I may be so bold", before a delicate question.
- 恥(は)ずかしながら - "I'm ashamed to say."
- 我(われ)ながら - "even if I say so myself."
- いつもながら - "as ever."
- 生(う)まれながら（に） - "from birth, innately."

`ながらも` is the emphatic version. The `も` makes the concession unmistakable, which
is useful precisely because bare `ながら` could be read as "while". It also adds a
faint note of admiration, which is why it collocates so happily with things that are
small, poor or difficult but turn out well.

`いながらにして` is a fixed expression meaning "without stirring from where you are",
and it has drifted into technology writing: 自宅(じたく)にいながらにして
診察(しんさつ)が受(う)けられる.

## Contrast

### Concessive ～ながら vs simultaneous ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what is in front of it. An adjective, a noun or a `ている`
form can only be the **concessive** one. A plain action verb in ます-stem form is
normally the **"while"** one, unless the verb is about knowing or thinking.

</div>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>ながら</b>、洗(あら)い物(もの)をしています。
I do the washing up with music on.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)すると思(おも)い<b>ながら</b>、止(と)めなかった。
I thought it would go wrong, and I didn't stop him.
</pre>

Both take a ます-stem, and the second is concessive only because
<code>思(おも)う</code> is a verb of mental attitude - you cannot "do thinking while"
in the way you can do listening while. When in doubt, add `も`:
<code>思(おも)いながらも</code> can only mean "although."

The "while" `ながら` also demands two activities that last long enough to overlap;
the concessive one has no such requirement, because nothing is overlapping.

### ～ながらも vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same contradiction, different voice. `のに` is spoken and carries
the speaker's dissatisfaction. `ながらも` is written and carries composure, and often
respect.

</div>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて<b>ながらも</b>、落(お)ち着(つ)いて司会(しかい)を務(つと)めていた。
It was his first time, and yet he chaired it calmly.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めてな<b>のに</b>、ずいぶん落(お)ち着(つ)いていたね。
For a first go you were awfully calm, weren't you.
</pre>

The `ながらも` version would suit a written report of the event; the `のに` version is
what you say to the person afterwards. Note the small formal difference too: `のに`
needs `な` after a noun, `ながらも` needs nothing.

### ～ながらも vs ～つつも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - near twins, both literary. `つつも` attaches only to a ます-stem,
so it cannot concede an adjective or a noun; `ながらも` can.

</div>

<pre>
無理(むり)だと思(おも)い<b>つつも</b>、もう一度(いちど)応募(おうぼ)してみた。
Even while thinking it was hopeless, I applied one more time.
</pre>

<pre>
不安(ふあん)<b>ながらも</b>、自分(じぶん)で決(き)めた道(みち)を選(えら)んだ。
Uneasy though I was, I chose the path I'd decided on myself.
</pre>

`つつも` is the more bookish of the two and is almost always about an inner conflict - 
the speaker knew better and did it anyway. `ながらも` covers a much wider range,
including plain external facts like a room being small.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 知(し)るながら → ✅ 知(し)りながら
  `ながら` takes the ます-stem, exactly as the "while" version does. The dictionary
  form never appears before it.

- ❌ 残念(ざんねん)なながら → ✅ 残念(ざんねん)ながら
  な-adjectives attach by the bare stem. The same goes for nouns:
  ✅<code>子(こ)どもながら</code>, ❌<code>子(こ)どもだながら</code>.

- ❌ 残念(ざんねん)ながらも、今回(こんかい)は中止(ちゅうし)です → ✅ 残念(ざんねん)ながら、今回(こんかい)は中止(ちゅうし)です
  <code>残念(ざんねん)ながら</code> is a frozen adverb meaning "regrettably" and does
  not take the extra `も`. The `も` version belongs to live concessions such as
  <code>狭(せま)いながらも</code>.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しながら、成績(せいせき)が上(あ)がらない → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)している<b>のに</b>、成績(せいせき)が上(あ)がらない
  With an ordinary action verb, `ながら` is read as "while", and "while studying, my
  grades don't rise" is not a sentence. Use `のに`, or make the awareness explicit with
  <code>勉強(べんきょう)していながら</code>.

# ～にしては

**Meaning** · for ~; considering it's ~ - measured against this standard, the result is not what you would predict

`にしては` names a yardstick and then reports that the thing being judged does not fit
it. A first-year student who writes a firm hand; a March day that feels like May; a
first attempt that came out well. The yardstick is real and specific, and the result
overshoots or undershoots it.

On this batch's axis of how much the speaker minds, `にしては` sits at the mild end
alongside `わりに`. It is an **evaluation**, not a complaint - most often a compliment,
occasionally a gentle dig, and almost never anything sharper.

## Formation

`にしては` attaches to a noun or to a plain-form verb clause. Nothing intervenes: no
`だ`, no `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + にしては | 一年生(いちねんせい) → 一年生(いちねんせい)にしては |
| Noun of time／season | Noun + にしては | 三月(さんがつ) → 三月(さんがつ)にしては |
| Verb | plain form + にしては | 習(なら)う → 習(なら)うにしては |
| Verb (past) | plain past + にしては | 作(つく)った → 作(つく)ったにしては |
| Verb (ている) | ている + にしては | 練習(れんしゅう)している → 練習(れんしゅう)しているにしては |
| Referring back | それにしては | - → それにしては元気(げんき)だ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the standard must be **specific enough to predict something**.
✅<code>この値段(ねだん)にしては</code>, ❌<code>値段(ねだん)にしては</code>. A bare
"price" predicts nothing; *this* price predicts a great deal. When the standard is
general rather than definite, the pattern you want is `わりに`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - い-adjectives and な-adjectives do not attach to `にしては` directly.
❌<code>寒(さむ)いにしては</code> → ✅<code>寒(さむ)い日(ひ)にしては</code>, or
switch to <code>寒(さむ)いわりに</code>, which takes adjectives happily.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
一年生(いちねんせい)<b>にしては</b>、しっかりした字(じ)を書(か)きますね。
For a first-year, she writes a very steady hand.
</pre>

<pre>
三月(さんがつ)<b>にしては</b>暖(あたた)かい日(ひ)が続(つづ)いています。
It's been unusually warm for March.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて作(つく)った<b>にしては</b>、よくできていますよ。
For a first attempt, that's turned out well.
</pre>

<pre>
この値段(ねだん)<b>にしては</b>、生地(きじ)がしっかりしている。
For this price, the fabric is remarkably solid.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)乗(の)った<b>にしては</b>、傷(きず)が少(すく)ないですね。
For a car that's been driven ten years, there are hardly any scratches.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりだと言(い)っていた<b>にしては</b>、敬語(けいご)がとても自然(しぜん)だ。
Considering he said he'd only just arrived in Japan, his honorific language is very natural.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きな地震(じしん)だった<b>にしては</b>、被害(ひがい)は少(すく)なくて済(す)んだ。
For an earthquake that size, the damage was mercifully limited.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に決(き)めた<b>にしては</b>、旅行(りょこう)の計画(けいかく)が細(こま)かいところまでよくできている。
For something decided at the last minute, the trip is planned down to the details.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にしては` is register-neutral. It appears unchanged in `ます` speech, in plain speech
and in writing, and nothing about it is stiff or slangy. It pairs naturally with
degree adverbs - `ずいぶん`, `なかなか`, `けっこう`, <code>意外(いがい)に</code> - because the
whole point of the sentence is that the result is off the predicted mark.

What you do have to watch is the **social arithmetic**. `にしては` says out loud that
your expectations for the standard were low. That is fine for a beginner, a first
attempt or the weather, and it is not fine aimed upward at a person:
「先生(せんせい)にしてはお上手(じょうず)ですね」 tells your teacher you assumed
teachers were bad at this. With superiors, praise them without the yardstick, or use
`さすが` - 「さすが先生(せんせい)ですね」 - which says the opposite: exactly what one
would expect.

`それにしては` is the stand-alone version, pointing back at something already said:
「徹夜(てつや)したんです。」「それにしては元気(げんき)ですね。」 Note that this is
`それにしても` with one particle changed, and the meanings diverge completely - see the
next section.

## Contrast

### ～にしては vs ～わりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にしては` needs a **definite** standard; `わりに` accepts a
**general** one and attaches to adjectives as well. Where both fit, they mean much the
same thing.

</div>

<pre>
このかばんは値段(ねだん)の<b>わりに</b>じょうぶだ。
This bag is sturdy for what it cost.
</pre>

<pre>
三千円(さんぜんえん)のかばん<b>にしては</b>じょうぶだ。
For a three-thousand-yen bag, it's sturdy.
</pre>

Both are natural. The difference is that `わりに` can leave "price" as an abstract
scale, while `にしては` wants an actual figure or an actual category to measure
against. That is also why `わりに` handles adjectives - 
<code>安(やす)いわりに</code>, <code>若(わか)いわりに</code> - and `にしては` does
not.

### ～にしては vs ～にしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` evaluates, `も` concedes. `にしては` says "measured against
X, this is surprising." `にしても` says "even granting X, this still is not good
enough."

</div>

<pre>
初日(しょにち)<b>にしては</b>、よく売(う)れたほうだと思(おも)います。
For an opening day, I'd say it sold well.
</pre>

<pre>
初日(しょにち)<b>にしても</b>、この数字(すうじ)は少(すく)なすぎませんか。
Opening day or not, isn't this figure far too low?
</pre>

Same noun, same day, opposite verdicts. One particle decides whether you are praising
the result or refusing to accept the excuse.

### ～にしては vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` needs a **whole clause** and carries the speaker's
dissatisfaction. `にしては` takes a bare noun as well, and carries mild surprise at how
the measurement came out.

</div>

<pre>
一週間(いっしゅうかん)しか練習(れんしゅう)していない<b>にしては</b>、上手(じょうず)に弾(ひ)けている。
For someone who's only practised a week, she plays well.
</pre>

<pre>
一週間(いっしゅうかん)も練習(れんしゅう)した<b>のに</b>、まだうまく弾(ひ)けない。
I practised for a whole week and I still can't play it properly.
</pre>

The first weighs the result against the effort and finds it impressive. The second
weighs the same two things and feels cheated. If the sentence is a compliment,
`にしては` is nearly always the safer choice; `のに` used for praise needs the rest of
the sentence to make the warmth obvious.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)だにしては → ✅ 学生(がくせい)にしては
  Nouns attach bare. The `だ` here comes from thinking of `にしては` as following a
  complete sentence, which it does not have to.

- ❌ プロ野球(やきゅう)選手(せんしゅ)にしては足(あし)が速(はや)い → ✅ プロ野球(やきゅう)選手(せんしゅ)<b>だけあって</b>足(あし)が速(はや)い
  A professional athlete being fast is exactly what the standard predicts, and
  `にしては` requires the result to defy the standard. When the result confirms it,
  the pattern is `だけあって`.

- ❌ 値段(ねだん)にしては安(やす)い → ✅ この値段(ねだん)にしては安(やす)い ／ 値段(ねだん)の<b>わりに</b>安(やす)い
  A bare "price" is not a definite standard. Either pin it down with `この`, or switch
  to `わりに`.

- ❌ 寒(さむ)いにしては、人(ひと)が多(おお)い → ✅ 寒(さむ)い<b>わりに</b>、人(ひと)が多(おお)い
  い-adjectives do not attach to `にしては`. Use `わりに`, or supply a noun:
  <code>寒(さむ)い日(ひ)にしては</code>.

**4.** Find and fix the error: 高(たか)いにしては、この椅子(いす)は座(すわ)り心地(ごこち)がよくない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 高(たか)い<b>わりに</b>、この椅子(いす)は座(すわ)り心地(ごこち)がよくない。

An い-adjective cannot attach to `にしては`. `わりに` takes adjectives directly and
carries the same "measured against that, this is unexpected" meaning. Keeping
`にしては` would mean naming a definite standard instead:
この値段(ねだん)にしては.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子(こ)どもが作(つく)ったにしては、よくできている。
(b) 子(こ)どもが作(つく)ったのに、よくできている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the natural sentence. It sets "made by a child" as the yardstick, and reports
that the result clears it. The praise is measured against a deliberately low bar,
which is exactly what `にしては` announces.

(b) is odd, and if anything faintly rude. `のに` treats the two facts as being in
conflict and adds the speaker's feeling about that conflict - which here comes out as
"a child made it, and it has no business being this good." Where a comparison against
a standard is what you mean, `にしては` is the pattern.

</details>

# ～わりに

**Meaning** · for ~; considering ~ - the result is out of proportion to what the premise leads you to expect

The `わり` in `わりに` is the noun meaning "proportion, ratio", and that is exactly what
the pattern measures. Set one fact against another - the price against the quality,
the age against the speed, the hours put in against the marks that came back - and
`わりに` says the two do not scale together.

On this batch's axis of how much the speaker minds, `わりに` is mild, like `にしては`.
It notices a mismatch and comments on it. Whether the comment is admiring or slightly
disappointed depends entirely on which way the mismatch runs.

## Formation

`わりに` behaves like the noun it came from: nouns link with `の`, な-adjectives with
`な`. Adding `は` - `わりには` - is optional and very common.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + の + わりに（は） | 年(とし) → 年(とし)のわりに |
| Verb | plain form + わりに（は） | よく食(た)べる → よく食(た)べるわりに |
| Verb (past) | plain past + わりに（は） | 練習(れんしゅう)した → 練習(れんしゅう)したわりに |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わりに（は） | 寝(ね)ていない → 寝(ね)ていないわりに |
| い-adjective | plain form + わりに（は） | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いわりに |
| な-adjective | な + わりに（は） | 地味(じみ) → 地味(じみ)なわりに |
| Adverb (different word) | わりに／わりと + adjective | わりとおいしい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two halves must be **out of proportion**. If the result is
what the premise predicts, `わりに` has nothing to say:
❌<code>たくさん練習(れんしゅう)したわりに上手(じょうず)になった</code>. Effort
matching result is `だけあって`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - usually written in kana, though 割(わり)に is not wrong. Adding `は`
sharpens the contrast a little: <code>安(やす)いわりに</code> simply notes the
mismatch, while <code>安(やす)いわりには</code> leans on it. Both are entirely
ordinary.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>わりに</b>、味(あじ)がいい。
This place is good for what it charges.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は年(とし)の<b>わりに</b>足(あし)が速(はや)い。
My father is quick on his feet for his age.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)はよく食(た)べる<b>わりに</b>、全然(ぜんぜん)太(ふと)らないんです。
My sister eats a lot and never puts on any weight.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)に向(む)かっている時間(じかん)が長(なが)い<b>わりには</b>、点数(てんすう)が伸(の)びない。
For all the hours he spends at his desk, his marks aren't going up.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は駅(えき)から遠(とお)い<b>わりに</b>、家賃(やちん)が高(たか)すぎませんか。
Isn't the rent rather high for somewhere this far from the station?
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)に半年(はんとし)かけた<b>わりには</b>、当日(とうじつ)はあっという間(ま)に終(お)わってしまった。
For six months of preparation, the day itself was over in no time.
</pre>

<pre>
あの映画(えいが)は評判(ひょうばん)がいい<b>わりに</b>、席(せき)がずいぶん空(す)いていた。
For a film with such good word of mouth, the cinema was pretty empty.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)が好(す)きだと言(い)っている<b>わりに</b>、道具(どうぐ)は一(ひと)つも持(も)っていない。
For someone who says he loves the mountains, he doesn't own a single piece of kit.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`わりに` is colloquial-neutral: at home in conversation, in reviews, in casual writing,
and unremarkable in `ます` speech. It carries no politeness of its own and no strong
emotion. What it does carry is a faint air of **appraisal** - the speaker has weighed
two things against each other and is reporting the ratio.

Which direction the appraisal runs is decided by the content, and both are common:

- **In the thing's favour** - 安(やす)いわりにおいしい, 年(とし)のわりに元気(げんき)だ.
- **Against it** - 高(たか)いわりに量(りょう)が少(すく)ない,
  時間(じかん)をかけたわりに中身(なかみ)がない.

Because the second use is so easy, `わりに` aimed at a person can sting a little more
than `にしては` does: 「若(わか)いわりに体力(たいりょく)がないね」 is not a
compliment. As with `にしては`, avoid pointing it at someone senior to you.

Then there is the **adverb** `わりに`, more often said as `わりと`, meaning "fairly,
rather": 「今日(きょう)はわりと暖(あたた)かいね。」 It has no second half and
compares nothing in particular - it is just a hedge, roughly the strength of
`けっこう`. Same string, different word, and context separates them easily once you
know both exist.

## Contrast

### ～わりに vs ～にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にしては` needs a **definite** yardstick and usually flatters.
`わりに` takes a **general** one, attaches to adjectives, and is just as happy pointing
out that something under-delivers.

</div>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)習(なら)った<b>にしては</b>、よく話(はな)せるほうだと思(おも)う。
For three years of lessons, I'd say he speaks well.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)習(なら)った<b>わりに</b>、まだ自己(じこ)紹介(しょうかい)しかできない。
For three years of lessons, he can still only introduce himself.
</pre>

Both take the same clause, and both are grammatical. The give-away is the direction of
the verdict: `にしては` sets a low bar and reports it cleared, so a disappointed
`にしては` sentence sounds strange, while `わりに` is neutral about which way the
imbalance falls. And only `わりに` can attach to a bare adjective:
✅<code>安(やす)いわりに</code>, ❌<code>安(やす)いにしては</code>.

### ～わりに vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わりに` weighs two things and finds the ratio off. `のに` says
two facts should not go together at all, and adds the speaker's dissatisfaction.
`わりに` needs a scale; `のに` does not.

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は厚(あつ)い<b>わりに</b>、軽(かる)い。
This dictionary is light for its thickness.
</pre>

<pre>
辞書(じしょ)を買(か)った<b>のに</b>、一度(いちど)も開(ひら)いていない。
I bought a dictionary and haven't opened it once.
</pre>

Thickness and weight are two points on scales, so they can be out of proportion - 
`わりに`. Buying a dictionary and never opening it is not a matter of proportion at
all; it is a plain contradiction, with the speaker's chagrin attached, which is `のに`.

### ～わりに (proportion) vs わりと (adverb)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if there is something to compare against, it is the proportion
`わりに` and it needs `の` or a clause in front. If there is nothing to compare
against, it is the adverb, and it means "fairly."

</div>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は<b>わりと</b>簡単(かんたん)でした。
That question was fairly straightforward.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は見(み)た目(め)の<b>わりに</b>簡単(かんたん)でした。
That question was easier than it looked.
</pre>

The first tells you only that it was not hard. The second measures the difficulty
against the intimidating look of it. In speech `わりと` is the commoner form of the
adverb; `わりに` on its own can be either word, and the presence or absence of a
comparison decides.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 年(とし)わりに若(わか)く見(み)える → ✅ 年(とし)<b>の</b>わりに若(わか)く見(み)える
  `わり` is a noun, so a noun in front of it links with `の` - exactly like
  <code>年(とし)のせいで</code> or <code>年(とし)の割合(わりあい)</code>.

- ❌ 静(しず)かわりに人(ひと)が多(おお)い → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>わりに人(ひと)が多(おお)い
  な-adjectives link with `な`. い-adjectives, by contrast, attach in their plain form
  with nothing added.

- ❌ よく寝(ね)たわりに、元気(げんき)になった → ✅ よく寝(ね)た<b>だけあって</b>、元気(げんき)になった
  Sleeping well and feeling well are in proportion, so there is no mismatch for
  `わりに` to report.

- ❌ 値段(ねだん)わりとおいしい → ✅ 値段(ねだん)<b>のわりに</b>おいしい ／ <b>わりと</b>おいしい
  Two different words have been run together. Either compare against the price, with
  `の`, or use the bare adverb and compare against nothing.

## Practice

**1.** 兄(あに)は体(からだ)が大(おお)きい____、力(ちから)はあまりない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

兄(あに)は体(からだ)が大(おお)きい<b>わりに</b>、力(ちから)はあまりない。

Size and strength are two scales that ought to move together, and the sentence reports
that they do not. い-adjectives attach in their plain form, so nothing goes between
<code>大(おお)きい</code> and `わりに`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この旅館(りょかん)は（値段(ねだん)のわりに・値段(ねだん)にしては）料理(りょうり)が立派(りっぱ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この旅館(りょかん)は<b>値段(ねだん)のわりに</b>料理(りょうり)が立派(りっぱ)だ。

"Price" here is a general scale rather than a definite figure, and `にしては` needs
something definite to measure against. Pin the figure down - この値段(ねだん)にしては
or 一万円(いちまんえん)にしては - and `にしては` becomes available again.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "For a small company, they pay surprisingly well."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

小(ちい)さい会社(かいしゃ)の<b>わりに</b>、給料(きゅうりょう)がいい。

A noun phrase links with `の`. You could also say
会社(かいしゃ)の規模(きぼ)のわりに, and <code>意外(いがい)に</code> before
<code>いい</code> would carry the "surprisingly" more explicitly.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この町(まち)は有名(ゆうめい)わりに、観光客(かんこうきゃく)が少(すく)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この町(まち)は有名(ゆうめい)<b>な</b>わりに、観光客(かんこうきゃく)が少(すく)ない。

<code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is a な-adjective, and `わりに` behaves like a noun, so
the link is `な`. Compare the noun pattern, which links with `の`:
有名(ゆうめい)さのわりに would be the noun version of the same idea.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 時間(じかん)をかけたわりに、いい結果(けっか)が出(で)なかった。
(b) 時間(じかん)をかけたのに、いい結果(けっか)が出(で)なかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an assessment of the ratio: a lot of time went in, not much came out, and the
speaker is stating the imbalance more than complaining about it. It would sit
comfortably in a written review of a project.

(b) is a complaint. `のに` says the time invested should have guaranteed a result, and
the speaker feels let down - frustration first, arithmetic second. Both are correct
Japanese, and the choice tells the listener how much you mind.

</details>
