# N3 - While it's happening


---

# ～うちに

**Meaning** · while a state still holds - and with the sense that it will not hold much longer

<code>～うちに</code> opens a window and tells you to act inside it. The window is a
state that is currently true - it's hot, you're young, the baby is asleep - and the
pattern quietly carries the reason for the hurry: **that state is going to end.**
A second, softer use drops the urgency and reports a change that crept up on you
while something was going on: <code>聞(き)いているうちに…</code>, "as I listened…".

## Formation

<code>うちに</code> is the noun <code>うち</code> ("interior, span") plus `に`, so
everything in front of it is in the shape that modifies a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ている) | ている + うちに | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)っているうちに |
| Verb (stative, dictionary) | Plain non-past + うちに | いる → 日本(にほん)にいるうちに |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + うちに | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れないうちに |
| い-adjective | Plain form + うちに | 熱(あつ)い → 熱(あつ)いうちに |
| な-adjective | な + うちに | 元気(げんき)だ → 元気(げんき)なうちに |
| Noun | の + うちに | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)のうちに |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the subject inside the <code>うちに</code> clause takes `が`, never `は`:
子(こ)どもが寝(ね)ているうちに. This is the same embedding rule you met with
たら and とき clauses at N4.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a plain dictionary-form verb before <code>うちに</code> is limited to verbs
that describe a *lasting situation* - いる, 住(す)む, 勤(つと)める. For an
ordinary action verb you need ている: 読(よ)んでいるうちに, not
読(よ)むうちに.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
スープが<b>熱(あつ)いうちに</b>どうぞ。
Have the soup while it's hot.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>明(あか)るいうちに</b>帰(かえ)ろう。
Let's head back while it's still light.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>若(わか)いうちに</b>、いろいろな国(くに)を見(み)ておきたい。
I want to see plenty of countries while I'm young.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>学生(がくせい)のうちに</b>車(くるま)の免許(めんきょ)を取(と)っておいた。
I made sure to get my driving licence while I was still a student.
</pre>

<pre>
両親(りょうしん)が<b>元気(げんき)なうちに</b>、一度(いちど)一緒(いっしょ)に温泉(おんせん)へ行(い)きたい。
I'd like to go to a hot spring with my parents once, while they're still in good health.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが昼寝(ひるね)を<b>しているうちに</b>、たまった洗濯物(せんたくもの)をたたんだ。
While the kids were napping I folded the laundry that had piled up.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)の話(はなし)を<b>聞(き)いているうちに</b>、だんだん意味(いみ)が分(わ)かってきた。
As I listened to the teacher it gradually began to make sense.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)で決(き)まったことは、<b>覚(おぼ)えているうちに</b>メモしておかないと、夕方(ゆうがた)には全部(ぜんぶ)忘(わす)れてしまいますよ。
If you don't jot down what was decided in the meeting while you still remember it, by evening you'll have lost the lot.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>うちに</code> is register-neutral. It takes ます or plain endings without changing
character, and it turns up in signage, in advice from your grandmother and in company
email alike.

What is *not* neutral is the shape of the main clause. Because the pattern exists to
say "act before the window shuts", it pulls strongly toward volitional and advisory
endings - よう, たい, ておく, ておこう, てください, たほうがいい, ないと. A flat past
statement with no connection to the window closing sounds off, and that is usually the
signal that you wanted <code>間(あいだ)に</code> instead.

The <code>～ているうちに</code> use is the exception, and it is worth learning as its own
thing. There the main clause is a **change that happened to you**, typically unnoticed
and typically expressed with てくる, てしまう or a なる verb:
歩(ある)いているうちに道(みち)に迷(まよ)ってしまった. Nobody is being advised
to hurry; the sentence is reporting drift.

Two fixed expressions to recognise. <code>そのうちに</code> (often just そのうち) is an
adverb meaning "before long, one of these days" - 
そのうち遊(あそ)びに行(い)きます is a polite, pleasantly vague "I'll drop by
sometime." And <code>知(し)らないうちに</code> means "without my noticing", not "while
I didn't know" in any literal sense.

There is a mild literary flavour to <code>若(わか)いうちに</code> and
<code>元気(げんき)なうちに</code>, which show up in proverbs and in the kind of advice
older speakers give. Nothing about them is stiff, but they carry a whiff of
life-lesson, which is exactly why they are used.

## Contrast

### ～うちに vs ～間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both put an event inside a window, but <code>間(あいだ)に</code>
only says *when*, while <code>うちに</code> adds *before it's too late*. If the window
closing does not matter, use <code>間(あいだ)に</code>.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)が買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)っている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、宅配便(たくはいびん)が届(とど)いた。
A delivery arrived while my mother was out shopping.
</pre>

Nothing here is racing the clock; the shopping trip is just a stretch of time that
happened to contain the delivery. Swap in <code>うちに</code> and the sentence starts to
imply the courier deliberately used her absence, which is not what happened.

<pre>
母(はは)が買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)っている<b>うちに</b>、ケーキを隠(かく)しておこう。
Let's hide the cake while Mum's out shopping.
</pre>

Now it fits: she is coming back, and that is the whole point.

The mirror-image restriction is also useful. <code>うちに</code> resists windows defined
by the clock or the calendar, because a clock window has no "state" to run out:
❌<code>三時(さんじ)から五時(ごじ)のうちに</code> →
✅<code>三時(さんじ)から五時(ごじ)の間(あいだ)に</code>.

### ～うちに vs ～前(まえ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)に</code> orders two events; <code>うちに</code>
judges an opportunity. <code>前(まえ)に</code> is neutral, and it looks forward at the
event; <code>うちに</code> looks around at the situation you are still standing in.

</div>

<pre>
年(とし)を取(と)る<b>前(まえ)に</b>、家(いえ)を建(た)てるつもりだ。
I intend to build a house before I get old.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>若(わか)いうちに</b>、家(いえ)を建(た)てるつもりだ。
I intend to build a house while I'm still young.
</pre>

The first is a plain schedule: house, then old age. The second says youth is what makes
it possible, and that the possibility is draining away. Note also that
<code>前(まえ)に</code> takes a dictionary-form verb naming the *event*, while
<code>うちに</code> takes the *state* that precedes it - which is why the two versions
above look nothing alike.

### ～のうちに vs ～中(ちゅう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に</code> is a deadline;
<code>今日(きょう)のうちに</code> is an opportunity. Same span, different argument.

</div>

<pre>
この書類(しょるい)は今日(きょう)<b>中(じゅう)に</b>出(だ)してください。
Please submit these documents by the end of today.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は忙(いそが)しいから、今日(きょう)<b>のうちに</b>出(だ)しておこう。
Tomorrow's going to be busy, so let's get it submitted while we still have today.
</pre>

<code>中(じゅう)に</code> states a limit somebody has imposed. <code>のうちに</code>
gives a reason, and the reason is always some version of "afterwards will be worse".
<code>中(ちゅう)に</code> also attaches only to nouns naming a measured span; you
cannot say ❌<code>元気(げんき)中(ちゅう)に</code> for
<code>元気(げんき)なうちに</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>学生(がくせい)うちに</b>留学(りゅうがく)したい。 → ✅ <b>学生(がくせい)のうちに</b>留学(りゅうがく)したい。
  <code>うち</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`. A な-adjective
  likewise needs な: ❌<code>元気(げんき)のうちに</code> →
  ✅<code>元気(げんき)なうちに</code>.

- ❌ 九時(くじ)から十時(じゅうじ)<b>のうちに</b>電話(でんわ)します。 → ✅ 九時(くじ)から十時(じゅうじ)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>電話(でんわ)します。
  A window fixed by the clock has no state to lose, so <code>うちに</code> has nothing
  to work with. Use <code>間(あいだ)に</code>.

- ❌ 本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)むうちに</b>眠(ねむ)くなった。 → ✅ 本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)んでいるうちに</b>眠(ねむ)くなった。
  An action verb has to be in ている. The dictionary form is reserved for verbs that
  already describe a standing situation, like いる or 住(す)む.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>は</b>寝(ね)ているうちに地震(じしん)があった。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>が</b>寝(ね)ているうちに地震(じしん)があった。
  The subject of a subordinate clause takes `が`. `は` cannot be trapped inside a clause
  that only modifies <code>うち</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 赤(あか)ちゃんが寝(ね)ている____、急(いそ)いで晩(ばん)ご飯(はん)を作(つく)ってしまおう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

赤(あか)ちゃんが寝(ね)ている<b>うちに</b>、急(いそ)いで晩(ばん)ご飯(はん)を作(つく)ってしまおう。

The baby is going to wake up, and that is exactly why the cooking has to happen now - 
the window is closing, which is <code>うちに</code>'s job. <code>間(あいだ)に</code>
would also be grammatical but would drop the urgency that いそいで and てしまおう are
already signalling.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 試験(しけん)は一時(いちじ)から三時(さんじ)までです。その（うちに・間(あいだ)に）一度(いちど)トイレに行(い)ってもいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

その<b>間(あいだ)に</b>一度(いちど)トイレに行(い)ってもいいです。

The window is defined by two clock times, not by a state that is about to expire, so
<code>うちに</code> has no opportunity to point at. This is the plain "at some point
inside the span" meaning, which belongs to <code>間(あいだ)に</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "While I was living in Osaka, I got used to the local accent."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大阪(おおさか)に住(す)んでいる<b>うちに</b>、関西弁(かんさいべん)に慣(な)れた。

This is the drift use: nobody decided to get used to the accent, it simply happened
over the course of the stay. The main clause is a change of state, which is the
signature of <code>～ているうちに</code>. 住(す)む also allows the dictionary form
here, since it names a lasting situation.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 暗(くら)いのうちに山(やま)を下(お)りたほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ <b>明(あか)るいうちに</b>山(やま)を下(お)りたほうがいい。

Two problems. An い-adjective attaches directly, with no `の` - 
<code>暗(くら)いうちに</code>, not ❌<code>暗(くら)いのうちに</code>. And the
meaning was inverted: <code>暗(くら)いうちに</code> would mean "while it is still
dark", i.e. set off before dawn. The state you want to use up is the daylight.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日本(にほん)にいる<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)りました。
(b) 日本(にほん)にいる<b>うちに</b>、富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral report: the climb took place somewhere inside the period of the stay.
It answers "when did you climb it?" and says nothing about why then.

(b) adds a motive. It frames the stay as a limited chance that was running out, so the
climb reads as something done deliberately before the opportunity disappeared - 
closer to "I made sure to climb Fuji while I was still in Japan." With a plain past
main clause (b) also sounds slightly more natural if you keep a marker of intent, such
as 登(のぼ)っておきました.

</details>

# ～ないうちに

**Meaning** · before something happens - said from inside the time when it still hasn't

English says "before it rains." Japanese takes the opposite angle and says "while it
is not-yet raining": <code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らないうちに</code>. There is no
natural English shape for that, which is why the pattern feels strange for a long time
and then suddenly feels obvious. The thing in the ない clause is nearly always
something you would rather avoid, and the main clause is what you do to get ahead of
it.

## Formation

Take the plain negative and put <code>うちに</code> straight after it. The negative
does the work; nothing else changes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form + うちに | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)らないうちに |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + うちに | 冷(さ)める → 冷(さ)めないうちに |
| Verb (する) | しないうちに | 変化(へんか)する → 変化(へんか)しないうちに |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)ないうちに | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないうちに |
| い-adjective (via なる) | ～く + ならないうちに | 暗(くら)い → 暗(くら)くならないうちに |
| な-adjective / Noun (via なる) | ～に + ならないうちに | 病気(びょうき) → 病気(びょうき)にならないうちに |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the labels used throughout this book; you will also
see them as 五段(ごだん)/Group 1 and 一段(いちだん)/Group 2. The irregular one to watch is
<code>来(く)る</code>, whose negative stem is <b>こ</b>: 来(こ)ないうちに, never
❌くないうちに.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - adjectives almost never attach directly. ❌<code>寒(さむ)くないうちに</code>
is not how this is said; route it through ならない - 
<code>寒(さむ)くならないうちに</code>. The negative has to name an **event that has
not started**, and adjectives name states, so なる supplies the missing event.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)らないうちに</b>帰(かえ)ろう。
Let's go home before it rains.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>忘(わす)れないうちに</b>メモしておきます。
I'll write it down before I forget.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>暗(くら)くならないうちに</b>洗濯物(せんたくもの)を取(と)り込(こ)んだ。
I brought the washing in before it got dark.
</pre>

<pre>
コーヒーが<b>冷(さ)めないうちに</b>飲(の)んでしまってください。
Do drink your coffee before it goes cold.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>知(し)らないうちに</b>財布(さいふ)がなくなっていた。
My wallet had vanished without my noticing.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)が<b>増(ふ)えないうちに</b>席(せき)を取(と)っておこう。
Let's grab seats before the place fills up.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が<b>戻(もど)ってこないうちに</b>、机(つくえ)の上(うえ)だけでも片付(かたづ)けておいたほうがいいよ。
You'd better at least clear your desk before the manager gets back.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもは、こちらが<b>気(き)づかないうちに</b>背(せ)が伸(の)びていて、去年(きょねん)買(か)った服(ふく)がもう入(はい)らない。
Children grow before you notice, and the clothes we bought last year already don't fit.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and common in both speech and writing. What is not free is the shape
of the main clause: it is almost always something the speaker **chooses to do in
response** - よう, ておく, てしまう, たほうがいい, てください, なさい, ないと. That is the
whole reason the pattern exists.

The ない clause is normally a change for the worse: rain, cold food, darkness, crowds,
your boss returning. This is a strong tendency rather than a hard rule, but if the
event is welcome the sentence starts to sound odd, and you probably wanted
<code>前(まえ)に</code>.

Three expressions have hardened into set phrases, and they behave like adverbs rather
than like clauses:

- <code>知(し)らないうちに</code> - without one's knowledge
- <code>気(き)づかないうちに</code> - without noticing
- <code>分(わ)からないうちに</code> - before one has worked out what is going on

In these the main clause is not an action you took but a change you discovered
afterwards, usually with ていた or てしまう:
知(し)らないうちに時間(じかん)が過(す)ぎていた.

In casual speech the whole thing survives intact - this is not a pattern that gets
contracted. What does happen is that the main clause loses its particles:
「雨(あめ)降(ふ)らないうちに帰(かえ)ろ」.

## Contrast

### ～ないうちに vs ～前(まえ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)に</code> puts two events in order and stops there.
<code>ないうちに</code> says the later event is a **problem you are outrunning**.

</div>

<pre>
出(で)かける<b>前(まえ)に</b>窓(まど)を閉(し)めた。
I shut the windows before going out.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が入(はい)って<b>こないうちに</b>窓(まど)を閉(し)めた。
I shut the windows before the rain could get in.
</pre>

The first is a checklist item. The second has a threat in it. Notice too that
<code>前(まえ)に</code> takes a **dictionary form** and <code>ないうちに</code> takes
a **negative**, even though the English translation is "before" both times - this is
the single most reliable source of errors with the pattern.

One more practical difference: <code>前(まえ)に</code> is happy with a scheduled,
neutral event (会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる前(まえ)に), while
<code>ないうちに</code> wants something that might land on you at any moment. You would
not normally say ❌<code>会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まらないうちに</code> for a meeting
that starts at ten sharp - unless you mean you are racing it.

### ～ないうちに vs the affirmative ～うちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same situation can be framed from either side. Affirmative
<code>うちに</code> names the **good state you still have**; <code>ないうちに</code>
names the **bad state that hasn't arrived**.

</div>

<pre>
<b>明(あか)るいうちに</b>洗濯物(せんたくもの)を干(ほ)してしまおう。
Let's get the washing hung out while it's still light.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>暗(くら)くならないうちに</b>洗濯物(せんたくもの)を干(ほ)してしまおう。
Let's get the washing hung out before it gets dark.
</pre>

These are interchangeable here, and either is natural. The choice matters when only one
side of the pair has a name. There is a comfortable word for the good state in
明(あか)るい, so both work; but for "before the shop closes" there is no adjective
for "still open in the sense that matters", so you reach for
<code>店(みせ)が閉(し)まらないうちに</code>. Conversely, 若(わか)いうちに has no
useful negative twin - ❌年(とし)を取(と)らないうちに is understandable but
distinctly odd.

### ～ないうちに vs ～ない間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ない間(あいだ)に</code> is a **stretch of non-happening**
with a start and an end. <code>ないうちに</code> is the run-up **before the thing
starts**.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)が見(み)ていない<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、猫(ねこ)が魚(さかな)を食(た)べてしまった。
The cat ate the fish while I wasn't looking.
</pre>

<pre>
猫(ねこ)が起(お)き<b>ないうちに</b>、魚(さかな)をしまっておこう。
Let's put the fish away before the cat wakes up.
</pre>

In the first, "not looking" is a period that has already begun and already ended - the
cat operated inside it. In the second, "waking up" has not happened at all yet, and the
sentence is about getting in first. A quick test: if you can put ずっと in front of the
negative clause, it is <code>ない間(あいだ)に</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)るないうちに</b>帰(かえ)ります。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)らないうちに</b>帰(かえ)ります。
  <code>うちに</code> attaches to the finished ない-form, not to the dictionary form
  plus ない. Build 降(ふ)らない first, then add うちに.

- ❌ <b>暗(くら)いうちに</b>帰(かえ)りましょう。（meaning "before it gets dark"） → ✅ <b>暗(くら)くならないうちに</b>帰(かえ)りましょう。
  <code>暗(くら)いうちに</code> is perfectly grammatical, but it means "while it is
  still dark" - the pre-dawn start, not the evening deadline. The two are opposites.

- ❌ <b>忘(わす)れないうちで</b>書(か)いておきます。 → ✅ <b>忘(わす)れないうちに</b>書(か)いておきます。
  The particle is fixed at `に`. <code>うちで</code> is not a variant of this pattern.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)<b>は</b>来(こ)ないうちに片付(かたづ)けよう。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)<b>が</b>来(こ)ないうちに片付(かたづ)けよう。
  The ない clause is embedded, so its subject takes `が`. And note the stem:
  <code>来(く)る</code> goes to 来(こ)ない, not ❌くない.

## Practice

**1.** ご飯(はん)が____うちに食(た)べてください。（冷(さ)める）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ご飯(はん)が<b>冷(さ)めない</b>うちに食(た)べてください。

English wants "before it gets cold", but Japanese frames the same moment as "while it
has not yet gone cold", so the verb goes into the plain negative. Using the affirmative
冷(さ)めるうちに would be ungrammatical here - there is no state of "being in the
middle of cooling" to sit inside.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)が（来(く)る前(まえ)に・来(こ)ないうちに）買(か)い物(もの)を済(す)ませておこう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are grammatical, but <b>来(こ)ないうちに</b> is the better fit.

<code>来(く)る前(まえ)に</code> would simply order the two events, as though the
typhoon were an appointment. <code>来(こ)ないうちに</code> treats it as a threat you
are outrunning, which is what ておこう and 済(す)ませて are already telling us. Note the
irregular negative stem: 来(こ)ない.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The line will get long, so let's buy the tickets before people find out."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

列(れつ)が長(なが)くなるから、みんなに<b>知(し)られないうちに</b>チケットを買(か)っておこう。

The negative passive 知(し)られない is what carries "before people find out" - the
event you are getting ahead of is other people learning about it. ておこう in the main
clause is typical: this pattern nearly always ends in something volitional.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 子(こ)どもが起(お)きるないうちに、しずかに出(で)かけた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 子(こ)どもが<b>起(お)きないうちに</b>、しずかに出(で)かけた。

起(お)きる is a る-verb, so its negative is 起(お)きない - drop る, add ない.
❌起(お)きるない stacks the dictionary form and ない together, which never happens in
Japanese.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 留守(るす)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、だれかが部屋(へや)に入(はい)ったようだ。
(b) 帰(かえ)って<b>こないうちに</b>、部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けておこう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) looks backwards at a window that has closed. The absence was a real stretch of
time, and the sentence places an event somewhere inside it - a neutral report of when
something happened.

(b) looks forwards from inside the window. Nobody has come back yet, and the speaker is
using the remaining time to get something done first. The clue is the pairing of a
negative clause with a volitional ending: that combination is almost always
<code>ないうちに</code>, not <code>間(あいだ)に</code>.

</details>

# 間 / 間に - while, during

**Meaning** · a window of time (<code>間(あいだ)</code>) - and `に` decides whether the main clause **fills** it or **sits inside** it

<code>間(あいだ)</code> is a noun meaning "the space between". Used with time it
marks off a stretch: while the meeting is on, while she was out, all through the summer.
The one thing you have to get right is the little `に`, and it is not decoration:

- <b>間(あいだ)</b> - the main clause is true for the **whole** window, start to end.
- <b>間(あいだ)に</b> - the main clause happens **at some point** inside the window.

## Formation

<code>間(あいだ)</code> is a noun, so whatever comes before it is in noun-modifying
shape. The `に` is then added or not according to the rule above.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ている) | ている + 間(あいだ)（に） | 寝(ね)る → 寝(ね)ている間(あいだ) |
| Verb (stative, dictionary) | Plain non-past + 間(あいだ)（に） | いる → 日本(にほん)にいる間(あいだ)に |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 間(あいだ)（に） | 見(み)る → 見(み)ていない間(あいだ)に |
| い-adjective | Plain form + 間(あいだ)（に） | 若(わか)い → 若(わか)い間(あいだ)に |
| な-adjective | な + 間(あいだ)（に） | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かな間(あいだ)に |
| Noun | の + 間(あいだ)（に） | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みの間(あいだ) |
| Bare time span | Span + の間(あいだ) | 三日(みっか) → 三日(みっか)の間(あいだ) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the subject inside the <code>間(あいだ)</code> clause takes `が`, or `の` in
more compact writing: 母(はは)が寝(ね)ている間(あいだ) ≒
母(はは)の寝(ね)ている間(あいだ). `は` cannot appear there.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not confuse this 間(あいだ) with the counter 間(かん):
一週間(いっしゅうかん), 二時間(にじかん), 三日間(みっかかん). Those are lengths
of time. The あいだ of this page is a *window* you locate something in. You can stack
them: 三日間(みっかかん)の間(あいだ), though 三日(みっか)の間(あいだ) is
tidier.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みの<b>間(あいだ)</b>、ずっと田舎(いなか)にいた。
I was in the countryside for the whole of the summer holiday.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)が寝(ね)ている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けた。
I tidied the room while my mother was asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)にいる<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、一度(いちど)北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)きたい。
I want to get to Hokkaido once while I'm in Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)の<b>間(あいだ)</b>、彼(かれ)は一言(ひとこと)も話(はな)さなかった。
He didn't say a single word for the whole lesson.
</pre>

<pre>
留守(るす)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>宅配便(たくはいびん)が届(とど)いたらしい。
It seems a parcel arrived while I was out.
</pre>

<pre>
信号(しんごう)を待(ま)っている<b>間(あいだ)</b>、弟(おとうと)はずっとスマホを見(み)ていた。
My brother stared at his phone the entire time we were waiting at the lights.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が席(せき)を外(はず)している<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、課長(かちょう)が三回(さんかい)も電話(でんわ)をくださったそうだ。
Apparently the section chief rang three times while I was away from my desk.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)をしている<b>間(あいだ)</b>、この道(みち)は車(くるま)が通(とお)れませんので、回(まわ)り道(みち)をお願(ねが)いします。
For as long as the roadworks are going on this street is closed to traffic, so please take a detour.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. <code>間(あいだ)</code> is at home in a train announcement, a
contract and a text message alike, and it does not change shape for politeness.

The useful stylistic observations are elsewhere. First, in relaxed speech ている
contracts as usual, so you hear
待(ま)ってる間(あいだ)に rather than 待(ま)っている間(あいだ)に. Second,
writing sometimes prefers `の` over `が` for the embedded subject
(父(ちち)の留守(るす)の間(あいだ)に), which reads as slightly compact and formal.

Two adverbial fossils are worth recognising. <code>間(あいだ)に</code> can appear with
no clause at all as そのあいだに, "meanwhile, in the meantime". And
<code>間(あいだ)じゅう</code> - 授業(じゅぎょう)の間(あいだ)じゅう - is an
emphatic version of the no-`に` reading: "the entire time, without a break". If you find
yourself wanting to add ずっと, <code>間(あいだ)じゅう</code> says it for you.

Finally, a piece of pronunciation. Written <code>間</code>, this word is あいだ here, かん in counters,
ま in 間(ま)に合(あ)う and 居間(いま), and けん in some measurements. Kanji alone
will not tell you which; the grammar around it will.

## Contrast

### 間(あいだ) vs 間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask what the main clause does with the window. If it **occupies**
the whole window, drop the `に`. If it **lands somewhere inside** it, keep the `に`.

</div>

<pre>
赤(あか)ちゃんが寝(ね)ている<b>間(あいだ)</b>、本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいました。
I read a book the whole time the baby was asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
赤(あか)ちゃんが寝(ね)ている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、本(ほん)を一冊(いっさつ)読(よ)んでしまいました。
I got through a whole book while the baby was asleep.
</pre>

Same nap, two different reports. In the first, reading and sleeping are the same length;
in the second, the book was finished at some moment before the baby woke.

That is why the two versions attract different main verbs. Without `に` you find
**durative** predicates - ている, ずっと, adjectives, negatives of ongoing action:

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)の<b>間(あいだ)</b>、天気(てんき)はずっとよかった。
The weather was good for the whole trip.
</pre>

With `に` you find **bounded** ones - a completed action, a sudden event, てしまう, a
counted quantity:

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>財布(さいふ)をなくしてしまった。
I lost my wallet at some point during the trip.
</pre>

Swap those and both sentences break. ❌旅行(りょこう)の間(あいだ)財布(さいふ)をなくした
would claim the losing went on for the length of the holiday.

<div class="note">

**Note** - one honest complication. When the main clause is durative but *shorter* than
the window, `に` comes back: 授業(じゅぎょう)の間(あいだ)に少(すこ)し寝(ね)た.
The test is not "is the verb punctual" but "does the main clause stretch across the
whole window". Punctual verbs simply always fail that test.

</div>

### 間(あいだ)に vs うちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間(あいだ)に</code> tells you **when**;
<code>うちに</code> tells you **when, and hurry**. Only <code>うちに</code> implies the
window is about to shut.

</div>

<pre>
冬休(ふゆやす)みの<b>間(あいだ)に</b>歯(は)を治(なお)した。
I got my teeth seen to over the winter break.
</pre>

<pre>
冬休(ふゆやす)みの<b>うちに</b>歯(は)を治(なお)しておこう。
Let's get my teeth seen to while we've still got the winter break.
</pre>

The second says the holiday is a chance that will not come again soon.
<code>うちに</code> pairs naturally with ておく, よう and たほうがいい for that reason,
while <code>間(あいだ)に</code> is content with a plain past.

The division of labour is clean in one direction: a window defined by clock or calendar
takes <code>間(あいだ)に</code> and refuses <code>うちに</code> - 
九時(くじ)から十時(じゅうじ)の間(あいだ)に, not
❌九時(くじ)から十時(じゅうじ)のうちに. A window defined by a state that will end
accepts both, with <code>うちに</code> adding the pressure.

### 間(あいだ) vs ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` requires **one person** doing both things at once.
<code>間(あいだ)</code> does not care who the subjects are, and is the only option
when they differ.

</div>

<pre>
姉(あね)が料理(りょうり)をしている<b>間(あいだ)</b>、私(わたし)は皿(さら)を洗(あら)っていた。
I washed dishes the whole time my sister was cooking.
</pre>

Two people, so `ながら` is impossible: ❌姉(あね)が料理(りょうり)をしながら私(わたし)は… is
simply ungrammatical. Where the subject *is* shared, both work, and
the difference becomes one of framing: `ながら` welds the two actions into one activity,
while <code>間(あいだ)</code> treats one as a container for the other.

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)っている<b>間(あいだ)</b>、ずっと音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)いていた。
I listened to music the whole time I was on the train.
</pre>

Here <code>間(あいだ)</code> is better than
電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)りながら, because riding a train is a *situation* you are in
rather than an *action* you perform - and `ながら` wants two actions.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 寝(ね)ている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、ずっといい夢(ゆめ)を見(み)ていた。 → ✅ 寝(ね)ている<b>間(あいだ)</b>、ずっといい夢(ゆめ)を見(み)ていた。
  ずっと…ていた fills the whole window, so the `に` has to go. `に` would ask you to
  locate the dreaming at one moment inside the sleep.

- ❌ 留守(るす)の<b>間(あいだ)</b>、泥棒(どろぼう)が入(はい)った。 → ✅ 留守(るす)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>泥棒(どろぼう)が入(はい)った。
  A break-in is a single moment, not a state lasting all day. A bounded event inside
  the window needs `に`.

- ❌ 夏休(なつやす)み<b>間(あいだ)</b>アルバイトをしました。 → ✅ 夏休(なつやす)み<b>の間(あいだ)</b>アルバイトをしました。
  <code>間(あいだ)</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`. Compare the
  counter 一週間(いっしゅうかん), which is a single word and takes nothing.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>は</b>旅行(りょこう)している間(あいだ)に、猫(ねこ)を友(とも)だちに預(あず)けた。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>が</b>旅行(りょこう)している間(あいだ)に、猫(ねこ)を友(とも)だちに預(あず)けた。
  The subject of the embedded clause takes `が`. A topic `は` cannot live inside a clause
  that is only modifying <code>間(あいだ)</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 会議(かいぎ)の____、一言(ひとこと)も発言(はつげん)しなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会議(かいぎ)の<b>間(あいだ)</b>、一言(ひとこと)も発言(はつげん)しなかった。

Saying nothing at all is a state that covers the entire meeting, so the window is
filled and no `に` appears. Adding `に` would try to place a moment of not-speaking
somewhere inside the meeting, which makes no sense.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 私(わたし)が買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)っている（間(あいだ)・間(あいだ)に）、犬(いぬ)がソファをかじってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)が買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)っている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、犬(いぬ)がソファをかじってしまった。

The chewing is a completed incident located somewhere inside the shopping trip, not
something that lasted exactly as long as it. てしまった is a reliable signal: a bounded
result takes `に`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please don't use your phone while the film is showing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

映画(えいが)を上映(じょうえい)している<b>間(あいだ)</b>は、携帯(けいたい)を使(つか)わないでください。

The prohibition holds for the whole screening, so no `に`. The extra `は` after
<code>間(あいだ)</code> is optional and very natural in notices and announcements: it
sets this window against the time before and after it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 兄(あに)は大学(だいがく)にいる間(あいだ)に、ずっと同(おな)じアパートに住(す)んでいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 兄(あに)は大学(だいがく)にいる<b>間(あいだ)</b>、ずっと同(おな)じアパートに住(す)んでいた。

Living in one flat covers the whole of his university years, so the `に` must be
dropped. The ずっと in the main clause is the giveaway - it is incompatible with
<code>間(あいだ)に</code>, which asks for a point rather than a span.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 二人(ふたり)が話(はな)している<b>間(あいだ)</b>、外(そと)で待(ま)っていました。
(b) 二人(ふたり)が話(はな)している<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、外(そと)に出(で)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) matches the waiting to the conversation exactly: the two ran in parallel, and the
speaker was outside for all of it. This is the no-`に` reading, and ていました reinforces
it.

(b) places a single act - stepping outside - at one moment inside the conversation. The
speaker may well have been in the room before and after. The verb is bounded rather than
durative, and that is what pulls the `に` in.

</details>

# 間 / 間に - between

**Meaning** · the gap between two things - physically, or between two parties, or across a group

Same word (<code>間(あいだ)</code>), same core idea of "the space in the middle", but
here the two edges are **things** rather than the start and end of a stretch of time.
Between the station and the bank. Between the two of them. Popular among students. The
grammar to get right is which particle follows: `に` for something *located* in the gap,
`で` for something *happening* across a group, `の` when you are modifying a noun.

## Formation

<code>間(あいだ)</code> is a noun. Two nouns joined by `と` form the two edges, and
`の` links the pair to <code>間(あいだ)</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two nouns (space) | AとBの間(あいだ) | 机(つくえ)・いす → 机(つくえ)といすの間(あいだ) |
| Location of something | …の間(あいだ)に + ある/いる | 木(き) → 木(き)と木(き)の間(あいだ)に道(みち)がある |
| Across a group | Noun (plural sense) + の間(あいだ)で | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)の間(あいだ)で |
| Between two parties | AとBの間(あいだ)で / に | 両国(りょうこく) → 両国(りょうこく)の間(あいだ)で |
| Two endpoints | AからBまでの間(あいだ) | 東京(とうきょう)・大阪(おおさか) → 東京(とうきょう)から大阪(おおさか)までの間(あいだ) |
| Modifying a noun | …の間(あいだ)の + Noun | 二人(ふたり) → 二人(ふたり)の間(あいだ)の約束(やくそく) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with two edges of the same kind you may repeat the noun instead of listing
two: 木(き)と木(き)の間(あいだ), 建物(たてもの)と建物(たてもの)の間(あいだ).
This is idiomatic and reads as "between one X and the next".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)と銀行(ぎんこう)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>花屋(はなや)がある。
There's a florist between the station and the bank.
</pre>

<pre>
ソファとかべの<b>間(あいだ)</b>から鍵(かぎ)が出(で)てきた。
The keys turned up from between the sofa and the wall.
</pre>

<pre>
その歌(うた)は若(わか)い人(ひと)たちの<b>間(あいだ)で</b>はやっているらしい。
That song is apparently a hit among young people.
</pre>

<pre>
九時(くじ)から五時(ごじ)までの<b>間(あいだ)に</b>電話(でんわ)をください。
Please call me between nine and five.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)と大阪(おおさか)の<b>間(あいだ)</b>を何度(なんど)も往復(おうふく)した。
I shuttled back and forth between Tokyo and Osaka more times than I can count.
</pre>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>何(なに)があったのか、だれも知(し)らない。
Nobody knows what went on between those two.
</pre>

<pre>
両国(りょうこく)の<b>間(あいだ)で</b>新(あたら)しい貿易(ぼうえき)の話(はな)し合(あ)いが始(はじ)まった。
Fresh trade talks have opened between the two countries.
</pre>

<pre>
建物(たてもの)と建物(たてもの)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>細(ほそ)い道(みち)があって、そこを通(とお)ると駅(えき)まで五分(ごふん)で着(つ)きます。
There's a narrow lane between the two buildings, and if you cut through it you reach the station in five minutes.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The physical sense is entirely everyday. The other two lean slightly formal.

<code>…の間(あいだ)で</code> meaning "among" belongs to news writing, market reports
and anything describing a trend:
中高生(ちゅうこうせい)の間(あいだ)で人気(にんき)が高(たか)まっている. In casual
speech people usually say <code>…の中(なか)で</code> or just name the group with `は`.
It is not stiff, but it does sound like something you read.

<code>二人(ふたり)の間(あいだ)</code> for a relationship is idiomatic and slightly
delicate. 二人(ふたり)の間(あいだ)に子(こ)どもが生(う)まれた is a neutral way
of saying a couple had a child; 二人(ふたり)の間(あいだ)に何(なに)かあった
gestures at trouble without naming it, and that discretion is exactly why the phrase
gets used. The related noun <code>間柄(あいだがら)</code> ("the relationship between
parties") is a step more formal again and belongs to writing.

A note on the spatial sense in speech: when the two edges are obvious, Japanese often
drops one and says <code>…の間(あいだ)</code> with a plural or a repeated noun rather
than spelling out A と B. 「本(ほん)の間(あいだ)にはさんでおいた」 - "I slipped it in
between the books" - needs no list.

Finally, this <code>間(あいだ)</code> is a full noun and can take any particle a noun
can: を (間(あいだ)を通(とお)る), から, まで, は. The time-window use on the
previous page is much more restricted.

## Contrast

### 間(あいだ) (between) vs 間(あいだ) (while)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what precedes it. **Two nouns joined by と**, or a
から…まで pair, gives you *between*. **A clause, or a single noun naming a period**,
gives you *while*.

</div>

<pre>
夏(なつ)と秋(あき)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>もう一度(いちど)海(うみ)へ行(い)きたい。
I want to get to the sea once more between summer and autumn.
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>もう一度(いちど)海(うみ)へ行(い)きたい。
I want to get to the sea once more during the summer.
</pre>

One `と` is the entire difference. The first locates the trip in the gap *between* two
seasons; the second locates it *inside* one of them.

The ambiguity is real but rarely a problem, because the two readings need different
material in front of them. Where it does bite is with から…まで:
<code>九時(くじ)から五時(ごじ)までの間(あいだ)に</code> is simultaneously "between
nine and five" and "during nine-to-five", and Japanese does not distinguish them - the
window *is* the gap.

### 間(あいだ)に vs 間(あいだ)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に` puts something **in** the gap; `で` makes the gap the **arena**
in which something goes on. Existence and placement take `に`; activity, exchange and
popularity take `で`.

</div>

<pre>
棚(たな)と壁(かべ)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>ほこりがたまっていた。
Dust had built up between the shelf and the wall.
</pre>

<pre>
社員(しゃいん)の<b>間(あいだ)で</b>その話(はなし)はもう広(ひろ)まっている。
Word of it has already got around among the staff.
</pre>

Both particles are possible with two named parties, and then they say different things:

<pre>
両親(りょうしん)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>問題(もんだい)がある。
There is a problem between my parents. (it exists, sitting there)
</pre>

<pre>
両親(りょうしん)の<b>間(あいだ)で</b>話(はな)し合(あ)いが続(つづ)いている。
Discussions are going on between my parents. (an activity they are conducting)
</pre>

Verbs of existence and appearance - ある, いる, できる, 生(う)まれる - take `に`.
Verbs of doing, spreading and agreeing take `で`. If in doubt, ask whether the thing
*sits* there or *happens* there.

### 間(あいだ) vs 中(なか)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間(あいだ)</code> is the gap **between** things;
<code>中(なか)</code> is the inside of a **container or a set**. Comparison and
superlatives always take <code>中(なか)</code>.

</div>

<pre>
クラスの<b>中(なか)で</b>彼女(かのじょ)が一番(いちばん)足(あし)が速(はや)い。
She's the fastest in the class.
</pre>

<pre>
クラスの<b>間(あいだ)で</b>そのうわさが広(ひろ)がった。
The rumour spread through the class.
</pre>

<code>中(なか)で</code> looks into a group and picks a member out;
<code>間(あいだ)で</code> looks at the space between the members and describes what
travels across it. That is why ❌三人(さんにん)の間(あいだ)で一番(いちばん)背(せ)が高(たか)い
is wrong: choosing one of three is a <code>中(なか)</code> job.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the <code>中</code> in this contrast is <b>なか</b>, the ordinary noun. It is a different
word from the suffix <code>中</code> read ちゅう/じゅう, which means "during" or "throughout" and
attaches with no `の`. 会議(かいぎ)の中(なか)で = "within the meeting's content";
会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう) = "the meeting is in progress".

</div>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 机(つくえ)とベッドの<b>間(あいだ)で</b>本棚(ほんだな)があります。 → ✅ 机(つくえ)とベッドの<b>間(あいだ)に</b>本棚(ほんだな)があります。
  ある and いる state that something is *located* somewhere, and location in a gap takes
  `に`. `で` would need an action to host.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>人気(にんき)があります。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)の<b>間(あいだ)で</b>人気(にんき)があります。
  Popularity, rumour, trends and discussions circulate **across** a group, and that is
  the `で` use. This one is worth memorising as a set:
  <code>…の間(あいだ)で人気(にんき)がある</code>.

- ❌ 四人(よにん)の間(あいだ)で一番(いちばん)若(わか)いのは彼(かれ)です。 → ✅ 四人(よにん)の<b>中(なか)</b>で一番(いちばん)若(わか)いのは彼(かれ)です。
  Picking the extreme member of a set is a <code>中(なか)</code> job.
  <code>間(あいだ)</code> would be the space between them, where nobody stands.

- ❌ 駅(えき)と郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)<b>間(あいだ)</b>に → ✅ 駅(えき)と郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)<b>の間(あいだ)</b>に
  The `の` linking the pair to <code>間(あいだ)</code> is not optional. Both edges go
  in front, joined by `と`, then `の`.

## Practice

**1.** コンビニと銀行(ぎんこう)の間(あいだ)____細(ほそ)い路地(ろじ)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

コンビニと銀行(ぎんこう)の間(あいだ)<b>に</b>細(ほそ)い路地(ろじ)があります。

あります reports that something exists in a place, and a place expressed as a gap takes
`に`. `で` would be looking for an action to locate, and existence is not an action.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新(あたら)しいアプリが高校生(こうこうせい)の（間(あいだ)で・中(なか)で）急(きゅう)に広(ひろ)まった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

高校生(こうこうせい)の<b>間(あいだ)で</b>急(きゅう)に広(ひろ)まった。

Spreading is movement *across* a group rather than selection *from* it, so
<code>間(あいだ)で</code> is the natural choice.
<code>中(なか)で</code> is not impossible here, but it invites you to look for a
member being singled out, which is not what 広(ひろ)まる does.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "A small park has been built between the two schools."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

二(ふた)つの学校(がっこう)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>小(ちい)さな公園(こうえん)ができました。

できる belongs with ある and 生(う)まれる - verbs of coming into existence - so the
gap takes `に`. You could also write
学校(がっこう)と学校(がっこう)の間(あいだ)に, repeating the noun, which is
idiomatic when the two edges are the same kind of thing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 五人(ごにん)の間(あいだ)で一番(いちばん)上手(じょうず)に歌(うた)えるのは妹(いもうと)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 五人(ごにん)の<b>中(なか)</b>で一番(いちばん)上手(じょうず)に歌(うた)えるのは妹(いもうと)です。

A superlative compares members inside a set, and the set is a container - that is
<code>中(なか)</code>. <code>間(あいだ)</code> names the space separating the five
people, which is not where the singing happens.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 二人(ふたり)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>約束(やくそく)があった。
(b) 二人(ふたり)の<b>間(あいだ)で</b>約束(やくそく)をした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the promise as something that simply existed between them - a standing
arrangement, perhaps unspoken, reported from outside. ある plus `に` gives it the
feel of a fact about their relationship.

(b) presents it as something the two of them actively did: they made the promise, with
each other, on some occasion. `で` supplies the arena for an action, so the sentence is
about an event rather than a state.

</details>

# ～中

**Meaning** · a suffix meaning "in the middle of" (read <b>ちゅう</b>) or "all through, all over" (read <b>じゅう</b>)

This is not the noun 中(なか). It is a suffix that glues straight onto the back of
another noun with no `の`, and it comes in two flavours that share one kanji:

- <b>ちゅう</b> - something is **in progress**, or a period is a **deadline** you must
  act inside. 会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう), 午前(ごぜん)中(ちゅう)に.
- <b>じゅう</b> - a span or a space is covered **from end to end**.
  一日(いちにち)中(じゅう), 世界(せかい)中(じゅう).

## Formation

Nothing conjugates; you are building a compound noun. What varies is the reading and
whether `に` follows.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Activity noun → in progress | Noun + 中(ちゅう) | 工事(こうじ) → 工事(こうじ)中(ちゅう) |
| Period → deadline | Noun + 中(ちゅう)に / 中(じゅう)に | 午前(ごぜん) → 午前(ごぜん)中(ちゅう)に |
| Period → whole extent | Noun + 中(じゅう) | 一年(いちねん) → 一年(いちねん)中(じゅう) |
| Place or body → whole extent | Noun + 中(じゅう) | 世界(せかい) → 世界(せかい)中(じゅう) |
| As a predicate | Noun + 中(ちゅう)だ／です | 使用(しよう) → 使用(しよう)中(ちゅう)です |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading is a property of the word, not something you derive. Learn these
in two lists.

<b>ちゅう</b>: 会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう), 食事(しょくじ)中(ちゅう),
授業(じゅぎょう)中(ちゅう), 電話(でんわ)中(ちゅう),
営業(えいぎょう)中(ちゅう), 使用(しよう)中(ちゅう),
工事(こうじ)中(ちゅう), 午前(ごぜん)中(ちゅう),
今月(こんげつ)中(ちゅう)に

<b>じゅう</b>: 一日(いちにち)中(じゅう), 一年(いちねん)中(じゅう),
今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に, 世界(せかい)中(じゅう),
町(まち)中(じゅう), 家(いえ)中(じゅう), 体(からだ)中(じゅう)

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
父(ちち)は今(いま)電話(でんわ)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>です。
My father is on the phone at the moment.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は一日(いちにち)<b>中(じゅう)</b>雨(あめ)だった。
It rained all day yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>はスマホをかばんにしまってください。
Please keep your phone in your bag during class.
</pre>

<pre>
この温泉(おんせん)は一年(いちねん)<b>中(じゅう)</b>営業(えいぎょう)しています。
This hot spring is open for business all year round.
</pre>

<pre>
そのニュースはあっという間(ま)に世界(せかい)<b>中(じゅう)</b>に広(ひろ)がった。
The news spread around the world in no time.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は体(からだ)<b>中(じゅう)</b>が痛(いた)くて、何(なに)もする気(き)が起(お)きなかった。
I ached all over yesterday and couldn't summon the will to do anything.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>のため、こちらの入(い)り口(ぐち)はお使(つか)いいただけません。
This entrance is out of use owing to construction work.
</pre>

<pre>
午前(ごぜん)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>は会議(かいぎ)が入(はい)っておりますので、午後(ごご)にもう一度(いちど)お電話(でんわ)いただけますか。
I'm in meetings all morning, so could I ask you to call again in the afternoon?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The suffix itself is neutral, but the words it builds cluster in particular places.
<code>中(ちゅう)</code> compounds are the language of **signs and status messages**:
営業(えいぎょう)中(ちゅう) on a shop door, 使用(しよう)中(ちゅう) on a toilet
lock, 準備(じゅんび)中(ちゅう) in a restaurant window,
会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう) on a colleague's chat status. Learning to read them is worth
more than learning to produce them.

<code>中(じゅう)</code> compounds lean the other way: they are emphatic and slightly
emotional, and they turn up in complaints and enthusiasm alike - 
部屋(へや)中(じゅう)ほこりだらけだ, 町(まち)中(じゅう)探(さが)した.
The nuance is "and I mean *everywhere*", so a flat, factual sentence often reads better
with 全部(ぜんぶ) or ずっと instead.

Two things about the particle. When <code>中(ちゅう)</code>/<code>中(じゅう)</code>
names the window an action must land in, `に` is required:
今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に送(おく)ります. When it merely describes the background,
`に` is absent, and `は` often appears instead:
授業(じゅぎょう)中(ちゅう)は… - this is the same window-filling logic as
<code>間(あいだ)</code> without `に`.

One reading to flag honestly: for 今週(こんしゅう) and 今月(こんげつ) both
readings circulate, and dictionaries generally give ちゅう - 
今月(こんげつ)中(ちゅう)に. For 今日(きょう) the reading is solidly じゅう. If
you hear a native speaker say it the other way round, they are not wrong; this is a
genuinely unsettled corner.

## Contrast

### 中(ちゅう) vs 中(じゅう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>中(ちゅう)</code> looks at a **point inside** a period:
something is going on, or must be finished, within it. <code>中(じゅう)</code> sweeps
the **whole extent**: nothing is left out.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)<b>中(じゅう)に</b>見積(みつ)もりをお送(おく)りします。
I'll send the quote before the day is out.
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は一日(いちにち)<b>中(じゅう)</b>寝(ね)ていた。
On Sunday I was asleep the whole day.
</pre>

Note that 今日(きょう)中(じゅう) takes じゅう even though it is a deadline - the
reading follows the *word*, not the meaning. The pattern is only a tendency.

Where a noun genuinely takes both, the meanings separate cleanly:
試験(しけん)中(ちゅう) is "the exam is under way", while
夏(なつ)中(じゅう) is "all summer long". You will not find a word that means both
at once.

### ～中(ちゅう)に vs ～のうちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>中(ちゅう)に</code> is a **limit**, usually one somebody
else set. <code>のうちに</code> is an **opportunity** you are choosing to use.

</div>

<pre>
レポートは今月(こんげつ)<b>中(ちゅう)に</b>提出(ていしゅつ)してください。
Please hand the report in by the end of this month.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)は出張(しゅっちょう)が多(おお)いから、今月(こんげつ)<b>のうちに</b>書(か)いてしまおう。
I've a lot of business trips next month, so let's get it written while we still have this month.
</pre>

The first is an instruction; the second is a plan with a reason attached. There is also
a hard structural difference: <code>中(ちゅう)</code> takes only nouns naming a
measured span, while <code>うちに</code> takes clauses, adjectives and states - 
元気(げんき)なうちに has no <code>中(ちゅう)</code> equivalent at all.

### ～中(ちゅう) vs ～の最中(さいちゅう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>中(ちゅう)</code> just says the activity is on.
<code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> says you are in the **thick** of it, and something has
cut across it.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>ですので、後(あと)でかけ直(なお)します。
I'm in a meeting, so I'll call back later.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>停電(ていでん)して、全部(ぜんぶ)やり直(なお)しになった。
The power went out in the middle of the meeting and we had to start over.
</pre>

<code>中(ちゅう)</code> is a neutral status label and works fine as a whole predicate.
<code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> is dramatic and expects an interruption to follow.
Note the different attachment too: <code>会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)</code> with no `の`,
but <code>会議(かいぎ)の最中(さいちゅう)</code> with one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)<b>の中(ちゅう)</b>に電話(でんわ)が来(き)ました。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>に電話(でんわ)が来(き)ました。
  This <code>中</code> is a suffix and takes no `の`. Writing
  会議(かいぎ)の中(なか)で changes the word to なか and means "within the content
  of the meeting".

- ❌ 一日(いちにち)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>歩(ある)きました。 → ✅ 一日(いちにち)<b>中(じゅう)</b>歩(ある)きました。
  "All day" is the whole-extent sense, which is じゅう. The kanji gives you no clue, so
  the reading has to be learned with the word.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)中(じゅう)<b>で</b>終(お)わらせます。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)中(じゅう)<b>に</b>終(お)わらせます。
  A window that an action must land inside takes `に`, exactly as with
  <code>間(あいだ)に</code>. `で` would be marking a place or a means.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)は勉強(べんきょう)中(ちゅう)<b>しています</b>。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)は勉強(べんきょう)<b>中(ちゅう)です</b>。
  <code>中(ちゅう)</code> makes a **noun**, so it ends in だ／です, not in a verb. Say
  either 勉強(べんきょう)中(ちゅう)です or 勉強(べんきょう)しています, never both.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、山田(やまだ)は今(いま)外出(がいしゅつ)____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)は今(いま)外出(がいしゅつ)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>です。

外出(がいしゅつ) is an activity currently under way, which is the ちゅう sense, and
the suffix attaches with no `の`. This is the standard way of telling a caller that
somebody is out of the office.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 夏(なつ)になると、この川(かわ)には（一日(いちにち)中(ちゅう)・一日(いちにち)中(じゅう)）子(こ)どもが集(あつ)まる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一日(いちにち)<b>中(じゅう)</b>子(こ)どもが集(あつ)まる。

The sentence covers the whole of the day from end to end, which is the じゅう sense, and
一日(いちにち)中(じゅう) is fixed in that reading. ❌一日(いちにち)中(ちゅう)
is not an alternative pronunciation of the same phrase - it is simply wrong.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Could you reply by the end of today? I'm out all morning tomorrow."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)<b>中(じゅう)に</b>お返事(へんじ)をいただけますか。明日(あした)の午前(ごぜん)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>は外(そと)に出(で)ておりますので。

Both halves use the suffix, with different readings and different jobs.
今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に is a deadline, so it takes `に`;
午前(ごぜん)中(ちゅう)は describes a window that the absence fills, so it takes `は`
and no `に`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この店(みせ)は準備(じゅんび)の中(ちゅう)なので、まだ入(はい)れません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この店(みせ)は<b>準備(じゅんび)中(ちゅう)</b>なので、まだ入(はい)れません。

The `の` has to go: this <code>中</code> welds directly onto the noun in front of it. With `の` the
kanji would be read なか and the phrase would mean "inside the preparations", which is
not a thing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 旅行(りょこう)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>に財布(さいふ)をなくした。
(b) 旅行(りょこう)の<b>間(あいだ)</b>ずっと天気(てんき)がよかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the suffix to name a period the trip occupied and drops a single event into it,
so it behaves exactly like <code>間(あいだ)に</code>: the wallet was lost at one moment
somewhere inside the trip. The `に` is what places the event.

(b) has no `に` because the good weather lasted the whole trip. You could equally write
旅行(りょこう)中(ちゅう)ずっと天気(てんき)がよかった - the suffix works there too,
and the absence of `に` is doing the same job in both patterns.

</details>

# ～最中に

**Meaning** · right in the thick of it - the peak of an activity, cut across by something else

<code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> is <code>中(ちゅう)</code> with the kanji for
"most" bolted on the front, and that is exactly what it adds: not merely *during*, but
*at the busiest, most involved point of*. Nine times out of ten the main clause is an
interruption - the phone rings, the power fails, somebody walks in. If nothing cuts
across, you probably wanted <code>中(ちゅう)</code> or
<code>間(あいだ)に</code>.

## Formation

<code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> is a noun, so what precedes it is in noun-modifying
shape. Unlike the suffix <code>中(ちゅう)</code>, it **requires** the `の` after a
noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Activity noun | Noun + の + 最中(さいちゅう)に | 試合(しあい) → 試合(しあい)の最中(さいちゅう)に |
| Verb | ている + 最中(さいちゅう)に | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)している最中(さいちゅう)に |
| As a predicate | …の最中(さいちゅう)だ／です | 準備(じゅんび) → 準備(じゅんび)の最中(さいちゅう)です |
| Emphatic | 真(ま)っ最中(さいちゅう) | 工事(こうじ) → 工事(こうじ)の真(ま)っ最中(さいちゅう)だ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun in front has to name an **activity with a middle**:
会議(かいぎ), 食事(しょくじ), 試合(しあい), 授業(じゅぎょう),
発表(はっぴょう), 引(ひ)っ越(こ)し. States and life stages are refused:
❌若(わか)い最中(さいちゅう), ❌留守(るす)の最中(さいちゅう).

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same two kanji, <code>最中</code>, also spell <b>もなか</b>, a wafer-and-bean-paste
sweet. Context separates them without difficulty, but the word does exist and you will
meet it in a confectionery shop.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
食事(しょくじ)の<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)った。
The phone rang right in the middle of dinner.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)の<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>おなかが鳴(な)ってしまった。
My stomach rumbled in the middle of class.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)の<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
It started raining partway through the match.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>居眠(いねむ)りして、部長(ぶちょう)ににらまれた。
I nodded off in the middle of the meeting and got a glare from the manager.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)が話(はな)している<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>口(くち)をはさむのはやめなさい。
Don't cut in while someone is speaking.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの準備(じゅんび)の<b>真(ま)っ最中(さいちゅう)</b>なので、散(ち)らかっていてすみません。
Sorry about the state of the place - I'm right in the middle of packing to move.
</pre>

<pre>
発表(はっぴょう)の<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>パソコンが固(かた)まってしまい、頭(あたま)が真(ま)っ白(しろ)になった。
My laptop froze in the middle of my presentation and my mind went completely blank.
</pre>

<pre>
夕(ゆう)ご飯(はん)を作(つく)っている<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>停電(ていでん)して、結局(けっきょく)カップラーメンで済(す)ませた。
The power went out while I was in the middle of making dinner, and in the end we made do with instant noodles.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself, and usable in both ます and plain speech. What marks it is
**tone**: <code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> is vivid. It dramatises the timing, and the
speaker is nearly always annoyed, embarrassed or startled. A neutral report of when
something happened does not want it - that is
<code>間(あいだ)に</code>'s job.

Because of that, the pattern is at home in complaints and anecdotes and comparatively
rare in formal writing, where <code>中(ちゅう)</code> or
<code>際(さい)に</code> would be used instead. A company notice says
工事(こうじ)中(ちゅう); a colleague telling you about their week says
工事(こうじ)の最中(さいちゅう)に.

<code>真(ま)っ最中(さいちゅう)</code> intensifies it further and is common as a
whole predicate: 今(いま)準備(じゅんび)の真(ま)っ最中(さいちゅう)です. Note
that the intensified form usually appears *without* `に`, describing the current state
rather than locating an event.

One structural habit worth internalising: the clause before
<code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> is almost always ている or a noun + `の`. A bare
dictionary form is wrong - ❌話(はな)す最中(さいちゅう)に - because the pattern
needs the activity to be visibly under way, and only ている says that.

## Contrast

### 最中(さいちゅう)に vs 間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間(あいだ)に</code> puts an event anywhere in the window,
including the quiet edges. <code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> puts it at the **peak**,
and implies it should not have happened there.

</div>

<pre>
映画(えいが)を見(み)ている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>二回(にかい)メールが来(き)た。
Two emails came in while I was watching the film.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)の一番(いちばん)いいところの<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>前(まえ)の人(ひと)が立(た)ち上(あ)がった。
The person in front stood up right at the best bit of the film.
</pre>

The first is a neutral count of when the mail arrived.
The second is a grievance. Note also that
<code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> resists a durative main clause:
❌映画(えいが)の最中(さいちゅう)にずっと寝(ね)ていた does not work, because a
peak is a point and cannot host something lasting. Use
<code>間(あいだ)</code>, with no `に`, for that.

### 最中(さいちゅう)に vs ～中(ちゅう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>中(ちゅう)</code> is a **label**: this activity is on.
<code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> is a **judgement**: this activity was at full tilt, and
look what happened.

</div>

<pre>
運転(うんてん)<b>中(ちゅう)</b>は電話(でんわ)に出(で)られません。
I can't take calls while driving.
</pre>

<pre>
運転(うんてん)している<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>タイヤがパンクした。
I had a blowout in the middle of driving.
</pre>

The forms differ too, and the difference is easy to get wrong:
<code>運転(うんてん)中(ちゅう)</code> glues on with no `の`, whereas
<code>運転(うんてん)の最中(さいちゅう)</code> demands one.
<code>中(ちゅう)</code> also happily takes `は` for a rule that holds throughout;
<code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> almost never does, because a peak is not a period you
lay rules over.

### 最中(さいちゅう)に vs ～ているところに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both host interruptions, but
<code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> emphasises **how deep into it** you were, while
<code>ところ</code> emphasises **which stage** you were at - about to, just doing, just
finished.

</div>

<pre>
ケーキを焼(や)いている<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>客(きゃく)が来(き)た。
Guests turned up while I was right in the middle of baking a cake.
</pre>

<pre>
ケーキを焼(や)こうとしている<b>ところに</b>客(きゃく)が来(き)た。
Guests turned up just as I was about to bake a cake.
</pre>

<code>ところ</code> can slide along the timeline - 焼(や)くところ,
焼(や)いているところ, 焼(や)いたところ - and each position means something
different. <code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> has only one position: the middle. That is
why it cannot be used for "just about to" or "just after".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>席(せき)を立(た)った。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)<b>の最中(さいちゅう)に</b>席(せき)を立(た)った。
  A noun in front needs `の`. It is the suffix <code>中(ちゅう)</code> that attaches
  bare - 会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう) - and mixing the two rules is the commonest slip.

- ❌ 勉強(べんきょう)<b>する最中(さいちゅう)に</b>眠(ねむ)くなった。 → ✅ 勉強(べんきょう)<b>している最中(さいちゅう)に</b>眠(ねむ)くなった。
  The activity has to be shown as under way, which means ている. A dictionary form points
  at the activity as a whole, not at its middle.

- ❌ <b>若(わか)い最中(さいちゅう)に</b>海外(かいがい)に住(す)みたい。 → ✅ <b>若(わか)いうちに</b>海外(かいがい)に住(す)みたい。
  Youth is a state, not an activity in progress, so it has no peak for
  <code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> to point at. States take <code>うちに</code>.

- ❌ 旅行(りょこう)の最中(さいちゅう)に<b>ずっと</b>天気(てんき)がよかった。 → ✅ 旅行(りょこう)の間(あいだ)<b>ずっと</b>天気(てんき)がよかった。
  <code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> wants a single intruding event. Something that
  lasted the whole trip belongs with <code>間(あいだ)</code> and no `に`.

# ～途中に / ～途中で

**Meaning** · partway along - on the way from A to B, or partway through something that has a beginning and an end

<code>途中(とちゅう)</code> is "the middle stretch of a route". Japanese uses the same
word for a physical journey (駅(えき)へ行(い)く途中(とちゅう)) and for any
process that runs from start to finish (話(はなし)の途中(とちゅう)), so "on the way"
and "partway through" are one word here. The choice between `で` and `に` is not free:
`で` for something that **happens**, `に` for something that **is there**.

## Formation

<code>途中(とちゅう)</code> is a noun. A verb in front is in plain non-past form - 
even when the whole sentence is in the past.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb of motion | Plain non-past + 途中(とちゅう)で／に | 帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)る途中(とちゅう)で |
| Other verb | ている + 途中(とちゅう)で | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)している途中(とちゅう)で |
| Noun | の + 途中(とちゅう)で／に | 会議(かいぎ) → 会議(かいぎ)の途中(とちゅう)で |
| Partial extent | 途中(とちゅう)まで／から | 読(よ)む → 途中(とちゅう)まで読(よ)んだ |
| As a predicate | …の途中(とちゅう)だ／です | 準備(じゅんび) → まだ準備(じゅんび)の途中(とちゅう)です |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the plain non-past in front is fixed:
帰(かえ)る途中(とちゅう)で雨(あめ)に降(ふ)られた, never
❌帰(かえ)った途中(とちゅう)で. The verb names the journey, and the journey has not
finished - that is the whole point of being partway along it.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the bare non-past belongs to verbs of **going**: 行(い)く,
来(く)る, 帰(かえ)る, 向(む)かう, 登(のぼ)る, 戻(もど)る. For other
activities use ている (読(よ)んでいる途中(とちゅう)で) or the noun form
(読書(どくしょ)の途中(とちゅう)で).

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
学校(がっこう)へ行(い)く<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>友(とも)だちに会(あ)った。
I ran into a friend on the way to school.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)へ行(い)く<b>途中(とちゅう)に</b>大(おお)きな公園(こうえん)がある。
There's a big park on the way to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
映画(えいが)の<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>寝(ね)てしまった。
I fell asleep partway through the film.
</pre>

<pre>
帰(かえ)る<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>雨(あめ)に降(ふ)られた。
I got caught in the rain on the way home.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の話(はなし)の<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>席(せき)を立(た)つのは失礼(しつれい)だ。
Getting up in the middle of what someone is saying is rude.
</pre>

<pre>
この本(ほん)はまだ<b>途中(とちゅう)まで</b>しか読(よ)んでいません。
I've only read part of this book so far.
</pre>

<pre>
空港(くうこう)へ向(む)かう<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>事故(じこ)に巻(ま)き込(こ)まれ、飛行機(ひこうき)に乗(の)り遅(おく)れてしまった。
I got caught up in an accident on the way to the airport and missed my flight.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)を登(のぼ)る<b>途中(とちゅう)に</b>小(ちい)さな茶屋(ちゃや)があって、そこで休(やす)んでから頂上(ちょうじょう)を目指(めざ)しました。
There's a little teahouse partway up the mountain; we rested there before pressing on for the summit.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and extremely common in everyday speech. There is no polite or plain
variant to learn; only the particle choice matters.

The journey sense is so ordinary that it has produced fixed compounds:
<code>帰(かえ)り道(みち)</code> for the way home (more idiomatic than
帰(かえ)る途中(とちゅう) when you mean the route rather than the timing), and
<code>通勤(つうきん)途中(とちゅう)</code>, <code>帰宅(きたく)途中(とちゅう)</code>
in news writing and insurance forms, where the noun attaches with no `の`.

<code>途中(とちゅう)まで</code> and <code>途中(とちゅう)から</code> are worth
learning as separate items. 「途中(とちゅう)まで一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)きましょう」
 - "let's walk part of the way together" - is a normal, friendly thing to say, and
「途中(とちゅう)から見(み)たので、よく分(わ)かりませんでした」 explains that you
joined a film or a meeting late.

Used as a predicate, <code>まだ途中(とちゅう)です</code> is a mild, useful hedge: "I'm
not finished yet." It sounds considerably softer than
まだ終(お)わっていません, because it presents the work as in motion rather than as
incomplete.

## Contrast

### 途中(とちゅう)で vs 途中(とちゅう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` for an **event** that occurs partway along; `に` for a **thing**
that sits partway along. If the verb is ある or いる, it is `に`.

</div>

<pre>
散歩(さんぽ)の<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>昔(むかし)の同僚(どうりょう)に会(あ)った。
I bumped into a former colleague partway through my walk.
</pre>

<pre>
散歩(さんぽ)コースの<b>途中(とちゅう)に</b>小(ちい)さな神社(じんじゃ)がある。
There's a small shrine partway along the walking route.
</pre>

The distinction mirrors the ordinary `で`/`に` split you already know: `で` marks where
an action takes place, `に` marks where something exists. It follows that the great
majority of <code>途中(とちゅう)</code> sentences take `で`, because most of them
report events. `に` shows up mainly with ある, いる, 立(た)っている and the like.

<div class="note">

**Note** - with a noun naming a process rather than a route, `で` is effectively the only
option: 会議(かいぎ)の途中(とちゅう)で, 説明(せつめい)の途中(とちゅう)で. A
meeting is not a road, so nothing can be standing beside it.

</div>

### 途中(とちゅう)で vs 最中(さいちゅう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>途中(とちゅう)で</code> measures **progress**: you had got
partway and had not finished. <code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> measures **intensity**:
you were at the busiest point.

</div>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)の<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>質問(しつもん)してもいいですか。
May I ask a question partway through your explanation?
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)の<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>質問(しつもん)されて、話(はなし)が止(と)まってしまった。
I was asked a question right in the middle of my explanation and lost my thread.
</pre>

The first is a polite request; the second is a complaint. Only
<code>途中(とちゅう)</code> works with a journey - 
駅(えき)へ行(い)く途中(とちゅう)で, never
❌駅(えき)へ行(い)く最中(さいちゅう)に - and only
<code>途中(とちゅう)</code> gives you まで and から, because only
<code>途中(とちゅう)</code> thinks of the activity as a line you can stop on.

### 途中(とちゅう)で vs 間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間(あいだ)に</code> needs a **window with two ends** and
places an event inside it. <code>途中(とちゅう)で</code> needs a **process with a
direction** and says how far along you were.

</div>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>三回(さんかい)質問(しつもん)した。
I asked three questions during the lesson.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)の<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>教室(きょうしつ)を出(で)て行(い)った。
He walked out of the classroom partway through the lesson.
</pre>

Both are grammatical here, and the difference is one of emphasis:
<code>間(あいだ)に</code> is neutral about position within the window, while
<code>途中(とちゅう)で</code> insists that the lesson was left unfinished. That is why
<code>途中(とちゅう)で</code> pairs so naturally with verbs of stopping, leaving and
giving up - やめる, 帰(かえ)る, 降(お)りる, あきらめる.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 家(いえ)に<b>帰(かえ)った途中(とちゅう)で</b>買(か)い物(もの)をした。 → ✅ 家(いえ)に<b>帰(かえ)る途中(とちゅう)で</b>買(か)い物(もの)をした。
  The verb stays in plain non-past however far in the past the sentence is. A past-tense
  verb would say the journey was already over, which contradicts
  <code>途中(とちゅう)</code>.

- ❌ 駅(えき)へ行(い)く途中(とちゅう)<b>で</b>コンビニがあります。 → ✅ 駅(えき)へ行(い)く途中(とちゅう)<b>に</b>コンビニがあります。
  あります states existence, and existence takes `に`. Use `で` only when something
  happens there.

- ❌ <b>会議(かいぎ)途中(とちゅう)で</b>抜(ぬ)けてもいいですか。 → ✅ <b>会議(かいぎ)の途中(とちゅう)で</b>抜(ぬ)けてもいいですか。
  An ordinary noun needs `の`. The bare-attachment compounds
  (通勤(つうきん)途中(とちゅう) and similar) are fixed vocabulary items, not a
  general rule.

- ❌ 本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)む途中(とちゅう)で</b>寝(ね)てしまった。 → ✅ 本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)んでいる途中(とちゅう)で</b>寝(ね)てしまった。
  Only verbs of going take the bare non-past. Anything else needs ている, or recast it as
  a noun: 読書(どくしょ)の途中(とちゅう)で.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)、駅(えき)へ<b>行(い)く途中(とちゅう)で</b>財布(さいふ)を落(お)としました。

Two things are wrong. The verb must be plain non-past, and it must be one that describes

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 駅(えき)へ行(い)く<b>途中(とちゅう)で</b>本屋(ほんや)に寄(よ)った。
(b) 駅(えき)へ行(い)く<b>途中(とちゅう)に</b>本屋(ほんや)がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports an action performed partway along the journey: you interrupted the walk to
drop into the shop. `で` is the particle of "where an action happens", and 寄(よ)った is
plainly an action.

(b) reports a fact about the route itself: a bookshop stands somewhere along it,
regardless of whether anyone goes in. `に` is the particle of location, and ある is the
verb that demands it. The two sentences describe the same street and answer completely
different questions.

</details>
