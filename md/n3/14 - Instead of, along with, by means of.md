# によって / による

**Meaning** · because of; by means of; by (the one who did it); varying with

One shape, four jobs. `によって` names the **cause** of an outcome, the **method**
that produced it, the **agent** of a passive verb, or the thing a result
**varies with**. Which of the four you are reading is decided by the verb at the
end of the sentence, not by `によって` itself.

## Formation

`によって` attaches to a noun and leaves the noun untouched. The variants in the
last three rows are the same word in different clothes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - cause | Noun + によって | 台風(たいふう) → 台風(たいふう)によって中止(ちゅうし)になった |
| Noun - method | Noun + によって | 話(はな)し合(あ)い → 話(はな)し合(あ)いによって解決(かいけつ)する |
| Noun - agent of a passive | Noun + によって + passive verb | ゴッホ → ゴッホによって描(えが)かれた |
| Noun - what a result varies with | Noun + によって + 違(ちが)う・異(こと)なる | 人(ひと) → 人(ひと)によって違(ちが)う |
| Before a noun | Noun + による + Noun | 地震(じしん) → 地震(じしん)による被害(ひがい) |
| Stiff written style, notices | Noun + により | 工事(こうじ) → 工事(こうじ)により通(とお)れません |
| "in some cases, depending on" | Noun + によっては | 場合(ばあい) → 場合(ばあい)によっては延期(えんき)します |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `による` is what `によって` turns into when it has to modify a noun:
事故(じこ)によって遅(おく)れる (before a verb) but 事故(じこ)による遅(おく)れ
(before a noun). There is also <code>によるもの</code> as a predicate:
今回(こんかい)の失敗(しっぱい)は準備(じゅんび)不足(ぶそく)によるものです。

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雪(ゆき)<b>によって</b>電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まりました。
The trains stopped because of the snow.
</pre>

<pre>
味(あじ)は店(みせ)<b>によって</b>違(ちが)います。
The taste varies from shop to shop.
</pre>

<pre>
この橋(はし)は百年(ひゃくねん)前(まえ)にフランスの技師(ぎし)<b>によって</b>造(つく)られました。
This bridge was built a hundred years ago by a French engineer.
</pre>

<pre>
集合(しゅうごう)時間(じかん)は話(はな)し合(あ)い<b>によって</b>決(き)めましょう。
Let's settle the meeting time by talking it over.
</pre>

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)<b>による</b>遅(おく)れで、到着(とうちゃく)が三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ほど遅(おそ)くなります。
Because of a delay caused by the heavy rain, we will arrive about thirty minutes late.
</pre>

<pre>
天候(てんこう)<b>によっては</b>、頂上(ちょうじょう)まで行(い)かずに引(ひ)き返(かえ)すこともあります。
Depending on the weather, we sometimes turn back before reaching the summit.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)<b>により</b>、東口(ひがしぐち)の階段(かいだん)は今月(こんげつ)いっぱいご利用(りよう)いただけません。
Owing to construction work, the east-exit stairs are unavailable for the rest of this month.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ出来事(できごと)でも、聞(き)く人(ひと)の立場(たちば)<b>によって</b>受(う)け取(と)り方(かた)がまるで変(か)わってくる。
Even one and the same event is taken quite differently depending on where the listener stands.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`によって` is a **written** connector. Reports, notices, textbooks and news copy
are full of it; ordinary conversation reaches for `で`, `から`, `ので` or
<code>～のため</code> instead. Saying 「バスが来(こ)なかったことによって遅(おく)れました」 to a
friend sounds like you are reading out a formal apology.

`により` is the stiffest of the family. It belongs to station announcements,
municipal notices and contracts, where it is often followed by a bare noun
predicate: 「安全(あんぜん)確認(かくにん)のため停車(ていしゃ)しております」-type language. In
polite speech it can stretch to `によりまして`.

`によっては` is not simply the topic-marked version of `によって`. It narrows the
sentence to *some* values of the noun: 「人(ひと)によって違(ちが)う」 says everyone is
different, while 「人(ひと)によっては怒(おこ)るかもしれない」 says that among the range of
people, some would be angry. It very often ends in `かもしれない`, `こともある` or
`場合がある`.

One member of the family is thoroughly colloquial: `による` used as an ordinary
predicate, meaning "it depends". 「行(い)くかどうかは天気(てんき)による。」 - 
"Whether we go depends on the weather." Shortened further to 「時(とき)と場合(ばあい)
によるね」 or a flat 「人(ひと)によるよ」, it is everyday spoken Japanese, and it is
worth learning separately from the formal uses above.

Finally, the passive-agent `によって` carries a whiff of the encyclopaedia. It is
the natural choice for creating, discovering, building and founding - 
発見(はっけん)される, 建(た)てられる, 書(か)かれる - and for institutional
actors. For ordinary "someone did something to me" passives, see the contrast
below.

## Contrast

### によって vs で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` is the everyday particle for cause and means. `によって`
is its formal counterpart, and it prefers **methods and procedures that produce a
result** over plain physical tools.

</div>

For an instrument you hold in your hand, `で` is not merely more casual - `によって`
is wrong:

<pre>
はし<b>で</b>食(た)べてもいいですか。
May I eat with chopsticks?
</pre>

But once the "means" is a procedure, a system or a decision-making method,
`によって` becomes available and sounds appropriately weighty:

<pre>
会長(かいちょう)は投票(とうひょう)<b>によって</b>選(えら)ばれます。
The chairperson is chosen by a vote.
</pre>

There is also a hard grammatical line: `で` cannot mark the **agent** of a passive.
❌<code>ピカソで描(えが)かれた絵(え)</code> is not a stylistic slip, it is broken.

### によって vs に (agent of a passive)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に` for someone doing something *to* you; `によって` for the
person or body that **produced** the thing. Inside a clause modifying a noun,
`によって` is usually the only option.

</div>

`に` keeps the subject in the picture as an affected party:

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は先生(せんせい)<b>に</b>ほめられて、一日(いちにち)中(じゅう)うれしそうだった。
My little brother was praised by the teacher and looked pleased all day.
</pre>

`によって` steps back and reports a fact of record:

<pre>
この寺(てら)は江戸(えど)時代(じだい)の職人(しょくにん)<b>によって</b>建(た)てられた。
This temple was built by craftsmen of the Edo period.
</pre>

Swap in `に` before a noun-modifying passive and the sentence goes strange:
❌<code>職人(しょくにん)に建(た)てられた寺(てら)</code> reads as though the temple suffered
something at the craftsmen's hands.

### によって（違(ちが)う） vs によっては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>Aによって違(ちが)う</code> says the whole set varies with A, so
there is no single answer. <code>Aによっては</code> picks out *some* values of A where
something special happens.

</div>

<pre>
開(あ)いている時間(じかん)は店(みせ)<b>によって</b>違(ちが)います。
Opening hours differ from shop to shop.
</pre>

<pre>
店(みせ)<b>によっては</b>、夜中(よなか)まで開(あ)いているところもあります。
Some shops stay open until the middle of the night.
</pre>

The second sentence makes no claim about shops in general - it only says that
somewhere in the range there are late-opening ones. That is why it pairs so
naturally with `～もある`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ボールペン<b>によって</b>書(か)いてください。 → ✅ ボールペン<b>で</b>書(か)いてください。
  A physical tool takes `で`. `によって` wants a method or a procedure, not an
  object you hold.

- ❌ 人(ひと)<b>によっては</b>考(かんが)え方(かた)が違(ちが)います。 → ✅ 人(ひと)<b>によって</b>考(かんが)え方(かた)が違(ちが)います。
  "Everyone thinks differently" is a claim about the whole set, so no `は`.
  With `は` you are saying "there are some people who…", which needs a different
  ending.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)<b>によって</b>遅(おく)れのため、会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)められません。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)<b>による</b>遅(おく)れのため、会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)められません。
  Directly in front of a noun, `によって` has to become `による`.

- ❌ この寺(てら)は有名(ゆうめい)な大工(だいく)<b>に</b>建(た)てられた建物(たてもの)です。 → ✅ この寺(てら)は有名(ゆうめい)な大工(だいく)<b>によって</b>建(た)てられた建物(たてもの)です。
  A passive that modifies a noun takes its agent with `によって`.

## Practice

**1.** 大雪(おおゆき)____試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大雪(おおゆき)<b>によって</b>（or <b>により</b>）試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になりました。

The snow is the cause of the cancellation, and 「中止(ちゅうし)になる」 is a reported
outcome rather than a chatty remark, so the formal cause marker fits. In
conversation you would more likely say 「大雪(おおゆき)で中止(ちゅうし)になった」.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この花瓶(かびん)は有名(ゆうめい)な作家(さっか)（に・によって）作(つく)られたものです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この花瓶(かびん)は有名(ゆうめい)な作家(さっか)<b>によって</b>作(つく)られたものです。

The artist is the *producer* of the vase, not someone doing something to an
affected subject, and the passive is modifying <code>もの</code>. Both facts point to
`によって`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Ticket prices vary depending on the season."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

チケットの値段(ねだん)は季節(きせつ)<b>によって</b>違(ちが)います。

<code>違(ちが)う</code> or <code>異(こと)なる</code> after `によって` is the fixed frame for
"varies with". Note that `によっては` would be wrong here: you are describing the
whole range of seasons, not singling out a few of them.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 地震(じしん)によって被害(ひがい)を調(しら)べる人(ひと)たちが町(まち)に入(はい)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 地震(じしん)<b>による</b>被害(ひがい)を調(しら)べる人(ひと)たちが町(まち)に入(はい)った。

<code>被害(ひがい)</code> is a noun, and `によって` cannot sit directly in front of one.
Left as it was, the sentence tries to attach 「地震(じしん)によって」 to
<code>調(しら)べる</code>, which would mean the earthquake did the investigating.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この町(まち)の宿(やど)は時期(じき)<b>によって</b>値段(ねだん)が違(ちが)います。
(b) この町(まち)の宿(やど)は時期(じき)<b>によっては</b>値段(ねだん)が倍(ばい)になります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a general statement about the whole year: whatever season you name, the
price attached to it is a different one. It is the answer to "is the price
always the same?"

(b) reaches into that range and pulls out the extreme cases - there exist
periods, not all of them, when the price doubles. The `は` is doing its usual
contrastive work, and that is why the sentence needs a striking predicate like
<code>倍(ばい)になる</code> rather than a flat <code>違(ちが)う</code>.

</details>

# にかわって / にかわり

**Meaning** · in place of; standing in for; taking over from

Read <code>にかわって</code>, and written ～に代(か)わって when kanji is used. Two
situations, one pattern: a **person** doing something that somebody else was
supposed to do, and a **thing** taking over the role another thing used to fill.

## Formation

The pattern attaches only to nouns. `にかわり` is the written form of exactly the
same thing.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person) - filling in | Noun + にかわって | 父(ちち) → 父(ちち)にかわって出席(しゅっせき)する |
| Noun (thing) - superseding | Noun + にかわって | ろうそく → ろうそくにかわって電気(でんき)が使(つか)われる |
| Written notices, reports | Noun + にかわり | 担当者(たんとうしゃ) → 担当者(たんとうしゃ)にかわりご案内(あんない)します |
| Formal spoken, ceremonies | Noun + にかわりまして | 社長(しゃちょう) → 社長(しゃちょう)にかわりましてお礼(れい)申(もう)し上(あ)げます |
| Before a noun | Noun + にかわる + Noun | 石油(せきゆ) → 石油(せきゆ)にかわるエネルギー |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kanji is <code>代</code> (substitute), not <code>変</code> (change). <code>変(か)わって</code>
is a different verb altogether and produces a different sentence; an IME will
happily offer you the wrong one. If you are unsure, kana is safe.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
父(ちち)<b>にかわって</b>私(わたし)が会(かい)に出(で)ます。
I'll attend the meeting in my father's place.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>にかわって</b>山口(やまぐち)さんが司会(しかい)をします。
Yamaguchi will chair the session in place of Tanaka.
</pre>

<pre>
けがをした選手(せんしゅ)<b>にかわって</b>、一年生(いちねんせい)がコートに入(はい)った。
A first-year went onto the court in place of the injured player.
</pre>

<pre>
留守(るす)の母(はは)<b>にかわり</b>、荷物(にもつ)を受(う)け取(と)っておきました。
I took delivery of the parcel on behalf of my mother, who was out.
</pre>

<pre>
紙(かみ)の辞書(じしょ)<b>にかわって</b>、スマホのアプリを使(つか)う学生(がくせい)が増(ふ)えている。
Students are increasingly using phone apps in place of paper dictionaries.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)では、昔(むかし)からの市場(いちば)<b>にかわって</b>大(おお)きなスーパーが買(か)い物(もの)の中心(ちゅうしん)になった。
In this town a big supermarket has taken over from the old market as the centre of shopping.
</pre>

<pre>
本日(ほんじつ)は部長(ぶちょう)<b>にかわりまして</b>、私(わたし)中村(なかむら)がご説明(せつめい)いたします。
Today I, Nakamura, will give the presentation in the department manager's place.
</pre>

<pre>
石油(せきゆ)<b>にかわる</b>エネルギーを探(さが)す研究(けんきゅう)が、世界中(せかいじゅう)で進(すす)められている。
Research into an energy source to replace oil is under way all over the world.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **formal, mostly written** Japanese. Between friends it sounds like a
press release: to ask someone to watch your bag while you step out, you want
<code>代(か)わりに</code>, not `にかわって`.

`にかわり` narrows the register further - notices, minutes, business email. And
`にかわりまして` is the ceremonial form you hear at weddings, funerals, opening
speeches and company presentations, where somebody speaks for an absent superior
or for a family. 「家族(かぞく)にかわりまして、ひとことごあいさつ申(もう)し上(あ)げます」 is
about as fixed as a phrase gets.

The "thing supersedes thing" use belongs to news and essay writing, and it
almost always ends in a verb of change: 増(ふ)える, 広(ひろ)がる,
使(つか)われるようになる, 中心(ちゅうしん)になる. A static predicate after it sounds
unfinished.

Note also the order. In <code>Aにかわって B</code>, **A is the one being replaced** and
B is the newcomer. Learners reverse this constantly, and because both halves are
plausible nouns the mistake produces a perfectly grammatical sentence that means
the opposite of what you intended.

## Contrast

### にかわって vs 代(か)わりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same substitution meaning, but `にかわって` is formal and
attaches only to nouns, while <code>代(か)わりに</code> is everyday, attaches to verbs
and adjectives too, and has a second sense - "in exchange for" - that
`にかわって` does not have at all.

</div>

<pre>
ちょっと席(せき)を外(はず)すので、私(わたし)の<b>代(か)わりに</b>電話(でんわ)に出(で)てもらえますか。
I'm stepping out for a moment - could you take any calls for me?
</pre>

An office favour asked with 「私(わたし)にかわって電話(でんわ)に出(で)てください」 is not
wrong, but it lands with the weight of an official handover.

The exchange sense is where the two genuinely part company:

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを手伝(てつだ)う<b>代(か)わりに</b>、ごはんをおごってね。
I'll help you move - you're buying dinner in return.
</pre>

Nothing here is standing in for anything, so `にかわって` is simply unavailable.

### にかわって vs として

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にかわって` says the person who should be doing this is
absent. `として` says you hold the role in your own right.

</div>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)<b>にかわって</b>報告(ほうこく)いたします。
I'll deliver the report in the section chief's place.
</pre>

<pre>
チームの代表(だいひょう)<b>として</b>報告(ほうこく)いたします。
I'll deliver the report as the team's representative.
</pre>

The first implies an empty chair; the second implies a title. Combining them is
possible and common in speeches - 「会社(かいしゃ)を代表(だいひょう)し、社長(しゃちょう)にかわりましてごあいさつ申(もう)し上(あ)げます」 - precisely because they say different
things.

### AにかわってB vs AがBに変(か)わる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two patterns put opposite things in front of `に`. In
`にかわって`, the noun before `に` is what gets **replaced**. In
<code>～に変(か)わる</code>, the noun before `に` is what something **turns into**.

</div>

<pre>
現金(げんきん)<b>にかわって</b>カードで払(はら)う人(ひと)が多(おお)くなった。
More and more people pay by card rather than cash.
</pre>

<pre>
信号(しんごう)が青(あお)<b>に変(か)わった</b>ので、車(くるま)が動(うご)き出(だ)した。
The light turned green, so the cars started moving.
</pre>

In the first, cash is on the way out. In the second, green is what has arrived.
Reading the second pattern into the first is the single most productive source of
back-to-front sentences with `にかわって`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 石炭(せきたん)が石油(せきゆ)<b>にかわって</b>使(つか)われるようになった。（meaning: oil replaced coal） → ✅ 石炭(せきたん)<b>にかわって</b>石油(せきゆ)が使(つか)われるようになった。
  The noun before `に` is the one being pushed out. As written, the first
  sentence says coal replaced oil.

- ❌ 買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)く<b>にかわって</b>、ネットで注文(ちゅうもん)した。 → ✅ 買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)く<b>代(か)わりに</b>、ネットで注文(ちゅうもん)した。
  `にかわって` takes a noun and nothing else. A verb needs
  <code>代(か)わりに</code>.

- ❌ 皿(さら)を洗(あら)ってあげる<b>にかわって</b>、アイスを買(か)って。 → ✅ 皿(さら)を洗(あら)ってあげる<b>代(か)わりに</b>、アイスを買(か)って。
  There is no "in exchange for" sense in `にかわって`, even with a noun in front
  of it.

- ❌ ねえ、私(わたし)<b>にかわって</b>ちょっと見(み)ててくれない? → ✅ ねえ、私(わたし)の<b>代(か)わりに</b>ちょっと見(み)ててくれない?
  The grammar is fine but the register is not. Casual speech does not reach for
  `にかわって`, and note that <code>代(か)わりに</code> needs `の` after a noun.

## Practice

**1.** 病気(びょうき)の先生(せんせい)____、校長(こうちょう)先生(せんせい)が話(はなし)をしてくださった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

病気(びょうき)の先生(せんせい)<b>にかわって</b>、校長(こうちょう)先生(せんせい)が話(はなし)をしてくださった。

The sick teacher was the one who should have spoken; the principal filled the
gap. Because the sentence is describing a school event in a slightly formal way,
`にかわって` fits better than <code>の代(か)わりに</code> would.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来週(らいしゅう)の説明会(せつめいかい)は、部長(ぶちょう)（にかわりまして・の代(か)わりに）私(わたし)がお話(はな)しいたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)<b>にかわりまして</b>私(わたし)がお話(はな)しいたします。

The rest of the sentence is in humble form (<code>お話(はな)しいたします</code>), so it
is being said to an audience, not to a colleague. `にかわりまして` matches that
level; <code>の代(か)わりに</code> would drop the register mid-sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Buses have taken over from trams in this city."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この市(し)では、路面(ろめん)電車(でんしゃ)<b>にかわって</b>バスが走(はし)るようになった。

Trams are the thing being replaced, so they go before `に`. The "thing
supersedes thing" use wants a verb of change at the end - here
<code>走(はし)るようになった</code>; <code>増(ふ)えた</code> would work too.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 兄(あに)にかわりに私(わたし)が荷物(にもつ)を取(と)りに行(い)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 兄(あに)<b>にかわって</b>私(わたし)が荷物(にもつ)を取(と)りに行(い)った。
✅ 兄(あに)の<b>代(か)わりに</b>私(わたし)が荷物(にもつ)を取(と)りに行(い)った。

Two patterns have been blended into one. `にかわって` carries its own `に` and
takes nothing else; <code>代(か)わりに</code> needs `の` after a noun. Since this is a
plain report about a brother, the second version is the one you would actually
say.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 母(はは)<b>にかわって</b>私(わたし)が挨拶(あいさつ)をしました。
(b) 母(はは)の<b>代(か)わりに</b>私(わたし)が挨拶(あいさつ)をしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say the mother did not speak and the child did, and both are correct
Japanese. The difference is entirely one of register and framing.

(a) sounds like a formal occasion - a ceremony, a funeral, a wedding - where
speaking for an absent family member is a recognised role. (b) is the plain
everyday report of what happened, the version you would give a friend
afterwards. In writing, (a); in conversation, (b).

</details>

# 代わりに

**Meaning** · instead of; in place of - and, in its other life, in return for

Read <code>代(か)わりに</code>. Two senses live in this one word and learners
routinely melt them together. **Substitution**: A was going to fill the slot, but
B fills it instead. **Compensation**: A and B are both true, and the second
balances out the first. Which one you get depends almost entirely on what the
word attaches to.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + の + 代(か)わりに | バター → バターの代(か)わりにオリーブオイル |
| Verb (plain, non-past) | Verb + 代(か)わりに | 行(い)く → 行(い)く代(か)わりに |
| い-adjective | い-adj + 代(か)わりに | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)い代(か)わりに |
| な-adjective | な-adj + な + 代(か)わりに | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かな代(か)わりに |
| Standalone adverb | …。代(か)わりに… | バスが来(こ)ない。代(か)わりに歩(ある)いた |
| Sentence connector | …。その代(か)わり(に)、… | 今日(きょう)は休(やす)む。その代(か)わり明日(あした)やる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the attachment decides the sense. An **adjective** in front can only
give the compensation reading, because an adjective is not something that can
fill a slot for something else. A **noun** or a **verb** in front leaves both
readings open, and context settles it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
さとうの<b>代(か)わりに</b>はちみつを入(い)れました。
I put honey in instead of sugar.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)の<b>代(か)わりに</b>私(わたし)が犬(いぬ)を散歩(さんぽ)に連(つ)れて行(い)った。
I took the dog for a walk instead of my sister.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが来(こ)なかったので、<b>代(か)わりに</b>歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)りました。
The bus never came, so I walked home instead.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い<b>代(か)わりに</b>、駅(えき)からかなり遠(とお)い。
This flat is cheap, but the trade-off is that it's a long way from the station.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを手伝(てつだ)う<b>代(か)わりに</b>、夜(よる)ごはんをごちそうしてね。
I'll help you move, but you're treating me to dinner in return.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)らせてください。その<b>代(か)わり</b>、明日(あした)は朝(あさ)早(はや)く来(き)ます。
Please let me leave early today - in return, I'll come in early tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>代(か)わりに</b>、近(ちか)くの温泉(おんせん)で三日間(みっかかん)ゆっくりすることにした。
Instead of going abroad, we decided to take three quiet days at a nearby hot spring.
</pre>

<pre>
この機械(きかい)は操作(そうさ)が簡単(かんたん)な<b>代(か)わりに</b>、細(こま)かい設定(せってい)がまったくできません。
This machine is easy to operate, but in exchange you get no fine control over the settings at all.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>代(か)わりに</code> is register-neutral. It works in conversation, in email and
in writing, and neither sense sounds childish or stiff. In casual writing it is
often left in kana as かわりに; in print, kanji. Watch the kanji itself - <code>代</code> is
"substitute", and <code>変</code> ("change") is a different word that an IME will offer you.

The standalone connector その代(か)わり is extremely common in speech and usually
loses its `に`: 「わかった。その代(か)わり、次(つぎ)は君(きみ)ね。」 It is the fastest way
to propose a deal in Japanese, and it can be said with a smile or with an edge
depending on tone alone.

The compensation sense is neutral about which side is the good one. 「安(やす)い代(か)わりに遠(とお)い」 and 「遠(とお)い代(か)わりに安(やす)い」 are both fine; the word
only asserts that the two facts come as a package. What it will not do is join two
facts that have nothing to do with each other - there must be a felt balance
between them.

When the exchange is a favour you are asking for, <code>代(か)わりに</code> makes the
bargain explicit, which is friendly among equals and blunt with a superior.
Softened forms - 「手伝(てつだ)いますので、その代(か)わりと言(い)っては何(なん)ですが…」 - 
exist precisely because the bare version is so direct.

For business and ceremonial situations, the formal counterpart of the
substitution sense is `にかわって` / `にかわりまして`, not this word.

## Contrast

### Substitution 代(か)わりに vs compensation 代(か)わりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the two things **compete for one slot** (only one of them
actually happens), it is substitution. If **both are true** and the second
offsets the first, it is compensation. An adjective in front forces the second
reading.

</div>

Substitution - the honey is in the cake, the sugar is not:

<pre>
牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)の<b>代(か)わりに</b>豆乳(とうにゅう)を使(つか)っても作(つく)れます。
You can make it with soy milk in place of cow's milk.
</pre>

Compensation - both halves happened:

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は少(すく)ない<b>代(か)わりに</b>、休(やす)みがきちんと取(と)れる。
The pay is low, but on the other side of it you can actually take your holidays.
</pre>

With a verb, context decides. 「私(わたし)が運転(うんてん)する代(か)わりに兄(あに)が寝(ね)ていた」 is substitution (one of us drove). 「私(わたし)が運転(うんてん)する代(か)わりに昼(ひる)ごはんをおごってね」 is compensation (I drive *and* you pay). The tell is
whether the second half is a **consequence for the other person** - if it is, you
are trading, not substituting.

### 代(か)わりに vs にかわって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same substitution meaning at a different altitude.
`にかわって` is formal, attaches only to nouns, and has no compensation sense.

</div>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に休(やす)んだ同僚(どうりょう)<b>にかわって</b>、受付(うけつけ)に立(た)つことになった。
I ended up on the front desk in place of a colleague who called in sick.
</pre>

Said to a friend that evening, the same fact comes out as 「同僚(どうりょう)の代(か)わりに受付(うけつけ)やらされた」. Neither is more correct; they belong to
different rooms.

### 代(か)わりに vs ～ないで・～ずに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English "instead of doing X" is `～ないで` when X is simply
**not done**. <code>代(か)わりに</code> is only right when X and Y are genuine
alternatives for one job, or when they trade off against each other.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはんを<b>食(た)べないで</b>学校(がっこう)へ行(い)きました。
I went to school without eating breakfast.
</pre>

❌<code>朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べる代(か)わりに学校(がっこう)へ行(い)きました</code> is odd
because going to school is not an alternative way of having breakfast. Give the
two things the same job and the pattern comes back:

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはんの<b>代(か)わりに</b>バナナを一本(いっぽん)食(た)べました。
I had a banana in place of breakfast.
</pre>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 母(はは)<b>代(か)わりに</b>私(わたし)が行(い)きます。 → ✅ 母(はは)の<b>代(か)わりに</b>私(わたし)が行(い)きます。
  A noun needs `の` in front of the pattern. Only verbs and adjectives attach
  bare.

- ❌ 安(やす)いの<b>代(か)わりに</b>、味(あじ)は普通(ふつう)です。 → ✅ 安(やす)い<b>代(か)わりに</b>、味(あじ)は普通(ふつう)です。
  The `の` belongs after nouns only. An い-adjective connects directly.

- ❌ テレビを見(み)る<b>代(か)わりに</b>早(はや)く寝(ね)ました。（meaning: I skipped TV and went to bed） → ✅ テレビを見(み)<b>ないで</b>早(はや)く寝(ね)ました。
  Going to bed is not a substitute way of watching television. Where nothing is
  standing in for anything, use `～ないで`.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は静(しず)か<b>代(か)わりに</b>、値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は静(しず)かな<b>代(か)わりに</b>、値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い。
  A な-adjective keeps its `な` before the pattern.

## Practice

**1.** 今日(きょう)は時間(じかん)がないので、メールの____電話(でんわ)で伝(つた)えます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

メールの<b>代(か)わりに</b>電話(でんわ)で伝(つた)えます。

Email and a phone call are two ways of doing the same job, and only one of them
will happen - that is the substitution sense. Note the `の`, which a noun always
requires here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この仕事(しごと)は大変(たいへん)（な代(か)わりに・の代(か)わりに）、勉強(べんきょう)になることが多(おお)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この仕事(しごと)は大変(たいへん)<b>な代(か)わりに</b>、勉強(べんきょう)になることが多(おお)い。

<code>大変(たいへん)</code> is a な-adjective, so it keeps `な`. The sense is
compensation: the job is hard *and* it teaches you a lot, and the sentence
presents those two as a package deal.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll look after your cat while you're away - in return, bring me back some sweets."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

留守(るす)の間(あいだ)ねこの世話(せわ)をしてあげる<b>代(か)わりに</b>、おみやげにお菓子(かし)を買(か)ってきてね。

Both halves are going to happen, and the second is your price for the first, so
this is the compensation sense. You could equally break it in two and use
その代(か)わり as a connector at the start of the second sentence.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 運動(うんどう)する代(か)わりに、テレビばかり見(み)ていました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 運動(うんどう)し<b>ないで</b>、テレビばかり見(み)ていました。

Watching television is not a way of getting exercise, so nothing is filling
anything else's slot. When the first activity is simply omitted, `～ないで` or
`～ずに` is the pattern. <code>代(か)わりに</code> would only work if the second
activity did the same job - for example
「ジムに行(い)く代(か)わりに家(いえ)で体操(たいそう)をした」.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 弟(おとうと)が皿(さら)を洗(あら)う<b>代(か)わりに</b>、私(わたし)がごみを出(だ)した。
(b) 弟(おとうと)が皿(さら)を洗(あら)う<b>代(か)わりに</b>、私(わたし)がごみを出(だ)してあげた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is most naturally read as substitution or as a straight division of labour:
he took the dishes, I took the rubbish, and between us the chores were done.

(b) tips into compensation because of <code>～てあげた</code>, which frames the rubbish
as a favour done *for him*. Now the sentence sounds like a deal: he washes up,
and in return I handle the bins on his behalf. The grammar is identical - the
benefactive verb is doing all the work.

</details>

# と共に

**Meaning** · together with; at the same moment as; as one thing goes, so goes the other

Read <code>と共(とも)に</code>, and just as often written in kana as とともに. It
starts as a formal replacement for <code>と一緒(いっしょ)に</code>, but it stretches much
further than that: it can join two events at a point in time, track two things
that move in step, and pin two properties onto one subject at once.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person, group) - accompaniment | Noun + と共(とも)に | 家族(かぞく) → 家族(かぞく)と共(とも)に暮(く)らす |
| Noun (event, point in time) - simultaneity | Noun + と共(とも)に | 卒業(そつぎょう) → 卒業(そつぎょう)と共(とも)に家(いえ)を出(で)る |
| Noun (something that rises or falls) - in step | Noun + と共(とも)に | 年齢(ねんれい) → 年齢(ねんれい)と共(とも)に増(ふ)える |
| Verb (dictionary form) | Verb + と共(とも)に | 進(すす)む → 研究(けんきゅう)が進(すす)むと共(とも)に |
| Noun / な-adj + である - "and also" | N + である + と共(とも)に | 医者(いしゃ) → 医者(いしゃ)であると共(とも)に作家(さっか)でもある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no い-adjective row, because い-adjectives do not attach here.
For "busy and also…" use <code>忙(いそが)しいうえに</code> or simply the て-form,
<code>忙(いそが)しくて</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
家族(かぞく)と<b>共(とも)に</b>新(あたら)しい町(まち)へ移(うつ)りました。
I moved to a new town together with my family.
</pre>

<pre>
年(とし)をとると<b>共(とも)に</b>、朝(あさ)起(お)きるのが早(はや)くなった。
As I've got older, I've started waking up earlier.
</pre>

<pre>
卒業(そつぎょう)と<b>共(とも)に</b>、三年間(さんねんかん)住(す)んだ寮(りょう)を出(で)ることになっている。
When I graduate I'm due to leave the dormitory I've lived in for three years.
</pre>

<pre>
気温(きおん)が上(あ)がると<b>共(とも)に</b>、アイスの売(う)り上(あ)げも伸(の)びた。
As the temperature rose, ice-cream sales climbed with it.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は農家(のうか)であると<b>共(とも)に</b>、村(むら)の小(ちい)さな学校(がっこう)の先生(せんせい)でもあった。
My grandfather was a farmer and at the same time a teacher at the village's little school.
</pre>

<pre>
スマホが広(ひろ)まると<b>共(とも)に</b>、紙(かみ)の地図(ちず)を持(も)ち歩(ある)く人(ひと)はほとんどいなくなった。
As smartphones spread, hardly anyone carries a paper map any more.
</pre>

<pre>
本日(ほんじつ)は皆様(みなさま)と<b>共(とも)に</b>この日(ひ)を祝(いわ)えることを、たいへんうれしく思(おも)います。
I am delighted to be able to mark this day together with all of you.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)が郊外(こうがい)へ移(うつ)ると<b>共(とも)に</b>、駅前(えきまえ)の商店街(しょうてんがい)も少(すこ)しずつ静(しず)かになっていった。
As the factory moved out to the suburbs, the shopping street by the station gradually grew quieter too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>と共(とも)に</code> belongs to **written and formal spoken** Japanese: essays,
reports, news copy, and speeches. In everyday conversation the accompaniment
sense sounds comically grand - 「友(とも)だちとともにラーメンを食(た)べた」 is the sort
of thing said as a joke. Use <code>と一緒(いっしょ)に</code> there.

The word <code>共(とも)に</code> also lives on its own as an adverb meaning "together",
and it turns up in set encouragements: 「共(とも)に頑張(がんば)りましょう」,
「共(とも)に歩(あゆ)む」. This is speech-making register, warm and slightly
old-fashioned, and it is one of the few places the pattern feels emotional rather
than neutral.

The <code>であると共(とも)に</code> use - "A and also B" - is firmly essay language. It
sets two roles or two qualities side by side and is usually followed by `～でもある` or `～でもあった`. In speech you would say 「医者(いしゃ)でもあり、作家(さっか)でもある」 instead.

One point of care with the "in step" sense: both halves have to be things that
can **move**. Growing older, prices rising, technology advancing, a season
turning - these work. A fixed fact does not: 「背(せ)が高(たか)いとともに」 is not
Japanese.

## Contrast

### と共(とも)に vs と一緒(いっしょ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for plain accompaniment they mean the same thing;
<code>と一緒(いっしょ)に</code> is spoken and neutral, <code>と共(とも)に</code> is written and
ceremonious. Only <code>と共(とも)に</code> can do simultaneity and change-in-step.

</div>

<pre>
昼休(ひるやす)みに同僚(どうりょう)と<b>一緒(いっしょ)に</b>弁当(べんとう)を食(た)べた。
I ate my packed lunch with a colleague at lunchtime.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の成長(せいちょう)と<b>共(とも)に</b>、社員(しゃいん)の数(かず)も三倍(さんばい)になった。
As the company grew, the number of employees tripled along with it.
</pre>

Substituting <code>と一緒(いっしょ)に</code> into the second sentence fails: growth is not
a companion you do things alongside.

### と共(とも)に vs につれて・にしたがって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `につれて` needs **gradual, continuous change on both sides**.
<code>と共(とも)に</code> also accepts a single moment or a one-off event.
`にしたがって` overlaps heavily with `につれて`, but it has a second, unrelated
everyday sense: "in accordance with (instructions)".

</div>

Gradual on both sides - all three are possible, and `につれて` is the most
idiomatic:

<pre>
試合(しあい)が進(すす)む<b>につれて</b>、応援(おうえん)の声(こえ)が大(おお)きくなっていった。
As the match went on, the cheering grew louder and louder.
</pre>

A single moment - only <code>と共(とも)に</code> works:

<pre>
開店(かいてん)と<b>共(とも)に</b>、客(きゃく)が次々(つぎつぎ)と店(みせ)に入(はい)ってきた。
The moment the shop opened, customers began pouring in.
</pre>

And the other `にしたがって`, which has nothing to do with gradual change at all:

<pre>
係員(かかりいん)の指示(しじ)<b>にしたがって</b>、外(そと)へ出(で)てください。
Please go outside in accordance with the staff's instructions.
</pre>

Be honest with yourself about the first group: in the gradual-change sense,
`につれて` and `にしたがって` are very close, and most sentences accept either.
`にしたがって` is a shade more formal and is the one you meet in written reports.

### と共(とも)に vs と同時(どうじ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> pins two things to the **same instant**.
<code>と共(とも)に</code> can do that too, but it also stretches over a period, which
<code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> does not.

</div>

<pre>
ベルが鳴(な)る<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、生徒(せいと)たちが廊下(ろうか)へ飛(と)び出(だ)した。
The instant the bell rang, the pupils shot out into the corridor.
</pre>

<pre>
時(とき)が経(た)つ<b>と共(とも)に</b>、あのときの悔(くや)しさもうすれてきた。
As time has passed, the bitterness of that day has faded.
</pre>

Put <code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> in the second sentence and it claims that time passing
and the feeling fading happened at one identifiable moment, which is not what
anyone means.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は友(とも)だちと<b>共(とも)に</b>カラオケに行(い)った。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は友(とも)だちと<b>一緒(いっしょ)に</b>カラオケに行(い)った。
  Not ungrammatical, but the register is three sizes too big for a night out.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いと<b>共(とも)に</b>、品物(しなもの)もいい。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>うえに</b>、品物(しなもの)もいい。
  い-adjectives do not attach to <code>と共(とも)に</code>. For "and on top of that",
  use `うえに` or `し`.

- ❌ 父(ちち)は教師(きょうし)と<b>共(とも)に</b>画家(がか)でもあった。 → ✅ 父(ちち)は教師(きょうし)<b>であると共(とも)に</b>画家(がか)でもあった。
  In the "A and also B" use, a noun needs `である` in front. Without it the
  sentence reads as "together with a teacher".

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ると<b>共(とも)に</b>、試合(しあい)をやめた。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた<b>ので</b>、試合(しあい)をやめた。
  <code>と共(とも)に</code> reports that two things move together; it is not a way of
  saying "because".

## Practice

**1.** 技術(ぎじゅつ)の進歩(しんぽ)____、新(あたら)しい問題(もんだい)も生(う)まれてきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

技術(ぎじゅつ)の進歩(しんぽ)<b>と共(とも)に</b>、新(あたら)しい問題(もんだい)も生(う)まれてきた。

Both sides are things that develop over time, and the register - an abstract
noun plus <code>生(う)まれてきた</code> - is written Japanese. <code>につれて</code> would also
fit here; <code>と一緒(いっしょ)に</code> would not.

</details>

**2.** Choose: ドアが開(ひら)く（とともに・につれて）、冷(つめ)たい風(かぜ)が入(はい)ってきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ドアが開(ひら)く<b>とともに</b>、冷(つめ)たい風(かぜ)が入(はい)ってきた。

Opening a door is a single event, not a gradual process, and `につれて` requires
change on both sides that unfolds by degrees. Where you can substitute "the
moment that", <code>とともに</code> is the pattern.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "As the population fell, the number of shops in the town fell as well."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人口(じんこう)が減(へ)る<b>と共(とも)に</b>、町(まち)の店(みせ)の数(かず)も減(へ)っていった。

Two quantities moving in the same direction over time - the core "in step" use.
The `も` in the second half is doing useful work: it marks the shops as
following the population rather than falling independently.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)は研究者(けんきゅうしゃ)とともに、優(すぐ)れた教師(きょうし)でもある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は研究者(けんきゅうしゃ)<b>であるとともに</b>、優(すぐ)れた教師(きょうし)でもある。

As written, the sentence says she is an excellent teacher *alongside a
researcher* - some other person. To stack two roles on one subject, the noun
takes `である` before the pattern, and the second half keeps `でもある`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 春(はる)が来(く)る<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、庭(にわ)の花(はな)が咲(さ)き始(はじ)めた。
(b) 春(はる)が来(く)る<b>と共(とも)に</b>、庭(にわ)の花(はな)が咲(さ)き始(はじ)めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) fixes a moment: the arrival of spring and the first flowers are presented as
coinciding, almost as if one triggered the other on a given day. It has a
slightly dramatic, "no sooner than" feel.

(b) is looser and warmer. Spring and the flowers are moving along together over
a stretch of weeks, with no claim about a single instant. For a seasonal
description this is the more natural of the two.

</details>

# を通じて / を通して

**Meaning** · by way of - through a go-between, a channel or an experience; or right through a stretch of time

Read <code>を通(つう)じて</code> and <code>を通(とお)して</code>. Same kanji, two readings, and
 - this is the honest part - very nearly the same meaning. Both put something in
the middle of you and your goal: a person who passes the message on, a medium
that carries it, an experience you came out the other side of. Both also mark a
period of time that a state holds right across.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (go-between) | Noun + を通(つう)じて／を通(とお)して | 友人(ゆうじん) → 友人(ゆうじん)を通(つう)じて知(し)り合(あ)う |
| Noun (channel, medium) | as above | テレビ → テレビを通(とお)して知(し)る |
| Noun (experience) | as above | 経験(けいけん) → 経験(けいけん)を通(とお)して学(まな)ぶ |
| Noun (period, range) - throughout | as above | 一年(いちねん) → 一年(いちねん)を通(つう)じて暖(あたた)かい |
| Before a noun | Noun + を通(つう)じての／を通(とお)しての + Noun | ネット → ネットを通(つう)じての申(もう)し込(こ)み |
| Physical passage (only 通(とお)して) | Noun + を通(とお)して | カーテン → カーテンを通(とお)して光(ひかり)が入(はい)る |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the last row is the one place the two really do split.
<code>を通(とお)して</code> keeps the literal sense of the verb <code>通(とお)す</code>, "to
pass something through", so light, sound and air can travel through things with
it. <code>を通(つう)じて</code> has lost that literal meaning entirely.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
友人(ゆうじん)を<b>通(つう)じて</b>彼(かれ)に連絡(れんらく)しました。
I got in touch with him through a friend.
</pre>

<pre>
この島(しま)は一年(いちねん)を<b>通(つう)じて</b>暖(あたた)かいです。
This island is warm all year round.
</pre>

<pre>
インターネットを<b>通(とお)して</b>、世界中(せかいじゅう)の人(ひと)と話(はな)せる時代(じだい)になった。
We've reached an age where you can talk to people all over the world via the internet.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)を<b>通(とお)して</b>学(まな)んだことのほうが、あとまで残(のこ)る。
What you learn through failure is what stays with you.
</pre>

<pre>
市(し)の窓口(まどぐち)を<b>通(つう)じて</b>申(もう)し込(こ)めば、費用(ひよう)が半分(はんぶん)で済(す)みます。
If you apply via the city office counter, it costs only half as much.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)の一年間(いちねんかん)を<b>通(とお)して</b>、自分(じぶん)の考(かんが)え方(かた)がずいぶん変(か)わった。
Over the whole year I spent studying abroad, my way of thinking changed a good deal.
</pre>

<pre>
通訳(つうやく)を<b>通(とお)して</b>話(はな)すと、どうしても細(こま)かい気持(きも)ちまでは伝(つた)わりにくい。
When you speak through an interpreter, the finer feelings inevitably fail to carry.
</pre>

<pre>
あの事件(じけん)のことは、新聞(しんぶん)やテレビの報道(ほうどう)を<b>通(つう)じて</b>知(し)った人(ひと)がほとんどだろう。
Most people will have learned of that incident through newspaper and television coverage.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Both forms are a step above casual speech. When the "channel" is an ordinary
everyday tool, Japanese uses plain `で`: 「メールで送(おく)ります」, not
「メールを通(つう)じて送(おく)ります」. Reach for these patterns when the point is that
something had to **pass through** an intermediary, or when you are writing.

The difference between the two is real but slight, and it is a matter of
tendency rather than rule. Where speakers do feel a difference:

- <code>を通(とお)して</code> leans towards the subject **actively using** the medium,
  and towards experience: <code>経験(けいけん)を通(とお)して</code>,
  <code>活動(かつどう)を通(とお)して</code>, <code>三年間(さんねんかん)を通(とお)して</code>. It is a
  little more at home in speech.
- <code>を通(つう)じて</code> leans towards **routing and connection**, and towards
  written and journalistic style: <code>マスコミを通(つう)じて</code>,
  <code>代理店(だいりてん)を通(つう)じて</code>, <code>一年(いちねん)を通(つう)じて</code>.

Say plainly what is true: in the great majority of sentences either is
acceptable, native speakers disagree about which sounds better, and no examiner
can build a fair question on the choice alone. The one place you can be
confident is the literal physical sense - light through a window, a voice through
a wall - which belongs to <code>を通(とお)して</code> only.

Before a noun, both need `の`: <code>友人(ゆうじん)を通(つう)じての紹介(しょうかい)</code>.
This form is stiff and mostly appears in notices and forms.

## Contrast

### を通(つう)じて vs を通(とお)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the go-between, channel and time-span senses, treat them
as interchangeable. Prefer <code>を通(とお)して</code> when the subject deliberately
works *through* something, and when the passage is physical.

</div>

<pre>
部活(ぶかつ)を<b>通(とお)して</b>、人(ひと)に頼(たよ)ることを覚(おぼ)えた。
Through club activities I learned how to rely on other people.
</pre>

<pre>
大使館(たいしかん)を<b>通(つう)じて</b>正式(せいしき)に申(もう)し入(い)れがあった。
A formal approach was made through the embassy.
</pre>

Each sentence would survive the swap. The first has a person putting themselves
through an experience, which suits <code>通(とお)して</code>; the second is a routed,
official message, which suits <code>通(つう)じて</code>. Those are leanings, not laws.

The genuine split:

<pre>
すりガラスを<b>通(とお)して</b>、人影(ひとかげ)がぼんやりと見(み)えた。
A figure showed dimly through the frosted glass.
</pre>

❌<code>すりガラスを通(つう)じて</code> does not work - nothing is being routed, something
is physically passing through.

### を通(とお)して vs で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` names the tool you used. These patterns name a
**path something took**, and they imply that the path mattered.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の件(けん)はメール<b>で</b>知(し)らせておきました。
I let them know about yesterday's business by email.
</pre>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)の考(かんが)えは、秘書(ひしょ)<b>を通(とお)して</b>部長(ぶちょう)に伝(つた)えられた。
The president's thinking was conveyed to the manager through his secretary.
</pre>

Replace `で` in the first sentence and it starts to sound as though the email
were a diplomatic channel. Replace <code>を通(とお)して</code> in the second with
`で` and the secretary becomes an instrument rather than a person passing a
message on.

### を通(つう)じて (a period) vs にわたって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with a stretch of time, <code>を通(つう)じて</code> says a state held
at **every point inside** it. `にわたって` says an **event spanned** it from one
end to the other.

</div>

<pre>
この地方(ちほう)は冬(ふゆ)を<b>通(つう)じて</b>雪(ゆき)が少(すく)ない。
This region gets little snow right through the winter.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)し合(あ)いは三日間(みっかかん)<b>にわたって</b>続(つづ)けられた。
The talks went on over three days.
</pre>

The first describes a condition you could check on any given day; the second
measures how far a single continuous event reached. A one-off event with
<code>を通(つう)じて</code> - ❌「会議(かいぎ)が三日(みっか)を通(つう)じて開(ひら)かれた」 - sounds
wrong for exactly that reason.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、電話(でんわ)を<b>通(つう)じて</b>母(はは)と話(はな)しました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)、電話(でんわ)<b>で</b>母(はは)と話(はな)しました。
  An ordinary tool takes `で`. The pattern would suggest the phone was an
  intermediary you had to go through to reach her.

- ❌ カーテンを<b>通(つう)じて</b>朝(あさ)の光(ひかり)が入(はい)ってきた。 → ✅ カーテンを<b>通(とお)して</b>朝(あさ)の光(ひかり)が入(はい)ってきた。
  Physical passage is <code>通(とお)して</code> only.

- ❌ ネットを通(つう)じて申(もう)し込(こ)みは来週(らいしゅう)開始(かいし)です。 → ✅ ネットを通(つう)じて<b>の</b>申(もう)し込(こ)みは来週(らいしゅう)開始(かいし)です。
  Directly in front of a noun, both forms need `の`.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)は二日(ふつか)を通(つう)じて行(おこな)われた。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)は二日間(ふつかかん)<b>にわたって</b>行(おこな)われた。
  A bounded event spanning a period wants `にわたって`. Save
  <code>を通(つう)じて</code> for a state that holds all the way through.

## Practice

**1.** この会社(かいしゃ)とは、知(し)り合(あ)いの弁護士(べんごし)____話(はな)し合(あ)いを進(すす)めている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

知(し)り合(あ)いの弁護士(べんごし)<b>を通(つう)じて</b>（or <b>を通(とお)して</b>）話(はな)し合(あ)いを進(すす)めている。

The lawyer is a go-between: the two sides are not talking directly. Either form
is fine, with <code>通(つう)じて</code> perhaps a shade more natural for a formal,
routed negotiation.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 障子(しょうじ)（を通(つう)じて・を通(とお)して）、外(そと)の明(あか)るさが感(かん)じられる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

障子(しょうじ)<b>を通(とお)して</b>、外(そと)の明(あか)るさが感(かん)じられる。

Light physically passing through paper screens is the literal sense of
<code>通(とお)す</code>, and <code>を通(つう)じて</code> has no literal sense left. This is one of
the few places where the choice is genuinely determined.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I learned a lot about myself through the volunteer work."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ボランティア活動(かつどう)<b>を通(とお)して</b>、自分(じぶん)について多(おお)くのことを学(まな)んだ。

Learning *through an experience* is the collocation <code>通(とお)して</code> is most at
home in. <code>を通(つう)じて</code> is not wrong, but essays and self-introductions
overwhelmingly use <code>通(とお)して</code> in this frame.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 工事(こうじ)は一週間(いっしゅうかん)を通(つう)じて行(おこな)われました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 工事(こうじ)は一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>にわたって</b>行(おこな)われました。

The construction is a single event measured from one end of the week to the
other, which is what `にわたって` does. <code>を通(つう)じて</code> would need a
continuing *state* - 「一週間(いっしゅうかん)を通(つう)じて天気(てんき)がよかった」 is fine.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) テレビ<b>で</b>その歌手(かしゅ)を知(し)った。
(b) テレビ<b>を通(とお)して</b>その歌手(かしゅ)を知(し)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the ordinary way to say it: the television was where you happened to
come across the singer. Plain, spoken, no comment on the process.

(b) makes the television a **medium** and quietly draws attention to it - you
know the singer only as television presented them, not in person. It is the
version you would write in an essay about how media shapes what we know, and in
casual conversation it sounds a little heavy.

</details>

# を中心に

**Meaning** · with X at the centre of it; centring on X; mainly X

Read <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code>. The noun before it is the **core**, and everything
else in the sentence is arranged around that core - physically, as a circle on a
map; or in terms of weight, as the part of an activity that gets most of the
attention.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + を中心(ちゅうしん)に | 駅(えき) → 駅(えき)を中心(ちゅうしん)に広(ひろ)がる |
| Noun (stiffer, written) | Noun + を中心(ちゅうしん)として | 若者(わかもの) → 若者(わかもの)を中心(ちゅうしん)として広(ひろ)まる |
| Noun (deliberate arrangement) | Noun + を中心(ちゅうしん)にして | 文法(ぶんぽう) → 文法(ぶんぽう)を中心(ちゅうしん)にして復習(ふくしゅう)する |
| Before a noun | Noun + を中心(ちゅうしん)とする／とした + Noun | 大学(だいがく) → 大学(だいがく)を中心(ちゅうしん)とする研究(けんきゅう)チーム |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> modifies a **verb**. To modify a noun you must
switch to <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)とする</code> or <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)とした</code>; the two are
interchangeable, and the `とした` form is slightly more common in advertising and
headlines.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>店(みせ)が集(あつ)まっています。
The shops are clustered around the station.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は漢字(かんじ)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>練習(れんしゅう)しましょう。
Let's make kanji the focus of today's practice.
</pre>

<pre>
その歌(うた)は中学生(ちゅうがくせい)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>人気(にんき)が広(ひろ)がった。
The song caught on above all among junior-high students.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)は九州(きゅうしゅう)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>大(おお)きな被害(ひがい)をもたらした。
The typhoon did serious damage, Kyushu worst of all.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は、会話(かいわ)でよく使(つか)う言葉(ことば)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)として</b>集(あつ)められている。
This dictionary is built around the words that come up most in conversation.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)では、来年度(らいねんど)の予算(よさん)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>話(はな)し合(あ)う予定(よてい)です。
At next week's meeting we plan to centre the discussion on next year's budget.
</pre>

<pre>
市役所(しやくしょ)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)とする</b>半径(はんけい)一(いち)キロの中(なか)に、病院(びょういん)が三(みっ)つもある。
There are no fewer than three hospitals within a one-kilometre radius of the city hall.
</pre>

<pre>
この地域(ちいき)では、昔(むかし)から米(こめ)づくりを<b>中心(ちゅうしん)にして</b>人(ひと)びとの暮(く)らしが続(つづ)いてきた。
In this area life has long been organised around growing rice.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is at home in **written and semi-formal** Japanese - reports,
weather bulletins, class plans, meeting agendas - but unlike many N3 noun
patterns it is not stiff. 「今日(きょう)は文法(ぶんぽう)を中心(ちゅうしん)にやります」 is a
perfectly ordinary thing for a teacher to say out loud.

The three variants shade upward in formality: <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> is the plain
one, <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)にして</code> puts a little more weight on the arranging being
deliberate, and <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)として</code> is the essay-and-report form. Nothing
turns on the choice; use the first unless you are writing.

<code>中心(ちゅうしん)</code> is still an ordinary noun meaning "centre", so the literal
reading never quite goes away. That is a feature: 「東京(とうきょう)を中心(ちゅうしん)に雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る」 genuinely pictures rain spreading outward from a
point on the map, and weather forecasts use it exactly that way. When the noun is
a group of people rather than a place, the picture becomes one of concentration - 
the thing is happening most intensely there and thinning out at the edges.

One consequence worth noticing: the pattern implies that **other things are also
included**, just less prominently. If a course covers grammar and nothing else,
「文法(ぶんぽう)<b>だけ</b>」 is the honest wording; <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> promises a
core plus a periphery.

## Contrast

### を中心(ちゅうしん)に vs 主(おも)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>主(おも)に</code> is a plain adverb meaning "mostly".
<code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> takes the thing as its object and adds the picture of a
core with other material arranged around it.

</div>

<pre>
この授業(じゅぎょう)では<b>主(おも)に</b>話(はな)す練習(れんしゅう)をします。
In this class we mostly do speaking practice.
</pre>

<pre>
この授業(じゅぎょう)では話(はな)す練習(れんしゅう)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>、聞(き)き取(と)りもやります。
This class centres on speaking practice, with listening as well.
</pre>

The second sentence needs the rest of the field to exist; the first does not
care. That is why <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> so often has a `も` clause after it.

### を中心(ちゅうしん)に vs をはじめ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> ranks by **weight**: X is the biggest
part. `をはじめ` ranks by **order of mention**: X is the first and most
representative member of a group, and the group is then named.

</div>

<pre>
関東(かんとう)地方(ちほう)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>、朝(あさ)から強(つよ)い風(かぜ)が吹(ふ)いている。
Strong winds have been blowing since morning, centred on the Kanto region.
</pre>

<pre>
関東(かんとう)地方(ちほう)を<b>はじめ</b>、全国(ぜんこく)の多(おお)くの地域(ちいき)で風(かぜ)が強(つよ)まっている。
Winds are strengthening in many regions across the country, Kanto among the first of them.
</pre>

The first sentence maps intensity; the second opens a list. Swapping them
produces sentences that are grammatical and slightly beside the point.

### を中心(ちゅうしん)に vs をめぐって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `をめぐって` means the thing is the **point at issue**, and it
usually brings disagreement with it. <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> is neutral about
whether anyone is arguing.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)を<b>めぐって</b>、社内(しゃない)で意見(いけん)が分(わ)かれている。
Opinion inside the company is split over the new rules.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>、一時間(いちじかん)ほど説明(せつめい)があった。
There was about an hour of explanation, focused on the new rules.
</pre>

`をめぐって` implies parties pulling in different directions; if all you mean is
that a topic took up most of the time, it is the wrong word.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 文法(ぶんぽう)<b>に</b>中心(ちゅうしん)に勉強(べんきょう)します。 → ✅ 文法(ぶんぽう)<b>を</b>中心(ちゅうしん)に勉強(べんきょう)します。
  The particle is `を`. The core is treated as something you *take* as the
  centre, not somewhere you put yourself.

- ❌ この会(かい)は大学生(だいがくせい)を中心(ちゅうしん)に集(あつ)まりです。 → ✅ この会(かい)は大学生(だいがくせい)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)とした</b>集(あつ)まりです。
  <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> needs a verb to modify. In front of a noun the
  pattern must become <code>中心(ちゅうしん)とした</code> or
  <code>中心(ちゅうしん)とする</code>.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)を中心(ちゅうしん)に、両国(りょうこく)が激(はげ)しく対立(たいりつ)している。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)を<b>めぐって</b>、両国(りょうこく)が激(はげ)しく対立(たいりつ)している。
  A dispute is fought *over* something, not *around* it.

- ❌ 野菜(やさい)を中心(ちゅうしん)に食(た)べています。肉(にく)は一切(いっさい)食(た)べません。 → ✅ 野菜(やさい)<b>だけ</b>食(た)べています。
  <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> promises that other things are in the picture too. If
  there is no periphery, the pattern contradicts the next sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 雨(あめ)は明日(あす)の朝(あさ)、西日本(にしにほん)____強(つよ)くなるでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

西日本(にしにほん)<b>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>強(つよ)くなるでしょう。

Weather forecasts use this pattern constantly: the rain will be heaviest in
western Japan and lighter elsewhere. The literal "centre on a map" picture is
exactly what is wanted.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会議(かいぎ)では、来年(らいねん)の計画(けいかく)（を中心(ちゅうしん)に・をめぐって）説明(せつめい)が行(おこな)われた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来年(らいねん)の計画(けいかく)<b>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>説明(せつめい)が行(おこな)われた。

Nobody is arguing - someone is explaining. `をめぐって` would suggest the plan
was contested, which does not fit <code>説明(せつめい)</code>. Save it for
<code>議論(ぎろん)</code>, <code>対立(たいりつ)</code> and the like.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's a small company built around three engineers."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三人(さんにん)の技術者(ぎじゅつしゃ)<b>を中心(ちゅうしん)とする</b>小(ちい)さな会社(かいしゃ)です。

<code>会社(かいしゃ)</code> is a noun, so the pattern has to take its noun-modifying
shape. <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)とした</code> would be equally good here.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: このイベントは子(こ)どもに中心(ちゅうしん)にして計画(けいかく)されました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ このイベントは子(こ)ども<b>を中心(ちゅうしん)にして</b>計画(けいかく)されました。

The particle must be `を`. The `に` in the pattern is already supplied by
<code>中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> itself, and doubling it is a common slip because so many
neighbouring N3 patterns begin with `に`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)の調査(ちょうさ)は若者(わかもの)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>行(おこな)われた。
(b) 今回(こんかい)の調査(ちょうさ)は<b>主(おも)に</b>若者(わかもの)に対(たい)して行(おこな)われた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say that most of the people surveyed were young. (a) adds shape: young
people form the core of the sample and other age groups sit around the edges,
so the reader expects to hear about those edges later.

(b) simply reports a proportion. It makes no promise that anyone else was
included and no claim about how the sample was designed - it is the flatter,
more purely statistical of the two.

</details>

# をはじめ / をはじめとする

**Meaning** · starting with X - X being the leading member of a group, with the rest following

Written ～を始(はじ)め in kanji, though kana is now more usual. The pattern names
one item as the **most prominent or most representative** of a set, and then goes
on to talk about the set as a whole. It is a listing device, not a substitute for
"for example": what follows it has to be the group X belongs to.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + をはじめ、… | 京都(きょうと) → 京都(きょうと)をはじめ、各地(かくち)から人(ひと)が集(あつ)まる |
| Noun (formal, written) | Noun + をはじめとして、… | 校長(こうちょう) → 校長(こうちょう)をはじめとして、先生方(せんせいがた)が並(なら)んだ |
| Before a noun | Noun + をはじめとする + Noun | 東京(とうきょう) → 東京(とうきょう)をはじめとする大都市(だいとし) |
| Before a noun (alternative) | Noun + をはじめとした + Noun | パンダ → パンダをはじめとした動物(どうぶつ)たち |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the clause after the pattern must state the wider group, usually with
a quantity word: 全員(ぜんいん), 多(おお)くの～, さまざまな～, ～など. A
sentence that names the leading item and then never mentions the set feels cut
off halfway.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)<b>をはじめ</b>、社員(しゃいん)全員(ぜんいん)が式(しき)に出(で)ました。
The whole staff attended the ceremony, the president included.
</pre>

<pre>
パンダ<b>をはじめ</b>、この動物園(どうぶつえん)には珍(めずら)しい動物(どうぶつ)が多(おお)い。
Starting with the pandas, this zoo has a lot of unusual animals.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)では、東京(とうきょう)<b>をはじめとする</b>大都市(だいとし)に人口(じんこう)が集(あつ)まっている。
In Japan the population is concentrated in the big cities, Tokyo foremost among them.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)では、米(こめ)や味噌(みそ)<b>をはじめとする</b>地元(じもと)の品(しな)を売(う)っている。
This shop sells local produce - rice and miso and much else besides.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)先生(せんせい)<b>をはじめとして</b>、お世話(せわ)になった方々(かたがた)に手紙(てがみ)を書(か)いた。
I wrote to everyone who had looked after me, beginning with Tanaka-sensei.
</pre>

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みには、水(みず)の事故(じこ)<b>をはじめ</b>、子(こ)どもが巻(ま)き込(こ)まれる事故(じこ)が増(ふ)える。
Over the summer holidays, accidents involving children - drownings first of all - increase.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の大会(たいかい)には、アジア各国(かっこく)<b>をはじめとする</b>三十(さんじゅう)以上(いじょう)の国(くに)と地域(ちいき)が参加(さんか)する。
More than thirty countries and territories will take part in this tournament, the nations of Asia among the first of them.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)は、江戸(えど)時代(じだい)の旅館(りょかん)<b>をはじめ</b>、昔(むかし)のままの建物(たてもの)が数多(かずおお)く残(のこ)っている珍(めずら)しい場所(ばしょ)だ。
This is an unusual town where a great many buildings survive unchanged from the past, an Edo-period inn above all.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **formal, largely written** Japanese: guidebooks, news reports,
company announcements, and the opening of speeches. It is not something you say
about your evening. 「昨日(きのう)はビールをはじめ、いろいろ飲(の)んだ」 sounds like a
press release about a night out; 「ビールとか、いろいろ飲(の)んだ」 is what a person
says.

It is especially common in **thanks and greetings**, where naming the highest-
ranking person first and then sweeping in everyone else is a fixed courtesy:
「会長(かいちょう)をはじめ、皆様(みなさま)に心(こころ)よりお礼(れい)申(もう)し上(あ)げます」.
Getting the order right matters - the named person should genuinely be the most
senior or the most representative, and putting a junior name there reads as a
slight.

On the kanji: modern writing usually leaves it in kana. If you do write it,
it is <code>始</code> (as in <code>始(はじ)める</code>, "to begin"), never <code>初</code>. And keep it apart
from <code>初(はじ)めて</code>, "for the first time" - a different word that sounds
almost the same and produces nonsense if it slips in here.

Of the four forms, <code>をはじめ</code> is the everyday one, <code>をはじめとして</code> the most
formal, and <code>をはじめとする</code> / <code>をはじめとした</code> are what you use in front of a
noun. There is no meaning difference between the last two.

## Contrast

### をはじめ vs など

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `など` only says the list is partial. `をはじめ`
additionally **ranks**: the named item leads the group, and the group has to be
stated afterwards.

</div>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)には、ペンやノート<b>など</b>が置(お)いてあった。
There were pens, notebooks and so on lying on the desk.
</pre>

<pre>
この図書館(としょかん)には、古(ふる)い地図(ちず)<b>をはじめ</b>、貴重(きちょう)な資料(しりょう)がそろっている。
This library holds a full range of valuable materials, old maps above all.
</pre>

Nothing about the pens is special; the old maps are the library's pride. The two
often appear together - 「Aをはじめ、B や C など」 - with `をはじめ` picking the
headline item and `など` closing the list off.

### をはじめ vs を中心(ちゅうしん)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `をはじめ` opens a **list**; <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> describes a
**distribution**, with the named thing carrying the most weight and everything
else thinning out around it.

</div>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)を<b>中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>、夜(よる)のうちに雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もった。
Snow settled overnight, most heavily up the mountain.
</pre>

<pre>
北(きた)の地方(ちほう)<b>をはじめ</b>、各地(かくち)で雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もった。
Snow settled in various places, in the northern regions first of all.
</pre>

The first is a map with a hot spot. The second is a roll call. `をはじめ` needs
the group to be countable in some sense; a gradient of intensity is
<code>中心(ちゅうしん)</code>'s job.

### をはじめ vs 例(たと)えば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>例(たと)えば</code> is an adverb that opens a clause and
introduces an illustration. `をはじめ` is a postposition that sits on a noun
inside the sentence, and it cannot stand on its own.

</div>

<pre>
果物(くだもの)が好(す)きです。<b>例(たと)えば</b>、りんごやぶどうをよく買(か)います。
I like fruit - apples and grapes, for instance, are things I buy often.
</pre>

<pre>
りんご<b>をはじめ</b>、秋(あき)の果物(くだもの)はどれも安(やす)くなってきた。
Autumn fruit has all come down in price, apples first among them.
</pre>

<code>例(たと)えば</code> picks a random illustration and makes no claim about
importance. `をはじめ` claims the item is the leading one, and it needs the group
in the same sentence. ❌「をはじめ、りんごが好(す)きです」 is not a sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ケーキ<b>をはじめて</b>、甘(あま)いものが好(す)きです。 → ✅ ケーキ<b>をはじめ</b>、甘(あま)いものが好(す)きです。
  <code>初(はじ)めて</code> ("for the first time") is a different word. The pattern ends
  in はじめ, or in はじめとして.

- ❌ 東京(とうきょう)をはじめ都市(とし)で家賃(やちん)が上(あ)がっている。 → ✅ 東京(とうきょう)<b>をはじめとする</b>都市(とし)で家賃(やちん)が上(あ)がっている。
  In front of a noun the pattern needs `とする` or `とした`. Without it the
  reader stumbles over which word <code>都市(とし)</code> belongs to.

- ❌ カレー<b>をはじめ</b>、パンも好(す)きです。 → ✅ カレー<b>をはじめ</b>、辛(から)い料理(りょうり)が好(す)きです。
  The named item has to be a member of the group that follows. Curry does not
  lead a list whose next entry is bread.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さん<b>をはじめ</b>、来(き)てくれました。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さん<b>をはじめ</b>、大勢(おおぜい)の人(ひと)が来(き)てくれました。
  The group must actually appear. On its own the pattern leaves the sentence
  unfinished.

## Practice

**1.** 校長(こうちょう)先生(せんせい)____、多(おお)くの先生方(せんせいがた)が見送(みおく)りに来(き)てくださった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

校長(こうちょう)先生(せんせい)<b>をはじめ</b>（or <b>をはじめとして</b>）、多(おお)くの先生方(せんせいがた)が見送(みおく)りに来(き)てくださった。

The principal is the most senior member of the group named in the second half,
and the second half supplies exactly the wider set the pattern needs. The
honorific verb makes <code>をはじめとして</code> equally at home here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この地方(ちほう)は、りんご（をはじめ・を中心(ちゅうしん)に）果物(くだもの)の産地(さんち)として知(し)られている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

りんご<b>をはじめ</b>、果物(くだもの)の産地(さんち)として知(し)られている。

Apples are one member of the category "fruit", and the sentence goes on to name
that category - a list, not a distribution.
<code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> would suggest apples dominate the output and other
fruit trails off around them, which is a claim about proportions rather than
about what the region is famous for.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Many countries, China and India above all, are increasing their imports."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中国(ちゅうごく)やインド<b>をはじめとする</b>多(おお)くの国(くに)が、輸入(ゆにゅう)を増(ふ)やしている。

Two items can share the leading position, joined with `や`. Because
<code>多(おお)くの国(くに)</code> is a noun being modified, the pattern takes the `とする`
shape rather than the bare `をはじめ`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この美術館(びじゅつかん)には有名(ゆうめい)な絵(え)をはじめ、あります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この美術館(びじゅつかん)には有名(ゆうめい)な絵(え)<b>をはじめ</b>、さまざまな作品(さくひん)があります。

The pattern names the leading item and then owes you the group. Left as it was,
the sentence promises a set and never delivers one. Anything that names the
wider category - <code>さまざまな作品(さくひん)</code>,
<code>多(おお)くの美術品(びじゅつひん)</code> - repairs it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この大学(だいがく)には、アジア<b>をはじめとする</b>世界中(せかいじゅう)の留学生(りゅうがくせい)がいる。
(b) この大学(だいがく)には、アジア<b>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>世界中(せかいじゅう)から留学生(りゅうがくせい)が来(き)ている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents Asia as the first entry on a list of origins. It says nothing about
how many students come from where - only that Asia is the region you would name
first.

(b) makes a quantitative claim: most of the students are Asian, and the numbers
thin out as you move away from that core. If a reader wanted to know the shape
of the student body, (b) is the sentence that answers them.

</details>

# をこめて

**Meaning** · with X poured into it - putting a feeling, a wish or physical force into what you make or do

Written ～を込(こ)めて when kanji is used. The verb <code>込(こ)める</code> means to
pack something into a container, and that is the picture: you are packing a
feeling into an object or an action. The English gloss "filled with" is a trap - 
this pattern is never about ordinary physical fullness.

## Formation

The noun in front comes from a small, closed set: feelings, wishes, and force.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (feeling) | Noun + をこめて + verb | 心(こころ) → 心(こころ)をこめて作(つく)る |
| Noun (wish, prayer) | Noun + をこめて + verb | 願(ねが)い → 願(ねが)いをこめて贈(おく)る |
| Noun (physical force) | Noun + をこめて + verb | 力(ちから) → 力(ちから)をこめて押(お)す |
| Longer feeling phrase | Noun + の気持(きも)ち + をこめて | 感謝(かんしゃ) → 感謝(かんしゃ)の気持(きも)ちをこめて |
| Before a noun | Noun + をこめた + Noun | 愛情(あいじょう) → 愛情(あいじょう)をこめた料理(りょうり) |
| Passive | Noun + がこめられている | 思(おも)い → 思(おも)いがこめられている |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the nouns that work here are few enough to learn as a list:
心(こころ), 愛(あい), 愛情(あいじょう), 感謝(かんしゃ), 思(おも)い,
願(ねが)い, 祈(いの)り, 気持(きも)ち, 力(ちから). If your noun is not an
emotion, a wish or force, the pattern is almost certainly the wrong one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
心(こころ)<b>をこめて</b>ケーキを焼(や)きました。
I baked the cake with all my heart.
</pre>

<pre>
力(ちから)<b>をこめて</b>重(おも)いドアを押(お)した。
I pushed the heavy door with all my strength.
</pre>

<pre>
感謝(かんしゃ)の気持(きも)ち<b>をこめて</b>、先生(せんせい)に花(はな)を渡(わた)した。
We handed the teacher flowers as a token of our thanks.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)は毎朝(まいあさ)、愛情(あいじょう)<b>をこめて</b>お弁当(べんとう)を作(つく)ってくれた。
Every morning my mother made me a packed lunch with real love in it.
</pre>

<pre>
「早(はや)く元気(げんき)になってね」という願(ねが)い<b>をこめて</b>、折(お)り鶴(づる)を届(とど)けた。
We sent origami cranes, carrying the wish that she'd get well soon.
</pre>

<pre>
この曲(きょく)には、作(つく)った人(ひと)の強(つよ)い思(おも)い<b>がこめられて</b>いる。
There is a powerful feeling packed into this piece of music.
</pre>

<pre>
卒業(そつぎょう)する先輩(せんぱい)に、感謝(かんしゃ)<b>をこめた</b>アルバムをみんなで作(つく)った。
We all made an album, full of our thanks, for the graduating seniors.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)してほしいという願(ねが)い<b>をこめて</b>、応援席(おうえんせき)から最後(さいご)まで声(こえ)を出(だ)し続(つづ)けた。
Willing them to win, we kept up our shouting from the stands right to the end.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **warm and slightly literary**. It belongs to gift-giving, cards,
speeches, product labelling and song lyrics, and it always implies that the
speaker took care. Said about your own work it can sound either sincere or a
little self-congratulatory, which is why advertising loves it: 「職人(しょくにん)が心(こころ)をこめて一(ひと)つずつ作(つく)りました」 is close to a cliché on Japanese
packaging.

Two of the collocations behave differently from the rest.
<code>力(ちから)をこめて</code> is plain and physical with no emotional colour at all - 
gripping, pushing, swinging. And <code>～がこめられている</code>, the passive, is the form
you use to talk about meaning already inside something: a name, a song, a
tradition. 「この名前(なまえ)には親(おや)の願(ねが)いがこめられている。」

In casual speech people mostly do not use it. To say a friend's cooking was made
with care you would more likely say 「丁寧(ていねい)に作(つく)ってあるね」. Where it
does turn up in conversation is in fixed farewells and dedications - 
「感謝(かんしゃ)をこめて」 written at the bottom of a card, or announced before a
performance.

Note finally that the verb after it has to be an act of **making, giving or
doing**. There is nothing to pack a feeling into if the predicate is a state:
❌「心(こころ)をこめてうれしいです」 has no container.

## Contrast

### をこめて vs ～でいっぱい・～だらけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the English "filled with" covers three unrelated Japanese
patterns. `をこめて` is a **feeling put into an act**. Plain physical fullness is
`でいっぱい`. Unpleasant covering is `だらけ`.

</div>

<pre>
バケツは水(みず)<b>でいっぱい</b>だった。
The bucket was full of water.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの服(ふく)は泥(どろ)<b>だらけ</b>になっていた。
The child's clothes were covered in mud.
</pre>

<pre>
心(こころ)<b>をこめて</b>手紙(てがみ)を書(か)いた。
I wrote the letter with everything I had.
</pre>

❌<code>水(みず)をこめたバケツ</code> is not a stylistic problem, it is meaningless - 
water is not a feeling, and a bucket is not something you make. `でいっぱい` also
handles abstract fullness where no act is involved:
<code>不安(ふあん)でいっぱいだ</code>, "I'm full of worry."

### をこめて vs をもって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `をもって` is formal "with / by means of". It states the
manner or instrument of an official act. `をこめて` says an emotion went **into**
what you produced.

</div>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)として、誠意(せいい)<b>をもって</b>対応(たいおう)してまいります。
As a company, we will respond to this in good faith.
</pre>

<pre>
感謝(かんしゃ)<b>をこめて</b>、この歌(うた)を皆様(みなさま)に贈(おく)ります。
With our thanks, we dedicate this song to you all.
</pre>

`をもって` is business and legal register - <code>書面(しょめん)をもって</code>,
<code>本日(ほんじつ)をもって</code> - and it never carries warmth. Swap the two and the
first sentence becomes oddly emotional while the second turns bureaucratic.

### 力(ちから)をこめて vs 力(ちから)を入(い)れる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>力(ちから)をこめる</code> is **physical force at a moment**.
<code>～に力(ちから)を入(い)れる</code> is **effort and resources put into an area over
time**.

</div>

<pre>
バットに力(ちから)<b>をこめて</b>、思(おも)い切(き)り振(ふ)った。
I put everything into the bat and swung as hard as I could.
</pre>

<pre>
市(し)は去年(きょねん)から子育(こそだ)て支援(しえん)に力(ちから)<b>を入(い)れて</b>いる。
Since last year the city has been putting real effort into childcare support.
</pre>

The second is a policy, not a muscle. Note the particle too: `をこめる` takes the
force as its object, while <code>力(ちから)を入(い)れる</code> marks the field with `に`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ コップに水(みず)<b>をこめて</b>ください。 → ✅ コップに水(みず)<b>を入(い)れて</b>ください。
  Only feelings, wishes and force can be packed in. For putting an actual
  substance somewhere, the verb is <code>入(い)れる</code>.

- ❌ 心(こころ)<b>にこめて</b>歌(うた)います。 → ✅ 心(こころ)<b>をこめて</b>歌(うた)います。
  The particle is `を`. The feeling is what gets packed, not the place it goes.

- ❌ 感謝(かんしゃ)<b>をこめて</b>手紙(てがみ)をもらった。 → ✅ 感謝(かんしゃ)<b>をこめた</b>手紙(てがみ)をもらった。
  In front of a noun the pattern takes `た`. As written, the sentence says *you*
  poured the gratitude in while receiving the letter.

- ❌ 心(こころ)<b>をこめて</b>この町(まち)が好(す)きです。 → ✅ 心(こころ)から この町(まち)が好(す)きです。
  A feeling needs something to be packed into - an act of making, giving or
  doing. For "from the bottom of my heart" plus a state, the phrase is
  <code>心(こころ)から</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 退職(たいしょく)する部長(ぶちょう)に、感謝(かんしゃ)の気持(きも)ち____花束(はなたば)を渡(わた)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

感謝(かんしゃ)の気持(きも)ち<b>をこめて</b>花束(はなたば)を渡(わた)した。

Handing over flowers is an act you can pack a feeling into, and
<code>感謝(かんしゃ)の気持(きも)ち</code> is one of the pattern's standard nouns. The
longer <code>の気持(きも)ち</code> version is slightly warmer than a bare
<code>感謝(かんしゃ)をこめて</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)については、書面(しょめん)（をこめて・をもって）お知(し)らせいたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

書面(しょめん)<b>をもって</b>お知(し)らせいたします。

A document is an instrument, not an emotion, and the sentence is formal business
Japanese. <code>をもって</code> is the pattern for the manner or means of an official
act; `をこめて` would be nonsense here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She sings every song as though she means every word of it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)はどの曲(きょく)も心(こころ)<b>をこめて</b>歌(うた)う。

<code>心(こころ)をこめて</code> is the standard way to say that an action is performed
with genuine feeling rather than mechanically. Singing is an act, so it gives
the feeling somewhere to go.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この人形(にんぎょう)は作(つく)った人(ひと)の思(おも)いをこめている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この人形(にんぎょう)<b>には</b>作(つく)った人(ひと)の思(おも)い<b>がこめられている</b>。

The doll is not the one doing the packing. When you want to describe the feeling
already sitting inside an object, use the passive
<code>がこめられている</code> and mark the object with `には`. The active form would need
the maker as its subject: 「作(つく)った人(ひと)が思(おも)いをこめた人形(にんぎょう)」.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 力(ちから)<b>をこめて</b>ロープを引(ひ)いた。
(b) 心(こころ)<b>をこめて</b>ロープを引(ひ)いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a description of muscles: the rope was pulled hard, and nothing at all is
being said about the puller's feelings. This is the one collocation in the
family that is purely physical.

(b) is strange as a plain report and would only work in something like a
festival or a ceremony, where pulling the rope is a meaningful act - hauling a
shrine float, ringing a temple bell. The pattern always asks "what is this
feeling being packed into?", and an ordinary tug of a rope has no room for it.

</details>
