# ～ものだ

**Meaning** · says how things generally are, or how people generally ought to behave - a step back from the case in front of you to the rule behind it

`もの` starts life as the plain noun "thing", and at the end of a sentence it keeps
just enough of that meaning to turn a statement into a statement about a *category*.
<code>忘(わす)れるものだ</code> is not "he forgot"; it is "forgetting is the kind of
thing that happens." Everything on this page follows from that one move: a general
truth, a social norm ("that is what one does"), and an exclamation at how strongly
something is so.

## Formation

`ものだ` closes a sentence, so it follows the plain form of whatever comes before it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ものだ | 変(か)わる → 変(か)わるものだ |
| Verb (prohibition) | dictionary form + ものではない | 言(い)う → 言(い)うものではない |
| Verb (wish) | ～たい + ものだ | 会(あ)いたい → 会(あ)いたいものだ |
| Verb (recollection) | た-form + ものだ | 遊(あそ)んだ → 遊(あそ)んだものだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものだ | 早(はや)い → 早(はや)いものだ |
| な-adjective | な + ものだ | 勝手(かって) → 勝手(かって)なものだ |
| Noun | does not attach | ❌学生(がくせい)なものだ |
| Casual | ものだ → もんだ | 大変(たいへん)なもんだ |
| Polite / written | ものです ／ ものである | 変(か)わるものです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - where you put the `ない` changes the meaning. On the **verb** you
get a negative general truth: <code>人(ひと)は変(か)わらないものだ</code>, "people
don't change, as a rule." On **もの** you get a prohibition:
<code>人(ひと)を笑(わら)うものではない</code>, "you don't laugh at people."

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the past form <code>～たものだ</code> does not mean "it used to be a general
truth". It is a separate, warmly nostalgic use - looking back on something you did over
and over - and it behaves differently enough to be treated on its own.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
人(ひと)の名前(なまえ)は<b>忘(わす)れるものだ</b>。
Names are the sort of thing you forget.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもは外(そと)で<b>遊(あそ)ぶものだ</b>。
Children are meant to play outside.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の日記(にっき)を勝手(かって)に<b>読(よ)むものではない</b>。
You don't go reading other people's diaries.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の天気(てんき)は<b>変(か)わりやすいものだ</b>。
Mountain weather is a changeable thing.
</pre>

<pre>
いつか北海道(ほっかいどう)の流氷(りゅうひょう)を<b>見(み)てみたいものだ</b>。
I'd love to see the drift ice in Hokkaido one day.
</pre>

<pre>
年(とし)を取(と)ると、昔(むかし)のことを<b>よく覚(おぼ)えているものです</b>。
Once you get older, it's the old things you remember clearly.
</pre>

<pre>
世話(せわ)になった人(ひと)には、きちんとお礼(れい)を<b>言(い)うものだよ</b>。
When someone's gone out of their way for you, you thank them properly.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに準備(じゅんび)をしても、本番(ほんばん)では思(おも)いがけないことが<b>起(お)こるものだ</b>。
However much you prepare, something you didn't see coming happens on the day.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The form itself is neutral - `ものだ` plain, `ものです` polite, <code>ものである</code>
in essays and editorials, `もんだ` in relaxed speech. What is *not* neutral is the
stance. Ending a sentence with `ものだ` claims to speak for common sense, and a
listener who does not share your common sense hears a small lecture. That is exactly
why the "should" use turns up so often from parents, teachers and older colleagues,
and why it can sound presumptuous coming from a junior. If you want to tell your boss
what to do, `ものだ` is not the tool.

The exclamation use is the friendliest of the three. `よく` plus a past verb plus
`ものだ` expresses amazement rather than instruction - 
<code>よくあんな狭(せま)い道(みち)を通(とお)れたものだ</code>, "how on earth did
they get down that narrow road." Said of your own past it becomes rueful; said of
someone else's it can be admiring or sarcastic, and only tone tells you which.

<code>～たいものだ</code> is a wistful "I do wish I could", noticeably more literary
than a bare <code>～たい</code>. Sentence-final `ものね` and `ものよ` lean feminine and a
little old-fashioned in speech; `もんだな` leans the other way. These are tendencies,
not rules - plenty of speakers use all of them.

Two neighbours to keep separate, because they share the syllable and mean nothing
like it. Sentence-final `もの` or `もん` as an excuse - <code>だって知(し)らなかった
んだもん</code>, "but I *didn't know*" - is casual and reads as sulky or childish.
And <code>ものだから</code> is a causal conjunction, "because", with no generalising
force at all.

## Contrast

### ～ものだ vs ～べきだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ものだ` says *that is how it is done*; `べきだ` says *you are
obliged to do it, here, now*. One describes the custom, the other issues the
obligation.

</div>

<pre>
人(ひと)に物(もの)を借(か)りたら、きちんと返(かえ)す<b>ものだ</b>。
You give back what you borrow - that's how it works.
</pre>

<pre>
借(か)りた傘(かさ)は明日(あした)返(かえ)す<b>べきだ</b>。
You ought to give that umbrella back tomorrow.
</pre>

Because `ものだ` generalises, it cannot carry the particulars: no deadline, no named
person, no *this* umbrella. The moment a sentence contains
<code>明日(あした)までに</code> or <code>君(きみ)は</code>, `ものだ` starts to
sound wrong and `べきだ` takes over. `べきだ` also turns on the speaker - 
<code>私(わたし)が謝(あやま)るべきだ</code>, "I'm the one who should apologise" - 
and `ものだ` cannot single anybody out that way.

### ～ものだ vs ～ことだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことだ` is advice pointed at the listener: *what you need to do
is…*. `ものだ` is a remark about people in general, and any advice in it arrives
sideways.

</div>

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)のときは、とにかく寝(ね)る<b>ことだ</b>。
When you've got a cold, the thing to do is sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)は寝(ね)ていれば治(なお)る<b>ものだ</b>。
A cold clears up if you stay in bed - that's colds for you.
</pre>

The first sentence has a listener in it even though no listener is named; it is a
recommendation and can sound blunt from someone junior. The second names nobody and
commits to nothing about *your* cold. That indirectness is its whole value: you can
correct someone's behaviour with `ものだ` without ever saying "you".

### ～ものだ vs ～のだ（んだ）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `んだ` explains **this** situation. `ものだ` states what is true of
the **type**.

</div>

<pre>
沖縄(おきなわ)の冬(ふゆ)は暖(あたた)かい<b>んです</b>。
Winter in Okinawa is warm, you see. (that's why I'm dressed like this)
</pre>

<pre>
南(みなみ)へ行(い)けば行(い)くほど、冬(ふゆ)は暖(あたた)かい<b>ものです</b>。
The further south you go, the milder the winter - that's simply how it goes.
</pre>

Both endings are built on a noun, both feel "explanatory", and learners reach for
whichever they met first. The test is whether the sentence would still be true in a
conversation about somewhere else entirely. If yes, it is `ものだ`. Note that `ものだ`
therefore refuses a one-off subject: ❌<code>昨日(きのう)のテストは難(むずか)しい
ものだ</code> is broken, because one particular test is not a category.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は早(はや)く来(く)るものだ。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は早(はや)く<b>来(き)てください</b>。
  `ものだ` generalises, so it cannot deliver a one-off instruction to one person.
  Use a request, or `べきだ` if you want the weight of an obligation.

- ❌ 汚(きたな)い言葉(ことば)を使(つか)わないものだ。 → ✅ 汚(きたな)い言葉(ことば)を<b>使(つか)うものではない</b>。
  For a prohibition the `ない` goes on `もの`. With `ない` on the verb the sentence
  becomes a claim that people generally don't swear, which is not what you meant.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は正直(しょうじき)な人(ひと)なものだ。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は正直(しょうじき)な人(ひと)だ。
  Nouns do not take `ものだ`. The pattern needs a verb or an adjective in front of it.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)るものだ。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は毎朝(まいあさ)<b>走(はし)ることにしている</b>。
  Your own routine is not a truth about humanity. `ものだ` would read as "running
  every morning is what people do."

## Practice

**1.** 失敗(しっぱい)は誰(だれ)にでもある____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

失敗(しっぱい)は誰(だれ)にでもある<b>ものだ</b>。

The sentence is consoling someone by widening the frame from their mistake to
mistakes in general - "everybody slips up sometimes." That widening is precisely
what `ものだ` does, and it is why the sentence comforts rather than judges.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この資料(しりょう)は今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に出(だ)す（ものだ・べきだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この資料(しりょう)は今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に出(だ)す<b>べきだ</b>。

There is a specific document and a specific deadline in the sentence, so it is an
obligation in one particular case, not a custom. `ものだ` needs a statement that would
still hold in somebody else's office next year.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Children don't listen to their parents - that's just how it is."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)どもは親(おや)の言(い)うことを<b>聞(き)かないものだ</b>。

The `ない` belongs on the verb here, because this is a negative *generalisation*, not
a prohibition. Putting it on `もの` would give
<code>聞(き)くものではない</code> - "one shouldn't listen to one's parents" - which
is a rather different piece of parenting advice.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 人(ひと)の失敗(しっぱい)を笑(わら)わないものだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 人(ひと)の失敗(しっぱい)を<b>笑(わら)うものではない</b>。

The speaker is telling someone off, and a telling-off takes
<code>ものではない</code>. As written, the sentence claims that people generally
refrain from laughing at others' mistakes - a claim the world does not support.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 時間(じかん)が経(た)つのは早(はや)い<b>ものだ</b>。
(b) 時間(じかん)が経(た)つのは早(はや)い<b>です</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker stepping back and marvelling - "how quickly time goes." The
`ものだ` frames it as a truth about time itself rather than about today, and it
carries feeling: it is what you say at a reunion or on a birthday.

(b) is a flat report. Grammatically fine, but it lands like a line from a survey
result, with no speaker attitude in it at all. Where English would put a sigh, (a) is
the sentence you want.

</details>

# ～たものだ

**Meaning** · looking back on something you used to do again and again, with feeling - "we'd always…", "I used to…"

This is the past-tense face of `ものだ`, and it is not simply "`ものだ` in the past".
The general-truth `ものだ` steps back from one case to the category; `たものだ` steps
back from now to *then*. It reports a repeated past habit and, in the same breath,
marks it as gone. That is why the natural English is not "I did" but "I used to" or
"we'd always" - and why the sentence usually arrives with a little warmth on it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + ものだ | 通(かよ)う → 通(かよ)ったものだ |
| Verb (habit in progress) | ていた + ものだ | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んでいたものだ |
| Verb (negative) | なかった + ものだ | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べなかったものだ |
| い-adjective | かった + ものだ | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)かったものだ |
| Polite | たものです | 遊(あそ)んだものです |
| Casual | たもんだ | 遊(あそ)んだもんだ |
| Usual company | よく・昔(むかし)・～ころ + たものだ | 昔(むかし)はよく叱(しか)られたものだ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the past sits on the **verb**, and `ものだ` stays in the
non-past. ✅<code>行(い)ったものだ</code>, not
❌<code>行(い)ったものだった</code>. The polite form is
<code>たものです</code>, never <code>たものでした</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs are this pattern's home ground. い-adjectives work when you are
describing how things used to be - <code>安(やす)かったものだ</code> - but nouns and
な-adjectives sit awkwardly with it, and a plain <code>昔(むかし)は～だった</code> is
the safer sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子(こ)どものころは、この川(かわ)でザリガニを<b>とったものだ</b>。
As a kid I used to catch crayfish in this river.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)はよく父(ちち)に<b>叱(しか)られたものだ</b>。
My father used to tell me off a lot back then.
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)のころは、一日(いちにち)に三本(さんぼん)も映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)たものです</b>。
When I was a student I'd watch three films in a day.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)のパソコンは動(うご)き出(だ)すまでに時間(じかん)が<b>かかったものだ</b>。
Computers back then took an age just to start up.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)も昔(むかし)は夜(よる)になると本当(ほんとう)に<b>暗(くら)かったものだ</b>。
This area used to go properly dark at night.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は毎年(まいとし)この時期(じき)になると梅(うめ)を<b>漬(つ)けていたものだ</b>。
Every year around this time my grandmother would pickle plums.
</pre>

<pre>
高校(こうこう)のときは、終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)してよく友(とも)だちの家(いえ)に<b>泊(と)まったものです</b>。
In high school I was always missing the last train and ending up at a friend's place.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は安(やす)かったが、あのころは毎晩(まいばん)のように同僚(どうりょう)と飲(の)みに<b>行(い)ったものだ</b>。
The pay was terrible, but back then we'd go out drinking with colleagues nearly every night.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たものだ` is the voice of reminiscence. It belongs to anecdotes, to essays, to the
part of a conversation that begins <code>昔(むかし)はねえ</code>. Because of that it
carries an age signal: a speaker in their twenties using it about last year sounds
like they are doing an impression of someone older, while the same form from a
grandparent about the 1970s is completely at home. It is common in writing - 
memoirs and personal columns lean on it heavily - and in speech it usually comes
with `よく`, <code>昔(むかし)</code>, <code>あのころ</code> or
<code>～のころは</code> propping it up.

`たもんだ` is the casual contraction and is very frequent in spoken reminiscence,
often with a sigh attached: <code>よく歩(ある)いたもんだ</code>. `たものです` is the
polite version and keeps the same warmth; it does not become stiff.

Two limits are worth learning as facts about the pattern rather than as style advice.
First, it needs **repetition**. One trip, one accident, one meeting cannot take
`たものだ`. Second, it wants to be **your own memory**. Reaching for it about a
historical period you only read about - say
<code>江戸(えど)時代(じだい)の人(ひと)はよく歩(ある)いたものだ</code> - sounds off,
and a reported form such as
<code>歩(ある)いていたそうだ</code> is what you want there.

Finally, `よく` plus `たものだ` is genuinely two-way. <code>よく遊(あそ)んだものだ</code>
is nostalgia, "we played a lot." <code>よくあんな所(ところ)に住(す)めたものだ</code>
is astonishment, "how did anyone manage to live there." The astonished reading tends
to come with a potential verb or with `よくもまあ`, and the context decides the rest.

## Contrast

### ～たものだ vs the plain past

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the plain past **reports** an event. `たものだ` **recalls** a
habit. If you could put "once" in the English, `たものだ` is wrong.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)、家族(かぞく)で北海道(ほっかいどう)へ<b>行(い)った</b>。
Last year we went to Hokkaido as a family.
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)になると、家族(かぞく)で北海道(ほっかいどう)へ<b>行(い)ったものだ</b>。
Come summer, we'd go up to Hokkaido as a family.
</pre>

The first is one trip and takes a date. The second is a pattern of trips and takes a
recurring time expression instead - <code>夏(なつ)になると</code>,
<code>毎年(まいとし)</code>, `よく`. Note also what the second adds that the first
does not: an implied "and we don't any more." A plain past says nothing about whether
the thing still happens; `たものだ` quietly closes the door on it.

### ～たものだ vs ～ていた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ていた` describes a past habit **neutrally**, from outside.
`たものだ` describes the same habit **from inside the memory**, with the speaker's
feeling attached.

</div>

<pre>
父(ちち)は毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に<b>起(お)きていた</b>。
My father got up at six every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に<b>起(お)きたものだ</b>。
My father would be up at six every morning, without fail.
</pre>

Where a form is required rather than optional, `ていた` is the one that works: a
police statement, a résumé, a lab notebook. `たものだ` in any of those reads as
sentimental. The two combine happily as
<code>起(お)きていたものだ</code>, which spells out both the duration and the
fondness, and that combination is very common with `ていた`-friendly verbs of state.

### ～たものだ vs ～ものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same ending, two different jobs. Non-past `ものだ` says *this is
how the world is*. Past `たものだ` says *this is how my life used to be*.

</div>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の前(まえ)は誰(だれ)でも<b>不安(ふあん)になるものだ</b>。
Everyone gets anxious before an exam - that's normal.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の前(まえ)はいつも<b>眠(ねむ)れなかったものだ</b>。
I could never sleep before an exam, back in the day.
</pre>

The first makes a claim about people at large and would be equally true said by a
stranger. The second makes no claim about anyone but the speaker. This is also why
the "should" sense never appears in the past form: you cannot instruct anyone about
what to have done in 1995, so ❌<code>挨拶(あいさつ)したものだ</code> as advice is
simply not available.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 先週(せんしゅう)、沖縄(おきなわ)へ行(い)ったものだ。 → ✅ 先週(せんしゅう)、沖縄(おきなわ)へ<b>行(い)った</b>。
  One trip on one date. `たものだ` needs something that happened over and over.

- ❌ 昔(むかし)はよく海(うみ)で泳(およ)いだものでした。 → ✅ 昔(むかし)はよく海(うみ)で<b>泳(およ)いだものです</b>。
  The tense is already carried by <code>泳(およ)いだ</code>. `ものだ` itself stays
  non-past; doubling the past is a very common slip.

- ❌ 来年(らいねん)から毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)るものだ。（meaning "I used to run"）→ ✅ 昔(むかし)は毎朝(まいあさ)<b>走(はし)ったものだ</b>。
  Recollection lives in the た-form. The dictionary form gives you a general truth
  about the world instead, which is not what the sentence was trying to say.

- ❌ 明治(めいじ)時代(じだい)の人(ひと)は着物(きもの)を着(き)たものだ。 → ✅ 明治(めいじ)時代(じだい)の人(ひと)は着物(きもの)を<b>着(き)ていたそうだ</b>。
  You did not live through it, so it is not your memory to be nostalgic about.
  Report it instead.

## Practice

**1.** 学生(がくせい)のころは、お金(かね)がなくて、よく歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)っ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

よく歩(ある)いて<b>帰(かえ)ったものだ</b>（or 帰(かえ)ったものです）

<code>学生(がくせい)のころは</code> and `よく` together announce a repeated past
habit that is over, which is exactly the slot `たものだ` fills. A plain
<code>帰(かえ)った</code> would be grammatical but would lose the "those were the
days" colouring the rest of the sentence has set up.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)、一度(いちど)だけ富士山(ふじさん)に（登(のぼ)った・登(のぼ)ったものだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)、一度(いちど)だけ富士山(ふじさん)に<b>登(のぼ)った</b>。

<code>一度(いちど)だけ</code> settles it. `たものだ` reports a habit, and a habit
cannot happen exactly once. The plain past is the only option for a single event,
however memorable it was.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When I was little, my grandfather would often take me fishing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

小(ちい)さいころ、祖父(そふ)がよく釣(つ)りに<b>連(つ)れていってくれたものだ</b>。

"Would often" plus a childhood frame is the core `たものだ` situation. Keeping
<code>くれた</code> in the sentence matters too: the memory is of something done for
you, and dropping it would flatten a warm sentence into a schedule.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昔(むかし)はこの町(まち)にも映画館(えいがかん)があったものでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昔(むかし)はこの町(まち)にも映画館(えいがかん)が<b>あったものです</b>。

`ものだ` does not conjugate into the past here - <code>あった</code> is already doing
that work. Learners produce <code>ものでした</code> by analogy with
<code>でした</code> endings elsewhere; the fix is simply to leave `ものです` alone.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 母(はは)は毎晩(まいばん)本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)んでくれていた</b>。
(b) 母(はは)は毎晩(まいばん)本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)んでくれたものだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the fact of the habit. It is the sentence you would write in an account of
your childhood routine, and it would sit comfortably in a form or an interview
transcript.

(b) states the same fact and adds the speaker standing in the present looking back at
it fondly. It implies the nights of reading are over, and it invites the listener to
feel something about that. Only (b) would be odd in a neutral report.

</details>

# ～ことがある

**Meaning** · there are times when it happens - occasionally, not as a rule

<code>ことがある</code> takes a situation and says that instances of it *exist*.
Literally "there are occasions of ～". The English that fits is "sometimes", "now and
then", "it can happen that" - and the sentence always implies the opposite is the
norm. If something happens every single day, this is the wrong pattern: the whole
point of <code>ことがある</code> is that the thing is an exception.

Two neighbours share the shape and mean something else entirely. <code>たことがある</code>
with a past verb is *experience* - "I have done it before" - and
<code>ことができる</code> is *ability*. Both are covered earlier in the book; this page
is only about the dictionary form.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ことがある | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れることがある |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことがある | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないことがある |
| い-adjective | plain form + ことがある | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いことがある |
| な-adjective | な + ことがある | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)なことがある |
| Noun | recast with なる or だ | 中止(ちゅうし) → 中止(ちゅうし)になることがある |
| Past ("there used to be times") | dictionary form + ことがあった | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)くことがあった |
| Polite | ことがあります | 迷(まよ)うことがあります |
| Softened | ことも + ある | 一人(ひとり)で行(い)くこともある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the frequency adverbs that go with this are
<code>ときどき</code>, `たまに`, `まれに`, <code>年(ねん)に数回(すうかい)</code>.
They make explicit what the pattern only implies. Adverbs of *constancy* - 
<code>いつも</code>, <code>毎日(まいにち)</code>, <code>必(かなら)ず</code> - 
contradict it and produce a broken sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この道(みち)は朝(あさ)とても<b>混(こ)むことがある</b>。
This road sometimes gets badly congested in the morning.
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)でも山(やま)の上(うえ)は<b>寒(さむ)いことがあります</b>。
Even in summer it can be cold at the top of the mountain.
</pre>

<pre>
たまに電車(でんしゃ)が<b>来(こ)ないことがある</b>。
Every so often the train just doesn't come.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は忙(いそが)しくて、夕飯(ゆうはん)に<b>間(ま)に合(あ)わないことがある</b>。
My father is busy, and there are times he doesn't make it back for dinner.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の日(ひ)は、試合(しあい)が<b>中止(ちゅうし)になることがあります</b>。
On typhoon days the match is sometimes called off.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころは、緊張(きんちょう)して声(こえ)が<b>出(で)ないことがあった</b>。
As a child there were times when I got so nervous no voice came out.
</pre>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)は人(ひと)によって<b>眠(ねむ)くなることがある</b>ので、運転(うんてん)する前(まえ)には飲(の)まないでください。
This medicine can make some people drowsy, so don't take it before driving.
</pre>

<pre>
年(ねん)に一(いち)、二度(にど)、海外(かいがい)の工場(こうじょう)まで<b>出張(しゅっちょう)することがあります</b>。
Once or twice a year I have occasion to travel out to the overseas plant.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral and conjugates like any `ある` sentence:
<code>ことがある</code> plain, <code>ことがあります</code> polite,
<code>ことがございます</code> in service language. Nothing about choosing it makes you
sound formal or casual.

Where it *is* strongly marked is by **genre**. Instructions, warnings and manuals live
on it, because it is the natural way to state a possible but non-routine outcome
without alarming anyone:
<code>まれに故障(こしょう)することがあります</code> on an appliance leaflet,
<code>副作用(ふくさよう)が出(で)ることがあります</code> on a medicine insert. If
you read Japanese product documentation you will meet it constantly.

In conversation it does a quieter job: it hedges. Answering
<code>自炊(じすい)しますか</code> with <code>することがあります</code> concedes that
you do, sometimes, while refusing to claim a habit. Swapping `が` for `も` - 
<code>することもあります</code> - softens it further and adds an explicit "and other
times I don't", which is why `も` is so common when you are being modest or
non-committal about your own behaviour.

One structural point that trips people up: `ある` here is a genuine verb of existence
and `こと` is its subject. That is why the particle on `こと` can change - 
<code>ことも</code> for "there are also times when", <code>ことは</code> when the phrase
is negated or contrasted - and why frequency adverbs such as `めったに` and `ほとんど`
attach to `ある` rather than to the main verb:
<code>まちがえることはめったにない</code>, "I hardly ever get it wrong." Keep in mind
that <code>～ことはない</code> is also a separate pattern meaning "there's no need
to ～"; the frequency adverb is usually what tells you which one you are looking at.

## Contrast

### ～ことがある vs ～たことがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - dictionary form = **sometimes it happens**, an ongoing pattern.
た-form = **I have done it**, an experience in your past. The tense of the verb before
`こと` carries the entire difference.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が夜(よる)まで<b>続(つづ)くことがある</b>。
Meetings sometimes run into the evening.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が夜(よる)まで<b>続(つづ)いたことがある</b>。
There was a time when a meeting ran into the evening.
</pre>

The first is a standing fact about how meetings go here; it says nothing about whether
one has actually run late this month. The second reports that at least one such
meeting has occurred and stops there. Because they answer different questions, the
adverbs differ too: `ときどき` fits the first, <code>一度(いちど)だけ</code> the
second.

### ～ことがある vs a bare adverb

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ときどき遅(おく)れる</code> says the delays are part of the
picture. <code>遅(おく)れることがある</code> says they are the exception, and often
carries a warning or an excuse with it.

</div>

<pre>
このバスは<b>ときどき遅(おく)れます</b>。
This bus runs late from time to time.
</pre>

<pre>
このバスは<b>遅(おく)れることがあります</b>。
This bus is sometimes late. (so leave yourself a margin)
</pre>

Both are true of the same bus timetable, but the second frames lateness as a
possibility you should be aware of rather than a property of the route. That framing
is why the `ことがある` version is what a station announcement or a guidebook uses. The
two can of course stack - <code>ときどき遅(おく)れることがあります</code> - and this
is common; the adverb pins down how often, the pattern keeps the "not normally" tone.

### ～ことがある vs ～かもしれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことがある</code> reports what **does** happen, from
experience. <code>かもしれない</code> guesses about what **may** happen, on this
occasion.

</div>

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)は水道(すいどう)が<b>凍(こお)ることがある</b>。
The pipes do freeze here in winter.
</pre>

<pre>
今夜(こんや)は水道(すいどう)が<b>凍(こお)るかもしれない</b>。
The pipes might freeze tonight.
</pre>

`ことがある` is a claim about a track record, so it needs more than one occasion behind
it and cannot be used about a single future event: ❌<code>明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ることがある</code> is wrong for "it might rain
tomorrow." Conversely
`かもしれない` cannot summarise a pattern - it always leaves the outcome open, so it is
useless for explaining what winters are like around here.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)残業(ざんぎょう)することがあります。 → ✅ ときどき<b>残業(ざんぎょう)することがあります</b>。
  "Every day" and "sometimes" cannot share a sentence. Either drop
  <code>ことがある</code> and say <code>毎日(まいにち)残業(ざんぎょう)します</code>,
  or keep the pattern and use an adverb that matches it.

- ❌ 富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)ることがあります。（meaning "I have climbed Fuji"）→ ✅ 富士山(ふじさん)に<b>登(のぼ)ったことがあります</b>。
  Experience needs the た-form. As written, the sentence claims you climb Fuji from
  time to time, which is a very different life.

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)を話(はな)すことがあります。（meaning "I can speak Japanese"）→ ✅ 日本語(にほんご)を<b>話(はな)すことができます</b>。
  Ability is <code>ことができる</code>. <code>ことがある</code> never means "can";
  the two are separated only by the verb at the end.

- ❌ 寒(さむ)いなことがある。 → ✅ <b>寒(さむ)いことがある</b>。
  Only な-adjectives take `な` before `こと`. い-adjectives attach in their plain form,
  exactly as they do before any other noun.

**1.** この辺(へん)は夏(なつ)でも夜(よる)は____ことがあります。（涼(すず)しい）

い-adjectives go in front of `こと` unchanged - no `な`, no `の`. The sentence works
because coolness in summer is presented as an occasional exception, which is what
<code>夏(なつ)でも</code> has already set up.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 海外(かいがい)のホテルではお湯(ゆ)が出(で)ない（ことがある・ことがあった）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The sentence is a general warning about hotels abroad, valid now, so the non-past

happen, or to a specific stay in the past - and a warning about a trip already over
helps nobody.

</details>

</details>

stands, the sentence says you are occasionally late - and dates it to last year, which
makes no sense.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

It is the sentence for explaining the route to someone new, and it says nothing at all
about today.

(b) is a prediction about the situation at hand - the wind is picking up, so the
trains may well stop. It carries no claim that this has ever happened before, and it
would be strange as a general description of the line.

</details>

# ～ように - in order to

**Meaning** · so that something will be the case - you act now with an eye on a result you cannot simply perform yourself

The clause in front of `ように` is not another thing you do. It is a **state you are
aiming at**: something becoming possible, something not happening, something turning
out right. You then do whatever the main clause says in the hope of getting there.
<code>見(み)えるように前(まえ)に座(すわ)る</code> - "sit at the front so I can
see." Sitting is the action; being able to see is the target.

That is the whole of it, and it is also what separates `ように` from
<code>ために</code>, which takes an action you fully control.

## Formation

The clause before `ように` describes a result, so it is built from verbs that are not
straightforwardly willed: potentials, negatives, and intransitives.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (potential) | potential form + ように | 聞(き)こえる → 聞(き)こえるように |
| Verb (intransitive / uncontrollable) | dictionary form + ように | 間(ま)に合(あ)う → 間(ま)に合(あ)うように |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ように | 忘(わす)れない → 忘(わす)れないように |
| As an instruction | ように + してください | 遅(おく)れないようにしてください |
| As a wish or prayer | ますように（sentence-final） | 受(う)かる → 受(う)かりますように |
| Reporting an instruction | ようにと + 言(い)う・伝(つた)える | 休(やす)むようにと伝(つた)える |
| Formal written | ように → よう | 注意(ちゅうい)なさいますよう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nouns and adjectives cannot sit in front of purpose `ように`. Turn
the goal into a verb first: not ❌<code>静(しず)かなように</code> but
<code>静(しず)かになるように</code> or <code>静(しず)かにするように</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a `ように` you meet after a noun plus `の`, or after a verb meaning "as I
said", is the *comparison* `ように` from N4: <code>鳥(とり)のように</code>, "like a
bird". Same syllables, unrelated job. Purpose `ように` always has a full clause in
front of it and a second clause after it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
みんなに<b>聞(き)こえるように</b>大(おお)きな声(こえ)で話(はな)した。
I spoke up so that everyone could hear.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>忘(わす)れないように</b>メモしておきます。
I'll make a note of it so I don't forget.
</pre>

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)を<b>ひかないように</b>、上着(うわぎ)を持(も)っていきなさい。
Take a jacket so you don't catch cold.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもでも<b>使(つか)えるように</b>、ボタンを大(おお)きくしました。
We made the buttons big so that even a child can use it.
</pre>

<pre>
どうか明日(あした)<b>晴(は)れますように</b>。
Please let it be fine tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)の会議(かいぎ)に<b>間(ま)に合(あ)うように</b>、一本(いっぽん)早(はや)い電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)った。
I caught a train one earlier so I'd make the morning meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
後(あと)で誰(だれ)が見(み)ても<b>分(わ)かるように</b>、ファイルの名前(なまえ)に日付(ひづけ)を入(い)れています。
I put the date in the file names so that anyone can make sense of them later.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)が<b>転(ころ)ばないように</b>、廊下(ろうか)に手(て)すりをつけてもらった。
We had a handrail fitted in the hallway so my grandfather wouldn't fall.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ように` itself is neutral, but it turns up in three settings often enough to be worth
recognising.

**Instructions and notices.** Written Japanese drops the `に` and uses bare `よう`:
<code>足元(あしもと)にご注意(ちゅうい)くださいますよう、お願(ねが)い申(もう)し上(あ)げます</code>.
That `よう` is stiff and formal, and it is everywhere in announcements, signs and
business letters. Learners often read it as a typo for `ように`; it is not.

**A softened order.** A sentence can stop at `ように` and let the verb be understood:
<code>明日(あした)は遅(おく)れないように。</code> - "Don't be late tomorrow, now."
This is common from teachers and supervisors and lands rather more gently than
<code>～ないでください</code>, because grammatically it is a statement of the goal
rather than a command aimed at a person.

**Wishes.** The prayer form is fixed as <code>～ますように</code> and keeps its `ます`
even in the most casual speech: <code>元気(げんき)で過(す)ごせますように</code>
written on a shrine tablet, or muttered while crossing your fingers. Note that it has
no main clause at all - the sentence simply ends there, which is why it feels like a
prayer rather than a plan.

Finally, `ように` inside reported speech becomes <code>ようにと</code> before verbs of
saying, asking and hoping: <code>早(はや)く治(なお)るようにと祈(いの)っている</code>.
The `と` is optional in speech and usual in writing.

## Contrast

### ～ように vs ～ために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ために` takes a goal **you can simply go and do**, and the two
clauses share a subject. `ように` takes a goal **that has to come about**, and the
subjects may differ.

</div>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)<b>するために</b>、お金(かね)をためています。
I'm saving up in order to study abroad.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)が留学(りゅうがく)<b>できるように</b>、お金(かね)をためています。
I'm saving up so that my son can study abroad.
</pre>

In the first, going abroad is an act the saver will perform: same person, fully
volitional, so `ために`. In the second the goal belongs to somebody else and is phrased
as a possibility - hence the potential form and `ように`. Notice that changing the verb
to a potential is often all it takes to move a sentence from one pattern to the other:
<code>読(よ)むために</code> versus <code>読(よ)めるように</code>.

A useful side effect: because `ために` promises a deliberate act, it sounds purposeful
and even a little formal in a plan or a proposal. `ように` sounds considerate, because
it usually has someone else's convenience in it.

### ～ないように vs ～ないために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for a **negative** purpose, use `ないように`. `ないために` normally
gets read as a *cause* - "because ～ not" - which is not what you meant.

</div>

<pre>
遅(おく)れ<b>ないように</b>、三十分(さんじゅっぷん)早(はや)く家(いえ)を出(で)た。
I left half an hour early so as not to be late.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ら<b>ないために</b>、野菜(やさい)の値段(ねだん)が上(あ)がっている。
Vegetable prices are rising because there has been no rain.
</pre>

The asymmetry is easy to explain: "not doing something" is not an act you perform, so
it fails `ために`'s volitional test from the start. Learners who translate "in order
not to" word by word land on ❌<code>忘(わす)れないために</code> almost every time.
The fix is mechanical - any English "so as not to" or "in order not to" becomes
`ないように`.

### purpose ～ように vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ように` sets a **goal for an action**. `のに` names the **task a
thing is for**, and the clause after it judges that thing: it is useful, it takes
time, it costs money, you need one.

</div>

<pre>
野菜(やさい)を<b>切(き)るのに</b>、この包丁(ほうちょう)が便利(べんり)です。
This knife is handy for cutting vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
野菜(やさい)が早(はや)く<b>切(き)れるように</b>、包丁(ほうちょう)を研(と)いだ。
I sharpened the knife so the vegetables would cut quickly.
</pre>

The `のに` sentence is an evaluation - what follows is an adjective or a verb of
needing, taking or costing. The `ように` sentence is an action taken with an outcome in
mind. If you can put "for the job of ～ing" in the English, it is `のに`; if you can put
"so that", it is `ように`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 日本(にほん)へ行(い)くように、お金(かね)をためています。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)へ<b>行(い)くために</b>、お金(かね)をためています。
  Going to Japan is something the speaker will simply do. A controllable act with the
  same subject takes `ために`.

- ❌ 忘(わす)れないために、カレンダーに書(か)いた。 → ✅ <b>忘(わす)れないように</b>、カレンダーに書(か)いた。
  Negative purpose is always `ないように`. `ないために` would be heard as "because I
  didn't forget".

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かなように、ドアを閉(し)めた。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が<b>静(しず)かになるように</b>、ドアを閉(し)めた。
  A な-adjective cannot stand in front of purpose `ように`. Give the goal a verb - 
  `なる` or `する` - and the sentence works.

- ❌ 明日(あした)晴(は)れるようにします。 → ✅ 明日(あした)<b>晴(は)れますように</b>。
  You cannot undertake to make the weather behave. When the goal is entirely out of
  your hands, the sentence is a wish, not a plan.

The goal is that the screen *be visible* to other people - a state, not an act the

</details>

The person who reads and the person who writes are different, and "can read" is a

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子(こ)どもが<b>寝(ね)られるように</b>、テレビを消(け)した。
(b) 子(こ)どもを<b>寝(ね)かせるために</b>、テレビを消(け)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the child's sleep in the position of a hoped-for outcome: the speaker removes
an obstacle and the child, one hopes, drops off. It sounds considerate, and it leaves
room for the child to stay awake anyway.

(b) makes it the speaker's project. The causative <code>寝(ね)かせる</code> is
something the parent actively does, so `ために` fits, and the sentence sounds more like
a plan being carried out than a kindness being offered.

</details>

# ～ようにしている

**Meaning** · to make a continuing effort to do (or avoid) something - you are trying, as a standing policy, without claiming you always succeed

N4 gave you <code>ようにする</code>: steering your own behaviour towards some result.
Put it in the `ている` form and the steering becomes **ongoing**. The speaker is not
reporting a decision taken this morning, nor a fact about what they do - they are
reporting an effort that has been running for a while and is still running.

That built-in modesty is the point of the pattern. <code>歩(ある)くようにしています</code>
means "I try to walk"; it politely declines to promise that you walked yesterday.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ようにしている | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)くようにしている |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ようにしている | 残(のこ)さない → 残(のこ)さないようにしている |
| Verb (potential) | potential form + ようにしている | 取(と)れる → 連絡(れんらく)が取(と)れるようにしている |
| Polite | ようにしています | 歩(ある)くようにしています |
| Effort beginning now | ようにする／ようにします | これから歩(ある)くようにします |
| Effort that has lapsed | ようにしていた | 去年(きょねん)までは歩(ある)くようにしていた |
| Casual speech | ようにしてる | 歩(ある)くようにしてる |
| Business / humble | ようにしております | ご連絡(れんらく)するようにしております |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `ない` belongs to the **main verb**, never to `する`.
<code>食(た)べないようにしている</code> = "I'm trying not to eat it."
❌<code>食(た)べるようにしていない</code> says you are not making the effort, which
is a confession, not a policy.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)、コップ一杯(いっぱい)の水(みず)を<b>飲(の)むようにしている</b>。
I try to drink a glass of water every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
エレベーターは<b>使(つか)わないようにしています</b>。
I try not to use the lift.
</pre>

<pre>
夜(よる)遅(おそ)くはコーヒーを<b>飲(の)まないようにしている</b>。
I try to stay off coffee late at night.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の話(はなし)は最後(さいご)まで<b>聞(き)くようにしています</b>。
I try to hear people out to the end.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)には物(もの)を<b>置(お)かないようにしている</b>。
I try to keep the desktop clear.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)までは自転車(じてんしゃ)で<b>通(かよ)うようにしていた</b>が、今(いま)は電車(でんしゃ)だ。
Up to last year I made a point of trying to cycle in, but now I take the train.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)と食(た)べるときは、テレビを消(け)して<b>話(はな)すようにしています</b>。
When we eat as a family I try to switch the television off and talk.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)くなりそうなときは、一言(ひとこと)だけでも先(さき)に<b>返(かえ)すようにしている</b>。
When I know a proper reply will take a while, I try to send at least a line back first.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral in form - `ようにしています` polite,
<code>ようにしてる</code> in relaxed speech, <code>ようにしております</code> in
business - but it is strongly marked in **attitude**. It presents you as someone who
is working at something, which is why it is the default answer to questions about
health, study and self-improvement:
<code>健康(けんこう)のために何(なに)かしていますか</code> almost invites it.

Its social value is that it claims effort rather than achievement. Saying
<code>毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しています</code> can sound like a boast, and
it commits you: skip a day and you have misspoken.
<code>勉強(べんきょう)するようにしています</code> commits you to nothing but trying,
which is both humbler and safer. Japanese conversation about one's own habits leans
heavily on that hedge.

The same modesty makes it awkward in the wrong slot. Reporting a company rule with it
sounds like you personally are having a go at complying;
<code>ことになっています</code> is what you want there. And promising a superior
<code>明日(あした)までにやるようにします</code> is weaker than it looks - it offers
effort where a commitment was expected. <code>やります</code> or
<code>やっておきます</code> is the reassuring answer.

There is a second, less-taught use worth recognising: setting things up so a state
holds. <code>いつでも連絡(れんらく)が取(と)れるようにしている</code> is not about
willpower at all - it means "I keep it arranged so that I can be reached." When the
verb before `ように` is a potential and the object is a thing rather than your own
behaviour, this is usually the reading.

## Contrast

### ～ようにしている vs ～ことにしている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことにしている` is a **rule you keep**; `ようにしている` is an
**effort you make**. Only the second one survives a bad week.

</div>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は仕事(しごと)を<b>しないことにしている</b>。
I don't work on Sundays - that's my rule.
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は仕事(しごと)を<b>しないようにしている</b>。
I try not to work on Sundays.
</pre>

The first speaker has decided, and the decision holds: if a job comes in on Sunday, it
waits. The second speaker is aiming at the same thing and would not be contradicting
themselves by working this Sunday. That is why
<code>ようにしているが、なかなかできない</code> is a perfectly coherent sentence while
<code>ことにしているが、なかなかできない</code> sounds like someone who does not keep
their own rules.

There is a grammatical consequence too. Because `ようにしている` only claims effort, it
accepts goals you cannot fully control - <code>早(はや)く寝(ね)る</code>,
<code>太(ふと)らない</code>, <code>忘(わす)れない</code>. `ことにしている` needs
something you can simply decide to do, so
❌<code>太(ふと)らないことにしている</code> is odd: you cannot resolve your way out
of gaining weight.

### ～ようにしている vs ～ようにする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようにする` is the effort **starting now**; `ようにしている` is the
effort **already under way**.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)から野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べるようにします</b>。
From tomorrow I'm going to try to eat vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
このごろ野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べるようにしています</b>。
Lately I've been trying to eat vegetables.
</pre>

The non-past form is a resolution or a promise, and it is what you say when someone
has just pointed out a problem: <code>気(き)をつけるようにします</code>, "I'll be
more careful." The `ている` form is a report on how you have been living, and it needs
some past behind it - using it about a plan you formed five minutes ago is wrong.

### ～ようにしている vs plain ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `ている` states the habit. `ようにしている` states the
intention behind the habit, and quietly admits it may not always hold.

</div>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に<b>起(お)きています</b>。
I get up at six every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に<b>起(お)きるようにしています</b>。
I try to get up at six every morning.
</pre>

Use the plain form for facts - a timetable, a medical questionnaire, an account of
what actually happens. Use `ようにしている` when the behaviour is deliberate and you
want that showing: it tells the listener there is a reason behind the six o'clock, and
that you are the one who chose it. Note also that a plain `ている` habit can be
involuntary - <code>よく忘(わす)れています</code> - while `ようにしている` never can.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)るのようにしている。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)<b>走(はし)るようにしている</b>。
  `よう` follows the plain form directly. There is no `の` and no `こと` in front of
  it - that shape belongs to the `こと` patterns.

- ❌ 甘(あま)い物(もの)を食(た)べるようにしていません。 → ✅ 甘(あま)い物(もの)を<b>食(た)べないようにしています</b>。
  Negating `する` says you have given up trying. To try *not* to do something, the
  `ない` goes on the main verb.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は早(はや)く寝(ね)るようにしました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)ました</b>。
  One night is not a policy. `ようにする` describes how you intend to behave from now
  on, so it cannot report a single completed evening.

- ❌ 会社(かいしゃ)では制服(せいふく)を着(き)るようにしています。（describing a company rule）→ ✅ 会社(かいしゃ)では制服(せいふく)を<b>着(き)ることになっています</b>。
  A rule imposed on you is not an effort you are making. `ようにしている` here would
  suggest the uniform is your own private initiative.

## Practice

leaves the door open for the mornings when it does not happen, which (a) does not.

</details>

# ～ことにしている

**Meaning** · to make a point of doing something - a rule you set for yourself once and have kept ever since

N4 gave you <code>ことにする</code>, the moment of deciding. The `ている` form takes
that decision and reports that it is **still in force**. Nothing is being decided in
the sentence; the deciding happened at some unspecified point in the past, and what
you are describing now is a standing personal policy.

The English that fits is "I make a point of ～", "I always ～", "I make it a rule to ～".
The crucial word is *rule*: the speaker set it, and the speaker keeps it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ことにしている | 断(ことわ)る → 断(ことわ)ることにしている |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことにしている | 飲(の)まない → 飲(の)まないことにしている |
| Verb (treating as true) | た-form + ことにしている | 聞(き)かなかった → 聞(き)かなかったことにしている |
| Polite | ことにしています | 断(ことわ)ることにしています |
| Business / humble | ことにしております | ご案内(あんない)することにしております |
| The moment of deciding | ことにする／ことにした | 断(ことわ)ることにした |
| A rule no longer kept | ことにしていた | 以前(いぜん)は断(ことわ)ることにしていた |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `こと` is a noun being modified, so the verb in front of it is
**plain**: ✅<code>断(ことわ)ることにしている</code>,
❌<code>断(ことわ)りますことにしている</code>. The politeness lives on `する` at the
end of the sentence, and nowhere else.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the subject does not have to be one person. A shop, a household or a team
can set its own policy, and then `ことにしている` is still correct because the deciding
was done by the speaker's own side:
<code>当店(とうてん)では…使(つか)うことにしております</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)は仕事(しごと)を<b>しないことにしている</b>。
I make a point of not working on Sundays.
</pre>

<pre>
寝(ね)る前(まえ)に必(かなら)ず歯(は)を<b>磨(みが)くことにしています</b>。
I always brush my teeth before bed, without fail.
</pre>

<pre>
初対面(しょたいめん)の人(ひと)には年(とし)を<b>聞(き)かないことにしている</b>。
I make it a rule not to ask people I've just met their age.
</pre>

<pre>
お酒(さけ)は週末(しゅうまつ)だけと決(き)めて、平日(へいじつ)は<b>飲(の)まないことにしています</b>。
I've settled on weekends only for alcohol, so I don't drink on weekdays.
</pre>

<pre>
夫(おっと)の誕生日(たんじょうび)には、必(かなら)ず手紙(てがみ)を<b>書(か)くことにしている</b>。
On my husband's birthday I always write him a letter.
</pre>

<pre>
当店(とうてん)では、材料(ざいりょう)は地元(じもと)のものを<b>使(つか)うことにしております</b>。
Here at the shop we make a point of using local ingredients.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がない朝(あさ)でも、朝食(ちょうしょく)だけは<b>抜(ぬ)かないことにしています</b>。
Even on mornings when I'm short of time, breakfast is the one thing I never skip.
</pre>

<pre>
誘(さそ)われても、翌日(よくじつ)が早(はや)い日(ひ)は二次会(にじかい)には<b>行(い)かないことにしている</b>。
Even when I'm invited, if I have an early start the next day I don't go on to the second round.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The form is neutral - `ことにしている` plain, `ことにしています` polite,
<code>ことにしております</code> in business - but the content is not. The pattern
announces that you are a person with principles, so it carries a faint weight of
self-discipline that a plain habit sentence does not have. Used about something
trivial it can sound slightly self-important:
<code>毎朝(まいあさ)コーヒーを飲(の)むことにしています</code> is grammatical but
makes an oddly solemn announcement out of a cup of coffee.

Its most useful job in real conversation is **declining**. Because the sentence
attributes the refusal to a rule that predates the invitation, it takes the sting out:

<pre>
すみません、平日(へいじつ)は飲(の)まない<b>ことにしている</b>んです。
Sorry - I have a rule about not drinking on weeknights.
</pre>

Nothing there judges the invitation or the person offering, which is precisely why it
works. The business register does the same thing at a counter:
<code>お受(う)けしないことにしております</code> is a firm refusal that no one can
take personally.

Two smaller points. The <code>た-form + ことにしている</code> variant means treating
something as though it were so, as a standing arrangement:
<code>見(み)なかったことにしている</code>, "I make a policy of pretending I didn't
see." And <code>ことにしていた</code> puts the rule in the past and implies it lapsed - 
useful, but be careful not to use it for a rule you still keep.

## Contrast

### ～ことにしている vs ～ことになっている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one letter, two worlds. `ことにしている` is **my** rule.
`ことになっている` is a rule **I am subject to** - the company's, the school's, the
custom's.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)には必(かなら)ず資料(しりょう)を読(よ)んでおく<b>ことにしている</b>。
I make a point of reading the documents before a meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)には資料(しりょう)を読(よ)んでおく<b>ことになっている</b>。
You're expected to read the documents before a meeting.
</pre>

The first speaker could stop tomorrow and answer to nobody. The second speaker cannot,
because the arrangement is not theirs to change. This is why explaining a workplace
rule with `ことにしている` sounds strange - it claims authorship of something you were
handed - and why explaining a private habit with `ことになっている` sounds evasive, as
though you were blaming a rulebook that does not exist.

### ～ことにしている vs ～ことにした

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことにした` is the **moment** of deciding, and it is news.
`ことにしている` is the **state** of the decision holding, and it is background.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から車(くるま)に乗(の)るのを<b>やめることにしました</b>。
I've decided to stop driving from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
近(ちか)い所(ところ)へは車(くるま)を<b>使(つか)わないことにしています</b>。
I make a point of not taking the car anywhere nearby.
</pre>

The past form answers "what's new?" and expects a reaction. The `ている` form answers
"what are you like?" and expects none. Saying <code>ことにしました</code> about a rule
you have kept for ten years makes it sound like this morning's resolution, and saying
<code>ことにしています</code> about a decision you reached an hour ago is simply not
yet true.

### ～ことにしている vs ～ようにしている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことにしている` says the thing **happens**. `ようにしている` says
you **try** to make it happen. The gap between them is where all the honesty lives.

</div>

<pre>
毎月(まいつき)一万円(いちまんえん)ずつ<b>貯金(ちょきん)することにしている</b>。
I put ten thousand yen aside every month, as a rule.
</pre>

<pre>
毎月(まいつき)少(すこ)しずつ<b>貯金(ちょきん)するようにしている</b>。
I try to save a little each month.
</pre>

The first is a commitment with a number on it and no room to fail; the second is an
intention with a hedge built in. Adverbs sort them: <code>必(かなら)ず</code> and
<code>どんなときも</code> belong to `ことにしている`, while
<code>なるべく</code> and <code>できるだけ</code> belong to `ようにしている`.

The other difference is what each can take as its verb. `ことにする` needs an action you
can simply perform, so ❌<code>風邪(かぜ)をひかないことにしている</code> is
nonsense - you cannot resolve not to fall ill. `ようにしている` accepts it happily,
because trying is always possible.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 会社(かいしゃ)の規則(きそく)で、残業(ざんぎょう)をしないことにしています。 → ✅ 会社(かいしゃ)の規則(きそく)で、残業(ざんぎょう)を<b>しないことになっています</b>。
  <code>規則(きそく)で</code> already tells us somebody else decided. A rule handed
  down to you takes `ことになっている`.

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)になることにしている。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)に<b>なるようにしている</b>。
  Getting good at something is not an act you can decide to perform on a given day.
  `ことにする` needs a controllable action; for a goal, use `ようにする`.

- ❌ 毎朝(まいあさ)散歩(さんぽ)しますことにしています。 → ✅ 毎朝(まいあさ)<b>散歩(さんぽ)することにしています</b>。
  The verb before `こと` is plain. Only the final `する` carries the politeness.

- ❌ 今(いま)でも毎日(まいにち)日記(にっき)を書(か)くことにしていました。 → ✅ 今(いま)でも毎日(まいにち)日記(にっき)を<b>書(か)くことにしています</b>。
  <code>ことにしていた</code> implies the rule has since been dropped, which
  contradicts <code>今(いま)でも</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 肉(にく)は<b>食(た)べないことにしています</b>。
(b) 肉(にく)は<b>食(た)べないようにしています</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a rule, and it is absolute: the speaker does not eat meat. A host hearing it
will plan the menu around it, because there is nothing to negotiate.

(b) is an effort. The speaker is cutting down and would rather not, but a plate with
meat on it is not a crisis. Say (b) when you mean "I'm trying to", and (a) only when
you really mean never - the two get very different reactions at a dinner table.

</details>

# ～ことになっている

**Meaning** · the standing arrangement is that ～ - a rule, a custom or a schedule that someone other than you has settled, and that you are subject to

N4 gave you <code>ことになる</code>: a decision landing, arrived at by circumstances or
by other people. Put it in the `ている` form and you get the **state left behind** by
that decision - the rule that is now on the books, the appointment that is now in the
diary, the way things are simply done here.

The speaker's own will is absent by design. That is the pattern's whole character: it
reports an arrangement without taking responsibility for it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ことになっている | 提出(ていしゅつ)する → 提出(ていしゅつ)することになっている |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことになっている | 持(も)ち込(こ)まない → 持(も)ち込(こ)まないことになっている |
| Noun / whole statement | ということになっている | 休(やす)み → 明日(あした)は休(やす)みということになっている |
| Polite | ことになっています | 集(あつ)まることになっています |
| Business / humble | ことになっております | お預(あず)かりすることになっております |
| The rule at the time | ことになっていた | 着(き)ることになっていた |
| The moment it was settled | ことになった | 転勤(てんきん)することになった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことになっている` and `ことにしている` differ by one syllable and by
who decided. `なっている` = the rule came to you. `している` = you made the rule.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - for a schedule involving other people, this is the natural pattern even
without a formal rule: <code>三時(さんじ)に会(あ)うことになっている</code> means
the meeting has been arranged between you and someone else. A plan you formed alone
takes <code>つもりだ</code> or a plain future instead.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ここでは靴(くつ)を<b>脱(ぬ)ぐことになっている</b>。
You take your shoes off here - that's the rule.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は毎週(まいしゅう)月曜日(げつようび)に<b>開(ひら)くことになっています</b>。
The meeting is held every Monday.
</pre>

<pre>
寮(りょう)では夜(よる)十一時(じゅういちじ)までに<b>帰(かえ)ることになっています</b>。
In the dormitory you're expected to be back by eleven at night.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)に健康(けんこう)診断(しんだん)を<b>受(う)けることになっている</b>。
I'm down for a health check next Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
図書館(としょかん)の本(ほん)は外(そと)へ<b>持(も)ち出(だ)さないことになっています</b>。
Library books aren't to be taken outside.
</pre>

<pre>
表向(おもてむ)きは誰(だれ)も知(し)らなかった<b>ということになっている</b>。
Officially, nobody knew a thing.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)では、領収書(りょうしゅうしょ)がないと経費(けいひ)として<b>落(お)とせないことになっております</b>。
Under the rules, we're unable to put it through as an expense without a receipt.
</pre>

<pre>
担当者(たんとうしゃ)が不在(ふざい)のときは、私(わたし)が代(か)わりに<b>対応(たいおう)することになっています</b>。
When the person in charge is away, I'm the one who deals with it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral in form, institutional in flavour. `ことになっています` is the ordinary way to
explain how an office, a school, a club or a country does things, and
<code>ことになっております</code> is its business-humble twin - you will hear it across
counters, on the phone and in customer-facing email constantly.

Its social function is worth spelling out, because it is the reason the pattern
survives. It removes the speaker from the decision. Compare a flat
<code>規則(きそく)ですから</code>, "it's the rules", which puts a wall in front of
the customer, with <code>～ことになっておりまして</code>, which reports the same wall
while implying the speaker did not build it and is sorry it is there. Refusing with it
is polite precisely because it is impersonal.

The `ということになっている` variant adds a further layer: *officially*, as opposed to
actually. <code>この話(はなし)はなかったということになっている</code> - "as far as
anyone is concerned, this conversation never happened." That "on paper" reading is
common in fiction and in workplace gossip, and it is not always cynical; it can simply
mean "that is the version of record."

One more thing to notice. Because the pattern describes a state, it does not announce
change. Use <code>ことになりました</code> for news - a transfer, a new policy, a
wedding - and switch to <code>ことになっています</code> once the thing has settled into
being how it is. Reversing the two is a very audible mistake: announcing a rule from
1958 with <code>ことになりました</code> makes it sound like this morning's memo.

## Contrast

### ～ことになっている vs ～ことにしている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask who could cancel it. If only you could, it is
`ことにしている`. If you would have to ask someone, it is `ことになっている`.

</div>

<pre>
うちの課(か)では水曜日(すいようび)は残業(ざんぎょう)を<b>しないことになっている</b>。
In our section we don't do overtime on Wednesdays.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は水曜日(すいようび)は残業(ざんぎょう)を<b>しないことにしている</b>。
I make a point of not working late on Wednesdays.
</pre>

The first is departmental policy and applies to everyone, including people who would
rather stay. The second is one person's rule and binds nobody else. Learners tend to
overuse `ことにしている` when explaining their workplace, which quietly claims they
invented the policy; the reverse error, using `ことになっている` for a personal habit,
sounds like hiding behind a rulebook.

### ～ことになっている vs ～ことになった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことになった` is the **moment** the arrangement was made, and it
is news. `ことになっている` is the arrangement **standing**, and it is background.

</div>

<pre>
四月(しがつ)から大阪(おおさか)の支店(してん)で<b>働(はたら)くことになりました</b>。
It's been settled that I'll be working at the Osaka branch from April.
</pre>

<pre>
新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)は一年目(いちねんめ)に支店(してん)で<b>働(はたら)くことになっています</b>。
New employees spend their first year at a branch office.
</pre>

The past form is what you say when you tell people; the `ている` form is what you say
when you explain the system. A useful test: if the sentence would naturally be
followed by <code>おめでとう</code> or <code>そうなんですか</code>, it is
`ことになった`.

### ～ことになっている vs ～予定(よてい)だ・～はずだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことになっている` = the arrangement is on the books.
<code>予定(よてい)だ</code> = it is on the schedule. `はずだ` = I reason that it
should be so.

</div>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)は三時(さんじ)に到着(とうちゃく)する<b>ことになっています</b>。
The president is due to arrive at three. (that's the arrangement)
</pre>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)は三時(さんじ)に到着(とうちゃく)する<b>はずです</b>。
The president should be arriving at three. (as far as I can work out)
</pre>

`はずだ` reports the speaker's inference and therefore admits doubt; `ことになっている`
reports an external fact and does not. <code>予定(よてい)だ</code> sits between them
 - it states the plan without the flavour of obligation that `ことになっている` carries,
which is why a timetable takes <code>予定(よてい)</code> and a company regulation
does not. Note the adverb here is <code>予定(よてい)どおり</code>, "as planned",
with `どおり` rather than `とおり` after a noun.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ うちの会社(かいしゃ)では制服(せいふく)を着(き)ることにしています。（describing the company's rule）→ ✅ うちの会社(かいしゃ)では制服(せいふく)を<b>着(き)ることになっています</b>。
  You did not write the staff handbook. A rule imposed on you takes `なっている`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は一人(ひとり)で映画(えいが)を見(み)ることになっています。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は一人(ひとり)で映画(えいが)を<b>見(み)るつもりです</b>。
  Nobody arranged this with you. The pattern implies an agreement or a rule, so a
  private plan needs <code>つもり</code> or a plain future.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ることになっています。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)るそうです</b>。
  Weather is not an arrangement. `ことになっている` is only for things human beings
  have settled.

- ❌ 受付(うけつけ)で名前(なまえ)を書(か)きますことになっています。 → ✅ 受付(うけつけ)で名前(なまえ)を<b>書(か)くことになっています</b>。
  `こと` modifies a plain verb. The politeness belongs on `なる` at the end.

## Practice

**1.** この会社(かいしゃ)では、会議(かいぎ)の記録(きろく)は新人(しんじん)が____ことになっています。（とる）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会議(かいぎ)の記録(きろく)は新人(しんじん)が<b>とることになっています</b>。

The verb goes in its plain dictionary form directly in front of `こと`, and the
sentence explains a company practice that applies to whoever the newest person happens
to be - nobody in particular decided it this morning, which is exactly the
`ことになっている` situation.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 私(わたし)は毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きる（ことにしています・ことになっています）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)は毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きる<b>ことにしています</b>。

Getting up at six is the speaker's own rule, kept by the speaker alone, so it takes
`にしている`. `になっている` would imply some external authority requires it, which
would be a strange thing to say about your own alarm clock.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Guests are asked to leave their luggage at the front desk."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お荷物(にもつ)はフロントで<b>お預(あず)かりすることになっております</b>。

The humble <code>ことになっております</code> is the hotel register: it states the
arrangement without any individual taking credit or blame for it, which is why it
sounds courteous rather than bossy. A plain <code>預(あず)けてください</code> would
be an order.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来月(らいげつ)、東京(とうきょう)へ転勤(てんきん)することになっています。（telling a colleague the news for the first time）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来月(らいげつ)、東京(とうきょう)へ<b>転勤(てんきん)することになりました</b>。

News takes the past form. The `ている` form presents the transfer as long-established
background, so as an announcement it would puzzle the listener - they would wonder why
you were only mentioning it now.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に始(はじ)まる<b>ことになっています</b>。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に始(はじ)まる<b>はずです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the arrangement as a fact of the schedule. The speaker is passing on what
was fixed by someone with the authority to fix it, and takes no position on whether it
will actually happen on time.

(b) is the speaker's own inference - from the agenda they saw, or from what somebody
told them. It admits room for error, so it is the honest choice when you are not
certain, and it would be evasive if you had the schedule in front of you.

</details>
