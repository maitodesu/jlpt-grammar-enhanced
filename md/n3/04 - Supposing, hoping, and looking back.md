# N3 - Supposing, hoping, and looking back


---

# ～としたら / ～とすれば

**Meaning** · supposing that A is true, then B - a premise granted for the sake of working out what follows

The `と` here is the quotative `と`, and `する` is "take it as". <code>～としたら</code>
literally means *if we take it that…*. It does not report a condition on its way to
happening; it **grants a proposition** and asks the listener to see what falls out of
it. That is why the main clause is nearly always a judgement - a deduction, an
estimate, a question, a "then there's nothing for it but…" - rather than a plan or an
instruction.

## Formation

Everything goes into its plain form and takes `と`. Nouns and な-adjectives need `だ`
in front - the opposite of `なら`, which forbids it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain non-past + としたら／とすれば | 行(い)く → 行(い)くとしたら |
| Verb (past) | た-form + としたら | 落(お)とした → 落(お)としたとしたら |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + としたら | 間(ま)に合(あ)わない → 間(ま)に合(あ)わないとしたら |
| い-adjective | plain form + としたら（no だ） | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いとしたら |
| な-adjective | stem + **だ** + としたら | 本当(ほんとう) → 本当(ほんとう)だとしたら |
| Noun | Noun + **だ** + としたら | 嘘(うそ) → 嘘(うそ)だとしたら |
| Explanatory | plain form + のだとしたら | 知(し)っている → 知(し)っているのだとしたら |
| Variants | とすると／となると | 来(く)る → 来(く)るとすると |
| Polite (in a clause) | としましたら／といたしますと | 事実(じじつ)だ → 事実(じじつ)だとしましたら |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the adverbs もし, 仮(かり)に and 万一(まんいち) sit naturally in front of
this pattern and announce the supposition early: 仮(かり)に勝(か)ったとしたら…
They are optional, but they make a long sentence much easier to follow.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今(いま)から行(い)く<b>としたら</b>、何時(なんじ)に着(つ)きますか。
If we were to set off now, what time would we get there?
</pre>

<pre>
これが本当(ほんとう)だ<b>とすれば</b>、大(おお)きな問題(もんだい)です。
If this is true, then it's a serious problem.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で全部(ぜんぶ)やる<b>としたら</b>、三日(みっか)はかかるだろう。
If I had to do the whole thing alone, it would take a good three days.
</pre>

<pre>
仮(かり)に宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たった<b>としたら</b>、何(なに)に使(つか)いますか。
Supposing you won the lottery, what would you spend it on?
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が来(こ)ない<b>とすれば</b>、司会(しかい)は私(わたし)がやるしかない。
If he isn't coming, then I have no choice but to run the thing myself.
</pre>

<pre>
この数字(すうじ)が正(ただ)しい<b>とすると</b>、去年(きょねん)より売(う)り上(あ)げが減(へ)っていることになる。
If these figures are right, it means sales are down on last year.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)する<b>としたら</b>、やっぱり英語(えいご)が通(つう)じる国(くに)がいいなあ。
If I were going to study abroad, I'd still want somewhere English gets you by.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)で新幹線(しんかんせん)が止(と)まっている<b>としたら</b>、明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は延期(えんき)するほかないでしょう。
If the bullet trains are down because of the typhoon, there'll be nothing for it but to postpone tomorrow's meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)の条件(じょうけん)のまま引(ひ)き受(う)ける<b>とすれば</b>、人(ひと)を二人(ふたり)増(ふ)やしていただく必要(ひつよう)があります。
If we're to take this on under the current terms, we'd need two more people on it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The family is register-neutral in the sense that no member is rude or childish, but
the three are not evenly distributed. <code>としたら</code> is the everyday spoken
member and the one that handles frankly unreal suppositions - 
生(う)まれ変(か)わったとしたら, 私(わたし)が社長(しゃちょう)だとしたら.
<code>とすれば</code> leans towards written argument, reports and analysis, where it
introduces an inference the writer is prepared to defend. <code>とすると</code> tends to
mark a consequence the speaker has just this second worked out, and often carries a
small "ah - then…" in it.

Because the clause is subordinate, it stays plain even in the politest sentence:
事実(じじつ)だとすれば、大変(たいへん)申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません。 If you
want the politeness to reach into the clause itself - service-counter Japanese, a
careful reply to a superior - the forms are <code>としましたら</code> and, at the top of
the scale, <code>といたしますと</code>. These are common at a bank or a travel desk and
sound stiff anywhere else.

One restriction is worth internalising. The main clause is where the **speaker's
judgement** goes: だろう, はずだ, ことになる, しかない, ～たい, or a question. A bare
statement of scheduled fact does not belong there, and neither does a request. If
what you mean is "let me know once you get home", the sentence wants `たら`, not
`としたら` - you are not supposing anything, you are waiting for something.

Finally, `としたら` freely takes a **past** clause in front without referring to past
time: 落(お)としたとしたら、どこだろう。 The `た` marks the supposed situation as
already complete at the moment you are reasoning about it, exactly as it does with
`たら`.

## Contrast

### ～としたら vs ～たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たら` supposes an **event** that could actually turn up; `としたら`
supposes a **proposition** so that you can reason from it. If you could replace the
clause with "assuming that…", you want `としたら`.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)に<b>着(つ)いたら</b>電話(でんわ)してね。
Give me a call when you get to the station.
</pre>

That one cannot take `としたら`: arriving is not a hypothesis, it is the plan. Swap in
a genuine supposition and the pattern comes back:

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)に乗(の)り遅(おく)れた<b>としたら</b>、彼女(かのじょ)はどこで泊(と)まるつもりだろう。
Supposing she did miss the last train, where is she planning to stay?
</pre>

The same split explains why `としたら` sounds odd with an instruction after it. You
cannot act on a premise that is only being entertained.

### ～としたら vs ～なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なら` picks up something the other person has just put on the
table and answers it, usually with advice or an offer. `としたら` may suppose something
nobody has claimed, and answers itself with a conclusion.

</div>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)に<b>行(い)くなら</b>、朝(あさ)早(はや)く出(で)たほうがいいよ。
If you're going to Kyoto, you'd do better to leave early.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)に<b>行(い)くとしたら</b>、日帰(ひがえ)りでは無理(むり)だろうね。
If one were to go to Kyoto, a day trip probably wouldn't work.
</pre>

The first is aimed at a person with a plan; the second weighs an idea. Note also the
form: `なら` refuses `だ` (本当(ほんとう)なら) while `としたら` requires it
(本当(ほんとう)だとしたら). They look like a pair and conjugate as opposites.

### ～としたら vs ～とすれば vs ～とすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three grant a premise. `としたら` is the freest and most
spoken; `とすれば` is the reasoning one and dominates writing; `とすると` presents the
consequence as something that simply falls out, and often marks a realisation.

</div>

<pre>
もし私(わたし)が部長(ぶちょう)だ<b>としたら</b>、まずこの制度(せいど)を変(か)えますね。
If I were the manager, the first thing I'd change is this system.
</pre>

<pre>
この説明(せつめい)が正(ただ)しい<b>とすれば</b>、原因(げんいん)は一(ひと)つしか考(かんが)えられない。
If this account is correct, only one cause is conceivable.
</pre>

<pre>
木村(きむら)さんがもう出(で)た<b>とすると</b>、さっき廊下(ろうか)ですれ違(ちが)ったのかな。
If Kimura's already left, then maybe that was him I passed in the corridor.
</pre>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `となると` is a fourth cousin and is *not* interchangeable. It takes a
situation that has genuinely arisen and asks what now becomes of things:
来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)となると、準備(じゅんび)が間(ま)に合(あ)わない。
Where `としたら` supposes, `となると` concedes.

</div>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)としたら、中止(ちゅうし)です。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>だ</b>としたら、中止(ちゅうし)です。
  Nouns and な-adjectives take `だ` before `と`. `なら` is the one that drops it, and
  learners routinely apply the wrong half of the pair.

- ❌ 安(やす)い<b>だ</b>とすれば買(か)います。 → ✅ 安(やす)いとすれば買(か)います。
  い-adjectives are already complete predicates. No `だ`.

- ❌ 家(いえ)に着(つ)いたとしたら、連絡(れんらく)してください。 → ✅ 家(いえ)に着(つ)いたら、連絡(れんらく)してください。
  Arriving home is expected, not supposed, and the main clause is a request. Both
  point away from `としたら` and towards plain `たら`.

- ❌ 春(はる)になるとしたら、桜(さくら)が咲(さ)きます。 → ✅ 春(はる)になると、桜(さくら)が咲(さ)きます。
  A regularity that always holds is `と`. Nothing is being supposed.

## Practice

**1.** 仮(かり)に明日(あした)大雪(おおゆき)____、学校(がっこう)はどうなりますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大雪(おおゆき)<b>だとしたら</b>（or <b>だとすれば</b>）

The clause ends in a noun, so `だ` is obligatory before `と`. The main clause is a
question about consequences, which is exactly the slot this pattern was built for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 荷物(にもつ)が届(とど)い（たら・たとしたら）、すぐ開(あ)けてみてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

荷物(にもつ)が届(とど)い<b>たら</b>、すぐ開(あ)けてみてください。

The parcel is on its way - this is an expected event, not a hypothesis - and the main
clause is a request. `としたら` refuses both of those.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If this report is accurate, we'll have to change the plan."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この報告(ほうこく)が正(ただ)しい<b>とすれば</b>、計画(けいかく)を変(か)えなければなりません。

`とすれば` is the reasoning member of the family and fits a written or workplace
inference. The main clause states what necessarily follows, which is the natural
partner for this pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)としたら、どうして逃(に)げなかったんだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)<b>だとしたら</b>、どうして逃(に)げなかったんだろう。

<code>犯人(はんにん)</code> is a noun, so `だ` has to appear. Everything else about the
sentence is right: a supposition followed by a puzzled question is a textbook use.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 留学(りゅうがく)する<b>なら</b>、アメリカがいいですよ。
(b) 留学(りゅうがく)する<b>としたら</b>、アメリカがいいですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) responds to a plan the listener has already announced. You have heard that they
intend to study abroad, and you are advising them where to go - hence the
listener-directed `よ`.

(b) entertains the idea without attributing it to anyone. The speaker is weighing a
hypothetical, quite possibly about themselves, and `ね` invites the listener to weigh
it too. Nobody has necessarily decided anything.

</details>

# ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば

**Meaning** · if only A, then B - A named as the single condition that is enough on its own

`さえ` picks out **one** thing and the `ば`-clause says that this one thing is all it
takes. Everything else is waved away: it may be missing, it may be against you, it
does not matter. The main clause then reports that the result is assured - or, in the
past, that it *would have been*, which is why this pattern is also how Japanese sighs
over a small thing that was not done.

## Formation

Two halves have to arrive together: the particle `さえ` on the crucial element, and a
`ば`-form on the verb or adjective that governs it. Which helper you need depends on
what the crucial element is.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (subject or object) | Noun + さえ + ば-form（が・を drop） | お金(かね)がある → お金(かね)さえあれば |
| Noun (other particle) | Particle + さえ + ば-form | 駅(えき)に着(つ)く → 駅(えき)にさえ着(つ)けば |
| Verb (action) | ます-stem + さえすれば | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)みさえすれば |
| Verb (ongoing state) | て-form + さえいれば | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ってさえいれば |
| Verb (negative) | ます-stem + さえしなければ | 言(い)う → 言(い)いさえしなければ |
| Noun + する verb | Noun + さえすれば | 練習(れんしゅう)する → 練習(れんしゅう)さえすれば |
| い-adjective | く + さえあれば | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くさえあれば |
| な-adjective | で + さえあれば | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かでさえあれば |
| Noun (predicate) | で + さえあれば | 本物(ほんもの) → 本物(ほんもの)でさえあれば |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さえ` **replaces** `が` and `を`, and **follows** every other
particle. ❌<code>お金(かね)がさえ</code>, ❌<code>これをさえ</code>; but
<code>駅(えき)にさえ</code>, <code>ここでさえ</code>, <code>友(とも)だちからさえ</code>
are all fine.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
お金(かね)<b>さえあれば</b>、問題(もんだい)ないんだけどな。
If only we had the money, there'd be no problem.
</pre>

<pre>
これ<b>さえ覚(おぼ)えれば</b>、テストは大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですよ。
Learn just this and you'll be fine on the test.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)<b>さえよければ</b>、明日(あした)の朝(あさ)出発(しゅっぱつ)できます。
As long as the weather holds, we can set off tomorrow morning.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)か<b>でさえあれば</b>、部屋(へや)は狭(せま)くてもかまいません。
So long as it's quiet, I don't mind the room being small.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)み<b>さえすれば</b>、だれでも組(く)み立(た)てられますよ。
Anyone can put it together, as long as they actually read the instructions.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)ん<b>でさえいれば</b>、熱(ねつ)はもっと早(はや)く下(さ)がっていたはずです。
If you'd just kept taking the medicine, the fever would have come down sooner.
</pre>

<pre>
あなたが元気(げんき)<b>でさえいてくれれば</b>、ほかには何(なに)も望(のぞ)みません。
As long as you stay well, I don't ask for anything else.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)り<b>さえ守(まも)ってくれれば</b>、やり方(かた)はそちらに任(まか)せます。
Provided you hit the deadline, I'll leave the method entirely to you.
</pre>

<pre>
あの時(とき)、一言(ひとこと)謝(あやま)り<b>さえすれば</b>、こんな大(おお)ごとにはならなかったのに。
If I had just said one word of apology back then, none of this would have blown up.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral and extremely common in speech, but it always carries
an attitude, and the attitude changes with the tense of the main clause.

With a **non-past** main clause it is reassuring or businesslike: here is the one
hurdle, clear it and the rest takes care of itself. This is the tone of instructions,
of a manager delegating, of somebody talking a nervous friend down - 
落(お)ち着(つ)きさえすれば、できるよ。

With a **past** main clause, and especially with `のに` or `んだけど` hanging off the
end, it turns wistful or accusing. The clause names a trivially small thing that was
not done, and the main clause names the disaster that followed. Said about yourself it
is regret; said about the person in front of you it is a reproach, and a fairly sharp
one - 連絡(れんらく)さえしてくれれば is not a neutral remark. Soften it, or say it
about the situation rather than the person.

Two spoken habits worth knowing. First, the `ます`-stem construction
(<code>読(よ)みさえすれば</code>) sounds slightly emphatic and bookish; in casual
speech people more often hang `さえ` on a noun and leave the verb alone
(<code>それさえ読(よ)めば</code>). Second, `すら` is a near-synonym of `さえ` in the
"even" sense but is confined to writing and almost never appears in this
minimum-condition frame - do not swap them.

## Contrast

### ～さえ○○ば vs plain ～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `ば` says *this is a condition*. `さえ…ば` says *this is the
only condition*. Removing `さえ` costs you the whole point of the sentence.

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)が<b>あれば</b>、手伝(てつだ)います。
If I have time, I'll help.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)<b>さえあれば</b>、手伝(てつだ)えるんですが。
It's purely a question of time - if I had it, I could help.
</pre>

The second sentence claims that willingness, skill and permission are all already in
place, so that time is the last thing standing in the way. The first claims nothing of
the sort. Notice too that `が` disappears once `さえ` arrives.

### ～さえ○○ば vs ～だけで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけ` counts; `さえ` selects. `だけで` says the amount is small;
`さえ…ば` says this particular thing is the one that matters, however large it is.

</div>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)を書(か)く<b>だけで</b>参加(さんか)できます。
You can take part just by writing your name.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)<b>さえ書(か)けば</b>、あとはこちらでやっておきます。
Once you've put your name down, we'll take care of the rest.
</pre>

`だけで` is about how little effort is involved and is a natural thing to put in an
advertisement. `さえ…ば` is about clearing the one remaining obstacle, and it implies a
list of other things that are already settled. That is also why `さえ…ば` can take a
heavy condition - 一億円(いちおくえん)さえあれば - where `だけで` would sound absurd.

### ～さえ○○ば vs さえ meaning "even"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same particle, opposite ends of the scale. Without a `ば` after it,
`さえ` picks an **extreme** case ("even X"). With a `ば` after it, it picks the
**minimum** sufficient case ("if only X").

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>さえ</b>知(し)っている言葉(ことば)です。
It's a word even children know.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>さえ静(しず)かにしていれば</b>、いい店(みせ)なんですけどね。
It's a lovely restaurant, as long as the children keep quiet.
</pre>

The first ranks children at the bottom of a scale of knowledge and says the word
reaches even there. The second says the children are the sole problem. The `ば` is what
flips the reading, so leaving it off does not weaken the sentence - it changes it into
a different one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ お金(かね)<b>が</b>さえあれば → ✅ お金(かね)さえあれば
  `さえ` takes the place of `が` and `を`. It stacks only on `に`, `で`, `から`, `と` and
  the like.

- ❌ 来(く)るさえすれば → ✅ 来(き)さえすれば
  <code>さえする</code> attaches to the `ます`-stem, never the dictionary form. For a
  `する` verb built on a noun, put `さえ` after the noun instead:
  連絡(れんらく)さえすれば.

- ❌ 安(やす)いさえあれば買(か)います。 → ✅ 安(やす)くさえあれば買(か)います。
  An い-adjective goes into its `く` form before <code>さえある</code>, exactly as it
  does before <code>ない</code>.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)さえある、終(お)わります。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)さえあれば、終(お)わります。
  The `ば` is not decoration. Without it the sentence says "even time exists", which is
  not what anybody means.

- ❌ この書類(しょるい)さえ出(だ)せば、受付(うけつけ)に行(い)ってください。 → ✅ この書類(しょるい)を出(だ)したら、受付(うけつけ)に行(い)ってください。
  A sufficient-condition clause is a claim about what will follow, not a step in a set
  of instructions. Like plain `ば` with an action verb, it does not take a request or a
  command after it.

## Practice

**1.** パスポート____、あとは何(なに)もいりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

パスポート<b>さえあれば</b>

The sentence lists one requirement and dismisses everything else, which is the core
job of this pattern. `が` disappears in front of `さえ`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この本(ほん)を（読(よ)むだけで・読(よ)みさえすれば）、全体(ぜんたい)の流(なが)れが分(わ)かります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are grammatical, but they say different things.
<code>読(よ)むだけで</code> advertises how little work it is; <code>読(よ)みさえすれば</code>
implies you have been putting it off and the reading is the one thing left to do. Pick
the second if the sentence is aimed at somebody who has not opened the book yet.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If only he'd told me earlier, I could have changed the booking."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もっと早(はや)く言(い)ってくれ<b>さえすれば</b>、予約(よやく)を変(か)えられたのに。

A past counterfactual with `のに` is the standard shape for this feeling. Note that
<code>くれる</code> is what makes the complaint land on him rather than on the
situation.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部屋(へや)が明(あか)るいさえあれば、古(ふる)くてもいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部屋(へや)が明(あか)る<b>くさえあれば</b>、古(ふる)くてもいいです。

An い-adjective must become `く` before <code>さえある</code>. A tidier version moves
the particle too: 部屋(へや)さえ明(あか)るければ、古(ふる)くてもいいです。

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)<b>さえ</b>来(こ)なかった。
(b) 彼(かれ)<b>さえ来(こ)なければ</b>、楽(たの)しい会(かい)だったのに。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the "even" `さえ`: of all the people who might have failed to show up, even he
did - he was the last person you expected to miss it.

(b) is the sufficient-condition `さえ…ば`: everything else about the party was fine, and
his presence was the single thing that spoiled it. The `ば` and the counterfactual
`のに` together turn the same particle into a complaint about one man.

</details>

# ～ばいい

**Meaning** · that's all you have to do; you should just ~; it's fine if ~

`ば` names a condition and `いい` says that meeting it is **enough**. Put together they
give advice not by ordering someone about but by pointing at the one action that will
settle the matter: *do that, and we're done*. The same shape asks the question in
reverse - <code>どうすればいいですか</code>, "what would be enough here?" - which is the
single most useful sentence in the book when you are lost.

## Formation

The first half is the ordinary `ば`-form. Nouns and な-adjectives use `なら`, since
`なら` is the copula's `ば`-form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ば-form + いい | 押(お)す → 押(お)せばいい |
| Verb (る-verb) | れば + いい | 入(い)れる → 入(い)れればいい |
| Verb (negative) | なければ + いい | 触(さわ)る → 触(さわ)らなければいい |
| い-adjective | ければ + いい | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)ければいい |
| な-adjective | stem + なら + いい | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かならいい |
| Noun | Noun + なら + いい | 明日(あした) → 明日(あした)ならいい |
| Polite | ばいいです／ばいいと思(おも)います | 押(お)せばいいです |
| Explanatory | ばいいんです | 押(お)せばいいんです |
| Asking | どうすればいいですか | |
| Past (regret) | ばよかった | 押(お)せばよかった |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the polite form goes on the **end**: 押(お)せばいいです, never
❌<code>押(お)しますればいい</code>. A `ば`-clause has no polite form of its own.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
分(わ)からなければ、先生(せんせい)に<b>聞(き)けばいい</b>よ。
If you don't know, just ask the teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
ここに名前(なまえ)を<b>書(か)けばいい</b>ですか。
Do I just write my name here?
</pre>

<pre>
寒(さむ)かったら、そこの毛布(もうふ)を<b>使(つか)えばいい</b>からね。
If you get cold, just use that blanket over there.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)に迷(まよ)ったときは、どう<b>すればいい</b>ですか。
What should I do if I get lost?
</pre>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)覚(おぼ)えなくても、大事(だいじ)なところだけ<b>見(み)ておけばいい</b>んです。
You don't have to memorise all of it - going over the important bits is enough.
</pre>

<pre>
文句(もんく)があるなら、自分(じぶん)で<b>やればいい</b>じゃないか。
If you've got complaints, do it yourself then.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)は急(いそ)ぎませんので、来週(らいしゅう)までに<b>決(き)めていただければいい</b>です。
There's no rush on the reply - it's fine if you decide by next week.
</pre>

<pre>
コピーで受(う)け付(つ)けますから、原本(げんぽん)は後(あと)から<b>送(おく)っていただければいい</b>んですよ。
We'll accept a copy, so you can just send the original on afterwards.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ばいい` is short, and short can be cold. Said flatly to somebody who has asked for
help, 自分(じぶん)で調(しら)べればいい is not friendly advice - it means "look it
up yourself, then." The pattern's structure is partly to blame: naming the sufficient
action implies the listener could have worked it out. Softeners matter more here than
with most patterns.

The usual repairs, roughly from casual to careful: `ばいいよ` (warm, to a friend),
`ばいいんじゃない?` (a suggestion offered rather than handed down), `ばいいですよ`,
<code>ばいいと思(おも)います</code>, and - with a superior or a customer - dropping the pattern
entirely for <code>～ていただければと思(おも)います</code>. Telling your section head
「メールで送(おく)ればいいです」 is technically an answer to their question and still
lands badly.

There is a deliberately dismissive use, and you should recognise it even if you never
say it: 勝手(かって)にすればいい, 帰(かえ)りたければ帰(かえ)ればいい. Attached
to a `～たければ` clause and delivered flatly, `ばいい` means "suit yourself" and is a
way of ending a conversation badly.

Two everyday frames run in the opposite direction and are entirely safe. `ばいいんです`
is the reassuring "all you have to do is…" of a helpful colleague. And
<code>どうすればいいですか</code> is the standard polite request for instructions - 
neutral, businesslike, usable with anyone.

Finally, note what the past tense does. `ばいい` has **no** ❌<code>ばいいでした</code>.
Its past is `ばよかった`, and that form means regret, not advice.

## Contrast

### ～ばいい vs ～たらいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばいい` presents the action as *sufficient*: that, and nothing
more, will do it. `たらいい` presents it as a *suggestion*: how about doing that? The
first is closer to a solution, the second to a proposal.

</div>

<pre>
きっぷは券売機(けんばいき)で<b>買(か)えばいい</b>んです。
You buy your ticket at the machine - that's all there is to it.
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れてるなら、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)たらいい</b>よ。
If you're tired, why not get an early night today.
</pre>

Swapping them is not ungrammatical, but the flavour shifts: 寝(ね)ればいい sounds
like you are closing the topic, while 買(か)ったらいい sounds oddly tentative about a
fixed procedure. The same split shows in the two questions:
<code>どうすればいいですか</code> asks for the correct procedure, while
<code>どうしたらいいですか</code> more often comes from somebody genuinely at a loss.
That is a tendency rather than a rule, and either is acceptable in either situation.

### ～ばいい vs ～たほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たほうがいい` **compares**: this course is better than the other
one, and there is an implication that you might be about to choose wrongly. `ばいい`
does not compare; it simply supplies an answer.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるなら、病院(びょういん)に<b>行(い)ったほうがいい</b>よ。
If you've got a fever you'd better go to a clinic.
</pre>

<pre>
保険証(ほけんしょう)がなければ、後(あと)で<b>出(だ)せばいい</b>そうです。
Apparently if you don't have your insurance card you can just hand it in later.
</pre>

The first weighs going against not going and comes down on one side, which is why it
can sound like mild pressure. The second answers a procedural worry and applies no
pressure at all. Use `たほうがいい` when there is a real risk in the alternative.

### ～ばいい vs ～てもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てもいい` grants **permission**: doing it would not be a problem.
`ばいい` states **sufficiency**: doing it is the thing that solves this.

</div>

<pre>
この席(せき)、座(すわ)っ<b>てもいい</b>ですか。
Is it all right if I sit here?
</pre>

<pre>
席(せき)が空(あ)いていなければ、後(うし)ろに<b>立(た)てばいい</b>ですよ。
If there are no seats free, you can just stand at the back.
</pre>

English "you can" covers both, which is exactly why learners mix them up. Ask yourself
whether you are being *allowed* to do something or *told what will work*. Answering
「はい、座(すわ)ればいいです」 to a request for permission sounds brusque, almost like
"well, sit down then."

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ここに書(か)くばいいですか。 → ✅ ここに書(か)<b>けば</b>いいですか。
  `いい` follows the `ば`-form, not the dictionary form. The `ば` is part of the verb,
  not a separate particle.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かだばいいです。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)か<b>なら</b>いいです。
  Nouns and な-adjectives have no `ば`-form of their own; they borrow the copula's,
  which is `なら`.

- ❌ もっと早(はや)く出(で)ればいいでした。 → ✅ もっと早(はや)く出(で)れば<b>よかった</b>です。
  `いい` cannot be put into the past behind `ば`. The past of this pattern is
  `ばよかった`, and it means "I should have", not "it was fine to".

- ❌ (課長(かちょう)に) 「この書類(しょるい)はメールで送(おく)ればいいです。」 → ✅ 「この書類(しょるい)はメールでお送(おく)りいただければと思(おも)います。」
  Grammatical, but telling a superior what is sufficient sounds like issuing them
  instructions. Move to a `ていただければ` frame.

## Practice

**1.** 「この機械(きかい)、どう使(つか)うんですか。」「このボタンを____だけです。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このボタンを<b>押(お)せばいい</b>だけです。

The question asks for a procedure, and the answer names the one sufficient action.
<code>だけ</code> reinforces the "and nothing else" that `ばいい` already implies.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらないなら、今日(きょう)は（休(やす)めばいい・休(やす)んだほうがいい）ですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は<b>休(やす)んだほうがいい</b>ですよ。

There is a real risk in the alternative - going in with a fever - so the sentence
should weigh the two courses. `ばいい` would sound as though you were merely informing
them that taking the day off is permitted.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "What should I say when I answer the phone?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電話(でんわ)に出(で)るとき、何(なん)と<b>言(い)えばいい</b>ですか。

<code>～ばいいですか</code> is the standard way of asking for the correct procedure, and
this is a procedure question rather than a request for permission. <code>何(なん)と</code>
is the quotative `と`, not `を`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの時(とき)、田中(たなか)さんに相談(そうだん)すればいいでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの時(とき)、田中(たなか)さんに相談(そうだん)すれば<b>よかった</b>です。

`ばいい` has no past form; you put `いい` itself into the past. The result no longer
means "it was enough to consult him" but "I wish I had consulted him", which is what
the past of this pattern always means.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 嫌(いや)なら、行(い)かなく<b>てもいい</b>よ。
(b) 嫌(いや)なら、行(い)かなけれ<b>ばいい</b>じゃない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) releases the listener from an obligation: if you don't want to go, you are not
required to. It is kind, and the speaker is taking responsibility for the permission.

(b) hands the problem back. It says the solution was obvious all along - just don't go
 - and with `じゃない` on the end it carries a distinct edge of impatience. The same
words in a gentler voice can be genuinely helpful, but the default reading is a
brush-off.

</details>

# ～たらいい / ～といい - should

**Meaning** · you should ~; why not ~ - advice given by describing a good outcome rather than issuing an order

Read the words literally and they say *if you did it, that would be good*. That is the
whole trick. Instead of telling somebody what to do, you sketch a small hypothetical
world in which they have already done it, and pronounce that world good. The listener
is left to draw the obvious conclusion, which is far easier to accept than an
instruction.

This page covers the advice reading only. The identical string also means "I hope" and
"it would be nice if"; what tells them apart is **who acts** and **whether they can**.

## Formation

The advice reading is essentially a **verb** pattern, and the verb has to be something
the listener could actually decide to do.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain past + ら + いい | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだらいい |
| Verb | plain non-past + と + いい | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)むといい |
| Verb (る-verb) | plain past + ら + いい | 調(しら)べる → 調(しら)べたらいい |
| Verb (する) | したら + いい／すると + いい | 相談(そうだん)する → 相談(そうだん)したらいい |
| Polite | たらいいです／といいですよ | 休(やす)んだらいいですよ |
| Softened | たらいいと思(おも)います | 休(やす)んだらいいと思(おも)います |
| Softened further | たらいいんじゃないですか | 休(やす)んだらいいんじゃないですか |
| Asking for advice | どうしたらいいですか | |

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no negative in this table on purpose. <code>無理(むり)しないと
いい</code> does not mean "you shouldn't overdo it"; it is heard as a hope that you
won't. Negative advice belongs to <code>ないほうがいい</code>. This is the commonest
place where the advice reading quietly turns into the hope reading behind a learner's
back.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いなら、この薬(くすり)を<b>飲(の)むといい</b>よ。
If your head hurts, take this - it works.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で悩(なや)まないで、先生(せんせい)に<b>相談(そうだん)したらいい</b>ですよ。
Don't sit there worrying on your own - go and talk to your teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)は毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ<b>書(か)くといい</b>ですよ。
With kanji you're best off writing a little every day.
</pre>

<pre>
お土産(みやげ)なら、駅(えき)の地下(ちか)で<b>買(か)ったらいい</b>と思(おも)います。
If it's souvenirs you're after, I'd buy them in the basement floor at the station.
</pre>

<pre>
迷(まよ)っているなら、いちど見(み)に<b>行(い)ってみたらいい</b>んじゃないですか。
If you're torn about it, why not just go and have a look once?
</pre>

<pre>
敬語(けいご)が苦手(にがて)なら、まずドラマの会話(かいわ)をまねして<b>覚(おぼ)えるといい</b>。
If polite language is your weak spot, start by copying the dialogue in dramas.
</pre>

<pre>
将来(しょうらい)のことを急(いそ)いで決(き)める必要(ひつよう)はない。三年(さんねん)ほど働(はたら)いてから<b>考(かんが)えたらいい</b>。
There's no rush to settle your future. Work for three years or so and think about it then.
</pre>

<pre>
パソコンが重(おも)いなら、要(い)らないアプリを<b>消(け)してみたらいい</b>ですよ。驚(おどろ)くほど速(はや)くなります。
If your laptop is sluggish, try deleting the apps you don't use. It gets startlingly faster.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Because the pattern hides advice inside a conditional, it is gentler than
<code>～なさい</code> or the plain imperative, and slightly gentler than
<code>～たほうがいい</code>. It is the register of a helpful colleague, a friendly
teacher, a shop assistant who has decided to be kind to you.

`たらいい` and `といい` are close, with a small difference of angle. `たらいい` responds
to *this* person's situation - the conditional takes their circumstances as read.
`といい` states something closer to a general good practice, which is why it turns up so
often in teacherly and written advice: 辞書(じしょ)はときどき引(ひ)くといい。 Both
are entirely ordinary in speech.

The pattern is still advice, and advice flows downhill in Japanese. To a friend or a
junior, 休(やす)んだらいいよ is warm. To your section head the same words are
presumptuous, and you climb the ladder of hedges instead:
<code>たらいいと思(おも)います</code> → `たらいいんじゃないでしょうか` → dropping the pattern
altogether for <code>～てはいかがでしょうか</code>.

`たらいいでしょう` deserves a warning. It is grammatical, but it lands as distant and
lecturing - a teacher addressing a hall rather than a person addressing a person. In
conversation prefer `たらいいですよ`.

Two things do **not** happen. `いい` here is an い-adjective, so it never takes `だ`
(❌<code>たらいいだ</code>). And the pattern has no past form of its own: putting `いい`
into the past gives you `たらよかった`, which is regret, not advice.

## Contrast

### ～たらいい vs ～ばいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばいい` names a **sufficient** action: do that, and the matter is
settled. `たらいい` makes a **suggestion**: how about doing that? The first closes a
question, the second opens one.

</div>

<pre>
きっぷは自動(じどう)券売機(けんばいき)で<b>買(か)えばいい</b>んです。
You buy the ticket from the machine - that's all there is to it.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)があるなら、窓口(まどぐち)で<b>聞(き)いてみたらいい</b>ですよ。
If you've got time, you might try asking at the counter.
</pre>

Neither swap is ungrammatical, but the tone moves. `ばいい` on a suggestion can sound
like you are shutting the conversation down; `たらいい` on a fixed procedure sounds
oddly unsure of a thing everybody knows.

### ～たらいい vs ～たほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たほうがいい` weighs this course against the alternative and warns
you off the other one. `たらいい` does not weigh anything; it simply offers an idea.

</div>

<pre>
夜中(よなか)に一人(ひとり)であの道(みち)を<b>通(とお)らないほうがいい</b>よ。
You'd better not take that road alone at night.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がかかってもいいなら、あの道(みち)を<b>通(とお)ったらいい</b>よ。
If you don't mind it taking longer, take that road.
</pre>

The first implies a risk; the second implies a choice. That is also why `たほうがいい`
has a natural negative and `たらいい` does not: warning somebody off is exactly the job
`たほうがいい` was built for.

### The advice reading vs the "I hope" reading

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the listener could decide to do it, the sentence is advice. If
nobody in the room controls it, the sentence is a hope. Same words, and the test is
about the world, not the grammar.

</div>

<pre>
早(はや)めに<b>予約(よやく)したらいい</b>ですよ。
You'd do well to book early.
</pre>

<pre>
席(せき)が<b>空(あ)いているといい</b>ですね。
I hope there are seats free.
</pre>

Booking is up to the listener, so the first is advice. Whether seats are free is up to
nobody, so the second is a hope - and note that hopes take `ですね`, which invites the
listener to share the feeling, while advice takes `ですよ`, which hands them something
they did not have. The final particle is often the fastest clue.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 少(すこ)し休(やす)むたらいいですよ。 → ✅ 少(すこ)し休(やす)<b>んだら</b>いいですよ。
  `たら` attaches to the plain **past**. The dictionary form goes with `と`, not with
  `たら`.

- ❌ 早(はや)く寝(ね)たらいいだ。 → ✅ 早(はや)く寝(ね)たらいい。
  `いい` is an い-adjective and is already a complete predicate. No `だ` - in the polite
  form it becomes `いいです`.

- ❌ 無理(むり)しないといいですよ。（meaning "you shouldn't push yourself"） → ✅ 無理(むり)しない<b>ほうが</b>いいですよ。
  A negative clause in front of `といい` is read as a wish, so your advice turns into "I
  hope you don't", which is not what you meant.

- ❌ (部長(ぶちょう)に)「この方法(ほうほう)で進(すす)めたらいいです。」 → ✅ 「この方法(ほうほう)で進(すす)めてはいかがでしょうか。」
  The grammar is fine; the direction is not. Advice aimed upwards needs a different
  frame, not just a `です` on the end.

## Practice

**1.** 日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)になりたいなら、毎日(まいにち)声(こえ)に出(だ)して____いいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Reading aloud is something the listener can decide to do, so this is the advice
reading. Either conditional works; `と` sounds a little more like a general
recommendation.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 傘(かさ)を持(も)っていないんですか。じゃあ、コンビニで（買(か)えばいい・買(か)ったらいい）ですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are used, but <b>買(か)えばいい</b>ですよ fits best.

The listener has a concrete problem with one obvious fix, and `ばいい` is the form that
names a sufficient solution. <code>買(か)ったらいい</code> is not wrong, but it
frames an obvious remedy as a tentative idea.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If you're nervous, you should practise in front of a mirror."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

緊張(きんちょう)するなら、鏡(かがみ)の前(まえ)で<b>練習(れんしゅう)したらいい</b>ですよ。

The action is fully under the listener's control, so this is advice rather than hope,
and `ですよ` is the particle that goes with handing somebody a new idea.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 風邪(かぜ)が治(なお)らないなら、薬(くすり)を飲(の)まないといいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 風邪(かぜ)が治(なお)らないなら、病院(びょういん)に行(い)ったらいいですよ。

<code>飲(の)まないといい</code> would be a hope that the person avoids medicine,
which makes no sense as advice for a cold that won't clear. If you really do want
negative advice, the form is <code>飲(の)まないほうがいい</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)、早(はや)く<b>来(き)たらいい</b>ですよ。
(b) 明日(あした)、早(はや)く<b>来(こ)られるといい</b>ですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is advice. Coming early is the listener's decision, and `ですよ` presents the idea
as something the speaker is giving them.

(b) is a hope. The potential form <code>来(こ)られる</code> puts the outcome partly
outside the listener's hands - traffic, work, whatever - so the speaker can only wish
for it, and `ですね` invites the listener to wish along. Swapping the two particles
would make both sentences sound slightly off.

</details>

# ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope

**Meaning** · I hope ~ ; here's hoping ~ - a wish about an outcome nobody in the conversation controls

Same words as the advice pattern, different job. The conditional still sketches a world
and calls it good; what changes is that neither speaker nor listener can bring that
world about. The weather, an exam result, somebody's recovery, whether the train is on
time - once the outcome is out of human hands, "you should" is impossible and the only
reading left is **hope**.

This is the everyday Japanese for "I hope so", and the fixed phrase
<code>～といいですね</code> is worth learning as a single unit.

## Formation

Unlike the advice reading, the hope reading takes adjectives and nouns as happily as
verbs, because a hope can be about a *state*.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain non-past + と + いい | 晴(は)れる → 晴(は)れるといい |
| Verb | plain past + ら + いい | 晴(は)れる → 晴(は)れたらいい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + と + いい | 降(ふ)らない → 降(ふ)らないといい |
| Verb (result already fixed) | ている／ていた + と + いい | 受(う)かる → 受(う)かっているといい |
| い-adjective | plain form + と + いい | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いといい |
| な-adjective | stem + **だ** + と + いい | 元気(げんき) → 元気(げんき)だといい |
| Noun | Noun + **だ** + と + いい | いい天気(てんき) → いい天気(てんき)だといい |
| Hope for the listener | ～といいですね／～といいね | 受(う)かるといいですね |
| Hope of your own | ～といいんですが／～といいのですが | 間(ま)に合(あ)うといいんですが |
| To yourself | ～といいなあ／～たらいいなあ | 晴(は)れるといいなあ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - before `と`, nouns and な-adjectives need `だ`. This is the same `と`
you met as a conditional at N4, and it has the same requirement: ❌<code>いい天気(てんき)といい</code>,
✅<code>いい天気(てんき)だといい</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)、<b>晴(は)れるといい</b>ですね。
I hope it's fine tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)、<b>うまくいくといい</b>ね。
I hope the exam goes well.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)らないといい</b>んですが。
I'm hoping it doesn't rain at the weekend.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいクラスに早(はや)く<b>慣(な)れるといい</b>ですね。
I hope she settles into the new class quickly.
</pre>

<pre>
おばあちゃんの足(あし)が<b>早(はや)くよくなるといい</b>なあ。
I do hope Grandma's leg gets better soon.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)が行(い)きたがっていた大学(だいがく)に<b>受(う)かっているといい</b>んですけど。
I hope my son's got into the university he had his heart set on.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの日(ひ)が<b>いい天気(てんき)だといい</b>んですが、今(いま)のところ予報(よほう)は微妙(びみょう)です。
I hope it's fine on moving day next week, though the forecast is iffy so far.
</pre>

<pre>
退院(たいいん)したばかりですから、しばらくは無理(むり)をしないで<b>ゆっくり過(す)ごせるといい</b>ですね。
He's only just out of hospital, so I hope he can take it easy for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めての海外(かいがい)出張(しゅっちょう)だそうですね。いい経験(けいけん)に<b>なるといい</b>ですね。
I hear it's your first business trip abroad. I hope it turns out to be a good experience.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is neutral and usable at every level of politeness, but **whose** hope it is
changes the ending, and getting that wrong is the most audible mistake learners make
here.

For a hope about the **listener's** situation, the ending is `ですね` (or plain `ね`).
`ね` asks the listener to share a feeling, and since the feeling is about them, that
works: 試合(しあい)に勝(か)てるといいですね。 This is the standard way of wishing
somebody luck, and it is far more common than 「がんばってください」 in careful adult
speech.

For a hope of **your own**, the natural ending trails off: `～といいんですが`,
`～といいのですが`, `～といいんですけど`. The unfinished `が`/`けど` does the work of
English "…though" - it stops the sentence from sounding like a demand.
間(ま)に合(あ)うといいんですが is modest; 間(ま)に合(あ)うといいですね about
your own train sounds like you are asking a stranger to agree with your wishes.

`なあ` is the private version. It is spoken to yourself or to somebody very close, it
draws the vowel out, and it is one of the most natural-sounding things a learner can
say: 明日(あした)休(やす)みだといいなあ。 In writing it appears mostly in dialogue
and diaries.

Two small points of substance. First, the negative `ないといい` is fully idiomatic for
hoping something **doesn't** happen - 渋滞(じゅうたい)していないといいですね - and is
the normal way to say it. Second, when the outcome is already settled but unknown to
you, put the clause into `ている`/`ていた`: 忘(わす)れずに持(も)っていってくれて
いるといいんですが. The hope is not that it will happen, but that it already did.

## Contrast

### ～といい vs ～たらいい in the hope reading

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are used and neither is wrong. `と` presents the hoped-for
thing as simply happening, and is the default in the set phrases. `たら` frames it a
little more as a condition being met, so it leans slightly further from expectation.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の旅行(りょこう)、<b>楽(たの)しいといい</b>ですね。
I hope next month's trip is fun.
</pre>

<pre>
宝(たから)くじ、<b>当(あ)たったらいい</b>なあ。
Wouldn't it be something if the lottery came in.
</pre>

The trip is a real event with a decent chance of being fun; `と` fits. Winning the
lottery is remote, and `たら` sits more comfortably with remoteness. Do not turn this
into a rule - <code>当(あ)たるといいなあ</code> is perfectly natural too. What is
close to fixed is the polite formula: 「～といいですね」 is heard far more often than
「～たらいいですね」.

### ～といいですね vs ～といいんですが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ですね` for somebody else's hope, `んですが` for your own. `ね`
asks the listener to agree, and you cannot ask people to agree that your wishes should
come true.

</div>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)、<b>うまくいくといい</b>ですね。
I hope your interview goes well.
</pre>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)、<b>うまくいくといい</b>んですが。
I hope my interview goes well - we'll see.
</pre>

The second is what you say about yourself, and the trailing `が` is not laziness; it is
the politeness. Ending flatly on 「うまくいくといいです」 about your own affairs sounds
blunt in a way that is hard to explain and easy to hear.

### ～といい vs ～てほしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てほしい` aims at a **person** and asks them to act. `といい` aims at
the **world** and asks nothing of anybody.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)までに返事(へんじ)を<b>もらえるといい</b>んですが。
I hope I can get an answer by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)までに返事(へんじ)を<b>もらいたい</b>んですが。
I'd like an answer by tomorrow, please.
</pre>

The first leaves the other party free; the second is a request wearing a soft coat.
Said to the person who owes you the answer, `といい` is a hint and `てほしい`／`たい` is a
push. Choosing the wrong one either fails to get you your answer or annoys the person
who has to give it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)、いい天気(てんき)といいですね。 → ✅ 明日(あした)、いい天気(てんき)<b>だ</b>といいですね。
  A noun needs `だ` before this `と`. The same goes for な-adjectives:
  元気(げんき)だといいですね.

- ❌ 私(わたし)の荷物(にもつ)が無事(ぶじ)に着(つ)くといいですね。 → ✅ …着(つ)くといい<b>んですが</b>。
  `ですね` invites agreement, which only works when the hope is about the listener.
  About your own luggage it sounds like you are recruiting them into your worry.

- ❌ 手伝(てつだ)ってくれるといいです。（meaning "I want you to help"） → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)って<b>ほしい</b>です。
  Said to the person who could help, a hope is not a request. Use `てほしい` when you
  actually want them to act.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らないといいだね。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らないといいね。
  `いい` is an い-adjective and never takes `だ` in front of `ね`.

## Practice

**1.** 「来週(らいしゅう)、北海道(ほっかいどう)に行(い)くんです。」「そうですか。雪(ゆき)が____ですね。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

雪(ゆき)が<b>見(み)られるといい</b>ですね。

The hope is about the listener's trip and about something no one controls, so `といい`
plus `ですね` is exactly right. `ですね` is what makes it a wish *for them* rather than a
remark about yourself.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)の発表(はっぴょう)、（練習(れんしゅう)したらいいですよ・うまくいくといいですね）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are natural, but they do different jobs.
<code>練習(れんしゅう)したらいいですよ</code> is advice about something the listener
controls; <code>うまくいくといいですね</code> is a hope about how it turns out. If you
are seeing them off on the day, only the second one still makes sense.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I hope the parcel arrives before Friday, though."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

金曜日(きんようび)までに荷物(にもつ)が<b>届(とど)くといい</b>んですが。

The hope is the speaker's own, so it takes the trailing `んですが` rather than `ですね`.
The English "though" is doing the same softening work as the Japanese `が`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 息子(むすこ)が試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)するといいですねと思(おも)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 息子(むすこ)が試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)すると<b>いいなと思(おも)っています</b>。

`ですね` addresses the listener, so it cannot sit inside a quoted thought. Inside
<code>と思(おも)う</code> the clause goes plain, and `な` is the particle that carries
the wistfulness.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 早(はや)く元気(げんき)に<b>なるといい</b>ですね。
(b) 早(はや)く元気(げんき)に<b>なってほしい</b>です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a hope handed to the person you are talking to, or to somebody's family on their
behalf. It asks nothing of anyone and is the safe thing to say to a patient or their
relatives.

(b) puts the recovery on the other person as something you want from them. Between
close family that reads as affection; said to a colleague who is ill it can sound like
you need them back at work. The grammar is fine either way - it is the direction of the
wish that differs.

</details>

# ～たらいい / ～といい - it would be nice if

**Meaning** · it would be nice if ~; I wish ~ were the case - a wish aimed at something that is not so and probably won't be

The third reading of the same words. Advice supposes the listener will act; hope
supposes the world might oblige. This one supposes **neither**. The clause describes a
state that is not this one - a bigger flat, a shorter commute, a father who listens - 
and the speaker looks at it and sighs.

Nothing in the conjugation marks the difference. What marks it is `のに` (and its
spoken relatives `なあ`, `んだけど`), which quietly supplies the second half of the
thought: *…but it isn't*.

## Formation

The wish reading takes anything that can be a state, and normally carries `のに` or
`なあ` on the end. Without one of those, the sentence usually slides back into the plain
"I hope" reading.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain past + ら + いい + のに | 飛(と)べる → 飛(と)べたらいいのに |
| Verb | plain non-past + と + いい + のに | ある → あるといいのに |
| Verb (potential) | ～えたら + いい + のに | 住(す)める → 住(す)めたらいいのに |
| い-adjective | かったら + いい + のに | 広(ひろ)い → 広(ひろ)かったらいいのに |
| な-adjective | stem + だったら + いい + のに | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)だったらいいのに |
| Noun | Noun + だったら + いい + のに | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みだったらいいのに |
| To yourself | ～のになあ／～のにな | 広(ひろ)かったらいいのになあ |
| Trailing off in speech | ～んだけど／～んですが | 住(す)めたらいいんだけど |
| Polite | ～たらいいのですが | 住(す)めたらいいのですが |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` is what turns a hope into a wish. 晴(は)れるといいですね
expects rain to be avoidable; 晴(は)れたらいいのに says you have already looked at the
sky and given up.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し部屋(へや)が<b>広(ひろ)かったらいい</b>のになあ。
I wish this room were a bit bigger.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)が<b>金曜日(きんようび)だったらいい</b>のに。
If only today were Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
家(いえ)から駅(えき)まで、もっと<b>近(ちか)かったらいい</b>んだけどね。
It'd be nice if it weren't so far from the house to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)に大(おお)きい本屋(ほんや)が<b>あるといい</b>のになあ。
I wish there were a proper bookshop round here.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)がもう少(すこ)し人(ひと)の話(はなし)を<b>聞(き)いてくれたらいい</b>のに。
I wish my father would listen to people a bit more.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)を止(と)めることが<b>できたらいい</b>のに、と何度(なんど)思(おも)ったか分(わ)からない。
I've lost count of how many times I've wished I could stop time.
</pre>

<pre>
通勤(つうきん)に一時間半(いちじかんはん)もかかる。会社(かいしゃ)のそばに<b>住(す)めたらいい</b>んだけど、家賃(やちん)が高(たか)すぎる。
My commute takes an hour and a half. I'd love to live near the office, but the rent is impossible.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの猫(ねこ)が何(なに)を考(かんが)えているのか、<b>話(はな)せたらいい</b>のになあといつも思(おも)う。
I always find myself wishing our cat could just tell me what he's thinking.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the emotional member of the family, and it sounds like it. `のに` on its own is
already a small complaint; `のになあ` drawn out is a sigh; `んだけど` trailing away is
the resigned version that adults use in conversation. All of them are casual, and none
of them belongs in a report.

The polite equivalent is `～たらいいのですが`, which sounds measured rather than wistful,
and is what you would write in an email. Note that `～のに` does not politely become
`～のにです`; if you need politeness, the sentence has to be rebuilt.

Direction matters more here than in the other two readings. Aimed at yourself or at the
world, `のに` is harmless. Aimed at a **person present**, it becomes a reproach:
早(はや)く言(い)ってくれたらいいのに is not a wish, it is a complaint that they
didn't. That can be affectionate between friends and is close to insubordinate said
upwards at work. If a superior did something inconvenient, do not reach for this
pattern.

One consequence of the "…but it isn't" is worth spelling out: using `のに` about
something that could still easily happen sounds defeatist, and occasionally rude.
Saying 「明日(あした)のピクニック、晴(は)れたらいいのに」 to the person who
organised it tells them you have written the day off. The live-hope form
「晴(は)れるといいですね」 is what you actually want.

Finally, note that the wish reading loves the **potential** form - 
話(はな)せたら, 住(す)めたら, 飛(と)べたら. That is not an accident: the things
we wish for are usually things we cannot do, and the potential is where that shows up
in the grammar.

## Contrast

### ～たらいいのに vs ～といいですね

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the difference is **expectation**, not form. `といいですね` treats
the outcome as still open; `たらいいのに` treats it as closed and disappointing.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の試合(しあい)、<b>勝(か)てるといい</b>ですね。
I hope we can win next week's match.
</pre>

<pre>
うちのチームにもう一人(ひとり)いい選手(せんしゅ)が<b>いたらいい</b>のに。
If only our team had one more decent player.
</pre>

The match has not been played, so hope is available. The squad is what it is, so only a
wish is. Learners who use `のに` for every wish end up sounding pessimistic about
perfectly ordinary plans.

### ～たらいいのに vs ～たい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たい` is for what **you** could go and do. `たらいいのに` is for
what you cannot arrange: a state of the world, somebody else's behaviour, an ability you
do not have.

</div>

<pre>
沖縄(おきなわ)に<b>行(い)きたい</b>なあ。
I want to go to Okinawa.
</pre>

<pre>
沖縄(おきなわ)が電車(でんしゃ)で<b>行(い)けたらいい</b>のになあ。
I wish you could get to Okinawa by train.
</pre>

The first is a desire you could act on tomorrow with a plane ticket. The second wishes
the map were different. Using `たらいいのに` where `たい` belongs makes you sound oddly
helpless about your own life; using `たい` where the world is the obstacle simply
doesn't parse.

### ～たらいいのに vs ～ばよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たらいいのに` wishes about **now**: the state is wrong at this
moment. `ばよかった` regrets the **past**: a decision went the wrong way and cannot be
retaken.

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)、もっと軽(かる)<b>かったらいい</b>のに。
I wish this dictionary were lighter.
</pre>

<pre>
軽(かる)いほうを<b>買(か)えばよかった</b>。
I should have bought the lighter one.
</pre>

The dictionary's weight is a present fact you are stuck with; the purchase is a past
act you are second-guessing. If you can name the moment you would go back and change,
you want `ばよかった`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が広(ひろ)いだったらいいのに。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が広(ひろ)<b>かったら</b>いいのに。
  An い-adjective forms its own past - `ひろかった` - and never borrows `だった`.

- ❌ あたたかいスープが食(た)べたらいいなあ。 → ✅ あたたかいスープが食(た)<b>べたい</b>なあ。
  Eating soup is something you can go and do, so the desire form is `たい`. The wish
  pattern is for what you cannot arrange.

- ❌ (運動会(うんどうかい)の前日(ぜんじつ)に)「明日(あした)晴(は)れたらいいのに。」 → ✅ 「明日(あした)晴(は)れるといいですね。」
  `のに` announces that you expect it not to happen. For a genuinely open outcome, use
  the live-hope form.

- ❌ (部長(ぶちょう)に)「もっと早(はや)く教(おし)えてくれたらいいのに。」 → ✅ 「もう少(すこ)し早(はや)くお知(し)らせいただけると助(たす)かります。」
  Pointed at a person, `のに` is a complaint about what they did. Upwards, rebuild the
  sentence as a request.

The potential form is what makes this a wish about an ability the speaker does not

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) 山田(やまだ)さんも<b>来(く)ればいい</b>のに。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an open hope shared with the listener: it would be good if Yamada came too, and
he still might.

(b) says he isn't coming and the speaker thinks he should have. Directed at an absent
person it is a mild grumble; said in front of Yamada himself it is a complaint about his
decision. The `のに` is doing all of that work.

</details>

# ～ば○○のに

**Meaning** · if only A were so, B would be - and A is not, which is exactly the problem

Two pieces do the work. The `ば`-clause names a condition the speaker asserts is **not**
met; the main clause names what would follow if it were. `のに` then sits at the end and
marks the gap between those two worlds as a grievance.

This is the same `のに` you met at N4 meaning "although" - a marker of a contrast that
ought not to be there. Here the contrast is between the reality you are stuck with and
the one you can see perfectly well from where you are standing.

## Formation

The front half is an ordinary `ば`-form. The back half is a plain clause plus `のに`,
and **its tense is what tells you whether the wish is about now or about the past**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ば-form + …のに | 来(く)る → 来(く)れば…のに |
| Verb (negative) | なければ + …のに | 言(い)わない → 言(い)わなければ…のに |
| い-adjective | ければ + …のに | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)ければ…のに |
| いい | irregular | いい → よければ…のに |
| な-adjective | stem + なら + …のに | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かなら…のに |
| Noun | Noun + なら + …のに | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みなら…のに |
| Main clause - present wish | plain non-past + のに | 安(やす)ければ買(か)うのに |
| Main clause - past wish | plain past + のに | 安(やす)ければ買(か)ったのに |
| `たら` version | たら + …のに | 言(い)ってくれたら…のに |
| Truncated | ～ばいいのに／～ばよかったのに | 来(く)ればいいのに |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 安(やす)ければ買(か)う**のに** = it isn't cheap, so I'm not
buying. 安(やす)ければ買(か)った**のに** = it wasn't cheap, so I didn't. Only the
main verb moves; the `ば`-clause looks identical in both.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - at N4 you learned that a `ば`-clause with an action verb cannot be followed
by a request, a command or a statement of intention. That restriction never bites here,
because the main clause of a counterfactual is a statement about an imaginary world, not
something anyone is being asked to do. This is one of the places where `ば` is at its
most natural.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し安(やす)<b>ければ</b>買(か)う<b>のに</b>。
If it were a bit cheaper I'd buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)が<b>あれば</b>手伝(てつだ)える<b>のに</b>。
I'd help if I had the time.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)が<b>よければ</b>、ここから富士山(ふじさん)が見(み)えた<b>のに</b>。
If the weather had been better, you'd have been able to see Mt Fuji from here.
</pre>

<pre>
車(くるま)が<b>あれば</b>、こんなに荷物(にもつ)で苦労(くろう)しない<b>のに</b>。
If we had a car we wouldn't be struggling with the luggage like this.
</pre>

<pre>
もっと早(はや)く病院(びょういん)に<b>行(い)っていれば</b>、こんなに長(なが)く休(やす)まずにすんだ<b>のに</b>。
If I'd gone to the doctor sooner, I wouldn't have had to take so much time off.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで走(はし)<b>れば</b>間(ま)に合(あ)った<b>のに</b>、彼(かれ)はのんびり歩(ある)いていた。
He'd have made it if he'd run to the station, but he just strolled along.
</pre>

<pre>
一言(ひとこと)相談(そうだん)してくれ<b>れば</b>、力(ちから)になれた<b>のに</b>と今(いま)でも思(おも)う。
I still think that if she'd said one word to me, I could have helped.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)にもう一本(いっぽん)電車(でんしゃ)が通(とお)って<b>いれば</b>、若(わか)い人(ひと)もこんなに出(で)ていかなかった<b>のに</b>。
If this town had one more rail line, the young people wouldn't have left in these numbers.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`のに` is not neutral. It is the audible sound of dissatisfaction, and the sentence is
usually delivered with a fall on the last syllable and nothing after it. That is also
why the main clause is so often left out entirely - 
「もう少(すこ)し早(はや)く言(い)ってくれれば…」 - with the trailing silence doing
what `のに` would have done. Both versions are casual.

Who the complaint lands on is the thing to watch. When the unmet condition is about the
speaker or about the world, the sentence is simply regret, and nobody is hurt by it.
When the condition is about **the person you are talking to** - 「連絡(れんらく)して
くれれば迎(むか)えに行(い)ったのに」 - it is a reproach, however gently you say it.
Between friends and family that is normal and often affectionate. Upwards at work it is
close to unusable; rebuild the thought as
「次回(じかい)はご連絡(れんらく)いただければ幸(さいわ)いです」 and let the
complaint go.

There is no polite form of `のに` itself. If you need the register lifted, the sentence
becomes `～のですが` - 時間(じかん)があればお手伝(てつだ)いできるのですが - which
is measured and slightly formal, and loses most of the sting.

`ば` and `たら` are both usable in this frame and neither is wrong. `ば` is the tighter,
more reasoned one and dominates when the sentence sounds like an argument;
`たら` is a shade more conversational. `なら` is the odd one out: it can appear
(私(わたし)が社長(しゃちょう)ならこんな決定(けってい)はしないのに) but only where
the condition is genuinely a premise about identity or circumstance, not an event.

## Contrast

### ～ば○○のに vs ～ばよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばよかった` names the **action** you wish had been taken and stops
there. `ば…のに` names the **consequence** you were denied. The first is self-directed
regret; the second easily points at somebody else.

</div>

<pre>
もっと早(はや)く<b>出(で)ればよかった</b>。
I should have left earlier.
</pre>

<pre>
もっと早(はや)く<b>出(で)れば</b>、渋滞(じゅうたい)に巻(ま)き込(こ)まれなかった<b>のに</b>。
If we'd left earlier we wouldn't have got caught in the traffic.
</pre>

The first is a verdict on a decision. The second spells out what the decision cost. Note
that only the second can comfortably take a second person as its subject - turn
`ばよかった` on somebody else and you have to add `のに` anyway to make it land:
言(い)ってくれればよかったのに.

### ～ば○○のに vs ～ばいいのに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばいいのに` is about **now**: I wish this were the case, and it
still could be. `ば…のに` with a past main clause is about a chance that has closed.

</div>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんも来(く)<b>ればいいのに</b>。
I wish Yamada would come too.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんも来(く)<b>れば</b>、みんなで写真(しゃしん)が撮(と)れた<b>のに</b>。
If Yamada had come too, we could have had a photo with everyone in it.
</pre>

The first is aimed at a party that has not started; the second at one that is over. In
speech the difference is carried entirely by the tense of the main verb, so it is worth
practising the pair aloud until the switch is automatic.

### ～ば○○のに vs plain ～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - without `のに`, the condition is left open and might yet be met.
With `のに`, the speaker has asserted that it is not.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)晴(は)<b>れれば</b>、海(うみ)に行(い)きます。
If it's fine tomorrow, we'll go to the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)晴(は)<b>れれば</b>海(うみ)に行(い)ける<b>のに</b>。
We could go to the sea if only it were going to be fine tomorrow.
</pre>

The first is a plan with a condition attached. The second has already read the forecast.
That is why `のに` cannot be added to a sentence about something you are genuinely
waiting to find out - it gives away an answer you do not have.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ もっと安(やす)<b>いければ</b>買(か)うのに。 → ✅ もっと安(やす)<b>ければ</b>買(か)うのに。
  The `い` of an い-adjective is replaced, not kept. `やすい` → `やすければ`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)が休(やす)み<b>だなら</b>、海(うみ)に行(い)くのに。 → ✅ 明日(あした)が休(やす)み<b>なら</b>、海(うみ)に行(い)くのに。
  `なら` is itself the copula's `ば`-form, so no `だ` goes in front of it. Compare
  `としたら`, which requires one.

- ❌ (終(お)わった試験(しけん)について) もっと勉強(べんきょう)すれば合格(ごうかく)する<b>のに</b>。 → ✅ …合格(ごうかく)<b>した</b>のに。
  The main clause carries the time. A non-past main verb says the chance is still open,
  which contradicts an exam you have already sat.

- ❌ (部長(ぶちょう)に)「手伝(てつだ)ってくださればもっと早(はや)く終(お)わったのに。」 → ✅ 「次回(じかい)はお手(て)を貸(か)していただけると助(たす)かります。」
  Aimed upwards, `のに` is a complaint about a superior's conduct. Convert it into a
  forward-looking request.

**1.** ここに自転車(じてんしゃ)が____、駅(えき)まで５分(ふん)で行(い)けるのに。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自転車(じてんしゃ)が<b>あれば</b>、駅(えき)まで５分(ふん)で行(い)けるのに。

The speaker has no bicycle, and `のに` announces as much. The non-past main verb
<code>行(い)ける</code> keeps the wish in the present.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)電話(でんわ)をくれれば、駅(えき)まで（迎(むか)えに行(い)くのに・迎(むか)えに行(い)ったのに）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The condition is about yesterday, so the consequence has to be in the past too. A
non-past main clause would say you are still prepared to go and meet a train that has
already arrived.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If this flat had one more room, we wouldn't need to move."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The flat has the rooms it has, so `のに` is the right marker for the gap. A non-past

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 上手(じょうず)なら、私(わたし)もピアノを弾(ひ)きたいのに。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 上手(じょうず)なら、私(わたし)もピアノを<b>弾(ひ)くのに</b>。（or 弾(ひ)ける<b>のに</b>）

The main clause of a counterfactual reports what **would happen**, not what you want.
<code>弾(ひ)きたい</code> is a desire you have regardless of skill, so it does not

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 早(はや)く言(い)ってくれ<b>ればよかった</b>。
(b) 早(はや)く言(い)ってくれ<b>れば</b>、準備(じゅんび)できた<b>のに</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a bare verdict: telling me sooner would have been the better course. Without
`のに` it stays fairly mild and can even sound like the speaker blaming themselves for
not asking.

(b) supplies the damage. It says exactly what the silence cost - the speaker could have
been ready - and `のに` puts that cost on the person who stayed quiet. The second is
noticeably sharper, and between colleagues you would soften it or leave it unsaid.

</details>

# ～ばよかった

**Meaning** · I should have ~; I wish I had ~ - looking back at a choice and wanting it back

`ば` names an action; `よかった` is `いい` in the past, passing judgement on the world in
which that action was taken. Put together they say: *doing it would have been the good
outcome* - and since you are saying so afterwards, you didn't.

This is the most useful single thing an intermediate learner can say about their own
past. English needs four different constructions - "I should have", "I wish I'd", "I
ought to have", "why didn't I" - and Japanese does all of them with one ending. It is
worth over-practising.

## Formation

Any `ば`-form plus `よかった`. The `よかった` is frozen: it is always past, and it never
becomes ❌<code>ばいいでした</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final -u → -e + ばよかった | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)けばよかった |
| Verb (る-verb) | る → ればよかった | やめる → やめればよかった |
| Verb (する) | すれば + よかった | 予約(よやく)する → 予約(よやく)すればよかった |
| Verb (来(く)る) | irregular | 来(く)る → 来(く)ればよかった |
| Verb (negative) | なければ + よかった | 買(か)う → 買(か)わなければよかった |
| Preparation | ておけば + よかった | 調(しら)べる → 調(しら)べておけばよかった |
| Potential | ～えれば + よかった | 行(い)ける → 行(い)ければよかった |
| い-adjective | ければ + よかった | 大(おお)きい → 大(おお)きければよかった |
| な-adjective / Noun | なら + よかった | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かならよかった |
| Polite | ばよかったです | 聞(き)けばよかったです |
| Measured | ばよかったと思(おも)う | 聞(き)けばよかったと思(おも)う |
| Aimed at somebody else | ばよかったのに | 言(い)ってくれればよかったのに |
| To yourself | ばよかったなあ | 聞(き)けばよかったなあ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative is not a softener, it is a reversal.
買(か)えばよかった = I wish I *had* bought it. 買(か)わなければよかった = I wish I
*hadn't*. Learners reach for the negative when they mean the positive more often than
for any other pattern in this group.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もっと早(はや)く<b>寝(ね)ればよかった</b>。
I should have gone to bed earlier.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに降(ふ)るなら、傘(かさ)を<b>持(も)ってくればよかった</b>。
If it was going to rain like this, I should have brought an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなこと、<b>言(い)わなければよかった</b>。
I wish I hadn't said that.
</pre>

<pre>
人気(にんき)の店(みせ)だから、前(まえ)の日(ひ)に<b>予約(よやく)しておけばよかった</b>ですね。
It's a popular place - we should have booked the day before.
</pre>

<pre>
地図(ちず)を<b>確(たし)かめておけばよかった</b>。おかげで３０分(ぷん)も迷(まよ)ってしまった。
I should have checked the map. As it is I spent half an hour lost.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに時間(じかん)がかかると分(わ)かっていたら、朝(あさ)のうちに<b>始(はじ)めればよかった</b>。
If I'd known it would take this long, I'd have started first thing.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)のとき、もっといろいろな人(ひと)と<b>話(はな)しておけばよかった</b>と今(いま)になって思(おも)います。
I find myself thinking now that I should have talked to more people at university.
</pre>

<pre>
せっかく京都(きょうと)まで行(い)ったのだから、もう一日(いちにち)<b>泊(と)まればよかった</b>ですね。
Since we'd gone all the way to Kyoto, we really should have stayed one more night.
</pre>

<pre>
あのとき会社(かいしゃ)を<b>やめなければよかった</b>のかな、と時々(ときどき)考(かんが)えることがあります。
Now and then I wonder whether I should have stayed at that company after all.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Bare `ばよかった` is casual, self-directed and extremely common. With `なあ` on the end
it is close to a sigh - 買(か)っておけばよかったなあ - and that is how it most often
turns up in speech, half muttered, with nobody expected to answer.

The polite `ばよかったです` is perfectly usable and you will hear it, though in careful
or written Japanese <code>～ばよかったと思(おも)います</code> sounds more measured and
less like an outburst. In an apology at work the natural frame is a humble verb:
早(はや)めにご報告(ほうこく)すればよかったです、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。

Two combinations carry most of the traffic. `ておけばよかった` - "should have done it in
advance" - is the workhorse, because most regret is about preparation you skipped.
And `なければよかった` is the regret of having done something, which learners chronically
avoid because the double negative feels backwards in English.

Direction is what changes the temperature. Pointed at yourself, `ばよかった` is harmless
and even endearing. Pointed at somebody else it needs `のに`, and then it becomes a
gentle "why on earth didn't you": 言(い)ってくれればよかったのに。 That is milder
than it looks - it often functions as consolation rather than blame - but it is still a
comment on their decision, so aim it downwards or sideways, not upwards.

Finally, a limit worth respecting. `ばよかった` is about a **choice**, so its most
natural subject is somebody who could have chosen otherwise. For things nobody
controlled - the weather, an illness, a train - a bare `ばよかった` sounds incomplete;
add `のに` and let it be a wish instead: 昨日(きのう)晴(は)れればよかったのに。

`たらよかった` exists and is heard, particularly in casual speech and in western Japan.
`ばよかった` is the standard set form and the one to produce; treat `たらよかった` as
something to recognise.

## Contrast

### ～ばよかった vs ～てよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てよかった` looks at what **did** happen and is glad. `ばよかった`
looks at what **didn't** and wishes it had. One is relief, the other is regret, and the
only visible difference is the conditional.

</div>

<pre>
早(はや)めに家(いえ)を<b>出(で)てよかった</b>。
I'm glad I left the house early.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)めに家(いえ)を<b>出(で)ればよかった</b>。
I should have left the house early.
</pre>

Both end in `よかった`, and beginners hear them as the same sentence. The `て`-form
reports something real; the `ば`-form floats something imaginary. If you are ever unsure
which one you have just said, check whether the thing actually happened.

### ～ばよかった vs ～ばよかったのに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - without `のに`, the regret is yours. With `のに`, it is aimed at
somebody else and means "you should have".

</div>

<pre>
会(かい)に<b>来(く)ればよかった</b>。
I should have come to the gathering.
</pre>

<pre>
会(かい)に<b>来(く)ればよかったのに</b>。
You should have come to the gathering - it's a shame you didn't.
</pre>

The second is what you say to a friend who stayed home, and it usually reads as warmth
rather than criticism: *we'd have liked you there*. Drop the `のに` while talking about
somebody else and the sentence sounds like you are regretting **their** existence at
your own event, which is not the effect anyone wants.

### ～ばよかった vs ～べきだった / ～たほうがよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばよかった` is felt: it is a wish. `べきだった` is judged: it is an
obligation you failed. `たほうがよかった` is weighed: of two options, the other one was
better.

</div>

<pre>
親(おや)に<b>相談(そうだん)すればよかった</b>。
I wish I'd talked to my parents about it.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)に<b>相談(そうだん)すべきだった</b>。
I ought to have consulted my parents.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)より、バスで<b>行(い)ったほうがよかった</b>ですね。
It would have been better to go by bus than by train.
</pre>

`べきだった` is stiffer, more written, and carries a moral edge - it is the form of a
public apology and of self-reproach. `たほうがよかった` is the coolest of the three; it
compares two courses without much feeling and is what you use when reviewing a decision
with colleagues. `ばよかった` is the one that sounds like a person.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるばよかった。 → ✅ もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)<b>べれば</b>よかった。
  `よかった` follows the `ば`-form. The dictionary form never appears here.

- ❌ 傘(かさ)を持(も)ってこなければよかった。（meaning "I should have brought an umbrella"） → ✅ 傘(かさ)を持(も)って<b>くれば</b>よかった。
  The negative reverses the regret. As written, the sentence says you wish you had left
  the umbrella at home - which, standing in the rain, is the opposite of your point.

- ❌ もっと勉強(べんきょう)すればいいでした。 → ✅ もっと勉強(べんきょう)すれば<b>よかった</b>です。
  `いい` cannot be put into the past behind `ば` while staying `いい`. The past of `いい`
  is `よかった`, and it takes the whole meaning with it.

- ❌ (課長(かちょう)に)「課長(かちょう)が先(さき)に言(い)ってくれればよかったのに。」 → ✅ 「次(つぎ)からは早(はや)めに確認(かくにん)するようにいたします。」
  `ばよかったのに` is a judgement on the other person's choice. Aimed at a superior that
  is insubordinate however politely you say it; convert it into what you will do next
  time.

## Practice

**1.** 映画(えいが)のチケットが売(う)り切(き)れだった。ネットで先(さき)に____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ネットで先(さき)に<b>買(か)っておけばよかった</b>。

Buying in advance is exactly the "preparation I skipped" case, which is why
`ておけばよかった` is the natural combination. A bare
<code>買(か)えばよかった</code> works too but loses the sense of advance planning.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 傘(かさ)を持(も)ってきたから、濡(ぬ)れずにすんだ。（持(も)ってくればよかった・持(も)ってきてよかった）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

持(も)って<b>きてよかった</b>。

The umbrella *was* brought, so the speaker is relieved, not regretful.
<code>持(も)ってくればよかった</code> would contradict the first half of the sentence
by claiming the umbrella stayed at home.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I wish I hadn't sent that email."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あのメール、<b>送(おく)らなければよかった</b>。

The regret is about something the speaker did, so the `ば`-form has to be negative. In a
more measured register: 送(おく)らなければよかったと思(おも)っています。

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 友(とも)だちが一人(ひとり)で大変(たいへん)そうだった。手伝(てつだ)わなければよかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 手伝(てつだ)って<b>あげればよかった</b>。

The negative says the speaker wishes they had *not* helped, which contradicts the first
sentence. The regret is about help that was never offered, so the `ば`-form must be
positive - and <code>てあげる</code> is what makes it clear the help was for the friend.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もっと早(はや)く<b>言(い)えばよかった</b>。
(b) もっと早(はや)く<b>言(い)ってくれればよかったのに</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker taking it on themselves: I had the information and I sat on it. It is
an apology in embryo, and in a workplace it is often the polite thing to say even when
the fault was shared.

(b) hands it back. <code>くれる</code> makes the other person the one who failed to
speak, and `のに` supplies the disappointment. Between friends it is close to "you should
have said!" and is usually said kindly - but it is still an allocation of blame, so it
does not travel upwards.

</details>

# ～つもりだった

**Meaning** · I had meant to (but didn't); I thought I had (but I hadn't) - an intention or a belief that reality declined to confirm

`つもり` is what is in your head. Put it in the past and you announce that what was in
your head and what happened in the world came apart. Nothing in the words says the plan
failed - but the past tense sets up a "but" so reliably that a Japanese listener hears
it before you finish the sentence.

The pattern has **two** readings, and which one you get depends entirely on the tense of
the verb in front:

- <code>出(だ)すつもりだった</code> - I meant to send it. (I didn't.)
- <code>出(だ)したつもりだった</code> - I thought I had sent it. (I hadn't.)

## Formation

`つもり` is a noun, so what precedes it takes the shape a noun-modifying clause takes:
な-adjectives get `な`, nouns get `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - intention | plain non-past + つもりだった | 行(い)く → 行(い)くつもりだった |
| Verb - intention (negative) | ない-form + つもりだった | 言(い)わない → 言(い)わないつもりだった |
| Verb - mistaken belief | plain past + つもりだった | かけた → かけたつもりだった |
| Verb - belief about a state | ている + つもりだった | やっている → やっているつもりだった |
| い-adjective | plain form + つもりだった | 優(やさ)しい → 優(やさ)しいつもりだった |
| な-adjective | stem + **な** + つもりだった | 親切(しんせつ) → 親切(しんせつ)なつもりだった |
| Noun | Noun + **の** + つもりだった | 冗談(じょうだん) → 冗談(じょうだん)のつもりだった |
| Polite | つもりでした | 行(い)くつもりでした |
| With the "but" spelled out | つもりだったのに／つもりだったが | 行(い)くつもりだったのに |
| Denying an intention | ～つもりはなかった | 傷(きず)つける → 傷(きず)つけるつもりはなかった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもり` needs a **will** behind it. Only something that can decide
things can have one. A train, a parcel or the weather cannot: for those the pattern is
`はずだった`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)銀行(ぎんこう)に行(い)く<b>つもりだった</b>んですが、時間(じかん)がありませんでした。
I'd meant to go to the bank today, but I didn't have time.
</pre>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)をかけた<b>つもりだった</b>のに、ドアが開(あ)いていた。
I could have sworn I locked up, but the door was open.
</pre>

<pre>
あれは冗談(じょうだん)の<b>つもりだった</b>んです。本当(ほんとう)にすみませんでした。
It was meant as a joke. I really am sorry.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)では丁寧(ていねい)に説明(せつめい)した<b>つもりだった</b>が、まったく伝(つた)わっていなかった。
I thought I'd explained it carefully, but none of it had got through.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)を出(で)たら地元(じもと)に帰(かえ)る<b>つもりだった</b>けれど、結局(けっきょく)東京(とうきょう)に残(のこ)った。
I'd intended to go back home after university, but in the end I stayed in Tokyo.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)は全部(ぜんぶ)返(かえ)した<b>つもりでした</b>が、まだ三千円(さんぜんえん)残(のこ)っていたようです。
I was under the impression I'd paid it all back, but apparently three thousand yen is still outstanding.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は何(なに)もしない<b>つもりだった</b>のに、気(き)がついたら一日中(いちにちじゅう)片付(かたづ)けをしていた。
I'd planned to do nothing all weekend, and then found I'd spent the whole day tidying.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)にはお伝(つた)えした<b>つもりでした</b>が、私(わたし)の勘違(かんちが)いだったようです。申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません。
I was sure I'd passed it on to the manager, but it seems I was mistaken. My apologies.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral - `つもりでした` sits comfortably in a business email - 
but it is socially loaded, because most of the time you are using it to explain
yourself.

The intention reading is the everyday softener of excuses. The full apology shape is
`～つもりだったんですが` with a trailing clause: 連絡(れんらく)するつもりだったんです
が、会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)びいてしまって。 The unfinished ending is deliberate. It
lets the listener supply the conclusion, which is more polite than announcing it.

The mistaken-belief reading (`～たつもりだった`) is the Japanese for "I could have sworn
I…". It is honest and unglamorous, and it is what you say when you are the one who
dropped something. Its slightly sharper cousin is `～ているつもりだった` - "I thought I
was doing that" - which comes up when somebody tells you your effort has not shown:
気(き)をつけているつもりだったんですが。

There is one construction here you should learn as a unit: <code>～つもりはなかった</code>,
"I didn't mean to." 傷(きず)つけるつもりはありませんでした is the standard way to
apologise for an accidental hurt. Note the `は`: it is contrastive, and it does the work
of "whatever else I may have done, that was not my intention." The casual version is
`つもりじゃなかった`.

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not confuse this with `～たつもりで`, a separate idiom meaning "as if you
had": 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)ったつもりで貯金(ちょきん)する - "save the money as
though you'd taken the trip." The `で` form is a way of pretending on purpose; the
`だった` form is finding out you were wrong.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～つもりだった vs ～はずだった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもり` is what **you decided**. `はず` is what the **facts led you
to expect**. If somebody asked どうして？ and your answer would be "because I'd made up
my mind to", it is `つもり`; if it would be "because that's what the schedule said", it
is `はず`.

</div>

<pre>
七時(しちじ)の電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)る<b>つもりだった</b>んだけど、寝坊(ねぼう)した。
I'd meant to catch the seven o'clock train, but I overslept.
</pre>

<pre>
七時(しちじ)の電車(でんしゃ)は八時(はちじ)に着(つ)く<b>はずだった</b>のに、まだ動(うご)かない。
The seven o'clock was supposed to arrive at eight, and it still hasn't moved.
</pre>

Only a person can hold an intention, which is why the second sentence cannot take
`つもり`. Trains have timetables, not plans.

### ～るつもりだった vs ～たつもりだった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - non-past in front means the action was never performed. Past in
front means you believe it was performed, and you were wrong.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>閉(し)めるつもりだった</b>。
I meant to close the window.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を<b>閉(し)めたつもりだった</b>。
I thought I'd closed the window.
</pre>

In the first, the window was never touched. In the second, you did something to it and
it wasn't enough. That is a real difference in what you are confessing to, and getting
it backwards in an apology gives entirely the wrong impression of how careful you were.

### ～つもりだった vs ～(よ)うと思(おも)っていた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもりだった` is a **decision** you were carrying around.
<code>～(よ)うと思(おも)っていた</code> is a **thought** you were entertaining. The
second is vaguer, and therefore softer.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>つもりだった</b>んですが、事情(じじょう)が変(か)わりました。
I'd been planning to move next month, but circumstances changed.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)そうと思(おも)っていたんですが、まだ決(き)めていません。
I'd been thinking of moving next month, but I haven't decided yet.
</pre>

Because `つもり` reports something settled, it can sound firm when you are asked about
your plans by somebody with authority over them. If you want to leave yourself room,
<code>思(おも)っていた</code> is the more diplomatic choice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)きますつもりでした。 → ✅ 行(い)<b>く</b>つもりでした。
  `つもり` is a noun and takes a plain noun-modifying clause. The politeness goes on the
  end of the sentence, never inside it.

- ❌ 冗談(じょうだん)つもりだったんです。 → ✅ 冗談(じょうだん)<b>の</b>つもりだったんです。
  A noun in front of `つもり` needs `の`; a な-adjective needs `な`
  (親切(しんせつ)なつもり).

- ❌ 電車(でんしゃ)は三時(さんじ)に着(つ)くつもりだった。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)は三時(さんじ)に着(つ)く<b>はずだった</b>。
  `つもり` requires somebody with a will. Timetables, deliveries and the weather take
  `はずだった`.

- ❌ 怒(おこ)らせるつもりじゃなかったです。 → ✅ 怒(おこ)らせるつもり<b>はありませんでした</b>。
  The meaning is right but the registers clash: casual `じゃなかった` under a polite
  `です`. In an apology, use the full form.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)返事(へんじ)を____んですが、忙(いそが)しくてできませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

返事(へんじ)を<b>書(か)くつもりだった</b>んですが

The reply was never written, so the verb in front stays non-past. `んですが` with a
trailing explanation is the standard apology frame for an intention that fell through.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 荷物(にもつ)は昨日(きのう)届(とど)く（つもりだった・はずだった）のに、まだ来(き)ません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)届(とど)く<b>はずだった</b>のに

A parcel has no intentions. The expectation came from a delivery date, which is exactly
what `はずだった` reports.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I thought I'd turned the air conditioning off, but it was still running."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

エアコンを<b>消(け)したつもりだった</b>のに、まだついていた。

The past-tense verb in front is what gives the "I thought I had" reading. With
<code>消(け)すつもりだった</code> the sentence would mean you never touched it, which
contradicts the second half.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: すみません、失礼(しつれい)なことを言(い)うつもりでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 失礼(しつれい)なことを言(い)うつもり<b>はありませんでした</b>。

As written, the sentence apologises by announcing that being rude was the plan. To deny
an intention you need <code>つもりはない</code> in the past, with the contrastive `は`
carrying "that, at least, was not what I meant."

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部屋(へや)を<b>片付(かたづ)けるつもりだった</b>。
(b) 部屋(へや)を<b>片付(かたづ)けたつもりだった</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the tidying never began. The speaker had it on their list, the day got away
from them, and the room is exactly as it was.

(b) says the speaker did tidy - and then somebody looked at the room and disagreed. It
is a confession of a **judgement** that was wrong rather than a plan that was dropped,
and it carries a faint note of being caught out.

</details>

# ～はずだった

**Meaning** · it was supposed to ~, and it didn't - a well-grounded expectation that the world declined to meet

At N4, `はずだ` was an expectation you could justify if asked: a timetable, a promise, a
rule, a fact somebody told you. `はずだった` keeps the grounds and loses the outcome. It
says *this is what should have followed from what I knew* - and the fact that you are
saying it in the past tense tells the listener that it did not.

Where `つもりだった` reports a plan of your own that came to nothing, `はずだった` reports
the world failing to behave as the evidence promised. Nobody's will is involved, which
is why it works for parcels, trains and weather forecasts.

## Formation

`はず` is a noun, so the clause in front takes noun-modifying shapes: `な` for
な-adjectives, `の` for nouns.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain non-past + はずだった | 届(とど)く → 届(とど)くはずだった |
| Verb (past) | た-form + はずだった | 入(い)れた → 入(い)れたはずだった |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + はずだった | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないはずだった |
| い-adjective | plain form + はずだった | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いはずだった |
| な-adjective | stem + **な** + はずだった | 簡単(かんたん) → 簡単(かんたん)なはずだった |
| Noun | Noun + **の** + はずだった | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みのはずだった |
| Polite | はずでした | 届(とど)くはずでした |
| With the "but" spelled out | はずだったのに／はずだったが | 届(とど)くはずだったのに |
| Set phrase | こんなはずではなかった | |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - say `はずだった` and you have as good as said the rest. If the
expectation *was* met, you do not use this pattern at all - you report the fact, or say
予定(よてい)どおり. Using `はずだった` about something that worked out puzzles the
listener, who is still waiting for the "but".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に終(お)わる<b>はずだった</b>。
The meeting was supposed to finish at three.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は休(やす)みの<b>はずだった</b>のに、急(きゅう)に呼(よ)び出(だ)された。
Today was supposed to be my day off, and then I got called in out of nowhere.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)にお金(かね)が入(はい)っている<b>はずだった</b>んですが、空(から)っぽでした。
There was supposed to be money in my wallet, but it was empty.
</pre>

<pre>
簡単(かんたん)な仕事(しごと)の<b>はずでした</b>が、けっきょく三日(みっか)もかかりました。
It was meant to be a simple job, and it ended up taking three days.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいパソコンは昨日(きのう)届(とど)く<b>はずでした</b>が、まだ届(とど)いておりません。
The new laptop was due to arrive yesterday, but it still hasn't come.
</pre>

<pre>
あの試合(しあい)は、最後(さいご)の５分(ふん)までは、うちのチームが勝(か)つ<b>はずだった</b>。
Until the last five minutes, that match was ours to win.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)して一年(いちねん)で帰(かえ)ってくる<b>はずだった</b>弟(おとうと)が、もう五年(ごねん)向(む)こうに住(す)んでいる。
My brother, who was supposed to study abroad for a year and come home, has been living over there for five now.
</pre>

<pre>
書類(しょるい)は先週(せんしゅう)のうちに全部(ぜんぶ)お渡(わた)しした<b>はずでした</b>が、一枚(いちまい)足(た)りなかったようです。
I was sure I'd handed over all the documents last week, but it seems one was missing.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`はずだった` is register-neutral and travels everywhere, which makes it unusually useful
at work. The polite `はずでした` plus a trailing `が` is the standard way of raising a
problem without accusing anybody: 本日(ほんじつ)午前中(ごぜんちゅう)に届(とど)く
はずでしたが、まだ確認(かくにん)できておりません。 It is firm - you are pointing out
that a commitment was not kept - while attributing the failure to the situation rather
than to the person you are speaking to. That combination is hard to achieve in English
and easy here.

Two flavours are worth separating. With a **non-past** clause in front, the pattern is
about a scheduled or predicted event that failed to occur. With a **past** clause
(<code>渡(わた)したはずだった</code>) it is about your own confident memory being
wrong - "I was sure I had." That second one shades into `～たつもりだった`, and the
difference is where the confidence came from: `つもり` from your intention, `はず` from
evidence you thought you had.

The negative deserves its own line. <code>こんなはずではなかった</code> - "this isn't how
it was supposed to go" - is a fixed phrase, slightly dramatic, and extremely common in
novels, films and genuine bad days. Its casual form is
<code>こんなはずじゃなかった</code>.

One honest qualification. In a narrative, `はずだった` can simply report what was
expected at a point in the story, and the next sentence may confirm it. But in
conversation, standing alone, it is heard as a defeated expectation almost without
exception - so treat the failure reading as the default and do not rely on the neutral
one.

## Contrast

### ～はずだった vs ～つもりだった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもり` is what somebody **decided**; `はず` is what the **evidence
implied**. Only a being with a will can have a `つもり`; anything at all can have a
`はず`.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)休(やす)みを取(と)る<b>つもりだった</b>んですが、仕事(しごと)が入(はい)りました。
I'd been meaning to take tomorrow off, but work came up.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は会社(かいしゃ)が休(やす)みの<b>はずだった</b>んですが、出勤(しゅっきん)になりました。
Tomorrow was supposed to be a company holiday, but now we're working.
</pre>

The first is about your own decision; the second about the calendar. Note what happens
when you get it backwards: ❌<code>電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れるつもりだった</code>
attributes an intention to a train, which is not merely unusual but funny.

### ～はずだった vs ～予定(よてい)だった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>予定(よてい)だった</code> reports a **schedule** and says
nothing about whether it held. `はずだった` reports an **expectation** and implies it
did not.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)大阪(おおさか)に出張(しゅっちょう)する<b>予定(よてい)でした</b>が、中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
I was scheduled to go to Osaka next week, but it's been called off.
</pre>

<pre>
飛行機(ひこうき)は十時(じゅうじ)に着(つ)く<b>はずでした</b>。
The plane was supposed to land at ten.
</pre>

<code>予定(よてい)だった</code> can be followed by "…and that's what happened", which
is why it is the neutral word for an itinerary. `はずでした` said on its own already
tells the listener the plane is late. If the flight landed on time, you say
予定(よてい)どおり十時(じゅうじ)に着(つ)きました and the `はず` never appears.

### ～はずだった vs ～ことになっていた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことになっていた</code> reports an **arrangement** somebody
made - a rule, an agreement, a rota. `はずだった` reports your **inference** from
whatever grounds you had, which may include such an arrangement.

</div>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)は私(わたし)が担当(たんとう)する<b>ことになっていた</b>のに、誰(だれ)も教(おし)えてくれなかった。
I was down to cover reception, and nobody told me.
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)には誰(だれ)かいる<b>はずだった</b>のに、席(せき)が空(あ)いていた。
There was supposed to be somebody on reception, but the desk was empty.
</pre>

The first points at a decision that was taken; the second at a conclusion you drew.
`ことになっていた` is the better choice whenever you can name who agreed to what, and it
is the safer one in a workplace because it appeals to a shared arrangement rather than
to your own reasoning.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 簡単(かんたん)はずだったのに。 → ✅ 簡単(かんたん)<b>な</b>はずだったのに。
  `はず` is a noun, so a な-adjective needs `な` and a noun needs `の`
  (休(やす)みのはず). Compare `かもしれない`, which takes both bare.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は七時(しちじ)に起(お)きるはずだった。（meaning "I meant to get up at seven"） → ✅ 明日(あした)は七時(しちじ)に起(お)きる<b>つもりだった</b>。
  Your own resolve is `つもり`. `はず` would need external grounds - an alarm somebody
  else set, a schedule you were given.

- ❌ 予定(よてい)どおり三時(さんじ)に着(つ)くはずでした。（meaning "and we did"） → ✅ 予定(よてい)どおり三時(さんじ)に<b>着(つ)きました</b>。
  `はずでした` announces that the expectation was not met. If it was, simply report what
  happened.

- ❌ こんなはずじゃなかったです。 → ✅ こんなはず<b>ではありませんでした</b>。
  The casual contraction under a polite `です` clashes. In careful speech, expand it - 
  or keep the whole sentence casual.

The expectation came from what kind of train it is, not from anyone's intention, so
`はず` is the right noun. `のに` then supplies the contradiction that the second half
spells out.

</details>

<code>今年(ことし)こそ</code> marks this as the speaker's own resolve, and resolve is
`つもり` territory. `はずだった` would need something external - a booking already made,
a trip somebody else had arranged for you.

</details>

The expectation rests on a delivery date, so `はず` rather than `つもり`. The polite
`はずでした` plus `が` is the standard shape for raising this kind of problem without
blaming the person you are speaking to.

</details>

Two things: a noun before `はず` needs `の`, and <code>休(やす)み</code> is odd for a

itself is exactly right.

(a) この部屋(へや)の鍵(かぎ)は閉(し)めた<b>はずだった</b>。
(b) この部屋(へや)の鍵(かぎ)は閉(し)めた<b>つもりだった</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) rests on evidence: the speaker remembers the click, or knows it is on their checklist
every evening. The surprise is that the facts they were relying on have let them down.

(b) rests on intention: the speaker meant to lock it and believed they had followed
through. It admits more personal responsibility, which is why (b) is the better sentence
in an apology and (a) the better one when you are genuinely baffled.
