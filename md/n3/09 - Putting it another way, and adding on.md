# N3 - Putting it another way, and adding on


---

# という - called; named

**Meaning** · attaches a name to the thing it names - "a Y called X"

<code>山本(やまもと)さんという人(ひと)</code> is "a person called Yamamoto";
<code>「そら」という店(みせ)</code> is "a shop called Sora". The name goes first,
the ordinary noun goes last, and `という` - literally "which one says" - is the joint
between them. The same joint fastens a whole clause to a noun:
<code>中止(ちゅうし)になったという話(はなし)</code>, "word that it has been called
off."

## Formation

`という` sits between two nouns, or between a plain-form clause and a noun. Nothing
in front of it changes shape, and a clause ending in a noun or a な-adjective keeps
its `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Name + common noun | Name + という + Noun | 山本(やまもと)さん → 山本(やまもと)さんという人(ひと) |
| Title, quoted | 「…」+ という + Noun | 「そら」 → 「そら」という店(みせ) |
| Asking a name | 何(なん)という + Noun | 何(なん)という映画(えいが)ですか |
| Clause + noun | plain form + という + Noun | 始(はじ)まる → 始(はじ)まるという話(はなし) |
| Clause ending in a noun | Noun + **だ** + という + Noun | 事故(じこ)だ → 事故(じこ)だという知(し)らせ |
| Clause ending in な-adj | ～**だ** + という + Noun | 危険(きけん)だ → 危険(きけん)だという情報(じょうほう) |
| Casual speech | っていう／って | 山本(やまもと)さんって人(ひと) |
| Formal equivalent | ～と呼(よ)ばれる | 台風(たいふう)と呼(よ)ばれる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the **name comes first**. ❌<code>人(ひと)という山本(やまもと)さん</code>
reverses the two and says something nonsensical. Whatever `という` points forward to
is the general word; whatever sits behind it is the label.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `という` is for names the listener may not know. Once a name is common
ground it drops out: you say <code>京都(きょうと)に行(い)きました</code>, not
<code>京都(きょうと)という町(まち)に行(い)きました</code>, unless you are
genuinely introducing the place to someone who has never heard of it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>

There was a call from someone called Tanaka.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)が辞(や)める<b>という</b>噂(うわさ)が社内(しゃない)に広(ひろ)がっています。

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

I heard that a novel called "The Night the Stars Fell" is being made into a film, so I went to a bookshop for the first time in ages.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`という` is register-neutral and enormously common. It is not decoration: leaving it
out where it is needed produces a noun phrase that will not parse, and putting it in
where it is not needed makes you sound as though you doubt the listener has heard of
Kyoto.

**In speech it shrinks.** <code>という</code> becomes `っていう`, and then simply
`って`: <code>田中(たなか)さんって人(ひと)</code>,
<code>辞(や)めるって話(はなし)</code>. You will also see it written
<code>ってゆう</code> in casual messages, which reflects how the reduced form is
actually pronounced. All of these are conversational only - in a report or an email
to a client, write `という` in full. Note too that the bare `って` is doing several
jobs at once in speech (quotation, topic-marking, hearsay), so context rather than
form tells you which one you are hearing.

With a **person's name**, the phrase carries a small social signal: it presents the
person as unknown to the speaker as well as to the listener.
<code>田中(たなか)さんという方(かた)がいらっしゃいました</code> is what a
receptionist says about a stranger. Said about a colleague you see daily, it would
sound as though you were disowning them.

For **your own** name the pattern is not used. Introducing yourself, you say
<code>田中(たなか)と申(もう)します</code> - the `という` frame describes a name
from the outside, and you are on the inside of yours.

One shape to keep separate. When the **same noun appears on both sides** of
`という` - <code>家(いえ)という家(いえ)</code>, <code>店(みせ)という店(みせ)</code> -
it is not naming anything. That is a different pattern, treated at N1, meaning
"every single one of them". The test is mechanical: two different nouns means
"called"; the same noun twice means "every".

## Contrast

### X という Y vs X の Y

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `という` says the two nouns are the **same thing**, one naming
the other. `の` says they are **different things** in some relationship.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>という</b>医者(いしゃ)に見(み)てもらいました。
I was seen by a doctor called Tanaka.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>の</b>医者(いしゃ)に見(み)てもらいました。
I was seen by Tanaka's doctor.
</pre>

In the first, Tanaka *is* the doctor. In the second, Tanaka is somebody else and the
doctor belongs to him. The same split explains
<code>「坊(ぼっ)ちゃん」という本(ほん)</code> (a book whose title is Botchan)
against <code>先生(せんせい)の本(ほん)</code> (a book belonging to, or written
by, the teacher).

Japanese does have an appositive `の` - <code>友(とも)だちの木村(きむら)さん</code>,
"my friend Kimura" - but notice the order there: the general noun comes **first** and
the name second. With `という` the name always leads. If you find yourself wanting
`の` between a name and the word it names, in that order, you want `という`.

### Clause + という + noun vs a plain noun-modifying clause

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the noun is a **container for words or information** -
話(はなし), 噂(うわさ), 知(し)らせ, 情報(じょうほう), 意見(いけん),
点(てん) - the clause needs `という`. If the clause simply **describes** the
noun, attach it directly.

</div>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんが入院(にゅういん)した<b>という</b>知(し)らせが届(とど)いた。
Word came that Hayashi had gone into hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんが入院(にゅういん)した病院(びょういん)は駅(えき)の前(まえ)です。
The hospital Hayashi went into is in front of the station.
</pre>

The first noun holds the *content* of the clause - the news is that he was admitted -
and `という` is the join. The second noun is a place the clause simply describes, so
the clause attaches with nothing in between. Slipping `という` in there is not
ungrammatical, but it changes the sentence:
<code>入院(にゅういん)したという病院(びょういん)</code> is "the hospital he is
*said* to have gone into", with the information marked as second-hand. That
reportive flavour is a side effect of the quotative, and it is worth knowing about
before you add a `という` you did not need.

### Naming という vs the repeated-noun という

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - count the nouns. Two **different** nouns is the naming pattern
on this page. The **same** noun on both sides is the N1 pattern meaning "every
single one".

</div>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)のあと、港(みなと)に「あおば」<b>という</b>船(ふね)だけが残(のこ)っていた。
After the typhoon, the only boat left in the harbour was one called Aoba.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)のあと、港(みなと)の<b>船(ふね)という船(ふね)</b>が傷(きず)ついていた。
After the typhoon, every last boat in the harbour was damaged.
</pre>

Same three characters, two unrelated grammars, and no real ambiguity once you have
seen the repetition. The repeated form is vivid and slightly exaggerated, and it
needs a set big enough to sweep across; the naming form is neutral and works with
one item.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 人(ひと)という山本(やまもと)さんから電話(でんわ)がありました。 → ✅ 山本(やまもと)さん<b>という</b>人(ひと)から電話(でんわ)がありました。
  The name goes in front, the general noun behind. Reversing them is the single
  commonest error with this pattern, and it comes straight from the English word
  order "a person called Yamamoto".

- ❌ 給料(きゅうりょう)が高(たか)い<b>だという</b>話(はなし)を聞(き)いた。 → ✅ 給料(きゅうりょう)が高(たか)い<b>という</b>話(はなし)を聞(き)いた。
  い-adjectives never take `だ`. The `だ` in the Formation table belongs to clauses
  that end in a noun or a な-adjective.

- ❌ それは何(なん)という意味(いみ)ですか。 → ✅ それは<b>どういう</b>意味(いみ)ですか。
  Asking what something *means* is <code>どういう意味(いみ)</code>, not
  <code>何(なん)という</code>. Keep <code>何(なん)という</code> for asking a
  name: <code>何(なん)という店(みせ)ですか</code>.

- ❌（in a written report）「中村(なかむら)」って人(ひと)が来(き)ました。 → ✅ 中村(なかむら)さん<b>という</b>方(かた)が来(こ)られました。
  `って` is the spoken reduction of `という` and looks careless on paper. The full
  form is the one that belongs in writing.

## Practice

**1.** 受付(うけつけ)に木村(きむら)さん____方(かた)が来(き)ています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

木村(きむら)さん<b>という</b>方(かた)が来(き)ています。

The name is being attached to the general noun <code>方(かた)</code>, which is what
`という` is for. The phrasing also signals that the visitor is a stranger to
everyone in the room - exactly what a receptionist means.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)、工場(こうじょう)で事故(じこ)があった（という・の）知(し)らせが入(はい)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

事故(じこ)があった<b>という</b>知(し)らせが入(はい)りました。

<code>知(し)らせ</code> is a container for information, and the clause is that
information, so the two are joined with `という`. `の` would make the clause into a
possessor, which is not what a piece of news is.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm reading a book called 'Kitchen Window'."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「台所(だいどころ)の窓(まど)」<b>という</b>本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいます。

The title goes first, in quotation marks, then `という`, then the general noun. In
casual speech the same sentence comes out as
<code>「台所(だいどころ)の窓(まど)」って本(ほん)</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 映画(えいが)という「七(なな)つの海(うみ)」を見(み)に行(い)きませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 「七(なな)つの海(うみ)」<b>という</b>映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きませんか。

The two halves are the wrong way round. The title is the label and goes in front;
<code>映画(えいが)</code> is the general noun and goes behind.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference:
(a) 通(とお)りの店(みせ)<b>という</b>店(みせ)が閉(し)まっていた。
(b) 通(とお)りの「みどり」<b>という</b>店(みせ)が閉(し)まっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) repeats the same noun on both sides, so it is not naming anything. It means
every single shop on the street was shut, and it carries a note of surprise or
dismay at the scale of it. That repeated-noun pattern is treated at N1.

(b) has two different nouns: a name and the general word it labels. It reports one
shop, identified by the name Midori. If you are unsure which pattern you are looking
at, check whether the words on either side of `という` are the same.

</details>

# ～というのは - means; is

**Meaning** · picks a word up and announces that its definition is coming - "X means…", "X is…"

<code>言(い)う</code> is doing its literal work here: <code>というのは</code> is "the one
that gets *called* X". Everything in front of it is a **label**; everything after it is
what the label stands for. This is the frame you use when you know the listener does not
know the word - and it is how dictionaries, glossaries and patient colleagues all sound.

## Formation

<code>というのは</code> attaches to whatever you are holding up as a *term*: usually a
noun, sometimes a quoted phrase, sometimes a whole clause when the thing being defined is
an action.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というのは | 敷金(しききん) → 敷金(しききん)というのは |
| Quoted word or phrase | 「…」+ というのは | 「遠慮(えんりょ)」 → 「遠慮(えんりょ)」というのは |
| Clause - defining an action | plain form + というのは | 人(ひと)に頼(たよ)る → 人(ひと)に頼(たよ)るというのは |
| Usual closing | …ことだ／…という意味(いみ)だ | …預(あず)けるお金(かね)のことです |
| Casual contraction | っていうのは／ってのは | 敷金(しききん)ってのは |
| Written, compact | Noun + とは | 敷金(しききん)とは |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun in front takes no `だ`: ❌<code>敷金(しききん)だというのは</code>.
And the sentence normally lands on <code>…ことです</code> or
<code>…という意味(いみ)です</code>, because what you are describing is a *word*, not
an object.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
「大家(おおや)」<b>というのは</b>、アパートの持(も)ち主(ぬし)のことです。
"Ōya" means the owner of the building you rent from.
</pre>

<pre>
「寝坊(ねぼう)」<b>というのは</b>、朝(あさ)起(お)きられないことです。
"Nebō" means failing to get up in the morning.
</pre>

<pre>
敷金(しききん)<b>というのは</b>、部屋(へや)を借(か)りるときに大家(おおや)さんに預(あず)けるお金(かね)です。
A shikikin is the money you leave with the landlord when you rent a room.
</pre>

<pre>
自由(じゆう)<b>というのは</b>、何(なに)をしてもいいという意味(いみ)ではない。
Freedom does not mean that anything goes.
</pre>

<pre>
「花見(はなみ)」<b>というのは</b>、桜(さくら)の下(した)で飲(の)んだり食(た)べたりする春(はる)の行事(ぎょうじ)です。
Hanami is the spring custom of eating and drinking under the cherry trees.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの会社(かいしゃ)で「直行(ちょっこう)」<b>というのは</b>、家(いえ)から直接(ちょくせつ)取引先(とりひきさき)へ行(い)くことを指(さ)します。
At our company, "chokkō" refers to going straight from home to a client.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の会社(かいしゃ)で「検討(けんとう)します」<b>というのは</b>、やんわり断(ことわ)る言(い)い方(かた)だと考(かんが)えたほうがいいでしょう。
At a Japanese company, "we'll look into it" is best understood as a gentle refusal.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)を育(そだ)てる<b>というのは</b>、自分(じぶん)も一緒(いっしょ)に変(か)わっていくことなのだと、最近(さいきん)思(おも)うようになりました。
I've come to feel lately that raising someone means changing alongside them.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern itself is register-neutral, but the *act* is not: you are explaining a word,
so you are momentarily the one who knows. Used on a word the listener obviously knows, it
sounds like a lecture. Used on a word you have just introduced yourself, it is simply
helpful, and it is exactly what a Japanese speaker will produce if you ask
「敷金(しききん)って何(なん)ですか」.

In speech the whole thing collapses. <code>っていうのは</code> is the everyday form,
<code>ってのは</code> is looser still and leans male in casual conversation, and plain
`って` - the N4 workhorse - does the same job with less ceremony. In writing you go the
other way: `とは` is the compact form that opens dictionary headwords and legal
definitions.

One structural habit worth copying: the definition normally ends on
<code>…ことです</code>, <code>…という意味(いみ)です</code> or
<code>…ということです</code>. Because the subject of the sentence is a *word* rather than
a thing, closing on a bare noun can feel like you stopped halfway.
<code>大家(おおや)というのはアパートの持(も)ち主(ぬし)のことです</code> is the
polished version of a sentence many learners end at <code>持(も)ち主(ぬし)です</code>.

## Contrast

### というのは vs とは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different medium. <code>というのは</code> is what you
*say*; `とは` is what you *write*. `とは` is shorter, drier, and expects a full definition
after it.

</div>

<pre>
「時効(じこう)」<b>とは</b>、一定(いってい)の時間(じかん)が過(す)ぎて権利(けんり)がなくなることをいう。
"Jikō" denotes the lapsing of a right after a fixed period of time.
</pre>

<pre>
「時効(じこう)」<b>というのは</b>、時間(じかん)が経(た)つともう訴(うった)えられなくなる、ということですね。
So "jikō" means that after enough time passes you can't be sued any more, right?
</pre>

The first belongs in a textbook; the second belongs in a conversation about one. `とは`
also turns up in speech with a raised eyebrow - <code>プロとは思(おも)えない</code>,
"you'd never call that professional" - but that is a different `とは` and does not define
anything.

### というのは vs ということは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>というのは</code> explains a **word**.
<code>ということは</code> draws a conclusion from a **fact**. If English wants "which
means…", you need <code>ということは</code>.

</div>

<pre>
「定休日(ていきゅうび)」<b>というのは</b>、毎週(まいしゅう)決(き)まっている休(やす)みの日(ひ)のことです。
"Teikyūbi" means the fixed day each week when a shop is closed.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は定休日(ていきゅうび)だ。<b>ということは</b>、買(か)い物(もの)は今日(きょう)済(す)ませないといけない。
Tomorrow's their closing day. Which means the shopping has to be done today.
</pre>

The giveaway is what sits in front. A bare label takes <code>というのは</code>; a finished
statement of fact takes <code>ということは</code>.

### というのは vs のは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のは` takes an **act** and comments on it.
<code>というのは</code> takes the **idea or the name** of that act and explains it. If you
could put quotation marks round the front half, you want <code>というのは</code>.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)早(はや)く起(お)きる<b>のは</b>苦手(にがて)です。
I'm no good at getting up early.
</pre>

<pre>
「朝活(あさかつ)」<b>というのは</b>、出勤(しゅっきん)前(まえ)の時間(じかん)を勉強(べんきょう)や運動(うんどう)に使(つか)うことです。
"Asakatsu" means using the time before work for study or exercise.
</pre>

The first is about your actual mornings. The second never touches anyone's morning - it is
about a term. Swapping them produces sentences that are grammatical and beside the point.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 敷金(しききん)<b>だ</b>というのは、預(あず)けるお金(かね)のことです。 → ✅ 敷金(しききん)というのは、預(あず)けるお金(かね)のことです。
  No `だ` on the noun being defined. The `だ` version is the quoting <code>という</code>
  ("saying that it's a deposit"), which is not what you mean.

- ❌ 「花見(はなみ)」というのは、とても楽(たの)しいです。 → ✅ 「花見(はなみ)」は、とても楽(たの)しいです。
  <code>というのは</code> promises a definition, not an opinion. If you are saying how you
  feel about the thing itself, plain `は` is what you want.

- ❌ 私(わたし)というのは田中(たなか)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は田中(たなか)です。
  You are not a vocabulary item. <code>というのは</code> frames its noun as a term to be
  explained, so using it on yourself sounds like you are defining the word "I".

- ❌ 残業(ざんぎょう)ということは、決(き)められた時間(じかん)の後(あと)に働(はたら)くことです。 → ✅ 残業(ざんぎょう)というのは、…
  A single word takes <code>というのは</code>. <code>ということは</code> needs a whole
  fact in front of it and produces an inference, not a definition.

## Practice

**1.** 「三日坊主(みっかぼうず)」____、何(なに)を始(はじ)めてもすぐにやめてしまう人(ひと)のことです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「三日坊主(みっかぼうず)」<b>というのは</b>、…

A word is being held up for explanation and the sentence closes on
<code>…人(ひと)のことです</code>, which is the definition frame. `は` alone would work
grammatically but loses the "here is what this term means" signal.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 電気(でんき)が消(け)えている。（というのは・ということは）、もう帰(かえ)ったんだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

…（電気(でんき)が消(け)えている。）<b>ということは</b>、もう帰(かえ)ったんだろう。

The first half is a finished observation, not a label, and the second half is a conclusion
drawn from it. That is <code>ということは</code>. <code>というのは</code> would need a
vocabulary item in front of it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "'Zangyō' means working after your fixed hours are over."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「残業(ざんぎょう)」<b>というのは</b>、決(き)められた時間(じかん)が終(お)わったあとも働(はたら)くことです。

The quoted term takes <code>というのは</code> with no `だ`, and the explanation closes on
<code>…ことです</code> because you are defining an activity.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山田(やまだ)さんだというのは、営業(えいぎょう)の人(ひと)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山田(やまだ)さんというのは、営業(えいぎょう)の人(ひと)です。

Drop the `だ` - the noun attaches directly. Note also that this only works if
山田(やまだ)さん is being treated as a *name the listener has just heard*; if you are
simply telling someone about him, <code>山田(やまだ)さんは営業(えいぎょう)の人(ひと)です</code>
is the natural sentence.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一人暮(ひとりぐ)らしをする<b>のは</b>大変(たいへん)です。
(b) 一人暮(ひとりぐ)らし<b>というのは</b>、思(おも)っているより大変(たいへん)なものです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) comments on the act itself, and most naturally on your own or a specific person's
situation: living alone is hard work.

(b) lifts the word up and pronounces on the whole idea of it - "living alone, as a thing,
is harder than people imagine." It sounds like advice from someone who has done it, and
the <code>ものです</code> ending reinforces that it is a general truth rather than a
report.

</details>

# というのは - because

**Meaning** · opens the sentence that gives the reason for what you have just said - "the reason being that…"

You make a statement. You can hear the listener's silent "why?". <code>というのは</code>
starts a new sentence and answers it. Unlike `から` or `ので`, which live inside the
sentence they explain, this one stands at the head of its own - which is why it can carry
a long, unhurried reason that would sag if you tried to bolt it on.

## Formation

This <code>というのは</code> is a **sentence-opening connective**. It follows a full stop,
takes a comma after it, and the clause it introduces almost always closes on `からだ`,
`ためだ` or `のだ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| After a finished sentence | …。というのは、…からだ。 | 行(い)けない。というのは、熱(ねつ)があるからだ。 |
| Polite style | …。というのは、…からです。 | …熱(ねつ)があるからです。 |
| With のだ instead of から | …。というのは、…のです。 | …急(きゅう)に熱(ねつ)が出(で)たのです。 |
| Near-identical variant | というのも、…からだ | というのも、熱(ねつ)があるからだ |
| Casual speech | っていうのは、…んだ | っていうのは、熱(ねつ)があるんだ |
| Formal writing | なぜなら（ば）、…からである | - |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you delete the reason clause, what is left must still be a complete,
sensible sentence. <code>というのは</code> is an afterthought by design; it cannot be the
grammatical backbone of anything.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)ります。<b>というのは</b>、母(はは)が来(く)るからです。
I'm going home early today - my mother is coming, you see.
</pre>

<pre>
その仕事(しごと)は断(ことわ)った。<b>というのは</b>、時間(じかん)が足(た)りなかったからだ。
I turned that job down. The reason is that there wasn't enough time.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の集(あつ)まりには行(い)けません。<b>というのは</b>、その日(ひ)は出張(しゅっちょう)が入(はい)っているからです。
I can't make next week's get-together - I've got a business trip that day.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)をずっと使(つか)っています。<b>というのは</b>、例文(れいぶん)が多(おお)くて分(わ)かりやすいからです。
I've been using this dictionary for years, because it has plenty of example sentences and is easy to follow.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の話(はなし)はあまり信(しん)じないほうがいい。<b>というのは</b>、前(まえ)にも同(おな)じことで人(ひと)を困(こま)らせているからだ。
You'd do better not to take what he says at face value; he's landed people in trouble with the same story before.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は旅行(りょこう)をやめることにした。<b>というのは</b>、去年(きょねん)買(か)った車(くるま)のローンがまだ残(のこ)っているからだ。
We've decided against a holiday this summer - the loan on the car we bought last year still isn't paid off.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが、明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は欠席(けっせき)させてください。<b>というのは</b>、工場(こうじょう)のほうで急(きゅう)に問題(もんだい)が起(お)きてしまったのです。
I'm sorry, but please let me miss tomorrow's meeting - a problem has suddenly come up at the plant.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)はこの町(まち)を離(はな)れられない。<b>というのは</b>、ここには祖父(そふ)が残(のこ)してくれた畑(はたけ)があるからだ。
I can't leave this town. The reason is that my grandfather's fields are here.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **considered** connective. Splitting the reason off into its own sentence buys
you a beat of pause, so it suits explanations you have thought about: declining an
invitation, justifying a decision, walking a reader through an argument. It is at home in
essays, reports, formal e-mail and prepared speech, and it appears in careful conversation
too - but in fast, casual talk people reach for `だって`, `から` or a bare `のだ` instead.

<code>というのも</code> is the same connective with `も` and is, if anything, slightly more
common in writing. Treat them as interchangeable at this level; `も` adds a faint sense of
"and there is this on top of it", which is why it often follows a reason already given.

Two small register facts. First, the polite version is fully polite - the ending carries
the politeness (<code>…からです</code>), while <code>というのは</code> itself is fixed and
never becomes <code>といいますのは</code> in ordinary speech. Second, dropping the
`からだ` at the end and simply stating the reason flat sounds abrupt in writing, though it
is normal in speech: 「行(い)けない。というのは、熱(ねつ)があってね。」

## Contrast

### というのは vs なぜなら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same slot, different formality. `なぜなら` is written, structured and a
little insistent; <code>というのは</code> is the version you can say out loud without
sounding like a textbook.

</div>

<pre>
この方法(ほうほう)はすすめられない。<b>なぜなら</b>、コストが高(たか)すぎるからである。
This approach cannot be recommended, because the cost is too high.
</pre>

<pre>
この方法(ほうほう)はあまりすすめません。<b>というのは</b>、コストが高(たか)すぎるからです。
I wouldn't really recommend this approach - the cost is too high.
</pre>

`なぜなら` all but demands `からだ／からである` at the end; leaving it off feels unfinished.
<code>というのは</code> is looser about it. Note too that `なぜなら` announces a reason
before you have heard any of it, which is what makes it feel like an argument being built.

### というのは vs だって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だって` is the same move at the bottom of the register ladder, and it
carries a defensive tone: you are answering a criticism, not filling in background.

</div>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)食(た)べちゃった。<b>だって</b>、おいしかったんだもん。
I ate the lot. Well - it was good!
</pre>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)食(た)べてしまいました。<b>というのは</b>、残(のこ)すと失礼(しつれい)になると思(おも)ったからです。
I finished everything, because I thought leaving food would be rude.
</pre>

`だって` is casual and reads as childish or petulant from an adult in a formal setting.
<code>というのは</code> is neutral. If you are explaining yourself to a superior, the
choice is not stylistic.

### The "because" というのは vs the "means" というのは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what sits **in front**. A finished sentence and a full stop →
"because". A bare noun or quoted word → "means". The endings confirm it: `…からだ` versus
`…ことだ`.

</div>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)っていったほうがいい。<b>というのは</b>、午後(ごご)から雨(あめ)になるからだ。
You'd better take an umbrella - it's going to rain from the afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
「夕立(ゆうだち)」<b>というのは</b>、夏(なつ)の午後(ごご)に急(きゅう)に降(ふ)る雨(あめ)のことだ。
"Yūdachi" means the rain that falls suddenly on a summer afternoon.
</pre>

Nothing about the phrase itself distinguishes them; position and closing do all the work.
In speech, a clear pause before <code>というのは</code> also marks the "because" one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 熱(ねつ)があるからというのは、行(い)けません。 → ✅ 行(い)けません。というのは、熱(ねつ)があるからです。
  The order is fixed: statement first, then <code>というのは</code>, then the reason.
  Putting the reason in front leaves the connective with nothing to attach back to.

- ❌ 行(い)けません。というのは、熱(ねつ)があります。 → ✅ …熱(ねつ)があるからです。
  In writing, the reason clause needs `からだ／からです`, `ためだ` or `のです` to close it.
  A bare statement leaves the sentence sounding cut off.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っているというのは、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っているので、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。
  Here the reason comes first and the result second - that is `ので`/`から` territory.
  <code>というのは</code> can only look backwards.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れてすみません。というのは、電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったからです。（to a customer, in person）→ ✅ 遅(おく)れて申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まってしまいまして…
  Not ungrammatical, but announcing "the reason is" while apologising sounds like you are
  building a defence. Apologies usually run the reason in with `て` and trail off.

## Practice

**1.** 今日(きょう)は外(そと)で食(た)べましょう。____、冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に何(なに)も残(のこ)っていないからです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

…。<b>というのは</b>、冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に何(なに)も残(のこ)っていないからです。

The first sentence is complete and the second supplies its reason, closing on
<code>からです</code>. <code>というのも</code> would fit the same slot with almost no
change of meaning.

</details>

**2.** Choose the more natural connective for a written report: この案(あん)は採用(さいよう)できない。（だって・というのは）、予算(よさん)を大(おお)きく超(こ)えているからだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

…（<b>というのは</b>）、予算(よさん)を大(おお)きく超(こ)えているからだ。

`だって` belongs to casual spoken Japanese and sounds defensive, which is wrong for a
report. <code>というのは</code> - or the still more formal `なぜなら` - is what written
argument uses.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I gave up on the plan. The reason is that nobody else was interested."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

その計画(けいかく)はあきらめました。<b>というのは</b>、ほかに興味(きょうみ)を持(も)つ人(ひと)がいなかったからです。

Statement, full stop, connective, reason ending in <code>からです</code>. English "the
reason is that" maps onto this pattern almost exactly.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)は休(やす)みます。というのは、病院(びょういん)へ行(い)きます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)は休(やす)みます。というのは、病院(びょういん)へ行(い)くからです。

The reason clause has to be marked as a reason. Without <code>から</code> the second
sentence reads as a second plain announcement, and the connective is left explaining
nothing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今日(きょう)は参加(さんか)できません。子(こ)どもが熱(ねつ)を出(だ)したので。
(b) 今日(きょう)は参加(さんか)できません。というのは、子(こ)どもが熱(ねつ)を出(だ)したからです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) tucks the reason in behind the statement with a trailing `ので`. It is soft, spoken,
and slightly apologetic - the classic way to excuse yourself without making a production
of it.

(b) formally announces that an explanation is coming, then delivers it. It is clearer and
more deliberate, and it suits writing or a situation where you want the reason on the
record. Said quickly in casual conversation it can sound a shade over-prepared.

</details>

# ～というのは - the thing about X

**Meaning** · lifts a noun up and holds it there while you say what it is really like

This is <code>というのは</code> used not to define a word but to **single a topic out**.
`は` would just say "as for X"; <code>というのは</code> says "X - now, the thing about X
is…", and it commits you to a verdict. The noun in front can be a whole class
（親(おや), 時間(じかん), 人生(じんせい)） or one particular thing you want to
pick out of the room, and the sentence that follows is almost always an opinion, a
generalisation, or something you have discovered the hard way.

## Formation

The shape is identical to the defining <code>というのは</code>. What tells them apart is
what comes after: a definition, or a judgement.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a class) | Noun + というのは | 親(おや) → 親(おや)というのは |
| Noun phrase (one specific thing) | Noun + というのは | この店(みせ)のカレー → この店(みせ)のカレーというのは |
| Clause - an activity | plain form + というのは | 人(ひと)を待(ま)つ → 人(ひと)を待(ま)つというのは |
| Typical closing | …ものだ／…ものです | …ありがたいものだ |
| Casual contraction | って／っていうのは | 親(おや)って |
| Heavier, more emphatic | Noun + というものは | 親(おや)というものは |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ものだ</code> at the end is not compulsory, but it belongs to the same
gesture. It marks the sentence as a truth about how things generally are, which is exactly
what this <code>というのは</code> sets up.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
親(おや)<b>というのは</b>ありがたいものだ。
Parents are something to be grateful for.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)<b>というのは</b>あっという間(ま)に過(す)ぎるね。
Time really does fly, doesn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の気持(きも)ち<b>というのは</b>、そんなに簡単(かんたん)に変(か)わるものではない。
People's feelings don't change as easily as all that.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)のカレー<b>というのは</b>、一度(いちど)食(た)べたらしばらく忘(わす)れられない。
The curry at this place - one bowl and you won't forget it for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で暮(く)らす<b>というのは</b>、自由(じゆう)だけれど寂(さび)しいものです。
Living on your own is free, certainly, but it's a lonely business.
</pre>

<pre>
上(うえ)に立(た)つ人(ひと)<b>というのは</b>、自分(じぶん)の失敗(しっぱい)をなかなか認(みと)めないものらしい。
People at the top, it seems, are slow to admit their own mistakes.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の前(まえ)の日(ひ)<b>というのは</b>、どうして部屋(へや)の掃除(そうじ)がしたくなるんだろう。
Why is it that the day before an exam always makes you want to tidy your room?
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)<b>というのは</b>、なくしてはじめてそのありがたさが分(わ)かるものだと父(ちち)がよく言(い)っていました。
My father often said that you only appreciate your health once you've lost it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This use has a **reflective** feel. It sets the noun down, steps back, and pronounces - 
which is why it turns up in essays, in advice, in the mouths of older speakers, and in
anyone's voice at the end of a long day. Said about something trivial it sounds pompous:
<code>このボールペンというのは安(やす)い</code> is a strange sentence, because nobody
needs a verdict on a pen.

In conversation the contraction `って` does the same work and does it constantly:
「親(おや)ってありがたいよね」, 「会議(かいぎ)って長(なが)いよね」. That form is
casual but not rude, and it is the single most common way this idea gets expressed out
loud. <code>っていうのは</code> sits in between - spoken, but with a beat of deliberation.

Because the pattern promises a generalisation, it pairs naturally with `ものだ`,
`ものではない`, `らしい` and `のだろう`, and it likes adverbs of the "as a rule" family - 
`なかなか`, `どうしても`, `つい`, `やはり`. If your sentence has none of that flavour and
you only want to say what one thing is like right now, plain `は` is the better particle.

## Contrast

### というのは vs は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` reports on **this** thing. <code>というのは</code> raises the
noun to "X, as a kind of thing" and warns the listener that a verdict is coming.

</div>

<pre>
父(ちち)<b>は</b>厳(きび)しい人(ひと)でした。
My father was a strict man.
</pre>

<pre>
父親(ちちおや)<b>というのは</b>、子(こ)どもの前(まえ)では弱(よわ)いところを見(み)せないものだ。
Fathers, as a rule, don't let their children see them weak.
</pre>

The first is a fact about one man. The second is a claim about fathers in general, and the
speaker is prepared to defend it. Note that you cannot swap them freely: the second
sentence with plain `は` would sound like a report on some specific father, and the first
with <code>というのは</code> would sound like you were about to define the word "father".

### というのは vs って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different register. `って` is the spoken default;
<code>というのは</code> is what you write, and what you say when you want the weight.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)し<b>って</b>、思(おも)ったよりお金(かね)がかかるね。
Moving house costs more than you'd think, doesn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)し<b>というのは</b>、思(おも)っている以上(いじょう)にお金(かね)も時間(じかん)もかかるものです。
Moving house takes far more money and time than people imagine.
</pre>

The second would sound stiff between friends and exactly right in an article or a piece of
advice to a junior colleague. The first would look sloppy in writing. Nothing else
separates them.

### というのは vs というものは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>というものは</code> is the same gesture with more weight behind
it. It sounds like the opening of a proverb, and it is nearly always followed by a
sweeping claim.

</div>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)<b>というのは</b>、誰(だれ)にでもあることです。
Failure is something that happens to anybody.
</pre>

<pre>
時(とき)<b>というものは</b>、待(ま)ってはくれない。
Time, in the end, waits for no one.
</pre>

Use <code>というものは</code> sparingly. In ordinary conversation it can sound
theatrical - the linguistic equivalent of standing up before you speak - whereas
<code>というのは</code> passes unnoticed.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)というのは広(ひろ)いです。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いです。
  A plain description of one thing takes `は`. <code>というのは</code> promises either a
  definition or a verdict, and "this room is big" is neither.

- ❌ 親(おや)<b>だ</b>というのは、ありがたいものだ。 → ✅ 親(おや)というのは、ありがたいものだ。
  No `だ` between the noun and <code>という</code>. With `だ` it becomes a quotation - 
  "the fact that it's a parent" - which is not the topic-raising pattern.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんというのは、昨日(きのう)会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みました。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは、昨日(きのう)会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みました。
  A one-off event about a named person is straightforward news, so it takes `は`.
  <code>というのは</code> would suggest you were about to characterise Tanaka as a type.

- ❌ あなたというのは、いつも親切(しんせつ)ですね。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんはいつも親切(しんせつ)ですね。
  Grammatical, but pointing this pattern at the person in front of you announces a
  character assessment. In practice that frame is used far more often for complaints
  than compliments（<code>君(きみ)というのは、本当(ほんとう)に人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)かないな</code>）,
  so keep it off your listener unless you mean it.

## Practice

**1.** 習慣(しゅうかん)____、一度(いちど)身(み)についてしまうとなかなか変(か)えられないものだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

習慣(しゅうかん)<b>というのは</b>、…

The sentence makes a general claim about habits and closes on <code>ものだ</code>, which
is the natural partner of this pattern. `は` alone would be grammatical but flatter - it
would read as a remark rather than a verdict.

</details>

**2.** Choose: あの人(ひと)（は・というのは）昨日(きのう)京都(きょうと)へ行(い)ったそうです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの人(ひと)<b>は</b>昨日(きのう)京都(きょうと)へ行(い)ったそうです。

This is a single piece of reported news about one person, not a characterisation of a
class. <code>というのは</code> needs a generalisation or an evaluation to follow it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Children are surprisingly good at seeing what the adults around them are feeling."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)ども<b>というのは</b>、周(まわ)りの大人(おとな)の気持(きも)ちを驚(おどろ)くほどよく見(み)ているものだ。

"Children" here means children as a class, and the sentence is a general observation, so
the topic gets raised with <code>というのは</code> and the sentence closes on
<code>ものだ</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 旅行(りょこう)だというのは、準備(じゅんび)が一番(いちばん)楽(たの)しいものだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 旅行(りょこう)というのは、準備(じゅんび)が一番(いちばん)楽(たの)しいものだ。

Remove the `だ`. Nouns attach straight to <code>という</code> in this pattern; adding `だ`
turns it into a quoted proposition and breaks the topic-raising reading.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日本(にほん)の夏(なつ)<b>は</b>暑(あつ)いです。
(b) 日本(にほん)の夏(なつ)<b>というのは</b>、想像(そうぞう)している以上(いじょう)に暑(あつ)いものです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of fact - summer here is hot - and could open a weather report or
a guidebook entry.

(b) puts summer up for judgement and delivers one, with the extra implication that the
listener has underestimated it. The <code>以上に</code> and <code>ものです</code> both
belong to that gesture: this is what someone says to a visitor who has just arrived in
June, from experience.

</details>

# ～というと - speaking of; when you say…

**Meaning** · takes a word that has just been said and uses it as a springboard - either for what it brings to mind, or for a "wait, which one do you mean?"

<code>というと</code> is `と`, the conditional: "**if** we say X, then…". That is why it has
two faces. Point it forward and you get automatic association - 
京都(きょうと)というと、お寺(てら). Point it back at the person who spoke and you get a
request for clarification - 田中(たなか)さんというと、営業(えいぎょう)の? Both start
from the same place: a word has landed, and you are working out what follows from it.

## Formation

The phrase attaches to whatever word you are picking up. It is a connective, so a comma
usually follows.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というと | 京都(きょうと) → 京都(きょうと)というと |
| Noun phrase | Noun phrase + というと | 冬(ふゆ)の北海道(ほっかいどう) → 冬(ふゆ)の北海道(ほっかいどう)というと |
| Clause | plain form + というと | 一人(ひとり)で行(い)く → 一人(ひとり)で行(い)くというと |
| Standing alone, as a question | というと？ | - |
| Casual | っていうと／ってゆうと | 京都(きょうと)っていうと |
| Polite | といいますと | 京都(きょうと)といいますと |
| Close relatives | といえば／といったら | 京都(きょうと)といえば |

<div class="note">

**Note** - nouns and な-adjectives attach with no `だ`:
❌<code>京都(きょうと)だというと</code>. A bare <code>というと？</code> with rising
intonation is a complete utterance meaning "meaning?" - one of the most useful two-second
phrases in the language.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
夏(なつ)<b>というと</b>、花火(はなび)を思(おも)い出(だ)します。
Summer makes me think of fireworks.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん<b>というと</b>、営業(えいぎょう)の田中(たなか)さんのことですか。
When you say Tanaka, do you mean Tanaka in sales?
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)にいい食(た)べ物(もの)<b>というと</b>、まず野菜(やさい)が頭(あたま)に浮(う)かびます。
Say "healthy food" and vegetables are the first thing that comes to mind.
</pre>

<pre>
「来月(らいげつ)から担当(たんとう)が変(か)わります。」「<b>というと</b>、山田(やまだ)さんはもう来(こ)られないんですか。」
"The person in charge changes from next month." "Meaning Yamada won't be coming any more?"
</pre>

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)の北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>というと</b>雪(ゆき)まつりが有名(ゆうめい)ですが、温泉(おんせん)もなかなかいいですよ。
Winter in Hokkaido means the snow festival to most people, but the hot springs are worth it too.
</pre>

<pre>
「例(れい)の話(はなし)、進(すす)んでる?」「例(れい)の話(はなし)<b>というと</b>、工場(こうじょう)の移転(いてん)のこと?」
"Any progress on that matter?" "That matter - you mean the factory move?"
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころの夏休(なつやす)み<b>というと</b>、毎日(まいにち)朝(あさ)から川(かわ)へ行(い)って、日(ひ)が暮(く)れるまで遊(あそ)んでいました。
Summer holidays as a child meant going down to the river every morning and playing until dark.
</pre>

<pre>
お土産(みやげ)<b>というと</b>お菓子(かし)を選(えら)びがちですが、地元(じもと)の調味料(ちょうみりょう)のほうが喜(よろこ)ばれることもあります。
People tend to default to sweets for souvenirs, but a local seasoning is sometimes better received.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>というと</code> is **reactive**. Something has to be on the table already - a word
the other person used, a place name that has just come up, a topic you yourself introduced
one sentence earlier. That is what separates it from simply starting to talk about
something.

Register is neutral. <code>といいますと</code> is the polite form and is standard in
customer-facing speech: 「来週(らいしゅう)といいますと、何日(なんにち)ごろでしょうか。」
In casual speech it becomes <code>っていうと</code> or, with the vowel stretched,
<code>ってゆうと</code>, which is very common and mildly sloppy in writing.

The clarification use deserves separate practice, because it is the politest way to say
"I'm not sure I follow." A bare <code>というと？</code> puts the burden back on the speaker
without accusing them of being unclear, and in a meeting it is far softer than
「どういう意味(いみ)ですか」. The full form
<code>…というと、…ということですか</code> is the careful version.

One thing <code>というと</code> does not do is give advice. It draws out what a word
implies; it does not tell the listener what to do about it. That job belongs to `なら`.

## Contrast

### というと vs といえば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - heavily overlapping. As a tendency: `といえば` freely *raises* a topic
by association, including one nobody asked about; <code>というと</code> leans on a word
that has just been said, and is the one that turns into a question.

</div>

<pre>
温泉(おんせん)<b>といえば</b>、来月(らいげつ)草津(くさつ)に行(い)くんです。
Speaking of hot springs - I'm off to Kusatsu next month.
</pre>

<pre>
温泉(おんせん)<b>というと</b>、やはり冬(ふゆ)に行(い)きたくなりますね。
Hot springs - you do want to go in winter, don't you.
</pre>

The first changes the subject to the speaker's own news; that is `といえば` territory, and
`そういえば` ("that reminds me") is its most common form. The second stays on the general
association. In the association use they are close to interchangeable, and nobody will
misunderstand you either way.

### というと vs といったら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `といったら` is the emotional one. It sets the noun up so you can
exclaim about it: "when it comes to X - honestly!"

</div>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)の夏(なつ)の暑(あつ)さ<b>といったら</b>、外(そと)を歩(ある)く気(き)にもなれない。
The heat of a Kyoto summer - you don't even feel like stepping outside.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)の夏(なつ)<b>というと</b>、祇園祭(ぎおんまつり)を思(おも)い出(だ)す人(ひと)が多(おお)いでしょう。
Summer in Kyoto brings the Gion Festival to mind for most people.
</pre>

`といったら` almost always ends in something extreme - an exclamation, a negative you
cannot fix, a comparison nobody could beat. Used for a flat, neutral association it sounds
like you started an exclamation and forgot to finish it.

### というと vs なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both pick up what the other person said. <code>というと</code> asks
what it means or what it evokes; `なら` accepts it as a premise and gives advice.

</div>

<pre>
「京都(きょうと)に行(い)こうと思(おも)っています。」「京都(きょうと)<b>なら</b>、秋(あき)がおすすめですよ。」
"I'm thinking of going to Kyoto." "If it's Kyoto, I'd recommend autumn."
</pre>

<pre>
「京都(きょうと)に行(い)こうと思(おも)っています。」「京都(きょうと)<b>というと</b>、お寺(てら)を回(まわ)るんですか。」
"I'm thinking of going to Kyoto." "Kyoto - so you'll be doing the temples?"
</pre>

Swap them and both replies go wrong: `なら` cannot ask what a word meant, and
<code>というと</code> cannot recommend a season.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 春(はる)になるというと、花見(はなみ)に行(い)きます。 → ✅ 春(はる)になると、花見(はなみ)に行(い)きます。
  For "when spring comes", plain `と` is the conditional you want.
  <code>というと</code> needs a *word* or a *statement* in front of it, not an event.

- ❌ 京都(きょうと)だというと、お寺(てら)ですね。 → ✅ 京都(きょうと)というと、お寺(てら)ですね。
  Nouns attach directly. The `だ` version quotes a proposition - "if we say that it is
  Kyoto" - which is not what you mean.

- ❌ 「時間(じかん)がなくて困(こま)っています。」「というと、手伝(てつだ)いましょうか。」 → ✅ 「それなら、手伝(てつだ)いましょうか。」
  <code>というと</code> opens a question about meaning, so following it with an offer
  leaves the listener waiting for the rest of the question.

- ❌ 寿司(すし)というと、私(わたし)は好(す)きです。 → ✅ 寿司(すし)といえば、この前(まえ)おいしい店(みせ)を見(み)つけました。／寿司(すし)は好(す)きです。
  What follows <code>というと</code> should be what the word calls up *in general*, or a
  question about it - not a bare personal fact. For "as for sushi, I like it", plain `は`
  is enough.

**1.** 日本(にほん)の正月(しょうがつ)____、おせち料理(りょうり)とお雑煮(ぞうに)でしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

日本(にほん)の正月(しょうがつ)<b>というと</b>、…

The sentence gives what New Year in Japan automatically calls to mind, which is the

about what to eat.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The reply takes the other person's Osaka trip as a given and offers help on that basis -

</details>

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) 山(やま)の上(うえ)の寒(さむ)さ<b>といったら</b>、手袋(てぶくろ)をしていても指(ゆび)の感覚(かんかく)がなくなるほどでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It reads as information, and it could open a guidebook paragraph.

(b) is an exclamation. `といったら` sets the noun up so the speaker can pile on how extreme
it was, and the sentence duly ends in something you could not exaggerate further. It is
speech, it is personal, and it expects a reaction.

</details>

# ～というより - rather than

**Meaning** · rejects one description as not quite right and puts a better one in its place - "it's not so much X as Y"

<code>というより</code> compares **two ways of describing the same thing**, not two things.
The first label is usually the one already in the air - the word the other person used, or
the polite word you started to reach for - and the second is what you actually mean. The
first is never called wrong, only inexact, which is why the pattern is so useful for
saying blunt things gently.

## Formation

Everything attaches in its plain form, and nouns and な-adjectives normally drop `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というより | 趣味(しゅみ) → 趣味(しゅみ)というより |
| な-adjective | な-adj + というより（no `な`） | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かというより |
| い-adjective | plain form + というより | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いというより |
| Verb | plain form + というより | 走(はし)る → 走(はし)っているというより |
| Emphatic | というよりも／というよりむしろ | 趣味(しゅみ)というよりむしろ |
| Casual | っていうより | 趣味(しゅみ)っていうより |
| Common closing | …に近(ちか)い／…のほうが近(ちか)い | 仕事(しごと)に近(ちか)い |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the **better** label goes second. English puts it first ("more a hobby
than a job"), Japanese puts it last
（<code>仕事(しごと)というより趣味(しゅみ)だ</code>）. Reversing the order reverses
the meaning, and this is the single commonest error with the pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは食事(しょくじ)<b>というより</b>おやつだね。
This is more of a snack than a meal.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は涼(すず)しい<b>というより</b>、もう寒(さむ)いくらいだ。
Today isn't so much cool as downright cold.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は静(しず)か<b>というより</b>、人(ひと)と話(はな)すのが苦手(にがて)なんだと思(おも)う。
He's not quiet so much as bad at talking to people, I think.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)の話(はなし)は、説明(せつめい)<b>というより</b>言(い)い訳(わけ)に聞(き)こえました。
What he said sounded less like an explanation than an excuse.
</pre>

<pre>
向(む)こうから来(く)る人(ひと)は、走(はし)っている<b>というより</b>急(いそ)いで歩(ある)いているように見(み)えた。
The person coming towards us looked less like they were running than walking in a hurry.
</pre>

<pre>
この値段(ねだん)は安(やす)い<b>というより</b>、安(やす)すぎて逆(ぎゃく)に心配(しんぱい)になる。
This price isn't cheap so much as suspiciously cheap.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)が毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っているのは、やせたい<b>というより</b>、一日(いちにち)を気持(きも)ちよく始(はじ)めたいからだそうです。
She runs every morning not so much to lose weight as to get the day off to a good start.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の変更(へんこう)は改善(かいぜん)<b>というより</b>、前(まえ)のやり方(かた)に戻(もど)しただけだという声(こえ)も社内(しゃない)にはあります。
Some inside the company say this change is less an improvement than simply a return to the old way.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself, but the *move* it makes is a politeness device. Naming the
gentler word first and correcting it downwards lets you land a hard judgement without
having stated it flatly: <code>まずいというより、味(あじ)が薄(うす)い</code> is far
kinder than starting with 「まずい」. The same works upwards - 
<code>上手(じょうず)というより、もうプロだ</code> - where the pattern reads as genuine
admiration rather than flattery, because you appear to be searching for the accurate word.

`むしろ` is its natural companion and adds "if anything":
<code>嫌(きら)いというより、むしろ好(す)きなほうです</code>. `も` on the end
(<code>というよりも</code>) simply adds weight. In casual speech the whole thing contracts
to <code>っていうより</code>, and in fast conversation you will hear it trailing off
entirely - 「怒(おこ)ってるっていうより…ね」 - where the listener is expected to supply
the better word.

Because the pattern is essentially a self-correction, it also works as a hedge on your own
speech. Starting a sentence over with <code>というより</code> mid-turn signals that you
are refining your point, not contradicting yourself, and it buys you a moment to find the
word you want.

## Contrast

### というより vs より

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `より` compares **two things** on one scale.
<code>というより</code> compares **two labels** for one thing. If you can insert
「のほうが」 after the second item, you want plain `より`.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)<b>より</b>今年(ことし)のほうが雨(あめ)が多(おお)い。
There's more rain this year than last.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)は雨(あめ)が多(おお)い<b>というより</b>、ずっと降(ふ)り続(つづ)いている感(かん)じだ。
It's not that there's a lot of rain this year - it feels like it simply hasn't stopped.
</pre>

In the first sentence there are two years and one quantity of rain. In the second there is
one year and two ways of putting it. That is the whole distinction, and the grammar follows
from it: `より` takes a noun, <code>というより</code> takes a full description.

### というより vs ではなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ではなく` says the first label is **wrong**.
<code>というより</code> says it is **understandable but inexact**.

</div>

<pre>
これは誤解(ごかい)<b>ではなく</b>、はっきりとした間違(まちが)いです。
This isn't a misunderstanding - it's an outright error.
</pre>

<pre>
これは誤解(ごかい)<b>というより</b>、説明(せつめい)の仕方(しかた)が悪(わる)かったのだと思(おも)います。
I'd call this less a misunderstanding than a failure to explain properly.
</pre>

`ではなく` corrects; <code>というより</code> negotiates. In a disagreement the second is
much easier to say to someone senior, because it concedes that their word was a reasonable
first attempt.

### というより vs どころか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どころか` overshoots on purpose: not X - in fact something far beyond
X, often in the opposite direction. <code>というより</code> stays calm and simply
re-labels.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は泳(およ)げない<b>どころか</b>、水(みず)に顔(かお)をつけることもできない。
Far from being unable to swim, he can't even put his face in the water.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は泳(およ)げない<b>というより</b>、水(みず)が怖(こわ)いのだろう。
It's not that he can't swim - it's more that he's afraid of the water.
</pre>

`どころか` is emphatic and often a little dramatic; <code>というより</code> is analytical.
Choosing the wrong one is not ungrammatical, but it misreports how strongly you feel.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ これはおやつというより食事(しょくじ)だ。（meaning "it's more a snack than a meal"） → ✅ これは食事(しょくじ)というよりおやつだ。
  The accurate label goes **second**. Word order alone decides which description you are
  endorsing, so a reversed pair says the opposite of what you meant.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>というより → ✅ 静(しず)かというより
  な-adjectives lose their `な` here, and they do not take `だ` either. Only the bare stem
  goes in front of <code>という</code>.

- ❌ 兄(あに)というより弟(おとうと)のほうが背(せ)が高(たか)い。 → ✅ 兄(あに)より弟(おとうと)のほうが背(せ)が高(たか)い。
  Two different people on one scale is a plain `より` comparison.
  <code>というより</code> would mean you were choosing between calling the same person an
  older or a younger brother.

- ❌ 嫌(きら)いというより。 → ✅ 嫌(きら)いというより、苦手(にがて)なんです。
  The pattern needs both halves. Stopping after the rejected label leaves the listener
  with the word you have just told them not to use. (In casual speech people do trail off
  like this - but only when the better word is obvious from context.)

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)の部屋(へや)は汚(きたな)い____、物(もの)が多(おお)すぎて片付(かたづ)けられないだけだ。

汚(きたな)い<b>というより</b>、…

The sentence sets aside "dirty" as an unfair word and offers a fairer account of the same

</details>

**2.** Choose: この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しい（より・というより）、説明(せつめい)が分(わ)かりにくいのだと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

There is only one problem here and two competing descriptions of it, so you need

The gentle label goes first and is set aside; the sharper, more accurate one follows. The

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの店(みせ)は喫茶店(きっさてん)というより静(しず)かなというより図書館(としょかん)のようだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの店(みせ)は喫茶店(きっさてん)というより図書館(としょかん)のようだ。

Two problems: な-adjectives take no `な` before <code>という</code>, and a single sentence

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)の案(あん)は新(あたら)しい<b>というより</b>、昔(むかし)のやり方(かた)に近(ちか)い。
(b) 今回(こんかい)の案(あん)は新(あたら)しい<b>ではなく</b>、昔(むかし)のやり方(かた)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) allows that someone could reasonably have called the plan new, then quietly moves the
description somewhere more accurate. It is the version you can say in a meeting without
embarrassing the person who wrote the plan.

(b) - leaving aside that it should be
<code>新(あたら)しいのではなく</code> with an い-adjective - flatly denies the first
description. It is clearer and colder, and it is what you use when the point of the
sentence is that someone was wrong.

</details>

# ～とおり - in the way; the same as

**Meaning** · exactly as some model specifies - as told, as taught, as planned, as expected

The noun behind it is <code>道(みち)</code>'s cousin <code>通(とお)り</code>, "a
route", and the metaphor still shows: something has laid down a path - an instruction, a
schedule, a prediction - and the action follows it without stepping off. What
<code>とおり</code> insists on is **exactness**. If there is room for interpretation, this
is the wrong word.

## Formation

Two things decide the shape: whether a verb or a noun comes first, and - for nouns - 
whether you use `の`. The `どおり` form with rendaku is not optional.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain non-past) | V(る) + とおり（に） | 言(い)う → 言(い)うとおりに |
| Verb (plain past) | V(た) + とおり（に） | 習(なら)う → 習(なら)ったとおりに |
| Noun, with の | Noun + の + とおり（に） | 説明(せつめい) → 説明(せつめい)のとおりに |
| Noun, bare | Noun + どおり（に） - rendaku | 予定(よてい) → 予定(よてい)どおりに |
| Modifying a noun | ～とおりの + Noun | 思(おも)ったとおりの味(あじ) |
| Demonstratives | このとおり／そのとおり／あのとおり | そのとおりです |
| Set phrases | ご覧(らん)のとおり、おっしゃるとおり、文字(もじ)どおり | - |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` + とおり, or bare noun + **どおり**. Never `の` + どおり, and never
a bare noun + とおり. <code>予定(よてい)のとおり</code> and
<code>予定(よてい)どおり</code> are both correct;
❌<code>予定(よてい)のどおり</code> and ❌<code>予定(よてい)とおり</code> are not.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `に` is dropped when the phrase is followed by `だ／です` or stands as a
comment: <code>思(おも)ったとおりだ</code>, <code>そのとおりです</code>. Keep the `に`
when it modifies a verb: <code>思(おも)ったとおりに進(すす)んだ</code>. Written in
kanji the bare-noun form is <code>予定通(よていどお)り</code> - same reading, and the
rendaku is still there.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
先生(せんせい)が言(い)った<b>とおりに</b>やってみます。
I'll try doing it exactly as the teacher said.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)は時間(じかん)<b>どおり</b>に着(つ)きました。
The train arrived right on schedule.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)の<b>とおりに</b>組(く)み立(た)てたのに、なぜか動(うご)きません。
I put it together exactly as the manual said, and for some reason it doesn't work.
</pre>

<pre>
思(おも)った<b>とおり</b>、あの店(みせ)は今日(きょう)も混(こ)んでいた。
Just as I thought, that place was packed again today.
</pre>

<pre>
「準備(じゅんび)は早(はや)めにということですね。」「その<b>とおり</b>です。」
"So you're saying we should prepare early." "Exactly."
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は予定(よてい)<b>どおり</b>進(すす)んでいますので、来月(らいげつ)には終(お)わる見(み)こみです。
The work is going to plan, so we expect it to finish next month.
</pre>

<pre>
ご覧(らん)の<b>とおり</b>、会場(かいじょう)はもう満席(まんせき)ですので、立(た)ち見(み)になってしまいます。
As you can see, the hall is already full, so you'd have to stand.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)に教(おそ)わった<b>とおりに</b>作(つく)ったつもりでしたが、味(あじ)はまったく別物(べつもの)になってしまいました。
I thought I'd made it just the way my mother taught me, but it came out tasting like something else entirely.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral and equally at home in a manual, a news report and a
kitchen. What varies is the fixed phrases built on it, and several of them are worth
learning as units.

<code>そのとおりです</code> is strong agreement - "precisely so" - and is common in
business Japanese, where a bare 「はい」 can sound thin. Its polite version,
<code>おっしゃるとおりです</code>, agrees with a superior and is one of the most useful
phrases in an office; <code>おっしゃるとおりですが…</code> is how you begin to disagree
politely. <code>ご覧(らん)のとおり</code> ("as you can see") belongs to presentations,
and <code>文字(もじ)どおり</code> means "literally".

The bare-noun `どおり` forms behave almost like single words - 
<code>予定(よてい)どおり</code>, <code>時間(じかん)どおり</code>,
<code>計画(けいかく)どおり</code>, <code>期待(きたい)どおり</code>,
<code>注文(ちゅうもん)どおり</code> - and they carry a faint official tone, the sound of
things going as they were supposed to. The verb forms with `とおりに` are more personal:
someone told you, taught you or predicted, and you matched it.

One tense point that trips people up: both <code>言(い)うとおりに</code> and
<code>言(い)ったとおりに</code> are correct, and they are not quite the same. The
non-past form is habitual or still ongoing - "as he says", the way he always tells you.
The past form points at one particular occasion - "as he said (that time)".

## Contrast

### とおりに vs ように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とおりに</code> means "matching the model exactly". `ように`
means "in a manner resembling", and it also does purpose ("so that"), which
<code>とおりに</code> cannot do at all.

</div>

<pre>
見本(みほん)の<b>とおりに</b>書(か)いてください。
Please write it exactly like the sample.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもにも読(よ)める<b>ように</b>、大(おお)きく書(か)いてください。
Please write it large, so that children can read it too.
</pre>

The second sentence has no model to copy - it states a goal. Note also that `ように` can
take a resemblance with no instruction behind it
（<code>鳥(とり)のように飛(と)ぶ</code>）, where <code>とおりに</code> would be
nonsense: a bird has issued no instructions.

### とおりに vs まま

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とおりに</code> is about **following** something. `まま` is
about **not changing** something. One copies a model; the other leaves a state alone.

</div>

<pre>
習(なら)った<b>とおりに</b>包丁(ほうちょう)を使(つか)えば、指(ゆび)を切(き)ることはありません。
If you use the knife the way you were taught, you won't cut your finger.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)けた<b>まま</b>出(で)かけてしまいました。
I went out and left the window open.
</pre>

The two look similar after a past-tense verb, which is where the confusion starts. Ask what
the previous action is doing: supplying a pattern to imitate (<code>とおりに</code>), or
supplying a condition that persists (`まま`)?

### とおりに vs にしたがって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にしたがって</code> is the formal "in accordance with", used for
rules, orders and procedures. It also has a second life meaning "as X progresses, Y", which
<code>とおりに</code> never has.

</div>

<pre>
係員(かかりいん)の指示(しじ)<b>にしたがって</b>移動(いどう)してください。
Please move in accordance with the staff's instructions.
</pre>

<pre>
係員(かかりいん)が言(い)った<b>とおりに</b>階段(かいだん)を下(お)りました。
We went down the stairs exactly as the attendant told us.
</pre>

<code>にしたがって</code> sounds like a public announcement; <code>とおりに</code> sounds
like a person recounting what they did. For the "as X increases" meaning - 
<code>年(とし)をとるにしたがって</code> - only <code>したがって</code> works.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 予定(よてい)のどおり進(すす)んでいます。 → ✅ 予定(よてい)どおり／予定(よてい)のとおり進(すす)んでいます。
  Pick one: `の` plus <code>とおり</code>, or a bare noun plus `どおり`. The two cannot be
  combined, and this is the single most common error with the pattern.

- ❌ 地図(ちず)とおりに歩(ある)きました。 → ✅ 地図(ちず)どおりに歩(ある)きました。
  A noun attaching directly triggers rendaku. <code>とおり</code> only stays unvoiced
  after `の` or after a verb.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)が言(い)ったのとおりに → ✅ 先生(せんせい)が言(い)ったとおりに
  No `の` after a verb. The `の` in <code>説明(せつめい)のとおり</code> belongs to the
  noun, not to the pattern.

- ❌ 鳥(とり)のとおりに空(そら)を飛(と)んでみたい。 → ✅ 鳥(とり)のように空(そら)を飛(と)んでみたい。
  <code>とおりに</code> needs an instruction, plan or prediction to follow. For plain
  resemblance you want `ように`.

## Practice

**1.** バスは時間(じかん)____来(き)ませんでしたが、会議(かいぎ)には間(ま)に合(あ)いました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

バスは時間(じかん)<b>どおりに</b>来(き)ませんでしたが、…

A bare noun in front means the rendaku form `どおり`. Writing
<code>時間(じかん)のとおりに</code> is grammatical but sounds oddly heavy;
<code>時間(じかん)どおり</code> is the set phrase everyone uses.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 子(こ)どもが喜(よろこ)ぶ（とおりに・ように）部屋(へや)を飾(かざ)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)どもが喜(よろこ)ぶ<b>ように</b>部屋(へや)を飾(かざ)りました。

This is a purpose - decorating *so that* the children will be pleased - and purpose is
`ように` only. <code>とおりに</code> would require the children to have specified how the
room was to be decorated.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It turned out exactly as she predicted."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)が言(い)った<b>とおりに</b>なりました。

A prediction is a model that reality then matched, which is exactly what
<code>とおり</code> is for. The past-tense verb points at the one occasion when she said
it. <code>彼女(かのじょ)の予想(よそう)どおりになりました</code> is an equally
natural noun version.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 係(かかり)の人(ひと)が説明(せつめい)したのとおりに、書類(しょるい)を出(だ)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 係(かかり)の人(ひと)が説明(せつめい)したとおりに、書類(しょるい)を出(だ)しました。

Remove the `の`. Verbs attach straight to <code>とおり</code>; only nouns take `の`, and a
noun version here would be
<code>説明(せつめい)のとおりに</code> or <code>説明(せつめい)どおりに</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)が言(い)う<b>とおりに</b>やっています。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)が言(い)った<b>とおりに</b>やりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the non-past, so the model is standing and general: this is how the manager
always wants it done, and you are still doing it that way. It describes a working
practice.

(b) uses the past, which pins the instruction to one occasion - he said something, and you
did precisely that. It is the sentence you produce when the result has gone wrong and you
would like it on record whose instructions you were following.

</details>

# ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand

**Meaning** · sets two true sides of the same situation next to each other, without cancelling either

<code>一方(いっぽう)</code> is "one side". The pattern says: here is one side, and here
is the other, and both are the case. That is what separates it from `でも` and `しかし`,
which put a stronger fact on top of a weaker one. <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code>
weighs; it does not overrule. It is also how you say that one person or one thing is doing
two things at once.

## Formation

The clause in front is plain form. Only nouns and な-adjectives need help joining.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + 一方(いっぽう)（で） | 働(はたら)く → 働(はたら)く一方(いっぽう)で |
| い-adjective | plain form + 一方(いっぽう)で | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)い一方(いっぽう)で |
| な-adjective | ～な／～である + 一方(いっぽう)で | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)な一方(いっぽう)で |
| Noun | Noun + である + 一方(いっぽう)で | 教師(きょうし) → 教師(きょうし)である一方(いっぽう)で |
| Past tense | V(た) + 一方(いっぽう)で | 増(ふ)えた → 増(ふ)えた一方(いっぽう)で |
| Starting a new sentence | 一方(いっぽう)、／その一方(いっぽう)で、 | 一方(いっぽう)、地方(ちほう)では… |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - do not confuse <code>～する一方(いっぽう)で</code> with
<code>～する一方(いっぽう)だ</code>. The first joins two sides; the second means "keeps
getting more so, and only that". <code>物価(ぶっか)が上(あ)がる一方(いっぽう)だ</code>
means prices just keep climbing, and it needs no second clause at all.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
兄(あに)は働(はたら)く<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、大学(だいがく)にも通(かよ)っています。
My brother works and goes to university at the same time.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)は静(しず)かでいい<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、買(か)い物(もの)が不便(ふべん)です。
This town is nicely quiet, but on the other side of that, shopping is inconvenient.
</pre>

<pre>
スマホは便利(べんり)な<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、時間(じかん)をうばうものでもある。
Smartphones are convenient, and by the same token they eat your time.
</pre>

<pre>
都会(とかい)の人口(じんこう)は増(ふ)え続(つづ)けている。<b>一方(いっぽう)</b>、地方(ちほう)では人(ひと)が減(へ)っている。
City populations keep growing. In the regions, meanwhile, people are leaving.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は厳(きび)しい<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、困(こま)ったときには必(かなら)ず助(たす)けてくれました。
My father was strict, and at the same time he never failed to help when I was in trouble.
</pre>

<pre>
この仕事(しごと)は給料(きゅうりょう)がいい<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、休(やす)みがほとんど取(と)れないそうです。
The pay in that job is good, but on the other hand you can hardly take any time off.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)は若(わか)い社員(しゃいん)に喜(よろこ)ばれている<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、長(なが)く勤(つと)めてきた人(ひと)たちからは不満(ふまん)の声(こえ)も出(で)ています。
The new system has gone down well with younger staff, while long-serving employees have voiced complaints.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は畑(はたけ)の仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)ける<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、週(しゅう)に一度(いちど)は町(まち)の教室(きょうしつ)で習字(しゅうじ)を習(なら)っています。
My grandmother keeps working her field, and on top of that goes into town once a week for a calligraphy class.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** pattern first. It belongs to newspaper articles, reports, essays and
prepared presentations, and it is the standard way to present a balanced picture: an
advantage and its cost, a national trend and its regional exception, a policy and the
grumbling it caused. In casual conversation people join the same two ideas with `けど`, `し`
or 「でも」, and reaching for <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> over a drink sounds like
you are reading from notes.

The tone is **even-handed**, and this is what learners most often miss. Because the
construction gives both clauses equal footing, the writer comes across as fair rather than
critical, which is why it appears so often in business writing where a flat complaint would
be impolitic. If you want the second clause to win, use `が` or `しかし`.

The two clauses do not have to be opposites. A perfectly ordinary use is simple
simultaneity - one subject doing two things
（<code>働(はたら)く一方(いっぽう)で大学(だいがく)にも通(かよ)う</code>） - 
where English reaches for "while also". When the clauses *are* opposites, the `で` is often
dropped in more formal writing:
<code>増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)、質(しつ)は落(お)ちている</code>. Sentence-initial <code>一方(いっぽう)、</code> and
<code>その一方(いっぽう)で、</code> do the same job across a full stop and are extremely
common in news writing.

## Contrast

### 一方(いっぽう)で vs 反面(はんめん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>反面(はんめん)</code> needs the two sides to be **opposite in
evaluation**: a merit and its matching drawback, in the same subject.
<code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> is wider - the two clauses may simply be parallel, and may
even have different subjects.

</div>

<pre>
この素材(そざい)は軽(かる)い<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、傷(きず)がつきやすい。
This material is light, but by the same token it scratches easily.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)は銀行(ぎんこう)で働(はたら)く<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、週末(しゅうまつ)はボランティアもしています。
My sister works at a bank, and on top of that volunteers at weekends.
</pre>

Put <code>反面(はんめん)</code> in the second sentence and it goes wrong, because
volunteering is not the downside of banking. Anywhere
<code>反面(はんめん)</code> works, <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> also works; the
reverse is not true.

### 一方(いっぽう)で vs が・しかし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` and `しかし` make the second clause the point.
<code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> leaves both clauses standing. If you are building to a
conclusion, you want the adversative; if you are describing a situation, you want
<code>一方(いっぽう)で</code>.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は安(やす)い<b>が</b>、品質(ひんしつ)が心配(しんぱい)だ。
It's cheap, but I'm worried about the quality.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が安(やす)い<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、品質(ひんしつ)にはばらつきがあるようだ。
While the price is low, the quality seems to vary.
</pre>

The first is heading towards a decision not to buy. The second is a report, and the reader
is left to weigh it. In a recommendation or a complaint, `が` is what you want; in an
analysis, it can sound like you have prejudged.

### ～一方(いっぽう)で vs ～一方(いっぽう)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `で` it needs a second clause and means "on the other side of
that". With `だ` it ends the sentence and means "keeps going that way, more and more".

</div>

<pre>
外国人(がいこくじん)の観光客(かんこうきゃく)は増(ふ)える<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
The number of foreign tourists just keeps rising.
</pre>

<pre>
観光客(かんこうきゃく)が増(ふ)える<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、地元(じもと)の生活(せいかつ)は不便(ふべん)になっている。
As visitor numbers rise, life for locals is getting less convenient.
</pre>

<code>～一方(いっぽう)だ</code> takes only a non-past verb of change - 
増(ふ)える, 減(へ)る, 悪(わる)くなる - and always carries a sense that the trend
is one-way and usually unwelcome.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この方法(ほうほう)は簡単(かんたん)だ一方(いっぽう)で、時間(じかん)がかかる。 → ✅ この方法(ほうほう)は簡単(かんたん)な一方(いっぽう)で、時間(じかん)がかかる。
  な-adjectives take `な` (or the formal `である`), never plain `だ`.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)一方(いっぽう)で、小説(しょうせつ)も書(か)いている。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は医者(いしゃ)である一方(いっぽう)で、小説(しょうせつ)も書(か)いている。
  A noun cannot attach bare. It needs `である` - and note that this makes the sentence
  distinctly written in tone.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った一方(いっぽう)で、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったので、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  Cause and effect is not two sides of anything.
  <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> joins clauses that are independently true, not a reason
  and its result.

- ❌ 仕事(しごと)が増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)で。 → ✅ 仕事(しごと)が増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)だ。
  If you mean "the work just keeps piling up" and have no second clause, you want
  <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code>. With `で` the sentence is left hanging.

## Practice

**1.** この学校(がっこう)は授業料(じゅぎょうりょう)が安(やす)い____、設備(せつび)が古(ふる)いという問題(もんだい)もある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

安(やす)い<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、…

Two facts about the same school, both true, neither cancelling the other - the balanced
frame. <code>反面(はんめん)</code> would also fit here, since cheap fees and old
facilities really are two sides of one coin.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 事故(じこ)の件数(けんすう)は減(へ)る（一方(いっぽう)だ・一方(いっぽう)で）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

事故(じこ)の件数(けんすう)は減(へ)る<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。

The sentence ends there, so it has to be the `だ` form: accident numbers keep falling. The
`で` form is a connective and demands a second clause.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This job pays well; on the other hand, you can never plan your weekends."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この仕事(しごと)は収入(しゅうにゅう)がいい<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、週末(しゅうまつ)の予定(よてい)が立(た)てられません。

The two clauses are the up side and the down side of one job, given equal weight, which is
what <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> is for. Using `が` would tip the sentence towards a
complaint.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この地域(ちいき)は自然(しぜん)が豊(ゆた)かだ一方(いっぽう)で、交通(こうつう)が不便(ふべん)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この地域(ちいき)は自然(しぜん)が豊(ゆた)かな一方(いっぽう)で、交通(こうつう)が不便(ふべん)だ。

<code>豊(ゆた)か</code> is a な-adjective, so it joins with `な`. The `だ` form only
appears before <code>という</code>-type quoting, not before
<code>一方(いっぽう)で</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 外国語(がいこくご)の授業(じゅぎょう)を増(ふ)やしたが、効果(こうか)は出(で)ていない。
(b) 外国語(がいこくご)の授業(じゅぎょう)を増(ふ)やした<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、効果(こうか)はまだ見(み)えてこない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses `が`, so the second clause is the punchline: the extra lessons did not work. It
reads as a criticism, and the reader expects a proposal to follow.

(b) presents both facts as findings and stops short of a verdict. It is the sentence you
write in a report you have to show to the people who added the lessons - even-handed,
and noticeably more diplomatic.

</details>

# ～だけでなく - not only… but also

**Meaning** · the first item is true, and it is not the whole story - something further comes with it

<code>だけ</code> draws a boundary; <code>でなく</code> says the boundary is in the wrong
place. Together they set up a second item that the listener was not counting on. This is
the everyday, neutral member of the "not only… but also" family - the one you can use in
a meeting, an essay or a conversation without anyone noticing the choice.

## Formation

The clause or noun in front takes its plain form. The **second half almost always carries
`も`**, and leaving it out is the commonest thing that makes these sentences sound
unfinished.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だけでなく | 値段(ねだん) → 値段(ねだん)だけでなく |
| Noun + が／を | particle is **dropped** | ❌漢字(かんじ)をだけでなく → ✅漢字(かんじ)だけでなく |
| Noun + に／で／と | particle is **kept** | 子(こ)どもに → 子(こ)どもにだけでなく |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけでなく | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いだけでなく |
| な-adjective | ～な + だけでなく | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)なだけでなく |
| Verb | plain form + だけでなく | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)むだけでなく |
| Second clause | …も／…まで／…さえ | …味(あじ)もいい |
| Casual | だけじゃなく（て） | 値段(ねだん)だけじゃなくて |
| Formal writing | のみならず | 値段(ねだん)のみならず |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both halves must point the **same way**. A good point followed by
another good point, or a complaint followed by a worse one. If the second half reverses the
first, you want `が` or <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code>, not <code>だけでなく</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>だけでなく</b>、味(あじ)もいいです。
This place isn't just cheap - the food is good too.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は英語(えいご)<b>だけでなく</b>、フランス語(ご)も話(はな)せます。
She speaks not only English but French as well.
</pre>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)は眠(ねむ)くなる<b>だけでなく</b>、食欲(しょくよく)も落(お)ちるそうです。
This medicine doesn't just make you drowsy - apparently it kills your appetite too.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まった<b>だけでなく</b>、道路(どうろ)も通(とお)れなくなった。
The typhoon didn't just stop the trains; the roads became impassable as well.
</pre>

<pre>
この仕事(しごと)は経験(けいけん)<b>だけでなく</b>、体力(たいりょく)も必要(ひつよう)です。
This job takes stamina as well as experience.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は畑(はたけ)の野菜(やさい)を近所(きんじょ)に配(くば)る<b>だけでなく</b>、料理(りょうり)まで作(つく)って届(とど)けています。
My grandfather doesn't just hand out vegetables from his field - he cooks meals and delivers those too.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいアプリは操作(そうさ)が簡単(かんたん)な<b>だけでなく</b>、外国語(がいこくご)にも対応(たいおう)しているので、旅行先(りょこうさき)でも使(つか)えます。
The new app isn't just easy to operate - it handles foreign languages too, so you can use it while travelling.
</pre>

<pre>
今度(こんど)の大会(たいかい)には市内(しない)の学校(がっこう)<b>だけでなく</b>、隣(となり)の県(けん)からも参加(さんか)するチームがあるそうです。
I hear teams are coming to this tournament from the neighbouring prefecture as well as from schools in the city.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the **neutral default** of its family. It is equally natural in ます-form speech,
in plain-form conversation, in a report and in a school essay, and nothing about it marks
you as formal or casual. The casual contraction <code>だけじゃなく</code> - often
<code>だけじゃなくて</code> - is what you actually hear in relaxed talk, and it is the only
register move the pattern makes. At the other end, <code>のみならず</code> is the written
formal equivalent and belongs in documents rather than speech.

The `も` in the second half is doing real work: it marks the second item as an **addition**
to the first, which is what stops the sentence sounding like a correction. Compare
<code>安(やす)いだけでなく、味(あじ)もいい</code> with
<code>安(やす)いだけでなく、味(あじ)がいい</code> - the second is not wrong, but it
reads as if the taste were a replacement for the cheapness rather than a bonus on top. When
the second item goes further than you might expect, `まで` or `さえ` replaces `も` and adds
a note of surprise.

One use worth naming: <code>だけでなく</code> is a tactful way to widen a demand. Saying
<code>今回(こんかい)だけでなく、次回(じかい)からもお願(ねが)いします</code>
adds a request without repeating it, because the first half is already agreed.

## Contrast

### だけでなく vs ばかりでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same structure, one shade apart in register.
<code>だけでなく</code> is neutral and the more frequent in speech;
<code>ばかりでなく</code> leans written and puts a little more weight on the second item.

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は説明(せつめい)が詳(くわ)しい<b>だけでなく</b>、例文(れいぶん)も多(おお)い。
This dictionary isn't just detailed in its explanations - it has plenty of examples too.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は一(ひと)つの会社(かいしゃ)<b>ばかりでなく</b>、業界(ぎょうかい)全体(ぜんたい)にかかわっている。
This issue concerns not just one company but the industry as a whole.
</pre>

You could swap them in either sentence and be understood. The difference is the room they
sit in: the first belongs to a conversation about a dictionary, the second to a newspaper.

### だけでなく vs うえに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけでなく</code> corrects an assumption: "you thought it was
just X - it's Y as well." <code>上(うえ)に</code> simply piles on: "X, and on top of
that Y." Only <code>だけでなく</code> implies somebody was underestimating.

</div>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>、日(ひ)も当(あ)たらない。
This room is cramped, and on top of that it gets no sun.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>だけでなく</b>、日(ひ)も当(あ)たらないんです。
It isn't just that the room is small - it gets no sun either.
</pre>

The first is a straight list of grievances. The second answers someone who thought the
size was the only problem. Note also that <code>上(うえ)に</code> is almost always
used within one topic and one evaluation direction, while <code>だけでなく</code> can span
two separate subjects.

### だけでなく vs はもちろん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>はもちろん</code> presents the first item as **too obvious to
argue about** and moves straight to the second. <code>だけでなく</code> makes no such
claim; it treats both items as news.

</div>

<pre>
この温泉(おんせん)は地元(じもと)の人(ひと)<b>はもちろん</b>、外国(がいこく)からの客(きゃく)にも人気(にんき)があります。
This hot spring is popular with locals, needless to say, and with visitors from abroad too.
</pre>

<pre>
この温泉(おんせん)は地元(じもと)の人(ひと)<b>だけでなく</b>、外国(がいこく)からの客(きゃく)にも人気(にんき)があります。
This hot spring is popular not only with locals but with visitors from abroad.
</pre>

Nearly interchangeable. The first flatters the reader slightly - of course you knew about
the locals - and is common in advertising for that reason. The second is plainer and safer
when the first item may genuinely be new information.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 英語(えいご)だけでなく、フランス語(ご)が話(はな)せます。 → ✅ 英語(えいご)だけでなく、フランス語(ご)<b>も</b>話(はな)せます。
  The second half needs `も` (or `まで`, `さえ`). Without it, the addition reads as a
  substitution and the sentence feels cut short.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は便利(べんり)だけでなく、安(やす)いです。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は便利(べんり)<b>な</b>だけでなく、安(やす)くもあります。
  な-adjectives keep their `な` in front of <code>だけ</code>. The `だ` form never appears
  here.

- ❌ 漢字(かんじ)をだけでなく、文法(ぶんぽう)も勉強(べんきょう)した。 → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)だけでなく、文法(ぶんぽう)も勉強(べんきょう)した。
  `が` and `を` drop before <code>だけ</code>. `に`, `で` and `と` stay:
  <code>友(とも)だちにだけでなく</code>.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いだけでなく、まずいです。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いですが、まずいです。
  The two halves have to run in the same direction. A good point followed by a bad one is a
  contrast, and needs `が` or `けれど`.

**1.** この町(まち)は交通(こうつう)が便利(べんり)な____、家賃(やちん)____安(やす)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

便利(べんり)<b>なだけでなく</b>、家賃(やちん)<b>も</b>安(やす)い。

The な-adjective keeps `な`, and the second half takes `も` to mark the low rent as an
addition rather than a replacement.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)は遅刻(ちこく)した（だけでなく・一方(いっぽう)で）、資料(しりょう)も忘(わす)れてきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は遅刻(ちこく)した<b>だけでなく</b>、資料(しりょう)も忘(わす)れてきた。

Both halves are complaints running the same way, which is what <code>だけでなく</code> is
for. <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> would present them as two balanced sides, which makes
no sense when both are failings.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

An い-adjective attaches in its plain form, and the second benefit takes `も`. Note that
English "not only… but also" maps onto this pattern almost mechanically, as long as both
halves are positive.

</details>

✅ 地震(じしん)で電気(でんき)が止(と)まっただけでなく、水(みず)<b>も</b>出(で)なくなりました。

Change `が` to `も` in the second clause. The water is being added to the list of things

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) この旅館(りょかん)は料理(りょうり)がおいしい<b>だけでなく</b>、部屋(へや)も広(ひろ)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) stacks two good points as a straightforward list. It reads like someone
enthusiastically recommending an inn they enjoyed.

(b) carries a quiet correction: you may have heard about the food, but that is not all
there is to it. It fits a conversation where the food has already been mentioned, and it
is the version that answers an assumption rather than simply reporting.

</details>

# ～ばかりでなく - not only… but also; as well as

**Meaning** · one item is not the limit of it - and what follows often goes further than the listener expected

Structurally this is the twin of <code>だけでなく</code>: first item, then a second one
added with `も`, `まで` or `さえ`. What <code>ばかり</code> brings is the attitude it
always brings. It says the first item was being treated as the whole picture, and it very
often introduces a second item that is heavier, worse or more surprising than the first.
It also leans towards the written page.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ばかりでなく | 水(みず) → 水(みず)ばかりでなく |
| Noun + が／を | particle is **dropped** | ❌水(みず)をばかりでなく → ✅水(みず)ばかりでなく |
| Noun + に／で／から | particle is **kept** | 会社(かいしゃ)に → 会社(かいしゃ)にばかりでなく |
| い-adjective | plain form + ばかりでなく | 強(つよ)い → 強(つよ)いばかりでなく |
| な-adjective | ～な + ばかりでなく | きれい → きれいなばかりでなく |
| Verb | plain form + ばかりでなく | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)むばかりでなく |
| Second clause | …も／…まで／…さえ | …電気(でんき)も止(と)まる |
| Stronger variant | ばかりか | 水(みず)ばかりか |
| Casual | ばかりじゃなく | 水(みず)ばかりじゃなく |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ばかりでなく</code> and <code>ばかりか</code> are not interchangeable at
the end of the sentence. <code>ばかりでなく</code> can be followed by a request, a
suggestion or an instruction; <code>ばかりか</code> only reports.
✅<code>水(みず)ばかりでなく、食料(しょくりょう)も用意(ようい)してください</code> /
❌<code>水(みず)ばかりか、食料(しょくりょう)も用意(ようい)してください</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は野球(やきゅう)<b>ばかりでなく</b>、水泳(すいえい)も得意(とくい)だ。
He's good not just at baseball but at swimming too.
</pre>

<pre>
この花(はな)は色(いろ)がきれいな<b>ばかりでなく</b>、香(かお)りもいい。
This flower isn't just a fine colour - it smells good as well.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れた<b>ばかりでなく</b>、謝(あやま)りもしなかった。
Not only did he forget the arrangement, he didn't even apologise.
</pre>

<pre>
災害(さいがい)のときは、水(みず)<b>ばかりでなく</b>、電気(でんき)やガスも止(と)まることがあります。
In a disaster it isn't only the water that stops - electricity and gas can go too.
</pre>

<pre>
本(ほん)を読(よ)む<b>ばかりでなく</b>、実際(じっさい)に書(か)いてみることが大切(たいせつ)です。
It matters to actually write, not just to read.
</pre>

<pre>
その事故(じこ)で彼(かれ)は仕事(しごと)を失(うしな)った<b>ばかりでなく</b>、家族(かぞく)とも離(はな)れて暮(く)らすことになった。
The accident cost him not only his job but life with his family.
</pre>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は働(はたら)く人(ひと)<b>ばかりでなく</b>、会社(かいしゃ)にとっても大(おお)きな利益(りえき)があると言(い)われています。
The system is said to benefit not just employees but employers substantially as well.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)の家(いえ)では、正月(しょうがつ)になると親戚(しんせき)<b>ばかりでなく</b>、近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)まで集(あつ)まってきてにぎやかになります。
At my grandmother's house, New Year brings not just the relatives but the neighbours too, and the place fills up.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>ばかりでなく</code> is the **written-leaning** member of the family. It is at home in
newspapers, reports, formal letters and public announcements, and it is perfectly usable in
careful speech - but in relaxed conversation most speakers say
<code>だけでなく</code> or <code>だけじゃなくて</code>. Nothing about it is stiff enough
to sound wrong out loud; it simply sounds considered.

The attitude carried over from plain <code>ばかり</code> is worth noticing. The first item
is presented as something that was being taken for the whole, and the second often lands
harder - which is why the pattern is so common in accounts of things going wrong:
<code>遅(おく)れたばかりでなく、連絡(れんらく)もなかった</code>. When the second item
is genuinely startling, `まで` or `さえ` replaces `も` and the sentence tips into
indignation or admiration.

<code>ばかりか</code> is the same idea intensified, and it is common in writing. Two
practical differences: it is more emphatic, and it cannot be followed by a request,
suggestion or command - a restriction <code>ばかりでなく</code> does not share. That makes
<code>ばかりでなく</code> the safer choice whenever your sentence is heading towards
「～てください」 or 「～べきだ」.

## Contrast

### ばかりでなく vs だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same grammar, different room. <code>だけでなく</code> is neutral and
dominates speech; <code>ばかりでなく</code> is a shade more formal and puts more weight on
what follows.

</div>

<pre>
この大学(だいがく)は授業料(じゅぎょうりょう)が高(たか)い<b>だけでなく</b>、寮(りょう)の費用(ひよう)もかかります。
This university isn't just expensive in tuition - there are dormitory costs as well.
</pre>

<pre>
環境(かんきょう)の問題(もんだい)は一(ひと)つの国(くに)<b>ばかりでなく</b>、世界(せかい)全体(ぜんたい)で考(かんが)えるべきだ。
Environmental problems must be considered not by one country alone but by the world as a whole.
</pre>

Put <code>ばかりでなく</code> in a chat about dormitory fees and it sounds like you are
quoting a brochure. Put <code>だけでなく</code> in the second sentence and nothing breaks - 
it simply reads a little lighter.

### ばかりでなく vs ばかりか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ばかりか</code> is stronger and reports only.
<code>ばかりでなく</code> is milder and can lead into a request or a recommendation.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は道(みち)を教(おし)えてくれた<b>ばかりか</b>、駅(えき)まで送(おく)ってくれた。
He not only told me the way - he walked me to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>ばかりでなく</b>、大人(おとな)も一緒(いっしょ)に参加(さんか)してください。
Please join in together - adults as well as children.
</pre>

Reverse them and the second sentence goes wrong: <code>ばかりか</code> cannot carry an
invitation. The first sentence would survive the swap, just with less punch.

### ばかりでなく vs plain ばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain <code>ばかり</code> says "**nothing but** X, and that's too
much". <code>ばかりでなく</code> says "**not only** X - Y as well". Same word, opposite
work: one narrows, the other widens.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は漫画(まんが)<b>ばかり</b>読(よ)んでいる。
My brother reads nothing but comics.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は漫画(まんが)<b>ばかりでなく</b>、小説(しょうせつ)も読(よ)むようになった。
My brother has started reading novels as well as comics.
</pre>

The giveaway is <code>でなく</code>, which negates the boundary. Learners who have met
<code>ばかり</code> first often read the second sentence as a complaint; it is the
opposite - a small piece of good news.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)はきれいばかりでなく、静(しず)かだ。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)はきれい<b>な</b>ばかりでなく、静(しず)か<b>でもある</b>。
  な-adjectives keep `な` in front of <code>ばかり</code>, and the second half needs
  something (`も`, `でもある`) marking it as an addition.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)をばかりでなく、お金(かね)もかかる。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)ばかりでなく、お金(かね)もかかる。
  `を` and `が` disappear before <code>ばかり</code>. `に`, `で`, `から` and `と` survive.

- ❌ 水(みず)ばかりか、食料(しょくりょう)も持(も)っていってください。 → ✅ 水(みず)ばかりでなく、食料(しょくりょう)も持(も)っていってください。
  <code>ばかりか</code> can only report a state of affairs. Once the sentence ends in a
  request or an instruction, you need <code>ばかりでなく</code>.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はゲームばかりでなくしている。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はゲームばかりしている。
  If you mean "he does nothing but play games", that is plain <code>ばかり</code>.
  <code>ばかりでなく</code> requires a second item to follow.

## Practice

The first season is presented as not being the whole story, and the second is added with
`も`. <code>だけでなく</code> would fit equally well and sound slightly more conversational.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

説明(せつめい)を聞(き)く<b>ばかりでなく</b>、自分(じぶん)でも試(ため)してみてください。

The sentence ends in a request, and <code>ばかりか</code> cannot be followed by one. It
reports what happened; it does not tell anyone what to do.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He lost his wallet, and on top of that his phone was stolen."

Both halves are misfortunes running the same way, and the second is worse than the first - 
which is why `まで` rather than `も` is natural here. <code>ばかりか</code> would also work,
with more indignation.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの人(ひと)は親切(しんせつ)ばかりでなく、仕事(しごと)も早(はや)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの人(ひと)は親切(しんせつ)<b>な</b>ばかりでなく、仕事(しごと)も早(はや)い。

<code>親切(しんせつ)</code> is a な-adjective and needs its `な` before
<code>ばかり</code>. Only nouns attach bare.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 息子(むすこ)は野菜(やさい)<b>ばかり</b>食(た)べている。
(b) 息子(むすこ)は野菜(やさい)<b>ばかりでなく</b>、魚(さかな)も食(た)べるようになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) draws a boundary and complains about it: vegetables and nothing else. Plain
<code>ばかり</code> nearly always carries the sense that there is too much of one thing.

(b) breaks that boundary open. The <code>でなく</code> negates the "only", so the sentence
reports an expansion - fish has joined the list - and the tone flips from complaint to
mild satisfaction.

</details>

# ～か何か - or something

**Meaning** · names one example and deliberately leaves the door open - "X, or something like it"

<code>か何(なに)か</code> is `か` ("or") plus <code>何(なに)か</code> ("something"),
and it means exactly what the parts say: this, or whatever else would do. You use it when
you do not know
（<code>風邪(かぜ)か何(なに)かだろう</code>）, when it does not matter
（<code>お茶(ちゃ)か何(なに)か買(か)ってくる</code>）, or when being precise would
sound demanding. It has a small family - <code>か誰(だれ)か</code> for people and
<code>かどこか</code> for places - built exactly the same way.

## Formation

The whole phrase behaves as a single noun, so any particle attaches after it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - thing | Noun + か何(なに)か | コーヒー → コーヒーか何(なに)か |
| Noun - person | Noun + か誰(だれ)か | 田中(たなか)さん → 田中(たなか)さんか誰(だれ)か |
| Noun - place | Noun + かどこか | 京都(きょうと) → 京都(きょうと)かどこか |
| Modifying a noun | ～か何(なに)かの + Noun | 病気(びょうき)か何(なに)かの理由(りゆう) |
| With が／を | particle usually **dropped** in speech | ペンか何(なに)か（を）貸(か)して |
| With other particles | particle follows the phrase | 山田(やまだ)さんか誰(だれ)か<b>に</b>聞(き)く |
| As a predicate | ～か何(なに)かだ／でしょう | 風邪(かぜ)か何(なに)かでしょう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the question word has to match the category. Things take
<code>何(なに)か</code>, people take <code>誰(だれ)か</code>, places take
`どこか`. ❌<code>田中(たなか)さんか何(なに)か</code> is not vague - it is rude,
because it files a person under "things".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒー<b>か何(なに)か</b>飲(の)みませんか。
Would you like a coffee or something?
</pre>

<pre>
かばんの中(なか)にペン<b>か何(なに)か</b>入(はい)っていませんか。
Is there a pen or something in your bag?
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるみたいだから、風邪(かぜ)<b>か何(なに)か</b>だと思(おも)う。
I seem to have a temperature, so I think it's a cold or something.
</pre>

<pre>
お礼(れい)にお菓子(かし)<b>か何(なに)か</b>送(おく)ろうと思(おも)っています。
I'm thinking of sending sweets or something as a thank-you.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)にコンビニ<b>か何(なに)か</b>ありませんか。
Is there a convenience store or anything around here?
</pre>

<pre>
さっきから外(そと)で音(おと)がするけど、風(かぜ)<b>か何(なに)か</b>かな。
There's been a noise outside for a while - the wind or something, maybe.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に連絡(れんらく)が取(と)れなくなったので、事故(じこ)<b>か何(なに)か</b>にあったのではないかと心配(しんぱい)しています。
I suddenly can't reach him, and I'm worried he's had an accident or something.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で行(い)くのが不安(ふあん)なら、山田(やまだ)さん<b>か誰(だれ)か</b>に一緒(いっしょ)に来(き)てもらったらどうですか。
If you're nervous about going alone, why not ask Yamada or somebody to come with you?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is neutral in politeness and extremely common in speech. What it adds is
**vagueness on purpose**, and vagueness is a social tool. Asking
<code>ペンか何(なに)か貸(か)してもらえますか</code> is softer than asking for a pen,
because you have told the listener that anything will do and left them a way to help
without going to trouble. The same applies to gifts, invitations and suggestions:
<code>お茶(ちゃ)か何(なに)かどうですか</code> commits nobody to anything.

The other main use is a guess you cannot verify - 
<code>工事(こうじ)か何(なに)かでしょう</code>, <code>病気(びょうき)か何(なに)かだったらしい</code>.
Here the phrase protects you from asserting something you do not know, which is why it
appears so often with `でしょう`, `かな`, `らしい` and `みたい`.

Two register limits. In formal writing, `など` does this job instead:
<code>身分証明書(みぶんしょうめいしょ)などをご用意(ようい)ください</code>, never
<code>か何(なに)か</code>. And pointing it at a person is risky. The correct member of
the family for people is <code>か誰(だれ)か</code>, and even that should be used for
someone not present - saying it about the person in front of you sounds like they are
interchangeable.

## Contrast

### ～か何(なに)か vs でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `でも` is for **offers and suggestions**: "how about X or something?"
<code>か何(なに)か</code> is for **reference**: naming a thing loosely because the exact
identity is unknown or unimportant.

</div>

<pre>
お茶(ちゃ)<b>でも</b>飲(の)みに行(い)きませんか。
Shall we go for a cup of tea or something?
</pre>

<pre>
のどが渇(かわ)いたので、お茶(ちゃ)<b>か何(なに)か</b>買(か)ってきます。
I'm thirsty, so I'll go and buy some tea or something.
</pre>

The first is an invitation, and `でも` is what makes it casual enough to refuse. The
second is a plan of your own, where there is nothing to be polite about - and `でも` there
would sound as though you were inviting the tea. As a rough guide: if the sentence ends in
「～ませんか」 or 「～ましょう」, use `でも`.

### ～か何(なに)か vs とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とか` gives **two or more** examples of a set.
<code>か何(なに)か</code> gives **one**, and leaves the rest blank.

</div>

<pre>
果物(くだもの)<b>とか</b>お菓子(かし)<b>とか</b>を持(も)っていこう。
Let's take fruit or sweets or something like that.
</pre>

<pre>
果物(くだもの)<b>か何(なに)か</b>を持(も)っていこう。
Let's take fruit or something.
</pre>

`とか` sketches the shape of the category by listing; <code>か何(なに)か</code> points
at one member and shrugs. Both are casual, and both are avoided in formal writing.

### ～か何(なに)か vs 何(なに)か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>何(なに)か</code> alone means "something" with **no anchor**.
<code>か何(なに)か</code> needs a noun in front of it to be an alternative *to*.

</div>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に<b>何(なに)か</b>残(のこ)っていますか。
Is there anything left in the fridge?
</pre>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に卵(たまご)<b>か何(なに)か</b>残(のこ)っていますか。
Are there eggs or something left in the fridge?
</pre>

The first asks about the contents in general. The second has a specific thing in mind and
is willing to accept a substitute - which, incidentally, makes it the more useful sentence
when you are about to cook.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 何(なに)かコーヒー飲(の)む? → ✅ コーヒー<b>か何(なに)か</b>飲(の)む?
  The example noun comes **first** and <code>か何(なに)か</code> follows it. Putting
  <code>何(なに)か</code> in front produces "something coffee", which is not a phrase.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんか何(なに)かに聞(き)いてみます。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さん<b>か誰(だれ)か</b>に聞(き)いてみます。
  People take <code>か誰(だれ)か</code>. Using
  <code>何(なに)か</code> for a person is not merely wrong; it is insulting.

- ❌ 果物(くだもの)や何(なに)か買(か)ってきて。 → ✅ 果物(くだもの)<b>か</b>何(なに)か買(か)ってきて。
  The particle is `か` ("or"), not `や`. `や` lists things that are all actually present.

- ❌ 申込書(もうしこみしょ)か何(なに)かをご提出(ていしゅつ)ください。 → ✅ 申込書(もうしこみしょ)<b>など</b>をご提出(ていしゅつ)ください。
  In formal or written instructions, `など` is the vague-example word.
  <code>か何(なに)か</code> sounds too offhand when you are telling someone what to
  submit.

## Practice

**1.** かぜをひいたみたいだから、薬(くすり)____飲(の)んで早(はや)く寝(ね)ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

薬(くすり)<b>か何(なに)か</b>飲(の)んで早(はや)く寝(ね)ます。

You are naming one obvious option and leaving room for whatever is actually in the cupboard.
Note that `を` is comfortably dropped here, as it usually is in speech.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 時間(じかん)があったら、映画(えいが)（か何(なに)か・でも）見(み)に行(い)きませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

映画(えいが)<b>でも</b>見(み)に行(い)きませんか。

The sentence is an invitation ending in 「～ませんか」, and `でも` is the particle that makes
an invitation easy to decline. <code>か何(なに)か</code> would sound as though you were
listing options rather than asking.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Could you ask Suzuki or somebody where the meeting room is?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

鈴木(すずき)さん<b>か誰(だれ)か</b>に会議室(かいぎしつ)がどこか聞(き)いてもらえますか。

A person takes <code>か誰(だれ)か</code>, and the particle `に` attaches after the whole
phrase, not inside it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に何(なに)かはさみ置(お)いていませんでしたか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にはさみ<b>か何(なに)か</b>置(お)いていませんでしたか。

The word order was reversed. The concrete example goes first and

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)に何(なに)か持(も)っていったほうがいいですか。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)に資料(しりょう)<b>か何(なに)か</b>持(も)っていったほうがいいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an open question: is there anything at all I should bring? The asker has no idea
what would be appropriate.

(b) shows that the asker has already guessed - handouts, probably - and is checking while
leaving room to be corrected. It sounds better prepared, and it gives the listener an
easier question to answer, which is why it is the more natural thing to say to a colleague.

</details>

# なんか / なんて - things like; something

**Meaning** · throws a word out lightly - as an example, as something beneath discussion, or as something you can hardly believe

Both come from <code>何(なに)</code>. `なんか` is the worn-down form of
<code>何(なに)か</code> and of `など`; `なんて` is the worn-down form of `などと` and of
<code>という</code>. What survives in both is a **dismissive shrug** - the speaker is
holding the word at arm's length. Aim that shrug at yourself and it becomes modesty; aim it
at your listener and it becomes an insult. Aim it at a fact and it becomes disbelief.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - downplaying it | Noun + なんか | マンガ → マンガなんか |
| Noun - stronger, colder | Noun + なんて | お金(かね) → お金(かね)なんて |
| Noun + が／を | particle is **dropped** | ❌私(わたし)をなんか → ✅私(わたし)なんか |
| Noun + に／で／と | particle is **kept** | 駅(えき)で → 駅(えき)でなんか待(ま)ちたくない |
| Clause - marking it as astonishing | plain form + なんて | 来(く)る → 来(く)るなんて思(おも)わなかった |
| Nothing - adverbial "somehow" | なんか + predicate | なんか変(へん)だ |
| Exclamation | なんて + adjective／noun + んだろう | なんて寒(さむ)い日(ひ)なんだろう |
| Naming, in place of <code>という</code> | Noun + なんて + Noun | 田中(たなか)なんて人(ひと) |
| Formal written equivalents | など／などと | 値段(ねだん)など |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the "somehow" `なんか`
（<code>なんか変(へん)だ</code>）is a different animal from the particle `なんか`
（<code>マンガなんか</code>）. The first stands in front of the predicate with no noun
attached; the second always follows a noun. Both are casual.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>なんか</b>今日(きょう)は疲(つか)れたなあ。
I'm somehow worn out today.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>なんか</b>にはとてもできません。
Someone like me could never do it.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)<b>なんて</b>いらないよ。
I don't want your money.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が来(く)る<b>なんて</b>思(おも)わなかった。
I never imagined he'd come.
</pre>

<pre>
マンガ<b>なんか</b>読(よ)んでいないで、少(すこ)しは手伝(てつだ)ってよ。
Stop sitting there with your comics and give me a hand.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>なんて</b>きれいな夕焼(ゆうや)けなんだろう。
What a beautiful sunset.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の前(まえ)の日(ひ)に朝(あさ)までゲームをする<b>なんて</b>、落(お)ちても文句(もんく)は言(い)えないよ。
Playing games till dawn the night before an exam - you can hardly complain if you fail.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に直接(ちょくせつ)言(い)う<b>なんて</b>、よくそんな勇気(ゆうき)がありましたね。
Telling the manager to his face - I'm impressed you had the nerve.
</pre>

<pre>
「あの店(みせ)のラーメン、どうだった?」「うーん、<b>なんか</b>思(おも)っていたのと違(ちが)ったかな。」
"How was the ramen at that place?" "Mm - somehow not what I was expecting."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Both words are **casual and spoken**. In writing they become `など` and `などと`, and a
report that says <code>値段(ねだん)なんか</code> reads as though it slipped out. Neither
is rude in itself; what matters is where the shrug lands.

Pointed at **yourself**, `なんか` is the standard humble move:
<code>私(わたし)なんかでよければ</code>, <code>私(わたし)なんかまだまだです</code>.
This is polite, common, and expected when you are accepting praise you do not want to
accept out loud. Pointed at **your listener**, the same word is an attack - 
<code>お前(まえ)なんて</code> is the language of an argument, and learners should treat
`あなたなんて` as unusable. Pointed at a **thing**, it downgrades it, which is fine for
your own possessions and risky for someone else's.

`なんて` is the stronger of the two, and it has one job `なんか` cannot do: marking a whole
clause as astonishing. <code>一人(ひとり)で登(のぼ)ったなんてすごい</code> works;
<code>一人(ひとり)で登(のぼ)ったなんかすごい</code> does not. That use is not always
negative - surprise cuts both ways, and admiration is as common as disapproval. `なんて`
also appears as an exclamation before an adjective
（<code>なんて寒(さむ)い日(ひ)だろう</code>）, where it is the spoken cousin of
literary `なんと`.

Finally, the filler `なんか` - "like", "sort of" - is everywhere in casual speech,
especially among younger speakers. One or two soften a sentence; a string of them reads as
vague, and it is one of the first habits teachers try to break in presentations.

## Contrast

### なんか vs など

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical grammar, different register. `など` is the written and formal
form; `なんか` is what it sounds like in relaxed speech.

</div>

<pre>
回答(かいとう)では、値段(ねだん)や品質(ひんしつ)<b>など</b>に対(たい)する不満(ふまん)の声(こえ)が多(おお)く見(み)られた。
In the responses, complaints about price and quality among other things were prominent.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は値段(ねだん)<b>なんか</b>気(き)にしない人(ひと)が行(い)く店(みせ)だよ。
That shop is for people who don't worry about little things like price.
</pre>

Note that `など` is neutral in attitude - it simply means "and the like" - while `なんか`
usually keeps the downgrading tone even when it is only listing.

### なんか vs なんて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なんか` follows a **noun** and downplays it. `なんて` is stronger, and
it is the only one that can take a **whole clause** and mark it as unbelievable.

</div>

<pre>
テレビ<b>なんか</b>見(み)ていないで、早(はや)く寝(ね)なさい。
Never mind the television - get to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな時間(じかん)までテレビを見(み)ている<b>なんて</b>、明日(あした)起(お)きられないよ。
Watching television at this hour - you'll never get up tomorrow.
</pre>

After a noun the two overlap and `なんて` is simply harsher:
<code>テレビなんて見(み)ない</code> rejects television more firmly than
<code>テレビなんか見(み)ない</code>. After a clause, only `なんて` is possible.

### なんか・なんて vs とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とか` lists examples neutrally. `なんか` and `なんて` name one thing
**and comment on it**. If you have no attitude to express, `とか` is the safer word.

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は洗濯(せんたく)<b>とか</b>掃除(そうじ)<b>とか</b>で終(お)わってしまう。
Weekends get eaten up by laundry, cleaning and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
掃除(そうじ)<b>なんか</b>明日(あした)でいいよ。
The cleaning can wait until tomorrow.
</pre>

The first sentence is a flat report of how the weekend goes. The second dismisses cleaning
as not worth doing now - and if you said it to the person who has just started cleaning, it
would sting.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんなんて、いつも親切(しんせつ)ですね。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは、いつも親切(しんせつ)ですね。
  Aimed at another person, `なんて` and `なんか` belittle. A compliment built on them
  reads as sarcasm.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が合格(ごうかく)したなんか、信(しん)じられない。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が合格(ごうかく)した<b>なんて</b>、信(しん)じられない。
  A clause marked as astonishing takes `なんて`. `なんか` only attaches to nouns (or stands
  alone meaning "somehow").

- ❌ 私(わたし)をなんか誘(さそ)わないでください。 → ✅ 私(わたし)なんか誘(さそ)わないでください。
  `が` and `を` drop in front of `なんか`. `に`, `で` and `と` stay.

- ❌ アンケートの結果(けっか)では、時間(じかん)なんかが問題(もんだい)だという意見(いけん)が多(おお)かった。 → ✅ …時間(じかん)<b>など</b>が問題(もんだい)だという意見(いけん)が多(おお)かった。
  In a written report the neutral `など` is the right word. `なんか` drags in a dismissive
  tone the writer did not intend.

## Practice

**1.** こんな大雨(おおあめ)の中(なか)を歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)る____、風邪(かぜ)をひくよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

…歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)る<b>なんて</b>、風邪(かぜ)をひくよ。

A whole clause is being held up as unwise, and only `なんて` can mark a clause that way.
`なんか` here would need a noun in front of it.

</details>

**2.** Choose the right word for a written questionnaire summary: 通勤(つうきん)時間(じかん)や給料(きゅうりょう)（なんか・など）について質問(しつもん)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

通勤(つうきん)時間(じかん)や給料(きゅうりょう)<b>など</b>について質問(しつもん)しました。

`など` is the written form and carries no attitude. `なんか` would be casual and would
suggest the writer thought commuting time hardly mattered.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Someone like me couldn't possibly teach the class."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)<b>なんか</b>には、とても教(おし)えられません。

Turned on yourself, `なんか` is the standard humble gesture, and it pairs naturally with
`とても…ない`. The `に` stays because only `が` and `を` drop before `なんか`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)なんて、いつも丁寧(ていねい)に教(おし)えてくださいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は、いつも丁寧(ていねい)に教(おし)えてくださいます。

`なんて` on a superior belittles them, which collides head-on with the honorific
<code>くださいます</code>. Praise takes plain `は`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) お礼(れい)<b>なんか</b>いりませんよ。
(b) お礼(れい)<b>なんて</b>いりませんよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both wave away an offer of thanks, and in most conversations either would pass unnoticed.

(a) with `なんか` is the softer of the two: a light shrug, "there's no need for that sort
of thing." (b) with `なんて` pushes harder - it rejects the whole idea of being thanked,
and depending on your tone it can sound either warmly emphatic or faintly annoyed. When you
simply mean "please don't worry about it", `なんか` is the safer choice.

</details>

# ～っけ - what was… again?

**Meaning** · asks for something you once knew and have mislaid - "what was it again?", "did I…?"

`っけ` is a sentence-final particle for **retrieval**, not enquiry. Using it says two
things at once: I do not have this information right now, and I ought to. That admission
is why it sounds friendly rather than demanding - you are not asking the listener to
inform you, you are asking them to jog your memory. It also explains where it cannot go:
you cannot `っけ` something you were never told.

## Formation

`っけ` wants a `だ` or a past tense in front of it. That is the whole difficulty, and it is
where nearly every mistake comes from.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だっけ／だったっけ | 明日(あした) → 明日(あした)だっけ |
| な-adjective | ～だっけ／～だったっけ | 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ) → 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だったっけ |
| い-adjective | ～かったっけ／～いんだっけ | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)かったっけ |
| Verb, past | V(た) + っけ | 言(い)った → 言(い)ったっけ |
| Verb, non-past | V(る) + **んだっけ** | 来(く)る → 来(く)るんだっけ |
| Negative | ～ないんだっけ／～なかったっけ | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)なかったっけ |
| Polite | ～でしたっけ／～ましたっけ | 何時(なんじ) → 何時(なんじ)でしたっけ |
| With a question word | どこ・誰(だれ)・何時(なんじ) + だっけ | どこだっけ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a plain non-past verb does not take `っけ` directly. Insert `んだ`:
❌<code>何時(なんじ)に来(く)るっけ</code> → ✅<code>何時(なんじ)に来(く)るんだっけ</code>.
Past-tense verbs need nothing: <code>言(い)ったっけ</code> is fine on its own.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)から<b>だっけ</b>。
What time was the meeting again?
</pre>

<pre>
あれ、傘(かさ)、どこに置(お)いた<b>っけ</b>。
Hm - where did I put my umbrella?
</pre>

<pre>
これ、もう話(はな)した<b>っけ</b>?
Have I told you this already?
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)って休(やす)み<b>だったっけ</b>。
Tomorrow's a day off, isn't it - or was I imagining that?
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、受付(うけつけ)は2階(かい)<b>でしたっけ</b>。
Sorry - reception was on the second floor, wasn't it?
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんって、去年(きょねん)結婚(けっこん)した<b>んだっけ</b>?
Yamada got married last year, didn't he?
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`っけ` is **casual**. In plain form it belongs among friends, family and colleagues you are
comfortable with, and it carries a slight thinking-out-loud quality - it often arrives with
「あれ」, 「えーと」 or a tilt of the head. It is used by speakers of every age and gender and
is not marked either way.

The polite forms <code>でしたっけ</code> and <code>ましたっけ</code> are the useful ones
for work, and they do something clever: they ask for information while implying you were
already given it. 「会議(かいぎ)は3時(じ)からでしたっけ」 is softer than
「何時(なんじ)からですか」 because it credits the listener with having told you. That
said, the particle stays casual underneath the polite ending, so it is for colleagues, not
for a first meeting with a client - and asking a senior person their name with
<code>お名前(なまえ)は何(なん)でしたっけ</code> is memorably bad, because it
advertises that you were told and forgot.

There is a second, quieter use: with a past-tense verb and no question intended, `っけ`
becomes **reminiscence**. <code>よく二人(ふたり)で歩(ある)いたっけ</code> is not
asking anything; it is "we used to walk here, didn't we", said half to yourself. Falling
intonation marks it. This use is warm and slightly wistful and turns up constantly in
song lyrics and in older speakers' reminiscing.

## Contrast

### っけ vs か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か` asks for information. `っけ` asks for information **back**. Use
`っけ` only when you had it once.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)からです<b>か</b>。
What time does the meeting start?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)からでした<b>っけ</b>。
What time was the meeting again?
</pre>

The first is a plain question and works with a stranger, at an information desk, about
anything at all. The second only makes sense if the meeting time has already been
announced - otherwise the listener is left wondering when they told you.

### っけ vs かな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かな` wonders about something **unsettled**; `っけ` reaches for
something **already settled** but forgotten.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は晴(は)れる<b>かな</b>。
I wonder if it'll be fine tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みだった<b>っけ</b>。
Tomorrow was a day off - was it?
</pre>

Tomorrow's weather is not a fact anyone possesses yet, so `っけ` is impossible there.
Tomorrow's holiday is a fact on a calendar, and you have simply lost it. The two do combine
 - <code>どこに置(お)いたっけかな</code> - when you are searching your own memory aloud.

### っけ vs ね

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ね` checks something you **believe**; `っけ` admits you have **lost**
it. `ね` invites agreement, `っけ` asks to be told.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は10時(じ)からです<b>ね</b>。
It's from ten tomorrow, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は10時(じ)からでした<b>っけ</b>。
Was it ten tomorrow?
</pre>

The `ね` version is close to a confirmation of something you are fairly sure of, and the
listener need only say 「はい」. The `っけ` version concedes that you might be wrong, which
is more modest - and, if you have already asked once, more honest.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は休(やす)みっけ? → ✅ 明日(あした)は休(やす)み<b>だっけ</b>?
  A noun needs `だ` (or `だった`) before `っけ`. The bare noun form does not exist in
  standard speech.

- ❌ 何時(なんじ)に出(で)るっけ? → ✅ 何時(なんじ)に出(で)る<b>んだっけ</b>?
  A plain non-past verb takes `んだ` first. With the past tense you can attach `っけ`
  directly: <code>何時(なんじ)に出(で)たっけ</code>.

- ❌ この店(みせ)、高(たか)いっけ? → ✅ この店(みせ)、高(たか)<b>かったっけ</b>?／高(たか)<b>いんだっけ</b>?
  い-adjectives behave like verbs here: past tense, or `んだ` inserted.

- ❌ （初対面(しょたいめん)の人(ひと)に）お名前(なまえ)は何(なん)でしたっけ。 → ✅ お名前(なまえ)をうかがってもよろしいでしょうか。
  `っけ` claims you were told before. Used on someone you have just met it is merely
  confusing; used on someone who *did* tell you, it announces that you forgot.

## Practice

**1.** あれ、会議室(かいぎしつ)の鍵(かぎ)、誰(だれ)に返(かえ)した____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

誰(だれ)に返(かえ)した<b>っけ</b>。

The verb is already in the past tense, so `っけ` attaches directly. The sentence is
addressed as much to yourself as to anyone - the classic use.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来週(らいしゅう)の試験(しけん)、範囲(はんい)はどこまで（ですか・でしたっけ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

範囲(はんい)はどこまで<b>でしたっけ</b>。

If the exam range has already been announced and you have forgotten it, `でしたっけ` is the
natural and slightly apologetic choice. 「ですか」 would be right only if you had never been
told.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Did I already send you the file?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そのファイル、もう送(おく)りました<b>っけ</b>。

A past-tense polite verb takes `っけ` directly. This is exactly the situation the particle
exists for: you did something recently and cannot now recall whether you did.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山田(やまだ)さんの誕生日(たんじょうび)は来月(らいげつ)っけ?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山田(やまだ)さんの誕生日(たんじょうび)は来月(らいげつ)<b>だっけ</b>?

<code>来月(らいげつ)</code> is a noun, so it needs `だ` before `っけ`.
<code>来月(らいげつ)だったっけ</code> is equally natural and sounds a shade less certain.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 去年(きょねん)も同(おな)じ店(みせ)で飲(の)みました<b>ね</b>。
(b) 去年(きょねん)も同(おな)じ店(みせ)で飲(の)んだ<b>っけ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states something you remember clearly and invites the listener to share the memory. It
is polite, and the listener is expected to agree.

(b) is hazier and more casual. Depending on the intonation it is either a genuine question
 - was it this place last year? - or, with a falling tone, a piece of fond reminiscence
said half to yourself. Either way it admits that the memory has gone soft, which is
something `ね` never does.

</details>
