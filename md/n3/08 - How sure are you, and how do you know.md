# N3 - How sure are you, and how do you know


---

# ～によると / ～によれば

**Meaning** · according to ～ - names where a piece of information came from, when the information is not your own

`によると` attaches to a **source**: a person, a broadcast, a survey, a rumour, a
map. It says "this is not mine - here is who or what it came from." Because of
that, it is only half of the pattern. The other half is the ending, which has to
agree that the information is second-hand: そうだ, ということだ, とのことだ, らしい,
or, in newspaper style, という.

## Formation

`によると` follows a noun. It never attaches to a verb or an adjective directly - a
clause has to be turned into a noun-like source first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (source) | Noun + によると | 天気(てんき)予報(よほう) → 天気(てんき)予報(よほう)によると |
| Noun (source), written / formal | Noun + によれば | 調査(ちょうさ) → 調査(ちょうさ)によれば |
| Noun (source), announcements and broadcasting | Noun + によりますと | 気象庁(きしょうちょう) → 気象庁(きしょうちょう)によりますと |
| Clause repackaged as a source | Clause + ところによると | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)いたところによると |
| A person's account, casual | Person + の話(はなし)では | 姉(あね) → 姉(あね)の話(はなし)では |
| Ending the frame requires | … + そうだ / ということだ / とのことだ / らしい / という | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るそうです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the source has to be **outside the speaker**. ❌<code>私(わたし)によると</code>
is not Japanese; for your own view, say <code>私(わたし)の考(かんが)えでは</code> or
<code>私(わたし)に言(い)わせれば</code>. The one exception people do say is
<code>私(わたし)の記憶(きおく)によると</code> - "as far as I remember" - where the
source is your memory rather than you.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
天気(てんき)予報(よほう)<b>によると</b>、午後(ごご)から風(かぜ)が強(つよ)くなるそうです。
According to the forecast, the wind picks up from the afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)<b>によると</b>、あの店(みせ)は水曜日(すいようび)が休(やす)みだそうだ。
My sister says that shop is closed on Wednesdays.
</pre>

<pre>
この地図(ちず)<b>によると</b>、橋(はし)はもう少(すこ)し先(さき)にあるはずだ。
Going by this map, the bridge should be a little further on.
</pre>

<pre>
うわさ<b>によれば</b>、来年(らいねん)から制服(せいふく)が変(か)わるらしい。
Rumour has it the uniforms are changing from next year.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)の放送(ほうそう)<b>によると</b>、事故(じこ)の影響(えいきょう)で電車(でんしゃ)が20分(ぷん)ほど遅(おく)れているとのことです。
According to the station announcement, the trains are running about twenty minutes late because of an accident.
</pre>

<pre>
昨夜(ゆうべ)届(とど)いたメール<b>によりますと</b>、面接(めんせつ)の場所(ばしょ)が変(か)わったということです。
According to the email that came last night, the interview venue has changed.
</pre>

<pre>
市(し)が行(おこな)った調査(ちょうさ)<b>によれば</b>、この町(まち)に住(す)む外国人(がいこくじん)は10年(ねん)で倍(ばい)に増(ふ)えたという。
According to a survey carried out by the city, the number of foreign residents in this town has doubled in ten years.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)から聞(き)いた話(はなし)<b>によると</b>、向(む)かいの家(いえ)は来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)すそうですよ。
From what I heard from the neighbours, the family across the road are moving out next month.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The three shapes differ only in formality, and the ordering is easy to hold on to.
`によると` is the everyday one and is at home in conversation, email and writing
alike. `によれば` - literally the ば-conditional of the same verb `よる` - leans
written and formal; newspapers, reports and academic prose use it constantly, and
it sounds a touch stiff in chat. `によりますと` is the polite-verb version and
belongs to announcements: news readers, station staff and company briefings use it,
and it would sound theatrical over dinner.

Below all three sits the casual option, <code>話(はなし)では</code>:
<code>山田(やまだ)さんの話(はなし)では、今週(こんしゅう)は来(こ)られないって。</code>
That is what you actually say to a friend.

The ending is not decoration. Because `によると` announces that the information is
somebody else's, closing with <code>と思(おも)います</code> pulls the sentence in two
directions at once - the frame disclaims ownership and the ending claims it. Keep
the ending on the hearsay side. The one systematic exception is that a source you
are *reading off* rather than being told - a map, a timetable, a set of figures - 
can take an inference ending instead: <code>この表(ひょう)によると、去年(きょねん)より安(やす)いですね</code>.

One limit worth stating. A source is something that carries a **statement**: a
person, a document, a broadcast, a survey. Physical evidence you interpret yourself
is not a source. For the sound of rain on the window or the look of the sky, you
want <code>この音(おと)からすると</code> or plain `ようだ`, not `によると`.

## Contrast

### によると vs によれば vs によりますと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, three registers: `によると` neutral, `によれば`
written and formal, `によりますと` for announcements. If in doubt in speech, use
`によると`.

</div>

<pre>
となりのクラスの子(こ)<b>によると</b>、テストは来週(らいしゅう)に延(の)びたらしいよ。
A kid in the next class says the test has been pushed back to next week.
</pre>

<pre>
政府(せいふ)の発表(はっぴょう)<b>によれば</b>、制度(せいど)の見直(みなお)しは来年度(らいねんど)以降(いこう)になるという。
According to the government announcement, the review of the system will not happen until next fiscal year or later.
</pre>

<pre>
ただいま入(はい)った情報(じょうほう)<b>によりますと</b>、高速(こうそく)道路(どうろ)の通行止(つうこうど)めは解除(かいじょ)されたもようです。
According to information just in, the motorway closure has been lifted.
</pre>

### によると vs によって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both come from the verb `よる`, and they do unrelated jobs.
`によると` frames **where information came from**; `によって` marks a **means, an
agent, a cause, or a thing that varies**.

</div>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>によると</b>、この建物(たてもの)は地震(じしん)に強(つよ)いそうだ。
According to the experts, this building stands up well to earthquakes.
</pre>

<pre>
この建物(たてもの)は有名(ゆうめい)な建築家(けんちくか)<b>によって</b>設計(せっけい)された。
This building was designed by a famous architect.
</pre>

The second sentence is a passive with an agent; swapping in `によると` there would
mean "according to the architect", which is not what it says. The other `によって`
you will meet is the "it varies" one - <code>人(ひと)によって考(かんが)え方(かた)が違(ちが)う</code> - 
and again the two are not interchangeable in either direction.

### によると vs ～と言(い)っていた

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `によると` **frames** the information and lets the ending mark
it as hearsay. <code>～と言(い)っていた</code> **quotes** the person and is closer to
their actual words.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)<b>によると</b>、会議(かいぎ)は30分(ぷん)早(はや)まったとのことです。
According to the manager, the meeting has been moved half an hour earlier.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が「会議(かいぎ)を30分(ぷん)早(はや)めよう」<b>と言(い)っていました</b>。
The manager said, "let's move the meeting half an hour earlier."
</pre>

The quoting version keeps the manager's own wording, including his volitional form,
so it reports an utterance. The `によると` version reports a *fact he is the source
of*, which is what you want when you pass information on to a third person. In
casual speech the quoting version contracts to `って`:
<code>部長(ぶちょう)、会議(かいぎ)早(はや)めるって言(い)ってたよ。</code>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)によると、この映画(えいが)は面白(おもしろ)いです。 → ✅ 私(わたし)の考(かんが)えでは、この映画(えいが)は面白(おもしろ)いです。
  `によると` points *away* from the speaker. You cannot cite yourself as a source of
  news you are inventing on the spot.

- ❌ 天気(てんき)予報(よほう)によると、明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ると思(おも)います。 → ✅ …雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうです。
  The frame says "not my information"; <code>と思(おも)います</code> says "my judgment".
  Finish with そうです, ということです, とのことです or らしいです.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんによると聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんから聞(き)きました。
  `によると` frames a whole reported sentence. It is not a particle for marking who
  you heard something from - that is `から`.

- ❌ この音(おと)によると、外(そと)は雨(あめ)のようだ。 → ✅ この音(おと)からすると、外(そと)は雨(あめ)のようだ。
  A sound is evidence you are interpreting, not a source that told you something.

**1.** ニュース____、台風(たいふう)は夜中(よなか)に関東(かんとう)に近(ちか)づくそうです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ニュース<b>によると</b>、台風(たいふう)は夜中(よなか)に関東(かんとう)に近(ちか)づくそうです。

The news is the source of information, and the sentence already ends in そうです, so
the frame that agrees with it is `によると` (or the more formal `によれば`).

</details>

**2.** Choose: この論文(ろんぶん)（によると・によって）、睡眠(すいみん)時間(じかん)は記憶(きおく)に大(おお)きく影響(えいきょう)するという。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この論文(ろんぶん)<b>によると</b>、睡眠(すいみん)時間(じかん)は記憶(きおく)に大(おお)きく影響(えいきょう)するという。

The paper is where the claim comes from, so it is a source: `によると`. `によって`
would make the paper the *agent* of something, which fits a passive like
<code>論文(ろんぶん)によって明(あき)らかにされた</code>, not a reported claim.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "According to my grandfather, there used to be a river here."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

祖父(そふ)<b>によると</b>、昔(むかし)ここに川(かわ)があったそうです。

The person is the source, so `によると` (casually,
<code>祖父(そふ)の話(はなし)では</code>), and because you are passing on what he told

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山田(やまだ)さんによると、新(あたら)しい係長(かかりちょう)はとても厳(きび)しいだと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山田(やまだ)さん<b>によると</b>、新(あたら)しい係長(かかりちょう)はとても厳(きび)しいそうです。

Two problems. <code>厳(きび)しいだ</code> is not a possible form - い-adjectives never
take `だ`. And the ending has to keep the information on Yamada's side of the fence,
so そうです replaces <code>と思(おも)います</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 中村(なかむら)さん<b>によると</b>、工事(こうじ)は来週(らいしゅう)終(お)わるそうです。
(b) 中村(なかむら)さんは「工事(こうじ)は来週(らいしゅう)終(お)わります」<b>と言(い)っていました</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents Nakamura as the source of a piece of information you are now handing
on as fact-you-heard. The listener's attention goes to the content - the work
finishes next week - and Nakamura is the guarantee behind it.

(b) reports the act of speaking, close to his actual words. It is what you say when
the point is that *he said it* - useful if you want to leave room for "well, that
is what he told me" and step back from the claim yourself.

</details>

# ～ということだ / ～とのことだ

**Meaning** · I hear that ～; word is that ～ - relays information you received from somewhere else, with the wording kept at arm's length

Both endings hand a piece of information on. `ということだ` is the general relay:
whatever you were told, read or heard, repackaged as a report. `とのことだ` is the
message-carrier's version - it fits a phone call, an email or a note you are
passing to someone else, and it is the standard shape in business Japanese.

## Formation

Both attach to a **plain-form clause**. Nothing changes shape except the noun and
な-adjective slots.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ということだ | 戻(もど)る → 戻(もど)るということだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ということだ | 届(とど)いた → 届(とど)いたということです |
| い-adjective | plain form + ということだ | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいということだ |
| な-adjective | ～だ + ということだ | 不便(ふべん)だ → 不便(ふべん)だということだ |
| Noun | Noun + だ + ということだ | 中止(ちゅうし) → 中止(ちゅうし)だということです |
| Noun, with とのことだ | Noun + とのことだ | 中止(ちゅうし) → 中止(ちゅうし)とのことです |
| Polite past | … + ということでした / とのことでした | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れるとのことでした |
| Casual speech | … + ってことだ | 無理(むり)だ → 無理(むり)だってことだ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - before `とのことだ`, the `だ` after a noun is normally left out:
<code>休(やす)みとのことです</code> rather than
<code>休(やす)みだとのことです</code>. Before `ということだ` the `だ` is usually
there. This is a tendency in how the two are written, not a hard rule of grammar.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)は来週(らいしゅう)戻(もど)る<b>ということです</b>。
I'm told the president is back next week.
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)は5時(じ)まで<b>とのことです</b>。
Reception is open until five, apparently.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)のお話(はなし)では、試験(しけん)の範囲(はんい)は変(か)わらない<b>ということだ</b>。
According to the teacher, the scope of the exam isn't changing.
</pre>

<pre>
林(はやし)さんから連絡(れんらく)があって、渋滞(じゅうたい)で少(すこ)し遅(おく)れる<b>とのことでした</b>。
Hayashi got in touch - he says he'll be a little late because of the traffic.
</pre>

<pre>
歓迎会(かんげいかい)は来月(らいげつ)に延期(えんき)になった<b>ということなので</b>、みんなに伝(つた)えておきます。
The welcome party has been put off until next month, I gather, so I'll let everyone know.
</pre>

<pre>
お客様(きゃくさま)は明日(あした)の午前中(ごぜんちゅう)にもう一度(いちど)電話(でんわ)をくださる<b>とのことです</b>。
The customer says she'll ring again tomorrow morning.
</pre>

<pre>
担当者(たんとうしゃ)に確認(かくにん)したところ、書類(しょるい)は金曜日(きんようび)までに出(だ)せば間(ま)に合(あ)う<b>とのことでした</b>。
I checked with the person in charge, and she said we'll be in time if we submit the papers by Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)の音(おと)がうるさいという苦情(くじょう)が何件(なんけん)か来(き)ている<b>ということです</b>ので、作業(さぎょう)の時間(じかん)を見直(みなお)しましょう。
Word is there have been several complaints about the noise from the building work, so let's reconsider the working hours.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Both are more formal than the everyday hearsay `そうだ`. `ということだ` is the
ending a news bulletin closes on and a report uses to attribute a fact; in speech
it sounds careful and slightly detached, which is exactly why it is useful when you
do not want to be held responsible for the content. `とのことだ` is narrower and
more human: somebody told you, and you are passing it along. In an office you will
hear <code>部長(ぶちょう)は本日(ほんじつ)直帰(ちょっき)とのことです</code> many
times a week.

In plain form, `ということだ` and `とのことだ` both read as written or announcement
Japanese. Casual speech contracts the first to `ってことだ` - 「あの店(みせ)、
今日(きょう)休(やす)みだってことだよ」 - and often drops the frame altogether in
favour of a bare `って`: 「休(やす)みだって」.

`ということだ` has a **second job** that shares the same form: drawing a conclusion,
"which means", "so what it amounts to is". It usually arrives with つまり or
要(よう)するに and restates something already said rather than reporting news.

<pre>
返事(へんじ)がないということは、興味(きょうみ)がない<b>ということだ</b>。
No reply means they aren't interested.
</pre>

Context tells the two apart without much effort: a named source, or a fresh fact,
points to hearsay; a premise sitting right in front of it points to the conclusion
reading.

## Contrast

### ということだ vs そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same information, different formality - and one grammatical
difference: hearsay `そうだ` does not conjugate, while `ということだ` does.

</div>

Hearsay `そうだ` has exactly one shape. There is no ❌`そうでした`, no ❌`そうだった`
and no ❌`そうではない` in the hearsay sense; those forms all belong to the
*looks-like* `そうだ`. So when you need a past or a negative on the reporting frame
itself, `ということだ` is the pattern that has one.

<pre>
昨日(きのう)問(と)い合(あ)わせたときは、在庫(ざいこ)がまだある<b>ということでした</b>。
When I enquired yesterday, they said there was still stock.
</pre>

<pre>
在庫(ざいこ)がまだある<b>そうです</b>。
Apparently there's still stock.
</pre>

The second is what you say to a colleague in passing. The first pins the report to
a moment in time, and only `ということだ` can do it.

### とのことだ vs ということだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とのことだ` relays a **message from an identifiable person**,
usually one you received directly. `ということだ` covers that too, but also general
report, rumour and conclusion.

</div>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)の旅館(りょかん)は来年(らいねん)閉(し)めるらしい、<b>ということだ</b>。
Word going round is that the inn up the mountain is closing next year.
</pre>

<pre>
予約(よやく)の変更(へんこう)はメールでお願(ねが)いしたい<b>とのことです</b>。
They'd like changes to bookings to be made by email, they said.
</pre>

Swapping them is rarely *ungrammatical*, but `とのことだ` on an anonymous rumour
sounds oddly official, and `ということだ` on a message you took two minutes ago
sounds needlessly distant.

### ということだ ("I hear") vs ということだ ("which means")

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if it introduces **new** information from a source, it is
hearsay. If it restates something **already** on the table, it is a conclusion.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい支店(してん)は駅前(えきまえ)にできる<b>ということです</b>。
I hear the new branch is going up in front of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)の土地(とち)を買(か)ったのなら、そこに支店(してん)を作(つく)る<b>ということですね</b>。
If they've bought the land by the station, that means they're going to build a branch there.
</pre>

The second one has its premise attached with `なら`, and it adds no facts - it just
spells out what follows. That is the conclusion use, and it is the one that turns up
in <code>ということは</code> at the head of a sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)だそうでした。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)だということでした。
  Hearsay `そうだ` has no past form. When the reporting itself happened in the past,
  use `ということでした` or `とのことでした`.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が狭(せま)いだということです。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が狭(せま)いということです。
  い-adjectives never take `だ`. Only nouns and な-adjectives do, and only before
  `ということだ`.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)休(やす)むそうということです。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)休(やす)むということです。
  One hearsay marker per sentence. Stacking `そうだ` and `ということだ` reports the
  same second-hand status twice.

- ❌ 私(わたし)はこの案(あん)に賛成(さんせい)だということです。 → ✅ 私(わたし)はこの案(あん)に賛成(さんせい)です。
  The frame exists to put distance between you and the content. Your own position
  needs no distance.

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になった<b>そうです</b>。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になった<b>とのことです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is everyday hearsay: you heard it somewhere and you are mentioning it. It fits
conversation with a colleague on the stairs.

(b) reads as a message being passed on in an official capacity - somebody informed
you, and you are relaying the notification. It is what you would write in an email
to the team, and it quietly implies that the information is reliable and came
through a proper channel.

</details>

# ～に違いない

**Meaning** · it must be ～; I'm sure ～ - read as *ちがいない*, the speaker's near-certainty about something they have worked out rather than been told

<code>に違(ちが)いない</code> is the top of the guessing range. You have not been told, and you
cannot check; but the evidence in front of you leaves you in no real doubt. The
conviction is the speaker's own, which is why the pattern belongs to inference and
not to fact - you never use it about something you simply know.

## Formation

Attaches to a **plain form**, with the usual noun and な-adjective trimming: the `だ`
disappears.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + に違(ちが)いない | 落(お)とした → 落(お)としたに違(ちが)いない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + に違(ちが)いない | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないに違(ちが)いない |
| い-adjective | plain form + に違(ちが)いない | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いに違(ちが)いない |
| な-adjective | drop だ + に違(ちが)いない | 不安(ふあん)だ → 不安(ふあん)に違(ちが)いない |
| Noun | Noun + に違(ちが)いない | 本物(ほんもの) → 本物(ほんもの)に違(ちが)いない |
| Polite | … + に違(ちが)いありません | 疲(つか)れている → 疲(つか)れているに違(ちが)いありません |
| Very formal / legal writing | … + に相違(そうい)ない | 事実(じじつ) → 事実(じじつ)に相違(そうい)ない |

## Examples

<pre>
この字(じ)を書(か)いたのは姉(あね)<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
My sister must be the one who wrote this.
</pre>

<pre>
電気(でんき)が消(き)えているから、もう寝(ね)た<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
The lights are off, so they must have gone to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに人(ひと)が並(なら)んでいるのだから、この店(みせ)はおいしい<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
With a queue like that, the food here must be good.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)がまだ来(こ)ない。きっと忙(いそが)しい<b>に違(ちが)いありません</b>。
Still no reply. They must be snowed under.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)が割(わ)れているところを見(み)ると、誰(だれ)かが入(はい)った<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
Judging by the broken window, someone must have got in.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)に置(お)いたはずの封筒(ふうとう)がない。誰(だれ)かが片付(かたづ)けてしまった<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
The envelope I'm sure I left on the desk is gone. Somebody must have tidied it away.
</pre>

<pre>
あれだけ練習(れんしゅう)を重(かさ)ねてきたのだから、本人(ほんにん)もこの結果(けっか)には納得(なっとく)していない<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
After all the practice he put in, he can't possibly be satisfied with this result himself.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)が何(なに)も言(い)わずに帰(かえ)ったのは、よほど嫌(いや)なことがあった<b>に違(ちが)いありません</b>。
For her to leave without a word, something must have upset her badly.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everything from `かもしれない` up to a flat assertion is a ladder, and it pays to
have the whole thing in view rather than learning each rung separately.

| Pattern | English | What it rests on |
|---|---|---|
| かもしれない | might, could | one possibility among several |
| だろう / でしょう | probably | a general guess, no working shown |
| はずだ | should be, is supposed to be | a fact or rule you can point to |
| に違(ちが)いない | must be, I'm certain | evidence plus the speaker's conviction |
| に決(き)まっている | obviously, of course | conviction alone, stated bluntly |

<code>に違(ちが)いない</code> is a strong, slightly dramatic ending. It is completely at home in
writing - novels lean on it for a character's inner deduction, and essays use it to
press a point - and in speech it sounds emphatic and a little declamatory. It is
not rude; it just takes up space. In relaxed conversation people more often reach
for きっと with a plain ending, or for <code>に決(き)まってる</code>, or simply `はずだ`.

<code>に違(ちが)いありません</code> is the polite form and is unremarkable in a careful spoken report.
<code>に相違(そうい)ない</code> is the same pattern in legal and formal-document register and is worth
recognising rather than using.

Two things it will not do. It does not stack with other hedges - ❌<code>に違(ちが)いないでしょう</code>
and ❌<code>に違(ちが)いないかもしれない</code> cancel the certainty they just claimed - and it does not
apply to your own plans and feelings, which are not things you deduce. Say
明日(あした)行(い)きます, not ❌<code>明日(あした)行(い)くに違(ちが)いない</code>.

## Contrast

### に違(ちが)いない vs はずだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずだ` reasons from something you can point to and survives
being wrong; <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> states your conviction and does not.

</div>

`はずだ` has an escape hatch built in. It says "given what I know, this follows",
which is why <code>はずだったのに</code> - "it was supposed to be, and yet" - is such a
common sentence. <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> has no such form; you cannot walk back a certainty you
just declared.

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は3時(じ)に始(はじ)まる<b>はず</b>です。
The meeting should start at three.
</pre>

<pre>
あの様子(ようす)では、会議(かいぎ)はまだ終(お)わっていない<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
Going by the look of things, the meeting can't have finished yet.
</pre>

The first reads off a schedule; you did not deduce anything, so <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> there
would sound oddly overwrought. The second has no schedule to read - only evidence
and the speaker's reading of it, which is exactly <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> territory.

### に違(ちが)いない vs かもしれない・だろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same guess, three strengths: `かもしれない` leaves the other
possibilities open, `だろう` picks one without much working, <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> closes the
question.

</div>

<pre>
あの音(おと)は雷(かみなり)<b>かもしれない</b>。
That noise might be thunder.
</pre>

<pre>
あの音(おと)は雷(かみなり)<b>だろう</b>。
That noise is probably thunder.
</pre>

<pre>
あの音(おと)は雷(かみなり)<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
That noise must be thunder.
</pre>

Notice what changes with the evidence. The first is fine when you barely heard it;
the third needs the sky to have flashed a second earlier. Using the top rung on
thin evidence is the commonest way learners sound strange with this pattern - it is
not wrong, it is simply overclaiming.

### に違(ちが)いない vs に決(き)まっている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> is a considered conclusion; <code>に決(き)まっている</code> is a
blunt "obviously", often impatient, and much more casual.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)から走(はし)っても間(ま)に合(あ)わない<b>に決(き)まっている</b>よ。
Of course we won't make it, even if we run.
</pre>

<pre>
この時間(じかん)に出(で)たのだから、渋滞(じゅうたい)に巻(ま)き込(こ)まれた<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
Leaving at that hour, they must have got caught in traffic.
</pre>

<code>に決(き)まっている</code> treats the conclusion as too obvious to argue with, which can come
across as dismissive of the person you are talking to. <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> shows its
working and stays civil.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この辺(へん)は静(しず)か<b>だに違(ちが)いない</b>。 → ✅ この辺(へん)は静(しず)か<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
  The `だ` of a な-adjective or a noun drops before <code>に違(ちが)いない</code>, exactly as it does
  before `らしい`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来週(らいしゅう)大阪(おおさか)に行(い)く<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来週(らいしゅう)大阪(おおさか)に行(い)きます。
  Your own plans are not something you deduce. The pattern is for conclusions about
  the world outside your head.

- ❌ 受付(うけつけ)は9時(じ)からだ<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>ので、少(すこ)し待(ま)ちましょう。 → ✅ 受付(うけつけ)は9時(じ)から<b>のはず</b>なので、少(すこ)し待(ま)ちましょう。
  Opening times come from a notice, not from deduction. Facts you can look up take
  `はずだ`.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は知(し)っていた<b>に違(ちが)いないでしょう</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は知(し)っていた<b>に違(ちが)いありません</b>。
  `でしょう` weakens what <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> has just declared. Use the polite form instead
  of piling a hedge on top.

## Practice

**1.** 靴(くつ)がぬれているから、外(そと)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

外(そと)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。

Wet shoes are evidence, and the speaker draws a confident conclusion from them
rather than reading a fact off a timetable - the classic setting for <code>に違(ちが)いない</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新幹線(しんかんせん)は定刻(ていこく)に着(つ)く（はずです・に違(ちが)いありません）から、駅(えき)で待(ま)っていてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The arrival time comes from the timetable, so this is expectation built on a known
fact - `はずだ`. <code>に違(ちが)いありません</code> would present a published schedule as a personal

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Silence from the office is the evidence, and "must have gone home" is the

<code>帰(かえ)ったに違(ちが)いない</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの人(ひと)は有名(ゆうめい)だに違(ちが)いないかもしれません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Two repairs. The `だ` drops after a な-adjective, and `かもしれません` cannot sit on

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山田(やまだ)さんはもう着(つ)いている<b>はずです</b>。
(b) 山田(やまだ)さんはもう着(つ)いている<b>に違(ちが)いありません</b>。

hour, so by now he should be there. If it turns out he is not, you say
<code>着(つ)いているはずなのに…</code> and nobody is embarrassed.

(b) is the speaker's conviction, usually prompted by fresh evidence - his coat is on
the chair, his laptop is open. It is stronger, more personal, and harder to retreat
from; you have said the question is settled.

</details>

# ～そうもない

**Meaning** · there's no sign of it happening; very unlikely to ～ - the negative of the "looks like it will" そうだ, and considerably stronger than a plain "probably not"

`～そうもない` says that you have looked at the situation and can see **no prospect
at all** of the thing happening. It is not a calculation and not a rumour: it is
what the evidence in front of you looks like. Because the `も` is a "not even"
particle, the pattern lands harder than <code>～ないだろう</code> - the rain is not
merely unlikely to stop, it shows no sign whatsoever of stopping.

## Formation

This is a **verb** pattern. It attaches to the ます-stem, exactly like the
"looks like" そうだ it negates.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + そうもない | 止(や)みます → 止(や)みそうもない |
| Verb, common variant | ます-stem + そうにない | 終(お)わります → 終(お)わりそうにない |
| Verb, emphatic variant | ます-stem + そうにもない | 勝(か)てます → 勝(か)てそうにもない |
| Potential verb (very common) | potential ます-stem + そうもない | 着(つ)けます → 着(つ)けそうもない |
| Polite | … + そうもありません / そうにありません | 間(ま)に合(あ)います → 間(ま)に合(あ)いそうもありません |
| い-adjective (use なさそう instead) | ～く + なさそうだ | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くなさそうだ |
| な-adjective / noun (use なさそう instead) | ～では + なさそうだ | 簡単(かんたん) → 簡単(かんたん)ではなさそうだ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the plain "looks like it will" そうだ has an irregular pair worth keeping
straight: いい → よさそう, ない → なさそう. Those `さ` insertions are what produce the
adjective negatives in the last two rows.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)は当分(とうぶん)止(や)み<b>そうもない</b>。
There's no sign of the rain stopping any time soon.
</pre>

<pre>
この仕事(しごと)は今日中(きょうじゅう)に終(お)わり<b>そうにない</b>。
This job isn't going to get finished today by the look of it.
</pre>

<pre>
席(せき)が空(あ)き<b>そうもない</b>から、別(べつ)の店(みせ)にしよう。
No sign of a table coming free - let's try somewhere else.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに考(かんが)えても、いい案(あん)が出(で)てき<b>そうにもない</b>。
However hard I think about it, no good idea looks likely to come out.
</pre>

<pre>
渋滞(じゅうたい)がひどくて、約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)には着(つ)け<b>そうもありません</b>。
The traffic is dreadful - I don't think I can possibly get there for the time we agreed.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)は下(さ)がりましたが、咳(せき)のほうはしばらく治(なお)り<b>そうにありません</b>。
The fever has come down, but the cough shows no sign of clearing for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
隣(となり)の部屋(へや)の話(はなし)し合(あ)いは、まだ当分(とうぶん)終(お)わり<b>そうにない</b>ですね。
The discussion in the next room doesn't look like ending for some time yet, does it.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)がまだ届(とど)いていないので、来週(らいしゅう)の発売(はつばい)には間(ま)に合(あ)い<b>そうもありません</b>。
The parts still haven't arrived, so there's no realistic chance of making next week's launch.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. You will hear it from a site foreman, a schoolchild and a news
reporter alike, and the polite forms `そうもありません` / `そうにありません` are
ordinary business Japanese for delivering bad news gently - the fault lies with the
situation, not with you.

The three shapes are near-identical in meaning. `そうにない` is the plainest,
`そうもない` adds the "not even" flavour of `も`, and `そうにもない` piles both on and
is the most emphatic. Treat this as a tendency rather than a rule: in most sentences
the three are freely interchangeable, and speakers vary.

Two things follow from `そうもない` being the negative of *looks-like* そうだ. First,
it is about **observable prospects**, so it wants a subject whose behaviour you can
watch: rain, a queue, a deadline, a person's progress. It sits badly on abstract
impossibility, which belongs to `わけがない`. Second, it does not mean "I hear that
… not". The hearsay そうだ negates inside the clause instead:
<code>来(こ)ないそうだ</code> - "I hear they aren't coming."

The pattern loves potential verbs, and that combination is the everyday way to
decline gracefully: <code>今週(こんしゅう)は行(い)けそうもありません</code>
softens "I can't come" into "it doesn't look as though I'll manage it."

## Contrast

### ～そうもない vs ～なさそうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` negates the *appearance of it happening*;
`なさそうだ` negates the *verb* and then reports how that looks. The first says "no
sign of it"; the second says "looks like not".

</div>

<pre>
バスは来(き)<b>そうもない</b>。
There's no sign of a bus coming at all.
</pre>

<pre>
この時間(じかん)だと、バスは来(こ)<b>なさそうだ</b>ね。
At this hour it looks like no bus is coming.
</pre>

Both are natural, and the difference is one of weight. The first is what you say
after twenty minutes at the stop, with impatience; the second is a calmer reading of
the timetable and the empty road. With **adjectives** you have no choice - only the
`なさそう` route exists, which is why
❌<code>高(たか)そうもない</code> should be
<code>高(たか)くなさそうだ</code>.

### ～そうもない vs ～ないだろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` is grounded in what you can see right now.
`ないだろう` is a guess that needs no evidence at all.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)は降(ふ)ら<b>ないだろう</b>。
It probably won't snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
空(そら)が明(あか)るくなってきた。もう降(ふ)り<b>そうもない</b>。
The sky's brightening. It doesn't look like it's going to come down any more.
</pre>

Because `そうもない` reports signs, it usually talks about the near future or a
situation already under way. A guess about next year takes `ないだろう`; a guess
about the next twenty minutes, with the sky in front of you, takes `そうもない`.

### ～そうもない vs わけがない・はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` says there is **no sign** of it. `わけがない` and
`はずがない` say it is **impossible**. Unlikely is not the same as ruled out.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)から始(はじ)めても、今日中(きょうじゅう)には終(お)わり<b>そうもない</b>。
Even if we start now, it doesn't look like finishing today.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)でこの量(りょう)を運(はこ)べる<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way one person can shift this much.
</pre>

The first leaves the door open - a miracle, extra hands, a shorter route. The second
slams it. Learners reach for `わけがない` when they only mean the first, and it comes
out sounding much more forceful than they intended.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>そうもない</b>。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>そうもない</b>。
  It attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form plus
  そうだ is the *hearsay* pattern, which is a different thing entirely.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は簡単(かんたん)<b>そうもない</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は簡単(かんたん)<b>ではなさそうだ</b>。
  Adjectives do not take `そうもない`. Negate the adjective first, then add そう.

- ❌ 今日中(きょうじゅう)に終(お)わら<b>なそうもない</b>。 → ✅ 今日中(きょうじゅう)に終(お)わり<b>そうもない</b>。
  The negative is already inside `そうもない`. Negating the verb as well leaves you
  saying it looks like it will finish.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは来(く)る<b>そうもない</b>（meaning "I hear he isn't coming"）。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは来(こ)ない<b>そうだ</b>。
  For reported information, put the negative in the clause and keep plain `そうだ`.

## Practice

The wind is visible evidence, and the speaker reads it as leaving no prospect of the
boat sailing. <code>出(で)る</code> is a る-verb, so its ます-stem is <code>出(で)</code>.

</details>

presents the refusal as a reading of the circumstances rather than as a flat "no".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)は今日(きょう)来(く)るそうもないと聞(き)きました。

Two things went wrong. `そうもない` needs the ます-stem
（<code>来(き)そうもない</code>）, and more importantly the sentence is reporting
what you were told, which is the hearsay そうだ with the negative inside the clause.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)が試合(しあい)に勝(か)て<b>そうもない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)が試合(しあい)に勝(か)てる<b>わけがない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a reading of what you can see - the score, his condition, the opponent. It
says the prospects look bad, and it leaves room for a surprise.

(b) declares the thing impossible on principle, and it carries a note of contempt or
exasperation that (a) does not have. Said in front of him, (a) is a discouraged
observation and (b) is an insult.

</details>

# ～ような気がする

**Meaning** · I have a feeling that ～ - a hunch, offered without the evidence that would make it a claim

Read as *ような き がする*. The pattern reports an impression that has formed in
you without your being able to show your working. It is the softest thing in the
whole certainty range: weaker than <code>と思(おも)う</code>, weaker than `ようだ`,
and much weaker than `はずだ`. Japanese uses it constantly, both to hedge a real
opinion and to describe a genuine sensation you cannot account for.

## Formation

`ような` is the noun-modifying form of `ようだ`, and <code>気(き)</code> is the
noun it modifies. So the clause attaches exactly the way it does before any noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ような気(き)がする | 忘(わす)れた → 忘(わす)れたような気(き)がする |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ような気(き)がする | 合(あ)わない → 合(あ)わないような気(き)がする |
| い-adjective | plain form + ような気(き)がする | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いような気(き)がする |
| な-adjective | ～な + ような気(き)がする | 無理(むり)な → 無理(むり)なような気(き)がする |
| Noun | ～の + ような気(き)がする | 夢(ゆめ) → 夢(ゆめ)のような気(き)がする |
| Shorter version | plain form + 気(き)がする | 忘(わす)れた → 忘(わす)れた気(き)がする |
| Polite | … + ような気(き)がします | 遅(おそ)い → 遅(おそ)いような気(き)がします |
| Past (recollection) | … + ような気(き)がした | 呼(よ)ばれた → 呼(よ)ばれたような気(き)がした |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with な-adjectives the full form is a mouthful, and speakers usually drop
the `よう`: <code>無理(むり)な気(き)がする</code> is far more common than
<code>無理(むり)なような気(き)がする</code>. Both are grammatical.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どこかで会(あ)った<b>ような気(き)がする</b>。
I feel like I've met them somewhere before.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)、少(すこ)し寒(さむ)い<b>ような気(き)がします</b>。
This room feels a bit cold to me.
</pre>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)をかけ忘(わす)れた<b>ような気(き)がして</b>、家(いえ)まで戻(もど)った。
I had a feeling I'd forgotten to lock up, so I went all the way back home.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は、どこか本当(ほんとう)ではない<b>ような気(き)がする</b>。
Something about his explanation feels untrue to me.
</pre>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)、一日(いちにち)が過(す)ぎるのが速(はや)くなった<b>ような気(き)がしませんか</b>。
Don't you feel like the days go by faster lately?
</pre>

<pre>
まだ連絡(れんらく)はないけれど、今回(こんかい)はうまくいく<b>ような気(き)がする</b>。
I haven't heard anything yet, but I've got a feeling it'll work out this time.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて来(き)た町(まち)のはずなのに、前(まえ)にもこの坂(さか)を歩(ある)いた<b>ような気(き)がした</b>。
It was supposed to be my first time in the town, and yet I felt as though I'd walked up that hill before.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)は分(わ)かりやすかったのですが、一番(いちばん)大事(だいじ)なところを見落(みお)としている<b>ような気(き)がします</b>。
The explanation was clear enough, but I have the feeling we're overlooking the most important part.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and one of the highest-frequency hedges in ordinary conversation.
<code>気(き)がする</code> in plain form is casual, <code>気(き)がします</code> fits any polite situation, and
nothing about it is stiff or literary. In writing, the close relative
<code>～ように思(おも)う</code> does the same job with a more composed tone.

Two flavours share the one form, and context separates them. The first is a real
**sensation**: something smells faintly of smoke, a room feels colder, you half
heard your name. The second is **politeness** - you have an opinion, and you present
it as a mere feeling so that disagreeing with you costs nothing. In a meeting,
<code>この案(あん)は少(すこ)し急(いそ)ぎすぎているような気(き)がします</code> is a
carefully wrapped objection, and everyone hears it as one.

The variant <code>ような気(き)もする</code> is worth having. Replacing `が` with `も`
turns it into "part of me feels", and it is how you sit on the fence out loud:
<code>安(やす)いような気(き)もするし、高(たか)いような気(き)もする</code>.

Do not confuse the phrase with its neighbours. <code>気(き)になる</code> means
something is on your mind or bothering you; <code>気(き)に入(い)る</code> means you
like it. Only <code>気(き)がする</code> means "I have the impression", and the
particle is `が`, never `を`.

## Contrast

### ような気(き)がする vs と思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と思(おも)う</code> is an opinion you could defend if
pressed. <code>ような気(き)がする</code> is an impression you could not.

</div>

<pre>
この料理(りょうり)には塩(しお)が足(た)りない<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I think this dish needs more salt.
</pre>

<pre>
この料理(りょうり)には塩(しお)が足(た)りない<b>ような気(き)がします</b>。
This dish feels like it could do with more salt.
</pre>

The first invites a reply - agreement, or "no, it's fine as it is". The second
barely puts the claim on the table, and that is often the point. Note the practical
consequence: if a superior asks for your judgment, answering only with
<code>ような気(き)がします</code> can read as evasive rather than modest.

### ような気(き)がする vs ようだ・みたいだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` is a conclusion **drawn from evidence**;
<code>ような気(き)がする</code> is a feeling that arrives **without** it.

</div>

<pre>
玄関(げんかん)に靴(くつ)がある。誰(だれ)か来(き)ている<b>ようだ</b>。
There are shoes in the entrance. It seems someone's here.
</pre>

<pre>
家(いえ)に誰(だれ)かいる<b>ような気(き)がする</b>。
I have a feeling there's someone in the house.
</pre>

The first points at the shoes; anybody could check the reasoning. The second points
at nothing at all, which is exactly why it is the sentence that belongs in a ghost
story. Because they sit next to each other, the two combine happily:
<code>いるような気(き)がしただけかもしれない</code>.

### ような気(き)がする vs 気(き)がする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the bare <code>気(き)がする</code> is a shade more direct;
adding `よう` puts one more layer of "sort of" between you and the claim.

</div>

<pre>
前(まえ)にも同(おな)じ話(はなし)を聞(き)いた<b>気(き)がする</b>。
I think I've heard this story before.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)にも同(おな)じ話(はなし)を聞(き)いた<b>ような気(き)がする</b>。
I have some vague feeling I've heard this story before.
</pre>

Both are natural, and in casual speech the short form is if anything the commoner of
the two. Choose the long one when you want the extra padding - an awkward opinion,
a memory you distrust, a criticism you would rather not sharpen.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そのやり方(かた)は危(あぶ)ない<b>気(き)になります</b>。 → ✅ そのやり方(かた)は危(あぶ)ない<b>気(き)がします</b>。
  <code>気(き)になる</code> means "it bothers me / I keep wondering about it". For "I
  have the impression", the verb is <code>気(き)がする</code>.

- ❌ 忘(わす)れ物(もの)をしたような<b>気(き)をします</b>。 → ✅ 忘(わす)れ物(もの)をしたような<b>気(き)がします</b>。
  The particle is `が`. Nothing is being done to the feeling; the feeling simply
  occurs.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>だような気(き)がする</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>な気(き)がする</b>。
  Before a noun, な-adjectives take `な`, never `だ` - and with this pattern the short
  form is the one people actually say.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)30歳(さい)になる<b>ような気(き)がします</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)30歳(さい)になります。
  A fact you know about yourself is not a hunch. Hedging it sounds as though you are
  unsure how old you are.

## Practice

**1.** この字(じ)、どこかで見(み)た____気(き)がする。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この字(じ)、どこかで見(み)た<b>ような</b>気(き)がする。

<code>気(き)</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it has to be in
noun-modifying shape: past plain form plus `ような`. Dropping `よう` altogether is
also fine and slightly more direct.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 窓(まど)が開(あ)いている。誰(だれ)かが入(はい)った（ようだ・ような気(き)がする）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

誰(だれ)かが入(はい)った<b>ようだ</b>。

The open window is concrete evidence, and a conclusion drawn from evidence is
`ようだ`. <code>ような気(き)がする</code> would throw that evidence away and present the whole thing
as an unaccountable feeling.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I get the feeling this train is slower than usual."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この電車(でんしゃ)、いつもより遅(おそ)い<b>ような気(き)がします</b>。

An い-adjective attaches directly to `ような`. The pattern fits because the speaker
has no timetable in hand - only a sense that the journey is dragging.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この映画(えいが)はもう一度(いちど)見(み)たいような気(き)になります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この映画(えいが)はもう一度(いちど)見(み)たいような<b>気(き)がします</b>。

<code>気(き)になる</code> would mean the film is preying on your mind. The verb that
reports an impression is <code>気(き)がする</code>, with `が`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この計画(けいかく)には無理(むり)がある<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
(b) この計画(けいかく)には無理(むり)がある<b>ような気(き)がします</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states a position. You are prepared to say why the plan is overambitious, and
the room will expect you to.

(b) floats the same worry without committing to it, and invites the others to pick
it up or let it drop. In a meeting it is the politer opening move; the risk is that
if you never move to (a), nobody acts on it.

</details>

# ～ふりをする

**Meaning** · to pretend to ～; to act as if - deliberately putting on an appearance you know to be false

The noun <code>ふり</code> (sometimes written 振(ふ)り) is the *show* you put on, and
する is doing it. Two things are built into the pattern: the
appearance is produced on purpose, and the person producing it knows it is not
true. That is what separates it from every "it seems" pattern in the book - those
report how things look, while this one reports somebody staging how things look.

## Formation

<code>ふり</code> is a noun, so whatever comes in front of it takes the shape a noun
modifier takes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (present) | plain form + ふりをする | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)くふりをする |
| Verb (past state) | た-form + ふりをする | 寝(ね)た → 寝(ね)たふりをする |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ふりをする | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないふりをする |
| Verb (progressive) | ている + ふりをする | 聞(き)いている → 聞(き)いているふりをする |
| い-adjective | plain form + ふりをする | 痛(いた)い → 痛(いた)いふりをする |
| な-adjective | ～な + ふりをする | 平気(へいき)な → 平気(へいき)なふりをする |
| Noun | ～の + ふりをする | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)のふりをする |
| Continuing the sentence | ふりをして + verb | 気(き)づかない → 気(き)づかないふりをして通(とお)る |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the tense inside matters. <code>寝(ね)たふり</code> is the normal way to
say "pretending to be asleep", because what you are faking is the *state* of having
fallen asleep. <code>寝(ね)るふり</code> would be miming the act of dropping off - 
possible, but rarely what people mean.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は寝(ね)た<b>ふりをして</b>いる。
My little brother is pretending to be asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
知(し)らない<b>ふりをしないで</b>ください。
Please don't pretend you don't know.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)で先生(せんせい)に会(あ)ったが、気(き)づかない<b>ふりをして</b>通(とお)り過(す)ぎた。
I ran into my teacher in the street, but I walked past pretending not to notice.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は平気(へいき)な<b>ふりをして</b>いたが、手(て)が震(ふる)えていた。
She was acting as though nothing was wrong, but her hands were shaking.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちは警察官(けいさつかん)の<b>ふりをして</b>遊(あそ)んでいる。
The children are playing at being police officers.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう)はどうしても眠(ねむ)くて、話(はなし)を聞(き)いている<b>ふりをする</b>だけでやっとだった。
I was so sleepy in the meeting that it was all I could do to look as if I was listening.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)の冗談(じょうだん)に、みんな面白(おもしろ)い<b>ふりをして</b>笑(わら)っていた。
Everyone laughed at the boss's joke, pretending to find it funny.
</pre>

<pre>
困(こま)っている人(ひと)を見(み)かけても見(み)て見(み)ぬ<b>ふりをする</b>ような大人(おとな)にはなりたくない。
I don't want to grow into the sort of adult who looks the other way when someone is in trouble.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and very common in ordinary speech. In writing you will meet both
<code>ふり</code> and <code>振(ふ)り</code>; kana is the safer default in a sentence,
since the kanji is also used for unrelated words.

The pattern carries a faint moral charge, because pretending is a small deception.
How heavy that charge is depends entirely on the content: <code>寝(ね)たふり</code>
to avoid a chore is comic, while <code>知(し)らないふり</code> about something you
were told is an accusation. Said about yourself it is often self-deprecating;
said about someone else it is rarely a compliment.

Two shortenings are worth recognising. In casual speech `を` drops - 
<code>聞(き)こえないふりした</code> - and the fixed expression
<code>知(し)らんぷり</code> ("playing dumb", "blanking someone") turns up constantly
in conversation with no `する` in sight: 「あいつ、知(し)らんぷりしてたよ」.

The idiom <code>見(み)て見(み)ぬふりをする</code> - literally "see and do the not-
seeing show" - means turning a blind eye, and it is worth learning whole; the `ぬ`
is an old negative you will otherwise not meet at this level.

One limitation. Because the pattern needs an actor, an inanimate subject cannot use
it. Clouds do not pretend to be a face; for that you want `ようだ` or `みたいだ`.

## Contrast

### ふりをする vs まねをする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ふりをする</code> is pretending to **be** something you are
not. <code>まねをする</code> is **copying** somebody's behaviour, with no claim about
who you are.

</div>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は父親(ちちおや)の<b>まねをして</b>、新聞(しんぶん)を広(ひろ)げて読(よ)んでいる。
My son copies his father, spreading out the newspaper and reading it.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は大人(おとな)の<b>ふりをして</b>、映画館(えいがかん)に入(はい)ろうとした。
My son tried to get into the cinema pretending to be an adult.
</pre>

Copying can be admiring, playful or educational - <code>まねをする</code> is how you
learn a craft. Pretending is aimed at somebody's belief: the point of the second
sentence is that the ticket seller was meant to be fooled.

### ふりをする vs ように見(み)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ふりをする</code> describes what the **actor is doing**;
<code>ように見(み)える</code> describes what the **observer sees**. Only the first
claims intent.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は元気(げんき)な<b>ふりをしている</b>。
He's putting on a brave face.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は元気(げんき)そうに<b>見(み)える</b>。
He looks well.
</pre>

The first says you have seen through him; the second says nothing about whether the
appearance is genuine. Getting these backwards is how learners accidentally accuse
people of lying, so keep the intent inside `ふり` firmly in mind.

### ふりをする vs ～つもりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `ふり`, **you know it is false and want others to believe
it**. With the "acting as if" `つもり`, **you have convinced yourself**.

</div>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)はまだ若(わか)い<b>つもり</b>で、重(おも)い荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)ぼうとする。
My grandfather still thinks of himself as young and tries to carry heavy things.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は若(わか)い<b>ふりをして</b>、階段(かいだん)を駆(か)け上(あ)がってみせた。
My grandfather put on a show of youth and ran up the stairs.
</pre>

The `つもり` version is about his self-image; nobody is being deceived except
possibly himself. The `ふり` version has an audience. Note that `つもり` here is not
the "intend to" `つもり` you learned at N5 - same word, and context does the sorting.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 医者(いしゃ)<b>ふりをする</b>。 → ✅ 医者(いしゃ)<b>のふりをする</b>。
  <code>ふり</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`, exactly as in
  <code>医者(いしゃ)の息子(むすこ)</code>.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)<b>だふりをする</b>。 → ✅ 元気(げんき)<b>なふりをする</b>。
  な-adjectives take `な` before a noun. The `だ` form never modifies anything.

- ❌ 忙(いそが)しい<b>ふりがする</b>。 → ✅ 忙(いそが)しい<b>ふりをする</b>。
  The particle is `を` - you *do* a pretence. <code>気(き)がする</code> has `が`
  because a feeling happens to you; a performance does not.

- ❌ 授業(じゅぎょう)中(ちゅう)に寝(ね)る<b>ふりをした</b>。（meaning "I pretended to be asleep"） → ✅ 授業(じゅぎょう)中(ちゅう)に寝(ね)た<b>ふりをした</b>。
  What you fake is the state of being asleep, and that state is the た-form.

## Practice

**1.** 質問(しつもん)されたくなかったので、下(した)を向(む)いて聞(き)こえない____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

聞(き)こえない<b>ふりをした</b>。

The negative plain form attaches directly, and the whole point is that the speaker
could hear perfectly well - a deliberate performance, which is what `ふり` marks.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 妹(いもうと)は歌手(かしゅ)の（ふりをして・まねをして）、鏡(かがみ)の前(まえ)で歌(うた)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

妹(いもうと)は歌手(かしゅ)の<b>まねをして</b>、鏡(かがみ)の前(まえ)で歌(うた)っている。

She is copying a singer's manner for fun, with nobody to deceive. `ふりをして`
would suggest she is trying to pass herself off as a professional.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He pretended not to have heard the question and changed the subject."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は質問(しつもん)が聞(き)こえなかった<b>ふりをして</b>、話(はなし)を変(か)えた。

Use the past negative inside, because what he is faking is the state of not having
caught it, and continue the sentence with <code>ふりをして</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は病気(びょうき)だふりをして、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は病気(びょうき)<b>のふりをして</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)んだ。

<code>病気(びょうき)</code> is a noun, so it links to <code>ふり</code> with `の`.
Neither `だ` nor a bare noun will do.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 母(はは)は<b>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)なふりをしていた</b>。
(b) 母(はは)は<b>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)そうだった</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says your mother was staging it and you saw through the performance. The
sentence quietly tells us she was not all right, and that she had a reason to hide
it.

(b) reports only how she struck you: she seemed fine. It makes no claim about what
was going on underneath, and it could easily be followed by "…but I was wrong".

</details>

# ～わけだ

**Meaning** · so that's why; it follows that; that means - presents the sentence as the conclusion that falls out of something already known

<code>わけ</code> is the noun 訳(わけ), "reason, logic, the way a thing works out".
`わけだ` therefore never stands alone: it always looks back at a premise - something
just said, just discovered, or plainly visible - and says "given that, this is how
it adds up." Three flavours share the one form: *no wonder* (a realisation),
*it follows that* (a calculation), and *in other words* (a restatement).

## Formation

<code>わけ</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + わけだ | 知(し)っている → 知(し)っているわけだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけだ | 通(つう)じない → 通(つう)じないわけだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + わけだ | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いわけだ |
| な-adjective | ～な + わけだ | 不便(ふべん)な → 不便(ふべん)なわけだ |
| Noun | ～な / ～という + わけだ | 留学生(りゅうがくせい) → 留学生(りゅうがくせい)なわけだ |
| Polite | … + わけです | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いわけです |
| Restating what was just said | ～という + わけだ | 来(こ)られない → 来(こ)られないというわけだ |
| Connecting two sentences | というわけで | - → というわけで、中止(ちゅうし)します |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the realisation use is almost always announced in advance by どうりで
("no wonder") or だから. Hearing どうりで, a Japanese listener is already expecting
`わけだ` at the end of the sentence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
窓(まど)が開(あ)いている。どうりで寒(さむ)い<b>わけだ</b>。
The window's open. No wonder it's cold.
</pre>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)のご出身(しゅっしん)ですか。雪(ゆき)に慣(な)れている<b>わけですね</b>。
You're from Hokkaido? That explains why snow doesn't bother you.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は10年(ねん)大阪(おおさか)に住(す)んでいたのだから、関西弁(かんさいべん)が話(はな)せる<b>わけだ</b>。
He lived in Osaka for ten years, so of course he can speak Kansai dialect.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが一時間(いちじかん)に一本(いっぽん)しかないのだから、不便(ふべん)な<b>わけだ</b>。
There's only one bus an hour - no wonder the place is inconvenient.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)3時間(じかん)練習(れんしゅう)すれば、一週間(いっしゅうかん)で21時間(じかん)になる<b>わけです</b>。
If you practise three hours a day, that works out at twenty-one hours a week.
</pre>

<pre>
つまり、今回(こんかい)の旅行(りょこう)には参加(さんか)できない<b>というわけですね</b>。
So what you're saying is that you can't join this trip.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)に工場(こうじょう)が閉(し)まったのだから、この町(まち)の人口(じんこう)が減(へ)っている<b>わけだ</b>。
The factory shut last summer, so it stands to reason the town's population is falling.
</pre>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)5時(じ)に起(お)きて走(はし)っているそうです。あんなに速(はや)く走(はし)れる<b>わけですね</b>。
Apparently he's up at five every morning to run. No wonder he's that fast.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`わけだ` is register-neutral in itself; `わけです` fits any polite setting and turns
up constantly in teaching, briefings and explanations, because its whole job is
showing how a conclusion follows. In casual speech it contracts to `わけ` with the
`だ` dropped - 「あ、それで静(しず)かなわけか」 - and `わけか` is the shape a
realisation takes when you are talking to yourself.

The realisation use is warm and companionable: you have just understood something,
and saying so out loud gives the other person credit for explaining it. The
restating use - <code>つまり…というわけですね</code> - is the standard way to confirm
you have followed a complicated explanation, and in business it is a genuinely
useful move. Handle it with a little care, though. Restating something the listener
already knows perfectly well can sound as if you are explaining them to themselves,
and delivered flatly it comes across as condescending. That is a tendency, not a
rule; tone and context decide.

<code>というわけで</code> deserves separate mention. As a sentence opener it means "and
so", wrapping up a line of reasoning and moving to what follows from it:
<code>というわけで、今日(きょう)はここまでにします</code>. It is extremely common in
spoken explanation and in blog-style writing.

One thing `わけだ` cannot do: announce your own decision. A decision is not something
that follows from evidence, so <code>行(い)くことにしました</code> or
<code>行(い)くつもりです</code> is what you want there.

## Contrast

### わけだ vs のだ・んです

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `んです` supplies the **background** to a situation. `わけだ`
draws the **conclusion** from a background already established.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は休(やす)みました。熱(ねつ)があった<b>んです</b>。
I was off yesterday. I had a temperature.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があったのなら、休(やす)んだ<b>わけだ</b>。
If you had a temperature, then naturally you took the day off.
</pre>

The two point in opposite directions. `んです` runs from the situation back to its
explanation; `わけだ` runs from the explanation forward to the situation. That is
why `わけだ` is so often preceded by だから, どうりで or a `のだから` clause - the
premise has to already be on the table.

### わけだ vs はずだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずだ` is an expectation about something you have **not
confirmed**. `わけだ` accounts for something you **already know to be so**.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは中国語(ちゅうごくご)が話(はな)せる<b>はずです</b>。
Tanaka should be able to speak Chinese.
</pre>

<pre>
上海(シャンハイ)で育(そだ)ったのだから、中国語(ちゅうごくご)が話(はな)せる<b>わけです</b>。
He grew up in Shanghai, so of course he speaks Chinese.
</pre>

In the first, you have not heard him speak it; you are predicting. In the second you
have just heard him, and the sentence explains why. This is why
<code>はずだったのに</code> exists and there is no natural equivalent for `わけだ` - you
cannot be disappointed by a conclusion about a fact you have already observed.

The overlap that confuses people is that both can translate as "so he must speak
Chinese". Ask which way the sentence is facing: forward to an unverified prediction
(`はずだ`), or backward to explain a known fact (`わけだ`).

### わけだ vs ということだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the "that means" sense the two overlap; `わけだ` stresses that
the conclusion **follows logically**, `ということだ` simply **restates** the content.

</div>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)が反対(はんたい)したのなら、この案(あん)は通(とお)らない<b>わけだ</b>。
If everyone opposed it, then the proposal isn't going through.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)が反対(はんたい)した。この案(あん)は通(とお)らない<b>ということだ</b>。
Everyone opposed it. That means the proposal isn't going through.
</pre>

The difference is thin here, and in many sentences either will pass. Where they part
company is that `ということだ` also carries the hearsay meaning - "I hear that" - and
`わけだ` never does. If a source is named, only `ということだ` is possible.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あの人(ひと)は医者(いしゃ)<b>だわけだ</b>。 → ✅ あの人(ひと)は医者(いしゃ)<b>なわけだ</b>。（or 医者(いしゃ)というわけだ）
  A noun links to <code>わけ</code> with `な` or `という`, never with `だ`.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>わけだ</b>。（said out of nowhere） → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るでしょう。
  `わけだ` needs a premise behind it. With nothing to conclude *from*, the sentence
  leaves the listener waiting for the missing first half.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)する<b>わけです</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)する<b>つもりです</b>。
  Your own plans are decisions, not conclusions. `わけだ` would suggest your studying
  abroad was somehow deduced from circumstances.

- ❌ どうりで道(みち)が込(こ)んでいる<b>はずだ</b>。 → ✅ どうりで道(みち)が込(こ)んでいる<b>わけだ</b>。
  どうりで introduces an explanation for something you are looking at right now, and
  that pairs with `わけだ`. `はずだ` would turn a fact into a prediction.

## Practice

**1.** 時計(とけい)が10分(じゅっぷん)遅(おく)れていたのか。それで電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)り遅(おく)れた____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

それで電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)り遅(おく)れた<b>わけだ</b>。

The clock is the premise, and missing the train is the fact being accounted for.
それで plus `わけだ` is the classic realisation shape - "ah, so *that's* why".

</details>

**2.** Choose: 山田(やまだ)さんは昨日(きのう)出張(しゅっちょう)に行(い)ったから、今日(きょう)は会社(かいしゃ)にいない（はずだ・わけだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は会社(かいしゃ)にいない<b>はずだ</b>。

You have not checked his desk; you are predicting from what you know. That is
`はずだ`. `わけだ` would fit only if you had already found the desk empty and were
explaining why.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The heating was off all night - no wonder the room is freezing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一晩中(ひとばんじゅう)暖房(だんぼう)が切(き)れていたのか。どうりで部屋(へや)が寒(さむ)い<b>わけだ</b>。

"No wonder" is どうりで, and the conclusion it introduces takes `わけだ`. The
い-adjective attaches with no change at all.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)は元気(げんき)だわけだから、心配(しんぱい)しなくていい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は元気(げんき)<b>なわけだから</b>、心配(しんぱい)しなくていい。

な-adjectives take `な` before <code>わけ</code>. The `だ` of
<code>元気(げんき)だ</code> cannot modify a noun, and <code>わけ</code> is a noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)している<b>んです</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)は毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)している<b>わけです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) offers his daily practice as the explanation for something else - why he is
good, why he is never free, why he looks tired. It supplies background.

(b) presents the daily practice as what follows from something already said: you
have just been told he trains with a club six evenings a week, and this sentence
adds it up. Said with no premise in front of it, (b) sounds unfinished.

</details>

# ～わけがない

**Meaning** · there's no way ～; it's impossible that ～ - denies that any path of reasoning could lead to the thing being true

Keep the literal sense in view: <code>わけ</code> is the 訳(わけ), the reason or
logic by which something could come about, and `がない` says there is none. So
`わけがない` does not say "unlikely" or "I doubt it". It says the door is shut. This
is the strongest denial in the わけ family, and it is the one learners over-use.

**What is negated: the possibility itself.** That single fact separates it from
`わけではない`, which leaves the possibility standing and denies only an inference.

## Formation

<code>わけ</code> is a noun, so the clause in front takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + わけがない | 勝(か)てる → 勝(か)てるわけがない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけがない | 気(き)づかない → 気(き)づかないわけがない |
| い-adjective | plain form + わけがない | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いわけがない |
| な-adjective | ～な + わけがない | 簡単(かんたん)な → 簡単(かんたん)なわけがない |
| Noun | ～な / ～という + わけがない | 本物(ほんもの) → 本物(ほんもの)なわけがない |
| Slightly softer / more written | … + わけはない | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れるわけはない |
| Polite | … + わけがありません | 届(とど)く → 届(とど)くわけがありません |
| Casual speech | … + わけない | できる → できるわけないよ |

## Examples

<pre>
子(こ)どもにこの漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)める<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way a child can read this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)がうそをつく<b>わけがありません</b>。
He would never lie - it's simply not possible.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)もしないで、試合(しあい)に勝(か)てる<b>わけがない</b>よ。
You don't practise, so of course you can't win the match.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに甘(あま)いのに、カロリーが低(ひく)い<b>わけがない</b>。
As sweet as this is, there is no chance it's low in calories.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)会(あ)っただけの人(ひと)の名前(なまえ)を覚(おぼ)えている<b>わけがない</b>じゃないか。
Come on, nobody remembers the name of someone they met once.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)も読(よ)まずに組(く)み立(た)てて、うまくいく<b>わけがない</b>でしょう。
Putting it together without even reading the instructions - that was never going to work.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)夜中(よなか)まで残業(ざんぎょう)が続(つづ)いているのだから、体(からだ)に影響(えいきょう)が出(で)ない<b>わけがない</b>。
With overtime running until the middle of the night every day, it cannot possibly be leaving him unaffected.
</pre>

<pre>
あれほど楽(たの)しみにしていたのだから、中止(ちゅうし)と聞(き)いて娘(むすめ)ががっかりしない<b>わけがありません</b>。
She'd been looking forward to it so much that there is no way our daughter isn't crushed to hear it's cancelled.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a forceful, emotionally coloured pattern. It does not weigh evidence the way
`はずがない` does; it announces that the speaker regards the idea as out of the
question. That makes it excellent for arguing, for reassuring - 
<code>忘(わす)れるわけがないよ</code> - and for sarcasm, and risky when the thing you are ruling out is
what the person in front of you just said. Contradicting a superior with
<code>そんなわけがありません</code> is blunt to the point of rudeness; softer options are
<code>そうとは思(おも)えません</code> or <code>はずがありません</code>.

Casual speech drops `が`: <code>できるわけない</code>, <code>知(し)ってるわけないじゃん</code>.
That short form is very common among friends and carries a shrug with it. Going the
other way, `わけはない` - with `は` instead of `が` - reads a shade calmer and more
written, because the `は` gives it a whiff of contrast rather than flat denial.

Two neighbours to keep separate. <code>わけにはいかない</code> looks similar and means
something else entirely - "I can't, for reasons of duty or circumstance" - and
`わけではない` is the partial denial treated on its own page. Confusing `わけがない`
with `わけではない` is the single most common error in this corner of the grammar, so
the first contrast below is worth reading twice.

## Contrast

### わけがない vs わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけがない` denies **the thing itself**: it cannot be true.
`わけではない` denies **an inference**: the thing may well be partly true, but it
does not follow the way you assumed.

</div>

<pre>
大好(だいす)きな先輩(せんぱい)に会(あ)えるのに、嬉(うれ)しくない<b>わけがない</b>。
I get to see the senior I adore - there's no way I'm not delighted.
</pre>

<pre>
会(あ)えて嬉(うれ)しいが、緊張(きんちょう)していない<b>わけではない</b>。
I'm glad to see her, though that isn't to say I'm not nervous.
</pre>

Try the substitution test. Replace `わけではない` with "it's not that…" and the
sentence should still work; replace `わけがない` with "there's no way…" and *that*
should work. If both readings feel possible, look at what the speaker is doing: are
they rejecting a claim outright, or trimming an over-reading?

<pre>
彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)である<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way he's the culprit.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)を疑(うたが)っている<b>わけではない</b>。
It's not that I suspect him.
</pre>

The first defends him absolutely. The second only corrects an impression you may
have given - you are not accusing him, but you have not said he is innocent either.
That gap between the two is exactly the gap between the patterns.

### わけがない vs はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずがない` argues **from a known fact**: given X, Y cannot
follow. `わけがない` states the speaker's **conviction** that Y is impossible, with
or without an argument.

</div>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)は私(わたし)が持(も)っていたのだから、誰(だれ)かが入(はい)れた<b>はずがない</b>。
I had the key on me, so nobody can have got in.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに優(やさ)しい人(ひと)が、そんなひどいことを言(い)う<b>わけがない</b>。
Someone that kind would never say something so cruel.
</pre>

The first is a piece of reasoning you could write out step by step. The second is
conviction about a person's character, and it would be odd to demand the speaker's
evidence. In many sentences either pattern is acceptable and the difference is
temperature: `はずがない` cool, `わけがない` warm. Note also that `はずがない` more
comfortably admits it was wrong - <code>はずがないと思(おも)っていたのに</code> - while
`わけがない` sounds strange doing so.

### わけがない vs わけにはいかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけがない` is about what is **possible**. `わけにはいかない` is
about what is **permissible**: you could do it, but circumstances or obligation stop
you.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は大事(だいじ)な試験(しけん)だから、休(やす)む<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
There's an important exam tomorrow, so I can't very well take the day off.
</pre>

<pre>
あの忙(いそが)しさで、ゆっくり休(やす)める<b>わけがない</b>。
As busy as that, there's no chance of getting any real rest.
</pre>

In the first the speaker is perfectly able to stay home; what rules it out is what
is at stake. In the second nothing is stopping him except reality. Because the two
forms sit one syllable apart, they get swapped constantly - check whether you mean
"cannot happen" or "mustn't happen".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この仕事(しごと)が簡単(かんたん)<b>だわけがない</b>。 → ✅ この仕事(しごと)が簡単(かんたん)<b>なわけがない</b>。
  A な-adjective takes `な` before <code>わけ</code>. The same goes for nouns, which
  take `な` or `という`.

- ❌ 嫌(きら)いな<b>わけがない</b>が、毎日(まいにち)は食(た)べたくない。 → ✅ 嫌(きら)いな<b>わけではない</b>が、毎日(まいにち)は食(た)べたくない。
  The speaker means "it's not that I dislike it" - a partial denial. `わけがない`
  would claim disliking it is impossible, which clashes with the second half.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>わけがない</b>から、傘(かさ)はいらないだろう。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>そうもない</b>から、傘(かさ)はいらないだろう。
  Tomorrow's weather is uncertain, not impossible. `わけがない` is far too absolute
  for a forecast.

- ❌ 車(くるま)で来(き)たので、お酒(さけ)を飲(の)む<b>わけがない</b>。 → ✅ 車(くるま)で来(き)たので、お酒(さけ)を飲(の)む<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
  You are able to drink; you must not. Impossibility and prohibition are different
  patterns.

## Practice

**1.** 徹夜(てつや)で作(つく)った資料(しりょう)だ。間違(まちが)いが一(ひと)つもない____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

間違(まちが)いが一(ひと)つもない<b>わけがない</b>。

Something written overnight cannot plausibly be flawless, and the speaker rules the
idea out rather than merely doubting it - the categorical denial `わけがない`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 甘(あま)いものが（嫌(きら)いなわけがない・嫌(きら)いなわけではない）が、健康(けんこう)のために控(ひか)えている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

甘(あま)いものが<b>嫌(きら)いなわけではない</b>が、健康(けんこう)のために控(ひか)えている。

The second half admits the speaker avoids sweets, so the first half is correcting a
likely misreading - "not that I dislike them". `わけがない` would declare disliking
them impossible and leave the `が` clause hanging.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He only started last month - there's no way he knows the whole system."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先月(せんげつ)入(はい)ったばかりの人(ひと)が、システム全体(ぜんたい)を分(わ)かっている<b>わけがない</b>。

The point is impossibility, not doubt, so `わけがない` (or, arguing more coolly from
the same fact, <code>はずがありません</code>).

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 大切(たいせつ)な会議(かいぎ)だから、遅(おく)れるわけがない。（intended: "I mustn't be late"）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 大切(たいせつ)な会議(かいぎ)だから、遅(おく)れる<b>わけにはいかない</b>。

As written, the sentence claims that being late is impossible. What the speaker
means is that it is not allowed to happen, and that is `わけにはいかない`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の気持(きも)ちが分(わ)からない<b>わけがない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)の気持(きも)ちが分(わ)からない<b>わけではない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) insists that failing to understand him is impossible - you know him too well, or
the situation is too obvious. It is emphatic, and often reproachful: "of course you
understand how he feels."

(b) concedes the opposite way round. You do, to some extent, understand him; the
sentence heads off the assumption that you are unsympathetic, and it almost always
continues with が or けど into whatever the objection really is.

</details>

# ～わけではない

**Meaning** · it's not that ～; that doesn't mean ～ - cancels a conclusion somebody has drawn, without denying the facts behind it

`わけではない` is a **partial** denial. Something has been said, or is about to be
assumed, and the natural inference from it is wrong - so you reach in and take away
the inference, leaving everything else standing. English does the same job with
"it's not that…", "that's not to say…" and "not necessarily".

**What is negated: the inference, not the possibility.** `わけがない` slams the door
on the whole idea; `わけではない` only says the idea does not follow from what you
have just heard. Learners swap the two constantly, and the results are much ruder
than intended.

## Formation

<code>わけ</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + わけではない | 嫌(きら)っている → 嫌(きら)っているわけではない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけではない | 払(はら)えない → 払(はら)えないわけではない |
| い-adjective | plain form + わけではない | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いわけではない |
| な-adjective | ～な + わけではない | 嫌(きら)いな → 嫌(きら)いなわけではない |
| Noun | ～な / ～という + わけではない | 専門家(せんもんか) → 専門家(せんもんか)というわけではない |
| Polite | … + わけではありません | 反対(はんたい)している → 反対(はんたい)しているわけではありません |
| Casual speech | … + わけじゃない | 怒(おこ)っている → 怒(おこ)っているわけじゃない |
| Softened further | … + というわけではない | 嫌(きら)いだ → 嫌(きら)いというわけではない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern is at its most natural with a partner phrase in front of it:
必(かなら)ずしも ("not necessarily"), 全(まった)く ("not entirely"), いつも,
全部(ぜんぶ), or a からといって clause ("just because…"). Those words all set up the
over-general idea that `わけではない` then trims back.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っているが、大会(たいかい)に出(で)る<b>わけではない</b>。
I run every day, but that doesn't mean I'm going to enter a race.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)している<b>わけではありません</b>が、もう少(すこ)し考(かんが)えたいです。
It's not that I'm against it - I'd just like a bit more time to think.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)くなっただけで、怒(おこ)っている<b>わけじゃない</b>よ。
I was just slow replying; it's not that I'm cross with you.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)がない<b>わけではない</b>が、これは高(たか)すぎる。
It's not that I can't afford it - this is simply too expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)が全(まった)く話(はな)せない<b>わけではない</b>けれど、会議(かいぎ)についていくのは難(むずか)しい。
It isn't that I can't speak Japanese at all, but keeping up in meetings is hard.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で暮(く)らしているからといって、寂(さび)しい<b>わけではない</b>。
Living alone doesn't automatically mean being lonely.
</pre>

<pre>
この方法(ほうほう)がいつでもうまくいく<b>というわけではない</b>ので、状況(じょうきょう)を見(み)て判断(はんだん)してください。
This approach doesn't always work, so use your judgment as the situation requires.
</pre>

<pre>
辞(や)めたいと言(い)ったのは本当(ほんとう)ですが、今(いま)すぐ会社(かいしゃ)を出(で)ていく<b>わけではありません</b>。
It's true that I said I want to quit, but that doesn't mean I'm walking out tomorrow.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and indispensable in polite conversation. Its social function is
**face**: it lets you disagree with an interpretation while accepting the facts, so
nobody has to be wrong. In a meeting,
<code>予算(よさん)が足(た)りないというわけではありません</code> concedes that money
has been discussed and merely corrects what the listener made of it.

Casual speech contracts it to `わけじゃない`, which is one of the most frequent
phrases in everyday Japanese - 「べつに嫌(きら)いなわけじゃないけど」. The `べつに`
in front is almost a fixed companion, and adding `けど` at the end leaves room for
whatever the real point turns out to be.

Because the pattern *corrects an inference*, it needs the inference to exist. Said
out of the blue, with nothing for it to push back against, it sounds like an answer
to a question nobody asked. Give it a premise: a からといって clause, a previous
remark, or a visible situation.

Two combinations are worth memorising whole. <code>必(かなら)ずしも～わけではない</code>
is the standard "not necessarily", and it is common in written argument.
<code>～ないわけではない</code> is a double negative meaning "it isn't that I can't" - 
grudging agreement, one step short of saying yes.

## Contrast

### わけではない vs わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけではない` denies **the inference**; `わけがない` denies **the
possibility**. One trims, the other refuses.

</div>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)が苦手(にがて)な<b>わけではない</b>。ただ書(か)く練習(れんしゅう)が足(た)りないだけだ。
It isn't that I'm bad at kanji - I just haven't practised writing them enough.
</pre>

<pre>
習(なら)ってもいない漢字(かんじ)が書(か)ける<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way anyone can write kanji they've never been taught.
</pre>

The first accepts that the speaker's kanji are weak and rejects only the explanation
offered for it. The second rules the whole proposition out. Swap them and you get
nonsense: ❌<code>漢字(かんじ)が苦手(にがて)なわけがない</code> would insist that being
bad at kanji is impossible.

### わけではない vs ではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `ではない` denies the **fact**. `わけではない` leaves the
fact alone and denies **what you would read into it**.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)のコーヒーはおいし<b>くない</b>。
The coffee at this place isn't good.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)のコーヒーがおいしい<b>わけではない</b>が、静(しず)かなのでよく来(く)る。
It's not as if the coffee here is any good, but it's quiet, so I come often.
</pre>

The first is a verdict on the coffee. The second is a verdict on your reasoning: you
assumed the coffee was why the speaker keeps coming, and they are correcting that,
not reviewing the drink. This is why `わけではない` so often has a `が` or `けど`
clause attached - the real point is usually in the second half.

### わけではない vs とは限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> attacks a **generalisation**:
it isn't always so. `わけではない` attacks an **inference about the case in front of
you**.

</div>

<pre>
高(たか)い店(みせ)の料理(りょうり)がおいしいとは<b>限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Expensive restaurants aren't always good.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)で選(えら)んだ<b>わけではない</b>。味(あじ)が気(き)に入(い)ったから選(えら)んだのだ。
I didn't choose it on price - I chose it because I liked the taste.
</pre>

The first would sit comfortably in an essay about restaurants in general. The second
is about one decision, one speaker, one misreading being corrected. With
必(かなら)ずしも in front, the two come very close together, and in sentences about
general rules they are often interchangeable.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 嫌(きら)い<b>だわけではない</b>。 → ✅ 嫌(きら)い<b>なわけではない</b>。（or 嫌(きら)いというわけではない）
  な-adjectives and nouns link to <code>わけ</code> with `な` or `という`, never `だ`.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)が解(と)ける<b>わけではない</b>。（intended: "there's no way I can solve this"） → ✅ この問題(もんだい)が解(と)ける<b>わけがない</b>。
  For flat impossibility you want `わけがない`. `わけではない` only says that being
  able to solve it does not follow from whatever was just said.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は医者(いしゃ)な<b>わけではありません</b>。（simply stating you are not a doctor） → ✅ 私(わたし)は医者(いしゃ)<b>ではありません</b>。
  A plain fact takes a plain negative. `わけではない` is for cancelling an inference,
  and using it here implies somebody had just concluded that you were one.

- ❌ 寒(さむ)くない<b>わけではない</b>から、上着(うわぎ)はいらない。 → ✅ 寒(さむ)くない<b>わけではない</b>から、上着(うわぎ)を持(も)っていこう。
  <code>～ないわけではない</code> is a double negative: "it isn't that it's not cold",
  i.e. it *is* somewhat cold. Make sure the second half agrees with the arithmetic.

## Practice

**1.** ダイエット中(ちゅう)だと言(い)っても、何(なに)も食(た)べない____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)も食(た)べない<b>わけではない</b>。

Saying you are on a diet invites the conclusion that you eat nothing, and the
sentence heads that conclusion off while leaving the diet itself intact - the
textbook use of `わけではない`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)はまだ新人(しんじん)だから、一人(ひとり)で全部(ぜんぶ)任(まか)せられる（わけがない・わけではない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一人(ひとり)で全部(ぜんぶ)任(まか)せられる<b>わけがない</b>。

He is brand new, and the speaker is ruling the idea out entirely. `わけではない`
would say only that being new does not *mean* he can be trusted with everything,
which is a much milder claim than the からだ clause sets up.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Just because a book is famous doesn't mean it's interesting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

有名(ゆうめい)な本(ほん)だからといって、面白(おもしろ)い<b>わけではない</b>。

からといって sets up the inference - fame implies quality - and `わけではない` removes
it. The famousness of the book is not being denied at all.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: お茶(ちゃ)が好(す)きだわけではないが、毎朝(まいあさ)飲(の)んでいる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ お茶(ちゃ)が好(す)き<b>なわけではない</b>が、毎朝(まいあさ)飲(の)んでいる。

<code>好(す)きだ</code> is a な-adjective, so it becomes <code>好(す)きな</code>
before the noun <code>わけ</code>. The sense is right: drinking it daily does not
prove fondness.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)のことを信(しん)じていない<b>わけがない</b>。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)のことを信(しん)じていない<b>わけではない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) declares that distrusting her is impossible - of course the speaker believes
her, and the sentence is emphatic, almost indignant.

(b) is a careful concession: the speaker does trust her, up to a point, and is
correcting the impression that they do not. It nearly always continues with が or
けど into the reservation that prompted the sentence in the first place. The
grammatical distance between the two is one particle; the social distance is
enormous.

</details>

# ～とは限らない

**Meaning** · not necessarily ～; ～ isn't always the case - denies that a general rule holds every time

Read as *とは かぎらない*. The verb 限(かぎ)る means "to be limited to", so
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> literally says "things are not limited to that
outcome". You use it when somebody - the listener, common sense, or the speaker's
own earlier sentence - is treating something as a rule, and you want to point out
the exceptions without denying the rule's usual force.

## Formation

It attaches to a whole **plain-form clause**, because what it denies is a whole
proposition, not a word.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + とは限(かぎ)らない | 勝(か)つ → 勝(か)つとは限(かぎ)らない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + とは限(かぎ)らない | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないとは限(かぎ)らない |
| い-adjective | plain form + とは限(かぎ)らない | 正(ただ)しい → 正(ただ)しいとは限(かぎ)らない |
| な-adjective | ～だ + とは限(かぎ)らない | 安全(あんぜん)だ → 安全(あんぜん)だとは限(かぎ)らない |
| Noun | Noun (+ だ) + とは限(かぎ)らない | 本当(ほんとう) → 本当(ほんとう)だとは限(かぎ)らない |
| Polite | … + とは限(かぎ)りません | 届(とど)く → 届(とど)くとは限(かぎ)りません |
| With the usual partner adverb | 必(かなら)ずしも + … + とは限(かぎ)らない | 安(やす)い → 必(かなら)ずしも安(やす)いとは限(かぎ)らない |
| Double-negative variant | ～ない + とも限(かぎ)らない | 起(お)こらない → 起(お)こらないとも限(かぎ)らない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - 必(かなら)ずしも is not required, but it is the adverb the pattern
expects, and it makes the "not always" reading unmistakable. In written argument the
pair <code>必(かなら)ずしも～とは限(かぎ)らない</code> is close to a fixed phrase.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
高(たか)いものが長持(ながも)ちする<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Expensive things don't always last longer.
</pre>

<pre>
日本人(にほんじん)なら誰(だれ)でも漢字(かんじ)が得意(とくい)だ<b>とは限(かぎ)りません</b>。
Not every Japanese person is good at kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)予報(よほう)が必(かなら)ずしも当(あ)たる<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
The forecast isn't necessarily right.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)く並(なら)べば席(せき)が取(と)れる<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>よ。
Queuing early doesn't guarantee you a seat, you know.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)に書(か)いてあることが全(すべ)て正(ただ)しい<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Not everything written in the manual is necessarily correct.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)の高(たか)い会社(かいしゃ)が働(はたら)きやすい<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
A company that pays well isn't necessarily a comfortable place to work.
</pre>

<pre>
経験(けいけん)が長(なが)いからといって、教(おし)え方(かた)が上手(じょうず)だ<b>とは限(かぎ)りません</b>。
Long experience doesn't automatically make someone a good teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)は静(しず)かですが、夜中(よなか)に工事(こうじ)の音(おと)がしない<b>とも限(かぎ)りません</b>ので、窓(まど)は閉(し)めておきましょう。
It's quiet around here, but you never know when there might be noise from the roadworks at night, so let's keep the windows shut.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, with a slight lean towards careful speech and writing. It is the
standard way to introduce a caveat in an argument, so it fills newspaper columns,
textbooks and advice of every kind, and it is equally at home in conversation when
you want to warn someone off an assumption.

Socially it is gentle. Because it denies only universality, not the claim itself, it
lets you push back without contradicting anyone flatly: "true as a rule, not true
every time." <code>必(かなら)ずしもそうとは限(かぎ)りません</code> is a stock polite
disagreement in a meeting, and it is much softer than
<code>そんなわけがありません</code>.

The variant <code>～ないとも限(かぎ)らない</code> deserves attention, because it is a
double negative and learners routinely read it backwards. Literally "it is not
limited to not happening", it means "it might well happen - you never know", and it
almost always introduces a **risk** worth preparing for:
<code>地震(じしん)が起(お)きないとも限(かぎ)らない</code>. If a sentence with
`とも限らない` seems to be arguing for complacency, you have the polarity the wrong
way round.

One boundary: the pattern needs something general to push against - a rule, a
tendency, a "just because" clause, an *everyone* or an *always*. Applied to a plain
one-off prediction it sounds oddly stiff, and <code>かもしれません</code> or
<code>とは思(おも)えません</code> is what a speaker would actually use.

## Contrast

### とは限(かぎ)らない vs わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> denies that something is **always**
so. `わけではない` denies that a conclusion **follows in this case**.

</div>

<pre>
人気(にんき)のある店(みせ)がいつも安(やす)い<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Popular shops aren't always cheap.
</pre>

<pre>
その店(みせ)が安(やす)い<b>わけではない</b>。ただ近(ちか)いから通(かよ)っているだけだ。
It's not that the shop is cheap - I go there simply because it's close.
</pre>

The first is about shops in general and could open a paragraph of consumer advice.
The second is about one shop and one listener's mistaken assumption. Where a
sentence is about a general rule, the two patterns often both fit, and
<code>必(かなら)ずしも</code> can appear with either.

### とは限(かぎ)らない vs ～ないとも限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>Xとは限(かぎ)らない</code> = "X is not always so".
<code>Xないとも限(かぎ)らない</code> = "it's not impossible that X happens" - a
warning.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)晴(は)れる<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
It won't necessarily be fine tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らない<b>とも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、傘(かさ)を持(も)っていきなさい。
It's not out of the question that it'll suddenly rain, so take an umbrella.
</pre>

The first removes a guarantee. The second raises a possibility you had dismissed,
and it points forward to a precaution. Note the different attitudes: `とは限らない`
dampens optimism, `とも限らない` disturbs complacency.

### とは限(かぎ)らない vs かもしれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かもしれない` floats **one possibility**.
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> corrects **someone's generalisation**.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になる<b>かもしれない</b>。
Tomorrow's match might be called off.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったら必(かなら)ず中止(ちゅうし)になる<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Rain doesn't automatically mean the match is off.
</pre>

The first adds a new thought to the conversation. The second takes an existing
belief - rain means cancellation - and puts a limit on it. If nobody in the
conversation holds the belief, `とは限らない` has nothing to work on.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この道(みち)が安全(あんぜん)な<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。 → ✅ この道(みち)が安全(あんぜん)<b>だとは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
  What comes before `と` is a full sentence, not a noun modifier, so a な-adjective
  keeps its `だ` rather than turning into `な`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は明日(あした)行(い)く<b>とは限(かぎ)りません</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は明日(あした)行(い)くかどうか、まだ分(わ)かりません。
  Your own undecided plans are not a general rule with exceptions. The pattern is
  for correcting what is taken to be always true.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ない<b>とも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、準備(じゅんび)しなくてもいい。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ない<b>とも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、準備(じゅんび)しておこう。
  The double negative means "he may well turn up after all". That is a reason to get
  ready, not a reason to relax.

- ❌ 必(かなら)ずしも安(やす)い<b>とは限(かぎ)らないではない</b>。 → ✅ 必(かなら)ずしも安(やす)い<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
  <code>限(かぎ)らない</code> is already the negative of 限(かぎ)る. Adding a
  second negative on the end leaves the sentence saying the opposite of your point.

## Practice

**1.** 有名(ゆうめい)な大学(だいがく)を出(で)た人(ひと)が仕事(しごと)ができる____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

仕事(しごと)ができる<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

The sentence takes a widely held generalisation - good university, capable worker - 
and denies that it holds every time. Adding 必(かなら)ずしも in front would make
the reading even clearer.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 夜道(よみち)は危(あぶ)ないので、何(なに)も起(お)こらない（とは限(かぎ)らない・とも限(かぎ)らない）から、気(き)をつけて。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)も起(お)こらない<b>とも限(かぎ)らない</b>から、気(き)をつけて。

The speaker is raising a risk - something could happen - and that is the
<code>ないとも限(かぎ)らない</code> shape. <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> would say
"nothing happening isn't always the case", which is a clumsy way to state the same
thing and does not sit well with the warning.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Just because a book sells well doesn't mean it's well written."

A generalisation is being limited, so <code>とは限(かぎ)りません</code>. You could
open with <code>売(う)れているからといって</code> instead, and the ending would stay
the same.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 上手(じょうず)な人(ひと)が教(おし)えるのが得意(とくい)なとは限(かぎ)らない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 上手(じょうず)な人(ひと)が教(おし)えるのが得意(とくい)<b>だとは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

<code>得意(とくい)</code> is a な-adjective, but here it ends a clause rather than
modifying a noun, so it keeps `だ` before `と`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 説明書(せつめいしょ)の通(とお)りにやれば直(なお)る<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
(b) 説明書(せつめいしょ)の通(とお)りにやっても直(なお)らない<b>かもしれない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is aimed at a belief: you think following the manual guarantees a fix, and the
speaker is removing the guarantee while granting that the manual usually works. It
argues.

(b) simply raises a possibility about this attempt. Nobody's assumption is being
corrected, and the sentence would be equally natural said to yourself. In practice
(a) is what a senior colleague says to set expectations; (b) is what you mutter with
the screwdriver in your hand.

</details>

# ～ないことはない

**Meaning** · it's not that ～ can't be done; it isn't impossible - a double negative that concedes a small, reluctant yes

Two negatives make a weak positive. <code>飲(の)めないことはない</code> does not mean
"I can drink"; it means "it isn't the case that I can't drink" - which in practice
is a hedged, half-hearted "I can, up to a point". The pattern exists precisely so
that you can agree without committing, and a `が` or `けど` clause spelling out the
reservation almost always follows.

## Formation

The clause inside is in the **negative**, and <code>ことはない</code> negates it again.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ない-form + ことはない | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないことはない |
| Potential verb (most common) | potential ない-form + ことはない | 飲(の)めない → 飲(の)めないことはない |
| い-adjective | ～くない + ことはない | 安(やす)くない → 安(やす)くないことはない |
| な-adjective | ～ではない + ことはない | 不安(ふあん)ではない → 不安(ふあん)ではないことはない |
| Noun | ～ではない + ことはない | 無理(むり)ではない → 無理(むり)ではないことはない |
| Softer, commoner in speech | ～ない + こともない | 間(ま)に合(あ)わない → 間(ま)に合(あ)わないこともない |
| Polite | … + ないことはありません | できない → できないことはありません |
| Usual continuation | … + が / けど + reservation | - → できないことはないが、時間(じかん)がかかる |

## Examples

<pre>
お酒(さけ)は飲(の)め<b>ないことはない</b>。
It's not that I can't drink.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)は読(よ)め<b>ないことはない</b>が、時間(じかん)がかかる。
I can read kanji well enough, though it takes me a while.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で行(い)か<b>ないこともない</b>けど、できれば誰(だれ)かについてきてほしい。
I could go on my own, I suppose, but I'd rather someone came with me.
</pre>

<pre>
この服(ふく)、似合(にあ)わ<b>ないことはない</b>けれど、色(いろ)が少(すこ)し派手(はで)かな。
It's not that these clothes don't suit me - the colour is a bit loud, that's all.
</pre>

<pre>
走(はし)れば終電(しゅうでん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わ<b>ないこともない</b>。
If we run, we might just make the last train.
</pre>

<pre>
「土曜日(どようび)、お願(ねが)いできますか。」「出(で)られ<b>ないことはありません</b>が、午後(ごご)からになります。」
"Could I ask you to come in on Saturday?" "I can manage it, but not before the afternoon."
</pre>

<pre>
予算(よさん)を増(ふ)やせば来月(らいげつ)までに完成(かんせい)でき<b>ないこともありません</b>が、品質(ひんしつ)が心配(しんぱい)です。
With a bigger budget it wouldn't be impossible to finish by next month, but I'd worry about the quality.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の言(い)い分(ぶん)も分(わ)から<b>ないことはない</b>が、あのやり方(かた)には賛成(さんせい)できない。
I can see his side of it, up to a point, but I can't agree with the way he went about it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and heavily used in polite negotiation, because it is the shape of
a qualified yes. When a Japanese colleague answers a request with
<code>できないことはありませんが…</code>, the important information is in whatever comes
after `が`; the first half has only agreed that the thing is theoretically possible.
Hearing it as an enthusiastic acceptance is a classic misreading.

`ないこともない` - with `も` instead of `は` - is softer again and is the version you
hear most in conversation. Both forms keep company with hedging words:
少(すこ)し, なんとか, どうにか and a trailing けど all cluster around them.

Tone matters. Said about your own abilities the pattern is modest, which is usually
welcome. Said about someone else's work - <code>悪(わる)くないことはない</code> - it
can come out grudging or faintly damning, so it is worth being aware that you are
praising with the handbrake on. Used constantly, it starts to sound evasive; a
speaker who never gives a straight yes is noticed.

Finally, note that the pattern says nothing about *willingness* on its own. It
reports that the door is not closed. Whether you actually intend to walk through it
is the job of the sentence that follows.

## Contrast

### ～ないことはない vs ～ことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with the negative inside, it means "it's not impossible".
Without it, <code>～ことはない</code> means **"there's no need to"** - a completely
different pattern that happens to look similar.

</div>

<pre>
そんなに急(いそ)ぐ<b>ことはない</b>よ。時間(じかん)はまだある。
There's no need to rush. We've still got time.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)が<b>ないことはない</b>が、走(はし)るほどではない。
We do need to get a move on, though not to the point of running.
</pre>

The first tells someone to relax; the second grudgingly admits some hurry is called
for. The only visible difference is the negative on the inner verb, so read these
two slowly - mixing them up reverses your advice.

### ～ないことはない vs ～できる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the potential form states an ability plainly. The double
negative concedes it **with reservations attached**.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)までに直(なお)せ<b>ます</b>。
I can have it fixed by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)までに直(なお)せ<b>ないことはありません</b>が、ほかの仕事(しごと)が止(と)まります。
I could have it fixed by tomorrow, but everything else would have to stop.
</pre>

The first is a promise; the second is an opening position in a negotiation. If you
mean the first and say the second, you will be heard as reluctant - and if you mean
the second and say the first, you have just agreed to something you did not intend.

### ～ないことはない vs ～ないわけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they overlap a great deal. The tendency:
<code>ないことはない</code> concedes a **possibility or ability**;
<code>ないわけではない</code> corrects an **impression** the listener has formed.

</div>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で作(つく)れ<b>ないことはない</b>が、買(か)ったほうが早(はや)い。
I could make one myself, but buying one is quicker.
</pre>

<pre>
料理(りょうり)が嫌(きら)い<b>なわけではない</b>。ただ時間(じかん)がないだけだ。
It's not that I dislike cooking - I just haven't the time.
</pre>

The first weighs whether the thing can be done; the second repairs a wrong reading
of the speaker's feelings. In many sentences either would pass, and a native speaker
would not always be able to say why they chose one. Where the sentence is about
capability, `ないことはない` is the safer choice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>ないことはない</b>。 → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>ないことはない</b>。
  The inner clause has to be a proper ない-form. Attaching `ない` to the dictionary
  form is not a possible shape in Japanese.

- ❌ この辺(へん)は静(しず)か<b>ないことはない</b>。 → ✅ この辺(へん)は静(しず)か<b>ではないことはない</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns negate with `ではない` (casually `じゃない`) before the
  pattern.

- ❌ 「一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)きませんか。」「行(い)け<b>ないことはありません</b>!」 → ✅ 「ぜひ行(い)きたいです。」
  As a reply to an invitation you are pleased to accept, the double negative sounds
  as if you are being dragged along. Save it for when you really do have a
  reservation.

- ❌ そんなに心配(しんぱい)し<b>ないことはない</b>よ。（intended: "there's no need to worry"） → ✅ そんなに心配(しんぱい)する<b>ことはない</b>よ。
  "No need to" takes the dictionary form plus <code>ことはない</code>, with no inner
  negative.

## Practice

**1.** 「この辞書(じしょ)、貸(か)してもらえますか。」「貸(か)せ____が、明日(あした)返(かえ)してくださいね。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

貸(か)せ<b>ないことはありません</b>が、明日(あした)返(かえ)してくださいね。

A conditional yes: lending it is possible, and the condition follows after `が`.
<code>貸(か)せないこともありません</code> would be a shade softer still.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 時間(じかん)はたっぷりあるから、そんなに急(いそ)ぐ（ことはない・ないことはない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そんなに急(いそ)ぐ<b>ことはない</b>。

With plenty of time in hand, the speaker is saying there is no need to hurry, and
that is the dictionary form plus <code>ことはない</code>. The double negative would
mean the opposite: that some hurry is required.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I can't say I don't understand your feelings, but rules are rules."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お気持(きも)ちが分(わ)から<b>ないことはありません</b>が、規則(きそく)は規則(きそく)です。

The double negative concedes sympathy without endorsing the request - exactly the
job this pattern does in polite refusals.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しいことはないが、時間(じかん)がかかる。（intended: "it's not that it's difficult"）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)し<b>くないことはない</b>が、時間(じかん)がかかる。

Better still: この問題(もんだい)は<b>難(むずか)しくない</b>が、時間(じかん)がかかる。
As originally written, <code>難(むずか)しいことはない</code> means "there is no need
for it to be hard", which is not what the speaker had in mind. い-adjectives take
the `くない` form inside the pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日曜日(にちようび)も働(はたら)け<b>ます</b>。
(b) 日曜日(にちようび)も働(はたら)け<b>ないことはありません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) offers Sunday freely. Your manager will write it in the rota and think no more
about it.

(b) says the same thing on paper and something quite different in the room: Sunday
is technically possible, you would rather it did not happen, and there is a
condition coming. A Japanese listener will wait for the `が` before answering.

</details>

# ～のでしょうか

**Meaning** · I wonder if ～; might I ask ～ - a question wrapped in two layers of softening

Take it apart and it explains itself. The `の` is the explanatory `の` you met in
`んです`: it asks about the *circumstances behind* something rather than the bare
fact. The `でしょう` is the tentative ending: it presents the question as a musing
rather than a demand. Put together, `のでしょうか` is the politest ordinary way to
ask about something, and it is what fills enquiry emails, service counters and the
rhetorical questions in speeches.

## Formation

The clause takes the same shape it takes before `んです`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のでしょうか | 届(とど)く → 届(とど)くのでしょうか |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のでしょうか | 止(と)まった → 止(と)まったのでしょうか |
| い-adjective | plain form + のでしょうか | 正(ただ)しい → 正(ただ)しいのでしょうか |
| な-adjective | ～な + のでしょうか | 必要(ひつよう)な → 必要(ひつよう)なのでしょうか |
| Noun | ～な + のでしょうか | 無料(むりょう) → 無料(むりょう)なのでしょうか |
| Spoken contraction | … + んでしょうか | 開(あ)く → 開(あ)くんでしょうか |
| Plain, written musing | … + のだろうか | 続(つづ)く → 続(つづ)くのだろうか |
| Casual, to yourself | … + のかな / のかしら | 来(く)る → 来(く)るのかな |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with no `の`, plain `でしょうか` attaches straight to a noun or a
ます-form and asks a simple tentative question:
<code>お名前(なまえ)は何(なん)でしょうか</code>. Adding `の` changes what you are
asking about - see the second contrast below.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この席(せき)、空(あ)いている<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Is this seat free, I wonder?
</pre>

<pre>
どうして今日(きょう)はこんなに人(ひと)が多(おお)い<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Why are there so many people here today?
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の説明会(せつめいかい)は何時(なんじ)に終(お)わる<b>のでしょうか</b>。
What time does tomorrow's briefing finish, may I ask?
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、この書類(しょるい)はこちらの窓口(まどぐち)に出(だ)せばいい<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Excuse me - is this the window I should hand these papers in at?
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)はどうしてあんなに急(きゅう)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)した<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Why on earth did he move away so suddenly?
</pre>

<pre>
お申込(もうしこ)みは来週(らいしゅう)まで受(う)け付(つ)けていただける<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Would you still be accepting applications up to next week?
</pre>

<pre>
なぜ人(ひと)は同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)を何度(なんど)も繰(く)り返(かえ)してしまう<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Why is it that people repeat the same mistakes over and over?
</pre>

<pre>
先週(せんしゅう)注文(ちゅうもん)した品物(しなもの)がまだ届(とど)かないのですが、いつごろ発送(はっそう)していただける<b>のでしょうか</b>。
The item I ordered last week still hasn't arrived - could I ask roughly when it will be dispatched?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`のでしょうか` is polite and slightly formal. It fits enquiry emails, counters,
customer calls, and any moment when you would rather not sound as though you were
interrogating someone. `んでしょうか` is the same thing in spoken shape: natural in
conversation, a little too casual for a written enquiry to a company you do not
know. `のだろうか` is the plain form and reads as thinking aloud - it is very common
in essays and in a narrator's inner voice, and rare as a real question to a person.

`のかな` is the casual, mostly self-directed version - 「間(ま)に合(あ)うのかな」
 - and can be aimed gently at a friend. `のかしら` does the same job with a softer
ending that leans feminine in most people's speech; that is a tendency, not a rule,
and it also sounds a touch old-fashioned to younger speakers.

The rhetorical use is worth noticing. In a presentation or an essay,
<code>なぜ～のでしょうか</code> asks a question nobody is expected to answer, and the
speaker answers it themselves in the next sentence. It is one of the standard ways
to open a paragraph in Japanese exposition.

Two cautions. First, the softness is real but it is not infinite politeness: for a
**request**, you still need a request form - <code>いただけませんか</code>,
<code>お願(ねが)いできますでしょうか</code> - because a question about circumstances
is not an ask. Second, business Japanese is fond of stacking
<code>ますでしょうか</code> (as in <code>いただけますでしょうか</code>). You will hear it
constantly; some style guides call it redundant. It is safe to recognise it and to
use the simpler <code>いただけないでしょうか</code> yourself.

## Contrast

### のでしょうか vs ですか・ますか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ますか` asks for a **fact**. `のでしょうか` asks about the
**situation behind** the fact, and takes the edge off the asking.

</div>

<pre>
この電車(でんしゃ)は東京(とうきょう)に止(と)まり<b>ますか</b>。
Does this train stop at Tokyo?
</pre>

<pre>
この電車(でんしゃ)は東京(とうきょう)に止(と)まらない<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Am I right that this train doesn't stop at Tokyo?
</pre>

The first is a clean request for information and is perfectly polite. The second
implies you have noticed something - the train shot past the platform, the board
says something odd - and are asking about *that*. Note the practical effect: with a
stranger, `のでしょうか` sounds a little more deferential, but a long string of them
can come across as roundabout. For a simple factual question, `ますか` is not rude.

### のでしょうか vs でしょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `の`: you are asking **which/whether**. With `の`: you are
asking **what lies behind it**. Why-questions almost always need the `の`.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)から<b>でしょうか</b>。
What time is the meeting, please?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)が変(か)わった<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Has the meeting time been changed, then?
</pre>

The first asks for a scheduled fact. The second asks about a change you suspect has
happened and want explained. This is also why ❌<code>どうして来(こ)なかったでしょうか</code>
sounds broken while <code>どうして来(こ)なかったのでしょうか</code> is fine: a "why"
question is by definition about circumstances, and circumstances are what `の` marks.

### のでしょうか vs のかな・のだろうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same question, three registers: `のでしょうか` polite and
addressed to someone, `のかな` casual and half to yourself, `のだろうか` plain and
written.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)、まだやっている<b>のかな</b>。
Is this place still open, I wonder.
</pre>

<pre>
こちらのお店(みせ)は何時(なんじ)まで営業(えいぎょう)している<b>のでしょうか</b>。
Until what time is your shop open, may I ask?
</pre>

<pre>
人間(にんげん)はなぜ物語(ものがたり)を必要(ひつよう)とする<b>のだろうか</b>。
Why is it that human beings need stories?
</pre>

The first would be muttered to a friend outside a dark window; the second said to
the person behind the counter; the third printed at the head of a chapter. Using the
casual one at the counter is not offensive, only childish-sounding; using the polite
one to yourself is merely odd.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 受付(うけつけ)は3階(がい)<b>のでしょうか</b>。 → ✅ 受付(うけつけ)は3階(がい)<b>なのでしょうか</b>。（or 3階(がい)でしょうか）
  A noun needs `な` before `のでしょうか`, exactly as it needs `な` before `んです`.

- ❌ どうして昨日(きのう)休(やす)んだ<b>でしょうか</b>。 → ✅ どうして昨日(きのう)休(やす)んだ<b>のでしょうか</b>。
  A why-question asks about circumstances, and that is precisely the job of `の`.
  Leaving it out makes the sentence sound unfinished.

- ❌ この荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)んでくださる<b>のでしょうか</b>。（intended as a request） → ✅ この荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)んで<b>いただけませんか</b>。
  Softening a question does not turn it into a request. As written, you are asking
  whether the person happens to be about to carry it.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、この件(けん)はどうする<b>んでしょうか</b>。（in a formal email） → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、この件(けん)はどのように進(すす)めればよろしい<b>でしょうか</b>。
  `んでしょうか` is the spoken contraction and reads as casual on the page; in
  writing, use `のでしょうか` or a plainly deferential request form.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この荷物(にもつ)はどこで受(う)け取(と)ればいい____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

どこで受(う)け取(と)ればいい<b>のでしょうか</b>。

You are asking about the arrangements rather than a bare fact, and you are asking a
stranger, so both the `の` and the tentative `でしょうか` are doing work.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 試験(しけん)の結果(けっか)は来週(らいしゅう)（でしょうか・なのでしょうか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

試験(しけん)の結果(けっか)は来週(らいしゅう)<b>でしょうか</b>。

A plain "is it next week?" needs no explanatory `の`. Adding it - 
<code>来週(らいしゅう)なのでしょうか</code> - is grammatical too, but it implies you
have heard something surprising and are checking the story behind it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I wonder why the shop closed down so suddenly."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの店(みせ)はどうしてあんなに急(きゅう)に閉(し)まってしまった<b>のでしょうか</b>。

The question is about the reason behind an event, which calls for `の`; `でしょうか`
keeps it as a wondering rather than a demand. To yourself you would say
<code>閉(し)まってしまったのかな</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この部屋(へや)は静(しず)かだのでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>なのでしょうか</b>。

<code>静(しず)かだ</code> is a な-adjective, and before `の` it takes `な`. The `だ`
form cannot sit in front of a nominalising `の`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)は来(き)ますか。
(b) 明日(あした)は来(こ)られる<b>のでしょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a straightforward question about the listener's plans, and there is nothing
impolite about it.

(b) steps back twice. The potential form asks whether coming is feasible rather than
whether they intend to, and `のでしょうか` asks about the circumstances, leaving them
room to say no without refusing anything. It is what you use when you suspect the
answer is no and would rather not force it.

</details>
