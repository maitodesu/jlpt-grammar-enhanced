# N3 - Talking about a topic, and who it's for


---

# ～について

**Meaning** · about; concerning - names the subject matter that the talking, writing, thinking or investigating is *about*

`について` marks the **topic of an activity**. Something is being said, asked,
written, researched or thought - and `について` says what it is about. It does not
say who it is aimed at, and it does not say whose point of view we are taking.
Those are jobs for other members of this same `に` family, and keeping them apart
is most of the work at N3.

## Formation

`について` is a compound: the particle `に` plus the て-form of <code>つく</code> ("attach
to"). It behaves as a unit and attaches to a bare noun - never to a verb or an
adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, before a verb | Noun + について + verb | 事故(じこ) → 事故(じこ)について話(はな)す |
| Noun, modifying another noun | Noun + についての + Noun | 環境(かんきょう) → 環境(かんきょう)についての記事(きじ) |
| Noun, as topic | Noun + については | 費用(ひよう) → 費用(ひよう)については後(あと)で |
| Noun, as addition | Noun + についても | 安全(あんぜん) → 安全(あんぜん)についても考(かんが)える |
| Noun, formal / business | Noun + につきまして（は） | 日程(にってい) → 日程(にってい)につきましては |
| Question word | 何(なに) / どこ など + について | 何(なに) → 何(なに)について書(か)きますか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `の` in <code>についての</code> is not optional. `について` is adverbial: it
can modify a verb on its own, but to hang it on a noun you have to convert it with
`の`, exactly as you would with <code>東京(とうきょう)での会議(かいぎ)</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
地震(じしん)<b>について</b>調(しら)べています。
I'm looking into earthquakes.
</pre>

<pre>
その件(けん)<b>について</b>質問(しつもん)があります。
I have a question about that matter.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)に進路(しんろ)<b>について</b>相談(そうだん)しました。
I talked to my teacher about which path to take after school.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の祭(まつ)り<b>についての</b>番組(ばんぐみ)を録画(ろくが)しておいた。
I recorded a programme about Japanese festivals.
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)の費用(ひよう)<b>については</b>、あとでまとめてメールで知(し)らせます。
As for the cost of the trip, I'll send you the whole thing by email later.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)<b>について</b>、社員(しゃいん)からいくつも意見(いけん)が出(で)ている。
Staff have raised a number of points about the new rules.
</pre>

<pre>
その画家(がか)<b>について</b>何(なに)も知(し)らないまま展覧会(てんらんかい)に行(い)っても、たぶん楽(たの)しめないだろう。
If I go to the exhibition knowing nothing about the painter, I probably won't enjoy it.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あす)の説明会(せつめいかい)では、来年度(らいねんど)の予算(よさん)<b>につきまして</b>ご説明(せつめい)いたします。
At tomorrow's briefing I will go over next year's budget.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`について` sits in the middle of the register scale, and that is why it is the one
to learn first. It is at home in a polite conversation, an email, a report, a
university seminar and a news broadcast alike. Nothing about it is stiff; nothing
about it is casual either.

Casual speech mostly does without it. Friends say <code>昨日(きのう)のこと</code>,
<code>あの人(ひと)のこと</code>, or just <code>～って</code>:

- あの映画(えいが)のこと、どう思(おも)った? - "What did you think about that film?"
- 試験(しけん)って、いつだっけ。 - "The exam - when was it again?"

Using について there is not wrong, but it lifts the sentence a register: it sounds
like you are opening an agenda item rather than chatting. Conversely, an email to
a client that says <code>納期(のうき)のことですが</code> where it could have said
<code>納期(のうき)につきましては</code> reads as slightly under-dressed.

The `につきまして` form is worth recognising even if you never produce it. It is
the same word with a humble-polite `ます` stem inserted, and it belongs to shop
announcements, customer service and business correspondence:
<code>本日(ほんじつ)の営業(えいぎょう)時間(じかん)につきましては…</code>. In ordinary
polite speech it would sound over-formal, almost parodic.

One structural habit to build: `について` likes to be followed by a verb of
**speaking, writing or finding out** - <code>話(はな)す</code>,
<code>書(か)く</code>, <code>説明(せつめい)する</code>,
<code>調(しら)べる</code>, <code>考(かんが)える</code>,
<code>議論(ぎろん)する</code>, <code>質問(しつもん)する</code>,
<code>発表(はっぴょう)する</code>. If the predicate is an emotion or an evaluation
instead, `について` is usually the wrong tool.

## Contrast

### ～について vs ～に関(かん)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register and different width.
`について` points at a **specific** topic and is fine in speech;
<code>に関(かん)して</code> is more formal, mostly written, and takes in the
**surrounding area** of a topic as well.

</div>

You can swap them in most sentences without producing anything ungrammatical. What
changes is how the sentence sounds, and how tightly the topic is drawn.

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)の読(よ)み方(かた)<b>について</b>先生(せんせい)に聞(き)いた。
I asked the teacher about how this kanji is read.
</pre>

<pre>
外国人(がいこくじん)の受(う)け入(い)れに<b>関(かん)して</b>、市(し)は新(あたら)しい方針(ほうしん)を示(しめ)した。
The city has set out a new policy on accepting foreign residents.
</pre>

The first is one narrow question, asked out loud - `について` fits. The second
covers a whole policy area, in writing, in a public register - <code>に関(かん)して</code>
fits. Reverse them and neither is wrong, but the first would sound like a
committee paper and the second like small talk.

### ～について vs ～に対(たい)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `について` is the **topic**; <code>に対(たい)して</code> is the
**target**. "Talk about the customer" is `について`; "be polite to the customer" is
<code>に対(たい)して</code>.

</div>

This is the pair that costs learners the most marks, because English "about" and
"toward" both come out as "regarding" in a dictionary.

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は新人(しんじん)<b>について</b>何(なに)か言(い)っていましたか。
Did the manager say anything about the new hire?
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は新人(しんじん)に<b>対(たい)して</b>厳(きび)しすぎると思(おも)う。
I think the manager is too hard on the new hire.
</pre>

In the first, the new hire is the subject matter of some remarks. In the second,
the new hire is the person the manager's behaviour is aimed at. A useful test: if
you can replace the phrase with "on the subject of", it is `について`. If you can
replace it with "at / to / against", it is <code>に対(たい)して</code>.

### ～について vs ～のこと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のこと</code> is the everyday equivalent and, unlike
`について`, it can be the **object of a feeling**. Verbs and adjectives of emotion
take <code>のこと</code> (or a plain particle), never `について`.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)のこと<b>を</b>心配(しんぱい)しています。
I'm worried about my little brother.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)<b>について</b>心配(しんぱい)な点(てん)を先生(せんせい)に伝(つた)えました。
I told the teacher the points about my brother that concern me.
</pre>

`について` needs the sentence to be *presenting information on a topic*. In the
first sentence there is no presenting going on - there is only the feeling, and the
brother is its object. The second sentence adds an act of communication, and
`について` becomes available again.

The same trap catches <code>好(す)き</code>, <code>嫌(きら)い</code>,
<code>怒(おこ)る</code> and <code>覚(おぼ)えている</code>. ❌<code>この歌手(かしゅ)について好(す)きです</code>
is simply not Japanese; you want <code>この歌手(かしゅ)が好(す)きです</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 事故(じこ)について記事(きじ)を読(よ)んだ。（intending "an article about the accident"） → ✅ 事故(じこ)<b>についての</b>記事(きじ)を読(よ)んだ。
  Without `の`, `について` modifies the verb <code>読(よ)んだ</code>, which gives "I
  read an article, about the accident" - a different and odd sentence. To attach it
  to a noun you need <code>についての</code>.

- ❌ アニメについて好(す)きです。 → ✅ アニメ<b>が</b>好(す)きです。
  `について` introduces the subject matter of an act of communication or thought.
  A feeling takes its object with `が` or `を`, not with `について`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)についてこの仕事(しごと)は大切(たいせつ)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>にとって</b>この仕事(しごと)は大切(たいせつ)です。
  "For me, this job is important" is a standpoint, not a topic. Standpoints take
  `にとって`.

- ❌ 客(きゃく)について失礼(しつれい)な態度(たいど)を取(と)った。 → ✅ 客(きゃく)に<b>対(たい)して</b>失礼(しつれい)な態度(たいど)を取(と)った。
  An attitude is directed *at* somebody. That is <code>に対(たい)して</code>; `について`
  would mean the rudeness was somehow on the topic of the customer.

## Practice

**1.** 来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)では、工場(こうじょう)の移転(いてん)____話(はな)し合(あ)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

工場(こうじょう)の移転(いてん)<b>について</b>話(はな)し合(あ)います。

The factory move is the subject matter of the discussion, and
<code>話(はな)し合(あ)う</code> is exactly the kind of communication verb `について`
was built for. <code>に関(かん)して</code> would also be grammatical, just a notch
more formal than a normal meeting warrants.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 店長(てんちょう)はアルバイトの学生(がくせい)（について・に対(たい)して）とても親切(しんせつ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

店長(てんちょう)はアルバイトの学生(がくせい)<b>に対(たい)して</b>とても親切(しんせつ)だ。

<code>親切(しんせつ)だ</code> describes how the manager behaves **toward** somebody,
so the students are the target of an attitude, not a topic of conversation. Say
`について` and the sentence would try to mean that he is kind on the subject of
part-timers, which is nonsense.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'd like to hear your opinion about the new plan."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しい計画(けいかく)<b>について</b>ご意見(いけん)を伺(うかが)いたいのですが。

<code>ご意見(いけん)</code> is information being given on a topic, so `について` is the
natural choice. In a written request you could raise the register with
<code>新(あたら)しい計画(けいかく)につきましては</code>, and in casual speech you would
drop the whole thing: <code>新(あたら)しい計画(けいかく)、どう思(おも)う?</code>

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この問題(もんだい)についてのレポートを来週(らいしゅう)までに出(だ)してください。それから、締(し)め切(き)りについて質問(しつもん)は受(う)け付(つ)けません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ …締(し)め切(き)り<b>についての</b>質問(しつもん)は受(う)け付(つ)けません。

The first `について` is correct because it already carries `の` before the noun
<code>レポート</code>. The second one sits directly in front of the noun
<code>質問(しつもん)</code> and needs the same `の`. This is the single most common
slip with this pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 田中(たなか)さん<b>について</b>話(はな)しました。
(b) 田中(たなか)さん<b>と</b>話(はな)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) makes 田中(たなか)さん the **subject matter**: somebody discussed him, and he
may well not have been in the room. Depending on the context it can sound slightly
ominous, in the same way English "we talked about you" does.

(b) makes him the **conversation partner** - you and he talked, about anything at
all. `と` marks the person you do something jointly with; `について` marks what the
doing is on the topic of. The two can even be stacked:
<code>山田(やまだ)さんと田中(たなか)さんについて話(はな)しました</code> - "I talked with
Yamada about Tanaka."

</details>

# ～に関する / ～に関して

**Meaning** · concerning; relating to; in connection with - the formal, largely written cousin of について（かんする・かんして）

<code>に関(かん)する</code> and <code>に関(かん)して</code> mark the subject matter of a
statement, a document, a rule or a piece of research. They mean what `について`
means, but they belong to a different register - reports, notices, contracts,
academic writing, the news - and they draw the topic **wider**: not just the thing
named, but everything connected to it.

The two forms are one word doing two jobs. <code>関(かん)する</code> is the dictionary
form of a verb, so it modifies **nouns**. <code>関(かん)して</code> is its て-form, so it
modifies **verbs**.

## Formation

`に` + <code>関(かん)する</code> ("to be connected to"). The noun in front takes `に`,
never `を`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + に関(かん)する + Noun | 安全(あんぜん) → 安全(あんぜん)に関(かん)する規則(きそく) |
| Noun, modifying a verb | Noun + に関(かん)して + verb | 費用(ひよう) → 費用(ひよう)に関(かん)して話(はな)す |
| Noun, as topic | Noun + に関(かん)しては | その点(てん) → その点(てん)に関(かん)しては |
| Noun, as addition | Noun + に関(かん)しても | 人数(にんずう) → 人数(にんずう)に関(かん)しても |
| Noun, business / keigo | Noun + に関(かん)しまして（は） | 納期(のうき) → 納期(のうき)に関(かん)しましては |
| Noun, modifying a noun (rarer) | Noun + に関(かん)しての + Noun | 事故(じこ) → 事故(じこ)に関(かん)しての報告(ほうこく) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>関(かん)する</code> before a noun, <code>関(かん)して</code>
before a verb. <code>関(かん)するの</code> does not exist: the `の` you are reaching for
is already built into the verb form.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは安全(あんぜん)に<b>関(かん)する</b>規則(きそく)です。
These are the safety rules.
</pre>

<pre>
その点(てん)に<b>関(かん)して</b>は、後(のち)ほどお答(こた)えします。
I'll answer that point later.
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)に<b>関(かん)する</b>本(ほん)を何冊(なんさつ)も買(か)ってしまった。
I've ended up buying any number of books on health.
</pre>

<pre>
料金(りょうきん)に<b>関(かん)して</b>ご質問(しつもん)のある方(かた)は、受付(うけつけ)までお越(こ)しください。
If you have questions concerning the fees, please come to the reception desk.
</pre>

<pre>
気候(きこう)の変化(へんか)に<b>関(かん)する</b>研究(けんきゅう)が世界中(せかいじゅう)で進(すす)められている。
Research relating to climate change is being carried forward all over the world.
</pre>

<pre>
この事件(じけん)に<b>関(かん)して</b>、警察(けいさつ)はまだ何(なに)も発表(はっぴょう)していない。
The police have released nothing in connection with this incident.
</pre>

<pre>
給与(きゅうよ)に<b>関(かん)して</b>は不満(ふまん)はないが、残業(ざんぎょう)の多(おお)さに<b>関(かん)して</b>は会社(かいしゃ)に考(かんが)え直(なお)してほしい。
I have no complaints where pay is concerned, but on the amount of overtime I'd like the company to think again.
</pre>

<pre>
個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)の扱(あつか)いに<b>関(かん)しまして</b>は、当社(とうしゃ)のホームページをご覧(らん)ください。
Regarding the handling of personal data, please see our website.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** pattern first and a formal-spoken pattern second. Its natural
homes are notices, headings, minutes, contracts, textbooks, news copy and the
polished parts of a presentation. If you say it to a friend over coffee you will
sound like you are reading from a slide.

A useful gauge: the more institutional the speaker, the more likely
<code>に関(かん)して</code> is. A company says
<code>停電(ていでん)に関(かん)してお知(し)らせします</code>; a housemate says
<code>停電(ていでん)のことなんだけど</code>. Both are correct in their own place, and
swapping them is the mistake.

<code>に関(かん)しまして</code> raises it one more step, into customer-facing business
Japanese. It is extremely common in announcements and service email, and you should
expect to hear it far more often than you produce it.

There is also a **width** difference that survives the register difference.
`について` picks out a topic; <code>に関(かん)して</code> draws a circle around it.
<code>台風(たいふう)に関(かん)する情報(じょうほう)</code> covers the typhoon, the
train stoppages, the school closures and the shop opening hours - everything
connected to it. <code>台風(たいふう)についての情報(じょうほう)</code> is more likely
to be information about the typhoon itself. This is a tendency rather than a hard
line, but it is why headings and document titles overwhelmingly prefer
<code>に関(かん)する</code>.

Finally, do not confuse the phrase with the noun <code>関係(かんけい)</code>.
<code>関係(かんけい)がある</code> claims a real-world connection between two things;
<code>に関(かん)する</code> merely announces a topic. <code>この問題(もんだい)に関(かん)する資料(しりょう)</code>
is "material on this problem"; <code>この問題(もんだい)と関係(かんけい)がある資料(しりょう)</code>
is "material that turns out to be connected to this problem."

## Contrast

### ～に関(かん)して vs ～について

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different clothes. `について` is the everyday
form and works in speech; <code>に関(かん)して</code> is formal, mostly written, and
covers a broader area around the topic.

</div>

Nearly every sentence accepts both. Choose by asking who is speaking and where.

<pre>
夏休(なつやす)みの予定(よてい)<b>について</b>家族(かぞく)で話(はな)した。
The family talked about plans for the summer holiday.
</pre>

<pre>
市(し)の予算(よさん)に<b>関(かん)して</b>、議会(ぎかい)で激(はげ)しい議論(ぎろん)があった。
There was a fierce debate in the assembly concerning the city budget.
</pre>

Put <code>に関(かん)して</code> in the first sentence and the family sounds like a
board of directors. Put `について` in the second and nothing breaks - it is simply
one notch less formal than assembly minutes would be.

### ～に関(かん)する + Noun vs ～についての + Noun

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both attach a topic to a noun, but the shapes differ:
<code>に関(かん)する</code> takes **no** `の`, <code>について</code> takes an obligatory one.
Titles, headings and formal documents prefer <code>に関(かん)する</code>.

</div>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)の住(す)まいに<b>関(かん)する</b>アンケート
A survey concerning housing for international students
</pre>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)の住(す)まい<b>についての</b>アンケート
A survey about housing for international students
</pre>

The second is perfectly good Japanese and is what you would say out loud. The first
is what would be printed at the top of the form. Note the ❌<code>に関(かん)するの</code>
error is impossible to make with `について`, and the ❌<code>について質問(しつもん)</code>
error (dropping `の`) is impossible to make with <code>に関(かん)する</code> - the two
patterns fail in opposite directions.

### ～に関(かん)して vs ～において

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に関(かん)して</code> gives the **topic**; `において` gives
the **setting** - the place, the era, the field or the situation in which something
holds.

</div>

Both are formal, both translate as "in" or "regarding" at some point in a
dictionary, and both appear in the same kind of document, so they blur together.

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)対策(たいさく)に<b>関(かん)して</b>、新(あたら)しい案(あん)が出(だ)された。
A new proposal has been put forward concerning safety measures.
</pre>

<pre>
建設(けんせつ)現場(げんば)<b>において</b>、安全(あんぜん)対策(たいさく)は何(なに)よりも優先(ゆうせん)される。
On construction sites, safety measures take priority over everything.
</pre>

In the first, safety measures are what the proposal is *about*. In the second, the
building site is *where* the rule applies. If the noun could be preceded by "on the
subject of", it is <code>に関(かん)して</code>; if it could be preceded by "in" or "at",
it is `において`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 環境(かんきょう)に関(かん)する<b>の</b>問題(もんだい) → ✅ 環境(かんきょう)に<b>関(かん)する</b>問題(もんだい)
  <code>関(かん)する</code> is already a verb in its noun-modifying form, so it attaches
  to the noun directly. The `の` belongs to <code>についての</code>, not here.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)に関(かん)して報告書(ほうこくしょ)を見(み)せてください。（intending "the report on the accident"） → ✅ 事故(じこ)に<b>関(かん)する</b>報告書(ほうこくしょ)を見(み)せてください。
  Before a noun you need <code>関(かん)する</code>. <code>関(かん)して</code> would
  attach the topic to <code>見(み)せる</code> instead - "show me a report, on the
  subject of the accident."

- ❌ ゆうべの飲(の)み会(かい)に関(かん)して話(はな)そうよ。 → ✅ ゆうべの飲(の)み会(かい)<b>のこと</b>話(はな)そうよ。
  Not a grammar error - a register error. Casual invitations do not take formal
  written vocabulary; the sentence sounds like a parody of a manager.

- ❌ 私(わたし)に関(かん)して、この決定(けってい)は残念(ざんねん)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>にとって</b>、この決定(けってい)は残念(ざんねん)です。
  "For me" as a standpoint is `にとって`. <code>に関(かん)して</code> would make *you*
  the subject matter of the decision.

## Practice

**1.** 会社(かいしゃ)は社員(しゃいん)の健康(けんこう)に____調査(ちょうさ)を行(おこな)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

健康(けんこう)に<b>関(かん)する</b>調査(ちょうさ)を行(おこな)った。

The topic is being hung on the noun <code>調査(ちょうさ)</code>, so you need the
noun-modifying form <code>関(かん)する</code> - with no `の` after it.
<code>健康(けんこう)についての調査(ちょうさ)</code> would also work and would sound
slightly less official.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会議(かいぎ)の場所(ばしょ)（に関(かん)して・において）は、また連絡(れんらく)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会議(かいぎ)の場所(ばしょ)<b>に関(かん)して</b>は、また連絡(れんらく)します。

The venue is the **subject matter** of the message you will send, not the place
where the sending happens. `において` would have to mean "I will contact you at the
meeting venue", which is not what the sentence is doing.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a company notice: "We are currently investigating the cause of the problem. We will inform you separately regarding compensation."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

現在(げんざい)、問題(もんだい)の原因(げんいん)を調査(ちょうさ)しております。補償(ほしょう)に<b>関(かん)しまして</b>は、別(べつ)にご連絡(れんらく)いたします。

A public notice is exactly where <code>に関(かん)しまして</code> belongs, and the `は`
after it marks compensation as a separate item being set aside for later.
<code>補償(ほしょう)については</code> would be acceptable but slightly plainer than a
formal apology notice usually goes.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)に関(かん)してのついて説明(せつめい)は、来週(らいしゅう)行(おこな)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)に<b>関(かん)する</b>説明(せつめい)は、来週(らいしゅう)行(おこな)います。

Two patterns have been welded together. Pick one:
<code>制度(せいど)に関(かん)する説明(せつめい)</code> or
<code>制度(せいど)についての説明(せつめい)</code>. Both mean "the explanation of the
new system"; the first is the one you would print on an agenda.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 地震(じしん)に<b>関(かん)する</b>情報(じょうほう)
(b) 地震(じしん)<b>についての</b>情報(じょうほう)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the phrase a broadcaster or a city website would use, and it takes in
everything connected to the earthquake - the tremor itself, the damage, the trains,
the evacuation centres. The topic is drawn as a **field**.

(b) is what you would say in conversation, and it points more narrowly at
information **about the earthquake itself**: how big it was, where it struck. The
difference is a tendency rather than a rule, but the register difference is real
and reliable: (a) is written and institutional, (b) is spoken and ordinary.

</details>

# ～において / ～における

**Meaning** · in; at; on - the formal written stand-in for で, naming the place, time, situation or field in which something holds（において・における）

`において` sets the **stage**. It tells you where something happens, when it
happens, or within what field or respect a statement is true. Everything it does,
the particle `で` also does - but `で` is the everyday word and `において` is the
one that appears in announcements, papers, contracts and ceremonies.

`における` is the same word in noun-modifying dress: <code>会場(かいじょう)において行(おこな)う</code>
("carry out at the venue") but <code>会場(かいじょう)における注意(ちゅうい)事項(じこう)</code>
("points to note at the venue").

## Formation

Attaches to a bare noun. The `に` is part of the pattern and is not replaceable.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (place), modifying a verb | Noun + において | 本社(ほんしゃ) → 本社(ほんしゃ)において行(おこな)う |
| Noun (time or era) | Noun + において | 明治(めいじ)時代(じだい) → 明治(めいじ)時代(じだい)において |
| Noun (field or respect) | Noun + において | 技術(ぎじゅつ)面(めん) → 技術(ぎじゅつ)面(めん)において |
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + における + Noun | 職場(しょくば) → 職場(しょくば)における関係(かんけい) |
| Noun, as topic | Noun + においては | 日本(にほん) → 日本(にほん)においては |
| Noun, as addition | Noun + においても | 海外(かいがい) → 海外(かいがい)においても |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>においての</code> exists but is uncommon and slightly clumsy. Before a
noun, reach for `における`. Before a verb or an adjective, `において`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は本社(ほんしゃ)<b>において</b>行(おこな)われます。
The meeting will be held at head office.
</pre>

<pre>
この点(てん)<b>において</b>、両者(りょうしゃ)の意見(いけん)は一致(いっち)している。
On this point, the two sides are in agreement.
</pre>

<pre>
大会(たいかい)は来月(らいげつ)七日(なのか)、市民(しみん)ホール<b>において</b>開催(かいさい)いたします。
The tournament will be held on the seventh of next month at the civic hall.
</pre>

<pre>
江戸(えど)時代(じだい)<b>における</b>町人(ちょうにん)の暮(く)らしを調(しら)べています。
I'm researching the lives of townspeople in the Edo period.
</pre>

<pre>
医療(いりょう)の分野(ぶんや)<b>における</b>AIの利用(りよう)が急速(きゅうそく)に広(ひろ)がっている。
The use of AI in the field of medicine is spreading rapidly.
</pre>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)<b>においては</b>、話(はな)す内容(ないよう)だけでなく話(はな)し方(かた)も見(み)られている。
In an interview it is not only what you say but how you say it that is being judged.
</pre>

<pre>
我(わ)が国(くに)<b>においても</b>、人口(じんこう)の減少(げんしょう)は深刻(しんこく)な問題(もんだい)となりつつある。
In our country too, the falling population is becoming a serious problem.
</pre>

<pre>
価格(かかく)<b>においては</b>他社(たしゃ)に負(ま)けているが、品質(ひんしつ)<b>においては</b>自信(じしん)がある。
We lose to our competitors on price, but where quality is concerned we are confident.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This pattern is **formal and written**, with essentially no casual life at all.
Notices, minutes, academic papers, legal text, ceremonial speech and the polished
register of the news are its habitat. Saying
❌<code>コンビニにおいてパンを買(か)った</code> is not a grammar error - it is a
tonal disaster, roughly like writing "I did procure bread at the convenience store."

Two practical consequences.

First, **`において` covers only some of what `で` covers**. `で` marks place, but
also means ("with a knife"), cause ("because of a cold"), material, and limit
("within a week"). `において` inherits only the **place, time, situation and field**
senses. There is no ❌<code>ナイフにおいて切(き)る</code>.

Second, the "field or respect" sense is where `において` earns its keep, because
`で` there can be ambiguous. <code>この点(てん)において</code> - "in this respect" - 
is crisper in formal prose than <code>この点(てん)で</code>, which could momentarily
be read as a location.

`において` also carries a faint ceremonial weight when used of an event. A wedding
invitation says <code>ホテルの大(だい)ホールにおいて</code>; a text to a friend says
<code>ホテルで</code>. The formality is doing real work in the first case - it signals
that this is an occasion.

One last thing worth noticing: `における` is a fixture of **titles**.
<code>現代(げんだい)社会(しゃかい)における家族(かぞく)の役割(やくわり)</code> is exactly
the shape of an essay title, a chapter heading, a conference paper. If you are
writing academic Japanese, this is a phrase you will use constantly.

## Contrast

### ～において vs ～で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register, narrower coverage. `において`
can replace the **place / time / situation / field** uses of `で` and nothing else,
and only in formal writing or formal speech.

</div>

<pre>
試合(しあい)は大阪(おおさか)<b>で</b>行(おこな)われた。
The match was held in Osaka.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)は大阪(おおさか)<b>において</b>行(おこな)われた。
The match was held in Osaka. (announcement register)
</pre>

Identical in meaning; the second belongs in a written report. Now watch it fail:

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が事故(じこ)<b>で</b>止(と)まっている。
The trains are stopped because of an accident.
</pre>

❌<code>事故(じこ)において止(と)まっている</code> is ungrammatical, because this
`で` marks a **cause**, and `において` has no cause sense. The same goes for
<code>バスで行(い)く</code> (means) and <code>木(き)で作(つく)る</code>
(material) - neither converts.

### ～における + Noun vs ～の + Noun

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` says the two nouns are connected somehow;
`における` says specifically **"within / inside / in the context of"**. When the
relationship needs pinning down, `における` does it.

</div>

<pre>
日本(にほん)<b>の</b>教育(きょういく)
Japanese education
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)<b>における</b>外国語(がいこくご)教育(きょういく)
foreign-language education in Japan
</pre>

The first could mean education that is Japanese in style, education owned by Japan,
education about Japan - `の` does not say. The second is unambiguous: it is
education happening **inside** Japan, seen as a setting. This precision is why
academic titles reach for `における` rather than `の`, and why replacing it with `の`
in an essay title makes the title vaguer even though it stays grammatical.

Between the two sits `での`, which is the neutral-register version of the same idea:
<code>日本(にほん)での生活(せいかつ)</code>, "life in Japan". Use `での` when you
are speaking, `における` when you are writing formally.

### ～において vs ～について

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `において` is the **setting**; `について` is the **subject
matter**. "Discussed at the meeting" is `において`; "discussed about the meeting" is
`について`.

</div>

Both are formal-ish, both hang off `に`, and English "regarding" is offered as a
translation of both, which is how learners end up swapping them.

<pre>
その問題(もんだい)は先日(せんじつ)の会議(かいぎ)<b>において</b>議論(ぎろん)された。
That issue was debated at the meeting the other day.
</pre>

<pre>
先日(せんじつ)の会議(かいぎ)<b>について</b>報告書(ほうこくしょ)を書(か)いた。
I wrote a report about the meeting the other day.
</pre>

In the first, the meeting is the setting the debating happened in. In the second, the
meeting is what the report is on. Substituting one for the other produces a
sentence that is grammatical and simply says something else - which is the most
dangerous kind of mistake.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ はさみにおいて紙(かみ)を切(き)った。 → ✅ はさみ<b>で</b>紙(かみ)を切(き)った。
  `において` inherits only the place, time, situation and field senses of `で`. It
  never marks an instrument.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)において学校(がっこう)が休(やす)みになった。 → ✅ 台風(たいふう)<b>で</b>学校(がっこう)が休(やす)みになった。
  This `で` is a cause, and there is no cause sense in `において`. If you want the
  formal register, use <code>台風(たいふう)のため</code>.

- ❌ 職場(しょくば)において問題(もんだい)が増(ふ)えている。（intending "problems in the workplace") → ✅ 職場(しょくば)<b>における</b>問題(もんだい)が増(ふ)えている。
  Before a noun you need `における`. As written, the sentence says the increasing is
  happening at the workplace, which is not quite the same claim.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、駅前(えきまえ)のカフェにおいて友(とも)だちに会(あ)った。 → ✅ 駅前(えきまえ)のカフェ<b>で</b>友(とも)だちに会(あ)った。
  Grammatically fine, tonally absurd. A private, everyday event does not take
  announcement vocabulary.

**1.** 20世紀(せいき)____、人々(ひとびと)の暮(く)らしは大(おお)きく変(か)わった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

20世紀(せいき)<b>において</b>、人々(ひとびと)の暮(く)らしは大(おお)きく変(か)わった。

A century is a **time setting**, which is one of the four senses `において` inherits
from `で`. The sentence is written prose, so the formal form fits;
<code>20世紀(せいき)に</code> would also be possible and plainer.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この会社(かいしゃ)は技術(ぎじゅつ)（において・について）他社(たしゃ)より進(すす)んでいる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この会社(かいしゃ)は技術(ぎじゅつ)<b>において</b>他社(たしゃ)より進(すす)んでいる。

The sentence names the **respect** in which the company is ahead, which is the
field sense of `において`. `について` would make technology the subject matter of
some statement, leaving <code>進(すす)んでいる</code> with nothing to be true *of*.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as an essay title: "The role of the family in modern society."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

現代(げんだい)社会(しゃかい)<b>における</b>家族(かぞく)の役割(やくわり)

Before a noun, `において` becomes `における`. This is the standard shape of an
academic title in Japanese. <code>現代(げんだい)社会(しゃかい)での家族(かぞく)の役割(やくわり)</code>
means the same but is too plain for a title, and
<code>現代(げんだい)社会(しゃかい)の家族(かぞく)の役割(やくわり)</code> stacks two `の`
and blurs the relationship.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 大雨(おおあめ)において試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になり、来週(らいしゅう)、同(おな)じ球場(きゅうじょう)において行(おこな)われることになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 大雨(おおあめ)<b>のため</b>試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になり、来週(らいしゅう)、同(おな)じ球場(きゅうじょう)において行(おこな)われることになった。

The second `において` is correct - a stadium is a place. The first is not: heavy
rain is the **cause** of the cancellation, and `において` has no cause sense. In this
formal register <code>のため</code> is the natural repair; in speech you would say
<code>大雨(おおあめ)で</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会場(かいじょう)<b>で</b>のアンケート
(b) 会場(かいじょう)<b>における</b>アンケート

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both mean "a survey at the venue", and neither is wrong. The difference is
register and, faintly, framing.

(a) is ordinary Japanese - spoken, or written in an email to colleagues. `での` is
just `で` fitted to modify a noun.

(b) is document Japanese. It is what would be printed on the cover of the report,
and it frames the venue as a **defined setting** whose conditions matter, rather
than merely as the spot where the clipboards were handed out. Choosing (b) in
conversation would sound as though you were quoting the report out loud.

</details>

# ～に対して

**Meaning** · toward; at; in response to - and, from a clause, "whereas"（にたいして）

<code>に対(たい)して</code> points. It names the **person or thing an action, an
attitude or a reaction is aimed at**: who you are polite to, what you are objecting
to, what a reply is a reply to. Say "about" in your head and you will reach for it
wrongly - this is not the topic of the sentence, it is the target.

A second use falls out of the same image. If you set one thing opposite another,
you get contrast: <code>Aのに対(たい)して、B</code> - "A, whereas B". And a third,
narrower use gives ratios: "per one participant, two bottles."

## Formation

<code>対(たい)する</code> is a verb ("face, confront"), so it inflects: the て-form
modifies verbs and adjectives, the dictionary form modifies nouns.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (target) | Noun + に対(たい)して | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもに対(たい)してやさしい |
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + に対(たい)する + Noun | 客(きゃく) → 客(きゃく)に対(たい)する態度(たいど) |
| Verb / い-adjective (contrast) | Plain form + のに対(たい)して | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いのに対(たい)して |
| な-adjective (contrast) | ～な + のに対(たい)して | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かなのに対(たい)して |
| Noun (contrast) | Noun + なのに / であるのに + 対(たい)して | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)なのに対(たい)して |
| Quantity (ratio) | Number + に対(たい)して + Number | 一人(ひとり) → 一人(ひとり)に対(たい)して二枚(にまい) |
| Written / curt variant | Noun + に対(たい)し | 会社(かいしゃ) → 会社(かいしゃ)に対(たい)し求(もと)める |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the contrastive use needs `の` before it. A clause cannot attach
directly: ❌<code>高(たか)いに対(たい)して</code>. The `の` turns the clause into
something noun-like, which is what <code>に対(たい)して</code> requires.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>に対(たい)して</b>やさしい人(ひと)です。
He's a person who is kind to children.
</pre>

<pre>
その質問(しつもん)<b>に対(たい)して</b>、彼(かれ)は何(なに)も答(こた)えなかった。
He gave no answer to that question.
</pre>

<pre>
目上(めうえ)の人(ひと)<b>に対(たい)して</b>そんな言(い)い方(かた)をしてはいけない。
You mustn't speak that way to someone senior to you.
</pre>

<pre>
市(し)の決定(けってい)<b>に対(たい)して</b>、住民(じゅうみん)が強(つよ)く反発(はんぱつ)している。
Residents are pushing back hard against the city's decision.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)一人(ひとり)<b>に対(たい)して</b>、飲(の)み物(もの)を二本(にほん)ずつ用意(ようい)した。
We laid on two drinks per participant.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)が外(そと)で体(からだ)を動(うご)かすのが好(す)きな<b>のに対(たい)して</b>、妹(いもうと)は一日中(いちにちじゅう)本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいる。
Where my older sister loves being outside and moving about, my younger sister reads all day.
</pre>

<pre>
お客様(きゃくさま)<b>に対(たい)する</b>言葉遣(ことばづか)いについては、入社(にゅうしゃ)したばかりのころに厳(きび)しく注意(ちゅうい)された。
I got told off sharply about how I spoke to customers when I had only just joined.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の売上(うりあげ)がまったく伸(の)びなかった<b>のに対(たい)して</b>、今年(ことし)は春(はる)からずっと好調(こうちょう)だ。
Whereas last year's sales didn't grow at all, this year has been strong right through from spring.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The target use leans **formal**, but not so far that it is out of place in ordinary
polite conversation. <code>部下(ぶか)に対(たい)して厳(きび)しい</code> is
something a colleague might say out loud. What marks it as slightly weighty is that
plain `に` would usually do the job:
<code>子(こ)どもにやさしい</code> and <code>子(こ)どもに対(たい)してやさしい</code>
mean the same thing, and the second is more deliberate - it puts the relationship
itself under a spotlight. In relaxed speech you would normally drop it.

Where <code>に対(たい)して</code> is not optional is when the noun is the target of
a **reaction**: a protest, a reply, a complaint, a demand, an evaluation.
<code>説明(せつめい)に対(たい)して質問(しつもん)が出(で)た</code> - 
the questions came *back at* the explanation. Bare `に` there is thinner and
sometimes ambiguous.

The contrastive use is squarely **written**. It belongs in comparisons, reports and
essays, and it has a cool, analytical tone: the writer is laying two facts side by
side, not complaining. That distinguishes it from `のに`, which contrasts *and*
carries dissatisfaction.

<code>に対(たい)し</code>, without `て`, is the clipped written form. It is standard
in newspapers and official documents - 
<code>組合(くみあい)は会社(かいしゃ)に対(たい)し回答(かいとう)を求(もと)めた</code> - 
and sounds stiff if spoken aloud outside a formal announcement.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the ratio use is common in explanations of quantity and in statistics:
<code>人口(じんこう)千人(せんにん)に対(たい)して医師(いし)が三人(さんにん)</code>.
It is the one sense where the noun in front is a **number**, so it is easy to spot
and easy to keep separate from the other two.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～に対(たい)して vs ～について

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に対(たい)して</code> is the **target** of an action or
attitude; `について` is the **subject matter** of a statement. Aim vs topic.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)<b>について</b>社員(しゃいん)に説明(せつめい)した。
I explained the new system to the staff.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)<b>に対(たい)して</b>社員(しゃいん)から不満(ふまん)が出(で)た。
Complaints came from the staff against the new system.
</pre>

In the first the system is what the explanation is *about*. In the second it is
what the complaints are aimed *at*. Note that the two sentences have different
verbs, and that is the reliable clue: verbs of **saying, writing and investigating**
take `について`, while verbs of **reacting** - <code>反対(はんたい)する</code>,
<code>抗議(こうぎ)する</code>, <code>答(こた)える</code>,
<code>文句(もんく)を言(い)う</code> - take <code>に対(たい)して</code>.

### ～に対(たい)して vs ～にとって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に対(たい)して</code> looks **outward from the subject
toward** the noun; `にとって` looks **outward from the noun** at the world. Target vs
standpoint.

</div>

<pre>
親(おや)<b>に対(たい)して</b>あんな口(くち)をきくべきではない。
You shouldn't talk to your parents like that.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)<b>にとって</b>、子(こ)どもの健康(けんこう)が一番(いちばん)の心配(しんぱい)だ。
For parents, their children's health is the biggest worry.
</pre>

In the first, the parents are on the receiving end of the speaking. In the second,
nothing is aimed at them at all - we have simply climbed inside their heads to judge
what matters. A quick test: `にとって` is nearly always followed by an **evaluation**
(<code>大切(たいせつ)だ</code>, <code>難(むずか)しい</code>,
<code>ありがたい</code>), whereas <code>に対(たい)して</code> is followed by an
**action or a stance**.

### ～のに対(たい)して vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both put two clauses against each other, but
<code>のに対(たい)して</code> is a **neutral comparison** and `のに` is a
**complaint**. If you are annoyed, you want `のに`.

</div>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)の冬(ふゆ)は晴(は)れの日(ひ)が多(おお)い<b>のに対(たい)して</b>、日本海側(にほんかいがわ)は雪(ゆき)の日(ひ)が続(つづ)く。
Winter in Tokyo has many clear days, whereas on the Japan Sea side the snow goes on and on.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)した<b>のに</b>、彼(かれ)は来(こ)なかった。
He promised, and yet he didn't come.
</pre>

The first sentence has no feeling in it: two regions, two climates, stated side by
side. The second is a grievance. There is also a structural difference - 
<code>のに対(たい)して</code> compares two things **of the same kind** along the
same axis (winters with winters, sales with sales), while `のに` simply says the
second clause is unexpected given the first.

For the same neutral comparison you will also meet
<code>一方(いっぽう)（で）</code>, which does much the same work and joins sentences
rather than clauses.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)に対(たい)して話(はな)し合(あ)いましょう。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)<b>について</b>話(はな)し合(あ)いましょう。
  <code>話(はな)し合(あ)う</code> takes a topic, not a target. Use
  <code>に対(たい)して</code> only when something is being aimed at the noun.

- ❌ 私(わたし)に対(たい)して、この写真(しゃしん)は宝物(たからもの)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>にとって</b>、この写真(しゃしん)は宝物(たからもの)です。
  "For me" meaning "from my point of view" is `にとって`.
  <code>私(わたし)に対(たい)して</code> would mean the photograph is doing
  something to you.

- ❌ 兄(あに)は背(せ)が高(たか)いに対(たい)して、弟(おとうと)は小(ちい)さい。 → ✅ 兄(あに)は背(せ)が高(たか)い<b>のに対(たい)して</b>、弟(おとうと)は小(ちい)さい。
  The contrastive use attaches to a **nominalised** clause. The `の` is compulsory,
  and with a な-adjective it becomes <code>な+のに対(たい)して</code>.

- ❌ 上司(じょうし)に対(たい)して態度(たいど)が悪(わる)い。（intending "his attitude toward his boss is bad"） → ✅ 上司(じょうし)に<b>対(たい)する</b>態度(たいど)が悪(わる)い。
  Before a noun the verb takes its dictionary form. As written, the sentence
  attaches the target to <code>悪(わる)い</code> and drifts toward "he is badly
  behaved in the direction of his boss."

# ～にとって

**Meaning** · for; from the standpoint of - the sentence is judged from inside the position of the person or group named

`にとって` hands the microphone to somebody else. It says: seen from where **they**
stand, here is how things look. What follows is almost always an **evaluation** - 
important, hard, welcome, painful, worthless - because that is the only kind of
statement a standpoint can change.

It cannot mean "about", and it cannot mark the target of an action. Those two facts
do most of the work of keeping it apart from its neighbours.

## Formation

Attaches to a bare noun naming somebody capable of having a stake in the matter: a
person, a group, a company, a country, sometimes a living thing.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person or group) | Noun + にとって | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)にとって大切(たいせつ)だ |
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + にとっての + Noun | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)にとっての理想(りそう) |
| Noun, as topic | Noun + にとっては | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)にとっては簡単(かんたん)だ |
| Noun, as addition | Noun + にとっても | 会社(かいしゃ) → 会社(かいしゃ)にとっても損(そん)だ |
| Noun, business keigo | Noun + にとりまして | 弊社(へいしゃ) → 弊社(へいしゃ)にとりましては |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after `にとって` you need an **evaluation**, not an action.
<code>大切(たいせつ)だ・必要(ひつよう)だ・難(むずか)しい・ありがたい・問題(もんだい)だ・忘(わす)れられない</code>
all work. A plain report of what somebody did does not:
❌<code>私(わたし)にとって毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っています</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>にとって</b>、家族(かぞく)が一番(いちばん)大切(たいせつ)です。
For me, family comes first.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>にとって</b>この本(ほん)は難(むずか)しすぎる。
This book is too hard for a child.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>にとっては</b>少(すこ)し高(たか)いが、買(か)う価値(かち)はあると思(おも)う。
It's a bit pricey as far as I'm concerned, but I think it's worth buying.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)<b>にとって</b>、人(ひと)を育(そだ)てることは一番(いちばん)確実(かくじつ)な投資(とうし)だ。
For a company, developing its people is the surest investment there is.
</pre>

<pre>
北国(きたぐに)で育(そだ)った人(ひと)<b>にとって</b>、東京(とうきょう)の冬(ふゆ)はほとんど寒(さむ)くないらしい。
Apparently, for people raised in the far north, a Tokyo winter is barely cold at all.
</pre>

<pre>
あのときの一言(ひとこと)は、彼女(かのじょ)<b>にとって</b>一生(いっしょう)忘(わす)れられないものになった。
That one remark became something she would never forget as long as she lived.
</pre>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>にとっての</b>最初(さいしょ)の壁(かべ)は、言葉(ことば)よりも生活(せいかつ)習慣(しゅうかん)かもしれない。
The first wall for an exchange student may be daily customs rather than language.
</pre>

<pre>
地方(ちほう)の小(ちい)さな町(まち)<b>にとって</b>、若(わか)い人(ひと)が出(で)ていくことは放(ほう)っておけない問題(もんだい)だ。
For a small rural town, young people leaving is a problem that cannot be left alone.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にとって` is close to register-neutral. It is at home in an essay, a news article,
a job interview and a conversation with a friend. Nothing about it is stiff, and
unlike <code>に関(かん)して</code> or `において` it has not been pushed out of speech
by a plainer alternative.

What it does carry is a mild rhetorical weight. Because it explicitly frames a
judgement as belonging to somebody, it invites the reader to notice that other
people might see it differently. <code>私(わたし)にとっては高(たか)い</code> is
politer than <code>高(たか)い</code> for exactly that reason: it presents the
verdict as personal rather than absolute. Sales staff and diplomats lean on this.

In casual speech, `には` covers most of the same ground with fewer syllables - 
<code>僕(ぼく)にはちょっと無理(むり)かな</code> - and younger speakers also reach
for <code>個人的(こじんてき)には</code>, "personally speaking". `にとって` is not
unnatural in conversation, but a whole conversation full of it would sound like an
essay read aloud.

<code>にとりまして</code> is the business-keigo form, common in apologies and in
speeches: <code>弊社(へいしゃ)にとりまして大変(たいへん)ありがたいお話(はなし)です</code>.
Recognise it; you will rarely need to produce it.

One restriction worth stating plainly: the noun has to be something that can
**have interests**. People, groups, companies, countries, and living things all
qualify - <code>植物(しょくぶつ)にとって水(みず)は欠(か)かせない</code> is
perfectly normal. A pure abstraction or an inanimate object usually does not:
❌<code>この机(つくえ)にとって</code> is odd, because a desk has no point of view to
speak from.

## Contrast

### ～にとって vs ～には

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they overlap heavily, and `には` is the shorter, more spoken
option. `にとって` is unambiguous; `には` is a stack of two particles and can also
mean "to" or "in", so context has to sort it out.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>には</b>この説明(せつめい)がよく分(わ)からなかった。
I didn't really follow this explanation.
</pre>

<pre>
初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>にとって</b>、この説明(せつめい)は不親切(ふしんせつ)だ。
For a beginner, this explanation is unhelpful.
</pre>

Both frame a judgement from somebody's position. The difference shows up when the
sentence could be read another way. <code>山田(やまだ)さんには話(はな)した</code>
means "I told Yamada" - `に` marking the recipient, `は` marking contrast - and has
nothing to do with standpoints. <code>山田(やまだ)さんにとって</code> can only ever
mean "from Yamada's point of view". In writing, where you cannot lean on
intonation, that certainty is worth the extra syllables.

### ～にとって vs ～に対(たい)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にとって` is the **viewer**; <code>に対(たい)して</code> is the
**target**. Nothing is being aimed at the noun in a `にとって` sentence.

</div>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>にとって</b>、最初(さいしょ)の一年(いちねん)は覚(おぼ)えることばかりだ。
For a new employee, the first year is nothing but things to learn.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>に対(たい)して</b>、もう少(すこ)し丁寧(ていねい)に説明(せつめい)したほうがいい。
You'd do better to explain things to a new employee a bit more carefully.
</pre>

The first sentence describes the world as the newcomer experiences it. The second
describes what somebody else should do **to** them. A quick test: if you can add
"…and they may be the only one who sees it that way", it is `にとって`. If you can
add "…and they are on the receiving end", it is <code>に対(たい)して</code>.

### ～にとって vs ～として

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にとって` = "**for** X, from X's side of the fence".
`として` = "**as** X, in the role of X". `にとって` looks out from the noun;
`として` puts the subject *into* the noun.

</div>

Both come out as English "as" or "for" often enough to blur, and they can even sit
in the same slot with different meanings.

<pre>
親(おや)<b>にとって</b>、子(こ)どもの進路(しんろ)は口(くち)を出(だ)しにくい問題(もんだい)だ。
For a parent, a child's career choice is a hard thing to weigh in on.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)<b>として</b>、一度(いちど)は本音(ほんね)を伝(つた)えておきたい。
As a parent, I'd like to say what I really think at least once.
</pre>

In the first, parents are the group whose viewpoint we are borrowing; the speaker
need not be one. In the second, the speaker **is** a parent and is acting in that
capacity. Notice also what follows each: `にとって` is followed by an evaluation,
`として` by an action.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)にとって毎朝(まいあさ)コーヒーを飲(の)みます。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>は</b>毎朝(まいあさ)コーヒーを飲(の)みます。
  `にとって` frames a judgement, so it needs an evaluative predicate. A plain report
  of what you do takes an ordinary topic `は`.

- ❌ この計画(けいかく)にとって意見(いけん)を聞(き)かせてください。 → ✅ この計画(けいかく)<b>について</b>意見(いけん)を聞(き)かせてください。
  `にとって` cannot mean "about". A plan is not a viewpoint-holder; it is a topic.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)にとって失礼(しつれい)なことを言(い)ってしまった。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)に<b>対(たい)して</b>失礼(しつれい)なことを言(い)ってしまった。
  Rudeness is aimed **at** someone. That is <code>に対(たい)して</code>;
  `にとって` would put the teacher in the judge's seat instead of the victim's.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)にとって感謝(かんしゃ)しています。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>に</b>感謝(かんしゃ)しています。
  Gratitude has a recipient, not a standpoint. Plain `に` is what
  <code>感謝(かんしゃ)する</code> takes.

The whole point of the sentence is that the verdict is personal - someone else

The second `にとって` is wrong: the employees are who the explanation is delivered

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) この仕事(しごと)は簡単(かんたん)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

experiences it**, and it quietly implies that this is not everybody's experience:
someone else might struggle. That built-in "…though not necessarily for others" is
the whole reason the pattern exists, and it is why `にとって` is useful for
softening an opinion you do not want to present as objective truth.

</details>

# ～として

**Meaning** · as; in the capacity of; in the role of - the hat somebody or something is wearing while it does what it does

`として` assigns a **role**. A person acts *as* a representative, a building is used
*as* a gallery, a sum of money is handed over *as* a gift. The thing itself does not
change; what `として` supplies is the capacity in which it is being treated.

Two extensions follow naturally. `としては` sets a **standard of judgement** - 
"measured as an X" - and, with a person, "speaking for myself". And a handful of
fixed adverbs like <code>原則(げんそく)として</code> and
<code>参考(さんこう)として</code> are simply this pattern frozen solid.

## Formation

Attaches to a bare noun. No `な`, no `の` before it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (role), modifying a verb | Noun + として | 医者(いしゃ) → 医者(いしゃ)として働(はたら)く |
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + としての + Noun | 親(おや) → 親(おや)としての責任(せきにん) |
| Noun, as standard or standpoint | Noun + としては | 新人(しんじん) → 新人(しんじん)としてはよくやっている |
| Noun, as addition | Noun + としても | 趣味(しゅみ) → 趣味(しゅみ)としても楽(たの)しめる |
| Noun, ruling one out | Noun + としてではなく | 仕事(しごと) → 仕事(しごと)としてではなく |
| Fixed adverbs | 原則(げんそく)／例外(れいがい)／参考(さんこう) + として | 原則(げんそく) → 原則(げんそく)として禁止(きんし)です |

<div class="note">

**Note** - beyond N3 you will meet <code>一人(ひとり)として…ない</code>,
"not a single person", where `として` combines with a counter and a negative. It is
the same word doing an emphatic job, and it is worth recognising even though you do
not need to produce it yet.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
兄(あに)は医者(いしゃ)<b>として</b>働(はたら)いています。
My older brother works as a doctor.
</pre>

<pre>
趣味(しゅみ)<b>として</b>絵(え)を描(か)いています。
I paint as a hobby.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の代表(だいひょう)<b>として</b>式(しき)に出席(しゅっせき)しました。
I attended the ceremony as the company's representative.
</pre>

<pre>
この建物(たてもの)は今(いま)、美術館(びじゅつかん)<b>として</b>使(つか)われている。
This building is now used as an art museum.
</pre>

<pre>
参考(さんこう)<b>として</b>、去年(きょねん)の資料(しりょう)もお送(おく)りしておきます。
I'll send last year's materials along as well, for reference.
</pre>

<pre>
入(はい)ったばかりの人(ひと)<b>としては</b>、よくやっているほうだと思(おも)う。
For someone who's only just joined, I'd say he's doing well.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は友(とも)だち<b>としては</b>最高(さいこう)だが、仕事(しごと)のパートナー<b>としては</b>少(すこ)し心配(しんぱい)だ。
She's wonderful as a friend, but as a working partner I have my doubts.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)は語学(ごがく)の勉強(べんきょう)<b>としてだけでなく</b>、人生(じんせい)の経験(けいけん)<b>としても</b>大(おお)きな意味(いみ)がある。
Studying abroad matters not only as language study but as a life experience too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`として` is **register-neutral**. It appears in casual conversation, in job
interviews, in newspapers and in contracts without changing shape or feeling.
Nothing about it is stiff.

What is register-marked is the company it keeps. The fixed adverbs - 
<code>原則(げんそく)として</code> ("as a rule"),
<code>例外(れいがい)として</code> ("by way of exception"),
<code>結果(けっか)として</code> ("as a result") - belong to written and formal
speech. A friend says <code>だいたいダメだよ</code>; a notice says
<code>原則(げんそく)としてお断(ことわ)りしております</code>.

`としては` deserves its own line, because it does two different things and speakers
rely on both.

- **As a category standard**: <code>中古(ちゅうこ)としては高(たか)い</code> - 
  "expensive for a second-hand one." The judgement is made against what is normal
  for the category.
- **As a personal position**: <code>私(わたし)としては反対(はんたい)です</code> - 
  "my own position is that I'm against it." This is a politeness device. It marks
  the opinion as yours alone and leaves room for the group to decide otherwise,
  which is why it is everywhere in meetings.

The second of those is worth practising. Where English says "personally, I…",
Japanese very often says <code>私(わたし)としては</code>, and a learner who reaches
for <code>私(わたし)は</code> instead sounds more insistent than they meant to.

## Contrast

### ～として vs ～にとって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `として` puts the subject **into** the role; `にとって` looks
out **from** the noun's viewpoint. "As a teacher, I…" is `として`; "for a teacher,
this is…" is `にとって`.

</div>

<pre>
教師(きょうし)<b>として</b>、生徒(せいと)の話(はなし)はきちんと聞(き)きたい。
As a teacher, I want to listen properly to what my students say.
</pre>

<pre>
教師(きょうし)<b>にとって</b>、生徒(せいと)に信頼(しんらい)されることほどうれしいことはない。
For a teacher, nothing is more gratifying than being trusted by the students.
</pre>

In the first sentence the speaker **is** a teacher and is acting in that capacity.
In the second the speaker is standing outside, describing how the world looks to
teachers - and need not be one. The predicate gives it away too: `として` is
followed by an action or an intention, `にとって` by an evaluation.

### ～として vs ～で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `で` can also mark a capacity, but only loosely and only in
casual speech. `として` states the role explicitly and works at any register.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)はアルバイト<b>で</b>入(はい)っていました。
I was there on a part-time basis last year.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)はアルバイト職員(しょくいん)<b>として</b>採用(さいよう)されました。
Last year I was taken on as a part-time member of staff.
</pre>

Both are natural; the second is what you would write on a form. The gap widens when
the role is formal or contested - ❌<code>代表(だいひょう)で意見(いけん)を述(の)べた</code>
is at best vague about whether you were *the* representative, while
<code>代表(だいひょう)として意見(いけん)を述(の)べた</code> states it plainly.
When the role is what matters, use `として`.

### ～としては vs ～にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `としては` measures something **within** its category;
`にしては` says the result is **surprising for** that category. Add "considering"
and a raised eyebrow, and you have `にしては`.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>としては</b>よく考(かんが)えているほうだ。
As children go, he thinks things through well.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>にしては</b>よく考(かんが)えている。
He thinks things through well, for a child.
</pre>

The first ranks him among children - a neutral placement inside the category. The
second is mildly astonished: given that he is a child, this is more than expected.
Where they part company completely is the personal-position use.
<code>私(わたし)としては賛成(さんせい)です</code> means "my position is that I'm in
favour"; ❌<code>私(わたし)にしては賛成(さんせい)です</code> would have to mean
"surprisingly for me, I'm in favour", which is rarely what anyone intends.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 通訳(つうやく)になって会議(かいぎ)に出(で)ました。（intending "attended as an interpreter"） → ✅ 通訳(つうやく)<b>として</b>会議(かいぎ)に出(で)ました。
  <code>になる</code> means *become*: the wrong version says you turned into an
  interpreter and then went. `として` names the capacity you attended in.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は倉庫(そうこ)にして使(つか)っています。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は倉庫(そうこ)<b>として</b>使(つか)っています。
  <code>にする</code> is "turn it into"; <code>として使(つか)う</code> is "use it as".
  Only the second describes an ongoing role.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)としての働(はたら)いています。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)<b>として</b>働(はたら)いています。
  <code>としての</code> exists only in front of a **noun**
  (<code>先生(せんせい)としての経験(けいけん)</code>). Before a verb, drop the `の`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)として、この写真(しゃしん)はとても大切(たいせつ)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>にとって</b>、この写真(しゃしん)はとても大切(たいせつ)です。
  This is a judgement made from your side, not a role you are performing.
  <code>私(わたし)としては</code> would be possible only if you were stating a
  position in a discussion.

## Practice

**1.** その画家(がか)は長(なが)い間(あいだ)、教師(きょうし)____生活(せいかつ)していた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

教師(きょうし)<b>として</b>生活(せいかつ)していた。

The painter was living **in the capacity of** a teacher - a role, held over a long
period. <code>教師(きょうし)になって</code> would say he became one at a particular
moment, which is a different claim.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 初(はじ)めて作(つく)った(としては・にしては)、なかなかうまくできた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

初(はじ)めて作(つく)った<b>にしては</b>、なかなかうまくできた。

The sentence is expressing surprise: given that it was a first attempt, the result
beat expectations. That is `にしては`. `としては` also attaches to nouns rather than
clauses, so <code>初(はじ)めて作(つく)ったとしては</code> would not work here
anyway.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'd like to send this as a thank-you, not as a gift."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

これはプレゼント<b>としてではなく</b>、お礼(れい)<b>として</b>送(おく)りたいのです。

<code>としてではなく</code> rules out one role before naming the intended one. The
`は` is the ordinary contrastive `は` sitting on top of the pattern, which is why
the shape is <code>として+では+なく</code> rather than <code>としてじゃない</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 山田(やまだ)さんはリーダーにとって優(すぐ)れているが、技術者(ぎじゅつしゃ)としてこの仕事(しごと)は向(む)いていないと思(おも)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 山田(やまだ)さんはリーダー<b>としては</b>優(すぐ)れているが、技術者(ぎじゅつしゃ)<b>としては</b>この仕事(しごと)は向(む)いていないと思(おも)う。

Both slots are judging him **within a role**, so both need `として` - and `は` on
each, because the whole sentence is built on setting one role against the other.
`にとって` would make the leader the one doing the judging.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)<b>は</b>この案(あん)に反対(はんたい)です。
(b) 私(わたし)<b>としては</b>この案(あん)に反対(はんたい)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat statement of opposition. In a meeting it lands as a personal stand
and can sound confrontational, especially if others have already spoken in favour.

(b) frames the same opposition as **your position within the discussion** - "my
own view is against it" - which implicitly grants that the group may decide
otherwise. It is softer and more collegial, and it is the standard shape for
disagreeing in a Japanese meeting. The propositional content is identical; only the
social move differs.

</details>

# ～向き - facing

**Meaning** · facing such-and-such a way - the direction a thing is turned toward（むき）

<code>向(む)き</code> is the noun form of <code>向(む)く</code>, "to face". Stuck
onto a direction word it gives you the orientation of something that is standing
still: a south-facing room, an arrow pointing down, an envelope placed face up.

There is no movement in it. A south-facing window is not going anywhere; it is
simply turned that way. That single fact separates this pattern from
<code>に向(む)かって</code>, and it is where learners slip.

The same word has a second, unrelated-looking life meaning "suited to", as in
<code>子(こ)ども向(む)きの本(ほん)</code>. That sense is treated separately;
this page is only about direction.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direction word | 南(みなみ)・北(きた)・東(ひがし)・西(にし) + 向(む)き | 南(みなみ) → 南(みなみ)向(む)きの部屋(へや) |
| Up / down / sideways | 上(うわ)・下(した)・横(よこ) + 向(む)き | 下(した) → 下(した)向(む)きの矢印(やじるし) |
| Front / back (also figurative) | 前(まえ)・後(うし)ろ + 向(む)き | 前(まえ) → 前(まえ)向(む)きに考(かんが)える |
| As a bare noun | Noun + の + 向(む)き | いす → いすの向(む)きを変(か)える |
| Adverbially, before a verb | ～向(む)きに + verb | 上(うわ)向(む)き → 上(うわ)向(む)きに置(お)く |
| As a predicate | ～向(む)きだ／である | 東(ひがし) → 玄関(げんかん)は東(ひがし)向(む)きだ |

<div class="note">

**Note on readings** - two of these are worth memorising as whole words rather
than as sums. <code>上(うわ)向(む)き</code> is standardly うわむき, not うえむき.
And <code>風(かざ)向(む)き</code> is かざむき: <code>風(かぜ)</code> takes its
bound form かざ inside compounds, as it does in
<code>風車(かざぐるま)</code> and <code>風上(かざかみ)</code>. In both words the
<code>向(む)き</code> half is regular. <code>下(した)向(む)き</code> is regular
throughout.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は南(みなみ)<b>向(む)き</b>で、日当(ひあ)たりがいい。
This room faces south and gets plenty of sun.
</pre>

<pre>
いすの<b>向(む)き</b>を変(か)えてください。
Please turn the chair around.
</pre>

<pre>
矢印(やじるし)が下(した)<b>向(む)き</b>になっている。
The arrow is pointing downward.
</pre>

<pre>
封筒(ふうとう)は表(おもて)を上(うわ)<b>向(む)き</b>にして箱(はこ)に入(い)れてください。
Put the envelopes in the box face up.
</pre>

<pre>
玄関(げんかん)が東(ひがし)<b>向(む)き</b>なので、朝日(あさひ)がよく入(はい)る。
The entrance faces east, so the morning sun comes right in.
</pre>

<pre>
風(かざ)<b>向(む)き</b>が変(か)わって、煙(けむり)がこちらに流(なが)れてきた。
The wind shifted and the smoke drifted over to our side.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)しても、いつまでも後(うし)ろ<b>向(む)き</b>でいるより前(まえ)<b>向(む)き</b>に考(かんが)えたほうがいい。
Even when things go wrong, it's better to think forward than to stay stuck looking backward.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)を借(か)りるときは、家賃(やちん)や広(ひろ)さだけでなく、窓(まど)の<b>向(む)き</b>も確(たし)かめておいたほうがいい。
When you rent a flat, it's worth checking which way the windows face, not just the rent and the floor space.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is an ordinary, register-neutral noun. Nothing about it is formal or casual;
it turns up in estate agents' listings, in assembly instructions, in weather
reports and in conversation, all in the same shape.

Two of its habitats are worth knowing about, because they are where you will
actually meet it.

**Property.** <code>南(みなみ)向(む)き</code> is a selling point in Japan, and
listings advertise it the way an English listing advertises a garden. The reason is
practical - sun for the laundry and warmth in winter - and the vocabulary is fixed:
<code>南(みなみ)向(む)きバルコニー</code>,
<code>東(ひがし)向(む)きの窓(まど)</code>.

**Attitude.** <code>前(まえ)向(む)き</code> and
<code>後(うし)ろ向(む)き</code> have drifted from literal facing to mean
"positive / constructive" and "negative / defeatist". These are extremely common in
work Japanese: <code>前(まえ)向(む)きに検討(けんとう)します</code> is what a
company says when it wants to sound willing.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>前(まえ)向(む)きに検討(けんとう)します</code> is a famous soft
refusal. Literally "we will consider it positively", it very often means the matter
will quietly go nowhere. Take it as encouragement, not as a promise.

</div>

The bare noun <code>向(む)き</code> also appears in a fixed phrase you will hear
in shops and offices: <code>向(む)きを変(か)える</code>, "turn it round". And
<code>風(かざ)向(む)き</code> has its own figurative use - 
<code>風(かざ)向(む)きが変(か)わった</code>, "the wind has changed", said of
a situation turning in someone's favour.

## Contrast

### ～向(む)き vs ～に向(む)かって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>向(む)き</code> is a **static orientation**;
<code>に向(む)かって</code> is **movement or action toward** something. If anything
is travelling, you need <code>に向(む)かって</code>.

</div>

<pre>
その像(ぞう)は海(うみ)のほうを向(む)いて立(た)っている。
The statue stands facing the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
船(ふね)は島(しま)に<b>向(む)かって</b>進(すす)んでいる。
The ship is heading toward the island.
</pre>

The statue is not going to the sea; it is merely turned that way, and
<code>海(うみ)向(む)き</code> would describe it. The ship is under way, so
<code>に向(む)かって</code> is the only option: ❌<code>島(しま)向(む)きに進(すす)む</code>
does not work. Note also the grammatical shape - <code>向(む)き</code> attaches
directly to the noun with no particle, while <code>向(む)かって</code> demands `に`.

### 向(む)き vs 方向(ほうこう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>向(む)き</code> is **which way a thing is turned**;
<code>方向(ほうこう)</code> is **a direction out in the world**, the way you go.
A chair has a <code>向(む)き</code>; a road has a <code>方向(ほうこう)</code>.

</div>

<pre>
テーブルの<b>向(む)き</b>を少(すこ)し右(みぎ)に回(まわ)した。
I turned the table a little to the right.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)はこの<b>方向(ほうこう)</b>でいいですか。
Is the station this way?
</pre>

Swapping them produces sentences that sound off rather than wrong. ❌<code>テーブルの方向(ほうこう)を変(か)える</code>
suggests moving the table somewhere else, not rotating it; ❌<code>駅(えき)はこの向(む)きですか</code>
sounds as though the station is facing you. The pair
<code>向(む)きを変(か)える</code> (rotate) versus
<code>方向(ほうこう)を変(か)える</code> (change course) is the cleanest way to
hold them apart.

### ～向(む)き vs ～向(む)け

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - only <code>向(む)き</code> does direction.
<code>向(む)け</code> means "made for, aimed at an audience" and never means
"facing".

</div>

<pre>
北(きた)<b>向(む)き</b>の窓(まど)からは一日中(いちにちじゅう)日(ひ)が入(はい)らない。
A north-facing window gets no sun all day.
</pre>

<pre>
これは海外(かいがい)<b>向(む)け</b>に作(つく)られたモデルです。
This is a model built for overseas markets.
</pre>

❌<code>北(きた)向(む)けの窓(まど)</code> is not Japanese. The two suffixes do
overlap in the *other* sense of <code>向(む)き</code> - "suitable for" - where
<code>子(こ)ども向(む)き</code> and <code>子(こ)ども向(む)け</code> are both
possible and differ subtly. In the direction sense there is no overlap at all, so
here at least the choice is easy.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は南(みなみ)に向(む)きです。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は南(みなみ)<b>向(む)き</b>です。
  The direction word joins directly to <code>向(む)き</code> as a compound noun.
  There is no particle in between. With `に` you would need the verb:
  <code>南(みなみ)に向(む)いています</code>.

- ❌ 北(きた)向(む)けのベランダは洗濯物(せんたくもの)が乾(かわ)きにくい。 → ✅ 北(きた)<b>向(む)き</b>のベランダは洗濯物(せんたくもの)が乾(かわ)きにくい。
  <code>向(む)け</code> means "intended for" and cannot express facing. Only
  <code>向(む)き</code> takes a compass point.

- ❌ 子(こ)どもたちは公園(こうえん)向(む)きに走(はし)っていった。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもたちは公園(こうえん)に<b>向(む)かって</b>走(はし)っていった。
  Running is movement, and <code>向(む)き</code> has no movement in it. Anything
  that travels takes <code>に向(む)かって</code>.

- ❌ カメラの方向(ほうこう)を上(うえ)にしてください。 → ✅ カメラの<b>向(む)き</b>を上(うわ)<b>向(む)き</b>にしてください。
  Tilting the camera changes how it is turned, not where it is going.
  <code>方向(ほうこう)</code> would suggest carrying it somewhere.

## Practice

**1.** この建物(たてもの)の入口(いりぐち)は西(にし)____なので、夕方(ゆうがた)はまぶしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

入口(いりぐち)は西(にし)<b>向(む)き</b>なので、夕方(ゆうがた)はまぶしい。

A compass point plus <code>向(む)き</code> gives the orientation of a fixed
thing. No particle comes between the two, and
<code>西(にし)向(む)け</code> would be meaningless here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)は北(きた)（向(む)きに・に向(む)かって）進(すす)んでいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

台風(たいふう)は北(きた)<b>に向(む)かって</b>進(すす)んでいます。

A typhoon is moving, and <code>向(む)き</code> describes only static facing.
Weather forecasts say <code>北(きた)に向(む)かって</code> or
<code>北(きた)へ進(すす)む</code>; ❌<code>北(きた)向(む)きに進(すす)む</code>
would try to give a storm a fixed orientation.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please lay the photographs face up on the desk."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

写真(しゃしん)は上(うわ)<b>向(む)き</b>にして机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に置(お)いてください。

<code>上(うわ)向(む)き</code> - note the うわ reading - plus
<code>にする</code> gives "put it into a face-up orientation". A common alternative
is <code>表(おもて)を上(うえ)にして</code>, which says the same thing by naming
the front rather than the direction.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 寝(ね)るときは下(した)向(む)きより上(うえ)向(む)きのほうが体(からだ)にいいと言(い)われている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 寝(ね)るときは下(した)向(む)きより<b>上(うわ)向(む)き</b>のほうが体(からだ)にいいと言(い)われている。

The grammar is fine; the **reading** is not. The standard reading of
<code>上(うわ)向(む)き</code> is うわむき, even though
<code>下(した)向(む)き</code> is the regular したむき. This is a compound whose
first element keeps the older うわ form, as in
<code>上着(うわぎ)</code> and <code>上手(うわて)</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) いすを窓(まど)のほうに<b>向(む)けた</b>。
(b) いすの<b>向(む)き</b>を変(か)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the transitive verb <code>向(む)ける</code> and names the destination of
the turning: the chair now faces the window specifically. The window is the point of
the sentence.

(b) uses the noun and says only that the orientation is now different from what it
was. Where it ended up is left open. In practice (b) is what you say while doing it
and (a) is what you say when the direction matters - for instance because you wanted
to look outside.

</details>

# ～向き - suited to

**Meaning** · suitable for; a good fit for - it happens to suit that person, group or purpose, whether or not anyone planned it that way（むき）

This is the same <code>向(む)き</code> that gives you
<code>南(みなみ)向(む)きの部屋(へや)</code>, but with the direction sense worn
away. Here it says: **this thing and that audience match**. A book that suits
beginners, a dish that suits summer, a job that suits people who like talking.

The key word is *happens to*. <code>向(む)き</code> is the speaker's assessment of
a fit. It makes no claim that anybody designed the thing for that audience - and
that is exactly what separates it from <code>向(む)け</code>, which does.

## Formation

Attaches to a bare noun: a person, a group, a season, an activity, a purpose.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person or group) | Noun + 向(む)きだ | 初心者(しょしんしゃ) → この本(ほん)は初心者(しょしんしゃ)向(む)きだ |
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + 向(む)きの + Noun | 家族(かぞく) → 家族(かぞく)向(む)きのコース |
| Noun (season or purpose) | Noun + 向(む)きだ | 夏(なつ) → 夏(なつ)向(む)きの服(ふく) |
| Negative | Noun + 向(む)きではない | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)ども向(む)きではない |
| Unsuited (one word) | 不向(ふむ)きだ | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)には不向(ふむ)きだ |
| Fixed noun pair | 向(む)き不向(ふむ)き | 仕事(しごと) → 仕事(しごと)には向(む)き不向(ふむ)きがある |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - before a noun it is <code>向(む)きの</code>, not
<code>向(む)きな</code>. The `な` form belongs only to
<code>前(まえ)向(む)き</code> and <code>後(うし)ろ向(む)き</code> in their
figurative "positive / negative" sense, which have hardened into adjectives:
<code>前(まえ)向(む)きな意見(いけん)</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この本(ほん)は初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>向(む)き</b>です。
This book suits beginners.
</pre>

<pre>
辛(から)い料理(りょうり)は子(こ)ども<b>向(む)き</b>ではない。
Spicy food isn't right for children.
</pre>

<pre>
この服(ふく)は軽(かる)くて涼(すず)しいので、夏(なつ)<b>向(む)き</b>だ。
These clothes are light and cool, so they're good for summer.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かで席(せき)も広(ひろ)いから、勉強(べんきょう)<b>向(む)き</b>の店(みせ)だと思(おも)う。
It's quiet and the seats are roomy, so I'd call it a good café for studying.
</pre>

<pre>
この山(やま)は道(みち)がよく整(ととの)っていて、家族(かぞく)<b>向(む)き</b>のコースが多(おお)い。
The paths on this mountain are well maintained, and many of the routes suit families.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)が細(こま)かすぎて、入門書(にゅうもんしょ)としては初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>向(む)き</b>とは言(い)えない。
The explanations are too fine-grained; as an introductory book it can't really be called beginner-friendly.
</pre>

<pre>
営業(えいぎょう)は人(ひと)と話(はな)すのが好(す)きな人(ひと)<b>向(む)き</b>の仕事(しごと)で、私(わたし)には不向(ふむ)きだと思(おも)う。
Sales suits people who enjoy talking to others; I don't think it's for me.
</pre>

<pre>
このアプリは文字(もじ)が大(おお)きくて操作(そうさ)も少(すく)ないので、スマホに慣(な)れていない人(ひと)<b>向(む)き</b>だ。
This app has big text and few controls, so it suits people who aren't used to smartphones.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and fully at home in speech. You can say
<code>これ、子(こ)ども向(む)きだね</code> to a friend in a shop without sounding
formal, and the same phrase can appear in a product review.

What the word carries is a **judgement**, and judgements belong to somebody. When
you say <code>初心者(しょしんしゃ)向(む)きだ</code> you are giving your reading of
how well the thing fits - you are not reporting a fact about the publisher's
intentions. This is why <code>向(む)き</code> works so naturally in reviews,
recommendations and warnings, and why it so often appears with hedges:
<code>向(む)きかもしれない</code>, <code>向(む)きだと思(おも)う</code>,
<code>向(む)きとは言(い)えない</code>.

The negative <code>不向(ふむ)き</code> is a single word, not
<code>向(む)き</code> plus a prefix you can apply freely, and it is used above all
of **people and jobs**: <code>彼(かれ)は接客(せっきゃく)には不向(ふむ)きだ</code>.
It is blunter than <code>向(む)きではない</code> and can sting, so in careful
speech people soften it: <code>あまり向(む)いていないかもしれませんね</code>.

<code>向(む)き不向(ふむ)き</code> as a pair is a small fixed noun meaning
"aptitude" - literally the suits and the does-not-suits.
<code>人(ひと)には向(む)き不向(ふむ)きがある</code> is a stock consoling phrase:
"people are cut out for different things."

## Contrast

### ～向(む)き vs ～向(む)け

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>向(む)き</code> = it **happens to suit** them.
<code>向(む)け</code> = it was **made for** them. Fit versus intention.

</div>

This is the pair the whole page exists for, and the difference is real even though
many sentences accept both.

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は字(じ)が大(おお)きいので高齢者(こうれいしゃ)<b>向(む)き</b>だ。
This dictionary has large print, so it works well for older readers.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は高齢者(こうれいしゃ)<b>向(む)け</b>に作(つく)られている。
This dictionary was made for older readers.
</pre>

The first is a reader's verdict: whatever the publisher had in mind, the big print
suits them. The second is a statement about the design brief. Consequently
<code>向(む)け</code> is what appears in business and marketing language - 
<code>海外(かいがい)向(む)け</code>, <code>法人(ほうじん)向(む)け</code> - while
<code>向(む)き</code> is what appears in reviews and advice.

One useful consequence: <code>向(む)け</code> takes `に` before a verb naturally
(<code>向(む)けに作(つく)る</code>, <code>向(む)けに開(ひら)く</code>) because
intention modifies an action. <code>向(む)き</code> mostly stays a predicate or a
noun modifier, which is why ❌<code>子(こ)ども向(む)きに作(つく)る</code> sounds
wrong to a native ear.

### ～向(む)き vs ～用(よう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>用(よう)</code> states a **designated use**: this item
is *for* that and not for anything else. <code>向(む)き</code> only rates a fit.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>用(よう)</b>のいすはあちらにございます。
The children's chairs are over there.
</pre>

<pre>
この映画(えいが)は子(こ)ども<b>向(む)き</b>だと思(おも)う。
I think this film is one for children.
</pre>

<code>子(こ)ども用(よう)</code> is a category label - the chair is a child's
chair, and an adult using it is misusing it. <code>子(こ)ども向(む)き</code> is a
recommendation; an adult can watch the film perfectly well, it just fits children
better. Labels on shelves and menus overwhelmingly use <code>用(よう)</code>:
<code>業務用(ぎょうむよう)</code>, <code>洗濯用(せんたくよう)</code>,
<code>家庭用(かていよう)</code> - it is the suffix of designated purpose.

### ～向(む)きだ vs ～に向(む)いている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two say the same thing from opposite ends.
<code>A は B 向(む)きだ</code>: the **thing** is described, B is the audience.
<code>B は A に向(む)いている</code>: the **person** is described, A is the activity.

</div>

<pre>
この仕事(しごと)は細(こま)かい作業(さぎょう)が好(す)きな人(ひと)<b>向(む)き</b>だ。
This job suits people who like detailed work.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は細(こま)かい作業(さぎょう)に<b>向(む)いている</b>。
My younger sister is well suited to detailed work.
</pre>

Getting these crossed is the commonest error with the pattern. Remember that
<code>向(む)いている</code> is a verb and needs `に`, while
<code>向(む)き</code> is a suffix and needs nothing at all. Also note that with a
named individual, <code>に向(む)いている</code> is far more natural:
<code>妹(いもうと)向(む)きだ</code> would be odd, because
<code>向(む)き</code> prefers a **type** of person rather than a particular one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 子(こ)ども向(む)きに作(つく)られた番組(ばんぐみ)です。 → ✅ 子(こ)ども<b>向(む)け</b>に作(つく)られた番組(ばんぐみ)です。
  Being *made for* an audience is intention, and intention is
  <code>向(む)け</code>. <code>向(む)き</code> would only rate the result.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はこの仕事(しごと)向(む)きです。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はこの仕事(しごと)に<b>向(む)いています</b>。
  The two ends are reversed. <code>向(む)き</code> attaches to the **audience**,
  so the correct form with this word order would be
  <code>この仕事(しごと)は彼(かれ)のような人(ひと)向(む)きです</code>.

- ❌ 初心者(しょしんしゃ)向(む)きな教材(きょうざい)を探(さが)しています。 → ✅ 初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>向(む)きの</b>教材(きょうざい)を探(さが)しています。
  <code>向(む)き</code> is a noun and links to the next noun with `の`. Only
  <code>前(まえ)向(む)き</code> and <code>後(うし)ろ向(む)き</code> take `な`.

- ❌ このコースは体力(たいりょく)に自信(じしん)がない人(ひと)に向(む)きです。 → ✅ …自信(じしん)がない人(ひと)<b>向(む)き</b>です。
  No particle stands between the audience and <code>向(む)き</code>; they form a
  compound noun. The `に` belongs to the verb version,
  <code>人(ひと)に向(む)いています</code>.

**1.** この旅館(りょかん)は部屋(へや)が広(ひろ)くて静(しず)かなので、小(ちい)さい子(こ)どもがいる家族(かぞく)____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

家族(かぞく)<b>向(む)き</b>です。

The sentence gives reasons and then delivers a verdict on the fit - big rooms, quiet,

<code>家族(かぞく)向(む)け</code> would claim the inn was designed with families

外国人(がいこくじん)<b>向(む)け</b>に書(か)かれています。

<code>書(か)かれている</code> is an act of creation, so the sentence is about the

<code>向(む)け</code> is the form that combines with `に` before a verb.

</details>

A judgement about fit, hedged with <code>あまり</code> - the classic environment for

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The sentence is describing a **person**, so the verb form is what you want, with
`に` marking the activity. Written with <code>向(む)き</code>, the job would have

<code>接客(せっきゃく)の仕事(しごと)は田中(たなか)さんのような人(ひと)向(む)きではない</code>.

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) この雑誌(ざっし)は若(わか)い女性(じょせい)<b>向(む)け</b>だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker's assessment: whatever the magazine was meant to be, its
contents suit young women. It is the sort of thing you say while flicking through
it in a shop, and it leaves open the possibility that other people read it too.

(b) is a statement about the publisher: this magazine is **aimed at** young women - 
that is its market. It is the sentence an editor or a bookshop clerk would use, and
it says nothing about whether it actually suits them. Note also that (b) can be
extended with `に` and a verb - <code>若(わか)い女性(じょせい)向(む)けに作(つく)られた</code> - 
where (a) cannot.

</details>

# ～向け

**Meaning** · intended for; aimed at - made, written, priced or organised with that audience or market in mind（むけ）

<code>向(む)け</code> comes from <code>向(む)ける</code>, "to point something at".
Somebody has aimed this thing at somebody: a programme at children, a car at the
export market, a seminar at second-year students. The word carries **intention**,
and that is its whole meaning.

It says nothing about whether the aim succeeded. A textbook can be
<code>初心者(しょしんしゃ)向(む)け</code> and still be far too hard for a
beginner - the publisher aimed, and missed. Judging the fit is the job of
<code>向(む)き</code>.

## Formation

Attaches to a bare noun naming an audience, a market, a region or a customer type.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (audience), as predicate | Noun + 向(む)けだ | 海外(かいがい) → この商品(しょうひん)は海外(かいがい)向(む)けだ |
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + 向(む)けの + Noun | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)ども向(む)けの番組(ばんぐみ) |
| Noun, modifying a verb | Noun + 向(む)けに + verb | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)向(む)けに作(つく)る |
| Market or destination | 輸出(ゆしゅつ)・国内(こくない) + 向(む)け | 輸出(ゆしゅつ) → 輸出(ゆしゅつ)向(む)けの車(くるま) |
| Contrasted pair | A向(む)けとB向(む)け | 法人(ほうじん) → 法人(ほうじん)向(む)けと個人(こじん)向(む)け |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>向(む)けに</code> before a verb,
<code>向(む)けの</code> before a noun, <code>向(む)けだ</code> at the end of the
sentence. There is no <code>向(む)けな</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは子(こ)ども<b>向(む)け</b>の番組(ばんぐみ)です。
This is a programme for children.
</pre>

<pre>
この商品(しょうひん)は海外(かいがい)<b>向(む)け</b>です。
This product is for overseas markets.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)が学生(がくせい)<b>向(む)け</b>の説明会(せつめいかい)を開(ひら)いた。
The company held a briefing session for students.
</pre>

<pre>
この教材(きょうざい)は日本語(にほんご)を学(まな)び始(はじ)めたばかりの人(ひと)<b>向(む)け</b>に作(つく)られている。
These materials were made for people who have only just started learning Japanese.
</pre>

<pre>
輸出(ゆしゅつ)<b>向(む)け</b>の車(くるま)は、ハンドルが左(ひだり)についている。
Cars built for export have the steering wheel on the left.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から、高校生(こうこうせい)<b>向(む)け</b>のオンライン講座(こうざ)が始(はじ)まります。
An online course aimed at high-school students starts next month.
</pre>

<pre>
この雑誌(ざっし)はもともと専門家(せんもんか)<b>向(む)け</b>に作(つく)られたが、今(いま)では一般(いっぱん)の読者(どくしゃ)のほうが多(おお)い。
This magazine was originally produced for specialists, but these days most of its readers are ordinary members of the public.
</pre>

<pre>
国内(こくない)<b>向(む)け</b>と海外(かいがい)<b>向(む)け</b>では、同(おな)じ製品(せいひん)でも説明書(せつめいしょ)の厚(あつ)さがまったく違(ちが)う。
For the domestic market and for overseas, the manual that comes with the same product is a completely different thickness.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The word itself is register-neutral, but its **habitat** is not. You will meet it
in product descriptions, publishers' catalogues, course listings, TV schedules,
press releases and company presentations, far more often than in casual chat. That
is not because it sounds formal - it is because talking about target audiences is
mostly a business activity.

In conversation it turns up whenever the topic is products or content:
<code>これ、大人(おとな)向(む)けだよね</code> is perfectly ordinary spoken
Japanese. What you would not do is use it about your own private arrangements - 
see the contrast with <code>のために</code> below.

A few collocations are worth having ready-made, because they are how the pattern
actually appears in the wild: <code>海外(かいがい)向(む)け</code>,
<code>国内(こくない)向(む)け</code>, <code>輸出(ゆしゅつ)向(む)け</code>,
<code>法人(ほうじん)向(む)け</code> ("for businesses"),
<code>個人(こじん)向(む)け</code> ("for individuals"),
<code>初心者(しょしんしゃ)向(む)け</code>,
<code>女性(じょせい)向(む)け</code>. The last of these has become slightly
loaded in recent years - marketing categories drawn along gender lines attract
comment - so it is worth noticing that it describes a **marketing decision** and not
a claim about who may buy the thing.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>向(む)け</code> wants a **group or market**, not a named
individual. <code>部長(ぶちょう)向(む)けの資料(しりょう)</code> is odd unless
you mean managers as a category. For one person, use
<code>部長(ぶちょう)に見(み)せる資料(しりょう)</code> or
<code>部長(ぶちょう)のための資料(しりょう)</code>.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～向(む)け vs ～向(む)き

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>向(む)け</code> = somebody **aimed** it there.
<code>向(む)き</code> = it **turns out to suit** them. Intention versus fit.

</div>

<pre>
この問題集(もんだいしゅう)は受験生(じゅけんせい)<b>向(む)け</b>に作(つく)られた。
This question book was produced for exam candidates.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題集(もんだいしゅう)は説明(せつめい)が丁寧(ていねい)なので独学(どくがく)の人(ひと)<b>向(む)き</b>だ。
The explanations are thorough, so this question book is good for people studying alone.
</pre>

The first reports a decision made by the publisher. The second is the speaker's
verdict, and it could easily be about an audience the publisher never considered.
This is why a shop sign says <code>子(こ)ども向(む)けコーナー</code> - the shop
has designated it - while a review says
<code>子(こ)ども向(む)きだと思(おも)う</code>.

Only <code>向(む)け</code> combines comfortably with `に` plus a verb of making,
writing, opening or selling, precisely because those verbs describe the act of
aiming. And only <code>向(む)き</code> can be preceded by a compass point:
<code>南(みなみ)向(む)き</code> is a direction, and
❌<code>南(みなみ)向(む)け</code> is nothing at all.

### ～向(む)け vs ～用(よう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>向(む)け</code> names the **audience** something was
aimed at; <code>用(よう)</code> names the **use** something is for. Who versus
what-for.

</div>

<pre>
自宅(じたく)<b>用(よう)</b>ですか、贈(おく)り物(もの)<b>用(よう)</b>ですか。
Is it for yourself, or is it a gift?
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)<b>向(む)け</b>のサービスも始(はじ)めました。
We've launched a service for businesses as well.
</pre>

The shop assistant's question is about the **purpose** the item will serve, so
<code>用(よう)</code>. The second sentence names the **customer segment**, so
<code>向(む)け</code>. They can even stack in the same phrase without redundancy:
<code>学生(がくせい)向(む)けの試験(しけん)用(よう)アプリ</code> - an
exam-taking app aimed at students.

Note that <code>用(よう)</code> attaches to activities and occasions as readily as
to people (<code>旅行用(りょこうよう)</code>,
<code>非常用(ひじょうよう)</code>), whereas <code>向(む)け</code> is essentially
restricted to audiences and markets.

### ～向(む)けに vs ～のために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のために</code> is **for someone's sake**, personal and
warm. <code>向(む)けに</code> is **targeted at a market**, impersonal and
commercial.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)<b>のために</b>大(おお)きい字(じ)の本(ほん)を買(か)った。
I bought a large-print book for my mother.
</pre>

<pre>
目(め)が悪(わる)い人(ひと)<b>向(む)けに</b>大(おお)きい字(じ)の本(ほん)が出(だ)されている。
Large-print books are published for people with poor eyesight.
</pre>

Swapping them is a tonal mistake rather than a grammatical one, and it is a
memorable one. ❌<code>母(はは)向(む)けに本(ほん)を買(か)った</code> makes
your mother sound like a demographic segment. Conversely, a publisher's catalogue
would not write <code>目(め)が悪(わる)い人(ひと)のために</code> unless it
wanted to sound charitable rather than commercial - which, occasionally, it does.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 南(みなみ)向(む)けのベランダがある部屋(へや)を探(さが)しています。 → ✅ 南(みなみ)<b>向(む)き</b>のベランダがある部屋(へや)を探(さが)しています。
  Compass points take <code>向(む)き</code>. A balcony faces south; nobody aimed
  it at south as a market.

- ❌ 初心者(しょしんしゃ)向(む)けな教科書(きょうかしょ)を使(つか)っています。 → ✅ 初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>向(む)けの</b>教科書(きょうかしょ)を使(つか)っています。
  <code>向(む)け</code> is a noun and joins the next noun with `の`. There is no
  な-adjective form.

- ❌ 今夜(こんや)は家族(かぞく)向(む)けにカレーを作(つく)りました。 → ✅ 今夜(こんや)は家族(かぞく)<b>のために</b>カレーを作(つく)りました。
  Cooking dinner at home is done for people's sake, not aimed at a market segment.
  <code>向(む)けに</code> here sounds as though you are running a canteen.

- ❌ この靴(くつ)は私(わたし)向(む)けです。 → ✅ この靴(くつ)は私(わたし)に<b>合(あ)っています</b>。
  <code>向(む)け</code> takes a group or a market, never a single named person.
  For an individual fit, use <code>合(あ)う</code>, or
  <code>向(む)いている</code> for an aptitude.

## Practice

**1.** この会社(かいしゃ)は数年前(すうねんまえ)から高齢者(こうれいしゃ)____のスマートフォンを作(つく)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

高齢者(こうれいしゃ)<b>向(む)け</b>のスマートフォンを作(つく)っている。

The company deliberately designs the phones for that market, so the sentence is
about **intention**. <code>高齢者(こうれいしゃ)向(む)き</code> would be a
reviewer's verdict on the result, which is not what a manufacturer's own activity
is described with.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この部屋(へや)は東(ひがし)（向(む)き・向(む)け）なので、朝(あさ)は明(あか)るいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この部屋(へや)は東(ひがし)<b>向(む)き</b>なので、朝(あさ)は明(あか)るいです。

A compass direction is pure orientation, which only <code>向(む)き</code>
expresses. <code>向(む)け</code> requires an audience or a market, and "east" is
neither.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The museum has started an English-language guided tour for foreign visitors."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

美術館(びじゅつかん)が外国人(がいこくじん)観光客(かんこうきゃく)<b>向(む)け</b>の英語(えいご)のツアーを始(はじ)めました。

The museum has aimed a service at a defined group of visitors - a planning
decision, so <code>向(む)け</code>. You could equally write
<code>外国人(がいこくじん)観光客(かんこうきゃく)向(む)けに英語(えいご)のツアーを始(はじ)めました</code>,
using the `に` form before the verb.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この辞書(じしょ)は小学生(しょうがくせい)向(む)けに作(つく)られているが、説明(せつめい)が短(みじか)くて分(わ)かりやすいので、大人(おとな)向(む)けにもいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ …説明(せつめい)が短(みじか)くて分(わ)かりやすいので、大人(おとな)<b>向(む)き</b>でもある。

The first <code>向(む)け</code> is right - the publisher aimed it at primary
school pupils. The second is not, because nobody aimed it at adults; the speaker is
judging that it **happens to suit** them. That is exactly the
<code>向(む)き</code> job.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子(こ)ども<b>向(む)け</b>に書(か)かれた説明(せつめい)
(b) 子(こ)ども<b>のために</b>書(か)かれた説明(せつめい)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a statement about the **target readership**: the writer pitched the level,
vocabulary and length at children. It is neutral and professional, the kind of thing
said about published material, and it implies nothing about the writer's feelings.

(b) is about **benefit**. Somebody wrote it so that children would be helped, and
the phrase carries warmth - it would suit a hospital leaflet or a note written by a
teacher for their own class. In practice (b) also allows the reader and the
beneficiary to differ: an explanation written *for the sake of* children might in
fact be addressed to their parents, which (a) rules out.

</details>
