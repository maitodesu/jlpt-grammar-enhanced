# N3 - Word endings that describe a state


---

# ～っぽい

**Meaning** · has the quality of X; comes across as X-ish

`～っぽい` says that something *gives off* the character of the word it attaches to,
without necessarily being it. Attached to a verb stem it names a personal
disposition - the kind of person who keeps doing that thing. Both uses lean mildly
critical: `～っぽい` is what you reach for when a thing is X in a way you would
rather it were not.

## Formation

Whatever it attaches to, the result is an **い-adjective** and conjugates like one:
水(みず)っぽい → 水(みず)っぽくない → 水(みず)っぽかった → 水(みず)っぽくて.
There is no `だ` after it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + っぽい | 水(みず) → 水(みず)っぽい |
| Noun (person type) | Noun + っぽい | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)っぽい |
| い-adjective (stem, drop い) | Stem + っぽい | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)っぽい |
| Colour word (stem) | Stem + っぽい | 白(しろ)い → 白(しろ)っぽい |
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + っぽい | 忘(わす)れます → 忘(わす)れっぽい |
| Result before a noun | っぽい + Noun | 子供(こども)っぽい話(はなし) |
| Result as an adverb | っぽく + Verb | 安(やす)っぽく見(み)える |
| **Casual speech only** - plain form + っぽい | Verb/Noun/Adj plain + っぽい | 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るっぽい（"looks like it'll rain"） |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is **not** a suffix you can bolt onto any noun you like. The
everyday stock is short and worth learning as vocabulary:
水(みず)っぽい・油(あぶら)っぽい・湿(しめ)っぽい・ほこりっぽい・安(やす)っぽい・荒(あら)っぽい・子供(こども)っぽい・色(いろ)っぽい・白(しろ)っぽい,
and from verbs 忘(わす)れっぽい・飽(あ)きっぽい・怒(おこ)りっぽい・惚(ほ)れっぽい.
The last row of the table is a separate, very productive **slang** pattern that
young speakers use for "seems like"; keep it out of writing and out of exams.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
このスープはちょっと<b>水(みず)っぽい</b>。
This soup is a bit watery.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は<b>忘(わす)れっぽくて</b>、いつも鍵(かぎ)をさがしている。
My little brother is forgetful and is always hunting for his keys.
</pre>

<pre>
そんな<b>子供(こども)っぽい</b>ことを言(い)うのはやめなさい。
Stop saying such childish things.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は年(とし)をとってから、前(まえ)より<b>怒(おこ)りっぽく</b>なった。
My father has got quicker-tempered as he's aged.
</pre>

<pre>
霧(きり)の向(む)こうに<b>白(しろ)っぽい</b>建物(たてもの)がぼんやり見(み)えた。
A whitish building showed faintly through the fog.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)の揚(あ)げ物(もの)は<b>油(あぶら)っぽくて</b>、半分(はんぶん)しか食(た)べられなかった。
Today's fried food was so greasy I could only get through half of it.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)のわりに<b>安(やす)っぽく</b>見(み)えるので、お祝(いわ)いには向(む)かないと思(おも)う。
For the price it looks cheap and nasty, so I don't think it works as a celebration gift.
</pre>

<pre>
姉(あね)は<b>飽(あ)きっぽい</b>ので、今年(ことし)始(はじ)めた習(なら)い事(ごと)はもう三(みっ)つ目(め)だ。
My sister loses interest quickly - the class she started this year is already her third.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The default flavour is **mildly negative**. 子供(こども)っぽい is not "sweetly
childlike", it is "immature"; 安(やす)っぽい is not "affordable", it is
"cheap-looking". If you want the approving version of "properly X", that is a
different suffix (`らしい`), and mixing them up is how learners accidentally insult
people.

Two families escape the negativity. **Colour** words - 
白(しろ)っぽい, 黒(くろ)っぽい, 赤(あか)っぽい - are purely descriptive
"-ish", the polite way to hedge a colour you cannot name exactly. And
色(いろ)っぽい ("alluring") is a compliment, though a fairly forward one.

Register is neutral-to-casual. `～っぽい` in its ordinary sense appears in novels and
newspapers without comment. The **slang** extension does not: attaching っぽい to a
whole plain-form clause - 無理(むり)っぽい, 行(い)くっぽい, 終(お)わったっぽい - 
is conversational shorthand for `みたい` that spread through younger speech and
still reads as loose. It is worth being able to understand; it is not worth writing
in an email to a client.

Because the result is an い-adjective, everything an い-adjective does is available:
水(みず)っぽくないです, 油(あぶら)っぽかったら残(のこ)してください,
忘(わす)れっぽくなる. Learners who mentally file っぽい as "a noun ending" produce
❌<code>子供(こども)っぽいだ</code>, which is the same error as
❌<code>高(たか)いだ</code>.

## Contrast

### ～っぽい vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `らしい` says the thing has the qualities it *ought* to have and
approves; `っぽい` says it has the surface flavour of something it is *not supposed*
to be, and disapproves.

</div>

The two attach to the same nouns and mean nearly opposite things about the speaker's
attitude:

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは<b>男(おとこ)らしい</b>人(ひと)だと、みんなが言(い)う。
Everyone says Tanaka is a manly sort of person.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)し方(かた)が<b>男(おとこ)っぽい</b>とよく言(い)われるが、気(き)にしていない。
People often tell me I talk in a blokey way, but it doesn't bother me.
</pre>

The clearest test is to apply both to the *same* subject. A child described as
子供(こども)らしい is behaving exactly as a child should - lively, honest,
unaffected. An adult described as 子供(こども)っぽい is behaving as a child
would, and should not be.

<pre>
大人(おとな)なのに<b>子供(こども)っぽい</b>ところが、彼(かれ)の欠点(けってん)だ。
His weakness is that he's childish for a grown man.
</pre>

Note that `らしい` has a second, unrelated job - hearsay, "apparently" - attached to
a whole clause. `っぽい` never carries hearsay, only quality.

### ～っぽい vs ～みたい / ～のよう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `みたい` compares one thing to *another thing*; `っぽい` reports
a quality the thing has *in itself*.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は先生(せんせい)<b>みたい</b>だ。
He's like a teacher. (he resembles one - maybe he isn't one)
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)し方(かた)が先生(せんせい)<b>っぽい</b>ね。
You've got a rather teacherly way of talking.
</pre>

`みたい` makes a comparison you could spell out ("like a teacher does"). `っぽい`
picks out an attribute and hangs it on the subject directly, which is why it works
on materials and textures where a comparison would be strange:
<code>水(みず)っぽいスープ</code> is a soup that *is* thin, not a soup that
resembles water. ❌<code>水(みず)みたいなスープ</code> would be an exaggeration
about a completely different thing.

The one place they overlap is the slang use, where 雨(あめ)っぽい and
雨(あめ)みたい both mean "looks like rain" - and there `みたい` is the safe
choice in any register above chatting with friends.

### ～っぽい vs ～やすい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - verb-stem + `っぽい` describes a **person's standing
disposition**; verb-stem + `やすい` describes how **easy the action is**, for anyone.

</div>

`やすい` was covered at N4 as "easy to do", and it does double duty as "prone to
happen". That second sense is what collides with `っぽい`:

<pre>
母(はは)は<b>忘(わす)れっぽい</b>ので、買(か)い物(もの)メモを渡(わた)している。
My mother is forgetful, so I hand her a shopping list.
</pre>

<pre>
このパスワードは長(なが)すぎて<b>忘(わす)れやすい</b>。
This password is so long it's easy to forget.
</pre>

The first is a claim about my mother. The second is a claim about the password - 
anybody would forget it. Swap them and both sentences go wrong: a password does not
have a personality, and ❌<code>母(はは)は忘(わす)れやすい</code> sounds like my
mother is an object that is easily mislaid.

Only a handful of verbs take っぽい at all
（忘(わす)れる・飽(あ)きる・怒(おこ)る・惚(ほ)れる）, and every one of them
names a temperament. `やすい` attaches to almost any verb.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 忘(わす)れる<b>っぽい</b>（meaning "forgetful"） → ✅ 忘(わす)れ<b>っぽい</b>
  With verbs, `っぽい` takes the ます-stem. <code>忘(わす)れるっぽい</code> is not
  ungrammatical, but it is the slang "seems like they'll forget" - a different
  sentence.

- ❌ この人(ひと)は子供(こども)っぽい<b>だ</b>。 → ✅ この人(ひと)は子供(こども)っぽい。
  The result is an い-adjective. No `だ`, and the polite form is
  <code>子供(こども)っぽいです</code>.

- ❌ 白(しろ)い<b>っぽい</b>服(ふく) → ✅ 白(しろ)<b>っぽい</b>服(ふく)
  With an い-adjective, the final `い` drops before っぽい - the same trim you make
  before `さ` or `すぎる`.

- ❌ お父(とう)さんは男(おとこ)っぽくて尊敬(そんけい)しています。 → ✅ お父(とう)さんは男(おとこ)らしくて尊敬(そんけい)しています。
  Praise wants `らしい`. `っぽい` would read as "he's a bit of a lad", which is not
  what you meant to say about someone's father.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)はほこり<b>っぽいだらけ</b>だ。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)はほこり<b>っぽい</b>。
  Two different suffixes cannot stack. Pick one: っぽい for the stuffy feel of the
  air, だらけ for visible dust lying on everything.

## Practice

**1.** このみそ汁(しる)、お湯(ゆ)を入(い)れすぎて水(みず)____なってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

水(みず)<b>っぽく</b>なってしまった。

`っぽい` builds an い-adjective, so before <code>なる</code> it takes the adverbial
form <code>っぽく</code> - exactly like <code>安(やす)くなる</code>. The sentence
complains that the soup has gone thin and watery.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 妹(いもうと)は（飽(あ)きっぽい・飽(あ)きやすい）ので、どの楽器(がっき)も長(なが)く続(つづ)かない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

妹(いもうと)は<b>飽(あ)きっぽい</b>ので、どの楽器(がっき)も長(なが)く続(つづ)かない。

The sentence is about my sister's temperament, and a standing personal disposition
is `っぽい` territory. <code>飽(あ)きやすい</code> would describe something that
*is easily got bored of* - a property of the instrument, not of the player.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The room smelled dusty, so I opened the window."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部屋(へや)がほこり<b>っぽかった</b>ので、窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。

<code>ほこりっぽい</code> is the standing expression for air that feels dusty and
stale. The past tense is the ordinary い-adjective past, <code>っぽかった</code>.
<code>ほこりだらけ</code> would be a different complaint - dust you can see lying on
the furniture.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新入生(しんにゅうせい)のスピーチは元気(げんき)で、とても学生(がくせい)っぽくてよかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新入生(しんにゅうせい)のスピーチは元気(げんき)で、とても学生(がくせい)らしくてよかった。

The speaker is praising the speech, and `っぽい` cannot carry praise - 
<code>学生(がくせい)っぽい</code> would suggest the speech was amateurish. When
the point is that something has the qualities it should have, use `らしい`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は大人(おとな)<b>っぽい</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)は大人(おとな)<b>らしい</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says he gives off a grown-up air without actually being one - the natural
reading is that he is young, and that his manner, clothes or voice are more adult
than his age. It is often said of teenagers, and can be admiring or faintly
uneasy.

(b) says he is an adult and behaves as an adult properly should: responsible,
measured, the way the role is supposed to be filled. It is straightforwardly
approving, and it presupposes that he really is one.

</details>

# ～ぎみ

**Meaning** · showing a slight touch of; a bit on the ～ side

`～ぎみ` (written 気味(きみ) when it stands alone, voiced to ぎみ as a suffix)
reports that something is **a little way** toward a state, right now. Not fully
there - just far enough that you can see it. It is the word for a cold you have not
properly caught yet and a train that is running four minutes behind.

## Formation

The result is a **noun**. It predicates with だ/です, modifies a following noun with
`の`, and takes `な` before `ので` and `のに` exactly as any noun does.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ぎみ | 風邪(かぜ) → 風邪(かぜ)ぎみ |
| Noun (する-verb noun) | Noun + ぎみ | 緊張(きんちょう) → 緊張(きんちょう)ぎみ |
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + ぎみ | 太(ふと)ります → 太(ふと)りぎみ |
| Predicate | ～ぎみ + だ / です | 疲(つか)れぎみです |
| Before a noun | ～ぎみ + の + Noun | 疲(つか)れぎみの顔(かお) |
| Before ので・のに | ～ぎみ + な + ので | 遅(おく)れぎみなので |
| As an adverb | ～ぎみ + に + Verb | 焦(あせ)りぎみに話(はな)す |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading is ぎみ, with rendaku. The unvoiced 気味(きみ) is a
separate word meaning "sensation", and it survives in
<code>気味(きみ)が悪(わる)い</code> - "creepy, unsettling" - which has nothing
to do with this suffix. Say きみわるい for that and ぎみ for this.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)はちょっと風邪(かぜ)<b>ぎみ</b>です。
I've got a bit of a cold today.
</pre>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)、少(すこ)し太(ふと)り<b>ぎみ</b>だ。
I've put on a little weight lately.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は予定(よてい)より遅(おく)れ<b>ぎみ</b>で進(すす)んでいる。
The meeting is running slightly behind schedule.
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れ<b>ぎみ</b>の顔(かお)をしていたので、早(はや)く帰(かえ)るように言(い)った。
He looked a bit worn out, so I told him to go home early.
</pre>

<pre>
緊張(きんちょう)<b>ぎみ</b>の声(こえ)で、新人(しんじん)は自己紹介(じこしょうかい)を始(はじ)めた。
In a slightly nervous voice, the new employee began her self-introduction.
</pre>

<pre>
このところ売(う)り上(あ)げが下(さ)がり<b>ぎみ</b>なので、来月(らいげつ)から広告(こうこく)を増(ふ)やします。
Sales have been trending a little down lately, so we'll step up the advertising from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は受験(じゅけん)が近(ちか)づいてから寝不足(ねぶそく)<b>ぎみ</b>で、朝(あさ)なかなか起(お)きられない。
Since the entrance exams got close my son has been a bit short of sleep and struggles to get up.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は焦(あせ)り<b>ぎみ</b>に話(はな)していたが、結局(けっきょく)結論(けつろん)は出(で)なかった。
The manager spoke with a hint of impatience, but in the end nothing was decided.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～ぎみ` almost always points **away from normal in an unwelcome direction**. Health
that is slipping, a schedule that is slipping, a number that is slipping. You will
not hear ❌<code>元気(げんき)ぎみ</code> or ❌<code>上手(じょうず)ぎみ</code>:
there is no such thing as being slightly too well.

It is also about **degree, not frequency**. 風邪(かぜ)ぎみ does not mean "I often
catch colds"; it means "right now, a small amount of cold". That is why it pairs so
naturally with 少(すこ)し and ちょっと, which reinforce rather than contradict it:
少(すこ)し疲(つか)れぎみです.

The register is neutral and it is entirely at home in business Japanese:
下(さ)がりぎみ, 遅(おく)れぎみ and 伸(の)びぎみ are ordinary vocabulary in a
sales report. In writing it is normally spelled with the kanji 気味(きみ); the
reading of the suffix is still ぎみ.

One social use worth knowing: `～ぎみ` softens. Telling a colleague
疲(つか)れぎみですね is far gentler than 疲(つか)れていますね, because it
presents the observation as slight and provisional rather than as a verdict on how
they look. The same hedging instinct makes 風邪(かぜ)ぎみなので休(やす)みます a
polite way to call in sick without claiming to be seriously ill.

## Contrast

### ～ぎみ vs ～がち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ぎみ` is **how much**, `がち` is **how often**. `ぎみ` says one
state is slightly present now; `がち` says an event keeps recurring.

</div>

<pre>
今朝(けさ)の電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れ<b>ぎみ</b>だった。
This morning's train was running a bit late.
</pre>

<pre>
この路線(ろせん)の電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れ<b>がち</b>だ。
Trains on this line tend to run late.
</pre>

The first sentence is about one morning and a small number of minutes. The second
is about a pattern across many mornings and says nothing about how late. Use the
wrong one and you make a claim you did not intend: telling your boss
遅(おく)れがちです about a single delayed delivery accuses the whole operation of
being unreliable.

They also behave differently in a sentence. `ぎみ` builds a noun and takes `の`
before a following noun; `がち` builds a な-adjective and takes `な`:
疲(つか)れぎみ<b>の</b>顔(かお) but 病気(びょうき)がち<b>な</b>子(こ)ども.

### ～ぎみ vs ～っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ぎみ` says a *little of the real thing is present*; `っぽい`
says it *feels like* the thing, whether or not it is.

</div>

The pair that shows this best is a cold, because both are said constantly:

<pre>
昨日(きのう)から風邪(かぜ)<b>ぎみ</b>で、薬(くすり)を飲(の)んでいる。
I've had a touch of a cold since yesterday, so I'm taking medicine.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なん)となく風邪(かぜ)<b>っぽい</b>から、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く寝(ね)る。
I feel like I might be coming down with something, so I'm going to bed early.
</pre>

`ぎみ` states a mild fact: there is a cold, in small quantity. `っぽい` states an
impression: something about how I feel resembles a cold. Which is why
風邪(かぜ)っぽい is what you say before you are sure, and 風邪(かぜ)ぎみ once
you are.

Grammatically they part company completely. `っぽい` produces an い-adjective - 
風邪(かぜ)っぽかった, 風邪(かぜ)っぽくない - while `ぎみ` produces a noun:
風邪(かぜ)ぎみだった, 風邪(かぜ)ぎみではない.

### ～ぎみ vs ちょっと・すこし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ちょっと turns *any* predicate down a notch. `ぎみ` only attaches
to a fixed kind of word, and it carries a judgement that ちょっと does not.

</div>

For a plain matter of degree, an adverb is the natural choice, and `ぎみ` is simply
not available:

<pre>
このコーヒーは<b>ちょっと</b>苦(にが)い。
This coffee is a little bitter.
</pre>

❌<code>苦(にが)いぎみ</code> is impossible - `ぎみ` does not attach to
い-adjectives at all. Nor does it attach to happy states:
❌<code>うれしいぎみ</code>, ❌<code>楽(たの)しみぎみ</code>.

Where both are possible, `ぎみ` adds the sense of a **deviation from where things
should be**:

<pre>
体重(たいじゅう)が増(ふ)え<b>ぎみ</b>なので、夜(よる)は食(た)べないようにしている。
My weight has been creeping up, so I'm trying not to eat at night.
</pre>

<code>体重(たいじゅう)が少(すこ)し増(ふ)えた</code> is a neutral measurement.
<code>増(ふ)えぎみ</code> is a measurement plus a frown. And the two combine
happily: 少(すこ)し増(ふ)えぎみ.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 太(ふと)る<b>ぎみ</b> → ✅ 太(ふと)り<b>ぎみ</b>
  With verbs, `ぎみ` attaches to the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

- ❌ 風邪(かぜ)ぎみ<b>の</b>です。 → ✅ 風邪(かぜ)ぎみ<b>です</b>。
  `の` goes between ～ぎみ and a following **noun**
  （<code>風邪(かぜ)ぎみの学生(がくせい)</code>）, not before です.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は元気(げんき)<b>ぎみ</b>です。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は元気(げんき)です。
  `ぎみ` marks a slide toward something unwanted. There is no polite way to be a
  little bit too healthy.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は学校(がっこう)を休(やす)み<b>ぎみ</b>だ。（meaning "he's often absent"） → ✅ 彼(かれ)は学校(がっこう)を休(やす)み<b>がち</b>だ。
  Repeated absences are frequency, and frequency is `がち`.

- ❌ かぜきみです。 → ✅ かぜぎみです。
  The suffix voices. きみ unvoiced belongs to
  <code>気味(きみ)が悪(わる)い</code>, an unrelated expression.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)あまり寝(ね)ていないので、今日(きょう)は疲(つか)れ____です。

# ～がち

**Meaning** · has a habit of ～ing; keeps ending up that way

`～がち` says that something happens **often, and shouldn't**. It is not a
measurement of degree and not a statement about one occasion - it is a complaint
about a pattern, whether the pattern belongs to a person, a machine or the weather.

## Formation

The result is a **な-adjective**: `がちだ` as a predicate, `がちな` before a noun,
`がちに` before a verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + がち | 忘(わす)れます → 忘(わす)れがち |
| Verb (ます-stem of a する-verb) | Noun + し + がち | 無理(むり)します → 無理(むり)しがち |
| Noun (short fixed list) | Noun + がち | 病気(びょうき) → 病気(びょうき)がち |
| Predicate | ～がち + だ / です | 遅(おく)れがちです |
| Before a noun | ～がち + な + Noun | 見落(みお)としがちな点(てん) |
| Before a verb | ～がち + に + Verb | 遠慮(えんりょ)がちに話(はな)す |
| Common set phrase | ～がち + になる | 休(やす)みがちになる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with verbs, `がち` is broadly productive. With **nouns** it is not: the
usable list is essentially 病気(びょうき)がち, 曇(くも)りがち,
留守(るす)がち, 遠慮(えんりょ)がち, 伏(ふ)し目(め)がち, and the
lexicalised ありがち ("all too common"). Inventing new noun combinations - ❌
<code>元気(げんき)がち</code>, ❌<code>雨(あめ)がち</code> - does not work.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)は運動不足(うんどうぶそく)に<b>なりがち</b>だ。
In winter you end up short of exercise.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は小(ちい)さいころ<b>病気(びょうき)がち</b>でした。
My little brother was ill a lot when he was small.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は<b>曇(くも)りがち</b>で、洗(あら)ったシーツがなかなか乾(かわ)かない。
It's cloudy on and off today, and the sheets I washed just won't dry.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しいと、朝(あさ)ごはんを<b>抜(ぬ)きがち</b>になる。
When you're busy you get into the habit of skipping breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>遠慮(えんりょ)がちに</b>手(て)を挙(あ)げて、小(ちい)さな声(こえ)で質問(しつもん)した。
He raised his hand hesitantly and asked his question in a small voice.
</pre>

<pre>
メールの返事(へんじ)を後回(あとまわ)しにすると、そのまま<b>忘(わす)れがち</b>だ。
If you put off replying to an email, you tend to forget about it altogether.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)の細(こま)かい注意(ちゅうい)は<b>見落(みお)としがちな</b>ところなので、声(こえ)に出(だ)して読(よ)んでください。
The small print in the manual is the sort of thing people miss, so read it aloud.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしを始(はじ)めてから、野菜(やさい)が足(た)りなく<b>なりがち</b>で、自分(じぶん)でも反省(はんせい)している。
Since I started living alone I keep ending up short on vegetables, and I'm not proud of it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The judgement built into `がち` is the thing to remember. Every ordinary use points
at something the speaker would rather did not keep happening: illness, absence,
oversight, delay, cloud. Turn it on a good outcome and the sentence collapses - 
❌<code>成功(せいこう)しがち</code>, ❌<code>元気(げんき)になりがち</code> - not
because the grammar breaks but because the word carries a frown that has nowhere to
land.

The frown can be aimed at yourself, and often is: 私(わたし)も
無理(むり)しがちなので気(き)をつけます is a standard, slightly rueful thing to
say. It can also be aimed at people in general, which is where the pattern earns its
keep in writing - ～と考(かんが)えがちだが, "we tend to think that, but", is a
staple opening move in essays and newspaper columns.

Register is neutral. `がち` appears in conversation, in business email and in print
without shifting tone. Two combinations have drifted into fixed vocabulary and no
longer feel like grammar at all: 遠慮(えんりょ)がち ("diffident, hanging back")
and ありがち ("the sort of thing that happens all the time" - usually said of a
predictable plot or a familiar mistake).

Finally, `がち` needs **repetition**. It cannot describe one event. Reporting a
single late train as 遅(おく)れがちだった is wrong in the same way that saying
"the train tends to be late" about one specific morning is wrong in English.

## Contrast

### ～がち vs ～やすい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `やすい` is about **how easily** something can happen, and can
be good or bad. `がち` is about **how often** it does happen, and is always bad.

</div>

N4 taught `やすい` as "easy to do". Its second sense, "liable to", is what overlaps
here - and the two still split cleanly:

<pre>
この皿(さら)は薄(うす)いので<b>割(わ)れやすい</b>。
These plates are thin, so they break easily.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)にまかせると、皿(さら)を<b>割(わ)りがち</b>だ。
If I leave it to my son, he's forever breaking plates.
</pre>

The first is a property of the plates that would hold even if nobody ever broke one.
The second counts actual breakages and blames somebody for them. This is also why
`やすい` is happy in praise - 使(つか)いやすい, 分(わ)かりやすい - and `がち`
never is.

They also build different words: `やすい` makes an い-adjective
（割(わ)れやすかった, 割(わ)れやすくない）, `がち` a な-adjective
（割(わ)りがちだった, 割(わ)りがちな人(ひと)）.

### ～がち vs ～ぎみ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がち` counts occasions; `ぎみ` measures amount. If you could
answer with "three times a week", it is `がち`. If you could answer with "just a
little", it is `ぎみ`.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は去年(きょねん)から会社(かいしゃ)を<b>休(やす)みがち</b>だ。
He's been off work a lot since last year.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は少(すこ)し<b>疲(つか)れぎみ</b>なので、残業(ざんぎょう)はやめておく。
I'm a bit worn out today, so I'll skip the overtime.
</pre>

Some verbs take both, and then the difference is unmistakable.
太(ふと)りぎみ means I am currently a couple of kilos heavier than I should be;
太(ふと)りがち means I am the kind of person who puts weight on again and again.
Only one of those is a comment about a body shape, and only one is a comment about a
life.

### ～がち vs ことが多(おお)い

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことが多(おお)い</code> reports frequency as a neutral
fact. `がち` reports the same frequency and disapproves of it.

</div>

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)は山(やま)に雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)る<b>ことが多(おお)い</b>。
It often snows in the mountains in winter.
</pre>

That sentence is a piece of information. Nothing is wrong with the snow. Swap in
`がち` and you have implied that the snow is a nuisance - fine if you are a driver,
odd in a geography textbook.

The neutral phrase is therefore the safe default for objective writing, and it takes
a whole clause rather than a stem, so it works with verbs `がち` cannot reach:
<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ることが多(おお)い</code> is fine, while
❌<code>雨(あめ)がちだ</code> is not Japanese.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 遅(おく)れる<b>がち</b> → ✅ 遅(おく)れ<b>がち</b>
  `がち` takes the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 病気(びょうき)がち<b>子(こ)ども</b> → ✅ 病気(びょうき)がち<b>な</b>子(こ)ども
  The result is a な-adjective, so it needs `な` before a noun.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)の電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れ<b>がち</b>だった。（about one morning） → ✅ 今日(きょう)の電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れていた。
  `がち` requires a repeated pattern. One late train is just a late train.

- ❌ 最近(さいきん)よく眠(ねむ)れて、元気(げんき)になり<b>がち</b>です。 → ✅ 最近(さいきん)よく眠(ねむ)れて、元気(げんき)になってきました。
  `がち` only fits tendencies the speaker regrets. Nobody regrets getting healthier.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)<b>がち</b>な天気(てんき) → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>がち</b>な天気(てんき)
  Only a handful of nouns take `がち` directly. 曇(くも)り is on the list;
  雨(あめ) is not, so go through the verb.

# ～だらけ - covered all over

**Meaning** · with X all over it; caked in X

`～だらけ` attaches to a noun and says that the stuff named is **all over the
surface** of something, in an amount nobody wanted. Mud on a coat, dust on a shelf,
scratches on a table. The word is a complaint before it is a description.

## Formation

`だらけ` attaches to a **bare noun** - no particle in front of it - and the result
is itself a **noun**. It predicates with だ/です, modifies a following noun with
`の`, and pairs constantly with なる.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だらけ | 泥(どろ) → 泥(どろ)だらけ |
| Predicate | ～だらけ + だ / です | 机(つくえ)がほこりだらけだ |
| Before a noun | ～だらけ + の + Noun | 泥(どろ)だらけの靴(くつ) |
| Becoming that way | ～だらけ + に + なる | 砂(すな)だらけになる |
| Negation | ～だらけ + では ない | 傷(きず)だらけではない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `だらけ` never attaches to a verb or an adjective, and never takes `な`
before a noun. It is also **not** freely productive in the way it looks: the noun
has to name something you would rather were not there. 泥(どろ)・砂(すな)・血(ち)・ほこり・傷(きず)・毛(け)・しわ
are all natural; ❌<code>花(はな)だらけ</code> and ❌<code>光(ひかり)だらけ</code>
are not, unless you are being pointedly sarcastic.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
子(こ)どもの服(ふく)が<b>泥(どろ)だらけ</b>になった。
The kids' clothes ended up covered in mud.
</pre>

<pre>
棚(たな)の上(うえ)が<b>ほこりだらけ</b>だ。
The top of the shelf is thick with dust.
</pre>

<pre>
転(ころ)んで、手(て)が<b>血(ち)だらけ</b>になってしまった。
I fell over and my hands ended up covered in blood.
</pre>

<pre>
海(うみ)から上(あ)がったら、体中(からだじゅう)<b>砂(すな)だらけ</b>だった。
When I came out of the sea I was covered in sand from head to foot.
</pre>

<pre>
猫(ねこ)を抱(だ)き上(あ)げたら、コートが<b>毛(け)だらけ</b>になった。
I picked the cat up and my coat ended up covered in fur.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)が終(お)わったばかりの部屋(へや)は、窓(まど)わくまで白(しろ)い<b>粉(こな)だらけ</b>だった。
The room where the work had just finished was covered in white dust, right up to the window frames.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)の手(て)は<b>傷(きず)だらけ</b>で、長(なが)く畑(はたけ)仕事(しごと)をしてきたことがすぐに分(わ)かった。
My grandfather's hands were covered in scars - you could tell at once he'd worked the fields for years.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)の中(なか)を走(はし)ってきたらしく、弟(おとうと)は靴(くつ)も鞄(かばん)も<b>泥(どろ)だらけ</b>のまま玄関(げんかん)に立(た)っていた。
He must have run through the rain - my brother was standing in the entryway with his shoes and bag still caked in mud.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`だらけ` is **evaluative**. Even when the fact reported is neutral, the word supplies
the reaction: dismay, disgust, or a weary "look at the state of this". A parent says
泥(どろ)だらけ about a child's trousers with the sigh already in it.

It is also **hyperbolic**. 傷(きず)だらけ does not mean every square centimetre is
scarred; it means enough of them that the scars are what you notice. Nobody counts.

Register is ordinary spoken and written Japanese. It is not slangy, but it is blunt,
so in a formal report or a polite complaint to a company you would soften it - 
汚(よご)れがひどい状態(じょうたい)でした rather than
泥(どろ)だらけでした. Between friends and inside a family it is completely
standard.

Note the shape of the sentence: what is covered is the **subject**, and what covers
it goes in front of だらけ with nothing between.
机(つくえ)<b>が</b>ほこりだらけだ - the desk is the topic of the complaint, the
dust is the incriminating substance. Learners often reverse this and produce
❌<code>ほこりが机(つくえ)だらけだ</code>, which says the dust is covered in desks.

## Contrast

### ～だらけ vs ～まみれ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まみれ` is for a **liquid or a fine powder smeared over a
surface**. `だらけ` is for **many separate things scattered all over**, and it also
reaches abstract nouns, which `まみれ` cannot.

</div>

Where the substance is genuinely a coating, both work, and `まみれ` is the more
vivid, slightly more literary of the two:

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)から出(で)てきた作業員(さぎょういん)は、顔(かお)まで<b>油(あぶら)まみれ</b>だった。
The worker who came out of the factory was covered in oil right up to his face.
</pre>

<pre>
台所(だいどころ)の床(ゆか)が<b>油(あぶら)だらけ</b>で、滑(すべ)りそうだった。
The kitchen floor was greasy all over and felt like it would be slippery.
</pre>

The line shows itself at the edges. Sweat forms a film, so
汗(あせ)まみれ is normal and ❌<code>汗(あせ)だらけ</code> is odd. Scars are
countable marks, so 傷(きず)だらけ is normal and ❌<code>傷(きず)まみれ</code>
is odd. Dust, mud and blood sit in the middle and take either - with
血(ち)まみれ sounding like a crime scene and 血(ち)だらけ sounding like a
kitchen accident.

`まみれ` extends metaphorically to only a very small set of fixed expressions
（借金(しゃっきん)まみれ, 罪(つみ)にまみれる）, always with the image of being
smeared. It does not take scattered abstract defects:
❌<code>間違(まちが)いまみれ</code>, ❌<code>穴(あな)まみれ</code>.

### ～だらけ vs ～っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だらけ` says the stuff is **physically there, all over it**.
`っぽい` says the thing merely **has the quality** of the stuff.

</div>

The clearest pair is dust, because both are everyday complaints about a room:

<pre>
使(つか)っていない部屋(へや)は<b>ほこりだらけ</b>で、まず掃除(そうじ)から始(はじ)めた。
The unused room was thick with dust, so we started with the cleaning.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を閉(し)めきっていたので、部屋(へや)が<b>ほこりっぽくて</b>息苦(いきぐる)しい。
We'd kept the windows shut, so the room feels dusty and stuffy.
</pre>

The first is about what is lying on the surfaces; you could photograph it. The
second is about how the air feels; you could not. Grammatically they are unrelated
too - `だらけ` builds a noun, `っぽい` an い-adjective - so the endings never look
alike: ほこりだらけだった against ほこりっぽかった.

### ～だらけ vs ～がついている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～がついている</code> reports that some of the stuff is on
there. `だらけ` reports that it is *everywhere* on there. The difference is amount,
and with it, tone.

</div>

<pre>
シャツに醤油(しょうゆ)が<b>ついている</b>よ。
You've got soy sauce on your shirt.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)のあと、ユニフォームは<b>泥(どろ)だらけ</b>だった。
After the match the uniform was caked in mud.
</pre>

The first is a helpful heads-up about one spot. The second is a verdict on the whole
garment. This is why <code>ついている</code> is the polite way to tell a stranger
about a stain and `だらけ` is not: pointing at someone's jacket and saying
泥(どろ)だらけですよ makes it sound as though they had rolled in a field.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 泥(どろ)<b>が</b>だらけ → ✅ 泥(どろ)だらけ
  Nothing goes between the noun and `だらけ` - no `が`, no `で`, no `の`.

- ❌ 泥(どろ)だらけ<b>な</b>靴(くつ) → ✅ 泥(どろ)だらけ<b>の</b>靴(くつ)
  The result is a noun, not a な-adjective, so it links with `の`.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が汚(よご)れている<b>だらけ</b>だ。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)がごみだらけだ。
  `だらけ` attaches only to nouns. A verb or an adjective in front of it is not
  possible.

- ❌ 汗(あせ)<b>だらけ</b>になって走(はし)っていた → ✅ 汗(あせ)<b>まみれ</b>になって走(はし)っていた
  Sweat is a film over the skin, not a scatter of separate marks, so it takes
  `まみれ`. <code>汗(あせ)びっしょり</code> is the everyday alternative.

- ❌ 庭(にわ)が花(はな)<b>だらけ</b>できれいだ。 → ✅ 庭(にわ)が花(はな)でいっぱいできれいだ。
  `だらけ` cannot be used approvingly. Saying it about flowers turns a pretty garden
  into an infestation.

## Practice

**1.** 自転車(じてんしゃ)で転(ころ)んで、ズボンが砂(すな)____になってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ズボンが砂(すな)<b>だらけ</b>になってしまった。

Sand is a scatter of separate grains all over the surface, so `だらけ`, and it
attaches straight to the bare noun. <code>～だらけになる</code> is the standard way
to say something *ended up* in that state.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 試合(しあい)が終(お)わったとき、選手(せんしゅ)たちは（汗(あせ)だらけ・汗(あせ)まみれ）だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

選手(せんしゅ)たちは<b>汗(あせ)まみれ</b>だった。

Sweat is a liquid coating the skin, and liquids take `まみれ`. `だらけ` wants
countable things or a layer of loose material, which is why
<code>泥(どろ)だらけ</code> is fine but <code>汗(あせ)だらけ</code> sounds
wrong to a native ear.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Don't come into the house with muddy shoes."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

泥(どろ)<b>だらけ</b>の靴(くつ)で家(いえ)に入(はい)らないで。

Before a noun, `だらけ` links with `の`, giving
<code>泥(どろ)だらけの靴(くつ)</code>. The English adjective "muddy" has no
single-word Japanese equivalent here - the noun-plus-suffix construction does the
whole job.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 棚(たな)の奥(おく)から、ほこりだらけな箱(はこ)が出(で)てきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 棚(たな)の奥(おく)から、ほこりだらけ<b>の</b>箱(はこ)が出(で)てきた。

`だらけ` produces a noun, and a noun modifying another noun links with `の`. The `な`
you may be thinking of belongs before ので and のに
（<code>ほこりだらけなので</code>）, which is the same `な` any noun takes there.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 台所(だいどころ)の窓(まど)に油(あぶら)がついている。
(b) 台所(だいどころ)の窓(まど)が油(あぶら)<b>だらけ</b>だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply notes that there is grease on the window - perhaps one patch, perhaps a
thin film. It is a neutral observation and could be the first step in offering to
clean it.

(b) says the grease is all over the glass and adds the speaker's reaction: this has
been neglected, and it is unpleasant. Said in someone else's kitchen it would land
as criticism, which (a) would not.

</details>

# ～だらけ - full of

**Meaning** · riddled with X; nothing but X wherever you look

This is the same suffix used of things you can **count** rather than a substance
smeared over a surface: mistakes in an essay, holes in a jumper, tourists in a
street, debts on a balance sheet. The tone is unchanged - `だらけ` never reports
abundance neutrally. It reports abundance and objects to it.

## Formation

Identical to the physical sense: a **bare noun** in front, and a **noun** as the
result.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Countable noun | Noun + だらけ | 間違(まちが)い → 間違(まちが)いだらけ |
| Abstract noun | Noun + だらけ | 借金(しゃっきん) → 借金(しゃっきん)だらけ |
| Verb-derived noun (ます-stem) | ます-stem + だらけ | 間違(まちが)えます → 間違(まちが)いだらけ |
| Predicate | ～だらけ + だ / です | この計画(けいかく)は問題(もんだい)だらけだ |
| Before a noun | ～だらけ + の + Noun | 穴(あな)だらけの説明(せつめい) |
| Becoming that way | ～だらけ + に + なる | 雑草(ざっそう)だらけになる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun still has to name something unwelcome. That includes people when
you resent them being there - 観光客(かんこうきゃく)だらけ,
子(こ)どもだらけ - but not things you are glad of. ❌<code>いい考(かんが)えだらけ</code>
is not a compliment; it is not anything.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この作文(さくぶん)は<b>間違(まちが)いだらけ</b>だ。
This essay is full of mistakes.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が<b>ごみだらけ</b>で、友(とも)だちを呼(よ)べない。
My room is full of rubbish, so I can't have friends over.
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の京都(きょうと)は<b>観光客(かんこうきゃく)だらけ</b>だった。
Kyoto in summer was heaving with tourists.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)は<b>借金(しゃっきん)だらけ</b>で、家族(かぞく)にも相談(そうだん)できずにいるらしい。
My brother is up to his neck in debt and apparently can't even bring himself to talk to the family.
</pre>

<pre>
古(ふる)いセーターが<b>穴(あな)だらけ</b>になっていたので、とうとう捨(す)てた。
The old sweater had got so full of holes that I finally threw it out.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)は分(わ)かりやすかったが、計画(けいかく)自体(じたい)は<b>問題(もんだい)だらけ</b>だと思(おも)う。
The explanation was clear, but the plan itself strikes me as riddled with problems.
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の終(お)わりに山小屋(やまごや)へ行(い)ったら、窓(まど)の内側(うちがわ)が<b>虫(むし)だらけ</b>で、中(なか)に入(はい)る気(き)になれなかった。
When we got to the mountain hut at the end of summer the inside of the windows was crawling with insects, and I couldn't face going in.
</pre>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)手(て)を入(い)れていない庭(にわ)は<b>雑草(ざっそう)だらけ</b>で、どこから片(かた)づければいいのか分(わ)からない。
The garden hasn't been touched for a year and is choked with weeds; I've no idea where to start.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This sense of `だらけ` is a **verdict**. Calling a document
間違(まちが)いだらけ is not the same as saying it contains errors; it is saying
the document is not worth reading in its present state. Aimed at a colleague's work
it is genuinely rude, and Japanese office language has softer replacements - 
間違(まちが)いが多(おお)いようです, 少(すこ)し直(なお)すところがあります.
Aimed at your own work it is a normal, slightly theatrical form of apology.

Like the physical sense it is **hyperbolic**: 穴(あな)だらけ needs three or four
holes, not fifty. And it is **saturating**, not gradable, so it does not take degree
adverbs. ❌<code>とても間違(まちが)いだらけ</code> is impossible for the same reason
"very full of holes" is odd in English - the phrase already means "as many as
matters".

One extension worth knowing because it looks like an exception: `まみれ`, which
otherwise refuses abstract nouns, does take 借金(しゃっきん) - 
借金(しゃっきん)まみれ is as common as 借金(しゃっきん)だらけ. The image there
is of debt smeared over a person like grease, which is why that one combination
works and ❌<code>間違(まちが)いまみれ</code> does not.

Register: everyday spoken and written Japanese, blunt but not vulgar. Headlines and
opinion columns use it freely; contracts and formal reports do not.

## Contrast

### ～だらけ vs ～ばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばかり` says **nothing but X, to the exclusion of other
things**. `だらけ` says **X everywhere you look**, and adds a complaint.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は肉(にく)<b>ばかり</b>食(た)べて、野菜(やさい)に手(て)をつけない。
My brother eats nothing but meat and won't touch the vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)の中(なか)が古(ふる)い食(た)べ物(もの)<b>だらけ</b>で、捨(す)てるところから始(はじ)めた。
The fridge was full of old food, so I started by throwing things out.
</pre>

`ばかり` sets up a contrast with what is *absent*, and it is neutral about whether
that is good. You can say 本(ほん)ばかり読(よ)んでいる about a studious child
admiringly. `だらけ` has no such flexibility, and it never comments on absence - only
on how much of the offending thing is present.

They also fit differently into a sentence. `ばかり` follows verbs, particles and
quantities as well as nouns（見(み)てばかりいる, 東京(とうきょう)にばかり）;
`だらけ` follows a bare noun and nothing else.

### ～だらけ vs ～でいっぱい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>でいっぱい</code> reports a container being **full**, with
no judgement, and works for good things. `だらけ` reports a **mess**.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は開演(かいえん)前(まえ)から人(ひと)<b>でいっぱい</b>だった。
The hall was packed with people well before the performance began.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)は人(ひと)<b>だらけ</b>で、前(まえ)に進(すす)めなかった。
The area in front of the station was jammed with people and I couldn't get through.
</pre>

Both describe crowds; only the second is annoyed about it. That is why
<code>でいっぱい</code> is the one that can carry positive nouns - 
幸(しあわ)せでいっぱい, 花(はな)でいっぱいの公園(こうえん) - while `だらけ`
cannot go near them.

Note the particle: <code>でいっぱい</code> takes `で` before it,
`だらけ` takes nothing.

### ～だらけ vs ～が多(おお)い

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>多(おお)い</code> is a gradable, neutral measurement.
`だらけ` is an ungradable verdict. Anywhere you need to be polite or precise, use
<code>多(おお)い</code>.

</div>

<pre>
この報告書(ほうこくしょ)は数字(すうじ)の間違(まちが)いが<b>少(すこ)し多(おお)い</b>ようです。
There seem to be rather a lot of numerical errors in this report.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で読(よ)み返(かえ)したら、一(いち)枚目(まいめ)から<b>間違(まちが)いだらけ</b>だった。
When I read it back myself, it was riddled with mistakes from page one.
</pre>

The first sentence can be handed to the person who wrote the report. The second
cannot - but it is exactly right for talking about your own draft. The grammatical
consequence of the difference is that <code>多(おお)い</code> takes degree words
（少(すこ)し多(おお)い, とても多(おお)い）and `だらけ` refuses them.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 間違(まちが)い<b>が</b>だらけの作文(さくぶん) → ✅ 間違(まちが)いだらけの作文(さくぶん)
  No particle before `だらけ`, and `の` after it when a noun follows.

- ❌ この町(まち)は静(しず)か<b>だらけ</b>だ。 → ✅ この町(まち)はとても静(しず)かだ。
  `だらけ` needs a countable or concrete noun. Adjectives and states cannot go in
  front of it.

- ❌ 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)が<b>とても</b>本(ほん)だらけだ。 → ✅ 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)が本(ほん)だらけだ。
  The suffix already means "saturated", so it takes no degree adverb.

- ❌ 新(あたら)しい公園(こうえん)は花(はな)だらけで、気持(きも)ちがいい。 → ✅ 新(あたら)しい公園(こうえん)は花(はな)でいっぱいで、気持(きも)ちがいい。
  Praise wants <code>でいっぱい</code>. `だらけ` would make the flowers sound like a
  problem the council should deal with.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)のレポートは間違(まちが)いだらけですね。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)のレポート、少(すこ)し直(なお)すところがあるようです。
  Grammatically fine, socially not. Save `だらけ` for your own work, or for things
  rather than people.

## Practice

**1.** 三年(さんねん)使(つか)っていない物置(ものおき)を開(あ)けたら、中(なか)はくもの巣(す)____だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中(なか)はくもの巣(す)<b>だらけ</b>だった。

Cobwebs are countable things scattered everywhere, and they are unwelcome - both
conditions `だらけ` needs. It attaches straight to the noun and takes だった for the
past, because the whole phrase is a noun.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この案(あん)は（問題(もんだい)だらけ・問題(もんだい)でいっぱい）で、このままでは会議(かいぎ)に出(だ)せない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この案(あん)は<b>問題(もんだい)だらけ</b>で、このままでは会議(かいぎ)に出(だ)せない。

<code>でいっぱい</code> describes a container being full and is neutral, so it fits
badly with an abstract defect like <code>問題(もんだい)</code>. `だらけ` supplies
exactly the disapproval the second half of the sentence assumes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The first draft was full of holes, so I rewrote it from scratch."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

最初(さいしょ)の原稿(げんこう)は穴(あな)<b>だらけ</b>だったので、一(いち)から書(か)き直(なお)した。

<code>穴(あな)だらけ</code> works for a physical sweater and, as here, for gaps in
an argument - one of the few abstract nouns that keeps the concrete image. Since the
speaker is criticising their own work, the bluntness is fine.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会場(かいじょう)は子(こ)どもだらけがいて、とてもにぎやかだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会場(かいじょう)は子(こ)どもだらけで、とてもにぎやかだった。

`だらけ` already contains "there are lots of them", so it cannot be followed by
<code>がいる</code>. It behaves as a noun, so it joins the next clause with `で`.
Note that with にぎやか the sentence reads as good-humoured exaggeration rather than
a real complaint - that is about as positive as `だらけ` ever gets.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この辞書(じしょ)は間違(まちが)いが多(おお)い。
(b) この辞書(じしょ)は間違(まちが)い<b>だらけ</b>だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a measured criticism: more errors than you would like, but the dictionary is
still a dictionary. You could follow it with a figure, or with
<code>少(すこ)し</code>, because <code>多(おお)い</code> is a matter of degree.

(b) writes the thing off. It says errors are everywhere in it and, by implication,
that it should not be trusted or bought. It admits no degree word and no polite
softening - which is why you would say it to a friend and not to the publisher.

</details>

# ～まま

**Meaning** · in the same state, unchanged; while still ～

`まま` freezes a state. Something reached a condition, nobody changed it, and
whatever happens next happens **with that condition still in place**. Shoes still on,
light still burning, town still exactly as it was ten years ago.

## Formation

`まま` is a formal noun. What comes in front of it must be in a form that can modify
a noun, which is why verbs use the **た-form** and not the dictionary form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (past) | た-form + まま | 立(た)つ → 立(た)ったまま |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + まま | 食(た)べない → 食(た)べないまま |
| Noun | Noun + の + まま | 昔(むかし) → 昔(むかし)のまま |
| い-adjective | Plain form + まま | 熱(あつ)い → 熱(あつ)いまま |
| な-adjective | Stem + な + まま | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かなまま |
| Demonstrative | この / その / あの + まま | そのまま |
| As a predicate | ～まま + だ / です | 昔(むかし)のままです |
| Deliberately keeping it so | ～まま + に + する / しておく | そのままにしておく |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the た-form here is **not** past tense. <code>立(た)ったまま</code>
does not mean "having stood in the past"; it means the standing-up has happened and
the resulting posture is still current. This is why only verbs that leave a visible
result work well: 着(き)たまま, つけたまま, 開(あ)けたまま. Verbs that leave
nothing behind - ❌<code>食(た)べたまま話(はな)す</code> for "talk while
eating" - produce nonsense.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
靴(くつ)をはいた<b>まま</b>上(あ)がらないでください。
Please don't come up with your shoes on.
</pre>

<pre>
電気(でんき)をつけた<b>まま</b>寝(ね)てしまった。
I fell asleep with the light on.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)は十年(じゅうねん)前(まえ)の<b>まま</b>だ。
This town is exactly as it was ten years ago.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)も言(い)わない<b>まま</b>、彼(かれ)は部屋(へや)を出(で)て行(い)った。
He left the room without ever saying anything.
</pre>

<pre>
スープは熱(あつ)い<b>まま</b>運(はこ)んでください。
Please carry the soup while it's still hot.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)けた<b>まま</b>出(で)かけてしまい、一日中(いちにちじゅう)気(き)になっていた。
I went out having left the window open, and it bothered me all day.
</pre>

<pre>
借(か)りた本(ほん)を読(よ)まない<b>まま</b>返(かえ)すことになりそうだ。
It looks as though I'll end up returning the book I borrowed without having read it.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)の部屋(へや)は亡(な)くなったときの<b>まま</b>にしてあり、机(つくえ)の上(うえ)のめがねも動(うご)かしていない。
My grandmother's room has been left just as it was when she died - even the glasses on her desk haven't been moved.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`まま` itself is completely neutral and register-free. It appears in children's
speech, in polite requests, in legal notices（<code>そのままお待(ま)ちください</code>）
and in literary description without changing colour.

What supplies the colour is the **combination**. When the frozen state is one that
ought to have been undone, the sentence sounds careless: 靴(くつ)をはいたまま,
電気(でんき)をつけたまま, 化粧(けしょう)をしたまま寝(ね)る. When the
frozen state is one being deliberately preserved, it sounds respectful or careful:
昔(むかし)のまま, 熱(あつ)いまま. The suffix is only holding the state still;
the judgement comes from what the state is.

Three connected forms are worth separating. Bare `まま` and `ままで` are largely
interchangeable in the adverbial slot - 立(た)ったまま食(た)べる and
立(た)ったままで食(た)べる are both ordinary, with `で` a shade more explicit.
`ままに` is different: it is literary, and it also heads a small family of fixed
expressions about following someone else's lead - 
言(い)われるままに書類(しょるい)にサインした, "I signed the papers just as I
was told to."

Finally, そのまま is so common that it has become a word in its own right: "as is,
leave it, don't touch it". <code>そのままでいいです</code> ("it's fine like that")
is one of the most useful short sentences in the language.

## Contrast

### ～まま vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` joins **two actions** happening at once. `まま` holds
**one state** in place while an action happens. If both halves are things you *do*,
you want `ながら`.

</div>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>ながら</b>宿題(しゅくだい)をしている。
He's doing his homework while listening to music.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が込(こ)んでいて、立(た)った<b>まま</b>本(ほん)を読(よ)んだ。
The train was crowded, so I read standing up.
</pre>

Listening and doing homework are two ongoing activities, so `ながら`. Standing is
not an activity you keep performing - you stand up once and then you are standing - 
so it takes `まま`. That is why ❌<code>立(た)ちながら読(よ)む</code> sounds
wrong to a native ear, and why ❌<code>聞(き)いたまま宿題(しゅくだい)をする</code>
does too.

A second, purely grammatical difference: `ながら` needs the **same subject** for both
halves, while `まま` does not care - 
窓(まど)を開(あ)けたまま雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した is fine.

### ～まま vs ～っぱなし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `っぱなし` is `まま` **with blame attached**, and it only
attaches to verb stems. `まま` is neutral and attaches to nouns and adjectives too.

</div>

<pre>
テレビをつけた<b>まま</b>、台所(だいどころ)で洗(あら)い物(もの)をしていた。
I was doing the washing-up in the kitchen with the TV left on.
</pre>

<pre>
テレビを<b>つけっぱなし</b>にして出(で)かけるのはやめてほしい。
I wish you'd stop going out and leaving the TV on.
</pre>

The first simply reports the arrangement; the TV may well have been on for a reason.
The second is a complaint about carelessness, and `っぱなし` is what carries it.

There is one thing `っぱなし` can do that `まま` cannot: describe an action going on
**without a break**. 立(た)ちっぱなしだった means "I was on my feet the whole
time", which is about duration, not about a posture held during some other action.
立(た)ったまま cannot mean that.

### ～ないまま vs ～ないで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ないで</code> says you did B **instead of** A.
<code>ないまま</code> says the un-done state of A **persisted** - usually longer than
it should have.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>ないで</b>家(いえ)を出(で)た。
I left the house without eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)から何(なに)も食(た)べ<b>ないまま</b>、夜(よる)になってしまった。
I got all the way to evening without having eaten anything since morning.
</pre>

The first is about one moment: at the point of leaving, breakfast had not happened.
The second stretches the emptiness across a whole day and invites you to notice how
long it went on. Where a stretch of time is involved - an unread book, an unanswered
letter, an unresolved argument - `ないまま` is the natural choice and carries a
faint regret that <code>ないで</code> does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 立(た)つ<b>まま</b>待(ま)つ → ✅ 立(た)った<b>まま</b>待(ま)つ
  Verbs take the た-form. The dictionary form before `まま` is not possible.

- ❌ 昔(むかし)<b>まま</b>の建物(たてもの) → ✅ 昔(むかし)<b>のまま</b>の建物(たてもの)
  A noun needs `の` to reach `まま`, exactly as it does to reach any other noun.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>まま</b>だった → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>なまま</b>だった
  A な-adjective keeps its `な`.

- ❌ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べた<b>まま</b>話(はな)さないでください。（meaning "don't talk while eating"） → ✅ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べ<b>ながら</b>話(はな)さないでください。
  Eating is an ongoing action, not a state left behind, so it takes `ながら`. The
  た-form version would mean "don't talk in the state of having eaten", which is not
  a thing.

- ❌ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けたまま<b>する</b> → ✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けたまま<b>にする</b>
  To say you are deliberately leaving something in a state, the pattern is
  <code>～ままにする</code> or <code>～ままにしておく</code>.

## Practice

**1.** テレビをつけた____寝(ね)てしまって、朝(あさ)まで音(おと)が出(で)ていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

テレビをつけた<b>まま</b>寝(ね)てしまって…

The switching-on happened once and its result - the TV being on - carried through
the whole night, which is exactly what `まま` expresses. Note the た-form
<code>つけた</code>, even though nothing about the sentence is past-tense in
meaning.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)くて、後(うし)ろの人(ひと)たちは（立(た)ったまま・立(た)ちながら）聞(き)いていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

後(うし)ろの人(ひと)たちは<b>立(た)ったまま</b>聞(き)いていた。

Standing is a posture you enter once and then hold, not an activity you keep
performing, so it is a state - `まま` territory.
<code>立(た)ちながら</code> is not used; `ながら` wants two genuine ongoing
actions, like listening and taking notes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Don't leave the door open - just leave it as it is and I'll close it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ドアを開(あ)けた<b>まま</b>にしないで。そのまま<b>にしておいて</b>くれれば、私(わたし)が閉(し)めます。

Two uses in one answer: <code>開(あ)けたままにする</code> for deliberately
leaving something in a state, and <code>そのままにしておく</code> - the everyday
"leave it be" - where そのまま stands in for the whole state.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に入(い)れないまま、牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を飲(の)みながら一晩(ひとばん)置(お)いてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に入(い)れないまま、牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を一晩(ひとばん)置(お)いてしまった。

<code>飲(の)みながら</code> has no business here - nobody is drinking while
leaving the milk out, and `ながら` would need a second ongoing action by the same
person. The <code>ないまま</code> clause is correct as it stands: the milk stayed
un-refrigerated for a stretch of time.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 返事(へんじ)を書(か)か<b>ないで</b>、手紙(てがみ)を引(ひ)き出(だ)しにしまった。
(b) 返事(へんじ)を書(か)か<b>ないまま</b>、手紙(てがみ)を引(ひ)き出(だ)しにしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a single choice at a single moment: rather than writing back, I put the
letter away. It is a plain statement of what I did instead.

(b) puts weight on the reply never having been written. The un-answered state has
been going on and is still going on, and the sentence leans faintly toward regret - 
the letter went into the drawer and the reply is still owed.

</details>

# ～っぱなし

**Meaning** · left in that state and never put right; kept going without a break

`～っぱなし` comes from 放(はな)す, "to let go", and it keeps the word's flavour.
Something was set going or set open and then **abandoned** - the tap still running,
the drawer still hanging out. A second use stretches an action across a whole span
of time with no pause: on your feet the entire journey, losing every match for three
years.

## Formation

`っぱなし` attaches to the **ます-stem** of a verb and produces a **noun**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + っぱなし | 出(だ)します → 出(だ)しっぱなし |
| る-verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + っぱなし | 開(あ)けます → 開(あ)けっぱなし |
| Predicate | ～っぱなし + だ / です | 窓(まど)が開(あ)けっぱなしだ |
| Before a noun | ～っぱなし + の + Noun | つけっぱなしのテレビ |
| Deliberate / accusing | ～っぱなし + に + する | 出(だ)しっぱなしにする |
| Ending up that way | ～っぱなし + に + なる | つけっぱなしになる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - two families of verb take this suffix and they give the two senses.
**Transitive** verbs that put something into a state give the "left that way"
reading: つけっぱなし・開(あ)けっぱなし・出(だ)しっぱなし・脱(ぬ)ぎっぱなし・置(お)きっぱなし.
**Intransitive or durative** verbs give the "without a break" reading:
立(た)ちっぱなし・降(ふ)りっぱなし・鳴(な)りっぱなし・負(ま)けっぱなし・言(い)われっぱなし.
Verbs outside those two groups do not take it at all - 
❌<code>食(た)べっぱなし</code>, ❌<code>知(し)りっぱなし</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
テレビを<b>つけっぱなし</b>にして寝(ね)てしまった。
I fell asleep with the TV left on.
</pre>

<pre>
水(みず)を<b>出(だ)しっぱなし</b>にしないでね。
Don't leave the tap running.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)が<b>開(あ)けっぱなし</b>だったので、部屋(へや)がすっかり冷(ひ)えていた。
The window had been left open, so the room had gone completely cold.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)から名古屋(なごや)まで席(せき)が空(あ)かず、<b>立(た)ちっぱなし</b>だった。
No seat came free from Tokyo to Nagoya, so I was on my feet the whole way.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は朝(あさ)から電話(でんわ)が<b>鳴(な)りっぱなし</b>で、仕事(しごと)がまったく進(すす)まなかった。
The phone rang nonstop from morning yesterday and I got nothing done.
</pre>

<pre>
上着(うわぎ)を<b>脱(ぬ)ぎっぱなし</b>にしておくと、母(はは)に必(かなら)ずしかられる。
If I leave my jacket where I took it off, my mother tells me off without fail.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)では部長(ぶちょう)が<b>話(はな)しっぱなし</b>で、こちらは一言(ひとこと)も意見(いけん)を言(い)えなかった。
The manager talked for the whole meeting and I couldn't get a single word in.
</pre>

<pre>
今度(こんど)の試合(しあい)こそ勝(か)ちたい。ここ三年(さんねん)、あのチームには<b>負(ま)けっぱなし</b>なのだ。
I really want to win this one. We've lost to that team every single time for three years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The first sense is **never neutral**. `っぱなし` is the word you use when you are
annoyed, or admitting fault. Saying 電気(でんき)がつけっぱなしだ is not reporting
that a light is on; it is saying somebody left it on and should not have. Parents,
flatmates and office managers use it constantly, and it is one of the standard
building blocks of Japanese nagging.

The second sense - "without a break" - trades blame for **endurance**.
立(た)ちっぱなし, 歩(ある)きっぱなし, 走(はし)りっぱなし are complaints about
being worn out. 負(ま)けっぱなし and 言(い)われっぱなし add a note of being on
the receiving end and doing nothing about it, which is why
言(い)われっぱなしではいられない - "I can't just take this lying down" - is a
fixed expression.

Register is casual to neutral. It is perfectly normal at work between colleagues,
but in a written report or a formal apology you would step up to something flatter:
電源(でんげん)を切(き)り忘(わす)れておりました rather than
つけっぱなしでした. The kanji spelling <code>～っ放(ぱな)し</code> exists but is rare; kana is the
norm.

Watch the small tsu. 出(だ)す gives 出(だ)しっぱなし, not
❌<code>出(だ)すっぱなし</code>; the っ belongs to the suffix and the stem stays
short.

## Contrast

### ～っぱなし vs ～てある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てある` says somebody did it **on purpose and left it that way
for a reason**. `っぱなし` says somebody did it and **couldn't be bothered to undo
it**. Same physical scene, opposite verdicts.

</div>

<pre>
空気(くうき)を入(い)れかえるため、窓(まど)が開(あ)け<b>てある</b>。
The window has been left open to air the room out.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)も閉(し)めないので、窓(まど)が開(あ)け<b>っぱなし</b>だ。
Nobody ever shuts it, so the window just stays open.
</pre>

Both sentences describe a window that is open because a person opened it. `てある`
supplies a purpose - and can be followed by one, as here. `っぱなし` supplies
negligence, and it cannot take a purpose clause without sounding sarcastic.

This is the single most useful thing to know about the pattern, because the English
"left open" covers both and gives you no clue which to choose.

### ～っぱなし vs ～たまま

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たまま` is the **neutral** version. `っぱなし` is the same
state with a complaint attached, and it is limited to verb stems.

</div>

<pre>
靴(くつ)をはいた<b>まま</b>畳(たたみ)に上(あ)がってはいけない。
You mustn't step onto the tatami with your shoes on.
</pre>

<pre>
玄関(げんかん)に靴(くつ)が<b>脱(ぬ)ぎっぱなし</b>で、足(あし)の踏(ふ)み場(ば)もない。
Shoes have been kicked off and left all over the entryway - there's nowhere to stand.
</pre>

`まま` will also attach to nouns and adjectives（昔(むかし)のまま,
熱(あつ)いまま）, which `っぱなし` never does.

And only `っぱなし` has the "without a break" sense.
立(た)ったまま食(た)べる means eating in a standing posture;
立(た)ちっぱなしだった means being on your feet for hours. Substituting one for
the other changes the sentence completely.

### ～っぱなし vs ～ておく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ておく` is leaving something as it is **because that is the
useful thing to do**. `っぱなし` is leaving it as it is because nobody thought about
it.

</div>

<pre>
午後(ごご)もまた使(つか)うので、パソコンはつけ<b>ておきます</b>。
I'll leave the computer on, since I'll be using it again this afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
三日(みっか)も<b>つけっぱなし</b>にしていたら、電気代(でんきだい)が大変(たいへん)なことになる。
Leave it on for three days and the electricity bill will be horrifying.
</pre>

Notice that both can appear with the same verb in the same room. The choice is
entirely about how the speaker wants the action judged - which makes `っぱなし` a
useful thing to *avoid* when you are talking about your own deliberate decisions.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ つける<b>っぱなし</b> → ✅ つけ<b>っぱなし</b>
  The suffix takes the ます-stem. Dictionary form in front of it is not possible.

- ❌ 立(た)った<b>っぱなし</b> → ✅ 立(た)ち<b>っぱなし</b>
  The た-form belongs to `まま`, not to `っぱなし`.

- ❌ 開(あ)けっぱなし<b>な</b>窓(まど) → ✅ 開(あ)けっぱなし<b>の</b>窓(まど)
  The result is a noun, so a following noun is linked with `の`.

- ❌ お客(きゃく)さんのために、入口(いりぐち)のドアを開(あ)け<b>っぱなし</b>にしておきました。 → ✅ お客(きゃく)さんのために、入口(いりぐち)のドアを開(あ)け<b>ておきました</b>。
  You did it on purpose and for a good reason, so `ておく`. `っぱなし` would tell
  your boss you were careless.

- ❌ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べ<b>っぱなし</b>で出(で)かけた → ✅ 食器(しょっき)を出(だ)し<b>っぱなし</b>にして出(で)かけた
  Eating leaves no object in a state, so the verb does not take the suffix. Name
  the thing that was actually left - the dishes.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)はエアコンを____にして出(で)かけてしまい、一日中(いちにちじゅう)動(うご)いていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

エアコンを<b>つけっぱなし</b>にして出(で)かけてしまい…

The air conditioner was switched on and then abandoned for the day, which is the
core "left that way" sense. <code>～っぱなしにする</code> is the standard frame when
a person is responsible for the state.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)の日(ひ)、雨(あめ)は朝(あさ)から夜(よる)まで（降(ふ)りっぱなし・降(ふ)ったまま）だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

雨(あめ)は朝(あさ)から夜(よる)まで<b>降(ふ)りっぱなし</b>だった。

This is the "no break" sense: the rain continued for a whole stretch of time.
`まま` cannot express continuous action - <code>降(ふ)ったまま</code> would try to
mean "in the state left behind by raining", which is not a state anyone recognises.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please don't leave your books lying on the table."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本(ほん)をテーブルに<b>置(お)きっぱなし</b>にしないでください。

<code>置(お)きっぱなし</code> is the everyday way to complain about things left
where they were put down. The <code>にする</code> frame makes the listener the one
responsible, which is what a request of this kind needs.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 冷房(れいぼう)をつけっぱなしなので、部屋(へや)が寒(さむ)すぎる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 冷房(れいぼう)がつけっぱなしなので、部屋(へや)が寒(さむ)すぎる。

<code>つけっぱなし</code> describes the **state the thing is in**, so the thing takes
`が`, not `を`. `を` would need a verb - <code>つけっぱなしにしている</code> - because
`を` marks what someone is doing something to.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に資料(しりょう)が置(お)い<b>てある</b>。
(b) 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に資料(しりょう)が<b>置(お)きっぱなし</b>だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the papers were put there deliberately and are meant to stay - for you to
read, or for the next meeting. It is neutral and often helpful information.

(b) says somebody dumped them there and never cleared up. The papers are clutter,
and the sentence contains a small accusation. Said to the person who left them, it
is a request to move them.

</details>

# ～てばかりいる

**Meaning** · do nothing but ～; be forever ～ing

`～てばかりいる` says a person spends their time on **one activity to the exclusion
of everything else** - and that the speaker thinks they should be doing something
different. It is a complaint dressed as a description of a schedule.

## Formation

`ばかり` slots between the て-form and `いる`. The whole thing is an ordinary
る-verb, so it conjugates exactly like `いる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ばかり + いる | 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)んでばかりいる |
| Polite | て-form + ばかり + います | 寝(ね)てばかりいます |
| Past | て-form + ばかり + いた | 泣(な)いてばかりいた |
| Joining a clause | て-form + ばかり + いて / いても | 見(み)てばかりいても |
| Without いる | て-form + ばかり + だ | 謝(あやま)ってばかりだ |
| Noun + する verb | Noun + ばかり + している | 心配(しんぱい)ばかりしている |
| Casual contraction | て-form + ばっかり(いる) | 遊(あそ)んでばっかり |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `～てばかりいる` and `～てばかりだ` are close, and both are common. The
version with `いる` presents the behaviour as going on right now or over a stretch of
time; the version with `だ` is flatter, closer to a verdict on the person. Do not
mix them into ❌<code>～てばかりする</code>, which is not a form.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は<b>遊(あそ)んでばかりいる</b>。
My little brother does nothing but play.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は文句(もんく)を<b>言(い)ってばかりいて</b>、自分(じぶん)では何(なに)もしない。
He does nothing but complain and never does anything himself.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>泣(な)いてばかりいて</b>も何(なに)も変(か)わらないよ。
Crying about it isn't going to change anything.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)はスマホを<b>見(み)てばかりいて</b>、話(はな)しかけても返事(へんじ)をしない。
My son is forever staring at his phone and doesn't answer when I speak to him.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)に<b>寝(ね)てばかりいる</b>と、かえって疲(つか)れが取(と)れない。
If you do nothing but sleep on your days off, you actually end up less rested.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい職場(しょくば)では<b>失敗(しっぱい)してばかりで</b>、毎日(まいにち)落(お)ち込(こ)んでいる。
At my new job I do nothing but make mistakes, and I'm down about it every day.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)のことを<b>思(おも)い出(だ)してばかりいて</b>も前(まえ)に進(すす)めないので、何(なに)か新(あたら)しいことを始(はじ)めたい。
Dwelling on the past won't get me anywhere, so I want to start something new.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は会議(かいぎ)で<b>反対(はんたい)してばかりいた</b>が、自分(じぶん)の案(あん)は一(ひと)つも出(だ)さなかった。
The manager did nothing but object throughout the meeting, yet didn't offer a single idea of his own.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **almost always critical**. Even when the activity is one you would
normally praise, `てばかりいる` turns it into an imbalance:
勉強(べんきょう)してばかりいる is not "such a hard worker", it is "he never comes
out of his room" - worry, not admiration. If you want the approving version, use a
plain progressive: よく勉強(べんきょう)しています.

Aimed at yourself it becomes self-reproach, and this is extremely common:
謝(あやま)ってばかりですみません, 迷惑(めいわく)をかけてばかりで. In that use
it functions almost as an apology formula.

The casual contraction is `ばっかり`, and in fast speech it shortens further to
`ばっか`: 遊(あそ)んでばっかりいないで手伝(てつだ)ってよ. That is friends and
family register; keep `ばかり` for anything else.

One genuine ambiguity to be aware of. `ばかり` has a second life after the **た-form**
meaning "just did" - 着(つ)いたばかりです, "I've only just arrived". The two are
different patterns with nothing in common but the word, and the て-form versus
た-form is what tells them apart. Confusing them produces sentences that are
grammatical and mean the wrong thing, which is the worst kind of error.

Register is otherwise neutral: `てばかりいる` is at home in speech, in fiction and in
a newspaper column about people who spend too long online.

## Contrast

### ～てばかりいる vs Noun + ばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a **noun**, `ばかり` restricts *what* you do it to. After a
**て-form**, `ばかり` restricts *what you do* - the whole activity.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は肉(にく)<b>ばかり</b>食(た)べて、野菜(やさい)を残(のこ)す。
My brother eats nothing but meat and leaves the vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は朝(あさ)から<b>食(た)べてばかりいる</b>。
My brother has done nothing but eat since this morning.
</pre>

In the first, eating is fine; the problem is the menu. In the second, eating itself
is the problem - he should be doing something else with his day. The noun version is
also neutral enough to be used approvingly（野菜(やさい)ばかり食(た)べている
can be a compliment）, which the て-form version essentially never is.

### ～てばかりいる vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` reports what is happening. `てばかりいる` reports the
same thing plus "and nothing else, and I object".

</div>

<pre>
娘(むすめ)は部屋(へや)でゲームを<b>している</b>。
My daughter is playing a game in her room.
</pre>

<pre>
娘(むすめ)は部屋(へや)でゲームを<b>してばかりいる</b>。
My daughter does nothing but play games in her room.
</pre>

`ている` covers this moment or a neutral habit. `てばかりいる` needs a **stretch of
repeated behaviour**, so it cannot describe a single instance: pointing at someone
right now and saying 見(み)てばかりいます about one glance is wrong the way "he's
forever looking" would be wrong about one look.

### ～てばかりいる vs ～がち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がち` says it happens **often**, leaving room for other things.
`てばかりいる` says it is **all** that happens.

</div>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しい時期(じき)は外食(がいしょく)が多(おお)くなり<b>がち</b>だ。
When things get busy I tend to eat out more.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしを始(はじ)めてから、外(そと)で<b>食(た)べてばかりいる</b>。
Since I started living alone I've done nothing but eat out.
</pre>

Both disapprove, but they measure different things - frequency against exclusivity.
They also attach differently: `がち` takes the ます-stem and builds a な-adjective,
while `てばかりいる` takes the て-form and stays a verb. That means only
`てばかりいる` can carry tense and aspect of its own:
食(た)べてばかりいた, 食(た)べてばかりいるだろう.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 遊(あそ)ぶ<b>ばかりいる</b> → ✅ 遊(あそ)ん<b>でばかりいる</b>
  `ばかり` in this pattern follows the **て-form**, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 食(た)べてばかり<b>する</b> → ✅ 食(た)べてばかり<b>いる</b>
  The verb at the end is `いる`. <code>している</code> belongs to the noun pattern
  <code>心配(しんぱい)ばかりしている</code>.

- ❌ お昼(ひる)を食(た)べ<b>てばかりいます</b>。（meaning "I've just eaten lunch"） → ✅ お昼(ひる)を食(た)べ<b>たばかりです</b>。
  "Just did" is `た-form + ばかり`. The て-form version says you have been eating
  all day.

- ❌ 娘(むすめ)は毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)してばかりいて、えらいですね。 → ✅ 娘(むすめ)さんは毎日(まいにち)よく練習(れんしゅう)していて、えらいですね。
  The pattern carries disapproval, so it cannot be used to praise someone.

- ❌ 今(いま)、彼(かれ)はこちらを見(み)てばかりいます。（about one moment） → ✅ 今(いま)、彼(かれ)はこちらを見(み)ています。
  `てばかりいる` needs behaviour repeated over time; it cannot report a single
  instant.

## Practice

**1.** 試験(しけん)が近(ちか)いのに、兄(あに)はテレビを見(み)____いる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

兄(あに)はテレビを見(み)<b>てばかり</b>いる。

<code>見(み)る</code> goes into its て-form <code>見(み)て</code>, then
`ばかり`, then `いる`. The <code>のに</code> in the first half tells you a complaint
is coming, which is exactly the tone this pattern supplies.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 山田(やまだ)さんは（甘(あま)い物(もの)ばかり食(た)べている・食(た)べてばかりいる）ので、歯(は)が心配(しんぱい)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)さんは<b>甘(あま)い物(もの)ばかり食(た)べている</b>ので、歯(は)が心配(しんぱい)だ。

The worry is about *what* he eats, not about him eating instead of doing other
things - so `ばかり` goes after the noun.
<code>食(た)べてばかりいる</code> would make the eating itself the problem, which
has nothing to do with his teeth.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You can't just keep apologising - please explain what happened."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>謝(あやま)ってばかりいないで</b>、何(なに)があったのか説明(せつめい)してください。

<code>いる</code> takes its ordinary negative て-form <code>いないで</code>, which is
how you tell someone to stop doing only that one thing. This
<code>～てばかりいないで</code> frame is very common in real speech.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)東京(とうきょう)に着(つ)いてばかりなので、まだ何(なに)も見(み)ていません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)東京(とうきょう)に着(つ)い<b>たばかり</b>なので、まだ何(なに)も見(み)ていません。

The meaning wanted is "I only arrived yesterday", which is `た-form + ばかり`. The
て-form version would say the speaker has been arriving over and over - the same
word, an entirely different pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は最近(さいきん)よく本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいる。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)は最近(さいきん)本(ほん)を<b>読(よ)んでばかりいる</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral report of a habit, and in most contexts it is mildly positive - she
has got into reading lately.

(b) says reading has crowded everything else out. Even though reading is a
respectable activity, the pattern makes it sound like an escape or a neglect of
something: she is not going out, not studying, not talking to anyone. The speaker is
worried or exasperated, and that comes entirely from `てばかりいる`.

</details>

# ～かけ / ～かける

**Meaning** · started and not finished; on the point of ～ing

`～かけ` marks an action that got under way and then stopped short of its end. With
an action you chose to start, that means **half-done**: a half-eaten sandwich, a
half-written email. With a change that happens to you or to the world, it means
**almost happened**: nearly fell, nearly forgot, sun starting to set.

## Formation

The suffix has a **noun** form, `かけ`, and a **verb** form, `かける`
(a る-verb, from 掛(か)ける).

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) → noun | ます-stem + かけ | 読(よ)みます → 読(よ)みかけ |
| Before a noun | ～かけ + の + Noun | 読(よ)みかけの本(ほん) |
| Predicate | ～かけ + だ / です | この本(ほん)は読(よ)みかけだ |
| Verb (ます-stem) → verb | ます-stem + かける | 言(い)います → 言(い)いかける |
| Past - began and stopped | ～かけた | 言(い)いかけた |
| Progressive - is on the verge | ～かけている | 沈(しず)みかけている |
| Before a noun (verb form) | ～かけた + Noun | 消(き)えかけた字(じ) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb has to name something with an **endpoint** to fall short of.
読(よ)む, 食(た)べる, 書(か)く, 言(い)う, 落(お)ちる,
消(き)える, 沈(しず)む all qualify. States and open-ended activities do not:
❌<code>住(す)みかけ</code>, ❌<code>遊(あそ)びかけ</code>,
❌<code>知(し)りかけ</code>. Note also that ます-stem + かける has a separate,
purely lexical life in words like 話(はな)しかける ("speak *to* someone") and
出(で)かける ("go out") - those are vocabulary, not this pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
食(た)べ<b>かけ</b>のパンがテーブルに置(お)いてある。
There's a half-eaten piece of bread sitting on the table.
</pre>

<pre>
読(よ)み<b>かけ</b>の本(ほん)が何(なん)冊(さつ)もたまっている。
I've got several books piled up that I started and never finished.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)か言(い)い<b>かけて</b>、彼(かれ)は急(きゅう)に黙(だま)ってしまった。
He started to say something and then suddenly went quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)で転(ころ)び<b>かけた</b>が、手(て)すりをつかんで助(たす)かった。
I nearly fell on the stairs but grabbed the handrail and was fine.
</pre>

<pre>
書(か)き<b>かけ</b>のメールを送(おく)ってしまい、あわてて謝(あやま)った。
I accidentally sent a half-written email and hurried to apologise.
</pre>

<pre>
日(ひ)が沈(しず)み<b>かけている</b>ので、そろそろ山(やま)を下(お)りましょう。
The sun is starting to go down, so let's head off the mountain.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)の声(こえ)を忘(わす)れ<b>かけていた</b>ことに気(き)づいて、少(すこ)し悲(かな)しくなった。
I realised I'd nearly forgotten what my grandfather's voice sounded like, and it made me sad.
</pre>

<pre>
やり<b>かけ</b>の仕事(しごと)を机(つくえ)に置(お)いたまま帰(かえ)ってしまったので、朝(あさ)一番(いちばん)で片(かた)づけます。
I went home leaving unfinished work on my desk, so I'll deal with it first thing in the morning.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The noun form is a plain, everyday word - you can point at a plate and say
食(た)べかけ with no more ceremony than saying "leftovers". It does carry a faint
sense of **someone else's**: a 飲(の)みかけのコーヒー is a cup you would rather
not drink from, which is why offering one is slightly awkward.

The verb form is where the two senses separate cleanly, and the split follows the
verb. With a **volitional** verb, `かけた` means the speaker began and was
interrupted: 言(い)いかけた, 立(た)ち上(あ)がりかけた. With a
**non-volitional** change, it means the process was under way and did not complete - 
or is still under way: 消(き)えかけた火(ひ), 倒(たお)れかけた木(き),
沈(しず)みかけている船(ふね). Fiction leans on the second heavily.

Register is neutral throughout. Nothing about `かけ` is casual or formal; it turns up
in conversation, in instructions and in literature alike.

One shape worth memorising because it is so useful:
<code>死(し)にかける</code> → 死(し)にかけた, "came close to dying". It is the
standard way to say a near-miss with something serious, and it is much more common
than any construction with "almost".

## Contrast

### ～かけ vs ～ている途中(とちゅう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>途中(とちゅう)</code> means you are **in the middle of
it right now**. `かけ` means you **stopped** partway and are not doing it at the
moment.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)読(よ)んでいる<b>途中(とちゅう)</b>なので、あとで話(はな)してもいいですか。
I'm in the middle of reading it, so can we talk later?
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)買(か)った小説(しょうせつ)が、まだ<b>読(よ)みかけ</b>のままだ。
The novel I bought last year is still sitting there half-read.
</pre>

The first sentence has a person actively engaged. The second has a book on a shelf
and nobody reading it. This is why `かけ` so often appears with `まま` and
`そのまま` - the state has been abandoned and left standing.

### ～かける vs ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` predicts from appearances: it **looks like** it will
happen. `かける` reports that the process **actually began**.

</div>

<pre>
棚(たな)の本(ほん)が落(お)ち<b>そう</b>だから、少(すこ)し奥(おく)に入(い)れて。
The books on the shelf look like they're going to fall - push them back a bit.
</pre>

<pre>
棚(たな)の本(ほん)が落(お)ち<b>かけた</b>ので、あわてて手(て)を出(だ)した。
The books started to fall, so I grabbed at them.
</pre>

Nothing has moved in the first sentence; it is a judgement about a risk. In the
second, the books were on their way down. Learners who reach for `かける` when they
mean "looks like" end up reporting events that never occurred.

### ～かけ vs ～たばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - opposite ends of the same action. `かけ` = **begun, not
finished**. <code>たばかり</code> = **finished a moment ago**.

</div>

<pre>
そのケーキ、まだ<b>食(た)べかけ</b>だから捨(す)てないで。
Don't throw that cake out - I haven't finished it.
</pre>

<pre>
ケーキなら、さっき<b>食(た)べたばかり</b>です。
If you mean cake, I've only just had some.
</pre>

The forms make the difference visible: `かけ` sits on the ます-stem,
<code>ばかり</code> in this sense sits on the た-form. If you can see a た, the action
is over.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 読(よ)む<b>かけ</b>の本(ほん) → ✅ 読(よ)み<b>かけ</b>の本(ほん)
  `かけ` attaches to the ます-stem.

- ❌ 読(よ)みかけ<b>な</b>本(ほん) → ✅ 読(よ)みかけ<b>の</b>本(ほん)
  The noun form links to a following noun with `の`.

- ❌ もう少(すこ)しで事故(じこ)に<b>あいかけだった</b>。 → ✅ もう少(すこ)しで事故(じこ)に<b>あいかけた</b>。
  The bare noun `かけ` is for objects left unfinished. The "on the verge" sense needs
  the verb <code>かける</code>, conjugated.

- ❌ 昼(ひる)ごはんは食(た)べ<b>かけ</b>です。（meaning "I've just eaten"） → ✅ 昼(ひる)ごはんは食(た)べ<b>たばかり</b>です。
  <code>食(た)べかけ</code> means the plate is still half full.

- ❌ 三年(さんねん)この町(まち)に<b>住(す)みかけている</b>。 → ✅ 三年(さんねん)この町(まち)に住(す)んでいる。
  <code>住(す)む</code> has no endpoint to stop short of, so it does not take
  `かける` at all.

## Practice

**1.** 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に、書(か)き____の手紙(てがみ)が置(お)いてあった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

書(か)き<b>かけ</b>の手紙(てがみ)が置(お)いてあった。

The letter was started and abandoned, which is the noun form on the ます-stem,
linked to <code>手紙(てがみ)</code> with `の`. <code>書(か)きかけ</code> is one
of the commonest examples of this pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 自転車(じてんしゃ)が倒(たお)れ（そうだった・かけた）ので、手(て)で支(ささ)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自転車(じてんしゃ)が倒(たお)れ<b>かけた</b>ので、手(て)で支(ささ)えた。

The second half says the speaker physically caught it, so the bike must already have
begun to go over - that is `かける`. <code>倒(たお)れそうだった</code> would only
say it looked unstable, and would not explain the sudden catch.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please don't leave your food half-eaten."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

食(た)べ<b>かけ</b>で残(のこ)さないでください。

<code>食(た)べかけ</code> is the standard noun for food left partway through, and
it joins the following clause with `で` exactly as any noun does. You could also say
<code>食(た)べかけのまま残(のこ)さないで</code>, which adds the sense of the
plate being left standing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は何(なに)か言(い)いかけのに、途中(とちゅう)でやめてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は何(なに)か言(い)い<b>かけた</b>のに、途中(とちゅう)でやめてしまった。

An action that began and was cut short needs the **verb** <code>かける</code>, past
tense <code>かけた</code>, before のに. The bare noun `かけ` cannot take のに directly
and would in any case describe an object, not an event.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 火(ひ)が消(き)え<b>そうだ</b>。
(b) 火(ひ)が消(き)え<b>かけている</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a prediction from how the fire looks - small, low, unsteady. It might still
recover, and the speaker is judging from the outside.

(b) says the going-out has already started: the flame is in the process of dying,
and without intervention it will finish. It reports a change in progress rather than
a risk, which makes it the more urgent of the two.

</details>

# ～たて

**Meaning** · freshly ～ed; only just ～ed

`～たて` says a thing came out of some process **moments ago and is still in that
condition**. Bread out of the oven, rice out of the cooker, paint still wet, a
graduate still blinking at the world. The freshness is presented as a quality of the
thing, and it is a quality that will fade.

## Formation

`たて` attaches to the **ます-stem** and produces a **noun**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + たて | 焼(や)きます → 焼(や)きたて |
| する-verb | Noun + し + たて | 卒業(そつぎょう)します → 卒業(そつぎょう)したて |
| Before a noun | ～たて + の + Noun | 焼(や)きたてのパン |
| Predicate | ～たて + だ / です | ご飯(はん)は炊(た)きたてです |
| Joining a clause | ～たて + で | 習(なら)いたてで、まだ下手(へた)だ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is the most **lexically restricted** suffix in the group. Two small
families use it and nothing else does. Things coming out of a **process**:
焼(や)きたて・揚(あ)げたて・炊(た)きたて・入(い)れたて・塗(ぬ)りたて・できたて・とれたて・生(う)まれたて.
People who have just **entered a status**: 習(なら)いたて・覚(おぼ)えたて・卒業(そつぎょう)したて・入社(にゅうしゃ)したて・なりたて.
Outside those, it simply is not said: ❌<code>買(か)いたて</code>,
❌<code>読(よ)みたて</code>, ❌<code>見(み)たて</code>. (The existing words
見立(みた)て, 仕立(した)て and 組(く)み立(た)て are separate vocabulary,
not this suffix.)

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>焼(や)きたて</b>のパンはやっぱりおいしい。
Freshly baked bread really is delicious.
</pre>

<pre>
ご飯(はん)は<b>炊(た)きたて</b>が一番(いちばん)だ。
Rice is best straight out of the cooker.
</pre>

<pre>
ペンキ<b>塗(ぬ)りたて</b>なので、さわらないでください。
The paint is still wet, so please don't touch.
</pre>

<pre>
市場(いちば)で<b>とれたて</b>の魚(さかな)を買(か)って帰(かえ)った。
I bought some freshly caught fish at the market and took it home.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>入(い)れたて</b>のコーヒーのにおいで目(め)が覚(さ)めた。
The smell of freshly brewed coffee woke me up.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は運転(うんてん)免許(めんきょ)を<b>とりたて</b>で、まだ高速(こうそく)道路(どうろ)が怖(こわ)いらしい。
He's only just got his driving licence and is apparently still nervous of the motorway.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>覚(おぼ)えたて</b>の敬語(けいご)を使(つか)おうとして、かえって変(へん)な日本語(にほんご)になってしまった。
I tried to use the keigo I'd just learned and ended up with odder Japanese than before.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は<b>揚(あ)げたて</b>のコロッケを一(ひと)つずつ紙(かみ)に包(つつ)んでくれるので、帰(かえ)り道(みち)でつい食(た)べてしまう。
This shop wraps each freshly fried croquette in paper for you, so I always end up eating one on the way home.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たて` is a **warm, appetising** word, and it is the only suffix in this group that
is reliably positive. Shops and menus lean on it - 
焼(や)きたてパン, できたてのお弁当(べんとう) - because it promises the best
version of the thing. The two combinations 焼(や)きたて and できたて are so
entrenched that they function as ordinary nouns.

The freshness has a **short shelf life** built in, and this is what makes it
different from "new". Bread is 焼(や)きたて for an hour, not a week. A person is
卒業(そつぎょう)したて for a few months. If the interval you mean is long, the
word is wrong.

Applied to people, the tone is affectionate but a little indulgent:
新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)なりたて and 習(なら)いたて both carry "and it
shows". It is not an insult, but it is not a compliment on competence either.

There is no register restriction - 塗(ぬ)りたて appears on public signs,
揚(あ)げたて on menus, 覚(おぼ)えたて in casual conversation. Some
combinations take the kanji 立(た)て in writing（焼(や)き立(た)て）, but kana
is far more usual.

## Contrast

### ～たて vs ～たばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たばかり</code> works with **any verb** and measures time
since an event. `たて` works with a **short fixed list** and describes the thing as
being in a fresh condition.

</div>

<pre>
この車(くるま)は先週(せんしゅう)買(か)っ<b>たばかり</b>だ。
I only bought this car last week.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)六時(ろくじ)の店(みせ)には、<b>焼(や)きたて</b>のパンが並(なら)んでいた。
At six in the morning the shop had freshly baked bread lined up.
</pre>

Where both are available, they say slightly different things.
焼(や)いたばかりのパン tells you when the baking happened;
焼(や)きたてのパン tells you what the bread is like. The second is what you want
on a sign, and the first is what you want in a sentence about your morning.

The safe rule for production: if the combination is not one of the fixed set, use
<code>たばかり</code>. ❌<code>買(か)いたての車(くるま)</code> is not Japanese;
<code>買(か)ったばかりの車(くるま)</code> is.

### ～たて vs 新(あたら)しい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>新(あたら)しい</code> means not old. `たて` means
straight out of the process that made it, with the heat still in it.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいパンを二(ふた)つください。
Two of the fresh loaves, please.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)すぎに行(い)くと、もう<b>焼(や)きたて</b>は残(のこ)っていない。
If you go after midday, there's nothing left that's just out of the oven.
</pre>

<code>新(あたら)しい</code> covers anything baked today, and it would still be true
tomorrow morning relative to last week's loaf. `たて` is a claim about minutes. This
is also why <code>新(あたら)しい</code> can describe things that were never made - 
新(あたら)しい山(やま)の道(みち) - while `たて` needs a process and an agent.

### ～たて vs ～かけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two ends of one action. `かけ` = begun and **abandoned
partway**; `たて` = **just completed**.

</div>

<pre>
飲(の)み<b>かけ</b>のお茶(ちゃ)が、まだテーブルに残(のこ)っていた。
There was a half-drunk cup of tea still on the table.
</pre>

<pre>
入(い)れ<b>たて</b>のお茶(ちゃ)を、来客(らいきゃく)に出(だ)した。
I served the visitors tea I'd just made.
</pre>

Structurally they are twins: both sit on the ます-stem, both produce a noun, both
take `の` before a following noun. The difference is entirely one of where in the
action they point - and, as a result, of mood. A 飲(の)みかけ cup is a small
domestic mess; an 入(い)れたて cup is hospitality.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 焼(や)く<b>たて</b>のパン → ✅ 焼(や)き<b>たて</b>のパン
  `たて` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 焼(や)きたて<b>な</b>パン → ✅ 焼(や)きたて<b>の</b>パン
  The result is a noun and links with `の`.

- ❌ 買(か)い<b>たて</b>のパソコン → ✅ 買(か)っ<b>たばかり</b>のパソコン
  Buying is not a process that leaves a thing freshly made, and
  <code>買(か)いたて</code> is not used. The productive pattern is
  <code>たばかり</code>.

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)卒業(そつぎょう)したてなので、まだ仕事(しごと)に慣(な)れていません。 → ✅ 先月(せんげつ)卒業(そつぎょう)したてなので、まだ仕事(しごと)に慣(な)れていません。
  The freshness must be genuinely recent. A year is too long for `たて`; use
  <code>去年(きょねん)卒業(そつぎょう)したばかり</code> if you really mean a
  year ago.

- ❌ この服(ふく)は洗(あら)いたてで古(ふる)いです。 → ✅ この服(ふく)は洗(あら)いたてでいいにおいがします。
  `たて` is a positive word about a fresh state. Pairing it with a negative
  assessment of the same thing pulls the sentence in two directions.

**1.** 駅前(えきまえ)のパン屋(や)では、夕方(ゆうがた)に____のパンが出(で)る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>焼(や)く</code> gives the stem <code>焼(や)き</code>, and
<code>焼(や)きたて</code> is the most standard member of this small family. Note
the `の` linking it to <code>パン</code>.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

He has joined - the process is complete and he is fresh in the role, which is `たて`.
<code>入社(にゅうしゃ)しかけ</code> would mean he started joining and stopped

</details>

</details>

</details>

(b) simply reports when it was made: not long before it was served. It is the more
neutral, factual phrasing, and it would fit equally well in a sentence explaining
why there was none left over.

</details>

# ～きる

**Meaning** · do all of it, right to the end; be completely in a state

`～きる` (from 切(き)る, "to cut") takes a verb and adds **completion with nothing
left over**. Not merely finishing, but getting through the whole quantity: the last
page, the last spoonful, the last yen. A second use pushes a state to its limit - 
utterly exhausted, thoroughly cold, perfectly obvious.

## Formation

`きる` attaches to the **ます-stem** and produces a verb. Because it is
切(き)る, it conjugates as an **う-verb** (a Group 1 / 五段(ごだん) verb),
despite ending in -る.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + きる | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べきる |
| する-verb | Noun + し + きる | 整理(せいり)します → 整理(せいり)しきる |
| Past | ～きった | 読(よ)みきった |
| て-form | ～きって | 使(つか)いきって |
| Negative (う-verb!) | ～きらない | 食(た)べきらない |
| Potential | ～きれる / ～きれない | 食(た)べきれない |
| State pushed to its limit | ～きった + Noun | 疲(つか)れきった顔(かお) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the completion sense needs a **quantity or a course** to run to the end
of: a plateful, a book, a savings account, a race. The intensifying sense needs a
verb of **change of state**: 疲(つか)れる, 冷(ひ)える, 弱(よわ)る,
澄(す)む, 困(こま)る. Verbs with neither - ❌<code>見(み)きる</code> in the
sense "look completely", ❌<code>住(す)みきる</code> - do not take it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
大(おお)きなカレーを一人(ひとり)で<b>食(た)べきった</b>。
I finished off a huge plate of curry by myself.
</pre>

<pre>
今月(こんげつ)のお金(かね)を、もう<b>使(つか)いきって</b>しまった。
I've already spent all of this month's money.
</pre>

<pre>
長(なが)い小説(しょうせつ)を三日(みっか)で<b>読(よ)みきった</b>。
I got through a long novel in three days.
</pre>

<pre>
最後(さいご)まで<b>走(はし)りきった</b>選手(せんしゅ)に、大(おお)きな拍手(はくしゅ)が送(おく)られた。
The runners who made it all the way to the finish got a big round of applause.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの片(かた)づけで<b>疲(つか)れきって</b>、夕(ゆう)ご飯(はん)も食(た)べずに寝(ね)た。
I was completely worn out from unpacking after the move and went to bed without dinner.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなことは<b>分(わ)かりきった</b>話(はなし)だから、わざわざ言(い)わなくていい。
That's blindingly obvious, so there's no need to spell it out.
</pre>

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)の朝(あさ)、<b>冷(ひ)えきった</b>部屋(へや)でストーブをつけるのは、なかなかつらい。
On winter mornings, lighting the heater in a room that's gone stone cold is pretty grim.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)分(ぶん)の資料(しりょう)を一(いっ)週間(しゅうかん)で<b>整理(せいり)しきる</b>のは、さすがに無理(むり)だと思(おも)います。
Getting three years of documents completely sorted in one week honestly isn't going to happen.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The completion sense carries a note of **achievement or effort**.
読(よ)みきった is not a neutral "I finished reading"; it says the book was long
and you got to the end of it. This is why the pattern is common in sports reporting
and in talking about anything demanding - 走(はし)りきる, やりきる,
戦(たたか)いきる.

The intensifying sense produces a small set of very common `～きった` adjectives.
疲(つか)れきった, 困(こま)りきった, 冷(ひ)えきった,
澄(す)みきった空(そら), 分(わ)かりきったこと. These feel a shade literary
before a noun and are entirely ordinary in speech as predicates
（もう疲(つか)れきっています）.

Register is neutral. Several combinations have hardened into everyday vocabulary and
no longer feel like grammar: 売(う)り切(き)れる ("sell out"),
締(し)め切(き)り ("deadline"), 思(おも)い切(き)って ("taking the
plunge"). You already know those as words; this page is about the productive pattern
behind them.

The conjugation trap is worth stating plainly. 切(き)る **is an う-verb**, so the
negative is 食(た)べきらない, not ❌<code>食(た)べきない</code>, and the
polite past is 食(た)べきりました. Learners who see the -る and treat it as a
る-verb get every form wrong at once.

## Contrast

### ～きる vs ～てしまう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てしまう` is about the action being **over and irreversible**,
often with regret. `きる` is about the **quantity being exhausted**, often with
satisfaction.

</div>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)のケーキを食(た)べ<b>てしまった</b>。
I've gone and eaten the cake that was in the fridge.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きなケーキだったが、みんなで食(た)べ<b>きった</b>。
It was a big cake, but between us we finished the lot.
</pre>

The first invites "…and I shouldn't have". The second invites "…and we're rather
pleased". They stack, too, and the combination is very common:
使(つか)いきってしまった - used it all up, and now there is none left.

### ～きる vs ～終(お)わる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>終(お)わる</code> means the **activity stopped**
because you reached the end of it. `きる` means the **whole amount was got through**.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)やっとレポートを書(か)き<b>終(お)わった</b>。
I finally finished writing the report yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
三(さん)時間(じかん)かけて、残(のこ)っていた問題(もんだい)を全部(ぜんぶ)やり<b>きった</b>。
I spent three hours on it and got through every one of the remaining questions.
</pre>

<code>終(お)わる</code> is the neutral, always-available choice and attaches to
almost any verb. `きる` adds difficulty and completeness, and it needs something
countable to be complete *of*. That is why
<code>書(か)き終(お)わる</code> is unremarkable while
書(か)ききる sounds like the report nearly defeated you.

### ～きる vs ～抜(ぬ)く

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "to the very end". `きる` stresses that **nothing was
left**; <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> stresses that you **kept going despite hardship**.

</div>

<pre>
苦(くる)しかったが、最後(さいご)まで<b>走(はし)りきった</b>。
It hurt, but I made it all the way to the end.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに反対(はんたい)されても、最後(さいご)までやり<b>抜(ぬ)く</b>つもりだ。
However much people object, I intend to see it through to the end.
</pre>

With a race, either works and they shade differently: 走(はし)りきる is about
covering the whole distance, 走(はし)り抜(ぬ)く about enduring it. With a
quantity - money, food, paper - only `きる` fits, because there is nothing to endure.
<code>抜(ぬ)く</code> belongs to a later level, but the pair is worth seeing now
so that `きる` does not get stretched to cover perseverance.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>きる</b> → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>きる</b>
  `きる` attaches to the ます-stem.

- ❌ 食(た)べ<b>きない</b> → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>きらない</b>
  切(き)る is an う-verb, so its negative is きらない. The same goes for
  きります, きって, きった.

- ❌ 財布(さいふ)をなくし<b>きった</b>。 → ✅ 財布(さいふ)をなくし<b>てしまった</b>。
  A wallet is one object, not a quantity to work through, so there is nothing for
  `きる` to exhaust. Regret over a single completed action is `てしまう`.

- ❌ この町(まち)に三十年(さんじゅうねん)住(す)み<b>きった</b>。 → ✅ この町(まち)に三十年(さんじゅうねん)住(す)んでいる。
  <code>住(す)む</code> has no endpoint, so it takes neither sense of `きる`.

- ❌ 疲(つか)れきる顔(かお) → ✅ 疲(つか)れきった顔(かお)
  In the intensifying sense, the state has already been reached, so the form before
  a noun is the past <code>きった</code>.

## Practice

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The point is that every last vegetable went into the pot - a quantity worked through
to nothing, which is exactly what `きる` marks. The past is
<code>きった</code>, formed as for any う-verb ending in -る.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The sentence is a plain report that the writing reached its end, and

now: there is none left.

of effort or of a job done. It is what you would say about finishing a large crate
between friends, not about carelessly emptying the fridge.

</details>

# ～きれない

**Meaning** · there is too much of it to get through; more than one can manage

`～きれない` is the negative potential of 切(き)る in its "do it all" sense.
It does not say you are incapable of the action - it says the **amount defeats
you**. You can eat; there is simply more food than you can eat. You can count; there
are simply more stars than you can count.

## Formation

`きれない` attaches to the **ます-stem**. It is already a negative potential, so it
never takes a second potential ending.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + きれない | 食(た)べます → 食(た)べきれない |
| する-verb | Noun + し + きれない | 説明(せつめい)します → 説明(せつめい)しきれない |
| Polite | ～きれません | 覚(おぼ)えきれません |
| Past | ～きれなかった | 読(よ)みきれなかった |
| Before a noun (with ほど/くらい) | ～きれない + ほど / くらい + の | 数(かぞ)えきれないほどの星(ほし) |
| Affirmative counterpart | ～きれる | 一人(ひとり)で運(はこ)びきれる |
| Literary negative て-form | ～きれず(に) | 待(ま)ちきれずに出(で)かけた |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the affirmative <code>きれる</code> exists but is far less common than
the negative. When you mean "I managed to finish it", the ordinary
<code>食(た)べきった</code> is what people actually say;
<code>食(た)べきれた</code> is reserved for contexts where the *capacity* is the
point（一人(ひとり)でも食(た)べきれる量(りょう)です）. The pattern is
lopsided, and it is worth learning it as a negative first.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
料理(りょうり)が多(おお)すぎて、<b>食(た)べきれない</b>。
There's too much food - I can't finish it.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)が多(おお)くて、一日(いちにち)では<b>覚(おぼ)えきれない</b>。
There are so many kanji that I can't learn them all in one day.
</pre>

<pre>
空(そら)には<b>数(かぞ)えきれない</b>ほどの星(ほし)が光(ひか)っていた。
There were countless stars shining in the sky.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の旅行(りょこう)が楽(たの)しみで、<b>待(ま)ちきれない</b>。
I'm so looking forward to next week's trip that I can hardly wait.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)が多(おお)くて一度(いちど)では<b>運(はこ)びきれない</b>ので、二回(にかい)に分(わ)けます。
There's too much to carry in one trip, so I'll split it into two.
</pre>

<pre>
お世話(せわ)になった方々(かたがた)への感謝(かんしゃ)は、言葉(ことば)では<b>伝(つた)えきれません</b>。
I can't put into words how grateful I am to everyone who looked after me.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)が多(おお)すぎて会議(かいぎ)までに<b>読(よ)みきれなかった</b>ので、要点(ようてん)だけまとめてもらった。
There was too much material to get through before the meeting, so I had someone summarise the key points.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)では<b>抱(かか)えきれない</b>問題(もんだい)を、何年(なんねん)も誰(だれ)にも言(い)わずにいたそうだ。
Apparently he kept a problem too big for one person to handle to himself for years, without telling anyone.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The core meaning is **capacity overrun**, and it is the amount that overruns, not
your skill. That distinction does real work in a sentence:
読(よ)みきれない means the book was too long, while
読(よ)めない would mean you cannot read it at all - bad eyesight, wrong language,
handwriting you cannot decipher. Learners who use `きれない` for lack of ability end
up claiming that a two-line note is too long for them.

`きれない` is register-neutral and it turns up constantly in one particular shape:
<code>～きれないほど</code>, "so many/much that you couldn't ～".
数(かぞ)えきれないほど is the standard Japanese for "countless", and
食(た)べきれないほどの料理(りょうり) is what you say about a table that groans.

Two idiomatic uses do not follow the "too much" template, and both are worth
knowing. 待(ま)ちきれない is eager anticipation - the waiting exceeds what you can
bear - and it is a warm, positive expression.
信(しん)じきれない and 割(わ)りきれない (in its emotional sense) describe a
feeling you cannot fully settle into; they are more literary.

In writing you will also meet the て-form <code>きれず(に)</code>:
我慢(がまん)しきれずに笑(わら)ってしまった. That is standard written Japanese
and sounds slightly formal in speech.

## Contrast

### ～きれない vs the plain potential negative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>られない</code> denies the **ability**. `きれない` grants
the ability and denies that it is **enough for the quantity**.

</div>

<pre>
辛(から)い物(もの)が苦手(にがて)なので、このカレーは<b>食(た)べられない</b>。
I'm no good with spicy food, so I can't eat this curry.
</pre>

<pre>
おいしいけれど量(りょう)が多(おお)くて、このカレーは<b>食(た)べきれない</b>。
It's delicious, but there's so much of it that I can't finish this curry.
</pre>

In the first sentence the speaker cannot eat one spoonful. In the second they have
been eating happily and have simply run out of room. Notice that `きれない`
presupposes the action is under way or possible - which is why it fits so naturally
with a half-empty plate.

### ～きれない vs ～すぎる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>すぎる</code> describes **the thing**: there is too much of
it. `きれない` describes **the person**: they cannot get to the end of it.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)の宿題(しゅくだい)は<b>多(おお)すぎる</b>。
There's far too much homework today.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)の宿題(しゅくだい)は夜(よる)までに<b>やりきれない</b>。
I won't get through today's homework by tonight.
</pre>

They are two views of the same problem and they combine constantly - the commonest
frame in the whole pattern is <code>～すぎて～きれない</code>:
多(おお)すぎて食(た)べきれない. Using both is not redundant; the first names
the cause and the second the consequence.

### ～きれない vs ～きった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are the two halves of one pattern.
<code>きった</code> = got through the whole lot. `きれない` = could not.

</div>

<pre>
大(おお)きなピザだったが、三人(さんにん)で<b>食(た)べきった</b>。
It was a big pizza, but the three of us polished it off.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きなピザで、二人(ふたり)では<b>食(た)べきれなかった</b>。
It was a big pizza, and the two of us couldn't finish it.
</pre>

The asymmetry to remember is that the affirmative side normally uses the **plain**
form <code>食(た)べきる / 食(た)べきった</code>, not the potential
<code>食(た)べきれる</code>, while the negative side almost always uses the
**potential** <code>食(た)べきれない</code> rather than the plain
<code>食(た)べきらない</code>. Both plain negatives exist, but they sound
deliberate - "doesn't finish it" rather than "can't finish it".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>きれない</b> → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>きれない</b>
  The suffix goes on the ます-stem.

- ❌ 食(た)べ<b>きれられない</b> → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>きれない</b>
  <code>きれない</code> is already the potential negative. Adding another potential
  ending doubles it.

- ❌ この字(じ)は小(ちい)さくて<b>読(よ)みきれない</b>。 → ✅ この字(じ)は小(ちい)さくて<b>読(よ)めない</b>。
  Small print is a problem of ability, not of quantity. `きれない` needs an amount to
  fall short of.

- ❌ 数(かぞ)えきれない<b>の</b>星(ほし) → ✅ 数(かぞ)えきれない<b>ほどの</b>星(ほし)
  <code>きれない</code> is a negative い-adjective form, so it modifies a noun
  directly（数(かぞ)えきれない星(ほし)）or through
  <code>ほどの</code>. It never takes a bare `の`.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)のライブが<b>待(ま)ちきれる</b>! → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)のライブが<b>待(ま)ちきれない</b>!
  The excited "I can't wait!" is the negative. The affirmative would mean you are
  perfectly capable of waiting, which rather kills the mood.

## Practice

**1.** パーティーの料理(りょうり)が多(おお)すぎて、全部(ぜんぶ)は食(た)べ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

全部(ぜんぶ)は食(た)べ<b>きれなかった</b>。

<code>多(おお)すぎて</code> names the cause and <code>きれない</code> names the
consequence - the standard pairing. The past
<code>きれなかった</code> is needed because the party is over.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 目(め)が悪(わる)くて、遠(とお)くの字(じ)が（読(よ)めない・読(よ)みきれない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

目(め)が悪(わる)くて、遠(とお)くの字(じ)が<b>読(よ)めない</b>。

Poor eyesight removes the ability altogether, which is the plain potential negative.
`きれない` would claim that the text is too long to finish, and it says nothing about
eyes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I got so many messages that I couldn't reply to them all."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

メッセージが多(おお)すぎて、全部(ぜんぶ)には<b>返事(へんじ)しきれなかった</b>。

<code>返事(へんじ)する</code> is a する-verb, so the stem is
<code>返事(へんじ)し</code>. The sentence is about volume defeating you rather
than about not knowing how to reply, which is precisely `きれない`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: おみやげが多(おお)くて、一(いっ)回(かい)では持(も)ちきれられませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ おみやげが多(おお)くて、一(いっ)回(かい)では持(も)ち<b>きれませんでした</b>。

<code>きれる</code> already carries the potential meaning, so
<code>きれられる</code> stacks two potentials. The polite negative past is simply
<code>きれませんでした</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この本(ほん)は一日(いちにち)では読(よ)め<b>ない</b>。
(b) この本(ほん)は一日(いちにち)では読(よ)み<b>きれない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says reading it in a day is beyond you - which could be because it is long, but
could equally be because it is in a language you do not have, or too difficult. The
sentence does not say which.

(b) narrows it to volume. You can read the book perfectly well; there are just more
pages than fit into a day. It implicitly grants that you would get through it given
longer, which (a) does not.

</details>

# ～がたい

**Meaning** · hard to bring oneself to ～; almost impossible to ～

`～がたい` (from 難(かた)い, voiced to がたい as a suffix) marks a difficulty that
lives in the **mind, not in the hands**. Something is hard to believe, hard to
accept, hard to part with, hard to put into words. It is not about a task being
physically awkward - for that Japanese has a different suffix entirely.

## Formation

`がたい` attaches to the **ます-stem** and produces an **い-adjective**:
信(しん)じがたい → 信(しん)じがたかった → 信(しん)じがたく → no `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + がたい | 信(しん)じます → 信(しん)じがたい |
| する-verb | Noun + し + がたい | 理解(りかい)します → 理解(りかい)しがたい |
| Before a noun | ～がたい + Noun | 忘(わす)れがたい思(おも)い出(で) |
| Past | ～がたかった | 受(う)け入(い)れがたかった |
| Polite | ～がたいです | 信(しん)じがたいです |
| Adverbial | ～がたく | 捨(す)てがたく思(おも)う |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this suffix is **not productive**. It attaches to a short list of verbs
of thinking, feeling, saying and judging:
信(しん)じる・理解(りかい)する・想像(そうぞう)する・忘(わす)れる・捨(す)てる・言(い)う・認(みと)める・近寄(ちかよ)る・動(うご)かす・得(え)る・耐(た)える.
Everyday physical verbs do not take it: ❌<code>開(あ)けがたい</code>,
❌<code>歩(ある)きがたい</code>, ❌<code>食(た)べがたい</code>. If you are
unsure whether a combination exists, it almost certainly does not - use `にくい`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの人(ひと)の話(はなし)は<b>信(しん)じがたい</b>。
His story is hard to believe.
</pre>

<pre>
一年間(いちねんかん)の留学(りゅうがく)は<b>忘(わす)れがたい</b>経験(けいけん)になった。
The year abroad turned into an experience I'll never forget.
</pre>

<pre>
どちらの案(あん)も<b>捨(す)てがたい</b>ので、もう少(すこ)し考(かんが)えさせてください。
Both proposals have something going for them, so let me think a bit longer.
</pre>

<pre>
なぜ彼(かれ)があんな嘘(うそ)をついたのか、どうしても<b>理解(りかい)しがたい</b>。
I really cannot understand why he told a lie like that.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は少(すこ)し<b>近寄(ちかよ)りがたい</b>雰囲気(ふんいき)があるが、話(はな)してみると優(やさ)しい人(ひと)だ。
The manager comes across as a bit unapproachable, but he's kind once you talk to him.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)の段階(だんかい)では、この計画(けいかく)が成功(せいこう)したとは<b>言(い)いがたい</b>。
At this stage it would be hard to call the project a success.
</pre>

<pre>
信頼(しんらい)できる同僚(どうりょう)は<b>得(え)がたい</b>存在(そんざい)だと、転職(てんしょく)して初(はじ)めて気(き)づいた。
It took changing jobs for me to see how rare a colleague you can trust really is.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)がこれだけそろっているのだから、この結論(けつろん)は<b>動(うご)かしがたい</b>だろう。
With this many figures lined up, this conclusion is going to be hard to shift.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`がたい` is **noticeably formal**. It belongs to written Japanese, prepared speech,
news copy and careful argument. In everyday conversation the same idea comes out as
信(しん)じられない, ちょっとわからない, 捨(す)てるのはもったいない. Saying
信(しん)じがたいですね in a chat with a friend is not wrong, but it lands like
"that strains credulity" in English - correct, and a little theatrical.

The kind of difficulty it names is **psychological or principled**. A conclusion
that is 動(うご)かしがたい is not heavy; it is well supported. A person who is
近寄(ちかよ)りがたい is not physically distant; they are intimidating. Note that
this is a comment on how they come across and not necessarily a criticism - 
近寄(ちかよ)りがたい often shades into "commands respect".

The suffix is also useful for **hedged negatives**, and this is where business
Japanese likes it. 成功(せいこう)したとは言(い)いがたい means "I would not go so
far as to call it a success" - a way of saying it failed without saying it failed.
賛成(さんせい)しがたい softens a refusal in the same way.

Two lexical footnotes. ありがたい ("grateful, welcome") comes from this suffix
historically but is now simply a word, and it is not built the same way. And the
reading voices: がたい, not かたい. 固(かた)い and 硬(かた)い are unrelated
adjectives about hardness.

## Contrast

### ～がたい vs ～にくい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にくい` is **practical** difficulty and attaches to almost any
verb. `がたい` is **psychological or moral** difficulty and attaches to a short
fixed list. When in doubt, `にくい` is nearly always the right answer.

</div>

<pre>
この靴(くつ)は小(ちい)さすぎて<b>歩(ある)きにくい</b>。
These shoes are too small and hard to walk in.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)のことは家族(かぞく)には<b>言(い)いがたい</b>。
It's hard to bring myself to tell my family about the accident.
</pre>

Where a verb takes both, the difference is exactly this. 言(い)いにくい is
awkward to pronounce or awkward to raise - a practical obstacle.
言(い)いがたい is something you cannot in conscience assert. Likewise
信(しん)じにくい ("hard to credit, on the evidence") is milder than
信(しん)じがたい ("beyond belief").

Register follows the same line: `にくい` is at home everywhere, `がたい` is formal.
Learners producing ❌<code>使(つか)いがたいアプリ</code> for "an app that's hard
to use" are picking the wrong suffix twice over - wrong kind of difficulty and wrong
level of formality.

### ～がたい vs ～ようがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がたい` says it is hard and you would rather not.
<code>ようがない</code> says there is **no method at all**, so the question of
willingness never arises.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の言(い)い訳(わけ)は<b>信(しん)じがたい</b>が、証拠(しょうこ)もない。
His excuse is hard to believe, though there's no evidence either way.
</pre>

<pre>
連絡先(れんらくさき)を知(し)らないので、知(し)らせ<b>ようがない</b>。
I don't have his contact details, so there's no way to let him know.
</pre>

The first speaker *could* believe the excuse and is declining to. The second speaker
would happily make the call and cannot. That is why `がたい` combines naturally with
`が` and けれど - it admits the other side - while <code>ようがない</code> tends to
close a topic down.

### ～がたい vs ～られない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>られない</code> reports that you **cannot**. `がたい`
reports that it is **hard to**, leaving the possibility open.

</div>

<pre>
そんな話(はなし)、とても<b>信(しん)じられない</b>。
I simply can't believe a story like that.
</pre>

<pre>
報告(ほうこく)の内容(ないよう)には、いくつか<b>信(しん)じがたい</b>点(てん)がある。
There are several points in the report that are hard to credit.
</pre>

The first is an emotional reaction, spoken, and it slams the door.
The second is an assessment, written, and it leaves room for the report to turn out
right after all. Choosing `がたい` where <code>られない</code> was wanted makes you
sound as though you are drafting a memo in the middle of an argument; choosing
<code>られない</code> in a formal document sounds hot-headed.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 信(しん)じる<b>がたい</b> → ✅ 信(しん)じ<b>がたい</b>
  `がたい` attaches to the ます-stem.

- ❌ 忘(わす)れがたい<b>だ</b> → ✅ 忘(わす)れがたい
  The result is an い-adjective. The polite form is
  <code>忘(わす)れがたいです</code>.

- ❌ この席(せき)は狭(せま)くて<b>座(すわ)りがたい</b>。 → ✅ この席(せき)は狭(せま)くて<b>座(すわ)りにくい</b>。
  Physical awkwardness is `にくい`. `がたい` is for difficulty of the mind.

- ❌ しんじ<b>かたい</b> → ✅ しんじ<b>がたい</b>
  The suffix voices. Unvoiced かたい belongs to 固(かた)い and 硬(かた)い,
  which mean "hard" in the physical sense.

- ❌ この漢字(かんじ)は<b>読(よ)みがたい</b>。 → ✅ この漢字(かんじ)は<b>読(よ)みにくい</b>。
  A character that is hard to make out is a practical problem, and
  <code>読(よ)みがたい</code> is not a combination Japanese uses.

## Practice

**1.** 三十年(さんじゅうねん)前(まえ)の写真(しゃしん)を見(み)ると、あの夏(なつ)の旅行(りょこう)は今(いま)でも忘(わす)れ____思(おも)い出(で)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

忘(わす)れ<b>がたい</b>思(おも)い出(で)だ。

<code>忘(わす)れがたい</code> is one of the fixed combinations, and as an
い-adjective it sits directly in front of the noun with no `の` and no `な`. It is
warmer and more literary than <code>忘(わす)れられない</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この説明書(せつめいしょ)は字(じ)が小(ちい)さくて（読(よ)みにくい・読(よ)みがたい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この説明書(せつめいしょ)は字(じ)が小(ちい)さくて<b>読(よ)みにくい</b>。

Small print is a practical obstacle, and practical obstacles take `にくい`.
<code>読(よ)みがたい</code> is not used at all - the verb
<code>読(よ)む</code> is outside the small set that takes `がたい`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "At this point it would be hard to say the plan has gone well."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

現時点(げんじてん)では、計画(けいかく)がうまくいっているとは<b>言(い)いがたい</b>。

The frame <code>～とは言(い)いがたい</code> is the standard formal way to reject a
claim without flatly contradicting it, and it is exactly what the hedged English
sentence calls for.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)の態度(たいど)は理解(りかい)しがたいだと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)の態度(たいど)は理解(りかい)しがたいと思(おも)います。

`がたい` builds an い-adjective, and い-adjectives take no `だ` before
<code>と思(おも)う</code>. The `だ` in the wrong sentence is the same error as
❌<code>高(たか)いだと思(おも)う</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部長(ぶちょう)は<b>近寄(ちかよ)りがたい</b>人(ひと)だ。
(b) 部長(ぶちょう)は話(はな)しかけにくい人(ひと)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) describes the impression the manager gives off - a certain distance or gravity
that makes people hesitate. It is formal in tone and can shade into respect: an
imposing person is often described this way.

(b) is a practical complaint from the speaker's side: it is awkward to start a
conversation with him, perhaps because he is always busy or always frowning. It is
the more everyday phrasing, and it is about the speaker's difficulty rather than
about his aura.

</details>

# ～ようがない / ～ようもない

**Meaning** · there is no way of doing it; no method exists

`よう` here is 様(よう), "manner, way of doing". `～ようがない` therefore says
that the **means are missing**: not that you lack the skill, not that you are
unwilling, but that there is nothing available to do it with. No address, so no way
to send it. No pieces left, so no way to mend it.

## Formation

`ようがない` attaches to the **ます-stem** and the result is an **い-adjective**,
because it ends in `ない`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + ようがない | 直(なお)します → 直(なお)しようがない |
| する-verb | Noun + し + ようがない | 連絡(れんらく)します → 連絡(れんらく)しようがない |
| Emphatic variant | ます-stem + ようもない | 想像(そうぞう)しようもない |
| Polite | ～ようがありません | 直(なお)しようがありません |
| Past | ～ようがなかった | 助(たす)けようがなかった |
| て-form / joining | ～ようがなく(て) | 書(か)きようがなくて困(こま)った |
| Before a noun | ～ようがない + Noun | 直(なお)しようがない時計(とけい) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ようもない</code> is the same pattern with `も` in place of `が`,
and the `も` adds "not even". It is a shade stronger and a shade more literary;
everything else is identical. Do not confuse either with the **volitional**
<code>行(い)こう</code>, <code>食(た)べよう</code> - those come from a
different base and cannot take this ending.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
住所(じゅうしょ)が分(わ)からないので、送(おく)り<b>ようがない</b>。
I don't know the address, so there's no way to send it.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに壊(こわ)れていては、直(なお)し<b>ようがない</b>。
Broken like this, there's no fixing it.
</pre>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)番号(ばんごう)も知(し)らないから、連絡(れんらく)し<b>ようがない</b>。
I don't even know his phone number, so there's no way to get in touch.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)が大(おお)きく書(か)いてあるので、間違(まちが)え<b>ようがない</b>。
The name is written in big letters, so there's no way you can get it wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)が話(はな)してくれない以上(いじょう)、こちらからは助(たす)け<b>ようがない</b>。
As long as he won't talk to us, there's nothing we can do to help.
</pre>

<pre>
材料(ざいりょう)が何(なに)もないのだから、作(つく)り<b>ようがない</b>じゃないか。
There aren't any ingredients, so how exactly am I supposed to make anything?
</pre>

<pre>
あのときの彼女(かのじょ)の気持(きも)ちは、想像(そうぞう)し<b>ようもない</b>。
I cannot even begin to imagine how she felt at the time.
</pre>

<pre>
データがすべて消(き)えてしまったので、報告書(ほうこくしょ)を書(か)き<b>ようがなく</b>、締(し)め切(き)りを延(の)ばしてもらった。
All the data was wiped, so there was simply no way to write the report and I had the deadline pushed back.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The feeling is **resignation without excuse-making**. Because the sentence claims
that no method exists, it removes blame from everybody: 直(なお)しようがない is
not "I failed to fix it" but "it was not fixable". That makes the pattern useful for
declining responsibility gracefully, and it also makes it a strong claim - if a
method *did* exist, the sentence is simply untrue.

There is a mirror-image use that surprises learners: `ようがない` on a verb of
error or failure produces a **guarantee**. 間違(まちが)えようがない means "you
couldn't get it wrong if you tried"; 負(ま)けようがない, "there's no way we lose".
The logic is the same - no method available - but the effect is confident rather than
defeated.

Register is neutral, leaning slightly written for `ようもない`. The one thing that
is *not* neutral is the contraction. 仕方(しかた)がない has a spoken cousin
しょうがない, and しょうがない is a contraction of しようがない - so the two patterns
share a form. In practice, しょうがない has drifted away into a fixed phrase meaning
"oh well, nothing to be done", and it is quite casual; if you mean specifically
"there is no way to do it", say しようがない with the full vowel.

Note also that しょうがない人(ひと) means "hopeless case, incorrigible" - an
affectionate insult, not a comment on methods.

## Contrast

### ～ようがない vs the potential negative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>られない</code> says **I** can't. `ようがない` says
**nobody** can, because there is nothing to do it with.

</div>

<pre>
道具(どうぐ)がないので、私(わたし)には<b>直(なお)せない</b>。
I haven't got the tools, so I can't fix it.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)がもう作(つく)られていないので、<b>直(なお)しようがない</b>。
The parts aren't made any more, so it simply can't be repaired.
</pre>

In the first sentence, a repair shop could still do it. In the second, nobody can.
This is why `ようがない` is the right choice when you want to close off the
possibility entirely - and the wrong choice when the obstacle is personal.
❌忙(いそが)しくて行(い)きようがない is a common learner error; being busy is
your circumstance, not an absence of method, so it should be
忙(いそが)しくて行(い)けない.

### ～ようがない vs ～がたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がたい` is **hard**; `ようがない` is **impossible**. `がたい`
concedes that the thing could be done and says you can hardly bring yourself to;
`ようがない` says the question does not arise.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は<b>理解(りかい)しがたい</b>が、一応(いちおう)聞(き)いておこう。
His explanation is hard to make sense of, but let's hear him out anyway.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)が一(いち)枚(まい)もなくては、<b>理解(りかい)しようがない</b>。
Without a single document to go on, there's no way to understand it.
</pre>

The give-away is what can follow. `がたい` sits comfortably before `が` or けれど,
because there is another side. `ようがない` normally ends the matter.

### ～ようがない vs 仕方(しかた)がない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>仕方(しかた)がない</code> is about **accepting a
situation**: it can't be helped. `ようがない` is about **an action having no
method**: there's no way to do it.

</div>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まってしまったのだから、<b>仕方(しかた)がない</b>。
The trains have stopped, so there's nothing for it.
</pre>

<pre>
地図(ちず)も電波(でんぱ)もないので、道(みち)を調(しら)べ<b>ようがない</b>。
With no map and no signal, there's no way to look up the route.
</pre>

<code>仕方(しかた)がない</code> stands alone as a comment on the whole state of
affairs and attaches to nothing. `ようがない` must attach to a verb stem and names a
specific action that cannot be carried out. They often appear in the same breath:
調(しら)べようがないのだから、仕方(しかた)がない.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 直(なお)す<b>ようがない</b> → ✅ 直(なお)し<b>ようがない</b>
  The suffix takes the ます-stem.

- ❌ 行(い)こう<b>がない</b> → ✅ 行(い)き<b>ようがない</b>
  The volitional <code>行(い)こう</code> is a different form entirely. `よう` here
  is the noun 様(よう), and it follows the stem.

- ❌ 連絡(れんらく)する<b>ようがない</b> → ✅ 連絡(れんらく)し<b>ようがない</b>
  With a する-verb, the stem is `し`.

- ❌ 直(なお)しようがない<b>だ</b> → ✅ 直(なお)しようがない
  It ends in `ない`, so it is an い-adjective and takes no `だ`. Polite is
  <code>直(なお)しようがありません</code>.

- ❌ 眠(ねむ)くて勉強(べんきょう)し<b>ようがない</b>。 → ✅ 眠(ねむ)くて勉強(べんきょう)<b>できない</b>。
  Being sleepy is a personal state, not a missing method. `ようがない` needs the
  means themselves to be absent.

## Practice

**1.** メールアドレスも電話(でんわ)番号(ばんごう)も分(わ)からないので、知(し)らせ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

知(し)らせ<b>ようがない</b>。

Every possible channel is missing, so there is literally no method - which is what
`ようがない` claims. Note the ます-stem <code>知(し)らせ</code>, and that the
whole thing behaves as an い-adjective.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 説明(せつめい)が丁寧(ていねい)すぎるくらいなので、作(つく)り方(かた)を（間違(まちが)えようがない・間違(まちが)えがたい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

作(つく)り方(かた)を<b>間違(まちが)えようがない</b>。

This is the confident use: the instructions are so thorough that no route to a
mistake exists. <code>間違(まちが)えがたい</code> is not a combination Japanese
uses, and `がたい` would in any case only mean "hard to", not "impossible".

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The shop has closed down, so there's no way to complain about it now."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

店(みせ)がなくなってしまったので、今(いま)となっては文句(もんく)の言(い)い<b>ようがない</b>。

The shop's disappearance removes the means, not the speaker's ability - exactly the
distinction the pattern draws. <code>言(い)いようがない</code> sits on the
ます-stem of <code>言(い)う</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 時間(じかん)がなくて、昨日(きのう)は返事(へんじ)しようがなかったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 時間(じかん)がなくて、昨日(きのう)は返事(へんじ)できませんでした。

Lack of time is a personal circumstance, so the potential negative is what is wanted.
`ようがない` would claim that no channel for replying existed at all - which would be
true if the phone were dead, but not merely because you were busy.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この時計(とけい)は<b>直(なお)せない</b>。
(b) この時計(とけい)は<b>直(なお)しようがない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the speaker cannot repair it. Somebody with more skill, better tools or the
right parts might well be able to, and the sentence quietly invites you to take it
somewhere else.

(b) closes that door. There is no method - the parts do not exist, or the damage is
past any repair - so taking it elsewhere would be pointless. It is a statement about
the watch rather than about the speaker.

</details>

# ～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる

**Meaning** · four suffixes that bolt a direction, a partner, a second attempt or a
habit onto a verb

Japanese builds a great deal of its vocabulary by welding a second verb onto the
ます-stem of a first. These four are the ones an N3 learner meets constantly:
<code>～込(こ)む</code> sends the action **inward or deep**, <code>～合(あ)う</code> makes it
**mutual**, <code>～直(なお)す</code> makes it a **second go**, and
<code>～慣(な)れる</code> says you have **done it often enough to be comfortable**.

## Formation

All four attach to the **ます-stem** and produce an ordinary verb.
込(こ)む, 合(あ)う and 直(なお)す are **う-verbs**; 慣(な)れる is a
**る-verb**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) - inward | ます-stem + 込(こ)む | 飛(と)びます → 飛(と)び込(こ)む |
| Verb (ます-stem) - deeply, at length | ます-stem + 込(こ)む | 考(かんが)えます → 考(かんが)え込(こ)む |
| Verb (ます-stem) - mutually | ます-stem + 合(あ)う | 話(はな)します → 話(はな)し合(あ)う |
| Verb (ます-stem) - do over | ます-stem + 直(なお)す | 書(か)きます → 書(か)き直(なお)す |
| Verb (ます-stem) - get used to doing | ます-stem + 慣(な)れる | 使(つか)います → 使(つか)い慣(な)れる |
| Usual shape of the last one | ～慣(な)れて + いる | 使(つか)い慣(な)れている |
| Noun instead of a stem | Noun + に + 慣(な)れる | 生活(せいかつ)に慣(な)れる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - these are **word-formation**, not free grammar. Each combination is a
separate item of vocabulary, and only some combinations exist:
考(かんが)え込(こ)む is a word, ❌<code>走(はし)り込(こ)む</code> in the
sense "run inward" mostly is not. 込(こ)む is the least predictable of the four - 
使(つか)い込(こ)む means both "use for years" and "embezzle" - so treat new
combinations as vocabulary to look up rather than forms to generate.
直(なお)す and 合(あ)う are the two you can most safely build yourself.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)は間違(まちが)えたので、<b>書(か)き直(なお)します</b>。
I got this kanji wrong, so I'll write it again.
</pre>

<pre>
クラスのみんなで<b>話(はな)し合(あ)った</b>。
The whole class talked it over together.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが水(みず)たまりに<b>飛(と)び込(こ)んだ</b>。
The child jumped into the puddle.
</pre>

<pre>
この道具(どうぐ)は十年(じゅうねん)使(つか)っているので、すっかり<b>使(つか)い慣(な)れて</b>いる。
I've been using this tool for ten years, so it's completely second nature by now.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は質問(しつもん)を聞(き)いて、しばらく<b>考(かんが)え込(こ)んで</b>いた。
The manager heard the question and sat there thinking hard for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)をやめるかどうか、もう一度(いちど)<b>考(かんが)え直(なお)して</b>みることにした。
I decided to give the question of quitting the exchange programme another think.
</pre>

<pre>
困(こま)ったときに<b>助(たす)け合(あ)える</b>友(とも)だちが近(ちか)くにいるのは、ありがたいことだ。
It's a blessing to have friends nearby who can help each other out when things go wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)の満員(まんいん)電車(でんしゃ)にもだんだん<b>乗(の)り慣(な)れて</b>きて、今(いま)では本(ほん)も読(よ)める。
I've gradually got used to Tokyo's packed trains, and these days I can even read on them.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)で昔(むかし)の友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)い、喫茶店(きっさてん)で二(に)時間(じかん)も<b>話(はな)し込(こ)んで</b>しまった。
I ran into an old friend at the station and ended up talking for two solid hours in a coffee shop.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

All four are ordinary, register-neutral vocabulary. Nothing about them is formal or
casual; what varies is how freely you can invent new combinations.

<code>～直(なお)す</code> is the most productive and the most useful for a learner. Almost
any deliberate action can be redone: 書(か)き直(なお)す,
読(よ)み直(なお)す, 考(かんが)え直(なお)す,
作(つく)り直(なお)す, やり直(なお)す. It always implies the first
attempt was unsatisfactory, which is why it does not work for pleasant repetition - 
you do not 聞(き)き直(なお)す a song you love, you listen again.

<code>～合(あ)う</code> requires **two or more parties acting on each other**. That is a
real constraint: 助(たす)け合(あ)う needs both sides helping, and if only one
person helps, it is plain 助(たす)ける. This use produces a warm, cooperative
tone, which is why 話(はな)し合(あ)い is the word for a proper discussion
rather than an argument.

<code>～込(こ)む</code> splits into a physical direction（飛(と)び込(こ)む,
乗(の)り込(こ)む, 流(なが)れ込(こ)む）and an intensity or duration
sense（考(かんが)え込(こ)む, 話(はな)し込(こ)む,
黙(だま)り込(こ)む, 教(おし)え込(こ)む）. The second group tends to
suggest being absorbed to the point of losing track of the outside world.

<code>～慣(な)れる</code> almost always appears as **～慣(な)れている** or
**～慣(な)れてくる**, because it names a state you have arrived at rather than an
event. And keep it apart from its noun-taking twin:
日本(にほん)の生活(せいかつ)に慣(な)れる takes a noun and `に`;
日本語(にほんご)で話(はな)し慣(な)れる takes a stem.

## Contrast

### ～直(なお)す vs もう一度(いちど)～する

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>もう一度(いちど)</code> is neutral repetition.
<code>～直(なお)す</code> says the first attempt was **wrong or inadequate** and this is the
corrected version.

</div>

<pre>
好(す)きな映画(えいが)なので、<b>もう一度(いちど)</b>見(み)たい。
It's a film I love, so I'd like to watch it again.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)が抜(ぬ)けていたので、申込書(もうしこみしょ)を<b>書(か)き直(なお)した</b>。
The name was missing, so I filled the application form in again.
</pre>

Swap them and the meanings shift audibly.
映画(えいが)を見(み)直(なお)す would mean watching it again to check
something, or - in another sense - coming to think better of it.
もう一度(いちど)書(か)く is simply writing a second copy, with no implication
that the first was faulty.

### ～合(あ)う vs 一緒(いっしょ)に～する

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～合(あ)う</code> means the action goes **between** the
participants. <code>一緒(いっしょ)に</code> means they do it **side by side**,
usually to something or someone else.

</div>

<pre>
三人(さんにん)で荷物(にもつ)を<b>一緒(いっしょ)に</b>運(はこ)んだ。
The three of us carried the luggage together.
</pre>

<pre>
困(こま)っている人(ひと)を、みんなで<b>助(たす)け合(あ)おう</b>。
Let's all help each other out when we're in trouble.
</pre>

The luggage is a third party; nobody is carrying anybody. That is why
❌<code>運(はこ)び合(あ)う</code> is odd. With 助(たす)ける, the sentence
only takes 合(あ)う if the helping flows in both directions - 
一緒(いっしょ)に助(たす)ける would mean two people helping a third.

### ～込(こ)む vs ～出(だ)す

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - as directional suffixes they are opposites: <code>込(こ)む</code> points
**in**, <code>出(だ)す</code> points **out**. Note that
<code>出(だ)す</code> also has the unrelated "suddenly begin" sense you met at N4.

</div>

<pre>
かばんから財布(さいふ)を<b>取(と)り出(だ)した</b>。
I took my wallet out of my bag.
</pre>

<pre>
ポケットに切符(きっぷ)を<b>押(お)し込(こ)んだ</b>。
I shoved the ticket into my pocket.
</pre>

The same verb takes both and the pair is worth memorising:
飛(と)び出(だ)す ("dash out, leap out") against
飛(と)び込(こ)む ("dive in").

The trap is that <code>出(だ)す</code> has a second job - marking the sudden
start of an action, as in 泣(な)き出(だ)す - and that job has nothing to do
with direction. Context tells them apart:
走(はし)り出(だ)す is "break into a run", not "run outwards".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 書(か)く<b>直(なお)す</b> → ✅ 書(か)き<b>直(なお)す</b>
  All four attach to the ます-stem.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)に相談(そうだん)し<b>合(あ)った</b>。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)に相談(そうだん)した。
  <code>～合(あ)う</code> needs the action to run both ways. Consulting a teacher goes one
  way, so the plain verb is correct.

- ❌ 日本(にほん)の生活(せいかつ)を住(す)み<b>慣(な)れた</b>。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)の生活(せいかつ)に慣(な)れた。
  With a **noun**, use <code>に慣(な)れる</code>. The stem form needs a verb in
  front of it: <code>この町(まち)に住(す)み慣(な)れた</code>.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)をもう一度(いちど)<b>考(かんが)え込(こ)もう</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)をもう一度(いちど)<b>考(かんが)え直(なお)そう</b>。
  <code>考(かんが)え込(こ)む</code> is to brood, absorbed and silent. To
  rethink something is <code>考(かんが)え直(なお)す</code>.

- ❌ このパソコンは<b>使(つか)い慣(な)れる</b>。 → ✅ このパソコンは<b>使(つか)い慣(な)れている</b>。
  The suffix names a state you have reached, so the ている form is what you almost
  always want.

## Practice

**1.** 漢字(かんじ)を一(いち)文字(もじ)間違(まちが)えたので、はじめから書(か)き____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

はじめから書(か)き<b>直(なお)した</b>。

The first version was faulty, which is exactly what <code>～直(なお)す</code> marks.
<code>直(なお)す</code> is an う-verb, so the past is
<code>直(なお)した</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会議(かいぎ)では全員(ぜんいん)で意見(いけん)を（言(い)い合(あ)った・一緒(いっしょ)に言(い)った）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

全員(ぜんいん)で意見(いけん)を<b>言(い)い合(あ)った</b>。

Opinions were exchanged between the participants, which is the mutual sense.
<code>一緒(いっしょ)に言(い)った</code> would mean everyone said the same thing
in unison, which is not what a meeting is.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've got used to cooking for myself, so it doesn't bother me any more."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自分(じぶん)で料理(りょうり)を<b>作(つく)り慣(な)れた</b>ので、もう苦(く)にならない。

<code>作(つく)り慣(な)れる</code> takes the ます-stem because the thing you are
used to is an **action**. Had the sentence been about being used to *life alone*, it
would need the noun pattern <code>一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしに慣(な)れる</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 電車(でんしゃ)が来(き)たので、いそいで乗(の)り出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 電車(でんしゃ)が来(き)たので、いそいで乗(の)り<b>込(こ)んだ</b>。

Getting onto a train is movement **inward**, so the suffix is
<code>込(こ)む</code>. <code>乗(の)り出(だ)す</code> exists but means
"lean out" or "launch into a venture" - not boarding.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は黙(だま)って下(した)を見(み)ていた。
(b) 彼(かれ)は<b>黙(だま)り込(こ)んで</b>しまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply describes what he was doing: not speaking, eyes down. It is a neutral
piece of stage direction and says nothing about why.

(b) says he shut down into silence and stayed there. The <code>込(こ)む</code> supplies both
depth and duration - he has gone somewhere inward - and <code>しまった</code> adds
that this was not what anyone wanted. It is a much heavier silence than (a).

</details>
