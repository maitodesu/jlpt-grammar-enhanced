# N3 - Polite and humble speech


---

# 敬語

**Meaning** · the honorific system - three separate mechanisms for showing where
you stand in relation to the person you are talking to and the person you are
talking about

敬語(けいご) is not one politeness dial. It is three tools with different jobs:
尊敬(そんけい)語(ご) raises the other person, 謙譲(けんじょう)語(ご)
lowers you, and 丁寧(ていねい)語(ご) keeps the sentence itself polite. You
choose the first two by asking *whose action it is*; the third you simply keep on.

## Formation

The individual frames belong to their own pages; the table below is the map. Read
"Attaches to" as "the form the frame is built on".

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - 尊敬(そんけい)語(ご) | お + ます-stem + になる | 使(つか)う → お使(つか)いになる |
| Verb - 尊敬(そんけい)語(ご), light | passive form ～れる／られる | 帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)られる |
| Noun + する - 尊敬(そんけい)語(ご) | ご／お + noun + なさる・になる | 出席(しゅっせき)する → ご出席(しゅっせき)なさる |
| Verb - 謙譲(けんじょう)語(ご) | お + ます-stem + する／いたす | 届(とど)ける → お届(とど)けいたします |
| Noun + する - 謙譲(けんじょう)語(ご) | ご／お + noun + する／いたす | 説明(せつめい)する → ご説明(せつめい)いたします |
| Verb - 丁寧(ていねい)語(ご) | ます／です | 行(い)く → 行(い)きます |
| Noun - 美化(びか)語(ご) | お／ご + noun | 茶(ちゃ) → お茶(ちゃ) |
| Common verbs | a separate word replaces the verb | 見(み)る → ご覧(らん)になる／拝見(はいけん)する |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask one question: **whose action is it?** Their action takes
尊敬(そんけい)語(ご). Your action takes 謙譲(けんじょう)語(ご). Both
sit on top of 丁寧(ていねい)語(ご), which never goes away in a polite
conversation.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the line between "us" and "them" moves. Inside your company your
manager is above you and takes 尊敬(そんけい)語(ご). On the phone to a
client, your manager is on your side of the line and takes
謙譲(けんじょう)語(ご):
田中(たなか)はただ今(いま)外出(がいしゅつ)しております. This is the single
biggest structural difference from European politeness systems, and the mistake
that most marks out a beginner.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
先生(せんせい)はもう<b>お帰(かえ)りになりました</b>か。
Has the teacher already gone home?
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)まで<b>ご案内(あんない)します</b>。
I'll show you to the reception desk.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)は<b>拝見(はいけん)しました</b>。ありがとうございます。
I've read the documents. Thank you.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の三時(さんじ)に、そちらへ<b>伺(うかが)います</b>。
I'll call on you at three tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません、田中(たなか)はただ今(いま)席(せき)を<b>外(はず)しております</b>。
I'm sorry - Tanaka is away from his desk at the moment.
</pre>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)が<b>おっしゃった</b>方針(ほうしん)に沿(そ)って進(すす)めております。
We are proceeding along the lines the president set out.
</pre>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しいところ恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、少(すこ)しお時間(じかん)を<b>いただけますでしょうか</b>。
I'm sorry to trouble you when you're busy, but might I have a moment of your time?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

敬語(けいご) is not simply "very polite Japanese". It is a way of drawing
relationships, and the relationships it draws are situational rather than fixed:
seniority at work, customer and shop, host and guest, first meeting versus tenth.
The same two people may use it in the morning meeting and drop it entirely at
dinner.

Two practical points. First, **volume matters less than direction**. A sentence
built with the wrong branch - honorific verbs for your own actions - is far more
jarring than a plain ます sentence would have been. When you are unsure, staying in
丁寧(ていねい)語(ご) and speaking clearly is safe; guessing at
尊敬(そんけい)語(ご) and pointing it at yourself is not.

Second, **more is not better**. Stacking two honorific mechanisms on one verb
produces 二重(にじゅう)敬語(けいご) - おっしゃられる, お読(よ)みになられる
 - which is widely regarded as incorrect, though you will certainly hear it. So-called
manual keigo (よろしかったでしょうか, ～のほう) has the same over-polished quality
and is criticised for the same reason.

Regionally and generationally there is variation: Kansai speech uses ～はる as a
light honorific, and older speakers tend to use fuller forms than younger ones.
Written business Japanese is the most conservative register of all, which is why
email templates are worth collecting.

## Contrast

### 尊敬(そんけい)語(ご) vs 謙譲(けんじょう)語(ご)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the branch is chosen by the **actor**, not by who you are
speaking to. Their action, 尊敬(そんけい)語(ご); your action,
謙譲(けんじょう)語(ご).

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が資料(しりょう)を<b>ご覧(らん)になりました</b>。
The department head looked at the documents.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が資料(しりょう)を<b>拝見(はいけん)しました</b>。
I looked at the documents.
</pre>

Both sentences are addressed to the same superior and both are polite. Swapping the
verbs - ❌私(わたし)がご覧(らん)になりました - claims honour for yourself, and
it is the error native listeners notice fastest.

### お○○になる vs お○○する

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one frame, two endings, opposite directions. `になる` raises
the actor; `する` lowers the actor.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)が<b>お待(ま)ちになっています</b>。
The teacher is waiting for you.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)で<b>お待(ま)ちします</b>。
I'll wait outside.
</pre>

The trap is that they look almost identical. A useful check: if the subject is
you, the ending must be `する` or `いたす`. There is no situation in which
❌お待(ま)ちになります describes your own waiting.

### 丁寧(ていねい)語(ご) vs 尊敬(そんけい)語(ご)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ます` is politeness to the **listener**. It says nothing at
all about the person in the sentence.

</div>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は三時(さんじ)に来(き)ます。
The section chief is coming at three.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は三時(さんじ)に<b>いらっしゃいます</b>。
The section chief will be arriving at three.
</pre>

The first sentence is polite Japanese and perfectly grammatical; spoken to a
visitor about your own section chief it is exactly right. Spoken to a junior
colleague about the chief, the second is the expected form. Politeness and honour
are separate axes, and `ます` only moves one of them.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 社長(しゃちょう)がおっしゃられました。 → ✅ 社長(しゃちょう)が<b>おっしゃいました</b>。
  おっしゃる is already honorific; adding the honorific passive `られる` makes it
  二重(にじゅう)敬語(けいご). One mechanism per verb.

- ❌ 私(わたし)が明日(あした)いらっしゃいます。 → ✅ 私(わたし)が明日(あした)<b>伺(うかが)います</b>。
  いらっしゃる raises its subject, so pointing it at yourself is the one thing
  敬語(けいご) never allows.

- ❌（取引先(とりひきさき)への電話(でんわ)で）部長(ぶちょう)はいらっしゃいません。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)の山田(やまだ)は<b>おりません</b>。
  To someone outside the company, your own manager belongs to your side and takes
  humble forms - and the honorific `さん` and title drop away too.

- ❌ どうぞ拝見(はいけん)してください。 → ✅ どうぞ<b>ご覧(らん)ください</b>。
  拝見(はいけん)する is humble, so inviting someone to do it lowers them. The
  honorific counterpart is ご覧(らん)になる.

## Practice

**1.** 先生(せんせい)はコーヒーを____か。（飲(の)む → 尊敬(そんけい)語(ご)）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先生(せんせい)はコーヒーを<b>召(め)し上(あ)がります</b>か。

The action belongs to the teacher, so it takes an honorific form, and
飲(の)む is one of the verbs with its own replacement word rather than a frame.
お飲(の)みになりますか is also correct, if a little stiffer.

</details>

**2.** Choose the correct form: （私(わたし)が・部長(ぶちょう)が）お持(も)ちします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>私(わたし)が</b>お持(も)ちします。

The frame <code>お～する</code> is humble, so its subject has to be you or someone on
your side. With 部長(ぶちょう) as subject you would need
お持(も)ちになります instead.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, speaking to a client on the phone about your
colleague 鈴木(すずき): "Suzuki is out at the moment."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

鈴木(すずき)はただ今(いま)<b>外出(がいしゅつ)しております</b>。

Your colleague is inside your circle and the client is outside it, so Suzuki takes
humble forms and loses the `さん`. おります is the humble counterpart of います, and
`ておる` is its て-form partner.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 田中(たなか)様(さま)がお越(こ)しになられました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 田中(たなか)様(さま)が<b>お越(こ)しになりました</b>。

<code>お越(こ)しになる</code> is already an honorific frame, and `られる` adds a
second one on top. Doubling the mechanism is the classic
二重(にじゅう)敬語(けいご) error.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 山田(やまだ)先生(せんせい)は来週(らいしゅう)来(き)ます。
(b) 山田(やまだ)先生(せんせい)は来週(らいしゅう)いらっしゃいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are polite to the listener, because both end in `ます`. Only (b) also raises
Professor Yamada himself. Sentence (a) is what you would say to someone outside the
school if the professor were a colleague of yours; said to a student about their own
teacher, it sounds oddly flat, as though you did not think the professor merited the
honorific.

</details>

# ～させてもらう・～ていただく

**Meaning** · do something with your kind permission; have something done for me - 
humble framing built out of the receiving verbs

Two related frames live on this page. <code>～ていただく</code> is the humble
<code>～てもらう</code>: **you** do something, and I receive the favour.
<code>～させていただく</code> adds the causative in front of it: **I** do something,
and what I receive is your permission to do it. Getting them the right way round is
the whole lesson.

## Formation

Build the causative, put it in the て-form, then attach もらう (plain) or いただく
(humble). Every causative verb is a る-verb, so the て-form step never varies.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | あ-stem + せていただく | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)わせていただきます |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る + させていただく | 始(はじ)める → 始(はじ)めさせていただきます |
| Verb (する) | させていただく | 説明(せつめい)する → 説明(せつめい)させていただきます |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(こ)させていただく | 来(く)る → 来(こ)させていただきます |
| Casual equivalent | causative て-form + もらう | 帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)らせてもらうね |
| Someone else's action | て-form + いただく | 教(おし)える → 教(おし)えていただきました |
| Asking permission | causative て-form + いただけませんか | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)ませていただけませんか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - count the actors. <code>させていただく</code>: I act, you permit.
<code>ていただく</code>: you act, I benefit. If the verb describes something you are
doing, the causative has to be in there.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verbs take `せる`, not `させる`. ❌読(よ)まさせていただきます is the
error known as さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば), and it is common enough among native
speakers to be a running complaint in style guides. The correct form is
読(よ)ませていただきます. る-verbs and する genuinely do take `させる`, which is
where the confusion comes from.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
コピーを<b>取(と)らせていただきます</b>。
I'll take a copy, if I may.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)に漢字(かんじ)の由来(ゆらい)を<b>教(おし)えていただきました</b>。
The teacher was kind enough to explain where the character comes from.
</pre>

<pre>
本日(ほんじつ)司会(しかい)を<b>務(つと)めさせていただきます</b>、田中(たなか)と申(もう)します。
I'm Tanaka, and I'll be chairing today's session.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが、明日(あした)は午後(ごご)から<b>休(やす)ませていただけませんか</b>。
I'm sorry to ask, but might I take tomorrow afternoon off?
</pre>

<pre>
こちらのお部屋(へや)、写真(しゃしん)を<b>撮(と)らせていただいても</b>よろしいでしょうか。
Would it be all right if I took a photograph of this room?
</pre>

<pre>
貴重(きちょう)なご意見(いけん)を<b>聞(き)かせていただき</b>、ありがとうございました。
Thank you for sharing your valuable thoughts with me.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)な話(はなし)で恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが、来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)は<b>欠席(けっせき)させていただきます</b>。
I'm sorry for the short notice, but I'll be absent from next week's meeting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>させていただく</code> is business Japanese's workhorse. It appears in
announcements, presentations, shop signs and apologetic emails, and its effect is
to present your own action as something granted rather than simply done. That is
why it so often accompanies bad news: 閉店(へいてん)させていただきます on a shop
door softens a closure by implying the customers' permission.

Note that a sentence ending in させていただきます is usually **not** a request. It
announces. The genuine request forms are させていただけませんか,
させていただけますでしょうか and させてください, and the difference matters: if you
need an answer, use one of those.

The plain <code>させてもらう</code> is the same grammar without the humility, and it is
ordinary casual speech: 先(さき)に帰(かえ)らせてもらうね, "I'm going to head
off, if that's all right." It can also carry a faint note of asserting your right,
which is why it sometimes sounds slightly assertive between equals.

Overuse is a real phenomenon. When nobody has granted anything - 
❌担当(たんとう)させていただいております for a job you were simply assigned - the
form is criticised as inflation, and plain いたします or しております is better
Japanese. The test is whether a permission-giver exists in the situation. In a
presentation the audience has given you the floor, so
説明(せつめい)させていただきます is well founded; in a self-introduction, nobody
gave you permission to have your own name.

## Contrast

### ～させていただく vs ～させてください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - させてください **asks**. させていただきます **announces**, on
the assumption that permission is already there.

</div>

<pre>
少(すこ)し考(かんが)え<b>させてください</b>。
Please let me think about it a little.
</pre>

<pre>
結果(けっか)は後日(ごじつ)ご連絡(れんらく)<b>させていただきます</b>。
We'll be in touch with the result at a later date.
</pre>

Both are polite. させてください is a direct request and needs a yes; it can be a
touch too direct toward a customer, which is why business speech leans on
させていただきます and on the interrogative させていただけますでしょうか.

### ～させていただく vs ～ていただく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - whose hands are moving? Mine: させていただく. Yours:
ていただく.

</div>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)を<b>送(おく)らせていただきます</b>。
I'll send you the documents.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)を<b>送(おく)っていただけますか</b>。
Could you send me the documents?
</pre>

One causative apart, and the two sentences point in opposite directions. Mixing
them up is the most consequential mistake on this page: you can easily promise to
do something when you meant to ask for it.

### ～させていただく vs ～いたします

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>いたします</code> is plain humility. Add
させていただく only when there is somebody whose permission is genuinely in play.

</div>

<pre>
ご案内(あんない)<b>いたします</b>。
Allow me to show you the way.
</pre>

<pre>
本日(ほんじつ)は代(か)わりに私(わたし)が担当(たんとう)<b>させていただきます</b>。
I'll be standing in and handling this today.
</pre>

The second sentence works because the listener is being asked to accept a
substitute. Where no such acceptance is involved, いたします is shorter, older and
better regarded.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 読(よ)まさせていただきます。 → ✅ <b>読(よ)ませていただきます</b>。
  読(よ)む is an う-verb, so the causative is 読(よ)ませる with `せ` alone.
  The extra `さ` is the さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば) error.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)に説明(せつめい)させていただきました。（meaning the teacher explained）→ ✅ 先生(せんせい)に<b>説明(せつめい)していただきました</b>。
  With the causative in place, the sentence says *you* did the explaining after the
  teacher allowed it. Drop the causative when the other person is the one acting.

- ❌ お名前(なまえ)を書(か)かせていただかせていただきます。 → ✅ お名前(なまえ)を<b>書(か)かせていただきます</b>。
  One layer of humility per verb. Stacking the frame on itself is
  二重(にじゅう)敬語(けいご), not extra politeness.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)と申(もう)させていただきます。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)と<b>申(もう)します</b>。
  Nobody grants you permission to be called by your own name, so there is nothing
  for the frame to receive. This is the textbook case of overuse.

## Practice

**1.** 体調(たいちょう)が悪(わる)いので、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)____ませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今日(きょう)は早(はや)く<b>帰(かえ)らせていただけ</b>ませんか。

帰(かえ)る is an う-verb, so the causative is 帰(かえ)らせる, and the
potential of いただく in the negative question form is what turns the sentence into
a polite request rather than an announcement.

</details>

**2.** Choose the correct form: 部長(ぶちょう)に推薦状(すいせんじょう)を（書(か)かせていただきました・書(か)いていただきました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)に推薦状(すいせんじょう)を<b>書(か)いていただきました</b>。

The manager is the one who wrote the letter, so the plain て-form plus いただく is
correct. The causative version would mean the speaker wrote it after being given
permission.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "May I use this room?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この部屋(へや)を<b>使(つか)わせていただけますか</b>。
（or 使(つか)わせていただいてもよろしいでしょうか。）

使(つか)う is an う-verb, giving 使(つか)わせる - not ❌使(つか)わさせる, which
is the さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば) error - and the potential form of いただく turns
the humble frame into an actual question.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)、休(やす)まさせていただきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)、<b>休(やす)ませていただきます</b>。

休(やす)む is an う-verb and takes `せる`, not `させる`. Only る-verbs, する and
来(く)る take the `させ` form; the mistaken extra `さ` here is
さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば).

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)休(やす)ませていただきます。
(b) 明日(あした)休(やす)ませていただけませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) is an announcement dressed in humble clothing: the speaker has decided
to take the day off and is informing you courteously. Sentence (b) actually asks,
and leaves the decision with the listener. Say (a) to a manager who has already
approved the leave; say (b) when you still need approval, or you will sound as
though you are helping yourself to it.

</details>
