# N3 - Trying, having to, and having no choice


---

# ～(よ)うとする - try to

**Meaning** · make a move to do something - the attempt itself, with the result left hanging

The volitional form carries the will; `とする` turns that will into a physical
move. <code>～(よ)うとする</code> photographs the effort, not the outcome - and
Japanese only bothers to photograph the effort when the outcome was not the
obvious one. That is why so many of these sentences end in `が` or `けれど`.

## Formation

The volitional is frozen. `する` at the end is an ordinary verb and carries all the
tense and politeness for the whole phrase.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + とする | 押(お)す → 押(お)そうとする |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + とする | 逃(に)げる → 逃(に)げようとする |
| Verb (する) | しよう + とする | 説明(せつめい)する → 説明(せつめい)しようとする |
| Verb (来(く)る) | こよう + とする | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようとする |
| Attempt already made | ～(よ)うとした | 押(お)そうとした |
| Attempt that ran into something | ～(よ)うとしたが／けれど | 押(お)そうとしたが、重(おも)かった |
| Attempt plus its consequence | ～(よ)うとして + clause | 押(お)そうとして、腰(こし)を痛(いた)めた |
| Polite | する → します | 押(お)そうとしました |
| Even if you try | ～(よ)うとしても | 押(お)そうとしても |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb and る-verb are the same categories as 五段(ごだん) (Group 1)
and 一段(いちだん) (Group 2). The only stem to memorise here is <code>来(く)る</code>, whose
volitional is こよう - <code>来(こ)ようとした</code>, never くよう.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
妹(いもうと)が一人(ひとり)で靴(くつ)を<b>はこうとした</b>。
My little sister tried to put her shoes on by herself.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は何(なに)か<b>言(い)おうとして</b>、やめた。
He started to say something, then stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
重(おも)いドアを<b>押(お)そうとした</b>が、びくともしなかった。
I tried to push the heavy door, but it didn't budge an inch.
</pre>

<pre>
逃(に)げた鳥(とり)を<b>つかまえようとして</b>、木(き)に登(のぼ)りました。
I climbed the tree trying to catch the bird that had got out.
</pre>

<pre>
割(わ)れたコップを素手(すで)で<b>拾(ひろ)おうとして</b>、指(ゆび)を切(き)ってしまいました。
I went to pick up the broken glass with my bare hands and cut my finger.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが道(みち)に<b>飛(と)び出(だ)そうとした</b>ので、あわてて腕(うで)をつかんだ。
The child made a dash for the road, so I grabbed his arm.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)に<b>相談(そうだん)しようとした</b>のに、一日中(いちにちじゅう)席(せき)を外(はず)していて話(はな)せなかった。
I meant to talk it over with my boss, but he was away from his desk all day and I never got the chance.
</pre>

<pre>
停電(ていでん)のとき、懐中電灯(かいちゅうでんとう)を<b>探(さが)そうとしました</b>が、真(ま)っ暗(くら)でどこにあるのか分(わ)かりませんでした。
When the power went out I tried to find a torch, but it was pitch dark and I had no idea where it was.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>思(おも)い出(だ)そうとしても</b>、その人(ひと)の名前(なまえ)だけが出(で)てこないんです。
However hard I try to remember, that one person's name just won't come to me.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The single most useful fact: **in the past tense, the attempt normally failed.**
If you tried and got there, Japanese reports the arrival - 
<code>窓(まど)を閉(し)めました</code> - and says nothing about trying. So
<code>閉(し)めようとしました</code> tells your listener the window is still
open. English "I tried to" is often modest understatement for success; this
pattern is not.

The `て`-form is where the pattern earns its keep at N3.
<code>～(よ)うとして</code> plus a following clause says *the attempt is what
caused what came next*: 拾(ひろ)おうとして指(ゆび)を切(き)った - the
cutting happened in the course of the reaching. This is the natural way to narrate
a small accident, and it is far more common in speech than the textbook
<code>～(よ)うとしたが</code>.

The subject has to have a will. That is what separates this sense from the "about
to" sense, where a typhoon or a sunset can be the subject. Here, only a creature
that can decide something gets to try.

For **your own standing effort** the plain non-past is wrong:
❌<code>毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)こうとしています</code>. A policy you are
keeping up is <code>～ようにしている</code>. The plain non-past
<code>～(よ)うとする</code> is comfortable in general statements about people - 
人(ひと)は楽(らく)な道(みち)を選(えら)ぼうとする - where it describes a
tendency rather than one person's morning.

Register is neutral; `する` takes whatever politeness the sentence needs. In very
casual speech the whole thing is often replaced by
<code>～(し)かけた</code> or by <code>一応(いちおう)やってみた</code>, so if
you hear <code>開(あ)けようとしたんだけど</code> in conversation, note that it
already sounds a shade more deliberate than the alternatives.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うとする vs ～ようと思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～ようと思(おも)う</code> happens in your head;
<code>～(よ)うとする</code> happens with your hands. The first is a decision, the
second is a movement.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、車(くるま)を<b>買(か)おうと思(おも)っています</b>。
I'm thinking of buying a car next year.
</pre>

<pre>
車(くるま)を<b>買(か)おうとした</b>ら、貯金(ちょきん)が足(た)りませんでした。
I went to buy a car and found I didn't have enough saved.
</pre>

The first sentence is compatible with never going near a dealership. The second
one puts you in front of the price tag. This is also why
<code>～ようと思(おも)う</code> is the polite way to announce a plan and
<code>～(よ)うとする</code> is not: telling your boss
<code>辞(や)めようとしています</code> sounds like you are halfway out of the
building.

### ～(よ)うとする vs ～てみる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are "try", but they doubt different things.
<code>～(よ)うとする</code> doubts whether the action can happen at all.
`～てみる` takes the action for granted and doubts what it will be *like*.

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいアプリを<b>使(つか)ってみました</b>が、前(まえ)のほうが便利(べんり)でした。
I gave the new app a go, but the old one was handier.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいアプリを<b>使(つか)おうとしました</b>が、登録(とうろく)できませんでした。
I tried to use the new app, but I couldn't even register.
</pre>

In the first the app ran. In the second it never opened. Because they are about
different things, they stack quite happily:
<code>使(つか)ってみようとした</code> - "I made an attempt at giving it a go."

### ～(よ)うとする vs ～ようにする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～(よ)うとする</code> is one moment of trying.
<code>～ようにする</code> is a rule you have set yourself and are keeping to.

</div>

<pre>
エレベーターを<b>使(つか)わないようにしています</b>。
I make a point of not using the lift.
</pre>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)を<b>上(のぼ)ろうとしました</b>が、足(あし)が痛(いた)くて無理(むり)でした。
I tried to go up the stairs, but my leg hurt too much.
</pre>

Anything with <code>毎日(まいにち)</code>, <code>最近(さいきん)</code> or
なるべく in it wants <code>～ようにする</code>. Anything with
<code>その時(とき)</code> in it wants <code>～(よ)うとする</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 鍵(かぎ)を探(さが)す**とした** → ✅ 鍵(かぎ)を探(さが)<b>そうとした</b>
  `とする` here attaches to the **volitional**, not the dictionary form. Dictionary
  form + とする exists, but it is a separate written pattern meaning "supposing
  that", and a learner who mixes them will be misread.

- ❌ 荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)び**ましょうとしました** → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)<b>ぼうとしました</b>
  Only the final `する` gets the polite ending. The volitional inside stays plain - 
  ましょう never appears in front of `と`.

- ❌ その映画(えいが)を見(み)**ようとしました**。とても面白(おもしろ)かったです。 → ✅ その映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>てみました</b>。
  You watched it and formed an opinion, so the attempt succeeded. A successful
  "try it and see" is `てみる`.

- ❌ 夜(よる)十一時(じゅういちじ)までに寝(ね)**ようとしています** → ✅ 夜(よる)十一時(じゅういちじ)までに寝(ね)<b>るようにしています</b>
  A bedtime you are trying to keep is a standing policy, not a single lunge at the
  duvet.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)が島(しま)に近(ちか)づ**こうとしました** → ✅ 台風(たいふう)が島(しま)に近(ちか)づ<b>こうとしていました</b>
  A typhoon cannot attempt anything. With a subject that has no will, only the
  "on the verge of" reading is available, and that reading needs `ている`.

## Practice

**1.** バスに乗(の)____としたとき、定期券(ていきけん)を家(いえ)に忘(わす)れたことに気(き)づいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

バスに乗(の)<b>ろう</b>としたとき…

<code>乗(の)る</code> is a う-verb, so the volitional is
<code>乗(の)ろう</code>. <code>～(よ)うとしたとき</code> pins the sentence to the
instant before boarding, which is exactly when a missing pass would announce
itself.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 去年(きょねん)から、朝(あさ)ごはんを必(かなら)ず（食(た)べようとしています・食(た)べるようにしています）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

必(かなら)ず<b>食(た)べるようにしています</b>。

<code>去年(きょねん)から</code> and <code>必(かなら)ず</code> make this a rule
you have been keeping for a year - a standing policy, which is
<code>～ようにする</code>. <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> would describe you at one
particular breakfast, fork in hand.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I tried to open the jar, but the lid wouldn't come off."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

びんを<b>開(あ)けようとしました</b>が、ふたが取(と)れませんでした。

The lid stayed on, so the action never completed - that is exactly the job of
<code>～(よ)うとした</code>. Using <code>開(あ)けてみました</code> would say the
jar opened and you looked inside.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は立(た)つとして、テーブルに手(て)をついた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は立(た)<b>とうとして</b>、テーブルに手(て)をついた。
"He put a hand on the table as he went to stand up."

<code>立(た)つ</code> is a う-verb ending in つ, so the volitional is
<code>立(た)とう</code>. The `て`-form then links the attempt to what it caused - 
the hand on the table happened in the course of getting up.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けてみました。
(b) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けようとしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the window opened. `てみる` is "do it and see what happens", so the
interesting part is what followed - a breeze, a noise from the street, the smell
of rain. The action itself was never in doubt.

(b) says the window did not open, or at least that something interrupted you. The
interesting part is the obstacle, and a listener will wait for the `が` clause that
explains it. Said with no follow-up, (b) sounds unfinished.

</details>

# ～(よ)うとする - be about to

**Meaning** · on the very point of happening - the instant before the event tips over

Same three syllables as the "try to" pattern, but a different job. Here nobody is
trying anything: <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> freezes the frame just before a
change completes. The winter that has not quite ended, the doors that have not
quite shut. Because no will is involved, the subject can be a season, a train or
a storm.

## Formation

This sense almost always wears `ている` or `ていた`, or else feeds into `とき` or
`ところ`. The bare non-past <code>～(よ)うとする</code> does **not** carry the
"about to" reading.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + としている | 終(お)わる → 終(お)わろうとしている |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + としている | 消(き)える → 消(き)えようとしている |
| Verb (する) | しよう + としている | 発車(はっしゃ)する → 発車(はっしゃ)しようとしている |
| Verb (来(く)る) | こよう + としている | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようとしている |
| Was on the point of | ～(よ)うとしていた | 消(き)えようとしていた |
| Just as it did | ～(よ)うとしたとき | 閉(し)まろうとしたとき |
| Right at that moment | ～(よ)うとしたところ(に・へ・を) | 出(で)ようとしたところに |
| Polite | している → しています | 終(お)わろうとしています |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verbs that turn up here are overwhelmingly **intransitive verbs of
change**: 始(はじ)まる, 終(お)わる, 沈(しず)む, 暮(く)れる,
消(き)える, 散(ち)る, 倒(たお)れる, 変(か)わる. If your verb is one of
those, the "be about to" reading is the only one available.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
長(なが)い冬(ふゆ)が<b>終(お)わろうとしている</b>。
The long winter is drawing to a close.
</pre>

<pre>
船(ふね)が<b>出(で)ようとしています</b>。
The ship is about to leave.
</pre>

<pre>
エレベーターのドアが<b>閉(し)まろうとした</b>とき、誰(だれ)かが大(おお)きな声(こえ)で呼(よ)び止(と)めた。
Just as the lift doors were closing, someone called out to stop them.
</pre>

<pre>
お風呂(ふろ)に<b>入(はい)ろうとしたところに</b>、電話(でんわ)がかかってきました。
The phone rang just as I was about to get in the bath.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)の電気(でんき)が<b>消(き)えようとしていた</b>ので、急(いそ)いで荷物(にもつ)をまとめました。
The lights in the hall were on the point of going out, so I gathered my things in a hurry.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい時代(じだい)が<b>始(はじ)まろうとしている</b>と、その日(ひ)の新聞(しんぶん)は書(か)いていた。
That day's paper wrote that a new age was about to begin.
</pre>

<pre>
眠(ねむ)りに<b>落(お)ちようとしていた</b>ところを、上(うえ)の階(かい)の物音(ものおと)に起(お)こされた。
I was just drifting off when a noise from upstairs woke me.
</pre>

<pre>
ホームで最終(さいしゅう)電車(でんしゃ)が<b>発車(はっしゃ)しようとしている</b>のを見(み)て、走(はし)るのをあきらめました。
Seeing the last train on the point of pulling out, I gave up on running for it.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)を<b>辞(や)めようとしていた</b>ちょうどその時(とき)、部長(ぶちょう)から異動(いどう)の話(はなし)が来(き)た。
Just when I was on the point of quitting, my manager raised the idea of a transfer.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The reliable test for which sense you are in is **the subject**. If it could not
possibly decide anything - a season, a candle, a train, a century - you are in the
"about to" sense, and no amount of context will make it mean "tried to". With a
person, the form is genuinely ambiguous, and this is harmless: both readings point
at the same instant, and the sentence is normally interrupted by something anyway.

The register is **narrative**. This pattern belongs to news copy, novels, essays
and the sort of speech that is consciously describing a scene. In ordinary
conversation people reach for もうすぐ, ところ or `～そう`:
<code>もうすぐ着(つ)くよ</code>, not
<code>着(つ)こうとしているよ</code>. Saying
<code>ご飯(はん)が炊(た)けようとしています</code> to a housemate sounds like
you are narrating a documentary about your rice cooker.

<code>今(いま)にも</code> pairs with it naturally and raises the tension a
notch: <code>今(いま)にも倒(たお)れようとしている</code>. So does
<code>まさに</code> in written Japanese.

One boundary worth marking: this is about a **moment**, not about the near future.
Next week's roadworks are not
<code>始(はじ)まろうとしています</code>; they simply
<code>始(はじ)まります</code>. If you can attach a date to it, you have left the
pattern's territory.

Finally, the `ところ` frame is the most conversational corner of this sense.
<code>～(よ)うとしたところに</code> plus an interruption - 
a phone call, a visitor, rain - is a small, very common storytelling move, and it
survives in casual speech where the plain `ている` form would sound stiff.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うとしている vs ～ところだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ところだ` needs a subject with a schedule; it is the neutral
"I'm just about to". <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> is a camera angle, and it works
on things that never planned anything.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)から出(で)かける<b>ところです</b>。
I'm just heading out now.
</pre>

<pre>
雪(ゆき)が屋根(やね)から<b>落(お)ちようとしている</b>。
The snow is on the point of sliding off the roof.
</pre>

Swap them and both go wrong. Snow has no `ところ` of its own to be standing at,
and narrating your own departure with
<code>出(で)かけようとしています</code> makes you sound like a character in a
film rather than a person telling a friend where you are.

### ～(よ)うとしている vs ～かけている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> means it has **not started**.
`～かけている` means it **started and stopped partway**.

</div>

<pre>
ろうそくの火(ひ)が<b>消(き)えようとしている</b>。
The candle is about to go out.
</pre>

<pre>
ろうそくの火(ひ)が<b>消(き)えかけている</b>。
The candle is guttering out.
</pre>

The first candle is still burning steadily; you can see that it has almost no wax
left. The second is already halfway gone - the flame is failing as you watch. The
same split explains
<code>読(よ)みかけの本(ほん)</code> (a book you are partway through) against
<code>読(よ)もうとしている本(ほん)</code> (one you have not opened).

### ～(よ)うとしている vs 今(いま)にも～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "any second now", but `～そうだ` is a **guess read
off the surface** of something, while <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> reports the
event as genuinely underway.

</div>

<pre>
棚(たな)の箱(はこ)が<b>落(お)ちそうです</b>。
That box on the shelf looks like it's going to fall.
</pre>

<pre>
棚(たな)の箱(はこ)が<b>落(お)ちようとしています</b>。
The box on the shelf is on the point of falling.
</pre>

The first is your inference from how the box is sitting; it may sit there all
year. The second commits: the box is going. In warnings and in speech, `そうだ` is
almost always what you want - it is the one that leaves you room to be wrong.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 月(つき)が出(で)**ようとする**。 → ✅ 月(つき)が出(で)<b>ようとしている</b>。
  The "about to" reading lives in `ている`. Without it, the plain non-past reads as
  a general statement about attempting, which the moon cannot do.

- ❌ バスが来(き)**ようとしている**。 → ✅ バスが来(こ)<b>ようとしている</b>。
  The volitional of <code>来(く)る</code> is こよう, not きよう. The ます-stem
  reading き belongs to <code>来(き)ます</code> and nowhere near this pattern.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)、駅前(えきまえ)の工事(こうじ)が始(はじ)**まろうとしています**。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、駅前(えきまえ)の工事(こうじ)が始(はじ)<b>まります</b>。
  A dated future event is just the plain non-past. This pattern is for the second
  before something happens, not for next week.

- ❌ もうすぐ映画(えいが)を見(み)**ようとしています**。 → ✅ もうすぐ映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>るところです</b>。
  About yourself, with a plan, `ところだ` is the natural form.
  <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> here narrates you from outside, which is why it
  sounds oddly grand.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わろうと**しましたとき** → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わろうと<b>したとき</b>
  A clause in front of `とき` takes the plain form. Politeness goes on the end of
  the sentence, not inside it.

## Practice

**1.** 太陽(たいよう)が山(やま)の向(む)こうに沈(しず)____としていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

太陽(たいよう)が山(やま)の向(む)こうに沈(しず)<b>もう</b>としていた。

<code>沈(しず)む</code> is a う-verb ending in む, so the volitional is
<code>沈(しず)もう</code>. The sun has no will, which confirms this is the "on
the point of" sense rather than an attempt.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 屋根(やね)の雪(ゆき)が（落(お)ちそうです・落(お)ちようとしています）から、下(した)を通(とお)らないでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

屋根(やね)の雪(ゆき)が<b>落(お)ちそうです</b>から…

A warning is an inference - you are reading the risk off the roof, not announcing
that the snow is already moving. `～そうだ` is the form for that.
<code>落(お)ちようとしています</code> would claim the fall has begun, which
would make "don't walk under it" oddly late advice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Just as I was about to leave the house, it started to rain."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

家(いえ)を<b>出(で)ようとしたとき</b>、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)しました。

<code>～(よ)うとしたとき</code> is the standard frame for "just as I was about to,
something cut in". <code>出(で)ようとしたところに</code> works equally well and
sounds a shade more conversational.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 一年(いちねん)が終(お)わろうとしていますので、来年(らいねん)の計画(けいかく)を立(た)てましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 一年(いちねん)が終(お)わろうと<b>している</b>ので、来年(らいねん)の計画(けいかく)を立(た)てましょう。

The sentence is fine in meaning; the error is placement of politeness. A clause in
front of `ので` normally takes the plain form, with ます kept for the sentence
ending. <code>終(お)わろうとしていますので</code> is not ungrammatical, but it
belongs to very formal announcements, not to a suggestion among colleagues.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 花(はな)が散(ち)ろうとしている。
(b) 花(はな)が散(ち)りかけている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says none of the petals have come down yet, but the moment is here - the tree
is at its last full day. It is a narrator's sentence, the kind that opens a
paragraph.

(b) says the falling has already begun and is partway through: petals on the
ground, gaps in the branches. `かける` is about an interrupted or incomplete
process, so it reports a state you can photograph, while (a) reports a threshold
about to be crossed.

</details>

# ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない

**Meaning** · won't even try - the person is unwilling, and the speaker is not pleased about it

Negating the attempt does something the plain negative cannot: it puts the other
person's **will** in the frame. <code>食(た)べない</code> reports that no eating
is happening. <code>食(た)べようとしない</code> says they have decided against
it, and you can hear the speaker's patience running out.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + としない | 動(うご)く → 動(うご)こうとしない |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + としない | 認(みと)める → 認(みと)めようとしない |
| Verb (する) | しよう + としない | 返事(へんじ)する → 返事(へんじ)しようとしない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | こよう + としない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようとしない |
| Past | ～(よ)うとしなかった | 認(みと)めようとしなかった |
| Polite | ～(よ)うとしません | 認(みと)めようとしません |
| Emphatic (contrastive) | ～(よ)うとはしない | 認(みと)めようとはしない |
| Strongest - "didn't even" | ～(よ)うともしない | 認(みと)めようともしない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative goes on `する` at the end, never on the verb
inside. ❌<code>認(みと)めまいとする</code> is not a variant of this pattern;
the shape is always positive volitional + <code>としない</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は部屋(へや)から<b>出(で)ようとしない</b>。
My son won't come out of his room.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)も席(せき)を<b>立(た)とうとしなかった</b>。
Nobody would get up from their seat.
</pre>

<pre>
その犬(いぬ)は知(し)らない人(ひと)に<b>近(ちか)づこうとしない</b>。
That dog won't go anywhere near strangers.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら謝(あやま)っても、彼女(かのじょ)は顔(かお)を<b>上(あ)げようとはしなかった</b>。
However much I apologised, she would not so much as look up.
</pre>

<pre>
同僚(どうりょう)は自分(じぶん)のミスを<b>認(みと)めようとしません</b>。
My colleague refuses to admit his own mistakes.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は足(あし)が痛(いた)いのに、病院(びょういん)へ<b>行(い)こうとしない</b>んです。
My grandmother's leg hurts, and still she won't go to the hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
市役所(しやくしょ)は住民(じゅうみん)の質問(しつもん)に<b>答(こた)えようともしなかった</b>。
The city office would not even attempt to answer the residents' questions.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)し合(あ)いは三時間(さんじかん)続(つづ)きましたが、どちらの側(がわ)も一歩(いっぽ)も<b>ゆずろうとしませんでした</b>。
The talks ran for three hours, but neither side would give an inch.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいやり方(かた)をどれだけ説明(せつめい)しても、ベテランの職人(しょくにん)たちは耳(みみ)を<b>貸(か)そうとしなかった</b>。
No matter how thoroughly the new method was explained, the veteran craftsmen wouldn't hear of it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This pattern is a **complaint**, and that is not a side effect - it is the reason
the pattern exists. Using it means asserting that you know what is going on inside
someone else's head. That makes it comfortable about children, animals, machines
and institutions, awkward about equals, and genuinely risky about people you owe
deference to. <code>部長(ぶちょう)は決(き)めようとしません</code> is not a
neutral status report; it is an accusation of obstruction, and it will be heard as
one.

Unlike the "try to" sense, the **non-past is completely natural here**, because it
describes a standing state of refusal rather than one moment of effort.
<code>食(た)べようとする</code> about a habit is odd;
<code>食(た)べようとしない</code> about a habit is the normal thing to say.

The three degrees are worth learning as a set:

- <code>～(よ)うとしない</code> - plain report of unwillingness.
- <code>～(よ)うとはしない</code> - the `は` sets this action against everything
  else the person did do: *that* one thing, they would not do. More pointed, and
  commoner in writing.
- <code>～(よ)うともしない</code> - "would not so much as". The most dismissive of
  the three, and often paired with どれだけ, いくら or 一度(いちど)も.

Applied to objects, the pattern is a deliberate personification and reads as
exasperated rather than mistaken: <code>車(くるま)が動(うご)こうとしない</code>
casts the car as sulking. That is a stylistic choice, not the default - a bus that
is merely late is <code>なかなか来(き)ません</code>.

About yourself, the present tense is strange - you would simply say
<code>やりたくない</code>. The past is fine in reflective narration:
<code>あのころの私(わたし)は誰(だれ)の話(はなし)も聞(き)こうとしなかった</code>,
looking back at your younger self as if at someone else.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うとしない vs ～たがらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～たがらない` says they **don't want to**.
<code>～(よ)うとしない</code> says they **won't do it**, whatever they may want.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)られ<b>たがりません</b>。
My little brother doesn't like having his photo taken.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は写真(しゃしん)の前(まえ)に<b>立(た)とうとしません</b>。
My little brother won't stand in front of the camera.
</pre>

`たがる` reports a desire you have inferred from the outside; the sentence is about
his feelings. <code>～(よ)うとしない</code> reports behaviour, and it works even
where desire is irrelevant - someone may badly want to apologise and still
<code>謝(あやま)ろうとしない</code>. That gap is the useful part: it is how you
describe pride, fear or stubbornness overriding what a person actually wants.

### ～(よ)うとしない vs ～てくれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～(よ)うとしない</code> criticises the other person's
attitude. `～てくれない` complains that **you** did not get what you needed.

</div>

<pre>
店員(てんいん)が説明(せつめい)し<b>てくれなかった</b>。
The assistant didn't explain it to me.
</pre>

<pre>
店員(てんいん)が説明(せつめい)し<b>ようとしなかった</b>。
The assistant wouldn't even try to explain.
</pre>

The first is about the loss to you; the assistant might have been busy or new. The
second is about the assistant's unwillingness. Stacking them makes the strongest
version of the complaint:
<code>説明(せつめい)しようとしてくれなかった</code> - they would not make the
effort *for me*.

### ～(よ)うとしない vs ～(よ)うとはしない vs ～(よ)うともしない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare `と` states it, `とは` contrasts it against what they did
do, `とも` says they did not even get as far as starting.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は理由(りゆう)を<b>話(はな)そうとしなかった</b>。
He wouldn't tell me why.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は言(い)い訳(わけ)はしたが、理由(りゆう)を<b>話(はな)そうとはしなかった</b>。
He made excuses, but the actual reason he would not give.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は理由(りゆう)を<b>話(はな)そうともしなかった</b>。
He didn't even attempt to tell me why.
</pre>

The middle sentence needs the first clause to earn its `は`; without something to
contrast against, <code>とはしない</code> can sound as though a comparison has gone
missing. The third needs no setup at all - `も` is doing the work of "not even" by
itself.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 子(こ)どもが靴(くつ)を履(は)く**としない** → ✅ 子(こ)どもが靴(くつ)を履(は)<b>こうとしない</b>
  The dictionary form never appears here. It is volitional + <code>としない</code>,
  so <code>履(は)く</code> becomes <code>履(は)こう</code>.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ**ようとしません**。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>ません</b>。
  About your own present habit, the pattern is odd - it makes you sound like a
  bystander disapproving of yourself. Use the plain negative, or
  <code>食(た)べたくないんです</code> if you want the reason in.

- ❌ バスがなかなか来(こ)**ようとしません**。 → ✅ バスがなかなか来(き)<b>ません</b>。
  A late bus is not refusing anything. Reserve the pattern for subjects with a
  will, or for a personification you actually mean.

- ❌（to your manager）部長(ぶちょう)はメールに返事(へんじ)を**しようとしませんね**。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、メールの件(けん)、まだお返事(へんじ)をいただいていないのですが。
  Said to the person themselves, this accuses them of deliberate obstruction. When
  the subject is someone you must be polite to, drop the pattern entirely and
  report the fact.

## Practice

**1.** 赤(あか)ちゃんが眠(ねむ)____としないので、母(はは)は一晩中(ひとばんじゅう)だっこしていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

赤(あか)ちゃんが眠(ねむ)<b>ろう</b>としないので…

<code>眠(ねむ)る</code> is a う-verb, so the volitional is
<code>眠(ねむ)ろう</code>. The pattern fits a baby perfectly: it is exactly the
sentence of a parent who has decided the child is doing this on purpose.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)は人前(ひとまえ)で歌(うた)い（たがりません・ようとしません）。カラオケでもずっとすみに座(すわ)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are possible, and they say different things.
<b>歌(うた)いたがりません</b> reports that he has no desire to sing, which fits
someone quietly sitting in the corner.
<b>歌(うた)おうとしません</b> would add that people have been pushing him and he
is holding out. Given that the second sentence only describes him sitting there,
<b>歌(うた)いたがりません</b> is the more natural fit.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I offered several times, but he wouldn't take the money."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何度(なんど)もすすめましたが、彼(かれ)はお金(かね)を<b>受(う)け取(と)ろうとしませんでした</b>。

The point of the sentence is that he could have taken it and chose not to - that
is <code>～(よ)うとしない</code> rather than a plain
<code>受(う)け取(と)りませんでした</code>, which would merely say no money
changed hands.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 雨(あめ)がやもうとしないので、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 雨(あめ)が<b>やまない</b>ので、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。

Rain has no will, so it cannot refuse to stop. The plain negative is what you
want. <code>やもうとしない</code> is possible only as a conscious personification
in literary writing - in a report of a cancelled match it just reads as an error.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)はこちらを見(み)なかった。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)はこちらを見(み)ようともしなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) records that her eyes did not come this way. She may not have known you were
there; the sentence takes no view on why.

(b) says she knew perfectly well and would not even turn her head. `とも` adds
"not even", so the sentence measures her refusal against the smallest possible
effort and finds she did not make it. It is the harshest of the three degrees, and
it tells you as much about the speaker's feelings as about hers.

</details>

# ～べき

**Meaning** · ought to; is the right thing to do - measured against a principle, not against convenience

`べき` passes a **judgement**. It says the action is correct - morally, socially,
professionally - and it does so with the weight of a standard behind it. That is
what separates it from `ほうがいい`, which merely says the action would work out
better for you.

## Formation

`べき` is a survival from classical Japanese, which is why it attaches to the
dictionary form and why `する` has two shapes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + べきだ | 守(まも)る → 守(まも)るべきだ |
| Verb (する) | するべき / すべき | 報告(ほうこく)する → 報告(ほうこく)するべきだ・報告(ほうこく)すべきだ |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(く)るべき | 来(く)る → 来(く)るべきだ |
| Before a noun | ～べき + noun | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)むべき本(ほん) |
| Regret about the past | ～べきだった | 言(い)う → 言(い)うべきだった |
| Polite | ～べきです・べきでした | 守(まも)るべきです |
| Noun / な-adjective (written) | ～である + べき | 公平(こうへい) → 公平(こうへい)であるべきだ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>するべき</code> and <code>すべき</code> mean the same thing.
<code>すべき</code> is the older form and is preferred in writing, editorials and
formal speech; <code>するべき</code> is easier on the ear in conversation. Neither
is wrong. In noun-modifying position the `だ` disappears - 
<code>言(い)うべきこと</code>, not ❌<code>言(い)うべきだこと</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もっと野菜(やさい)を<b>食(た)べるべきだ</b>。
You ought to eat more vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)は<b>守(まも)るべきです</b>。
Promises should be kept.
</pre>

<pre>
こういうことは、早(はや)めに上司(じょうし)に<b>報告(ほうこく)すべきだ</b>。
Something like this ought to be reported to your manager early.
</pre>

<pre>
あの時(とき)、はっきり「できません」と<b>言(い)うべきだった</b>。
I should have said "I can't" clearly at the time.
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)のうちに<b>読(よ)むべき</b>本(ほん)を、先生(せんせい)が紹介(しょうかい)してくださいました。
My teacher recommended some books one ought to read while still a student.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)に、資料(しりょう)に目(め)を<b>通(とお)しておくべきでした</b>。
I should have looked through the handouts before the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
災害(さいがい)に備(そな)えて、水(みず)と食料(しょくりょう)を<b>用意(ようい)しておくべきだ</b>と思(おも)います。
I think we ought to keep water and food ready in case of a disaster.
</pre>

<pre>
本来(ほんらい)この制度(せいど)は、一番(いちばん)困(こま)っている人(ひと)を<b>助(たす)けるべきもの</b>です。
This system is supposed, by its very nature, to help the people in the greatest difficulty.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)したときにどうするかを、チーム全員(ぜんいん)で<b>決(き)めておくべきだった</b>と後悔(こうかい)しています。
I regret that we didn't agree as a team, in advance, what to do if it went wrong.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`べき` is heavy. It appeals to something outside the speaker - a principle, a duty,
the way things are done - and that is why it turns up constantly in newspaper
editorials, essays, court judgements and meeting minutes, and why it can land
badly in a kitchen conversation. Told
<code>もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるべきだ</code>, a friend hears a small
sermon.

Aimed at a listener, therefore, `べき` needs softening, and Japanese has a standard
kit for it:

- <code>～べきだと思(おも)います</code> - makes it your opinion rather than the
  law.
- <code>～べきではないでしょうか</code> - a rhetorical question, the politest form
  of the same claim, very common in meetings.
- <code>～べきかもしれません</code> - hedged almost to invisibility.

Bare <code>～べきだ</code> pointed at someone senior to you is close to
insubordinate. Between friends it is fine if you are half-joking, and it is
completely fine when the subject is *you* - 私(わたし)が行(い)くべきでした - 
or an abstraction: 政治(せいじ)は公平(こうへい)であるべきだ.

The past form is the one learners under-use.
<code>～べきだった</code> is the everyday way to express regret about a choice you
made: 買(か)うべきだった, 聞(き)いておくべきだった. It is neither formal nor
preachy in that use, because the person being judged is yourself.

<div class="note">

**Note** - you will meet the same word as <code>～べく</code> ("in order to") and
<code>～べからず</code> ("do not") on signs and in stiff writing:
立(た)ち入(い)るべからず. Both are frozen classical leftovers; recognise them,
but write <code>～ために</code> and <code>～ないでください</code> yourself.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～べきだ vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` is an obligation **imposed** on you: a rule,
a deadline, a law. `べきだ` is a judgement you are **making**: this is the right
thing.

</div>

<pre>
外国人(がいこくじん)は在留(ざいりゅう)カードを持(も)ち歩(ある)か<b>なければなりません</b>。
Foreign residents are required to carry their residence card.
</pre>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)にもっと情報(じょうほう)を出(だ)す<b>べきです</b>。
More information ought to be made available to international students.
</pre>

The first is the law; you have no view about it, you are reporting it. The second
is an argument, and someone could disagree with you. Swap them and the first
sentence becomes an opinion about immigration policy, while the second sounds as
if a regulation exists that you have not cited.

This also explains a common error. If the requirement is already binding, `べき` is
the weaker and stranger choice: a visa application that *must* include a photo
takes <code>入(い)れなければなりません</code>.

### ～べきだ vs ～ほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほうがいい` compares outcomes for **this person, this
situation**. `べきだ` measures the action against a **standard** and is indifferent
to whether it suits you.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるなら、今日(きょう)は休(やす)んだ<b>ほうがいいですよ</b>。
If you've got a fever, you'd be better off taking today off.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるのに出勤(しゅっきん)させる会社(かいしゃ)のほうが、考(かんが)えを改(あらた)める<b>べきです</b>。
It's the company making people come in with a fever that ought to change its thinking.
</pre>

`ほうがいい` is advice and is heard as kindness. `べき` is a verdict and is heard as
criticism - which is exactly why the second sentence uses it. For everyday
suggestions to a friend, `ほうがいい` is almost always the safer form.

### ～べきだった vs ～ばよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both look back at a wrong choice. `ばよかった` is **wishing**;
`べきだった` is **judging yourself**.

</div>

<pre>
もっと早(はや)く出(で)れ<b>ばよかった</b>。
I wish I'd left earlier.
</pre>

<pre>
もっと早(はや)く出(で)る<b>べきだった</b>。
I should have left earlier.
</pre>

The first is a sigh; it fits missing a nice sunset. The second accepts fault and
fits missing a flight you were responsible for. `ばよかった` can also be used about
things nobody controlled - <code>晴(は)れればよかったのに</code> - where
`べきだった` is impossible, because weather cannot be blamed.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 教師(きょうし)は公平(こうへい)**べきだ**。 → ✅ 教師(きょうし)は公平(こうへい)<b>であるべきだ</b>。
  `べき` needs a verb in front of it. Nouns and な-adjectives get there through
  `である`.

- ❌ 注意(ちゅうい)**しるべき** → ✅ 注意(ちゅうい)<b>するべき</b>・注意(ちゅうい)<b>すべき</b>
  `する` gives two legitimate forms and neither is しる. Pick <code>すべき</code>
  for writing, <code>するべき</code> for speech.

- ❌ ビザの申請(しんせい)には写真(しゃしん)を入(い)れる**べきです**。 → ✅ ビザの申請(しんせい)には写真(しゃしん)を入(い)れ<b>なければなりません</b>。
  A published requirement is not your judgement. Reporting a rule takes
  `なければならない`.

- ❌（to your manager）部長(ぶちょう)、もっと休(やす)む**べきです**。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、少(すこ)しお休(やす)みになったほうがよろしいのではないでしょうか。
  Bare `べきだ` aimed upwards is a reprimand. Move to `ほうがいい`, or at the very
  least to <code>～べきではないでしょうか</code>.

- ❌ 言(い)うべき**だ**ことがあります。 → ✅ 言(い)う<b>べき</b>ことがあります。
  In front of a noun, `べき` stands alone. The `だ` only appears at the end of a
  sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 自分(じぶん)が悪(わる)いと分(わ)かっているなら、すぐに謝(あやま)____だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すぐに謝(あやま)<b>るべき</b>だ。

`べき` attaches to the dictionary form, so <code>謝(あやま)る</code> stays
whole. The sentence is a moral judgement - apologising is the right thing - which
is precisely `べき` territory rather than `ほうがいい`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 試験(しけん)の日(ひ)は受験票(じゅけんひょう)を持(も)ってこ（なければなりません・るべきです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

持(も)ってこ<b>なければなりません</b>。

The exam board requires the admission slip; this is a rule being reported, not an
opinion you are advancing. <code>持(も)ってくるべきです</code> would suggest you
personally think it would be a good idea, which understates a hard requirement.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I should have asked her before I decided."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

決(き)める前(まえ)に、彼女(かのじょ)に<b>聞(き)くべきでした</b>。

<code>～べきだった／べきでした</code> is the standard way to say "I should have",
with the fault accepted. <code>聞(き)けばよかった</code> is also possible but
softer - closer to "I wish I'd asked" than to "that was my mistake".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この問題(もんだい)については、全員(ぜんいん)で話(はな)し合(あ)うべきだことだと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この問題(もんだい)については、全員(ぜんいん)で話(はな)し合(あ)う<b>べき</b>ことだと思(おも)います。

`べき` modifies a noun directly, with no `だ` in between. The `だ` you already have
at the end of <code>ことだ</code> is doing the sentence-final work.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この映画(えいが)は見(み)たほうがいいよ。
(b) この映画(えいが)は見(み)るべきだよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a friendly recommendation aimed at you: you would enjoy it, so go and see
it. Nothing is at stake if you don't.

(b) appeals to a standard outside both of you - the film matters, and anyone
serious about films is failing in some small duty by missing it. Between close
friends this comes across as enthusiastic overstatement, and that is often exactly
the joke; said flatly to someone you do not know well, it sounds like being told
off.

</details>

# ～べきではない

**Meaning** · ought not to; it is wrong to do this - a verdict, not a rule posted on a wall

The negative of `べき` keeps everything that made `べき` heavy. It appeals to a
principle and finds the action falls short of it. Nobody is forbidding you - 
`べきではない` has no authority behind it, only judgement - which is why it feels
at once softer than a prohibition and harder to argue with.

## Formation

The negative goes on `べき`, never on the verb inside it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + べきではない | 言(い)う → 言(い)うべきではない |
| Verb (する) | するべきではない / すべきではない | 優先(ゆうせん)する → 優先(ゆうせん)すべきではない |
| Casual | ～べきじゃない | 言(い)うべきじゃない |
| Polite | ～べきではありません | 言(い)うべきではありません |
| Regret about the past | ～べきではなかった | 言(い)うべきではなかった |
| Polite past | ～べきではありませんでした | 言(い)うべきではありませんでした |
| Noun / な-adjective (written) | ～である + べきではない | あいまい → あいまいであるべきではない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>言(い)わないべきだ</code> does not exist. Japanese
negates the judgement, not the action: <code>言(い)うべきではない</code>. The
same is true of the past - <code>言(い)うべきではなかった</code>, never
❌<code>言(い)わないべきだった</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
人(ひと)の日記(にっき)を<b>読(よ)むべきではない</b>。
You shouldn't read other people's diaries.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなことを<b>言(い)うべきじゃない</b>よ。
You shouldn't say things like that.
</pre>

<pre>
運転(うんてん)するなら、お酒(さけ)を<b>飲(の)むべきではありません</b>。
If you're driving, you shouldn't be drinking.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの前(まえ)で夫婦(ふうふ)げんかを<b>するべきではなかった</b>。
We shouldn't have had that argument in front of the children.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)の意見(いけん)だけで全部(ぜんぶ)を<b>決(き)めるべきではない</b>と思(おも)います。
I don't think everything should be decided on one person's opinion alone.
</pre>

<pre>
あんな高(たか)い時計(とけい)を、給料(きゅうりょう)が入(はい)る前(まえ)に<b>買(か)うべきではありませんでした</b>。
I shouldn't have bought a watch that expensive before payday.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)は社員(しゃいん)の健康(けんこう)より利益(りえき)を<b>優先(ゆうせん)すべきではない</b>。
A company should not put profit ahead of its employees' health.
</pre>

<pre>
知(し)らないことを、知(し)っているふりをして<b>話(はな)すべきではありません</b>。
You shouldn't talk about things you don't know as if you did.
</pre>

<pre>
うわさだけで人(ひと)を<b>判断(はんだん)すべきではない</b>というのが、祖父(そふ)の口(くち)ぐせでした。
"Never judge someone on rumour alone" was my grandfather's favourite saying.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The force of `べきではない` is **moral**, not institutional. It is what an editorial
says about a government, what a parent says about lying, what you say to yourself
at three in the morning. There is no penalty attached and no authority cited; the
sentence simply asserts that the action is wrong, and invites you to agree.

Because of that, aimed straight at a listener it preaches. Between close friends
<code>言(い)うべきじゃないよ</code> is normal and even warm - the casual
contraction takes most of the weight off - but <code>べきではありません</code> to
someone you barely know reads as a dressing-down. Softening works the same way as
with the positive: put <code>と思(おも)います</code> on it, or move to
`ないほうがいい`.

The past form is the everyday one.
<code>～べきではなかった</code> is how you own a mistake, and it carries no
preachiness at all when the subject is yourself:
言(い)うべきではなかった, 信(しん)じるべきではなかった.

<div class="note">

**Note** - one trap that catches almost everybody.
<code>～べきではないでしょうか</code> and <code>～べきではないか</code> are
**rhetorical questions**, and they argue *for* the action, not against it.
<code>もっと話(はな)し合(あ)うべきではないでしょうか</code> means "shouldn't we
talk about it more?" - that is, we should. If you want to argue against something
politely, you need
<code>～べきではないと思(おも)います</code>.

</div>

Register is otherwise straightforward: <code>すべきではない</code> for writing and
formal speech, <code>するべきではない</code> and <code>べきじゃない</code> for
conversation. The word is at home in essays, opinion columns and meetings, and
slightly stiff in small talk.

## Contrast

### ～べきではない vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てはいけない` is a **prohibition**: someone with authority
says no, and there are consequences. `べきではない` is an **opinion about right and
wrong**, with nobody enforcing it.

</div>

<pre>
館内(かんない)では写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っ<b>てはいけません</b>。
Photography is not permitted inside the building.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)の許可(きょか)なく写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る<b>べきではありません</b>。
You shouldn't photograph someone without their permission.
</pre>

The first is the museum's rule; you would find it on a sign, and breaking it gets
you stopped. The second is an argument about how people should behave, and it
holds in places where no such sign exists. Put `べきではない` on a notice and it
reads oddly - as if the building were expressing a hope rather than setting a
rule.

`てはいけない` also works for immediate, physical danger, where `べきではない` is far
too abstract: <code>熱(あつ)いから触(さわ)ってはいけません</code>.

### ～べきではない vs ～ないほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないほうがいい` weighs **outcomes for this person**.
`べきではない` weighs the action against a **principle** and does not care whether
it suits you.

</div>

<pre>
夜(よる)遅(おそ)くにコーヒーを飲(の)ま<b>ないほうがいいですよ</b>。
You're better off not drinking coffee late at night.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の失敗(しっぱい)を笑(わら)う<b>べきではありません</b>。
You should not laugh at other people's mistakes.
</pre>

Swap them and both go wrong in an instructive way.
<code>コーヒーを飲(の)むべきではない</code> turns caffeine into a moral failing.
<code>笑(わら)わないほうがいい</code> turns cruelty into a tactical error - as
though the problem were that you might get caught. For advice to a friend,
`ないほうがいい` is nearly always the right register; save `べきではない` for when
you actually mean to make a judgement.

### ～べきではない vs ～べきではないでしょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - adding <code>でしょうか</code> flips the meaning. The statement
opposes the action; the question **recommends** it.

</div>

<pre>
この案(あん)は今(いま)決(き)める<b>べきではない</b>。
We shouldn't decide this proposal now.
</pre>

<pre>
この案(あん)は今(いま)決(き)める<b>べきではないでしょうか</b>。
Shouldn't we decide this proposal now?
</pre>

The second sentence is arguing for a decision today. Japanese does this with
negative questions generally - <code>いいんじゃない?</code> is approval, not
doubt - but with `べき` the reversal catches learners because the words look so
much like a refusal. In a meeting, hearing
<code>見直(みなお)すべきではないでしょうか</code> means someone wants a review,
and nodding along while believing the opposite is an expensive mistake.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そのことは言(い)わ**ないべきだ**。 → ✅ そのことは言(い)う<b>べきではない</b>。
  The negation attaches to `べき`, not to the verb. There is no ないべき form.

- ❌（on a notice）ここでたばこを吸(す)う**べきではありません**。 → ✅ ここでたばこを吸(す)っ<b>てはいけません</b>。
  A posted rule is a prohibition. `べきではない` on a sign sounds like the building
  is sharing its personal views.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る**べきではない**。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ら<b>ないでほしい</b>。
  `べき` judges a chosen action. Weather chooses nothing, so there is nothing to
  judge - what you mean is a wish.

- ❌「この計画(けいかく)はやめるべきではないでしょうか」（intending "we shouldn't cancel this plan"） → ✅ この計画(けいかく)はやめる<b>べきではないと思(おも)います</b>。
  The <code>でしょうか</code> version argues *for* cancelling. To argue against
  something politely, keep the statement and soften it with
  <code>と思(おも)います</code>.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)、そんな言(い)い方(かた)を**するべきではありません**。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、少(すこ)し言(い)い方(かた)を変(か)え<b>ていただけないでしょうか</b>。
  Aimed upwards, `べきではない` is a reprimand. Reframe it as a request rather than
  a verdict.

## Practice

**1.** 人(ひと)から聞(き)いた秘密(ひみつ)を、勝手(かって)に他人(たにん)に話(はな)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

勝手(かって)に他人(たにん)に話(はな)<b>すべきではない</b>。

The sentence is a judgement about how one ought to behave, so it wants
`べきではない` rather than `ないほうがいい`, which would only say that letting the
secret out tends to backfire. <code>話(はな)すべきではない</code> and
<code>話(はな)すべきではありません</code> differ only in politeness.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 図書館(としょかん)の中(なか)では大(おお)きな声(こえ)で話(はな)（してはいけません・すべきではありません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

話(はな)<b>してはいけません</b>。

A library has rules and enforces them, so this is a prohibition.
<code>話(はな)すべきではありません</code> would recast a house rule as a moral
position, which is both weaker and stranger - it would suit an essay about
manners, not a librarian.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I shouldn't have lent him that much money."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)にあんなに大(おお)きな金額(きんがく)を<b>貸(か)すべきではありませんでした</b>。

<code>～べきではなかった／べきではありませんでした</code> is the standard "I
shouldn't have", with fault accepted. Note that the verb stays positive:
❌<code>貸(か)さないべきでした</code> is not available.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)に相談(そうだん)しないべきだったと後悔(こうかい)しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に相談(そうだん)<b>するべきではなかった</b>と後悔(こうかい)しています。

Two things: `べき` takes the positive dictionary form, and the negation goes on
`べき` itself. <code>相談(そうだん)すべきではなかった</code> is the same sentence
in a more formal key.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この問題(もんだい)は来月(らいげつ)まで待(ま)つべきではない。
(b) この問題(もんだい)は来月(らいげつ)まで待(ま)つべきではないでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) opposes waiting: the speaker wants the matter dealt with now, and is stating
that delay would be wrong.

(b) argues the opposite. It is a rhetorical negative question - "shouldn't we wait
until next month?" - and it is a polite way of proposing exactly the delay that
(a) rejects. The only difference on the page is <code>でしょうか</code>, which is
why this pair is worth memorising as a pair.

</details>

# ～こと - written rules and instructions

**Meaning** · you are to do this - the sentence-ending `こと` of notices, house rules and exam papers

A verb followed by a bare `こと` and a full stop is not a noun phrase that has lost
its verb. It is a complete sentence, and it issues an instruction. Nobody in
particular is being addressed: the rule simply exists, and anyone it applies to is
expected to follow it.

## Formation

Nothing follows the `こと`. No `だ`, no `です`, no `。ます`. The sentence stops.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + こと | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)すこと |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + こと | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)わないこと |
| Verb (する) | ～すること | 連絡(れんらく)する → 連絡(れんらく)すること |
| Verb (with ておく) | ～ておくこと | 読(よ)んでおく → 読(よ)んでおくこと |
| Terse memo style | Noun + のこと | 至急(しきゅう)連絡(れんらく) → 至急(しきゅう)連絡(れんらく)のこと |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can put `です` after it, it is not this pattern.
<code>静(しず)かにすること</code> is a rule;
<code>静(しず)かにすることです</code> is the *advice* pattern
<code>～ことだ</code>, which is a different thing said to a different person.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - only verbs work here. There is no ❌<code>静(しず)かなこと</code>
instruction; an adjective has to be turned into an action first - 
<code>静(しず)かにすること</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
教室(きょうしつ)では静(しず)かに<b>すること</b>。
Be quiet in the classroom.
</pre>

<pre>
使(つか)った道具(どうぐ)は元(もと)に<b>戻(もど)すこと</b>。
Return tools to where you found them.
</pre>

<pre>
遅(おく)れる場合(ばあい)は、必(かなら)ず連絡(れんらく)<b>すること</b>。
If you are going to be late, be sure to get in touch.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)中(ちゅう)は辞書(じしょ)を<b>使(つか)わないこと</b>。
Dictionaries are not to be used during the examination.
</pre>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)する前(まえ)に、名前(なまえ)が書(か)いてあるか確(たし)かめる<b>こと</b>。
Check that your name is written on it before handing it in.
</pre>

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)は決(き)められた場所(ばしょ)に止(と)め、鍵(かぎ)を<b>かけること</b>。
Park bicycles in the designated area and lock them.
</pre>

<pre>
アレルギーのある方(かた)は、申(もう)し込(こ)みの際(さい)に必(かなら)ず申(もう)し出(で)る<b>こと</b>。
Anyone with an allergy must declare it when applying.
</pre>

<pre>
見学(けんがく)を希望(きぼう)する学生(がくせい)は、前日(ぜんじつ)までに受付(うけつけ)に名前(なまえ)を書(か)いておく<b>こと</b>。
Students wishing to attend the site visit are to sign up at reception by the day before.
</pre>

<pre>
作業(さぎょう)が終(お)わったら、火(ひ)が消(き)えているかどうかを二人(ふたり)で確認(かくにん)する<b>こと</b>。
When work is finished, two people are to confirm that the fire is out.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** form, and specifically the written form of an institution
talking to whoever is covered by its rules. You meet it on classroom walls, in
staff handbooks, on the front page of exam papers, in club regulations and in the
list of conditions on an application form. It is often set out as a bulleted list,
one `こと` per line.

It has no politeness level, because it has no addressee. That is worth pausing on:
`てください` is polite because there is a person you are being polite *to*, whereas
`こと` speaks past everyone to the rule itself. The effect is impersonal and
slightly cold, which in a notice is exactly right.

Said to an actual human being, it is another matter. A manager who writes
<code>明日(あした)までに出(だ)すこと</code> in an email to one named person
sounds curt and rather military. It is not ungrammatical - some workplaces use it
deliberately for terse internal memos - but a learner should assume it will be
heard as brusque, and write <code>出(だ)してください</code> instead.

Spoken, it survives in one narrow place: a teacher or leader reading a list of
rules aloud, essentially quoting the notice. Outside that, it is not conversational
Japanese.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same word ends several other patterns that look identical on the
page. <code>～ことだ</code> gives advice, <code>～ことがある</code> reports
occasional events, <code>～ことにする</code> announces a decision. The instruction
`こと` is the one with **nothing after it**.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～こと vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てください` asks **a person**; `こと` states **a rule**. If you
could name who you are talking to, use `てください`.

</div>

<pre>
すみません、この紙(かみ)にお名前(なまえ)を書(か)い<b>てください</b>。
Excuse me - please write your name on this form.
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)で用紙(ようし)を受(う)け取(と)り、名前(なまえ)を書(か)く<b>こと</b>。
Collect a form at reception and write your name on it.
</pre>

The first is a receptionist speaking to the person in front of her. The second is
what the form itself says. Note also that `てください` can be softened
(<code>書(か)いていただけますか</code>) and `こと` cannot - there is no polite
version, because there is nobody to be polite to.

### ～こと vs ～ようにしてください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `こと` is a requirement. <code>～ようにしてください</code> asks
people to **make an effort** in that direction, and leaves room for exceptions.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の十分(じゅっぷん)前(まえ)までに集(あつ)まる<b>こと</b>。
Assemble by ten minutes before the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
なるべく早(はや)めに集(あつ)まる<b>ようにしてください</b>。
Please try to gather a little early if you can.
</pre>

The first admits no exceptions and reads as policy; the second is a request from
one person to a group and expects some slippage. A written instruction in a
customer-facing context - a hotel, a hospital - will usually choose
<code>ようにしてください</code> or the still politer
<code>お願(ねが)いいたします</code>, because `こと` addresses people as rule
subjects rather than as guests.

### ～こと vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `こと` **issues** the requirement. `なければならない` **reports**
that a requirement exists.

</div>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は保険(ほけん)に入(はい)る<b>こと</b>。
Participants are to take out insurance.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は保険(ほけん)に入(はい)ら<b>なければなりません</b>。
Participants have to take out insurance.
</pre>

Both appear in regulations, and they are close enough that many documents use them
interchangeably. The difference in feel: the first is the rule speaking in its own
voice, and belongs in the list of conditions itself; the second is someone
explaining the rule, and belongs in the covering letter or in speech. Only the
second can be said aloud comfortably to an applicant on the phone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 教室(きょうしつ)を出(で)るときは電気(でんき)を消(け)す**ことです**。 → ✅ 電気(でんき)を消(け)す<b>こと</b>。
  Adding `です` turns a notice into personal advice. On a wall, the bare form is
  what you want.

- ❌ 辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)わ**なくこと** → ✅ 辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)わ<b>ないこと</b>
  The negative is the plain ない-form. `なく` belongs to `なくて` and `なくても`, not
  here.

- ❌（email to one colleague）山田(やまだ)さん、水曜日(すいようび)までに返事(へんじ)をする**こと**。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さん、水曜日(すいようび)までにお返事(へんじ)をお願(ねが)いします。
  Aimed at a named person, `こと` reads as an order barked down a corridor. Use a
  request form.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)は**きれいなこと**。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)はきれいに<b>しておくこと</b>。
  Only verbs take the instruction `こと`. Turn the adjective into an action first.

## Practice

**1.** 実験(じっけん)室(しつ)に入(はい)る前(まえ)に、白衣(はくい)を着(き)____。（as a posted rule）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

白衣(はくい)を着(き)<b>ること</b>。

A rule on a laboratory door is issued to nobody in particular, which is exactly
the instruction `こと`. Note the bare ending: no `です`, and no `。ます`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （お客様(きゃくさま)へのご案内(あんない)）ロビーでは静(しず)かに（すること・していただきますようお願(ねが)いいたします）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

静(しず)かに<b>していただきますようお願(ねが)いいたします</b>。

Guests are being addressed as guests, so the notice needs a politeness level, and
`こと` has none. <code>すること</code> would be normal on a staff noticeboard and
distinctly unwelcoming in a hotel lobby.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese as a written club rule: "Members are not to bring food into the practice room."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部員(ぶいん)は練習室(れんしゅうしつ)に食(た)べ物(もの)を<b>持(も)ち込(こ)まないこと</b>。

The negative instruction is ない-form + `こと`. A club noticeboard is the classic
home of this pattern, and the impersonal tone is a feature rather than a problem.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 使用後(しようご)は、必(かなら)ず電源(でんげん)を切(き)ることです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 使用後(しようご)は、必(かなら)ず電源(でんげん)を切(き)る<b>こと</b>。

With `です` attached, the sentence becomes advice - "the thing to do is switch it
off" - which is a reasonable sentence but not a rule. A notice ends bare at
`こと`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)までにレポートを出(だ)すこと。
(b) 明日(あした)までにレポートを出(だ)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the rule as written on the assignment sheet. It speaks to the whole class at
once, has no politeness level, and does not invite a reply - you either comply or
you don't.

(b) is a person speaking to a person. It is polite, it can be softened further
(<code>出(だ)していただけますか</code>), and it allows the listener to say that
tomorrow is difficult. If a teacher said (a) aloud to one student, it would sound
as though the student were being quoted a regulation rather than asked.

</details>

# ～ことだ

**Meaning** · the thing to do is… - advice offered as the obvious answer to a problem

`～ことだ` hands over a remedy. It does not weigh options or hedge; it names the
one action that solves the problem, in the tone of somebody who has seen this
situation before. That confidence is the whole flavour of the pattern, and it is
also the reason it has to be used carefully.

## Formation

Only verbs. The sentence almost always follows a clause that sets up the problem - 
a `たら`, a `なら`, a `ければ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ことだ | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)むことだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことだ | 無理(むり)をする → 無理(むり)をしないことだ |
| Verb (する) | ～することだ | 相談(そうだん)する → 相談(そうだん)することだ |
| Polite | ～ことです | 休(やす)むことです |
| Softened | ～ことですね・～ことでしょうね | 休(やす)むことですね |
| Typical frame | ～たい／～たら + ～ことだ | 治(なお)したいなら、休(やす)むことだ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `だ`/`です` is what makes this advice. Strip it and you get
the notice pattern <code>休(やす)むこと。</code>, which is a rule on a wall
rather than a suggestion to a friend.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)のときは、よく寝(ね)る<b>ことです</b>。
When you've got a cold, the thing to do is sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)ぐなら、タクシーに乗(の)る<b>ことだ</b>。
If you're in a hurry, take a taxi.
</pre>

<pre>
上手(じょうず)になりたいなら、毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ続(つづ)ける<b>ことだ</b>。
If you want to get good, keep at it a little every day.
</pre>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)なら、先(さき)に電話(でんわ)して確(たし)かめる<b>ことです</b>。
If you're worried, ring ahead and check.
</pre>

<pre>
やせたいなら、夜(よる)遅(おそ)く食(た)べない<b>ことだ</b>。
If you want to lose weight, don't eat late at night.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)がなかなか覚(おぼ)えられないなら、手(て)を動(うご)かして何度(なんど)も書(か)いてみる<b>ことです</b>。
If kanji won't stick, write them out by hand over and over.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)で道(みち)に迷(まよ)ったら、あちこち歩(ある)き回(まわ)らないで、その場(ば)で待(ま)つ<b>ことだ</b>。
If you get lost in the mountains, don't wander about - stay where you are.
</pre>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)が不安(ふあん)なら、前(まえ)の日(ひ)に一度(いちど)声(こえ)に出(だ)して練習(れんしゅう)してみる<b>ことです</b>。
If you're nervous about the interview, run through it out loud the day before.
</pre>

<pre>
嫌(いや)なことがあった日(ひ)は、無理(むり)に考(かんが)えないで早(はや)く寝(ね)てしまう<b>ことだ</b>。
On a day when something horrible has happened, don't force yourself to think it through - just go to bed early.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The speaker of a `ことだ` sentence has taken a position: *I know the answer to
this.* That is fine coming from a coach, a teacher, a doctor, a parent, or a
friend who has been through the same thing, and it is the standard voice of advice
columns and self-help writing. It is much less comfortable between strangers, and
it is not appropriate aimed upwards - telling your section chief
<code>休(やす)むことです</code> puts you in the role of the person who knows
better.

`ことです` is polite in form, but politeness of form does not remove the stance.
If you want to advise a senior colleague, leave this pattern alone and use
<code>お休(やす)みになってはいかがでしょうか</code> or
<code>ほうがよろしいかと思(おも)います</code>.

The advice is **general**, not a request about one occasion. It answers "what
should I do about X?" rather than "will you do X for me?" So
<code>窓(まど)を開(あ)けることだ</code> is not a way to ask someone to open a
window; it is what you say when the room's stuffiness is a recurring problem.

You cannot use it about **yourself**. ❌<code>私(わたし)は早(はや)く寝(ね)ることです</code>
is not a plan; for that you want `つもりだ` or `ようにしている`.

<div class="note">

**Note** - a different `ことだ` turns up in exclamations:
<code>なんと静(しず)かなことだろう</code>, <code>困(こま)ったことだ</code>.
That one takes adjectives and な-adjectives, sits at the end of a written or
old-fashioned sentence, and expresses feeling rather than advice. Context separates
them easily - advice always has an action verb in front.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～ことだ vs ～ほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほうがいい` **compares**: this option beats that one.
`ことだ` **prescribes**: this is what solves it, and no comparison is offered.

</div>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだ<b>ほうがいいですよ</b>。
You'd be better off taking something for it.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらないなら、病院(びょういん)へ行(い)く<b>ことです</b>。
If the fever isn't coming down, go to a doctor.
</pre>

`ほうがいい` leaves the decision with the listener and is the gentler everyday
choice - it is what you say to a friend at their desk. `ことだ` closes the
question. That is why it fits the second sentence, where the situation has already
gone past the point of weighing things up.

### ～ことだ vs ～べきだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことだ` is **practical**: here is the route to the goal *you*
said you wanted. `べきだ` is **moral**: here is what is right, whether you want it
or not.

</div>

<pre>
試験(しけん)に受(う)かりたいなら、過去(かこ)問(もん)を解(と)く<b>ことだ</b>。
If you want to pass the exam, work through past papers.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)した以上(いじょう)、最後(さいご)までやる<b>べきだ</b>。
Having promised, you ought to see it through.
</pre>

The first is conditional on your goal: no goal, no advice, and you are free to
drop the exam. The second holds regardless of what you want - that is what makes
it a judgement. In practice `ことだ` follows <code>～たいなら</code> and `べきだ`
follows <code>～のだから</code>.

### ～ことだ vs ～ものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことだ` advises **this person about this problem**. `ものだ`
states a **general truth or social norm** that applies to everyone.

</div>

<pre>
先輩(せんぱい)に会(あ)ったら、自分(じぶん)から挨拶(あいさつ)する<b>ものだ</b>。
When you meet someone senior, you greet them first - that's how it's done.
</pre>

<pre>
職場(しょくば)になじみたいなら、自分(じぶん)から挨拶(あいさつ)する<b>ことだ</b>。
If you want to settle in at work, be the one who says hello first.
</pre>

The same action, two different arguments. `ものだ` appeals to custom and would be
just as true if you did not care about fitting in; `ことだ` is a piece of tactical
advice tied to the goal in the first clause. `ものだ` therefore sounds
old-fashioned and moralising in a way `ことだ` does not - and can be dropped into a
scolding, which `ことだ` cannot.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)は明日(あした)早(はや)く起(お)きる**ことです**。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は明日(あした)早(はや)く起(お)きる<b>つもりです</b>。
  `ことだ` is advice you give someone else. It cannot announce your own plan.

- ❌ すみません、この書類(しょるい)をコピーする**ことです**。 → ✅ すみません、この書類(しょるい)をコピーし<b>ていただけますか</b>。
  A request about one occasion is not advice. `ことだ` prescribes a general
  solution, not a favour.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)な**ことです**。 → ✅ 元気(げんき)を出(だ)す<b>ことです</b>。
  Advice `ことだ` takes a verb. An adjective has to become an action first.

- ❌ 課長(かちょう)、たまには休(やす)む**ことですよ**。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、たまにはお休(やす)みになってはいかがでしょうか。
  Polite endings do not change the stance. Advising upwards needs a different
  pattern altogether.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れる場合(ばあい)は連絡(れんらく)する**ことです**。（as a posted rule） → ✅ 遅(おく)れる場合(ばあい)は連絡(れんらく)する<b>こと</b>。
  A notice drops the `です`. With it, the wall appears to be offering friendly
  guidance.

## Practice

**1.** 日本語(にほんご)が上達(じょうたつ)したいなら、まちがいをおそれずにたくさん話(はな)す____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The first clause names a goal with <code>～たいなら</code>, which is the standard
set-up for `ことだ`. The sentence then hands over the single action that gets you
there, with no weighing of alternatives.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)試験(しけん)なんだから、今夜(こんや)は早(はや)く（寝(ね)たほうがいいよ・寝(ね)ることだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Between friends about tonight in particular, `ほうがいい` is the natural register -

<code>寝(ね)ることだ</code> would sound like a coach issuing instructions, which

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If you want to stop being nervous, the thing to do is practise in front of people."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>～なりたいなら</code> sets up the goal and `ことだ` delivers the remedy. The

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The grammar of the original is fine; the problem is who it is aimed at. `ことだ`

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is advice from one person to another. There is a speaker with an opinion
behind it, and the listener could argue back or ignore it.

(b) is the same words as a rule. Without `だ` it becomes the notice form - 
something printed in a staff handbook, addressed to nobody in particular, with no
speaker to argue with. The single syllable changes the sentence from a
conversation into a regulation.

</details>

# ～ことはない

**Meaning** · there's no need to; it never happens - two readings of one shape, sorted out by who the subject is

With **you** as the subject, `～ことはない` is reassurance: the effort or the worry
is unnecessary, so stop. With **somebody or something else** as the subject, it is
a statement about the world: that does not happen. The words are identical; the
subject decides.

## Formation

The verb is in the **dictionary form** in both senses.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - "no need to" | dictionary form + ことはない | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)することはない |
| Verb - "never happens" | dictionary form + ことはない | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れることはない |
| Verb (negative) - "it's not that ～ can't" | ない-form + ことはない | 飲(の)めない → 飲(の)めないことはない |
| Polite | ～ことはありません | 心配(しんぱい)することはありません |
| Casual, は dropped | ～ことない | 心配(しんぱい)することない |
| Very formal | ～ことはございません | 心配(しんぱい)することはございません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with 決(けっ)して, めったに or ほとんど in front of it, only the "never
happens" reading survives:
決(けっ)して遅(おく)れることはない. With よ, だいじょうぶ or a comforting tone,
only the "no need to" reading survives. Japanese almost never leaves the two
genuinely ambiguous.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)する<b>ことはない</b>よ。
There's no need to worry.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなに急(いそ)ぐ<b>ことはありません</b>。
There's no need to rush like that.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ時間(じかん)があるから、あわてて準備(じゅんび)する<b>ことはない</b>。
There's still time, so there's no need to go rushing at the preparations.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)失敗(しっぱい)したくらいで、あきらめる<b>ことはない</b>よ。
One failure is no reason to give up.
</pre>

<pre>
わざわざ駅(えき)まで迎(むか)えに来(き)ていただく<b>ことはありません</b>。
There's really no need for you to come all the way to the station to meet me.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)が約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)に遅(おく)れる<b>ことはない</b>。
He is never late for an appointment.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)は冬(ふゆ)でも雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もる<b>ことはありません</b>。
Round here the snow never settles, even in winter.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は厳(きび)しい人(ひと)ですが、理由(りゆう)もなく怒(おこ)る<b>ことはありません</b>。
Our manager is strict, but he never gets angry without a reason.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)しても誰(だれ)かに笑(わら)われる<b>ことはない</b>ので、安心(あんしん)して挑戦(ちょうせん)してみてください。
Nobody is going to laugh at you if it goes wrong, so give it a try without worrying.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The "no need to" sense is **consolation**, and it is one of the kindest patterns
in the language. Its natural home is comforting someone who is upset, anxious, or
apologising too much: <code>泣(な)くことないよ</code>,
<code>謝(あやま)ることはありません</code>. In speech the `は` regularly drops - 
<code>気(き)にすることないよ</code> - and that shortened version is what you
will actually hear between friends.

The same words can turn cool. Said flatly about someone else's plan,
<code>わざわざ行(い)くことはない</code> means "there's no point going", and the
warmth disappears. Tone and context carry that difference entirely; the grammar
does not mark it.

The "never happens" sense is a statement of reliability. It is stronger than
`あまり～ない` and softer than an absolute: it claims a pattern, not a law of
physics. It reads well in descriptions, product blurbs and character sketches, and
it takes `ほとんど` comfortably - 
故障(こしょう)することはほとんどありません.

Two register cautions. Turning down help from someone senior with
<code>ことはありません</code> is grammatical but a little blunt; the set humble
phrase is <code>～には及(およ)びません</code>. And `ことはない` **never** means
"must not" - reading <code>遅(おく)れることはない</code> as a warning about
lateness reverses the sentence.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negative row in the table is a separate, very common idiom.
<code>～ないことはない</code> is a double negative meaning "it's not that I
can't": 辛(から)い物(もの)が食(た)べられないことはないんですが、あまり
得意(とくい)ではありません. It is a hedge, and it usually signals a polite
reluctance rather than genuine ability.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～ことはない vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくてもいい` **permits** you to skip it. `ことはない` says the
action would be **pointless or unwarranted**, and normally comes with a reason.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は来(こ)<b>なくてもいいですよ</b>。
You don't have to come in tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるのに来(く)る<b>ことはありませんよ</b>。
There's no call for you to come in with a fever.
</pre>

The first grants permission - the speaker has the authority to excuse you, and is
doing so. The second argues that coming would be unreasonable, and it carries
concern for the listener. That is why `ことはない` fits consolation and
`なくてもいい` fits a manager approving a day off. Note also that `なくてもいい`
answers a question; `ことはない` heads off an intention.

### ～ことはない vs ～ことがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことがない</code> with the **た-form** is experience: you
have never done it. <code>ことはない</code> with the **dictionary form** is about
need or frequency.

</div>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)った<b>ことがありません</b>。
I've never been to Hokkaido.
</pre>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)では、夏(なつ)に冷房(れいぼう)を使(つか)う<b>ことはありません</b>。
In Hokkaido nobody uses air conditioning in summer.
</pre>

The first looks back over your life; the second describes a standing state of
affairs. The `は`/`が` swap is not decorative - `が` presents the experience as a
fact you possess, `は` sets the situation against expectation ("as for that
happening: it doesn't"). If you meet <code>行(い)ったことはない</code> with `は`
and the た-form, it is still experience, now with a contrastive edge: "been there,
no - heard of it, yes."

### ～ことはない vs ～はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことはない` reports that something **does not happen**, from
observation. `はずがない` says it **could not possibly** be so, from reasoning.

</div>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)が約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れる<b>ことはない</b>。
She never forgets an arrangement.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)が約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れる<b>はずがない</b>。
There's no way she's forgotten the arrangement.
</pre>

The first is a general character reference and could be said about her at any
time. The second is about **this** occasion and is an inference - you are ruling
out a possibility that has just been raised. So `はずがない` answers "do you think
she forgot?", while `ことはない` answers "what's she like?"

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れる**ことはない**。（intending "you mustn't be late"） → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れ<b>てはいけない</b>。
  `ことはない` never prohibits. As written, the sentence praises someone's
  punctuality.

- ❌ 泣(な)いた**ことはない**よ、大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)。 → ✅ 泣(な)く<b>ことはない</b>よ、大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)。
  Consolation takes the dictionary form. With the た-form the sentence flips to
  experience - "I have never cried" - which is not what a crying friend needs to
  hear.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しい**ことはない**。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)し<b>くはない</b>。
  The pattern takes verbs. An い-adjective negates on its own stem.

- ❌（to a senior colleague）部長(ぶちょう)、わざわざお越(こ)しいただく**ことはありません**。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、お越(こ)しいただくには<b>及(およ)びません</b>。
  Declining a superior's offer with `ことはありません` sounds like you are telling
  them not to bother. <code>～には及(およ)びません</code> is the set humble
  phrase for exactly this.

## Practice

The second sentence gives the reason - there will be another chance - which marks

<code>落(お)ち込(こ)むことないよ</code>, with the `は` dropped, is if anything
more natural.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The organiser is granting permission to skip an optional event, and permission is

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 一度(いちど)も遅刻(ちこく)するはずがない人(ひと)なので、今日(きょう)も時間(じかん)どおりに来(く)るでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 一度(いちど)も遅刻(ちこく)<b>することはない</b>人(ひと)なので、今日(きょう)も時間(じかん)どおりに来(く)るでしょう。

The clause is describing what the person is *like* - an observed pattern over
time, which is `ことはない`. `はずがない` is an inference about one specific
occasion and cannot sit inside a general character description with
<code>一度(いちど)も</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は人(ひと)の悪口(わるぐち)を言(い)うことはない。
(b) 彼(かれ)は人(ひと)の悪口(わるぐち)を言(い)ったことがない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) describes his character as it stands: speaking ill of people is simply not
something he does. It is about a tendency, and it makes a claim about the future
as well as the past.

(b) reports his record: in your experience, no such occasion has occurred. It is a
statement about history, and it leaves open the possibility that today is
different. (a) would be your answer to "what kind of person is he?"; (b) to "has
he ever said anything about me?"

</details>

# ～しかない

**Meaning** · there's nothing for it but to… - the circumstances have closed every other door

`しか` means "nothing other than", and it always drags a negative along with it.
Put it after a verb and the sentence says: of all the things one might do here,
exactly one remains. Nobody ordered it and nobody judged it - the situation simply
ran out of options.

## Formation

The verb stays in the dictionary form. `しか` here does the same work it does after
a noun, which you already know from <code>千円(せんえん)しかない</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + しかない | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つしかない |
| Verb (する) | ～するしかない | 我慢(がまん)する → 我慢(がまん)するしかない |
| Noun | Noun + しかない | 千円(せんえん) → 千円(せんえん)しかない |
| Past | ～しかなかった | 待(ま)つしかなかった |
| Polite | ～しかありません | 待(ま)つしかありません |
| Hedged guess | ～しかなさそうだ・～しかないだろう | 待(ま)つしかなさそうだ |
| Formal written equivalent | ～ほかない・～よりほかない | 待(ま)つよりほかない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no positive form. ❌<code>待(ま)つしかある</code> does not
exist; `しか` is grammatically bound to `ない`. The negative is doing the work of
"other than", not of "not".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
バスがないから、歩(ある)く<b>しかない</b>。
There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.
</pre>

<pre>
こうなったら、やる<b>しかありません</b>。
Now that it's come to this, we just have to do it.
</pre>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)したので、タクシーで帰(かえ)る<b>しかなかった</b>。
I missed the last train, so there was nothing for it but to take a taxi home.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)がやむまで、ここで待(ま)つ<b>しかないですね</b>。
Looks like we've no choice but to wait here until the rain stops.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)がもう作(つく)られていないので、新(あたら)しいのを買(か)う<b>しかありません</b>。
The part isn't made any more, so there's nothing for it but to buy a new one.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)も手伝(てつだ)ってくれないなら、一人(ひとり)で全部(ぜんぶ)やる<b>しかない</b>。
If nobody's going to help, I'll just have to do the whole thing on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を何度(なんど)読(よ)んでも分(わ)からないので、メーカーに電話(でんわ)して聞(き)く<b>しかなさそうです</b>。
I've read the manual over and over and still don't understand it, so I'll probably just have to ring the maker.
</pre>

<pre>
店(みせ)の売上(うりあげ)がここまで落(お)ちてしまった以上(いじょう)、営業(えいぎょう)時間(じかん)を短(みじか)くする<b>しかないでしょう</b>。
With takings down this far, we're going to have to cut the opening hours.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)に勝(か)つには、残(のこ)り五分(ごふん)で二点(にてん)取(と)る<b>しかない</b>。
To win the match, we have to score twice in the last five minutes - there's no other way.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The feeling is **resignation**, but not always gloom. The same sentence can be a
sigh or a squaring of the shoulders, depending on delivery:
<code>やるしかない</code> is one of the most common things a Japanese speaker says
before starting something difficult, and it is closer to "right, let's get on with
it" than to despair. What is constant is that the alternatives have been
considered and found gone.

That is the pattern's boundary. `しかない` reports what **circumstances** have left
you. It does not report a rule, an order, or a moral duty. A deadline set by your
boss is <code>出(だ)さなければならない</code>; a duty you feel is
<code>行(い)かないわけにはいかない</code>; a situation in which the only
remaining option is to hand it in unfinished is
<code>出(だ)すしかない</code>.

Register is neutral, and the polite form is simply
<code>しかありません</code>. Two siblings sit above it in formality:
<code>～ほかない</code> and <code>～よりほかない</code> are written, slightly
literary equivalents, and <code>～ざるを得(え)ない</code> is formal and adds a
sense of being compelled against your will - an inspection you must accept, a
resignation you must tender. In conversation, `しかない` covers almost everything
those two do.

One small caution. Applied to somebody else's situation, `しかない` can sound
dismissive - <code>あきらめるしかないですよ</code> tells a person their problem is
over. Softening it to <code>しかないかもしれませんね</code> leaves them their
options.

## Contrast

### ～しかない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` comes from **outside**: a rule, an order, a
deadline. `しかない` comes from the **situation**: the other options have
disappeared.

</div>

<pre>
外国人(がいこくじん)登録(とうろく)の書類(しょるい)は本人(ほんにん)が出(だ)さ<b>なければなりません</b>。
The registration paperwork has to be submitted by the person themselves.
</pre>

<pre>
郵便(ゆうびん)が間(ま)に合(あ)わないので、自分(じぶん)で持(も)っていく<b>しかありません</b>。
The post won't get there in time, so I'll have to take it in myself.
</pre>

The first would still be true if the post were quick, the office were near and you
had all week; a regulation does not care. The second exists only because of a
timing problem, and it would evaporate if the problem did. Ask yourself: **who
says so?** If the answer is a person or a rulebook, use `なければならない`.

### ～しかない vs ～ざるを得(え)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "have no choice", but `しかない` is about **options
running out**, while <code>～ざるを得(え)ない</code> is about being **compelled
against your own wishes**, usually by logic, duty or authority. It is also
distinctly formal.

</div>

<pre>
終(お)わらないので、明日(あした)続(つづ)きをやる<b>しかない</b>。
It isn't going to be finished, so I'll just carry on tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
これだけの証拠(しょうこ)がそろえば、事実(じじつ)だと<b>認(みと)めざるを得(え)ない</b>。
With this much evidence, one is forced to accept it as fact.
</pre>

<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> belongs to reports, editorials and formal speech,
and it carries a note of reluctance that `しかない` does not require. Saying it
about tomorrow's leftover work would sound as though the work had defeated you in
some tragic way. Conversely, `しかない` in the evidence sentence would make the
conclusion sound like a practical inconvenience rather than an intellectual
surrender.

### ～しかない vs ～だけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけだ` says **that is all that's left to do**, neutrally, and
often happily. `しかない` says **there is nothing else you could do**, with the
other options mourned.

</div>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)は終(お)わった。あとは本番(ほんばん)を待(ま)つ<b>だけだ</b>。
The preparation's done. All that's left is to wait for the day.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)が間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。このまま本番(ほんばん)に出(で)る<b>しかない</b>。
We didn't get everything ready. We'll just have to go on as we are.
</pre>

`だけだ` looks forward from a job well done; `しかない` looks back at options that
closed. The two are not interchangeable even where both are grammatical - telling
a nervous performer <code>楽(たの)しむだけだよ</code> is encouragement, while
<code>楽(たの)しむしかないよ</code> implies the performance itself is a write-off.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ バスがないから歩(ある)か**ないしかない**。 → ✅ バスがないから歩(ある)く<b>しかない</b>。
  `しかない` attaches to the **dictionary form**. The negative is already inside
  `ない` at the end; putting another one on the verb reverses the sentence.

- ❌ さいふには百円(ひゃくえん)**しかあります**。 → ✅ さいふには百円(ひゃくえん)<b>しかありません</b>。
  `しか` can never end in a positive. It is one half of a pair, and `ない` is the
  other half.

- ❌ パスポートの申請(しんせい)は本人(ほんにん)が行(い)く**しかありません**。（reporting the rule） → ✅ パスポートの申請(しんせい)は本人(ほんにん)が行(い)か<b>なければなりません</b>。
  A published requirement is an obligation from outside, not a situation that ran
  out of options.

- ❌ このお菓子(かし)はここに**しか売(う)っています**。 → ✅ このお菓子(かし)はここで<b>しか売(う)っていません</b>。
  Two things: the sentence needs its negative, and `しか` follows the particle it
  attaches to - <code>ここで</code>, since the shop is the location of selling.

# ～わけにはいかない

**Meaning** · I can't very well do that - it is perfectly possible, and I still can't

This is not a pattern about ability and not a pattern about rules. Everything
`わけにはいかない` describes is physically available to you: you could take the
day off, you could tell the secret, you could go home first. What stops you is a
promise, a position, a debt of gratitude, or your own sense of what is decent. It
is obligation as you *feel* it, rather than obligation as somebody imposes it.

## Formation

`いく` here is written in kana. It is the verb of "things not going", not of going
somewhere, and it never becomes いける.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + わけにはいかない | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)むわけにはいかない |
| Verb (negative) - "I have to" | ない-form + わけにはいかない | 行(い)く → 行(い)かないわけにはいかない |
| Verb (する) | ～するわけにはいかない | 無視(むし)する → 無視(むし)するわけにはいかない |
| Polite | ～わけにはいきません | 休(やす)むわけにはいきません |
| Past | ～わけにはいかなかった | 休(やす)むわけにはいかなかった |
| Casual, は dropped | ～わけにいかない | 休(やす)むわけにいかない |
| Added to a list | ～わけにもいかない | 断(ことわ)るわけにもいかない |
| Humble / very formal | ～わけにはまいりません | 休(やす)むわけにはまいりません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>わけにはいけない</code> is the single most common error
with this pattern. The verb is <code>いく</code> in its negative
<code>いかない</code>; there is no potential form here.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)は休(やす)む<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
I can't very well take today off.
</pre>

<pre>
これは人(ひと)に話(はな)す<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
This isn't something I can tell anyone.
</pre>

<pre>
車(くるま)で来(き)たので、お酒(さけ)を飲(の)む<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
I drove here, so I can hardly have a drink.
</pre>

<pre>
図書館(としょかん)の本(ほん)だから、線(せん)を引(ひ)く<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
It's a library book, so I can't go underlining it.
</pre>

<pre>
後輩(こうはい)が残(のこ)って働(はたら)いているのに、先(さき)に帰(かえ)る<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
My juniors are still here working - I can't just leave ahead of them.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)から言(い)い出(だ)した以上(いじょう)、今(いま)さらやめる<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
I was the one who proposed it, so backing out now is out of the question.
</pre>

<pre>
大事(だいじ)な資料(しりょう)を預(あず)かっているので、この部屋(へや)を空(あ)ける<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
I'm looking after some important documents, so I can't leave this room unattended.
</pre>

<pre>
わざわざ招待(しょうたい)してくれたのだから、出席(しゅっせき)し<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
They went to the trouble of inviting me, so I can't really not go.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)はありますが、今日(きょう)の打(う)ち合(あ)わせにだけは出(で)<b>ないわけにはいきません</b>。
I do have a fever, but today's meeting is the one I can't get out of.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Ask, of any sentence you are tempted to build with this: **could I physically do
it?** If the answer is no, the pattern is wrong. A person who cannot swim does not
say <code>泳(およ)ぐわけにはいかない</code>; they say
<code>泳(およ)げません</code>. The pattern's whole content is the gap between
*could* and *can't bring myself to*.

For the same reason the subject is nearly always **the speaker**. You are
reporting the inside of your own head - the obligation you feel, the awkwardness
you would not survive. Said about somebody else it presumes to know their
conscience, which is why <code>君(きみ)は帰(かえ)るわけにはいかない</code>
sounds less like an observation than like pressure. Describing a third person's
position is possible when you have grounds:
<code>社長(しゃちょう)としては認(みと)めるわけにはいかないだろう</code> - note
the <code>だろう</code> doing the hedging.

The `も` variant is worth having. <code>～わけにもいかない</code> adds this
impossibility to one already mentioned, and it is how the pattern usually appears
when you are boxed in from both sides:
断(ことわ)るわけにもいかず、引(ひ)き受(う)けてしまった.

Register is neutral in itself. The polite <code>わけにはいきません</code> is
completely ordinary at work; <code>わけにはまいりません</code> is humble and belongs
to formal apologies and customer-facing speech. In casual conversation the `は`
often drops - <code>寝(ね)るわけにいかないんだよ</code>.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the double negative <code>～ないわけにはいかない</code> means "I have to",
and the reluctance is part of the meaning. It is the sentence of someone who has
looked for a way out and not found one. Do not use it for things you are glad to
do: ❌<code>好(す)きな人(ひと)に会(あ)わないわけにはいかない</code> is a
strange thing to say about a date.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～わけにはいかない vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てはいけない` is a **prohibition from outside**: a rule, a
parent, a sign. `わけにはいかない` is **you stopping yourself**, for reasons you
would have to explain.

</div>

<pre>
館内(かんない)では飲食(いんしょく)し<b>てはいけません</b>。
Eating and drinking are not allowed inside.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の途中(とちゅう)なので、席(せき)を立(た)つ<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
We're in the middle of a meeting, so I can hardly get up and leave.
</pre>

Nothing forbids leaving a meeting; you simply cannot do it and remain the person
you want to be at that table. Put `てはいけない` there and you invent a rule that
does not exist. Conversely, `わけにはいかない` on a museum notice would be
peculiar - the building has no feelings to protect.

A quick test: if you can name **who** forbids it, use `てはいけない`.

### ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` is **neutral necessity**; it says nothing
about how you feel. <code>～ないわけにはいかない</code> says you would rather not,
and cannot see a way out.

</div>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)には印鑑(いんかん)を押(お)さ<b>なければなりません</b>。
You have to put your seal on the application form.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に直接(ちょくせつ)頼(たの)まれたので、引(ひ)き受(う)け<b>ないわけにはいかなかった</b>。
The manager asked me personally, so I couldn't very well refuse.
</pre>

The first is a form's requirement and carries no emotion at all - it would be
identical on a website's help page. The second is a small story about pressure:
you did not want the job, you looked for an exit, and the way it was asked closed
it. Swap them and the form sentence becomes oddly resentful, while the second
becomes a flat report that misses the whole point.

This is also why <code>ないわけにはいかない</code> almost always has a **reason
clause** in front of it. Without one, the reluctance has nothing to attach to.

### ～わけにはいかない vs ～ことができない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことができない` is genuine **inability**: the skill, the
permission or the physics is missing. `わけにはいかない` is **unwillingness on
principle**, with the ability intact.

</div>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)めないので、この書類(しょるい)に署名(しょめい)する<b>ことができません</b>。
I can't read the kanji, so I'm unable to sign this document.
</pre>

<pre>
中身(なかみ)を確認(かくにん)していないので、この書類(しょるい)に署名(しょめい)する<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
I haven't checked what's in it, so I'm not prepared to sign this document.
</pre>

The first speaker cannot sign. The second could sign in a second, and is refusing
on principle. That distinction is often the difference between an apology and a
stand, and choosing the wrong one misrepresents you badly - the first sentence
sounds helpless where you meant to sound careful.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は帰(かえ)る**わけにはいけません**。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は帰(かえ)る<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
  The verb is <code>いく</code>, negated as <code>いかない／いきません</code>. There
  is no potential form in this pattern.

- ❌ 泳(およ)げないので、向(む)こう岸(ぎし)まで行(い)く**わけにはいかない**。 → ✅ 泳(およ)げないので、向(む)こう岸(ぎし)まで行(い)く<b>ことができない</b>。
  Real inability is `ことができない`. `わけにはいかない` requires that you *could*
  do the thing.

- ❌（on a sign）ここに車(くるま)を止(と)める**わけにはいきません**。 → ✅ ここに車(くるま)を止(と)め<b>てはいけません</b>。
  A notice issues a prohibition. `わけにはいかない` reports a private conflict, and
  car parks do not have those.

- ❌ 君(きみ)は約束(やくそく)を破(やぶ)る**わけにはいかないよ**。 → ✅ 君(きみ)は約束(やくそく)を破(やぶ)っ<b>てはいけないよ</b>。
  Aimed at someone else, the pattern claims to know what their conscience will
  allow. If you mean to tell them not to, say so directly.

- ❌ 大好(だいす)きなケーキだから、食(た)べ**ないわけにはいかない**。 → ✅ 大好(だいす)きなケーキだから、食(た)べ<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
  <code>ないわけにはいかない</code> carries reluctance. For something you cannot
  resist, you want the "can't help it" pattern instead.

## Practice

**1.** 明日(あした)は大事(だいじ)な試験(しけん)だから、今夜(こんや)遊(あそ)びに行(い)く____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今夜(こんや)遊(あそ)びに行(い)く<b>わけにはいかない</b>。

Nothing physically prevents you going out; the exam does. That gap between "could"
and "can't bring myself to" is exactly what the pattern expresses, and the reason
clause in front supplies the pressure.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 足(あし)をけがしているので、今日(きょう)の練習(れんしゅう)に参加(さんか)する（ことができません・わけにはいきません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

参加(さんか)する<b>ことができません</b>。

An injury is real inability, so `ことができない` is the honest form.
<code>わけにはいきません</code> would imply your leg is fine and you are declining
on principle, which would puzzle the coach.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "They lent me the money without asking any questions, so I can't not pay it back."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)も聞(き)かずにお金(かね)を貸(か)してくれたのだから、返(かえ)さ<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。

The double negative is the point: paying it back is not something you chose, it is
something the kindness has made unavoidable. <code>返(かえ)さなければならない</code>
would state the obligation flatly and lose the sense of being morally cornered.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)に誘(さそ)われたので、飲(の)み会(かい)に行(い)かないわけにはいけませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に誘(さそ)われたので、飲(の)み会(かい)に行(い)か<b>ないわけにはいきませんでした</b>。

The only fault is <code>いけません</code> for <code>いきません</code>. The rest is
a model use of the double negative: an invitation from a superior is precisely the
kind of pressure that removes your options without anyone ordering anything.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)は早(はや)く起(お)きなければなりません。
(b) 明日(あした)は早(はや)く起(お)き<b>ないわけにはいきません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of necessity. There is an early train or an early
meeting, and the speaker reports the fact without any attitude to it. You could
say it cheerfully.

(b) adds a person who does not want to. It implies you have already thought about
staying in bed, considered whether anyone would mind, and concluded that you
cannot get away with it. The obligation in (a) comes from the timetable; in (b) it
comes from your own sense of what you owe someone.

</details>

# ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません

**Meaning** · it's fine either way; I don't mind - permission given by disclaiming any objection

`かまう` means "to mind, to be bothered". So `～てもかまわない` does not say the
action is *good*; it says the speaker has **no objection** to it. That small
difference in angle is why the pattern is at home in written rules and business
Japanese, and why it can sound a shade cooler than a warm "go ahead".

## Formation

`かまわない` sits on top of the same `～ても` you already know. Everything before it
takes the ordinary `ても` shapes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + もかまわない | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)ってもかまわない |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + もかまわない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)なくてもかまわない |
| い-adjective | ～くて + もかまわない | 狭(せま)い → 狭(せま)くてもかまわない |
| な-adjective | ～で + もかまわない | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)でもかまわない |
| Noun | Noun + でもかまわない | 明日(あした) → 明日(あした)でもかまわない |
| Polite | ～てもかまいません | 使(つか)ってもかまいません |
| Asking permission | ～てもかまいませんか | 使(つか)ってもかまいませんか |
| Past | ～てもかまわなかった | 使(つか)ってもかまわなかった |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kanji is 構(かま)う, but this pattern is written in kana far more
often than not. Both <code>かまいません</code> and
<code>構(かま)いません</code> are correct; kana is the safer default.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ここに座(すわ)っ<b>てもかまいませんか</b>。
Do you mind if I sit here?
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)では辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)っ<b>てもかまいません</b>。
You may use a dictionary in the exam.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がなければ、全部(ぜんぶ)やらなく<b>てもかまいません</b>。
If you're short of time, you don't have to do all of it.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は狭(せま)く<b>てもかまわない</b>ので、駅(えき)に近(ちか)いところがいいです。
I don't mind a small flat - I'd rather have somewhere near the station.
</pre>

<pre>
お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)でもカードでも<b>かまいません</b>。
You're welcome to pay by cash or by card.
</pre>

<pre>
書(か)き方(かた)が少(すこ)しくらい違(ちが)っ<b>てもかまいません</b>から、まず出(だ)してみてください。
It doesn't matter if the format isn't quite right - just hand something in first.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の集合(しゅうごう)は八時(はちじ)でも九時(くじ)でも<b>かまわない</b>と部長(ぶちょう)は言(い)っていました。
The manager said eight or nine would both be fine for tomorrow's start.
</pre>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っ<b>てもかまいませんか</b>。後(あと)でブログに載(の)せたいのですが。
Would you mind if I took a photo? I'd like to put it on my blog later.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)ぎではありませんので、お返事(へんじ)は来週(らいしゅう)になっ<b>てもかまいません</b>。
There's no rush, so it's perfectly all right if you reply next week.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`てもいい` and `てもかまわない` are close, but they are not saying quite the same
thing. `てもいい` calls the action **good** - a green light. `てもかまわない` says
the speaker **is not bothered** - the light was never red in the first place. Out
of that comes the pattern's characteristic flatness: it is generous without being
warm.

That flatness is an advantage in **writing and business**, which is where you meet
it most: exam instructions, application forms, hotel policies, emails from
suppliers. <code>キャンセルは前日(ぜんじつ)までにご連絡(れんらく)いただければかまいません</code>
sounds like an institution, and is meant to.

It is also, spoken with the wrong intonation, the sentence of someone who is
politely annoyed. <code>別(べつ)にかまいませんけど</code> ("I don't *mind*, but…")
is a recognisable way of not being fine at all. Learners often reach for
<code>かまいません</code> when they mean to be reassuring and land somewhere colder
than intended; if warmth is the goal,
<code>もちろんいいですよ</code> or <code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですよ</code> does
the job better.

As a **question**, <code>～てもかまいませんか</code> is politer and more formal
than <code>～てもいいですか</code>, and slightly less formal than
<code>～てもよろしいでしょうか</code>. All three are usable at work; the middle one
is the safe everyday choice with someone you do not know well.

One direction to avoid: **granting** permission to someone senior. Telling your
manager <code>座(すわ)ってもかまいませんよ</code> puts you in charge of the
chairs. Answering their question with a bare <code>かまいません</code> is normal
and fine - it is the unprompted granting that presumes.

## Contrast

### ～てもかまわない vs ～てもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てもいい` says the action is **all right**. `てもかまわない`
says the speaker **doesn't mind**. The first is permission; the second is the
absence of objection.

</div>

<pre>
そのペン、使(つか)っ<b>てもいいよ</b>。
Sure, use that pen.
</pre>

<pre>
どのペンを使(つか)っ<b>てもかまいません</b>。
It doesn't matter which pen you use.
</pre>

The first is a friend handing something over. The second is an invigilator stating
that pen choice is not regulated. Notice how naturally `かまわない` takes
どの, どちら, いつ and でも - it is the form of choice for saying "any of these is
acceptable", because indifference is exactly what it expresses.

In casual conversation between friends, `てもいい` and simply
<code>いいよ</code> are far commoner. <code>かまわないよ</code> is not wrong there,
but it sounds a little grown-up and measured.

### ～てもかまいませんか vs ～てもよろしいでしょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same request, three rungs of politeness:
<code>～てもいいですか</code> ＜ <code>～てもかまいませんか</code> ＜
<code>～てもよろしいでしょうか</code>.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>てもいいですか</b>。
Can I open the window?
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>てもかまいませんか</b>。
Would you mind if I opened the window a little?
</pre>

<pre>
恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>てもよろしいでしょうか</b>。
Excuse me - would it be all right if I opened the window?
</pre>

Treat these as a ladder rather than as synonyms. The first is what you say to a
friend or a shop assistant, the second to a colleague or a stranger, the third to
a customer or in a formal setting. Going too far up the ladder is a mild error
too: <code>よろしいでしょうか</code> to a close friend sounds like a joke.

### ～てもかまわない vs ～ても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different worlds.
<code>ても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)</code> is the spoken, everyday version;
`てもかまわない` is the written and formal one.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)、少(すこ)し遅(おく)れ<b>ても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですか</b>。
Is it OK if I'm a bit late tomorrow?
</pre>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)が明日(あした)になっ<b>てもかまいません</b>。
It is acceptable for submission to be tomorrow.
</pre>

<code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)</code> has spread enormously in modern spoken
Japanese and now covers most of what `かまわない` used to do in conversation. The
reverse swap is what sounds off: an official notice reading
<code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です</code> feels chatty, and in a contract it would
be unusable.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)行(い)く**でもかまいません**。 → ✅ 明日(あした)行(い)っ<b>てもかまいません</b>。
  Verbs come in on the て-form. `でもかまわない` is for nouns and な-adjectives:
  <code>明日(あした)でもかまいません</code>.

- ❌ 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い**でもかまいません**。 → ✅ 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)く<b>てもかまいません</b>。
  An い-adjective becomes <code>～くても</code> first. The `でも` route is not open
  to it.

- ❌ 全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)まなく**でもかまいません**。 → ✅ 全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)まなく<b>てもかまいません</b>。
  The negative is <code>～なくても</code>. `なくで` does not exist.

- ❌（to your manager, unprompted）部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの席(せき)に座(すわ)っ**てもかまいませんよ**。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの席(せき)に<b>どうぞ</b>。
  Handing out permission upwards presumes authority you do not have. Offer, don't
  permit.

## Practice

**1.** コピーの紙(かみ)は裏(うら)が使(つか)ってあるものでも____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

裏(うら)が使(つか)ってあるものでも<b>かまいません</b>。

The thing being permitted is a **noun** - paper of a certain kind - so it takes
<code>でもかまいません</code> rather than the て-form. The sentence says the
speaker has no objection either way, which is precisely this pattern rather than
<code>いいです</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （友達(ともだち)に）このおかし、一(ひと)つ食(た)べ（てもいい?・てもかまいませんか?）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一(ひと)つ食(た)べ<b>てもいい?</b>

To a friend, <code>てもいい?</code> is the natural register.
<code>てもかまいませんか</code> is a step up in formality and would sound as though
you had suddenly started addressing them as a client - which is occasionally a
joke, but not the default.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You don't have to write your name if you'd rather not."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

書(か)きたくなければ、お名前(なまえ)は書(か)か<b>なくてもかまいません</b>。

The negative permission is <code>～なくてもかまいません</code>. On a survey form
this is exactly the register you want - it disclaims any requirement without
sounding as if the form is doing you a favour.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かくてもかまいませんから、安(やす)いほうがいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部屋(へや)が<b>静(しず)かでもかまいません</b>から、安(やす)いほうがいいです。

<code>静(しず)か</code> is a な-adjective, so it takes `でも`, not
<code>～くても</code>; ❌<code>静(しず)かくて</code> is not a form. (Note the
sentence is odd in meaning too - nobody objects to quiet - so
<code>狭(せま)くてもかまいません</code> would make a better sentence.)

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)でもいいですよ。
(b) 明日(あした)でもかまいませんよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) offers tomorrow as a positively acceptable option. It is warm and helpful, the
sort of thing you say to put someone at ease about a deadline.

(b) says you would not be bothered by tomorrow. The content is the same, but the
tone is a step cooler and more businesslike - it disclaims an objection rather
than extending an offer. In an email to a client that neutrality is an advantage;
to a friend who is apologising for being late, (a) is the kinder sentence.

</details>

# ～てごらん

**Meaning** · go on, try it; have a look - a warm, gentle command, always aimed downward

`ごらん` comes from the honorific verb for "look", but do not let that mislead you:
in `～てごらん` the respect has been worn away completely. What is left is an
**encouraging imperative** that an adult uses to a child, a teacher to a student, a
senior to a junior. It is kind. It is also unmistakably from above.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ごらん | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べてごらん |
| Verb (する) | ～してごらん | 想像(そうぞう)する → 想像(そうぞう)してごらん |
| Fuller, more instructional | ～てごらんなさい | 食(た)べてごらんなさい |
| Softened with a particle | ～てごらんよ・～てごらんね | 食(た)べてごらんよ |
| As a gentle invitation | ～てごらん? | 読(よ)んでごらん? |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - there is **no negative**. ❌<code>見(み)ないでごらん</code>
does not exist. If you want someone not to do something, you are outside this
pattern altogether.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kanji is ご覧(らん), and you will see
<code>見(み)てご覧(らん)</code> in print. In the `～てごらん` pattern kana is
much commoner, and it helps keep this use visually separate from the honorific
<code>ご覧(らん)ください</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これ、食(た)べ<b>てごらん</b>。おいしいよ。
Here, try this. It's good.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)<b>てごらん</b>。
Have a look outside the window.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で考(かんが)え<b>てごらん</b>。きっと分(わ)かるから。
Have a think about it yourself. You'll get there.
</pre>

<pre>
こわがらないで、一度(いちど)乗(の)っ<b>てごらんなさい</b>。
Don't be frightened - get on and try it once.
</pre>

<pre>
お母(かあ)さんに聞(き)い<b>てごらん</b>。知(し)っているかもしれないよ。
Go and ask your mum. She might know.
</pre>

<pre>
その漢字(かんじ)、もう一度(いちど)書(か)い<b>てごらん</b>。今度(こんど)はゆっくりね。
Write that kanji once more. Slowly this time.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)してもいいから、まず自分(じぶん)でやっ<b>てごらん</b>。
It doesn't matter if you get it wrong - have a go on your own first.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かにして、目(め)を閉(と)じて鳥(とり)の声(こえ)を聞(き)い<b>てごらん</b>。何(なん)種類(しゅるい)いるか分(わ)かるかな。
Be quiet, close your eyes and listen to the birds. See if you can tell how many kinds there are.
</pre>

<pre>
難(むずか)しそうに見(み)えますが、説明書(せつめいしょ)のとおりにやっ<b>てごらんなさい</b>。十分(じゅっぷん)でできますよ。
It looks difficult, but try following the instructions - it takes ten minutes.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The one thing to fix in your head is **direction**. `～てごらん` flows downhill:
parent to child, teacher to pupil, coach to player, an older friend to a much
younger one. Between adults of equal standing it sounds patronising, and pointed
at anyone senior it is close to rude - no amount of politeness elsewhere in the
sentence rescues it. This surprises learners, because the word inside it is an
honorific one; the honorific is directed at the *looking*, historically, not at
the listener.

Within its range, though, it is genuinely affectionate. Compared with the plain
imperative <code>食(た)べろ</code> or even with
<code>食(た)べなさい</code>, `～てごらん` invites rather than orders. It very
often comes with a reason or a reassurance attached - 
<code>おいしいよ</code>, <code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だから</code> - and that is
part of its character: it is the grammar of coaxing.

<code>～てごらんなさい</code> is the fuller form. It is a little more formal and a
little more instructional, which is why it turns up in children's books, teaching
materials and the speech of teachers; <code>～てごらん</code> is the clipped,
homelier version you hear at a kitchen table. Neither is polite in the ます sense - 
adding です or ます to them is not possible.

You will also meet it addressed to nobody in particular, in lyrics, adverts and
narration: <code>空(そら)を見(み)てごらん</code>. There it reads as gentle,
slightly poetic, and the downward angle becomes warmth rather than authority.

## Contrast

### ～てごらん vs ～てみて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てみて` is neutral and works in **any** direction.
`てごらん` adds warmth and a difference in standing, and only works **downward**.

</div>

<pre>
これ、おいしいので食(た)べ<b>てみてください</b>。
Do try this, it's delicious.
</pre>

<pre>
これ、おいしいから食(た)べ<b>てごらん</b>。
Go on, try this - it's delicious.
</pre>

To a guest, a colleague or a customer, the first is the only safe option. The
second is what you would say to your nephew. Note that they are not doing the same
grammatical work either: <code>てみる</code> means "do it and find out", so
<code>食(た)べてみて</code> is literally "eat it and see", while
<code>食(た)べてごらん</code> is closer to "let's see you eat it".

### ～てごらん vs ～てみなさい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are downward. `てみなさい` is an **instruction** and can
be stern; `てごらん` is an **invitation** and is warm.

</div>

<pre>
もう一度(いちど)、最初(さいしょ)から読(よ)ん<b>でみなさい</b>。
Read it again from the beginning.
</pre>

<pre>
もう一度(いちど)、最初(さいしょ)から読(よ)ん<b>でごらん</b>。
Have another go at it from the start.
</pre>

A teacher who has said the same thing three times uses the first; a teacher
sitting beside a struggling child uses the second. `なさい` carries the authority
of an order that expects compliance, which is why parents reach for it when
patience is running out. `ごらん` expects willingness rather than obedience.

### ～てごらん vs ご覧(らん)ください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same word, opposite directions.
<code>ご覧(らん)ください</code> and <code>ご覧(らん)になる</code> are
**honorific**: they raise the listener. `～てごらん` does not.

</div>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)の三(さん)ページ目(め)を<b>ご覧(らん)ください</b>。
Please look at page three of the handout.
</pre>

<pre>
ここ、見(み)<b>てごらん</b>。名前(なまえ)が書(か)いてあるよ。
Look here - your name's written on it.
</pre>

The first is what you say to an audience, a client or your manager; the second is
what you say to a seven-year-old. Because they look so similar, learners produce
the fatal hybrid ❌<code>見(み)てごらんください</code>, which staples an
honorific ending onto a downward command. It is not a politer `てごらん`; it is a
sentence that cannot be repaired without changing pattern.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)、この写真(しゃしん)を見(み)**てごらんください**。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、この写真(しゃしん)を<b>ご覧(らん)ください</b>。
  `てごらん` cannot be made polite by adding ください. To ask a superior to look,
  use the honorific verb on its own.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの席(せき)に座(すわ)っ**てごらん**。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの席(せき)に<b>お座(すわ)りください</b>。
  The pattern is downward only. Aimed at a superior it reads as though you were
  addressing a child.

- ❌ その箱(はこ)は開(あ)けない**でごらん**。 → ✅ その箱(はこ)は開(あ)け<b>ないでね</b>。
  There is no negative form. Use an ordinary negative request instead.

- ❌ もう一度(いちど)言(い)う**ごらん**。 → ✅ もう一度(いちど)言(い)っ<b>てごらん</b>。
  `ごらん` attaches to the て-form, like `てみる` and `てください`.

## Practice

**1.** 自転車(じてんしゃ)、こわくないから一人(ひとり)で乗(の)っ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一人(ひとり)で乗(の)っ<b>てごらん</b>。

The reassurance <code>こわくないから</code> in front of it is the giveaway: this is
an adult coaxing a child onto a bike, which is the pattern's home ground. The
fuller <code>乗(の)ってごらんなさい</code> would sound more like an instructor
than a parent.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （お客様(きゃくさま)に）こちらの新製品(しんせいひん)を試(ため)し（てごらん・てみてください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

試(ため)し<b>てみてください</b>。

A customer must never be spoken to downward, and `てごらん` is downward by
definition. <code>てみてください</code> is neutral and polite; in a shop you would
often go further still with
<code>お試(ため)しください</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, said by a parent to a small child: "Look - the moon's come out."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

見(み)<b>てごらん</b>。お月(つき)さまが出(で)てきたよ。

`てごらん` fits the speaker and the listener exactly, and the final `よ` matches
its warm, coaxing tone. <code>見(み)なさい</code> would turn the same sentence
into an instruction.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 社長(しゃちょう)、こちらのグラフを見(み)てごらんください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 社長(しゃちょう)、こちらのグラフを<b>ご覧(らん)ください</b>。

<code>てごらんください</code> combines a downward command with an honorific
ending, and the two cannot coexist. The honorific verb
<code>ご覧(らん)になる</code> supplies the polite request on its own.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう少(すこ)し待(ま)ってみて。
(b) もう少(すこ)し待(ま)ってごらん。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is neutral. You are asking someone - anyone, of any standing - to wait and see
what happens. Between adults it is the ordinary way to say it.

(b) adds a difference in position and a note of encouragement. The speaker knows
something the listener does not, and is reassuring them that waiting will be
worthwhile. Said to a child or a junior colleague it is warm; said to your boss it
would sound as though you were patting them on the head.

</details>

# ～ずに

**Meaning** · without doing - the written, slightly formal twin of `～ないで`

`ずに` and `ないで` mean the same thing. What separates them is texture: `ずに` is
tighter, older and more literary, and it belongs to writing, to careful speech and
to a large family of set expressions. Learn the two irregular stems - 
<code>せずに</code> and <code>来(こ)ずに</code> - and the rest is
straightforward.

## Formation

Take the ない-form, drop the ない, add `ずに`. Two verbs break the rule.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ずに | 言(い)わない → 言(い)わずに |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ずに | 食(た)べない → 食(た)べずに |
| Verb (する) | **せずに** | 連絡(れんらく)する → 連絡(れんらく)せずに |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ずに</b> | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ずに |
| Joining clauses (literary) | ～ず、 | 言(い)わず、去(さ)っていった |
| Common set forms | ～ずにすむ・～ずにおく・～ずにいる | 行(い)かずにすんだ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>しずに</code> is the commonest error on this page.
`する` does not use its ordinary stem here; the form is <code>せずに</code>, from
an older negative. Likewise <code>来(く)る</code> gives
<code>来(こ)ずに</code>, never ❌<code>きずに</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ある</code> and adjectives have no `ずに` form. "Because there
wasn't any" is <code>なくて</code>, and that is a different pattern with a
different job.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>ずに</b>家(いえ)を出(で)た。
I left the house without eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)も言(い)わ<b>ずに</b>帰(かえ)ってしまった。
He went home without saying a word.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)た<b>ずに</b>出(で)かけて、ずぶぬれになりました。
I went out without an umbrella and got soaked.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)にも相談(そうだん)せ<b>ずに</b>、一人(ひとり)で決(き)めてしまった。
I decided it on my own, without consulting anybody.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は靴(くつ)もぬが<b>ずに</b>部屋(へや)に上(あ)がってきた。
My son came into the room without even taking his shoes off.
</pre>

<pre>
辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)わ<b>ずに</b>この文章(ぶんしょう)が読(よ)めたら、なかなかの実力(じつりょく)です。
If you can read this passage without a dictionary, you're doing rather well.
</pre>

<pre>
昨夜(さくや)は一睡(いっすい)もせ<b>ずに</b>原稿(げんこう)を書(か)いていました。
I was up writing the manuscript last night without a wink of sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
事前(じぜん)に連絡(れんらく)せ<b>ずに</b>訪(たず)ねるのは、日本(にほん)では失礼(しつれい)にあたります。
Calling on someone without letting them know in advance counts as rude in Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
あきらめ<b>ずに</b>続(つづ)けていれば、いつか結果(けっか)はついてくるものです。
If you keep going without giving up, results do come eventually.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ずに` is a survival from classical Japanese, and it still carries a faint
tightness that `ないで` does not. In writing - essays, news, instructions, business
email - it is the default. In conversation, `ないで` is the default, and `ずに`
sounds a shade formal or deliberate: <code>寝(ね)ずに待(ま)っていた</code> is
perfectly sayable, just slightly more composed than
<code>寝(ね)ないで待(ま)っていた</code>.

Certain combinations, however, are `ずに` only or nearly so, and they are worth
learning whole:

- <code>～ずにすむ</code> - "get away without having to":
  払(はら)わずにすんだ.
- <code>～ずにはいられない</code> - "can't help doing".
- <code>～ずじまい</code> - "ended up never doing it":
  会(あ)えずじまいだった.

The bare `ず` without `に` is a further step into literary Japanese. It links
clauses rather than modifying a verb - 
<code>振(ふ)り返(かえ)らず、歩(ある)き続(つづ)けた</code> - and it belongs
to novels, speeches and song lyrics. Recognise it; write `ずに`.

The one thing `ずに` cannot do is make a **request**. `ないで` has a second life as
a negative command - <code>触(さわ)らないでください</code> - and `ずに` has no
share in it. ❌<code>触(さわ)らずにください</code> is not a formal version of
anything.

## Contrast

### ～ずに vs ～ないで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register: `ずに` written and formal,
`ないで` spoken and neutral. But only `ないで` can form a **request**.

</div>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)ま<b>ないで</b>寝(ね)てしまった。
I fell asleep without taking my medicine.
</pre>

<pre>
所定(しょてい)の欄(らん)に記入(きにゅう)せ<b>ずに</b>提出(ていしゅつ)しないでください。
Please do not submit the form without filling in the designated fields.
</pre>

The second sentence shows both at once, and the division of labour is exact: `ずに`
does the "without", `ないで` does the "please don't". Swapping them produces
❌<code>記入(きにゅう)しないで提出(ていしゅつ)せずにください</code>, which is
not repairable.

Note also that the choice is partly one of **rhythm**: a formal written sentence
that has already used `ないで` for its request will reach for `ずに` for the
"without", simply to avoid saying ない twice.

### ～ずに vs ～なくて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ずに` says **without doing it**, describing how the main action
happened. `なくて` says **because it didn't happen**, giving a cause.

</div>

<pre>
地図(ちず)を見(み)<b>ずに</b>駅(えき)まで行(い)けました。
I made it to the station without looking at a map.
</pre>

<pre>
地図(ちず)が読(よ)め<b>なくて</b>、駅(えき)に着(つ)けませんでした。
I couldn't read the map, so I never got to the station.
</pre>

The first describes your method; the second explains a failure. A quick test: if
you could insert "because" into the English, you want `なくて`. `なくて` also takes
adjectives and `ある` (<code>お金(かね)がなくて</code>), which `ずに` cannot
touch at all.

### ～ずに vs ～ないまま

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ずに` modifies **the action**: you did B without doing A.
`ないまま` describes **a state left unchanged**: A never got done, and things
stayed that way.

</div>

<pre>
電気(でんき)を消(け)さ<b>ずに</b>出(で)かけてしまった。
I went out without turning the light off.
</pre>

<pre>
電気(でんき)が消(き)え<b>ないまま</b>、三日(みっか)留守(るす)にしていた。
The light stayed on the whole three days I was away.
</pre>

`ずに` is tied to a single moment - the moment of leaving. `ないまま` stretches over
a period and emphasises that nothing changed during it. Where both are possible,
`ないまま` adds a sense of something being left unresolved:
<code>理由(りゆう)を聞(き)かないまま別(わか)れた</code> lingers in a way
<code>聞(き)かずに別(わか)れた</code> does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 予約(よやく)**しずに**行(い)ったら、満席(まんせき)でした。 → ✅ 予約(よやく)<b>せずに</b>行(い)ったら、満席(まんせき)でした。
  `する` becomes <code>せずに</code>. This is irregular and there is no way to
  derive it from the ordinary stem - memorise it.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は誰(だれ)も<b>来(き)ずに</b>、一人(ひとり)で片付(かたづ)けた。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は誰(だれ)も<b>来(こ)ずに</b>、一人(ひとり)で片付(かたづ)けた。
  <code>来(く)る</code> uses こ- here, the same stem as
  <code>来(こ)ない</code>. き- belongs to <code>来(き)ます</code>.

- ❌ 廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)ら**ずにください**。 → ✅ 廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)ら<b>ないでください</b>。
  `ずに` never forms a request. There is no formal-sounding upgrade of
  <code>ないでください</code> along these lines.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が**なずに**、全部(ぜんぶ)見(み)られなかった。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)が<b>なくて</b>、全部(ぜんぶ)見(み)られなかった。
  `ある` has no `ずに` form, and in any case this sentence is giving a reason,
  which is `なくて` territory.

## Practice

**1.** 急(いそ)いでいたので、朝(あさ)ごはんも食(た)べ____会社(かいしゃ)へ向(む)かった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

朝(あさ)ごはんも食(た)べ<b>ずに</b>会社(かいしゃ)へ向(む)かった。

The sentence describes *how* you set off - without eating - so it wants `ずに`, not
`なくて`. <code>食(た)べないで</code> would be equally correct and simply sound
more conversational.

</details>

**2.** Choose: レポートは下書(したが)きを（せずに・しなくて）いきなり書(か)き始(はじ)めました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

下書(したが)きを<b>せずに</b>いきなり書(か)き始(はじ)めました。

This is the method you used, not the reason for something, so `ずに` is right - and
note the irregular <code>せずに</code> for `する`. <code>しなくて</code> would try
to make the missing draft the *cause* of starting, which is not what the sentence
means.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He left without telling anyone where he was going."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)はどこへ行(い)くのか誰(だれ)にも言(い)わ<b>ずに</b>出(で)ていった。

The "without" attaches to the leaving, so `ずに` (or the more colloquial
<code>言(い)わないで</code>) is the form. In a novel you might see the bare
literary <code>言(い)わず、出(で)ていった</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 危(あぶ)ないですから、工事中(こうじちゅう)の場所(ばしょ)には近(ちか)づかずにください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 危(あぶ)ないですから、工事中(こうじちゅう)の場所(ばしょ)には<b>近(ちか)づかないでください</b>。

`ずに` cannot carry a request. However formal the notice, a negative instruction
takes <code>ないでください</code> - or, in stiffer written style,
<code>近(ちか)づかないこと</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 返事(へんじ)を待(ま)たずに出発(しゅっぱつ)した。
(b) 返事(へんじ)が来(こ)なくて出発(しゅっぱつ)できなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) describes the manner of departing: you chose to go, and the reply had not come
yet. The action succeeded, and the missing reply is background.

(b) gives a cause: no reply arrived, and *therefore* you could not set off. The
action failed, and the missing reply is the reason. `ずに` never carries that
causal weight - it only tells you what accompanied the action, or rather what
didn't.

</details>

# ～ずにはいられない

**Meaning** · can't help doing it; can't stop myself - an urge that overrides you

This is the only "no choice" pattern in which the pressure comes from **inside**.
Nobody has forbidden anything, no rule applies, and the circumstances have not
closed off your options - you simply cannot keep yourself from doing it. Laughing,
crying, saying the thing you should not say: `ずにはいられない` is the grammar of
being overruled by your own feelings.

## Formation

Built on the `ずに` you already know, plus the potential of `いる`: literally
"cannot be, without doing it".

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ずにはいられない | 笑(わら)う → 笑(わら)わずにはいられない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ずにはいられない | 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)えずにはいられない |
| Verb (する) | **せずにはいられない** | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)せずにはいられない |
| Colloquial variant | ～ないではいられない | 笑(わら)わないではいられない |
| Shorter variant | ～ずにいられない | 笑(わら)わずにいられない |
| Polite | ～ずにはいられません | 笑(わら)わずにはいられません |
| Past | ～ずにはいられなかった | 笑(わら)わずにはいられなかった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `する` gives <code>せずには</code>, never
❌<code>しずには</code>, exactly as it does in plain `ずに`. Since half the verbs
that fit this pattern are する-verbs - 
心配(しんぱい)する, 同情(どうじょう)する, 応援(おうえん)する - this is the
form to drill.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この映画(えいが)を見(み)ると、泣(な)か<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
I can't watch this film without crying.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)のことを心配(しんぱい)せ<b>ずにはいられません</b>。
I can't help worrying about my son.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)の前(まえ)を通(とお)ると、パンを買(か)わ<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
I can't walk past that shop without buying bread.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)の話(はな)し方(かた)がおかしくて、笑(わら)わ<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>。
The way she told it was so funny I couldn't keep a straight face.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなひどい記事(きじ)を読(よ)むと、一言(ひとこと)言(い)わ<b>ずにはいられません</b>。
When I read an article this bad, I can't let it pass without saying something.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが一人(ひとり)で泣(な)いているのを見(み)て、声(こえ)をかけ<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>。
Seeing a child crying on his own, I couldn't not go over and speak to him.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)の最後(さいご)の五分間(ごふんかん)は、立(た)ち上(あ)がって応援(おうえん)せ<b>ずにはいられませんでした</b>。
For the last five minutes of the match I simply had to get up and cheer.
</pre>

<pre>
災害(さいがい)のニュースを見(み)ていると、自分(じぶん)に何(なに)ができるのかを考(かんが)え<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
Watching the news about the disaster, I can't stop asking myself what I could do.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)の手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)み返(かえ)すたびに、あの夏(なつ)の台所(だいどころ)を思(おも)い出(だ)さ<b>ずにはいられません</b>。
Every time I reread my grandmother's letters, I find myself back in that kitchen, that summer.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The compulsion is **emotional**, and the verbs that appear here reflect that: to
laugh, to cry, to worry, to remember, to sympathise, to say something, to cheer.
Physical needs and practical necessities do not belong - 
<code>傘(かさ)を買(か)わずにはいられなかった</code> is odd if it merely
started raining, and natural if you saw an umbrella so beautiful you had no
defence against it.

Because it reports an inner state, the subject is normally **you**, or a narrator
speaking of themselves. About a third person you need a hedge, exactly as with
`たい`: <code>彼(かれ)も笑(わら)わずにはいられなかったようだ</code>. Written
flat about someone else, it claims access to a mind you cannot see into.

The register leans **written and expressive**: essays, reviews, letters, blog
posts, novels. Spoken, it is entirely usable but a touch dramatic - in casual
conversation people say
<code>つい笑(わら)っちゃった</code> instead. That is worth knowing, because a
learner who uses `ずにはいられない` about a mildly amusing thing sounds as though
they are narrating their own memoir.

<div class="note">

**Note** - two neighbours, distinguished mainly by register.
<code>～ないではいられない</code> means the same and is a little more
conversational. <code>～ずにいられない</code>, without the `は`, is a shortened
form that turns up in speech and in lyrics. The `は` adds a touch of emphasis;
none of the three differs in meaning.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～ずにはいられない vs ～てしまう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てしまう` reports that the action **happened despite you**.
`ずにはいられない` reports the **pressure to do it**, and usually says something
about you as a person.

</div>

<pre>
おかしくて笑(わら)っ<b>てしまいました</b>。
It was so funny I ended up laughing.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の顔(かお)を見(み)るたびに笑(わら)わ<b>ずにはいられません</b>。
I can't look at his face without laughing.
</pre>

`てしまう` is about one occasion and often carries regret. `ずにはいられない` states
a standing susceptibility - every time, without fail - and carries no regret at
all, only recognition. That is why the second sentence takes
<code>たびに</code> comfortably and the first does not.

### ～ずにはいられない vs ～ないわけにはいかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both come out as "I have to" in English, and they are
opposites underneath. `ずにはいられない` is a **want** you cannot suppress;
`ないわけにはいかない` is a **duty** you cannot escape.

</div>

<pre>
友達(ともだち)の作品(さくひん)なので、見(み)に行(い)か<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
It's my friend's work, so I can hardly not go and see it.
</pre>

<pre>
評判(ひょうばん)がよすぎて、見(み)に行(い)か<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
Everyone's raving about it - I can't stay away.
</pre>

In the first you would rather stay home; friendship has removed the exit. In the
second nothing would keep you away. Choosing wrongly says something unfortunate
about your feelings: telling a friend
<code>見(み)に行(い)かないわけにはいかなくて</code> admits you went out of
obligation.

### ～ずにはいられない vs ～しかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しかない` is about **the world** running out of options.
`ずにはいられない` is about **you** running out of self-control.

</div>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)がないので、歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)る<b>しかない</b>。
There's no last train, so I'll have to walk home.
</pre>

<pre>
あんまり夜(よる)がきれいだったので、遠回(とおまわ)りせ<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>。
The night was so lovely I couldn't help taking the long way round.
</pre>

Both sentences end with the speaker walking, and they explain the walking in
opposite ways. If the sentence would still be true of someone with a completely
different personality, you want `しかない`; if it depends on how *this* person
feels, you want `ずにはいられない`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 結果(けっか)が気(き)になって、確認(かくにん)**しずにはいられない**。 → ✅ 結果(けっか)が気(き)になって、確認(かくにん)<b>せずにはいられない</b>。
  `する` takes the irregular <code>せずに</code>. There is no しずに anywhere in
  Japanese.

- ❌ 明日(あした)までにレポートを出(だ)さ**ずにはいられない**。 → ✅ 明日(あした)までにレポートを出(だ)さ<b>なければならない</b>。
  A deadline is an obligation, not an urge. This pattern reports a feeling you
  cannot suppress, and nobody is emotionally overwhelmed by a report.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れそうだったので、タクシーに乗(の)ら**ずにはいられなかった**。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れそうだったので、タクシーに乗(の)る<b>しかなかった</b>。
  Circumstances closing off the alternatives is `しかない`. `ずにはいられない`
  would suggest you have an emotional weakness for taxis.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さんは感動(かんどう)**せずにはいられません**。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さんも感動(かんどう)<b>せずにはいられなかったようです</b>。
  About someone else, the claim needs hedging - <code>ようだ</code>,
  <code>だろう</code>, <code>らしい</code> - because you cannot see inside their
  head.

## Practice

**1.** あんなに一生懸命(いっしょうけんめい)練習(れんしゅう)していたのを知(し)っているので、応援(おうえん)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

応援(おうえん)<b>せずにはいられません</b>。

`する` gives the irregular <code>せずに</code>. The sentence describes an emotional
pull rather than a duty, which is what makes this pattern right - nobody has asked
the speaker to cheer.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったので、会社(かいしゃ)に泊(と)まる（しかなかった・ずにはいられなかった）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会社(かいしゃ)に泊(と)まる<b>しかなかった</b>。

A typhoon closing the railway is the world removing your options, which is
`しかない`. <code>泊(と)まらずにはいられなかった</code> would claim you were
seized by a longing to sleep at the office.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Whenever I hear that song, I can't help remembering my school days."

その曲(きょく)を聞(き)くと、学生(がくせい)時代(じだい)を思(おも)い出(だ)さ<b>ずにはいられません</b>。

Memory arriving uninvited is a textbook use of the pattern.

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)に頼(たの)まれたので、残業(ざんぎょう)せずにはいられませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に頼(たの)まれたので、<b>残業(ざんぎょう)しないわけにはいきませんでした</b>。

Being asked by your manager creates a duty, and duty you cannot escape is
<code>ないわけにはいかない</code>. As written, the sentence says you were unable to
restrain your appetite for overtime.

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) その手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)んで、泣(な)いてしまった。
(b) その手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)むと、泣(な)かずにはいられない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports one occasion. You read the letter, and the tears arrived without your
consent; `てしまう` adds a faint sense of "I didn't mean to", which can shade into
embarrassment.

(b) reports a rule about yourself. Note the non-past
<code>読(よ)むと</code>: this happens every time, and the speaker is stating a
known feature of their own reaction. (a) is an event, (b) is a disposition.

</details>
