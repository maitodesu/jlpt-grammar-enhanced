# N3 - Only, even, and how much


---

# ～だけ - as much as

**Meaning** · as much as ～, as many as ～ - up to the limit of what is possible, wanted, or available

This is the same `だけ` you already know as "only", pointed the other way. Instead
of shrinking the amount ("only three"), it names a **ceiling and then fills it**:
as much as you like, as fast as I can, everything there was. The word is
identical; what changes it is the kind of word in front of it.

## Formation

The extent reading appears when `だけ` follows a word that names a **capacity, a
desire, or a stock** - a potential form, `～たい`, `ある`, or <code>好(す)きな</code>.
Very often the same verb is repeated on both sides of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Potential verb | Vれる／Vられる + だけ | 買(か)える → 買(か)えるだけ買(か)う |
| ～たい | Vたい + だけ | 寝(ね)たい → 寝(ね)たいだけ寝(ね)る |
| ある | ある + だけ | ある → あるだけ使(つか)う |
| な-adjective 好(す)き | 好(す)きな + だけ | 好(す)きだ → 好(す)きなだけ取(と)る |
| できる (fixed adverb) | できるだけ + adverb／clause | できるだけ早(はや)く |
| ほしい | ほしい + だけ | 欲(ほ)しい → 欲(ほ)しいだけ持(も)っていく |
| Amount, as a modifier | Noun + だけの + Noun | これ → これだけの人数(にんずう) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - repeat the verb (<code>買(か)えるだけ買(か)う</code>) and the
"as much as" reading is unmistakable. Native speakers reach for that doubling
precisely because it removes the ambiguity with "only".

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `が` and `を` behave as they do with ordinary `だけ`: they are dropped.
<code>好(す)きなだけ取(と)ってください</code>, not
❌<code>好(す)きなだけを取(と)ってください</code>. Other particles survive:
話(はな)したいだけ話(はな)したあとで.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
好(す)きな<b>だけ</b>食(た)べてください。
Eat as much as you like.
</pre>

<pre>
できる<b>だけ</b>早(はや)く返事(へんじ)をします。
I'll reply as soon as I can.
</pre>

<pre>
ある<b>だけ</b>全部(ぜんぶ)かばんに入(い)れた。
I put every last one I had into my bag.
</pre>

<pre>
眠(ねむ)りたい<b>だけ</b>眠(ねむ)ったら、頭(あたま)がすっきりした。
After I'd slept as much as I wanted, my head felt clear.
</pre>

<pre>
文句(もんく)を言(い)いたい<b>だけ</b>言(い)って、弟(おとうと)はさっさと部屋(へや)に戻(もど)った。
My little brother complained to his heart's content and then marched back to his room.
</pre>

<pre>
走(はし)れる<b>だけ</b>走(はし)ったが、終電(しゅうでん)には間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。
I ran as hard as I could, but I still missed the last train.
</pre>

<pre>
これ<b>だけの</b>人数(にんずう)を集(あつ)めるのは、簡単(かんたん)ではなかったでしょう。
Getting this many people together can't have been easy.
</pre>

<pre>
欲(ほ)しい<b>だけ</b>持(も)っていっていいと言(い)われたが、そんなに入(はい)る袋(ふくろ)がなかった。
They told me to take as much as I wanted, but I didn't have a bag big enough.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern itself is register-neutral: <code>できるだけ早(はや)く</code> is at home in a
business email and in a text message alike. What varies is the flavour the
surrounding words give it.

<code>好(す)きなだけ</code> and <code>Vたいだけ</code> are **generous or indulgent** when
you say them to someone else - "help yourself" - and slightly **self-critical or
exasperated** when you say them about someone: <code>言(い)いたいだけ言(い)う</code>
carries a clear "and never mind the consequences." Tone of voice does the rest.

<code>できるだけ</code> is the workhorse. It attaches to an adverb
(<code>できるだけ早(はや)く</code>, <code>できるだけ安(やす)く</code>) or to a whole clause
(<code>できるだけ参加(さんか)するようにします</code>) and appears constantly in
polite requests and commitments. Its stiffer written cousin is
<code>できる限(かぎ)り</code>, and in formal writing you also meet
<code>可能(かのう)な限(かぎ)り</code>.

<code>あるだけ</code> and <code>残(のこ)っているだけ</code> imply the stock is now
**exhausted**, which is why they so often pair with <code>全部(ぜんぶ)</code>. If you
do not mean "and that emptied it", say <code>たくさん</code> instead.

One structural point worth noticing: because <code>だけ</code> here measures an amount,
<code>これだけ</code>・<code>それだけ</code>・<code>あれだけ</code> mean "this much" rather than
"only this" - <code>あれだけ注意(ちゅうい)したのに</code>, "after all those warnings."
Beginners read those as "only" and get the sentence backwards.

## Contrast

### ～だけ "as much as" vs ～だけ "only"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what sits **in front** of `だけ`. A fixed thing
(a number, a noun, a plain dictionary verb) gives "only". A capacity or a wish
(potential form, `～たい`, `ある`, <code>好(す)きな</code>) gives "as much as".

</div>

<pre>
一(ひと)つ<b>だけ</b>買(か)った。
I bought only one.
</pre>

<pre>
買(か)える<b>だけ</b>買(か)った。
I bought as many as I could.
</pre>

The second sentence does not say the amount was small - it may have been a
trolley-load. It says the amount ran up against a limit.

The trap is the plain dictionary form, which stays on the "only" side:

<pre>
見(み)る<b>だけ</b>にします。
I'll just look. (only looking, not buying)
</pre>

Put that verb into its potential form and the meaning flips completely:
<code>見(み)られるだけ見(み)ておこう</code>, "let's see as much of it as we can."

### できるだけ vs なるべく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>できるだけ</code> aims at the **maximum**: as much as is
possible. `なるべく` aims at a **preference**: if it can be managed. `なるべく` is
the gentler of the two in a request.

</div>

<pre>
なるべく現金(げんきん)でお願(ねが)いします。
Cash if at all possible, please.
</pre>

<pre>
できるだけ現金(げんきん)でお願(ねが)いします。
Please pay in cash as far as you possibly can.
</pre>

Asking a colleague to hurry, `なるべく` leaves them room; <code>できるだけ</code> tells
them to push. Neither is impolite, but a string of <code>できるだけ</code> requests
starts to sound demanding, and `なるべく` never does.

### ～だけ vs ～かぎり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they overlap on ability and stock
(<code>できるだけ／できる限(かぎ)り</code>), and `かぎり` is the more formal and more
emphatic of the two. But `かぎり` also works on **knowledge and range**, where
`だけ` cannot follow at all.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)の知(し)っている<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、その店(みせ)はまだ営業(えいぎょう)しています。
As far as I know, that shop is still open.
</pre>

❌<code>知(し)っているだけ</code> cannot mean "as far as I know" - it means "only
knowing (and not acting)". The same goes for
<code>見(み)わたす限(かぎ)り</code> "as far as the eye can see" and
<code>命(いのち)のある限(かぎ)り</code> "as long as I live". Keep `だけ` for
countable amounts and effort, and let `かぎり` handle scope.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 好(す)きだけ取(と)ってください。 → ✅ 好(す)き**な**だけ取(と)ってください。
  <code>好(す)き</code> is a な-adjective, so it needs `な` before `だけ` - the same
  rule as ordinary `だけ`.

- ❌ 好(す)きなだけ**を**取(と)ってください。 → ✅ 好(す)きなだけ取(と)ってください。
  `を` and `が` drop after `だけ`. Leaving them in sounds like a translation.

- ❌ 走(はし)るだけ走(はし)った。（meaning "I ran as far as I could"） → ✅ 走(はし)**れる**だけ走(はし)った。
  A plain dictionary form gives the "only" reading. The extent meaning needs the
  potential form, the `～たい` form, or `ある`.

- ❌ できるだけ**に**早(はや)く送(おく)ります。 → ✅ できるだけ早(はや)く送(おく)ります。
  <code>できるだけ</code> is already adverbial. Nothing goes between it and the word
  it modifies.

- ❌ 好(す)きなほど食(た)べてください。 → ✅ 好(す)きな**だけ**食(た)べてください。
  `ほど` measures a degree by comparison, not a helping. An invitation to take as
  much as you want is always `だけ`.

## Practice

**1.** 時間(じかん)がないので、____だけ準備(じゅんび)しておきます。（できる・一(ひと)つ）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**できる**だけ準備(じゅんび)しておきます。

The sentence gives a reason - there is no time - and then promises effort up to a
limit, which is the "as much as" reading. <code>一(ひと)つだけ</code> would be
grammatical but would mean "I'll prepare only one thing", a very different promise.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option: 遠慮(えんりょ)しないで、（好(す)きなだけ・好(す)きだけ）持(も)っていってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

遠慮(えんりょ)しないで、<b>好(す)きなだけ</b>持(も)っていってください。

<code>好(す)き</code> conjugates as a な-adjective, so the modifying form before
`だけ` is <code>好(す)きな</code>. The sentence means "take as many as you want",
a standard offer when handing out food or samples.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll gather as much information as I can and report back tomorrow."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

できる**だけ**情報(じょうほう)を集(あつ)めて、明日(あした)ご報告(ほうこく)します。

<code>できるだけ</code> covers "as much as I can" here and sits in front of the whole
verb phrase. <code>集(あつ)められるだけ集(あつ)めて</code> is also good, and slightly
more emphatic - it foregrounds the limit of what is physically possible.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)にある野菜(やさい)をあるだけを全部(ぜんぶ)使(つか)いました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)にある野菜(やさい)を、あるだけ全部(ぜんぶ)使(つか)いました。
"I used up every vegetable that was in the fridge."

The `を` after `だけ` has to go. One `を` already marks
<code>野菜(やさい)</code> as the object; <code>あるだけ</code> is an adverbial amount
phrase and takes no particle of its own.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 話(はな)したい**だけ**話(はな)してから帰(かえ)った。
(b) 話(はな)す**だけ**話(はな)して帰(かえ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the extent reading: they talked until they had had their fill, and only
then left. The measure is their own appetite for talking, and the sentence can
sound either satisfied or faintly exasperated depending on the context.

(b) is the "only" reading with a common twist: they did the talking and **nothing
else** - no listening, no helping - and left. That
<code>話(はな)すだけ話(はな)して</code> shape almost always carries a critical
edge: "all they did was talk."

</details>

# ～きり

**Meaning** · only; just that much - and, after a past-tense verb, "and that was the last of it"

`きり` limits, like `だけ` does, but it leaves a mark that `だけ` does not: a sense
of **finality**. <code>二人(ふたり)きり</code> is not merely two people, it is two
people and nobody else was let in. <code>出(で)かけたきり</code> is not merely "went
out", it is "went out, and whatever should have happened next never did."

## Formation

`きり` follows a noun, a counter, or a **past-tense verb**. Note the asymmetry: as
a limiter it takes nouns; as "and that was the last of it" it takes `～た`, never
the dictionary form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (people, things) | Noun + きり | 二人(ふたり) → 二人(ふたり)きり |
| Counter (number of times) | Counter + きり | 一度(いちど) → 一度(いちど)きり |
| Demonstrative | これ／それ／あれ + きり | それ → それきり |
| Noun + きり + で | Noun + きりで | 一回(いっかい)きりで終(お)わる |
| Verb, past | Vた + きり + (usually a negative) | 出(で)かけた → 出(で)かけたきり帰(かえ)らない |
| Verb, past, as a state | Vた + きり + だ／の + Noun | 寝(ね)た → 寝(ね)たきりの生活(せいかつ) |
| Emphatic, colloquial | っきり | 二人(ふたり)っきり／これっきり |
| Reinforced with しか | Noun + きりしか + negative | 千円(せんえん)きりしかない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after <code>Vた</code>, the second clause is nearly always
**negative**. <code>Vたきり</code> exists in order to say that the expected sequel
did not arrive: went out → did not come back, borrowed → did not return it, met
once → have not met since.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>寝(ね)たきり</code> has hardened into an ordinary noun meaning
"bedridden": <code>寝(ね)たきりになる</code>, <code>寝(ね)たきりの老人(ろうじん)</code>.
The same freezing happened to <code>つきっきり</code>, "staying right beside someone
the whole time."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は二人(ふたり)<b>きり</b>で話(はな)しました。
Yesterday we talked just the two of us.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)に会(あ)ったのは一度(いちど)<b>きり</b>です。
I've met her exactly once.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)は朝(あさ)出(で)かけた<b>きり</b>、まだ帰(かえ)ってきません。
My brother went out this morning and still isn't back.
</pre>

<pre>
貸(か)した本(ほん)は、それ<b>きり</b>返(かえ)ってこなかった。
That book I lent out never came back.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)には千円(せんえん)<b>きり</b>しか入(はい)っていなかった。
There was only a thousand yen in my wallet.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は三年(さんねん)前(まえ)から寝(ね)た<b>きり</b>の生活(せいかつ)をしています。
My grandfather has been bedridden for three years.
</pre>

<pre>
入学式(にゅうがくしき)で挨拶(あいさつ)をした<b>きり</b>、同(おな)じクラスの人(ひと)とはほとんど話(はな)していません。
Apart from saying hello at the entrance ceremony, I've hardly spoken to anyone in my class.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)は部屋(へや)に入(はい)った<b>きり</b>、夕(ゆう)ごはんの時間(じかん)になっても出(で)てきませんでした。
My son shut himself in his room and didn't come out even when dinner time came.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`きり` is **spoken** Japanese first. It is perfectly usable in polite speech - 
<code>一度(いちど)きりです</code>, <code>出(で)かけたきりです</code> - but formal
writing prefers <code>だけ</code>, <code>のみ</code>, or an explicit
<code>それ以来(いらい)</code> for the "since then" idea.

The doubled form `っきり` is a straight intensifier and a clear register marker:
<code>二人(ふたり)っきり</code>, <code>これっきり</code>, <code>それっきり</code>. It is
warm and casual with friends and slightly childish or blunt in a work setting. In
particular, <code>これっきりにして</code> ("that's the last time - stop it") is a
telling-off, not a neutral request.

The limiting `きり` on nouns has narrowed over the last century. In modern Japanese
it is alive mainly in set combinations - <code>二人(ふたり)きり</code>,
<code>一人(ひとり)きり</code>, <code>一度(いちど)きり</code>,
<code>一回(いっかい)きり</code>, <code>これきり</code>, <code>それきり</code> - and it sounds
dated or regional in freer combinations. <code>千円(せんえん)きりしかない</code> is
still heard, and it is colloquial and a touch old-fashioned; if in doubt, plain
<code>千円(せんえん)しかない</code> is what most speakers say today.

Emotionally, `きり` leans toward **shortage and loss**. <code>一度(いちど)きり</code>
of a chance means "one shot only, so don't waste it". <code>二人(ふたり)きり</code>
can be romantic or claustrophobic depending on the story, but it is never neutral - 
it always says that the rest of the world has been shut out.

## Contrast

### きり vs だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけ` draws a boundary and reports it. `きり` draws the same
boundary and adds "and nothing further" - that is either not enough, or the end of
the matter.

</div>

<pre>
会(あ)ったのは一度(いちど)<b>だけ</b>です。
I've met them once.
</pre>

<pre>
会(あ)ったのは一度(いちど)<b>きり</b>です。
I've met them just the once - and that was that.
</pre>

The `だけ` sentence is a plain count; you could follow it with "so we're meeting
again next week". The `きり` sentence closes the door, so a follow-up meeting would
sound like a contradiction.

`だけ` is also far more flexible. It attaches to verbs in the dictionary form, to
adjectives, and to desires (<code>好(す)きなだけ</code>); `きり` does none of these.
Conversely, `だけ` cannot do the <code>Vたきり</code> job at all: ❌<code>出(で)かけただけ帰(かえ)ってこない</code>
is simply broken.

### ～たきり vs ～たまま

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～たまま` describes a **state left unchanged**. `～たきり`
describes a **sequel that never came**. `まま` looks at the thing; `きり` looks at
what should have happened next and didn't.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たまま</b>出(で)かけてしまった。
I went out and left the window open.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たきり</b>、一度(いちど)も閉(し)めていない。
I opened the window and haven't closed it once since.
</pre>

Both are true of the same window, but they are aimed differently. The `まま`
sentence says how the window is; the `きり` sentence complains about the closing
that never happened. That is why a `きり` clause pulls a negative in behind it,
while a `まま` clause happily takes a positive one.

### きり vs しか～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>しか～ない</code> **requires** a negative predicate and
states a shortfall. `きり` takes a positive predicate and states a limit. They can
be stacked (<code>きりしか～ない</code>) for emphasis, but neither one replaces the
other.

</div>

<pre>
部屋(へや)には二人(ふたり)<b>しか</b>い<b>ません</b>でした。
There were only two people in the room.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)で二人(ふたり)<b>きり</b>で話(はな)しました。
We talked in the room, just the two of us.
</pre>

The `しか` version counts heads and finds them few. The `きり` version is about
privacy, and it needs a positive verb - ❌<code>二人(ふたり)きりで話(はな)しませんでした</code>
would have to mean "we didn't talk alone", which is not what the pattern is for.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 出(で)かける**きり**、帰(かえ)ってこない。 → ✅ 出(で)かけ**た**きり、帰(かえ)ってこない。
  The "and that was the last of it" reading is built on the past form. A dictionary
  form before `きり` does not work.

- ❌ 家(いえ)を出(で)たきり、三時間(さんじかん)後(ご)に帰(かえ)ってきた。 → ✅ 家(いえ)を出(で)たきり、帰(かえ)ってこない。
  <code>Vたきり</code> announces that the sequel is missing, so a second clause
  reporting that it did happen contradicts the pattern. Use `～てから` for a plain
  sequence.

- ❌ 好(す)きな**きり**食(た)べてください。 → ✅ 好(す)きな**だけ**食(た)べてください。
  `きり` cannot follow an adjective or express a generous amount. It limits; it does
  not invite.

- ❌ 二人(ふたり)きり**しか**話(はな)しました。 → ✅ 二人(ふたり)きり**で**話(はな)しました。
  `しか` demands a negative verb. If the verb is positive, `きり` stands on its own,
  usually followed by `で`.

## Practice

**1.** 田中(たなか)さんは去年(きょねん)国(くに)に帰(かえ)っ____、連絡(れんらく)がありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

帰(かえ)っ**たきり**、連絡(れんらく)がありません。

The second clause is negative and reports a missing sequel - he went home and the
expected contact never came. That is exactly what <code>Vたきり</code> is for, and
the verb must be in its past form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この服(ふく)は一度(いちど)（だけ・きり）着(き)て、あとはずっとしまってあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are possible, but **きり** is the better fit: 一度(いちど)**きり**着(き)て…

The sentence goes on to say the clothes have been in storage ever since, which is
the "one time and no more" feeling `きり` carries. `だけ` would merely count the
wearings and would sit oddly with the "ever since" clause.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I lent him my umbrella last month and haven't got it back since."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先月(せんげつ)傘(かさ)を貸(か)し**たきり**、返(かえ)してもらっていません。

<code>貸(か)したきり</code> carries both halves of the English - the lending, and
the return that never came. Note that the second clause has to be negative;
`まま` here would describe the umbrella's state rather than your grievance.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は彼(かれ)と二人(ふたり)きりしか映画(えいが)を見(み)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は彼(かれ)と二人(ふたり)きり**で**映画(えいが)を見(み)ました。
"Yesterday I watched a film with him, just the two of us."

`しか` can only appear with a negative predicate, and the verb here is positive.
<code>二人(ふたり)きりで</code> is the natural phrase for doing something with one
other person and nobody else.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 電気(でんき)をつけ**たまま**寝(ね)てしまった。
(b) 電気(でんき)をつけ**たきり**、消(け)していない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a state: the light was on, and in that condition I fell asleep. The
focus is on how things were while something else happened.

(b) reports an omission: I turned the light on, and the switching-off that should
have followed has not taken place. The `きり` clause needs that negative second
half, and it carries a faint note of "I really ought to have done it."

</details>

# ～さえ

**Meaning** · even ～ (of all things); and, with `ば`, "if only ～ - that alone is enough"

`さえ` picks the **most extreme member** of a scale and says the statement holds
even there. If even a child knows it, everyone knows it; if she cannot even drink
water, she certainly cannot eat. The same word has a second life in
<code>さえ～ば</code>, which turns that extreme into a **minimum**: one condition,
and nothing else, is all that is required.

## Formation

`さえ` is a focus particle. It sits where the noun's own particle would sit, and
`が` and `を` are pushed out.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (subject or object) | Noun + さえ（が・を drop） | 水(みず) → 水(みず)さえ飲(の)めない |
| Noun + other particle | に／で／から／と + さえ | 親(おや)に → 親(おや)にさえ話(はな)さない |
| Emphatic variant | Noun + でさえ | 専門家(せんもんか) → 専門家(せんもんか)でさえ |
| Verb | Vて + さえ | 見(み)る → 見(み)てさえいない |
| Minimum condition, noun | Noun + さえ + ～ば | 時間(じかん) → 時間(じかん)さえあれば |
| Minimum condition, verb | Vます-stem + さえすれば | 押(お)す → 押(お)しさえすれば |
| Minimum condition, い-adjective | ～く + さえあれば | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くさえあれば |
| Minimum condition, な-adjective / noun | ～で + さえあれば | 元気(げんき)だ → 元気(げんき)でさえあれば |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さえ` on its own means "even". `さえ` with a `ば` clause behind
it means "if only". The `ば` is what switches the meaning, so look for it before
you translate.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the conditional half of <code>さえ～ば</code> is normally `ば`, or `なら`
after a noun. Pairing `さえ` with `たら` is much rarer and many speakers avoid it,
so keep to <code>時間(じかん)さえあれば</code> rather than
<code>時間(じかん)さえあったら</code> until you have heard otherwise.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)は子(こ)ども<b>さえ</b>知(し)っている。
Even children know this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しくて、ごはんを食(た)べる時間(じかん)<b>さえ</b>ありません。
I'm so busy I don't even have time to eat.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)が高(たか)くて、水(みず)<b>さえ</b>飲(の)めなかった。
My fever was so high I couldn't even drink water.
</pre>

<pre>
パスワード<b>さえ</b>分(わ)かれば、すぐログインできます。
As long as you know the password, you can log in straight away.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)を書(か)き<b>さえすれば</b>、申(もう)し込(こ)みは完了(かんりょう)です。
Just write your name and the application is done.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)すことを、まだ親(おや)に<b>さえ</b>話(はな)していない。
I haven't told even my parents that I'm moving.
</pre>

<pre>
それは専門家(せんもんか)で<b>さえ</b>説明(せつめい)できない現象(げんしょう)なのだそうだ。
Apparently it's a phenomenon that not even specialists can explain.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)<b>さえ</b>よければ、明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は予定(よてい)どおり行(おこな)われます。
Provided the weather holds, tomorrow's match will go ahead as scheduled.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`さえ` is more **emphatic and more written** than the everyday `も`. In relaxed
conversation people say <code>子(こ)どもも知(し)っている</code>; `さえ` raises the
volume and is common in complaints, in news writing, and in argument. It is not
stiff, though - nobody will blink at it in speech, and
<code>これさえあれば</code> is thoroughly colloquial.

<code>でさえ</code> is `さえ` with the noun's `で` kept in place, and it is the usual
choice with **people presented as authorities or as extreme cases**:
<code>先生(せんせい)でさえ間違(まちが)える</code>. The plain
<code>先生(せんせい)さえ間違(まちが)える</code> is also correct; `でさえ` is a
little heavier and a little more formal.

`すら` is the literary sibling. It means the same as this `さえ` and appears in
essays, editorials and formal speeches - <code>挨拶(あいさつ)すらしない</code>.
It sounds bookish in casual conversation, and it has no
<code>すら～ば</code> conditional use, so it is not a drop-in replacement.

Emotionally, the "even" `さえ` almost always leans **negative**. It turns up with
negative verbs and with complaints far more often than with happy news, because
its job is to show that a floor has been breached: not even water, not even a
greeting, not even the specialists. <code>さえ～ば</code>, by contrast, is optimistic
and often relieved - "one thing, and we're fine."

## Contrast

### さえ vs も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mark an unexpected member, but `も` is the neutral,
everyday word and `さえ` is the **scale-topping** one. `さえ` says "this is the
last case you would expect, and even it holds." `も` can also mean plain "also";
`さえ` never can.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>も</b>楽(たの)しめる映画(えいが)です。
It's a film children can enjoy too.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>さえ</b>楽(たの)しめる映画(えいが)です。
It's a film that even children can enjoy.
</pre>

The first adds children to the audience. The second uses children as the hardest
case and implies that adults are covered a fortiori. Swap them the wrong way round
and you get the classic error: ❌<code>私(わたし)さえ行(い)きたいです</code> for
"I want to go too" - that says "even I, of all people, want to go."

### さえ vs まで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さえ` marks a **low bar** that was not cleared, or a hardest
case that still holds. `まで` marks a range that was **extended further than
expected** - "went so far as to."

</div>

<pre>
挨拶(あいさつ)<b>さえ</b>してくれなかった。
He didn't even say hello.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は部長(ぶちょう)に<b>まで</b>文句(もんく)を言(い)った。
He went so far as to complain to the department head.
</pre>

The `さえ` sentence points downward: greeting is the minimum, and the minimum was
missing. The `まで` sentence points upward: complaining to colleagues would be
normal, and he pushed past that. Where the sentence is about something falling
short, `さえ` is nearly always the one you want.

### さえ～ば vs plain ～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `ば` states **a** condition. <code>さえ～ば</code> states
**the only** condition: satisfy this one thing and nothing else matters.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らなければ、出発(しゅっぱつ)します。
If it doesn't rain, we'll set off.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)<b>さえ</b>降(ふ)らなければ、出発(しゅっぱつ)します。
As long as it doesn't rain, we'll set off.
</pre>

The second sentence dismisses everything else - wind, cold, a late start - as
irrelevant. That is why <code>さえ～ば</code> is the natural pattern for reassurance
(<code>体(からだ)さえ丈夫(じょうぶ)なら、何(なん)とかなる</code>) and for
regret when the condition was not met
(<code>もう少(すこ)し早(はや)く出(で)さえすれば、間(ま)に合(あ)ったのに</code>).

Note the verb form: with a verb, the pattern splits the verb open - 
<code>押(お)しさえすれば</code>, <code>言(い)いさえすれば</code> - putting `さえ`
after the ます-stem and letting `する` carry the conditional.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 水(みず)<b>を</b>さえ飲(の)めない。 → ✅ 水(みず)さえ飲(の)めない。
  `が` and `を` are replaced by `さえ`, not stacked with it. `に`, `で`, `と` and
  `から` are kept: <code>親(おや)にさえ</code>.

- ❌ ボタンを押(お)す<b>さえ</b>すれば動(うご)きます。 → ✅ ボタンを押(お)<b>し</b>さえすれば動(うご)きます。
  With a verb, `さえ` follows the ます-stem, and `する` takes the conditional ending.

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>さえ</b>参加(さんか)したいです。（meaning "I'd like to join too"） → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>も</b>参加(さんか)したいです。
  `さえ` is not "too". Using it here says you are the least likely person to want to
  come.

- ❌ 一度(いちど)も見(み)<b>さえ</b>いない。 → ✅ 一度(いちど)も見(み)<b>て</b>さえいない。
  Attached to a verb in this sense, `さえ` follows the て-form.

- ❌ お金(かね)さえあったら、買(か)いたいです。 → ✅ お金(かね)さえあれば、買(か)います。
  The <code>さえ～ば</code> frame wants `ば` (or `なら` after a noun), and its main
  clause states a result rather than a wish.

## Practice

**1.** 忙(いそが)しくて、昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べる時間(じかん)____ありませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

時間(じかん)<b>さえ</b>ありませんでした。

Eating lunch is a bare minimum, and the sentence says even that floor was not
reached. `さえ` with a negative predicate is the standard way to show a low bar
being missed; `も` would work but would sound flatter.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この問題(もんだい)は先生(せんせい)（も・でさえ）すぐには解(と)けなかったそうです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先生(せんせい)<b>でさえ</b>すぐには解(と)けなかったそうです。

The teacher is being held up as the hardest case - if even the expert was stuck,
the problem is genuinely hard. `も` would merely add the teacher to a list of
people who struggled, losing the point of the sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "As long as I have my phone, I don't need a map."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

携帯(けいたい)<b>さえ</b>あれば、地図(ちず)はいりません。

<code>さえ～ば</code> is the "one thing is enough" pattern, and the noun takes `さえ`
directly. A plain <code>携帯(けいたい)があれば</code> would be a neutral condition;
the `さえ` adds "and nothing else is needed."

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この機械(きかい)はスイッチを入(い)れるさえすれば、自動(じどう)で止(と)まります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この機械(きかい)はスイッチを入(い)れ<b>さえすれば</b>、自動(じどう)で止(と)まります。
"With this machine, you only have to switch it on and it stops by itself."

`さえ` follows the ます-stem of the verb, so <code>入(い)れる</code> becomes
<code>入(い)れ</code> and `する` carries the conditional: <code>入(い)れさえすれば</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 弟(おとうと)<b>も</b>自分(じぶん)の名前(なまえ)が書(か)けます。
(b) 弟(おとうと)<b>さえ</b>自分(じぶん)の名前(なまえ)が書(か)けます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply adds the little brother to the group of people who can write their own
name - "he can do it as well."

(b) presents him as the least likely candidate in the family and says the ability
extends even to him. It implies everyone else certainly can, and depending on tone
it can sound faintly rude about the brother, which is why `さえ` about a person is
worth handling carefully.

</details>

# ～こそ

**Meaning** · this one and no other; precisely this - emphasis on the word in front of it

`こそ` has no English word behind it. It does not add information and it does not
change what the sentence claims; it takes one element and **presses on it**, so
that "this year I'll do it" becomes "*this* year I'll do it". The most useful way
to learn it is by what it **replaces**: `こそ` pushes `は` and `が` out of the
sentence and stands in their place, carrying their grammatical job plus an
insistence they do not have.

## Formation

`こそ` goes straight after the element it emphasises. What that element is matters
less than what particle it was wearing before.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun that would take が | Noun + こそ（が drops） | 今(いま) → 今(いま)こそ言(い)うべきだ |
| Noun that would take は | Noun + こそ（は drops） | これ → これこそ本物(ほんもの)だ |
| Noun + another particle | に／で／と／から + こそ | あなたに → あなたにこそ話(はな)したい |
| Time or occasion | 今度(こんど)／来年(らいねん) + こそ | 今度(こんど) → 今度(こんど)こそ |
| Reason clause | ～から + こそ | 好(す)きだから → 好(す)きだからこそ |
| Verb | ～て + こそ | 続(つづ)ける → 続(つづ)けてこそ |
| Fixed reply | こちらこそ | ありがとう → こちらこそ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `こそ` never stacks with `が`. ❌<code>これがこそ</code> does not
exist. With `は` the picture is softer: `は` normally drops, but
<code>今度(こんど)こそは</code> and <code>これこそは</code> are heard when a speaker
wants to lean on the point even harder.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>～てこそ</code> is a different shape with a related job: it says the
result holds **only by way of** the action. <code>自分(じぶん)でやってこそ意味(いみ)がある</code>,
"it only means something if you do it yourself." Its second clause is positive; for
the negative version Japanese uses <code>～なければ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今度(こんど)<b>こそ</b>勝(か)ちたい。
This time I really want to win.
</pre>

<pre>
「ありがとうございました。」「いいえ、こちら<b>こそ</b>。」
"Thank you very much." "No, thank *you*."
</pre>

<pre>
これ<b>こそ</b>私(わたし)がずっと探(さが)していた辞書(じしょ)です。
This is exactly the dictionary I've been looking for all this time.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)<b>こそ</b>行動(こうどう)すべきときだ。
Now is precisely the time to act.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)した人(ひと)に<b>こそ</b>、話(はな)を聞(き)いてみたい。
It's the people who failed that I'd most like to hear from.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)は心配(しんぱい)だから<b>こそ</b>、うるさいことを言(い)うのです。
Parents nag precisely because they're worried.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ続(つづ)け<b>てこそ</b>、力(ちから)がつくのだと思(おも)います。
I think it's only by keeping at it a little every day that you build any strength.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)<b>こそ</b>家族(かぞく)そろって旅行(りょこう)に行(い)こうと話(はな)しています。
We keep saying that next year, for sure, we'll all get away together as a family.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`こそ` is **emphatic and slightly formal** in free use. Speeches, editorials,
slogans and advertising love it - <code>今(いま)こそ</code>,
<code>これこそ</code>, <code>本物(ほんもの)こそ</code> - and in ordinary conversation
too much of it sounds declamatory, as if you were addressing a room rather than a
friend.

That said, several `こそ` phrases are completely everyday and you should simply
learn them whole:

- <code>こちらこそ</code> - the standard reply that turns thanks or an apology back
  on the speaker. It is polite, warm and used at every register.
- <code>今度(こんど)こそ</code> - "this time for real", after previous attempts
  failed.
- <code>だからこそ</code> - "that is exactly why", used to push back on someone who
  drew the opposite conclusion.
- <code>今年(ことし)こそ</code> - the particle of New Year's resolutions.

Because `こそ` insists, it very often arrives in the middle of a **disagreement or
a correction**. It implies that some other candidate was in play and is being set
aside: <code>今度(こんど)こそ</code> implies the earlier attempts,
<code>これこそ</code> implies the things that were not it. If nothing is being set
aside, `こそ` sounds strange and a plain `が` or `は` is what you want.

One thing `こそ` does **not** do is change the truth of the sentence.
<code>山田(やまだ)さんが担当(たんとう)です</code> and
<code>山田(やまだ)さんこそ担当(たんとう)にふさわしい</code> both leave Yamada in the
job; only the speaker's investment differs. Learners who translate `こそ` as "only"
or "must" end up asserting more than the Japanese does.

## Contrast

### こそ vs が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` **identifies**: it fills the empty slot in "which one?".
`こそ` **insists**: it argues for one candidate against others already in the
listener's mind. Answering a question takes `が`; arguing takes `こそ`.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>が</b>担当(たんとう)です。
I'm the one in charge.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)に<b>こそ</b>責任(せきにん)があります。
The responsibility is mine, and mine above all.
</pre>

Asked <code>どなたが担当(たんとう)ですか</code>, you answer with `が`; replying
<code>私(わたし)こそ担当(たんとう)です</code> would sound like a protest against
someone else's claim to the role. The reverse holds too: in a heated exchange,
<code>あなたこそ</code> - "*you're* the one who…" - cannot be replaced by `が`
without losing the whole point.

### こそ vs は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` sets its noun quietly against alternatives. `こそ` sets it
loudly against alternatives **and rules them out**. `こそ` occupies the same slot,
so you never write both.

</div>

<pre>
今年(ことし)<b>は</b>禁煙(きんえん)します。
This year I'm going to quit smoking.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)<b>こそ</b>禁煙(きんえん)します。
This year I'm really going to quit smoking.
</pre>

Both sentences make the same promise. The `は` version compares this year with
other years neutrally; the `こそ` version admits that previous attempts failed and
stakes a claim on this one. That admission is why `こそ` fits resolutions,
rematches and second chances so well - and why it sounds odd on a first attempt at
anything.

### ～からこそ vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` gives a reason. <code>からこそ</code> gives **the real
reason**, usually correcting a wrong one the listener has assumed.

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がない<b>から</b>、手伝(てつだ)えません。
I can't help, because I don't have time.
</pre>

<pre>
大切(たいせつ)な友(とも)だちだ<b>からこそ</b>、はっきり言(い)うんです。
It's precisely because you're a good friend of mine that I'm being blunt.
</pre>

The second sentence answers an unspoken "if we're friends, why are you being so
harsh?" That defensive shape is typical: <code>からこそ</code> almost always
appears where the listener might have expected the opposite conclusion. Note also
that a <code>からこそ</code> sentence usually closes with `のだ`／`んです`, because
it is explaining rather than reporting.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ これ<b>が</b>こそ本物(ほんもの)です。 → ✅ これこそ本物(ほんもの)です。
  `こそ` takes the place of `が`; the two never appear together. `に`, `で`, `と`
  and `から` do survive in front of it.

- ❌ どれ<b>こそ</b>あなたの傘(かさ)ですか。 → ✅ どれ<b>が</b>あなたの傘(かさ)ですか。
  A question word is an unknown, and `こそ` exists to insist on a known candidate.
  Question words take `が`.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)する<b>こそ</b>、上手(じょうず)になる。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)し<b>てこそ</b>、上手(じょうず)になる。
  With a verb, `こそ` follows the て-form. A dictionary form plus `こそ` is not a
  pattern.

- ❌ 「どなたが司会(しかい)ですか。」「私(わたし)<b>こそ</b>です。」 → ✅ 「私(わたし)<b>が</b>司会(しかい)です。」
  Filling in an unknown is the job of `が`. `こそ` in an answer sounds like you are
  contradicting someone.

## Practice

**1.** 去年(きょねん)は一回(いっかい)も山(やま)に登(のぼ)れなかった。今年(ことし)____富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)りたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今年(ことし)<b>こそ</b>富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)りたい。

The first sentence sets up a failed attempt, which is exactly the ground `こそ`
needs: this year, unlike last year, for real. <code>今年(ことし)は</code> would be
grammatical but would throw away the connection the two sentences have.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 「遅(おそ)くなってすみません。」「いいえ、（こちらが・こちらこそ）連絡(れんらく)が遅(おそ)くなってすみません。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いいえ、<b>こちらこそ</b>連絡(れんらく)が遅(おそ)くなってすみません。

<code>こちらこそ</code> is the fixed way of turning an apology or a thank-you back
on the other person: "no, it's me who should apologise." <code>こちらが</code> would
be identifying which party did something, which is not what a reply like this does.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's because the work is hard that it's worth doing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大変(たいへん)な仕事(しごと)だ<b>からこそ</b>、やる価値(かち)があるのです。

<code>からこそ</code> marks the difficulty as the real reason, against the listener's
likely assumption that difficulty is a drawback. The closing `のです` is natural here
because the sentence is explaining a position rather than reporting a fact.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 話(はな)し合(あ)うこそ、問題(もんだい)が解決(かいけつ)すると思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 話(はな)し合(あ)っ<b>てこそ</b>、問題(もんだい)が解決(かいけつ)すると思(おも)います。
"It's only by talking it through that the problem will be solved."

Attached to a verb, `こそ` needs the て-form in front of it. The resulting
<code>～てこそ</code> says the result comes about by way of that action and by no
other route.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 田中(たなか)さん<b>が</b>リーダーに向(む)いています。
(b) 田中(たなか)さん<b>こそ</b>リーダーに向(む)いています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) identifies Tanaka as the suitable person. It is the natural answer to "who
would be good as leader?" - informative and neutral.

(b) argues for him. It implies other names have been floated, or that someone else
has been proposed, and pushes back: Tanaka is the one who really fits. Nothing
about the facts changes; what changes is that the speaker is now taking a side.

</details>

# 数量 + は - at least

**Meaning** · at least this much; this much as a minimum (reading: すうりょう + は)

Put `は` on a number and the number stops being a measurement and becomes a
**floor**. <code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)かかります</code> reports a journey time;
<code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)はかかります</code> says "reckon on thirty minutes - 
it will not be less, and it may well be more." It is the same contrastive `は` you
already know, doing arithmetic.

## Formation

Any expression of amount can take this `は` - a number plus counter, a money
amount, a period of time, or a vague quantity word. The predicate is normally
**positive**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Number + counter | Quantity + は + predicate | 三十分(さんじゅっぷん) → 三十分(さんじゅっぷん)はかかる |
| Money | Amount + は + する | 五千円(ごせんえん) → 五千円(ごせんえん)はする |
| Number of people | Counter + は + いる／来(く)る | 十人(じゅうにん) → 十人(じゅうにん)はいる |
| Object of a verb | Quantity + は + Vた（を drops） | 三回(さんかい) → 三回(さんかい)は読(よ)んだ |
| Vague quantity | 半分(はんぶん)／ある程度(ていど) + は | 半分(はんぶん) → 半分(はんぶん)は終(お)わった |
| With an approximator | Quantity + くらい／ぐらい + は | 一時間(いちじかん)ぐらいはかかる |
| With an estimate marker | Quantity + は + だろう／はずだ／必要(ひつよう)だ | 二日(ふつか)はかかるだろう |
| Negative predicate - **different meaning** | Quantity + は + negative | 全部(ぜんぶ)は覚(おぼ)えていない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `は` goes on the **quantity**, not on the noun.
<code>水(みず)を二本(にほん)は買(か)っておこう</code>, not
❌<code>水(みず)は二本(にほん)買(か)っておこう</code>, which would be
contrasting water with something else instead of setting a minimum.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - an approximator and this `は` can stack, and the order is fixed:
<code>ぐらいは</code>, never ❌<code>はぐらい</code>. The combination softens the
floor to "about that much at the very least."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)まで三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>は</b>かかります。
It takes a good thirty minutes to the station.
</pre>

<pre>

A dictionary like this must cost five thousand yen at least.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)の前(まえ)にはいつも十人(じゅうにん)<b>は</b>並(なら)んでいる。
There are always at least ten people queuing outside that shop.
</pre>

<pre>
その小説(しょうせつ)なら三回(さんかい)<b>は</b>読(よ)みました。
I've read that novel at least three times.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの準備(じゅんび)には一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>は</b>必要(ひつよう)です。
You'll need a week at the very least to get ready for the move.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の影響(えいきょう)で、電車(でんしゃ)が動(うご)くまでに三時間(さんじかん)<b>は</b>かかりそうです。
Because of the typhoon it looks like it'll be three hours or more before the trains run.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)したいなら、毎日(まいにち)二時間(にじかん)<b>は</b>勉強(べんきょう)したほうがいいですよ。
If you want to pass the exam you should be studying two hours a day minimum.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)には百人(ひゃくにん)<b>は</b>集(あつ)まっていたと思(おも)いますが、正確(せいかく)な人数(にんずう)は分(わ)かりません。
I'd say a hundred people had gathered at the venue, though I don't know the exact number.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a plain contrastive `は` and it is register-neutral: it appears in casual
speech, in polite speech and in writing without changing shape. In conversation the
**quantity takes the stress** - <code>さんじゅっぷんは かかるよ</code> - and that
stress is doing much of the work, so listen for it.

What the pattern conveys is **confidence about the low end**. The speaker is not
claiming precision; they are claiming that the true figure will not fall below the
one given. That is why it pairs so naturally with hedges:
<code>だろう</code>, <code>でしょう</code>, <code>と思(おも)う</code>,
<code>はずだ</code>, <code>そうです</code>. Combining a floor with a hedge is not a
contradiction - "at least this much, probably more, I can't say exactly."

The pattern is common in three everyday situations: estimating **time and cost**
(<code>一時間(いちじかん)はかかる</code>,
<code>一万円(いちまんえん)はする</code>), asserting **effort you have already put
in** (<code>三回(さんかい)は確認(かくにん)した</code> - "I checked it three times,
thank you very much"), and setting a **requirement** for someone else
(<code>三日(みっか)は休(やす)んだほうがいい</code>). The second of these often
carries a defensive edge, because the contrastive `は` invites the comparison "and
that's more than you're giving me credit for."

Beside it stands the adverb <code>少(すく)なくとも</code>, which says "at least"
explicitly. It is a shade more formal and more logical in tone, it does not replace
the `は`, and the two frequently appear together in the same sentence.

## Contrast

### 数量(すうりょう) + は vs 数量(すうりょう) + も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` sets a **floor**: at least this much. `も` expresses
**surprise at the size**: as much as this. `は` is sober; `も` is impressed or
appalled.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は二時間(にじかん)<b>は</b>かかります。
The meeting will take two hours at least.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は二時間(にじかん)<b>も</b>かかりました。
The meeting dragged on for two whole hours.
</pre>

The first is planning; the second is a complaint. Note the tense that comes with
them: `は` looks forward to an amount not yet known, so it sits with non-past
and with hedges, while `も` reacts to an amount already established, so it sits
comfortably in the past. Using `も` for a forecast - 
❌<code>二時間(にじかん)もかかります</code> as a neutral estimate - makes you sound
outraged by your own schedule.

### 数量(すうりょう) + は vs 少(すく)なくとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> is an adverb and states the
minimum in words; the `は` marks it grammatically. Use `は` in speech, add
<code>少(すく)なくとも</code> when you want the floor spelled out.

</div>

<pre>
少(すく)なくとも三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>は</b>待(ま)たされた。
I was kept waiting at least half an hour.
</pre>

<code>少(すく)なくとも</code> can also modify things that are not quantities at all
 - <code>少(すく)なくとも連絡(れんらく)くらいはしてほしい</code>, "I'd at least like
to be told" - where the number-plus-`は` pattern has nothing to attach to. In
return, `は` is the one that works when no adverb is wanted, which in ordinary
conversation is most of the time.

### 数量(すうりょう) + は with a positive predicate vs with a negative one

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same shape does two opposite jobs. **Positive** predicate:
at least this much. **Negative** predicate: **not all** of this much - partial
negation.

</div>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を百(ひゃく)<b>は</b>覚(おぼ)えた。
I've learned a hundred kanji at least.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を全部(ぜんぶ)<b>は</b>覚(おぼ)えていない。
I haven't learned all the kanji. (I have learned some)
</pre>

This is not two grammars; it is one `は` marking a contrast, with the negative
scoping over the quantity rather than over the verb. Still, the practical effect is
that a careless negative reverses your meaning. If you want to say "it takes at
least thirty minutes", the verb must be positive:
<code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)はかかります</code>.
<code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)はかかりません</code> tells the listener the opposite - 
it will not take a full thirty minutes.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 一時間(いちじかん)<b>も</b>かかると思(おも)うので、早(はや)めに出(で)ましょう。 → ✅ 一時間(いちじかん)<b>は</b>かかると思(おも)うので、早(はや)めに出(で)ましょう。
  For a forward-looking minimum you need `は`. `も` would make the hour sound
  outrageous, which is odd in a sentence that is calmly planning around it.

- ❌ 三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>は</b>かかりません。（meaning "it takes at least thirty minutes"） → ✅ 三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>は</b>かかります。
  With a negative predicate the pattern flips to partial negation: "it doesn't take
  a full thirty minutes."

- ❌ 一時間(いちじかん)<b>はぐらい</b>待(ま)った。 → ✅ 一時間(いちじかん)<b>ぐらいは</b>待(ま)った。
  The approximator goes next to the number and `は` goes outside it. The order is
  fixed.

- ❌ 水(みず)<b>は</b>二本(にほん)買(か)っておきます。（meaning "I'll buy at least two bottles"） → ✅ 水(みず)を二本(にほん)<b>は</b>買(か)っておきます。
  The `は` has to land on the quantity. On the noun it contrasts water with the
  other shopping instead.

- ❌ 少(すく)なくとも三日(みっか)<b>も</b>必要(ひつよう)です。 → ✅ 少(すく)なくとも三日(みっか)<b>は</b>必要(ひつよう)です。
  <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> already announces a minimum, so the particle that
  goes with it is `は`. `も` pulls in the opposite direction.

## Practice

**1.** この荷物(にもつ)を送(おく)るのに、二千円(にせんえん)____かかりますよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

二千円(にせんえん)<b>は</b>かかりますよ。

The speaker is warning about a cost that has not been paid yet, so the figure is a
floor rather than a measurement. <code>二千円(にせんえん)も</code> would sound like
shock at the price, and bare <code>二千円(にせんえん)かかります</code> would claim a
precision the speaker does not have.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)のパーティーには五十人(ごじゅうにん)（は・も）来(き)て、席(せき)が足(た)りなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

五十人(ごじゅうにん)<b>も</b>来(き)て、席(せき)が足(た)りなかった。

The party is over and the number is known; the sentence is reacting to how large it
was, which is `も`. `は` would set a minimum for something already counted, which
makes no sense once the guests have gone home.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You'll need at least two weeks to get used to this job."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この仕事(しごと)に慣(な)れるには、二週間(にしゅうかん)<b>は</b>かかります。

Putting `は` on <code>二週間(にしゅうかん)</code> makes it the minimum.
<code>少(すく)なくとも二週間(にしゅうかん)は必要(ひつよう)です</code> is equally
good and states the floor twice over, which is common in careful speech.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: レポートは全部(ぜんぶ)は書(か)きました。あと少(すこ)しで終(お)わります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ レポートは半分(はんぶん)<b>は</b>書(か)きました。あと少(すこ)しで終(お)わります。

<code>全部(ぜんぶ)は</code> with a positive verb clashes with the second sentence:
if you have written all of it, nothing is left. A quantity plus `は` claims a floor,
so the quantity has to be one you have genuinely passed - 
<code>半分(はんぶん)は</code>, <code>八割(はちわり)は</code>, and so on.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 昨日(きのう)は三時間(さんじかん)<b>は</b>寝(ね)た。
(b) 昨日(きのう)は三時間(さんじかん)<b>しか</b>寝(ね)ていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats three hours as a floor and, by implication, as enough to get by on:
"I got a good three hours." The speaker is arguing that the amount was not as bad
as it might sound.

(b) treats the same three hours as a shortfall: "three hours and no more." The
`しか` frame needs its negative verb and carries an unmistakable complaint. The
number is identical in both sentences - only the particle decides whether it counts
as sufficient or as scandalous.

</details>

# くらい / ぐらい

**Meaning** · about, roughly; to the extent that ～; and - dismissively - "no more than that"

`くらい` names a **degree**. Attached to a number it makes the number approximate
("about thirty minutes"); attached to a clause it measures one thing against
another ("so tired I couldn't stand"); attached to a small task it belittles it
("it's only washing up"). Those three uses look unrelated in English, but they are
one idea: this is the level we are talking about.

## Formation

`くらい` and `ぐらい` are the same word. Both attach the same way, and nothing about
the attachment decides which of the two you say.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Number + counter | Quantity + くらい／ぐらい | 三十分(さんじゅっぷん) → 三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ぐらい |
| Noun | Noun + くらい | 挨拶(あいさつ) → 挨拶(あいさつ)くらい |
| Demonstrative | これ／それ／あれ／どれ + くらい | どれ → どれくらい |
| Verb | plain form + くらい | 泣(な)きたい → 泣(な)きたいくらい |
| い-adjective | plain form + くらい | 痛(いた)い → 痛(いた)いくらい |
| な-adjective | ～な + くらい | 大変(たいへん)だ → 大変(たいへん)なくらい |
| Modifying a noun | ～くらいの + Noun | 同(おな)じ → 同(おな)じくらいの大(おお)きさ |
| Asking a degree | どのくらい／どれくらい | どのくらいかかりますか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `くらい` measures **how much**, so it goes with spans, amounts
and degrees. It does not name a point on the calendar or the clock; that is `ごろ`.
<code>三時間(さんじかん)ぐらい</code> but <code>三時(さんじ)ごろ</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>～くらいなら</code> is a related pattern worth recognising: it
rejects one option in favour of another. <code>待(ま)つくらいなら、歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)る</code>
 - "if it comes to waiting, I'd rather walk home." The first clause names the level
the speaker refuses to sink to.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)まで歩(ある)いて十分(じゅっぷん)<b>ぐらい</b>です。
It's about ten minutes on foot to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
パーティーには二十人(にじゅうにん)<b>ぐらい</b>来(き)ました。
Around twenty people came to the party.
</pre>

<pre>
どの<b>くらい</b>日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)していますか。
How long have you been studying Japanese?
</pre>

<pre>
お皿(さら)を洗(あら)う<b>くらい</b>、私(わたし)がやりますよ。
Washing a few dishes - leave that to me.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は立(た)っていられない<b>くらい</b>疲(つか)れていた。
Yesterday I was so tired I could barely stay on my feet.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は私(わたし)と同(おな)じ<b>ぐらい</b>背(せ)が高(たか)くなった。
My little brother has grown about as tall as me.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しいのは分(わ)かるけれど、メールの返事(へんじ)<b>ぐらい</b>してほしかった。
I know you're busy, but you could at least have answered my email.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)まではバスで一時間(いちじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>かかりますので、余裕(よゆう)をもって出(で)てください。
The bus to the venue takes about an hour, so please leave yourself plenty of time.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりに聞(き)いたその曲(きょく)は、涙(なみだ)が出(で)る<b>くらい</b>懐(なつ)かしかった。
Hearing that song again after so long brought back memories so strongly it almost made me cry.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`くらい` and `ぐらい` mean exactly the same thing and both are correct everywhere.
The tendency - and it is only a tendency - is that `ぐらい` is a little more
frequent in speech and after the demonstratives (<code>これぐらい</code>,
<code>どれぐらい</code>), while `くらい` turns up slightly more often in careful
writing. No listener will notice which you chose, so pick one and be consistent
within a sentence.

The **approximate** use is completely neutral and extremely common; Japanese
conversation prefers a hedged number to a bare one, and a bare
<code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)かかります</code> can sound oddly exact where
<code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)ぐらい</code> is expected.

The **belittling** use is the one to handle with care. Applied to your own effort it
is modest and generous: <code>それくらい、喜(よろこ)んでやります</code> - "that's
nothing, happy to." Applied to someone **else's** effort it says their contribution
is trivial, and in a request it says the bar you are setting is embarrassingly low:
<code>挨拶(あいさつ)くらいしなさい</code> is something a parent says to a child.
Aimed upward at a manager or a customer it is rude, so keep it for peers, family and
yourself.

There is also a superlative frame worth recognising:
<code>～くらい～はない</code>, "nothing is as ～ as this."
<code>夏(なつ)の試験(しけん)くらいつらいものはない</code> - "there's nothing as
grim as summer exams." It behaves the same way as the more formal
<code>～ほど～はない</code>.

## Contrast

### くらい vs ごろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ごろ` marks a **point in time**; `くらい` marks a **span or an
amount**. If the word answers "when?", use `ごろ`. If it answers "how long?" or
"how many?", use `くらい`.

</div>

<pre>
七時(しちじ)<b>ごろ</b>家(いえ)を出(で)ます。
I leave the house around seven.
</pre>

<pre>
七時間(しちじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>寝(ね)ました。
I slept for about seven hours.
</pre>

The counter tells you which is which:
<code>三時(さんじ)</code> is a clock time and takes `ごろ`;
<code>三時間(さんじかん)</code> is a duration and takes `ぐらい`. Dates and days
work like clock times - <code>来週(らいしゅう)の水曜日(すいようび)ごろ</code>.
Money, distance, weight and headcount are all amounts, so they can only take
`くらい`: ❌<code>千円(せんえん)ごろ</code> is not Japanese.

<div class="note">

**Note** - you will hear <code>三時(さんじ)ぐらいに</code> in casual speech, and
it is not an error, but `ごろ` is the standard choice for a clock time and the safe
one in writing.

</div>

### くらい vs ほど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for approximation they overlap, with `ほど` sounding more
formal. For degree, `ほど` points **up** the scale and `くらい` can point **down**
it. Only `くらい` can belittle.

</div>

<pre>
一時間(いちじかん)<b>ほど</b>お待(ま)ちいただけますか。
Could I ask you to wait for about an hour?
</pre>

<pre>
一時間(いちじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>待(ま)ってよ。
Come on, wait an hour, would you?
</pre>

Those two sentences carry the same number and completely different manners: `ほど`
belongs to the polite register and shows up constantly in service Japanese, while
the second sentence is a friend being pushy.

The split is sharper with degrees. <code>死(し)ぬほど疲(つか)れた</code> and
<code>死(し)ぬくらい疲(つか)れた</code> are both "dead tired", but move to the
bottom of the scale and `ほど` stops working:
<code>お茶(ちゃ)くらい出(だ)してください</code> ("you could at least offer tea")
cannot become ❌<code>お茶(ちゃ)ほど出(だ)してください</code>. Remember also that
`ほど` owns two frames `くらい` does not share:
<code>～ば～ほど</code> and comparative <code>～ほど～ない</code>.

### Belittling くらい vs だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけ` states a limit neutrally. `くらい` states the same limit
and adds a judgement: that little is not worth making a fuss about.

</div>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を五(いつ)つ<b>だけ</b>覚(おぼ)えました。
I learned just five kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を五(いつ)つ<b>ぐらい</b>覚(おぼ)えてください。
Learn five kanji at least, would you.
</pre>

`だけ` reports; `くらい` evaluates. That is why the second sentence works as a
complaint and the first does not, and why swapping them in a request changes what
you are doing socially. <code>一人(ひとり)だけ来(こ)なかった</code> counts the
absentees; <code>一人(ひとり)ぐらい来(こ)なくてもいい</code> waves them away as
unimportant.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 三時間(さんじかん)<b>ごろ</b>勉強(べんきょう)しました。 → ✅ 三時間(さんじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>勉強(べんきょう)しました。
  `ごろ` needs a point in time. A duration counter such as
  <code>三時間(さんじかん)</code> can only take `くらい`／`ぐらい`.

- ❌ 大変(たいへん)くらい忙(いそが)しかった。 → ✅ 大変(たいへん)<b>な</b>くらい忙(いそが)しかった。
  A な-adjective keeps its `な` before `くらい`, exactly as it does before `だけ`
  and `ので`.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、確認(かくにん)<b>ぐらい</b>してください。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、ご確認(かくにん)をお願(ねが)いできますか。
  Not a grammar error but a social one. Belittling `くらい` aimed at a superior is
  openly rude; save it for yourself and for people you are close to.

- ❌ 千円(せんえん)<b>ごろ</b>の店(みせ) → ✅ 千円(せんえん)<b>ぐらい</b>の店(みせ)
  Money is an amount, never a point in time.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しい<b>ぐらい</b>です。（meaning "it's really hard"） → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は解(と)けない<b>ぐらい</b>難(むずか)しいです。
  `くらい` needs something to measure the degree **against**. On its own after an
  adjective it produces the opposite, dismissive reading: "it's merely hard."

## Practice

**1.** 「会議(かいぎ)はどの____かかりますか。」「そうですね、一時間半(いちじかんはん)____だと思(おも)います。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both slots are asking about and answering a **duration**, so both take
`くらい`／`ぐらい`. Note how naturally the hedge fits the answer: a bare
<code>一時間半(いちじかんはん)です</code> would claim a precision meetings rarely
have.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)は八時(はちじ)（ごろ・ぐらい）伺(うかが)います。移動(いどう)に一時間(いちじかん)（ごろ・ぐらい）かかりますので。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)は八時(はちじ)<b>ごろ</b>伺(うかが)います。移動(いどう)に一時間(いちじかん)<b>ぐらい</b>かかりますので。

<code>八時(はちじ)</code> is a clock time - a point - and takes `ごろ`.
<code>一時間(いちじかん)</code> is a length of time and takes `ぐらい`. The polite
register here also makes `ごろ` the safer of the two for the first slot.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I was so surprised I couldn't say anything."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)も言(い)えない<b>くらい</b>びっくりしました。

The `くらい` clause gives the yardstick for the degree of surprise: the level at
which speech fails. <code>何(なに)も言(い)えないほど</code> is equally correct and
sounds a little more formal or literary.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)ぐらいまでに、三千円(さんぜんえん)ごろの品物(しなもの)を用意(ようい)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)<b>ごろ</b>までに、三千円(さんぜんえん)<b>ぐらい</b>の品物(しなもの)を用意(ようい)してください。

The two words have been swapped. A day of the week is a point in time and takes

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)<b>だけ</b>掃除(そうじ)しました。
(b) 自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)<b>ぐらい</b>掃除(そうじ)しなさい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports the boundary of what was cleaned: one room, no others. It is
neutral, and it could be an answer to "what did you get done today?"

(b) uses `ぐらい` to belittle the task - cleaning your own room is the bare minimum
anyone could be expected to manage - and pairs it with a command. It is a telling-off,
and it only works aimed at someone below or close to you.

</details>

# ～あまり

**Meaning** · out of too much ～; so ～ that … - an excess of feeling or effort that produces an unintended result

<code>～あまり</code> names a **cause that went too far**. Worry, nerves, delight,
haste or overwork build past a reasonable level, and the second half of the
sentence reports what that excess brought about - usually something the person did
not intend. English reaches for "so worried that…" or "in his haste…"; Japanese
puts the excess in a noun-like phrase and hangs the consequence off it.

## Formation

The excess comes first, `あまり` closes it, and the result follows after a comma.
Almost anything before `あまり` has to name a **feeling, a state or a degree**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Emotion noun | Noun + の + あまり | 心配(しんぱい) → 心配(しんぱい)のあまり |
| Degree noun in ～さ | ～さ + の + あまり | 忙(いそが)しさ → 忙(いそが)しさのあまり |
| Verb, non-past | Vる + あまり | 急(いそ)ぐ → 急(いそ)ぐあまり |
| Verb, past | Vた + あまり | 緊張(きんちょう)した → 緊張(きんちょう)したあまり |
| Fronted degree | あまりの + Noun／～さ + に | 寒(さむ)さ → あまりの寒(さむ)さに |
| Result clause | ～あまり、（unintended outcome） | 驚(おどろ)きのあまり、声(こえ)が出(で)なかった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the second clause reports something **beyond the speaker's
control**: could not sleep, dropped it, forgot, burst into tears, missed the
deadline. If the outcome was planned and ordinary, the sentence wants `ので` or
`から`, not <code>あまり</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>あまりの～に</code> is the same idea rearranged, with the degree
word turned into a `さ`-noun: <code>あまりの暑(あつ)さに、外(そと)に出(で)られなかった</code>.
Learn both shapes; news writing uses them about equally.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)の<b>あまり</b>、昨夜(さくや)は眠(ねむ)れませんでした。
I was so worried I couldn't sleep last night.
</pre>

<pre>
緊張(きんちょう)の<b>あまり</b>、声(こえ)が出(で)なかった。
I was so nervous my voice wouldn't come out.
</pre>

<pre>
驚(おどろ)きの<b>あまり</b>、手(て)に持(も)っていたコップを落(お)としてしまった。
I was so startled that I dropped the glass I was holding.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)ぐ<b>あまり</b>、財布(さいふ)を家(いえ)に忘(わす)れてきてしまった。
In my hurry I went and left my wallet at home.
</pre>

<pre>
喜(よろこ)びの<b>あまり</b>、彼女(かのじょ)はその場(ば)で泣(な)き出(だ)した。
Overcome with joy, she burst into tears on the spot.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しさの<b>あまり</b>、友人(ゆうじん)の誕生日(たんじょうび)をすっかり忘(わす)れていました。
Things were so hectic that I completely forgot my friend's birthday.
</pre>

<pre>
完璧(かんぺき)を求(もと)める<b>あまり</b>、締(し)め切(き)りに間(ま)に合(あ)わなくなることがある。
Chasing perfection can go so far that you end up missing the deadline.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)の成績(せいせき)を気(き)にする<b>あまり</b>、母親(ははおや)は毎晩(まいばん)同(おな)じ小言(こごと)を繰(く)り返(かえ)すようになった。
So preoccupied did she become with her son's grades that his mother started repeating the same scolding every night.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** pattern. It belongs to novels, newspaper reports, formal
speeches and essays; in ordinary conversation people say
<code>心配(しんぱい)で眠(ねむ)れなかった</code> or
<code>心配(しんぱい)しすぎて眠(ねむ)れなかった</code>. Using
<code>心配(しんぱい)のあまり</code> with a friend is not wrong, but it sounds as
if you are narrating your own life.

The nouns that fit in front of it form a fairly closed set - emotions and
pressures: <code>心配(しんぱい)</code>, <code>緊張(きんちょう)</code>,
<code>驚(おどろ)き</code>, <code>喜(よろこ)び</code>,
<code>悲(かな)しみ</code>, <code>怒(いか)り</code>,
<code>疲(つか)れ</code>, <code>忙(いそが)しさ</code>,
<code>恥(は)ずかしさ</code>. A neutral noun does not work:
❌<code>雨(あめ)のあまり</code> is not Japanese, because rain is not a degree of
anything.

The tone is nearly always **regretful or cautionary**. Even with a positive
emotion, the result is an overreaction: joy that ends in tears, enthusiasm that
ends in a missed deadline. If the result is simply good news, the sentence needs a
different connector - <code>喜(よろこ)びのあまり合格(ごうかく)した</code> is
incoherent, because passing an exam is not something excess joy causes.

Grammatically, the phrase behaves like an adverbial and does not take `は` or `が`.
It sits at the head of the sentence, or right after the subject:
<code>母親(ははおや)は心配(しんぱい)のあまり…</code>.

## Contrast

### ～あまり vs ～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ので` gives a plain reason. <code>あまり</code> gives a reason
that was **excessive**, and its result is correspondingly abnormal.

</div>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)だった<b>ので</b>、何度(なんど)も電話(でんわ)しました。
I was worried, so I rang several times.
</pre>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)の<b>あまり</b>、夜中(よなか)に何度(なんど)も電話(でんわ)してしまった。
I was so worried that I ended up ringing over and over in the middle of the night.
</pre>

The `ので` version reports reasonable behaviour. The `あまり` version admits the
behaviour got out of hand - which is why <code>てしまう</code> is such a frequent
companion. Swap them and you either lose the excess (`ので`) or accidentally
confess to one (<code>あまり</code>).

### ～あまり vs ～すぎて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>すぎて</code> is the everyday word for "too much" and
attaches to any verb or adjective. <code>あまり</code> is literary and restricted to
degrees of feeling and effort.

</div>

<pre>
食(た)べ<b>すぎて</b>、お腹(なか)が痛(いた)くなった。
I ate too much and got a stomach ache.
</pre>

<pre>
考(かんが)え<b>すぎる</b><b>あまり</b>、何(なに)も決(き)められなくなった。
He thought about it so hard that he could no longer decide anything.
</pre>

Notice that the second sentence uses both: <code>すぎる</code> supplies the excess
and <code>あまり</code> frames it as the cause of the paralysis. That stacking is
common and perfectly natural. What you cannot do is put an everyday physical action
straight in front of <code>あまり</code>: ❌<code>食(た)べるあまり</code> asks the
reader to treat eating as an emotion.

### ～あまり vs あまり～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same word, opposite structures. **Something + `の`／verb +
あまり** at the *head of a clause* means "so much that". Bare **`あまり` in front of
a negative** means "not very". The `の` and the negative are the tells.

</div>

<pre>
疲(つか)れの<b>あまり</b>、食欲(しょくよく)がなくなった。
I was so exhausted that I lost my appetite.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は<b>あまり</b>食欲(しょくよく)がない。
I don't have much appetite today.
</pre>

The first `あまり` follows its noun; the second precedes the whole predicate and
demands a negative. In speech the two are also stressed differently, and the
"not very" one has the casual variants `あんまり` and `あんま`, which the
<code>～あまり</code> pattern never takes: ❌<code>疲(つか)れのあんまり</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 心配(しんぱい)あまり、電話(でんわ)した。 → ✅ 心配(しんぱい)**の**あまり、電話(でんわ)した。
  A noun needs `の` to join `あまり`. Only verbs attach directly.

- ❌ 忙(いそが)し**い**あまり、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れました。 → ✅ 忙(いそが)し**さ**のあまり、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れました。
  An い-adjective has to become a `さ`-noun first. The pattern wants a degree, and
  `～さ` is what turns an adjective into one.

- ❌ 嬉(うれ)しさのあまり、予定(よてい)どおり準備(じゅんび)を進(すす)めた。 → ✅ 嬉(うれ)しさのあまり、大声(おおごえ)を上(あ)げてしまった。
  The result clause has to be something the excess pushed the person into. An
  orderly, planned outcome contradicts the pattern.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が**あまりの**なかった。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)が**あまり**なかった。
  The "not very" adverb takes no `の` and no following noun. If you find yourself
  writing `のあまり` in a negative sentence, you have mixed the two patterns.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)のあまり、試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)のため、試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  <code>あまり</code> needs a degree of feeling or effort, not an ordinary event.
  For a plain cause in written Japanese, use <code>のため</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 恥(は)ずかしさ____あまり、顔(かお)が真(ま)っ赤(か)になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

恥(は)ずかしさ**の**あまり、顔(かお)が真(ま)っ赤(か)になった。

<code>恥(は)ずかしさ</code> is a noun, so it joins `あまり` with `の`. The result - 
going scarlet - is exactly the involuntary kind of outcome the pattern calls for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)は（疲(つか)れのあまり・あまり疲(つか)れて）、夕(ゆう)ごはんも食(た)べずに寝(ね)てしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昨日(きのう)は<b>疲(つか)れのあまり</b>、夕(ゆう)ごはんも食(た)べずに寝(ね)てしまった。

The sentence needs "so tired that", which is the <code>疲(つか)れのあまり</code>
shape. The other option starts the "not very" adverb, which requires a negative
predicate and cannot lead into a result clause.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "So absorbed was he in his work that he forgot to eat."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

仕事(しごと)に夢中(むちゅう)になる**あまり**、食事(しょくじ)をするのを忘(わす)れていた。

A verb joins `あまり` directly, with no `の`. <code>夢中(むちゅう)になるあまり</code>
frames the absorption as excessive, and forgetting to eat is the unintended
consequence that the pattern expects.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの人(ひと)は優(やさ)しいあまり、人(ひと)に頼(たの)まれると断(ことわ)れない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの人(ひと)は優(やさ)し**さ**のあまり、人(ひと)に頼(たの)まれると断(ことわ)れない。

An い-adjective cannot sit directly in front of `あまり`. Turn it into the
`さ`-noun <code>優(やさ)しさ</code> and link it with `の`.
<code>優(やさ)しすぎて断(ことわ)れない</code> is the everyday alternative.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 緊張(きんちょう)した**ので**、水(みず)を飲(の)んだ。
(b) 緊張(きんちょう)の**あまり**、水(みず)を飲(の)むことも忘(わす)れていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain cause and a sensible response: nerves, so a drink of water. Nothing
in the sentence suggests the nerves were extreme.

(b) presents the nerves as overwhelming and reports a consequence the speaker did
not choose - being so far gone that even drinking slipped their mind. It is also
noticeably more literary, and would sound theatrical in casual chat.

</details>

# あまりに

**Meaning** · too ～; excessively ～ - so far past the normal level that something follows from it

`あまりに` is a degree adverb, like `とても`, but with a verdict attached. `とても`
says the level is high; `あまりに` says the level has gone **past where it should
be**, and the sentence usually goes on to say what that cost. Too expensive to
buy, too sudden to answer, too detailed for anyone to remember.

## Formation

`あまりに` stands **in front of** the word whose degree it is measuring. It never
follows it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | あまりに + い-adjective | 高(たか)い → あまりに高(たか)い |
| な-adjective | あまりに + な-adjective | 静(しず)かだ → あまりに静(しず)かだ |
| な-adjective + noun | あまりに + ～な + Noun | 急(きゅう)な話(はなし) → あまりに急(きゅう)な話(はなし) |
| Verb | あまりに + Verb | 驚(おどろ)く → あまりに驚(おどろ)いて |
| Emphatic | あまりにも + … | あまりにも寒(さむ)い |
| With a result | あまりに～て／～ので、… | あまりに長(なが)くて、飽(あ)きた |
| Degree turned into a noun | あまりの + ～さ／Noun + に | 暑(あつ)さ → あまりの暑(あつ)さに |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あまりに` is an **adverb**, so it takes no `の` and no `な`
of its own. If you want a `の` after it, the following word must become a noun:
あまり**に**寒(さむ)くて／あまり**の**寒(さむ)**さ**に.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a bare <code>あんまりだ</code> is a fixed colloquial complaint meaning
"that's not fair", used of someone's behaviour:
<code>それはあんまりじゃない?</code>. It comes from this same word but works as a
`な`-adjective predicate, not as an adverb.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が<b>あまりに</b>高(たか)くて、買(か)えませんでした。
It was so expensive that I couldn't buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>あまりに</b>突然(とつぜん)のことで、言葉(ことば)が出(で)なかった。
It was all so sudden that I couldn't find any words.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)は<b>あまりにも</b>寒(さむ)くて、散歩(さんぽ)はやめました。
It was far too cold outside, so we gave up on the walk.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)が<b>あまりに</b>うますぎるので、かえって心配(しんぱい)になった。
The offer sounded too good, which in fact made me suspicious.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は<b>あまりに</b>長(なが)く、途中(とちゅう)で眠(ねむ)くなってしまった。
His explanation went on far too long and I got sleepy halfway through.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>あまりの</b>人(ひと)の多(おお)さに、会場(かいじょう)に入(はい)るのをあきらめた。
There were so many people that I gave up on getting into the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)は<b>あまりにも</b>細(こま)かくて、全部(ぜんぶ)覚(おぼ)えられる人(ひと)はいないでしょう。
The new rules are so fiddly that nobody is going to remember all of them.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>あまりに</b>急(きゅう)なお知(し)らせだったので、参加(さんか)できない人(ひと)も多(おお)かったようです。
The announcement came at such short notice that a lot of people apparently couldn't attend.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`あまりに` is **emphatic and a little formal**. In casual conversation the usual
words are `すごく`, `めっちゃ` and the `～すぎる` ending; `あまりに` sounds
deliberate, and it is at home in writing, in complaints made carefully, and in
speech where you want the excess to register.

<code>あまりにも</code> adds another notch of emphasis and is, if anything, more
common than the bare form in modern speech. There is no difference in meaning;
`も` simply presses harder.

The word carries an **evaluation**, and that evaluation is usually negative: too
expensive, too sudden, too strict. It can front a positive adjective, but even then
the sentence is reporting a reaction that overwhelmed the speaker - 
<code>あまりに美(うつく)しくて、言葉(ことば)が出(で)なかった</code>. What it
cannot do is state a plain, contented high degree. For "the cake was really
delicious", say <code>とても</code>; <code>あまりにおいしかった</code> without a
consequence sounds unfinished, as though a "so I…" clause had been cut off.

That expectation of a consequence is the practical point. Most `あまりに` sentences
have a second half - <code>～て</code>, <code>～ので</code>, <code>～ため</code> - or
end in an exclamation. When you write one, ask yourself what followed from the
excess; if nothing did, you probably wanted `とても`.

## Contrast

### あまりに vs とても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とても` measures. `あまりに` judges. `とても` says "very";
`あまりに` says "more than it should be".

</div>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は<b>とても</b>広(ひろ)いですね。
This room is really spacious, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は<b>あまりに</b>広(ひろ)くて、掃除(そうじ)が大変(たいへん)です。
This room is so big that cleaning it is a chore.
</pre>

Both sentences agree about the size of the room; only the second one has a problem
with it. That is why `とても` fits compliments and `あまりに` fits complaints,
warnings and excuses. Note too that `とても` has a second life in front of a
negative (<code>とても信(しん)じられない</code>, "I simply can't believe it") which
`あまりに` does not share.

### あまりに vs ～すぎる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～すぎる</code> is a **verb ending** attached to the word
itself; `あまりに` is a separate **adverb** placed before it. They mean much the
same thing, and Japanese happily uses both at once.

</div>

<pre>
この服(ふく)は大(おお)き<b>すぎ</b>ます。
These clothes are too big.
</pre>

<pre>
この服(ふく)は<b>あまりに</b>大(おお)き<b>すぎ</b>ます。
These clothes are far too big.
</pre>

<code>すぎる</code> is the everyday choice and the one to reach for in conversation.
`あまりに` is what you add when you want to underline the excess or when the
sentence is written. One structural difference matters: <code>すぎる</code> conjugates
and can end a sentence on its own, while `あまりに` cannot - 
❌<code>この服(ふく)はあまりにです</code> is not a sentence.

### あまりに vs ～あまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あまりに` **precedes** the word it modifies and is an adverb.
<code>～あまり</code> **follows** a noun (with `の`) or a verb and introduces a
clause. Word order is the whole test.

</div>

<pre>
<b>あまりに</b>緊張(きんちょう)して、手(て)が震(ふる)えた。
I was so nervous my hands shook.
</pre>

<pre>
緊張(きんちょう)の<b>あまり</b>、手(て)が震(ふる)えた。
Out of sheer nerves, my hands shook.
</pre>

The two sentences mean nearly the same thing, which is why the pair is so easily
muddled. The difference is in what each one attaches to: `あまりに` needs a verb or
adjective **after** it, while <code>あまり</code> needs a noun or verb **before**
it. Get the order backwards - ❌<code>緊張(きんちょう)にあまり</code>,
❌<code>あまりの緊張(きんちょう)して</code> - and neither pattern survives.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 寒(さむ)いあまりに、外(そと)に出(で)なかった。 → ✅ **あまりに**寒(さむ)くて、外(そと)に出(で)なかった。
  `あまりに` is an adverb and comes first. Putting it after the adjective produces
  neither pattern.

- ❌ あまり**の**寒(さむ)くて眠(ねむ)れなかった。 → ✅ あまり**に**寒(さむ)くて眠(ねむ)れなかった。／✅ あまり**の**寒(さむ)**さ**に眠(ねむ)れなかった。
  `の` has to be followed by a noun. Either keep the adjective and use `に`, or turn
  the adjective into a `さ`-noun and use `の`.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が**あまりに**ありません。（meaning "I don't have much time"） → ✅ 時間(じかん)が**あまり**ありません。
  The "not very" adverb is the bare `あまり`. Adding `に` switches you to the "too
  much" word, which does not belong in front of a plain negative.

- ❌ あまりに静(しず)か部屋(へや)で、落(お)ち着(つ)かなかった。 → ✅ あまりに静(しず)か**な**部屋(へや)で、落(お)ち着(つ)かなかった。
  `あまりに` changes nothing about how the な-adjective attaches to its noun.

- ❌ ケーキがあまりにおいしかったです。（as a simple compliment） → ✅ ケーキが**とても**おいしかったです。
  `あまりに` expects a consequence. With nothing following, a listener waits for the
  rest of the sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)のテストは____難(むずか)しくて、半分(はんぶん)も答(こた)えられなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**あまりに**（あまりにも）難(むずか)しくて、半分(はんぶん)も答(こた)えられなかった。

The second clause reports the damage the difficulty caused, which is exactly the
frame `あまりに` sets up. `とても` would be possible but flatter - it would not
present the difficulty as beyond what was reasonable.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この辞書(じしょ)は（とても・あまりに）重(おも)くて、持(も)ち歩(ある)けません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この辞書(じしょ)は**あまりに**重(おも)くて、持(も)ち歩(ある)けません。

The sentence is a complaint: the weight has crossed the line into unusable. `とても`
would simply rate the dictionary as heavy and would sit oddly with a clause about
what you therefore cannot do.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The news was so unexpected that I couldn't say anything for a while."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

その知(し)らせは**あまりに**意外(いがい)で、しばらく何(なに)も言(い)えなかった。

`あまりに` goes in front of the な-adjective, and the second clause supplies the
consequence. <b>あまりにも</b>意外(いがい)で would be a slightly stronger version of
the same sentence.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 仕事(しごと)が忙(いそが)しいあまりに、返事(へんじ)を書(か)く時間(じかん)もなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 仕事(しごと)が**あまりに**忙(いそが)しくて、返事(へんじ)を書(か)く時間(じかん)もなかった。
（or ✅ 仕事(しごと)の忙(いそが)し**さ**の**あまり**、…）

`あまりに` must precede the adjective. If you want the phrase to come after, you
need the other pattern - a `さ`-noun plus `の` plus `あまり`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この映画(えいが)は**とても**長(なが)いです。
(b) この映画(えいが)は**あまりにも**長(なが)いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the running time as a fact and can be perfectly neutral - a long film
may be exactly what you wanted.

(b) passes judgement: the length has gone beyond what an audience should be asked
to sit through. The `も` sharpens it further, and a listener will expect you to go
on and say what was wrong with sitting there.

</details>

# ～ことか

**Meaning** · how ～! - an exclamation shaped like a question, for a feeling too big to state plainly

<code>～ことか</code> ends a sentence that looks like a question and expects no
answer. The speaker is not asking how worried they were; they are saying the amount
cannot be put into words. English does the same trick with "how many times have I
told you!" - a question mark would be wrong there too.

## Formation

The pattern needs two halves: a **degree word** near the front, and
<code>ことか</code> at the end. Without the degree word the sentence collapses into
an ordinary question.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb, plain | Vる／Vた／Vている + ことか | 待(ま)っていた → どれほど待(ま)っていたことか |
| い-adjective | ～い + ことか | 嬉(うれ)しい → どんなに嬉(うれ)しいことか |
| な-adjective | ～な + ことか | 大変(たいへん)だ → どんなに大変(たいへん)なことか |
| Noun predicate | ～な + ことか | 幸(しあわ)せ → なんと幸(しあわ)せなことか |
| Degree words used | どんなに／どれほど／どれだけ／何度(なんど)／何回(なんかい)／なんと | 何度(なんど)言(い)ったことか |
| Softer variants | ～ことだろう／～ことでしょう | どんなに喜(よろこ)ぶことでしょう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause before <code>ことか</code> takes the **plain form**,
however polite the rest of the passage is. Politeness is expressed by switching to
<code>ことでしょう</code>, not by inserting `です` or `ます`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ことだろう</code> and <code>ことでしょう</code> are the same
exclamation with a softer edge, and they point outward at someone else's feelings:
<code>ご家族(かぞく)はどんなに安心(あんしん)されたことでしょう</code>. Use
<code>ことか</code> for your own feelings and <code>ことでしょう</code> when
imagining another person's.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どんなに心配(しんぱい)した<b>ことか</b>。
You have no idea how worried I was.
</pre>

<pre>
この日(ひ)をどれほど待(ま)っていた<b>ことか</b>。
How long I've waited for this day.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)同(おな)じことを注意(ちゅうい)した<b>ことか</b>。
How many times have I told you the same thing!
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で暮(く)らすのがどんなに大変(たいへん)な<b>ことか</b>、やってみて初(はじ)めて分(わ)かった。
Only when I tried it did I realise how hard living alone is.
</pre>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)の知(し)らせを聞(き)いて、両親(りょうしん)はどんなに喜(よろこ)んだ<b>ことか</b>。
When they heard I'd passed, how delighted my parents must have been.
</pre>

<pre>
留学中(りゅうがくちゅう)、家族(かぞく)からの手紙(てがみ)にどれだけ励(はげ)まされた<b>ことか</b>。
While I was studying abroad, those letters from home kept me going more than I can say.
</pre>

<pre>
あのとき助(たす)けてもらえなかったら、今(いま)ごろどうなっていた<b>ことか</b>。
If nobody had helped me then, goodness knows where I'd be now.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)から見(み)える景色(けしき)の、なんと美(うつく)しい<b>ことか</b>。
How beautiful the view from the mountaintop is.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>ことか</code> is **emotive and literary**. It lives in essays, memoirs,
letters, speeches, drama and song lyrics; in everyday conversation it sounds
heightened, and younger speakers use it sparingly. That is not a reason to avoid
it - it is a reason to notice what you are doing when you say it. Dropping a
<code>どんなに心配(しんぱい)したことか</code> into a conversation makes the moment
weightier, which is sometimes exactly right.

The everyday equivalents are worth having side by side:

- <code>本当(ほんとう)に心配(しんぱい)したよ</code> - plain and warm.
- <code>どれだけ心配(しんぱい)したと思(おも)ってるの</code> - colloquial, and the
  usual spoken way of scolding someone with the same content.
- <code>どんなに心配(しんぱい)したことか</code> - the written, weightier version.

The emotion is usually one of two kinds: **long-suffering** (worry, waiting,
repeated warnings ignored) or **overflowing gratitude and relief**. Both are
retrospective, which is why the verb before <code>ことか</code> is so often in the
past or the <code>～ている</code> form.

Punctuation is a small giveaway. Because the sentence is not a real question, it is
normally written with a full stop rather than a question mark, and it is said with
falling intonation. If you catch yourself waiting for an answer, you have
misread it.

## Contrast

### ～ことか vs ～だろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だろう` **guesses**. <code>ことか</code> **exclaims**. `だろう`
is uncertain about a fact; <code>ことか</code> is certain about a feeling and only
uncertain about how to measure it.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)はもう着(つ)いた<b>だろう</b>。
He's probably arrived by now.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の到着(とうちゃく)をどれほど待(ま)った<b>ことか</b>。
How long we waited for him to arrive.
</pre>

The halfway house is <code>ことだろう</code>, which keeps the exclamation and adds a
guess about someone else's inner state:
<code>お母(かあ)さんはどんなに悲(かな)しんだことだろう</code> - "how his mother
must have grieved." Note that a plain conjecture cannot take
<code>ことか</code>: ❌<code>明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ることか</code> is
not Japanese, because there is no emotion being measured.

### ～ことか vs ～ものか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are rhetorical questions, and they do opposite things.
<code>ことか</code> says "**so** much!". <code>ものか</code> says "**not** a chance!"
 - it is a flat refusal or denial.

</div>

<pre>
どんなに感謝(かんしゃ)している<b>ことか</b>。
I'm more grateful than I can say.
</pre>

<pre>
あんな店(みせ)、二度(にど)と行(い)く<b>ものか</b>。
As if I'd ever go back to that place.
</pre>

<code>ものか</code> attaches to the plain non-past and needs no degree word; its
casual spoken form is <code>もんか</code>, and it is distinctly rough. Mixing the two
up reverses your meaning entirely, so check which of "how much!" and "no way!" you
mean before you choose.

### ～ことか vs なんて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same feeling, different register and different position.
`なんて` fronts the exclamation and is ordinary spoken Japanese;
<code>ことか</code> closes the sentence and is written.

</div>

<pre>
<b>なんて</b>きれいな海(うみ)なんだろう。
What a beautiful sea.
</pre>

<pre>
なんときれいな海(うみ)である<b>ことか</b>。
How beautiful this sea is.
</pre>

The first is what you would actually say standing on the shore. The second belongs
in a travel essay about standing on the shore. Note also that `なんて` has a second,
unrelated use meaning "things like ～" (<code>運動(うんどう)なんて嫌(きら)いだ</code>),
which <code>ことか</code> never has.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 心配(しんぱい)したことか。 → ✅ **どんなに**心配(しんぱい)したことか。
  Without a degree word there is nothing for the exclamation to measure, and the
  sentence reads as a broken question.

- ❌ どんなに嬉(うれ)しかった**です**ことか。 → ✅ どんなに嬉(うれ)しかったことか。／✅ どんなに嬉(うれ)しかったことでしょう。
  The clause before <code>ことか</code> stays plain. For a polite register, switch
  the ending to <code>ことでしょう</code>.

- ❌ どんなに大変(たいへん)**だ**ことか。 → ✅ どんなに大変(たいへん)**な**ことか。
  A な-adjective keeps its `な` in front of <code>こと</code>, as it does before any
  noun.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)は忙(いそが)しいことか。（meaning "next week will probably be busy"） → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)は忙(いそが)しいだろう。
  <code>ことか</code> is not a conjecture ending. Use it only where a strong feeling
  is being exclaimed over.

- ❌ 「どんなに心配(しんぱい)したことか。」「三日(みっか)ぐらいです。」 → the question is rhetorical; it takes no answer.
  Treat it as a statement with an exclamation behind it, not as something to reply
  to.

## Practice

**1.** ____会(あ)いたかったことか。やっと会(あ)えましたね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**どんなに**（どれほど）会(あ)いたかったことか。

<code>ことか</code> needs a degree word to exclaim over, and
<code>どんなに</code> is the most common one. The sentence says the longing was
beyond measuring, which the following line confirms.

</details>

**2.** Choose: あんな失礼(しつれい)な人(ひと)に、二度(にど)と頼(たの)む（ことか・ものか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

二度(にど)と頼(たの)む**ものか**。

The speaker is refusing outright - "I'd never ask him again" - which is the job of
<code>ものか</code>. <code>ことか</code> would turn the sentence into an exclamation
about how much they ask him, the opposite of what is meant.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "How many times have I asked you to turn off the light!"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何度(なんど)電気(でんき)を消(け)すように言(い)った**ことか**。

<code>何度(なんど)</code> supplies the degree, and the past-tense verb before
<code>ことか</code> gives the pattern its long-suffering tone. In casual speech the
same complaint comes out as
<code>何度(なんど)言(い)ったと思(おも)ってるの</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 子(こ)どもが生(う)まれたとき、両親(りょうしん)はどんなに幸(しあわ)せだことか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 子(こ)どもが生(う)まれたとき、両親(りょうしん)はどんなに幸(しあわ)せ**な**ことか。
（more natural still: …どんなに幸(しあわ)せだった**ことでしょう**。）

<code>幸(しあわ)せ</code> is a な-word, so it needs `な` before <code>こと</code>.
Because the feeling belongs to someone else, <code>ことでしょう</code> fits the
sentence even better than <code>ことか</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 祖母(そぼ)に**どれだけ**助(たす)けられた**ことか**。
(b) 祖母(そぼ)に**とても**助(たす)けられました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the help as beyond counting and asks the reader to feel it. It is
written, emotional and slightly formal, and it works well in a letter or a piece of
reflective writing.

(b) simply reports that the help was considerable. It is polite, plain and
unremarkable - the right choice in conversation, where (a) would sound as if you
were delivering a eulogy.

</details>

# ～ことに

**Meaning** · to my surprise / delight / regret - the speaker's reaction, stated before the news itself

<code>～ことに</code> puts your feeling **first** and the fact **second**.
<code>驚(おどろ)いたことに、彼(かれ)はもう帰(かえ)っていた</code> - "to my
astonishment, he had already gone home." The listener is told how to receive the
news before they hear it, which is exactly what English does with "sadly,"
"remarkably," "to my relief."

## Formation

The slot in front of <code>ことに</code> takes a word of **evaluation or emotion**,
in the form it would use to modify a noun. The clause after the comma states a
fact.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | ～い + ことに | 嬉(うれ)しい → 嬉(うれ)しいことに |
| な-adjective | ～な + ことに | 残念(ざんねん)だ → 残念(ざんねん)なことに |
| Verb of reaction, past | Vた + ことに | 驚(おどろ)く → 驚(おどろ)いたことに |
| With a topic marker | ～ことに + は | 困(こま)った → 困(こま)ったことには |
| Usual い-adjectives | 嬉(うれ)しい／悲(かな)しい／ありがたい／面白(おもしろ)い／おかしい | ありがたいことに |
| Usual な-adjectives | 残念(ざんねん)／不思議(ふしぎ)／幸(さいわ)い／意外(いがい) | 幸(さいわ)いなことに |
| Usual verbs | 驚(おどろ)いた／困(こま)った／がっかりした | がっかりしたことに |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the second clause must be a **statement of fact**. A request, an
invitation or a command cannot follow: ❌<code>嬉(うれ)しいことに、一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)きましょう</code>.
The pattern comments on news; it does not introduce a proposal.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reaction belongs to the **speaker** (or, in a story, to the narrator
or the viewpoint character). You cannot use it to report someone else's feeling
about their own news; for that, Japanese says
<code>彼(かれ)は驚(おどろ)いたようで、…</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
驚(おどろ)いた<b>ことに</b>、彼(かれ)はもう帰(かえ)っていた。
To my surprise, he had already gone home.
</pre>

<pre>
残念(ざんねん)な<b>ことに</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
Sadly, the match was called off.
</pre>

<pre>
不思議(ふしぎ)な<b>ことに</b>、同(おな)じ夢(ゆめ)を三日(みっか)続(つづ)けて見(み)た。
Strangely enough, I had the same dream three nights running.
</pre>

<pre>
ありがたい<b>ことに</b>、近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)が荷物(にもつ)を預(あず)かってくれていた。
Thankfully, a neighbour had taken the parcel in for me.
</pre>

<pre>
困(こま)った<b>ことに</b>、会場(かいじょう)の鍵(かぎ)を持(も)っている人(ひと)と連絡(れんらく)が取(と)れません。
Awkwardly, we can't get hold of the person who has the key to the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
面白(おもしろ)い<b>ことに</b>、同(おな)じ質問(しつもん)をしても年代(ねんだい)によって答(こた)えが全(まった)く違(ちが)った。
Interestingly, the same question got completely different answers depending on the age group.
</pre>

<pre>
幸(さいわ)いな<b>ことに</b>、地震(じしん)のとき家族(かぞく)は全員(ぜんいん)外(そと)にいて、けがはありませんでした。
Fortunately my whole family was outside when the earthquake struck, and nobody was hurt.
</pre>

<pre>
情(なさ)けない<b>ことに</b>、一年(いちねん)かけて準備(じゅんび)したのに、本番(ほんばん)では名前(なまえ)すら言(い)えなかった。
Embarrassingly, after a year of preparation I couldn't even get my name out on the day.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern leans **written**. It is a staple of essays, reports, travel writing and
news features, where it does the work of an English sentence adverb. In speech it is
perfectly usable and does not sound stiff, but conversation more often reaches for
the plain adverbs <code>幸(さいわ)い</code>, `あいにく`,
<code>残念(ざんねん)ながら</code>, or simply for `びっくりした!` followed by the
news.

Two combinations have drifted into everyday speech on their own:
<code>困(こま)ったことに</code>, which introduces a problem you are still standing
in, and <code>ありがたいことに</code>, which is a modest, slightly formal way of
acknowledging help. Both are safe at work.

Adding `は` - <code>困(こま)ったことには</code> - makes the phrase heavier and more
literary. It is the same pattern with the contrastive `は` doing its usual job of
setting this circumstance against the rest, and you will meet it far more often in
print than in conversation.

Because the emotion is stated up front, the pattern has a mild **framing** effect:
it tells the listener how to take what follows, which is why journalists like it and
why it can sound editorial. If you want the facts to speak for themselves, leave it
out.

## Contrast

### ～ことに vs ～ことにする / ～ことになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what `こと` is followed by. <code>ことに、</code> with a
comma and a following clause is a comment. <code>ことにする</code> is a decision and
<code>ことになる</code> is an arrangement - both of them **verbs**, both attached to
a plain-form verb rather than to an emotion word.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)で働(はたら)く<b>ことになりました</b>。
It's been decided that I'll be working in Osaka from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
嬉(うれ)しい<b>ことに</b>、来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)で働(はたら)けることになりました。
Happily, it's been settled that I can work in Osaka from next month.
</pre>

The second sentence contains both, which is the clearest way to see the difference:
<code>嬉(うれ)しいことに</code> is the speaker's reaction, and
<code>ことになりました</code> is the arrangement being reacted to. An emotion word
can never take <code>ことにする</code>:
❌<code>嬉(うれ)しいことにしました</code> would have to mean "I decided on
happiness."

### ～ことに vs 幸(さいわ)い・あいにく・残念(ざんねん)ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the adverbs are shorter, more spoken, and fixed in form. The
<code>ことに</code> frame is open: any evaluation word can go into it, including ones
that have no adverb of their own.

</div>

<pre>
<b>あいにく</b>、担当者(たんとうしゃ)は席(せき)を外(はず)しております。
Unfortunately the person in charge is away from their desk.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>情(なさ)けないことに</b>、自分(じぶん)の傘(かさ)をまた電車(でんしゃ)に忘(わす)れた。
Pathetically, I've left my umbrella on the train again.
</pre>

There is no single adverb for "pathetically" in this sense, and that is the frame's
value: it manufactures one on demand. Where a ready-made adverb exists, prefer it in
speech - <code>あいにく品切(しなぎ)れです</code> is what a shop assistant says, not
<code>残念(ざんねん)なことに品切(しなぎ)れです</code>.

### ～ことに vs ～て

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>驚(おどろ)いて</code> makes the surprise a **cause** of
what follows. <code>驚(おどろ)いたことに</code> makes it a **comment** on what
follows. The て-form joins two events; <code>ことに</code> joins a feeling to a fact.

</div>

<pre>
驚(おどろ)い<b>て</b>、声(こえ)を上(あ)げてしまった。
I was so startled that I cried out.
</pre>

<pre>
驚(おどろ)いた<b>ことに</b>、窓(まど)が開(あ)いたままだった。
To my surprise, the window had been left open.
</pre>

In the first sentence the surprise causes the shout; in the second it causes
nothing at all - it merely tells you how the open window struck the speaker. That is
why the て-form sentence needs an action after it, while the
<code>ことに</code> sentence needs a state of affairs.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 残念(ざんねん)ことに、来(こ)られませんでした。 → ✅ 残念(ざんねん)**な**ことに、来(こ)られませんでした。
  A な-adjective keeps its `な` before `こと`. The same goes for
  <code>不思議(ふしぎ)な</code> and <code>幸(さいわ)いな</code>.

- ❌ 驚(おどろ)く**ことに**、値段(ねだん)が半分(はんぶん)になっていた。 → ✅ 驚(おどろ)い**た**ことに、値段(ねだん)が半分(はんぶん)になっていた。
  With a verb of reaction the form is the past. The reaction has already happened by
  the time you report it.

- ❌ 嬉(うれ)しいことに、一緒(いっしょ)に飲(の)みに行(い)きませんか。 → ✅ 嬉(うれ)しいことに、彼(かれ)も来(き)てくれるそうです。
  The clause after the comma has to be a fact. Invitations, requests and commands
  cannot follow.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは嬉(うれ)しいことに、試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)しました。 → ✅ 嬉(うれ)しいことに、田中(たなか)さんが試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)しました。
  The feeling is the speaker's, so the phrase should not be tucked in behind another
  person's topic where it looks like theirs.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)休(やす)む嬉(うれ)しいことにしました。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)休(やす)む**ことにしました**。
  <code>ことにする</code> is a separate pattern that attaches to a plain verb and
  means "decide to". Nothing emotional goes in front of it.

## Practice

**1.** ____ことに、財布(さいふ)は交番(こうばん)に届(とど)けられていました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**ありがたい**（幸(さいわ)いな）ことに、財布(さいふ)は交番(こうばん)に届(とど)けられていました。

The news is good, so the slot takes a word of relief or gratitude. Any evaluation
word fits the frame as long as it is in its noun-modifying form - 
<code>ありがたい</code> unchanged, <code>幸(さいわ)い</code> with `な`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来年(らいねん)から支店(してん)に移(うつ)る（ことに・ことになり）ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来年(らいねん)から支店(してん)に移(うつ)る**ことになり**ました。

This is the arrangement pattern attached to a plain verb, not the comment pattern.
<code>ことに</code> as taught on this page follows an emotion word and is followed by
a comma and a clause, never by `ます`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Unfortunately, the shop had already closed."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

残念(ざんねん)な**ことに**、店(みせ)はもう閉(し)まっていました。

<code>残念(ざんねん)なことに</code> puts the speaker's disappointment in front of
the fact. In speech <code>あいにく店(みせ)はもう閉(し)まっていました</code> is the
more idiomatic version of the same sentence.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 驚(おどろ)くことに、みんなで写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)りましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 驚(おどろ)い**た**ことに、みんながもう集(あつ)まっていました。

Two things were wrong. The verb needs its past form, <code>驚(おどろ)いたことに</code>,
and the second clause cannot be an invitation - the pattern comments on facts, so
<code>撮(と)りましょう</code> has to be replaced by a statement.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 困(こま)っ**たことに**、電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっている。
(b) 電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっていて、**困(こま)っている**。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) frames the news: before you hear about the trains, you are told that this is a
problem. It reads as narration or as a report to a colleague, and it keeps a little
distance from the speaker's own state.

(b) puts the same content the other way round - the stopped trains are the cause and
the speaker's difficulty is the result. It is plainer, more conversational, and more
directly about how the speaker is doing right now.

</details>
