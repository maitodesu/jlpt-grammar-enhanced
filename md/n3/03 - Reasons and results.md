# N3 - Reasons and results


---

# ～おかげで

**Meaning** · thanks to; because of - a good result, with the credit handed to whatever made it possible

`おかげで` does two things at once: it names a cause, and it thanks it. The second
half of the sentence has to be something the speaker is glad about, and the first
half gets the credit for it. That built-in gratitude is also what makes the pattern
usable as sarcasm - put a plainly awful result after it and you are not thanking
anybody.

## Formation

`おかげ` is a noun, so whatever comes in front of it attaches the way it would
attach to any noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + の + おかげで | 先生(せんせい) → 先生(せんせい)のおかげで |
| Verb (past) | た-form + おかげで | 手伝(てつだ)ってくれた → 手伝(てつだ)ってくれたおかげで |
| Verb (non-past) | plain form + おかげで | 住(す)んでいる → 住(す)んでいるおかげで |
| い-adjective | plain form + おかげで | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いおかげで |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + おかげで | 丈夫(じょうぶ)だ → 丈夫(じょうぶ)なおかげで |
| Sentence-final | ～おかげだ／おかげです | 皆(みな)さんのおかげです |
| Fixed courtesy | おかげさまで + clause | おかげさまで元気(げんき)です |

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no ❌<code>雨(あめ)だおかげで</code> and no
❌<code>静(しず)かだおかげで</code>. A noun takes `の`, a な-adjective takes `な`. The
past forms are fine as they stand: <code>休(やす)みだったおかげで</code>.

</div>

`おかげで` can also open a sentence on its own once the cause is already on the
table: 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んで寝(ね)た。おかげで朝(あさ)にはすっかり良(よ)くなっていた。

## Examples

<pre>
先生(せんせい)の<b>おかげで</b>試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)できました。
Thanks to you, sir, I passed the exam.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)の<b>おかげで</b>熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がった。
The medicine brought my fever down.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が空(す)いていた<b>おかげで</b>、予定(よてい)より早(はや)く着(つ)きました。
The roads were clear, so we got there earlier than planned.
</pre>

<pre>
アプリが使(つか)いやすい<b>おかげで</b>、祖母(そぼ)でも一人(ひとり)で予約(よやく)できます。
Because the app is easy to use, even my grandmother can book on her own.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)が車(くるま)を出(だ)してくれた<b>おかげで</b>、重(おも)い荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)ばずにすんだ。
My brother drove us, so I got out of hauling the heavy bags.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>おかげさまで</b>、店(みせ)は今年(ことし)で十年(じゅうねん)になります。
Thanks to everyone's support, the shop turns ten this year.
</pre>

<pre>
君(きみ)が余計(よけい)なことを言(い)ってくれた<b>おかげで</b>、話(はなし)がすっかりこじれたよ。
Thanks to you sticking your oar in, the whole thing has come apart.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)の職場(しょくば)で数字(すうじ)に厳(きび)しい上司(じょうし)に鍛(きた)えられた<b>おかげで</b>、今(いま)は決算(けっさん)の時期(じき)でも落(お)ち着(つ)いていられます。
A boss who was strict about the figures put me through my paces at my last job, and thanks to that I can keep my head even at year-end close.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`おかげで` itself is register-neutral. It sits happily in plain speech and in a
formal address alike - 助(たす)かったよ、君(きみ)のおかげ on one side,
皆様(みなさま)のおかげをもちまして on the other - and nothing about choosing it makes
you sound stiff or casual. It is normally written in kana; the kanji spelling
お陰(かげ)で turns up in older or deliberately literary text.

<code>おかげさまで</code> is a separate, fixed courtesy. It thanks a vague, unnamed
"everyone" and needs no stated cause, which is why it is the standard answer to
お元気(げんき)ですか and the standard opener when reporting good news to someone
outside your circle. It is warm and slightly formal. Bare `おかげで` cannot take that
slot: 「はい、おかげで」 sounds like a sentence someone abandoned halfway.

**The sarcastic use.** This is the part learners cannot parse when they first meet
it. Because `おかげで` builds gratitude into the grammar, pairing it with a result
that is obviously unwanted produces irony - thanks you clearly do not mean:

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が三回(さんかい)も延(の)びた<b>おかげで</b>、夏(なつ)の予定(よてい)が全部(ぜんぶ)飛(と)びました。
Three postponements of the meeting, and my whole summer schedule is gone.
</pre>

Three things flag it. The result is plainly bad; the cause is often a person, and
often the person you are talking to; and `～てくれた` frequently sits in front of it,
since a benefactive "did me the favour of" is exactly the form you twist. Aimed at
someone, it is not a gentle jab - it bites harder than `せいで`, because `せいで`
only blames, while ironic `おかげで` also needles the other person for thinking they
helped. Aimed at yourself it is merely rueful:
寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>おかげで</b>、一日(いちにち)バタバタだった。

Two smaller tendencies. Speakers normally credit other people or circumstances
rather than themselves, so 自分(じぶん)のおかげ, while grammatical, tends to come
across as crowing. And when the result is bad and you are *not* being ironic,
`おかげで` is simply the wrong word - you want `せいで`.

## Contrast

### ～おかげで vs ～せいで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same slot, opposite verdict. `おかげで` says the outcome was
good and hands out credit; `せいで` says it was bad and hands out blame. The grammar
is identical, so the choice is pure evaluation.

</div>

<pre>
雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もった<b>おかげで</b>、子(こ)どもたちは大(おお)よろこびだった。
The snow settled, and the children were over the moon.
</pre>

<pre>
雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もった<b>せいで</b>、朝(あさ)の電車(でんしゃ)が全部(ぜんぶ)止(と)まった。
The snow settled, and it stopped every train that morning.
</pre>

The cause is one and the same event. What decides the word is whether the speaker
is pleased, and a listener hears that verdict immediately - which is why the
sarcastic use lands at all.

### ～おかげで vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` and `ので` merely connect a cause to a result.
`おかげで` connects them *and* passes judgement. If you have nothing to be grateful
for, use the N5 conjunctions.

</div>

<pre>
安(やす)い席(せき)が残(のこ)っていた<b>ので</b>、買(か)っておきました。
There were cheap seats left, so I went ahead and bought them.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)い席(せき)が残(のこ)っていた<b>おかげで</b>、家族(かぞく)全員(ぜんいん)で行(い)けることになりました。
Cheap seats were still going, and thanks to that the whole family can come.
</pre>

There is also a hard structural difference. `から` and `ので` can be followed by a
request, an invitation or a command; `おかげで` cannot, because you cannot be
grateful in advance for something that has not happened yet.
❌<code>雨(あめ)が止(や)んだおかげで、出(で)かけましょう</code> - use `ので`.

### ～おかげで vs ～ため（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ため` is the flat, formal cause with no opinion in it.
`おかげで` is warm. Announcements and reports take `ため` precisely because it
declines to evaluate.

</div>

<pre>
補助金(ほじょきん)が出(で)た<b>ため</b>、設備(せつび)を入(い)れ替(か)えることができた。
A subsidy came through, and the equipment could be replaced.
</pre>

<pre>
補助金(ほじょきん)が出(で)た<b>おかげで</b>、古(ふる)い機械(きかい)をやっと手放(てばな)せました。
The subsidy came through, and thanks to it we could finally get rid of the old machines.
</pre>

The first is a line from a report. The second has a person in it who is relieved.
Put `ため` into a thank-you speech and it sounds like minutes of a meeting.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)の<b>おかげで</b>旅行(りょこう)が中止(ちゅうし)になりました。 → ✅ 台風(たいふう)の<b>せいで</b>旅行(りょこう)が中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
  A cancelled trip is not good news, and a typhoon cannot be teased. With no target
  for the irony, this just reads as a mistake.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)だおかげで → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>の</b>おかげで
  `おかげ` is a noun. A noun in front of it links with `の`, never with `だ`.

- ❌ 静(しず)かだおかげで → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>おかげで
  な-adjectives take `な` before it, exactly as they do before `ので`.

- ❌ 「お元気(げんき)ですか。」「はい、おかげで。」 → ✅ 「はい、おかげさまで。」
  The fixed reply is <code>おかげさまで</code>. Bare `おかげで` in that slot sounds like
  an unfinished sentence.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)ができた<b>おかげで</b>、少(すこ)し休(やす)んでください。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)ができた<b>ので</b>、少(すこ)し休(やす)んでください。
  A request cannot follow `おかげで`; what comes after it has to be something that
  already turned out well.

## Practice

**1.** 先輩(せんぱい)が練習(れんしゅう)に付(つ)き合(あ)ってくれた____、大会(たいかい)で勝(か)てました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

付(つ)き合(あ)ってくれた<b>おかげで</b>、大会(たいかい)で勝(か)てました。

The result is a win, and someone else made it possible, so this is exactly the slot
`おかげで` exists for. `ので` would be grammatical, but it would throw the thanks
away.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 電池(でんち)が切(き)れた（おかげで・せいで）、写真(しゃしん)が一枚(いちまい)も撮(と)れなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電池(でんち)が切(き)れた<b>せいで</b>、写真(しゃしん)が一枚(いちまい)も撮(と)れなかった。

Losing all your photos is a bad outcome, so the blaming word is the right one.
`おかげで` would only work here as heavy sarcasm aimed at whoever forgot to charge
the battery, and with no such person in the sentence it simply reads as an error.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Thanks to the map you drew for me, I didn't get lost."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

地図(ちず)を書(か)いてくれた<b>おかげで</b>、道(みち)に迷(まよ)わずにすみました。

<code>書(か)いてくれた</code> already marks the favour, and `おかげで` picks it up as
the reason for the good outcome. <code>地図(ちず)のおかげで</code> is also fine and a
little shorter; keeping <code>てくれた</code> makes the thanks more personal.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 体(からだ)が丈夫(じょうぶ)だおかげで、一度(いちど)も入院(にゅういん)したことがありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 体(からだ)が丈夫(じょうぶ)<b>な</b>おかげで、一度(いちど)も入院(にゅういん)したことがありません。

<code>丈夫(じょうぶ)</code> is a な-adjective and `おかげ` is a noun, so the two are
joined with `な`. The `だ` survives only in the past form:
<code>丈夫(じょうぶ)だったおかげで</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 君(きみ)がアドバイスしてくれた<b>おかげで</b>、全部(ぜんぶ)やり直(なお)すことになったよ。
(b) 君(きみ)のアドバイスの<b>おかげで</b>、うまくいったよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is the straightforward use: the advice helped, the speaker is grateful, and
`おかげで` says so.

(a) is sarcasm. Having to redo everything is plainly not a good outcome, so the
gratitude built into `おかげで` cannot be sincere - the speaker is complaining, and
<code>してくれた</code> sharpens it into "you did me the *favour* of advising me." Said
to a close friend it is a rueful jab; said to a colleague it is a real reproach.

</details>

# ～せいで

**Meaning** · because of; owing to - a bad result, with the blame pinned on what caused it

`せい` is a noun meaning *fault*. `せいで` therefore does not just report a cause;
it points a finger at it. Everything after `せいで` is something the speaker wishes
had not happened, and everything before it is what they hold responsible.

## Formation

Structurally `せいで` is the mirror image of `おかげで` - `せい` is a noun, so the
same attachment rules apply.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + の + せいで | 風邪(かぜ) → 風邪(かぜ)のせいで |
| Verb (past) | た-form + せいで | 寝坊(ねぼう)した → 寝坊(ねぼう)したせいで |
| Verb (non-past) | plain form + せいで | 混(こ)んでいる → 混(こ)んでいるせいで |
| い-adjective | plain form + せいで | うるさい → うるさいせいで |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + せいで | 複雑(ふくざつ)だ → 複雑(ふくざつ)なせいで |
| Sentence-final | ～せいだ／せいです | 私(わたし)の判断(はんだん)のせいです |
| Hedged cause | ～せいか | 年(とし)のせいか |
| Assigning blame | Noun + の + せいにする | 人(ひと)のせいにする |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `せいか` swaps certainty for a guess: "perhaps because ～". It is how you
name a cause you cannot prove, and it is very common with age, weather and mood:
天気(てんき)のせいか、客(きゃく)の入(はい)りが悪(わる)い。 The frozen phrase
気(き)のせい means "it's only your imagination" and never takes `で`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)の<b>せいで</b>声(こえ)が出(で)ません。
I've lost my voice because of a cold.
</pre>

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>せいで</b>、バスに乗(の)り遅(おく)れた。
I overslept and missed the bus because of it.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)の音(おと)がうるさい<b>せいで</b>、全然(ぜんぜん)集中(しゅうちゅう)できない。
The noise from the roadworks is so loud I can't concentrate at all.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が複雑(ふくざつ)な<b>せいで</b>、何度(なんど)も同(おな)じ所(ところ)に戻(もど)ってしまった。
The streets are so tangled that I kept ending up back in the same place.
</pre>

<pre>
年(とし)の<b>せいか</b>、夜中(よなか)に何度(なんど)も目(め)が覚(さ)めるようになった。
Maybe it's my age, but I've started waking up several times a night.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)の<b>せいで</b>皆(みな)さんにご迷惑(めいわく)をおかけして、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。
I'm very sorry to have caused you all this trouble on my account.
</pre>

<pre>
負(ま)けたのは私(わたし)の判断(はんだん)が遅(おそ)かった<b>せいです</b>。
We lost because I was too slow to make the call.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)無理(むり)な計画(けいかく)を引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>せいで</b>、チーム全体(ぜんたい)が半年(はんとし)休(やす)みなしで働(はたら)くことになってしまった。
Taking on an impossible schedule last year has left the whole team working six months without a break.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Grammatically `せいで` is neutral - it appears in plain speech and in ます-form
without changing shape, and neither men nor women lean on it more. What is not
neutral is what it *does*. Naming someone's `せい` is an accusation, and Japanese
tends to make accusations obliquely, so the word carries more weight than a learner
expects from its plain English translation "because of".

Three practical consequences.

**Aimed at a person, it is strong.** 君(きみ)のせいだ is close to "this is on
you", and it is not a phrase you use with a superior or a customer. Between friends
it is normal; in a workplace it usually gets softened to
私(わたし)の確認(かくにん)不足(ぶそく)でした or to a `ため` clause with nobody in it.

**Aimed at yourself, it is an apology.** 私(わたし)のせいで is how you take
responsibility, and it is completely at home in polite speech - the sentence around
it simply goes into keigo.

**Public language avoids it entirely.** Station announcements, notices and press
releases use `ため`, because an organisation reporting a delay is describing a
situation, not assigning fault. A `せいで` in that register would sound like the
railway company complaining about its own accident.

The related verb <code>～のせいにする</code> means "blame it on ～", and
<code>人(ひと)のせいにする</code> - "blame other people" - is a stock criticism of
character. `せいか`, by contrast, is soft and slightly musing; it is the one member
of the family that does not accuse anyone.

## Contrast

### ～せいで vs ～おかげで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical grammar, opposite verdict. `せいで` says "and that was
bad"; `おかげで` says "and thank goodness". Neither is available for a neutral
statement of cause.

</div>

<pre>
試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった<b>せいで</b>、一日(いちにち)無駄(むだ)にした。
The match was called off, and it cost me a whole day.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった<b>おかげで</b>、久(ひさ)しぶりに家族(かぞく)と過(す)ごせた。
The match was called off, and thanks to that I got a rare day with my family.
</pre>

Same event, two speakers, two verdicts. Note that `おかげで` can be turned ironic
and used for a bad outcome, but the reverse does not work: `せいで` with a genuinely
happy result is not sarcasm, just an error.

### ～せいで vs ～ため

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ため` reports; `せいで` complains. Anything written on behalf of
an organisation takes `ため`.

</div>

<pre>
人身(じんしん)事故(じこ)の<b>ため</b>、上(のぼ)り線(せん)に遅(おく)れが出(で)ております。
Owing to an accident involving a passenger, northbound services are running late.
</pre>

<pre>
人身(じんしん)事故(じこ)の<b>せいで</b>、大事(だいじ)な面接(めんせつ)に遅(おく)れそうだ。
Because of some accident on the line I'm going to be late for an important interview.
</pre>

The first is the station announcement; the second is the passenger swearing at it.
The facts are the same, and only the speaker's stake in them has changed. This is
the single most useful register test in this family of patterns: if the sentence
belongs on a sign, it takes `ため`.

### ～せいで vs ～ばかりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せいで` blames the cause; `ばかりに` regrets it. `ばかりに`
means "just because of that one small thing", and the speaker is usually the one
who has to live with the consequence.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の一言(ひとこと)の<b>せいで</b>、場(ば)の空気(くうき)が悪(わる)くなった。
One remark from him soured the whole mood of the room.
</pre>

<pre>
一言(ひとこと)余計(よけい)なことを言(い)った<b>ばかりに</b>、長年(ながねん)の友人(ゆうじん)をなくしてしまった。
Just because I said one thing too many, I lost a friend of many years.
</pre>

`せいで` points outward at an ordinary-sized cause. `ばかりに` insists that the cause
was trivial and the price enormous, and it is characteristically self-directed.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)の<b>せいで</b>合格(ごうかく)できました。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)の<b>おかげで</b>合格(ごうかく)できました。
  Passing is a good outcome, so this reads as if you were blaming your teacher for
  your own success. The evaluation, not the grammar, is what is wrong.

- ❌ 大雪(おおゆき)の<b>せいで</b>本日(ほんじつ)は休業(きゅうぎょう)させていただきます。 → ✅ 大雪(おおゆき)の<b>ため</b>本日(ほんじつ)は休業(きゅうぎょう)させていただきます。
  A notice on a shop door is written for the public, and public language does not
  blame. Use `ため`.

- ❌ 静(しず)かだせいで → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>せいで
  `せい` is a noun, so a な-adjective needs `な` and a noun needs `の`.

- ❌ 暑(あつ)い<b>せいで</b>、窓(まど)を開(あ)けてください。 → ✅ 暑(あつ)い<b>ので</b>、窓(まど)を開(あ)けてください。
  A request cannot follow `せいで`; the second clause has to be a result that has
  already gone wrong.

- ❌ それは気(き)の<b>せいで</b>ですよ。 → ✅ それは気(き)の<b>せい</b>ですよ。
  <code>気(き)のせい</code> ("you're imagining it") is a fixed noun phrase. It takes
  `だ`／`です`, never `で`.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)____、楽(たの)しみにしていた花火(はなび)大会(たいかい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

台風(たいふう)<b>のせいで</b>、楽(たの)しみにしていた花火(はなび)大会(たいかい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。

A cancelled event the speaker was looking forward to is a bad outcome, so the
blaming form fits. The `の` is obligatory because <code>台風(たいふう)</code> is a noun
and `せい` is also a noun.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 大雨(おおあめ)（のせいで・のため）、本日(ほんじつ)の見学会(けんがくかい)は中止(ちゅうし)とさせていただきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大雨(おおあめ)<b>のため</b>、本日(ほんじつ)の見学会(けんがくかい)は中止(ちゅうし)とさせていただきます。

The keigo ending shows this is an organisation speaking to the public, and public
notices report causes rather than blaming them. `のせいで` would make the announcer
sound personally aggrieved about the weather.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I couldn't sleep last night because of the noise next door."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

隣(となり)の部屋(へや)がうるさかった<b>せいで</b>、昨夜(ゆうべ)はよく眠(ねむ)れませんでした。

The result is a bad night's sleep and the speaker is grumbling about the neighbours,
which is exactly `せいで`. <code>隣(となり)の音(おと)のせいで</code> works too; with a
plain noun in front, remember the `の`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 説明(せつめい)が不十分(ふじゅうぶん)だせいで、参加者(さんかしゃ)が会場(かいじょう)を間違(まちが)えてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 説明(せつめい)が不十分(ふじゅうぶん)<b>だった</b>せいで、参加者(さんかしゃ)が会場(かいじょう)を間違(まちが)えてしまった。

Plain `だ` cannot sit in front of `せい`. Either use the past form
<code>不十分(ふじゅうぶん)だったせいで</code>, or the non-past attributive
<code>不十分(ふじゅうぶん)なせいで</code>. Both are correct; the past reads more naturally
here because the explanation is over and done with.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 工事(こうじ)の<b>せいで</b>、この辺(へん)は夜(よる)も静(しず)かじゃない。
(b) 工事(こうじ)の<b>せいか</b>、この辺(へん)は夜(よる)も静(しず)かじゃない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the cause as established fact and complains about it: the roadworks are
why the area is noisy at night, and the speaker resents it.

(b) offers the cause as a guess - "maybe it's the roadworks". The speaker has
noticed the noise, has a suspicion, and is not committing to it. `せいか` also
softens the accusation considerably, which is why it is the safer choice when you
are speculating about something a person did.

</details>

# ～ために - purpose

**Meaning** · in order to; for the sake of - the aim you are acting towards

`ために` states a **goal** and then the action taken to reach it. The goal comes
first, the action second, and the two are joined by the speaker's intention: I want
that, therefore I do this. With a noun in front, it shifts slightly to "for the
benefit of" - the person or thing the effort is aimed at.

## Formation

`ため` is a noun, so it takes `の` after another noun and the plain attributive form
after a verb or adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain non-past) | dictionary form + ために | 覚(おぼ)える → 覚(おぼ)えるために |
| Noun | Noun + の + ために | 家族(かぞく) → 家族(かぞく)のために |
| Modifying a noun | ～ため + **の** + Noun | 試験(しけん) → 試験(しけん)のための参考書(さんこうしょ) |
| Setting up a condition | ～ため**には** | 続(つづ)ける → 続(つづ)けるためには |
| Sentence-final | ～ためだ／ためです | 貯金(ちょきん)するためです |
| Written style | ～ため（`に` dropped） | 確認(かくにん)するため、連絡(れんらく)しました |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front of purpose `ために` must be **non-past** and
must be something you can decide to do. Past tense in that slot switches the whole
sentence to the *cause* meaning, and a verb nobody can will - 治(なお)る,
喜(よろこ)ぶ, potential forms, negatives - normally calls for `ように` instead.

</div>

Both clauses normally share a subject: the person with the goal is the person who
acts. The `Noun + のために` version escapes that restriction, which is why you can do
something 子(こ)どものために without the child doing anything at all.

## Examples

<pre>
日本(にほん)で働(はたら)く<b>ために</b>、日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)しています。
I'm studying Japanese in order to work in Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)の<b>ために</b>、毎朝(まいあさ)お弁当(べんとう)を作(つく)ります。
I make packed lunches every morning for my family.
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)の<b>ために</b>、エレベーターを使(つか)わないようにしている。
For my health, I make a point of not using the lift.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)する<b>ために</b>、三年(さんねん)かけてお金(かね)をためた。
I spent three years saving up so that I could study abroad.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしを続(つづ)ける<b>ためには</b>、もう少(すこ)し収入(しゅうにゅう)がいる。
If I'm going to keep living on my own, I need a bit more income.
</pre>

<pre>
これは新人(しんじん)が現場(げんば)に慣(な)れる<b>ための</b>研修(けんしゅう)です。
This is training designed to get new staff used to the site.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)いでいるのは、終電(しゅうでん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わせる<b>ためです</b>。
The reason I'm rushing is to make the last train.
</pre>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)の人(ひと)たちの安全(あんぜん)を守(まも)る<b>ために</b>、毎年(まいとし)同(おな)じ時期(じき)に避難(ひなん)訓練(くんれん)を行(おこな)っています。
To keep the people of the area safe, we run an evacuation drill at the same time every year.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ために` sits a step above casual conversation. It is entirely normal in speech, but
it has a slightly deliberate, purposeful ring, and friends chatting are more likely
to say 日本(にほん)で働(はたら)きたいから勉強(べんきょう)してる than to build a
purpose clause. Written Japanese goes the other way and often drops the `に`
altogether: 安全(あんぜん)確認(かくにん)のため、一時(いちじ)停止(ていし)します。

<code>ためには</code> is worth learning as a unit. The `は` turns the goal into a
premise - "if the aim is X, then…" - and it is the standard opening for advice,
requirements and analysis: 上達(じょうたつ)するためには、毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ
続(つづ)けることです。 <code>ためだけに</code> adds "solely, and for nothing else",
often with a hint that the effort was excessive.

One social note. <code>君(きみ)のために言(い)っているんだ</code> - "I'm saying this
for your own good" - is a stock line of someone overstepping, and Japanese speakers
hear it that way. `のために` aimed at a listener claims to know what is good for
them, so in a request or a piece of advice it is often safer to leave the phrase out
and let the kindness be implied.

## Contrast

### ～ために vs ～ように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ために` for goals you act on directly; `ように` for outcomes you
can only arrange for. If the verb in front is a potential form, a negative, or
something that happens to someone else, you want `ように`.

</div>

This is the split that decides most sentences, and it is mechanical enough to test.

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)える<b>ために</b>、毎晩(まいばん)手(て)で書(か)いています。
I write kanji out by hand every night in order to learn them.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を忘(わす)れない<b>ように</b>、毎晩(まいばん)手(て)で書(か)いています。
I write kanji out by hand every night so that I don't forget them.
</pre>

Learning is something you set out to do; not forgetting is a state you can only
make likely. The same test explains the subject rule:

<pre>
子(こ)どもが自分(じぶん)で片付(かたづ)けられる<b>ように</b>、棚(たな)を低(ひく)くしました。
I lowered the shelves so the children can tidy up by themselves.
</pre>

The person acting is the parent; the person doing the tidying is the child, and
<code>片付(かたづ)けられる</code> is a potential form. Both point to `ように`. Replace it
with `ために` and the sentence claims the parent is going to do the tidying.

### ～ために for purpose vs ～ため for cause

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - check the tense and the verb type. Purpose: non-past, willed,
pointing forward. Cause: past tense, adjectives, or events nobody chose, pointing
backward.

</div>

<pre>
資格(しかく)を取(と)る<b>ために</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を一週間(いっしゅうかん)休(やす)みました。
I took a week off work in order to get the qualification.
</pre>

<pre>
資格(しかく)が取(と)れなかった<b>ため</b>、昇進(しょうしん)が一年(いちねん)遅(おく)れました。
Because I failed to get the qualification, my promotion was put back a year.
</pre>

Nothing but the form of the first verb has changed. `～る + ために` is read as an
aim; `～た + ため` and `～ない + ため` are read as a reason. The cause use also
accepts adjectives and nouns freely - <code>寒(さむ)いため</code>,
<code>大雨(おおあめ)のため</code> - while the purpose use cannot, because an adjective
is not something you do on purpose.

### ～ために vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ために` names your aim. The `のに` of purpose comments on
whether something is *suited* to a task: convenient, necessary, expensive,
time-consuming.

</div>

<pre>
缶(かん)を開(あ)ける<b>ために</b>、新(あたら)しい道具(どうぐ)を買(か)いました。
I bought a new tool in order to open the tins.
</pre>

<pre>
この道具(どうぐ)は缶(かん)を開(あ)ける<b>のに</b>便利(べんり)です。
This tool is handy for opening tins.
</pre>

The tell is the predicate that follows. If it is an ordinary action verb, use
`ために`; if it is <code>便利(べんり)だ</code>, <code>必要(ひつよう)だ</code>,
<code>時間(じかん)がかかる</code>, <code>いくらかかる</code> or the like, `のに` is the
natural partner. Note that this is the *purpose* `のに`, not the "although" `のに`
learners meet first; the two are told apart by exactly this test.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 家族(かぞく)ために働(はたら)いています。 → ✅ 家族(かぞく)<b>の</b>ために働(はたら)いています。
  `ため` is a noun, so another noun links to it with `の`.

- ❌ 健康(けんこう)になったために毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っています。 → ✅ 健康(けんこう)になるために毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っています。
  Purpose `ために` never takes past tense. With `なった` the sentence flips to a cause
  reading - "because I got healthy" - which is not what you meant.

- ❌ 風邪(かぜ)を引(ひ)かない<b>ために</b>、早(はや)く寝(ね)ます。 → ✅ 風邪(かぜ)を引(ひ)かない<b>ように</b>、早(はや)く寝(ね)ます。
  Catching a cold is not something you decide, so the negative goal takes `ように`.

- ❌ 子(こ)どもが喜(よろこ)ぶ<b>ために</b>、ケーキを買(か)いました。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもが喜(よろこ)ぶ<b>ように</b>、ケーキを買(か)いました。
  Two different subjects and a verb the speaker cannot perform.
  ✅子(こ)どもの<b>ために</b>ケーキを買(か)いました is the other repair - with a noun,
  `のために` is free of the same-subject rule.

- ❌ これは外国人(がいこくじん)が日本(にほん)の生活(せいかつ)に慣(な)れるために本(ほん)です。 → ✅ …慣(な)れる<b>ための</b>本(ほん)です。
  In front of a noun, `ため` links with `の`. `ために` can only modify a predicate.

## Practice

**1.** 自分(じぶん)の店(みせ)を持(も)つ____、十年(じゅうねん)働(はたら)いてお金(かね)をためました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自分(じぶん)の店(みせ)を持(も)つ<b>ために</b>、十年(じゅうねん)働(はたら)いてお金(かね)をためました。

Owning a shop is a deliberate aim, the verb is non-past, and the same person holds
the goal and does the saving - all three conditions for purpose `ために`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 遅刻(ちこく)しない（ために・ように）、目覚(めざ)まし時計(どけい)を二(ふた)つ置(お)いています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

遅刻(ちこく)しない<b>ように</b>、目覚(めざ)まし時計(どけい)を二(ふた)つ置(お)いています。

The goal is stated as a negative - *not* being late - and nobody decides to not
oversleep; you can only stack the odds. Negative goals take `ように`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I moved closer to the station in order to shorten my commute."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

通勤(つうきん)時間(じかん)を短(みじか)くする<b>ために</b>、駅(えき)の近(ちか)くに引(ひ)っ越(こ)しました。

<code>短(みじか)くする</code> is the transitive, willed form, so `ために` is right.
Had you written <code>通勤(つうきん)時間(じかん)が短(みじか)くなるように</code> - "so that
the commute would get shorter" - `ように` would be the one to use.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)で使(つか)うために資料(しりょう)を三十部(さんじゅうぶ)印刷(いんさつ)しました。それは参加者(さんかしゃ)に配(くば)るために資料(しりょう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ それは参加者(さんかしゃ)に配(くば)る<b>ための</b>資料(しりょう)です。

The first sentence is fine - `ために` modifying a verb. The second one puts `ため` in
front of a **noun**, and there it has to link with `の`. The rule is the same one
that gives you <code>試験(しけん)のための参考書(さんこうしょ)</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子(こ)どもの<b>ために</b>転校(てんこう)を決(き)めました。
(b) 子(こ)どもが新(あたら)しい友(とも)だちを作(つく)れる<b>ように</b>転校(てんこう)を決(き)めました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the decision was made **for the child's sake**. It names the beneficiary
and leaves the actual hoped-for effect unstated - the listener supplies it.

(b) names the effect instead: the parent hopes the child will be able to make new
friends. Because <code>作(つく)れる</code> is a potential form belonging to the child,
not the parent, the sentence has to use `ように`; ❌<code>作(つく)れるために</code> is
not available. (a) is warmer and vaguer, (b) more specific about what the parent was
actually aiming at.

</details>

# ～ために - cause

**Meaning** · because of; owing to - the flat, formal statement of a cause, with no opinion attached

This is the same `ため` as the purpose one, doing the opposite job. Here it looks
backwards: something happened, and this clause names what brought it about. Its
defining quality is that it **refuses to evaluate**. It neither thanks nor blames,
which is exactly why signs, announcements, reports and business email are full of
it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (past) | た-form + ため | 遅(おく)れた → 遅(おく)れたため |
| Verb (non-past) | plain form + ため | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かないため |
| い-adjective | plain form + ため | 古(ふる)い → 古(ふる)いため |
| い-adjective (past) | ～かった + ため | 寒(さむ)かった → 寒(さむ)かったため |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + ため | 不便(ふべん)だ → 不便(ふべん)なため |
| Noun | Noun + **の** + ため | 工事(こうじ) → 工事(こうじ)のため |
| Noun (past) | ～だった + ため | 休日(きゅうじつ)だった → 休日(きゅうじつ)だったため |
| Sentence-final | ～ためです | 中止(ちゅうし)になったのは大雨(おおあめ)のためです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in the cause sense the `に` is very often dropped. <code>～ため、</code> with
a comma is the standard shape on a notice; <code>～ために</code> is equally correct and
sounds a shade less clipped. The purpose sense, by contrast, keeps its `に` most of
the time. That is a tendency and not a test - the tense of the verb in front is the
reliable signal.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)の<b>ため</b>、試合(しあい)は延期(えんき)されました。
Owing to heavy rain, the match has been postponed.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の<b>ため</b>、道路(どうろ)が込(こ)んでいます。
The roads are congested because of an accident.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)が届(とど)かない<b>ため</b>、出荷(しゅっか)は来週(らいしゅう)になります。
As the parts have not arrived, shipping will be next week.
</pre>

<pre>
建物(たてもの)が古(ふる)い<b>ため</b>、この棟(とう)にはエレベーターがありません。
This wing has no lift, the building being an old one.
</pre>

<pre>
交通(こうつう)が不便(ふべん)な<b>ため</b>、この辺(へん)は家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い。
Rents are low around here because the transport links are poor.
</pre>

<pre>
停電(ていでん)になった<b>ために</b>、一日(いちにち)作業(さぎょう)が止(と)まってしまった。
The power went out, and work was at a standstill for a whole day.
</pre>

<pre>
お申(もう)し込(こ)みが定員(ていいん)に達(たっ)した<b>ため</b>、受付(うけつけ)を終了(しゅうりょう)いたしました。
Applications having reached the limit, registration is now closed.
</pre>

<pre>
担当者(たんとうしゃ)が急(きゅう)に入院(にゅういん)した<b>ため</b>、来月(らいげつ)の説明会(せつめいかい)は日程(にってい)を改(あらた)めてご案内(あんない)いたします。
As the member of staff in charge has been taken into hospital, we will write again with new dates for next month's briefing.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ため` is the written cause. It belongs to notices, station announcements, news
copy, minutes, academic prose and any email where you are speaking as a company
rather than as yourself. In relaxed conversation it sounds starched: a friend who
says 眠(ねむ)かったため遅(おく)れたよ is either joking or reading from a form.
The everyday equivalents are `から` and `ので`.

What makes it worth the stiffness is its neutrality. `せいで` blames and `おかげで`
thanks; `ため` does neither, so a railway can announce
人身(じんしん)事故(じこ)のため without implying anything about anybody. When you
need to explain a problem to a customer or a superior and want no accusation
audible in the sentence, `ため` is the safe word.

Two structural habits follow from the register. First, what comes after a cause
`ため` is normally a **statement**, not a request or an invitation; when a notice
does end in お願(ねが)いします, the `ため` in front of it is usually the purpose one
（安全(あんぜん)確認(かくにん)のため、…）. If you need "because X, please do Y",
reach for `ので`. Second, `ため` prefers reasons that are impersonal and verifiable - 
weather, accidents, rules, numbers - over reasons that are matters of feeling.
寂(さび)しかったため電話(でんわ)した is grammatical and slightly absurd.

Finally, be aware that `ため` is genuinely ambiguous in some sentences.
父(ちち)のために会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めた can mean "I quit for my father's sake"
or "I quit because of my father", and only context decides. When it matters, make
the cause reading explicit with a verb:
父(ちち)が倒(たお)れたため会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めた。

## Contrast

### ～ため vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same relationship, different register. `から` is personal and
pushes; `ので` is polite and explains; `ため` is institutional and reports.

</div>

<pre>
雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もっている<b>から</b>、今日(きょう)は車(くるま)をやめよう。
The snow's piled up, so let's not take the car today.
</pre>

<pre>
積雪(せきせつ)の<b>ため</b>、本日(ほんじつ)は路線(ろせん)バスを運休(うんきゅう)といたします。
Owing to snowfall, bus services are suspended today.
</pre>

The second sentence is not more polite than the first in any simple sense - it is
*less personal*. Notice what else changed: the vocabulary went Sino-Japanese
（積雪(せきせつ), 運休(うんきゅう)）and the ending went humble. `ため` travels with
that whole register, and dropping it into casual speech drags the register along
with it.

### ～ため for cause vs ～ために for purpose

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - read the verb in front. Past tense, a negative, an adjective or
a noun means cause. A non-past verb that names something you can decide to do means
purpose.

</div>

<pre>
道(みち)が凍(こお)っていた<b>ため</b>、会社(かいしゃ)まで一時間(いちじかん)かかった。
The roads were frozen, so it took an hour to get to the office.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)を節約(せつやく)する<b>ために</b>、会社(かいしゃ)の近(ちか)くに住(す)んでいる。
I live near the office in order to save time.
</pre>

Adjectives and nouns settle the question on their own: nobody acts *in order to be*
cold, so <code>寒(さむ)いため</code> and <code>大雨(おおあめ)のため</code> can only be
causes. The one shape that stays genuinely ambiguous is
<code>Noun + のために</code> with a person in it, which is why the note above suggests
spelling the cause out with a verb.

### ～ため vs ～せいで・～おかげで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three name a cause. `せいで` adds blame, `おかげで` adds
thanks, `ため` adds nothing. Choosing `ため` is choosing not to have an opinion on
the record.

</div>

<pre>
システムの不具合(ふぐあい)の<b>ため</b>、ご注文(ちゅうもん)の確認(かくにん)が遅(おく)れております。
Owing to a system fault, confirmation of your order has been delayed.
</pre>

<pre>
システムの不具合(ふぐあい)の<b>せいで</b>、注文(ちゅうもん)がまだ確認(かくにん)できない。
Thanks to some system fault I still can't confirm my order.
</pre>

The first is what the shop writes; the second is what the customer says. Note that
`ため` is also the escape hatch when the real cause is somebody's mistake and you do
not want to say so - this is why company apologies so often name a system, a
process or the weather in a `ため` clause.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)だため中止(ちゅうし)になりました。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>の</b>ため中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
  `ため` is a noun; a noun in front of it links with `の`.

- ❌ 不便(ふべん)だため人(ひと)が集(あつ)まらない。 → ✅ 不便(ふべん)<b>な</b>ため人(ひと)が集(あつ)まらない。
  な-adjectives take `な`, exactly as they do before `ので`.

- ❌ お腹(なか)がすいた<b>ため</b>、コンビニに行(い)こう。 → ✅ お腹(なか)がすいた<b>から</b>、コンビニに行(い)こう。
  A personal impulse and a volitional ending are the opposite of what `ため`
  is for. It sounds like a hunger incident report.

- ❌ 寒(さむ)い<b>ため</b>、窓(まど)を閉(し)めてください。 → ✅ 寒(さむ)い<b>ので</b>、窓(まど)を閉(し)めてください。
  A cause `ため` clause is normally followed by a statement. Requests take `ので`.

- ❌ 海外(かいがい)へ行(い)ったため、パスポートを取(と)りました。 → ✅ 海外(かいがい)へ行(い)く<b>ために</b>、パスポートを取(と)りました。
  With the past tense the sentence claims you went abroad first and got the passport
  afterwards. Purpose needs the non-past form.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)接近(せっきん)____、本日(ほんじつ)の営業(えいぎょう)は午後(ごご)三時(さんじ)までとさせていただきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

台風(たいふう)接近(せっきん)<b>のため</b>、本日(ほんじつ)の営業(えいぎょう)は午後(ごご)三時(さんじ)までとさせていただきます。

A shop notice in keigo: impersonal cause, no blame, statement of fact. That is `ため`
territory, and the noun in front needs `の`. `せいで` would sound as though the shop
resented the typhoon personally.

</details>

**2.** You are texting a friend you have kept waiting. Choose:
渋滞(じゅうたい)（のため・のせいで）、待(ま)ち合(あ)わせに遅(おく)れてごめん。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

渋滞(じゅうたい)<b>のせいで</b>、待(ま)ち合(あ)わせに遅(おく)れてごめん。

Both are grammatical, but `ため` belongs to institutional language, and using it in a
text to a friend reads as a parody of a station announcement. Between people,
`せいで` - or simply <code>渋滞(じゅうたい)で</code> - is what you say.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a notice on a lift: "The lift is out of service today for inspection."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

点検(てんけん)の<b>ため</b>、本日(ほんじつ)エレベーターはご利用(りよう)いただけません。

Public notices take `ため` - impersonal, unevaluating, paired with humble verb forms.
Note that <code>点検(てんけん)のため</code> could be read as purpose ("in order to
inspect it"), and on a sign nobody minds: both readings point at the same fact.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は熱(ねつ)があったためで、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は熱(ねつ)があった<b>ため</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みました。

A `ため` clause connects straight into the next clause; there is no `で` after it.
The `で` only appears in <code>のため</code> after a noun - that is the `の` of noun
linking, not a copula. <code>ためです</code> exists, but only at the very end of a
sentence: <code>休(やす)んだのは熱(ねつ)があったためです</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 工事(こうじ)の<b>ため</b>、来週(らいしゅう)は駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)が使(つか)えません。
(b) 工事(こうじ)の<b>せいで</b>、来週(らいしゅう)は駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)が使(つか)えない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both give the same fact and the same cause. (a) is written by whoever is responsible
for the building: it reports the situation, declines to comment on it, and pairs
naturally with the polite ending. It is what the sign in the lobby says.

(b) is a resident complaining to another resident. `せいで` puts fault on the
roadworks, and the plain ending completes the picture of someone grumbling. Putting
(b) on the sign would sound like the management company blaming its own contractors.

</details>

# ～もの - because

**Meaning** · because - the reason you give when you are defending yourself

`もの` is the excuse-maker's "because". It comes in two shapes. At the end of a
sentence, `もの` (almost always shortened to `もん`) answers back: *because, that's
why*. In front of a clause, `ものだから` and `もので` explain away something that went
wrong: *it's just that…*. Both belong to speech, and both put the speaker's feelings
on display in a way `から` and `ので` do not.

## Formation

Always written in kana in this use. Note the split: the sentence-final form takes
`だ` after a noun or な-adjective, while `ものだから` takes `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb, sentence-final | plain form + もの／もん | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないもん |
| い-adjective, sentence-final | plain form + もの／もん | 眠(ねむ)い → 眠(ねむ)いもん |
| な-adj・Noun, sentence-final | ～**だ** + もの／もん | 嫌(いや)だ → 嫌(いや)だもん |
| After ～んだ | ～んだ + もの／もん | 忙(いそが)しかったんだ → 忙(いそが)しかったんだもん |
| Verb・い-adj, excuse clause | plain form + ものだから | 急(いそ)いでいた → 急(いそ)いでいたものだから |
| な-adj・Noun, excuse clause | ～**な** + ものだから | 病気(びょうき)な → 病気(びょうき)なものだから |
| Polite excuse | plain form + もので／ものですから | 慣(な)れていない → 慣(な)れていないもので |
| Casual contractions | もん／もんだから／もんで | 遅(おく)れた → 遅(おく)れたもんだから |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the sentence-final `もの` is very often introduced by `だって` at the head
of the reply. だって…もん is a set frame, and hearing `だって` should make you expect
a `もん` at the other end.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
「どうして行(い)かないの?」「だって、寒(さむ)い<b>もん</b>。」
"Why aren't you going?" "Because it's cold, that's why."
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)じゃ怖(こわ)かった<b>もん</b>。
Well, I was scared on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
だって、誰(だれ)も教(おし)えてくれなかったんだ<b>もん</b>。
Nobody told me, though!
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に呼(よ)ばれた<b>ものだから</b>、何(なに)も持(も)たずに来(き)てしまいました。
They called me in out of nowhere, so I've turned up empty-handed.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)に迷(まよ)った<b>もので</b>、遅(おく)れてしまいました。
I lost my way, I'm afraid, and that's why I'm late.
</pre>

<pre>
あまりに静(しず)かな<b>ものだから</b>、留守(るす)かと思(おも)いました。
It was so quiet in there that I assumed you were out.
</pre>

<pre>
パソコンに慣(な)れていない<b>ものですから</b>、入力(にゅうりょく)にどうしても時間(じかん)がかかります。
I'm not used to computers, you see, so the typing does take me a while.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりに会(あ)った友(とも)だちが昔(むかし)とまったく変(か)わっていなかった<b>ものだから</b>、つい夜中(よなか)まで話(はな)し込(こ)んでしまいました。
My friend hadn't changed a bit since the old days, and one thing led to another until we'd talked half the night away.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Sentence-final `もん`.** This is spoken Japanese only. It never appears in an
essay, a report or an email, and it cannot be aimed upward at a teacher, a client or
a boss - even in ます-form, ❌忙(いそが)しかったんですもん to a manager will land
badly. What it does is *push back*. It is the reply of somebody who has been
criticised, questioned or told they were wrong, and it insists that their reason was
good enough. That defensive edge is why it so easily sounds petulant.

Register tendencies, and they are only tendencies: drawn out with a soft, coaxing
delivery - ～だも～ん - it is associated with small children and with a cute,
wheedling style that leans young and feminine. Said flatly and quickly it is simply
blunt casual speech and is used by everyone; the clipped 知(し)らないもん of an
irritated adult has nothing coaxing about it. What is constant is the defensiveness,
not the gender.

**`ものだから` and `もので`.** These make excuses, and they do it by framing the
reason as something that happened *to* the speaker. That is the whole point: the
speaker is not owning the outcome, they are showing you the circumstance that
overtook them. `ものだから` is the more emphatic and more spoken; `もので` and its
fuller `ものですから` are softer and combine happily with keigo, which makes
<code>不慣(ふな)れなもので</code> and
<code>急(きゅう)いでおりましたもので</code> stock apologies in service and office
Japanese.

Two cautions. Used too often, they mark you as somebody who always has an excuse - 
Japanese speakers notice this exactly as English speakers notice a run of "it's just
that…". And the excuse works only when the situation really was outside your
control; 面倒(めんどう)だったものだから ("because I couldn't be bothered") is
grammatical and makes things worse.

The casual `もんだから` / `もんで` are extremely common in ordinary speech and should
be recognised on sight, but `もんで` in particular is quite blunt and is better kept
for people you are relaxed with.

## Contrast

### sentence-final ～もん vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` states a reason. `もん` *defends* one. If nothing is being
challenged, `もん` has nothing to do.

</div>

<pre>
「なんで残(のこ)したの?」「苦(にが)い<b>もん</b>。」
"Why did you leave it?" "Because it's bitter."
</pre>

<pre>
苦(にが)い<b>から</b>、砂糖(さとう)を入(い)れて飲(の)んでいます。
It's bitter, so I drink it with sugar.
</pre>

The first is an answer given under mild attack; the speaker is justifying
themselves, and a small note of "leave me alone about it" comes with the `もん`. The
second is a flat piece of information and could not take `もん` at all - there is
nobody to argue with. The same test explains why `もん` almost always ends the
utterance: a defence is the last word, not the first half of a sentence.

### ～ものだから・～もので vs ～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ので` gives a reason. `ものだから` gives an excuse, and adds
"…so it wasn't really my doing".

</div>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まった<b>ので</b>、会議(かいぎ)を三十分(さんじゅっぷん)遅(おく)らせます。
The trains have stopped, so we'll push the meeting back half an hour.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まってしまった<b>ものですから</b>、会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。
The trains went and stopped, I'm afraid, so I didn't make the meeting.
</pre>

`ので` is compatible with a decision, an instruction or a plan; `ものだから` is not.
What follows an excuse clause is almost always a **past-tense account of what went
wrong**, often with `てしまった` reinforcing it. If your second clause is a request or
a proposal, you need `ので`.

### sentence-final ～もの vs ～ものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same three syllables, unrelated meanings. Reason `もの` follows a
plain clause and ends the utterance. `ものだ` is a predicate in its own right, and
states a general truth, a piece of advice, or a fond memory.

</div>

<pre>
昔(むかし)はよくこの川(かわ)で泳(およ)いだ<b>ものだ</b>。
We used to swim in this river all the time.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)はこの川(かわ)で泳(およ)げた<b>もん</b>。
We *could* swim in this river back then, you know.
</pre>

The first is reminiscence, and it is at home in writing. The second is an argument - 
somebody has just said the river was always filthy. Position tells them apart in
practice: `ものだ`／`ものです` carries a copula and can be followed by more sentence;
reason `もの` is bare, sits at the very end, and is nearly always `もん`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、まだ終(お)わっていないもん。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、確認(かくにん)に時間(じかん)がかかっているものですから、もう少(すこ)しお時間(じかん)をいただけますか。
  Sentence-final `もん` cannot be aimed at a superior. The polite excuse form
  <code>ものですから</code> can.

- ❌ だって、静(しず)かなもん。 → ✅ だって、静(しず)かだもん。
  Sentence-final `もの` takes `だ` after a な-adjective. The `な` form belongs to
  <code>静(しず)かなものだから</code>, which is the other pattern.

- ❌ 病気(びょうき)のものですから、欠席(けっせき)させていただきました。 → ✅ 病気(びょうき)<b>な</b>ものですから、欠席(けっせき)させていただきました。
  A noun links to `ものだから`／`もので` with `な`, not `の`. Compare `ため` and `せい`,
  which take `の` - the three are not interchangeable on this point.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れそうな<b>ものだから</b>、先(さき)に始(はじ)めてください。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れそうな<b>ので</b>、先(さき)に始(はじ)めてください。
  You cannot follow an excuse clause with a request. `ものだから` explains something
  that has already gone wrong.

- ❌ この地域(ちいき)は雨(あめ)が多(おお)いもんだから、米作(こめづく)りが盛(さか)んである。 → ✅ …雨(あめ)が多(おお)い<b>ため</b>、米作(こめづく)りが盛(さか)んである。
  `もんだから` is personal, spoken and defensive. A written statement of geographical
  fact wants the neutral `ため`.

## Practice

**1.** 「どうして食(た)べないの?」「だって、ピーマンは嫌(きら)い____。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

だって、ピーマンは嫌(きら)い<b>だもん</b>。

The speaker is being challenged and pushing back, which is the sentence-final `もん`.
<code>嫌(きら)い</code> is a な-adjective, so it takes `だ` in front - not `な`, which
would belong to the <code>ものだから</code> pattern instead.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 上司(じょうし)に説明(せつめい)するとき - 
資料(しりょう)が届(とど)かなかった（もんだから・ものですから）、作業(さぎょう)が止(と)まってしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

資料(しりょう)が届(とど)かなかった<b>ものですから</b>、作業(さぎょう)が止(と)まってしまいました。

Both are the same pattern, but `もんだから` is the blunt casual contraction and
sounds too familiar for a superior. The full <code>ものですから</code> keeps the
apologetic framing while matching the register of the ます-ending.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, apologising to a shopkeeper: "I'm sorry - I'm new here and I didn't know the rules."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません、越(こ)してきたばかりで、決(き)まりを知(し)らなかった<b>もので</b>。

<code>もので</code> trailing off at the end is a very natural way to close an apology:
the excuse is offered, and the conclusion - "so please forgive me" - is left unsaid.
<code>知(し)らなかったものですから</code> is the fuller, slightly more formal version.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)は試験(しけん)があるもんだから、今夜(こんや)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)らせていただけませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)は試験(しけん)があります<b>ので</b>、今夜(こんや)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)らせていただけませんか。

Two problems, both about `もんだから`. It cannot be followed by a request, and its
casual register clashes with the keigo ending. `ので` handles both; in this very
formal frame the ます-form in front of it is natural too.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた<b>ので</b>、傘(かさ)を借(か)りました。
(b) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた<b>ものだから</b>、勝手(かって)に傘(かさ)を借(か)りてしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain report. Rain, umbrella, no comment: `ので` says only that one led to
the other.

(b) is a confession. `ものだから` frames the rain as a circumstance that overtook the
speaker, and the sentence goes on to admit something they should not have done - 
<code>勝手(かって)に</code> and <code>てしまいました</code> both point the same way. Read
aloud, (b) is somebody apologising for borrowing the umbrella; (a) is somebody
explaining why they have one.

</details>

# ～ばかりに

**Meaning** · just because ～; simply on account of ～ - one small thing went wrong, and the price was out of all proportion

`ばかりに` is regret with a grammar attached. It names a cause that looks trivial or
avoidable in hindsight, then reports a consequence far larger than the cause
deserved. The English that fits it is not "because" but "**just** because" or "if
only I hadn't", and the speaker is normally the one paying.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (past) | た-form + ばかりに | 信(しん)じた → 信(しん)じたばかりに |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ばかりに | 確(たし)かめなかった → 確(たし)かめなかったばかりに |
| Verb (non-past) | plain form + ばかりに | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないばかりに |
| い-adjective | plain form + ばかりに | 気(き)が弱(よわ)い → 気(き)が弱(よわ)いばかりに |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + ばかりに | 正直(しょうじき)だ → 正直(しょうじき)なばかりに |
| Noun | Noun + **である** + ばかりに | 新人(しんじん)だ → 新人(しんじん)であるばかりに |
| Sheer desire to ～ | ～たい + ばかりに | 勝(か)ちたい → 勝(か)ちたいばかりに |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause after `ばかりに` has to be a **bad** outcome, and one
that has already happened. `てしまった`, `ことになった` and plain negatives cluster
there for that reason. A good result, or a plan for the future, cannot follow it.

</div>

The `～たいばかりに` row is a separate sense worth learning on its own: there the
cause is the *wanting*, and what follows is usually something excessive or unwise
that the speaker did in pursuit of it.

## Examples

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)たなかった<b>ばかりに</b>、ずぶぬれになった。
Just because I didn't take an umbrella, I got soaked through.
</pre>

<pre>
一言(ひとこと)嘘(うそ)をついた<b>ばかりに</b>、信用(しんよう)をなくしてしまった。
One lie, and I lost their trust for good.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)い方(ほう)を選(えら)んだ<b>ばかりに</b>、何度(なんど)も修理(しゅうり)に出(だ)すことになった。
Just because I went for the cheaper one, I've had to send it in for repairs again and again.
</pre>

<pre>
気(き)が弱(よわ)い<b>ばかりに</b>、断(ことわ)れずに全部(ぜんぶ)引(ひ)き受(う)けてしまった。
Simply because I'm a soft touch, I couldn't say no and ended up taking on the lot.
</pre>

<pre>
保証人(ほしょうにん)になった<b>ばかりに</b>、大(おお)きな借金(しゃっきん)を背負(せお)うことになった。
He stood as guarantor, and for that alone he was saddled with an enormous debt.
</pre>

<pre>
一位(いちい)を取(と)りたい<b>ばかりに</b>、体(からだ)を壊(こわ)すまで走(はし)り続(つづ)けてしまった。
Out of sheer determination to come first, she ran herself into the ground.
</pre>

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)が読(よ)めなかった<b>ばかりに</b>、大事(だいじ)な知(し)らせを見(み)のがしていた。
Only because I couldn't read the Japanese, I'd been missing an important notice all along.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい職場(しょくば)で早(はや)く認(みと)められたいと思(おも)った<b>ばかりに</b>、一人(ひとり)で抱(かか)え込(こ)み、結局(けっきょく)周(まわ)りに迷惑(めいわく)をかけてしまいました。
Wanting so badly to prove myself at the new job, I kept everything to myself, and in the end I made trouble for everyone around me.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ばかりに` is a shade more literary than the rest of this family. It is at home in
narrative - a novel, a personal essay, the part of a conversation where somebody is
telling a story with feeling - and it sounds heavy in a passing remark. If a friend
asks why you are wet, you say
傘(かさ)忘(わす)れたから; you would only reach for `ばかりに` if you were
recounting the disaster afterwards.

The emotion is **regret**, not blame. That is what separates it from `せいで`.
`せいで` points outward and can be aimed at anyone; `ばかりに` looks back at a hinge
point and wishes it had gone the other way, which is why the cause is so often
something the speaker themselves did, was, or failed to do. When it *is* aimed at
someone else, it still keeps the "if only" shape:
彼(かれ)の一言(ひとこと)を真(ま)に受(う)けたばかりに - the speaker is still the
one who acted.

Two smaller points. The cause is presented as **small** - a single word, one
decision, one missing precaution - and the disproportion is the point of the
sentence; a cause that is already enormous does not need `ばかりに`. And because
`ばかりに` looks back on a settled outcome, it does not combine with plans,
suggestions or anything in the future.

Bare `ため`, `せいで` and `から` all remain the ordinary choices for everyday causes;
`ばかりに` is what you use when the sentence is really about the regret.

## Contrast

### ～ばかりに vs ～せいで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both give a bad outcome. `せいで` says *whose fault* it was;
`ばかりに` says *how small* the cause was and how much it cost.

</div>

<pre>
係(かかり)の人(ひと)の説明(せつめい)が分(わ)かりにくかった<b>せいで</b>、列(れつ)に並(なら)び直(なお)すはめになった。
Because the attendant's explanation was so unclear, I had to go and queue all over again.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)を最後(さいご)まで聞(き)かなかった<b>ばかりに</b>、列(れつ)に並(なら)び直(なお)すはめになった。
Just because I didn't listen to the end of the explanation, I had to go and queue all over again.
</pre>

Same misfortune, different posture. The first sentence blames the staff; the second
is the speaker kicking themselves. Note also that `せいで` attaches to a noun with
`の` - <code>説明(せつめい)のせいで</code> - while `ばかりに` needs a clause, or
`である` if you insist on a noun.

### ～ばかりに vs ～だけに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけ` and `ばかり` may both mean "only", but these two
compounds do not overlap at all. `ばかりに` = "just because, unfortunately".
`だけに` = "precisely because, as you would expect".

</div>

<pre>
期待(きたい)していた<b>だけに</b>、負(ま)けたときのショックが大(おお)きかった。
Precisely because I'd had high hopes, the defeat hit me hard.
</pre>

<pre>
期待(きたい)しすぎた<b>ばかりに</b>、相手(あいて)にきつい言(い)い方(かた)をしてしまった。
Just because I expected too much, I ended up speaking harshly to her.
</pre>

`だけに` says the result follows *naturally and fittingly* from the cause, and it
works with good outcomes as happily as bad ones:
経験(けいけん)が長(なが)いだけに、説明(せつめい)が分(わ)かりやすい。 `ばかりに`
can never do that. If "no wonder" fits the English, you want `だけに`.

### ～ばかりに vs ～たばかり and ～ばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one `に` changes everything. `～たばかり` = "only just did it";
`～ばかり` = "nothing but"; `～たばかりに` = "just because I did it, and I regret
it".

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)してきた<b>ばかり</b>で、この辺(へん)のことはまだよく知(し)りません。
I've only just moved here, so I don't know the area yet.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)してきた<b>ばかりに</b>、町内会(ちょうないかい)の仕事(しごと)を全部(ぜんぶ)任(まか)された。
Just because I was the new arrival, I got landed with every job in the neighbourhood association.
</pre>

Two sentences identical but for one syllable. The first is about **time** - the move
was recent. The second is about **cause and regret** - being the newcomer is why the
work landed on him. When you meet `ばかり` after a た-form, check for the `に` before
you decide which one you are reading.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 宝(たから)くじを買(か)った<b>ばかりに</b>、百万円(ひゃくまんえん)当(あ)たりました。 → ✅ 宝(たから)くじを買(か)った<b>おかげで</b>、百万円(ひゃくまんえん)当(あ)たりました。
  Winning is a good outcome, and `ばかりに` has regret built into it. It cannot be
  used for luck that went your way.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)のばかりに、遅(おく)れました。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)にあった<b>ばかりに</b>… ／ 事故(じこ)の<b>せいで</b>遅(おく)れました。
  `ばかりに` does not take a bare noun with `の`. Give it a clause, or use `せいで`.

- ❌ 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べた<b>ばかりに</b>、お腹(なか)がいっぱいです。 → ✅ 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べた<b>ばかりで</b>、お腹(なか)がいっぱいです。
  This is the "only just did it" `ばかり`. Adding `に` turns lunch into a regrettable
  mistake.

- ❌ 急(いそ)いでいる<b>ばかりに</b>、明日(あした)は早(はや)く出(で)ます。 → ✅ 急(いそ)いでいる<b>ので</b>、明日(あした)は早(はや)く出(で)ます。
  `ばかりに` reports a consequence that has already landed. A plan for tomorrow
  cannot follow it.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)が来(き)た<b>ばかりに</b>、飛行機(ひこうき)が飛(と)ばなかった。 → ✅ 台風(たいふう)の<b>ため</b>、飛行機(ひこうき)が飛(と)ばなかった。
  A typhoon is not a small avoidable slip that anybody could have chosen
  differently, so there is nothing for the "if only" to attach to. Use the neutral
  `ため`, or `せいで` if you want to grumble.

## Practice

**1.** 少(すこ)し欲(よく)を出(だ)した____、今(いま)まで貯(た)めたお金(かね)をほとんど失(うしな)ってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

少(すこ)し欲(よく)を出(だ)した<b>ばかりに</b>、今(いま)まで貯(た)めたお金(かね)をほとんど失(うしな)ってしまった。

A small lapse, an enormous loss, and the speaker looking back at it - the exact
shape `ばかりに` exists for. <code>少(すこ)し</code> and <code>てしまった</code> are both
typical company for it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 人気(にんき)の店(みせ)（ばかりに・だけに）、昼(ひる)は一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)たされた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人気(にんき)の店(みせ)<b>だけに</b>、昼(ひる)は一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)たされた。

"It's a popular place, so of course there was a wait" - the result follows
naturally from the cause, which is `だけに`. `ばかりに` would have to mean the
popularity was a trivial, regrettable accident, which makes no sense here. Note the
attachment too: `だけに` takes the bare noun, whereas `ばかりに` would need `である`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Just because I didn't check the date, I missed the deadline."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

日付(ひづけ)を確(たし)かめなかった<b>ばかりに</b>、締(し)め切(き)りに間(ま)に合(あ)わなくなってしまいました。

The negative た-form goes straight in front of `ばかりに`, and the result clause
reports damage already done. <code>確(たし)かめなかったせいで</code> is also possible,
but it blames the oversight rather than regretting it, and loses the "if only I
had" colour of the English.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 正直(しょうじき)だばかりに、いつも損(そん)な役(やく)を引(ひ)き受(う)けている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 正直(しょうじき)<b>な</b>ばかりに、いつも損(そん)な役(やく)を引(ひ)き受(う)けている。

Plain `だ` cannot stand in front of `ばかりに`. A な-adjective takes its attributive
`な`; a noun would take `である`, as in
<code>新人(しんじん)であるばかりに</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)かった<b>せいで</b>、その部屋(へや)は別(べつ)の人(ひと)に決(き)まってしまった。
(b) 返事(へんじ)を一日(いちにち)延(の)ばした<b>ばかりに</b>、その部屋(へや)は別(べつ)の人(ひと)に決(き)まってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both report the same loss. (a) identifies the cause and holds it responsible; with
no subject stated it could as easily be blaming the agent's slow reply as the
speaker's own.

(b) is unmistakably self-directed regret. <code>一日(いちにち)</code> insists on how
small the cause was, and `ばかりに` sets that smallness against the size of what was
lost - the whole sentence means "one day, and the flat was gone." (a) explains; (b)
laments.

</details>

# ～ことから

**Meaning** · from the fact that ～ - the evidence you reasoned from, not the cause of what happened

`ことから` introduces **grounds**, not a cause. What comes before it is something
observed or established; what comes after is the conclusion somebody drew from it.
The two clauses are linked by an act of reasoning, which is why the second half so
often ends in 考(かんが)えられる, 分(わ)かる, 判断(はんだん)した or
呼(よ)ばれている rather than in a plain event.

## Formation

`こと` nominalises the clause in front of it, and `から` here is the `から` of source
 - "out of", "starting from".

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | plain form + ことから | 似(に)ている → 似(に)ていることから |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ことから | 続(つづ)いた → 続(つづ)いたことから |
| い-adjective | plain form + ことから | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いことから |
| な-adjective | ～**な**／**である** + ことから | 丁寧(ていねい)だ → 丁寧(ていねい)なことから |
| Noun | Noun + **である** + ことから | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)であることから |
| Referring back | このことから、～ | このことから、～と言(い)える |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - what follows `ことから` must be a **conclusion**: an inference, a
judgement, a name, or a statement of what is generally said. It cannot be a
decision, a request, an invitation or a plain report of an event.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)が片付(かたづ)いていた<b>ことから</b>、彼(かれ)はもう帰(かえ)ったと分(わ)かった。
The tidy desk told me he had already gone home.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)が開(あ)いていた<b>ことから</b>、犯人(はんにん)はここから入(はい)ったと考(かんが)えられる。
From the fact that the window was open, it is thought the intruder came in this way.
</pre>

<pre>
葉(は)の形(かたち)が手(て)のひらに似(に)ている<b>ことから</b>、この木(き)はそう名付(なづ)けられた。
The tree got its name from the fact that its leaves look like the palm of a hand.
</pre>

<pre>
気温(きおん)が例年(れいねん)より高(たか)い<b>ことから</b>、開花(かいか)は早(はや)まる見込(みこ)みです。
Given that temperatures are above the seasonal average, the blossom is expected early.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)が丁寧(ていねい)である<b>ことから</b>、この本(ほん)は初心者(しょしんしゃ)に向(む)いていると言(い)える。
Its careful explanations make this a book one can call well suited to beginners.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ間違(まちが)いが三度(さんど)続(つづ)いた<b>ことから</b>、手順(てじゅん)そのものに問題(もんだい)があると判断(はんだん)しました。
Since the same error occurred three times running, we concluded that the procedure itself was at fault.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>このことから</b>、事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)は整備(せいび)不足(ぶそく)であったと結論(けつろん)づけられた。
From this it was concluded that the accident had been caused by inadequate maintenance.
</pre>

<pre>
回答(かいとう)の多(おお)くが三十代(さんじゅうだい)以下(いか)に集中(しゅうちゅう)している<b>ことから</b>、この調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)をそのまま全体(ぜんたい)にあてはめることはできないと考(かんが)えられます。
Because the responses are concentrated among people in their thirties and below, the findings cannot simply be generalised to the population as a whole.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ことから` is written Japanese. It belongs to reports, analyses, news copy, museum
captions, academic prose and the more careful kind of explanation - the registers in
which somebody shows their working. In casual speech it sounds like a person reading
aloud from a document; the spoken equivalent is simply `から` plus a verb of
thinking: 机(つくえ)が片付(かたづ)いてたから、もう帰(かえ)ったんだと思(おも)った。

Its second home is **naming**. A very large number of Japanese explanations of how
something got its name are built on this pattern - the shape of a mountain, the
colour of a fish, a line in an old poem - and they nearly always close with
呼(よ)ばれるようになった or 名前(なまえ)がついた. If you read guidebooks or
由来(ゆらい) sections, you will meet it constantly.

The thing to keep straight is that `ことから` **does not claim causation**. In
電気(でんき)がついていることから、在宅(ざいたく)だと分(わ)かる, the light did not
cause anybody to be at home; it is merely how the speaker knows. Feeding it a
cause-and-effect pair produces a sentence that sounds oddly indirect - as though you
had inferred your own headache from evidence.

There is no politeness dimension to worry about: the pattern itself does not change
shape, and the register lives in the vocabulary and the sentence ending around it.

## Contrast

### ～ことから vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` and `ので` join a cause to its result. `ことから` joins
evidence to a conclusion. Ask whether the first clause *made* the second happen, or
merely *revealed* it.

</div>

<pre>
部屋(へや)の電気(でんき)がついていた<b>ので</b>、窓(まど)から声(こえ)をかけた。
The light was on in the room, so I called in at the window.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)の電気(でんき)がついている<b>ことから</b>、彼(かれ)はもう戻(もど)っていると判断(はんだん)した。
From the fact that the light was on, I judged that he was already back.
</pre>

In the first, the light is why the speaker acted. In the second, the light is the
speaker's evidence for a belief - and the belief was true before anybody looked. The
`ので` version can be followed by anything at all; the `ことから` version can only be
followed by an inference.

### ～ことから vs ～ことだから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one syllable apart, and neither is a variant of the other.
`ことから` = "from the fact that…". <code>Noun + のことだから</code> = "knowing what X
is like, naturally…".

</div>

<pre>
机(つくえ)に荷物(にもつ)が置(お)いてある<b>ことから</b>、彼女(かのじょ)はもう着(つ)いていると分(わ)かる。
The bag on the desk tells me she has already arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)の<b>ことだから</b>、約束(やくそく)の十分(じゅっぷん)前(まえ)には着(つ)いているだろう。
Knowing her, she'll have got there ten minutes early.
</pre>

`ことだから` takes a **person** (or occasionally an organisation) with `の` in front
of it, and its conclusion rests on that person's known character rather than on
anything observed today. `ことから` takes a whole clause and rests on the observation
itself. If you can insert "typical of them" into the English, it is `ことだから`.

### ～ことから vs ～ものだから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same length, opposite worlds. `ことから` is written reasoning
from evidence; `ものだから` is spoken excuse-making. Neither can be swapped for the
other in any sentence.

</div>

<pre>
地面(じめん)がぬれている<b>ことから</b>、夜中(よなか)に雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったことが分(わ)かる。
The wet ground shows that it rained during the night.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた<b>ものだから</b>、途中(とちゅう)で引(ひ)き返(かえ)してきました。
It suddenly started raining, you see, so I turned back halfway.
</pre>

The first sentence is cool and impersonal, and its conclusion is a fact about the
world. The second is warm and defensive, and its conclusion is about what the
speaker did and would rather not be criticised for. Learners who have met both
patterns in the same week tend to blur them; the register test settles it instantly.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い<b>ことから</b>、今日(きょう)は休(やす)みます。 → ✅ 頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い<b>ので</b>、今日(きょう)は休(やす)みます。
  A personal reason followed by a decision. `ことから` needs observed evidence and an
  inference, not a headache and a plan.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>ことから</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)ってください。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>ので</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)ってください。
  Requests cannot follow `ことから` at all.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)のことから、割引(わりびき)が受(う)けられる。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>である</b>ことから、割引(わりびき)が受(う)けられる。
  A noun links with `である`, not `の`. (With `の` you have accidentally written the
  opening of <code>のことだから</code>.)

- ❌ 静(しず)かだことから → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>な</b>ことから ／ 静(しず)か<b>である</b>ことから
  Plain `だ` cannot stand in front of `こと`; use the attributive `な`, or `である` in
  formal writing.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんの<b>ことから</b>、きっと時間(じかん)どおりに来(き)ますよ。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんの<b>ことだから</b>、きっと時間(じかん)どおりに来(き)ますよ。
  Predicting from somebody's character is the `ことだから` pattern. `ことから` would
  need an observation, not a person.

## Practice

**1.** 足跡(あしあと)が一人分(ひとりぶん)しかなかった____、犯人(はんにん)は単独(たんどく)であったと見(み)られている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

足跡(あしあと)が一人分(ひとりぶん)しかなかった<b>ことから</b>、犯人(はんにん)は単独(たんどく)であったと見(み)られている。

Physical evidence in the first clause, an inference marked by
<code>見(み)られている</code> in the second - the textbook shape for `ことから`.
`ので` would suggest the footprints somehow caused the criminal to work alone.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部長(ぶちょう)の（ことから・ことだから）、今回(こんかい)も細(こま)かいところまで確認(かくにん)してくるはずだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)の<b>ことだから</b>、今回(こんかい)も細(こま)かいところまで確認(かくにん)してくるはずだ。

The prediction rests on what the manager is *like*, and the phrase in front is a
person plus `の`. That combination is always `ことだから`. `ことから` needs a clause
of observed fact.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The fact that the shop is always crowded shows that the food is good."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いつも混(こ)んでいる<b>ことから</b>、あの店(みせ)の料理(りょうり)はおいしいと言(い)える。

The crowd is evidence, not a cause, so `ことから` is right and the conclusion is
marked with <code>と言(い)える</code>. Ending with a bare
<code>おいしいです</code> would be odd - a `ことから` clause wants a verb of judgement
after it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この港(みなと)は波(なみ)が静(しず)かだことから、昔(むかし)から船(ふね)が集(あつ)まった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この港(みなと)は波(なみ)が静(しず)か<b>な</b>ことから、昔(むかし)から船(ふね)が集(あつ)まった。

<code>静(しず)か</code> is a な-adjective and `こと` is a noun, so they join with `な`
 - or with `である` if the surrounding prose is formal. Plain `だ` never appears in
that slot.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)が割(わ)れていた<b>ので</b>、警察(けいさつ)に連絡(れんらく)しました。
(b) 窓(まど)が割(わ)れていた<b>ことから</b>、泥棒(どろぼう)が入(はい)ったと考(かんが)えられます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a chain of events: the window was broken, and because of that the speaker
picked up the phone. The second clause is an action.

(b) is a chain of reasoning: the broken window is the evidence, and the burglary is
what the speaker deduces from it. The second clause is a belief, and it is marked as
one by <code>考(かんが)えられます</code>. Put (a)'s action after `ことから` and the
sentence stops making sense; put (b)'s inference after `ので` and you get a looser,
more conversational version of the same thought.

</details>

# その結果

**Meaning** · as a result - what came out of the process just described

<code>その結果(けっか)</code> is a connector between sentences. The sentence before it
describes something that ran its course - an effort, an investigation, a
negotiation, a habit kept up for years - and <code>その結果(けっか)</code> announces
what that produced. It is neutral about whether the outcome was welcome; it reports
outcomes, it does not evaluate them.

## Formation

<code>その結果(けっか)</code> is a fixed phrase, not a suffix, so nothing conjugates
in front of it. What the table below lists is the **position** it can occupy.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A completed sentence | ～。その結果(けっか)、～。 | 話(はな)し合(あ)った。その結果(けっか)、方針(ほうしん)が決(き)まった。 |
| A clause, mid-sentence | ～し／～て、その結果(けっか)、～ | 値上(ねあ)げをして、その結果(けっか)、客(きゃく)が減(へ)った |
| Formal written variant | その結果(けっか)として | その結果(けっか)として見直(みなお)しが必要(ひつよう)になった |
| Bare connective (speech) | 結果(けっか)、～ | 結果(けっか)、何(なに)も変(か)わらなかった |
| As an ordinary noun | ～のは、その結果(けっか)だ | 今(いま)の人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)はその結果(けっか)です |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the comma after <code>その結果(けっか)</code> is standard and worth
writing. There is no ❌<code>その結果(けっか)で</code> and no
❌<code>その結果(けっか)に</code> in this connective use; the phrase stands on its own.
Bare <code>結果(けっか)、</code> without `その` is common in speech and journalism and
sounds a little more clipped and modern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)三十分(さんじゅっぷん)歩(ある)いた。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、体重(たいじゅう)が五(ご)キロ減(へ)った。
I walked for half an hour every day. As a result, I lost five kilos.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)で話(はな)し合(あ)いました。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)を一日(いちにち)遅(おく)らせることにしました。
We all talked it over, and as a result we've decided to put departure back a day.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)かけて改良(かいりょう)を重(かさ)ねた。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、故障(こしょう)が半分(はんぶん)に減(へ)った。
Three years of successive improvements followed, and as a result faults were halved.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)を下(さ)げすぎた。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、売(う)れば売(う)るほど赤字(あかじ)が増(ふ)えた。
They cut the price too far, and the upshot was that the more they sold, the deeper the losses got.
</pre>

<pre>
三社(さんしゃ)に見積(みつ)もりを依頼(いらい)しました。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、価格(かかく)に大(おお)きな差(さ)があることが分(わ)かりました。
We asked three firms to quote, and it emerged that the prices differ widely.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も徹夜(てつや)をした。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、体(からだ)を壊(こわ)してしまった。
He pulled all-nighter after all-nighter, and in consequence his health gave out.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい駅(えき)ができ、<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、周辺(しゅうへん)の地価(ちか)が大(おお)きく上(あ)がった。
A new station was built, and as a result land prices in the area rose sharply.
</pre>

<pre>
市(し)は五年(ごねん)にわたって住民(じゅうみん)の意見(いけん)を聞(き)き取(と)り、計画(けいかく)を何度(なんど)も作(つく)り直(なお)した。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、当初(とうしょ)の案(あん)とはまったく違(ちが)う形(かたち)で工事(こうじ)が始(はじ)まることになった。
The city spent five years gathering residents' views and redrafting the plan again and again, with the result that the work has begun in a form quite unlike the original proposal.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written and formal-spoken** connector: reports, minutes, news, a
presentation, the explanatory half of an essay. In relaxed conversation it sounds
like somebody quoting a document, and the natural spoken equivalents are `それで`
and `で`. That said, it is not stiff in the way keigo is stiff - a colleague
summarising what a meeting decided will use it happily.

Two things govern whether it fits. First, the sentence in front has to describe a
**process**, not a single flat fact. Years of effort, repeated attempts,
negotiations, a policy left running: something with duration or intention behind it.
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った。その結果(けっか)、… is odd for exactly this reason - rain
is an event, not a process anybody conducted.

Second, it is **evaluatively neutral**. Unlike `おかげで` and `せいで`, it takes no
view: the outcome that follows can be a triumph, a disaster or a set of numbers.
That neutrality is what makes it the natural connector for a report on something
that went badly, where naming a culprit would be out of place.

One habit worth copying from native writing: <code>その結果(けっか)</code> very often
introduces a clause ending in <code>～ことになった</code>,
<code>～ようになった</code> or <code>～という結果(けっか)になった</code>. The pattern likes
outcomes stated as settled states of affairs rather than as momentary actions.

## Contrast

### その結果(けっか) vs そのため

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そのため` treats the previous sentence as a **cause** and can be
followed by anything, including a plan or an instruction.
<code>その結果(けっか)</code> treats it as a **process** and can only be followed by
its outcome.

</div>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいています。<b>そのため</b>、明日(あした)の便(びん)は欠航(けっこう)となります。
A typhoon is approaching. Accordingly, tomorrow's flights are cancelled.
</pre>

<pre>
欠航(けっこう)が三日(みっか)続(つづ)きました。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、空港(くうこう)に一万人(いちまんにん)近(ちか)い客(きゃく)が足止(あしど)めされました。
Flights were cancelled for three days, and as a result nearly ten thousand passengers were stranded at the airport.
</pre>

Swap them and you feel the difference immediately. ❌An approaching typhoon is not a
process whose *result* is a cancellation - the cancellation is a decision taken
because of it. Three days of cancellations, on the other hand, is a situation that
ran on and produced something.

`そのため` is also the one you can put in front of a measure to be taken:
そのため、窓口(まどぐち)を増(ふ)やすことにいたしました。

### その結果(けっか) vs それで・だから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `それで` and `だから` are conversation; <code>その結果(けっか)</code>
is report. `だから` also carries the speaker's own reasoning in a way the other two
do not.

</div>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れちゃって。<b>それで</b>、一度(いちど)家(いえ)に戻(もど)ったんだ。
I forgot my wallet - so I went back home for it.
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)を三年(さんねん)続(つづ)けた。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、同(おな)じ傾向(けいこう)が各地(かくち)で確認(かくにん)された。
The survey ran for three years, and as a result the same trend was confirmed across the country.
</pre>

Reading the first sentence with <code>その結果(けっか)</code> in it is comic: it
promotes forgetting your wallet to the status of a research programme. Conversely,
`だから` in a report reads as though the writer were arguing with the reader rather
than presenting findings.

### その結果(けっか) vs 結局(けっきょく)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>その結果(けっか)</code> says "and this is what it produced".
<code>結局(けっきょく)</code> says "and in the end, after all that". The second carries
a shrug: the effort was long, and often it did not get you where you hoped.

</div>

<pre>
いろいろ試(ため)してみたが、<b>結局(けっきょく)</b>元(もと)のやり方(かた)に戻(もど)した。
We tried all sorts of things, but in the end we went back to the old way of doing it.
</pre>

<pre>
手順(てじゅん)を見直(みなお)した。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、作業(さぎょう)時間(じかん)が三割(さんわり)短(みじか)くなった。
We reviewed the procedure, and as a result the job now takes thirty per cent less time.
</pre>

<code>結局(けっきょく)</code> is also an adverb rather than a connector, so it sits
inside its clause and needs no comma after it, and it is perfectly at home in casual
speech. If your English is "in the end" or "it turned out that after all", it is
<code>結局(けっきょく)</code>; if it is "as a result", it is
<code>その結果(けっか)</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った。その結果(けっか)、試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)の<b>ため</b>、試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  A shower is an event, not a process with an outcome. Use a cause word.

- ❌ その結果(けっか)で、方針(ほうしん)が変(か)わりました。 → ✅ その結果(けっか)、方針(ほうしん)が変(か)わりました。
  As a connector the phrase takes no particle after it - just a comma.

- ❌ その結果(けっか)、明日(あした)九時(くじ)に集(あつ)まってください。 → ✅ <b>そのため</b>、明日(あした)九時(くじ)に集(あつ)まってください。
  An instruction is not an outcome. `そのため` accepts one; this phrase does not.

- ❌ お腹(なか)がすいた。その結果(けっか)、コンビニでパンを買(か)った。 → ✅ お腹(なか)がすいた。<b>それで</b>、コンビニでパンを買(か)った。
  Both the register and the missing process are wrong. This is `それで` territory.

# ～た結果・～の結果

**Meaning** · as a result of ～ - the outcome of something that was deliberately worked through

<code>～た結果(けっか)</code> packs a process and its outcome into a single sentence.
The clause in front names something that was carried out - a discussion, an
investigation, repeated attempts - and what follows is what came of it. With a noun,
<code>～の結果(けっか)</code> does the same job in fewer syllables. Both are the
language of reports and decisions.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (past) | た-form + 結果(けっか) | 話(はな)し合(あ)った → 話(はな)し合(あ)った結果(けっか) |
| Noun of activity | Noun + の + 結果(けっか) | 検査(けんさ) → 検査(けんさ)の結果(けっか) |
| Very formal writing | ます-form (past) + 結果(けっか) | 検討(けんとう)いたしました結果(けっか) |
| Sentence-final | ～のは～した結果(けっか)だ | 残(のこ)ったのは努力(どりょく)した結果(けっか)です |
| Ordinary noun (not this pattern) | 結果(けっか) + を／が／は | 結果(けっか)が出(で)るまで待(ま)つ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb must be **past tense** and must name something somebody
*did*: 調(しら)べる, 相談(そうだん)する, 議論(ぎろん)する, 続(つづ)ける,
繰(く)り返(かえ)す. A momentary accident that simply happened to you does not
take this pattern.

</div>

The nouns that work with <code>の結果(けっか)</code> are the matching ones - 
調査(ちょうさ), 検査(けんさ), 話(はな)し合(あ)い, 投票(とうひょう),
努力(どりょく), 選考(せんこう) - all of them names of activities rather than of
things.

## Examples

<pre>
話(はな)し合(あ)った<b>結果(けっか)</b>、会場(かいじょう)を変(か)えることになった。
After talking it over we ended up changing the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
検査(けんさ)の<b>結果(けっか)</b>、異常(いじょう)は見(み)つかりませんでした。
The tests came back showing nothing wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も計算(けいさん)し直(なお)した<b>結果(けっか)</b>、数字(すうじ)の間違(まちが)いが一(ひと)つ見(み)つかった。
Working through the sums again and again turned up one error in the figures.
</pre>

<pre>
投票(とうひょう)の<b>結果(けっか)</b>、新(あたら)しい案(あん)が採用(さいよう)されました。
The vote resulted in the new proposal being adopted.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)で相談(そうだん)した<b>結果(けっか)</b>、祖父(そふ)の家(いえ)は売(う)らないことに決(き)めました。
Having talked it through as a family, we've decided not to sell my grandfather's house.
</pre>

<pre>
無理(むり)な練習(れんしゅう)を続(つづ)けた<b>結果(けっか)</b>、肩(かた)を痛(いた)めてしまった。
Keeping up a punishing training routine left me with a bad shoulder.
</pre>

<pre>
社内(しゃない)で検討(けんとう)いたしました<b>結果(けっか)</b>、今回(こんかい)は見送(みおく)らせていただくことになりました。
Following our internal review, we have decided to pass on the opportunity this time.
</pre>

<pre>
各地(かくち)の農家(のうか)に聞(き)き取(と)りを行(おこな)い、十年分(じゅうねんぶん)の記録(きろく)を比(くら)べた<b>結果(けっか)</b>、収穫(しゅうかく)の時期(じき)が少(すこ)しずつ早(はや)まっていることが分(わ)かった。
Interviews with farmers around the country and a comparison of ten years of records showed that the harvest is creeping steadily earlier.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This belongs to written and formal-spoken Japanese: minutes, reports, medical
results, business email, the news. It is not stiff enough to be comic in
conversation - a colleague may well say
話(はな)し合(あ)った結果(けっか)こうなりました - but among friends the ordinary
choice is `から`, `ので` or simply `て`.

Its most conspicuous habitat is the **polite refusal**. The frame
検討(けんとう)いたしました結果(けっか)、～見送(みおく)らせていただきます is close to a fixed
formula in business Japanese, and the `結果` is doing real work in it: by presenting
the answer as the outcome of a process, the writer makes the refusal sound
considered rather than personal. Note the shape there - a ます-form in front of
`結果`, which is otherwise unusual and belongs to this very formal register only.

Two things to keep in view. `結果` is evaluatively neutral, so what follows can be
good, bad or merely numerical; that is why it is safe in writing where blame would
be awkward. And the pattern insists on a **process**: 
転(ころ)んだ結果(けっか)、けがをした is odd, because falling over is not something
you conduct. Where the cause is an accident or a state of affairs, the neutral
`ため` or the blaming `せいで` fit better.

Finally, <code>検査(けんさ)の結果(けっか)、～</code> can be read two ways: as this
connective pattern, or as the plain noun phrase "the results of the test" used
adverbially. Nothing turns on the distinction - both readings deliver the same
meaning - but it explains why `結果` also turns up with `を`, `が` and `は` in
sentences that have nothing to do with this pattern.

## Contrast

### ～た結果(けっか) vs その結果(けっか)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same idea, different architecture.
<code>～た結果(けっか)</code> tucks the process into a subordinate clause;
<code>その結果(けっか)</code> gives it a sentence of its own and then comments on it.

</div>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)を見直(みなお)した<b>結果(けっか)</b>、契約(けいやく)がまとまりました。
Revising the terms brought the contract together.
</pre>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)を何度(なんど)も見直(みなお)しました。<b>その結果(けっか)</b>、契約(けいやく)がまとまりました。
We revised the terms several times over. As a result, the contract came together.
</pre>

Use the subordinate version when the process is background and the outcome is the
news. Use the two-sentence version when the process deserves description in its own
right - which is why it is the one that appears after a long account of what was
tried.

### ～た結果(けっか) vs ～たあとで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たあとで` is pure sequence: this happened, then that.
<code>た結果(けっか)</code> claims the second thing **came out of** the first.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わった<b>あとで</b>、資料(しりょう)を配(くば)りました。
I handed out the documents after the meeting finished.
</pre>

<pre>
長(なが)く議論(ぎろん)した<b>結果(けっか)</b>、資料(しりょう)を作(つく)り直(なお)すことになりました。
A long discussion ended with our having to redo the documents.
</pre>

Handing out papers is not produced by the meeting ending; it merely follows it. If
you can insert "and then" and lose nothing, you want `あとで`. If the second clause
answers "what came of it?", you want `結果`.

### ～た結果(けっか) vs ～たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both can report what you found out by doing something. `たら` is
the spoken discovery - "I did X, and lo". <code>た結果(けっか)</code> is its written
counterpart and frames the finding as the product of a procedure.

</div>

<pre>
調(しら)べて<b>みたら</b>、去年(きょねん)も同(おな)じことが起(お)きていた。
I looked into it, and it turns out the same thing happened last year too.
</pre>

<pre>
過去(かこ)の記録(きろく)を調(しら)べた<b>結果(けっか)</b>、去年(きょねん)にも同(おな)じ事例(じれい)が確認(かくにん)された。
An examination of past records confirmed a comparable case last year as well.
</pre>

`たら` carries surprise and belongs to speech; `結果` carries none and belongs to the
report that gets written afterwards. Notice how the whole sentence changes register
with it - 事例(じれい) and 確認(かくにん)された arrive in its company.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 転(ころ)んだ<b>結果(けっか)</b>、足(あし)をけがしました。 → ✅ 転(ころ)んで足(あし)をけがしました。
  Falling over is an accident, not a procedure. `結果` needs something that was
  carried out on purpose or sustained over time.

- ❌ 話(はな)し合(あ)う<b>結果(けっか)</b>、決(き)めましょう。 → ✅ 話(はな)し合(あ)った<b>結果(けっか)</b>、こうなりました。
  The verb has to be in the past. An outcome cannot precede the process that
  produced it.

- ❌ 検討(けんとう)した結果(けっか)で、中止(ちゅうし)と決(き)まりました。 → ✅ 検討(けんとう)した結果(けっか)、中止(ちゅうし)と決(き)まりました。
  No particle after `結果` in this pattern - a comma does the joining.

- ❌ 調査(ちょうさ)した結果(けっか)、来週(らいしゅう)までに報告(ほうこく)してください。 → ✅ 調査(ちょうさ)が終(お)わりました<b>ので</b>、来週(らいしゅう)までに報告(ほうこく)してください。
  An instruction is not an outcome. Requests take `ので`.

- ❌ 努力(どりょく)した結果(けっか)の<b>おかげで</b>合格(ごうかく)しました。 → ✅ 努力(どりょく)した<b>結果(けっか)</b>合格(ごうかく)しました。 ／ ✅ 努力(どりょく)の<b>おかげで</b>合格(ごうかく)しました。
  Stacking the two is redundant, and it mixes a neutral report with a word of
  thanks. Pick one.

# なぜなら・なぜかというと

**Meaning** · because; the reason is - you make a claim, then turn round and justify it

`から` and `ので` bury the reason inside the sentence, ahead of the point.
`なぜなら` and `なぜかというと` do the opposite: the claim goes first, in a sentence
of its own, and the reason follows in a second sentence flagged as a reason. That
order is the whole value of the pattern - it is how you argue rather than merely
explain.

## Formation

These are sentence-opening phrases, not suffixes, so nothing conjugates in front of
them. What matters is what comes **after**: the explanation is normally closed off
with からだ／からです, and leaving that off makes the sentence sound truncated.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A stated claim (written) | ～。なぜなら、～からだ／からです。 | 反対(はんたい)です。なぜなら、危(あぶ)ないからです。 |
| Emphatic written variant | ～。なぜならば、～からである。 | なぜならば、記録(きろく)が残(のこ)らないからである |
| Spoken and explanatory | ～。なぜかというと、～からです。 | なぜかというと、順番(じゅんばん)が逆(ぎゃく)だからです |
| Same frame, other shapes | なぜかといえば／どうしてかというと | どうしてかというと、寒(さむ)いからです |
| Closing with a noun-reason | なぜなら、～ためだ／～のだ | なぜなら、人手(ひとで)が足(た)りないためだ |
| Casual equivalent | だって、～から／～んだもん | だって、高(たか)いんだもん |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the claim comes **first**. `なぜなら` can never open the pair;
if you find yourself writing it at the head of a paragraph with nothing before it,
what you actually want is `から` or `ので`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)ります。<b>なぜなら</b>、明日(あした)試験(しけん)があるからです。
I'm going home early today. The reason is that I have an exam tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
この方法(ほうほう)はおすすめしません。<b>なぜなら</b>、時間(じかん)がかかりすぎるからです。
I wouldn't recommend this method, because it takes far too long.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りは早(はや)めに設定(せってい)しています。<b>なぜかというと</b>、必(かなら)ず何(なに)かが起(お)きるからです。
I set the deadlines early. The reason being, something always goes wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は紙(かみ)の辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)い続(つづ)けている。<b>なぜなら</b>、前後(ぜんご)の語(ご)が目(め)に入(はい)るからだ。
I still use a paper dictionary, because the words on either side catch my eye.
</pre>

<pre>
この道(みち)は夕方(ゆうがた)通(とお)らないようにしている。<b>どうしてかというと</b>、学校(がっこう)帰(がえ)りの子(こ)どもが多(おお)いからだ。
I try not to use this road in the late afternoon. Why? Because it's full of children on their way home from school.
</pre>

<pre>
記録(きろく)はすべて残(のこ)しておくべきである。<b>なぜならば</b>、後(あと)から確(たし)かめる方法(ほうほう)が他(ほか)にないからである。
All records should be kept, for the reason that there is no other way of checking them later.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げには反対(はんたい)です。<b>なぜなら</b>、いちばん困(こま)るのは毎日(まいにち)使(つか)っている人(ひと)たちだからです。
I'm against the price rise, because the people it will hurt most are the ones who use it every day.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)には最初(さいしょ)の一(いっ)か月(げつ)、失敗(しっぱい)しても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)な仕事(しごと)だけを任(まか)せています。<b>なぜかというと</b>、その時期(じき)に叱(しか)られた人(ひと)ほど、あとで質問(しつもん)をしなくなるからです。
For their first month I give new staff only work where a mistake does no harm - because the ones who get told off in that first month are exactly the ones who stop asking questions later.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`なぜなら` is written Japanese, and specifically **argumentative** written Japanese:
opinion columns, essays, a slide deck, the paragraph in a report where you defend a
recommendation. It marks the sentence after it as *support for a claim*, which is
why it sounds slightly grand in ordinary chat - dropping it into a conversation
about lunch is a little like saying "for the following reason" out loud. `なぜならば`
is the same word with an extra formal weight, at home with `である` endings.

`なぜかというと` is the spoken member of the family. Literally "if you ask why", it
builds the imagined question into the phrase, and that makes it friendly and
explanatory rather than argumentative: teachers, guides and anyone walking a
listener through their thinking use it constantly. `どうしてかというと` is the same
thing a step more casual. All of them still expect からです at the end.

Down at the casual end sits `だって`, which is a different animal - it is defensive
rather than expository, it belongs at the head of a reply, and it pairs with the
sentence-final `もん`. Between friends, 行(い)かない。だって眠(ねむ)いんだもん is
what actually gets said.

One usage note that catches learners out: when somebody has **asked** you どうして?,
you do not answer with `なぜなら`. The question has already flagged that a reason is
coming, so the signpost is redundant, and the natural answer is a bare
電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったからです。 `なぜなら` is for reasons **you** decide to
supply, unprompted.

Finally, do not sprinkle it. An essay in which every paragraph turns on `なぜなら`
reads as mechanical to a Japanese reader, exactly as a run of "because of this" does
in English. Use it where the reason genuinely needs marking as a reason.

## Contrast

### なぜなら vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same content, opposite order. `から` and `ので` put the reason
first and inside the sentence; `なぜなら` puts the claim first and gives the reason a
sentence of its own.

</div>

<pre>
人手(ひとで)が足(た)りない<b>ので</b>、土曜日(どようび)の営業(えいぎょう)をやめました。
We stopped opening on Saturdays because we're short-staffed.
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)の営業(えいぎょう)をやめました。<b>なぜなら</b>、人手(ひとで)が足(た)りないからです。
We've stopped opening on Saturdays. The reason is that we're short-staffed.
</pre>

Choose by asking what the sentence is *for*. If you are simply explaining a
situation, the `ので` version is shorter and more natural. If you are defending a
decision that the listener may not like, putting it first and then supporting it - 
claim, evidence - is what `なぜなら` was made for. Note that the reason clause still
ends in からです even though `なぜなら` already means "because"; the two are not
redundant in Japanese, they bracket the reason at both ends.

### なぜなら vs なぜかというと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical function, different register. `なぜなら` argues on
paper; `なぜかというと` explains out loud.

</div>

<pre>
この案(あん)は見直(みなお)すべきだ。<b>なぜなら</b>、費用(ひよう)の見積(みつ)もりが甘(あま)すぎるからだ。
This proposal needs revisiting, because the cost estimates are far too optimistic.
</pre>

<pre>
ここだけ先(さき)に説明(せつめい)しますね。<b>なぜかというと</b>、あとの話(はなし)が全部(ぜんぶ)ここにつながるからです。
Let me explain this bit first - the reason being that everything after it hangs on it.
</pre>

The first is a written argument and could not be said aloud without sounding as
though you were reading it. The second builds in the listener's imagined "why?" and
is warm; in a written report it would look chatty. `どうしてかというと` shifts one
notch further towards conversation again.

### なぜなら vs というのは・だって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なぜなら` supports a claim. `というのは` fills in background the
listener is missing. `だって` defends the speaker against criticism.

</div>

<pre>
今週(こんしゅう)は顔(かお)を出(だ)せません。<b>というのは</b>、母(はは)が入院(にゅういん)してしまったんです。
I can't come by this week - the thing is, my mother's been taken into hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
「どうして先(さき)に言(い)わなかったの?」「<b>だって</b>、心配(しんぱい)させたくなかったんだもん。」
"Why didn't you say something earlier?" "Because I didn't want to worry you."
</pre>

`というのは` is doing something closer to "the thing is" - it supplies circumstances
rather than proving a point, and it does not require からです at the end. `だって`
answers an accusation and belongs to casual speech; used to a superior it sounds
like a child talking back. Between them and `なぜなら` you have most of the register
range covered.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>なぜなら</b>、明日(あした)試験(しけん)があります。今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)ります。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)ります。<b>なぜなら</b>、明日(あした)試験(しけん)があるからです。
  The claim has to come first. `なぜなら` looks backwards at something already said.

- ❌ 行(い)きません。<b>なぜなら</b>、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていました。 → ✅ 行(い)きません。<b>なぜなら</b>、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていたからです。
  The reason clause needs its からだ／からです. Without it the sentence stops in
  mid-air.

- ❌ 「どうして遅(おく)れたんですか。」「なぜなら、電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったからです。」 → ✅ 「電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったからです。」
  When the reason has been asked for, announcing that a reason is coming is
  redundant and sounds oddly formal.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。<b>だって</b>、資料(しりょう)が届(とど)かなかったんです。 → ✅ …資料(しりょう)が届(とど)かなかった<b>ものですから</b>。
  `だって` is a child's defence. Aimed upward at a manager it is close to insolent.

- ❌ この色(いろ)が好(す)き。<b>なぜならば</b>、落(お)ち着(つ)くからである。 （casual chat） → ✅ この色(いろ)が好(す)き。だって落(お)ち着(つ)くんだもん。
  `なぜならば` and `である` belong to formal writing. In a conversation about
  paint colours they read as a joke.

## Practice

**1.** 自転車(じてんしゃ)で通勤(つうきん)することにしました。____、電車(でんしゃ)より早(はや)く着(つ)くことが分(わ)かったからです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>なぜなら</b>、電車(でんしゃ)より早(はや)く着(つ)くことが分(わ)かったからです。

The claim has already been made in the first sentence, and the second exists to
support it - the exact shape this connector marks. <code>なぜかというと</code> would fit
too, and would sound a shade more conversational.

</details>

**2.** Choose, in a conversation with a close friend:
行(い)かないよ。（なぜならば・だって）、会費(かいひ)が高(たか)すぎるんだもん。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

行(い)かないよ。<b>だって</b>、会費(かいひ)が高(たか)すぎるんだもん。

The plain endings and the sentence-final `もん` set the register, and `だって` is the
member of this family that belongs there. `なぜならば` with `んだもん` after it is a
collision of the most formal and the most casual options available.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a line in a written report: "We recommend postponing the launch. The reason is that testing is not yet complete."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

発売(はつばい)は延期(えんき)すべきだと考(かんが)えます。<b>なぜなら</b>、試験(しけん)がまだ終(お)わっていないからです。

A recommendation followed by its justification, in writing, is `なぜなら` at its most
typical. Keep the からです: the connector opens the reason and からです closes it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)った方(ほう)がいいですよ。なぜなら、午後(ごご)から雨(あめ)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ …なぜなら、午後(ごご)から雨(あめ)<b>だからです</b>。

The reason clause has to close with からだ／からです, and after a noun that means
`だから`. Without it the second sentence reads as an unfinished thought - the
listener is still waiting for the end of it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今年(ことし)は旅行(りょこう)を見送(みおく)ります。<b>なぜなら</b>、工事(こうじ)にお金(かね)がかかるからです。
(b) 今年(ことし)は旅行(りょこう)を見送(みおく)ります。<b>というのは</b>、家(いえ)の工事(こうじ)をすることになったんです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) argues. The decision is stated, then defended with a reason marked as a reason,
and the からです closes the argument off. It is what you would write in a message
that expects to be questioned.

(b) confides. <code>というのは</code> supplies background the listener did not have - 
"the thing is, we're having work done on the house" - and it needs no からです.
It is softer and more conversational, and it invites sympathy rather than
agreement.

</details>
