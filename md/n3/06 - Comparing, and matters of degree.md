# ～につれて

**Meaning** · as one thing gradually progresses, another gradually changes with it

`～につれて` ties two slow changes together and says they move as a pair. Neither
side is a single event: something is *in the process of* increasing, advancing,
ageing, warming, and something else shifts along the way. English usually says
this with "as", and Japanese wants both halves to be movement along a scale.

## Formation

The clause before `につれて` is always **non-past**, however clearly the sentence
is set in the past. Tense is carried by the final verb only.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (change of state) | V(dictionary) + につれて | 増(ふ)える → 増(ふ)えるにつれて |
| Verb, formal writing | V(dictionary) + につれ | 進(すす)む → 進(すす)むにつれ |
| Noun naming a process | Noun + につれて | 成長(せいちょう) → 成長(せいちょう)につれて |
| Noun with no change in it | Noun + が + verb + につれて | 時間(じかん) → 時間(じかん)がたつにつれて |

<div class="note">

**Note** - only nouns that already contain the idea of change work directly:
<code>成長(せいちょう)</code>, <code>変化(へんか)</code>,
<code>発展(はってん)</code>, <code>回復(かいふく)</code>. A plain noun needs a
verb first - ❌<code>時間(じかん)につれて</code> →
✅<code>時間(じかん)がたつにつれて</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
時間(じかん)がたつ<b>につれて</b>、風(かぜ)が強(つよ)くなった。
As time went on, the wind picked up.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)を登(のぼ)る<b>につれて</b>、空気(くうき)が冷(つめ)たくなった。
As we climbed the mountain, the air turned colder.
</pre>

<pre>
日(ひ)が短(みじか)くなる<b>につれて</b>、朝(あさ)起(お)きるのがつらくなる。
As the days get shorter, getting up in the morning gets harder.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)が進(すす)む<b>につれて</b>、スタンドの声(こえ)が大(おお)きくなっていった。
As the match went on, the crowd got louder and louder.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの成長(せいちょう)<b>につれて</b>、靴(くつ)のサイズが毎年(まいとし)変(か)わる。
As a child grows, their shoe size changes every year.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)を重(かさ)ねる<b>につれて</b>、指(ゆび)が自然(しぜん)に動(うご)くようになってきた。
As I put in more practice, my fingers started moving on their own.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)が進(すす)む<b>につれて</b>、駅前(えきまえ)の景色(けしき)はすっかり変(か)わってしまった。
As the construction went ahead, the area in front of the station changed completely.
</pre>

<pre>
人口(じんこう)が減(へ)る<b>につれ</b>、村(むら)の行事(ぎょうじ)は一(ひと)つずつなくなっていった。
As the population fell, the village's annual events disappeared one by one.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～につれて` is neutral in politeness - it sits inside the sentence, so the
formality of the page is set by the ending (<code>変(か)わる</code> /
<code>変(か)わります</code>), not by the pattern. What it *does* carry is a mildly
written flavour. It is at home in explanations, reports, narration and the sort of
thing a newsreader says; in relaxed conversation people often reach for
<code>～と、だんだん…</code> instead: 夜(よる)になると、だんだん寒(さむ)くなる.

Clipping it to `につれ` raises the register another notch. That form belongs to
written Japanese - newspapers, official notices, essays - and sounds stiff spoken
aloud.

Two habits go with the pattern. The second clause is normally a **description of
change**, so it very often ends in <code>～くなる</code>, <code>～ていく</code> or
<code>～てくる</code>. And because the sentence is reporting an observed process,
the second clause cannot be a request, a command, an invitation or a statement of
intention. "As it gets colder, please close the window" is not a `につれて`
sentence; it is a `たら` or `と` sentence.

Finally, the speaker is standing outside watching. `～につれて` describes; it does
not persuade. If you want the emotional weight of "the more, the more", the
correlative <code>～ば～ほど</code> is the pattern with the pressure in it.

## Contrast

### ～につれて vs ～に従(したが)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the parallel-change sense the two are close to
interchangeable. `に従って` additionally has a life `につれて` does not have at all:
**following** rules, instructions and arrows.

</div>

Where both work, `につれて` describes a change that simply happens, while
`に従って` leans a little towards one thing being led by the other, and is the
stiffer of the two:

<pre>
北(きた)へ進(すす)む<b>につれて</b>、雪(ゆき)が深(ふか)くなった。
The further north we went, the deeper the snow got.
</pre>

But only `に従って` can take an instruction as its object:

<pre>
係員(かかりいん)の指示(しじ)<b>に従(したが)って</b>外(そと)に出(で)てください。
Please go outside following the attendant's instructions.
</pre>

❌<code>指示(しじ)につれて</code> is simply not Japanese. If you can replace the
English "as" with "in accordance with", you need `に従って`.

### ～につれて vs ～とともに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `につれて` needs both sides to be **gradual**. `とともに`
only needs them to be **simultaneous**, so it also covers sudden events and plain
accompaniment.

</div>

<pre>
春(はる)が近(ちか)づく<b>につれて</b>、山(やま)の色(いろ)が変(か)わってきた。
As spring drew near, the colour of the mountains began to change.
</pre>

Swap in `とともに` there and nothing breaks. But `とともに` also does this, and
`につれて` cannot:

<pre>
ベルが鳴(な)る<b>とともに</b>、生徒(せいと)たちが走(はし)り出(だ)した。
The moment the bell rang, the students broke into a run.
</pre>

The bell is instantaneous - there is no scale to travel along - so
❌<code>鳴(な)るにつれて</code> is wrong. `とともに` is also the one that means
"together with (a person)": 家族(かぞく)とともに暮(く)らす.

### ～につれて vs ～ば～ほど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `につれて` reports one actual process unfolding.
<code>～ば～ほど</code> states a general proportion, true whenever you test it, and
it can be hypothetical.

</div>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)する<b>につれて</b>、発音(はつおん)がよくなってきた。
As I practised, my pronunciation improved.
</pre>

That is a report on your own past few months. The correlative version makes a
claim about the world instead:

<pre>
発音(はつおん)は練習(れんしゅう)すれ<b>ば</b>する<b>ほど</b>よくなる。
The more you practise, the better your pronunciation gets.
</pre>

The second one can be said by someone who has never practised in their life. It
also allows advice and predictions after it, which `につれて` does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 電気(でんき)を消(け)す**につれて**、部屋(へや)が暗(くら)くなった。 → ✅ 電気(でんき)を消(け)す**と**、部屋(へや)が暗(くら)くなった。
  Flipping a switch is instantaneous. `につれて` needs a change that takes time and
  passes through middle stages.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)が**たったにつれて** → ✅ 時間(じかん)が**たつにつれて**
  The clause before `につれて` is always non-past. The past tense goes on the end
  of the sentence.

- ❌ 暑(あつ)くなる**につれて**、水(みず)をたくさん飲(の)んでください。 → ✅ 暑(あつ)くなる**と**、水(みず)をたくさん飲(の)んでください。
  A request cannot follow `につれて`. The second clause has to describe a change,
  not tell the listener to do something.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)**につれて**、痛(いた)みが消(き)えた。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がたつ**につれて**、痛(いた)みが消(き)えていった。
  <code>時間(じかん)</code> is not itself a change. Add the verb that supplies
  one.

## Practice

**1.** 年(とし)を____につれて、昔(むかし)の友(とも)だちに会(あ)いたくなる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

年(とし)を<b>取(と)る</b>につれて、昔(むかし)の友(とも)だちに会(あ)いたくなる。

The verb before `につれて` stays in the dictionary form, and
<code>年(とし)を取(と)る</code> is the standard collocation for ageing, which
is exactly the kind of slow, stage-by-stage change this pattern wants.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 地図(ちず)（につれて・に従(したが)って）歩(ある)いたら、海(うみ)に出(で)た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

地図(ちず)<b>に従(したが)って</b>歩(ある)いたら、海(うみ)に出(で)た。

A map is something you obey, not a process that unfolds. `につれて` only links two
gradual changes, so it cannot take a set of directions as its object.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "As the meeting went on, everyone got tired."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会議(かいぎ)が進(すす)む<b>につれて</b>、みんな疲(つか)れてきた。

Both halves move by degrees, which is what licenses `につれて`, and the ending
<code>～てきた</code> is the natural way to show the tiredness accumulating rather
than arriving all at once.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 駅(えき)に着(つ)くにつれて、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 駅(えき)に近(ちか)づく<b>につれて</b>、雨(あめ)が強(つよ)くなった。

<code>着(つ)く</code> happens in an instant, and so does
<code>降(ふ)り出(だ)す</code> - neither side offers a scale. Replacing them
with <code>近(ちか)づく</code> and <code>強(つよ)くなる</code> gives two
gradual changes to link.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 人口(じんこう)が増(ふ)える<b>につれて</b>、ごみの量(りょう)も増(ふ)えた。
(b) 人口(じんこう)が増(ふ)えれ<b>ば</b>増(ふ)える<b>ほど</b>、ごみの量(りょう)も増(ふ)える。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a report about a particular place over a particular stretch of time: the
population went up, and the rubbish went up alongside it. It is history.

(b) is a general law with no dates attached - for any town, more people means more
rubbish. It would fit in an argument or a warning, and unlike (a) it can be said
about a population increase that has not happened yet.

</details>

# ～に従って

**Meaning** · (1) in accordance with ～, following ～; (2) as ～ progresses, ～ changes with it

<code>～に従(したが)って</code> is the て-form of the verb <code>従(したが)う</code>, "to
follow, to obey", and both of its meanings come straight out of that. Follow a set
of instructions and you get sense (1). Let one change follow another and you get
sense (2), where it is close to <code>～につれて</code>. Written as a single
sentence-opening word, したがって is a third thing entirely: "therefore".

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun: rule, order, model | Noun + に従(したが)って | 説明書(せつめいしょ) → 説明書(せつめいしょ)に従(したが)って |
| Noun naming a process | Noun + に従(したが)って | 時代(じだい)の変化(へんか) → 時代(じだい)の変化(へんか)に従(したが)って |
| Verb (change of state) | V(dictionary) + に従(したが)って | 進(すす)む → 進(すす)むに従(したが)って |
| Formal writing | ～に従(したが)い | 規則(きそく) → 規則(きそく)に従(したが)い |
| Modifying a noun | ～に従(したが)った + Noun | 指示(しじ) → 指示(しじ)に従(したが)った行動(こうどう) |
| As an ordinary verb | Noun + に従(したが)う | ルールに従(したが)う |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>従(したが)う</code> is an う-verb (五段(ごだん) / godan / Group 1), so
its て-form is <code>従(したが)って</code> with a small つ. ❌<code>従(したが)て</code>
and ❌<code>従(したが)いて</code> are both wrong. As with
<code>～につれて</code>, the clause before it stays **non-past**.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
矢印(やじるし)<b>に従(したが)って</b>進(すす)んでください。
Please go on, following the arrows.
</pre>

<pre>
ルール<b>に従(したが)って</b>ゲームをしましょう。
Let's play the game by the rules.
</pre>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)の注意(ちゅうい)<b>に従(したが)って</b>、塩分(えんぶん)を減(へ)らしています。
I'm cutting down on salt as the doctor told me to.
</pre>

<pre>
年(とし)を取(と)る<b>に従(したが)って</b>、夜(よ)更(ふ)かしがつらくなってきた。
As I get older, staying up late has started to take its toll.
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)が進(すす)む<b>に従(したが)って</b>、昔(むかし)の道具(どうぐ)は見(み)かけなくなった。
As technology moved on, the old tools stopped being a common sight.
</pre>

<pre>
地元(じもと)の習慣(しゅうかん)<b>に従(したが)って</b>、靴(くつ)を脱(ぬ)いで部屋(へや)に上(あ)がった。
Following the local custom, I took off my shoes and stepped up into the room.
</pre>

<pre>
法律(ほうりつ)の改正(かいせい)<b>に従(したが)い</b>、来月(らいげつ)から手続(てつづ)きが変(か)わります。
In accordance with the revision of the law, the procedure changes from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
マニュアル<b>に従(したが)って</b>操作(そうさ)したはずなのに、機械(きかい)はまったく動(うご)かなかった。
I'm sure I operated it according to the manual, but the machine wouldn't move at all.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a formal expression in both of its senses. In sense (1) it carries the
weight of the verb it comes from: obeying, complying, submitting. Told to a friend
that you cooked <code>レシピに従(したが)って</code>, it sounds oddly solemn - 
conversation prefers <code>レシピどおりに</code> or
<code>レシピを見(み)ながら</code>. Where it belongs is signage, safety
announcements, contracts, court reporting and workplace instructions.

Trimming to <code>に従(したが)い</code> is the written-only variant, one register above the
て-form and common in notices: <code>規則(きそく)に従(したが)い処理(しょり)いたします</code>.

Note who is doing the following. <code>に従(したが)って</code> puts the subject in the
position of complying with something with authority behind it - a rule, an order,
a doctor, a custom. Using it about a mere suggestion overstates the case, and using
it about a person can sound like obedience rather than agreement:
<code>部長(ぶちょう)に従(したが)って</code> is "doing as the manager
directs", not "along with the manager".

In sense (2), the parallel-change use, it is drier and more analytical than
<code>～につれて</code>, and turns up mostly in writing that is explaining a trend.
It also has a faint implication that the second change is *driven* by the first
rather than merely accompanying it.

<div class="note">

**Note** - sentence-initially, したがって means "therefore". It is one of the most
formal connectives in the language, at home in academic papers and legal writing:
台風(たいふう)が接近(せっきん)している。したがって、明日(あす)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)とする。
In speech you would say だから or ですから.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～に従(したが)って vs ～につれて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for two changes moving together, both work and `に従って` is
the stiffer choice. For following instructions, only `に従って` is possible.

</div>

<pre>
南(みなみ)へ下(くだ)る<b>に従(したが)って</b>、畑(はたけ)が広(ひろ)くなっていく。
As you go further south, the fields get wider.
</pre>

Replace `に従って` with `につれて` there and almost nothing changes; the sentence
just sounds a shade less like a geography textbook. Now the half `につれて` cannot
reach:

<pre>
先生(せんせい)のアドバイス<b>に従(したが)って</b>、書(か)き直(なお)してみました。
I rewrote it following my teacher's advice.
</pre>

There is no gradual change here at all - just compliance - so
❌<code>アドバイスにつれて</code> is impossible. Test it in English: if "as" can
become "in accordance with", it is `に従って`.

### ～に従(したが)って vs ～とおりに・～どおりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どおりに` means the result **matches the model exactly**.
`に従って` means you **took the instruction as your authority**. Everyday speech
prefers `どおりに`.

</div>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)の<b>とおりに</b>組(く)み立(た)てたら、ねじが一(ひと)つ余(あま)った。
I assembled it exactly as the instructions said, and had one screw left over.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の方針(ほうしん)<b>に従(したが)って</b>、報告(ほうこく)書(しょ)の形式(けいしき)を変(か)えました。
We changed the format of the report in line with company policy.
</pre>

A policy is an authority you obey; a diagram is a model you copy. Note also the
shape of the word: after a noun with `の` it is <code>とおりに</code>
(<code>思(おも)ったとおりに</code>), but glued straight onto a noun it becomes
<code>どおりに</code> - <code>予定(よてい)どおりに</code>,
<code>時間(じかん)どおりに</code>.

### に従(したが)って inside a sentence vs したがって at the head of one

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the word is attached to a noun or verb, it means
"following". If it is standing alone at the start of a sentence with a comma after
it, it means "therefore".

</div>

<pre>
前例(ぜんれい)<b>に従(したが)って</b>判断(はんだん)しました。
We made the decision following precedent.
</pre>

<pre>
前例(ぜんれい)がない。<b>したがって</b>、慎重(しんちょう)に判断(はんだん)する必要(ひつよう)がある。
There is no precedent. Therefore, the decision must be made carefully.
</pre>

Same characters, unrelated jobs. The connective is normally written in kana in
modern Japanese, which helps, but not reliably - and hearing it aloud, only the
position in the sentence tells you which one it is.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 説明書(せつめいしょ)に従(したが)**て** → ✅ 説明書(せつめいしょ)に従(したが)**って**
  <code>従(したが)う</code> is an う-verb, so its て-form doubles the consonant:
  <code>従(したが)って</code>.

- ❌ 指示(しじ)<b>につれて</b>動(うご)いてください。 → ✅ 指示(しじ)<b>に従(したが)って</b>動(うご)いてください。
  Instructions are obeyed, not gradually undergone. `につれて` has no "in
  accordance with" meaning at all.

- ❌ 年(とし)を取(と)<b>ったに</b>従(したが)って → ✅ 年(とし)を取(と)<b>るに</b>従(したが)って
  In the parallel-change sense the verb before it stays in the dictionary form,
  whatever the tense of the sentence.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)が来(き)ました。<b>従(したが)って</b>中止(ちゅうし)です。（in casual speech） → ✅ 台風(たいふう)が来(き)ました。**だから**中止(ちゅうし)です。
  Not ungrammatical, but したがって in a chat sounds like reading out a legal
  notice. Keep it for writing.

## Practice

**1.** 係員(かかりいん)の指示(しじ)____従(したが)って、ゆっくり外(そと)へ出(で)てください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

係員(かかりいん)の指示(しじ)<b>に</b>従(したが)って…

<code>従(したが)う</code> takes its object with `に`, never `を` - you comply
*to* something in Japanese. The same `に` shows up in
<code>規則(きそく)に従(したが)う</code> and
<code>命令(めいれい)に従(したが)う</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 川(かわ)を下(くだ)る（に従(したが)って・のとおりに）、水(みず)の色(いろ)が茶色(ちゃいろ)くなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

川(かわ)を下(くだ)る<b>に従(したが)って</b>、水(みず)の色(いろ)が茶色(ちゃいろ)くなった。

This is the parallel-change sense: two things shifting together along the length of
the river. <code>とおりに</code> would need a model to copy, and a river is not
giving anybody instructions.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Following the doctor's advice, I stopped drinking coffee at night."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

医者(いしゃ)のアドバイス<b>に従(したが)って</b>、夜(よる)はコーヒーを飲(の)むのをやめました。

Advice from a doctor counts as an authority you comply with, so
<code>に従(したが)って</code> fits. In casual conversation
<code>医者(いしゃ)に言(い)われたとおりに</code> would be the lighter way to
say the same thing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 時代(じだい)が変(か)わったに従(したが)い、働(はたら)き方(かた)も変(か)わってきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 時代(じだい)が変(か)わる<b>に従(したが)い</b>、働(はたら)き方(かた)も変(か)わってきた。

The verb in front has to be non-past. The past sense is already carried by
<code>変(か)わってきた</code> at the end, and doubling it up is what makes the
original ungrammatical.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 先輩(せんぱい)のやり方(かた)<b>に従(したが)って</b>作(つく)りました。
(b) 先輩(せんぱい)のやり方(かた)の<b>とおりに</b>作(つく)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) frames the senior colleague's method as something you were expected to comply
with. It suits a workplace where that method is the rule, and it quietly says you
did not deviate from it.

(b) is about the product matching the model: you copied the method, step for step.
It says nothing about authority, so it is the neutral, more conversational choice - 
and it is what you would say if you simply thought their way was good.

</details>

# ～ば～ほど

**Meaning** · the more ～, the more ～ - two quantities that rise and fall together

`～ば～ほど` links a cause and a result and says they are **proportional**: turn the
first one up and the second one goes up with it. The same word appears twice, once
in the `ば` form and once in the plain form, which is what makes the pattern
instantly recognisable. Unlike <code>～につれて</code>, it is not a report on
something that happened - it is a claim about how things work.

## Formation

The first copy of the word goes into the conditional `ば` form; the second copy goes
into the form that can sit in front of `ほど`, which is the plain form for verbs and
い-adjectives and the `な` form for な-adjectives.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | ば-form + V(dictionary) + ほど | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)めば読(よ)むほど |
| る-verb | ば-form + V(dictionary) + ほど | 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)えれば考(かんが)えるほど |
| する | すれば + する + ほど | 練習(れんしゅう)する → 練習(れんしゅう)すれば練習(れんしゅう)するほど |
| い-adjective | ～ければ + ～い + ほど | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)ければ安(やす)いほど |
| な-adjective | ～なら + ～な + ほど | 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かなら静(しず)かなほど |
| な-adjective (formal) | ～であれば + あるほど | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)であればあるほど |
| Noun | ～であれば + あるほど | プロ → プロであればあるほど |
| Negative | ～なければ + ～ないほど | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らなければ知(し)らないほど |
| Shortened | ～ほど（the ば half dropped） | 早(はや)い → 早(はや)いほどいい |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the labels this book uses for what other books call
五段(ごだん) / godan / Group 1 and 一段(いちだん) / ichidan / Group 2. For な-adjectives, both
<code>静(しず)かなら静(しず)かなほど</code> and
<code>静(しず)かであればあるほど</code> are correct; the second is the written,
formal one, and it is the only option after a noun.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この本(ほん)は読(よ)め<b>ば</b>読(よ)む<b>ほど</b>おもしろくなる。
The more you read this book, the more interesting it gets.
</pre>

<pre>
野菜(やさい)は新(あたら)しけれ<b>ば</b>新(あたら)しい<b>ほど</b>おいしい。
The fresher the vegetables, the better they taste.
</pre>

<pre>
考(かんが)えれ<b>ば</b>考(かんが)える<b>ほど</b>、答(こた)えが分(わ)からなくなった。
The more I thought about it, the less I understood the answer.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)は簡単(かんたん)なら簡単(かんたん)な<b>ほど</b>いいです。
The simpler the explanation, the better.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)けれ<b>ば</b>広(ひろ)い<b>ほど</b>冬(ふゆ)は寒(さむ)い。
The bigger the room, the colder it is in winter.
</pre>

<pre>
道具(どうぐ)は単純(たんじゅん)であれ<b>ば</b>ある<b>ほど</b>壊(こわ)れにくい。
The simpler a tool is, the less likely it is to break.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りが近(ちか)づけ<b>ば</b>近(ちか)づく<b>ほど</b>、なぜか部屋(へや)の掃除(そうじ)がしたくなる。
The closer the deadline gets, the more I somehow want to clean my room.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の前(まえ)で話(はな)すのは、準備(じゅんび)をすれ<b>ば</b>する<b>ほど</b>落(お)ち着(つ)いて話(はな)せるようになりますよ。
When it comes to speaking in public, the more you prepare, the more calmly you'll be able to talk.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern itself is register-neutral. Politeness lives on the final verb, so
<code>安(やす)ければ安(やす)いほどいい</code> and
<code>安(やす)ければ安(やす)いほどいいです</code> are the same grammar at
different levels, and both are perfectly ordinary in conversation.

Because it states a proportion rather than an event, it is the natural shape for
advice, opinions, slogans and rules of thumb - 
<code>早(はや)ければ早(はや)いほどいい</code> is something you will hear said
about applications, hospital visits and train tickets alike. It is equally at home
in a report describing a correlation.

Two useful habits. First, the result half is very often an evaluation:
<code>いい</code>, <code>楽(らく)だ</code>, <code>大変(たいへん)だ</code>,
<code>安心(あんしん)する</code>. Second, the result is frequently a *deterioration*
despite the "more … more" English - <code>考(かんが)えれば考(かんが)えるほど
分(わ)からなくなる</code> is one of the most common sentences built on this
pattern, and English speakers reaching for "the more … the less" should notice that
Japanese puts the "less" in the verb, not in the pattern.

One restriction worth remembering: the two halves must be **the same word**. This
is not a general "if … then" - it is one scale echoing itself. If the cause and the
result are different things, you want a plain `ば`, `と` or `たら` sentence.

## Contrast

### ～ば～ほど vs ～につれて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `につれて` narrates a change that actually took place.
`ば～ほど` states a rule that holds every time, including in situations that have not
happened.

</div>

<pre>
夏(なつ)が近(ちか)づく<b>につれて</b>、店(みせ)の前(まえ)に花(はな)が増(ふ)えてきた。
As summer approached, more and more flowers appeared in front of the shop.
</pre>

That is one shop, one summer. The correlative version is a general truth and can be
said in the middle of winter:

<pre>
気温(きおん)が上(あ)がれ<b>ば</b>上(あ)がる<b>ほど</b>、アイスがよく売(う)れる。
The higher the temperature, the better ice cream sells.
</pre>

Practical consequence: only `ば～ほど` combines comfortably with advice, guesses
and conditions - <code>早(はや)く申(もう)し込(こ)めば申(もう)し込(こ)む
ほど安(やす)くなりますよ</code>. Try that with `につれて` and it sounds like you
are describing prices you have already watched fall.

### ～ば～ほど vs the shortened ～ほど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `ば` half can be dropped when the word is an adjective and
the result is a simple evaluation. Dropping it is shorter and slightly more written;
keeping it adds emphasis.

</div>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は明(あか)るい<b>ほど</b>いい。
The brighter the room, the better.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は明(あか)るけれ<b>ば</b>明(あか)るい<b>ほど</b>いい。
The brighter the room, the better it is - really, as bright as you can get it.
</pre>

The short version reads like a specification; the full version sounds like someone
insisting. With verbs the short form is much rarer, and
❌<code>練習(れんしゅう)するほど上手(じょうず)になる</code> can be understood
but sounds clipped - say the verb twice.

### ～ば～ほど vs どんどん・ますます

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ば～ほど` states a **relationship between two scales**.
`どんどん` and `ますます` are adverbs that simply say a single thing keeps
increasing, with no second quantity attached.

</div>

<pre>
待(ま)っている人(ひと)が<b>どんどん</b>増(ふ)えてきた。
The number of people waiting kept growing.
</pre>

Nothing is being proportioned there - it is one line on a graph going up. Compare:

<pre>
人気(にんき)が出(で)れ<b>ば</b>出(で)る<b>ほど</b>、予約(よやく)が取(と)りにくくなる。
The more popular it gets, the harder it becomes to get a reservation.
</pre>

Two scales, tied together. The two often appear in the same sentence, and that is
fine: <code>練習(れんしゅう)すればするほどどんどん上手(じょうず)になる</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 読(よ)む<b>ば</b>読(よ)む<b>ほど</b> → ✅ 読(よ)め<b>ば</b>読(よ)む<b>ほど</b>
  The first copy of the verb has to be the `ば` form. Only the second one stays in
  the dictionary form.

- ❌ 高(たか)<b>いければ</b>高(たか)い<b>ほど</b> → ✅ 高(たか)<b>ければ</b>高(たか)い<b>ほど</b>
  An い-adjective drops its final `い` before `ければ`. The `い` survives only on the
  second copy, in front of `ほど`.

- ❌ 食(た)べれ<b>ば</b>太(ふと)る<b>ほど</b> → ✅ 食(た)べれ<b>ば</b>食(た)べる<b>ほど</b>太(ふと)る
  The word before `ば` and the word before `ほど` must be the same one. The
  consequence goes at the end of the sentence.

- ❌ 便利(べんり)なら便利(べんり)**ならほど** → ✅ 便利(べんり)なら便利(べんり)**なほど**
  What sits in front of `ほど` is the noun-modifying form, so a な-adjective needs
  its `な`. The formal alternative is
  <code>便利(べんり)であればあるほど</code>.

## Practice

**1.** この問題(もんだい)は急(いそ)____急(いそ)ぐほど間違(まちが)えやすい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

急(いそ)<b>げば</b>急(いそ)ぐほど間違(まちが)えやすい。

<code>急(いそ)ぐ</code> is an う-verb, so the `ば` form replaces the final `ぐ`
with `げば`. The second copy is left in the dictionary form because `ほど` attaches
to a noun-modifying form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 子(こ)どもは大(おお)きくなる（につれて・ばなるほど）、親(おや)と話(はな)さなくなる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)どもは大(おお)きくなる<b>につれて</b>、親(おや)と話(はな)さなくなる。

The slot in the sentence already contains a full dictionary-form verb, so only
`につれて` can attach there. To use the correlative you would have to rebuild the
whole thing as
<code>大(おお)きくなればなるほど</code>, with the verb appearing twice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The more expensive the wine, the more carefully everyone drinks it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ワインは高(たか)けれ<b>ば</b>高(たか)い<b>ほど</b>、みんな大切(たいせつ)に飲(の)む。

<code>高(たか)い</code> supplies both halves - `ければ` first, plain `い` second - 
and the consequence, everyone's carefulness, comes last. Note that Japanese needs no
comparative form on either adjective.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この仕事(しごと)は慣(な)れるば慣(な)れるほど楽(らく)になります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この仕事(しごと)は慣(な)れ<b>れば</b>慣(な)れる<b>ほど</b>楽(らく)になります。

<code>慣(な)れる</code> is a る-verb, so its `ば` form is
<code>慣(な)れれば</code> - you cannot simply glue `ば` onto the dictionary form.
The second copy is correct as it stands.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 資料(しりょう)は多(おお)い<b>ほど</b>いい。
(b) 資料(しりょう)は多(おお)けれ<b>ば</b>多(おお)い<b>ほど</b>読(よ)むのが大変(たいへん)になる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the clipped, specification-like form: more material is better, full stop. It
is the kind of line you would write in a note or say when handing out instructions.

(b) spells the scale out twice, which gives it more force, and its result half runs
the other way - the same increase that (a) welcomes is the thing making the work
harder. Both are proportional statements; only the evaluation differs.

</details>

# ～ほど

**Meaning** · to the extent that ～; so much that ～ - and, with numbers, "about"

`ほど` measures. Its main job at N3 is to state *how much* by describing what that
amount is enough to cause: you were tired **to the point of not being able to
stand**, the room was quiet **to the point that it was frightening**. Attached to a
number it does something simpler and softer - it turns the number into an
approximation, "about".

## Formation

The measuring clause comes **before** `ほど`, and the thing being measured comes
after. That is the reverse of the English order, and it is the single most important
fact on this page.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | V(plain) + ほど | 歩(ある)けない → 歩(ある)けないほど疲(つか)れた |
| い-adjective | ～い + ほど | 恥(は)ずかしい → 恥(は)ずかしいほど赤(あか)くなった |
| な-adjective | ～な + ほど | 不思議(ふしぎ) → 不思議(ふしぎ)なほどよく眠(ねむ)れた |
| Noun used as a yardstick | Noun + ほど | 山(やま) → 山(やま)ほどある |
| Number / quantity | Number + ほど（≒ぐらい） | 三日(みっか) → 三日(みっか)ほど休(やす)む |
| Modifying a noun | ～ほど + の + Noun | 涙(なみだ)が出(で)るほどの話(はなし) |
| Proportional | ～ば～ほど | 安(やす)ければ安(やす)いほど |
| Negative comparison | A ほど B ない | 去年(きょねん)ほど寒(さむ)くない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English says "so X that Y"; Japanese says "Y ほど X". Build the
sentence by writing the vivid consequence first, then `ほど`, then the plain
statement it is decorating.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)まで十分(じゅっぷん)<b>ほど</b>かかります。
It takes about ten minutes to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は宿題(しゅくだい)が山(やま)<b>ほど</b>ある。
I've got a mountain of homework today.
</pre>

<pre>
声(こえ)が出(で)ない<b>ほど</b>叫(さけ)んでしまった。
I yelled so much I lost my voice.
</pre>

<pre>
信(しん)じられない<b>ほど</b>静(しず)かな朝(あさ)だった。
It was an unbelievably quiet morning.
</pre>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)の知(し)らせを聞(き)いて、泣(な)きたくなる<b>ほど</b>うれしかった。
When I heard I'd passed, I was so happy I could have cried.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は立(た)っていられない<b>ほど</b>風(かぜ)が強(つよ)い。
The wind today is strong enough to knock you off your feet.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)の話(はなし)は、時計(とけい)を見(み)たくなる<b>ほど</b>長(なが)かった。
The manager's speech was long enough to have you checking your watch.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)は一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>ほど</b>出張(しゅっちょう)ですので、打(う)ち合(あ)わせは再来週(さらいしゅう)にしていただけませんか。
I'll be away on business for about a week, so could we make the meeting the week after next?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The degree use is neutral and works in any register, though it is a shade more
written and a shade more emphatic than `ぐらい`. With numbers it is the more formal
of the pair: a shop assistant says
<code>三日(みっか)ほどおまちください</code>, a friend says
<code>三日(みっか)ぐらい待(ま)って</code>.

Because the pattern works by naming a consequence, it invites exaggeration, and
spoken Japanese takes the invitation. <code>死(し)ぬほど眠(ねむ)い</code>,
<code>嫌(いや)になるほど聞(き)いた</code> and
<code>山(やま)ほどある</code> are set phrases you will hear constantly. They are
casual and strong: <code>死(し)ぬほど</code> is fine among friends and out of
place in a meeting, where <code>信(しん)じられないほど</code> or plain
<code>とても</code> is safer.

The consequence clause does not have to be real. Nobody who says
<code>泣(な)きたくなるほどうれしい</code> is claiming to have cried; the clause
is a measuring stick, not a report. This is why it so often appears in the negative
potential - <code>歩(ある)けないほど</code>,
<code>見(み)ていられないほど</code> - describing what the degree *prevented*.

One caution: `ほど` measures a **large** degree. Japanese has a separate job of
marking a small or minimal one - "at least this much", "even just a coffee" - and
that belongs to `ぐらい`. Reaching for `ほど` there produces a sentence native
speakers will not follow.

## Contrast

### ～ほど vs ～くらい・ぐらい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for a **high** degree the two overlap, with `ほど` the stronger
and more formal. For a **low** or minimum degree only `ぐらい` works.

</div>

Overlapping:

<pre>
足(あし)が痛(いた)くなる<b>ほど</b>歩(ある)き回(まわ)った。
I walked around until my feet hurt.
</pre>

Swap in `ぐらい` and you get the same meaning in a lighter, chattier voice. Now the
place they part company:

<pre>
コーヒー<b>ぐらい</b>おごりますよ。
Let me at least buy you a coffee.
</pre>

Here `ぐらい` says "this much and no more, it's a trivial amount", and
❌<code>コーヒーほど</code> is impossible. The same goes for
<code>挨拶(あいさつ)ぐらいしなさい</code> - "the least you could do is say
hello."

### ～ほど vs ～すぎて・～て（result）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same facts, opposite order. `ほど` puts the consequence
**first** and the plain statement last; a `て` or `すぎて` clause puts the cause
first and the consequence last.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は声(こえ)も出(で)ない<b>ほど</b>疲(つか)れていた。
Yesterday I was so tired I couldn't even speak.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は疲(つか)れすぎて、声(こえ)も出(で)なかった。
Yesterday I was too tired, and couldn't even speak.
</pre>

The difference is where the emphasis lands. The `ほど` version is a statement about
**how tired you were**, with the silence used as a ruler. The `すぎて` version is a
narrative: tiredness happened, and then this followed. Learners translating "so … that"
word by word reliably produce the second when they wanted the first.

### ～ほど for degree vs ～ほど～ない for comparison

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at the main predicate. If it is **negative** and the thing
before `ほど` is a rival being measured against, you are reading a comparison, not a
degree.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は眠(ねむ)れない<b>ほど</b>暑(あつ)かった。
Yesterday was so hot I couldn't sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は昨日(きのう)<b>ほど</b>暑(あつ)くない。
Today isn't as hot as yesterday.
</pre>

In the first, <code>眠(ねむ)れない</code> is inside the measuring clause and the
sentence itself is positive. In the second, the negative is on the main adjective and
<code>昨日(きのう)</code> is the standard being beaten. When both readings are
formally available the negative predicate decides it, which is why
<code>山(やま)ほど高(たか)くない</code> means "not as high as a mountain", not
"mountainously un-high".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 疲(つか)れた<b>ほど</b>歩(ある)けなかった。 → ✅ 歩(ある)けない<b>ほど</b>疲(つか)れた。
  The clause before `ほど` is the measuring stick, so the *consequence* goes there.
  English word order pushes learners into writing this one backwards.

- ❌ お茶(ちゃ)<b>ほど</b>飲(の)んでいきませんか。 → ✅ お茶(ちゃ)<b>ぐらい</b>飲(の)んでいきませんか。
  "At least a cup of tea" is a *minimum*, and `ほど` cannot mark a minimum. That is
  `ぐらい` territory.

- ❌ 涙(なみだ)が出(で)るほど<b>な</b>話(はなし) → ✅ 涙(なみだ)が出(で)るほど<b>の</b>話(はなし)
  `ほど` behaves like a noun, so it links to a following noun with `の`.

- ❌ 不思議(ふしぎ)**い**ほど静(しず)かだ。 → ✅ 不思議(ふしぎ)**な**ほど静(しず)かだ。
  A な-adjective keeps its `な` in front of `ほど`, exactly as it would in front of
  any other noun.

## Practice

**1.** 息(いき)が____ほど走(はし)って、なんとか電車(でんしゃ)に間(ま)に合(あ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

息(いき)が<b>できない</b>ほど走(はし)って…

The slot needs a consequence extreme enough to measure the running by, and the
negative potential - literally "to the point of not being able to breathe" - is the
most idiomatic filler for it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 返事(へんじ)（ほど・ぐらい）してくれてもいいのに。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

返事(へんじ)<b>ぐらい</b>してくれてもいいのに。

The speaker is complaining that they did not get even the smallest response, and a
minimum like that is marked with `ぐらい`. `ほど` only measures large degrees, so it
cannot carry "the least you could do".

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The room was so cold that I couldn't take my coat off."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部屋(へや)はコートが脱(ぬ)げない<b>ほど</b>寒(さむ)かった。

Japanese states the consequence first and the plain fact last, so "couldn't take my
coat off" goes in front of `ほど` and <code>寒(さむ)かった</code> ends the
sentence. Writing it in English order would produce the ungrammatical
<code>寒(さむ)かったほど…</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 忙(いそが)しいほど昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べる時間(じかん)もなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べる時間(じかん)もない<b>ほど</b>忙(いそが)しかった。

The two halves were the wrong way round. Skipping lunch is the evidence, so it
belongs in front of `ほど`, and the busyness is what the sentence is actually
asserting, so it goes at the end.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一時間(いちじかん)<b>ほど</b>待(ま)ちました。
(b) 一時間(いちじかん)も待(ま)ちました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral approximation: roughly an hour, no complaint attached. The `ほど`
softens the number, and it is the version you would use in a polite report or when
telling a colleague what happened.

(b) uses `も` to mark the hour as *more than expected* - "I waited a whole hour."
The amount is now the point of the sentence, and the speaker is registering
impatience or surprise.

</details>

# ～ほど～ない

**Meaning** · not as ～ as ～ - the standard side takes ほど, and it is the *bigger* one

This is how Japanese says "A isn't as X as B". The frame is
<code>A は B ほど X ない</code>, and the trap is which noun goes where: `ほど` marks
**B**, the one that *is* more X. English puts the winner last ("Osaka isn't as
crowded as Tokyo"); Japanese puts it in the middle, and the sentence must end in a
negative.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (the standard) | Noun + ほど + ～ない | 兄(あに) → 兄(あに)ほど背(せ)が高(たか)くない |
| Verb (plain) | V(plain) + ほど + ～ない | 話(はな)す → 話(はな)すほど簡単(かんたん)ではない |
| An expectation | V(た) + ほど + ～ない | 思(おも)った → 思(おも)ったほど高(たか)くない |
| い-adjective predicate | ～くない | 寒(さむ)い → 去年(きょねん)ほど寒(さむ)くない |
| な-adjective predicate | ～ではない | 静(しず)か → 夜(よる)ほど静(しず)かではない |
| Noun predicate | ～ではない | 問題(もんだい) → 前回(ぜんかい)ほどの問題(もんだい)ではない |
| "nothing is as ～ as" | Nほど + ～ + Nはない | これ → これほどうれしいことはない |
| Colloquial variant | ほど → くらい | 去年(きょねん)くらい寒(さむ)くない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - whatever ranks **higher** takes `ほど`. Say the English out
loud: "not as X as **B**" - B is the one that gets the particle, and everything
after it must be negative.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)は去年(きょねん)<b>ほど</b>寒(さむ)く<b>ない</b>。
This winter isn't as cold as last year's.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は姉(あね)<b>ほど</b>料理(りょうり)が上手(じょうず)じゃ<b>ない</b>。
I'm not as good a cook as my older sister.
</pre>

<pre>
テストは思(おも)った<b>ほど</b>難(むずか)しく<b>なかった</b>。
The test wasn't as hard as I'd expected.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)は駅前(えきまえ)<b>ほど</b>にぎやかでは<b>ない</b>が、買(か)い物(もの)には困(こま)らない。
This area isn't as lively as the area by the station, but you can get everything you need.
</pre>

<pre>
口(くち)で言(い)う<b>ほど</b>簡単(かんたん)では<b>ありません</b>でした。
It wasn't as easy as it sounds.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)<b>ほど</b>字(じ)のきれいな人(ひと)を見(み)たことが<b>ない</b>。
I've never met anyone whose handwriting is as beautiful as his.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)に教(おし)えること<b>ほど</b>勉強(べんきょう)になることは<b>ない</b>。
There's nothing that teaches you as much as teaching someone else.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい職場(しょくば)は前(まえ)の会社(かいしゃ)<b>ほど</b>残業(ざんぎょう)が多(おお)く<b>ない</b>ので、夕(ゆう)ご飯(はん)を家(いえ)で食(た)べられるようになりました。
The new workplace doesn't have as much overtime as my old company, so I've been able to start eating dinner at home.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. The pattern is as ordinary in a chat as in a report, and the
politeness is set by the ending - 
<code>寒(さむ)くない</code> / <code>寒(さむ)くないです</code> /
<code>寒(さむ)くありません</code>.

What it does carry is a quiet **concession**. Saying
<code>去年(きょねん)ほど寒(さむ)くない</code> grants that last year set a
high bar and places this year below it, without denying that it is cold. That makes
it a polite way to decline a comparison you are being pushed into:
<code>私(わたし)は先輩(せんぱい)ほどできません</code> is modest, where
<code>できません</code> alone would just be flat.

`くらい` / `ぐらい` can replace `ほど` here in casual speech, but the two are not
equally safe. `ぐらい` also has its own "at least, merely" meaning, so
<code>田中(たなか)さんぐらい話(はな)せない</code> can be heard two ways.
`ほど` is unambiguous, and it is what you want in writing.

The pattern's second life is as a **superlative in disguise**:
<code>N ほど … は ない</code>, "there is nothing as … as N". It sounds more
emphatic and more personal than the plain
<code>いちばん</code> superlative, and it is the frame of a great many proverbs and
strong opinions - <code>健康(けんこう)ほど大切(たいせつ)なものはない</code>.

## Contrast

### AはBほど～ない vs AよりBのほうが～

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほど` attaches to the **greater** item and needs a negative.
`より` attaches to the **lesser** item and needs a positive. Get the two mixed up and
you say the opposite of what you meant.

</div>

The same fact, from both ends:

<pre>
バスは電車(でんしゃ)<b>ほど</b>速(はや)く<b>ない</b>。
The bus isn't as fast as the train.
</pre>

<pre>
バス<b>より</b>電車(でんしゃ)の<b>ほうが</b>速(はや)い。
The train is faster than the bus.
</pre>

Notice that <code>電車(でんしゃ)</code>, the faster one, carries `ほど` in the
first sentence and follows `より` in the second. Learners who translate word by word
tend to produce ❌<code>電車(でんしゃ)はバスほど速(はや)くない</code>, which
claims that trains are the slow ones.

There is also a form restriction. Japanese does not like `より` in front of a
negative predicate: ❌<code>私(わたし)は兄(あに)より背(せ)が高(たか)くない</code>
is rejected by most textbooks and sounds wrong to most speakers. If your English has
"not as … as" in it, use `ほど`.

### ～ほど～ない vs あまり／そんなに～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほど～ない` needs a **named standard**. `あまり～ない` and
`そんなに～ない` measure against a vague expectation instead.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は<b>あまり</b>高(たか)く<b>ない</b>。
This place isn't very expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は駅前(えきまえ)の店(みせ)<b>ほど</b>高(たか)く<b>ない</b>。
This place isn't as expensive as the one by the station.
</pre>

The first sets an absolute, if fuzzy, level; the second says nothing at all about
whether the place is cheap - only that another shop is worse. This is why
<code>思(おも)ったほど</code> is so common: it names your own expectation as the
standard, which is the most useful yardstick a speaker has.

### Nほど～はない vs the いちばん superlative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いちばん` ranks inside a stated group and is neutral.
`N ほど～はない` claims **nothing anywhere** beats N, and it is an opinion with
feeling behind it.

</div>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の山(やま)で飲(の)む水(みず)<b>ほど</b>おいしいもの<b>はない</b>。
There's nothing as good as water drunk on a mountain in summer.
</pre>

<pre>
飲(の)み物(もの)の中(なか)で水(みず)が<b>いちばん</b>おいしいです。
Of all drinks, water tastes the best.
</pre>

The second is a survey result; the first is a declaration. Note the shape of the
`ほど` version: the noun being praised comes first, and the sentence ends with a
negated <code>ない</code> plus a general noun such as
<code>もの</code>, <code>こと</code>, <code>人(ひと)</code> or
<code>所(ところ)</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 富士山(ふじさん)は高尾山(たかおさん)<b>ほど</b>高(たか)くない。 → ✅ 高尾山(たかおさん)は富士山(ふじさん)<b>ほど</b>高(たか)くない。
  `ほど` goes on the taller mountain. Written the first way, the sentence says Fuji
  is the smaller one.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は兄(あに)<b>より</b>背(せ)が高(たか)くない。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は兄(あに)<b>ほど</b>背(せ)が高(たか)くない。
  `より` does not sit comfortably in front of a negative. "Not as … as" is a `ほど`
  sentence.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は昨日(きのう)<b>ほど</b>暑(あつ)いです。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は昨日(きのう)<b>ほど</b>暑(あつ)くないです。
  This `ほど` only exists inside a negative sentence. With a positive predicate the
  sentence has no meaning.

- ❌ 健康(けんこう)<b>ほど</b>大切(たいせつ)なものが<b>ある</b>。 → ✅ 健康(けんこう)<b>ほど</b>大切(たいせつ)なもの<b>はない</b>。
  The superlative frame ends in <code>はない</code>. Its whole force comes from
  denying that anything else measures up.

## Practice

**1.** 弟(おとうと)は兄(あに)____背(せ)が高(たか)くない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弟(おとうと)は兄(あに)<b>ほど</b>背(せ)が高(たか)くない。

The older brother is the taller of the two, so he is the standard and takes `ほど`.
The sentence then has to close in the negative, which is what turns it into "not as
tall as".

</details>

**2.** Choose: 北海道(ほっかいどう)の冬(ふゆ)は東京(とうきょう)（ほど・より）寒(さむ)くない。 - is this sentence true to what you mean if you think Hokkaido is colder?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Neither version says what you mean. With `ほど`, the sentence reads "Hokkaido's
winter isn't as cold as Tokyo's", which is the wrong way round; `より` cannot take a
negative predicate at all. To say Hokkaido is colder, flip it:
<b>東京(とうきょう)の冬(ふゆ)は北海道(ほっかいどう)ほど寒(さむ)くない</b>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My new apartment isn't as quiet as the old one."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しいアパートは前(まえ)のアパート<b>ほど</b>静(しず)かでは<b>ない</b>。

The old flat is the quieter one, so it is the standard and takes `ほど`. Because
<code>静(しず)か</code> is a な-adjective, the negative is
<code>ではない</code> (casually <code>じゃない</code>), not
<code>静(しず)かくない</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この映画(えいが)ほどおもしろい映画(えいが)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この映画(えいが)<b>ほど</b>おもしろい映画(えいが)は<b>ありません</b>。

The <code>Nほど…はない</code> frame is a superlative built out of a denial, so it
cannot end positively. As written, the original says something like "there is a film
as interesting as this one", which is the opposite of the intended praise.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この仕事(しごと)は思(おも)った<b>ほど</b>大変(たいへん)じゃ<b>ない</b>。
(b) この仕事(しごと)は<b>あまり</b>大変(たいへん)じゃ<b>ない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) measures the job against your own earlier expectation. It admits you were
braced for something worse, and it therefore says the job *is* demanding - just less
so than you feared.

(b) has no standard in it at all. It simply rates the job low on an absolute scale:
not much trouble. Someone who had never thought about the job before could say (b),
but only someone with a prior expectation can say (a).

</details>

# ～に比べて

**Meaning** · compared with ～; against the standard of ～

<code>～に比(くら)べて</code> sets a backdrop. It names the thing you are measuring against,
then leaves the rest of the sentence free to say whatever you like about the other
side - bigger, cheaper, quieter, twenty per cent down. Unlike `より`, which has to
sit next to a comparative predicate, <code>に比(くら)べて</code> hands you a whole clause to
work in.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に比(くら)べて | 去年(きょねん) → 去年(きょねん)に比(くら)べて |
| Noun (mutual comparison) | Noun + と比(くら)べて | 兄(あに) → 兄(あに)と比(くら)べて |
| Clause | V(plain) + の + に比(くら)べて | 歩(ある)く → 歩(ある)くのに比(くら)べて |
| Noun clause | ～のに比(くら)べて | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いのに比(くら)べて |
| Formal writing | ～に比(くら)べ | 昨年(さくねん) → 昨年(さくねん)に比(くら)べ |
| Softer, spoken | ～に比(くら)べると／～に比(くら)べたら | 都会(とかい) → 都会(とかい)に比(くら)べると |
| As an ordinary verb | A と B を比(くら)べる | 値段(ねだん)と質(しつ)を比(くら)べる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the particle is `に` or `と`, never `を`. `を` belongs to the plain verb
<code>比(くら)べる</code> and marks the *pair* being compared:
<code>二(ふた)つのプランを比(くら)べる</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
去年(きょねん)<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、今年(ことし)は雨(あめ)が少(すく)ない。
Compared with last year, there's been little rain this year.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、弟(おとうと)はよくしゃべる。
Compared with his older brother, the younger one is quite the talker.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)のモデル<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、音(おと)がずいぶん静(しず)かになりました。
Compared with the previous model, it's got a lot quieter.
</pre>

<pre>
都会(とかい)<b>に比(くら)べると</b>、この町(まち)は時間(じかん)の流(なが)れがゆっくりしている。
Compared with the city, time moves slowly in this town.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)で行(い)くの<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、車(くるま)は時間(じかん)が読(よ)めない。
Compared with going by train, you can't predict how long the car will take.
</pre>

<pre>
十年前(じゅうねんまえ)<b>に比(くら)べ</b>、市内(しない)の人口(じんこう)は二割(にわり)減(へ)っている。
Compared with ten years ago, the population of the city is down twenty per cent.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、電気(でんき)代(だい)が倍(ばい)になって驚(おどろ)いた。
Compared with last summer, the electricity bill has doubled - I was shocked.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ時期(じき)に入(はい)った人(ひと)たち<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、自分(じぶん)だけ仕事(しごと)が遅(おそ)い気(き)がして落(お)ち込(こ)んでいます。
Compared with the others who started at the same time, I feel like I'm the only slow one, and it's getting me down.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral, with a slight lean towards the explanatory: news bulletins, sales reports,
weather summaries and any sentence with a percentage in it. It is perfectly normal in
conversation too, especially in the <code>に比(くら)べると</code> and
<code>に比(くら)べたら</code> shapes, which soften it and sound more like personal
impression than analysis.

Clipping to <code>に比(くら)べ</code> is written-only, one notch up in formality, and
extremely common in newspapers: <code>前年(ぜんねん)に比(くら)べ三(さん)パーセント増(ふ)えた</code>.

`に` and `と` are close to interchangeable, with a tendency: `に` frames one thing as
the fixed reference point you are working from, while `と` puts the two side by side
as equals. <code>去年(きょねん)に比(くら)べて</code> takes last year as the
baseline; <code>兄(あに)と比(くら)べて</code> sounds more like holding two
people up against each other, and it is the one that can carry an edge of
"why are you comparing us?"

One phrase worth having ready: <code>～に比(くら)べれば</code> means "compared with that,
this is nothing" - <code>去年(きょねん)の忙(いそが)しさに比(くら)べれば、
今(いま)は楽(らく)なものだ</code>. It is a standard way of putting a
complaint in perspective, your own or someone else's.

## Contrast

### ～に比(くら)べて vs ～より

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `より` is glued to a comparative statement and needs a gradable
predicate. `に比べて` sets up a frame and then lets you say anything at all.

</div>

Where a single adjective is doing the work, both are fine and `より` is lighter:

<pre>
地下鉄(ちかてつ)はバス<b>より</b>速(はや)い。
The subway is faster than the bus.
</pre>

But `に比べて` can head a whole clause, and even a clause with no comparative in it:

<pre>
バス<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、地下鉄(ちかてつ)は時間(じかん)が読(よ)めるので安心(あんしん)だ。
Compared with the bus, the subway runs to time, so it's less stressful.
</pre>

❌<code>バスより安心(あんしん)だ</code> strains, because
<code>安心(あんしん)だ</code> is not being measured on a scale - the sentence is
describing a situation, not ranking two numbers. A second practical difference:
`に比べて` normally opens the sentence, which makes it the natural choice when the
comparison is the topic of a paragraph.

### ～に比(くら)べて vs ～に対(たい)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に比べて` puts two things on **one scale**. `に対して` sets
them in **opposition**, "whereas", with no shared measure required.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、新入生(しんにゅうせい)の数(かず)が増(ふ)えた。
Compared with last year, the number of new students has gone up.
</pre>

Numbers, one axis. Now two facts that share no axis at all:

<pre>
姉(あね)が外(そと)で遊(あそ)ぶのが好(す)きなの<b>に対(たい)して</b>、妹(いもうと)は一日中(いちにちじゅう)本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいる。
Where my older sister loves being outdoors, my younger sister reads all day.
</pre>

Nothing is more or less than anything here; the two halves are simply different.
Substituting `に比べて` would suggest reading and playing outside are two settings of
one dial, which is not what the speaker means.

### ～に比(くら)べて vs ～と違(ちが)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `に比べて` is a difference of **degree**. `と違って` is a
difference of **kind**: A does this, B does not, and there is no "more" about it.

</div>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)の店(みせ)<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、こちらは値段(ねだん)が安(やす)い。
Compared with the shops in Tokyo, things here are cheap.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)の店(みせ)<b>と違(ちが)って</b>、この店(みせ)は日曜(にちよう)が休(やす)みだ。
Unlike the shops in Tokyo, this one closes on Sundays.
</pre>

Closing on Sunday is not a lesser amount of anything, so it takes <code>と違(ちが)って</code>.
A quick test: if you could add "by twenty per cent" to the English, you want
<code>に比(くら)べて</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)<b>を</b>比(くら)べて、今年(ことし)は暑(あつ)い。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)<b>に</b>比(くら)べて、今年(ことし)は暑(あつ)い。
  The standard is marked with `に` or `と`. `を` would make last year the object
  being examined, which needs a second object to pair it with.

- ❌ 歩(ある)く<b>に</b>比(くら)べて自転車(じてんしゃ)は楽(らく)だ。 → ✅ 歩(ある)く<b>のに</b>比(くら)べて自転車(じてんしゃ)は楽(らく)だ。
  A verb has to be nominalised with `の` before <code>に比(くら)べて</code> can attach to it.

- ❌ 電車(でんしゃ)に比(くら)べて、バス<b>より</b>安(やす)い。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)に比(くら)べて、バス<b>は</b>安(やす)い。
  One comparison, one marker. Once <code>に比(くら)べて</code> has named the standard, `より`
  is redundant and the sentence reads as if it had two of them.

- ❌ 姉(あね)<b>に</b>比(くら)べて、妹(いもうと)はめがねをかけていない。 → ✅ 姉(あね)<b>と違(ちが)って</b>、妹(いもうと)はめがねをかけていない。
  Wearing glasses is not a matter of degree, so there is nothing to compare. That is
  a <code>と違(ちが)って</code> sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 先月(せんげつ)____比(くら)べて、今月(こんげつ)は残業(ざんぎょう)が少(すく)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先月(せんげつ)<b>に</b>比(くら)べて、今月(こんげつ)は残業(ざんぎょう)が少(すく)ない。

Last month is the fixed reference point, and a reference point takes `に`.
<code>と</code> is also grammatical here but would present the two months as rivals
rather than as a baseline and a result.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 姉(あね)は文系(ぶんけい)なの（に比(くら)べて・に対(たい)して）、弟(おとうと)は理系(りけい)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

姉(あね)は文系(ぶんけい)なの<b>に対(たい)して</b>、弟(おとうと)は理系(りけい)だ。

Arts and sciences are two different categories, not two points on one scale, so the
sentence is contrasting rather than measuring. <code>に比(くら)べて</code> would imply one
of them has more of something than the other.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Compared with last week, there are far fewer people in the park."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

先週(せんしゅう)<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、公園(こうえん)の人(ひと)がずいぶん少(すく)ない。

The comparison heads the sentence, exactly as "compared with" does in English, and
the rest is a plain statement - no `より` and no comparative form needed anywhere
else in the sentence.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しいパソコンは古(ふる)いのを比(くら)べて動(うご)きが速(はや)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しいパソコンは古(ふる)いの<b>に比(くら)べて</b>動(うご)きが速(はや)い。

The standard takes `に`, not `を`. With `を` the sentence would need the shape
<code>古(ふる)いのと新(あたら)しいのを比(くら)べる</code>, "compare the old
one and the new one", which is a different sentence altogether.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 去年(きょねん)<b>に比(くら)べて</b>、今年(ことし)は忙(いそが)しい。
(b) 去年(きょねん)<b>に比(くら)べれば</b>、今年(ことし)は楽(らく)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain measurement: this year scores higher on busyness than last year did.
It could open a report and carries no particular feeling.

(b) uses the conditional form, which adds a "when you put it that way" tone. It
concedes that this year is hard but insists last year was worse, and it is the
standard phrasing for talking yourself, or someone else, out of a complaint.

</details>

# ～一方だ

**Meaning** · keeps going in one direction; more and more ～, with no sign of turning back

<code>～一方(いっぽう)だ</code> reports a **trend**. Something is moving along a scale - 
rising, falling, worsening, spreading - and the speaker's point is that it has been
moving that way steadily and shows no sign of stopping. It is the sentence-final
shape of the newspaper headline "costs continue to climb".

## Formation

Only verbs that contain a direction of change can take it. What goes in front is
always the dictionary form; tense and politeness are carried by the
<code>だ</code> at the end.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb of change | V(dictionary) + 一方(いっぽう)だ | 増(ふ)える → 増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)だ |
| Polite | ～一方(いっぽう)です | 減(へ)る → 減(へ)る一方(いっぽう)です |
| Past | ～一方(いっぽう)だった | 下(さ)がる → 下(さ)がる一方(いっぽう)だった |
| い-adjective (via なる) | ～くなる + 一方(いっぽう)だ | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しくなる一方(いっぽう)だ |
| な-adjective / Noun (via なる) | ～になる + 一方(いっぽう)だ | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)になる一方(いっぽう)だ |
| Continuing the sentence | ～一方(いっぽう)で、… | 増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)で、手(て)が足(た)りない |
| Modifying a noun | ～一方(いっぽう)の + Noun | 増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)の仕事(しごと) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the verb cannot be pictured as an arrow on a graph, it cannot
take <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code>. Adjectives and nouns get in only by first becoming a
change verb with <code>なる</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
物価(ぶっか)は上(あ)がる<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
Prices just keep going up.
</pre>

<pre>
この村(むら)の人口(じんこう)は減(へ)る<b>一方(いっぽう)です</b>。
The population of this village keeps falling.
</pre>

<pre>
待(ま)っているだけでは、状況(じょうきょう)は悪(わる)くなる<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
If we just wait, the situation will only get worse.
</pre>

<pre>
スマートフォンのおかげで、生活(せいかつ)は便利(べんり)になる<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
Thanks to smartphones, life keeps getting more convenient.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づき、雨(あめ)は強(つよ)くなる<b>一方(いっぽう)だった</b>。
As the typhoon drew closer, the rain just kept getting heavier.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしを始(はじ)めてから、コンビニに行(い)く回数(かいすう)が増(ふ)える<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
Since I started living alone, the number of trips I make to the convenience store has done nothing but go up.
</pre>

<pre>
医療(いりょう)費(ひ)は増(ふ)える<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、若(わか)い世代(せだい)の負担(ふたん)が重(おも)くなっている。
Medical costs keep on rising, and the burden on the younger generation is growing heavier.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)を何度(なんど)開(ひら)いても結論(けつろん)が出(で)なくて、メンバーの不満(ふまん)は大(おお)きくなる<b>一方(いっぽう)でした</b>。
However many meetings we held, no conclusion came out of them, and the members' frustration only grew.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a written-leaning pattern. It belongs to news copy, reports, essays and the
kind of speech that summarises a situation; in relaxed conversation the same idea
comes out as <code>どんどん増(ふ)えてる</code> or
<code>だんだん悪(わる)くなってる</code>. Saying
<code>宿題(しゅくだい)が増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)だ</code> to a friend is not
wrong, but it has a faintly editorial ring - which can be the joke.

The tone is **observational**. The speaker is standing back and reporting a curve,
not reacting to it, and that is why the pattern sits so easily next to numbers and
statistics. It is often used for unwelcome trends, simply because unwelcome trends
are what people write about, but there is nothing negative in the grammar itself:
<code>便利(べんり)になる一方(いっぽう)だ</code> and
<code>技術(ぎじゅつ)は進(すす)む一方(いっぽう)だ</code> are perfectly
ordinary.

One structural point. The subject of <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> is normally a situation
or a quantity rather than a person choosing to do something. A trend is not a
decision, so <code>毎日(まいにち)走(はし)る一方(いっぽう)だ</code> does not
work as "I keep running every day" - what can rise is
<code>走(はし)る距離(きょり)</code>, not the act itself.

## Contrast

### ～一方(いっぽう)だ vs ～ばかりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "only goes one way". <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> is
neutral and can describe a welcome trend; `ばかりだ` is almost always about
something getting worse, and it sounds more emotional.

</div>

<pre>
輸出(ゆしゅつ)は伸(の)びる<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
Exports keep growing.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)を待(ま)つ間(あいだ)、不安(ふあん)は大(おお)きくなる<b>ばかりだった</b>。
While I waited for a reply, my anxiety did nothing but grow.
</pre>

Swapping them is not ungrammatical but does change the colour.
<code>輸出(ゆしゅつ)は伸(の)びるばかりだ</code> makes growth sound like a
runaway problem, and <code>不安(ふあん)は大(おお)きくなる一方(いっぽう)
だった</code> makes your own anxiety sound like a line on a chart. Choose by whether
you are reporting or complaining.

### ～一方(いっぽう)だ vs ～ていく・～てくる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～ていく</code> and <code>～てくる</code> simply track a
change over time. <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> adds the claim that it has gone **only** that
way and is still going.

</div>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの数(かず)が少(すく)なくなっ<b>ていく</b>。
The number of children is decreasing.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの数(かず)は少(すく)なくなる<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
The number of children just keeps decreasing.
</pre>

The first is a neutral description that leaves room for the line to level off; the
second insists it has not and will not. `～ていく` is also the everyday spoken form
and works with any verb at all, while <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> demands a verb of change
and a register to match.

### ～一方(いっぽう)だ vs ～一方(いっぽう)（で） - "on the other hand"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - at the **end** of a sentence, <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> is a trend.
Between two clauses that describe **different** things, <code>一方(いっぽう)（で）</code> means
"meanwhile, on the other hand".

</div>

<pre>
田舎(いなか)の土地(とち)は安(やす)くなる<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
Land in the countryside just keeps getting cheaper.
</pre>

<pre>
都心(としん)の家賃(やちん)は上(あ)がっている。<b>一方(いっぽう)</b>、地方(ちほう)では空(あ)き家(や)が増(ふ)えている。
Rents in the city centre are rising. Meanwhile, empty houses are increasing in rural areas.
</pre>

The two are told apart by position and by what surrounds them. A sentence-final
<code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> after a dictionary-form verb of change is the trend
reading; a clause-initial <code>一方(いっぽう)</code>, or
<code>～一方(いっぽう)で</code> joining two clauses about *different* subjects, is
the contrast reading. The tricky middle case - 
<code>増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)で、人手(ひとで)が足(た)りない</code> - is
the trend pattern in its て-form, and you can tell because both halves are about the
same rising thing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 人口(じんこう)が<b>少(すく)ない</b>一方(いっぽう)だ。 → ✅ 人口(じんこう)が<b>減(へ)る</b>一方(いっぽう)だ。
  An adjective describes a state, and a state is not a trend. You need a verb with
  movement in it, or an adjective converted with <code>なる</code>.

- ❌ 生活(せいかつ)が<b>苦(くる)しい</b>一方(いっぽう)です。 → ✅ 生活(せいかつ)が<b>苦(くる)しくなる</b>一方(いっぽう)です。
  Same problem in polite clothing. <code>～くなる</code> is the bridge from an
  い-adjective to a change verb.

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)から売上(うりあげ)が<b>増(ふ)えた</b>一方(いっぽう)だ。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)から売上(うりあげ)が<b>増(ふ)える</b>一方(いっぽう)だ。
  The verb stays in the dictionary form. If you need the past, put it on the end:
  <code>増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)だった</code>.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は<b>毎日(まいにち)走(はし)る</b>一方(いっぽう)だ。 → ✅ 走(はし)る<b>距離(きょり)が伸(の)びる</b>一方(いっぽう)だ。
  A habit you have chosen is not a trend that is happening to you. The subject has to
  be something that can rise or fall on its own.

</details>

# ～ばかりだ

**Meaning** · things only go one way, and it is the wrong way - "it just keeps getting ～"

Put a verb of change in front of `ばかりだ` and you get a one-directional slide with
a complaint attached. The pain only gets sharper, the misunderstanding only grows,
the field only gets more overgrown. Where <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> reports a trend from
a distance, `ばかりだ` is the voice of someone watching it happen and not liking it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb of change | V(dictionary) + ばかりだ | 悪(わる)くなる → 悪(わる)くなるばかりだ |
| Polite | ～ばかりです | 増(ふ)える → 増(ふ)えるばかりです |
| Past | ～ばかりだった | 深(ふか)くなる → 深(ふか)くなるばかりだった |
| Continuing the sentence | ～ばかりで、… | 荒(あ)れる → 荒(あ)れるばかりで、人(ひと)が来(こ)ない |
| い-adjective (via なる) | ～くなる + ばかりだ | ひどい → ひどくなるばかりだ |
| な-adjective / Noun (via なる) | ～になる + ばかりだ | 不安(ふあん) → 不安(ふあん)になるばかりだ |
| "all that is left" | あとは + V(dictionary) + ばかりだ | 待(ま)つ → あとは待(ま)つばかりだ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb in front stays in the **dictionary form**. Swap it for the
た-form and you have the completely different N4 pattern
<code>～たばかりだ</code>, "have just done": <code>着(つ)いたばかりだ</code> means
"I've only just arrived".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
痛(いた)みは強(つよ)くなる<b>ばかりだ</b>。
The pain just keeps getting worse.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)も言(い)わなければ、誤解(ごかい)は大(おお)きくなる<b>ばかりです</b>。
If you say nothing, the misunderstanding will only grow.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)し合(あ)っても、二人(ふたり)の溝(みぞ)は深(ふか)くなる<b>ばかりだった</b>。
However much they talked, the gap between them only got deeper.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んでも、せきはひどくなる<b>ばかりだった</b>。
Even with the medicine, my cough did nothing but get worse.
</pre>

<pre>
畑(はたけ)は荒(あ)れる<b>ばかりで</b>、手入(てい)れをする人(ひと)がいない。
The fields just get more overgrown, and there's no one to look after them.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)は終(お)わりました。あとは本番(ほんばん)を待(ま)つ<b>ばかりです</b>。
The preparations are done. All that's left now is to wait for the real thing.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)が減(へ)った商店街(しょうてんがい)では、シャッターの閉(し)まった店(みせ)が増(ふ)える<b>ばかりだ</b>。
In a shopping street that has lost its people, the number of shuttered shops does nothing but grow.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)は全部(ぜんぶ)車(くるま)に積(つ)んだので、あとは明日(あした)の朝(あさ)出発(しゅっぱつ)する<b>ばかりです</b>。
The luggage is all in the car, so all that remains is to set off tomorrow morning.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ばかりだ` in this sense is more literary than
<code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code>. It is at home in narration, in a written complaint, in a
doctor's notes and in the sentence a novel uses to end a paragraph;
in ordinary chat people say <code>どんどんひどくなってる</code>. Using it aloud gives
what you say a slightly grave, resigned colour - which is often exactly the point.

It carries an **evaluation**, and the evaluation is negative. That is the clearest
difference from <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code>, which is happy to report good news.
Native speakers find <code>元気(げんき)になるばかりだ</code> odd for a recovering
patient, because the pattern is reaching for the wrong emotional register; the trend
version of that sentence is
<code>元気(げんき)になる一方(いっぽう)だ</code>.

Two form notes. The casual contraction `ばっかり` belongs to the "nothing but"
`ばかり` you met at N4 - <code>マンガばっかり読(よ)んでる</code> - and is rarely
heard in this trend use, where the tone is too serious for it. And the
`あとは～ばかりだ` frame, "everything is done except this", is a set expression with a
faintly formal, tidy feel; the everyday equivalent is
<code>あとは～だけだ</code>.

## Contrast

### ～ばかりだ vs ～一方(いっぽう)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same one-way movement, different speaker. <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code>
reports; `ばかりだ` complains. Only <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> can be used about a change
you welcome.

</div>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)は進(すす)む<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
Technology keeps advancing.
</pre>

<pre>
待(ま)たされて、いらいらが増(ふ)える<b>ばかりだった</b>。
Being kept waiting, I only got more and more irritated.
</pre>

You could put `ばかりだ` on the first sentence, but it would turn progress into a
threat. Going the other way,
<code>いらいらが増(ふ)える一方(いっぽう)だった</code> sounds like a report on
someone else's mood. Register differs too: <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> is the newspaper,
`ばかりだ` is the diary.

### 変化(へんか)の動詞(どうし) vs 動作(どうさ)の動詞(どうし) in front of ばかりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb decides the meaning. A verb of **change** gives "keeps
getting worse". A verb of **action** gives "does nothing but ～".

</div>

<pre>
状況(じょうきょう)は悪(わる)くなる<b>ばかりだ</b>。
The situation only gets worse.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は文句(もんく)を言(い)う<b>ばかりで</b>、何(なに)も手伝(てつだ)ってくれない。
All he does is complain - he doesn't help with anything.
</pre>

<code>悪(わる)くなる</code> moves along a scale, so the first is a trend.
<code>言(い)う</code> is something a person chooses to do, so the second is about
exclusivity: complaining and nothing else. With an ongoing action the more common
shape is <code>～てばかりいる</code> - 
<code>文句(もんく)を言(い)ってばかりいる</code> - and the two are close, with
`ばかりで` leaning towards "…and therefore nothing else gets done".

### V(dictionary)ばかりだ vs V(た)ばかりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - dictionary form before `ばかり` = a change that keeps going.
た-form before `ばかり` = something that finished a moment ago.

</div>

<pre>
外(そと)は暗(くら)くなる<b>ばかりだ</b>。
It's only getting darker outside.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べた<b>ばかりで</b>、まだおなかがいっぱいだ。
I've only just eaten lunch, so I'm still full.
</pre>

One string, two grammars, and the only visible difference is the form of the verb.
Read the verb ending before you decide which one you are looking at - this is one of
the most reliable ways to misread an N3 sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 寒(さむ)い**ばかりだ**。（meaning "it keeps getting colder"） → ✅ 寒(さむ)くなる**ばかりだ**。
  The pattern needs a verb with movement in it. An い-adjective must first become
  <code>～くなる</code>.

- ❌ 病気(びょうき)は<b>良(よ)くなるばかりだ</b>。 → ✅ 病気(びょうき)は<b>良(よ)くなる一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
  Not a form error but a tone error: `ばかりだ` frames the change as unwelcome, so
  using it about a recovery sounds contradictory.

- ❌ あとは出発(しゅっぱつ)**した**ばかりだ。 → ✅ あとは出発(しゅっぱつ)**する**ばかりだ。
  In the "all that's left" frame the verb is still in the future, so it takes the
  dictionary form. With the た-form the sentence flips to "we've only just left".

- ❌ 不満(ふまん)が<b>大(おお)きくなったばかりだった</b>。 → ✅ 不満(ふまん)が<b>大(おお)きくなるばかりだった</b>。
  Tense lives on <code>だ</code> at the end. The verb in front of `ばかり` stays in
  the dictionary form however far in the past the events are.

## Practice

**1.** 問題(もんだい)を放(ほう)っておくと、状況(じょうきょう)は悪(わる)く____ばかりだ。

`ばかりだ` in this sense attaches to a dictionary-form verb of change, and
<code>なる</code> is what turns the い-adjective <code>悪(わる)い</code> into one.
<code>悪(わる)いばかりだ</code> has no movement in it and cannot express a trend.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 輸出(ゆしゅつ)は伸(の)びる（ばかりだ・一方(いっぽう)だ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Growing exports are good news, and `ばかりだ` colours its trend as unwelcome. The
neutral, report-like <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> is what a newspaper would print
here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Everything's ready - all that's left is to press the button."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

準備(じゅんび)はできました。あとはボタンを押(お)す<b>ばかりです</b>。

The <code>あとは～ばかりだ</code> frame says every other step is finished and one
action remains. The remaining action is still to come, so the verb stays in the
dictionary form.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 雨(あめ)がやまなくて、水(みず)が増(ふ)えたばかりだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 雨(あめ)がやまなくて、水(みず)が増(ふ)える<b>ばかりだった</b>。

As written, the sentence says the water had only just risen. For the trend reading
the verb must be <code>増(ふ)える</code>, with the past tense carried by
<code>だった</code> at the end.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 弟(おとうと)はゲームをする<b>ばかりで</b>、勉強(べんきょう)しない。
(b) 弟(おとうと)の成績(せいせき)は下(さ)がる<b>ばかりだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about exclusivity. <code>する</code> is an action he chooses, so `ばかり`
says that action fills the whole picture and crowds everything else out.

(b) is about direction. <code>下(さ)がる</code> is a movement along a scale, so
`ばかり` says the marks have gone one way only, with the speaker's disapproval
attached. Same word, and the verb type is what tells them apart.

</details>
