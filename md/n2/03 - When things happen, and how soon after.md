# N2 - When things happen, and how soon after


---

# ～て以来

**Meaning** · ever since - from one definite past event, right through to now

<code>以来(いらい)</code> plants a flag at a single moment in the past and then
reports what has been true of the whole stretch between that moment and the
present. It is not a way of saying "and then"; it is a way of saying "and it has
been like that ever since".

## Formation

<code>以来(いらい)</code> is a noun. It takes either a て-form clause or a bare
event noun in front of it - never a dictionary form, and never `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + 以来(いらい) | 入社(にゅうしゃ)する → 入社(にゅうしゃ)して以来(いらい) |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(き)て + 以来(いらい) | 日本(にほん)に来(く)る → 日本(にほん)に来(き)て以来(いらい) |
| Noun (an event) | Noun + 以来(いらい) | 卒業(そつぎょう) → 卒業(そつぎょう)以来(いらい) |
| Noun (a time point) | Noun + 以来(いらい) | 去年(きょねん)の春(はる) → 去年(きょねん)の春(はる)以来(いらい) |
| Demonstrative | それ + 以来(いらい) | それ以来(いらい)、連絡(れんらく)がない |
| ❌ Dictionary form | ❌卒業(そつぎょう)する以来(いらい) | ✅卒業(そつぎょう)して以来(いらい) |
| ❌ Noun + の | ❌卒業(そつぎょう)の以来(いらい) | ✅卒業(そつぎょう)以来(いらい) |
| ❌ Future starting point | ❌来月(らいげつ)入社(にゅうしゃ)して以来(いらい) | ✅来月(らいげつ)入社(にゅうしゃ)してから |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three conditions. The starting event happened **once**, in the
**past**, and the stretch it opens runs **up to now**. Fail any one and you want
`てから`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the main clause normally says what has held true across that whole
stretch: a continuing state, an unbroken habit, or something that has *never*
happened (一度(いちど)も…ていない). A single later incident is possible, but only
when it is measured against the span - 日本(にほん)に来(き)て以来(いらい)、初(はじ)めて雪(ゆき)を見(み)た.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
入社(にゅうしゃ)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、一度(いちど)も遅刻(ちこく)していない。
I haven't been late once since I joined the company.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)引(ひ)っ越(こ)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、彼(かれ)とは会(あ)っていない。
I haven't seen him since I moved last year.
</pre>

<pre>
あの映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、飛行機(ひこうき)が苦手(にがて)になった。
Ever since I saw that film, flying has made me uneasy.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は退院(たいいん)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、毎朝(まいあさ)の散歩(さんぽ)を欠(か)かさない。
Since she came out of hospital, my grandmother hasn't missed a single morning walk.
</pre>

<pre>
開店(かいてん)<b>以来(いらい)</b>、この店(みせ)の味(あじ)は変(か)わっていません。
The flavour at this shop hasn't changed since the day it opened.
</pre>

<pre>
大学(だいがく)を卒業(そつぎょう)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、同級生(どうきゅうせい)とのやり取(と)りは年賀状(ねんがじょう)だけになってしまった。
Since I graduated, my contact with my classmates has shrunk to New Year cards.
</pre>

<pre>
震災(しんさい)<b>以来(いらい)</b>、この地区(ちく)では防災(ぼうさい)訓練(くんれん)が年(ねん)に二回(にかい)行(おこな)われている。
Since the earthquake, disaster drills have been held twice a year in this district.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)痛(いた)い目(め)に遭(あ)っ<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は知(し)らない人(ひと)の話(はなし)をすぐには信(しん)じなくなった。
Ever since she got burned once, she has stopped taking strangers at their word.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>て以来(いらい)</code> leans written and formal. It is at home in a report, a
profile, a news paragraph or a careful spoken account; in relaxed chat most people
say `てから` instead. Nothing about it is stiff enough to sound wrong in speech,
but if you use it for the ten minutes since lunch it will sound comically grand.

The span matters. <code>以来(いらい)</code> wants a *long* one - months and years,
not hours. This is why it so often pairs with 一度(いちど)も, ずっと, まだ and a
negative: the whole point is that a great deal of time has gone by and the
situation has not moved.

There is a strong pull toward outcomes the speaker did not choose. A change in
health, a change of feeling, a habit that stuck, contact that dried up - these are
the natural main clauses. Announcing your own deliberate plan after
<code>以来(いらい)</code> is odd, because a plan belongs to a moment, not to a
stretch of years.

<code>それ以来(いらい)</code> is worth learning as a unit. Dropped at the head of a
sentence it means "and it has been that way ever since", and it is one of the most
useful connectives in narration: 「一度(いちど)断(ことわ)られた。それ以来(いらい)、誘(さそ)っていない。」

## Contrast

### ～て以来(いらい) vs ～てから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てから` orders two events. <code>て以来(いらい)</code> opens a
span and describes the whole of it.

</div>

`てから` is the workhorse, and it is neutral about how much time passed, whether the
span reaches the present, and whether the starting event is unique. It also happily
takes a future starting point.

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)ん<b>でから</b>、30分(ぷん)横(よこ)になってください。
Lie down for thirty minutes after taking the medicine.
</pre>

Swap in <code>以来(いらい)</code> there and the sentence collapses: you cannot issue
an instruction about a stretch of years that has already gone by.

<pre>
手術(しゅじゅつ)を受(う)け<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、体調(たいちょう)がずっといい。
I've been in good shape ever since I had the operation.
</pre>

Here `てから` would also be grammatical, and slightly flatter - it would sequence the
operation and the good health rather than presenting the good health as the
character of everything since.

### ～て以来(いらい) vs 間(あいだ) / 間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 間(あいだ) fences off an interval with **both** ends fixed.
<code>て以来(いらい)</code> fixes only the **start** and leaves the far end open at
the present moment.

</div>

This is the N3 pattern <code>て以来(いらい)</code> is closest to, and the divergence is
exactly that missing right-hand edge.

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)している<b>間(あいだ)</b>、一度(いちど)も実家(じっか)に帰(かえ)らなかった。
I didn't go home once during my time studying abroad.
</pre>

That interval is over and closed; the sentence says nothing about today.

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)から戻(もど)っ<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、一度(いちど)も実家(じっか)に帰(かえ)っていない。
I haven't been home once since I got back from studying abroad.
</pre>

Now the span is still running, which is why the verb has to be `ていない` rather
than `なかった`. That tense difference is the practical test: <code>間(あいだ)</code>
takes a closed past, <code>て以来(いらい)</code> takes a present perfect.

### 以来(いらい) vs 以降(いこう) / 以後(いご)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three mean "from that point on", but only
<code>以来(いらい)</code> is locked to the past. <code>以降(いこう)</code> and
<code>以後(いご)</code> can point at the future.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)<b>以降(いこう)</b>なら、いつでも大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です。
Any time from next week onward is fine.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の事故(じこ)<b>以来(いらい)</b>、夜(よる)の運転(うんてん)はしていない。
I haven't driven at night since last year's accident.
</pre>

Two further splits. <code>以降(いこう)</code> and <code>以後(いご)</code> attach to
nouns only - there is no <code>…て以降(いこう)</code>. And
<code>以後(いご)</code> used alone means "from now on", with a faint scolding
edge: 「以後(いご)、気(き)をつけます」 is what you say after being told off.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 卒業(そつぎょう)する<b>以来(いらい)</b> → ✅ 卒業(そつぎょう)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>
  A verb reaches <code>以来(いらい)</code> through the て-form. The dictionary form
  never appears in front of it.

- ❌ 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、何(なに)も飲(の)んでいない。 → ✅ 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>てから</b>、何(なに)も飲(の)んでいない。
  A few hours is far too short. <code>以来(いらい)</code> needs a span worth
  remarking on.

- ❌ 毎朝(まいあさ)起(お)き<b>て以来(いらい)</b> → ✅ 毎朝(まいあさ)起(お)き<b>てから</b>
  The starting event has to be a one-off. A daily routine cannot be an origin
  point.

- ❌ 入学(にゅうがく)<b>の以来(いらい)</b> → ✅ 入学(にゅうがく)<b>以来(いらい)</b>
  A noun joins directly, with no `の`.

- ❌ 来月(らいげつ)転職(てんしょく)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、忙(いそが)しくなります。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)転職(てんしょく)し<b>てから</b>、忙(いそが)しくなります。
  The origin has to be behind you already. There is no future
  <code>以来(いらい)</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)に喧嘩(けんか)し____、彼(かれ)とは一度(いちど)も話(はな)していない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

喧嘩(けんか)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>

One definite past quarrel opens the span, and 一度(いちど)も…ていない reports the
unbroken state that has held from then until now - the exact shape
<code>て以来(いらい)</code> exists for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: うがいをし（てから・て以来(いらい)）、10分(じゅっぷん)待(ま)ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

うがいをし<b>てから</b>、10分(じゅっぷん)待(ま)ってください。

The sentence is an instruction about the next few minutes.
<code>て以来(いらい)</code> can neither look forward nor cover so short a stretch,
and it cannot sit under a request.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I haven't touched a piano since I gave up lessons in high school."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

高校(こうこう)でレッスンをやめ<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、ピアノに触(さわ)っていない。

Quitting is a single past event, and the main clause is a negative perfect running
to the present, so <code>て以来(いらい)</code> is the natural choice over
`てから`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めて以来(いらい)、去年(きょねん)新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を見(み)つけました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)め<b>てから</b>、去年(きょねん)新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を見(み)つけました。

The main clause is a one-off event pinned to its own date, not a description of the
whole span. <code>て以来(いらい)</code> wants a state, a habit or a "not once";
plain sequencing is `てから`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 入院(にゅういん)し<b>てから</b>、たばこをやめました。
(b) 入院(にゅういん)し<b>て以来(いらい)</b>、たばこを吸(す)っていません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply puts the two events in order: hospital first, giving up smoking after.
It reports a decision, and says nothing about whether it stuck.

(b) treats the hospital stay as an origin point and describes the entire stretch
since. The `ていません` is doing real work - the claim is that the whole period from
then until this moment has been smoke-free.

</details>

# ～と同時に

**Meaning** · at the very same moment as; and equally, at the same time

Two jobs share one form. With an event in front of it,
<code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> says the second thing happened in the same instant as
the first - true overlap, not "and then". With a description in front of it, it
says one thing is **both** of two things at once.

## Formation

<code>同時(どうじ)</code> is a noun, and `と` is the ordinary "with" particle: the
literal sense is "simultaneously with X".

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (timing) | dictionary form + と同時(どうじ)に | 鳴(な)る → 鳴(な)ると同時(どうじ)に |
| Noun (an event) | Noun + と同時(どうじ)に | 卒業(そつぎょう) → 卒業(そつぎょう)と同時(どうじ)に |
| Noun (two roles) | Noun + であると同時(どうじ)に | 医師(いし) → 医師(いし)であると同時(どうじ)に |
| な-adjective (two qualities) | ～であると同時(どうじ)に | 危険(きけん) → 危険(きけん)であると同時(どうじ)に |
| い-adjective (two qualities) | plain form + と同時(どうじ)に | 難(むずか)しい → 難(むずか)しいと同時(どうじ)に |
| As a bare adverb | 同時(どうじ)に + verb | 同時(どうじ)に信号(しんごう)が変(か)わった |
| ❌ ます-form | ❌着(つ)きますと同時(どうじ)に | ✅着(つ)くと同時(どうじ)に |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in the timing sense the verb in front stays in the **dictionary form**
however the sentence ends. The tense of the whole sentence lives on the main clause:
<code>ドアが閉(し)まると同時(どうじ)に、電車(でんしゃ)は動(うご)き出(だ)した</code> is
firmly past.

</div>

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a **noun of an event** joins directly
(開店(かいてん)と同時(どうじ)に). A **noun of a category or role** needs
`である` first (作家(さっか)であると同時(どうじ)に). Getting this backwards is
the commonest error on this pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ベルが鳴(な)る<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、みんな席(せき)を立(た)った。
The moment the bell rang, everyone stood up.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)終了(しゅうりょう)<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
It started raining just as the match ended.
</pre>

<pre>
ドアを開(あ)ける<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、猫(ねこ)が飛(と)び出(だ)してきた。
The cat shot out the instant I opened the door.
</pre>

<pre>
卒業(そつぎょう)<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、田舎(いなか)に帰(かえ)るつもりです。
I intend to move back to the countryside the moment I graduate.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は研究者(けんきゅうしゃ)である<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、二人(ふたり)の子(こ)の母親(ははおや)でもある。
She is a researcher and, at the same time, a mother of two.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいダムの完成(かんせい)<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、この村(むら)の水(みず)不足(ぶそく)は解消(かいしょう)された。
The village's water shortage was solved the moment the new dam was finished.
</pre>

<pre>
信号(しんごう)が青(あお)に変(か)わる<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、後(うし)ろの車(くるま)にクラクションを鳴(な)らされた。
The car behind honked at me the instant the light went green.
</pre>

<pre>
値下(ねさ)げは客(きゃく)にとってありがたい<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、生産者(せいさんしゃ)にとっては重(おも)い負担(ふたん)になる。
A price cut is welcome for customers and, at the same time, a heavy burden on producers.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> is mildly formal. It reads naturally in news copy,
reports, announcements and careful speech; in casual conversation people reach for
`とすぐに` or a plain `たら`. It is not stiff, though - saying
「着(つ)くと同時(どうじ)に電話(でんわ)するね」 to a friend is perfectly
ordinary.

The timing sense is neutral about who is doing what. Unlike most of this family,
the two clauses may have completely different subjects, and either event may be
deliberate. It happily takes a future main clause, a plan, even a promise - which
is what marks it off from the surprised, past-only members of the group.

The two-roles sense belongs to written argument and speeches. It is how you set up
a balanced judgement - "this is X, and equally it is Y" - and it very often pairs
with `でもある` at the end, which reinforces the "both" reading. Learners tend to
under-use it; it is a cheap way to make an essay sentence sound adult.

Bare <code>同時(どうじ)に</code>, with no `と`, is just the adverb
"simultaneously", and it is fully register-neutral.

## Contrast

### ～と同時(どうじ)に vs ～たとたん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たとたん</code> is X finishing and Y arriving as a
**surprise**, always in the past. <code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> is genuine
**overlap**, with no surprise required and no tense restriction.

</div>

This is the N3 pattern <code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> sits closest to, and there are
three clean splits: the form of the first verb (`た` vs dictionary), the tense of
the main clause (past only vs anything), and whether the result may be planned.

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たとたん</b>、冷(つめ)たい風(かぜ)が吹(ふ)き込(こ)んできた。
The instant I opened the window, cold air blew in.
</pre>

Nobody arranged that draught, and the sentence could not end in `ます` or a plan.

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わる<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、資料(しりょう)をお送(おく)りします。
I'll send you the documents the moment the meeting finishes.
</pre>

A promise about the future - impossible with <code>たとたん</code>, ordinary with
<code>と同時(どうじ)に</code>.

### ～と同時(どうじ)に vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` needs **one person** doing two things, one of them
background. <code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> joins **two events**, and their subjects
may differ.

</div>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>ながら</b>走(はし)っている。
He runs while listening to music.
</pre>

Both actions are his, and the listening is the backdrop.

<pre>
彼(かれ)が走(はし)り出(だ)す<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、審判(しんぱん)が笛(ふえ)を吹(ふ)いた。
The referee blew the whistle the instant he started running.
</pre>

Two people, two events, one instant. `ながら` cannot express that at all, and
<code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> is poor at the durative background `ながら` is built
for.

### ～と同時(どうじ)に vs ～とともに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> points at a single **instant**.
<code>とともに</code> covers a **stretch** over which two things move together.

</div>

<pre>
年(とし)を取(と)る<b>とともに</b>、好(この)みが変(か)わってきた。
My tastes have changed as I've got older.
</pre>

Nothing here happens in an instant; the two changes keep pace with each other over
years. That proportional reading is <code>とともに</code>'s own, and
<code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> does not carry it.

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)が立(た)ち上(あ)がる<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、全員(ぜんいん)が拍手(はくしゅ)した。
Everyone applauded the moment the president stood up.
</pre>

Where the event really is instantaneous, the two overlap and either will pass;
<code>とともに</code> then sounds a shade more written.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 駅(えき)に着(つ)き<b>ますと同時(どうじ)に</b>電話(でんわ)します。 → ✅ 駅(えき)に着(つ)く<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>電話(でんわ)します。
  The politeness belongs to the end of the sentence. The verb in front of
  <code>同時(どうじ)</code> stays plain.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は学生(がくせい)<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、バンドのリーダーでもある。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は学生(がくせい)<b>であると同時(どうじ)に</b>、バンドのリーダーでもある。
  A role noun needs `である`. Without it the sentence reads as timing - "at the
  moment of student", which means nothing.

- ❌ テレビを見(み)る<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>ご飯(はん)を食(た)べた。 → ✅ テレビを見(み)<b>ながら</b>ご飯(はん)を食(た)べた。
  Two ongoing activities by one person is `ながら` territory.
  <code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> would claim the two started in the same instant.

- ❌ 授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わる<b>と同時(どうじ)の</b>、帰(かえ)った。 → ✅ 授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)わる<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、帰(かえ)った。
  The connective form is `に`. <code>同時(どうじ)の</code> only appears in front of
  a noun (同時(どうじ)の通訳(つうやく)).

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The moment the door opened, a smell of coffee drifted out."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ドアが開(あ)く<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、コーヒーの香(かお)りが流(なが)れてきた。

An event noun clause plus the dictionary form. <code>開(あ)いたとたん</code> would
also work and would sound more startled;
<code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> just reports the overlap.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 荷物(にもつ)を受(う)け取(と)ったと同時(どうじ)に、サインをお願(ねが)いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 荷物(にもつ)を受(う)け取(と)る<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、サインをお願(ねが)いします。

The verb in front takes the dictionary form, not `た`, regardless of when the event
happens. A request in the main clause is fine here - that is one of the things
<code>と同時(どうじ)に</code> can do and <code>たとたん</code> cannot.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 部屋(へや)に入(はい)っ<b>たとたん</b>、電気(でんき)が消(き)えた。
(b) 部屋(へや)に入(はい)る<b>と同時(どうじ)に</b>、電気(でんき)が消(き)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is told from the speaker's shock: the lights going out was unexpected, and the
sentence carries that jolt. It can only ever be a past report.

(b) is flat description of the timing - the entering and the lights going out
occupied the same instant. It leaves open the possibility that the two were
connected on purpose, say by a sensor, which (a) rules out.

</details>

# ～次第

**Meaning** · as soon as X is done, I will do Y - a promise about what happens the moment something becomes possible

<code>次第(しだい)</code> is the business-Japanese "as soon as". It always looks
**forward**: the first event has not happened yet, and the speaker is committing to
what they will do the second it does. Nothing about it can be a past report.

## Formation

<code>次第(しだい)</code> follows the ます-stem of a verb, or a する-noun with
nothing between them.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + 次第(しだい) | 着(つ)く → 着(つ)き次第(しだい) |
| Verb | ます-stem + 次第(しだい) | 分(わ)かる → 分(わ)かり次第(しだい) |
| する-noun | Noun + 次第(しだい) | 到着(とうちゃく) → 到着(とうちゃく)次第(しだい) |
| する-noun (long form) | Noun + し + 次第(しだい) | 入荷(にゅうか) → 入荷(にゅうか)し次第(しだい) |
| ❌ た-form | ❌着(つ)いた次第(しだい) | ✅着(つ)き次第(しだい) |
| ❌ Dictionary form | ❌届(とど)く次第(しだい) | ✅届(とど)き次第(しだい) |
| ❌ Noun + の | ❌到着(とうちゃく)の次第(しだい) | ✅到着(とうちゃく)次第(しだい) |
| ❌ Past main clause | ❌…次第(しだい)、送(おく)りました | ✅…次第(しだい)、送(おく)ります |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the main clause must be **non-past** and must express somebody's
**will**: a plan, a promise, a request, an instruction. If the second event is just
something that happens by itself, <code>次第(しだい)</code> is the wrong word.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb in front is almost always one that describes a condition
falling into place rather than an act of will: 決(き)まる, 分(わ)かる,
届(とど)く, 終(お)わる, 戻(もど)る, できる, 空(あ)く. You are waiting on
it, which is why you cannot say when it will be.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same word <code>次第(しだい)</code> also appears in
<code>Nしだいだ／しだいで</code> ("it depends on N") and in the adverb
<code>次第(しだい)に</code> ("gradually"). Those are separate grammar points. You
can tell this one instantly: it sits on a ます-stem and the sentence carries on
after it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)ができ<b>次第(しだい)</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)します。
We'll set off as soon as we're ready.
</pre>

<pre>
分(わ)かり<b>次第(しだい)</b>、ご連絡(れんらく)します。
I'll let you know as soon as I find out.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)がやみ<b>次第(しだい)</b>、試合(しあい)を再開(さいかい)します。
The match will resume as soon as the rain stops.
</pre>

<pre>
席(せき)が空(あ)き<b>次第(しだい)</b>、お呼(よ)びいたします。
We'll call you the moment a table comes free.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が戻(もど)り<b>次第(しだい)</b>、この書類(しょるい)を渡(わた)しておいてください。
Hand over these papers as soon as the manager gets back.
</pre>

<pre>
商品(しょうひん)が入荷(にゅうか)し<b>次第(しだい)</b>、メールでお知(し)らせします。
We'll email you as soon as the item comes back into stock.
</pre>

<pre>
到着(とうちゃく)<b>次第(しだい)</b>、フロントで受付(うけつけ)をお済(す)ませください。
Please check in at the front desk as soon as you arrive.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の会場(かいじょう)が決(き)まり<b>次第(しだい)</b>、参加者(さんかしゃ)全員(ぜんいん)に案内(あんない)をお送(おく)りいたします。
As soon as next month's venue is settled, we'll send the details to everyone taking part.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **formal, largely written** pattern, and it is everywhere in business
Japanese. Notices, order confirmations, internal mail and telephone Japanese all
run on it, usually alongside 敬語(けいご): 「確認(かくにん)でき次第(しだい)、ご連絡(れんらく)いたします」
is close to a fixed sentence.

What it buys you is a particular kind of politeness. Saying
<code>次第(しだい)</code> concedes that you do not control the timing - you are
waiting on a delivery, a decision, a table - while promising that you will act with
no delay once it lands. That combination of *I can't promise when* and *I promise
it will be immediate* is why it sounds reassuring rather than evasive.

In casual conversation it is unusual. Among friends you would say
<code>決(き)まったらすぐ教(おし)えるね</code>, not
<code>決(き)まり次第(しだい)教(おし)えるね</code> - the latter is not wrong,
but it sounds like you are reading from a form. Younger speakers do use it jokingly
for exactly that effect.

One quiet restriction that trips people up: because the main clause must carry
somebody's intention, <code>次第(しだい)</code> cannot be used for two events
that simply follow one another in nature.
<code>気温(きおん)が下(さ)がり次第(しだい)、雪(ゆき)に変(か)わります</code>
is odd, because nobody is deciding to turn the rain into snow.

## Contrast

### ～次第(しだい) vs ～たとたん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these two are mirror images. <code>たとたん</code> is
**past + surprise**; <code>次第(しだい)</code> is **future + intention**. Neither
can cross into the other's half.

</div>

<code>たとたん</code> is the N3 pattern <code>次第(しだい)</code> is closest to in
meaning - both compress the gap between two events to nothing - and everything else
about them is opposite.

<pre>
名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれ<b>たとたん</b>、手(て)が震(ふる)えだした。
The moment my name was called, my hands started shaking.
</pre>

Past, involuntary, and reported with a little shock.

<pre>
名前(なまえ)が呼(よ)ばれ<b>次第(しだい)</b>、こちらの部屋(へや)にお入(はい)りください。
Please come into this room as soon as your name is called.
</pre>

Future, and an instruction. Try to swap them and both sentences break: there is no
past <code>次第(しだい)</code> and no imperative <code>たとたん</code>.

### ～次第(しだい) vs ～たらすぐに / ～てから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たらすぐに` says the same thing in ordinary Japanese.
<code>次第(しだい)</code> adds formality and narrows the first clause to something
outside your control.

</div>

<pre>
家(いえ)に着(つ)い<b>たらすぐに</b>電話(でんわ)するね。
I'll ring you as soon as I get home.
</pre>

<pre>
検査(けんさ)の結果(けっか)が出(で)<b>次第(しだい)</b>、担当医(たんとうい)よりご説明(せつめい)いたします。
The doctor in charge will explain as soon as the test results are in.
</pre>

Note what the first clauses are. Getting home is your own doing, so `たら` is
natural and <code>次第(しだい)</code> would sound overblown. Test results arrive
when they arrive - that is exactly the shape <code>次第(しだい)</code> wants.
`てから` differs again: it only orders the events and carries no sense of "with no
delay at all".

### ～次第(しだい) vs ～次第(しだい)だ / ～次第(しだい)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - what comes **before** it decides which word you are looking at.
A **ます-stem** gives "as soon as"; a **plain noun** gives "depends on".

</div>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が届(とど)き<b>次第(しだい)</b>、皆(みな)さんにお伝(つた)えします。
I'll pass it on to everyone as soon as the reply arrives.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)行(い)くかどうかは天気(てんき)<b>次第(しだい)</b>です。
Whether we go tomorrow depends on the weather.
</pre>

The second one ends the sentence with `です` and has a bare noun in front; the first
sits mid-sentence on a ます-stem. Once you look at that one spot the two never
collide - and note that a する-noun like <code>到着(とうちゃく)</code> can feed
either, so read the rest of the sentence before deciding.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 駅(えき)に着(つ)い<b>た次第(しだい)</b>、電話(でんわ)します。 → ✅ 駅(えき)に着(つ)<b>き次第(しだい)</b>、電話(でんわ)します。
  The base is the ます-stem. There is no た-form or dictionary form in front of this
  <code>次第(しだい)</code>.

- ❌ 荷物(にもつ)が届(とど)き<b>次第(しだい)</b>、返事(へんじ)を書(か)きました。 → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)が届(とど)き<b>次第(しだい)</b>、返事(へんじ)を書(か)きます。
  The main clause cannot be past. If it already happened, use `たらすぐに` or
  `てすぐ`.

- ❌ 春(はる)になり<b>次第(しだい)</b>、桜(さくら)が咲(さ)きます。 → ✅ 春(はる)になると、桜(さくら)が咲(さ)きます。
  Nobody wills the cherry blossom open. With no intention in the main clause,
  <code>次第(しだい)</code> has nothing to attach its promise to.

- ❌ 出発(しゅっぱつ)<b>する次第(しだい)</b> → ✅ 出発(しゅっぱつ)<b>次第(しだい)</b>（or 出発(しゅっぱつ)し次第(しだい)）
  A する-noun joins bare, or through the stem `し`. The full
  <code>する</code> never appears.

## Practice

**1.** 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わり____、議事録(ぎじろく)をお送(おく)りします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わり<b>次第(しだい)</b>

A ます-stem in front, a polite promise behind - the standard business shape. You are
not deciding when the meeting ends, which is exactly why
<code>次第(しだい)</code> fits.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 窓(まど)を開(あ)け（たとたん・次第(しだい)）、ハチが入(はい)ってきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たとたん</b>、ハチが入(はい)ってきた。

The sentence is a past report of something nobody wanted.
<code>次第(しだい)</code> only points forward and only into an intended action, so
it cannot host a bee.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "We will notify you by post as soon as the examination date is fixed."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

試験(しけん)の日程(にってい)が決(き)まり<b>次第(しだい)</b>、郵便(ゆうびん)でお知(し)らせします。

<code>決(き)まる</code> is the intransitive "get settled", which is the kind of
verb <code>次第(しだい)</code> takes, and the main clause is a polite undertaking.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部品(ぶひん)が入荷(にゅうか)する次第(しだい)、修理(しゅうり)を始(はじ)めました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部品(ぶひん)が入荷(にゅうか)<b>し次第(しだい)</b>、修理(しゅうり)を始(はじ)め<b>ます</b>。

Two faults. A する-noun joins as <code>入荷(にゅうか)し次第(しだい)</code> or
bare, never with the full <code>する</code>; and the main clause has to be non-past,
because the whole pattern is a promise about what is still to come.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 資料(しりょう)が届(とど)い<b>てから</b>、検討(けんとう)します。
(b) 資料(しりょう)が届(とど)き<b>次第(しだい)</b>、検討(けんとう)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) only puts the two in order: the documents arrive first, the review comes after.
It leaves the gap open - it might be next week.

(b) promises there will be no gap at all. It also quietly signals that the delay is
not yours: you are waiting, and the moment the material lands you will act. In an
email (b) is markedly more reassuring than (a).

</details>

# ～か～ないかのうちに

**Meaning** · had barely finished X when Y - so fast that you could not say whether X was even over

The whole force of this pattern is in the middle: the same verb is said once
affirmative and once negative, so the sentence literally means "in the interval
where you could not tell whether it had happened or not". It is the tightest
"as soon as" Japanese has, and it is always a past report of something watched.

## Formation

The verb appears **twice**, and it must be the same verb both times.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary + か + ない-form + かのうちに | 座(すわ)る → 座(すわ)るか座(すわ)らないかのうちに |
| Verb (past variant) | た-form + か + ない-form + かのうちに | 終(お)わる → 終(お)わったか終(お)わらないかのうちに |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(く)る か 来(こ)ない かのうちに | 来(く)る → 来(く)るか来(こ)ないかのうちに |
| Passive | passive dictionary + か + passive ない | 開(ひら)かれる → 開(ひら)かれるか開(ひら)かれないかのうちに |
| ❌ Two different verbs | ❌着(つ)くか座(すわ)らないかのうちに | ✅着(つ)くか着(つ)かないかのうちに |
| ❌ Negative first | ❌閉(し)まらないか閉(し)まるかのうちに | ✅閉(し)まるか閉(し)まらないかのうちに |
| ❌ Non-past main clause | ❌…かのうちに、出(で)かけます | ✅…かのうちに、出(で)かけた |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - affirmative first, negative second, and the main clause in the
**past**. This pattern narrates; it cannot request, invite, promise or predict.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>来(く)る</code> is the one form worth writing out. Its dictionary
form is <code>来(く)る</code> and its negative stem is <code>来(こ)</code>, so
the pair is <code>来(く)るか来(こ)ないか</code> - two different readings of the
same character in the same breath.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ドアが<b>閉(し)まるか閉(し)まらないかのうちに</b>、男(おとこ)の人(ひと)が飛(と)び乗(の)ってきた。
A man leapt aboard just as the doors were closing.
</pre>

<pre>
ベルが<b>鳴(な)るか鳴(な)らないかのうちに</b>、子(こ)どもたちは教室(きょうしつ)を出(で)て行(い)った。
The children were out of the classroom before the bell had properly rung.
</pre>

<pre>
布団(ふとん)に<b>入(はい)るか入(はい)らないかのうちに</b>、弟(おとうと)はいびきをかき始(はじ)めた。
My little brother was snoring almost before he was under the covers.
</pre>

<pre>
店(みせ)が<b>開(あ)くか開(あ)かないかのうちに</b>、入口(いりぐち)には行列(ぎょうれつ)ができていた。
There was already a queue at the entrance before the shop had even opened.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)が<b>終(お)わったか終(お)わらないかのうちに</b>、彼女(かのじょ)はかばんを抱(かか)えて廊下(ろうか)へ飛(と)び出(だ)した。
She was out in the corridor with her bag before the lesson had properly ended.
</pre>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)が席(せき)に<b>着(つ)くか着(つ)かないかのうちに</b>、記者(きしゃ)たちが質問(しつもん)を浴(あ)びせ始(はじ)めた。
The reporters were firing questions before the president had even sat down.
</pre>

<pre>
新製品(しんせいひん)が<b>発表(はっぴょう)されるか発表(はっぴょう)されないかのうちに</b>、注文(ちゅうもん)が殺到(さっとう)した。
Orders flooded in the moment the new product was announced - if not before.
</pre>

<pre>
画面(がめん)に名前(なまえ)が<b>出(で)るか出(で)ないかのうちに</b>、会場(かいじょう)のあちこちから歓声(かんせい)が上(あ)がった。
Cheers went up all round the hall almost before the name appeared on the screen.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **narrative** pattern. It belongs to reportage, fiction, sports
commentary and the kind of anecdote you tell at length; it is long, deliberate and
slightly theatrical, and nobody uses it for something trivial. In everyday speech
the same content comes out as <code>閉(し)まる直前(ちょくぜん)に</code> or a
plain <code>たとたん</code>.

Register aside, it is neutral - no gender or age marking, equally at home in a
novel and in a news bulletin, and it takes ます-form endings without difficulty
(飛(と)び乗(の)ってきました).

The reason it is so long is that it is doing something precise. It does not claim
the first event finished; it claims you could not tell. That is why it so often
describes rudeness, impatience or eagerness - somebody who could not wait the extra
half-second. Used of your own actions it is faintly self-mocking, which is a real
use: 「席(せき)に着(つ)くか着(つ)かないかのうちに寝(ね)てしまった」.

Because it narrates, the whole apparatus of intention is unavailable. No
`てください`, no `ましょう`, no `つもり`, no prediction about tomorrow. If you want
the same tightness pointing forward, that is <code>次第(しだい)</code>.

## Contrast

### ～か～ないかのうちに vs ～たとたん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たとたん</code> says X **finished** and then Y hit.
<code>か～ないかのうちに</code> says Y arrived **before X was over** - the gap is not
merely zero, it is negative.

</div>

<code>たとたん</code> is the N3 pattern this one is closest to, and both are past-only,
so the split is not tense but the boundary itself.

<pre>
電話(でんわ)を切(き)っ<b>たとたん</b>、また着信(ちゃくしん)があった。
The instant I hung up, another call came in.
</pre>

The call ended; then the phone rang. Two events in order.

<pre>
電話(でんわ)を<b>切(き)るか切(き)らないかのうちに</b>、また着信(ちゃくしん)があった。
The next call was coming in before I'd even got the phone down.
</pre>

Now the two overlap at the edge, and the speaker is admitting they cannot separate
them. There is a second difference of feel: <code>たとたん</code> is built on the
speaker's surprise at the *result*, while this pattern is built on how little time
there was, and the result may be entirely unsurprising.

### ～か～ないかのうちに vs ～ないうちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ないうちに</code> looks at a window **before** X, and X may
never happen at all. <code>か～ないかのうちに</code> sits exactly **on** X's edge, and
X does happen.

</div>

<pre>
暗(くら)く<b>ならないうちに</b>帰(かえ)りましょう。
Let's head back before it gets dark.
</pre>

That is a plan, made inside a window that is still open - and note that it can be an
invitation.

<pre>
空(そら)が<b>暗(くら)くなるか暗(くら)くならないかのうちに</b>、大(おお)粒(つぶ)の雨(あめ)が落(お)ちてきた。
Fat drops of rain began falling almost before the sky had gone dark.
</pre>

Here the darkening did occur, and the sentence pins the rain to its very edge.
Swapping in <code>ないうちに</code> would change the claim to "while it was still
light", which is a different fact.

### ～か～ないかのうちに vs ～たかと思(おも)うと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "no sooner than". This one is about **measurement**:
the interval was too small to measure. <code>たかと思うと</code> is about the
speaker's **reaction**: the second event contradicts what the first led you to
expect.

</div>

<pre>
空(そら)が明(あか)るくなっ<b>たかと思(おも)うと</b>、また降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
Just when the sky had brightened, it started raining again.
</pre>

The point is the reversal, not the stopwatch - and this is why
<code>かと思(おも)うと</code> readily describes something repeating
(泣(な)いたかと思(おも)うと笑(わら)う) while
<code>か～ないかのうちに</code> reports one occasion only.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 席(せき)に<b>着(つ)くか座(すわ)らないかのうちに</b> → ✅ 席(せき)に<b>着(つ)くか着(つ)かないかのうちに</b>
  It has to be the same verb twice. The pattern's meaning comes from asserting and
  denying one single event.

- ❌ ドアが<b>閉(し)まらないか閉(し)まるかのうちに</b> → ✅ ドアが<b>閉(し)まるか閉(し)まらないかのうちに</b>
  Affirmative first. The order is fixed and reversing it sounds like a stumble.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わるか終(お)わらないかのうちに、連絡(れんらく)してください。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わり次第(しだい)、連絡(れんらく)してください。
  Requests and plans are out. This pattern only reports what already happened.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)がやむか<b>やまないかのうちで</b> → ✅ 雨(あめ)がやむか<b>やまないかのうちに</b>
  The particle is `に`. <code>うち</code> here marks a moment, not a place.

## Practice

**1.** 信号(しんごう)が青(あお)に変(か)わる____変(か)わらないかのうちに、バイクが走(はし)り出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

変(か)わる<b>か</b>変(か)わらないかのうちに

The `か` between the two halves is what makes the sentence mean "you could not tell
which". Both halves use the same verb, affirmative then negative.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 暗(くら)く（なるかならないかのうちに・ならないうちに）、洗濯物(せんたくもの)を取(と)り込(こ)んでおこう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

暗(くら)く<b>ならないうちに</b>、洗濯物(せんたくもの)を取(と)り込(こ)んでおこう。

The sentence ends in a volitional - the speaker is planning. That rules out
<code>か～ないかのうちに</code>, which can only narrate the past, and calls for the
"while there is still time" window of <code>ないうちに</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He had barely got through the door when he started complaining."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は<b>入(はい)ってくるか入(はい)ってこないかのうちに</b>、文句(もんく)を言(い)い始(はじ)めた。

"Barely ... when" is exactly this pattern. The compound
<code>入(はい)ってくる</code> repeats whole, and the main clause is past.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 先生(せんせい)が教室(きょうしつ)に来(く)るか来(く)らないかのうちに、みんな静(しず)かになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先生(せんせい)が教室(きょうしつ)に<b>来(く)るか来(こ)ないかのうちに</b>、みんな静(しず)かになった。

<code>来(く)る</code> is irregular: the negative is <code>来(こ)ない</code>, not
❌<code>来(く)らない</code>. The same character is read
<code>く</code> in the first half and <code>こ</code> in the second.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ベルが鳴(な)っ<b>たとたん</b>、生徒(せいと)たちが立(た)ち上(あ)がった。
(b) ベルが<b>鳴(な)るか鳴(な)らないかのうちに</b>、生徒(せいと)たちが立(た)ち上(あ)がった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the two in order - the bell finished, then they stood - and colours the
standing as something the speaker did not expect.

(b) refuses to put them in order. The students were on their feet while the bell was
still going, and the sentence is quietly judging them for it: they could not even
wait for it to finish.

</details>

# ～かと思ったら / ～かと思うと

**Meaning** · just when it looked as though X, Y - and Y is the opposite of what X promised

The literal shape is "no sooner had I thought X". The speaker is watching, forms a
quick impression, and is immediately proved wrong. The gap between the two events
is tiny, but the real content of the pattern is the **reversal**: what happens next
undercuts what the first half led you to expect.

## Formation

The clause in front normally ends in the **た-form**. The two endings
<code>かと思(おも)うと</code> and <code>かと思(おも)ったら</code> are
interchangeable in this meaning; <code>かと思(おも)えば</code> is a third variant.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + かと思(おも)うと | 止(や)む → 止(や)んだかと思(おも)うと |
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + かと思(おも)ったら | 着(つ)く → 着(つ)いたかと思(おも)ったら |
| Verb (ている) | ているかと思(おも)えば | 泣(な)いている → 泣(な)いているかと思(おも)えば |
| Noun / question word | Noun + かと思(おも)ったら | 何(なに) → 何(なに)かと思(おも)ったら |
| ❌ Dictionary form | ❌止(や)むかと思(おも)うと | ✅止(や)んだかと思(おも)うと |
| ❌ Request or volitional | ❌…かと思(おも)ったら、休(やす)みましょう | ✅…たら、休(やす)みましょう |
| ❌ The speaker's own deliberate act | ❌私(わたし)は座(すわ)ったかと思(おも)うと、食(た)べ始(はじ)めた | ✅座(すわ)るとすぐ食(た)べ始(はじ)めた |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the first verb is **past**, the main clause is a **past report**
or a **habitual present**, and the speaker is an **observer**. You cannot be
surprised by your own decisions, which is why your own volitional acts do not fit
here.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - with a noun or question word in front,
<code>かと思(おも)ったら</code> keeps its literal sense of a mistaken guess:
<code>誰(だれ)かと思(おも)ったら田中(たなか)さんだった</code>, "I wondered who
it was - turned out to be Tanaka." Same words, and the timing sense grows straight
out of it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)が止(や)んだ<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、また降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
Just when the rain had stopped, down it came again.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は帰(かえ)ってきた<b>かと思(おも)ったら</b>、もう出(で)かけていった。
My brother was barely home before he went out again.
</pre>

<pre>
赤(あか)ちゃんが泣(な)きやんだ<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、また大声(おおごえ)で泣(な)き出(だ)した。
The baby had only just gone quiet when it started howling again.
</pre>

<pre>
空(そら)が明(あか)るくなった<b>かと思(おも)ったら</b>、急(きゅう)に雷(かみなり)が鳴(な)り始(はじ)めた。
Just as the sky was brightening, thunder suddenly started up.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かになった<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、子(こ)どもたちは台所(だいどころ)でお菓子(かし)をあさっていた。
The moment it went quiet, the children turned out to be raiding the kitchen for sweets.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)の外(そと)で大(おお)きな音(おと)がしたので何(なに)<b>かと思(おも)ったら</b>、隣(となり)の家(いえ)の植木鉢(うえきばち)が倒(たお)れていた。
There was a crash outside the window, and when I wondered what it was, it turned out a neighbour's plant pot had blown over.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は机(つくえ)に向(む)かっている<b>かと思(おも)えば</b>、次(つぎ)の瞬間(しゅんかん)にはスマホをのぞいている。
One moment he's at his desk working, the next he's peering at his phone.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しく入(はい)った人(ひと)は、やっと仕事(しごと)を覚(おぼ)えた<b>かと思(おも)ったら</b>、一(いっ)か月(げつ)で辞(や)めてしまった。
The new hire had barely learned the job when he quit after a month.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Both endings are ordinary in speech and in writing.
<code>かと思(おも)ったら</code> is the more conversational of the two;
<code>かと思(おも)うと</code> leans a little more written and turns up in
narration and essays. <code>かと思(おも)えば</code> is the one you want when you
are listing two habits that contradict each other, and it commonly pairs with
<code>今度(こんど)は</code> or <code>次(つぎ)の瞬間(しゅんかん)には</code>.

The feeling is exasperation or bemusement more often than delight. Rain that stops
and restarts, a child who calms down and erupts again, a colleague who settles in
and resigns - the pattern is drawn to things that will not stay put. Used of a
person it is close to a complaint, and it is worth knowing that before you aim it
at your boss.

There is one shape that is not a complaint at all. With a question word in front -
<code>何(なに)かと思(おも)ったら</code>,
<code>誰(だれ)かと思(おも)ったら</code>,
<code>どこかと思(おも)ったら</code> - it simply reports that a guess turned out
wrong, often with relief. That use is completely neutral and extremely common in
conversation.

Since the sentence reports what the speaker saw, it does not take `ください`,
`ましょう`, `つもり` or a prediction about tomorrow. A habitual present is fine
(泣(な)いたかと思(おも)うと笑(わら)う), because a habit is still something
observed.

## Contrast

### ～かと思(おも)うと vs ～たとたん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たとたん</code> is surprise at **how fast**.
<code>かと思(おも)うと</code> is surprise at **what**: the second event contradicts
the first.

</div>

<code>たとたん</code> is the N3 pattern this one grew up next to, and the two share
the た-form, the past main clause and the ban on requests. The divergence is the
relationship between the halves.

<pre>
席(せき)に座(すわ)っ<b>たとたん</b>、名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれた。
The instant I sat down, my name was called.
</pre>

Sitting and being called have nothing to do with each other; only the timing is
remarkable.

<pre>
席(せき)に座(すわ)っ<b>たかと思(おも)うと</b>、彼(かれ)はまた立(た)ち上(あ)がった。
He had barely sat down before he was on his feet again.
</pre>

Here the two halves are in tension - he sat, so you expected him to stay sitting.
Take that tension away and <code>かと思(おも)うと</code> loses its point.
<code>たとたん</code> also allows the speaker to be the one who acted, as in the
first sentence; <code>かと思(おも)うと</code> generally does not.

### ～かと思(おも)うと vs ～か～ないかのうちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>か～ないかのうちに</code> measures the **interval** and
finds it too small to measure. <code>かと思(おも)うと</code> measures the
speaker's **expectation** and finds it wrong.

</div>

<pre>
ドアが<b>開(あ)くか開(あ)かないかのうちに</b>、犬(いぬ)が飛(と)び出(だ)してきた。
The dog was out before the door had properly opened.
</pre>

<pre>
犬(いぬ)は外(そと)に出(で)た<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、すぐに戻(もど)ってきた。
No sooner was the dog outside than it came straight back in.
</pre>

The first sentence would be strange if the gap were a whole second; the second one
survives a longer gap perfectly well, because what makes it worth saying is the
about-turn. That is also why only <code>かと思(おも)うと</code> can describe a
repeating habit.

### ～かと思(おも)うと vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `と` reports a **regular** sequence with no comment.
<code>かと思(おも)うと</code> adds that the sequence is unreasonably quick or
contrary to expectation.

</div>

<pre>
父(ちち)は家(いえ)に帰(かえ)る<b>と</b>、すぐテレビをつける。
My father turns the television on as soon as he gets home.
</pre>

Flat description of a routine; nobody is being judged.

<pre>
父(ちち)は家(いえ)に帰(かえ)った<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、もうソファで寝(ね)ている。
My father is barely through the door before he's asleep on the sofa.
</pre>

Same routine, now with an eyebrow raised. Note the form difference too: `と` takes
the dictionary form, this pattern takes `た`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が止(や)む<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、また降(ふ)り出(だ)した。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が止(や)んだ<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、また降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
  The impression has already formed, so the verb in front is `た`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は家(いえ)に着(つ)いた<b>かと思(おも)うと</b>、シャワーを浴(あ)びた。 → ✅ 家(いえ)に着(つ)くとすぐシャワーを浴(あ)びた。
  You cannot be startled by your own plan. The pattern needs an observer and
  something observed.

- ❌ 晴(は)れた<b>かと思(おも)ったら</b>、出(で)かけましょう。 → ✅ 晴(は)れ<b>たら</b>、出(で)かけましょう。
  Invitations, requests and intentions are all shut out; this pattern only reports.

- ❌ 止(や)んだ<b>かと思(おも)った</b>、また降(ふ)り出(だ)した。 → ✅ 止(や)んだ<b>かと思(おも)ったら</b>、また降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
  Without `ら` (or `と`) the first half is a finished sentence and the two clauses
  no longer join.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The full connector is <code>かと思(おも)うと</code>, and the `か` is essential - it

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The sentence reports a past event with a note of disbelief.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the speed and nothing more: the phone call followed the entrance with
no gap, and the speaker found that abrupt.

(b) adds a judgement. Coming into a room sets up an expectation - a greeting, a
pause, sitting down - and the call cuts across it. (b) sounds faintly critical in a
way (a) does not.

</details>

# ～ところに / ～ところへ

**Meaning** · right at that point in what I was doing, something turned up

<code>ところ</code> names a stage of an action - about to start, in the middle,
just finished - and `に`／`へ` drops a second event onto it. The second event
almost always comes **from outside**: someone arrives, the phone goes, a voice
calls you. That intrusion is the whole pattern.

## Formation

Which form goes in front decides **which stage** the interruption lands on. This is
the part learners under-use.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - about to | dictionary form + ところに／へ | 出(で)かける → 出(で)かけるところに |
| Verb - in progress | ている + ところに／へ | 探(さが)している → 探(さが)しているところへ |
| Verb - just finished | た-form + ところに／へ | 着(つ)いた → 着(つ)いたところへ |
| Verb - on the very brink | ようとしている + ところに | 寝(ね)ようとしている → 寝(ね)ようとしているところに |
| い-adjective | plain form + ところに／を | お忙(いそが)しい → お忙(いそが)しいところに |
| Fixed praise | いい + ところに | - → いいところに来(き)てくれた |
| ❌ ます-form | ❌出(で)かけますところに | ✅出(で)かけるところに |
| ❌ Main clause the speaker chose | ❌出(で)かけるところに電話(でんわ)をした | ✅出(で)かけるところに電話(でんわ)がかかってきた |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the main clause must be something that **happens to** you:
来(く)る, 現(あらわ)れる, 電話(でんわ)がかかる, 声(こえ)をかけられる,
呼(よ)ばれる, 見(み)つかる. If you are the one who decides to act, the
sentence is not this pattern.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `に` and `へ` are close to interchangeable here. `へ` leans a little
harder on the arrival travelling towards you, so it is the more vivid choice with
来(く)る and 現(あらわ)れる; `に` is the safer default and the only comfortable
choice when nothing physically moves.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
出(で)かける<b>ところに</b>、友(とも)だちが来(き)た。
A friend turned up just as I was leaving.
</pre>

<pre>
ご飯(はん)を食(た)べている<b>ところへ</b>、電話(でんわ)がかかってきた。
The phone rang right in the middle of dinner.
</pre>

<pre>
寝(ね)ようとしている<b>ところに</b>、上(うえ)の階(かい)で物音(ものおと)がした。
Just as I was dropping off, there was a noise from the floor above.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)に着(つ)いた<b>ところへ</b>、ちょうどバスが来(き)た。
I'd just reached the station when a bus pulled in.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が分(わ)からなくて困(こま)っている<b>ところに</b>、後(うし)ろから声(こえ)をかけられた。
I was lost and stuck when someone spoke to me from behind.
</pre>

<pre>
重(おも)い荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)んでいる<b>ところへ</b>、近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)が手伝(てつだ)いに来(き)てくれた。
A neighbour came over to help just as I was hauling the heavy boxes.
</pre>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しい<b>ところに</b>恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが、少(すこ)しお時間(じかん)をいただけますか。
I'm sorry to trouble you when you're busy - could you spare me a moment?
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)をやっと片付(かたづ)け終(お)わった<b>ところへ</b>、子(こ)どもたちが泥(どろ)だらけの靴(くつ)で入(はい)ってきた。
I'd only just finished tidying the room when the children tramped in with muddy shoes.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern itself is register-neutral and very common in ordinary speech. What
carries the colour is the situation: an intrusion is normally unwelcome, so
<code>ところに</code> often sounds like a small complaint - the phone always goes
during dinner. It is not obliged to be negative, though.
<code>いいところに来(き)てくれた</code>, "you turned up at exactly the right
moment", is a warm and very frequent use, and rescue arriving at the worst point is
a standard story beat.

<code>お忙(いそが)しいところ</code> deserves memorising whole. It opens requests,
apologies and thanks in polite Japanese - お忙(いそが)しいところ恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが,
お忙(いそが)しいところありがとうございます - and it is one of the phrases that
makes a learner's business Japanese sound suddenly grown-up. In that formula both
`に` and `を` are heard, and many speakers use neither.

In casual speech <code>ところ</code> contracts to <code>とこ</code>:
「今(いま)出(で)るとこに電話(でんわ)が来(き)てさ」. This is very common and
completely normal among friends, but keep the full form in writing and in ます-form
speech.

## Contrast

### ～ところに vs ～最中(さいちゅう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> demands that the action be in
**full swing**. <code>ところに</code> lets you choose the stage: before, during, or
just after.

</div>

<code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> is the N3 pattern this one is closest to, and the
difference is coverage. <code>最中(さいちゅう)</code> means the thick of
something, so it will not accept an action that has not started.

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の<b>最中(さいちゅう)に</b>、携帯(けいたい)が鳴(な)ってしまった。
My phone went off in the middle of the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)める<b>ところに</b>、部長(ぶちょう)が入(はい)ってきた。
The manager came in just as we were about to start the meeting.
</pre>

Try <code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> in the second sentence and it fails - there is
no "middle" of a meeting that has not begun. Going the other way,
<code>最中(さいちゅう)に</code> carries a stronger sense of a bad moment, and it
attaches to a noun with `の` as well as to `ている`.

### ～ところに vs ～ところを

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `に`／`へ` something **arrives**. With `を` **you** are what
the main verb acts on - caught, seen, stopped, helped.

</div>

<pre>
サボっている<b>ところを</b>課長(かちょう)に見(み)られた。
The section chief caught me slacking off.
</pre>

<pre>
サボっている<b>ところに</b>課長(かちょう)が来(き)た。
The section chief came by while I was slacking off.
</pre>

The test is the main verb. <code>見(み)られる</code>,
<code>助(たす)けられる</code>, <code>呼(よ)び止(と)められる</code>,
<code>捕(つか)まる</code> all take the scene as their object, so they need `を`.
<code>来(く)る</code>, <code>現(あらわ)れる</code>,
<code>鳴(な)る</code> do not, so they take `に`／`へ`.

### ～ところに vs 間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間(あいだ)に</code> means "at some point inside this
stretch". <code>ところに</code> means "at exactly this instant", and it needs the
second event to break in.

</div>

<pre>
留守(るす)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、宅配便(たくはいびん)が届(とど)いたらしい。
A parcel seems to have arrived while I was out.
</pre>

You were not there to be interrupted; the delivery just happened somewhere inside
that window.

<pre>
出(で)かけようとしている<b>ところに</b>、宅配便(たくはいびん)が届(とど)いた。
The parcel arrived just as I was on my way out.
</pre>

Now the timing collides with you, which is precisely what
<code>間(あいだ)に</code> cannot express.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 出(で)かける<b>ところに</b>、テレビを見(み)た。 → ✅ 出(で)かける<b>ところに</b>、友(とも)だちが来(き)た。
  The main clause has to break in from outside. Something you chose to do next is
  just a sequence, and wants `てから` or a plain `て`.

- ❌ 帰(かえ)り<b>ますところへ</b> → ✅ 帰(かえ)る<b>ところへ</b>
  <code>ところ</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it is plain. Politeness
  goes on the end of the sentence.

- ❌ 昼寝(ひるね)している<b>ところに</b>写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)られた。 → ✅ 昼寝(ひるね)している<b>ところを</b>写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)られた。
  <code>撮(と)られる</code> takes the sleeping scene as its object, so the particle
  is `を`.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)出(で)かける<b>ところに</b>、友(とも)だちが来(き)ます。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)出(で)かけるときに、友(とも)だちが来(き)ます。
  <code>ところ</code> is about the immediate present of a scene, not a date in the
  diary. For a scheduled future, use <code>とき</code>.

## Practice

**1.** シャワーを浴(あ)びている____、宅配便(たくはいびん)が来(き)てしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

シャワーを浴(あ)びている<b>ところに</b>（or ところへ）

The `ている` sets the interruption in the middle of the shower, and a delivery
arriving is exactly the sort of outside event this pattern takes.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 試験中(しけんちゅう)にカンニングをしている（ところに・ところを）先生(せんせい)に見(み)つかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

カンニングをしている<b>ところを</b>先生(せんせい)に見(み)つかった。

<code>見(み)つかる</code> here takes the scene as what was found, so the particle
is `を`. With `に` the sentence would only say the teacher turned up, not that you
were caught.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Just as I was about to leave the office, the client rang."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会社(かいしゃ)を出(で)る<b>ところに</b>、取引先(とりひきさき)から電話(でんわ)がかかってきた。

"About to" is the dictionary form in front of <code>ところ</code>, and the call
arriving from outside is the intrusion the pattern needs.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 料理(りょうり)を作(つく)りますところへ、母(はは)が帰(かえ)ってきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 料理(りょうり)を作(つく)っている<b>ところへ</b>、母(はは)が帰(かえ)ってきた。

Two fixes. The clause in front of <code>ところ</code> is plain, never ます; and
`ている` is what places the interruption in the middle of the cooking rather than
before it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 勉強(べんきょう)している<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、友(とも)だちが来(き)た。
(b) 勉強(べんきょう)している<b>ところに</b>、友(とも)だちが来(き)た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) locates the visit somewhere inside the study session and treats the two as
merely co-occurring. It would be equally natural if you had been asleep at your
desk.

(b) makes the arrival land on you: you were at work on something, and the friend
cut across it. Depending on tone that is either an annoyance or a rescue, but
either way the two events are felt as connected.

</details>

# ～ところだった

**Meaning** · came within an inch of happening - and did not

<code>ところだった</code> describes an outcome that was one step away and never
arrived. Usually the speaker is relieved; sometimes they are only reporting that
they were on the very brink of acting when something cut in. Either way the event
in front of <code>ところ</code> **did not happen**, and that is the fact the whole
pattern turns on.

## Formation

The verb takes the **dictionary form**. Nothing else will do, and the た-form means
something else entirely.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ところだった | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れるところだった |
| Polite | dictionary form + ところでした | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れるところでした |
| With the averting condition | …たら／ば + ところだった | 寝坊(ねぼう)したら遅刻(ちこく)するところだった |
| Interrupted plan | dictionary form + ところだったが | 出(で)かける → 出(で)かけるところだったが |
| Usual companions | もう少(すこ)しで・危(あや)うく・あと少(すこ)しで | 危(あや)うく落(お)ちるところだった |
| **た-form = a different point** | た-form + ところだった = "had just done" | 着(つ)いたところだった |
| ❌ ます-form | ❌忘(わす)れますところでした | ✅忘(わす)れるところでした |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - dictionary form + <code>ところだった</code> means it **nearly**
happened. た-form + <code>ところだった</code> means it **had just** happened. One
vowel of okurigana separates "I nearly forgot" from "I had just forgotten".

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no useful negative form here. To say "I nearly failed to do X",
Japanese negates inside the clause instead:
<code>もう少(すこ)しで間(ま)に合(あ)わないところだった</code>, "I nearly
didn't make it."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あぶない。落(お)とす<b>ところだった</b>。
Careful - I nearly dropped it.
</pre>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)しで遅刻(ちこく)する<b>ところでした</b>。
I very nearly turned up late.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あや)うく財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れる<b>ところだった</b>。
I almost left my wallet behind.
</pre>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)をもらったとき、ちょうど家(いえ)を出(で)る<b>ところだった</b>。
When you rang I was just on my way out of the house.
</pre>

<pre>
信号(しんごう)を見(み)ていなかったら、事故(じこ)を起(お)こす<b>ところだった</b>。
If I hadn't been watching the light, I'd have caused an accident.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)って来(こ)なかったら、びしょぬれになる<b>ところでした</b>。
If I hadn't brought an umbrella I'd have been soaked through.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あや)うく大事(だいじ)なファイルを消(け)してしまう<b>ところだった</b>が、同僚(どうりょう)が止(と)めてくれた。
I came within a hair of deleting an important file, but a colleague stopped me.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)へ向(む)かう途中(とちゅう)で道(みち)に迷(まよ)ってしまい、もう少(すこ)しで開演(かいえん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わない<b>ところでした</b>。
I got lost on the way to the venue and very nearly missed the curtain.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and one of the most useful sentence endings in daily life. It is
just as natural in ます-form (遅(おく)れるところでした) as it is muttered to
yourself in plain form, and there is nothing formal or informal about it.

What it carries is **relief**, and Japanese usually makes that explicit. The
sentence is regularly preceded by あぶない, よかった or ああ, and reinforced by
もう少(すこ)しで or 危(あや)うく. Between the adverb and the ending, the
listener knows twice over that the bad thing was avoided.

The "was about to" use is quieter and often apologetic or explanatory - you are
accounting for where you were and what you were doing when something intervened:
「ちょうど出(で)る<b>ところだった</b>んです」. Add `のに` and it becomes regret at
being interrupted: 「やっと寝(ね)るところだったのに」.

Casual speech contracts <code>ところ</code> to <code>とこ</code> here as elsewhere:
「あぶない、転(ころ)ぶとこだった」. Keep the full form in writing.

## Contrast

### ～ところだった vs ～たところだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the dictionary form means it **never happened**. The `た`-form
means it **just happened**. This is the highest-value distinction on the page.

</div>

The nearest thing already taught is the N4 <code>ところ</code> family, not any of
the N3 timing patterns - and the divergence is entirely about which side of the
event you are standing on.

<pre>
駅(えき)に着(つ)く<b>ところだった</b>。
I was just about to reach the station.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)に着(つ)いた<b>ところだった</b>。
I had just reached the station.
</pre>

In the first the speaker is not there yet; in the second they are, and arrived
seconds ago. English "just" covers both, which is exactly why learners collapse
them. Read the okurigana before you decide what the sentence says.

### ～ところだった vs ～そうになった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>そうになる</code> describes a movement that **started** and
was arrested. <code>ところだった</code> describes an outcome that **never began**
but was one step away.

</div>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)で転(ころ)び<b>そうになった</b>。
I nearly fell on the stairs.
</pre>

Your foot slipped and you caught yourself: the falling actually got under way.

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)して、試験(しけん)を受(う)けられない<b>ところだった</b>。
I overslept and nearly missed sitting the exam.
</pre>

Nothing physical began here. Missing an exam is a state of affairs, not a motion,
so <code>そうになる</code> has nothing to fasten onto and
<code>ところだった</code> is the natural choice. Where a verb does describe a bodily
motion, both are possible and <code>そうになった</code> is the more vivid.

### ～ところだった vs ～つもりだった / ～はずだった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three translate as "was going to".
<code>ところだった</code> means you were **on the brink**;
<code>つもりだった</code> means you **intended**;
<code>はずだった</code> means it was **arranged**.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)する<b>つもりだった</b>が、やめた。
I was planning to study abroad next year, but I dropped the idea.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は3時(じ)に始(はじ)まる<b>はずだった</b>のに、誰(だれ)も来(こ)なかった。
The meeting was supposed to start at three, but nobody came.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょうど会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)める<b>ところだった</b>。
We were just about to start the meeting.
</pre>

Only the third one puts you at the very edge of the action. The first is about a
decision that could have been months away, and the second about an arrangement that
somebody else was party to. Note also that
<code>ところだった</code> refuses distant time words - ❌来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)するところだった
is not a sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 危(あや)うく忘(わす)れた<b>ところだった</b>。 → ✅ 危(あや)うく忘(わす)れる<b>ところだった</b>。
  With `た` the sentence claims you *did* forget. For "nearly", the verb must be in
  the dictionary form.

- ❌ もう少(すこ)しで終電(しゅうでん)に乗(の)り遅(おく)れました。 → ✅ もう少(すこ)しで終電(しゅうでん)に乗(の)り遅(おく)れる<b>ところでした</b>。
  もう少(すこ)しで sets up a near miss, but a plain past verb reports that it
  actually happened. The two halves contradict each other.

- ❌ 遅刻(ちこく)し<b>ますところでした</b>。 → ✅ 遅刻(ちこく)する<b>ところでした</b>。
  <code>ところ</code> is a noun and takes a plain clause. The politeness sits on
  `でした` at the end.

- ❌ 来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>ところだった</b>。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>つもりだった</b>。
  <code>ところ</code> means the immediate brink of an action, so it cannot be
  stretched to next month. A cancelled plan is <code>つもりだった</code>.

## Practice

**1.** あぶない、もう少(すこ)しで階段(かいだん)から落(お)ち____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

落(お)ちる<b>ところだった</b>

もう少(すこ)しで announces a near miss, and the ending that completes it is the
dictionary form plus <code>ところだった</code>. The fall did not happen.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 「もしもし。」「あ、ちょうど電話(でんわ)を（かけるところでした・かけたところでした）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ちょうど電話(でんわ)を<b>かけるところでした</b>。

You had not yet dialled - you were about to, and they beat you to it. With
<code>かけたところでした</code> you would be saying you had already made the call,
which makes no sense as an answer to their ringing you.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If the neighbour hadn't noticed the smoke, the house would nearly have burned down."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)が煙(けむり)に気(き)づかなかったら、家(いえ)が燃(も)えてしまう<b>ところだった</b>。

The `たら` clause supplies what averted it, and
<code>ところだった</code> supplies the outcome that never came about. English "would
have" maps onto this pattern very often.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 危(あや)うく大切(たいせつ)な約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れますところでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 危(あや)うく大切(たいせつ)な約束(やくそく)を<b>忘(わす)れるところでした</b>。

The ます-form cannot sit in front of a noun. <code>ところ</code> takes the plain
dictionary form; the sentence is still perfectly polite, because `でした` is doing
that work.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べる<b>ところだった</b>。
(b) 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べた<b>ところだった</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the speaker with chopsticks in hand and nothing yet eaten - typically said
to explain why the timing of something else was awkward.

(b) puts the meal in the past by a matter of minutes: they had only just finished.
Both are "just", but (a) is before the event and (b) is after it, and the only
signal is the form of the verb.

</details>

# もう少しで

**Meaning** · almost; nearly; a little more and…

<code>もう少(すこ)しで</code> measures a remaining distance and finds it tiny. It
does two jobs with that one measurement: "we are nearly there", said of something
still coming, and "we nearly had a disaster", said of something that never arrived
at all.

## Usage

The word is transparent: <code>もう少(すこ)し</code> is "a little more" and `で`
turns it into "with a little more". Everything then depends on what the rest of the
sentence does with that little.

With an ordinary **non-past** clause it means the thing is close and still on its
way - <code>もう少(すこ)しで春(はる)だ</code>, <code>もう少(すこ)しで着(つ)きます</code>.
Nothing is being avoided here; the sentence is simply counting down.

With a **near-miss ending** it means the opposite: the thing came within a hair and
did not happen. The endings that do this are
<code>ところだった</code>, <code>そうになった</code> and
<code>てしまうところだった</code>. This is worth being clear about, because
<code>もう少(すこ)しで</code> **does not itself create** the "nearly" meaning - the
ending does. On its own in front of a plain past verb the adverb is helpless, which
is where the pattern's one real trap lies.

Two forms sit right beside it. <code>もうちょっとで</code> is the casual version and
is extremely common in speech. <code>あと少(すこ)しで</code> is close to
interchangeable, and leans a shade more towards counting down what is left.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>夏(なつ)だ。
It's nearly summer.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>宿題(しゅくだい)が終(お)わる。
I'm almost done with my homework.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>大切(たいせつ)な約束(やくそく)をすっぽかすところだった。
I came within a hair of standing someone up on an important appointment.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>完成(かんせい)というところで、パソコンが固(かた)まってしまった。
With the thing all but finished, the computer froze on me.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Completely register-neutral. It is as ordinary in a text message as in a report, it
carries no gender or age marking, and it needs no politeness adjustment of its own -
the ending of the sentence handles that.

Tone comes from which of the two uses you are in. The countdown use is cheerful and
encouraging; it is what you say to someone struggling up a hill. The near-miss use
is charged with relief, and in speech it usually arrives with あぶない, ああ or
よかった attached, plus a `ところだった` at the far end. Between the adverb and the
ending, a listener hears twice over that nothing bad actually happened.

One structural habit is worth copying: <code>もう少(すこ)しで…というところで</code>
freezes a scene at the last moment before completion - もう少(すこ)しで優勝(ゆうしょう)というところで
逆転(ぎゃくてん)された. It is a neat, natural-sounding shape and learners rarely
produce it.

## Contrast

### もう少(すこ)しで vs もう少(すこ)し

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `で` it modifies a whole **event** ("a little more and X").
Without `で` it modifies a **quantity or degree** ("a little more X").

</div>

<pre>
<b>もう少(すこ)し</b>待(ま)ってください。
Please wait a little longer.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>順番(じゅんばん)が回(まわ)ってきます。
Your turn will come round very shortly.
</pre>

The first asks for more of something - more waiting, more salt, a bigger size. The
second says the event is nearly upon us. Dropping or adding the `で` changes which
of those two you said, and the mistake is common enough to be worth a deliberate
check.

### もう少(すこ)しで vs 危(あや)うく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>危(あや)うく</code> is **only** for near misses, and only
bad ones. <code>もう少(すこ)しで</code> covers near misses **and** ordinary
countdowns.

</div>

<pre>
<b>危(あや)うく</b>反対(はんたい)方向(ほうこう)の電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)るところだった。
I almost got on a train going the wrong way.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>目的地(もくてきち)です。
We're nearly at our destination.
</pre>

Swap them and only one direction works.
<code>もう少(すこ)しで</code> would be fine in the first sentence;
<code>危(あや)うく</code> is impossible in the second, because arriving is not a
danger. <code>危(あや)うく</code> is also the stronger and slightly more written
of the two, and it always wants a `ところだった` behind it.

### もう少(すこ)しで vs まもなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the countdown sense, <code>まもなく</code> measures **time
until** an event. <code>もう少(すこ)しで</code> measures **how much is left** of
whatever you are doing.

</div>

<pre>
<b>まもなく</b>電車(でんしゃ)が参(まい)ります。
The train will arrive shortly.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>頂上(ちょうじょう)だ。がんばろう。
Nearly at the summit - keep going.
</pre>

<code>まもなく</code> is an announcement word: stations, airports, broadcasts. It is
formal, it says nothing about effort, and it cannot be used for near misses at all.
<code>もう少(すこ)しで</code> is personal and encouraging, and it is the one you
use when there is a remaining stretch someone has to get through.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>コップを割(わ)った。 → ✅ <b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>コップを割(わ)るところだった。
  A plain past verb says the glass broke, which contradicts
  <code>もう少(すこ)しで</code>. The adverb needs a near-miss ending to lean on.

- ❌ <b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>待(ま)ってください。 → ✅ <b>もう少(すこ)し</b>待(ま)ってください。
  Asking for more waiting is a matter of degree, so the `で` has no place. This is
  the single most frequent slip with this word.

- ❌ <b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>大(おお)きいサイズはありますか。 → ✅ <b>もう少(すこ)し</b>大(おお)きいサイズはありますか。
  Same fault inside a noun phrase: what is "a little more" here is the size, not an
  event.

- ❌ <b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)するところだった。 → ✅ <b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>留学(りゅうがく)をあきらめるところだった。
  A near miss is a matter of a hair's breadth, not of months.
  <code>もう少(すこ)しで</code> will not stretch to a distant date.

## Practice

**1.** あぶない！ ____自転車(じてんしゃ)とぶつかるところだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>（or 危(あや)うく）

The `ところだった` ending is already there, so what the blank needs is the adverb
that measures how close it came. あぶない at the head of the sentence tells you it
is a near miss rather than a countdown.

</details>

**2.** Choose: すみません、（もう少(すこ)し・もう少(すこ)しで）静(しず)かにしていただけますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もう少(すこ)し</b>静(しず)かにしていただけますか。

You are asking for more quiet - a degree, not an event. The `で` form would try to
mean "a little more and it will be quiet", which is not a request at all.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) <b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>仕事(しごと)が終(お)わる。
(b) <b>もう少(すこ)しで</b>仕事(しごと)が終(お)わらないところだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a countdown. The work is nearly done and the speaker is reporting progress,
probably with some satisfaction.

(b) is a near miss, and the negative inside it is doing the work: the job very
nearly failed to get finished. The relief in (b) is the opposite feeling from the
steady progress in (a), and the only structural difference is the ending the adverb
is leaning on.

</details>

# ～つつある

**Meaning** · is in the process of changing - the shift has begun, has a direction, and is not finished

<code>つつある</code> is not a general "-ing". It reports a **change** that is under
way and heading somewhere: numbers rising, a patient recovering, a custom
disappearing. If nothing is changing, this is the wrong pattern, however ongoing
the action is.

## Formation

<code>つつある</code> follows the **ます-stem**, exactly like the older
<code>つつ</code> it is built from.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + つつある | 増(ふ)える → 増(ふ)えつつある |
| する-verb | ます-stem + つつある | 回復(かいふく)する → 回復(かいふく)しつつある |
| Polite | ます-stem + つつあります | 減(へ)る → 減(へ)りつつあります |
| Past | ます-stem + つつあった | 変(か)わる → 変(か)わりつつあった |
| Passive | passive ます-stem + つつある | 失(うしな)われる → 失(うしな)われつつある |
| Modifying a noun | ます-stem + つつある + Noun | 消(き)えつつある方言(ほうげん) |
| ❌ Dictionary form | ❌増(ふ)えるつつある | ✅増(ふ)えつつある |
| ❌ Plain activity verb | ❌本(ほん)を読(よ)みつつある | ✅本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb must name a **change of state**: 増(ふ)える,
減(へ)る, 変(か)わる, 進(すす)む, 広(ひろ)がる, 回復(かいふく)する,
発展(はってん)する, 失(うしな)われる, 近(ちか)づく. Verbs of plain activity -
読(よ)む, 食(た)べる, 走(はし)る - have no direction to move in and take
`ている` instead.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not confuse this with bare <code>つつ</code>, which is a separate,
literary connector meaning "while" or "although"
(悪(わる)いと知(し)りつつ, "knowing it was wrong"). Same morpheme, same stem,
completely different job - and the giveaway is whether ある follows.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この町(まち)の人口(じんこう)は減(へ)り<b>つつある</b>。
This town's population is falling.
</pre>

<pre>
景気(けいき)は少(すこ)しずつ回復(かいふく)し<b>つつあります</b>。
The economy is gradually recovering.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)は北(きた)へ進(すす)み<b>つつある</b>。
The typhoon is moving north.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)の体調(たいちょう)は、ゆっくりとよくなり<b>つつある</b>。
My grandfather's condition is slowly improving.
</pre>

<pre>
この村(むら)に古(ふる)くから伝(つた)わる習慣(しゅうかん)は失(うしな)われ<b>つつある</b>。
The customs handed down in this village are being lost.
</pre>

<pre>
働(はたら)き方(かた)についての考(かんが)え方(かた)は、この10年(ねん)で大(おお)きく変(か)わり<b>つつある</b>。
Attitudes to how we work have been changing a great deal over the past decade.
</pre>

<pre>
再生(さいせい)エネルギーの利用(りよう)は、世界(せかい)各国(かっこく)で急速(きゅうそく)に広(ひろ)がり<b>つつある</b>。
The use of renewable energy is spreading rapidly in countries around the world.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい治療法(ちりょうほう)のおかげで、この病気(びょうき)は治(なお)る病気(びょうき)になり<b>つつある</b>。
Thanks to a new treatment, this illness is becoming a curable one.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** pattern. Newspapers, white papers, academic prose, company
reports and formal speeches are its home ground, and it is one of the cheapest ways
to make a sentence sound like published Japanese. In conversation it is rare;
people say <code>だんだん増(ふ)えている</code> or
<code>増(ふ)えてきている</code> instead. Using it over dinner is not wrong, but
it will sound as though you are quoting a broadcast - which is occasionally the
joke people intend.

Its emotional temperature is low. Unlike several of its neighbours it passes no
judgement: a population can be falling and a patient recovering in the same
register. What it always insists on is that the change is **not complete**. That is
why it sits badly with 完全(かんぜん)に or もう, and why it so often appears with
少(すこ)しずつ, ゆっくりと, 急速(きゅうそく)に and
着実(ちゃくじつ)に - adverbs of rate, which is the only thing left to say once
direction is settled.

The noun-modifying use is worth practising:
<code>失(うしな)われつつある技術(ぎじゅつ)</code>, "skills that are being lost".
It is compact, it sounds adult, and learners almost never reach for it.

## Contrast

### ～つつある vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` covers **any** ongoing action or resulting state.
<code>つつある</code> covers only a **change in progress**, and adds that it has
further to go.

</div>

<pre>
雪(ゆき)が溶(と)け<b>ている</b>。
The snow is melting. / The snow has melted.
</pre>

That sentence is genuinely ambiguous in Japanese - it can describe the melting or
the puddle it left.

<pre>
雪(ゆき)が溶(と)け<b>つつある</b>。
The snow is in the process of melting.
</pre>

<code>つつある</code> removes the ambiguity: the snow is neither untouched nor gone,
and the sentence is a snapshot of the middle. Where `ている` would be ambiguous or
merely descriptive, <code>つつある</code> is precise and formal. Going the other
way, `ている` is the only option for an activity that is not a change -
❌走(はし)りつつある is not Japanese.

### ～つつある vs ～一方(いっぽう)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both describe change with a direction.
<code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> says it **keeps going and shows no sign of stopping**,
usually with dismay. <code>つつある</code> is **neutral** and often implies an
endpoint being approached.

</div>

<code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> is the N3 pattern this one sits closest to, and the
divergence is attitude plus endpoint.

<pre>
医療費(いりょうひ)は増(ふ)える<b>一方(いっぽう)だ</b>。
Medical costs just keep going up.
</pre>

There is a note of alarm there, and no suggestion of a ceiling.

<pre>
待(ま)ち時間(じかん)は短(みじか)くなり<b>つつある</b>。
Waiting times are getting shorter.
</pre>

Flat report of a trend, with an implied destination - shorter, and heading towards
acceptable. Note the forms too: <code>一方(いっぽう)だ</code> takes the dictionary
form, <code>つつある</code> takes the ます-stem.

### ～つつある vs ～つつ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with ある it means "**is changing**". Without ある it means
"**while**" or "**even though**", and needs a second clause.

</div>

<pre>
体(からだ)に悪(わる)いと知(し)り<b>つつ</b>、夜(よる)遅(おそ)くまで飲(の)んでしまった。
Even knowing it was bad for me, I drank until late.
</pre>

<pre>
体力(たいりょく)は落(お)ち<b>つつある</b>。
My fitness is declining.
</pre>

The two are built on the same stem and the same particle, which is why they get
mixed up on paper. The test is mechanical: if the sentence stops there,
<code>つつある</code>; if another clause follows and the sense is "while" or "even
so", bare <code>つつ</code>. Both are literary, and neither belongs in casual
speech.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今(いま)レポートを書(か)き<b>つつある</b>。 → ✅ 今(いま)レポートを書(か)い<b>ている</b>。
  Writing a report is an activity, not a change of state. There is no direction for
  <code>つつある</code> to point in.

- ❌ 物価(ぶっか)が上(あ)がる<b>つつある</b>。 → ✅ 物価(ぶっか)が上(あ)がり<b>つつある</b>。
  The base is the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

- ❌ 来月(らいげつ)、大学生(だいがくせい)になり<b>つつある</b>。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)、大学生(だいがくせい)になる。
  Becoming a student happens on one day. A change has to be gradual before it can
  be "in progress".

- ❌ 音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>つつある</b>勉強(べんきょう)する。 → ✅ 音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>つつ</b>勉強(べんきょう)する。
  "While listening" is bare <code>つつ</code>. Adding ある turns the clause into a
  claim that the listening is itself changing.

## Practice

**1.** 地球(ちきゅう)の平均(へいきん)気温(きおん)は年々(ねんねん)上昇(じょうしょう)し____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

上昇(じょうしょう)し<b>つつある</b>

<code>上昇(じょうしょう)する</code> is a change with a direction, the subject is a
long-term trend, and the sentence is written in report register - three signals all
pointing the same way.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 弟(おとうと)は今(いま)、自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)でゲームを（しつつある・している）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)でゲームを<b>している</b>。

Playing a game is an activity, not a change of state, so
<code>つつある</code> has nothing to work with. The casual subject matter rules it
out a second time.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The number of small bookshops in this area is steadily decreasing."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この地域(ちいき)の小(ちい)さな書店(しょてん)の数(かず)は、着実(ちゃくじつ)に減(へ)り<b>つつある</b>。

A countable trend moving in one direction, described in written register. The
adverb 着実(ちゃくじつ)に is the kind of rate word this pattern attracts.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この地方(ちほう)の方言(ほうげん)は完全(かんぜん)に消(き)えつつある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この地方(ちほう)の方言(ほうげん)は消(き)え<b>つつある</b>。（or 完全(かんぜん)に消(き)えてしまった）

<code>つつある</code> states that the change is **not** finished, so
完全(かんぜん)に contradicts it. Drop the adverb, or drop the pattern and say
outright that the dialect is gone.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 森(もり)が減(へ)っ<b>ている</b>。
(b) 森(もり)が減(へ)り<b>つつある</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the everyday statement. It reports the situation and would be at home in
conversation; on its own it can even be read as a settled result rather than an
ongoing process.

(b) fixes the sentence in the middle of a movement and is unambiguous about it -
the forest is neither intact nor gone. It also raises the register sharply, and
would be the natural choice in a report or a news script.

</details>

# ～たあげく

**Meaning** · after all that, it ended up like this - and the speaker is not pleased

<code>あげく</code> reports the last stop of a long, wearing process: dithering,
arguing, searching, being messed about. Two things are always true. The process
**dragged on**, and the outcome is one the speaker regards as a poor return for
it - often absurd, often a waste of everything that went before.

## Formation

<code>あげく</code> is the noun 挙(あ)げ句(く), "the closing line of a linked
poem", so it takes a noun-modifying clause: a **た-form** verb, or a noun with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + あげく | 悩(なや)む → 悩(なや)んだあげく |
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + あげくに | 迷(まよ)う → 迷(まよ)ったあげくに |
| Noun | Noun + の + あげく | 口論(こうろん) → 口論(こうろん)のあげく |
| Emphatic | あげくの果(は)てに | - → あげくの果(は)てに泣(な)き出(だ)した |
| ❌ Dictionary form | ❌悩(なや)むあげく | ✅悩(なや)んだあげく |
| ❌ Noun with no の | ❌口論(こうろん)あげく | ✅口論(こうろん)のあげく |
| ❌ Future or plan | ❌…あげく、辞(や)めるつもりだ | ✅…あげく、辞(や)めてしまった |
| ❌ An instant process | ❌ひと目(め)見(み)たあげく | ✅さんざん見(み)比(くら)べたあげく |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three conditions, and all three must hold. The first clause is
**long and troublesome**, the main clause is **past and definite**, and the outcome
is **bad, wasteful or absurd**. Lose the third and you want
<code>末(すえ)に</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - normally written in kana. The kanji 挙(あ)げ句(く) exists and is
occasionally used, but あげく is the ordinary spelling and the one to produce.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
さんざん考(かんが)え<b>たあげく</b>、旅行(りょこう)を中止(ちゅうし)した。
After agonising over it endlessly, we called the trip off.
</pre>

<pre>
長(なが)い間(あいだ)悩(なや)ん<b>だあげく</b>、彼(かれ)は会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めてしまった。
After a long period of wrestling with it, he ended up quitting the company.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)に迷(まよ)っ<b>たあげく</b>、集合(しゅうごう)場所(ばしょ)にたどり着(つ)けなかった。
After getting thoroughly lost, I never made it to the meeting point at all.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も話(はな)し合(あ)っ<b>たあげくに</b>、二人(ふたり)は別(わか)れることになった。
After talking it over again and again, the two of them ended up splitting up.
</pre>

<pre>
長時間(ちょうじかん)の口論(こうろん)<b>のあげく</b>、会議(かいぎ)は何(なに)も決(き)まらずに終(お)わった。
After hours of argument, the meeting broke up without deciding anything.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)い部屋(へや)を探(さが)して一日中(いちにちじゅう)歩(ある)い<b>たあげく</b>、結局(けっきょく)駅前(えきまえ)の不動産屋(ふどうさんや)に戻(もど)ってきた。
After walking all day looking for a cheap flat, I ended up back at the estate agent by the station.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)は部下(ぶか)を一時間(いちじかん)叱(しか)っ<b>たあげく</b>、それが自分(じぶん)のミスだったことに気(き)づいた。
After laying into his subordinate for an hour, the boss realised the mistake had been his own.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なん)週間(しゅうかん)も修理(しゅうり)に出(だ)し<b>たあげく</b>、結局(けっきょく)新(あたら)しいものを買(か)うはめになった。
After weeks of sending it off for repair, I ended up having to buy a new one anyway.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral in formality - equally at home in a novel, a complaint to a friend and a
newspaper column - but never neutral in **attitude**. Using
<code>あげく</code> is a small act of commentary: you are saying the effort was not
worth it. It pairs naturally with さんざん, 何度(なんど)も, 長(なが)い間(あいだ),
結局(けっきょく) and endings in `てしまう`, all of which push the same way.

Aimed at yourself it is rueful and rather likeable. Aimed at somebody else it is
close to an accusation, and <code>あげくの果(は)てに</code> - "and to cap it all"
- is openly indignant: 「文句(もんく)を言(い)い続(つづ)け、あげくの果(は)てに帰(かえ)ってしまった」.
Keep that one for situations where you mean it.

Because it delivers a verdict on something finished, the pattern cannot look
forward. No plans, no predictions, no requests. And it needs a stretch of time in
front of it - a decision made in three seconds has no
<code>あげく</code>, however bad the result.

## Contrast

### ～たあげく vs ～た末(すえ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical structure, opposite verdict.
<code>あげく</code> means the effort was **wasted**;
<code>末(すえ)に</code> is **neutral** and often crowns a genuine effort with a
real result.

</div>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)考(かんが)え続(つづ)け<b>た末(すえ)に</b>、留学(りゅうがく)を決(き)めた。
After a year of thinking it through, I decided to study abroad.
</pre>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)迷(まよ)っ<b>たあげく</b>、留学(りゅうがく)をあきらめた。
After dithering for a year, I gave up on studying abroad.
</pre>

Both took a year. The first presents that year as deliberation that led somewhere;
the second presents it as time thrown away. Swapping the two endings would make the
first sentence sneer at its own decision and the second sound oddly proud of
failing.

### ～たあげく vs ～た結果(けっか)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>結果(けっか)</code> states an outcome **objectively**
and explains what caused it. <code>あげく</code> adds duration and a sigh.

</div>

<code>た結果(けっか)</code> is the N3 pattern <code>あげく</code> is closest to,
and the divergence is that <code>結果(けっか)</code> has no feelings.

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)し<b>た結果(けっか)</b>、原因(げんいん)は配線(はいせん)のミスだと分(わ)かった。
Investigation showed the cause to be a wiring error.
</pre>

That sentence would be at home in an official report, where
<code>あげく</code> would be unusable - it would suggest the investigators had
wasted everyone's time. <code>結果(けっか)</code> is also free of the duration
requirement: a single test can have a <code>結果(けっか)</code>, but only a long
ordeal has an <code>あげく</code>.

### ～たあげく vs ～た後(あと)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>後(あと)で</code> merely puts two events in **order**.
<code>あげく</code> makes the first one the drawn-out cause of a disappointing
second.

</div>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)を片付(かたづ)け<b>た後(あと)で</b>、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)きます。
I'll go shopping after I've put the luggage away.
</pre>

Neutral sequencing, and note that it can point at the future - something
<code>あげく</code> can never do.

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)び続(つづ)け<b>たあげく</b>、腰(こし)を痛(いた)めてしまった。
After hauling boxes about for hours on end, I ended up hurting my back.
</pre>

Here the first clause is not just earlier, it is what wore the speaker down. Drop
<code>あげく</code> for <code>後(あと)で</code> and the sentence stops explaining
anything.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ さんざん悩(なや)む<b>あげく</b>、断(ことわ)った。 → ✅ さんざん悩(なや)ん<b>だあげく</b>、断(ことわ)った。
  <code>あげく</code> is a noun, so the clause in front is in noun-modifying shape -
  and specifically the た-form, because the process is over.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)し<b>たあげく</b>、大会(たいかい)で優勝(ゆうしょう)した。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)し<b>た末(すえ)に</b>、大会(たいかい)で優勝(ゆうしょう)した。
  A good outcome kills <code>あげく</code> outright. Winning is what
  <code>末(すえ)に</code> is for.

- ❌ 迷(まよ)っ<b>たあげく</b>、明日(あした)決(き)めます。 → ✅ 迷(まよ)っ<b>たあげく</b>、昨日(きのう)決(き)めました。
  The verdict is passed on something already finished. There is no future
  <code>あげく</code>.

- ❌ 長(なが)い議論(ぎろん)<b>あげく</b> → ✅ 長(なが)い議論(ぎろん)<b>のあげく</b>
  A noun needs `の` in front of it, exactly as it would before any other noun.

## Practice

**1.** 何時間(なんじかん)も列(れつ)に並(なら)んだ____、目当(めあ)ての商品(しょうひん)は売(う)り切(き)れていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

並(なら)んだ<b>あげく</b>

Hours of queueing followed by an empty shelf is the textbook shape: a long ordeal,
a past outcome, and the outcome makes the ordeal pointless.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 三年間(さんねんかん)研究(けんきゅう)を続(つづ)けた（あげく・末(すえ)に）、ついに新(あたら)しい薬(くすり)を完成(かんせい)させた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三年間(さんねんかん)研究(けんきゅう)を続(つづ)けた<b>末(すえ)に</b>、ついに新(あたら)しい薬(くすり)を完成(かんせい)させた。

The outcome is a success, and ついに celebrates it.
<code>あげく</code> would turn three years of research into a sad story, which is
the opposite of what the sentence says.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "After keeping us waiting for two hours, he ended up not turning up at all."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

二時間(にじかん)も待(ま)たせ<b>たあげく</b>、彼(かれ)は結局(けっきょく)来(こ)なかった。

Long, trying, and a thoroughly annoying result - all three conditions met. Note
<code>来(こ)なかった</code>: the negative of <code>来(く)る</code> takes the
こ- stem.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 長(なが)い相談(そうだん)あげく、来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)すつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 長(なが)い相談(そうだん)<b>のあげく</b>、引(ひ)っ越(こ)すことになってしまった。

Two faults. A noun takes `の` before <code>あげく</code>; and the main clause cannot
be a plan, because <code>あげく</code> passes judgement on a process that has already
run its course.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 散々(さんざん)迷(まよ)っ<b>たあげく</b>、一番(いちばん)安(やす)いものを買(か)った。
(b) 散々(さんざん)迷(まよ)っ<b>た末(すえ)に</b>、一番(いちばん)安(やす)いものを買(か)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is self-mocking. All that agonising, and the speaker went for the cheapest item
anyway - the buying up front makes the deliberation look silly.

(b) treats the same deliberation as work that produced a considered choice. The
cheapest item was what the thinking arrived at, not what the thinking was wasted
on.

</details>

# ～た末 / ～の末

**Meaning** · after a long process of effort or deliberation, this is where it came out

<code>末(すえ)</code> is the far end of something that took a while: months of
thinking, hours of negotiation, years of struggle. The sentence presents the
outcome as the **conclusion that process arrived at**. Unlike its close cousin
<code>あげく</code>, it passes no negative verdict - the effort is treated as having
been real work.

## Formation

<code>末(すえ)</code> is a noun, so it takes a た-form clause or a noun with `の`.
The following `に` is optional and very common.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + 末(すえ)（に） | 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)えた末(すえ)に |
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + 末(すえ) | 話(はな)し合(あ)う → 話(はな)し合(あ)った末(すえ) |
| Noun | Noun + の + 末(すえ)（に） | 苦労(くろう) → 苦労(くろう)の末(すえ) |
| Modifying a noun | …末(すえ)の + Noun | 努力(どりょく) → 努力(どりょく)の末(すえ)の成功(せいこう) |
| ❌ Dictionary form | ❌考(かんが)える末(すえ) | ✅考(かんが)えた末(すえ) |
| ❌ Noun with no の | ❌苦労(くろう)末(すえ) | ✅苦労(くろう)の末(すえ) |
| ❌ A momentary process | ❌ちょっと見(み)た末(すえ)に | ✅何度(なんど)も見(み)比(くら)べた末(すえ)に |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause in front must describe something **sustained and
purposeful**: thinking, discussing, searching, struggling. The main clause reports
what it finally produced, and normally reports it in the **past**.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading here is <code>すえ</code>. The same character is
<code>まつ</code> in 週末(しゅうまつ), 月末(げつまつ) and 年末(ねんまつ),
where it means "the end of a period" rather than "the far end of a process". Only
<code>すえ</code> works in this grammar point.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
長(なが)い話(はな)し合(あ)いの<b>末(すえ)に</b>、やっと結論(けつろん)が出(で)た。
After a long discussion we finally reached a conclusion.
</pre>

<pre>
よく考(かんが)えた<b>末(すえ)に</b>、この道(みち)を選(えら)んだ。
I chose this path after thinking it through carefully.
</pre>

<pre>
苦労(くろう)の<b>末(すえ)</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は自分(じぶん)の店(みせ)を持(も)った。
After a hard struggle she got a shop of her own.
</pre>

<pre>
激(はげ)しい議論(ぎろん)の<b>末(すえ)</b>、新(あたら)しい案(あん)が採用(さいよう)された。
After a fierce debate the new proposal was adopted.
</pre>

<pre>
両親(りょうしん)と何度(なんど)も話(はな)し合(あ)った<b>末(すえ)に</b>、一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしを許(ゆる)してもらった。
After talking it over with my parents many times, they let me live on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)に及(およ)ぶ手術(しゅじゅつ)の<b>末(すえ)</b>、患者(かんじゃ)の命(いのち)は救(すく)われた。
After an operation lasting three hours, the patient's life was saved.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も設計(せっけい)をやり直(なお)した<b>末(すえ)に</b>、ようやく納得(なっとく)のいく作品(さくひん)ができた。
After redoing the design over and over, I finally produced something I was happy with.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)の努力(どりょく)の<b>末(すえ)に</b>手(て)に入(い)れた資格(しかく)だから、大切(たいせつ)にしたい。
It's a qualification I got through years of effort, so I want to look after it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>末(すえ)</code> leans **formal and written**. Newspapers use it constantly -
<code>協議(きょうぎ)の末(すえ)、合意(ごうい)に達(たっ)した</code> is close
to boilerplate - and it turns up in company announcements, court reports and
biography. In speech it is used, but it sounds considered rather than chatty; a
friend describing a shopping trip would more likely say
<code>さんざん迷(まよ)って</code>.

The tone is one of respect for the process. Even when the outcome is unwelcome, the
sentence treats the deliberation as having been serious:
<code>検討(けんとう)の末(すえ)、今回(こんかい)は見送(みおく)ることになりました</code>
is a polite refusal precisely because <code>末(すえ)</code> tells the reader you
did not decide lightly. That makes it genuinely useful in business writing.

Two shapes are worth taking whole. <code>…の末(すえ)の…</code> compresses the
whole story into a noun phrase - <code>苦労(くろう)の末(すえ)の成功(せいこう)</code>,
"success won the hard way". And <code>末(すえ)に</code> attracts
やっと, ようやく and ついに in the main clause, which is a reliable signal that you
have picked the right pattern.

## Contrast

### ～た末(すえ) vs ～たあげく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same structure, opposite verdict.
<code>末(すえ)</code> respects the process; <code>あげく</code> says it was
wasted.

</div>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)準備(じゅんび)をした<b>末(すえ)に</b>、店(みせ)を開(ひら)いた。
After a year of preparation, I opened the shop.
</pre>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)準備(じゅんび)をした<b>あげく</b>、開店(かいてん)をあきらめた。
After a year of preparation, I ended up giving up on opening at all.
</pre>

The first outcome justifies the year; the second wipes it out.
<code>あげく</code> also insists the outcome be disagreeable, while
<code>末(すえ)</code> is free to be good, bad or simply final - a decision to
postpone is perfectly comfortable with <code>末(すえ)</code>.

### ～た末(すえ) vs ～た結果(けっか)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>結果(けっか)</code> reports **cause and effect** and does
not care how long it took. <code>末(すえ)</code> insists the process was
**long**, and puts the weight on the process rather than the causal link.

</div>

<code>た結果(けっか)</code> is the N3 pattern <code>末(すえ)</code> is closest
to, and this is where they part.

<pre>
アンケートを集計(しゅうけい)し<b>た結果(けっか)</b>、意外(いがい)な傾向(けいこう)が見(み)えてきた。
Tallying the questionnaires revealed an unexpected trend.
</pre>

Nobody suffered for that; the counting simply produced a finding, and it might have
taken an afternoon. Swap in <code>末(すえ)に</code> and you imply weeks of
grinding work.

<pre>
三年(さんねん)争(あらそ)った<b>末(すえ)に</b>、両者(りょうしゃ)は和解(わかい)した。
After three years of dispute, the two sides settled.
</pre>

Here the three years are the point. <code>結果(けっか)</code> would flatten them
into a mere cause.

### ～た末(すえ) vs ～た上(うえ)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>末(すえ)</code> looks **back** at a long process that is
over. <code>上(うえ)で</code> looks **forward**: first do X, and only then do Y.

</div>

<pre>
よく検討(けんとう)し<b>た上(うえ)で</b>、ご返事(へんじ)いたします。
I'll give you my answer after due consideration.
</pre>

That is a promise about what is still to come, and
<code>末(すえ)</code> cannot take its place - there is no completed process to
look back on yet.

<pre>
よく検討(けんとう)し<b>た末(すえ)に</b>、お断(ことわ)りすることにしました。
After careful consideration, we have decided to decline.
</pre>

Now the thinking is finished and the sentence reports where it landed.
<code>上(うえ)で</code> also carries a sense of *necessary preliminary* - you
cannot properly answer without considering - which <code>末(すえ)</code> does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 考(かんが)える<b>末(すえ)に</b>決(き)めました。 → ✅ 考(かんが)えた<b>末(すえ)に</b>決(き)めました。
  The process is over by the time you report it, so the verb in front is `た`.

- ❌ 苦労(くろう)<b>末(すえ)</b>、夢(ゆめ)をかなえた。 → ✅ 苦労(くろう)<b>の末(すえ)</b>、夢(ゆめ)をかなえた。
  <code>末(すえ)</code> is a noun and a noun in front of it needs `の`.

- ❌ ずっと迷(まよ)った<b>末(すえ)</b>、時間(じかん)を無駄(むだ)にしただけだった。 → ✅ ずっと迷(まよ)った<b>あげく</b>、時間(じかん)を無駄(むだ)にしただけだった。
  If the whole point is that the effort came to nothing, the pattern you want is
  <code>あげく</code>. <code>末(すえ)</code> would be quietly at odds with the
  main clause.

- ❌ ちょっと迷(まよ)った<b>末(すえ)に</b>、コーヒーにした。 → ✅ 少(すこ)し迷(まよ)って、コーヒーにした。
  Ten seconds at a counter is not a process.
  <code>末(すえ)</code> needs something substantial in front of it or it sounds
  like a joke.

交渉(こうしょう)した<b>末(すえ)に</b>

Hours of negotiation is a long, purposeful process, and ようやく in the main clause
is the marker that goes with <code>末(すえ)</code>. The verb in front takes `た`.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A whole day spent and nothing to show for it is a wasted effort, which is

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A noun phrase plus `の` plus <code>末(すえ)</code>, and ついに in the main clause.
The sustained, purposeful effort is exactly what this pattern honours.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Two faults. The noun 議論(ぎろん) needs `の`; and the main clause should report

<code>ことになりました</code> makes the decision the outcome.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 検討(けんとう)し<b>た上(うえ)で</b>、ご連絡(れんらく)します。
(b) 検討(けんとう)し<b>た末(すえ)に</b>、ご連絡(れんらく)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a commitment about the future: the considering has not happened yet, and the
sentence promises that it will happen first, before any answer comes.

(b) looks back on considering that is finished, presents it as having taken real
time, and reports the contact as its conclusion. In business writing (a) buys you
time; (b) explains why your answer took so long.

</details>

# ～にわたって

**Meaning** · spanning the whole of it - over a stretch of time, across an area, or through a series of occasions

<code>にわたって</code> takes a measured extent and says the event filled it. What it
adds over a plain "for three hours" is **scale**: the speaker thinks the span is
worth remarking on. It is one of the standard ways Japanese news writing signals
that something was long, wide or repeated.

## Formation

<code>にわたって</code> attaches to **nouns only**, and the noun has to name an extent
rather than a point.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a period) | Noun + にわたって | 三日間(みっかかん) → 三日間(みっかかん)にわたって |
| Noun (an area) | Noun + にわたって | 広(ひろ)い範囲(はんい) → 広(ひろ)い範囲(はんい)にわたって |
| Noun (a count of occasions) | Counter + にわたって | 三回(さんかい) → 三回(さんかい)にわたって |
| Written variant | Noun + にわたり | 長期間(ちょうきかん) → 長期間(ちょうきかん)にわたり |
| Modifying a noun | Noun + にわたる + Noun | 十年(じゅうねん) → 十年(じゅうねん)にわたる調査(ちょうさ) |
| As the main verb | Noun + にわたった | 交渉(こうしょう)は三日(みっか)にわたった |
| ❌ A verb clause | ❌話(はな)し合(あ)うにわたって | ✅長時間(ちょうじかん)にわたって話(はな)し合(あ)う |
| ❌ A point in time | ❌三時(さんじ)にわたって | ✅三時間(さんじかん)にわたって |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun must be a **measured extent**: a duration, a territory,
a number of sessions. If it names a moment or a single place, the pattern has
nothing to span.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb is <code>わたる</code>, "to cross over", and it is normally
written in kana in this pattern. The `に` is fixed; there is no
❌<code>をわたって</code> here.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は三日間(みっかかん)<b>にわたって</b>続(つづ)いた。
The roadworks went on for three whole days.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は五時間(ごじかん)<b>にわたって</b>行(おこな)われた。
The meeting ran for five hours.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の影響(えいきょう)は広(ひろ)い範囲(はんい)<b>にわたって</b>出(で)ている。
The effects of the typhoon are being felt over a wide area.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は三回(さんかい)<b>にわたって</b>同(おな)じ質問(しつもん)を繰(く)り返(かえ)した。
He repeated the same question on three separate occasions.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)<b>にわたる</b>調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)が、ようやく発表(はっぴょう)された。
The findings of a ten-year study have finally been published.
</pre>

<pre>
この画家(がか)の作品(さくひん)は、半世紀(はんせいき)<b>にわたって</b>愛(あい)され続(つづ)けてきた。
This painter's work has been loved for half a century.
</pre>

<pre>
停電(ていでん)は市内(しない)の広(ひろ)い地域(ちいき)<b>にわたり</b>、復旧(ふっきゅう)までに半日(はんにち)かかった。
The power cut extended across a wide part of the city, and it took half a day to restore supply.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)の移転(いてん)について、労使(ろうし)は二(に)か月(げつ)<b>にわたって</b>話(はな)し合(あ)いを重(かさ)ねた。
Management and the union held talks over a period of two months about relocating the plant.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **news and report** Japanese. Broadcasts, newspapers, minutes, academic
abstracts and formal announcements are full of it, and it is one of the surest ways
to make a written sentence sound properly adult.
<code>にわたり</code> is the stiffer of the two endings and is commoner in print;
<code>にわたって</code> is the everyday form of the same thing.

In conversation it is used, but sparingly, and always about something genuinely
large: 「三時間(さんじかん)にわたって説明(せつめい)された」 said with a sigh is
natural. About small quantities it is absurd -
❌5分(ふん)にわたって sounds like a parody of a news reader.

The attributive <code>にわたる</code> is worth drilling separately.
<code>十年(じゅうねん)にわたる研究(けんきゅう)</code>,
<code>広範囲(こうはんい)にわたる被害(ひがい)</code> - it packs the whole span
into a noun phrase and is extremely common in headlines and titles. Learners tend to
know only the `て` form and produce clumsy relative clauses instead.

One thing the pattern does **not** say is that the event was continuous every
minute. <code>三回(さんかい)にわたって</code> is precisely a series of separate
occasions; what is unbroken is the speaker's framing of them as one stretch.

## Contrast

### ～にわたって vs 間(あいだ) / 間(あいだ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間(あいだ)</code> **locates** an event inside a stretch.
<code>にわたって</code> says the event **filled** the stretch, and treats its size as
the news.

</div>

<code>間(あいだ)</code> is the N3 pattern closest to this one, and there are two
divergences: what they attach to, and what they claim.

<pre>
留守(るす)の<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、郵便(ゆうびん)が届(とど)いていた。
The post arrived while I was out.
</pre>

The delivery took a second; the stretch merely says when.

<pre>
二週間(にしゅうかん)<b>にわたって</b>、全国(ぜんこく)で調査(ちょうさ)が行(おこな)われた。
A survey was conducted nationwide over a two-week period.
</pre>

Here the survey occupies the fortnight, and the fortnight is part of what makes the
sentence worth writing. Note also the grammar:
<code>間(あいだ)</code> happily takes a whole clause
(寝(ね)ている間(あいだ)), while <code>にわたって</code> takes nothing but a
noun.

### ～にわたって vs 中(じゅう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>じゅう</code> is a **suffix** on a small set of ready-made
time and place words. <code>にわたって</code> is **productive** and works with any
measured span, including counts of occasions.

</div>

<pre>
一日中(いちにちじゅう)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた。
It rained all day.
</pre>

Everyday speech, and you cannot build new words with it freely -
一日中(いちにちじゅう), 一年中(いちねんじゅう), 世界中(せかいじゅう) are set
items.

<pre>
雨(あめ)は三日間(みっかかん)<b>にわたって</b>降(ふ)り続(つづ)いた。
The rain kept falling for three days on end.
</pre>

Same weather, higher register, and the span can be any figure you like.
<code>じゅう</code> is the one to use with a friend; <code>にわたって</code> is the
one to use in the report.

### ～にわたって vs ～を通(つう)じて / ～を通(つう)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を通(つう)じて</code> means something was true **at any
point** within a period. <code>にわたって</code> means an event **occupied** the
period, and stresses how big that is.

</div>

<pre>
この島(しま)は一年(いちねん)<b>を通(つう)じて</b>暖(あたた)かい。
This island is warm all year round.
</pre>

Nothing is happening for a year here; a condition simply holds whenever you check.

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は一年(いちねん)<b>にわたって</b>続(つづ)いた。
The construction work went on for a year.
</pre>

An event with a beginning and an end, filling the span. The two are also different
words in another way: <code>を通(つう)じて</code> has a second life meaning
"via" or "by way of" (友人(ゆうじん)を通(つう)じて知(し)り合(あ)った),
which <code>にわたって</code> never has. That tendency is worth treating as a guide
rather than a hard boundary - with some period nouns either will pass.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)<b>にわたって</b>続(つづ)いた。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)は三時間(さんじかん)<b>にわたって</b>続(つづ)いた。
  三時(さんじ) is a clock reading - a point. You need
  三時間(さんじかん), a duration.

- ❌ 長(なが)く話(はな)し合(あ)う<b>にわたって</b> → ✅ 長時間(ちょうじかん)<b>にわたって</b>話(はな)し合(あ)う
  Only a noun goes in front. A verb clause has to be turned into a noun of duration
  first.

- ❌ 十年(じゅうねん)<b>にわたって</b>研究(けんきゅう)が評価(ひょうか)された。（meaning "a ten-year study"） → ✅ 十年(じゅうねん)<b>にわたる</b>研究(けんきゅう)が評価(ひょうか)された。
  To modify the following noun you need the attributive
  <code>にわたる</code>. With <code>にわたって</code> the sentence says the *praising*
  went on for ten years.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、5分(ごふん)<b>にわたって</b>話(はな)した。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)、5分(ごふん)ほど話(はな)した。
  The pattern presents a span as impressively large. Five minutes is not, and the
  mismatch sounds comic.

## Practice

**1.** 工事(こうじ)のため、来週(らいしゅう)から一(いっ)か月(げつ)____この道(みち)は通(とお)れません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一(いっ)か月(げつ)<b>にわたって</b>

A month is a measured duration, and the closure fills the whole of it. The formal
register of a notice suits this pattern exactly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 沖縄(おきなわ)は一年(いちねん)（にわたって・を通(つう)じて）温暖(おんだん)な気候(きこう)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

沖縄(おきなわ)は一年(いちねん)<b>を通(つう)じて</b>温暖(おんだん)な気候(きこう)だ。

A climate is a standing condition, true whenever you look, not an event occupying a
year. <code>にわたって</code> would make the warmth sound like a twelve-month
incident.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The interview was carried out over four sessions."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

インタビューは四回(よんかい)<b>にわたって</b>行(おこな)われた。

<code>にわたって</code> is not restricted to time: a count of occasions works just as
well, and it is the standard way to report a series of sittings.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 三十年(さんじゅうねん)にわたって友情(ゆうじょう)が、一通(いっつう)の手紙(てがみ)で終(お)わってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 三十年(さんじゅうねん)<b>にわたる</b>友情(ゆうじょう)が、一通(いっつう)の手紙(てがみ)で終(お)わってしまった。

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議(かいぎ)をしている<b>間(あいだ)に</b>、大(おお)きな地震(じしん)があった。
(b) 会議(かいぎ)は六時間(ろくじかん)<b>にわたって</b>行(おこな)われた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the meeting only as a window: the earthquake happened at some moment inside
it, and how long the meeting ran is beside the point.

(b) makes the length itself the information. The meeting filled six hours, and the
sentence expects the reader to find that a lot - which is why it reads as a line
from a report rather than a remark to a colleague.

</details>

# ～の中を

**Meaning** · through it - moving across a space or a condition that surrounds you

<code>中(なか)を</code> takes rain, a crowd, darkness, applause, somebody's packed
schedule, and treats it as ground to be crossed. The `を` is the same one as in
道(みち)を歩(ある)く: it marks the stretch a movement passes through, which is
why the verb that follows must be a verb of **motion**.

## Usage

The shape is **Noun + の + 中(なか) + を + a verb of movement**:
歩(ある)く, 走(はし)る, 進(すす)む, 通(とお)る, 帰(かえ)る,
出(で)かける, 泳(およ)ぐ, 来(く)る. Once you see the pattern as "traverse",
everything about it follows. You cannot sit, read or wait
<code>中(なか)を</code>, because those verbs go nowhere - they take
<code>中(なか)で</code> instead.

The noun in front does not have to be a physical space. Conditions work just as
well - 雨(あめ)の中(なか)を, 混乱(こんらん)の中(なか)を,
拍手(はくしゅ)の中(なか)を - and this is where the pattern earns its keep,
because it quietly adds "despite" to the sentence. Crossing weather or noise or
disorder implies that it took something to do it.

An い-adjective can stand in for the noun with no `の`:
<code>お忙(いそが)しい中(なか)を</code>. That phrase, and
<code>雨(あめ)の中(なか)を</code>, head two of the most common set expressions
in polite Japanese - thanking someone for making the journey.

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)の<b>中(なか)を</b>走(はし)って帰(かえ)った。
I ran home through the rain.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)ごみの<b>中(なか)を</b>ゆっくり進(すす)んだ。
I made my way slowly through the crowd.
</pre>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しい<b>中(なか)を</b>お越(こ)しいただき、ありがとうございます。
Thank you for coming when you are so busy.
</pre>

<pre>
激(はげ)しい雪(ゆき)の<b>中(なか)を</b>、選手(せんしゅ)たちは一列(いちれつ)になってゴールを目指(めざ)した。
Through the driving snow, the runners strung out in a line and pressed on for the finish.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The construction itself is neutral, but it has a faintly literary weight when used
in narration. A novel or a news report will say
<code>嵐(あらし)の中(なか)を船(ふね)は進(すす)んだ</code>; in casual speech
you would more often hear <code>雨(あめ)なのに走(はし)って帰(かえ)った</code>.
The exception is the two set phrases, which are completely ordinary and which every
learner should be able to produce on demand:
<code>お忙(いそが)しい中(なか)をありがとうございます</code> and
<code>雨(あめ)の中(なか)をわざわざすみません</code>.

In those polite formulas the `を` is often dropped -
<code>お忙(いそが)しい中(なか)恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが</code> is at least
as common - and nothing is lost. Elsewhere the `を` is doing real grammatical work
and cannot go.

The "despite" flavour is a tendency rather than a rule, but a strong one. Because
you only mention the surrounding condition when it was worth mentioning,
<code>中(なか)を</code> almost always implies difficulty, inconvenience or drama.
Saying <code>いい天気(てんき)の中(なか)を歩(ある)いた</code> is not
ungrammatical, but it sounds slightly odd - fine weather is not something you have
to get through.

## Contrast

### ～の中(なか)を vs ～の中(なか)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` means you **cross** it. `で` means you **stay in** it and do
something there.

</div>

<pre>
森(もり)の<b>中(なか)を</b>歩(ある)いた。
I walked through the woods.
</pre>

<pre>
森(もり)の<b>中(なか)で</b>弁当(べんとう)を食(た)べた。
I ate my lunch in the woods.
</pre>

The test is the verb. If it takes you from one place to another, `を`; if it happens
on the spot, `で`. Note that a single verb can go either way -
<code>中(なか)を走(はし)る</code> is running through, while
<code>中(なか)で走(はし)る</code> is running about inside.

### ～の中(なか)を vs ～の中(なか)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` is a path **across**. `に` is a destination **into**, or a
location where something exists.

</div>

<pre>
建物(たてもの)の<b>中(なか)に</b>入(はい)った。
I went into the building.
</pre>

<pre>
建物(たてもの)の<b>中(なか)を</b>案内(あんない)してもらった。
I was shown around inside the building.
</pre>

With `に` the movement stops at the boundary; that is the whole event. With `を` the
inside is the ground being covered. English "in" and "into" hide this distinction,
which is why learners reach for `に` and produce ❌森(もり)の中(なか)に歩(ある)いた.

### ～の中(なか)を vs 中(じゅう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>じゅう</code> means "**all through**" a period or a place,
and is a suffix on the noun itself. <code>の中(なか)を</code> means "**across**"
something, and needs a verb of motion after it.

</div>

<code>中(なか)</code> read <code>じゅう</code> is the N3 pattern this one is
closest to, and it is closest only because the two share a character. The reading is
different, the attachment is different, and so is the meaning.

<pre>
一日中(いちにちじゅう)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた。
It rained all day.
</pre>

<pre>
その雨(あめ)の<b>中(なか)を</b>、彼(かれ)は傘(かさ)も差(さ)さずに歩(ある)いていった。
He walked off through that rain without even putting up an umbrella.
</pre>

Watch the readings when you meet 中(なか) in the wild:
<code>じゅう</code> after a time or place word means "throughout",
<code>ちゅう</code> in 工事中(こうじちゅう) means "in progress", and
<code>なか</code> with `の` in front and `を` after is this pattern.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)の<b>中(なか)を</b>本(ほん)を読(よ)んだ。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)の<b>中(なか)で</b>本(ほん)を読(よ)んだ。
  Reading goes nowhere, so there is no path for `を` to mark. A stationary action
  takes `で`.

- ❌ 森(もり)の<b>中(なか)に</b>一時間(いちじかん)歩(ある)いた。 → ✅ 森(もり)の<b>中(なか)を</b>一時間(いちじかん)歩(ある)いた。
  `に` would make the woods a destination. Walking *through* them is `を`.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)<b>中(なか)を</b>来(き)てくれた。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>の中(なか)を</b>来(き)てくれた。
  A noun needs `の` before 中(なか). Only an い-adjective attaches bare
  (お忙(いそが)しい中(なか)を).

- ❌ 一日中(いちにちじゅう)を歩(ある)いた。 → ✅ 一日中(いちにちじゅう)歩(ある)いた。
  <code>じゅう</code> is already an adverb and takes no particle. It is a different
  word from the <code>なか</code> of this pattern.

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 人(ひと)ごみの<b>中(なか)で</b>友(とも)だちを探(さが)した。
(b) 人(ひと)ごみの<b>中(なか)を</b>友(とも)だちを探(さが)して歩(ある)いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the speaker standing in the crowd, looking around. The crowd is where the
searching took place, and nothing says they moved.

(b) makes the crowd something being pushed through: the speaker is on the move,
covering ground, and the sentence conveys the effort of it. The `を` needs the verb
歩(ある)く to lean on, which is why (b) has to spell the walking out.

</details>
