# N2 - If you keep doing that: conditions and what follows


---

# ～ては

**Meaning** · if that happens - or if that keeps happening - the result is bad

`ては` is the て-form plus the particle `は`, and it is read *wa*. Making a condition
into a topic frames it as a standing state of affairs rather than a single event, so
the sentence almost always closes on a judgement: *if that is how things are, this
will not end well.*

## Formation

The condition goes in front of `ては`; the consequence follows, and it is negative.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + は | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んでは |
| Verb (negative) | ない → なくては | 言(い)う → 言(い)わなくては |
| い-adjective | ～く + ては | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)くては |
| な-adjective | stem + では | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)では |
| Noun | Noun + では | 雨(あめ) → 雨(あめ)では |
| Casual, after て | ては → ちゃ | 言(い)っては → 言(い)っちゃ |
| Casual, after で | では → じゃ | 休(やす)んでは → 休(やす)んじゃ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the consequence clause is a fixed shortlist: 困(こま)る, 大変(たいへん)だ,
だめだ, いけない, 無理(むり)だ, or any plain statement of something going wrong. If you
cannot put one of those at the end, `ては` is the wrong pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そんなに飲(の)ん<b>では</b>、体(からだ)に悪(わる)いよ。
Drinking that much is bad for you.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)<b>では</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)だ。
In this rain the match is off.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れ<b>ては</b>困(こま)ります。
It's a problem if you forget what you promised.
</pre>

<pre>
家賃(やちん)がこんなに高(たか)く<b>ては</b>、一人暮(ひとりぐ)らしは無理(むり)だ。
With rent this high, living alone is out of the question.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなこと言(い)っ<b>ちゃ</b>、彼女(かのじょ)が怒(おこ)るよ。
Say something like that and she'll get angry.
</pre>

<pre>
ここまで来(き)<b>ては</b>、もう後(あと)には引(ひ)けない。
Now that we've come this far, there's no backing out.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)まずに組(く)み立(た)て<b>ては</b>、あとで部品(ぶひん)が余(あま)ることになりますよ。
Assemble it without reading the instructions and you'll end up with parts left over.
</pre>

<pre>
売(う)り上(あ)げが落(お)ちるたびに社員(しゃいん)を減(へ)らし<b>ては</b>、この会社(かいしゃ)に将来(しょうらい)はないだろう。
If they cut staff every time sales dip, this company has no future.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ては` itself is register-neutral. It appears in newspaper editorials and in
grandmothers' warnings alike, and it does not change shape for politeness - the
politeness lives in the consequence clause, so <code>困(こま)ります</code> and
<code>困(こま)る</code> are the only difference between the polite and plain versions.

What *is* strongly marked is the contraction. `ては` → `ちゃ` and `では` → `じゃ` are
how this pattern is normally pronounced in conversation, and a learner who has only
seen the written form will not recognise it: <code>見(み)ちゃだめ</code>,
<code>飲(の)んじゃだめ</code>, <code>そんなことしちゃ困(こま)る</code>. The rule is
mechanical - whatever the て-form ends in, `て`+`は` fuses to `ちゃ` and `で`+`は` fuses
to `じゃ`. Contracted forms are casual only; do not write them in a report.

In speech `ては` is often left dangling, with the bad outcome unstated because it is
obvious: 「そんなに急(いそ)いでは…」 - the listener supplies "you'll have an accident".
This trailing-off is polite, not lazy; spelling out the disaster would be blunter.

One hard restriction: the second half of a `ては` sentence cannot be a request, an
invitation, a command or a plan. `ては` delivers a verdict, and a verdict is not
something you can attach 「～てください」 to.

## Contrast

### ～ては vs ～たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たら` is a neutral "if / when" and lets you say anything next.
`ては` builds the bad outcome into the grammar.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たら</b>、涼(すず)しい風(かぜ)が入(はい)ってきた。
When I opened the window, a cool breeze came in.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな寒(さむ)い日(ひ)に窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>ては</b>、風邪(かぜ)をひく。
Opening the window on a cold day like this, you'll catch a cold.
</pre>

`たら` can report a happy discovery, issue an instruction, or set up a plan. `ては`
can do none of those. That is also why 「運動(うんどう)しては健康(けんこう)になる」
is broken - the outcome is good, so the pattern has nothing to complain about.

### ～ては vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ては` says the condition **causes** the trouble. `ても` says the
trouble holds **in spite of** the condition.

</div>

<pre>
毎晩(まいばん)夜更(よふ)かしをし<b>ては</b>、体(からだ)が持(も)たない。
Staying up late every night, your body won't hold out.
</pre>

<pre>
毎晩(まいばん)夜更(よふ)かしをし<b>ても</b>、彼(かれ)は平気(へいき)な顔(かお)をしている。
He stays up late every night and still looks perfectly fine.
</pre>

The single syllable flips the direction of the arrow. Learners who translate both as
"even if" will write the first when they mean the second, and end up blaming the
staying-up for a result the sentence was meant to shrug off.

### ～ては vs ～ていては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare `ては` treats one act as representative. `ていては` says the
behaviour is **already under way** and continuing it is what will sink you.

</div>

<pre>
嘘(うそ)をつい<b>ては</b>、信用(しんよう)をなくす。
Tell lies and you lose people's trust.
</pre>

<pre>
嘘(うそ)ばかりつい<b>ていては</b>、だれも信(しん)じてくれなくなるぞ。
If you carry on lying like this, nobody's going to believe you any more.
</pre>

The second is pointed at a person who is doing it right now. The first is closer to a
general maxim. When you want to criticise a habit in front of you, `ていては` is the
form that lands, and in speech it contracts to `てちゃ`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 疲(つか)れ<b>ては</b>、休(やす)んでください。 → ✅ 疲(つか)れ<b>たら</b>、休(やす)んでください。
  A `ては` clause cannot be followed by a request. Use `たら` when you want to tell
  someone what to do.

- ❌ 高(たか)い<b>では</b>買(か)えない。 → ✅ 高(たか)く<b>ては</b>買(か)えない。
  い-adjectives change to `～くて` first. Only nouns and な-adjectives take `では`.

- ❌ 不便(ふべん)な<b>では</b>住(す)めない。 → ✅ 不便(ふべん)<b>では</b>住(す)めない。
  The `な` of a な-adjective drops before `では`, exactly as it does before `だ`.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)運動(うんどう)し<b>ては</b>、健康(けんこう)になる。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)運動(うんどう)す<b>れば</b>、健康(けんこう)になる。
  A good outcome needs `ば` or `たら`. `ては` is reserved for what goes wrong.

- ❌ そんな本(ほん)を読(よ)ん<b>でちゃ</b>だめ。 → ✅ そんな本(ほん)を読(よ)ん<b>じゃ</b>だめ。
  <code>読(よ)んでちゃ</code> is the contraction of <code>読(よ)んでいては</code>,
  "if you are in the middle of reading". For plain <code>読(よ)んでは</code> the
  contraction is `じゃ`.

## Practice

**1.** 部屋(へや)がこんなに散(ち)らかって____、友(とも)だちを呼(よ)べないよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

散(ち)らかって<b>は</b>（casual 散(ち)らかってちゃ）

The room being a mess is the standing condition, and the consequence - not being
able to invite anyone - is exactly the kind of trouble `ては` sets up.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 甘(あま)いものを食(た)べすぎ（ては・ても）、歯(は)が悪(わる)くなるよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

食(た)べすぎ<b>ては</b>、歯(は)が悪(わる)くなるよ。

The sweets are the cause of the bad teeth, not something the bad teeth happen in
spite of. `ても` would mean "even if you eat too many sweets", which leaves the
sentence saying something strange.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You can't get to work in an hour from a place this far out."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

こんなに遠(とお)く<b>ては</b>、一時間(いちじかん)で会社(かいしゃ)に着(つ)けない。

The い-adjective <code>遠(とお)い</code> becomes <code>遠(とお)くては</code>, and
the impossibility that follows is the negative consequence the pattern requires.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 時間(じかん)がなくては、明日(あした)続(つづ)きをやってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 時間(じかん)がな<b>ければ</b>、明日(あした)続(つづ)きをやってください。

The problem is the request at the end. `ては` can only be followed by a statement of
what goes wrong, so a sentence ending in 「～てください」 has to switch to `ば` or `たら`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 毎日(まいにち)インスタントラーメン<b>では</b>、栄養(えいよう)が足(た)りない。
(b) 毎日(まいにち)インスタントラーメン<b>でも</b>、栄養(えいよう)は足(た)りている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats the instant noodles as the cause: a diet like that produces a nutritional
shortfall, and the speaker disapproves.

(b) concedes the same diet and then denies the expected result - the nutrition is
fine anyway. Only `では` blames the noodles; `でも` defends them.

</details>

# ～ていては

**Meaning** · if you carry on doing that, it will not work out

Take the `ては` of "if that happens, we have a problem" and build it on `ている`
instead of the plain て-form. The condition is no longer a single act but a habit or a
state that is **already running**, and the sentence says: keep this up and here is the
wall you hit.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ている-form + は | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)っていては |
| Verb (う-verb in ん / い sound) | ～でいる + は | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んでいては |
| Noun / state, with いる | Noun + でいては | 学生(がくせい)のまま → 学生(がくせい)のままでいては |
| Casual contraction | ていては → てちゃ | 待(ま)っていては → 待(ま)ってちゃ |
| Casual contraction | でいては → でちゃ | 休(やす)んでいては → 休(やす)んでちゃ |
| Polite consequence | ～ていては + ～ません | 間(ま)に合(あ)いません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a common written variant is <code>～ていたのでは</code>, which says the same
thing a little more argued and a little less scolding. Both take the same negative
consequence.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ここで休(やす)ん<b>でいては</b>、間(ま)に合(あ)わないよ。
If we stop for a rest here, we won't make it in time.
</pre>

<pre>
テレビばかり見(み)<b>ていては</b>、目(め)が悪(わる)くなる。
Watching nothing but television, you'll ruin your eyes.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなに慌(あわ)て<b>てちゃ</b>、また忘(わす)れ物(もの)をするよ。
Rushing about like that, you'll leave something behind again.
</pre>

<pre>
いつまでも親(おや)に頼(たよ)っ<b>ていては</b>、独立(どくりつ)できない。
As long as you keep leaning on your parents, you'll never stand on your own feet.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)を恐(おそ)れ<b>ていては</b>、新(あたら)しいことは何(なに)も始(はじ)められません。
If you go on being afraid of failure, you'll never start anything new.
</pre>

<pre>
一(いち)から全部(ぜんぶ)自分(じぶん)でやっ<b>ていては</b>、締(し)め切(き)りに間(ま)に合(あ)いませんよ。
If you insist on doing every bit of it yourself, you won't hit the deadline.
</pre>

<pre>
毎回(まいかい)上司(じょうし)の顔色(かおいろ)をうかがっ<b>ていては</b>、おもしろい企画(きかく)は出(で)てこないだろう。
If you're always watching the boss's face, no interesting proposal is ever going to come out.
</pre>

<pre>
昼間(ひるま)からゲームばかりし<b>ていては</b>、そろそろ周(まわ)りに何(なに)か言(い)われるぞ。
Carry on gaming from midday like this and people are going to start saying things.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ていては` is aimed at behaviour the speaker can see. That makes it, more often than
not, a scolding or a piece of advice handed down: a parent to a child, a supervisor to
a new hire, a coach to a player. Used upward - to a boss, to a customer - it is
presumptuous, because you are telling them their current conduct is a mistake. Between
equals it is fine if the tone is warm, and it is very common in self-talk:
<code>こんなことで悩(なや)んでいては、前(まえ)に進(すす)めない</code>.

The contraction is where learners come unstuck. `ていては` becomes `てちゃ`, and
`でいては` becomes `でちゃ` - the `い` disappears along with everything else. So
<code>寝(ね)てちゃだめだよ</code> is "you mustn't be lying there asleep", built on
<code>寝(ね)ている</code>, while <code>寝(ね)ちゃだめだよ</code> is "you mustn't go
to sleep", built on the plain て-form. One syllable, two different warnings. Women's and
men's speech use both freely; the sentence-final particle - `よ`, `ぞ`, `わよ` - carries
whatever gender colouring there is, not the contraction itself.

In writing, `ていては` shows up constantly in opinion pieces, where the ongoing thing is
a national habit rather than a person's: 「従来(じゅうらい)のやり方(かた)を続(つづ)けていては、競争(きょうそう)に勝(か)てない」.
There it is neutral and carries no scolding at all.

## Contrast

### ～ていては vs ～ては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ては` puts up one act as a sample. `ていては` says the act is a
**pattern already in progress**, and the trouble comes from its continuing.

</div>

<pre>
夜中(よなか)に甘(あま)いものを食(た)べ<b>ては</b>、太(ふと)るのも当然(とうぜん)だ。
Eating sweets in the middle of the night, of course you put on weight.
</pre>

<pre>
夜中(よなか)に甘(あま)いものを食(た)べ<b>ていては</b>、いくら運動(うんどう)しても意味(いみ)がない。
As long as you keep eating sweets at midnight, no amount of exercise will help.
</pre>

Both criticise, but the second one is about a habit the speaker knows is still going
on, and the consequence is framed as unavoidable *while it lasts*. Where you can insert
"as long as you keep …", `ていては` is the better fit.

### ～ていては vs ～ていたら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ていたら` is a neutral conditional and can end anywhere.
`ていては` is locked to a bad outcome.

</div>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)で待(ま)っ<b>ていたら</b>、偶然(ぐうぜん)昔(むかし)の友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)った。
I was waiting in front of the station and happened to run into an old friend.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)で待(ま)っ<b>ていては</b>、向(む)こうが見(み)つけられませんよ。
If you stand waiting in front of the station, they'll never spot you.
</pre>

`ていたら` also does the "I was doing X and then discovered Y" job, which `ていては`
cannot touch at all. And `ていたら` can be followed by a request; `ていては` cannot.

### ～ていては vs ～ようでは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ていては` blames an **ongoing action**. `ようでは` treats a state
of affairs as **evidence** that the person or plan is not up to it.

</div>

<pre>
この程度(ていど)の作業(さぎょう)に一日(いちにち)かかっ<b>ていては</b>、納期(のうき)が守(まも)れない。
If a job this size keeps taking a whole day, we can't meet the delivery date.
</pre>

<pre>
この程度(ていど)の作業(さぎょう)に一日(いちにち)もかかる<b>ようでは</b>、一人前(いちにんまえ)とは言(い)えない。
If it takes you a whole day for a job this size, you can't call yourself trained yet.
</pre>

The first is practical - the schedule breaks. The second is a verdict on the person,
read off the slow work as a symptom. `ようでは` looks down; `ていては` looks forward.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 待(ま)つ<b>ていては</b>遅(おそ)くなる。 → ✅ 待(ま)っ<b>ていては</b>遅(おそ)くなる。
  The base is the ている-form, so the verb has to reach its て-form first.
  <code>待(ま)つ</code> → <code>待(ま)って</code> → <code>待(ま)っている</code>.

- ❌ そんなところで寝(ね)<b>ていちゃ</b>だめだよ。 → ✅ そんなところで寝(ね)<b>てちゃ</b>だめだよ。
  The contraction swallows the `い` as well: `ていては` → `てちゃ`, never `ていちゃ`.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)し<b>ていては</b>、きっと上手(じょうず)になる。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)し<b>ていれば</b>、きっと上手(じょうず)になる。
  A good result cancels the pattern. Use `ていれば` or `ていたら` when the continuing
  action is a *good* thing.

- ❌ 無理(むり)をし<b>ていては</b>、少(すこ)し休(やす)んでください。 → ✅ 無理(むり)をし<b>ているなら</b>、少(すこ)し休(やす)んでください。
  A request cannot follow `ていては`. The pattern ends in a statement of what goes
  wrong, not in an instruction.

## Practice

**1.** 辞書(じしょ)を一(ひと)つずつ調(しら)べ____、試験(しけん)時間(じかん)が足(た)りなくなりますよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

調(しら)べ<b>ていては</b>（casual 調(しら)べてちゃ）

Looking every word up is presented as an ongoing method the listener is using, and
running out of exam time is the wall it leads to - the exact job of `ていては`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)（ていては・ていたら）、雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

見(み)<b>ていたら</b>、雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。

Nothing bad happens here - the speaker simply notices something while looking out.
`ていては` demands a negative consequence, so it cannot report a discovery.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If you keep worrying about what other people think, you'll never decide anything."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人(ひと)の目(め)ばかり気(き)にし<b>ていては</b>、何(なに)も決(き)められない。

<code>気(き)にする</code> becomes <code>気(き)にしている</code> before `は`, and
ばかり reinforces that the worrying is a settled habit rather than one occasion.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 文句(もんく)を言(い)っていては、自分(じぶん)でやってみてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 文句(もんく)を言(い)っている<b>なら</b>、自分(じぶん)でやってみてください。

The problem is the request. `ていては` can only be completed by a statement of the bad
result; to tell the complainer to try it themselves you need `なら` or `たら`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会議中(かいぎちゅう)にスマホを見(み)<b>ちゃ</b>だめだよ。
(b) 会議中(かいぎちゅう)にスマホを見(み)<b>てちゃ</b>だめだよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) contracts <code>見(み)ては</code> and forbids the act itself: don't look at your
phone during the meeting at all.

(b) contracts <code>見(み)ていては</code> and is said to someone whose eyes are on
the screen right now - "you can't be sitting there staring at your phone". It reports
an ongoing state and tells them to stop it.

</details>

# ～ようでは

**Meaning** · if things are in that state, there is no hope for it

This is `ようだ` - "it appears that, it looks like" - wearing the same `では` as the
rest of this family. The speaker takes an observed state of affairs, treats it as a
symptom, and delivers a verdict: *if that is what we are seeing, this is not going to
work.* The verdict is always unfavourable.

## Formation

`よう` is a noun, so the clause in front of it takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ようでは | 続(つづ)く → 続(つづ)くようでは |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ようでは | 解(と)けない → 解(と)けないようでは |
| い-adjective | plain form + ようでは | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いようでは |
| な-adjective | ～な + ようでは | 不安定(ふあんてい) → 不安定(ふあんてい)なようでは |
| Noun | ～の + ようでは | この調子(ちょうし) → この調子(ちょうし)のようでは |
| Casual contraction | ようでは → ようじゃ | 読(よ)めないようじゃ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with a bare noun, most speakers drop `よう` and say
<code>この調子(ちょうし)では</code>. The `のようでは` row is grammatical but far less
common than the verb rows, which are where this pattern really lives.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こんな簡単(かんたん)な問題(もんだい)も解(と)けない<b>ようでは</b>、合格(ごうかく)は難(むずか)しい。
If you can't even solve a question this easy, passing is going to be hard.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)めない<b>ようじゃ</b>、この仕事(しごと)は続(つづ)かないよ。
If you can't read the kanji, you won't last in this job.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)自分(じぶん)で起(お)きられない<b>ようでは</b>、社会人(しゃかいじん)としてやっていけない。
If you can't get yourself up in the morning, you won't manage in the working world.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)まで反対(はんたい)している<b>ようでは</b>、この案(あん)は通(とお)らないだろう。
If even the department head is against it, this proposal isn't going through.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)みがちな<b>ようでは</b>、レギュラーにはなれませんよ。
If you're inclined to skip practice, you're not going to make the first team.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)がこれほど高(たか)い<b>ようでは</b>、買(か)ってくれるお客(きゃく)さんはいない。
At a price this steep, there won't be a customer willing to buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
初日(しょにち)から遅刻(ちこく)してくる<b>ようでは</b>、先(さき)が思(おも)いやられる。
Turning up late on day one - it doesn't bode well.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ説明(せつめい)を三度(さんど)聞(き)いても分(わ)からない<b>ようでは</b>、一人(ひとり)で任(まか)せるわけにはいきません。
If you still don't follow after hearing the same explanation three times, we can't leave you to it alone.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ようでは` is a judgement, and judgements come from above. The natural speakers of this
pattern are teachers, coaches, supervisors and critics; aimed upward at a boss or a
customer it is close to insulting, because you are grading their performance out loud.
Between friends it works when the tone is teasing. About yourself it is possible and
rather rueful - <code>この程度(ていど)で疲(つか)れているようでは、まだまだだ</code> -
and that self-directed use is one of the safest ways for a learner to deploy it.

The consequence half is a small set of set expressions, and knowing them is half of
knowing the pattern: <code>無理(むり)だ</code>, <code>できない</code>,
<code>難(むずか)しい</code>, <code>話(はなし)にならない</code>,
<code>先(さき)が思(おも)いやられる</code>, <code>おしまいだ</code>. All of them say
the same thing in different tempers.

In speech `ようでは` contracts to `ようじゃ`, which is very common and slightly rougher:
<code>そんなことも知(し)らないようじゃ困(こま)るな</code>. In editorial writing the
uncontracted form is standard and carries no personal sting - there the failing state
belongs to a policy or an industry rather than to the listener.

## Contrast

### ～ようでは vs ～ようなら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようなら` is a neutral "if it turns out that" and can be followed
by a request or an instruction. `ようでは` can only be followed by a bad verdict.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらない<b>ようなら</b>、もう一度(いちど)病院(びょういん)へ行(い)ってください。
If the fever doesn't come down, please go back to the hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んでも熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらない<b>ようでは</b>、ただの風邪(かぜ)ではないかもしれない。
If the fever won't come down even with medicine, this may not be an ordinary cold.
</pre>

Both start from the same observed state. `ようなら` then tells you what to do about it;
`ようでは` refuses to give advice and instead announces the conclusion. Swap them and
the first sentence loses its instruction, the second gains one it cannot carry.

### ～ようでは vs ～ては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ては` blames a **repeated act**. `ようでは` reads a **state** as
evidence about someone's ability or a plan's prospects.

</div>

<pre>
大事(だいじ)な書類(しょるい)をなくし<b>ては</b>、信用(しんよう)を失(うしな)う。
Lose an important document and you forfeit people's trust.
</pre>

<pre>
大事(だいじ)な書類(しょるい)をなくす<b>ようでは</b>、この部署(ぶしょ)は任(まか)せられない。
If documents like this get lost, we can't hand this section over to you.
</pre>

The first says losing the paper causes the damage. The second treats the loss as a
diagnostic - it reveals what sort of worker you are - and pronounces on your future.
That diagnostic flavour is what `よう` adds, and it is why the sentence feels colder.

### ～ようでは vs ～のでは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のでは` takes the situation as an established fact. `ようでは`
frames it as an appearance the speaker has inferred from what is in front of them.

</div>

<pre>
毎月(まいつき)これだけ赤字(あかじ)が出(で)る<b>のでは</b>、店(みせ)を閉(し)めるしかない。
Running this much of a loss every month, there's nothing for it but to close the shop.
</pre>

<pre>
毎月(まいつき)これだけ赤字(あかじ)が出(で)ている<b>ようでは</b>、店(みせ)は長(なが)くもたないだろう。
If the shop is losing this much every month, it isn't going to last long.
</pre>

The accounts are on the table in the first; in the second the speaker is reading the
signs. `のでは` also contracts, to `んじゃ` - <code>これだけ高(たか)いんじゃ買(か)えない</code> - 
which is one of the most frequent shapes in casual complaint, and worth recognising
alongside `ようじゃ`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>ようでは</b>無理(むり)だ。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>のようでは</b>無理(むり)だ。
  <code>よう</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の` - the same link
  <code>学生(がくせい)のようだ</code> uses.

- ❌ 不安(ふあん)<b>ようでは</b>続(つづ)けられない。 → ✅ 不安(ふあん)<b>なようでは</b>続(つづ)けられない。
  な-adjectives keep their `な` before <code>よう</code>.

- ❌ よく勉強(べんきょう)する<b>ようでは</b>、きっと合格(ごうかく)できる。 → ✅ よく勉強(べんきょう)す<b>れば</b>、きっと合格(ごうかく)できる。
  The verdict has to be unfavourable. A good outcome needs an ordinary conditional.

- ❌ 痛(いた)みが続(つづ)く<b>ようでは</b>、医者(いしゃ)に相談(そうだん)してください。 → ✅ 痛(いた)みが続(つづ)く<b>ようなら</b>、医者(いしゃ)に相談(そうだん)してください。
  An instruction to the listener needs `ようなら`. `ようでは` ends the sentence in
  judgement, not in advice.

## Practice

**1.** この程度(ていど)のミスに気(き)づかない____、検査(けんさ)の意味(いみ)がない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

気(き)づかない<b>ようでは</b>（casual 気(き)づかないようじゃ）

The missed mistake is being read as a symptom of how the inspection is being run, and
the conclusion is a flat negative verdict - both hallmarks of `ようでは`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)までに返事(へんじ)が来(こ)ない（ようでは・ようなら）、こちらから電話(でんわ)してみます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

返事(へんじ)が来(こ)ない<b>ようなら</b>、こちらから電話(でんわ)してみます。

The second half is a plan of action, not a verdict. `ようでは` cannot be followed by
what the speaker intends to do about it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If you get this nervous at practice, you'll be no use on the day."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

練習(れんしゅう)でこんなに緊張(きんちょう)している<b>ようでは</b>、本番(ほんばん)では使(つか)いものにならない。

The present nervousness is evidence about the performance to come, which is exactly
what `ようでは` is for, and the harsh closing phrase suits its register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部品(ぶひん)が足(た)りないようでは、すぐに注文(ちゅうもん)しておきましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部品(ぶひん)が足(た)りない<b>ようなら</b>、すぐに注文(ちゅうもん)しておきましょう。

The sentence ends in a proposal, so it needs `ようなら`. Keeping `ようでは` would demand
something like <code>作業(さぎょう)が進(すす)まない</code> instead.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新人(しんじん)が三日(みっか)で辞(や)める<b>ようでは</b>、この職場(しょくば)に問題(もんだい)がある。
(b) 新人(しんじん)が三日(みっか)で辞(や)め<b>ては</b>、人(ひと)が育(そだ)たない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats the quick resignations as a symptom and diagnoses the workplace from them:
the sentence is about what the fact reveals.

(b) treats the resignations as the cause of a practical consequence - nobody stays long
enough to be trained. It complains about the effect rather than reading the evidence.

</details>

# ～ものなら

**Meaning** · if it were actually possible - which the speaker doubts

`ものなら` sits on a **potential** verb and turns it into a wish the speaker has already
half given up on: *if I could, I would.* The same shape, said to someone else, becomes
a dare - *go on then, do it if you can.* Either way the pattern quietly asserts that
the thing is out of reach.

## Formation

The verb in front of `ものなら` must be in potential form. That is the whole trick.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | potential ～える + ものなら | 戻(もど)る → 戻(もど)れるものなら |
| る-verb | potential ～られる + ものなら | やめる → やめられるものなら |
| する | できる + ものなら | 旅行(りょこう)する → 旅行(りょこう)できるものなら |
| 来(く)る | 来(こ)られる + ものなら | 来(く)る → 来(こ)られるものなら |
| Casual contraction | ものなら → もんなら | 勝(か)てるもんなら |
| Dare, with the verb repeated | potential + ものなら + imperative | 止(と)められるものなら止(と)めてみろ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the labels used throughout this book for what other
courses call 五段(ごだん)/Group 1 and 一段(いちだん)/Group 2. The second half of a `ものなら` sentence is
almost always <code>～たい</code>, <code>～たかった</code>, <code>～のに</code>, or an
imperative. A flat statement of fact does not fit.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
できる<b>ものなら</b>、もう一度(いちど)やり直(なお)したい。
If I could, I'd like to start over.
</pre>

<pre>
戻(もど)れる<b>ものなら</b>、高校時代(こうこうじだい)に戻(もど)りたいなあ。
If it were possible to go back, I'd go back to my high school days.
</pre>

<pre>
止(と)められる<b>ものなら</b>止(と)めてみろ。
Stop me if you can.
</pre>

<pre>
代(か)われる<b>ものなら</b>、私(わたし)が代(か)わってあげたかった。
If I could have taken her place, I would have.
</pre>

<pre>
勝(か)てる<b>もんなら</b>勝(か)ってみろよ。口(くち)だけじゃないか。
Beat us then, if you can. You're all talk.
</pre>

<pre>
もう一度(いちど)会(あ)える<b>ものなら</b>、祖母(そぼ)にちゃんとお礼(れい)を言(い)いたかった。
If I could have seen her once more, I'd have wanted to thank my grandmother properly.
</pre>

<pre>
この忙(いそが)しさから逃(に)げられる<b>ものなら</b>、明日(あした)にでも田舎(いなか)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)したい。
If I could escape this workload, I'd move out to the countryside tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)でも早(はや)く完成(かんせい)させられる<b>ものなら</b>そうしたいのですが、人手(ひとで)がまったく足(た)りません。
I'd finish it a day earlier if I possibly could, but we're badly short of hands.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The wishing use is emotional, and it is the reason this pattern turns up in songs,
letters and eulogies. It carries longing rather than planning: the speaker has already
concluded that the thing is impossible, and the sentence is a sigh. That is why
<code>もう一度(いちど)</code>, <code>今(いま)すぐ</code> and past-tense
<code>～たかった</code> are such frequent companions. Said about something that is in
fact perfectly easy, `ものなら` sounds theatrical.

The dare is a different animal. <code>止(と)められるものなら止(と)めてみろ</code> is
confrontational, and the `～てみろ` ending is blunt - it leans male and it leans angry.
Softened to <code>止(と)められるものなら止(と)めてみてください</code> it becomes a
sardonic challenge instead of a shouted one, which in an office is arguably worse. A
learner should be able to recognise this use without needing to produce it.

`もんなら` is the casual contraction and belongs to speech only. Note also that
<code>もの</code> here has drifted a long way from "thing": you cannot swap in
<code>こと</code>, and <code>できることなら</code>, which does exist, is a softer,
more matter-of-fact "if possible" with none of the resignation.

## Contrast

### ～ものなら vs ～（よ）うものなら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ものなら` takes the **potential** form and means "if I could".
`(よ)うものなら` takes the **volitional** and means "if I ever dared to".

</div>

<pre>
文句(もんく)が言(い)える<b>ものなら</b>、はっきり言(い)ってやりたい。
If I could complain, I'd give them a piece of my mind.
</pre>

<pre>
文句(もんく)を言(い)お<b>うものなら</b>、あとが大変(たいへん)なことになる。
If I so much as complained, there'd be hell to pay afterwards.
</pre>

Same verb, same `ものなら`, opposite sentences. The first wishes for an ability the
speaker lacks; the second imagines choosing to act and flinches at the fallout. Check
the form in front: <code>言(い)える</code> is potential, <code>言(い)おう</code>
is volitional.

### ～ものなら vs ～られたら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a plain potential + `たら` is a live hypothetical. `ものなら`
signals the speaker does **not** expect it to happen.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)休(やす)みが取(と)れ<b>たら</b>、温泉(おんせん)に行(い)こうと思(おも)っている。
If I can get time off next week, I'm thinking of going to a hot spring.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)みが取(と)れる<b>ものなら</b>、一週間(いっしゅうかん)ぐらい何(なに)もせずに過(す)ごしたい。
If I could actually get time off, I'd like to spend a week doing nothing at all.
</pre>

The first speaker may well be booking a room. The second is daydreaming and knows it.
This is also why `ものなら` will not take a following plan - 「～ものなら、行(い)きます」 -
since you cannot schedule something you have written off.

### ～ものなら vs ～ば…のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ば…のに` complains about how reality **is**. `ものなら` reaches
for something the speaker knows reality will not supply.

</div>

<pre>
もっと時間(じかん)があ<b>れば</b>、ゆっくり準備(じゅんび)できる<b>のに</b>。
If only there were more time, I could prepare properly.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)を止(と)められる<b>ものなら</b>、止(と)めてしまいたい。
If I could stop time, I'd stop it dead.
</pre>

`ば…のに` points at a specific obstacle - the schedule - with a note of grievance.
`ものなら` does not blame anything; it names an impossibility and lets the wish stand.
The more fantastical the wish, the more `ものなら` is the only option.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>ものなら</b>、今(いま)すぐ行(い)きたい。 → ✅ 行(い)ける<b>ものなら</b>、今(いま)すぐ行(い)きたい。
  The verb has to be in potential form. A dictionary form in front of `ものなら`
  produces the volitional pattern's meaning by accident, or simply nothing.

- ❌ 来(く)る<b>ものなら</b>来(き)てほしい。 → ✅ 来(こ)られる<b>ものなら</b>来(き)てほしい。
  <code>来(く)る</code> is irregular: the potential is <code>来(こ)られる</code>,
  built on the こ- stem, while the て-form is <code>来(き)て</code>.

- ❌ できる<b>ものなら</b>、明日(あした)手伝(てつだ)います。 → ✅ できる<b>ものなら</b>、明日(あした)手伝(てつだ)いたいのですが。
  A firm commitment contradicts the pattern. `ものなら` needs a wish, a regret or a
  challenge at the end.

- ❌ 勝(か)てる<b>ものなら</b>勝(か)ちます。 → ✅ 勝(か)てる<b>ものなら</b>勝(か)ってみろ。
  In the dare use the same verb comes back as an imperative or `～てみろ`. Without
  that shape the sentence loses the challenge entirely.

## Practice

**1.** 忘(わす)れられる____、あの日(ひ)のことは全部(ぜんぶ)忘(わす)れてしまいたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

忘(わす)れられる<b>ものなら</b>

The verb is already in potential form and the sentence ends in <code>～たい</code>, so
`ものなら` fits exactly: the speaker wants to forget and knows perfectly well that they
cannot.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 社長(しゃちょう)に意見(いけん)を（言(い)えるものなら・言(い)おうものなら）、その場(ば)で怒鳴(どな)られるだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

意見(いけん)を<b>言(い)おうものなら</b>、その場(ば)で怒鳴(どな)られるだろう。

The consequence is a disaster that follows from *choosing* to speak, so the volitional
`(よ)うものなら` is needed. The potential version would have to end in a wish instead.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If I could swap places with him, I'd take the exam myself."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)と代(か)われる<b>ものなら</b>、私(わたし)が試験(しけん)を受(う)けたい。

<code>代(か)わる</code> becomes the potential <code>代(か)われる</code>, and the
closing <code>～たい</code> supplies the wish that `ものなら` requires.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 逃(に)げるものなら逃(に)げてみろ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 逃(に)げ<b>られる</b>ものなら逃(に)げてみろ。

The dare use still needs the potential form in the first half - the challenge is
precisely about whether the other person *can*. The imperative half stays as it is.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 留学(りゅうがく)でき<b>たら</b>、アメリカに行(い)きたい。
(b) 留学(りゅうがく)できる<b>ものなら</b>、アメリカに行(い)きたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an ordinary hypothetical: study abroad is on the table, and the speaker is
saying where they would choose to go. It could be part of a real plan.

(b) adds resignation. The speaker doubts the chance will ever come - money, family,
work stand in the way - so the sentence reads as a wistful "if only I could".

</details>

# ～ないことには～ない

**Meaning** · unless this first thing happens, nothing else can

`ないことには` names an indispensable precondition. Not merely a helpful one - the
sentence insists that until it is met, the second half is impossible, unknowable or
simply will not start. The closing clause is therefore always negative.

## Formation

The first clause is negative, and so is the second. Both halves are part of the
pattern; a positive ending breaks it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ない-form + ことには | 試(ため)す → 試(ため)さないことには |
| Verb (state) | ～ていない + ことには | そろう → そろっていないことには |
| い-adjective | ～くない + ことには | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くないことには |
| な-adjective | ～でない + ことには | 健康(けんこう) → 健康(けんこう)でないことには |
| Noun | ～でない + ことには | 本人(ほんにん) → 本人(ほんにん)でないことには |
| Main clause | must be negative | ～ない / できない / 無理(むり)だ / わからない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a very common fixed ending is
<code>～ないことには始(はじ)まらない</code>, "nothing gets going until…". It is worth
learning as one unit, because it turns up constantly in planning meetings.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
食(た)べてみ<b>ないことには</b>、おいしいかどうか分(わ)からない。
Until you taste it, you can't tell whether it's good.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が止(や)ま<b>ないことには</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)できません。
Unless the rain stops, we can't set off.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)会(あ)ってみ<b>ないことには</b>、どんな人(ひと)か判断(はんだん)できないよ。
Without meeting them once, you can't judge what sort of person they are.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)がそろわ<b>ないことには</b>、会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)められない。
Until the documents are all here, we can't begin the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)に確(たし)かめ<b>ないことには</b>、こちらでは何(なに)とも言(い)えません。
Without checking with the person themselves, there's nothing we can say from our side.
</pre>

<pre>
予算(よさん)がつか<b>ないことには</b>、この計画(けいかく)は一歩(いっぽ)も進(すす)まない。
Unless a budget comes through, this plan won't move a single step.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)現地(げんち)を見(み)<b>ないことには</b>、この土地(とち)が店(みせ)に向(む)いているかどうか判断(はんだん)のしようがありません。
Without seeing the site in person, there's no way to judge whether this plot suits a shop.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)の理解(りかい)が得(え)られ<b>ないことには</b>、海外(かいがい)赴任(ふにん)の話(はなし)はお受(う)けできません。
Unless I can get my family's understanding, I can't accept the overseas posting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ないことには` is level-headed. It belongs to the register of people working out what
has to happen before anything else can - project meetings, medical explanations, news
analysis - and it sounds equally at home in polite and plain speech. It is neither
formal nor casual, but it is deliberate: choosing it over `なければ` signals that you
have thought about the order of things.

What it adds over a plain conditional is the sense of a **gate**. `なければ` states a
condition; `ないことには` says that condition stands between you and everything after
it. That is why the pattern so often appears with expressions of total blockage -
<code>一歩(いっぽ)も進(すす)まない</code>, <code>何(なに)とも言(い)えない</code>,
<code>どうにもならない</code>, <code>話(はなし)にならない</code>.

The negative ending is not a stylistic preference; it is required. A learner who
attaches a positive result - "unless you do X, Y happens" - has written a sentence a
Japanese reader will stop at. Likewise, `ないことには` cannot be followed by a request or
an instruction: it explains why nothing can proceed, it does not tell anyone what to do.

## Contrast

### ～ないことには vs ～なければ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なければ` is the general-purpose conditional and can end
positively. `ないことには` insists the first thing is a **precondition** and must end
negatively.

</div>

<pre>
早(はや)く申(もう)し込(こ)ま<b>なければ</b>、席(せき)がなくなりますよ。
If you don't apply soon, the seats will be gone.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し込(こ)ま<b>ないことには</b>、参加(さんか)できませんよ。
Unless you apply, you can't take part.
</pre>

The first warns about a consequence. The second says the application is the door you
have to walk through. Where English would say "unless" rather than "if not",
`ないことには` is usually the closer fit.

### ～ないことには vs ～ないと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないと` is short, spoken and often a nudge. `ないことには` is
longer, cooler, and makes a point of the dependency.

</div>

<pre>
急(いそ)が<b>ないと</b>、終電(しゅうでん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わないよ。
If we don't hurry, we'll miss the last train.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)の都合(つごう)を聞(き)か<b>ないことには</b>、日程(にってい)が決(き)められない。
Until we've heard everyone's availability, we can't fix a date.
</pre>

`ないと` also does duty as a casual "you have to" when the second half is left off -
<code>そろそろ帰(かえ)らないと</code>. `ないことには` has no such elliptical use; it
always needs its negative main clause spelled out.

### ～ないことには vs ～てからでないと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てからでないと` is about **sequence in time**: not until after X.
`ないことには` is about **dependency**: without X there is no basis for Y at all.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に相談(そうだん)し<b>てからでないと</b>、お返事(へんじ)できません。
I can't give you an answer until after I've consulted my manager.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に相談(そうだん)し<b>ないことには</b>、お返事(へんじ)のしようがありません。
Without consulting my manager there's no way for me to answer at all.
</pre>

They overlap heavily, and in this pair either is natural. They separate when time is
not the issue: 「才能(さいのう)がないことには続(つづ)けられない」 is fine, while
`てからでないと` would be nonsense - talent is not a step you complete first.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 見(み)る<b>ことには</b>、分(わ)からない。 → ✅ 見(み)<b>ないことには</b>、分(わ)からない。
  The first clause has to be negative. `ことには` on its own is not this pattern.

- ❌ 練習(れんしゅう)し<b>ないことには</b>、下手(へた)になる。 → ✅ 練習(れんしゅう)し<b>ないことには</b>、上手(じょうず)にならない。
  The main clause must be negative in form, not merely negative in feeling.
  <code>下手(へた)になる</code> is a positive verb and breaks the pattern.

- ❌ 会員(かいいん)<b>ないことには</b>入(はい)れません。 → ✅ 会員(かいいん)<b>でないことには</b>入(はい)れません。
  Nouns and な-adjectives need `でない` in front of <code>ことには</code>.

- ❌ 準備(じゅんび)ができ<b>ないことには</b>、先(さき)に始(はじ)めてください。 → ✅ 準備(じゅんび)ができ<b>ないなら</b>、先(さき)に始(はじ)めてください。
  An instruction cannot follow this pattern. `ないことには` explains an impossibility;
  it does not issue directions.

## Practice

**1.** 実物(じつぶつ)を見(み)____、大(おお)きさの感(かん)じがつかめない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

見(み)<b>ないことには</b>

Seeing the real thing is the gate: until it is passed, the second half is impossible,
and that closing <code>つかめない</code> supplies the required negative.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 薬(くすり)を飲(の)ま（ないと・ないことには）、また熱(ねつ)が上(あ)がるよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

薬(くすり)を飲(の)ま<b>ないと</b>、また熱(ねつ)が上(あ)がるよ。

The main clause is positive - the fever goes up - so `ないことには` is ruled out.
`ないと` handles warnings about what will happen.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Until we know the cause, we can't take any measures."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

原因(げんいん)が分(わ)から<b>ないことには</b>、対策(たいさく)の立(た)てようがありません。

Knowing the cause is the precondition, and <code>～ようがない</code> gives the flat
impossibility that the pattern's second half wants.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 許可(きょか)をもらわないことには、工事(こうじ)を始(はじ)めましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 許可(きょか)をもらわないことには、工事(こうじ)を始(はじ)め<b>られません</b>。

The proposal at the end has to go. Replacing it with a negative potential keeps the
sense - without permission there is no starting - and satisfies the pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)ま<b>なければ</b>、使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)からない。
(b) 説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)ま<b>ないことには</b>、使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)からない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain conditional: not reading leads to not understanding. It could be a mild
reproach to someone who skipped the manual.

(b) frames the manual as the only route in - there is no other way to find out, so
reading it is not optional but prior. The tone is less reproachful and more matter of
fact about the order things have to happen in.

</details>

# ～てでも

**Meaning** · even if it takes doing that - I am going to get this done

`てでも` names a means the speaker would rather not use - borrowing money, staying up
all night, swallowing their pride - and says they will use it anyway. The て-clause is
the *method*, not a concession, and the sentence always ends in determination.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + でも | 借(か)りる → 借(か)りてでも |
| Verb (て-form in で) | ～で + でも | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んででも |
| する-verb | ～して + でも | 徹夜(てつや)する → 徹夜(てつや)してでも |
| Noun as instrument | Noun + で + でも | タクシー → タクシーででも |
| Set phrase | どんな手(て)を使(つか)ってでも | - |
| Main clause | will, wish or obligation | ～たい / ～つもりだ / ～なければならない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>休(やす)んででも</code> looks like a typo and is not. The て-form of
<code>休(やす)む</code> already ends in `で`, and `でも` is added whole, so two `で`
land side by side.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
徹夜(てつや)し<b>てでも</b>、明日(あした)までに仕(し)上(あ)げます。
I'll finish it by tomorrow even if it means staying up all night.
</pre>

<pre>
借金(しゃっきん)をし<b>てでも</b>、あの絵(え)が欲(ほ)しい。
I want that painting even if I have to borrow to get it.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)ん<b>ででも</b>、息子(むすこ)の試合(しあい)を見(み)に行(い)きたい。
I want to go and watch my son's match even if I have to take the day off work.
</pre>

<pre>
どんな手(て)を使(つか)っ<b>てでも</b>、犯人(はんにん)を見(み)つけ出(だ)す。
I'll find the culprit by whatever means it takes.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっているなら、歩(ある)い<b>てでも</b>会場(かいじょう)に向(む)かいます。
If the trains are down, I'll walk to the venue if I have to.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)に頭(あたま)を下(さ)げ<b>てでも</b>、留学(りゅうがく)の費用(ひよう)を出(だ)してもらうつもりだ。
I intend to get them to fund my study abroad, even if it means going cap in hand to my parents.
</pre>

<pre>
多少(たしょう)無理(むり)をし<b>てでも</b>、今年(ことし)のうちに資格(しかく)を取(と)っておきたいと考(かんが)えています。
I'd like to get the qualification before the year is out, even if I have to push myself a bit.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)を下(さ)げ<b>てでも</b>、在庫(ざいこ)を年内(ねんない)に売(う)り切(き)らなければならない。
We have to clear the stock by the end of the year, even if that means cutting the price.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is neutral in politeness - it takes whatever ending the sentence needs -
but it is emotionally loaded. Choosing `てでも` announces that you know the method is
costly and have decided it is worth it, so the sentence carries resolve, and sometimes
a hint of desperation. That makes it excellent in a job interview and slightly alarming
at a dinner table.

The costliness matters. A means that costs nothing sounds odd here: saying
<code>歩(ある)いてでも駅(えき)まで行(い)く</code> only works if walking is a real
hardship in the situation - the trains are down, it is pouring, it is five kilometres.
Otherwise `て` alone will do.

The second half has to look forward: a wish, an intention, an obligation, an order.
Occasionally you meet a past-tense report of the same resolve
(<code>タクシーを使(つか)ってでも間(ま)に合(あ)わせた</code>), which is fine, but a
plain description of a state - "I was tired" - cannot close a `てでも` sentence, because
there is no will in it for the pattern to attach to.

## Contrast

### ～てでも vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てでも` presents the clause as the **means** you will use. `ても`
presents it as an **obstacle** you would put up with.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われます。
The match will go ahead even if it rains.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を買(か)っ<b>てでも</b>、今日(きょう)届(とど)けに行(い)きます。
I'll go and deliver it today even if I have to buy an umbrella.
</pre>

Rain is not something you *do* in order to hold a match, so `ても` is right there. The
umbrella is a step the speaker will actively take, which is what `でも` adds. If the
clause is something the subject chooses to do, `てでも` is available; if it merely
happens to them, only `ても` is.

### ～てでも vs ～てまで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てでも` is the speaker's own resolve, and approves of it.
`てまで` measures how far someone went and usually **disapproves**.

</div>

<pre>
列(れつ)に三時間(さんじかん)並(なら)ん<b>ででも</b>、この店(みせ)のケーキを買(か)いたい。
I'd queue three hours to buy a cake from this shop.
</pre>

<pre>
列(れつ)に三時間(さんじかん)も並(なら)ん<b>でまで</b>、ケーキを買(か)いたいとは思(おも)わない。
I wouldn't want a cake badly enough to queue three hours for it.
</pre>

`てまで` characteristically appears with a negative or a rhetorical question - it asks
whether the goal was worth that price and implies not. Swapping the two reverses the
speaker's attitude, so this is a pair worth keeping straight.

### ～てでも vs Noun + でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a noun, `でも` means "or something, for instance". Only after
a **て-form** does it mean "even by doing".

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)があるなら、お茶(ちゃ)<b>でも</b>飲(の)みませんか。
If you've got time, shall we have some tea or something?
</pre>

<pre>
自転車(じてんしゃ)を飛(と)ばし<b>てでも</b>、一番(いちばん)に着(つ)きたかった。
I wanted to get there first, even if it meant tearing along on my bike.
</pre>

The first `でも` softens a suggestion; the second commits to a hard method. To make a
noun into a means you need a verb between it and `でも` - <code>タクシーを使(つか)ってでも</code> -
or the instrumental particle, <code>タクシーででも</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 走(はし)る<b>てでも</b>間(ま)に合(あ)わせる。 → ✅ 走(はし)っ<b>てでも</b>間(ま)に合(あ)わせる。
  `でも` attaches to the て-form, so the verb has to be conjugated first.

- ❌ タクシー<b>てでも</b>行(い)きます。 → ✅ タクシーを使(つか)っ<b>てでも</b>行(い)きます。
  A bare noun cannot take `てでも`. Supply a verb, or use <code>タクシーででも</code>.

- ❌ 無理(むり)をし<b>てでも</b>、疲(つか)れてしまった。 → ✅ 無理(むり)をし<b>て</b>、疲(つか)れてしまった。
  The second half must express will or obligation. A plain report of how you ended up
  feeling has nothing for `でも` to reinforce.

- ❌ 少(すこ)し待(ま)っ<b>てでも</b>、コーヒーが飲(の)みたい。 → ✅ 少(すこ)し待(ま)っ<b>ても</b>、コーヒーが飲(の)みたい。
  Waiting a little is not a sacrifice. `てでも` needs a means the speaker genuinely
  wants to avoid.

## Practice

**1.** 会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れそうなので、タクシーを使(つか)っ____間(ま)に合(あ)わせます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

使(つか)っ<b>てでも</b>間(ま)に合(あ)わせます

The taxi is an expensive method the speaker would normally skip, and the ending states
a firm intention - both conditions `てでも` requires.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 嘘(うそ)をつい（てでも・てまで）勝(か)ちたいとは思(おも)わない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

嘘(うそ)をつい<b>てまで</b>勝(か)ちたいとは思(おも)わない。

The sentence rejects the method, and that judgement is `てまで`'s territory. `てでも`
would commit the speaker to lying, which contradicts the ending.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll ask my old teacher for help if I have to, but I want to finish this research."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昔(むかし)の先生(せんせい)に頼(たの)み込(こ)ん<b>ででも</b>、この研究(けんきゅう)をやり遂(と)げたい。

<code>頼(たの)み込(こ)む</code> has a て-form ending in `で`, so `でも` follows it
directly and you get `ででも`. The closing <code>～たい</code> carries the resolve.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 頭(あたま)を下(さ)げででも、許(ゆる)してもらうつもりだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 頭(あたま)を下(さ)げ<b>てでも</b>、許(ゆる)してもらうつもりだ。

<code>下(さ)げる</code> is a る-verb, so its て-form is <code>下(さ)げて</code>. The
doubled `ででも` only appears when the て-form itself ends in `で`, as with
<code>休(やす)んで</code> or <code>急(いそ)いで</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 親(おや)に反対(はんたい)され<b>ても</b>、彼(かれ)と結婚(けっこん)する。
(b) 親(おや)と縁(えん)を切(き)っ<b>てでも</b>、彼(かれ)と結婚(けっこん)する。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) concedes an obstacle: the parents may object, and the speaker will marry regardless.
Nothing is being chosen except to carry on.

(b) names a price the speaker is willing to pay - cutting ties with the family - as a
deliberate act. It is far more extreme, because the sacrifice is volunteered rather
than merely endured.

</details>

# ～つもりで

**Meaning** · doing something in a particular frame of mind - with that intention, or *as if* it were so

`つもりだ` states an intention. Put it in the `で` form and it stops being the point of
the sentence and becomes the **attitude you carry into** the action that follows. With
a past-tense verb in front, it slides one step further: not "intending to" but
"treating it as though you already had".

## Formation

<code>つもり</code> is a noun, so what comes in front of it is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + つもりで | 手伝(てつだ)う → 手伝(てつだ)うつもりで |
| Verb (past) | ～た + つもりで | 買(か)う → 買(か)ったつもりで |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + つもりで | 言(い)わない → 言(い)わないつもりで |
| い-adjective | plain form + つもりで | 若(わか)い → 若(わか)いつもりで |
| Noun | ～の + つもりで | 冗談(じょうだん) → 冗談(じょうだん)のつもりで |
| Set phrase | そのつもりで | - → そのつもりでお願(ねが)いします |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>買(か)うつもりで</code> and <code>買(か)ったつもりで</code> are two
different patterns, not two tenses of one. The first means "intending to buy"; the
second means "as if you had bought it" - money you spend in imagination and keep in the
bank.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)<b>のつもりで</b>言(い)ったのに、彼(かれ)を傷(きず)つけてしまった。
I meant it as a joke, but I ended up hurting him.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>のつもりで</b>、もう一度(いちど)一(いち)から勉強(べんきょう)します。
I'll study it again from scratch, as though I were a new recruit.
</pre>

<pre>
遊(あそ)び<b>のつもりで</b>始(はじ)めたのに、いつのまにか本気(ほんき)になっていた。
I took it up as a bit of fun, and somehow ended up serious about it.
</pre>

<pre>
買(か)った<b>つもりで</b>、その一万円(いちまんえん)を貯金(ちょきん)することにした。
I decided to bank that ten thousand yen as if I'd already spent it.
</pre>

<pre>
死(し)んだ<b>つもりで</b>、あと一年(いちねん)だけ頑張(がんば)ってみます。
I'll give it one more year and throw everything into it.
</pre>

<pre>
三十分(さんじゅっぷん)で終(お)わらせる<b>つもりで</b>取(と)りかかったが、結局(けっきょく)半日(はんにち)かかった。
I set out meaning to finish in half an hour, and in the end it took half a day.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)は一年(いちねん)だけの<b>つもりで</b>来(き)たのですが、気(き)がついたら五年(ごねん)も住(す)んでいました。
I came intending to study here for just a year, and before I knew it I'd lived here five.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)が客(きゃく)になった<b>つもりで</b>、この案内(あんない)が分(わ)かりやすいかどうか考(かんが)えてみてください。
Put yourself in the customer's shoes and think about whether these directions are clear.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`つもりで` is register-neutral and turns up as readily in a training manual as in a
conversation between friends. What it changes is the *frame* of the action, so it very
often carries a note of self-persuasion: you are choosing to adopt a mindset that is not
literally true in order to get something done.

The `～たつもりで` shapes are close to idioms and worth memorising whole.
<code>死(し)んだつもりで</code> is "with nothing left to lose"; <code>だまされたつもりで</code>
is "just humour me and try it" - a shopkeeper's phrase;
<code>買(か)ったつもり貯金(ちょきん)</code> is a recognised savings trick. None of them
implies actual deception of anyone else, which is the crucial difference from
<code>ふりをする</code>.

<code>そのつもりで</code> deserves separate mention. As a standalone it means "so bear
that in mind, and act accordingly" - <code>来週(らいしゅう)から担当(たんとう)が変(か)わりますので、そのつもりで</code>.
Delivered flatly it is a warning, and it lands harder than its length suggests; softened
with <code>お願(ねが)いします</code> it is ordinary workplace politeness.

One restriction learners trip on: because `つもりで` describes the *actor's* frame of
mind, the person holding it should be the subject of the following verb. Attributing a
mindset to somebody else while describing your own action produces a sentence that
does not add up.

## Contrast

### ～つもりで vs ～つもりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもりだ` **is** the predicate: it announces the plan. `つもりで`
is adverbial: it colours a different verb that follows.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)車(くるま)を買(か)う<b>つもりです</b>。
I'm planning to buy a car next year.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)車(くるま)を買(か)う<b>つもりで</b>、毎月(まいつき)少(すこ)しずつ貯(た)めている。
Meaning to buy a car next year, I'm putting a little aside each month.
</pre>

The first sentence is finished at `です`; the second cannot stand without the clause it
introduces. If you find yourself ending a sentence on `つもりで`, the form you actually
wanted was `つもりだ` or `つもりです`.

### ～つもりで vs ～ふりをして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つもりで` is a frame of mind you adopt **for yourself**.
`ふりをする` is a pretence staged **for other people**.

</div>

<pre>
寝(ね)た<b>ふりをして</b>、母(はは)が部屋(へや)を出(で)ていくのを待(ま)った。
I pretended to be asleep and waited for my mother to leave the room.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)んだ<b>つもりで</b>、また机(つくえ)に向(む)かった。
I told myself I'd had my break and went back to my desk.
</pre>

Nobody is being fooled in the second sentence. `つもりで` is honest about being a
mental device, which is why it can appear in advice and encouragement, where
`ふりをする` would sound like an instruction to deceive.

### ～つもりで vs ～と思(おも)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と思(おも)って</code> reports what you believed. `つもりで` reports the
stance you deliberately took up.

</div>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)だ<b>と思(おも)って</b>参加(さんか)したら、いきなり本番(ほんばん)だった。
I turned up thinking it was a rehearsal and it was the real thing.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)の<b>つもりで</b>、気楽(きらく)に話(はな)してみてください。
Treat it as practice and just talk without worrying.
</pre>

<code>と思(おも)って</code> can be a mistaken belief - the first speaker was simply wrong.
`つもりで` cannot be a mistake in that sense, because it is chosen on purpose. That is
also why encouragement takes `つもりで`: you are being invited to adopt a stance, not
informed of a fact.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 新人(しんじん)<b>つもりで</b>働(はたら)きます。 → ✅ 新人(しんじん)<b>のつもりで</b>働(はたら)きます。
  <code>つもり</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`.

- ❌ 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>つもりで</b>、そのお金(かね)を寄付(きふ)した。 → ✅ 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)った<b>つもりで</b>、そのお金(かね)を寄付(きふ)した。
  For the "as if I already had" sense the verb must be past. The dictionary form gives
  a live intention, which contradicts donating the money instead.

- ❌ 来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>つもりで</b>。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>つもりです</b>。
  `つもりで` needs a following clause. On its own at the end of a sentence it leaves
  the listener waiting.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)が来(く)る<b>つもりで</b>、私(わたし)は資料(しりょう)を用意(ようい)した。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)が来(く)ると思(おも)って、私(わたし)は資料(しりょう)を用意(ようい)した。
  The mindset belongs to whoever performs the main action. Someone else's intention has
  to be reported with <code>と思(おも)って</code> or <code>らしいので</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 外国(がいこく)の方(かた)に説明(せつめい)する____、やさしい言葉(ことば)を使(つか)ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

説明(せつめい)する<b>つもりで</b>

The listener is being asked to adopt a stance before speaking, and `つもりで` is the
form that frames the following instruction rather than ending the sentence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 病気(びょうき)の（つもりで・ふりをして）会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

病気(びょうき)の<b>ふりをして</b>会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)んだ。

Taking a day off on a false illness is a performance aimed at other people, which is
`ふりをする`. `つもりで` would mean the speaker had privately decided to think of
themselves as ill, which explains nothing about the absence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Save the money as if you had eaten out, and you'll have quite a sum by the end of the year."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

外食(がいしょく)した<b>つもりで</b>貯(た)めておけば、年末(ねんまつ)にはかなりの額(がく)になりますよ。

The past form <code>外食(がいしょく)した</code> is essential: the trick is to treat
money as already spent, not to plan a meal.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しい企画(きかく)を出(だ)すつもりで。来週(らいしゅう)説明(せつめい)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しい企画(きかく)を出(だ)す<b>つもりです</b>。来週(らいしゅう)説明(せつめい)します。

`つもりで` cannot close a sentence. To state the plan itself you need the predicate
form `つもりです`; `つもりで` would have to run straight on into what you did about it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一年(いちねん)で完成(かんせい)させる<b>つもりで</b>始(はじ)めた。
(b) 一年(いちねん)で完成(かんせい)させた<b>つもりで</b>始(はじ)めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of the aim held at the outset: the speaker began the project
targeting one year, and the sentence usually goes on to say whether that held.

(b) is strange, and worth seeing as strange. A past `つもりで` means "as if it were
already done", so this says the speaker started the work while pretending it was
finished - which is why the dictionary form is the one you want for intentions.

</details>

# ～には

**Meaning** · in order to do this - here is what it takes

`には` sets up a goal and then measures the world against it. The main clause never
says what you are going to do; it says what is **needed**, what **works**, or how the
situation **rates** for that purpose. The `は` is doing its usual contrastive job: for
*this* aim, as opposed to any other, the following is true.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + には | 働(はたら)く → 働(はたら)くには |
| Verb (compound) | dictionary form + には | 申(もう)し込(こ)む → 申(もう)し込(こ)むには |
| Noun (purpose) | Noun + には | 合格(ごうかく) → 合格(ごうかく)には |
| Noun (person as yardstick) | Noun + には | 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)には |
| Main clause | requirement, means or rating | ～が必要(ひつよう)だ / ～ばいい / ～すぎる / ～しかない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - only the plain dictionary form goes in front of `には`. Not
<code>働(はたら)きますには</code>, not <code>働(はたら)いたには</code>. Politeness is
carried entirely by the ending of the main clause.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
日本(にほん)で働(はたら)く<b>には</b>、ビザが必要(ひつよう)です。
To work in Japan you need a visa.
</pre>

<pre>
この服(ふく)は子供(こども)<b>には</b>大(おお)きすぎる。
These clothes are too big for a child.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)まで行(い)く<b>には</b>、どのバスに乗(の)ればいいですか。
Which bus should I take to get to the station?
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)を保(たも)つ<b>には</b>、規則(きそく)正(ただ)しい生活(せいかつ)が一番(いちばん)だ。
For keeping healthy, nothing beats a regular routine.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は持(も)ち歩(ある)く<b>には</b>重(おも)すぎますね。
This dictionary is too heavy to carry around.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で住(す)む<b>には</b>、この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)すぎるかもしれない。
For living alone, this flat may be rather too big.
</pre>

<pre>
外国語(がいこくご)を身(み)につける<b>には</b>、毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつでも続(つづ)けるしかありません。
The only way to get a foreign language into your bones is to keep at it a little every day.
</pre>

<pre>
会員(かいいん)割引(わりびき)を受(う)ける<b>には</b>、事前(じぜん)にアプリで登録(とうろく)しておく必要(ひつよう)があります。
To get the member's discount you have to register on the app in advance.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`には` is the natural voice of instructions. Guidebooks, recipes, help pages, station
signs and advice columns are built out of it, because its whole shape is *goal, then
requirement*. It is register-neutral and takes whatever politeness the main clause
carries, so it is equally at home in <code>ビザが必要(ひつよう)です</code> and
<code>ビザがいる</code>.

The evaluation use - <code>子供(こども)には大(おお)きすぎる</code>,
<code>私(わたし)には難(むずか)しい</code> - is worth noticing as the same pattern from
a different angle. There the noun is the yardstick rather than the goal, and the `は`
still contrasts: too big *for a child*, whatever it may be for an adult. Learners find
this softening useful, because <code>私(わたし)には分(わ)かりません</code> claims only
that *you* cannot follow it, where <code>分(わ)かりません</code> can sound like a verdict
on the explanation.

Two things `には` cannot do. It cannot be followed by a statement of what you intend to
do - that is `ために` - and it does not attach comfortably to negatives; "in order not
to" is <code>～ないように</code> or <code>～ないために</code>, not <code>～ないには</code>.

## Contrast

### ～には vs ～ために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the main clause says **what you do**, use `ために`. If it says
**what is required or how things rate**, use `には`.

</div>

<pre>
資格(しかく)を取(と)る<b>ために</b>、毎晩(まいばん)二時間(にじかん)勉強(べんきょう)している。
I study two hours every evening in order to get the qualification.
</pre>

<pre>
資格(しかく)を取(と)る<b>には</b>、実務(じつむ)経験(けいけん)が三年(さんねん)以上(いじょう)要(い)る。
To get the qualification you need three years or more of practical experience.
</pre>

The first reports the speaker's own effort; the second states a condition of the world
that holds for anybody. This is also why job adverts and application forms use `には`
almost exclusively - they are describing requirements, not intentions.

### ～には vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` frames the task as something you spend resources on -
time, tools, effort. `には` frames it as a goal you are trying to reach.

</div>

<pre>
このソフトは写真(しゃしん)を整理(せいり)する<b>のに</b>とても便利(べんり)だ。
This software is very handy for organising photos.
</pre>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)を整理(せいり)する<b>には</b>、まずフォルダを作(つく)りましょう。
To organise your photos, start by making folders.
</pre>

`のに` pairs with <code>便利(べんり)だ</code>, <code>使(つか)う</code>,
<code>時間(じかん)がかかる</code>, <code>役(やく)に立(た)つ</code> - all of which
measure a cost or a usefulness. `には` pairs with requirements and methods. Where either
fits, as in 「駅(えき)まで歩(ある)くには/のに二十分(にじゅっぷん)かかる」, `のに` is the
smoother choice.

### ～には vs ～なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なら` supposes the listener might do it. `には` takes the goal as
given and gets straight to the requirements.

</div>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)へ行(い)く<b>なら</b>、秋(あき)がおすすめですよ。
If you're going to Kyoto, I'd recommend autumn.
</pre>

<pre>
紅葉(こうよう)の時期(じき)に泊(と)まる<b>には</b>、半年(はんとし)前(まえ)から予約(よやく)が要(い)ります。
To get a room during the autumn leaves you need to book six months ahead.
</pre>

`なら` responds to something the other person has just raised and can be followed by a
suggestion. `には` does not care whether anyone is planning the trip; it states what the
goal costs. In a guidebook you will see `には`, in a conversation `なら`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 働(はたら)きます<b>には</b>、ビザが要(い)ります。 → ✅ 働(はたら)く<b>には</b>、ビザが要(い)ります。
  The verb in front of `には` is always in plain dictionary form, however polite the
  rest of the sentence is.

- ❌ 風邪(かぜ)をひかない<b>には</b>、うがいをしましょう。 → ✅ 風邪(かぜ)をひかない<b>ように</b>、うがいをしましょう。
  "In order not to" is not `には` territory. Use <code>～ないように</code>, or
  <code>～ないために</code> in writing.

- ❌ 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)る<b>には</b>、毎日(まいにち)塾(じゅく)に通(かよ)おう。 → ✅ 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)る<b>には</b>、毎日(まいにち)塾(じゅく)に通(かよ)うのが近道(ちかみち)だ。
  A `には` clause cannot be answered with a resolution. Turn the second half into a
  statement about what is needed or what works.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)した<b>には</b>、努力(どりょく)が必要(ひつよう)だった。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)する<b>には</b>、努力(どりょく)が必要(ひつよう)だった。
  A past form in front of `には` is not this pattern. The goal is stated in the
  dictionary form even when the main clause is in the past.

## Practice

**1.** この機械(きかい)を動(うご)かす____、まず電源(でんげん)を入(い)れてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

動(うご)かす<b>には</b>

The sentence names a goal and then gives the first step towards it, which is the
instruction-manual shape `には` was made for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 海外(かいがい)で生活(せいかつ)する（には・ために）、語学(ごがく)学校(がっこう)に通(かよ)っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

海外(かいがい)で生活(せいかつ)する<b>ために</b>、語学(ごがく)学校(がっこう)に通(かよ)っています。

The main clause reports what the speaker is doing about the goal, not what the goal
requires, so `ために` is correct. `には` would need an ending like
<code>語学力(ごがくりょく)が欠(か)かせない</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This report is a bit long to read in one sitting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この報告書(ほうこくしょ)は一度(いちど)で読(よ)む<b>には</b>少(すこ)し長(なが)い。

Here `には` supplies the yardstick - reading it in one go - and the main clause rates
the report against it. <code>～すぎる</code> would work equally well in place of
<code>少(すこ)し長(なが)い</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 太(ふと)らないには、夜(よる)遅(おそ)く食(た)べないほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 太(ふと)らない<b>ように</b>、夜(よる)遅(おそ)く食(た)べないほうがいい。

`には` does not sit on a negative verb. A goal phrased as something you want to avoid
takes <code>～ないように</code>, or you can flip it to
<code>体重(たいじゅう)を保(たも)つには</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この問題(もんだい)は私(わたし)<b>に</b>難(むずか)しいです。
(b) この問題(もんだい)は私(わたし)<b>には</b>難(むずか)しいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is not natural Japanese - a bare `に` cannot mark the person a judgement is
relative to, so the sentence reads as an unfinished thought.

(b) works because `は` adds the contrast the meaning needs: hard *for me*, whatever it
may be for someone else. That contrastive shading is the reason this pattern is `には`
and not `に`.

</details>

# ～てはならない

**Meaning** · must not - a prohibition stated as a rule, not as a request

Same `ては` as the rest of this family, with the bad outcome spelled out in full:
<code>ならない</code>, "it will not do". The result is the most formal of the ordinary
prohibitions - the one that belongs to statutes, safety manuals, contracts and moral
maxims rather than to the kitchen table.

## Formation

The pattern attaches to verbs only.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + はならない | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れてはならない |
| Verb (て-form in で) | ～で + はならない | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んではならない |
| Polite | ～てはなりません | 吸(す)う → 吸(す)ってはなりません |
| Literary / archaic | ～てはならぬ | 言(い)ってはならぬ |
| Casual, gruff | ちゃならない / じゃならない | 言(い)っちゃならない |
| Not this pattern | ～なくてはならない = **must do** | 払(はら)わなくてはならない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the last row is the trap. <code>行(い)ってはならない</code> is "you must not
go"; <code>行(い)かなくてはならない</code> is "you must go". One `なく` reverses the
sentence, and both end in the same four syllables.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
試験中(しけんちゅう)に話(はな)し<b>てはならない</b>。
You must not talk during the examination.
</pre>

<pre>
ここでたばこを吸(す)っ<b>てはなりません</b>。
Smoking is not permitted here.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の日記(にっき)を勝手(かって)に読(よ)ん<b>ではならない</b>。
You must not read someone's diary without asking.
</pre>

<pre>
どんな状況(じょうきょう)でも、決(けっ)して希望(きぼう)を捨(す)て<b>てはならない</b>。
Whatever the circumstances, you must never give up hope.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ過(あやま)ちを二度(にど)と繰(く)り返(かえ)し<b>てはならない</b>。
The same mistake must never be repeated.
</pre>

<pre>
個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)を本来(ほんらい)の目的(もくてき)以外(いがい)に利用(りよう)し<b>てはならない</b>。
Personal data must not be used for any purpose other than the one it was collected for.
</pre>

<pre>
運転中(うんてんちゅう)に携帯(けいたい)電話(でんわ)を操作(そうさ)し<b>てはなりません</b>。
You must not operate a mobile phone while driving.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに急(いそ)いでいても安全(あんぜん)確認(かくにん)を省(はぶ)い<b>てはならない</b>と、新人(しんじん)のころに教(おそ)わった。
I was taught as a new employee that however rushed you are, you must never skip the safety check.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`てはならない` is the written cousin of `てはいけない`. It is impersonal: the prohibition
comes from a rule, a law or a principle rather than from the speaker, which is why it
fills legal texts, company regulations, safety notices and the sort of speech a
headmaster gives. It is also the natural form for a moral absolute -
<code>戦争(せんそう)を繰(く)り返(かえ)してはならない</code> - where the weight of the
statement is the point.

In ordinary conversation people do not say it. Telling a friend
<code>遅(おく)れてはならない</code> sounds like reading from a manual, and speakers
sometimes do exactly that as a joke. What you actually hear is `てはいけない`,
`ちゃだめ`, `ちゃいけない`. The contraction `ちゃならない` does exist and is gruff and
somewhat old-fashioned; `ならぬ` is archaic, surviving mainly in fiction, historical
drama and set phrases.

Politeness moves only in the ending: `なりません` for polite, `ならない` for plain. The
pattern has no honorific or humble variant, because a rule has no addressee to be
polite to. When a rule must be conveyed courteously, Japanese usually rewrites it as
<code>ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください</code> or <code>お控(ひか)えください</code> rather than
softening `てはならない`.

## Contrast

### ～てはならない vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てはいけない` is a prohibition **someone is giving you**.
`てはならない` is a prohibition that simply **holds**, stated from nowhere in particular.

</div>

<pre>
まだこの部屋(へや)に入(はい)っ<b>てはいけません</b>よ。
You mustn't come into this room yet.
</pre>

<pre>
許可(きょか)なくこの区域(くいき)に立(た)ち入(い)っ<b>てはならない</b>。
Entry to this area without permission is prohibited.
</pre>

The first has a speaker and a listener; the `よ` proves it. The second could be printed
on a fence. If you can imagine a person saying it to you in the moment, `てはいけない`
is the form; if you can imagine it on a sign, `てはならない`.

### ～てはならない vs ～なくてはならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てはならない` forbids. `なくてはならない` obliges. They are
opposites that differ by a single `なく` in the middle.

</div>

<pre>
書類(しょるい)に嘘(うそ)を書(か)い<b>てはならない</b>。
You must not write anything false on the form.
</pre>

<pre>
書類(しょるい)に本名(ほんみょう)を書(か)か<b>なくてはならない</b>。
You must write your real name on the form.
</pre>

The obligation form has a second life as a modifier: <code>なくてはならない存在(そんざい)</code>
means "an indispensable presence", literally "a presence one cannot do without". That
phrase is common and warm, and has nothing to do with prohibition.

### ～てはならない vs ～べきではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てはならない` says the act is **not permitted**. `べきではない`
says it is **not the right thing to do** - a judgement the speaker is making.

</div>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の情報(じょうほう)を外部(がいぶ)に漏(も)らし<b>てはならない</b>。
Company information must not be leaked outside.
</pre>

<pre>
そんな言(い)い方(かた)をす<b>べきではなかった</b>と反省(はんせい)している。
I regret that I shouldn't have put it that way.
</pre>

`べきではない` leaves room for disagreement; it is an opinion, and it can be applied to
your own past behaviour with hindsight. `てはならない` does not argue, and it does not
look backwards - it states the rule and stops.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 忘(わす)れる<b>てはならない</b>。 → ✅ 忘(わす)れ<b>てはならない</b>。
  The base is the て-form. <code>忘(わす)れる</code> → <code>忘(わす)れて</code>
  before `はならない` is attached.

- ❌ 遅刻(ちこく)<b>ではならない</b>。 → ✅ 遅刻(ちこく)し<b>てはならない</b>。
  Unlike the plain `ては`, this pattern does not take nouns or な-adjectives. Make the
  noun into a verb with `する` first.

- ❌ この水(みず)は飲(の)ま<b>なくてはならない</b>。（intending "you must not drink this water"） → ✅ この水(みず)は飲(の)ん<b>ではならない</b>。
  <code>飲(の)まなくてはならない</code> means you *must* drink it. The `なく` turns a
  ban into an obligation, which is the reverse of what was meant.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は遅(おく)れ<b>てはならない</b>よ。（to a friend） → ✅ 明日(あした)は遅(おく)れ<b>ちゃだめ</b>だよ。
  Not a grammar error, a register error. Between friends the rulebook form sounds
  either pompous or ironic.

## Practice

**1.** この薬(くすり)は、医師(いし)の指示(しじ)なく量(りょう)を増(ふ)やし____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

増(ふ)やし<b>てはならない</b>（or 増(ふ)やしてはなりません）

The sentence is a piece of medical instruction with no personal speaker behind it, so
the impersonal, rulebook prohibition is the right register.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この書類(しょるい)は明日(あした)までに提出(ていしゅつ)し（てはならない・なくてはならない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

提出(ていしゅつ)し<b>なくてはならない</b>。

A deadline creates an obligation, not a ban. `てはならない` would forbid submitting the
document at all, which makes no sense with 「明日(あした)までに」.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Under no circumstances may the key be handed to a third party."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いかなる場合(ばあい)でも、鍵(かぎ)を第三者(だいさんしゃ)に渡(わた)し<b>てはならない</b>。

The English is contract language, and `てはならない` is its Japanese counterpart.
<code>いかなる場合(ばあい)でも</code> matches the same stiff register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 図書館(としょかん)で大(おお)きな声(こえ)で話(はな)すてはならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 図書館(としょかん)で大(おお)きな声(こえ)で話(はな)し<b>てはならない</b>。

<code>話(はな)す</code> has to become the て-form <code>話(はな)して</code> before
`はならない`. A dictionary form cannot carry this pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ここに車(くるま)を止(と)め<b>てはならない</b>。
(b) ここに車(くるま)を止(と)め<b>ちゃだめ</b>だよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reads as a regulation - the sort of line painted on a sign or printed in a
building's rules. There is no speaker in it, and no room for negotiation.

(b) is one person telling another, casually and in the moment. It is friendly, it
implies the speaker has noticed you about to park, and it would be out of place on a
sign.

</details>

# ～となると・～となれば

**Meaning** · if it really comes to that - then here is what follows

`となると` takes a situation that is now genuinely on the table and works out its
consequences: *supposing this actually happens, look what it drags with it.* A second,
lighter use shifts the topic - "when it comes to X" - and hands the floor to whatever
the speaker wants to say about X.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + となると / となれば | 結婚(けっこん) → 結婚(けっこん)となると |
| Verb | plain form + となると | 引(ひ)っ越(こ)す → 引(ひ)っ越(こ)すとなると |
| Verb (past) | ～た + となると | 決(き)まった → 決(き)まったとなると |
| い-adjective | plain form + となると | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいとなると |
| な-adjective | stem + となると | 必要(ひつよう) → 必要(ひつよう)となると |
| Casual variant | となったら | 買(か)う → 買(か)うとなったら |

<div class="note">

**Note** - nouns attach bare. There is no `だ`: <code>海外(かいがい)勤務(きんむ)となると</code>,
never <code>海外(かいがい)勤務(きんむ)だとなると</code>. The `と` in `となる` is already
doing that work.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)する<b>となると</b>、やはりお金(かね)がかかる。
Once you're actually going abroad to study, it does cost money.
</pre>

<pre>
旅行(りょこう)が一週間(いっしゅうかん)<b>となれば</b>、荷物(にもつ)も多(おお)くなりますね。
If the trip is going to be a week, that's a lot more to pack.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の城(しろ)の話(はなし)<b>となると</b>、彼(かれ)は止(と)まらない。
Once the subject turns to Japanese castles, there's no stopping him.
</pre>

<pre>
家(いえ)を買(か)う<b>となると</b>、そう簡単(かんたん)には決(き)められない。
When it actually comes to buying a house, you can't decide so easily.
</pre>

<pre>
いざ人前(ひとまえ)で話(はな)す<b>となると</b>、急(きゅう)に声(こえ)が震(ふる)えてきた。
When it came to actually speaking in front of people, my voice suddenly started shaking.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)を説得(せっとく)する<b>となれば</b>、一日(いちにち)では足(た)りないでしょう。
If we have to talk every one of them round, a single day won't be enough.
</pre>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)勤務(きんむ)<b>となると</b>、子供(こども)の学校(がっこう)のことも考(かんが)えなければならない。
If it means a posting abroad, we'll have to think about the children's schooling too.
</pre>

<pre>
工場(こうじょう)を移転(いてん)させる<b>となれば</b>、地元(じもと)の理解(りかい)を得(え)るのに何年(なんねん)もかかるはずだ。
If the plant is really to be relocated, winning over the local community will take years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The heart of this pattern is *realism*. `となると` is not for daydreams; it is for a
possibility that has just become concrete enough to have consequences - the transfer
has been floated, the wedding date is being discussed, the budget has arrived. That is
why it so often follows <code>いざ</code>, "when it actually comes to it", and why it
appears at the moment a conversation turns practical.

`となると` and `となれば` are close enough to swap in most sentences. `となると` leans
slightly towards a situation already taking shape; `となれば` leans slightly towards
supposing, and is the more common of the two in writing and in careful speech. The
casual spoken variant is `となったら`, and in relaxed conversation you also hear plain
`だと` doing part of the same work.

The topic-shift use - <code>和食(わしょく)となると、この店(みせ)が一番(いちばん)だ</code> -
carries a hint that the topic is a weighty or specialist one. Applied to something
trivial it sounds mock-grand, which is occasionally the joke. For an everyday "if it's
lunch you're after", `なら` is the natural choice.

The consequence clause is flexible: a prediction, an obligation, a difficulty, or - with
<code>そうとなれば</code> - a call to action. Unlike most of the `では` family in this
group, `となると` does not require the outcome to be bad, only to be a real consequence.

## Contrast

### ～となると vs ～とすると・～としたら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とすると` **supposes** something for the sake of argument.
`となると` treats the situation as one that is genuinely arising.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)雨(あめ)だ<b>とすると</b>、運動会(うんどうかい)は延期(えんき)ですね。
Supposing it rains tomorrow, sports day gets postponed, then.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が直撃(ちょくげき)する<b>となると</b>、延期(えんき)の連絡(れんらく)を今日中(きょうじゅう)に出(だ)さなければ。
If the typhoon is really going to hit us, we'll have to send out the postponement notice today.
</pre>

Both are conditionals about the same event, but the second speaker has looked at the
forecast and is now organising. `とすると` can entertain anything; `となると` implies the
speaker takes the premise seriously enough to act on it.

### ～となると vs ～なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なら` picks up what the other person just said. `となると` weighs
what a situation would entail.

</div>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)に行(い)く<b>なら</b>、この地図(ちず)を持(も)っていくといいよ。
If you're going to Kyoto, take this map with you.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)全員(ぜんいん)で行(い)く<b>となると</b>、宿(やど)を探(さが)すのが大変(たいへん)だ。
If the whole family is going, finding somewhere to stay is going to be a job.
</pre>

`なら` responds and advises; `となると` calculates. Notice too that `なら` sits happily on
a light, immediate topic, while `となると` wants something with consequences attached -
a decision, a commitment, an expense.

### ～となると vs ～というと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the topic-shift use, `というと` calls up an **association**.
`となると` asks what the topic **demands** or who is best placed for it.

</div>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>というと</b>、まず雪(ゆき)を思(おも)い浮(う)かべます。
Say Hokkaido and the first thing I picture is snow.
</pre>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)旅行(りょこう)の計画(けいかく)<b>となると</b>、やはり山田(やまだ)さんに相談(そうだん)したほうがいい。
When it comes to planning a Hokkaido trip, you really ought to consult Yamada.
</pre>

`というと` is about what the word brings to mind. `となると` is about what the matter
requires now that it has come up, and it very often ends by nominating a person, a
method or a cost.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 転勤(てんきん)だ<b>となると</b>、家族(かぞく)と相談(そうだん)が必要(ひつよう)だ。 → ✅ 転勤(てんきん)<b>となると</b>、家族(かぞく)と相談(そうだん)が必要(ひつよう)だ。
  A noun attaches with nothing in between. The `だ` is already contained in `となる`.

- ❌ 宝(たから)くじで一億円(いちおくえん)当(あ)たる<b>となると</b>、世界(せかい)を旅(たび)したい。 → ✅ 宝(たから)くじで一億円(いちおくえん)当(あ)たっ<b>たら</b>、世界(せかい)を旅(たび)したい。
  A lottery win is a daydream, not a prospect. `となると` needs a situation that is
  actually in view.

- ❌ 春(はる)になる<b>となると</b>、桜(さくら)が咲(さ)く。 → ✅ 春(はる)になる<b>と</b>、桜(さくら)が咲(さ)く。
  An automatic natural consequence is the plain `と` of N4. `となると` would suggest
  spring were a decision somebody had to weigh.

- ❌ お昼(ひる)ごはん<b>となると</b>、パンでいいです。 → ✅ お昼(ひる)ごはん<b>なら</b>、パンでいいです。
  Lunch is too small a matter to carry `となると`, which makes the topic sound like a
  grave undertaking.

## Practice

**1.** 来月(らいげつ)から一人(ひとり)で担当(たんとう)する____、今(いま)のうちに引(ひ)き継(つ)ぎをしておかないと。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

担当(たんとう)する<b>となると</b>（or となれば）

The handover has become a real prospect with practical consequences attached, which is
precisely when `となると` is used rather than a plain conditional.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 飲(の)み物(もの)は何(なに)がいい? - お茶(ちゃ)（なら・となると）冷(つめ)たいのがいいな。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お茶(ちゃ)<b>なら</b>冷(つめ)たいのがいいな。

This is a light reply picking up what the other person just offered. `となると` would
make choosing a drink sound like a matter of state.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If we're going to redo the whole thing, we'll need to talk to the client first."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

全部(ぜんぶ)やり直(なお)す<b>となれば</b>、まず取引先(とりひきさき)に話(はなし)を通(とお)す必要(ひつよう)があります。

The redo is a live possibility with knock-on obligations, so `となれば` fits; `となると`
would be equally acceptable here.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: パーティーの司会(しかい)だとなると、準備(じゅんび)が大変(たいへん)そうだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ パーティーの司会(しかい)<b>となると</b>、準備(じゅんび)が大変(たいへん)そうだ。

The noun <code>司会(しかい)</code> attaches directly. Inserting `だ` in front of
`となると` is the single most common slip with this pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)が辞(や)める<b>としたら</b>、後任(こうにん)はだれだろう。
(b) 彼(かれ)が辞(や)める<b>となると</b>、後任(こうにん)を急(いそ)いで探(さが)さなければならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is speculation. The speaker is entertaining a possibility and idly wondering who
would replace him; nothing has happened yet.

(b) treats the resignation as something now in prospect - perhaps he has hinted at it -
and moves straight to the work it creates. `となると` is what turns the thought into a
practical problem.

</details>

# ～（よ）うものなら

**Meaning** · if you so much as dared to do that, look what would happen

The volitional form plus `ものなら`. The condition is not an accident but a *choice* -
if someone were to go ahead and do this - and the consequence is a disaster
disproportionate to the act. The exaggeration is not a side effect; it is the whole
point of the pattern.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | volitional ～おう + ものなら | 言(い)う → 言(い)おうものなら |
| る-verb | volitional ～よう + ものなら | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れようものなら |
| する-verb | しよう + ものなら | 失敗(しっぱい)する → 失敗(しっぱい)しようものなら |
| 来(く)る | 来(こ)よう + ものなら | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようものなら |
| Casual contraction | ものなら → もんなら | 遅(おく)れようもんなら |
| Main clause | a dire outcome | 大変(たいへん)なことになる / ～てしまう / ～か分(わ)からない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern loves a minimiser in front of it:
<code>少(すこ)しでも</code>, <code>一言(ひとこと)でも</code>,
<code>一度(いちど)でも</code>. Tiny cause, enormous effect - that contrast is what the
sentence is built to deliver.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
少(すこ)しでも遅(おく)れ<b>ようものなら</b>、置(お)いていかれるよ。
Be even slightly late and they'll go without you.
</pre>

<pre>
一言(ひとこと)でも文句(もんく)を言(い)<b>おうものなら</b>、一日中(いちにちじゅう)説教(せっきょう)される。
Utter one word of complaint and you'll be lectured all day.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)の前(まえ)でその話(はなし)を<b>しようものなら</b>、家中(いえじゅう)が静(しず)まり返(かえ)る。
Bring that subject up in front of my father and the whole house goes silent.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)で傘(かさ)を忘(わす)れ<b>ようものなら</b>、びしょ濡(ぬ)れで帰(かえ)ることになる。
Forget your umbrella around here and you'll be going home soaked.
</pre>

<pre>
新製品(しんせいひん)の情報(じょうほう)を漏(も)ら<b>そうものなら</b>、その場(ば)でクビだ。
Let a word about the new product slip and you're fired on the spot.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)の前日(ぜんじつ)に夜(よ)ふかしを<b>しようものなら</b>、監督(かんとく)に何(なに)を言(い)われるか分(わ)からない。
Stay up late the night before a match and there's no telling what the coach will say.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は厳(きび)しい人(ひと)で、食事中(しょくじちゅう)に肘(ひじ)をつ<b>こうものなら</b>、すぐに箸(はし)が飛(と)んできた。
My grandfather was strict - put an elbow on the table at dinner and chopsticks came flying.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りを一日(いちにち)でも過(す)ぎ<b>ようものなら</b>、担当者(たんとうしゃ)から何度(なんど)も電話(でんわ)がかかってくるでしょう。
Miss the deadline by even a day and the person in charge will be ringing you again and again.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`(よ)うものなら` is theatrical by design. The speaker is not calmly predicting a
consequence; they are telling you how frightening, how strict or how absurd a situation
is by showing what a trivial slip would cost. It therefore turns up in complaints, in
reminiscences about a severe teacher or parent, and in warnings to a newcomer about how
things work here.

Because it dramatises, it is common in the past tense as a description of how life used
to be - <code>口答(くちごた)えしようものなら、殴(なぐ)られたものだ</code> - and there it
carries a slightly nostalgic, slightly appalled tone at once. In the present tense it
is a warning, and it can be affectionate or genuinely alarming depending on delivery.

Register is mid-to-formal in writing and perfectly ordinary in speech, where it
contracts to `もんなら`. It is not stiff, but it is emphatic, so it sounds odd attached
to something the speaker does not actually care about. Two rules never bend: the
consequence must be bad, and the subject must be someone who could choose to act. You
cannot say <code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ろうものなら</code> of the weather, because rain does
not decide anything.

## Contrast

### ～（よ）うものなら vs ～ものなら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - volitional + `ものなら` = "if you dared to". Potential + `ものなら`
= "if only it were possible".

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)を欠席(けっせき)し<b>ようものなら</b>、あとで何(なに)を言(い)われるか分(わ)からない。
Skip the meeting and heaven knows what they'll say about you afterwards.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)を欠席(けっせき)でき<b>るものなら</b>、今日(きょう)こそ休(やす)みたい。
If I could get out of the meeting, today of all days I'd like to.
</pre>

The first fears the act; the second wishes for the ability. Since both end in the same
five syllables, the form in front is the only clue, so it is worth training your eye on
it: <code>し<b>よう</b></code> against <code>でき<b>る</b></code>.

### ～（よ）うものなら vs ～たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たら` states a consequence. `(よ)うものなら` states an
**overreaction**, and expects you to notice how out of proportion it is.

</div>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)り遅(おく)れ<b>たら</b>、次(つぎ)のに乗(の)ればいいよ。
If you miss the train, just take the next one.
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)り遅(おく)れ<b>ようものなら</b>、一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)たされる路線(ろせん)だ。
This is a line where missing your train means an hour's wait.
</pre>

`たら` is neutral and can end kindly, as the first sentence does. `(よ)うものなら` cannot
end kindly at all. If your consequence is mild or pleasant, the pattern is wrong.

### ～（よ）うものなら vs ～ては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ては` complains about something **repeated**. `(よ)うものなら`
inflates a **single, hypothetical** act into a catastrophe.

</div>

<pre>
毎回(まいかい)返事(へんじ)を忘(わす)れ<b>ては</b>、信用(しんよう)されなくなる。
Forgetting to reply every time, you'll stop being trusted.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)でも返事(へんじ)を忘(わす)れ<b>ようものなら</b>、二度(にど)と仕事(しごと)は来(こ)ないだろう。
Forget to reply even once and you'll never get work from them again.
</pre>

`ては` needs the behaviour to be a habit; `(よ)うものなら` works precisely because one
occasion would be enough. That is why the two take opposite adverbs -
<code>毎回(まいかい)</code> against <code>一度(いちど)でも</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 遅(おく)れる<b>ものなら</b>、大変(たいへん)なことになる。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れ<b>ようものなら</b>、大変(たいへん)なことになる。
  The dictionary form does not work here. Without the volitional ending the sentence
  looks like the potential pattern with the potential missing.

- ❌ 来(き)よう<b>ものなら</b> → ✅ 来(こ)よう<b>ものなら</b>
  <code>来(く)る</code> is irregular: the volitional is <code>来(こ)よう</code>,
  built on the こ- stem, not on the き- of <code>来(き)ます</code>.

- ❌ 早(はや)く着(つ)こ<b>うものなら</b>、いい席(せき)が取(と)れる。 → ✅ 早(はや)く着(つ)け<b>ば</b>、いい席(せき)が取(と)れる。
  The outcome has to be a disaster. A good result cancels the pattern entirely.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)が来(こ)よ<b>うものなら</b>、電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まる。 → ✅ 台風(たいふう)が来(く)れ<b>ば</b>、電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まる。
  A typhoon does not choose to arrive. The volitional needs a subject capable of
  deciding, which is why this pattern is nearly always about people.

## Practice

**1.** この会社(かいしゃ)では、定時(ていじ)で帰(かえ)ろう____、やる気(き)がないと思(おも)われる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

帰(かえ)ろ<b>うものなら</b>

Leaving on time is a small, ordinary act, and the penalty - being written off as
unmotivated - is wildly out of proportion. That gap is exactly what the pattern
dramatises.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 外(そと)へ（出(で)られるものなら・出(で)ようものなら）、久(ひさ)しぶりに散歩(さんぽ)がしたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

外(そと)へ<b>出(で)られるものなら</b>、久(ひさ)しぶりに散歩(さんぽ)がしたい。

The sentence ends in a wish, so it needs the potential form. The volitional version
would demand a calamity in the second half instead.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "In that house, if you left so much as one grain of rice, you got told off."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの家(いえ)では、ご飯(はん)を一粒(ひとつぶ)でも残(のこ)<b>そうものなら</b>叱(しか)られた。

<code>残(のこ)す</code> is an う-verb, so its volitional is <code>残(のこ)そう</code>,
and <code>一粒(ひとつぶ)でも</code> supplies the minimiser this pattern likes.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 嘘(うそ)をつくものなら、二度(にど)と信(しん)じてもらえない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 嘘(うそ)をつ<b>こうものなら</b>、二度(にど)と信(しん)じてもらえない。

<code>つく</code> has to become the volitional <code>つこう</code>. As written, the
sentence reaches for the potential pattern and finds no potential form there.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)ん<b>だら</b>、先輩(せんぱい)に注意(ちゅうい)される。
(b) 練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)<b>もうものなら</b>、先輩(せんぱい)に何(なに)を言(い)われるか分(わ)からない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of what follows: skip practice, get a word from your senior.
It sounds like a fair and predictable rule.

(b) makes the senior sound formidable and the consequence unpredictable. The speaker is
not informing you of a rule so much as warning you about an atmosphere, and the
exaggeration is doing that work.

</details>

# ～くらいなら

**Meaning** · rather than put up with that, I'd sooner do this

`くらいなら` names something the speaker finds intolerable and then reaches for an
alternative - often an extreme one - as the lesser evil. Neither option is attractive.
The sentence is a choice made in exasperation, and it usually ends in
<code>ほうがましだ</code>, "that would be preferable", which itself means "less bad"
rather than "good".

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + くらいなら | 謝(あやま)る → 謝(あやま)るくらいなら |
| Verb (state) | ～ている + くらいなら | 待(ま)っている → 待(ま)っているくらいなら |
| Verb phrase, not a bare noun | ～する + くらいなら | バス → バスで行(い)くくらいなら |
| Casual variant | ぐらいなら | 並(なら)ぶ → 並(なら)ぶぐらいなら |
| Main clause | the lesser evil | ～ほうがましだ / ～ほうがいい / ～たい / ～ない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ましだ</code> is the pattern's signature ending and it is not a
compliment. It means "preferable to that", with the strong implication that the
preferred option is only marginally better. Substituting
<code>すばらしい</code> or <code>最高(さいこう)だ</code> breaks the logic.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
捨(す)てる<b>くらいなら</b>、私(わたし)にください。
If you're going to throw it away, give it to me.
</pre>

<pre>
並(なら)んで待(ま)つ<b>ぐらいなら</b>、別(べつ)の店(みせ)に行(い)こうよ。
Rather than stand in a queue, let's just go somewhere else.
</pre>

<pre>
あんな上司(じょうし)に謝(あやま)る<b>くらいなら</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めたほうがましだ。
I'd sooner quit than apologise to a boss like that.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)に頼(たの)む<b>くらいなら</b>、自分(じぶん)でやったほうが早(はや)い。
Rather than ask someone else, it's quicker to do it myself.
</pre>

<pre>
途中(とちゅう)でやめる<b>くらいなら</b>、最初(さいしょ)から始(はじ)めないほうがいい。
If you're going to give up halfway, better not to start at all.
</pre>

<pre>
満員(まんいん)電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)る<b>くらいなら</b>、一時間(いちじかん)歩(ある)くほうがましです。
I'd rather walk an hour than get on a packed train.
</pre>

<pre>
嫌(いや)な思(おも)いをしてまで付(つ)き合(あ)いを続(つづ)ける<b>くらいなら</b>、いっそ連絡(れんらく)を絶(た)ってしまいたい。
Rather than keep up a friendship that makes me miserable, I'd honestly sooner cut off contact.
</pre>

<pre>
中途半端(ちゅうとはんぱ)な物(もの)を世(よ)に出(だ)す<b>くらいなら</b>、発売(はつばい)を延(の)ばしてでも完成度(かんせいど)を上(あ)げるべきだ。
Rather than put out something half-finished, we should push back the release and raise the quality.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is an emotional pattern. Choosing `くらいなら` announces that the first option is
not merely worse but *unacceptable*, and speakers reach for deliberately extreme
alternatives to make the point - quitting, walking an hour, never starting.
<code>死(し)んだほうがましだ</code> is a real and common ending, and it is understood as
hyperbole, not as a statement about wanting to die.

`くらいなら` and `ぐらいなら` are the same thing; `ぐらい` is a little more casual and a
little more frequent in speech. The pattern itself is register-neutral and appears in
polite speech (<code>～ほうがましです</code>) and in editorials as readily as in
grumbling, though the grumbling is its home ground.

Two limits are worth holding on to. First, the alternative has to be a *choice between
bad options*: if you actually like the second thing, plain `より` or `ほうがいい` is what
you want. Second, `くらいなら` needs a clause in front of it, not a bare noun -
<code>バスくらいなら</code> means "if it's only as much as a bus", which is a different
pattern entirely. Put a verb in: <code>バスで行(い)くくらいなら</code>.

## Contrast

### ～くらいなら vs ～より（は）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `より` compares two options neutrally. `くらいなら` **rejects** the
first outright and settles for the second.

</div>

<pre>
バス<b>より</b>電車(でんしゃ)のほうが速(はや)いですよ。
The train is faster than the bus.
</pre>

<pre>
渋滞(じゅうたい)したバスに乗(の)る<b>くらいなら</b>、駅(えき)まで走(はし)ります。
Rather than sit on a bus stuck in traffic, I'll run to the station.
</pre>

`より` can compare two good things - <code>コーヒーよりお茶(ちゃ)が好(す)きだ</code> -
and carries no feeling. `くらいなら` cannot be neutral: the thing in front of it is
always something the speaker refuses.

### ～くらいなら vs ～のなら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のなら` supposes the thing might happen and responds to it.
`くらいなら` treats it as unbearable and turns away.

</div>

<pre>
本当(ほんとう)に謝(あやま)る<b>のなら</b>、こちらも水(みず)に流(なが)しますよ。
If you're genuinely going to apologise, I'll let the matter drop.
</pre>

<pre>
心(こころ)にもないことを言(い)う<b>くらいなら</b>、何(なに)も言(い)わないでほしい。
Rather than say something you don't mean, I'd rather you said nothing.
</pre>

`のなら` leaves the door open and can be followed by an offer, an instruction or a
condition. `くらいなら` slams it, and can only be followed by the speaker's preferred
alternative.

### ～くらいなら vs the "extent" ～くらい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if what precedes is an **action you would hate**, the meaning is
"rather than". If it is a **degree or amount**, the meaning is "if it's only that
much".

</div>

<pre>
少(すこ)し高(たか)い<b>くらいなら</b>、買(か)ってもいいかな。
If it's only a bit expensive, I might buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
定価(ていか)で買(か)う<b>くらいなら</b>、セールまで待(ま)ちます。
Rather than pay full price, I'll wait for the sale.
</pre>

Same four syllables, two patterns. The giveaway is what comes before: an adjective or a
quantity gives you the "only that much" reading, a volitional action gives you the
"rather than" one. The main clauses differ too - the first ends in permission, the
second in a rejection.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>くらいなら</b>、海(うみ)がいいです。 → ✅ 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く<b>なら</b>、海(うみ)がいいです。
  Travelling is not something the speaker is refusing, so there is nothing for
  `くらいなら` to reject. A neutral supposition takes `なら`.

- ❌ バス<b>くらいなら</b>、歩(ある)きます。 → ✅ バスに乗(の)る<b>くらいなら</b>、歩(ある)きます。
  A bare noun in front of `くらい` gives the "about, only that much" reading. Supply a
  verb to get the "rather than" sense.

- ❌ 待(ま)たされる<b>くらいなら</b>、時間(じかん)がかかります。 → ✅ 待(ま)たされる<b>くらいなら</b>、予約(よやく)して行(い)きます。
  The second half must be the alternative the speaker chooses. A plain description of
  the situation leaves the comparison unfinished.

- ❌ この仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)ける<b>くらいなら</b>、転職(てんしょく)したほうがすばらしい。 → ✅ この仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)ける<b>くらいなら</b>、転職(てんしょく)したほうがましだ。
  <code>ましだ</code> is the right word because the alternative is only *less bad*.
  Praising it outright contradicts the resignation the pattern carries.

## Practice

**1.** こんな安(やす)い値段(ねだん)で売(う)る____、自分(じぶん)で使(つか)ったほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

売(う)る<b>くらいなら</b>

Selling at that price is being rejected as not worth doing, and the second half offers
the alternative the speaker prefers - the standard shape of this pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)来(こ)られる（なら・くらいなら）、資料(しりょう)を用意(ようい)しておきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)来(こ)られる<b>なら</b>、資料(しりょう)を用意(ようい)しておきます。

The speaker is arranging something helpfully, not refusing anything. `くらいなら` would
mean the visit was intolerable, which contradicts preparing documents for it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'd rather be told to my face than hear about it from someone else."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

他人(たにん)から聞(き)かされる<b>くらいなら</b>、直接(ちょくせつ)言(い)ってもらったほうがましだ。

Hearing it secondhand is the rejected option, and <code>ほうがましだ</code> supplies
the "lesser evil" ending the pattern expects.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 残(のこ)すくらいなら、最初(さいしょ)から少(すく)なく注文(ちゅうもん)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 残(のこ)すくらいなら、最初(さいしょ)から少(すく)なく注文(ちゅうもん)した<b>ほうがいい</b>。

The problem is the request. `くらいなら` presents the speaker's own preference between
two options, so it is completed by a comparative judgement rather than by an
instruction.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)つ<b>より</b>、歩(ある)いたほうが早(はや)い。
(b) 一時間(いちじかん)も待(ま)つ<b>くらいなら</b>、歩(ある)いたほうがましだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a calm calculation: walking takes less time than waiting, and the speaker is
simply reporting which is quicker.

(b) is a refusal. An hour's wait is presented as unbearable, walking is grudgingly
accepted as the lesser evil, and <code>も</code> and <code>ましだ</code> both carry the
speaker's irritation.

</details>

# ～ては～ては

**Meaning** · doing A, then B, then A again, then B again - a cycle that keeps turning

The same `ては` as the rest of this family, but doubled. Instead of setting a condition,
the repetition paints a picture: two actions alternating, over and over, until the
shape of the sentence *is* the repetition being described. Waves that come in and go
out. A writer who writes and deletes and writes and deletes.

## Formation

Two verbs form a pair, and the pair is stated twice.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb pair | V1て + は + V2 (ます-stem), repeated | 書(か)く・消(け)す → 書(か)いては消(け)し、書(か)いては消(け)し |
| Verb (て-form in で) | ～で + は | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)んでは置(お)き |
| Closing the cycle | the last verb takes a finite form | ～ては～した / ～ては～している |
| As a noun phrase | ～ては～の + noun | ～ては～の繰(く)り返(かえ)し / ～ては～の毎日(まいにち) |
| Single descriptive pair | Aては B | 波(なみ)が寄(よ)せては返(かえ)す |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb in the middle of the cycle appears in its ます-stem -
<code>消(け)し</code>, <code>寝(ね)</code>, <code>置(お)き</code> - not its
dictionary form. That connective form is what lets one clause run into the next and
gives the pattern its rhythm.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
波(なみ)が<b>寄(よ)せては返(かえ)し、寄(よ)せては返(かえ)す</b>。
The waves roll in and draw back, in and back again.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)は<b>食(た)べては寝(ね)、食(た)べては寝(ね)</b>の生活(せいかつ)だ。
On my days off it's eat, sleep, eat, sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は原稿(げんこう)を<b>書(か)いては消(け)し、書(か)いては消(け)し</b>ていた。
He wrote and deleted, wrote and deleted.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちは<b>笑(わら)っては泣(な)き、泣(な)いては笑(わら)い</b>、一日中(いちにちじゅう)忙(いそが)しい。
The children laugh, then cry, then cry, then laugh - busy all day long.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)は新聞(しんぶん)を<b>読(よ)んでは置(お)き、読(よ)んでは置(お)き</b>して、落(お)ち着(つ)かない様子(ようす)だった。
My father kept picking the paper up and putting it down, unable to settle.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の天気(てんき)は<b>晴(は)れては曇(くも)り、曇(くも)っては晴(は)れ</b>で、まったく予想(よそう)がつかない。
Mountain weather clears and clouds over and clears again - there's no predicting it.
</pre>

<pre>
受験生(じゅけんせい)のころは、<b>覚(おぼ)えては忘(わす)れ、覚(おぼ)えては忘(わす)れ</b>の繰(く)り返(かえ)しだった。
When I was studying for entrance exams, it was learn and forget, learn and forget.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は<b>外(そと)を見(み)ては時計(とけい)を見(み)、また外(そと)を見(み)ては時計(とけい)を見(み)</b>、息子(むすこ)の帰(かえ)りを待(ま)っていた。
My grandmother kept looking outside, then at the clock, outside, then at the clock, waiting for her son to come home.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a describing pattern rather than an arguing one. It belongs to narrative -
novels, essays, reminiscence, nature writing - and it works by rhythm. Read one aloud
and you can hear why the middle verb is in the ます-stem: the clauses run into each
other without ever coming to a stop, which is the point.

The feeling it carries is usually one of two. Either the cycle is futile or wearying -
learning and forgetting, writing and deleting, eating and sleeping - or it is calm and
natural, as with waves and weather. It very rarely sounds cheerful. Applied to the
speaker's own life, as in <code>食(た)べては寝(ね)の毎日(まいにち)</code>, the tone is
self-deprecating.

In conversation people reach for `～たり～たり` far more often, and this pattern in
speech sounds a little literary - which is sometimes exactly the effect wanted. The
noun-phrase version, <code>～ては～の繰(く)り返(かえ)し</code> and
<code>～ては～の毎日(まいにち)</code>, is the shape that does turn up in ordinary talk,
and it is the most useful one for a learner to be able to produce.

One structural point: the two verbs should form a genuine pair, usually opposites or a
natural sequence - write and erase, open and close, stand and sit, come and go. Two
unrelated actions do not make a cycle, and the sentence will simply read as broken.

## Contrast

### ～ては～ては vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たり～たり` lists a **sample** of things done, in no particular
order. `ては～ては` describes a **cycle** in a fixed order, repeating.

</div>

<pre>
休日(きゅうじつ)は本(ほん)を読(よ)ん<b>だり</b>映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>たり</b>しています。
On my days off I read, watch films, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じページを<b>読(よ)んでは戻(もど)り、読(よ)んでは戻(もど)り</b>して、なかなか先(さき)へ進(すす)まない。
I read the same page, go back, read it, go back - I'm getting nowhere.
</pre>

Swap them and the sentences change shape entirely. The `たり` version says nothing about
order or repetition; the `ては` version is *about* order and repetition and would be
pointless without them.

### ～ては～ては vs the conditional ～ては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one `ては` followed by a bad outcome is the conditional. Two `ては`
with a matching pair of verbs is the repetition pattern.

</div>

<pre>
途中(とちゅう)で投(な)げ出(だ)し<b>ては</b>、何(なに)も身(み)につかない。
If you keep abandoning things halfway, nothing will ever stick.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい趣味(しゅみ)を<b>始(はじ)めては飽(あ)き、始(はじ)めては飽(あ)き</b>で、何(なに)も続(つづ)かない。
Taking up a new hobby, getting bored, taking up another, getting bored - nothing lasts.
</pre>

The first delivers a verdict; the second describes a habit. They are close in feeling,
because both are usually a little disapproving, but only the first one is a conditional.
The test is whether a second `ては` clause is coming.

### ～ては～ては vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` means the two actions run **at the same time**.
`ては～ては` means they take **turns**.

</div>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>ながら</b>料理(りょうり)をするのが好(す)きです。
I like to cook with music on.
</pre>

<pre>
レシピを<b>見(み)ては手(て)を止(と)め、見(み)ては手(て)を止(と)め</b>、料理(りょうり)に一時間(いちじかん)もかかってしまった。
Checking the recipe, stopping, checking again, stopping again - the cooking took a whole hour.
</pre>

With `ながら` the hands never leave the pan. With `ては～ては` they do, repeatedly, and
that interruption is the whole content of the sentence. If the two actions could
genuinely be done together, this pattern is the wrong choice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 書(か)く<b>ては</b>消(け)す。 → ✅ 書(か)い<b>ては</b>消(け)し。
  The first verb must be in て-form. A dictionary form in front of `は` is not a form
  Japanese has.

- ❌ 覚(おぼ)えては忘(わす)れた。（meaning "over and over"） → ✅ 覚(おぼ)えては忘(わす)れ、覚(おぼ)えては忘(わす)れた。
  One turn of the wheel is not a cycle. The pair has to be stated twice for the
  repetition to be there.

- ❌ 朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べ<b>ては</b>出(で)かけた。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べ<b>てから</b>出(で)かけた。
  A single sequence of two events takes `てから`. `ては` would claim the speaker ate and
  left again and again.

- ❌ 押(お)しては引(ひ)<b>く</b>、押(お)しては引(ひ)<b>く</b>した。 → ✅ 押(お)しては引(ひ)<b>き</b>、押(お)しては引(ひ)<b>いた</b>。
  The verb in the middle of the cycle goes into the ます-stem, and only the final one
  takes a finished form.

## Practice

**1.** 母(はは)はスーツケースに服(ふく)を詰(つ)め____出(だ)し、詰(つ)めては出(だ)しして、なかなか決(き)められないようだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

詰(つ)め<b>ては</b>出(だ)し

The て-form plus `は`, with the second verb in its ます-stem, sets up the alternation
that the repeated phrase then completes.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)の夜(よる)は、映画(えいが)を見(み)（たり・ては）音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)い（たり・ては）していた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

映画(えいが)を見(み)<b>たり</b>音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)い<b>たり</b>していた。

Watching a film and listening to music are two examples of how the evening was spent,
not a cycle turning over and over, so `たり～たり` is what the sentence needs.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He kept standing up and sitting down again, unable to calm himself."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は<b>立(た)っては座(すわ)り、立(た)っては座(すわ)り</b>して、どうしても落(お)ち着(つ)けないようだった。

Standing and sitting are natural opposites, which is what makes them a usable pair, and
stating the pair twice is what conveys "kept".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)は手紙(てがみ)を書(か)いては破(やぶ)る、書(か)いては破(やぶ)るした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は手紙(てがみ)を書(か)いては<b>破(やぶ)り</b>、書(か)いては破(やぶ)<b>った</b>。

The middle verb takes the ます-stem <code>破(やぶ)り</code>, and the cycle is closed by
putting the last verb into a finished form. <code>破(やぶ)るした</code> is not a
possible ending.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たり</b>閉(し)め<b>たり</b>している。
(b) 窓(まど)を<b>開(あ)けては閉(し)め、開(あ)けては閉(し)め</b>している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports two things the person does with the window, loosely and without order. It
could describe someone adjusting the room temperature over an afternoon.

(b) makes it a fixed, restless cycle - open, close, open, close - and invites you to
read something into it: indecision, agitation, or an inability to settle. The pattern
supplies that reading, not the verbs.

</details>
