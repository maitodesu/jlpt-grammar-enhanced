# N2 - Adding on, listing, and alternatives


---

# その上

**Meaning** · besides; on top of that; what is more

<code>その上(うえ)</code> starts a new sentence and stacks a second fact onto the one
just stated. Both facts push the same way - two reasons to be pleased, or two
reasons to complain - and the second arrives as extra weight, not as a twist.

## Usage

<code>その上(うえ)</code> is a conjunction. It stands at the head of its sentence,
normally with a comma after it, and nothing attaches to it:

- 部屋(へや)が広(ひろ)い。その上(うえ)、静(しず)かだ。

The <code>上(うえ)</code> is the everyday noun for "top", and the image is literal:
the new fact is laid on top of the old one. That image gives the one hard rule -
**both halves must lean the same way.** Two pluses stack, two minuses stack, but a
plus and a minus do not; that needs が or しかし.

It joins finished sentences, not phrases. To fold the same addition into a single
sentence, the clause-internal form is <code>上(うえ)に</code>:
<code>広(ひろ)い上(うえ)に静(しず)かだ</code>. Same word, different job.

The added element very often carries も, まで or さえ, because
<code>その上(うえ)</code> announces an addition and the particle marks what is being
added. Both the kanji spelling <code>その上(うえ)</code> and the all-kana そのうえ are
standard; kana looks a shade softer and is common in email.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>その上(うえ)で</code>, with で, is a different expression meaning
"and then, on that basis". 見積(みつ)もりを取(と)って、その上(うえ)で決(き)めます
is "I'll get a quote and decide after that" - nothing to do with "besides".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この店(みせ)のパンは安(やす)い。<b>その上(うえ)</b>やわらかい。
The bread at this shop is cheap. And it's soft, too.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)に迷(まよ)って、<b>その上(うえ)</b>雨(あめ)まで降(ふ)ってきた。
We got lost, and on top of that it started raining.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は日(ひ)当(あ)たりがよく、<b>その上(うえ)</b>家賃(やちん)も相場(そうば)より安(やす)いんです。
This flat gets good light, and on top of that the rent is below the going rate.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい課長(かちょう)は指示(しじ)が具体的(ぐたいてき)で、<b>その上(うえ)</b>こちらの事情(じじょう)もきちんと聞(き)いてくれる。
The new section chief gives concrete instructions, and on top of that he listens properly to our side of things.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral to slightly formal, and more written than spoken. It is completely at home
in a report, a product description, a polite complaint or a piece of persuasive
writing, and it is fine in ます-form conversation. In quick casual talk it sounds a
touch composed - friends reach for それに or しかも first - but nobody would call it
wrong.

Because it stacks rather than surprises, <code>その上(うえ)</code> tends to appear
where a case is being built: a landlord listing selling points, a customer listing
grievances. Two or three items in a row is normal, and the last one usually gets
the strongest particle: …、その上(うえ)返事(へんじ)さえない.

It carries no judgement of its own. Whether the sentence sounds delighted or fed up
comes entirely from the two facts you stack; the connector just supplies the plus
sign. That is what separates it from おまけに, which drags the sentence towards
"and to make things worse" all by itself.

## Contrast

### その上(うえ) vs それに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different weight. `それに` is light and conversational;
<code>その上(うえ)</code> presents the added fact as substantial.

</div>

<pre>
もう遅(おそ)いし、<b>それに</b>明日(あした)早(はや)いから。
It's late, and besides I have an early start tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
工期(こうき)が延(の)びております。<b>その上(うえ)</b>、資材(しざい)の価格(かかく)も上(あ)がっております。
The build is running late. On top of that, material prices have risen.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences still parse, but the first would sound oddly formal
for a friend at a bar, and the second oddly breezy in a letter to a client.

### その上(うえ) vs 上(うえ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>その上(うえ)</code> joins **two sentences**;
<code>上(うえ)に</code> joins **two clauses inside one sentence**.

</div>

<pre>
この机(つくえ)は軽(かる)い。<b>その上(うえ)</b>丈夫(じょうぶ)だ。
This desk is light. And it's sturdy as well.
</pre>

<pre>
この机(つくえ)は軽(かる)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>丈夫(じょうぶ)だ。
This desk is sturdy as well as light.
</pre>

The meaning is the same; the packaging is not. Putting a full stop before
<code>その上(うえ)</code> gives each fact its own beat, which is why it suits spoken
delivery and lists. <code>上(うえ)に</code> keeps everything in one breath and reads
more tightly on the page.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い。その上(うえ)、サービスが悪(わる)い。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い。<b>が</b>、サービスは悪(わる)い。
  Cheap is a plus and bad service is a minus, so they cannot be stacked. Anything
  that changes direction needs が, しかし or ただ.

- ❌ この机(つくえ)は軽(かる)いその上(うえ)丈夫(じょうぶ)だ。 → ✅ この机(つくえ)は軽(かる)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>丈夫(じょうぶ)だ。
  <code>その上(うえ)</code> cannot sit inside a sentence as a joint. Either end the
  first clause or switch to <code>上(うえ)に</code>.

- ❌ 説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いて、その上(うえ)申(もう)し込(こ)みます。 → ✅ 説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いて、<b>その上(うえ)で</b>申(もう)し込(こ)みます。
  "And then, having done that" needs the で. Without it the sentence reads
  "besides, I'll apply", which is not what you meant.

## Practice

**1.** この旅館(りょかん)は料理(りょうり)がすばらしい。____、温泉(おんせん)も広(ひろ)くて気持(きも)ちがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>その上(うえ)</b>、温泉(おんせん)も広(ひろ)くて気持(きも)ちがいい。

Two selling points in a row, so they stack. Notice the も on
<code>温泉(おんせん)</code>: the added item is normally marked, because the connector
has already announced that something is being added.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)の部屋(へや)は狭(せま)いその上(うえ)物(もの)が多(おお)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)の部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>物(もの)が多(おお)い。（or 狭(せま)い。その上(うえ)、物(もの)が多(おお)い。）

<code>その上(うえ)</code> begins a sentence; it cannot glue two clauses together. Use
<code>上(うえ)に</code> inside one sentence, or put a full stop in and keep
<code>その上(うえ)</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 条件(じょうけん)を確認(かくにん)して、その上(うえ)連絡(れんらく)します。
(b) 条件(じょうけん)を確認(かくにん)して、その上(うえ)で連絡(れんらく)します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is what a working adult actually says: "I'll check the terms and get back to
you once I have." The で makes it sequential - the checking is the basis for the
call.

(a) tries to use the additive connector after a て-form, so it comes out as
"I'll check the terms, and besides, I'll contact you" - two unrelated items rather
than one plan. Native listeners will assume you meant (b) and heard a missing で.

</details>

# ～上に

**Meaning** · as well as; not only that, but also - adds a second point of the same
kind about the same thing

<code>上(うえ)に</code> is the noun <code>上(うえ)</code>, "top", pressed into
service as a joint: the second clause is laid on top of the first. It is the
one-sentence version of "and on top of that", and it comes with two conditions that
learners routinely break - both halves must be the same *kind* of point, and both
must be about the same thing.

## Formation

The clause in front is in noun-modifying shape, because <code>上(うえ)</code> is a
noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + 上(うえ)に | 寝坊(ねぼう)する → 寝坊(ねぼう)した上(うえ)に |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 上(うえ)に | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ない上(うえ)に |
| い-adjective | plain form + 上(うえ)に | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)い上(うえ)に |
| な-adjective | ～な + 上(うえ)に | 静(しず)かな → 静(しず)かな上(うえ)に |
| な-adjective (written) | ～である + 上(うえ)に | 明朗(めいろう)である上(うえ)に |
| Noun | ～の + 上(うえ)に | 休日(きゅうじつ) → 休日(きゅうじつ)の上(うえ)に |
| Noun (written) | ～である + 上(うえ)に | 新人(しんじん)である上(うえ)に |
| Polite | … + 上(うえ)に、…です／ます | 狭(せま)い上(うえ)に暗(くら)いです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the second clause almost always marks its new element with も, まで or
さえ. That particle is not decoration: it is what tells the listener the sentence is
adding rather than explaining. 遅刻(ちこく)した上(うえ)に資料(しりょう)<b>まで</b>忘(わす)れた。

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は重(おも)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>高(たか)い。
This dictionary is heavy, and expensive on top of that.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は字(じ)がきれいな<b>上(うえ)に</b>書(か)くのが速(はや)い。
Her handwriting is beautiful, and she writes fast as well.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが来(こ)ない<b>上(うえ)に</b>、傘(かさ)も忘(わす)れてしまった。
The bus wasn't coming, and on top of that I'd left my umbrella behind.
</pre>

<pre>
休日(きゅうじつ)の<b>上(うえ)に</b>天気(てんき)もよかったので、海岸(かいがん)は家族(かぞく)連(づ)れでいっぱいだった。
It was a public holiday, and the weather was good as well, so the beach was packed with families.
</pre>

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>上(うえ)に</b>電車(でんしゃ)まで止(と)まって、会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。
I overslept, and then the trains stopped as well, so I missed the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
その中古車(ちゅうこしゃ)は走行(そうこう)距離(きょり)が短(みじか)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>、整備(せいび)の記録(きろく)もきちんと残(のこ)っていた。
The used car had low mileage, and its service records were all there as well.
</pre>

<pre>
この地区(ちく)は坂(さか)が多(おお)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>街灯(がいとう)が少(すく)ないので、夜道(よみち)の一人(ひとり)歩(ある)きはお勧(すす)めしません。
This area has a lot of hills and few street lights as well, so I wouldn't recommend walking alone after dark.
</pre>

<pre>
あの旅館(りょかん)は料金(りょうきん)が明朗(めいろう)である<b>上(うえ)に</b>、急(きゅう)なキャンセルにも柔軟(じゅうなん)に対応(たいおう)してくれる。
That inn is transparent about its prices, and it deals flexibly with last-minute cancellations too.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself, but it reads a little more composed than the casual
alternatives, so it is common in reviews, reports, complaints and polite speech, and
less common in fast talk between friends, where し does the same work with less
effort.

Two restrictions do the real damage, and neither is visible in the English:

**Same direction.** The two clauses must be the same kind of evaluation. Two
advantages stack; two problems stack; an advantage and a problem do not. The
sentence is an accumulation, and an accumulation cannot change sign halfway.

**Same subject.** Both halves have to be about the same thing or person. You can say
that a flat is small *and* dark; you cannot use <code>上(うえ)に</code> to say that
the flat is small and the neighbour is noisy unless the whole sentence is plainly
about living there. Where the topics genuinely differ, し is the connector you want.

The tense of the first clause behaves normally: た-form for something completed
(<code>寝坊(ねぼう)した上(うえ)に</code>), plain non-past for a standing property
(<code>狭(せま)い上(うえ)に</code>). A run of misfortunes in the past is one of the
commonest shapes this pattern takes, and in that use it carries an audible sigh.

<code>上(うえ)に</code> describes; it does not ask. A second clause that is a
request, an invitation or an order sits badly here - for those, だけでなく is the
pattern that will take them.

<div class="note">

**Note** - keep <code>上(うえ)に</code> apart from <code>上(うえ)で</code>, which
means "after doing, on the basis of": 相談(そうだん)した上(うえ)で決(き)めます,
"I'll decide after consulting". One kana apart, completely different meanings.

</div>

## Contrast

### 上(うえ)に vs だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>上(うえ)に</code> **accumulates** two facts of the same
sign. `だけでなく` **widens the scope** from a smaller case to a bigger one.

</div>

<pre>
この機械(きかい)は音(おと)が大(おお)きい<b>上(うえ)に</b>電気代(でんきだい)もかかる。
This machine is loud, and it runs up the electricity bill as well.
</pre>

<pre>
この機械(きかい)は工場(こうじょう)<b>だけでなく</b>家庭(かてい)でも使(つか)われている。
This machine is used not just in factories but in homes as well.
</pre>

The first sentence lists two drawbacks of one machine. The second says the set of
places it is used in is wider than you assumed. `だけでなく` therefore has no
same-sign restriction, and it can be followed by a request -
<code>お茶(ちゃ)だけでなく氷(こおり)も買(か)ってきてください</code> - where
<code>上(うえ)に</code> cannot.

### 上(うえ)に vs に加(くわ)えて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>上(うえ)に</code> stacks **evaluations**;
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> stacks **items**, and does it in a report voice.

</div>

<pre>
この冬(ふゆ)は寒(さむ)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>雪(ゆき)も多(おお)い。
This winter is cold, and there's a lot of snow as well.
</pre>

<pre>
記録的(きろくてき)な寒(さむ)さ<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、積雪(せきせつ)も平年(へいねん)を上回(うわまわ)っている。
On top of record cold, snowfall is also running above the seasonal average.
</pre>

<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> takes a noun, not a clause, and belongs to written and
broadcast Japanese; it is neutral about whether the additions are welcome. In
conversation it sounds like a news bulletin, which is exactly where you hear it.

### 上(うえ)に vs ～し

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `し` leaves the list **open**, often implying "and other reasons
besides". <code>上(うえ)に</code> closes it: these two, and they weigh.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は疲(つか)れている<b>し</b>、また今度(こんど)にしよう。
I'm tired today - let's leave it for another time.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は疲(つか)れている<b>上(うえ)に</b>資料(しりょう)もそろっていない。
I'm tired today, and the documents aren't ready either.
</pre>

`し` is the casual workhorse: it can trail off at the end of a sentence, it can be
repeated three or four times, and it lets the listener supply the rest. It also
leads happily into a suggestion. <code>上(うえ)に</code> does none of those; it
presents a closed pair as a weight of evidence, which is why it turns up in
complaints and product reviews.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>上(うえ)に</b>明(あか)るい。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>な上(うえ)に</b>明(あか)るい。
  <code>上(うえ)</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective keeps its な and a noun needs
  の.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>味(あじ)がよくない。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>が</b>、味(あじ)はよくない。
  A plus and a minus cannot be stacked. A change of direction needs が or しかし.

- ❌ 兄(あに)は背(せ)が高(たか)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>、弟(おとうと)は足(あし)が速(はや)い。 → ✅ 兄(あに)は背(せ)が高(たか)い<b>し</b>、弟(おとうと)は足(あし)が速(はや)い。
  Two different people, so there is nothing for the second fact to sit on top of.
  `し` lists facts about separate subjects without complaint.

- ❌ 説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いた<b>上(うえ)に</b>申(もう)し込(こ)みます。 → ✅ 説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いた<b>上(うえ)で</b>申(もう)し込(こ)みます。
  With に it means "besides"; with で it means "after doing". Here you want で.

## Practice

**1.** この物件(ぶっけん)は駅(えき)から遠(とお)い____、スーパーも近(ちか)くにない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)から遠(とお)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>、スーパーも近(ちか)くにない。

Two drawbacks of one and the same property, so they stack. The も on スーパー is the
giveaway that an addition is being made rather than a reason given.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新(あたら)しいアパートは家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い（上(うえ)に・が）、日(ひ)当(あ)たりが悪(わる)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い<b>が</b>、日(ひ)当(あ)たりが悪(わる)い。

Cheap rent is a plus and poor light is a minus, so the sentence changes sign and
<code>上(うえ)に</code> is blocked. Had the second clause been
<code>駅(えき)も近(ちか)い</code>, <code>上(うえ)に</code> would be the natural
choice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He is an expert in the field, and he's good at teaching as well."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)はこの分野(ぶんや)の専門家(せんもんか)である<b>上(うえ)に</b>、教(おし)え方(かた)も上手(じょうず)だ。

A noun attaches with である in written style, or with の in speech -
専門家(せんもんか)の上(うえ)に. Both halves are compliments about the same person,
so the pattern applies cleanly.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は財布(さいふ)をなくした上(うえ)で、携帯(けいたい)も壊(こわ)れた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)は財布(さいふ)をなくした<b>上(うえ)に</b>、携帯(けいたい)も壊(こわ)れた。

<code>上(うえ)で</code> would make the lost wallet the basis on which the phone
broke, which is nonsense. A run of bad luck is an accumulation, so it takes に.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 安(やす)くておいしい店(みせ)です。
(b) 安(やす)い上(うえ)においしい店(みせ)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat description: the shop has two qualities, joined by the neutral
て-form, and neither is foregrounded.

(b) presents the second quality as a bonus on top of the first - "cheap, and on top
of that the food is good." The speaker is making a case, so it fits a recommendation
or a review. Use (a) to describe, (b) to persuade.

</details>

# ～に加えて

**Meaning** · in addition to; on top of - adds a second item to one already named,
in a report voice

<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> is the て-form of <code>加(くわ)える</code>, "to add",
frozen into a connector. Whatever it follows is the thing already on the table; what
comes after is the second item being put beside it. It is arithmetic rather than
emotion - the sentence does not say the addition is welcome or unwelcome, only that
it is there.

## Formation

The single most important fact about this pattern: **it attaches to a noun.** A
clause has to be nominalised first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に加(くわ)えて | 会費(かいひ) → 会費(かいひ)に加(くわ)えて |
| Noun (written, clipped) | Noun + に加(くわ)え | 高騰(こうとう) → 高騰(こうとう)に加(くわ)え |
| Verb (nominalised) | plain form + こと + に加(くわ)えて | 広(ひろ)がる → 広(ひろ)がったことに加(くわ)えて |
| い-adjective (nominalised) | ～こと／～さ + に加(くわ)えて | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いことに加(くわ)えて |
| な-adjective (nominalised) | ～なこと／～さ + に加(くわ)えて | 静(しず)かな → 静(しず)かなことに加(くわ)えて |
| Sentence opener | 加(くわ)えて、… | - → 加(くわ)えて、費用(ひよう)も上(あ)がる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the added element normally takes も or まで. Without it the sentence still
parses, but it reads as though the second item were a separate announcement rather
than an addition: …に加(くわ)えて、送料(そうりょう)<b>も</b>かかります。

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
会費(かいひ)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、教材費(きょうざいひ)も必要(ひつよう)です。
On top of the membership fee, there is also a charge for materials.
</pre>

<pre>
英語(えいご)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>スペイン語(ご)も習(なら)っている。
As well as English, she's learning Spanish.
</pre>

<pre>
強(つよ)い風(かぜ)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>波(なみ)も高(たか)く、フェリーは欠航(けっこう)になった。
On top of the strong wind the swell was high too, so the ferry was cancelled.
</pre>

<pre>
原材料(げんざいりょう)の高騰(こうとう)<b>に加(くわ)え</b>、人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)も深刻(しんこく)になっている。
On top of soaring raw-material prices, the labour shortage is becoming severe as well.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい体育館(たいいくかん)は床(ゆか)が広(ひろ)くなった<b>ことに加(くわ)えて</b>、照明(しょうめい)も明(あか)るくなった。
The new gym has a bigger floor, and on top of that the lighting is brighter.
</pre>

<pre>
この奨学金(しょうがくきん)は成績(せいせき)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、地域(ちいき)での活動(かつどう)も審査(しんさ)の対象(たいしょう)となります。
For this scholarship, community activity is assessed in addition to academic results.
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)では身分(みぶん)証明書(しょうめいしょ)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、招待(しょうたい)メールの画面(がめん)もご提示(ていじ)ください。
At reception, please show the invitation email on screen in addition to your ID.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から営業時間(えいぎょうじかん)が短(みじか)くなります。<b>加(くわ)えて</b>、日曜(にちよう)は終日(しゅうじつ)休業(きゅうぎょう)といたします。
Our opening hours are being cut from next month. In addition, we will close all day on Sundays.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is written Japanese. It belongs to news copy, notices, contracts, minutes,
academic prose and formal presentation, and it is what a company writes when it has
two things to tell you. Said to a friend in a café it lands like a press release;
there, それに or <code>その上(うえ)</code> is what you want.

The clipped form <code>に加(くわ)え</code>, without て, is one notch more formal
again, and is standard in headlines and in the middle of long written sentences.
Bare <code>加(くわ)えて、</code> at the head of a sentence is the same word doing
the job of "In addition," in an English paragraph.

Because the pattern is neutral, it is the safe choice when the two items are simply
facts - fees, requirements, causes, symptoms. It does not scold and it does not
enthuse. In practice the two items usually do point the same way, since people
rarely bother to add a good thing to a bad one, but that is a habit of use rather
than a rule the grammar enforces: <code>上(うえ)に</code> would actually be blocked
in a mixed sentence, and this pattern merely sounds odd.

One genuine convenience: unlike <code>上(うえ)に</code>, the second half may be a
request or an instruction. <code>印鑑(いんかん)に加(くわ)えて通帳(つうちょう)もお持(も)ちください</code>
is normal officialese.

## Contrast

### に加(くわ)えて vs 上(うえ)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に加(くわ)えて</code> follows a **noun** and reports;
<code>上(うえ)に</code> follows a **clause** and evaluates.

</div>

<pre>
運賃(うんちん)の値上(ねあ)げ<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、本数(ほんすう)の削減(さくげん)も発表(はっぴょう)された。
Along with the fare rise, a cut in the number of services was announced.
</pre>

<pre>
運賃(うんちん)が上(あ)がった<b>上(うえ)に</b>、本数(ほんすう)まで減(へ)らされた。
Fares went up, and on top of that they cut the number of services.
</pre>

The first is a transport authority's announcement; the second is a commuter's
complaint. Note the shape as well as the tone: the first hangs off the noun
<code>値上(ねあ)げ</code>, the second off the finished clause
<code>運賃(うんちん)が上(あ)がった</code>. Getting this backwards -
❌<code>上(あ)がるに加(くわ)えて</code> - is the single commonest error with this
pattern.

### に加(くわ)えて vs だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけでなく` **corrects an assumption** ("not only A").
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> makes no claim about what you assumed; it just adds.

</div>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は社員(しゃいん)<b>だけでなく</b>アルバイトにも適用(てきよう)される。
This scheme applies not just to permanent staff but to part-timers as well.
</pre>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は社員(しゃいん)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>アルバイトにも適用(てきよう)される。
This scheme applies to part-timers in addition to permanent staff.
</pre>

`だけでなく` says the reader probably thought the scheme stopped at permanent staff,
and pushes the boundary outwards. <code>に加(くわ)えて</code> simply lists two
covered groups. Where the point of the sentence is that the second item is
*surprising*, the additive connector wastes the effect.

### に加(くわ)えて vs のみならず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal, but <code>のみならず</code> carries the "not
merely" argument; <code>に加(くわ)えて</code> carries no argument at all.

</div>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の被害(ひがい)は農地(のうち)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>漁港(ぎょこう)にも及(およ)んだ。
The typhoon damage extended to the fishing port as well as to farmland.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の被害(ひがい)は農地(のうち)<b>のみならず</b>漁港(ぎょこう)にまで及(およ)んだ。
The typhoon damage reached not merely the farmland but even the fishing port.
</pre>

<code>のみならず</code> also attaches to whole clauses, which
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> cannot do without a nominaliser. If you find yourself
adding こと just to make the grammar fit, check whether
<code>のみならず</code> or <code>上(うえ)に</code> was the shape you actually wanted.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は安(やす)い<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>広(ひろ)い。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は安(やす)い<b>ことに加(くわ)えて</b>広(ひろ)い。（or 安(やす)い上(うえ)に広(ひろ)い）
  The pattern needs a noun in front of it. An adjective or verb has to be
  nominalised with こと first, and in speech <code>上(うえ)に</code> is simpler.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)<b>を加(くわ)えて</b>風(かぜ)も強(つよ)かった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>風(かぜ)も強(つよ)かった。
  <code>を加(くわ)える</code> is the live verb "add something". The connector is
  fixed with に and never varies.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)さ、残業(ざんぎょう)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>終電(しゅうでん)も逃(のが)したんだ。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)さ、残業(ざんぎょう)した<b>上(うえ)に</b>終電(しゅうでん)も逃(のが)したんだ。
  Not ungrammatical, just tone-deaf: a written connector in a grumble to a friend.
  Casual complaint takes <code>上(うえ)に</code>, その上(うえ) or おまけに.

## Practice

**1.** 入場料(にゅうじょうりょう)____、ガイドツアーの料金(りょうきん)も別(べつ)にかかります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

入場料(にゅうじょうりょう)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、ガイドツアーの料金(りょうきん)も別(べつ)にかかります。

<code>入場料(にゅうじょうりょう)</code> is a noun, so the pattern attaches directly,
and the notice register fits a fee announcement exactly. The も on the second charge
is what marks it as the added item.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 今日(きょう)は寝坊(ねぼう)した（上(うえ)に・に加(くわ)えて）鍵(かぎ)まで失(な)くした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>上(うえ)に</b>鍵(かぎ)まで失(な)くした。

Two reasons. Grammatically, <code>寝坊(ねぼう)した</code> is a clause, and
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> needs a noun. Tonally, this is a bad-day story, and the
report voice would be comic in the wrong way.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "In addition to the written test, an interview will be held."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

筆記試験(ひっきしけん)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、面接(めんせつ)も行(おこな)われます。

Both items are nouns and the sentence is an official announcement, which is this
pattern's home ground. <code>に加(くわ)え</code> without て would be equally correct
and a shade more formal.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 物価(ぶっか)が上(あ)がるに加(くわ)えて、給料(きゅうりょう)は下(さ)がっている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 物価(ぶっか)の上昇(じょうしょう)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、給料(きゅうりょう)も下(さ)がっている。

Two repairs. A plain verb cannot sit in front of the pattern, so the clause becomes
the noun <code>上昇(じょうしょう)</code>. And は in the second half fights the
additive sense - it sets up a contrast - so も is the particle you want.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会員(かいいん)証(しょう)に加(くわ)えて、保険証(ほけんしょう)もお持(も)ちください。
(b) 会員(かいいん)証(しょう)だけでなく、保険証(ほけんしょう)もお持(も)ちください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reads as a plain list of what to bring: two documents, stated neutrally. It is
what a clinic prints on a form.

(b) implies the reader may have been planning to bring only the membership card, and
corrects that. Spoken to someone at a counter it can sound faintly like a
reminder-with-eyebrows; on a printed notice it is fine, and useful precisely when
people do forget the second item.

</details>

# しかも

**Meaning** · moreover; and what's more; and on top of that

`しかも` adds a second point and flags it as the one worth noticing. Where
<code>その上(うえ)</code> merely stacks, `しかも` leans in: the speaker is presenting
the addition as the clincher, the fact that settles the matter one way or the other.

## Usage

`しかも` is a conjunction. Its two positions:

- **Between sentences**, at the head of the second one.
  席(せき)が広(ひろ)い。しかも安(やす)い。
- **Inside one sentence**, after a て-form or a comma-broken clause.
  席(せき)が広(ひろ)くて、しかも安(やす)い。

Nothing attaches to it and it never changes shape. It is written in kana; the kanji
spelling exists but is not used in modern text.

The same-direction rule that governs <code>その上(うえ)</code> applies here too:
two good points or two bad points, never one of each. What `しかも` adds on top is
**emphasis** - the second fact is offered as surprising, impressive or damning, not
merely present. That is why it is the connector of advertising copy and of
indignation, and why a flat, unremarkable second fact makes it fall oddly:
❌<code>木曜日(もくようび)です。しかも明日(あした)は金曜日(きんようび)です</code>
adds nothing anyone would raise an eyebrow at.

A very common spoken habit is to front-load the sentence with it and let the
listener wait: 「しかもさ、値段(ねだん)同(おな)じなんだよ」.

## Examples

<pre>
この傘(かさ)は軽(かる)い。<b>しかも</b>丈夫(じょうぶ)だ。
This umbrella is light. And it's tough as well.
</pre>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)した。<b>しかも</b>財布(さいふ)を家(いえ)に忘(わす)れてきた。
I missed the last train. And what's more, I'd left my wallet at home.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は三(みっ)つの言語(げんご)を話(はな)す。<b>しかも</b>どれも発音(はつおん)がきれいだ。
He speaks three languages - and his accent is good in all of them.
</pre>

<pre>
この席(せき)は舞台(ぶたい)が正面(しょうめん)に見(み)えて、<b>しかも</b>通路(つうろ)に近(ちか)いので出(で)入(い)りが楽(らく)だ。
This seat looks straight at the stage, and on top of that it's near the aisle, so getting in and out is easy.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and extremely frequent. It works in a sales pitch, a complaint to a
call centre, a newspaper column and a conversation between teenagers, with no change
of form. What varies is only how hard you hit it: drawn out - しかもぉ - it is pure
gossip; clipped in a written sentence it is simply "moreover".

Two habitats where it dominates. **Advertising**, because the whole grammar of a
sales point is "and there's more":
<code>送料(そうりょう)無料(むりょう)、しかも翌日(よくじつ)お届(とど)け</code>.
And **grievance**, because the same shape works for piling on:
<code>一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)たされて、しかも謝罪(しゃざい)もなかった</code>.

Do not use it for "but". English speakers reach for "moreover" and "however" from
the same shelf of connectors, and `しかも` only does the first. If the second clause
pulls against the first, you want しかし, が or ただ.

<div class="note">

**Note** - in formal written Japanese you will occasionally meet `しかも` joining two
statements that sit in tension - "and yet" rather than "and". Recognise it there and
read on; it is not a use to imitate, and in speech it will be heard as ordinary
addition.

</div>

## Contrast

### しかも vs その上(うえ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>その上(うえ)</code> stacks; `しかも` stacks **and points
at what it just stacked**.

</div>

<pre>
この宿(やど)は駅(えき)から近(ちか)い。<b>その上(うえ)</b>朝食(ちょうしょく)もつく。
This guesthouse is near the station. Breakfast is included as well.
</pre>

<pre>
この宿(やど)は駅(えき)から近(ちか)い。<b>しかも</b>朝食(ちょうしょく)までつく。
This guesthouse is near the station - and they even throw in breakfast.
</pre>

Both are correct and a native speaker would not blink at either. The difference is
that the second sentence expects a reaction. <code>その上(うえ)</code> is the
connector of a balanced list; `しかも` is the connector of an argument you are
winning. `しかも` is also the more comfortable of the two in fast casual speech,
where <code>その上(うえ)</code> can sound slightly written.

### しかも vs おまけに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `おまけに` leans **negative and casual** by default. `しかも`
leans neither way; it takes its colour from the sentence.

</div>

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)して、<b>おまけに</b>バスにも乗(の)り遅(おく)れた。
I overslept, and to cap it all I missed the bus too.
</pre>

<pre>
新型(しんがた)は静(しず)かで、<b>しかも</b>電気代(でんきだい)が安(やす)い。
The new model is quiet, and cheaper to run as well.
</pre>

Swapping them shows the limit: `おまけに` in the second sentence would sound like a
throwaway freebie rather than a selling point, and while `しかも` is perfectly
possible in the first, it makes the complaint sound a shade more argued and less
rueful.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い。しかも席(せき)が少(すく)ない。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い。<b>ただ</b>、席(せき)が少(すく)ない。
  A plus followed by a minus is a turn, not an addition. `しかも` cannot mark a
  change of direction; ただ, が or しかし can.

- ❌ この鍋(なべ)は軽(かる)いしかも洗(あら)いやすい。 → ✅ この鍋(なべ)は軽(かる)くて、<b>しかも</b>洗(あら)いやすい。
  It joins clauses, it does not glue words. Close the first clause with a て-form or
  a full stop first.

- ❌ 説明(せつめい)は長(なが)かった。しかも、結論(けつろん)はよく分(わ)からなかった。 → ✅ 説明(せつめい)は長(なが)かった。<b>その上(うえ)</b>、結論(けつろん)もよく分(わ)からなかった。
  Not wrong so much as unmarked: if you do use `しかも` here, the second half needs
  も or まで to show it is being piled on, or the emphasis has nothing to land on.

## Practice

**1.** このイヤホンは音(おと)がいい。____、一回(いっかい)の充電(じゅうでん)で三十時間(さんじゅうじかん)も使(つか)える。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>しかも</b>、一回(いっかい)の充電(じゅうでん)で三十時間(さんじゅうじかん)も使(つか)える。

Two selling points, and the second is the impressive one - the も on
<code>三十時間(さんじゅうじかん)</code> makes that explicit. That is exactly the shape
`しかも` wants. <code>その上(うえ)</code> would also be grammatical, just less
enthusiastic.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いしかも家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)くて、<b>しかも</b>家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い。（or 広(ひろ)い。しかも家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い。）

`しかも` needs a completed clause in front of it. The い-adjective has to be put into
its て-form, or the sentence has to be split in two.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は遅(おく)れてきた。しかも謝(あやま)らなかった。
(b) 彼(かれ)は遅(おく)れてきたが、謝(あやま)らなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) piles the second fact onto the first as further evidence for the same verdict -
he behaved badly, and here is the worse half of it. The speaker is building a case.

(b) sets the two facts against each other, so it reads as "he turned up late, yet
offered no apology" - the emphasis falls on the mismatch rather than on the total.
Both are natural; (a) is what you say when you are annoyed and listing, (b) when you
are noting an inconsistency.

</details>

# おまけに

**Meaning** · on top of that; and as if that weren't enough - usually said about
something unwelcome

`おまけに` is the noun おまけ, the little freebie a shop throws in with your purchase,
turned into a connector. That origin explains its whole personality: the second fact
arrives *unasked for*, and nine times out of ten the speaker is not pleased about it.

## Usage

`おまけに` is a conjunction. It sits at the head of the second sentence, or inside one
sentence after a て-form:

- 寝坊(ねぼう)した。おまけに電車(でんしゃ)も遅(おく)れた。
- 寝坊(ねぼう)して、おまけに電車(でんしゃ)も遅(おく)れた。

Nothing attaches to it, and it is always written in kana. Like the other additive
connectors it obeys the same-direction rule: two complaints stack, two pieces of
good luck stack, but a plus and a minus do not.

What sets it apart is that the direction is **pre-set**. Hearing おまけに, a listener
braces for more bad news, and the added item is felt as one thing too many. The
literal "free extra" reading survives - a restaurant that throws in a coffee, a
hotel that upgrades your room - and there the word is cheerful. But if you say
nothing to steer it, grumbling is where it lands.

Because it is a piece of conversational Japanese, it does not go into a report, a
notice or a formal email. There the neutral <code>その上(うえ)</code> or the written
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> takes over.

## Examples

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)をひいて、<b>おまけに</b>熱(ねつ)まで出(で)てきた。
I've caught a cold, and now I've got a temperature on top of it.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は残業(ざんぎょう)で、<b>おまけに</b>夕飯(ゆうはん)も食(た)べそこねた。
I was working late yesterday, and on top of that I missed dinner.
</pre>

<pre>
この定食(ていしょく)は量(りょう)が多(おお)くて、<b>おまけに</b>食後(しょくご)のコーヒーまでついてくる。
This set meal is generous, and they throw in a coffee afterwards as well.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの日(ひ)にエレベーターが故障(こしょう)して、<b>おまけに</b>業者(ぎょうしゃ)が一時間(いちじかん)も遅(おく)れてきた。
The lift broke down on moving day, and to cap it all the removal firm turned up an hour late.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Colloquial. It belongs to speech, to chat messages, to a blog post about a bad day -
anywhere you would say "and to cap it all" in English. In a written report it sounds
like someone rolling their eyes in the middle of a set of figures.

The tone is rueful rather than angry. A speaker using `おまけに` is usually telling
the story afterwards, half laughing at their own run of luck, and the connector
carries that shrug. For genuine indignation - the tone of a complaint being lodged -
しかも hits harder, because it presents the extra fact as evidence rather than as
another indignity.

The second half is normally marked with も or まで, and まで is especially at home
here: it is the particle of "even that". Because the pattern is a running commentary
on what has happened to you, it does not lead into a request, an invitation or an
order. <code>おまけに窓(まど)も閉(し)めてください</code> is not how anyone asks for a
second favour.

<div class="note">

**Note** - おまけ on its own is a live noun and worth knowing:
<code>おまけに一(ひと)つ付(つ)けておきますね</code> from a shopkeeper means "I'll put
in an extra one for you" - the particle に is doing ordinary work there, not
connecting sentences. Context separates them easily; the connector always begins a
clause.

</div>

## Contrast

### おまけに vs その上(うえ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same slot, different clothes. <code>その上(うえ)</code> is
neutral and writable; `おまけに` is spoken and comes with a grimace.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)が変更(へんこう)になり、<b>その上(うえ)</b>開始(かいし)時刻(じこく)も早(はや)まった。
The venue was changed, and the start time was brought forward as well.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)が変(か)わってさ、<b>おまけに</b>時間(じかん)まで早(はや)くなったんだよ。
They moved the venue - and then they went and made it start earlier, too.
</pre>

The facts are identical; only the speaker's posture differs. Put `おまけに` into the
first sentence and the announcement starts editorialising; put
<code>その上(うえ)</code> into the second and the friend you are moaning to will
wonder why you have gone formal.

### おまけに vs さらに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さらに` pushes **further along the same scale**; `おまけに` tacks
on a **different item** altogether.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が上(あ)がり、来月(らいげつ)からは<b>さらに</b>上(あ)がるらしい。
Prices have gone up, and apparently they'll rise further from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が上(あ)がって、<b>おまけに</b>量(りょう)まで減(へ)った。
The price went up, and on top of that they cut the portion size.
</pre>

`さらに` needs something measurable to move along - more expensive, later, worse.
`おまけに` needs no scale at all: the price and the portion are unrelated, and the
only thing joining them is that both happened to you.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 上半期(かみはんき)は売上(うりあげ)が減少(げんしょう)し、おまけに経費(けいひ)も増加(ぞうか)いたしました。 → ✅ …売上(うりあげ)が減少(げんしょう)し、<b>加(くわ)えて</b>経費(けいひ)も増加(ぞうか)いたしました。
  A conversational grumble inside a business report. Formal writing takes
  <code>加(くわ)えて</code> or <code>その上(うえ)</code>.

- ❌ コーヒーおまけにケーキを注文(ちゅうもん)した。 → ✅ コーヒー<b>と</b>ケーキを注文(ちゅうもん)した。
  It joins clauses, never nouns. Two things on an order take と, や or か.

- ❌ この辞書(じしょ)は説明(せつめい)が詳(くわ)しくて、おまけに高(たか)い。 → ✅ この辞書(じしょ)は説明(せつめい)が詳(くわ)しい<b>が</b>、高(たか)い。
  A good point followed by a bad one is a turn, not an accumulation. Nothing in the
  additive family will carry it; you need が or ただ.

## Practice

**1.** 財布(さいふ)を落(お)として、____携帯(けいたい)の電池(でんち)まで切(き)れてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

財布(さいふ)を落(お)として、<b>おまけに</b>携帯(けいたい)の電池(でんち)まで切(き)れてしまった。

Two misfortunes in a row, told casually, with まで marking the second as the last
straw. That is `おまけに` in its home habitat. しかも would also work and would sound
a little more indignant.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しい自転車(じてんしゃ)は軽(かる)くて、おまけに値段(ねだん)が高(たか)かった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しい自転車(じてんしゃ)は軽(かる)かった<b>が</b>、値段(ねだん)が高(たか)かった。

Light is a plus and expensive is a minus, so the sentence changes direction and the
additive connector is blocked. If you did want a complaint on both sides:
<code>重(おも)くて、おまけに高(たか)かった</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 工事(こうじ)が延(の)びて、おまけに費用(ひよう)も増(ふ)えた。
(b) 工事(こうじ)が延(の)びた上(うえ)に、費用(ひよう)も増(ふ)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say the same two things about the same job. (a) is spoken: a shrug, told to
someone over a drink, with the cost overrun as one more thing to put up with.

(b) is tighter and more neutral, and would be the natural shape in writing - a
message to the client, a note in a diary. Neither is more correct; (a) reports how
you feel about it, (b) reports what happened.

</details>

# さらに

**Meaning** · further; even more; another (amount) - and, as a sentence opener,
furthermore

`さらに` always presupposes a level that has already been reached, and moves past it.
Something is cold; `さらに` makes it colder. A job needs three days; `さらに` adds
three more. A paragraph has made one point; `さらに` starts the next one.

## Usage

`さらに` is an adverb, so it goes in front of what it modifies and never changes
form. It is written both さらに and 更(さら)に; kana is the safer default outside formal
prose. Three shapes cover almost everything:

- **Before a verb or adjective** - degree. 風(かぜ)がさらに強(つよ)くなる。
- **Before a quantity** - "another N on top". さらに三日(みっか)かかる。
- **At the head of a sentence** - "furthermore". さらに、参加費(さんかひ)も必要(ひつよう)です。

The one condition worth memorising is the baseline. `さらに` is not a general-purpose
"more"; it says the scale was already up there and is now going higher. On the first
cold morning of the year <code>今日(きょう)は寒(さむ)い</code> is what you say;
`さらに` only becomes available once yesterday was cold too.

Two related forms you will meet in writing. The prenominal <code>さらなる</code>
modifies a noun directly - <code>さらなる改善(かいぜん)</code>, "further
improvement" - and belongs to formal announcements. And <code>さらに一歩(いっぽ)</code>,
"one step further", is close to a set phrase in speeches and editorials.

## Examples

<pre>
風(かぜ)が<b>さらに</b>強(つよ)くなってきた。
The wind has got stronger still.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)には<b>さらに</b>一週間(いっしゅうかん)かかるそうだ。
Apparently the work will take another week.
</pre>

<pre>
結果(けっか)は来週(らいしゅう)発表(はっぴょう)されます。<b>さらに</b>、希望者(きぼうしゃ)には個別(こべつ)の面談(めんだん)も行(おこな)います。
The results will be announced next week. In addition, individual interviews will be held for those who want one.
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)を進(すす)めた結果(けっか)、汚染(おせん)が<b>さらに</b>広(ひろ)い範囲(はんい)に及(およ)んでいることが分(わ)かった。
As the survey progressed it became clear that the contamination extended over a wider area still.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral to formal, and noticeably more written than spoken. Weather forecasts,
company announcements, news copy and academic prose are full of it; friends
arranging a weekend are not. In casual speech the degree sense usually comes out as
もっと and the additive sense as それに.

That does not make it stiff in the way <code>のみならず</code> is stiff. `さらに` is
perfectly sayable - <code>さらに三十分(さんじゅっぷん)待(ま)たされた</code> is
ordinary spoken Japanese - it simply sounds measured, which is why an announcer
reaches for it and a teenager does not.

As a connective it behaves like a numbered list rather than an argument. It says
"here is the next item", not "and this one is the killer". Where the second point is
meant to impress or to sting, しかも and <code>その上(うえ)</code> carry that
weight; `さらに` deliberately does not, and in an official notice that neutrality is
the point.

A small trap in the degree use: `さらに` measures the *increase*, not the total.
<code>さらに二日(ふつか)かかります</code> means two days beyond what was already
expected - not that the job takes two days.

## Contrast

### さらに vs もっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もっと` is the everyday comparative and can be a demand.
`さらに` reports a further step from a level already established.

</div>

<pre>
すみません、<b>もっと</b>ゆっくり話(はな)していただけますか。
Sorry - could you speak more slowly?
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いて、<b>さらに</b>分(わ)からなくなった。
After hearing the explanation I was more confused than ever.
</pre>

`もっと` looks at what the speaker wants and asks for a change; it is the natural
word in requests and complaints, and it is casual. `さらに` looks at a scale from the
outside and notes that it has moved. Ask a stranger
❌<code>さらにゆっくり話(はな)してください</code> and you will be understood, but it
sounds like a technician adjusting a dial.

### さらに vs その上(うえ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さらに` adds the **next item**; <code>その上(うえ)</code> adds
**weight** to a case being made.

</div>

<pre>
参加費(さんかひ)は三千円(さんぜんえん)です。<b>さらに</b>、昼食代(ちゅうしょくだい)が別途(べっと)かかります。
The participation fee is 3,000 yen. There is also a separate charge for lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は待(ま)たされた。<b>その上(うえ)</b>、注文(ちゅうもん)を間違(まちが)えられた。
They kept us waiting at that place. And on top of that they got the order wrong.
</pre>

The first is a form telling you what you will pay; the second is a person deciding
never to go back. Swap them and the notice sounds resentful while the complaint
sounds like a price list. `さらに` is also the only one of the two that can attach
directly to an adjective or a number.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 初雪(はつゆき)だ。今日(きょう)はさらに寒(さむ)い。 → ✅ 初雪(はつゆき)だ。今日(きょう)は本当(ほんとう)に寒(さむ)い。
  Nothing has been established to go beyond. `さらに` needs a previous level in the
  conversation or in the situation.

- ❌ パンさらに牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を買(か)ってきて。 → ✅ パン<b>と</b>牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)を買(か)ってきて。
  It is an adverb, not a conjunction between nouns. Two shopping items take と.

- ❌ ねえ、さらに大(おお)きい声(こえ)で言(い)ってよ。 → ✅ ねえ、<b>もっと</b>大(おお)きい声(こえ)で言(い)ってよ。
  Asking a friend for something is `もっと` territory. `さらに` in a casual request
  sounds oddly clinical.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいており、雨(あめ)は夜(よる)にかけて____強(つよ)くなる見込(みこ)みです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夜(よる)にかけて<b>さらに</b>強(つよ)くなる見込(みこ)みです。

It is already raining, so there is a baseline for the rain to exceed, and the
register - a forecast - is exactly where this word lives. もっと would sound too
conversational for a broadcast.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に始(はじ)まります。さらに資料(しりょう)を持(も)ってきてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に始(はじ)まります。<b>なお</b>、資料(しりょう)をお持(も)ちください。（or …資料(しりょう)も忘(わす)れずに）

The problem is the missing link. `さらに` introduces a further item of the *same*
kind, and a request is not another fact about the start time. なお is the connector
for tacking a separate instruction onto an announcement.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 来月(らいげつ)から料金(りょうきん)がさらに上(あ)がります。
(b) 来月(らいげつ)から料金(りょうきん)がもっと上(あ)がります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is what a company writes on a notice: prices have already risen once, and there
is another rise coming. The word points back at that earlier increase.

(b) sounds like the speaker grumbling about it. `もっと` carries no implication that
a first rise has happened - it simply compares with now - and its casual colouring
makes the sentence read as a personal reaction rather than an announcement.

</details>

# ～のみならず

**Meaning** · not only ... but also - the formal written counterpart of だけでなく

<code>のみ</code> is the literary word for だけ, and <code>ならず</code> is an old
negative form of the copula. Put together they say "not being merely" - the first
item is true, and the sentence goes on to say the case is wider than that. The
meaning is nothing new; what this pattern adds over `だけでなく` is **register**. It
is the shape the same thought takes in an editorial, a report or a speech.

## Formation

<code>のみ</code> attaches exactly where だけ does, so the forms are easy once you see
the parallel. The one thing to watch is nouns and な-adjectives, which need
<code>である</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + のみならず | 国内(こくない) → 国内(こくない)のみならず |
| Noun + particle | Noun + に／で + のみならず | 都市部(としぶ)においてのみならず |
| Verb | plain form + のみならず | 下(さ)げる → 下(さ)げるのみならず |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のみならず | 認(みと)めた → 認(みと)めたのみならず |
| い-adjective | plain form + のみならず | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いのみならず |
| な-adjective | ～である + のみならず | 複雑(ふくざつ)である → 複雑(ふくざつ)であるのみならず |
| Noun (predicative) | ～である + のみならず | 問題(もんだい)であるのみならず |
| Second clause | …も／まで／さえ + predicate | …海外(かいがい)<b>でも</b>評価(ひょうか)された |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the second clause must mark its element with も, まで or さえ. This is not
optional politeness: <code>のみならず</code> only says "the case does not stop here",
and without も the sentence never says where it goes instead.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この薬(くすり)は大人(おとな)<b>のみならず</b>子(こ)どもにも使(つか)える。
This medicine can be used not only by adults but by children as well.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の作品(さくひん)は国内(こくない)<b>のみならず</b>海外(かいがい)でも高(たか)く評価(ひょうか)されている。
His work is highly regarded not only at home but abroad as well.
</pre>

<pre>
地震(じしん)は建物(たてもの)<b>のみならず</b>、水道管(すいどうかん)にも被害(ひがい)を及(およ)ぼした。
The earthquake damaged not only buildings but the water mains as well.
</pre>

<pre>
この制度(せいど)は手続(てつづ)きが複雑(ふくざつ)である<b>のみならず</b>、費用(ひよう)も高額(こうがく)である。
This scheme is not only complicated to apply for; the fees are high as well.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は研究(けんきゅう)を続(つづ)けている<b>のみならず</b>、後進(こうしん)の指導(しどう)にも力(ちから)を注(そそ)いでいる。
She has not only carried on her research but has put real effort into training those coming after her.
</pre>

<pre>
その技術(ぎじゅつ)は製造(せいぞう)コストを下(さ)げる<b>のみならず</b>、廃棄物(はいきぶつ)の量(りょう)まで減(へ)らすという。
The technology is said not merely to cut production costs but even to reduce the volume of waste.
</pre>

<pre>
ひとり一社(いっしゃ)<b>のみならず</b>、業界(ぎょうかい)全体(ぜんたい)が同(おな)じ課題(かだい)を抱(かか)えている。
It is not one company alone; the whole industry faces the same problem.
</pre>

<pre>
この条例(じょうれい)は市内(しない)の住民(じゅうみん)<b>のみならず</b>、市外(しがい)から通勤(つうきん)する人(ひと)にも適用(てきよう)される。
This ordinance applies not only to residents of the city but to people commuting in from outside it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Stiff, and deliberately so. <code>のみならず</code> belongs to written Japanese:
editorials, official reports, academic articles, formal speeches, and the kind of
company announcement that is read rather than said. A learner who deploys it over
coffee will sound like a leader column being recited aloud.

The stiffness is historical. <code>ならず</code> is the negative of the classical
copula <code>なり</code>, a form that has otherwise vanished from the modern language
and survives only in fossils like this one. That is why the pattern feels older than
its meaning warrants, and why nothing about it contracts or softens - there is no
casual variant, and no ます version. When you need the same idea in speech, you drop
back to <code>だけでなく</code> or <code>だけじゃなくて</code>.

<code>のみ</code> itself is worth recognising as a live word in the same register:
<code>会員(かいいん)のみ入場(にゅうじょう)できます</code> on a sign, or
<code>残(のこ)るのは記録(きろく)のみだ</code> in a piece of writing that wants
weight. If you can read だけ into it, you have understood it.

Something the pattern does **not** carry: surprise. It states that the scope is
wider, and it makes no claim that the wider case is remarkable. When the second item
is meant to astonish, ばかりか is the pattern that supplies that colouring;
<code>のみならず</code> stays flat on purpose, which is exactly what a report wants.

The intensified <code>ひとり…のみならず</code>, where ひとり means "merely" rather than
"one person", is a set literary shape meaning "not merely X". It is common in
speeches and worth recognising, though it is not something to attempt casually.

## Contrast

### のみならず vs だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different register.
<code>だけでなく</code> is neutral and speakable; <code>のみならず</code> is written and
formal.

</div>

<pre>
この公園(こうえん)は地元(じもと)の人(ひと)<b>だけでなく</b>観光客(かんこうきゃく)にも人気(にんき)がある。
This park is popular not just with locals but with tourists too.
</pre>

<pre>
当(とう)公園(こうえん)は地元(じもと)住民(じゅうみん)<b>のみならず</b>観光客(かんこうきゃく)からも支持(しじ)を得(え)ている。
The park enjoys support not only from local residents but from visitors as well.
</pre>

Look at what moved with the connector. <code>地元(じもと)の人(ひと)</code> became
<code>地元(じもと)住民(じゅうみん)</code>, <code>人気(にんき)がある</code> became
<code>支持(しじ)を得(え)ている</code>. That is the honest lesson of this page: a
formal connector wants formal vocabulary around it. Dropping
<code>のみならず</code> into an otherwise casual sentence is the commonest way learners
misuse it.

`だけでなく` is also the flexible one. It attaches to a noun with no である, it takes
a following request comfortably, and it survives in the contracted spoken form
<code>だけじゃなくて</code>. <code>のみならず</code> does none of that.

### のみならず vs ばかりか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のみならず</code> reports a wider scope.
<code>ばかりか</code> reports a wider scope **and is impressed or appalled by it**.

</div>

<pre>
欠航(けっこう)は国際線(こくさいせん)<b>のみならず</b>国内線(こくないせん)にも及(およ)んだ。
The cancellations extended to domestic flights as well as international ones.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は遅(おく)れてきた<b>ばかりか</b>、資料(しりょう)さえ持(も)ってこなかった。
Not only did he turn up late - he didn't even bring the handouts.
</pre>

The first sentence could be read out on the news. The second could not, because it
has an attitude. <code>ばかりか</code> also lives comfortably in speech, where
<code>のみならず</code> does not.

### のみならず vs はもとより

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のみならず</code> treats the two items as **equally worth
saying**. <code>はもとより</code> declares the first one **too obvious to need
saying**.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は学生(がくせい)<b>のみならず</b>一般(いっぱん)の方(かた)にも開放(かいほう)されています。
The venue is open not only to students but to members of the public as well.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は学生(がくせい)<b>はもとより</b>、一般(いっぱん)の方(かた)にも開放(かいほう)されています。
The venue is open to the public, students needless to say.
</pre>

Both are formal and both are correct here. The second says that student access goes
without saying, and that the news is the public access. Where the first item is not
in fact self-evident, <code>はもとより</code> will puzzle the reader and
<code>のみならず</code> is the safe choice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この店(みせ)、コーヒーのみならずケーキもおいしいよ。 → ✅ この店(みせ)、コーヒー<b>だけじゃなくて</b>ケーキもおいしいよ。
  Grammatically flawless and socially wrong. Casual sentence endings - よ, ね, plain
  adjectives - cannot carry a written connector.

- ❌ この機械(きかい)は便利(べんり)<b>のみならず</b>安全(あんぜん)でもある。 → ✅ この機械(きかい)は便利(べんり)<b>であるのみならず</b>安全(あんぜん)でもある。
  な-adjectives and nouns need である in front of <code>のみ</code>. Verbs and
  い-adjectives attach in their plain form with nothing added.

- ❌ この制度(せいど)は正社員(せいしゃいん)のみならず契約(けいやく)社員(しゃいん)が対象(たいしょう)となる。 → ✅ …契約(けいやく)社員(しゃいん)<b>も</b>対象(たいしょう)となる。
  Without も the sentence sounds as though contract staff had *replaced* permanent
  staff. The second half has to be marked as an addition.

- ❌ 会員(かいいん)だけ<b>のみならず</b>… → ✅ 会員(かいいん)<b>のみならず</b>…
  <code>のみ</code> *is* だけ. Using both stacks the same word twice.

## Practice

**1.** この問題(もんだい)は一部(いちぶ)の地域(ちいき)____、全国(ぜんこく)で報告(ほうこく)されている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一部(いちぶ)の地域(ちいき)<b>のみならず</b>、全国(ぜんこく)で報告(ほうこく)されている。

A noun attaches directly, and the report register fits. Note that
<code>全国(ぜんこく)で</code> already carries the "wider case" sense, so the sentence
does not need a further も - but adding one
（全国(ぜんこく)でも）would be equally natural.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （のみならず・だけじゃなくて）を使(つか)って、友(とも)だちに:「あの店(みせ)、安(やす)い____おいしいんだよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの店(みせ)、安(やす)い<b>だけじゃなくて</b>おいしいんだよ。

The sentence has よ, a contracted んだ and a plain adjective - it is speech. The
written connector would clash with every other word in it. Register decides this,
not grammar.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese (formal written style): "The new law affects not only businesses but individual households as well."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しい法律(ほうりつ)は企業(きぎょう)<b>のみならず</b>、個人(こじん)の家庭(かてい)にも影響(えいきょう)を与(あた)える。

Two nouns, a report subject and a formal instruction, so the pattern fits. The に and
も on the second item are what complete the "but also" half.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この地域(ちいき)は交通(こうつう)が不便(ふべん)のみならず、病院(びょういん)も少(すく)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この地域(ちいき)は交通(こうつう)が不便(ふべん)<b>であるのみならず</b>、病院(びょういん)も少(すく)ない。

<code>不便(ふべん)</code> is a な-adjective, and a な-adjective cannot sit bare in
front of <code>のみ</code>. である supplies the missing predicate. In speech you would
sidestep the whole issue with
<code>不便(ふべん)な上(うえ)に</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この技術(ぎじゅつ)は医療(いりょう)のみならず農業(のうぎょう)にも使(つか)われている。
(b) この技術(ぎじゅつ)は医療(いりょう)ばかりか農業(のうぎょう)にまで使(つか)われている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral statement of range, of the kind you would find in a technical
overview: two fields, both covered, no comment.

(b) adds the writer's reaction. まで and ばかりか together say that agriculture is
further than you would have expected the technology to reach, and the sentence
invites the reader to be impressed. Choose (a) when you are describing, (b) when you
are making a point.

</details>

# ～はもとより

**Meaning** · not to mention; needless to say; let alone - the first item goes
without saying, and the second one is the news

<code>もとより</code> means "from the outset, as a matter of course". The pattern uses
that to rank two items: what stands in front of it is treated as **already obvious**,
and the sentence spends its energy on what follows. It is the formal written cousin
of <code>はもちろん</code>.

## Formation

It attaches to a noun. A clause has to be nominalised first, which is one reason the
pattern is far commoner with nouns than with anything else.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + はもとより | 英語(えいご) → 英語(えいご)はもとより |
| Noun + particle | Noun + で／に + はもとより | 国内(こくない)ではもとより |
| Verb (nominalised) | plain form + の／こと + はもとより | 読(よ)める → 読(よ)めるのはもとより |
| い-adjective (nominalised) | ～こと + はもとより | 安(やす)いことはもとより |
| な-adjective (nominalised) | ～なこと + はもとより | 静(しず)かなことはもとより |
| Second item | Noun + も／まで／さえ + predicate | …中国語(ちゅうごくご)<b>も</b>話(はな)せる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the は is part of the pattern and does not change. A noun that would
normally take を or が loses that particle here, exactly as it does before an ordinary
topic は: ❌<code>漢字(かんじ)をもとより</code> → ✅<code>漢字(かんじ)はもとより</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は英語(えいご)<b>はもとより</b>、中国語(ちゅうごくご)も話(はな)せる。
He speaks Chinese as well as English, needless to say.
</pre>

<pre>
この港(みなと)は漁業(ぎょぎょう)<b>はもとより</b>、観光(かんこう)でも知(し)られている。
This port is known for tourism as well as, of course, for fishing.
</pre>

<pre>
入学式(にゅうがくしき)には保護者(ほごしゃ)<b>はもとより</b>、近所(きんじょ)の方(かた)まで集(あつ)まった。
Parents turned out for the entrance ceremony as you would expect - and even the neighbours came.
</pre>

<pre>
山小屋(やまごや)には電気(でんき)<b>はもとより</b>、水道(すいどう)さえ通(とお)っていない。
The mountain hut has no electricity, and not even running water.
</pre>

<pre>
この資格(しかく)は建築(けんちく)業界(ぎょうかい)<b>はもとより</b>、不動産(ふどうさん)業界(ぎょうかい)でも評価(ひょうか)される。
This qualification counts for something in the property business as well as, obviously, in construction.
</pre>

<pre>
当社(とうしゃ)では安全性(あんぜんせい)<b>はもとより</b>、環境(かんきょう)への配慮(はいりょ)も重視(じゅうし)しております。
Safety goes without saying at this company, and we place equal weight on the environment.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)める<b>のはもとより</b>、手書(てが)きの文字(もじ)まで判別(はんべつ)できるという。
It can read printed characters as a matter of course, and apparently even handwriting.
</pre>

<pre>
震災(しんさい)の記録(きろく)は、当時(とうじ)を知(し)る人(ひと)<b>はもとより</b>、まだ生(う)まれていなかった世代(せだい)にも読(よ)み継(つ)がれている。
The records of the disaster are read by those who lived through it, of course, and also by a generation that had not yet been born.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and written. Company statements, tourist brochures, prospectuses, newspaper
features and prepared speeches are where it lives. In ordinary conversation the same
sentence takes <code>はもちろん</code>, which means the same thing and costs nothing
in formality.

The pattern makes a claim, and it is worth being deliberate about it: **the first
item is uncontroversial.** Saying <code>英語(えいご)はもとより</code> asserts that
this person speaking English is not news. If the listener would in fact be surprised
by it, the sentence misfires - it sounds as if you are pretending everyone already
knew. When neither item is obvious, <code>のみならず</code> or
<code>だけでなく</code> is the honest choice.

In a negative sentence the same ranking comes out as English "let alone". The mountain
hut example above works because both facts are absences, and さえ marks the second as
the more striking one. This is a common shape in writing about hardship or shortage.

The second item is normally marked with も, まで or さえ - a plain が or を there leaves
the sentence sounding truncated. Note also that this pattern describes; it does not
introduce a request. A notice asking you to bring two documents wants
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code>, not this.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>もとより</code> without は is a separate adverb meaning "from the
start, all along": <code>危険(きけん)はもとより承知(しょうち)の上(うえ)だった</code>,
"I knew the risk from the outset". Same word, different job; the connector always
follows a noun with は.

</div>

## Contrast

### はもとより vs はもちろん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different room. <code>はもちろん</code> is
everyday; <code>はもとより</code> is written and formal.

</div>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は昼(ひる)<b>はもちろん</b>、夜(よる)も混(こ)んでるよ。
That place is packed in the evening as well as at lunchtime, obviously.
</pre>

<pre>
当(とう)施設(しせつ)は平日(へいじつ)<b>はもとより</b>、休日(きゅうじつ)も多(おお)くの方(かた)にご利用(りよう)いただいております。
The facility is used by large numbers of people at weekends as well as, naturally, on weekdays.
</pre>

There is no difference in what the two sentences claim. The difference is who is
speaking: a friend, and an organisation. <code>はもちろん</code> is also the one that
can be softened, doubled or thrown into the middle of a sentence in speech;
<code>はもとより</code> is fixed.

### はもとより vs のみならず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal, but <code>はもとより</code> **ranks** the two
items, while <code>のみならず</code> merely **adds** the second one.

</div>

<pre>
市(し)の補助(ほじょ)は高齢者(こうれいしゃ)<b>はもとより</b>、子育(こそだ)て世帯(せたい)にも広(ひろ)がった。
The city's support has been extended to households with children, as well as to the elderly of course.
</pre>

<pre>
市(し)の補助(ほじょ)は高齢者(こうれいしゃ)<b>のみならず</b>、子育(こそだ)て世帯(せたい)にも広(ひろ)がった。
The city's support has been extended not only to the elderly but to households with children.
</pre>

The first assumes the reader already takes support for the elderly for granted; the
second makes no assumption at all and simply reports both. If you are not certain
your reader shares the assumption, <code>のみならず</code> is safer - a wrong
"obviously" is a small rudeness.

### はもとより vs もとより

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with は it is a **connector** between two items. Without は it is
an **adverb** meaning "from the beginning".

</div>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)される<b>ことはもとより</b>、批判(ひはん)まで覚悟(かくご)していた。
He was prepared for opposition as a matter of course, and for criticism as well.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)されることは<b>もとより</b>覚悟(かくご)していた。
He had been prepared for opposition from the start.
</pre>

They look almost identical on the page. The test is whether a second item follows:
the connector always has one, marked with も, まで or さえ. The adverb has none, and
modifies the verb directly.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 漢字(かんじ)<b>をもとより</b>、敬語(けいご)も難(むずか)しい。 → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)<b>はもとより</b>、敬語(けいご)も難(むずか)しい。
  The は is part of the pattern, and it displaces を or が the way a topic marker
  always does.

- ❌ 野菜(やさい)はもとより、果物(くだもの)が値上(ねあ)がりしている。 → ✅ 野菜(やさい)はもとより、果物(くだもの)<b>も</b>値上(ねあ)がりしている。
  Without も the second half reads as a replacement rather than an addition, and the
  sentence loses its point.

- ❌ この映画(えいが)、音楽(おんがく)はもとより映像(えいぞう)もすごいんだよ。 → ✅ この映画(えいが)、音楽(おんがく)<b>はもちろん</b>映像(えいぞう)もすごいんだよ。
  A written connector in a sentence that ends in んだよ. Nothing is ungrammatical;
  it simply does not belong in that room.

- ❌ 会員(かいいん)証(しょう)はもとより、保険証(ほけんしょう)もお持(も)ちください。 → ✅ 会員(かいいん)証(しょう)<b>に加(くわ)えて</b>、保険証(ほけんしょう)もお持(も)ちください。
  This pattern comments on how things are; it does not issue instructions. A notice
  telling you what to bring takes the neutral additive.

# ～以上に

**Meaning** · even more than; more than just - the thing named was already at a high
level, and this goes past it

<code>以上(いじょう)</code> is the noun "that amount and above". Used as
<code>以上(いじょう)に</code> it sets a benchmark and says the sentence clears it.
That benchmark is the whole point: <code>去年(きょねん)以上(いじょう)に暑(あつ)い</code>
does not just mean "hotter than last year" - it takes for granted that last year was
hot, and says this year beats it.

## Formation

<code>以上(いじょう)</code> is a noun, so it hangs off a noun or a noun-modifying
clause with nothing in between.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + 以上(いじょう)に | 去年(きょねん) → 去年(きょねん)以上(いじょう)に |
| Verb (past) | た-form + 以上(いじょう)に | 思(おも)った → 思(おも)った以上(いじょう)に |
| Verb (progressive) | ～ている + 以上(いじょう)に | 考(かんが)えている以上(いじょう)に |
| Demonstrative | これ／それ + 以上(いじょう)に | これ以上(いじょう)に安(やす)い店(みせ) |
| Modifying a noun | ～以上(いじょう)の + Noun | 予想(よそう)以上(いじょう)の反響(はんきょう) |
| Set phrases | 必要(ひつよう)／予想(よそう)／想像(そうぞう) + 以上(いじょう)に | 想像(そうぞう)以上(いじょう)に大(おお)きい |

<div class="note">

**Note** - に or の depends on what comes next. Before a predicate the form is
<code>以上(いじょう)に</code>; before a noun it is <code>以上(いじょう)の</code>.
予想(よそう)以上(いじょう)<b>に</b>売(う)れた／予想(よそう)以上(いじょう)<b>の</b>売(う)れ行(ゆ)き。

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は去年(きょねん)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>暑(あつ)い。
This summer is even hotter than last year.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)は思(おも)った<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>難(むずか)しかった。
The exam was harder than I'd expected.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は必要(ひつよう)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>心配(しんぱい)している。
He's worrying more than the situation warrants.
</pre>

<pre>
その映画(えいが)は評判(ひょうばん)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>おもしろかった。
The film was even better than its reputation.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい路線(ろせん)は予想(よそう)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>利用者(りようしゃ)が多(おお)く、朝(あさ)の混雑(こんざつ)が問題(もんだい)になっている。
The new line has far more passengers than anticipated, and morning crowding has become a problem.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)が下手(へた)だったせいで、相手(あいて)を想像(そうぞう)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>怒(おこ)らせてしまった。
I explained it badly and made him angrier than I ever imagined I would.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)して分(わ)かったのは、語学力(ごがくりょく)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>人(ひと)に頼(たよ)る勇気(ゆうき)が要(い)るということだった。
What studying abroad taught me is that you need the nerve to lean on people even more than you need the language.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)の接客(せっきゃく)は、料理(りょうり)の味(あじ)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>客(きゃく)を引(ひ)き止(と)めているのだと思(おも)う。
I suspect the service at this place keeps customers coming back even more than the food does.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It is at home in conversation, in a review, in a report and in an
academic paper, with no adjustment. A handful of collocations do most of the work in
practice - <code>思(おも)った以上(いじょう)に</code>,
<code>予想(よそう)以上(いじょう)に</code>,
<code>想像(そうぞう)以上(いじょう)に</code>,
<code>期待(きたい)以上(いじょう)に</code> - and learning those four as units gets
you most of the way.

Two of the fixed expressions carry their own colour.
<code>必要(ひつよう)以上(いじょう)に</code> means "more than is called for" and is
almost always a criticism: worrying too much, apologising too much, charging too
much. And <code>これ以上(いじょう)</code>, "beyond this point", is usually followed by
a negative - <code>これ以上(いじょう)待(ま)てない</code>, "I can't wait any longer" -
and in that use it drops the に.

The comparison is not always about degree. In the last two examples above the
sentence is ranking two *factors* rather than measuring one thing twice: the nerve
matters more than the language, the service more than the food. This is a very common
rhetorical move in Japanese essays and interviews, and it is where the "no less than"
side of the gloss comes from.

One thing the pattern quietly asserts: the benchmark was already substantial. Said of
a mild summer, <code>去年(きょねん)以上(いじょう)に暑(あつ)い</code> would strike
a listener as odd, because there was nothing much to exceed. Plain より carries no such
implication and is the neutral choice for ordinary comparison.

## Contrast

### 以上(いじょう)に vs より

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - より simply **compares**. <code>以上(いじょう)に</code> compares
**and treats the benchmark as already high**.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は私(わたし)<b>より</b>背(せ)が高(たか)い。
My younger brother is taller than me.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は私(わたし)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>背(せ)が高(たか)い。
My brother is even taller than I am.
</pre>

The first is a measurement and says nothing about whether either of us is tall. The
second implies that I am tall, and that he clears even that. Note also that the two
are not interchangeable in shape: より sits between the two things compared, while
<code>以上(いじょう)に</code> attaches to the benchmark and then runs straight into
the predicate. Stacking them - ❌<code>私(わたし)より以上(いじょう)に</code> - is a
common slip and says the same thing twice.

### 以上(いじょう)に vs 以上(いじょう)は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>以上(いじょう)に</code> is a **comparison**;
<code>以上(いじょう)は</code> is a **premise** - "now that", "since".

</div>

<pre>
予想(よそう)していた<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>時間(じかん)がかかった。
It took longer than I had anticipated.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>以上(いじょう)は</b>、最後(さいご)までやります。
Now that I've taken it on, I'll see it through.
</pre>

Same three characters, unrelated jobs. The に one measures; the は one reasons, and it
is usually followed by a resolution, an obligation or a should. If the second half of
your sentence contains <code>べきだ</code>, つもり or a determined ます-form, you almost
certainly wanted は.

### 以上(いじょう)に vs さらに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>以上(いじょう)に</code> **names** the benchmark it is
exceeding; `さらに` leaves it implicit.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は去年(きょねん)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>高(たか)くなっている。
Prices are higher still than they were last year.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は<b>さらに</b>高(たか)くなっている。
Prices have gone up further.
</pre>

Both say the level has risen past something. `さらに` relies on the listener already
knowing what that something was, which is why it belongs in the second or third
sentence of a paragraph. <code>以上(いじょう)に</code> puts the benchmark on the page,
so it can open a paragraph cold.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 去年(きょねん)<b>より以上(いじょう)に</b>雨(あめ)が多(おお)い。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>雨(あめ)が多(おお)い。
  <code>以上(いじょう)に</code> already contains the comparison. Adding より stacks
  two comparatives on one benchmark.

- ❌ 予想(よそう)以上(いじょう)<b>に</b>結果(けっか)が出(で)た。（meaning "a better-than-expected result"） → ✅ 予想(よそう)以上(いじょう)<b>の</b>結果(けっか)が出(で)た。
  Before a noun the form is の. Keep に for when a predicate follows.

- ❌ 約束(やくそく)した以上(いじょう)<b>に</b>守(まも)るべきだ。 → ✅ 約束(やくそく)した以上(いじょう)<b>は</b>守(まも)るべきだ。
  This is the "since you've promised" pattern, not a comparison. べきだ in the second
  half is the clue.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)はこれ以上(いじょう)に考(かんが)えても仕方(しかた)がない。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は<b>これ以上(いじょう)</b>考(かんが)えても仕方(しかた)がない。
  <code>これ以上(いじょう)</code> meaning "any further" works adverbially on its own.
  The に appears only when you are genuinely comparing against "this":
  <code>これ以上(いじょう)に安(やす)い物(もの)はない</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 今回(こんかい)のイベントには、昨年(さくねん)____多(おお)くの人(ひと)が集(あつ)まった。

The sentence takes for granted that last year's turnout was already good, and says
this year beat it. <code>昨年(さくねん)より</code> would also be grammatical but
would drop that implication.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 引(ひ)き受(う)けた（以上(いじょう)に・以上(いじょう)は）、途中(とちゅう)でやめるわけにはいかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Nothing is being compared. The first clause is the premise that generates the
obligation in the second, and <code>わけにはいかない</code> is the sort of ending that
premise takes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The repairs cost more than I had expected."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

修理(しゅうり)代(だい)は思(おも)っていた<b>以上(いじょう)に</b>高(たか)かった。

<code>思(おも)っていた以上(いじょう)に</code> is one of the four collocations worth
memorising whole. The past progressive is what fits here: the expectation was in
place before the bill arrived.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は必要(ひつよう)以上(いじょう)の謝(あやま)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は必要(ひつよう)以上(いじょう)<b>に</b>謝(あやま)っている。

<code>謝(あやま)っている</code> is a predicate, not a noun, so the adverbial に is
required. The の form would need a noun after it, as in
<code>必要(ひつよう)以上(いじょう)の説明(せつめい)</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この仕事(しごと)は経験(けいけん)より体力(たいりょく)が必要(ひつよう)だ。
(b) この仕事(しごと)は経験(けいけん)以上(いじょう)に体力(たいりょく)が必要(ひつよう)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) ranks the two flatly: of the two, stamina matters more. It leaves open the
possibility that experience hardly matters at all.

(b) grants that experience matters - a lot - and then says stamina matters even more.
It is the more generous and the more persuasive sentence, which is why it is what you
would say to someone who has just told you they have no experience.

</details>

# も○○ば○○も

**Meaning** · both ... and ...; there are ... and there are ... - two parallel facts
in one balanced frame

The frame is <code>Aも…ば、Bも…</code>. Two things are named, each marked with も, and
the first predicate goes into its ば-form. The ば is **not** a conditional here; it is
the old "listing" use of the same ending, and its job is to hold the two halves in
parallel. Everything the pattern adds over a plain list is in that symmetry: the
sentence sounds composed, and it characterises rather than merely enumerates.

## Formation

The second predicate is in its ordinary plain or polite form. Only the first one
takes ば.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ～も + ば-form, ～も + plain | 描(か)く → 絵(え)も描(か)けば詩(し)も書(か)く |
| Verb (する) | ～も + すれば | 世話(せわ)をする → 世話(せわ)もすれば |
| い-adjective | ～も + ければ | 安(やす)い → 値段(ねだん)も安(やす)ければ |
| な-adjective | ～も + なら（ば） | 静(しず)かな → 席(せき)も静(しず)かなら |
| Noun | ～も + なら（ば） | 教師(きょうし) → 父(ちち)も教師(きょうし)なら |
| Existence | ～も + あれば／いれば | 日(ひ)もあれば → 晴(は)れる日(ひ)もあれば |
| Polite | second half in ます／です | …味(あじ)もいいです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - both も are obligatory, and they replace が or を. ❌<code>絵(え)を描(か)けば詩(し)も書(か)く</code>
is not this pattern at all - with を it reads as a genuine conditional, "if he paints,
he also writes poetry."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は絵(え)<b>も描(か)けば</b>詩(し)<b>も</b>書(か)く。
He paints, and he writes poetry too.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は値段(ねだん)<b>も安(やす)ければ</b>味(あじ)<b>も</b>いい。
This place is cheap and the food is good.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)<b>も教師(きょうし)なら</b>、母(はは)<b>も</b>教師(きょうし)だ。
My father is a teacher and so is my mother.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の天気(てんき)は、晴(は)れる日(ひ)<b>もあれば</b>一日中(いちにちじゅう)霧(きり)の日(ひ)<b>も</b>ある。
Up in the hills there are clear days and there are days of fog from morning to night.
</pre>

<pre>
あの喫茶店(きっさてん)は席(せき)<b>も静(しず)かなら</b>、コーヒー<b>も</b>本格的(ほんかくてき)だ。
That coffee shop has quiet seating, and the coffee is the real thing as well.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は指示(しじ)<b>も細(こま)かければ</b>、催促(さいそく)<b>も</b>多(おお)い。
The section chief gives fussy instructions, and chases you about them constantly.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ大学(だいがく)を出(で)ても、すぐに就職(しゅうしょく)する人(ひと)<b>もいれば</b>、何年(なんねん)も旅(たび)を続(つづ)ける人(ひと)<b>も</b>いる。
Out of the same university, some go straight into a job and others spend years travelling.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は畑(はたけ)仕事(しごと)<b>もすれば</b>、近所(きんじょ)の子(こ)どもの世話(せわ)<b>も</b>する。
My grandmother works the vegetable plot and looks after the neighbourhood children as well.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern has a formal, balanced rhythm - it is the shape of a sentence that has
been composed rather than blurted - and it turns up in writing, in careful speech and
in a great many proverbs. It is not casual. Friends listing two things about a
restaurant say <code>安(やす)いし、おいしいし</code>; a review says
<code>値段(ねだん)も安(やす)ければ味(あじ)もいい</code>.

It comes in two flavours, and telling them apart is the key to using it:

**Characterising.** Two facts of the same sign about one subject, adding up to a
verdict. He paints *and* writes; the boss is fussy *and* nags. Here the pattern behaves
like the other additive patterns and the two halves must lean the same way.

**Range.** Two deliberately *opposite* cases, adding up to "both kinds exist". This is
where <code>あれば</code> and <code>いれば</code> dominate - clear days and foggy days,
people who do and people who don't - and here the opposition is the whole point. If
your sentence is of this second type, mixing good and bad is not a fault but the
design.

The ば-form is doing something learners often misread. There is no condition being
set: <code>絵(え)も描(か)けば</code> does not mean "if he paints". A reliable test
is the second も - a real conditional has no reason to put も on the second subject.
Because there is no condition, the second half cannot be a request, an invitation or a
command; the frame states how things are.

## Contrast

### も○○ば○○も vs ～し

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `し` is the **casual, open** list. This pattern is the **composed,
closed** pair.

</div>

<pre>
あそこ、安(やす)い<b>し</b>近(ちか)い<b>し</b>、いいと思(おも)うよ。
That place is cheap, it's close - I reckon it's a good bet.
</pre>

<pre>
あの宿(やど)は値段(ねだん)<b>も手(て)ごろなら</b>、場所(ばしょ)<b>も</b>いい。
That guesthouse is reasonably priced and well located.
</pre>

`し` can be repeated indefinitely, can trail off leaving the rest to the listener, and
leads naturally into a suggestion. This pattern does exactly two items, finishes them
both, and does not lead anywhere - it delivers a verdict and stops. That is why it
reads well in a written review and stiffly in a text message.

### も○○ば○○も vs ～も～も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～も～も</code> hangs two nouns off **one** predicate. This
pattern gives each noun **its own** predicate.

</div>

<pre>
兄(あに)はギター<b>も</b>ピアノ<b>も</b>弾(ひ)ける。
My brother can play both the guitar and the piano.
</pre>

<pre>
兄(あに)はギター<b>も弾(ひ)けば</b>、曲(きょく)<b>も</b>作(つく)る。
My brother plays the guitar, and he writes songs as well.
</pre>

The first sentence is about two instruments and one ability. The second is about two
different activities, and needs two verbs - which is precisely what the ば-frame is
for. If both your items take the same verb, the simpler <code>～も～も</code> is the
right tool and this pattern would be clumsy.

### も○○ば○○も vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～たり～たり</code> gives **samples of what happens**. The
ば-frame gives **two established facts** that add up to a description.

</div>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)は本(ほん)を読(よ)ん<b>だり</b>散歩(さんぽ)し<b>たり</b>している。
On days off I read, go for walks, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は本(ほん)<b>も読(よ)めば</b>絵(え)<b>も</b>描(か)く。
She reads, and she paints as well.
</pre>

<code>～たり～たり</code> is explicitly incomplete - it means "among other things" - and
is thoroughly casual. The ば-frame names two things and asserts them flatly, which is
why it sounds like a summing-up and <code>～たり～たり</code> sounds like an anecdote.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は英語(えいご)が話(はな)せば、中国語(ちゅうごくご)も話(はな)せる。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は英語(えいご)<b>も</b>話(はな)せば、中国語(ちゅうごくご)も話(はな)せる。
  The first も is not optional. With が the sentence turns into a conditional and
  stops meaning "both".

- ❌ 父(ちち)も教師(きょうし)<b>だば</b>… → ✅ 父(ちち)も教師(きょうし)<b>なら</b>…
  Nouns and な-adjectives have no ば-form of だ. They use なら, or ならば in formal
  writing.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)もあれば、手伝(てつだ)ってください。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)が<b>あれば</b>、手伝(てつだ)ってください。
  A genuine conditional takes が, not も, and drops the second half of the frame.
  With も the listener expects another <code>…も…</code> and gets a request instead.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)ければ、家賃(やちん)も高(たか)い。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)い<b>が</b>、家賃(やちん)は高(たか)い。
  In the characterising use both halves must lean the same way. A plus and a minus
  need a contrastive connector - unless you are deliberately building the "both kinds
  exist" shape, which needs two parallel subjects.

## Practice

**1.** この地域(ちいき)は夏(なつ)の祭(まつ)り____冬(ふゆ)の市(いち)____有名(ゆうめい)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

夏(なつ)の祭(まつ)り<b>も有名(ゆうめい)なら</b>、冬(ふゆ)の市(いち)<b>も</b>有名(ゆうめい)だ。

<code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is a な-adjective, so its ば-form is なら. Both halves are
points in the region's favour, so the characterising use applies.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)はギター（も弾(ひ)けば・を弾(ひ)けば）、歌(うた)も歌(うた)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)はギター<b>も弾(ひ)けば</b>、歌(うた)も歌(うた)う。

The second half already has も, which tells you the frame is the listing one. を
would force a conditional reading - "if he plays the guitar, he also sings" - which is
not what anyone means here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Some students hand the work in early, and others never hand it in at all."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

早(はや)めに提出(ていしゅつ)する学生(がくせい)<b>もいれば</b>、最後(さいご)まで出(だ)さない学生(がくせい)<b>も</b>いる。

"Some … and others …" is the range use, and it is almost always built on
<code>いる</code> or <code>ある</code>. The two halves are opposites here, and that is
exactly right for this flavour of the pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この旅館(りょかん)は料理(りょうり)もおいしいければ、温泉(おんせん)も広(ひろ)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この旅館(りょかん)は料理(りょうり)も<b>おいしければ</b>、温泉(おんせん)も広(ひろ)い。

The ば-form of an い-adjective replaces the final い with ければ - おいしい → おいしければ.
Keeping the い as well is the commonest conjugation slip in this pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は料理(りょうり)も上手(じょうず)なら、片付(かたづ)けも早(はや)い。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)は料理(りょうり)が上手(じょうず)だし、片付(かたづ)けも早(はや)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a balanced two-part verdict. The symmetry of the frame makes it sound
considered, as though the speaker has weighed her up and is reporting the conclusion.
It would fit a written recommendation.

(b) is the spoken version. `し` implies the list could go on - there are probably
other good points the speaker has not bothered to mention - and it often leads on to
a conclusion such as <code>だから助(たす)かる</code>. Same facts, looser frame.

</details>

# やら○○やら

**Meaning** · what with ... and ...; ... and ... and goodness knows what else - names
two things out of a jumble and implies there were more

<code>やら</code> comes in pairs. It picks two representative items from a situation
that had too many, and it carries a distinct feeling with it: things piled up, the
speaker was busy or bewildered, and this is a summary rather than a list. Where や
merely says "among others", <code>やら…やら</code> says "among others, and it was a
lot".

## Formation

Each listed item takes its own <code>やら</code>. The pattern is almost always
followed by で and a predicate describing the resulting state.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + やら + Noun + やら | 書類(しょるい)やら雑誌(ざっし)やら |
| Verb | plain form + やら | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)くやら笑(わら)うやら |
| い-adjective | plain form + やら | 嬉(うれ)しい → 嬉(うれ)しいやら悲(かな)しいやら |
| な-adjective (as noun) | stem + やら | 不安(ふあん) → 不安(ふあん)やら心配(しんぱい)やら |
| Usual continuation | …やら…やら + で + predicate | …で忙(いそが)しい／大変(たいへん)だ |
| Subject of a clause | …やら…やら + が | …やら…やらが出(で)てきた |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the items in a <code>やら…やら</code> list must be the same part of speech.
Two nouns, or two verbs, or two adjectives - never a noun paired with a verb. The
symmetry is what makes the pattern read as a summary.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
机(つくえ)の上(うえ)は書類(しょるい)<b>やら</b>雑誌(ざっし)<b>やら</b>で散(ち)らかっている。
The desk is a mess of papers and magazines and who knows what else.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの準備(じゅんび)<b>やら</b>手続(てつづ)き<b>やら</b>で忙(いそが)しい。
I'm run off my feet, what with packing for the move and all the paperwork.
</pre>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)の知(し)らせを聞(き)いて、嬉(うれ)しい<b>やら</b>信(しん)じられない<b>やら</b>だった。
When I heard I'd passed I didn't know whether to be delighted or disbelieving.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちは泣(な)く<b>やら</b>笑(わら)う<b>やら</b>で、大(おお)騒(さわ)ぎだった。
The children were crying and laughing and generally making bedlam.
</pre>

<pre>
棚(たな)の奥(おく)から、古(ふる)い写真(しゃしん)<b>やら</b>使(つか)っていない充電器(じゅうでんき)<b>やら</b>が出(で)てきた。
Out of the back of the cupboard came old photos, chargers for nothing in particular, and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
年末(ねんまつ)は大掃除(おおそうじ)<b>やら</b>年賀状(ねんがじょう)<b>やら</b>で、落(お)ち着(つ)いて本(ほん)を読(よ)む時間(じかん)もない。
At the end of the year, between the big clean and the new year cards, there's no time to sit down with a book.
</pre>

<pre>
退院(たいいん)した父(ちち)は、薬(くすり)の管理(かんり)<b>やら</b>食事(しょくじ)の制限(せいげん)<b>やら</b>で、前(まえ)より忙(いそが)しそうだ。
Since coming out of hospital my father seems busier than before, what with managing his pills and watching what he eats.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)では予算(よさん)の話(はなし)<b>やら</b>人事(じんじ)の話(はなし)<b>やら</b>が次々(つぎつぎ)に出(で)て、結局(けっきょく)何(なに)も決(き)まらなかった。
Budget came up, then staffing, then something else again, and in the end nothing was decided.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Conversational, and warmer than it looks on paper. The pattern is how a person
describes a period they have just survived - moving house, the week before a
deadline, the day the relatives came. It is not neutral: choosing
<code>やら…やら</code> tells the listener that the speaker felt swamped, amused,
harassed or all three.

It leans slightly grown-up. Younger speakers listing things in a hurry are more
likely to reach for とか; <code>やら…やら</code> is commoner in the speech of older
speakers and in writing that wants that settled, slightly rueful voice. That is a
tendency, not a rule, and nobody will find it odd in anyone's mouth.

What it is not is a formal list. A report saying which documents are required uses
や, および or <code>など</code>; <code>やら…やら</code> in that position would sound as
though the writer had given up sorting them out - which is, precisely, its meaning.

Two related uses of the same word are worth recognising. With a question word,
<code>やら</code> marks something the speaker cannot pin down:
<code>何(なに)やら様子(ようす)がおかしい</code>, "something seems off". And the set
phrase <code>どうなることやら</code>, "heaven knows how this will turn out", is a
complete sentence in itself. Both come from the same sense of vagueness that the
paired listing pattern trades on.

Emotions are its other home ground. <code>嬉(うれ)しいやら恥(は)ずかしいやら</code>
describes a state that cannot be named with one adjective, and this shape often ends
in だ or だった rather than continuing with で.

## Contrast

### やら○○やら vs とか○○とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とか` is a **neutral casual list**. <code>やら</code> adds
**disorder** - the speaker was overwhelmed by the number of them.

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は洗濯(せんたく)<b>とか</b>掃除(そうじ)<b>とか</b>してた。
At the weekend I did the washing, cleaning, that kind of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は洗濯(せんたく)<b>やら</b>掃除(そうじ)<b>やら</b>で終(お)わってしまった。
The whole weekend went on washing and cleaning and the rest of it.
</pre>

The first is a relaxed answer to "what did you get up to?". The second is a small
complaint. `とか` is also far more flexible - it can list a single item, it can offer
options, it can soften a suggestion - while <code>やら</code> does only this one job.

### やら○○やら vs だの○○だの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だの</code> is **critical**: the speaker disapproves of the
items or of the person who produced them. <code>やら</code> is merely swamped.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は報告書(ほうこくしょ)<b>だの</b>反省文(はんせいぶん)<b>だの</b>と次々(つぎつぎ)に要求(ようきゅう)してくる。
The department head keeps demanding reports, written reflections, one thing after another.
</pre>

<pre>
報告書(ほうこくしょ)<b>やら</b>反省文(はんせいぶん)<b>やら</b>で、机(つくえ)に向(む)かう時間(じかん)が足(た)りない。
Between reports and written reflections there aren't enough hours at the desk.
</pre>

Both sentences are unhappy, but they are unhappy about different things. The first
aims its irritation at the boss; the second at the workload. <code>だの</code> is the
stronger and ruder of the two, and pointing it at a person present is a real
discourtesy.

### やら○○やら vs や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `や` is the **plain partial list** for nouns, usable anywhere.
<code>やら</code> is the same list plus an attitude, and it also handles verbs and
adjectives.

</div>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)では申込書(もうしこみしょ)<b>や</b>身分証(みぶんしょう)などをご用意(ようい)ください。
At the desk please have your application form, ID and so on ready.
</pre>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)<b>やら</b>身分証(みぶんしょう)<b>やら</b>集(あつ)めるのに一日(いちにち)かかった。
It took me a whole day to round up the application form, the ID and everything else.
</pre>

The first is an official instruction and could not take <code>やら</code>; the second
is the applicant's account of doing it and would sound oddly bureaucratic with や.
Note too that や joins nouns only - <code>泣(な)くやら笑(わら)うやら</code> has no
や equivalent.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 書類(しょるい)やらで忙(いそが)しかった。 → ✅ 書類(しょるい)<b>やら</b>電話(でんわ)<b>やら</b>で忙(いそが)しかった。
  The pattern is a pair. A single <code>やら</code> after a plain noun leaves the
  listener waiting for the second one.

- ❌ 不安(ふあん)なやら心配(しんぱい)なやらで眠(ねむ)れない。 → ✅ 不安(ふあん)<b>やら</b>心配(しんぱい)<b>やら</b>で眠(ねむ)れない。
  な-adjectives go in bare, as nouns. The な belongs only in front of a noun it is
  modifying.

- ❌ コーヒーやら紅茶(こうちゃ)やらにします。 → ✅ コーヒー<b>か</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)にします。
  This is not a pattern for choosing. It describes a jumble that already exists;
  offering alternatives takes か, または or あるいは.

- ❌ 本件(ほんけん)は費用(ひよう)やら工期(こうき)やらに課題(かだい)がございます。 → ✅ 本件(ほんけん)は費用(ひよう)<b>や</b>工期(こうき)<b>など</b>に課題(かだい)がございます。
  Keigo endings and a colloquial jumble-marker fight each other. Formal writing lists
  with や, および or など.

## Practice

**1.** 連休(れんきゅう)は来客(らいきゃく)____買(か)い物(もの)____で、ほとんど休(やす)めなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来客(らいきゃく)<b>やら</b>買(か)い物(もの)<b>やら</b>で、ほとんど休(やす)めなかった。

Two nouns naming a fraction of what actually filled the holiday, followed by で and a
statement of the toll it took. That final clause is the tell: this pattern nearly
always ends in one.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 飲(の)み物(もの)は冷(つめ)たいお茶(ちゃ)（やら・か）コーヒーにします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

冷(つめ)たいお茶(ちゃ)<b>か</b>コーヒーにします。

The speaker is choosing one drink, not describing a heap of them. <code>やら</code>
lists what is jumbled together; it cannot offer an alternative.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "What with the noise and the smell, I couldn't sleep at all."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

音(おと)<b>やら</b>におい<b>やら</b>で、全然(ぜんぜん)眠(ねむ)れなかった。

"What with X and Y" is almost a one-to-one translation of this pattern, and the
で before the result clause is doing the work of "with". Both items are nouns, so the
symmetry holds.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 試験(しけん)の前(まえ)は勉強(べんきょう)やら不安(ふあん)なやらで落(お)ち着(つ)かなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 試験(しけん)の前(まえ)は勉強(べんきょう)<b>やら</b>心配(しんぱい)<b>やら</b>で落(お)ち着(つ)かなかった。

Two repairs. The な has to go, and the two items should match in kind - a noun paired
with a な-adjective reads unevenly, so swapping in the noun
<code>心配(しんぱい)</code> balances the pair.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 荷物(にもつ)やら書類(しょるい)やらで部屋(へや)がいっぱいだ。
(b) 荷物(にもつ)や書類(しょるい)で部屋(へや)がいっぱいだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a flat report: those things, and possibly others, fill the room. It could
appear in a written description with no change of tone.

(a) adds the speaker. The doubled <code>やら</code> makes the room sound chaotic and
the speaker faintly defeated by it - the difference between "the room is full of
boxes and papers" and "the room is buried under boxes and papers and heaven knows
what". Nothing in the facts has changed; the posture has.

</details>

# ～といった

**Meaning** · such ... as; ... and the like - names one or two members of a category
and then names the category

The shape is <code>AやB + といった + C</code>. A and B are examples; C is the class they
belong to. The pattern is doing the job of English "such as" in reverse order - the
samples come first, the label second - and its one absolute requirement is that a noun
must follow it.

## Formation

<code>といった</code> is the past form of <code>という</code> used as a modifier, so it
behaves like an adjective: it always has a noun after it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two nouns | Noun + や + Noun + といった + Noun | 梅(うめ)や桜(さくら)といった花(はな) |
| Three or more | Noun、Noun、Noun + といった + Noun | 米(こめ)、麦(むぎ)、豆(まめ)といった作物(さくもつ) |
| One noun | Noun + といった + Noun | 漢字(かんじ)といった課題(かだい) |
| Quoted phrase | 「…」 + といった + Noun | 「検討(けんとう)します」といった返事(へんじ) |
| Fixed negative | これ + といった + Noun + ない | これといった変化(へんか)はない |
| Softened | …といったような + Noun | …といったような意見(いけん) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - two examples is the norm, joined by や. One works but sounds thin, because
a single member is a poor advertisement for a whole category. Three or more are
separated by commas and only the last one takes <code>といった</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
梅(うめ)や桜(さくら)<b>といった</b>春(はる)の花(はな)が好(す)きだ。
I love the flowers of spring - plum, cherry, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
トマトやきゅうり<b>といった</b>夏(なつ)野菜(やさい)が安(やす)くなってきた。
Summer vegetables such as tomatoes and cucumbers have been getting cheaper.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)や奈良(なら)<b>といった</b>古(ふる)い町(まち)を一度(いちど)訪(たず)ねてみたい。
I'd like to visit the old cities - Kyoto, Nara and the like - at least once.
</pre>

<pre>
この図書館(としょかん)には、古(ふる)い地図(ちず)や写真(しゃしん)<b>といった</b>郷土(きょうど)資料(しりょう)が多(おお)く残(のこ)っている。
This library holds a lot of local-history material: old maps, photographs and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
睡眠(すいみん)不足(ぶそく)や運動(うんどう)不足(ぶそく)<b>といった</b>生活(せいかつ)習慣(しゅうかん)の問題(もんだい)が背景(はいけい)にある。
Behind it lie lifestyle problems such as lack of sleep and lack of exercise.
</pre>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)では、志望(しぼう)動機(どうき)や将来(しょうらい)の目標(もくひょう)<b>といった</b>定番(ていばん)の質問(しつもん)がまず来(く)る。
In an interview the standard questions come first - why you applied, where you see yourself going.
</pre>

<pre>
台所(だいどころ)には、母(はは)が使(つか)っていた木(き)のしゃもじや鉄(てつ)の鍋(なべ)<b>といった</b>道具(どうぐ)が今(いま)も並(なら)んでいる。
In the kitchen the tools my mother used - the wooden rice paddle, the iron pan - are still lined up.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の調査(ちょうさ)では、これ<b>といった</b>変化(へんか)は見(み)られなかった。
This round of testing showed no change worth reporting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral, leaning written. It is the connector of explanation - newspaper features,
textbooks, reports, the paragraph in a proposal where you say what you mean by a
term. In relaxed speech people say とか instead, and <code>といった</code> there sounds
a little as though you were reading from notes. It is not stiff in the way
<code>のみならず</code> is stiff; it simply belongs to considered sentences.

The one structural rule is worth repeating because it is where nearly all errors come
from: **a noun must follow.** <code>など</code> can end a sentence, take a particle,
stand as a subject; <code>といった</code> can do none of that. If you cannot supply the
category noun, you have chosen the wrong pattern.

The examples you choose have to be genuine members of the category, and readers do
check. Naming two fruits and then calling them vegetables is not a grammatical error
but a factual one, and it is the sort of slip that makes a sentence unreadable.

<code>これといった</code> plus a negative is a set phrase worth learning whole:
<code>これといった理由(りゆう)もない</code>, "no particular reason";
<code>これといった成果(せいか)が出(で)ていない</code>, "nothing much to show for
it". It always appears with a negative predicate, and never in the affirmative.

A quoted remark can also take <code>といった</code> -
<code>「前向(まえむ)きに考(かんが)えたい」といった発言(はつげん)</code> - where the
category noun is something like <code>発言(はつげん)</code>,
<code>意見(いけん)</code> or <code>表現(ひょうげん)</code>. This is common in
journalism.

## Contrast

### といった vs など

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>など</code> is a **word that can stand on its own**;
<code>といった</code> is a **modifier and needs a noun after it**.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)には机(つくえ)や椅子(いす)<b>など</b>が運(はこ)び込(こ)まれた。
Desks, chairs and so on were carried into the hall.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)には机(つくえ)や椅子(いす)<b>といった</b>備品(びひん)が運(はこ)び込(こ)まれた。
Furnishings such as desks and chairs were carried into the hall.
</pre>

Both sentences are correct, and the second one tells you something the first does
not: what class the items belong to. That is the real payoff of
<code>といった</code> - it is a way of defining a term by example. When you have no
label to offer, <code>など</code> is the honest choice.

### といった vs という

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>という</code> **names** one specific thing.
<code>といった</code> **samples** a category.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)<b>という</b>人(ひと)から電話(でんわ)がありました。
Someone called Tanaka rang for you.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんや鈴木(すずき)さん<b>といった</b>古(ふる)くからの社員(しゃいん)が残(のこ)っている。
Long-standing staff such as Tanaka and Suzuki are still here.
</pre>

In the first, Tanaka *is* the person. In the second, Tanaka is one instance of a
group. The past-tense look of <code>といった</code> has nothing to do with time; it is
simply the modifying form this expression settled into.

### といった vs とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different register. `とか` is casual speech;
<code>といった</code> is neutral-to-written.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)はパン<b>とか</b>ヨーグルト<b>とか</b>食(た)べてる。
In the mornings I have bread, yoghurt, that kind of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
朝食(ちょうしょく)にはパンやヨーグルト<b>といった</b>軽(かる)いものが選(えら)ばれている。
For breakfast, light items such as bread and yoghurt are what people choose.
</pre>

Notice again that the register moved the vocabulary with it -
<code>朝(あさ)</code> became <code>朝食(ちょうしょく)</code>, the verb became
passive. `とか` also attaches to each item and can appear after only one;
<code>といった</code> appears once, at the end of the list, immediately before the
category.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昼食(ちゅうしょく)はうどんや寿司(すし)といった。 → ✅ 昼食(ちゅうしょく)はうどんや寿司(すし)<b>など</b>だった。
  The pattern cannot end a sentence. Something has to follow it, and if there is no
  category noun to supply, <code>など</code> is the word you want.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)<b>といった</b>学生(がくせい)が来(き)ました。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)<b>という</b>学生(がくせい)が来(き)ました。
  One named individual takes <code>という</code>. <code>といった</code> would imply
  there are several students of the Yamada type.

- ❌ りんごやみかん<b>といった</b>野菜(やさい)が好(す)きだ。 → ✅ りんごやみかん<b>といった</b>果物(くだもの)が好(す)きだ。
  The examples must actually belong to the category named. Grammar will not save a
  sentence that puts apples in with the vegetables.

- ❌ ねえ、映画(えいが)やカラオケといった遊(あそ)びに行(い)かない? → ✅ ねえ、映画(えいが)<b>とか</b>カラオケ<b>とか</b>行(い)かない?
  An invitation to a friend is not the place for a written connector. `とか` carries
  the same list at the right temperature.

## Practice

**1.** 地震(じしん)や台風(たいふう)____自然(しぜん)災害(さいがい)に備(そな)えておく必要(ひつよう)がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

地震(じしん)や台風(たいふう)<b>といった</b>自然(しぜん)災害(さいがい)に備(そな)えておく必要(ひつよう)がある。

Two examples, joined by や, followed immediately by the category noun they belong to.
The written register of the sentence - <code>備(そな)えておく必要(ひつよう)がある</code> -
fits the pattern exactly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新入生(しんにゅうせい)に佐藤(さとう)（という・といった）人(ひと)がいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

佐藤(さとう)<b>という</b>人(ひと)がいます。

One specific person is being named, not sampled. <code>といった</code> would suggest
Sato was an example of a type of first-year student, which is not what the sentence
means.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Skills such as reading and writing are tested first."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

読(よ)み書(か)きや会話(かいわ)<b>といった</b>能力(のうりょく)がまず試(ため)される。

The English "such as" maps straight onto this pattern, with the category noun
<code>能力(のうりょく)</code> in the slot after it. Adding a second example keeps
the list from sounding thin.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この辺(あた)りには銀行(ぎんこう)や郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)といったが多(おお)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この辺(あた)りには銀行(ぎんこう)や郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)<b>といった施設(しせつ)</b>が多(おお)い。（or …郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)<b>など</b>が多(おお)い）

<code>といった</code> was left dangling in front of the particle が. Either supply the
category noun it is waiting for, or switch to <code>など</code>, which needs nothing
after it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 漫画(まんが)やアニメなどが海外(かいがい)でも人気(にんき)だ。
(b) 漫画(まんが)やアニメといった日本(にほん)の文化(ぶんか)が海外(かいがい)でも人気(にんき)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) lists two popular things and leaves it there. The reader is free to decide what,
if anything, the two have in common.

(b) makes that claim explicitly: manga and anime are being offered as instances of
Japanese culture, and the sentence is now partly an argument about how to classify
them. That is the extra work <code>といった</code> does - it labels as well as lists,
which is why it is so common in explanatory writing.

</details>

# あるいは

**Meaning** · or; alternatively - and, used adverbially, possibly

`あるいは` is the written "or". It sets two options side by side without asking anyone
to choose between them on the spot, which is why it belongs to instructions,
regulations and prose rather than to conversation. Its second life is as an adverb
meaning "possibly", where it hedges a whole sentence rather than joining two things.

## Usage

Two jobs, and it is worth keeping them apart.

**Connecting alternatives.** It stands between two nouns, two phrases or two clauses:

- 鉛筆(えんぴつ)あるいはボールペンでご記入(きにゅう)ください。
- 窓口(まどぐち)で申(もう)し込(こ)むか、あるいは郵送(ゆうそう)してください。

When it joins clauses, the first one usually ends in か, exactly as it would before
それとも.

**Hedging a statement.** At the head of a sentence it means "it may be that", and it
almost always ends in かもしれない, だろう or でしょう:

- あるいは間(ま)に合(あ)うかもしれない。

Nothing attaches to it and it never changes form. The kanji spelling
<code>或(ある)いは</code> exists but is rarely used; kana is standard.

One thing it does not do: ask. `あるいは` presents alternatives to a reader; it does
not put a choice to a listener and wait for an answer. That is それとも's job, and the
two are not interchangeable.

## Examples

<pre>
メール<b>あるいは</b>電話(でんわ)でご連絡(れんらく)ください。
Please contact us by email or telephone.
</pre>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)は窓口(まどぐち)に出(だ)すか、<b>あるいは</b>郵送(ゆうそう)してください。
Either hand the application in at the desk or send it by post.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>あるいは</b>彼(かれ)はもうその話(はなし)を知(し)っているのかもしれない。
It may be that he already knows about it.
</pre>

<pre>
作業(さぎょう)は土曜(どよう)の午前中(ごぜんちゅう)、<b>あるいは</b>翌週(よくしゅう)の平日(へいじつ)に行(おこな)う予定(よてい)です。
The work is scheduled for Saturday morning, or alternatively a weekday the following week.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and formal. Forms, manuals, contracts, academic papers, official notices and
careful prose are where it lives. Spoken between friends it sounds like a document
being read aloud - there, か does the joining
（<code>土曜(どよう)か日曜(にちよう)</code>）and それとも does the asking.

The adverbial "possibly" use is a shade more literary again, and carries a
reflective tone: the speaker is entertaining a possibility rather than asserting it.
It fits an essay or an internal monologue better than a briefing. Note that it needs
a hedging ending to complete it - `あるいは` followed by a flat assertion leaves the
sentence sounding as though a word has gone missing.

Because the pattern is neutral about which option is preferred, it is the polite
choice in an instruction: telling someone they may do A or B, with no steer either
way. If you *do* want to steer, the ordinary route is to state the preferred option
and add the other with <code>または</code> or a な-clause of explanation.

<div class="note">

**Note** - in a list of three or more, Japanese usually marks only the last boundary:
<code>現金(げんきん)、振(ふ)り込(こ)み、あるいはクレジットカードでお支払(しはら)いいただけます</code>.
Repeating `あるいは` between every pair reads as heavily as it would in English.

</div>

## Contrast

### あるいは vs または

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are the formal "or", and in a plain list they are
interchangeable. <code>または</code> is the stricter **either/or**; `あるいは` also
carries the "or possibly" sense that <code>または</code> lacks.

</div>

<pre>
保険証(ほけんしょう)<b>または</b>運転免許証(うんてんめんきょしょう)をご提示(ていじ)ください。
Please show your health insurance card or your driving licence.
</pre>

<pre>
原因(げんいん)は設定(せってい)の誤(あやま)り、<b>あるいは</b>機器(きき)の故障(こしょう)だと考(かんが)えられる。
The cause is thought to be a setting error, or possibly a hardware fault.
</pre>

The first sentence lists two documents either of which will do - a closed choice, and
<code>または</code> is what a form prints. The second is not offering a choice at all;
it is a writer weighing two explanations, and `あるいは` carries that uncertainty
naturally. Where the sentence is simply "A or B, take your pick",
either word is correct and <code>または</code> is marginally the commoner in
officialese.

### あるいは vs それとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あるいは` **presents** alternatives in a statement. `それとも`
**asks** the listener to pick, and only appears in questions.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は駅前(えきまえ)、<b>あるいは</b>市民(しみん)ホールになります。
The venue will be either the space by the station or the civic hall.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は駅前(えきまえ)ですか。<b>それとも</b>市民(しみん)ホールですか。
Is the venue the place by the station, or the civic hall?
</pre>

Put `あるいは` into the second sentence and it stops being a question and becomes a
strange list; put それとも into the first and the reader waits for a question mark that
never comes. The test is simple: if you expect an answer, you want それとも.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ コーヒーあるいは紅茶(こうちゃ)、どちらがいいですか。 → ✅ コーヒーですか。<b>それとも</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)ですか。
  You are asking someone to choose, and `あるいは` cannot do that. In this shape
  「コーヒーか紅茶(こうちゃ)、どちらがいいですか」 also works.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)あるいは明日(あした)、飲(の)みに行(い)こうよ。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)<b>か</b>明日(あした)、飲(の)みに行(い)こうよ。
  An invitation to a friend is spoken Japanese, and spoken Japanese joins two options
  with か. `あるいは` here sounds like a memo.

- ❌ あるいは明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る。 → ✅ あるいは明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>かもしれない</b>。
  The adverbial use is a hedge, so it needs an ending that hedges. Attached to a flat
  assertion it leaves the sentence unfinished.

## Practice

**1.** お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)、____クレジットカードでお願(ねが)いいたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

現金(げんきん)、<b>あるいは</b>クレジットカードでお願(ねが)いいたします。

A formal instruction offering two acceptable methods, with no question being asked -
which is exactly the slot this word fills. <code>または</code> would be equally
correct here.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きますか。あるいは家(いえ)でゆっくりしますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きますか。<b>それとも</b>家(いえ)でゆっくりしますか。

Two questions, and the listener is being asked to choose between them. `あるいは`
presents options in a statement; it cannot stand between two questions.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この症状(しょうじょう)は疲(つか)れ、あるいはストレスによるものだ。
(b) この症状(しょうじょう)は疲(つか)れか、それともストレスによるものか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a statement, and a fairly confident one: the writer has narrowed the cause to
two candidates and is reporting both. `あるいは` here shades towards "or possibly",
leaving which of the two open.

(b) is a question the speaker is putting - to a doctor, or to themselves. それとも
sets up a genuine fork that someone is expected to resolve, which is why the sentence
ends in か and not だ.

</details>

# それとも

**Meaning** · or; or else - puts two alternatives to someone and waits for an answer

`それとも` lives in questions. It stands between two of them and asks the listener to
take one: this, or that? Everything that distinguishes it from the other Japanese
words for "or" follows from that single fact - it needs a question in front of it and
a question after it.

## Usage

The standard frame is two complete questions:

- 歩(ある)いて行(い)きますか。それともタクシーを呼(よ)びますか。

The second question is often shortened to just the new part, especially in speech:

- 歩(ある)いて行(い)く? それともタクシー?

It also appears inside a single sentence when the speaker is weighing two courses of
action, with each option marked by か:

- 今日(きょう)出(だ)すか、それとも明日(あした)にするか、まだ決(き)めていない。

Nothing attaches to it and it never changes form. It is written in kana.

The restriction that matters: **it cannot join two nouns inside a statement.**
❌<code>明日(あした)それとも明後日(あさって)行(い)きます</code> is not Japanese.
Statements join their options with か, または or あるいは; `それとも` is reserved for
the moment a choice is handed over.

## Examples

<pre>
コーヒーにしますか。<b>それとも</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)にしますか。
Would you like coffee, or tea?
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)出(で)ますか、<b>それとも</b>雨(あめ)がやむまで待(ま)ちますか。
Shall we set off now, or wait until the rain stops?
</pre>

<pre>
これ、君(きみ)が描(か)いたの? <b>それとも</b>お姉(ねえ)さん?
Did you draw this? Or was it your big sister?
</pre>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)で謝(あやま)るべきか、<b>それとも</b>直接(ちょくせつ)会(あ)いに行(い)くべきか、一晩中(ひとばんじゅう)迷(まよ)っていた。
Whether to apologise by phone or go and see him in person - I was torn all night.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Completely register-neutral. It works in a plain question between friends, in
keigo at a hotel desk, and in a written questionnaire, with no change of form.
Politeness is carried entirely by the endings around it:
<code>…ですか。それとも…ですか</code> is polite,
<code>…? それとも…?</code> is casual, and the word itself is the same.

Two habits of use are worth copying. In speech the second alternative is usually
clipped to the minimum - nobody repeats the whole question - and a rising intonation
does the rest. And when the speaker is talking to themselves rather than asking
anyone, the frame becomes <code>…か、それとも…か</code> followed by a verb of
deliberation: <code>迷(まよ)っている</code>, <code>考(かんが)えている</code>,
<code>分(わ)からない</code>.

One caution about tone. Because `それとも` presents a fork, adding a second option to
someone else's statement can sound like a challenge:
「忘(わす)れたんですか。それともわざとですか」 is an accusation, not a question. That
is a matter of what you put in the second slot, not of the word itself, but the shape
sharpens it.

Finally, `それとも` marks the alternatives as genuinely exclusive - one or the other,
not both. Where both might be true, the sentence wants か, or a plain
<code>両方(りょうほう)</code>.

## Contrast

### それとも vs か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - か joins two options **inside one question**. `それとも` joins
**two whole questions**.

</div>

<pre>
昼(ひる)は和食(わしょく)<b>か</b>中華(ちゅうか)にしませんか。
Shall we have Japanese or Chinese for lunch?
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)は和食(わしょく)にしますか。<b>それとも</b>中華(ちゅうか)にしますか。
Shall we have Japanese for lunch? Or Chinese?
</pre>

The first is one question with two nouns in it; the second is two questions, put one
after the other. The second gives each option its own weight and its own moment,
which is why a waiter uses it and a friend in a hurry does not. The particle か is
also the only one of the two that can appear in a statement.

### それとも vs または・あるいは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `それとも` **asks**; <code>または</code> and `あるいは` **tell**.

</div>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)は一階(いっかい)<b>または</b>二階(にかい)にございます。
Reception is on the ground floor or the first floor.
</pre>

<pre>
受付(うけつけ)は一階(いっかい)ですか。<b>それとも</b>二階(にかい)ですか。
Is reception on the ground floor, or the first?
</pre>

The first sentence informs; the alternatives are both true as far as the reader is
concerned and no reply is expected. The second demands one. Swapping them produces
sentences that are not merely odd but incoherent - a notice that asks the reader a
question, and a question that lists.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)それとも明後日(あさって)伺(うかが)います。 → ✅ 明日(あした)<b>か</b>明後日(あさって)伺(うかが)います。
  A statement, so the choice has to be joined with か. `それとも` needs a question on
  both sides of it.

- ❌ 赤(あか)それとも青(あお)を選(えら)んでください。 → ✅ 赤(あか)<b>か</b>青(あお)を選(えら)んでください。
  A request is not a question either, even though it offers a choice. The listener is
  being told to pick, not being asked which they would pick.

- ❌ 飲(の)み物(もの)はビールですか。それともお酒(さけ)も飲(の)みますか。 → ✅ …ビールですか。それとも<b>ワインですか</b>。
  The two options have to be exclusive. If both could be true, the sentence needs か
  or <code>両方(りょうほう)</code> instead.

## Practice

**1.** 会議(かいぎ)は今週(こんしゅう)にしますか。____来週(らいしゅう)まで待(ま)ちますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それとも</b>来週(らいしゅう)まで待(ま)ちますか。

Two complete questions, and the listener is being asked to choose one. That is the
only environment this word lives in. Note that both halves keep their own ですか.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)それともカードでお願(ねが)いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)<b>か</b>カードでお願(ねが)いします。（or お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)ですか。<b>それとも</b>カードですか。）

As written it is a request, not a question, so `それとも` has nothing to attach to.
Either join the options with か and keep the request, or turn the whole thing into two
questions.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一人(ひとり)で行(い)きますか、誰(だれ)かと行(い)きますか。
(b) 一人(ひとり)で行(い)きますか。それとも誰(だれ)かと行(い)きますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) runs the two questions together and sounds like a form being filled in - brisk,
almost like ticking a box.

(b) puts a beat between them. `それとも` announces that a second, separate possibility
is coming, which gives the listener a moment to consider and makes the question feel
offered rather than processed. In service Japanese that pause is the whole point.

</details>

# ～ばかりか

**Meaning** · not only ... but even ... - and the second thing is further than you
would have expected

<code>ばかり</code> here is "merely", the same word as in
<code>三日(みっか)ばかり</code>. <code>ばかりか</code> says the case is not merely the
first thing, and then names a second that goes beyond it. What it adds over the
N3 pattern <code>だけでなく</code> is **escalation and feeling**: the speaker is
surprised, impressed or appalled, and the sentence expects the listener to be too.

## Formation

The clause in front is in plain, noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ばかりか | 止(と)まる → 止(と)まったばかりか |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ばかりか | 謝(あやま)らない → 謝(あやま)らないばかりか |
| い-adjective | plain form + ばかりか | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いばかりか |
| な-adjective | ～な + ばかりか | 失礼(しつれい)な → 失礼(しつれい)なばかりか |
| Noun | Noun + ばかりか | 大人(おとな) → 大人(おとな)ばかりか |
| Noun (written) | ～である + ばかりか | 専門家(せんもんか)であるばかりか |
| Second clause | …も／さえ／まで + predicate | …道路(どうろ)<b>まで</b>通(とお)れない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the second half must go **further out** than the first. Local fame
extending to the whole country works; national fame "extending" to one city does not.
If your second item is the smaller case, the two halves are in the wrong order.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この花(はな)は香(かお)りがいい<b>ばかりか</b>、冬(ふゆ)でも咲(さ)く。
This flower not only smells lovely - it even blooms in winter.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まった<b>ばかりか</b>、道路(どうろ)まで通(とお)れなくなった。
The typhoon stopped the trains, and even the roads became impassable.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は道(みち)を教(おし)えてくれた<b>ばかりか</b>、駅(えき)まで案内(あんない)してくれた。
She didn't just tell me the way - she walked me to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
その店員(てんいん)は謝(あやま)らない<b>ばかりか</b>、こちらのせいだと言(い)い出(だ)した。
Far from apologising, the assistant started saying it was our fault.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい校舎(こうしゃ)は教室(きょうしつ)が明(あか)るい<b>ばかりか</b>、廊下(ろうか)にも暖房(だんぼう)が入(はい)っている。
In the new school building the classrooms are bright, and there's even heating in the corridors.
</pre>

<pre>
祭(まつ)りの日(ひ)は、町内(ちょうない)の人(ひと)<b>ばかりか</b>、隣町(となりまち)からも見物(けんぶつ)客(きゃく)が押(お)し寄(よ)せる。
On the day of the festival, crowds pour in not just from the neighbourhood but from the next town.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は分(わ)かりにくい<b>ばかりか</b>、ときどき事実(じじつ)と違(ちが)っていることさえある。
His explanations are not merely hard to follow; now and then they are actually wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は八十(はちじゅう)を過(す)ぎても畑(はたけ)に出(で)る<b>ばかりか</b>、近所(きんじょ)の草(くさ)刈(か)りまで引(ひ)き受(う)けている。
Past eighty, my grandfather is still out in the fields - and he takes on the neighbours' mowing as well.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral in formality but not in temperature. <code>ばかりか</code> is usable in
speech and in writing, in a magazine article or in an account of a bad afternoon, and
it always brings an attitude with it. The listener is meant to react: *even that?*
That is the difference between it and the flat additives, and it is why the pattern
clusters around three feelings - indignation, admiration, and dismay at how far
something has spread.

The escalation is structural. Some element in the second clause almost always carries
も, さえ or まで, and まで and さえ are the strongest of the three because they mean
"even". Without one of them the sentence loses its point and reads like a plain list
with an oddly emphatic connector.

The restriction learners hit hardest: **the second clause states a fact.** It cannot
be a request, an instruction, an invitation or a statement of your own intention. Ask
someone to buy two things and <code>だけでなく</code> or
<code>に加(くわ)えて</code> is what you need. This is because
<code>ばかりか</code> is a comment on how the world turned out, not a way of
organising what you want.

<code>ばかりでなく</code> is the same word with the emotional charge removed - the
straightforward "not only A but also B" that behaves like
<code>だけでなく</code> and does accept a following request. If you find yourself
wanting the escalation without the surprise, that is the form to reach for.

A grammatical detail worth noticing: with a negative first clause -
<code>謝(あやま)らないばかりか</code> - the pattern comes very close to English
"far from apologising". This is one of its most natural uses, and it is
overwhelmingly negative in tone.

## Contrast

### ばかりか vs だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけでなく` is **neutral and flexible**;
<code>ばかりか</code> is **emphatic and descriptive only**.

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は学生(がくせい)<b>だけでなく</b>社会人(しゃかいじん)にも役(やく)に立(た)つ。
This dictionary is useful for working adults as well as students.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は学生(がくせい)<b>ばかりか</b>、翻訳者(ほんやくしゃ)まで使(つか)っているそうだ。
This dictionary is apparently used not just by students but even by translators.
</pre>

The first is a product description. The second is a claim with a raised eyebrow in
it. The practical difference is what each will tolerate afterwards: `だけでなく`
happily leads into <code>買(か)ってください</code> or
<code>見(み)てみましょう</code>, while <code>ばかりか</code> cannot end in a request
at all.

### ばかりか vs のみならず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same widening of scope. <code>のみならず</code> is **formal and
flat**; <code>ばかりか</code> is **speakable and loaded**.

</div>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)は沿岸部(えんがんぶ)<b>のみならず</b>内陸部(ないりくぶ)にも及(およ)んだ。
The damage extended to inland areas as well as the coast.
</pre>

<pre>
被害(ひがい)は沿岸部(えんがんぶ)<b>ばかりか</b>内陸部(ないりくぶ)にまで及(およ)んだ。
The damage reached not just the coast but even inland.
</pre>

Both are correct and both would pass in a written report. The first is what an
official summary says; the second is what a feature article says, because it invites
the reader to feel the extent of it. In conversation only the second survives.

### ばかりか vs どころか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ばかりか</code> **affirms** the first item and adds to it.
<code>どころか</code> **denies** it and replaces it.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>ばかりか</b>、風(かぜ)まで強(つよ)くなった。
It rained, and on top of that the wind got up as well.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>どころか</b>、一日中(いちにちじゅう)晴(は)れていた。
Far from raining, it was sunny all day.
</pre>

This is the pair that catches people out, because both translate with "far from" in
some sentences. Look at whether the first item is true. In the
<code>ばかりか</code> sentence it rained; in the <code>どころか</code> sentence it did
not. When the first clause is negative -
<code>謝(あやま)らないばかりか</code> - the two come close in feel, but even there
<code>ばかりか</code> is asserting that no apology was made, and building on it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 水(みず)ばかりか、パンも買(か)ってきてください。 → ✅ 水(みず)<b>だけでなく</b>、パンも買(か)ってきてください。
  The second clause cannot be a request. <code>ばかりか</code> reports; it does not
  ask.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>ばかりか</b>広(ひろ)い。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は静(しず)か<b>なばかりか</b>広(ひろ)い。
  <code>ばかり</code> follows a noun-modifying form, so な-adjectives keep their な.
  Verbs and い-adjectives attach in the plain form with nothing added.

- ❌ この歌手(かしゅ)は全国(ぜんこく)で有名(ゆうめい)な<b>ばかりか</b>、地元(じもと)でも人気(にんき)がある。 → ✅ この歌手(かしゅ)は地元(じもと)で人気(にんき)がある<b>ばかりか</b>、全国(ぜんこく)でも知(し)られている。
  The escalation runs outwards. Going from the whole country back to one town is a
  step *down*, and the "even" has nothing to do.

- ❌ 給料(きゅうりょう)が上(あ)がった<b>ばかりか</b>、残業(ざんぎょう)が増(ふ)えた。 → ✅ 給料(きゅうりょう)が上(あ)がった<b>が</b>、残業(ざんぎょう)も増(ふ)えた。
  A gain followed by a loss changes direction, and no additive pattern will carry
  that. Both halves have to point the same way.

## Practice

**1.** その子(こ)は自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けた____、弟(おとうと)の机(つくえ)まで整理(せいり)してくれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

片付(かたづ)けた<b>ばかりか</b>、弟(おとうと)の机(つくえ)まで整理(せいり)してくれた。

Two good deeds, the second further than anyone asked for, and まで marking it as the
surprise. The admiring tone is exactly what this pattern is for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)（ばかりか・だけでなく）卵(たまご)も買(か)ってきてくれる?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)<b>だけでなく</b>卵(たまご)も買(か)ってきてくれる?

The sentence is a request. <code>ばかりか</code> comments on what has happened and
cannot be followed by one, however emphatic you feel about the eggs.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He didn't just forget the meeting - he didn't even answer his phone."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は会議(かいぎ)を忘(わす)れた<b>ばかりか</b>、電話(でんわ)にさえ出(で)なかった。

"Didn't just … didn't even" is the shape this pattern was made for, and さえ carries
the second half. <code>のみならず</code> would be grammatical but would drain the
exasperation out of it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は親切(しんせつ)ばかりか、仕事(しごと)も速(はや)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は親切(しんせつ)<b>なばかりか</b>、仕事(しごと)も速(はや)い。

<code>親切(しんせつ)</code> is a な-adjective and needs its な before
<code>ばかり</code>. A plain noun would attach directly:
<code>新人(しんじん)ばかりか</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は返事(へんじ)をしないばかりか、席(せき)を立(た)ってしまった。
(b) 彼(かれ)は返事(へんじ)をするどころか、席(せき)を立(た)ってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are complaints, and both end the same way, but they treat the first clause
differently. (a) asserts it: he gave no answer, and on top of that he walked out. The
sentence stacks two facts.

(b) sets up the answer as something that might have been expected and then knocks it
down: far from replying, he got up and left. <code>どころか</code> rejects its first
item, so what follows feels like a correction of the listener's assumption rather
than an addition to the record.

</details>
