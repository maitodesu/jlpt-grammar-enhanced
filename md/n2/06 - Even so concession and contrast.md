# N2 - Even so: concession and contrast


---

# ～ものの

**Meaning** · but; although - the first clause is granted as true, and then the thing it led you to expect turns out not to have happened

`ものの` concedes a fact and immediately reports the shortfall. You applied, and did
not get in. You bought it, and never used it. The tone is not accusation; it is the
slightly deflated voice of someone reporting that reality did not follow through.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`ものの` sits at *mildly resigned* and *decidedly written*. `のに` in the same slot
would sound aggrieved and would sound like speech; `ものの` sounds like a report.

## Formation

`ものの` follows a plain form. It began as the noun <code>もの</code>, which is why a
noun needs `である` and a な-adjective needs `な` in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ものの | 通(かよ)う → 通(かよ)うものの |
| Verb (past) | plain past + ものの | 申(もう)し込(こ)んだ → 申(もう)し込(こ)んだものの |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ものの | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かないものの |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものの | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いものの |
| な-adjective | な + ものの | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)なものの |
| Noun | である + ものの | 新人(しんじん) → 新人(しんじん)であるものの |
| Emphatic noun / な-adjective | ではある + ものの | 不安(ふあん) → 不安(ふあん)ではあるものの |
| Fixed opener | とはいうものの + remark | - → とはいうものの、時間(じかん)がない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause after `ものの` **states what is actually so**. It is a
report, not a demand. A request, an invitation or an order does not go there:
❌<code>高(たか)いものの、買(か)ってください</code>. Use `が` or `けど` for that.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>とはいうものの</code> is the same word doing sentence-opener duty:
you have just stated something, and now you take part of it back. It is one of the
most useful written connectives at this level, and it is a shade less stiff than
`とはいえ`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
チケットは買(か)った<b>ものの</b>、けっきょく行(い)けなかった。
I bought the ticket, but in the end I couldn't go.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)い<b>ものの</b>、質(しつ)はあまりよくない。
It's cheap, but the quality isn't great.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)はした<b>ものの</b>、中身(なかみ)をきちんと読(よ)んでいなかった。
I did reply, though I hadn't read it properly.
</pre>

<pre>
免許(めんきょ)は持(も)っている<b>ものの</b>、もう十年(じゅうねん)運転(うんてん)していない。
I have a licence, but I haven't driven in ten years.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>ものの</b>、正直(しょうじき)なところ自信(じしん)はない。
I took the job on, but to be honest I'm not confident.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)が始(はじ)まった<b>ものの</b>、利用(りよう)している人(ひと)はまだごくわずかだ。
The new scheme has started, yet hardly anyone is using it.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は「検討(けんとう)する」と言(い)った<b>ものの</b>、あれから一(いっ)か月(げつ)何(なん)の連絡(れんらく)もない。
The department head said he'd look into it, but there's been no word for a month.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は駅(えき)から近(ちか)い<b>ものの</b>、案内(あんない)が少(すく)なくて迷(まよ)う客(きゃく)が多(おお)いらしい。
The venue is close to the station, but there are so few signs that a lot of visitors get lost.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)である<b>ものの</b>、彼女(かのじょ)の判断(はんだん)は誰(だれ)よりも落(お)ち着(つ)いていた。
She may be new, but her judgement was steadier than anyone's.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ものの` is a written-register word. It belongs in reports, articles, essays and
formal e-mail, and in speech it turns up mainly in prepared remarks - a presentation,
a speech at a leaving party - rather than in chatting. Nobody says
<code>チケットは買(か)ったものの…</code> to a friend over coffee; they say
<code>買(か)ったんだけどさ</code>. Learners who put `ものの` into casual conversation
sound like they are reading aloud.

There is no politeness switch on `ものの` itself. It is a conjunction in the middle of
the sentence, so politeness is decided at the end:
<code>安(やす)いものの、おすすめしません</code> is perfectly polite.

Emotionally it is cool. `のに` leaks disappointment; `ものの` merely records it. That
detachment is why `ものの` is so common in self-description - people use it to admit a
gap in their own situation without whining about it. The first clause is very often
in the past and very often something the speaker themselves did, which is why
<code>～たものの</code> is the shape you meet most.

The two clauses usually share a subject, but they need not:
<code>彼(かれ)は約束(やくそく)したものの、上司(じょうし)が許(ゆる)さなかった</code>
is fine. That freedom is worth remembering, because the close relative `ながらも` is
much fussier about it.

## Contrast

### ものの vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` is a **complaint**, and it belongs to speech. `ものの` is a
**report**, and it belongs to writing.

</div>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)も並(なら)んだ<b>のに</b>、売(う)り切(き)れだった。
I queued for three hours and it was sold out! (and I'm annoyed)
</pre>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)並(なら)んだ<b>ものの</b>、目当(めあ)ての品(しな)は買(か)えなかった。
Although I queued for three hours, I couldn't get what I wanted.
</pre>

There is also a shape difference that decides some sentences for you: `のに` can end a
sentence on its own - <code>来(き)てくれると言(い)ったのに。</code> trailing off is
a whole reproach. `ものの` cannot. It is a joint between two clauses and needs both of
them.

### ものの vs が・けれど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` and `けれど` join **any** two clauses. `ものの` needs a real
expectation to defeat.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)と申(もう)します<b>が</b>、山田(やまだ)さんはいらっしゃいますか。
My name is Tanaka - is Yamada in?
</pre>

That `が` is pure punctuation; nothing is being conceded, so `ものの` is impossible
there. Where something *is* conceded, both work and `ものの` is the more formal choice:

<pre>
説明(せつめい)は受(う)けた<b>ものの</b>、仕組(しく)みはよく分(わ)からなかった。
I did get an explanation, but I never really understood how it works.
</pre>

### ものの vs ながらも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are bookish concessives, but `ながらも` grants a **quality or
state** and keeps one subject; `ものの` grants a **whole event** and may change subject.

</div>

<pre>
小(ちい)さい<b>ながらも</b>、手入(てい)れの行(ゆ)き届(とど)いた庭(にわ)だった。
It was a small garden, but a beautifully kept one.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は始(はじ)まった<b>ものの</b>、資金(しきん)が足(た)りず途中(とちゅう)で止(と)まった。
The work did begin, but the money ran out and it stopped halfway.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences go wrong: the garden sentence needs a state, and the
building-site sentence needs an event with a past tense in front of the joint.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>だものの</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>であるものの</b>（or 学生(がくせい)ではあるものの）
  <code>もの</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it links with `である`, never `だ`.

- ❌ 便利(べんり)<b>だものの</b>、高(たか)い。 → ✅ 便利(べんり)<b>なものの</b>、高(たか)い。
  な-adjectives take `な`, exactly as they do before any other noun.

- ❌ 遠(とお)いものの、ぜひ来(き)てください。 → ✅ 遠(とお)いですが、ぜひ来(き)てください。
  The second clause of a `ものの` sentence reports a fact. Invitations and requests
  need `が` or `けど`.

- ❌ 待(ま)っていたものの。 → ✅ 待(ま)っていたのに。
  `ものの` cannot end a sentence. `のに` can, and that dangling `のに` is the reproach
  you were reaching for.

## Practice

**1.** 辞書(じしょ)は引(ひ)いた____、意味(いみ)がやはり分(わ)からなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

辞書(じしょ)は引(ひ)いた<b>ものの</b>、意味(いみ)がやはり分(わ)からなかった。

The first clause is granted - you really did look it up - and the second reports that
it did not deliver. A past-tense verb plus `ものの` is the pattern's commonest shape.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)に着(つ)いた（ものの・のに）、だれも来(き)ていなかった。ひどいよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)に着(つ)いた<b>のに</b>、だれも来(き)ていなかった。

The <code>ひどいよ</code> settles it: this is a spoken complaint, and complaints are
`のに` territory. `ものの` would report the same facts in a neutral written voice and
would clash with the grumble that follows.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The machine is old, but it still works without trouble."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

機械(きかい)は古(ふる)い<b>ものの</b>、今(いま)でも問題(もんだい)なく動(うご)いている。

An い-adjective attaches to `ものの` with no change at all. The sentence grants the age
of the machine and then denies the conclusion you would draw from it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 経験(けいけん)不足(ぶそく)だものの、彼(かれ)にやらせてみたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 経験(けいけん)不足(ぶそく)<b>ではあるものの</b>、彼(かれ)にやらせてみたい。

<code>経験(けいけん)不足(ぶそく)</code> is a noun, so it needs `である` - and
`ではある` is the natural choice here because the clause is a concession. `だものの`
does not exist.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 返信(へんしん)はもらった<b>ものの</b>、答(こた)えになっていなかった。
(b) 返信(へんしん)はもらった<b>けど</b>、答(こた)えになってなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say the same thing. (a) is written Japanese - a line from a report, a complaint
letter or a summary of what happened, cool in tone and structured as a concession
followed by its shortfall.

(b) is what you would actually say out loud to a colleague. `けど` is register-neutral
and carries no bookish weight, and the contracted <code>なってなかった</code> pins it
firmly to speech. Writing (a) in a chat message would read as oddly stiff.

</details>

# ～ことは～が

**Meaning** · granted, it is X - but; yes it happened, but - the speaker repeats the very word being offered, agrees to exactly that much, and no more

`ことは` sandwiched between two copies of the same predicate is a way of agreeing on
paper while withholding the endorsement. *Cheap? Yes, it's cheap. Now let me tell you
what's wrong with it.* The repetition is the whole trick: by echoing the other
person's word back, you accept the literal claim and quietly refuse everything that
usually travels with it.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
this one is *barely minding* and *register-neutral*. It is not a complaint; it is a
hedge, and it works equally well in a meeting and over a beer.

## Formation

The predicate appears twice: once in plain noun-modifying form before `ことは`, then
again, carrying the tense and politeness, before `が`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | V(plain) + ことは + V + が | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)むことは読(よ)むが |
| Verb (past) | V(た) + ことは + V(た) + が | 直(なお)った → 直(なお)ったことは直(なお)ったが |
| Verb (polite second copy) | V(plain) + ことは + V(ます) + が | 行(い)く → 行(い)くことは行(い)きますが |
| い-adjective | Adj + ことは + Adj + が | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いことは安(やす)いが |
| な-adjective | Adj な + ことは + Adj だ + が | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)なことは便利(べんり)だが |
| Casual | … + ことは … + けど | できる → できることはできるけど |
| Polite whole sentence | … + ことは … + んですが | 痛(いた)い → 痛(いた)いことは痛(いた)いんですが |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the **same word twice**, and the tense lives on the **second**
copy. ✅<code>直(なお)ったことは直(なお)った</code>,
❌<code>直(なお)ることは直(なお)ったが</code> is at best odd. Pick the tense you
mean and use it in both slots.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `が` half is obligatory in meaning even when it is left unsaid.
「おいしいことはおいしいけど…」 trailing off is a complete, and rather eloquent,
piece of damning with faint praise. The listener supplies the rest.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
安(やす)い<b>ことは</b>安(やす)い<b>が</b>、デザインが好(す)きではない。
It's cheap, sure, but I don't like the design.
</pre>

<pre>
読(よ)む<b>ことは</b>読(よ)んだ<b>が</b>、半分(はんぶん)も覚(おぼ)えていない。
I read it, yes, but I don't remember half of it.
</pre>

<pre>
直(なお)った<b>ことは</b>直(なお)った<b>けど</b>、また同(おな)じ音(おと)がしている。
It got fixed, technically, but it's making the same noise again.
</pre>

<pre>
便利(べんり)な<b>ことは</b>便利(べんり)だ<b>が</b>、使(つか)い方(かた)が複雑(ふくざつ)すぎる。
It's handy enough, but it's far too complicated to operate.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はな)せる<b>ことは</b>話(はな)せる<b>が</b>、敬語(けいご)になると急(きゅう)に自信(じしん)がなくなる。
I can speak it, after a fashion, but the moment honorifics come up my confidence goes.
</pre>

<pre>
痛(いた)い<b>ことは</b>痛(いた)い<b>んですが</b>、仕事(しごと)を休(やす)むほどではありません。
It does hurt, but not enough to take the day off.
</pre>

<pre>
その案(あん)に反対(はんたい)した<b>ことは</b>反対(はんたい)した<b>が</b>、代(か)わりの案(あん)を出(だ)せたわけでもない。
I did object to the proposal, but it's not as though I offered an alternative.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>ことは</b>参加(さんか)します<b>が</b>、当日(とうじつ)は夕方(ゆうがた)までしかいられません。
I will be attending, but on the day I can only stay until early evening.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This pattern is neutral as to formality - what changes is the ending. In writing and
in polite speech the second copy takes ます or です and the joint is `が`; in casual
speech both copies stay plain and the joint is `けど`. The first copy is always plain,
even in the politest version: ❌<code>安(やす)いですことは</code> is impossible,
because `ことは` needs a noun-modifying form in front of it.

What the pattern *does* to a conversation is worth more than its grammar. It is the
standard Japanese move for a lukewarm review, and it is polite precisely because it
starts by agreeing. Asked whether the new office is convenient, answering
<code>便利(べんり)なことは便利(べんり)です</code> and stopping there tells your
listener the answer is "not really" without your ever having said so.

It also softens a commitment you are not thrilled about.
<code>行(い)くことは行(い)きます</code> means you will turn up, and that the
speaker wants credit for it. Used about your own actions it can sound a little
grudging, so it fits a friend or a colleague better than it fits a client.

The negative is where learners slip. To concede a negative you do **not** double the
negative into <code>ないことはない</code> - that is a different pattern meaning "it's
not that I don't". Concede the negative the same way as any other predicate:
<code>知(し)らないことは知(し)らないが、調(しら)べればすぐ分(わ)かる</code>.

## Contrast

### ～ことは～が vs ～には～が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same move, but `には` is built on **verbs**, and it stresses that the
action *is carried out*. `ことは` covers verbs and adjectives alike.

</div>

<pre>
謝(あやま)る<b>には</b>謝(あやま)った<b>が</b>、納得(なっとく)はしていない。
I apologised, yes, but I'm not convinced.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい<b>ことは</b>新(あたら)しい<b>が</b>、中身(なかみ)は前(まえ)の版(ばん)と変(か)わらない。
It's new, all right, but the content is the same as the old edition.
</pre>

The second sentence resists `には`: adjective versions such as
<code>新(あたら)しいには新(あたら)しいが</code> are heard, but they are marginal, and
the safe frame for an adjective is `ことは`. Where both are possible, `には` sounds
slightly more spoken and slightly more grudging.

### ～ことは～が vs ものの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことは～が` concedes **the very predicate under discussion**.
`ものの` concedes **some other fact** and then reports a shortfall.

</div>

<pre>
集(あつ)まった<b>ことは</b>集(あつ)まった<b>が</b>、決(き)まったことは何(なに)もない。
We met, yes we met - and decided precisely nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)を開(ひら)いた<b>ものの</b>、結論(けつろん)は出(で)なかった。
We held a meeting, but no conclusion came out of it.
</pre>

The first sentence is arguing with somebody who has just said "well, we did meet".
The second is simply narrating. That is why `ものの` reads as a report and
`ことは～が` reads as a rejoinder.

### ～ことは～が vs たしかに～が

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たしかに` grants the point **fully** and then adds another. The
repetition frame grants it **only in the narrowest sense** and undercuts it.

</div>

<pre>
<b>たしかに</b>値段(ねだん)は高(たか)い<b>が</b>、それだけの価値(かち)はある。
It's certainly expensive, but it's worth every yen.
</pre>

<pre>
高(たか)い<b>ことは</b>高(たか)い<b>が</b>、それだけの価値(かち)があるとは思(おも)えない。
Expensive it is, but I can't see that it's worth it.
</pre>

`たしかに` is generous; the doubled predicate is stingy. Choosing the second when you
meant the first makes you sound as if you are grudging the other person their point.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 安(やす)い<b>ですことは</b>安(やす)いです。 → ✅ 安(やす)い<b>ことは</b>安(やす)いです。
  `ことは` needs a plain noun-modifying form in front of it. Politeness goes on the
  second copy only.

- ❌ 便利(べんり)<b>ことは</b>便利(べんり)だが → ✅ 便利(べんり)<b>なことは</b>便利(べんり)だが
  A な-adjective modifies <code>こと</code> with `な`, then reappears with `だ`.

- ❌ 来(き)た<b>ことは</b>来(く)るが、すぐ帰(かえ)った。 → ✅ 来(き)た<b>ことは</b>来(き)たが、すぐ帰(かえ)った。
  Both copies carry the same tense. Mixing them leaves the sentence pointing at two
  different times.

- ❌ 知(し)ら<b>ないことはない</b>が、説明(せつめい)できない。（intending "I don't know it, granted"） → ✅ 知(し)ら<b>ないことは</b>知(し)ら<b>ない</b>が、説明(せつめい)できない。
  <code>ないことはない</code> is the separate "it's not that I don't" pattern and means
  the opposite. To concede a negative, repeat the negative.

## Practice

**1.** 使(つか)える____使(つか)えるが、動(うご)きがとても遅(おそ)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

使(つか)える<b>ことは</b>使(つか)えるが、動(うご)きがとても遅(おそ)い。

The frame is the same word twice with `ことは` between. It concedes that the thing
technically works, and reserves everything else.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会場(かいじょう)を予約(よやく)した（ことは・ものの）予約(よやく)したが、人数(にんずう)が入(はい)りきらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会場(かいじょう)を予約(よやく)した<b>ことは</b>予約(よやく)したが、人数(にんずう)が入(はい)りきらない。

The verb is repeated, so only `ことは` fits the slot. `ものの` never takes a second copy
of the predicate - it would have to be <code>予約(よやく)したものの</code> with the
repetition deleted.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I understood the explanation, up to a point, but I couldn't repeat it to anyone."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

説明(せつめい)は分(わ)かった<b>ことは</b>分(わ)かったが、人(ひと)に説明(せつめい)し直(なお)すことはできなかった。

"Up to a point" is exactly what the doubled predicate does: it grants the bare fact of
understanding and withholds the useful part of it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 静(しず)かことは静(しず)かですが、駅(えき)から遠(とお)すぎます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 静(しず)か<b>なことは</b>静(しず)かですが、駅(えき)から遠(とお)すぎます。

<code>静(しず)か</code> is a な-adjective, and <code>こと</code> is a noun, so the
link is `な`. The second copy then takes です for politeness.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この案(あん)は<b>おもしろいことはおもしろい</b>ですね。
(b) この案(あん)は<b>たしかにおもしろい</b>ですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a polite way of not liking it. Repeating the adjective limits the praise to the
bare word - it is interesting in the sense that it is interesting, and nothing follows
from that. A Japanese listener hears the unspoken `が…` and starts preparing for the
objection.

(b) is straightforward praise. `たしかに` grants the point wholeheartedly, and if a `が`
comes afterwards it introduces a separate consideration rather than undercutting the
compliment.

</details>

# ～つつ

**Meaning** · while doing; and yet, knowing full well - two things held together in one clause, either at the same time or in open contradiction

`つつ` is the written cousin of `ながら`, and it has the same two jobs. Attached to a
verb of action it means *while*: two things going on at once. Attached to a verb of
knowing, thinking or feeling it means *although* - and then it says something sharper
than plain "although", because the knowledge is built into the sentence. The speaker
knew, and did it anyway.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
concessive `つつ` is *quietly self-reproachful* and *firmly literary*. It is the
concession you write about your own weakness, not one you throw at somebody else.

## Formation

`つつ` attaches to the ます-stem of a verb, and to nothing else. It has no adjective
form and no noun form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (simultaneous) | ます-stem + つつ | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)きつつ |
| Verb (concessive) | ます-stem + つつ | 知(し)る → 知(し)りつつ |
| Verb (concessive, emphatic) | ます-stem + つつも | 思(おも)う → 思(おも)いつつも |
| する-verb | stem + しつつ | 考慮(こうりょ)する → 考慮(こうりょ)しつつ |
| Verb (negative) | - | not available; use ないながらも or のに |
| い-adjective, な-adjective, noun | - | not available; use ながらも or ものの |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **one subject, two clauses.** The person doing the `つつ` verb and
the person doing the main verb must be the same. ❌
<code>弟(おとうと)が泣(な)きつつ、母(はは)がなだめた</code> - two people, so this
needs `のに` or a `て`-clause instead.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `つつ` is not `つつある`. Same three syllables, unrelated job:
<code>回復(かいふく)しつつある</code> means "is in the process of recovering" and is a
predicate that ends the sentence. `つつ` on its own is a joint in the middle of one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
悪(わる)いと知(し)り<b>つつ</b>、うそをついてしまった。
Knowing it was wrong, I lied anyway.
</pre>

<pre>
音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)き<b>つつ</b>、資料(しりょう)に目(め)を通(とお)した。
I looked over the documents while listening to music.
</pre>

<pre>
体(からだ)に悪(わる)いと思(おも)い<b>つつ</b>、夜中(よなか)にラーメンを食(た)べてしまう。
I know it's bad for me, and I still end up eating ramen in the middle of the night.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)がなければと焦(あせ)り<b>つつも</b>、足(あし)はまったく動(うご)かなかった。
Frantic as I was to hurry, my legs simply wouldn't move.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)し<b>つつも</b>、最後(さいご)には書類(しょるい)に署名(しょめい)した。
He objected, and yet in the end he signed the papers.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)し<b>つつ</b>、一人(ひとり)で夜(よる)の川沿(かわぞ)いを歩(ある)いた。
I walked along the river at night by myself, turning over old memories.
</pre>

<pre>
無理(むり)だと知(し)り<b>つつも</b>、彼(かれ)は最後(さいご)の一人(ひとり)まで説得(せっとく)をあきらめなかった。
He knew it was hopeless, and still he kept trying to talk every last one of them round.
</pre>

<pre>
環境(かんきょう)への影響(えいきょう)を考慮(こうりょ)し<b>つつ</b>、開発(かいはつ)を進(すす)めてまいります。
We will proceed with the development while giving due regard to the environmental impact.
</pre>

<pre>
部下(ぶか)の意見(いけん)を聞(き)き<b>つつ</b>、最終(さいしゅう)的(てき)な判断(はんだん)は自分(じぶん)で下(くだ)すつもりだ。
I intend to hear my staff out and still make the final call myself.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`つつ` is written Japanese. It lives in essays, novels, reports, minutes and formal
addresses, and in speech it appears only where the speaker is being deliberately
formal - a company announcement, a wedding speech, a lecture. Saying
<code>ラーメンを食(た)べつつ</code> to a friend is not wrong so much as comic; the
spoken word is `ながら`.

The concessive use is narrower than it looks. It nearly always attaches to a verb of
awareness - <code>知(し)る</code>, <code>思(おも)う</code>,
<code>分(わ)かる</code>, <code>感(かん)じる</code> - and nearly always describes
the speaker acting against their own better judgement. That is why so many `つつ`
sentences end in <code>～てしまう</code>. The feeling is rueful rather than angry:
you are not blaming anyone, you are confessing.

`つつも` is the same thing with the contradiction underlined. Where bare `つつ` before a
verb of thinking could in principle still be read as "while", `つつも` can only be
concessive, so writers reach for it whenever the sentence might otherwise be
ambiguous.

One stylistic habit worth copying: because `つつ` is a written form, it pairs
comfortably with other written forms - <code>である</code>, `ぬ`, `～まい`,
<code>まいります</code>. Mixing it into a sentence that is otherwise casual produces a
jarring seam.

## Contrast

### つつ vs ながら (the "while" sense)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical grammar, different register. `ながら` is what you say;
`つつ` is what you write.

</div>

<pre>
テレビを見(み)<b>ながら</b>晩(ばん)ごはんを食(た)べた。
I ate dinner watching television.
</pre>

<pre>
各国(かっこく)の動向(どうこう)を見(み)<b>つつ</b>、対応(たいおう)を決(き)めていく。
We will decide how to respond while watching what other countries do.
</pre>

The subject matter follows the register. Concrete, everyday, physical pairings go to
`ながら`; abstract, deliberative ones go to `つつ`. Swapping them in either direction
sounds like the wrong suit for the occasion.

### つつも vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つつも` needs **one subject** and a **verb of awareness**. `のに`
takes anything, and points the finger outward.

</div>

<pre>
危(あぶ)ないと分(わ)かり<b>つつも</b>、彼(かれ)は川(かわ)に飛(と)び込(こ)んだ。
He knew it was dangerous, and jumped into the river anyway.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あぶ)ないと言(い)った<b>のに</b>、彼(かれ)は川(かわ)に飛(と)び込(こ)んだ。
I told him it was dangerous, and he jumped in anyway.
</pre>

In the first sentence the knowledge is *his*: it is a portrait of a man overriding
himself. In the second the warning is *mine* and the annoyance is mine too. That
one-subject rule is what makes `つつも` unsuitable for complaining about other people.

### つつ vs つつある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つつ` joins two clauses. `つつある` **ends** a clause and means
"is in the middle of becoming".

</div>

<pre>
景気(けいき)は回復(かいふく)し<b>つつある</b>。
The economy is in the process of recovering.
</pre>

<pre>
景気(けいき)の回復(かいふく)を見守(みまも)り<b>つつ</b>、投資(とうし)の時期(じき)を考(かんが)える。
I'll think about when to invest while keeping an eye on the recovery.
</pre>

The test is simple: if `ある` follows, it is the progressive; if a comma and a second
clause follow, it is the joint. Note also that `つつある` describes gradual change and
so refuses instantaneous verbs - ❌<code>死(し)につつある</code> is not how you say
someone is dying; that is <code>死(し)にかけている</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>つつ</b> → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>つつ</b>
  `つつ` attaches to the ます-stem, never to the dictionary form.

- ❌ 給料(きゅうりょう)は安(やす)い<b>つつ</b>、仕事(しごと)は楽(たの)しい。 → ✅ 給料(きゅうりょう)は安(やす)い<b>ながらも</b>、仕事(しごと)は楽(たの)しい。
  There is no adjective form of `つつ`. `ながらも`, `ものの` and `が` all take
  adjectives; `つつ` does not.

- ❌ 妹(いもうと)が泣(な)き<b>つつ</b>、父(ちち)が慰(なぐさ)めた。 → ✅ 妹(いもうと)が泣(な)いているので、父(ちち)が慰(なぐさ)めた。
  Two different subjects. `つつ` binds both clauses to one person.

- ❌ 人口(じんこう)は減(へ)り<b>つつ</b>。 → ✅ 人口(じんこう)は減(へ)り<b>つつある</b>。
  `つつ` cannot close a sentence. If you mean "is decreasing", you need the `ある`.

## Practice

**1.** 遅(おく)れると知(し)り____、連絡(れんらく)を忘(わす)れてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

遅(おく)れると知(し)り<b>つつ</b>（or 知(し)り<b>つつも</b>）、連絡(れんらく)を忘(わす)れてしまった。

A verb of awareness plus `つつ` is the concessive shape: the speaker knew and failed
to act on the knowledge. The <code>～てしまった</code> ending is the give-away that this
is a confession, not a "while".

</details>

**2.** Choose: 技術(ぎじゅつ)は年々(ねんねん)進歩(しんぽ)し（つつ・つつある）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

技術(ぎじゅつ)は年々(ねんねん)進歩(しんぽ)し<b>つつある</b>。

The sentence ends here, and only `つつある` can end a sentence. Bare `つつ` would leave
the reader waiting for the second clause that never arrives.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in written style: "While respecting the opinions of local residents, the city will continue the survey."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

市(し)は住民(じゅうみん)の意見(いけん)を尊重(そんちょう)し<b>つつ</b>、調査(ちょうさ)を続(つづ)けていく。

An abstract, institutional "while" is exactly what `つつ` is for. `ながら` here would
not be wrong, but it would read as a press release written by someone in a hurry.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 忙(いそが)しいつつも、毎日(まいにち)三十分(さんじゅっぷん)は走(はし)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 忙(いそが)し<b>いながらも</b>、毎日(まいにち)三十分(さんじゅっぷん)は走(はし)っている。

`つつ` has no adjective form. The concessive that does take い-adjectives is
`ながらも`; <code>忙(いそが)しいものの</code> would also work in writing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 反省(はんせい)し<b>つつ</b>、同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)をくり返(かえ)している。
(b) 反省(はんせい)している<b>のに</b>、同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)をくり返(かえ)している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a written, self-directed admission. The regret and the repeated failure belong
to one person, and `つつ` presents them as a single contradictory act - I am sorry
about it and I keep doing it. It reads like a diary entry.

(b) is looser and more likely to be about somebody else. `のに` sets the two facts side
by side and expresses surprise or irritation that they coexist, and it does not
require the same subject at all - said of a colleague, (b) is a criticism, whereas (a)
could only be a confession.

</details>

# ～たって

**Meaning** · even if; even though; no matter how - the spoken, shrugging version of `～ても`

`たって` says exactly what `ても` says and says it in a T-shirt. It concedes a
condition and dismisses it in the same breath: *fine, suppose it rains - we're playing
anyway.* Because it only lives in casual speech, it carries the attitude of casual
speech with it, which is usually defiance, impatience, or a wave of the hand.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`たって` is *audibly impatient* and *as informal as the group gets*. `とも`, later in
this batch, means the same thing at the opposite end of both scales.

## Formation

Take the plain **past** form and swap the final `た` for `たって`. A verb whose past
ends in `だ` gives `だって`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (た) | past た → たって | 走(はし)った → 走(はし)ったって |
| Verb (だ) | past だ → だって | 呼(よ)んだ → 呼(よ)んだって |
| Verb (negative) | ～なく + たって | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かなくたって |
| い-adjective | ～く + たって | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)くたって |
| い-adjective (negative) | ～くなく + たって | 安(やす)くない → 安(やす)くなくたって |
| な-adjective | stem + だって | 下手(へた) → 下手(へた)だって |
| Noun | Noun + だって | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもだって |
| Permission | ～たって + いい | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んだっていい |
| With an intensifier | いくら／どんなに + ～たって | - → いくら言(い)ったって |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - build it from the **past tense**, not the dictionary form:
✅<code>飲(の)んだって</code>, ❌<code>飲(の)むたって</code>. If you can say the past
tense, you can say the `たって`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - nouns and な-adjectives come out as `だって`, which is the same shape as the
"even X" particle in <code>子(こ)どもだって知(し)っている</code>. That is not a
coincidence and not a problem: they are the casual counterpart of `でも`, and Japanese
speakers do not keep them apart either.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
急(いそ)い<b>だって</b>、もう間(ま)に合(あ)わないよ。
Even if we hurry, we're not going to make it now.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)く<b>たって</b>、こんな服(ふく)はいらない。
I don't want clothes like this however cheap they are.
</pre>

<pre>
いくら呼(よ)ん<b>だって</b>、返事(へんじ)なんかしてくれない。
No matter how much you call him, he won't answer.
</pre>

<pre>
謝(あやま)らなく<b>たって</b>いいよ。君(きみ)のせいじゃないんだから。
You don't have to apologise. It isn't your fault.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>たって</b>、試合(しあい)はやるらしいよ。
Apparently the match is going ahead even if it rains tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)から走(はし)っ<b>たって</b>、終電(しゅうでん)には乗(の)れないでしょ。
Even if we run now we're not catching the last train, are we.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに練習(れんしゅう)し<b>たって</b>、あの人(ひと)に勝(か)てる気(き)がしない。
However much I practise, I can't see myself beating him.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らし<b>だって</b>、それくらいの料理(りょうり)はちゃんと作(つく)るさ。
I may live on my own, but I can manage cooking on that level, thanks.
</pre>

<pre>
文句(もんく)を言(い)っ<b>たって</b>もう決(き)まったことなんだから、今回(こんかい)はあきらめようよ。
Complaining won't change what's already been decided, so let's let this one go.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たって` is spoken Japanese and nothing else. It belongs in conversation, in chat
messages, in dialogue in a novel and in the chattier kind of blog post. It does not
belong in a report, an examination answer, a business e-mail or a news article, where
`ても` is the neutral form and `としても` or `にせよ` the formal ones. Putting `たって`
into a written assignment is the single most likely way to misuse it.

Within speech it is register-neutral as to age and gender - everybody says it - but it
is not tone-neutral. It leans towards brushing something off, so it pairs naturally
with `いくら`, `どんなに`, `なんか`, `よ` and `さ`, and with rhetorical questions. Said
to a superior it can sound flippant even when the content is polite, which is why
<code>急(いそ)いだって無理(むり)です</code> sounds worse than
<code>急(いそ)いでも無理(むり)です</code> despite meaning the same.

Two very common fixed shapes are worth learning whole. <code>～たっていい</code> is the
casual <code>～てもいい</code>: <code>先(さき)に帰(かえ)ったっていいよ</code>,
"you can go home first if you like." And `なんたって`, a worn-down
<code>何(なん)と言(い)ったって</code>, means "more than anything" -
<code>なんたって安(やす)いのが一番(いちばん)だ</code>.

Do not confuse the register with the strength. `たって` is casual, but a casual
concession can be very firm: <code>何(なに)を言(い)われたってやめない</code> is
not a soft sentence.

## Contrast

### たって vs ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning; `ても` is neutral and works everywhere, `たって`
is casual and works only in speech.

</div>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)を読(よ)ん<b>でも</b>、使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)かりませんでした。
Even after reading the instructions I couldn't work out how to use it.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)読(よ)ん<b>だって</b>、使(つか)い方(かた)なんか分(わ)かんないよ。
Reading the instructions isn't going to tell you how to use it.
</pre>

Notice what travels with the choice: the second sentence has also dropped its particle
and contracted <code>分(わ)からない</code>. Casual grammar arrives in convoys. A
`たって` sitting inside an otherwise polite sentence is the seam that gives a learner
away.

### たって vs the quotative って after a past tense

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the sentence **stops** there, or <code>言(い)う</code>／<code>聞(き)く</code> follows, it is
hearsay. If a **second clause** follows, it is the concession.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)、もう出(で)かけ<b>たって</b>。
He says he's already gone out.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)がもう出(で)かけ<b>たって</b>、待(ま)っていれば戻(もど)ってくるよ。
Even if he's already gone out, he'll come back if you wait.
</pre>

The syllables are identical; only the shape of the sentence tells them apart. The
hearsay one is <code>～と言(い)っていた</code> worn down to `って`, and it ends the
sentence with a falling tone. The concessive one is a joint and demands a main clause
after it.

### たって vs たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たら` says the result **follows**; `たって` says it **doesn't**.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たら</b>、涼(すず)しくなった。
I opened the window and it got cooler.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)け<b>たって</b>、涼(すず)しくならないよ。
Opening the window isn't going to cool it down.
</pre>

Both are built on the past tense, and beginners who have just learned `たら` regularly
hear `たって` as a variant of it. They are opposites: `たら` is a conditional that
delivers, `たって` is a conditional that is refused.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>たって</b> → ✅ 行(い)っ<b>たって</b>
  Build it from the past tense. The dictionary form never takes `たって`.

- ❌ 高(たか)い<b>たって</b> → ✅ 高(たか)く<b>たって</b>
  い-adjectives go through the `く` form first, exactly as they do for `ても`
  (<code>高(たか)くても</code>).

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>でたって</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>だって</b>
  Nouns and な-adjectives take `だって` directly; there is no `で` in front of it.

- ❌ (in a report) 多少(たしょう)費用(ひよう)がかかっ<b>たって</b>、安全(あんぜん)を優先(ゆうせん)すべきである。 → ✅ 多少(たしょう)費用(ひよう)がかかっ<b>ても</b>、安全(あんぜん)を優先(ゆうせん)すべきである。
  The sentence is written in `である` style, and `たって` cannot appear there. Register
  has to match end to end.

## Practice

**1.** いくら待(ま)っ____、バスは来(こ)ないよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いくら待(ま)っ<b>たって</b>、バスは来(こ)ないよ。

<code>いくら</code> sets up a "no matter how much", and in casual speech that is
completed by `たって`. The past-tense stem is <code>待(ま)った</code>, so the joint
is <code>待(ま)ったって</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)は、多少(たしょう)遅(おく)れ（たって・ても）必(かなら)ず提出(ていしゅつ)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

多少(たしょう)遅(おく)れ<b>ても</b>必(かなら)ず提出(ていしゅつ)してください。

The sentence ends in <code>ください</code>, so it is polite, and a polite sentence
takes `ても`. `たって` would clash with the request form and sound offhand about
somebody else's deadline.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "You don't have to come early - I'll be here."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

早(はや)く来(こ)なく<b>たって</b>いいよ。僕(ぼく)がここにいるから。

The negative goes through <code>～なく</code> before `たって`, and
<code>～たっていい</code> is the casual <code>～てもいい</code> that grants permission.
Note <code>来(こ)ない</code>: <code>来(く)る</code> takes こ- in the negative.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るたって、傘(かさ)はいらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>たって</b>、傘(かさ)はいらない。

<code>降(ふ)る</code> is a う-verb whose past is <code>降(ふ)った</code>, so the
concessive is <code>降(ふ)ったって</code>. Attaching `たって` to the dictionary form
is the commonest error with this pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 試(ため)し<b>ても</b>同(おな)じだと思(おも)います。
(b) 試(ため)し<b>たって</b>同(おな)じだよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral, polite opinion. `ても` carries no attitude of its own, and
<code>と思(おも)います</code> softens it further - this is something you could say to
a client.

(b) is the same content thrown away over your shoulder. `たって` plus the plain ending
and `よ` makes it dismissive: don't waste your time. Between friends that is friendly
bluntness; said upwards at work it would sound as though you could not be bothered.

</details>

# ～としても

**Meaning** · even if; even supposing - grant the proposition for the sake of argument, and show that it changes nothing

`としても` is the concession of debate. The `と` is the quoting `と`: you take a whole
proposition, hold it up as a hypothesis - *suppose that is so* - and then knock down
the conclusion somebody wanted to draw from it. Nothing is claimed to be true. The
clause in front is a supposition, and often one the speaker does not believe.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`としても` is *unemotional* and *neutral to formal*. It is the pattern that argues
rather than complains, which is why it fills discussion, analysis and negotiation.

## Formation

`としても` follows a plain form. Nouns and な-adjectives need `だ`, because `と` here is
quoting a complete sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + としても | 断(ことわ)る → 断(ことわ)るとしても |
| Verb (past) | plain past + としても | 失敗(しっぱい)した → 失敗(しっぱい)したとしても |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + としても | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かないとしても |
| い-adjective | plain form + としても | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いとしても |
| な-adjective | stem + だ + としても | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)だとしても |
| Noun | Noun + だ + としても | うそ → うそだとしても |
| Formal written | Noun + である + としても | 事実(じじつ) → 事実(じじつ)であるとしても |
| Marked as hypothetical | 仮(かり)に／たとえ + … + としても | - → 仮(かり)に勝(か)ったとしても |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だ` is **required** after a noun. ✅<code>うそだとしても</code>,
❌<code>うそとしても</code>. Dropping it produces the unrelated `として` "in the capacity
of", so <code>学生(がくせい)としても</code> means "as a student, too" - a different
sentence entirely.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the past tense inside `としても` does not push the whole sentence into the
past. <code>失敗(しっぱい)したとしても</code> means "even if it should fail", looking
forward: the `た` marks the hypothetical event as completed *relative to* the main
clause, not relative to now.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
安(やす)い<b>としても</b>、今月(こんげつ)は買(か)えない。
Even if it's cheap, I can't buy it this month.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)った<b>としても</b>、大会(たいかい)は行(おこな)われます。
The tournament will go ahead even if it snows tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
うそだ<b>としても</b>、なかなかよくできた話(はなし)だ。
Even supposing it's a lie, it's a well-made story.
</pre>

<pre>
仮(かり)に全員(ぜんいん)が賛成(さんせい)した<b>としても</b>、予算(よさん)が足(た)りない。
Even if everyone were in favour, there isn't the budget.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ失敗(しっぱい)した<b>としても</b>、挑戦(ちょうせん)した価値(かち)はあると思(おも)う。
Even if it fails, I think it will have been worth attempting.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)だ<b>としても</b>、証拠(しょうこ)がなければ逮捕(たいほ)はできない。
Even if he is the culprit, there can be no arrest without evidence.
</pre>

<pre>
この数字(すうじ)が正(ただ)しい<b>としても</b>、去年(きょねん)と比(くら)べて大(おお)きな改善(かいぜん)とは言(い)えない。
Even granting that these figures are right, they are hardly a big improvement on last year.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)すぐ工事(こうじ)を始(はじ)めた<b>としても</b>、完成(かんせい)は再来年(さらいねん)の春(はる)になるでしょう。
Even if the work started this minute, it wouldn't be finished until the spring of the year after next.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)が決(き)まった<b>としても</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)までにやるべきことが山(やま)ほど残(のこ)っている。
Even once the exchange is confirmed, there'll still be a mountain of things to do before departure.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`としても` is register-neutral, and it survives in polite and plain style alike. What
marks it is not formality but *stance*: it is analytical. The speaker is reasoning
about a possibility, not reacting to a fact, so the pattern has almost no emotional
colour of its own. That makes it safe in situations where `のに` or `くせに` would be
rude, and it is the reason `としても` is so common in meetings, legal writing, academic
prose and any argument that has to stay civil.

It pairs naturally with the hypothetical adverbs. <code>仮(かり)に</code> is the
formal one, `たとえ` the neutral one, `もし` the loosest; all three simply announce in
advance that a supposition is coming, and none of them changes the meaning.

There is a diplomatic use worth knowing. Prefacing a refusal with `としても` lets you
decline without ever disputing the other side's premise:
<code>ご指摘(してき)のとおりだとしても、今回(こんかい)は見送(みおく)らせていただきます</code>.
You have not called anyone wrong; you have simply said it does not settle the matter.

In casual speech the shorter `～たって` usually does this job, and `としても` sounds a
touch bookish among friends. The one place it stays fully colloquial is with a
question word: <code>何(なに)があったとしても味方(みかた)するよ</code> is ordinary
warm speech.

## Contrast

### としても vs ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` covers both the real and the hypothetical. `としても` is
**always** a supposition, and says so out loud.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っている。
I run every morning even when it rains.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>としても</b>、予定(よてい)は変(か)えない。
Even if it rains tomorrow, I'm not changing the plan.
</pre>

The first sentence reports a habit and a real recurring fact - `としても` cannot go
there, because there is nothing to suppose. The second is a hypothesis about tomorrow,
and both forms would work; `としても` makes the "let us suppose" explicit.

### としても vs にしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にしても` grants something **already on the table**; `としても`
supposes something **not yet established**.

</div>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しかった<b>にしても</b>、一言(ひとこと)連絡(れんらく)するべきだった。
Busy or not - and I accept you were - you should have sent a word.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しかった<b>としても</b>、連絡(れんらく)できない理由(りゆう)にはならない。
Even supposing you were busy, that is no reason not to get in touch.
</pre>

Both are objections, but the first accepts the excuse as a fact and objects anyway,
while the second treats it as a claim that has not been proved. The second is
therefore the colder of the two, and in an argument it is heard as scepticism.

### としても vs としたら・とすれば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same supposition, opposite second half. `としたら` asks **what
follows**; `としても` says **nothing follows**.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)を変(か)える<b>としたら</b>、いつまでに決(き)めればいいですか。
If we're going to change the venue, by when do we need to decide?
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)を変(か)える<b>としても</b>、日程(にってい)はこのままでいきます。
Even if we change the venue, the dates stay as they are.
</pre>

The `も` is doing all the work. `としたら` opens the door to the consequence; the extra
`も` slams it. If you can insert "then what?" after the clause, you want `としたら`; if
you can insert "so what?", you want `としても`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>としても</b>許(ゆる)されない。（intending "even if he is a student"） → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>だとしても</b>許(ゆる)されない。
  Without `だ` the phrase is `として` "in the role of", plus `も` - "as a student too".
  The `だ` is what makes it a supposition.

- ❌ 不便(ふべん)<b>としても</b> → ✅ 不便(ふべん)<b>だとしても</b>
  な-adjectives need the same `だ` as nouns, because `と` is quoting a whole sentence.

- ❌ 今(いま)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>としても</b>、出(で)かけた。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>のに</b>、出(で)かけた。
  You cannot suppose what you can see. For a fact already in front of you, use `のに`
  or `ても`.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)来(く)る<b>としても</b>、何時(なんじ)に着(つ)きますか。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)来(く)る<b>としたら</b>、何時(なんじ)に着(つ)きますか。
  A question about the consequence needs `としたら`. `としても` announces that the
  consequence does not arise, so it cannot be followed by a question about it.

## Practice

**1.** 仮(かり)にその話(はなし)が本当(ほんとう)だ____、今(いま)さらどうにもならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

仮(かり)にその話(はなし)が本当(ほんとう)<b>だとしても</b>、今(いま)さらどうにもならない。

<code>仮(かり)に</code> flags a supposition, and the noun-plus-`だ` before `としても`
completes it. The main clause then refuses the conclusion: true or not, it is too
late.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 参加(さんか)する（としたら・としても）、申(もう)し込(こ)みはどこですればいいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

参加(さんか)する<b>としたら</b>、申(もう)し込(こ)みはどこですればいいですか。

The second half asks what follows from the supposition, so the door has to stay open.
`としても` would mean "even if I take part", which cannot be followed by a question
about how to take part.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if the price came down, I still wouldn't buy a car that size."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

値段(ねだん)が下(さ)がった<b>としても</b>、あの大(おお)きさの車(くるま)は買(か)わないと思(おも)う。

The price coming down is a hypothesis, so the past tense inside `としても` looks
forward rather than back. The main clause denies the expected consequence of a
cheaper price.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 事実(じじつ)としても、こんな書(か)き方(かた)はよくない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 事実(じじつ)<b>だとしても</b>、こんな書(か)き方(かた)はよくない。
（Formally: 事実(じじつ)<b>であるとしても</b>）

<code>事実(じじつ)としても</code> reads as "as a fact, too", which is not the intended
meaning. A noun needs `だ` - or `である` in written style - to become a supposition.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 知(し)らなかった<b>にしても</b>、責任(せきにん)は残(のこ)る。
(b) 知(し)らなかった<b>としても</b>、責任(せきにん)は残(のこ)る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) accepts that you genuinely did not know. The speaker grants the excuse as true and
argues that it does not remove the responsibility - firm, but not doubting you.

(b) leaves the not-knowing as an untested hypothesis: *even supposing you didn't
know*. Because it declines to concede the fact, it can sound sceptical, and in a
dispute it is the more adversarial of the two. Where you want to sound fair-minded,
`にしても` is the safer pick.

</details>

# ～とも

**Meaning** · even if; no matter what - a literary, emphatic "whatever happens, this stands"

`とも` is `ても` in formal dress. It concedes a condition and then declares that the
outcome is fixed regardless, and because it is a written and oratorical form it lands
much harder than `ても` does. Declarations, vows, mission statements, speeches and the
narration of novels are its natural home.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`とも` is *emphatic to the point of defiance* and *the most formal member of the
group*. Set it beside `たって`, which means the same thing in a T-shirt, and the whole
axis is visible in one pair.

## Formation

Verbs use the **volitional**; い-adjectives use the `く` form. Nouns and な-adjectives
go through `であろう`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | volitional (よ)う + とも | 進(すす)む → 進(すす)もうとも |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～よう + とも | 負(ま)ける → 負(ま)けようとも |
| Verb (irregular) | しよう／こよう + とも | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ようとも |
| Verb (passive) | ～(ら)れよう + とも | 言(い)われる → 言(い)われようとも |
| Verb (negative) | ～なく + とも | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らなくとも |
| い-adjective | ～く + とも | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)くとも |
| な-adjective | stem + であろう + とも | 困難(こんなん) → 困難(こんなん)であろうとも |
| Noun | Noun + であろう + とも | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもであろうとも |
| With an intensifier | どんなに／たとえ／誰(だれ)が … + とも | - → たとえ負(ま)けようとも |
| Fossilised adverbs | 少(すく)なくとも／遅(おそ)くとも | - → 遅(おそ)くとも金曜日(きんようび) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - verbs take the **volitional**, not the dictionary form:
✅<code>降(ふ)ろうとも</code>, ❌<code>降(ふ)るとも</code>. This is the one shape
learners get wrong, and it is worth drilling on its own: 書(か)こうとも,
見(み)ようとも, しようとも, 来(こ)ようとも.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> "at least" and
<code>遅(おそ)くとも</code> "at the latest" are this `とも` frozen into single words.
They are ordinary vocabulary and are used freely in speech, which is why you meet them
long before you meet the pattern that built them.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どんなに高(たか)<b>くとも</b>、これだけは買(か)いたい。
However expensive it is, this is the one thing I want to buy.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)が起(お)ころ<b>うとも</b>、私(わたし)はもう驚(おどろ)かない。
Whatever happens now, nothing surprises me any more.
</pre>

<pre>
遅(おそ)<b>くとも</b>来週(らいしゅう)までにはご返事(へんじ)いたします。
I will reply by next week at the latest.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)が何(なん)と言(い)お<b>うとも</b>、私(わたし)の気持(きも)ちは変(か)わらない。
Whatever anyone says, my mind is not going to change.
</pre>

<pre>
たとえ失敗(しっぱい)しよ<b>うとも</b>、後悔(こうかい)はしないだろう。
Even if it ends in failure, I don't imagine I'll regret it.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ろ<b>うとも</b>風(かぜ)が吹(ふ)こ<b>うとも</b>、式(しき)は予定(よてい)どおり行(おこな)います。
Come rain or wind, the ceremony will take place as scheduled.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに困難(こんなん)であろ<b>うとも</b>、この計画(けいかく)を中止(ちゅうし)するつもりはありません。
However difficult it proves, we have no intention of abandoning this plan.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りに何(なん)と思(おも)われよ<b>うとも</b>、彼女(かのじょ)は自分(じぶん)の信(しん)じる道(みち)を進(すす)んでいった。
Whatever those around her might think, she went on down the road she believed in.
</pre>

<pre>
価格(かかく)が高(たか)<b>くとも</b>、品質(ひんしつ)に見合(みあ)うものであれば客(きゃく)は離(はな)れない。
Customers do not desert a product that is worth its price, however high that price is.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`とも` is a written and ceremonial form. In everyday conversation it sounds theatrical,
and a learner who says <code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ろうとも行(い)きます</code> to a
colleague will get a smile - it is the register of a war film. The neutral spoken
equivalents are `ても` and, more casually, `たって`.

Where it genuinely belongs: speeches and toasts, company statements, editorials,
literary narration, song lyrics, and the fixed adverbs. It is also alive in a certain
kind of self-consciously firm business Japanese -
<code>いかなる事情(じじょう)があろうとも例外(れいがい)は認(みと)められません</code> - 
where the stiffness is the point.

The emotional content is resolve. `ても` is neutral about how the speaker feels;
`とも` almost always carries determination, defiance or a solemn promise, which is why
it so often sits in front of <code>変(か)わらない</code>,
<code>やめない</code>, <code>認(みと)めない</code>,
<code>後悔(こうかい)しない</code>. If your main clause is a bland statement of fact,
`とも` will look overdressed.

Repeating it - <code>Aうとも Bうとも</code> - is a set rhetorical figure meaning "come
what may", and it is the shape most worth imitating, because it is the one that still
sounds natural to a modern ear.

## Contrast

### とも vs ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning; `ても` is the everyday form and takes the て-form,
`とも` is the literary form and takes the volitional.

</div>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)され<b>ても</b>、考(かんが)えを変(か)えるつもりはありません。
Even if I meet opposition, I don't intend to change my mind.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)されよ<b>うとも</b>、考(かんが)えを変(か)えるつもりはありません。
Whatever opposition I may meet, I have no intention of changing my mind.
</pre>

The content is identical; the second is a line from a speech. Note that the shapes are
not interchangeable mechanically: <code>されても</code> uses the て-form and
<code>されようとも</code> the volitional, and mixing them
(❌<code>されてとも</code>, ❌<code>されようても</code>) produces nothing.

### とも vs としても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `としても` **reasons**; `とも` **declares**.

</div>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)が見(み)つからなかった<b>としても</b>、捜査(そうさ)は続(つづ)けられる。
Even if no evidence is found, the investigation can continue.
</pre>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)が見(み)つから<b>なくとも</b>、捜査(そうさ)は続(つづ)ける。
Evidence or no evidence, the investigation goes on.
</pre>

The first is an analyst weighing a case; the second is somebody making a promise about
it. `としても` is also far more flexible - it attaches to any plain form, past or
present, positive or negative, and it is at home in ordinary polite speech, which
`とも` is not.

### Concessive とも vs the other とも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - concessive `とも` sits **mid-sentence** after a volitional or a
`く` form. Anything else spelled `とも` is a different word.

</div>

<pre>
「手伝(てつだ)ってくれる?」「いい<b>とも</b>。」
"Will you give me a hand?" "Of course."
</pre>

That sentence-final `とも` is emphatic agreement - hearty, slightly old-fashioned, and
common in fiction. Different again is the counting `とも` that means "all of them":
<code>三人(さんにん)とも合格(ごうかく)した</code>, "all three passed." None of the
three can be confused once you look at where in the sentence it sits and what comes
before it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 負(ま)ける<b>とも</b> → ✅ 負(ま)けよ<b>うとも</b>
  Verbs go through the volitional. る-verbs add `よう`, う-verbs shift to the `お` row.

- ❌ 苦(くる)しい<b>とも</b> → ✅ 苦(くる)し<b>くとも</b>
  い-adjectives use the `く` form, the same one that feeds
  <code>苦(くる)しくても</code>.

- ❌ 大変(たいへん)だ<b>とも</b> → ✅ 大変(たいへん)であろ<b>うとも</b>
  な-adjectives and nouns need `であろう`. `だとも` is not a concessive.

- ❌（chatting to a friend）お金(かね)が<b>なくとも</b>映画(えいが)は見(み)たい。 → ✅ お金(かね)がなく<b>ても</b>映画(えいが)は見(み)たい。
  Not ungrammatical, just wildly over-formal. `とも` in casual conversation sounds like
  a stage declaration.

**1.** 何(なに)を言(い)わ____、方針(ほうしん)は変(か)えません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)を言(い)わ<b>れようとも</b>、方針(ほうしん)は変(か)えません。

The passive <code>言(い)われる</code> becomes the volitional
<code>言(い)われよう</code> before `とも`. A question word in front is the classic
frame: whatever is said, the answer is the same.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この製品(せいひん)は、多少(たしょう)値段(ねだん)が（高(たか)くとも・高(たか)いとも）売(う)れ続(つづ)けるだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

多少(たしょう)値段(ねだん)が<b>高(たか)くとも</b>売(う)れ続(つづ)けるだろう。

い-adjectives take the `く` form in front of `とも`. <code>高(たか)いとも</code>
would be the quotative `と` plus `も` and means something else entirely.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese in a formal, written register: "Whatever the circumstances, the deadline will not be extended."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いかなる事情(じじょう)があろ<b>うとも</b>、締(し)め切(き)りを延(の)ばすことはありません。

<code>いかなる</code> is the written counterpart of <code>どんな</code>, and
<code>ある</code> gives the volitional <code>あろう</code>. The whole sentence has to
stay in that register - a casual ending would jar against `とも`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: たとえ一人(ひとり)になるとも、続(つづ)けるつもりだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ たとえ一人(ひとり)になろ<b>うとも</b>、続(つづ)けるつもりだ。

<code>なる</code> is a う-verb, so the volitional is <code>なろう</code>. The dictionary
form plus `とも` is not a concessive; it would be read as a quotation with `も`
attached.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 何(なに)を言(い)われ<b>ても</b>平気(へいき)だ。
(b) 何(なに)を言(い)われよ<b>うとも</b>平気(へいき)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is ordinary spoken Japanese: whatever people say, it doesn't bother me. `ても` adds
no colour, so the sentence is as strong or as light as the speaker's tone makes it.

(b) is a declaration. The literary volitional raises the register and, with it, the
stakes - this is somebody planting a flag, not shrugging. In casual conversation it
would sound faintly grandiose, but in a speech or a piece of narration it is exactly
right.

</details>

# ～にしろ / ～にせよ

**Meaning** · even if; whatever it may be; granted that - the concession is made formally, and made in order to object

`にしろ` and `にせよ` are the same word twice over: both are the imperative of
<code>～にする</code>, `しろ` being the everyday imperative and `せよ` the written one.
The pattern takes a reason, an excuse or one possibility out of several, grants it,
and then says the important thing anyway. *Whatever your reasons were, that was not
acceptable.*

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
these two sit at *minds a good deal* and *distinctly formal*. That combination is
their whole point: they let you push back hard while sounding measured.

## Formation

Both attach to a plain form. Nouns and な-adjective stems attach **bare** - no `だ`,
no `な` - or with `である` in more formal writing.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + にしろ／にせよ | 断(ことわ)る → 断(ことわ)るにせよ |
| Verb (past) | plain past + にしろ／にせよ | 落(お)ちた → 落(お)ちたにしろ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + にしろ／にせよ | 参加(さんか)しない → 参加(さんか)しないにしろ |
| い-adjective | plain form + にしろ／にせよ | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいにせよ |
| な-adjective | stem（+ である）+ にしろ／にせよ | 不便(ふべん) → 不便(ふべん)であるにせよ |
| Noun | Noun（+ である）+ にしろ／にせよ | 冗談(じょうだん) → 冗談(じょうだん)にせよ |
| With a question word | どんな／誰(だれ)が … + にしろ | - → 誰(だれ)がやるにしろ |
| Fixed opener | いずれにしろ／いずれにせよ | - → いずれにせよ、時間(じかん)がない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `だ` in front. ✅<code>冗談(じょうだん)にせよ</code>,
❌<code>冗談(じょうだん)だにせよ</code>. If you want the copula visible, the written
option is `である`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the two are interchangeable in meaning. `にせよ` is the more literary of the
pair and dominates in editorials, contracts and formal speech; `にしろ` is a step down
and turns up in careful conversation. Neither is casual, and `にしても` is the ordinary
spoken member of the family.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どんな理由(りゆう)がある<b>にせよ</b>、暴力(ぼうりょく)は許(ゆる)されない。
Whatever the reasons, violence cannot be excused.
</pre>

<pre>
断(ことわ)る<b>にしろ</b>、早(はや)めに返事(へんじ)をしたほうがいい。
Even if you're going to turn it down, you should reply soon.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)<b>にせよ</b>、言(い)っていいことと悪(わる)いことがある。
Joke or not, there are things you don't say.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)が担当(たんとう)する<b>にしろ</b>、手順(てじゅん)は変(か)わりません。
Whoever takes it on, the procedure stays the same.
</pre>

<pre>
いずれ<b>にせよ</b>、来週(らいしゅう)までに結論(けつろん)を出(だ)さなければならない。
Either way, we have to reach a conclusion by next week.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)に落(お)ちた<b>にせよ</b>、これだけ勉強(べんきょう)したことは無駄(むだ)にならない。
Even if you did fail the exam, studying this hard will not have been wasted.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)しない<b>にしろ</b>、一言(ひとこと)理由(りゆう)を伝(つた)えるのが礼儀(れいぎ)だろう。
Even if you're not taking part, common courtesy says you give a reason.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げが避(さ)けられない<b>にせよ</b>、利用者(りようしゃ)への説明(せつめい)は十分(じゅうぶん)にすべきである。
Even if the price rise is unavoidable, users are owed a proper explanation.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)な話(はなし)だった<b>にしろ</b>、部長(ぶちょう)のあの対応(たいおう)は少(すこ)し冷(つめ)たすぎたと思(おも)う。
Short notice or not, I do think the manager handled that rather coldly.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neither form is casual. `にせよ` in particular reads as written Japanese, and it is the
one you meet in newspaper columns, official statements and the formal parts of
business correspondence. `にしろ` is a little softer and can be spoken, but it still
belongs to careful speech - a meeting, a complaint made politely, a teacher addressing
a class. Among friends, `にしても` or plain `ても` is what people actually say.

The rhetorical shape is worth naming, because it is the reason this pattern exists.
You concede first so that your objection cannot be brushed aside as ignorance of the
other side. <code>忙(いそが)しかったにせよ</code> tells your listener that yes, you
know they were busy - and precisely because you have granted it, the criticism that
follows is harder to answer. Used well, this is one of the most effective moves in
polite Japanese argument. Used carelessly, it is unmistakably a telling-off.

Question words are its natural companions: <code>どんな</code>,
<code>誰(だれ)が</code>, <code>いつ</code>, <code>どちらを</code>. With one of these
in front, the meaning shifts from "even if" to "whatever / whoever", and the sentence
becomes a statement about all the cases at once.

<code>いずれにせよ</code> and <code>いずれにしろ</code> are frozen and extremely
common - "either way", "in any case". They close off a list of options rather than
conceding a single one, and they are usable in speech where the bare pattern would
sound stiff.

## Contrast

### にしろ・にせよ vs にしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one family, three registers. `にしても` is neutral speech,
`にしろ` careful speech, `にせよ` writing. The formality rises and so does the force.

</div>

<pre>
疲(つか)れていた<b>にしても</b>、あの言(い)い方(かた)はないよ。
Tired or not, that's no way to speak to someone.
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れていた<b>にせよ</b>、あのような発言(はつげん)は適切(てきせつ)ではなかった。
Even allowing for tiredness, remarks of that kind were not appropriate.
</pre>

Same objection, two different rooms. The first is a friend across a table; the second
belongs in minutes of a meeting. Choosing `にせよ` for the first sentence would make a
small grumble sound like a formal reprimand - which, in the wrong company, is exactly
the mistake to avoid.

### にしろ・にせよ vs ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` concedes **one condition**. `にしろ` concedes **one case out
of several**, and quietly implies the others.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われます。
The match will be held even if it rains.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>にせよ</b>、試合(しあい)の準備(じゅんび)は進(すす)めておきます。
Rain or no rain, we'll press on with the preparations.
</pre>

The second sentence has the unspoken alternative built in: it rains, or it does not,
and either way the preparations continue. That is why `にしろ` and `にせよ` pair so
easily into the A-or-B construction, and why `ても` does not.

### いずれにせよ vs それにしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いずれにせよ` **closes** the alternatives; `それにしても` **reopens**
the subject to comment on it.

</div>

<pre>
<b>いずれにせよ</b>、今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に決(き)めなければならない。
Either way, it has to be decided today.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>それにしても</b>、こんなに時間(じかん)がかかるとは思(おも)わなかった。
All the same, I never expected it to take this long.
</pre>

The first sweeps the options aside and gets on with it. The second accepts everything
that has been said and then adds a remark of surprise or complaint on top. Learners
mix them up because both translate as "even so"; the test is whether options are being
dismissed or a comment is being added.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>だにせよ</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>にせよ</b>（or 学生(がくせい)であるにせよ）
  Nouns attach bare. The `だ` has no place in front of `にしろ` or `にせよ`.

- ❌ 新人(しんじん)<b>にせよ</b>、よくやっている。（intending "for a newcomer, he's doing well"） → ✅ 新人(しんじん)<b>にしては</b>、よくやっている。
  `にしては` measures against a standard and is usually complimentary. `にせよ` concedes
  and then objects, so the two lead in opposite directions.

- ❌（in a text message to a friend）眠(ねむ)かった<b>にせよ</b>、寝(ね)ちゃだめでしょ。 → ✅ 眠(ねむ)かった<b>にしても</b>、寝(ね)ちゃだめでしょ。
  The rest of the sentence is casual, and `にせよ` is not. Match the register at both
  ends.

- ❌ 不便(ふべん)<b>なにしろ</b> → ✅ 不便(ふべん)<b>にしろ</b>（or 不便(ふべん)であるにしろ）
  There is no `な` here either. Unlike <code>くせに</code> or <code>ものの</code>, this
  pattern does not treat what precedes it as a noun modifier.

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

acknowledge the person's side before disagreeing, (a) is the more generous choice.

# ～にしても～にしても

**Meaning** · whether A or B; either way - two alternatives are named, both are granted, and the same thing holds for both

Doubling `にしても` turns a single concession into a pair of them. You put both options
on the table - go or don't go, buy or rent, meat or fish - concede each in turn, and
then state the one thing that is true whichever way it falls. The main clause is
always something that survives the choice.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
this is the *least bothered* and the *most conversational* of the paired concessives.
It is not an argument; it is somebody being practical.

## Formation

Each half takes a plain form or a bare noun, and the two halves must be built the same
way.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb + its negative | V する + にしても + V しない + にしても | 行(い)く → 行(い)くにしても行(い)かないにしても |
| Two verbs | V + にしても + V + にしても | 買(か)う・借(か)りる → 買(か)うにしても借(か)りるにしても |
| Two nouns | N + にしても + N + にしても | 肉(にく)・魚(さかな) → 肉(にく)にしても魚(さかな)にしても |
| Two い-adjectives | Adj + にしても + Adj + にしても | 高(たか)い・安(やす)い → 高(たか)いにしても安(やす)いにしても |
| Two な-adjectives | stem + にしても（×2） | 得意(とくい)・苦手(にがて) → 得意(とくい)にしても苦手(にがて)にしても |
| Formal written | N + であるにしても（×2） | 事実(じじつ)・うそ → 事実(じじつ)であるにしてもうそであるにしても |
| Three or more items | A にしても B にしても C にしても | 朝(あさ)にしても昼(ひる)にしても夜(よる)にしても |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two alternatives must **cover the ground**, and the main clause
must be true of **both**. ❌<code>行(い)くにしても行(い)かないにしても、電車(でんしゃ)で行(い)こう</code>
is nonsense: you cannot take the train if you are not going.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - keep the frame parallel. Both halves take `にしても`, both stay in the same
tense, and both are the same part of speech. Mixing in `にしろ` or `ても` for the second
half is a common learner slip and reads as carelessness rather than variety.

</div>

Whether you go or not, let me know.

Meat or fish, freshness matters most.

Whether we buy or rent, let's settle on a budget first.
</pre>

<pre>

Win or lose, all we can do is give it everything.
</pre>

<pre>
春(はる)<b>にしても</b>秋(あき)<b>にしても</b>、この辺(あた)りは観光客(かんこうきゃく)で混(こ)み合(あ)う。

</pre>

<pre>

University or a job, you need to decide soon or it'll be too late.
</pre>

<pre>

Whether you end up for it or against it, I'd like you to read the papers before deciding.

Whether we raise the price or hold it, we need grounds we can explain to customers.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the everyday member of a family of three. `にしても…にしても` is what people say
out loud; `にしろ…にしろ` is a notch more formal; `にせよ…にせよ` belongs in writing. All
three mean the same thing, and swapping between them changes only how the sentence
sounds in the room.

It is emotionally flat, and that is a virtue. Because it concedes both options equally,
it does not commit the speaker to either, which makes it the natural way to give
advice without taking sides:
<code>続(つづ)けるにしてもやめるにしても、一度(いちど)ご両親(りょうしん)と話(はな)したほうがいい</code>.
Nobody is being criticised, and no option is being pushed.

The pair is often used with a verb and its own negative, and that version is the most
common of all in speech, because it covers every case: <code>やるにしてもやらないにしても</code>,
<code>来(く)るにしても来(こ)ないにしても</code>. Note the irregular verb:
the stem vowel shifts, so it is <code>来(く)る</code> in the positive and
<code>来(こ)ない</code> in the negative.

In fast speech the second `にしても` is sometimes reduced or dropped altogether, leaving
<code>行(い)くにしても行(い)かなくても</code> or just a rising intonation on the
first half. Recognise it, but write the full frame.

## Contrast

### にしても…にしても vs にしろ…にしろ・にせよ…にせよ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning; the register climbs `にしても` → `にしろ` →
`にせよ`.

</div>

<pre>
参加(さんか)する<b>にしても</b>しない<b>にしても</b>、金曜日(きんようび)までに教(おし)えて。
Coming or not, tell me by Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
採用(さいよう)する<b>にせよ</b>見送(みおく)る<b>にせよ</b>、理由(りゆう)を文書(ぶんしょ)で残(のこ)すこと。
Whether the candidate is hired or passed over, the reasons are to be recorded in writing.
</pre>

The second sentence is an instruction in a manual; nothing but `にせよ` would fit it.
The first is a message to a friend, and `にせよ` there would be absurd.

### にしても…にしても vs ～ても～なくても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `ても` pair is the **plain** version and works only with verbs
and adjectives. The `にしても` pair also takes **nouns**, and sounds more deliberate.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>降(ふ)らなく<b>ても</b>、明日(あした)は出発(しゅっぱつ)します。
Rain or no rain, we set off tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)<b>にしても</b>雪(ゆき)<b>にしても</b>、この道(みち)は通(とお)れなくなる。
Rain or snow, this road becomes impassable.
</pre>

The second cannot be rebuilt with `ても` without turning the nouns into verbs. Where
both are possible, the `ても` pair is shorter and lighter, and the `にしても` pair
sounds as though the options have actually been weighed.

### にしても…にしても vs にかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the paired form **names** the alternatives. `にかかわらず` refers to
the whole variable and names none of them.

</div>

<pre>
大人(おとな)<b>にしても</b>子(こ)ども<b>にしても</b>、料金(りょうきん)は同(おな)じです。
Adults or children, the fare is the same.
</pre>

<pre>
年齢(ねんれい)に<b>かかわらず</b>、料金(りょうきん)は同(おな)じです。
The fare is the same regardless of age.
</pre>

The information is the same; the framing is not. Naming the two ends is warmer and more
concrete, and it works well in speech. `にかかわらず` states a policy, and it is what a
notice on a wall would say.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 買(か)う<b>にしても</b>借(か)りる<b>にしろ</b> → ✅ 買(か)う<b>にしても</b>借(か)りる<b>にしても</b>
  Keep the two halves identical. Alternating the forms is not stylistic variation; it
  reads as a slip.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>だにしても</b>社会人(しゃかいじん)<b>だにしても</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>にしても</b>社会人(しゃかいじん)<b>にしても</b>
  Nouns attach bare. `である` is available in formal writing, but `だ` never is.

- ❌ 参加(さんか)する<b>にしても</b>参加(さんか)しない<b>にしても</b>、会場(かいじょう)で会(あ)おう。 → ✅ 参加(さんか)する<b>にしても</b>参加(さんか)しない<b>にしても</b>、返事(へんじ)だけはください。
  The main clause has to hold for **both** options. Meeting at the venue is only
  possible under one of them.

- ❌ 安(やす)い<b>にしても</b>高(たか)かった<b>にしても</b> → ✅ 安(やす)い<b>にしても</b>高(たか)い<b>にしても</b>
  Both halves carry the same tense. A present and a past in the same frame leaves the
  sentence pointing at two different times.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 来(く)る<b>にしても</b>来(こ)ない<b>にしても</b>、一言(ひとこと)ほしい。
(b) 来(く)るか来(こ)ないか、一言(ひとこと)ほしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) concedes both possibilities on the way through and then makes a request that holds
for either. The speaker is not asking which one it will be; they are asking to be told
something, whichever it turns out to be.

(b) asks for the answer to the question itself - which of the two is it? The `か…か`
frame poses an alternative question, while the doubled `にしても` sets the question
aside as already settled either way. The two are close in English and quite distinct
in Japanese.

</details>

# ～にしろ～にしろ

**Meaning** · whether A or B - both possibilities are named and granted, and the sentence states what is true of both, in a formal frame

This is the paired form of `にしろ`, and its written twin `にせよ…にせよ`. It sets out
two options - the two ends of a decision, two kinds of thing, an action and its
opposite - concedes each, and then delivers the requirement, the consequence or the
judgement that neither option escapes.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
this is the *even-handed* one at the *formal* end. It does not take sides and it does
not complain; it lays down what holds in every case. That is why it is the shape rules
and policies are written in.

## Formation

Both halves take a plain form or a bare noun, and both halves must be built alike.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb + its opposite | V + にしろ + V + にしろ | 辞(や)める・残(のこ)る → 辞(や)めるにしろ残(のこ)るにしろ |
| Verb + its negative | V + にしろ + V ない + にしろ | やる → やるにしろやらないにしろ |
| Two nouns | N + にしろ + N + にしろ | 紙(かみ)・データ → 紙(かみ)にしろデータにしろ |
| い-adjectives | Adj + にしろ（×2） | 多(おお)い・少(すく)ない → 多(おお)いにしろ少(すく)ないにしろ |
| な-adjectives | stem + にしろ（×2） | 好(す)き・嫌(きら)い → 好(す)きにしろ嫌(きら)いにしろ |
| Written register | … + にせよ … + にせよ | 認(みと)める・退(しりぞ)ける → 認(みと)めるにせよ退(しりぞ)けるにせよ |
| Formal copula | N + であるにしろ（×2） | 事実(じじつ) → 事実(じじつ)であるにしろ… |
| Past tense in both halves | V た + にしろ（×2） | 勝(か)った・負(ま)けた → 勝(か)ったにしろ負(ま)けたにしろ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しろ` and `せよ` are both imperatives of <code>する</code>, and they
do not mix inside one sentence. ✅ にしろ…にしろ, ✅ にせよ…にせよ,
❌ にしろ…にせよ.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the frame is not limited to two. Three or four items in a row are perfectly
normal in written Japanese, and the effect is to sweep the whole field:
<code>春(はる)にせよ夏(なつ)にせよ秋(あき)にせよ冬(ふゆ)にせよ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
やる<b>にしろ</b>やめる<b>にしろ</b>、早(はや)く決(き)めてほしい。
Whether you do it or drop it, I'd like a decision soon.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)<b>にしろ</b>大阪(おおさか)<b>にしろ</b>、家賃(やちん)は安(やす)くない。
Tokyo or Osaka, the rent isn't cheap.
</pre>

<pre>
辞(や)める<b>にしろ</b>残(のこ)る<b>にしろ</b>、上司(じょうし)には正直(しょうじき)に話(はな)すべきだ。
Whether you leave or stay, you ought to be straight with your boss.
</pre>

<pre>
紙(かみ)<b>にしろ</b>データ<b>にしろ</b>、保存(ほぞん)の方法(ほうほう)を決(き)めておく必要(ひつよう)がある。
Paper or digital, we need to settle on how things are stored.
</pre>

<pre>
英語(えいご)<b>にしろ</b>中国語(ちゅうごくご)<b>にしろ</b>、毎日(まいにち)触(ふ)れなければ身(み)につかない。
English or Chinese, you won't pick it up without daily contact.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)を借(か)りる<b>にせよ</b>自社(じしゃ)で開(ひら)く<b>にせよ</b>、費用(ひよう)は見積(みつ)もっておくこと。
Whether we hire a venue or host it in-house, the costs are to be estimated in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
直接(ちょくせつ)会(あ)う<b>にしろ</b>メールで済(す)ませる<b>にしろ</b>、謝罪(しゃざい)は早(はや)いほどいい。
In person or by e-mail, an apology is better made sooner.
</pre>

<pre>
制度(せいど)を変(か)える<b>にせよ</b>今(いま)のまま続(つづ)ける<b>にせよ</b>、現場(げんば)の声(こえ)を聞(き)くべきである。
Whether the system is reformed or left as it is, those doing the work should be consulted.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)<b>にしろ</b>故障(こしょう)<b>にしろ</b>、原因(げんいん)がはっきりするまで運行(うんこう)は再開(さいかい)できない。
Accident or breakdown, services cannot resume until the cause is clear.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にしろ…にしろ` is careful Japanese; `にせよ…にせよ` is written Japanese. Neither is
casual. In relaxed conversation the same job is done by `にしても…にしても`, or simply by
`～ても～なくても`, and a learner who reaches for `にせよ` in a chat message will sound
like a memo.

Where it belongs is anywhere a general rule is being stated: internal guidelines,
notices, editorials, academic prose, and the summing-up part of a formal discussion.
Because it commits to nothing, it is also the polite way to give counsel about a
decision that is not yours to make - you concede both of the other person's options
and then say the one thing you actually want them to hear.

The imperative origin is audible. `しろ` and `せよ` are commands, and the pattern
inherits a faint tone of *take it as you like*. That is what stops the sentence from
sounding indecisive: it is not "I don't know which"; it is "settle that however you
wish - here is what does not change."

Ending the main clause with <code>こと</code>, <code>べきだ</code>,
<code>必要がある</code> or a passive is very common, because the whole construction is
built for general statements rather than personal reactions.

## Contrast

### にしろ…にしろ vs にせよ…にせよ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same pattern, two imperatives. `しろ` is the spoken imperative and
`せよ` the written one, and the pair inherits exactly that difference.

</div>

<pre>
受(う)ける<b>にしろ</b>受(う)けない<b>にしろ</b>、今週中(こんしゅうちゅう)に返事(へんじ)をしよう。
Whether I take it or not, I should reply this week.
</pre>

<pre>
受理(じゅり)する<b>にせよ</b>却下(きゃっか)する<b>にせよ</b>、理由(りゆう)を明記(めいき)すること。
Whether the application is accepted or rejected, the reasons are to be stated.
</pre>

The first is a person thinking aloud. The second is a line from a procedures document,
and the <code>こと</code> ending confirms it. Mixing the two halves - `にしろ` then
`にせよ` - is a straightforward error.

### The paired form vs a single にしろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **one** `にしろ` concedes and then **objects**. **Two** concede
symmetrically and then state what holds either way.

</div>

<pre>
急(いそ)いでいた<b>にしろ</b>、あの運転(うんてん)は危(あぶ)なすぎる。
Even if he was in a hurry, that was far too dangerous a piece of driving.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)いでいる<b>にしろ</b>いない<b>にしろ</b>、制限(せいげん)速度(そくど)は守(まも)らなければならない。
In a hurry or not, the speed limit has to be observed.
</pre>

The first has a target and is a criticism of one person. The second has no target at
all and is a rule. If your sentence is aimed at somebody, you want the single form.

### にしろ…にしろ vs も…ば…も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にしろ…にしろ` says **whichever one it is**. `も…ば…も` says **both
of them are so**.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)<b>にしろ</b>品質(ひんしつ)<b>にしろ</b>、他社(たしゃ)には勝(か)てない。
On price or on quality, we can't beat the competition.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)<b>も</b>高(たか)けれ<b>ば</b>品質(ひんしつ)<b>も</b>悪(わる)い。
It's expensive and the quality is poor into the bargain.
</pre>

Both frames pair two nouns, and learners reach for the wrong one because both come out
in English with "and" or "or". The `にしろ` frame is about alternatives - pick either,
the verdict is the same. The `も…ば…も` frame is additive - it piles the second fact on
top of the first.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 事実(じじつ)<b>だにしろ</b>うそ<b>だにしろ</b> → ✅ 事実(じじつ)<b>にしろ</b>うそ<b>にしろ</b>
  Nouns attach bare. In formal writing `である` is available; `だ` is not.

- ❌ 行(い)く<b>にしろ</b>行(い)かない<b>にせよ</b> → ✅ 行(い)く<b>にしろ</b>行(い)かない<b>にしろ</b>
  Pick one imperative and use it in both halves.

- ❌（to a friend, over coffee）コーヒー<b>にせよ</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)<b>にせよ</b>、熱(あつ)いのがいいよね。 → ✅ コーヒー<b>にしても</b>紅茶(こうちゃ)<b>にしても</b>、熱(あつ)いのがいいよね。
  Not wrong, but comically formal for the subject. `にしても` is the conversational
  member of the family.

- ❌ 勝(か)つ<b>にしろ</b>負(ま)けた<b>にしろ</b> → ✅ 勝(か)つ<b>にしろ</b>負(ま)ける<b>にしろ</b>
  Keep the tense the same on both sides. A mixed pair reads as two unrelated clauses.

**1.** 賛成(さんせい)する____反対(はんたい)する____、理由(りゆう)を述(の)べてください。

Two opposite verbs, both in the plain form, both followed by the same `にしろ`. The

transparent, and you could substitute any other pair of opposites into the frame.

(b) is a fossilised idiom - "sooner or later" - built from an older concessive form.
It cannot be extended or altered, and it reads as a set phrase rather than as
grammar. Where (a) sounds like a considered statement, (b) sounds proverbial.

</details>

# ～につけ

**Meaning** · every time; whenever - and, doubled, "in good times and bad" - a stimulus that always brings the same feeling with it

`につけ` has two shapes and one idea. Attached to a verb of seeing, hearing or
thinking, it says that whenever the speaker meets a certain thing, a feeling rises
unbidden. Doubled around a pair of opposites - good and bad, rain and wind - it says
the same feeling comes in either circumstance.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`につけ` is *not minding at all* and *literary*. Nothing is being conceded against
anybody. What it shares with its neighbours is the shape: two cases named, one
constant result.

## Formation

The verb goes in the **dictionary form**, and it comes from a short list - seeing,
hearing, reading, remembering, thinking. `て` may be added with no change of meaning.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb of perception | dictionary form + につけ（て） | 見(み)る → 見(み)るにつけ |
| Verb of hearing | dictionary form + につけ（て） | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)くにつけ |
| Verb of thought | dictionary form + につけ（て） | 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)えるにつけ |
| Paired い-adjectives | Adj + につけ + Adj + につけ | いい・悪(わる)い → いいにつけ悪(わる)いにつけ |
| Paired nouns | N + につけ + N + につけ | 雨(あめ)・風(かぜ) → 雨(あめ)につけ風(かぜ)につけ |
| Emphatic | … + につけても | 思(おも)う → 思(おも)うにつけても |
| Fixed adverb | 何(なに)かにつけ（て） | - → 何(なに)かにつけ口(くち)を出(だ)す |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the main clause must be something that **happens to** the speaker,
not something they decide to do. Feelings, memories and involuntary thoughts belong
there. ❌<code>桜(さくら)を見(み)るにつけ写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る</code> - 
taking a photograph is a choice, so this needs `たびに`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is a pattern to recognise fluently and produce sparingly. Outside the
handful of perception verbs and the standard antonym pairs, it barely occurs; modern
speech uses `たびに` for "every time" and simply repeats the two cases for "either
way". Its liveliest survivor is <code>何(なに)かにつけ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この写真(しゃしん)を見(み)る<b>につけ</b>、子(こ)どものころを思(おも)い出(だ)す。
Every time I look at this photograph I think of my childhood.
</pre>

<pre>
あの曲(きょく)を聞(き)く<b>につけ</b>、胸(むね)が痛(いた)む。
That song hurts to hear, every time.
</pre>

<pre>
いい<b>につけ</b>悪(わる)い<b>につけ</b>、うわさはすぐに広(ひろ)まる。
For better or worse, rumours travel fast.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の話(はなし)を聞(き)く<b>につけ</b>、自分(じぶん)の勉強(べんきょう)不足(ぶそく)を感(かん)じる。
Listening to him always makes me aware of how little I've studied.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)は何(なに)か<b>につけ</b>私(わたし)の服装(ふくそう)に文句(もんく)を言(い)う。
My mother finds fault with what I wear at every opportunity.
</pre>

<pre>
うれしい<b>につけ</b>悲(かな)しい<b>につけ</b>、人(ひと)は音楽(おんがく)を求(もと)めるものだ。
In joy and in sorrow alike, people turn to music.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)の手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)み返(かえ)す<b>につけ</b>、あのころの自分(じぶん)が恥(は)ずかしくなる。
Whenever I read back over those old letters I feel embarrassed by the person I was.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の報道(ほうどう)を目(め)にする<b>につけ</b>、安全(あんぜん)対策(たいさく)の大切(たいせつ)さを思(おも)わずにはいられない。
Every time I see an accident reported, I cannot help thinking how much safety measures matter.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)<b>につけ</b>風(かぜ)<b>につけ</b>、海(うみ)に出(で)た息子(むすこ)のことが心配(しんぱい)でならなかった。
Rain or wind, I could not stop worrying about my son out at sea.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`につけ` is written and slightly old-fashioned. It suits essays, memoirs, letters,
novels and speeches, and it carries a reflective, faintly nostalgic colour that
`たびに` does not have. Said in ordinary conversation it sounds like a line from a
book - which is sometimes exactly the effect a speaker wants at a memorial or in a
farewell address.

The main clause is nearly always an emotion or an involuntary thought, and it very
often ends in a form that underlines the lack of choice:
<code>～てならない</code>, <code>～ずにはいられない</code>,
<code>～ものだ</code>, <code>思(おも)い出(だ)す</code>,
<code>感(かん)じる</code>. If your main clause is a plan or an instruction, this is
the wrong pattern.

The paired form draws on a small stock of conventional opposites - いい／悪(わる)い,
うれしい／悲(かな)しい, 雨(あめ)／風(かぜ), 見(み)る／聞(き)く. Inventing new pairs is possible but tends to
sound laboured; the construction reads best when the pair is one a reader already
knows.

<code>何(なに)かにつけ</code> is the one part of this entry that is genuinely common
in speech. It means "at every turn", "on any excuse", and it usually introduces
something mildly unwelcome - interference, complaints, comparisons. Its close relative
<code>何(なに)かと</code> is neutral by comparison.

## Contrast

### につけ vs たびに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たびに` is the neutral "every time" and takes **any** verb and
**any** main clause. `につけ` needs a **perception verb** and a **feeling** in reply.

</div>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)へ行(い)く<b>たびに</b>、この店(みせ)に寄(よ)っている。
I stop in at this shop every time I go to Kyoto.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)の町(まち)を歩(ある)く<b>につけ</b>、時間(じかん)の流(なが)れの遅(おそ)さを感(かん)じる。
Walking through Kyoto, I am struck each time by how slowly time moves there.
</pre>

Stopping at a shop is a decision, so it can only take `たびに`. Being struck by
something is not, and that is where `につけ` is at home. Note too that `たびに` accepts
nouns - <code>試験(しけん)のたびに</code> - while `につけ` in its single form does
not.

### につけ…につけ vs にしろ…にしろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `につけ` pair is about **circumstances that produce a feeling**.
The `にしろ` pair is about **options in a decision**.

</div>

<pre>
勝(か)つ<b>につけ</b>負(ま)ける<b>につけ</b>、監督(かんとく)の言葉(ことば)を思(おも)い出(だ)す。
Win or lose, I find myself remembering what the coach said.
</pre>

<pre>
勝(か)つ<b>にしろ</b>負(ま)ける<b>にしろ</b>、最後(さいご)まで全力(ぜんりょく)で戦(たたか)う。
Whether we win or lose, we fight flat out to the end.
</pre>

The first sentence reports what happens inside the speaker in both cases; the second
states a policy that both cases fall under. Where a rule, a requirement or a decision
is involved, `につけ` is the wrong frame.

### 何(なに)かにつけ vs 何(なに)かと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>何(なに)かにつけ</code> means "at every opportunity" and
usually introduces something **unwelcome**. <code>何(なに)かと</code> means "in
various ways" and is **neutral**.

</div>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は何(なに)か<b>につけ</b>昔(むかし)の話(はなし)を持(も)ち出(だ)す。
The section chief drags up the old days at every opportunity.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの前(まえ)は何(なに)かと忙(いそが)しい。
There's all sorts to do before a move.
</pre>

The first is a complaint; the second is not. Learners who have only met one of the two
tend to use it for both, and putting <code>何(なに)かにつけ</code> into the second
sentence would turn a neutral remark into a grumble about moving house.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 桜(さくら)を見(み)る<b>につけ</b>、写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る。 → ✅ 桜(さくら)を見(み)る<b>たびに</b>、写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る。
  The main clause of a `につけ` sentence is a feeling that arises, not an action the
  speaker chooses.

- ❌ 写真(しゃしん)を見(み)た<b>につけ</b> → ✅ 写真(しゃしん)を見(み)る<b>につけ</b>
  The dictionary form only. `につけ` describes a recurring situation, so a past tense
  in front of it is not possible.

- ❌ ごはんを食(た)べる<b>につけ</b>、国(くに)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)す。 → ✅ ふるさとの味(あじ)を口(くち)にする<b>につけ</b>、国(くに)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)す。
  The verb has to be one of perception or reflection. Eating on its own is not; tasting
  something and being reminded by it is.

- ❌ いい<b>につけ</b>悪(わる)く<b>につけ</b> → ✅ いい<b>につけ</b>悪(わる)い<b>につけ</b>
  Both halves keep the plain form. The `く` form belongs to `ても` and `とも`, not here.

## Practice

**1.** この絵(え)を見(み)る____、亡(な)くなった祖父(そふ)を思(おも)い出(だ)す。

A verb of seeing in the dictionary form, and a main clause that is an involuntary
memory. That combination is the core use of the pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 駅(えき)を通(とお)る（につけ・たびに）、新(あたら)しいパン屋(や)に寄(よ)ることにしている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

駅(えき)を通(とお)る<b>たびに</b>、新(あたら)しいパン屋(や)に寄(よ)ることにしている。

<code>寄(よ)ることにしている</code> is a decision the speaker has made, and `につけ`
cannot introduce a deliberate action. `たびに` is neutral and accepts it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "In good times and bad, this town has looked after its river."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いい<b>につけ</b>悪(わる)い<b>につけ</b>、この町(まち)は川(かわ)を守(まも)ってきた。

"In good times and bad" is the standard antonym pair
<code>いいにつけ悪(わる)いにつけ</code>, kept in the plain form on both sides. The
literary frame suits a sentence about a town's history.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 息子(むすこ)の成績(せいせき)を見(み)るにつけ、塾(じゅく)に通(かよ)わせようと決(き)めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 息子(むすこ)の成績(せいせき)を見(み)る<b>につけ</b>、不安(ふあん)になる。
（or 成績(せいせき)を見(み)て、塾(じゅく)に通(かよ)わせようと決(き)めた）

The original main clause is a decision, and `につけ` requires a feeling. Either change
the main clause to an emotion, or drop the pattern and use a plain て-form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 義母(ぎぼ)は何(なに)か<b>につけ</b>口(くち)を出(だ)してくる。
(b) 義母(ぎぼ)は何(なに)かと口(くち)を出(だ)してくる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says she seizes on any excuse to interfere. <code>何(なに)かにつけ</code> implies
a pattern the speaker has noticed and resents, and the sentence is plainly a
complaint.

(b) is milder. <code>何(なに)かと</code> means "in one way or another" and is
neutral in itself, so the disapproval in (b) comes only from
<code>口(くち)を出(だ)す</code> and could be softened away entirely by a different
verb - <code>何(なに)かと助(たす)けてくれる</code> is warm praise.

</details>

# ～にもかかわらず

**Meaning** · despite; in spite of; and yet - a fact is stated, and something that flatly contradicts it is stated next

`にもかかわらず` is the heavyweight of written concession. It literally says "without
this being involved": the first fact ought to have determined the outcome, and the
outcome ignored it. Because it is long, formal and unmistakable, it is what reports,
articles and speeches use when the mismatch is the point of the sentence.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`にもかかわらず` is *pointedly aware that something is wrong* and *at the formal end*.
It is the pattern that criticises without raising its voice - and, in a fixed polite
formula, the pattern that thanks.

## Formation

It attaches to a plain form or directly to a noun. な-adjectives and nouns take
`である` when a clause reading is wanted.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + にもかかわらず | 雨(あめ) → 雨(あめ)にもかかわらず |
| Verb | plain form + にもかかわらず | 反対(はんたい)する → 反対(はんたい)するにもかかわらず |
| Verb (past) | plain past + にもかかわらず | 注意(ちゅうい)した → 注意(ちゅうい)したにもかかわらず |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + にもかかわらず | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かないにもかかわらず |
| い-adjective | plain form + にもかかわらず | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いにもかかわらず |
| な-adjective | stem + である + にもかかわらず | 不利(ふり) → 不利(ふり)であるにもかかわらず |
| Noun, as a clause | Noun + である + にもかかわらず | 初日(しょにち) → 初日(しょにち)であるにもかかわらず |
| Sentence opener | にもかかわらず、… | - → にもかかわらず、決定(けってい)は覆(くつがえ)らなかった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one `も` decides everything. `にもかかわらず` = **despite the fact
that** (X is true, and Y happened anyway). `にかかわらず` = **regardless of whether**
(X may or may not be true; Y holds either way). They are not variants of each other.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>お忙(いそが)しいにもかかわらず</code> is a set opening of thanks in
speeches, e-mails and ceremonies: "despite your busy schedule". It is one of the very
few places this pattern carries warmth rather than criticism, and it is worth learning
whole.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、大勢(おおぜい)の人(ひと)が集(あつ)まった。
A large crowd turned out despite the rain.
</pre>

<pre>
努力(どりょく)した<b>にもかかわらず</b>、結果(けっか)は出(で)なかった。
For all the effort put in, there were no results.
</pre>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しい<b>にもかかわらず</b>、ご出席(しゅっせき)いただきありがとうございます。
Thank you for attending despite your busy schedule.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い<b>にもかかわらず</b>、この商品(しょうひん)はよく売(う)れている。
This product sells well despite its high price.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も注意(ちゅうい)した<b>にもかかわらず</b>、同(おな)じミスがくり返(かえ)された。
The same mistake was repeated despite repeated warnings.
</pre>

<pre>
医師(いし)の警告(けいこく)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、彼(かれ)はたばこをやめようとしない。
He will not give up smoking, whatever his doctor tells him.
</pre>

<pre>
初日(しょにち)である<b>にもかかわらず</b>、会場(かいじょう)は半分(はんぶん)も埋(う)まっていなかった。
Even though it was opening day, the hall was less than half full.
</pre>

<pre>
十分(じゅうぶん)な説明(せつめい)がなされなかった<b>にもかかわらず</b>、計画(けいかく)は承認(しょうにん)された。
The plan was approved despite the absence of any adequate explanation.
</pre>

<pre>
事前(じぜん)に連絡(れんらく)してあった<b>にもかかわらず</b>、部屋(へや)の準備(じゅんび)はまったくできていなかった。
We had let them know in advance, and yet the room had not been prepared at all.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is written Japanese and formal spoken Japanese. It appears in newspapers, annual
reports, academic writing, legal documents, complaint letters and the opening lines of
speeches. In relaxed conversation it is far too heavy - a friend describing a wet
festival says <code>雨(あめ)なのに</code>, not
<code>雨(あめ)にもかかわらず</code>.

Emotionally it is not neutral, although it is controlled. Because the pattern
foregrounds the mismatch, the reader is invited to find the mismatch improper: *warnings
were given, and the mistake happened anyway*. That makes it the natural vehicle for
formal criticism, and it is why so many `にもかかわらず` sentences appear in the passive
and name no culprit.

The exception is the thanks formula. <code>お忙(いそが)しいところ</code> and
<code>お忙(いそが)しいにもかかわらず</code> both acknowledge that the listener has
gone out of their way, and there the surprise is flattering rather than accusing.

Two structural facts worth remembering. First, it can open a sentence on its own, as a
conjunction meaning "nevertheless" - handy in written argument. Second, it cannot end
one: unlike `のに`, it has no dangling, reproachful use. If you want the sentence to
trail off in complaint, you want a different pattern.

## Contrast

### にもかかわらず vs にかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `も`: **despite** a fact that is true. Without `も`:
**regardless of** a variable that may go either way.

</div>

<pre>
悪天候(あくてんこう)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われた。
The match was played despite the bad weather.
</pre>

<pre>
天候(てんこう)<b>にかかわらず</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われます。
The match will be played whatever the weather.
</pre>

The first reports what happened on one particular rainy day. The second announces a
policy that ignores the weather entirely, and the noun in front of it is the *variable*
(weather), not a *value* of it. That is the reliable test: if you can put "good or
bad", "large or small", "yes or no" after the noun, you want `にかかわらず`.

### にもかかわらず vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical logic, opposite registers. `のに` is spoken and
emotional; `にもかかわらず` is written and formal.

</div>

<pre>
予約(よやく)した<b>のに</b>、席(せき)がなかったんですよ。
We'd booked, and there wasn't a table!
</pre>

<pre>
予約(よやく)が入(はい)っていた<b>にもかかわらず</b>、席(せき)は用意(ようい)されていなかった。
No table had been prepared, despite the reservation on record.
</pre>

The first is the story told to a friend; the second is the complaint sent to head
office. Note the second's passive and its impersonality - the register pulls the whole
sentence with it, not just the joint.

### にもかかわらず vs ものの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are written concessives. `にもかかわらず` points at a
**contradiction** and takes nouns. `ものの` reports a **shortfall** and needs a clause.

</div>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)の反対(はんたい)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、計画(けいかく)は実行(じっこう)された。
The plan was carried out in the teeth of unanimous opposition.
</pre>

<pre>
計画(けいかく)は実行(じっこう)された<b>ものの</b>、成果(せいか)はほとんどなかった。
The plan was carried out, but it achieved next to nothing.
</pre>

`ものの` cannot take the bare noun <code>反対(はんたい)</code> the way the first
sentence does, and `にもかかわらず` would sound overheated in the second, where nothing
is being condemned - only reported as disappointing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)<b>だにもかかわらず</b> → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>にもかかわらず</b>（or 雨(あめ)であるにもかかわらず）
  A noun attaches directly. `だ` never appears in front of this pattern.

- ❌ 年齢(ねんれい)<b>にもかかわらず</b>応募(おうぼ)できます。 → ✅ 年齢(ねんれい)<b>にかかわらず</b>応募(おうぼ)できます。
  Age here is the variable, not an obstacle that was overcome. Dropping the `も` is
  what turns "despite" into "regardless of".

- ❌ 不利(ふり)<b>にもかかわらず</b> → ✅ 不利(ふり)<b>であるにもかかわらず</b>
  な-adjectives need `である`. Without it the phrase reads as a bare noun and the
  sentence goes vague.

- ❌（chatting）走(はし)った<b>にもかかわらず</b>、遅(おく)れちゃった。 → ✅ 走(はし)った<b>のに</b>、遅(おく)れちゃった。
  The contracted <code>遅(おく)れちゃった</code> is casual, and `にもかかわらず` is
  not. Register has to match across the whole sentence.

**1.** 厳(きび)しい条件(じょうけん)____、多(おお)くの応募(おうぼ)があった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The tough conditions should have deterred applicants and did not, so this is a
contradiction rather than an irrelevance. A noun attaches with no copula.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 経験(けいけん)の有無(うむ)（にかかわらず・にもかかわらず）、どなたでも参加(さんか)できます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

経験(けいけん)の有無(うむ)<b>にかかわらず</b>、どなたでも参加(さんか)できます。

<code>有無(うむ)</code> means "whether or not there is", which is a variable with two
values - the textbook environment for `にかかわらず`. Nothing is being overcome here, so
the `も` would be wrong.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, formally: "Despite having received the report three weeks earlier, the department took no action."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三週間(さんしゅうかん)前(まえ)に報告(ほうこく)を受(う)けていた<b>にもかかわらず</b>、担当(たんとう)部署(ぶしょ)は何(なん)の対応(たいおう)も取(と)らなかった。

A past-tense clause attaches directly, and the impersonal main clause keeps the whole
sentence in the register of a written finding. This is `にもかかわらず` doing its
commonest job: formal criticism.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会員(かいいん)だにもかかわらず、割引(わりびき)が受(う)けられなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会員(かいいん)<b>であるにもかかわらず</b>、割引(わりびき)が受(う)けられなかった。
（also acceptable: 会員(かいいん)<b>にもかかわらず</b>）

`だ` cannot stand in front of `にもかかわらず`. Use `である` when you want the copula
audible, or attach the noun bare.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 国籍(こくせき)<b>にかかわらず</b>応募(おうぼ)できます。
(b) 外国(がいこく)籍(せき)である<b>にもかかわらず</b>採用(さいよう)された。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) sets out a rule. Nationality is a variable, and the sentence says that whatever
value it takes, the door is open. There is no surprise and no obstacle - only a
declared irrelevance.

(b) reports one case, and treats the nationality as something that would normally have
stood in the way. The `も` carries the implication that this outcome was against
expectation, which is why (b) reads as a comment on an unusual event and (a) as a line
from a recruitment notice.

</details>

# ～反面

**Meaning** · on the other hand; while - one thing is shown to have two opposite faces, usually a merit and the drawback that comes with it

<code>反面(はんめん)</code> is literally "the opposite face". The sentence names a
quality of something and then names the quality on its other side, and the two belong
to the *same* thing: the same job, the same city, the same person. It is the sentence
you write when you are being fair about something.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
<code>反面(はんめん)</code> is the one that *minds nothing at all*, and sits at the *neutral-to-written* end.
It is not conceding to an opponent; it is describing something honestly from both
sides.

## Formation

<code>反面(はんめん)</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + 反面(はんめん) | 減(へ)る → 減(へ)る反面(はんめん) |
| Verb (past) | plain past + 反面(はんめん) | 簡単(かんたん)になった → 簡単(かんたん)になった反面(はんめん) |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 反面(はんめん) | 売(う)れない → 売(う)れない反面(はんめん) |
| い-adjective | plain form + 反面(はんめん) | 軽(かる)い → 軽(かる)い反面(はんめん) |
| な-adjective | な（or である）+ 反面(はんめん) | 便利(べんり) → 便利(べんり)な反面(はんめん) |
| Noun | である + 反面(はんめん) | 自由(じゆう) → 自由(じゆう)である反面(はんめん) |
| With で | … + 反面(はんめん)で | 強(つよ)い → 強(つよ)い反面(はんめん)で |
| Written variant | 半面(はんめん) | same word, same meaning, different kanji |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **one topic, two faces.** Both clauses must describe the same thing,
and the two descriptions must genuinely pull against each other - typically one good
and one bad.
❌<code>兄(あに)は背(せ)が高(たか)い反面(はんめん)、弟(おとうと)は低(ひく)い</code> compares two people,
and needs
<code>一方(いっぽう)で</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>半面(はんめん)</code> with the "half" kanji is the same word and is
equally correct; newspapers use both. Nothing in the meaning changes, so pick one and
be consistent inside a document.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この仕事(しごと)は給料(きゅうりょう)がいい<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、休(やす)みが少(すく)ない。
The pay in this job is good, but there's little time off.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は厳(きび)しい<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、面倒見(めんどうみ)がとてもいい。
He's strict, but he looks after people extremely well.
</pre>

<pre>
都会(とかい)は便利(べんり)な<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、家賃(やちん)がとても高(たか)い。
Cities are convenient, but the rent is very high.
</pre>

<pre>
この素材(そざい)は軽(かる)い<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、傷(きず)がつきやすい。
This material is light, but it scratches easily.
</pre>

<pre>
ネットは情報(じょうほう)が早(はや)く手(て)に入(はい)る<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、正確(せいかく)さに欠(か)けることもある。
The internet gets you information fast, but it can be short on accuracy.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)は手続(てつづ)きが簡単(かんたん)になった<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、不正(ふせい)が見(み)つけにくくなった。
The new system has simplified the paperwork, but it has also made fraud harder to spot.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしは自由(じゆう)である<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、体調(たいちょう)を崩(くず)したときに心細(こころぼそ)い。
Living alone is free, but it is a lonely business when you fall ill.
</pre>

<pre>
在宅(ざいたく)勤務(きんむ)は通勤(つうきん)の負担(ふたん)が減(へ)る<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、仕事(しごと)と生活(せいかつ)の境(さかい)があいまいになりやすい。
Working from home cuts out the commute, but the line between work and life easily blurs.
</pre>

<pre>
あの監督(かんとく)の作品(さくひん)は映像(えいぞう)が美(うつく)しい<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、話(はなし)の展開(てんかい)が遅(おそ)いとよく言(い)われる。
That director's films are visually beautiful, but they are often said to be slow.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>反面(はんめん)</code> is neutral-to-formal and thoroughly at home in writing: reviews, reports,
comparisons, essay paragraphs weighing pros and cons. It is perfectly usable in polite
speech - explaining a product to a customer, answering an interview question about
your own character - and it is only in very casual conversation that it sounds a
little written, where people say <code>けど</code> instead.

It carries no complaint. That is what separates it from most of this group: nobody's
expectations have been defeated, and nobody is being criticised. The two halves are
simply both true. Because of that even-handedness it is a useful softener - naming a
drawback after a merit sounds honest rather than negative, and naming a merit after a
drawback sounds generous.

The order matters. Whatever comes **second** is what the sentence leaves you with, so
<code>安(やす)い反面(はんめん)、質(しつ)が悪(わる)い</code> is a warning and
<code>質(しつ)が悪(わる)い反面(はんめん)、安(やす)い</code> is a
recommendation. Writers use that deliberately.

One limit to respect: the two faces have to belong to one thing. If your second clause
introduces a new subject, <code>反面(はんめん)</code> will read as a mistake, and
<code>一方(いっぽう)で</code> is the pattern you want.

## Contrast

### 反面(はんめん) vs 一方(いっぽう)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>反面(はんめん)</code> needs **one topic with two sides**. <code>一方(いっぽう)で</code>
can also set **two different things** beside each other.

</div>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)はよく効(き)く<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、眠(ねむ)くなりやすい。
This medicine works well, but it tends to make you drowsy.
</pre>

<pre>
都心(としん)の人口(じんこう)は増(ふ)えている。<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、地方(ちほう)では減少(げんしょう)が続(つづ)く。
The population of the city centre is rising. Meanwhile, the regions continue to lose people.
</pre>

The second sentence has two separate subjects and no shared thing to have two faces of,
so <code>反面(はんめん)</code> is impossible there. Where the topic *is* shared, both work, and <code>反面(はんめん)</code>
sounds tighter because it insists on the connection.

### 反面(はんめん) vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>反面(はんめん)</code> reports two coexisting facts **without surprise**. `のに`
says the second fact **should not have happened**.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、待(ま)ち時間(じかん)が長(なが)い。
This place is cheap, but the wait is long.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>のに</b>、いつもすいている。
This place is cheap, and yet it's always empty.
</pre>

In the first, cheapness and queueing are two sides of the same bargain and nothing is
odd. In the second, the emptiness contradicts what cheapness leads you to expect, and
the speaker is puzzled. If nobody is surprised, <code>反面(はんめん)</code> is the honest choice.

### 反面(はんめん) vs に反(はん)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the kanji is shared and the jobs are not. <code>反面(はんめん)</code> = **the other side
of the same thing**. <code>に反(はん)して</code> = **contrary to an expectation, a
rule or a prediction**.

</div>

<pre>
この車(くるま)は力(ちから)が強(つよ)い<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、燃費(ねんぴ)が悪(わる)い。
This car is powerful, but it's thirsty.
</pre>

<pre>
予想(よそう)に<b>反(はん)して</b>、燃費(ねんぴ)はかなりよかった。
Contrary to expectations, the fuel economy was rather good.
</pre>

<code>に反(はん)して</code> takes a noun that names something to be defied -
expectation, prediction, the rules, someone's wishes. <code>反面(はんめん)</code> takes a whole clause and
names no adversary at all. Learners who go by the kanji alone regularly swap them.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 便利(べんり)<b>だ反面(はんめん)</b> → ✅ 便利(べんり)<b>な反面(はんめん)</b>（or 便利(べんり)である反面(はんめん)）
  <code>反面(はんめん)</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective links with `な`.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>反面(はんめん)</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>である反面(はんめん)</b>
  A bare noun cannot modify another noun. `である` supplies the link.

- ❌ 姉(あね)は静(しず)かな<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、妹(いもうと)はよくしゃべる。 → ✅ 姉(あね)は静(しず)かだ。<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、妹(いもうと)はよくしゃべる。
  Two different people, so there is no single thing with two faces.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は寒(さむ)い<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、風(かぜ)も強(つよ)い。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は寒(さむ)い<b>上(うえ)に</b>、風(かぜ)も強(つよ)い。
  Cold and windy are not opposites; they pile up on the same side. <code>反面(はんめん)</code> needs the
  second clause to pull the other way.

## Practice

**1.** この辞書(じしょ)は説明(せつめい)がくわしい____、重(おも)くて持(も)ち運(はこ)びにくい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この辞書(じしょ)は説明(せつめい)がくわしい<b>反面(はんめん)</b>、重(おも)くて持(も)ち運(はこ)びにくい。

One dictionary, two opposing qualities - detail on one face, weight on the other. An
い-adjective attaches with no change.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 去年(きょねん)の売上(うりあげ)は好調(こうちょう)だった。（反面(はんめん)・一方(いっぽう)で）、人件費(じんけんひ)も大(おお)きく増(ふ)えた。

<b>一方(いっぽう)で</b>、人件費(じんけんひ)も大(おお)きく増(ふ)えた。

The sentence has already ended, and <code>反面(はんめん)</code> cannot open a new one - it needs a clause in
front of it inside the same sentence. Rewritten as one sentence,

</details>

</details>

</details>

# ～に反して

**Meaning** · contrary to; against - what actually happened went the other way from what was expected, intended or laid down

<code>に反(はん)して</code> needs something to run against. The noun in front of it
names a prediction, a hope, an intention or a rule, and the sentence reports that
reality did the opposite. That noun is the pattern's whole grammar: no expectation, no
<code>に反(はん)して</code>.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
this one *minds on somebody else's behalf* and is *written and formal*. It does not
express the speaker's feelings; it records a mismatch between a stated standard and
the facts. That neutrality is why it fills news reports and official findings.

## Formation

Only nouns attach. The form changes according to what it modifies.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (adverbial) | Noun + に反(はん)して | 予想(よそう) → 予想(よそう)に反(はん)して |
| Noun (written, curt) | Noun + に反(はん)し | 予測(よそく) → 予測(よそく)に反(はん)し |
| Modifying a noun | Noun + に反(はん)する + Noun | 規則(きそく) → 規則(きそく)に反(はん)する行為(こうい) |
| Modifying a noun (past) | Noun + に反(はん)した + Noun | 期待(きたい) → 期待(きたい)に反(はん)した結果(けっか) |
| As a predicate | Noun + に反(はん)する | 方針(ほうしん) → 方針(ほうしん)に反(はん)する |
| A clause, nominalised first | …こと + に反(はん)して | 約束(やくそく)したこと → 約束(やくそく)したことに反(はん)して |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun must be something that can be **defied**:
予想(よそう), 期待(きたい), 予測(よそく), 意思(いし),
願(ねが)い, 命令(めいれい), 規則(きそく), 法律(ほうりつ),
方針(ほうしん). An ordinary fact cannot go there:
❌<code>雨(あめ)に反(はん)して</code>. For a fact, use `にもかかわらず`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - two flavours live in one pattern. With
<code>予想(よそう)</code> or <code>期待(きたい)</code> it means "contrary to", and
the tone is surprise. With <code>規則(きそく)</code> or
<code>命令(めいれい)</code> it means "in breach of", and the tone is condemnation.
The noun decides which you have written.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
予想(よそう)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、試合(しあい)は早(はや)く終(お)わった。
Contrary to expectations, the match finished early.
</pre>

<pre>
期待(きたい)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、売上(うりあげ)は伸(の)びなかった。
Sales did not grow, contrary to what had been hoped.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)<b>に反(はん)する</b>行為(こうい)は認(みと)められません。
Conduct in breach of the rules will not be permitted.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)の願(ねが)い<b>に反(はん)して</b>、彼(かれ)は大学(だいがく)を辞(や)めてしまった。
Against his parents' wishes, he dropped out of university.
</pre>

<pre>
事前(じぜん)の予測(よそく)<b>に反(はん)し</b>、今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は涼(すず)しかった。
Contrary to earlier forecasts, this summer was a cool one.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)の意思(いし)<b>に反(はん)して</b>治療(ちりょう)を行(おこな)うことはできない。
Treatment cannot be given against the patient's own wishes.
</pre>

<pre>
大方(おおかた)の予想(よそう)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、新人(しんじん)候補(こうほ)が当選(とうせん)した。
Against most predictions, the newcomer won the seat.
</pre>

<pre>
その発言(はつげん)は会社(かいしゃ)の方針(ほうしん)<b>に反(はん)する</b>ものとして問題(もんだい)になった。
The remark caused trouble as something contrary to company policy.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)書(しょ)の記載(きさい)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、実際(じっさい)の重(おも)さは倍(ばい)近(ちか)くあった。
Contrary to what the manual stated, the actual weight was nearly double.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a written pattern. It belongs to journalism, reports, contracts, notices and
academic prose, and in speech it appears only in formal registers - a presentation, a
news broadcast, a manager explaining a decision. In conversation people say
<code>思(おも)っていたのと違(ちが)って</code> or simply
<code>意外(いがい)に</code>.

It has almost no emotional content of its own. What colour the sentence has comes from
the noun: <code>予想(よそう)に反(はん)して</code> reads as mild surprise,
<code>命令(めいれい)に反(はん)して</code> as insubordination,
<code>意思(いし)に反(はん)して</code> as a violation of someone's rights. The
pattern itself just registers the mismatch, which is precisely why formal writing likes
it - it can report a breach without the writer appearing to editorialise.

The `する`-form is worth practising, because it is where learners stall.
<code>に反(はん)する</code> before a noun is extremely common in fixed collocations:
<code>規則(きそく)に反(はん)する行為(こうい)</code>,
<code>事実(じじつ)に反(はん)する報道(ほうどう)</code>,
<code>良心(りょうしん)に反(はん)する判断(はんだん)</code>. The bare
<code>に反(はん)し</code>, without `て`, is the clipped written form you meet in
headlines and formal prose.

Finally, note that a clause cannot attach directly. If what is being defied is
something someone said or promised, nominalise it first -
<code>約束(やくそく)したことに反(はん)して</code> - or, more naturally, find the
noun that names it.

## Contrast

### に反(はん)して vs とは違(ちが)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とは違(ちが)って</code> reports a **difference**.
<code>に反(はん)して</code> reports a **defiance**: something had a claim on the
outcome, and the outcome ignored it.

</div>

<pre>
兄(あに)<b>とは違(ちが)って</b>、弟(おとうと)は人前(ひとまえ)で話(はな)すのが得意(とくい)だ。
Unlike his older brother, the younger one is good at speaking in public.
</pre>

<pre>
周囲(しゅうい)の予想(よそう)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、弟(おとうと)が代表(だいひょう)に選(えら)ばれた。
Contrary to what everyone around them expected, it was the younger brother who was chosen.
</pre>

A person can be different *from* somebody; a person cannot be contrary *to* somebody,
so ❌<code>兄(あに)に反(はん)して</code> is wrong. Only expectations, rules,
intentions and the like can be run against.

### に反(はん)して vs にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にもかかわらず` concedes a **fact** that was overcome.
<code>に反(はん)して</code> names a **standard** that was contradicted.

</div>

<pre>
悪天候(あくてんこう)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、工事(こうじ)は予定(よてい)どおり進(すす)んだ。
Despite the bad weather, the work went ahead on schedule.
</pre>

<pre>
当初(とうしょ)の計画(けいかく)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、工事(こうじ)は大幅(おおはば)に遅(おく)れた。
Contrary to the original plan, the work fell badly behind.
</pre>

Bad weather is a fact and an obstacle; a plan is a standard the outcome failed to
match. Swap them and both sentences break: weather predicts nothing to be defied, and
a plan is not an obstacle you push through.

### に反(はん)して vs とおりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they are opposites built on the same noun. `どおり` = **as
expected**; <code>に反(はん)して</code> = **the other way from what was expected**.

</div>

<pre>
予想(よそう)<b>どおり</b>、決勝(けっしょう)は接戦(せっせん)になった。
As expected, the final was a close-run thing.
</pre>

<pre>
予想(よそう)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、決勝(けっしょう)は一方的(いっぽうてき)な試合(しあい)になった。
Contrary to expectations, the final turned into a one-sided affair.
</pre>

Learning them as a pair is efficient, because they take the same nouns:
<code>期待(きたい)どおり</code> and
<code>期待(きたい)に反(はん)して</code>,
<code>指示(しじ)どおり</code> and
<code>指示(しじ)に反(はん)して</code>. Note the rendaku:
<code>予想(よそう)どおり</code>, not とおり, after a noun.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われた。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われた。
  Rain is a fact, not an expectation. Only something that predicts or prescribes can be
  run against.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)<b>に反(はん)して</b>、私(わたし)は朝(あさ)が苦手(にがて)だ。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)<b>とは違(ちが)って</b>、私(わたし)は朝(あさ)が苦手(にがて)だ。
  People are compared with <code>とは違(ちが)って</code>. <code>に反(はん)して</code> cannot take a
  person as its noun.

- ❌ 規則(きそく)に反(はん)して行為(こうい) → ✅ 規則(きそく)<b>に反(はん)する</b>行為(こうい)
  Modifying a noun needs the `する` form. The `して` form is adverbial and cannot sit in
  front of a noun.

- ❌ 思(おも)った<b>に反(はん)して</b> → ✅ 予想(よそう)<b>に反(はん)して</b>（or 思(おも)っていたのとは違(ちが)って）
  A verb clause cannot attach directly. Use the noun that names the expectation.

# ～てまで

**Meaning** · going so far as to; even at the cost of - an extreme means is named, and the speaker doubts it was worth it

`てまで` measures how far somebody was willing to go. The `まで` is the ordinary "as far
as": the て-clause marks the outer limit of what was done, and the main clause then
asks, or denies, whether the goal justified going that far. *Lying to win. Borrowing
money to travel. Wrecking your health to keep working.*

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`てまで` is *the one that minds most openly*, and it is *register-neutral*. It is not a
concession made to be polite; it is a concession made in order to disapprove.

## Formation

The verb takes the て-form. A noun naming the extreme act uses `までして` instead.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + まで | 借(か)りる → 借(か)りてまで |
| Verb (て → で) | で-form + まで | 並(なら)ぶ → 並(なら)んでまで |
| Verb, emphatic | て-form + までも | 傷(きず)つける → 傷(きず)つけてまでも |
| する-verb | ～してまで | 徹夜(てつや)する → 徹夜(てつや)してまで |
| Noun | Noun + までして | 借金(しゃっきん) → 借金(しゃっきん)までして |
| Typical main clause | ～たくない／～必要(ひつよう)はない／～ことはない | - → うそをついてまで勝(か)ちたくない |
| Typical main clause | ～だろうか／～価値(かち)があるのか | - → そこまでする価値(かち)があるのか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the main clause must **judge**. A want, a refusal, a "there's no
need", or a question. ❌<code>走(はし)ってまで間(ま)に合(あ)った</code> is not a
sentence, because nothing is being weighed - that is just
<code>走(はし)って間(ま)に合(あ)った</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the mirror image of this pattern is `～てでも`, "even if I have to". The two
share almost the same picture and point in opposite directions: `てでも` is the
speaker's own determination, `てまで` is usually somebody else's excess.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
うそをつい<b>てまで</b>勝(か)ちたいとは思(おも)わない。
I don't want to win badly enough to lie about it.
</pre>

<pre>
徹夜(てつや)し<b>てまで</b>やる必要(ひつよう)はないよ。
There's no need to go so far as to stay up all night over it.
</pre>

<pre>
借金(しゃっきん)をし<b>てまで</b>旅行(りょこう)に行(い)くのはどうかと思(おも)う。
Going on holiday on borrowed money strikes me as questionable.
</pre>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)並(なら)ん<b>でまで</b>食(た)べたいラーメンではない。
It isn't ramen I want badly enough to queue three hours for.
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)をこわし<b>てまで</b>働(はたら)くことはない。
There is no call to work yourself into ill health.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)を傷(きず)つけ<b>てまで</b>手(て)に入(い)れたい地位(ちい)なのだろうか。
Is it really a position worth hurting people to get?
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)め<b>てまで</b>、父親(ちちおや)の介護(かいご)を選(えら)んだ。
She went as far as leaving her job in order to care for her father.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りに嫌(きら)われ<b>てまで</b>自分(じぶん)の意見(いけん)を通(とお)す勇気(ゆうき)は、私(わたし)にはなかった。
I didn't have the courage to push my own view at the cost of everyone disliking me.
</pre>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)のためとはいえ、家族(かぞく)との時間(じかん)を削(けず)っ<b>てまで</b>勉強(べんきょう)を続(つづ)けるべきだろうか。
Passing matters, but should one really carry on studying at the expense of time with one's family?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`てまで` is neutral as to formality - it works in conversation, in essays and in
newspaper columns alike - but it is not neutral as to attitude. The default reading is
critical. Naming an extreme act and then attaching a want, a need or a question invites
the listener to find the act excessive, and that is how a Japanese listener will take
it unless something else in the sentence says otherwise.

Two things can shift it. First, a straightforwardly positive main clause turns it into
admiration or at least neutral report:
<code>会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めてまで夢(ゆめ)を追(お)った</code> can be told
either way, and the tone of voice decides. Second, in the first person it can express
your own limit: <code>そこまでしてほしくない</code> - I would not want anyone to go to
that trouble for me.

The noun frame `までして` is worth having as well.
<code>借金(しゃっきん)までして</code>, <code>徹夜(てつや)までして</code>,
<code>手術(しゅじゅつ)までして</code> - the noun names the extreme act directly, and
the meaning is identical. Choose it when the extremity is a *thing* rather than an
action you want to spell out with a verb.

Because the pattern is a judgement, it can be sharp. Saying
<code>そんなことをしてまで勝(か)ちたいの?</code> to somebody's face is a rebuke, not a
question. Used about yourself it is safe; used about the person you are talking to,
handle it as you would any criticism.

## Contrast

### てまで vs てでも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てでも` = "even if I have to", and the speaker **will**. `てまで` =
"going so far as to", and the speaker thinks somebody **shouldn't have**.

</div>

<pre>
タクシーを使(つか)っ<b>てでも</b>開演(かいえん)までに着(つ)きたい。
I want to get there before curtain-up even if I have to take a taxi.
</pre>

<pre>
タクシーを使(つか)っ<b>てまで</b>見(み)に行(い)く映画(えいが)ではない。
It isn't a film worth taking a taxi to get to.
</pre>

The same means, the opposite verdict. `てでも` looks forward and expresses resolve, so
it pairs with <code>～たい</code>, <code>～つもりだ</code> and volitional endings;
`てまで` looks at the price and finds it too high, so it pairs with negatives and
rhetorical questions. Swapping them reverses what you meant.

### てまで vs ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` concedes a **condition**. `てまで` measures the **cost of the
means**.

</div>

<pre>
高(たか)く<b>ても</b>この辞書(じしょ)は買(か)うつもりだ。
I'm going to buy this dictionary even if it's expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
生活(せいかつ)を切(き)りつめ<b>てまで</b>買(か)うような辞書(じしょ)ではない。
It isn't the kind of dictionary you cut back on living expenses to buy.
</pre>

`ても` accepts a circumstance the speaker does not control - the price is what it is.
`てまで` names something the speaker chooses to do, and asks whether the choice makes
sense. If the clause is an action taken deliberately in pursuit of the goal, you want
`てまで`.

### てまで vs plain まで "until"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - dictionary form + `まで` = **until**. て-form + `まで` = **to the
extent of**.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が止(や)む<b>まで</b>ここで待(ま)ちましょう。
Let's wait here until the rain stops.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)に濡(ぬ)れ<b>てまで</b>写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)りたくはない。
I don't want a photograph badly enough to get soaked for it.
</pre>

The particle is the same word in both - `まで` marks a limit - but the limit is
temporal in the first and a limit of effort in the second, and the verb form is what
tells them apart. Getting the form wrong turns "going so far as to get wet" into "until
I get wet", which is a different sentence entirely.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 徹夜(てつや)する<b>まで</b>準備(じゅんび)した。 → ✅ 徹夜(てつや)し<b>てまで</b>準備(じゅんび)した。
  The dictionary form gives "until", not "to the extent of". This pattern needs the
  て-form.

- ❌ 走(はし)っ<b>てまで</b>電車(でんしゃ)に間(ま)に合(あ)った。 → ✅ 走(はし)って電車(でんしゃ)に間(ま)に合(あ)った。
  Nothing is being weighed here, so the pattern has no work to do. `てまで` needs a
  main clause that judges the cost.

- ❌ 無理(むり)をし<b>てまで</b>今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に仕上(しあ)げます。 → ✅ 無理(むり)をし<b>てでも</b>今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に仕上(しあ)げます。
  Announcing your own determination is `てでも`. `てまで` would have you criticising
  the very thing you are promising to do.

- ❌ 借金(しゃっきん)まで<b>してまで</b>車(くるま)を買(か)った。 → ✅ 借金(しゃっきん)<b>までして</b>車(くるま)を買(か)った。（or 借金(しゃっきん)をし<b>てまで</b>）
  Pick one frame. `までして` after the noun and `てまで` after the verb do the same job,
  and using both at once doubles the particle.

## Practice

**1.** 順番(じゅんばん)を抜(ぬ)かし____乗(の)りたい電車(でんしゃ)ではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

順番(じゅんばん)を抜(ぬ)かし<b>てまで</b>乗(の)りたい電車(でんしゃ)ではない。

Queue-jumping is the extreme means, and the negative main clause delivers the verdict:
not worth it. The て-form plus `まで` is the frame.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)したら、タクシーを使(つか)っ（てまで・てでも）帰(かえ)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

タクシーを使(つか)っ<b>てでも</b>帰(かえ)ります。

The speaker is stating their own intention, and `てでも` is the determined one. `てまで`
would mean the speaker considers taking a taxi excessive - which contradicts the
promise to get home.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I wouldn't go so far as to skip work to see a concert."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

仕事(しごと)を休(やす)ん<b>でまで</b>コンサートに行(い)こうとは思(おも)わない。

<code>休(やす)む</code> gives the で-form, so the joint is
<code>休(やす)んでまで</code>. The main clause is a refusal, which is the natural
company this pattern keeps.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 家(いえ)を売(う)るまで、息子(むすこ)を留学(りゅうがく)させた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 家(いえ)を売(う)っ<b>てまで</b>、息子(むすこ)を留学(りゅうがく)させた。

With the dictionary form, `まで` means "until the house is sold", which is not the
intended sense. The て-form turns it into "went as far as selling the house".

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 睡眠(すいみん)時間(じかん)を削(けず)っ<b>てまで</b>練習(れんしゅう)している。
(b) 睡眠(すいみん)時間(じかん)を削(けず)っ<b>ても</b>練習(れんしゅう)している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents cutting back on sleep as the extreme lengths the person has gone to, and
invites a judgement on it - usually "that's too far", occasionally "that's real
dedication", depending on the rest of the sentence and the speaker's tone.

(b) is awkward, and that awkwardness is instructive. `ても` concedes a condition the
subject does not control, but losing sleep here is the very choice being described, so
there is nothing to concede. Where the clause names a deliberate sacrifice made in
pursuit of the goal, `てまで` is the pattern that fits.

</details>

# ～くせして

**Meaning** · even though; and yet - the same jab as `くせに`, delivered more roughly

`くせして` is `くせに` with the `に` swapped for `して`, and the swap does exactly what
you would expect: it drags the phrase further into spoken Japanese and sharpens the
sneer. The grammar is identical - a gap between what somebody is and how they behave,
with the speaker holding it against them - but the tone is blunter, more personal and
markedly less printable.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`くせして` occupies the far corner: *minds the most*, *least formal of all*. Put it
beside `とも` from earlier in this batch and you have the two extremes of the group in
one glance.

## Formation

Identical to `くせに`. <code>くせ</code> is the noun "habit, trait", so what precedes it
is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + くせして | 知(し)っている → 知(し)っているくせして |
| Verb (past) | plain past + くせして | 遅刻(ちこく)した → 遅刻(ちこく)したくせして |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + くせして | 泳(およ)げない → 泳(およ)げないくせして |
| い-adjective | plain form + くせして | 若(わか)い → 若(わか)いくせして |
| な-adjective | な + くせして | 下手(へた) → 下手(へた)なくせして |
| Noun | の + くせして | 新人(しんじん) → 新人(しんじん)のくせして |
| Sentence-final jab | …くせして。 | - → 分(わ)かってるくせして。 |
| Contracted in speech | くせして → くせしてさ | - → 知(し)らないくせしてさ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two restrictions from `くせに` carry over unchanged. The clause
must be about a **person**, and **both halves must be about the same person**.
❌<code>私(わたし)が手伝(てつだ)ったくせして、彼(かれ)はお礼(れい)も言(い)わない</code>
splits its attention across two people and needs `のに`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>くせ</code> is a noun, which is why a noun in front of it takes `の`
and a な-adjective takes `な`. If you can build the `くせに` version, you can build this
one; nothing about the attachment changes.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
知(し)ってる<b>くせして</b>、とぼけないでよ。
You know perfectly well - don't play innocent.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの<b>くせして</b>、生意気(なまいき)なことを言(い)うな。
You're a child - don't talk back like that.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)は片付(かたづ)けない<b>くせして</b>、人(ひと)にばかりうるさい。
He never tidies up himself, and yet he's forever on at everyone else.
</pre>

<pre>
泳(およ)げない<b>くせして</b>、深(ふか)いところまで行(い)こうとする。
He can't even swim and he still tries to go out of his depth.
</pre>

<pre>
本当(ほんとう)は行(い)きたい<b>くせして</b>、興味(きょうみ)ないふりをしている。
She's dying to go and she's putting on this act of not being interested.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の<b>くせして</b>、あいさつもろくにしない。
He's brand new here and he can't even manage a greeting.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)が遅刻(ちこく)した<b>くせして</b>、人(ひと)の時間(じかん)にはうるさいんだから。
He was the one who turned up late, and he's the one who fusses about other people's time.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに反対(はんたい)していた<b>くせして</b>、いざ始(はじ)まったら誰(だれ)よりも楽(たの)しんでいた。
He'd argued so hard against it, and once it started he enjoyed it more than anybody.
</pre>

<pre>
料理(りょうり)なんてしたことない<b>くせして</b>、味(あじ)にだけは文句(もんく)を言(い)うんだよね。
He's never cooked a thing in his life and yet he's got opinions about the seasoning.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`くせして` is casual spoken Japanese and belongs nowhere else. It does not appear in
essays, reports, business e-mail or polite conversation, and it does not take
ます-endings comfortably - a sentence that begins
<code>新人(しんじん)のくせして</code> and ends
<code>…していません</code> pulls in two directions at once. In writing it turns up
almost exclusively as dialogue.

It is ruder than `くせに`, which is itself not polite. The difference is a matter of
degree rather than of kind: `くせに` covers everything from affectionate teasing to
real contempt, whereas `くせして` leans towards the harsh end and is heard as
name-calling more readily. Among close friends and within a family that harshness can
read as banter - <code>下手(へた)なくせしてよく言(い)うよ</code> between two
people who like each other is a joke. Said upwards at work, or about somebody who is
present and is not a friend, it is an insult.

There is a tendency for `くせして` to sound a shade more casual and more everyday-spoken
than `くせに`, and it turns up often in the speech of children and in television
dialogue. That is a tendency, not a rule; plenty of adults use it in ordinary
grumbling.

The sentence-final use is worth having.
<code>分(わ)かってるくせして。</code> - trailing off, with nothing after it - is a
complete accusation, and it is one of the commonest ways the pattern actually occurs.

Because the whole point is disapproval, `くせして` cannot introduce praise. If you want
to say somebody is doing well for what they are, you need `にしては`, which is a
compliment; `くせして` would take the same facts and turn them into an insult.

## Contrast

### くせして vs くせに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same grammar, same restrictions. `くせに` is the standard form;
`くせして` is rougher, more spoken, and harder to soften.

</div>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の<b>くせに</b>、なかなか度胸(どきょう)があるな。
For a newcomer, he's got some nerve about him.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の<b>くせして</b>、口(くち)ばかり達者(たっしゃ)だ。
He's the new boy and all he's got is a big mouth.
</pre>

The first can be said with a grin and half-admiration. The second resists that
reading: `くせして` presses the speaker's contempt into the sentence, so it needs a
warm delivery and a close relationship to come out as teasing. If you are unsure of
the relationship, `くせに` is the safer of the two - and `のに` is safer still.

### くせして vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` is about a **situation**; `くせして` is about a **person**,
and it is always a judgement on them.

</div>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだ<b>のに</b>、熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらない。
I took the medicine and the fever still hasn't gone down.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)まない<b>くせして</b>、治(なお)らないって文句(もんく)ばかり言(い)う。
He won't take the medicine and then complains that he isn't getting better.
</pre>

The first sentence has no target. Fevers cannot be blamed, so `くせして` is impossible
there. The second is aimed squarely at a person, and both halves are about that same
person - the two conditions this pattern always imposes.

### くせして vs にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both measure somebody against what they are. `にしては` is usually
**generous**; `くせして` is always **hostile**.

</div>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて<b>にしては</b>、うまく焼(や)けているよ。
For a first attempt, that's baked rather well.
</pre>

<pre>
料理(りょうり)が得意(とくい)な<b>くせして</b>、人(ひと)には作(つく)ってくれない。
She's a good cook and she still won't make anything for anybody.
</pre>

`にしては` allows the outcome to exceed the standard and is a compliment more often
than not. `くせして` insists the outcome falls short of what the person's position
demands, or that it contradicts it. Note that only `にしては` is usable with a
non-person: <code>値段(ねだん)のわりに</code> and
<code>値段(ねだん)にしては</code> are both fine, and
❌<code>値段(ねだん)のくせして</code> is not standard.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>だくせして</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>のくせして</b>
  <code>くせ</code> is a noun, so a noun links to it with `の`. `だ` cannot modify a
  noun.

- ❌ 上手(じょうず)<b>くせして</b> → ✅ 上手(じょうず)<b>なくせして</b>
  な-adjectives keep their `な`. The link is the same as before any other noun.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)が決(き)めた<b>くせして</b>、私(わたし)が叱(しか)られた。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)が決(き)めた<b>のに</b>、私(わたし)が叱(しか)られた。
  Two different people. `くせして` judges one person across both clauses and cannot
  split its attention.

- ❌ 新人(しんじん)の<b>くせして</b>、よく頑張(がんば)っています。 → ✅ 新人(しんじん)<b>にしては</b>、よく頑張(がんば)っています。
  Praise and `くせして` do not mix, and neither do `くせして` and ます-form. If you mean
  it kindly, `にしては` is the pattern.

## Practice

**1.** 自分(じぶん)では何(なに)もしない____、人(ひと)の失敗(しっぱい)にはうるさい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自分(じぶん)では何(なに)もしない<b>くせして</b>、人(ひと)の失敗(しっぱい)にはうるさい。

Both halves are about the same person, and the sentence is a criticism of that person,
which is exactly what `くせして` is for. The ない-form attaches with no change.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 課長(かちょう)にメールを送(おく)るとき、「ご多忙(たぼう)____、ご対応(たいおう)ありがとうございました」と書(か)く。(のくせして・にもかかわらず)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「ご多忙(たぼう)<b>にもかかわらず</b>、ご対応(たいおう)ありがとうございました」

An e-mail to a superior is formal written Japanese, and `くせして` is a spoken insult.
`にもかかわらず` is the polite way to acknowledge that someone went out of their way.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually and with an edge: "You're the one who forgot, and you're blaming me?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

忘(わす)れたのは自分(じぶん)の<b>くせして</b>、人(ひと)のせいにするの?

Both clauses are about the listener, and the speaker is plainly annoyed - the
conditions `くせして` needs. Note that this is genuinely sharp; say it only where you
would say the English.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この店(みせ)は安(やす)いくせして、味(あじ)がいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>のに</b>、味(あじ)がいい。

Two problems. `くせして` wants a person as its subject, and it cannot be followed by
something complimentary. `のに` is neutral about both, and here it carries the pleasant
surprise the sentence intends.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新人(しんじん)の<b>くせに</b>、なかなかやるじゃないか。
(b) 新人(しんじん)の<b>くせして</b>、なかなかやるじゃないか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is teasing praise. `くせに` is wide enough to be said with a grin, and the
<code>じゃないか</code> ending makes it warm - the speaker is impressed and pretending
not to be.

(b) says the same words with a rougher edge. Because `くせして` leans harder on the
contempt, the compliment has to fight the frame, and the sentence only reads as
friendly if the speaker's tone and the relationship carry it. Between people who are
not close, (b) risks sounding like a put-down dressed up as praise.

</details>
