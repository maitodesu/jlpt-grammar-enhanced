# N2 - Speaking directly to people


---

# ～ではないか

**Meaning** · come on, let's do it - a rallying call; and, on a plain clause, "but look, it *is* so" - a point pushed in front of the listener

`ではないか` is a negative question that is not asking anything. On a volitional it
turns "shall we?" into a call to arms: <code>始(はじ)めようではないか</code>, "let
us begin". On an ordinary clause it holds a fact up to the listener's face: "you
*did* promise." Both uses are aimed at a person, and both expect agreement rather
than an answer.

## Formation

`ではないか` attaches to the plain form. Nothing is added in front of it - in
particular, no `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (volitional) - the appeal | volitional + ではないか | 戦(たたか)う → 戦(たたか)おうではないか |
| Verb (plain) - pressing a point | plain form + ではないか | 約束(やくそく)した → 約束(やくそく)したではないか |
| い-adjective | plain form + ではないか | 危(あぶ)ない → 危(あぶ)ないではないか |
| な-adjective | stem + ではないか | 立派(りっぱ)だ → 立派(りっぱ)ではないか |
| Noun | noun + ではないか | 嘘(うそ)だ → 嘘(うそ)ではないか |
| Casual | では → じゃ | 行(い)こうじゃないか・言(い)ったじゃないか |
| Polite | ではありませんか・じゃないですか | 進(すす)めようではありませんか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the volitional row is the "let's" meaning; every other row is the
"isn't it / look at that" meaning. One form, two jobs, and the form of the verb in
front of it tells you which.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
さあ、みんなで<b>始(はじ)めようではないか</b>。
Right then - let's all get started.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)した<b>じゃないか</b>。
You promised, didn't you.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あぶ)ない<b>じゃないか</b>。急(きゅう)に飛(と)び出(だ)すな。
That's dangerous! Don't go running out like that.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな時間(じかん)に電話(でんわ)してくるなんて、非常識(ひじょうしき)<b>ではないか</b>。
Ringing someone at this hour is downright inconsiderate.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)失敗(しっぱい)したくらいで、あきらめることはない<b>じゃないか</b>。
You've failed once - that's hardly a reason to give up.
</pre>

<pre>
ほら、外(そと)を見(み)てごらん。雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もっている<b>ではないか</b>。
Look outside - the snow has piled right up.
</pre>

<pre>
今年(ことし)こそ優勝(ゆうしょう)を目指(めざ)そう<b>ではありませんか</b>、皆(みな)さん。
This year, let's go for the title - what do you say, everyone?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)で決(き)まったことなのだから、まずは一度(いちど)やってみよう<b>ではないか</b>。
It was settled at the meeting, so let's at least give it a try.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The full <code>ではないか</code> is platform Japanese. It belongs to speeches, editorials,
pep talks and fiction, and in ordinary conversation it sounds declamatory - a
tendency that also makes it lean male and older when it does turn up in speech. A
manager can open a meeting with <code>考(かんが)えようではありませんか</code>; a
friend suggesting lunch cannot.

Everyday speech uses the contraction. <code>じゃないか</code> is the casual form and
leans male; <code>じゃない</code> with a rising tail is the version everyone uses,
regardless of gender; <code>じゃないですか</code> is its polite counterpart. The
meaning does not shift as you move down that ladder - only how theatrical you
sound.

Two warnings about the "pressing a point" use. First, it is delivered downward or
sideways: aimed at a superior, <code>言(い)ったじゃないですか</code> reads as an
accusation, and <code>申(もう)し上(あ)げましたよね</code> or
<code>～のではないでしょうか</code> is what you want. Second,
<code>じゃないですか</code> has developed a filler use in modern conversation -
<code>私(わたし)、辛(から)いものが苦手(にがて)じゃないですか</code>, "as you
know, I can't take spicy food" - said about things the listener could not possibly
know. Many speakers find it grating. Recognise it; do not build a habit of it.

The volitional appeal is also warm rather than bossy: it includes the speaker.
That is why it works for a coach or a chairman and reads oddly from someone who is
not part of the effort.

## Contrast

### ～ではないか vs ～ましょう・～ようか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～ましょう</code> and <code>～ようか</code> *invite*, and
leave room for no. <code>～ではないか</code> *rallies*, and assumes yes.

</div>

<pre>
そろそろ出(で)かけ<b>ましょうか</b>。
Shall we head out soon?
</pre>

<pre>
最後(さいご)まであきらめずに走(はし)り続(つづ)けよう<b>ではないか</b>。
Let us keep running to the finish, and not give up.
</pre>

The first checks with the listener. The second announces a shared resolve and
carries the room with it - which is why it fits a captain before a match and not a
colleague at the lift.

### ～ではないか vs ～でしょう・～だろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>でしょう</code> asks the listener to *agree*.
<code>ではないか</code> tells the listener they already know, and points.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)、おいしい<b>でしょう</b>。
This place is good, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
ここは禁煙(きんえん)だと書(か)いてある<b>ではないか</b>。
It says right there that this is a no-smoking area.
</pre>

<code>でしょう</code> is companionable; it hands the judgement over. <code>ではないか</code>
is not asking - the listener has overlooked something obvious, and the sentence
says so. That edge is exactly why it so often carries reproach.

### ～ではないか vs ～のではないか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a bare clause + <code>ではないか</code> points at a fact in
front of you. <code>の・ん</code> + <code>ではないか</code> floats a possibility you
are not sure of.

</div>

<pre>
この計算(けいさん)、間違(まちが)っている<b>ではないか</b>。
This calculation is wrong - look at it.
</pre>

<pre>
この計算(けいさん)、間違(まちが)っている<b>のではないか</b>。
I wonder whether this calculation might be wrong.
</pre>

One `の` turns a rebuke into a hedge. The second sentence is the polite one in an
office, and <code>間違(まちが)っているのではないでしょうか</code> is politer still.
Note also that the conjecture use does **not** take a volitional in front of it:
there is no ❌<code>行(い)こうのではないか</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あれは嘘(うそ)<b>だではないか</b>。 → ✅ あれは嘘(うそ)<b>ではないか</b>。
  Nouns and な-adjectives attach bare. The `だ` is already inside <code>では</code>.

- ❌ みんなで行(い)き<b>ましょうではないか</b>。 → ✅ みんなで行(い)こう<b>ではありませんか</b>。
  Keep one register. Plain volitional pairs with <code>ではないか</code>, and the
  polite version puts the politeness in <code>ありません</code>, not in the verb.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)こう<b>ではないか</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)き<b>ませんか</b>。
  The rallying use is spoken downward, to a group you are leading. Pointed at your
  own boss it sounds as though you have taken charge of him.

- ❌ 「寒(さむ)いではないか。」「はい、寒(さむ)くないです。」 → ✅ 「寒(さむ)いではないか。」「本当(ほんとう)ですね、窓(まど)を閉(し)めます。」
  It looks like a yes/no question and is not one. Answering the literal negative
  misses that the speaker is asserting, not asking.

## Practice

**1.** 来年(らいねん)は新(あたら)しい工場(こうじょう)を建(た)てよう____。（rallying a meeting, plain style）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

建(た)てよう<b>ではないか</b>。

The volitional plus <code>ではないか</code> is the standard shape for a leader
putting a shared goal to a room. In polite style the same sentence ends
<code>建(た)てようではありませんか</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この数字(すうじ)、どこかで見(み)た覚(おぼ)えがある。去年(きょねん)の資料(しりょう)と同(おな)じ（ではないか・のではないか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

同(おな)じ<b>ではないか</b>。

You are looking straight at the two documents, so this is the "point at the fact"
use. <code>のではないか</code> would mean you merely suspect a match and have not
checked - a reasonable thing to say, but a different sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casual and male-leaning: "Hey, you left the light on, didn't you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

おい、電気(でんき)をつけっぱなしにした<b>じゃないか</b>。

The reproach use on a plain past verb. The contracted <code>じゃないか</code> is the
form this actually takes in speech; the full <code>ではないか</code> here would sound
like a line from a novel.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 先生(せんせい)、もっと早(はや)く教(おし)えてくれたじゃないですか。ひどいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先生(せんせい)、もっと早(はや)く教(おし)えてくださればよかったのに。

Two problems. <code>くれた</code> is too plain for a teacher, and more importantly
<code>じゃないですか</code> aimed upward turns a complaint into an accusation. A
regretful <code>～ばよかったのに</code> keeps the same content without the finger
pointing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 時間(じかん)が足(た)りない<b>じゃないですか</b>。
(b) 時間(じかん)が足(た)りない<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) asserts a shortfall the listener should already have seen, and pushes it at
them. Between friends it is ordinary; in a meeting it lands as blame, and it can
also be heard as the conversational filler "we're short on time, as you know".

(b) offers the same observation as a possibility for the group to consider. It is
the safe form in any professional setting: the speaker takes responsibility for
raising it without accusing anyone of missing it.

</details>

# お～願う

**Meaning** · a formal, businesslike "please do this" - the request form of announcements, notices and office correspondence

<code>お + verb stem + 願(ねが)います</code> is how an organisation asks you to do
something. Literally it is "I request that you…", and that is how it behaves: it is
unmistakably polite, but it is the politeness of a station platform or a memo
rather than of one person asking another for a favour. Bend it into a real favour
by switching to the potential, <code>願(ねが)えますか</code>.

## Formation

The frame is a honorific prefix, then the verb, then <code>願(ねが)う</code>. The
verb never carries `する` or `て`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb / る-verb) | お + ます-stem + 願(ねが)います | 待(ま)つ → お待(ま)ち願(ねが)います |
| する-verb (Sino-Japanese noun) | ご + noun + 願(ねが)います | 連絡(れんらく)する → ご連絡(れんらく)願(ねが)います |
| Softened into a request | お・ご … 願(ねが)えますか | 確認(かくにん)する → ご確認(かくにん)願(ねが)えますか |
| Softer still | お・ご … 願(ねが)えないでしょうか | 知(し)らせる → お知(し)らせ願(ねが)えないでしょうか |
| Written / letter style | お・ご … 願(ねが)いたく存(ぞん)じます | 了承(りょうしょう)する → ご了承(りょうしょう)願(ねが)いたく存(ぞん)じます |
| Prefix exceptions | a few Sino-Japanese nouns take お | 電話(でんわ) → お電話(でんわ)願(ねが)います |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the two regular classes (五段(ごだん) / 一段(いちだん), Group 1 /
Group 2). The rule of thumb for the prefix is お before a native Japanese word and
ご before a Sino-Japanese one, with a short list of everyday exceptions -
お電話(でんわ), お返事(へんじ), お食事(しょくじ), お約束(やくそく) - that
take お despite being Sino-Japanese.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
しばらくお待(ま)ち<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
Please wait a short while.
</pre>

<pre>
こちらにご記入(きにゅう)<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
Please fill this in here.
</pre>

<pre>
白線(はくせん)の内側(うちがわ)までお下(さ)がり<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
Please step back behind the white line.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)では携帯電話(けいたいでんわ)の電源(でんげん)をお切(き)り<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
Please switch mobile phones off inside the hall.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あす)の午前中(ごぜんちゅう)までにお返事(へんじ)<b>願(ねが)えますか</b>。
Could I ask for your reply by tomorrow morning?
</pre>

<pre>
恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、こちらの書類(しょるい)にご署名(しょめい)<b>願(ねが)えますか</b>。
Sorry to trouble you - could you sign this document?
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)が長引(ながび)いておりますので、今(いま)しばらくご辛抱(しんぼう)<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
The work is running long, so we ask for your patience a little longer.
</pre>

<pre>
詳(くわ)しい日程(にってい)が決(き)まりましたら、お知(し)らせ<b>願(ねが)えないでしょうか</b>。
If the detailed schedule gets settled, might I ask you to let me know?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is institutional Japanese. You meet it in station and airport announcements,
on notices in banks and hospitals, in internal memos, and in business email - and
almost never between friends. Nobody says お待(ま)ち願(ねが)います to a mate
outside a cinema; that is 待(ま)ってて.

Its particular flavour is that it is polite without being soft. Grammatically it
reports the speaker's request rather than asking the listener's willingness, so it
does not really leave room for no. That is exactly right for a public
announcement and slightly cold when one person asks another for something. The
potential form is the repair: <code>ご確認(かくにん)願(ねが)えますか</code>
means "could I have your confirmation", and now the listener's ability to agree is
part of the sentence. <code>願(ねが)えないでしょうか</code> is humbler again and
is safe upward, to a client or a senior colleague.

In fully polite correspondence <code>願(ねが)う</code> tends to be absorbed into
the longer formula
<code>ご確認(かくにん)くださいますようお願(ねが)い申(もう)し上(あ)げます</code>.
That is the ceiling of written politeness; the bare
<code>ご確認(かくにん)願(ねが)います</code> sits a good way below it and reads as
brisk, internal, everyday business style. Some readers find the bare form curt when
it is aimed at them personally - a tendency worth knowing before you use it on a
customer.

## Contrast

### お～願(ねが)います vs お～ください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>お～ください</code> frames the action as the listener's
kindness; <code>お～願(ねが)います</code> frames it as the speaker's request.

</div>

<pre>
こちらにお<b>掛(か)けください</b>。
Please have a seat.
</pre>

<pre>
順番(じゅんばん)にお並(なら)び<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
Please queue in order.
</pre>

<code>ください</code> comes from <code>くださる</code>, "to graciously give", so the
listener is doing you a kindness - it is warm, and it is what service staff use
when they want you to feel looked after. <code>願(ねが)います</code> is drier and
more procedural; it is the voice that manages a crowd. Both are formal, and in many
notices either would pass, but the tone is not identical.

### お～願(ねが)えますか vs ～ていただけますか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～ていただけますか</code> is the all-purpose spoken
request. <code>お～願(ねが)えますか</code> is a fixed formula with a written
flavour, and only works with verbs that take the honorific prefix.

</div>

<pre>
この資料(しりょう)を直(なお)して<b>いただけますか</b>。
Could you fix this document for me?
</pre>

<pre>
この資料(しりょう)をご修正(しゅうせい)<b>願(ねが)えますか</b>。
Could I ask for a correction to this document?
</pre>

They sit at a similar level of politeness. The difference is texture: the first
sounds like a person speaking, the second like a line in a message. Note also that
<code>～ていただけますか</code> attaches to any verb at all, while the
<code>お～</code> frame does not - there is no ❌お死(し)に願(ねが)います and no
natural お-form for many ordinary verbs. When in doubt in speech, use
<code>～ていただけますか</code>.

### お～願(ねが)います vs お願(ねが)いします

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>お願(ねが)いします</code> takes a **noun** in front of
it. <code>お～願(ねが)います</code> puts a **verb stem inside** the frame. They are
two constructions, not one.

</div>

<pre>
確認(かくにん)を<b>お願(ねが)いします</b>。
Please check it. / I'd like to ask you to check it.
</pre>

<pre>
ご確認(かくにん)<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
Please confirm.
</pre>

The everyday <code>お願(ねが)いします</code> is the one learners already know, and
it is far more common in speech; it can even stand alone as "please" when the
request is obvious from context. Fusing the two produces
❌<code>お待(ま)ちお願(ねが)いします</code>, which has the frame twice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ しばらくお<b>待(ま)つ願(ねが)います</b>。 → ✅ しばらくお<b>待(ま)ち願(ねが)います</b>。
  The frame takes the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

- ❌ ご連絡(れんらく)<b>し願(ねが)います</b>。 → ✅ ご連絡(れんらく)<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
  With a Sino-Japanese noun the `する` disappears entirely -
  <code>ご + noun + 願(ねが)います</code>.

- ❌ こちらで<b>待(ま)ち願(ねが)います</b>。 → ✅ こちらでお<b>待(ま)ち願(ねが)います</b>。
  The honorific prefix is part of the pattern, not an optional decoration. Without
  it the sentence is simply broken.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、明日(あす)までにご確認(かくにん)<b>願(ねが)います</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、明日(あす)までにご確認(かくにん)<b>願(ねが)えないでしょうか</b>。
  Aimed at one senior person, the plain <code>願(ねが)います</code> issues an
  instruction. The potential plus <code>でしょうか</code> turns it back into a
  request.

## Practice

**1.** 順番(じゅんばん)が来(き)ましたらお呼(よ)びしますので、あちらの席(せき)でお座(すわ)り____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あちらの席(せき)でお座(すわ)り<b>願(ねが)います</b>。

A waiting-room instruction to the public, which is the pattern's home ground.
お掛(か)けください would also be natural here and is a shade warmer.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 内容(ないよう)に間違(まちが)いがないか、（お確認(かくにん)願(ねが)います・ご確認(かくにん)願(ねが)います）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ご確認(かくにん)願(ねが)います</b>。

確認(かくにん) is a Sino-Japanese noun, so it takes ご. お goes with native verbs
in this frame - お待(ま)ち, お知(し)らせ, お下(さ)がり - apart from the short
list of exceptions such as お電話(でんわ) and お返事(へんじ).

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese as a written notice: "Please refrain from eating and drinking inside the library."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

図書館内(としょかんない)での飲食(いんしょく)はご遠慮(えんりょ)<b>願(ねが)います</b>。

<code>ご遠慮(えんりょ)願(ねが)います</code> is the standard notice wording for
"please refrain from". ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください is equally idiomatic and a little
softer.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、こちらの用紙(ようし)にお書(か)くお願(ねが)いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、こちらの用紙(ようし)にご記入(きにゅう)<b>願(ねが)います</b>。

Two frames have been welded together, and the verb is in the dictionary form. Pick
one: ご記入(きにゅう)願(ねが)います, or the noun construction
記入(きにゅう)をお願(ねが)いします.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ご署名(しょめい)<b>願(ねが)います</b>。
(b) ご署名(しょめい)<b>願(ねが)えますでしょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the requirement. It is polite and entirely normal from a member of staff
processing a form, but it does not invite a reply - you sign.

(b) puts the request in the potential and adds a hedge, so it asks whether the
listener is willing. It is what you use when the person could reasonably say no, or
when they outrank you. It is also noticeably heavier; stacking politeness like this
is common in customer-facing speech, and some speakers consider
願(ねが)えますでしょうか overdone next to the plainer 願(ねが)えますか.

</details>

# ～たまえ

**Meaning** · an old-fashioned command spoken downward - "do this" from someone who assumes he outranks you

<code>たまえ</code> is the imperative of <code>給(たま)う</code>, an honorific verb
meaning "to bestow", so a thousand years ago it was a *softened* order. It is not
soft now. In modern Japanese it survives as the voice of the professor, the
commanding officer and the old company president - overwhelmingly in fiction. Learn
to read it; say <code>～てください</code> instead.

## Formation

<code>たまえ</code> attaches to the ます-stem, exactly where <code>なさい</code> goes.
It does not conjugate: there is no past, no negative and no polite form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb / る-verb) | ます-stem + たまえ | 座(すわ)る → 座(すわ)りたまえ |
| する-verb | し + たまえ | 説明(せつめい)する → 説明(せつめい)したまえ |
| 来(く)る | き + たまえ | 来(く)る → 来(き)たまえ |
| Negative | no negative form exists | 騒(さわ)ぐ → 騒(さわ)ぐな（not ❌騒(さわ)ぎたまえない） |
| Spelling | usually kana, sometimes 給(たま)え | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)みたまえ |
| Same verb as an honorific auxiliary | ます-stem + たまう（literary） | 与(あた)える → 与(あた)えたまう |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the two regular classes (五段(ごだん) / 一段(いちだん), Group 1 /
Group 2). 来(く)る is irregular, and its stem here is **き**, the same stem as
来(き)ます - not こ, which belongs to 来(こ)ない and 来(こ)られる.

</div>

## Examples

These are the kinds of line the form actually occurs in: dialogue by a superior in
a novel, a manga or a dubbed film.

<pre>
まあ、座(すわ)り<b>たまえ</b>。
Well now - sit down.
</pre>

<pre>
君(きみ)、ちょっと来(き)<b>たまえ</b>。
You there - come here a moment.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あす)までに報告書(ほうこくしょ)を出(だ)し<b>たまえ</b>。
Have the report on my desk by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)するな。あとは私(わたし)に任(まか)せ<b>たまえ</b>。
Don't worry. Leave the rest to me.
</pre>

<pre>
「失礼(しつれい)します。」「うむ、入(はい)り<b>たまえ</b>。」
"Excuse me." "Mm. Come in."
</pre>

<pre>
その辺(へん)でやめておき<b>たまえ</b>。君(きみ)のためを思(おも)って言(い)っているのだ。
That's enough of that. I'm saying it for your own good.
</pre>

<pre>
諸君(しょくん)、この事件(じけん)が何(なに)を意味(いみ)するのか、よく考(かんが)え<b>たまえ</b>。
Gentlemen, think hard about what this incident means.
</pre>

<pre>
若(わか)いうちに、できるだけ多(おお)くの本(ほん)を読(よ)み<b>たまえ</b>。
While you are young, read as many books as you possibly can.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Do not produce this form.** It is not a register that is available to a modern
learner, and there is no situation in ordinary life where it is the right choice.
Where you will meet it is on the page and the screen: pre-war and mid-century
novels, detective fiction, manga and anime, and above all the dubbing and
subtitling of foreign films, where <code>たまえ</code> is the standard way to make an
English-speaking colonel or headmaster sound like an English-speaking colonel or
headmaster. Between friends it appears occasionally as a joke - someone doing the
voice on purpose.

Three things mark it. It is strongly **male**. It is **old-fashioned**: even the
older men who might once have used it are mostly gone from workplaces. And it is
**downward** - it announces that the speaker considers the listener his junior.
That last point is why it cannot be rescued by tone. A student saying
座(すわ)りたまえ to a classmate is not being quaint; they are being rude, or
funny, and it will be read as one of the two.

Older reference books sometimes describe <code>たまえ</code> as a "polite command".
That was true in the Meiji era, when it was the mild alternative to a bare
imperative, and it is why the form is filed as N2 grammar at all. It has not been
true in living speech for a long time.

Use instead: <code>～てください</code> for anyone at all in normal life,
<code>～なさい</code> when an adult is instructing a child or a teacher a class, and
the plain imperative <code>座(すわ)れ</code> when bluntness is genuinely wanted -
a coach, an emergency, a shout.

One relative worth recognising: the same verb survives as an honorific auxiliary
<code>～たまう</code> in literary and religious prose -
<code>神(かみ)が与(あた)えたまう恵(めぐ)み</code>. That is a different beast
again, respectful rather than commanding, and equally not for production.

## Contrast

### ～たまえ vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～てください</code> is what you say. <code>～たまえ</code>
is what you read. Whenever you are tempted by the second, use the first.

</div>

<pre>
詳(くわ)しいことは、あとで説明(せつめい)し<b>たまえ</b>。
Explain the details to me later. （fiction - a superior speaking）
</pre>

<pre>
詳(くわ)しいことは、あとで説明(せつめい)し<b>てください</b>。
Please explain the details later. （anyone, any workplace）
</pre>

The propositional content is identical. What changes is everything social: the
first says the speaker is senior, male and probably in a book; the second says
nothing about the speaker at all, which is what you want from a request form.

### ～たまえ vs ～なさい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are commands from above and both take the ます-stem, but
<code>なさい</code> is alive and <code>たまえ</code> is not.

</div>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)を終(お)わらせてから遊(あそ)び<b>なさい</b>。
Finish your homework before you go and play.
</pre>

<pre>
終(お)わったら報告(ほうこく)し<b>たまえ</b>。
Report to me when you've finished.
</pre>

<code>なさい</code> has a defined modern home: parent to child, teacher to pupil,
and printed instructions on exam papers. It sounds caring or strict depending on
delivery, and adults do not say it to each other. <code>たまえ</code> has no
equivalent live home - only the fictional one - and where <code>なさい</code> leans
maternal and instructional, <code>たまえ</code> leans masculine and hierarchical.

### ～たまえ vs the plain imperative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>座(すわ)れ</code> is blunt. <code>座(すわ)りたまえ</code>
is not politer to a modern ear - it is haughtier.

</div>

<pre>
おい、そこに<b>座(すわ)れ</b>。
Oi - sit down there.
</pre>

<pre>
そこに座(すわ)り<b>たまえ</b>。
Take a seat there. （said by someone who is about to interrogate you）
</pre>

The plain imperative is rough but socially readable: a coach, a parent losing
patience, a warning shout. <code>たまえ</code> adds a layer of assumed rank on top,
which is why it feels theatrical rather than merely rude. Neither belongs in a
learner's active speech; of the two, the plain imperative is at least a form you
will need to understand every day.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 座(すわ)る<b>たまえ</b> → ✅ 座(すわ)り<b>たまえ</b>
  It attaches to the ます-stem, in the same slot as <code>なさい</code>, not to the
  dictionary form.

- ❌ 来(こ)<b>たまえ</b> → ✅ 来(き)<b>たまえ</b>
  来(く)る takes こ only in 来(こ)ない, 来(こ)よう and 来(こ)られる. The
  ます-stem is き.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)、こちらに座(すわ)り<b>たまえ</b>。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、こちらにお掛(か)け<b>ください</b>。
  The form points downward. Aimed at anyone senior it is not merely impolite, it is
  faintly absurd.

- ❌ 静(しず)かにし<b>たまえない</b> → ✅ 静(しず)かにし<b>ないでくれ</b>（or 騒(さわ)ぐな）
  <code>たまえ</code> is a frozen imperative. It has no negative, no past and no
  ます-form.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this line, and who is speaking?
君(きみ)、この数字(すうじ)をもう一度(いちど)調(しら)べ直(なお)し<b>たまえ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In fiction - a novel, a manga, or a dubbed film - spoken by an older man to a
junior: a professor to a student, a department head to a new employee, an officer
to a subordinate. The combination of 君(きみ) as the address term and
<code>たまえ</code> as the command is close to a signature for that character type.
In a real modern office you would hear
もう一度(いちど)調(しら)べ直(なお)してもらえますか.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: まず私(わたし)の話(はなし)を聞(き)き<b>たまえ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

まず私(わたし)の話(はなし)を聞(き)い<b>てください</b>。

<code>～てください</code> is the default replacement in almost every case. If the
speaker is an adult addressing a child, 聞(き)き<b>なさい</b> also works; if the
situation is urgent and informal, 聞(き)い<b>てくれ</b>.

</details>

**3.** Find and fix the errors: 明日(あす)の朝(あさ)、九時(くじ)に来(こ)たまえない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あす)の朝(あさ)、九時(くじ)に来(き)<b>たまえ</b>。

Two faults. The stem of 来(く)る before <code>たまえ</code> is き, not こ. And
<code>たまえ</code> cannot be negated - to forbid something you would need a
different form altogether, such as 来(く)るな.

</details>

**4.** You are a new employee and want your section chief to look over a document.
Which is possible: ご確認(かくにん)し<b>たまえ</b> / 確認(かくにん)し<b>てください</b> /
ご確認(かくにん)いただけますか - and why?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ご確認(かくにん)いただけますか is the right choice; 確認(かくにん)してください is
grammatical but a little bare when aimed upward. ご確認(かくにん)したまえ is
impossible twice over: <code>たまえ</code> is a downward command, and it does not
combine with the honorific ご frame - the two are pulling in opposite directions.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 待(ま)て。
(b) 待(ま)ち<b>たまえ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the plain imperative: sharp, urgent, and socially neutral in the sense that
anybody might shout it - a policeman, a friend stopping you from stepping into the
road. You will hear it in real life.

(b) is the same instruction issued by someone claiming authority over you, in an
old-fashioned masculine voice. It is unhurried where (a) is urgent, and it belongs
to a character rather than to a person. A learner needs to understand (b) and
should never say it.

</details>

# だって

**Meaning** · but…! because…! - the word you push back with when you are defending yourself against what the other person just said

`だって` opens a reply. Someone has told you off, told you to do something, or
asked why you did not, and `だって` announces that you are about to give your side.
It is not neutral: built into it is the refusal to simply accept what was said.

## Usage

`だって` stands at the **head of a whole clause**, usually at the very start of your
turn in a conversation, and it needs something to push against. It cannot join two
clauses inside one sentence - that is the job of `から` and `ので`.

The typical shape is a two-part exchange:

- **A** asks or complains.
- **B** answers with `だって` + the reason, very often finished off with `んだもん`,
  `んだから` or `じゃない`.

That final `もん` is what makes the combination sound so much like a child; without
it the same sentence lands as an ordinary, if unrepentant, explanation. `だって`
itself has no polite form. The politeness of the sentence lives in its ending -
<code>だって、忙(いそが)しかったんです</code> is possible, though the word still colours the
whole reply as an excuse.

Two other `だって` exist and are unrelated to this one. There is the particle
`だって` meaning "even, too" (<code>子供(こども)だってわかる</code>, "even a child
knows that"), which follows a noun rather than opening a clause; and there is the
hearsay `だって` at the *end* of a sentence
(<code>来(こ)ないんだって</code>, "apparently he isn't coming"). Position tells
you which you have: front of the clause, this one; after a noun, "even"; at the end,
"I hear".

## Examples

<pre>
「どうして食(た)べないの。」「<b>だって</b>、嫌(きら)いなんだもん。」
"Why aren't you eating it?" "Because I don't like it, that's why."
</pre>

<pre>
「もう寝(ね)なさい。」「<b>だって</b>、まだ眠(ねむ)くないよ。」
"Go to bed now." "But I'm not sleepy yet."
</pre>

<pre>
「走(はし)れば間(ま)に合(あ)ったのに。」「<b>だって</b>、こんな靴(くつ)じゃ走(はし)れないよ。」
"You'd have made it if you'd run." "Come on - not in these shoes."
</pre>

<pre>
「また遅刻(ちこく)ですか。」「<b>だって</b>、事故(じこ)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっていたんです。」
"Late again?" "But the trains were stopped because of an accident."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`だって` is spoken, casual, and never written except in dialogue. It does not appear
in a report, a business email or a formal apology.

Its core flavour is self-defence, and self-defence sounds childish when the other
person has a point. The stereotype attached to it is a sulking child or a
girlfriend in a drama, and the pairing <code>だって…んだもん</code> is the full
version of that voice. Adults do use it - constantly, with friends, with family,
and often with a knowing smile that acknowledges the excuse is an excuse - so the
word is not off-limits to grown-ups. It leans young, and in the stereotype somewhat
feminine, but those are tendencies rather than rules; a man protesting to a friend
that <code>だって、時間(じかん)がなかったんだよ</code> is entirely ordinary.

Where it genuinely goes wrong is upward and outward: to a boss, a client, a
teacher or a police officer. There, any sentence beginning `だって` turns an apology
into an argument, however polite the ending is. The adult repair is to put the
reason before the apology and drop the protest word:
<code>電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっておりまして、遅(おく)れました。申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません</code>.
In writing, <code>～ものですから</code> and <code>～ため</code> carry the same content
with none of the attitude.

Compare `でも`, the other way to start a reply with "but". `でも` simply contradicts
- it can be followed by anything, including another opinion. `だって` promises a
**reason**, and specifically a reason why you are not at fault. If nothing follows
that could serve as a justification, `だって` sounds unfinished.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がなかった<b>だって</b>行(い)けませんでした。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がなかった<b>から</b>行(い)けませんでした。
  `だって` cannot sit between two clauses. It opens a clause; it does not link one
  to another.

- ❌ 「報告(ほうこく)が遅(おく)れていますね。」「<b>だって</b>、忙(いそが)しかったんです。」（to your manager） → ✅ 「申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。急(きゅう)な対応(たいおう)が続(つづ)いておりまして…」
  The polite ending does not rescue it. Opening with `だって` tells your manager you
  are contesting the criticism.

- ❌ <b>だってです</b>、知(し)らなかったんです。 → ✅ <b>だって</b>、知(し)らなかったんです。
  `だって` never inflects. It has no ですform, no past, no negative.

- ❌ <b>だって</b>私(わたし)は新人(しんじん)です。（meaning "even I am a beginner"） → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>だって</b>新人(しんじん)です。
  The "even" `だって` follows the noun it applies to. Moving it to the front of the
  sentence changes it into the protest word.

## Practice

**1.** Fill the blank: 「どうして電話(でんわ)してくれなかったの。」「____、番号(ばんごう)を知(し)らなかったんだもん。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「<b>だって</b>、番号(ばんごう)を知(し)らなかったんだもん。」

The reply is defending itself against a complaint and ends in `んだもん`, which is
the partner `だって` almost always turns up with. `でも` would also fit the slot
grammatically but would sound like the start of a different point rather than an
excuse.

</details>

**2.** Translate into Japanese, casual: "Don't blame me - you're the one who said we didn't need a reservation."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)のせいにしないでよ。<b>だって</b>、予約(よやく)はいらないって言(い)ったのは君(きみ)じゃない。

`だって` introduces the justification for refusing the blame, and the
<code>～じゃない</code> ending pushes the fact back at the listener. In this shape
`だって` is doing exactly the job it was made for.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance, and say which one you could use with a
client:
(a) <b>だって</b>、資料(しりょう)が届(とど)かなかったんです。
(b) 資料(しりょう)が届(とど)かなかった<b>ものですから</b>、準備(じゅんび)が間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) and (b) give the same reason, but (a) leads with a protest: it answers an
accusation and declines to take the blame, and however polite the `んです` ending
is, the client hears an argument.

(b) presents the same fact as background to an apology and never contradicts
anyone. Only (b) is usable with a client, and it is also the form you would write.
Keep (a) for friends and family.

</details>

# ～んだって

**Meaning** · apparently…, I hear… - passing on something you were told, in casual conversation

`んだって` is <code>のだ</code> plus the quoting `って`. The `って` is what makes it
hearsay; the `ん` is what makes it *news* - it frames the information as the story
behind something, which is why it is the shape gossip and updates naturally take.
With a rising tail it turns round and asks: <code>辞(や)めるんだって？</code>, "is
it true you're quitting?"

## Formation

Everything in front of `んだって` is in plain form, exactly as it would be before
`んだ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + んだって | 辞(や)める → 辞(や)めるんだって |
| Verb (past) | plain past + んだって | 受(う)かった → 受(う)かったんだって |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + んだって | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないんだって |
| い-adjective | plain form + んだって | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いんだって |
| な-adjective | ～な + んだって | 苦手(にがて)だ → 苦手(にがて)なんだって |
| Noun | ～な + んだって | 双子(ふたご)だ → 双子(ふたご)なんだって |
| Polite-flavoured | ～んですって | 中止(ちゅうし)だ → 中止(ちゅうし)なんですって |
| Asking for confirmation | ～んだって？（rising） | 引(ひ)っ越(こ)す → 引(ひ)っ越(こ)すんだって？ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `な` on nouns and な-adjectives is not optional and not a `だ`. It is
the same `な` that appears in <code>双子(ふたご)なんです</code>. Speakers often add
`さ` on the end - <code>来(く)るんだってさ</code> - which makes the relay sound
more offhand.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)、雨(あめ)な<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently it's going to rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん、来月(らいげつ)結婚(けっこん)する<b>んだって</b>。
I hear Tanaka's getting married next month.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)、先週(せんしゅう)閉(し)まっちゃった<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently that shop closed down last week.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)くん、試験(しけん)に受(う)かった<b>んだって</b>？おめでとう。
Yamada - I hear you passed the exam? Congratulations.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)、来週(らいしゅう)から大阪(おおさか)に転勤(てんきん)な<b>んですって</b>。
They say the section chief is transferring to Osaka from next week.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの近(ちか)くに大(おお)きな公園(こうえん)ができる<b>んだって</b>。楽(たの)しみだね。
I hear a big park's going in near us. Something to look forward to.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しくできたパン屋(や)、朝(あさ)早(はや)く行(い)かないと売(う)り切(き)れちゃう<b>んだってさ</b>。
Apparently that new bakery sells out unless you get there early.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)が言(い)ってたんだけど、あのドラマ、最終回(さいしゅうかい)がとんでもなかった<b>んだって</b>。
My sister was telling me the last episode of that drama was something else.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a conversation word. It belongs to speech and to the writing that imitates
speech - messages, chat, dialogue in fiction - and it is out of place in an essay,
a report or anything addressed to a customer. In those, the same content goes into
`そうだ`, `とのことだ` or <code>と伺(うかが)っております</code>.

The source is usually left vague. `んだって` says "this reached me", not "X told
me", and that vagueness is part of its texture: it is the form news travels in. If
you do want to name the source you put it in front as a separate clause -
<code>母(はは)が言(い)ってたんだけど、…んだって</code>.

`んですって` is the polite-flavoured version, and in adult speech it leans female;
men relaying the same news more often say `んだってさ` or `んだとさ`. Those are
tendencies, not rules, and neither form is formal - `んですって` is polite in shape
but still chatty, so it does not fix the register problem in a business setting.

The rising <code>んだって？</code> is worth handling carefully. It means "I've heard
this about you - true?", and while it is friendly between people who know each
other, aimed at someone you barely know it can sound like you have been discussing
them. <code>ご結婚(けっこん)されるとお聞(き)きしました</code> is the safe version.

One structural limit: you cannot hearsay yourself. Facts you know first-hand -
your own plans, your own feelings - take plain `んだ`, not `んだって`.

## Contrast

### ～んだって vs ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same information, different room. `そうだ` is the neutral
reporting form; `んだって` is its casual spoken twin.

</div>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、駅(えき)の前(まえ)にホテルが建(た)つ<b>そうです</b>。
I gather a hotel is going up in front of the station next year.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)、駅(えき)の前(まえ)にホテルが建(た)つ<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently they're putting a hotel up by the station next year.
</pre>

The content is identical. `そうです` can be written down, said to a client, and
used in a news summary; `んだって` cannot. Note too that hearsay `そうだ` attaches to
the plain form with nothing in between - ❌<code>建(た)つなそうです</code> - while
`んだって` inserts `な` after nouns and な-adjectives.

### ～んだって vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `んだって` relays **words somebody said**. `らしい` draws a
conclusion, and needs no speaker behind it at all.

</div>

<pre>
隣(となり)の人(ひと)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently the neighbour is moving out. （someone told me so）
</pre>

<pre>
隣(となり)の人(ひと)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>らしい</b>。
It looks like the neighbour is moving out. （boxes in the hallway, a van booked）
</pre>

`らしい` covers both reported speech and inference from evidence, so it is the
wider word; `んだって` is specifically a relay. That is why `らしい` can sound
non-committal - it may be your own guess - while `んだって` puts the responsibility
on whoever told you.

### ～んだって vs bare ～って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `って` can quote **anything** somebody said, including orders
and questions. `んだって` only reports a **statement of fact**.

</div>

<pre>
早(はや)く来(こ)い<b>って</b>。
He says get over here, quick.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)な<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently the meeting is off.
</pre>

You cannot rewrite the first with `んだって`; there is no ❌<code>来(こ)いんだって</code>,
because `のだ` needs a proposition, not a command. Where both are possible - 
<code>中止(ちゅうし)だって</code> versus <code>中止(ちゅうし)なんだって</code> -
the bare `って` is a flat relay of the message, while the `ん` version presents it as
the explanation for what is going on around you, which is why it is the natural
choice when you are breaking news to someone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あの人(ひと)、留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>だんだって</b>。 → ✅ あの人(ひと)、留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>なんだって</b>。
  Nouns link with `な`. The `だ` of <code>留学生(りゅうがくせい)だ</code> cannot sit
  in front of `ん`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)、来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>んだって</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)、来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>んだ</b>。
  Your own plans are not hearsay. Reporting yourself this way sounds as though you
  learned of your move from a third party.

- ❌ 来(き)ます<b>んだって</b>。 → ✅ 来(く)る<b>んだって</b>。
  `ん` takes the plain form in front of it, never ます.

- ❌ （in a written report）市(し)の人口(じんこう)が減(へ)っている<b>んだって</b>。 → ✅ 市(し)の人口(じんこう)が減(へ)っている<b>とのことだ</b>。
  The information is fine; the register is not. Written hearsay is `そうだ`,
  `とのことだ` or <code>ということだ</code>.

## Practice

**1.** Fill the blank: 山田(やまだ)さん、来週(らいしゅう)から東京(とうきょう)の本社(ほんしゃ)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本社(ほんしゃ)<b>なんだって</b>。

A noun predicate takes `な` before `ん`. If you wanted the same news in a written
message to a colleague you would switch to
本社(ほんしゃ)勤務(きんむ)になるそうです.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部屋(へや)の電気(でんき)がついているから、誰(だれ)か中(なか)にいる（んだって・らしい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

誰(だれ)か中(なか)にいる<b>らしい</b>。

You are reasoning from the light, not repeating what anyone said, and only `らしい`
covers inference. `んだって` would require somebody to have told you there was
someone in the room.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casual: "I hear the new teacher is really strict."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今度(こんど)の先生(せんせい)、すごく厳(きび)しい<b>んだって</b>。

An い-adjective attaches straight to `ん` with no `な`. Between friends this is the
natural form; in a staffroom you would say
厳(きび)しいそうです.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 友(とも)だちが言(い)ってたけど、あの映画(えいが)、とても有名(ゆうめい)だんですって。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 友(とも)だちが言(い)ってたけど、あの映画(えいが)、とても有名(ゆうめい)<b>なんですって</b>。

<code>有名(ゆうめい)</code> is a な-adjective, so it links with `な`, not `だ`. The
named source in front is perfectly normal - `んですって` does not object to you
saying who told you.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 来週(らいしゅう)のテスト、中止(ちゅうし)だ<b>って</b>。
(b) 来週(らいしゅう)のテスト、中止(ちゅうし)な<b>んだって</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat relay of a message: "word is, the test is cancelled." It reports the
words and nothing more, and it is what you would say if you were simply passing on
an announcement you half heard.

(b) frames the cancellation as the news of the moment - the thing that explains why
everyone is talking, or why you no longer need to revise. It is the form you use
when you are telling someone something you expect them to react to. The difference
is small, and in most conversations either would pass without comment.

</details>

# ～とか

**Meaning** · I gather that…, something about… - relaying information at arm's length, without vouching for the details

This `とか` reports what you heard while quietly declining to guarantee it. Two
things drive it: you genuinely are not sure of the details, and - just as often -
you do not want to be the person who stated something firmly about somebody else.
It is hearsay with a shrug built in, and in adult speech that shrug is frequently a
courtesy rather than vagueness.

## Formation

`とか` follows the plain form. The reporting verb behind it -
<code>聞(き)きました</code>, <code>言(い)っていました</code> - is optional, and
leaving it off is what produces the characteristic trailing sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + とか | 戻(もど)る → 戻(もど)るとか |
| Verb (past) | plain past + とか | 辞(や)めた → 辞(や)めたとか |
| い-adjective | plain form + とか | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいとか |
| な-adjective | ～だ + とか | 大変(たいへん)だ → 大変(たいへん)だとか |
| Noun | noun（+ だ）+ とか | 風邪(かぜ) → 風邪(かぜ)だとか |
| With the verb spelled out | … とか聞(き)きました・言(い)っていました | 来(こ)ないとか聞(き)きました |
| As a hedged reason | … とかで | 事故(じこ)があったとかで遅(おく)れています |
| Naming something vaguely | … とかいう + noun | 田中(たなか)とかいう人(ひと) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a sentence may simply stop at `とか`. <code>来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)だとか。</code> is complete as spoken Japanese: the "…is what I
heard" is left for the listener to supply, and the omission is part of the softness.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は風邪(かぜ)だ<b>とか</b>で、今日(きょう)はお休(やす)みです。
The section chief is off today - something about a cold.
</pre>

<pre>
あの二人(ふたり)、来年(らいねん)結婚(けっこん)する<b>とか</b>聞(き)きました。
I heard those two are getting married next year.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)<b>とか</b>いう人(ひと)から電話(でんわ)がありましたよ。
Someone called - Tanaka, or something like that.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)のせいで来月(らいげつ)まで通(とお)れない<b>とか</b>言(い)っていましたよ。
They were saying it's closed to traffic until next month because of the works.
</pre>

<pre>
なんでも、新(あたら)しい社長(しゃちょう)はまだ30代(だい)だ<b>とか</b>。
Word is the new president is still in his thirties.
</pre>

<pre>
体調(たいちょう)を崩(くず)された<b>とか</b>で、今回(こんかい)のご出席(しゅっせき)は難(むずか)しいそうです。
He's apparently unwell, so attending this time is going to be difficult for him.
</pre>

<pre>
あの辺(あた)りは昔(むかし)川(かわ)だった<b>とか</b>で、地面(じめん)が弱(よわ)いらしいですよ。
That area used to be a river, from what I hear, so the ground is supposed to be soft.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)さんが海外(かいがい)の大学(だいがく)に進(すす)まれた<b>とか</b>、奥(おく)さんから伺(うかが)いました。
I gather from your wife that your son has gone on to a university abroad.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`とか` is spoken Japanese, but unlike most casual hearsay markers it is at home in
polite adult conversation. Combined with honorific verbs -
<code>退職(たいしょく)なさるとか</code>,
<code>お戻(もど)りになるとか</code> - it is a standard way to raise something you
have heard about a third party without asserting it. The hedge protects everyone:
if the report turns out to be wrong, you never claimed it was right.

That same hedge is its weakness. In a context where accuracy is the point - a
handover, a report to a manager, a written record - `とか` reads as if you could not
be bothered to check. <code>中止(ちゅうし)になるとか</code> to your boss invites
「とか、じゃなくて、どっちなの」. Use `とのことです` or name the source when it
matters.

Two related uses are worth keeping straight from this one. `とか` also lists
examples - <code>お茶(ちゃ)とかコーヒーとか</code> - and in modern casual speech
that listing `とか` has spread into a general softener attached to single nouns
(<code>お茶(ちゃ)とか飲(の)む？</code>). That softener leans young and is one of
the forms criticised as vague, padded speech; it is not the hearsay pattern, though
the family resemblance is obvious - both are ways of not being definite.

<code>～とかいう</code> deserves its own line. Attached to a name it means "some X
or other" and carries a faint distancing: you do not know the person, or you are
signalling that they are of no great importance to you. Said about someone the
listener is close to, it can sting.

## Contrast

### ～とか vs ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` reports and stands behind the report. `とか` reports
and steps back from it.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から料金(りょうきん)が上(あ)がる<b>そうです</b>。
I hear the fee is going up from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)から料金(りょうきん)が上(あ)がる<b>とか</b>。
Something about the fee going up next month.
</pre>

Both are hearsay and both are polite. `そうです` presents the information as
reliable enough to act on; `とか` leaves the door open for it to be wrong, or for
the details to be off. If a colleague asks you to confirm a date, `そうです` answers
the question and `とか` does not.

### ～とか vs ～んだって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `んだって` is casual and delivers the news. `とか` can be
polite, and softens the news on the way out.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さん、会社(かいしゃ)辞(や)める<b>んだって</b>。
Tanaka's quitting, apparently.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんは会社(かいしゃ)をお辞(や)めになる<b>とか</b>。
I gather Mr Tanaka may be leaving the company.
</pre>

The first is what you say to a friend at lunch; the second is what you say to a
senior colleague when you are not certain and would rather not be quoted. `んだって`
cannot be made polite enough for the second situation - `んですって` is still chatty
- and `とか` in the first would sound oddly guarded among friends.

### hearsay ～とか vs listing ～とか…とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a **clause**, `とか` is hearsay. After **nouns, in a
series**, it is "things like X and Y".

</div>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は映画(えいが)<b>とか</b>買(か)い物(もの)<b>とか</b>に行(い)きます。
At weekends I go to the cinema, shopping, that kind of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
週末(しゅうまつ)は出(で)かけられない<b>とか</b>言(い)っていましたよ。
She was saying something about not being able to go out at the weekend.
</pre>

The listing use needs at least the possibility of a second item and attaches to
nouns; the hearsay use follows a finished clause and usually stands alone or leads
into a verb of saying. In practice the shape in front of `とか` settles it
immediately.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は来(き)ます<b>とか</b>言(い)っていました。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は来(く)る<b>とか</b>言(い)っていました。
  The clause in front of `とか` is plain form, not ます.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、私(わたし)も会場(かいじょう)で見(み)た<b>とか</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)、私(わたし)も会場(かいじょう)で見(み)ました。
  You were there. Hedged hearsay about your own direct experience sounds either
  evasive or as though you doubt your own eyes.

- ❌ （reporting to your manager）納品(のうひん)が遅(おく)れる<b>とか</b>です。 → ✅ 納品(のうひん)が遅(おく)れる<b>とのことです</b>。
  Where the information has to be acted on, drop the hedge and name the fact - or
  the source.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)<b>とかいう</b>人(ひと)に聞(き)いてください。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に聞(き)いてください。
  <code>とかいう</code> distances you from the person named. Applied to someone the
  listener works under, it is close to insulting.

## Practice

**1.** Fill the blank: 山田(やまだ)さんは急(きゅう)な用事(ようじ)ができた____で、先(さき)に帰(かえ)られました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

用事(ようじ)ができた<b>とか</b>で、先(さき)に帰(かえ)られました。

<code>とかで</code> is the hedged-reason shape: you are passing on the reason you
were given without vouching for it. <code>ということで</code> would state the reason
as established.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会議(かいぎ)の開始(かいし)は10時(じゅうじ)に変更(へんこう)になった（とか・とのこと）です。 You are
telling the whole team the time has moved.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

変更(へんこう)になった<b>とのこと</b>です。

Everyone is about to plan their morning around this, so the hedge is a liability.
`とか` would leave the team unsure whether to turn up at ten, and someone would have
to ask you again.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Apparently he'd hurt his leg, so he didn't run in the end."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

足(あし)を痛(いた)めた<b>とか</b>で、結局(けっきょく)走(はし)らなかったそうです。

<code>とかで</code> carries "apparently, because…" in one piece. The main clause can
keep its own hearsay marker; the two are reporting different layers, the reason and
the outcome.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 先生(せんせい)が来週(らいしゅう)試験(しけん)をしますとか言(い)っていました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 先生(せんせい)が来週(らいしゅう)試験(しけん)をする<b>とか</b>言(い)っていました。

`とか` takes the plain form. The politeness of the sentence belongs at the end, on
<code>言(い)っていました</code> - quoted clauses in Japanese are not made polite
to match the frame.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新(あたら)しい支店(してん)が来年(らいねん)できる<b>そうです</b>。
(b) 新(あたら)しい支店(してん)が来年(らいねん)できる<b>とか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) passes on the information as something you consider solid. The listener can
repeat it, and if pressed you would be willing to say where you heard it.

(b) passes on the same words while declining to stand behind them - the timing, or
the whole plan, might be wrong. In polite conversation this is often deliberate
courtesy rather than ignorance: you are raising a rumour about the company without
appearing to spread it. In a briefing, though, it would sound underprepared.

</details>

# ～ものか

**Meaning** · like hell I will; as if that were true - a question in shape, a flat and angry refusal in force

<code>負(ま)けるものか</code> looks like "is it a thing that I lose?" and means "I
am **not** going to lose." The form is positive and the meaning is negative, because
it is a rhetorical question thrown at the listener or at yourself. What comes with
it is emotion: defiance, contempt, or a refusal to believe a word of it. Recognise
it on the page; in your own speech say <code>絶対(ぜったい)に～ない</code> or
<code>～わけがない</code>.

## Formation

The clause in front is in noun-modifying shape, because <code>もの</code> is a noun.
The verb is almost always in the **plain non-past**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ものか | 負(ま)ける → 負(ま)けるものか |
| Verb (potential) | potential + ものか | 信(しん)じられる → 信(しん)じられるものか |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものか | 怖(こわ)い → 怖(こわ)いものか |
| な-adjective | ～な + ものか | 平気(へいき)だ → 平気(へいき)なものか |
| Noun | ～な + ものか | 本物(ほんもの)だ → 本物(ほんもの)なものか |
| Casual contraction | もんか | 行(い)く → 行(い)くもんか |
| Rough emphatic | もんか + よ | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)くもんかよ |
| Polite in shape | ものですか・もんですか | 許(ゆる)す → 許(ゆる)すものですか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not put `ない` in front of it to get a negative meaning. The pattern is
already negative. <code>行(い)くものか</code> means "I'm not going";
<code>行(い)けないものか</code> is a completely different sentence, a wistful "isn't
there some way I could go?" - see the third contrast below.

</div>

## Examples

These are the kinds of line the pattern really occurs in: fiction dialogue, interior
monologue, narration.

<pre>
負(ま)ける<b>ものか</b>。まだ終(お)わっていない。
I will not lose. It isn't over yet.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなやつに頼(たの)む<b>もんか</b>。
Like hell I'd ask him for help.
</pre>

<pre>
二度(にど)とこの店(みせ)に来(く)る<b>もんか</b>。
I am never setting foot in this shop again.
</pre>

<pre>
そんな話(はなし)、信(しん)じられる<b>ものか</b>。
As if anyone could believe a story like that.
</pre>

<pre>
平気(へいき)な<b>ものか</b>。手(て)が震(ふる)えているじゃないか。
Fine, is he? His hands are shaking.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)が本当(ほんとう)のことを言(い)う<b>ものですか</b>。
That man, tell the truth? Hardly.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)があっても、この子(こ)だけは渡(わた)す<b>ものか</b>と思(おも)った。
Whatever happened, I told myself, this child I will not hand over.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなところで終(お)われる<b>ものか</b>。やり残(のこ)したことが多(おお)すぎる。
I can't be finished here - there is far too much I haven't done.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this pattern; do not reach for it.** It is not a neutral way of saying
no. Every occurrence carries heat, and the heat is the point: refusal
(<code>謝(あやま)るものか</code>, "I'll never apologise"), contempt
(<code>頼(たの)むもんか</code>), or open disbelief
(<code>信(しん)じられるものか</code>). You will meet it constantly in novels, manga
and anime, in sports narration, in song lyrics, and in the internal voice of a
character bracing themselves - and you will also hear it in real arguments between
people who know each other well enough to shout.

What makes it a poor choice for a learner is that there is no mild setting. The
casual `もんか` leans male and young and reads as petulant when the speaker is not
genuinely angry - it is the sound of a child refusing to give in. The apparently
polite `ものですか` and `もんですか` are worse, not better: the politeness is the
frame, and what sits inside it is a sneer. <code>いらっしゃるものですか</code> aimed
at a colleague is far more cutting than a plain refusal, and it leans female in the
stereotype. Say it by accident and you have picked a fight in keigo.

Note also that the target is often not the listener at all - a great many examples
are addressed to the speaker's own resolve, which is why narration is full of them.
That does not make the form usable in conversation; it means the sentences you
remember from fiction are mostly monologue.

Say instead: <code>絶対(ぜったい)に～ません</code> or
<code>二度(にど)と～ません</code> when you are refusing;
<code>～わけがありません</code> or <code>～はずがありません</code> when you disbelieve
something; and <code>それはちょっと…</code> or
<code>遠慮(えんりょ)しておきます</code> when you simply want to say no politely.

## Contrast

### ～ものか vs 絶対(ぜったい)に～ない・二度(にど)と～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this is the modern equivalent for the **refusal** sense. Same
content, no shouting.

</div>

<pre>
あんな会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)く<b>ものか</b>。
Like hell I'd work for a company like that.
</pre>

<pre>
あの会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)く<b>つもりは絶対(ぜったい)にありません</b>。
I have absolutely no intention of working for that company.
</pre>

The second is the sentence you can say to a careers adviser. It is just as firm -
<code>絶対(ぜったい)に</code> is doing the work the rhetorical question was doing -
but it states a decision rather than performing an outburst. In casual speech among
friends, <code>二度(にど)と行(い)かない</code> covers the "never again" flavour.

### ～ものか vs ～わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this is the modern equivalent for the **disbelief** sense:
"there is no way that is so."

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)がそんなことを言(い)う<b>ものか</b>。
As if he would say a thing like that.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)がそんなことを言(い)う<b>わけがありません</b>。
There's no way he would say a thing like that.
</pre>

<code>わけがない</code> is fully usable in any register, spoken or written, and it is
what you want in a meeting, in an argument you intend to win on the facts, and in
anything you publish. It denies the proposition; `ものか` denies it *and* looks down
on whoever raised it, which is why the two are not interchangeable even though the
English translation often is.

### ～ものか vs ～ないものか・どうしたものか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same string with `ない` in front, or with a question word,
is not a refusal at all. It is a wish, or a deliberation.

</div>

<pre>
何(なん)とかならない<b>ものか</b>。
Isn't there something that can be done about this?
</pre>

<pre>
この件(けん)、どうした<b>ものか</b>。
What on earth should we do about this business?
</pre>

<code>～ないものか</code> is a sigh of longing - the speaker wants a way out and
cannot see one - and it is genuinely usable in adult speech and writing, including
in a workplace. <code>どうしたものか</code> is the sound of someone thinking a problem
over, and it too is unmarked. Neither is hostile. The difference is entirely in what
precedes `ものか`, so read that part before you decide what tone you are looking at.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あれが本物(ほんもの)<b>だものか</b>。 → ✅ あれが本物(ほんもの)<b>なものか</b>。
  <code>もの</code> is a noun, so a noun predicate links with `な`, never with `だ`.

- ❌ もう行(い)か<b>ないものか</b>。（intending "I'm never going again"） → ✅ もう行(い)く<b>ものか</b>。
  The pattern is negative already. Adding `ない` turns an angry refusal into a
  wistful "isn't there some way not to go?"

- ❌ 「飲(の)みに行(い)きませんか。」「行(い)く<b>もんですか</b>。」 → ✅ 「すみません、今日(きょう)は遠慮(えんりょ)しておきます。」
  The `です` does not make it polite. Answering an invitation this way tells your
  colleague you find the suggestion contemptible.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は行(い)った<b>ものか</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は行(い)かなかった。
  The rhetorical refusal takes the plain non-past. A past-tense verb in front of
  `ものか` belongs to the deliberating pattern instead - as in
  <code>どう答(こた)えたものか</code>, "how should one answer this?" - so it will
  not be read as a denial.

## Practice

**1.** What does the speaker mean by
「あいつに謝(あやま)る<b>もんか</b>。」? Choose:
(a) I suppose I ought to apologise. (b) I will never apologise to him.
(c) Should I apologise to him?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b)** I will never apologise to him.

The positive verb plus `もんか` is a rhetorical question with the force of a flat
refusal, and the speaker is angry. (c) would need
<code>謝(あやま)ったものか</code> or <code>謝(あやま)るべきだろうか</code> - the
deliberating use, which takes a different shape.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in neutral modern Japanese, keeping the meaning:
そんな噂(うわさ)、本当(ほんとう)な<b>ものか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そんな噂(うわさ)が本当(ほんとう)である<b>はずがありません</b>。（or 本当(ほんとう)である<b>わけがありません</b>）

This is the disbelief sense, so the modern equivalents are
<code>はずがない</code> and <code>わけがない</code>. Both can be said out loud in any
company; the original cannot.

</details>

**3.** Where would you expect to meet this line, and who is speaking?
ここで倒(たお)れる<b>ものか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In fiction or narration - a character's interior monologue at a low point in a
novel, a manga or a sports drama, or a voice-over. It is addressed to the speaker's
own resolve rather than to another person. A real athlete interviewed after a match
would say something like
<code>絶対(ぜったい)にあきらめたくなかったです</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)の話(はなし)が嘘(うそ)だものか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)の話(はなし)が嘘(うそ)<b>なものか</b>。

A noun attaches to <code>もの</code> with `な`. Note that the corrected sentence
means "her story is certainly **not** a lie" - the positive <code>嘘(うそ)な</code>
is denied by `ものか`, which is the opposite of what an English reader expects at
first glance.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance, and say which one you could use at work:
(a) 何(なん)とかなる<b>ものか</b>。
(b) 何(なん)とかならない<b>ものか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the rhetorical refusal: "as if this is going to sort itself out." It is
bitter, and it is aimed at whoever suggested that things would be fine. Keep it for
reading.

(b) is the wish: "isn't there something we can do?" The speaker wants a solution and
is appealing, not attacking. This one is perfectly usable in a workplace - said to
yourself or to a colleague over a problem - and in writing it appears in editorials
and opinion pieces.

The single `ない` reverses the entire social meaning of the sentence, which is the
main reason this pattern is worth being able to read accurately.

</details>
