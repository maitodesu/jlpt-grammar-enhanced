# N2 - How certain are you


---

# ～に決まっている

**Meaning** · of course it is; it's bound to be - the speaker refuses to entertain any other answer

<code>に決(き)まっている</code> sits at the loud end of the confidence ladder. `かもしれない`
leaves the door open, `はずだ` produces grounds, <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> reads the
evidence and lands on one answer. <code>に決(き)まっている</code> does none of that: it
declares the question closed. The certainty is the speaker's **attitude**, not a
measurement, which is why it is at home in an argument and out of place in a report.

## Formation

Attaches to a **plain form**. Nouns and な-adjectives connect bare - no `だ`, and no `な`
either.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + に決(き)まっている | 勝(か)つ → 勝(か)つに決(き)まっている |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + に決(き)まっている | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないに決(き)まっている |
| Verb (past) | plain past + に決(き)まっている | 忘(わす)れた → 忘(わす)れたに決(き)まっている |
| い-adjective | plain form + に決(き)まっている | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いに決(き)まっている |
| な-adjective | drop だ + に決(き)まっている | 無理(むり)だ → 無理(むり)に決(き)まっている |
| Noun | Noun + に決(き)まっている | うそ → うそに決(き)まっている |
| Polite | … + に決(き)まっています | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いに決(き)まっています |
| Casual speech | … + に決(き)まってる | 疲(つか)れる → 疲(つか)れるに決(き)まってる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the form is frozen in the ている shape. There is no
<code>に決(き)まる</code> and no <code>に決(き)まった</code> in this meaning; those
belong to the ordinary verb 決(き)まる, which is a different thing entirely - see the
Contrast section.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そんな話(はなし)、うそ<b>に決(き)まっている</b>。
A story like that is a lie, obviously.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)から急(いそ)いでも間(ま)に合(あ)わない<b>に決(き)まっている</b>よ。
Even if we rush now, there's no way we'll make it.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな時間(じかん)に電話(でんわ)したら、迷惑(めいわく)<b>に決(き)まっています</b>。
Ringing someone at this hour is bound to be a nuisance.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもがあんなに静(しず)かなときは、いたずらをしている<b>に決(き)まってる</b>。
When the children are that quiet, they're up to something - guaranteed.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の前(まえ)の晩(ばん)に徹夜(てつや)すれば、眠(ねむ)くなる<b>に決(き)まっています</b>。
Stay up all night before the exam and of course you'll be half asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
「部長(ぶちょう)、怒(おこ)るかな。」「怒(おこ)る<b>に決(き)まってる</b>だろ。」
"Reckon the boss will be cross?" "Course he will."
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)も確(たし)かめずに申(もう)し込(こ)んだら、あとで後悔(こうかい)する<b>に決(き)まっている</b>。
Sign up without even checking the price and you'll regret it later, no question.
</pre>

<pre>
あれだけ練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)んでいたのだから、本人(ほんにん)も不安(ふあん)だった<b>に決(き)まっています</b>。
After skipping that much practice, he must have been anxious himself - it could hardly be otherwise.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Blunt, emotional and thoroughly spoken. The contraction
<code>に決(き)まってる</code> is the everyday form, and
<code>に決(き)まってるじゃない</code> or <code>に決(き)まってるでしょ</code> is how it
usually reaches the listener: "obviously - why are you even asking?" That is also the
danger in it. Because the pattern presents your conviction as though it were common
sense, using it on someone who has just offered a different opinion tells them their
opinion was silly. Among friends that is ordinary banter; aimed upward at a manager or a
client it lands close to rude.

<code>に決(き)まっています</code> is grammatically polite and still forceful - です
softens the packaging, not the content. When you need to disagree politely, the gentler
rungs of the ladder are the tools: <code>違(ちが)うと思(おも)います</code>,
<code>そうとは限(かぎ)らないのではないでしょうか</code>.

Two things it will not do. It cannot report somebody else's certainty - the conviction
has to be the speaker's own - and it does not form a genuine question, because you cannot
ask another person to confirm a feeling that only you have.

It is very often turned on oneself, half-jokingly, to head off sympathy in advance:
<code>私(わたし)のことだから、三日(みっか)でやめる<b>に決(き)まってる</b></code>.
That self-directed use is the friendliest home the pattern has.

## Contrast

### に決(き)まっている vs に違(ちが)いない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> is a **conclusion drawn from
evidence**. <code>に決(き)まっている</code> is a **refusal to consider alternatives**.

</div>

<pre>
靴(くつ)が濡(ぬ)れているから、外(そと)は雨(あめ)<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
His shoes are wet, so it must be raining outside.
</pre>

<pre>
あの空(そら)の色(いろ)を見(み)てよ。降(ふ)る<b>に決(き)まってる</b>。
Look at the colour of that sky. It's going to rain, obviously.
</pre>

The first shows its working, and a listener could inspect the shoes and agree. The second
does not argue - it asserts, and dares you to disagree. This is why a report, a paper or
an exam answer takes <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> or
<code>と考(かんが)えられる</code>, however certain the writer happens to feel.

### に決(き)まっている vs はずだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずだ` can be **let down**; <code>に決(き)まっている</code>
cannot.

</div>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)はかばんに入(い)れた<b>はずです</b>。
I'm fairly sure I put the key in my bag.
</pre>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)はかばんの中(なか)<b>に決(き)まっています</b>。
The key is in the bag - where else would it be?
</pre>

`はずだ` rests on grounds you could produce if asked, and grounds can turn out to be
wrong, which is why <code>はずだったのに</code> is such a common thing to say. There is no
comfortable equivalent for <code>に決(き)まっている</code>: having declared the matter
closed, you have left yourself nothing to be surprised by except your own certainty.

### に決(き)まっている vs the ordinary verb 決(き)まる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if `に` marks **when, where or what was settled**, it is the plain
verb 決(き)まる. If `に` is glued to the pattern and the sentence could take きっと, it
is the conjecture.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の会議(かいぎ)は水曜日(すいようび)<b>に決(き)まっています</b>。
Next month's meeting is fixed for Wednesday.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)の会議(かいぎ)も長(なが)くなる<b>に決(き)まっています</b>。
Next month's meeting is going to run long as well, needless to say.
</pre>

Identical endings, unrelated meanings. In the first, 水曜日(すいようび) is a noun that
somebody chose and `に` marks the choice; the sentence reports an administrative fact. In
the second the pattern hangs off a whole predicate. Notice that the conjecture reading is
blocked after a bare time or place noun, which is exactly why
<code>明日(あした)に決(き)まっている</code> can only mean "it is scheduled for
tomorrow".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は大切(たいせつ)<b>だに決(き)まっている</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は大切(たいせつ)<b>に決(き)まっている</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns attach bare. Both the `だ` and the `な` are dropped.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)が来(く)る<b>に決(き)まっている</b>と天気予報(てんきよほう)で言(い)っていました。 → ✅ 台風(たいふう)が来(く)る<b>でしょう</b>と天気予報(てんきよほう)で言(い)っていました。
  The pattern carries the speaker's own conviction, so it cannot be put into somebody
  else's mouth - least of all a forecaster's.

- ❌ その話(はなし)は本当(ほんとう)<b>に決(き)まっていますか</b>。 → ✅ その話(はなし)は本当(ほんとう)<b>でしょうか</b>。
  You cannot ask a listener to confirm your own certainty. The only question shape the
  pattern has is the rhetorical
  <code>～に決(き)まってるじゃないですか</code>.

- ❌ 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)、この数字(すうじ)は誤(あやま)り<b>に決(き)まっている</b>。 → ✅ 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)、この数字(すうじ)は誤(あやま)り<b>だと考(かんが)えられる</b>。
  In a written report the reader wants the evidence, not the writer's temperature. Swap
  to <code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> or <code>に違(ちが)いない</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 朝(あさ)からずっと立(た)ちっぱなしだったのだから、足(あし)が痛(いた)い____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

足(あし)が痛(いた)い<b>に決(き)まっている</b>（or に決(き)まっています）。

The speaker treats sore feet as the only thinkable outcome of standing all morning, so
the closed-question pattern fits. An い-adjective attaches with no change at all.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新幹線(しんかんせん)は3時(じ)に着(つ)く（はずです・に決(き)まっています）とアプリに出(で)ていました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

3時(じ)に着(つ)く<b>はずです</b>。

The grounds here are an app - somebody else's information, and a timetable that could
still be wrong. That is `はずだ` territory. The other pattern would assert the speaker's
personal conviction, which sits oddly beside a quoted source.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "A theme park on a Sunday? It's going to be packed, obviously."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

日曜日(にちようび)の遊園地(ゆうえんち)なんて、混(こ)んでいる<b>に決(き)まっています</b>。

なんて sets up the dismissive tone and the pattern completes it. Note the ている shape of
混(こ)む: a crowd is a present state, not a coming event.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)はそんなことを言(い)わないに決(き)まったよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)はそんなことを言(い)わない<b>に決(き)まってる</b>よ。

The pattern only exists in the ている form. <code>に決(き)まった</code> would be the
plain verb reporting that something got settled, which makes no sense with a negative
clause in front of it. Casual speech drops the い, giving
<code>に決(き)まってる</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)はもう知(し)っている<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)はもう知(し)っている<b>に決(き)まっている</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is detective work. Something told you he knows - his face, a message he sent, the
speed of his reply - and you are stating the conclusion. A listener could reasonably ask
どうして？ and you would have an answer ready.

(b) is exasperation. It says the question was not worth asking: of course he knows,
everybody does. There is usually a second speaker being corrected, and (b) tells them
their doubt was naive. Fine between friends, sharp anywhere else.

</details>

# ～に相違ない

**Meaning** · there is no doubt that ～ - read *そういない*; the same near-certainty as に違(ちが)いない, dressed for a document

<code>相違(そうい)</code> is a stiff Sino-Japanese noun meaning *a discrepancy*, so
<code>に相違(そうい)ない</code> literally says "there is no discrepancy here": what you
have worked out and what is actually the case do not differ. In meaning it is the twin of
<code>に違(ちが)いない</code> and sits on the same high rung of the confidence ladder.
Everything that separates them is register.

## Formation

Identical to <code>に違(ちが)いない</code>: a **plain form** in front, with the `だ` of a
noun or な-adjective trimmed away.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + に相違(そうい)ない | 逃(に)げた → 逃(に)げたに相違(そうい)ない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + に相違(そうい)ない | 気(き)づかない → 気(き)づかないに相違(そうい)ない |
| い-adjective | plain form + に相違(そうい)ない | 苦(くる)しい → 苦(くる)しいに相違(そうい)ない |
| な-adjective | drop だ + に相違(そうい)ない | 重大(じゅうだい)だ → 重大(じゅうだい)に相違(そうい)ない |
| Noun | Noun + に相違(そうい)ない | 本物(ほんもの) → 本物(ほんもの)に相違(そうい)ない |
| Polite / document style | … + に相違(そうい)ありません | 事実(じじつ) → 事実(じじつ)に相違(そうい)ありません |
| Certifying a document | Noun + に相違(そうい)ありません | 上記(じょうき)のとおり → 上記(じょうき)のとおり相違(そうい)ありません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the reading is そうい, not そうちがい. 相(そう) and 違(い) here are both
on-readings; the word is the same one that appears in
相違点(そういてん), "points of difference".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この字(じ)は本人(ほんにん)の筆跡(ひっせき)<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。
This handwriting is unquestionably his own.
</pre>

<pre>
以上(いじょう)の内容(ないよう)に<b>相違(そうい)ありません</b>。
The above is correct as stated.
</pre>

<pre>
犯人(はんにん)はこの窓(まど)から入(はい)った<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。
The intruder came in through this window - of that there can be no doubt.
</pre>

<pre>
報告(ほうこく)された数字(すうじ)が事実(じじつ)<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>ことを確認(かくにん)いたしました。
We have confirmed that the reported figures are accurate.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)の記録(きろく)から見(み)て、この寺(てら)は十五世紀(じゅうごせいき)に建(た)てられた<b>に相違(そうい)ありません</b>。
Judging from the records of the period, this temple was without doubt built in the fifteenth century.
</pre>

<pre>
残(のこ)された手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)めば、彼(かれ)が最後(さいご)まで故郷(こきょう)を思(おも)っていた<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>とわかる。
Read the letters he left and it is clear he thought of his home village to the very end.
</pre>

<pre>
筆(ふで)づかいから判断(はんだん)すると、これも同(おな)じ画家(がか)の作品(さくひん)<b>に相違(そうい)ありません</b>。
To judge by the brushwork, this too is beyond question by the same painter.
</pre>

<pre>
その知(し)らせを受(う)け取(と)ったとき、母(はは)の胸(むね)にはさまざまな思(おも)いが去来(きょらい)した<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。
When she received the news, all manner of feelings must surely have passed through my mother's mind.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **written Japanese**, and formal written Japanese at that. It belongs to legal
and administrative documents, police statements, appraisals and certifications, academic
prose of an older cast, and the narration of novels and essays. Said out loud in an
ordinary conversation it sounds as though you are reading from a form - the effect is
closer to English *"of that there can be no doubt"* than to *"it must be"*.

**What a learner should actually say is <code>に違(ちが)いない</code>** - or, in relaxed
speech, きっと … <code>だろう</code>. Keep
<code>に相違(そうい)ない</code> for recognition, and for the one place you may really
need to write it: the certifying line at the foot of a form.

That certifying use is worth separating out, because it is not a guess at all.
<code>上記(じょうき)のとおり相違(そうい)ありません</code> under your signature means
"the above does not differ from the facts" - you are attesting, not speculating. Japanese
application forms, insurance claims and witness statements end this way constantly, and
it is the form most learners will meet first.

In literary narration the pattern has a third flavour, close to English *"must surely
have"*: the narrator reaches into a character's head and declares what they felt. That
use is warm rather than bureaucratic, and it is why the pattern turns up in the middle of
otherwise gentle prose.

## Contrast

### に相違(そうい)ない vs に違(ちが)いない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different clothes.
<code>に違(ちが)いない</code> is the everyday form and works in speech and writing
alike; <code>に相違(そうい)ない</code> is written-only and formal.

</div>

<pre>
あの様子(ようす)だと、試験(しけん)に落(お)ちた<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
From the look on his face, he must have failed the exam.
</pre>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)された書類(しょるい)は偽造(ぎぞう)された物(もの)<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。
The documents submitted are without question forgeries.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences become odd - the first stilted, the second oddly chatty for
a fraud finding. Because they are semantically identical, the only question you ever have
to ask is: *would this sentence be spoken or filed?*

### に相違(そうい)ない vs 相違(そうい)ありません as a certification

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `に` after a **clause**, it is a conjecture. Standing after
<code>上記(じょうき)のとおり</code> or a set of stated details, it is a **declaration of
accuracy** and involves no guessing whatever.

</div>

<pre>
申請(しんせい)内容(ないよう)は事実(じじつ)<b>に相違(そうい)ありません</b>。
The details given in this application are true and correct.
</pre>

<pre>
この印鑑(いんかん)は届(とど)け出(で)されたもの<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。
This seal is unquestionably the one that was registered.
</pre>

The first is what you sign; you are personally vouching for the facts. The second still
carries the "I have worked it out and I am certain" flavour of a conjecture. The dividing
line is whether the speaker is in a position to *know* - and note that the certifying use
almost always appears in the polite negative
<code>相違(そうい)ありません</code>, never contracted or casualised.

### に相違(そうい)ない vs と考(かんが)えられる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are written, but they aim at different readers.
<code>に相違(そうい)ない</code> announces the writer's **conviction**;
<code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> presents a **reasoned conclusion** and leaves room for
another.

</div>

<pre>
この土器(どき)は同(おな)じ時代(じだい)のもの<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。
This pottery is beyond doubt from the same period.
</pre>

<pre>
この土器(どき)は同(おな)じ時代(じだい)のものだ<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
This pottery can be taken to date from the same period.
</pre>

Modern academic Japanese overwhelmingly prefers the second. A journal reviewer reading the
first would hear a writer who has stopped arguing, which is why
<code>に相違(そうい)ない</code> now feels slightly old-fashioned outside law and
literature.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ その話(はなし)は事実(じじつ)<b>なに相違(そうい)ない</b>。 → ✅ その話(はなし)は事実(じじつ)<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。
  A noun attaches bare. Neither `だ` nor `な` survives in front of
  <code>に相違(そうい)ない</code>.

- ❌ ねえ、明日(あした)雨(あめ)<b>に相違(そうい)ないよ</b>。 → ✅ ねえ、明日(あした)雨(あめ)<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>よ。
  Nothing is ungrammatical here; it is simply the wrong outfit. Chatting to a friend in
  document Japanese is a joke, and only funny once.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)する<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)する予定(よてい)です。
  Like every member of this family, it is a guess about something you cannot check
  directly. Your own diary is not a guess.

- ❌ 読(よ)み方(かた)は「そうちがいない」 → ✅ 「そういない」
  相違(そうい) is a two-character on-reading compound. Mixing the kun-reading ちがい
  into it produces a word that does not exist.

## Practice

**1.** 保険(ほけん)の申込書(もうしこみしょ): 記入(きにゅう)した内容(ないよう)は事実(じじつ)に____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

事実(じじつ)に<b>相違(そうい)ありません</b>。

This is the certifying line, not a conjecture: you are attesting that what you wrote
matches the facts. Forms almost always use the polite negative
<code>相違(そうい)ありません</code> here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 飲(の)み会(かい)で - 「山田(やまだ)さん、また遅(おく)れてくる（に相違(そうい)ない・に違(ちが)いない）ね。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

また遅(おく)れてくる<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>ね。

Identical meaning, but a drinks party is not a deposition.
<code>に相違(そうい)ない</code> in casual speech makes the speaker sound like a court
clerk, which is exactly the effect to avoid unless you want the laugh.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a written appraisal: "This piece is beyond doubt the work of a craftsman of the same school."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この品(しな)は同(おな)じ流派(りゅうは)の職人(しょくにん)の手(て)によるもの<b>に相違(そうい)ありません</b>。

An appraisal is written, formal and asserts certainty, which is exactly the pattern's
home ground. The noun もの attaches bare, and the polite negative keeps the document
register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 落(お)とし主(ぬし)はこの近(ちか)くに住(す)んでいる人(ひと)だに相違(そうい)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 落(お)とし主(ぬし)はこの近(ちか)くに住(す)んでいる人(ひと)<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。

The `だ` has to go. A noun - here 人(ひと), with a relative clause in front of it -
connects directly, exactly as it does with
<code>に違(ちが)いない</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この資料(しりょう)は本物(ほんもの)<b>に相違(そうい)ない</b>。
(b) この資料(しりょう)は本物(ほんもの)だ<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) closes the matter. The writer has examined the document and is prepared to stake
their judgement on it; the sentence would be at home in an expert's report or a legal
finding, and it invites no further discussion.

(b) opens the matter to the reader. It says the evidence points this way and a reasonable
person would conclude the same, while leaving the conclusion revisable. That is why
academic writing lives on (b) and reserves (a) for the rare moment it wants to sound
final.

</details>

# ～と考えられる

**Meaning** · it can be concluded that ～; it is reasonable to think that ～ - a conclusion stated with the person who reached it removed from the sentence

<code>考(かんが)えられる</code> is 考(かんが)える with the -られる ending, and here it
carries the *impersonal* sense: not "I think", but "one can think". The pattern is how
Japanese states a judgement in a report or a paper. It sits a rung below
<code>に違(ちが)いない</code> - the writer is not claiming certainty, only that the
evidence supports this reading - and it earns its authority precisely by not sounding
like a personal opinion.

## Formation

The clause in front is a **quotation**, so it takes the plain form and the `と` of quoted
speech. That means nouns and な-adjectives keep their `だ` - the opposite of what
<code>に違(ちが)いない</code> does.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + と考(かんが)えられる | 増(ふ)える → 増(ふ)えると考(かんが)えられる |
| Verb (past) | plain past + と考(かんが)えられる | 起(お)きた → 起(お)きたと考(かんが)えられる |
| い-adjective | plain form + と考(かんが)えられる | 少(すく)ない → 少(すく)ないと考(かんが)えられる |
| な-adjective | keep だ + と考(かんが)えられる | 安全(あんぜん)だ → 安全(あんぜん)だと考(かんが)えられる |
| Noun | keep だ + と考(かんが)えられる | 原因(げんいん) → 原因(げんいん)だと考(かんが)えられる |
| Polite | … + と考(かんが)えられます | 難(むずか)しい → 難(むずか)しいと考(かんが)えられます |
| Established view | … + と考(かんが)えられている | 有効(ゆうこう)だ → 有効(ゆうこう)だと考(かんが)えられている |
| Naming the conclusion | … + と考(かんが)えられる + Noun | 要因(よういん) → 要因(よういん)だと考(かんが)えられる点(てん) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same form also does duty as a straight potential: "such a thing is
conceivable". <code>その可能性(かのうせい)も考(かんが)えられる</code> means "that
possibility can also be entertained". Context separates the two easily, because the
conclusion use always has a quoted clause and `と` in front of it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この結果(けっか)は偶然(ぐうぜん)だ<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
This result can be put down to chance.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)は整備(せいび)不足(ぶそく)だ<b>と考(かんが)えられます</b>。
The cause of the accident is believed to be poor maintenance.
</pre>

<pre>
気温(きおん)の上昇(じょうしょう)が今年(ことし)の収穫(しゅうかく)に影響(えいきょう)した<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
The rise in temperature is thought to have affected this year's harvest.
</pre>

<pre>
この数字(すうじ)だけで結論(けつろん)を出(だ)すのは難(むずか)しい<b>と考(かんが)えられます</b>。
It would seem unwise to draw a conclusion from this figure alone.
</pre>

<pre>
実験(じっけん)の条件(じょうけん)が十分(じゅうぶん)に管理(かんり)されていなかった<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
The experimental conditions appear not to have been adequately controlled.
</pre>

<pre>
若者(わかもの)の車(くるま)離(ばな)れの背景(はいけい)には都市部(としぶ)の交通(こうつう)事情(じじょう)がある<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
Behind young people's turn away from cars, one can see the transport situation in the cities.
</pre>

<pre>
以上(いじょう)のことから、この地域(ちいき)の人口(じんこう)減少(げんしょう)はしばらく続(つづ)く<b>と考(かんが)えられます</b>。
From all of the above, the decline in population in this region looks set to continue for some time.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の不具合(ふぐあい)は、複数(ふくすう)の要因(よういん)が重(かさ)なって生(しょう)じたもの<b>だと考(かんが)えられている</b>。
The current fault is held to have arisen from several factors coinciding.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Squarely **written and formal**: research papers, company reports, news analysis, expert
commentary, the discussion section of a lab write-up. The polite
<code>と考(かんが)えられます</code> is also standard in presentations and in careful
business speech, where it makes a claim sound examined rather than felt.

The pattern's whole trick is *depersonalisation*. Japanese academic prose avoids
<code>私(わたし)は … と思(おも)う</code> for the same reason English academic prose
avoids "I reckon": the reader is being asked to accept the reasoning, not the writer.
Because the thinker has been deleted, though, you cannot put yourself back in.
<code>私(わたし)はそう考(かんが)えられます</code> is not a polite version of "I
think"; it reads as "I am capable of thinking that", which is not what anybody means.

**In conversation, say <code>と思(おも)います</code>.** A friend asking whether the
restaurant is any good does not want a conclusion from the literature. Keep
<code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> for the moments when you genuinely are summarising
evidence - a meeting, a written explanation, an exam essay.

One small caution about hedging. The pattern is already cautious, so piling more on top
of it - <code>と考(かんが)えられると思(おも)います</code> - reads as nervous rather
than careful. One hedge is enough.

## Contrast

### と考(かんが)えられる vs と思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と思(おも)う</code> names an opinion and its owner.
<code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> presents a conclusion and hides its owner.

</div>

<pre>
この案(あん)はうまくいく<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I think this plan will work.
</pre>

<pre>
この案(あん)は一定(いってい)の効果(こうか)をもたらす<b>と考(かんが)えられます</b>。
This plan can be expected to produce a measurable effect.
</pre>

The first invites you to weigh the speaker; the second invites you to weigh the evidence.
That is also why the second is harder to argue with politely - and why overusing it in
conversation makes a person sound as though they are issuing findings rather than
chatting.

### と考(かんが)えられる vs と考(かんが)えられている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - without ている, **the writer** is drawing the conclusion here and now.
With ている, it is **the accepted view** in the field, and the writer is reporting it.

</div>

<pre>
この習慣(しゅうかん)は江戸(えど)時代(じだい)に始(はじ)まった<b>と考(かんが)えられている</b>。
This custom is generally held to have begun in the Edo period.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の調査(ちょうさ)から、その説(せつ)には無理(むり)がある<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
The present survey suggests that this theory is untenable.
</pre>

The ている form is close to <code>とされている</code> and
<code>と言(い)われている</code>: all three hand the opinion to the scholarly community.
Use it for background, and drop the ている at the moment you start making your own case -
that switch is one of the clearest signals in a Japanese paper that the argument has
begun.

### と考(かんが)えられる vs と言(い)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と言(い)える</code> for what the data **shows**;
<code>と考(かんが)えられる</code> for the interpretation you **build on top of** the
data.

</div>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)を見(み)る限(かぎ)り、効果(こうか)は明(あき)らかだ<b>と言(い)える</b>。
As far as the figures go, the effect can be said to be clear.
</pre>

<pre>
その効果(こうか)は価格(かかく)の変化(へんか)によるもの<b>だと考(かんが)えられる</b>。
That effect is likely attributable to the change in price.
</pre>

The first is a claim you could defend by pointing at a table. The second goes beyond the
table to say *why*, and is therefore the weaker, more revisable statement of the two -
even though the grammar looks equally confident.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は重要(じゅうよう)<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は重要(じゅうよう)<b>だと考(かんが)えられる</b>。
  `と` is the quoting particle, so what precedes it must be a complete plain-form
  sentence. な-adjectives and nouns keep their `だ` here - the exact opposite of
  <code>に違(ちが)いない</code>.

- ❌ 私(わたし)はこの方法(ほうほう)がいい<b>と考(かんが)えられます</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)はこの方法(ほうほう)がいい<b>と考(かんが)えています</b>。
  Putting 私(わたし)は back into a sentence whose whole purpose is to remove the thinker
  produces a potential reading: "I am able to think so." For a stated personal position,
  use <code>考(かんが)えています</code>.

- ❌ この店(みせ)のラーメンはおいしい<b>と考(かんが)えられるよ</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)のラーメンはおいしい<b>と思(おも)うよ</b>。
  Grammatical, and comically over-dressed. Taste is an opinion, and a friend wants your
  opinion, not your findings.

- ❌ 原因(げんいん)は人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)だ<b>と考(かんが)えられると思(おも)います</b>。 → ✅ 原因(げんいん)は人手(ひとで)不足(ぶそく)だ<b>と考(かんが)えられます</b>。
  Two hedges do not make a claim twice as careful. Pick one.

## Practice

**1.** 停電(ていでん)の原因(げんいん)は落雷(らくらい)____考(かんが)えられます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

落雷(らくらい)<b>だと</b>考(かんが)えられます。

落雷(らくらい) is a noun, and `と` needs a finished sentence in front of it, so the `だ`
has to be there. Dropping it is the single most common slip with this pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来週(らいしゅう)映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)かない？　あの監督(かんとく)の新作(しんさく)、おもしろい（と思(おも)うよ・と考(かんが)えられるよ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

おもしろい<b>と思(おも)うよ</b>。

An invitation to the cinema is exactly where a personal opinion belongs. The other
pattern strips the speaker out of the sentence, which in casual conversation sounds like
a press release.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a report: "The decline in sales is thought to have been caused by the summer's unusual weather."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

売上(うりあげ)の減少(げんしょう)は夏(なつ)の異常(いじょう)気象(きしょう)によるもの<b>だと考(かんが)えられます</b>。

<code>によるもの</code> gives you a noun to hang the `だ` on, which is the ordinary way to
say "caused by" in this register. English "is thought to" almost always maps to this
pattern in reports.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)から、この方法(ほうほう)は安全(あんぜん)と考(かんが)えられていると思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)から、この方法(ほうほう)は安全(あんぜん)<b>だと考(かんが)えられます</b>。

Two repairs. 安全(あんぜん) is a な-adjective and needs `だ` before `と`; and the
sentence stacks three hedges at once. Since the conclusion comes from
<code>調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)</code> - the writer's own survey - the plain
<code>と考(かんが)えられます</code> is right, without ている and without
<code>と思(おも)います</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この技術(ぎじゅつ)は来年(らいねん)実用化(じつようか)される<b>と考(かんが)えられる</b>。
(b) この技術(ぎじゅつ)は来年(らいねん)実用化(じつようか)される<b>と考(かんが)えられている</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the writer's own reading of the evidence just presented. If a reader disagrees,
they are disagreeing with this author, and the author is expected to have grounds in the
preceding paragraph.

(b) reports what the field currently assumes. The writer takes no responsibility for it -
indeed (b) is very often the setup for a "however", with the next sentence arguing that
the accepted view is mistaken.

</details>

# どうやら

**Meaning** · it looks as though; apparently; by all appearances - flags at the front of the sentence that what follows is the speaker's reading of the signs, not a fact

`どうやら` is an early warning. Dropped in near the head of the sentence, it tells the
listener that the speaker has been piecing evidence together and has arrived, tentatively,
somewhere. The conclusion itself is delivered by the ending - `らしい`, `ようだ`, `みたいだ`
- and `どうやら` is what colours it: *the picture is becoming clear, and I do not much like
where it points.*

## Usage

`どうやら` is an adverb, so it conjugates for nothing and attaches to nothing. Two things
govern where it goes and what it needs.

**It wants a hedged ending.** Almost every natural sentence with `どうやら` finishes in
`ようだ`, `らしい`, `みたいだ`, `そうだ`, `のだろう` or `かもしれない`. The adverb sets up a
guess; something has to complete it. A flat assertion after `どうやら` leaves the listener
waiting - the exception is the second meaning below.

**It goes early.** Sentence-initial is the default, and it can also follow the topic:
<code>彼(かれ)はどうやら気(き)づいていないらしい</code>. Late in the sentence it
sounds displaced.

The second meaning is unrelated and worth knowing: **"somehow, just barely"**, followed by
a plain past. <code>どうやら間(ま)に合(あ)った</code> is "we scraped in". The
reduplicated <code>どうやらこうやら</code> is the emphatic version, and it is always about
managing something with difficulty. Nothing tentative about it - the outcome is known, and
the speaker is exhaling.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>どうやら</b>道(みち)を間違(まちが)えたようだ。
Looks as though we've taken a wrong turn.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>どうやら</b>彼(かれ)は何(なに)も聞(き)いていないらしい。
Apparently nobody has told him anything.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらないので、<b>どうやら</b>明日(あした)の遠足(えんそく)は無理(むり)みたいだ。
His temperature isn't coming down, so it's looking like tomorrow's outing is off.
</pre>

<pre>
長(なが)い話(はな)し合(あ)いのすえ、<b>どうやら</b>全員(ぜんいん)が納得(なっとく)してくれたようです。
After a long discussion, it seems everyone has come round.
</pre>

<pre>
大雨(おおあめ)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったが、<b>どうやら</b>会議(かいぎ)には間(ま)に合(あ)った。
The trains stopped in the downpour, but I just about made the meeting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. `どうやら` is at home in conversation, in news copy and in narration
alike, and it carries no marking for age or gender. What it does carry is a faint
reluctance: the speaker did not want this conclusion, but the evidence keeps arriving.
That is why so many `どうやら` sentences are about rain, delays, illness and things going
wrong.

In polite speech it is a gentle way to hand over bad news, because it puts the blame on
the evidence rather than on the speaker:
<code>どうやら今週中(こんしゅうじゅう)の納品(のうひん)は難(むずか)しいようです</code>
softens a missed deadline into something both parties are merely observing. That makes it
genuinely useful at work.

It also has a nice self-deprecating use, where the speaker discovers something about
themselves: <code>どうやら私(わたし)は向(む)いていないらしい</code> - "it appears I'm
not cut out for this."

## Contrast

### どうやら vs どうも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どうやら` reports a **conclusion taking shape**. `どうも` reports a
**feeling that will not go away**, and it is the one that pairs with negatives.

</div>

<pre>
<b>どうやら</b>財布(さいふ)を落(お)としたらしい。
It appears I've dropped my wallet.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>どうも</b>この説明(せつめい)が分(わ)からない。
Somehow I just can't make sense of this explanation.
</pre>

They overlap in the middle - <code>どうも変(へん)だ</code> and
<code>どうやら変(へん)らしい</code> are both natural - but the edges are clean. `どうも`
is the one that goes with a plain negative to mean "I can't quite …", and the one that
lives in fixed greetings (<code>どうもありがとう</code>, <code>どうもすみません</code>, and
`どうも` alone as an all-purpose hello). `どうやら` does none of that. Conversely `どうやら`
is the one that means "barely", which `どうも` cannot do.

### どうやら vs たぶん・おそらく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たぶん` estimates a **probability**. `どうやら` reads the **evidence
in front of it**.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)は<b>たぶん</b>雪(ゆき)になるでしょう。
It'll probably snow next week.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)が白(しろ)い。<b>どうやら</b>夜中(よなか)に降(ふ)ったらしい。
Everything outside is white. Looks as though it came down in the night.
</pre>

`たぶん` needs nothing to look at; you can say it about next year. `どうやら` needs
something observable - a symptom, a silence, a wet street - and so it usually talks about
now or the very recent past. `おそらく` is `たぶん` in a suit: same job, formal register,
common in writing and speeches.

### どうやら vs らしい・ようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they are not rivals. `らしい` and `ようだ` are the **ending**;
`どうやら` is the **flag at the start** that announces one is coming.

</div>

<pre>
店(みせ)が閉(し)まっている。<b>どうやら</b>定休日(ていきゅうび)のようだ。
The shop's shut. Seems it's their regular closing day.
</pre>

<pre>
店(みせ)が閉(し)まっている。定休日(ていきゅうび)のようだ。
The shop's shut. Must be their regular closing day.
</pre>

Both sentences are correct, and the second is complete without the adverb. Adding
`どうやら` does two things: it warns the listener a guess is coming, and it adds the sense
that the speaker has been working the problem for a moment rather than reacting instantly.
Dropping the ending instead is what does not work - the adverb alone cannot carry the
guess.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>どうやら</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る。 → ✅ <b>どうやら</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうだ。
  The adverb sets up a guess and then needs something to finish it. Left with a flat
  assertion, the sentence sounds cut off.

- ❌ <b>どうやら</b>ありがとうございました。 → ✅ <b>どうも</b>ありがとうございました。
  The fixed greetings belong to `どうも` and only to `どうも`. `どうやら` never appears in a
  set phrase of thanks or apology, and the substitution is a very common early slip.

- ❌ <b>どうやら</b>この味(あじ)が好(す)きになれない。 → ✅ <b>どうも</b>この味(あじ)が好(す)きになれない。
  "Somehow I just can't …" with a plain negative is `どうも` territory. `どうやら` needs a
  guess-marking ending, and there is none here.

- ❌ <b>どうやら</b>彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)に違(ちが)いない。 → ✅ <b>どうやら</b>彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)らしい。
  <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> claims near-certainty, and `どうやら` announces
  tentativeness. Putting them in one sentence has the speaker hedging and insisting at the
  same time.

## Practice

**1.** 窓(まど)の外(そと)が静(しず)かだ。____雨(あめ)はやんだらしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>どうやら</b>雨(あめ)はやんだらしい。

The silence outside is the evidence, and `らしい` at the end is the guess it produces.
`どうやら` is the flag that ties the two together - exactly the shape this adverb wants.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 何度(なんど)読(よ)んでも、（どうやら・どうも）この文(ぶん)の意味(いみ)が分(わ)からない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（どうも）この文(ぶん)の意味(いみ)が分(わ)からない。

With a plain negative meaning "I somehow can't", only `どうも` works. `どうやら` would need
a hedged ending such as `らしい` or `ようだ`, and here the speaker is not guessing at all -
they are describing their own stubborn incomprehension.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) <b>どうやら</b>試験(しけん)に受(う)かったようだ。
(b) <b>どうやら</b>試験(しけん)に受(う)かった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the guessing `どうやら`: the result is not official yet, but the signs - a friend's
message, a number on a list - point that way, and `ようだ` keeps it tentative.

(b) is the other `どうやら` entirely, the one that means "just barely". The result is known
and confirmed; what the speaker is conveying is relief that it was a near thing. The plain
past ending is what forces this second reading.

</details>

# ～かねない

**Meaning** · could well ～; is liable to ～; I wouldn't put it past ～ - always about an outcome the speaker does not want

`かねない` is the negative of `かねる`, the verb-ending that means *cannot bring oneself
to*. Read it literally and it says "it is not the case that this could not happen" - which
is a roundabout way of saying **don't be surprised if it does**. That double negative is
the whole flavour of the pattern: it is not a neutral estimate of odds but a warning, and
it only ever points at bad news.

## Formation

A **verb** pattern, attaching to the ます-stem. Adjectives and nouns cannot take it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + かねない | なる → なりかねない |
| する-verb | ～し + かねない | 失敗(しっぱい)する → 失敗(しっぱい)しかねない |
| Compound outcome | ます-stem + かねない | 招(まね)く → 招(まね)きかねない |
| Polite | ます-stem + かねません | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れかねません |
| Modifying a noun | ます-stem + かねない + Noun | 起(お)こる → 起(お)こりかねない事態(じたい) |
| Positive counterpart (opposite meaning) | ます-stem + かねる | 決(き)める → 決(き)めかねる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no negative version. To say something is *unlikely*, you want
`そうにない` or <code>ないだろう</code>; `かねない` has the negation built into it already,
and <code>かねなくない</code> is not Japanese.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
寝(ね)不足(ぶそく)が続(つづ)くと、体(からだ)を壊(こわ)し<b>かねない</b>。
Keep sleeping this badly and you could easily make yourself ill.
</pre>

<pre>
そのままにしておくと、大(おお)きな事故(じこ)になり<b>かねません</b>。
Leave it as it is and it could well turn into a serious accident.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)のつもりでも、相手(あいて)を傷(きず)つけ<b>かねない</b>。
Even if you mean it as a joke, you could easily hurt the other person.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)なら、平気(へいき)でうそをつき<b>かねない</b>。
I wouldn't put it past him to lie without blinking.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げが続(つづ)けば、客(きゃく)が離(はな)れていき<b>かねません</b>。
If the price rises keep coming, we could well lose our customers.
</pre>

<pre>
確認(かくにん)せずに送信(そうしん)すると、個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)が外部(がいぶ)に漏(も)れ<b>かねない</b>。
Send it without checking and personal data could end up leaking outside.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)の一部(いちぶ)だけを切(き)り取(と)って伝(つた)えると、誤解(ごかい)を招(まね)き<b>かねません</b>。
Passing on only part of what was said is liable to cause a misunderstanding.
</pre>

<pre>
安易(あんい)な発言(はつげん)は、会社(かいしゃ)全体(ぜんたい)の信用(しんよう)を失(うしな)うことになり<b>かねない</b>。
A careless remark could well cost the company its reputation as a whole.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`かねない` is a shade formal but nowhere near stiff. It is everywhere in news writing,
editorials, safety notices and business talk, and it is also perfectly ordinary in speech
when one person is warning another. The polite `かねません` is the standard form for
raising a risk in a meeting without accusing anybody.

The speaker's attitude is built in. Saying <code>なりかねない</code> is not a forecast; it
is an argument that something ought to be prevented, which is why the pattern so often
follows a conditional - <code>そのままにしておくと</code>, <code>～すれば</code>,
<code>～たら</code> - and why the natural response to it is to change the plan.

With a **person** as the subject it turns into an accusation.
<code>あの人(ひと)ならやりかねない</code> means "he's quite capable of it", and it is not
a compliment. Used about someone present, it is a serious thing to say. Speakers do use it
about themselves, half-ruefully - <code>私(わたし)も忘(わす)れかねない</code> - but
that is a confession, not a neutral statement.

One structural point worth holding on to: because the pattern is a **double negative**, it
lands heavier than `かもしれない` while being grammatically shorter. Learners often reach
for it as a fancier "maybe" and end up sounding alarmed about nothing.

## Contrast

### ～かねない vs ～かもしれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かもしれない` weighs a possibility of **any kind**. `かねない` warns
about a **bad** one, and implies something should be done.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)になる<b>かもしれない</b>。
It might turn to snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
この坂(さか)は凍(こお)ると、すべって転(ころ)び<b>かねない</b>。
If this slope ices over, someone could easily slip and fall.
</pre>

Swap them and you can hear the difference. The first sentence with `かねない` would be odd
unless snow were a genuine hazard; the second with `かもしれない` becomes a shrug, which is
the wrong thing to say about a hazard. Note also that `かもしれない` attaches to plain
forms, including adjectives and nouns, while `かねない` only ever follows a verb stem.

### ～かねない vs ～恐(おそ)れがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>恐(おそ)れがある</code> is **institutional**: an official
statement that a risk exists. `かねない` is the **speaker's** judgement, and it can be
aimed at a person.

</div>

<pre>
夜(よる)にかけて川(かわ)の水(みず)が増(ふ)える<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
There is a risk of the river rising through the night.
</pre>

<pre>
このままでは締(し)め切(き)りに遅(おく)れ<b>かねません</b>。
At this rate we could well miss the deadline.
</pre>

The first is what a broadcaster says; it reports a risk without anyone owning it, and it
attaches to a plain form rather than a stem. The second is a colleague telling you the
truth. And where a person is the subject, only `かねない` is available:
<code>彼(かれ)なら断(ことわ)りかねない</code> works, while the
<code>恐(おそ)れ</code> version would sound like a police bulletin.

### ～かねない vs ～かねる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the positive form means the negative thing. `かねる` = *cannot*;
`かねない` = *could well*.

</div>

<pre>
その件(けん)については、お答(こた)え<b>しかねます</b>。
I'm afraid I am not in a position to answer that.
</pre>

<pre>
はっきり断(ことわ)らないと、相手(あいて)は了承(りょうしょう)したと思(おも)い<b>かねない</b>。
If you don't refuse clearly, the other side could well take it as agreement.
</pre>

`かねる` is the polite refusal machine of business Japanese -
<code>いたしかねます</code>, <code>わかりかねます</code>, <code>決(き)めかねる</code> -
and it says the speaker cannot or will not do the thing. Attach `ない` to it and the
meaning flips to its opposite. This pair catches almost everyone once, and the way out is
to remember which one is polite: refusals are `かねます`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今度(こんど)の試合(しあい)は勝(か)ち<b>かねない</b>。 → ✅ 今度(こんど)の試合(しあい)は勝(か)つ<b>かもしれない</b>。
  Winning is good news, and `かねない` only takes bad news. The sentence as written sounds
  as if victory would be a disaster.

- ❌ 大(おお)きな問題(もんだい)になる<b>かねない</b>。 → ✅ 大(おお)きな問題(もんだい)になり<b>かねない</b>。
  It attaches to the ます-stem, never to the dictionary form.

- ❌ この方法(ほうほう)では失敗(しっぱい)し<b>かねます</b>。（meaning "we could well fail"） → ✅ この方法(ほうほう)では失敗(しっぱい)し<b>かねません</b>。
  `かねます` says the speaker is unable to fail, which is nonsense here. The warning form
  is the negative one.

- ❌ その薬(くすり)は危険(きけん)<b>かねない</b>。 → ✅ その薬(くすり)は危険(きけん)な状態(じょうたい)を招(まね)き<b>かねない</b>。
  Verbs only. To get an adjective idea into the pattern, turn it into something that
  happens.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

後(あと)でトラブルになり<b>かねない</b>（or かねません）。

Trouble is an unwanted outcome and the sentence is a warning against cutting the

<code>なり</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この辺(あた)りは夜(よる)になると暗(くら)いので、事故(じこ)が（起(お)こりかねません・起(お)こるかもしれません）から気(き)をつけてください。

Both are grammatical, but the sentence ends in a warning, and `かねない` is the form that

</details>

</details>

and might follow up with a friendly reminder.

(b) is about his character. It says forgetting is the sort of thing he does, and the
speaker disapproves - close to English "he's perfectly capable of forgetting". Said where
he can hear it, (b) is an insult that (a) is not.

</details>

# ～恐れがある

**Meaning** · there is a risk that ～; there are fears that ～ - read *おそれ*; the official way to state that something bad may happen

<code>恐(おそ)れ</code> is the noun made from 恐(おそ)れる, "to fear", and the pattern
is literally "a fear of ～ exists". Nobody in particular does the fearing, and that is the
point: the risk is presented as a fact about the world rather than as one person's worry.
It is the voice of a weather bulletin, a warning label and a company announcement, and it
attaches only to outcomes people would rather avoid.

## Formation

<code>恐(おそ)れ</code> is a noun, so what comes in front of it is in noun-modifying
shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + 恐(おそ)れがある | 倒(たお)れる → 倒(たお)れる恐(おそ)れがある |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 恐(おそ)れがある | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かない恐(おそ)れがある |
| Noun | Noun + の + 恐(おそ)れがある | 津波(つなみ) → 津波(つなみ)の恐(おそ)れがある |
| Polite | … + 恐(おそ)れがあります | 遅(おく)れる → 遅(おく)れる恐(おそ)れがあります |
| Modifying a noun | … + 恐(おそ)れのある + Noun | 落(お)ちる → 落(お)ちる恐(おそ)れのある場所(ばしょ) |
| Risk newly emerging | … + 恐(おそ)れが出(で)てきた | 中止(ちゅうし)になる → 中止(ちゅうし)になる恐(おそ)れが出(で)てきた |
| No such risk | … + 恐(おそ)れはない | 広(ひろ)がる → 広(ひろ)がる恐(おそ)れはない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - news copy often writes the word in kana as おそれ, and legal drafting uses the
old character 虞(おそれ). All three spellings are the same word. Keep it apart from the
adverb 恐(おそ)らく, "probably", which is related but does a completely different job.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の影響(えいきょう)で停電(ていでん)する<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
There is a risk of power cuts because of the typhoon.
</pre>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)を飲(の)むと眠(ねむ)くなる<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
This medicine may cause drowsiness.
</pre>

<pre>
古(ふる)い配線(はいせん)をそのままにしておくと、火事(かじ)の<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
Leaving old wiring untouched carries a risk of fire.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の斜面(しゃめん)が崩(くず)れる<b>恐(おそ)れがある</b>ため、この道(みち)は通行止(つうこうど)めです。
This road is closed as there is a danger of the hillside giving way.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)の遅(おく)れで、開業(かいぎょう)が来年(らいねん)に延(の)びる<b>恐(おそ)れが出(で)てきました</b>。
With the building work behind schedule, there is now a risk the opening will slip to next year.
</pre>

<pre>
強(つよ)い風(かぜ)で看板(かんばん)が落(お)ちてくる<b>恐(おそ)れのある</b>場所(ばしょ)には近(ちか)づかないでください。
Please keep away from places where signs could be blown down.
</pre>

<pre>
感染(かんせん)が再(ふたた)び広(ひろ)がる<b>恐(おそ)れがある</b>として、専門家(せんもんか)は注意(ちゅうい)を呼(よ)びかけています。
Warning that the infection may spread again, specialists are urging people to be careful.
</pre>

<pre>
報告(ほうこく)がこれ以上(いじょう)遅(おく)れれば、会社(かいしゃ)の責任(せきにん)が問(と)われる<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
If the report is delayed any further, the company itself risks being held to account.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **public, institutional language**. Its natural homes are weather and disaster
warnings, medical leaflets, product labels, safety notices, news reporting and corporate
statements. What it buys you is distance: the sentence names a risk without naming anyone
who is afraid, and without accusing anyone of causing it. That is exactly what an
announcement needs.

Turned on small private matters it is comically heavy.
<code>遅刻(ちこく)する恐(おそ)れがあります</code> about your own morning sounds like a
transport authority talking about itself. **In ordinary speech, say
<code>遅(おく)れるかもしれません</code> or
<code>遅(おく)れそうです</code>.** The pattern earns its place when the risk is real,
shared and worth announcing - which is why it does turn up in ordinary conversation about
storms, illness and money.

Two restrictions follow from the meaning of the noun. The outcome must be **undesirable**:
there is no such thing as
<code>当(あ)たる恐(おそ)れがある</code> about a lottery win, unless you are joking.
And it wants an **event**, not a static description - adjectives have to be turned into
something that happens: not
<code>高(たか)い恐(おそ)れ</code> but <code>高(たか)くなる恐(おそ)れ</code>.

The negative <code>恐(おそ)れはありません</code> is a set piece of reassurance in public
announcements: "there is no danger of …". It is the sentence a spokesperson says after an
incident, and hearing it, a Japanese audience knows the worst has been ruled out.

## Contrast

### ～恐(おそ)れがある vs ～可能性(かのうせい)がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>可能性(かのうせい)</code> is **neutral** and works for good
outcomes and bad. <code>恐(おそ)れ</code> is only ever bad.

</div>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)、新(あたら)しい店(みせ)が開(ひら)く<b>可能性(かのうせい)があります</b>。
There is a chance a new shop will open next month.
</pre>

<pre>
大雪(おおゆき)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まる<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
There is a risk the trains will stop because of the heavy snow.
</pre>

Both are formal, and in a report about hazards they are close to interchangeable - which
is why the choice is a good test of whether a writer has understood the sentence. If the
outcome would please anybody, <code>恐(おそ)れ</code> is wrong. Note too that
<code>可能性(かのうせい)</code> takes な-adjectives and nouns through `の`, so it is the
more flexible of the two grammatically.

### ～恐(おそ)れがある vs ～かねない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>恐(おそ)れがある</code> states that a risk **exists**;
`かねない` is somebody **warning** you about it. The first has no speaker in it, the second
is all speaker.

</div>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)の不足(ふそく)で生産(せいさん)が止(と)まる<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
There is a risk production will halt owing to the shortage of parts.
</pre>

<pre>
このまま注文(ちゅうもん)を増(ふ)やせば、在庫(ざいこ)を抱(かか)え込(こ)み<b>かねません</b>。
If we keep increasing orders like this we could well end up stuck with stock.
</pre>

The first could appear in a press release; the second is a colleague arguing against a
plan. `かねない` also takes people as subjects, which
<code>恐(おそ)れがある</code> resists: a sentence saying that a named person
<code>恐(おそ)れがある</code> reads like a police report about them.

### ～恐(おそ)れがある vs ～かもしれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same territory, different altitude. `かもしれない` is what a person
says; <code>恐(おそ)れがある</code> is what an institution announces.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は会場(かいじょう)が混(こ)む<b>かもしれない</b>から、早(はや)めに行(い)こう。
The venue might be crowded tomorrow, so let's go early.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)が大変(たいへん)混雑(こんざつ)する<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>ので、時間(じかん)に余裕(よゆう)を持(も)ってお越(こ)しください。
As the venue is expected to become extremely crowded, please allow plenty of time.
</pre>

Identical facts, two registers. The second is the announcement the organisers put on the
website; the first is you texting a friend about it. Learners who have just met
<code>恐(おそ)れがある</code> tend to use it where `かもしれない` belongs, and the effect
is of somebody reading their own life out over a public address system.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)は天気(てんき)が回復(かいふく)する<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は天気(てんき)が回復(かいふく)する<b>でしょう</b>。
  Good news cannot take <code>恐(おそ)れ</code>. The noun means *fear*, and clearing
  weather is nobody's fear.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)<b>恐(おそ)れがある</b>ため、通行止(つうこうど)めです。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)<b>の恐(おそ)れがある</b>ため、通行止(つうこうど)めです。
  <code>恐(おそ)れ</code> is a noun, so another noun in front of it needs `の`. Only a
  verb or adjective clause attaches directly.

- ❌ この川(かわ)は危(あぶ)ない<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。 → ✅ この川(かわ)は増水(ぞうすい)する<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
  The pattern wants an event that might occur, not a description of how things already
  are. Turn the adjective into something that happens.

- ❌ ごめん、待(ま)ち合(あ)わせに遅(おく)れる<b>恐(おそ)れがある</b>。 → ✅ ごめん、待(ま)ち合(あ)わせに遅(おく)れ<b>そう</b>。
  Grammatical, and far too grand for a text message to a friend. Save the pattern for
  risks that would be announced rather than mentioned.

## Practice

**1.** 強(つよ)い地震(じしん)のあとは、津波(つなみ)____恐(おそ)れがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

津波(つなみ)<b>の</b>恐(おそ)れがあります。

津波(つなみ) is a noun and <code>恐(おそ)れ</code> is a noun, so `の` has to link
them. Had the sentence used a verb - <code>津波(つなみ)が来(く)る</code> - it would
attach directly with no particle.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 今年(ことし)は野菜(やさい)の値段(ねだん)が（下(さ)がる恐(おそ)れ・下(さ)がる可能性(かのうせい)）があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

For a shopper, falling vegetable prices are good news, and
<code>恐(おそ)れ</code> cannot describe a welcome outcome. Written from a farmer's point
of view the risk version would suddenly make sense - which shows that the choice depends
on whose interests the sentence is written from.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a public notice: "Heavy rain may cause the river to overflow. Please move to higher ground early."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A flood warning is exactly the register this pattern exists for: an impersonal statement

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この建物(たてもの)は古(ふる)くて、倒(たお)れるかもしれない恐(おそ)れがある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この建物(たてもの)は古(ふる)くて、倒(たお)れる<b>恐(おそ)れがある</b>。

<code>恐(おそ)れがある</code> already says the thing may happen, so `かもしれない` in
front of it doubles the guess. Attach the plain form of the verb straight to the noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 台風(たいふう)で停電(ていでん)する<b>恐(おそ)れがあります</b>。
(b) 台風(たいふう)で停電(ていでん)し<b>かねません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the risk impersonally, the way a power company or a broadcaster would. There is
no speaker in the sentence and no implied criticism - it is information, and the listener
can do with it as they like.

(b) puts a person behind it. Someone has looked at the situation and is telling you it
could go wrong, usually as an argument for doing something now - charging a battery,
cancelling a plan. The facts are the same; (b) comes with a recommendation attached.

</details>

# ～そうにない

**Meaning** · shows no sign of ～ing; there's no prospect of it - the speaker has looked at the situation and can see nothing that points to the thing happening

`～そうにない` is the negative of the *looks like it will* `そうだ`, and it reports
**prospects, not probabilities**. Nothing is being calculated. The speaker is describing
what the queue, the sky, the pile of work or the other person's face actually looks like,
and what it looks like is: not happening. On the confidence ladder it sits low down and on
the negative side - weaker than <code>わけがない</code>, which rules the thing out
altogether, but far more definite than a shrugged <code>ないだろう</code>.

## Formation

A **verb** pattern. It attaches to the ます-stem, exactly where the positive `そうだ`
attaches.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + そうにない | 進(すす)みます → 進(すす)みそうにない |
| する-verb | ～し + そうにない | 回復(かいふく)する → 回復(かいふく)しそうにない |
| 来(く)る | 来(き) + そうにない | 来(く)る → 来(き)そうにない |
| Potential verb (very common) | potential ます-stem + そうにない | 行(い)けます → 行(い)けそうにない |
| Polite | ます-stem + そうにありません | 届(とど)きます → 届(とど)きそうにありません |
| Modifying a noun | … + そうにない + Noun | 直(なお)る → 直(なお)りそうにない時計(とけい) |
| Emphatic variants | ます-stem + そうもない / そうにもない | 勝(か)てます → 勝(か)てそうもない |
| い-adjective (no direct form) | ～く + なさそうだ | 甘(あま)い → 甘(あま)くなさそうだ |

## Examples

<pre>
この列(れつ)はしばらく進(すす)み<b>そうにない</b>。
This queue doesn't look like moving for a while.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)はまだ起(お)き<b>そうにない</b>。
There's no sign of my little brother getting up yet.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)が長(なが)くて、昼(ひる)ごはんは食(た)べられ<b>そうにない</b>。
This is dragging on - it doesn't look as though I'll get any lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな字(じ)では、誰(だれ)にも読(よ)んでもらえ<b>そうにありません</b>。
Written like this, it doesn't look as if anyone is going to read it.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)が多(おお)すぎて、一度(いちど)では運(はこ)び切(き)れ<b>そうにない</b>。
There's too much luggage - no chance of shifting it all in one go.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)の機嫌(きげん)が悪(わる)くて、今日(きょう)は相談(そうだん)を切(き)り出(だ)せ<b>そうにありません</b>。
The boss is in a foul mood, so it doesn't look as if I can raise it with him today.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)計算(けいさん)しても数字(すうじ)が合(あ)わず、報告書(ほうこくしょ)は予定(よてい)どおりには出(だ)せ<b>そうにない</b>。
However often I redo the sums the figures won't match, and the report isn't going to be ready on schedule.
</pre>

<pre>
遠(とお)くから来(き)てくれたのに、ゆっくり話(はな)す時間(じかん)は取(と)れ<b>そうにありません</b>。
You came all this way, and yet it doesn't look as though we'll get a proper chance to talk.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and equally at home in speech and writing. The polite
`そうにありません` is standard business Japanese for delivering an inconvenience: it puts
the blame on circumstances rather than on the speaker, which is why the potential form
plus this ending - <code>伺(うかが)えそうにありません</code>,
<code>間(ま)に合(あ)わせられそうにありません</code> - is the workhorse of polite bad
news.

Because it reads **signs**, it belongs to the near future and to situations already under
way. It also carries a faint note of the speaker having waited: a queue that
<code>進(すす)みそうにない</code> is one you have been standing in. That is what makes
<code>気配(けはい)がない</code> and
<code>見込(みこ)みがない</code> feel like relatives - all three describe an absence of
signs rather than a judgement about the world.

The noun-modifying use is worth practising because it is compact and very common:
<code>終(お)わりそうにない仕事(しごと)</code>, "work that shows no sign of ending".
English needs a relative clause for this; Japanese just puts it in front of the noun.

<div class="note">

**Note** - `そうにない`, `そうもない` and `そうにもない` are the same pattern in three
shapes. `そうにない` is the plainest; the ones with `も` carry a "not even" colouring and
feel a shade more emphatic. In most sentences speakers use them interchangeably, so treat
the difference as a tendency rather than a rule.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～そうにない vs ～そうもない・～そうにもない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three mean the same thing. `も` adds emphasis, not information.

</div>

<pre>
バスは30分(ぷん)待(ま)っても来(き)<b>そうにない</b>。
Half an hour of waiting and there's still no sign of a bus.
</pre>

<pre>
バスは30分(ぷん)待(ま)っても来(き)<b>そうにもない</b>。
Half an hour of waiting and not the slightest sign of a bus.
</pre>

The second is the one you say through your teeth. Since the difference is only emphasis,
the practical advice is to produce `そうにない` and recognise the other two - and to notice
that a sentence with `も` usually has something else in it doing emotional work as well
(いくら, どんなに, まったく, an exasperated ending).

### ～そうにない vs ～っこない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうにない` reports **what it looks like**. `っこない` declares the
thing **flatly impossible**, in casual speech, with feeling.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)から出(で)ても、開演(かいえん)には着(つ)け<b>そうにない</b>。
Even leaving now, it doesn't look as though I'll get there for the curtain.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)から出(で)たって、着(つ)け<b>っこない</b>よ。
Leave now? No way you'd make it.
</pre>

`っこない` also takes the ます-stem, so the two are easy to swap by accident. The register
is not the same at all: `っこない` is blunt, casual and slightly rough, fine among friends
and wrong in front of a client. It is also absolute, where `そうにない` leaves the door
open a crack - and that crack is often exactly what politeness needs.

### ～そうにない vs ～ようがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうにない` says there is **no sign** of it happening.
<code>ようがない</code> says there is **no way to do it** - the means do not exist.

</div>

<pre>
連絡(れんらく)が取(と)れ<b>そうにない</b>。
It doesn't look as though I'll be able to reach them.
</pre>

<pre>
電話番号(でんわばんごう)も住所(じゅうしょ)も知(し)らないので、連絡(れんらく)の<b>しようがない</b>。
I have neither their number nor their address, so there is simply no way to contact them.
</pre>

The first is a forecast from circumstances: the line is busy, nobody is picking up, but
you might get through later. The second is structural - the method itself is missing, and
waiting will not help. Learners often use `そうにない` where the real problem is a missing
method, which sounds oddly optimistic.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)は来(く)る<b>そうにない</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は来(き)<b>そうにない</b>。
  It attaches to the ます-stem, and the ます-stem of 来(く)る is 来(き). Writing the
  dictionary form here also risks being read as the hearsay `そうだ`.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は今日中(きょうじゅう)に解決(かいけつ)できる<b>そうにない</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は今日中(きょうじゅう)に解決(かいけつ)でき<b>そうにない</b>。
  Potential verbs follow the same rule as any other verb: strip to the stem.
  <code>できる</code> → <code>でき</code>.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ら<b>なさそうにない</b>。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>そうにない</b>。
  The negation is already inside the ending. Negating the verb as well flips the meaning
  back to "it looks like it will rain".

- ❌ このスープは熱(あつ)<b>そうにない</b>。 → ✅ このスープは熱(あつ)<b>くなさそうだ</b>。
  Adjectives have no direct form here. Negate the adjective first, then add そう - which
  inserts the さ.

## Practice

**1.** 窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ると工事(こうじ)がまだ続(つづ)いている。この音(おと)では集中(しゅうちゅう)でき____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この音(おと)では集中(しゅうちゅう)でき<b>そうにない</b>（or できそうにありません）。

The noise is visible, audible evidence, and the speaker is reading it: there is no prospect
of concentrating. The potential <code>できる</code> drops to the stem
<code>でき</code> before the ending.

</details>

**2.** Choose, thinking about who you are speaking to - a client on the phone:
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません、本日中(ほんじつちゅう)のご返答(へんとう)は（できっこありません・できそうにありません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本日中(ほんじつちゅう)のご返答(へんとう)は<b>できそうにありません</b>。

`っこない` is casual and blunt, and putting it in polite clothes does not rescue it - to a
client it sounds like a flat refusal thrown across a table. `そうにありません` presents the
same fact as a reading of the circumstances, which is what an apology needs.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It doesn't look as though this cold is going to clear up before the weekend."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この風邪(かぜ)は週末(しゅうまつ)までには治(なお)り<b>そうにない</b>。

A cold you are currently suffering is exactly the kind of thing whose signs you can read,
which is why English "doesn't look as though" maps so neatly onto this pattern.
治(なお)る is an う-verb, so its stem is 治(なお)り.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は暑(あつ)そうにないですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は<b>暑(あつ)くなさそう</b>ですね。

暑(あつ)い is an い-adjective and cannot take `そうにない` directly. Negate it first -
<code>暑(あつ)くない</code> - and then add そう, which inserts さ to give
<code>暑(あつ)くなさそうだ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)に勝(か)て<b>そうにない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)に勝(か)て<b>っこない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a reading of the evidence in front of you - the score so far, his form, your own
tired legs. It is discouraged rather than final, and it would be a perfectly ordinary
thing to say to a coach.

(b) shuts the question. It is casual, emphatic and slightly rough, closer to "there's no
chance whatsoever", and it carries an emotional charge (resignation, or contempt) that (a)
does not. Said about a teammate rather than yourself, (b) is an insult.

</details>

# ～ものがある

**Meaning** · there is something ～ about it - the speaker names a quality they can feel in something but could not fully spell out

The `もの` here is deliberately vague: *something*. The pattern picks out a quality the
speaker has genuinely registered - moving, remarkable, unbearable, faintly sad - and
locates it **in the thing itself** rather than in the speaker's mood. That is what makes
it a judgement rather than a description. It is not a guess about facts, so it does not
compete with `かもしれない` and its relatives; it is the sentence you reach for once you
have stopped guessing and started evaluating.

## Formation

`もの` is a noun, so what precedes it is in noun-modifying shape. The frame is very often
<code>Noun + には … ものがある</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ものがある | 心(こころ)を打(う)つ → 心(こころ)を打(う)つものがある |
| Verb (passive of feeling) | ～させられる + ものがある | 考(かんが)えさせられる → 考(かんが)えさせられるものがある |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ものがある | 捨(す)てられない → 捨(す)てられないものがある |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものがある | 寂(さび)しい → 寂(さび)しいものがある |
| な-adjective | ～な + ものがある | 異常(いじょう)だ → 異常(いじょう)なものがある |
| Polite | … + ものがあります | 厳(きび)しい → 厳(きび)しいものがあります |
| Past impression | … + ものがあった | 光(ひか)る → 光(ひか)るものがあった |
| Noun | not available | ❌ 才能(さいのう)ものがある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern needs a predicate that **evaluates**. Verbs of effect
（心(こころ)を打(う)つ, 人(ひと)を引(ひ)きつける,
考(かんが)えさせられる, 目(め)を見(み)張(は)る）and adjectives of impression
（厳(きび)しい, 寂(さび)しい, 見事(みごと)な）are its natural partners. A plain
physical fact - <code>狭(せま)い</code>, <code>重(おも)い</code> - produces a
sentence that means nothing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)の歌声(うたごえ)には心(こころ)を打(う)つ<b>ものがある</b>。
There is something in his singing that moves you.
</pre>

<pre>
この絵(え)には人(ひと)を引(ひ)きつける<b>ものがある</b>。
There is something about this painting that draws you in.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)の努力(どりょく)には目(め)を見(み)張(は)る<b>ものがある</b>。
The effort she has put in is genuinely remarkable.
</pre>

<pre>
その一言(ひとこと)には考(かんが)えさせられる<b>ものがありました</b>。
That one remark really gave me something to think about.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)の作品(さくひん)だが、色(いろ)の使(つか)い方(かた)には光(ひか)る<b>ものがある</b>。
It's a newcomer's piece, but there is something that shines in the way the colour is used.
</pre>

<pre>
近年(きんねん)の夏(なつ)の暑(あつ)さは異常(いじょう)な<b>ものがある</b>。
There is something quite abnormal about the heat of recent summers.
</pre>

<pre>
定年(ていねん)まで勤(つと)め上(あ)げた父(ちち)の背中(せなか)には、言葉(ことば)では説明(せつめい)できない<b>ものがあった</b>。
There was something about my father's back, after a working life seen through to the end, that I could not put into words.
</pre>

<pre>
古(ふる)い町並(まちな)みが少(すこ)しずつ消(き)えていくのを見(み)るのは、やはり寂(さび)しい<b>ものがある</b>。
Watching the old streets disappear a little at a time does leave you with a certain sadness.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and considered rather than chatty. It belongs to reviews, essays, commentary,
speeches, sports reporting and the kind of conversation where somebody is summing
something up. Nobody says it about lunch. The polite `ものがあります` is common in
presentations and formal remarks, and the past `ものがあった` is how a writer reports an
impression that has stayed with them.

The pattern is usually - not always - **appreciative**. Its most frequent partners are
compliments: <code>目(め)を見(み)張(は)るものがある</code> for astonishing
progress, <code>光(ひか)るものがある</code> for raw talent,
<code>心(こころ)を打(う)つものがある</code> for something moving. It also handles
weight and difficulty well - <code>厳(きび)しいものがある</code>,
<code>耐(た)えがたいものがある</code> - where it lends a measured, adult tone to a
complaint.

What it will not do is report your own condition. The quality has to be **in the object**,
observed from outside; <code>今日(きょう)は疲(つか)れたものがある</code> is not
Japanese. Nor does it take a bare noun: the slot in front of `もの` is a modifying clause,
so a noun has to be turned into something like
<code>天才(てんさい)的(てき)な</code> first.

There is a real vagueness in the meaning, and it is deliberate. Choosing this pattern
signals that the speaker is pointing at something they can sense but not itemise - which
is precisely why it sounds thoughtful, and why over-using it sounds evasive.

## Contrast

### ～ものがある vs ～ものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ものだ` states **how things are in general**.
`ものがある` picks out a quality **in one particular thing**.

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)というのは早(はや)く過(す)ぎる<b>ものだ</b>。
Time does go by quickly, doesn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりに母校(ぼこう)を訪(たず)ねると、なつかしい<b>ものがある</b>。
Visiting my old school after so long, there is something quite nostalgic about it.
</pre>

`ものだ` generalises: it says this is the nature of time, or of people, or of youth.
`ものがある` refuses to generalise - it is anchored to this school, this painting, this
performance. If you could put <code>人間(にんげん)は</code> or
<code>というのは</code> at the front of the sentence, you want `ものだ`.

### ～ものがある vs ～ところがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ところがある</code> names a **definable trait**, usually of a
person. `ものがある` names an **indefinable quality**, usually of a work, a scene or a
performance.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)には人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)かない<b>ところがある</b>。
He has a habit of not listening to people.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の文章(ぶんしょう)には人(ひと)を引(ひ)き込(こ)む<b>ものがある</b>。
There is something about his writing that pulls you in.
</pre>

<code>ところ</code> is a place, so it identifies a spot in someone's character you could
point at - and because you can point at it, the pattern is often mildly critical.
`もの` is the opposite: the whole appeal of the sentence is that the speaker cannot point
at it. Describing a person's flaw with `ものがある` sounds grandly vague; describing an
artwork's magic with <code>ところがある</code> makes it sound like a checklist item.

### ～ものがある vs the literal もの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the sentence could be answered with **"which one?"**, `もの` is an
ordinary noun meaning *thing*. The pattern cannot be pointed at.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)には安(やす)い<b>ものがある</b>。
This shop has some cheap items.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)の品(しな)ぞろえには見事(みごと)な<b>ものがある</b>。
There is something quite admirable about this shop's range.
</pre>

The first sentence has real objects in it; you could pick one up. The second has nothing
you could pick up, only a quality. In practice the evaluative predicate does most of the
disambiguating - <code>安(やす)い</code> describes goods,
<code>見事(みごと)な</code> describes an impression - and so does the
<code>には</code> frame, which sets up an *aspect of* rather than a *contents of*.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は親切(しんせつ)<b>ものがある</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)には親切(しんせつ)な<b>ところがある</b>。
  Two repairs. A な-adjective needs its `な`, and a nameable trait in a person belongs to
  <code>ところがある</code>.

- ❌ 私(わたし)の部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い<b>ものがある</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)の部屋(へや)は狭(せま)い。
  A measurable physical fact has no hidden quality to point at. The pattern needs a
  predicate that evaluates.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は疲(つか)れた<b>ものがある</b>。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)はかなり疲(つか)れた。
  Your own tiredness is a state, not a quality perceived in something outside you.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)の才能(さいのう)には天才(てんさい)<b>ものがある</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)の才能(さいのう)には天才(てんさい)的(てき)な<b>ものがある</b>。
  A bare noun cannot sit in front of `もの`. Make it a modifier first.

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)のスピーチには、聞(き)く人(ひと)を動(うご)かす____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

聞(き)く人(ひと)を動(うご)かす<b>ものがある</b>（or ものがあります）。

<code>動(うご)かす</code> is a verb of effect, exactly the kind this pattern wants, and
the <code>には</code> frame sets up "there is something about his speech that …". The verb
attaches in plain form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 田中(たなか)さんには時々(ときどき)冷(つめ)たい（ものがある・ところがある）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

時々(ときどき)冷(つめ)たい<b>ところがある</b>。

A recurring streak in a person's character is a nameable trait, and
<code>ところ</code> is the pattern for that. `ものがある` would suggest some ineffable
quality of coldness radiating from him, which is far grander than the sentence intends.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "There is something about the ending of that film that stays with you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの映画(えいが)の終(お)わり方(かた)には、心(こころ)に残(のこ)る<b>ものがある</b>。

English "there is something about X that …" maps almost word for word onto the
<code>Xには … ものがある</code> frame, and <code>心(こころ)に残(のこ)る</code> supplies
the evaluative verb.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この工場(こうじょう)の技術(ぎじゅつ)は見事(みごと)ものがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この工場(こうじょう)の技術(ぎじゅつ)<b>には見事(みごと)なものがあります</b>。

見事(みごと) is a な-adjective and must keep its `な` before the noun `もの`. Changing
<code>は</code> to <code>には</code> is not strictly required, but it is the frame the
pattern normally appears in and it makes the sentence read naturally.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この作品(さくひん)は素晴(すば)らしいです。
(b) この作品(さくひん)には素晴(すば)らしい<b>ものがあります</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a straight verdict on the whole work: it is excellent, and the speaker is willing
to say so plainly.

(b) is narrower and more careful. It says that somewhere in the work there is something of
real quality - which leaves open that the rest may be uneven, and which suggests the
speaker has looked closely enough to find it. In a review, (b) is the sentence you write
about a promising newcomer and (a) the one you write about a masterpiece.

</details>

# ～ではあるまいか

**Meaning** · might it not be that ～?; is it not perhaps ～? - a literary way of asserting something while wearing the face of a question

`まい` is the classical negative of `だろう`: where `だろう` guesses that something *is*,
`まい` guesses that something *is not*. So <code>ではあるまい</code> means "surely it is
not", and adding `か` turns that into "surely it is not …, is it?" - which, by the logic of
rhetorical questions, is how a writer says **I believe it is**. In force it is identical to
<code>のではないだろうか</code>. In register it is a century older.

## Formation

The `ある` in the middle is the `ある` of `である`, so the pattern behaves like a formal
copula. Verbs and い-adjectives need `の` to nominalise the clause first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ではあるまいか | 偶然(ぐうぜん) → 偶然(ぐうぜん)ではあるまいか |
| な-adjective | drop だ + ではあるまいか | 乱暴(らんぼう)だ → 乱暴(らんぼう)ではあるまいか |
| Verb | plain form + のではあるまいか | 忘(わす)れている → 忘(わす)れているのではあるまいか |
| い-adjective | plain form + のではあるまいか | 危(あぶ)ない → 危(あぶ)ないのではあるまいか |
| Statement, no question | … + ではあるまい | 簡単(かんたん) → 簡単(かんたん)ではあるまい |
| Modern equivalent (write this) | … + のではないだろうか | 偶然(ぐうぜん) → 偶然(ぐうぜん)なのではないだろうか |
| Spoken equivalent (say this) | … + のではないでしょうか | 偶然(ぐうぜん) → 偶然(ぐうぜん)なのではないでしょうか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `まい` does attach to verbs directly（<code>行(い)くまい</code>,
<code>するまい</code>）, and the rules for which stem it takes are messy. In this pattern
you are spared them: `まい` follows `ある` and nothing else, so
<code>ではあるまいか</code> can be learnt as one fixed block.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
それは偶然(ぐうぜん)<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Might that not have been a coincidence?
</pre>

<pre>
我々(われわれ)は大切(たいせつ)なことを忘(わす)れているの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Are we not forgetting something important?
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)だけを見(み)て判断(はんだん)するのは、いささか乱暴(らんぼう)<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
To judge on the figures alone is surely a little crude.
</pre>

<pre>
この静(しず)けさこそ、この町(まち)の本当(ほんとう)の魅力(みりょく)なの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
It is this quietness, surely, that is the real charm of the town.
</pre>

<pre>
効率(こうりつ)ばかりを求(もと)めるあまり、大事(だいじ)なものを失(うしな)いつつあるの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
In chasing efficiency above all, are we not in the process of losing something that matters?
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)の進歩(しんぽ)を急(いそ)ぎすぎるのは、かえって危(あぶ)ないの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Might it not in fact be dangerous to hurry technological progress so?
</pre>

<pre>
少年(しょうねん)はあの日(ひ)、何(なに)もかも分(わ)かっていたの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Had the boy not, on that day, understood everything?
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)は皆(みな)、どこかで誰(だれ)かに支(ささ)えられて生(い)きているの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Every one of us, surely, is being held up by somebody somewhere.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is written, literary Japanese, and a learner should not produce it in
conversation.** Its homes are newspaper editorials, essays, criticism, the narration of
novels and the more elevated kind of speech - a memorial address, a retirement lecture. In
an ordinary exchange it lands like Victorian English: not wrong, but audibly costumed.

**What to say instead**, in order of how much weight you want:
<code>んじゃないですか</code> in relaxed speech,
<code>のではないでしょうか</code> in polite speech and business,
<code>のではないだろうか</code> in ordinary writing. All three do exactly the job that
<code>ではあるまいか</code> does; the only thing you lose is the antique varnish.

The varnish is not decorative, though - it does work. `まい` is grave, and a writer
choosing it is signalling that the question is a serious one, often a moral one. This is
why the pattern clusters around sentences beginning
<code>我々(われわれ)は</code> and <code>人(ひと)は</code>: it is the grammar of an
essayist asking the reader to reconsider something.

Note also that the question is **rhetorical**. Nobody answers it. A speaker who says
<code>ではあるまいか</code> has already decided, and is inviting agreement rather than
information - which is exactly the trick the whole family of negative-question hedges is
built on.

Two smaller points. The `か`-less form <code>ではあるまい</code> is a flat literary
negative guess: "it will surely not be". And in older prose you will meet
<code>あるまいか</code> written after であろう-style copulas and inside long sentences; if
you can spot the `まい`, you can read the rest.

## Contrast

### ～ではあるまいか vs ～のではないだろうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different century. Read
<code>ではあるまいか</code>; write and say
<code>のではないだろうか</code>.

</div>

<pre>
この方法(ほうほう)には無理(むり)があるの<b>ではないだろうか</b>。
Isn't there something unworkable about this approach?
</pre>

<pre>
この方法(ほうほう)には無理(むり)があるの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Is there not something unworkable about this approach?
</pre>

The English translations show the difference fairly: "isn't there" against "is there not".
Both hedge, both mean the writer thinks so, and the second sounds like a leading article.
In a university essay the first is safe everywhere; the second will read as a deliberate
stylistic choice, and had better be one.

### ～まい as a negative guess vs ～まい as a refusal

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - about **someone else**, `まい` means *surely will not*. About
**oneself**, it means *I will not* - a resolution.

</div>

<pre>
こんな時間(じかん)に来(く)る客(きゃく)もある<b>まい</b>。
No customer is going to turn up at this hour.
</pre>

<pre>
二度(にど)とあの店(みせ)には行(い)く<b>まい</b>と心(こころ)に決(き)めた。
I resolved never to set foot in that shop again.
</pre>

The first is <code>ないだろう</code>; the second is a literary
<code>ないつもりだ</code>. Both are worth recognising, because `まい` survives in modern
Japanese mostly inside fixed shapes - <code>負(ま)けるまい</code>,
<code>人(ひと)には言(い)うまい</code> - where a character is steeling themselves.

### ～ではあるまいか vs ～だろうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だろうか</code> genuinely **wonders**.
<code>ではあるまいか</code> has already **decided** and is asking you to agree.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は本当(ほんとう)のことを話(はな)している<b>だろうか</b>。
Is he telling the truth, I wonder?
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は何(なに)かを隠(かく)しているの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Is he not hiding something?
</pre>

The first leaves the writer genuinely uncertain, and the paragraph after it can go either
way. The second is a claim: what follows will be the evidence for it. This is the single
most useful thing to know about the whole negative-question family - the negative shape is
not doubt, it is politeness wrapped around a position.

## Common mistakes

- ❌「この店(みせ)、高(たか)いの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。」（to a friend in a shop） → ✅「この店(みせ)、高(たか)い<b>んじゃない</b>?」
  Grammatical and absurd. The literary register cannot be switched on for a small everyday
  remark without sounding like a parody.

- ❌ この判断(はんだん)は早(はや)すぎる<b>ではあるまいか</b>。 → ✅ この判断(はんだん)は早(はや)すぎるの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
  A verb or い-adjective clause has to be nominalised with `の` first. Only nouns and
  な-adjectives connect directly.

- ❌ 危険(きけん)だ<b>ではあるまいか</b>。 → ✅ 危険(きけん)<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
  The `だ` is already inside <code>ではある</code>. Leaving the original `だ` in place
  stacks two copulas.

- ❌「無理(むり)ではあるまいか。」「はい、無理(むり)です。」 → ✅「無理(むり)ではあるまいか。」（no answer expected）
  It is a rhetorical question. Treating it as a request for information misreads the
  sentence: the writer has already concluded, and is bringing the reader along.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence?
我々(われわれ)は便利(べんり)さと引(ひ)き換(か)えに、大切(たいせつ)な時間(じかん)を手放(てばな)しているの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a newspaper editorial, an essay or a formal address - written Japanese with a moral
argument in it. The giveaways are <code>我々(われわれ)</code> and the literary `まい`.
Nobody says this to a colleague at lunch; in speech it would be
<code>手放(てばな)しているのではないでしょうか</code>.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: 説明(せつめい)が足(た)りなかったのではあるまいか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

説明(せつめい)が足(た)りなかったの<b>ではないだろうか</b>。（polite: のではないでしょうか）

<code>ではあるまいか</code> maps one-to-one onto
<code>のではないだろうか</code>. Nothing else in the sentence changes, which is the
clearest possible demonstration that the difference between them is register alone.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese in a literary register: "Might it not be that we have been looking in the wrong place all along?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

我々(われわれ)は初(はじ)めから違(ちが)う場所(ばしょ)を探(さが)していたの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。

The verb clause takes `の` before <code>ではあるまいか</code>, and
<code>我々(われわれ)</code> matches the register the pattern brings with it. English
"might it not be that" is the closest natural equivalent.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この計画(けいかく)は危険(きけん)だではあるまいか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この計画(けいかく)は危険(きけん)<b>ではあるまいか</b>。

<code>ではある</code> already contains the copula, so the `だ` of
<code>危険(きけん)だ</code> has to go. The same trimming applies to nouns:
<code>偶然(ぐうぜん)ではあるまいか</code>, never
<code>偶然(ぐうぜん)だではあるまいか</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の説(せつ)は正(ただ)しい<b>だろうか</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)の説(せつ)は正(ただ)しいの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an open question. The writer does not know whether the theory holds, and the next
paragraph could argue either side.

(b) is a defence of the theory dressed as a question. The writer thinks it is right, and
is asking the reader to entertain that view; the paragraph after it will supply reasons.
The literary `まい` also raises the temperature, so (b) reads as the turn in an argument
rather than a passing thought.

</details>

# ～ないものか

**Meaning** · isn't there some way to ～?; couldn't it somehow ～? - a wish said out loud, half to oneself, that something difficult might be possible after all

The shape is a negative question, but nobody is being asked. `ないものか` is what a speaker
says when a situation is stuck and they want it unstuck: it names the thing they wish for
and hangs a `か` on it, which is a very Japanese way of wishing without demanding. The
frustration is audible, and so is the hope - the speaker has not quite given up.

## Formation

Attaches to the **ない-form of a verb**. Adjectives get in through
<code>～くならない</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ものか | ならない → ならないものか |
| Potential verb (very common) | potential ない-form + ものか | 直(なお)せない → 直(なお)せないものか |
| ある | ない + ものか | 方法(ほうほう)はない → 方法(ほうほう)はないものか |
| い-adjective (through なる) | ～くならない + ものか | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)くならないものか |
| Softer, more musing | ～ない + ものだろうか | できない → できないものだろうか |
| Polite | ～ない + ものでしょうか | 届(とど)かない → 届(とど)かないものでしょうか |
| Reported wish | ～ないものか + と思(おも)う | 会(あ)えない → 会(あ)えないものかと思(おも)う |
| Standard opener | 何(なん)とか + ～ないものか | ならない → 何(なん)とかならないものか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negative is not optional. After a **positive** plain form, `ものか` is an
entirely different pattern meaning emphatic denial:
<code>行(い)くものか</code> is "as if I'd go!" See the Contrast section - this is the one
place where getting the polarity wrong reverses the sentence completely.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
何(なん)とかなら<b>ないものか</b>。
Isn't there something that can be done about it?
</pre>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し安(やす)くなら<b>ないものか</b>。
Couldn't it be a bit cheaper?
</pre>

<pre>
この渋滞(じゅうたい)を何(なん)とかでき<b>ないものだろうか</b>。
Is there really no way of doing something about this congestion?
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちが安心(あんしん)して遊(あそ)べる場所(ばしょ)を作(つく)れ<b>ないものか</b>。
Could we not make somewhere the children can play safely?
</pre>

<pre>
捨(す)てるには惜(お)しい。誰(だれ)か使(つか)ってくれ<b>ないものだろうか</b>。
It seems a shame to throw it out. Might somebody not have a use for it?
</pre>

<pre>
地元(じもと)の人(ひと)たちの声(こえ)が、もっと市(し)の計画(けいかく)に反映(はんえい)され<b>ないものでしょうか</b>。
Could local people's views not be reflected rather more in the city's plans?
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ失敗(しっぱい)を繰(く)り返(かえ)さずに済(す)む方法(ほうほう)は<b>ないものか</b>と考(かんが)え続(つづ)けている。
I keep wondering whether there isn't some way to avoid repeating the same mistake.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)使(つか)ってきた道具(どうぐ)だけに、どうにかして直(なお)せ<b>ないものか</b>と思(おも)っている。
Precisely because I have used the tool for so long, I keep hoping there is some way to mend it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Mildly formal, and thoughtful rather than chatty. It is at home in editorials and opinion
columns - <code>行政(ぎょうせい)は何(なん)とかできないものか</code> is the
grammar of a letter to the paper - and equally at home in speech when somebody is genuinely
stuck and thinking aloud. What it is not is casual. Among friends the everyday version is
<code>～ないかなあ</code>: <code>もう少(すこ)し安(やす)くならないかなあ</code>.

The three endings are a politeness gradient, and it is a real one.
<code>ないものか</code> is the bluntest and can sound impatient;
<code>ないものだろうか</code> softens it into musing;
<code>ないものでしょうか</code> is the form to use if anyone senior is listening.

The important pragmatic point: **it is a wish, not a request.** Because the sentence is
formally addressed to nobody, aiming it at a person turns it into a complaint about them.
<code>もう少(すこ)し早(はや)くできないものか</code> said to a colleague is a
rebuke; if you actually want them to hurry, you need a request form such as
<code>～ていただけませんか</code>.

It also wants something **outside the speaker's control** - traffic, prices, other people's
decisions, a broken object. Applied to your own free choices it sounds strange, because
there is nothing to wish about; for those, <code>～たいものだ</code> or
<code>～たらいいのに</code> is what you want.

## Contrast

### ～ないものか vs ～ものか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `ない`, it is a **wish**. Without `ない`, it is an **emphatic
denial**.

</div>

<pre>
何(なん)とか間(ま)に合(あ)わせられ<b>ないものか</b>。
Isn't there some way of getting it done in time?
</pre>

<pre>
あんな話(はなし)、信(しん)じられる<b>ものか</b>。
As if anyone could believe a story like that!
</pre>

The second is one of the strongest denials in the language, and it is casual and heated -
<code>負(ま)けるものか</code>, <code>誰(だれ)が行(い)くものか</code>. The two
patterns look almost identical on the page, and the whole difference is the `ない`. Read
the polarity first, before you read the tone.

### ～ないものか vs ～ないかなあ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same wish, different room.
<code>ないものか</code> belongs to writing and considered speech;
<code>ないかなあ</code> is what you mutter.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)、やま<b>ないかなあ</b>。
I wish this rain would let up.
</pre>

<pre>
この不便(ふべん)な仕組(しく)みを改(あらた)められ<b>ないものか</b>。
Could this inconvenient system not be reformed?
</pre>

Notice that the subject matter shifts with the register. `ないかなあ` handles small private
wishes - rain, a delivery, a text message - while `ないものか` reaches for things that
concern more people than the speaker. Using the formal one about your own umbrella is
faintly comic; using the casual one in an editorial would be unpublishable.

### ～ないものか vs ～てほしい・～ていただけませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないものか` wishes **into the air**.
<code>～てほしい</code> and <code>～ていただけませんか</code> point at a **person** who is
expected to act.

</div>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し待(ま)っていただけ<b>ませんか</b>。
Could you possibly wait a little longer?
</pre>

<pre>
もう少(すこ)し待(ま)ってもらえ<b>ないものか</b>。
Is there really no chance of being given a little longer?
</pre>

The first is a request, and the listener can say yes. The second is the speaker's own
frustration, and a listener who hears it aimed at them will hear pressure rather than a
question. If you want an action from someone, ask for it directly; keep
<code>ないものか</code> for the times when there is nobody to ask.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ もう少(すこ)し安(やす)い<b>ものか</b>。 → ✅ もう少(すこ)し安(やす)く<b>ならないものか</b>。
  Two problems at once. An adjective has to come in through <code>なる</code>, and without
  the `ない` the sentence becomes the denial pattern - "cheap, my foot!"

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)が解決(かいけつ)する<b>ものか</b>。（meaning "I wish it could be solved"） → ✅ この問題(もんだい)を解決(かいけつ)でき<b>ないものか</b>。
  Same trap from the other direction: the positive form denies. The wish needs the negative,
  and the potential makes the wish sound like a search for a way.

- ❌ すみません、窓(まど)を開(あ)けてくれ<b>ないものか</b>。 → ✅ すみません、窓(まど)を開(あ)けていただけ<b>ませんか</b>。
  A wish addressed to a person is not a polite request; it reads as impatience. Use a
  request form when you want someone to do something.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)でき<b>ないものか</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)も来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)し<b>たいものだ</b>。
  If the decision is yours to make, there is nothing to wish for. The pattern wants
  something the speaker cannot simply go and do.

## Practice

**1.** 毎年(まいとし)この坂道(さかみち)で事故(じこ)が起(お)きている。何(なん)とか____ものか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なん)とか<b>ならない</b>ものか。

<code>何(なん)とかならないものか</code> is the pattern's most idiomatic frame: a problem
outside the speaker's control, and a wish that somebody, somehow, will deal with it.
Without the `ない` the sentence would flip into a denial.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部長(ぶちょう)に向(む)かって - 「申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません、締(し)め切(き)りを（延(の)ばせないものか・延(の)ばしていただけないでしょうか）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

締(し)め切(き)りを<b>延(の)ばしていただけないでしょうか</b>。

You are asking a specific person to do a specific thing, so you need a request form. The
other version is a wish flung at the ceiling, and directed at a manager it sounds like a
complaint about the deadline rather than a request for help.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Could the bus service not be made a little more frequent?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

バスの本数(ほんすう)を、もう少(すこ)し増(ふ)やせ<b>ないものだろうか</b>。

A public matter outside the speaker's control is exactly this pattern's subject, and the
potential <code>増(ふ)やせる</code> turns it into "is there no way to increase it".
<code>ものだろうか</code> keeps it musing rather than impatient.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの古(ふる)い写真(しゃしん)、きれいに直(なお)せるものか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの古(ふる)い写真(しゃしん)、きれいに直(なお)せ<b>ないものか</b>。

As written, the sentence means "as if that could be restored!" - a flat denial. Adding the
`ない` turns it into the intended wish that some way of restoring the photo might exist.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 値段(ねだん)をもう少(すこ)し下(さ)げてもらえ<b>ないでしょうか</b>。
(b) 値段(ねだん)がもう少(すこ)し下(さ)がら<b>ないものでしょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a negotiation. It is addressed to the seller, it uses もらえる to name them as the
one who would act, and it expects an answer - yes, no, or a counter-offer.

(b) steps back from the person. The verb is intransitive, so nobody is being asked to do
anything; the speaker is lamenting the price in general. In a shop (a) may get you a
discount and (b) will get you sympathy, which is sometimes exactly the difference you want.

</details>

# ～のではないだろうか

**Meaning** · isn't it the case that ～?; I would suggest that ～ - the writer states a view and dresses it as a question so the reader is invited rather than told

Take it apart and it is four familiar pieces: `の` nominalises the clause, `ではない`
negates it, `だろう` guesses, `か` questions. Put together they do something none of them
does alone. **The speaker is not in doubt.** A negative question in Japanese, as in
English, is a way of asserting politely - "isn't this a bit expensive?" means you think it
is - and this pattern is the standard machinery for advancing a claim while leaving the
other person room to disagree. On the confidence ladder it sits close to
<code>と思(おも)う</code>, well above `かもしれない`, and it is nowhere near as tentative
as it looks.

## Formation

`の` is a nominaliser, so the clause in front of it is in noun-modifying shape. Nouns and
な-adjectives need `な` before the `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のではないだろうか | 違(ちが)う → 違(ちが)うのではないだろうか |
| Verb (past) | plain past + のではないだろうか | 間違(まちが)えた → 間違(まちが)えたのではないだろうか |
| い-adjective | plain form + のではないだろうか | 早(はや)い → 早(はや)いのではないだろうか |
| な-adjective | ～な + のではないだろうか | 無理(むり)だ → 無理(むり)なのではないだろうか |
| Noun | ～な + のではないだろうか | 誤解(ごかい) → 誤解(ごかい)なのではないだろうか |
| Polite (speech, business) | … + のではないでしょうか | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いのではないでしょうか |
| Casual speech | … + んじゃない / んじゃないかな | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いんじゃない |
| Literary | … + のではあるまいか | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いのではあるまいか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in speech the `の` contracts to `ん` almost without exception:
<code>のではないでしょうか</code> becomes
<code>んじゃないでしょうか</code>, and in relaxed conversation
<code>んじゃない?</code>. Written out in full, the pattern belongs to the page.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは少(すこ)し高(たか)すぎる<b>のではないだろうか</b>。
Isn't this a little too expensive?
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は何(なに)か悩(なや)んでいる<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。
Might he not have something on his mind?
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)を変(か)えたほうがいい<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。
Wouldn't it be better to move the time of the meeting?
</pre>

<pre>
この結果(けっか)は、調査(ちょうさ)の方法(ほうほう)そのものに問題(もんだい)があった<b>のではないだろうか</b>。
This result suggests that the fault lay with the survey method itself.
</pre>

<pre>
お忙(いそが)しいところ恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが、もう一度(いちど)確認(かくにん)したほうがよろしい<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。
I'm sorry to trouble you, but might it not be wise to check it once more?
</pre>

<pre>
便利(べんり)さを追(お)い求(もと)めた結果(けっか)、人(ひと)と人(ひと)の距離(きょり)はかえって遠(とお)くなった<b>のではないだろうか</b>。
In pursuing convenience, have we not in fact pushed people further apart?
</pre>

<pre>
若(わか)い世代(せだい)が本(ほん)を読(よ)まなくなったというより、読(よ)む時間(じかん)を奪(うば)われている<b>のではないだろうか</b>。
Rather than the young having stopped reading, is it not that their time for reading has been taken from them?
</pre>

<pre>
問題(もんだい)は制度(せいど)そのものではなく、運用(うんよう)の仕方(しかた)にある<b>のではないだろうか</b>。
The problem, surely, lies not in the system itself but in the way it is operated.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **the academic hedge**, and it is worth being clear-eyed about what it does. A
writer who ends a paragraph with <code>のではないだろうか</code> has just made a claim.
The negative question does not soften the claim's content at all; it softens the writer's
posture. The reader is left with a formal opportunity to disagree, and the argument moves
on as though the point were made. Japanese academic prose, editorials and thoughtful
essays run on this, and reading them requires taking the hedged sentences as the author's
actual position.

The family is a register ladder, and every rung says the same thing:

- <code>んじゃない?</code> - casual speech, friends
- <code>んじゃないですか</code> - polite conversation
- <code>のではないでしょうか</code> - business, presentations, careful speech
- <code>のではないだろうか</code> - writing: essays, papers, columns
- <code>のではあるまいか</code> - literary, and not for production

**What a learner should actually use in speech is
<code>のではないでしょうか</code>, or its contracted
<code>んじゃないでしょうか</code>.** It is one of the most valuable sentence endings in
business Japanese, because it lets you disagree with someone senior without contradicting
them: <code>少(すこ)し早(はや)すぎるのではないでしょうか</code> raises the objection
and simultaneously hands them the decision. Saying
<code>早(はや)すぎると思(おも)います</code> in the same meeting is not rude, but it
puts your judgement against theirs.

Two cautions. First, **overuse**. A paper in which every sentence hedges this way reads as
evasive, and Japanese style guides say so; use it at the points where you are actually
asking the reader to come with you. Second, **intonation**. Spoken with a rising tone it
can be heard as a genuine question; falling, it is the assertion described above. If you
really want to know something, ask directly.

## Contrast

### ～のではないだろうか vs ～と思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both express the speaker's view.
<code>と思(おも)う</code> puts the speaker's name on it;
<code>のではないだろうか</code> leaves the view on the table and steps back.

</div>

<pre>
この案(あん)には無理(むり)がある<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I think there's something unworkable about this proposal.
</pre>

<pre>
この案(あん)には無理(むり)がある<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。
Isn't there something unworkable about this proposal?
</pre>

The content is identical. What differs is who is exposed. The first is a position you can
be argued with about; the second is an observation the room can take up or let drop.
Japanese meetings lean heavily on the second, and learners who use only the first can come
across as more combative than they intend - a tendency, not a rule, but a reliable one.

### ～のではないだろうか vs ～だろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だろう</code> is a **guess** the speaker owns.
<code>のではないだろうか</code> is a **judgement** the speaker offers.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は雨(あめ)になる<b>だろう</b>。
It'll probably rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
判断(はんだん)を急(いそ)ぎすぎた<b>のではないだろうか</b>。
Was the decision not made too hastily?
</pre>

Weather takes `だろう`, because there is nothing to argue about and nobody to persuade.
The second sentence is an argument: it invites the reader to look again at something that
has already happened and to reach the speaker's conclusion. That is why
<code>だろう</code> is at home in forecasts and
<code>のではないだろうか</code> is at home in criticism.

### ～のではないだろうか vs ～のではないか・んじゃない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same pattern at three volumes. With `だろう` it muses; without
`だろう` it presses; contracted to `んじゃない` it is casual speech.

</div>

<pre>
それは話(はなし)が違(ちが)う<b>のではないか</b>。
Surely that is not what was agreed.
</pre>

<pre>
それ、話(はなし)が違(ちが)う<b>んじゃない</b>?
Hang on - that's not what we said, is it?
</pre>

<code>のではないか</code> without `だろう` is noticeably firmer, and in speech it can sound
like a challenge; it is the shape a superior uses when questioning a subordinate's work,
and it turns up in written argument where the author wants an edge. Inserting `だろう` puts
the writer back into the position of thinking aloud, which is usually the safer place to
stand.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この条件(じょうけん)では無理(むり)<b>だのではないだろうか</b>。 → ✅ この条件(じょうけん)では無理(むり)<b>なのではないだろうか</b>。
  `の` is a nominaliser, so what precedes it must be in noun-modifying shape. な-adjectives
  and nouns take `な`, never `だ`.

- ❌ ねえ、この店(みせ)、高(たか)い<b>のではないだろうか</b>。 → ✅ ねえ、この店(みせ)、高(たか)い<b>んじゃない</b>?
  Nothing is wrong with the grammar; it is the wrong rung of the ladder. Written-register
  Japanese aimed at a friend in a shop sounds like a lecture.

- ❌ 今(いま)、3時(じ)なの<b>ではないでしょうか</b>。（with a clock on the wall） → ✅ 今(いま)、3時(じ)です。
  The pattern is for judgements that could be disputed. Hedging a fact anyone can check
  makes the speaker sound oddly unsure of the room.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は来週(らいしゅう)の会(かい)には出(で)ない<b>のではないだろうか</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は来週(らいしゅう)の会(かい)には出(で)ない<b>つもりです</b>。
  Your own intentions are not something you reason your way to. Guess about other people's
  situations, not about your own decisions.

## Practice

**1.** この数字(すうじ)の急(きゅう)な変化(へんか)には、何(なに)か理由(りゆう)がある____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)か理由(りゆう)がある<b>のではないだろうか</b>（or のではないでしょうか）。

The writer has spotted something in the data and is proposing an explanation while leaving
the reader room to weigh it - the pattern's core job. The verb ある attaches in plain form
with no `な`.

</details>

**2.** Choose, speaking to a section head in a meeting: この計画(けいかく)は予算(よさん)が足(た)りない（と思(おも)います・のではないでしょうか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

予算(よさん)が足(た)りない<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。

Both are polite Japanese, but the second raises the concern without setting your judgement
against your section head's. It is the standard way to disagree upward, and it leaves the
decision visibly with them.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in written register: "Is the real problem not that nobody checked the figures?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本当(ほんとう)の問題(もんだい)は、誰(だれ)も数字(すうじ)を確(たし)かめなかったこと<b>なのではないだろうか</b>。

The predicate here is the noun phrase <code>確(たし)かめなかったこと</code>, so it needs
`な` before the `の`. English "is it not that …" is this pattern almost exactly.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この説明(せつめい)は少(すこ)し不十分(ふじゅうぶん)だのではないだろうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この説明(せつめい)は少(すこ)し不十分(ふじゅうぶん)<b>なのではないだろうか</b>。

不十分(ふじゅうぶん) is a な-adjective, and the nominaliser `の` requires the
noun-modifying form. The `だ` cannot modify a noun, and `の` is functioning as one here.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この方法(ほうほう)は効果(こうか)がない<b>かもしれません</b>。
(b) この方法(ほうほう)は効果(こうか)がない<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) genuinely leaves the question open. The speaker is flagging a possibility they have not
made their mind up about, and would be unsurprised to be shown that the method works
perfectly well.

(b) is a view, politely packaged. The speaker thinks the method does not work and is saying
so in a shape that lets the listener push back without anyone losing face. Read as a
neutral "maybe", (b) is routinely underestimated by learners - and by anyone who takes the
question mark at face value.

</details>
