# N2 - Based on, depending on, and in accordance with


---

# ～に基づいて

**Meaning** · on the basis of; grounded in - names the law, data, contract or fact that the action is built on and may not depart from

<code>～に基(もと)づいて</code> is the て-form of <code>基(もと)づく</code>, "to have
as its foundation". What stands in front of it is the **grounds**: a statute, an
agreement, a set of figures, an established fact. What follows is built on those
grounds and answers to them. Because the base is an *authority* rather than raw
material, this is a formal written pattern - regulations, reports, court rulings,
company announcements. It is close to non-existent in relaxed conversation.

## Formation

<code>基(もと)づく</code> is a う-verb (五段(ごだん)/godan/Group 1) and takes its
foundation with `に`, never `を`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に基(もと)づいて | 法律(ほうりつ) → 法律(ほうりつ)に基(もと)づいて |
| Noun (stiffer, documents) | Noun + に基(もと)づき | 規則(きそく) → 規則(きそく)に基(もと)づき |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + に基(もと)づく + Noun | 事実(じじつ) → 事実(じじつ)に基(もと)づく報道(ほうどう) |
| Same, ～た shape | Noun + に基(もと)づいた + Noun | データ → データに基(もと)づいた判断(はんだん) |
| As the predicate | Noun + に基(もと)づいている | 経験(けいけん) → 経験(けいけん)に基(もと)づいている |
| Negative | Noun + に基(もと)づかない | 証拠(しょうこ) → 証拠(しょうこ)に基(もと)づかない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this pattern takes a **noun only**. A clause has to be packaged into a
noun first: 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)に基(もと)づいて,
値上(ねあ)げが必要(ひつよう)だという判断(はんだん)に基(もと)づいて. There is no
form in which a verb attaches directly.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この映画(えいが)は事実(じじつ)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>作(つく)られている。
This film is based on fact.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の規則(きそく)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>決(き)めます。
We will decide it on the basis of company rules.
</pre>

<pre>
アンケートの結果(けっか)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>、メニューを見直(みなお)すことになった。
Acting on the survey results, we have decided to overhaul the menu.
</pre>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)<b>に基(もと)づかない</b>主張(しゅちょう)は、裁判(さいばん)では通(とお)らない。
A claim that is not grounded in evidence will not stand up in court.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)の同意(どうい)<b>に基(もと)づき</b>、個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)を第三者(だいさんしゃ)に提供(ていきょう)することがあります。
On the basis of the individual's consent, personal data may be passed to third parties.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)については、専門家(せんもんか)の調査(ちょうさ)<b>に基(もと)づく</b>報告書(ほうこくしょ)が来月(らいげつ)公表(こうひょう)される。
A report grounded in the specialists' investigation into the cause of the accident will be released next month.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)の経験(けいけん)<b>に基(もと)づいた</b>アドバイスだから、一度(いちど)聞(き)いてみる価値(かち)はあると思(おも)う。
It is advice grounded in years of experience, so I reckon it is worth hearing out.
</pre>

<pre>
この案(あん)は去年(きょねん)の失敗(しっぱい)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>練(ね)り直(なお)したものですので、同(おな)じ問題(もんだい)は起(お)こらないはずです。
This proposal was reworked on the basis of last year's failure, so the same problem should not recur.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and formal, and unusually narrow in what it will accept as a base. A law,
a contract, a regulation, a set of data, a survey, a fact, a principle, somebody's
consent - things that can be *cited*. Your hunch about the weather is not grounds,
and the pattern sounds absurd on it.

<code>基(もと)づき</code> is the 連用形(れんようけい) connective, one notch stiffer again: it
belongs to printed documents, official notices and prepared speech, and it never
ends a sentence. In conversation, native speakers reach for
<code>～をもとに</code>, <code>～を見(み)て</code> or plain `から` instead.

Before a noun, <code>に基(もと)づく</code> and <code>に基(もと)づいた</code> are
both correct and largely interchangeable. <code>に基(もと)づく</code> is the more
compact of the two and is what you will usually see in headings and titles.

There is a rhetorical edge worth knowing. Saying
<code>規則(きそく)に基(もと)づいて処理(しょり)しました</code> is a claim that the
decision was *not* arbitrary - which is exactly why it turns up in apologies and in
defences of unpopular decisions. Turned negative,
<code>事実(じじつ)に基(もと)づかない</code> is an accusation, and a sharp one.

## Contrast

### ～に基(もと)づいて vs ～をもとに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に基(もと)づいて</code> makes the base a **standard you
must not depart from**. <code>をもとに</code> makes it **material you work up into
something new**, and you are free to add, cut and rearrange.

</div>

<pre>
法律(ほうりつ)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>処分(しょぶん)が決(き)まった。
The penalty was determined in accordance with the law.
</pre>

<pre>
取材(しゅざい)のメモ<b>をもとに</b>記事(きじ)を書(か)き上(あ)げた。
I wrote the article up from my interview notes.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences go wrong in an interesting way. Notes you may not
depart from are no longer notes; a law you are free to rework is no longer a law.

The clearest case is film. <code>小説(しょうせつ)をもとに映画(えいが)を作(つく)る</code>
is the ordinary phrasing, because a film cuts and invents freely. Say
<code>事実(じじつ)に基(もと)づく映画(えいが)</code> and you are making a claim about
truth - that the events really happened - not about where the script came from.

### ～に基(もと)づいて vs ～に沿(そ)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に基(もと)づいて</code> names what the action **comes out
of**. <code>に沿(そ)って</code> names a line the action **runs alongside** as it
proceeds - a policy, a plan, an expectation - without straying off it.

</div>

<pre>
統計(とうけい)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>来年度(らいねんど)の方針(ほうしん)を立(た)てた。
We drew up next year's policy on the basis of the statistics.
</pre>

<pre>
その方針(ほうしん)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>各(かく)部署(ぶしょ)が準備(じゅんび)を進(すす)めている。
Each department is preparing in line with that policy.
</pre>

The two sit at different points of one process - figures produce a policy, and the
policy is then run along. That is why the pair so often turns up in consecutive
sentences of the same report.

One collocation settles it in a hurry:
<code>期待(きたい)に沿(そ)う</code> ("live up to expectations") is fixed, and
<code>期待(きたい)に基(もと)づく</code> is not Japanese. Expectations are a line to
follow, not evidence to reason from.

### ～に基(もと)づいて vs ～に従(したが)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に従(したが)って</code> is **obedience** to something that
tells you what to *do*. <code>に基(もと)づいて</code> is **derivation** from
something that tells you what is *so*.

</div>

<pre>
係員(かかりいん)の指示(しじ)<b>に従(したが)って</b>避難(ひなん)してください。
Please evacuate following the instructions of the staff.
</pre>

<pre>
防災(ぼうさい)計画(けいかく)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>訓練(くんれん)が行(おこな)われた。
The drill was carried out on the basis of the disaster-preparedness plan.
</pre>

A law can be both obeyed and cited, so both are possible with it - but they say
different things. <code>法律(ほうりつ)に従(したが)って</code> is compliance in the
moment, the sense of a person doing as they are told.
<code>法律(ほうりつ)に基(もと)づいて</code> points at the authority standing behind
the whole action, and is what an official document will choose.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ アンケート結果(けっか)<b>を基(もと)づいて</b> → ✅ アンケート結果(けっか)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>
  <code>基(もと)づく</code> takes its foundation with `に`. The `を` comes from
  <code>をもとに</code> next door, and mixing the two is the commonest slip here.

- ❌ 話(はな)し合(あ)う<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>決(き)めた。 → ✅ 話(はな)し合(あ)い<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>決(き)めた。
  Only a noun can attach. Nominalise the clause first - here the verb has a ready-made
  noun form, and elsewhere <code>～という判断(はんだん)</code> or
  <code>～の結果(けっか)</code> will do the packaging.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)の指示(しじ)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>並(なら)んでください。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)の指示(しじ)<b>に従(したが)って</b>並(なら)んでください。
  A live instruction is something you obey on the spot. Grounding a queue in the
  teacher's instruction makes lining up sound like a legal act.

- ❌ 天気(てんき)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>傘(かさ)を持(も)ってきた。 → ✅ 天気(てんき)予報(よほう)を見(み)て傘(かさ)を持(も)ってきた。
  Everyday reasoning is not grounds. Reserve this pattern for bases you could
  actually cite to somebody.

## Practice

**1.** 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)____、対策(たいさく)を立(た)てることになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>、対策(たいさく)を立(た)てることになった。

Survey findings are citable grounds, and the countermeasures are derived from them.
Note the particle: the results take `に`, not `を`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 祖母(そぼ)の日記(にっき)（に基(もと)づいて・をもとに）小説(しょうせつ)を書(か)いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

祖母(そぼ)の日記(にっき)<b>をもとに</b>小説(しょうせつ)を書(か)いた。

A novel reworks its source freely, so the diary is material rather than authority.
<code>に基(もと)づいて</code> would promise that the novel departs from the diary
nowhere - a claim a novelist would not want to make.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The decision was made on the basis of the figures, not on somebody's hunch."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

その決定(けってい)は誰(だれ)かの勘(かん)ではなく、数字(すうじ)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>下(くだ)されました。

The contrast between citable grounds and a hunch is exactly the line this pattern
draws, which is why the sentence sounds natural in Japanese. A hunch could never
take <code>に基(もと)づいて</code> itself.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 規則(きそく)を基(もと)づいて注意(ちゅうい)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 規則(きそく)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>注意(ちゅうい)しました。

The verb <code>基(もと)づく</code> is intransitive and marks its foundation with
`に`. Nothing here is being acted on, so `を` has no work to do.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 計画(けいかく)に基(もと)づいて工事(こうじ)を進(すす)める。
(b) 計画(けいかく)に沿(そ)って工事(こうじ)を進(すす)める。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the plan as the authority the work derives from - the schedule, the
budget and the method all come out of that document, and the sentence reads like a
formal report.

(b) presents the plan as a line the work runs alongside as it goes: the emphasis
falls on not drifting off course while the job proceeds. In practice (b) is the
warmer, more ordinary of the two, and it is what a site manager would say.

</details>

# をもとに

**Meaning** · from; on the basis of; taking X as the starting material - X is what you worked *from*, and the result is a new thing

The noun inside is <code>元(もと)</code> / <code>基(もと)</code>, "source,
origin, base". `をもとに` names the **material**: a folk tale, an interview, a set
of figures, a sketch, somebody's advice. Out of that material something new is
built, and the new thing is free to add, cut and rearrange. That freedom is the
whole difference between this pattern and
<code>～に基(もと)づいて</code>, which treats its noun as an authority you may not
depart from.

## Formation

The base is marked with `を`. Almost every sentence ends in a verb of *making* -
作(つく)る, 書(か)く, 描(か)く, 考(かんが)える,
開発(かいはつ)する, 編集(へんしゅう)する.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + をもとに | 昔話(むかしばなし) → 昔話(むかしばなし)をもとに |
| Noun (fuller, more verbal) | Noun + をもとにして | データ → データをもとにして |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + をもとにした + Noun | 実話(じつわ) → 実話(じつわ)をもとにした物語(ものがたり) |
| Nominalised clause | ～という + Noun + をもとに | 噂(うわさ) → 事故(じこ)があったという噂(うわさ)をもとに |
| Written in kanji (optional) | Noun + を基(もと)に / を元(もと)に | 資料(しりょう) → 資料(しりょう)を基(もと)に |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the spelling. <code>基(もと)</code> is the "foundation" kanji and
<code>元(もと)</code> the "origin" one, and writers disagree about which fits here.
Modern practice is simply to write it in kana, which sidesteps the choice. Do that
and you will never be wrong.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この歌(うた)は古(ふる)い昔話(むかしばなし)<b>をもとに</b>作(つく)られた。
This song was made from an old folk tale.
</pre>

<pre>
写真(しゃしん)<b>をもとに</b>絵(え)を描(か)きます。
I paint from photographs.
</pre>

<pre>
生徒(せいと)の意見(いけん)<b>をもとにして</b>、文化祭(ぶんかさい)の企画(きかく)を練(ね)った。
We worked the school-festival plan up out of what the students said.
</pre>

<pre>
実話(じつわ)<b>をもとにした</b>ドラマが来春(らいしゅん)放送(ほうそう)されるそうだ。
Apparently a drama based on a true story will be broadcast next spring.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の売上(うりあげ)データ<b>をもとに</b>、今年(ことし)の仕入(しい)れの量(りょう)を決(き)めた。
We set this year's purchasing volume from last year's sales figures.
</pre>

<pre>
先輩(せんぱい)にもらったアドバイス<b>をもとに</b>履歴書(りれきしょ)を書(か)き直(なお)したら、面接(めんせつ)に呼(よ)ばれるようになった。
Once I rewrote my CV on the strength of a senior colleague's advice, I started getting called to interviews.
</pre>

<pre>
聞(き)き取(と)り調査(ちょうさ)で集(あつ)めた証言(しょうげん)<b>をもとに</b>、当時(とうじ)の村(むら)の様子(ようす)を再現(さいげん)した地図(ちず)が作(つく)られた。
From testimony gathered in interviews, a map was drawn up recreating the village as it stood at the time.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は明治(めいじ)時代(じだい)の原稿(げんこう)<b>をもとに</b>、三十年(さんじゅうねん)かけて編集(へんしゅう)されたものです。
This dictionary was compiled over thirty years from a Meiji-era manuscript.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and written, but a notch friendlier than
<code>に基(もと)づいて</code>. It is entirely at home in a news report, a
presentation, a school newsletter or a piece of careful speech; what it is not is
chatty. In relaxed conversation people say
<code>～を見(み)て</code> or <code>～から考(かんが)えて</code> and leave `もとに`
for when they are being deliberate.

The three shapes divide up cleanly. `をもとに` is the default. `をもとにして` is the
same thing with the verb spelled out; it feels a touch more explicit about the
process and sits comfortably at the head of a long sentence. `をもとにした` is the
only one that can attach to a following noun - there is no
<code>実話(じつわ)をもとに映画(えいが)</code> without the `した`.

The base has to be **information or stuff**: a text, a recording, data, a memory,
an object. A person cannot be the base of this pattern. What you worked from is
their advice, their notes or their testimony, and Japanese makes you say so.

## Contrast

### をもとに vs ～に基(もと)づいて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `をもとに` = **material**, and you may transform it.
<code>に基(もと)づいて</code> = **authority**, and you may not depart from it.

</div>

<pre>
漫画(まんが)<b>をもとに</b>脚本(きゃくほん)を書(か)いた。
I wrote the screenplay from the manga.
</pre>

<pre>
契約書(けいやくしょ)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>支払(しはら)いが行(おこな)われる。
Payment is made in accordance with the contract.
</pre>

A screenplay that could not depart from the manga would be a transcription, and a
payment that treated the contract as a rough draft would be a breach. Each pattern
is doing the job the other cannot.

Both do appear with creative work, and there the choice is a claim about content.
<code>小説(しょうせつ)をもとにした映画(えいが)</code> says where the story came
from. <code>事実(じじつ)に基(もと)づいた映画(えいが)</code> says the events
really happened. A studio that wants both will say both, in that order.

### をもとに vs を参考(さんこう)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `をもとに`: X supplies the **substance** of the result.
<code>を参考(さんこう)に</code>: X was merely **consulted**, and the result may owe
it very little.

</div>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の資料(しりょう)<b>をもとに</b>今年(ことし)の案(あん)を作(つく)った。
I built this year's proposal out of last year's documents.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の資料(しりょう)を<b>参考(さんこう)に</b>今年(ことし)の案(あん)を作(つく)った。
I put this year's proposal together with last year's documents for reference.
</pre>

The first admits that most of the content is recycled. The second says you had a
look. That makes <code>を参考(さんこう)に</code> the modest option, and it is
routinely chosen for exactly that reason - it is also what you say when you do not
want to claim someone else's work as your source.

### をもとに vs ～によって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `によって` names the **means** something was done by, or the
**agent** who did it. `をもとに` names the **source material** it was made out of.

</div>

<pre>
この像(ぞう)は有名(ゆうめい)な彫刻家(ちょうこくか)<b>によって</b>作(つく)られた。
This statue was made by a famous sculptor.
</pre>

<pre>
この像(ぞう)は古(ふる)い写真(しゃしん)数枚(すうまい)<b>をもとに</b>作(つく)られた。
This statue was made from a handful of old photographs.
</pre>

Both sentences end in <code>作(つく)られた</code>, and English hides the
difference under one word, "by". `によって` has four jobs - cause, means, passive
agent, and the thing a result varies with - and *source material* is not among
them. That gap is precisely what `をもとに` fills, which is why the two so often
appear in the same sentence without competing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 事実(じじつ)<b>にもとに</b>した話(はなし) → ✅ 事実(じじつ)<b>をもとに</b>した話(はなし)
  The base takes `を`. The `に` leaks in from <code>に基(もと)づいて</code>, and the
  two patterns swap particles in learners' heads constantly.

- ❌ 実話(じつわ)<b>をもとに</b>映画(えいが)を見(み)た。 → ✅ 実話(じつわ)<b>をもとにした</b>映画(えいが)を見(み)た。
  To modify a noun you need the `した`. Without it the phrase tries to attach to the
  verb at the end, and the sentence says you watched the film *using a true story*.

- ❌ 聞(き)いた<b>をもとに</b>記事(きじ)を書(か)いた。 → ✅ 聞(き)いた話(はなし)<b>をもとに</b>記事(きじ)を書(か)いた。
  A noun has to be there. A bare verb cannot attach, so supply the noun the clause
  was describing - 話(はなし), 内容(ないよう), 証言(しょうげん).

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)<b>をもとに</b>作文(さくぶん)を直(なお)した。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)のアドバイス<b>をもとに</b>作文(さくぶん)を直(なお)した。
  People are not material. Name the thing they gave you - the advice, the comments,
  the corrections - and the sentence works.

## Practice

**1.** 町(まち)に伝(つた)わる昔話(むかしばなし)____、新(あたら)しい絵本(えほん)を作(つく)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

昔話(むかしばなし)<b>をもとに</b>、新(あたら)しい絵本(えほん)を作(つく)った。

A folk tale is material, and the picture book is a new thing built out of it - the
retelling is expected to change things. Note the particle `を`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 校則(こうそく)（をもとに・に基(もと)づいて）処分(しょぶん)が決(き)まった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

校則(こうそく)<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>処分(しょぶん)が決(き)まった。

School rules are an authority the decision has to answer to, not raw material a
punishment is fashioned from. `をもとに` would suggest the school treated its own
rules as a rough draft.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This play was written from a diary a soldier left behind."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この劇(げき)は、兵士(へいし)が残(のこ)した日記(にっき)<b>をもとに</b>書(か)かれました。

The diary is the source the playwright worked from, and a play necessarily invents
around it - textbook `をもとに`. The whole relative clause counts as a noun, so it
attaches with no extra machinery.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 実話(じつわ)をもとに映画(えいが)が話題(わだい)になっている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 実話(じつわ)<b>をもとにした</b>映画(えいが)が話題(わだい)になっている。

`をもとに` is adverbial - it modifies a verb. Here it is meant to modify the noun
<code>映画(えいが)</code>, and that requires the `した` form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 先輩(せんぱい)の意見(いけん)をもとに企画(きかく)を立(た)てた。
(b) 先輩(せんぱい)の意見(いけん)を参考(さんこう)に企画(きかく)を立(た)てた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the senior colleague's opinion is what the plan is made of - the shape of
the proposal comes from them, and you are giving them credit for it.

(b) says you took their opinion into account. The plan is yours; theirs was one
input among several. This is the more modest phrasing, and the one you would use if
you did not in fact follow much of their advice.

</details>

# ～に沿って

**Meaning** · along; in line with; in keeping with - runs the length of a line and does not leave it, whether the line is a river or a plan

<code>沿(そ)う</code> is "to run along the side of something". Physically that is a
road beside a river. Figuratively it is an action that keeps to a line already laid
down - a plan, a policy, an agenda, a theme, somebody's wishes - from beginning to
end, without wandering off. The line exists first; the action tracks it.

## Formation

<code>沿(そ)う</code> is intransitive, so the line it follows is marked with `に`.
The pattern takes a **noun** and nothing else.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に沿(そ)って | 川(かわ) → 川(かわ)に沿(そ)って |
| Noun (stiffer, written) | Noun + に沿(そ)い | 方針(ほうしん) → 方針(ほうしん)に沿(そ)い |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + に沿(そ)った + Noun | 希望(きぼう) → 希望(きぼう)に沿(そ)った提案(ていあん) |
| Same, non-past shape | Noun + に沿(そ)う + Noun | 期待(きたい) → 期待(きたい)に沿(そ)う結果(けっか) |
| Negative (business set phrase) | Noun + に沿(そ)えず | ご希望(きぼう) → ご希望(きぼう)に沿(そ)えず |
| As a noun suffix | Noun + 沿(ぞ)い + の + Noun | 線路(せんろ) → 線路沿(せんろぞ)いの家(いえ) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the suffix in the last row voices: 川沿(かわぞ)い,
海沿(うみぞ)い, 線路沿(せんろぞ)い - **ぞ**い, never そい. That is ordinary
rendaku after a noun, and it is a reading learners get wrong for years because the
kanji does not change.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
川(かわ)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>歩(ある)きましょう。
Let's walk along the river.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>組(く)み立(た)てました。
I assembled it following the instruction booklet.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>桜(さくら)の木(き)が並(なら)んでいる。
Cherry trees stand in a row along the road.
</pre>

<pre>
ご希望(きぼう)<b>に沿(そ)えず</b>、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません。
We are sorry that we could not meet your wishes.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は事前(じぜん)に配(くば)られた進行表(しんこうひょう)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>進(すす)められた。
The meeting ran in line with the agenda circulated beforehand.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の展示(てんじ)は、「水(みず)と暮(く)らし」というテーマ<b>に沿(そ)った</b>作品(さくひん)だけを集(あつ)めています。
This exhibition gathers only works in keeping with the theme "Water and Daily Life".
</pre>

<pre>
政府(せいふ)の方針(ほうしん)<b>に沿(そ)い</b>、来年度(らいねんど)から補助金(ほじょきん)の制度(せいど)が見直(みなお)されることになった。
In line with government policy, the subsidy scheme is to be revised from next year.
</pre>

<pre>
マニュアル<b>に沿(そ)って</b>対応(たいおう)するのは大事(だいじ)ですが、それだけではお客(きゃく)さまの気持(きも)ちまでは届(とど)きません。
Handling things by the manual matters, but on its own it does not reach how the customer feels.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Two registers in one pattern. The **physical** sense -
<code>川(かわ)に沿(そ)って</code>, <code>道(みち)に沿(そ)って</code> - is
completely neutral and turns up in ordinary speech, in directions, in weather
reports. The **figurative** sense - a plan, a policy, an agenda - is formal and
mostly written; in casual talk you would say
<code>決(き)めたとおりに</code> or <code>そのままやる</code> instead.

<code>に沿(そ)い</code> is the stiff connective form, at home in notices and
prepared statements and nowhere else.

Learn the business boilerplate as a block. <code>ご期待(きたい)に沿(そ)えるよう努力(どりょく)いたします</code>
is what a company says when it accepts your custom, and
<code>ご希望(きぼう)に沿(そ)いかねます</code> or
<code>ご期待(きたい)に沿(そ)えず申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません</code> is what a
rejection letter says. The second one is worth recognising on sight: it is the
polite Japanese for "no".

One spelling footnote. In the "meet someone's wishes" sense you will also see the
kanji <code>添(そ)う</code>, and both spellings are current;
<code>沿(そ)う</code> is the commoner modern choice and the safe one to write.

## Contrast

### ～に沿(そ)って vs ～に従(したが)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に従(したが)って</code> is **obeying** a source of
instruction. <code>に沿(そ)って</code> is **keeping to the shape** of something that
lays out the whole course.

</div>

<pre>
案内(あんない)の矢印(やじるし)<b>に従(したが)って</b>進(すす)んでください。
Please proceed following the arrows.
</pre>

<pre>
海岸(かいがん)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>細(ほそ)い道(みち)が続(つづ)いている。
A narrow road runs on along the coast.
</pre>

The physical use separates them cleanly: only
<code>に沿(そ)って</code> can mean "physically alongside", because only
<code>沿(そ)う</code> is about parallel lines.

Where they overlap - a plan can be both obeyed and kept to -
<code>に従(したが)って</code> casts the plan as an instruction you comply with,
step by step, while <code>に沿(そ)って</code> casts it as an outline you stay
inside. <code>に従(したが)って</code> also has a second life meaning "as X
progresses", which <code>に沿(そ)って</code> never has.

### ～に沿(そ)って vs ～に基(もと)づいて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に基(もと)づいて</code> says where the thing **came
from**. <code>に沿(そ)って</code> says what line it **keeps to while it runs**.

</div>

<pre>
実験(じっけん)データ<b>に基(もと)づいて</b>仮説(かせつ)を立(た)てた。
We formed the hypothesis on the basis of the experimental data.
</pre>

<pre>
学会(がっかい)の規定(きてい)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>論文(ろんぶん)の形式(けいしき)を整(ととの)えた。
I put the paper into shape in line with the society's formatting rules.
</pre>

The quickest test is <code>期待(きたい)</code> and <code>希望(きぼう)</code>.
<code>期待(きたい)に沿(そ)う</code> is fixed and extremely common;
<code>期待(きたい)に基(もと)づく</code> is not Japanese. A wish is a line you try
to keep to, never evidence you can reason from.

### ～に沿(そ)って vs ～に応(おう)じて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に沿(そ)って</code> follows **one fixed line**.
<code>に応(おう)じて</code> means the result **changes as the other thing changes**.

</div>

<pre>
予算(よさん)案(あん)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>内容(ないよう)を決(き)めます。
We will settle the contents in line with the draft budget.
</pre>

<pre>
予算(よさん)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>内容(ないよう)を変(か)えます。
We adjust the contents according to the budget.
</pre>

The first has one budget document and one outcome that stays inside it. The second
has a range of budgets and a matching range of outcomes - big budget, big plan;
small budget, small plan. If you can add "the bigger the X, the bigger the Y", you
want <code>に応(おう)じて</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 川(かわ)<b>を沿(そ)って</b>走(はし)る → ✅ 川(かわ)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>走(はし)る
  <code>沿(そ)う</code> is intransitive. The `を` comes from
  <code>川(かわ)を渡(わた)る</code>-type verbs, which cross the thing rather than
  run beside it.

- ❌ 決(き)めた<b>に沿(そ)って</b>進(すす)めよう。 → ✅ 決定(けってい)事項(じこう)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>進(すす)めよう。
  A noun is required. A bare verb cannot attach, so either supply the noun or switch
  to <code>決(き)めたとおりに</code>, which does take a clause.

- ❌ ご期待(きたい)<b>に基(もと)づく</b>結果(けっか)を出(だ)したい。 → ✅ ご期待(きたい)<b>に沿(そ)う</b>結果(けっか)を出(だ)したい。
  Expectations are a line to keep to, not grounds to argue from. This collocation is
  fixed and there is no leeway in it.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)帯(たい)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>料金(りょうきん)が変(か)わります。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)帯(たい)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>料金(りょうきん)が変(か)わります。
  Nothing varies inside <code>に沿(そ)って</code>. As soon as the sentence says one
  thing changes with another, you need <code>に応(おう)じて</code>.

# ～に応じて

**Meaning** · depending on; according to; to suit - the second thing is set to **match** the first, and changes when the first changes

<code>応(おう)じる</code> is "to respond, to suit, to comply". What
<code>～に応(おう)じて</code> marks is a **variable**: age, income, budget, the
number of people, the season, the situation. Whatever follows is adjusted to fit
that variable case by case. Change the input and you change the output - that
covariation is the whole pattern, and a noun with no range of values in it will not
take it.

## Formation

The variable is marked with `に`. This pattern takes a **noun only** - it has no
form that attaches to a verb, which is the fastest way to tell it apart from
<code>～につれて</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に応(おう)じて | 年齢(ねんれい) → 年齢(ねんれい)に応(おう)じて |
| Noun (stiffer, written) | Noun + に応(おう)じ | 状況(じょうきょう) → 状況(じょうきょう)に応(おう)じ |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + に応(おう)じた + Noun | 能力(のうりょく) → 能力(のうりょく)に応(おう)じた仕事(しごと) |
| Fixed adverb | 必要(ひつよう)に応(おう)じて | - → 必要(ひつよう)に応(おう)じて追加(ついか)する |
| The bare verb (a separate use) | Noun + に応(おう)じる | 要求(ようきゅう) → 要求(ようきゅう)に応(おう)じる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the last row is not this pattern. On its own,
<code>応(おう)じる</code> means "agree to, accede to":
<code>取材(しゅざい)に応(おう)じる</code> is to consent to be interviewed,
<code>要求(ようきゅう)に応(おう)じる</code> is to give in to a demand. That second
life is why <code>に応(おう)じて</code> sometimes shades into "in compliance with"
rather than "varying with".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
季節(きせつ)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>メニューが変(か)わります。
The menu changes with the season.
</pre>

<pre>
人数(にんずう)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>料理(りょうり)の量(りょう)を決(き)めます。
We decide how much food to cook according to the number of people.
</pre>

<pre>
お客(きゃく)さまのご予算(よさん)<b>に応(おう)じた</b>プランをご用意(ようい)しております。
We have plans available to suit your budget.
</pre>

<pre>
会員(かいいん)は利用(りよう)回数(かいすう)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>ポイントがもらえる。
Members earn points in proportion to how often they use it.
</pre>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)の事情(じじょう)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>、ごみの分(わ)け方(かた)は市町村(しちょうそん)ごとに決(き)められている。
The way rubbish is sorted is decided municipality by municipality to suit local circumstances.
</pre>

<pre>
この奨学金(しょうがくきん)は、家庭(かてい)の収入(しゅうにゅう)<b>に応(おう)じ</b>、三(さん)段階(だんかい)に分(わ)けて支給(しきゅう)される。
This scholarship is paid at three levels according to household income.
</pre>

<pre>
部下(ぶか)には経験(けいけん)<b>に応(おう)じた</b>仕事(しごと)を任(まか)せるようにしている。
I make a point of handing my staff work that matches their experience.
</pre>

<pre>
状況(じょうきょう)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>判断(はんだん)してくださいと言(い)われても、入(はい)ったばかりの社員(しゃいん)にはどこまでやっていいのか分(わ)かりません。
Being told to use your judgement as the situation requires is not much help to an employee who has only just joined and has no idea how far they may go.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal-leaning, but far more everyday than
<code>に基(もと)づいて</code>. You meet it on menus, price lists, job adverts,
insurance documents and council notices, and it is perfectly natural in careful
speech - a shop assistant will say
<code>ご予算(よさん)に応(おう)じて</code> without sounding stiff. What it is not
is chatty; among friends you would just say
<code>人(ひと)によって変(か)える</code>.

<code>に応(おう)じ</code> is the connective form, and it belongs to documents.
<code>に応(おう)じた</code> is the form that modifies a following noun.

Three collocations are worth learning whole:
<code>必要(ひつよう)に応(おう)じて</code> ("as required", "as necessary") has
hardened into a plain adverb and is one of the most common phrases in Japanese
officialese; <code>状況(じょうきょう)に応(おう)じて</code> is "as the situation
demands"; <code>場合(ばあい)に応(おう)じて</code> is "case by case".

There is a soft register point worth flagging.
<code>状況(じょうきょう)に応(おう)じて判断(はんだん)してください</code> is a
genuinely common workplace instruction, and it tends to devolve the risk along with
the decision. Hearing it, a Japanese employee will often ask for the boundaries
before acting.

## Contrast

### ～に応(おう)じて vs ～によって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the "varies with" sense the two overlap heavily. `によって`
reports the **bare fact** that things differ. <code>に応(おう)じて</code> presents
the difference as a **deliberate adjustment made to fit**.

</div>

<pre>
習慣(しゅうかん)は国(くに)<b>によって</b>違(ちが)う。
Customs differ from country to country.
</pre>

<pre>
国(くに)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>説明(せつめい)の仕方(しかた)を変(か)えている。
We vary the way we explain it to suit the country.
</pre>

Ask whether anybody is doing the adjusting. If the variation simply exists in the
world, `によって` is the word, and with
<code>違(ちが)う</code> or <code>異(こと)なる</code> it is effectively fixed. If
somebody is tailoring something to fit, <code>に応(おう)じて</code> is available and
sounds more considerate.

Remember also how much `によって` does that this pattern cannot: cause
(<code>事故(じこ)によって</code>), means
(<code>話(はな)し合(あ)いによって</code>) and the agent of a passive
(<code>彼(かれ)によって書(か)かれた</code>). <code>に応(おう)じて</code> has
exactly the one job.

### ～に応(おう)じて vs ～に応(こた)えて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same kanji, two verbs.
<code>に応(おう)じて</code> **matches** a variable.
<code>に応(こた)えて</code> **answers** a request, a demand or an expectation aimed
at you.

</div>

<pre>
注文(ちゅうもん)の数(かず)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>割引(わりびき)いたします。
We give a discount according to the size of the order.
</pre>

<pre>
地元(じもと)の要望(ようぼう)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>終電(しゅうでん)が遅(おそ)くなった。
In response to requests from local people, the last train now runs later.
</pre>

The memory hook: <code>応(こた)える</code> answers somebody;
<code>応(おう)じる</code> matches something. With a request, both readings are
possible and they say different things -
<code>要望(ようぼう)に応(こた)えて</code> is living up to what people hoped for,
while <code>要望(ようぼう)に応(おう)じて</code> is simply acceding to it, with no
warmth attached.

### ～に応(おう)じて vs ～につれて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `につれて` is a **gradual change over time** that nobody
controls. <code>に応(おう)じて</code> is a value **set to match**, case by case,
usually by somebody.

</div>

<pre>
年(とし)をとる<b>につれて</b>、朝(あさ)早(はや)く目(め)が覚(さ)めるようになった。
As I get older, I have started waking up early.
</pre>

<pre>
年齢(ねんれい)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>保険料(ほけんりょう)が決(き)まる。
The premium is set according to age.
</pre>

The formal difference settles most cases: `につれて` takes a **verb clause** and
<code>に応(おう)じて</code> takes a **noun**. <code>年(とし)をとるにつれて</code>
is a process unfolding; <code>年齢(ねんれい)に応(おう)じて</code> is a table of
ages and matching prices, with no unfolding in it at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 人数(にんずう)が増(ふ)える<b>に応(おう)じて</b>席(せき)を足(た)す。 → ✅ 人数(にんずう)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>席(せき)を足(た)す。
  A verb cannot attach. Either reduce the clause to its noun, or switch to
  <code>増(ふ)えるにつれて</code>, which does take verbs.

- ❌ この計画(けいかく)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>工事(こうじ)を進(すす)める。 → ✅ この計画(けいかく)<b>に沿(そ)って</b>工事(こうじ)を進(すす)める。
  One fixed plan has no range of values, so there is nothing for the work to vary
  with. A single document is a line to follow.

- ❌ ファンの期待(きたい)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>優勝(ゆうしょう)した。 → ✅ ファンの期待(きたい)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>優勝(ゆうしょう)した。
  Expectations are answered, not matched. Winning is not something you scaled to the
  size of the hope.

## Practice

**1.** 店(みせ)の飾(かざ)りつけは、季節(きせつ)____変(か)えるようにしている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

季節(きせつ)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>変(か)えるようにしている。

The season is a variable with four settings, and the decorations are deliberately
matched to it. Somebody is doing the adjusting, which is what tips this away from
`によって`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: ファンの声(こえ)（に応(おう)じて・に応(こた)えて）再(さい)公演(こうえん)が決(き)まった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ファンの声(こえ)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>再(さい)公演(こうえん)が決(き)まった。

Fans asked, and the company answered them. Nothing is being scaled here - there is
one extra run of the show, not an amount of show proportional to the noise.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The charge varies according to how many hours you use the room."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

料金(りょうきん)は部屋(へや)を使(つか)う時間(じかん)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>変(か)わります。

Hours are a scale and the charge tracks it, so this is the core use. Notice that the
verb clause <code>部屋(へや)を使(つか)う</code> is safely inside the noun phrase -
it modifies <code>時間(じかん)</code>, and it is the noun that attaches.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 習慣(しゅうかん)は地域(ちいき)に応(おう)じて違(ちが)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 習慣(しゅうかん)は地域(ちいき)<b>によって</b>違(ちが)います。

Nobody adjusts local customs to fit the region - they simply differ, and the bare
fact of variation is `によって`'s job. With
<code>違(ちが)う</code> and <code>異(こと)なる</code> the pairing is fixed.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) お客(きゃく)さまのご要望(ようぼう)に応(おう)じて変更(へんこう)いたします。
(b) お客(きゃく)さまのご要望(ようぼう)に応(こた)えて変更(へんこう)いたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is procedural: if you ask, we will change it, and what we change will be shaped
to what you asked for. It describes a service that exists, and it is what a menu of
options would say.

(b) is warmer and looks backwards: customers asked for this, and we are living up to
that. It frames the change as a response to people rather than as a setting on a
form, which is why it turns up in announcements rather than in terms and conditions.

</details>

# ～に応えて

**Meaning** · in response to; living up to - answers a request, a hope or a demand that other people directed at you, and satisfies it

<code>応(こた)える</code> is "to answer, to live up to". The noun in front of
<code>～に応(こた)えて</code> is something aimed at you from outside:
期待(きたい), 要望(ようぼう), 声(こえ), 要求(ようきゅう),
声援(せいえん), ニーズ. What follows is your answer to it - and, importantly,
an answer that **delivers**. The pattern carries success in it, which is why the
negative has to be spelled out separately.

## Formation

Take a **noun only**, and one that names a call of some kind. `に` marks it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に応(こた)えて | 期待(きたい) → 期待(きたい)に応(こた)えて |
| Noun (stiffer, written) | Noun + に応(こた)え | 要請(ようせい) → 要請(ようせい)に応(こた)え |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + に応(こた)える + Noun | 期待(きたい) → 期待(きたい)に応(こた)える活躍(かつやく) |
| Negative | Noun + に応(こた)えられず | 信頼(しんらい) → 信頼(しんらい)に応(こた)えられず |
| As the plain predicate | Noun + に応(こた)える | 要望(ようぼう) → 要望(ようぼう)に応(こた)える |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same verb has an unrelated second life meaning "to take a toll":
寒(さむ)さが身(み)に応(こた)える, "the cold really gets to you". Nothing about
requests there; if the subject is cold, tiredness or age, it is this other sense.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
みんなの期待(きたい)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>、彼(かれ)は一位(いちい)になった。
Living up to everyone's hopes, he came first.
</pre>

<pre>
お客(きゃく)さんの声(こえ)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>、営業(えいぎょう)時間(じかん)を延(の)ばしました。
In response to what customers told us, we extended our opening hours.
</pre>

<pre>
市民(しみん)の要望(ようぼう)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>、駅前(えきまえ)に新(あたら)しい図書館(としょかん)が建(た)てられた。
In response to residents' requests, a new library was built by the station.
</pre>

<pre>
応援(おうえん)してくれた人(ひと)たちの気持(きも)ち<b>に応(こた)えたい</b>。
I want to live up to the feelings of the people who cheered me on.
</pre>

<pre>
農家(のうか)の要請(ようせい)<b>に応(こた)え</b>、県(けん)は補助(ほじょ)の拡大(かくだい)を決(き)めた。
Answering appeals from farmers, the prefecture decided to widen the subsidy.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)からの拍手(はくしゅ)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>、歌手(かしゅ)はもう一曲(いっきょく)歌(うた)ってくれた。
Answering the applause from the hall, the singer gave us one more song.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)の期待(きたい)<b>に応(こた)えられず</b>、進路(しんろ)を変(か)えたことを今(いま)でも少(すこ)し後(うし)ろめたく思(おも)っている。
I still feel a little guilty about changing direction and failing to live up to what my parents hoped for.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)通(かよ)ってくださった常連(じょうれん)さんの信頼(しんらい)<b>に応(こた)える</b>ためにも、値上(ねあ)げは最小限(さいしょうげん)に抑(おさ)えたいと考(かんが)えております。
Precisely in order to live up to the trust of the regulars who have come for years, we intend to keep the price rise to a minimum.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal, written and public-facing. It belongs to announcements, press releases,
speeches, sports reporting and interviews, and it presupposes that the call being
answered is **weighty** - a public demand, a body of expectation, a crowd's support.
Using it for a small favour is comic: nobody answers
<code>お茶(ちゃ)を入(い)れてという要望(ようぼう)</code>. Say
<code>言(い)われたとおりにした</code> for that.

The negative is fixed and worth memorising:
<code>ご期待(きたい)に応(こた)えられず申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません</code> -
"I'm sorry to have let you down." It is the standard apology of a losing team, a
failed candidate and a company withdrawing a product.

Two spelling traps sit right next to each other, and both are read こたえる.
<code>答(こた)える</code> answers a **question**:
<code>質問(しつもん)に答(こた)える</code>. <code>応(こた)える</code> lives up to a
**hope or demand**: <code>期待(きたい)に応(こた)える</code>. And then the same
kanji read おうじる gives you <code>応(おう)じる</code>, which matches a variable.
One kanji, two readings, three jobs; keep them apart by asking what the noun in front
is - a question, a hope, or a scale.

## Contrast

### ～に応(こた)えて vs ～に応(おう)じて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に応(こた)えて</code> **answers people**.
<code>に応(おう)じて</code> **matches a variable**, or accedes to a demand with no
warmth attached.

</div>

<pre>
読者(どくしゃ)の要望(ようぼう)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>、続編(ぞくへん)が出(で)ることになった。
In response to readers' requests, a sequel is now to be published.
</pre>

<pre>
注文(ちゅうもん)の内容(ないよう)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>包装(ほうそう)を変(か)えます。
We vary the wrapping to suit what has been ordered.
</pre>

Two tests. If "living up to" fits the English, the reading is こたえて. If "for each,
according to each" fits, it is おうじて. Only
<code>応(こた)える</code> takes <code>期待(きたい)</code> in the sense of
fulfilling it - <code>期待(きたい)に応(おう)じて</code> would mean adjusting
something to the size of the hope, which is not a thing people say.

### ～に応(こた)えて vs ～を受(う)けて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を受(う)けて</code> reports a **trigger**: X happened, and
this followed. <code>に応(こた)えて</code> says X was a **call aimed at you** and
you satisfied it.

</div>

<pre>
事故(じこ)<b>を受(う)けて</b>、会社(かいしゃ)は安全(あんぜん)対策(たいさく)を見直(みなお)した。
Following the accident, the company reviewed its safety measures.
</pre>

<pre>
住民(じゅうみん)の訴(うった)え<b>に応(こた)えて</b>、会社(かいしゃ)は点検(てんけん)の回数(かいすう)を増(ふ)やした。
Answering complaints from residents, the company increased how often it inspected.
</pre>

An accident asks for nothing, so it can never take
<code>に応(こた)えて</code>. Where the trigger genuinely is a request, both are
available and journalists choose between them on purpose:
<code>を受(う)けて</code> is cool and merely sequential, while
<code>に応(こた)えて</code> credits the company with wanting to satisfy people.

### ～に応(こた)えて vs ～に対(たい)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に対(たい)して</code> aims an action **at** something and
says nothing about the outcome. <code>に応(こた)えて</code> says the demand **got
what it wanted**.

</div>

<pre>
批判(ひはん)<b>に対(たい)して</b>、市長(しちょう)は何(なに)も答(こた)えなかった。
In the face of the criticism, the mayor said nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
批判(ひはん)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>、市長(しちょう)は計画(けいかく)を見直(みなお)した。
Responding to the criticism, the mayor reviewed the plan.
</pre>

The first sentence is perfectly compatible with refusal; the second is not.
Success is built into <code>に応(こた)えて</code>, which is why
<code>期待(きたい)に応(こた)えて負(ま)けた</code> is a contradiction and the
negative form exists to carry the other half of the meaning.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ メールの質問(しつもん)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>おきました。 → ✅ メールの質問(しつもん)<b>に答(こた)えて</b>おきました。
  A question is answered with <code>答(こた)える</code>. Same reading, different
  kanji, and the wrong one turns a reply into a heroic act of living up to something.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>営業(えいぎょう)を中止(ちゅうし)した。 → ✅ 台風(たいふう)<b>を受(う)けて</b>営業(えいぎょう)を中止(ちゅうし)した。
  A typhoon is a trigger, not a request. Only things that ask for something can be
  answered.

- ❌ 期待(きたい)<b>に応(こた)えて</b>失敗(しっぱい)してしまった。 → ✅ 期待(きたい)<b>に応(こた)えられず</b>、失敗(しっぱい)してしまった。
  The pattern entails that the hope was met, so it cannot head a sentence about
  falling short. Use the potential negative instead.

## Practice

**1.** 社員(しゃいん)の声(こえ)____、在宅(ざいたく)勤務(きんむ)が認(みと)められることになった。

Staff asked for something and management delivered it, which is exactly what this
pattern reports. Note the reading - こたえて, not おうじて, because people are being
answered rather than a scale being matched.

</details>

Build is a scale, and the height is set to match it - pure covariation. A body is

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Trust is something placed in you from outside, so it belongs to the same family as

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is neutral reporting: the opposition happened, and the cancellation followed. It
leaves open that the developer gave in reluctantly, and it is what a newspaper would
write. Journalists pick between these two deliberately.

</details>

# ～次第だ / ～次第で

**Meaning** · it depends on; it is decided by - the outcome hangs on the noun in front, and until that noun is settled the outcome is not

The noun <code>次第(しだい)</code> is roughly "how things stand, what it comes down
to". Stuck straight onto another noun, it hands that noun **control of the
outcome**: <code>天気(てんき)次第(しだい)だ</code> means the weather decides.
This is not the neutral "varies with" of `によって`, and it is not the tailoring of
<code>に応(おう)じて</code> - it is a result that is genuinely in the balance and
waiting on one deciding factor.

A second, quite separate use closes formal letters:
<code>～次第(しだい)です</code> meaning "that is how it comes to be". And a third
thing that merely looks alike, <code>V-ます次第(しだい)</code>, means "as soon as".

## Formation

The deciding noun attaches **bare** - no `の`, no `な`, no particle at all.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + 次第(しだい)だ | 努力(どりょく) → 努力(どりょく)次第(しだい)だ |
| Noun | Noun + 次第(しだい)で | 天気(てんき) → 天気(てんき)次第(しだい)で |
| Noun (singling out one case) | Noun + 次第(しだい)では | 返事(へんじ) → 返事(へんじ)次第(しだい)では |
| Polite | Noun + 次第(しだい)です | 相手(あいて) → 相手(あいて)次第(しだい)です |
| "that is how it came about" | Clause (plain) + 次第(しだい)です | ご連絡(れんらく)申(もう)し上(あ)げた → ご連絡(れんらく)申(もう)し上(あ)げた次第(しだい)です |
| "as soon as" (a different pattern) | V-ます stem + 次第(しだい) | 決(き)まる → 決(き)まり次第(しだい) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>次第(しだい)に</code> is a fourth item and an unrelated one: it is
an adverb meaning "gradually" (次第(しだい)に暗(くら)くなってきた). It is not the
`に` form of anything on this page.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)行(い)くかどうかは天気(てんき)<b>次第(しだい)だ</b>。
Whether we go tomorrow depends on the weather.
</pre>

<pre>
どうするかは、あなた<b>次第(しだい)です</b>。
What to do is up to you.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)<b>次第(しだい)では</b>、中古(ちゅうこ)のものでもかまいません。
Depending on the price, I don't mind it being second-hand.
</pre>

<pre>
結果(けっか)は本人(ほんにん)の努力(どりょく)<b>次第(しだい)で</b>大(おお)きく変(か)わってくる。
The outcome shifts a great deal depending on how hard the person works.
</pre>

<pre>
使(つか)い方(かた)<b>次第(しだい)で</b>、この道具(どうぐ)は便利(べんり)にも危険(きけん)にもなる。
Depending on how it is used, this tool can be either handy or dangerous.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ言葉(ことば)でも、言(い)い方(かた)<b>次第(しだい)で</b>相手(あいて)の受(う)け取(と)り方(かた)はまったく違(ちが)ってくる。
Even with the same words, how the other person takes them changes completely depending on how you say them.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)留学(りゅうがく)できるかどうかは、奨学金(しょうがくきん)の審査(しんさ)の結果(けっか)<b>次第(しだい)</b>です。
Whether I can study abroad next year depends on the outcome of the scholarship screening.
</pre>

<pre>
以上(いじょう)のような事情(じじょう)がございまして、急(きゅう)きょご連絡(れんらく)申(もう)し上(あ)げた<b>次第(しだい)です</b>。
Such being the circumstances, that is why I am writing to you at short notice.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The "depends on" use is the friendliest thing in this whole family. It attaches to
a bare noun, it is short, and it is entirely at home in speech:
<code>きみ次第(しだい)だよ</code>, <code>やり方(かた)次第(しだい)だね</code>.
Alongside the stiff <code>に基(もと)づいて</code> and
<code>に応(おう)じて</code>, this is the one you can actually say to a friend.

<code>次第(しだい)では</code> narrows to a single branch: "if the price turns out
right", "if his reply is the right one". The `は` does what it always does and marks
one case off against the others, so the sentence usually continues with something
conditional or hedged.

<code>～次第(しだい)です</code> at the end of a clause is a different animal
altogether - stiff written business Japanese, the close of an explanation in a
letter or an apology. It means roughly what
<code>～というわけです</code> means but sounds several notches more formal. Say it
out loud in conversation and you will sound like a memo.

Finally, <code>V-ます次第(しだい)</code> ("as soon as") is also business register,
and it is future-facing: <code>決(き)まり次第(しだい)ご連絡(れんらく)します</code>
is the standard promise. It carries no `だ` and no `で`.

## Contrast

### ～次第(しだい)で vs ～によって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `によって` reports a **settled pattern** of variation.
<code>次第(しだい)で</code> puts a **single outcome in the balance**, usually one
nobody has decided yet.

</div>

<pre>
開始(かいし)時間(じかん)は曜日(ようび)<b>によって</b>異(こと)なります。
The starting time differs according to the day of the week.
</pre>

<pre>
開始(かいし)できるかどうかは、参加者(さんかしゃ)の数(かず)<b>次第(しだい)です</b>。
Whether we can start at all depends on how many people turn up.
</pre>

The first is a timetable that already exists. The second is a question still open,
and the sentence leans forward. That difference also explains the shapes each
allows: `によって` can carry a cause, a means and the agent of a passive, none of
which <code>次第(しだい)</code> can touch.

There is a plain formal difference too. `によって` needs `に`;
<code>次第(しだい)</code> takes its noun bare, with no particle whatsoever.

### ～次第(しだい)で vs ～に応(おう)じて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に応(おう)じて</code>: somebody **adjusts** something to
fit, on a scale that already exists. <code>次第(しだい)で</code>: the thing is
**decided** by it, and it may not happen at all.

</div>

<pre>
人数(にんずう)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>部屋(へや)の大(おお)きさを変(か)えます。
We change the size of the room to fit the number of people.
</pre>

<pre>
人数(にんずう)<b>次第(しだい)で</b>、会場(かいじょう)を借(か)りるのをやめるかもしれません。
Depending on numbers, we may drop the idea of hiring a venue at all.
</pre>

<code>に応(おう)じて</code> presupposes a scheme with a setting for every value -
more people, bigger room. <code>次第(しだい)で</code> tolerates an all-or-nothing
result, and that is something <code>に応(おう)じて</code> cannot express, because
it needs a range on both sides.

### Noun + 次第(しだい) vs V-ます stem + 次第(しだい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what sits in front. After a **noun** it means "depends
on". After a **ます stem** it means "as soon as", and the two are unrelated.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)は部長(ぶちょう)の都合(つごう)<b>次第(しだい)です</b>。
The time of the meeting depends on the manager's availability.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)の都合(つごう)が分(わ)かり<b>次第(しだい)</b>、ご連絡(れんらく)いたします。
I will be in touch as soon as I know the manager's availability.
</pre>

The "as soon as" one never takes `だ` or `で`, cannot end a sentence, and always
looks forward to something that will certainly happen. If you find
<code>次第(しだい)</code> with no `だ`, no `で` and a comma after it, that is the
one you are reading.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 安(やす)い<b>次第(しだい)で</b>買(か)います。 → ✅ 値段(ねだん)<b>次第(しだい)で</b>買(か)います。
  Only a noun attaches. An adjective has to be turned into the noun it is talking
  about - the price, the quality, the size.

- ❌ 値段(ねだん)<b>次第(しだい)に</b>買(か)うかどうか決(き)めます。 → ✅ 値段(ねだん)<b>次第(しだい)で</b>買(か)うかどうか決(き)めます。
  <code>次第(しだい)に</code> is a separate adverb meaning "gradually". It cannot
  follow a noun in this sense at all.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)次第(しだい)で昨日(きのう)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)のため昨日(きのう)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
  <code>次第(しだい)</code> points at an outcome still hanging. A finished event has
  a cause, and causes take <code>ため</code> or `で`.

- ❌ 着(つ)く<b>次第(しだい)</b>連絡(れんらく)します。 → ✅ 着(つ)き<b>次第(しだい)</b>連絡(れんらく)します。
  The "as soon as" pattern takes the ます stem, not the dictionary form.
  <code>到着(とうちゃく)次第(しだい)</code> with the noun works too.

## Practice

**1.** 試合(しあい)をやるかどうかは天候(てんこう)____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

試合(しあい)をやるかどうかは天候(てんこう)<b>次第(しだい)</b>です。

The weather is the one factor that will settle a question still open, which is this
pattern's core job. The noun attaches with nothing in between.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会員(かいいん)の年齢(ねんれい)（次第(しだい)で・に応(おう)じて）会費(かいひ)が決(き)まっています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

年齢(ねんれい)<b>に応(おう)じて</b>会費(かいひ)が決(き)まっています。

The <code>決(き)まっています</code> gives it away: the scale of fees already exists,
with a price for each age band. <code>次第(しだい)で</code> would mean the fee had
not been worked out yet.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Whether the project goes ahead depends entirely on next month's budget."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

その計画(けいかく)を進(すす)めるかどうかは、来月(らいげつ)の予算(よさん)<b>次第(しだい)です</b>。

"Depends entirely on" is exactly what this pattern says on its own, so no adverb is
needed. The <code>かどうか</code> clause in front is what makes the outcome sound
genuinely undecided.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 返事(へんじ)の次第(しだい)では、考(かんが)え直(なお)すつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 返事(へんじ)<b>次第(しだい)では</b>、考(かんが)え直(なお)すつもりです。

The deciding noun attaches bare. The `の` is pulled in from ordinary noun-plus-noun
phrases, but <code>次第(しだい)</code> in this use behaves like a suffix rather
than a free noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 結果(けっか)が出(で)次第(しだい)、お知(し)らせします。
(b) お知(し)らせできるかどうかは結果(けっか)次第(しだい)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the ます-stem pattern: the results *will* come, and the moment they do you
will be told. It is a promise about timing, and it is standard business courtesy.

(b) is the noun pattern: whether you get told at all is in the balance, and the
results decide it. The two sentences share a word and share nothing else - one
guarantees contact, the other refuses to.

</details>

# を○○として

**Meaning** · as; taking X as Y - somebody treats one thing as belonging to a named role, purpose, category or standard

The frame is `を` + a noun naming a **capacity** + `として`. The `を` marks the thing
being handled; the noun in the middle says what it is being handled *as*. The slot
takes a small, learnable set of words - 目的(もくてき),
対象(たいしょう), 中心(ちゅうしん), 基準(きじゅん),
前提(ぜんてい), 例(れい), きっかけ, モデル, and role nouns like
代表(だいひょう) - and the result is the standard way official Japanese states
what a project is for and who it is aimed at.

## Formation

Both slots are nouns. The middle noun cannot be a verb or an adjective, and a clause
in the first slot has to be closed off with `こと`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun + Noun | Noun + を + Noun + として | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)を代表(だいひょう)として |
| Modifying a following noun | … + とした + Noun | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)を対象(たいしょう)とした調査(ちょうさ) |
| Same, non-past shape | … + とする + Noun | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもを対象(たいしょう)とする番組(ばんぐみ) |
| Stiff connective | … + とし、 | 支援(しえん) → 支援(しえん)を目的(もくてき)とし、 |
| Clause in the first slot | Clause + こと + を … として | 無料(むりょう)だ → 無料(むりょう)であることを前提(ぜんてい)として |
| Ordinary predicate | … + とする / としている | 営利(えいり) → 営利(えいり)を目的(もくてき)としない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern has a plainer twin, <code>を○○にして</code>. Both exist and
both are correct; `として` is the formal, official-sounding member of the pair, and
it is the one that shows up in constitutions, articles of association and research
abstracts.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)<b>を代表(だいひょう)として</b>選(えら)びました。
We chose him as our representative.
</pre>

<pre>
この会(かい)は交流(こうりゅう)<b>を目的(もくてき)として</b>開(ひら)かれた。
This gathering was held for the purpose of getting people talking to each other.
</pre>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>を対象(たいしょう)とした</b>説明会(せつめいかい)が来週(らいしゅう)行(おこな)われます。
A briefing aimed at international students will be held next week.
</pre>

<pre>
入院(にゅういん)<b>をきっかけとして</b>、父(ちち)はたばこをやめた。
Taking his spell in hospital as the moment to do it, my father gave up smoking.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の平均(へいきん)点(てん)<b>を基準(きじゅん)として</b>、今年(ことし)の合格(ごうかく)ラインを決(き)めた。
We fixed this year's pass mark taking last year's average as the benchmark.
</pre>

<pre>
この団体(だんたい)は、災害(さいがい)で家(いえ)を失(うしな)った人(ひと)への支援(しえん)<b>を目的(もくてき)とし</b>、全国(ぜんこく)で活動(かつどう)している。
This body exists to support people who have lost their homes to disasters, and it operates nationwide.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)の若(わか)いころの写真(しゃしん)<b>をモデルとして</b>描(えが)かれた絵(え)が、今(いま)も居間(いま)にかかっている。
A painting done from a photograph of my grandfather in his youth still hangs in the living room.
</pre>

<pre>
参加(さんか)は自由(じゆう)であること<b>を前提(ぜんてい)として</b>話(はなし)を進(すす)めておりますが、それでよろしいでしょうか。
We have been proceeding on the premise that taking part is voluntary - is that acceptable?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and institutional. Where a person would say
<code>子(こ)ども向(む)けの番組(ばんぐみ)</code>, a broadcaster's own
documentation says
<code>子(こ)どもを対象(たいしょう)とする番組(ばんぐみ)</code>. Where a friend
would say <code>運動(うんどう)のために</code>, a leaflet says
<code>健康(けんこう)増進(ぞうしん)を目的(もくてき)として</code>. Nothing about
the pattern is difficult; what marks it is the setting.

Two fillers are worth learning as fixed phrases, because they are near-obligatory in
their genres. <code>～を目的(もくてき)とする</code> is how an organisation, a law or
an event states its aim - the negative
<code>営利(えいり)を目的(もくてき)としない</code>, "non-profit", is a set term. And
<code>～を対象(たいしょう)とする</code> is how a survey, a service or a policy states
who it covers.

The connective <code>とし、</code> is stiffer again and belongs to documents. In
speech, keep to `として`.

## Contrast

### を○○として vs bare ～として

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare `として` gives a role to the **subject**, the one acting. The
`を` frame gives a role to the **object**, and somebody else is doing the assigning.

</div>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は通訳(つうやく)<b>として</b>会議(かいぎ)に出(で)た。
She attended the meeting as an interpreter.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)は彼女(かのじょ)<b>を通訳(つうやく)として</b>会議(かいぎ)に出(だ)した。
The company sent her to the meeting as an interpreter.
</pre>

The first is about how she was there; the second is about a decision somebody made
about her. That is why the `を` frame needs an outside party in the sentence - a
company, a committee, a law - and why it cannot describe how you see yourself. For
that, the bare form is the only option.

### を○○として vs を○○にして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にして` **actually turns** X into it, in practice. `として`
**designates** X as it, on paper or in principle.

</div>

<pre>
空(あ)き教室(きょうしつ)を物置(ものおき)<b>にして</b>いる。
They are using the empty classroom as a storeroom.
</pre>

<pre>
空(あ)き教室(きょうしつ)を会議室(かいぎしつ)<b>として</b>登録(とうろく)した。
They have registered the empty classroom as a meeting room.
</pre>

The first sentence is about what happens in the room; the second is about what the
paperwork says. With <code>きっかけ</code> both forms are common -
<code>をきっかけに</code> in speech, <code>をきっかけとして</code> in writing - and
there the difference has flattened out to register alone.

### を目的(もくてき)として vs ～ために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ために` states a purpose in ordinary prose and takes a **verb**.
<code>を目的(もくてき)として</code> states an institutional aim and takes a **noun**.

</div>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)の人(ひと)と交流(こうりゅう)する<b>ために</b>、夏祭(なつまつ)りを開(ひら)いています。
We hold the summer festival so that we can mix with people from the area.
</pre>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)交流(こうりゅう)<b>を目的(もくてき)として</b>、夏祭(なつまつ)りが開(ひら)かれている。
The summer festival is held with local community exchange as its aim.
</pre>

Same festival, two registers: a neighbour explaining, and a line from the leaflet.
The grammatical difference is the useful one to hold on to -
<code>ために</code> attaches to a verb clause or to a noun with `の`, while
<code>を目的(もくてき)として</code> demands a bare noun and will only take a clause
that has been closed off with `こと`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>に対象(たいしょう)として</b>調査(ちょうさ)した。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>を対象(たいしょう)として</b>調査(ちょうさ)した。
  The thing being treated is marked with `を`. The `に` drifts in from
  <code>に対(たい)して</code>, which looks similar and means something else entirely.

- ❌ 交流(こうりゅう)する<b>を目的(もくてき)として</b>会(かい)を開(ひら)いた。 → ✅ 交流(こうりゅう)すること<b>を目的(もくてき)として</b>会(かい)を開(ひら)いた。
  A verb cannot sit in the `を` slot. Close the clause with `こと`, or use the plain
  noun 交流(こうりゅう) and drop the verb altogether.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は自分(じぶん)<b>を医者(いしゃ)として</b>働(はたら)いています。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は医者(いしゃ)<b>として</b>働(はたら)いています。
  You do not assign yourself a role from the outside. When the subject is the one
  wearing the hat, the bare form is the only correct choice.

## Practice

**1.** 市(し)は高齢者(こうれいしゃ)____対象(たいしょう)として、無料(むりょう)の健康(けんこう)診断(しんだん)を行(おこな)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

高齢者(こうれいしゃ)<b>を</b>対象(たいしょう)として

The people being covered by the service go in the `を` slot. This is the standard
wording for who a public service is aimed at, and it is worth learning whole.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部員(ぶいん)全員(ぜんいん)が彼(かれ)（は・を）リーダーとして認(みと)めている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部員(ぶいん)全員(ぜんいん)が彼(かれ)<b>を</b>リーダーとして認(みと)めている。

The club members are doing the assigning and he is the one being assigned, so he is
the object. With `は` he would be the one acknowledging somebody else.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This survey was carried out with first-year students as its subjects."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この調査(ちょうさ)は一年生(いちねんせい)<b>を対象(たいしょう)として</b>行(おこな)われました。

<code>を対象(たいしょう)とする</code> is the fixed phrase for the population a study
covers, and the passive <code>行(おこな)われました</code> keeps the register where
research writing wants it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 子(こ)どもを対象(たいしょう)として番組(ばんぐみ)が増(ふ)えている。

`として` is adverbial and modifies verbs. To modify the noun

<code>とする</code>; either is fine here.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 空(あ)き家(や)を店(みせ)にしている。
(b) 空(あ)き家(や)を店(みせ)として登録(とうろく)している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

and is trading from it. It says nothing about permissions.

(b) is about status: the property is on the books as commercial premises. It is
compatible with the place standing empty, which is exactly the gap between doing and
designating that separates these two forms.

</details>

# をめぐって

**Meaning** · over; surrounding; concerning - names the thing that an argument, a negotiation or a swirl of rumour has formed *around*

<code>めぐる</code> is "to go round, to circle". `をめぐって` puts a noun in the middle
of the room and several parties around it, circling. Look at what follows and you
will nearly always find 議論(ぎろん), 対立(たいりつ),
争(あらそ)う, 話(はな)し合(あ)う, もめる,
意見(いけん)が分(わ)かれる, 噂(うわさ). If nothing contentious follows, this
is the wrong word and `について` is the right one.

## Formation

Takes a **noun only**, marked with `を`, and that noun has to be an **issue** -
something people can take sides about.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + をめぐって | 遺産(いさん) → 遺産(いさん)をめぐって |
| Noun (headline register) | Noun + をめぐり | 条約(じょうやく) → 条約(じょうやく)をめぐり |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + をめぐる + Noun | 土地(とち) → 土地(とち)をめぐる争(あらそ)い |
| Nominalised clause | Clause + か + をめぐって | 中止(ちゅうし)するか → 中止(ちゅうし)するかどうかをめぐって |
| Written with kanji (uncommon) | Noun + を巡(めぐ)って | 人事(じんじ) → 人事(じんじ)を巡(めぐ)って |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the sentence needs **more than one party**. One person turning something
over in their own head cannot be circling it, so a single-subject verb of thinking,
studying or writing rules this pattern out.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
土地(とち)<b>をめぐって</b>兄弟(きょうだい)が争(あらそ)っている。
The brothers are fighting over the land.
</pre>

<pre>
その発言(はつげん)<b>をめぐって</b>議論(ぎろん)が続(つづ)いている。
Debate over that remark is still going on.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい規則(きそく)<b>をめぐり</b>、社内(しゃない)で意見(いけん)が分(わ)かれている。
Opinion inside the company is split over the new rule.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)の再開発(さいかいはつ)<b>をめぐる</b>話(はな)し合(あ)いは、まだ終(お)わっていない。
The talks over the redevelopment by the station are still not finished.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)<b>をめぐって</b>、二(ふた)つのチームが最終(さいしゅう)戦(せん)まで競(きそ)い合(あ)った。
The two sides fought it out for the title right up to the final match.
</pre>

<pre>
遺産(いさん)の分(わ)け方(かた)<b>をめぐって</b>、親戚(しんせき)どうしが口(くち)をきかなくなってしまった。
Over how to split the inheritance, the relatives have stopped speaking to one another.
</pre>

<pre>
移民(いみん)政策(せいさく)<b>をめぐる</b>対立(たいりつ)が、今回(こんかい)の選挙(せんきょ)の大(おお)きな争点(そうてん)になっている。
The split over immigration policy has become a central issue in this election.
</pre>

<pre>
あの俳優(はいゆう)の結婚(けっこん)<b>をめぐって</b>、根拠(こんきょ)のない噂(うわさ)がしばらく流(なが)れていた。
Groundless rumours went round for a while about that actor's marriage.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

News register. Newspapers, broadcast bulletins, court reports, political
commentary, and the kind of company memo that is trying to sound like a newspaper.
`をめぐり` in particular is a headline and lead-sentence word - you will meet it far
more often in print than you will ever have cause to say it. In conversation people
use <code>～のことで</code>: <code>お金(かね)のことでもめている</code>.

The pattern itself is neutral, but it keeps bad company. Almost every predicate that
takes it involves friction, so `をめぐって` has picked up the colour of its
collocates: hearing it, a Japanese listener expects trouble. Even in the
<code>噂(うわさ)</code> case, what is being described is talk swirling round a
focus rather than a calm report.

The kanji spelling <code>巡(めぐ)って</code> exists and is not wrong, but kana is
overwhelmingly the norm in journalism, which is where this pattern lives.

## Contrast

### をめぐって vs ～について

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `について` names the **topic** of one party's activity.
`をめぐって` names a **focus that several parties are circling**, usually while
disagreeing.

</div>

<pre>
環境(かんきょう)問題(もんだい)<b>について</b>レポートを書(か)いた。
I wrote a report on environmental problems.
</pre>

<pre>
環境(かんきょう)問題(もんだい)<b>をめぐって</b>各国(かっこく)が対立(たいりつ)している。
Countries are at odds over environmental problems.
</pre>

The test is arithmetic: count the voices. One person writing, studying, thinking or
explaining takes `について`. Two or more pulling in different directions takes
`をめぐって`. <code>に関(かん)して</code> behaves like `について` here - it is the
stiffer twin of it, and it is just as unavailable for a quarrel.

### をめぐって vs ～に対(たい)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に対(たい)して</code> aims something **at** a target.
`をめぐって` puts the noun **in the middle** and the parties around it.

</div>

<pre>
市長(しちょう)の説明(せつめい)<b>に対(たい)して</b>批判(ひはん)が集(あつ)まった。
Criticism was directed at the mayor's explanation.
</pre>

<pre>
市長(しちょう)の説明(せつめい)<b>をめぐって</b>市議会(しぎかい)が紛糾(ふんきゅう)した。
The council was thrown into uproar over the mayor's explanation.
</pre>

In the first the explanation is being hit; the criticism travels towards it. In the
second the explanation just sits there while the councillors fight each other about
it. Notice the difference in who the sentence is about: with
<code>に対(たい)して</code> the noun is one end of a line, and with `をめぐって` it
is the centre of a circle.

### をめぐって vs を中心(ちゅうしん)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> makes X the **core** of an
activity and is neutral. `をめぐって` makes X the **bone of contention**.

</div>

<pre>
若者(わかもの)<b>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</b>この映画(えいが)が人気(にんき)を集(あつ)めている。
This film is proving popular, chiefly among younger viewers.
</pre>

<pre>
この映画(えいが)の内容(ないよう)<b>をめぐって</b>賛否(さんぴ)が分(わ)かれている。
Opinion is divided for and against over the content of this film.
</pre>

Both are geometric metaphors and they sound as though they should overlap, but the
noun they accept is different. <code>を中心(ちゅうしん)に</code> happily takes a
**group of people** - "mainly among the young" - while `をめぐって` cannot: its noun
has to be something arguable, and a group of people is not a proposition.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 将来(しょうらい)<b>をめぐって</b>考(かんが)えています。 → ✅ 将来(しょうらい)<b>について</b>考(かんが)えています。
  One person thinking has a topic, not an argument. There is nobody on the other side
  of the circle.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>をめぐって</b>調査(ちょうさ)した。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>を対象(たいしょう)として</b>調査(ちょうさ)した。
  The noun has to be an issue. A group of people can be surveyed, targeted or written
  about, but not disputed over - unless the dispute really is about them.

- ❌ 問題(もんだい)<b>にめぐって</b>議論(ぎろん)になった。 → ✅ 問題(もんだい)<b>をめぐって</b>議論(ぎろん)になった。
  The particle is `を`. `に` leaks in from the several other
  <code>に</code>-patterns that crowd around this one in the textbook.

## Practice

**1.** 新(あたら)しいダムの建設(けんせつ)____、住民(じゅうみん)の意見(いけん)が分(わ)かれている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

建設(けんせつ)<b>をめぐって</b>、住民(じゅうみん)の意見(いけん)が分(わ)かれている。

<code>意見(いけん)が分(わ)かれる</code> is the giveaway: two camps, one issue in
the middle. That is precisely the shape this pattern draws.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 江戸(えど)時代(じだい)の農業(のうぎょう)（をめぐって・について）論文(ろんぶん)を書(か)いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

江戸(えど)時代(じだい)の農業(のうぎょう)<b>について</b>論文(ろんぶん)を書(か)いた。

One writer, one paper, one topic - no circle of disputants. `をめぐって` would
require the paper itself to be the subject of a row.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Negotiations over the price have been dragging on for six months."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

値段(ねだん)<b>をめぐる</b>交渉(こうしょう)が、半年(はんとし)も続(つづ)いている。

Because the phrase modifies the noun <code>交渉(こうしょう)</code>, the form is
<code>をめぐる</code>, not <code>をめぐって</code>. The `も` after
<code>半年(はんとし)</code> is what carries "dragging on".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 税金(ぜいきん)をめぐって問題(もんだい)が新聞(しんぶん)に載(の)っていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 税金(ぜいきん)<b>をめぐる</b>問題(もんだい)が新聞(しんぶん)に載(の)っていた。

Here the phrase is describing which problem, so it has to modify the noun, and that
takes <code>をめぐる</code>. As written, the て-form tries to attach to
<code>載(の)っていた</code> and the sentence says the *appearing in the paper* was
the disputed thing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新(あたら)しい校則(こうそく)について話(はな)し合(あ)った。
(b) 新(あたら)しい校則(こうそく)をめぐって話(はな)し合(あ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral report of the agenda: the new school rules were what the meeting was
about. It is compatible with everybody agreeing in five minutes.

(b) presents the rules as contested - people had come with positions, and the talk
was a working-out of that. Journalists exploit exactly this difference, and (b) in a
news report is a signal that the meeting did not go smoothly.

</details>

# ～に向かって

**Meaning** · towards; facing; heading for - points the subject at a target, whether that target is a place, a person or a goal

<code>向(む)かう</code> is "to face, to head for". <code>～に向(む)かって</code>
gives the **orientation** of whatever the subject is doing: a body walking that way,
a face turned that way, a voice thrown that way, a situation trending that way. What
it does not do is deliver anything to the target. It describes the heading, not the
arrival - which is why arrival verbs refuse it.

## Formation

<code>向(む)かう</code> is intransitive, so the target takes `に` (or `へ`), never
`を`. A **noun only**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (place or direction) | Noun + に向(む)かって | 駅(えき) → 駅(えき)に向(む)かって |
| Noun (stiffer, written) | Noun + に向(む)かい | 北(きた) → 北(きた)に向(む)かい |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + に向(む)かう + Noun | 山頂(さんちょう) → 山頂(さんちょう)に向(む)かう道(みち) |
| Person, before a verb of speech | Person + に向(む)かって | 上司(じょうし) → 上司(じょうし)に向(む)かって |
| A state trending somewhere | Noun + に向(む)かっている | 回復(かいふく) → 回復(かいふく)に向(む)かっている |
| With `へ` instead | Noun + へ向(む)かって | 南(みなみ) → 南(みなみ)へ向(む)かって |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>机(つくえ)に向(む)かう</code> has hardened into an idiom meaning
"to sit down to work" - homework, writing, study. It is not really about the
furniture: 一日中(いちにちじゅう)机(つくえ)に向(む)かっていた means you spent
the day working, wherever you were sitting.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)<b>に向(む)かって</b>歩(ある)いています。
I'm walking towards the station.
</pre>

<pre>
鏡(かがみ)<b>に向(む)かって</b>スピーチの練習(れんしゅう)をした。
I practised my speech in front of the mirror.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)は北(きた)<b>に向(む)かって</b>進(すす)んでいる。
The typhoon is moving northwards.
</pre>

<pre>
目標(もくひょう)<b>に向(む)かって</b>一歩(いっぽ)ずつ進(すす)むしかない。
There is nothing for it but to move towards the goal one step at a time.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)の体調(たいちょう)は、ゆっくりですが回復(かいふく)<b>に向(む)かっています</b>。
My grandmother's health is slowly turning towards recovery.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)<b>に向(む)かって</b>そんな口(くち)のきき方(かた)をするものではない。
That is no way to talk to the department head.
</pre>

<pre>
毎晩(まいばん)机(つくえ)<b>に向(む)かって</b>いるのに考(かんが)えがまとまらず、一行(いちぎょう)も書(か)けない日(ひ)が続(つづ)いている。
I sit down to it every evening, but my thoughts will not come together and I have gone days without writing a line.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)の外(そと)では、夕日(ゆうひ)<b>に向(む)かって</b>鳥(とり)の群(む)れが飛(と)んでいくのが見(み)えた。
Outside the window I could see a flock of birds flying off towards the setting sun.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

In its physical sense the pattern is completely register-neutral and belongs to
ordinary speech as much as to writing. Weather forecasts, directions, narration -
all of it.

The use with a **person** is the one to handle with care. Putting somebody in front
of <code>に向(む)かって</code> and then a verb of speech frames you as squared up
to them, and the sentence usually turns out to be about rudeness or defiance:
<code>先生(せんせい)に向(む)かって何(なん)だ、その態度(たいど)は</code>. Neutral
"say to somebody" is plain `に`. Use <code>に向(む)かって</code> deliberately, when
the facing-up-to is the point.

The **figurative** use - <code>解決(かいけつ)に向(む)かう</code>,
<code>回復(かいふく)に向(む)かう</code>,
<code>悪化(あっか)に向(む)かう</code> - reads as measured and slightly formal, and
is common in news and in medical or economic commentary. It nearly always appears in
the ている form, because it describes a trend in progress.

<code>に向(む)かい</code> is the written connective. `へ` in place of `に` is
slightly more literary and stresses the direction over the destination; both are
correct.

## Contrast

### ～に向(む)かって vs bare ～へ・～に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `へ` and `に` name a **destination**.
<code>に向(む)かって</code> names the **heading**, and says nothing about getting
there.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)、京都(きょうと)<b>へ</b>行(い)きます。
I'm going to Kyoto next week.
</pre>

<pre>
車(くるま)は京都(きょうと)<b>に向(む)かって</b>走(はし)り続(つづ)けた。
The car kept driving in the direction of Kyoto.
</pre>

The first is a trip, arrival included; the second is a stretch of road with the
front of the car pointed a certain way, and it is perfectly compatible with never
arriving. That gap is also why <code>山(やま)に向(む)かって叫(さけ)ぶ</code>
works and <code>山(やま)へ叫(さけ)ぶ</code> does not: a shout has a direction but
no destination.

The corollary is that arrival verbs - <code>着(つ)く</code>,
<code>入(はい)る</code>, <code>到着(とうちゃく)する</code> - take plain `に` and
reject this pattern outright.

### ～に向(む)かって vs ～に向(む)けて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に向(む)かって</code>: the subject **itself** faces or
moves that way. <code>に向(む)けて</code>: something is **aimed** at the target, or
work is being done with a **future date** in view.

</div>

<pre>
海(うみ)<b>に向(む)かって</b>大声(おおごえ)で叫(さけ)んだ。
I shouted at the top of my voice out towards the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)の大会(たいかい)<b>に向(む)けて</b>練習(れんしゅう)を始(はじ)めた。
We have started training with next year's tournament in view.
</pre>

The split is reliable. A physical target you could point at takes
<code>に向(む)かって</code>. A scheduled event you are preparing for takes
<code>に向(む)けて</code> - <code>開催(かいさい)に向(む)けて</code>,
<code>実現(じつげん)に向(む)けて</code>, <code>試験(しけん)に向(む)けて</code> -
and this is one of the most common phrases in Japanese news writing.

### ～に向(む)かって vs ～に対(たい)して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に向(む)かって</code> is **physical facing**, and with a
person it tends to flag confrontation. <code>に対(たい)して</code> is **abstract
targeting**, and is neutral.

</div>

<pre>
お客(きゃく)さま<b>に対(たい)して</b>失礼(しつれい)な態度(たいど)を取(と)ってはいけません。
You must not behave rudely towards customers.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)<b>に向(む)かって</b>大(おお)きな声(こえ)を出(だ)すな。
Don't raise your voice at your parents.
</pre>

The first states a policy about a category of people; the second is about a person
standing in front of another person. <code>に対(たい)して</code> will happily take
institutions, abstractions and even numbers -
<code>昨年(さくねん)に対(たい)して一割(いちわり)増(ぞう)</code>, "up ten per
cent on last year". <code>に向(む)かって</code> needs something you could physically
turn and face.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 空港(くうこう)<b>に向(む)かって</b>着(つ)いた。 → ✅ 空港(くうこう)<b>に</b>着(つ)いた。
  Arrival verbs take plain `に`. This pattern describes the journey's heading, and a
  heading cannot arrive.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)<b>に向(む)かって</b>毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しています。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)<b>に向(む)けて</b>毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)しています。
  A scheduled event you are preparing for takes
  <code>に向(む)けて</code>. You are not walking at the exam.

- ❌ 先生(せんせい)<b>に向(む)かって</b>質問(しつもん)しました。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)<b>に</b>質問(しつもん)しました。
  An ordinary question to somebody takes plain `に`. With
  <code>に向(む)かって</code> the sentence suggests you rounded on the teacher, which
  is presumably not what happened.

## Practice

**1.** 船(ふね)は小(ちい)さな島(しま)____ゆっくりと進(すす)んでいった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

島(しま)<b>に向(む)かって</b>ゆっくりと進(すす)んでいった。

The island is where the bow is pointed. Nothing in the sentence says the boat got
there, and that is exactly the space this pattern occupies.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 来月(らいげつ)の発表(はっぴょう)（に向(む)かって・に向(む)けて）資料(しりょう)を集(あつ)めています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

来月(らいげつ)の発表(はっぴょう)<b>に向(む)けて</b>資料(しりょう)を集(あつ)めています。

A presentation is a date in the calendar, not a place you can face, so preparation
for it takes <code>に向(む)けて</code>. This is the standard phrasing for working
towards a deadline.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The situation is finally moving towards a settlement."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

事態(じたい)はようやく解決(かいけつ)<b>に向(む)かっています</b>。

The figurative sense - a state of affairs trending somewhere - is standard here, and
it wants the ている form because the movement is still under way. This is the
register a news bulletin uses.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 目標(もくひょう)を向(む)かって一年(いちねん)頑張(がんば)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 目標(もくひょう)<b>に向(む)かって</b>一年(いちねん)頑張(がんば)ります。

<code>向(む)かう</code> is intransitive: nothing is being acted on, so `を` has no
role. The target of a heading is always `に`, or occasionally `へ`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 課長(かちょう)に向(む)かって「それは違(ちが)います」と言(い)った。
(b) 課長(かちょう)に「それは違(ちが)います」と言(い)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) stages the moment: you turned to face your section head and contradicted him. The
pattern foregrounds the confrontation, and in most contexts the sentence is being told
as a story about nerve, or about bad manners.

(b) simply reports who was told what. It is the neutral option, and it is what you
would use if the point of the sentence were the content of the correction rather than
the act of making it.

</details>

# ～抜きで / ～抜きにして

**Meaning** · without; leaving out; skipping - takes out something that would normally be part of the package

<code>抜(ぬ)く</code> is "to pull out, to omit", and <code>抜(ぬ)き</code> is its
noun. The word in front names a **component that is ordinarily there** - wasabi in
the sushi, breakfast in the morning, preamble before the business, the boss at the
drinks - and the pattern removes it on purpose. That word *ordinarily* is doing real
work: if the thing was never part of the arrangement, this is not the pattern for it.

## Formation

The noun attaches **bare** - no `を`, no `の` in front of
<code>抜(ぬ)き</code> - and only a noun will do.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + 抜(ぬ)きで | わさび → わさび抜(ぬ)きで |
| Noun (a proposal to skip it) | Noun + は抜(ぬ)きにして | 冗談(じょうだん) → 冗談(じょうだん)は抜(ぬ)きにして |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + 抜(ぬ)きの + Noun | 砂糖(さとう) → 砂糖(さとう)抜(ぬ)きのコーヒー |
| As the predicate | Noun + 抜(ぬ)きだ | 昼食(ちゅうしょく) → 今日(きょう)は昼食(ちゅうしょく)抜(ぬ)きだ |
| "could not have, without it" | Noun + 抜(ぬ)きには / 抜(ぬ)きでは … ない | 協力(きょうりょく) → 協力(きょうりょく)抜(ぬ)きには成功(せいこう)しない |
| Bare compound (an order) | Noun + 抜(ぬ)き | ネギ → ネギ抜(ぬ)き |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the last row is what you actually say across a counter.
<code>ネギ抜(ぬ)きで</code> or just <code>ネギ抜(ぬ)き</code> is a complete,
polite-enough request when it follows the name of the dish, and no Japanese speaker
will hear it as brusque.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
わさび<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>お願(ねが)いします。
No wasabi, please.
</pre>

<pre>
今朝(けさ)は朝食(ちょうしょく)<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>出(で)かけた。
I went out this morning with no breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
前置(まえお)き<b>は抜(ぬ)きにして</b>、本題(ほんだい)に入(はい)りましょう。
Let's skip the preamble and get to the point.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)<b>は抜(ぬ)きにして</b>、そろそろ真面目(まじめ)に考(かんが)えないか。
Joking aside - isn't it about time we thought about this seriously?
</pre>

<pre>
堅(かた)い挨拶(あいさつ)<b>抜(ぬ)きの</b>、気楽(きらく)な集(あつ)まりにしたいと思(おも)っています。
I'd like to make it a relaxed gathering, with none of the formal speeches.
</pre>

<pre>
皆(みな)さんのご協力(きょうりょく)<b>抜(ぬ)きには</b>、この催(もよお)しは成(な)り立(た)たなかったでしょう。
Without all of your help, this event would never have come together.
</pre>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は部長(ぶちょう)<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>飲(の)みに行(い)こうという話(はなし)になって、急(きゅう)に店(みせ)を探(さが)すことになった。
The talk turned to going for a drink without the boss, and all of a sudden we had to find somewhere.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>いきなり結論(けつろん)だけ言(い)われても、何(なに)をどう判断(はんだん)すればいいのか分(わ)かりません。
Handed the conclusion out of nowhere with no explanation, I have no idea what I am supposed to make of it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everyday and neutral. Ordering food is where most learners first meet it, and there
it is completely unmarked. Nothing about
<code>抜(ぬ)き</code> is formal or literary.

<code>～は抜(ぬ)きにして</code> is a **proposal**, and a brisk one: "let's drop
that and move on". <code>前置(まえお)きは抜(ぬ)きにして</code> and
<code>堅(かた)い話(はなし)は抜(ぬ)きにして</code> are the standard ways of
cutting to the chase, and they are friendly rather than rude - the speaker is
proposing to save everybody time. <code>冗談(じょうだん)抜(ぬ)きで</code> as an
adverb is "seriously, no joke", and is very common in casual speech.

<code>～抜(ぬ)きには…ない</code> pulls in the other direction: it is formal, and it
is the standard shape of thanks in a speech or an acknowledgement -
<code>皆(みな)さまのお力(ちから)抜(ぬ)きには</code>. Note that it **requires a
negative** at the end; the frame is built to say that the thing would not have
happened.

One point of care. With a **person**, <code>抜(ぬ)きで</code> means excluding them,
and it can land pointedly - <code>課長(かちょう)抜(ぬ)きで</code> tells the
listener that the exclusion was deliberate. That is often exactly what the speaker
means, but it is not a neutral way to describe who happened to be free.

## Contrast

### ～抜(ぬ)きで vs ～なしで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なしで` is plain **absence**: the thing simply is not there.
<code>抜(ぬ)きで</code> is **removal**: the thing would normally be included and
somebody took it out.

</div>

<pre>
傘(かさ)<b>なしで</b>出(で)かけたら、ひどい目(め)にあった。
I went out without an umbrella and had a miserable time of it.
</pre>

<pre>
氷(こおり)<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>お願(ねが)いします。
No ice, please.
</pre>

An umbrella is not a standard component of leaving the house, so `なしで` is right.
Ice is a standard component of the drink, so leaving it out is
<code>抜(ぬ)き</code>. Where the thing genuinely is part of the package both are
possible, and <code>抜(ぬ)き</code> then sounds like an instruction:
<code>砂糖(さとう)なしのコーヒー</code> describes the cup, while
<code>砂糖(さとう)抜(ぬ)きのコーヒー</code> sounds like what you asked for.

`なしで` also takes nouns that nobody could remove -
<code>許可(きょか)なしで入(はい)る</code>, "enter without permission" - where
<code>抜(ぬ)き</code> is impossible.

### ～抜(ぬ)きで vs ～を除(のぞ)いて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を除(のぞ)いて</code> marks an **exception to a
statement**. <code>抜(ぬ)きで</code> says the **activity was carried out** with the
item taken out.

</div>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)<b>を除(のぞ)いて</b>毎日(まいにち)開(あ)いています。
We are open every day except Sunday.
</pre>

<pre>
日曜日(にちようび)<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>予定(よてい)を組(く)んでもらえますか。
Could you draw up the schedule with Sunday left out of it?
</pre>

The first sentence is about the scope of a claim - which days the claim covers. The
second is about how a piece of work was done.
<code>を除(のぞ)いて</code> is also stiffer, and it will not take
<code>冗談(じょうだん)</code> or <code>遠慮(えんりょ)</code>, because those are not
members of a list you are quantifying over.

### ～抜(ぬ)きで vs ～ないで・～ずに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないで` and `ずに` attach to a **verb**: you did not perform the
action. <code>抜(ぬ)きで</code> attaches to a **noun**: the slot was left empty.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べ<b>ないで</b>学校(がっこう)へ行(い)った。
I went to school without eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>学校(がっこう)へ行(い)った。
I went to school with no breakfast.
</pre>

Almost the same fact, two grammars. The first is about an action you did not carry
out; the second presents breakfast as a slot in the morning that was left empty, which
is why it fits so naturally into talk about dieting or oversleeping. And when there is
no action to negate at all - <code>冗談(じょうだん)抜(ぬ)きで</code>,
<code>税(ぜい)抜(ぬ)きで</code> - the noun pattern is the only one available.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 砂糖(さとう)<b>を抜(ぬ)きで</b>お願(ねが)いします。 → ✅ 砂糖(さとう)<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>お願(ねが)いします。
  <code>抜(ぬ)き</code> is a noun and forms a compound with the word in front. It
  takes no particle at all, even though the underlying verb would.

- ❌ 食(た)べる<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>出(で)かけた。 → ✅ 食(た)べ<b>ないで</b>出(で)かけた。
  A verb cannot attach. Either name the thing that was skipped -
  <code>朝食(ちょうしょく)抜(ぬ)きで</code> - or negate the verb instead.

- ❌ 雪(ゆき)<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>冬(ふゆ)が終(お)わった。 → ✅ 雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)らないまま冬(ふゆ)が終(お)わった。
  Weather is not a component anyone leaves out. This pattern needs something that
  belongs to the package and that somebody could have chosen to include.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、ねぎ____お願(ねが)いできますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ねぎ<b>抜(ぬ)きで</b>お願(ねが)いできますか。

Spring onion is a standard part of the dish, so leaving it out is a removal. This is
the everyday restaurant request, and it needs no particle before
<code>抜(ぬ)き</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 許可(きょか)（抜(ぬ)きで・なしで）中(なか)に入(はい)ってはいけません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

許可(きょか)<b>なしで</b>中(なか)に入(はい)ってはいけません。

Permission is not an ingredient anybody removes from anything - it is either present
or absent. <code>抜(ぬ)き</code> needs a component that could have been included.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Without your advice, I could never have finished this."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あなたのアドバイス<b>抜(ぬ)きには</b>、これを仕上(しあ)げることはできなかったでしょう。

This is the formal <code>抜(ぬ)きには…ない</code> frame, and it is the standard
shape for thanking someone in a speech. The negative at the end is obligatory - the
whole point is what would *not* have happened.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)の協力(きょうりょく)抜(ぬ)きには、完成(かんせい)できました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)の協力(きょうりょく)<b>抜(ぬ)きには</b>、完成(かんせい)できませんでした。

<code>抜(ぬ)きには</code> sets up a condition without which something fails, so it
has to be answered by a negative. As written, the sentence says her help was an
obstacle.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今日(きょう)は昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)抜(ぬ)きだ。
(b) 今日(きょう)は昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents lunch as a slot in the day that has been struck out - because you are
dieting, because there is no time, or because somebody has decided you go without. It
sounds like a policy for the day.

(b) simply reports the state of affairs so far. It carries no suggestion that the
omission was planned, and it leaves the afternoon open, whereas (a) rather implies
lunch is not happening at all.

</details>
