# N2 - Not at all, and not necessarily


---

# ちっとも～ない

**Meaning** · not at all; not in the least - the spoken, faintly exasperated member of the "zero" family

`ちっとも` is <code>ちっと</code> ("a tiny bit") plus `も`, and like every
`も`-word it demands a negative to lean on. What it adds to a plain negative is
**disappointment**. Something was supposed to change, work, arrive or improve, and
it has not budged. That expectation is baked in, which is why `ちっとも` sounds
alive in conversation and stiff in a report.

**What is denied: the amount, all the way down to nothing.** `ちっとも` is not
denying an inference the way <code>わけではない</code> does, and not denying that a
rule always holds the way <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> does. It denies the
quantity itself: not one bit happened.

## Formation

`ちっとも` is an adverb. It never changes shape; it sits before the predicate, and
the predicate must be negative.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | ちっとも + ない-form | 変(か)わる → ちっとも変(か)わらない |
| Verb (polite) | ちっとも + ません | 届(とど)く → ちっとも届(とど)きません |
| Verb (past) | ちっとも + なかった | 進(すす)む → ちっとも進(すす)まなかった |
| い-adjective | ちっとも + ～くない | 面白(おもしろ)い → ちっとも面白(おもしろ)くない |
| な-adjective | ちっとも + ～ではない／じゃない | 平気(へいき)だ → ちっとも平気(へいき)じゃない |
| Noun | ちっとも + ～ではない | 問題(もんだい) → ちっとも問題(もんだい)ではない |
| Change of state | ちっとも + ～くならない | よい → ちっともよくならない |
| Standalone reply | ちっとも。 | 「眠(ねむ)れた?」「ちっとも。」 |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can put the word 全然(ぜんぜん) there, `ちっとも` fits
grammatically. The standalone 「ちっとも。」 is not an exception - the negative verb
is simply left off, and both speakers hear it anyway.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この薬(くすり)は<b>ちっとも</b>効(き)か<b>ない</b>。
This medicine isn't working at all.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが<b>ちっとも</b>来(こ)<b>ない</b>。
The bus just isn't coming.
</pre>

<pre>
注意(ちゅうい)しても、息子(むすこ)は<b>ちっとも</b>聞(き)いてくれ<b>ません</b>。
I tell him off, but my son doesn't listen to a word of it.
</pre>

<pre>
「仕事(しごと)、進(すす)んだ?」「<b>ちっとも</b>。」
"Get anywhere with the work?" "Not a bit."
</pre>

<pre>
エアコンをつけたのに、この部屋(へや)は<b>ちっとも</b>涼(すず)しく<b>ならない</b>。
I've put the air conditioning on and the room isn't getting any cooler at all.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)から習(なら)っているのに、発音(はつおん)が<b>ちっとも</b>よく<b>ならなくて</b>困(こま)っています。
I've been taking lessons since last year and my pronunciation isn't improving in the slightest - it's getting me down.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい機械(きかい)を入(い)れたが、作業(さぎょう)は<b>ちっとも</b>楽(らく)に<b>なっていない</b>。
We brought in a new machine, and the work has not got any easier at all.
</pre>

<pre>
三十分(さんじゅっぷん)も並(なら)んでいるのに、列(れつ)が<b>ちっとも</b>動(うご)か<b>ない</b>。
I have been queuing a solid thirty minutes and the line is not moving an inch.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ちっとも` belongs to speech. It is at home in conversation, in dialogue in a novel,
in a blog post - and it looks out of place in a report, a contract or an academic
paragraph, where 全(まった)く or 少(すこ)しも does the same work with a straight
face. It is not rude and not childish; it is simply informal, and it carries a
grumble.

That grumble is the point. 「ちっとも変(か)わらないね」 said about an old friend is
affectionate; said about a company's service it is a complaint. The word implies
that the speaker was entitled to expect movement, so it is naturally at home after
のに and after ても: 待(ま)っているのに, 注意(ちゅうい)しても.

It is neutral as to the speaker's age or gender, though it is one of those words
that can read as slightly homely in writing - younger speakers reach for
全然(ぜんぜん) more often in casual talk. That is a tendency, not a rule;
`ちっとも` is understood by everyone and used by everyone.

It combines happily with ます-form: 「ちっとも存(ぞん)じませんでした」 is perfectly
polite. What makes `ちっとも` informal is the word itself, not the ending you attach
it to, so you cannot rescue a formal document by adding ます.

## Contrast

### ちっとも vs 全然(ぜんぜん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ちっとも` **only** ever takes a negative. 全然(ぜんぜん) in
modern casual speech has escaped that rule and now turns up with affirmatives too.

</div>

<pre>
試験(しけん)は<b>ちっとも</b>むずかしく<b>なかった</b>。
The exam was not difficult in the slightest.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)は<b>全然(ぜんぜん)</b>簡単(かんたん)だったよ。
The exam was dead easy.
</pre>

The second sentence is ordinary spoken Japanese today and the first pattern cannot
copy it: ❌<code>ちっとも簡単(かんたん)だった</code> is broken. Where both are
negative, they overlap almost completely, and the choice is texture -
全然(ぜんぜん) is the neutral default, `ちっとも` adds the note of thwarted
expectation.

### ちっとも vs あまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `あまり～ない` is **not much**. `ちっとも～ない` is **none**.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は<b>あまり</b>寝(ね)<b>ていない</b>。三時間(さんじかん)くらいかな。
I did not get much sleep last night. About three hours, I would say.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は<b>ちっとも</b>寝(ね)<b>ていない</b>。
I did not sleep a wink last night.
</pre>

Because `ちっとも` claims zero, it cannot be followed by an exception. Saying
「ちっとも食(た)べなかった。パンは食(た)べたけど」 contradicts itself; あまり is
what that sentence needs.

### ちっとも～ない vs ～わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ちっとも` denies **the fact, at full strength**.
<code>わけではない</code> leaves the fact alone and denies **the conclusion someone
drew from it**.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)のやり方(かた)に<b>ちっとも</b>賛成(さんせい)でき<b>ない</b>。
I cannot agree with the way he does things at all.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)のやり方(かた)に賛成(さんせい)している<b>わけではない</b>。
It is not that I am endorsing the way he does things.
</pre>

The first shuts the door. The second keeps it open a crack: the speaker may well
have said something that sounded like approval, and is trimming back the
interpretation, not the facts. Reaching for `ちっとも` when you meant to soften is
one of the fastest ways to sound harsher than you intended.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この映画(えいが)は<b>ちっとも面白(おもしろ)かった</b>。 → ✅ この映画(えいが)は<b>ちっとも面白(おもしろ)くなかった</b>。
  `ちっとも` cannot sit in front of an affirmative. The negative is not optional
  decoration; it is what the word is holding on to.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は<b>ちっとも勉強(べんきょう)しませんでした</b>が、漢字(かんじ)は少(すこ)しやりました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は<b>あまり勉強(べんきょう)しませんでした</b>が…
  Zero admits no exceptions. If part of it did happen, you need あまり.

- ❌（報告書(ほうこくしょ)に）売上(うりあげ)は<b>ちっとも</b>伸(の)びて<b>いない</b>。 → ✅ 売上(うりあげ)は<b>全(まった)く</b>伸(の)びて<b>いない</b>。
  Grammatically fine, tonally wrong. In written business Japanese use
  全(まった)く or 少(すこ)しも.

- ❌ 文句(もんく)を言(い)っている<b>ちっともではない</b>。 → ✅ 文句(もんく)を言(い)っている<b>わけではない</b>。
  `ちっとも` is an adverb, not a sentence-ending form. It cannot cancel an
  inference; only <code>わけではない</code> does that job.

## Practice

**1.** 一週間(いっしゅうかん)練習(れんしゅう)したのに、____上手(じょうず)にならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一週間(いっしゅうかん)練習(れんしゅう)したのに、<b>ちっとも</b>上手(じょうず)にならない。

The のに clause has already set up an expectation that was let down, which is
exactly the environment `ちっとも` lives in. 全然(ぜんぜん) or 少(すこ)しも would
also be grammatical; `ちっとも` is the one that sounds like the speaker is fed up.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しくて、（ちっとも・あまり）休(やす)めなかった。昼(ひる)に十分(じゅっぷん)だけ座(すわ)ったが。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>あまり</b>休(やす)めなかった。

Ten minutes of sitting down is not nothing, and `ちっとも` claims nothing at all.
`あまり` scales the amount down without taking it to zero, so it can live alongside
the が clause that admits the exception.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I have read it three times and I still do not understand it at all."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三回(さんかい)読(よ)んだが、<b>ちっとも</b>分(わ)から<b>ない</b>。

"At all" after an effort that should have worked is the `ちっとも` situation. Note
that the negative goes on the verb - `ちっとも` itself never conjugates.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この説明(せつめい)を読(よ)んでも、ちっともわかるでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この説明(せつめい)を読(よ)んでも、<b>ちっともわかりませんでした</b>。

Two things went wrong: <code>わかる</code> needs a polite past **negative**
（わかりませんでした）, and `ちっとも` requires that negative to be there at all.
<code>わかるでした</code> is not a form of anything.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 寒(さむ)くないですか。- いえ、<b>ちっとも</b>寒(さむ)く<b>ないです</b>。
(b) 寒(さむ)くないですか。- いえ、<b>べつに</b>寒(さむ)く<b>ないです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) answers the question head-on and warmly: there is no cold here whatsoever, so
please stop worrying. It reassures.

(b) says there is nothing about the temperature worth remarking on. It answers a
question the speaker did not think needed asking, and delivered flatly it can sound
like a shrug - which is why べつに so easily reads as sulky and `ちっとも` does not.

</details>

# まったく～ない

**Meaning** · not at all; not in the slightest - the neutral, all-register absolute negative

全(まった)く is the word you reach for when you want a negative to be
**complete** and you do not want the sentence to sound casual. It works in speech,
in email, in a legal notice and in an academic paper alike, which is what separates
it from ちっとも. Written 全(まった)く or in kana as まったく; both are common,
and the kana spelling is a little softer on the eye in informal writing.

**What is denied: the proposition, in full.** 全(まった)く does not touch an
inference and it does not carve out exceptions. Where
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> says "not always" and
<code>わけではない</code> says "that does not follow", 全(まった)く～ない says
"none of this, at any point". If you find yourself wanting to add "well, except…",
you have chosen the wrong word.

Be warned early: this same adverb has a second job in affirmative sentences, where
it means "entirely". That double life is the whole difficulty of the word.

## Formation

全(まった)く is an adverb and never changes shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | 全(まった)く + ない-form | 知(し)る → 全(まった)く知(し)らない |
| Verb (polite) | 全(まった)く + ません | 覚(おぼ)える → 全(まった)く覚(おぼ)えていません |
| い-adjective | 全(まった)く + ～くない | 痛(いた)い → 全(まった)く痛(いた)くない |
| な-adjective・Noun | 全(まった)く + ～ではない | 平気(へいき) → 全(まった)く平気(へいき)ではない |
| Existence | 全(まった)く + ない／ありません | 興味(きょうみ) → 興味(きょうみ)が全(まった)くない |
| Intensified, formal | 全(まった)くもって + negative | - → 全(まった)くもって理解(りかい)できない |
| **Affirmative** sense | 全(まった)く + 同(おな)じ／違(ちが)う／逆(ぎゃく)／その通(とお)り | 同(おな)じ → 全(まった)く同(おな)じだ |
| **Prenominal** sense | 全(まった)くの + Noun | 偶然(ぐうぜん) → 全(まった)くの偶然(ぐうぜん) |
| Interjection | 全(まった)く（もう） | - → 全(まった)く、困(こま)った人(ひと)だ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the affirmative rows are not a different word. 全(まった)く means
"to the full extent" in every row; a negative predicate simply takes that extent
down to zero. But the two readings pull sentences apart in practice, so the
Contrast section below is worth reading twice.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
意味(いみ)が<b>全(まった)く</b>分(わ)から<b>ない</b>。
I do not understand the meaning at all.
</pre>

<pre>
その人(ひと)のことは<b>全(まった)く</b>知(し)り<b>ません</b>。
I do not know that person at all.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の前後(ぜんご)のことは<b>全(まった)く</b>覚(おぼ)えて<b>いない</b>。
I have no memory whatsoever of what happened around the accident.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが、その分野(ぶんや)には<b>全(まった)く</b>自信(じしん)が<b>ございません</b>。
I am sorry, but I have no confidence in that field at all.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の件(けん)について、会社(かいしゃ)からは<b>全(まった)く</b>説明(せつめい)が<b>ない</b>。
The company has offered no explanation at all regarding this matter.
</pre>

<pre>
検査(けんさ)の結果(けっか)、異常(いじょう)は<b>全(まった)く</b>認(みと)められ<b>ませんでした</b>。
The examination found no abnormality of any kind.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)ぶりに会(あ)ったのに、彼女(かのじょ)の話(はな)し方(かた)は<b>全(まった)く</b>変(か)わって<b>いなかった</b>。
We had not met for ten years, and yet the way she spoke had not changed in the slightest.
</pre>

<pre>
この規則(きそく)は現場(げんば)の事情(じじょう)を<b>全(まった)く</b>考慮(こうりょ)して<b>いない</b>と言(い)わざるを得(え)ない。
One is forced to say that this rule takes no account whatsoever of conditions on the ground.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

全(まった)く is the register-neutral choice in this family. It is equally at home
in 「全(まった)く知(し)らなかった」 between friends and in
「全(まった)く該当(がいとう)いたしません」 in a formal reply. That breadth is
its main practical advantage: when you are unsure whether ちっとも is too casual for
the setting, 全(まった)く is never wrong.

全(まった)くもって is a stiffer, more emphatic variant that belongs to formal
speech and writing - 全(まった)くもって同感(どうかん)です, "I could not agree
more". It is not archaic, but it is noticeably weighty, and overusing it sounds
theatrical.

Then there is the bare interjection. 「全(まった)く。」 or
「全(まった)くもう。」 on its own is an exasperated "honestly!", muttered about
someone who has done something annoying. Learners meet it in drama subtitles and
misread it as agreement. It leans casual, and it is one of those words a listener
can hear as either affectionate exasperation or genuine annoyance depending
entirely on delivery.

Finally, 「全(まった)くです」 is a set reply meaning "quite so, absolutely" - an
affirmative, agreeing with what the other person just said. It has no negative in
it at all.

## Contrast

### 全(まった)く～ない vs 全(まった)く + affirmative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 全(まった)く means "100%". Put a negative after it and the
100% lands on the negation ("not at all"); put an affirmative after it and the 100%
lands on the adjective ("completely"). Nothing else changes.

</div>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)の意見(いけん)は<b>全(まった)く違(ちが)う</b>。
Their two opinions are completely different.
</pre>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)の意見(いけん)は<b>全(まった)く</b>違(ちが)わ<b>ない</b>。
Their two opinions do not differ in the slightest.
</pre>

The affirmative use is not free: 全(まった)く happily modifies
同(おな)じ, 違(ちが)う, 逆(ぎゃく), 別(べつ), 新(あたら)しい and その通(とお)り,
but it is not a general-purpose "very". ❌<code>全(まった)く速(はや)い</code>
is not Japanese.

The trap worth memorising is
<code>全(まった)く同(おな)じではない</code>. It reads as "not *exactly* the
same" - the 100% attaches to 同(おな)じ and then that is denied - and **not** as
"not the same at all". If you mean the latter, say
<code>全(まった)く違(ちが)う</code>.

### 全(まった)く vs ちっとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning in the negative; 全(まった)く is
**register-free**, ちっとも is **spoken and complaining**.

</div>

<pre>
昨年度(さくねんど)の指摘(してき)は<b>全(まった)く</b>改善(かいぜん)され<b>ていない</b>。
The points raised last year have not been addressed at all.
</pre>

<pre>
言(い)ったのに<b>ちっとも</b>直(なお)し<b>てくれない</b>んだよ。
I told them, and they will not fix a single thing.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences sound wrong for their setting - the first becomes a
grumble in a document, the second becomes oddly official between friends.
少(すこ)しも sits between the two: usable in writing, slightly more emphatic than
全(まった)く.

### 全(まった)く～ない vs 必(かなら)ずしも～とは限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 全(まった)く～ない denies the claim **everywhere**.
必(かなら)ずしも～とは限(かぎ)らない denies only that it holds **every time**.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)と品質(ひんしつ)は<b>全(まった)く</b>関係(かんけい)が<b>ない</b>。
Price and quality have nothing to do with each other.
</pre>

<pre>
高(たか)いものが必(かなら)ずしもよい<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Expensive things are not always good.
</pre>

The first is a strong, arguable claim about the world; the second concedes the
general tendency and only removes the word "always". Learners who reach for
全(まった)く when they mean "not necessarily" end up asserting far more than they
believe - and then have to defend it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いたが、<b>全(まった)く分(わ)かる</b>。 → ✅ <b>全(まった)く分(わ)からない</b>。
  In the "not at all" sense the negative is compulsory. Without it the sentence
  reads as an odd attempt at "I completely understand", for which you want
  <code>よく分(わ)かりました</code>.

- ❌ この二(ふた)つは<b>全(まった)く同(おな)じではない</b>。（meaning "these are nothing alike"） → ✅ この二(ふた)つは<b>全(まった)く違(ちが)う</b>。
  全(まった)く同(おな)じではない means "not quite identical", which is nearly
  the opposite of what was intended.

- ❌ <b>全(まった)くの知(し)らない人(ひと)</b>に声(こえ)をかけられた。 → ✅ <b>全(まった)く知(し)らない人(ひと)</b>／<b>全(まった)くの他人(たにん)</b>に声(こえ)をかけられた。
  <code>全(まった)くの</code> attaches to a bare noun. In front of a verb
  clause the adverb stays bare.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は<b>全(まった)く急(いそ)いだ</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は<b>全力(ぜんりょく)で急(いそ)いだ</b>。
  全(まった)く is not a general intensifier for verbs. It scales a negation, or
  it modifies a small set of words like 同(おな)じ and 違(ちが)う.

## Practice

**1.** 会議(かいぎ)で反対(はんたい)した人(ひと)は____いませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会議(かいぎ)で反対(はんたい)した人(ひと)は<b>全(まった)く</b>いませんでした。

A meeting report calls for the register-free adverb. ちっとも would be too
conversational here, and 一人(ひとり)も is also good if you want to count heads
rather than deny the whole category.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 安(やす)い店(みせ)の料理(りょうり)が（全(まった)くおいしくない・必(かなら)ずしもおいしいとは限(かぎ)らない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

安(やす)い店(みせ)の料理(りょうり)が<b>必(かなら)ずしもおいしいとは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

The sentence is about a general tendency having exceptions, not about cheap food
being uniformly bad. 全(まった)くおいしくない would be a blanket claim that every
cheap restaurant is inedible.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I had absolutely no idea that he had quit."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)が辞(や)めたことを<b>全(まった)く</b>知(し)り<b>ませんでした</b>。

"Absolutely no idea" is the total-negation slot, and 全(まった)く fits any
politeness level. In casual speech
<code>全然(ぜんぜん)知(し)らなかった</code> is the everyday equivalent.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)と私(わたし)の記憶(きおく)は全(まった)くの違(ちが)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)と私(わたし)の記憶(きおく)は<b>全(まった)く違(ちが)う</b>。

<code>全(まった)くの</code> is the prenominal shape and needs a noun after it
（全(まった)くの別人(べつじん)）. 違(ちが)う is a verb, so the plain adverb
全(まった)く is what modifies it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の意見(いけん)に<b>全(まった)く</b>賛成(さんせい)でき<b>ない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)の意見(いけん)に賛成(さんせい)できる<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat refusal covering the whole of his position. There is no part of it
the speaker accepts, and the sentence invites a counter-argument.

(b) does not refuse anything yet. It says agreement is not guaranteed - the speaker
might agree, might not, and is warning the listener against assuming. In a meeting
(a) opens a fight and (b) reserves a position.

</details>

# 少しも～ない

**Meaning** · not one bit; not even a little - denies the smallest amount anyone could have expected

少(すこ)しも is 少(すこ)し ("a little") with `も` clamped on the end, and that
`も` is what forces the negative. The literal shape is "not even a little", and the
force of the sentence comes from how modest the amount being denied is: the speaker
picks the smallest quantity imaginable and says even *that* did not happen.

**What is denied: the quantity, at its lowest setting.** This matters when you set
it beside the N3 pair. <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> denies that a rule holds
every time; <code>わけではない</code> denies an inference. 少(すこ)しも denies the
thing itself, and does it by ruling out even the token amount that would have let
the claim survive.

## Formation

少(すこ)しも is an adverb. It does not conjugate, it goes before the predicate,
and the predicate is negative.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | 少(すこ)しも + ない-form | 変(か)わる → 少(すこ)しも変(か)わらない |
| Verb (polite) | 少(すこ)しも + ません | 触(ふ)れる → 少(すこ)しも触(ふ)れませんでした |
| い-adjective | 少(すこ)しも + ～くない | 痛(いた)い → 少(すこ)しも痛(いた)くない |
| な-adjective・Noun | 少(すこ)しも + ～ではない | 平気(へいき) → 少(すこ)しも平気(へいき)ではない |
| Existence | 少(すこ)しも + ない | 余裕(よゆう) → 余裕(よゆう)が少(すこ)しもない |
| Literary | 少(すこ)しも + ～ず | 動(うご)く → 少(すこ)しも動(うご)かず |
| Same family, counted | 一(ひと)つも／一人(ひとり)も／何(なに)も + negative | 残(のこ)る → 一(ひと)つも残(のこ)っていない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `も` is not decoration. Drop it and the sentence stops being
a denial of everything and becomes a statement about a small amount. Every member
of this family - 一(ひと)つも, 一人(ひとり)も, 何(なに)も, 決(けっ)して -
behaves the same way: quantity word plus `も` plus a negative.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>変(か)わら<b>ない</b>。
He has not changed one bit.
</pre>

<pre>
傷(きず)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>痛(いた)く<b>ない</b>。
The cut does not hurt at all.
</pre>

<pre>
叱(しか)られても、妹(いもうと)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>気(き)にして<b>いない</b>。
My little sister gets told off and it does not bother her in the slightest.
</pre>

<pre>
三日(みっか)も降(ふ)り続(つづ)いたのに、川(かわ)の水(みず)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>増(ふ)えて<b>いない</b>。
It rained for three solid days and the river has not risen a bit.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いても、疑問(ぎもん)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>解(と)け<b>なかった</b>。
However many times I heard the explanation, it did not resolve my doubts in the least.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げの理由(りゆう)について、担当者(たんとうしゃ)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>触(ふ)れ<b>ませんでした</b>。
The person in charge said nothing whatsoever about the reason for the price rise.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りが騒(さわ)いでいても、祖父(そふ)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>動(どう)じ<b>ない</b>人(ひと)だった。
However much fuss went on around him, my grandfather was a man who never turned a hair.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)前(まえ)の報告書(ほうこくしょ)を読(よ)み返(かえ)したが、状況(じょうきょう)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>改善(かいぜん)され<b>ていなかった</b>。
I reread the report from ten years ago, and the situation had not improved in any degree at all.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

少(すこ)しも is comfortable in both speech and writing. It is a shade more
emphatic than 全(まった)く and noticeably more careful than ちっとも, so it fits
a written argument, a formal complaint or a considered spoken remark. Nothing about
it is old-fashioned, though the bare literary form
<code>少(すこ)しも動(うご)かず</code> does belong to narration rather than
conversation.

What gives the word its colour is the implied concession. Because the speaker has
gone down to "even a little", the sentence quietly acknowledges that a little would
have been reasonable to expect. 「少(すこ)しも悪(わる)いと思(おも)っていない」
is therefore sharper than 「全(まった)く悪(わる)いと思(おも)っていない」: it
says not even a flicker of remorse, when a flicker was the minimum anyone was
hoping for.

It also appears in a softer, concessive shape that learners often misparse:
<code>少(すこ)しも～ないわけではない</code>, "it is not as though there is none at
all". That is two denials stacked, and it comes out as a grudging admission - the
same territory as なくはない.

## Contrast

### 少(すこ)しも vs 少(すこ)し

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 少(すこ)し is a quantity. 少(すこ)しも is a **denial of any
quantity**. The `も` is the whole difference.

</div>

<pre>
この文章(ぶんしょう)は<b>少(すこ)し</b>分(わ)かりにくい。
This passage is a little hard to follow.
</pre>

<pre>
この文章(ぶんしょう)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>分(わ)から<b>ない</b>。
I cannot make out a word of this passage.
</pre>

Putting 少(すこ)し directly in front of a negative does not produce "not much";
it produces something that sounds unfinished, because 少(すこ)し wants to measure
a positive amount. If you want "not much", the word is あまり.

### 少(すこ)しも vs 全(まった)く

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both reach zero. 全(まった)く measures **the whole**;
少(すこ)しも measures **the smallest part**, so it sounds more insistent when the
listener expected at least something.

</div>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は事件(じけん)のことを<b>全(まった)く</b>話(はな)さ<b>なかった</b>。
She said nothing at all about the incident.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は事件(じけん)のことを<b>少(すこ)しも</b>話(はな)そうとし<b>なかった</b>。
She would not talk about the incident even a little.
</pre>

In most sentences either works and the difference is emphasis. Where they part is
the affirmative side: 全(まった)く has a second life in
<code>全(まった)く同(おな)じ</code> and <code>全(まった)くの偶然(ぐうぜん)</code>,
while 少(すこ)しも has none. 少(すこ)しも is negative-only, always.

### 少(すこ)しも～ない vs ～なくはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 少(すこ)しも～ない is **zero**. ～なくはない is **a reluctant
minimum**, which is what you say when zero would be a lie.

</div>

<pre>
興味(きょうみ)は<b>少(すこ)しもない</b>。
I have no interest in it whatsoever.
</pre>

<pre>
興味(きょうみ)が<b>なくはない</b>が、時間(じかん)がない。
It is not that I have no interest - I just do not have the time.
</pre>

These are the two ends of the same scale, and choosing wrongly is socially
expensive. The first closes a topic; the second keeps it alive while declining. Say
少(すこ)しもない to a colleague's proposal and you have refused the person as well
as the idea.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 事情(じじょう)は<b>少(すこ)しも分(わ)かる</b>。 → ✅ 事情(じじょう)は<b>少(すこ)しも分(わ)からない</b>。
  少(すこ)しも cannot stand in front of an affirmative at all. Unlike
  全然(ぜんぜん), it has no colloquial escape hatch.

- ❌ 注意(ちゅうい)されても<b>少(すこ)し気(き)にしていない</b>。 → ✅ <b>少(すこ)しも気(き)にしていない</b>。
  Without `も` the adverb tries to measure an amount of not-caring, and the sentence
  falls apart. The `も` is what licenses the negative.

- ❌ ゆうべは<b>少(すこ)しも眠(ねむ)れなかった</b>が、二時間(にじかん)は寝(ね)た。 → ✅ ゆうべは<b>あまり眠(ねむ)れなかった</b>が、二時間(にじかん)は寝(ね)た。
  Two hours is not zero. 少(すこ)しも rules out even a token amount, so it cannot
  be walked back in the next clause.

- ❌ <b>少(すこ)しもの問題(もんだい)もない</b>。 → ✅ <b>何(なん)の問題(もんだい)もない</b>。
  少(すこ)しも is an adverb and never takes の to modify a noun. For "not the
  slightest X", the noun frame is <code>何(なん)の + Noun + も + negative</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 一時間(いちじかん)話(はな)し合(あ)ったが、意見(いけん)の差(さ)は____縮(ちぢ)まらなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

意見(いけん)の差(さ)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>縮(ちぢ)まらなかった。

An hour of talking is exactly the effort that should have narrowed the gap by at
least a little, and 少(すこ)しも denies even that minimum. 全(まった)く would
also be correct and slightly flatter in tone.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この案(あん)に（少(すこ)しも賛成(さんせい)できない・賛成(さんせい)できなくはない）が、もう少(すこ)し時間(じかん)がほしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この案(あん)に<b>賛成(さんせい)できなくはない</b>が、もう少(すこ)し時間(じかん)がほしい。

The が clause asks for more time, which only makes sense if some agreement is
possible. 少(すこ)しも賛成(さんせい)できない refuses outright, and the second

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I apologised twice, and he did not soften one bit."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

二回(にかい)謝(あやま)ったのに、彼(かれ)の態度(たいど)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>変(か)わら<b>なかった</b>。

"One bit" after a genuine effort is the 少(すこ)しも slot. のに carries the sense
that the apologies should have had some effect, which is what makes the adverb bite.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの人(ひと)は少(すこ)しもの遠慮(えんりょ)がない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの人(ひと)は<b>少(すこ)しも遠慮(えんりょ)がない</b>。（or あの人(ひと)には<b>何(なん)の遠慮(えんりょ)もない</b>）

少(すこ)しも modifies the predicate, not the noun, so の has no business there.
Keep the adverb bare and let it reach the negative <code>ない</code> at the end.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は<b>少(すこ)しも</b>悪(わる)いと思(おも)って<b>いない</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)が悪(わる)いと思(おも)っている<b>わけではない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about *his* state of mind and it is an accusation: not a flicker of remorse,
when a flicker was the least anyone expected. The smallness of 少(すこ)し is what
makes it cutting.

(b) is about *the speaker's* position, and it withdraws an inference rather than a
fact. Something the speaker said sounded like blame, and this sentence takes the
blame reading back without denying whatever happened.

</details>

# ろくに～ない

**Meaning** · hardly at all; not properly - denies that the thing was done to any decent standard, and disapproves while doing it

`ろくに` comes from <code>ろく</code>, an old word meaning "level, proper, decent".
So <code>ろくに食(た)べていない</code> is not simply "has not eaten much" - it is
"has not eaten anything you could call a meal". The word carries a judgement in it:
somebody skimped, or was prevented from doing the thing properly, and the speaker
minds.

**What is denied: the adequacy of the action.** 少(すこ)しも and 全(まった)く
deny a quantity outright; `ろくに` denies that whatever quantity there was reached a
reasonable standard. That is why a small amount can still be there - the sentence
is not claiming zero, it is claiming *not enough to count*.

## Formation

`ろくに` is an adverb and takes a negative predicate. It modifies **actions and
abilities**, not degrees, so it does not sit in front of an adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | ろくに + ない-form | 食(た)べる → ろくに食(た)べない |
| Verb (polite) | ろくに + ません | 休(やす)む → ろくに休(やす)んでいません |
| Ongoing state | ろくに + ～ていない | 寝(ね)る → ろくに寝(ね)ていない |
| Potential | ろくに + ～ない (potential) | 話(はな)す → ろくに話(はな)せない |
| Object marked with も | ろくに + Noun + も + negative | 挨拶(あいさつ) → ろくに挨拶(あいさつ)もしない |
| Before a second clause | ろくに + ～ないで／～ずに | 調(しら)べる → ろくに調(しら)べずに |
| Prenominal cousin | ろくな + Noun + negative | もの → ろくなものがない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `ろくに` and <code>ろくな</code> are the same word wearing two hats.
`ろくに` modifies the verb（ろくに働(はたら)かない）; <code>ろくな</code> modifies
a noun（ろくな仕事(しごと)がない）. Both need the negative at the end of the
sentence; neither can appear in an affirmative one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は<b>ろくに</b>寝(ね)<b>ていない</b>。
I barely slept last night.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しくて<b>ろくに</b>食(た)べ<b>ていません</b>。
I have been so busy I have hardly eaten a proper meal.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>ろくに</b>挨拶(あいさつ)も<b>しない</b>で出(で)て行(い)った。
He left without so much as a decent goodbye.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい上司(じょうし)は現場(げんば)を<b>ろくに</b>見(み)<b>ないで</b>決(き)めてしまう。
The new manager makes decisions without properly looking at how things work on the floor.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)したばかりで、近所(きんじょ)の人(ひと)と<b>ろくに</b>話(はな)した<b>ことがない</b>。
I have only just moved in, so I have barely spoken to the neighbours.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を<b>ろくに</b>読(よ)ま<b>ずに</b>組(く)み立(た)てたので、部品(ぶひん)が余(あま)ってしまった。
I put it together without reading the instructions properly, and ended up with parts left over.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)の時間(じかん)が<b>ろくに</b>取(と)れ<b>ないまま</b>、本番(ほんばん)の日(ひ)を迎(むか)えた。
The day of the performance arrived without our having had any real time to prepare.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は店員(てんいん)が足(た)りないらしく、質問(しつもん)しても<b>ろくに</b>答(こた)えて<b>もらえない</b>。
That shop seems short-staffed - ask a question and you will not get anything like a proper answer.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ろくに` is everyday spoken Japanese that also passes in ordinary writing: a news
article can say
<code>説明(せつめい)もろくにされていない</code> without lowering its tone. What it
cannot do is stay neutral. The word always evaluates, and the evaluation is
negative, so you cannot use it to report a fact you have no feelings about.

Two directions of complaint are both common. Aimed outward it criticises somebody:
<code>ろくに調(しら)べもしないで</code> is an accusation of carelessness, and it
is not gentle. Aimed at the speaker's own circumstances it becomes a lament:
<code>最近(さいきん)ろくに休(やす)めていない</code> asks for sympathy rather than
assigning blame. The grammar is identical; the target decides how it lands.

Because it judges, `ろくに` is a poor fit for formal correspondence directed at the
person being criticised. In a complaint letter you would be more likely to write
<code>十分(じゅうぶん)な説明(せつめい)がありませんでした</code>, which states
the same shortfall without the audible irritation.

The `も` variant - <code>ろくに返事(へんじ)もしない</code> - is worth learning as a
unit. Adding `も` to the object makes the criticism sharper, because it frames even
the object as a bare minimum that was not met.

## Contrast

### ろくに vs ちゃんと～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ちゃんと～ない</code> reports that something was **not done
properly**. `ろくに` says the same thing and adds that the speaker **thinks poorly of
it**.

</div>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を<b>ちゃんと</b>たたん<b>でいない</b>から、水(みず)が落(お)ちている。
The umbrella has not been closed properly, so it is dripping.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)は自分(じぶん)の机(つくえ)も<b>ろくに</b>片付(かたづ)け<b>ない</b>。
That man will not even keep his own desk in a decent state.
</pre>

There is also a plain grammatical difference: ちゃんと works in affirmative
sentences（ちゃんとたたんでね）and `ろくに` never does. If you want to praise, you
need ちゃんと or きちんと.

### ろくに vs あまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - あまり grades **how much**. `ろくに` grades **whether it counted
as doing it at all**.

</div>

<pre>
今週(こんしゅう)は<b>あまり</b>走(はし)っ<b>ていない</b>。
I have not run much this week.
</pre>

<pre>
今週(こんしゅう)は<b>ろくに</b>走(はし)っ<b>ていない</b>。
I have not managed anything like a proper run this week.
</pre>

The first is a neutral report and could easily be followed by 「まあいいか」. The
second is dissatisfied, and expects the listener to hear that dissatisfaction. When
you simply want to scale a quantity down, あまり is the safe choice.

### ろくに～ない vs ろくな～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ろくに` goes with a **verb**; <code>ろくな</code> goes with a
**noun**. Both still need the negative at the end.

</div>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に<b>ろくな</b>ものが入(はい)っ<b>ていない</b>。
There is nothing decent in the fridge.
</pre>

<pre>
冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)の中(なか)を<b>ろくに</b>確(たし)かめ<b>ないで</b>買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)った。
I went shopping without properly checking what was in the fridge.
</pre>

<code>ろくな</code> has a few near-fixed uses worth recognising:
<code>ろくな目(め)に遭(あ)わない</code> ("no good will come of it") and
<code>ろくな結果(けっか)にならない</code>. Both are ordinary spoken Japanese and
both are warnings.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は<b>ろくに寝(ね)た</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は<b>ろくに寝(ね)ていない</b>。
  `ろくに` exists to deny adequacy. With an affirmative verb there is nothing for it
  to deny, and the sentence is simply broken.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は<b>ろくに暖(あたた)かくない</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は<b>あまり暖(あたた)かくない</b>。
  `ろくに` grades an action, not a temperature. Adjectives take あまり or
  全(まった)く.

- ❌ この店(みせ)には<b>ろくにものがない</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)には<b>ろくなものがない</b>。
  In front of a noun the word is <code>ろくな</code>. Getting this backwards is the
  single most common slip with this pattern.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>ろくに準備(じゅんび)した</b>ので成功(せいこう)した。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>しっかり準備(じゅんび)した</b>ので成功(せいこう)した。
  The word cannot be turned around into praise. Its meaning includes the speaker's
  disapproval, so a positive outcome clause contradicts it.

## Practice

のに sets up the sense that studying was clearly called for, and `ろくに` says what
was done falls short of anything worth the name. あまり would fit grammatically but
would drain out the self-reproach.

</details>

The complaint is about the shops themselves, not about an action, so the word must
be the prenominal <code>ろくな</code>. If you wanted the adverb you would need a
verb for it to modify: <code>ろくに買(か)い物(もの)もできない</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 説明(せつめい)は<b>ろくに</b>されて<b>いない</b>。
(b) 説明(せつめい)が十分(じゅうぶん)だった<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) asserts a shortfall and is annoyed about it: whatever explaining happened did
not deserve the name. It accuses.

(b) makes no assertion about this case at all. It only removes the assumption that
the explanation was necessarily sufficient - a cautious, argumentative move that
leaves open the possibility that it was fine. In a written complaint (a) states
your grievance and (b) merely refuses to concede the point.

</details>

# なにも～ない

**Meaning** · there is no need to; there is no call for that - argues that a reaction is more than the situation deserves

At N5 you met <code>何(なに)も</code> plus a negative meaning "nothing at all":
<code>何(なに)も食(た)べていない</code>. This is a **different** use of the same
word, and it is the one that trips people at N2. Here <code>何(なに)も</code> does
not count things. It attaches to a whole clause that says an action is
*unnecessary*, and its job is to protest at the scale of what somebody is doing:
"there's no reason to go that far."

**What is denied: the necessity, not the fact.** Somebody is apologising, panicking,
resigning or paying too much, and the speaker does not deny that the trigger
happened. What they deny is that it obliges the response. That places this pattern
next to <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> and <code>わけではない</code> rather than
next to 少(すこ)しも: all three leave the facts alone and attack the step someone
took from them.

## Formation

<code>何(なに)も</code> is an adverb sitting at the front of the clause. The
clause itself must end in an expression of **unnecessity**; the word cannot carry
that meaning alone.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | 何(なに)も + dictionary form + ことはない | 急(いそ)ぐ → 何(なに)も急(いそ)ぐことはない |
| Verb | 何(なに)も + dictionary form + 必要(ひつよう)はない | 謝(あやま)る → 何(なに)も謝(あやま)る必要(ひつよう)はない |
| Verb | 何(なに)も + ない-form + なくてもいい | 泣(な)く → 何(なに)も泣(な)かなくてもいい |
| Whole proposition | 何(なに)も + … + わけではない | 反対(はんたい)している → 何(なに)も反対(はんたい)しているわけではない |
| Polite | … + ことはありません | 慌(あわ)てる → 何(なに)も慌(あわ)てることはありません |
| Softened ending | … + ことはないだろう／じゃない | 捨(す)てる → 何(なに)も捨(す)てることはないだろう |
| Regret about the past | … + ことはなかったのに | 買(か)う → 何(なに)も買(か)うことはなかったのに |
| Frequent partners | そんなに／そこまで／わざわざ／今(いま) | - → 何(なに)もそこまですることはない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at the end of the sentence, not at
<code>何(なに)も</code>. If the predicate is a bare negative
（食(た)べていない）it is the N5 "nothing" sense. If the predicate says the action
is unnecessary（ことはない・必要(ひつよう)はない・なくてもいい）it is this one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>何(なに)も</b>急(いそ)ぐ<b>ことはない</b>。
There is no need to rush.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>何(なに)も</b>泣(な)か<b>なくてもいい</b>よ。
There is nothing to cry about.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>何(なに)も</b>君(きみ)が謝(あやま)る<b>必要(ひつよう)はない</b>。
There is no call for you of all people to apologise.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>何(なに)も</b>そこまで怒(おこ)る<b>ことはない</b>でしょう。
Surely there is no need to get that angry about it.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)は来月(らいげつ)ですから、<b>何(なに)も</b>今日中(きょうじゅう)に覚(おぼ)える<b>ことはありません</b>。
The exam is next month, so there is no need to learn it all today.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)だし、<b>何(なに)も</b>わざわざ出(で)かける<b>ことはない</b>んじゃない?
It is raining - is there really any need to go out specially?
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)失敗(しっぱい)したからといって、<b>何(なに)も</b>辞(や)める<b>ことはない</b>と思(おも)います。
One failure is hardly a reason to quit, in my view.
</pre>

<pre>
近(ちか)くにも同(おな)じものがあったのに、<b>何(なに)も</b>あんな遠(とお)い店(みせ)まで買(か)いに行(い)く<b>ことはなかった</b>のに。
There was the same thing nearby - you really did not have to go all the way out to that shop for it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **spoken** pattern, and it is interpersonal: you use it because you are
watching somebody do too much. That gives it two very different tones depending on
who is speaking to whom.

Said kindly, it is reassurance. 「何(なに)も心配(しんぱい)することはないよ」
tells a worried friend to stand down, and it is warm. Said flatly to someone senior
to you, the same shape becomes presumptuous: you are judging their reaction as
excessive. In polite settings soften it with でしょう, と思(おも)います or
ではないでしょうか, and prefer
<code>ことはありません</code> to the blunt <code>ことはない</code>.

<code>ことはなかったのに</code> is the past version, and it is pure hindsight - part
sympathy, part reproach: "you needn't have". It is what you say when someone has
already spent the money, sent the apology, or thrown the thing away.

The adverbs it travels with are worth memorising as a set, because they do half the
work: そんなに, そこまで, わざわざ, 今(いま)すぐ. Each of them names the excess that
<code>何(なに)も</code> is objecting to.

One pronunciation note: in casual speech, especially in western dialects, you will
hear なんも for both senses of this word. It is dialectal, not standard, and worth
recognising rather than producing.

## Contrast

### 何(なに)も "no need" vs 何(なに)も "nothing"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the ending decides. Bare negative → **nothing**. Negated
necessity → **no need**.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)から<b>何(なに)も</b>食(た)べ<b>ていない</b>。
I have not eaten anything since this morning.
</pre>

<pre>
おなかがすいていないなら、<b>何(なに)も</b>食(た)べる<b>ことはない</b>よ。
If you are not hungry, there is no need to eat anything.
</pre>

In the first, <code>何(なに)も</code> is the object: nothing got eaten. In the
second it is not an object at all - it comments on the whole idea of eating, and the
sentence would still make sense with the word removed. That removability is a
reliable test.

### 何(なに)も～ことはない vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>なくてもいい</code> **grants permission** neutrally.
<code>何(なに)も～ことはない</code> **argues against** something already happening.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は来(こ)<b>なくてもいい</b>ですよ。
You do not have to come in tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>何(なに)も</b>熱(ねつ)があるのに来(く)る<b>ことはない</b>でしょう。
There is really no need to come in when you have a temperature.
</pre>

The first could be said out of nowhere, as information. The second responds to a
person who is already putting their coat on, and it contains an opinion about their
judgement. That is why the second can sting and the first cannot.

### 何(なに)も～ことはない vs 何(なに)も～わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことはない</code> denies that the **action** is needed.
<code>わけではない</code> denies that the **interpretation** is right.

</div>

<pre>
<b>何(なに)も</b>全部(ぜんぶ)捨(す)てる<b>ことはない</b>よ。
There is no need to throw all of it away.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>何(なに)も</b>全部(ぜんぶ)捨(す)てろと言(い)っている<b>わけではない</b>。
I am not saying throw all of it away.
</pre>

The first talks to the person holding the bin bag. The second defends the speaker
against a misreading of what they said a moment ago - the facts of the conversation
stand, and only the conclusion drawn from them is withdrawn. Mixing these up turns
a defence of yourself into an instruction to somebody else.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>何(なに)も心配(しんぱい)しない</b>。（intending "there's no need to worry"） → ✅ <b>何(なに)も心配(しんぱい)することはない</b>。
  With a bare negative the sentence reverts to the N5 meaning: "he does not worry
  about anything". The unnecessity has to be spelled out at the end.

- ❌ <b>何(なに)も怒(おこ)らないでください</b>。 → ✅ <b>何(なに)も怒(おこ)ることはないでしょう</b>。
  A plain request tells the person to stop. This pattern instead argues that the
  reaction outsizes the cause, and it needs the ことはない frame to do that.

- ❌ <b>何(なに)も全部(ぜんぶ)やれと言(い)っていることはない</b>。 → ✅ <b>何(なに)も全部(ぜんぶ)やれと言(い)っているわけではない</b>。
  <code>ことはない</code> comments on an action somebody might take.
  Denying what *you* meant is <code>わけではない</code> territory.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、<b>何(なに)もそんなに急(いそ)ぐことはない</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、<b>何(なに)もそんなにお急(いそ)ぎになることはないかと思(おも)います</b>。
  The judgement is fine; the bluntness is not. Upward, the pattern needs polite
  verb morphology and a hedged ending.

**1.** 時間(じかん)はまだたっぷりあるんだから、____あわてる____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The first clause establishes that there is plenty of time, so the speaker is
objecting to a reaction that does not fit the facts. <code>必要(ひつよう)はない</code>
would also work and sounds a shade more matter-of-fact.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Neither is a good reply, but the sense-test is the point: <b>何(なに)も食(た)べていない</b>
means "I have not eaten anything", which contradicts turning dinner down.
<b>何(なに)も食(た)べることはない</b> would be telling the *other* person not to

<code>大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)、おなかすいてないから</code>.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"Did not have to", looking back at something already done, is

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は何(なに)も会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)める必要(ひつよう)がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は<b>何(なに)も会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)める必要(ひつよう)はない</b>。

<code>何(なに)も</code> in this sense only lives in front of a **negated**

means the opposite of what the frame promises.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) 君(きみ)が行(い)く<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

*you* who goes, and the speaker is trying to get you out of it. It contains an
opinion and a mild defence of you.

(b) makes no such argument. It only refuses the assumption that you are always the
one who goes - a neutral, factual hedge that could appear in a scheduling
discussion. (a) is warm and personal; (b) is cool and general.

</details>

# ～なくはない

**Meaning** · it is not that I don't ～; I can, up to a point - a double negative that concedes the bare minimum

Two negatives cancel, but they do not cancel cleanly. <code>飲(の)めなくはない</code>
is not "I can drink"; it is "it is not the case that I cannot drink", which in
practice means *a little, if I have to, and do not make a thing of it*. The pattern
exists so that you can avoid saying no without saying a proper yes.

**What is denied: a total negative that somebody has assumed.** Compare the two N3
neighbours. <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> denies that a rule always holds;
<code>わけではない</code> denies an inference drawn from what was said. `なくはない`
denies **zero** - it answers the assumption "so you don't X at all, then" with "well,
not *none*". Almost always a が or けど clause follows, spelling out the reservation
that made the speaker hedge in the first place.

## Formation

The `ない` of an ordinary negative becomes `なく`, and `はない` is added on top.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ～ない → ～なくはない | 飲(の)まない → 飲(の)まなくはない |
| Verb (potential) | ～ない → ～なくはない | 行(い)けない → 行(い)けなくはない |
| い-adjective | ～くない → ～くなくはない | おいしくない → おいしくなくはない |
| な-adjective | ～で + なくはない | 便利(べんり)でない → 便利(べんり)でなくはない |
| Noun | Noun + で + なくはない | 問題(もんだい)でない → 問題(もんだい)でなくはない |
| ある | ない → なくはない | 不安(ふあん)がない → 不安(ふあん)がなくはない |
| Polite | ～なくはありません／なくはないです | 分(わ)かる → 分(わ)からなくはありません |
| Softer variants | ～なくもない／～ないでもない | 言(い)えない → 言(い)えなくもない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with な-adjectives and nouns the double negative gets clumsy fast
（静(しず)かでなくはない）, and Japanese speakers usually switch to
<code>静(しず)かでないわけではない</code> or simply rephrase. Verbs and potential
forms are where this pattern really lives.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
お酒(さけ)は飲(の)め<b>なくはない</b>。
It is not that I can't drink.
</pre>

<pre>
興味(きょうみ)が<b>なくはない</b>です。
It is not that I'm not interested.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)げば間(ま)に合(あ)わ<b>なくはない</b>が、タクシーを使(つか)うことになる。
We could just make it if we hurry, but it would mean taking a taxi.
</pre>

<pre>
辛(から)い料理(りょうり)は苦手(にがて)だが、食(た)べ<b>なくはない</b>。
Spicy food isn't really my thing, though I do eat it.
</pre>

<pre>
この値段(ねだん)なら買(か)え<b>なくはない</b>。ただ、置(お)く場所(ばしょ)がない。
At that price I could just about buy it. The problem is where to put it.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の言(い)い分(ぶん)も分(わ)から<b>なくはない</b>んですが、やはり順番(じゅんばん)が逆(ぎゃく)だと思(おも)います。
I do see his point, up to a point - but I still think he has done this in the wrong order.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)からでもお申(もう)し込(こ)みいただけ<b>なくはありません</b>が、お席(せき)は後(うし)ろのほうになります。
It is not impossible to apply even now, but your seat would be towards the back.
</pre>

<pre>
不安(ふあん)が<b>なくはない</b>ものの、この条件(じょうけん)であれば引(ひ)き受(う)けようと思(おも)います。
I do have some misgivings, but on these terms I am inclined to take it on.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`なくはない` is hedging equipment, and everything about how it feels follows from
that. It is polite, it is non-committal, and it buys the speaker room. In business
Japanese it is the standard way to say "possible, but I would rather not", and a
listener who knows the language hears the reluctance immediately - so it is not a
way of hiding your reservations, only of stating them courteously.

Register is broad. <code>なくはないです</code> and
<code>なくはありません</code> are fine in formal speech; the plain form is fine among
friends. What it is *not* is enthusiastic. Answering an invitation with
<code>行(い)けなくはない</code> is technically a yes and socially a shrug; if you
actually want to go, say so.

The variants shade the feeling. `なくもない` swaps the contrastive `は` for `も` and
comes out vaguer and more musing - the speaker is thinking aloud rather than
answering an accusation. <code>ないでもない</code> is the most literary of the three
and turns up more in writing than in speech. All three are grudging.

There is a real intonation cue here too: the sentence usually trails off, and the
listener is expected to supply the "but". Delivering
<code>分(わ)からなくはない</code> and then stopping is a complete social act -
partial agreement, plus an invitation to keep talking.

## Contrast

### ～なくはない vs ～ないことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - very close in meaning. <code>ないことはない</code> nominalises
the whole idea and sounds a little more deliberate; `なくはない` is tighter and
attaches straight to the negative.

</div>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)でもでき<b>ないことはない</b>。
It is not as though it can't be done single-handed.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)でもでき<b>なくはない</b>。
I could just about do it on my own.
</pre>

Honest answer: in most sentences either is fine and native speakers will accept
both. The tendency - and it is a tendency, not a rule - is that
<code>ないことはない</code> concedes that the *event is possible at all*, while
`なくはない` concedes a *small degree or frequency*, which is why `なくはない` sits so
naturally on habits: <code>食(た)べなくはない</code>, "I do eat it, sometimes".

### ～なくはない vs ～なくもない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` answers a challenge; `も` muses. Same grudging concession,
different posture.

</div>

<pre>
「全然(ぜんぜん)興味(きょうみ)ないんでしょ?」「興味(きょうみ)が<b>なくはない</b>よ。」
"You're not interested at all, are you." "It's not that I'm not interested."
</pre>

<pre>
そう言(い)われてみると、彼(かれ)の案(あん)のほうがいいような気(き)がし<b>なくもない</b>。
Now that you put it like that, I half feel his plan might be the better one.
</pre>

The `は` version pushes back against something the other person just said - the
contrastive `は` is doing exactly what it always does. The `も` version has no
opponent; the speaker is turning an idea over.

### ～なくはない vs ～わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくはない` denies **zero**. <code>わけではない</code> denies
**a conclusion**.

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)使(つか)わ<b>なくはない</b>が、週(しゅう)に二回(にかい)程度(ていど)です。
It is not that I never use it daily - but realistically twice a week.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)使(つか)っている<b>わけではない</b>。
It is not as though I use it every day.
</pre>

These face in opposite directions. The first starts from "you never use it" and
concedes upward; the second starts from "you use it constantly" and trims downward.
Choosing the wrong one leaves your listener with the opposite impression of how much
you use the thing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 「行(い)きませんか。」「はい、<b>行(い)けなくはないです</b>。」 → ✅ 「はい、ぜひ<b>行(い)きたいです</b>。」
  As an acceptance this is chilly. The double negative always implies a reservation,
  so it reads as "I suppose I could", not as a keen yes.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は<b>広(ひろ)いなくはない</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は<b>広(ひろ)くなくはない</b>。
  An い-adjective has to reach its ～くない shape first; only then does the second
  negative go on.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は<b>学生(がくせい)なくはない</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は<b>学生(がくせい)でなくはない</b>。（more naturally 学生(がくせい)でないわけではない）
  Nouns need `で` before the negative. In practice this shape is clumsy enough that
  most speakers rephrase.

- ❌ <b>全(まった)く興味(きょうみ)がなくはない</b>。 → ✅ <b>興味(きょうみ)がなくはない</b>。
  全(まった)く insists on zero and `なくはない` refuses zero, so the two cancel each
  other out. If you want a partner adverb, 少(すこ)しは or それなりに fits.

**1.** 「この仕事(しごと)、今日中(きょうじゅう)にできますか。」「できな____が、残業(ざんぎょう)になります。」

The が clause names the price, which is the classic environment for this pattern:

</details>

The が clause limits the frequency rather than denying the liking, so a flat

</details>

✅ 彼(かれ)の気持(きも)ちは<b>分(わ)からなくはない</b>。（or 少(すこ)しは分(わ)かる）

少(すこ)しも demands a full negative underneath it, and `なくはない` is the

against each other, so one of them has to go.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 手伝(てつだ)え<b>なくはない</b>。
(b) 手伝(てつだ)う<b>わけではない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a reluctant offer. Starting from the assumption that the speaker cannot help,
it concedes that help is possible - and the listener is meant to hear the unspoken
"but not gladly, and not for long".

(b) is not an offer at all. It cancels an idea the listener had formed, probably
from something the speaker said: no, helping is not what this amounts to. (a) moves
towards yes; (b) pushes an interpretation away.

</details>

# ～からといって

**Meaning** · just because ～ (that doesn't mean…) - accepts a reason and refuses the conclusion drawn from it

`からといって` is the hinge of this whole family. It takes a reason that is perfectly
true, holds it up, and then knocks down the step somebody took from it. The truth of
the first clause is never in dispute; what the sentence attacks is the *inference*.

**What is denied: the inference, and the sentence says so twice.** The `からといって`
clause flags that a piece of reasoning is coming, and the main clause then cancels
it - typically with <code>わけではない</code>, <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code>,
<code>ことにはならない</code> or <code>ものではない</code>. Learners who leave the
main clause affirmative produce a sentence that promises a rebuttal and never
delivers one, which is the single commonest fault with this pattern.

## Formation

It attaches to a **plain-form clause**. Nouns and な-adjectives need `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + からといって | 勝(か)った → 勝(か)ったからといって |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + からといって | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないからといって |
| い-adjective | plain form + からといって | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いからといって |
| な-adjective | ～だ + からといって | 得意(とくい)だ → 得意(とくい)だからといって |
| Noun | ～だ + からといって | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもだからといって |
| Casual speech | ～からって | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいからって |
| Literary | ～からとて | 若(わか)い → 若(わか)いからとて |
| Sentence opener | だからといって | - → だからといって、許(ゆる)されるものではない |
| Expected main clause | …わけではない／とは限(かぎ)らない／ことにはならない／ものではない | - → 安(やす)いからといって買(か)うわけではない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the main clause must **push back**. If the second half of your
sentence agrees with the first half, you wanted plain `から`, not `からといって`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>からといって</b>、中止(ちゅうし)にはならない。
Rain alone doesn't mean it's cancelled.
</pre>

<pre>
若(わか)い<b>からといって</b>、体力(たいりょく)があるとは限(かぎ)らない。
Being young doesn't necessarily mean being fit.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に住(す)んでいる<b>からといって</b>、敬語(けいご)が完璧(かんぺき)なわけではありません。
Living in Japan doesn't mean my honorific language is flawless.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しい<b>からって</b>、返事(へんじ)くらいはしてほしい。
I know you're busy, but the least you could do is reply.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)だ<b>からといって</b>、子(こ)どもの進路(しんろ)を勝手(かって)に決(き)めていいものではない。
Being the parent does not entitle you to decide a child's future for them.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が下(さ)がった<b>からといって</b>、すぐに飛(と)びつくのは危(あぶ)ない。
A drop in price is no reason to jump straight in.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)が改善(かいぜん)した<b>からといって</b>、問題(もんだい)が解決(かいけつ)したことにはならない。
An improvement in the figures does not amount to the problem being solved.
</pre>

<pre>
経験(けいけん)が長(なが)い<b>からといって</b>、必(かなら)ずしもよい指導者(しどうしゃ)になるとは限(かぎ)りません。
Long experience does not automatically make somebody a good mentor.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`からといって` itself is register-neutral: it is at home in an essay, a news
editorial, a meeting and a conversation. What moves with register is the contracted
form. `からって` is casual and very common in speech
（「疲(つか)れてるからって、八(や)つ当(あ)たりしないでよ」）; `からとて` is
literary and dated, the kind of thing you meet in an older novel or a deliberately
formal speech, not in a business email.

<code>だからといって</code> as a **sentence opener** is worth learning separately. It
picks up the previous sentence whole and objects to what it seems to license:
「確(たし)かに規則(きそく)は古(ふる)い。だからといって、守(まも)らなくていいということにはならない。」 In argumentative writing it is one of the most useful
connectives there is, and it is exactly the move an examiner is looking for.

There is a second, unrelated-feeling use you will meet in narration: `からといって`
reporting somebody's **stated** reason for doing something, with no rebuttal at all.
「頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いからといって、彼(かれ)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)った」
means "he went home early, saying he had a headache", and it often carries a faint
whiff of doubt about whether the reason was genuine. The `という` is doing its
ordinary quoting job there. Context separates the two easily: if the main clause is
an action somebody took, it is the reporting use; if the main clause is a denial, it
is the pattern this page is about.

Tonally, the rebuttal use can be sharp. Aimed at a person's excuse it is a
correction, and in a formal setting you would soften the ending -
<code>とは限(かぎ)らないのではないでしょうか</code> rather than a flat
<code>わけがない</code>.

## Contrast

### からといって vs から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` gives a reason **you are standing behind**. `からといって`
gives a reason **someone else is leaning on**, so that you can remove the weight.

</div>

<pre>
安(やす)かった<b>から</b>買(か)いました。
I bought it because it was cheap.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)い<b>からといって</b>買(か)うわけではありません。
I don't buy things simply because they're cheap.
</pre>

The first explains the speaker's own action; the second refuses a rule someone has
attributed to them. Swapping them produces the classic learner error:
❌<code>安(やす)いからといって買(か)いました</code> announces a rebuttal and then
supplies a plain fact.

### からといって vs から言(い)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one `と` apart, and opposite in direction.
<code>からといって</code> **rejects** what follows from the evidence;
<code>から言(い)って</code> **draws** a conclusion from it.

</div>

<pre>
この結果(けっか)<b>から言(い)って</b>、計画(けいかく)は成功(せいこう)したと言(い)えるでしょう。
Judging from these results, the plan can be called a success.
</pre>

<pre>
この結果(けっか)が良(よ)かった<b>からといって</b>、計画(けいかく)が成功(せいこう)したとは言(い)えません。
Good results here don't in themselves let us call the plan a success.
</pre>

Note the shapes as well as the meanings: <code>から言(い)って</code> follows a
**noun**（この結果(けっか)から）and belongs with からすると and から見(み)ると,
while `からといって` follows a **clause**. In handwriting and in fast reading the two
are easy to confuse, and they lead the reader in opposite directions.

### からといって vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` concedes a **condition**: even if that happens, this
holds. `からといって` concedes a **reason** and denies what was concluded from it.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>ても</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)います。
Even if it rains tomorrow, the match will go ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>からといって</b>、試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になるとは限(かぎ)りません。
The fact that it's raining doesn't necessarily mean the match is off.
</pre>

`ても` looks forward at a hypothetical; `からといって` looks back at reasoning
somebody has already done. That is why `ても` sits happily with an affirmative main
clause and `からといって` does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がなかった<b>からといって</b>、宿題(しゅくだい)を出(だ)さなかった。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がなかった<b>から</b>、宿題(しゅくだい)を出(だ)さなかった。
  A plain explanation of your own behaviour takes `から`. `からといって` promises to
  reject the reasoning and then never does.

- ❌ <b>学生(がくせい)からといって</b>、割引(わりびき)になるとは限(かぎ)らない。 → ✅ <b>学生(がくせい)だからといって</b>、割引(わりびき)になるとは限(かぎ)らない。
  Nouns and な-adjectives need `だ` in front of `からといって`. This is the same
  requirement as ordinary `から`.

- ❌ この数字(すうじ)<b>からといって</b>、来年(らいねん)も伸(の)びるだろう。 → ✅ この数字(すうじ)<b>から言(い)って</b>、来年(らいねん)も伸(の)びるだろう。
  With a bare noun and a forward-looking conclusion, the phrase you want is
  <code>から言(い)って</code>. `からといって` needs a clause and a denial.

- ❌（報告書(ほうこくしょ)に）新(あたら)しい<b>からって</b>、安全(あんぜん)だとは限(かぎ)らない。 → ✅ 新(あたら)しい<b>からといって</b>、安全(あんぜん)だとは限(かぎ)らない。
  `からって` is the spoken contraction. In written work restore the full form.

## Practice

**1.** 外国(がいこく)に住(す)んでいた____、その国(くに)の言葉(ことば)が話(はな)せるとは限(かぎ)らない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

外国(がいこく)に住(す)んでいた<b>からといって</b>、その国(くに)の言葉(ことば)が話(はな)せるとは限(かぎ)らない。

The second half already carries <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code>, so the first half
has to be the reason being rejected. Plain `から` would leave the sentence saying
"because I lived abroad, I can't necessarily speak the language", which is not
Japanese reasoning or English reasoning.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この成績(せいせき)（からといって・から言(い)って）、合格(ごうかく)は難(むずか)しいだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この成績(せいせき)<b>から言(い)って</b>、合格(ごうかく)は難(むずか)しいだろう。

The sentence draws a forecast *from* the marks, so it needs the "judging from"
phrase, which follows a bare noun. `からといって` would require a clause and a
rebuttal, neither of which is present.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Just because he apologised, that doesn't mean the matter is over."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)が謝(あやま)った<b>からといって</b>、この件(けん)が終(お)わったことにはならない。

"Just because… doesn't mean…" maps almost word for word:
<code>からといって</code> for the first half, and a cancelling ending for the second.
<code>わけではない</code> would also work and would sound slightly softer than
<code>ことにはならない</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 有名(ゆうめい)からといって、いい店(みせ)だとは限(かぎ)らない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ <b>有名(ゆうめい)だからといって</b>、いい店(みせ)だとは限(かぎ)らない。

有名(ゆうめい) is a な-adjective, so it needs `だ` before `からといって` -
exactly as it would before ordinary `から`. The rest of the sentence was already
correct.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 熱(ねつ)がある<b>からといって</b>、彼(かれ)は会議(かいぎ)を欠席(けっせき)した。
(b) 熱(ねつ)がある<b>からといって</b>、会議(かいぎ)を欠席(けっせき)していいわけではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the reporting use. The main clause is an action, so `からといって` is simply
quoting his stated reason: "he missed the meeting, saying he had a temperature."
Depending on delivery it can hint that the speaker is not fully convinced.

(b) is the rebuttal use. The main clause denies an entitlement, so the sentence
concedes the fever and refuses the conclusion: a temperature is not a licence to
skip the meeting. The first half is identical; the second half decides which pattern
you are reading.

</details>

# ～ことにはならない

**Meaning** · that doesn't amount to ～; that doesn't count as ～ - refuses to let one thing be treated as another

<code>ことになる</code> means "it works out that", "it comes to". Negate it with a
contrastive `は` and you get a refusal: whatever just happened, it does **not** come
to the thing somebody wants to call it. Paying the money back does not come to the
lie never having been told; not objecting does not come to agreeing.

**What is denied: the equation between the fact and the label.** This is the
sharpest tool in the batch, and the most argumentative. Where
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> concedes "usually, but not always" and
<code>わけではない</code> quietly withdraws an inference,
<code>ことにはならない</code> says the step is not available *at all* - not
sometimes, not usually, not in this case. It is the natural partner of
`からといって`, and the pair is the standard shape of a written rebuttal.

## Formation

The clause in front is in plain, noun-modifying form. Nouns and な-adjectives take
`という`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ことにはならない | 終(お)わった → 終(お)わったことにはならない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことにはならない | 払(はら)っていない → 払(はら)っていないことにはならない |
| い-adjective | plain form + ことにはならない | 正(ただ)しい → 正(ただ)しいことにはならない |
| な-adjective | ～だ + という + ことにはならない | 安全(あんぜん)だ → 安全(あんぜん)だということにはならない |
| Noun | ～だ + という + ことにはならない | 失敗(しっぱい) → 失敗(しっぱい)だということにはならない |
| Polite | … + ことにはなりません | 解決(かいけつ)した → 解決(かいけつ)したことにはなりません |
| Usual partner | ～からといって + … + ことにはならない | 勝(か)った → 勝(か)ったからといって強(つよ)いことにはならない |
| Set-up phrase | ～だけでは／～たとしても | 見(み)る → 見(み)ただけでは理解(りかい)したことにはならない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `は` is not optional decoration. It is the contrastive `は`
that marks the equation being refused. Without it,
<code>ことになりません</code> drifts towards "it will not be arranged that…", which
is a different sentence about schedules and decisions.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
黙(だま)っていても、許(ゆる)した<b>ことにはならない</b>。
Saying nothing does not amount to forgiving it.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)読(よ)んだだけでは、理解(りかい)した<b>ことにはならない</b>。
Reading it once does not count as understanding it.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)しなかったからといって、賛成(さんせい)した<b>ことにはならない</b>。
Not objecting does not amount to having agreed.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)を返(かえ)したとしても、嘘(うそ)をついた事実(じじつ)が消(き)えた<b>ことにはなりません</b>。
Even paying the money back does not make the fact of the lie go away.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)に書(か)かれていないからといって、何(なに)をしてもいい<b>ということにはならない</b>。
The rules not mentioning it does not mean anything goes.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)を交換(こうかん)しただけでは、原因(げんいん)を取(と)り除(のぞ)いた<b>ことにはならない</b>。
Simply swapping the part does not amount to removing the cause.
</pre>

<pre>
署名(しょめい)が集(あつ)まったとしても、それだけで市民(しみん)の総意(そうい)を示(しめ)した<b>ことにはなりません</b>。
Even a pile of signatures does not on its own constitute a demonstration of what the public wants.
</pre>

<pre>
多数(たすう)が選(えら)んだからといって、その判断(はんだん)が正(ただ)しい<b>ということにはならない</b>だろう。
That the majority chose it hardly establishes that the judgement was right.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This pattern belongs to **argument**. You meet it in editorials, in exam essays, in
courtroom drama, in a meeting where somebody is being held to a standard, and in the
kind of conversation where two people are working out what a fact does and does not
prove. It is not casual chat furniture; nobody says
<code>ことにはならない</code> about what to have for lunch.

Register is neutral-to-formal. The plain ending is fine in writing and in speech
among equals; <code>ことにはなりません</code> and
<code>ことにはならないでしょう</code> are what you would use in a meeting. Because the
force of it is absolute, softening the ending matters more here than with the other
members of the family: <code>とは思(おも)いません</code>,
<code>のではないでしょうか</code> and だろう all take the edge off a claim that would
otherwise sound like a verdict.

The set-up phrases are worth learning as a unit, because they are what makes the
sentence read naturally. <code>～だけでは</code> ("merely by ～"),
<code>～たとしても</code> ("even if ～") and `からといって` all announce that a
disqualification is coming. A reader who sees だけでは at the start is already
expecting <code>ことにはならない</code> at the end.

One genuinely useful move: the affirmative <code>ことになる</code> is common in the
same register with the opposite job - 「それでは嘘(うそ)をついた<b>ことになる</b>」,
"that would mean you had lied". Learning the pair together makes both easier, because
the negative is simply the affirmative with the equation refused.

## Contrast

### ～ことにはならない vs ～わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>わけではない</code> withdraws **an inference somebody
might draw**. <code>ことにはならない</code> denies that the fact **qualifies as** the
thing at all.

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)している<b>わけではない</b>。
It's not that I practise every day.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)したからといって、うまくなった<b>ことにはならない</b>。
Practising every day does not in itself amount to having improved.
</pre>

<code>わけではない</code> is defensive and quiet: it protects the speaker from being
misread. <code>ことにはならない</code> is on the attack: it rules out somebody else's
reasoning. The first is about what you meant, the second about what follows.

### ～ことにはならない vs ～とは限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> allows the outcome
**sometimes**. <code>ことにはならない</code> allows it **never** - the two things are
simply not the same thing.

</div>

<pre>
謝(あやま)れば許(ゆる)してもらえる<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Apologising doesn't guarantee you'll be forgiven.
</pre>

<pre>
謝(あやま)ったからといって、許(ゆる)された<b>ことにはならない</b>。
Having apologised does not amount to having been forgiven.
</pre>

The first is a prediction with exceptions: sometimes an apology works. The second is
a point about definitions: an apology is one act, forgiveness is another, and doing
the first never *constitutes* the second. In an exam this distinction is exactly what
the question is testing.

### ～ことにはならない vs ～ことになる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same word, opposite verdict.
<code>ことになる</code> grants the equation; <code>ことにはならない</code> refuses it,
with `は` marking the refusal.

</div>

<pre>
今(いま)断(ことわ)れば、約束(やくそく)を破(やぶ)った<b>ことになる</b>。
If you back out now, that means you'll have broken your promise.
</pre>

<pre>
事情(じじょう)を説明(せつめい)した以上(いじょう)、約束(やくそく)を破(やぶ)った<b>ことにはならない</b>。
Given that you explained the circumstances, this doesn't amount to breaking a promise.
</pre>

Watch the `は`. <code>ことになる</code> also has a wholly separate everyday sense -
"it has been decided that"（来月(らいげつ)転勤(てんきん)することになりました）-
which has nothing to do with this page. That sense practically never appears in the
<code>ことにはならない</code> shape, so the `は` is a reliable signal that you are
reading a rebuttal.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ それで<b>安全(あんぜん)ことにはならない</b>。 → ✅ それで<b>安全(あんぜん)だということにはならない</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns cannot sit straight in front of <code>こと</code> here.
  They need <code>だという</code> to turn the clause into something quotable.

- ❌ 謝(あやま)ったから、許(ゆる)された<b>ことにはならない</b>。 → ✅ 謝(あやま)った<b>からといって</b>、許(ゆる)された<b>ことにはならない</b>。
  Plain `から` asserts the reason; the pattern needs `からといって`, which offers the
  reason up precisely so it can be knocked down.

- ❌ 私(わたし)はその映画(えいが)が嫌(きら)いな<b>ことにはならない</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)はその映画(えいが)が嫌(きら)いな<b>わけではない</b>。
  Your own feelings are not a logical consequence of anything, so there is no
  equation to refuse. Denying an impression about yourself is
  <code>わけではない</code> work.

- ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)は会議(かいぎ)をする<b>ことにはならない</b>。（intending "the meeting won't be held"） → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)は会議(かいぎ)を<b>しないことになりました</b>。
  For decisions and arrangements, use plain <code>ことになる</code> without the
  contrastive `は`. With `は` the sentence reads as a rebuttal and puzzles the
  listener.

## Practice

**1.** 名前(なまえ)を書(か)いただけでは、申(もう)し込(こ)んだ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

名前(なまえ)を書(か)いただけでは、申(もう)し込(こ)んだ<b>ことにはならない</b>。

<code>～だけでは</code> at the front is a standing invitation for this ending: it
names an act that falls short, and the pattern says what the act does not add up to.
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> would wrongly suggest that writing your name
sometimes does count as applying.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 安(やす)く売(う)っている（からといって・から）、品質(ひんしつ)が悪(わる)いことにはならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

安(やす)く売(う)っている<b>からといって</b>、品質(ひんしつ)が悪(わる)いことにはならない。

The main clause is a rebuttal, so the reason clause has to be the one that offers
the reasoning up for rejection. Plain `から` would assert the price as the speaker's
own grounds, and the sentence would then contradict itself.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if the numbers go up, that does not mean the policy worked."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

数字(すうじ)が上(あ)がったとしても、政策(せいさく)がうまくいった<b>ことにはならない</b>。

<code>としても</code> concedes the fact and <code>ことにはならない</code> refuses the
conclusion drawn from it. Note that "does not mean" here is about what the rise
*establishes*, which is why <code>わけではない</code> would be too mild.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 払(はら)わなくても、問題(もんだい)が解決(かいけつ)したことになりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 払(はら)っただけでは、問題(もんだい)が解決(かいけつ)した<b>ことにはなりません</b>。

Two repairs. The `は` belongs in <code>ことにはなりません</code> - it is what marks
the equation as the thing being denied. And the first clause has to name the act that
falls short, so <code>払(はら)っただけでは</code> makes the argument the ending
expects.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 急(いそ)いで直(なお)したからといって、直(なお)った<b>ことにはならない</b>。
(b) 急(いそ)いで直(なお)したからといって、直(なお)った<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a claim about categories: a hurried repair is one thing and a working machine
is another, and the first never constitutes the second. The speaker is refusing to
let the repair be counted as a fix at all.

(b) is a claim about probability: a hurried repair may well have worked, and may not.
It leaves the door open and asks for a check. In a meeting (a) rejects the report;
(b) asks for evidence.

</details>

# ～というものでもない

**Meaning** · it's not necessarily so; that's not all there is to it - rejects a proposed principle as too simple

Somebody has put forward a rule for how things work - cheaper is better, more staff
means faster, an apology settles it - and `というものでもない` says that the rule does
not hold up. The `も` is what makes it courteous: "that is not *necessarily* how it
works either", rather than a flat contradiction.

**What is denied: a principle, not a fact.** This is the distinction that keeps this
page separate from its N3 neighbours.
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> denies that a generalisation holds every time;
<code>わけではない</code> withdraws an inference about a particular situation.
`というものでもない` attacks the *maxim itself* - the "all you have to do is X" way of
thinking - and says it is not an adequate account of the matter. That is why the
clause in front is nearly always a conditional: <code>～ばいい</code>,
<code>～たらいい</code>, <code>～ば～する</code>.

## Formation

A whole plain-form clause goes in front, and `という` quotes it as a proposition.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + というものでもない | 勝(か)てばいい → 勝(か)てばいいというものでもない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + というものでもない | 言(い)わない → 言(い)わなければいいというものでもない |
| い-adjective | plain form + というものでもない | 安(やす)ければいい → 安(やす)ければいいというものでもない |
| な-adjective | ～だ + というものでもない | 丁寧(ていねい)だ → 丁寧(ていねい)だというものでもない |
| Noun | ～だ + というものでもない | 一番(いちばん) → 一番(いちばん)だというものでもない |
| Polite | … + というものでもありません | 安全(あんぜん)だ → 安全(あんぜん)だというものでもありません |
| Casual speech | … + ってものでもない | 早(はや)ければいい → 早(はや)ければいいってものでもない |
| Flatter variant | … + というものではない | 多(おお)ければいい → 多(おお)ければいいというものではない |
| Usual set-up | ～ば／～たら／～なら + いい／～する | 増(ふ)やす → 増(ふ)やせば済(す)むというものでもない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you cannot phrase the thing you are rejecting as "all you
have to do is ～" or "the more ～ the better", this is probably not the pattern you
want. It answers a principle, not a report.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
安(やす)ければいい<b>というものでもない</b>。
Cheap isn't the whole story.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しければいい<b>というものではありません</b>。
Newer is not automatically better.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)の時間(じかん)が長(なが)ければ上達(じょうたつ)する<b>というものでもない</b>。
Longer hours of practice do not in themselves make you better.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)をかければいい作品(さくひん)になる<b>というものでもありません</b>。
Throwing money at it does not guarantee you a good piece of work.
</pre>

<pre>
人手(ひとで)を増(ふ)やせば作業(さぎょう)が早(はや)く終(お)わる<b>ってものでもない</b>よ。
Putting more people on it doesn't necessarily get the job done any quicker, you know.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)が長(なが)ければ親切(しんせつ)だ<b>というものでもない</b>でしょう。
A longer explanation is not necessarily a kinder one, surely.
</pre>

<pre>
敬語(けいご)は丁寧(ていねい)にすればするほどいい<b>というものでもありません</b>。
With honorific language, it is not a case of the more polite the better.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)を細(こま)かくすれば事故(じこ)がなくなる<b>というものでもない</b>と現場(げんば)の人(ひと)たちは言(い)っている。
The people on the ground say that finer-grained rules will not by themselves put an end to accidents.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **considered, slightly formal** pattern. It belongs to opinion writing,
discussion, advice and the kind of conversation where somebody is being gently told
that their thinking is thin. It is not aggressive - the `も` sees to that - but it is
unmistakably a disagreement, so it needs the same care as any disagreement.

The `も` is worth dwelling on. <code>というものではない</code>, with `は`, is a
straight denial: that is not how it is. <code>というものでもない</code>, with `も`,
adds "either" - it leaves the other person's rule standing as *one* consideration
among others while denying that it settles the matter. In practice the `も` version
is the safer thing to say to a colleague and the more common one in speech.

The contraction `ってものでもない` is casual and very natural in conversation
（「謝(あやま)ればいいってもんでもないでしょ」, with もん for もの）. Keep it out of
written work.

Because the pattern criticises a way of thinking rather than a person, it is
comfortable in polite disagreement. Ending with でしょう, と思(おも)います or
のではないでしょうか softens it further, and in a meeting that softening is close to
obligatory when you are contradicting someone senior.

## Contrast

### ～というものでもない vs ～とは限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> denies that a generalisation
holds **every time**. `というものでもない` denies that a stated principle is
**the right way to look at it**.

</div>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い店(みせ)がおいしい<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Expensive restaurants aren't always good.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)を上(あ)げれば質(しつ)が上(あ)がる<b>というものでもない</b>。
Raising the price is not in itself going to raise the quality.
</pre>

The first is about the world and could be settled with statistics: sometimes the
expensive place is bad. The second is about somebody's reasoning: they think price
is the lever, and the speaker says that is not how quality works. If you can imagine
counting the exceptions, you want <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code>.

### ～というものでもない vs ～というわけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `というものでもない` rejects a **general rule**.
<code>というわけではない</code> withdraws **an inference about the case in front of
you**.

</div>

<pre>
大(おお)きい会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)れば安心(あんしん)だ<b>というものでもない</b>。
Getting into a big company is no guarantee of security.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きい会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)りたい<b>というわけではありません</b>。
It's not that I particularly want to join a big company.
</pre>

The first is an argument about careers in general and could open an essay. The
second is about this speaker, right now, correcting an impression they have just
given. Note also that <code>というわけではない</code> follows what you or the other
person actually *said*, while `というものでもない` follows a hypothetical rule that may
never have been uttered aloud.

### ～というものでもない vs ～ものではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `というものでもない` says a **principle is inadequate**.
<code>ものではない</code> says an **action is not done** - a social norm.

</div>

<pre>
人(ひと)の失敗(しっぱい)を笑(わら)う<b>ものではない</b>。
You shouldn't laugh at other people's mistakes.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)しなければいい<b>というものでもない</b>。
Simply not failing is not the point either.
</pre>

They look alike on the page and do quite different jobs. <code>ものではない</code>
tells somebody off for what they are doing; `というものでもない` disagrees with what
somebody believes. The `という` is the tell: it quotes a proposition, and a
proposition is what gets rejected.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ない<b>というものでもない</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ない<b>というわけではない</b>。
  A statement about one person on one occasion is not a principle. There is nothing
  general here for the pattern to reject.

- ❌ この方法(ほうほう)は<b>便利(べんり)ものでもない</b>。 → ✅ この方法(ほうほう)が<b>便利(べんり)だというものでもない</b>。
  Nouns and な-adjectives need `だ` and then `という`. Without them the clause cannot
  be quoted, and <code>もの</code> has nothing to attach to.

- ❌（論文(ろんぶん)に）データが多(おお)ければいい<b>ってもんでもない</b>。 → ✅ データが多(おお)ければいい<b>というものでもない</b>。
  ってもん is spoken contraction. In an essay or report, write it out in full.

- ❌ 安(やす)い<b>というものでもない</b>から、買(か)わなかった。 → ✅ 安(やす)い<b>わけでもなかった</b>から、買(か)わなかった。
  Used as a plain report of a price, the pattern promises a principle it never
  supplies. The sentence is about this one shop, so it needs the わけ family.

**1.** 給料(きゅうりょう)が高(たか)ければいい仕事(しごと)だ____。

The first half is exactly the shape of a maxim - "if the pay is good, it's a good
job" - which is what this pattern exists to knock down. The `も` keeps the

</details>

The clause states a general rule with a conditional, so it is a principle being

</details>

something.

(b) is aimed at a specific inference. The apology happened, and the sentence rules
out treating it as the end of the matter. It is colder and more absolute - a verdict
on this case rather than a criticism of a way of thinking.

</details>

# ～っこない

**Meaning** · there's no chance of ～; ～ couldn't possibly happen - flat, spoken impossibility

`っこない` is what you say when the idea is not worth discussing. It bolts onto the
ます-stem of a verb and declares the thing impossible, with none of the hedging that
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> or <code>わけではない</code> carry. It is
conversational, it is emphatic, and it is often a little defeatist.

**What is denied: the possibility itself.** Set it against the rest of this batch and
the difference is stark. <code>わけではない</code> withdraws an inference;
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> removes the word "always"; `っこない` says the event
will not occur, full stop, and dares the listener to argue. Because it is so
absolute, its casualness is what keeps it socially survivable - it sounds like
grumbling, not like a formal verdict.

## Formation

It attaches to the **ます-stem** and to nothing else. No adjectives, no nouns.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | ます-stem + っこない | 分(わ)かる → 分(わ)かりっこない |
| る-verb | ます-stem + っこない | 覚(おぼ)える → 覚(おぼ)えっこない |
| Potential form | potential ます-stem + っこない | 勝(か)てる → 勝(か)てっこない |
| する | し + っこない | できる → できっこない |
| 来(く)る | 来(き) + っこない | 来(く)る → 来(き)っこない |
| ある | あり + っこない | ある → ありっこない |
| Polite | … + っこないです／っこありません | 届(とど)く → 届(とど)きっこありません |
| Common partner | そんなこと／なんて + … + っこない | できる → そんなことできっこない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verbs and る-verbs are also called 五段(ごだん) (godan, Group 1) and 一段(いちだん) (ichidan, Group 2). The
form you need is the one you would use before ます, so
走(はし)る → 走(はし)り, 食(た)べる → 食(た)べ. The two irregulars follow
the same rule: する → し, 来(く)る → 来(き). Note the reading -
<code>来(き)っこない</code>, not こ.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こんな問題(もんだい)、分(わ)かり<b>っこない</b>。
There's no way I'll get a question like this.
</pre>

<pre>
あのチームに勝(か)て<b>っこない</b>よ。
We haven't a hope against that team.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)から走(はし)っても、間(ま)に合(あ)い<b>っこない</b>。
Even if we run now, we're never going to make it.
</pre>

<pre>
宝(たから)くじなんて当(あ)たり<b>っこない</b>と思(おも)っていた。
I always assumed there was no chance of winning the lottery.
</pre>

<pre>
嘘(うそ)をついてもすぐにばれるんだから、うまくいき<b>っこない</b>。
Lying will be found out straight away, so it can't possibly work.
</pre>

<pre>
漢字(かんじ)を三日(みっか)で全部(ぜんぶ)覚(おぼ)えられ<b>っこない</b>よ。
There is no way you can learn all the kanji in three days.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)でこんな量(りょう)、運(はこ)べ<b>っこありません</b>。
I could not possibly carry this much on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに急(きゅう)に言(い)われても、準備(じゅんび)なんてでき<b>っこない</b>じゃないですか。
Sprung on us like that, there was no way we could have got ready, was there?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`っこない` is **spoken Japanese**, and it stays spoken even when you dress it up.
<code>っこありません</code> exists and is grammatical, but it keeps the flavour of
the plain form, so it lands as an emphatic, slightly emotional protest rather than a
neutral statement. In a report, an email to a client or an exam essay, write
<code>不可能(ふかのう)です</code>, <code>できるはずがありません</code> or
<code>考(かんが)えにくいです</code> instead.

It is neutral as to who says it - all ages and genders use it - but it is
*attitudinally* marked. Depending on the sentence it comes out as resignation
（「受(う)かりっこない」）, as dismissal（「そんな話(はなし)、ありっこない」）or as
protest（「できっこないじゃないですか」）. What it never is, is measured. Telling a
junior colleague 「君(きみ)にはできっこない」 is not a prediction, it is an insult.

It loves company. なんて, そんなこと and どうせ turn up with it constantly, because
they carry the same dismissive posture: 「どうせ勝(か)てっこない」, "we're going to
lose anyway".

A useful softener, if you want the same idea without the sting, is
<code>難(むずか)しいと思(おも)います</code>. Japanese conversation reaches for
that far more often than for a bare impossibility claim, and a learner who deploys
`っこない` at work will sound harsher than they realise.

## Contrast

### ～っこない vs ～はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>はずがない</code> reasons: given what I know, that cannot
be so. `っこない` asserts: that is simply not happening.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんが忘(わす)れる<b>はずがない</b>。いつも手帳(てちょう)に書(か)いているから。
Tanaka can't have forgotten - he writes everything in his diary.
</pre>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんが忘(わす)れ<b>っこない</b>って。
There's no way Tanaka forgot, come on.
</pre>

<code>はずがない</code> invites you to check the reasoning and works in writing;
`っこない` offers no reasoning and belongs to speech. There is also a plain
grammatical difference: <code>はずがない</code> follows a full plain-form clause and
accepts adjectives and nouns（静(しず)かなはずがない）, while `っこない` only ever
follows a verb stem.

### ～っこない vs ～わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - near-identical meaning. <code>わけがない</code> is
**register-flexible**; `っこない` is **spoken only**, and blunter.

</div>

<pre>
この値段(ねだん)で本物(ほんもの)の<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way this is genuine at this price.
</pre>

<pre>
この値段(ねだん)で本物(ほんもの)であり<b>っこない</b>。
No chance this is the real thing at this price.
</pre>

Both deny the possibility outright. <code>わけがない</code> can open a paragraph in
an opinion column; `っこない` cannot. When in doubt in writing, use
<code>わけがない</code> - it says the same thing and costs you nothing in force.

### ～っこない vs ～ようがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `っこない` says the outcome **will not happen**.
<code>ようがない</code> says there is **no method available** to bring it about.

</div>

<pre>
連絡先(れんらくさき)を知(し)らないので、知(し)らせ<b>ようがない</b>。
I don't have their contact details, so there's no way for me to let them know.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに遠(とお)くから、声(こえ)なんて聞(き)こえ<b>っこない</b>。
From that far away there's no chance of hearing a voice.
</pre>

<code>ようがない</code> is about missing means and is register-neutral; you would
write it in a business email without a second thought. `っこない` is about the
outcome being out of the question, and it stays in conversation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そんなところに<b>行(い)くっこない</b>。 → ✅ そんなところに<b>行(い)きっこない</b>。
  The base is the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. 行(い)く → 行(い)き.

- ❌ あの人(ひと)が<b>来(こ)っこない</b>。 → ✅ あの人(ひと)が<b>来(き)っこない</b>。
  来(く)る is irregular, and the ます-stem is 来(き). The こ- shape belongs to
  来(こ)ない and 来(こ)よう, not here.

- ❌ こんな話(はなし)が<b>本当(ほんとう)っこない</b>。 → ✅ こんな話(はなし)が<b>本当(ほんとう)のわけがない</b>。
  `っこない` cannot attach to a noun or an adjective. For those, the わけがない and
  はずがない families are what you need.

- ❌（報告書(ほうこくしょ)に）その方法(ほうほう)では問題(もんだい)は<b>解決(かいけつ)しっこありません</b>。 → ✅ その方法(ほうほう)では問題(もんだい)は<b>解決(かいけつ)できないと考(かんが)えられます</b>。
  Polite morphology does not make the word formal. In writing, state the
  impossibility with a neutral expression.

and said in his hearing it would be taken as an insult.

(b) merely refuses a guarantee. It concedes that he might well manage it and only
objects to the assumption that he certainly will - the kind of thing you can say in a
planning meeting without offending anyone.

</details>

# ～ものではない

**Meaning** · one shouldn't do that; it isn't the sort of thing that ～ - appeals to how things simply are

<code>もの</code> here is not a physical thing. It is "the nature of the case", and
`ものではない` says that what somebody is doing, or expecting, is at odds with that
nature. Out of this come the two readings you will meet: **you don't do that** (a
norm nobody had to legislate) and **it doesn't work like that** (something is not
achievable the easy way).

**What is denied: not a fact, but a fit.** The other members of this family attack a
claim or an inference. `ものではない` attacks the *appropriateness* of an action or
the *reasonableness* of an expectation. Nobody is being told they are factually
wrong; they are being told that this is not how the world goes.

## Formation

The verb in front is in the **dictionary form**. The norm reading does not take a
past tense.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (norm reading) | dictionary form + ものではない | 笑(わら)う → 笑(わら)うものではない |
| Verb (not-easily reading) | dictionary form + ものではない | 変(か)わる → 変(か)わるものではない |
| Potential verb | potential + ものではない | 覚(おぼ)えられる → 覚(おぼ)えられるものではない |
| Polite | … + ものではありません | 残(のこ)す → 残(のこ)すものではありません |
| Casual speech | … + ものじゃない／もんじゃない | 言(い)う → 言(い)うもんじゃない |
| Affirmative counterpart | dictionary form + ものだ | 挨拶(あいさつ)する → 挨拶(あいさつ)するものだ |
| Rejecting a principle | ～という + ものではない | 安(やす)ければいい → 安(やす)ければいいというものではない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb tells you which reading you are in. A **deliberate
action** somebody could stop doing gives the norm reading; a verb of **becoming,
acquiring or being able** gives the "not easily" reading.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
食(た)べ物(もの)を残(のこ)す<b>ものではありません</b>。
You don't leave food.
</pre>

<pre>
そんな言(い)い方(かた)をする<b>ものじゃない</b>よ。
That's not a way to talk to people.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の手紙(てがみ)を勝手(かって)に読(よ)む<b>ものではない</b>。
You don't read other people's letters without asking.
</pre>

<pre>
信頼(しんらい)は一日(いちにち)で築(きず)ける<b>ものではない</b>。
Trust is not something you can build in a day.
</pre>

<pre>
人前(ひとまえ)で家族(かぞく)の悪口(わるぐち)を言(い)う<b>ものではない</b>と祖母(そぼ)に教(おそ)わった。
My grandmother taught me that you do not run your family down in front of others.
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)というのは、本(ほん)を読(よ)んだだけで身(み)につく<b>ものではありません</b>。
A skill is not something you acquire simply by reading about it.
</pre>

<pre>
辛(つら)いときこそ、一人(ひとり)で抱(かか)え込(こ)む<b>ものではない</b>。
It is precisely when things are hard that you should not keep it all to yourself.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)の習慣(しゅうかん)は、一度(いちど)注意(ちゅうい)されたくらいで変(か)わる<b>ものではない</b>。
A habit of many years is not going to change just because somebody mentioned it once.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The norm reading carries the flavour of received wisdom, which makes it both warm
and slightly preachy. It is what a grandparent says to a child, what a senior
colleague says to a new hire, and what a person says when they are appealing to
decency rather than to a rule. Because of that flow - from more experienced to less -
saying it **upward** is a serious misstep. Telling a manager
「そんなことをするものではありません」 amounts to lecturing them on manners.

That directionality has an age tint to it: the form is used more freely by older
speakers, and among younger friends もんじゃない is the shape you actually hear
（「そんなに急(きゅう)に決(き)めるもんじゃないよ」）. It is a tendency, not a
rule; a twenty-year-old can and does say it to a friend.

The "not easily" reading is different in feel: calm, a little philosophical, and
common in written Japanese - essays, prefaces, interviews. It often follows
<code>というのは</code> or a topic phrase, and it explains why the listener's
expectation is unrealistic without blaming them for holding it.

Politeness does not rescue the norm reading. <code>ものではありません</code> is
grammatically polite but socially still a correction. If you need to advise upward,
use <code>なさらないほうがよろしいかと存(ぞん)じます</code> or a similarly indirect
frame.

## Contrast

### ～ものではない vs ～べきではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>べきではない</code> is a **reasoned judgement about this
case**. `ものではない` invokes a **general truth about how people behave**.

</div>

<pre>
この段階(だんかい)で発表(はっぴょう)す<b>べきではない</b>と思(おも)います。
I don't think we should announce it at this stage.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)を軽(かる)く考(かんが)える<b>ものではない</b>。
Promises are not something you take lightly.
</pre>

<code>べきではない</code> can be argued with, and it belongs in meetings and
editorials where reasons are being weighed. `ものではない` is not offering reasons at
all - it is pointing at a norm and expecting you to recognise it, which is why it
suits maxims and why it can sound sententious in a discussion.

### ～ものではない vs ～ものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - two halves of one pattern. <code>ものだ</code> states the norm;
`ものではない` states its prohibition.

</div>

<pre>
お世話(せわ)になった人(ひと)には、お礼(れい)を言(い)う<b>ものだ</b>。
You thank the people who have looked after you.
</pre>

<pre>
お世話(せわ)になった人(ひと)の悪口(わるぐち)を言(い)う<b>ものではない</b>。
You don't badmouth the people who have looked after you.
</pre>

Learning the pair together saves work, but note one asymmetry:
<code>ものだ</code> has a further, nostalgic past form -
<code>子(こ)どものころ、よく遊(あそ)んだものだ</code>, "we used to play a lot" -
and `ものではない` has no equivalent. A past-tense
❌<code>遊(あそ)んだものではない</code> does not mean "we didn't use to play".

### ～ものではない as "shouldn't" vs as "not easily"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the person could simply stop doing it, it is **shouldn't**.
If nobody could do it on demand in the first place, it is **not easily**.

</div>

<pre>
順番(じゅんばん)を抜(ぬ)かす<b>ものではない</b>。
You don't push in.
</pre>

<pre>
語学(ごがく)は一年(いちねん)で完成(かんせい)する<b>ものではない</b>。
Language learning isn't something that gets finished in a year.
</pre>

Cutting a queue is a choice, so the first is a rebuke. Nobody chooses whether a
language is finished in a year, so the second is an observation about what is
realistic. Potential forms（覚(おぼ)えられる, 築(きず)ける, 得(え)られる）
tip a sentence firmly into the second reading, because you cannot forbid someone from
being able to do something.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)、嘘(うそ)を<b>ついたものではない</b>。 → ✅ 嘘(うそ)を<b>つくものではない</b>。
  The norm reading takes the dictionary form only. A past tense turns the sentence
  into a denial about yesterday, which this pattern cannot express.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、そんなに急(いそ)いで<b>決(き)めるものではありません</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、もう少(すこ)しお時間(じかん)をいただいた<b>ほうがよろしいかと存(ぞん)じます</b>。
  Polite endings do not remove the lecture. Advice upward has to be reframed, not
  just conjugated.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は<b>静(しず)かなものではない</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は<b>静(しず)かではない</b>。
  <code>もの</code> here needs a verb in front of it. An adjective describing a room
  is an ordinary negative sentence.

- ❌（機械(きかい)の注意書(ちゅういが)きに）このボタンを<b>押(お)すものではありません</b>。 → ✅ このボタンを<b>押(お)さないでください</b>。
  A manufacturer's instruction is a rule with an author, so it takes
  <code>ないでください</code> or <code>てはいけません</code>. `ものではない` appeals to
  shared common sense, which a warning label does not have.

Turning a blind eye is a deliberate act and the sentence appeals to decency rather

society at large.

</details>

# 一～として～ない

**Meaning** · not even one; not a single ～ - counts the smallest possible unit and rules it out

The frame is <code>一(いち) + counter + として</code> followed by a negative:
一人(ひとり)として, 一(ひと)つとして, 一日(いちにち)として,
一度(いちど)として. It means "taking them one at a time, not one of them", and it
is markedly more emphatic - and more formal - than the everyday
<code>一人(ひとり)も</code>.

**What is denied: every member of a set, individually.** That is what separates it
from the rest of this batch. 全(まった)く sweeps a whole claim away in one
gesture; <code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> only removes the word "always". This
pattern goes down the list and knocks out each item, which is why it sounds like
testimony: the speaker is claiming to have checked.

The `として` here is **not** the "in the capacity of" `として` you met at N3. Same
characters, different job.

## Formation

The number is always 一(いち). Any other number breaks the pattern.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| People | 一人(ひとり)として + negative | 反対(はんたい)する → 一人(ひとり)として反対(はんたい)しなかった |
| Things | 一(ひと)つとして + negative | 同(おな)じだ → 一(ひと)つとして同(おな)じものはない |
| Days | 一日(いちにち)として + negative | 休(やす)む → 一日(いちにち)として休(やす)んでいない |
| Occasions | 一度(いちど)として + negative | 遅(おく)れる → 一度(いちど)として遅(おく)れたことがない |
| Words | 一言(ひとこと)として + negative | 漏(も)らす → 一言(ひとこと)として漏(も)らさなかった |
| Cases, items | 一件(いっけん)／一冊(いっさつ)として + negative | 届(とど)く → 一件(いっけん)として届(とど)いていない |
| Polite | … + ません／ありません | ある → 一(ひと)つとしてありません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - watch the counter readings: 一人(ひとり), 一(ひと)つ,
一言(ひとこと) take the native series, while 一日(いちにち),
一度(いちど), 一件(いっけん), 一冊(いっさつ) take the Sino-Japanese one with
its gemination. These are fixed; there is no choice to make.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
一人(ひとり)<b>として</b>反対(はんたい)する者(もの)はい<b>なかった</b>。
Not one person objected.
</pre>

<pre>
一(ひと)つ<b>として</b>同(おな)じ形(かたち)のものは<b>ない</b>。
No two of them are the same shape.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)<b>として</b>練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)んだことは<b>ありません</b>。
I have not skipped practice for a single day.
</pre>

<pre>
苦情(くじょう)は一件(いっけん)<b>として</b>届(とど)いてい<b>ません</b>。
We have not received a single complaint.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は一言(ひとこと)<b>として</b>言(い)い訳(わけ)をし<b>なかった</b>。
He did not offer one word of excuse.
</pre>

<pre>
この三年間(さんねんかん)、一日(いちにち)<b>として</b>気(き)を抜(ぬ)いたことは<b>ありません</b>。
In these three years there has not been a single day when I let my guard down.
</pre>

<pre>
提出(ていしゅつ)された資料(しりょう)のうち、一(ひと)つ<b>として</b>条件(じょうけん)を満(み)たすものは<b>なかった</b>。
Of the documents submitted, not one met the conditions.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)から五十年(ごじゅうねん)、一度(いちど)<b>として</b>品質(ひんしつ)で妥協(だきょう)したことは<b>ございません</b>。
In the fifty years since we were founded we have not once compromised on quality.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **formal, emphatic** pattern, and it is written far more often than it is
spoken. Where it does appear in speech, the setting is raised: a company statement, a
speech, a witness answering a question, a person defending their record. Saying
「一人(ひとり)として来(こ)なかった」 to a friend about a party sounds theatrical;
「誰(だれ)も来(こ)なかった」 is what you would actually say.

The force of it comes from the enumeration. Because the speaker has apparently gone
through the set item by item, the sentence carries an implicit "I checked" - which is
why it fits testimony, audits and records so well, and why it is a favourite in
corporate apologies and in advertising copy about tradition.

It is register-formal but not archaic; nothing about it sounds dated. In polite
registers it takes ません, ありません or, at the top end, ございません, and that top end
is common precisely because the pattern is already reaching for gravity.

One caution: the claim is absolute, so a single counterexample destroys it. Japanese
speakers reach for <code>ほとんど～ない</code> or
<code>一人(ひとり)もいなかったと思(おも)います</code> when they are not certain.
Overstating with this pattern and then being corrected is worse than using the plain
form.

## Contrast

### 一(いち)～として～ない vs 一(いち)～も～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different weight.
<code>一人(ひとり)も</code> is the **everyday** form;
<code>一人(ひとり)として</code> is the **formal, emphatic** one.

</div>

<pre>
教室(きょうしつ)に学生(がくせい)が<b>一人(ひとり)も</b>い<b>なかった</b>。
There wasn't a single student in the classroom.
</pre>

<pre>
関係者(かんけいしゃ)のうち<b>一人(ひとり)として</b>事実(じじつ)を知(し)る者(もの)はい<b>なかった</b>。
Not one of those involved knew the facts.
</pre>

Both are grammatical in either sentence, but the register clashes if you swap them:
<code>として</code> in the classroom sentence sounds like a formal inquiry, and
<code>も</code> in the second flattens what is clearly a considered statement. In
speech, default to `も`.

### として "not even one" vs として "as, in the capacity of"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look left and right. Preceded by
<code>一(いち) + counter</code> **and** followed by a negative → "not even one".
Otherwise it is the N3 `として`, "as".

</div>

<pre>
通訳(つうやく)<b>として</b>会議(かいぎ)に参加(さんか)しました。
I took part in the meeting as an interpreter.
</pre>

<pre>
通訳(つうやく)は一人(ひとり)<b>として</b>残(のこ)ってい<b>なかった</b>。
Not one of the interpreters was still there.
</pre>

The two never really collide in practice, because the "as" version follows a role
noun and the counting version follows a number. But in a reading passage, under time
pressure, learners do misread the second as "as one person", so it is worth fixing
the test in your head: **number in front, negative behind**.

### 一(いち)～として～ない vs 全(まった)く～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 全(まった)く denies **the whole in one stroke**;
<code>一(いち)～として</code> denies **each item in turn**, so it needs something
countable.

</div>

<pre>
その説明(せつめい)には<b>全(まった)く</b>説得力(せっとくりょく)が<b>ない</b>。
That explanation has no persuasive force whatsoever.
</pre>

<pre>
提案(ていあん)のうち<b>一(ひと)つとして</b>採用(さいよう)され<b>なかった</b>。
Not one of the proposals was adopted.
</pre>

Persuasive force cannot be counted, so the first sentence has no unit for
<code>として</code> to work with. Whenever the thing being denied is a mass or a
quality rather than a set of items, 全(まった)く or 少(すこ)しも is what you
need.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>二人(ふたり)として</b>反対(はんたい)しなかった。 → ✅ <b>一人(ひとり)として</b>反対(はんたい)しなかった。
  The number is fixed at 一(いち). The pattern works by naming the smallest
  possible unit, and two is not the smallest.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)は<b>一人(ひとり)として賛成(さんせい)した</b>。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)は<b>一人(ひとり)として賛成(さんせい)しなかった</b>。
  Without a negative the frame collapses, and the sentence reads oddly as "one
  student agreed in the capacity of…". The negative is compulsory.

- ❌ <b>学生(がくせい)として</b>賛成(さんせい)しなかった。（intending "not a single student agreed"） → ✅ 学生(がくせい)は<b>一人(ひとり)として</b>賛成(さんせい)しなかった。
  A bare role noun in front of `として` gives you the "as a student" reading. The
  counter has to be there.

- ❌（友(とも)だちとの会話(かいわ)で）パーティーに<b>一人(ひとり)として来(こ)なかった</b>よ。 → ✅ パーティーに<b>誰(だれ)も来(こ)なかった</b>よ。
  The pattern is too formal for chat. In conversation use
  <code>誰(だれ)も</code> or <code>一人(ひとり)も</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 去年(きょねん)作(つく)った部品(ぶひん)のうち、____として不良品(ふりょうひん)は見(み)つかりませんでした。

Parts are counted with つ here, so the smallest unit is

was rejected - and the item-by-item framing makes the outcome sound thorough and a
little damning.

guaranteed, which is the sort of thing you say *before* the decisions are made. (a)
looks back at a set; (b) looks forward at a probability.

</details>

# ～というわけではない

**Meaning** · it's not that ～; that's not to say ～ - takes back an interpretation while leaving the facts alone

You already know <code>わけではない</code> from N3: a partial denial that removes an
inference without denying what produced it. `というわけではない` is that same tool with
`という` bolted on, and the `という` does two concrete jobs. It **quotes** - so the
thing being denied is presented as a *claim*, something somebody might say - and
because it quotes, a bare noun can attach to it directly, with no `な` in sight.

**What is denied: the wording of the conclusion, not the state of affairs.** That
extra step of distance is why this is the shape people reach for when correcting a
misunderstanding of what they just said. <code>嫌(きら)いなわけではない</code> talks
about the speaker's feelings; <code>嫌(きら)いというわけではない</code> talks about
whether "dislikes it" is the right description.

## Formation

`という` quotes the clause, so nouns and な-adjectives need no linking `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + というわけではない | 反対(はんたい)している → 反対(はんたい)しているというわけではない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + というわけではない | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないというわけではない |
| い-adjective | plain form + というわけではない | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いというわけではない |
| な-adjective | ～（だ）+ というわけではない | 不満(ふまん) → 不満(ふまん)だというわけではない |
| Noun | Noun + というわけではない | 専門家(せんもんか) → 専門家(せんもんか)というわけではない |
| Polite | … + というわけではありません | 急(いそ)いでいる → 急(いそ)いでいるというわけではありません |
| Casual speech | … + ってわけじゃない | 嫌(いや)だ → 嫌(いや)だってわけじゃない |
| Mid-sentence | … + というわけではなく | 値段(ねだん) → 値段(ねだん)が理由(りゆう)というわけではなく |
| Usual partner adverbs | 必(かなら)ずしも／全(まった)く／いつも／全部(ぜんぶ) | - → 必(かなら)ずしもそうというわけではない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if what you are denying is something **somebody said or might
say**, use `という`. If you are denying a **plain state of affairs**, the bare
<code>わけではない</code> is enough - and with a noun it would then need `な`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
嫌(きら)い<b>というわけではない</b>。
It's not that I dislike it.
</pre>

<pre>
全(まった)く知(し)らない<b>というわけではありません</b>。
It's not as though I know nothing about it.
</pre>

<pre>
専門家(せんもんか)<b>というわけではない</b>が、少(すこ)しは詳(くわ)しい。
I wouldn't call myself an expert, but I know a bit about it.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)残業(ざんぎょう)している<b>というわけではない</b>んですが、帰(かえ)りはだいたい遅(おそ)いです。
It isn't that I work late every single day, but I do generally get home late.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が理由(りゆう)<b>というわけではなく</b>、デザインが好(この)みではなかったのです。
It wasn't the price as such - the design just wasn't to my taste.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)した人(ひと)が多(おお)かったからといって、計画(けいかく)が失敗(しっぱい)だった<b>というわけではありません</b>。

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

It's not that I want to quit exactly - I'm just a bit worn out.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the polite way to say "you've slightly misunderstood me", and it is
everywhere in careful conversation. Because it denies a *description* rather than a
fact, it lets you correct someone without accusing them of being wrong about the
world - a genuinely useful social move.

Register runs the full range. <code>というわけではありません</code> is standard
business Japanese; <code>ってわけじゃない</code> is casual speech and very common
among friends; plain <code>というわけではない</code> is what you write in an essay.
The `という` itself is register-neutral and adds a shade of formality relative to the
bare form, mostly because it is longer.

The partner adverbs matter. 必(かなら)ずしも is the most frequent, and the pair
<code>必(かなら)ずしも～というわけではない</code> is close to a fixed expression in
written argument. 全(まった)く, いつも, 全部(ぜんぶ) and 誰(だれ)でも work the
same way: they set up a total claim so that this pattern can trim it back to
something defensible. Note what that combination does to
全(まった)く - normally an absolute, here it is *inside* the denial, so
<code>全(まった)く知(し)らないというわけではない</code> means "I do know
something", not "I know nothing".

A small point of politeness. Because the pattern hands the other person a correction,
it is often prefaced with a softener - いえ, あの, or a
<code>すみません、言(い)い方(かた)が悪(わる)かったのですが</code>. Delivered
bare it can sound as if you are picking them up on their words.

## Contrast

### ～というわけではない vs ～わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `という` denies **a description someone has offered**. The bare
form denies **a state of affairs**. With a noun, the difference is also visible:
`という` attaches directly, the bare form needs `な`.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が責任者(せきにんしゃ)<b>というわけではない</b>。
He isn't exactly what you'd call the person in charge.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が責任者(せきにんしゃ)な<b>わけではない</b>。
It isn't the case that he's the person in charge.
</pre>

The first quibbles with the label: he does some of what a person in charge does, but
the word overstates it. The second denies the arrangement itself. In practice
Japanese speakers use the `という` version far more often with nouns, because the
label is usually what is at issue.

### ～というわけではない vs ～とは限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `というわけではない` withdraws **this interpretation, here**.
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> removes **the word "always" from a general rule**.

</div>

<pre>
静(しず)かな人(ひと)が冷(つめ)たい<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
Quiet people aren't necessarily cold.
</pre>

<pre>
静(しず)かにしていたからといって、怒(おこ)っていた<b>というわけではありません</b>。
Just because I was quiet doesn't mean I was angry.
</pre>

The first is about people in general and would sit comfortably in an essay. The
second is about the speaker, on one occasion, defending against a reading of their
own behaviour. If the subject is *me, just now*, the pattern is nearly always
`というわけではない`.

### ～というわけではない vs ～わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one letter apart in the middle, opposite in strength.
<code>ではない</code> is a **partial** denial; <code>がない</code> is a **total** one.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が嘘(うそ)をついている<b>というわけではない</b>。
It's not that he's lying.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が嘘(うそ)をつく<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way he'd lie.
</pre>

The first leaves room - maybe he is mistaken, maybe he is only telling part of it.
The second slams the door and vouches for him completely. Learners swap these
constantly, and the social cost is real: reaching for
<code>わけがない</code> when you meant a gentle correction commits you to a defence
you may not want to make.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>なというわけではない</b>。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>というわけではない</b>。
  `という` already does the linking. The `な` belongs to the bare form
  （学生(がくせい)なわけではない）and cannot appear alongside it.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)だ<b>というわけではない</b>。（intending "there's no way he did it"） → ✅ 彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)の<b>わけがない</b>。
  This pattern only ever softens. For a flat denial of possibility you need
  <code>わけがない</code> or <code>はずがない</code>.

- ❌（レポートに）データが足(た)りない<b>ってわけじゃない</b>。 → ✅ データが足(た)りない<b>というわけではない</b>。
  ってわけじゃない is spoken contraction. Restore the full form in anything written.

- ❌ 必(かなら)ずしも安全(あんぜん)だ<b>というわけがない</b>。 → ✅ 必(かなら)ずしも安全(あんぜん)だ<b>というわけではない</b>。
  必(かなら)ずしも means "not necessarily" and therefore needs a partial denial to
  land on. Paired with the total <code>わけがない</code> the two halves fight each
  other.

## Practice

**1.** お酒(さけ)が飲(の)めない____。弱(よわ)いだけです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お酒(さけ)が飲(の)めない<b>というわけではありません</b>。弱(よわ)いだけです。

The second sentence corrects a description rather than a fact - the speaker does
drink, just not much - which is exactly what this pattern is for. Note that the thing
denied is itself a negative（飲(の)めない）, and that is perfectly normal here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼女(かのじょ)が約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れる（というわけではない・わけがない）。あんなに几帳面(きちょうめん)な人(ひと)なんだから。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The second sentence gives grounds for complete confidence, so the denial has to be

forgotten and merely quibble with how it was put.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm not saying the plan is bad - I'd just like more time."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

for a polite objection in a meeting.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The stray `な` was left hanging with no noun after it. Either supply the noun the

<code>簡単(かんたん)だというわけではない</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)している<b>というわけではない</b>。
(b) 毎日(まいにち)勉強(べんきょう)している<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about the speaker and corrects an impression: somebody has concluded that they
study daily, and this takes that conclusion back while conceding that they do study.
It is personal and defensive.

(b) is about somebody else, or about people in general, and refuses a guarantee: you
cannot assume they study every day. It is the sentence you would say about a student
whose habits you do not know, not about yourself.

</details>

# ～ぬ・～ん

**Meaning** · the classical negative - the older ancestor of `ない`, still alive in set phrases, signage, fiction and casual speech

Before `ない` took over, Japanese negated verbs with `ぬ`. The old form never fully
died: it survives frozen inside words（思(おも)わぬ, 見知(みし)らぬ）, inside
proverbs, inside formal set phrases, and - contracted to `ん` - inside perfectly
ordinary spoken Japanese（知(し)らん, 分(わ)からん）. You will meet it on a sign
or in a novel long before anyone says it to you.

**What is denied: exactly what `ない` denies.** There is no difference in meaning at
all. `ぬ` is `ない` in older clothes, and everything on this page is about *where the
clothes are appropriate* - which is a question of register, not of logic. It is worth
learning because a learner who does not recognise
<code>見(み)て見(み)ぬふり</code> will simply lose the sentence.

## Formation

`ぬ` attaches to the same base as `ない` - the 未然形(みぜんけい), the "a-row" stem - with two
irregular verbs to memorise.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | ない-base + ぬ | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らぬ |
| る-verb | ない-base + ぬ | 見(み)ない → 見(み)ぬ |
| する | せ + ぬ | しない → せぬ |
| 来(く)る | 来(こ) + ぬ | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ぬ |
| Colloquial contraction | ない-base + ん | 分(わ)からない → 分(わ)からん |
| する (colloquial) | せ + ん | しない → せん |
| Obligation | ない-base + ねば | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かねばならない |
| Before a noun | ～ぬ + Noun | 思(おも)わぬ → 思(おも)わぬ結果(けっか) |
| Literary adnominal | ～ざる + Noun | 知(し)らざる → 知(し)らざる世界(せかい) |
| Fossil inside ません | ませ + ぬ | - → 行(い)きません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - build the `ない` form first, then swap `ない` for `ぬ` or `ん`.
The only two that will catch you out are する → <b>せ</b>ぬ・<b>せ</b>ん and
来(く)る → <b>来(こ)</b>ぬ・<b>来(こ)</b>ん. Note that 来(こ) here is the same
base as in 来(こ)ない, so the reading is こ, not き.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `ぬ` does not attach to adjectives or to nouns. ❌高(たか)いぬ,
❌学生(がくせい)ぬ. Only verbs take it. And the polite `ません` you learned at N5 is
literally ませ + ぬ - the classical negative has been hiding in your first textbook
all along.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
知(し)ら<b>ぬ</b>が仏(ほとけ)。
What you don't know can't hurt you.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなことは知(し)ら<b>ん</b>。
I've no idea about that.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は見(み)て見(み)<b>ぬ</b>ふりをした。
He looked the other way.
</pre>

<pre>
思(おも)わ<b>ぬ</b>ところで昔(むかし)の友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)った。
I ran into an old friend in the last place I would have expected.
</pre>

<pre>
すま<b>ん</b>、今日(きょう)はどうしても行(い)け<b>ん</b>。
Sorry - I really can't come today.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)までに結論(けつろん)を出(だ)さ<b>ねば</b>ならない。
We have to reach a conclusion by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
変(か)わら<b>ぬ</b>ご支援(しえん)に、心(こころ)より感謝(かんしゃ)申(もう)し上(あ)げます。
We are deeply grateful for your unwavering support.
</pre>

<pre>
城(しろ)の中(なか)は、物音(ものおと)一(ひと)つせ<b>ぬ</b>静(しず)けさだった。
Inside the castle there was a stillness unbroken by a single sound.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Treat `ぬ` and `ん` as two different problems, because their registers have almost
nothing in common.

**`ぬ` is literary.** In modern Japanese it belongs to proverbs, set phrases, period
drama, narration in fiction, song lyrics, old-fashioned signage and a small number of
formal fixed expressions（変(か)わらぬ, 絶(た)えぬ, 尽(つ)きぬ）. Saying
「分(わ)かりませぬ」 in a shop would land as a joke or as costume drama. Recognise
it, enjoy it in reading, and use `ない` when you speak. The exception is the frozen
adnominals - 思(おも)わぬ事故(じこ), 見知(みし)らぬ人(ひと),
少(すく)なからぬ影響(えいきょう) - which are ordinary modern vocabulary and quite
safe to produce.

**`ん` is alive.** 知(し)らん, 分(わ)からん, できん, 行(い)かん and すまん are
everyday casual speech. The form is strongly associated with western Japan - it is
the default negative across the Kansai region and much of the west - and in eastern
casual speech it tends to sound blunt and to lean masculine, though
知(し)らん and 分(わ)からん in particular are used very widely regardless of
speaker. Those are tendencies rather than rules. What is a rule is that `ん` is
**casual**: it does not belong in ます-form speech, in a workplace email, or in
anything you write for marks.

**`ねば`** is the conditional shape of `ぬ` (its 仮定形(かていけい)), and
<code>ねばならない</code> is stiff, written, and slightly declamatory - editorials,
speeches, resolutions. In ordinary Japanese you would say
<code>なければならない</code> or <code>ないといけない</code>.

One last practical point. `ぬ` sometimes appears where a modern speaker would use
`ない` purely for **rhythm** - a five- or seven-syllable line in a slogan, a title, a
headline. If a sentence has no other archaic feature and the `ぬ` is doing nothing but
saving a syllable, that is usually what is going on.

## Contrast

### ～ぬ vs ～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, opposite register. `ない` is the modern
default; `ぬ` marks the sentence as literary, formulaic or deliberately old.

</div>

<pre>
そんな話(はなし)は聞(き)いてい<b>ない</b>。
I haven't heard anything about that.
</pre>

<pre>
過(す)ぎ去(さ)った日(ひ)は二度(にど)と戻(もど)ら<b>ぬ</b>。
The days that have gone will never return.
</pre>

The second is not more formal in the ます sense - it is *literary*, which is a
different axis. A business email is formal and would still use `ない`. Reserve `ぬ`
for writing that is meant to sound like writing.

### ～ん as a negative vs ～ん as `の`

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at the syllable in front. `ん` after the **a-row base**
（行(い)か-, 知(し)ら-, 分(わ)から-）is the negative. `ん` after a **plain
form**（行(い)く-, 来(き)た-）is the contraction of `の`.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は行(い)か<b>ん</b>。
I'm not going tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は行(い)く<b>ん</b>だ。
The thing is, I'm going tomorrow.
</pre>

One syllable apart and directly contradictory - this is the single most expensive
confusion on this page. The `の` version is what you met at N5 in
<code>んです</code>, and it always sits after a full plain form. The negative version
sits on the stem, and it can end the sentence bare.

Note also 「行(い)かんの?」, where **both** appear: the negative `ん` plus the
question `の`. "You're not going?"

### ～ぬ vs ～ず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same auxiliary in two shapes. `ぬ` **modifies a noun or ends
a sentence**; `ず` **connects to what comes next**.

</div>

<pre>
何(なに)も言(い)わ<b>ず</b>に部屋(へや)を出(で)て行(い)った。
He left the room without saying anything.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)も言(い)わ<b>ぬ</b>まま、彼(かれ)は去(さ)った。
Saying nothing, he departed.
</pre>

You already know `ず` and `ずに` from N3, and the two forms are the same word: `ず`
is the connective shape, `ぬ` the one that attaches to a noun or closes a clause. The
same family gives you `ざる` for literary noun-modification
（許(ゆる)されざる行為(こうい)）and `ねば` for the conditional.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 何(なに)も<b>しぬ</b>。 → ✅ 何(なに)も<b>せぬ</b>。
  する has the irregular base せ-. And the wrong form collides with a real and very
  different verb: 死(し)ぬ, "to die".

- ❌ 誰(だれ)も<b>来(き)ぬ</b>夜(よる)。 → ✅ 誰(だれ)も<b>来(こ)ぬ</b>夜(よる)。
  来(く)る takes the こ- base here, the same one as in 来(こ)ない. The き- base
  belongs to 来(き)ます and 来(き)っこない.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)くぬ</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)くない</b>。
  `ぬ` attaches to verbs only. Adjectives and nouns have no classical negative of
  this shape available to them in modern Japanese.

- ❌（会議(かいぎ)で）その件(けん)は<b>存(ぞん)じませぬ</b>。 → ✅ その件(けん)は<b>存(ぞん)じません</b>。
  `ません` already contains the `ぬ`. Adding another one produces a form that reads as
  costume drama.

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

speech both give way to <code>決(き)めないと</code>, which is what most people
actually use.

</details>
