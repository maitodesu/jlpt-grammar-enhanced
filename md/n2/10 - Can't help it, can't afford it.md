# N2 - Can't help it, can't afford it


---

# ～てはいられない

**Meaning** · can't afford to go on doing this; can't stay as I am - the situation has become too urgent for it

Underneath, this is just `～ている` put into the potential negative: `いられない`,
"cannot be". The `は` is what does the work. It picks out the state you are
currently in and rejects it: *as for staying like this* - not possible. Nothing
is forbidden and nothing is beyond your physical powers. Circumstances have
simply made carrying on untenable, and something else now has to happen.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + はいられない | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)ってはいられない |
| Verb (で-form) | で-form + はいられない | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んではいられない |
| Verb (する) | して + はいられない | のんびりする → のんびりしてはいられない |
| Noun | Noun + ではいられない | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもではいられない |
| な-adjective | stem + ではいられない | 冷静(れいせい) → 冷静(れいせい)ではいられない |
| Polite | ～てはいられません | 黙(だま)ってはいられません |
| Past | ～てはいられなかった | 座(すわ)ってはいられなかった |
| Becoming so | ～てはいられなくなる | 寝(ね)てはいられなくなった |
| Casual (て) | ～ちゃいられない | 待(ま)っちゃいられない |
| Casual (で) | ～じゃいられない | 遊(あそ)んじゃいられない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `は` can be dropped, and the result is a different sentence.
<code>待(ま)っていられない</code> is a plain statement that you are unable to
keep waiting; <code>待(ま)ってはいられない</code> pushes back against waiting as
a course of action. The Contrast section takes this pair apart.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もうこう<b>してはいられない</b>。
I can't just stand around like this any more.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)はテストだから、遊(あそ)ん<b>ではいられない</b>。
I've got a test tomorrow, so I can't be out enjoying myself.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があっても、今日(きょう)は休(やす)ん<b>ではいられません</b>。
Even with a temperature, I can't afford to take today off.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが泣(な)いているのに、黙(だま)って見(み)<b>てはいられなかった</b>。
A child was crying - I couldn't just stand there watching.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)が年(とし)を取(と)れば、いつまでも子(こ)ども<b>ではいられない</b>。
Once your parents start getting older, you can't stay a child forever.
</pre>

<pre>
バスがいつ来(く)るか分(わ)からないし、待(ま)っ<b>てはいられない</b>から歩(ある)こう。
There's no telling when the bus will come and we can't stand around waiting, so let's walk.
</pre>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)まであと十分(じゅっぷん)だから、のんびり飲(の)ん<b>じゃいられない</b>な。
Ten minutes to the last train - no time to be sitting here drinking.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)引(ひ)っ越(こ)しだというのに、こんなにのんびり<b>してはいられませんよ</b>。
We're moving next week - we really can't be taking it this easy.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りまで三日(みっか)しかないと気(き)づいて、急(きゅう)に落(お)ち着(つ)い<b>てはいられなくなった</b>。
Realising there were only three days to the deadline, I suddenly couldn't sit still.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral in itself, and `～てはいられません` is perfectly
at home in polite speech. What is marked is the contraction. In conversation
`ては` becomes `ちゃ` and `では` becomes `じゃ`, and
<code>こうしちゃいられない</code> is close to a set phrase - it is what people say
as they get up out of the chair. A learner who has only met the full form will
not recognise it when it goes past at speed.

Look at the verbs that appear here and you will notice they are almost all verbs
of **staying put**: 待(ま)つ, 休(やす)む, 寝(ね)る, 黙(だま)る,
座(すわ)る, のんびりする, ゆっくりする, and the pro-verb こうする. That is the
shape of the pattern's meaning. It says that a state you are comfortably in has
to be broken out of, so it pairs naturally with a following clause about what you
are going to do instead.

Two things it is not. It is **not a prohibition** - nobody has ruled anything out,
and using it for a rule is one of the commonest errors on this page. And it is
**not physical inability**; that is the plain `～ていられない` without the `は`.

The noun form, <code>Noun + ではいられない</code>, is a little more reflective and
turns up in writing about growing up, illness and responsibility -
<code>いつまでも学生(がくせい)ではいられない</code>. It means "cannot remain X",
which is a different sentence from "no time for X".

<div class="note">

**Note** - the fixed idiom
<code>居(い)ても立(た)ってもいられない</code> comes from the same `いられない`.
It means "unable to keep still" out of worry or excitement, and it is frozen: you
cannot alter the verbs in it.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～てはいられない vs ～ていられない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - without `は`, you are **unable** to keep it up. With `は`, you
have **no business** keeping it up.

</div>

<pre>
眠(ねむ)くて目(め)を開(あ)け<b>ていられない</b>。
I'm so sleepy I can't keep my eyes open.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)が来週(らいしゅう)なのだから、寝(ね)<b>てはいられない</b>。
The exam is next week - I can't be sleeping.
</pre>

The first reports a limit in the body: your eyelids are closing whatever you want.
The second reports a judgement about the situation: sleep is a luxury the calendar
no longer allows. Swapping them goes wrong in both directions.
<code>目(め)を開(あ)けてはいられない</code> would suggest you had decided open
eyes were an indulgence, and
<code>寝(ね)ていられない</code> makes it sound as though something physically
prevents you from sleeping.

### ～てはいられない vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てはいけない` is somebody **forbidding** it. `てはいられない`
is the situation **overtaking** it. English "can't" hides the difference
completely.

</div>

<pre>
ここで写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っ<b>てはいけません</b>。
You mustn't take photographs here.
</pre>

<pre>
列(れつ)が動(うご)き出(だ)したから、写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)っ<b>てはいられない</b>。
The queue has started moving, so there's no time to be taking photos.
</pre>

In the first there is a rule, a sign, or a person with the authority to say no. In
the second there is nothing but a queue that has started to move. This matters in
practice: telling a visitor
<code>ここでタバコを吸(す)ってはいられません</code> when you mean the building
has a smoking ban does not convey a rule at all - it sounds as though smoking has
suddenly become impractical.

### ～てはいられない vs ～どころではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てはいられない` rejects **staying in a state**, and needs a
verb. `どころではない` rejects **the whole topic**, and happily takes a bare noun.

</div>

<pre>
父(ちち)が入院(にゅういん)して、旅行(りょこう)<b>どころではない</b>。
My father is in hospital; a holiday is out of the question.
</pre>

<pre>
父(ちち)が入院(にゅういん)したので、のんびり旅行(りょこう)し<b>てはいられない</b>。
My father has gone into hospital, so I can't be off travelling at my leisure.
</pre>

The difference in what they attach to is the practical test. A bare noun before
`ではいられない` means "cannot remain X":
<code>旅行(りょこう)ではいられない</code> is not "no time for a trip", it is
something close to nonsense. If the thing you are ruling out is a noun, you want
`どころではない`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 試験(しけん)中(ちゅう)は話(はな)し<b>てはいられません</b>。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)中(ちゅう)は話(はな)し<b>てはいけません</b>。
  A rule laid down by someone is <code>てはいけない</code>. `てはいられない` would
  mean the exam left you no opportunity to chat.

- ❌ 休(やす)む<b>てはいられない</b> → ✅ 休(やす)ん<b>ではいられない</b>
  It attaches to the て-form. 休(やす)む is an う-verb whose て-form is
  <code>休(やす)んで</code>, so `は` follows `で`, not `て`.

- ❌ 重(おも)くて一人(ひとり)で持(も)っ<b>てはいられない</b>。 → ✅ 重(おも)くて一人(ひとり)で持(も)っ<b>ていられない</b>。
  Physical inability drops the `は`. With `は` the sentence claims that holding the
  thing is a self-indulgence you have no time for.

- ❌ 仕事(しごと)<b>ではいられない</b>から、遊(あそ)びに行(い)けない。 → ✅ 仕事(しごと)が忙(いそが)しくて、遊(あそ)び<b>どころではない</b>。
  <code>Noun + ではいられない</code> means "cannot remain that noun", so this reads
  "I cannot go on being work."

## Practice

**1.** 地震(じしん)のニュースを聞(き)いて、のんびりお茶(ちゃ)を飲(の)ん____なくなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

飲(の)ん<b>ではいられ</b>なくなった。

The news changed the situation, and sitting over tea is exactly the kind of staying
put this pattern breaks out of. <code>～てはいられなくなる</code> marks the moment
the change happens.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この部屋(へや)では靴(くつ)を脱(ぬ)ぐ決(き)まりです。靴(くつ)のまま入(はい)っ（てはいけません・てはいられません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

靴(くつ)のまま入(はい)っ<b>てはいけません</b>。

<code>決(き)まり</code> is a rule, and rules are `てはいけない`. `てはいられない`
would say that circumstances had made walking in with your shoes on impractical,
which is not what a house rule does.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The train's about to leave, so we can't stand here chatting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)が出(で)てしまうから、ここで話(はな)し込(こ)ん<b>ではいられない</b>。

<code>話(はな)し込(こ)む</code> gives the で-form, so the pattern appears as
`ではいられない`. Nothing forbids the conversation; the timetable has simply
overtaken it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)までに資料(しりょう)を作(つく)らなければならないので、寝(ね)るてはいられない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)までに資料(しりょう)を作(つく)らなければならないので、寝(ね)<b>てはいられない</b>。

The pattern attaches to the て-form, never the dictionary form. 寝(ね)る is a
る-verb, so the て-form is <code>寝(ね)て</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ここで待(ま)っ<b>てはいけない</b>。
(b) ここで待(ま)っ<b>てはいられない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says waiting here is not allowed. There is a rule behind it - a sign, a member
of staff, a building regulation - and the sentence is the kind of thing said *to*
someone.

(b) says waiting here is not something you can afford to keep doing. Nobody has
objected; the situation has moved on and standing about is no longer a viable
plan. It is normally said about yourself, and it usually leads straight into what
you are going to do instead.

</details>

# ～てばかりはいられない

**Meaning** · can't go on doing only this - it was fair enough up to now, but something else has to be dealt with

Take `～てばかりいる`, "do nothing but ~", and put `いる` into the potential
negative. What comes out is not a rejection of the action. It is a rejection of
the **proportion**. Grieving, resting, worrying and complaining are all allowed to
continue; what cannot continue is their being the only thing happening. This is
the grammar of pulling yourself together.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ばかりはいられない | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)いてばかりはいられない |
| Verb (で-form) | で-form + ばかりはいられない | 喜(よろこ)ぶ → 喜(よろこ)んでばかりはいられない |
| Verb (する) | して + ばかりはいられない | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)してばかりはいられない |
| With も instead of は | ～てばかりもいられない | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)んでばかりもいられない |
| With no particle | ～てばかりいられない | 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)んでばかりいられない |
| Polite | ～てばかりはいられません | 甘(あま)える → 甘(あま)えてばかりはいられません |
| Past | ～てばかりはいられなかった | 悩(なや)む → 悩(なや)んでばかりはいられなかった |
| Casual | ～てばっかりはいられない | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)いてばっかりはいられない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - only verbs, and only verbs describing something a person is
choosing to keep doing. There is no noun or adjective row here, and no row for the
weather: a situation that nobody controls cannot be told to stop being the only
thing going on.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
いつまでも泣(な)い<b>てばかりはいられない</b>。
I can't just go on crying forever.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)みだからといって、遊(あそ)ん<b>でばかりはいられません</b>。
It may be the holidays, but I can't spend the whole time playing.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)に勝(か)ったが、喜(よろこ)ん<b>でばかりはいられない</b>。次(つぎ)が本番(ほんばん)だ。
We won, but there's no time to celebrate. The real match is next.
</pre>

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)はまだ治(なお)っていないが、寝(ね)<b>てばかりはいられない</b>。
My cold isn't better yet, but I can't just stay in bed.
</pre>

<pre>
文句(もんく)を言(い)っ<b>てばかりもいられない</b>ので、自分(じぶん)でやり方(かた)を変(か)えてみた。
I couldn't keep on merely complaining, so I tried changing how I did it.
</pre>

<pre>
先輩(せんぱい)が手伝(てつだ)ってくれるのはありがたいが、いつまでも頼(たよ)っ<b>てばかりもいられない</b>。
It's good of my senior colleague to help, but I can't lean on him indefinitely.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい機械(きかい)は便利(べんり)だが、機械(きかい)に任(まか)せ<b>てばかりはいられない</b>だろう。
The new machines are convenient, but surely we can't leave everything to them.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)の将来(しょうらい)を心配(しんぱい)し<b>てばかりはいられない</b>と思(おも)い、本人(ほんにん)に任(まか)せることにした。
I decided that worrying about my son's future was getting us nowhere, and left it up to him.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and equally at home in speech and in writing. The one thing that
is consistent is its **position in a paragraph**: it almost always arrives as the
second half of a concession. Look at the examples and you will see
<code>～が</code>, <code>～けれど</code>, <code>～のはありがたいが</code>,
<code>だからといって</code> in front of nearly all of them. The speaker first
concedes that the behaviour was reasonable, then says it has to stop being the
whole picture. Used with no concession in front of it, the sentence sounds abrupt.

The subject is normally the speaker, or an inclusive "we". Aimed at somebody else
it becomes a nudge, and how it lands depends entirely on who is speaking. From a
friend, <code>いつまでも落(お)ち込(こ)んでばかりはいられないよ</code> is
encouragement. From a manager,
<code>甘(あま)えてばかりはいられませんよ</code> is a reprimand with a smile on
it. That is a tendency of the relationship, not a property of the grammar.

`は` and `も` are both extremely common here and the difference is slight. `は`
sets the action against the alternatives - *not only this*. `も` is softer and
more resigned - *nor can one just keep doing this*. Casual speech lengthens
`ばかり` to `ばっかり`, and drops the particle altogether often enough that
`てばかりいられない` should be recognised too.

## Contrast

### ～てばかりはいられない vs ～てばかりいる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てばかりいる` describes what someone **is doing**. Adding
`はいられない` says that it **has to stop being all they do**.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は朝(あさ)からゲームをし<b>てばかりいる</b>。
My brother does nothing but play games from the morning on.
</pre>

<pre>
来月(らいげつ)が試験(しけん)なのだから、ゲームをし<b>てばかりはいられない</b>。
The exam is next month, so I can't spend all my time on games.
</pre>

There is a strong difference of person between them. `てばかりいる` is usually a
complaint **about someone else** and often carries visible irritation.
`てばかりはいられない` is usually a resolution **about yourself**, and carries no
irritation at all - only a decision.

### ～てばかりはいられない vs ～てはいられない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - without `ばかり`, the action has to **stop**. With `ばかり`, it
has to stop being **the only thing**.

</div>

<pre>
結果(けっか)が出(で)るまで待(ま)っ<b>てはいられない</b>。
I can't sit around waiting for the results.
</pre>

<pre>
結果(けっか)を待(ま)っ<b>てばかりはいられない</b>から、次(つぎ)の準備(じゅんび)も始(はじ)めよう。
We can't just wait for the results - let's start preparing for the next step as well.
</pre>

Notice `も` in the second sentence. `ばかり` leaves the waiting in place and adds
something alongside it, so a "…as well" is the natural continuation. The first
sentence has no room for one: the waiting is over.

They also differ in what they attach to. `てはいられない` can follow a noun
(<code>子(こ)どもではいられない</code>); `てばかりはいられない` cannot.

### ～てばかりはいられない vs ～ているわけにはいかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てばかりはいられない` says the **balance** has gone wrong.
`ているわけにはいかない` says the state itself is **not permissible**, for a
reason you could put into words.

</div>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)に任(まか)せ<b>ているわけにはいかない</b>。
I can't leave it to my boss - that isn't how it should work.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)に任(まか)せ<b>てばかりはいられない</b>。
I can't leave everything to my boss all the time.
</pre>

The first is a point of propriety, and you could state the reason: it is my job,
not his. The second is a point of proportion, and the reason is simply that it has
gone on too long and covered too much. `わけにはいかない` rules the action out
entirely; `てばかりはいられない` keeps it and trims it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 泣(な)く<b>てばかりはいられない</b> → ✅ 泣(な)い<b>てばかりはいられない</b>
  `ばかり` here follows a て-form, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 心配(しんぱい)<b>ばかりはいられない</b> → ✅ 心配(しんぱい)し<b>てばかりはいられない</b>
  <code>心配(しんぱい)ばかり</code> is a perfectly good noun phrase, but this
  pattern is built on a て-form, so the する has to be there.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は遊(あそ)ん<b>でばかりはいられない</b>。（as a complaint about him） → ✅ 彼(かれ)は遊(あそ)ん<b>でばかりいる</b>。
  Describing what somebody actually does is `てばかりいる`. The negative form is a
  statement about what has to change, and it is normally the speaker's own.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>てばかりはいられない</b>。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>てばかりいる</b>。
  The pattern needs somebody who could choose otherwise. Weather cannot pull
  itself together.

## Practice

**1.** 失敗(しっぱい)したのは残念(ざんねん)だが、いつまでも落(お)ち込(こ)ん____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いつまでも落(お)ち込(こ)ん<b>でばかりはいられない</b>。

<code>落(お)ち込(こ)む</code> gives the で-form, so the pattern appears as
`でばかりはいられない`. Note the concession in front of it - `～が` is the shape
this pattern almost always arrives in.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 弟(おとうと)は一日中(いちにちじゅう)マンガを読(よ)ん（でばかりいる・でばかりはいられない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

読(よ)ん<b>でばかりいる</b>。

The sentence describes what the brother does, and it is a complaint about him.
`でばかりはいられない` would be a resolution - and it would have to be his own, not
the speaker's.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm grateful that everyone helps me, but I can't keep relying on it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

みんなが助(たす)けてくれるのはありがたいが、頼(たよ)っ<b>てばかりはいられない</b>。

<code>ありがたいが</code> supplies the concession the pattern wants. The help is
not being refused; what is being refused is help as the only arrangement.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 給料(きゅうりょう)が安(やす)いと文句(もんく)を言(い)うばかりはいられない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 給料(きゅうりょう)が安(やす)いと文句(もんく)を言(い)っ<b>てばかりはいられない</b>。

The dictionary form cannot carry this `ばかり`. Change 言(い)う to the て-form
<code>言(い)って</code> and the pattern is complete.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 休(やす)ん<b>ではいられない</b>。
(b) 休(やす)ん<b>でばかりはいられない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) rules resting out altogether. Whatever the situation is, it does not allow a
break at all, and the speaker is about to get up and do something.

(b) leaves the resting in place. A break is fine; what cannot continue is resting
*and nothing else*. It implies the speaker has been resting for a while, concedes
that this was reasonable, and is now going to add something to it.

</details>

# ～どころではない

**Meaning** · this is no time for ~; ~ is out of the question - something has taken over and left no room for it

`どころ` is <code>所(ところ)</code> with the `と` voiced: the place, the
occasion, the moment for something. `どころではない` says the occasion is not this
one. Trouble has arrived, or a deadline, or an emergency, and the thing named in
front of the pattern has been squeezed out entirely. A second, less common reading
runs the other way - not "no room for it" but "far beyond it" - and the shape of
what precedes `どころ` tells you which you are looking at.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + どころではない | 旅行(りょこう) → 旅行(りょこう)どころではない |
| Verb | dictionary form + どころではない | 寝(ね)る → 寝(ね)るどころではない |
| Verb (ている) | ～ている + どころではない | 笑(わら)っている → 笑(わら)っているどころではない |
| な-adjective | stem + どころではない | 安心(あんしん) → 安心(あんしん)どころではない |
| い-adjective ("far beyond" sense) | plain form + どころではない | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいどころではない |
| Polite | ～どころではありません | 花見(はなみ)どころではありません |
| Casual | ～どころじゃない | 勉強(べんきょう)どころじゃない |
| Past | ～どころではなかった | 食事(しょくじ)どころではなかった |
| Becoming so | ～どころではなくなる | 仕事(しごと)どころではなくなった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a noun goes in bare. No `だ`, no `な`, no `の`:
✅<code>旅行(りょこう)どころではない</code>,
❌<code>旅行(りょこう)だどころではない</code>. A verb goes in as the dictionary
form or as `～ている`, never as the て-form.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
忙(いそが)しくて、旅行(りょこう)<b>どころではない</b>。
I'm too busy - a trip is out of the question.
</pre>

<pre>
歯(は)が痛(いた)くて、食事(しょくじ)<b>どころじゃない</b>。
My tooth hurts so much I'm in no state to eat.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)面接(めんせつ)なので、テレビを見(み)ている<b>どころではありません</b>。
I have an interview tomorrow; this is no time to be watching television.
</pre>

<pre>
停電(ていでん)で、仕事(しごと)<b>どころではなくなった</b>。
The power cut put an end to any thought of working.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが熱(ねつ)を出(だ)したので、ゆっくり眠(ねむ)る<b>どころではなかった</b>。
My child had a fever, so there was no question of a proper night's sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の片(かた)づけに追(お)われて、今年(ことし)は花見(はなみ)<b>どころではありませんでした</b>。
We were busy clearing up after the typhoon, so there was no cherry blossom viewing for us this year.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)の音(おと)がひどくて、うるさい<b>どころではない</b>。会話(かいわ)も聞(き)き取(と)れない。
The noise from the building work goes well beyond "loud" - I can't even make out what people are saying.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)が急(きゅう)に倒産(とうさん)して、結婚式(けっこんしき)の準備(じゅんび)を進(すす)める<b>どころではなくなってしまった</b>。
The company suddenly went under, and preparing for the wedding became the last thing on our minds.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is an emphatic pattern with feeling in it - usually exasperation, sometimes
despair. `どころじゃない` is the everyday spoken shape and is very frequent;
`どころではありません` is its polite counterpart and is perfectly usable at work.

It almost always has a **cause in front of it**, joined with `～て`, `～ので` or
`～で`. That cause is the thing that has taken the room away. Standing on its own
the pattern works as a reply, thrown back at a suggestion:
「花見(はなみ)? 花見(はなみ)どころじゃないよ。」

Used as a refusal it can come out blunt, because it does not merely decline - it
says the invitation was misjudged. Speakers usually pad it for that reason:
<code>今(いま)ちょっと仕事(しごと)が立(た)て込(こ)んでいて、旅行(りょこう)どころではないんです</code>
is a good deal easier to receive than the bare form. That is a matter of politeness
strategy rather than grammar.

The "far beyond" reading is signalled by an **い-adjective** or a quantity in front
of `どころ`, and it is nearly always followed by a stronger restatement:
<code>うるさいどころではない。会話(かいわ)も聞(き)き取(と)れない</code>. With
a noun or a verb in front, you are in the ordinary "no room for it" reading.

## Contrast

### ～どころではない vs ～どころか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どころか` **replaces** what precedes it with something further
along the scale, and needs a following clause to do the replacing.
`どころではない` **rejects the occasion** for it, and ends the sentence.

</div>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が上(あ)がる<b>どころか</b>、下(さ)がってしまった。
Far from going up, my salary went down.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が下(さ)がって、旅行(りょこう)<b>どころではない</b>。
My salary has been cut - holidays are out of the question.
</pre>

The test is whether the sentence has somewhere to go. `どころか` is a hinge: it
needs the other half. `どころではない` is a full stop. That is why the "far beyond"
reading of `どころではない` sounds unfinished on its own too - it borrows the
hinge-like feel and wants a restatement after it.

### ～どころではない vs ～ている場合(ばあい)ではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - very close. <code>場合(ばあい)ではない</code> points at
**the moment** and needs a verb before it. `どころではない` points at **the whole
business** and can take a bare noun.

</div>

<pre>
笑(わら)っている<b>場合(ばあい)じゃない</b>よ。
This is no time to be laughing.
</pre>

<pre>
テストが近(ちか)いので、アルバイト<b>どころではない</b>。
The test is coming up, so a part-time job is out of the question.
</pre>

There is also a difference of direction that is worth noticing.
<code>場合(ばあい)じゃない</code> is very often addressed **to** somebody, as a
rebuke for how they are behaving right now. `どころではない` more often reports the
speaker's **own** circumstances. Both will stretch, but that is the default each
one starts from.

### ～どころではない vs ～わけにはいかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どころではない` says there is **no room** for it.
`わけにはいかない` says there is room, but doing it would be **wrong**.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)に頼(たの)まれたので、断(ことわ)る<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
The manager asked me, so I can hardly refuse.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)が入院(にゅういん)して、引(ひ)き受(う)ける<b>どころではない</b>。
My mother has been taken into hospital; taking it on is out of the question.
</pre>

In the first there is a social reason you could name out loud. In the second there
is no question of right and wrong at all - the speaker's week has simply been
taken over. If you can finish the sentence "…because that would look bad", you
want `わけにはいかない`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 忙(いそが)しくて、旅行(りょこう)<b>だどころではない</b>。 → ✅ 忙(いそが)しくて、旅行(りょこう)<b>どころではない</b>。
  A noun attaches bare. The `だ` has nothing to do here.

- ❌ 勉強(べんきょう)し<b>てどころではない</b>。 → ✅ 勉強(べんきょう)する<b>どころではない</b>。
  A verb goes in as the dictionary form, or as `～ている` if you want to stress
  that it is an activity in progress.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は謝(あやま)る<b>どころではなく</b>、笑(わら)っていた。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は謝(あやま)る<b>どころか</b>、笑(わら)っていた。
  When you go on to say what happened **instead**, the pattern you want is
  `どころか`.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は暇(ひま)だから、映画(えいが)を見(み)る<b>どころではない</b>。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は暇(ひま)だから、映画(えいが)でも見(み)よう。
  The pattern needs an obstacle to push against. With a free afternoon there is
  nothing squeezing the film out.

## Practice

**1.** 明日(あした)引(ひ)っ越(こ)しなので、のんびりテレビを見(み)ている____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

のんびりテレビを見(み)ている<b>どころではない</b>。

The move is the cause that takes the room away, and `～ている` in front of `どころ`
is the natural shape when you are ruling out an activity in progress.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 彼(かれ)はお礼(れい)を言(い)う（どころか・どころではなく）、文句(もんく)を言(い)い出(だ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お礼(れい)を言(い)う<b>どころか</b>、文句(もんく)を言(い)い出(だ)した。

The sentence goes on to say what he did instead, which is exactly what `どころか`
is for. `どころではない` would need the sentence to stop there, and it would mean
that circumstances had left him no opportunity to say thank you.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "With the roof leaking, we were in no position to think about a holiday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

屋根(やね)から雨(あめ)が漏(も)っていて、休暇(きゅうか)のことを考(かんが)える<b>どころではなかった</b>。

The leak is the cause, joined with `～ていて`. Note that the pattern takes the past
form itself - <code>どころではなかった</code> - rather than putting the past on the
verb in front of it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 熱(ねつ)が高(たか)くて、勉強(べんきょう)してどころではなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 熱(ねつ)が高(たか)くて、勉強(べんきょう)する<b>どころではなかった</b>。

The て-form cannot stand before `どころ`. Use the dictionary form
<code>勉強(べんきょう)する</code>, or <code>勉強(べんきょう)している</code> if
you want the sense of an activity that was under way.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 寒(さむ)い<b>どころではない</b>。
(b) 寒(さむ)い<b>どころか</b>、暑(あつ)いくらいだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the "far beyond" reading, triggered by the い-adjective in front. It does
not deny the cold - it says "cold" is far too mild a word, and it leaves you
expecting a stronger one to follow.

(b) denies the cold outright and supplies the correction in the same sentence: it
is nearly hot. `どころか` needs that second clause; `どころではない` does not have
one and simply refuses the word offered.

</details>

# ～ないではいられない

**Meaning** · can't help doing it; can't stop yourself - the conversational twin of `ずにはいられない`

The pressure here comes from **inside**. No rule applies, no deadline is closing,
and nothing in the world has removed your options; you simply cannot keep yourself
from doing the thing. The meaning is the same as `ずにはいられない`, which you met
at N3, but the build is different - ordinary `ない` plus `で` - and that difference
changes both how it sounds and, more sharply, how `する` and <code>来(く)る</code> behave.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form + ではいられない | 言(い)う → 言(い)わないではいられない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + ではいられない | 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)えないではいられない |
| Verb (する) | しないで + はいられない | 応援(おうえん)する → 応援(おうえん)しないではいられない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | ない-form + ではいられない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないではいられない |
| Polite | ～ないではいられません | 買(か)う → 買(か)わないではいられません |
| Past | ～ないではいられなかった | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)かないではいられなかった |
| Becoming so | ～ないではいられなくなる | 書(か)く → 書(か)かないではいられなくなる |
| Written twin | ～ずにはいられない | 泣(な)く → 泣(な)かずにはいられない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the irregular verbs behave **oppositely** in the two twins.
`する` gives <code>しないでは</code> here but <code>せずには</code> there;
❌<code>せないでは</code> and ❌<code>しずには</code> are both impossible. <code>来(く)る</code>
is the easy one: <code>こないでは</code> and <code>こずには</code>, `こ-` in both.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この話(はなし)を聞(き)くと、怒(おこ)ら<b>ないではいられない</b>。
When I hear this story I can't help getting angry.
</pre>

<pre>
甘(あま)いにおいがしてきて、台所(だいどころ)をのぞか<b>ないではいられなかった</b>。
A sweet smell drifted in, and I couldn't stop myself peering into the kitchen.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに頑張(がんば)っている姿(すがた)を見(み)ると、応援(おうえん)し<b>ないではいられません</b>。
Seeing him working that hard, I can't not cheer him on.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)で迷(まよ)っている人(ひと)がいたら、声(こえ)をかけ<b>ないではいられない</b>性格(せいかく)なんです。
I'm the sort of person who can't walk past someone who looks lost.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の結果(けっか)が気(き)になって、何度(なんど)も確認(かくにん)し<b>ないではいられなかった</b>。
I was so anxious about the exam results that I kept checking, over and over.
</pre>

<pre>
新聞(しんぶん)であの記事(きじ)を読(よ)んで、一言(ひとこと)書(か)か<b>ないではいられなくなった</b>。
After reading that article in the paper, I found I had to write in.
</pre>

<pre>
大(おお)きな地震(じしん)のあとは、遠(とお)くに住(す)む友人(ゆうじん)にも連絡(れんらく)を取(と)ら<b>ないではいられません</b>。
After a big earthquake, I can't rest until I've got hold of friends who live far away.
</pre>

<pre>
退院(たいいん)したという知(し)らせを受(う)け取(と)った夜(よる)、一人(ひとり)で乾杯(かんぱい)し<b>ないではいられませんでした</b>。
The night I got word that she'd been discharged, I had to raise a glass, even on my own.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The meaning is identical to `ずにはいられない`; what differs is texture. This form
is built out of the everyday `ない`, and it sits comfortably in speech and in
dialogue. `ずには` is the crisper, more literary one and dominates essays, reviews
and printed prose. Nobody will misunderstand you either way - this is a register
choice, not a meaning choice, and both are correct in both places. In fast casual
speech, people usually say neither, and reach for
<code>つい言(い)っちゃった</code> instead.

The urge is **emotional**, not practical. The verbs that turn up here reflect that:
to laugh, to cry, to get angry, to say something, to check, to reach out, to look.
A deadline, a fee or a rule produces an obligation, not an urge, and belongs to
`なければならない` or <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code>.

Because the sentence reports an inner state, the subject is normally **you**. About
a third person it needs a hedge - <code>らしい</code>, <code>ようだ</code>,
<code>のだろう</code> - exactly as plain `たい` does. Written flat about somebody
else, it claims to have read their mind.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `は` can drop, giving `～ないでいられない`, but that shape is
noticeably less common than the corresponding `～ずにいられない`. And do not confuse
this with `～ないではおかない`, a separate and much more forceful pattern meaning
"will not let it rest undone" - it is literary and rare in speech.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～ないではいられない vs ～ずにはいられない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning. `ないでは` is the **spoken** shape, `ずには` the
**written** one. The only thing you have to get right is `する`.

</div>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)のことを心配(しんぱい)し<b>ないではいられない</b>。
I can't help worrying about my son.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)のことを心配(しんぱい)せ<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
I can't help worrying about my son.
</pre>

Two sentences, one meaning. Put the first in a text message to a friend and the
second in a letter, and both land properly. Swap the stems -
❌<code>心配(しんぱい)せないでは</code>, ❌<code>心配(しんぱい)しずには</code> -
and neither is Japanese at all.

### ～ないではいられない vs ～てならない・～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないではいられない` reports an **action** you cannot suppress.
`てならない` and `てたまらない` report a **feeling** you cannot suppress.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の態度(たいど)が気(き)になっ<b>てならない</b>。
His manner is on my mind and I can't shake it.
</pre>

<pre>
気(き)になって、本人(ほんにん)に聞(き)か<b>ないではいられなかった</b>。
It bothered me so much that I had to go and ask him.
</pre>

The first stops at the feeling. The second is about what the feeling made you *do*,
which is why it needs a verb of doing. That is also why adjectives cannot enter
this pattern: ❌<code>悲(かな)しくないではいられない</code> is not a sentence.
For a bare feeling you want `てならない` or `てたまらない`.

### ～ないではいられない vs ～ざるを得(え)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both come out as "I had to". <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> is pressure from
**outside**; `ないではいられない` is pressure from **inside**.

</div>

<pre>
証拠(しょうこ)がこれだけ出(で)てきては、認(みと)め<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。
With this much evidence, I have no choice but to admit it.
</pre>

<pre>
昔(むかし)の写真(しゃしん)が出(で)てきて、一枚(いちまい)ずつ手(て)に取(と)ら<b>ないではいられなかった</b>。
Old photographs turned up, and I couldn't stop picking them up one by one.
</pre>

Here is a usable test. If you could point at the pressure - a rule, a deadline, a
document, a piece of evidence - it is <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code>. If the pressure lives in the
speaker and would disappear if somebody else were in the room, it is
`ないではいられない`. Getting this backwards says something unfortunate: telling a
host <code>おかわりせざるを得(え)なかった</code> announces that you ate the
second helping under duress.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 感動(かんどう)<b>せないではいられない</b> → ✅ 感動(かんどう)<b>しないではいられない</b>（or 感動(かんどう)せずにはいられない）
  The `ないで` half is built on ordinary `ない`, so `する` gives
  <code>しないで</code>. The `せ-` stem belongs to the `ずに` build.

- ❌ この映画(えいが)は悲(かな)しく<b>ないではいられない</b>。 → ✅ この映画(えいが)は悲(かな)しく<b>てならない</b>。
  Adjectives do not take this pattern. It needs a verb - something you found
  yourself doing.

- ❌ 会費(かいひ)を払(はら)わ<b>ないではいられない</b>。 → ✅ 会費(かいひ)を払(はら)わ<b>なければならない</b>。
  A membership fee is a duty. Nobody is emotionally overwhelmed into paying one.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは文句(もんく)を言(い)わ<b>ないではいられません</b>。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは文句(もんく)を言(い)わ<b>ないではいられないらしい</b>。
  Stated flat about a third person, the sentence claims direct knowledge of their
  inner state. Hedge it.

## Practice

**1.** 困(こま)っている様子(ようす)を見(み)て、手伝(てつだ)わ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

手伝(てつだ)わ<b>ないではいられなかった</b>。

手伝(てつだ)う is an う-verb, so the ない-stem is <code>手伝(てつだ)わ</code>.
The sentence describes a pull the speaker could not resist, not a duty - nobody
asked.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 規則(きそく)で決(き)まっているので、報告(ほうこく)（しないではいられない・せざるを得(え)ない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

報告(ほうこく)<b>せざるを得(え)ない</b>。

<code>規則(きそく)</code> is pressure you could point at, which puts the sentence
firmly in <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> territory. `しないではいられない` would claim the speaker
was seized by a longing to file a report.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Every time I pass a bookshop I can't help going in."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

本屋(ほんや)の前(まえ)を通(とお)るたびに、入(はい)ら<b>ないではいられません</b>。

<code>たびに</code> and the non-past tell you this is a standing feature of the
speaker, not a single occasion - which is exactly the kind of thing this pattern
reports.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 試合(しあい)の結果(けっか)が気(き)になって、確認(かくにん)せないではいられなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 試合(しあい)の結果(けっか)が気(き)になって、確認(かくにん)<b>しないではいられなかった</b>。

<code>せ-</code> is the stem for `ずに`, not for `ないで`. Either
<code>確認(かくにん)しないではいられなかった</code> or
<code>確認(かくにん)せずにはいられなかった</code> is correct; mixing the two is
not.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 寂(さび)しく<b>てたまらない</b>。
(b) 寂(さび)しくて、電話(でんわ)をかけ<b>ないではいられなかった</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the feeling itself, at full strength, and stops there. Nothing has been
done about it; the sentence is a description of a state.

(b) uses the same loneliness as a cause and reports what it drove the speaker to do.
The pattern needs that action - the loneliness is only the setup, and the sentence
is really about the phone call.

</details>

# ～てしょうがない

**Meaning** · unbearably ~; ~ and there's nothing I can do about it - a feeling or sensation at a strength beyond your management

`しょうがない` is the spoken squeeze of <code>しようがない</code>, "there is no way
of doing anything about it"; <code>仕方(しかた)がない</code> is the same idea
built on a different noun. Hang it off a state with `～て` and it becomes an
intensifier - but an intensifier with a shrug of helplessness inside it. The
speaker is not measuring how hot or how sleepy; they are reporting that the
feeling has got past them.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | ～くて + しょうがない | 眠(ねむ)い → 眠(ねむ)くてしょうがない |
| な-adjective | ～で + しょうがない | 退屈(たいくつ) → 退屈(たいくつ)でしょうがない |
| Verb (spontaneous state) | て-form + しょうがない | 気(き)になる → 気(き)になってしょうがない |
| Verb + たい | ～たくて + しょうがない | 会(あ)いたい → 会(あ)いたくてしょうがない |
| Polite | ～てしょうがありません | 暑(あつ)い → 暑(あつ)くてしょうがありません |
| Past | ～てしょうがなかった | 痛(いた)い → 痛(いた)くてしょうがなかった |
| Full form | ～てしようがない | 眠(ねむ)い → 眠(ねむ)くてしようがない |
| Written twin | ～てしかたがない | 残念(ざんねん) → 残念(ざんねん)でしかたがない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verbs that fit are ones that **happen to** you:
気(き)になる, 腹(はら)が立(た)つ, 思(おも)える, 汗(あせ)が出(で)る.
A verb you perform on purpose has to be turned into a desire with `たい` first:
❌<code>走(はし)ってしょうがない</code>,
✅<code>走(はし)りたくてしょうがない</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今日(きょう)は眠(ねむ)く<b>てしょうがない</b>。
I'm unbearably sleepy today.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)は暑(あつ)く<b>てしょうがありません</b>。
This room is impossibly hot.
</pre>

<pre>
授業(じゅぎょう)が退屈(たいくつ)<b>でしょうがなかった</b>。
The lesson was so boring I couldn't stand it.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の結果(けっか)が気(き)になっ<b>てしょうがない</b>。
I can't stop thinking about the exam results.
</pre>

<pre>
花粉(かふん)の季節(きせつ)は目(め)がかゆく<b>てしょうがない</b>。
In pollen season my eyes itch like mad.
</pre>

<pre>
上(うえ)の階(かい)の足音(あしおと)が気(き)になっ<b>てしょうがなくて</b>、夜中(よなか)に何度(なんど)も目(め)が覚(さ)めた。
The footsteps upstairs bothered me so much that I woke several times in the night.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)してすぐの頃(ころ)は、家族(かぞく)に会(あ)いたく<b>てしょうがありませんでした</b>。
Just after I went abroad to study, I missed my family terribly.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)のミスで迷惑(めいわく)をかけてしまい、情(なさ)けなく<b>てしょうがなかった</b>。
I had caused trouble through my own mistake, and I felt utterly wretched about it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the **colloquial** member of the family. There is a small ladder of forms
that mean the same thing: `しょうがない` is the spoken contraction,
`しようがない` its full form and slightly more careful, and
<code>仕方(しかた)がない</code> the one you write. In a report or an essay,
`～てしかたがない` or `～てしようがない` is the safe choice; `～てしょうがない` in
formal writing reads as speech that has been transcribed.

The polite `～てしょうがありません` exists and is fine, but plenty of speakers keep
`しょうがない` plain even inside a polite sentence -
<code>暑(あつ)くてしょうがないです</code>. Both are heard and neither is wrong.

It usually complains, but it does not have to.
<code>うれしくてしょうがない</code> is entirely normal. What is constant is that the
feeling is **beyond the speaker's management**, whichever direction it points in.
And because it reports the speaker's own experience, a third person needs a hedge:
`らしい`, `ようだ`, `そうだ`.

<div class="note">

**Note** - keep this apart from bare `しょうがない`, the everyday shrug meaning
"oh well, nothing to be done". 「遅(おく)れちゃったけど、しょうがないね」 is a
resigned comment about a situation; `～てしょうがない` is an intensifier attached to
a state. Same word, different job.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～てしょうがない vs ～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same slot, different temperature. `しょうがない` = "and I can do
nothing about it". `たまらない` = "and I cannot bear it" - a notch stronger, and
more physical.

</div>

<pre>
朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を抜(ぬ)いたので、おなかがすい<b>てしょうがない</b>。
I skipped breakfast and I'm hungry beyond words.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)何(なに)も食(た)べていなくて、おなかがすい<b>てたまらない</b>。
I haven't eaten all day - I'm starving.
</pre>

Both are correct, and in a great many sentences either will pass. `たまらない`
reaches a limit; `しょうがない` is exasperated at something it cannot fix.
`しょうがない` is also the more frequent of the two in ordinary conversation, which
makes it the safer default when you are unsure.

### ～てしょうがない vs ～てならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てならない` prefers **feelings that well up by themselves** and
is markedly more **written**. `てしょうがない` covers everything, physical
sensations included, and is spoken.

</div>

<pre>
亡(な)くなった祖父(そふ)のことが思(おも)い出(だ)され<b>てならない</b>。
Memories of my late grandfather keep coming back to me.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)歩(ある)き回(まわ)って、足(あし)が痛(いた)く<b>てしょうがない</b>。
I walked around all day and my feet are killing me.
</pre>

Reverse them and both go slightly wrong.
<code>足(あし)が痛(いた)くてならない</code> is not ungrammatical, but it sounds
oddly bookish for a sore foot, and
<code>思(おも)い出(だ)されてしょうがない</code> is too casual for the
sentiment it carries.

### ～てしょうがない vs ～すぎる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すぎる` is an **objective** verdict: more than is appropriate.
`てしょうがない` is a **subjective** report: more than I can handle.

</div>

<pre>
このスープは辛(から)<b>すぎる</b>。
This soup is too spicy.
</pre>

<pre>
このスープは辛(から)く<b>てしょうがない</b>。
This soup is so spicy I can hardly eat it.
</pre>

`すぎる` can be said about anything at all, including things that do not touch you:
<code>この靴(くつ)は大(おお)きすぎる</code> is a fine remark about a shoe on a
shelf. `てしょうがない` needs somebody undergoing the feeling, so
<code>この靴(くつ)は大(おお)きくてしょうがない</code> only works if the shoes
are on your feet and driving you mad.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 眠(ねむ)い<b>でしょうがない</b> → ✅ 眠(ねむ)く<b>てしょうがない</b>
  い-adjectives connect with `くて`. The `で` row belongs to な-adjectives.

- ❌ 心配(しんぱい)く<b>てしょうがない</b> → ✅ 心配(しんぱい)<b>でしょうがない</b>
  <code>心配(しんぱい)</code> is a な-adjective, so it takes `で`. Its ending
  looks like an い-adjective's and it is not one.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っ<b>てしょうがない</b>。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)走(はし)りたく<b>てしょうがない</b>。
  A deliberate action has to become a desire first. Only verbs that happen to you
  can go in raw.

- ❌ 妹(いもうと)は寂(さび)しく<b>てしょうがない</b>。 → ✅ 妹(いもうと)は寂(さび)しく<b>てしょうがないらしい</b>。
  Somebody else's inner state is not directly observable. Hedge it, as you would
  with `たい`.

## Practice

**1.** エアコンが効(き)きすぎていて、足(あし)が冷(つめ)た____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

足(あし)が冷(つめ)た<b>くてしょうがない</b>。

<code>冷(つめ)たい</code> is an い-adjective, so it connects with `くて`. The
air conditioning is something being done to the speaker and cannot be argued with -
which is the helpless note `しょうがない` adds.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新(あたら)しいパソコンが欲(ほ)し（すぎる・くてしょうがない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しいパソコンが欲(ほ)し<b>くてしょうがない</b>。

The sentence is about how badly the speaker wants the computer.
<code>欲(ほ)しすぎる</code> would say the desire itself was excessive - a
judgement about the wanting rather than a report of it, and it survives mainly as
a joke.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I was so nervous before the interview that my hands wouldn't stop shaking."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

面接(めんせつ)の前(まえ)は緊張(きんちょう)して、手(て)が震(ふる)え<b>てしょうがなかった</b>。

<code>震(ふる)える</code> is a verb that happens to you rather than one you
perform, so it goes in as a bare て-form with no `たい`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 弟(おとうと)のことが心配(しんぱい)くてしょうがない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 弟(おとうと)のことが心配(しんぱい)<b>でしょうがない</b>。

<code>心配(しんぱい)</code> is a な-adjective and connects with `で`, not `くて`.
The `くて` ending is only available to い-adjectives.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この映画(えいが)は長(なが)すぎる。
(b) この映画(えいが)は長(なが)くてしょうがない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a verdict on the film. It would have been a better film if it were shorter,
and you could say it about a film you enjoyed, or about one you have only read
about.

(b) is a report of your own suffering while sitting through it. It says nothing
about whether the film is any good - only that the length has become more than you
can deal with, and that you are still in the cinema.

</details>

# ～てたまらない

**Meaning** · unbearably ~; I can't stand it - a feeling that has reached the point where you cannot hold it

<code>たまらない</code> is the negative of <code>堪(た)まる</code>, "to hold out,
to endure" - a verb that survives in modern Japanese almost only in this negative
shape. Attached to a state with `～て`, it says that the state has passed the limit
of what you can contain. Unlike most of the "can't stand it" family, it works for
pleasant feelings as readily as for unpleasant ones: what matters is the
intensity, not the direction.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | ～くて + たまらない | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)くてたまらない |
| な-adjective | ～で + たまらない | 不安(ふあん) → 不安(ふあん)でたまらない |
| Verb (spontaneous state) | て-form + たまらない | 気(き)になる → 気(き)になってたまらない |
| Verb + たい | ～たくて + たまらない | 帰(かえ)りたい → 帰(かえ)りたくてたまらない |
| Polite | ～てたまりません | 嬉(うれ)しい → 嬉(うれ)しくてたまりません |
| Past | ～てたまらなかった | 悔(くや)しい → 悔(くや)しくてたまらなかった |
| Becoming so | ～てたまらなくなる | 懐(なつ)かしい → 懐(なつ)かしくてたまらなくなる |
| Casual | ～てたまんない | 眠(ねむ)い → 眠(ねむ)くてたまんない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - as with the rest of this family, a verb only goes in bare if it
is something that **happens to** you. Anything you do on purpose has to become a
desire first: ❌<code>国(くに)へ帰(かえ)ってたまらない</code>,
✅<code>国(くに)へ帰(かえ)りたくてたまらない</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
外(そと)は寒(さむ)く<b>てたまらない</b>。
It's unbearably cold outside.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の前(まえ)は不安(ふあん)<b>でたまりませんでした</b>。
Before the exam I was sick with nerves.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)歩(ある)いて、足(あし)が痛(いた)く<b>てたまらなかった</b>。
After walking all day my feet hurt beyond bearing.
</pre>

<pre>
国(くに)の母(はは)の料理(りょうり)が食(た)べたく<b>てたまりません</b>。
I'm desperate for my mother's cooking back home.
</pre>

<pre>
孫(まご)が生(う)まれて、祖父(そふ)は嬉(うれ)しく<b>てたまらない</b>様子(ようす)だ。
Now that his grandchild has been born, my grandfather looks beside himself with joy.
</pre>

<pre>
あと一点(いってん)で勝(か)てたのに負(ま)けてしまい、悔(くや)しく<b>てたまらなかった</b>。
We lost when we were a single point from winning, and it was agony.
</pre>

<pre>
隣(となり)の席(せき)の人(ひと)が何(なに)を書(か)いているのか気(き)になっ<b>てたまらなかった</b>。
I was dying to know what the person next to me was writing.
</pre>

<pre>
久(ひさ)しぶりに田舎(いなか)の空気(くうき)を吸(す)うと、懐(なつ)かしく<b>てたまらなくなる</b>。
Breathing country air again after so long fills me with an ache of nostalgia.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たまらない` is the **strong, bodily** member of the family. Hunger, cold, pain,
thirst, sleepiness, itching, longing for home: these are its natural territory,
and where `しょうがない` grumbles, `たまらない` is at the end of its rope. It is
also the one that works for good feelings -
<code>嬉(うれ)しくてたまらない</code>, <code>楽(たの)しくてたまらない</code> -
which separates it from `てかなわない`, a pattern that only ever complains.

`てたまりません` is the polite form and is unremarkable in formal speech.
`てたまんない` is the casual contraction, very common in conversation, and worth
recognising even if you never produce it.

The sentence reports the speaker's own experience, so a third person takes a hedge
- <code>様子(ようす)だ</code>, `らしい`, `ようだ` - exactly as in the example
about the grandfather above.

<div class="note">

**Note** - bare `たまらない` has a separate life as praise. 「この香(かお)り、
たまらない」 means the smell is irresistible, not that it is unbearable. Same word,
opposite emotional sign; the context and the intonation decide, and a learner meets
this far more often in food writing than anywhere else.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～てたまらない vs ～てしょうがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たまらない` is at the **limit of endurance**. `しょうがない` is
**beyond your control and annoying**. They overlap heavily; the difference is
temperature.

</div>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の工場(こうじょう)は汗(あせ)が出(で)<b>てたまらない</b>。
In summer the factory has you dripping with sweat.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)くて、時計(とけい)が気(き)になっ<b>てしょうがない</b>。
The meeting dragged on and I couldn't keep my eyes off the clock.
</pre>

Try each in the other's sentence and both remain possible - that is honest, and
worth saying plainly. The reliable difference is that `たまらない` is more likely
with the strongest physical sensations, while `しょうがない` dominates casual speech
generally and carries a note of resignation that `たまらない` does not.

### ～てたまらない vs ～てならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たまらない` takes **sensations and cravings**. `ならない` takes
**thoughts and impressions that arise on their own**, and is much more written.

</div>

<pre>
甘(あま)いものが食(た)べたく<b>てたまらない</b>。
I'm craving something sweet.
</pre>

<pre>
あの時(とき)断(ことわ)っておけばよかったと思(おも)え<b>てならない</b>。
I can't shake the feeling that I should have said no at the time.
</pre>

The first is a body wanting something; the second is a mind that will not let go of
a thought. Swap them and both read strangely.
<code>甘(あま)いものが食(た)べたくてならない</code> is odd rather than
impossible, and <code>思(おも)えてたまらない</code> does not work at all - there
is no sensation to endure.

### ～てたまらない vs ～てかなわない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たまらない` can be **good or bad**. `かなわない` is always a
**complaint**, about something being inflicted on you.

</div>

<pre>
合格(ごうかく)の知(し)らせを聞(き)いて、嬉(うれ)しく<b>てたまらなかった</b>。
When I heard I'd passed, I was overjoyed.
</pre>

<pre>
こう毎日(まいにち)残業(ざんぎょう)が続(つづ)い<b>てはかなわない</b>。
Overtime every day like this - I can't take it.
</pre>

❌<code>嬉(うれ)しくてかなわない</code> does not work, because there is nothing to
endure. That single test - is there something you would like removed? - sorts the
two patterns reliably. Note also the `こう…ては` frame around `かなわない`: it is
built for a burden that keeps repeating, and `たまらない` has no such preference.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 寒(さむ)い<b>でたまらない</b> → ✅ 寒(さむ)く<b>てたまらない</b>
  い-adjectives connect with `くて`.

- ❌ 国(くに)へ帰(かえ)っ<b>てたまらない</b>。 → ✅ 国(くに)へ帰(かえ)りたく<b>てたまらない</b>。
  Going home is something you do on purpose, so it has to become
  <code>帰(かえ)りたい</code> before it can be unbearable.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は寂(さび)しく<b>てたまりません</b>。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は寂(さび)しく<b>てたまらないようです</b>。
  Someone else's inner state has to be hedged.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しく<b>てたまらない</b>。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)し<b>すぎる</b>。
  <code>難(むずか)しい</code> here describes the problem, not a sensation the
  speaker is undergoing. `たまらない` wants a feeling with a person inside it.

## Practice

**1.** 来月(らいげつ)の旅行(りょこう)のことを考(かんが)えると、楽(たの)しみ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

楽(たの)しみ<b>でたまらない</b>。

<code>楽(たの)しみ</code> behaves as a な-adjective here, so it connects with `で`,
not `くて`. Note that the feeling is a pleasant one - `たまらない` is quite happy
with that.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この夏(なつ)は暑(あつ)く（てたまらない・てはならない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この夏(なつ)は暑(あつ)く<b>てたまらない</b>。

<code>てはならない</code> is a prohibition - "must not" - and a summer cannot be
forbidden to be hot. The two look alike on the page and do completely different
jobs.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My little sister was so pleased with her new bicycle that she wouldn't get off it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

妹(いもうと)は新(あたら)しい自転車(じてんしゃ)が嬉(うれ)しく<b>てたまらない</b>らしく、ずっと乗(の)っていた。

The `らしく` is doing necessary work. Without it the sentence would assert direct
knowledge of the sister's inner state, which this pattern does not license about
anyone but the speaker.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 一年(いちねん)会(あ)っていないので、彼(かれ)に会(あ)ってたまらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 一年(いちねん)会(あ)っていないので、彼(かれ)に会(あ)い<b>たくてたまらない</b>。

<code>会(あ)う</code> is a deliberate action, so the bare て-form gives nothing
for `たまらない` to work on. Turn it into the desire
<code>会(あ)いたい</code> and the sentence says what was meant.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 退屈(たいくつ)<b>でたまらない</b>。
(b) 退屈(たいくつ)<b>でしょうがない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are correct and both complain about the same boredom. (a) presents it as
almost physical - you have reached the limit of what you can sit through, and it is
the more dramatic of the two.

(b) presents it as a nuisance you can do nothing about. It is the more everyday
phrasing, the one you would more often hear in casual speech, and it carries a
shrug that (a) does not. The difference is intensity and register, not meaning.

</details>

# ～てならない

**Meaning** · I can't help feeling ~; it keeps coming over me - a thought or feeling that arises by itself and will not be put down

The `ならない` here is the same `なる` as in `なければならない`: nothing can be
*made* of the thing, it cannot be brought under control. What the pattern reports
is something that happens **to** the speaker's mind - a suspicion, a regret, a
memory, a sense that something is not right. The mind does it without being asked,
and the speaker cannot make it stop.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | ～くて + ならない | 悲(かな)しい → 悲(かな)しくてならない |
| な-adjective | ～で + ならない | 残念(ざんねん) → 残念(ざんねん)でならない |
| Verb (spontaneous) | て-form + ならない | 思(おも)える → 思(おも)えてならない |
| Verb (spontaneous passive) | ～れて + ならない | 悔(く)やまれる → 悔(く)やまれてならない |
| ～気(き)がする | 気(き)がして + ならない | 気(き)がする → 気(き)がしてならない |
| Polite | ～てなりません | 心配(しんぱい) → 心配(しんぱい)でなりません |
| Past | ～てならなかった | 気(き)になる → 気(き)になってならなかった |
| Written | ～てならないのである | 思(おも)われる → 思(おも)われてならないのである |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verbs that appear here are close to a **closed set**, and they have
something in common: 思(おも)える, 思(おも)われる, 感(かん)じられる,
気(き)がする, 気(き)になる, 悔(く)やまれる,
思(おも)い出(だ)される. Every one of them describes the mind doing something
of its own accord. Notice how many are in the spontaneous `～れる` shape - that is
half the reason the pattern reads as formal.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
試合(しあい)に負(ま)けて、残念(ざんねん)<b>でならない</b>。
Losing the match is a bitter disappointment.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)の留学(りゅうがく)が心配(しんぱい)<b>でなりません</b>。
I worry endlessly about my son studying abroad.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)か大事(だいじ)なことを忘(わす)れているような気(き)がし<b>てならない</b>。
I can't shake the feeling that I'm forgetting something important.
</pre>

<pre>
あの時(とき)の一言(ひとこと)が悔(く)やまれ<b>てならない</b>。
I cannot stop regretting what I said that day.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)の家(いえ)が取(と)り壊(こわ)されると聞(き)いて、寂(さび)しく<b>てなりませんでした</b>。
Hearing that my grandmother's house was to be pulled down left me desolate.
</pre>

<pre>
どこかで会(あ)ったことがあるように思(おも)え<b>てならない</b>のだが、名前(なまえ)が出(で)てこない。
I can't escape the feeling that we've met somewhere, but the name won't come.
</pre>

<pre>
この数字(すうじ)には何(なに)か裏(うら)があるように思(おも)われ<b>てなりません</b>。
I cannot help suspecting that there is something behind these figures.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の報道(ほうどう)を見(み)るたびに、あの日(ひ)の夕方(ゆうがた)のことが思(おも)い出(だ)され<b>てならない</b>。
Every time I see coverage of the accident, that evening comes back to me unbidden.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the **written and formal** member of the family. It belongs to essays,
editorial columns, letters of condolence, prepared speeches and the more considered
registers of speech. Said over coffee about a mild irritation it sounds weighty,
almost theatrical; that is not an error, but it is a mismatch a learner should hear
before making it.

Its vocabulary is the vocabulary of the mind. Physical sensation sits badly with
it: for hunger, cold, pain and itching you want `てたまらない` or `てしょうがない`.
The clean test is whether the state is something your *mind* is doing to you rather
than something your *body* is undergoing.

Unlike `たまらない`, it has no casual contraction, and it does not appear in the
relaxed shapes the rest of the family uses. If you meet it in dialogue, the
speaker is being serious.

The subject is the speaker. About a third person the sentence needs a hedge, or
the older narrative frame <code>～てならないようだった</code>.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this `ならない` has nothing to do with the obligation
`なければならない`, despite sharing a verb. `～なければならない` says you must;
`～てならない` says you cannot help it. They are different constructions that happen
to end the same way.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～てならない vs ～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ならない` = a **thought or impression** arising by itself.
`たまらない` = a **sensation or craving** at the limit.

</div>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)の説明(せつめい)にはどこか嘘(うそ)があるように感(かん)じられ<b>てならない</b>。
I can't help feeling there's something untrue in her explanation.
</pre>

<pre>
エアコンが止(と)まって、暑(あつ)く<b>てたまらない</b>。
The air conditioning has stopped and it's unbearably hot.
</pre>

Swap them and the results are unequal.
<code>感(かん)じられてたまらない</code> does not work at all - an impression is
not something you endure. <code>暑(あつ)くてならない</code> is possible, but it
reads like nineteenth-century prose, and a Japanese speaker would notice.

### ～てならない vs ～てしょうがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - often interchangeable in meaning; the difference is **register**
and **what they attach to**. `しょうがない` is spoken and takes almost anything;
`ならない` is written and takes states of mind.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)の発表(はっぴょう)のことが気(き)になっ<b>てしょうがない</b>。
I can't stop worrying about tomorrow's presentation.
</pre>

<pre>
亡(な)くなった恩師(おんし)のことが思(おも)い出(だ)され<b>てなりません</b>。
Memories of my late teacher keep returning to me.
</pre>

Both sentences mean "I can't stop - ". The first would look out of place in a
eulogy; the second would sound overwrought in a coffee break. Choose by the room
you are in, not by the meaning.

### ～てならない vs ～てはならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one `は` apart, and opposite in meaning. `てならない` is a
feeling you **cannot suppress**. `てはならない` is a **prohibition**: you must not.

</div>

<pre>
不安(ふあん)<b>でならない</b>。
I am terribly uneasy.
</pre>

<pre>
この部屋(へや)に入(はい)っ<b>てはならない</b>。
You must not enter this room.
</pre>

The two also differ in what they follow. `てはならない` attaches to a verb's
て-form and takes any verb at all, including deliberate ones. `てならない` attaches
to an adjective or to a spontaneous verb. Letting a `は` slip in by accident turns
"I can't stop worrying" into "worrying is forbidden", which is a strange thing to
say and an easy thing to type.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 残念(ざんねん)くて<b>ならない</b> → ✅ 残念(ざんねん)<b>でならない</b>
  <code>残念(ざんねん)</code> is a な-adjective and connects with `で`.

- ❌ おなかがすい<b>てならない</b>。 → ✅ おなかがすい<b>てたまらない</b>。
  Hunger is a sensation of the body. `ならない` wants a state of mind.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)のことが気(き)になっ<b>てはならない</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)のことが気(き)になっ<b>てならない</b>。
  The stray `は` converts the sentence into a prohibition against thinking about
  him.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)し<b>てならない</b>。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)し<b>なければならない</b>。
  An obligation is `なければならない`. Practising is a deliberate action, and
  deliberate actions cannot be things that well up in you unbidden.

## Practice

**1.** 優勝(ゆうしょう)まであと一歩(いっぽ)だったので、悔(くや)しく____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

悔(くや)しく<b>てなりません</b>（or 悔(くや)しくてならない）

<code>悔(くや)しい</code> is an い-adjective, so it connects with `くて`. Regret
that keeps welling up is exactly the emotional territory this pattern was built
for, and the slightly formal tone suits a public statement after a defeat.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この書類(しょるい)は他(ほか)の人(ひと)に見(み)せ（てならない・てはならない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

見(み)せ<b>てはならない</b>。

The sentence lays down a rule about a document, and rules take the `は`.
`てならない` would be trying to say that showing people the document was a feeling
the speaker could not suppress.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I can't help thinking that something has been left out of this report."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この報告書(ほうこくしょ)には何(なに)かが抜(ぬ)けているように思(おも)え<b>てならない</b>。

<code>思(おも)える</code> is the spontaneous form - the thought arrives on its
own rather than being reasoned out - and it is one of the handful of verbs that fit
this pattern most naturally.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)から歯(は)が痛(いた)くてならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)から歯(は)が痛(いた)く<b>てたまらない</b>（or 痛(いた)くてしょうがない）

Toothache is a physical sensation, and `ならない` wants a state of mind. The
sentence as written is not exactly ungrammatical, but it strikes a literary note
that a toothache does not deserve.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 残念(ざんねん)<b>でしょうがない</b>。
(b) 残念(ざんねん)<b>でなりません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both say the speaker is deeply disappointed, and neither is more disappointed than
the other. (a) is what you say to a friend on the way out of the stadium - casual,
exasperated, a little deflated.

(b) is what you say into a microphone afterwards. It is composed and public, and
the polite ending makes it more so; you would find it in a written statement or a
formal apology, where (a) would look careless.

</details>

# ～たいものだ・～てほしいものだ

**Meaning** · I do wish ~; one would like to ~ - a general, wistful longing, and emphatically not a request

Plain `たい` points at a want you have right now and could act on. Adding `ものだ`
steps back from the moment and turns the want into a **sentiment**: this is how one
wishes things were. The wish is usually distant, often unlikely, and it comes with
a small sigh attached. `～てほしいものだ` performs the same move on other people's
behaviour - and there the sigh very often turns into a complaint.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (own wish) | ます-stem + たいものだ | 訪(おとず)れる → 訪(おとず)れたいものだ |
| Verb (own wish, negative) | ～たくないものだ | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れたくないものだ |
| Verb (about others) | て-form + ほしいものだ | 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)えてほしいものだ |
| Verb (about others, negative) | ～ないでほしいものだ | 忘(わす)れる → 忘(わす)れないでほしいものだ |
| Stiffer variant | ～てもらいたいものだ | 直(なお)す → 直(なお)してもらいたいものだ |
| Polite | ～たいものです ／ ～てほしいものです | 会(あ)う → 会(あ)いたいものです |
| Written | ～たいものである ／ ～てほしいものである | 願(ねが)う → 願(ねが)いたいものである |
| Casual | ～たいもんだ ／ ～てほしいもんだ | 行(い)く → 行(い)きたいもんだ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - it is **not a request**. To ask the person in front of you for
something, use <code>～てほしいんですが</code> or
<code>～ていただけませんか</code>. Said to somebody's face,
<code>静(しず)かにしてほしいものです</code> is a rebuke, not a polite ask.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
いつかオーロラを見(み)<b>たいものだ</b>。
I'd love to see the northern lights one day.
</pre>

<pre>
年(とし)を取(と)っても元気(げんき)でい<b>たいものです</b>。
One does hope to stay in good health as one gets older.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の話(はなし)は最後(さいご)まで聞(き)い<b>てほしいものだ</b>。
I do wish people would hear you out.
</pre>

<pre>
若(わか)いうちに、一度(いちど)は外国(がいこく)で暮(く)らし<b>たいものだ</b>。
While one is still young, one really would like to live abroad at least once.
</pre>

<pre>
政治家(せいじか)には、もっと現場(げんば)の声(こえ)を聞(き)い<b>てほしいものです</b>。
I really do wish politicians would listen to people on the ground.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに忙(いそが)しくても、家族(かぞく)との時間(じかん)だけは失(うしな)い<b>たくないものだ</b>。
However busy one gets, time with one's family is the one thing one would hate to lose.
</pre>

<pre>
せっかく作(つく)った料理(りょうり)なのだから、残(のこ)さないで食(た)べ<b>てほしいもんだ</b>ね。
I went to the trouble of cooking it, so I do wish they'd finish it.
</pre>

<pre>
災害(さいがい)のときこそ、正(ただ)しい情報(じょうほう)が早(はや)く届(とど)い<b>てほしいものである</b>。
It is precisely in a disaster that accurate information ought to reach people quickly.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The voice here is **reflective**. It is the voice of a newspaper column, an
after-dinner speech, a diary entry, a grandparent looking out of a window.
`～ものである` is the written extreme and turns up in editorials and formal prose.
`～もんだ` is the casual end, and in speech it very often arrives with a grumble
attached: 「もう少(すこ)し考(かんが)えてほしいもんだ」.

It leans **older**. Among younger speakers in casual conversation it is not
especially common; `～たいなあ` and `～てほしいなあ` do the same work with none of
the formality. That is a tendency about who uses it most, not a rule about who may.

`～てほしいものだ` about a third party is very often **criticism**. Warm uses
certainly exist - <code>若(わか)い人(ひと)にはぜひ読(よ)んでほしいものです</code>
is generous - but the default colour is "why on earth don't they", and directed at
the person themselves it is a reproach. To a superior it would be close to
insubordinate.

The clearest boundary is this: **it cannot answer a question about a concrete
want.** 「何(なに)が飲(の)みたいですか」 must be answered with plain `たい`.
❌「コーヒーが飲(の)みたいものです」 is comic - it makes coffee sound like a
lifelong ambition. In the same way, `ものだ` sits badly on a plan you are about to
carry out this afternoon. The wish it expresses is long-range, unfulfilled, and
usually a little out of reach.

## Contrast

### ～たいものだ vs ～たい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たい` is a want you have **now** and could act on.
`たいものだ` is a want held up as a **sentiment** - distant, general, and often
unlikely to be satisfied.

</div>

<pre>
のどが渇(かわ)いたので、水(みず)が飲(の)み<b>たい</b>。
I'm thirsty; I want some water.
</pre>

<pre>
いつかゆっくり旅(たび)に出(で)<b>たいものだ</b>。
I do hope to get away on a proper trip someday.
</pre>

The traffic runs one way. <code>いつか旅(たび)に出(で)たい</code> is perfectly
good Japanese - plain `たい` is always available. But
❌<code>水(みず)が飲(の)みたいものだ</code> is not, because thirst is not the
kind of thing you hold as an ideal. Add `ものだ` when you want the sentence to
sound like a reflection, never when you want a glass of water.

### ～てほしいものだ vs ～てほしい・～ていただけませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てほしい` and its polite request forms **ask**.
`てほしいものだ` **comments** - usually about a third party, usually with an edge.

</div>

<pre>
すみません、窓(まど)を閉(し)め<b>ていただけませんか</b>。
Excuse me - could you close the window?
</pre>

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)の中(なか)では静(しず)かにし<b>てほしいものだ</b>。
People really ought to keep quiet on the train.
</pre>

The second sentence is said *about* the noisy passengers, typically muttered rather
than addressed. Turned on the person in front of you it becomes a sharp complaint,
and it is a genuine social risk for a learner who has read that `てほしい` means
"I want you to" and assumed `ものだ` merely makes it more polite. It does the
opposite.

### ～たいものだ vs ～たらいい・～といいのだが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たいものだ` is a wish about **something you would do**.
`たらいい` and `といい` wish for a **state of affairs**, including things you have
no part in.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)晴(は)れる<b>といいのだが</b>。
I do hope it clears up tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
定年(ていねん)のあとは畑(はたけ)をやり<b>たいものだ</b>。
After I retire I'd like to keep a vegetable patch.
</pre>

`たい` needs an action performed by the person wanting it, so the weather is out of
reach: ❌<code>明日(あした)晴(は)れたいものだ</code>. Both patterns are
wistful and both look forward, but only `といい` can wish for something the speaker
cannot do.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ コーヒーが飲(の)み<b>たいものです</b>。（answering "what would you like?"） → ✅ コーヒーが飲(の)み<b>たいです</b>。
  A concrete want in the moment takes plain `たい`. `ものだ` turns it into a
  life ambition.

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、この書類(しょるい)を見(み)<b>てほしいものです</b>。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、この書類(しょるい)を見(み)<b>ていただけませんか</b>。
  Aimed at the person you are addressing, `てほしいものだ` is a reproach. To a
  superior it is close to rude.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは留学(りゅうがく)し<b>たいものだ</b>。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは留学(りゅうがく)し<b>たがっている</b>。
  Like plain `たい`, this is about the speaker's own feelings. Somebody else's wish
  takes `たがる`.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が止(や)み<b>たいものだ</b>。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が止(や)む<b>といいのだが</b>。
  `たい` needs a subject who performs the action and wants to. Rain does not want
  things.

## Practice

**1.** 人(ひと)に注意(ちゅうい)する前(まえ)に、まず自分(じぶん)のことを考(かんが)え____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

考(かんが)え<b>てほしいものだ</b>。

The sentence is a comment on how people in general behave, not a request made to
anybody in particular - which is precisely the `てほしいものだ` slot. Note the
disapproval it carries: this is a grumble, and it is meant to be.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 「何(なに)が食(た)べたいですか。」「ラーメンが食(た)べ（たいです・たいものです）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「ラーメンが食(た)べ<b>たいです</b>。」

The question asks what the speaker wants right now, and a concrete answer takes
plain `たい`. <code>食(た)べたいものです</code> would present ramen as a
long-cherished aspiration, which is funny rather than wrong.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I do wish young people would read more books."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

若(わか)い人(ひと)にはもっと本(ほん)を読(よ)ん<b>でほしいものです</b>。

<code>読(よ)む</code> gives the で-form, so the pattern surfaces as
`でほしいものです`. This is the generous end of the pattern - a general hope about a
whole group - but even here you can hear a note of "they don't, though".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)には課長(かちょう)にも出席(しゅっせき)してほしいものですので、連絡(れんらく)しておきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)には課長(かちょう)にも出席(しゅっせき)し<b>てほしい</b>ので、連絡(れんらく)しておきます。

`てほしいものだ` is a wistful comment, not a practical want, so it cannot serve as
the reason for arranging something. Plain `てほしい` states the actual requirement.
As written, the sentence also grumbles about the section chief on the way past.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 静(しず)かにし<b>てほしい</b>。
(b) 静(しず)かにし<b>てほしいものだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a want directed at someone. Said to their face it is a request - a blunt one
in this plain form, but a request, and the natural response is for them to quieten
down.

(b) steps back and comments on how people ought to behave. It is normally said
about someone rather than to them, within earshot at most, and it carries an
audible sigh of disapproval. It is not how you ask for anything.

</details>

# ～てかなわない

**Meaning** · I can't stand it; it's more than I can take - a complaint about something being inflicted on you

`かなわない` is the negative of `かなう`, "to be a match for something". What you
cannot match, you cannot withstand. In this construction it is written in kana, and
it always **complains**: unlike `てたまらない`, which will happily report joy, this
pattern needs a burden - heat, noise, a commute, somebody's habits - and it wants
that burden gone.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | ～くて + かなわない | 暑(あつ)い → 暑(あつ)くてかなわない |
| な-adjective | ～で + かなわない | 面倒(めんどう) → 面倒(めんどう)でかなわない |
| Verb (passive / causative) | て-form + かなわない | 待(ま)たされる → 待(ま)たされてかなわない |
| Repeated burden | こう…～ては + かなわない | 降(ふ)る → こう降(ふ)られてはかなわない |
| Polite | ～てかないません | 痛(いた)い → 痛(いた)くてかないません |
| Past | ～てかなわなかった | 眠(ねむ)い → 眠(ねむ)くてかなわなかった |
| Casual grumble | ～てかなわん | 暑(あつ)い → 暑(あつ)くてかなわん |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `かなう` is an う-verb, so the strictly regular polite form is
`かないません`, and <code>暑(あつ)くてかないません</code> is correct. It is
noticeably less common than the plain form, though; in careful speech people more
often say <code>暑(あつ)くてかなわないです</code> or rephrase altogether.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)は暑(あつ)く<b>てかなわない</b>。
This summer's heat is more than I can take.
</pre>

<pre>
隣(となり)の工事(こうじ)の音(おと)がうるさく<b>てかなわない</b>。
The noise from the building work next door is unbearable.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)同(おな)じ書類(しょるい)を書(か)かされて、面倒(めんどう)<b>でかなわない</b>。
Having to fill in the same form every day is a nuisance beyond bearing.
</pre>

<pre>
この靴(くつ)は少(すこ)し小(ちい)さくて、歩(ある)くと痛(いた)く<b>てかないません</b>。
These shoes are slightly too small, and walking in them is agony.
</pre>

<pre>
こう毎日(まいにち)雨(あめ)に降(ふ)られ<b>てはかなわない</b>。洗濯物(せんたくもの)が乾(かわ)かない。
Being rained on day after day like this is too much. The washing won't dry.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)のたびに一時間(いちじかん)も待(ま)たされ<b>てはかなわない</b>ので、時間(じかん)を決(き)めてもらった。
Being kept waiting an hour before every meeting was intolerable, so I got them to fix a time.
</pre>

<pre>
家賃(やちん)が安(やす)いのはありがたいが、部屋(へや)がこんなに狭(せま)く<b>てはかなわない</b>。
The low rent is welcome, but a room this cramped is more than I can put up with.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)の昔話(むかしばなし)が長(なが)く<b>てかなわない</b>と、若(わか)い社員(しゃいん)たちがこぼしていた。
The younger staff were grumbling that the section chief's stories about the old days go on forever.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Three things are constant. It is always a **complaint** - there is no positive use,
because delight is not something you have to withstand. The source of the trouble
is normally **external**: the weather, a machine, a noise, another person's
behaviour, an institution's rules. And the burden is normally **repeated** rather
than a single incident, which is why the `こう…ては` frame - "if it keeps up like
this" - is so at home with it.

In tone it is a grumble, and a somewhat old-fashioned one. In casual speech it
leans older and more male, and `かなわん` in particular is a markedly blokey,
faintly regional-sounding growl. Those are tendencies: speakers of every
description use the pattern, and in writing it is unmarked. It is also simply less
frequent than `しょうがない` and `たまらない`, so a learner will meet it in fiction
dialogue, essays and letters of complaint more often than in everyday chat.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - keep this apart from `～にはかなわない`, "cannot compete with".
That is the same verb doing its ordinary job:
<code>兄(あに)にはかなわない</code> means your brother is better than you, and has
nothing to do with endurance. The give-away is what precedes `かなわない` - a
て-form, or a noun plus `には`.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～てかなわない vs ～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たまらない` is any feeling at its limit, **good or bad**.
`かなわない` is always a **burden you would like removed**.

</div>

<pre>
孫(まご)に会(あ)えるのが楽(たの)しみ<b>でたまらない</b>。
I can't wait to see my grandchildren.
</pre>

<pre>
毎週(まいしゅう)報告書(ほうこくしょ)を出(だ)させられ<b>てかなわない</b>。
Being made to file a report every week is too much.
</pre>

❌<code>楽(たの)しみでかなわない</code> does not work: there is nothing to
withstand. That single question - *would I like this taken away?* - sorts the two
patterns reliably, and it is worth asking before you choose.

### ～てかなわない vs ～てしょうがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they overlap on complaints. `しょうがない` = "and there is
nothing I can do about it". `かなわない` = "and I cannot take any more of it" - the
sharper grumble, and more often aimed at something someone else is doing.

</div>

<pre>
花粉(かふん)で鼻(はな)が出(で)<b>てしょうがない</b>。
The pollen has my nose running non-stop.
</pre>

<pre>
隣(となり)の犬(いぬ)が一晩中(ひとばんじゅう)鳴(な)い<b>てかなわない</b>。
The neighbour's dog barks all night and I've had enough of it.
</pre>

The pollen is nobody's fault and there is no one to appeal to; the dog has an
owner, and the sentence is a step away from a complaint to their face. Both
patterns will stretch beyond that division, but it is the one they start from, and
it explains why `かなわない` so often precedes a demand.

### ～てかなわない vs ～にはかなわない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same verb, two unrelated constructions. After a **て-form** you
are being worn down. After a **noun + には** you are being outmatched.

</div>

<pre>
走(はし)る速(はや)さでは弟(おとうと)<b>にはかなわない</b>。
When it comes to running speed, I'm no match for my younger brother.
</pre>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)のいびきがうるさく<b>てかなわない</b>。
My brother's snoring is unbearable.
</pre>

Both sentences are about the same brother and share a verb, and they have nothing
in common beyond that. Read what comes immediately before `かなわない` and the
ambiguity disappears at once.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 暑(あつ)い<b>でかなわない</b> → ✅ 暑(あつ)く<b>てかなわない</b>
  い-adjectives connect with `くて`; `で` is for な-adjectives.

- ❌ 合格(ごうかく)して嬉(うれ)しく<b>てかなわない</b>。 → ✅ 合格(ごうかく)して嬉(うれ)しく<b>てたまらない</b>。
  There is nothing to endure in good news. `かなわない` only ever complains.

- ❌ 将棋(しょうぎ)では祖父(そふ)<b>てかなわない</b>。 → ✅ 将棋(しょうぎ)では祖父(そふ)<b>にはかなわない</b>。
  With a noun the construction is `にはかなわない`, "no match for". The て-form
  version needs a て-form.

- ❌ 一度(いちど)待(ま)たされ<b>てかなわなかった</b>。 → ✅ 何度(なんど)も待(ま)たされ<b>てかなわなかった</b>。
  A single incident sits badly here. The pattern wants a burden that keeps coming
  back, which is also why `こう…ては` fits it so well.

## Practice

**1.** この季節(きせつ)は虫(むし)が多(おお)く____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

虫(むし)が多(おお)く<b>てかなわない</b>。

<code>多(おお)い</code> is an い-adjective, so it connects with `くて`. The insects
are an external nuisance that recurs every year, which is exactly the sort of
burden this pattern was made for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 将棋(しょうぎ)では祖父(そふ)（にはかなわない・てかなわない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

将棋(しょうぎ)では祖父(そふ)<b>にはかなわない</b>。

A noun in front of `かなわない` puts you in the "no match for" construction: the
grandfather is the better player. `てかなわない` requires a て-form and would be
saying that the grandfather was unbearable.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Changing trains three times every morning is more than I can stand."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

毎朝(まいあさ)三回(さんかい)も電車(でんしゃ)を乗(の)り換(か)えるのは、面倒(めんどう)<b>でかなわない</b>。

<code>面倒(めんどう)</code> is a な-adjective, so it takes `で`. Note
<code>毎朝(まいあさ)</code>: the daily repetition is what makes `かなわない` the
right choice rather than a one-off complaint.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 久(ひさ)しぶりに友達(ともだち)に会(あ)えて、嬉(うれ)しくてかなわなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 久(ひさ)しぶりに友達(ともだち)に会(あ)えて、嬉(うれ)しく<b>てたまらなかった</b>。

`かなわない` needs something the speaker would like to be rid of, and nobody wants
to be rid of seeing an old friend. For an intense pleasant feeling the pattern is
`てたまらない`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この部屋(へや)は寒(さむ)く<b>てしょうがない</b>。
(b) この部屋(へや)は寒(さむ)く<b>てかなわない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both complain about the same cold room. (a) says the speaker can do nothing about
it: the emphasis is on helplessness, and the sentence could easily end there with a
shrug.

(b) says the speaker has reached their limit with it, and it points a finger.
Somebody should have fixed the heating, and this is the sentence that comes just
before asking them to. (b) is the more irritated of the two, and the more likely to
be followed by a demand.

</details>

# ～ずじまい

**Meaning** · ended up never doing it - you meant to, the chance went by, and now it is too late

`じまい` is <code>仕舞(しま)い</code>, "the end", with the `し` voiced; `ず` is
the classical negative you already know from `ずに`. Put together they read almost
literally: *and that was the end of it, undone*. Three things have to be true for
the pattern to fit. You **intended** to do the thing. You **did not**. And the
opportunity is now **gone** - which is why the sentence always carries at least a
trace of regret.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-stem + ずじまい | 言(い)う → 言(い)わずじまい |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-stem + ずじまい | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べずじまい |
| Verb (する) | **せずじまい** | 連絡(れんらく)する → 連絡(れんらく)せずじまい |
| Verb (来(く)る) | ない-stem + ずじまい | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ずじまい |
| Potential verb | potential ない-stem + ずじまい | 渡(わた)せる → 渡(わた)せずじまい |
| Usual predicate | ～ずじまいだ ／ ～ずじまいだった | 会(あ)う → 会(あ)わずじまいだった |
| Ending up so | ～ずじまいになる ／ ～ずじまいに終(お)わる | 使(つか)う → 使(つか)わずじまいに終(お)わった |
| Modifying what follows | ～ずじまいの ／ ～ずじまいで | 出(だ)す → 出(だ)さずじまいの手紙(てがみ) |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative here is the classical `ず`, so `する` gives
<code>せず</code>: ✅<code>連絡(れんらく)せずじまい</code>,
❌<code>連絡(れんらく)しずじまい</code>. And it is never
❌<code>読(よ)まないじまい</code> - `ない` cannot take the place of `ず` in this
compound.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>結局(けっきょく)</code>, `とうとう` and `ついに` sit in front of this
pattern constantly. The sentence is a summing-up, so it likes an adverb that says
so.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)ったのに、差(さ)さ<b>ずじまい</b>だった。
I took an umbrella and never put it up.
</pre>

<pre>
結局(けっきょく)、本(ほん)は読(よ)ま<b>ずじまい</b>で返(かえ)した。
In the end I returned the book without ever reading it.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)まで行(い)ったのに、お寺(てら)は回(まわ)ら<b>ずじまい</b>だった。
I went all the way to Kyoto and never got round to the temples.
</pre>

<pre>
とうとう本人(ほんにん)には連絡(れんらく)せ<b>ずじまい</b>になってしまった。
In the end I never did get in touch with him directly.
</pre>

<pre>
渡(わた)そうと思(おも)っていた手紙(てがみ)は、渡(わた)せ<b>ずじまい</b>だ。
The letter I meant to hand over is still undelivered.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)買(か)ったスキーの道具(どうぐ)は、一度(いちど)も使(つか)わ<b>ずじまい</b>で物置(ものおき)に眠(ねむ)っている。
The ski gear I bought last year is still sitting in the shed, never once used.
</pre>

<pre>
お礼(れい)を言(い)おうと思(おも)っていたが、先生(せんせい)が転勤(てんきん)してしまい、言(い)わ<b>ずじまい</b>になった。
I meant to thank her, but the teacher was transferred and I never did.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの準備(じゅんび)に追(お)われて、近所(きんじょ)の方(かた)に挨拶(あいさつ)もでき<b>ずじまい</b>のまま町(まち)を出(で)た。
Busy with the move, I left town without ever managing to say goodbye to the neighbours.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The tone is retrospective and faintly literary. You will meet it in narration,
diaries, travel writing and reminiscence, and also in perfectly ordinary speech
whenever somebody is summing up a disappointment:
「結局(けっきょく)会(あ)わずじまいだったよ」. It is not formal exactly - it is
**wistful**, which is a different thing.

The **intention is not optional**. If buying a lottery ticket never crossed your
mind, <code>宝(たから)くじは買(か)わずじまいだった</code> is simply the wrong
sentence; you want plain <code>買(か)わなかった</code>. The regret is baked in too, so where
there is genuinely no regret, the plain past negative is again what you want.

In practice the pattern is almost always **past**, because it reports an outcome.
But it does reach forward when the outcome is already effectively settled:
<code>このままでは会(あ)わずじまいになりそうだ</code> - "at this rate we'll end
up never meeting" - is natural, and so is
<code>～ずじまいになってしまう</code>. What it cannot do is announce a decision. A
plan not to do something tomorrow is `～ないつもりだ`.

One structural point: `ずじまい` is verbal only. There is no noun row in the table
above because there is no noun form.

## Contrast

### ～ずじまい vs ～ないまま

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ないまま` describes a **state that persisted**: the thing was
simply not done. `ずじまい` adds that somebody **meant to** do it and the chance is
now gone.

</div>

<pre>
理由(りゆう)が分(わ)から<b>ないまま</b>、話(はなし)は終(お)わった。
The discussion ended with the reason still unclear.
</pre>

<pre>
理由(りゆう)は聞(き)か<b>ずじまい</b>だった。
I never did get round to asking why.
</pre>

`ないまま` is neutral. It will describe anything that stayed undone, including
things nobody had any intention of doing, and including states rather than actions
- <code>分(わ)からないまま</code> is about knowledge, not about a missed task.
`ずじまい` always has a person in it who wanted to and did not.

### ～ずじまい vs the plain ～なかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なかった` reports the **bare fact**. `ずじまい` reports the same
fact as a **missed opportunity**.

</div>

<pre>
北海道(ほっかいどう)では一度(いちど)も雪(ゆき)を見(み)<b>なかった</b>。
I didn't see any snow at all in Hokkaido.
</pre>

<pre>
楽(たの)しみにしていた流氷(りゅうひょう)は、見(み)<b>ずじまい</b>だった。
I never did get to see the drift ice I had been looking forward to.
</pre>

Take <code>楽(たの)しみにしていた</code> out of the second sentence and it
weakens noticeably, because the pattern is leaning on it: something in the context
has to establish that the speaker wanted this. `なかった` needs no such support and
carries no feeling of its own.

### ～ずじまい vs ～そびれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そびれる` names the **moment of failing** - you missed your
chance to. `ずじまい` names the **outcome** - the whole affair ended undone.

</div>

<pre>
話(はな)しかけようとしたが、言(い)い<b>そびれて</b>しまった。
I was about to say something, but I missed my moment.
</pre>

<pre>
結局(けっきょく)、最後(さいご)まで言(い)わ<b>ずじまい</b>だった。
In the end I never said it at all.
</pre>

`そびれる` - and its close relative <code>損(そこ)なう</code>, as in
<code>見(み)損(そこ)なう</code> - can describe one blocked attempt in the
middle of a story, with more attempts still to come. `ずじまい` is the verdict at
the end of the story, and there is nothing after it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 連絡(れんらく)<b>しずじまい</b> → ✅ 連絡(れんらく)<b>せずじまい</b>
  As everywhere with `ず`, `する` becomes <code>せず</code>. There is no
  <code>しず</code> anywhere in Japanese.

- ❌ 読(よ)ま<b>ないじまい</b> → ✅ 読(よ)ま<b>ずじまい</b>
  The negative in this compound is the classical `ず`, not `ない`. The two are not
  interchangeable here.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は行(い)か<b>ずじまい</b>だ。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は行(い)か<b>ないつもりだ</b>。
  The pattern looks back at a chance already lost. It cannot announce a decision
  about tomorrow.

- ❌ 興味(きょうみ)がなかったので、映画(えいが)は見(み)<b>ずじまい</b>だった。 → ✅ 興味(きょうみ)がなかったので、映画(えいが)は見(み)<b>なかった</b>。
  No intention, no regret, no `ずじまい`. The first clause has removed the very
  thing the pattern needs.

## Practice

**1.** 温泉(おんせん)に入(はい)ろうと思(おも)っていたのに、雨(あめ)で入(はい)ら____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

入(はい)ら<b>ずじまいだった</b>。

<code>入(はい)ろうと思(おも)っていた</code> supplies the intention the pattern
requires, and the rain removed the opportunity. Without that first clause, plain
<code>入(はい)らなかった</code> would be the natural sentence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 名前(なまえ)を聞(き)こうとしたが、人(ひと)が多(おお)くて（聞(き)きそびれた・聞(き)かずじまいだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

人(ひと)が多(おお)くて<b>聞(き)きそびれた</b>。

This is one blocked attempt in the middle of an evening - the crowd got in the way
at that moment. `ずじまい` would be the verdict once the whole evening was over and
the chance had gone for good.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I carried my camera all the way up the mountain and never took a single photo."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

カメラを山(やま)まで持(も)って行(い)ったのに、一枚(いちまい)も撮(と)ら<b>ずじまい</b>だった。

<code>のに</code> and the effort of carrying the camera up establish the intention.
<code>一枚(いちまい)も</code> with a negative gives "not a single one", and the
`ずじまい` supplies the regret.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 準備(じゅんび)が間(ま)に合(あ)わず、発表(はっぴょう)はしずじまいになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 準備(じゅんび)が間(ま)に合(あ)わず、発表(はっぴょう)は<b>せずじまい</b>になった。

`する` takes the irregular `せず`. Note that the same sentence already uses the
correct form earlier - <code>間(ま)に合(あ)わず</code> - which is a useful
reminder that this `ず` is one thing wherever it appears.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)には会(あ)わ<b>なかった</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)には会(あ)わ<b>ずじまい</b>だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the bare fact, and it is neutral about why. It could even be deliberate -
you may have gone out of your way to avoid him.

(b) says you meant to see him, that circumstances kept getting in the way, and that
the visit is now not going to happen. The sentence sighs. It also implies a
finished period - a trip that has ended, a stay that is over - after which the
opportunity no longer exists.

</details>
