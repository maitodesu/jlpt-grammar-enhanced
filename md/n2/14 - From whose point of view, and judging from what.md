# N2 - From whose point of view, and judging from what


---

# ～からして

**Meaning** · judging from ~; going by ~ - names the one clue the speaker is reasoning from

<code>からして</code> puts a finger on a single piece of evidence - a face, a price, a
tone of voice - and lets a conclusion fall out of it. It is a **grounds** marker,
not a viewpoint marker: it says *what* the judgement rests on, never *whose*
judgement it is. A second, closely related use picks the most basic item in a set
and condemns the whole through it: 名前(なまえ)からして - "even the name, before
you get to anything else."

## Formation

<code>からして</code> attaches to nouns only.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + からして | 顔色(かおいろ) → 顔色(かおいろ)からして |
| Demonstrative + noun | この / あの + Noun + からして | 様子(ようす) → あの様子(ようす)からして |
| Nominalised quality | ～さ + からして | 早(はや)さ → 返事(へんじ)の早(はや)さからして |
| Clause, nominalised | Clause + こと + からして | 断(ことわ)られた → 断(ことわ)られたことからして |
| "even ~" use | Noun + からして | 名前(なまえ) → 名前(なまえ)からして怪(あや)しい |
| Politeness | carried by the main clause | … → …からして無理(むり)でしょう |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a plain verb or adjective cannot sit in front of <code>からして</code>.
To reason from a whole clause you need <code>ことからして</code>, or the separate
pattern <code>ところを見(み)ると</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの顔色(かおいろ)<b>からして</b>、熱(ねつ)がありそうだ。
Going by the colour of his face, he's probably running a fever.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)<b>からして</b>怪(あや)しい会社(かいしゃ)だった。
Even the company's name was dodgy.
</pre>

<pre>
この値段(ねだん)<b>からして</b>、高級(こうきゅう)な店(みせ)にちがいない。
Judging by these prices, it must be an upmarket place.
</pre>

<pre>
服装(ふくそう)<b>からして</b>、彼(かれ)は面接(めんせつ)に来(き)たわけではないらしい。
Going by what he's wearing, he doesn't seem to be here for an interview.
</pre>

<pre>
この計画(けいかく)は予算(よさん)<b>からして</b>無理(むり)がある。
The budget alone already tells you this plan doesn't work.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)の早(はや)さ<b>からして</b>、前(まえ)から準備(じゅんび)していたに違(ちが)いない。
Going by how fast she replied, she must have had it ready in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
挨拶(あいさつ)の仕方(しかた)<b>からして</b>、あの二人(ふたり)はもう知(し)り合(あ)いだったのだろう。
From the way they greeted each other, those two must have already known one another.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)の静(しず)かさ<b>からして</b>、客(きゃく)は半分(はんぶん)も入(はい)っていなかったのではないかと思(おも)う。
Going by how quiet the hall was, I doubt the place was even half full.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>からして</code> is register-neutral in form, but it has a considered,
slightly analytical flavour - it is the sentence of someone who has stopped and
looked. It is at home in written commentary and in spoken deduction alike, and it
takes です/ます politeness on the main clause with no change to itself.

In the "judging from" use the conclusion is a **guess**, so the sentence normally
closes with だろう, らしい, ようだ, にちがいない or そうだ. Ending flat, as a bare
statement of fact, leaves the sentence sounding as though the speaker had confused
a clue with a proof.

The "even ~" use is usually **critical**. 字(じ)からして読(よ)めない,
説明書(せつめいしょ)からしてわかりにくい - the speaker picks the easiest thing to
get right, shows that it was got wrong, and leaves the rest implied. It can be
complimentary in principle, but in practice you will meet it far more often as a
complaint. That is a tendency of use, not a rule of grammar.

One thing <code>からして</code> cannot do: mark a person whose standpoint you are
adopting. ❌親(おや)からして心配(しんぱい)だ is not "from a parent's point of
view"; for that you need <code>からすれば</code>, <code>から見(み)れば</code> or
<code>にしたら</code>. If a person noun does appear with <code>からして</code>, it
is read as evidence - "going by the father, ..." - which is rarely what the learner
meant.

## Contrast

### からして vs からすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>からして</code> reasons from **one concrete clue**;
<code>からすると</code> weighs information, and can also mean "from X's standpoint".

</div>

<pre>
話(はな)し方(かた)<b>からして</b>、関西(かんさい)の人(ひと)だろう。
From the way he talks, he's probably from Kansai.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)までの記録(きろく)<b>からすると</b>、優勝(ゆうしょう)は難(むずか)しい。
Going by the record so far, winning the title looks unlikely.
</pre>

Swap them in the first sentence and little changes. Swap them in
親(おや)からすれば心配(しんぱい)だ and the sentence breaks, because
<code>からして</code> has no standpoint reading at all. Whenever the noun is a
**person whose eyes you are borrowing**, <code>からして</code> is the wrong choice.

### からして vs ところを見(み)ると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>からして</code> takes a **noun** you can point at;
<code>ところを見(み)ると</code> takes a whole **observed clause**.

</div>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の様子(ようす)<b>からして</b>、急(いそ)いで出(で)かけたようだ。
From the state of the desk, it looks like she left in a hurry.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)が散(ち)らかっている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、急(いそ)いで出(で)かけたようだ。
Judging by the fact that the desk is a mess, she left in a hurry.
</pre>

Same deduction, different packaging. If what you want to name is an event or a
state - something with a verb in it - you cannot compress it into
<code>からして</code> without nominalising it first.

### The "even ~" からして vs さえ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - "even" <code>からして</code> picks the **most basic** member
and judges the whole by it. `さえ` picks an **extreme** member and lets the ordinary
ones follow.

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は表紙(ひょうし)<b>からして</b>安(やす)っぽい。
This dictionary looks cheap right down to the cover.
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)は先生(せんせい)<b>さえ</b>読(よ)めなかった。
Even the teacher couldn't read this kanji.
</pre>

The direction of surprise is opposite. `さえ` means "if even *that* one, then
certainly the rest"; <code>からして</code> means "it fails at the very first
hurdle, so imagine the rest". Putting `さえ` into the first sentence loses the
meaning entirely.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 親(おや)<b>からして</b>心配(しんぱい)だ。 → ✅ 親(おや)<b>からすれば</b>心配(しんぱい)だ。
  <code>からして</code> marks the evidence, not the person whose view it is.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>からして</b>、中止(ちゅうし)だろう。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)の降(ふ)り方(かた)<b>からして</b>、中止(ちゅうし)だろう。
  A plain clause cannot precede it. Turn the clause into a noun, or use
  <code>ところを見(み)ると</code>.

- ❌ この様子(ようす)<b>からして</b>、合格(ごうかく)した。 → ✅ この様子(ようす)<b>からして</b>、合格(ごうかく)したのだろう。
  The main clause is an inference, so it needs a conjecture ending.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がない<b>からして</b>、もう帰(かえ)る。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がない<b>から</b>、もう帰(かえ)る。
  <code>からして</code> is not a fancier "because". The `から` inside it is the
  starting-point `から`, not the reason `から`.

The way he walks is a single visible clue, and the conclusion is a guess ending in
らしい - the standard shape for this pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 子(こ)ども（からして・からすれば）、大人(おとな)の都合(つごう)など関係(かんけい)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)ども<b>からすれば</b>、大人(おとな)の都合(つごう)など関係(かんけい)ない。

The noun here is a person whose viewpoint we are borrowing, and

</details>

The smell is the one clue being reasoned from, so it takes

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)が遅(おく)れているからして、電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっているのだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)が遅(おく)れていることからして、電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっているのだろう。
（or 彼(かれ)が遅(おく)れているところを見(み)ると、…）

<code>からして</code> needs a noun in front of it. Adding こと nominalises the
clause; otherwise switch to <code>ところを見(み)ると</code>, which takes clauses
directly.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この店(みせ)は値段(ねだん)<b>からして</b>高(たか)い。
(b) この店(みせ)は値段(ねだん)<b>からすると</b>高級(こうきゅう)な店(みせ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the "even ~" use: the prices are the first thing you notice, and they are
already steep - the implication is that everything else about the place is
expensive too. It reads as a complaint.

(b) is the "judging from" use: the speaker has looked at the prices and is drawing
a conclusion about what kind of shop this is. It is an assessment, not a grumble,
and the prices are evidence rather than the first item in a list of grievances.

</details>

# ～からすると / ～からすれば

**Meaning** · judging from ~; from ~'s point of view - either the evidence a judgement rests on, or the person whose eyes it is made through

This pattern does two jobs with one shape. With a noun that is **information** -
figures, weather, a track record - it says "weighing that up, here is my
conclusion." With a noun that is a **person or group** it says "step into their
position and this is how it looks." English "from" covers both, which is exactly
why learners stop noticing that they are different jobs.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (evidence) | Noun + からすると | この成績(せいせき) → この成績(せいせき)からすると |
| Noun (evidence), if-flavoured | Noun + からすれば | 前例(ぜんれい) → 前例(ぜんれい)からすれば |
| Noun (person / group) | Noun + からすれば | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)からすれば |
| Casual variant | Noun + からしたら | 親(おや) → 親(おや)からしたら |
| Modifying phrase + noun | Clause + Noun + からすると | 残(のこ)っている量(りょう) → 残(のこ)っている量(りょう)からすると |
| Politeness | carried by the main clause | … → …からすると難(むずか)しいでしょう |

<div class="note">

**Note** - only a noun may precede it. There is no ✗降(ふ)っているからすると.
Nominalise first（降(ふ)り方(かた)からすると）or switch to
<code>ところを見(み)ると</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この天気(てんき)<b>からすると</b>、明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)になりそうだ。
Going by this weather, tomorrow will probably turn to snow.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)ども<b>からすれば</b>、大人(おとな)の話(はなし)は退屈(たいくつ)なだけだ。
From a child's point of view, grown-ups' conversation is just boring.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)までの経験(けいけん)<b>からすると</b>、この工事(こうじ)は三日(みっか)では終(お)わらない。
Going by past experience, this work won't be finished in three days.
</pre>

<pre>
外国人(がいこくじん)観光客(かんこうきゃく)<b>からすれば</b>、券売機(けんばいき)の操作(そうさ)はかなり難(むずか)しいらしい。
For foreign tourists, working the ticket machines is apparently quite a struggle.
</pre>

<pre>
売(う)り上(あ)げ<b>からすると</b>、今年(ことし)のボーナスはあまり期待(きたい)できない。
Judging by the sales figures, we can't expect much of a bonus this year.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)の性格(せいかく)<b>からしたら</b>、自分(じぶん)から謝(あやま)ることはないだろうね。
Knowing what he's like, he's hardly going to apologise first, is he.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)<b>からすれば</b>、若(わか)い社員(しゃいん)に任(まか)せるのは不安(ふあん)なのかもしれない。
From the manager's side, handing it to a junior staff member is probably a worry.
</pre>

<pre>
残(のこ)っている材料(ざいりょう)の量(りょう)<b>からすると</b>、今日(きょう)の注文(ちゅうもん)をさばくのがやっとだ。
Going by how much stock is left, we'll only just manage today's orders.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The three forms are close, and in most sentences they swap freely. The tendencies
are worth knowing:

- <code>からすると</code> is the most neutral and the most analytical. It reads
  like "on the evidence of", and it dominates in written reasoning.
- <code>からすれば</code> carries the conditional `ば`, so it leans a little more
  towards "*if* you take that as your starting point" - which is why it is the
  natural choice when you are stepping into someone's position.
- <code>からしたら</code> is the spoken one. It is completely ordinary in
  conversation and slightly casual in writing.

The whole family is register-neutral otherwise: politeness sits on the main clause,
and です/ます changes nothing about the pattern.

The standpoint use has a social function worth noticing. Saying
部下(ぶか)からすれば… is almost always the opening of an argument for
**understanding** someone - it invites the listener to grant that the other party
has a reasonable case, and it very often sets up a contrast with the speaker's own
position. That is why it pairs so naturally with かもしれない and だろう rather than
with flat assertions.

One thing this pattern is not: an evaluation frame. <code>にとって</code> is the
word for "for X, this is important / necessary / difficult".
<code>からすれば</code> needs a **judgement** in the main clause, something the
person in that position would conclude, not an adjective describing how the thing
sits with them.

## Contrast

### からすると vs にとって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にとって</code> names the party something **matters to**;
<code>からすれば</code> names the party whose **judgement** you are reporting.

</div>

<pre>
この写真(しゃしん)は家族(かぞく)<b>にとって</b>大切(たいせつ)な思(おも)い出(で)です。
This photo is a precious memory for the family.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)<b>からすれば</b>、急(きゅう)に海外(かいがい)へ行(い)くと言(い)われても困(こま)る。
From the family's side, being told out of the blue that he's moving abroad is not easy.
</pre>

Swapping them breaks both. The first needs a party the photo *is precious to*; the
second needs a party doing the reacting. The test: if the main clause is
大切(たいせつ)だ, 必要(ひつよう)だ, 難(むずか)しい or similar - a plain
evaluation - reach for <code>にとって</code>.

### からすると vs から見(み)ると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they overlap heavily. <code>から見(み)ると</code> adds a
literal "seen from" reading that <code>からすると</code> does not have.

</div>

<pre>
飛行機(ひこうき)の窓(まど)<b>から見(み)ると</b>、畑(はたけ)が模様(もよう)のようだった。
Seen from the plane window, the fields looked like a pattern.
</pre>

<pre>
この数字(すうじ)<b>からすると</b>、来月(らいげつ)には在庫(ざいこ)が足(た)りなくなる。
Going by these figures, we'll run out of stock next month.
</pre>

The first cannot take <code>からすると</code>: a window is a vantage point, not
evidence. In the inference sense, though, the two are near-interchangeable, and no
one will notice which you chose.

### からすると vs にしたら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にしたら</code> takes **only a person**;
<code>からすると</code> takes a person **or** a body of evidence.

</div>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>にしたら</b>、敬語(けいご)は覚(おぼ)えることが多(おお)すぎる。
For an exchange student, honorific language is just too much to memorise.
</pre>

<pre>
留学生(りゅうがくせい)の数(かず)<b>からすると</b>、この教室(きょうしつ)では狭(せま)すぎる。
Going by the number of exchange students, this classroom is too small.
</pre>

Put <code>にしたら</code> into the second sentence and it fails - a *number* has no
point of view. That is the one-line test that keeps the two apart.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 家族(かぞく)<b>からすると</b>大切(たいせつ)な写真(しゃしん)だ。 → ✅ 家族(かぞく)<b>にとって</b>大切(たいせつ)な写真(しゃしん)だ。
  A bare evaluation adjective wants <code>にとって</code>. This pattern needs a
  judgement, not a rating.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>からすると</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)だろう。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)の強(つよ)さ<b>からすると</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)だろう。
  Only a noun can precede it.

- ❌ この様子(ようす)<b>からすると</b>、早(はや)く帰(かえ)れ。 → ✅ この様子(ようす)<b>からすると</b>、早(はや)く帰(かえ)ったほうがいい。
  What follows is the speaker's conclusion. A bare command does not sit in that
  slot.

- ❌ 私(わたし)に<b>からすれば</b>反対(はんたい)だ。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>からすれば</b>反対(はんたい)だ。（or 私(わたし)としては反対(はんたい)だ）
  No `に` before it. And for stating your own position, としては is the more usual
  choice.

**1.** この足跡(あしあと)の大(おお)きさ____、かなり大(おお)きな動物(どうぶつ)だったようだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The size of the tracks is the information being weighed, and the main clause is the
conclusion drawn from it - the core evidence use of the pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新入生(しんにゅうせい)（からすれば・にとって）、大学(だいがく)の手続(てつづ)きはとても複雑(ふくざつ)だ。

<code>複雑(ふくざつ)だ</code> is a plain evaluation of how the procedures sit with

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The neighbours are a group whose position we are stepping into, so
<code>からすれば</code>, and the sentence closes with だろう because we are
supposing on their behalf.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 話(はな)している様子(ようす)をからすると、二人(ふたり)は仲直(なかなお)りしたらしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 話(はな)している様子(ようす)<b>からすると</b>、二人(ふたり)は仲直(なかなお)りしたらしい。

The stray `を` has to go: <code>からすると</code> attaches straight onto the noun.
The noun-modifying clause in front of 様子(ようす) is fine as it stands.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 店長(てんちょう)<b>からすれば</b>、アルバイトを増(ふ)やしたい。
(b) 売(う)り上(あ)げ<b>からすると</b>、アルバイトを増(ふ)やしたほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a wish held by someone else. The manager is a person, and the sentence
imagines his position - the speaker is not necessarily the manager and may well
disagree.

(b) reports the speaker's own conclusion from data. The sales figures have no
opinion; they are the evidence, and the recommendation is the speaker's. Only (b)
would be at home in a written report.

</details>

# ～から言うと

**Meaning** · in terms of ~; judged on the scale of ~ - names the yardstick the speaker is measuring by

Read <code>から言(い)うと</code>. The noun in front of it is a **criterion**:
price, safety, experience, the rules, the current state of things. The sentence
then delivers a verdict measured on exactly that scale, and says nothing about the
others. 値段(ねだん)から言(い)えば得(とく)だ concedes, without saying so,
that on some other scale it might not be.

## Formation

Three endings, all built on the same core.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (criterion) | Noun + から言(い)うと | 値段(ねだん) → 値段(ねだん)から言(い)うと |
| Same, conditional shade | Noun + から言(い)えば | 経験(けいけん) → 経験(けいけん)から言(い)えば |
| Same, more assertive | Noun + から言(い)って | 常識(じょうしき) → 常識(じょうしき)から言(い)って |
| Aspect spelled out | Noun + の面(めん) + から言(い)えば | 栄養(えいよう) → 栄養(えいよう)の面(めん)から言(い)えば |
| Fixed discourse opener | 結論(けつろん) + から言(い)うと | - → 結論(けつろん)から言(い)うと、… |
| Politeness | carried by the main clause | … → …から言(い)うと難(むずか)しいです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun must be a measure or a body of information, not a person whose
opinion you are quoting. To say "if you ask me", Japanese uses
私(わたし)に言(い)わせれば or 私(わたし)としては.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
結論(けつろん)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、この案(あん)は採用(さいよう)されなかった。
To give you the conclusion first: the proposal wasn't adopted.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)<b>から言(い)えば</b>、こちらのほうがずっと得(とく)だ。
In terms of price, this one is far better value.
</pre>

<pre>
経験(けいけん)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、最初(さいしょ)の三日(みっか)が一番(いちばん)つらい。
Speaking from experience, the first three days are the worst.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)面(めん)<b>から言(い)って</b>、この設計(せっけい)は認(みと)められない。
On safety grounds, this design can't be approved.
</pre>

<pre>
栄養(えいよう)の面(めん)<b>から言(い)えば</b>、朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を抜(ぬ)くのはよくない。
Nutritionally speaking, skipping breakfast is a bad idea.
</pre>

<pre>
現時点(げんじてん)の売(う)り上(あ)げ<b>から言(い)うと</b>、目標(もくひょう)の達成(たっせい)は難(むずか)しそうだ。
Going by the sales figures as they stand, hitting the target looks unlikely.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)の立場(たちば)<b>から言(い)えば</b>、どちらの味方(みかた)もできないのです。
Given the position I'm in, I can't take either side.
</pre>

<pre>
過去(かこ)の統計(とうけい)<b>から言(い)って</b>、この時期(じき)に台風(たいふう)が来(く)ることはめったにない。
On the historical figures, typhoons hardly ever arrive at this time of year.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written and formal-spoken** pattern. It belongs to reports, meetings,
editorials and any conversation where someone is being careful. In relaxed speech
you are far more likely to hear 値段(ねだん)で言(い)うと or just
値段(ねだん)なら.

The three endings shade differently:

- <code>から言(い)うと</code> is the plain, neutral member.
- <code>から言(い)えば</code> adds the `ば` conditional's "supposing we take
  that as the measure", which makes it slightly softer and more concessive.
- <code>から言(い)って</code> is the most assertive. It very often precedes a
  refusal or a negative verdict - 常識(じょうしき)から言(い)って
  失礼(しつれい)だ - and it can sound blunt.

<code>結論(けつろん)から言(い)うと</code> deserves separate mention: it is a
set discourse opener meaning "cutting to the answer", and it is extremely useful.
正直(しょうじき)に言(い)うと ("honestly") looks similar but is a different
construction entirely - there the adverb modifies the *manner* of speaking, not
the scale of the judgement.

Because it names a scale, this pattern is naturally paired with contrast: one
sentence gives the verdict on price, the next on quality. If you use it and then
never mention the other scales, a Japanese listener may still hear the reservation.

## Contrast

### から言(い)うと vs から見(み)ると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>から言(い)うと</code> names a **scale** you are
measuring on. <code>から見(み)ると</code> names a **vantage point** you are
looking from.

</div>

<pre>
広(ひろ)さ<b>から言(い)うと</b>この部屋(へや)で十分(じゅうぶん)だ。
In terms of floor space this room is plenty.
</pre>

<pre>
客(きゃく)<b>から見(み)ると</b>この部屋(へや)は狭(せま)く感(かん)じるだろう。
To a customer this room will feel cramped.
</pre>

The second sentence has a pair of eyes in it; the first has a tape measure. That
is why a person noun goes readily with <code>から見(み)ると</code> and awkwardly
with <code>から言(い)うと</code>.

### から言(い)うと vs としては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>から言(い)うと</code> gives the **measure**;
<code>としては</code> gives the **capacity you are speaking in**.

</div>

<pre>
規則(きそく)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、それは認(みと)められません。
By the rules, that isn't permitted.
</pre>

<pre>
担当者(たんとうしゃ)<b>としては</b>、それは認(みと)められません。
Speaking as the person in charge, I can't permit that.
</pre>

Same refusal, different authority behind it. The first hides the speaker behind a
standard; the second puts them in front of it, wearing a role. In a difficult
conversation that difference is the whole message.

### から言(い)うと vs からすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>から言(い)うと</code> **evaluates on a stated scale**;
<code>からすると</code> **infers from evidence**.

</div>

<pre>
実力(じつりょく)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、優勝(ゆうしょう)して当然(とうぜん)のチームだ。
On ability alone, this is a team that ought to win.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)の試合(しあい)<b>からすると</b>、優勝(ゆうしょう)は難(むずか)しそうだ。
Judging from yesterday's match, the title looks out of reach.
</pre>

The first weighs the team against a yardstick called "ability". The second reasons
forward from something that happened. Where the noun is straightforwardly
evidence, either can appear and the difference thins out; where the noun is a
criterion - 規則(きそく), 値段(ねだん), 安全(あんぜん) - only
<code>から言(い)うと</code> is natural.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、その意見(いけん)には賛成(さんせい)できない。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>としては</b>、その意見(いけん)には賛成(さんせい)できない。（or 私(わたし)に言(い)わせれば）
  A bare person is not a measuring scale. Add one - 私(わたし)の
  経験(けいけん)から言(い)うと - or switch pattern.

- ❌ 高(たか)い<b>から言(い)うと</b>、買(か)わないほうがいい。 → ✅ 値段(ねだん)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、買(か)わないほうがいい。
  Only a noun goes in front of it. An adjective clause cannot.

- ❌ 安全(あんぜん)面(めん)<b>から言(い)って</b>、窓(まど)を閉(し)めろ。 → ✅ 安全(あんぜん)面(めん)<b>から言(い)って</b>、窓(まど)は閉(し)めるべきだ。
  The main clause is a judgement made on the stated scale, not a shouted order.

- ❌ 結論(けつろん)から<b>話(はな)すと</b>、この案(あん)は通(とお)りました。 → ✅ 結論(けつろん)から<b>言(い)うと</b>、この案(あん)は通(とお)りました。
  The opener is fixed with 言(い)う. Substituting 話(はな)す loses the set
  phrase.

## Practice

**1.** 効率(こうりつ)____、この方法(ほうほう)は時間(じかん)がかかりすぎる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

効率(こうりつ)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、この方法(ほうほう)は時間(じかん)がかかりすぎる。

Efficiency is the scale the method is being measured against, which is the core use
of the pattern. The sentence leaves open that on some other scale - accuracy, say -
the method might be fine.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 部下(ぶか)（から言(い)うと・からすれば）、急(きゅう)な残業(ざんぎょう)は迷惑(めいわく)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部下(ぶか)<b>からすれば</b>、急(きゅう)な残業(ざんぎょう)は迷惑(めいわく)だ。

部下(ぶか) is a person whose position we are taking, not a measuring scale.
<code>から言(い)うと</code> would need something like
部下(ぶか)の立場(たちば)から言(い)うと to work.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Legally speaking, there's no problem, but I'm not sure it's the right thing to do."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

法律(ほうりつ)<b>から言(い)えば</b>問題(もんだい)はないが、それが正(ただ)しいことなのかはわからない。

The English "legally speaking" is exactly this pattern's job: it fences the verdict
off to one scale, and the が clause then admits that another scale gives a different
answer.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この辺(へん)は交通(こうつう)が不便(ふべん)だから言(い)うと、家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この辺(へん)は交通(こうつう)の便(べん)<b>から言(い)うと</b>不便(ふべん)だが、家賃(やちん)は安(やす)い。

The original glues the pattern onto a `だから` clause. The pattern needs a bare
noun, so the criterion - 交通(こうつう)の便(べん) - has to be pulled out and
the verdict put in the main clause.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この計画(けいかく)は予算(よさん)<b>から言(い)うと</b>無理(むり)だ。
(b) この計画(けいかく)は予算(よさん)<b>から言(い)って</b>無理(むり)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both measure the plan against the budget and find it unworkable, so the propositions
are the same.

(b) is firmer. <code>から言(い)って</code> presents the budget as settling the
matter, and often closes a discussion; in a meeting it can land as a flat refusal.
(a) is the neutral report of a verdict, and leaves more room for someone to answer
"but on other grounds...".

</details>

# ～から見ると

**Meaning** · seen from ~; judging from ~ - the most flexible member of this family, covering both a vantage point and a body of evidence

Read <code>から見(み)ると</code>. It is the literal "looking from" phrase put to
grammatical work, and it kept both jobs. Put a **person or institution** in front
of it and you are borrowing their eyes: 外国人(がいこくじん)から見(み)れば.
Put **evidence** in front of it and you are reasoning from what you observed:
空(そら)の様子(ようす)から見(み)て. And it still means plain "seen from"
when the noun is a place.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person / group) | Noun + から見(み)ると | 外国人(がいこくじん) → 外国人(がいこくじん)から見(み)ると |
| Noun (evidence) | Noun + から見(み)て | 傷(きず)の深(ふか)さ → 傷(きず)の深(ふか)さから見(み)て |
| Noun (place) - literal | Noun + から見(み)ると | 山(やま)の上(うえ) → 山(やま)の上(うえ)から見(み)ると |
| Conditional shade | Noun + から見(み)れば | 会社(かいしゃ)側(がわ) → 会社(かいしゃ)側(がわ)から見(み)れば |
| Concessive - "even by" | Noun + から見(み)ても | 世界(せかい)の基準(きじゅん) → 世界(せかい)の基準(きじゅん)から見(み)ても |
| Politeness | carried by the main clause | … → …から見(み)ると大(おお)きいです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>から見(み)て</code> is the compact written form and is very
common in analysis. It is not the て-form of a request; nothing can follow it but
the conclusion.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
空(そら)の様子(ようす)<b>から見(み)ると</b>、もうすぐ雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうだ。
By the look of the sky, it's about to rain.
</pre>

<pre>
外国人(がいこくじん)<b>から見(み)れば</b>、日本(にほん)の敬語(けいご)はとても複雑(ふくざつ)だ。
Seen from a foreigner's side, Japanese honorifics are extremely complicated.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)<b>から見(み)ると</b>、町(まち)は模型(もけい)のように小(ちい)さかった。
Seen from the top of the mountain, the town looked as small as a model.
</pre>

<pre>
傷(きず)の深(ふか)さ<b>から見(み)て</b>、落(お)ちたのは一度(いちど)ではないだろう。
Judging by how deep the scratches are, it can't have been dropped just once.
</pre>

<pre>
売(う)れ行(ゆ)き<b>から見(み)ると</b>、この商品(しょうひん)は来年(らいねん)まで残(のこ)らないだろう。
Judging by how it's selling, this product won't still be around next year.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)側(がわ)<b>から見(み)れば</b>、残業(ざんぎょう)を減(へ)らすのは簡単(かんたん)なことではない。
From the company's side, cutting overtime is not a simple matter.
</pre>

<pre>
世界(せかい)の基準(きじゅん)<b>から見(み)ても</b>、日本(にほん)の水道水(すいどうすい)の質(しつ)は高(たか)い。
Even by international standards, the quality of Japanese tap water is high.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)だけ<b>から見(み)ると</b>順調(じゅんちょう)だが、現場(げんば)の声(こえ)を聞(き)くとまったく違(ちが)う印象(いんしょう)を受(う)ける。
On the numbers alone it looks healthy, but talking to the people on the ground gives a completely different impression.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very widely usable - this is the member of the family to
reach for when you are not sure which one fits. It takes a person, an
organisation, an abstract standard, a set of data or a physical location, and it
sounds equally natural in conversation, in a newspaper column and in a report.

The endings shade as you would expect from their conjugations.
<code>から見(み)ると</code> is the plain one. <code>から見(み)れば</code>
leans towards "if you take that as your position", which suits the standpoint use.
<code>から見(み)て</code> is terse and written, and is the form you will meet in
headlines and analysis. <code>から見(み)ても</code> means "even measured that
way", and is a strong way to close an argument.

Two small cautions. First, the standpoint use normally introduces a view that
differs from the speaker's own, or at least is worth pointing out as one side of
things; used about yourself it is unremarkable but slightly odd, and
私(わたし)としては is the usual choice. Second, the pattern is not an evaluation
frame: 子(こ)どもから見(み)て大切(たいせつ)だ is strange, because "being
important to someone" is <code>にとって</code>'s job.

## Contrast

### から見(み)ると vs からすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the inference sense they are near-synonyms. Only
<code>から見(み)ると</code> also works literally, with a place.

</div>

<pre>
ホテルの屋上(おくじょう)<b>から見(み)ると</b>港(みなと)まで見渡(みわた)せる。
From the hotel roof you can see all the way to the harbour.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の記録(きろく)<b>からすると</b>、今年(ことし)も同(おな)じころ咲(さ)くはずだ。
Going by last year's records, they should bloom around the same time this year.
</pre>

Try <code>からすると</code> on the roof and it collapses, because a roof is not
information. Try <code>から見(み)ると</code> on the records and it is perfectly
fine. That asymmetry is the practical difference: one is a superset of the other.

### から見(み)ると vs にとって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>から見(み)ると</code> reports how something
**appears** to someone. <code>にとって</code> reports what something **is worth**
to someone.

</div>

<pre>
新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)<b>から見(み)ると</b>、この会社(かいしゃ)は静(しず)かすぎるらしい。
To the new recruits, this company apparently feels far too quiet.
</pre>

<pre>
新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)<b>にとって</b>、最初(さいしょ)の一年(いちねん)は大切(たいせつ)だ。
For new recruits, the first year matters.
</pre>

The first ends in an impression; the second in a valuation. If you can rewrite the
English as "X looks/seems ~ to them", you want <code>から見(み)ると</code>.

### から見(み)ると vs ところを見(み)ると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both contain 見(み)る, and they are not variants of each
other. <code>から見(み)ると</code> takes a **noun**; <code>ところを
見(み)ると</code> takes an **observed clause** and only ever means "judging from".

</div>

<pre>
顔色(かおいろ)<b>から見(み)て</b>、昨夜(さくや)はよく眠(ねむ)れなかったようだ。
By the look of his face, he didn't sleep well last night.
</pre>

<pre>
あくびばかりしている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、昨夜(さくや)はよく眠(ねむ)れなかったようだ。
Judging from the fact that he keeps yawning, he didn't sleep well last night.
</pre>

<code>ところを見(み)ると</code> cannot take a standpoint: there is no
✗外国人(がいこくじん)のところを見(み)ると meaning "from a foreigner's point
of view".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>から見(み)ると</b>、中止(ちゅうし)だろう。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、中止(ちゅうし)だろう。
  A whole clause needs ところを; only a noun goes with から.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>から見(み)て</b>必要(ひつよう)な授業(じゅぎょう)だ。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>にとって</b>必要(ひつよう)な授業(じゅぎょう)だ。
  "Necessary for X" is a valuation, and that belongs to <code>にとって</code>.

- ❌ 窓(まど)<b>から見(み)て</b>、海(うみ)がきれいです。 → ✅ 窓(まど)<b>から見(み)ると</b>、海(うみ)がきれいです。
  In the literal "seen from" use the と form is the natural one;
  <code>から見(み)て</code> has drifted towards "judging from".

- ❌ 外国人(がいこくじん)<b>を見(み)ると</b>、敬語(けいご)は難(むずか)しい。 → ✅ 外国人(がいこくじん)<b>から見(み)ると</b>、敬語(けいご)は難(むずか)しい。
  With `を` the sentence means "when I look at foreigners", which is a different
  sentence altogether.

## Practice

**1.** この指紋(しもん)の大(おお)きさ____、子(こ)どもがさわったのだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この指紋(しもん)の大(おお)きさ<b>から見(み)て</b>、子(こ)どもがさわったのだろう。
（見(み)ると also fine）

The size of the print is physical evidence, so this is the inference use, and the
conclusion stays a guess with だろう.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 親(おや)（から見(み)れば・ところを見(み)ると）、子(こ)どもはいくつになっても子(こ)どもだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

親(おや)<b>から見(み)れば</b>、子(こ)どもはいくつになっても子(こ)どもだ。

The noun is a person whose eyes we are borrowing.
<code>ところを見(み)ると</code> needs a clause describing something observed, and
has no standpoint meaning at all.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Seen from the train window, the whole valley was white with snow."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)の窓(まど)<b>から見(み)ると</b>、谷(たに)全体(ぜんたい)が雪(ゆき)で白(しろ)くなっていた。

This is the literal vantage-point use, which is why
<code>から見(み)ると</code> works here and <code>からすると</code> or
<code>から言(い)うと</code> would not.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 学生(がくせい)から見(み)ると、この授業(じゅぎょう)は大切(たいせつ)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>にとって</b>、この授業(じゅぎょう)は大切(たいせつ)です。
（or 学生(がくせい)から見(み)ると、この授業(じゅぎょう)は退屈(たいくつ)らしい）

<code>大切(たいせつ)だ</code> states what the class is worth to the students, and
that is <code>にとって</code>. Keep <code>から見(み)ると</code> only if the main
clause reports how the class *looks* to them.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 数字(すうじ)<b>から見(み)ると</b>、経営(けいえい)は順調(じゅんちょう)だ。
(b) 数字(すうじ)<b>から見(み)ても</b>、経営(けいえい)は順調(じゅんちょう)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) restricts the verdict to one source of information. It quietly leaves room for
the possibility that something outside the figures tells a different story, and it
often sets up a but-clause.

(b) does the opposite. も means "this measure too", so the figures are being added
to other grounds already accepted - the business looks healthy, and the numbers
confirm it as well. It strengthens rather than hedges.

</details>

# ～ところを見ると

**Meaning** · judging from the fact that ~; seeing that ~ - names something the speaker has actually observed, and draws a guess from it

Read <code>ところを見(み)ると</code>. Unlike the rest of this family it takes a
whole **clause**, not a noun, and that clause has to describe something the speaker
can see, hear or otherwise verify right now. The main clause is then always a
**conjecture**. This is Japanese for detective reasoning: here is what I observe,
therefore this is probably what happened.

## Formation

The clause in front is in noun-modifying shape, because ところ is a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | plain form + ところを見(み)ると | 言(い)わない → 何(なに)も言(い)わないところを見(み)ると |
| Verb (ている) | ている + ところを見(み)ると | 消(き)えている → 電気(でんき)が消(き)えているところを見(み)ると |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ところを見(み)ると | 断(ことわ)った → 断(ことわ)ったところを見(み)ると |
| い-adjective | plain form + ところを見(み)ると | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいところを見(み)ると |
| な-adjective | ～な + ところを見(み)ると | 真剣(しんけん)な → 真剣(しんけん)なところを見(み)ると |
| Main clause must be | らしい / ようだ / のだろう / にちがいない / かもしれない | … → …寝(ね)たらしい |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a bare noun does not sit in front of ところ here. Say
留守(るす)のようだ or 留守(るす)だったところを見(み)ると with a verb, not
✗留守(るす)ところを見(み)ると.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
電気(でんき)が消(き)えている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、もう寝(ね)たらしい。
Judging by the lights being off, they've already gone to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
皿(さら)が空(から)になっている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、口(くち)に合(あ)ったようだ。
Seeing that the plates are empty, it must have been to their taste.
</pre>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)っていない<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、雨(あめ)はもう止(や)んだのだろう。
Seeing that nobody's carrying an umbrella, the rain must have stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)も言(い)わない<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、彼(かれ)も反対(はんたい)なのかもしれない。
Going by the fact that he isn't saying anything, he may be against it too.
</pre>

<pre>
靴(くつ)がひどく汚(よご)れている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、山(やま)を歩(ある)いてきたようだ。
By the state of his shoes, he's been walking in the hills.
</pre>

<pre>
表情(ひょうじょう)が真剣(しんけん)な<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、冗談(じょうだん)ではなさそうだ。
Judging by how serious her face is, this isn't a joke.
</pre>

<pre>
返信(へんしん)が来(こ)ない<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、メールが届(とど)いていないのかもしれない。
Seeing that there's been no reply, the email may not have got through.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)になってもカーテンが開(あ)いていない<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、隣(となり)の人(ひと)は旅行(りょこう)にでも出(で)かけたのだろう。
Given that the curtains are still shut at midday, the neighbours must have gone away somewhere.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral in politeness and very common in speech - this is how people reason out
loud in front of each other. In writing it appears in narration and in casual
essays; in formal reports the tighter
<code>ことから</code> or <code>から見(み)て</code> is more usual.
<code>ところを見(み)ますと</code> exists and is stiff.

Two constraints do real work here.

**The evidence must be observed.** The clause reports something the speaker has
witnessed, or has been told as a plain fact. Hearsay you doubt, or a hypothetical,
does not fit: ✗雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るところを見(み)ると about tomorrow's
weather is not a sentence.

**The conclusion must be a guess.** らしい, ようだ, のだろう, にちがいない,
かもしれない, and the softening のではないか all fit. A flat assertion does not -
if you already know the answer, there is nothing to judge from.

There is a mildly observational, even nosy, flavour to the pattern. You are
commenting on evidence about someone else, often without asking them, so it can
sound like a deduction made behind their back. Said to the person's face it is
usually light and teasing -
笑(わら)っているところを見(み)ると、うまくいったんだね - but a stack of them
about an absent third party reads as gossip.

## Contrast

### ところを見(み)ると vs からして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ところを見(み)ると</code> takes a **clause**;
<code>からして</code> takes a **noun**. That is the whole difference in most
sentences.

</div>

<pre>
列(れつ)が長(なが)く続(つづ)いている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、あの店(みせ)は人気(にんき)があるらしい。
Seeing that the queue goes on and on, that shop must be popular.
</pre>

<pre>
列(れつ)の長(なが)さ<b>からして</b>、あの店(みせ)は人気(にんき)があるらしい。
Going by the length of the queue, that shop must be popular.
</pre>

Identical reasoning. The choice is whether you want to name an event or name a
thing. Note also that <code>からして</code> has a second, "even ~" meaning that
<code>ところを見(み)ると</code> lacks entirely.

### ところを見(み)ると vs ことから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ところを見(み)ると</code> leads to a **guess**;
<code>ことから</code> leads to an **established fact**.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)が割(わ)れている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、誰(だれ)かが入(はい)ったにちがいない。
Judging by the broken window, somebody must have got in.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)が割(わ)れていた<b>ことから</b>、警察(けいさつ)は事件(じけん)だと判断(はんだん)した。
On the basis of the broken window, the police concluded it was a crime.
</pre>

<code>ことから</code> is the written, official-sounding one, and it happily reports
a firm conclusion someone else reached. Put にちがいない after
<code>ことから</code> and it works too, but put a settled fact after
<code>ところを見(み)ると</code> and the sentence sounds as though the speaker is
guessing at something they already know.

### ところを見(み)ると vs a bare ようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ところを見(み)ると</code> is not the guess. It is the
**grounds** for a guess that still has to be marked.

</div>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が片付(かたづ)いている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、もう引(ひ)っ越(こ)したようだ。
Given that the room is tidied up, they seem to have moved out already.
</pre>

Learners often drop the ようだ, leaving
❌…ところを見(み)ると、もう引(ひ)っ越(こ)した. That is like saying "judging
by the tidy room, they moved out" and stopping - the grounds are there but the
verdict has lost the hedge that made it a verdict. Conversely, a bare
もう引(ひ)っ越(こ)したようだ is a complete sentence; the pattern is only there
to show your working.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 静(しず)かなところを見(み)ると、誰(だれ)もいない。 → ✅ 静(しず)かなところを見(み)ると、誰(だれ)もいない<b>ようだ</b>。
  The main clause has to be a conjecture.

- ❌ 様子(ようす)<b>のところを見(み)ると</b> → ✅ 様子(ようす)<b>からして</b>（or …している<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>）
  A bare noun cannot precede ところ here. Use a clause, or switch pattern.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)<b>だところを見(み)ると</b> → ✅ 元気(げんき)<b>なところを見(み)ると</b>
  ところ is a noun, so a な-adjective links with な, never with だ.

- ❌ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)だろう。 → ✅ 空(そら)が暗(くら)くなってきた<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)だろう。
  The clause must be something already observed, not a future event.

## Practice

**1.** 大(おお)きな荷物(にもつ)を持(も)っている____、長期(ちょうき)の出張(しゅっちょう)らしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大(おお)きな荷物(にもつ)を持(も)っている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、長期(ちょうき)の出張(しゅっちょう)らしい。

The luggage is something the speaker can see, and the clause form fits ところ
directly. らしい in the main clause keeps it a deduction rather than a claim.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 返事(へんじ)の遅(おそ)さ（ところを見(み)ると・からして）、あまり乗(の)り気(き)ではないようだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

返事(へんじ)の遅(おそ)さ<b>からして</b>、あまり乗(の)り気(き)ではないようだ。

返事(へんじ)の遅(おそ)さ is a noun, and <code>ところを見(み)ると</code>
needs a clause. Rewriting it as 返事(へんじ)がなかなか来(こ)ないところを
見(み)ると would let the other pattern back in.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Seeing that the shoes are still by the door, he can't have left yet."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

靴(くつ)が玄関(げんかん)にある<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、まだ出(で)かけていないのだろう。

The shoes are observed evidence, so the clause goes in front of ところ, and のだろう
supplies the conjecture the pattern requires.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼女(かのじょ)が上機嫌(じょうきげん)だところを見(み)ると、面接(めんせつ)はうまくいった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼女(かのじょ)が上機嫌(じょうきげん)<b>なところを見(み)ると</b>、面接(めんせつ)はうまくいった<b>ようだ</b>。

Two repairs. 上機嫌(じょうきげん) is a な-adjective, so it links to the noun
ところ with な, not だ - and the main clause needs a conjecture ending, because the
pattern exists precisely to introduce one.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)が開(あ)いている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、誰(だれ)か来(き)ているらしい。
(b) 窓(まど)が開(あ)いているから、誰(だれ)か来(き)ているらしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) with から simply gives a reason; it is neutral and could be about anything,
including facts you were told rather than saw.

(a) foregrounds the act of observing. It says "I am looking at an open window, and
this is what I read off it" - the speaker is visibly working from evidence in front
of them, and the sentence carries the flavour of a deduction being made on the spot.

</details>

# ～にしたら

**Meaning** · from ~'s point of view; as far as ~ is concerned - imagines how a situation feels to a particular person

<code>にしたら</code> hands the sentence over to somebody else's feelings. The noun
in front is always a **person** (or a group, or an animal you are treating as one),
and what follows is what the situation looks like *to them*, usually with an
undertone of "give them some credit". It is the sympathy member of this family:
where <code>からすると</code> can weigh evidence and
<code>から言(い)うと</code> can measure on a scale, <code>にしたら</code> does
one job only.

## Formation

Four interchangeable shapes, all built on する.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Person | Noun + にしたら | 親(おや) → 親(おや)にしたら |
| Person, slightly more written | Noun + にすれば | 部下(ぶか) → 部下(ぶか)にすれば |
| Person, emphatic | Noun + にしてみれば | 本人(ほんにん) → 本人(ほんにん)にしてみれば |
| Person, emphatic and spoken | Noun + にしてみたら | 子(こ)ども → 子(こ)どもにしてみたら |
| Group or organisation | Noun + にしたら | 会社(かいしゃ)側(がわ) → 会社(かいしゃ)側(がわ)にしたら |
| Main clause typically | だろう / にちがいない / のだ / かもしれない | … → …つらいだろう |

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not confuse this with the identical-looking
<code>Ａを Ｂ にしたら</code> ("if you make A into B"), as in
部屋(へや)をきれいにしたら. There the する is a real verb with an object; here
the whole <code>にしたら</code> is one unit attached to a person.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
親(おや)<b>にしたら</b>、子(こ)どもの将来(しょうらい)が心配(しんぱい)なのだ。
For a parent, it's their child's future they worry about.
</pre>

<pre>
冗談(じょうだん)のつもりでも、言(い)われた人(ひと)<b>にしてみれば</b>ひどい言葉(ことば)だ。
You may mean it as a joke, but to the person on the receiving end it's a cruel thing to say.
</pre>

<pre>
犬(いぬ)<b>にしてみたら</b>、急(きゅう)に抱(だ)き上(あ)げられるのは怖(こわ)いことらしい。
From the dog's side of things, being suddenly picked up is apparently frightening.
</pre>

<pre>
店(みせ)<b>にすれば</b>、一人(ひとり)のお客(きゃく)さんが二時間(にじかん)席(せき)を使(つか)うのは困(こま)る。
For the café, one customer sitting at a table for two hours is a problem.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>にしたら</b>、最初(さいしょ)から一人(ひとり)で任(まか)されるのは不安(ふあん)だったにちがいない。
For someone brand new, being left to handle it alone from day one must have been unnerving.
</pre>

<pre>
留守番(るすばん)を頼(たの)まれた中学生(ちゅうがくせい)<b>にすれば</b>、一晩(ひとばん)は長(なが)すぎる。
To a middle-schooler asked to mind the house, a whole night is far too long.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)には小(ちい)さな失敗(しっぱい)に見(み)えても、本人(ほんにん)<b>にしてみれば</b>一年分(いちねんぶん)の努力(どりょく)が消(き)えたようなものだ。
It looks like a small slip to me, but for him it's as if a year of effort has evaporated.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)しは楽(たの)しみだが、転校(てんこう)する息子(むすこ)<b>にしたら</b>友(とも)だちと別(わか)れるのがつらいだろう。
The move is exciting for us, but for our son, who has to change schools, saying goodbye to his friends will be hard.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Politeness-neutral: the pattern itself does not change, and the main clause carries
です/ます as needed. <code>にしたら</code> and <code>にしてみたら</code> lean
spoken; <code>にすれば</code> and <code>にしてみれば</code> turn up more in
writing. <code>にしてみれば</code> and <code>にしてみたら</code> add the
見(み)る of "try it and see" - literally "if you put yourself in their place and
look" - so they press the empathy a little harder.

The typical shape of the sentence is a **contrast**: the speaker's own view first,
then the other party's. 私(わたし)は平気(へいき)だが、彼(かれ)
<b>にしてみれば</b>… That is why it so often introduces an argument for patience
with someone, and why it sits so naturally with だろう and にちがいない - you are
supposing on somebody's behalf, not reporting what they told you.

Two limits worth remembering. First, only animate perspectives. Machines,
statistics and abstractions have no point of view, so ✗数字(すうじ)にしたら is
not available; <code>からすると</code> covers that ground. Second, using it about
yourself is possible - 私(わたし)にしてみれば迷惑(めいわく)な話(はなし)だ -
but it is a slightly dramatic way to speak, as if narrating your own case from
outside. For plainly stating your own position, としては is the everyday choice.

## Contrast

### にしたら vs にとって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にとって</code> takes an **evaluation**
(大切(たいせつ)だ, 必要(ひつよう)だ). <code>にしたら</code> takes a whole
**judgement or feeling** that the person would have.

</div>

<pre>
この指輪(ゆびわ)は母(はは)<b>にとって</b>特別(とくべつ)なものだ。
This ring is something special to my mother.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)<b>にしたら</b>、指輪(ゆびわ)を売(う)るなど考(かんが)えられないのだろう。
For my mother, selling the ring is presumably unthinkable.
</pre>

Swap them and both go wrong. ❌母(はは)にしたら特別(とくべつ)なものだ is
missing the person's own reaction; ❌母(はは)にとって売(う)るなど
考(かんが)えられない is a whole judgement squeezed into a slot built for
adjectives. If the main clause is one word plus だ, reach for
<code>にとって</code>.

### にしたら vs からすれば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にしたら</code> takes **only people**;
<code>からすれば</code> takes people **or** evidence.

</div>

<pre>
受験生(じゅけんせい)<b>にしたら</b>、この時期(じき)の風邪(かぜ)は命(いのち)とりだ。
For someone sitting exams, catching a cold at this time of year is fatal.
</pre>

<pre>
過去(かこ)のデータ<b>からすれば</b>、この時期(じき)は風邪(かぜ)が流行(はや)りやすい。
Going by past data, colds spread easily at this time of year.
</pre>

Data has no feelings, so the second cannot take <code>にしたら</code>. Where the
noun *is* a person, the two overlap heavily, and <code>にしたら</code> is simply
the warmer of the pair.

### にしたら vs としては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>としては</code> is where **you** stand;
<code>にしたら</code> is where **someone else** stands, as imagined by you.

</div>

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としては</b>、この条件(じょうけん)で進(すす)めたいと思(おも)っています。
Speaking for myself, I'd like to go ahead on these terms.
</pre>

<pre>
先方(せんぽう)<b>にしたら</b>、この条件(じょうけん)は厳(きび)しすぎるかもしれません。
For the other side, these terms may well be too harsh.
</pre>

The first is a stated position you are responsible for; the second is a supposition
about someone whose mind you cannot see, which is why it ends in かもしれません. Put
としては on the second and you would be claiming to speak for them.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この制度(せいど)<b>にしたら</b>問題(もんだい)がある。 → ✅ この制度(せいど)<b>には</b>問題(もんだい)がある。
  A system has no point of view. <code>にしたら</code> needs an animate one.

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)<b>にしたら</b>必要(ひつよう)な本(ほん)だ。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)<b>にとって</b>必要(ひつよう)な本(ほん)だ。
  A bare evaluation belongs to <code>にとって</code>.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)<b>にしたら</b>残業(ざんぎょう)が嫌(いや)だと言(い)った。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>にしたら</b>残業(ざんぎょう)は嫌(いや)なのだろう。
  The pattern supposes; it does not report speech. If he said it, just quote him.

- ❌ 子(こ)ども<b>をにしたら</b> → ✅ 子(こ)ども<b>にしたら</b>
  The person attaches directly. Adding `を` produces the unrelated "turn A into B"
  construction.

## Practice

**1.** 毎日(まいにち)世話(せわ)をしている飼(か)い主(ぬし)____、ペットは家族(かぞく)の一員(いちいん)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

飼(か)い主(ぬし)<b>にしたら</b>、ペットは家族(かぞく)の一員(いちいん)だ。

The owner is a person whose perspective the sentence is adopting, and the main
clause states what the situation amounts to in their eyes rather than rating it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会社(かいしゃ)の数字(すうじ)（にしたら・からすると）、来年(らいねん)の採用(さいよう)は難(むずか)しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会社(かいしゃ)の数字(すうじ)<b>からすると</b>、来年(らいねん)の採用(さいよう)は難(むずか)しい。

Figures are evidence, not a party with a viewpoint. Note that
会社(かいしゃ)にしたら would be fine - it is the 数字(すうじ) that blocks
<code>にしたら</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's only a five-minute walk for me, but for my grandmother it's a long way."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)には歩(ある)いて五分(ごふん)だが、祖母(そぼ)<b>にしてみれば</b>遠(とお)い道(みち)のりだ。

The English "but for my grandmother" is exactly the contrastive shape this pattern
lives in: the speaker's own view first, then the other person's, marked with
<code>にしてみれば</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 新(あたら)しいルールは安全(あんぜん)にしたら必要(ひつよう)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 新(あたら)しいルールは安全(あんぜん)<b>のために</b>必要(ひつよう)だ。
（or 安全面(あんぜんめん)から言(い)えば必要(ひつよう)だ）

安全(あんぜん) is neither a person nor a viewpoint holder, so
<code>にしたら</code> cannot attach to it. Safety here is a purpose or a criterion,
and Japanese has other machinery for both.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>にとって</b>、漢字(かんじ)は大(おお)きな壁(かべ)だ。
(b) 留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>にしてみれば</b>、漢字(かんじ)をやめてほしいくらいだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an assessment stated from outside: kanji constitute a major obstacle for
that group. It is the kind of sentence a textbook or a report would carry, and the
speaker need not have any feeling about it.

(b) steps inside. It attributes a wish to the students themselves - they would
sooner kanji did not exist - and the だろう admits the speaker is imagining rather
than quoting. It asks the listener to sympathise, which (a) does not.

</details>

# ～としては

**Meaning** · speaking as ~; in the capacity of ~; by the standards of a ~

<code>としては</code> is として plus は, and the は is doing real work. として names
the role or category something is being taken in; は fences that role off from the
others, so the sentence delivers a verdict **within that role and no further**.
Two uses fall out of this: stating the position you speak from
（私(わたし)としては）, and judging something against the norm for its category
（初心者(しょしんしゃ)としては）.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (role) | Noun + としては | 教師(きょうし) → 教師(きょうし)としては |
| Person stating own position | 私(わたし) / うち + としては | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)としては |
| Noun (category as a standard) | Noun + としては | 初心者(しょしんしゃ) → 初心者(しょしんしゃ)としては |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + としての | 親(おや) → 親(おや)としての責任(せきにん) |
| Polite / business | Noun + としましては | 当社(とうしゃ) → 当社(とうしゃ)としましては |
| Without は - plain role | Noun + として | 通訳(つうやく) → 通訳(つうやく)として働(はたら)く |

<div class="note">

**Note** - only a noun goes in front. For a clause you need にしては:
作(つく)ったにしては上手(じょうず)だ, never ✗作(つく)ったとしては.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)<b>としては</b>、この案(あん)に賛成(さんせい)です。
Speaking for myself, I'm in favour of this proposal.
</pre>

<pre>
初心者(しょしんしゃ)<b>としては</b>よくできたほうだ。
For a beginner, that was pretty well done.
</pre>

<pre>
趣味(しゅみ)<b>としては</b>続(つづ)けるが、仕事(しごと)にするつもりはない。
I'll keep it up as a hobby, but I've no intention of making it my job.
</pre>

<pre>
社長(しゃちょう)<b>としては</b>、社員(しゃいん)の給料(きゅうりょう)を下(さ)げるわけにはいかない。
As president, he can't very well cut his staff's pay.
</pre>

<pre>
八月(はちがつ)の朝(あさ)<b>としては</b>涼(すず)しすぎるくらいだった。
For an August morning it was almost too cool.
</pre>

<pre>
中学生(ちゅうがくせい)<b>としては</b>背(せ)が高(たか)いほうだが、バスケ部(ぶ)では一番(いちばん)小(ちい)さい。
He's tall for a middle-school student, but he's the shortest on the basketball team.
</pre>

<pre>
当社(とうしゃ)<b>としましては</b>、これ以上(いじょう)の値引(ねび)きは難(むずか)しいと考(かんが)えております。
As a company, we consider any further discount difficult.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は歌手(かしゅ)<b>としては</b>有名(ゆうめい)だが、俳優(はいゆう)としての評価(ひょうか)はまだ低(ひく)い。
He's famous as a singer, but as an actor he's still not rated.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and one of the most useful phrases in the book for discussion.
私(わたし)としては is the standard way to put an opinion on the table while
signalling that you know it is only yours - it softens disagreement, and it is
common in meetings, interviews and everyday argument alike. The business polite
form <code>としましては</code> is stiff and is used exactly where you would expect:
customer service, negotiations, formal apologies.

The `は` is what separates <code>としては</code> from bare として. Compare
通訳(つうやく)として働(はたら)く ("work as an interpreter" - simply naming
the role) with 通訳(つうやく)としては経験(けいけん)が浅(あさ)い ("as an
interpreter he's inexperienced" - and by implication, maybe not as something else).
Whenever は appears, expect a limit, and often an unspoken "but".

In the category-standard use, the evaluation may be positive or negative; what
matters is that it is measured against what the category leads you to expect.
新人(しんじん)としては上出来(じょうでき)だ praises within a modest bar, and a
listener hears both the praise and the bar. That double edge is real: telling a
professional 「プロとしてはまあまあですね」 is not a compliment.

## Contrast

### としては vs にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>としては</code> judges **within** the category's own
norm. <code>にしては</code> says the result **contradicts** what the category led
you to expect.

</div>

<pre>
一年生(いちねんせい)<b>としては</b>よく書(か)けている。
For a first-year, it's well written. (good, measured by first-year standards)
</pre>

<pre>
一年生(いちねんせい)<b>にしては</b>よく書(か)けている。
It's well written for a first-year. (surprisingly good - better than a first-year should manage)
</pre>

The English is nearly identical and the Japanese is not. <code>としては</code>
applies the yardstick; <code>にしては</code> announces that the yardstick has been
overshot. There is a grammatical difference too: にしては attaches to clauses
(習(なら)っていないにしては上手(じょうず)だ), and としては never does.

### としては vs にしたら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>としては</code> is the position **you occupy and speak
from**. <code>にしたら</code> is the position of **someone else**, imagined.

</div>

<pre>
担任(たんにん)<b>としては</b>、そのやり方(かた)は認(みと)められません。
As their class teacher, I can't approve of doing it that way.
</pre>

<pre>
担任(たんにん)<b>にしたら</b>、そのやり方(かた)は認(みと)めにくいだろう。
For the class teacher, that way of doing things must be hard to approve of.
</pre>

The first is spoken by the teacher; the second is spoken about them. That is why
the second ends in だろう - you cannot assert someone else's inner position as
fact.

### としては vs から言(い)うと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>としては</code> names **who is judging**;
<code>から言(い)うと</code> names **what they are judging by**.

</div>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)<b>としては</b>、お酒(さけ)をすすめるわけにはいきません。
As a doctor, I'm not in a position to recommend drinking.
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)の面(めん)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、お酒(さけ)はすすめられません。
On health grounds, drinking is not something to recommend.
</pre>

Same refusal, differently sourced. The first leans on the speaker's role and
therefore on their responsibility; the second hides the speaker entirely behind a
criterion. In a delicate conversation, choosing between them is choosing how much
of yourself to put into the sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)<b>にしては</b>反対(はんたい)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>としては</b>反対(はんたい)です。
  To state your own position you need としては. にしては would mean "surprisingly,
  for me".

- ❌ 日本語(にほんご)を習(なら)っていない<b>としては</b>上手(じょうず)だ。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)を習(なら)っていない<b>にしては</b>上手(じょうず)だ。
  としては takes only a noun; a clause needs にしては.

- ❌ 通訳(つうやく)<b>としては</b>三年(さんねん)働(はたら)きました。 → ✅ 通訳(つうやく)<b>として</b>三年(さんねん)働(はたら)きました。
  Simply naming the role you worked in needs bare として. The は would add a
  contrast that the sentence has no use for.

- ❌ 親(おや)<b>としては</b>責任(せきにん) → ✅ 親(おや)<b>としての</b>責任(せきにん)
  Modifying a following noun takes の, not は.

## Practice

**1.** 店(みせ)____、土曜日(どようび)に休(やす)まれるのは困(こま)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（店(みせ)としては in plainer speech）

The shop is the party speaking, in its capacity as an employer, and the polite
としましては matches the ます ending of the main clause.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Two reasons. The phrase in front is a clause, which としては cannot take, and the

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

child upwards, out of the category, in a way (a) does not.

</details>

# ～上 - in terms of

**Meaning** · in terms of ~; as far as ~ is concerned - read じょう, welded onto the end of a noun

This character is read <b>じょう</b> here, not うえ. Stuck onto a noun of Chinese origin it turns
it into a dimension along which something holds:
法律(ほうりつ)上(じょう) "legally", 安全(あんぜん)上(じょう) "as regards
safety", 立場(たちば)上(じょう) "given the position I'm in". It is the most
compressed way Japanese has of saying "on the ~ front", and it belongs to formal
prose and workplace speech.

## Formation

There is no gap and no particle between the noun and the suffix.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sino-Japanese noun | Noun + 上(じょう) | 法律(ほうりつ) → 法律(ほうりつ)上(じょう) |
| With topic / contrast | Noun + 上(じょう)は | 計算(けいさん) → 計算(けいさん)上(じょう)は |
| Modifying a following noun | Noun + 上(じょう)の | 健康(けんこう) → 健康(けんこう)上(じょう)の理由(りゆう) |
| Adverbially, before a predicate | Noun + 上(じょう) + predicate | 安全(あんぜん) → 安全(あんぜん)上(じょう)問題(もんだい)がある |
| As a set adverb | 経験(けいけん) / 立場(たちば) + 上(じょう)、… | 経験(けいけん) → 経験(けいけん)上(じょう)、… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is not a productive suffix. It lives in a fixed set of compounds:
法律(ほうりつ)上(じょう), 教育(きょういく)上(じょう),
健康(けんこう)上(じょう), 安全(あんぜん)上(じょう),
仕事(しごと)上(じょう), 立場(たちば)上(じょう),
経験(けいけん)上(じょう), 計算(けいさん)上(じょう),
規則(きそく)上(じょう), 表面(ひょうめん)上(じょう),
事実(じじつ)上(じょう), 歴史(れきし)上(じょう),
都合(つごう)上(じょう). Learn these; do not coin new ones.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
健康(けんこう)<b>上(じょう)</b>の理由(りゆう)で会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めた。
He left the company for health reasons.
</pre>

<pre>
これは法律(ほうりつ)<b>上(じょう)</b>認(みと)められていない。
This is not permitted in law.
</pre>

<pre>
立場(たちば)<b>上(じょう)</b>、はっきりしたことは言(い)えません。
Given the position I'm in, I can't say anything definite.
</pre>

<pre>
経験(けいけん)<b>上(じょう)</b>、こういう時(とき)は一度(いちど)休(やす)んだほうがいい。
In my experience, times like this call for a break.
</pre>

<pre>
計算(けいさん)<b>上(じょう)</b>は間(ま)に合(あ)うはずだが、実際(じっさい)はどうだろう。
On paper it should be in time, but what will actually happen is another question.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)<b>上(じょう)</b>の問題(もんだい)から、この橋(はし)は通行(つうこう)止(ど)めになっている。
For safety reasons this bridge is closed to traffic.
</pre>

<pre>
表面(ひょうめん)<b>上(じょう)</b>は仲(なか)がいいが、二人(ふたり)の間(あいだ)には複雑(ふくざつ)な事情(じじょう)があるらしい。
On the surface they get on well, but there seems to be something complicated between them.
</pre>

<pre>
仕事(しごと)<b>上(じょう)</b>の付(つ)き合(あ)いだから断(ことわ)れないと言(い)っていたが、飲(の)みに行(い)く回数(かいすう)が多(おお)すぎる。
He says it's work socialising and he can't refuse, but he goes drinking far too often.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and compact. You will meet it constantly in notices, contracts, manuals,
news writing and the more careful register of office speech, and almost never in
relaxed conversation, where 健康(けんこう)のために or
安全(あんぜん)のことを考(かんが)えると do the same work at greater length.

Two of the compounds are worth singling out. 立場(たちば)上(じょう) is the
standard polite way to decline to say or do something because of your role - it
appeals to a constraint rather than to a preference, and so avoids blame.
表面(ひょうめん)上(じょう)は, by contrast, is almost always the setup for a
"but": it flags the surface appearance precisely in order to contradict it.

The reading trap is worth stating plainly. The same character is うえ in
机(つくえ)の上(うえ), あ in 上(あ)がる, のぼ in 上(のぼ)る, and じょう
here. Reading 仕事(しごと)上(じょう) as しごとうえ is a very common learner
error and marks the sentence immediately.

## Contrast

### 上(じょう) vs 上(うえ)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - じょう means "in the ~ dimension"; うえで means "after doing"
or, literally, "on top of".

</div>

<pre>
規則(きそく)<b>上(じょう)</b>、その申(もう)し込(こ)みは受(う)け付(つ)けられません。
Under the rules, we cannot accept that application.
</pre>

<pre>
規則(きそく)を確(たし)かめた<b>上(うえ)で</b>、お返事(へんじ)します。
I'll reply once I've checked the rules.
</pre>

Same character, unrelated grammar. じょう attaches to a bare noun with nothing
between; うえで follows a た-form verb or a noun + の, and always concerns
sequence. If there is a verb in front, the reading is うえ.

### 上(じょう) vs から言(い)うと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both name a criterion. じょう is a **fixed compound** and
terse; <code>から言(い)うと</code> is **free** and can take any noun.

</div>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)<b>上(じょう)</b>、ヘルメットの着用(ちゃくよう)が必要(ひつよう)です。
For safety, helmets must be worn.
</pre>

<pre>
作業(さぎょう)の効率(こうりつ)<b>から言(い)うと</b>、この方法(ほうほう)は悪(わる)くない。
In terms of working efficiency, this method isn't bad.
</pre>

You cannot say ✗効率(こうりつ)上(じょう) freely just because
安全(あんぜん)上(じょう) exists - though a handful, like
計算(けいさん)上(じょう), are established. When in doubt about whether a
compound exists, use <code>から言(い)うと</code>, which never fails.

### 上(じょう) vs 的(てき)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>上(じょう)</code> names the **domain a fact belongs
to**; <code>的(てき)に</code> names the **manner or angle** of a description.

</div>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)<b>上(じょう)</b>の問題(もんだい)で欠席(けっせき)します。
I'll be absent because of a health problem.
</pre>

<pre>
この食事(しょくじ)は健康(けんこう)的(てき)だ。
This meal is healthy.
</pre>

健康(けんこう)上(じょう)の問題(もんだい) is a problem located in the health
department; 健康(けんこう)的(てき) describes something as being of a healthy
character. They are not substitutes: ✗健康(けんこう)的(てき)な
理由(りゆう)で辞(や)めた does not mean what the learner intends.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 気持(きも)ち<b>上(じょう)</b> → ✅ 気持(きも)ちの<b>上(うえ)</b>では
  This suffix attaches to Sino-Japanese nouns, not to native words like
  気持(きも)ち.

- ❌ 健康(けんこう)<b>上(じょう)</b>な理由(りゆう) → ✅ 健康(けんこう)<b>上(じょう)</b>の理由(りゆう)
  <code>上(じょう)</code> forms a noun, so it links to a following noun with の.

- ❌ 仕事(しごと)の<b>上(じょう)</b>の都合(つごう) → ✅ 仕事(しごと)<b>上(じょう)</b>の都合(つごう)
  Nothing comes between the noun and the suffix - no の, no で, no を.

- ❌ reading 仕事(しごと)上(うえ) → ✅ 仕事(しごと)上(じょう)
  In this pattern the character is always じょう. うえ belongs to
  机(つくえ)の上(うえ) and to <code>～た上(うえ)で</code>.

</details>

# ～にかけては

**Meaning** · when it comes to ~, nobody does it better - singles out the one area in which someone excels

<code>にかけては</code> names a narrow field and then claims the top of it. The
noun in front is a skill, a subject, a quality; the clause after it is always
**praise** - 誰(だれ)にも負(ま)けない, 一番(いちばん)だ,
自信(じしん)がある, 右(みぎ)に出(で)る者(もの)はいない. The `は` matters:
it fences the claim in, and quietly concedes that outside this field things may be
different.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (skill or field) | Noun + にかけては | 記憶力(きおくりょく) → 記憶力(きおくりょく)にかけては |
| Nominalised quality | ～さ + にかけては | 速(はや)さ → 走(はし)る速(はや)さにかけては |
| Nominalised action | Verb + こと + にかけては | 覚(おぼ)える → 道(みち)を覚(おぼ)えることにかけては |
| Noun + の + skill | Noun + の + Noun + にかけては | 料理(りょうり)の腕(うで) → 料理(りょうり)の腕(うで)にかけては |
| Main clause must be | positive evaluation | … → …誰(だれ)にも負(ま)けない |
| Politeness | carried by the main clause | … → …自信(じしん)があります |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is not the same word as <code>にかけて</code> in
春(はる)から夏(なつ)にかけて ("from spring through summer"). That one needs a
from-to pair and spans time or space; this one needs a single field and a boast.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
走(はし)る速(はや)さ<b>にかけては</b>、クラスで一番(いちばん)だ。
When it comes to running speed, he's the fastest in the class.
</pre>

<pre>
記憶力(きおくりょく)<b>にかけては</b>誰(だれ)にも負(ま)けない。
When it comes to memory, I can beat anyone.
</pre>

<pre>
野菜(やさい)の新鮮(しんせん)さ<b>にかけては</b>、この店(みせ)にかなう店(みせ)はない。
For freshness of vegetables, no shop can match this one.
</pre>

<pre>
接客(せっきゃく)<b>にかけては</b>自信(じしん)があります。
When it comes to dealing with customers, I'm confident in what I do.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)の正確(せいかく)さ<b>にかけては</b>、彼女(かのじょ)の右(みぎ)に出(で)る者(もの)はいない。
For accuracy with figures, nobody comes near her.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)を覚(おぼ)えること<b>にかけては</b>、父(ちち)にはとてもかなわない。
When it comes to remembering routes, I'm no match at all for my father.
</pre>

<pre>
古(ふる)い映画(えいが)の知識(ちしき)<b>にかけては</b>、彼(かれ)は専門家(せんもんか)並(な)みだ。
On his knowledge of old films he's on a par with a specialist.
</pre>

<pre>
料理(りょうり)の腕(うで)<b>にかけては</b>母(はは)が一番(いちばん)だと思(おも)っていたが、祖母(そぼ)の漬物(つけもの)を食(た)べて考(かんが)えが変(か)わった。
I'd always thought my mother was the best cook in the family, until I tasted my grandmother's pickles.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Warm and slightly formulaic, and completely at home in both speech and writing.
Two settings account for most of its use. One is **praising someone else** -
introducing a colleague, recommending a shop, describing a teammate. The other is
**claiming something for yourself** without sounding arrogant: because `は` limits
the boast to one field, 接客(せっきゃく)にかけては自信(じしん)があります is a
polished thing to say in a job interview, where a flat
私(わたし)は優秀(ゆうしゅう)です would be unsayable.

The main clause has to be **positive**. Grammatically nothing stops you writing
遅刻(ちこく)にかけては彼(かれ)が一番(いちばん)だ, and Japanese speakers do
say things like that - but it lands as a joke that borrows the shape of a
compliment, and the humour depends on the listener hearing the mismatch. Do not
use it for a straight-faced criticism.

The noun must also be a **field of competence**. A place, a period or an object
does not work: ✗東京(とうきょう)にかけては is not a sentence, and neither is
✗去年(きょねん)にかけては in this meaning - which is exactly where the collision
with the time-span <code>にかけて</code> catches people out.

## Contrast

### にかけては vs にかけて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にかけて</code> with a from-word in front spans **time
or space**. <code>にかけては</code> with a skill in front makes a **claim of
superiority**. Same syllables, unrelated jobs.

</div>

<pre>
夕方(ゆうがた)から夜(よる)<b>にかけて</b>雨(あめ)が強(つよ)くなるでしょう。
The rain will get heavier from the evening through into the night.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)を当(あ)てること<b>にかけては</b>、この人(ひと)が一番(いちばん)頼(たよ)りになる。
When it comes to predicting the weather, he's the one to trust.
</pre>

The giveaway is what precedes. If there is a から phrase and two points of a
timeline, it is the span pattern. If there is one field and a superlative waiting
at the end, it is this one.

### にかけては vs に関(かん)しては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に関(かん)しては</code> merely announces a **topic**.
<code>にかけては</code> announces a topic **and ranks somebody first in it**.

</div>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)<b>に関(かん)しては</b>、後(あと)で説明(せつめい)します。
As regards this matter, I'll explain later.
</pre>

<pre>
機械(きかい)の修理(しゅうり)<b>にかけては</b>、彼(かれ)にまかせておけば安心(あんしん)だ。
When it comes to fixing machines, you can leave it to him with complete confidence.
</pre>

Put <code>にかけては</code> into the first sentence and it promises a boast that
never arrives; the listener waits for a superlative. Put
<code>に関(かん)しては</code> into the second and the praise still works, but the
sentence loses its point - the framing that says "this and nothing else is his
territory".

### にかけては vs だけは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both isolate one quality. <code>にかけては</code> is
**admiring**; <code>だけは</code> often implies **that is the only good thing**.

</div>

<pre>
足(あし)の速(はや)さ<b>にかけては</b>自信(じしん)がある。
I'm confident about my running speed.
</pre>

<pre>
足(あし)の速(はや)さ<b>だけは</b>自信(じしん)がある。
Running speed is the one thing I'm confident about.
</pre>

The second is self-deprecating, and said about someone else it can be a barb. The
first simply picks a field and claims it. When you mean to compliment, the choice
of particle carries the whole difference.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 三月(さんがつ)から四月(しがつ)<b>にかけては</b>忙(いそが)しい。 → ✅ 三月(さんがつ)から四月(しがつ)<b>にかけて</b>忙(いそが)しい。
  A from-to span takes plain <code>にかけて</code>. Adding は turns it into the
  wrong pattern.

- ❌ 忘(わす)れること<b>にかけては</b>彼(かれ)が一番(いちばん)困(こま)る。 → ✅ 忘(わす)れっぽさ<b>にかけては</b>彼(かれ)が一番(いちばん)だ。（joking）or ✅ 彼(かれ)は本当(ほんとう)に忘(わす)れっぽい。
  The clause after this pattern is a ranking, not a complaint. If you want to
  criticise plainly, drop the pattern.

- ❌ 大阪(おおさか)<b>にかけては</b>おいしい店(みせ)が多(おお)い。 → ✅ 大阪(おおさか)<b>には</b>おいしい店(みせ)が多(おお)い。
  A place name is not a field of competence.

- ❌ 歌(うた)<b>にかけて</b>は誰(だれ)にも負(ま)けない。 → ✅ 歌(うた)<b>にかけては</b>誰(だれ)にも負(ま)けない。
  The は is part of the pattern, not a particle you can move or drop.

## Practice

**1.** 漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えること____、クラスの誰(だれ)にも負(ま)けません。

The verb phrase is nominalised with こと so that the pattern has a noun to attach
to, and 負(ま)けません is the classic positive main clause.

</details>

The から phrase marks the start of a span, so this is the time-range pattern. The
は version would demand a field of skill and a superlative, neither of which is

</details>

The field is nominalised with こと, and かなう人(ひと)はいない is one of the

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（or 字(じ)のきれいさ<b>にかけては</b>誰(だれ)にも負(ま)けない - if he is in fact good）

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 料理(りょうり)<b>にかけては</b>自信(じしん)があります。
(b) 料理(りょうり)<b>だけは</b>自信(じしん)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) implies a comparison the speaker is losing elsewhere: cooking is the one thing
they can vouch for. It is modest, faintly apologetic, and would be an odd thing to
volunteer when trying to impress.

</details>

# ～限りでは

**Meaning** · as far as I know / have seen / have heard - fences a claim inside the information the speaker actually has

Read <code>限(かぎ)りでは</code>. The verb in front is nearly always one of
**finding out**: 知(し)る, 見(み)る, 聞(き)く, 調(しら)べる,
読(よ)む, 覚(おぼ)えている. The pattern then says: within that evidence the
following is true, and outside it I make no claim. It is not a marker of viewpoint
and not a marker of grounds for inference - it is a marker of **limits**.

## Formation

限(かぎ)り is a noun, so the clause in front is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary) | plain form + 限(かぎ)りでは | 知(し)る → 私(わたし)の知(し)る限(かぎ)りでは |
| Verb (past) | た-form + 限(かぎ)りでは | 聞(き)いた → 聞(き)いた限(かぎ)りでは |
| Verb (ている) | ている + 限(かぎ)りでは | 覚(おぼ)えている → 覚(おぼ)えている限(かぎ)りでは |
| With a subject | Noun + が / の + Verb + 限(かぎ)りでは | 私(わたし) → 私(わたし)が調(しら)べた限(かぎ)りでは |
| Without では | Verb + 限(かぎ)り、… | 見(み)る → 見(み)る限(かぎ)り、… |
| Politeness | carried by the main clause | … → …問題(もんだい)はありません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb has to be one of perceiving, checking or remembering. There is
no ✗食(た)べた限(かぎ)りでは or ✗走(はし)った限(かぎ)りでは; those verbs
produce no information to limit a claim to.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
私(わたし)の知(し)る<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、彼(かれ)はまだ独身(どくしん)だ。
As far as I know, he's still single.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)から見(み)た<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、中(なか)の様子(ようす)はわからなかった。
From what I could see from outside, there was no telling what was going on inside.
</pre>

<pre>
新聞(しんぶん)で読(よ)んだ<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、事故(じこ)でけがをした人(ひと)はいなかったようだ。
From what I read in the paper, nobody appears to have been hurt in the accident.
</pre>

<pre>
調(しら)べた<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、その会社(かいしゃ)に問題(もんだい)は見(み)つかりませんでした。
As far as our checks went, we found nothing wrong with that company.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)予報(よほう)を聞(き)いた<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、週末(しゅうまつ)は晴(は)れるらしい。
Going by what the forecast said, the weekend should be fine.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が覚(おぼ)えている<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、この辺(へん)には昔(むかし)畑(はたけ)しかなかった。
As far as I can remember, there was nothing round here but fields.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)を一通(ひととお)り読(よ)んだ<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、今回(こんかい)の計画(けいかく)に大(おお)きな無理(むり)はなさそうです。
Having read through the documents once, I don't see anything unworkable in this plan.
</pre>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)で話(はな)した<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>元気(げんき)そうだったが、実際(じっさい)に会(あ)ってみないと本当(ほんとう)のところはわからない。
He sounded fine on the phone, but until I actually see him I won't really know.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **hedge**, and a socially useful one. It says the speaker is being
careful about the boundary of their knowledge, which is why it is standard in
business reporting, in medical and legal contexts, and any time you are asked about
something you only half know. It is register-neutral: the pattern is identical in
plain and polite speech.

私(わたし)の知(し)る限(かぎ)り(では) has hardened into a near-set phrase -
the English "as far as I'm aware" - and is the shape you will hear most often.
見(み)た限(かぎ)りでは and 調(しら)べた限(かぎ)りでは are the other two
that come up constantly.

Dropping では costs a little. 見(み)る限(かぎ)り, without it, is crisper and
common in writing. Adding では brings in は's contrastive edge - "at least by that
measure, whatever other measures may say" - and it pairs naturally with a following
が clause that admits the limits of the evidence.

One caution: because the pattern advertises incomplete knowledge, it makes an
assertion weaker, not stronger. Using it about something you are sure of sounds
evasive, as though you were leaving yourself an exit. If you know, say so plainly.

## Contrast

### 限(かぎ)りでは vs 限(かぎ)り（as long as）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with a **knowing / seeing** verb, 限(かぎ)り limits the
**claim**. With a state, it limits the **time or condition**.

</div>

<pre>
聞(き)いた<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、来月(らいげつ)から値上(ねあ)げされるそうだ。
From what I've heard, prices go up from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
体(からだ)が元気(げんき)な<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、働(はたら)き続(つづ)けたい。
As long as I'm in good health, I want to keep working.
</pre>

The second is the "as long as" 限(かぎ)り, and its negative partner
ない限(かぎ)り means
"unless". They share a noun and nothing else. The test is the verb: a verb of
information gives you this page's pattern.

### 限(かぎ)りでは vs によると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>によると</code> names the **source** of a report;
限(かぎ)りでは names the **extent** of the speaker's own checking.

</div>

<pre>
天気(てんき)予報(よほう)<b>によると</b>、明日(あした)は雨(あめ)だそうだ。
According to the forecast, it'll rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で確(たし)かめた<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、明日(あした)の予約(よやく)は取(と)れていない。
As far as I've checked myself, there's no booking for tomorrow.
</pre>

<code>によると</code> passes responsibility to someone else and usually ends in
そうだ or らしい. 限(かぎ)りでは keeps responsibility with the speaker but caps
how far it extends. The two combine happily:
新聞(しんぶん)で読(よ)んだ限(かぎ)りでは… already implies the source.

### 限(かぎ)りでは vs ところを見(み)ると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 限(かぎ)りでは **narrows a claim**;
<code>ところを見(み)ると</code> **supplies grounds for a guess**.

</div>

<pre>
見(み)た<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、部屋(へや)に変(か)わったところはなかった。
As far as I could see, there was nothing unusual about the room.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が荒(あ)らされている<b>ところを見(み)ると</b>、誰(だれ)かが探(さが)し物(もの)をしたらしい。
Judging by the state of the room, somebody was searching for something.
</pre>

Both involve looking, and they point in opposite directions. The first reports what
was found and admits it may not be everything; the second takes what was found and
leaps to a conclusion. That is why the first ends in a plain statement and the
second must end in a conjecture.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 走(はし)った<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、この道(みち)は安全(あんぜん)だ。 → ✅ 走(はし)って<b>みた限(かぎ)りでは</b>、この道(みち)は安全(あんぜん)そうだ。
  The verb has to yield information. Plain 走(はし)る does not; てみる rescues
  it by turning the running into a trial.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は知(し)る<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b> → ✅ 私(わたし)<b>の</b>知(し)る限(かぎ)りでは（or 私(わたし)<b>が</b>知(し)っている限(かぎ)りでは）
  The clause modifies a noun, so its subject takes の or が - never は.

- ❌ 聞(き)く<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 聞(き)いた<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>中止(ちゅうし)になったそうだ。
  If the hearing has already happened, use the た-form; the dictionary form suits
  standing knowledge like 知(し)る.

- ❌ 調(しら)べた<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、絶対(ぜったい)に問題(もんだい)はない。 → ✅ 調(しら)べた<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、問題(もんだい)はなさそうだ。
  The pattern admits the evidence is partial. 絶対(ぜったい)に contradicts it in
  the same breath.

## Practice

**1.** 私(わたし)が____限(かぎ)りでは、会議(かいぎ)の時間(じかん)はまだ決(き)まっていません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（知(し)っている also fits）

A verb of information is required, and the ている form suits knowledge the speaker
currently holds. Note the subject takes が, not は, inside the modifying clause.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 体力(たいりょく)がある（限(かぎ)り・限(かぎ)りでは）、この仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)けたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This is the "as long as" 限(かぎ)り: a state sets a condition on the main clause. The
限(かぎ)りでは of this page needs a verb of knowing or checking in front of it.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The photos are the whole of the evidence, and the pattern fences the judgement

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 私(わたし)<b>が</b>覚(おぼ)えている限(かぎ)りでは、去年(きょねん)も同(おな)じ場所(ばしょ)でやりました。

限(かぎ)り is a noun and the clause in front modifies it, so the subject inside

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an unqualified guarantee. The speaker takes responsibility for the whole
claim, which for a medicine is a very large thing to do.

(b) states exactly how far the speaker's knowledge reaches and stops there. It is
the careful, professional version: nothing has turned up in the checking that was
done, and the sentence quietly admits that other checking might turn up something
else.

</details>

# ～に値する

**Meaning** · to be worth ~; to deserve ~ - says that something has earned a particular response

Read <code>に値(あたい)する</code>. The verb 値(あたい)する means "to be worth,
to merit", and what goes in front of it is the **response the thing deserves**:
尊敬(そんけい)に値(あたい)する "deserves respect",
注目(ちゅうもく)に値(あたい)する "is worth paying attention to". Note which way
round it runs - the noun is not what the thing is worth *in*, but what it is worth
*of*.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sino-Japanese action noun | Noun + に値(あたい)する | 尊敬(そんけい) → 尊敬(そんけい)に値(あたい)する |
| Verb (dictionary) | plain form + に値(あたい)する | 見(み)る → 見(み)るに値(あたい)する |
| Negative | Noun + に値(あたい)しない | 同情(どうじょう) → 同情(どうじょう)に値(あたい)しない |
| Modifying a noun | Noun + に値(あたい)する + Noun | 注目(ちゅうもく) → 注目(ちゅうもく)に値(あたい)する結果(けっか) |
| Polite | … + に値(あたい)します | 検討(けんとう) → 検討(けんとう)に値(あたい)します |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun is already an action, so no する comes between it and the
pattern: 注目(ちゅうもく)に値(あたい)する, never
✗注目(ちゅうもく)するに値(あたい)する. Common partners:
尊敬(そんけい), 注目(ちゅうもく), 賞賛(しょうさん), 検討(けんとう),
同情(どうじょう), 信頼(しんらい), 一読(いちどく), 評価(ひょうか).

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
その研究(けんきゅう)は注目(ちゅうもく)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。
That research is worth paying attention to.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の努力(どりょく)は尊敬(そんけい)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。
His hard work deserves respect.
</pre>

<pre>
この小説(しょうせつ)は一読(いちどく)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>と思(おも)います。
I think this novel is worth a read.
</pre>

<pre>
あんな言(い)い訳(わけ)は同情(どうじょう)<b>に値(あたい)しない</b>。
An excuse like that deserves no sympathy.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の提案(ていあん)は検討(けんとう)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>内容(ないよう)だ。
This proposal has enough in it to be worth considering.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)をかけて見(み)る<b>に値(あたい)する</b>映画(えいが)は、年(ねん)に数本(すうほん)しかない。
Films worth spending the time on come along only a few times a year.
</pre>

<pre>
災害(さいがい)の時(とき)に真(ま)っ先(さき)に動(うご)いた人(ひと)たちの行動(こうどう)は、賞賛(しょうさん)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>ものだった。
The way those who moved first in the disaster acted was worthy of the highest praise.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)を並(なら)べただけの報告書(ほうこくしょ)は、信頼(しんらい)<b>に値(あたい)しない</b>とまでは言(い)わないが、判断(はんだん)の材料(ざいりょう)にはならない。
I wouldn't go so far as to call a report that just lists figures untrustworthy, but it gives you nothing to decide on.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Firmly **written and formal**. It belongs to reviews, editorials, academic writing,
speeches, awards citations and formal assessments. In conversation it sounds
weighty, almost oratorical; a friend recommending a film says
見(み)る価値(かち)があるよ or just すごくよかったよ, not
見(み)るに値(あたい)する.

That weight is exactly why the pattern is useful. Saying
尊敬(そんけい)に値(あたい)する about someone is a considered public
judgement, not a casual compliment, and the negative
信頼(しんらい)に値(あたい)しない is correspondingly severe - it is the
language of a formal accusation, not of a grumble.

Grammatically, 値(あたい)する is a する-verb in its own right and conjugates
normally: 値(あたい)します, 値(あたい)しない, 値(あたい)した. The kanji
値(あたい) is also read ね in 値段(ねだん) and あたい in the mathematical
値(あたい); here it is あたい.

## Contrast

### に値(あたい)する vs 価値(かち)がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same idea at two registers.
<code>価値(かち)がある</code> is everyday; <code>に値(あたい)する</code> is
formal and takes a narrow set of evaluation nouns.

</div>

<pre>
あの美術館(びじゅつかん)は行(い)く<b>価値(かち)がある</b>よ。
That gallery's worth a visit.
</pre>

<pre>
あの美術館(びじゅつかん)の収蔵品(しゅうぞうひん)は注目(ちゅうもく)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。
The gallery's collection merits serious attention.
</pre>

The first is something you say to a friend; the second is something you write in a
column. Note also that <code>価値(かち)がある</code> attaches freely to any verb,
while <code>に値(あたい)する</code> is happiest with a formal noun of response.

### に値(あたい)する vs にふさわしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に値(あたい)する</code> = **has earned it**.
<code>にふさわしい</code> = **is a good match for it**.

</div>

<pre>
その働(はたら)きは表彰(ひょうしょう)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。
That performance deserves a commendation.
</pre>

<pre>
その服(ふく)は式(しき)<b>にふさわしい</b>とは言(い)えない。
I wouldn't say those clothes are appropriate for the ceremony.
</pre>

Desert versus suitability. Clothes can be unsuitable without having deserved
anything, and a piece of work can deserve a prize without being a good "match" for
it. Swapping the two produces sentences that are grammatical and mean the wrong
thing.

### に値(あたい)する vs に足(た)る

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - near-synonyms. <code>に足(た)る</code> is rarer, more
literary, and survives mostly in set phrases.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は信頼(しんらい)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>人物(じんぶつ)だ。
He is a person who deserves to be trusted.
</pre>

<pre>
信頼(しんらい)<b>に足(た)る</b>証拠(しょうこ)はまだ出(で)ていない。
No evidence solid enough to rely on has yet emerged.
</pre>

<code>に足(た)る</code> leans towards "sufficient for" and turns up in legal and
scholarly prose; its negative 取(と)るに足(た)りない ("trivial") is the form
most learners meet first. For ordinary formal writing,
<code>に値(あたい)する</code> is the safer choice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この本(ほん)は読(よ)んだ<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は読(よ)む<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。
  A verb attaches in its dictionary form.

- ❌ 注目(ちゅうもく)する<b>に値(あたい)する</b>発見(はっけん) → ✅ 注目(ちゅうもく)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>発見(はっけん)
  With a する-noun, drop the する. The noun itself is what the thing deserves.

- ❌ このケーキは食(た)べる<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。 → ✅ このケーキは食(た)べてみる価値(かち)がある。
  Grammatical but comically grand. Everyday judgements want
  <code>価値(かち)がある</code>.

- ❌ reading 値(ね)する → ✅ 値(あたい)する
  ね belongs to 値段(ねだん) and 値上(ねあ)げ. The verb is
  値(あたい)する.

## Practice

**1.** この論文(ろんぶん)の結論(けつろん)は検討(けんとう)____。

この論文(ろんぶん)の結論(けつろん)は検討(けんとう)<b>に値(あたい)する</b>。

検討(けんとう) is one of the stock nouns this pattern takes, and no する comes

</details>

あれは見(み)る<b>価値(かち)があった</b>。

Both are grammatical, but this is a casual remark about a weekend outing.
<code>に値(あたい)する</code> would sound as though the speaker were delivering a

</details>

</details>

way to say it in a meeting or an email. Choosing (a) in casual speech does not make
you wrong, only conspicuously stiff.

</details>

# ～傾向がある

**Meaning** · there is a tendency to ~; ~ tends to happen - a measured statement about which way things lean

Read <code>傾向(けいこう)がある</code>. 傾向(けいこう) is the noun
"inclination, trend", and the whole phrase reports a **direction observable across
many cases** - a group, a season, a market, a type of person. It is the vocabulary
of surveys and observation, and unlike ～がち it carries no built-in criticism.

## Formation

傾向(けいこう) is a noun, so what precedes it is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + 傾向(けいこう)がある | 増(ふ)える → 増(ふ)える傾向(けいこう)がある |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 傾向(けいこう)がある | 読(よ)まない → 読(よ)まない傾向(けいこう)がある |
| い-adjective | ～くなる + 傾向(けいこう)がある | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)くなる傾向(けいこう)がある |
| な-adjective | ～になる + 傾向(けいこう)がある | 静(しず)かな → 静(しず)かになる傾向(けいこう)がある |
| Noun | Noun + の + 傾向(けいこう)がある | 上昇(じょうしょう) → 上昇(じょうしょう)の傾向(けいこう)がある |
| Compound, more formal | Noun + 傾向(けいこう)にある | 減少(げんしょう) → 減少(げんしょう)傾向(けいこう)にある |
| Other predicates on 傾向(けいこう) | …傾向(けいこう)が見(み)られる / 強(つよ)まる | - → 傾向(けいこう)が見(み)られる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - an adjective cannot attach directly. ✗忙(いそが)しい
傾向(けいこう)がある has to become 忙(いそが)しくなる傾向(けいこう)がある:
a tendency is a movement, so the adjective needs なる to supply one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
若(わか)い人(ひと)は新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)まない<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
Young people tend not to read newspapers.
</pre>

<pre>
冬(ふゆ)は電気(でんき)代(だい)が上(あ)がる<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
Electricity bills tend to go up in winter.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)の人口(じんこう)は減少(げんしょう)<b>傾向(けいこう)にある</b>。
Japan's population is on a downward trend.
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れている時(とき)ほど甘(あま)いものが欲(ほ)しくなる<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
The more tired you are, the more you tend to crave something sweet.
</pre>

<pre>
この地域(ちいき)では夏(なつ)の雨(あめ)が年々(ねんねん)減(へ)る<b>傾向(けいこう)が見(み)られる</b>。
In this region a year-on-year decline in summer rainfall can be observed.
</pre>

<pre>
まじめな人(ひと)ほど一人(ひとり)で問題(もんだい)を抱(かか)え込(こ)む<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>ので、周(まわ)りの声(こえ)かけが大切(たいせつ)だ。
The more conscientious someone is, the more they tend to shoulder problems alone, so a word from those around them matters.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い商品(しょうひん)ほどよく売(う)れるという<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>わけではない。
It isn't the case that the more expensive an item is, the better it sells.
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)によると、在宅(ざいたく)勤務(きんむ)が増(ふ)えてから、通勤(つうきん)時間(じかん)の長(なが)い人(ひと)ほど引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを考(かんが)える<b>傾向(けいこう)が強(つよ)まっている</b>。
According to the survey, since working from home became common, the longer people's commute, the more strongly they tend to consider moving.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Analytical and slightly formal, but entirely usable in careful speech. It is the
default phrasing in survey reports, news writing, medical advice and business
analysis, and it also turns up whenever a speaker wants to generalise responsibly -
傾向(けいこう)がある says "on the whole, more often than not", and so protects
the claim from a listener who has a counterexample ready.

Three things follow from what 傾向(けいこう) means.

It describes **many cases, not one**. A single occasion is just a fact:
昨日(きのう)は遅(おく)れた, not ✗昨日(きのう)は遅(おく)れる
傾向(けいこう)があった.

It is **neutral**. Unlike ～がち, which almost always reports something unwelcome,
傾向(けいこう)がある is happy with good trends, bad ones and indifferent ones.

<code>傾向(けいこう)にある</code> is stiffer and narrower. It pairs with
directional nouns - 増加(ぞうか), 減少(げんしょう), 回復(かいふく), and
with 上昇(じょうしょう) - and belongs to written reporting. Everywhere else,
傾向(けいこう)がある is the form to use.

## Contrast

### 傾向(けいこう)がある vs ～がち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ～がち reports a **failing** of one person or thing, and is
usually negative. <code>傾向(けいこう)がある</code> reports a **distribution**,
and is neutral.

</div>

<pre>
弟(おとうと)は朝(あさ)寝坊(ねぼう)し<b>がち</b>だ。
My little brother has a habit of oversleeping.
</pre>

<pre>
中学生(ちゅうがくせい)は睡眠(すいみん)時間(じかん)が短(みじか)くなる<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
Middle-school students tend to get less sleep.
</pre>

They also attach differently: がち follows the ます-stem
（忘(わす)れがち, 休(やす)みがち）and a few nouns（病気(びょうき)がち）,
while 傾向(けいこう) follows the dictionary form. Putting がち on a whole
population sounds like a complaint about them; putting
傾向(けいこう)がある on your own brother sounds like a research finding.

### 傾向(けいこう)がある vs ことが多(おお)い

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことが多(おお)い</code> counts **how often**.
<code>傾向(けいこう)がある</code> claims a **direction**, and often a cause
behind it.

</div>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)は込(こ)む<b>ことが多(おお)い</b>。
Saturdays are often crowded.
</pre>

<pre>
土曜日(どようび)は時間(じかん)が経(た)つほど込(こ)む<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
On Saturdays it tends to get busier as the day goes on.
</pre>

The first is a frequency; the second describes a movement with a shape to it. That
is why 傾向(けいこう) takes 強(つよ)まる, 見(み)られる and
続(つづ)く as predicates, and ことが多(おお)い takes none of them.

### 傾向(けいこう)がある vs ～っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ～っぽい describes the **character of one individual**.
<code>傾向(けいこう)がある</code> describes a **pattern across a set**.

</div>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は最近(さいきん)忘(わす)れ<b>っぽく</b>なった。
My grandfather has got forgetful lately.
</pre>

<pre>
年(とし)を取(と)ると名前(なまえ)が出(で)てこなくなる<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
As people get older they tend to have trouble producing names.
</pre>

っぽい makes a colloquial, impressionistic claim about a person in front of you;
傾向(けいこう)がある makes a cautious general claim about people at large. Only
the second belongs in writing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)くなる<b>傾向(けいこう)があった</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)は長(なが)かった。
  One meeting is an event, not a tendency. A trend needs repetition behind it.

- ❌ 最近(さいきん)忙(いそが)しい<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。 → ✅ 最近(さいきん)忙(いそが)しくなる<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
  An い-adjective needs なる before 傾向(けいこう) - a tendency is a movement,
  not a state.

- ❌ 物価(ぶっか)が上(あ)がる<b>傾向(けいこう)をある</b>。 → ✅ 物価(ぶっか)が上(あ)がる<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
  ある takes が. This is the ordinary existence verb, not a transitive one.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は風邪(かぜ)を引(ひ)く<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は風邪(かぜ)を引(ひ)き<b>がち</b>だ。
  For one person's recurring weakness, がち is the natural word.
  傾向(けいこう)がある makes him sound like a data set.

## Practice

**1.** 都会(とかい)では結婚(けっこん)する年齢(ねんれい)が高(たか)く____傾向(けいこう)がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

高(たか)い is an い-adjective, so なる has to supply the movement that
傾向(けいこう) needs. The claim is about a population, which is what makes the
pattern appropriate.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

姉(あね)は約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)に<b>遅(おく)れがち</b>だ。

One family member's habit, and a mildly unwelcome one - exactly がち's territory.
傾向(けいこう)がある would give a personal complaint the tone of a statistical

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

最近(さいきん)の若者(わかもの)は店(みせ)よりネットで買(か)い物(もの)をする<b>傾向(けいこう)が強(つよ)まっている</b>。

The claim is about a whole age group and about a direction that is intensifying, so
傾向(けいこう) takes 強(つよ)まる rather than a plain ある.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この会社(かいしゃ)の売(う)り上(あ)げは増加(ぞうか)する傾向(けいこう)をある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この会社(かいしゃ)の売(う)り上(あ)げは増加(ぞうか)<b>傾向(けいこう)にある</b>。
（or 増(ふ)える傾向(けいこう)<b>が</b>ある）

The particle must be が with ある, never を. With a directional noun like
増加(ぞうか) the tighter compound 増加(ぞうか)傾向(けいこう)にある is the

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日本人(にほんじん)ははっきり断(ことわ)らない<b>傾向(けいこう)がある</b>。
(b) 日本人(にほんじん)ははっきり断(ことわ)らない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a flat generalisation about a whole nationality, and it is the kind of claim
a single counterexample destroys. It also sounds like a stereotype stated as fact.

(a) makes the same observation as a **tendency**: more often than not, on the whole.
It admits exceptions in advance, which is both more accurate and more careful, and
it is why 傾向(けいこう)がある is the standard phrasing for generalisations about
groups.

</details>
