# N2 - Reasons, and why it's only natural


---

# ～ものだから

**Meaning** · because - the reason you put forward when you feel you owe the listener one

`ものだから` is a defence, not a neutral cause. You were late, you forgot, you did
something that needs accounting for, and this clause is the account: *the reason
was strong enough that anyone would have done the same*. N3 met the short reply
`もの／もん` ("because, that's why"); `ものだから` is that same instinct stretched
into a full subordinate clause, and it is one of the most useful things a learner
can have ready when apologising.

## Formation

Always written in kana. The clause in front is in plain form, and な-adjectives and
nouns link with `な` - `だ` is not possible.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ものだから | 寝坊(ねぼう)した → 寝坊(ねぼう)したものだから |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ものだから | 気(き)づかなかった → 気(き)づかなかったものだから |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものだから | 眠(ねむ)かった → 眠(ねむ)かったものだから |
| な-adjective | ～な + ものだから | 不安(ふあん)だ → 不安(ふあん)なものだから |
| Noun | ～な + ものだから | 初(はじ)めて → 初(はじ)めてなものだから |
| Casual | ～もんだから | 慌(あわ)てた → 慌(あわ)てたもんだから |
| Polite | ～ものですから | 急(きゅう)だ → 急(きゅう)なものですから |
| Left hanging | ～ものですから。 | 道(みち)が混(こ)んでいたものですから。 |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause after `ものだから` reports a **fact**, normally one
that has already happened. It cannot be an order, a request, an invitation or a
statement of intention. Those all need plain `から`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
外(そと)が暗(くら)かった<b>ものだから</b>、道(みち)を間違(まちが)えました。
It was dark out, so I took the wrong road.
</pre>

<pre>
あまりに安(やす)かった<b>もんだから</b>、3つも買(か)っちゃった。
It was so cheap I went and bought three of them.
</pre>

<pre>
子供(こども)が熱(ねつ)を出(だ)した<b>ものですから</b>、昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)は欠席(けっせき)しました。
My child came down with a fever, so I missed yesterday's meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、地図(ちず)を見(み)るのが苦手(にがて)な<b>ものですから</b>。
Sorry - it's just that I'm hopeless with maps.
</pre>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した<b>ものだから</b>、洗濯物(せんたくもの)がびしょびしょになってしまった。
It started pouring out of nowhere, so the washing got soaked.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は耳(みみ)が遠(とお)い<b>ものですから</b>、テレビの音(おと)をいつも大(おお)きくしています。
My grandmother is hard of hearing, so she always has the television turned right up.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)してきたばかりで、この辺(へん)の地理(ちり)がまだよく分(わ)からない<b>ものですから</b>、とんでもない遠回(とおまわ)りをしてしまいました。
I've only just moved here and still don't know my way around, so I took an absurd detour.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)にどうしても今日中(きょうじゅう)にと言(い)われた<b>ものだから</b>、昨日(きのう)は終電(しゅうでん)まで残(のこ)っていました。
The department head insisted it had to be done that day, so I stayed till the last train.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ものだから` belongs to speech and to the spoken end of writing - a message to a
colleague, an explanation offered at the door. It is not used in reports, papers or
official notices, where <code>ため</code> does the same work without the emotional
colour.

The register ladder is worth memorising as a set: `もんだから` (friends),
`ものだから` (plain but not rough), `ものですから` (polite, and the standard shape of
a workplace apology). The polite one is often left hanging with nothing after it -
「遅(おく)れてすみません。電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっていたものですから。」 The
listener supplies the rest, and trailing off is part of the apology: you are not
going to insist on your excuse out loud.

What `ものだから` always adds is that the cause was **outsized** - too cold, too
sudden, too much. That is why it sits so comfortably with あまりに, とても and つい.
The flip side is that using it for a trivial or uncontroversial reason sounds
oddly defensive, as though you had expected to be told off and got in first.

One structural point trips people up. Because the clause is an account of something
that already stands, the main clause cannot be forward-looking: `ものだから` will not
introduce a request, an order, an invitation or a plan. If you want to *ask* for
something on the strength of a reason, use plain `から`, or the gentler
<code>もので</code>, which tolerates a set phrase like
<code>よろしくお願(ねが)いします</code> after it.

## Contrast

### ～ものだから vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` *states* a reason. `ものだから` *pleads* one.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があった<b>から</b>、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みました。
I had a temperature, so I took the day off.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があった<b>ものですから</b>、会社(かいしゃ)をお休(やす)みしました。
I had a temperature, so I'm afraid I took the day off.
</pre>

The first is information. The second is an apology with the information built in -
the speaker knows that being absent needed justifying. The other half of the
difference is grammatical: `から` will carry anything after it, including
窓(まど)を開(あ)けてください or 行(い)きましょう, and `ものだから` will not.

### ～ものだから vs ～もので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different temperature. `もので` is deferential and
self-effacing; `ものだから` leans harder on the excuse.

</div>

<pre>
不慣(ふな)れな<b>もので</b>、お時間(じかん)をいただいてしまいました。
I'm new to this, so I've taken up more of your time than I should have.
</pre>

<pre>
不慣(ふな)れな<b>ものだから</b>、同(おな)じところで3回(かい)も間違(まちが)えた。
I'm new to it, so I made the same mistake three times over.
</pre>

`もので` and `ものですから` shade toward humility, which is why they survive in
customer-facing speech; `ものだから` keeps more of the "look, it really was like
that" energy. `もので` is also the more flexible of the two about what follows: a
polite formula after it is fine, where `ものだから` would clash.

### ～ものだから vs ～のだから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ものだから` faces **inward** and excuses the speaker.
`のだから` faces **outward** and presses the listener: given this, obviously…

</div>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で決(き)めた<b>のだから</b>、最後(さいご)までやりなさい。
You decided this yourself, so see it through.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で決(き)めた<b>ものだから</b>、誰(だれ)にも相談(そうだん)しませんでした。
I'd decided it on my own, so I didn't consult anybody.
</pre>

`のだから` takes the premise as agreed between you and builds a demand or a firm
conclusion on it - which is exactly the sentence type `ものだから` refuses.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 暑(あつ)い<b>ものだから</b>、窓(まど)を開(あ)けてください。 → ✅ 暑(あつ)い<b>から</b>、窓(まど)を開(あ)けてください。
  `ものだから` accounts for something already done. It cannot license a request.

- ❌ 不安(ふあん)<b>だものだから</b> → ✅ 不安(ふあん)<b>なものだから</b>
  な-adjectives and nouns link with `な`. <code>学生(がくせい)だものだから</code> is
  wrong the same way; it has to be <code>学生(がくせい)なものだから</code>.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>ものだから</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になるだろう。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>から</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になるだろう。
  The reason has to be an actual circumstance, not a forecast. `ものだから` cannot
  excuse something that has not happened yet.

- ❌ この地域(ちいき)は降水量(こうすいりょう)が多(おお)い<b>ものだから</b>、土砂(どしゃ)災害(さいがい)が起(お)こりやすい。 → ✅ …多(おお)い<b>ため</b>、土砂(どしゃ)災害(さいがい)が起(お)こりやすい。
  In a report or an article the personal, apologetic colour of `ものだから` is out of
  place. Written analysis uses <code>ため</code>.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れた____、約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)に間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れた<b>ものですから</b>

You are apologising, and the clause is your defence, so the excuse-flavoured
connector is what fits. The polite <code>ものですから</code> matches the ました ending
and the すみません in front of it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 危(あぶ)ない（ものだから・から）、手(て)すりにつかまってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

危(あぶ)ない<b>から</b>、手(て)すりにつかまってください。

The main clause is a request, and `ものだから` cannot introduce one. It only accounts
for a fact that already stands, so a following てください breaks the sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's just that this is my first time doing it, so I got more nervous than I expected."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

初(はじ)めて<b>なものですから</b>、思(おも)っていた以上(いじょう)に緊張(きんちょう)してしまいました。

A bare noun links to <code>もの</code> with `な`, never with `だ`. "It's just that"
is exactly the apologetic weight <code>ものですから</code> carries, and the polite
shape fits an explanation offered to someone you are not close to.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 忙(いそが)しかったものだから、来週(らいしゅう)連絡(れんらく)するつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 忙(いそが)しかったものだから、連絡(れんらく)が遅(おそ)くなってしまいました。
（or 忙(いそが)しい<b>ので</b>、来週(らいしゅう)連絡(れんらく)するつもりです。）

A statement of intention cannot follow `ものだから`. Either keep the excuse and put
an accomplished fact after it, or keep the plan and switch to `ので`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 子供(こども)が生(う)まれた<b>ものですから</b>、引(ひ)っ越(こ)すことにしました。
(b) 子供(こども)が生(う)まれた<b>のですから</b>、引(ひ)っ越(こ)したほうがいいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker explaining their own move to someone who might wonder about it -
"we had a baby, that's the reason." Nobody is being asked to do anything; the
sentence heads off the question before it is put.

(b) turns the same fact on the listener. `のですから` treats the birth as common
ground and pushes a recommendation off it: "you've had a baby, so you really ought
to move." Swap the two and both stop working - (a) cannot carry advice, and (b)
stops sounding like an account of the speaker's own situation.

</details>

# ～ことだから

**Meaning** · knowing them as we both do, it follows that… - a prediction argued from someone's established character

`ことだから` does not explain what happened. It reaches into what you and your
listener **already know about a person** and predicts from it: *he's the sort who
always…, so of course he'll…*. The reason it gives is a reputation, and the clause
that follows is a guess, never a report. Socially it is a vouching move - you are
staking your prediction on shared knowledge, and inviting the listener to agree.

## Formation

`こと` here is a noun, so the thing in front joins with `の`. The overwhelmingly
common shape is **a person the two of you know** + `の` + `ことだから`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Person (name) | Noun + の + ことだから | 田中(たなか)さん → 田中(たなか)さんのことだから |
| Person (pronoun) | Noun + の + ことだから | 彼(かれ) → 彼(かれ)のことだから |
| Described person | Modifier + Noun + の + ことだから | 優(やさ)しい母(はは) → 優(やさ)しい母(はは)のことだから |
| Group or organisation | Noun + の + ことだから | あの会社(かいしゃ) → あの会社(かいしゃ)のことだから |
| A known circumstance | Noun + の + ことだから | 初(はじ)めて → 初(はじ)めてのことだから |
| な-adjective | ～な + ことだから | 急(きゅう)だ → 急(きゅう)なことだから |
| Polite | ～のことですから | 部長(ぶちょう) → 部長(ぶちょう)のことですから |
| Typical ending | …だろう／に違(ちが)いない／はずだ／でしょう | きっと来(く)るだろう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `の` is not optional, and the main clause must be a
**conjecture**. ❌<code>彼(かれ)ことだから</code> and
❌<code>彼(かれ)のことだから、昨日(きのう)来(き)ました</code> are both broken.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)<b>のことだから</b>、また遅(おく)れて来(く)るだろう。
Knowing him, he'll turn up late again.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)<b>のことだから</b>、もう準備(じゅんび)を終(お)えているはずだ。
Knowing my mother, she'll have finished packing already.
</pre>

<pre>
真面目(まじめ)な田中(たなか)さん<b>のことだから</b>、約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れるわけがない。
Tanaka being the conscientious sort he is, there's no way he'd forget.
</pre>

<pre>
心配性(しんぱいしょう)の姉(あね)<b>のことですから</b>、着(つ)いたらすぐ電話(でんわ)をくれると思(おも)います。
My sister's a worrier, so I'm sure she'll ring the moment she lands.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めて<b>のことだから</b>、多少(たしょう)の失敗(しっぱい)は覚悟(かくご)しておいたほうがいい。
It's our first time at this, so we'd better be ready for a few things to go wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
あの会社(かいしゃ)<b>のことだから</b>、値上(ねあ)げの発表(はっぴょう)は連休(れんきゅう)の直前(ちょくぜん)にするに違(ちが)いない。
Being the outfit they are, they're bound to announce the price rise right before the holiday.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)に慣(な)れている祖父(そふ)<b>のことですから</b>、天気(てんき)が崩(くず)れる前(まえ)に下(お)りてきているでしょう。
My grandfather knows those mountains, so he'll have come down before the weather turned.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りにうるさい編集長(へんしゅうちょう)<b>のことだから</b>、一日(いちにち)でも遅(おく)れたら大変(たいへん)なことになるだろう。
Our editor is a stickler for deadlines, so being even a day late would be a disaster.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ことだから` runs on shared knowledge. If your listener has never heard of the person,
the sentence collapses, because there is no reputation to reason from - you would
have to spell the character out first, or use plain `から`. This is why the noun so
often comes with a describing phrase attached:
<code>心配性(しんぱいしょう)の姉(あね)</code>,
<code>時間(じかん)に正確(せいかく)な彼(かれ)</code>. The modifier is doing the
work of reminding the listener what you both know.

The tone is usually warm or wry. Said about a friend it is affectionate; said about
a company or an official body it is often faintly cynical -
「あの役所(やくしょ)のことだから、返事(へんじ)は来月(らいげつ)になるよ」. It is not
neutral either way; there is always a judgement of character riding along.

Register is unremarkable - `のことだから` in casual speech, `のことですから` in polite
speech and in letters. It is common in both, and there is no written/spoken split
worth worrying about.

The one thing it cannot do is state a fact. `ことだから` sets up a guess, so the
sentence ends in だろう, でしょう, はずだ, に違(ちが)いない, かもしれない, or a
prediction phrased as a plain non-past. A past-tense report after it is wrong, even
if the report happens to match the person's character.

## Contrast

### ～ことだから vs ～ものだから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ものだから` excuses something that **already happened**;
`ことだから` predicts something that **has not happened yet**.

</div>

The two look alike and do opposite jobs. `ものだから` takes a whole clause in front
of it and faces backwards; `ことだから` takes a person's name and faces forwards.

<pre>
道(みち)が混(こ)んでいた<b>ものだから</b>、開演(かいえん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。
The traffic was bad, so I didn't make it before the curtain.
</pre>

<pre>
慎重(しんちょう)な彼(かれ)<b>のことだから</b>、一時間(いちじかん)前(まえ)には着(つ)いているだろう。
Careful as he is, he'll have been there an hour early.
</pre>

Swapping them is not a nuance error but a structural one: ❌<code>彼(かれ)のものだから</code>
means "it belongs to him", and ❌<code>道(みち)が混(こ)んでいたことだから</code>
is not Japanese at all.

### ～ことだから vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` needs no shared knowledge and accepts any main clause.
`ことだから` needs both a known character and a conjecture.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は朝(あさ)が苦手(にがて)だ<b>から</b>、早朝(そうちょう)の会議(かいぎ)はやめよう。
He's not a morning person, so let's drop the early meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
朝(あさ)が苦手(にがて)な彼(かれ)<b>のことだから</b>、早朝(そうちょう)の会議(かいぎ)には来(こ)ないだろう。
Knowing how bad he is in the mornings, he won't show up to an early meeting.
</pre>

The `から` sentence proposes an action; the `ことだから` sentence forecasts a
behaviour. Only `から` can be followed by やめよう.

### ～ことだから vs ～ことだし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことだから` argues from **who someone is**. `ことだし` piles up
**circumstances** - and there is usually more than one - to justify a decision.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)も降(ふ)っている<b>ことだし</b>、今日(きょう)は家(いえ)で過(す)ごそう。
It's raining, among other things, so let's stay in today.
</pre>

<pre>
出無精(でぶしょう)の兄(あに)<b>のことだから</b>、誘(さそ)っても来(こ)ないと思(おも)う。
My brother being the homebody he is, I doubt he'd come even if we asked.
</pre>

`ことだし` leads to what the speaker will *do*; `ことだから` leads to what someone else
will *turn out to have done*. They are not interchangeable in either direction.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)<b>ことだから</b>、きっと大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だ。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)<b>のことだから</b>、きっと大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だ。
  <code>こと</code> is a noun and the phrase in front modifies it, so `の` is
  obligatory.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんのことだから、昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)に来(き)ました。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんのことだから、昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)にも来(き)ていたはずだ。
  The main clause has to be a conjecture. A flat report of what happened leaves the
  reputation with nothing to do.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>ことだから</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>から</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  `ことだから` is not a general-purpose "because". It cannot take an ordinary event
  clause as its reason.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)会(あ)ったばかりの人(ひと)のことだから、たぶん来(こ)ないだろう。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)会(あ)ったばかりだから、来(く)るかどうか分(わ)からない。
  You cannot argue from a character neither of you knows. `ことだから` presupposes
  that the listener recognises the person and will nod along.

## Practice

**1.** 時間(じかん)に正確(せいかく)な山田(やまだ)さん____、もう会場(かいじょう)に着(つ)いているでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山田(やまだ)さん<b>のことですから</b>

The sentence reasons from a known trait - punctuality - to a guess about where he is
now, which is exactly the job of <code>のことだから</code>. Note the `の`, and note
that the ending でしょう supplies the required conjecture.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 急(きゅう)に熱(ねつ)が出(で)た（ことだから・ものだから）、旅行(りょこう)を取(と)り消(け)しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

急(きゅう)に熱(ねつ)が出(で)た<b>ものだから</b>、旅行(りょこう)を取(と)り消(け)しました。

This is an account of something already done, offered as an excuse - `ものだから`.
`ことだから` would need a person's name in front of it and a prediction after it,
and it has neither here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Knowing how stubborn my father is, he probably hasn't changed his mind."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

頑固(がんこ)な父(ちち)<b>のことだから</b>、たぶん考(かんが)えを変(か)えていないだろう。

The describing phrase <code>頑固(がんこ)な</code> reminds the listener of the trait
being reasoned from, and だろう supplies the conjecture. Dropping the `の` or ending
in a bare past tense would both break the pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 優(やさ)しい先生(せんせい)のことですから、昨日(きのう)遅刻(ちこく)した学生(がくせい)を叱(しか)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 優(やさ)しい先生(せんせい)のことですから、遅刻(ちこく)した学生(がくせい)も叱(しか)らなかったでしょう。

Two problems in the original: a past-tense report where a conjecture belongs, and a
prediction that contradicts the trait being cited. `ことだから` only works when the
main clause is what that character would lead you to expect.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は準備(じゅんび)がいい<b>から</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)っているだろう。
(b) 準備(じゅんび)のいい彼女(かのじょ)<b>のことだから</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)っているだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states a fact about her and draws a guess from it. It works even if the listener
has never met her - the sentence supplies the information it needs.

(b) does not supply the information so much as *invoke* it. The speaker is treating
her thoroughness as something the two of them already agree on, and the sentence has
a nod in it: "you know what she's like." It also sounds a shade more confident, and
faintly fond.

</details>

# ～からこそ

**Meaning** · precisely because - this reason and no other, and the speaker is arguing for it

`からこそ` is `から` with the N3 emphatic particle `こそ` welded on. That `こそ`
changes what the sentence is *doing*: an ordinary `から` reports a cause, while
`からこそ` **contends** for one. It says the listener may have assumed some other
explanation, or may have read the situation the wrong way round, and here is the
real reason. The cause it names is nearly always one the speaker values - so the
sentence often comes out as a defence of something that looks harsh, or a tribute
to something that looks ordinary.

## Formation

Plain form + `からこそ`. Nouns and な-adjectives keep their `だ`, because `だから` is
what `こそ` is attaching to.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + からこそ | 信(しん)じる → 信(しん)じるからこそ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + からこそ | 苦労(くろう)した → 苦労(くろう)したからこそ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + からこそ | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないからこそ |
| い-adjective | plain form + からこそ | 難(むずか)しい → 難(むずか)しいからこそ |
| な-adjective | ～だ + からこそ | 大切(たいせつ)だ → 大切(たいせつ)だからこそ |
| Noun | ～だ + からこそ | 家族(かぞく) → 家族(かぞく)だからこそ |
| Usual ending | …からこそ、…のだ／のです | 続(つづ)けられたのです |
| Sentence-final | …のは…からこそだ | 勝(か)てたのは練習(れんしゅう)したからこそだ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `からこそ` and a final <code>のだ／んです</code> travel together. The
sentence is an assertion *about* a cause, and <code>のだ</code> is what marks a
sentence as an explanation. Without it, `からこそ` can feel as though it stops short.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
好(す)きだ<b>からこそ</b>、厳(きび)しいことも言(い)う。
It's because I care that I say the hard things too.
</pre>

<pre>
家族(かぞく)だ<b>からこそ</b>、言(い)えないこともある。
There are things you can't say precisely because you're family.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)した<b>からこそ</b>、次(つぎ)に何(なに)をすべきかが見(み)えてきた。
It was failing that let me see what to do next.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)が限(かぎ)られている<b>からこそ</b>、一日(いちにち)を大切(たいせつ)にできるのだ。
It is exactly because time is limited that a day can be treasured.
</pre>

<pre>
田舎(いなか)で育(そだ)った<b>からこそ</b>、季節(きせつ)の変(か)わり目(め)に敏感(びんかん)なのかもしれません。
Perhaps it's growing up in the countryside that has made me alert to the turn of the seasons.
</pre>

<pre>
君(きみ)に実力(じつりょく)がある<b>からこそ</b>、部長(ぶちょう)はあえて難(むずか)しい仕事(しごと)を任(まか)せたんだよ。
The boss gave you the hard job on purpose, precisely because you're good enough for it.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)が三十年(さんじゅうねん)続(つづ)いてきたのは、常連(じょうれん)のお客(きゃく)さんに支(ささ)えられてきた<b>からこそ</b>です。
This shop has lasted thirty years only because its regulars have kept it going.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)が当(あ)たり前(まえ)になっている<b>からこそ</b>、日々(ひび)の点検(てんけん)を怠(おこた)ってはいけないのです。
It is exactly because safety is taken for granted that the daily checks must never be skipped.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`からこそ` is emphatic, a little rhetorical, and entirely at home in speeches,
essays, editorials and heartfelt conversation. It is register-neutral in the sense
that nothing about it is rude or over-formal, but it is **marked**: you do not reach
for it to explain why you took an umbrella. Attach it to a humdrum cause and the
sentence sounds as though it is auditioning for something.

The two shapes it most often takes are worth learning whole. Mid-sentence,
<code>…からこそ、…のだ</code>. Sentence-final, <code>…のは…からこそだ</code> - which
front-loads the result and lands the cause at the end, and is a favourite of
closing lines.

Semantically, `からこそ` almost always frames the cause as something **worth having**,
even when the result is uncomfortable. 「大切(たいせつ)だからこそ厳(きび)しくする」
works; a blame sentence like 「彼(かれ)が嘘(うそ)をついたからこそ失敗(しっぱい)した」
does not - for assigning fault Japanese uses `せいで`. Nor does `からこそ` make
excuses. If your reason is a defence of your own behaviour, `ものだから` or `ので` is
the honest choice; `からこそ` would sound as though you were proud of it.

## Contrast

### ～からこそ vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` gives *a* reason. `からこそ` insists it is *the* reason,
and quietly rules the alternatives out.

</div>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)だ<b>から</b>、毎日(まいにち)電話(でんわ)している。
I'm worried, so I ring every day.
</pre>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)だ<b>からこそ</b>、あえて何(なに)も聞(き)かないでいるのです。
It's exactly because I'm worried that I'm deliberately not asking anything.
</pre>

The second sentence is answering an unspoken objection - *if you were worried you'd
ask* - and reversing it. That is the natural habitat of `からこそ`: a cause whose
effect is not the one the listener would have predicted.

### ～からこそ vs ～だけに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `からこそ` **argues** a causal link. `だけに` **measures** one:
given this circumstance, the outcome is proportionately stronger.

</div>

<pre>
期待(きたい)していた<b>だけに</b>、中止(ちゅうし)の知(し)らせはこたえた。
I'd had my hopes up, so the cancellation hit that much harder.
</pre>

<pre>
期待(きたい)していた<b>からこそ</b>、はっきり意見(いけん)を言(い)わせてもらいます。
It's because I had hopes for it that I'm going to say plainly what I think.
</pre>

`だけに` observes a matching of degrees and does not need the listener to agree with
anything; the speaker is commenting. `からこそ` is making a case, and expects a
response. That is also why `だけに` sits comfortably in a news report and `からこそ`
sits comfortably in a speech.

### ～からこそ vs ～てこそ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `からこそ` names the cause of something that **is already so**.
`てこそ` names the action **without which** the result would not come about at all.

</div>

<pre>
支(ささ)えてくれる人(ひと)がいる<b>からこそ</b>、挑戦(ちょうせん)できるのだ。
It's because there are people behind me that I can take the risk.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)の足(あし)で歩(ある)い<b>てこそ</b>、その土地(とち)のことが分(わ)かる。
You only get to know a place by walking it yourself.
</pre>

Grammatically they are easy to keep apart - `からこそ` follows a full plain-form
clause, `てこそ` follows a て-form. Semantically, the `からこそ` clause is a fact
already in place; the `てこそ` clause is a condition that has to be met.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>なからこそ</b>、集中(しゅうちゅう)できる。 → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>だからこそ</b>、集中(しゅうちゅう)できる。
  `こそ` is attaching to `だから`, so the な-adjective keeps its `だ`. Nouns behave the
  same way: <code>新人(しんじん)だからこそ</code>.

- ❌ 信(しん)じている<b>こそ</b>、任(まか)せたのです。 → ✅ 信(しん)じている<b>からこそ</b>、任(まか)せたのです。
  A whole clause cannot take bare `こそ`. `こそ` needs a `から` to sit on, or a て-form.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>からこそ</b>、試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>ので</b>、試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  Nothing is being contended here - rain cancels matches, and everyone knows it.
  `からこそ` on a routine cause sounds theatrical.

- ❌ 寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>からこそ</b>、遅刻(ちこく)してしまいました。 → ✅ 寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>ものですから</b>、遅刻(ちこく)してしまいました。
  `からこそ` cannot carry an excuse. It presents its cause as something to be proud
  of, which is the opposite of what an apology needs.

## Practice

**1.** 親(おや)は、子(こ)どものことを思(おも)っている____、時(とき)に厳(きび)しくなるのだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

思(おも)っている<b>からこそ</b>

The sentence is defending strictness against the assumption that strictness means
coldness - exactly the reversal `からこそ` exists for. Note the のだろう at the end;
`からこそ` and an explanatory <code>のだ</code> normally arrive together.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 準備(じゅんび)に一年(いちねん)かけた（からこそ・だけに）、中止(ちゅうし)の知(し)らせはつらかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

準備(じゅんび)に一年(いちねん)かけた<b>だけに</b>、中止(ちゅうし)の知(し)らせはつらかった。

The second clause reports how much something hurt, and the first clause explains why
it hurt *that much*. That is degree-matching, which is `だけに`. `からこそ` would
promise an argument the sentence never makes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's precisely because it's a difficult job that I want to try it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

難(むずか)しい仕事(しごと)だ<b>からこそ</b>、やってみたいのです。

The noun phrase <code>難(むずか)しい仕事(しごと)</code> keeps its `だ` before
`からこそ`. The whole point of the sentence is that difficulty would normally be a
reason *not* to, so the emphatic form is doing real work here.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)がミスをしたからこそ、納品(のうひん)が遅(おく)れてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)がミスをした<b>せいで</b>、納品(のうひん)が遅(おく)れてしまった。

`からこそ` presents its cause as something of value. Using it to assign blame reads
as bitter sarcasm at best. Fault takes `せいで`, and a neutral report takes `ため`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 経験(けいけん)がない<b>から</b>、自由(じゆう)な発想(はっそう)ができる。
(b) 経験(けいけん)がない<b>からこそ</b>、自由(じゆう)な発想(はっそう)ができるのだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply links the two: no experience, hence free thinking. It could be a passing
remark, and a listener might not even notice the claim being made.

(b) plants a flag. `からこそ` marks inexperience as the very thing that makes the
free thinking possible, and does so against the obvious objection that inexperience
is a handicap. The final <code>のだ</code> confirms that the sentence is an argument,
not an observation - it is the kind of line that ends a paragraph.

</details>

# ～てこそ

**Meaning** · only by actually doing it - X is what makes Y true, and short of X there is no Y

`てこそ` sets a bar. It takes an action or a state, marks it with the emphatic `こそ`
you met at N3, and declares that **this and nothing less** is what brings the result
about. What follows is a verdict of worth: it counts, it means something, it becomes
possible. Socially the pattern is an exhortation - a person saying `てこそ` is
usually telling you, or telling the room, what the real standard is.

## Formation

て-form + `こそ`. Nouns and な-adjectives go through `である`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + こそ | 続(つづ)ける → 続(つづ)けてこそ |
| Verb (potential) | て-form + こそ | 説明(せつめい)できる → 説明(せつめい)できてこそ |
| Verb (passive) | て-form + こそ | 支(ささ)えられる → 支(ささ)えられてこそ |
| Noun | ～であってこそ | 健康(けんこう) → 健康(けんこう)であってこそ |
| な-adjective | ～であってこそ | 公平(こうへい)だ → 公平(こうへい)であってこそ |
| Usual endings | …意味(いみ)がある／価値(かち)がある／～と言(い)える／～のだ | 本物(ほんもの)と言(い)える |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause after `てこそ` is **positive**: something gains value,
becomes possible, or finally deserves its name. There is no negative counterpart -
"if you don't do X, Y won't happen" is <code>～なければ～ない</code>, never
❌<code>～なくてこそ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
自分(じぶん)でやっ<b>てこそ</b>、身(み)につく。
You only really learn it by doing it yourself.
</pre>

<pre>
使(つか)っ<b>てこそ</b>、道具(どうぐ)は生(い)きる。
A tool only comes into its own when it is used.
</pre>

<pre>
最後(さいご)まで走(はし)りきっ<b>てこそ</b>、完走(かんそう)と言(い)える。
It only counts as finishing if you run all the way to the end.
</pre>

<pre>
ルールは、全員(ぜんいん)が守(まも)っ<b>てこそ</b>意味(いみ)がある。
Rules mean something only when everyone keeps them.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)の話(はなし)を最後(さいご)まで聞(き)い<b>てこそ</b>、本当(ほんとう)の話(はな)し合(あ)いになる。
It becomes a real discussion only once you hear the other side out.
</pre>

<pre>
健康(けんこう)であっ<b>てこそ</b>、好(す)きなことに打(う)ち込(こ)めるのだと気(き)づきました。
I've come to see that health is what lets you throw yourself into what you love.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)の原因(げんいん)を自分(じぶん)の言葉(ことば)で説明(せつめい)でき<b>てこそ</b>、その経験(けいけん)を次(つぎ)に生(い)かせる。
Only when you can say in your own words what went wrong can you carry the experience forward.
</pre>

<pre>
地域(ちいき)の人(ひと)たちに支(ささ)えられ<b>てこそ</b>、この祭(まつ)りは百年(ひゃくねん)続(つづ)いてきたのです。
It is only because the neighbourhood has carried it that this festival has lasted a hundred years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`てこそ` is aphoristic. It turns up in mottoes, speeches, editorials, coaching talk
and advice from someone older, and it has a faint air of a maxim even in an ordinary
conversation. That is not a warning - it is used, and used often - but it does mean
a `てこそ` sentence sounds as though it is stating a principle rather than describing
Tuesday.

Politeness lives at the end of the sentence, not in `てこそ` itself:
<code>続(つづ)けてこそ意味(いみ)があります</code> is perfectly polite. The pattern
does not shift register the way `もんだから` or `ものですから` do.

Two limits are worth holding onto. First, the second clause has to be **favourable**
- worth, meaning, possibility, the real version of a thing. A sentence whose payoff
is a misfortune wants `から` or `せいで`. Second, the `てこそ` clause is normally
something achievable and, more often than not, something a person chooses to do or
to be. A purely accidental event in that slot reads oddly, because the pattern is
implicitly holding someone to a standard.

Finally, `てこそ` frequently pairs with 初(はじ)めて for emphasis -
<code>自分(じぶん)で払(はら)ってこそ初(はじ)めて物(もの)の値段(ねだん)が分(わ)かる</code> -
and the two reinforce each other without redundancy.

## Contrast

### ～てこそ vs ～てはじめて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>てはじめて</code> is about **when you found out**;
`てこそ` is about **what makes it count**.

</div>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で暮(く)らし<b>てはじめて</b>、親(おや)のありがたさが分(わ)かった。
It was only after living alone that I understood what my parents had done for me.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で暮(く)らし<b>てこそ</b>、生活(せいかつ)の大変(たいへん)さが身(み)にしみる。
It's living alone that really brings home how hard daily life is.
</pre>

<code>てはじめて</code> is a report, usually in the past, of a realisation that came
too late; it collocates with 分(わ)かる, 気(き)づく, 知(し)る. `てこそ` is a
general claim about value, usually in the non-past, and it collocates with
意味(いみ)がある and 言(い)える. Where the sentence is your own experience, choose
<code>てはじめて</code>; where it is a principle you are recommending, choose `てこそ`.

### ～てこそ vs ～からこそ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `からこそ` explains something that **is already the case**.
`てこそ` states the condition on which something **would** be the case.

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)している<b>からこそ</b>、あの安定(あんてい)した演奏(えんそう)ができるのだ。
It's the daily practice that lets him play that steadily.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)し<b>てこそ</b>、本番(ほんばん)で力(ちから)が出(だ)せる。
Only if you practise every day can you deliver on the night.
</pre>

The first looks at a musician who already plays well and identifies the reason. The
second looks at nobody in particular and lays down a rule. The forms differ too -
plain clause + `からこそ` against て-form + `こそ` - so the shape alone will usually
tell you which one a sentence is using.

### ～てこそ vs ～なければ～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they are the positive and negative faces of one idea, and
Japanese only lets you build the negative face with `なければ`.

</div>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で調(しら)べ<b>てこそ</b>、記憶(きおく)に残(のこ)る。
Looking it up yourself is what makes it stick.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)で調(しら)べ<b>なければ</b>、記憶(きおく)に残(のこ)らない。
If you don't look it up yourself, it won't stick.
</pre>

The content is nearly the same; the effect is not. `てこそ` is encouraging and sets a
goal, `なければ～ない` is a warning. When you want the warning, do not try to force it
through `てこそ` - ❌<code>調(しら)べなくてこそ</code> does not exist.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 練習(れんしゅう)する<b>こそ</b>、上達(じょうたつ)する。 → ✅ 練習(れんしゅう)し<b>てこそ</b>、上達(じょうたつ)する。
  `こそ` in this meaning attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 努力(どりょく)し<b>てこそ</b>、成功(せいこう)できない。 → ✅ 努力(どりょく)し<b>なければ</b>、成功(せいこう)できない。
  The clause after `てこそ` cannot be negative. Flip the whole sentence into
  <code>なければ～ない</code> instead.

- ❌ 健康(けんこう)<b>でこそ</b>、何(なん)でもできる。 → ✅ 健康(けんこう)<b>であってこそ</b>、何(なん)でもできる。
  A noun or な-adjective needs the full <code>である</code> before `こそ`.

- ❌ 寝坊(ねぼう)し<b>てこそ</b>、遅刻(ちこく)した。 → ✅ 寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>から</b>、遅刻(ちこく)した。
  `てこそ` announces that something gains value. A plain unwanted consequence has no
  business in that slot.

## Practice

**1.** 約束(やくそく)は、守(まも)っ____信頼(しんらい)が生(う)まれる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

守(まも)っ<b>てこそ</b>信頼(しんらい)が生(う)まれる。

Keeping the promise is the condition on which trust becomes possible, and the payoff
is a positive one - the exact shape `てこそ` is for. Note the て-form: 守(まも)る → 守(まも)って.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 海外(かいがい)で暮(く)らし（てこそ・てはじめて）、日本(にほん)の良(よ)さに気(き)づきました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

海外(かいがい)で暮(く)らし<b>てはじめて</b>、日本(にほん)の良(よ)さに気(き)づきました。

This is a past realisation the speaker had personally, with 気(き)づく as the verb -
<code>てはじめて</code>'s home ground. `てこそ` would turn a memory into a piece of
advice for everyone, which the ました ending contradicts.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "A dictionary is only worth having if you actually use it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

辞書(じしょ)は実際(じっさい)に使(つか)っ<b>てこそ</b>価値(かち)がある。

<code>価値(かち)がある</code> is one of the standard endings for `てこそ`, and the
English "only… if" is precisely the bar-setting the pattern draws. A conditional with
`たら` would state a fact; `てこそ` states a principle.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 毎日(まいにち)水(みず)をやってこそ、花(はな)は枯(か)れてしまう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 毎日(まいにち)水(みず)をやってこそ、花(はな)は元気(げんき)に育(そだ)つ。
（or 毎日(まいにち)水(みず)をやらなければ、花(はな)は枯(か)れてしまう。）

The original pairs `てこそ` with a bad outcome, which the pattern will not carry.
Either keep `てこそ` and give it a positive result, or keep the withering and switch
to <code>なければ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)い<b>てこそ</b>、いい指導者(しどうしゃ)だ。
(b) 人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)く<b>から</b>、いい指導者(しどうしゃ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) defines the standard. It says that listening is the thing that qualifies someone
as a good leader at all, and by implication that a leader who does not listen is not
one. It is a claim about what the word ought to mean.

(b) simply explains an assessment already made of a particular person: he listens,
and that is why you rate him. No standard is being set, and nobody else is being
judged by it.

</details>

# ～だけに

**Meaning** · being what it is, all the more so - the circumstance is X, and that makes the outcome stronger than it would otherwise have been

`だけに` weighs. It names a circumstance and then reports a result whose **size is
proportionate to it**: the more you had riding on something, the harder the
disappointment; the closer to the station, the higher the rent. The speaker is not
excusing anything and not arguing anything - they are commenting, and the comment is
that the outcome fits the circumstance and is therefore no smaller than you would
expect. Unlike most patterns in this group, `だけに` is perfectly happy for the
outcome to be bad.

## Formation

The clause in front is in noun-modifying shape; a plain noun attaches directly.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + だけに | 期待(きたい)していた → 期待(きたい)していただけに |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + だけに | 慣(な)れていない → 慣(な)れていないだけに |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけに | 若(わか)い → 若(わか)いだけに |
| な-adjective | ～な + だけに | 真剣(しんけん)だ → 真剣(しんけん)なだけに |
| Noun | Noun + だけに | プロ → プロだけに |
| Noun (formal) | ～である + だけに | 責任者(せきにんしゃ) → 責任者(せきにんしゃ)であるだけに |
| Referring back | それ + だけに | - → それだけに、残念(ざんねん)だ |
| Reinforcing adverbs | …だけに、なおさら／余計(よけい)に… | なおさらつらい |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>それだけに</code> works as a sentence opener, picking up the whole
previous sentence as the circumstance: 「準備(じゅんび)に一年(いちねん)かけた。それだけに、中止(ちゅうし)は痛(いた)かった。」
This is very common in writing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
楽(たの)しみにしていた<b>だけに</b>、中止(ちゅうし)はショックだった。
I'd been looking forward to it, so the cancellation came as a real blow.
</pre>

<pre>
プロの選手(せんしゅ)<b>だけに</b>、動(うご)きがまるで違(ちが)う。
He's a professional, and it shows the moment he moves.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)に近(ちか)い<b>だけに</b>、この辺(へん)の家賃(やちん)は高(たか)い。
Being this close to the station, rents around here are steep.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)に半年(はんとし)かけた<b>だけに</b>、失敗(しっぱい)したときの落(お)ち込(こ)み方(かた)はひどかった。
We'd spent six months preparing, so the slump after it fell through was awful.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は責任感(せきにんかん)が強(つよ)い<b>だけに</b>、一人(ひとり)で抱(かか)え込(こ)んでしまうことがある。
She has a strong sense of duty, and for that very reason she sometimes shoulders it all alone.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の優勝(ゆうしょう)チーム<b>だけに</b>、一回戦(いっかいせん)での敗退(はいたい)は大(おお)きく報(ほう)じられた。
As last year's champions, their first-round exit was widely reported.
</pre>

<pre>
地元(じもと)で長(なが)く愛(あい)されてきた店(みせ)<b>だけに</b>、閉店(へいてん)を惜(お)しむ声(こえ)が絶(た)えない。
It's a shop the neighbourhood has loved for years, so there's no end to the people sorry to see it go.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)に関(かか)わる問題(もんだい)<b>だけに</b>、報告(ほうこく)が遅(おく)れたことが余計(よけい)に問題視(もんだいし)されている。
Because it is a safety matter, the delay in reporting it is being treated as all the more serious.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`だけに` leans written and analytical. It is a staple of news copy, reviews,
commentary and the more considered kind of speech; in relaxed conversation people
would more often say <code>から余計(よけい)に</code> or
<code>ので、なおさら</code>. Nothing about it is stiff, but it does sound like
someone stepping back to assess.

Its distinctive contribution is **degree**, not causation. A sentence with `だけに`
implies a comparison you never have to state: the disappointment is larger than an
ordinary disappointment, the rent higher than ordinary rent, the criticism sharper
than it would have been about a smaller matter. This is why なおさら and
<code>余計(よけい)に</code> sit so naturally in the second clause - they say out
loud what `だけに` implies.

Because it is a measurement rather than a compliment, `だけに` is neutral about
whether the result is welcome. That is its real advantage over `だけあって`, which
can only praise. Half of the most natural `だけに` sentences end in something
regrettable.

One consequence worth noticing: since `だけに` reports rather than proposes, the
second clause is a statement about how things are or how they turned out. If you
find yourself wanting to put an instruction or an invitation after it, you are
reaching for a different pattern - `から` or `ので` will do that job cleanly.

## Contrast

### ～だけに vs ～だけあって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけあって` **praises**: the outcome does the circumstance
credit. `だけに` **measures**: the outcome is proportionate, for better or worse.

</div>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)習(なら)っていた<b>だけあって</b>、発音(はつおん)がきれいだ。
Three years of lessons, and you can hear it in her pronunciation.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)習(なら)っていた<b>だけに</b>、やめる決心(けっしん)がつかなかった。
She'd been at it for three years, which made the decision to quit that much harder.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence goes flat while the second becomes strange, because
failing to quit is not a credit to anybody. When the second clause is a misfortune, a
loss or a complication, `だけに` is your only option.

### ～だけに vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` and `ので` say *this caused that*. `だけに` says *this made
that more so than usual*.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた<b>ので</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
It was raining, so the match was called off.
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)がかかった試合(しあい)だった<b>だけに</b>、中止(ちゅうし)の判断(はんだん)には批判(ひはん)も多(おお)い。
The title was on the line, so the decision to call it off has drawn a lot of criticism.
</pre>

Rain is a plain cause of cancellation, and `だけに` there would be odd - there is no
scale to speak of. In the second sentence the stakes explain not that criticism
happened but that there is *so much* of it. Ask yourself whether your sentence has a
dial in it; if it does not, use `ので`.

### ～だけに vs ～からこそ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `からこそ` is the speaker **arguing** for a cause the listener may
not accept. `だけに` is the speaker **observing** a result that fits.

</div>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)だ<b>からこそ</b>、思(おも)い切(き)った提案(ていあん)ができるのだ。
It's precisely because he's new that he can make a bold proposal.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)<b>だけに</b>、会議(かいぎ)での発言(はつげん)には緊張(きんちょう)が見(み)えた。
Being new, he was visibly nervous speaking up in the meeting.
</pre>

The `からこそ` sentence pushes back against the assumption that a newcomer has less to
offer; it expects agreement. The `だけに` sentence expects nothing - it simply notes
that the nerves were what you would predict, and if anything a bit more so.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 有名(ゆうめい)<b>だだけに</b> → ✅ 有名(ゆうめい)<b>なだけに</b>
  な-adjectives take `な` before <code>だけ</code>, which is a noun. A plain noun,
  though, attaches bare: <code>プロだけに</code>.

- ❌ 一度(いちど)会(あ)った<b>だけに</b>、よく知(し)りません。 → ✅ 一度(いちど)会(あ)った<b>だけで</b>、よく知(し)りません。
  This `だけに` has nothing to do with `だけ` meaning "only". If your English is
  "just… so I don't…", you want <code>だけで</code>.

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は雨(あめ)<b>だけに</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)って出(で)かけた。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は雨(あめ)<b>なので</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)って出(で)かけた。
  A routine consequence has no degree to it. `だけに` needs an outcome that is
  *heightened* by the circumstance.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は三年(さんねん)勉強(べんきょう)した<b>だけに</b>、日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)です。 → ✅ 三年(さんねん)勉強(べんきょう)した<b>ので</b>、少(すこ)しは話(はな)せます。
  Applied to your own accomplishments, the "no wonder it's good" reading turns into
  self-congratulation. Japanese leaves that verdict to other people.

# ～だけあって

**Meaning** · as you would expect from something like that - the result does the credential credit

`だけあって` is a compliment with its evidence attached. It names a credential - a
reputation, a price, a stretch of training, a place - and then reports something that
**lives up to it**. The speaker has seen the result, thought "well, of course", and
is saying so. That admiring note is not optional decoration: it is the pattern's
whole content, and it is what separates `だけあって` from the neutral `だけに`.

## Formation

The clause in front is in noun-modifying shape. A plain noun attaches with nothing
in between.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + だけあって | 習(なら)っていた → 習(なら)っていただけあって |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけあって | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いだけあって |
| な-adjective | ～な + だけあって | 有名(ゆうめい)だ → 有名(ゆうめい)なだけあって |
| Noun | Noun + だけあって | 元(もと)プロ → 元(もと)プロだけあって |
| Noun (formal) | ～である + だけあって | 専門家(せんもんか) → 専門家(せんもんか)であるだけあって |
| Longer variant | ～だけのことはあって | 練習(れんしゅう)した → 練習(れんしゅう)しただけのことはあって |
| Usual opener | さすが（に）+ … + だけあって | さすが京都(きょうと)だけあって |

<div class="note">

**Note** - さすが and `だけあって` are close companions. さすが carries the admiration
on its own, and `だけあって` supplies the grounds for it, so the two together are a
set frame rather than a redundancy.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
さすが京都(きょうと)<b>だけあって</b>、古(ふる)い町並(まちな)みが美(うつく)しい。
It's Kyoto, all right - the old streets are lovely.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)している<b>だけあって</b>、字(じ)がとてもきれいだ。
She practises every day, and her handwriting shows it.
</pre>

<pre>
この旅館(りょかん)は値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い<b>だけあって</b>、サービスが行(い)き届(とど)いている。
The inn is expensive, and the service is attentive to match.
</pre>

<pre>
元(もと)アナウンサー<b>だけあって</b>、話(はな)し方(かた)に無駄(むだ)がない。
You can tell she used to be a broadcaster - not a wasted word anywhere.
</pre>

<pre>
この宿(やど)は山(やま)の上(うえ)に建(た)っている<b>だけあって</b>、夏(なつ)でも夜(よる)は涼(すず)しい。
Perched up the mountain as it is, the lodge is cool at night even in summer.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)かけて作(つく)った<b>だけあって</b>、この映画(えいが)は細部(さいぶ)まで凝(こ)っている。
Three years in the making, and the film is meticulous down to the smallest detail.
</pre>

<pre>
通訳(つうやく)として長(なが)く働(はたら)いてきた<b>だけあって</b>、彼(かれ)は言葉(ことば)の選(えら)び方(かた)がとても慎重(しんちょう)だ。
Having worked years as an interpreter, he is extremely careful about the words he picks.
</pre>

<pre>
創業(そうぎょう)百年(ひゃくねん)の和菓子屋(わがしや)<b>だけあって</b>、包(つつ)み紙(がみ)一枚(いちまい)にも品(ひん)がある。
A hundred-year-old confectioner - even the wrapping paper has a certain class to it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`だけあって` is register-neutral in form: it appears in conversation, in reviews and
in feature writing without changing shape. What it is not neutral about is
**evaluation**. The second clause has to be something the speaker rates - skilful,
beautiful, thorough, well made, unusually good of its kind. Feed it a plain
misfortune and the sentence misfires, because the admiring "of course" has nothing to
land on.

It follows that `だけあって` is awkward about yourself. Saying
「三年(さんねん)習(なら)っただけあって上手(じょうず)です」 is your own credential
plus your own praise, and it reads as boasting. Japanese leaves this verdict to other
people; about yourself, `ので` and a modest claim are the safe route. This is a strong
tendency rather than an absolute - people do use it self-mockingly - but a learner is
better off treating it as a rule.

The credential also has to be one the listener can recognise. `だけあって` works by
inviting agreement: *you know what Kyoto is like, so this will not surprise you*. If
the premise means nothing to the listener, the sentence has no leverage, and plain
`ので` is clearer.

Where the outcome matches the premise but is regrettable - cheap and therefore
flimsy, inexperienced and therefore slow - Japanese normally reaches for `だけに`
instead, which measures without praising.

## Contrast

### ～だけあって vs ～だけに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけあって` says the result is **worthy** of the premise.
`だけに` says the result is **proportionate** to it, welcome or not.

</div>

<pre>
人気(にんき)の店(みせ)<b>だけあって</b>、味(あじ)は評判(ひょうばん)どおりだった。
It's a popular place, and the food was every bit as good as they say.
</pre>

<pre>
人気(にんき)の店(みせ)<b>だけに</b>、一時間(いちじかん)並(なら)ばされた。
It's a popular place, so we were left queueing for an hour.
</pre>

Queueing is not a credit to the restaurant, so `だけあって` cannot host it. Whenever
you can put "and rightly so" or "and it shows" into the English, `だけあって` fits;
whenever the English is "and that's why it was such a nuisance", it does not.

### ～だけあって vs ～だけのことはある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same admiration, different position. `だけあって` sits **inside**
the sentence and the praise arrives after it; <code>だけのことはある</code> **ends**
the sentence and is itself the praise.

</div>

<pre>
毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っている<b>だけあって</b>、階段(かいだん)でも息(いき)が切(き)れない。
He runs every morning, and it shows - stairs don't leave him puffing.
</pre>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)でも平気(へいき)だ。毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っている<b>だけのことはある</b>。
Stairs don't bother him at all. That morning running really does pay off.
</pre>

Notice the ordering. `だけあって` puts the credential first and the evidence second;
<code>だけのことはある</code> puts the evidence first and delivers the verdict at the
end, which is why it so often follows a sentence rather than starting one.

### ～だけあって vs さすが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - さすが is the admiration. `だけあって` is the reason for it. Each
can stand alone, and together they are a set phrase.

</div>

<pre>
さすが専門家(せんもんか)ですね。説明(せつめい)がとても分(わ)かりやすい。
You really are the expert - that explanation was so clear.
</pre>

<pre>
さすが専門家(せんもんか)<b>だけあって</b>、説明(せつめい)がとても分(わ)かりやすい。
As you'd expect from a specialist, the explanation was beautifully clear.
</pre>

さすが alone is a reaction and can be a whole utterance. `だけあって` is a connective
and cannot: it always needs the result clause it is introducing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 有名(ゆうめい)<b>だだけあって</b> → ✅ 有名(ゆうめい)<b>なだけあって</b>
  <code>だけ</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective in front of it takes `な`. Plain
  nouns attach bare: <code>元(もと)プロだけあって</code>.

- ❌ 寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>だけあって</b>、遅刻(ちこく)した。 → ✅ 寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>ので</b>、遅刻(ちこく)した。
  `だけあって` needs a result worth admiring. Being late is a plain consequence and
  has nothing for the "of course" to attach to.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は留学(りゅうがく)していた<b>だけあって</b>、英語(えいご)が得意(とくい)です。 → ✅ 留学(りゅうがく)していた<b>ので</b>、英語(えいご)には少(すこ)し自信(じしん)があります。
  You cannot compliment yourself with `だけあって`. Cite the reason plainly and let
  the assessment go unsaid.

- ❌ さすが、毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)している<b>だけあって</b>。 → ✅ さすが、毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)している<b>だけのことはある</b>。
  `だけあって` cannot end a sentence. The sentence-final form of the same idea is
  <code>だけ（のことは）ある</code>.

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一年(いちねん)通(かよ)った<b>だけあって</b>、会話(かいわ)に困(こま)らなくなった。
(b) 一年(いちねん)通(かよ)った<b>だけに</b>、やめるのが惜(お)しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

fluency is the result that justifies it - the speaker is nodding approvingly at

(b) is a weighing-up with no praise in it. The year explains why quitting feels like
a waste, and "reluctance to quit" is not an achievement that could do the year credit.
Substituting `だけあって` into (b) would leave the listener looking for the compliment
that never comes.

</details>

# ～だけのことはある

**Meaning** · it lives up to it; no wonder - the money, the effort or the reputation behind this thing has been repaid

<code>だけのことはある</code> ends a sentence with a verdict. You have seen the
result - the knife cuts, the queue was worth it, the training shows - and you sign
off on it: *there was something in all that after all*. The premise is always an
investment or a reputation, and the pattern's job is to declare it honoured. It is a
compliment, and it is normally said out loud to or about someone else.

## Formation

The clause in front is in noun-modifying shape. The whole thing sits at the end of
the sentence, so the tense and politeness ride on `ある`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + だけのことはある | 練習(れんしゅう)した → 練習(れんしゅう)しただけのことはある |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけのことはある | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いだけのことはある |
| な-adjective | ～な + だけのことはある | 有名(ゆうめい)だ → 有名(ゆうめい)なだけのことはある |
| Noun | Noun + だけのことはある | プロ → プロだけのことはある |
| Short forms | ～だけある／～だけはある | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)いだけある |
| Past | ～だけのことはあった | 待(ま)った → 待(ま)っただけのことはあった |
| Polite | ～だけのことはあります | 走(はし)っている → 走(はし)っているだけのことはあります |
| Continuing a sentence | ～だけのことはあって、… | 留学(りゅうがく)した → 留学(りゅうがく)しただけのことはあって |
| Usual opener | さすが（に）… | さすが、毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っているだけのことはある |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `は` is fixed. ❌<code>だけのことがある</code> is a different
sentence altogether ("there are things that…"), and it is a common slip.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
さすが、毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っている<b>だけのことはある</b>。
No wonder - he really does run every day.
</pre>

<pre>
この包丁(ほうちょう)は高(たか)かったが、高(たか)い<b>だけのことはある</b>。
This knife was expensive, but it's worth every yen.
</pre>

<pre>
三時間(さんじかん)並(なら)んだが、並(なら)んだ<b>だけのことはあった</b>。
We queued for three hours, and it was worth the wait.
</pre>

<pre>
一年間(いちねんかん)留学(りゅうがく)した<b>だけのことはあって</b>、発音(はつおん)がすっかり変(か)わった。
A year abroad has clearly paid off - his pronunciation is completely different.
</pre>

<pre>
老舗(しにせ)の和菓子(わがし)は、値段(ねだん)が張(は)る<b>だけのことはある</b>。
The sweets from that old-established shop justify their price.
</pre>

<pre>
毎晩(まいばん)遅(おそ)くまで資料(しりょう)を読(よ)み込(こ)んだ<b>だけのことはあり</b>、質問(しつもん)にはすべて答(こた)えられた。
All those late nights with the documents paid off: he fielded every question.
</pre>

<pre>
苦労(くろう)して山頂(さんちょう)まで登(のぼ)った<b>だけのことはある</b>眺(なが)めだった。
It was a view that repaid the hard climb.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)修業(しゅぎょう)した<b>だけのことはある</b>と、一口(ひとくち)食(た)べて誰(だれ)もが納得(なっとく)した。
One bite and everybody agreed the ten years of training had been worth it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a spoken pattern first and a written one second. It belongs to the moment
after you have tasted, watched or tried something, and it is the standard way to hand
out credit in Japanese without gushing. The polite
<code>だけのことはあります</code> is fine in any setting; the clipped
<code>だけある</code> is what friends actually say.

Three shapes worth keeping apart:

- <code>だけのことはある</code> - the full form, and the most emphatic.
- <code>だけある</code> - the same thing, shorter and more casual.
- <code>だけはある</code> - concessive. The `は` singles the point out and implies a
  reservation elsewhere: 「値段(ねだん)は高(たか)いが、うまいだけはある」 -
  *say what you like about the price, it does taste good*. This is a real difference
  in feel, not just a contraction, and it is worth hearing.

さすが at the head of the sentence is close to obligatory in speech. Two related
formulas do the same work slightly differently:
<code>さすが～だけあって</code> puts the praise mid-sentence, while
<code>さすが～だけのことはある</code> saves it for the end.

Like `だけあって`, this is other-directed. Applied to your own effort it sounds
self-satisfied; the natural way to say your own work paid off is
<code>甲斐(かい)があった</code>. Said about someone else's investment it is warm.
Said flatly about a person whose reputation you doubt, the same words can be heard as
sarcasm - tone carries most of that, so listen for it before you produce it.

## Contrast

### ～だけのことはある vs ～だけあって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけのことはある</code> **closes** a sentence with the
verdict. `だけあって` **opens** the second half of one and lets the evidence follow.

</div>

<pre>
海外(かいがい)で働(はたら)いていた<b>だけあって</b>、交渉(こうしょう)が落(お)ち着(つ)いている。
Having worked abroad, she negotiates with real composure.
</pre>

<pre>
交渉(こうしょう)のしかたが落(お)ち着(つ)いている。海外(かいがい)で働(はたら)いていた<b>だけのことはある</b>。
The way she negotiates is so composed. Working abroad clearly left its mark.
</pre>

They carry the same admiration in a different order, and which you pick depends on
where the evidence already is. If the listener has just seen the result, close with
<code>だけのことはある</code>; if you are about to describe it, open with `だけあって`.

### ～だけのことはある vs ～わけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけだ` works out a **logical consequence**;
<code>だけのことはある</code> passes a **judgement of worth**.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)が開(あ)いていたのか。どうりで部屋(へや)が冷(ひ)えている<b>わけだ</b>。
The window was open? No wonder the room's gone cold.
</pre>

<pre>
材料(ざいりょう)から選(えら)んでいるそうだ。この味(あじ)になる<b>だけのことはある</b>。
Apparently they choose the ingredients themselves. That would explain a taste like this.
</pre>

Both English translations can be "no wonder", which is where the confusion starts.
The test is whether anything is being praised. A cold room is not an achievement, so
only `わけだ` will do; a flavour that repays careful sourcing is, so
<code>だけのことはある</code> becomes available - though `わけだ` would still be
possible, just colder.

### ～だけのことはある vs ～かいがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>甲斐(かい)がある</code> is the **doer's** relief that
the effort was repaid. <code>だけのことはある</code> is an **onlooker's** verdict that
the thing measures up.

</div>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)水(みず)をやった<b>かいがあって</b>、やっと花(はな)が咲(さ)いた。
All that daily watering paid off - it's finally flowered.
</pre>

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)水(みず)をやっていた<b>だけのことはある</b>。見事(みごと)な花(はな)だ。
It shows that she watered it every day. What a magnificent bloom.
</pre>

The first is naturally said by the gardener, the second by the visitor. That is why
<code>かいがある</code> is the safe form for talking about your own effort, where
<code>だけのことはある</code> would sound like patting yourself on the back.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 高(たか)い<b>だけのことがある</b>。 → ✅ 高(たか)い<b>だけのことはある</b>。
  The particle is `は`, and it is fixed. With `が` the phrase reverts to its literal
  meaning and the sentence stops making sense.

- ❌ 有名(ゆうめい)<b>だだけのことはある</b> → ✅ 有名(ゆうめい)<b>なだけのことはある</b>
  <code>だけ</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective takes `な` in front of it. A plain
  noun attaches bare: <code>プロだけのことはある</code>.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)した<b>だけのことはあります</b>。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)した<b>甲斐(かい)がありました</b>。
  Turned on yourself the verdict becomes a boast. <code>甲斐(かい)がある</code>
  reports the same satisfaction from the inside.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>だけのことはある</b>。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>わけだ</b>。
  The premise has to be an investment or a reputation. A weather event is nobody's
  effort, so there is nothing for the verdict to endorse.

## Practice

**1.** さすが、三年(さんねん)かけて準備(じゅんび)した____。見事(みごと)な舞台(ぶたい)でした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

準備(じゅんび)した<b>だけのことはあります</b>

The evidence - a magnificent performance - has already been given, so the sentence
delivers the verdict at the end. さすが at the head is the standard companion, and
the polite ending matches でした in the next sentence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 説明書(せつめいしょ)をなくしてしまった。使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)からない（わけだ・だけのことはある）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)からない<b>わけだ</b>。

Nothing is being praised - the speaker is just working out why they are stuck. That
is `わけだ`. <code>だけのことはある</code> needs an investment worth endorsing, and a
lost manual is not one.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It really is worth getting up early for - the morning market was wonderful."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The early start is the investment and the market is the payoff, so the verdict goes
first and the evidence follows, or the two can be reversed. The past

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この辞書(じしょ)は便利(べんり)だだけのことはあって、みんなが使(つか)っている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この辞書(じしょ)は便利(べんり)<b>なだけのことはあって</b>、みんなが使(つか)っている。

A な-adjective links to <code>だけ</code> with `な`, never with `だ`. The continuative

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この店(みせ)は評判(ひょうばん)どおり、うまい<b>だけのことはある</b>。
(b) この店(みせ)は値段(ねだん)は高(たか)いが、うまい<b>だけはある</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) concedes. The `は` on <code>だけ</code> singles the taste out as the one thing
that holds up, and the clause in front of it says what does not. You would say (b)
about a place you would recommend with a warning attached - the grumble is part of the
compliment.

</details>

# ～のも当然だ

**Meaning** · it's only natural that…; no wonder - given what has just been established, this outcome could not really have gone any other way

The kanji word here is 当然(とうぜん), "natural, a matter of course". Where `わけだ` at N3 worked a conclusion out,
<code>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> **passes sentence on** it: the speaker has the
premise in hand and rules that the result was inevitable. The `の` packages a whole
clause into a noun, so the pattern can take a long, argued lead-up - which is exactly
what it usually has. Socially it is a judgement, and it cuts both ways: it can be
sympathy ("of course you're upset") or condemnation ("well, what did they expect?").

## Formation

`の` nominalises the clause; <code>当然(とうぜん)</code> is a な-adjective-shaped
noun, so `だ` / `です` follows it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のも当然(とうぜん)だ | 怒(おこ)る → 怒(おこ)るのも当然(とうぜん)だ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のも当然(とうぜん)だ | 落(お)ちた → 落(お)ちたのも当然(とうぜん)だ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + のも当然(とうぜん)だ | 信(しん)じない → 信(しん)じないのも当然(とうぜん)だ |
| い-adjective | plain form + のも当然(とうぜん)だ | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いのも当然(とうぜん)だ |
| な-adjective | ～な + のも当然(とうぜん)だ | 不安(ふあん)だ → 不安(ふあん)なのも当然(とうぜん)だ |
| Noun | ～な + のも当然(とうぜん)だ | 赤字(あかじ) → 赤字(あかじ)なのも当然(とうぜん)だ |
| Polite | …のも当然(とうぜん)です | 驚(おどろ)く → 驚(おどろ)くのも当然(とうぜん)です |
| With は | …のは当然(とうぜん)だ | 反対(はんたい)する → 反対(はんたい)するのは当然(とうぜん)だ |
| Modifying a noun | 当然(とうぜん)の + Noun | 結果(けっか) → 当然(とうぜん)の結果(けっか) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the premise normally arrives in the same sentence, in a
<code>～のだから</code> or <code>～んだから</code> clause. Without it the verdict has
nothing to rest on and sounds abrupt.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あんなに練習(れんしゅう)すれば、勝(か)てる<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
Practise that much and of course you win.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)けっ放(ぱな)しだったのだから、部屋(へや)が寒(さむ)い<b>のも当然(とうぜん)です</b>。
The window was left wide open, so naturally the room is cold.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)もなく値上(ねあ)げしたのだから、お客(きゃく)さんが怒(おこ)る<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
They raised prices without a word of explanation, so of course the customers are angry.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めての一人暮(ひとりぐ)らしなのだから、不安(ふあん)な<b>のも当然(とうぜん)でしょう</b>。
It's your first time living alone, so it's only natural to feel uneasy.
</pre>

<pre>
寝(ね)る時間(じかん)を削(けず)って働(はたら)いていたのだから、体(からだ)を壊(こわ)した<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
He was working on almost no sleep, so it is hardly surprising his health gave out.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)も動(うご)かしていなかったのだから、エンジンがかからない<b>のも当然(とうぜん)です</b>。
It hadn't been started in three years, so of course the engine won't turn over.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)確認(かくにん)を省(はぶ)いていたというのだから、行政(ぎょうせい)から指導(しどう)が入(はい)る<b>のも当然(とうぜん)</b>だろう。
They were skipping the safety checks, so a warning from the authorities was only to be expected.
</pre>

<pre>
地元(じもと)の人(ひと)に一言(ひとこと)の相談(そうだん)もなく計画(けいかく)を進(すす)めたのだから、反対(はんたい)の声(こえ)が上(あ)がる<b>のも当然(とうぜん)です</b>。
They pushed the plan through without consulting a single resident, so it is no surprise that objections are being raised.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>当然(とうぜん)</code> is an everyday word and the pattern is neither formal
nor casual - it turns up in conversation, in newspaper commentary and in the middle
of an argument alike. What varies is how hard it lands. The verdict is absolute:
`当然` says the outcome *had* to follow, and a listener who disagrees with the premise
will hear it as high-handed.

That gives the pattern two very different social lives. Aimed at a person's feelings
it is kind - <code>不安(ふあん)になるのも当然(とうぜん)ですよ</code> tells someone
their reaction is normal, and the ですよ softens it further. Aimed at an organisation
or at somebody's conduct it is severe, and it is a favourite of editorials for exactly
that reason.

The bare word `当然` used as a reply ("obviously") is blunt, and used about a favour
someone has done you it is worse - it implies you were owed. Keep
<code>当然(とうぜん)です</code> for endorsing an outcome, not for accepting a
kindness.

<div class="note">

**Note** - `も` and `は` both appear here. <code>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> is
slightly the softer, adding the outcome to a picture the listener can already see;
<code>のは当然(とうぜん)だ</code> asserts it more squarely, and is what you use when
you are arguing the point rather than observing it. The difference is small, and both
are common.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～のも当然(とうぜん)だ vs ～のももっともだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>当然(とうぜん)</code> = it could not have been otherwise.
<code>もっとも</code> = you can hardly blame them. The first ranges over any outcome;
the second judges a **person's reaction** and takes their side.

</div>

<pre>
一晩中(ひとばんじゅう)停電(ていでん)していたのだから、冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)の中身(なかみ)がだめになった<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
The power was out all night, so of course everything in the fridge was ruined.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も約束(やくそく)を破(やぶ)られたのだから、彼(かれ)が腹(はら)を立(た)てる<b>のももっともだ</b>。
He's had promises broken again and again, so you can hardly blame him for being angry.
</pre>

A ruined fridge has no feelings and no case to make, so `もっともだ` is not available
there. Where the subject *is* a human reaction, both work, and the choice is one of
stance: `当然だ` rules, `もっともだ` sympathises.

### ～のも当然(とうぜん)だ vs ～て当然(とうぜん)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same verdict on two different chassis.
<code>て当然(とうぜん)だ</code> hangs straight off the predicate and lands
compactly; <code>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> nominalises first and can carry a
longer, heavier clause.

</div>

<pre>
これだけ準備(じゅんび)したのだから、合格(ごうかく)し<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
After preparation like that, passing is the least you'd expect.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の前(まえ)の晩(ばん)に徹夜(てつや)でゲームをしていたのだから、落(お)ちた<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
He was up all night gaming before the exam, so it's no wonder he failed.
</pre>

The second clause in the `のも` version has its own past tense and its own subject,
which the て-form version would strain to carry. Where the clause is short, either
works and the difference is one of weight.

### ～のも当然(とうぜん)だ vs ～わけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけだ` reports that the pieces **add up**.
<code>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> adds that they could not have added up any other
way.

</div>

<pre>
時計(とけい)が五分(ごふん)進(すす)んでいたのか。どうりで早(はや)く着(つ)いた<b>わけだ</b>。
The clock was five minutes fast? That explains why I got here early.
</pre>

<pre>
整備(せいび)を一度(いちど)もしていなかったのだから、途中(とちゅう)で止(と)まった<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
It had never once been serviced, so of course it broke down on the way.
</pre>

`わけだ` can be pure arithmetic and carries no attitude. `のも当然だ` always carries
one: there is a judgement in it that somebody should have seen this coming.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 怒(おこ)る当然(とうぜん)だ。 → ✅ 怒(おこ)る<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
  <code>当然(とうぜん)</code> is a noun-like word, so a verb clause has to be
  nominalised with `の` before it can be the subject.

- ❌ 不安(ふあん)<b>だのも当然(とうぜん)だ</b> → ✅ 不安(ふあん)<b>なのも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>
  な-adjectives and nouns take `な` before `の`. <code>赤字(あかじ)なのも当然(とうぜん)だ</code>
  works the same way.

- ❌ 手伝(てつだ)ってくれるのは当然(とうぜん)です。（thanking someone） → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)ってくださって助(たす)かりました。
  Applied to a favour, the verdict says you were entitled to it. Use it on outcomes,
  not on other people's kindness.

- ❌ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るのも当然(とうぜん)だ。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうだ。
  The pattern rules on something already the case or already decided. A forecast has
  nothing established behind it, so there is no verdict to give.

The <code>のだから</code> clause supplies the premise, and the sentence closes by

critical, not sympathetic - that is normal for this pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 初(はじ)めての発表(はっぴょう)だから、緊張(きんちょう)だのも当然(とうぜん)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 初(はじ)めての発表(はっぴょう)だから、緊張(きんちょう)する<b>のも当然(とうぜん)です</b>。

<code>緊張(きんちょう)</code> is a noun that needs する to become a clause; as a
bare noun it would have to be <code>緊張(きんちょう)なのも</code>, which is not what
the sentence means. Either way, `だ` cannot stand before `の`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 社員(しゃいん)が辞(や)めていく<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
(b) 社員(しゃいん)が辞(や)めていく<b>のももっともだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a verdict on the company. Given whatever has just been described, people
leaving was the only possible result, and the sentence carries an implied "they
brought it on themselves". It is cool and a little severe.

(b) sides with the employees. It treats leaving as a reasonable response to their
situation and says the speaker would have done the same. Where (a) judges the
organisation, (b) defends the people - which is why (b) so often continues with
「が、…」 and a qualification.

</details>

# ～のももっともだ

**Meaning** · you can hardly blame them; that reaction is perfectly fair - the speaker endorses somebody's feeling or response as justified

もっとも here is an adjective meaning "reasonable, well-founded". Where
<code>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> rules an outcome inevitable,
<code>のももっともだ</code> **takes somebody's side**: it looks at an angry customer,
a doubting colleague, a parent who refuses, and says that in their position anyone
would feel the same. It is a concession, and it is almost always about a human
reaction rather than about the way the world works.

## Formation

`の` nominalises the clause; もっとも is a な-adjective, so `だ` / `です` follows and
`な` appears before a noun. It is normally written in kana.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のももっともだ | 怒(おこ)る → 怒(おこ)るのももっともだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のももっともだ | 断(ことわ)った → 断(ことわ)ったのももっともだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + のももっともだ | 信(しん)じない → 信(しん)じないのももっともだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + のももっともだ | 悔(くや)しい → 悔(くや)しいのももっともだ |
| な-adjective | ～な + のももっともだ | 不満(ふまん)だ → 不満(ふまん)なのももっともだ |
| Polite | …のももっともです | 驚(おどろ)く → 驚(おどろ)くのももっともです |
| With は | …のはもっともだ | 怒(おこ)る → 怒(おこ)るのはもっともだ |
| Modifying a noun | もっともな + Noun | 意見(いけん) → もっともな意見(いけん) |
| Polite formula | ごもっともです | - → おっしゃることはごもっともです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - kana もっとも hides three different words. This one is the adjective
（尤(もっと)もだ）. There is also the adverb 最(もっと)も, "most", and a
conjunction もっとも meaning "though, having said that". Here the giveaways are the
`の` in front and the `だ` / `です` behind.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あんな言(い)い方(かた)をされたら、怒(おこ)る<b>のももっともだ</b>。
If somebody spoke to you like that, you'd be right to be angry.
</pre>

<pre>
三回(さんかい)も約束(やくそく)を破(やぶ)られたのだから、彼女(かのじょ)が信(しん)じない<b>のももっともです</b>。
She's had promises broken three times over, so it's understandable that she doesn't believe it.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が何年(なんねん)も上(あ)がらないのだから、不満(ふまん)が出(で)る<b>のももっともだ</b>。
Wages haven't moved in years, so the discontent is fair enough.
</pre>

<pre>
一年(いちねん)かけた企画(きかく)が急(きゅう)に中止(ちゅうし)になったのだから、悔(くや)しい<b>のももっともだろう</b>。
A year's work cancelled overnight - no wonder he's gutted.
</pre>

<pre>
初対面(しょたいめん)でいきなり年収(ねんしゅう)を聞(き)かれたら、驚(おどろ)く<b>のももっともだ</b>。
Asked your salary the moment you meet, of course you'd be taken aback.
</pre>

<pre>
説明会(せつめいかい)が平日(へいじつ)の昼間(ひるま)では、保護者(ほごしゃ)から苦情(くじょう)が出(で)る<b>のももっともです</b>。
If the briefing is held on a weekday afternoon, the parents' complaints are entirely reasonable.
</pre>

<pre>
長年(ながねん)通(かよ)っていた店(みせ)が突然(とつぜん)なくなったのだから、常連(じょうれん)が寂(さび)しがる<b>のももっともだ</b>。
The shop they'd gone to for years vanished overnight, so it's only natural the regulars miss it.
</pre>

<pre>
現場(げんば)の意見(いけん)を一度(いちど)も聞(き)かずに決(き)めたのだから、社員(しゃいん)が納得(なっとく)できない<b>のももっともでしょう</b>。
The decision was made without once asking the people doing the work, so you can hardly expect the staff to accept it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The move `もっともだ` makes is **conceding**. You are granting that somebody's
reaction is well-founded, and very often you are doing it on the way to a "but":
<code>…のももっともだが、…</code> is one of the most common shapes it takes. That is
worth knowing before you use it, because a bare
<code>おっしゃるとおりです</code> agrees outright, while `もっともだ` agrees with the
*reasonableness* of a position without necessarily agreeing with the position.

It is a measured, slightly formal word. Editorials, mediation, management writing and
careful speech are its home; among friends people say
<code>無理(むり)ないよ</code> or <code>そりゃそうだ</code> instead. Nothing about it
is stiff enough to avoid, but it does sound considered.

<code>ごもっともです</code> deserves separate mention. It is the standard polite
formula for receiving a complaint or a criticism - 「ご指摘(してき)はごもっともです」 -
and it buys you a moment without conceding the substance. It is heard constantly in
customer service and in meetings.

The one real limit is what `もっともだ` can be said about. Its subject has to be a
person's judgement, feeling or behaviour: getting angry, doubting, refusing,
complaining, being surprised, giving up. A purely physical outcome - the room being
cold, the engine not starting - has no reasoning to endorse, and there
<code>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> is the word.

## Contrast

### ～のももっともだ vs ～のも当然(とうぜん)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - もっとも judges a **reaction** and finds it fair.
<code>当然(とうぜん)</code> judges an **outcome** and finds it inevitable.

</div>

<pre>
エアコンが壊(こわ)れていたのだから、部屋(へや)が暑(あつ)い<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
The air conditioning was broken, so of course the room was hot.
</pre>

<pre>
エアコンが壊(こわ)れたままなのだから、客(きゃく)が文句(もんく)を言(い)う<b>のももっともだ</b>。
The air conditioning is still broken, so the guests are quite right to complain.
</pre>

Heat has no case to argue; a complaint does. Where the clause *is* a human reaction
both patterns are grammatical, and then the difference is one of stance: `当然だ`
hands down a ruling, `もっともだ` offers sympathy.

### ～のももっともだ vs ～のも無理(むり)はない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>無理(むり)はない</code> says the reaction cannot be
**blamed**. もっともだ says it is positively **reasonable**.

</div>

<pre>
あれだけ待(ま)たされたのだから、怒(おこ)り出(だ)した<b>のも無理(むり)はない</b>。
Kept waiting that long, it's no surprise he lost his temper.
</pre>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)が説明(せつめい)と違(ちが)うのだから、契約(けいやく)を断(ことわ)った<b>のももっともだ</b>。
The terms weren't what he'd been told, so turning the contract down was perfectly sound.
</pre>

<code>無理(むり)はない</code> excuses; it is compatible with a reaction the speaker
would not defend on its merits - losing your temper is understandable, not admirable.
`もっともだ` endorses the reasoning itself, which is why it is the natural choice for
a considered decision. <code>無理(むり)はない</code> is also the more colloquial of
the two.

### ～のももっともだ vs ～のも仕方(しかた)がない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もっともだ` **agrees** with someone.
<code>仕方(しかた)がない</code> **gives up** on something.

</div>

<pre>
これだけ雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)れば、電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まる<b>のも仕方(しかた)がない</b>。
With snow like this there's nothing to be done about the trains stopping.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)が確認(かくにん)できないのだから、乗客(じょうきゃく)が不安(ふあん)がる<b>のももっともだ</b>。
Safety hasn't been confirmed, so the passengers' anxiety is entirely reasonable.
</pre>

<code>仕方(しかた)がない</code> is resignation and takes nobody's side; it works
just as well for weather and machinery as for people. `もっともだ` is an endorsement
and needs somebody whose position can be endorsed.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 怒(おこ)るもっともだ。 → ✅ 怒(おこ)る<b>のももっともだ</b>。
  もっとも is an adjective describing a thing, so the clause has to be nominalised
  with `の` first.

- ❌ 不満(ふまん)<b>だのももっともだ</b> → ✅ 不満(ふまん)<b>なのももっともだ</b>
  な-adjectives take `な` before `の`. もっとも itself is a な-adjective too, hence
  <code>もっともな意見(いけん)</code>.

- ❌ 電池(でんち)が切(き)れていたのだから、時計(とけい)が止(と)まっている<b>のももっともだ</b>。 → ✅ …止(と)まっている<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
  A stopped clock has no reasoning behind it. もっともだ needs a person's judgement or
  feeling as its subject.

- ❌ もっとも意見(いけん)ですね。 → ✅ もっとも<b>な</b>意見(いけん)ですね。（or ごもっともです）
  Before a noun the `な` is obligatory. The stand-alone polite acknowledgement is
  <code>ごもっともです</code>, with the honorific `ご`.

## Practice

**1.** 説明(せつめい)が二転三転(にてんさんてん)したのだから、住民(じゅうみん)が不信感(ふしんかん)を持(も)つ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

不信感(ふしんかん)を持(も)つ<b>のももっともだ</b>

The clause names a human reaction - distrust - and the sentence endorses it as a
reasonable response to shifting explanations. The <code>のだから</code> clause in
front supplies the grounds, which this pattern normally expects.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 停電(ていでん)していたのだから、部屋(へや)が真(ま)っ暗(くら)だった（のももっともだ・のも当然(とうぜん)だ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部屋(へや)が真(ま)っ暗(くら)だった<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>

A dark room is a physical consequence, not somebody's judgement, so there is nothing
for もっともだ to side with. Had the clause been 「子供(こども)が怖(こわ)がる」,
either would work.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "They were never told about the change, so it's quite reasonable that the staff are annoyed."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

変更(へんこう)を知(し)らされていなかったのだから、スタッフが怒(おこ)る<b>のももっともだ</b>。

"Quite reasonable" applied to somebody's anger is the core use. Note that the speaker
is siding with the staff; <code>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> would make the same
sentence a cool judgement on whoever failed to tell them.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)の意見(いけん)はもっとも意見(いけん)だと思(おも)うが、予算(よさん)が足(た)りない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)の意見(いけん)はもっとも<b>な</b>意見(いけん)だと思(おも)うが、予算(よさん)が足(た)りない。
（more naturally: 彼(かれ)の言(い)うことはもっともだが、予算(よさん)が足(た)りない。）

もっとも is a な-adjective, so it needs `な` to modify a noun. The が in the second
half is worth noticing too - conceding with もっともだ and then qualifying is the
pattern's most typical shape.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)が辞退(じたい)した<b>のももっともだ</b>。
(b) 彼(かれ)が辞退(じたい)した<b>のも無理(むり)はない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) endorses his reasoning. The speaker has looked at his situation and concluded
that declining was a sound decision - the kind of thing you say when you would have
done the same and think well of him for it.

(b) merely excuses it. It says nobody could hold the withdrawal against him, which
leaves room for the speaker privately wishing he had stayed. (b) is also the warmer
and more colloquial of the two; (a) sounds like a considered verdict.

</details>

# ～て当然だ

**Meaning** · of course it is; only natural; no surprise there - a verdict that the outcome is exactly what the circumstances called for

<code>当然(とうぜん)</code> (とうぜん) is a noun-cum-な-adjective meaning "a matter of
course". Hung off a て-form, it turns the clause in front of it into a judgement:
given what we both already know, *this* result needed no explaining. It looks
backward at a fact on the table, and it can be warm ("of course you're tired") or
sharp ("naturally they walked out").

## Formation

The pattern is the て-form of the predicate plus <code>当然(とうぜん)だ</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + 当然(とうぜん)だ | 怒(おこ)る → 怒(おこ)って当然(とうぜん)だ |
| Verb (negative) | ～なくて + 当然(とうぜん)だ | 読(よ)めない → 読(よ)めなくて当然(とうぜん)だ |
| い-adjective | ～くて + 当然(とうぜん)だ | 眠(ねむ)い → 眠(ねむ)くて当然(とうぜん)だ |
| な-adjective | ～で + 当然(とうぜん)だ | 不安(ふあん)な → 不安(ふあん)で当然(とうぜん)だ |
| Noun | ～で + 当然(とうぜん)だ (rare) | 無料(むりょう) → 無料(むりょう)で当然(とうぜん)だ |
| Polite | … + 当然(とうぜん)です | 疲(つか)れて当然(とうぜん)です |
| Before a noun | 当然(とうぜん)の + noun | 当然(とうぜん)の権利(けんり) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun row is genuinely uncommon. A bare noun rarely reads as a
result you can pass judgement on, so writers recast it as
<code>無料(むりょう)なのは当然(とうぜん)だ</code>. Verbs and adjectives are
where this pattern actually lives.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
三日(みっか)も寝(ね)ていないんだから、眠(ねむ)く<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
You haven't slept in three days - of course you're sleepy.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ習(なら)っていない漢字(かんじ)です。読(よ)め<b>なくて当然(とうぜん)です</b>。
It's a kanji you haven't been taught yet. Naturally you can't read it.
</pre>

<pre>
これだけ練習(れんしゅう)したのだから、勝(か)っ<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
After that much practice, winning was only to be expected.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)が足(た)りなかったのだから、相手(あいて)が怒(おこ)っ<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
The explanation was thin, so it's hardly surprising the other side got annoyed.
</pre>

<pre>
一日中(いちにちじゅう)立(た)ちっぱなしだったのだから、足(あし)が痛(いた)く<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
You were on your feet all day - your legs are bound to hurt.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めての海外出張(かいがいしゅっちょう)なんだから、不安(ふあん)<b>で当然(とうぜん)だよ</b>。
It's your first trip abroad for work - of course you're uneasy about it.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)も使(つか)っている洗濯機(せんたくき)だから、壊(こわ)れ<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。文句(もんく)は言(い)えない。
The washing machine is ten years old, so it dying on us is fair enough. I can't complain.
</pre>

<pre>
あれだけ準備(じゅんび)に時間(じかん)をかけたのだから、発表(はっぴょう)がうまくいっ<b>て当然(とうぜん)です</b>。
They put that much time into the preparation, so the presentation going well was no accident.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が十年(じゅうねん)上(あ)がっていないのだから、若(わか)い社員(しゃいん)が辞(や)め<b>て当然(とうぜん)でしょう</b>。
Pay hasn't moved in ten years - you can hardly blame the younger staff for leaving.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The form itself is neutral. <code>当然(とうぜん)です</code> is at home in a
report, a meeting or a newspaper column, and <code>当然(とうぜん)だ</code> in
ordinary plain speech. What carries the tone is not the ending but the *stance*,
and this pattern has two very different ones.

Turned outward at somebody else's feelings it is generous - it grants permission.
<code>不安(ふあん)で当然(とうぜん)だよ</code> tells the listener they are not
overreacting, and that is one of the kindest things this construction does.

Turned toward what you think you are owed, it hardens fast.
<code>これくらいしてもらって当然(とうぜん)だ</code> - "I'm entitled to at least
this much" - sounds arrogant, and entitlement is the reading a listener will reach
for whenever the subject is the speaker's own advantage. Japanese even has a stock
complaint for it: <code>当然(とうぜん)だと思(おも)っている</code>, "takes it
for granted". Notice which way your sentence points before you say it.

<code>当(あ)たり前(まえ)だ</code> is the everyday spoken twin and is blunter
in both directions. In writing - especially argumentative writing -
<code>～のは当然(とうぜん)だ</code> and <code>～のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> are
more frequent than the て-form version, and
<code>当然(とうぜん)のことながら</code> ("as would be expected") is a stiff
adverbial set phrase that belongs to written style only.

## Contrast

### ～て当然(とうぜん)だ vs ～のも当然(とうぜん)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>て当然(とうぜん)だ</code> hangs the verdict straight
off the predicate; <code>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> first packages the whole
clause as a noun, so it carries longer, more argued material.

</div>

<pre>
寝(ね)ていないのだから、集中(しゅうちゅう)できなく<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
You haven't slept, so of course you can't concentrate.
</pre>

<pre>
三人(さんにん)分(ぶん)の仕事(しごと)を一人(ひとり)でやっているのだから、彼女(かのじょ)が倒(たお)れた<b>のも当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
She's been doing three people's work single-handed, so it's no wonder she collapsed.
</pre>

Because <code>の</code> nominalises, the second shape takes a past tense, a long
subordinate clause or a subject of its own without straining. The first is compact
and lands harder. There is no meaning difference worth agonising over; choose by
the weight of the clause.

### ～て当然(とうぜん)だ vs ～て当(あ)たり前(まえ)だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same judgement, different room.
<code>当然(とうぜん)</code> is the Sino-Japanese word and survives into writing;
<code>当(あ)たり前(まえ)</code> is native, spoken, and more forceful.

</div>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)なんだから、失敗(しっぱい)し<b>て当(あ)たり前(まえ)だ</b>。
He's brand new - of course he makes mistakes.
</pre>

<code>当(あ)たり前(まえ)</code> can be dismissive on its own -
<code>そんなの当(あ)たり前(まえ)でしょ</code> is close to "well, obviously" -
so it is a poor choice when you are speaking up the hierarchy. In a written
report, use <code>当然(とうぜん)</code>. That is a tendency of register, not a
rule of grammar; both are grammatical anywhere.

### ～て当然(とうぜん)だ vs ～はずだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずだ` predicts something you have **not** confirmed.
<code>て当然(とうぜん)だ</code> passes judgement on something you already know
to be so.

</div>

<pre>
この時間(じかん)なら道(みち)はすいている<b>はずだ</b>。
The roads should be clear at this hour.
</pre>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)の初日(しょにち)だから、道(みち)が混(こ)ん<b>で当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
It's the first day of a long weekend - naturally the roads are jammed.
</pre>

You can be wrong about a `はずだ`, which is why <code>はずだったのに</code> exists.
There is no equivalent regret form here: you cannot be let down by a verdict on a
fact you are looking straight at.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 疲(つか)れる<b>当然(とうぜん)だ</b> → ✅ 疲(つか)れ<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>
  A dictionary form cannot sit directly in front of <code>当然(とうぜん)だ</code>.
  Use the て-form, or nominalise: <code>疲(つか)れるのは当然(とうぜん)だ</code>.

- ❌ 不安(ふあん)<b>くて当然(とうぜん)だ</b> → ✅ 不安(ふあん)<b>で当然(とうぜん)だ</b>
  <code>不安(ふあん)</code> is a な-adjective, so its て-form is
  <code>不安(ふあん)で</code>. Only い-adjectives take くて.

- ❌ 読(よ)め<b>ないで当然(とうぜん)だ</b> → ✅ 読(よ)め<b>なくて当然(とうぜん)だ</b>
  ないで is the "without doing" form and carries no cause. A negative that feeds a
  judgement about cause takes なくて.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b> → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>でしょう</b>
  Tomorrow's weather is not yet a fact, so there is nothing to call natural. This
  pattern judges; it does not forecast.

## Practice

**1.** 荷物(にもつ)をこんなに持(も)たされたら、重(おも)____当然(とうぜん)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

重(おも)<b>くて当然(とうぜん)だ</b>

<code>重(おも)い</code> is an い-adjective, so the て-form drops い and adds くて.
The sentence says the weight is no surprise, given how much was loaded on.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 渋滞(じゅうたい)しているから、彼(かれ)はもう少(すこ)し遅(おく)れる（て当然(とうぜん)だ・はずだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

遅(おく)れる<b>はずだ</b>

He has not arrived yet, so this is a prediction about an unconfirmed event, and
predictions take `はずだ`. Once he does turn up late, you could then say
<code>遅(おく)れて当然(とうぜん)だ</code> about it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It was his first time cooking, so of course it burned."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

初(はじ)めての料理(りょうり)だったのだから、焦(こ)げ<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。

The premise goes in front with のだから, and the て-form of
<code>焦(こ)げる</code> carries the verdict.
<code>焦(こ)げたのも当然(とうぜん)だ</code> is equally correct and a shade more
explanatory in tone.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あんな練習(れんしゅう)をしていたら、けがをする当然(とうぜん)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あんな練習(れんしゅう)をしていたら、けがをし<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。

<code>当然(とうぜん)だ</code> needs a て-form in front of it, never a dictionary
form. Nominalising also works: <code>けがをするのも当然(とうぜん)だ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 休(やす)みが取(と)れなく<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。
(b) 休(やす)みが取(と)れ<b>て当然(とうぜん)だ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) accepts the situation: given the workload or the season, nobody could have
expected leave to be granted. It is resigned, and often sympathetic.

(b) claims an entitlement: time off is something the speaker is owed, and being
refused it is unfair. Because the judgement is about the speaker's own advantage,
(b) easily sounds demanding - it is a sentence for arguing a point, not for small
talk.

</details>

# ～からには

**Meaning** · now that; if it's come to this; given that X is so, then obviously Y - a premise the speaker then draws a duty or a resolve out of

`からには` starts from something settled - a decision already made, a fact you
cannot now unmake - and says: *that being so*, this is what has to follow. The
second half is never a neutral report. It is a determination, an obligation, a
piece of advice, or a firm judgement. English reaches for "if I'm going to do it
at all…" or "now that you've said it…".

## Formation

The clause in front of `からには` is in plain form. Nouns and な-adjectives need
である, not だ.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + からには | やる → やるからには |
| Verb (past) | た-form + からには | 決(き)めた → 決(き)めたからには |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + からには | 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないからには |
| い-adjective | plain form + からには | 苦(くる)しい → 苦(くる)しいからには |
| な-adjective | ～である + からには | 必要(ひつよう)な → 必要(ひつよう)であるからには |
| Noun | ～である + からには | 医者(いしゃ) → 医者(いしゃ)であるからには |
| Shortened variant | plain form + からは | 始(はじ)めた → 始(はじ)めたからは |

<div class="note">

**Note** - tense inside the clause is real, not decorative.
<code>やるからには</code> looks forward from a decision not yet acted on;
<code>やったからには</code> looks back from something already done and treats it as
a bridge you have burned.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
やる<b>からには</b>、最後(さいご)までやろう。
If we're going to do this, let's see it through.
</pre>

<pre>
約束(やくそく)した<b>からには</b>、守(まも)らなければならない。
You made the promise, so now you have to keep it.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)に出(で)る<b>からには</b>、優勝(ゆうしょう)を目指(めざ)します。
If I'm entering the tournament at all, I'm aiming to win it.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)で働(はたら)く<b>からには</b>、敬語(けいご)をきちんと覚(おぼ)えたい。
Since I'm going to be working in Japan, I want to learn honorific language properly.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>からには</b>、途中(とちゅう)で投(な)げ出(だ)すわけにはいかない。
Having taken the job on, I can hardly walk away halfway through.
</pre>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)である<b>からには</b>、まず患者(かんじゃ)の話(はなし)を聞(き)くべきだ。
If you are a doctor, listening to the patient has to come first.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)に反対(はんたい)されても自分(じぶん)で決(き)めた<b>からには</b>、あとで文句(もんく)は言(い)わない。
My parents were against it, but I chose this myself, so I'm not going to complain later.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の名前(なまえ)で出(だ)す資料(しりょう)である<b>からには</b>、数字(すうじ)のミスは一(ひと)つも許(ゆる)されません。
Since this goes out under the company's name, not one numerical error is acceptable.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)する<b>からには</b>、遊(あそ)んでいる時間(じかん)はないと覚悟(かくご)しています。
I'm going abroad to study, and I've accepted that there'll be no time for messing about.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`からには` is register-neutral in form - it takes plain or polite endings and
appears in conversation, essays and speeches alike - but it is not tone-neutral.
It has spine. Saying it commits you, or commits the person you are speaking to,
and Japanese speakers use it exactly when they want that weight: a captain before
a match, a new employee on the first day, somebody talking themselves into
finishing what they started.

That is also its limit. Because it demands a conclusion the speaker is willing to
stand behind, `からには` fits badly with a shrug. A sentence that ends in a bare
observation leaves the listener waiting for the point.

Two shapes are worth noticing. First, the subject of the `からには` clause is very
often the speaker, and the second half is very often <code>つもりだ</code>,
<code>たい</code>, <code>べきだ</code>,
<code>なければならない</code> or
<code>わけにはいかない</code>. Second, `からには` is common in the fixed rhetorical
frame <code>やるからには</code> - "if we're doing it, we're doing it properly" -
which turns up in pep talks constantly.

`からは` is the same pattern with the に dropped. It is a little more literary and
noticeably less common in speech; if you are unsure, `からには` is always safe.

## Contrast

### ～からには vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` states a cause for anything at all. `からには` states a
premise, and the second half must be something the speaker *concludes* from it.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>から</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
The match was called off because it's raining.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)に出(で)ると決(き)めた<b>からには</b>、毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)ります。
Now that I've decided to compete, I'm going to run every morning.
</pre>

Swap them and both break. The rain sentence reports what happened, which is not a
conclusion anyone drew, so `からには` would sound absurd there. The training
sentence is a commitment, and plain `から` would flatten it into a bare
explanation.

### ～からには vs ～以上(いじょう)（は）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two are close to interchangeable. `からには` leans
personal and resolute; <code>以上(いじょう)</code> leans logical and is more at
home in writing.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)き受(う)ける<b>からには</b>、全力(ぜんりょく)でやります。
If I'm taking it on, I'll give it everything.
</pre>

<pre>
契約(けいやく)を結(むす)んだ<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、条件(じょうけん)は変(か)えられません。
Now that the contract has been signed, the terms cannot be changed.
</pre>

The first is a person promising something. The second is a rule being applied.
<code>以上(いじょう)</code> also handles standing conditions comfortably -
<code>会員(かいいん)である以上(いじょう)</code>, "for as long as you are a
member" - where `からには` would sound like a pledge rather than a condition. This
is a tendency, and plenty of sentences accept either.

### ～からには vs ～上(うえ)は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>上(うえ)は</code> is the stiff written cousin. Almost
anywhere it fits, `からには` also fits and sounds more human.

</div>

<pre>
こうなった<b>上(うえ)は</b>、自分(じぶん)で説明(せつめい)するしかない。
Things being what they are now, I have no choice but to explain in person.
</pre>

<pre>
こうなった<b>からには</b>、自分(じぶん)で説明(せつめい)するしかない。
Now that it's come to this, I'll have to explain in person myself.
</pre>

Both are correct and the meaning is the same. <code>上(うえ)は</code> belongs to
written Japanese, formal announcements and the resolute register of fiction, and
it strongly prefers a た-form or こうなった in front of it. In everyday
conversation you will hear `からには` many times before you hear
<code>上(うえ)は</code> once.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)だ<b>からには</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)である<b>からには</b>
  A noun links to `からには` with である. Plain だ cannot sit there, and
  <code>学生(がくせい)な</code> is wrong too.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)に落(お)ちた<b>からには</b>、とても悲(かな)しいです。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)に落(お)ちて、とても悲(かな)しいです。
  A feeling is not a conclusion drawn from a premise. `からには` needs a resolve, a
  duty or a judgement after it.

- ❌ 約束(やくそく)した<b>からには</b>、彼(かれ)は来(き)ませんでした。 → ✅ 約束(やくそく)したのに、彼(かれ)は来(き)ませんでした。
  The second half here contradicts the premise instead of following from it. That
  is のに territory.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がない<b>からには</b>、タクシーで行(い)きました。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がなかった<b>から</b>、タクシーで行(い)きました。
  A plain past-tense report of what you did is an ordinary `から` sentence. Nothing
  is being resolved.

## Practice

**1.** 社長(しゃちょう)の前(まえ)で発表(はっぴょう)する____、準備(じゅんび)を完璧(かんぺき)にしておきたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

発表(はっぴょう)する<b>からには</b>

The second half is a determination - wanting the preparation to be perfect - which
is exactly what `からには` sets up. The verb stays in plain dictionary form because
the presentation has not happened yet.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)は熱(ねつ)があった（からには・から）、会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This is a plain report of a past event and its cause. There is no conclusion being

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

やると言(い)った<b>からには</b>、今(いま)さら断(ことわ)るわけにはいかない。

The た-form marks the promise as already made, and
<code>わけにはいかない</code> is the classic partner for `からには` - a duty the

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Nouns take である in front of `からには`. The rest of the sentence is fine -

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 引(ひ)っ越(こ)す<b>からには</b>、部屋(へや)をきれいにして出(で)たい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

*if* I'm leaving, then I'm leaving the place properly. There is a hint of pride in
it.

(b) simply gives moving out as the reason for wanting to clean. It is neutral
information, with no self-imposed standard attached - the sort of thing you would
say to a landlord rather than to yourself.

</details>

# ～上は

**Meaning** · now that; since it has come to this - read <code>上(うえ)は</code>. Once the situation has reached a certain point, only one course of action is left

<code>上(うえ)は</code> takes something that has already happened - a decision
announced, a die cast, a fact that can no longer be walked back - and treats it as
closing off the alternatives. What follows is resolve, duty, or resignation, very
often with <code>しかない</code>, <code>ほかない</code> or
<code>なければならない</code>. It is a formal, written-leaning pattern with a
distinctly resolute ring.

## Formation

<code>上(うえ)は</code> follows a clause in noun-modifying (plain) form. The
past tense dominates, because the pattern is about a point already passed.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (past) | た-form + 上(うえ)は | 決(き)めた → 決(き)めた上(うえ)は |
| Verb (non-past) | plain form + 上(うえ)は | やる → やる上(うえ)は (uncommon) |
| Fixed opener | こうなった + 上(うえ)は | - → こうなった上(うえ)は |
| Noun / な-adjective | ～である + 上(うえ)は | 社員(しゃいん) → 社員(しゃいん)である上(うえ)は (rare) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - three unrelated patterns share this kanji and learners mix them
constantly. <code>上(うえ)は</code> = "now that"; <code>た上(うえ)で</code> =
"after doing, and on that basis"; <code>上(うえ)に</code> = "on top of that,
moreover". A fourth, read <code>じょう</code>, attaches to nouns and means "from
the standpoint of": <code>法律上(ほうりつじょう)</code>,
<code>健康上(けんこうじょう)</code>. Reading and meaning move together.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こうなった<b>上(うえ)は</b>、やるしかない。
Now that it's come to this, there's nothing for it but to go ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>上(うえ)は</b>、責任(せきにん)を持(も)ちます。
Having accepted the job, I will see it through.
</pre>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めると決(き)めた<b>上(うえ)は</b>、早(はや)く次(つぎ)を探(さが)さなければならない。
Now that I've decided to leave the company, I have to find something next quickly.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)の前(まえ)で宣言(せんげん)した<b>上(うえ)は</b>、後(あと)には引(ひ)けない。
Having announced it in front of everyone, I can't back down now.
</pre>

<pre>
裁判(さいばん)になった<b>上(うえ)は</b>、弁護士(べんごし)に任(まか)せるほかない。
Now that it has gone to court, there is no option but to leave it to a lawyer.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)が分(わ)かった<b>上(うえ)は</b>、速(すみ)やかに対策(たいさく)を取(と)るべきだ。
Now that the cause of the accident is known, measures should be taken without delay.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)世(よ)に出(だ)した<b>上(うえ)は</b>、どんな批判(ひはん)も受(う)け入(い)れる覚悟(かくご)でいます。
Having put it out into the world, I am prepared to accept whatever criticism comes.
</pre>

<pre>
代表(だいひょう)に選(えら)ばれた<b>上(うえ)は</b>、自分(じぶん)のためではなくチームのために戦(たたか)います。
Now that I've been picked for the national side, I'll be playing for the team, not for myself.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a stiff pattern. You will meet it in written statements, formal
announcements, editorials, courtroom language and the resolute speech of fiction -
and comparatively rarely in ordinary conversation, where `からには` does the same
job and sounds like a person rather than a document. If you say
<code>上(うえ)は</code> over lunch, it will land as deliberately dramatic. That
is sometimes exactly the effect you want; just know that you are reaching for it.

The tone is resolve with the exits closed. There is no cheerfulness in
<code>上(うえ)は</code>: the situation has narrowed, and the speaker is
accepting the narrowing. That is why <code>しかない</code> and
<code>ほかない</code> - "there is no alternative" - are its most frequent
companions, and why <code>こうなった上(うえ)は</code> has become a set opening
for bad news being faced squarely.

Because the pattern is written-leaning, its second half tends to be written-leaning
too: <code>べきだ</code>, <code>なければならない</code>,
<code>覚悟(かくご)だ</code>, <code>つもりだ</code>. A casual ending after it
sounds mismatched - <code>こうなった上(うえ)は、まあいいや</code> is a joke, not
a sentence. Note also that the second half must be something still open: an
intention, an obligation, a judgement. It cannot be a finished report of what
happened next.

## Contrast

### ～上(うえ)は vs ～からには

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the meaning is the same. `からには` is the everyday form;
<code>上(うえ)は</code> is the formal one, and it strongly prefers a completed
action in front of it.

</div>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)すると決(き)めた<b>からには</b>、お金(かね)をためないと。
Now that I've decided to study abroad, I need to start saving.
</pre>

<pre>
留学(りゅうがく)を決意(けつい)した<b>上(うえ)は</b>、覚悟(かくご)を決(き)めて準備(じゅんび)を進(すす)めます。
Having resolved to study abroad, I shall steel myself and get on with preparing.
</pre>

Same event, two registers. The second sentence's vocabulary -
<code>決意(けつい)</code>, <code>覚悟(かくご)</code> - is not accidental;
<code>上(うえ)は</code> pulls the whole sentence upward in formality. A
non-past verb, which `からには` takes happily, sits awkwardly before
<code>上(うえ)は</code>.

### ～上(うえ)は vs ～た上(うえ)で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>上(うえ)は</code> is a *reason*: given that X has
happened, Y must follow. <code>た上(うえ)で</code> is a *sequence*: do X first,
then decide Y on that basis.

</div>

<pre>
検査(けんさ)の結果(けっか)が出(で)た<b>上(うえ)は</b>、治療(ちりょう)を始(はじ)めるしかない。
Now that the test results are in, there's no choice but to start treatment.
</pre>

<pre>
検査(けんさ)の結果(けっか)を見(み)た<b>上(うえ)で</b>、治療(ちりょう)の方針(ほうしん)を決(き)めます。
We'll decide on a course of treatment after looking at the test results.
</pre>

One は, one で, and the sentences do different work. The first says the results
have removed the alternatives. The second says the results are an input to a
decision still to be made. Mixing them up produces sentences that sound almost
right and mean something else entirely.

### ～上(うえ)は vs ～以上(いじょう)（は）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>以上(いじょう)</code> covers standing states as well
as completed acts; <code>上(うえ)は</code> really wants a completed act.

</div>

<pre>
親(おや)である<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、子(こ)どもの安全(あんぜん)に責任(せきにん)がある。
As long as you are a parent, you are responsible for your child's safety.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)になった<b>上(うえ)は</b>、生活(せいかつ)を見直(みなお)さなければならない。
Now that I've become a parent, I have to rethink how I live.
</pre>

The first describes an ongoing condition and the duty that rides along with it.
The second names the moment things changed and the obligation created by it. This
is a leaning rather than a hard wall, but <code>である上(うえ)は</code> is
distinctly rare, while <code>である以上(いじょう)</code> is everywhere in formal
writing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)だ<b>上(うえ)は</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)である<b>以上(いじょう)</b>
  A plain だ cannot precede <code>上(うえ)は</code>, and even the である version
  is unusual here. For a standing state, reach for
  <code>以上(いじょう)</code> instead.

- ❌ 引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>上(うえ)は</b>、大変(たいへん)でした。 → ✅ 引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>上(うえ)は</b>、最後(さいご)までやります。
  The second half has to be an intention or an obligation, not a report of how it
  turned out.

- ❌ 相談(そうだん)した<b>上(うえ)は</b>決(き)めます。 → ✅ 相談(そうだん)した<b>上(うえ)で</b>決(き)めます。
  "After consulting" is <code>上(うえ)で</code>. With は the sentence claims the
  consultation left you no choice, which is not what you meant.

- ❌ こうなった<b>上(うえ)は</b>、どうしようかな。 → ✅ こうなった<b>上(うえ)は</b>、自分(じぶん)でやるしかない。
  Wavering undercuts the pattern. <code>上(うえ)は</code> announces that the
  deliberation is over.

## Practice

**1.** 試合(しあい)に出(で)ると決(き)まった____、練習(れんしゅう)を増(ふ)やすしかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

決(き)まった<b>上(うえ)は</b>

The decision is settled and the second half is <code>しかない</code>, the classic
partner. Note the past tense: <code>上(うえ)は</code> looks back at a point
already passed.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 専門家(せんもんか)の意見(いけん)を聞(き)いた（上(うえ)は・上(うえ)で）、結論(けつろん)を出(だ)したいと思(おも)います。

everyday, and carries no sense of a bridge being burned - which for most house
moves is the more natural thing to say.

</details>

# 以上 - or more

**Meaning** · that much or more; upwards of; at least - read <code>以上(いじょう)</code>, and note that the stated figure is **included**

Stuck on the end of a quantity, <code>以上(いじょう)</code> draws a line and
points upward from it. <code>十八歳(じゅうはっさい)以上(いじょう)</code> means
eighteen and everyone older - an eighteen-year-old qualifies. That inclusiveness is
not a detail: it is the whole reason application forms, contracts and exam rules
use this word instead of a vaguer one.

## Formation

<code>以上(いじょう)</code> is a suffix. It attaches directly to whatever
expresses the amount, with no particle in between.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Number + counter | quantity + 以上(いじょう) | 三回(さんかい) → 三回(さんかい)以上(いじょう) |
| Bare number | number + 以上(いじょう) | 100 → 100以上(いじょう) |
| Noun naming a level | noun + 以上(いじょう) | 高校生(こうこうせい) → 高校生(こうこうせい)以上(いじょう) |
| Demonstrative | これ・それ + 以上(いじょう) | これ → これ以上(いじょう) |
| Before a noun | 以上(いじょう)の + noun | 予想(よそう) → 予想(よそう)以上(いじょう)の人出(ひとで) |
| Before a predicate | 以上(いじょう)に + predicate | 想像(そうぞう) → 想像(そうぞう)以上(いじょう)に難(むずか)しい |
| Closing a statement | 以上(いじょう)です | - → 以上(いじょう)です |

<div class="note">

**Note** - keep three lookalikes apart.
<code>以上(いじょう)</code> = that figure and up (inclusive).
<code>以下(いか)</code> = that figure and down (also inclusive).
<code>以内(いない)</code> = within a limit, counting from zero -
<code>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)以内(いない)</code>, "inside thirty minutes".
<code>以外(いがい)</code> is a different word altogether: "other than".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
十八歳(じゅうはっさい)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>の方(かた)はこちらへどうぞ。
Those aged eighteen and over, this way please.
</pre>

<pre>
この荷物(にもつ)は5キロ<b>以上(いじょう)</b>あります。
This parcel weighs five kilos or more.
</pre>

<pre>
応募(おうぼ)には三年(さんねん)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>の経験(けいけん)が必要(ひつよう)です。
Applicants need three or more years of experience.
</pre>

<pre>
これ<b>以上(いじょう)</b>待(ま)てない。もう行(い)こう。
I can't wait any longer than this. Let's go.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)は60点(てん)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>で合格(ごうかく)です。ちょうど60点(てん)も含(ふく)みます。
You pass the exam at 60 or above. Exactly 60 counts too.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)の夏(なつ)は三十日(さんじゅうにち)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りませんでした。
Last summer it didn't rain for thirty days or more.
</pre>

<pre>
予想(よそう)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>に人(ひと)が集(あつ)まって、会場(かいじょう)に入(はい)りきらなかった。
More people turned up than we'd expected, and the venue couldn't hold them.
</pre>

<pre>
この奨学金(しょうがくきん)は、成績(せいせき)が平均(へいきん)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>で、かつ世帯(せたい)収入(しゅうにゅう)が400万円(まんえん)未満(みまん)の学生(がくせい)が対象(たいしょう)です。
The scholarship is for students whose grades are at or above average and whose household income is under four million yen.
</pre>

<pre>
報告(ほうこく)は<b>以上(いじょう)</b>です。ご質問(しつもん)があればどうぞ。
That concludes my report. Questions, if you have any.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>以上(いじょう)</code> is precise, neutral vocabulary. It belongs to
notices, forms, contracts, statistics and exam rules, and it is equally at home in
ordinary conversation once a number is involved -
<code>二時間(にじかん)以上(いじょう)待(ま)った</code> is what anybody would
say. There is nothing formal about the word itself; the formality of the sentences
you meet it in comes from their subject matter.

The one thing to hold onto is the boundary. Japanese draws the inclusive/exclusive
line lexically where English mumbles it: "over 20" in English is genuinely
ambiguous, whereas <code>20以上(いじょう)</code> definitely includes 20 and
<code>20を超(こ)える</code> definitely does not. In a legal or numerical context
that distinction is load-bearing, and getting it backwards in a job application or
a specification is a real error, not a stylistic one.

Two idiomatic uses are worth learning as units.
<code>これ以上(いじょう)</code> - "any more than this" - is extremely common in
speech and almost always runs into a negative or a limit:
<code>これ以上(いじょう)は無理(むり)</code>,
<code>これ以上(いじょう)言(い)わないで</code>. And
<code>以上(いじょう)です</code> is the standard full stop at the end of a
presentation, an announcement or a written list - the spoken equivalent of drawing
a line under the page. Ending a report with it is polite and expected, not curt.

## Contrast

### 以上(いじょう) vs 以下(いか)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - mirror images, and **both include the number**.
<code>以上(いじょう)</code> counts upward from it,
<code>以下(いか)</code> counts downward.

</div>

<pre>
小学生(しょうがくせい)<b>以下(いか)</b>は無料(むりょう)です。
Primary-school children and younger get in free.
</pre>

<pre>
中学生(ちゅうがくせい)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>は料金(りょうきん)がかかります。
Middle-school age and above pay the fare.
</pre>

Read together, the pair tiles the whole range with no gap and no overlap - which is
exactly how price lists are written. <code>以下(いか)</code> has a second,
non-numerical life meaning "the following": <code>以下(いか)のとおり</code>,
"as below". <code>以上(いじょう)</code> mirrors that too, in
<code>以上(いじょう)のように</code>, "as above".

### 以上(いじょう) vs ～を超(こ)える・未満(みまん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>以上(いじょう)</code> and
<code>以下(いか)</code> include the boundary;
<code>を超(こ)える</code> and <code>未満(みまん)</code> exclude it.

</div>

<pre>
体重(たいじゅう)が50キロ<b>以上(いじょう)</b>の人(ひと)。
People weighing 50 kg or more (50 counts).
</pre>

<pre>
体重(たいじゅう)が50キロを<b>超(こ)える</b>人(ひと)。
People weighing more than 50 kg (50 does not count).
</pre>

<code>未満(みまん)</code> is the exclusive partner at the bottom end:
<code>十八歳(じゅうはっさい)未満(みまん)</code> is seventeen and younger, and
does *not* include eighteen. Notice that these four words carve up a number line
between them, and that Japanese forms rely on you knowing which is which.

### 以上(いじょう) (quantity) vs 以上(いじょう) (now that)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what comes **before** it. A number or a level-noun
means "or more". A whole clause - a verb, an adjective, ～である - means "now
that; given that".

</div>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>日本(にほん)に住(す)んでいます。
I've been living in Japan for three years or more.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)に住(す)んでいる<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、税金(ぜいきん)は払(はら)わなければならない。
As long as I'm living in Japan, I have to pay the taxes.
</pre>

Same two kanji, unrelated jobs. The first is arithmetic; the second is a reason
clause meaning roughly the same as `からには`. In practice the front of the phrase
tells you instantly which one you are reading, and there is no sentence where both
are plausible.

## Common mistakes

- ❌「20歳(さい)以上(いじょう)」は21歳(さい)から。 → ✅「20歳(さい)以上(いじょう)」は20歳(さい)から。
  The boundary is included. If you need to exclude it, write
  <code>20歳(さい)を超(こ)える</code>.

- ❌ 以上(いじょう)三年(さんねん)の経験(けいけん) → ✅ 三年(さんねん)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>の経験(けいけん)
  English puts "more than" in front of the number; Japanese puts
  <code>以上(いじょう)</code> after it. This is one of the most common word-order
  slips at N2.

- ❌ 一時間(いちじかん)<b>の以上(いじょう)</b>待(ま)った → ✅ 一時間(いちじかん)<b>以上(いじょう)</b>待(ま)った
  No particle comes between the quantity and the suffix. The の only appears on the
  other side, when the phrase modifies a following noun.

- ❌ これ以上(いじょう)頑張(がんば)ります。 → ✅ これ以上(いじょう)は頑張(がんば)れません。
  <code>これ以上(いじょう)</code> marks a ceiling, so it runs into a negative or
  a limit. For "I'll try even harder" say
  <code>もっと頑張(がんば)ります</code>.

# 以上 - now that

**Meaning** · now that; given that; so long as X is the case - read <code>以上(いじょう)</code>. Once the premise holds, the conclusion is not optional

This <code>以上(いじょう)</code> has nothing to do with counting. It follows a
whole clause and says: take X as established, and Y follows from it as a matter of
logic. What comes after is a duty, a judgement, a resolution or a piece of firm
advice - never a bare report of events. It is the reasoning voice of contracts,
editorials and people explaining why a rule applies to them too.

## Formation

The clause in front is in plain, noun-modifying form. Nouns and な-adjectives
require である; だ and な are both wrong here.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + 以上(いじょう)（は） | やる → やる以上(いじょう) |
| Verb (past) | た-form + 以上(いじょう) | 引(ひ)き受(う)けた → 引(ひ)き受(う)けた以上(いじょう) |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 以上(いじょう) | 謝(あやま)らない → 謝(あやま)らない以上(いじょう) |
| い-adjective | plain form + 以上(いじょう) | 若(わか)い → 若(わか)い以上(いじょう) |
| な-adjective | ～である + 以上(いじょう) | 必要(ひつよう)な → 必要(ひつよう)である以上(いじょう) |
| Noun | ～である + 以上(いじょう) | 社員(しゃいん) → 社員(しゃいん)である以上(いじょう) |
| With topic marker | … + 以上(いじょう)は | 出(で)る → 出(で)る以上(いじょう)は |

<div class="note">

**Note** - adding は changes almost nothing; it puts a fraction more weight on the
premise, the way は always does. Both
<code>やる以上(いじょう)</code> and <code>やる以上(いじょう)は</code> are
completely ordinary.

</div>

## Examples

If I'm doing it at all, I want to do it properly.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

So long as you are an employee, you have to follow the company's rules.
</pre>

<pre>

In the absence of evidence, we cannot treat him as a suspect.
</pre>

<pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

<pre>
税金(ぜいきん)で運営(うんえい)されている施設(しせつ)である<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、市民(しみん)に説明(せつめい)する義務(ぎむ)がある。
Given that the facility is run on public money, there is a duty to explain matters to residents.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>以上(いじょう)</code> sounds *reasoned*. Where `からには` sounds like a
person steeling themselves, <code>以上(いじょう)</code> sounds like an argument
being made - which is why it is so common in written Japanese, in company
communication and in the parts of conversation where someone is explaining why a
principle applies. It is not stiff in the way <code>上(うえ)は</code> is; you
can say it at work without sounding theatrical.

Two things follow from that reasoned quality. First, it handles **standing
states** comfortably: <code>社員(しゃいん)である以上(いじょう)</code>,
<code>日本(にほん)にいる以上(いじょう)</code>, "for as long as this remains
true". Second, the premise is often something the speaker cannot change - a
contract, a legal status, a promise already given - and the sentence acknowledges
that it binds them. That gives it a faint air of resignation when the speaker is
talking about themselves, and a distinctly firm one when they are talking about
somebody else.

Handle that second case with some care. A sentence of the shape
<code>～である以上(いじょう)、～べきだ</code> aimed at a listener is a strong
move: it tells them their own
situation obliges them. In Japanese, as in English, "as long as you're living
under my roof" is an argument that wins the point and costs something. The polite
ending softens the form but not the content.

## Contrast

### ～以上(いじょう) vs ～からには

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "now that". `からには` is the speaker's resolve;
<code>以上(いじょう)</code> is a principle being applied.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)を任(まか)された<b>からには</b>、チームを変(か)えてみせます。
Now they've made me department head, I'm going to change this team, you'll see.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)である<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、部下(ぶか)の失敗(しっぱい)にも責任(せきにん)がある。
As department head, you bear responsibility for your subordinates' mistakes too.
</pre>

Notice what each takes in front of it. `からには` is happiest with an action the
speaker has taken or will take. <code>以上(いじょう)</code> takes that too, but
it also takes a plain standing state with である, and that is where `からには` starts
to sound odd - a state is not something you can resolve to do.

### ～以上(いじょう) vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` and `ので` explain *why something happened*.
<code>以上(いじょう)</code> explains *why something must be so*.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた<b>ので</b>、遠足(えんそく)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
The outing was cancelled because it was raining.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいている<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、中止(ちゅうし)にするしかない。
With a typhoon coming in, cancelling is the only option.
</pre>

The first sentence reports a completed event and its cause; nothing is being
argued. The second reasons forward from a fact to the decision it forces. Swap the
endings and both sentences go wrong - <code>以上(いじょう)</code> cannot lead
into a plain past-tense report, and `ので` drains the second sentence of its
"therefore".

### ～以上(いじょう) vs ～限(かぎ)り（は）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both can be "as long as".
<code>以上(いじょう)</code> says the fact *obliges* you.
<code>限(かぎ)り</code> marks the *duration or extent* over which something
holds.

</div>

<pre>
この会社(かいしゃ)にいる<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、ルールには従(したが)うべきだ。
As long as you work here, you ought to follow the rules.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が知(し)っている<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、そんな話(はなし)は出(で)ていない。
As far as I know, no such proposal has come up.
</pre>

<code>限(かぎ)り</code> draws a boundary around a period, a scope or a body of
knowledge, and the second half stays inside it. <code>以上(いじょう)</code> does
not measure anything; it converts a fact into an obligation. Where they genuinely
overlap - <code>生(い)きている以上(いじょう)</code> versus
<code>生(い)きている限(かぎ)り</code> - the first stresses the duty that
comes with being alive, the second the stretch of time.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 学生(がくせい)だ<b>以上(いじょう)</b> → ✅ 学生(がくせい)である<b>以上(いじょう)</b>
  <code>以上(いじょう)</code> is a noun-like element, and a noun links to it with
  である. Plain だ cannot modify it.

- ❌ 会員(かいいん)な<b>以上(いじょう)</b> → ✅ 会員(かいいん)である<b>以上(いじょう)</b>
  Do not carry the な over from <code>わけだ</code>, where
  <code>会員(かいいん)なわけだ</code> is correct. These two nouns behave
  differently, and である is the only safe link here.

- ❌ 遅(おく)れた<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れた<b>ので</b>、会議(かいぎ)に間(ま)に合(あ)いませんでした。
  The second half is a plain report of what happened. There is no obligation or
  judgement for the premise to force, so this is `ので` territory.

- ❌ 参加(さんか)する<b>以上(いじょう)</b>は、たぶん楽(たの)しいでしょう。 → ✅ 参加(さんか)する<b>以上(いじょう)</b>は、全力(ぜんりょく)で楽(たの)しみたい。
  A vague guess is not something the premise compels. What follows
  <code>以上(いじょう)</code> has to be a conclusion you are prepared to stand
  on.

## Practice

**1.** プロの選手(せんしゅ)である____、体調(たいちょう)管理(かんり)も仕事(しごと)のうちだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

プロの選手(せんしゅ)である<b>以上(いじょう)</b>

Being a professional is a standing state, and である is the required link. The
second half states the duty that the state creates - exactly the shape this
pattern is built for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっていた（以上(いじょう)・ので）、会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れてしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

止(と)まっていた<b>ので</b>、会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れてしまいました。

This is a past event explained by its cause - an apology, not an argument.
<code>以上(いじょう)</code> needs a conclusion with force behind it, and
"I ended up late" has none.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "As long as you have borrowed the money, you have to pay it back."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お金(かね)を借(か)りた<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、返(かえ)さなければならない。

The た-form marks the borrowing as an established fact, and
<code>なければならない</code> supplies the obligation that fact creates. With は -
<code>借(か)りた以上(いじょう)は</code> - the premise is pressed a little
harder.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 外国人(がいこくじん)な以上(いじょう)、ビザの規則(きそく)をよく調(しら)べておくべきだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 外国人(がいこくじん)である<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、ビザの規則(きそく)をよく調(しら)べておくべきだ。

Nouns take である before <code>以上(いじょう)</code>, not な. The rest of the
sentence is well formed: <code>べきだ</code> is a natural second half.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 日本(にほん)で働(はたら)く<b>以上(いじょう)</b>、敬語(けいご)は避(さ)けて通(とお)れない。
(b) 日本(にほん)で働(はたら)く<b>からには</b>、敬語(けいご)をものにしたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an argument about how things are. Working in Japan simply entails dealing
with honorific language, whoever you are - the sentence would sit comfortably in an
article.

(b) is a personal undertaking. The speaker has decided to work in Japan and is
committing themselves to master the language that goes with it. Swap the endings
and both sentences feel slightly off-key: a resolution wants `からには`, a general
principle wants <code>以上(いじょう)</code>.

</details>

# ～につき

**Meaning** · (1) owing to; on account of - the reason given on a notice; (2) per; for each; (3) regarding, in stiff written Japanese

`につき` does three unrelated-looking jobs, and the one you meet first depends
entirely on where you are reading. Taped to a shop door it means "because": 
<code>準備中(じゅんびちゅう)につき</code>, "we're getting ready, so…". In a price
list it means "per": <code>一人(ひとり)につき</code>. In a formal letter it means
"concerning". All three attach to a **noun**, never to a verb clause.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun of circumstance (cause) | noun + につき | 工事中(こうじちゅう) → 工事中(こうじちゅう)につき |
| な-adjective stem (cause) | stem + につき | 不在(ふざい) → 不在(ふざい)につき |
| Counter phrase (per) | quantity + につき | 一人(ひとり) → 一人(ひとり)につき |
| Noun (regarding) | noun + につき | この件(けん) → この件(けん)につき |
| Polite "regarding" | noun + につきまして | ご注文(ちゅうもん) → ご注文(ちゅうもん)につきまして |

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no verb row, and that is the single most useful fact on this
page. <code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っているにつき</code> is not Japanese. If your
reason is a clause, you need <code>ため</code> or `ので`; if you want `につき`, you
must compress the reason into a noun -
<code>雨天(うてん)</code>, <code>故障(こしょう)</code>,
<code>工事中(こうじちゅう)</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
工事中(こうじちゅう)<b>につき</b>、通行(つうこう)できません。
Closed to traffic owing to roadworks.
</pre>

<pre>
お一人様(ひとりさま)<b>につき</b>一(ひと)つまでとさせていただきます。
Limit one per customer.
</pre>

<pre>
本日(ほんじつ)は準備中(じゅんびちゅう)<b>につき</b>、しばらくお待(ま)ちください。
We are still setting up today, so please bear with us a little longer.
</pre>

<pre>
参加費(さんかひ)は一人(ひとり)<b>につき</b>3000円(えん)いただきます。
The fee is 3,000 yen per person.
</pre>

<pre>
雨天(うてん)<b>につき</b>、運動会(うんどうかい)は来週(らいしゅう)に延期(えんき)します。
Owing to rain, sports day is postponed to next week.
</pre>

<pre>
この用紙(ようし)は一家庭(いちかてい)<b>につき</b>一枚(いちまい)しか配(くば)られません。
Only one of these forms is distributed per household.
</pre>

<pre>
担当者(たんとうしゃ)が不在(ふざい)<b>につき</b>、あらためてご連絡(れんらく)いたします。
The person handling this is away, so we will contact you again later.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)接近(せっきん)<b>につき</b>、明日(あす)の営業(えいぎょう)は午前中(ごぜんちゅう)で終了(しゅうりょう)させていただきます。
With a typhoon approaching, we will be closing at midday tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
この件(けん)<b>につきまして</b>は、後日(ごじつ)書面(しょめん)でご回答(かいとう)いたします。
Regarding this matter, we will respond in writing at a later date.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The causal `につき` is notice Japanese. It lives on shop shutters, station boards,
municipal signs, printed apologies and business letters, and it is compact
precisely because those places have no room. Nobody says it over dinner. If a
colleague asks why you were out, the answer is
<code>熱(ねつ)が出(で)たので</code>, not
<code>発熱(はつねつ)につき</code> - the latter would sound like you were reading
your own sick note aloud.

That formality is worth using deliberately. Writing
<code>都合(つごう)によりお休(やす)みします</code> on a hand-written note and
<code>都合(つごう)につき</code> in a printed one are different acts of politeness;
`につき` signals institutional distance. In a personal apology, that distance can
read as cold.

The "per" sense is different in feel: it is neutral, unremarkable and used by
everyone. Prices, allowances, rations, dosages, quotas - anywhere a rate is being
stated - take `につき` without any sense of stiffness. This is the sense you are
most likely to need in ordinary life.

The "regarding" sense is the stiffest of the three, and in practice you will see it
mostly in the polite extended form <code>につきまして</code>, which is the business
register's version of `について`. Recognise it in correspondence; in speech, say
`について`.

## Contrast

### ～につき vs ～ため（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "owing to". <code>ため</code> takes a whole clause;
`につき` takes only a noun, and belongs to printed notices.

</div>

<pre>
機械(きかい)が故障(こしょう)している<b>ため</b>、本日(ほんじつ)は使用(しよう)できません。
Because the machine has broken down, it is unavailable today.
</pre>

<pre>
故障(こしょう)<b>につき</b>使用(しよう)中止(ちゅうし)
Out of order - do not use
</pre>

Look at what happened to the sentence. <code>ため</code> keeps the verb and reads
like prose; `につき` compresses the same reason into two characters and reads like
a sign. When you are writing anything longer than a label,
<code>ため</code> is usually the better fit, and it is the only option if the
reason will not compress into a noun.

### ～につき vs ～あたり・～ごとに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `につき` and <code>あたり</code> both give a **rate** per unit.
<code>ごとに</code> gives an **interval** - every so many units, repeatedly.

</div>

<pre>
一台(いちだい)<b>につき</b>二人(ふたり)まで乗(の)れます。
Up to two people per vehicle.
</pre>

<pre>
バスは20分(ぷん)<b>ごとに</b>出(で)ています。
The bus leaves every twenty minutes.
</pre>

<code>一人(ひとり)あたり</code> and <code>一人(ひとり)につき</code> are close
to interchangeable; <code>あたり</code> is a shade more statistical
(<code>一人(ひとり)あたりのGDP</code>) and `につき` a shade more regulatory.
<code>ごとに</code> is not interchangeable with either - swap it into the first
sentence and you get "two more people every vehicle", which is nonsense.

### ～につき vs ～について

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `について` means "about, concerning" and is everyday. `につき`
means that only in formal writing - and it additionally carries the two meanings
`について` never has, "owing to" and "per".

</div>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)に<b>ついて</b>説明(せつめい)します。
I'll explain the new system.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)<b>につきまして</b>ご説明(せつめい)申(もう)し上(あ)げます。
Allow me to explain the new system to you.
</pre>

The pair are the same sentence at two altitudes. The trap runs the other way as
well: <code>一人(ひとり)について2枚(まい)</code> does not mean "two sheets per
person" - it reads as "two sheets about one person", which is not what any form
means to say. For rates, only `につき` will do.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>につき</b>、中止(ちゅうし)します。 → ✅ 雨天(うてん)<b>につき</b>、中止(ちゅうし)します。
  `につき` attaches to nouns only. Either compress the reason into a noun or switch
  to <code>ため</code>.

- ❌ 風邪(かぜ)<b>につき</b>、今日(きょう)休(やす)むね。 → ✅ 風邪(かぜ)をひいたから、今日(きょう)休(やす)むね。
  Signage register in a text message to a friend is comic. Keep `につき` for
  printed notices and business writing.

- ❌ 一人(ひとり)に<b>ついて</b>2枚(まい)配(くば)ります。 → ✅ 一人(ひとり)<b>につき</b>2枚(まい)配(くば)ります。
  The "per" meaning belongs to `につき` alone. `について` would turn the people into
  a topic.

- ❌ この問題(もんだい)<b>につき</b>話(はな)し合(あ)おうよ。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)に<b>ついて</b>話(はな)し合(あ)おうよ。
  In casual speech the "concerning" sense is always `について`. `につき` there sounds
  like a memo read out loud.

## Practice

**1.** バスにお乗(の)りの方(かた)は、一人(ひとり)____荷物(にもつ)は二(ふた)つまでです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一人(ひとり)<b>につき</b>荷物(にもつ)は二(ふた)つまでです。

This is a rate stated per unit, which is the everyday sense of `につき`.
<code>一人(ひとり)あたり</code> would also be understood, though it sounds more
like a statistic than a rule.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいている（につき・ため）、明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

近(ちか)づいている<b>ため</b>、明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)です。

The reason is a verb clause, and `につき` cannot follow one. To use `につき` you
would have to compress it to a noun:
<code>台風(たいふう)接近(せっきん)につき</code>.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sign Japanese wants a noun reason plus `につき`, and

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>休(やす)んでいる</code> is a verb clause, so it cannot take `につき`.
Replacing it with the noun <code>不在(ふざい)</code> fixes the grammar and also
matches the formal register of the rest of the sentence.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) 工事(こうじ)をしている<b>ので</b>、この道(みち)は通(とお)れません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is what the barrier says. It is impersonal, printed, and gives the reason in
the fewest possible characters - nobody is speaking to you in particular.

(b) is what a person standing next to the barrier says to you. `ので` keeps the
verb and the softness, and it is the natural choice when you are actually
explaining something to someone face to face.

</details>

# ～手前

**Meaning** · (1) just short of; on this side of - a place you reach *before* the landmark; (2) with these people watching, and after what I've said - so I can't very well do otherwise

Read <code>手前(てまえ)</code>. The literal sense is spatial: the near side, the
stretch in front of something. The figurative sense keeps that image and turns it
social - the people in front of you, and the position you have put yourself in
where they can see. <code>子(こ)どもの手前(てまえ)</code> means "with the
children right there"; <code>約束(やくそく)した手前(てまえ)</code> means
"having said it out loud, in front of witnesses". Both are about appearances, and
what follows is almost always something the speaker *cannot* do.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (place) | noun + の + 手前(てまえ) | 信号(しんごう) → 信号(しんごう)の手前(てまえ) |
| Noun (person watching) | noun + の + 手前(てまえ) | 後輩(こうはい) → 後輩(こうはい)の手前(てまえ) |
| Verb (past) | た-form + 手前(てまえ) | 引(ひ)き受(う)けた → 引(ひ)き受(う)けた手前(てまえ) |
| Verb (ている) | ている + 手前(てまえ) | 言(い)っている → 言(い)っている手前(てまえ) |
| Verb (dictionary) | plain form + 手前(てまえ) | 教(おし)える → 教(おし)える手前(てまえ) |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the social sense has a strong preference for what comes after it:
<code>わけにはいかない</code>, <code>できない</code>,
<code>言(い)えない</code>, <code>～にくい</code>,
<code>なければならない</code>. If your second clause is not about being unable or
obliged, <code>手前(てまえ)</code> is probably the wrong pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
銀行(ぎんこう)の<b>手前(てまえ)</b>を右(みぎ)に曲(ま)がってください。
Turn right just before the bank.
</pre>

<pre>
信号(しんごう)の<b>手前(てまえ)</b>で止(と)まってください。
Please stop short of the traffic light.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもの<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、夫婦(ふうふ)げんかはできない。
With the children right there, we can hardly have a row.
</pre>

<pre>
後輩(こうはい)の<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、弱音(よわね)は吐(は)けない。
Not in front of the junior staff - I can't let myself complain.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)から言(い)い出(だ)した<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、今(いま)さらやめようとは言(い)えない。
I'm the one who suggested it, so I can hardly be the one to call it off now.
</pre>

<pre>
大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だと言(い)った<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、助(たす)けを求(もと)めにくい。
Having said I'd be fine, it's awkward to go asking for help.
</pre>

<pre>
交差点(こうさてん)の<b>手前(てまえ)</b>100メートルあたりから渋滞(じゅうたい)が始(はじ)まっていた。
The jam started about a hundred metres short of the junction.
</pre>

<pre>
先週(せんしゅう)みんなの前(まえ)で禁煙(きんえん)すると宣言(せんげん)した<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、ここでたばこを吸(す)うわけにはいかない。
I announced to everyone last week that I was quitting, so I can't exactly light one up here.
</pre>

<pre>
部下(ぶか)に残業(ざんぎょう)を減(へ)らせと言(い)っている<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、自分(じぶん)だけ遅(おそ)くまで残(のこ)るのも格好(かっこう)が悪(わる)い。
I'm the one telling my team to cut their overtime, so it looks bad if I'm the one staying late.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The social <code>手前(てまえ)</code> is a *face* word. It does not say the speaker is prevented by
circumstances, or by a rule; it says they are prevented by how it would look to
particular people who are watching or who heard them speak. That is a very
specific feeling, and Japanese speakers use it with a certain wryness - there is
usually a hint that the speaker would rather do the other thing, and can't. It is
common in ordinary conversation and in fiction, and rare in formal writing, where
the same content is put more neutrally.

Because the constraint is about appearances, <code>手前(てまえ)</code> also carries a light note of
vanity, self-mockery or duty depending on who is watching. Compare "I can't back
out in front of the students" with "as the person who proposed it, I can't back
out": the first is
<code>学生(がくせい)の手前(てまえ)</code>, the second
<code>提案(ていあん)した手前(てまえ)</code>, and both are things people admit
slightly ruefully.

One warning about the word outside this pattern.
<code>手前(てまえ)</code> is also an old first-person pronoun, surviving in the
business humble form <code>手前(てまえ)ども</code> ("we, our firm"). Contracted,
the very same word became <code>てめえ</code>, a genuinely insulting second-person
"you" that you will hear in fights and in anime and should never say. Do not use
<code>手前(てまえ)</code> to mean "you" under any circumstances - the polite
route is the person's name, or あなた.

## Contrast

### 手前(てまえ) vs 前(まえ)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>前(まえ)</code> is the space *in front of* a thing, as
seen from the thing. <code>手前(てまえ)</code> is the space *before you reach*
it, as seen from you.

</div>

<pre>
駅(えき)の<b>前(まえ)</b>で待(ま)っています。
I'll be waiting in front of the station.
</pre>

<pre>
駅(えき)の<b>手前(てまえ)</b>に小(ちい)さな橋(はし)があります。
There's a small bridge just before you get to the station.
</pre>

Taxi directions live on this distinction, and getting it wrong puts you a block
away: <code>コンビニの手前(てまえ)で止(と)めてください</code> means stop
short of the convenience store, not outside it. Note also that
<code>前(まえ)</code> covers time as well as space -
<code>食事(しょくじ)の前(まえ)</code> - while
<code>手前(てまえ)</code> never refers to time.

### ～手前(てまえ) vs ～からには・～以上(いじょう)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three turn a past commitment into a present obligation.
`からには` and <code>以上(いじょう)</code> appeal to logic or principle;
<code>手前(てまえ)</code> appeals to how it would look.

</div>

<pre>
引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>からには</b>、最後(さいご)まで責任(せきにん)を持(も)ちます。
Having taken it on, I'll see it through to the end.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)き受(う)けた<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、今(いま)さら断(ことわ)れない。
Having taken it on, I can't very well pull out now.
</pre>

The first is a promise; the speaker sounds committed. The second is a predicament;
the speaker sounds cornered. That difference is why `からには` is what you say in a
speech and <code>手前(てまえ)</code> is what you say to a friend afterwards.
Notice too that <code>手前(てまえ)</code> can take a *person* rather than an
action - <code>親(おや)の手前(てまえ)</code> - which neither of the others can
do.

### ～手前(てまえ) vs ～もので・～ものだから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>手前(てまえ)</code> explains why you **have to** do
something you would rather not. `もので` explains why you **failed** to do
something you should have.

</div>

<pre>
急(きゅう)に来客(らいきゃく)があった<b>もので</b>、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れました。
A visitor turned up unexpectedly, which is why my reply was late.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)をすると約束(やくそく)した<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、今日中(きょうじゅう)に書(か)かなければならない。
I promised a reply, so I have to write it before the day is out.
</pre>

Both are socially motivated, and both belong to spoken Japanese, but they point in
opposite directions: one asks to be excused, the other admits there is no excuse
available. They are not interchangeable in either direction.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 駅(えき)の<b>手前(てまえ)</b>で待(ま)ち合(あ)わせよう。(meaning "outside the station") → ✅ 駅(えき)の<b>前(まえ)</b>で待(ま)ち合(あ)わせよう。
  <code>手前(てまえ)</code> puts your friend somewhere short of the station with
  nothing to stand next to.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、出(で)かけられない。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っている<b>ので</b>、出(で)かけられない。
  Rain has no opinion of you. The social sense needs an audience or a statement you
  made, not a physical cause.

- ❌ 約束(やくそく)した<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、とても嬉(うれ)しいです。 → ✅ 約束(やくそく)した<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、断(ことわ)るわけにはいきません。
  The second half has to be an obligation or an inability. A pleasant feeling has
  no face to save.

- ❌ <b>手前(てまえ)</b>はどう思(おも)いますか。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんはどう思(おも)いますか。
  As a pronoun this word is either archaic-humble ("I") or, contracted to
  <code>てめえ</code>, an insult. Never use it for "you".

## Practice

**1.** 自分(じぶん)が決(き)めた____、文句(もんく)を言(い)うわけにはいかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自分(じぶん)が決(き)めた<b>手前(てまえ)</b>

The speaker made the decision publicly, so complaining now would look bad -
appearances, not logic, are doing the work. <code>わけにはいかない</code> is the
classic second half.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 次(つぎ)の角(かど)の（前(まえ)・手前(てまえ)）で止(と)めてください。ちょうど家(いえ)の入口(いりぐち)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

次(つぎ)の角(かど)の<b>手前(てまえ)</b>で止(と)めてください。

The entrance is on the near side, so you want the driver to stop before reaching
the corner. <code>角(かど)の前(まえ)</code> would be an odd thing to say at
all, since a corner has no front.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I told everyone I'd win, so I can't skip training."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

みんなに勝(か)つと言(い)った<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)むわけにはいかない。

The boast was made in front of people, which is exactly the setting for
<code>手前(てまえ)</code>, and skipping training would expose it. Note the た-form
before the pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)の手前(てまえ)、会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)くなりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)の手前(てまえ)、途中(とちゅう)で帰(かえ)るわけにはいきませんでした。

The original gives a result that has nothing to do with saving face - meetings do
not run long because the boss is present. <code>手前(てまえ)</code> needs a
second half about what the speaker could not bring themselves to do.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 先輩(せんぱい)の<b>手前(てまえ)</b>、先(さき)に帰(かえ)れなかった。
(b) 先輩(せんぱい)が<b>いたので</b>、先(さき)に帰(かえ)れなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says it was a question of how it would look. The senior colleague was there,
leaving first would have seemed rude, and the speaker stayed for appearances -
usually said with a small sigh.

(b) simply reports a cause and leaves the reason unstated. It could mean the same
thing, but it could equally mean the senior colleague had the car keys.
<code>手前(てまえ)</code> is what makes the social pressure explicit.

</details>

# ～もので

**Meaning** · because; the thing is…; you see - a reason offered as an explanation or an excuse, with a request for the listener's understanding folded into it

`もので` is not a neutral "because". It hands the listener a circumstance and asks
them to let something go: your lateness, your ignorance, your failure to reply. The
speaker is not analysing a cause; they are managing a small social debt. That is
why it lives in apologies, and why the second half is always a *state of affairs*,
never an order or an invitation.

## Formation

The clause in front takes plain, noun-modifying form - the same shape
<code>もの</code> always demands, because it is a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + もので | 遅(おく)れた → 遅(おく)れたもので |
| い-adjective | plain form + もので | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいもので |
| な-adjective | ～な + もので | 不慣(ふな)れな → 不慣(ふな)れなもので |
| Noun | ～な + もので | 初(はじ)めて → 初(はじ)めてなもので |
| Polite | … + ものですから | 急(いそ)いでいた → 急(いそ)いでいたものですから |
| Casual contraction | … + もんで | 寝坊(ねぼう)した → 寝坊(ねぼう)したもんで |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the hard restriction. The main clause may not be a command, a request,
an invitation, a suggestion or a statement of the speaker's will.
<code>忙(いそが)しいもので、手伝(てつだ)ってください</code> is
ungrammatical - and this is the single most common error learners make with the
pattern. If you want to ask for something, use `ので`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
電車(でんしゃ)が遅(おく)れた<b>もので</b>、遅刻(ちこく)してしまいました。
The train was delayed, so I'm afraid I'm late.
</pre>

<pre>
初(はじ)めてな<b>もので</b>、やり方(かた)がよく分(わ)かりません。
It's my first time, you see, so I'm not really sure how it's done.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)いでいた<b>もので</b>、あいさつもせずに失礼(しつれい)しました。
I was in a rush, so I left without even saying goodbye - sorry about that.
</pre>

<pre>
目(め)が悪(わる)い<b>もので</b>、後(うし)ろの席(せき)だとよく見(み)えないんです。
My eyesight isn't good, so I can't see well from the back row.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、道(みち)が分(わ)からなかった<b>もんで</b>遅(おく)れました。
Sorry - couldn't find the place, that's why I'm late.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが熱(ねつ)を出(だ)した<b>ものですから</b>、昨日(きのう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)らせていただきました。
My child had a fever, so I took the liberty of leaving early yesterday.
</pre>

<pre>
あまりに静(しず)かだった<b>もので</b>、誰(だれ)もいないのかと思(おも)いました。
It was so quiet that I assumed there was nobody in.
</pre>

<pre>
田舎(いなか)から出(で)てきたばかりな<b>もので</b>、東京(とうきょう)の電車(でんしゃ)はまだ苦手(にがて)なんです。
I've only just moved up from the countryside, so Tokyo trains are still beyond me.
</pre>

<pre>
前(まえ)の会議(かいぎ)が長引(ながび)いた<b>ものですから</b>、こちらへ伺(うかが)うのが遅(おそ)くなりました。
My previous meeting overran, which is why I've come to see you so late.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is spoken Japanese doing social work. <code>ものですから</code> is the polite
form and is standard in business apologies; `もので` is a touch lighter and
extremely common in ordinary courteous speech; `もんで` is the casual contraction
and is at home among friends and colleagues you are relaxed with. All three
frequently trail off unfinished - <code>すみません、急(いそ)いでいたもので…</code>
- and that unfinished quality is part of the politeness. The listener supplies the
rest, which saves the speaker having to spell out their own excuse.

Now the honest part. Because the pattern exists to soften, it also exists to
*deflect*, and Japanese listeners hear that. Someone who answers every question
with <code>ものですから</code> sounds evasive: the form keeps producing
circumstances that were not their fault, and after the third one the listener
starts to notice that no apology has actually been made. In business Japanese the
safe sequence is to apologise first and explain second -
<code>申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっていたものですから</code>
- not the reverse. Leading with the excuse and never arriving
at the apology is precisely the pattern that reads as making excuses, in Japanese
as in English.

One more limit worth internalising: `もので` presents a reason from the speaker's
point of view, so it is a poor fit for objective causation. Physics, statistics
and mechanisms take <code>ため</code>. A washing machine does not appeal to your
understanding.

## Contrast

### ～もので vs ～ものだから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same construction at different volumes. `もので` is
reserved and apologetic; <code>ものだから</code> is emphatic, and pushes the reason
at the listener.

</div>

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>もので</b>、朝食(ちょうしょく)を食(た)べずに出(で)てきました。
I overslept, so I came out without breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
目覚(めざ)まし時計(どけい)が鳴(な)らなかった<b>ものだから</b>、完全(かんぜん)に寝過(ねす)ごしてしまって。
The alarm didn't go off, and I mean I completely slept through.
</pre>

<code>ものだから</code> often carries surprise or agitation about the reason
itself, and it is the one you hear when somebody is defending themselves with some
heat. `もので` keeps its voice down. The politeness ranking runs roughly
<code>もんで</code> → <code>ものだから</code> → `もので` →
<code>ものですから</code>, though this is a gradient people feel rather than a rule
they obey.

### ～もので vs ～ので・～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ので` will carry any main clause you like, including requests
and decisions. `もので` will carry only a statement of how things are or were.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は早(はや)い<b>ので</b>、そろそろ失礼(しつれい)します。
I've an early start tomorrow, so I'll be making a move.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)が早(はや)い<b>もので</b>、先(さき)に失礼(しつれい)してしまいました。
I had an early start the next day, so I ended up leaving before the others.
</pre>

The first announces a decision; the second explains a completed act. Push a request
through `もので` and the sentence collapses:
<code>忙(いそが)しいもので、手伝(てつだ)ってください</code> is not Japanese.
There is also a difference of stance - `ので` states a reason, while `もので` asks
you to accept one - which is why a colleague's flat
<code>時間(じかん)がないので</code> and their softer
<code>時間(じかん)がないもので</code> land differently even though the facts are
identical.

### ～もので vs ～もの・～もん (sentence-final)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もので` connects a reason to a consequence.
Sentence-final <code>もの</code> or <code>もん</code> *is* the whole excuse, and it
sounds childish or petulant.

</div>

<pre>
だって、知(し)らなかった<b>もん</b>。
But I didn't know!
</pre>

<pre>
知(し)らなかった<b>もので</b>、ご迷惑(めいわく)をおかけしました。
I wasn't aware, and I'm sorry for the trouble it caused.
</pre>

The first is what a child says, and what adults say jokingly or when genuinely
sulking; <code>だって…もん</code> is a fixed pair. The second is what you say to a
client. They share an ancestor and nothing else - do not reach for the first at
work.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 忙(いそが)しい<b>もので</b>、手伝(てつだ)ってください。 → ✅ 忙(いそが)しい<b>ので</b>、手伝(てつだ)ってください。
  No requests, commands or invitations after `もので`. This restriction is absolute.

- ❌ 気温(きおん)が下(さ)がった<b>もので</b>、水(みず)が凍(こお)った。 → ✅ 気温(きおん)が下(さ)がった<b>ため</b>、水(みず)が凍(こお)った。
  Objective cause and effect takes <code>ため</code>. `もので` needs a speaker with
  a stake in being understood.

- ❌ 初(はじ)めて<b>もので</b>、うまくできません。 → ✅ 初(はじ)めてな<b>もので</b>、うまくできません。
  <code>もの</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it links with な - exactly as
  in <code>初(はじ)めてなのです</code>.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>もので</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)でしょう。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>ので</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)でしょう。
  `もので` explains something that has already happened or is already the case. It
  does not reason forward from a forecast.

## Practice

**1.** 昨日(きのう)は熱(ねつ)があった____、会(かい)を欠席(けっせき)してしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

熱(ねつ)があった<b>もので</b>（or <b>ものですから</b>）

The speaker is excusing an absence and asking the listener to accept the reason,
which is exactly what `もので` is for. The main clause reports what happened, so the
restriction on commands and requests is respected.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 外(そと)は寒(さむ)い（もので・ので）、上着(うわぎ)を持(も)っていってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The main clause is a request, and `もので` cannot support one. This is the
restriction to memorise; everything else about the two words is close enough to

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "I'm sorry for the late reply - I was away on a business trip."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Apology first, explanation second - that order is what keeps the excuse from
sounding like a dodge. <code>ものですから</code> is the polite form, and trailing

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 説明(せつめい)が下手(へた)もので、うまく伝(つた)わらなかったかもしれません。

<code>下手(へた)</code> is a な-adjective, and <code>もの</code> is a noun, so な

<code>新人(しんじん)なもので</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 道(みち)が込(こ)んでいた<b>もので</b>、遅(おく)れました。
(b) 道(みち)が込(こ)んでいた<b>ものだから</b>、遅(おく)れました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the quieter apology. The traffic is offered as a circumstance, and the
speaker keeps their voice down; it is what you would say to a client or a teacher.

(b) presses harder. <code>ものだから</code> puts feeling behind the reason - "the
traffic was *awful*, that's why" - and depending on tone it can come across as
defending yourself rather than apologising. Between friends it is perfectly
natural; to someone you have kept waiting, (a) is safer.

</details>

# ～ことだし

**Meaning** · what with X (among other things); since X is the case - one reason of several, offered as grounds for a decision or a suggestion

`ことだし` names a circumstance and then proposes something on the strength of it.
Two features define it. The reason is presented as **one among several**, not the
whole story - which is why も so often appears inside the clause. And the second
half is a **decision, invitation or suggestion**, never a report of what happened.
It is the sentence you use to talk a group gently into leaving early.

## Formation

The clause takes plain, noun-modifying form, because <code>こと</code> is a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ことだし | やんだ → やんだことだし |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことだし | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないことだし |
| い-adjective | plain form + ことだし | 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)いことだし |
| な-adjective | ～な + ことだし | 元気(げんき)な → 元気(げんき)なことだし |
| Noun | ～である + ことだし | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みであることだし (uncommon) |
| Typical shape | ～も … + ことだし | 遅(おそ)い → 時間(じかん)も遅(おそ)いことだし |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun row is stiff and rarely worth using. In speech people simply
say <code>休(やす)みだし</code>. Verbs and adjectives carry almost every real
example of this pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)もやんだ<b>ことだし</b>、そろそろ出(で)かけよう。
The rain's stopped, among other things - let's get going.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)も遅(おそ)い<b>ことだし</b>、今日(きょう)はこの辺(へん)で終(お)わりにしましょう。
It's getting late, so let's call it a day here.
</pre>

<pre>
せっかく集(あつ)まった<b>ことだし</b>、写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)りませんか。
Since we've all managed to get together, shall we take a photo?
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)も入(はい)った<b>ことだし</b>、たまには外(そと)で食(た)べようか。
Payday's been and gone - shall we eat out for once?
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)もいい<b>ことだし</b>、窓(まど)を開(あ)けて風(かぜ)を入(い)れませんか。
It's a nice day - why don't we open the windows and let some air in?
</pre>

<pre>
体調(たいちょう)も戻(もど)った<b>ことだし</b>、来週(らいしゅう)から仕事(しごと)に復帰(ふっき)します。
I'm back on my feet now, so I'll be returning to work from next week.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)も大学(だいがく)を出(で)た<b>ことだし</b>、そろそろ夫婦(ふうふ)で旅行(りょこう)でもしようと思(おも)っています。
Our son's finished university, so we're thinking of taking a trip, just the two of us.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)もだいたい終(お)わった<b>ことだし</b>、明日(あした)は少(すこ)し早(はや)めに集合(しゅうごう)して最終(さいしゅう)チェックだけしましょう。
The preparations are more or less done, so let's meet a bit earlier tomorrow and just do a final check.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)も承知(しょうち)している<b>ことだし</b>、この案(あん)で進(すす)めてしまってかまわないでしょう。
The manager knows about it, so I don't see any problem with going ahead on this plan.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ことだし` is spoken Japanese, and it is polite in a particular way: it makes a
proposal look like a natural consequence of facts everybody can see, rather than
something the speaker wants. That is genuinely useful. Suggesting the meeting end
with <code>時間(じかん)も遅(おそ)いことだし</code> is much easier to accept -
and much easier to refuse - than announcing that you would like to go home.

The も is doing real work. It signals "this reason, and others I'm not listing",
which is why the pattern feels considerate rather than pushy. It also suits
speakers who do not want to be seen deciding on their own: a chairperson, a host, a
parent proposing something to the family. There is a faintly mature, settled tone
to it; you hear it more from adults arranging things than from students making
plans.

The same softness has a shadow side, and it is worth naming. Because `ことだし`
implies unstated further reasons, it can be used to dress up a decision that was
already made and give it the appearance of following from circumstances. Piled up -
<code>時間(じかん)も遅(おそ)いことだし、雨(あめ)も降(ふ)っていることだし</code>
- it starts to sound like someone assembling justifications, and a
listener may reasonably suspect the conclusion came first. One `ことだし` per
suggestion is plenty.

Register is plain-to-polite; both
<code>行(い)こう</code> and <code>行(い)きましょう</code> follow it happily.
It is not used in formal writing, where <code>ため</code> or
<code>以上(いじょう)</code> would appear instead.

## Contrast

### ～ことだし vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` and `ので` give *the* reason. `ことだし` gives *a* reason,
one of several, and only ever leads into a decision or a proposal.

</div>

<pre>
寒(さむ)い<b>ので</b>、エアコンをつけました。
I put the air conditioning on because it was cold.
</pre>

<pre>
寒(さむ)くなってきた<b>ことだし</b>、そろそろこたつを出(だ)そうか。
It's turning cold - shall we get the kotatsu out?
</pre>

The first reports what the speaker did and why; nothing is being proposed. The
second invites agreement. Put a plain past-tense report after `ことだし` and the
sentence stalls, because the pattern is built to point forward at a choice.

### ～ことだし vs ～し

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `し` lists reasons casually and can stack.
`ことだし` picks one representative reason and presents it a little more
deliberately.

</div>

<pre>
眠(ねむ)い<b>し</b>、明日(あした)も早(はや)いし、もう寝(ね)るよ。
I'm sleepy, and I've an early start tomorrow, so I'm off to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)も早(はや)い<b>ことだし</b>、そろそろ失礼(しつれい)いたします。
I've an early start tomorrow, so I'll take my leave.
</pre>

They mean much the same thing. `し` is the everyday, slightly breezy version and
is what you say to a friend; `ことだし` is a shade more composed and fits the moment
when you are excusing yourself from someone else's party. Note that `ことだし` can
be built on `し` - it is literally <code>こと</code> plus `だ` plus `し` - which is
why the two feel related.

### ～ことだし vs ～もので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - mirror images. `ことだし` must be followed by a proposal or a
decision; `もので` must **not** be.

</div>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)も届(とど)いた<b>ことだし</b>、作業(さぎょう)を始(はじ)めましょう。
The delivery's arrived, so let's get started.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)が届(とど)かなかった<b>もので</b>、作業(さぎょう)が始(はじ)められませんでした。
The delivery didn't arrive, so we weren't able to start.
</pre>

Both are socially smooth reason clauses, and both are spoken rather than written,
but they face opposite ways: `ことだし` opens a door, `もので` apologises for one
that stayed shut. Swapping them produces sentences that are not merely odd but
ungrammatical.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>ことだし</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った<b>ので</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。
  The second half reports what happened. `ことだし` needs a decision the speaker is
  making now.

- ❌ 忙(いそが)しかった<b>ことだし</b>、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れてすみません。 → ✅ 忙(いそが)しかった<b>もので</b>、返事(へんじ)が遅(おく)れてすみません。
  Apologising for something you failed to do is `もので` territory. `ことだし` does
  not excuse; it proposes.

- ❌ 元気(げんき)<b>ことだし</b>、散歩(さんぽ)に行(い)きましょう。 → ✅ 元気(げんき)<b>なことだし</b>、散歩(さんぽ)に行(い)きましょう。
  <code>こと</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective needs its な.

- ❌ 台風(たいふう)も来(く)る<b>ことだし</b>、明日(あした)は休(やす)みになるでしょう。 → ✅ 台風(たいふう)も来(く)る<b>ことだし</b>、明日(あした)は休(やす)みにしましょう。
  A guess about what will happen is not a decision. Turn it into one and the
  sentence works.

## Practice

**1.** みんな集(あつ)まった____、そろそろ始(はじ)めましょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

みんな集(あつ)まった<b>ことだし</b>

Everyone being present is one of the reasons to begin, and the second half is a
suggestion - the two halves `ことだし` requires. Adding も
(<code>みんなも集(あつ)まったことだし</code>) makes the "among other things"
feeling explicit.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 寝坊(ねぼう)した（ことだし・もので）、朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べる時間(じかん)がありませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

寝坊(ねぼう)した<b>もので</b>、朝(あさ)ご飯(はん)を食(た)べる時間(じかん)がありませんでした。

This is an excuse for something that already went wrong, and the main clause is a
plain report. `ことだし` would need a proposal after it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The kids are asleep, so why don't we watch a film?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

子(こ)どもたちも寝(ね)た<b>ことだし</b>、映画(えいが)でも見(み)ようか。

The children being asleep is the visible reason, も hints that there are others,
and <code>でも</code> plus the volitional keeps the suggestion light. This is
`ことだし` in its most typical setting.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 準備(じゅんび)も終(お)わったことだし、昨日(きのう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 準備(じゅんび)も終(お)わった<b>ことだし</b>、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)りましょう。

`ことだし` points forward to a decision, so a past-tense report of yesterday
cannot follow it. Either change the second half to a proposal, as here, or switch
the connective to <code>ので</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 暗(くら)くなってきた<b>ことだし</b>、そろそろ帰(かえ)りましょう。
(b) 暗(くら)くなってきた<b>から</b>、そろそろ帰(かえ)りましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the fading light as one of several good reasons and lets the
suggestion arise from the situation rather than from the speaker. It is the softer,
more considerate version, and easy for the others to agree with.

(b) states the reason directly and is perfectly polite, but the speaker is more
visibly the one deciding. Between friends there is nothing wrong with it; when you
are the guest, or when you would rather not look like you are hurrying everyone
along, (a) does the job more gracefully.

</details>
