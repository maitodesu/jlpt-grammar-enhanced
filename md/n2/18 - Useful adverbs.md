# N2 - Useful adverbs

# いきなり

**Meaning** · out of nowhere, with no warning and no lead-up

`いきなり` is not just "suddenly". It says that the step which normally comes
first - the greeting, the explanation, the warm-up - was skipped entirely. That
missing run-up is why the word so often sounds like a complaint.

## Usage

`いきなり` is an adverb. It sits in front of the verb, or at the head of the
clause when it colours the whole event, and it never changes shape.

It attaches most naturally to things a *person* does without preparing you for
them:

- いきなり大声(おおごえ)を出(だ)す - to shout with no warning
- いきなり本題(ほんだい)に入(はい)る - to launch straight into the main point
- いきなり立(た)ち上(あ)がる - to jump to one's feet

Because the action is usually done *to* the speaker, the passive is extremely
common: いきなり呼(よ)ばれる, いきなり怒(おこ)られる,
いきなり話(はな)しかけられる.

A second, calmer use has no surprise in it at all - it simply means *straight to
the last step, skipping the ones in between*: 初日(しょにち)にいきなり
一人(ひとり)で店(みせ)を任(まか)された, "on day one they had me running the
shop by myself".

In casual speech it can also modify a noun with `の`: いきなりの雨(あめ),
いきなりの変更(へんこう). That is colloquial; written Japanese prefers
<code>突然(とつぜん)の</code>. There is no ❌いきなりな - the word is not a
な-adjective.

## Examples

<pre>
となりの部屋(へや)のドアが<b>いきなり</b>開(あ)いた。
The door of the next room flew open with no warning.
</pre>

<pre>
後(うし)ろから<b>いきなり</b>名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれて、びっくりした。
Someone called my name from behind out of nowhere and it startled me.
</pre>

<pre>
準備運動(じゅんびうんどう)もせずに<b>いきなり</b>走(はし)り出(だ)すと、足(あし)をいためますよ。
If you start running cold with no warm-up, you'll hurt your legs.
</pre>

<pre>
前置(まえお)きもなく<b>いきなり</b>数字(すうじ)の話(はなし)を始(はじ)められても、何(なん)の会議(かいぎ)か分(わ)かりません。
When you open straight onto the figures with no preamble, nobody can tell what the meeting is even about.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Colloquial and very frequent. It is at home in conversation, chat messages and
casual writing; in a news report or a formal document the word used is
<code>突然(とつぜん)</code> or <code>急(きゅう)に</code> instead. It is not
marked for age or gender.

The attitude it carries is worth learning together with the meaning. Nine times
out of ten, `いきなり` is mildly aggrieved: something was done to the speaker in
the wrong order, and the sentence is a small protest about it. Reporting your own
action with it - いきなり聞(き)いてすみません - is therefore a way of
apologising for the abruptness before anyone can object.

Because the complaint is about manners rather than about the event itself, the
word sits badly on grave news. For a death, an accident or an illness,
<code>突然(とつぜん)</code> is the word; `いきなり` would sound flippant.

## Contrast

### いきなり vs 突然(とつぜん)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>突然(とつぜん)</code> says the **event** was
unexpected. `いきなり` says the **order** was wrong: the thing that should have
come first never happened.

</div>

<pre>
<b>突然(とつぜん)</b>停電(ていでん)になり、ビル全体(ぜんたい)が暗(くら)くなった。
The power went out all at once and the whole building went dark.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)は<b>いきなり</b>来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)に行(い)けと言(い)ってきた。
My boss sprang it on me that I'm going on a business trip next week.
</pre>

A power cut has no manners to violate, so <code>突然(とつぜん)</code> fits and
`いきなり` would be odd. The instruction from the boss could have been raised in
advance and was not, which is exactly what `いきなり` objects to.

<code>突然(とつぜん)</code> is also the more written and more dramatic of the
two, and unlike `いきなり` it forms a noun modifier freely:
<code>突然(とつぜん)の訪問(ほうもん)</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 祖母(そぼ)がいきなり亡(な)くなりました。 → ✅ 祖母(そぼ)が<b>突然(とつぜん)</b>亡(な)くなりました。
  `いきなり` complains about a missing run-up, which makes it sound casual about a
  death. Use <code>突然(とつぜん)</code>.

- ❌ いきなりな質問(しつもん)ですみません。 → ✅ <b>いきなりの</b>質問(しつもん)ですみません。／<b>突然(とつぜん)の</b>質問(しつもん)ですみません。
  `いきなり` is an adverb, not a な-adjective. Before a noun it takes `の`, and in
  polite writing <code>突然(とつぜん)の</code> is safer.

- ❌ すみませんが、いきなり来(き)てください。 → ✅ すみませんが、<b>すぐに</b>来(き)てください。
  `いきなり` is never "right away" as an instruction. Asking someone to arrive
  without warning is not what you meant.

## Practice

**1.** ドラマの途中(とちゅう)で____画面(がめん)が真(ま)っ黒(くろ)になって、音(おと)も消(き)えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

途中(とちゅう)で<b>いきなり</b>画面(がめん)が真(ま)っ黒(くろ)になって…

The complaint is that the programme gave no sign it was about to cut out.
<code>突然(とつぜん)</code> would also be grammatical here; `いきなり` is the more
colloquial choice and adds the note of irritation.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 会議(かいぎ)の日(ひ)が（いきなり・突然(とつぜん)）変(か)わったと部長(ぶちょう)にメールで知(し)らされた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are possible, but <b>いきなり</b> is the one a colleague would actually say:
the date could have been discussed first and was not, so the missing consultation
is the point. <code>突然(とつぜん)</code> would frame the change as an event that
simply befell everyone, which is what you would write in a formal notice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He walked in without knocking and started talking about money."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ノックもせずに入(はい)ってきて、<b>いきなり</b>お金(かね)の話(はなし)を始(はじ)めた。

Two skipped preliminaries - the knock and any small talk - so `いきなり` is exactly
right. Note that it goes in front of the abrupt action itself, not at the very
head of the sentence.

</details>

# いよいよ

**Meaning** · at last - the moment everyone has been building towards is here

`いよいよ` points forward. It marks the threshold of something long awaited or
long dreaded: the day has come, the thing is about to start. A second, weaker
sense is "more and more", used of a change that keeps intensifying.

## Usage

`いよいよ` is an adverb and its position is free. Most often it opens the clause,
and it very commonly stands almost alone as a short remark:
<code>いよいよですね</code>, "well, this is it".

Two senses, and they behave differently.

**1. The awaited moment.** The predicate is imminent or has just arrived, so
`いよいよ` clusters with future and near-future expressions - いよいよ明日(あした)だ,
いよいよ来週(らいしゅう)始(はじ)まる, いよいよ本番(ほんばん)だ. It needs a
run-up behind it: preparation, waiting, a countdown. Without one there is nothing
for the word to be the culmination *of*.

**2. Increasingly.** With a verb of change, `いよいよ` means the process is going
further still: 雨(あめ)がいよいよ激(はげ)しくなる, 状況(じょうきょう)が
いよいよ悪(わる)くなる. This use is more written than spoken, and ますます is
the everyday word for it.

One set phrase is worth learning whole: <code>いよいよという時(とき)</code> - "when
it really comes to the crunch". The commoner synonym for that phrase is
<code>いざという時(とき)</code>.

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)から<b>いよいよ</b>夏休(なつやす)みだ。
The summer holidays start tomorrow at last.
</pre>

<pre>
長(なが)かった工事(こうじ)が<b>いよいよ</b>来月(らいげつ)終(お)わります。
The construction work that has dragged on so long finally finishes next month.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)練習(れんしゅう)してきて、<b>いよいよ</b>決勝(けっしょう)の舞台(ぶたい)に立(た)つ。
After three years of training, they are finally taking the stage in the final.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)は<b>いよいよ</b>風(かぜ)が強(つよ)くなってきたので、そろそろ帰(かえ)ったほうがいい。
The wind outside is getting stronger and stronger, so we ought to head home soon.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It appears in conversation, in speeches and in writing, and
carries no politeness of its own.

What it always carries is *tension*. The feeling can be excitement
（<code>いよいよ試合(しあい)だ</code>）or dread
（<code>いよいよ順番(じゅんばん)が回(まわ)ってきた</code>）, but never
indifference. A flat piece of scheduling - the meeting is at three - has no build-up
behind it, so `いよいよ` there sounds theatrical.

Because sense 1 looks forward, it pairs badly with a completed result. Once the
thing is over you have moved past the threshold, and the word for that is
ついに or やっと.

## Contrast

### いよいよ vs ついに・とうとう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いよいよ` stands **at the threshold** and looks forward;
`ついに` and `とうとう` stand **on the far side** and look back at the outcome.

</div>

<pre>
<b>いよいよ</b>来週(らいしゅう)、新(あたら)しい店(みせ)が開(ひら)く。
Next week, at last, the new shop opens.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)かかって、<b>ついに</b>新(あたら)しい店(みせ)を持(も)った。
After ten years, he finally got a shop of his own.
</pre>

`ついに` reports that the long process reached its end - the result is in hand.
`とうとう` does much the same job, but it leans towards outcomes the speaker did
not want: <code>とうとう最後(さいご)まで返事(へんじ)は来(こ)なかった</code>,
"in the end no reply ever came".

Because of that split, the tense usually gives the choice away: `いよいよ` with a
future or a just-now-arriving event, `ついに`・`とうとう` with a past one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 宿題(しゅくだい)がいよいよ終(お)わった。 → ✅ 宿題(しゅくだい)が<b>やっと</b>終(お)わった。
  The work is already finished, so there is no threshold left to stand on.
  `やっと` or `ついに` reports a completed relief.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)はいよいよ三時(さんじ)からです。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)からです。
  Plain scheduling information has no build-up behind it. `いよいよ` would imply the
  whole office had been counting the days.

- ❌ 道(みち)を歩(ある)いていたら、いよいよ雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた。 → ✅ 道(みち)を歩(ある)いていたら、<b>いきなり</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた。
  `いよいよ` is the opposite of a surprise: it needs something anticipated. An
  unexpected shower calls for `いきなり` or <code>急(きゅう)に</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 半年(はんとし)準備(じゅんび)してきた発表(はっぴょう)の日(ひ)だ。____本番(ほんばん)だね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>いよいよ</b>本番(ほんばん)だね。

Six months of preparation supply the run-up, and the event itself is still ahead,
which is precisely the position `いよいよ` describes. `ついに` would suggest the
presentation was already over.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 三度目(さんどめ)の挑戦(ちょうせん)で、（いよいよ・ついに）合格(ごうかく)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三度目(さんどめ)の挑戦(ちょうせん)で、<b>ついに</b>合格(ごうかく)した。

The result is already in: the exam has been passed. `ついに` looks back at the
culmination of the three attempts, while `いよいよ` would have to be followed by
something still to come, such as
<code>いよいよ明日(あした)発表(はっぴょう)だ</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) いよいよ冬(ふゆ)だ。
(b) もう冬(ふゆ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is an observation: winter has arrived, perhaps sooner than expected. Nothing
was being waited for.

(a) frames the season as an arrival that has been visible on the horizon for a
while - the cold mornings, the shorter days - and the speaker is now stepping into
it. `いよいよ` adds anticipation, whether the speaker is looking forward to the snow
or bracing for the heating bill.

</details>

# やがて

**Meaning** · before long; in time - a change that time itself will bring about

`やがて` watches something unfold at a distance. It says that, left to run its
course, the situation will reach a certain point. It is a narrator's word, and it
belongs to writing far more than to conversation.

## Usage

`やがて` is an adverb of time. It usually opens the clause or stands immediately
before the predicate, and it does not change shape.

The one restriction that matters: **`やがて` describes what happens on its own, not
what anybody decides to do.** It therefore does not appear with

- your own intentions - ❌やがて連絡(れんらく)します
- requests or commands - ❌やがて来(き)てください
- a named point in time - ❌やがて来週(らいしゅう)届(とど)きます

What it does take is a change that arrives by itself:
やがて春(はる)が来(く)る, やがて日(ひ)が暮(く)れる,
やがて忘(わす)れられる, やがて分(わ)かるだろう.

In written style it also attaches to a length of time, meaning "coming up on":
<code>日本(にほん)に来(き)てやがて十年(じゅうねん)になる</code>, "it will
soon be ten years since I came to Japan".

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)は<b>やがて</b>雪(ゆき)に変(か)わった。
Before long the rain turned to snow.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちの声(こえ)は<b>やがて</b>聞(き)こえなくなった。
In time the children's voices faded out of earshot.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)は苦(くる)しくても、この経験(けいけん)は<b>やがて</b>君(きみ)の力(ちから)になるはずだ。
It's hard now, but this experience will become your strength in time.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)の小(ちい)さな工場(こうじょう)は、<b>やがて</b>町(まち)を代表(だいひょう)する会社(かいしゃ)へと成長(せいちょう)していった。
The little workshop by the station grew, in time, into the firm that came to represent the town.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and narrative. `やがて` is the language of novels, essays, documentaries
and history writing, and it sounds noticeably literary in ordinary chat - a bit as
though you were reading your own life aloud. Speakers reach for そのうち,
もうすぐ or まもなく instead.

Its emotional colour is a quiet distance. The speaker is standing outside the
process, watching it take its course, which is why it suits both consolation
（<code>やがて時間(じかん)が解決(かいけつ)してくれる</code>）and resignation
（<code>やがて誰(だれ)も覚(おぼ)えていないだろう</code>）.

Note that `やがて` is vague about the interval. It can cover minutes in a piece of
narration and decades in a history book; the sentence around it, not the word,
tells you which.

## Contrast

### やがて vs まもなく・そのうち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まもなく` is **imminent and factual**, `そのうち` is
**vague and conversational**, `やがて` is **narrative**: the natural course of
things, seen from outside.

</div>

<pre>
<b>まもなく</b>三番線(さんばんせん)に電車(でんしゃ)がまいります。
The train will shortly be arriving at platform three.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>そのうち</b>ごはんでも食(た)べに行(い)こうよ。
Let's go and grab a meal one of these days.
</pre>

`まもなく` is the word of announcements: minutes away, and stated as fact.
`そのうち` is the only one of the three that happily carries the speaker's own
plans, which is exactly what `やがて` refuses to do. And `やがて` is the only one
that can span years without sounding strange.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ やがて電話(でんわ)しますね。 → ✅ <b>そのうち</b>電話(でんわ)しますね。
  A promise is something you decide, and `やがて` cannot carry a decision. It only
  reports what time will bring.

- ❌ やがて準備(じゅんび)ができますので、お待(ま)ちください。 → ✅ <b>まもなく</b>準備(じゅんび)ができますので、お待(ま)ちください。
  Telling a waiting customer something is nearly ready needs the announcement word
  `まもなく`. `やがて` would sound like the opening line of a novel.

- ❌ やがて三時(さんじ)に会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる。 → ✅ 三時(さんじ)に会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まる。
  `やがて` is deliberately vague about when. Pinning it to a clock time contradicts
  the word.

## Practice

**1.** 騒(さわ)いでいた子(こ)どもたちも、____眠(ねむ)ってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

騒(さわ)いでいた子(こ)どもたちも、<b>やがて</b>眠(ねむ)ってしまった。

Nobody made them sleep; the noise simply ran itself out. That is precisely the
kind of change `やがて` narrates, and the past tense here reads as storytelling
rather than as a report.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)、やがて資料(しりょう)をお送(おく)りします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 明日(あした)、資料(しりょう)をお送(おく)りします。／のちほど資料(しりょう)をお送(おく)りします。

Two problems at once: `やがて` cannot express the writer's own intention, and it
cannot sit beside a definite time word like <code>明日(あした)</code>. In a
business email the vague-but-polite word is `のちほど`.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) そのうち分(わ)かるよ。
(b) やがて分(わ)かるだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is something you say to a friend: light, spoken, and faintly teasing - you'll
work it out sooner or later.

(b) reads as narration or as a line with some weight behind it. `やがて` plus
<code>だろう</code> steps back from the listener and treats the understanding as
something time will deliver, which is why it turns up at the end of chapters and
rarely over coffee.

</details>

# とっくに

**Meaning** · already, long since - and a good deal earlier than you seem to think

`とっくに` is `もう` with an attitude. It does not just report that something has
happened; it corrects an assumption that it has not, and it insists that the event
is far back in the past.

## Usage

`とっくに` is an adverb and goes in front of the predicate. `もう` often precedes
it for emphasis: <code>もうとっくに終(お)わっている</code>.

The shape of the predicate is the thing to learn. `とっくに` looks **backwards**,
so it takes

- a plain past - とっくに帰(かえ)った
- a resultative <code>～ている</code> - とっくに過(す)ぎている, とっくに着(つ)いている
- <code>～てしまった</code>・<code>～ちゃった</code> - とっくに売(う)り切(き)れてしまった

It cannot take a plain future: ❌とっくに始(はじ)まります is not Japanese.

Negatives are possible where the negative itself describes a long-standing state:
<code>あの店(みせ)はとっくにないよ</code>, "that shop closed ages ago".

Two variants you will meet: <code>とっくの昔(むかし)に</code>, an emphatic "ages
and ages ago", and `とうに`, the same word in a more literary key.

## Examples

<pre>
バスは<b>とっくに</b>行(い)ってしまった。
The bus left ages ago.
</pre>

<pre>
その話(はなし)なら<b>とっくに</b>知(し)っているよ。
I've known about that for ages.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りは<b>とっくに</b>過(す)ぎていますが、まだ半分(はんぶん)しか書(か)けていません。
The deadline went by long ago and I've still only written half of it.
</pre>

<pre>
心配(しんぱい)しなくていいよ。息子(むすこ)は<b>とっくに</b>家(いえ)に着(つ)いて、ごはんまで食(た)べ終(お)わってる。
You don't need to worry - our son got home long ago and has even finished his dinner.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Colloquial. `とっくに` belongs to speech and to casual writing; a report or a
business email uses <code>既(すで)に</code>.

It is also faintly blunt. Because the word implies "obviously, and long before
now", replying <code>とっくに終(お)わっています</code> to a manager who has just
asked about your work can land as impatient - "why are you only asking now?" With
<code>もう終(お)わっています</code> the same fact carries no edge at all. Among
friends that edge is often the whole point, and `とっくに` is a standard way of
brushing off a warning you did not need.

The gap it implies must be a real one. Something that finished five minutes ago is
not とっくに; the word wants an interval long enough that the listener's picture of
the world has gone stale.

## Contrast

### とっくに vs 既(すで)に・もう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three mean "already". `もう` is the neutral default,
<code>既(すで)に</code> is its formal written twin, and `とっくに` adds **"far
earlier than you think"** plus the speaker's attitude.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は<b>もう</b>始(はじ)まっています。
The meeting has already started.
</pre>

<pre>
該当(がいとう)の商品(しょうひん)は<b>既(すで)に</b>販売(はんばい)を終了(しゅうりょう)しております。
Sales of the item in question have already ended.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)なら<b>とっくに</b>終(お)わって、みんな帰(かえ)ったよ。
The meeting finished ages ago - everyone's gone home.
</pre>

The first two state a fact. The third also comments on the listener: you are
behind. Note too that `もう` has a second life as "soon"
（<code>もう着(つ)きます</code>）and a third as "any more"
（<code>もう要(い)りません</code>）, neither of which `とっくに` shares.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 映画(えいが)はとっくに始(はじ)まります。 → ✅ 映画(えいが)は<b>とっくに</b>始(はじ)まっています。
  The word points into the past, so the predicate has to be past or resultative. A
  plain future contradicts it.

- ❌ ご連絡(れんらく)はとっくに受(う)け取(と)っております。 → ✅ ご連絡(れんらく)は<b>既(すで)に</b>受(う)け取(と)っております。
  Humble ます-form and a colloquial complaint do not belong in the same sentence.
  To a customer, use <code>既(すで)に</code>.

- ❌ 五分(ごふん)前(まえ)にとっくに出(で)ました。 → ✅ 五分(ごふん)前(まえ)に出(で)ました。
  Five minutes is not "long since". Naming a short interval cancels the one thing
  `とっくに` is for.

## Practice

**1.** 「宿題(しゅくだい)、そろそろやったら?」「____やったよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「<b>とっくに</b>やったよ。」

The reply is not simply reporting that the homework is done - it is pushing back
on the reminder. `とっくに` says the parent's information is out of date, which is
exactly the tone the exchange calls for.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません、その商品(しょうひん)は（とっくに・既(すで)に）完売(かんばい)いたしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

その商品(しょうひん)は<b>既(すで)に</b>完売(かんばい)いたしました。

Serving a customer, you want the neutral formal word. `とっくに` would imply the
customer should have known better, which is the opposite of an apology.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう気(き)づいていました。
(b) とっくに気(き)づいていました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement: by the time it came up, the speaker had noticed.

(b) puts a distance behind the noticing. It says the speaker worked it out long
before the other person got round to mentioning it, and depending on the voice it
can be reassuring （"don't worry, I was ahead of you"）or cutting （"you're the
last to know"）.

</details>

# ふたたび

**Meaning** · again, once more - after an interval, and usually back to how it was

Written 再(ふたた)び. This is the formal, written word for "again": news
reports, speeches, narration. Everyday speech says また.

## Usage

`ふたたび` is an adverb. It goes in front of the verb or opens the clause, and it
never changes form. The kanji spelling <code>再(ふたた)び</code> is the usual one
in print; the kana spelling is common in softer writing. Note the okurigana - the
reading covers only the kanji, and the `び` stands outside it.

Two things narrow where it can be used.

**It needs a gap.** `ふたたび` is about a return after an interruption or an
interval - <code>再(ふたた)び動(うご)き出(だ)す</code>,
<code>再(ふたた)び集(あつ)まる</code>,
<code>再(ふたた)び会(あ)う</code>. It is not the word for simple repetition;
"I read it again and again" is 何度(なんど)も読(よ)んだ.

**It cannot ask.** Requests and commands take もう一度(いちど):
❌<code>再(ふたた)び言(い)ってください</code> is wrong, and no amount of
formality makes it right.

The same kanji supplies a whole family of compounds with the same "again" sense -
再開(さいかい) (resume), 再会(さいかい) (meet again),
再利用(さいりよう) (reuse) - which is a useful hook for remembering it.

## Examples

<pre>
止(や)んでいた雨(あめ)が<b>ふたたび</b>降(ふ)り始(はじ)めた。
The rain, which had stopped, began to fall again.
</pre>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)は十年(じゅうねん)後(ご)に<b>ふたたび</b>出会(であ)った。
The two of them met again ten years later.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)で止(と)まっていた電車(でんしゃ)は、点検(てんけん)を終(お)えて<b>ふたたび</b>動(うご)き始(はじ)めた。
The trains, halted by the typhoon, started running again once the inspections were finished.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)失(うしな)った信用(しんよう)を<b>ふたたび</b>取(と)り戻(もど)すのは、簡単(かんたん)なことではない。
Winning back trust once it has been lost is no simple matter.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and written. Read `ふたたび` aloud in a casual conversation and you sound
like a newsreader; friends say また, and a request says もう一度(いちど). It is
neutral as to age and gender, but it is firmly marked for register.

It also carries a little weight of its own. Because it implies that something was
lost, stopped or ended and has now come back, it suits events the speaker treats
as significant - a reunion, a resumption, a relapse. Using it for something
trivial, <code>再(ふたた)びコーヒーを飲(の)んだ</code>, is not ungrammatical so
much as comic: the sentence sounds as though the second cup of coffee were a
historic occasion.

One thing `ふたたび` does not do is negation. "Never again" is
<code>二度(にど)と～ない</code> - <code>二度(にど)と同(おな)じ過(あやま)ちは繰(く)り返(かえ)さない</code> -
and that phrase has no `ふたたび` version.

## Contrast

### ふたたび vs また・もう一度(いちど)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `また` is the everyday word for any repetition,
もう一度(いちど) means "one more time" and is what requests use, and `ふたたび`
is the formal word for a **return after an interval**.

</div>

<pre>
<b>また</b>明日(あした)ね。
See you tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、<b>もう一度(いちど)</b>お名前(なまえ)をお願(ねが)いします。
Sorry, could I have your name once more?
</pre>

<pre>
中断(ちゅうだん)していた工事(こうじ)が<b>ふたたび</b>始(はじ)まった。
The suspended construction work has begun again.
</pre>

`また` covers all three situations and is never wrong; that is why it is the safe
default. `ふたたび` buys you formality and the sense of a restoration, and it pays
for them by losing the ability to greet anyone or to ask for anything.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ふたたびお願(ねが)いします。 → ✅ <b>もう一度(いちど)</b>お願(ねが)いします。
  A request cannot use `ふたたび`, however polite the rest of the sentence is.

- ❌ じゃあ、ふたたびね。 → ✅ じゃあ、<b>また</b>ね。
  Parting greetings are fixed phrases. Swapping in the formal word makes them
  sound like a translation exercise.

- ❌ この漢字(かんじ)をふたたび書(か)いて練習(れんしゅう)しました。 → ✅ この漢字(かんじ)を<b>何度(なんど)も</b>書(か)いて練習(れんしゅう)しました。
  Drilling is repetition, not a return after a break. `ふたたび` counts one comeback,
  not many rounds.

## Practice

**1.** 事故(じこ)のため閉鎖(へいさ)されていた道路(どうろ)が、今朝(けさ)____通(とお)れるようになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

今朝(けさ)<b>ふたたび</b>通(とお)れるようになった。

A road that was closed and is now open again is exactly a restoration after an
interval, and the sentence is written in report style, which is where `ふたたび`
lives. `また` would also be grammatical but would lower the register.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: すみません、ふたたび説明(せつめい)していただけませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ すみません、<b>もう一度(いちど)</b>説明(せつめい)していただけませんか。

The sentence is a request, and `ふたたび` cannot introduce one. Politeness is not
the problem - <code>いただけませんか</code> is already very polite; the adverb is
simply the wrong kind of word for asking.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) また会(あ)いましょう。
(b) いつか再(ふたた)び会(あ)える日(ひ)を楽(たの)しみにしています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an ordinary goodbye between people who expect to see each other soon. It
costs the speaker nothing.

(b) treats the next meeting as distant and uncertain, and the formal
<code>再(ふたた)び</code> is doing that work: it frames the reunion as a return
after a long separation. You would write it in a farewell letter, not call it over
your shoulder at the station.

</details>

# 直ちに

**Meaning** · at once, with no interval whatever - the formal word for "immediately"

Read <code>直(ただ)ちに</code>. This is the "immediately" of announcements,
manuals, regulations and emergency instructions. It allows no delay at all, and it
sounds official wherever it appears.

## Usage

<code>直(ただ)ちに</code> is an adverb and stands in front of the verb. It does
not modify nouns and has no `な` or `の` form.

Two uses, both worth knowing.

**1. Instructions and reports.** This is the workplace and public-notice use, and
it is by far the commoner of the two. It attaches to verbs of
response and reporting: 直(ただ)ちに避難(ひなん)する,
直(ただ)ちに中止(ちゅうし)する, 直(ただ)ちに報告(ほうこく)する,
直(ただ)ちに対応(たいおう)する. It very often carries an instruction:
<code>直(ただ)ちに連絡(れんらく)してください</code>,
<code>直(ただ)ちに中止(ちゅうし)すること</code>.

**2. "Not necessarily".** In written argument,
<code>直(ただ)ちに～とは言(い)えない</code> and
<code>直(ただ)ちに～わけではない</code> mean that one fact does not lead
*straight* to a conclusion. This shows up constantly in news and official
statements and is easy to misread as being about time.

Careful with the kanji. 直(なお)す is "fix", 直(じか)に is "directly, with
nothing in between" (<code>直(じか)に会(あ)って話(はな)す</code>), and
直接(ちょくせつ) is "direct". Only the okurigana `ちに` gives you
直(ただ)ちに, and ただ is a reading of this kanji you will meet almost nowhere
else.

## Examples

<pre>
火事(かじ)のときは<b>直(ただ)ちに</b>外(そと)に出(で)てください。
In the event of a fire, leave the building immediately.
</pre>

<pre>
問題(もんだい)が見(み)つかったら<b>直(ただ)ちに</b>報告(ほうこく)すること。
If a problem is found, report it at once.
</pre>

<pre>
強(つよ)い揺(ゆ)れを感(かん)じたら、<b>直(ただ)ちに</b>海岸(かいがん)から離(はな)れ、高(たか)い場所(ばしょ)へ避難(ひなん)してください。
If you feel strong shaking, move away from the coast immediately and evacuate to high ground.
</pre>

<pre>
数値(すうち)が基準(きじゅん)を超(こ)えたからといって、<b>直(ただ)ちに</b>健康(けんこう)に影響(えいきょう)が出(で)るわけではありません。
The figure exceeding the threshold does not mean that health effects will follow straight away.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and impersonal. <code>直(ただ)ちに</code> belongs to written and public
Japanese: safety announcements, station broadcasts, contracts, internal notices,
the news. It is not marked for age or gender, but it is heavily marked for
authority - the speaker is in a position to require the action.

Said to a friend it is comically stiff.
<code>直(ただ)ちに来(き)てください</code> sounds like a summons rather than an
invitation; the ordinary words are すぐ and <code>すぐに</code>.

Two smaller points. First, it is absolute: <code>直(ただ)ちに</code> means *no
gap*, so it does not combine with a stated interval. Second, in sense 2 the
negative is what carries the argument, and dropping it reverses the meaning
entirely - <code>直(ただ)ちに影響(えいきょう)はない</code> ("no immediate
effect") is a famously cautious formula, not a clean bill of health.

## Contrast

### 直(ただ)ちに vs すぐに・早速(さっそく)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すぐに` is the everyday "right away";
<code>直(ただ)ちに</code> is its formal, no-delay-permitted version; and
<code>早速(さっそく)</code> is "without waiting" in the sense of **eagerly taking
something up**.

</div>

<pre>
洗(あら)い物(もの)は<b>すぐに</b>やっておくね。
I'll do the washing-up right away.
</pre>

<pre>
違反(いはん)を確認(かくにん)した場合(ばあい)は<b>直(ただ)ちに</b>使用(しよう)を停止(ていし)します。
If a violation is confirmed, use will be suspended immediately.
</pre>

<pre>
いただいた本(ほん)を<b>早速(さっそく)</b>読(よ)んでみました。
I've already started on the book you gave me.
</pre>

<code>早速(さっそく)</code> is the odd one out: it is warm, it usually describes
the speaker's own action, and it implies pleasure at the chance to act. It cannot
be used to order somebody about, and <code>直(ただ)ちに</code> cannot be used to
thank anybody.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ おなかがすいたね。直(ただ)ちに食(た)べに行(い)こう。 → ✅ おなかがすいたね。<b>すぐに</b>食(た)べに行(い)こう。
  Between friends the formal word is absurd. Save
  <code>直(ただ)ちに</code> for notices and instructions.

- ❌ お土産(みやげ)、直(ただ)ちに食(た)べました。 → ✅ お土産(みやげ)、<b>早速(さっそく)</b>いただきました。
  Eating a present promptly is eagerness, not compliance.
  <code>早速(さっそく)</code> is the word that carries the pleasure.

- ❌ 直(ただ)ちに三日(みっか)以内(いない)にご返事(へんじ)します。 → ✅ 三日(みっか)以内(いない)にご返事(へんじ)します。
  <code>直(ただ)ちに</code> means no interval at all, so naming a three-day window
  contradicts it.

## Practice

**1.** 地震(じしん)が起(お)きたら、エレベーターを使(つか)わず____階段(かいだん)で外(そと)へ出(で)てください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

使(つか)わず<b>直(ただ)ちに</b>階段(かいだん)で外(そと)へ出(で)てください。

This is a safety instruction addressed to the public, which is the home register of
the word, and the whole point is that no delay is acceptable. `すぐに` would be
understood but would read as advice rather than as a direction.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 新(あたら)しいカメラを買(か)ったので、（直(ただ)ちに・早速(さっそく)）使(つか)ってみた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しいカメラを買(か)ったので、<b>早速(さっそく)</b>使(つか)ってみた。

Nobody ordered the speaker to use the camera; they were looking forward to it.
<code>早速(さっそく)</code> carries that eagerness, whereas
<code>直(ただ)ちに</code> would make a new toy sound like an emergency drill.

</details>

**3.** Explain what this sentence actually claims:
検査(けんさ)の結果(けっか)、直(ただ)ちに危険(きけん)であるとは言(い)えない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It claims very little. This is sense 2: the results do not lead *straight* to the
conclusion that there is danger. That is not the same as saying it is safe - the
sentence carefully leaves room for a risk that shows up later or under further
testing, which is precisely why officials reach for the phrase.

</details>

# どうせ

**Meaning** · anyway; whatever happens - said of an outcome the speaker has already
given up on

`どうせ` marks what follows as a foregone conclusion. The facts it states could be
stated without it; what `どうせ` adds is the speaker's resignation, and that
attitude is never optional.

## Usage

`どうせ` is an adverb, and it normally stands at the head of the clause whose
outcome is already settled. It is not the conclusion itself - it is the premise
that the conclusion rests on, so it almost always appears with a second clause.

Three frames carry most of its work.

**どうせ～んだから / のだから + so there is no point.** The commonest shape:
<code>どうせ間(ま)に合(あ)わないんだから、走(はし)っても無駄(むだ)だよ</code>.

**どうせ～なら / どうせ～のなら + so let's do it properly.** Here the tone flips
from defeat to decision: if the thing is happening either way, choose the better
version of it. <code>どうせやるなら楽(たの)しくやろう</code>.

**どうせ + a dismissal.** With no second clause the sentence is pure resignation,
often about oneself: <code>どうせ私(わたし)には無理(むり)だよ</code>. The set
phrase <code>どうせ私(わたし)なんて</code> is a small performance of self-pity and
is used, half-jokingly, all the time.

It often appears with に決(き)まっている, だろう, or a negative predicate,
because those are the shapes a foregone conclusion naturally takes.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>どうせ</b>明日(あした)やるんだから、今日(きょう)は休(やす)もう。
We're going to do it tomorrow anyway, so let's rest today.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>どうせ</b>買(か)うなら、長(なが)く使(つか)えるものがいい。
If I'm buying one anyway, I'd rather have something that lasts.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)いで行(い)っても、<b>どうせ</b>開店(かいてん)まで外(そと)で待(ま)たされるよ。
Even if we hurry, they'll just make us wait outside until opening time anyway.
</pre>

<pre>
「部長(ぶちょう)に相談(そうだん)してみたら?」「いや、<b>どうせ</b>だめだと言(い)われるに決(き)まってる。」
"Why not talk to the boss about it?" "Nah - he's bound to say no anyway."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Colloquial. `どうせ` is a spoken word; formal writing uses
<code>いずれにしても</code> or <code>いずれにせよ</code> for the neutral part of the
meaning and simply omits the attitude.

That attitude is the thing to handle carefully. Aimed at yourself it is
self-deprecating and often endearing. Aimed at *someone else's* effort it is
dismissive - <code>どうせ無理(むり)だよ</code> said to a person who is trying
tells them not to bother, and it stings. Aimed at a customer or a superior it is
close to unusable.

The <code>どうせ～なら</code> frame is the exception, and a genuinely useful one for
learners. There the resignation has been converted into a decision, and the
sentence comes out sounding positive: since it is happening regardless, we may as
well enjoy it. Nothing about that frame is rude.

Note also that `どうせ` needs the outcome to be *settled*, not merely likely. A
weather forecast is a guess, not a fate, so `たぶん` covers it and `どうせ` does not.

## Contrast

### どうせ vs どのみち・どっちみち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どのみち` is **logical**: whichever route you take, you end up
at the same place. `どうせ` is **emotional**: the ending is fixed and the speaker
has stopped fighting it.

</div>

<pre>
歩(ある)いてもタクシーに乗(の)っても、<b>どのみち</b>間(ま)に合(あ)わない。
Walking or taking a taxi, either way we won't make it.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>どうせ</b>間(ま)に合(あ)わないよ。連絡(れんらく)だけしておこう。
We're not going to make it anyway. Let's just send word.
</pre>

`どのみち` needs two or more options in view, stated or implied, and it is
neutral - a manager can say it in a meeting. `どうせ` needs no options at all,
because for the speaker there was never a real choice. `どっちみち` is the more
casual pronunciation of `どのみち`, and <code>いずれにしても</code> is the formal
equivalent of both.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ どうせ明日(あした)は晴(は)れるでしょう。 → ✅ <b>たぶん</b>明日(あした)は晴(は)れるでしょう。
  A forecast is a guess. `どうせ` requires an outcome the speaker treats as already
  decided, and it drags in a resignation the sentence does not want.

- ❌ お客様(きゃくさま)、どうせ明日(あした)お届(とど)けいたします。 → ✅ お客様(きゃくさま)、<b>いずれにしても</b>明日(あした)お届(とど)けいたします。
  To a customer the resigned tone is out of place, and the word is too casual for
  the humble form around it.

- ❌ どうせ頑張(がんば)ってください。 → ✅ <b>とにかく</b>頑張(がんば)ってください。
  `どうせ` cannot introduce a request or encouragement - it says the result is fixed,
  which undercuts the very thing you are asking for.

## Practice

**1.** ____同(おな)じお金(かね)を払(はら)うなら、景色(けしき)のいい部屋(へや)に泊(と)まりたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>どうせ</b>同(おな)じお金(かね)を払(はら)うなら…

This is the <code>どうせ～なら</code> frame. The cost is fixed whatever you choose,
so the speaker turns that fixed fact into an argument for choosing well. The tone
here is upbeat, not defeated.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 電車(でんしゃ)でもバスでも、（どうせ・どのみち）一時間(いちじかん)はかかります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

電車(でんしゃ)でもバスでも、<b>どのみち</b>一時間(いちじかん)はかかります。

Two routes are on the table and both lead to the same travel time, which is exactly
what `どのみち` says. `どうせ` would add a note of "not that it matters", turning a
neutral piece of information into a complaint.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)には無理(むり)です。
(b) どうせ私(わたし)には無理(むり)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain assessment - the task is beyond the speaker, and that is all.

(b) adds a history. `どうせ` says the speaker had already written the outcome off
before anyone asked, and it invites the listener to notice the resignation. Between
friends it reads as fishing for reassurance; in a work conversation it reads as
sulking, which is why it is a risky sentence to say to a manager.

</details>

# 果たして

**Meaning** · sure enough, just as expected - or, in a question, "but is it really?"

Read <code>果(は)たして</code>. It has two jobs, and they pull in opposite
directions: one confirms an expectation, the other casts doubt on one. Which you
get depends entirely on how the sentence ends.

## Usage

<code>果(は)たして</code> is an adverb, almost always at the head of the clause.

**Sense 1 - the expectation came true.** A prediction, a fear or a hope has to be
already in play; <code>果(は)たして</code> then reports that it landed. The
predicate is normally past.

<pre>
雨(あめ)になりそうだと思(おも)っていたら、<b>果(は)たして</b>降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
I thought it looked like rain, and sure enough it started coming down.
</pre>

Without a prior expectation in the text there is nothing to confirm, and the word
falls flat.

**Sense 2 - the doubting question.** Here the partner is compulsory:

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the doubting sense,
<code>果(は)たして</code> must be answered by a question ending -
だろうか, のだろうか, か, or かどうか. Without one, the sentence is not
Japanese.

</div>

<code>果(は)たしてそれは正(ただ)しいのだろうか</code>,
<code>果(は)たして間(ま)に合(あ)うかどうか分(わ)からない</code>. The
force is "one may well wonder whether" - the speaker is inviting doubt, not asking
for information.

Do not confuse the adverb with the verb <code>果(は)たす</code>, "to fulfil", as
in <code>役割(やくわり)を果(は)たす</code>. Same kanji, unrelated jobs.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>果(は)たして</b>その話(はなし)は本当(ほんとう)だろうか。
But is that story really true?
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)するだろうと言(い)われていたが、<b>果(は)たして</b>そのとおりになった。
People said it would fail, and sure enough that is what happened.
</pre>

<pre>
この予算(よさん)で<b>果(は)たして</b>間(ま)に合(あ)うのか、もう一度(いちど)確(たし)かめたほうがいい。
Whether this budget will really be enough is worth checking again.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)が本当(ほんとう)に必要(ひつよう)なのか、<b>果(は)たして</b>しっかり議論(ぎろん)されたと言(い)えるだろうか。
Can we really say there was a proper debate about whether the new system was needed at all?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and formal. <code>果(は)たして</code> lives in editorials, essays,
reports, court judgments and narration. In speech it sounds theatrical: a friend
who says <code>果(は)たしてそうだろうか</code> is either quoting a documentary or
being deliberately arch - which, to be fair, people do for effect.

The spoken equivalents split along the two senses. For sense 1 say `やっぱり`; for
sense 2 say <code>本当(ほんとう)に…かな</code> or
<code>本当(ほんとう)にそうかな</code>.

Sense 2 is also a rhetorical device, not a neutral question. A newspaper headline
that begins <code>果(は)たして</code> has usually decided the answer is no. If
you genuinely want to know something, ask directly; using
<code>果(は)たして</code> tells your reader that you doubt it.

## Contrast

### 果(は)たして vs やはり・やっぱり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "as expected", but `やはり` is the everyday word and
covers far more ground. <code>果(は)たして</code> is written, and only reports
that a **stated** expectation was borne out.

</div>

<pre>
<b>やっぱり</b>この店(みせ)のパンがいちばんおいしい。
This bakery really is the best after all.
</pre>

<pre>
危(あぶ)ないと注意(ちゅうい)されていたが、<b>果(は)たして</b>事故(じこ)が起(お)こった。
They had been warned it was dangerous, and sure enough there was an accident.
</pre>

`やはり` also means "after all" in senses that have nothing to do with prediction -
reverting to an earlier choice （<code>やっぱりやめておく</code>）, or stating what
everybody knows. None of those transfer to
<code>果(は)たして</code>. Going the other way, the doubting question of sense 2
has no `やはり` counterpart at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 果(は)たしてこのラーメンはおいしいね。 → ✅ <b>やっぱり</b>このラーメンはおいしいね。
  Casual agreement over a meal is `やっぱり` territory. The written word makes the
  sentence sound like a review being read aloud.

- ❌ 果(は)たして彼(かれ)は来(く)る。 → ✅ <b>果(は)たして</b>彼(かれ)は来(く)るだろうか。
  In the doubting sense the question ending is obligatory. A bare statement leaves
  the adverb with nothing to attach to.

- ❌ 果(は)たして雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った。 → ✅ 天気予報(てんきよほう)のとおり、<b>果(は)たして</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った。
  Sense 1 confirms an expectation, so the expectation has to be somewhere in the
  sentence or the paragraph before it.

## Practice

**1.** 多(おお)くの人(ひと)が賛成(さんせい)しているが、____この計画(けいかく)は本当(ほんとう)にうまくいくのだろうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>果(は)たして</b>この計画(けいかく)は本当(ほんとう)にうまくいくのだろうか。

This is sense 2, and the sentence already supplies its partner: the
<code>のだろうか</code> ending. The adverb signals to the reader that the writer,
unlike the majority, has doubts.

</details>

**2.** Which sense is this, and why?
嫌(いや)な予感(よかん)がしていたが、果(は)たしてその通(とお)りになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sense 1, "sure enough". The clue is the pair of clauses: an expectation is stated
first （<code>嫌(いや)な予感(よかん)がしていた</code>）and the second clause
reports that it came true, with a past-tense predicate and no question ending.
Sense 2 would have required <code>だろうか</code> or <code>のか</code>.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in natural spoken Japanese:
果(は)たしてこのやり方(かた)が最善(さいぜん)なのだろうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このやり方(かた)で本当(ほんとう)にいいのかな。／これがいちばんいい方法(ほうほう)なのかな。

The doubting sense survives the change of register perfectly well; what has to go
is the written adverb. Spoken Japanese puts the doubt in
<code>本当(ほんとう)に</code> and in the final <code>かな</code>.

</details>

# 幸い

**Meaning** · fortunately - and, in business writing, "I would be grateful if…"

Read <code>幸(さいわ)い</code>. As an adverb it reports relief: something bad
happened, and the damage stopped short of what it might have been. As a noun in a
fixed email formula it does something else entirely, and both are worth learning
together.

## Usage

**As an adverb.** <code>幸(さいわ)い</code>,
<code>幸(さいわ)いに</code> and <code>幸(さいわ)いにも</code> all work, usually
at the head of the clause and often followed by a comma.
<code>幸(さいわ)いにも</code> is the most emphatic and the most written. The
noun-modifying shape is <code>幸(さいわ)いなことに</code>.

The typical sentence has two halves - a setback, then the limit on it:

- 事故(じこ)があったが、幸(さいわ)いけが人(にん)はいなかった。
- 道(みち)に迷(まよ)ったが、幸(さいわ)い近(ちか)くに交番(こうばん)があった。

That background is what the word needs. It is not a general "how nice" - it is
relief that something worse did not happen.

**As a noun or な-adjective.** <code>不幸中(ふこうちゅう)の幸(さいわ)い</code>
("a small mercy in the middle of a disaster") is a set phrase. And in written
requests:

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～いただければ幸(さいわ)いです</code> is a fixed polite
request, "I would be grateful if you could…". Here
<code>幸(さいわ)い</code> is a **noun**, not the adverb, so it takes
<code>です</code> directly.

</div>

<code>ご検討(けんとう)いただければ幸(さいわ)いです</code>,
<code>ご返信(へんしん)いただけますと幸(さいわ)いです</code>. In very formal
correspondence <code>幸甚(こうじん)です</code> replaces it.

Keep the reading straight: <code>幸(さいわ)い</code> but
<code>幸(しあわ)せ</code> - same kanji, different words.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>幸(さいわ)い</b>雨(あめ)はすぐにやんだ。
Fortunately the rain stopped straight away.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)を落(お)としたが、<b>幸(さいわ)いにも</b>交番(こうばん)に届(とど)いていた。
I dropped my wallet, but luckily it had been handed in at the police box.
</pre>

<pre>
昨夜(さくや)の地震(じしん)は大(おお)きかったが、<b>幸(さいわ)い</b>棚(たな)の物(もの)が落(お)ちただけで済(す)んだ。
Last night's earthquake was a big one, but happily it came to nothing worse than things falling off the shelves.
</pre>

<pre>
ご意見(いけん)をお聞(き)かせいただければ<b>幸(さいわ)いです</b>。
I would be grateful if you could let me know your thoughts.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral to formal, and much commoner in writing than in speech. It is the standard
word of news reports - <code>幸(さいわ)い、けが人(にん)はいませんでした</code>
closes hundreds of them. In conversation people are more likely to say
<code>よかった</code> or <code>運(うん)よく</code>.

The presupposition is the thing to remember: <code>幸(さいわ)い</code> assumes a
bad background. Applied to plain good fortune with nothing dark behind it, it
sounds off - a lottery win is <code>運(うん)よく</code>, not
<code>幸(さいわ)い</code>. Its natural opposite, <code>あいにく</code>
("unfortunately"), works the same way from the other side and is the polite word
for declining: <code>あいにくその日(ひ)は予定(よてい)がありまして</code>.

The email formula deserves separate storage in memory. It is softer than
<code>お願(ねが)いします</code> because it never actually issues a request - it
describes how the writer would feel - and that indirection is exactly why Japanese
business correspondence leans on it so heavily.

## Contrast

### 幸(さいわ)い vs 運(うん)よく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>運(うん)よく</code> is about **the odds**: chance fell
your way. <code>幸(さいわ)い</code> is about **the outcome**: it could have been
worse and it wasn't.

</div>

<pre>
連休(れんきゅう)なのに、<b>運(うん)よく</b>窓側(まどがわ)の席(せき)が取(と)れた。
It's a holiday weekend, but I got lucky and managed to get a window seat.
</pre>

<pre>
火(ひ)が出(で)たが、<b>幸(さいわ)い</b>建物(たてもの)に被害(ひがい)はなかった。
A fire broke out, but fortunately the building was undamaged.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences go strange. Getting a seat is a small win against the
odds, with no disaster behind it. A fire is a disaster, and what the second
sentence reports is not luck in a draw but a bad situation that stopped short.

Note too that only <code>幸(さいわ)い</code> has the noun life -
<code>幸(さいわ)いです</code>, <code>不幸中(ふこうちゅう)の幸(さいわ)い</code> -
while <code>運(うん)よく</code> is an adverb and nothing else.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 幸(さいわ)い、宝(たから)くじに当(あ)たりました。 → ✅ <b>運(うん)よく</b>宝(たから)くじに当(あ)たりました。
  Nothing bad was avoided, so there is no relief for
  <code>幸(さいわ)い</code> to express.

- ❌ ご返事(へんじ)をいただければ幸(さいわ)いにです。 → ✅ ご返事(へんじ)をいただければ<b>幸(さいわ)いです</b>。
  In the email formula the word is a noun. The adverbial `に` has nothing to
  attach to and makes the phrase ungrammatical.

- ❌ 幸(さいわ)いな、けがはありませんでした。 → ✅ <b>幸(さいわ)い</b>、けがはありませんでした。／<b>幸(さいわ)いなことに</b>、けがはありませんでした。
  The `な` form only appears in front of a noun, which in practice means the fixed
  phrase <code>幸(さいわ)いなことに</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 台風(たいふう)で木(き)が倒(たお)れたが、____近(ちか)くに人(ひと)はいなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>幸(さいわ)い</b>近(ちか)くに人(ひと)はいなかった。

The first clause supplies the bad news and the second reports the limit on the
damage - the shape this word was made for. <code>幸(さいわ)いにも</code> would
also fit and would sound slightly more written.

</details>

**2.** Translate into Japanese (business email): "I would be grateful if you could
reply by Friday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

金曜日(きんようび)までにご返信(へんしん)いただければ<b>幸(さいわ)いです</b>。

This is the noun use in its fixed frame: a conditional clause, then
<code>幸(さいわ)いです</code>. Because it states the writer's feeling rather than
issuing an instruction, it is noticeably softer than
<code>ご返信(へんしん)ください</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 幸(さいわ)い、電車(でんしゃ)は動(うご)いていた。
(b) 運(うん)よく、電車(でんしゃ)は動(うご)いていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are natural, but they imply different stories. (a) suggests something had
gone wrong - a storm, an accident - and the trains running is the good news inside
a bad day.

(b) suggests uncertainty rather than trouble: the speaker did not know whether the
trains would be running and the coin came down the right way. The focus is on the
gamble, not on the relief.

</details>

# せいぜい

**Meaning** · at most; at best - and the speaker thinks that is not much

`せいぜい` puts a ceiling on something. The number or the achievement that follows
is the *maximum*, and the word carries the speaker's judgement that the maximum is
unimpressive.

## Usage

`せいぜい` is an adverb. It stands in front of the quantity or the predicate it
caps, and it never changes shape.

Most often it caps an **estimate**, so it keeps company with the hedges that
estimates use - ぐらい, くらい, ほど, 程度(ていど), だろう,
<code>しか～ない</code>:

- せいぜい三人(さんにん)ぐらいだろう
- せいぜい千円(せんえん)程度(ていど)
- せいぜい一週間(いっしゅうかん)しかもたない

It can also cap an outcome rather than a number:
<code>できるのはせいぜい半分(はんぶん)だ</code>,
<code>せいぜい時間(じかん)を稼(かせ)ぐくらいのことしかできない</code>.

Because it names an upper limit, it cannot appear with expressions of *lower*
limit. ❌<code>せいぜい三日(みっか)以上(いじょう)</code> is a contradiction;
so is ❌<code>せいぜい少(すく)なくとも</code>.

There is one more use to recognise. <code>せいぜい～てください</code> and
<code>せいぜい頑張(がんば)りなさい</code> literally say "do it to the limit of
your ability", and older usage really was sincere encouragement - but in modern
Japanese it is heard as sarcasm: *knock yourself out, for all the good it will do*.
Learners meet it in dramas and take it for a kind word. It is not.

## Examples

<pre>
この店(みせ)は狭(せま)くて、<b>せいぜい</b>十人(じゅうにん)しか入(はい)れない。
This place is small - ten people at the very most.
</pre>

<pre>
直(なお)すのにかかるのは<b>せいぜい</b>三十分(さんじゅっぷん)だろう。
Fixing it will take half an hour at the outside.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が上(あ)がるといっても、<b>せいぜい</b>数千円(すうせんえん)程度(ていど)だ。
They say our pay is going up, but it'll be a few thousand yen at best.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)は大(おお)きいけれど、あの会社(かいしゃ)に実際(じっさい)に作(つく)れるのは<b>せいぜい</b>試作品(しさくひん)までだろう。
They talk big, but the most that company can actually build is a prototype.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Conversational and neutral in politeness, but never neutral in attitude. `せいぜい`
always belittles a little: it says "no more than this, and this is not much". A
report that wants the same ceiling without the sneer writes
<code>最大(さいだい)でも</code> or <code>多(おお)くても</code>.

That edge matters when the ceiling is somebody else's.
<code>あの人(ひと)にできるのはせいぜいそのくらいだ</code> is a put-down, not an
assessment. Applied to your own resources it is modest and perfectly safe:
<code>出(だ)せるのはせいぜい五千円(ごせんえん)です</code>.

The sarcastic imperative is the trap. Say
<code>せいぜい楽(たの)しんでください</code> to someone leaving on holiday and you
have not wished them a good time - you have implied that the trip is a poor
consolation prize. The plain <code>楽(たの)しんでください</code> is what you
wanted.

## Contrast

### せいぜい vs たかが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せいぜい` caps a **quantity**: at most this many.
`たかが` dismisses a **thing**: it's only a…, and it modifies a noun.

</div>

<pre>
集(あつ)まったのは<b>せいぜい</b>二十人(にじゅうにん)くらいだった。
Twenty people turned up, at the very most.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>たかが</b>ゲームじゃないか。そんなに怒(おこ)るなよ。
It's only a game - don't get so worked up.
</pre>

The two share the dismissive tone, and they sit happily in the same breath -
<code>たかがメールだ。せいぜい五分(ごふん)で済(す)む</code>. But they are not
interchangeable. `たかが` takes a noun and belittles its
importance; `せいぜい` takes a measurement and caps it. `たかが` also has the fixed
frame <code>たかが～くらいで</code>, "just because of a mere…".

### せいぜい vs せめて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - opposite directions. `せいぜい` is an **upper** limit on what is
real; `せめて` is a **lower** limit on what the speaker wants.

</div>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は<b>せいぜい</b>十人(じゅうにん)だろう。
There'll be ten participants at most.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>せめて</b>十人(じゅうにん)は集(あつ)めたい。
I want to get at least ten people together.
</pre>

The first is a prediction about reality and the speaker is unimpressed by it. The
second is a wish, scaled down from a bigger hope - and because it is a wish, it
needs a predicate of wanting, asking or intending. Swapping the two adverbs makes
both sentences ungrammatical rather than merely odd.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ せいぜい一言(ひとこと)謝(あやま)ってほしい。 → ✅ <b>せめて</b>一言(ひとこと)謝(あやま)ってほしい。
  A wish takes `せめて`. `せいぜい` estimates what will actually happen and cannot
  head a request.

- ❌ この仕事(しごと)はせいぜい三日(みっか)以上(いじょう)かかる。 → ✅ この仕事(しごと)は<b>せいぜい</b>三日(みっか)で終(お)わる。／この仕事(しごと)は少(すく)なくとも三日(みっか)はかかる。
  <code>以上(いじょう)</code> sets a floor and `せいぜい` sets a ceiling. One of
  the two has to go.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)、せいぜい頑張(がんば)ってね。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)、頑張(がんば)ってね。
  Meant as encouragement, heard as sarcasm. Leave the adverb out.

## Practice

**1.** あの川(かわ)は浅(あさ)くて、深(ふか)いところでも____一(いち)メートルぐらいだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

深(ふか)いところでも<b>せいぜい</b>一(いち)メートルぐらいだ。

The sentence is capping a measurement and treating the figure as small, which is
exactly the job of `せいぜい`. Note the company it keeps: <code>でも</code> before
it and <code>ぐらい</code> after it, both typical of an estimate.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 全部(ぜんぶ)は無理(むり)でも、（せいぜい・せめて）半分(はんぶん)は終(お)わらせたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

全部(ぜんぶ)は無理(むり)でも、<b>せめて</b>半分(はんぶん)は終(お)わらせたい。

<code>終(お)わらせたい</code> is a wish, and the first clause has already given up
on the full amount. That is the `せめて` pattern: the ideal is out of reach, so the
speaker names a floor. `せいぜい` would turn the sentence into a gloomy forecast and
would clash with <code>たい</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この仕事(しごと)は三日(みっか)で終(お)わる。
(b) この仕事(しごと)はせいぜい三日(みっか)で終(お)わる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat estimate: three days.

(b) adds two things. It makes three days the *maximum* rather than the expected
figure, and it tells you what the speaker thinks of the job - small, quickly
disposed of, not worth the fuss someone is making about it. Said in front of the
person doing the work, that second layer can easily give offence.

</details>

# せめて

**Meaning** · at least - the reduced version of a wish that cannot be had in full

`せめて` is a compromise, spoken aloud. The speaker has accepted that the thing
they really want is out of reach, and names the smallest version they could live
with instead.

## Usage

`せめて` is an adverb and stands in front of the reduced amount. The rule that
matters is about what comes *after* it:

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せめて` belongs to a **wish, a request or a plan**, never to a
plain statement of fact. Expect ～たい, ～てほしい, ～てください,
～たかった, ～ばよかった, or a volitional.

</div>

- せめて一目(ひとめ)会(あ)いたい
- せめて連絡(れんらく)くらいしてほしかった
- せめて今日(きょう)だけは早(はや)く帰(かえ)ろう

Three particles do most of the work alongside it, and they all say "if not the
whole thing, then this much":

- <code>せめて～だけでも</code> - せめて水(みず)だけでも飲(の)んでください
- <code>せめて～くらい(は)</code> - せめてあいさつくらいはしてほしい
- <code>せめて～だけは</code> - せめてこれだけは守(まも)ってください

Because the whole word is built on giving something up, the sentence usually
contains the abandoned ideal too: <code>全部(ぜんぶ)は無理(むり)でも、せめて…</code>,
<code>優勝(ゆうしょう)できなくても、せめて…</code>.

## Examples

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)は覚(おぼ)えられないが、<b>せめて</b>名前(なまえ)だけは覚(おぼ)えたい。
I can't memorise all of it, but I want to learn at least the names.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>せめて</b>電話(でんわ)くらいしてほしかった。
You could at least have phoned.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しいのは分(わ)かるけれど、<b>せめて</b>朝(あさ)ごはんだけでも食(た)べていったら?
I know you're busy, but couldn't you at least eat something before you go?
</pre>

<pre>
優勝(ゆうしょう)は難(むずか)しいだろうが、<b>せめて</b>一勝(いっしょう)はして帰(かえ)りたい。
Winning the whole thing is probably beyond us, but I'd like to take at least one match home.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very common in speech. It works with plain and polite forms
alike and is not marked for age or gender.

Its emotional colour is not neutral, though. `せめて` always carries a shadow of
disappointment, because it announces that the real hope has already been written
off. In <code>せめて連絡(れんらく)くらいしてほしかった</code> the complaint is
audible: you did not have to come, but you could have done *this much*. That makes
it a common opening for reproach, and a risky word to use about something someone
has given you - <code>せめてこれくらいはいただきたい</code> tells your host that
what they offered fell short.

Turned on yourself it is modest and rather appealing:
<code>せめて自分(じぶん)の分(ぶん)だけは払(はら)わせてください</code> - if I
can't treat you, at least let me cover my own share.

## Contrast

### せめて vs 少(すく)なくとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> states an **objective**
minimum: the true figure is this or higher. `せめて` states a **desired** minimum:
this is the least the speaker would accept.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)には<b>少(すく)なくとも</b>三百人(さんびゃくにん)は来(き)ていた。
There were at least three hundred people in the hall.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>せめて</b>三百人(さんびゃくにん)は集(あつ)めたいところだ。
We'd like to get at least three hundred people in.
</pre>

The first is a count. It reports what happened, and `せめて` cannot do that:
❌<code>せめて三百人(さんびゃくにん)来(き)た</code> is not Japanese. The second
is a target, and <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> would flatten it into arithmetic.

<code>少(すく)なくとも</code> has a second life in argument, meaning "at any rate,
whatever else may be true" - <code>少(すく)なくとも私(わたし)はそう思(おも)う</code>.
`せめて` has no such use.

### せめて vs せいぜい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せめて` is a floor under a **wish**; `せいぜい` is a ceiling
over an **estimate**.

</div>

<pre>
<b>せめて</b>一日(いちにち)くらい休(やす)みたい。
I'd like at least one day off.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)めるのは<b>せいぜい</b>一日(いちにち)だろう。
A day off is about the most I'll get.
</pre>

Same number, opposite direction. The first asks for it, the second concedes it.
Learners meeting the two glosses "at least" and "at most" side by side reliably mix
them up; the test is whether the sentence is describing what the speaker *wants* or
what they *expect*.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ せめて三人(さんにん)来(き)ました。 → ✅ <b>少(すく)なくとも</b>三人(さんにん)来(き)ました。
  A completed fact has no wish in it. `せめて` cannot report attendance.

- ❌ せめて千円(せんえん)しか持(も)っていない。 → ✅ <b>せいぜい</b>千円(せんえん)くらいしか持(も)っていない。
  <code>しか～ない</code> caps the amount, which is the opposite of what `せめて`
  does.

- ❌ せめて全部(ぜんぶ)終(お)わらせたい。 → ✅ せめて<b>半分(はんぶん)だけでも</b>終(お)わらせたい。
  `せめて` introduces the scaled-down target, not the full ideal. Naming the whole
  thing after it contradicts the word.

## Practice

**1.** 旅行(りょこう)に行(い)く時間(じかん)はないけれど、____近(ちか)くの海(うみ)くらいは見(み)に行(い)きたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>せめて</b>近(ちか)くの海(うみ)くらいは見(み)に行(い)きたい。

The first clause abandons the real plan, and the second names a smaller version of
it with <code>たい</code>. Both halves of the `せめて` pattern are present: a wish,
and an ideal already given up on.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: きのうのパーティーには、せめて二十人(にじゅうにん)来(き)ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ きのうのパーティーには、<b>少(すく)なくとも</b>二十人(にじゅうにん)来(き)ました。

The sentence reports a fact about a party that has already happened, so there is
nothing for `せめて` to wish for. <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> is the word for an
objective floor under a count.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) せめて一言(ひとこと)説明(せつめい)してほしかった。
(b) 一言(ひとこと)説明(せつめい)してほしかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a straightforward complaint: I wanted a word of explanation and did not get
one.

(a) adds the compromise. `せめて` concedes that a full account was perhaps too much
to ask for, and then points out that even the smallest possible version - one
sentence - was withheld. Paradoxically that makes the reproach sharper, because it
shows the speaker had already lowered the bar.

</details>

# わずかに

**Meaning** · slightly; by only a small amount; only just

`わずかに` marks a difference or a change as very small - small enough that the
speaker wants you to notice how small it is. It is a measuring word, not a
softening word: it reports the size of a gap rather than making a statement
gentler.

## Usage

`わずかに` is the adverbial form of `わずか`, a noun and な-adjective meaning "a
mere trifle of something". It stands directly in front of the predicate.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わずかに` scales **an amount, a difference, or a faint
physical change**. If the thing being scaled cannot in principle be measured,
`わずかに` is the wrong word.

</div>

The verbs it goes with are a short and recognisable list, and learning them as
collocations is more useful than learning a rule:

- Differences and margins: わずかに上(うわ)回(まわ)る, わずかに下(した)回(まわ)る, わずかに及(およ)ばない
- Change in quantity: わずかに増(ふ)える, わずかに減(へ)る, わずかに下(さ)がる
- Faint movement or perception: わずかに揺(ゆ)れる, わずかに動(うご)く, わずかに聞(き)こえる, わずかに残(のこ)る

The same root appears in three other shapes, and the `に` is what tells them
apart:

- <code>わずか + 数字(すうじ)</code> - "only", straight in front of a number, **no** `に`:
  わずか三日(みっか)で完成(かんせい)した
- <code>わずかな + 名詞(めいし)</code> - "a slight ～": わずかな差(さ), わずかな望(のぞ)み
- <code>わずかしか～ない</code> - "only a tiny amount": 水(みず)がわずかしか残(のこ)っていない

In kanji it is 僅(わず)かに. The kanji is standard, but outside newspapers the
kana spelling is more common.

## Examples

<pre>
カーテンが風(かぜ)で<b>わずかに</b>揺(ゆ)れた。
The curtain stirred slightly in the breeze.
</pre>

<pre>
今月(こんげつ)の売(う)り上(あ)げは先月(せんげつ)を<b>わずかに</b>上(うわ)回(まわ)った。
This month's sales came in slightly above last month's.
</pre>

<pre>
廊下(ろうか)の奥(おく)から、ピアノの音(おと)が<b>わずかに</b>聞(き)こえてくる。
From the far end of the corridor you can faintly hear a piano.
</pre>

<pre>
決勝(けっしょう)では0.2秒(びょう)差(さ)で、世界(せかい)記録(きろく)に<b>わずかに</b>及(およ)ばなかった。
In the final she fell just short of the world record, by two tenths of a second.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`わずかに` belongs to written and formal-spoken Japanese: news copy, company
reports, sports commentary, technical description. It is not marked for age or
gender, and it is not stiff or old-fashioned - it simply sounds like precision.
In ordinary conversation the same idea comes out as 少(すこ)し or ちょっと, and
「ちょっと待(ま)って」would never be 「わずかに待(ま)って」.

It carries a faint evaluative colouring that 少(すこ)し does not: it invites you
to react to the smallness. わずかに上(うわ)回(まわ)った is a result the writer
thinks is worth calling narrow, and わずかに及(およ)ばなかった is a defeat framed
as a near miss. Depending on the sentence that lands as relief or as regret, but
it is rarely neutral.

Because the word is about measurable smallness, it does not scale feelings.
❌わずかに好(す)きです and ❌わずかに楽(たの)しかった are odd in the same way
"I liked it by a small margin" is odd in English.

## Contrast

### わずかに vs やや

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are written-register words for "slightly", but `やや`
grades **a state or a quality**, while `わずかに` measures **a quantity or a
difference**.

</div>

<pre>
今日(きょう)は<b>やや</b>寒(さむ)い。
It's somewhat cold today.
</pre>

<pre>
気温(きおん)は昨日(きのう)より<b>わずかに</b>下(さ)がった。
The temperature dropped slightly compared with yesterday.
</pre>

`やや` sits comfortably in front of an adjective; `わずかに` sits in front of a verb
of change or comparison. Where they overlap, `わずかに` is if anything the smaller
of the two, and it is the one that can take a numeral - わずか三日(みっか) is
fine, ❌やや三日(みっか) is not a phrase at all. Neither one belongs in casual
speech, where ちょっと covers the whole range.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 針(はり)がわずか動(うご)いた。 → ✅ 針(はり)が<b>わずかに</b>動(うご)いた。
  In front of a verb the `に` is obligatory. Bare `わずか` only stands in front of a
  number or a noun.

- ❌ わずかに三日(みっか)で終(お)わった。 → ✅ <b>わずか</b>三日(みっか)で終(お)わった。
  The mirror-image error. With a quantity, drop the `に`: this is the "only three
  days" sense, not "by a small amount".

- ❌ この映画(えいが)がわずかに好(す)きです。 → ✅ この映画(えいが)が少(すこ)し好(す)きです。
  Liking is not a measured quantity, so there is no margin for `わずかに` to
  report.

- ❌ ごめん、わずかに待(ま)ってください。 → ✅ ごめん、ちょっと待(ま)ってください。
  Grammatical but absurd in register - it is the tone of a laboratory notebook in
  the middle of a chat.

## Practice

**1.** 今年(ことし)の来場者数(らいじょうしゃすう)は去年(きょねん)を____上(うわ)回(まわ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

去年(きょねん)を<b>わずかに</b>上(うわ)回(まわ)った。

The sentence compares two figures, so what is being scaled is the size of the gap
between them - exactly what this adverb is for. The `に` is required because a
verb follows.

</details>

**2.** Choose わずか or わずかに: 彼女(かのじょ)は____一年(いちねん)でフランス語(ご)を身(み)につけた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>わずか</b>一年(いちねん)でフランス語(ご)を身(み)につけた。

A number follows, so this is the "only" use and the `に` drops. Adding it would
try to make the adverb modify 身(み)につけた, which would mean she acquired the
language to a slight degree.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この部屋(へや)は少(すこ)し暗(くら)い。
(b) 部屋(へや)の明(あか)るさが<b>わずかに</b>変(か)わった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an everyday judgement about how the room feels, and it is what a person
would actually say on walking in. (b) reports a measured change of state, and the
register goes with it - this is the sentence of someone watching a dial or writing
up an observation, not of someone commenting on a room.

</details>

# ばかり - about; approximately

**Meaning** · about; roughly; some ～ (after a stated quantity)

Put `ばかり` after a number and it stops meaning "only" and starts meaning
"about": 三日(みっか)ばかり is "three days or so". This is the oldest use of the
word and the one a learner is most likely to meet in print rather than in
conversation.

## Formation

`ばかり` follows the whole quantity expression - number plus counter - and never
comes in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Number + counter | 数量(すうりょう) + ばかり | 三日(みっか) → 三日(みっか)ばかり |
| Amount of money or measure | 数量(すうりょう) + ばかり | 千円(せんえん) → 千円(せんえん)ばかり |
| Quantity adverb | 少(すこ)し・ちょっと + ばかり | 少(すこ)し → 少(すこ)しばかり |
| Quantity + following particle | 数量(すうりょう) + ばかり + で・の・前(まえ)に | 一週間(いっしゅうかん) → 一週間(いっしゅうかん)ばかり前(まえ)に |

Note the last row: `ばかり` slots in where the quantity sat, and any particle the
sentence needed still follows it. The direct-object `を` is normally left out -
水(みず)を一杯(いっぱい)ばかり飲(の)んだ, not ❌一杯(いっぱい)ばかりを.

## Examples

<pre>
駅(えき)まで十分(じゅっぷん)<b>ばかり</b>かかります。
It takes about ten minutes to get to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
少(すこ)し<b>ばかり</b>お金(かね)を貸(か)してくれませんか。
Could you lend me just a little money?
</pre>

<pre>
二日(ふつか)<b>ばかり</b>休(やす)ませていただきます。
I'll be taking a couple of days off.
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)<b>ばかり</b>前(まえ)に届(とど)きました。
The parcel arrived about half an hour ago.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)には二十人(にじゅうにん)<b>ばかり</b>集(あつ)まっていた。
Some twenty people had gathered in the hall.
</pre>

<pre>
心(こころ)<b>ばかり</b>の品(しな)ですが、どうぞお受(う)け取(と)りください。
It's the smallest of tokens, but please do accept it.
</pre>

<pre>
学生(がくせい)のころ、一年(いちねん)<b>ばかり</b>大阪(おおさか)に住(す)んでいたことがあります。
When I was a student I lived in Osaka for a year or so.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は七十年(ななじゅうねん)<b>ばかり</b>前(まえ)の話(はなし)を、きのうのことのように語(かた)り始(はじ)めた。
My grandmother began telling a story from some seventy years ago as if it had happened yesterday.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This use of `ばかり` is bookish and a little old-fashioned in speech. You will meet
it constantly in novels, in translated literature, in formal letters, and in the
speech of older characters; a person under forty ordering coffee is far more
likely to say 十分(じゅっぷん)ぐらい than 十分(じゅっぷん)ばかり. Using it is
not rude and not wrong - it simply reads as a shade literary, the way "some
twenty people" does in English next to "about twenty people".

It also tends to imply that the amount is **modest**, which plain
approximation with ほど does not. That flavour is why the word survives in a
handful of humble set expressions that are still fully alive today:
少(すこ)しばかり ("just a little"), 心(こころ)ばかりの品(しな)
("a mere token", written on a gift), 形(かたち)ばかりの式(しき)
("a ceremony in name only"). Learn those as units - they are the most likely place
you will actually produce this pattern.

Written in kana. The kanji spelling <code>許り</code> exists in older texts and is essentially
never used now. There is no politeness difference between the plain and ます
forms of the surrounding sentence: `ばかり` itself carries the register, not the
verb ending.

Because the same four kana carry the "only; nothing but" meaning, context does all
the disambiguating - and the context is simply whether a quantity precedes it.

## Contrast

### 数量(すうりょう)ばかり vs ほど・ぐらい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three approximate a quantity. `ぐらい` is the everyday
spoken one, `ほど` the neutral written one, and `ばかり` the literary one that
also hints the amount is small.

</div>

<pre>
三日(みっか)<b>ぐらい</b>待(ま)ってください。
Please wait about three days.
</pre>

<pre>
三日(みっか)<b>ばかり</b>お時間(じかん)をいただけますか。
Might I have three days or so of your time?
</pre>

The two sentences describe the same interval. The second sounds like a letter,
and its `ばかり` quietly presents three days as not much to ask. If you want a
neutral approximation in writing with no such colouring, ほど is the safe default.

### ばかり - "about" vs "only"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a **quantity**, `ばかり` means "about". After a **noun or
a verb**, it means "only; nothing but".

</div>

<pre>
三日(みっか)<b>ばかり</b>東京(とうきょう)にいました。
I was in Tokyo for about three days.
</pre>

<pre>
東京(とうきょう)では仕事(しごと)<b>ばかり</b>していました。
In Tokyo I did nothing but work.
</pre>

The boundary is not perfectly sharp - 一人(ひとり)ばかり can be read either way
in isolation - but in practice a counter in front of `ばかり` forces the
approximate reading, and a bare noun forces the exclusive one.

### 数量(すうりょう)ばかり vs ～たばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばかり` after the **plain past form of a verb** is a third,
unrelated pattern meaning "have just done".

</div>

<pre>
一時間(いちじかん)<b>ばかり</b>寝(ね)ました。
I slept for an hour or so.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)起(お)き<b>たばかり</b>です。
I've only just woken up.
</pre>

Three patterns, one word. The way to keep them apart is to look at what sits
immediately before `ばかり`: a counter gives "about", a plain past verb gives "just
now", anything else gives "only".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ばかり三日(みっか)かかります。 → ✅ <b>三日(みっか)ばかり</b>かかります。
  `ばかり` always follows the quantity. Nothing in Japanese puts an approximator in
  front of the number the way English puts "about" there.

- ❌ 何人(なんにん)ばかり来(き)ましたか。 → ✅ 何人(なんにん)<b>ぐらい</b>来(き)ましたか。
  Approximating `ばかり` states an amount; it cannot combine with a question word.
  For "roughly how many", use ぐらい or ほど.

- ❌ この荷物(にもつ)は重(おも)いばかりです。 → ✅ この荷物(にもつ)は少(すこ)し重(おも)いです。
  It approximates quantities, not degrees, so it cannot be hung on an adjective to
  mean "fairly heavy". (The sentence as written is grammatical, but it means "this
  parcel is nothing but heavy".)

- ❌ コーヒーを一杯(いっぱい)ばかりを飲(の)んだ。 → ✅ コーヒーを<b>一杯(いっぱい)ばかり</b>飲(の)んだ。
  Do not add a second `を`. The quantity phrase already carries the one the
  sentence needs.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、五分(ごふん)____待(ま)っていただけますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

五分(ごふん)<b>ばかり</b>待(ま)っていただけますか。

A quantity precedes, so `ばかり` reads as "about five minutes", and the modest
flavour suits a request. In everyday speech ぐらい would be the ordinary choice.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 昨日(きのう)のパーティーには何人(なんにん)（ばかり・ぐらい）来(き)ましたか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何人(なんにん)<b>ぐらい</b>来(き)ましたか。

The sentence asks for a number rather than supplying one, and approximating
`ばかり` needs an actual quantity in front of it. ほど would also work; `ばかり`
would not.

</details>

**3.** Translate: "I waited about two hours, then went home."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

二時間(にじかん)<b>ばかり</b>待(ま)って、家(いえ)に帰(かえ)りました。

The quantity comes first and `ばかり` follows it. 二時間(にじかん)ほど or
二時間(にじかん)ぐらい are equally correct and sound more contemporary.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にばかり十冊(じゅっさつ)の本(ほん)があった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に本(ほん)が<b>十冊(じゅっさつ)ばかり</b>あった。

Two repairs. `ばかり` has to come after the counter, not before it, and once it
does the natural word order puts the counted noun ahead of the quantity phrase.
As originally written the sentence says the books were on the desk and nowhere
else.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 三日(みっか)<b>ばかり</b>休(やす)みました。
(b) 三日(みっか)休(やす)ん<b>だばかり</b>です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about the length of the break: roughly three days, and the speaker presents
that as not very long. (b) is about when the break ended: the three days are over
and the speaker has only just come back. The counter in (a) and the plain past
form in (b) are what select the two readings.

</details>

# いまだに

**Meaning** · still, even now - after all this time, and it should have changed by
now

`いまだに` is not a neutral "still". It says that a situation has gone on far
longer than it ought to have, and it puts the speaker's impatience, disbelief or
resignation into the sentence.

## Usage

`いまだに` is an adverb and sits in front of the predicate, or at the head of the
clause. It is written 未(いま)だに in kanji, and just as often in kana.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いまだに` needs a state that is **still continuing when it
shouldn't be**. Most often that state is a negative: いまだに～ない,
いまだに～ていない.

</div>

The two shapes it takes:

- With a negative - the commonest by far, and the one to learn first:
  いまだに連絡(れんらく)がない, いまだに原因(げんいん)が分(わ)からない,
  いまだに決(き)まっていない
- With an affirmative, which then has to be a **continuing state**, normally
  `～ている` or an existence verb: いまだに覚(おぼ)えている,
  いまだに人気(にんき)がある, いまだに信(しん)じている

A one-off past event will not do. ❌いまだに来(き)ました is not Japanese; the
sentence has to describe a condition that reaches into the present.

`いまだに` also refuses a stated span of time. ❌いまだに五分(ごふん)しか経(た)っていない is wrong, because five minutes is not "all this time" -
まだ五分(ごふん)しか経(た)っていない is the sentence you want.

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)からは<b>いまだに</b>返事(へんじ)がない。
I still haven't had a reply from him.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)は<b>いまだに</b>分(わ)かっていません。
The cause of the accident is still unknown.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どものころに聞(き)いた歌(うた)を、<b>いまだに</b>口(くち)ずさんでしまう。
I still catch myself humming songs I heard as a child.
</pre>

<pre>
申請(しんせい)してから半年(はんとし)になるのに、許可(きょか)が<b>いまだに</b>下(お)りない。
It's been six months since we applied and the permit still hasn't come through.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The word is a shade more formal and more written than まだ, but it is completely
at home in adult conversation, in news reports and in business email alike. It is
not marked for age or gender.

What it always carries is a judgement. いまだに返事(へんじ)がない is a complaint;
いまだに人気(にんき)がある is admiration that the popularity has outlasted
expectation; いまだに信(しん)じられない is astonishment. If you have no such
attitude and simply want to report that something is ongoing, まだ is the honest
choice, and using `いまだに` instead will sound as though you are needling someone.

That makes it worth handling carefully when the person responsible is in the
room. 「いまだに終(お)わっていないんですか」is close to "you *still* haven't
finished?" - grammatical, polite in form, and pointed.

The spelling 今(いま)だに is a very common native error and turns up even in
print. The word is 未(いま)だ, "not yet", plus `に`; the kanji is <code>未</code>, not <code>今</code>. If
you are unsure, write it in kana.

## Contrast

### いまだに vs まだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まだ` reports that something is unfinished. `いまだに` reports
that it is unfinished **and that this is unreasonable**.

</div>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は<b>まだ</b>終(お)わっていません。
The construction isn't finished yet.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は<b>いまだに</b>終(お)わっていません。
The construction still isn't finished, after all this time.
</pre>

`まだ` is neutral and can look forward - まだ時間(じかん)があります, "there's
still time" - which `いまだに` cannot do, because a resource you still have is not
an overdue situation. `まだ` also works with short intervals and with quantities;
`いまだに` works only with stretches long enough for the speaker to have expected a
change.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今(いま)だに連絡(れんらく)がありません。 → ✅ <b>未(いま)だに</b>連絡(れんらく)がありません。
  The kanji is <code>未</code>, from 未(いま)だ "not yet". Writing it with <code>今</code> is a spelling
  error, not a variant.

- ❌ バスが<b>いまだに</b>三分(さんぷん)遅(おく)れています。 → ✅ バスが三分(さんぷん)遅(おく)れています。
  Three minutes is not "after all this time". The adverb needs a delay long enough
  to be an affront.

- ❌ いまだに昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)に出(で)ました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)に出(で)ました。
  A finished one-off event cannot be "still" happening. `いまだに` needs a state
  that continues into the present.

- ❌ まだ十年(じゅうねん)前(まえ)のことを怒(おこ)っている。 → ✅ <b>いまだに</b>十年(じゅうねん)前(まえ)のことを怒(おこ)っている。
  Not ungrammatical, but flat. When the point of the sentence *is* that the
  grudge has lasted absurdly long, `まだ` throws that away.

## Practice

**1.** 三年(さんねん)前(まえ)に貸(か)したお金(かね)が、____返(かえ)ってこない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三年(さんねん)前(まえ)に貸(か)したお金(かね)が、<b>いまだに</b>返(かえ)ってこない。

Three years plus a negative predicate is exactly the shape this adverb wants, and
the complaint is the whole point of the sentence. まだ would be grammatical but
would drain the exasperation out of it.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 急(いそ)がなくていいですよ。（まだ・いまだに）時間(じかん)があります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>まだ</b>時間(じかん)があります。

Here "still" points forward at a resource that has not run out yet, which is a
neutral report. `いまだに` needs an overdue situation, and there is nothing overdue
about having time left.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) あの店(みせ)はまだ開(あ)いている。
(b) あの店(みせ)はいまだに開(あ)いている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply says the shop has not closed yet - useful information if you are
hurrying to get there. (b) says the shop is somehow still in business after all
these years, and the speaker is impressed, or faintly amazed, that it has
survived. The same four words describe a shop open late and a shop open for
decades.

</details>

# 何しろ

**Meaning** · なにしろ - the thing is …; after all; what with ～ being the way it is

<code>何(なに)しろ</code> introduces the one circumstance the speaker treats as settling the
matter. It flags what follows as the reason, and as a reason so obvious or so
overwhelming that there is nothing more to discuss.

## Usage

<code>何(なに)しろ</code> stands at the **head of the clause**, never in the middle of one.
It works in two shapes:

- **Reason first.** <code>何(なに)しろ ～ので／から、…</code> - the excuse or
  explanation comes first and the consequence follows:
  何(なに)しろ初(はじ)めてなので、緊張(きんちょう)しています。
- **Statement first, reason after.** The commoner shape in speech: you say the
  thing, then add <code>何(なに)しろ</code> and the circumstance that accounts for it. The
  second sentence often trails off without a main verb -
  「大変(たいへん)でしたよ。何(なに)しろ人(ひと)が多(おお)くて。」

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>何(なに)しろ</code> is not an "anyway" that changes the subject.
It hands you the **cause** of what was just said, and asks you to accept it as
sufficient.

</div>

It sits well with an exaggeration or a strong statement following it, because its
job is to justify one: 何(なに)しろ三十年(さんじゅうねん)前(まえ)のことですから, "it was thirty years ago, after all". Sentences with
<code>何(なに)しろ</code> very often end in から, ので, もので, or the explanatory
<code>んです</code>.

The near-identical <code>何(なに)せ</code> is interchangeable in almost every
sentence and slightly more casual. <code>何(なに)分(ぶん)</code> is the formal
written cousin, used in apologies: 何(なに)分(ぶん)急(きゅう)なことでしたので.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>何(なに)しろ</b>初(はじ)めてなので、緊張(きんちょう)しています。
It's my first time, you see, so I'm nervous.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は大変(たいへん)でした。<b>何(なに)しろ</b>朝(あさ)から雨(あめ)がひどくて。
Yesterday was rough - what with the rain being that bad from the morning on.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)はいつも行列(ぎょうれつ)ができている。<b>何(なに)しろ</b>値段(ねだん)のわりに量(りょう)が多(おお)いのだ。
There's always a queue at that place. The portions are enormous for the price, after all.
</pre>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)くなって申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。<b>何(なに)しろ</b>年度末(ねんどまつ)で手(て)が離(はな)せないものですから。
I'm sorry to be slow replying - it's the end of the financial year and I simply can't get away from things.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Conversational and emphatic. You hear it constantly in ordinary adult speech and
in the spoken parts of writing - interviews, columns, dialogue - and it is fine in
a polite email of the kind above, but it would look out of place in a report or a
contract, where そのため or というのも does the same work without the shrug.

The shrug is the point. <code>何(なに)しろ</code> presents its reason as common knowledge,
which is flattering when the listener really does know it and presumptuous when
they do not. Used to excuse yourself it is disarming; used too often it starts to
sound like a person who always has a circumstance to hand.

It is not marked for gender, though the clipped <code>何(なに)せ</code> leans a
little more casual and a little more male in tone. Neither is old-fashioned.

One thing it never means is "anyway, whatever". The gloss "in any case" that
dictionaries give is misleading: <code>何(なに)しろ</code> does not set anything aside, it
points at the single fact that explains everything.

## Contrast

### 何(なに)しろ vs とにかく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とにかく` **sets the details aside** and moves to what should
be done. <code>何(なに)しろ</code> **supplies the one detail** that explains everything.

</div>

<pre>
理由(りゆう)は後(あと)で聞(き)きます。<b>とにかく</b>早(はや)く来(き)てください。
I'll hear the reasons later. Just get here quickly.
</pre>

<pre>
遅(おく)れてすみません。<b>何(なに)しろ</b>電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっていて。
Sorry I'm late - the trains were stopped, you see.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences break. `とにかく` in the second would mean "never mind
about the trains", which is the opposite of an excuse; <code>何(なに)しろ</code> in the
first would promise a reason and then fail to give one. English "anyway" covers
both jobs, which is exactly why learners reach for the wrong one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 忙(いそが)しくて何(なに)しろ行(い)けません。 → ✅ <b>何(なに)しろ</b>忙(いそが)しくて行(い)けません。
  It opens the clause. Dropping it into the middle of one leaves it modifying
  nothing.

- ❌ 何(なに)しろ、後(あと)で決(き)めましょう。 → ✅ <b>とにかく</b>後(あと)で決(き)めましょう。
  "Let's decide later regardless" is a setting-aside, not a reason. That is
  とにかく's job.

- ❌ 何(なに)しろ雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りました。 → ✅ <b>何(なに)しろ</b>雨(あめ)がひどかったものですから。
  A bare finished event is not an explanation. The clause after
  <code>何(なに)しろ</code> normally ends in から, ので, もので or the て-form left hanging.

- ❌ 何(なに)しろは初(はじ)めてです。 → ✅ <b>何(なに)しろ</b>初(はじ)めてなんです。
  It is an adverb, not a noun, so it takes no particle.

## Practice

**1.** 説明(せつめい)が下手(へた)ですみません。____説明(せつめい)するのが苦手(にがて)なもので。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません。<b>何(なに)しろ</b>説明(せつめい)するのが苦手(にがて)なもので。

The second sentence gives the reason for the first and trails off in もので, which
is the shape this adverb likes. It opens the clause, immediately after the
sentence it accounts for.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 話(はなし)は後(あと)で聞(き)くから、（何(なに)しろ・とにかく）座(すわ)って。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>とにかく</b>座(すわ)って。

The speaker is explicitly postponing the explanation, so nothing is being
explained. <code>何(なに)しろ</code> would announce a reason and then not deliver one.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 人(ひと)が多(おお)いので、疲(つか)れました。
(b) 疲(つか)れました。何(なに)しろ人(ひと)が多(おお)くて。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of cause and effect, and it works with a listener who
knows nothing about the situation. (b) puts the tiredness first and then produces
the crowd as the obvious explanation, in a tone that assumes the listener will
immediately agree it was enough. (b) is warmer and more conversational; (a) is
what you would write in a report.

</details>

# せっかく

**Meaning** · after all the trouble of ～; now that we've got this rare chance

`せっかく` puts a value on effort or on an opportunity. Everything that follows is
then judged against that value: either the effort was wasted, which is a
complaint, or it must not be wasted, which is an invitation.

## Usage

`せっかく` is an adverb, but it is a half-sentence on its own. It sets up an
expectation and the listener waits for the second half.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せっかく` almost always needs a partner ending. Either
<code>せっかく～のに</code> - "and yet it came to nothing" - or
<code>せっかく～のだから</code> - "so let's make it count".

</div>

The three shapes worth learning:

- <code>せっかく～のに</code> - regret: せっかく作(つく)ったのに、誰(だれ)も食(た)べなかった
- <code>せっかく～のだから／なので</code> - encouragement: せっかく来(き)たんだから、ゆっくりしていって
- <code>せっかくの + 名詞(めいし)</code> - the noun itself is the precious thing:
  せっかくの休(やす)み, せっかくの機会(きかい), せっかくのごちそう

Two frozen expressions are worth having ready-made. <code>せっかくですが</code>
declines an offer while acknowledging the trouble behind it -
「せっかくですが、今日(きょう)は遠慮(えんりょ)しておきます」. And
<code>せっかくだから</code> stands alone as "well, while we're at it" - the phrase
that talks a group into dessert.

A bare `せっかく` with an ordinary flat predicate sounds unfinished:
❌せっかく来(き)ました leaves the listener waiting for the rest.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>せっかく</b>作(つく)ったのに、誰(だれ)も食(た)べてくれなかった。
I went to the trouble of making it and nobody would eat it.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>せっかく</b>来(き)たんだから、お茶(ちゃ)でも飲(の)んでいってよ。
You've come all this way - stay and have a cup of tea at least.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>せっかくの</b>日曜日(にちようび)が雨(あめ)で台無(だいな)しになった。
Our one precious Sunday was ruined by rain.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>せっかく</b>早(はや)く起(お)きて駅(えき)まで走(はし)ったのに、電車(でんしゃ)は目(め)の前(まえ)で行(い)ってしまった。
After getting up early and running all the way to the station, I watched the train pull out right in front of me.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everyday and emotional. It is used by everyone, in speech and in writing, and it
is neither formal nor slangy - but it is never neutral. The word always contains a
small ache: something cost something, and it is about to be wasted, or it had
better not be.

That is why <code>せっかくですが</code> is such a useful refusal. Turning down an
invitation flatly is hard in Japanese; opening with せっかく credits the other
person's effort before you decline, and 「せっかくお誘(さそ)いいただいたのに、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません」is about as graceful as a refusal gets.

The effort does not have to be the speaker's own, and it does not even have to be
anyone's - luck counts too. せっかく席(せき)が空(あ)いたんだから is fine
although nobody worked for the empty seat. What matters is that the situation is
rare and would be a shame to squander.

Written in kana in ordinary text. The kanji 折角(せっかく) exists and turns up in
older or more decorative writing; the reading is not deducible from the
characters, which is one reason kana won.

## Contrast

### せっかく vs わざわざ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わざわざ` is about **someone going out of their way**.
`せっかく` is about **the value that effort or chance created**, and whether it
survives.

</div>

<pre>
<b>わざわざ</b>駅(えき)まで迎(むか)えに来(き)てくれてありがとう。
Thank you for coming all the way to the station to meet me.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>せっかく</b>迎(むか)えに来(き)てくれたのに、待(ま)ち合(あ)わせ場所(ばしょ)を間違(まちが)えてしまった。
You came out to meet me and then I went to the wrong meeting point.
</pre>

`わざわざ` describes the trouble taken and usually thanks someone for it - or, said
coldly, accuses them of doing something unnecessary. `せっかく` looks past the
trouble to what became of it. Only `せっかく` can attach to a noun with `の`, and
only `せっかく` works when no one made any effort at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ せっかく東京(とうきょう)に来(き)ましたが、富士山(ふじさん)が見(み)えませんでした。 → ✅ <b>せっかく</b>東京(とうきょう)に来(き)た<b>のに</b>、富士山(ふじさん)が見(み)えませんでした。
  `が` is a neutral "but" and leaves the disappointment unexpressed. `せっかく`
  wants のに, which is the ending that carries regret.

- ❌ せっかくな休(やす)みが雨(あめ)だった。 → ✅ <b>せっかくの</b>休(やす)みが雨(あめ)だった。
  Before a noun it takes `の`, not `な`. It is not a な-adjective.

- ❌ せっかく宿題(しゅくだい)をしました。 → ✅ <b>せっかく</b>宿題(しゅくだい)をしたのに、先生(せんせい)が集(あつ)めなかった。
  A complete sentence needs the second half. On its own the adverb has raised an
  expectation and abandoned it.

- ❌ せっかく傘(かさ)を忘(わす)れて濡(ぬ)れました。 → ✅ 傘(かさ)を忘(わす)れて濡(ぬ)れました。
  Ordinary bad luck is not `せっかく`. There has to be something valuable - effort,
  a rare chance - going to waste.

## Practice

**1.** ____予約(よやく)したのに、店(みせ)が閉(し)まっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>せっかく</b>予約(よやく)したのに、店(みせ)が閉(し)まっていた。

The `のに` at the end is the giveaway: something was done for nothing. `せっかく`
is what tells you the booking cost the speaker some trouble and that the trouble
is what was wasted.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: （せっかく・わざわざ）遠(とお)くから来(き)ていただき、ありがとうございます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>わざわざ</b>遠(とお)くから来(き)ていただき、ありがとうございます。

This is a thank-you for trouble taken, which is precisely `わざわざ`. `せっかく`
would imply the journey was about to be wasted, which is not what you say to a
guest who has just arrived.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) せっかく京都(きょうと)に来(き)たんだから、お寺(てら)を見(み)ていこう。
(b) せっかく京都(きょうと)に来(き)たのに、雨(あめ)でどこにも行(い)けなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both open identically, and the ending decides everything. In (a) `んだから` turns
the rare chance into a reason to act, so the sentence is an invitation. In (b)
`のに` reports that the chance was lost, so the sentence is a complaint. This is
the clearest demonstration that the adverb only sets up the premise - the
conjunction supplies the verdict.

</details>

# ～せいか

**Meaning** · perhaps because of ～; maybe it's because ～

`せいか` offers a cause without committing to it. The speaker has noticed
something - a headache, a quiet street, a change in someone's mood - and is
guessing at the reason out loud.

## Usage

`せいか` is `せい` ("fault, cause") plus the question particle `か`, and that `か`
is the whole point: it turns a stated cause into a suggested one. It attaches to
the clause or noun that names the cause, and the result follows.

- Verb, plain form: 歩(ある)きすぎた<b>せいか</b>、足(あし)が痛(いた)い
- い-adjective, plain form: 暑(あつ)い<b>せいか</b>、客(きゃく)が少(すく)ない
- な-adjective + `な`: 静(しず)かな<b>せいか</b>、よく眠(ねむ)れた
- Noun + `の`: 年(とし)<b>のせいか</b>、字(じ)が読(よ)みにくくなった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せい` assigns **blame**. The result after `せいか` is normally
something unwelcome, or at least something the speaker did not plan.

</div>

That bias is inherited from `せいで`, "because of (and it was bad)". `せいか`
softens it in two ways at once - the cause is only a guess, and the guess is
hedged - so it is the mildest member of the family and can be used for results
that are merely surprising rather than harmful. It is still wrong for good news:
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだせいか元気(げんき)になった sounds as though
recovering were a nuisance. That sentence wants おかげか.

One fixed phrase to learn whole: <code>気(き)のせい</code>, "it's just your
imagination", and its hedged form <code>気(き)のせいか</code>, "maybe I'm
imagining it" - 「気(き)のせいか、部屋(へや)が寒(さむ)い」.

## Examples

<pre>
寝不足(ねぶそく)の<b>せいか</b>、頭(あたま)が痛(いた)い。
Maybe it's the lack of sleep, but I have a headache.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)の<b>せいか</b>、今日(きょう)は客(きゃく)が少(すく)ない。
Perhaps because of the rain, there aren't many customers today.
</pre>

<pre>
緊張(きんちょう)していた<b>せいか</b>、名前(なまえ)を書(か)くのを忘(わす)れてしまった。
I must have been nervous - I forgot to write my name on it.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)してから通勤(つうきん)が長(なが)くなった<b>せいか</b>、最近(さいきん)本(ほん)を読(よ)む時間(じかん)が増(ふ)えた。
Maybe it's the longer commute since I moved, but lately I've had more time for reading.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very common in ordinary speech. It also appears in writing,
though a report that wants to hedge a cause more formally will use
<code>～ためか</code> or <code>～からか</code>, which carry no blame.

The hedge is genuine, not merely polite. A speaker who says
年(とし)のせいか really is unsure whether age is the reason; if they were sure
they would say 年(とし)のせいで. So `せいか` is a good way to raise a possible
cause without arguing for it - and, when the cause involves someone else, a good
way to avoid accusing them. 「昨日(きのう)の話(はなし)のせいか、元気(げんき)がないみたいだ」floats a reason for someone's low mood without
pinning it on anyone.

Because `せい` means fault, using it about a person to their face is pointed.
あなたのせいで is an accusation; あなたのせいか is a slightly softened accusation,
not a neutral remark. If no blame is intended, restructure the sentence rather
than reaching for `か` to fix it.

## Contrast

### ～せいか vs ～せいで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せいで` **states** the cause and blames it. `せいか` **guesses**
at the cause and only half-blames it.

</div>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の<b>せいで</b>試合(しあい)が中止(ちゅうし)になった。
The game was cancelled because of the typhoon.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいている<b>せいか</b>、頭(あたま)が重(おも)い。
Maybe it's the typhoon coming, but my head feels heavy.
</pre>

The cancellation has an established cause, so `せいで` is right and `せいか` would
sound as though the organisers were unsure why they cancelled. The headache does
not, so `せいか` is right and `せいで` would claim a piece of medical knowledge the
speaker does not have.

### ～せいか vs ～おかげか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same structure, opposite sign. `せい` for a cause you resent,
`おかげ` for one you are grateful to.

</div>

<pre>
よく寝(ね)た<b>おかげか</b>、今日(きょう)は調子(ちょうし)がいい。
Maybe it's because I slept well, but I feel good today.
</pre>

<pre>
寝(ね)すぎた<b>せいか</b>、一日(いちにち)ぼんやりしている。
Maybe I overslept - I've been fuzzy all day.
</pre>

Both are hedged, and both are polite. Choosing the wrong one is not a grammar
error but a tone error: `せいか` before good news reads as ingratitude, and
`おかげか` before bad news reads as sarcasm - which is, in fact, exactly how it is
used when someone means it that way.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雨(あめ)せいか、道(みち)が空(す)いている。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)<b>の</b>せいか、道(みち)が空(す)いている。
  After a noun the `の` is obligatory. `せい` is itself a noun and has to be
  linked to the one in front of it.

- ❌ 便利(べんり)のせいか、よく使(つか)っている。 → ✅ 便利(べんり)<b>な</b>せいか、よく使(つか)っている。
  A な-adjective takes `な`, not `の`. Only true nouns take `の`.

- ❌ いい先生(せんせい)に習(なら)った<b>せいか</b>、上手(じょうず)になった。 → ✅ いい先生(せんせい)に習(なら)った<b>おかげか</b>、上手(じょうず)になった。
  Grammatical, and insulting. `せい` blames; improving under a good teacher calls
  for `おかげ`.

- ❌ 寝不足(ねぶそく)のせいか、頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いです<b>か</b>。 → ✅ 寝不足(ねぶそく)のせいか、頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いです。
  The `か` in `せいか` is already the whole hedge; the sentence is a statement, not
  a question, and does not take a second `か` at the end.

## Practice

**1.** 新(あたら)しい靴(くつ)____、足(あし)が痛(いた)くなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しい靴(くつ)<b>のせいか</b>、足(あし)が痛(いた)くなった。

A noun precedes, so `の` is needed before `せい`. The result is unwelcome and the
speaker has not verified the cause, which is exactly the situation `せいか` was
made for.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)いている（せいか・おかげか）、体重(たいじゅう)が減(へ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)いている<b>おかげか</b>、体重(たいじゅう)が減(へ)った。

Losing weight through daily walking is a welcome result, and `せい` would frame
the speaker's own healthy habit as something to complain about.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 暑(あつ)さのせいで食欲(しょくよく)がない。
(b) 暑(あつ)さのせいか食欲(しょくよく)がない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats the heat as the established reason and says so flatly; the speaker is
explaining, and could go on to say what they intend to do about it. (b) is
thinking aloud - the appetite is gone, the heat is the best guess, and the speaker
leaves room for it to be something else. In conversation (b) invites the listener
to agree or offer another idea, while (a) closes the question.

</details>

# 一応

**Meaning** · いちおう - for now; just to be safe; more or less

<code>一応(いちおう)</code> marks something as done to the minimum acceptable standard, or
done as a precaution rather than out of conviction. It is one of the most useful
hedges in spoken Japanese and one of the easiest to overuse.

## Usage

<code>一応(いちおう)</code> is an adverb. It goes in front of the predicate, or at the head
of the sentence when it is hedging the whole statement. It pulls in three
directions, and which one you get is decided entirely by context.

- **Just in case.** The action is a precaution: 一応(いちおう)傘(かさ)を持(も)っていく, 一応(いちおう)確認(かくにん)しておきます. This sense
  pairs constantly with <code>～ておく</code>, which is itself the "do it in
  advance" form.
- **For the time being; provisionally.** The thing is settled, but not finally:
  一応(いちおう)予約(よやく)しました, 一応(いちおう)これで完成(かんせい)です.
- **More or less; technically.** A hedge on a claim, very often about oneself:
  一応(いちおう)終(お)わりました, 「一応(いちおう)大学(だいがく)で習(なら)いました」.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>一応(いちおう)</code> always lowers the temperature of the claim
it modifies. Whatever the sentence asserts, it asserts a little less firmly.

</div>

It also works as a complete answer on its own. 「終(お)わった？」「一応(いちおう)。」means "yes - technically", and the listener will hear the
reservation.

The kanji is standard and normal in writing; the kana spelling いちおう appears in
casual text. There is no difference in meaning.

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るかもしれないので、<b>一応(いちおう)</b>傘(かさ)を持(も)っていきます。
It might rain, so I'll take an umbrella just in case.
</pre>

<pre>
宿題(しゅくだい)は<b>一応(いちおう)</b>終(お)わりました。
I've finished the homework - more or less.
</pre>

<pre>
出(で)かける前(まえ)に、<b>一応(いちおう)</b>窓(まど)のかぎを確(たし)かめておいた。
Before going out I checked the window locks, just to be safe.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ決定(けってい)ではありませんが、<b>一応(いちおう)</b>来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)ということでお願(ねが)いします。
It isn't final yet, but let's provisionally say next Friday.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Thoroughly everyday, in speech and in writing, and not marked for age or gender.
Japanese speakers reach for it dozens of times a day.

The reservation it carries is real, and that has consequences at work. Reporting
「一応(いちおう)やっておきました」tells your boss the job was done to the
minimum and invites them to check it; if you actually did it properly, say
「終(お)わりました」and leave the hedge out. In the other direction, <code>一応(いちおう)</code> is a
graceful way to be modest about your own ability -
「一応(いちおう)話(はな)せます」claims competence while declining to boast -
and it is standard practice in Japanese self-presentation.

Attaching it to someone else's work is where it turns rude:
「一応(いちおう)できていますね」says the thing passes, barely. Applied to a
person's status it is worse - 一応(いちおう)部長(ぶちょう)です about your
manager means "he's nominally the department head", which is an insult. About
yourself the same words are ordinary modesty.

## Contrast

### 一応(いちおう) vs とりあえず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とりあえず` is about **order**: this first, the rest later.
<code>一応(いちおう)</code> is about **degree**: this much, and no more claimed.

</div>

<pre>
<b>とりあえず</b>ビールをお願(ねが)いします。
We'll start with a beer, thanks.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>一応(いちおう)</b>全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)みましたが、よく分(わ)かりませんでした。
I did read all of it, for what that's worth, but I didn't really follow.
</pre>

The beer is first, not minimal - nobody is saying it is a barely adequate beer.
The reading is complete but the speaker will not vouch for how well it was done.
The two overlap in sentences like 一応(いちおう)／とりあえず連絡(れんらく)しておきます, where the contact is both the first step and a
precaution, and either word is natural. Where they differ, ask whether you mean
"before anything else" or "at least this much".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは一応(いちおう)課長(かちょう)です。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんは課長(かちょう)です。
  Used of another person's rank, it means "he holds the title, technically". Save
  it for yourself.

- ❌ 一応(いちおう)大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だから、絶対(ぜったい)心配(しんぱい)しないで。 → ✅ 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だから、心配(しんぱい)しないで。
  The hedge and the word 絶対(ぜったい) pull against each other. If you want to
  reassure someone, do not simultaneously tell them your assurance is provisional.

- ❌ 一応(いちおう)ビールをください。 → ✅ <b>とりあえず</b>ビールをください。
  Ordering "a beer to start with" is about sequence. <code>一応(いちおう)</code> would
  suggest a beer will do, grudgingly.

- ❌ 一応(いちおう)な返事(へんじ)をもらいました。 → ✅ <b>一応(いちおう)</b>返事(へんじ)をもらいました。
  It is an adverb and does not take `な` or `の` before a noun.

## Practice

**1.** 使(つか)い方(かた)は分(わ)かっていますが、____説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)んでおきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>一応(いちおう)</b>説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)んでおきます。

The speaker already knows how to use it, so reading the manual is a precaution
rather than a need - and the `～ておく` ending is the form this sense of the
adverb most often travels with.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 時間(じかん)がないので、（一応(いちおう)・とりあえず）急(きゅう)ぎの分(ぶん)だけ先(さき)に送(おく)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>とりあえず</b>急(きゅう)ぎの分(ぶん)だけ先(さき)に送(おく)ります。

The sentence is about what comes first, with the rest to follow. That is sequence,
which is `とりあえず`'s job, not a claim being hedged.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 資料(しりょう)を作(つく)りました。
(b) 一応(いちおう)資料(しりょう)を作(つく)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a straightforward report: the document exists and the speaker stands behind
it. (b) hands it over with a reservation - it may be rough, it may need checking,
and the speaker is not claiming it is finished work. Said about your own output
(b) is modest and normal; said about a colleague's, the same hedge becomes a
criticism.

</details>

# 一旦

**Meaning** · いったん - once ～ happens (there is no going back); or: for the
moment

Two jobs, one word. Before a conditional, <code>一旦(いったん)</code> marks a threshold:
cross it and the consequence is unavoidable. Before an ordinary verb it means
"for now", pausing something that will be resumed later.

## Usage

**Sense 1 - the threshold.** This use is grammatically incomplete without a
conditional. <code>一旦(いったん)</code> opens the clause and the clause ends in
`と`, `たら`, `ば`, or - most idiomatically - `～てしまうと`.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 一旦(いったん)～と／たら needs a **second half that says the
result cannot be undone or stopped**. If nothing irreversible follows, the adverb
has nothing to do.

</div>

- 一旦(いったん)決(き)めたら、最後(さいご)までやる。
- 一旦(いったん)燃(も)え始(はじ)めると、消(け)すのは難(むずか)しい。
- 一旦(いったん)口(くち)に出(だ)してしまうと、もう取(と)り消(け)せない。

The typical second half is a statement of impossibility, of inevitability, or a
resolution: 難(むずか)しい, ～ない, もう～できない, 必(かなら)ず～.

**Sense 2 - the pause.** Here <code>一旦(いったん)</code> is a plain adverb meaning "once,
for the moment, and then we'll continue": 一旦(いったん)家(いえ)に帰(かえ)る, 一旦(いったん)中止(ちゅうし)する, 一旦(いったん)保留(ほりゅう)にする. It implies the interruption is temporary and that
something will follow.

The two senses do not blur in practice: a conditional after it means sense 1, and
its absence means sense 2.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>一旦(いったん)</b>決(き)めたら、途中(とちゅう)でやめないでください。
Once you've decided, please don't give up halfway.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>一旦(いったん)</b>外(そと)に出(で)て、十分(じゅっぷん)後(ご)にまた集(あつ)まりましょう。
Let's step outside for now and gather again in ten minutes.
</pre>

<pre>
このアプリは<b>一旦(いったん)</b>使(つか)い始(はじ)めると、なかなか手放(てばな)せなくなる。
Once you start using this app you find you can't put it down.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は台風(たいふう)のため<b>一旦(いったん)</b>中止(ちゅうし)し、天候(てんこう)が回復(かいふく)してから再開(さいかい)する予定(よてい)です。
Work will be suspended for the time being because of the typhoon and is due to resume once the weather improves.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and formal-spoken. In sense 1 it sounds like a maxim - which is why it
shows up in proverbial statements, safety warnings and speeches - and in sense 2
it is standard business Japanese. 「一旦(いったん)持(も)ち帰(かえ)って検討(けんとう)します」("let me take this back to the office and consider it")
is one of the most heavily used sentences in Japanese corporate life.

In relaxed conversation the same ideas come out differently. For sense 1, a
friend says 一度(いちど)始(はじ)めたら instead. For sense 2, they say
とりあえず or ちょっと - although note that 「一旦(いったん)帰(かえ)るね」is perfectly ordinary between adults; the word is formal, not
stiff.

Not marked for age or gender. It is always written with the kanji;
`いったん` in kana is unusual and can be misread as the noun 一端(いったん)
("one end; a part").

## Contrast

### 一旦(いったん) vs 一度(いちど)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>一度(いちど)</code> counts an occasion: **one time**.
<code>一旦(いったん)</code> counts nothing - it marks a **switch being thrown**.

</div>

<pre>
富士山(ふじさん)には<b>一度(いちど)</b>登(のぼ)ったことがあります。
I've climbed Mt Fuji once.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>一旦(いったん)</b>登(のぼ)り始(はじ)めたら、頂上(ちょうじょう)まで行(い)くしかない。
Once you start climbing there's nothing for it but to go to the top.
</pre>

Only <code>一度(いちど)</code> can be a count - ❌一旦(いったん)行(い)ったことがあります is not Japanese. In conditional clauses the two overlap and
一度(いちど)決(き)めたら is the everyday version of the same thought;
<code>一旦(いったん)</code> is the more formal and slightly more emphatic choice, and it
adds the sense that the decision was a point of no return rather than simply an
event that happened once.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 一旦(いったん)京都(きょうと)に行(い)ったことがあります。 → ✅ <b>一度(いちど)</b>京都(きょうと)に行(い)ったことがあります。
  "Once" in the sense of "on one occasion" is 一度(いちど). <code>一旦(いったん)</code>
  never counts.

- ❌ 一旦(いったん)始(はじ)めると楽(たの)しいです。 → ✅ <b>一旦(いったん)</b>始(はじ)めると、やめられなくなります。
  The threshold reading needs a consequence you cannot escape. Simply being
  enjoyable is not one, and the sentence reads as a false alarm.

- ❌ 一旦(いったん)決(き)めて、変(か)えないでください。 → ✅ <b>一旦(いったん)</b>決(き)め<b>たら</b>、変(か)えないでください。
  In sense 1 the て-form is not enough; the clause has to be a genuine conditional
  in と, たら or ば.

- ❌ 一旦(いったん)やめて、もうやりません。 → ✅ 一旦(いったん)やめて、来月(らいげつ)また始(はじ)めます。
  Sense 2 promises a resumption. Stopping permanently is just やめる.

## Practice

**1.** ____習慣(しゅうかん)になってしまうと、直(なお)すのは大変(たいへん)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>一旦(いったん)</b>習慣(しゅうかん)になってしまうと、直(なお)すのは大変(たいへん)だ。

The `～てしまうと` clause and the difficulty in the second half are the two halves
of the threshold frame: cross the line and the consequence follows whether you
like it or not.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: 沖縄(おきなわ)には（一旦(いったん)・一度(いちど)）行(い)ったことがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

沖縄(おきなわ)には<b>一度(いちど)</b>行(い)ったことがあります。

The sentence counts visits, and only 一度(いちど) counts.
<code>一旦(いったん)</code> marks a threshold and cannot be attached to a number of
occasions at all.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 一旦(いったん)会社(かいしゃ)に戻(もど)ります。
(b) 一旦(いったん)会社(かいしゃ)に戻(もど)ったら、もう出(で)られません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the "for the moment" sense: the speaker is going back to the office and
will be out again, and this is ordinary business politeness. (b) is the threshold
sense, and the conditional is what creates it - going back becomes a line that,
once crossed, traps the speaker for the rest of the day. Same adverb, and the
ending decides which of its two lives it is leading.

</details>

# いつの間にか

**Meaning** · いつのまにか - before I knew it; without my noticing

<code>いつの間(ま)にか</code> reports a change that has already finished while the speaker
was not looking. The literal parts say "at what moment, I wonder" - and the
answer is that nobody was watching.

## Usage

The phrase behaves as a single adverb. It stands at the head of the sentence or
immediately before the predicate, and both positions are equally natural.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the predicate has to describe a **change already complete**.
In practice that means `～ていた`, `～ていました`, `～てしまった`, or
`～になっていた`.

</div>

- いつの間(ま)にか雨(あめ)が止(や)んでいた。
- いつの間(ま)にか外(そと)が暗(くら)くなっていた。
- いつの間(ま)にか寝(ね)てしまった。

A plain present or future will not work: ❌いつの間(ま)にか終(お)わります
claims the speaker will fail to notice something that has not happened yet, which
is not a thing a sentence can say. The whole expression looks backwards from a
moment of discovery.

It also sits badly with a deliberate act of the speaker's own. You cannot be
unaware of something you chose to do, so ❌いつの間(ま)にか手紙(てがみ)を書(か)きました is odd, while いつの間(ま)にか寝(ね)てしまった is fine -
falling asleep is not a decision.

Drop the `か` and you get a question rather than a statement:
<code>いつの間(ま)に作(つく)ったの？</code> - "when on earth did you make
this?" The `か` is what turns the unanswered question into a description.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>雨(あめ)が止(や)んでいた。
The rain had stopped without my noticing.
</pre>

<pre>
本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいるうちに、<b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>寝(ね)てしまった。
I was reading, and before I knew it I'd fallen asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)の古(ふる)い本屋(ほんや)が、<b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>コンビニに変(か)わっていた。
The old bookshop by the station had turned into a convenience store at some point without my realising.
</pre>

<pre>
入学(にゅうがく)したのがついこの間(あいだ)のようなのに、<b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>卒業(そつぎょう)の季節(きせつ)になっていた。
It feels like she started only the other day, and yet somehow graduation season has come round.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral: it is at home in casual speech, in essays, in song lyrics and in
news writing alike, and it is not marked for age or gender. It is slightly
evocative - it belongs to the same emotional family as "before you know it" in
English - so a purely technical report would prefer a flat
気(き)づかないうちに.

The feeling it carries is mild wonder at the passage of time, and it is used more
often for changes the speaker does not mind than for ones they resent. Children
growing up, seasons turning, a friendship forming: all classic
<code>いつの間(ま)にか</code> sentences. It can also mark a mildly unwelcome discovery -
いつの間(ま)にか財布(さいふ)がなくなっていた - but the tone stays puzzled
rather than accusing.

A softer literary variant, いつのまにやら, appears in fiction and in older writing.
The kanji spelling 何時(いつ)の間(ま)にか exists but is rare and slows the
reader down; the standard written form leaves いつ in kana.

## Contrast

### いつの間(ま)にか vs 知(し)らないうちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>いつの間(ま)にか</code> wonders **when** it happened.
<code>知(し)らないうちに</code> insists that the speaker **was not informed**.

</div>

<pre>
<b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>子(こ)どもたちが仲良(なかよ)くなっていた。
The children had become friends at some point without anyone noticing.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>知(し)らないうちに</b>会議(かいぎ)の日程(にってい)が変(か)わっていた。
The meeting had been rescheduled without my being told.
</pre>

The first is warm; the second has an edge, because "while I didn't know" implies
somebody else did. When a decision has been taken over your head,
知(し)らないうちに勝手(かって)に決(き)められていた is the complaint you
want, and <code>いつの間(ま)にか</code> would sound oddly mellow about it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ いつの間(ま)にか春(はる)になります。 → ✅ <b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>春(はる)になっていました。
  The expression looks back from the moment of discovery, so the predicate has to
  be a completed change - normally `～ていた`.

- ❌ いつの間(ま)に雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もっていた。 → ✅ <b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もっていた。
  Without the `か` the phrase is an interrogative and the sentence is left half a
  question. Keep the `か` for the statement.

- ❌ いつの間(ま)にか部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けました。 → ✅ 気(き)がついたら部屋(へや)が片付(かたづ)いていました。
  You cannot fail to notice your own deliberate tidying. Either the subject has to
  be someone else, or the verb has to be one that happens to you.

- ❌ いつの間(ま)にかに終(お)わっていた。 → ✅ <b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>終(お)わっていた。
  No extra particle. The `に` is already inside the phrase, and the whole thing
  attaches to the verb directly.

## Practice

**1.** 忙(いそが)しくしているうちに、____一年(いちねん)が過(す)ぎていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>いつの間(ま)にか</b>一年(いちねん)が過(す)ぎていた。

The `ていた` ending and the sense of time having slipped by are exactly what this
phrase is for. The speaker is not reporting the year ending, but the surprise of
discovering that it has.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural phrase: 相談(そうだん)もなく、（いつの間(ま)にか・知(し)らないうちに）私(わたし)の担当(たんとう)が変(か)わっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>知(し)らないうちに</b>私(わたし)の担当(たんとう)が変(か)わっていた。

「相談(そうだん)もなく」makes it clear that someone decided this and did not
tell the speaker, which is the complaint that phrase carries. いつの間(ま)にか would
treat a deliberate reassignment as if it had drifted into place by itself.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) いつの間(ま)に買(か)ったの。
(b) いつの間(ま)にか増(ふ)えていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a real question, directed at someone: when did you buy it, and how did I
miss it? The listener is expected to answer. (b) is a statement about the
speaker's own discovery, with no one to ask - the `か` closes the question off and
leaves only the impression that time passed unnoticed.

</details>

# 万が一

**Meaning** · まんがいち - in the unlikely event that ～; if by any chance

<code>万(まん)が一(いち)</code> says "one chance in ten thousand". It flags the
condition that follows as remote and, almost always, as something you would rather
avoid - which is why the sentence usually ends in a precaution.

## Usage

<code>万(まん)が一(いち)</code> opens the clause and hands the work to a conditional.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>万(まん)が一(いち)</code> **requires a conditional** -
たら, ば, なら, と, or 場合(ばあい)は. On its own it is only half a sentence.

</div>

- 万(まん)が一(いち)地震(じしん)が起(お)き**たら**、テーブルの下(した)に入(はい)ってください。
- 万(まん)が一(いち)間(ま)に合(あ)わ**なければ**、連絡(れんらく)します。
- 万(まん)が一(いち)の場合(ばあい)**は**、この番号(ばんごう)にかけてください。

Two other shapes are worth knowing:

- <code>万(まん)が一(いち)の + 名詞(めいし)</code> - "of emergency":
  万(まん)が一(いち)の場合(ばあい), 万(まん)が一(いち)の事故(じこ)
- <code>万(まん)が一(いち)に備(そな)える</code> - a fixed phrase, "to
  prepare for the worst", where the adverb has hardened into a noun

The second half is normally an instruction, a precaution, or a plan: ～てください,
～ことになっている, ～つもりです. A <code>万(まん)が一(いち)</code> clause followed by
nothing actionable feels unfinished.

The shorter 万一(まんいち) is the same word with the `が` dropped. It is a
degree more clipped and more written, and it is what you will see on printed
safety notices.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>万(まん)が一(いち)</b>遅(おく)れたら、先(さき)に始(はじ)めてください。
If by any chance I'm late, please start without me.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>万(まん)が一(いち)</b>のために、保険(ほけん)に入(はい)っておきました。
I took out insurance, just in case the worst happens.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>万(まん)が一(いち)</b>火事(かじ)が起(お)きた場合(ばあい)は、エレベーターを使(つか)わずに階段(かいだん)で避難(ひなん)してください。
In the event of a fire, please evacuate by the stairs and do not use the lift.
</pre>

<pre>
大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だとは思(おも)いますが、<b>万(まん)が一(いち)</b>データが消(き)えてしまうと取(と)り戻(もど)せませんので、必(かなら)ずバックアップをお願(ねが)いします。
I'm sure it will be fine, but on the small chance the data is lost there's no recovering it, so please do take a backup.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal-leaning but in constant everyday use. You meet it in announcements, safety
briefings, insurance documents, business email and instruction manuals, and also
in ordinary careful speech between adults - a parent saying
「万(まん)が一(いち)のときはここに電話(でんわ)して」is completely natural.
It is not marked for age or gender.

Two things it always adds to a plain もし. First, **improbability**: the speaker
is signalling that they do not expect this. Second, **undesirability**: the
default reading of a <code>万(まん)が一(いち)</code> clause is that the event would be
bad. Both matter, and getting them wrong is what makes learner sentences sound
strange. 万(まん)が一(いち)明日(あした)雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったら, said
of a rainy season forecast, is odd not because it is ungrammatical but because
rain tomorrow is neither remote nor a catastrophe.

Used of a windfall - 万(まん)が一(いち)宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たったら -
the word still works, but it reads as self-deprecating or jokey, because it is
lending the language of disaster planning to good luck. That is a stylistic
choice, not an error, and Japanese speakers make it deliberately.

There is also a euphemistic use: 「万(まん)が一(いち)のことがあったら」means
"if something should happen to him", i.e. if he dies. Recognise it; it is the
polite way the subject is raised.

## Contrast

### 万(まん)が一(いち) vs もし・もしも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もし` marks a clause as hypothetical and says nothing about
how likely it is. <code>万(まん)が一(いち)</code> says the chance is tiny **and** the
outcome is unwelcome.

</div>

<pre>
<b>もし</b>時間(じかん)があったら、映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きませんか。
If you have time, shall we go and see a film?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>万(まん)が一(いち)</b>時間(じかん)に間(ま)に合(あ)わなかったら、タクシーを使(つか)ってください。
On the off-chance you can't make it in time, take a taxi.
</pre>

Swapping them into each other's sentences shows the difference. もし in the second
would drain the urgency out of a contingency plan; <code>万(まん)が一(いち)</code> in
the first would make an invitation sound like a disaster drill. `もしも` sits
between them - more emphatic than もし, but without the "and it would be bad"
component.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 万(まん)が一(いち)、傘(かさ)を持(も)っていきます。 → ✅ <b>万(まん)が一(いち)</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ<b>たら</b>困(こま)るので、傘(かさ)を持(も)っていきます。
  A conditional clause is not optional. Without one the adverb has no condition to
  call unlikely. For a bare "just in case" before a verb, use
  一応(いちおう) or 念(ねん)のため.

- ❌ 万(まん)が一(いち)合格(ごうかく)したら、うれしいです。 → ✅ <b>もし</b>合格(ごうかく)したら、うれしいです。
  Passing is a good outcome, so the disaster framing works against the sentence.
  Said about yourself it can pass as modesty, but it is not the neutral choice.

- ❌ 万(まん)が一(いち)来週(らいしゅう)会議(かいぎ)があったら、参加(さんか)します。 → ✅ <b>もし</b>来週(らいしゅう)会議(かいぎ)があったら、参加(さんか)します。
  An ordinary uncertain event is not a one-in-ten-thousand event. Reserve the word
  for things you are genuinely not expecting.

- ❌ 万(まん)が一(いち)が起(お)きたら、連絡(れんらく)してください。 → ✅ <b>万(まん)が一(いち)のこと</b>が起(お)きたら、連絡(れんらく)してください。
  As a noun it needs `のこと` or <code>の場合(ばあい)</code>. The bare adverb cannot take a
  subject particle.

## Practice

**1.** ____停電(ていでん)になったら、この懐中電灯(かいちゅうでんとう)を使(つか)ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>万(まん)が一(いち)</b>停電(ていでん)になったら、この懐中電灯(かいちゅうでんとう)を使(つか)ってください。

A power cut is unlikely and unwelcome, and the second half is an instruction for
dealing with it - the three ingredients this adverb wants. The `たら` supplies the
conditional it cannot do without.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: （もし・万(まん)が一(いち)）よかったら、今度(こんど)うちに遊(あそ)びに来(き)てください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もし</b>よかったら、今度(こんど)うちに遊(あそ)びに来(き)てください。

This is an invitation, and there is nothing remote or unfortunate about the
listener being free. <code>万(まん)が一(いち)</code> would imply the speaker considers a
visit an unlikely calamity.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もし事故(じこ)が起(お)きたら、すぐ報告(ほうこく)してください。
(b) 万(まん)が一(いち)事故(じこ)が起(お)きたら、すぐ報告(ほうこく)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are correct instructions. (a) simply sets up a hypothetical and reads as a
neutral rule in a manual. (b) adds that the speaker regards an accident as very
unlikely, which softens the instruction into reassurance - we do not expect this,
but here is what to do. In a safety briefing (b) is the more considerate wording,
because it says the risk is small before it says what to do about it.

</details>

# および・ならびに

**Meaning** · および / ならびに - and; as well as (formal written enumeration)

Two connectives you will read far more often than you will say. <code>及(およ)び</code>
joins items in official prose - forms, notices, regulations, manuals - and
<code>並(なら)びに</code> does the same job one level up, joining groups rather than
items.

## Usage

Both connect **nouns and noun phrases**. They stand between the items, never at
the head of a sentence.

- 住所(じゅうしょ)及(およ)び氏名(しめい)
- 保護者(ほごしゃ)並(なら)びに関係者(かんけいしゃ)の皆様(みなさま)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - when a list has **two levels**, legal drafting puts
<code>及(およ)び</code> on the **inner, smaller** grouping and <code>並(なら)びに</code> on the
**outer, larger** one.

</div>

So 「A及(およ)びB並(なら)びにC」parses as (A and B) and C - never as A and (B
and C). This is a fixed convention of Japanese statutory drafting, and it is worth
knowing because it is the only way to read a long clause correctly. The
corresponding pair for "or" is arranged the **opposite** way round:
又(また)は takes the larger grouping and 若(も)しくは the smaller. Do not
assume the two systems mirror each other, because they do not.

Outside legal text the distinction relaxes. In speeches and formal announcements
<code>並(なら)びに</code> is simply the grander of the two, used when the writer wants
weight: ご来賓(らいひん)の皆様(みなさま)並(なら)びにご家族(かぞく)の皆様(みなさま).

Statutes also let <code>及(およ)び</code> join predicates, with a comma:
「報告(ほうこく)し、及(およ)び記録(きろく)する」. Recognise it; do not
imitate it. Treat both words as noun connectors and you will never be wrong in
ordinary formal writing.

## Examples

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)に住所(じゅうしょ)<b>及(およ)び</b>氏名(しめい)をご記入(きにゅう)ください。
Please enter your address and full name on the application form.
</pre>

<pre>
館内(かんない)での飲食(いんしょく)<b>及(およ)び</b>喫煙(きつえん)はご遠慮(えんりょ)ください。
Eating, drinking and smoking are not permitted inside the building.
</pre>

<pre>
本日(ほんじつ)は卒業生(そつぎょうせい)<b>並(なら)びに</b>ご家族(かぞく)の皆様(みなさま)にお集(あつ)まりいただきました。
Today we are joined by our graduating students and by their families.
</pre>

<pre>
第一条(だいいちじょう)及(およ)び第二条(だいにじょう)<b>並(なら)びに</b>附則(ふそく)の規定(きてい)は、来年度(らいねんど)から適用(てきよう)する。
Articles 1 and 2, together with the supplementary provisions, shall apply from the next fiscal year.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

These are reading words. <code>及(およ)び</code> appears on every other government form,
in contracts, in academic papers, in product manuals and on public signage, and it
is completely standard there. Said out loud in conversation it is comic - the
equivalent of saying "and/or the aforementioned" to a friend.

What you would say instead, in order of formality:

- Speech, everyday: 住所(じゅうしょ)<b>と</b>名前(なまえ) - `と` is the plain
  connector for a closed list, `や` for an open one
- Speech, polite or presentational: 住所(じゅうしょ)<b>と</b>名前(なまえ)、
  or それに to add an item
- Writing, formal but not legal: 及(およ)び

<code>並(なら)びに</code> is narrower still. Outside statutes you will meet it mainly in
ceremonial address - opening remarks at a graduation, an awards citation, a
formal letter of thanks - where its function is largely to sound solemn. Reading
it aloud is normal in exactly those settings and nowhere else.

Neither word is old-fashioned or marked for age or gender; they are marked for
**genre**. And neither is a sentence-opener: a paragraph that begins "And in
addition…" starts with また or なお in Japanese, not with 及(およ)び.

## Contrast

### 及(およ)び・並(なら)びに vs と・や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different genre. `と` and `や` are what people
say; <code>及(およ)び</code> and <code>並(なら)びに</code> are what documents print.

</div>

<pre>
パスポート<b>と</b>航空券(こうくうけん)を持(も)ってきてね。
Bring your passport and your ticket.
</pre>

<pre>
パスポート<b>及(およ)び</b>航空券(こうくうけん)をご持参(じさん)ください。
Please bring your passport and air ticket with you.
</pre>

One difference is not only stylistic. `と` gives an exhaustive list and `や` an
illustrative one; <code>及(およ)び</code> is exhaustive like `と`, which is precisely why
legal drafting uses it. If a notice says A及(およ)びB, the list is A and B and
that is all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は山田(やまだ)さん及(およ)び田中(たなか)さんと飲(の)みに行(い)った。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は山田(やまだ)さん<b>と</b>田中(たなか)さんと飲(の)みに行(い)った。
  Grammatical and absurd. A night out is not a regulation; use `と`.

- ❌ 及(およ)び、会場(かいじょう)は変更(へんこう)になりました。 → ✅ <b>なお</b>、会場(かいじょう)は変更(へんこう)になりました。
  It connects items inside a sentence and cannot open one. "In addition" at the
  head of a sentence is なお or また.

- ❌ 会員(かいいん)並(なら)びに家族(かぞく)及(およ)び友人(ゆうじん) → ✅ 会員(かいいん)及(およ)び家族(かぞく)<b>並(なら)びに</b>友人(ゆうじん)
  The levels are the wrong way round. In a two-level list <code>及(およ)び</code> binds
  the inner pair and <code>並(なら)びに</code> joins that pair to the rest.

- ❌ 平日(へいじつ)及(およ)び土曜日(どようび)などに営業(えいぎょう)しています。 → ✅ 平日(へいじつ)<b>や</b>土曜日(どようび)などに営業(えいぎょう)しています。
  <code>及(およ)び</code> gives a complete list, so pairing it with など - "and so on" -
  contradicts itself.

## Practice

**1.** 受験(じゅけん)の際(さい)は、受験票(じゅけんひょう)____身分証明書(みぶんしょうめいしょ)をご持参(じさん)ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

受験票(じゅけんひょう)<b>及(およ)び</b>身分証明書(みぶんしょうめいしょ)をご持参(じさん)ください。

This is printed instruction to candidates, which is exactly the genre the word
belongs to, and the list is meant to be complete. In speech the same sentence
would use `と`.

</details>

**2.** How would you say the same thing to a friend?
履歴書(りれきしょ)及(およ)び証明写真(しょうめいしゃしん)が必要(ひつよう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

履歴書(りれきしょ)<b>と</b>証明写真(しょうめいしゃしん)が要(い)るよ。

Swap the written connector for `と` and let the rest of the sentence come down to
match. Keeping 及(およ)び in casual speech sounds like reading the form aloud
instead of talking.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 社員(しゃいん)及(およ)びその家族(かぞく)
(b) 社員(しゃいん)並(なら)びにその家族(かぞく)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In an ordinary notice the two are interchangeable and (b) simply sounds more
ceremonial - the phrasing of a speech rather than a memo. Inside a statute or a
formal set of rules the choice is structural: (b) signals that the two items are
being joined at the higher of two levels, so the reader expects a
及(およ)び-joined pair somewhere else in the same clause.

</details>

# ちなみに

**Meaning** · incidentally; by the way; for your information

`ちなみに` hangs a footnote off what you have just said. The sentence it
introduces is extra, optional information that is still **about the same thing** - 
a price, a caveat, a related fact the listener may as well have.

## Usage

`ちなみに` is a conjunctive adverb. It stands at the **head of a sentence**,
normally followed by a comma, and joins that sentence to the one before it.

- ちなみに、料金(りょうきん)は前払(まえばら)いです。
- ちなみに私(わたし)は関西(かんさい)出身(しゅっしん)です。

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the sentence after `ちなみに` must be a **side note on the
topic already running**. If you are changing the subject, the word you want is
`ところで`, not `ちなみに`.

</div>

Because it announces a supplement, `ちなみに` needs something in front of it to
supplement. Opening an email or a conversation with it is odd; it wants a
preceding statement to lean on. Two frames it likes:

- <code>ちなみに、～は…です</code> - adding a specification: hours, price, location.
- <code>ちなみに、～はどうですか</code> - a follow-up question on the same subject,
  common in interviews and small talk.

It is written in kana in practice. The kanji spelling 因(ちな)みに exists but is
rare outside older or deliberately formal prose.

## Examples

<pre>
この店(みせ)は水曜日(すいようび)が休(やす)みです。<b>ちなみに</b>、駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)はありません。
This shop is closed on Wednesdays. Incidentally, there's no car park.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ちなみに</b>、会費(かいひ)は当日(とうじつ)受付(うけつけ)でお支払(しはら)いください。
For your information, the fee is payable at the desk on the day.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)からです。<b>ちなみに</b>、前回(ぜんかい)の資料(しりょう)はもうメールで送(おく)ってあります。
Next week's meeting starts at three. For reference, I've already emailed you the handouts from last time.
</pre>

<pre>
このカメラ、去年(きょねん)の型(かた)だから安(やす)くなってるんですよ。<b>ちなみに</b>、色(いろ)は黒(くろ)しか残(のこ)っていません。
This camera is cheap because it's last year's model. Just so you know, black is the only colour left.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-flexible, which is why it turns up everywhere. The same word works in a
business email (以上(いじょう)、ご確認(かくにん)ください。ちなみに…) and between
friends over lunch (ちなみに俺(おれ)、明日(あした)休(やす)みだから). It is neither
stiff nor slangy, and it carries no age or gender marking.

What it does carry is a slight air of **volunteering** something. The speaker is
saying "you did not ask, but here it is", so it fits an aside, a clarification, or
a piece of trivia. Presenters and writers use it to slip in a supporting figure
without breaking the flow of the argument.

In speech you will also hear the padded <code>ちなみにですが、…</code> and
<code>ちなみになんですけど、…</code>. Both are ordinary polite conversation, softening
the abruptness of dropping in an extra question.

One thing it is not: `ちなみに` never carries the **main** point. If the fact you
are adding is the reason for the whole message, put it in a sentence of its own.
Introducing your actual request with `ちなみに` sounds evasive, as though you were
pretending it were an afterthought.

## Contrast

### ちなみに vs ところで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ちなみに` **stays** on the current topic and adds to it.
`ところで` **leaves** the current topic for a new one.

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は来月(らいげつ)新(あたら)しい版(はん)が出(で)ます。<b>ちなみに</b>、値段(ねだん)は変(か)わりません。
A new edition of this dictionary comes out next month. The price, incidentally, is unchanged.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は来月(らいげつ)新(あたら)しい版(はん)が出(で)ます。<b>ところで</b>、昼(ひる)ごはんはもう食(た)べましたか。
A new edition of this dictionary comes out next month. Anyway, have you had lunch yet?
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences break. `ちなみに` in front of the lunch question
promises a connection that never arrives; `ところで` in front of the price
announces a change of subject and then does not change it.

Between them sits `そういえば`, "now that you mention it", which follows an
association triggered by what was said rather than adding information about it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ちなみに、週末(しゅうまつ)映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きませんか。 → ✅ <b>ところで</b>、週末(しゅうまつ)映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きませんか。
  An unrelated invitation is a change of topic. `ちなみに` needs the new sentence to
  be about what was already being discussed.

- ❌ ちなみにの情報(じょうほう)ですが… → ✅ <b>参考(さんこう)までに</b>申(もう)し上(あ)げますと…
  `ちなみに` is an adverb and cannot take `の` to modify a noun. For "supplementary
  information" in formal writing, use <code>参考(さんこう)まで(に)</code>.

- ❌ 値段(ねだん)は<b>ちなみに</b>三千円(さんぜんえん)です。 → ✅ <b>ちなみに</b>、値段(ねだん)は三千円(さんぜんえん)です。
  It belongs at the front of its sentence, before the topic phrase, not buried in
  the middle of it.

## Practice

**1.** 今日(きょう)のセミナーは六時(ろくじ)に終(お)わります。____、資料(しりょう)は後(あと)で全員(ぜんいん)に送(おく)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ちなみに</b>、資料(しりょう)は後(あと)で全員(ぜんいん)に送(おく)ります。

The second sentence is still about the same seminar, so it is a supplement rather
than a new topic. `ところで` would wrongly announce that the subject is about to
change.

</details>

**2.** Translate: "The museum is free on Mondays. Incidentally, photography is not
allowed inside."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

美術館(びじゅつかん)は月曜日(げつようび)は無料(むりょう)です。<b>ちなみに</b>、館内(かんない)での撮影(さつえい)は禁止(きんし)されています。

Both sentences describe the same museum, which is exactly the relationship
`ちなみに` marks. Note that it opens its own sentence and takes a comma; it does
not join the two clauses into one.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ちなみに、来年(らいねん)から料金(りょうきん)が上(あ)がります。
(b) 来年(らいねん)から料金(りょうきん)が上(あ)がります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a plain announcement, and the price rise is the point of the message.

(a) frames the same fact as an aside attached to whatever was just said - useful
to know, but not what the speaker called you about. If the price rise really is
the headline, (a) sounds oddly casual about it, and a listener may miss it.

</details>

# しかしながら

**Meaning** · however; nevertheless - the formal written form of しかし

`しかしながら` reverses the direction of an argument. It means exactly what
`しかし` means; what it adds is **weight**. This is the "however" of reports,
papers, official notices and prepared speeches.

## Usage

`しかしながら` is a sentence-opening conjunction. It stands at the **head of a new
sentence**, after a full stop, and is normally followed by a comma.

- 結果(けっか)は良好(りょうこう)だった。しかしながら、問題(もんだい)も残(のこ)っている。
- ご要望(ようぼう)は承(うけたまわ)りました。しかしながら、対応(たいおう)は困難(こんなん)です。

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しかしながら` joins **two sentences**, never two clauses
inside one sentence. To turn a corner mid-sentence, use `が` or `けれども`.

</div>

The `ながら` in it is the old concessive `ながら`, "even so" - the same element as
in <code>残念(ざんねん)ながら</code> - and not the `ながら` meaning "while doing".
Nothing about the word implies simultaneity, and it never attaches to a verb stem.

Two places it reliably appears:

- **Written argument.** A paper states a finding, then しかしながら introduces the
  limitation.
- **Business correspondence.** A refusal is softened by first accepting the
  request, then しかしながら. It is a standard hinge in a polite decline.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>しかしながら</b>、この方法(ほうほう)にも問題(もんだい)がある。
However, this method too has its problems.
</pre>

<pre>
工事(こうじ)は順調(じゅんちょう)に進(すす)んだ。<b>しかしながら</b>、費用(ひよう)は予想(よそう)を大(おお)きく上回(うわまわ)った。
The construction went smoothly. Costs, however, greatly exceeded projections.
</pre>

<pre>
本件(ほんけん)につきましては社内(しゃない)で検討(けんとう)いたしました。<b>しかしながら</b>、今回(こんかい)はお引(ひ)き受(う)けいたしかねます。
We have reviewed this matter internally. Regrettably, however, we are unable to take it on this time.
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)は急速(きゅうそく)に進歩(しんぽ)している。<b>しかしながら</b>、それが直(ただ)ちに人々(ひとびと)の幸福(こうふく)につながるとは限(かぎ)らない。
Technology is advancing rapidly. Nevertheless, that does not automatically translate into human happiness.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** word. In an essay, a white paper, a set of minutes or a
company announcement it is completely at home. Spoken, it appears only where the
speech is itself written first - a ceremony address, a press conference, a formal
presentation.

Say it to a friend and it lands like reading aloud from a memo. The everyday
spoken chain runs roughly:

- でも - casual, the default in conversation
- けど・けれども - conversational, joins clauses
- しかし - neutral; fine in writing, and in speech when the point is serious
- しかしながら - formal written, heavier than all of the above

That ordering is a tendency rather than a rule, and the boundary between
`しかし` and `しかしながら` is mostly one of **length and gravity**: writers reach for
the longer form when the reversal is important and they want the reader to slow
down. Used on every third sentence it becomes ponderous, so even formal prose
tends to space it out and fall back on `しかし`.

In business email it is often stretched further into
<code>しかしながら、誠(まこと)に申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございませんが…</code>, which is
the polite refusal in its full costume.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 安(やす)いしかしながら品質(ひんしつ)が悪(わる)い。 → ✅ 安(やす)い。<b>しかしながら</b>、品質(ひんしつ)が悪(わる)い。
  It opens a sentence; it cannot sit between two clauses. Inside one sentence you
  need <code>安(やす)いが</code> or <code>安(やす)いけれども</code>.

- ❌ しかしながらも、時間(じかん)がない。 → ✅ <b>しかしながら</b>、時間(じかん)がない。
  There is no `しかしながらも`. The word is fixed and takes no further particles.

- ❌ (to a friend, about the weather) しかしながら、今日(きょう)は暑(あつ)いね。 → ✅ <b>でも</b>、今日(きょう)は暑(あつ)いね。
  Not wrong grammatically, but the register is three steps too high for chat. It
  will read as a joke.

- ❌ 音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)きしかしながら勉強(べんきょう)する。 → ✅ 音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)きながら勉強(べんきょう)する。
  The `ながら` inside `しかしながら` is not the "while doing" `ながら`. The two cannot
  be substituted for each other.

## Practice

**1.** 申(もう)し込(こ)み書類(しょるい)は受(う)け取(と)りました。____、記入(きにゅう)漏(も)れがございました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>しかしながら</b>、記入(きにゅう)漏(も)れがございました。

The surrounding sentences are in formal business register - 受(う)け取(と)りました,
ございました - and the second sentence reverses the first. That is precisely the
slot `しかしながら` fills.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in casual speech: 参加(さんか)したい気持(きも)ちはある。しかしながら、今週(こんしゅう)は時間(じかん)が取(と)れない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

行(い)きたいんだけど、今週(こんしゅう)はちょっと時間(じかん)ないんだよね。

Casual Japanese does the same reversal with `けど` inside a single sentence, or
with `でも` at the head of the next one. Keeping `しかしながら` here would sound like
a formal announcement to one friend.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 売上(うりあげ)は増(ふ)えた。でも、利益(りえき)は減(へ)った。
(b) 売上(うりあげ)は増(ふ)えた。しかしながら、利益(りえき)は減(へ)った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The facts are identical; the setting is not. (a) is someone talking - a colleague
summarising over coffee. (b) is someone writing - a report, a board paper, a press
release - and the heavier connective signals that the reversal is a considered
point rather than a passing remark.

</details>

# 少なくとも

**Meaning** · at least - the lowest figure the speaker is willing to vouch for

<code>少(すく)なくとも</code> (すくなくとも) puts a **floor** under a statement. The
real number may be higher; what the speaker guarantees is that it is not lower.
Its second job is the same move in an argument: whatever else is in doubt, *this
much* holds.

## Usage

An adverb. It stands **immediately in front of the quantity or the claim** it puts
a floor under.

- <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> + amount: 少(すく)なくとも三日(みっか)、少(すく)なくとも半分(はんぶん)
- <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> + clause: 少(すく)なくとも私(わたし)はそう思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `少なくとも` states a **fact or an estimate**, never a wish.
The sentence it heads reports how things are, or how many there are.

</div>

The quantity after it very often takes `は`, which is the particle of the minimum:
<code>少(すく)なくとも一週間(いっしゅうかん)はかかる</code>. Dropping the `は` is
possible but the sentence loses the "and probably more" edge.

In argument it works without any number at all, meaning "at any rate, whatever
else may be true":

- 少(すく)なくとも彼(かれ)に悪気(わるぎ)はなかった。
- 少(すく)なくとも、今(いま)のところ問題(もんだい)は起(お)きていない。

Note the spelling. The word ends in **とも**, not `ても`.
<code>少(すく)なくても</code> is the て-form of <code>少(すく)ない</code> plus `も`
and means "even if there are few" - a different sentence entirely.

## Examples

<pre>
この作業(さぎょう)には<b>少(すく)なくとも</b>一週間(いっしゅうかん)はかかります。
This job will take at least a week.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)のあの行列(ぎょうれつ)なら、<b>少(すく)なくとも</b>一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)ちだろう。
With a queue like that outside the station, it'll be an hour's wait at the very least.
</pre>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)が賛成(さんせい)したわけではないが、<b>少(すく)なくとも</b>反対(はんたい)した人(ひと)はいなかった。
Not everyone was in favour, but at least nobody objected.
</pre>

<pre>
その説(せつ)が正(ただ)しいかどうかは分(わ)からない。<b>少(すく)なくとも</b>、現時点(げんじてん)でそれを裏付(うらづ)ける証拠(しょうこ)は見(み)つかっていない。
Whether that theory is right, I can't say. What is certain is that no evidence supporting it has turned up so far.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and equally at home in speech and in writing. Newspapers use it
for casualty figures and cost estimates; a colleague uses it to tell you how long
something will take. It is not stiff, and it is not casual.

Its tone is **cautious and factual**. Choosing it signals that you have thought
about the number and are deliberately quoting the safe end of it - which is why it
reads well in reports and badly in enthusiastic sales copy.

The clause-level use is a debating move. <code>少(すく)なくとも私(わたし)は…</code>
concedes the wider question and holds one narrow position, so it often carries a
faint note of disagreement: you may see it differently, but this much I will
defend. Used bluntly at someone it can sound like a rebuke, so speakers commonly
pad it - <code>少(すく)なくとも私(わたし)にはそう見(み)えました</code>.

## Contrast

### 少(すく)なくとも vs せめて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> is a floor under a **fact**;
`せめて` is a floor under a **wish**.

</div>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は<b>少(すく)なくとも</b>五十人(ごじゅうにん)はいた。
There were at least fifty people taking part.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は<b>せめて</b>五十人(ごじゅうにん)集(あつ)めたい。
I'd like to get at least fifty people to take part.
</pre>

The first counts; the second hopes, having already given up on a bigger number.
Because `せめて` always concedes something, it belongs with ～たい, ～てほしい,
～てください and the volitional, while <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> is comfortable
with a plain past tense that `せめて` cannot take at all.

The overlap is real when a target is being estimated rather than wished for.
<code>少(すく)なくとも五十人(ごじゅうにん)は必要(ひつよう)だ</code> is a
calculation - fifty is what the arithmetic demands. <code>せめて五十人(ごじゅうにん)
来(き)てほしい</code> is a plea. Ask which one the sentence is doing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 少(すく)なくても三日(みっか)はかかる。 → ✅ <b>少(すく)なくとも</b>三日(みっか)はかかる。
  The fixed adverb is `とも`. `ても` turns it back into the adjective
  <code>少(すく)ない</code> and the sentence stops meaning "at least".

- ❌ 三日(みっか)少(すく)なくともかかる。 → ✅ <b>少(すく)なくとも</b>三日(みっか)かかる。
  It goes in front of the amount it modifies. After the amount it modifies
  nothing.

- ❌ 少(すく)なくとも連絡(れんらく)くらいしてほしかった。 → ✅ <b>せめて</b>連絡(れんらく)くらいしてほしかった。
  This is a reproach, not a count. Measuring a favour you wanted with
  <code>少(すく)なくとも</code> makes it sound like an invoice.

## Practice

**1.** Choose: この工事(こうじ)が終(お)わるまで、（少(すく)なくとも / せめて）三(さん)か月(げつ)はかかる見込(みこ)みです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>少(すく)なくとも</b>三(さん)か月(げつ)はかかる見込(みこ)みです。

<code>見込(みこ)み</code> makes this a projection - a fact about the schedule, not
something anyone wants. `せめて` would need a wish for it to be the floor of.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 少(すく)なくても一度(いちど)はこの目(め)で見(み)た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ <b>少(すく)なくとも</b>一度(いちど)はこの目(め)で見(み)た。

Only the spelling is wrong. `ても` is the conditional て-form of the adjective; the
adverb meaning "at least" is written `とも`, and the two sound close enough that
this slips past learners constantly.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は完璧(かんぺき)ではなかった。
(b) 少(すく)なくとも彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)に嘘(うそ)はなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply judges the explanation and finds it short of perfect.

(b) narrows the ground on purpose. The speaker sets aside the larger question of
whether the explanation was good, and defends one thing that can be said for it.
That is the argumentative use of <code>少(すく)なくとも</code>: a concession and a
minimum claim in the same breath.

</details>

# たった(の)

**Meaning** · only; a mere; no more than - said of an amount the speaker finds
startlingly small

`たった` sits in front of a number and tells you the speaker's reaction to it. The
figure itself is neutral; `たった` is the raised eyebrow. `たったの` is the same
word with the emphasis turned up.

## Usage

`たった` modifies a **counted amount** - a number plus its counter, a sum of money,
a length of time. It cannot modify a bare noun, a verb or an adjective.

- たった一人(ひとり)、たった三日(みっか)、たった五分(ごふん)
- たったの百円(ひゃくえん)、たったの二回(にかい)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you cannot count it, `たった` cannot touch it. Behind it
there is always a number.

</div>

The optional `の` is pure emphasis and slightly more colloquial. Both
<code>たった千円(せんえん)</code> and <code>たったの千円(せんえん)</code> are
correct; the second sounds a shade more indignant, and it is the usual choice when
the amount stands alone as a comment - 「これで<code>たったの千円(せんえん)</code>?」

Two partners do most of the work with it:

- <code>たった～しか…ない</code> - 三人(さんにん)しか来(こ)なかった becomes
  たった三人(さんにん)しか来(こ)なかった, with the disappointment made explicit.
- <code>たった～で</code> - the small amount as the means or the elapsed time:
  たった一週間(いっしゅうかん)で覚(おぼ)えた.

<code>たった今(いま)</code> is a separate fixed expression meaning "just this
moment" (今(いま)着(つ)いたところ). It is not this pattern, and no other time
word can be substituted into it.

## Examples

<pre>
この本(ほん)は<b>たった</b>三日(みっか)で書(か)かれたそうだ。
Apparently this book was written in a mere three days.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)には<b>たったの</b>百円(ひゃくえん)しか残(のこ)っていなかった。
There was only a hundred yen left in my wallet.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)を<b>たった</b>一人(ひとり)で回(まわ)しているんですか。それはすごいですね。
You run this shop entirely on your own? That's really something.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)かけて準備(じゅんび)した計画(けいかく)が、<b>たったの</b>一言(ひとこと)で白紙(はくし)に戻(もど)った。
A plan ten years in the making was wiped off the table by a single remark.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and strongly **emotional**. Speech and writing both use it
freely, but it always editorialises: the speaker is amazed, indignant, or
impressed that so little was enough. A neutral report of the same figure simply
leaves `たった` out.

Which emotion depends on the sentence. <code>たった三日(みっか)で完成(かんせい)した</code>
is admiration - so little time, and yet. <code>たった三日(みっか)しか
休(やす)めない</code> is complaint - so little, and that is all there is. The word
does not choose for you; the predicate does.

Its formal written counterpart is <code>わずか</code>, which says the same thing
without raising its voice: a newspaper writes
<code>わずか三日(みっか)で</code>, a person says
<code>たった三日(みっか)で</code>. `ほんの` is the third member of the family and
covers small amounts that are **not counted** - ほんの少(すこ)し, ほんの一部(いちぶ) - 
exactly the territory `たった` cannot enter.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>たった</b>水(みず)を飲(の)んだ。 → ✅ <b>ただの</b>水(みず)を飲(の)んだ。
  `たった` needs a number. <code>ただの</code> is a different word meaning "plain,
  ordinary", and the two are one small `っ` apart.

- ❌ <b>たった</b>少(すこ)ししか食(た)べていない。 → ✅ <b>ほんの</b>少(すこ)ししか食(た)べていない。
  <code>少(すこ)し</code> is a vague quantity, not a counted one. The natural
  partner for it is `ほんの`.

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は<b>たった</b>狭(せま)い。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は本当(ほんとう)に狭(せま)い。
  `たった` is not a general intensifier and cannot modify an adjective. It scales
  amounts, not qualities.

- ❌ <b>たった</b>昨日(きのう)着(つ)いたばかりだ。 → ✅ <b>たった今(いま)</b>着(つ)いたばかりだ。
  <code>たった今(いま)</code> is frozen. You cannot swap in another time word and
  expect it to mean "just".

## Practice

**1.** 試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)したのは、三十人(さんじゅうにん)のうち____二人(ふたり)だけだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三十人(さんじゅうにん)のうち<b>たった</b>二人(ふたり)だけだった。

Two out of thirty is a counted amount, and the sentence is clearly commenting on
how few that is. <code>たったの二人(ふたり)</code> works too and sounds a little
more incredulous.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: この仕事(しごと)はたった大変(たいへん)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この仕事(しごと)は本当(ほんとう)に大変(たいへん)です。

`たった` cannot modify a な-adjective - or any adjective. If the intended meaning
was "this job takes only a short time", the number has to be there:
<code>この仕事(しごと)はたった十分(じゅっぷん)で終(お)わります</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 五分(ごふん)待(ま)ってください。
(b) たったの五分(ごふん)待(ま)ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain request: give me five minutes.

(b) argues with the listener before they have refused. `たったの` frames five
minutes as a trivial amount, so the sentence reads "it's only five minutes - just
wait". It presses, and with a stranger or a superior that pressure is audible.

</details>

# 次第に

**Meaning** · gradually; by degrees - a change that advances steadily in one
direction

<code>次第(しだい)に</code> (しだいに) describes a process, not a moment. Something
is moving from one state towards another, and each stage is a little further along
than the last.

## Usage

An adverb. It goes in front of the **verb or adjective that carries the change**,
and that word has to express change: なる, 変(か)わる, 増(ふ)える,
減(へ)る, 慣(な)れる, 近(ちか)づく, 消(き)える, 強(つよ)まる.

- 次第(しだい)に暗(くら)くなる
- 次第(しだい)に数(かず)が減(へ)る

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `次第に` needs a **process with a direction**. A single
completed event has no degrees for it to describe.

</div>

It pairs naturally with the aspect helpers that stretch a change over time:

- <code>次第(しだい)に…ていく</code> - the change moves away from now into the future
- <code>次第(しだい)に…てくる</code> - the change has been approaching up to now

Watch the homograph. The same 次第(しだい) appears in two unrelated N2 patterns - 
<code>～次第(しだい)</code> "as soon as" (着(つ)き次第(しだい)連絡(れんらく)します)
and <code>～次第(しだい)だ</code> "depends on" (天気(てんき)次第(しだい)だ). Only
the form with `に` after it is the adverb meaning "gradually", and it never
attaches to anything in front of it.

## Examples

<pre>
空(そら)が<b>次第(しだい)に</b>暗(くら)くなってきた。
The sky gradually grew darker.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)が効(き)いて、痛(いた)みは<b>次第(しだい)に</b>消(き)えていった。
The medicine took effect and the pain faded away little by little.
</pre>

<pre>
最初(さいしょ)は不安(ふあん)だったが、新(あたら)しい職場(しょくば)にも<b>次第(しだい)に</b>慣(な)れてきた。
I was nervous at first, but I've gradually settled in at the new workplace.
</pre>

<pre>
人口(じんこう)の減少(げんしょう)にともなって、この通(とお)りから<b>次第(しだい)に</b>店(みせ)が姿(すがた)を消(け)していった。
As the population fell, the shops along this street disappeared one after another.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`次第に` leans **written**. It is the word of narration, news reports, weather
forecasts and essays: 雨(あめ)は次第(しだい)に弱(よわ)まるでしょう. In
conversation it is not wrong, but it sounds composed - a speaker choosing their
words rather than chatting.

It also prefers changes that **happen** over changes you **perform**. Something
gets dark, gets used to, gets fewer. When the subject is deliberately doing
something in stages, <code>少(すこ)しずつ</code> or
<code>徐々(じょじょ)に</code> sits more comfortably:
<code>少(すこ)しずつ荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)んだ</code>. This is a tendency, not a
prohibition - <code>徐々(じょじょ)に</code> is the close formal neighbour and
overlaps with `次第に` across most of its range.

The change it describes is also **slow enough to notice in stages**. For a jump
you want <code>急(きゅう)に</code> or <code>一気(いっき)に</code>; `次第に`
promises the listener a gradient.

## Contrast

### 次第(しだい)に vs だんだん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different clothes. `だんだん` is the spoken
form, <code>次第(しだい)に</code> the written one.

</div>

<pre>
日(ひ)が長(なが)くなって、<b>だんだん</b>春(はる)らしくなってきたね。
The days are getting longer - it's starting to feel like spring.
</pre>

<pre>
三月(さんがつ)に入(はい)ると、気温(きおん)は<b>次第(しだい)に</b>上昇(じょうしょう)する。
From March onwards the temperature rises steadily.
</pre>

Put `だんだん` in the second sentence and the forecast sounds like a friend
guessing. Put `次第に` in the first and the small talk turns into a bulletin.

`だんだん` also doubles as an everyday intensifier in speech - だんだん眠(ねむ)く
なってきた - where the speaker is simply reporting a feeling. `次第に` keeps a
certain distance from the change, which is why it suits observation and report.

A third neighbour, `どんどん`, is not a synonym: it means the change is
**fast and gathering pace**, the opposite of the measured gradient `次第に`
describes.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 電気(でんき)が<b>次第(しだい)に</b>消(き)えた。 → ✅ 電気(でんき)が<b>急(きゅう)に</b>消(き)えた。
  A light going out is instantaneous. `次第に` needs something that can be half
  done.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わり<b>次第(しだい)に</b>連絡(れんらく)します。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わり<b>次第(しだい)</b>連絡(れんらく)します。
  "As soon as" is <code>～次第(しだい)</code> with no `に`. Adding the `に` here
  produces the adverb, and the sentence stops making sense.

- ❌ 次第(しだい)に三(さん)キロ痩(や)せた。 → ✅ 次第(しだい)に体重(たいじゅう)が減(へ)っていった。
  A finished total is a result, not a gradient. Pair `次第に` with the process, and
  let ～ていく carry it forward.

## Practice

**1.** 練習(れんしゅう)を続(つづ)けているうちに、____自信(じしん)がついてきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>次第(しだい)に</b>自信(じしん)がついてきた。

Confidence builds by degrees, and <code>ついてきた</code> already frames it as a
change arriving up to the present - exactly the aspect `次第に` likes. In casual
speech `だんだん` would be the more natural choice here.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 準備(じゅんび)ができ次第(しだい)に、出発(しゅっぱつ)しましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 準備(じゅんび)ができ<b>次第(しだい)</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)しましょう。

This is the "as soon as" pattern attached to a verb stem, which takes no `に`. The
adverb <code>次第(しだい)に</code> is a free-standing word and cannot follow
<code>でき</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 町(まち)の様子(ようす)は次第(しだい)に変(か)わっていった。
(b) 町(まち)の様子(ようす)はどんどん変(か)わっていった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both describe change over time, but at different speeds and from different
distances. (a) is measured and observational - the kind of line that opens a
documentary. (b) is faster and more involved: the changes came thick and fast, and
the speaker sounds caught up in them.

</details>

# さすが

**Meaning** · as one would expect (and it lives up to it); even ～, admittedly

`さすが` measures reality against a reputation and finds that the two match. You
knew this person was good, this shop was famous, this material was expensive - and
now the evidence is in front of you. Most of the time it is praise.

## Usage

Three distinct moves live under one word. They are worth learning separately.

**1. さすが(は)＋noun** - "that's ～ for you". A verdict on a person or thing whose
reputation has just been confirmed.

- さすが先生(せんせい)ですね。
- さすがはプロの仕事(しごと)だ。

The `は` is optional and adds a touch of formality; both are common.

**2. さすが～だけあって / だけのことはある** - the same praise with its evidence
attached. The `だけあって` clause states the qualification, the main clause states
the result that lives up to it.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>さすが～だけあって</code> = "being ～, as you'd expect,
…". The reason comes first and must be something **impressive in itself**:
training, experience, cost, pedigree.

</div>

- さすが三年(さんねん)習(なら)っただけあって、発音(はつおん)がきれいだ。
- 高(たか)かっただけのことはある。

**3. さすがに** - a different move. Here the speaker concedes a **limit**: even
granted everything, this far and no further. It heads a clause admitting that
something was too much, or that even an exceptional person could not manage it.

- さすがに疲(つか)れた。
- さすがにそれは無理(むり)だ。

Related to this is <code>さすがの～も</code>, "even ～, of all people":
さすがの彼(かれ)も黙(だま)ってしまった.

The kanji spelling 流石(さすが) is ateji and appears mostly in casual writing;
kana is the safe default.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>さすが</b>プロですね。仕上(しあ)がりが全然(ぜんぜん)違(ちが)います。
That's a professional for you - the finish is on a different level.
</pre>

<pre>
これを一人(ひとり)で全部(ぜんぶ)運(はこ)んだの?<b>さすが</b>だね。
You carried all this by yourself? Impressive, as always.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>さすが</b>十年(じゅうねん)向(む)こうに住(す)んでいた<b>だけあって</b>、彼(かれ)の英語(えいご)は発音(はつおん)まで自然(しぜん)だ。
Having lived over there for ten years, as you'd expect, even his English pronunciation is natural.
</pre>

<pre>
三日(みっか)続(つづ)けて徹夜(てつや)したら、<b>さすがに</b>体(からだ)がもたなくなってきた。
Three all-nighters in a row, and unsurprisingly my body is starting to give out.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Conversational and warm. `さすが` is what you say out loud when someone has just
done something well, and it appears constantly in speech, in reviews and in
advertising copy. In formal writing it is rarer, because it is an expression of
personal admiration.

Two cautions worth knowing.

First, it is **evaluation**. Praising a superior by ranking them -
さすが部長(ぶちょう)ですね - is extremely common, but some listeners feel that
grading the boss is presumptuous, and overdone it sounds like flattery. Tendency,
not rule: judge the relationship.

Second, the admiration has to be **expected**. `さすが` says "just as I thought",
so it does not fit a surprise. If the result was genuinely unforeseen, the natural
words are <code>意外(いがい)と</code> or a plain <code>すごい</code>.

`さすがに` is more neutral in colour than bare `さすが` and belongs to ordinary
speech and writing alike. Note that its "even so" sense can be faintly negative -
さすがに言(い)いすぎだ, "that really is going too far" - which surprises learners
who have only met the praising `さすが`.

## Contrast

### さすが vs やはり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "as expected", but `さすが` **admires** and
`やはり` merely **confirms**. Only `やはり` can report an expected bad outcome.

</div>

<pre>
<b>さすが</b>一流(いちりゅう)のホテルだ。サービスが違(ちが)う。
This is a first-class hotel all right - the service is another thing entirely.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>やはり</b>彼(かれ)は来(こ)なかった。
He didn't come, just as I thought.
</pre>

Reverse them and the second sentence becomes strange: there is nothing admirable
about someone failing to turn up, so `さすが` has nothing to praise. Conversely
`やはり` in the first sentence flattens it - the hotel is merely as predicted, and
the compliment evaporates.

The casual spoken form of `やはり` is `やっぱり`, and it is far more frequent in
conversation than the full form.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>さすが</b>彼(かれ)はまた遅刻(ちこく)した。 → ✅ <b>やはり</b>彼(かれ)はまた遅刻(ちこく)した。
  `さすが` only greets a good outcome. Using it for a predictable failure reads as
  sarcasm, whether or not you meant it.

- ❌ さすが毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)した<b>から</b>、うまくなった。 → ✅ さすが毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)した<b>だけあって</b>、うまくなった。
  Plain `から` states a cause; the frame that carries the "lives up to it" meaning
  is <code>だけあって</code> or <code>だけのことはある</code>.

- ❌ さすが疲(つか)れた。 → ✅ <b>さすがに</b>疲(つか)れた。
  The "even so, admittedly" sense requires the `に`. Without it the sentence looks
  like praise and lands nowhere.

## Practice

**1.** 三十年(さんじゅうねん)寿司(すし)を握(にぎ)ってきた____、包丁(ほうちょう)の使(つか)い方(かた)が美(うつく)しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

さすが三十年(さんじゅうねん)寿司(すし)を握(にぎ)ってきた<b>だけあって</b>、包丁(ほうちょう)の使(つか)い方(かた)が美(うつく)しい。

The first clause gives an impressive qualification and the second gives the result
that matches it. <code>だけのことはある</code> would also work if it were moved to
the end: 美(うつく)しい。さすが三十年(さんじゅうねん)やってきただけのことはある。

</details>

**2.** Choose: 山(やま)の上(うえ)は八月(はちがつ)でも（さすが / さすがに）涼(すず)しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

山(やま)の上(うえ)は八月(はちがつ)でも<b>さすがに</b>涼(すず)しい。

<code>でも</code> sets up a concession - even in August - and the clause that
answers it takes `さすがに`. Bare `さすが` would have to be followed by a noun it is
praising, and there is none here.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) さすが林(はやし)さんですね。
(b) やっぱり林(はやし)さんですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a compliment: Hayashi has just done something well, and the speaker is
saying that this is exactly the standard Hayashi is known for.

(b) only confirms a prediction. Depending on what Hayashi just did, it can be
admiring, resigned or quietly critical - the same words serve for "of course you
came through" and "of course you forgot again".

</details>

# まさか

**Meaning** · surely not; you can't be serious; I never imagined ～

`まさか` is disbelief, said in advance. The speaker raises a possibility only in
order to reject it - or, looking back, to say that they had rejected it and were
wrong.

## Usage

`まさか` sits at the **head of the sentence** and commits the ending. Whatever
follows must deny, doubt or express astonishment; a plain affirmative statement
leaves the word stranded.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まさか` is only half a sentence. It needs a partner at the
end: <code>…ないだろう</code>, <code>…まい</code>, <code>…はずがない</code>,
<code>…とは思(おも)わなかった</code>.

</div>

The three main frames:

- <code>まさか～ないだろう / まい</code> - rejecting a possibility about the future
  or the unknown. <code>まい</code> is the literary negative conjecture and gives
  the sentence a written, slightly old-fashioned air; most speakers say
  <code>ないだろう</code>.
- <code>まさか～とは思(おも)わなかった</code> - looking back at something that
  *did* happen and admitting it was unthinkable. Often strengthened to
  <code>夢(ゆめ)にも思(おも)わなかった</code>.
- <code>まさか～ではないだろうね / …じゃないよね</code> - checking an alarming
  suspicion with the listener.

On its own, 「まさか。」is a complete reply meaning "no way", and
「まさか!」with rising force is the shout of someone who has just realised
something terrible.

There is also a noun-like use with `の`: <code>まさかの時(とき)のために</code>,
"for a rainy day", and the newer journalistic <code>まさかの敗北(はいぼく)</code>,
"a shock defeat".

## Examples

<pre>
<b>まさか</b>彼(かれ)が来(く)るとは思(おも)わなかった。
I never dreamed he would actually come.
</pre>

<pre>
「田中(たなか)さん、会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めるらしいよ。」「<b>まさか</b>。」
"Apparently Tanaka's leaving the company." "You're joking."
</pre>

<pre>
<b>まさか</b>雨(あめ)は降(ふ)るまいと思(おも)って、傘(かさ)を持(も)たずに出(で)てきてしまった。
Convinced it surely wouldn't rain, I came out without an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>まさか</b>こんなところで昔(むかし)の同級生(どうきゅうせい)に会(あ)うとは、夢(ゆめ)にも思(おも)っていなかった。
It never once crossed my mind that I'd run into an old classmate in a place like this.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Fully conversational and fully at home in writing too. The bare interjection
「まさか」is casual; the full sentence patterns are register-neutral and take
polite endings without trouble
(<code>まさかそんなことはないでしょう</code>).

Emotionally it is loud. `まさか` is not measured scepticism - it is the word for
something you consider close to impossible, so using it about a perfectly ordinary
possibility sounds theatrical. For "maybe, just possibly", the word is
<code>もしかして</code>.

<code>まい</code> deserves a warning of its own. It is correct and it is what
examination questions pair with `まさか`, but in speech it sounds bookish, and
outside set phrases like <code>二度(にど)とするまい</code> most Japanese people
would say <code>ないだろう</code>. Recognise it; produce it only in writing.

Write `まさか` in kana. The ateji 真逆(まさか) is listed in dictionaries, but
真逆(まぎゃく) - "the exact opposite" - is how a modern reader will pronounce
those two characters, so the spelling actively misleads.

## Contrast

### まさか vs もしかして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もしかして` **opens** a possibility; `まさか` **closes** one.

</div>

<pre>
<b>もしかして</b>、山田(やまだ)さんの妹(いもうと)さんですか。
Excuse me - are you by any chance Yamada's sister?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>まさか</b>山田(やまだ)さんが忘(わす)れるはずがない。
There's no way Yamada would forget.
</pre>

`もしかして` is a polite feeler: the speaker thinks it might be true and is asking.
`まさか` starts from the conviction that it is not.

They do meet in the anxious question <code>まさか忘(わす)れたんじゃないよね</code>,
where the speaker fears the answer is yes. Even there the difference holds:
`もしかして` would make the question neutral, while `まさか` makes it a plea for the
answer to be no.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>まさか</b>彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)だ。 → ✅ <b>まさか</b>彼(かれ)が犯人(はんにん)だ<b>とは思(おも)わなかった</b>。
  An affirmative ending contradicts the word. `まさか` has to be answered by a
  denial or an expression of disbelief.

- ❌ <b>まさか</b>明日(あした)、時間(じかん)がありますか。 → ✅ <b>もしかして</b>明日(あした)、時間(じかん)がありますか。
  A neutral enquiry is not disbelief. `まさか` here tells the listener you consider
  their having free time absurd.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>まさか</b>来(こ)ないだろう。 → ✅ <b>まさか</b>彼女(かのじょ)は来(こ)ないだろう。
  `まさか` scopes over the whole sentence, so it belongs at the front, before the
  subject. Buried after the topic it reads as a stumble.

## Practice

**1.** ____優勝(ゆうしょう)できるとは思(おも)っていなかったので、名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれたとき声(こえ)が出(で)なかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>まさか</b>優勝(ゆうしょう)できるとは思(おも)っていなかった。

The frame <code>とは思(おも)っていなかった</code> is exactly the ending `まさか`
requires, and the sentence is about something that did happen despite being
unthinkable.

</details>

**2.** Translate: "Surely he wouldn't lie about something like this."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>まさか</b>彼(かれ)がこんなことで嘘(うそ)をつくはずがない。

<code>はずがない</code> supplies the denial that `まさか` demands.
<code>…嘘をつかないだろう</code> is equally good and a little softer;
<code>…嘘をつくまい</code> is correct but sounds like a novel.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 試験(しけん)に落(お)ちるとは思(おも)わなかった。
(b) まさか試験(しけん)に落(お)ちるとは思(おも)わなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a mistaken expectation: I thought I would pass, and I didn't.

(b) adds the size of the shock. `まさか` says failing was not merely unexpected but
something the speaker had ruled out entirely, so the sentence carries the jolt of
the result as well as the fact of it.

</details>

# たちまち

**Meaning** · in an instant; in no time at all - a change that is complete almost
before you notice it

`たちまち` reports a result that arrived with startling speed. It is a narrator's
word: something happened, and by the time the sentence ends it is already over.

## Usage

An adverb, placed in front of the verb that produces the change. Two things
control it.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たちまち` describes **what happened**, not what should
happen. It takes a completed change, almost always in the past tense; it never
appears in a request, an order or a promise.

</div>

- たちまち売(う)り切(き)れた
- たちまち人気(にんき)になった
- たちまち広(ひろ)がった

Second, the change has to be **visible and total**. Spreading, selling out,
vanishing, filling up, turning into something else - these suit it. A small
adjustment does not.

The frames it turns up in most:

- <code>～と、たちまち…</code> - a trigger and its instant consequence:
  火(ひ)をつけると、たちまち燃(も)え広(ひろ)がった。
- <code>たちまち～になる / になった</code> - an abrupt change of state.
- Advertising copy loves it: <code>発売(はつばい)直後(ちょくご)にたちまち
  完売(かんばい)</code>.

## Examples

<pre>
うわさは<b>たちまち</b>クラス中(じゅう)に広(ひろ)がった。
The rumour went round the whole class in no time.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しく出(だ)したパンは<b>たちまち</b>売(う)り切(き)れた。
The new bread they put out sold out in a flash.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら、熱(ねつ)は<b>たちまち</b>下(さ)がった。
He took the medicine and the fever came down almost at once.
</pre>

<pre>
その動画(どうが)は公開(こうかい)から数時間(すうじかん)で<b>たちまち</b>話題(わだい)になり、再生(さいせい)回数(かいすう)は百万(ひゃくまん)を超(こ)えた。
Within hours of going up the video was suddenly the thing everyone was talking about, and views passed a million.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たちまち` leans **written and narrative**. It belongs to stories, news copy,
product blurbs and folk tales - <code>けむりを浴(あ)びると、たちまち
おじいさんになってしまった</code>. It is not rare in speech, but a speaker who uses
it is telling a story rather than chatting, and in ordinary conversation
<code>あっという間(ま)に</code> or a plain <code>すぐ</code> is far more common.

It is also **vivid**. The word implies that the speed was worth remarking on, so
it goes with dramatic outcomes; using it for a mundane one sounds inflated.

Two neighbours worth keeping apart from it. <code>あっという間(ま)に</code>
covers stretches of time that felt short in retrospect - three years, a holiday,
an evening - which `たちまち` does not do; the change it describes is genuinely
sudden, not merely remembered as brief. <code>直(ただ)ちに</code> is an
instruction word from officialdom, "immediately, without delay", and is used for
what must be done, the opposite end from `たちまち`.

## Contrast

### たちまち vs すぐに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>すぐに</code> is about **how soon it starts**;
`たちまち` is about **how fast it finished**.

</div>

<pre>
電話(でんわ)したら、<b>すぐに</b>来(き)てくれた。
I phoned and she came right away.
</pre>

<pre>
強(つよ)い風(かぜ)で、火(ひ)は<b>たちまち</b>山(やま)全体(ぜんたい)に広(ひろ)がった。
Driven by a strong wind, the fire spread across the whole mountain in minutes.
</pre>

<code>すぐに</code> measures the gap between a cue and a response, and it is
perfectly usable about the future: <code>すぐに行(い)きます</code>, "I'll be
right there". `たちまち` has no such use - ❌<code>たちまち行(い)きます</code> is
not Japanese, because you cannot promise someone a completed transformation.

Where both fit, they say different things.
<code>氷(こおり)はすぐに溶(と)けた</code> means it did not take long to start
melting; <code>氷(こおり)はたちまち溶(と)けた</code> means it was gone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>たちまち</b>来(き)てください。 → ✅ <b>すぐに</b>来(き)てください。
  A request looks forward; `たちまち` looks back at a change that is already
  complete. It cannot appear with ください, ましょう or a command.

- ❌ 明日(あした)<b>たちまち</b>返事(へんじ)します。 → ✅ 明日(あした)<b>すぐに</b>返事(へんじ)します。
  Same problem in a promise. Your own future action is <code>すぐに</code> or
  <code>早(はや)めに</code>.

- ❌ <b>たちまち</b>三年(さんねん)が過(す)ぎた。 → ✅ <b>あっという間(ま)に</b>三年(さんねん)が過(す)ぎた。
  Three years are not instantaneous, however short they felt.
  <code>あっという間(ま)に</code> is the expression for time that flew.

## Practice

**1.** 窓(まど)を開(あ)けると、いい匂(にお)いが____部屋(へや)中(じゅう)に広(ひろ)がった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いい匂(にお)いが<b>たちまち</b>部屋(へや)中(じゅう)に広(ひろ)がった。

A smell filling a room is a complete change arriving at once, reported in the past
tense - the shape `たちまち` was made for. The <code>～と</code> clause supplies the
trigger.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 係(かかり)の人(ひと)に聞(き)いたら、（たちまち / すぐに）教(おし)えてくれました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

係(かかり)の人(ひと)に聞(き)いたら、<b>すぐに</b>教(おし)えてくれました。

This is a prompt response to a request, not a dramatic transformation. `たちまち`
would suggest the staff member underwent some sudden change, which is not what
happened.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 新(あたら)しい店(みせ)はすぐに有名(ゆうめい)になった。
(b) 新(あたら)しい店(みせ)はたちまち有名(ゆうめい)になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says fame arrived without much delay - a neutral report of the timing.

(b) says the shop was famous before anyone could take a breath. `たちまち` adds
speed and drama, and it also adds the speaker's astonishment, which is why
advertising copy prefers it.

</details>

# よほど・よっぽど

**Meaning** · considerably; ～ must really be ～; very nearly

`よほど` is the word you reach for when the evidence in front of you forces a
conclusion. Nobody told you he was exhausted - but he slept fourteen hours, so he
**must** have been. `よっぽど` is the same word, said with more feeling.

## Usage

`よほど` is an adverb of degree, but it is not a free intensifier. Almost
everywhere it appears, it is doing one of three jobs.

**1. Inference from evidence** - and this is the one that matters.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in its main use, `よほど` needs a **conjecture ending**:
<code>のだろう</code>, <code>んだ</code>, <code>らしい</code>,
<code>にちがいない</code>, <code>のか</code>. It reports a degree the speaker has
worked out, not one they have observed directly.

</div>

- よほど忙(いそが)しいのだろう。
- よっぽど嬉(うれ)しかったんだね。
- よほど事情(じじょう)があったにちがいない。

The `の / ん` is not decorative. It marks the sentence as an explanation of
something visible, which is exactly what the inference is for.

**2. Comparison** - "far more so", with two things in play.

- こっちのほうがよほど安(やす)い。
- 去年(きょねん)よりよほど楽(らく)になった。

**3. An impulse not acted on**, in the fixed frame
<code>よほど～(よ)うかと思(おも)った</code>: the speaker came close to doing
something and stopped.

- よほど帰(かえ)ろうかと思(おも)った。

The kanji 余程(よほど) exists but kana is normal.

## Examples

<pre>
一日中(いちにちじゅう)寝(ね)ていた。<b>よほど</b>疲(つか)れていたのだろう。
He slept all day. He must have been really tired.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに食(た)べるなんて、<b>よっぽど</b>おなかがすいていたんだね。
Eating that much - you must have been absolutely starving.
</pre>

<pre>
新(あたら)しいほうの辞書(じしょ)が<b>よほど</b>使(つか)いやすい。
The newer dictionary is far easier to use.
</pre>

<pre>
あまりに失礼(しつれい)な言(い)い方(かた)だったので、<b>よっぽど</b>文句(もんく)を言(い)おうかと思(おも)った。
He put it so rudely that I very nearly gave him a piece of my mind.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`よっぽど` is the colloquial variant: more emphatic, more emotional, and the
default in conversation. `よほど` is neutral and is the form to use in writing.
Neither is impolite, and both combine with ます・です endings without trouble, but
`よっぽど` in a report or a formal letter looks out of place, and `よほど` in an
excited retelling sounds a little stiff.

The tone is one of **inference about someone else**. Because the speaker is
reading a state off its symptoms, `よほど` naturally describes other people's
feelings, past states, or one's own past self seen from outside. About your own
present feelings there is nothing to infer, so
<code>今(いま)よほど嬉(うれ)しい</code> does not work - use
<code>とても</code>.

The inference is often sympathetic (何(なに)も言(い)わずに帰(かえ)った。
よほどつらかったのだろう), but it can be pointed: telling someone
<code>よっぽど暇(ひま)なんだね</code> is not a compliment, because you are
deducing something unflattering from their behaviour.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 今日(きょう)は<b>よほど</b>暑(あつ)い。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は<b>とても</b>暑(あつ)い。
  You can feel today's heat directly, so there is nothing to infer.
  <code>よほど暑(あつ)かったらしい</code> - about a day you did not experience - is
  fine.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は<b>よほど</b>驚(おどろ)いた。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は<b>よほど</b>驚(おどろ)いたのだろう。
  Without a conjecture ending the sentence claims direct knowledge of another
  person's inner state, which is what the `のだろう` is there to avoid.

- ❌ <b>よっぽど</b>効率(こうりつ)がよいと考(かんが)えられる。 → ✅ <b>よほど</b>効率(こうりつ)がよいと考(かんが)えられる。
  Register clash. Keep `よっぽど` for speech and `よほど` for anything written down
  formally.

- ❌ <b>よほど</b>会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めた。 → ✅ <b>よほど</b>会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めようかと思(おも)った。
  The "very nearly" sense only exists inside the volitional frame. On a plain past
  tense verb, `よほど` cannot mean "almost".

## Practice

**1.** 部屋(へや)の電気(でんき)もつけずに座(すわ)っている。____嫌(いや)なことがあった____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>よほど</b>嫌(いや)なことがあった<b>のだろう</b>。

The first sentence is the evidence; the second is the conclusion drawn from it.
That pairing - visible behaviour, then `よほど` plus a conjecture ending - is the
core use of the word. `よっぽど` would fit equally well in speech.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)のコンサートはよほど楽(たの)しかったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 昨日(きのう)のコンサートは<b>とても</b>楽(たの)しかったです。

If you were at the concert, your own enjoyment is not something you deduce. Keep
`よほど` for someone else's state: <code>妹(いもうと)はよほど楽(たの)しかったらしく、
帰(かえ)ってからもずっとその話(はなし)をしている</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)はよっぽど忙(いそが)しいんだろう。
(b) 彼(かれ)はとても忙(いそが)しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a statement of fact: the speaker knows his schedule.

(a) is a deduction from symptoms - unanswered messages, a cancelled meeting - and
the `んだろう` admits that the speaker is guessing. It also carries an attitude,
which the flat (b) does not: depending on the context it can be sympathetic or
faintly sarcastic.

</details>

# 一気に

**Meaning** · in one go; in a single burst, without stopping partway

<code>一気(いっき)に</code> (いっきに) is one breath, one push. Whatever the action
is, it runs from start to finish without a pause - and often with more force than
usual.

## Usage

An adverb, in front of the verb. It combines with three kinds of predicate.

- **Consuming or finishing something in one stretch**: 飲(の)む, 食(た)べる,
  読(よ)む, 書(か)く, 片付(かたづ)ける, 終(お)わらせる
- **A steep change in a quantity**: 下(さ)がる, 上(あ)がる, 増(ふ)える,
  減(へ)る
- **Covering distance or ground in one move**: 駆(か)け上(あ)がる,
  追(お)い抜(ぬ)く, 進(すす)む

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `一気に` says there was **no break in the middle**. If the
action was done in instalments, the word is wrong however fast it went.

</div>

It therefore sits badly with anything that must be spread out, and it clashes
outright with <code>少(すこ)しずつ</code> and <code>次第(しだい)に</code>.

The noun 一気(いっき) barely stands alone in modern Japanese. It survives in
<code>一気(いっき)飲(の)み</code>, downing a drink in one - a piece of student
drinking culture with a bad reputation and a chant to match - and in
<code>一気(いっき)に…片(かた)をつける</code>. Use the adverbial form with `に`
and nothing else.

## Examples

<pre>
のどが渇(かわ)いていたので、水(みず)を<b>一気(いっき)に</b>飲(の)んだ。
I was thirsty, so I drank the water straight down.
</pre>

<pre>
続(つづ)きが気(き)になって、この本(ほん)を<b>一気(いっき)に</b>読(よ)んでしまった。
I had to know what happened next, so I read the book in one sitting.
</pre>

<pre>
日(ひ)が暮(く)れると、気温(きおん)が<b>一気(いっき)に</b>下(さ)がった。
Once the sun went down the temperature dropped sharply.
</pre>

<pre>
残(のこ)っていた仕事(しごと)を<b>一気(いっき)に</b>片付(かたづ)けて、久(ひさ)しぶりに定時(ていじ)で帰(かえ)った。
I cleared the rest of my work in one push and got away on time for the first time in ages.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very common in both speech and writing. Nothing about it is
formal or slangy, and it is not marked for age or gender.

What it adds is **momentum**. A sentence with `一気に` sounds energetic, decisive,
sometimes reckless: the speaker did not stop to think, and there was no
opportunity for the thing to lose steam. That is why sports commentary, business
writing and headlines all reach for it -
<code>後半(こうはん)に一気(いっき)に攻(せ)めた</code>,
<code>株価(かぶか)が一気(いっき)に回復(かいふく)した</code>.

Because momentum is the point, `一気に` can carry a warning as easily as praise.
<code>一気(いっき)に飲(の)ませる</code> describes something genuinely
dangerous, and <code>一気(いっき)に決(き)めてしまった</code> can mean a decision
was rushed. Read the surrounding sentence for which colour is meant.

## Contrast

### 一気(いっき)に vs 一度(いちど)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>一気(いっき)に</code> means **without stopping**;
<code>一度(いちど)に</code> means **at the same time**, in one batch.

</div>

<pre>
階段(かいだん)を<b>一気(いっき)に</b>駆(か)け上(あ)がった。
I ran up the stairs without a break.
</pre>

<pre>
二人(ふたり)に<b>一度(いちど)に</b>話(はな)しかけられて、返事(へんじ)ができなかった。
Both of them spoke to me at once and I couldn't answer either.
</pre>

The stairs are a single continuous effort - a duration with no pause in it. The
two people are simultaneous, and no effort is being sustained at all, so
`一気に` has nothing to describe.

Where both are possible the emphasis shifts.
<code>荷物(にもつ)を一気(いっき)に運(はこ)んだ</code> praises the unbroken
effort; <code>荷物(にもつ)を一度(いちど)に運(はこ)んだ</code> says you did not
need a second trip.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 三人(さんにん)に<b>一気(いっき)に</b>質問(しつもん)された。 → ✅ 三人(さんにん)に<b>一度(いちど)に</b>質問(しつもん)された。
  Three simultaneous questions are a batch, not a sustained push.

- ❌ <b>一気(いっき)な</b>変化(へんか)に驚(おどろ)いた。 → ✅ <b>急(きゅう)な</b>変化(へんか)に驚(おどろ)いた。
  `一気に` is an adverb only. There is no な-adjective 一気(いっき)な, and the noun
  cannot modify 変化(へんか) directly either.

- ❌ 毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ<b>一気(いっき)に</b>覚(おぼ)えた。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ覚(おぼ)えた。
  The two adverbs contradict each other. Something learned in daily instalments is
  by definition not done in one go.

## Practice

**1.** 坂道(さかみち)を自転車(じてんしゃ)で____上(のぼ)りきったので、息(いき)が切(き)れている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

坂道(さかみち)を自転車(じてんしゃ)で<b>一気(いっき)に</b>上(のぼ)りきった。

<code>上(のぼ)りきる</code> already means getting all the way up, and
<code>息(いき)が切(き)れる</code> tells you it was done in one unbroken effort.
That is `一気に` exactly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 洗濯物(せんたくもの)を（一気(いっき)に / 一度(いちど)に）全部(ぜんぶ)洗濯機(せんたくき)に入(い)れた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

洗濯物(せんたくもの)を<b>一度(いちど)に</b>全部(ぜんぶ)洗濯機(せんたくき)に入(い)れた。

Loading everything in a single batch is simultaneity, not stamina. Choosing
<code>一気(いっき)に</code> would suggest a strenuous continuous act, which
stuffing a washing machine is not - though it would be fine if you were describing
getting all the laundry done in one unbroken session.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) レポートを三日(みっか)かけて書(か)いた。
(b) レポートを一気(いっき)に書(か)き上(あ)げた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply states how long it took, and implies the work was spread over three
days.

(b) says it was written in one sitting, start to finish. `一気に` adds both speed
and drive, and <code>書(か)き上(あ)げた</code> confirms that the momentum
carried all the way to the end.

</details>

# 何より

**Meaning** · above all; more than anything else; nothing could be better

<code>何(なに)より</code> (なにより) is a comparison against everything at once.
Whatever else is on the table, this outranks it - and in its set-phrase use,
`何よりです`, it is how Japanese says "I'm so glad."

## Usage

`何より` is <code>何(なに)</code> plus the comparative particle
<code>より</code>, and it behaves in three ways.

**1. Before an adjective** - "more ～ than anything".

- 健康(けんこう)が何(なに)より大切(たいせつ)だ。
- 何(なに)より嬉(うれ)しい。

The emphatic form <code>何(なに)よりも</code> is common and slightly stronger.

**2. The noun-modifying form** - <code>何(なに)よりの</code> before a noun means
"the finest possible ～". It takes `の`, never `な`.

- 何(なに)よりの証拠(しょうこ)
- 何(なに)よりのごちそう

**3. The set phrase** - <code>何(なに)よりです</code>.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～で何(なに)よりです</code> is a **reaction to good
news about someone else**: "that they are well / that it went safely is the best
thing of all." It is not used to report your own good fortune.

</div>

- お元気(げんき)そうで何(なに)よりです。
- 大事(だいじ)にいたらず何(なに)よりでした。

The past form <code>何(なに)よりでした</code> is used when the worry is now over.

## Examples

<pre>
体(からだ)が丈夫(じょうぶ)なことが<b>何(なに)より</b>ありがたい。
Being in good health is what I'm most grateful for.
</pre>

<pre>
ご家族(かぞく)が無事(ぶじ)で<b>何(なに)より</b>です。
I'm just relieved your family is safe.
</pre>

<pre>
毎年(まいとし)この席(せき)が埋(う)まることが、続(つづ)けてきたことの<b>何(なに)よりの</b>証拠(しょうこ)だ。
That these seats fill every year is the best proof there is that it was worth keeping going.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)も大事(だいじ)だが、自分(じぶん)の時間(じかん)が持(も)てることが<b>何(なに)よりも</b>ありがたい。
The pay matters too, but more than anything I'm grateful for having time of my own.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral to formal, and heavier in tone than its plain English gloss. `何より`
sounds like a considered judgement, which is why it turns up in speeches, letters
and thank-you notes as often as in conversation.

<code>何(なに)よりです</code> in particular is a **polite social formula**, and it
is worth learning as a unit. You use it on hearing that someone has recovered, got
home safely, or come through something unpleasant. It only ever reports good news:
attaching it to a bad outcome is not merely unidiomatic, it will read as
sarcasm. When speaking to a superior, the fuller
<code>何(なに)よりでございます</code> or a plain
<code>よかったです</code> are both safer than clipping it to a bare
<code>何(なに)より</code>, which sounds abrupt.

The reading is **なにより**. <code>何(なに)</code> takes なに before よ, in the
same way as <code>何(なに)か</code> and <code>何(なに)が</code>; なん appears
before で, だ and の.

## Contrast

### 何(なに)より vs いちばん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いちばん` ranks within a **set you have in mind**; `何より`
measures against **everything there is**.

</div>

<pre>
この中(なか)では、これが<b>いちばん</b>安(やす)い。
Of these, this one is the cheapest.
</pre>

<pre>
安全(あんぜん)が<b>何(なに)より</b>大切(たいせつ)です。
Safety matters more than anything.
</pre>

Put `何より` in the first sentence and it overreaches - you are not claiming the
item is the cheapest in existence, only the cheapest on this shelf. Put `いちばん`
in the second and the declaration shrinks into a ranking exercise.

`いちばん` is also the everyday word: it takes any adjective and any set, and it is
what you say in a shop. `何より` is evaluative and emphatic, closer to "above all",
and it carries the two constructions - <code>何(なに)よりの</code> and
<code>何(なに)よりです</code> - that `いちばん` does not share.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 何(なに)より<b>な</b>証拠(しょうこ)だ。 → ✅ <b>何(なに)よりの</b>証拠(しょうこ)だ。
  In front of a noun it takes `の`. There is no な-adjective here.

- ❌ 事故(じこ)に遭(あ)われたそうで何(なに)よりです。 → ✅ 事故(じこ)に遭(あ)われたそうで、お見舞(みま)い申(もう)し上(あ)げます。
  <code>何(なに)よりです</code> celebrates. Applied to bad news it sounds as
  though you are pleased about the accident.

- ❌ 三(さん)つの中(なか)で、これが何(なに)より軽(かる)い。 → ✅ 三(さん)つの中(なか)で、これが<b>いちばん</b>軽(かる)い。
  A closed set of three calls for `いちばん`. `何より` claims a comparison against
  everything, which contradicts the stated set.

## Practice

**1.** 試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)されたそうで、____です。おめでとうございます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

合格(ごうかく)されたそうで、<b>何(なに)より</b>です。

Good news about the listener, received second-hand with <code>そうで</code>, is
exactly the slot for this formula. <code>よかったですね</code> is the plainer
alternative and works with friends.

</details>

**2.** Translate: "Nothing makes me happier than a letter from my grandchildren."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

孫(まご)からの手紙(てがみ)が<b>何(なに)より</b>嬉(うれ)しい。

`何より` goes straight in front of the adjective, and no explicit "than" phrase is
needed - the word already means "than anything".
<code>何(なに)よりの楽(たの)しみだ</code>, "my greatest pleasure", is a natural
variant using the noun-modifying form.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 家族(かぞく)の健康(けんこう)がいちばん大事(だいじ)です。
(b) 家族(かぞく)の健康(けんこう)が何(なに)より大事(だいじ)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both put family health at the top, and either would pass unremarked in
conversation.

(a) implies a list - work, money, health - with health at number one. (b) refuses
the list altogether: nothing else is even in the running. (b) is the more emphatic
and the more formal of the two, and it is the one you would write in a card.

</details>
