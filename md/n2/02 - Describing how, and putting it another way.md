# N2 - Describing how, and putting it another way


---

# ～ということは

**Meaning** · which means; so that means - takes a fact that has just been
established and states the inference that falls out of it

`ということは` picks something up - a remark, a thing you can see, a fact you have
just checked - packages it as a *statement* with <code>という</code>, and then makes
that statement the topic with `は`. The rest of the sentence is what the speaker
concludes from it. It is the sound of someone working something out in real time.

## Formation

<code>ということ</code> is a noun phrase, so whatever precedes it is a plain-form
clause. `は` then topicalises the whole package.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ということは | 来(こ)ない → 来(こ)ないということは |
| い-adjective | plain form + ということは | 暗(くら)い → 暗(くら)いということは |
| な-adjective | ～だ + ということは | 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かだということは |
| Noun | ～だ + ということは | 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みだということは |
| Standalone sentence opener | ということは、… | - → ということは、中止(ちゅうし)ですね |
| Polite | … + ということは + ですか／でしょうか | - → ということは、明日(あした)でしょうか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - after a noun or a な-adjective the `だ` is regularly dropped in speech:
休(やす)みということは… is heard as often as 休(やす)みだということは…. In
writing, keep the `だ`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
電気(でんき)が消(き)えている。<b>ということは</b>、もう寝(ね)たんだ。
The lights are off. Which means he's already gone to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)が暗(くら)い<b>ということは</b>、まだ帰(かえ)っていないんですね。
The room's dark, so that means she isn't back yet.
</pre>

<pre>
名前(なまえ)がリストにない<b>ということは</b>、申(もう)し込(こ)みが届(とど)いていないのかもしれません。
Your name isn't on the list, which may mean the application never arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が九時(くじ)に終(お)わった<b>ということは</b>、昼(ひる)までに資料(しりょう)を直(なお)せるな。
The meeting finished at nine, so that means I can fix the handouts before lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
三年(さんねん)住(す)んでいた<b>ということは</b>、あの辺(あた)りの道(みち)には詳(くわ)しいはずだ。
He lived there three years, so he must know those streets well.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げはしないと言(い)っている。<b>ということは</b>、量(りょう)を減(へ)らすつもりなのだろうか。
They say there'll be no price rise. Does that mean they intend to shrink the portions?
</pre>

<pre>
外国語(がいこくご)を学(まな)ぶ<b>ということは</b>、その国(くに)の考(かんが)え方(かた)を学(まな)ぶことでもある。
To learn a foreign language is also to learn how that country thinks.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)から連絡(れんらく)が来(こ)ない<b>ということは</b>、手術(しゅじゅつ)は無事(ぶじ)に終(お)わったのだろう。
No word from my mother, which I take to mean the operation went fine.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and one of the most useful things you can carry into
conversation. In speech it is very often a sentence of its own, with a pause after
it: 「定休日(ていきゅうび)か。ということは、明日(あした)出直(でなお)しだな」.
Said on a rising tone it becomes a check rather than a statement -
「ということは、今日(きょう)はもう来(こ)ないってこと?」 - and that is probably
the shape you will use most.

Notice what the second half tends to end in. Because the sentence is an inference
rather than a report, it lands on `んだ`, `のか`, `のだろう`, <code>わけだ</code>
or `ですね` far more often than on a bare `だ`. That ending is part of the pattern's
music; a flat <code>ということは、中止(ちゅうし)だ</code> is grammatical but sounds
oddly clipped.

There is a small social edge to watch. Turning `ということは` on what somebody has
just told you is how you make them own an implication they did not spell out -
「ということは、責任(せきにん)はこちらにあるということですか」. In a negotiation
that is a deliberate and effective move; over coffee with a friend it can feel like
cross-examination. The softeners are the usual ones: `でしょうか`, `かな`, or a
`ね` on the end.

Finally, the seventh example above uses a different sense that shares the same
shape. <code>外国語(がいこくご)を学(まな)ぶということは…</code> is not an
inference at all - it topicalises *the act itself* in order to say what that act
amounts to. This use is at home in essays and speeches, always with a generic,
definitional predicate after it. If the second half states a general truth rather
than a conclusion about the here and now, you are looking at this sense.

## Contrast

### ～ということは vs つまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つまり` re-expresses the *same* content in different words.
`ということは` derives content that was **not** stated.

</div>

<pre>
会員(かいいん)以外(いがい)は入(はい)れません。<b>つまり</b>、受付(うけつけ)で登録(とうろく)が必要(ひつよう)です。
Non-members can't enter. That is, you need to register at reception.
</pre>

<pre>
会員(かいいん)以外(いがい)は入(はい)れない<b>ということは</b>、今日(きょう)来(き)た友達(ともだち)は外(そと)で待(ま)つことになる。
Non-members can't get in, which means the friend I brought today will be waiting outside.
</pre>

The first sentence says the same thing twice; delete the second half and nothing is
lost. The second sentence adds a fact about today that the rule alone did not
contain. That is the test: if the second half is genuinely new information *derived*
from the first, `ということは` is the word.

The two also sit differently. `つまり` is an adverb dropped in front of a clause;
`ということは` is a whole clause plus a topic particle, which is why it can carry the
premise inside itself - <code>暗(くら)いということは</code> - where `つまり` needs
the premise to have been a separate sentence.

### ～ということは vs ～わけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ということは` **opens** the sentence with the premise;
<code>わけだ</code> **closes** it with the conclusion. They are the two ends of the
same move and frequently appear in one sentence.

</div>

<pre>
靴(くつ)がない<b>ということは</b>、もう出(で)かけたわけだ。
His shoes are gone, so he must already have left.
</pre>

Here the premise is marked at the front and the conclusion at the back, and neither
marker is redundant. Where they genuinely compete is when only one appears, and
then the difference is which half you want to spotlight. <code>どうりで寒(さむ)いわけだ</code>
points at the conclusion and says "that explains it"; <code>窓(まど)が開(あ)いているということは</code>
points at the evidence and leaves the conclusion to follow.

One thing only `ということは` can do: stand alone as a sentence opener with the
premise left implicit, because it was the previous speaker's line. <code>わけだ</code>
cannot start a sentence at all.

### ～ということは vs ～ということだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - swap `は` for `だ` and the clause stops being the premise and
becomes the **conclusion**. `ということは` is the front half; `ということだ` is the
back half.

</div>

<pre>
返信(へんしん)がない<b>ということは</b>、断(ことわ)られたのだろう。
There's been no reply, so I suppose I've been turned down.
</pre>

<pre>
返信(へんしん)がないのだから、断(ことわ)られた<b>ということだ</b>。
There's been no reply, so it means I've been turned down.
</pre>

Same reasoning, opposite packaging. Note also that `ということだ` doubles as a
hearsay marker - "I hear that" - when a source is named, and `ということは` never
does. If the sentence reports somebody else's words rather than your own reasoning,
`は` is not available.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>ということは</b>、留守(るす)かな。（in writing）→ ✅ 静(しず)か<b>だということは</b>、留守(るす)かな。
  な-adjectives and nouns link to <code>という</code> with `だ`. Dropping it is fine
  in speech and sloppy on the page.

- ❌ ということは、今日(きょう)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうです。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうです。
  `ということは` has to conclude something from a premise. With nothing behind it and
  a piece of hearsay in front, there is no inference for it to introduce.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ないということは残念(ざんねん)です。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ないのは残念(ざんねん)です。
  To say that a fact is regrettable you want the plain nominaliser `の` or
  <code>こと</code>. <code>ということは</code> announces an inference, and "that he
  isn't coming is a shame" is not one.

- ❌ 田中(たなか)さんによると、中止(ちゅうし)だ<b>ということは</b>。 → ✅ 田中(たなか)さんによると、中止(ちゅうし)だ<b>ということです</b>。
  Reporting a source is the job of `ということだ`. `ということは` cannot end a
  sentence at all - something has to follow it.

## Practice

**1.** 窓(まど)に明(あ)かりがついていない。____、まだ誰(だれ)も来(き)ていないんだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ということは</b>、まだ誰(だれ)も来(き)ていないんだ。

The dark window is the evidence; the empty room is the conclusion drawn from it,
and it is information the first sentence did not state. Ending the second half in
`んだ` is what a spoken inference normally does.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 全員(ぜんいん)が反対(はんたい)した（ということは・つまり）、この案(あん)はもう一度(いちど)作(つく)り直(なお)すことになる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

全員(ぜんいん)が反対(はんたい)した<b>ということは</b>、この案(あん)はもう一度(いちど)作(つく)り直(なお)すことになる。

Redrafting the proposal is a consequence, not a rewording of "everyone objected",
so this is derivation rather than restatement. `つまり` would also need the first
clause to be a finished sentence of its own; here it is a clause inside the
sentence, which is exactly the slot `ということは` fills.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The shop's closed on Mondays? So that means we can't go tomorrow."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「あの店(みせ)は月曜(げつよう)が休(やす)みなんですか。<b>ということは</b>、明日(あした)は行(い)けませんね」

The premise came from the other speaker, so `ということは` opens a fresh sentence
with the premise left implicit. `ね` at the end invites agreement, which is the
natural tone for an inference about a shared plan.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)のメールによると、会議(かいぎ)は来週(らいしゅう)に延(の)びたということは。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)のメールによると、会議(かいぎ)は来週(らいしゅう)に延(の)びた<b>ということです</b>。

Reporting what a named source said calls for `ということだ／ということです`. The
`は` version topicalises a premise and therefore demands a second half; ending the
sentence on it leaves the listener waiting.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 試験(しけん)に受(う)かった<b>ということは</b>、四月(しがつ)から通(かよ)えるんだね。
(b) 試験(しけん)に受(う)かった<b>ということだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats passing the exam as the known premise and works forward from it to
something new - being able to attend from April. The speaker is reasoning out loud
and expects the listener to follow.

(b) presents passing the exam as the endpoint: either a conclusion just reached
from other evidence, or a report of what somebody else said. Nothing follows it,
and depending on context it can be read as "so I hear", which (a) can never mean.

</details>

# すなわち

**Meaning** · namely; that is to say - puts an equals sign between two expressions
for the same thing

`すなわち` is the written language's `つまり`. It announces that what follows is
not a new fact but the *same* fact under a different name: a technical term given
its plain equivalent, a date given its calendar form, a category given its precise
extent.

## Usage

`すなわち` is a conjunction. It sits between the two expressions it equates, almost
always after a comma, and it never changes form.

The commonest shape is noun phrase, comma, `すなわち`, noun phrase:

- 本社(ほんしゃ)の所在地(しょざいち)、すなわち大阪市(おおさかし)
- 創業(そうぎょう)の年(とし)、すなわち一九六二年(せんきゅうひゃくろくじゅうにねん)

It can also join two clauses, in which case the second restates the first in
plainer or more precise terms:

- 申込(もうしこみ)は先着順(せんちゃくじゅん)である。すなわち、定員(ていいん)に達(たっ)し次第(しだい)締(し)め切(き)る。

Modern editorial practice writes it in kana. The kanji 即(すなわ)ち still turns
up in older or deliberately formal texts.

<div class="note">

**Note** - in classical Japanese and in translations of the Chinese classics
`すなわち` also means "then; thereupon", often spelled 則(すなわ)ち. You will meet
it in that sense in a proverb or an old text; it is not available in modern prose,
where the equating sense is the only live one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
料金(りょうきん)は現地(げんち)の通貨(つうか)、<b>すなわち</b>ユーロで払(はら)う。
Payment is in the local currency, that is, in euros.
</pre>

<pre>
音(おと)の速(はや)さ、<b>すなわち</b>音速(おんそく)は気温(きおん)で変(か)わる。
The speed of sound - the sonic velocity - varies with air temperature.
</pre>

<pre>
本(ほん)契約(けいやく)は締結(ていけつ)の日(ひ)、<b>すなわち</b>四月(しがつ)一日(ついたち)から効力(こうりょく)を持(も)つ。
This contract takes effect on the date of signing, namely 1 April.
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)では回答者(かいとうしゃ)の三分(さんぶん)の二(に)、<b>すなわち</b>約(やく)六百人(ろっぴゃくにん)が値上(ねあ)げに反対(はんたい)であった。
In the survey two-thirds of respondents - some six hundred people - opposed the price rise.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a written word. It belongs to contracts, statutes, academic papers,
technical manuals, annual reports and the printed part of a museum caption. Spoken
aloud it survives in lectures, courtroom argument and formal presentations, where
the speaker is essentially reading. In ordinary conversation it lands like a clause
of a rental agreement read out over dinner, and a learner who deploys it in a café
will get a smile.

What it adds over `つまり`, which you already have from N3, is register and nothing
else - the meaning is the same. That is the honest answer, and it is worth saying
plainly: this page exists so that you recognise `すなわち` on the page and choose
`つまり` when you open your mouth.

One consequence of the equating sense is that `すなわち` commits you. It claims the
two expressions have exactly the same extension, so a writer who uses it and then
turns out to have listed only some examples has made a real error. When you are not
certain the two sides match, `たとえば` or `など` is the safer word.

`すなわち` also has no emotional colour at all. `つまり` can carry mild impatience,
<code>要(よう)するに</code> carries a judgement that the original was long-winded;
`すなわち` carries neither. That neutrality is exactly why formal documents like it.

## Contrast

### すなわち vs つまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, different clothes. `つまり` goes anywhere; `すなわち`
is written-register only, and it prefers two noun phrases rather than two sentences.

</div>

<pre>
第(だい)三条(さんじょう)に定(さだ)める期間(きかん)、<b>すなわち</b>六(ろっ)か月(げつ)を超(こ)えてはならない。
It may not exceed the period laid down in Article 3, namely six months.
</pre>

<pre>
半年(はんとし)まで、<b>つまり</b>六(ろっ)か月(げつ)までってことだね。
Up to half a year - six months, in other words.
</pre>

The content is identical; only the clothes differ. Notice too that the `つまり`
version ends in <code>ってこと</code>, which `すなわち` would never take. The
sentence-final material has to match the register of the connector, and mismatching
them is a very audible mistake.

### すなわち vs 言(い)い換(か)えれば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すなわち` says the two expressions **are** the same thing.
<code>言(い)い換(か)えれば</code> says the writer is **rephrasing**, and the
rewording may be looser.

</div>

<pre>
出席率(しゅっせきりつ)は七割(ななわり)、<b>すなわち</b>十人(じゅうにん)のうち七人(しちにん)であった。
Attendance was seventy per cent, that is, seven people in ten.
</pre>

<pre>
出席率(しゅっせきりつ)は七割(ななわり)だった。<b>言(い)い換(か)えれば</b>、三割(さんわり)の人(ひと)が来(こ)なかったのである。
Attendance was seventy per cent. To put it another way, three in ten did not come.
</pre>

The second version is not an equation - it turns the figure round and looks at it
from the other side. `すなわち` cannot do that, because the two halves would no
longer be the same statement. When your rewording adds a point of view, reach for
<code>言(い)い換(か)えれば</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ すなわち、今日(きょう)は行(い)けないんだ。（to a friend）→ ✅ <b>つまり</b>、今日(きょう)は行(い)けないんだ。
  The connector and the ending have to agree in register. `すなわち` in front of
  `んだ` sounds like a statute read aloud in a living room.

- ❌ 果物(くだもの)、<b>すなわち</b>りんごやみかん → ✅ 果物(くだもの)、<b>たとえば</b>りんごやみかん
  `すなわち` claims the two sides are the same set. Apples and oranges are only
  instances of fruit, so this needs `たとえば` or `など`.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が続(つづ)いた。<b>すなわち</b>試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が続(つづ)いた。<b>したがって</b>試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  A cancellation is a consequence, not a restatement of the rain. In modern
  Japanese `すなわち` does not express cause and effect; したがって or ゆえに does.

## Practice

**1.** 申込(もうしこみ)の締切(しめきり)は今年度(こんねんど)末(まつ)、____三月(さんがつ)三十一日(さんじゅういちにち)である。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>すなわち</b>三月(さんがつ)三十一日(さんじゅういちにち)である

The second expression is the same date as the first, written out precisely, and
the sentence ends in `である` - a written-style sentence needs a written-style
connector.

</details>

**2.** Choose: あの人(ひと)、優(やさ)しいっていうか、（すなわち・つまり）断(ことわ)れないだけだと思(おも)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（優(やさ)しいっていうか、）<b>つまり</b>断(ことわ)れないだけだと思(おも)う。

Everything around the blank is casual - <code>っていうか</code>, <code>だけだと思(おも)う</code>.
`すなわち` would clash with all of it, and in speech it is essentially unavailable
outside a lectern.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会長(かいちょう)、<b>すなわち</b>創業者(そうぎょうしゃ)の孫(まご)が挨拶(あいさつ)した。
(b) 会長(かいちょう)、<b>たとえば</b>創業者(そうぎょうしゃ)の孫(まご)が挨拶(あいさつ)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states that the chairman and the founder's grandson are one and the same
person; the second phrase simply identifies who the chairman is. This is the normal
reading and the one the sentence wants.

(b) is broken, and instructively so. `たとえば` presents the second phrase as one
possible instance of the first, which would mean the company has several chairmen
and this happened to be one of them. When the two expressions pick out the same
individual, only `すなわち` works.

</details>

# 要するに

**Meaning** · the point is; in short; when you boil it down - compresses a long
account into the one thing that actually matters

<code>要(よう)</code> is the noun "gist, essential point", and
<code>要(よう)するに</code> announces that the speaker is about to supply it.
Unlike a neutral restatement, it carries a small claim: *I have decided what the
important part was.*

## Usage

<code>要(よう)するに</code> is an adverb and opens the clause that carries the
summary. Nothing attaches to it and it never changes form.

Two habits are worth building. First, what comes **before** it. The pattern almost
always follows something long - an explanation, a list of conditions, somebody's
rambling account - either in the same sentence with `が` or `けど`, or as a
preceding sentence:

- 説明(せつめい)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)続(つづ)いたが、要(よう)するに…
- 条件(じょうけん)が五(いつ)つ書(か)いてある。要(よう)するに…

Second, what comes **after**. Because the clause is a verdict rather than a fresh
report, it lands very often on <code>ということだ</code>, `のだ`, `わけだ` or
`んです`: <code>要(よう)するに人手(ひとで)が足(た)りないということです</code>.

There is a shorter, more colloquial cousin, <code>要(よう)は</code>. Same job,
one syllable less ceremony, and very common in speech:
<code>要(よう)は、やる気(き)があるかどうかだよ</code>.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>要(よう)するに</b>、お金(かね)の問題(もんだい)だ。
It comes down to money.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>要(よう)するに</b>、君(きみ)は行(い)きたくないんだね。
So the point is you don't want to go.
</pre>

<pre>
いろいろ理由(りゆう)を並(なら)べていたが、<b>要(よう)するに</b>準備(じゅんび)が間(ま)に合(あ)わなかったのだろう。
He gave all sorts of reasons, but the truth of it is he simply wasn't ready in time.
</pre>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)が三(みっ)つ並(なら)んでいるが、<b>要(よう)するに</b>経験者(けいけんしゃ)以外(いがい)は応募(おうぼ)するなということだ。
Three conditions are listed, but the message is: don't apply unless you have experience.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in form - it is at home in a meeting, an essay and a conversation
alike - but not neutral in *attitude*. Every use of <code>要(よう)するに</code>
implies that what preceded it was longer than it needed to be, and that the speaker
has done the work of cutting it down. That is fine when the words being cut down
are your own, and it is what makes the pattern so useful for wrapping up your own
explanation: <code>要(よう)するに、私(わたし)の準備(じゅんび)不足(ぶそく)でした</code>.

Pointed at somebody else it can bite. Summarising a client's or a manager's
explanation with <code>要(よう)するに</code> tells them, politely but audibly,
that they went on too long. It is a tendency rather than a rule - tone, relationship
and a `ね` on the end all soften it - but the safe default when the words are not
yours is `つまり`, or a question shape:
<code>要(よう)するに、こういうことでしょうか</code>, which hands the judgement
back to them.

The word also has a genuinely combative use. In an argument,
<code>要(よう)するに、反対(はんたい)なんですね</code> strips away everything
diplomatic the other person said and names what they were avoiding saying. Speakers
use it that way on purpose. Recognise it when it is aimed at you.

<code>要(よう)は</code> is the casual variant and carries the same edge in a
lighter package; it is common among friends and colleagues and slightly too blunt
for a formal presentation.

## Contrast

### 要(よう)するに vs つまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つまり` re-expresses; <code>要(よう)するに</code> **discards**.
`つまり` keeps all the content in different words; <code>要(よう)するに</code>
throws most of it away and keeps the core.

</div>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は変更(へんこう)になりました。<b>つまり</b>、南口(みなみぐち)に集合(しゅうごう)です。
The venue has changed - that is, we're meeting at the south exit.
</pre>

<pre>
注意(ちゅうい)が十(じゅっ)か条(じょう)も書(か)いてあるが、<b>要(よう)するに</b>静(しず)かにしろということだ。
There are ten separate warnings, but what it all amounts to is: be quiet.
</pre>

Delete the second half of the `つまり` sentence and you lose nothing. Delete the
second half of the other one and you lose the whole point, because the ten warnings
never said "be quiet" - the speaker decided that was the gist. That decision is what
<code>要(よう)するに</code> adds, and it is also what makes it risky.

### 要(よう)するに vs すなわち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すなわち` is an equals sign between two expressions.
<code>要(よう)するに</code> is an arrow from a lot of material to a little.

</div>

<pre>
締切(しめきり)は年度末(ねんどまつ)、<b>すなわち</b>三月(さんがつ)末日(まつじつ)である。
The deadline is the end of the fiscal year, that is, the last day of March.
</pre>

<pre>
報告書(ほうこくしょ)は五十(ごじゅう)ページあるが、<b>要(よう)するに</b>計画(けいかく)は失敗(しっぱい)だったのである。
The report runs to fifty pages, but in essence the plan failed.
</pre>

`すなわち` could not replace the second one: fifty pages and "the plan failed" are
not the same expression, and no honest equals sign joins them. Conversely
<code>要(よう)するに</code> could not replace the first, because there is nothing
to boil down - a date is already as short as it gets.

Register differs too. `すなわち` is written-only; <code>要(よう)するに</code>
works in speech and on the page.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)のお話(はなし)は、<b>要(よう)するに</b>残業(ざんぎょう)するなということですね。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)のお話(はなし)は、<b>つまり</b>残業(ざんぎょう)するなということですね。
  Grammatically fine, socially loaded: it tells your manager he was long-winded.
  `つまり` restates without passing that judgement.

- ❌ <b>要(よう)するに</b>、明日(あした)は九時(くじ)集合(しゅうごう)です。（as the first thing said）→ ✅ 明日(あした)は九時(くじ)集合(しゅうごう)です。
  There has to be something to summarise. Opening with
  <code>要(よう)するに</code> leaves the listener hunting for the long version
  they apparently missed.

- ❌ 要(よう)する<b>に</b>は、準備(じゅんび)が大切(たいせつ)だ。 → ✅ <b>要(よう)するに</b>、準備(じゅんび)が大切(たいせつ)だ。
  It is a frozen adverb. No particle follows it, and it never conjugates - there is
  no ❌要(よう)した or ❌要(よう)しますに.

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は長(なが)かったが、____自分(じぶん)のせいではないと言(い)いたいのだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>要(よう)するに</b>自分(じぶん)のせいではないと言(い)いたいのだ

A long explanation is being cut down to the one thing behind it, and the speaker -
not the person who spoke - is deciding what that thing was. The <code>のだ</code>
ending is the usual landing place for such a verdict.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 「前向(まえむ)きに検討(けんとう)します」というのは、（要(よう)するに・すなわち）断(ことわ)られたということだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（…というのは、）<b>要(よう)するに</b>断(ことわ)られたということだ。

The two expressions are not equivalents - one is a polite formula, the other is
what the speaker judges it really meant - so `すなわち` is out. Reading through
diplomatic language to the blunt core is exactly the job
<code>要(よう)するに</code> does.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 先生(せんせい)のお話(はなし)は、つまり復習(ふくしゅう)が大事(だいじ)だということですね。
(b) 先生(せんせい)のお話(はなし)は、要(よう)するに復習(ふくしゅう)が大事(だいじ)だということですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply plays the teacher's point back to check that you followed it. It makes
no comment on how the point was delivered, so it is safe with anyone.

(b) adds an unspoken "…and you could have said so in one line." Aimed at a teacher
or a superior that is a small rudeness, even though nothing in the sentence is
grammatically different. Save <code>要(よう)するに</code> for summarising your
own words, or for a situation where you mean the edge.

</details>

# いわゆる

**Meaning** · so-called; what people call - hands the following noun over as a
label that is already in circulation

`いわゆる` says: this is the word other people use for it. It does not claim the
word is a good one, and - unlike English "so-called" - it does not usually claim it
is a bad one either. Most of the time it is simply a way of quoting the public
vocabulary.

## Usage

`いわゆる` is a 連体詞(れんたいし) - a word whose only job is to sit in front of a noun. It
never conjugates, never takes `な` or `の`, and cannot modify a verb or an
adjective. The shape is always the same:

- いわゆる + noun → いわゆる方言(ほうげん)
- いわゆる + noun phrase → いわゆる地方(ちほう)創生(そうせい)の政策(せいさく)
- いわゆる + a quoted term → いわゆる「終活(しゅうかつ)」

Because it is quoting somebody else's word, the noun after it is very often in
quotation marks, in katakana, or a piece of jargon the writer expects some readers
not to know. In casual speech the same move is made with
<code>いわゆる～ってやつ</code>:
<code>これがいわゆる時差(じさ)ぼけってやつか</code>.

It descends from <code>言(い)う</code> plus an old passive ending - literally
"which is said" - and is occasionally written 所謂(いわゆる), though modern
practice is kana.

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は<b>いわゆる</b>天才(てんさい)だ。
He is what you'd call a genius.
</pre>

<pre>
これが<b>いわゆる</b>五月病(ごがつびょう)です。
This is the thing people call "May sickness".
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(あた)りは<b>いわゆる</b>下町(したまち)で、昔(むかし)ながらの商店街(しょうてんがい)が残(のこ)っている。
This area is what's known as shitamachi - the old shopping streets are still here.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いわゆる</b>「働(はたら)き方(かた)改革(かいかく)」が始(はじ)まってから、残業(ざんぎょう)は本当(ほんとう)に減(へ)ったのだろうか。
Since the so-called "work-style reform" began, has overtime really gone down?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It is equally at home in a newspaper, a lecture and a
conversation, and it does not change form for politeness.

The trap for English speakers is the one the translation creates. English
"so-called" is nearly always sceptical - "his so-called friends" means they are not
really friends. `いわゆる` is only *mildly* distancing, and in most sentences it is
not distancing at all: it is a courtesy to the listener, marking a term as one that
circulates publicly so that nobody has to pretend they invented it. When a Japanese
writer wants the sceptical reading, they get it from the surrounding sentence, from
quotation marks, or from a following <code>とやら</code> or `らしい` - not from
`いわゆる` on its own.

The one condition the word does impose is that the label must already exist. You
cannot use `いわゆる` to introduce a name you have just made up, and you cannot use
it for a term you are about to define for the first time. If your reader could not
in principle have met the word before, `いわゆる` is the wrong signal.

There is a slightly bookish flavour to it in speech - a hair more considered than
「なんていうか」 - but nothing that would sound out of place among friends.

## Contrast

### いわゆる vs いわば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いわゆる` quotes a label **other people** already use.
`いわば` introduces a comparison **you** are making up on the spot: "so to speak;
as it were."

</div>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)はこの村(むら)の<b>いわゆる</b>世話役(せわやく)だった。
My grandfather was what the village called its fixer.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)はこの村(むら)の、<b>いわば</b>生(い)きた図書館(としょかん)だった。
My grandfather was, so to speak, the village's living library.
</pre>

Nobody in the village ever called him a library; that is the speaker's metaphor,
which is why `いわば` is the only option in the second sentence. The two words look
and sound close enough to be swapped by accident, and the swap is always audible:
`いわゆる` in front of a fresh metaphor sounds as if you are attributing your own
invention to the public, and `いわば` in front of an established term sounds as if
you had never heard it before.

Grammatically they differ too. `いわゆる` must be followed by a noun. `いわば` is an
adverb and can head a whole clause: <code>いわば、これは練習(れんしゅう)なのだ</code>.

### いわゆる vs 呼(よ)ばれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いわゆる` assumes the label is known and moves on.
<code>～と呼(よ)ばれる</code> stops to **state** that the label exists, and is
what you use when introducing a term for the first time.

</div>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)には<b>いわゆる</b>町家(まちや)が多(おお)く残(のこ)っている。
Kyoto still has many of what are called machiya townhouses.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)の古(ふる)い木造(もくぞう)住宅(じゅうたく)は町家(まちや)と<b>呼(よ)ばれる</b>。
Kyoto's old wooden houses are called machiya.
</pre>

The second sentence teaches the word; the first sentence uses it. That is why a
textbook or an encyclopaedia entry introduces a term with
<code>呼(よ)ばれる</code> and only afterwards allows itself `いわゆる`. Using
`いわゆる` for a word the reader has certainly never met is a small failure of
manners: it implies they should have known.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ いわゆる<b>な</b>天才(てんさい) → ✅ <b>いわゆる</b>天才(てんさい)
  `いわゆる` is a 連体詞(れんたいし) and is already in noun-modifying shape. It takes neither
  `な` nor `の`.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)の話(はなし)は<b>いわゆる</b>面白(おもしろ)い。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)の話(はなし)は<b>いわば</b>落語(らくご)だ。
  Something has to follow `いわゆる`, and that something must be a noun. It cannot
  modify an adjective or a verb at all.

- ❌ 私(わたし)が<b>いわゆる</b>「ゆる勉(べん)」と呼(よ)んでいる方法(ほうほう) → ✅ 私(わたし)が「ゆる勉(べん)」と呼(よ)んでいる方法(ほうほう)
  The sentence itself admits the name is the speaker's own coinage. `いわゆる`
  claims the opposite - that the term is already out there - so the two halves
  contradict each other.

## Practice

**1.** 最近(さいきん)よく聞(き)く____「副業(ふくぎょう)」を始(はじ)める人(ひと)が増(ふ)えている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

最近(さいきん)よく聞(き)く<b>いわゆる</b>「副業(ふくぎょう)」を始(はじ)める人(ひと)が増(ふ)えている。

<code>最近(さいきん)よく聞(き)く</code> already tells us the term is in
circulation, which is precisely the condition `いわゆる` needs. Note that it sits
directly in front of the noun, with the quotation marks doing the extra work of
flagging the word as borrowed.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この建物(たてもの)は町(まち)の（いわゆる・いわば）顔(かお)のような存在(そんざい)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この建物(たてもの)は町(まち)の<b>いわば</b>顔(かお)のような存在(そんざい)だ。

Nobody actually calls the building "the town's face" - the speaker is inventing the
image, and <code>のような</code> confirms it is a comparison rather than a name.
`いわゆる` would falsely present the metaphor as a term everyone uses.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は<b>いわゆる</b>優等生(ゆうとうせい)だ。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)は優等生(ゆうとうせい)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a plain description: she gets good marks and behaves well.

(a) holds the word at arm's length. It reports the label society attaches to her
without the speaker fully signing up to it, so depending on the sentence around it
you may hear anything from neutral quoting - "she's the model-student type" - to a
faint reservation about whether the label means much. The distancing is mild, and
much weaker than English "so-called": to say clearly that she is *not* really an
優等生(ゆうとうせい) you would need to add something like
<code>表向(おもてむ)きは</code> or a following `らしい`.

</details>

# ～というものだ

**Meaning** · that is what you call ～; that is simply ～ - names a situation with
the word the speaker thinks it deserves

<code>物(もの)</code> here is the abstract "thing", and
<code>という物(もの)だ</code> literally says "it is a thing called ～". In use it
is a verdict. The speaker takes a situation, puts a noun on it -
<code>無理(むり)</code>, <code>失礼(しつれい)</code>,
<code>友情(ゆうじょう)</code> - and presents that label as obvious to any
reasonable person.

## Formation

The slot in front of <code>というものだ</code> is a **noun** or a な-adjective stem:
the word that names the verdict.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というものだ | 常識(じょうしき) → 常識(じょうしき)というものだ |
| な-adjective | stem + というものだ | 失礼(しつれい) → 失礼(しつれい)というものだ |
| Polite | … + というものです | 無理(むり) → 無理(むり)というものです |
| Softened | … + というものだろう／ではないか | 贅沢(ぜいたく) → 贅沢(ぜいたく)というものだろう |
| Generic topic | Noun + というものは + general statement | 時間(じかん) → 時間(じかん)というものは |
| Denying a generalisation | plain clause + というものではない | 安(やす)ければいい → 安(やす)ければいいというものではない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negative is not a mirror image. <code>というものだ</code> takes a
noun and asserts a label; <code>というものではない</code> takes a whole clause and
denies a blanket rule. They are best learned as two patterns that happen to share a
shape.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a potential verb occasionally appears in the slot instead of a noun:
<code>これで安心(あんしん)して眠(ねむ)れるというものだ</code>, "now I can
finally sleep easy." It is a real construction but a narrow and rather literary
one. Learn the noun pattern; recognise this one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
それは無理(むり)<b>というものだ</b>。
That is simply asking too much.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)<b>というもの</b>は、待(ま)ってくれない。
Time, that thing, waits for no one.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)の日記(にっき)を読(よ)むなんて、失礼(しつれい)<b>というものです</b>。
Reading somebody's diary is plain bad manners.
</pre>

<pre>
一日(いちにち)で百(ひゃく)語(ご)覚(おぼ)えろというのは、無茶(むちゃ)<b>というものだ</b>。
Telling someone to learn a hundred words in a day is just unreasonable.
</pre>

<pre>
困(こま)っているときに黙(だま)って手(て)を貸(か)してくれる、それが友情(ゆうじょう)<b>というものだろう</b>。
Quietly lending a hand when you're stuck - surely that is what friendship is.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)に全部(ぜんぶ)任(まか)せておいて文句(もんく)を言(い)うのは、身勝手(みがって)<b>というものではないか</b>。
Dumping the whole thing on the new hire and then complaining is rather selfish, isn't it?
</pre>

<pre>
努力(どりょく)すれば必(かなら)ず報(むく)われる<b>というものでもない</b>。
It isn't as though hard work is always rewarded.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)さえ高(たか)ければいい仕事(しごと)だ<b>というものではない</b>。
A job isn't a good job just because the pay is high.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The positive form is a pronouncement, and it sounds like one. It presents the
speaker's judgement as something any sensible person would share, which makes it
useful for stating a principle and slightly grand for stating a preference. You will
hear it from older speakers, in essays and columns, in speeches, and in the mouths
of characters in fiction who are about to say something wise. Among friends it is
mildly theatrical - which is why it is also common as a joke.

Because it claims universality, aiming it at a specific person's behaviour is a
telling-off: <code>それは甘(あま)えというものだ</code> is not a description, it
is a rebuke. Soften it to <code>というものだろう</code> or
<code>というものではないか</code> and it becomes an invitation to agree rather than
a ruling. That is the shape to use if you are not the senior person in the
conversation.

<code>Nというものは</code> is a different animal and much safer. With `は` instead
of `だ`, it introduces a noun as a general category and says something true of the
category as a whole: <code>習慣(しゅうかん)というものは急(きゅう)には変(か)わらない</code>.
This is the reflective, essayistic register - it slows the sentence down and signals
"a general truth follows".

The negative <code>というものではない</code> is the most usable of the three and the
least marked. It is the standard way to push back on an oversimplification in a
meeting without contradicting anyone flatly, and it fits polite speech comfortably:
<code>人数(にんずう)を増(ふ)やせば解決(かいけつ)するというものではありません</code>.

## Contrast

### ～というものだ vs ～ものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ものだ</code> describes **how the world tends to be**.
<code>というものだ</code> puts a **name** on a particular case in front of you.

</div>

<pre>
年(とし)を取(と)ると昔(むかし)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)す<b>ものだ</b>。
People do tend to dwell on the past as they get older.
</pre>

<pre>
昔話(むかしばなし)ばかりして人(ひと)の話(はなし)を聞(き)かないのは、独(ひと)りよがり<b>というものだ</b>。
Talking only about the old days and never listening is what I'd call self-absorbed.
</pre>

<code>ものだ</code> attaches to a verb or adjective clause and generalises about
behaviour; <code>というものだ</code> attaches to a noun and labels. If you can put
the sentence in the form "X is (an instance of) Y", where Y is a noun,
<code>というものだ</code> is available. If the sentence has no such Y, it is not.

### ～というものだ vs ～というものではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative does **not** deny the positive. It denies a
*blanket rule* that somebody else has implied.

</div>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)すれば勝(か)てる<b>というものではない</b>。
It isn't simply a matter of practising and winning.
</pre>

The sentence does not say practice is useless. It says the "practise and you win"
formula is too crude. That is why the pattern needs a whole conditional-ish clause
in front of it - the thing being denied is a rule, not a label - and why
<code>でもない</code> is such a common variant: it adds "and that isn't the whole
story either".

Note also that this is where the pattern earns its keep in real conversation.
<code>それは違(ちが)います</code> contradicts a person;
<code>そういうものではありません</code> contradicts an idea.

### ～というものだ vs ～わけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>わけだ</code> draws a **logical** conclusion from a
premise. <code>というものだ</code> passes a **value judgement** on a situation.

</div>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)住(す)んでいたのだから、方言(ほうげん)が出(で)る<b>わけだ</b>。
He lived there ten years, so naturally the dialect slips out.
</pre>

<pre>
十年(じゅうねん)世話(せわ)になった人(ひと)に挨拶(あいさつ)もしないのは、恩(おん)知(し)らず<b>というものだ</b>。
Not even saying goodbye to someone who looked after you for ten years is sheer ingratitude.
</pre>

Both look backwards at a stated fact, and both end the sentence, which is why they
get confused. But <code>わけだ</code> could be replaced by "that follows", and
<code>というものだ</code> by "that deserves to be called". Only the second is an
opinion, and only the second can be argued with.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 時間(じかん)<b>というものだ</b>、大切(たいせつ)だ。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)<b>というものは</b>大切(たいせつ)だ。
  With `だ` the phrase ends the sentence. To introduce a category and then say
  something about it, you need the topic particle `は`.

- ❌ 失礼(しつれい)<b>だというものだ</b>。 → ✅ 失礼(しつれい)<b>というものだ</b>。
  The noun or な-adjective stem attaches to <code>という</code> bare here. A `だ`
  before it belongs to the ordinary quoting <code>という</code>, not to this
  pattern.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>というものだ</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)だ<b>そうだ</b>。
  Reporting a fact is not judging one. <code>というものだ</code> needs a noun that
  evaluates - <code>無理(むり)</code>, <code>当然(とうぜん)</code>,
  <code>贅沢(ぜいたく)</code> - not a neutral one.

- ❌ 安(やす)い<b>というものだ</b>から、買(か)わない。 → ✅ 安(やす)ければいい<b>というものではない</b>から、買(か)わない。
  "Cheap" on its own is not a verdict anyone would pronounce. What the speaker
  actually wants to reject is the rule "cheap is good", and rejecting a rule is the
  negative pattern's job.

## Practice

**1.** 約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)に三十分(さんじゅっぷん)も遅(おく)れて謝(あやま)りもしないとは、____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

失礼(しつれい)<b>というものだ</b>（or 失礼(しつれい)というものではないか）

The sentence sets up a behaviour and then wants a noun that names it. The plain
<code>だ</code> version is a flat rebuke; the
<code>ではないか</code> version invites the listener to agree, which is gentler and
usually the better choice unless you outrank them.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)ければ品質(ひんしつ)がいい（というものだ・というものではない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

値段(ねだん)が高(たか)ければ品質(ひんしつ)がいい<b>というものではない</b>。

What precedes the blank is a conditional rule, not a noun, and only the negative
pattern takes a clause. The meaning is also the natural one: the speaker is
rejecting an oversimplification, not endorsing it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Sending a one-line message after three years of silence is, frankly, rude."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

三年(さんねん)連絡(れんらく)もしなかったのに一行(いちぎょう)のメッセージだけとは、失礼(しつれい)<b>というものだ</b>。

The English "frankly" is doing exactly what
<code>というものだ</code> does in Japanese: presenting the judgement as one any
reasonable person would reach. <code>とは</code> in front is a natural partner,
since it flags the described behaviour as something to be marvelled at.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 習慣(しゅうかん)というものだ、一日(いちにち)では変(か)わらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 習慣(しゅうかん)<b>というものは</b>、一日(いちにち)では変(か)わらない。

The sentence wants to introduce "habits" as a general category and then state
something true of them, so the noun phrase has to be topicalised with `は`. With
<code>だ</code> the sentence has already finished before the interesting part
arrives.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 人(ひと)に頼(たよ)りすぎるのはよくない。
(b) 人(ひと)に頼(たよ)りすぎるのは甘(あま)え<b>というものだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an ordinary opinion, offered as the speaker's own and easy to disagree with.

(b) names the behaviour - <code>甘(あま)え</code>, leaning on others - and
presents that name as the obvious one. It is heavier, more moralising, and lands as
a judgement on whoever is doing it rather than as a general remark. Said to
someone's face it is a telling-off; softened to
<code>甘(あま)えというものではないか</code> it becomes a point offered for
agreement.

</details>

# ～ふうに

**Meaning** · in this / that / what way; in the style of ～ - names the *manner* in
which something is done or made

<code>ふう</code> is the noun 風(ふう), "manner, style, air". Attached to a
demonstrative it gives you the everyday way of pointing at a manner -
<code>こんなふうに</code>, "like this". Attached to a noun it gives you "in the
style of" - <code>昔(むかし)ふうの家(いえ)</code>, a house done in the old
manner.

## Formation

<code>ふう</code> is a noun, so it takes `に` to modify a verb, `の` or `な` to
modify a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| こんな・そんな・あんな・どんな | ～な + ふうに | こんな → こんなふうに書(か)く |
| Noun (adverbial) | Noun + ふうに | ヨーロッパ → ヨーロッパふうに飾(かざ)る |
| Noun (before another noun) | Noun + ふうの + Noun | 昔(むかし) → 昔(むかし)ふうの建物(たてもの) |
| Noun (before another noun) | Noun + ふうな + Noun | 大人(おとな) → 大人(おとな)ふうな話(はな)し方(かた) |
| Quoted clause | clause + という + ふうに | 嫌(いや)だ → 嫌(いや)だというふうに |
| Written in kanji | 風(ふう) | こんなふうに → こんな風(ふう)に |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ふうの</code> and <code>ふうな</code> are both used before a noun,
with <code>ふうの</code> the commoner and more neutral of the two.
<code>ふうな</code> leans slightly towards "having the air of", which is why it
turns up so often with words about people's manner.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a few noun + 風(ふう) combinations have hardened into single words and
are read as compounds: 和風(わふう), 洋風(ようふう), 欧風(おうふう). Those
are vocabulary, not this pattern, and they are always written in kanji. The
productive pattern - the one you can build new phrases with - is usually written in
kana.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
こんな<b>ふうに</b>書(か)いてください。
Please write it like this.
</pre>

<pre>
どんな<b>ふうに</b>使(つか)うんですか。
How exactly do you use it?
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)によく「そんな<b>ふうに</b>言(い)うものじゃない」と注意(ちゅうい)された。
My mother often told me off with "don't talk like that".
</pre>

<pre>
あんな<b>ふうに</b>堂々(どうどう)と話(はな)せるようになりたい。
I'd like to be able to speak with that kind of confidence.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は昔(むかし)<b>ふう</b>の言(い)い方(かた)をするので、時々(ときどき)意味(いみ)が分(わ)からない。
My grandmother uses old-fashioned turns of phrase, so sometimes I miss her meaning.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ材料(ざいりょう)でも、切(き)り方(かた)をこんな<b>ふうに</b>変(か)えるだけで見(み)た目(め)がずいぶん違(ちが)う。
Even with the same ingredients, cutting them this way makes them look completely different.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)にヨーロッパ<b>ふう</b>の建物(たてもの)が建(た)って、町(まち)の様子(ようす)が変(か)わった。
A European-style building went up by the station and changed the look of the town.
</pre>

<pre>
自分(じぶん)がどんな<b>ふうに</b>見(み)られているか、気(き)にしすぎない方(ほう)がいい。
You're better off not worrying too much about how you come across to other people.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral to slightly casual, and extremely common in speech. It is the word you
reach for when demonstrating something with your hands - <code>こんなふうに持(も)って</code> -
and when asking someone to describe a manner rather than a fact -
<code>どんなふうに感(かん)じましたか</code>. In polite conversation it is
entirely at home; in a formal written report it would usually be replaced by
<code>このように</code>.

The demonstrative forms follow the ordinary こ・そ・あ・ど logic. <code>こんなふうに</code>
points at something in front of you or at what you are about to demonstrate;
<code>そんなふうに</code> points at what the other person just did or said, and is
therefore the one that turns up in complaints -
<code>そんなふうに言(い)わなくてもいいでしょう</code>; <code>あんなふうに</code>
points at an absent third party, often admiringly.

The noun-plus-<code>ふう</code> use is a different register again. It is the
vocabulary of design, cooking and description -
<code>中華(ちゅうか)ふうのスープ</code>, <code>昭和(しょうわ)ふうの喫茶店(きっさてん)</code> -
and it makes no claim that the thing is authentic. That is precisely its value: it
means "done in the manner of", with the implication that it is not the real article.

One small caution. Applied to a person, <code>学者(がくしゃ)ふうの男(おとこ)</code>
means someone who *looks* like an academic, not someone who is one. Used about a
real professional it can sound faintly dismissive.

## Contrast

### ～ふうに vs ～ように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ように</code> compares to a **model**: like that thing.
<code>ふうに</code> names a **manner**: in that style. Where the two overlap,
<code>ように</code> is the more formal and the more literal.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)が書(か)いた<b>ように</b>書(か)いてください。
Please write it the way the teacher wrote it.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)はこんな<b>ふうに</b>書(か)いていました。
The teacher wrote it something like this.
</pre>

The first sentence points at a specific model and asks you to match it. The second
sketches a manner and leaves room for approximation, which is the flavour
<code>ふうに</code> always carries. That is also why <code>ふうに</code> cannot take
a plain clause in front of it the way <code>ように</code> can: you cannot say
❌<code>先生(せんせい)が書(か)いたふうに</code>. Give it a demonstrative, a
noun, or <code>という</code>.

### こんなふうに vs こう・このように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three registers of the same gesture. `こう` is terse,
<code>こんなふうに</code> is ordinary and conversational,
<code>このように</code> is formal and written.

</div>

<pre>
ここを<b>こう</b>押(お)すと開(あ)きます。
Press here like so and it opens.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>このように</b>、数字(すうじ)は三年(さんねん)続(つづ)けて下(さ)がっている。
As you can see, the figures have fallen three years running.
</pre>

`こう` is a single adverb and needs a gesture or an obvious context to complete it.
<code>このように</code> belongs on a slide or in a report and is the one to use when
summing up evidence. <code>こんなふうに</code> sits between them and is the safe
default in speech.

### ～ふうに vs ～方(かた)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>方(かた)</code> is a **noun**: the way of doing
something. <code>ふうに</code> is **adverbial**: doing it in that way.

</div>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)の書(か)き<b>方(かた)</b>を教(おし)えてください。
Please teach me how to write this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
この漢字(かんじ)はこんな<b>ふうに</b>書(か)きます。
This kanji is written like this.
</pre>

The first asks for the method as a thing you can be taught; the second demonstrates
it. They combine happily -
<code>書(か)き方(かた)をこんなふうに変(か)えてみた</code> - because they are
doing different jobs in the sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>この</b>ふうに書(か)きます。 → ✅ <b>こんな</b>ふうに書(か)きます。
  <code>ふう</code> is a common noun, and the demonstrative that modifies it is the
  な-adjective series こんな・そんな・あんな・どんな, not この・その・あの・どの.

- ❌ 楽(たの)しいふうに歌(うた)っていた。 → ✅ 楽(たの)し<b>そうに</b>歌(うた)っていた。
  An adjective cannot sit in front of <code>ふうに</code>. To say someone looks a
  certain way, use <code>そうに</code>.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が言(い)ったふうにやってみた。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が言(い)った<b>ように</b>やってみた。
  A bare clause needs <code>ように</code>. If you want <code>ふうに</code> after a
  clause, <code>という</code> has to intervene.

- ❌ ヨーロッパふう<b>に</b>建物(たてもの) → ✅ ヨーロッパふう<b>の</b>建物(たてもの)
  <code>に</code> modifies a verb; a noun needs <code>の</code> or <code>な</code>.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この機械(きかい)はどんな____動(うご)かすんですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

どんな<b>ふうに</b>動(うご)かすんですか

The question is about manner - by what method, in what way - and the questioning
member of the set is <code>どんなふうに</code>. Note the `に`, which is what lets
the phrase modify the verb.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 説明書(せつめいしょ)に書(か)いてある（ふうに・とおりに）組(く)み立(た)てた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

説明書(せつめいしょ)に書(か)いてある<b>とおりに</b>組(く)み立(た)てた。

A plain clause cannot be followed by <code>ふうに</code>. Beyond the grammar, the
meaning wants exactness - assembled *as the instructions say* - and
<code>とおりに</code> is the pattern for matching a model precisely, where
<code>ふうに</code> would suggest a rough approximation.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Don't look at me like that."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The manner being objected to is the listener's own, which is exactly the そ- slot.
<code>こんなふうに</code> would point at something the speaker is demonstrating, and
<code>あんなふうに</code> at someone else entirely.

</details>

和風(わふう) already contains 風(ふう); adding another is like saying

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は先生(せんせい)です。
(b) 彼(かれ)は先生(せんせい)<b>ふう</b>の男(おとこ)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states a fact about his job.

(b) states only how he strikes the observer: tweed jacket, careful speech, an air of
the classroom. Noun + <code>ふう</code> describes the *appearance* of belonging to a
category and quietly declines to say whether he really does. Said about a person who
genuinely is a teacher it would sound faintly dismissive, as though you were
describing a costume.

</details>

# ～というふうに

**Meaning** · in a manner that says ～; in the way of ～ - wraps a whole statement
up and presents it as the *manner* of an action

Bare <code>ふうに</code> needs a demonstrative or a noun in front of it. Put
<code>という</code> there and the slot opens up to a complete sentence: whatever
somebody's face, gesture or arrangement seems to *say*, you can now put it into
words and make it the manner. It also has a second job - marking one item of a
pattern as a sample of the rest.

## Formation

<code>という</code> quotes a plain-form clause, so everything in front behaves as it
would before an ordinary <code>という</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + というふうに | 終(お)わった → 終(お)わったというふうに |
| い-adjective | plain form + というふうに | 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいというふうに |
| な-adjective | ～だ + というふうに | 退屈(たいくつ)だ → 退屈(たいくつ)だというふうに |
| Noun | ～だ + というふうに | 冗談(じょうだん)だ → 冗談(じょうだん)だというふうに |
| Demonstrative | こう・そう・ああ・どう + いうふうに | そう → そういうふうに |
| Before a noun | ～というふうな + Noun | 不満(ふまん)だ → 不満(ふまん)だというふうな顔(かお) |
| Listing a pattern | A、B、というふうに | 月(げつ)は英語(えいご)、火(か)は数学(すうがく)、というふうに |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the demonstrative forms are built from `いう`, not from
<code>という</code>: そういうふうに, こういうふうに, どういうふうに. In fast speech
they contract to そういう<b>ふう</b> → そんなふう and even そんな, which is why the
same idea has so many shapes.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そう<b>いうふうに</b>考(かんが)えたことはなかった。
I'd never thought about it that way.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は興味(きょうみ)がない<b>というふうに</b>横(よこ)を向(む)いた。
He turned away as if to say he wasn't interested.
</pre>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)は「まあいいでしょう」<b>というふうに</b>軽(かる)くうなずいた。
The teacher gave a small nod that said "well, all right then".
</pre>

<pre>
月曜(げつよう)は英語(えいご)、火曜(かよう)は数学(すうがく)、<b>というふうに</b>毎日(まいにち)科目(かもく)が決(き)まっている。
Monday is English, Tuesday maths, and so on - there's a fixed subject for each day.
</pre>

<pre>
一年生(いちねんせい)は掃除(そうじ)、二年生(にねんせい)は受付(うけつけ)<b>というふうに</b>仕事(しごと)を分(わ)けた。
We split the work up - first-years on cleaning, second-years on reception.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は痛(いた)くもかゆくもない<b>というふうな</b>顔(かお)で注射(ちゅうしゃ)を受(う)けていた。
My grandfather took the injection with a look that said it bothered him not at all.
</pre>

<pre>
まず結論(けつろん)、次(つぎ)に理由(りゆう)、最後(さいご)に例(れい)、<b>というふうに</b>書(か)くと読(よ)みやすくなる。
Conclusion first, then reasons, then examples - write it that way and it reads much better.
</pre>

<pre>
課長(かちょう)は話(はなし)など聞(き)いていない<b>というふうに</b>パソコンの画面(がめん)を見(み)つめていた。
The section chief stared at his screen in a way that made clear he wasn't listening.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and considerably more common in speech than in formal writing,
where <code>といったふうに</code> or <code>のように</code> would often be preferred.
Nothing about it is stiff.

The two uses feel quite different in the mouth. The **manner-of-a-gesture** use is
descriptive and slightly novelistic: you are putting words to something wordless,
which is why it collects verbs of expression -
<code>うなずく</code>, <code>首(くび)を振(ふ)る</code>,
<code>横(よこ)を向(む)く</code>, <code>笑(わら)う</code> - and nouns like
<code>顔(かお)</code> and <code>目(め)つき</code>. It carries a mild hedge:
the speaker is interpreting, not reporting, and everyone understands that.

The **listing** use is practical and everywhere. Give one or two members of a
pattern, close with <code>というふうに</code>, and the listener extends the series
themselves. It is how people explain rotas, schedules, filing systems and recipes,
and it saves you from having to enumerate everything. In this use it is close to
`など` and `とか` but says something they do not: that the items form a *regular
pattern*, not just a sample.

There is one social use worth flagging.
<code>そういうふうに言(い)われても困(こま)ります</code> and
<code>そういうふうに考(かんが)えないでください</code> are standard, fairly gentle
ways of pushing back, because they object to the *manner* rather than to the person.
That indirectness is much of what makes the pattern useful.

## Contrast

### ～というふうに vs ～ふうに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - bare <code>ふうに</code> takes a **demonstrative or a noun**.
<code>というふうに</code> takes a **whole clause**. That is the entire difference.

</div>

<pre>
そんな<b>ふうに</b>見(み)ないでください。
Please don't look at me like that.
</pre>

<pre>
信(しん)じられない<b>というふうに</b>目(め)を丸(まる)くした。
Her eyes went wide as if to say she couldn't believe it.
</pre>

You cannot say ❌<code>信(しん)じられないふうに</code>, and you cannot say
❌<code>そんなというふうに</code>. <code>という</code> is the connector that lets a
sentence occupy a noun's slot, and <code>ふう</code> is a noun; once you see that,
the distribution is automatic.

### ～というふうに vs ～ように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a clause, <code>ように</code> says the action **resembles**
the clause. <code>というふうに</code> says the action **conveys** the clause, as
though it were speaking.

</div>

<pre>
分(わ)かった<b>ように</b>うなずいた。
He nodded as if he had understood.
</pre>

<pre>
分(わ)かった<b>というふうに</b>うなずいた。
He nodded to signal that he had understood.
</pre>

The first leans towards appearance and often towards doubt - he nodded, whether he
understood or not. The second treats the nod as a message and reports its content;
whether it was sincere is not the point of the sentence. The overlap is real and in
many sentences either would pass, but this is the direction the two lean.

<code>ように</code> is also far wider. It has purposes
(<code>忘(わす)れないように</code>) and comparisons
(<code>夢(ゆめ)のように</code>) that <code>というふうに</code> cannot touch.

### ～というふうに vs the bare quotative と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` reports **words that were actually said**.
<code>というふうに</code> reports **the sense of something wordless**, or hedges
words the speaker will not swear to.

</div>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は「もう帰(かえ)る」<b>と</b>言(い)った。
She said, "I'm going home now."
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)はもう帰(かえ)りたい<b>というふうに</b>時計(とけい)を見(み)た。
She looked at her watch in a way that said she wanted to go home.
</pre>

Nobody spoke in the second sentence, and that is the point. Note the consequence:
because <code>というふうに</code> hedges, using it with
<code>言(い)った</code> and a real quotation weakens the report - it suggests you
are paraphrasing. If you have the words, use `と`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 退屈(たいくつ)<b>というふうに</b>あくびをした。 → ✅ 退屈(たいくつ)<b>だというふうに</b>あくびをした。
  <code>という</code> quotes a *sentence*, so a な-adjective or noun needs its `だ`.
  Dropping it happens in speech, but here the `だ` is doing real work.

- ❌ こう<b>というふうに</b>やってみて。 → ✅ <b>こういうふうに</b>やってみて。
  With a demonstrative the connector is plain `いう`, not <code>という</code>. The
  set is こういう・そういう・ああいう・どういう.

- ❌ 彼女(かのじょ)は「行(い)きません」<b>というふうに</b>答(こた)えた。 → ✅ 彼女(かのじょ)は「行(い)きません」<b>と</b>答(こた)えた。
  When the exact words are quoted and a verb of speaking follows, the plain
  quotative `と` is what you want. <code>というふうに</code> would imply you were
  only approximating.

- ❌ 洗(あら)う、切(き)る、煮(に)る<b>というふうな</b>。 → ✅ 洗(あら)う、切(き)る、煮(に)る<b>というふうに</b>進(すす)めます。
  The listing use is adverbial and needs a verb to attach to. The
  <code>ふうな</code> form only works in front of a noun.

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)は何(なに)も聞(き)いていない____窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

何(なに)も聞(き)いていない<b>というふうに</b>窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ていた

A whole clause is being turned into the manner of the looking, which needs
<code>という</code> in front of <code>ふうに</code>. Nobody spoke; the gaze is what
carries the message.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 朝(あさ)は掃除(そうじ)、昼(ひる)は買(か)い物(もの)（というふうに・というものだ）一日(いちにち)の予定(よてい)が決(き)まっている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

朝(あさ)は掃除(そうじ)、昼(ひる)は買(か)い物(もの)<b>というふうに</b>一日(いちにち)の予定(よてい)が決(き)まっている。

Two items are given as samples of a regular pattern, and the sentence continues
afterwards - both point to the listing use of <code>というふうに</code>.
<code>というものだ</code> would end the sentence with a value judgement, which is
not what is happening here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She shrugged as if to say it had nothing to do with her."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)は自分(じぶん)には関係(かんけい)ない<b>というふうに</b>肩(かた)をすくめた。

The shrug is wordless, so its content has to be supplied as a quoted clause;
<code>という</code> is what lets that clause modify <code>ふうに</code>. Note the
plain form <code>関係(かんけい)ない</code> before <code>という</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)はそんなというふうに言(い)っていませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は<b>そういうふうに</b>言(い)っていませんでした。

そんな already modifies <code>ふう</code> by itself - そんなふうに - and it can
never be followed by <code>という</code>. The demonstrative that pairs with
<code>いうふうに</code> is そう.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は納得(なっとく)した<b>ように</b>帰(かえ)っていった。
(b) 彼(かれ)は納得(なっとく)した<b>というふうに</b>うなずいて帰(かえ)っていった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports how his leaving looked to the speaker, and leaves open - even hints -
that he may not really have been satisfied. <code>ように</code> after a past clause
often carries that shade of doubt.

(b) treats a specific gesture, the nod, as a message with content, and reports what
the message was. The focus moves from his inner state to the signal he gave; the
speaker is reading a gesture rather than guessing at a mind.

</details>

# ～かのようだ・～かのように

**Meaning** · as if; just as though - compares a real situation to something that is
plainly not the case

The `か` in the middle is the question particle, and it is doing real work:
<code>かのようだ</code> says "it is like *the question of whether* this were so".
That extra step is why the pattern always feels counterfactual. Plain
<code>ようだ</code> can mean "it seems", leaving the door open; <code>かのようだ</code>
shuts it. The thing compared to is not true, and both speaker and listener know it.

## Formation

<code>か</code> follows a plain-form clause, so nouns and な-adjectives need
<code>である</code> to become one.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + かのようだ | 見(み)た → 見(み)たかのようだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + かのようだ | 知(し)らない → 知(し)らないかのようだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + かのようだ | 苦(くる)しい → 苦(くる)しいかのようだ |
| な-adjective | ～である + かのようだ | 当然(とうぜん)だ → 当然(とうぜん)であるかのようだ |
| Noun | ～である + かのようだ | 夢(ゆめ) → 夢(ゆめ)であるかのようだ |
| Modifying a verb | ～かのように + verb | 忘(わす)れた → 忘(わす)れたかのように笑(わら)う |
| Modifying a noun | ～かのような + Noun | 春(はる)が来(き)た → 春(はる)が来(き)たかのような暖(あたた)かさ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `である` before <code>かのよう</code> is not optional in careful
writing. ❌<code>夢(ゆめ)だかのように</code> is wrong, and bare
<code>夢(ゆめ)かのように</code>, though heard, is not what an editor would let
through. Write <code>夢(ゆめ)であるかのように</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the literary variant is <code>かのごとく</code> / <code>かのごとき</code>,
which belongs to speeches, headlines and deliberately archaic prose. Recognise it;
it is not a form to produce at N2.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
夢(ゆめ)を見(み)ている<b>かのようだ</b>。
It's as though I were dreaming.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は何(なに)も知(し)らない<b>かのように</b>笑(わら)っていた。
He smiled as if he knew nothing about it.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)は時間(じかん)が止(と)まった<b>かのように</b>静(しず)かだった。
The room was as quiet as if time had stopped in it.
</pre>

<pre>
春(はる)が来(き)た<b>かのような</b>暖(あたた)かさで、上着(うわぎ)がいらなかった。
It was as warm as if spring had arrived, and I didn't need a jacket.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は昨日(きのう)のことなど忘(わす)れた<b>かのように</b>話(はな)しかけてきた。
She started chatting to me as though yesterday had never happened.
</pre>

<pre>
少年(しょうねん)は大人(おとな)である<b>かのような</b>話(はな)し方(かた)をする。
The boy talks as though he were a grown man.
</pre>

<pre>
その記事(きじ)は、事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)がすでに分(わ)かっている<b>かのように</b>書(か)かれていた。
The article was written as if the cause of the accident were already established.
</pre>

<pre>
外(そと)は真夜中(まよなか)である<b>かのように</b>暗(くら)く、時計(とけい)を見(み)ると正午(しょうご)だった。
Outside it was as dark as midnight, and when I looked at the clock it was noon.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a written pattern. It lives in novels, essays, newspaper features and
formal speeches, and it is the reason a description feels literary rather than
chatty. In ordinary conversation people say <code>みたいに</code>, or
<code>ように</code>, and a learner who deploys
<code>かのように</code> over dinner will sound as though they are narrating
themselves.

What it adds over the <code>ように</code> you already have is not meaning but
*commitment*. <code>ようだ</code> is famously double-jobbed: it does likeness and it
does conjecture, and a sentence like
<code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったようだ</code> means "it seems to have rained", not
"as if it had rained". <code>かのようだ</code> can only do likeness, so it is
unambiguous - and that unambiguity is why writers reach for it in the middle of a
description where a stray "seems" would be confusing.

Because the comparison is understood to be false, the pattern often carries a
critical edge when aimed at a person's behaviour.
<code>まるで自分(じぶん)が決(き)めたかのように話(はな)す</code> is not
neutral: it says he is claiming credit that is not his. Aimed at weather, silence,
light or landscape it is purely descriptive.

`まるで` is its natural partner and appears in front of it constantly. The two are
independent - each works alone - but together they are the standard shape:
<code>まるで～かのように</code>.

## Contrast

### ～かのようだ vs ～ようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ようだ</code> does two jobs, likeness **and** conjecture.
<code>かのようだ</code> does only likeness, and marks the likeness as untrue.

</div>

<pre>
誰(だれ)か来(き)た<b>ようだ</b>。
Somebody seems to have arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)か来(き)た<b>かのように</b>、犬(いぬ)が急(きゅう)に吠(ほ)え出(だ)した。
The dog suddenly started barking as though someone had arrived.
</pre>

In the first, somebody probably did arrive. In the second, nobody did. Swapping the
patterns reverses the meaning of the sentence, which makes this the one distinction
on the page you cannot afford to blur.

The corollary is that <code>かのようだ</code> can never be used for a guess. If you
are inferring from evidence - wet ground, a light left on, a closed shutter -
<code>ようだ</code>, `らしい` or <code>みたいだ</code> is the pattern, and
<code>かのようだ</code> would be nonsense.

### ～かのようだ vs ～みたいだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same comparison, opposite ends of the register scale.
<code>みたいだ</code> is casual speech; <code>かのようだ</code> is written prose.

</div>

<pre>
外(そと)、夜(よる)<b>みたい</b>に暗(くら)いね。
It's dark as night out there.
</pre>

<pre>
空(そら)は夜(よる)である<b>かのように</b>暗(くら)かった。
The sky was as dark as though it were night.
</pre>

The content is the same; only the clothes differ. Notice how the grammar follows the
register: <code>みたい</code> attaches straight to a noun, while
<code>かのよう</code> demands <code>である</code>, and the sentence around it moves
into plain written style to match. Mixing them -
❌<code>夜(よる)かのようだったよ</code> - is exactly the kind of register clash
that marks a sentence as learner Japanese.

### ～かのように vs ～ふりをする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ふりをする</code> says the person is **pretending on
purpose**. <code>かのように</code> is the **observer's simile** and claims nothing
about intention.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は寝(ね)た<b>ふりをして</b>いた。
He was pretending to be asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は眠(ねむ)っている<b>かのように</b>動(うご)かなかった。
He lay as still as if he were asleep.
</pre>

The first accuses him of an act. The second only describes how he looked - he may
genuinely have been unconscious, or dead, or simply very still. This is worth
holding onto, because English "as if he were asleep" and "pretending to be asleep"
are much closer in feel than the Japanese pair, and choosing
<code>ふりをする</code> where you meant a simile puts an accusation in the sentence
you did not intend.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 本物(ほんもの)<b>だかのように</b>光(ひか)っていた。 → ✅ 本物(ほんもの)<b>であるかのように</b>光(ひか)っていた。
  `か` follows a plain-form clause. A noun becomes one with <code>である</code>,
  never with `だ`.

- ❌ 財布(さいふ)を落(お)とした<b>かのようだ</b>。（meaning "I seem to have dropped my wallet"）→ ✅ 財布(さいふ)を落(お)とした<b>ようだ</b>。
  <code>かのようだ</code> cannot express a guess. Used here it would mean the wallet
  was not dropped and something merely resembles it having been.

- ❌ まるで子(こ)どもだ。 → ✅ まるで子(こ)ども<b>のようだ</b>。（or 子(こ)どもであるかのようだ）
  `まるで` announces a comparison and must be answered by a likeness marker. Left
  bare, the sentence asserts the impossible thing instead of comparing to it.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)のことを忘(わす)れた<b>かのように</b>してください。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)のことを忘(わす)れた<b>ことにして</b>ください。
  You cannot ask somebody to perform a simile. Requests about pretending need
  <code>ふりをする</code> or <code>ことにする</code>.

The hall was full of people - that is what makes it a comparison with something

<code>である</code>.

</details>

The wet window is evidence, and the speaker is inferring what actually happened.

# ～げ

**Meaning** · with an air of ～; looking ～ - describes the outward look of a feeling
you can only see from outside

`げ` is an old suffix that turns a word about an inner state into a word about its
visible surface. <code>寂(さび)しい</code> is how a person feels;
<code>寂(さび)しげ</code> is how they look to somebody watching. The difference
matters in Japanese, which does not let you announce another person's feelings as
fact.

## Formation

`げ` produces a な-adjective, which then behaves like any other.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | drop い + げ | 寂(さび)しい → 寂(さび)しげ |
| な-adjective | stem + げ | 得意(とくい)だ → 得意(とくい)げ |
| ある | あり + げ | 自信(じしん)がある → 自信(じしん)ありげ |
| Modifying a noun | ～げな + Noun | 不安(ふあん)げ → 不安(ふあん)げな顔(かお) |
| Modifying a verb | ～げに + verb | 楽(たの)しげ → 楽(たの)しげに歌(うた)う |
| As a noun | ～げ + が／も | 悲(かな)しげ → 悲(かな)しげもなく |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `げ` is not productive. It attaches to a smallish, fixed list of words
about feeling and sensation - 寂(さび)しげ, 悲(かな)しげ,
楽(たの)しげ, 嬉(うれ)しげ, 不安(ふあん)げ, 得意(とくい)げ,
満足(まんぞく)げ, 眠(ねむ)たげ, 恥(は)ずかしげ, 自信(じしん)ありげ,
意味(いみ)ありげ - and you cannot coin new ones freely. When in doubt, use
<code>そう</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a few `げ` words have frozen into fixed vocabulary and no longer follow
the pattern's logic: <code>大人(おとな)げない</code> ("childish"),
<code>何(なに)気(げ)ない</code> ("casual, offhand"),
<code>事(こと)もなげに</code> ("as though it were nothing"). Learn these as
words, not as constructions.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は寂(さび)し<b>げ</b>な顔(かお)をしていた。
My sister had a lonely look about her.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもたちが楽(たの)し<b>げ</b>に走(はし)っている。
The children are running about happily.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は自信(じしん)あり<b>げ</b>に手(て)を挙(あ)げた。
He raised his hand with an air of confidence.
</pre>

<pre>
先輩(せんぱい)は意味(いみ)あり<b>げ</b>な笑(え)みを浮(う)かべて、何(なに)も言(い)わなかった。
My senior gave a meaningful little smile and said nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)に勝(か)った息子(むすこ)は、得意(とくい)<b>げ</b>にメダルを見(み)せてくれた。
My son had won his match, and showed me the medal with obvious pride.
</pre>

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)の隅(すみ)で、一人(ひとり)の女性(じょせい)が不安(ふあん)<b>げ</b>に時計(とけい)を見(み)ていた。
In the corner of the hall a woman was watching the clock anxiously.
</pre>

<pre>
新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)は名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)ばれると、恥(は)ずかし<b>げ</b>に立(た)ち上(あ)がった。
When his name was called the new employee stood up, looking rather embarrassed.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は昔(むかし)の写真(しゃしん)を眺(なが)めながら、懐(なつ)かし<b>げ</b>に目(め)を細(ほそ)めた。
Gazing at the old photographs, my grandfather narrowed his eyes fondly.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`げ` is written and observational. It belongs to novels, essays, song lyrics and
careful description; in everyday speech <code>そう</code> does the same work and
sounds normal, while `げ` sounds a shade poetic. Nothing about it is impolite - it
simply raises the register of the sentence, which is why a spoken
<code>楽(たの)しげでしたね</code> can come across as gently literary rather than
stiff.

Two restrictions follow from what the suffix means. First, it is for **other
people**. Since it describes an appearance from outside, using it about yourself is
odd in the same way <code>寒(さむ)がっている</code> is odd about yourself: you do
not need to infer your own feelings. Second, it only handles **feelings**. There is
no ❌高(たか)げ or ❌赤(あか)げ, because a price and a colour have no inner
life to show on the surface.

There is also a quiet nuance of *restraint*. <code>そう</code> reports a plain
impression; `げ` suggests something showing through that the person has not stated -
a hint rather than a display. <code>悲(かな)しげ</code> is not a person crying;
it is a person not quite hiding it. That is a tendency rather than a rule, but it is
why `げ` is so at home in description-heavy writing, and why translators reach for
"with an air of".

## Contrast

### ～げ vs ～そう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>そう</code> is the everyday, unrestricted one. `げ` is
written, attaches only to feeling words, and hints rather than reports.

</div>

<pre>
その人(ひと)は眠(ねむ)<b>そう</b>な顔(かお)をしていた。
He looked sleepy.
</pre>

<pre>
その人(ひと)は眠(ねむ)た<b>げ</b>に目(め)をこすった。
He rubbed his eyes drowsily.
</pre>

Beyond register, one difference is decisive: <code>そうだ</code> can predict, and
`げ` cannot. <code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうだ</code> says rain is on the way;
there is no ❌降(ふ)りげ, because a suffix about visible feeling has nothing to
say about the weather's intentions. If your sentence is about what is *going to
happen*, `げ` is not an option at all.

<code>そう</code> also attaches to verb stems and to almost any adjective. `げ` has
its short list and stops there.

### ～げ vs ～がる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がる` makes a **verb**: the person keeps behaving as though they
feel it. `げ` makes an **adjective**: at this moment they look it.

</div>

<pre>
母(はは)は犬(いぬ)が死(し)んでから毎日(まいにち)悲(かな)し<b>がって</b>いた。
After the dog died my mother went about grieving day after day.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)は犬(いぬ)の写真(しゃしん)を悲(かな)し<b>げ</b>に見(み)ていた。
My mother was looking at the photograph of the dog with sadness in her face.
</pre>

The verb describes a stretch of behaviour and often implies the feeling was being
shown - fussing, complaining, weeping. The adjective describes one moment's
appearance and implies the opposite: something visible that was not being announced.

The two suffixes also take overlapping but different lists, so you cannot assume a
pair exists. <code>怖(こわ)がる</code> is everyday Japanese;
❌怖(こわ)しげ is not a word at all, and the natural partner there is
<code>怖(こわ)そうに</code>.

### ～げ vs ～っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `げ` reports a **feeling showing on the surface**. `っぽい`
reports a **resemblance or a tendency**, and often carries a shrug.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は子(こ)ども<b>っぽい</b>ところがある。
There's something childish about him.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は悲(かな)し<b>げ</b>な目(め)をしていた。
His eyes had a sad look to them.
</pre>

`っぽい` attaches happily to nouns, verbs and adjectives alike and is casual to the
point of being slangy; `げ` is none of those things. They collide only in sentences
about people's manner, and the test is whether an emotion is involved: if the word
in front names a feeling, `げ` is possible; if it names a type, `っぽい` is.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 私(わたし)は寂(さび)し<b>げ</b>だ。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は寂(さび)しい。
  `げ` describes how somebody looks from outside. About your own feelings you simply
  use the adjective - there is nothing to infer.

- ❌ 高(たか)<b>げ</b>な店(みせ) → ✅ 高(たか)<b>そう</b>な店(みせ)
  Only words about feeling and sensation take `げ`. For an impression about a thing,
  <code>そう</code> is the suffix.

- ❌ 悲(かな)しい<b>げ</b>な声(こえ) → ✅ 悲(かな)し<b>げ</b>な声(こえ)
  The い of the adjective is dropped before `げ`, exactly as before
  <code>そう</code> and `がる`.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>げ</b>だ。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>そう</b>だ。
  `げ` never attaches to a verb stem and never predicts. Appearance-of-a-coming-event
  is <code>そう</code>'s territory.

## Practice

**1.** 彼女(かのじょ)は返事(へんじ)をせず、____窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ていた。（寂(さび)しい）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

寂(さび)し<b>げ</b>に窓(まど)の外(そと)を見(み)ていた

The い is dropped and `げ` takes `に` to modify the verb. The sentence is describing
someone else from outside, in a narrative register - exactly where `げ` belongs.
<code>寂(さび)しそうに</code> would also be correct and would sound more
conversational.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 空(そら)が暗(くら)くなってきた。雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り（げだ・そうだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り<b>そうだ</b>。

Two things rule `げ` out at once: it cannot attach to a verb stem, and it cannot
express a prediction about what is going to happen. Only <code>そうだ</code> reads
the sky.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The little girl looked up at me with an anxious expression."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

女(おんな)の子(こ)は不安(ふあん)<b>げな</b>顔(かお)で私(わたし)を見上(みあ)げた。

<code>不安(ふあん)</code> is a な-adjective, so `げ` attaches straight to the
stem, and <code>げな</code> is the form that modifies
<code>顔(かお)</code>. "With an ～ expression" is one of the phrases `げ` was made
for.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)は忙(いそが)しげだから、私(わたし)も忙(いそが)しげです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)は忙(いそが)し<b>そう</b>だから、私(わたし)も忙(いそが)しいです。

Two problems. The second half describes the speaker's own state, where no
appearance-suffix belongs at all. And <code>忙(いそが)しげ</code> is a poor fit
even for the manager: busyness is a circumstance rather than an emotion showing on a
face, so <code>忙(いそが)しそう</code> is what a Japanese speaker would say.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は嬉(うれ)し<b>そうに</b>報告(ほうこく)した。
(b) 彼(かれ)は嬉(うれ)し<b>げに</b>報告(ほうこく)した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the ordinary sentence: he reported it and looked pleased. It would sit
comfortably in an email or in speech.

(b) says the same thing in a writer's voice. The register lifts - you would expect
it in a novel or a considered piece of prose - and `げ` adds a faint suggestion that
the pleasure was showing through rather than being displayed. The difference is one
of texture, not of fact, and in a conversation (b) would sound as though the speaker
were narrating the scene.

</details>

# なお

**Meaning** · furthermore, please note; still, even now - appends a supplementary
remark to something already complete

`なお` has one main modern job and two secondary ones. The main job is the notice
board's: the announcement has been made, and `なお` adds the small print. The others
are adverbial - "still, even now" and "still more so" - and both belong to careful
written Japanese.

## Usage

`なお` never changes form. Which of its senses you are looking at is decided by
where it sits and what it is next to.

**1. Adding a proviso (conjunction).** It opens a new sentence, always after the
main information, and is followed by a comma. This is the sense you will meet most,
because it is the fixed furniture of notices, minutes, invitations and official
email:

- 受付(うけつけ)は九時(くじ)からです。なお、上(うわ)履(ば)きをご持参(じさん)ください。
- 会場(かいじょう)が変更(へんこう)になりました。なお、時間(じかん)に変更(へんこう)はありません。

**2. Still; even now (adverb).** Usually with a time expression in front of it, and
very often in the fixed pairs <code>今(いま)なお</code> and
<code>今(いま)もなお</code>:

- 今(いま)なお議論(ぎろん)が続(つづ)いている。

**3. Still more; better still (adverb).** In front of an adjective or a comparison,
where English says "even":

- 説明(せつめい)を加(くわ)えれば、なおよい。
- <code>なおさら</code> and <code>なお一層(いっそう)</code> are the intensified
  forms and are commoner than bare `なお` in this sense.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>なお</b>、申(もう)し込(こ)みは来週(らいしゅう)の金曜日(きんようび)までです。
Please note that applications close next Friday.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)から十年(じゅうねん)たった今(いま)も<b>なお</b>、原因(げんいん)は分(わ)かっていない。
Ten years after the accident the cause is still unknown.
</pre>

<pre>
説明会(せつめいかい)は午後(ごご)三時(さんじ)に終(お)わります。<b>なお</b>、当日(とうじつ)は駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)をご利用(りよう)いただけません。
The briefing ends at three. Please also note that the car park will be unavailable on the day.
</pre>

<pre>
現状(げんじょう)でも十分(じゅうぶん)使(つか)えるが、図(ず)を加(くわ)えれば<b>なお</b>よい。
It is perfectly usable as it stands, but a diagram would make it better still.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Firmly written and formal. `なお` belongs to printed notices, company email, meeting
minutes, contracts and academic prose. It is not rude in speech - it simply sounds
as though you are reading aloud from a document, which is occasionally the effect a
speaker wants at the end of a formal announcement and almost never the effect anyone
wants in conversation. Among friends the same job is done by `あと`, `それと` or
`ちなみに`.

The proviso sense carries a structural signal that is worth internalising as a
reader: everything before `なお` is the message, and everything after it is
secondary. Japanese official writing relies on this heavily, and a reader who skims
past `なお` will miss exactly the sentence that says the deadline has moved. In your
own writing, the rule follows from the same logic - never put your main point after
`なお`, because you have just told the reader it is an afterthought.

The "still, even now" sense has a slightly elevated, almost elegiac feel and is
common in journalism and history writing:
<code>寺(てら)は今(いま)もなお同(おな)じ場所(ばしょ)に立(た)っている</code>.
The everyday word for "still" is `まだ`, and `なお` is not its substitute in speech.

## Contrast

### なお vs ちなみに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both append. `なお` adds a **condition or caveat the reader
needs**; `ちなみに` adds a **side fact the reader may enjoy**.

</div>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)は窓口(まどぐち)でお受(う)け取(と)りください。<b>なお</b>、印鑑(いんかん)が必要(ひつよう)です。
Collect the application form at the counter. Please note that you will need your seal.
</pre>

<pre>
この寺(てら)は江戸(えど)時代(じだい)の建物(たてもの)です。<b>ちなみに</b>、屋根(やね)は一度(いちど)も直(なお)していないそうです。
This temple dates from the Edo period. Incidentally, the roof has apparently never been repaired.
</pre>

Drop the `なお` sentence and somebody turns up without their seal; drop the
`ちなみに` sentence and nothing happens except that the conversation is a little less
interesting. `ちなみに` is also perfectly at home in speech, where `なお` is not - it
is the word to reach for when you want this move in conversation.

### なお vs また

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `また` adds a **second item of equal weight**. `なお` adds
something **subordinate** to what came before.

</div>

<pre>
会費(かいひ)は三千円(さんぜんえん)です。<b>また</b>、懇親会(こんしんかい)は別途(べっと)二千円(にせんえん)かかります。
The fee is three thousand yen. There is also a separate two thousand for the reception.
</pre>

<pre>
会費(かいひ)は三千円(さんぜんえん)です。<b>なお</b>、お釣(つ)りのないようご用意(ようい)ください。
The fee is three thousand yen. Please bring the exact amount if you can.
</pre>

The `また` sentence introduces a second charge - information of the same order as the
first. The `なお` sentence introduces a request about how to pay one, which only
exists because the first sentence does. Both words are equally formal; the choice is
purely about rank.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>なお</b>、明日(あした)遅(おく)れるかも。（text message to a friend）→ ✅ <b>あと</b>、明日(あした)遅(おく)れるかも。
  `なお` is document language. In a message to a friend it reads as though you had
  pasted in a paragraph from a company notice.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)の資料(しりょう)を送(おく)ります。<b>なお</b>、会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。資料(しりょう)は参考(さんこう)までにお送(おく)りします。
  Putting the important news after `なお` tells the reader to treat it as a footnote,
  and some of them will do exactly that. The main point goes first.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が<b>なお</b>降(ふ)っている。（spoken）→ ✅ 雨(あめ)が<b>まだ</b>降(ふ)っている。
  In the "still" sense `なお` is written and normally anchored by
  <code>今(いま)</code> or <code>今(いま)も</code>. For an ordinary spoken
  observation, `まだ` is the word.

## Practice

**1.** 次回(じかい)の練習(れんしゅう)は土曜日(どようび)です。____、体育館(たいいくかん)は使(つか)えませんので公園(こうえん)に集合(しゅうごう)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>なお</b>、体育館(たいいくかん)は使(つか)えませんので…

The main announcement - the date - has already been made, and what follows is a
condition attached to it. That subordinate relationship, plus the polite written
register of the whole notice, is exactly `なお`'s territory.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この辞書(じしょ)は百年(ひゃくねん)前(まえ)のものです。（なお・ちなみに）、今(いま)でも売(う)っているそうですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ちなみに</b>、今(いま)でも売(う)っているそうですよ。

The added remark is a piece of trivia offered in conversation - note the `そうですよ`
ending - and nothing depends on it. `なお` would clash with the spoken register and
would also promise a caveat the sentence does not deliver.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 受付(うけつけ)は五時(ごじ)までです。<b>また</b>、土曜日(どようび)も営業(えいぎょう)しております。
(b) 受付(うけつけ)は五時(ごじ)までです。<b>なお</b>、受付(うけつけ)終了(しゅうりょう)後(ご)のお問(と)い合(あ)わせはメールでお願(ねが)いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents Saturday opening as a second, independent fact of the same weight -
two things the reader should know about when the desk is open.

(b) presents the email instruction as depending on the first sentence: it only
matters *because* the desk closes at five. `なお` grades the information, telling the
reader "this is the follow-up detail", and swapping in `また` would oddly promote a
fallback procedure to equal billing with the opening hours.

</details>

# そういえば

**Meaning** · come to think of it; that reminds me - flags that what somebody just
said has jogged something loose in your memory

Literally "if one says that", `そういえば` credits the previous remark with the
thought that follows. It is the conversational marker for an association: not a new
topic dropped in from nowhere, but one the last few seconds of talk pulled up.

## Usage

`そういえば` opens a sentence and never changes form. The `そう` is the ordinary
そ-series demonstrative pointing at what was just said, and the shape is the
conditional of <code>言(い)う</code>, so it is occasionally written
<code>そう言(い)えば</code>.

What follows it is usually one of three things:

- **a question** - そういえば、あの件(けん)はどうなりましたか。
- **a remembered fact**, very often with `ね` or `な` - そういえば、今日(きょう)は燃(も)えるごみの日(ひ)だったな。
- **a half-remembered one**, with `っけ` or `かな` - そういえば、鍵(かぎ)は閉(し)めたっけ。

There is a longer variant, <code>そういえば話(はなし)は変(か)わりますが</code>,
used when the association is loose enough that you want to apologise for it, and a
polite fixed opener <code>そういえば、先日(せんじつ)は…</code> for raising
something from a previous meeting.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>そういえば</b>、田中(たなか)さんは来(こ)ないの?
Come to think of it, isn't Tanaka coming?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>そういえば</b>、傘(かさ)を会社(かいしゃ)に忘(わす)れてきた。
That reminds me - I left my umbrella at the office.
</pre>

<pre>
京都(きょうと)の話(はなし)が出(で)たが、<b>そういえば</b>去年(きょねん)一緒(いっしょ)に行(い)った店(みせ)はまだあるだろうか。
Kyoto came up, and it makes me wonder whether that place we went to last year is still there.
</pre>

<pre>
「来週(らいしゅう)から新(あたら)しい方(かた)が入(はい)ります」「<b>そういえば</b>、面接(めんせつ)にいらしていた方(かた)ですか」
"Someone new starts next week." "Ah - would that be the person who came for the interview?"
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Conversational and friendly, and usable at any politeness level: the word itself
does not change, and the sentence after it carries the register. With `ます` and
`です` around it, `そういえば` is perfectly acceptable with a manager or a client -
<code>そういえば、資料(しりょう)の件(けん)はいかがでしょうか</code> - and it is
a genuinely useful way to raise a small item without making a production of it.

Its social value is that it shares the credit. By saying "what you just said
reminded me", you make your new remark a continuation of theirs rather than an
interruption, which is why it softens what would otherwise be a change of subject.
Speakers use it that way slightly dishonestly all the time, attaching it to
something they had been meaning to bring up anyway.

The limit is that the association has to be *findable*. If the listener cannot see
any connection at all between the previous line and yours, `そういえば` misfires and
sounds either absent-minded or evasive. When there is genuinely no link, `ところで`
is the honest word.

In writing it appears in essays, blogs and letters - anywhere that imitates a
speaking voice - and not in reports or formal documents.

## Contrast

### そういえば vs そうだ・そうそう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そういえば` means **you reminded me**. `そうだ` and `そうそう`
mean **I've just remembered**, with or without any prompt.

</div>

<pre>
<b>そういえば</b>、来月(らいげつ)引(ひ)っ越(こ)すって言(い)ってたよね。
Come to think of it, you said you were moving next month, didn't you.
</pre>

<pre>
あ、<b>そうだ</b>、牛乳(ぎゅうにゅう)買(か)わなきゃ。
Oh - that's right, I need to buy milk.
</pre>

The milk has nothing to do with anything anybody said; it simply surfaced. `そうだ`
and `そうそう` therefore work in silence, walking down the street alone, where
`そういえば` would have nothing to point at. Conversely, when the trigger *was* the
other person's remark, `そうだ` throws away the connection and can sound as though
you had stopped listening.

<code>そうそう</code> has a second job worth keeping separate: as a response it means
"yes, exactly", agreeing enthusiastically with what was just said.

### そういえば vs ところで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そういえば` follows a **thread**. `ところで` cuts one and
starts a new one.

</div>

<pre>
<b>そういえば</b>、その映画(えいが)の監督(かんとく)、新(あたら)しい作品(さくひん)を撮(と)ったそうですよ。
Speaking of which, I hear that film's director has made something new.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>ところで</b>、来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)の件(けん)なんですが。
Anyway - about next week's meeting.
</pre>

`ところで` announces the break openly, which is honest and slightly abrupt; it is the
right word when you need to move business along. `そういえば` hides the break inside
an association, which is gentler but only works if the association exists. Using
`そういえば` for a genuinely unrelated topic is a small, transparent piece of theatre
and native speakers notice it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そういえば、はじめまして。山田(やまだ)と申(もう)します。 → ✅ はじめまして。山田(やまだ)と申(もう)します。
  Nothing has been said yet, so there is nothing for `そういえば` to point back to.
  It cannot open a conversation.

- ❌ そういえば、報告書(ほうこくしょ)の締(し)め切(き)りは明日(あした)です。（as the main point of an email）→ ✅ 報告書(ほうこくしょ)の締(し)め切(き)りは明日(あした)です。
  `そういえば` frames what follows as an incidental thought. Attached to the actual
  purpose of the message it reads as either careless or passive-aggressive.

- ❌ そういえた、明日(あした)は休(やす)みだったね。 → ✅ <b>そういえば</b>、明日(あした)は休(やす)みだったね。
  It is a frozen phrase. The <code>言(い)えば</code> inside it never becomes
  <code>言(い)えた</code> or <code>言(い)います</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 「北海道(ほっかいどう)の雪(ゆき)はすごいですよ」「____、去年(きょねん)札幌(さっぽろ)に行(い)ったとき大変(たいへん)でした」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>そういえば</b>、去年(きょねん)札幌(さっぽろ)に行(い)ったとき大変(たいへん)でした。

The other speaker's remark about Hokkaido snow is what pulled the memory up, and the
reply builds on it rather than replacing it. Note that the polite ending is
unaffected: `そういえば` sits comfortably in front of a です・ます sentence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （そういえば・ところで）、話(はなし)は変(か)わりますが、来月(らいげつ)の予算(よさん)の件(けん)はどうなりましたか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ところで</b>、話(はなし)は変(か)わりますが…

The speaker openly announces a change of subject, and the budget has nothing to do
with what came before. `そういえば` would claim an association that the sentence
itself denies two words later.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 「明日(あした)は雨(あめ)らしいよ」「<b>そういえば</b>、傘(かさ)を直(なお)してもらわないと」
(b) 「明日(あした)は雨(あめ)らしいよ」「あ、<b>そうだ</b>、傘(かさ)を直(なお)してもらわないと」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the umbrella as something the rain forecast brought to mind. The two
remarks are linked, and the speaker is visibly following the other person's lead.

(b) presents it as a sudden recollection of the speaker's own. It may well have been
triggered by the same remark, but `そうだ` does not say so, and the effect is a
slightly more self-absorbed turn - the speaker jumping to their own errand rather
than continuing the conversation. Neither is rude; (a) is the more sociable of the
two.

</details>

# ～というか～というか

**Meaning** · I'm not sure whether to call it A or B - offers two labels for one
thing and declines to choose between them

The `か` is the question particle attached to <code>という</code>: "shall we call it
A? shall we call it B?" Paired up, the two halves describe a single thing from two
angles and leave both standing. The speaker is not undecided about the *facts* -
they are undecided about the *word*.

## Formation

Both slots take the same material, and it is whatever <code>という</code> normally
quotes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というか | 趣味(しゅみ) → 趣味(しゅみ)というか |
| な-adjective | stem + というか | 正直(しょうじき) → 正直(しょうじき)というか |
| い-adjective | plain form + というか | 優(やさ)しい → 優(やさ)しいというか |
| Verb | plain form + というか | 飽(あ)きた → 飽(あ)きたというか |
| Paired | A というか B というか、… | 嬉(うれ)しい／恥(は)ずかしい → 嬉(うれ)しいというか恥(は)ずかしいというか |
| Single, as a hedge | A というか、B | 嫌(きら)い → 嫌(きら)いというか苦手(にがて)なんです |
| Casual contraction | ～ていうか／～っていうか／～ってか | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いっていうか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - な-adjectives and nouns normally drop the `だ` here:
<code>正直(しょうじき)というか</code>, not
<code>正直(しょうじき)だというか</code>. The `だ` version is the ordinary quoting
<code>という</code> and means something else.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pair almost always has a tail. Either a summary adverb such as
`とにかく` or `まあ`, or a phrase that gathers both halves up:
<code>…というか…というか、複雑(ふくざつ)な気持(きも)ちだ</code>. Learning that
tail is part of learning the pattern; a bare pair with nothing after it sounds
unfinished.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
嫌(きら)い<b>というか</b>、苦手(にがて)なんです。
It's not that I dislike it - I'm just no good with it.
</pre>

<pre>
嬉(うれ)しい<b>というか</b>、恥(は)ずかしい<b>というか</b>、複雑(ふくざつ)な気持(きも)ちだ。
Pleased, or embarrassed, or something - it's a complicated feeling.
</pre>

<pre>
優(やさ)しい<b>というか</b>、人(ひと)がいい<b>というか</b>、断(ことわ)れない性格(せいかく)なんだ。
Kind, or too nice, or whatever you want to call it - he just can't say no.
</pre>

<pre>
あの店(みせ)は静(しず)か<b>というか</b>、暗(くら)い<b>というか</b>、一人(ひとり)で行(い)くには少(すこ)し寂(さび)しい。
That place is quiet, or gloomy, or both - a bit lonely to go to on your own.
</pre>

<pre>
感動(かんどう)した<b>というか</b>、あきれた<b>というか</b>、見(み)終(お)わってしばらく席(せき)を立(た)てなかった。
Moved, or dumbfounded - either way I couldn't get out of my seat for a while after it ended.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)は厳(きび)しい<b>というか</b>、細(こま)かい<b>というか</b>、とにかく一緒(いっしょ)に仕事(しごと)をすると疲(つか)れる。
My boss is strict, or fussy, or something - working with him is exhausting either way.
</pre>

<pre>
引(ひ)っ越(こ)してきたばかりで、不安(ふあん)<b>というか</b>、わくわくする<b>というか</b>、自分(じぶん)でもよく分(わ)からない。
I've only just moved here, and I'm nervous, or excited, or - honestly, I couldn't tell you.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の発言(はつげん)は正直(しょうじき)<b>というか</b>無神経(むしんけい)<b>というか</b>、会議(かいぎ)が一瞬(いっしゅん)静(しず)まった。
His remark was honest, or tactless, depending how you look at it - the meeting went silent for a moment.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is spoken Japanese. It appears in writing only where writing imitates speech -
blogs, essays, dialogue in fiction, chat messages - and a formal report that used it
would sound as though nobody had proofread it. Within speech it works at any
politeness level, since the ending carries the register:
<code>正直(しょうじき)というか、失礼(しつれい)というか、驚(おどろ)きました</code>
is perfectly acceptable to a superior.

What the pattern buys you is **hedging**. By offering two words and choosing
neither, you avoid committing to the harsher one. That makes it the standard way to
say something faintly critical without quite saying it -
<code>元気(げんき)というか、うるさいというか</code> - and the standard way to
describe a feeling too mixed for one adjective. Overdone, it starts to sound
evasive; three or four <code>というか</code>s in a row is a recognisable verbal tic.

The contracted forms deserve attention because you will hear them constantly.
<code>ていうか</code> and <code>っていうか</code> are the ordinary casual shapes, and
<code>ってか</code> is the fastest. Beware that
<code>ていうか</code> has also drifted into a sentence-opener that means roughly
"actually, forget that -" and yanks the conversation somewhere else:
<code>ていうか、もう時間(じかん)ないよ</code>. That use is fine among friends and
distinctly blunt with anyone senior, so keep it out of your polite speech even
though the polite <code>というか</code> is safe.

Used alone rather than in a pair, <code>というか</code> is a self-correction: you say
one word, hear that it is not quite right, and immediately offer a better one. That
single use is far commoner than the paired one in real conversation.

## Contrast

### ～というか～というか vs ～というより

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>というより</code> **rejects** the first label in favour of
the second. <code>というか…というか</code> keeps **both** on the table.

</div>

<pre>
寒(さむ)い<b>というより</b>痛(いた)い。
It's not so much cold as painful.
</pre>

<pre>
寒(さむ)い<b>というか</b>痛(いた)い<b>というか</b>、外(そと)に立(た)っていられなかった。
Cold, or painful, or something between - I couldn't stand outside in it.
</pre>

The first sentence makes a decision and the second refuses to. Where you want to
correct somebody - "it's not *cheap*, it's *shoddy*" - <code>というより</code> is the
pattern, and it is the sharper of the two. <code>というか</code> is what you use when
you would rather not be that sharp, which is most of the time.

### ～というか vs ～か～か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>AかBか</code> is uncertainty about the **facts**.
<code>AというかBというか</code> is uncertainty about the **wording**.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)来(く)るのは山田(やまだ)さん<b>か</b>中村(なかむら)さん<b>か</b>、まだ分(わ)かりません。
We don't yet know whether it's Yamada or Nakamura who's coming tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
山田(やまだ)さんは真面目(まじめ)<b>というか</b>頑固(がんこ)<b>というか</b>、一度(いちど)決(き)めたら変(か)えない。
Yamada is conscientious, or stubborn, or whatever - once he's decided, that's that.
</pre>

In the first sentence two different people are candidates for one slot, and finding
out which will settle it. In the second there is only one person, and no fact could
settle which adjective is right, because both are. Swapping the patterns produces
nonsense in either direction, which makes this the cleanest test on the page.

### ～というか vs ～とか～とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とか…とか</code> lists **several things**.
<code>というか…というか</code> gives several **names for one thing**.

</div>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)は掃除(そうじ)<b>とか</b>洗濯(せんたく)<b>とか</b>で終(お)わってしまう。
My days off get eaten up by cleaning and laundry and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
休(やす)みの日(ひ)は自由(じゆう)<b>というか</b>暇(ひま)すぎる<b>というか</b>、かえって落(お)ち着(つ)かない。
Days off are free time, or too much free time - I actually find them unsettling.
</pre>

Cleaning and laundry are two separate jobs; freedom and excessive idleness are one
situation described twice. If you could point at the items separately, you want
`とか`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)はパン<b>というか</b>ご飯(はん)<b>というか</b>、どちらにしますか。 → ✅ 昼(ひる)ご飯(はん)はパン<b>か</b>ご飯(はん)<b>か</b>、どちらにしますか。
  Bread and rice are two different lunches, and the question asks you to choose one.
  <code>というか</code> cannot present a genuine either-or.

- ❌ 兄(あに)<b>というか</b>弟(おとうと)<b>というか</b>、二人(ふたり)います。 → ✅ 兄(あに)と弟(おとうと)が一人(ひとり)ずついます。
  The two labels have to describe the **same** thing. Two separate people need `と`.

- ❌ 本件(ほんけん)は失敗(しっぱい)というか、想定外(そうていがい)の結果(けっか)というか… （in a written report）→ ✅ 本件(ほんけん)は想定外(そうていがい)の結果(けっか)であった。
  A report is supposed to commit. The pattern's hedging is a virtue in speech and a
  defect on paper.

## Practice

**1.** あの人(ひと)は自由(じゆう)____勝手(かって)____、周(まわ)りのことを考(かんが)えない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

自由(じゆう)<b>というか</b>勝手(かって)<b>というか</b>、周(まわ)りのことを考(かんが)えない。

Both words describe one person's behaviour, and the speaker is softening the harsher
of them by refusing to pick. Note that both are な-adjectives and both drop the `だ`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会場(かいじょう)は東京(とうきょう)（というか・か）大阪(おおさか)（というか・か）、まだ決(き)まっていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会場(かいじょう)は東京(とうきょう)<b>か</b>大阪(おおさか)<b>か</b>、まだ決(き)まっていない。

Two candidate cities, one venue, and the matter will be settled by a decision - that
is factual uncertainty, which takes plain `か`. <code>というか</code> would claim
Tokyo and Osaka were two names for the same place.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He's confident, or arrogant, or something - either way, nobody argues with him."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)は自信(じしん)がある<b>というか</b>、偉(えら)そう<b>というか</b>、とにかく誰(だれ)も逆(さか)らわない。

The English "or something - either way" is doing exactly what the Japanese pattern
does, and `とにかく` is the natural tail that gathers the two labels up and moves on.
Note that <code>偉(えら)そう</code> behaves as a な-adjective and drops the `だ`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 週末(しゅうまつ)は映画(えいが)というかドライブというか、両方(りょうほう)行(い)きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 週末(しゅうまつ)は映画(えいが)<b>とか</b>ドライブ<b>とか</b>、両方(りょうほう)行(い)きました。

<code>両方(りょうほう)</code> gives it away: these are two separate outings, not
two names for one. Listing several actual things is `とか`'s job.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼女(かのじょ)は正直(しょうじき)<b>というより</b>失礼(しつれい)だ。
(b) 彼女(かのじょ)は正直(しょうじき)<b>というか</b>失礼(しつれい)<b>というか</b>…

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) makes a judgement and states it: "honest" is the wrong word, "rude" is the right
one. It is a clear criticism and the listener will hear it as one.

(b) puts both words up and steps back from choosing. The criticism is still audible
- you do not reach for <code>失礼(しつれい)</code> by accident - but the speaker
has left themselves room to mean the kinder reading, and the trailing 「…」 invites
the listener to supply their own verdict. It is the politer and much commoner move.

</details>

# である・のである

**Meaning** · the written-style copula, and its explanatory form - what `だ` becomes
when Japanese is written to be read rather than heard

`である` is not a politer `だ`, and it is not a stiffer `です`. It is the copula of a
text that has no speaker: newspaper editorials, academic papers, laws, reports,
encyclopaedia entries, essays. `のである` is the same move applied to the
explanatory `んです` you learned at N5. No textbook syllabus insists on this page,
and every N1 reading passage is written in it.

## Formation

`である` is historically <code>で</code> plus <code>ある</code>, which is why it
conjugates like the verb <code>ある</code> and can do things `だ` cannot.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + である | 事実(じじつ)だ → 事実(じじつ)である |
| な-adjective | stem + である | 明(あき)らかだ → 明(あき)らかである |
| い-adjective | no copula at all | 高(たか)い → 高(たか)い（❌高(たか)いである）|
| Past | ～であった | 学生(がくせい)だった → 学生(がくせい)であった |
| Negative | ～ではない／ではなかった | 簡単(かんたん)だ → 簡単(かんたん)ではない |
| Conjecture | ～であろう | 事実(じじつ)だろう → 事実(じじつ)であろう |
| Joining clauses | ～であり、… | 教師(きょうし)だ → 教師(きょうし)であり、作家(さっか)でもある |
| Before a noun | ～である + Noun | 必要(ひつよう)だ → 必要(ひつよう)である理由(りゆう) |
| Explanatory | plain clause + のである | 変(か)わった → 変(か)わったのである |
| Explanatory after noun / な-adj | ～な + のである | 問題(もんだい)だ → 問題(もんだい)なのである |
| Explanatory, past | ～のであった | 知(し)らなかった → 知(し)らなかったのであった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - written Japanese comes in two styles, and you pick one and stay
in it for the whole document. <code>だ・である体(たい)</code> is the plain,
impersonal style of reports and essays; <code>です・ます体(たい)</code> is the
polite style of letters, email and anything addressed to a reader as a person.
Mixing them inside one text is the single most visible flaw in learner writing.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `である` cannot follow an い-adjective. The い-adjective already contains
its own predicate: <code>正(ただ)しい。</code> is a complete written sentence, and
❌<code>正(ただ)しいである</code> is not Japanese. The same goes for verbs.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは重要(じゅうよう)な問題(もんだい)<b>である</b>。
This is an important problem.
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)は明(あき)らか<b>である</b>。
The findings of the survey are clear.
</pre>

<pre>
人口(じんこう)は減(へ)り続(つづ)けている。これは統計(とうけい)が示(しめ)す事実(じじつ)<b>である</b>。
The population is continuing to fall. This is a fact the statistics bear out.
</pre>

<pre>
当時(とうじ)、この地域(ちいき)は漁業(ぎょぎょう)の町(まち)<b>であった</b>。
At that time this district was a fishing town.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は三時間(さんじかん)に及(およ)んだ。それだけ意見(いけん)が分(わ)かれていた<b>のである</b>。
The meeting ran to three hours. That is how divided opinion was.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は一度(いちど)も振(ふ)り返(かえ)らなかった。戻(もど)る気(き)はなかった<b>のである</b>。
He did not look back once. He had no intention of returning.
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)が進(すす)めば幸(しあわ)せになるとは限(かぎ)らない。問題(もんだい)はその使(つか)い方(かた)な<b>のである</b>。
Advancing technology does not necessarily bring happiness. The question is how it is used.
</pre>

<pre>
本(ほん)研究(けんきゅう)の目的(もくてき)は、両者(りょうしゃ)の関係(かんけい)を明(あき)らかにすること<b>である</b>。
The aim of this study is to clarify the relationship between the two.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Start with what `である` does to a text's *voice*. `だ` sounds like a person
asserting something - it has the flavour of a spoken judgement, and in conversation
it can even sound blunt. `です` sounds like a person addressing another person.
`である` sounds like neither, because it removes the speaker altogether. What is left
is the proposition, standing on its own. That impersonality is exactly what a
newspaper leader, a court judgment, a laboratory report and an academic paper all
want: the claim is presented as belonging to the evidence rather than to the author.

It follows that `である` is **not polite**. It is not impolite either - it sits
outside the politeness system. Writing <code>会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)である</code>
in an email to a colleague is not rude in the way `だ` would be; it is *strange*, in
the way that answering a friend's text in the register of a company annual report is
strange. Speak it aloud to somebody and the effect is comic.

Two shapes you should be able to recognise. <code>であり</code> is the connecting
form and is how written Japanese strings predicates together without a full stop:
<code>彼女(かのじょ)は研究者(けんきゅうしゃ)であり、教育者(きょういくしゃ)でもある</code>.
<code>であろう</code> is the written `だろう`, and in academic prose it is the
standard way to hedge a claim:
<code>影響(えいきょう)は一時的(いちじてき)であろう</code>. There is also
<code>であります</code>, which is `である` plus politeness; it survives in speeches,
in the language of the Diet and the old military, and in some western dialects, and
it sounds formal and slightly dated. Do not confuse it with
<code>でございます</code>, the maximally polite copula of shop and hotel staff -
that one is alive and everywhere.

Now `のである`. Everything you know about `んです` transfers: it presents a sentence
as an explanation, a background, or the point the writer has been driving at. The
spoken chain runs `のです` → `んです` → `んだ`; the written chain runs `のである` →
`のだ`. In an essay, `のである` is the sound of the author stepping forward for one
sentence to say "and *this* is what it comes to". Used well, it is the punctuation
of an argument. Used every third sentence, it becomes insistent and even preachy -
Japanese style guides warn about this specifically, and it is the commonest fault in
non-native academic writing. Write the sentence plainly first; add the `の` only if
the sentence is genuinely explaining something.

The practical advice for a learner is simple. **Recognise `である` everywhere** - you
cannot read a Japanese newspaper, textbook or novel without it. **Produce it only in
essays, reports and academic writing**, and when you do, keep the whole document in
that style. Never speak it, and never put it in a message to a person.

## Contrast

### である vs だ・です

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `です` addresses a **reader**. `だ` is a **speaker's** assertion.
`である` has no speaker at all.

</div>

<pre>
水質(すいしつ)の改善(かいぜん)が必要(ひつよう)<b>です</b>。
Improving water quality is necessary.
</pre>

<pre>
水質(すいしつ)の改善(かいぜん)が必要(ひつよう)<b>である</b>。
Improvement of water quality is necessary.
</pre>

Nothing about the second is politer - if anything it is less accommodating, because
it has stopped talking to you. It is what you would write in the conclusion of a
report; the first is what you would write in the covering email that attaches it.

The choice also drags the rest of the sentence with it. A `である` text prefers
`しかし` to `でも`, <code>行(おこな)う</code> to `やる`, `ない` to `ません`, and it
does not use `ね` or `よ` at all. Register is a whole outfit, not one garment.

### のである vs んです・のだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical grammar, identical job, different clothes. `のである`
is the written twin of the `んです` you have had since N5.

</div>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は休(やす)みました。熱(ねつ)があった<b>んです</b>。
I was off yesterday. I had a temperature.
</pre>

<pre>
計画(けいかく)は中止(ちゅうし)された。予算(よさん)が尽(つ)きた<b>のである</b>。
The plan was abandoned. The budget had run out.
</pre>

That is the honest summary: this half of the page adds no new meaning to what you
already know, only a new register. What is worth learning is the shape after a noun
or a な-adjective, where `の` needs a `な` in front of it -
<code>問題(もんだい)なのである</code>, <code>明(あき)らかなのである</code> -
exactly as in <code>問題(もんだい)なんです</code>.

Note also the intermediate form `のだ`, which is `のである` with the copula
shortened. It is common in essays and columns and slightly less formal than
`のである`; both belong to the same written style.

### である vs のである

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `である` states. `のである` states **and claims that this
explains something**.

</div>

<pre>
原因(げんいん)は管理(かんり)の不備(ふび)<b>である</b>。
The cause is inadequate supervision.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)は偶然(ぐうぜん)ではない。原因(げんいん)は管理(かんり)の不備(ふび)な<b>のである</b>。
The accident was no accident. The cause was inadequate supervision.
</pre>

The first sentence could open a paragraph; the second could only close one, because
the `の` points back at something already on the table and says "here is what was
behind it." Ask yourself whether the sentence answers a question the previous
sentences raised. If it does not, delete the `の` - and most of the time it does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 結果(けっか)は明(あき)らかである。今後(こんご)の課題(かだい)を考(かんが)えたいと思(おも)います。 → ✅ …明(あき)らかである。今後(こんご)の課題(かだい)を考(かんが)えたい。
  One style per document. Sliding into です・ます in the middle of a
  だ・である essay is the most visible flaw in learner writing, and graders look for
  it.

- ❌ この方法(ほうほう)は新(あたら)し<b>いである</b>。 → ✅ この方法(ほうほう)は新(あたら)し<b>い</b>。
  An い-adjective is already a predicate. `である` follows nouns and な-adjective
  stems only.

- ❌ 静(しず)か<b>だである</b>。 → ✅ 静(しず)か<b>である</b>。
  `である` replaces `だ`; it does not stack on top of it.

- ❌ 明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)である。（in an email to a colleague）→ ✅ 明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)です。
  Email addresses a person, so it needs a copula that acknowledges one. `である`
  reads as though a notice had been machine-generated.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>だのである</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は留学生(りゅうがくせい)<b>なのである</b>。
  Before `の`, a noun or な-adjective takes `な` - the same rule as
  <code>留学生(りゅうがくせい)なんです</code>.

## Practice

**1.** 本論文(ほんろんぶん)の目的(もくてき)は、両国(りょうこく)の制度(せいど)を比較(ひかく)すること____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

比較(ひかく)すること<b>である</b>。

<code>本論文(ほんろんぶん)</code> announces an academic paper, and academic papers
are written in だ・である style throughout. <code>こと</code> is a noun, so the
copula attaches directly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)は複雑(ふくざつ)（だである・である・ですである）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)は複雑(ふくざつ)<b>である</b>。

<code>複雑(ふくざつ)</code> is a な-adjective, and `である` attaches to the bare
stem. The other two options try to stack copulas: `である` is itself the copula and
replaces `だ` or `です` rather than following one.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in written essay style: "The problem is not the technology. It is how we choose to use it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

問題(もんだい)は技術(ぎじゅつ)そのものではない。それをどう使(つか)うかな<b>のである</b>。

The first sentence states, and the second explains what the first was pointing at -
which is precisely when `の` earns its place. Note the `な` before it, required
because what precedes is a nominalised phrase functioning as a noun.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 以上(いじょう)のことから、この方法(ほうほう)は有効(ゆうこう)だと言(い)えるのであります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 以上(いじょう)のことから、この方法(ほうほう)は有効(ゆうこう)だと言(い)える<b>のである</b>。

<code>のであります</code> is `のである` with politeness added, and it belongs to
speeches rather than to written argument - in a paper it sounds like a politician at
a podium. In a report or essay the plain <code>のである</code> is the form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この制度(せいど)は三十年(さんじゅうねん)前(まえ)に作(つく)られた。
(b) この制度(せいど)は三十年(さんじゅうねん)前(まえ)に作(つく)られた<b>のである</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports the date. It could sit anywhere in a paragraph, including at the
start.

(b) offers the date as the explanation of something the reader has just been shown -
why the rules fit badly, why the wording is odd, why nobody remembers the reasoning.
It looks backwards, and dropped into a paragraph with nothing behind it, it leaves
the reader hunting for the point it was supposed to be explaining. That is the test
for every `のである` you write.

</details>
