# ～に限る

**Meaning** · nothing beats ～; ～ is the best thing there is - and, in notices, "restricted to ～"

<code>限(かぎ)る</code> means "to limit". <code>～に限(かぎ)る</code> therefore says
*the good options here narrow down to exactly one*. In everyday speech that comes out
as a verdict - "for a cold, sleep, full stop." In signs and rules the same word stays
literal: the eligible people narrow down to exactly one group.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に限(かぎ)る | 温泉(おんせん) → 温泉(おんせん)に限(かぎ)る |
| Verb | dictionary form + に限(かぎ)る | 寝(ね)る → 寝(ね)るに限(かぎ)る |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + に限(かぎ)る | 無理(むり)をしない → 無理(むり)をしないに限(かぎ)る |
| Polite | … + に限(かぎ)ります | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)むに限(かぎ)ります |
| Rule / notice sense | Noun + に限(かぎ)る・に限(かぎ)り | 会員(かいいん) → 会員(かいいん)に限(かぎ)ります |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>～に限(かぎ)る</code> does not attach to adjectives. For
<code>安(やす)い</code> or <code>静(しず)かだ</code> you want
<code>～のが一番(いちばん)だ</code> or <code>～に越(こ)したことはない</code> instead.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
夏(なつ)はやっぱり冷(つめ)たいそば<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
In summer there's nothing like cold soba.
</pre>

<pre>
疲(つか)れた日(ひ)は早(はや)く寝(ね)る<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
On a day when you're worn out, the best thing is an early night.
</pre>

<pre>
風邪(かぜ)のときは薬(くすり)より休(やす)む<b>に限(かぎ)ります</b>ね。
With a cold, rest beats medicine, doesn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
こういう話(はなし)は本人(ほんにん)に直接(ちょくせつ)聞(き)く<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
For something like this, you're best off asking the person directly.
</pre>

<pre>
行列(ぎょうれつ)が苦手(にがて)な人(ひと)は、開店(かいてん)直後(ちょくご)に行(い)く<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
If you hate queueing, the thing to do is go right after they open.
</pre>

<pre>
このイベントの参加(さんか)は中学生(ちゅうがくせい)以下(いか)<b>に限(かぎ)ります</b>。
Participation in this event is restricted to junior-high age and under.
</pre>

<pre>
話(はなし)がこじれる前(まえ)に、自分(じぶん)から謝(あやま)っておく<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
Before things get tangled, the smart move is to apologise first yourself.
</pre>

<pre>
寒(さむ)い朝(あさ)に外(そと)へ出(で)たくないなら、前(まえ)の晩(ばん)のうちに買(か)い物(もの)を済(す)ませておく<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
If you don't want to go out on a cold morning, the answer is to get the shopping done the night before.
</pre>

<pre>
眠(ねむ)れない夜(よる)は、無理(むり)に目(め)を閉(と)じているより、一度(いちど)起(お)きて温(あたた)かいものを飲(の)む<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
On a sleepless night, rather than lying there forcing your eyes shut, nothing works like getting up and drinking something warm.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The "nothing beats" use is warm, opinionated and thoroughly conversational. It is the
shape of a food recommendation, a piece of folk wisdom, an advertising slogan. Because
it announces a verdict rather than a fact, it belongs to **the speaker**: you are saying
*I have decided this is the best*. That is why it sits so comfortably with
<code>やっぱり</code> and with a final <code>ね</code> or <code>よ</code>, and why it is
odd for someone else's taste. To report a colleague's preference, say
<code>部長(ぶちょう)は温泉(おんせん)が一番(いちばん)好(す)きらしい</code>, not
<code>部長(ぶちょう)は温泉(おんせん)に限(かぎ)る</code>.

<code>に限(かぎ)ります</code> is the polite form and is perfectly natural in
conversation. It does not make the sentence formal; it just makes it polite.

The literal "restricted to" use lives in a different world - application forms, museum
signage, ticket terms - and there the shortened <code>に限(かぎ)り</code> is the usual
shape: <code>本日(ほんじつ)に限(かぎ)り半額(はんがく)</code>. Read out of
context, <code>学生(がくせい)に限(かぎ)る</code> is genuinely ambiguous between
"students only" and "nothing beats being a student". Register and surroundings settle it:
posted on a wall it is a rule, said over a beer it is a verdict.

There is no casual contraction, and nothing about the pattern is age- or gender-marked.

## Contrast

### に限(かぎ)る vs に越(こ)したことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> picks one option and dismisses the rest.
<code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> says the option is *preferable, other things being
equal*, and leaves the alternatives standing.

</div>

<pre>
暑(あつ)い日(ひ)はやっぱり冷(ひ)えたビール<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
On a hot day it really has to be a cold beer.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は高(たか)い<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>が、休(やす)みも大事(だいじ)だ。
A high salary is nice to have, but time off matters too.
</pre>

The second sentence expects a <code>が</code>. That is the giveaway:
<code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> is a hedged preference and often comes with a
qualification attached, while <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> is a closed verdict that would
sound strange if you immediately undercut it. Note also that
<code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> attaches happily to
<code>高(たか)い</code>; <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> cannot.

### に限(かぎ)る vs ～ほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ほうがいい</code> is advice aimed at **a person in a
situation**. <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> is a general verdict the speaker is issuing about
**how the world works**.

</div>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)があるなら、今日(きょう)は病院(びょういん)へ<b>行(い)ったほうがいい</b>。
If you've got a temperature you should go to the doctor today.
</pre>

<pre>
熱(ねつ)が出(で)たら、早(はや)めに病院(びょういん)へ行(い)く<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
When you run a fever, the thing to do is get to a doctor early.
</pre>

The first is about you, now. The second is a maxim; it would fit a magazine column with
no listener in front of it. This is also why
<code>行(い)ったほうがいい</code> can sound pushy and
<code>行(い)くに限(かぎ)る</code> cannot - the second is not telling anyone what to do.

### に限(かぎ)る vs だけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけ</code> **describes** how many there are.
<code>に限(かぎ)る</code> **ranks or rules**: it either crowns a best option or lays
down a restriction.

</div>

<pre>
入場(にゅうじょう)できるのは小学生(しょうがくせい)<b>だけです</b>。
The only ones who can go in are primary-school children.
</pre>

<pre>
入場(にゅうじょう)は小学生(しょうがくせい)<b>に限(かぎ)ります</b>。
Admission is restricted to primary-school children.
</pre>

Both are true of the same doorway, but the first reports a fact and the second is the
rule itself being stated - which is why the second is what goes on the sign and the
first is what you say to a friend who asks. And <code>だけ</code> never has the "nothing
beats" reading: <code>夏(なつ)はビールだけだ</code> means you drink nothing else all
summer, which is a different and sadder claim.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 夏(なつ)はビール<b>が限(かぎ)る</b>。 → ✅ 夏(なつ)はビール<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
  The particle is fixed at <code>に</code>. Nothing else goes in that slot.

- ❌ 疲(つか)れたときは<b>寝(ね)たに限(かぎ)る</b>。 → ✅ 疲(つか)れたときは<b>寝(ね)るに限(かぎ)る</b>。
  The verb stays in the dictionary form. The verdict is timeless, so there is nothing
  for a past tense to do.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)いに限(かぎ)る</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)いのが一番(いちばん)だ</b>。
  Adjectives do not attach. Nouns and verbs only.

- ❌ 山田(やまだ)さんは旅行(りょこう)なら北海道(ほっかいどう)<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>そうだ。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さんは旅行(りょこう)なら北海道(ほっかいどう)が一番(いちばん)だと言(い)っていた。
  The verdict is the speaker's own. Attributing it to a third party reads oddly, as
  though you were speaking on their behalf.

## Practice

**1.** 二日酔(ふつかよ)いの朝(あさ)は、とにかく水(みず)を____限(かぎ)る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

とにかく水(みず)を<b>飲(の)むに</b>限(かぎ)る。

A verb attaches in its dictionary form, followed by <code>に</code>. The sentence is a
piece of general wisdom about hangovers, which is exactly the job this pattern does.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 荷物(にもつ)は軽(かる)い（に限(かぎ)る・に越(こ)したことはない）が、必要(ひつよう)なものまで置(お)いていくのは困(こま)る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

荷物(にもつ)は軽(かる)い<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>が…

Two reasons. <code>軽(かる)い</code> is an い-adjective, and
<code>に限(かぎ)る</code> does not take adjectives at all. And the
<code>が</code> clause immediately qualifies the preference, which is
<code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> behaviour, not the closed verdict of
<code>に限(かぎ)る</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When you're this tired, nothing beats a hot bath."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

こんなに疲(つか)れているときは、熱(あつ)いお風呂(ふろ)<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。

A noun attaches directly. <code>熱(あつ)い</code> here modifies
<code>お風呂(ふろ)</code>, so the thing sitting in front of
<code>に限(かぎ)る</code> is still a noun and the pattern is happy.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 申(もう)し込(こ)みは一人(ひとり)一回(いっかい)が限(かぎ)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 申(もう)し込(こ)みは一人(ひとり)一回(いっかい)<b>に限(かぎ)ります</b>。

The restriction sense takes <code>に</code> just as the verdict sense does. This is the
notice use, so the shortened form also works in running text:
<code>申(もう)し込(こ)みは一人(ひとり)一回(いっかい)に限(かぎ)り受(う)け付(つ)けます</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この部屋(へや)を使(つか)えるのは社員(しゃいん)だけです。
(b) この部屋(へや)の使用(しよう)は社員(しゃいん)に限(かぎ)ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the situation to whoever asked - the same information you would give a
visitor who wondered whether they could sit down. It is descriptive and neutral.

(b) states the rule. It is the wording of a notice on the door or a line in the office
handbook, and it carries the authority of whoever wrote it. Saying (b) casually to a
friend would sound as if you were quoting regulations at them.

</details>

# ～に限って

**Meaning** · of all times, of all people - singles one case out as the exceptional one

<code>～に限(かぎ)って</code> narrows the field to a single member and then says
something that would not be true of the others. With a time expression the effect is
almost always rueful: *of all the days, it had to be that one*. With a person and a
negative it flips warm: *whoever else might have done it, not her*.

## Formation

The pattern attaches to a **noun**. A clause has to be nominalised first - with
<code>とき</code>, <code>日(ひ)</code>, <code>場合(ばあい)</code> and so on.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (time) | Noun + に限(かぎ)って | 今日(きょう) → 今日(きょう)に限(かぎ)って |
| Clause (time) | plain form + とき／日(ひ) + に限(かぎ)って | 急(いそ)いでいる → 急(いそ)いでいるときに限(かぎ)って |
| Noun (person) + negative | Noun + に限(かぎ)って … ない | 母(はは) → 母(はは)に限(かぎ)って…ない |
| Notice / announcement | Noun + に限(かぎ)り | 本日(ほんじつ) → 本日(ほんじつ)に限(かぎ)り |
| Topic-marked | Noun + に限(かぎ)っては | 今回(こんかい) → 今回(こんかい)に限(かぎ)っては |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>に限(かぎ)り</code> is the same word in its stem form. It is the
shape used in written announcements, and it is neutral: it carries none of the "of all
days" irony that <code>に限(かぎ)って</code> picks up in speech.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)っていない日(ひ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る。
It rains precisely on the days I don't have an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
急(いそ)いでいるとき<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>エレベーターがなかなか来(こ)ない。
Whenever I'm in a hurry, that's when the lift takes forever.
</pre>

<pre>
母(はは)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、そんなうそをつくはずがない。
My mother, of all people, would never tell a lie like that.
</pre>

<pre>
本日(ほんじつ)<b>に限(かぎ)り</b>、全品(ぜんぴん)二割引(にわりびき)となっております。
Today only, everything is twenty percent off.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の前(まえ)の日(ひ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、なぜか部屋(へや)の片付(かたづ)けがしたくなる。
It's always the night before an exam that I suddenly want to tidy my room.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)をかけて仕込(しこ)んだ日(ひ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、お客(きゃく)さんが一人(ひとり)も来(こ)なかった。
On the very day I'd spent hours on the prep, not one customer came in.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、約束(やくそく)をすっぽかすようなことはないと思(おも)います。
He's the last person who would stand someone up, I think.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)を家(いえ)に置(お)いてきた日(ひ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、帰(かえ)り道(みち)でおいしそうなパン屋(や)を見(み)つけてしまう。
It's always the day I've left my wallet at home that I stumble on a bakery that smells amazing.
</pre>

<pre>
普段(ふだん)は静(しず)かな息子(むすこ)が、大事(だいじ)な電話(でんわ)をしているとき<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>大(おお)きな声(こえ)で歌(うた)い出(だ)す。
My son is normally quiet, and yet the moment I'm on an important call he starts singing at the top of his voice.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The time use is the one you will meet most, and it is emotionally loaded. It is a
complaint about the universe: the speaker has noticed a pattern of bad luck and is
pointing at it. Because of that it fits grumbling, storytelling and comedy, and it
pairs naturally with <code>なぜか</code>, <code>どういうわけか</code> and
<code>～てしまう</code>. It is register-neutral in the sense that
<code>に限(かぎ)って</code> itself is neither polite nor rude, but the *attitude*
is casual, so you would not put it in a formal report.

The person use runs the other way and is a small act of loyalty. It needs a negative
predicate - <code>はずがない</code>, <code>ことはない</code>, <code>わけがない</code> -
because the whole point is "not that one, whatever the others are like". Said about
someone who has just been accused, it is a defence.

<code>に限(かぎ)り</code> is the written cousin and belongs to shop notices, ticket
terms and internal announcements: <code>先着(せんちゃく)百名様(ひゃくめいさま)に限(かぎ)り</code>.
There is nothing rueful about it - it simply carves out a special case. Saying
<code>に限(かぎ)り</code> in casual conversation sounds like reading the small print
aloud.

One thing the pattern cannot do is mark a plain habit. "I only go on Sundays" is
<code>日曜日(にちようび)だけ</code>; putting <code>に限(かぎ)って</code> there would
promise an ironic twist that never arrives.

## Contrast

### に限(かぎ)って vs だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけ</code> restricts and stops there.
<code>に限(かぎ)って</code> restricts **and comments on it** - either "of all things,
that one" or "that one is the exception".

</div>

<pre>
月曜日(げつようび)<b>だけ</b>朝(あさ)の会議(かいぎ)があります。
There's a morning meeting on Mondays only.
</pre>

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)した日(ひ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>朝(あさ)の会議(かいぎ)が入(はい)っている。
It's always the mornings I oversleep that turn out to have a meeting in them.
</pre>

The first is a timetable. The second is a grievance. If you can replace the sentence
with a shrug, you want <code>に限(かぎ)って</code>; if you are just listing when
something happens, you want <code>だけ</code>.

### に限(かぎ)って vs こそ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both single one case out, but <code>こそ</code> singles it out as
**the good one** ("this time for sure"), and <code>に限(かぎ)って</code> singles it
out as **the unlucky one**.

</div>

<pre>
今度(こんど)<b>こそ</b>この企画(きかく)を通(とお)してみせる。
This time I really will get this proposal through.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、いつもは通(とお)る企画(きかく)が通(とお)らなかった。
This time of all times, a proposal that normally sails through didn't get approved.
</pre>

They are not interchangeable even where both are grammatical. <code>こそ</code> looks
forward with resolve; <code>に限(かぎ)って</code> looks back and shakes its head.

### に限(かぎ)って vs に限(かぎ)らず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - mirror images. <code>に限(かぎ)って</code> says *this one and no
other*; <code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> says *not just this one - the others too*.

</div>

<pre>
土日(どにち)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、この道(みち)はひどく混(こ)む。
It's on Saturdays and Sundays in particular that this road gets badly jammed.
</pre>

<pre>
土日(どにち)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、この道(みち)は平日(へいじつ)も混(こ)んでいる。
It isn't just weekends - this road is jammed on weekdays too.
</pre>

Same noun, same road, opposite claims. The <code>ず</code> is an old negative ending, so
<code>限(かぎ)らず</code> is literally "not limiting it to".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>忙(いそが)しいに限(かぎ)って</b>電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)る。 → ✅ <b>忙(いそが)しいときに限(かぎ)って</b>電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)る。
  The pattern needs a noun in front of it. An adjective or verb clause has to be
  wrapped in <code>とき</code> or <code>日(ひ)</code> first.

- ❌ うちの子(こ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>そんなことをする。 → ✅ うちの子(こ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>そんなことは<b>しない</b>。
  The person use exists to *exclude* someone. Without a negative there is nothing for
  the exclusion to bite on.

- ❌ 傘(かさ)を持(も)っている日(ひ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る。 → ✅ 傘(かさ)を持(も)っていない日(ひ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る。
  Grammatical, but it misses the point. The pattern reports a mismatch between the
  situation and what happens in it; line them up and the irony disappears.

- ❌ 水曜日(すいようび)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>ジムに通(かよ)っています。 → ✅ 水曜日(すいようび)<b>だけ</b>ジムに通(かよ)っています。
  A plain habit is <code>だけ</code>. <code>に限(かぎ)って</code> would leave the
  listener waiting for the bad news.

## Practice

**1.** 洗(あら)った車(くるま)を外(そと)に止(と)めた日(ひ)____、夕方(ゆうがた)から雨(あめ)になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

止(と)めた日(ひ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、夕方(ゆうがた)から雨(あめ)になった。

The clause has already been nominalised with <code>日(ひ)</code>, so the pattern
attaches directly. The washed car and the rain are exactly the kind of mismatch this
pattern exists to complain about.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この美術館(びじゅつかん)は大人(おとな)（に限(かぎ)って・に限(かぎ)らず）、子(こ)どもも楽(たの)しめる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大人(おとな)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、子(こ)どもも楽(たの)しめる。

The second half adds children with <code>も</code>, so the sentence is widening the
field, not narrowing it. That is the <code>ず</code> form.
<code>に限(かぎ)って</code> would contradict what follows.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Of all days, the printer broke down on the morning of the presentation."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

発表(はっぴょう)の朝(あさ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、プリンターが壊(こわ)れた。

<code>朝(あさ)</code> is already a noun, so nothing has to be added. "Of all days"
is carried entirely by the pattern - there is no separate word for it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 田中(たなか)さんに限(かぎ)って、人(ひと)のものを勝手(かって)に使(つか)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 田中(たなか)さんに限(かぎ)って、人(ひと)のものを勝手(かって)に<b>使(つか)うわけがない</b>。

Applied to a person, this pattern is a vote of confidence, and it needs a negative
predicate to complete the thought. As written, the sentence sets up a defence and then
delivers an accusation.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 本日(ほんじつ)に限(かぎ)り、入場料(にゅうじょうりょう)が無料(むりょう)です。
(b) 来(き)てほしくない日(ひ)に限(かぎ)って、親戚(しんせき)が訪(たず)ねてくる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an announcement. <code>に限(かぎ)り</code> carves out today as a special case
and stops there - no feeling attached, which is why it fits a poster at the entrance.

(b) is a complaint. <code>に限(かぎ)って</code> in speech points at a run of bad
timing and invites the listener to sympathise. Swapping the two forms would make (a)
sound resentful about its own offer and (b) sound like a scheduling notice.

</details>

# ～に限らず

**Meaning** · not just ～; the same is true beyond ～ as well

<code>限(かぎ)らず</code> is the negative stem of <code>限(かぎ)る</code> - "not
limiting it to". The sentence names an obvious category, refuses to stop there, and then
extends the claim outward. The reader's expected boundary is stated precisely so that it
can be knocked down.

## Formation

The pattern attaches to a **noun**. The second clause almost always carries
<code>も</code> or <code>でも</code>, which is what does the extending.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に限(かぎ)らず | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)に限(かぎ)らず |
| Noun (time) | Noun + に限(かぎ)らず | 土日(どにち) → 土日(どにち)に限(かぎ)らず |
| Second clause | …に限(かぎ)らず、… も／でも | → 大人(おとな)に限(かぎ)らず、子(こ)どもも |
| As a predicate | Noun + に限(かぎ)らない | 日本(にほん) → 問題(もんだい)は日本(にほん)に限(かぎ)らない |
| Polite predicate | Noun + に限(かぎ)りません | → 東京(とうきょう)に限(かぎ)りません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the connective form is <code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> and the sentence-final
form is <code>に限(かぎ)らない</code>. Do not end a sentence with the
<code>ず</code> form; it is a mid-sentence connector, like the て-form of a negative.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この図書館(としょかん)は学生(がくせい)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、だれでも利用(りよう)できます。
This library isn't just for students - anyone can use it.
</pre>

<pre>
土日(どにち)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、平日(へいじつ)も営業(えいぎょう)しております。
We're open on weekdays as well, not only at weekends.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は英語(えいご)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、フランス語(ご)もスペイン語(ご)も話(はな)せる。
She speaks not just English but French and Spanish too.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)は日本(にほん)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、世界中(せかいじゅう)で起(お)きている。
This is happening all over the world, not only in Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
人手不足(ひとでぶそく)は飲食(いんしょく)業界(ぎょうかい)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、どの業界(ぎょうかい)でも深刻(しんこく)になっている。
The labour shortage is serious in every industry, not just catering.
</pre>

<pre>
最近(さいきん)は若者(わかもの)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、高齢者(こうれいしゃ)もスマートフォンで買(か)い物(もの)をするようになった。
These days it isn't only young people - older people shop on their phones too.
</pre>

<pre>
災害(さいがい)への備(そな)えは地震(じしん)の多(おお)い地域(ちいき)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、どこに住(す)んでいても欠(か)かせない。
Disaster preparation is essential wherever you live, not just in earthquake-prone areas.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの会社(かいしゃ)では、新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、ベテランも毎年(まいとし)研修(けんしゅう)を受(う)けることになっている。
At our company it isn't only new hires - veterans go through training every year as well.
</pre>

<pre>
温暖化(おんだんか)の影響(えいきょう)は農業(のうぎょう)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、漁業(ぎょぎょう)や観光業(かんこうぎょう)にも及(およ)んでいる。
The effects of warming reach fishing and tourism too, not agriculture alone.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> is a written-leaning form. Its natural homes are news
copy, company announcements, academic prose and the kind of careful spoken Japanese you
hear in a presentation. The <code>ず</code> ending is inherited from classical Japanese
and gives the phrase a tidy, slightly formal edge - not stiff, but noticeably more
composed than <code>だけでなく</code>.

It is not rude or archaic, and nobody would blink at it in a meeting. In a chat with a
friend it would simply be more words than the moment needs;
<code>学生(がくせい)だけじゃなくて</code> is what you would actually say.

Because the pattern names a boundary in order to reject it, the noun in front of it
should be one the listener plausibly assumed. <code>学生(がくせい)に限(かぎ)らず</code>
works because a library really does suggest students. Put a random noun there and the
sentence sounds like it is answering a question nobody asked.

There is a stronger, more literary sibling, <code>のみならず</code>, which does the same
job in heavier prose, and an emphatic one, <code>はもとより</code>, which adds "to say
nothing of". Both are treated on their own pages. If you only ever use
<code>に限(かぎ)らず</code>, you will still sound natural.

## Contrast

### に限(かぎ)らず vs だけでなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different reach.
<code>だけでなく</code> attaches to almost anything and fits any register;
<code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> takes **nouns only** and leans formal.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>だけでなく</b>、味(あじ)もいい。
This place isn't just cheap, the food is good too.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げは食品(しょくひん)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、日用品(にちようひん)にも広(ひろ)がっている。
The price rises aren't confined to food - they've spread to household goods as well.
</pre>

The first sentence cannot use <code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> at all, because
<code>安(やす)い</code> is an adjective. The second could use
<code>だけでなく</code> and would simply sound a shade more everyday. When in doubt,
<code>だけでなく</code> is the safe choice; <code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> is the choice
that signals you are writing rather than chatting.

### に限(かぎ)らず vs とは限(かぎ)らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they look like the same verb and mean quite different things.
<code>に限(かぎ)らず</code> = "not only X (but also Y)".
<code>とは限(かぎ)らない</code> = "not necessarily X".

</div>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は社員(しゃいん)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、家族(かぞく)も来(き)ています。
The people taking part aren't just employees - families are here too.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)が社員(しゃいん)だ<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
The participants aren't necessarily employees.
</pre>

The first counts more people in. The second declines to guarantee anything about who
they are. The clue is the particle: <code>に</code> introduces a category being
extended, <code>と</code> introduces a claim being doubted.

### に限(かぎ)らず vs に限(かぎ)って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same verb, positive and negative.
<code>に限(かぎ)って</code> narrows to one case; <code>に限(かぎ)らず</code>
refuses to narrow at all.

</div>

<pre>
夏(なつ)<b>に限(かぎ)って</b>、この坂道(さかみち)は観光客(かんこうきゃく)でいっぱいになる。
It's in summer specifically that this slope fills up with tourists.
</pre>

<pre>
夏(なつ)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、この坂道(さかみち)は一年中(いちねんじゅう)観光客(かんこうきゃく)でいっぱいだ。
It isn't only summer - this slope is packed with tourists all year round.
</pre>

If you swap them by accident the sentence still parses, which is what makes the mistake
hard to notice. Check the second clause: if it adds something with <code>も</code>, you
need <code>らず</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)いに限(かぎ)らず</b>、品物(しなもの)もいい。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)いだけでなく</b>、品物(しなもの)もいい。
  Nouns only. An adjective or a verb needs <code>だけでなく</code> or
  <code>ばかりでなく</code>.

- ❌ 会議(かいぎ)は月曜(げつよう)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、火曜(かよう)に行(おこな)う。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)は月曜(げつよう)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>、火曜(かよう)<b>にも</b>行(おこな)う。
  Without <code>も</code> the sentence reads as a replacement rather than an addition,
  and the first half is left dangling.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)が来(く)る<b>とは限(かぎ)らず</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が来(く)る<b>とは限(かぎ)らない</b>。
  <code>らず</code> connects; it cannot end a sentence. And with
  <code>と</code> the meaning is "not necessarily", which is a different pattern.

- ❌ 問題(もんだい)は東京(とうきょう)<b>だけに限(かぎ)らず</b>… → ✅ 問題(もんだい)は東京(とうきょう)<b>に限(かぎ)らず</b>…
  <code>だけ</code> is doing the same work as <code>限(かぎ)る</code>, so the two
  together are redundant. You hear it, but it is not what you want in writing.

# ～限り

**Meaning** · as long as ～ holds; within the bounds of ～ - and, in the negative, "unless"

<code>限(かぎ)り</code> is the noun form of "to limit". It draws a boundary around the
main clause and says: *inside this line, what follows is true; outside it, all bets are
off*. The negative <code>～ない限(かぎ)り</code> draws the same line from the other
side and comes out as English "unless".

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain non-past + 限(かぎ)り | 生(い)きている → 生(い)きている限(かぎ)り |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + 限(かぎ)り | 謝(あやま)らない → 謝(あやま)らない限(かぎ)り |
| Verb (past, "as far as I…") | た-form + 限(かぎ)り(では) | 調(しら)べた → 調(しら)べた限(かぎ)りでは |
| い-adjective (negative) | ～くない + 限(かぎ)り | ひどくない → ひどくない限(かぎ)り |
| な-adjective | ～な + 限(かぎ)り | 可能(かのう)な → 可能(かのう)な限(かぎ)り |
| Noun | Noun + の／である + 限(かぎ)り | 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)である限(かぎ)り |
| Noun (negative) | Noun + でない限(かぎ)り | 特別(とくべつ) → 特別(とくべつ)でない限(かぎ)り |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb before <code>限(かぎ)り</code> is non-past even when the
sentence is about the future: <code>雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らない限(かぎ)り、明日(あした)やります</code>.
A past tense there switches the meaning to "as far as I checked / heard".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らない<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われます。
Unless it rains, the match will go ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
体(からだ)が動(うご)く<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、この仕事(しごと)を続(つづ)けたい。
As long as my body holds out, I want to keep doing this job.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が知(し)っている<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、彼(かれ)はまだ返事(へんじ)をしていない。
As far as I know, he hasn't replied yet.
</pre>

<pre>
可能(かのう)な<b>限(かぎ)り</b>早(はや)くお返事(へんじ)いたします。
I'll get back to you as quickly as I possibly can.
</pre>

<pre>
締(し)め切(き)りを守(まも)る<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、進(すす)め方(かた)は皆(みな)さんに任(まか)せます。
So long as you meet the deadline, how you go about it is up to you.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)が謝(あやま)らない<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、この話(はなし)は終(お)わらないだろう。
Unless he apologises himself, this isn't going to be over.
</pre>

<pre>
特別(とくべつ)な事情(じじょう)がない<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、途中(とちゅう)退出(たいしゅつ)はご遠慮(えんりょ)ください。
Unless there are special circumstances, please refrain from leaving partway through.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)が元気(げんき)でいてくれる<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、私(わたし)は安心(あんしん)して外国(がいこく)で働(はたら)ける。
As long as my parents stay well, I can work abroad without worrying.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)見(み)た<b>限(かぎ)り</b>では、工事(こうじ)はまだ半分(はんぶん)も進(すす)んでいなかった。
From what I saw yesterday, the work wasn't even half done.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>限(かぎ)り</code> is neutral in politeness and lives comfortably in speech and
writing alike, though the density of it in official Japanese is high: contracts, notices
and terms of service are full of <code>～ない限(かぎ)り</code>, because that is how
you write a condition that has no exceptions.

Three shapes are worth knowing as units.
<code>可能(かのう)な限(かぎ)り</code> ("as far as possible") is a fixed adverbial
phrase and appears constantly in business Japanese, a step more formal than
<code>できるだけ</code>. <code>見(み)た限(かぎ)りでは</code>,
<code>聞(き)いた限(かぎ)りでは</code>, <code>調(しら)べた限(かぎ)りでは</code>
hedge a statement by naming the limits of your evidence - useful and modest, and worth
having ready before you report something you are not certain of. And
<code>私(わたし)の知(し)る限(かぎ)り</code> is a slightly bookish variant of
<code>知(し)っている限(かぎ)り</code>, common in writing.

The "as long as" use carries a faint sense of *dependency*: the main clause is not just
co-occurring with the condition, it is **propped up by it**. That is why
<code>生(い)きている限(かぎ)り</code> and
<code>体(からだ)が動(うご)く限(かぎ)り</code> sound determined and a little
emotional - the speaker is naming the one thing the promise rests on.

There is no casual contraction and no gender or age marking.

## Contrast

### ～限(かぎ)り vs ～間(あいだ)(は)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>間(あいだ)</code> is a **stretch of time**.
<code>限(かぎ)り</code> is a **condition** that happens to last a while.

</div>

<pre>
日本(にほん)にいる<b>間(あいだ)</b>に、東北(とうほく)を旅行(りょこう)したい。
While I'm in Japan I want to travel around Tohoku.
</pre>

<pre>
日本(にほん)にいる<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、この保険(ほけん)に入(はい)る義務(ぎむ)がある。
So long as you are in Japan, you are obliged to join this insurance scheme.
</pre>

The first measures a window. The second states what depends on your being here - leave
the country and the obligation lapses. Swap them and the first sentence would sound as
though your travel plans were somehow conditional on your presence, which is trivially
true and therefore odd to say.

### ～ない限(かぎ)り vs ～なければ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>なければ</code> is an ordinary "if not".
<code>ない限(かぎ)り</code> is "unless", and it presents the condition as **the only
thing that would change the outcome**.

</div>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)ま<b>なければ</b>熱(ねつ)は下(さ)がらないよ。
If you don't take the medicine your temperature won't come down.
</pre>

<pre>
体質(たいしつ)が変(か)わら<b>ない限(かぎ)り</b>、同(おな)じ症状(しょうじょう)は何度(なんど)でも出(で)る。
Unless your constitution itself changes, the same symptoms will keep coming back.
</pre>

The second is stronger. It shuts the door: nothing short of that condition will make any
difference. That is why it is the workhorse of rules and warnings, and why using it for
a small everyday if-not can sound heavier than you intended.

### ～た限(かぎ)りでは vs ～によると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both hedge, but they hedge different things.
<code>によると</code> names **someone else's report**;
<code>た限(かぎ)りでは</code> names **the limits of your own checking**.

</div>

<pre>
天気予報(てんきよほう)<b>によると</b>、週末(しゅうまつ)は荒(あ)れるそうだ。
According to the forecast, the weekend is going to be rough.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)から外(そと)を見(み)た<b>限(かぎ)りでは</b>、雪(ゆき)はもう止(や)んでいた。
As far as I could tell looking out of the window, the snow had already stopped.
</pre>

<code>によると</code> passes responsibility to the source and pairs with
<code>そうだ</code>. <code>た限(かぎ)りでは</code> keeps responsibility with you but
fences off how much you actually saw - which is why it is the polite way to answer a
question you cannot fully answer.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)らなかった限(かぎ)り</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)します。 → ✅ 明日(あした)雨(あめ)が<b>降(ふ)らない限(かぎ)り</b>、出発(しゅっぱつ)します。
  A condition about the future stays non-past. The past form would turn the phrase into
  "as far as I saw it not raining", which is not what you mean.

- ❌ <b>元気(げんき)だ限(かぎ)り</b>、働(はたら)きたい。 → ✅ <b>元気(げんき)な限(かぎ)り</b>、働(はたら)きたい。
  <code>限(かぎ)り</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective links with <code>な</code>,
  never with <code>だ</code>.

- ❌ <b>学生(がくせい)限(かぎ)り</b>、割引(わりびき)が受(う)けられる。 → ✅ <b>学生(がくせい)である限(かぎ)り</b>、割引(わりびき)が受(う)けられる。
  A bare noun cannot sit directly in front of it. Use <code>である</code> or
  <code>の</code>.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がある<b>限(かぎ)り</b>、少(すこ)し休(やす)みませんか。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がある<b>うちに</b>、少(すこ)し休(やす)みませんか。
  Here you mean "while there's still time", a window - that is
  <code>うちに</code>. <code>限(かぎ)り</code> would make the resting somehow
  contingent on time existing.

## Practice

The past form plus <code>では</code> is the "as far as I checked" shape. It fences the
claim off to what the map showed, which is exactly why the sentence can end with the
cautious <code>はずだ</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 日本(にほん)にいる（間(あいだ)・限(かぎ)り）、できるだけ温泉(おんせん)を回(まわ)りたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

日本(にほん)にいる<b>間(あいだ)</b>、できるだけ温泉(おんせん)を回(まわ)りたい。

This is a time window - a stay with a beginning and an end - not a condition the wish
depends on. <code>限(かぎ)り</code> would imply the desire itself is propped up by
being in Japan, which is a strange thing to say about a holiday plan.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Unless something unexpected happens, we'll finish by Friday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

予想外(よそうがい)のことが起(お)こら<b>ない限(かぎ)り</b>、金曜日(きんようび)までに終(お)わります。

"Unless" is the negative form plus <code>限(かぎ)り</code>, and the verb stays
non-past even though the sentence looks forward. The phrasing promises that nothing else
would derail the schedule.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 健康(けんこう)だ限(かぎ)り、旅行(りょこう)を続(つづ)けるつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 健康(けんこう)<b>である限(かぎ)り</b>、旅行(りょこう)を続(つづ)けるつもりです。（or 健康(けんこう)な限(かぎ)り）

<code>限(かぎ)り</code> is a noun and needs a noun-modifying shape in front of it.
<code>健康(けんこう)だ</code> is a finished predicate and cannot modify anything, so
it becomes <code>な</code> or <code>である</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)が知(し)っている限(かぎ)り、彼(かれ)は来(き)ません。
(b) 彼(かれ)は来(こ)ないと思(おも)います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) fences the statement off to your own information. You are saying he is not coming
*so far as your knowledge extends*, and quietly leaving room for someone better informed
to correct you. It is the careful thing to say in a meeting.

(b) is a straightforward opinion. It commits you to a judgement rather than to a body of
evidence, so if he walks in five minutes later you were simply wrong - whereas with (a)
you were merely under-informed.

</details>

# ～だけは

**Meaning** · at least the ～ part; this one thing, whatever else - and, in <code>やるだけはやった</code>, "I did all the doing there was to do"

<code>だけ</code> restricts; the <code>は</code> then lifts that one item out and sets
it against everything else. The result is not "only" in the flat sense. It is a **floor**:
whatever failed, whatever is missing, *this much is secured*. In the doubled shape
<code>言(い)うだけは言(い)った</code> the floor is one's own effort, and the
unspoken second half is almost always "…but it didn't work".

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb, doubled | dictionary form + だけは + same verb, past | やる → やるだけはやった |
| Verb, doubled (polite) | dictionary form + だけは + ました | 試(ため)す → 試(ため)すだけは試(ため)しました |
| Noun | Noun + だけは | これ → これだけは |
| Nominalised clause | ～こと + だけは | 謝(あやま)ること → 謝(あやま)ることだけは |
| Verb + ～ておく／～てみる | dictionary form + だけは + ～ておく | 聞(き)く → 聞(き)くだけは聞(き)いておく |
| Negative resolve | ～ことだけは + しない | 裏切(うらぎ)ること → 裏切(うらぎ)ることだけはしない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in the doubled shape the two verbs must be the **same verb**, and the first
one stays in the dictionary form no matter what tense the sentence is in. The tense is
carried entirely by the second.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これ<b>だけは</b>忘(わす)れないでください。
Whatever else, please don't forget this one thing.
</pre>

<pre>
やる<b>だけは</b>やった。あとは結果(けっか)を待(ま)とう。
I've done everything I could do. Now we wait for the result.
</pre>

<pre>
言(い)う<b>だけは</b>言(い)ってみたが、返事(へんじ)はなかった。
I said my piece, for what it was worth, but there was no reply.
</pre>

<pre>
嫌(きら)いな野菜(やさい)でも、一口(ひとくち)食(た)べる<b>だけは</b>食(た)べなさい。
Even if you hate the vegetables, at least take one bite.
</pre>

<pre>
練習(れんしゅう)する<b>だけは</b>練習(れんしゅう)したのに、本番(ほんばん)で手(て)が震(ふる)えてしまった。
I'd put in all the practice there was to put in, and then my hands shook on the day.
</pre>

<pre>
時間(じかん)がなくて全部(ぜんぶ)は読(よ)めなかったが、大事(だいじ)なところ<b>だけは</b>目(め)を通(とお)しておいた。
I didn't have time to read all of it, but I did at least look over the important parts.
</pre>

<pre>
謝(あやま)る<b>だけは</b>謝(あやま)ったのだから、あとは相手(あいて)が許(ゆる)すかどうかの問題(もんだい)だ。
I've apologised as far as apologising goes; whether she forgives me is up to her now.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)のことで苦労(くろう)しても、人(ひと)にうそをつくこと<b>だけは</b>したくない。
However much I struggle for money, the one thing I won't do is lie to people.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)に落(お)ちたのは悔(くや)しいが、できること<b>だけは</b>全部(ぜんぶ)やったので後悔(こうかい)はしていない。
Failing stings, but I did at least do every single thing I could, so I've no regrets.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The tone is resignation with dignity. <code>やるだけはやった</code> is what you say
when the outcome has gone against you and you want it on record that the effort was not
the problem. It is warm towards the speaker and slightly braced - there is always an
implied <code>が</code> after it, even when nothing follows.

That implication cuts both ways. Because the pattern separates *the doing* from *the
result*, pairing it with confidence is contradictory:
<code>やるだけはやったから絶対(ぜったい)受(う)かる</code> pulls in two directions
at once. If the result is good, drop the pattern and just say
<code>がんばってよかった</code>.

The noun use - <code>これだけは</code>, <code>命(いのち)だけは</code>,
<code>約束(やくそく)だけは</code> - is a different flavour: not resignation but
**insistence**. It marks the non-negotiable item in a list of things you are otherwise
willing to lose. That makes it strong, and in a request it is close to pleading:
<code>これだけはお願(ねが)いします</code> is heavier than
<code>これをお願(ねが)いします</code>.

Register-wise the pattern is neutral. It works in plain and ます form, in speech and in
writing, and it is not marked for age or gender. What is casual about
<code>やるだけはやった</code> is the sentiment, not the grammar; the same words in
<code>やるだけはやりました</code> are perfectly acceptable in a report to a manager.

## Contrast

### やるだけはやった vs やるだけやった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - without <code>は</code>, the sentence measures **how much** was done.
With <code>は</code>, it separates the doing from the outcome and quietly promises a
"but".

</div>

<pre>
時間(じかん)のある限(かぎ)り、やる<b>だけやった</b>。
I did as much as there was time to do.
</pre>

<pre>
やる<b>だけはやった</b>が、結果(けっか)はついてこなかった。
I did the doing part, at least - the results didn't follow.
</pre>

The difference is small enough that in many sentences either passes, and native speakers
will not always agree on a line between them. The reliable part is the pull of the
<code>は</code>: it invites a contrast, so a listener hearing
<code>やるだけはやった</code> is already braced for bad news.

### ～だけは vs ～だけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけは</code> sets a **floor** ("at least this much").
<code>だけだ</code> sets a **ceiling** ("nothing more than this").

</div>

<pre>
言(い)う<b>だけは</b>言(い)いました。
I did at least say it.
</pre>

<pre>
言(い)った<b>だけです</b>。
I only said it. (I didn't do anything about it.)
</pre>

The first defends you; the second is what someone says to belittle what you did, and
sits next to <code>にすぎない</code> in feel. Getting these the wrong way round in an
apology is genuinely awkward.

### ～だけは vs ～だけでも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけは</code> = *this one is non-negotiable*.
<code>だけでも</code> = *even just this much would do*.

</div>

<pre>
夜(よる)遅(おそ)くなるなら、連絡(れんらく)<b>だけは</b>してね。
If you're going to be late, the one thing you must do is get in touch.
</pre>

<pre>
全部(ぜんぶ)は無理(むり)でも、この一枚(いちまい)<b>だけでも</b>見(み)てもらえませんか。
I know you can't do all of it, but could you at least look at this one page?
</pre>

<code>だけは</code> is a demand or a promise; <code>だけでも</code> is a concession -
it lowers the ask because the full version is out of reach. Put the wrong one in a
request and you either sound stern when you meant to be accommodating, or the reverse.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 宿題(しゅくだい)<b>だけは</b>しました。（meaning "the homework was all I did"） → ✅ 宿題(しゅくだい)<b>だけ</b>しました。／宿題(しゅくだい)<b>しか</b>していません。
  <code>だけは</code> means "at least the homework, whatever else went undone" - a
  defence, not a confession. This is the single most common misreading of the pattern.

- ❌ <b>やっただけはやった</b>。 → ✅ <b>やるだけはやった</b>。
  The first verb stays in the dictionary form. Only the second one takes the tense.

- ❌ <b>言(い)うだけは話(はな)した</b>。 → ✅ <b>言(い)うだけは言(い)った</b>。
  The doubled shape needs the very same verb twice. A synonym breaks it.

- ❌ できること<b>だけは</b>やったので、今回(こんかい)は絶対(ぜったい)大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だ。 → ✅ できること<b>だけは</b>やったので、あとは運(うん)にまかせよう。
  The pattern concedes that the result is out of your hands. Following it with certainty
  contradicts the very thing it just said.

## Practice

**1.** 荷物(にもつ)は置(お)いていってもいいけれど、パスポート____持(も)っていってね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

パスポート<b>だけは</b>持(も)っていってね。

Everything else is negotiable; the passport is the floor. That contrast between one
protected item and the rest is what the <code>は</code> is doing.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 完成(かんせい)はしなかったが、下(した)調(しら)べ（だけ・だけは）しておいたので、次(つぎ)の人(ひと)が楽(らく)になるはずだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

下(した)調(しら)べ<b>だけは</b>しておいた…

The sentence is defending an incomplete job by pointing at what *was* secured, which
calls for the floor reading. Plain <code>だけ</code> would read as "all I did was the
groundwork", which turns the defence into an admission.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I applied, at least - I don't expect to get in."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

申(もう)し込(こ)む<b>だけは</b>申(もう)し込(こ)んだ。通(とお)るとは思(おも)っていないけれど。

The doubled shape is the natural fit: the effort is real, the outcome is not being
claimed. The English "at least" lands on the pattern, not on a separate adverb.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)に説明(せつめい)するだけは説明(せつめい)するが、聞(き)いてもらえなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に説明(せつめい)する<b>だけは説明(せつめい)した</b>が、聞(き)いてもらえなかった。

The second half is in the past, so the second verb must be too. The first verb stays as

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 片付(かたづ)けるだけは片付(かたづ)けた。
(b) 片付(かたづ)けただけだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the tidying itself was done properly, and braces the listener for a "but" -
perhaps the room still looks bad, or nobody noticed. It protects the speaker.

(b) belittles the act: tidying was *all* that happened, as if it hardly counted. Said
about your own work it sounds modest; said about someone else's it sounds dismissive.
The two sentences share every word except the particle, and they take opposite sides.

</details>

# ～だけましだ

**Meaning** · at least ～, which is something to be grateful for; it could have been worse

<code>まし</code> means *less bad* - preferable, given the alternatives. It is not
praise. <code>～だけましだ</code> therefore takes a situation that has already gone
wrong and points at the one part that did not, holding it up against the worse version
that almost happened. English "at least" catches it, as long as you hear the sigh in it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + だけましだ | 残(のこ)っている → 残(のこ)っているだけましだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + だけましだ | 怪我(けが)をしない → 怪我(けが)をしなかっただけましだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけましだ | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いだけましだ |
| な-adjective | ～な + だけましだ | 静(しず)かな → 静(しず)かなだけましだ |
| Noun | Noun + である／だった + だけましだ | 無事(ぶじ) → 無事(ぶじ)だっただけましだ |
| Polite | … + だけましです | 近(ちか)い → 近(ちか)いだけましです |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>まし</code> is written in kana in practice. The kanji
<code>増(ま)し</code> exists but belongs to other words
(<code>三割増(さんわりま)し</code>), and writing it here looks wrong to most readers.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)は安(やす)いが、残業(ざんぎょう)がない<b>だけましだ</b>。
The pay is poor, but at least there's no overtime.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)に降(ふ)られたけれど、傘(かさ)があった<b>だけましだ</b>。
I got caught in the rain, but at least I had an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
停電(ていでん)は困(こま)るけれど、水(みず)が出(で)る<b>だけましです</b>よ。
A power cut is a nuisance, but at least the water's still running.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)は難(むずか)しかったが、最後(さいご)まで解(と)けた<b>だけましだ</b>。
The exam was hard, but at least I got to the end of it.
</pre>

<pre>
部屋(へや)はせまいが、駅(えき)から近(ちか)い<b>だけましだ</b>と自分(じぶん)に言(い)い聞(き)かせている。
The flat is cramped, but I keep telling myself at least it's near the station.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)で屋根(やね)が壊(こわ)れたが、家族(かぞく)が無事(ぶじ)だった<b>だけましだ</b>。
The typhoon wrecked the roof, but at least the family came through unhurt.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ文句(もんく)を言(い)ってくれる<b>だけましだ</b>。何(なに)も言(い)わずに去(さ)られるほうがつらい。
At least she still complains to me. Being left without a word would be harder.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)のプロジェクトは赤字(あかじ)だったが、去年(きょねん)ほどひどくない<b>だけましだ</b>と部長(ぶちょう)は言(い)っていた。
The project ran at a loss, but the manager said at least it wasn't as bad as last year.
</pre>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)して一時間(いちじかん)歩(ある)いたが、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていなかった<b>だけましだった</b>。
I missed the last train and walked for an hour, but at least it wasn't raining.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everything about this pattern presupposes that the overall situation is bad. It is a
consolation offered from inside a mess - to yourself, or to someone else who is having a
worse day than you. It pairs naturally with <code>まだ</code> in front
(<code>まだ安(やす)いだけましだ</code>) and with a <code>が</code> or
<code>けれど</code> clause before it setting up the bad news.

The register is ordinary conversational Japanese, plain or polite as you like, and it is
not marked for age or gender. What needs care is **who you say it about**. Because
<code>まし</code> means "less bad", applying it to another person's work, cooking or
effort is faint praise at best and an insult at worst:
<code>先生(せんせい)の説明(せつめい)はましでした</code> would land badly. Keep it
for situations, weather, prices, damage - and for your own circumstances.

Said to someone in genuine distress it can also come out as dismissive, the Japanese
equivalent of "well, at least you've still got your health". The safe version is to apply
it to yourself and let the other person apply it to themselves if they want to.

## Contrast

### ～だけましだ vs ～ほうがましだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけましだ</code> evaluates **the situation you are in**.
<code>ほうがましだ</code> **chooses between two bad options**: "I'd rather".

</div>

<pre>
バスは遅(おそ)いが、座(すわ)れる<b>だけましだ</b>。
The bus is slow, but at least you get a seat.
</pre>

<pre>
満員電車(まんいんでんしゃ)で立(た)っているより、歩(ある)いた<b>ほうがましだ</b>。
I'd rather walk than stand on a packed train.
</pre>

Both contain <code>まし</code> and both concede that nothing here is good. The first is
stuck with its situation and finds a redeeming feature; the second still has a choice and
picks the less painful branch.

### まし vs いい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>いい</code> means good. <code>まし</code> means **less bad**.
They are not degrees of the same thing.

</div>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は安(やす)くておいしいから、いつも<b>いい</b>と思(おも)っている。
This place is cheap and good, so I always think well of it.
</pre>

<pre>
どの店(みせ)もいまいちだが、ここが一番(いちばん)<b>ましだ</b>。
None of the places are great, but this one is the least bad.
</pre>

Substituting one for the other changes a compliment into a complaint. If the thing you
are describing is genuinely good, <code>まし</code> is the wrong word no matter how
modest you are trying to sound.

### ～だけましだ vs ～だけでもありがたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけましだ</code> measures against **a worse outcome that was
avoided**. <code>だけでもありがたい</code> expresses **plain gratitude** for what you
did get.

</div>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が遅(おそ)かったが、連絡(れんらく)が来(き)た<b>だけましだ</b>。
The reply was slow, but at least something came.
</pre>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しいのに来(き)てくれた<b>だけでもありがたい</b>。
I'm just grateful you came at all, busy as you are.
</pre>

The second is warm and directed at someone. The first is cooler and directed at fate.
Say <code>だけましだ</code> about a favour someone did you and it will sound as though
you were grading them, which is not what you meant.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この部屋(へや)は<b>静(しず)かだだけましだ</b>。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)は<b>静(しず)かなだけましだ</b>。
  <code>だけ</code> follows a noun-modifying form, so a な-adjective links with
  <code>な</code>.

- ❌ この旅館(りょかん)は料理(りょうり)もお風呂(ふろ)もすばらしくて、<b>ましだった</b>。 → ✅ …すばらしくて、<b>とてもよかった</b>。
  <code>まし</code> only makes sense when the surroundings are bad. Applied to
  something genuinely enjoyable it reads as damning with faint praise.

- ❌ 「先生(せんせい)の授業(じゅぎょう)はましでした」 → ✅ 「先生(せんせい)の授業(じゅぎょう)はわかりやすかったです」
  Grammatical, socially disastrous. Never grade a person with <code>まし</code> to
  their face.

- ❌ 合格(ごうかく)できた<b>だけましだ</b>。（about a result you are pleased with） → ✅ 合格(ごうかく)できて<b>よかった</b>。
  The pattern insists the news is bad overall. Attaching it to good news puzzles the
  listener, who will start looking for the disaster you must be referring to.

## Practice

**1.** 財布(さいふ)をなくしたが、中(なか)に現金(げんきん)が____だけましだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

中(なか)に現金(げんきん)が<b>入(はい)っていなかった</b>だけましだ。

The comparison is with the version where the wallet was full of cash. A plain form
attaches directly, and the sentence works because losing a wallet is unambiguously bad
news - the precondition this pattern requires.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)したら、タクシーで帰(かえ)る（だけましだ・ほうがましだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

タクシーで帰(かえ)る<b>ほうがましだ</b>。

You still have a choice - taxi, or a night in an internet café - and you are picking the
less painful one. <code>だけましだ</code> would need you to be already stuck with the
taxi and finding a silver lining in it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The flat's old, but at least the rent is cheap."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部屋(へや)は古(ふる)いが、家賃(やちん)が安(やす)い<b>だけましだ</b>。

The <code>が</code> clause puts the bad news first, which is where this pattern likes
it. An い-adjective attaches with no change.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 大雨(おおあめ)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったが、会社(かいしゃ)が近(ちか)いだけよかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 大雨(おおあめ)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったが、会社(かいしゃ)が近(ちか)い<b>だけましだ</b>。

<code>だけいい</code> is not the pattern, and it would claim the situation is good,
which it plainly is not. <code>まし</code> is what carries the "less bad than it might
have been" sense.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 熱(ねつ)は下(さ)がらないが、食(た)べられるだけましだ。
(b) 熱(ねつ)は下(さ)がらないが、食(た)べられてよかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) frames eating as the floor of a bad situation. The speaker is comparing today with
the version where the patient could not keep anything down, and finding cold comfort.

(b) is simply relieved. It reports something positive without weighing it against a
worse scenario, so it sounds lighter and kinder - which is why (b) is what you would say
to the patient and (a) is what you would say about them.

</details>

# ～に越したことはない

**Meaning** · you can't do better than ～; ～ is the ideal, other things being equal

Literally "there is nothing that surpasses ～". The pattern states a mild general
preference, not an instruction: more of this is better, and if you can arrange it you
should. Because it is a preference rather than a requirement, it very often has a
<code>が</code> or <code>けれど</code> hanging off the end, admitting that life may not
allow it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + に越(こ)したことはない | 確(たし)かめる → 確(たし)かめるに越(こ)したことはない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + に越(こ)したことはない | 無理(むり)をしない → 無理(むり)をしないに越(こ)したことはない |
| い-adjective | plain form + に越(こ)したことはない | 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いに越(こ)したことはない |
| な-adjective | ～である + に越(こ)したことはない | 丈夫(じょうぶ)である → 丈夫(じょうぶ)であるに越(こ)したことはない |
| Noun | Noun + に越(こ)したことはない | 健康(けんこう) → 健康(けんこう)に越(こ)したことはない |
| Polite | … + に越(こ)したことはありません | 早(はや)い → 早(はや)いに越(こ)したことはありません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb is <code>越(こ)す</code>, "to surpass", not
<code>越(こ)える</code>. The whole tail is frozen:
<code>越(こ)したことはない</code>. Changing the <code>は</code> to
<code>が</code> or the <code>ない</code> to <code>ありません</code> mid-phrase breaks it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)は早(はや)い<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
When it comes to preparation, the earlier the better.
</pre>

<pre>
家賃(やちん)は安(やす)い<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
You can't go wrong with cheaper rent.
</pre>

<pre>
体(からだ)は丈夫(じょうぶ)である<b>に越(こ)したことはありません</b>。
There's nothing better than having a strong constitution.
</pre>

<pre>
面接(めんせつ)には少(すこ)し早(はや)めに着(つ)く<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
For an interview you're best off arriving a little early.
</pre>

<pre>
お金(かね)はある<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>が、多(おお)ければ幸(しあわ)せというものでもない。
Money is better to have than not, but more of it doesn't automatically mean happiness.
</pre>

<pre>
説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)まなくても動(うご)くが、一度(いちど)目(め)を通(とお)しておく<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
It runs without reading the manual, but you may as well glance through it once.
</pre>

<pre>
地震(じしん)は来(こ)ないかもしれないが、水(みず)と電池(でんち)を用意(ようい)しておく<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
The earthquake may never come, but there's no harm in keeping water and batteries ready.
</pre>

<pre>
相手(あいて)が怒(おこ)っていないとしても、早(はや)めに一言(ひとこと)謝(あやま)っておく<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>でしょう。
Even if she isn't angry, a quick word of apology early on can only help.
</pre>

<pre>
英語(えいご)ができなくても仕事(しごと)は回(まわ)るが、できる<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>というのが部長(ぶちょう)の考(かんが)えだ。
The work gets done without English, but the manager's view is that being able to speak it can only be a plus.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is calm, sensible and slightly proverbial. It is the voice of prudence:
insurance, deadlines, spare batteries, arriving early. It appears in both speech and
writing and is neutral in politeness -
<code>に越(こ)したことはありません</code> is completely at home in a business email.

Two things make it distinctive. First, it is **not an obligation**. It says the option is
preferable, not that it is required, so it can be used precisely to avoid pushing:
telling a colleague <code>確認(かくにん)しておくに越(こ)したことはない</code>
suggests without ordering. That softness is a genuine reason to reach for it.

Second, it **accepts that the ideal may be out of reach**. This is why the
<code>が</code> continuation is so common: <code>安(やす)いに越(こ)したことはないが、質(しつ)も大事(だいじ)だ</code>.
A speaker who says only the first half has already implied the second.

It does not sound old-fashioned, and it is not marked for age or gender. It is a little
more considered than everyday chat - <code>早(はや)いほうがいいよ</code> is what you
say to a friend running late - but nobody would call it stiff.

## Contrast

### に越(こ)したことはない vs に限(かぎ)る

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> crowns **one winner** and closes the
discussion. <code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> ranks an option **at the top of a
scale** and leaves the rest of the scale in place.

</div>

<pre>
夏(なつ)の昼(ひる)ごはんは、そうめん<b>に限(かぎ)る</b>。
For lunch in summer it has to be sōmen.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)ごはんは軽(かる)い<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>が、午後(ごご)まで持(も)たないと困(こま)る。
A light lunch is ideal, though it's no good if it doesn't see you through the afternoon.
</pre>

Three practical differences follow. <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> takes only nouns and
verbs; <code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> takes adjectives too. The first is a
personal declaration, the second a piece of general prudence. And only the second invites
a qualification.

### に越(こ)したことはない vs ～ほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ほうがいい</code> recommends **to a person**.
<code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> observes that something **is generally
preferable**, with nobody in particular addressed.

</div>

<pre>
傘(かさ)を持(も)っていった<b>ほうがいいですよ</b>。
You'd better take an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
折(お)りたたみ傘(かさ)は一本(いっぽん)かばんに入(い)れておく<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
Keeping a folding umbrella in your bag is never a bad idea.
</pre>

The first is aimed at the person standing in front of you and can, said flatly, sound
like an instruction. The second is a maxim, so it applies the pressure of common sense
rather than of the speaker. When you want to nudge a superior, the second is much safer.

### に越(こ)したことはない vs なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> is **desirable**.
<code>なければならない</code> is **required**. They are not two strengths of the same
thing; one of them permits the alternative and the other does not.

</div>

<pre>
書類(しょるい)は前日(ぜんじつ)までに出(だ)す<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
It's best to hand the documents in by the day before.
</pre>

<pre>
書類(しょるい)は前日(ぜんじつ)までに出(だ)さ<b>なければなりません</b>。
The documents must be handed in by the day before.
</pre>

If missing the date has consequences, only the second is honest. Using
<code>に越(こ)したことはない</code> for a hard rule is a real workplace hazard: the
listener will hear "if you get round to it".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 健康(けんこう)<b>だに越(こ)したことはない</b>。 → ✅ 健康(けんこう)<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。／健康(けんこう)<b>であるに越(こ)したことはない</b>。
  A noun attaches bare or with <code>である</code>. The plain <code>だ</code> cannot
  sit in front of <code>に</code>.

- ❌ 安(やす)い<b>に越(こ)したことがない</b>。 → ✅ 安(やす)い<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
  The <code>は</code> is part of the fixed phrase. Swapping in <code>が</code> is a
  very common slip and changes it into something that means nothing.

- ❌ 早(はや)めに出(で)る<b>に越(こ)えたことはない</b>。 → ✅ 早(はや)めに出(で)る<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。
  The verb is <code>越(こ)す</code>. <code>越(こ)える</code> is a different verb
  and does not appear in this phrase.

- ❌ パスポートは必(かなら)ず持(も)っていく<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。 → ✅ パスポートは必(かなら)ず持(も)っていか<b>なければならない</b>。
  <code>必(かなら)ず</code> announces a requirement, and this pattern only ever
  expresses a preference. The two contradict each other in the same sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 旅行(りょこう)の保険(ほけん)は使(つか)わないかもしれないが、入(はい)っておく____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

入(はい)っておく<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>。

A verb attaches in the dictionary form, and <code>～ておく</code> fits the pattern's
precautionary flavour perfectly. The first half already concedes the insurance may be
wasted, which is exactly the hedged setting this pattern lives in.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 会場(かいじょう)には開演(かいえん)の三十分(さんじゅっぷん)前(まえ)までに入(はい)ら（なければなりません・に越(こ)したことはありません）。遅(おく)れた方(かた)はご入場(にゅうじょう)いただけません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

開演(かいえん)の三十分(さんじゅっぷん)前(まえ)までに入(はい)ら<b>なければなりません</b>。

The second sentence spells out a consequence for being late, so this is a rule, not a
preference. <code>に越(こ)したことはありません</code> would tell the audience the
deadline is optional and then punish them for believing it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "A bag is better light than heavy, but you don't want to leave the charger behind."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

かばんは軽(かる)い<b>に越(こ)したことはない</b>が、充電器(じゅうでんき)を置(お)いていくのは困(こま)る。

An い-adjective attaches with no change, and the <code>が</code> clause is the natural
continuation this pattern expects. English "better… but" and the Japanese shape line up
almost word for word.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部屋(へや)は静(しず)かだに越(こ)したことはない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部屋(へや)は<b>静(しず)かであるに越(こ)したことはない</b>。

な-adjectives take <code>である</code> here. The plain <code>だ</code> is a finished
predicate and cannot be followed by <code>に</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 説明(せつめい)は短(みじか)いに越(こ)したことはない。
(b) 説明(せつめい)は短(みじか)いに限(かぎ)る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a general principle offered with a shrug: shorter is better, all else equal, and
the speaker would accept a longer explanation if there were a reason for it.

(b) is a verdict with the speaker's personality in it - brevity wins, end of discussion.
It is also grammatically strained, because <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> does not sit
comfortably after an い-adjective, which is one more reason (a) is what you would
actually write.

</details>

# ～に過ぎない

**Meaning** · no more than ～; merely ～ - the thing does not reach beyond this

<code>過(す)ぎない</code> is "does not exceed". The pattern takes something the
listener might have taken seriously - a figure, a claim, a person's role - and caps it:
*that is as far as it goes*. It is not a neutral count. It is an argument, and the point
of making it is usually to stop someone drawing a bigger conclusion.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + にすぎない | 言(い)い訳(わけ) → 言(い)い訳(わけ)にすぎない |
| Noun (formal) | Noun + である + にすぎない | 一例(いちれい) → 一例(いちれい)であるにすぎない |
| Verb | plain form + にすぎない | 聞(き)いた → 聞(き)いたにすぎない |
| Verb (ている) | ～ている + にすぎない | 真似(まね)している → 真似(まね)しているにすぎない |
| Quantity | number + にすぎない | 三割(さんわり) → 三割(さんわり)にすぎない |
| Polite | … + にすぎません | 想像(そうぞう) → 想像(そうぞう)にすぎません |
| Mid-sentence | … + にすぎず、… | 一部(いちぶ) → 一部(いちぶ)にすぎず、… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - although the page title uses the kanji, running text usually writes
<code>にすぎない</code> in kana. Both are correct; the kana spelling is more common
outside legal and academic writing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
それは言(い)い訳(わけ)<b>にすぎない</b>。
That's nothing but an excuse.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)は見(み)たことを話(はな)した<b>にすぎません</b>。
I merely reported what I saw.
</pre>

<pre>
回答(かいとう)したのは全体(ぜんたい)の三割(さんわり)<b>にすぎない</b>。
Only thirty percent of them responded - no more than that.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)はただの知(し)り合(あ)い<b>にすぎない</b>ので、詳(くわ)しいことは知(し)らない。
He's no more than an acquaintance, so I don't know the details.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の値上(ねあ)げは、これから続(つづ)く変化(へんか)の始(はじ)まり<b>にすぎない</b>だろう。
This price rise is probably just the beginning of changes still to come.
</pre>

<pre>
これは問題(もんだい)の一部(いちぶ)<b>にすぎず</b>、本当(ほんとう)の原因(げんいん)はもっと深(ふか)いところにある。
This is only one part of the problem; the real cause lies deeper.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は自分(じぶん)の仕事(しごと)をした<b>にすぎない</b>と言(い)うが、あの場面(ばめん)で動(うご)けた人(ひと)はほかにいなかった。
She says she was only doing her job, but nobody else in that moment was able to act.
</pre>

<pre>
統計(とうけい)の数字(すうじ)は現実(げんじつ)の一面(いちめん)を示(しめ)している<b>にすぎず</b>、そこから人(ひと)の暮(く)らしまでは見(み)えてこない。
Statistics show no more than one face of reality; they do not reveal how people actually live.
</pre>

<pre>
多(おお)くの人(ひと)が反対(はんたい)していると言(い)っても、実際(じっさい)に声(こえ)を上(あ)げているのは一部(いちぶ)の人(ひと)たち<b>にすぎません</b>。
People say there is widespread opposition, but those actually raising their voices are only a small number.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a written-leaning, argumentative pattern. Editorials, essays, research summaries
and formal speeches are full of it, because its job is to *deflate a claim* - to say that
a figure is smaller, a person less important, an event less significant than it was made
to look. It appears in speech too, but in speech it sounds considered rather than casual;
you would not use it complaining about the weather.

Because it deflates, it is dismissive by nature. Applied to your own actions it is modest
- <code>当然(とうぜん)のことをしたにすぎません</code> is a graceful way to wave off
thanks. Applied to someone else's work or opinion it is dismissive, and to their face it
is rude. <code>ご意見(いけん)は一(ひと)つの見方(みかた)にすぎません</code>
would be heard as a put-down whatever your tone.

The connective form <code>にすぎず</code> is a written-style linker (the classical
negative <code>ず</code> doing the work of a て-form). It signals essay register very
strongly and would be odd in conversation.

<code>ただの～にすぎない</code> is an extremely common reinforcement, and so is putting
a bare number in front of it. Both are fine and neither is redundant.

## Contrast

### にすぎない vs だけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけ</code> **counts**. <code>にすぎない</code>
**judges**: it says the amount or the role falls short of what someone assumed.

</div>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は五人(ごにん)<b>でした</b>。
There were five participants.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)は五人(ごにん)<b>にすぎませんでした</b>。
There were no more than five participants.
</pre>

The first is a count. The second implies a disappointment - five, when the hall was
booked for fifty. This is why <code>にすぎない</code> needs a background expectation to
push against; used where nobody expected anything in particular, it puzzles the reader.
It is also a register step up: <code>だけ</code> belongs to every level of Japanese,
<code>にすぎない</code> to careful prose.

### にすぎない vs でしかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にすぎない</code> caps a **quantity or degree** and reasons
about it. <code>でしかない</code> caps an **identity** and does it with feeling.

</div>

<pre>
支持率(しじりつ)は一割(いちわり)<b>にすぎない</b>。
Support stands at no more than ten percent.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)の計画(けいかく)は時間(じかん)の無駄(むだ)<b>でしかない</b>。
The plan as it stands is nothing but a waste of time.
</pre>

Swap them and you can feel the change: a percentage with <code>でしかない</code> sounds
angry, and a flat verdict with <code>にすぎない</code> sounds analytical. Numbers and
verbs go to <code>にすぎない</code> - <code>でしかない</code> cannot take a plain verb
at all. Where both are possible, <code>にすぎない</code> is the cooler of the two.

### にすぎない vs にほかならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same move in opposite directions. Both say "X is Y and nothing
else"; <code>にすぎない</code> **demotes** X, <code>にほかならない</code> **elevates**
it.

</div>

<pre>
あの発言(はつげん)は個人(こじん)の感想(かんそう)<b>にすぎない</b>。
That remark was no more than one person's impression.
</pre>

<pre>
あの発言(はつげん)は会社(かいしゃ)の本音(ほんね)<b>にほかならない</b>。
That remark was nothing other than the company's real position.
</pre>

Grammatically they are twins - both take a noun and <code>に</code>. Rhetorically they
are opposites, and mixing them up reverses your argument, which makes this the more
dangerous confusion of the two on this page.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ それは冗談(じょうだん)<b>をすぎない</b>。 → ✅ それは冗談(じょうだん)<b>にすぎない</b>。
  The particle is <code>に</code>. <code>を</code> and <code>が</code> both turn up in
  learner writing and neither is possible.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はまだ学生(がくせい)<b>だにすぎない</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はまだ学生(がくせい)<b>にすぎない</b>。
  A noun attaches bare, or with <code>である</code> in formal writing. Never with
  <code>だ</code>.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はアマチュア<b>にすぎる</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はアマチュア<b>にすぎない</b>。
  <code>～すぎる</code> ("too much") and <code>にすぎない</code> ("no more than") are
  unrelated despite sharing a verb. The negative is not optional here.

- ❌ 「先生(せんせい)のご指摘(してき)は一(ひと)つの見方(みかた)<b>にすぎません</b>」 → ✅ 「先生(せんせい)のご指摘(してき)は大変(たいへん)参考(さんこう)になりました」
  Perfect grammar, badly misjudged. The pattern shrinks whatever it touches, so pointing
  it at a senior person's opinion is a rebuke.

## Practice

**1.** 事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)についての話(はなし)は、まだ____過(す)ぎない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

まだ<b>うわさに</b>過(す)ぎない。

A noun attaches directly with <code>に</code>. The sentence is warning the listener not
to treat the story as established - exactly the deflating job this pattern does.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 昨日(きのう)の会議(かいぎ)で決(き)まったのは日程(にってい)（だけ・にすぎない）です。あとは全部(ぜんぶ)来週(らいしゅう)に回(まわ)りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

決(き)まったのは日程(にってい)<b>だけ</b>です。

<code>にすぎない</code> cannot be followed by <code>です</code> - it is already a
predicate. If you want the deflating flavour, the sentence has to be rebuilt as
<code>決(き)まったのは日程(にってい)にすぎません</code>, which adds a note of
frustration that plain <code>だけ</code> does not carry.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "What I did was merely pass on what I had been told."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

私(わたし)は聞(き)いたことを伝(つた)えた<b>にすぎません</b>。

A verb attaches in its plain form, here the past. Used about yourself the pattern reads
as modest rather than dismissive - it declines credit for anything beyond the message.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この数字(すうじ)は去年(きょねん)の半分(はんぶん)にすぎなくて、安心(あんしん)できない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この数字(すうじ)は去年(きょねん)の半分(はんぶん)<b>にすぎず</b>、安心(あんしん)できない。

In this register the connective form is <code>にすぎず</code>, not
<code>にすぎなくて</code>. The <code>て</code>-form of <code>ない</code> sounds
conversational and clashes with the essay-like tone of the rest of the sentence.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 私(わたし)は手伝(てつだ)っただけです。
(b) 私(わたし)は手伝(てつだ)ったにすぎません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply states what you did. It is what you would say when someone asks whether you
organised the event, and it carries no particular attitude.

(b) actively pushes back on the credit you are being given: whatever it looked like, your
role did not extend beyond helping. It is more formal, more deliberate, and slightly more
insistent - the sentence of someone declining praise rather than answering a question.

</details>

# ～でしかない

**Meaning** · is nothing but ～; is merely ～ and nothing more

Take it apart and it is transparent: <code>で</code> (the copula) +
<code>しか</code> + <code>ない</code>. "It is only ～ and is not anything else." The
<code>しか～ない</code> you already know is doing all the work, and it brings its
emotional colouring with it. <code>でしかない</code> does not measure - it **verdicts**.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + でしかない | 無駄(むだ) → 無駄(むだ)でしかない |
| な-adjective (stem) | stem + でしかない | 迷惑(めいわく) → 迷惑(めいわく)でしかない |
| Nominalised clause | ～こと／もの + でしかない | 逃(に)げること → 逃(に)げることでしかない |
| Polite | … + でしかありません | 想像(そうぞう) → 想像(そうぞう)でしかありません |
| Past | … + でしかなかった | 夢(ゆめ) → 夢(ゆめ)でしかなかった |
| Mid-sentence | … + でしかなく、… | 通過点(つうかてん) → 通過点(つうかてん)でしかなく、… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a plain verb cannot attach. <code>行(い)くでしかない</code> is not
Japanese. To put an action inside the pattern, nominalise it
(<code>行(い)くことでしかない</code>) or switch to
<code>行(い)ったにすぎない</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そんなやり方(かた)は時間(じかん)の無駄(むだ)<b>でしかない</b>。
Doing it that way is nothing but a waste of time.
</pre>

<pre>
今(いま)の私(わたし)にとって、それはただの夢(ゆめ)<b>でしかない</b>。
For me as I am now, that's no more than a daydream.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の言(い)っていることは自己満足(じこまんぞく)<b>でしかない</b>と思(おも)う。
What he's saying strikes me as pure self-indulgence and nothing else.
</pre>

<pre>
数字(すうじ)を並(なら)べただけの報告(ほうこく)は、上司(じょうし)にとっては迷惑(めいわく)<b>でしかありません</b>。
A report that just lines up figures is nothing but a nuisance to a manager.
</pre>

<pre>
あのときの約束(やくそく)は、今(いま)となっては苦(にが)い思(おも)い出(で)<b>でしかない</b>。
That promise is nothing now but a bitter memory.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)なしに始(はじ)めた計画(けいかく)は、周(まわ)りを巻(ま)き込(こ)む混乱(こんらん)<b>でしかなかった</b>。
A plan launched without preparation turned out to be nothing but chaos that dragged everyone in.
</pre>

<pre>
本人(ほんにん)は親切(しんせつ)のつもりでも、相手(あいて)にとっては余計(よけい)な口出(くちだ)し<b>でしかない</b>ことがある。
You may mean it kindly, but to the other person it can be nothing but unwanted interference.
</pre>

<pre>
データのない議論(ぎろん)は、どんなに熱(あつ)く語(かた)っても個人(こじん)の感想(かんそう)<b>でしかない</b>。
An argument without data is nothing but a personal impression, however passionately you make it.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)にとって昇進(しょうしん)は通過点(つうかてん)<b>でしかなく</b>、本当(ほんとう)の目標(もくひょう)はもっと先(さき)にある。
For her the promotion is nothing more than a waypoint; her real goal lies much further on.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>でしかない</code> is emphatic and slightly hot. Because it inherits
<code>しか～ない</code>, it carries that particle's built-in sense of *insufficiency* -
whatever is being named, it falls short. Add the flat copula and you get a verdict: this
is what the thing is, and it is not enough.

That makes it a favourite of opinion writing, reviews, editorials and argumentative
speech, and it is extremely common online. It is a shade more modern and more emotive
than <code>にすぎない</code>, which does the same work in a cooler voice. Neither is
casual chat, but <code>でしかない</code> can appear in an impassioned spoken argument in
a way <code>にすぎず</code> never would.

Two consequences follow for use. First, it is **strong**. Saying
<code>それは言(い)い訳(わけ)でしかありません</code> to a colleague is close to
calling them dishonest; the polite ending does not soften the content. Second, it needs a
**standard to fall short of**. The sentence works because the listener can supply what
the thing *ought* to have been - a real plan, a real argument, real kindness. Without
that background the verdict sounds arbitrary.

There is no casual contraction, and it is not marked for age or gender. What is marked is
temperature.

## Contrast

### でしかない vs にすぎない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different heat.
<code>にすぎない</code> is analytical and takes numbers and verbs;
<code>でしかない</code> is evaluative and takes nouns.

</div>

<pre>
賛成(さんせい)したのは委員(いいん)の半分(はんぶん)<b>にすぎなかった</b>。
No more than half the committee voted in favour.
</pre>

<pre>
中身(なかみ)のない議論(ぎろん)は時間(じかん)のむだづかい<b>でしかなかった</b>。
A discussion with nothing in it was pure squandering of time.
</pre>

Try the swap and the mismatch is audible. A committee count with
<code>でしかなかった</code> sounds like the speaker is furious about it; an angry verdict
with <code>にすぎなかった</code> sounds like a footnote. Also note the grammar: a plain
verb goes only with <code>にすぎない</code>.

### でしかない vs しかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>しかない</code> after a **verb** means "there's no choice but
to". <code>でしかない</code> after a **noun** means "is nothing but". The
<code>で</code> is the whole difference.

</div>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)が行(い)ってしまった。歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)る<b>しかない</b>。
The last train's gone. There's nothing for it but to walk home.
</pre>

<pre>
この案(あん)は問題(もんだい)の先送(さきおく)り<b>でしかない</b>。
This proposal is nothing but kicking the problem down the road.
</pre>

Learners produce <code>歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)るでしかない</code> regularly, and it
is not Japanese. Check what precedes: a verb takes bare
<code>しかない</code>, a noun takes <code>でしかない</code>.

### でしかない vs ではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ではない</code> **rejects** the label.
<code>でしかない</code> **accepts** it and caps it: yes, it is that, and that is all it
is.

</div>

<pre>
これは一時的(いちじてき)な対応(たいおう)<b>ではない</b>。根本(こんぽん)から変(か)えるつもりだ。
This is not a stopgap. We intend to change it at the root.
</pre>

<pre>
これは一時的(いちじてき)な対応(たいおう)<b>でしかない</b>。根本(こんぽん)の問題(もんだい)は残(のこ)ったままだ。
This is nothing but a stopgap. The underlying problem is still there.
</pre>

Same noun, opposite claims, one syllable apart. This is worth reading aloud a few times,
because in fast speech the difference between <code>では</code> and
<code>でしか</code> is easy to miss and the meanings are contradictory.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 無駄(むだ)<b>だでしかない</b>。 → ✅ 無駄(むだ)<b>でしかない</b>。
  <code>で</code> is already the copula. Adding <code>だ</code> in front doubles it.

- ❌ 待(ま)つ<b>でしかない</b>。 → ✅ 待(ま)つ<b>しかない</b>。
  A verb takes <code>しかない</code> on its own. With <code>で</code> the sentence
  becomes ungrammatical, and the meaning you wanted ("no choice but to wait") is a
  different pattern anyway.

- ❌ この絵(え)は<b>美(うつく)しいでしかない</b>。 → ✅ この絵(え)は<b>美(うつく)しいだけだ</b>。
  い-adjectives do not attach. Only nouns and な-adjective stems do.

- ❌ 「お客様(きゃくさま)のご意見(いけん)は個人(こじん)の感想(かんそう)<b>でしかありません</b>」 → ✅ 「貴重(きちょう)なご意見(いけん)をありがとうございます」
  The polite ending does not make the content polite. The pattern dismisses what it
  names, and a customer will hear exactly that.

## Practice

**1.** どんなに立派(りっぱ)な計画(けいかく)でも、実行(じっこう)しなければ紙(かみ)の上(うえ)の話(はなし)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

紙(かみ)の上(うえ)の話(はなし)<b>でしかない</b>。

A noun phrase attaches directly. The first half sets up the standard the plan fails to
meet, which is the background this pattern needs in order to land.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まっているので、タクシーを使(つか)う（しかない・でしかない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

タクシーを使(つか)う<b>しかない</b>。

<code>使(つか)う</code> is a verb, so it takes bare <code>しかない</code> and means
"no choice but to". <code>でしかない</code> needs a noun in front of it and would be
making a judgement about what taxis *are*, which is not the point here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Without evidence, it's nothing but speculation."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

証拠(しょうこ)がなければ、それは推測(すいそく)<b>でしかない</b>。

The English "nothing but" maps straight onto the pattern, and
<code>推測(すいそく)</code> is a noun so it attaches with no change.
<code>にすぎない</code> would also be grammatical and would sound cooler and more
academic.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)のやり方(かた)は乱暴(らんぼう)だでしかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)のやり方(かた)は乱暴(らんぼう)<b>でしかない</b>。

<code>乱暴(らんぼう)</code> is a な-adjective, and the pattern takes the bare stem.
The <code>で</code> in <code>でしかない</code> *is* the copula, so
<code>だ</code> has nothing left to do.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この方法(ほうほう)は効率(こうりつ)が悪(わる)いにすぎない。
(b) この方法(ほうほう)は効率(こうりつ)が悪(わる)いやり方(かた)でしかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reads as a cool qualification: the method's only shortcoming is inefficiency, so
nothing worse is being alleged. It reduces the size of the complaint.

(b) reads as a rejection: the method is *that and nothing else*, so there is no reason to
keep it. The first is a concession made in passing; the second is the sentence you write
when you want the method dropped.

</details>

# ～にほかならない

**Meaning** · is nothing other than ～; is precisely ～ and nothing else

<code>ほか</code> is "other"; <code>ほかならない</code> is "there is no other". The
pattern asserts an identity and slams the door on alternatives: whatever else you might
have thought this was, it is **this**. It is the strongest way in ordinary Japanese to
say "the reason is X" or "X is exactly what Y amounts to".

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + にほかならない | 証拠(しょうこ) → 証拠(しょうこ)にほかならない |
| Noun (written) | Noun + である + にほかならない | 結果(けっか) → 結果(けっか)であるにほかならない |
| Reason clause | plain form + から + にほかならない | 続(つづ)けた → 続(つづ)けたからにほかならない |
| Purpose clause | ～ため + にほかならない | 守(まも)るため → 守(まも)るためにほかならない |
| Nominalised clause | ～こと + にほかならない | 逃(に)げること → 逃(に)げることにほかならない |
| Polite | … + にほかなりません | 努力(どりょく)の結果(けっか) → …にほかなりません |
| Mid-sentence | … + にほかならず、… | 一部(いちぶ) → 一部(いちぶ)にほかならず、… |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ほかならない</code> is usually written in kana. The kanji form
<code>他(ほか)ならない</code> exists and turns up in older or heavier prose; both are
read the same way.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
言葉(ことば)は文化(ぶんか)そのもの<b>にほかならない</b>。
Language is nothing other than culture itself.
</pre>

<pre>
この成功(せいこう)は皆(みな)さんの協力(きょうりょく)の結果(けっか)<b>にほかなりません</b>。
This success is due to nothing other than everyone's cooperation.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が合格(ごうかく)できたのは、毎日(まいにち)机(つくえ)に向(む)かい続(つづ)けたから<b>にほかならない</b>。
He passed for one reason only: he sat down at that desk every single day.
</pre>

<pre>
値段(ねだん)が上(あ)がったのは、材料(ざいりょう)が不足(ふそく)しているから<b>にほかならない</b>。
The price went up for no other reason than a shortage of materials.
</pre>

<pre>
環境(かんきょう)を守(まも)ることは、自分(じぶん)たちの生活(せいかつ)を守(まも)ること<b>にほかならない</b>。
Protecting the environment is nothing other than protecting our own lives.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の改革(かいかく)が進(すす)まないのは、現場(げんば)の声(こえ)を聞(き)いてこなかったから<b>にほかならない</b>。
The reform has stalled for one reason and one reason only: nobody listened to the people doing the work.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)が厳(きび)しいことを言(い)うのは、後輩(こうはい)に育(そだ)ってほしいと願(ねが)っているから<b>にほかなりません</b>。
If she says harsh things, it is purely because she wants her juniors to grow.
</pre>

<pre>
翻訳(ほんやく)とは、別(べつ)の言葉(ことば)でもう一度(いちど)書(か)くこと<b>にほかならず</b>、単(たん)なる置(お)き換(か)えではない。
Translation is nothing other than writing the thing again in another language; it is not mere substitution.
</pre>

<pre>
この町(まち)が静(しず)かになったのは、若(わか)い人(ひと)が次々(つぎつぎ)に出(で)て行(い)ってしまったから<b>にほかならない</b>。
The town has gone quiet for exactly one reason: the young people have left, one after another.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written, declarative** pattern. Editorials, speeches, prefaces, academic
argument, the closing paragraph of a report - places where the writer wants to nail a
conclusion down. It sounds authoritative, and in a casual conversation it sounds like
someone has stepped up to a lectern. Nothing is wrong with saying it aloud; it just
announces that you are being serious.

The single most useful shape is <code>～のは～からにほかならない</code>: a topic clause,
then the reason, then the pattern. It is the standard way to insist that a cause is *the*
cause. Read it as "the reason … is nothing other than …".

<code>にほかなりません</code> is the polite form and is common in speeches of thanks,
where the assertive edge turns into warmth:
<code>皆様(みなさま)のおかげにほかなりません</code> gives credit emphatically rather
than merely politely. That is one of the few settings where the pattern is genuinely
common in speech.

One limit worth remembering: it asserts something the speaker treats as **established**,
not something being guessed at. It cannot be used for predictions, and it cannot be
softened - there is no <code>にほかならないかもしれない</code>. If you are not sure,
this is the wrong pattern.

## Contrast

### にほかならない vs にすぎない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical grammar, opposite rhetoric. Both cap X at Y;
<code>にほかならない</code> makes Y **the whole significant truth**,
<code>にすぎない</code> makes Y **disappointingly small**.

</div>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の受賞(じゅしょう)は長年(ながねん)の努力(どりょく)の結果(けっか)<b>にほかならない</b>。
This award is the outcome of nothing other than years of effort.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)の受賞(じゅしょう)は運(うん)<b>にすぎない</b>。
This award was no more than luck.
</pre>

The two sentences use the same frame to praise and to belittle the same event. If you
reach for the wrong one in a speech of thanks, you will have told the audience the prize
was meaningless.

### にほかならない vs に違(ちが)いない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に違(ちが)いない</code> is a **confident guess** about
something unverified. <code>にほかならない</code> is an **assertion** about something the
speaker treats as settled.

</div>

<pre>
この字(じ)を書(か)いたのは兄(あに)<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
My older brother must be the one who wrote this.
</pre>

<pre>
この字(じ)のくせは兄(あに)が急(いそ)いで書(か)いた証拠(しょうこ)<b>にほかならない</b>。
The way these letters are formed is proof of nothing other than my brother writing in a hurry.
</pre>

The first admits it is an inference and could be wrong. The second presents the
conclusion as already reached and invites no argument. That is why
<code>に違(ちが)いない</code> fits a mystery and
<code>にほかならない</code> fits a summing-up.

### ～からにほかならない vs ～からだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>からだ</code> gives **a** reason.
<code>からにほかならない</code> gives **the** reason and excludes the others.

</div>

<pre>
試合(しあい)に負(ま)けたのは練習(れんしゅう)が足(た)りなかった<b>からだ</b>。
We lost because we hadn't trained enough.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)に負(ま)けたのは練習(れんしゅう)が足(た)りなかった<b>からにほかならない</b>。
We lost for one reason only - we hadn't trained enough.
</pre>

The second shuts down excuses about the weather, the referee and the pitch before anyone
raises them. That is its whole function, and it is why the pattern turns up so often in
post-mortems and opinion pieces.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 彼(かれ)は責任者(せきにんしゃ)<b>だにほかならない</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)は責任者(せきにんしゃ)<b>にほかならない</b>。
  A noun attaches bare, or with <code>である</code>. The plain <code>だ</code> cannot
  precede <code>に</code>.

- ❌ 努力(どりょく)した<b>にほかならない</b>。 → ✅ 努力(どりょく)した<b>からにほかならない</b>。
  A clause needs <code>から</code> (or <code>ため</code>, or a nominaliser). The
  pattern attaches to nouns, not to bare verbs.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>にほかならない</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。
  Predictions are guesses. This pattern only states what the speaker treats as
  established fact.

- ❌ 「そのシャツ、似合(にあ)っているのはセンスがいいから<b>にほかならないよ</b>」 → ✅ 「そのシャツ似合(にあ)うね。センスいいんだね」
  Grammatical, but declaiming over a compliment is comic. Keep the pattern for writing
  and for speeches.

## Practice

**1.** この制度(せいど)が長(なが)く続(つづ)いてきたのは、多(おお)くの人(ひと)に支(ささ)えられてきた____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

支(ささ)えられてきた<b>からにほかならない</b>。

The topic clause with <code>のは</code> at the front is the signal: this is the
"the reason is nothing other than" frame, and a clause needs <code>から</code> before
the pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: あの音(おと)からすると、二階(にかい)にだれかいる（にほかならない・に違(ちが)いない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

二階(にかい)にだれかいる<b>に違(ちが)いない</b>。

<code>からすると</code> announces that you are reasoning from evidence towards an
unverified conclusion, which is the guess pattern.
<code>にほかならない</code> would claim the matter was already settled, and it would need
a noun in front of it besides.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "To keep silent here is nothing other than to approve of it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ここで黙(だま)っていることは、認(みと)めること<b>にほかならない</b>。

Both halves are nominalised with <code>こと</code>, which is what lets the pattern
attach. The English "nothing other than" is carried by the pattern alone; no extra
adverb is needed.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会社(かいしゃ)が立(た)ち直(なお)ったのは、社員(しゃいん)が努力(どりょく)したにほかなりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会社(かいしゃ)が立(た)ち直(なお)ったのは、社員(しゃいん)が努力(どりょく)した<b>からにほかなりません</b>。

The <code>のは</code> at the front sets up a reason, so the second half has to be a
reason too. Without <code>から</code> the sentence tries to equate an event with a
verb phrase and stalls.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この結果(けっか)は準備(じゅんび)の差(さ)にほかならない。
(b) この結果(けっか)は準備(じゅんび)の差(さ)だと思(おも)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the explanation as settled and rules out competing accounts. It is the
sentence of a report or a post-match analysis, and it leaves the listener no room to
offer an alternative without contradicting you.

(b) offers the same content as an opinion. It is softer, less formal, and invites
discussion - which makes it the safer thing to say in a meeting where other people were
also involved in the preparation.

</details>

# ～のみ

**Meaning** · only ～ - the written, formal counterpart of だけ

<code>のみ</code> means exactly what <code>だけ</code> means. That is the honest
starting point: there is no nuance hiding here that <code>だけ</code> lacks. What
differs is **register**. <code>のみ</code> is the word that appears on notices, in
contracts, in academic prose and in headlines, and reading it aloud in a normal
conversation sounds like quoting a document at your friend.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + のみ | 会員(かいいん) → 会員(かいいん)のみ |
| Noun + case particle | Noun + に／で／から + のみ | 書面(しょめん) → 書面(しょめん)でのみ |
| Noun (subject / object) | Noun + のみが／のみを | 事実(じじつ) → 事実(じじつ)のみを |
| Modifying a noun | Noun + のみの | 一回(いっかい) → 一回(いっかい)のみの |
| Verb | plain form + のみだ | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つのみだ |
| Polite | … + のみです／のみとなります | 現金(げんきん) → 現金(げんきん)のみとなります |
| Fixed emphatic shape | ただ … のみ | 祈(いの)る → ただ祈(いの)るのみ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>のみ</code> attaches to nouns and to plain verb forms. It does not sit
after an い-adjective: <code>安(やす)いのみだ</code> is wrong, and
<code>安(やす)いだけだ</code> is what you want. The one adjective-friendly shape is
the literary <code>のみならず</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この割引(わりびき)は会員(かいいん)<b>のみ</b>有効(ゆうこう)です。
This discount is valid for members only.
</pre>

あとは結果(けっか)を待(ま)つ<b>のみ</b>だ。
All that's left now is to wait for the result.
</pre>

Applications are accepted online only.

</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everything about <code>のみ</code> is register. It is a survival from classical Japanese
and it has stayed in the written language: signage, terms and conditions, official
notices, academic writing, formal announcements. In those places it is not stiff at all -
it is simply correct, and <code>だけ</code> in a contract would look slack.

In speech it is a different matter. <code>昨日(きのう)はラーメンのみ食(た)べた</code>
is grammatical and sounds absurd, in roughly the way "I consumed solely ramen" sounds in
English. Spoken Japanese does use it, but in marked places: staff announcements
(<code>本日(ほんじつ)は現金(げんきん)のみとなります</code>), formal presentations,
and the dramatic <code>ただ～のみだ</code>, which is why sports commentary and
determination speeches reach for <code>あとは走(はし)るのみ</code>.

That <code>ただ～のみ</code> shape is worth learning as a unit. It concentrates the
sentence on one remaining action and carries real weight -
<code>ただ祈(いの)るのみです</code> is what someone says when nothing else can be
done. Note that this is emphasis, not extra meaning: it is still just "only".

There is also <code>のみならず</code> ("not only… but"), the literary sibling of
<code>だけでなく</code>, which has its own page. Recognise it; you will meet it in
editorials.

Practical advice: **read** <code>のみ</code> everywhere, **write** it in formal
documents, and in conversation use <code>だけ</code> unless you are deliberately
quoting a rule.

## Contrast

### のみ vs だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning. <code>だけ</code> works at every level of the
language; <code>のみ</code> belongs to the written and the formal, and it attaches to
fewer things.

</div>

<pre>
残(のこ)っているのはこの一枚(いちまい)<b>だけ</b>だよ。
This one sheet is all that's left.
</pre>

<pre>
残部(ざんぶ)は一部(いちぶ)<b>のみ</b>となっております。
Only one copy remains.
</pre>

Both sentences say the same thing about the same piece of paper; one is a friend
speaking, the other a shop assistant. Note also the attachment difference:
<code>だけ</code> follows adjectives and many more shapes than <code>のみ</code> does,
so when in doubt <code>だけ</code> is always available and <code>のみ</code> is not.

### のみ vs しか～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のみ</code> states the restriction **positively and
neutrally**. <code>しか～ない</code> states it **negatively**, and adds a feeling that
it is not enough.

</div>

<pre>
お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)<b>のみ</b>承(うけたまわ)ります。
Payment is accepted in cash only.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)には千円(せんえん)<b>しか</b>入(はい)っていない。
There's only a thousand yen in my wallet.
</pre>

The first is a policy stated flatly. The second is a small complaint. This is why a shop
sign says <code>現金(げんきん)のみ</code> and never
<code>現金(げんきん)しか使(つか)えない</code> - the second would be siding with the
customer's disappointment.

### のみ vs に限(かぎ)る・に限(かぎ)り

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both appear on notices. <code>のみ</code> **labels** the eligible
group; <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> **states the rule** that restricts it to that group.

</div>

<pre>
関係者(かんけいしゃ)<b>のみ</b>入室(にゅうしつ)可(か)。
Authorised personnel only.
</pre>

<pre>
入室(にゅうしつ)は関係者(かんけいしゃ)<b>に限(かぎ)ります</b>。
Entry is restricted to authorised personnel.
</pre>

<code>のみ</code> is compact enough for a sign or a form field, which is why it wins
where space is tight. <code>に限(かぎ)る</code> is a full predicate and reads as a
regulation being pronounced. Where a document has room for a sentence, it usually chooses
the second.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 土曜日(どようび)<b>のみ</b>遊(あそ)びに行(い)こうよ。 → ✅ 土曜日(どようび)<b>だけ</b>遊(あそ)びに行(い)こうよ。
  An invitation to a friend is spoken Japanese. <code>のみ</code> clashes with the
  casual ending and sounds like a parody of officialese.

- ❌ 会員(かいいん)<b>のみしか</b>入(はい)れません。 → ✅ 会員(かいいん)<b>のみ</b>入(はい)れます。／会員(かいいん)<b>しか</b>入(はい)れません。
  <code>のみ</code> and <code>しか</code> both carry the restriction, so stacking them
  is redundant. Pick one.

- ❌ この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)いのみだ</b>。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は<b>安(やす)いだけだ</b>。
  <code>のみ</code> does not follow an い-adjective. Nouns and plain verb forms only.

- ❌ 試験(しけん)に必要(ひつよう)なのは鉛筆(えんぴつ)<b>のみだけ</b>です。 → ✅ 試験(しけん)に必要(ひつよう)なのは鉛筆(えんぴつ)<b>のみ</b>です。
  Same problem from the other side. The two words are synonyms, not a compound.

## Practice

**1.** ご入場(にゅうじょう)は事前(じぜん)に予約(よやく)された方(かた)____とさせていただきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

予約(よやく)された方(かた)<b>のみ</b>とさせていただきます。

<code>方(かた)</code> is a noun, so the particle attaches directly, and the formal
<code>とさせていただきます</code> around it is exactly the register
<code>のみ</code> belongs to. <code>だけ</code> would work grammatically but would sit
oddly against the humble ending.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 「ごめん、今日(きょう)の昼(ひる)は（おにぎりのみ・おにぎりだけ）食(た)べたんだ」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「今日(きょう)の昼(ひる)はおにぎり<b>だけ</b>食(た)べたんだ」

Casual speech, casual particle. <code>のみ</code> would be grammatical and would make
the speaker sound as though they were filing a report on their own lunch.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This offer is available to first-time customers only."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この特典(とくてん)は初(はじ)めてご利用(りよう)のお客様(きゃくさま)<b>のみ</b>対象(たいしょう)となります。

Promotional copy is written Japanese, so <code>のみ</code> is the natural choice. The
noun phrase in front of it can be as long as you like; the particle simply follows it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あとは結果(けっか)を待(ま)つのみしかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あとは結果(けっか)を待(ま)つ<b>のみだ</b>。（or あとは結果(けっか)を待(ま)つ<b>しかない</b>）

<code>のみ</code> and <code>しかない</code> are both doing the restricting, so the
sentence says it twice. The first version is the literary "all that remains is to wait";
the second is the plainer "there's nothing to do but wait".

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この資料(しりょう)は社内(しゃない)のみで使用(しよう)してください。
(b) この資料(しりょう)、社内(しゃない)だけで使(つか)ってね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reads as an instruction attached to the document itself - the sort of line printed in
the footer or written in a covering email. The formality makes it binding in tone, and
nobody would take it personally.

(b) is the same restriction passed on between colleagues who know each other. It relies
on goodwill rather than on policy, and it would look out of place stamped on the page.
The content is identical; only the social setting changes.

</details>
