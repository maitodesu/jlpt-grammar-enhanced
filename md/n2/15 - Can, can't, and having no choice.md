# N2 - Can, can't, and having no choice


---

# ～える・～うる

**Meaning** · it is possible for this to happen; it is conceivable - possibility in principle, not skill

Written 得(う)る, this suffix says that the world allows something, not that a
particular person is good at it. <code>起(お)こり得(う)る</code> means "this
can occur"; it says nothing about anybody's ability. That is the line to hold: for
"I can swim", Japanese uses the potential form, and this pattern would be wrong.

## Formation

`える` and `うる` are the same suffix. It attaches to the **ます-stem** and then
conjugates as a る-verb (a Group 2 / 一段(いちだん) verb) - except that the
dictionary and noun-modifying form has the extra, older shape `うる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + うる / える | 起(お)こる → 起(お)こり得(う)る |
| Dictionary form | ～うる or ～える | 考(かんが)え得(う)る ／ 考(かんが)え得(え)る |
| Before a noun | ～うる / ～える + Noun | 起(お)こり得(う)る事故(じこ) |
| Negative | **～えない only** | あり得(え)ない |
| Past | ～えた | あり得(え)た |
| Polite | ～えます | あり得(え)ます |
| する-verb | Noun + し + うる | 実現(じつげん)する → 実現(じつげん)し得(う)る |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `うる` exists **only** where the verb is not conjugated. The
moment you add anything - a negative, a past, a ます - it becomes `える`:
あり得(え)ない, あり得(え)た, あり得(え)ます. ❌あり<b>うない</b> is the
single commonest error on this page.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どんなミスも起(お)こり<b>得(う)る</b>。
Any mistake at all can happen.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)ることもあり<b>得(え)る</b>。
It could even snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
それは十分(じゅうぶん)あり<b>得(う)る</b>話(はなし)だ。
That's entirely plausible.
</pre>

<pre>
この薬(くすり)は、人(ひと)によって強(つよ)い副作用(ふくさよう)を起(お)こし<b>得(う)る</b>。
In some people this drug can produce serious side effects.
</pre>

<pre>
地震(じしん)は真夜中(まよなか)にも起(お)こり<b>得(う)る</b>ので、枕元(まくらもと)に靴(くつ)を置(お)いている。
An earthquake can strike in the middle of the night, so I keep shoes by my pillow.
</pre>

<pre>
考(かんが)え<b>得(う)る</b>限(かぎ)りの対策(たいさく)は、すでに取(と)ってあります。
We have already taken every countermeasure we could think of.
</pre>

<pre>
報告書(ほうこくしょ)は、あの事故(じこ)は防(ふせ)ぎ<b>得(う)る</b>ものだったと結論(けつろん)づけている。
The report concludes that the accident was one that could have been prevented.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)までに、起(お)こり<b>得(う)る</b>トラブルをすべて洗(あら)い出(だ)しておきたい。
Before the meeting I want to have listed every problem that could arise.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** pattern. It belongs to reports, editorials, manuals, academic
prose and the careful register of a briefing or a news broadcast. In ordinary
conversation it sounds bookish - a speaker who says
<code>起(お)こり得(う)る</code> over coffee is borrowing the voice of a
document.

Two words break that rule completely. <code>あり得(う)る</code> and especially
<code>あり得(え)ない</code> are everyday spoken Japanese;
<code>あり得(え)ない</code> in particular has become an all-purpose exclamation
of disbelief ("no way", "unbelievable"), and it is used that way well beyond its
literal sense, especially by younger speakers. That colloquial life belongs to
<code>あり得(え)ない</code> alone - it does not transfer to
<code>防(ふせ)ぎ得(え)ない</code> or anything else.

Between the two dictionary forms, `うる` is the more formal and more literary, and
it is what you will usually meet in print; `える` is the lighter of the pair. Either
is correct, and no one will misunderstand you. Read aloud,
<code>あり得(う)る</code> and <code>あり得(え)る</code> are both heard.

Finally, note what the pattern does **not** do. It does not grant permission, it
does not report a skill, and it does not predict. It states that a possibility is
open. Because of that it sits comfortably with non-volitional verbs -
起(お)こる, 変(か)わる, なる - and with verbs of thinking -
考(かんが)える, 想像(そうぞう)する, 予測(よそく)する.

## Contrast

### ～うる vs the potential form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the potential form says **this person is able**. `うる` says
**the situation permits it**. Ability belongs to a body and a skill; possibility
belongs to the world.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)なら一日(いちにち)で終(お)わらせられるだろう。
He could probably get it finished in a day.
</pre>

<pre>
これは一日(いちにち)で終(お)わり<b>得(う)る</b>仕事(しごと)ではない。
This is not a job that can be finished in a day.
</pre>

The first is about him; the second is about the job. This is why
❌<code>私(わたし)はピアノを弾(ひ)き得(う)る</code> is wrong - playing the
piano is a skill you either have or do not, so the potential
<code>弾(ひ)ける</code> is the only option.

### ～うる vs ～かもしれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `うる` describes a **property of the situation**: this is the
kind of thing that can occur. `かもしれない` is the **speaker's guess** about one
particular case.

</div>

<pre>
誰(だれ)にでも起(お)こり<b>得(う)る</b>ことです。
It's the kind of thing that can happen to anybody.
</pre>

<pre>
今回(こんかい)も同(おな)じことが起(お)こるかもしれません。
The same thing might happen again this time.
</pre>

The first makes no prediction at all - it can be said calmly about an event the
speaker considers very unlikely. The second commits the speaker to thinking this
particular occasion may go that way. That is also why `うる` attaches to a noun so
easily (<code>起(お)こり得(う)る事故(じこ)</code>) and `かもしれない`
does not.

### ～うる vs ～かねない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both say something is possible, but `かねない` only takes
**unwelcome** outcomes and warns about them. `うる` is neutral and merely opens the
door.

</div>

<pre>
このやり方(かた)では事故(じこ)が起(お)こり<b>得(う)る</b>。
With this method, an accident is possible.
</pre>

<pre>
このやり方(かた)では事故(じこ)が起(お)こりかねない。
With this method, we're asking for an accident.
</pre>

The second is a warning; the first is an observation. The restriction runs one way
only: ❌<code>成功(せいこう)しかねない</code> is impossible because success is not
a danger, while <code>成功(せいこう)し得(う)る</code> is perfectly ordinary.
Note the shapes too - `かねない` looks negative and means "may well", which catches
learners out in both directions.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そんなことはあり**うない**。 → ✅ そんなことはあり<b>得(え)ない</b>。
  `うる` has no conjugated forms. Every form except the plain dictionary and
  noun-modifying one is built on `える`.

- ❌ 私(わたし)は中国語(ちゅうごくご)が話(はな)し<b>得(う)る</b>。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は中国語(ちゅうごくご)が話(はな)せる。
  A personal skill takes the potential form. `うる` would claim that speaking
  Chinese is theoretically possible in this situation, which is not what you mean.

- ❌ 起(お)こる<b>得(う)る</b> → ✅ 起(お)こり<b>得(う)る</b>
  The base is the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ そんな失敗(しっぱい)はあり<b>得(う)ました</b>。 → ✅ そんな失敗(しっぱい)はあり<b>得(え)ました</b>。
  The same rule again, in the past and polite: `うる` cannot carry ます.

## Practice

**1.** 機械(きかい)である以上(いじょう)、故障(こしょう)は誰(だれ)にでも起(お)こり____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

起(お)こり<b>得(う)る</b>（or 起(お)こり得(え)る・起(お)こり得(え)ます）

The sentence is about what a machine makes possible, not about anyone's ability, so
the possibility suffix is right. The plain dictionary form may be either
<code>得(う)る</code> or <code>得(え)る</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 弟(おとうと)は自転車(じてんしゃ)に（乗(の)り得(う)る・乗(の)れる）ようになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

弟(おとうと)は自転車(じてんしゃ)に<b>乗(の)れる</b>ようになった。

Riding a bicycle is an acquired skill sitting in one person's body, which is
exactly what the potential form is for. <code>得(う)る</code> would be claiming
that cycling had become theoretically possible in the world.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "A delay of this size could well happen again, so let's rewrite the plan."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この規模(きぼ)の遅(おく)れは再(ふたた)び起(お)こり<b>得(う)る</b>ので、計画(けいかく)を見直(みなお)しましょう。

The English "could well happen" here is about the nature of the situation, which is
the `うる` sense. Note the noun 遅(おく)れ, from
<code>遅(おく)れる</code> - not the adjective <code>遅(おそ)い</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 専門家(せんもんか)にも予測(よそく)しうなかった事態(じたい)だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 専門家(せんもんか)にも予測(よそく)し<b>得(え)なかった</b>事態(じたい)だ。
"It was a situation that not even the experts could have foreseen."

The negative past has to be built on `える`. There is no ❌<code>うない</code> and
no ❌<code>うなかった</code> anywhere in the paradigm.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) その計画(けいかく)は失敗(しっぱい)し<b>得(う)る</b>。
(b) その計画(けいかく)は失敗(しっぱい)するかもしれない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an assessment of the plan itself: failure is one of the outcomes it allows.
It is cool and analytical, the sentence of a written risk report, and it does not
say the speaker expects failure.

(b) is the speaker's own hunch about how things will actually turn out. It is
ordinary spoken Japanese, and it does lean towards expecting trouble. Say (a) in a
meeting and you have named a risk; say (b) and you have voiced a worry.

</details>

# ～えない

**Meaning** · cannot possibly; there is no way - the thing is inconceivable, not merely difficult

`～えない` is the negative of the possibility suffix written 得(え)る. It does
not report that someone failed or lacked the skill; it says the possibility itself
is closed. <code>あり得(え)ない</code>, its most famous member, has escaped the
formal register entirely and become an everyday cry of disbelief.

## Formation

The suffix attaches to the **ます-stem**. Unlike the affirmative, which has the two
shapes `うる` and `える`, the negative has exactly one: `えない`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + えない | 止(と)める → 止(と)め得(え)ない |
| する-verb | Noun + し + えない | 予想(よそう)する → 予想(よそう)し得(え)ない |
| Past | ～えなかった | 想像(そうぞう)し得(え)なかった |
| Polite | ～えません | あり得(え)ません |
| Before a noun | ～えない + Noun | あり得(え)ない話(はなし) |
| Set expression | あり + えない | ある → あり得(え)ない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - there is no ❌<code>うない</code>. `うる` is a bare dictionary
form and nothing else; the instant a negative appears, the suffix is `える`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not confuse this with <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code>. The kanji is
the same and the reading is the same, but there `ざる` is an old negative, so
"cannot not do it" comes out as **must**. <code>行(い)き得(え)ない</code> =
"going is inconceivable"; <code>行(い)かざるを得(え)ない</code> = "I have no
choice but to go".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今(いま)さら中止(ちゅうし)なんてあり<b>得(え)ない</b>。
Calling it off at this stage is unthinkable.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が嘘(うそ)をつくなんてあり<b>得(え)ません</b>。
There is no way he would lie.
</pre>

<pre>
人間(にんげん)の力(ちから)では止(と)め<b>得(え)ない</b>流(なが)れだ。
It is a current that human strength cannot stop.
</pre>

<pre>
これは素人(しろうと)には判断(はんだん)し<b>得(え)ない</b>問題(もんだい)です。
This is a question that no layman is in a position to judge.
</pre>

<pre>
あの状況(じょうきょう)で冷静(れいせい)でいるなど、私(わたし)にはあり<b>得(え)ない</b>ことでした。
Staying calm in that situation was, for me, simply out of the question.
</pre>

<pre>
その時代(じだい)の技術(ぎじゅつ)では予測(よそく)し<b>得(え)なかった</b>規模(きぼ)の変化(へんか)が起(お)きた。
A change on a scale that the technology of the day could not have predicted took place.
</pre>

<pre>
試合(しあい)の結果(けっか)は、誰(だれ)にも予想(よそう)し<b>得(え)ない</b>ものだった。
The result of the match was something nobody could have predicted.
</pre>

<pre>
信頼(しんらい)していた部下(ぶか)に裏切(うらぎ)られるとは、当時(とうじ)の私(わたし)には想像(そうぞう)し<b>得(え)ない</b>ことだった。
That I would be betrayed by a subordinate I trusted was beyond anything I could imagine at the time.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Two registers live inside one form, and they barely speak to each other.

With most verbs - 止(と)め得(え)ない, 判断(はんだん)し得(え)ない,
予測(よそく)し得(え)ない - the pattern is **formal and written**. It belongs
to editorials, court judgements, reports and speeches. Used in casual conversation
it sounds like a quotation from a document.

<code>あり得(え)ない</code> is the exception, and it is now thoroughly
colloquial. It covers "impossible", "you're kidding" and, in a use that has spread
widely among younger speakers, plain outrage at something that has very obviously
happened: あの態度(たいど)、あり得(え)ない. That last use is emotional rather
than logical - nobody thinks the rudeness was literally impossible - and it is
casual enough that you would not aim it at a client. There is also the intensifier
<code>あり得(え)ないほど</code>, "unbelievably".

In polite speech, prefer <code>あり得(え)ません</code> to
<code>あり得(え)ないです</code>; the ます form is the settled one in careful
Japanese, though the です form is heard.

One structural point worth holding on to: because this is a statement about the
world and not about a person, `～えない` almost never takes a personal subject with
は. You say <code>それはあり得(え)ない</code>, not
❌<code>私(わたし)はあり得(え)ない</code>.

## Contrast

### ～えない vs the negative potential

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～られない・～できない</code> says **this person can't
manage it**. `～えない` says **it cannot be so at all**.

</div>

<pre>
暗(くら)くて時計(とけい)の文字(もじ)が読(よ)めない。
It's too dark to read the figures on the clock.
</pre>

<pre>
その説明(せつめい)では、誰(だれ)も納得(なっとく)し<b>得(え)ない</b>。
An explanation like that cannot satisfy anyone.
</pre>

The first is about one pair of eyes in one dark room. The second is a claim about
the explanation itself - no listener, anywhere, could be convinced by it. Swap them
and both sentences go wrong: ❌<code>読(よ)み得(え)ない</code> for a dark
room is nonsense, and <code>納得(なっとく)できない</code> would shrink the
sentence to one person's private reaction.

### ～えない vs ～はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずがない` reasons from what you know about **this case**.
`～えない` denies the possibility **as such**, without needing evidence.

</div>

<pre>
田中(たなか)さんが遅(おく)れるはずがない。いつも30分(さんじゅっぷん)前(まえ)に来(く)る人(ひと)だ。
Tanaka can't be late - he's the sort who turns up half an hour early.
</pre>

<pre>
一晩(ひとばん)で全(すべ)てが変(か)わるなど、あり<b>得(え)ない</b>。
Everything changing overnight is not a possibility.
</pre>

`はずがない` invites the follow-up "because…" and can be wrong when the evidence is
wrong. `～えない` does not argue; it rules the case out of the world. In speech
<code>あり得(え)ない</code> is also the more forceful and more emotional of the
two.

### ～えない vs ～かねる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～えない` is about **what is possible**. `～かねる` is about
**what the speaker is willing or able to undertake**, and it is a way of declining.

</div>

<pre>
その案(あん)には賛成(さんせい)しかねます。
I am afraid I cannot support that proposal.
</pre>

<pre>
その案(あん)が通(とお)ることはあり<b>得(え)ません</b>。
There is no possibility of that proposal passing.
</pre>

The first is a refusal, softened; the speaker could support it and chooses not to.
The second is a forecast about the proposal's fate and carries no politeness at
all. Aim the second at a customer's request and you have told them their idea is
absurd.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そんなことはあり**うない**。 → ✅ そんなことはあり<b>得(え)ない</b>。
  `うる` has no negative. This error is common enough that it is worth drilling on
  its own.

- ❌ 漢字(かんじ)が多(おお)すぎて、この本(ほん)は読(よ)み<b>得(え)ない</b>。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は読(よ)めない。
  Your own inability to read the book is the negative potential, not a statement
  that reading it is inconceivable.

- ❌ 起(お)こる<b>得(え)ない</b> → ✅ 起(お)こり<b>得(え)ない</b>
  The base is the ます-stem.

- ❌ 参加(さんか)し<b>得(え)ない</b>（meaning "I have no choice but to take part"） → ✅ 参加(さんか)せざるを<b>得(え)ない</b>
  Same kanji, opposite meaning. Without `ざる`, 得(え)ない simply denies the
  possibility.

## Practice

**1.** これだけ準備(じゅんび)したのだから、失敗(しっぱい)などあり____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

失敗(しっぱい)などあり<b>得(え)ない</b>（or あり得(え)ません）

The speaker is not saying that failure would be difficult - they are ruling it out.
Note that the affirmative <code>あり得(う)る</code> has no negative shape of its
own; ❌あり**うない** does not exist.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 熱(ねつ)が38度(ど)あるので、明日(あした)は（働(はたら)き得(え)ない・働(はたら)けない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)は<b>働(はたら)けない</b>。

A fever stops one particular body on one particular day, which is the negative
potential. <code>働(はたら)き得(え)ない</code> would claim that work is
inconceivable in principle - far too grand a statement for a temperature.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "A change on that scale is not something one person could have brought about."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あれほどの変化(へんか)は、一人(ひとり)の力(ちから)で起(お)こし<b>得(え)る</b>ものではない。（or 一人(ひとり)では起(お)こし得(え)ない変化(へんか)だ）

Either shape works. The first negates the whole noun phrase and keeps
得(え)る positive; the second negates the suffix directly. Both say that the
possibility is closed, rather than that somebody tried and failed.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 専門家(せんもんか)でも説明(せつめい)するえない現象(げんしょう)が続(つづ)いている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 専門家(せんもんか)でも説明(せつめい)し<b>得(え)ない</b>現象(げんしょう)が続(つづ)いている。
"Phenomena that not even the experts can explain are continuing."

A する-verb joins the suffix through its ます-stem <code>し</code>, giving
<code>説明(せつめい)し得(え)ない</code>. The dictionary form
<code>する</code> cannot sit in front of it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)の話(はなし)は信(しん)じられない。
(b) 彼(かれ)の話(はなし)はあり<b>得(え)ない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the speaker's own reaction: I am unable to believe it. It leaves room
for the story to be true and for the speaker to come round later.

(b) makes a claim about the story itself - the events it describes could not have
occurred. In writing that is a flat verdict; said out loud in casual speech,
<code>あり得(え)ない</code> also carries a strong note of indignation, closer to
"that is ridiculous" than to a calm judgement of probability.

</details>

# ～かねる

**Meaning** · I am not in a position to do that; I cannot bring myself to - a formal, face-saving "no"

`～かねる` is not physical inability. Everything it refuses is perfectly possible;
what blocks it is the speaker's role, the company's rules, or a genuine inability
to settle on something. In business and service Japanese it is the standard way of
saying no without ever using the word できません.

## Formation

`かねる` attaches to the **ます-stem** and then conjugates as a る-verb (a Group 2 /
一段(いちだん) verb).

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + かねる | わかる → わかりかねる |
| Polite - by far the commonest shape | ～かねます | わかりかねます |
| する-verb | Noun + し + かねる | 賛成(さんせい)する → 賛成(さんせい)しかねる |
| Humble | お + ます-stem + しかねます | 答(こた)える → お答(こた)えしかねます |
| Past | ～かねた | 決(き)めかねた |
| Progressive | ～かねている | 決(き)めかねている |
| て-form (set phrase) | 見(み)るに見(み)かねて | - |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かねる` takes verbs of **judging, answering, agreeing and
deciding**: わかる, 答(こた)える, 決(き)める, 判断(はんだん)する,
賛成(さんせい)する, 承知(しょうち)する, 認(みと)める. It does not take
verbs of physical action. ❌<code>重(おも)くて持(も)ちかねる</code> is not
Japanese; that is <code>持(も)てない</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>かねない</code> is **not** the negative of this pattern. It means
"may well do (something unwelcome)":
<code>この寒(さむ)さでは凍(こお)りかねない</code>. The negative of
<code>かねる</code> in the "cannot" sense simply does not get used - if you can do
it, you say so straightforwardly.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
その件(けん)については、お答(こた)え<b>しかねます</b>。
I am afraid I cannot answer on that matter.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが、返品(へんぴん)は<b>いたしかねます</b>。
I'm very sorry, but we are unable to accept returns.
</pre>

<pre>
どちらの案(あん)がいいか、決(き)め<b>かねて</b>います。
I can't make up my mind which of the two proposals is better.
</pre>

<pre>
ご事情(じじょう)は分(わ)かりますが、例外(れいがい)は認(みと)め<b>かねます</b>。
I do understand your situation, but we cannot make an exception.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は返事(へんじ)を決(き)め<b>かね</b>ている様子(ようす)だった。
The manager looked as though he could not settle on an answer.
</pre>

<pre>
重(おも)い箱(はこ)を一人(ひとり)で運(はこ)んでいるのを見(み)るに見(み)<b>かねて</b>、声(こえ)をかけた。
I couldn't just stand there watching her carry that heavy box alone, so I offered to help.
</pre>

<pre>
その条件(じょうけん)では、弊社(へいしゃ)としては承知(しょうち)し<b>かねます</b>。
On those terms, we as a company are not in a position to agree.
</pre>

<pre>
お客様(きゃくさま)の個人(こじん)情報(じょうほう)に関(かん)するご質問(しつもん)には、電話(でんわ)では回答(かいとう)し<b>かねます</b>。
Questions concerning customers' personal information are not something we can answer by telephone.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`かねる` is **formal**, and in practice it is nearly always polite: `かねます`,
`いたしかねます`, `お～しかねます`. Plain <code>かねる</code> appears in writing and
in narration, but at a counter or on the phone you will hear the ます form almost
without exception.

Its social work is worth understanding. <code>できません</code> aims the refusal
at the listener: *I* will not do this. <code>かねます</code> presents the same
answer as something the speaker's position does not allow - the refusal comes from
the situation, not from a person - which is exactly why service Japanese leans on
it so heavily. Paired with 申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございませんが or
あいにく, it is about as soft as a flat no can be made.

The other sense - "cannot bring oneself to" - is more emotional and less formal.
決(き)めかねる and 言(い)い出(だ)しかねる describe a person stuck in front
of a choice or a difficult sentence. The frozen phrase
見(み)るに見(み)かねて, "unable to go on watching", is common in ordinary
narrative and does not sound stiff at all. So is
待(ま)ちかねる, which has drifted to mean waiting impatiently rather than being
unable to wait.

One caution. Because `かねます` is so heavily associated with counters, call
centres and formal correspondence, dropping it into casual conversation with a
friend sounds like play-acting a customer-service voice. Among friends the honest
form is 無理(むり), できない, or ちょっと….

## Contrast

### ～かねる vs ～できません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same answer, different face. `できません` states an inability;
`かねます` presents the refusal as coming from the speaker's position, and softens
it.

</div>

<pre>
その質問(しつもん)にはお答(こた)え<b>しかねます</b>。
I'm afraid that is not something I can answer.
</pre>

<pre>
その質問(しつもん)には答(こた)えられません。
I can't answer that question.
</pre>

Both refuse. The second is blunt enough that, said to a customer, it can read as
unhelpful; the first leaves the impression that the speaker would answer if only
they could. Note also that `かねます` is not a euphemism for "maybe later" - it is a
real refusal, and a Japanese listener hears it as one.

### ～かねる vs ～かねない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - despite the shapes, these are not a positive-negative pair.
`かねる` = **cannot**. `かねない` = **might well**, and only of bad outcomes.

</div>

<pre>
その判断(はんだん)には同意(どうい)し<b>かねます</b>。
I cannot agree with that decision.
</pre>

<pre>
その判断(はんだん)は事故(じこ)につながりかねません。
That decision could well lead to an accident.
</pre>

The first is a refusal by the speaker; the second is a warning about the world.
Historically `かねる` meant "find hard to bear", so "hard to bear that it might
happen" drifted into "it may well happen" - which is how one form ended up facing
in two directions.

### ～かねる vs ～がたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がたい` says the thing is **hard to do at all**, for anyone.
`かねる` says **this speaker will not do it**, here, now.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は信(しん)じ<b>がたい</b>。
His explanation is hard to believe.
</pre>

<pre>
その説明(せつめい)では納得(なっとく)し<b>かねます</b>。
I'm afraid that explanation doesn't satisfy me.
</pre>

`がたい` is a judgement on the thing - anyone would struggle to believe it - and it
attaches happily to verbs of perception and evaluation
(忘(わす)れがたい, 理解(りかい)しがたい). `かねる` reports the speaker's own
stance and belongs to the language of dealing with people. `にくい` is a third,
lower-register member of this family, and it covers **physical** awkwardness -
書(か)きにくいペン - which neither of the other two can do.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この箱(はこ)は重(おも)すぎて持(も)ち<b>かねます</b>。 → ✅ この箱(はこ)は重(おも)すぎて持(も)てません。
  Physical inability is the negative potential. `かねる` needs a decision, a
  judgement or a response to refuse.

- ❌ その理由(りゆう)は<b>わかりかねません</b>。 → ✅ その理由(りゆう)は<b>わかりかねます</b>。
  `かねません` would mean "it may well turn out that I understand". The refusal is
  the affirmative form.

- ❌ お受(う)け<b>かねます</b>。 → ✅ お受(う)け<b>しかねます</b>。
  The humble frame is お + ます-stem + する, so the `し` has to be there before
  `かねる`.

- ❌ ごめん、明日(あした)は行(い)き<b>かねる</b>。 → ✅ ごめん、明日(あした)は行(い)けない。
  Aimed at a friend, `かねる` sounds like a shop assistant reading from a script.

## Practice

**1.** 申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございませんが、当日(とうじつ)のキャンセルはお受(う)け____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お受(う)け<b>しかねます</b>

The shop is refusing on the basis of its own rules, which is precisely the `かねる`
sense, and the humble frame お受(う)けする supplies the `し`.
<code>受(う)けられません</code> would be understood, but it is markedly blunter
at a counter.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 夜中(よなか)に大声(おおごえ)で騒(さわ)げば、近所(きんじょ)から苦情(くじょう)が（来(き)かねます・来(き)かねません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

近所(きんじょ)から苦情(くじょう)が<b>来(き)かねません</b>。

The sentence warns about an unwelcome thing that may well happen, which is
`かねない`. <code>かねます</code> would be a refusal, and complaints are not
something the speaker can decline to make.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm afraid I am not in a position to comment on another department's decision."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

他(ほか)の部署(ぶしょ)の決定(けってい)については、コメントし<b>かねます</b>。

"Not in a position to" is the heart of `かねる`: the speaker could comment, but
their role does not allow it. Keep the ます form - this is the register in which
the English sentence would be said, and plain
<code>コメントしかねる</code> would sound like a line from a report.



</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: どのプランにするか、まだ決(き)めるかねています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ どのプランにするか、まだ決(き)め<b>かねています</b>。
"I still can't decide which plan to go with."

`かねる` attaches to the ます-stem (決(き)め), never to the dictionary form. The
progressive <code>かねている</code> is the natural shape here, because being stuck
is a state that has lasted a while.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) その提案(ていあん)には賛成(さんせい)し<b>かねます</b>。
(b) その提案(ていあん)には賛成(さんせい)できません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) frames the disagreement as something the speaker's position or judgement will
not permit. It is measured, it keeps the meeting civil, and it is the sentence you
would say in front of people you have to keep working with.

(b) is a direct statement of refusal. Nothing about it is rude in itself, but it
puts the speaker squarely behind the no, and in a formal discussion it lands
noticeably harder than (a).

</details>

# ～ざるを得ない

**Meaning** · I have no choice but to; it cannot be helped - circumstances force it, and the speaker is not happy about it

`ざる` is an old negative and 得(え)ない is "cannot", so the literal shape is
"cannot not do it". What comes out is compulsion from outside: the rain, the
deadline, the evidence, the family. Reluctance is built in. Nobody says
<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> about something they wanted to do anyway.

## Formation

Take the ない-form, drop the `ない`, and add <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code>. Two verbs are irregular.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ざるを得(え)ない | 帰(かえ)らない → 帰(かえ)らざるを得(え)ない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ざるを得(え)ない | 認(みと)めない → 認(みと)めざるを得(え)ない |
| Verb (する) | **せざるを得(え)ない** | 中止(ちゅうし)する → 中止(ちゅうし)せざるを得(え)ない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ざるを得(え)ない</b> | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ざるを得(え)ない |
| Past | ～ざるを得(え)なかった | 辞(や)めざるを得(え)なかった |
| Polite | ～ざるを得(え)ません | 認(みと)めざるを得(え)ません |
| Set phrase | ～と言(い)わざるを得(え)ない | 失敗(しっぱい)だったと言(い)わざるを得(え)ない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>しざるを得(え)ない</code> is the standard error.
`する` uses the older negative stem `せ`, giving
<code>せざるを得(え)ない</code>. In the same way
<code>来(く)る</code> gives <code>来(こ)ざるを得(え)ない</code>, never
❌<code>きざるを得(え)ない</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the 得(え)ない here is the "cannot" suffix, so it is read
<b>えない</b>, never うない. It is also often written in kana as
<code>ざるをえない</code>, especially in email and on the web; both spellings are
standard.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
雨(あめ)なので、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)せ<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。
With this rain there is no choice but to call the match off.
</pre>

<pre>
これだけの証拠(しょうこ)があれば、事実(じじつ)を認(みと)め<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。
With this much evidence, one has to accept the facts.
</pre>

<pre>
終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)したので、タクシーで帰(かえ)ら<b>ざるを得(え)なかった</b>。
I missed the last train, so I had no option but to go home by taxi.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)が届(とど)かず、工場(こうじょう)は生産(せいさん)を止(と)め<b>ざるを得(え)なかった</b>。
The parts never arrived, and the plant was forced to halt production.
</pre>

<pre>
説明(せつめい)を聞(き)く限(かぎ)り、準備(じゅんび)不足(ぶそく)だったと言(い)わ<b>ざるを得(え)ません</b>。
From what I have heard, one is bound to say the preparation was inadequate.
</pre>

<pre>
親(おや)の介護(かいご)のために、彼女(かのじょ)は長年(ながねん)勤(つと)めた会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)め<b>ざるを得(え)なかった</b>。
To look after her parents she had to leave the company she had worked at for years.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が近(ちか)づいているため、明日(あした)の便(びん)は欠航(けっこう)と判断(はんだん)せ<b>ざるを得(え)ません</b>。
With the typhoon approaching, we are obliged to treat tomorrow's flight as cancelled.
</pre>

<pre>
条件(じょうけん)には不満(ふまん)が残(のこ)るが、期限(きげん)が迫(せま)っている以上(いじょう)、受(う)け入(い)れ<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。
I am still unhappy with the terms, but with the deadline closing in I have no choice but to accept.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is **formal and largely written**. Editorials, news copy, company
announcements, court decisions and formal apologies are its home ground, and in
speech it belongs to meetings, presentations and interviews rather than to
conversation. Among friends the same idea comes out as
<code>行(い)くしかない</code> or <code>行(い)くしかなかった</code>.

Two things it always carries. First, an **external cause**, usually named in the
same sentence with ので, ため, から or 以上(いじょう). Second, **reluctance**:
the speaker is doing this against their own preference. Both together are what
makes it different from a plain statement of obligation.

Because of that reluctance, the pattern is unusable for good news. ❌
<code>昇進(しょうしん)したので、喜(よろこ)ばざるを得(え)ない</code>
reads as a joke - the speaker is being dragged into happiness. A slightly wry use
of exactly this mismatch does exist among adults ("well, I suppose I shall have to
accept a free holiday"), but treat that as a joke you recognise rather than one you
make.

<code>～と言(い)わざるを得(え)ない</code> deserves separate mention. It is
how a Japanese speaker delivers a harsh verdict politely: the criticism is
presented as something the facts force out of them, not as an opinion they were
keen to volunteer. 残念(ざんねん)だと言(い)わざるを得(え)ません is a
standard line in a formal complaint, and it is considerably more cutting than its
mild surface suggests.

## Contrast

### ～ざるを得(え)ない vs ～しかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しかない` counts the options and finds one. <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code>
adds that the speaker is being **pushed** into it, and raises the register several
notches.

</div>

<pre>
バスがないから、歩(ある)く<b>しかない</b>ね。
There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.
</pre>

<pre>
交通(こうつう)が完全(かんぜん)に止(と)まった以上(いじょう)、徒歩(とほ)で向(む)かわ<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。
With transport at a complete standstill, we have no choice but to proceed on foot.
</pre>

`しかない` is neutral about feelings and can even be cheerful -
楽(たの)しむしかない, "we may as well enjoy it". <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> cannot do that. It also cannot
attach to nouns, whereas `しかない` can:
<code>方法(ほうほう)はこれしかない</code>.

### ～ざるを得(え)ない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` is obligation from a **rule, duty or plan**.
<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> is a conclusion **forced by circumstances that have already
happened**.

</div>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)までに書類(しょるい)を出(だ)さ<b>なければなりません</b>。
I have to submit the documents by next week.
</pre>

<pre>
システムが止(と)まったので、締(し)め切(き)りを延(の)ばさ<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。
The system went down, so we have no choice but to push the deadline back.
</pre>

The first is simply how things are arranged; the speaker may be perfectly content
with it. The second is a response to something that went wrong, and the speaker
would undo it if they could. This is also why `なければならない` fits a rule stated
in the abstract - 税金(ぜいきん)は払(はら)わなければならない - where
<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> would sound as though tax had ambushed you.

### ～ざるを得(え)ない vs ～ずにはいられない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both translate as "can't help it", and they point in opposite
directions. <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> = the situation forces me, **against** my feelings.
`ずにはいられない` = my feelings force me, whatever the situation.

</div>

<pre>
あの映画(えいが)を見(み)ると、毎回(まいかい)泣(な)か<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
I can't watch that film without crying, every single time.
</pre>

<pre>
上司(じょうし)の指示(しじ)なので、担当(たんとう)を外(はず)れ<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。
It's the boss's instruction, so I have no choice but to step off the project.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences fail. Tears are not imposed by circumstance, and
stepping off a project is not an urge welling up from inside. The test: if the
force comes from **inside** the speaker, it is `ずにはいられない`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 中止(ちゅうし)<b>しざるを得(え)ない</b> → ✅ 中止(ちゅうし)<b>せざるを得(え)ない</b>
  `する` takes the older negative stem `せ` here. This is the most frequent error
  with the pattern by a wide margin.

- ❌ 帰(かえ)る<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b> → ✅ 帰(かえ)ら<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>
  The base is the ない-form stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 来(き)<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b> → ✅ 来(こ)<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>
  来(く)る is irregular: its ない stem is こ, as in 来(こ)ない.

- ❌ 好(す)きな人(ひと)と話(はな)さ<b>ざるを得(え)ない</b>。 → ✅ 好(す)きな人(ひと)と話(はな)さ<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
  The pattern requires reluctance. Applied to something you want, it turns the
  sentence into a complaint about a pleasure.

- ❌ 認(みと)めざるを<b>得(え)ました</b>。 → ✅ 認(みと)めざるを<b>得(え)ませんでした</b>。
  The expression is negative throughout; there is no affirmative
  <code>ざるを得(え)る</code> in use.

## Practice

**1.** 大雪(おおゆき)で道(みち)が閉(と)ざされ、登山(とざん)は途中(とちゅう)で断念(だんねん)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

断念(だんねん)<b>せざるを得(え)なかった</b>

Snow made the decision for the climbers, and they gave up unwillingly - exactly the
<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> situation. 断念(だんねん)する is a する-verb, so the stem is
<code>せ</code>, not ❌し.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 久(ひさ)しぶりに故郷(こきょう)の景色(けしき)を見(み)て、感動(かんどう)（せざるを得(え)なかった・せずにはいられなかった）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

感動(かんどう)<b>せずにはいられなかった</b>。

Being moved by a landscape is a feeling rising from inside the speaker, so
`ずにはいられない` is the fit. <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> would suggest the scenery somehow
compelled the speaker to be moved against their will.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Given the numbers in this report, we are forced to say the plan has failed."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この報告書(ほうこくしょ)の数字(すうじ)を見(み)れば、計画(けいかく)は失敗(しっぱい)だったと言(い)わ<b>ざるを得(え)ません</b>。

"We are forced to say" is <code>言(い)わざるを得(え)ない</code> almost word
for word, and the set phrase is the natural way to deliver a hard judgement in a
formal setting. Keep the polite ます form for a report or a meeting.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 会社(かいしゃ)の方針(ほうしん)が変(か)わったので、計画(けいかく)を見直(みなお)すざるを得(え)ないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会社(かいしゃ)の方針(ほうしん)が変(か)わったので、計画(けいかく)を見直(みなお)さ<b>ざるを得(え)ません</b>。
"The company changed its policy, so we have no choice but to revise the plan."

Two repairs: the base must be the ない stem 見直(みなお)さ, and the polite form
of this expression is <code>ざるを得(え)ません</code>. The
<code>ないです</code> ending is too casual for a pattern that only ever appears in
formal Japanese.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 来週(らいしゅう)の出張(しゅっちょう)には行(い)か<b>なければなりません</b>。
(b) 来週(らいしゅう)の出張(しゅっちょう)には行(い)か<b>ざるを得(え)ません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a duty. It says nothing about whether the speaker minds; the trip is on
the schedule and that is that. It is also ordinary spoken Japanese.

(b) says the speaker would rather not go and is being pushed into it - by an
instruction, a cancellation, someone else's absence. The listener will expect a
reason, and the register is noticeably more formal, the sort of thing said in a
meeting rather than to a colleague at lunch.

</details>

# ～よりほかない

**Meaning** · there is nothing for it but to do this - every other road has been looked at and closed

Literally "there is nothing other than". The speaker is not choosing; they are
reporting the end of a search. `よりほかない` carries a note of resignation and a
faintly formal, written texture, which is what separates it from the everyday
`しかない`.

## Formation

The verb goes in the **dictionary form**. `ほか` is a noun (他(ほか)), so the
particles that cluster around it - は, に, も - can all appear, with only a small
change in weight.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + よりほかない | 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つよりほかない |
| Verb, with は | ～よりほかはない | 待(ま)つよりほかはない |
| Verb, with に | ～よりほかにない | 待(ま)つよりほかにない |
| Noun | Noun + よりほかない | 方法(ほうほう)はこれよりほかない |
| Polite | ～よりほかありません | 謝(あやま)るよりほかありません |
| Past | ～よりほかなかった | 断(ことわ)るよりほかなかった |
| Longer variant | ～よりほか仕方(しかた)がない | 待(ま)つよりほか仕方(しかた)がない |
| Short variant | ～ほかない ／ ～ほかはない | 待(ま)つほかない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `より` here is not the `より` of comparison. It is the older "other
than", the same one you meet in 駅(えき)より徒歩(とほ)五分(ごふん) on an
estate agent's advertisement. Nothing is being compared;
<code>より</code> is marking what gets excluded.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
今(いま)は待(ま)つ<b>よりほかない</b>。
For now there is nothing to do but wait.
</pre>

<pre>
こうなっては、謝(あやま)る<b>よりほかありません</b>。
Now that it has come to this, all I can do is apologise.
</pre>

<pre>
地図(ちず)もスマホもないので、人(ひと)に聞(き)く<b>よりほかない</b>。
I have neither a map nor my phone, so there is nothing for it but to ask someone.
</pre>

<pre>
材料(ざいりょう)が足(た)りないなら、明日(あした)買(か)い足(た)す<b>よりほかはない</b>。
If we are short of ingredients, the only course left is to buy more tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
事故(じこ)の原因(げんいん)については、調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)を待(ま)つ<b>よりほかにない</b>。
As for the cause of the accident, we can do nothing but wait for the findings.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で抱(かか)えきれない量(りょう)なら、誰(だれ)かに頼(たの)む<b>よりほかない</b>でしょう。
If it is more than one person can carry, there is really no option but to ask for help.
</pre>

<pre>
息子(むすこ)が自分(じぶん)で決(き)めたことですから、親(おや)としては見守(みまも)る<b>よりほかありません</b>。
My son decided it for himself, so as his parent I can only stand by and watch.
</pre>

<pre>
資金(しきん)が尽(つ)きた今(いま)、事業(じぎょう)を縮小(しゅくしょう)する<b>よりほかない</b>と社長(しゃちょう)は語(かた)った。
With the funds exhausted, the president said there was no alternative but to scale the business back.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`よりほかない` sits a step above ordinary conversation. You will meet it in
narration, in written accounts, in speeches and in the careful speech of an adult
explaining a difficult situation. It is not stiff in the way that
<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> is - a colleague could say it to you - but nobody
uses it to decide where to have lunch.

What it always carries is **resignation**. The other roads were real, and they are
gone. A sentence with `よりほかない` almost always comes after a statement of what
went wrong: the train stopped, the money ran out, the letter never came. Without
that, it sounds like a shrug with nothing behind it.

The variants are close in meaning. `ほかはない` slightly emphasises the exclusion
("nothing *else* at all"), `ほかにない` puts the weight on the absence of any other
item, and bare `ほかない` - with `より` dropped - is the lightest and now the most
common in speech. The longest member,
<code>よりほか仕方(しかた)がない</code>, adds a distinctly weary note; it is the
sentence of someone who has given up arguing.

Politeness works normally: <code>よりほかありません</code> in ます-speech,
<code>よりほかなかった</code> in the past. Avoid ❌<code>よりほかないです</code> in
writing - it mixes a literary frame with a casual ending, and
<code>ありません</code> is the settled polite form.

## Contrast

### ～よりほかない vs ～しかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning at two registers. `しかない` is what you say;
`よりほかない` is closer to what you write, and it sounds more resigned.

</div>

<pre>
終(お)わってないなら、徹夜(てつや)する<b>しかない</b>よ。
If it isn't finished, we'll just have to pull an all-nighter.
</pre>

<pre>
納期(のうき)が動(うご)かせない以上(いじょう)、徹夜(てつや)する<b>よりほかありません</b>。
Since the delivery date cannot be moved, there is nothing for it but to work through the night.
</pre>

There is one real difference under the register. `しかない` can be cheerful -
<code>こうなったら楽(たの)しむしかない</code>, "we may as well enjoy it" - and
it can simply count things: <code>席(せき)は二(ふた)つしかない</code>.
`よりほかない` does neither. It reports a dead end, and it does not count.

### ～よりほかない vs ～ざるを得(え)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よりほかない` says **no other option exists**.
<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> says **I am being forced**, and puts the speaker's
unwillingness at the centre.

</div>

<pre>
この時間(じかん)ではタクシーを使(つか)う<b>よりほかない</b>。
At this hour there's no way of getting there but by taxi.
</pre>

<pre>
上(うえ)からの指示(しじ)なので、方針(ほうしん)を変(か)えざるを得(え)ない。
It's an instruction from above, so we have no choice but to change course.
</pre>

The first is an observation about the world at one in the morning; the speaker may
not mind at all. The second is about pressure applied to the speaker, and it always
implies they would rather not. Notice too that `よりほかない` takes the dictionary
form and <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> takes the ない-stem - a quick way to tell
them apart on the page.

### ～よりほかない vs ～ようがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようがない` says there is **no method at all**; the action
cannot be performed. `よりほかない` says the method exists and it is the only one
left.

</div>

<pre>
住所(じゅうしょ)も電話番号(でんわばんごう)も知(し)らないので、連絡(れんらく)の<b>しようがない</b>。
I have neither an address nor a phone number, so there is no way to get in touch.
</pre>

<pre>
住所(じゅうしょ)しか分(わ)からないので、手紙(てがみ)を書(か)く<b>よりほかない</b>。
All I have is an address, so writing a letter is the only thing left to me.
</pre>

`ようがない` empties the shelf; `よりほかない` leaves exactly one item on it. That
is why they sound so different in practice: the first is helplessness, the second
is a decision the speaker did not want to make.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 待(ま)ち<b>よりほかない</b>。 → ✅ 待(ま)つ<b>よりほかない</b>。
  The verb takes its dictionary form. The ます-stem belongs to suffixes such as
  <code>かねる</code>, not to this one.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)なら、中止(ちゅうし)<b>よりほかない</b>。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)なら、中止(ちゅうし)する<b>よりほかない</b>。
  When the remaining option is an action, the verb has to appear. A bare
  する-noun leaves the sentence with nothing to do.

- ❌ 謝(あやま)るよりほか<b>ないでした</b>。 → ✅ 謝(あやま)るよりほか<b>ありませんでした</b>。
  <code>ない</code> is a real い-adjective ending here, so its polite past is
  <code>ありませんでした</code>.

- ❌ 合格(ごうかく)したので、喜(よろこ)ぶ<b>よりほかない</b>。 → ✅ 合格(ごうかく)したので、喜(よろこ)ばずにはいられない。
  The pattern reports a dead end, not an overflow of feeling. Applied to good
  news it sounds as though celebrating were a chore.

## Practice

**1.** バスも電車(でんしゃ)も止(と)まってしまった。ここから歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)る____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)る<b>よりほかない</b>（or よりほかありません・ほかない）

Both forms of transport are gone, so the sentence is reporting the last remaining
option - exactly what this pattern is for. The verb stays in the dictionary form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 連絡先(れんらくさき)を一(ひと)つも知(し)らないので、知(し)らせ（るよりほかない・ようがない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

知(し)らせ<b>ようがない</b>。

With no contact details at all there is no method left, and `ようがない` is the
pattern for a total absence of means. `よりほかない` would imply that one way of
telling them still exists.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The shop was already shut, so we had no choice but to give up and go home."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

店(みせ)はもう閉(し)まっていたので、あきらめて帰(かえ)る<b>よりほかなかった</b>。

The past is formed on <code>ない</code> as an い-adjective:
<code>よりほかなかった</code>. Note 閉(し)まる, the intransitive "to be shut" -
the shop closed on its own, so this is not 閉(し)める.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 部品(ぶひん)が届(とど)かないなら、作業(さぎょう)を止(と)めるよりしかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 部品(ぶひん)が届(とど)かないなら、作業(さぎょう)を止(と)める<b>よりほかない</b>。（or 止(と)める<b>しかない</b>）
"If the parts aren't coming, there's nothing to do but stop work."

<code>より</code> belongs with <code>ほか</code>, and <code>しか</code> stands on
its own. Mixing the two produces a form that exists in neither pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)は見送(みおく)る<b>しかないね</b>。
(b) 今回(こんかい)は見送(みおく)る<b>よりほかありません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is conversational and light. The `ね` invites agreement, and the sentence could
be said between colleagues who are mildly disappointed and already moving on.

(b) is the written or formal version of the same conclusion. It sounds as though
the speaker has weighed the alternatives and is now recording the outcome - the
sentence of an announcement or a report rather than a chat, and noticeably more
resigned in tone.

</details>

# ～ねばならない

**Meaning** · must; have to - the written, weighty twin of なければならない

`ねばならない` says nothing that `なければならない` does not say. What it changes is
the voice. `ね` is an old negative form, and the whole expression carries the
gravity of that older Japanese: it belongs to editorials, speeches, principles and
resolutions rather than to arranging the afternoon.

## Formation

Take the ない-form, drop the `ない`, and add `ねばならない`. The same two verbs are
irregular as always.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ねばならない | 守(まも)らない → 守(まも)らねばならない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ねばならない | 決(き)めない → 決(き)めねばならない |
| Verb (する) | **せねばならない** | 対応(たいおう)する → 対応(たいおう)せねばならない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ねばならない</b> | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ねばならない |
| Polite | ～ねばなりません | 決(き)めねばなりません |
| Past | ～ねばならなかった | 決(き)めねばならなかった |
| Archaic / literary | ～ねばならぬ | 働(はたら)かねばならぬ |
| Short, in speech | ～ねば | やらねば |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the stem is the same one that `ない` uses. ❌
<code>しねばならない</code> and ❌<code>来(き)ねばならない</code> are the two
standard slips; the forms are <code>せねばならない</code> and
<code>来(こ)ねばならない</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the shortened <code>～ねば</code> survives on its own as a mutter of
resolve: そろそろ行(い)かねば, 「やらねば」. Even there it sounds
half-theatrical, which is part of its charm; a speaker using it is aware of the
old-fashioned ring.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
約束(やくそく)は守(まも)ら<b>ねばならない</b>。
A promise must be kept.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)までに決(き)め<b>ねばならない</b>。
We have to decide by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
人(ひと)は過去(かこ)から学(まな)ば<b>ねばならない</b>。
People must learn from the past.
</pre>

<pre>
この問題(もんだい)については、もう一度(いちど)考(かんが)え直(なお)さ<b>ねばなりません</b>。
This is a question we shall have to think through again.
</pre>

<pre>
市民(しみん)の安全(あんぜん)を守(まも)るため、市(し)は速(すみ)やかに対応(たいおう)せ<b>ねばならない</b>。
To keep its citizens safe, the city must act without delay.
</pre>

<pre>
自由(じゆう)には責任(せきにん)が伴(ともな)うということを、心(こころ)に留(と)め<b>ねばならない</b>。
We must bear in mind that freedom comes with responsibility.
</pre>

<pre>
申(もう)し上(あ)げにくいのですが、今回(こんかい)の結果(けっか)は残念(ざんねん)だと言(い)わ<b>ねばなりません</b>。
It is not easy to say, but I must call this result a disappointment.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)は、男(おとこ)は黙(だま)って働(はたら)か<b>ねばならぬ</b>と言(い)われる時代(じだい)に育(そだ)った。
My grandfather grew up in an age when men were told they must work and say nothing.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Treat this as a **written** form first. Editorials, essays, policy documents,
company statements and the closing paragraph of a formal speech are where it lives.
Reading a newspaper column you will meet
考(かんが)えねばならない and 問(と)わねばならない constantly.

In speech it is much narrower. It turns up in prepared remarks, in the speech of
some older men, in period drama, and as a deliberate flourish - a colleague sighing
「そろそろ帰(かえ)らねば」 knows exactly how old-fashioned it sounds. Said to a
friend about an errand, ❌<code>買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)かねばならない</code>
is not wrong, but it lands like announcing a solemn duty to buy milk. The everyday
forms are なきゃ, ないと and なければならない.

There is also a difference in **weight**. Because `ねばならない` is the form used
for principles - 守(まも)らねばならない約束(やくそく),
果(は)たさねばならない責任(せきにん) - it tends to frame its obligation as a
matter of duty rather than of schedule. That is a tendency and not a rule: a
newspaper will happily write 提出(ていしゅつ)せねばならない about a form. But
if you want a sentence to sound like an appointment rather than a moral
imperative, `なければならない` is the safer choice.

The variant `ねばならぬ` is a step older again. It belongs to fiction, proverbs and
deliberate archaism; recognise it, and leave it alone in your own writing.

## Contrast

### ～ねばならない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `なければならない` is the
neutral standard; `ねばならない` is its formal, literary counterpart.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は七時(しちじ)に家(いえ)を出(で)<b>なければなりません</b>。
Tomorrow I have to leave the house at seven.
</pre>

<pre>
我々(われわれ)は次(つぎ)の世代(せだい)に何(なに)を残(のこ)すのかを問(と)わ<b>ねばならない</b>。
We must ask ourselves what we are leaving to the next generation.
</pre>

Neither could comfortably take the other's sentence. A seven o'clock start is a
schedule, and dressing it in `ねばならない` is comic; a question about the next
generation is exactly the register `ねばならない` was made for. Note that in casual
speech both compress - なければ→なきゃ, ねば→ねば as it stands - but only the first
compression is ordinary conversation.

### ～ねばならない vs ～べきだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ねばならない` says there is **no way around it**. `べきだ` says
it is **the right thing to do**, and leaves the choice open.

</div>

<pre>
規則(きそく)である以上(いじょう)、全員(ぜんいん)が従(したが)わ<b>ねばならない</b>。
As it is a rule, everyone must follow it.
</pre>

<pre>
間違(まちが)いに気(き)づいたなら、すぐに報告(ほうこく)する<b>べきだ</b>。
If you have noticed a mistake, you ought to report it at once.
</pre>

`べきだ` is a judgement the speaker is offering; someone could disagree with it and
the sentence would still stand. `ねばならない` presents the obligation as already
settled by the rule, the deadline or the situation. The two overlap most in
editorial writing, where both appear in the same paragraph - and even there,
`べきだ` is the writer's opinion and `ねばならない` is the writer's premise.

### ～ねばならない vs ～ざるを得(え)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ねばならない` is a **standing obligation**, and says nothing
about how the speaker feels. <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> is a **reluctant
response to what has just happened**.

</div>

<pre>
会員(かいいん)は毎年(まいとし)会費(かいひ)を納(おさ)め<b>ねばならない</b>。
Members are required to pay the annual fee.
</pre>

<pre>
参加者(さんかしゃ)が集(あつ)まらず、大会(たいかい)は延期(えんき)せざるを得(え)なかった。
Not enough people signed up, so we had no choice but to postpone the event.
</pre>

The first is a rule in the constitution of a club; it would be printed on a form.
The second is what somebody decided last week, unwillingly, because reality gave
way. The giveaway is that <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> nearly always has a
cause attached to it, while `ねばならない` often stands alone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 報告(ほうこく)<b>しねばならない</b> → ✅ 報告(ほうこく)<b>せねばならない</b>
  `する` uses the old stem `せ` in front of `ねば`, exactly as it does in
  <code>せずに</code> and <code>せざるを得(え)ない</code>.

- ❌ 決(き)める<b>ねばならない</b> → ✅ 決(き)め<b>ねばならない</b>
  The base is the ない-stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 決(き)めねば<b>ならないです</b>。 → ✅ 決(き)めねば<b>なりません</b>。
  A literary frame with a casual polite ending is the worst of both. The polite
  form built into the pattern is <code>なりません</code>.

- ❌ ごめん、そろそろ帰(かえ)ら<b>ねばならない</b>。（to a friend） → ✅ ごめん、そろそろ帰(かえ)ら<b>なきゃ</b>。
  Not ungrammatical, but the register is wrong by a wide margin. Among friends the
  short forms are what you want.

## Practice

**1.** 安全(あんぜん)の確認(かくにん)が取(と)れるまで、運転(うんてん)は見合(みあ)わせ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

見合(みあ)わせ<b>ねばならない</b>（or 見合(みあ)わせねばなりません）

A public announcement about suspending services is squarely in this pattern's
register. The base is the ない-stem 見合(みあ)わせ, from
<code>見合(みあ)わせる</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)、歯医者(はいしゃ)に（行(い)かねばならない・行(い)かなきゃいけない）んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

明日(あした)、歯医者(はいしゃ)に<b>行(い)かなきゃ</b>いけないんだ。

A dentist's appointment mentioned in conversation calls for the casual form. The
`んだ` ending in the question is itself casual, and `ねばならない` clashes with it -
mixing a literary obligation form with colloquial sentence-ending particles is one
of the clearest register errors a learner can make.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "We must not forget that this problem concerns everyone."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この問題(もんだい)が全(すべ)ての人(ひと)に関(かか)わることを、我々(われわれ)は忘(わす)れては<b>ならない</b>。

Note the trap: "must not forget" is a prohibition, and prohibitions take
<code>てはならない</code>. ❌<code>忘(わす)れねばならない</code> would mean
"must forget", the opposite of the English.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 委員会(いいんかい)は来週(らいしゅう)までに結論(けつろん)を出(だ)すねばなりません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 委員会(いいんかい)は来週(らいしゅう)までに結論(けつろん)を出(だ)さ<b>ねばなりません</b>。
"The committee must reach a conclusion by next week."

<code>出(だ)す</code> is an う-verb, so its ない-stem is
<code>出(だ)さ</code>. The dictionary form cannot stand in front of `ねば`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 環境(かんきょう)を守(まも)ら<b>ねばならない</b>。
(b) 環境(かんきょう)を守(まも)る<b>べきだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats protecting the environment as a duty that is not up for discussion. It
reads as a premise the writer expects the reader to share, and its register is that
of a speech or a leading article.

(b) puts the same content forward as the speaker's position: this is what we ought
to do. It invites agreement or argument in a way that (a) does not, and it is
noticeably easier to say out loud in ordinary conversation.

</details>

# ～まい

**Meaning** · I shall not; surely it will not - a single old form doing the work of both negative resolve and negative guess

`まい` is the negative counterpart of the volitional `う／よう`. With a first-person
subject it is a vow: I am not going to do this again. With anything else it is a
confident prediction in the negative: that surely will not happen. Which of the two
you are reading is decided by the subject and by whether the verb is something a
person can choose to do.

## Formation

`まい` is an unchanging ending: it has no past, no negative and no ordinary polite
form. What varies is the base it attaches to.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + まい | 言(い)う → 言(い)うまい |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + まい | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べるまい |
| Verb (る-verb), older | ます-stem + まい | 食(た)べる → 食(た)べまい |
| Verb (する) | するまい ／ すまい ／ しまい | 心配(しんぱい)するまい |
| Verb (来(く)る) | 来(く)るまい ／ <b>来(こ)まい</b> | - |
| い-adjective, noun (via ある) | ～ではあるまい | 簡単(かんたん)ではあるまい |
| Set: a vow | 二度(にど)と～まい | 二度(にど)と飲(の)むまい |
| Set: trying not to | ～まいとする | 笑(わら)うまいとする |
| Set: rhetorical question | ～ではあるまいか | 病気(びょうき)ではあるまいか |
| Set: whether or not | ～うが～まいが | 行(い)こうが行(い)くまいが |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まい` is **already negative**. ❌
<code>食(た)べないまい</code> doubles the negation and means nothing. The verb
in front of it is always affirmative in form.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - 来(く)る has two bases here and they are read differently:
<code>来(く)るまい</code> keeps the dictionary form, while the older
<code>来(こ)まい</code> uses the same こ- stem as 来(こ)ない. Never
❌<code>きまい</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう二度(にど)と嘘(うそ)はつく<b>まい</b>。
I will never tell a lie again.
</pre>

<pre>
この時間(じかん)なら、彼(かれ)はもう来(こ)<b>まい</b>。
At this hour he surely isn't coming.
</pre>

<pre>
こんな失敗(しっぱい)は二度(にど)と繰(く)り返(かえ)す<b>まい</b>と心(こころ)に誓(ちか)った。
I swore to myself that I would never repeat a mistake like that.
</pre>

<pre>
この雨(あめ)では、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われ<b>まい</b>。
In rain like this the match will surely not go ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
泣(な)く<b>まい</b>としたが、涙(なみだ)が止(と)まらなかった。
I tried not to cry, but the tears would not stop.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)が事情(じじょう)を知(し)らないわけでは<b>あるまい</b>。
It can hardly be that she is unaware of the situation.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が行(い)こうが行(い)く<b>まい</b>が、結果(けっか)は変(か)わらないだろう。
Whether I go or not, the outcome will be the same.
</pre>

<pre>
これほど反対(はんたい)の声(こえ)が強(つよ)い以上(いじょう)、計画(けいかく)がそのまま通(とお)ることは<b>あるまい</b>。
With opposition running this strong, the plan will surely not pass as it stands.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`まい` is **old, and it sounds old**. It belongs to written Japanese, to fiction and
narration, to speeches, and to the deliberate gravity of a person making a vow. A
learner who uses it casually will be understood, but will sound as though they had
stepped out of a novel.

The places it genuinely survives in modern Japanese are worth memorising as units:

- <code>二度(にど)と～まい</code> - the standard shape of a personal vow, and
  the most likely `まい` you will ever say out loud.
- <code>～まいとする</code> / <code>～まいとして</code> - struggling not to do
  something. This one is common in ordinary narrative prose.
- <code>～ではあるまいか</code> - "might it not be that…", a formal, slightly
  literary way of raising a possibility.
- <code>～うが～まいが</code> and <code>～うと～まいと</code> - "whether or not".
  These are fixed frames and appear in quite ordinary writing.

Register aside, there is a grammatical restriction that matters. The **vow** sense
is first-person only: you cannot use `まい` to describe somebody else's resolve.
The **prediction** sense is the opposite - it is normal about third parties and
about events, and odd about yourself, because you do not need to guess at your own
intentions.

There is no natural polite form. `ますまい` exists in old texts and is not worth
producing; in polite speech the equivalents are
<code>ないでしょう</code> for the prediction and
<code>ないつもりです</code> for the vow. Nor is there a past tense: to report a past
resolve you embed `まい` in a quotation, as in
<code>～まいと思(おも)った</code>.

## Contrast

### ～まい vs ～ないつもりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both express a decision not to do something, but `まい` is a
**vow** and `ないつもりだ` is a **plan**.

</div>

<pre>
もうあの店(みせ)には行(い)く<b>まい</b>。
I shall not set foot in that shop again.
</pre>

<pre>
もうあの店(みせ)には行(い)か<b>ないつもりです</b>。
I don't intend to go to that shop again.
</pre>

The second is a statement of current intention, and revising it next week costs the
speaker nothing. The first is a resolution, usually taken in the heat of a
disappointment, and it sounds like something that ought to be kept. `まい` also
cannot be turned into a polite ます-sentence, which is why the second is the only
one of the two you can say at work.

### ～まい vs ～ないだろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - as a prediction, `まい` and `ないだろう` mean the same thing.
`まい` is **more confident and much more literary**.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)にはならないだろう。
It probably won't snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)にはなる<b>まい</b>。
It will surely not snow tomorrow.
</pre>

In speech only the first is ordinary. The second is the sentence of a narrator or
an essayist, and it leaves less room for doubt: `だろう` hedges, `まい` states.
Note that with a non-volitional verb like なる, the vow reading is unavailable, so
the prediction sense is the only one on offer.

### ～ではあるまいか vs ～のではないだろうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical function - floating a possibility as a question to
oneself - at two very different registers.

</div>

<pre>
何(なに)か事情(じじょう)があるのでは<b>ないだろうか</b>。
Might there not be some reason behind it?
</pre>

<pre>
何(なに)か事情(じじょう)があるのでは<b>あるまいか</b>。
Might there not, one wonders, be some reason behind it?
</pre>

The first is what a person says. The second is what an essay says, and it carries a
faint air of a writer thinking aloud on the page. Both are softer than a flat
assertion; neither expects an answer.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 二度(にど)と食(た)べ<b>ないまい</b>。 → ✅ 二度(にど)と食(た)べ<b>まい</b>。
  `まい` supplies the negative by itself. Adding `ない` in front of it is the
  commonest error learners make with this form.

- ❌ 彼(かれ)はもう来(き)<b>まい</b>。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)はもう来(こ)<b>まい</b>。
  The older base of 来(く)る is こ, the same stem as 来(こ)ない. The
  alternative <code>来(く)るまい</code> is also correct.

- ❌ 弟(おとうと)はタバコを吸(す)う<b>まい</b>と決(き)めている。（as a report of his vow） → ✅ 弟(おとうと)はタバコを吸(す)わないと決(き)めている。
  The vow sense is the speaker's own. About someone else, `まい` is read as a
  prediction, which is not what the sentence means.

- ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)は降(ふ)る<b>まいです</b>。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)は降(ふ)らないでしょう。
  `まい` has no working polite form. In ます-speech, use
  <code>ないでしょう</code>.

## Practice

**1.** あんな思(おも)いは二度(にど)とする____と心(こころ)に決(き)めた。

二度(にど)とする<b>まい</b>と心(こころ)に決(き)めた

<code>二度(にど)と～まい</code> is the fixed frame for a vow, and embedding it
in <code>と心(こころ)に決(き)めた</code> is how a past resolve gets
reported - `まい` itself has no past form. <code>するまい</code> is the shape you
are most likely to meet, though <code>すまい</code> also occurs.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この時期(じき)に大雪(おおゆき)が降(ふ)ることは（あるまい・あらないまい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この時期(じき)に大雪(おおゆき)が降(ふ)ることは<b>あるまい</b>。

`まい` is negative on its own, so the verb in front of it stays affirmative -
and in any case there is no form ❌あらない. This is the prediction sense: heavy
snow is not something anyone decides on.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He tried not to look at the clock, but he could not help checking it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<code>～まいとする</code> is the standard way to say "tried not to", and unlike
the vow sense it is perfectly normal with a third-person subject, because it
describes an observable struggle rather than a private resolution.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 二度(にど)とこの道(みち)を通(とお)る<b>まい</b>と思(おも)いました。
"I thought I would never take this road again."

Two repairs. `まい` attaches to the dictionary form of an う-verb, not the

思(おも)いました.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

register.

(a) is ordinary Japanese - the sentence of somebody worrying out loud to a friend.
(b) is literary. It reads as a line of narration or an essayist's aside, and spoken
aloud in a normal conversation it would sound as though the speaker were quoting a
book.

</details>

# ～ずに済む

**Meaning** · to get away without having to do it - the thing you were braced for turned out to be unnecessary

済(す)む is "to be settled, to be done with". <code>ずに済(す)む</code> therefore says that the
matter closed **without** the step you expected to have to take: no apology, no
surgery, no second trip. Relief is the whole point of the pattern. It reports an
outcome, not a permission.

## Formation

Take the ない-form, drop the `ない`, add <code>ずに済(す)む</code>. The `ずに` behaves exactly as it
does on its own, irregular verbs included.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ずに済(す)む | 払(はら)わない → 払(はら)わずに済(す)む |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ずに済(す)む | 捨(す)てない → 捨(す)てずに済(す)む |
| Verb (する) | **せずに済(す)む** | 心配(しんぱい)する → 心配(しんぱい)せずに済(す)む |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ずに済(す)む</b> | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ずに済(す)む |
| Everyday variants | ～ないで済(す)む ／ ～なくて済(す)む | 払(はら)わないで済(す)む |
| Past - the usual shape | ～ずに済(す)んだ | 払(はら)わずに済(す)んだ |
| Noun | Noun + で済(す)む | 電話(でんわ)で済(す)む |
| Deliberately avoiding it | ～ずに済(す)ませる ／ ～ずに済(す)ます | 昼食(ちゅうしょく)を取(と)らずに済(す)ませた |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>しずに済(す)む</code> is the error to watch for.
`する` takes the older stem `せ`, giving
<code>せずに済(す)む</code> - the same `せ` as in
<code>せずに</code> and <code>せざるを得(え)ない</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>済(す)む</code> is intransitive: things settle themselves.
Its transitive partner <code>済(す)ませる</code> means you settled them, on
purpose. That difference carries straight over into this pattern, and it is the one
place where learners produce a sentence that is grammatical but says the wrong
thing.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
傘(かさ)を買(か)わ<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>。
I got away without having to buy an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
手術(しゅじゅつ)はせ<b>ずに済(す)みました</b>。
In the end I didn't need surgery.
</pre>

<pre>
早(はや)めに家(いえ)を出(で)たので、走(はし)ら<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>。
I left the house early, so I didn't have to run after all.
</pre>

<pre>
割引券(わりびきけん)があったから、千円(せんえん)しか払(はら)わ<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>。
I had a voucher, so I got away with paying only a thousand yen.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)が事情(じじょう)を説明(せつめい)してくれて、私(わたし)から謝(あやま)ら<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>。
The manager explained the situation, so I was spared having to apologise myself.
</pre>

<pre>
データをこまめに保存(ほぞん)しておけば、一(いち)から作(つく)り直(なお)さ<b>ずに済(す)む</b>。
If you save your work often, you won't have to build the whole thing again.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)に新(あたら)しい駅(えき)ができて、毎朝(まいあさ)バスに乗(の)ら<b>ずに済(す)む</b>ようになった。
A new station opened nearby, so I no longer have to catch the bus every morning.
</pre>

<pre>
事前(じぜん)に連絡(れんらく)しておいたおかげで、受付(うけつけ)で長(なが)く待(ま)た<b>ずに済(す)みました</b>。
Because I had got in touch beforehand, I didn't have to wait long at reception.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The three variants differ only in texture. <code>ずに済(す)む</code> is the tightest and slightly
the most written; <code>ないで済(す)む</code> is the everyday spoken form; <code>なくて済(す)む</code> sits
between them and is very common. All three are ordinary Japanese and none is
formal in the way that <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> is - you can say any of
them to a friend.

What the pattern always carries is **relief**. There was a cost - money, time,
embarrassment, a difficult conversation - hanging over the situation, and it did
not fall. That is why the past <code>ずに済(す)んだ</code> is by far the
commonest form: you usually only notice the escape once it has happened. Used about
something the speaker wanted to do, the sentence becomes odd:
❌<code>旅行(りょこう)に行(い)かずに済(す)んだ</code> tells your listener
the trip was a burden.

The relief can also be about **scale** rather than avoidance, and this is where the
noun pattern comes in. <code>軽(かる)いけがで済(す)んだ</code> - "it was only
a light injury" - and <code>電話(でんわ)で済(す)む話(はなし)</code> -
"that could be settled with a phone call" - both say that the situation ended more
cheaply than it might have. <code>済(す)む</code> is the same verb doing the
same job.

One useful frame for softening a request:
<code>～ずに済(す)むように</code>, "so that you won't have to…". It is a kind way
of framing advice, because it puts the listener's convenience in the sentence:
二度手間(にどでま)にならずに済(す)むように、先(さき)に確認(かくにん)しておきます.

## Contrast

### ～ずに済(す)む vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくてもいい` grants **permission in advance**: you don't have
to. <code>ずに済(す)む</code> reports an **outcome**: as it turned out, I didn't
have to.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は来(こ)<b>なくてもいい</b>ですよ。
You don't need to come in tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)が中止(ちゅうし)になって、昨日(きのう)は来(こ)<b>ずに済(す)みました</b>。
The meeting was called off, so in the end I didn't have to come in yesterday.
</pre>

The first is somebody giving you permission, and it is what a manager says. The
second describes how things fell out, with a note of relief that permission has
nothing to do with. This is also why
<code>なくてもいい</code> works in the present about the future, while
<code>ずに済(す)む</code> gravitates to the past.

### ～ずに済(す)む vs ～ずにいる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ずに済(す)む</code> = the **need** went away.
`ずにいる` = the **action** has not happened, and often the speaker wishes it had.

</div>

<pre>
忙(いそが)しくて、三日(みっか)も連絡(れんらく)せ<b>ずにいる</b>。
I've been so busy I've gone three days without getting in touch.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)が伝(つた)えてくれたので、連絡(れんらく)せ<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>。
He passed the message on, so I didn't have to make the call at all.
</pre>

The first is a state of not-doing that is still hanging over the speaker; the
obligation has not gone anywhere. The second closes the matter. If you can add
"thank goodness" to the English, you want <code>済(す)む</code>.

### ～ずに済(す)む vs ～ずに済(す)ませる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>済(す)む</code> is intransitive: **it worked out**
that way. <code>済(す)ませる</code> is transitive: **I arranged** for it not to
happen.

</div>

<pre>
昼(ひる)は時間(じかん)がなくて、パンだけで済(す)ませた。
I had no time at lunch, so I made do with just some bread.
</pre>

<pre>
昼(ひる)は来客(らいきゃく)が早(はや)く帰(かえ)って、外(そと)に出(で)<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>。
The visitor left early, so I didn't have to go out at lunchtime.
</pre>

In the first the speaker made the choice, and the sentence often carries a hint of
cutting corners. In the second circumstances did the work, and the speaker is
simply glad. Choosing the wrong one flips who is responsible - a small difference
in the verb, a large one in what you are admitting to.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 心配(しんぱい)<b>しずに済(す)んだ</b> → ✅ 心配(しんぱい)<b>せずに済(す)んだ</b>
  `する` uses the stem `せ` in front of `ずに`. This is the same irregularity you
  already met in <code>せずに</code>.

- ❌ 払(はら)う<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b> → ✅ 払(はら)わ<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>
  The base is the ない-stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ 来(き)<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b> → ✅ 来(こ)<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>
  来(く)る is irregular here, exactly as it is in 来(こ)ない.

- ❌ 楽(たの)しみにしていた旅行(りょこう)に行(い)か<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>。 → ✅ 楽(たの)しみにしていた旅行(りょこう)に行(い)けなくなった。
  The pattern means you were spared something. Applied to a treat, it says the trip
  was a chore you escaped.

- ❌ 夕食(ゆうしょく)はおにぎりで<b>済(す)んだ</b>。（meaning "I made do with a rice ball"） → ✅ 夕食(ゆうしょく)はおにぎりで<b>済(す)ませた</b>。
  If you chose it, the verb is transitive.

## Practice

**1.** 保険(ほけん)に入(はい)っていたおかげで、修理代(しゅうりだい)を自分(じぶん)で払(はら)わ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

払(はら)わ<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>（or 払(はら)わずに済(す)みました）

The insurance removed a cost the speaker had been expecting to bear, which is
exactly the relief this pattern reports. The past form is the natural one, because
the escape is already an accomplished fact.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 明日(あした)は祝日(しゅくじつ)だから、会社(かいしゃ)に（行(い)かなくてもいい・行(い)かずに済(す)んだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

会社(かいしゃ)に<b>行(い)かなくてもいい</b>。

Tomorrow has not happened yet, so there is no outcome to report. The sentence is
stating that the obligation does not apply, which is
<code>なくてもいい</code>. <code>ずに済(す)んだ</code> would need the day to be
over.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The rain stopped just in time, so we didn't have to cancel the barbecue."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ちょうど雨(あめ)がやんだので、バーベキューを中止(ちゅうし)せ<b>ずに済(す)みました</b>。

中止(ちゅうし)する is a する-verb, so the stem is <code>せ</code>. Note that the
relief here is about avoiding cancellation - the pattern attaches to the unwanted
action, not to the barbecue.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 傷(きず)は浅(あさ)かったので、病院(びょういん)に行(い)くずに済(す)ませました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 傷(きず)は浅(あさ)かったので、病院(びょういん)に行(い)か<b>ずに済(す)みました</b>。
"The cut was shallow, so I didn't have to go to hospital."

Two repairs. The base is the ない-stem 行(い)か, and the verb should be the
intransitive <code>済(す)む</code> - the shallow cut decided the matter, not
the speaker.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今日(きょう)は残業(ざんぎょう)し<b>なくてもよかった</b>。
(b) 今日(きょう)は残業(ざんぎょう)せ<b>ずに済(す)んだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the obligation was not there - nobody required overtime today. It is a
statement about the rules of the day, and it can even carry a shade of regret, as
in "I needn't have stayed late after all."

(b) says the speaker got out of overtime they had been expecting, and it is warm
with relief. Something specific made it unnecessary - the work finished, a meeting
moved - and the listener will assume there was such a reason.

</details>

# ～ことなく

**Meaning** · without ever doing it; without so much as doing it - the written, weighty way of saying "without"

`ことなく` says the same thing as `ずに`, at a different altitude. It belongs to
writing, and it carries a note of *not even once*: the runner never stopped, the
shop never changed its recipe, nobody ever found out. Use it where the absence is
remarkable. For "I went out without breakfast", it is far too grand.

## Formation

The verb goes in the **dictionary form**, and it stays affirmative - the negation
lives in `なく`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ことなく | 休(やす)む → 休(やす)むことなく |
| Verb (する) | ～することなく | 変化(へんか)する → 変化(へんか)することなく |
| Passive | ～られることなく | 知(し)られる → 知(し)られることなく |
| Emphatic variant | ～ことなしに | 断(ことわ)ることなしに |
| "not without doing" | ～ことなしには～ない | 努力(どりょく)することなしには続(つづ)かない |
| Often paired with | 一度(いちど)も／最後(さいご)まで／誰(だれ)にも | 一度(いちど)も休(やす)むことなく |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front is **positive**.
❌<code>休(やす)まないことなく</code> negates twice and means nothing. Compare
<code>休(やす)まずに</code>, where the negation sits on the verb instead.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `ことなく` takes verbs only. There is no
❌<code>静(しず)かなことなく</code> and no ❌<code>寒(さむ)いことなく</code>;
for adjectives and nouns you need a different construction altogether, such as
<code>～ではなく</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
彼(かれ)は一度(いちど)も休(やす)む<b>ことなく</b>走(はし)った。
He ran the whole way without a single break.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)にも知(し)られる<b>ことなく</b>、工事(こうじ)は終(お)わった。
The work was completed without anyone finding out.
</pre>

<pre>
彼女(かのじょ)は迷(まよ)う<b>ことなく</b>、一番(いちばん)難(むずか)しい道(みち)を選(えら)んだ。
She chose the hardest road without a moment's hesitation.
</pre>

<pre>
この店(みせ)は30年間(ねんかん)、味(あじ)を変(か)える<b>ことなく</b>続(つづ)いている。
For thirty years this shop has carried on without ever changing its flavour.
</pre>

<pre>
台風(たいふう)の夜(よる)も、工場(こうじょう)の機械(きかい)は止(と)まる<b>ことなく</b>動(うご)き続(つづ)けた。
Even on the night of the typhoon, the machines in the plant ran on without stopping.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は最後(さいご)まで一言(ひとこと)も文句(もんく)を言(い)う<b>ことなく</b>作業(さぎょう)を終(お)えた。
He finished the job without one word of complaint from beginning to end.
</pre>

<pre>
争(あらそ)いが再(ふたた)び起(お)こる<b>ことなく</b>、静(しず)かな日々(ひび)が続(つづ)いている。
Quiet days have continued, with the conflict never breaking out again.
</pre>

<pre>
多(おお)くの人(ひと)に支(ささ)えられたからこそ、途中(とちゅう)であきらめる<b>ことなく</b>ここまで来(こ)られた。
It is only because so many people supported me that I got this far without giving up on the way.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** pattern, and a slightly ceremonial one. Its home is narration,
biography, company histories, reports, obituaries and the closing sentence of a
speech. In conversation it sounds like reading aloud; even a formal meeting will
usually reach for `ずに` instead.

Two consequences follow from that register, and both are practical.

First, `ことなく` prefers a **whole stretch of time**. It goes naturally with
一度(いちど)も, 最後(さいご)まで, 30年間(ねんかん),
生涯(しょうがい) - anything that frames a long or complete span. A single
moment's action feels undersized: ❌<code>窓(まど)を閉(し)めることなく出(で)かけた</code>
is not ungrammatical, but it dresses a small oversight in formal robes, and a
Japanese reader would find it faintly comic.

Second, the thing that did not happen must be **worth remarking on**. Something was
expected - a pause, an argument, an interruption, a discovery - and it never came.
That is what makes 止(と)まることなく or 知(し)られることなく carry weight,
and it is why the pattern so often appears alongside 続(つづ)ける,
続(つづ)く and 終(お)える.

The variant `ことなしに` is a shade heavier again and is largely confined to
writing. Its most useful shape is the double negative
<code>～ことなしには～ない</code>, "you cannot Y without X":
努力(どりょく)することなしには上達(じょうたつ)しない. That frame is common
in essays and speeches, and it is worth recognising even if you never write one.

## Contrast

### ～ことなく vs ～ずに・～ないで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning, three registers. `ないで` is speech, `ずに` is
careful speech and writing, `ことなく` is writing with weight behind it.

</div>

<pre>
鍵(かぎ)をかけ<b>ないで</b>出(で)かけてしまった。
I went out without locking up.
</pre>

<pre>
会長(かいちょう)は最後(さいご)まで席(せき)を立(た)つ<b>ことなく</b>話(はなし)を聞(き)いていた。
The chairman listened to the end without once leaving his seat.
</pre>

Swap them and both sentences fail on tone. A forgotten door lock does not deserve
`ことなく`, and a formal account of the chairman's attention would look thin with
`ないで`. Note also the conjugation difference, which is a fast way of telling them
apart: `ずに` and `ないで` sit on a **negative** stem, `ことなく` on the
**dictionary form**.

### ～ことなく vs ～ないまま

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことなく` says something **never interrupted** what was going
on. `ないまま` says something is still **left undone**, and often that it should
not be.

</div>

<pre>
理由(りゆう)を聞(き)か<b>ないまま</b>、彼(かれ)は会社(かいしゃ)を去(さ)った。
He left the company with the reason still never asked.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は理由(りゆう)を話(はな)す<b>ことなく</b>会社(かいしゃ)を去(さ)った。
He left the company without ever explaining why.
</pre>

`ないまま` leaves a loose end hanging, and the sentence usually feels unfinished on
purpose - something ought to have been settled and was not. `ことなく` simply
records that the action never occurred, and can be entirely neutral or even
admiring, as in 一度(いちど)も休(やす)むことなく.

### ～ことなく vs ～ことなしに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことなく` describes **how something happened**. `ことなしに`,
in its usual frame <code>～ことなしには～ない</code>, states a **condition**:
without this, that is impossible.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は振(ふ)り返(かえ)る<b>ことなく</b>歩(ある)いて行(い)った。
He walked away without looking back.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)を重(かさ)ねる<b>ことなしに</b>は、本当(ほんとう)の技術(ぎじゅつ)は身(み)につかない。
Without a run of failures, no real skill is ever acquired.
</pre>

Used without the `は…ない` frame, `ことなしに` is simply a heavier `ことなく` and
the two are interchangeable. Used with it, the sentence becomes an argument rather
than a description, and `ことなく` cannot replace it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 休(やす)ま<b>ないことなく</b>働(はたら)いた。 → ✅ 休(やす)む<b>ことなく</b>働(はたら)いた。
  The negation is already in `なく`. The verb in front stays in the plain
  dictionary form.

- ❌ 休(やす)んだ<b>ことなく</b> → ✅ 休(やす)む<b>ことなく</b>
  Only the dictionary form appears here, whatever the tense of the main verb. The
  past sits at the end of the sentence.

- ❌ 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べる<b>ことなく</b>家(いえ)を出(で)た。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べ<b>ずに</b>家(いえ)を出(で)た。
  Grammatical, but the register is absurdly high for skipping breakfast. Save
  `ことなく` for absences that are actually notable.

- ❌ 部屋(へや)が<b>静(しず)かなことなく</b> → ✅ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かに<b>ならないまま</b>
  `ことなく` attaches to verbs only. Adjectives and nouns need another
  construction entirely.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

一度(いちど)も止(と)む<b>ことなく</b>

<code>一度(いちど)も</code> is the classic partner of this pattern, and three

stays in the dictionary form 止(と)む.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 傘(かさ)を（持(も)たずに・持(も)つことなく）出(で)かけて、びしょ濡(ぬ)れになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

傘(かさ)を持(も)た<b>ずに</b>出(で)かけて、びしょ濡(ぬ)れになった。

Forgetting an umbrella is a small, ordinary lapse, and `ずに` is the right size for
it. `ことなく` would give a rained-on afternoon the tone of a memorial address.
Note the form too: `ずに` needs the ない-stem 持(も)た.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "For fifty years she went on painting, without ever once holding an exhibition."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)は50年間(ねんかん)、一度(いちど)も個展(こてん)を開(ひら)く<b>ことなく</b>描(か)き続(つづ)けた。

A fifty-year span and the phrase "not once" are both signals for `ことなく`. Note

✅ 彼(かれ)は誰(だれ)にも相談(そうだん)する<b>ことなく</b>、一人(ひとり)で決(き)めてしまった。

The `なく` already carries the negative, so the verb in front must be positive.
Writing both produces a double negative that a reader has to stop and untangle.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:

(b) 返事(へんじ)が来(く)る<b>ことなく</b>、一年(いちねん)が過(す)ぎた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

speaker's frustration at a thing that ought to have been settled.

(b) is more detached and more literary - a line of narration recording that no
reply arrived across the whole year. It closes the year off rather than leaving it
hanging, and it would fit a written account far better than a complaint to a
friend.

</details>

# ～抜く

**Meaning** · to carry something right through to the end, in spite of how hard it is

The verb 抜(ぬ)く is "to pull out, to get all the way through". As a suffix it
adds **endurance**: not simply that the action finished, but that the person kept
going when stopping was the easier option. Nothing trivial takes it. You survive an
era, fight a match, think a problem through - you do not 抜(ぬ)く a sandwich.

## Formation

<code>抜(ぬ)く</code> attaches to the **ます-stem** and produces a new verb. It keeps its own
conjugation, which is that of an う-verb (a Group 1 / 五段(ごだん) verb).

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + 抜(ぬ)く | 走(はし)る → 走(はし)り抜(ぬ)く |
| Past | ～抜(ぬ)いた | 考(かんが)え抜(ぬ)いた |
| て-form | ～抜(ぬ)いて | 戦(たたか)い抜(ぬ)いて |
| Negative (う-verb!) | ～抜(ぬ)かない | やり抜(ぬ)かない |
| Potential | ～抜(ぬ)ける | 走(はし)り抜(ぬ)ける |
| Before a noun | ～抜(ぬ)いた + Noun | 考(かんが)え抜(ぬ)いた計画(けいかく) |
| Common members | 生(い)き抜(ぬ)く・やり抜(ぬ)く・考(かんが)え抜(ぬ)く・守(まも)り抜(ぬ)く・悩(なや)み抜(ぬ)く | - |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb must describe something **sustained and hard**:
生(い)きる, 戦(たたか)う, 走(はし)る, 守(まも)る, 考(かんが)える,
悩(なや)む, やる. A momentary action has nothing to endure, which is why
❌<code>メールを書(か)き抜(ぬ)いた</code> is not Japanese.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - not every verb ending in 抜(ぬ)く belongs to this pattern.
見(み)抜(ぬ)く (to see through a lie), 追(お)い抜(ぬ)く (to overtake),
引(ひ)き抜(ぬ)く (to pull out, or to poach a member of staff) are ordinary
compound verbs with their own meanings, built on the "pull out" sense rather than
the "endure to the end" one. Learn them as vocabulary.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
最後(さいご)まで<b>やり抜(ぬ)く</b>つもりだ。
I mean to see it through to the end.
</pre>

<pre>
どんな時代(じだい)でも<b>生(い)き抜(ぬ)く</b>力(ちから)がほしい。
I want the strength to come through whatever the times.
</pre>

<pre>
悩(なや)み<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>末(すえ)に、留学(りゅうがく)を決(き)めた。
After agonising over it at length, I decided to study abroad.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は足(あし)を痛(いた)めながらも、最後(さいご)の一周(いっしゅう)を走(はし)り<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。
Injured leg and all, he ran the final lap to the finish.
</pre>

<pre>
チームは一人(ひとり)退場(たいじょう)になっても、一点(いってん)のリードを守(まも)り<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。
Even a player down, the team held on to their one-goal lead.
</pre>

<pre>
考(かんが)え<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>企画(きかく)だけあって、説明(せつめい)に無駄(むだ)がない。
You can tell the proposal was thought right through - there isn't a wasted word in it.
</pre>

<pre>
祖母(そぼ)は子(こ)どもを三人(さんにん)抱(かか)えて、戦後(せんご)の苦(くる)しい時代(じだい)を生(い)き<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。
With three children to raise, my grandmother came through the hard years after the war.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)もやめたくなったが、仲間(なかま)のおかげで最後(さいご)までやり<b>抜(ぬ)く</b>ことができた。
I wanted to give up more than once, but thanks to my teammates I managed to see it through.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>抜(ぬ)く</code> is **emphatic rather than formal**. It fits speeches, sports commentary,
company slogans, memoirs and encouragement between friends, and it works in plain
and polite forms alike. Nothing about it is stiff; what it is, is *earnest*. A
sentence with <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> in it is paying tribute to somebody's persistence, including
the speaker's own.

That admiration is the reason it cannot be used lightly. Attach it to a small task
and the sentence sounds either boastful or sarcastic - claiming heroism for
answering three emails. The verbs that take it comfortably are ones that come with
a struggle already attached.

Two members have effectively become nouns of their own.
<code>やり抜(ぬ)く力(ちから)</code> is the standard Japanese rendering of
"grit", familiar from education writing, and
<code>生(い)き抜(ぬ)く</code> turns up constantly in war memoirs, disaster
reporting and anything about hard times. If you only ever produce these two, you
will still sound natural.

Watch the conjugation. Because the suffix is 抜(ぬ)く, everything built on it
follows う-verb rules: 抜(ぬ)いた, 抜(ぬ)いて, 抜(ぬ)かない,
抜(ぬ)きます. The `-く` ending misleads nobody, but the past
<code>抜(ぬ)いた</code> - not ❌抜(ぬ)くた - is worth saying out loud a few
times.

## Contrast

### ～抜(ぬ)く vs ～きる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `きる` is about **quantity**: every last one of them. <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> is
about **endurance**: all the way through, and it was hard.

</div>

<pre>
山(やま)のような資料(しりょう)を一晩(ひとばん)で<b>読(よ)みきった</b>。
I got through a mountain of documents in one night.
</pre>

<pre>
苦(くる)しい練習(れんしゅう)に耐(た)え、三年間(さんねんかん)走(はし)り<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。
He endured the punishing training and ran for three years without dropping out.
</pre>

The first counts pages; swap in <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> and it starts to sound like an ordeal
survived. The second measures resolve; swap in `きる` and the three years become a
quantity ticked off. There is a second, unrelated use of `きる` - pushing a state to
its limit, as in 疲(つか)れきった - that <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> does not share at all.

### ～抜(ぬ)く vs ～通(とお)す

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>通(とお)す</code> is about **consistency**: keeping
the same line from start to finish. <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> is about **hardship**: getting through
in spite of it.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は自分(じぶん)の意見(いけん)を最後(さいご)まで言(い)い<b>通(とお)した</b>。
He stuck to his opinion right to the end.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は批判(ひはん)に耐(た)えながら、方針(ほうしん)を守(まも)り<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。
He weathered the criticism and defended his approach to the end.
</pre>

They overlap in <code>やり通(とお)す</code> and
<code>やり抜(ぬ)く</code>, which are close enough to swap in most sentences.
Even there the flavours differ: <code>通(とお)す</code> hints at stubbornness
and can be mildly critical, while <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> is almost always admiring.

### ～抜(ぬ)く vs ～終(お)える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>終(お)える</code> is neutral completion. <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> adds
the difficulty, and the credit for getting past it.

</div>

<pre>
レポートを書(か)き<b>終(お)えて</b>、やっと眠(ねむ)れる。
I've finished writing the report and can finally sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)で山道(やまみち)を歩(ある)き<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。
I walked the whole mountain road alone, all the way.
</pre>

<code>終(お)える</code> says only that the action stopped because it was done, and it fits any
verb at all. <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> is a comment on the person as much as on the action, and the
narrower list of verbs it accepts follows directly from that.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 走(はし)る<b>抜(ぬ)く</b> → ✅ 走(はし)り<b>抜(ぬ)く</b>
  The base is the ます-stem. This is the same shape as
  <code>走(はし)り続(つづ)ける</code>.

- ❌ やり抜(ぬ)く<b>た</b> → ✅ やり<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>
  抜(ぬ)く is an う-verb, so its past is 抜(ぬ)いた and its negative is
  抜(ぬ)かない.

- ❌ ケーキを食(た)べ<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。 → ✅ ケーキを食(た)べ<b>きった</b>。
  Finishing a quantity is `きる`. There is nothing to endure in eating cake, so
  <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> has no work to do.

- ❌ Reading 前(まえ)の走者(そうしゃ)を追(お)い<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b> as "ran to the end" → ✅ it means "overtook the runner ahead".
  <code>追(お)い抜(ぬ)く</code> is an independent verb from the vocabulary,
  built on the "pull past" sense. Check the dictionary before reading a
  抜(ぬ)く compound as endurance.

## Practice

**1.** 三年(さんねん)かかったが、一人(ひとり)で辞書(じしょ)を作(つく)り____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

作(つく)り<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>

Three years of solitary work is exactly the sustained difficulty this suffix marks.
Note the う-verb past: <code>抜(ぬ)いた</code>, never ❌抜(ぬ)くた.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 大盛(おおも)りのラーメンを一人(ひとり)で（食(た)べきった・食(た)べ抜(ぬ)いた）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

大盛(おおも)りのラーメンを一人(ひとり)で<b>食(た)べきった</b>。

A large bowl is a quantity, and getting to the bottom of it is `きる`.
<code>食(た)べ抜(ぬ)く</code> is not an expression Japanese uses at all -
eating is not something one endures to the end.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Whatever people said, she protected her students to the very end."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

周(まわ)りに何(なに)を言(い)われても、彼女(かのじょ)は生徒(せいと)たちを守(まも)り<b>抜(ぬ)いた</b>。

<code>守(まも)り抜(ぬ)く</code> is one of the most natural members of this
family, because protecting somebody under pressure is precisely the kind of
sustained effort the suffix marks. Note 何(なに) before を - the reading would be
なん only in front of で, だ or の.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は最後(さいご)まであきらめるぬいて、ゴールした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 彼(かれ)は最後(さいご)まであきらめ<b>ずに走(はし)り抜(ぬ)いて</b>、ゴールした。
"He reached the finish without ever giving up."

Two problems. The base must be the ます-stem, and more importantly the verb itself
is wrong: <code>抜(ぬ)く</code> marks carrying something **through**, so it cannot attach to
"giving up". Put the endurance on the running and the negation on the giving up.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 課題(かだい)を最後(さいご)まで<b>やり終(お)えた</b>。
(b) 課題(かだい)を最後(さいご)まで<b>やり抜(ぬ)いた</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports that the assignment is done. It says nothing about what it cost,
and it would be the ordinary sentence for a piece of homework.

(b) puts the difficulty in the foreground: there was a real temptation to stop, and
the speaker did not. It is a small claim to credit, which is why it fits a long
project and sounds inflated on a short one.

</details>

# ～かいがある

**Meaning** · the effort was worth it - something came of it that makes the trouble look well spent

甲斐(かい), almost always written in kana, is the *return* on an effort. A
sentence with `かいがある` in it is doing two things at once: reporting a result,
and saying that the work behind it was justified. The negative
<code>かいもなく</code> is the same sentence with the payoff taken away, and it is
just as common.

## Formation

Two shapes matter, and they behave differently. The **clause** pattern
<code>～かいがある</code> reports a payoff; the **suffix** `がい`, voiced, builds
a noun out of a ます-stem.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (た-form) - the usual shape | ～た + かいがある | 練習(れんしゅう)した → 練習(れんしゅう)したかいがある |
| Leading into the result | ～た + かいがあって、… | 待(ま)ったかいがあって、席(せき)が取(と)れた |
| Verb (dictionary) - "worth doing" | ～る + かいがある | 読(よ)む → 読(よ)むかいがある |
| Noun | Noun + の + かいがある | 努力(どりょく)のかいがある |
| Negative | ～かいもなく ／ ～かいがない | 努力(どりょく)のかいもなく |
| Suffix on a ます-stem | ます-stem + がい | やる → やりがい |
| The suffix before a noun | ～がいのある + Noun | やりがいのある仕事(しごと) |
| Common suffix members | 生(い)きがい・働(はたら)きがい・教(おし)えがい・作(つく)りがい | - |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - when you are reporting a **result**, the verb is in the
**た-form**: <code>練習(れんしゅう)したかいがあった</code>. The dictionary form
means "worth doing" and looks forward instead - a different sentence, and one for
which <code>価値(かち)がある</code> is often the more usual phrasing.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the suffix voices: かい → **がい**. It attaches to the ます-stem, so
やる → やり**がい**, 生(い)きる → 生(い)き**がい**, 働(はたら)く →
働(はたら)き**がい**. These are ordinary nouns and take が, は and の like any
other.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)した<b>かいがあった</b>。
All that daily practice paid off.
</pre>

<pre>
やり<b>がいのある</b>仕事(しごと)を探(さが)しています。
I'm looking for work that feels worth doing.
</pre>

<pre>
早起(はやお)きした<b>かいがあって</b>、きれいな朝日(あさひ)が見(み)られた。
Getting up early paid off - I got to watch a beautiful sunrise.
</pre>

<pre>
祖父(そふ)にとって、畑(はたけ)の世話(せわ)が生(い)き<b>がい</b>になっている。
Looking after the vegetable patch is what makes life worth living for my grandfather.
</pre>

<pre>
半年(はんとし)教室(きょうしつ)に通(かよ)った<b>かいがあって</b>、会話(かいわ)が楽(たの)しくなってきた。
Six months of classes have paid off, and conversation is starting to be fun.
</pre>

<pre>
苦労(くろう)して予約(よやく)した<b>かいがある</b>店(みせ)だった。
It turned out to be a restaurant worth all the trouble of booking.
</pre>

<pre>
皆(みな)で夜(よる)遅(おそ)くまで準備(じゅんび)した<b>かいがあって</b>、発表(はっぴょう)は大成功(だいせいこう)だった。
All those late nights of preparation paid off - the presentation was a great success.
</pre>

<pre>
三年間(さんねんかん)練習(れんしゅう)を重(かさ)ねた<b>かいもなく</b>、チームは一回戦(いっかいせん)で敗(やぶ)れた。
For all three years of accumulated training, the team went out in the first round.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`かいがある` is **register-neutral and warm**. It fits ordinary conversation, a
thank-you message, a speech at a party, a magazine profile. Nothing about it is
formal, and nothing about it is slangy. What it always carries is a small emotional
charge: satisfaction that effort was not wasted.

Because of that charge, it needs **effort to have gone in**. A piece of good luck
does not take it - ❌<code>宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たったかいがあった</code>
makes no sense, because nobody worked for a lottery win. The effort can belong to
the speaker, to somebody else, or to a group, but it has to be in the picture.

<code>かいもなく</code> deserves its own line. It is the standard way to say "for
all their effort, …", and Japanese uses it constantly in sports reporting, in
memoirs and in commiseration. The phrase carries genuine sympathy; it is not a
sneer at the effort, but an acknowledgement of it.

The `がい` suffix has grown into a set of high-frequency nouns.
<code>生(い)きがい</code> - what makes life worth living - is culturally weighty
and appears in everything from newspaper columns to retirement planning.
<code>やりがい</code> and <code>働(はたら)きがい</code> are the standard
vocabulary of talking about work. A subtler member is
<code>教(おし)えがいのある学生(がくせい)</code>: a student who *repays* the
teaching, which is a compliment to the student rather than to the teacher.

## Contrast

### ～かいがある vs 価値(かち)がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かいがある` measures the return **on somebody's effort**.
<code>価値(かち)がある</code> measures **the thing's own worth**, effort or no
effort.

</div>

<pre>
この絵(え)には見(み)る<b>価値(かち)がある</b>。
This painting is worth seeing.
</pre>

<pre>
二時間(にじかん)並(なら)んだ<b>かいがあった</b>。
Queuing for two hours turned out to be worth it.
</pre>

The painting has value whether or not anybody troubled to look at it; the queue has
none until somebody has stood in it. That is why
<code>価値(かち)がある</code> attaches happily to things -
<code>価値(かち)のある情報(じょうほう)</code> - while `かいがある` needs a
verb describing what somebody did.

### ～かいがあって vs ～おかげで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>かいがあって</code> credits **the effort itself**.
`おかげで` credits **somebody or something else** for the good outcome.

</div>

<pre>
先生(せんせい)に教(おし)えていただいた<b>おかげで</b>、試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)できました。
Thanks to your teaching, I passed the exam.
</pre>

<pre>
毎晩(まいばん)復習(ふくしゅう)した<b>かいがあって</b>、試験(しけん)に合格(ごうかく)できました。
Reviewing every evening paid off, and I passed the exam.
</pre>

Said to a teacher, the two are very different social moves: the first gives them
the credit, the second keeps it. Both are perfectly polite, but a thank-you note
wants `おかげで`. Note also that `おかげで` takes any cause at all, including luck
and weather, where `かいがある` cannot.

### ～かいがある vs ～だけのことはある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かいがある` says **the effort was repaid**.
<code>だけのことはある</code> says **the reputation was deserved** - it lives up to
what you would expect of it.

</div>

<pre>
さすが一流(いちりゅう)の職人(しょくにん)<b>だけのことはある</b>。
It's exactly what you'd expect from a first-rate craftsman.
</pre>

<pre>
三日(みっか)かけて磨(みが)いた<b>かいがある</b>仕上(しあ)がりだ。
It's a finish that justifies the three days spent polishing it.
</pre>

<code>だけのことはある</code> looks back at a *reason to expect quality* - training,
price, a reputation - and confirms it. `かいがある` looks back at *work* and
confirms that it bought something. The two often fit the same scene, and they
compliment different things.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 練習(れんしゅう)する<b>かいがあって</b>、優勝(ゆうしょう)できた。 → ✅ 練習(れんしゅう)した<b>かいがあって</b>、優勝(ゆうしょう)できた。
  When you are reporting a payoff that has already arrived, the verb goes into the
  た-form.

- ❌ やる<b>かい</b>のある仕事(しごと) → ✅ やり<b>がい</b>のある仕事(しごと)
  The suffix attaches to the ます-stem and voices to `がい`. It is a noun in its own
  right, not the clause pattern.

- ❌ 努力(どりょく)<b>かいがあって</b> → ✅ 努力(どりょく)<b>の</b>かいがあって
  A noun links to <code>かい</code> with `の`, since <code>かい</code> is itself a
  noun.

- ❌ 宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たった<b>かいがあった</b>。 → ✅ 宝(たから)くじが当(あ)たって嬉(うれ)しかった。
  There has to be effort to be repaid. Pure luck leaves the pattern with nothing
  to weigh.

# 気がする・気になる

**Meaning** · 気(き)がする = I have a feeling that; 気(き)になる = it is on my mind, it bothers me

気(き) is the mind's weather - mood, attention, inclination. Japanese builds a
whole family of expressions on it, and two of them do work that English handles
with quite different verbs. <code>気(き)がする</code> reports a hunch you have
not reasoned your way to. <code>気(き)になる</code> reports something that has
snagged your attention and will not let go.

## Usage

<code>気(き)がする</code> follows a **plain-form clause** and turns it into a
hunch: 明日(あした)は晴(は)れる気(き)がする, "I've a feeling it'll clear
up". With a noun or な-adjective the link is `な` -
無理(むり)な気(き)がする - and the softened
<code>ような気(き)がする</code> is available for any of them when you want to
sound less certain still. It conjugates as an ordinary verb:
気(き)がした, 気(き)がします, 気(き)がしない.

<code>気(き)になる</code> is intransitive, so whatever is bothering you takes
**が**: 結果(けっか)が気(き)になる, あの一言(ひとこと)が気(き)になる.
A clause becomes a noun first, with `の` or `か`:
遅(おそ)いのが気(き)になる, 来(く)るかどうかが気(き)になる. The feeling
covers everything from mild curiosity to real anxiety, and nothing about the word
chooses between them - the context does.

Its transitive partner <code>気(き)にする</code> is the same emotion with a
person deciding to have it, and it takes **を**:
人(ひと)の目(め)を気(き)にする. That is why the standard reassurance is
<code>気(き)にしないで</code> - "don't let it get to you" - and not
❌<code>気(き)にならないで</code>, which would be asking someone's attention to
behave itself.

<div class="note">

**Note** - a third member, <code>気(き)に入(い)る</code>, looks like the
others and means something else entirely: to like, to take to. What you like is
marked with `が` or `を` - この色(いろ)が気(き)に入(い)った,
この色(いろ)を気(き)に入(い)っている - and the negative
<code>気(き)に入(い)らない</code> means "I don't care for it".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>気(き)がする</b>。
I have a feeling it's going to rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
誰(だれ)かに見(み)られている<b>気(き)がした</b>。
I had the feeling somebody was watching me.
</pre>

<pre>
さっきの彼(かれ)の一言(ひとこと)が<b>気(き)になって</b>、眠(ねむ)れない。
That one remark of his is preying on my mind, and I can't sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
周(まわ)りの目(め)を<b>気(き)にしすぎる</b>と、自分(じぶん)のやりたいことができなくなる。
If you worry too much about how others see you, you end up unable to do what you want.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

All three expressions are **register-neutral** and completely ordinary. They work
in plain and polite speech, in writing, and in every age group; nothing about them
is casual or formal.

<code>気(き)がする</code> is a hedge, and Japanese speakers use it constantly to
avoid sounding too sure. Compared with <code>と思(おも)う</code> it claims less:
it says the impression arrived on its own rather than that the speaker worked it
out. That makes it useful for softening opinions, and it is why
<code>そんな気(き)がします</code> is such a common answer to a question you do
not want to answer flatly.

<code>気(き)になる</code> spans a wide emotional range.
あの人(ひと)が気(き)になる can mean "I'm worried about her" or "I'm rather
taken with her",
and only the situation decides - which is a small trap, since the romantic reading
is very much alive in ordinary conversation. The noun
<code>気(き)になること</code>, "things I'm wondering about", is a standard
heading in a meeting or a checklist.

<code>気(き)にしないで</code> and its polite
<code>気(き)にしないでください</code> are among the most useful set phrases in
the language: they cover "never mind", "don't worry about it" and "no offence
taken". The blunter <code>気(き)にするな</code> is the plain command form and
belongs between close friends or from a senior to a junior.

## Contrast

### 気(き)になる vs 気(き)にする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>気(き)になる</code> is something happening **to
you**, and takes が. <code>気(き)にする</code> is something **you do**, and
takes を.

</div>

<pre>
試験(しけん)の結果(けっか)<b>が気(き)になって</b>、何(なに)も手(て)につかない。
I'm so anxious about the exam results that I can't settle to anything.
</pre>

<pre>
小(ちい)さなミスをいつまでも<b>気(き)にして</b>いても、仕方(しかた)がない。
There's no point dwelling on a small mistake for ever.
</pre>

The first describes a state the speaker did not choose; the second describes a
habit they could drop. This is exactly why advice and reassurance use
<code>気(き)にする</code>: you can be told to stop doing something, but not to
stop having something happen to you.

### 気(き)がする vs と思(おも)う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と思(おも)う</code> is a **judgement** you would
defend. <code>気(き)がする</code> is an **impression** that simply arrived.

</div>

<pre>
この案(あん)のほうがいい<b>と思(おも)います</b>。
I think this proposal is the better one.
</pre>

<pre>
この案(あん)のほうがいい<b>気(き)がします</b>。
I have a feeling this proposal is the better one.
</pre>

In a meeting the difference is real. The first invites a discussion of your
reasons; the second signals that you have none yet and are going on instinct.
Because it claims so little, <code>気(き)がする</code> is also the safer way to
disagree with someone senior.

### 気(き)になる vs 気(き)に入(い)る

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>気(き)になる</code> means it **occupies** your mind.
<code>気(き)に入(い)る</code> means it **pleases** you.

</div>

<pre>
この時計(とけい)のデザインが<b>気(き)に入(い)った</b>ので、買(か)うことにした。
I liked the design of this watch, so I decided to buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
この時計(とけい)の値段(ねだん)が<b>気(き)になって</b>、まだ買(か)えずにいる。
The price of this watch is nagging at me, and I still haven't been able to buy it.
</pre>

The shapes are close enough that learners swap them, and the result is a
compliment turning into a complaint. Note also the tense habits:
<code>気(き)に入(い)った</code> in the past is the normal way to say "I like
it", where English keeps the present.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 結果(けっか)<b>を</b>気(き)になる。 → ✅ 結果(けっか)<b>が</b>気(き)になる。
  <code>なる</code> is intransitive, so what bothers you is the subject.
  <code>を</code> belongs with <code>気(き)にする</code>.

- ❌ 気(き)に<b>ならないで</b>ください。 → ✅ 気(き)に<b>しないで</b>ください。
  You can ask a person to stop dwelling on something; you cannot ask their
  attention to stop being caught.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>のを</b>気(き)がする。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る<b>気(き)がする</b>。
  The clause attaches directly in its plain form. There is no particle between it
  and <code>気(き)がする</code>.

- ❌ 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)<b>だ気(き)がする</b>。 → ✅ 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)<b>な気(き)がする</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns link with `な` (or with
  <code>ような</code>), never with `だ`.

## Practice

**1.** 会議(かいぎ)で言(い)い過(す)ぎたかもしれない。部長(ぶちょう)の反応(はんのう)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

部長(ぶちょう)の反応(はんのう)<b>が気(き)になる</b>

The manager's reaction is what has caught the speaker's attention, and it did so
without being invited - so the intransitive <code>気(き)になる</code> with `が`
is the fit. <code>気(き)にする</code> would suggest the speaker is choosing to
brood.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 失敗(しっぱい)は誰(だれ)にでもあることだから、そんなに（気(き)にならないで・気(き)にしないで）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そんなに<b>気(き)にしないで</b>。

You are asking the listener to stop dwelling on it, which is an action they can
take, so the transitive <code>気(き)にする</code> is required.
<code>気(き)にならないで</code> would be instructing their attention to behave,
which is not something anyone can promise.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I have a feeling I've met him somewhere before, but I can't remember where."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼(かれ)とはどこかで会(あ)ったことがある<b>気(き)がする</b>が、どこだったか思(おも)い出(だ)せない。

"I have a feeling" is <code>気(き)がする</code>, and the clause in front of it
stays in its plain form with no particle. Using
<code>と思(おも)う</code> here would overstate the case - the speaker has an
impression, not a belief they could argue for.

</details>

# ～ないわけにはいかない

**Meaning** · I can't very well not do it - an obligation I would rather have avoided, and cannot

Read the double negative literally and it comes out as "I have to". That is where
learners stop, and it is why the pattern gets swapped for `なければならない`, which
is not the same sentence at all. Nothing here is compulsory in the sense of a rule.
The speaker *could* stay away, keep quiet, go home. What they cannot do is live
with themselves - or with the people watching - if they do.

## Formation

The verb goes into its **ない-form**, and `いかない` stays in kana. It is the
`いく` of things not going, and it never becomes `いける`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけにはいかない | 謝(あやま)らない → 謝(あやま)らないわけにはいかない |
| Verb (する) | ～しないわけにはいかない | 報告(ほうこく)する → 報告(ほうこく)しないわけにはいかない |
| Verb (来(く)る) | <b>来(こ)ない</b>わけにはいかない | 来(く)る → 来(こ)ないわけにはいかない |
| Polite | ～ないわけにはいきません | 出(だ)さないわけにはいきません |
| Past | ～ないわけにはいかなかった | 行(い)かないわけにはいかなかった |
| Casual, は dropped | ～ないわけにいかない | 謝(あやま)らないわけにいかない |
| Humble / very formal | ～ないわけにはまいりません | 伺(うかが)わないわけにはまいりません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>わけにはいけない</code> is the standard error and it is
worth checking every time. The expression is built on the **negative**
<code>いかない</code>; there is no potential form anywhere in it.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the sentence almost always comes with its **reason** attached, usually
through のだから, ので, 以上(いじょう) or 手前(てまえ). Without one, the
listener is left waiting: an obligation the speaker resents needs a source, and the
source is what makes the sentence intelligible.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
自分(じぶん)の失敗(しっぱい)だから、謝(あやま)ら<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
It was my own mistake, so I can hardly not apologise.
</pre>

<pre>
会費(かいひ)を預(あず)かったのだから、報告(ほうこく)し<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
I took charge of the membership fees, so I can't very well skip reporting on them.
</pre>

<pre>
恩師(おんし)の退職(たいしょく)のお祝(いわ)いですから、顔(かお)を出(だ)さ<b>ないわけにはいきません</b>。
It's a celebration for my old teacher's retirement, so I can hardly fail to show my face.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもが熱(ねつ)を出(だ)したので、今日(きょう)ばかりは早(はや)く帰(かえ)ら<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
My child has a temperature, so today of all days I really can't not go home early.
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)が困(こま)っているのを見(み)てしまった以上(いじょう)、声(こえ)をかけ<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
Having seen the new member of staff struggling, I can hardly walk past without saying something.
</pre>

<pre>
署名(しょめい)を頼(たの)んできたのが町内会(ちょうないかい)の会長(かいちょう)では、協力(きょうりょく)し<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
When it's the head of the neighbourhood association asking you to sign, you can hardly refuse.
</pre>

<pre>
値上(ねあ)げは心苦(こころぐる)しいのですが、材料費(ざいりょうひ)がこれだけ上(あ)がった今(いま)、価格(かかく)を見直(みなお)さ<b>ないわけにはいきません</b>。
Raising our prices pains us, but with material costs up this far we cannot simply leave them alone.
</pre>

<pre>
一度(いちど)約束(やくそく)してしまった手前(てまえ)、雨(あめ)でも息子(むすこ)を動物園(どうぶつえん)へ連(つ)れて行(い)か<b>ないわけにはいかなかった</b>。
Having made the promise, I could hardly not take my son to the zoo, rain or no rain.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everything on this page turns on one point, so it is worth stating plainly.

<div class="rule">

**This is not a synonym for `なければならない`.**
<code>なければならない</code> reports a requirement and says nothing about the
speaker. <code>ないわけにはいかない</code> reports a **reluctance overruled**: the
speaker would prefer not to, and something - a debt, a promise, a position, other
people's eyes - has taken the option away.

</div>

Three consequences follow, and each one rules out sentences that pass every
mechanical test.

**It needs reluctance.** Something you are glad to do cannot take it. ❌
<code>大好(だいす)きな映画(えいが)だから見(み)に行(い)かないわけにはいかない</code>
turns a treat into a duty, which is only funny on purpose.

**It needs a human obligation, not a physical one.** ❌
<code>人間(にんげん)は食(た)べないわけにはいかない</code> is wrong, because
nothing social is stopping you from starving; that is
食(た)べなければ生(い)きられない. Likewise a dosage on a medicine bottle is
an instruction, so it takes <code>飲(の)まなければなりません</code>.

**It needs a reason the listener can see.** Because the compulsion is social rather
than legal, the sentence carries a because-clause almost without exception. That
clause is doing real work: it is the speaker's evidence that they had no honourable
way out.

On register, the pattern is neutral to slightly formal. It fits ordinary
conversation between adults, workplace speech, and writing; nothing about it is
stiff. In quick speech the `は` often drops -
<code>謝(あやま)らないわけにいかない</code> - and in polite speech the whole
thing ends <code>いきません</code>, never ❌いけません.

One more thing it does well: **excusing yourself in advance**.
申(もう)し訳(わけ)ないのですが、明日(あした)は伺(うかが)わないわけにはいかないんです
tells the listener you are not choosing this, which softens a refusal to be
somewhere else.

## Contrast

### ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～わけにはいかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same social pressure, pointing opposite ways. Positive =
**I mustn't**, negative = **I must**. Both mean "I could, and I can't."

</div>

<pre>
会社(かいしゃ)の車(くるま)だから、私用(しよう)で使(つか)う<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
It's a company car, so I can't very well use it for personal errands.
</pre>

<pre>
私(わたし)が預(あず)かった鍵(かぎ)だから、最後(さいご)に戸締(とじ)まりを確認(かくにん)し<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
I'm the one holding the key, so I can't leave without checking the locks.
</pre>

The machinery underneath is identical: an act is available, and the speaker's
position closes it off. The polarity decides which act. Because the negative form
comes out in English as "I have to", it is easy to forget that it belongs to this
family at all - and that is exactly the mistake that makes learners reach for
`なければならない`.

### ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` states a **requirement**.
`ないわけにはいかない` states a **requirement the speaker resents**, and implies
they considered not complying.

</div>

<pre>
全員(ぜんいん)、九時(くじ)までに受付(うけつけ)を済(す)ませ<b>なければなりません</b>。
Everyone must complete registration by nine o'clock.
</pre>

<pre>
幹事(かんじ)を引(ひ)き受(う)けてしまった以上(いじょう)、最後(さいご)まで面倒(めんどう)を見(み)<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
Having taken on the role of organiser, I can hardly not see it through.
</pre>

The first would be printed on a notice; nobody's feelings are in it. The second
could not go on a notice at all, because it is an admission - the speaker wishes
they had never volunteered. Swap them and you either strip the feeling out of a
confession, or you make a registration desk sound as though it were nursing a
grievance.

### ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～ざるを得(え)ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are reluctant. <code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> is
**pushed by circumstances**, and is formal and written.
`ないわけにはいかない` is **held by other people**, and is ordinary speech.

</div>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)が間(ま)に合(あ)わず、納期(のうき)を遅(おく)らせざるを得(え)ませんでした。
The parts did not arrive in time, so we had no choice but to push the delivery date back.
</pre>

<pre>
先方(せんぽう)に頭(あたま)を下(さ)げてもらった手前(てまえ)、こちらも譲(ゆず)ら<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
They went so far as to apologise to us, so we can hardly not give some ground ourselves.
</pre>

In the first, the world made the decision: the parts were simply not there. In the
second, nothing physical has changed - what obliges the speaker is another person's
gesture and the awkwardness of ignoring it. That difference also sets the register:
<code>ざるを得(え)ない</code> belongs to written announcements,
`ないわけにはいかない` to a conversation about people.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 行(い)か<b>ないわけにはいけない</b>。 → ✅ 行(い)か<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。
  The verb is <code>いく</code> in its plain negative. There is no potential form
  in this expression, and ❌いけません is wrong for the same reason.

- ❌ 人間(にんげん)は眠(ねむ)ら<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。 → ✅ 人間(にんげん)は眠(ねむ)らなければ体(からだ)がもたない。
  The obligation has to be social or moral. Biology imposes nothing that a person
  could have chosen to defy for reasons of decency.

- ❌ この薬(くすり)は一日(いちにち)三回(さんかい)飲(の)ま<b>ないわけにはいきません</b>。 → ✅ この薬(くすり)は一日(いちにち)三回(さんかい)飲(の)ま<b>なければなりません</b>。
  Instructions, rules and timetables are requirements, not dilemmas. Save this
  pattern for the moments when you would rather not comply.

- ❌ 行(い)き<b>ないわけにはいかない</b> → ✅ 行(い)か<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>
  The base is the ない-form of the verb, not the ます-stem.

- ❌ 出席(しゅっせき)しないわけにはいかない<b>です</b>。 → ✅ 出席(しゅっせき)し<b>ないわけにはいきません</b>。
  The politeness goes on <code>いく</code> itself.

## Practice

**1.** 一年間(いちねんかん)お世話(せわ)になった先輩(せんぱい)の送別会(そうべつかい)だ。少(すこ)し体調(たいちょう)が悪(わる)くても、出(で)____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

出(で)<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>（or 出(で)ないわけにはいきません）

The speaker is unwell and would rather stay home; what stops them is a year of
being looked after. Obligation plus reluctance plus a reason is the full signature
of this pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: パスポートを申請(しんせい)するときは、写真(しゃしん)を用意(ようい)し（なければなりません・ないわけにはいきません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

写真(しゃしん)を用意(ようい)し<b>なければなりません</b>。

This is a procedural requirement, stated impersonally. Nobody's feelings are
involved and no one is watching to see whether the applicant does the decent thing,
so `なければならない` is the only natural choice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She lent me money without asking any questions, so I can hardly turn her down now."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

彼女(かのじょ)には理由(りゆう)も聞(き)かずにお金(かね)を貸(か)してもらった手前(てまえ)、今回(こんかい)は断(ことわ)ら<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。

"Can hardly turn her down" is the English that maps exactly onto this pattern: the
speaker would like to refuse and is not free to.
<code>手前(てまえ)</code> is a natural way to attach the debt that makes
refusing impossible.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 課長(かちょう)に直接(ちょくせつ)声(こえ)をかけられたので、会議(かいぎ)に出席(しゅっせき)しないわけにはいけませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 課長(かちょう)に直接(ちょくせつ)声(こえ)をかけられたので、会議(かいぎ)に出席(しゅっせき)し<b>ないわけにはいきませんでした</b>。
"The section head asked me directly, so I couldn't very well not attend."

<code>いけません</code> is the potential of a different verb. The polite past of
this expression is <code>いきませんでした</code>, built on
<code>いく</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)には出席(しゅっせき)し<b>なければなりません</b>。
(b) 明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)には出席(しゅっせき)し<b>ないわけにはいきません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply states that attendance is required. It is what you would say if the
meeting were on your schedule and the matter were settled; it reveals nothing about
whether you want to go.

(b) says you would rather not go and are going anyway. It implies you weighed
skipping it and found you could not - because of who asked, what you owe, or how it
would look. A listener hearing (b) will expect a reason, and will read the sentence
as a small confession rather than a report.

</details>
