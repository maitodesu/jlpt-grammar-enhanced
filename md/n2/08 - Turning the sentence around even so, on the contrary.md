# N2 - Turning the sentence around: even so, on the contrary


---

# それなのに

**Meaning** · and yet; despite that - the second sentence goes against what the first one led you to expect, and the speaker minds

`それなのに` is `それ` plus the `のに` you already know, standing at the head of its
own sentence. That inheritance matters: `のに` never reports a contradiction
coldly, and neither does this word. Someone is disappointed, indignant, or at
least taken aback.

## Usage

`それなのに` opens a sentence and is followed by a comma or a pause. The sentence
before it states something that ought to have led somewhere; the sentence after it
says where things actually went.

- After a stated fact: 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだ。それなのに、熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらない。
- After something both speakers can see: もう十時(じゅうじ)だ。それなのに、誰(だれ)も来(こ)ない。
- Clipped in casual speech to plain なのに at the head of the sentence:
  なのに、返事(へんじ)もくれなかった。

What follows is always a **statement**, never an instruction. Because the speaker
is protesting about how things are, an order, a request or an invitation after
`それなのに` collapses - ❌それなのに、早(はや)く来(き)てください. If you want to
lean on someone, the sentence needs だから or ～んだから instead.

It also needs a real let-down. Two sentences that simply agree with each other
have nothing for `それなのに` to do.

## Examples

<pre>
もう五月(ごがつ)だ。<b>それなのに</b>、毎日(まいにち)寒(さむ)い。
It's May already, and yet it's cold every single day.
</pre>

<pre>
妹(いもうと)は毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)している。<b>それなのに</b>、なかなか上達(じょうたつ)しない。
My sister practises every day, and still she's barely improving.
</pre>

<pre>
昨日(きのう)は一日中(いちにちじゅう)引(ひ)っ越(こ)しを手伝(てつだ)ってあげた。<b>それなのに</b>、お礼(れい)の一言(ひとこと)もなかった。
I spent all of yesterday helping him move house - and not so much as a thank you.
</pre>

<pre>
医者(いしゃ)に止(と)められているらしい。<b>それなのに</b>、祖父(そふ)は毎晩(まいばん)お酒(さけ)を飲(の)んでいる。
The doctor has apparently told him to stop, and yet my grandfather drinks every night.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The word is neutral for politeness - it sits happily in front of a です・ます
sentence - but it is never neutral in feeling. What it adds is 不満(ふまん): a
complaint, or at least a raised eyebrow. That is why it is worth thinking twice
before using it about a superior's behaviour, even in polite Japanese. The
grammar is respectful; the sentence is still an accusation.

In casual speech the それ is usually dropped and people simply start the sentence
with なのに. In writing, `それなのに` is fine in essays, letters and fiction. Formal
reports and academic prose prefer にもかかわらず or それにもかかわらず, which say the
same thing with the emotion drained out of it.

There is no gender or age marking here, and no contraction beyond the なのに above.

## Contrast

### それなのに vs それでも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `それなのに` complains that reality went the wrong way.
`それでも` says that somebody carried on regardless.

</div>

<pre>
雨(あめ)が激(はげ)しく降(ふ)っていた。<b>それでも</b>、試合(しあい)は最後(さいご)まで続(つづ)けられた。
It was pouring with rain. Even so, the match was played to the end.
</pre>

<pre>
天気(てんき)は最高(さいこう)だった。<b>それなのに</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
The weather was perfect. And yet the match was called off.
</pre>

The second half of a `それでも` sentence is usually somebody's will pushing through
an obstacle, and the tone is admiring or stubborn. The second half of a
`それなのに` sentence is the thing that went wrong, and the tone is sour. Swap them
and both sentences stop making sense.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 約束(やくそく)したよね。それなのに、今度(こんど)は忘(わす)れないでください。 → ✅ 約束(やくそく)したんだから、今度(こんど)は忘(わす)れないでください。
  A request cannot follow `それなのに`. The word looks backwards at a broken
  expectation; it cannot push the listener forwards.

- ❌ 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)いそれなのに、味(あじ)は普通(ふつう)だ。 → ✅ 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い。<b>それなのに</b>、味(あじ)は普通(ふつう)だ。（or 高(たか)い<b>のに</b>）
  `それなのに` starts a new sentence. Inside one sentence the connector is plain
  `のに`, with no それ and no full stop.

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)も忙(いそが)しかった。それなのに、今日(きょう)も忙(いそが)しい。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)も忙(いそが)しかった。<b>そのうえ</b>、今日(きょう)も忙(いそが)しい。
  Two busy days point the same way, so there is no contradiction to protest
  about. `それなのに` needs the second sentence to defeat the first.

## Practice

**1.** 三回(さんかい)もメールを送(おく)った。____、返事(へんじ)が来(こ)ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それなのに</b>、返事(へんじ)が来(こ)ない。

Three emails ought to produce one reply; they did not, and the speaker is
annoyed about it. That combination of a defeated expectation and a grumble is
exactly what this word marks.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
足(あし)を痛(いた)めていた。（それなのに・それでも）、彼(かれ)は最後(さいご)まで走(はし)りきった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それでも</b>、彼(かれ)は最後(さいご)まで走(はし)りきった。

The injury is an obstacle and he beat it, so the sentence admires him.
`それなのに` would turn finishing the race into something regrettable, which is
the opposite of what is meant.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 彼(かれ)は新人(しんじん)だ。<b>それなのに</b>、誰(だれ)よりも仕事(しごと)が速(はや)い。
(b) 彼(かれ)は新人(しんじん)だ。<b>それでも</b>、誰(だれ)よりも仕事(しごと)が速(はや)い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are grammatical, and both mean roughly "he is new but faster than anyone".
Sentence (a) treats being fast as something that clashes with being new - the
speaker is surprised, and depending on tone can even sound put out that a
newcomer is outperforming everyone. Sentence (b) treats his newness as a
handicap he overcomes, so it reads as straightforward praise.

</details>

# それにしても

**Meaning** · even so; all the same - you accept the reason you have just been given, and still find the situation excessive

`それにしても` grants the point and then refuses to let it settle the matter.
Traffic explains being late; it does not explain being ninety minutes late. The
word marks the gap between what the excuse covers and what actually happened.

## Usage

`それにしても` opens a sentence, followed by a comma or a pause. In front of it
sits a reason - stated by the other person, stated by you, or simply obvious.
After it comes a remark about **degree**, and that remark is usually loaded:

- with ～すぎる: それにしても、高(たか)すぎる。
- with よく～ね / ～ものだ: それにしても、よく覚(おぼ)えているね。
- with a rhetorical ～だろう / ～じゃないか: それにしても、ひどいんじゃないか。

It also works with no visible first half at all. Walking into a hot room, a
speaker can simply say それにしても暑(あつ)いですね - the "granted" half is the
weather everyone has been talking about all week.

What `それにしても` never does is deny the first half. If you want to contradict
it, you want しかし or でも.

## Examples

<pre>
渋滞(じゅうたい)だろう。<b>それにしても</b>、遅(おそ)すぎる。
He's probably stuck in traffic. Even so, this is far too late.
</pre>

<pre>
安(やす)いのはありがたい。<b>それにしても</b>、この値段(ねだん)は変(へん)じゃないか。
Cheap is welcome, but all the same, isn't this price a bit strange?
</pre>

<pre>
新人(しんじん)だから仕方(しかた)がないとは思(おも)う。<b>それにしても</b>、あの態度(たいど)はないだろう。
He's new, so I suppose it can't be helped - but even so, that attitude is out of order.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)が近(ちか)いのは知(し)っていた。<b>それにしても</b>、一日(いちにち)十時間(じゅうじかん)の勉強(べんきょう)をよく続(つづ)けられるものだ。
I knew the exam was coming up. All the same, ten hours of study a day - I don't know how he keeps it up.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a spoken favourite, and it carries evaluation rather than logic. Most of
the time the evaluation is あきれ - a mild "that's beyond a joke" - but it can
just as easily be admiration, as the last example above shows. The word itself is
neutral; the adjective after it decides the mood.

Politeness is unaffected: それにしても遅(おそ)いですね is perfectly polite. It
appears in writing too, in essays and columns, wherever a writer concedes a point
and then digs in.

In conversation it is often left hanging - 「それにしても……」with the rest
supplied by a sigh. That is standard, not lazy.

One structural note. The `にしても` you met attached to nouns and clauses -
高(たか)いにしても、買(か)うしかない - is the same construction with something
else in the それ slot. `それにしても` is just the version whose "that" is the whole
of what was said before, and long use has turned it into a fixed opener.

## Contrast

### それにしても vs それでも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `それでも` says the outcome held **despite** the obstacle.
`それにしても` accepts the explanation and complains that the **degree** is still
unreasonable.

</div>

<pre>
道(みち)が混(こ)んでいた。<b>それでも</b>、開演(かいえん)には間(ま)に合(あ)った。
The roads were jammed. Even so, we made it before curtain-up.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が混(こ)んでいたそうだ。<b>それにしても</b>、二時間(にじかん)はかかりすぎだ。
Apparently the roads were jammed. All the same, two hours is far too long.
</pre>

`それでも` reports a result that survived; `それにしても` passes judgement on a
result that did not. A quick test: if the second sentence contains a number, an
extreme adjective or ～すぎる, it is almost certainly `それにしても`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ それにしても、明日(あした)は晴(は)れるでしょう。 → ✅ <b>でも</b>、明日(あした)は晴(は)れるでしょう。
  A flat prediction gives the word nothing to react to. `それにしても` needs
  something striking, excessive or surprising to comment on.

- ❌ 忙(いそが)しいそれにしても、連絡(れんらく)ぐらいできる。 → ✅ 忙(いそが)しいにしても、連絡(れんらく)ぐらいできる。
  Attached directly to a clause, the form is plain `にしても`. The それ version can
  only stand at the head of its own sentence.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)だ。それにしても、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)ってください。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)だ。<b>だから</b>、傘(かさ)を持(も)って行(い)ってください。
  The word introduces a comment, not an instruction. Requests and orders after it
  sound like two unrelated sentences glued together.

## Practice

**1.** 忙(いそが)しいのはわかる。____、一週間(いっしゅうかん)も返事(へんじ)がないのは困(こま)る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それにしても</b>、一週間(いっしゅうかん)も返事(へんじ)がないのは困(こま)る。

The speaker accepts the excuse in the first sentence and then argues that a
whole week goes well beyond what it can cover. Granting the reason and objecting
to the degree is the core of this word.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
準備(じゅんび)が足(た)りなかった。（それにしても・それでも）、発表(はっぴょう)は無事(ぶじ)に終(お)わった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それでも</b>、発表(はっぴょう)は無事(ぶじ)に終(お)わった。

The presentation succeeded in spite of the poor preparation, so the sentence
needs the concessive that lets a result survive an obstacle. `それにしても` would
leave the listener waiting for a criticism that never arrives.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I know it was his first time. Even so, forgetting the tickets is a bit much."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

初(はじ)めてだったのはわかる。<b>それにしても</b>、チケットを忘(わす)れるのはひどい。

The first sentence concedes the excuse and the second one says it does not
stretch far enough, which is precisely the division of labour `それにしても`
marks. Ending with ～すぎる - 忘(わす)れるのはひどすぎる - is equally natural.

</details>

# もっとも

**Meaning** · but then again; mind you - a reservation the speaker adds to their own statement, trimming it back a little

You say something, and then you notice it was slightly too broad. `もっとも`
introduces the correction you make to yourself. It does not cancel what you said;
it fences off the part that was overstated.

## Usage

`もっとも` opens a sentence of its own, after your own preceding statement, and is
followed by a comma or a pause. Three things are typical of it:

- The reservation usually trails off in が / けれど / けど, leaving it soft:
  日本語(にほんご)は話(はな)せます。もっとも、日常(にちじょう)会話(かいわ)だけですが。
- It restricts, it does not reverse. Everything before it still stands.
- It is almost always the speaker qualifying **their own** claim, not somebody
  else's.

Written in kana. The kanji 尤(もっと)も exists but is rare in modern text, and
writing it invites confusion with two other words that sound identical: the
superlative <code>最(もっと)も</code> ("the most") and the adjective もっともな
("reasonable, quite right", as in ごもっともです). Only the word on this page opens
a sentence and takes a comma.

## Examples

<pre>
日本語(にほんご)は話(はな)せます。<b>もっとも</b>、簡単(かんたん)な会話(かいわ)だけですが。
I can speak Japanese - simple conversation only, mind you.
</pre>

<pre>
去年(きょねん)より売上(うりあげ)は伸(の)びた。<b>もっとも</b>、目標(もくひょう)には届(とど)かなかったが。
Sales were up on last year. They fell short of the target, though.
</pre>

<pre>
この辺(へん)は静(しず)かでとても住(す)みやすい。<b>もっとも</b>、駅(えき)まで歩(ある)いて三十分(さんじゅっぷん)かかるけれど。
This area is quiet and very easy to live in. Mind you, it's a thirty-minute walk to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
来週(らいしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)には出席(しゅっせき)するつもりです。<b>もっとも</b>、出張(しゅっちょう)が延(の)びた場合(ばあい)は欠席(けっせき)させていただきます。
I intend to be at next week's meeting - though if my business trip runs over, I'll have to miss it.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`もっとも` belongs to careful, considered language: essays, columns, and the kind
of speech where someone is weighing their words. It is not stiff, but it is a
half-step above everyday chat, where the same move is made with でも、まあ or
ただ - 「安(やす)いよ。まあ、味(あじ)は期待(きたい)しないでね」.

The tone it carries is honesty rather than retreat. Adding a `もっとも` clause
shows you have thought about the limits of your own claim, which is why it turns
up so often in book reviews, product write-ups and expert commentary. Overused it
can sound fussy, as though you cannot leave a sentence alone.

It is not marked for age or gender, and it has no contracted form.

## Contrast

### もっとも vs ただし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ただし` states a **condition or rule** that binds the reader.
`もっとも` adds the **speaker's own afterthought**, and binds nobody.

</div>

<pre>
入場(にゅうじょう)は無料(むりょう)です。<b>ただし</b>、予約(よやく)が必要(ひつよう)です。
Admission is free, provided you book in advance.
</pre>

<pre>
入場(にゅうじょう)は無料(むりょう)です。<b>もっとも</b>、中(なか)はいつも混(こ)んでいますが。
Admission is free. It's always packed inside, mind you.
</pre>

The first sentence tells you what you must do; the second tells you what the
speaker thinks you should bear in mind. Notices, rules and contracts therefore
take `ただし` and almost never `もっとも`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この案(あん)はよくできている。もっとも、私(わたし)は反対(はんたい)だ。 → ✅ この案(あん)はよくできている。<b>しかし</b>、私(わたし)は反対(はんたい)だ。
  Flat opposition is しかし's job. `もっとも` narrows a claim; it cannot throw it
  out.

- ❌ もっとも高(たか)い山(やま)に登(のぼ)った。（intending "but then again, we climbed a mountain"） → ✅ 山(やま)に登(のぼ)った。<b>もっとも</b>、低(ひく)い山(やま)だけれど。
  With no comma and an adjective straight after it, the word is read as the
  superlative <code>最(もっと)も</code>, "the highest mountain". The conjunction
  needs a pause and a full clause behind it.

- ❌ 便利(べんり)だもっとも、値段(ねだん)が高(たか)い。 → ✅ 便利(べんり)だ。<b>もっとも</b>、値段(ねだん)が高(たか)いが。
  It opens a new sentence. Inside a single sentence the connector is が or けれど.

## Practice

**1.** 今度(こんど)の部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)くて気(き)に入(い)っている。____、家賃(やちん)は前(まえ)より高(たか)いが。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もっとも</b>、家賃(やちん)は前(まえ)より高(たか)いが。

The speaker likes the flat and stands by that, then admits one point against it.
A self-added reservation ending in が is the standard shape for this word.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
会員(かいいん)以外(いがい)の方(かた)もご利用(りよう)になれます。（もっとも・ただし）、料金(りょうきん)が異(こと)なります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ただし</b>、料金(りょうきん)が異(こと)なります。

This is a notice setting out the terms on which non-members may use the
facility, so the qualification is binding. `もっとも` would make an official rule
sound like a personal aside.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この辞書(じしょ)は役(やく)に立(た)つ。<b>しかし</b>、例文(れいぶん)が少(すく)ない。
(b) この辞書(じしょ)は役(やく)に立(た)つ。<b>もっとも</b>、例文(れいぶん)が少(すく)ないが。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In (a) the two halves are set against each other as equals: the dictionary is
useful, but here is a real complaint, and the reader is left weighing them up.
In (b) the verdict has already been delivered - it is a useful dictionary - and
the shortage of examples is a footnote the speaker adds for honesty's sake. The
recommendation survives (b) intact; it wobbles in (a).

</details>

# かえって

**Meaning** · on the contrary; if anything - the result came out backwards from what was intended or expected

Somebody tries to fix something and makes it worse. Somebody takes the fast route
and arrives later. `かえって` is the word that marks that reversal, and it always
points at a real outcome, not at an opinion.

## Usage

`かえって` is an ordinary adverb, so unlike それなのに or しかし it lives **inside**
the second clause rather than in front of the sentence. It normally sits just
before the verb or adjective that names the backwards result.

- After a ～たら / ～と clause: 急(いそ)いだら、かえって時間(じかん)がかかった。
- After a full stop: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。かえって暑(あつ)くなった。
- After a ～ては clause: 手伝(てつだ)っては、かえって迷惑(めいわく)だろう。

Two things have to be in place. First, an action or a condition that gave you a
reason to expect one thing. Second, an outcome that is the reverse of it.
`かえって` cannot open a conversation, because on its own it has no expectation to
overturn.

Usually written in kana. The kanji 却(かえ)って appears in print, and is worth
recognising.

## Examples

<pre>
近道(ちかみち)をしたら、<b>かえって</b>時間(じかん)がかかった。
I took a shortcut and it actually took longer.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)けたら、<b>かえって</b>暑(あつ)くなった。
I opened the window and it just got hotter.
</pre>

<pre>
手伝(てつだ)おうとしたが、不慣(ふな)れで<b>かえって</b>邪魔(じゃま)になってしまった。
I tried to help, but I didn't know what I was doing and only got in the way.
</pre>

<pre>
子(こ)どもに勉強(べんきょう)しろと言(い)いすぎると、<b>かえって</b>やる気(き)をなくしてしまうものだ。
Push a child to study too hard and you end up killing their motivation instead.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and common in speech and writing alike. Nothing about it is
formal or casual, and it is not marked for age or gender.

The reversal it reports is usually unwelcome - the medicine that keeps you awake,
the apology that makes things worse - but it does not have to be. 
遅(おく)れたおかげで、かえってゆっくり座(すわ)れた, "being late actually got me a
seat", is perfectly natural. What is fixed is the reversal, not the misfortune.

It is also the polite way to decline a return favour. When someone gives you
something back for a gift, the set line is
かえって気(き)をつかわせてしまって、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません - "I've ended up
putting you to trouble instead". Learning that one sentence buys you a very
common social moment.

## Contrast

### かえって vs むしろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かえって` reports a **result** that came out backwards.
`むしろ` states the speaker's **judgement** that of two descriptions, the second
fits better.

</div>

<pre>
謝(あやま)ったら、<b>かえって</b>怒(おこ)らせてしまった。
I apologised and only made him angrier.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は冷(つめ)たいというより、<b>むしろ</b>照(て)れているのだと思(おも)う。
He isn't cold so much as embarrassed, I'd say.
</pre>

An English test that works surprisingly well: if you can say "and it only made
things worse" or "it ended up the other way round", the word is `かえって`. If you
can say "if anything, I'd say…", it is `むしろ`. The first describes the world,
the second describes the speaker's take on it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 兄(あに)より、かえって弟(おとうと)のほうが背(せ)が高(たか)い。 → ✅ 兄(あに)より、<b>むしろ</b>弟(おとうと)のほうが背(せ)が高(たか)い。
  Simply comparing two people is not a reversal of anything. Without a thwarted
  intention behind it, `かえって` has nothing to reverse.

- ❌ かえって、明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうです。 → ✅ ところが、明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうです。
  `かえって` is not a sentence connector meaning "however". It needs a stated
  action or condition in the same breath, and it sits next to the verb.

- ❌ 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら、熱(ねつ)がかえって下(さ)がった。 → ✅ 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら、熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がった。
  Medicine bringing a fever down is exactly what was expected, so nothing has
  been reversed. Adding `かえって` makes the sentence contradict itself.

## Practice

**1.** 雨(あめ)の日(ひ)に車(くるま)で行(い)ったら、渋滞(じゅうたい)で____遅(おそ)くなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

渋滞(じゅうたい)で<b>かえって</b>遅(おそ)くなった。

Taking the car was meant to save time in the rain and did the opposite. The
adverb goes immediately before the phrase that states the backwards outcome.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural adverb:
静(しず)かな場所(ばしょ)より、（かえって・むしろ）少(すこ)し音(おと)がある場所(ばしょ)のほうが集中(しゅうちゅう)できる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>むしろ</b>少(すこ)し音(おと)がある場所(ばしょ)のほうが集中(しゅうちゅう)できる。

Nothing has gone wrong here; the speaker is choosing between two kinds of place
and saying which one suits them. That preference between stated alternatives is
`むしろ`, not `かえって`.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら、眠(ねむ)れなくなった。
(b) 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら、<b>かえって</b>眠(ねむ)れなくなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) just reports two events in order: the pill was taken, and sleep did
not come. Sentence (b) adds the speaker's point - the pill was taken *in order*
to sleep, and it produced the exact opposite. `かえって` is what tells the
listener that an intention was defeated rather than merely unfulfilled.

</details>

# 逆に

**Meaning** · conversely; the other way round - the situation comes out flipped, roles and all

逆(ぎゃく) is the noun "the reverse", and 逆(ぎゃく)に is its adverb: *in
reverse*. Where かえって says an effort backfired, 逆(ぎゃく)に says the arrow
turned around - the scolder got scolded, the cheap shop turned out to be the
expensive one.

## Usage

逆(ぎゃく)に is an adverb and stands inside the clause it modifies, usually
right before the verb. It does three jobs:

- **A literal swap.** The two sides trade places:
  注意(ちゅうい)したら、逆(ぎゃく)に怒(おこ)られた。
- **The other side of an argument.** Fixed phrases:
  逆(ぎゃく)に言(い)えば…, 逆(ぎゃく)に考(かんが)えれば…, meaning "put the
  other way round…".
- **Turning a question back.** 逆(ぎゃく)に聞(き)きますが… - "let me ask you
  the same thing".

The related forms are worth knowing because they are ordinary noun grammar:
逆(ぎゃく)だ ("it's the other way round"), 逆(ぎゃく)の意見(いけん) ("the
opposite view"), 逆(ぎゃく)になる ("to be reversed"). Only the に form is the
adverb.

## Examples

<pre>
注意(ちゅうい)したら、<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>怒(おこ)られた。
I told him off and got told off myself instead.
</pre>

<pre>
駅前(えきまえ)のほうが高(たか)いと思(おも)っていたが、<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>安(やす)かった。
I'd assumed the shops by the station were pricier, but it was the other way round.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>言(い)えば、条件(じょうけん)さえ合(あ)えばいつでもお引(ひ)き受(う)けできます。
Turning that around: as long as the terms suit, we can take it on any time.
</pre>

<pre>
助(たす)けるつもりで口(くち)を出(だ)したのに、<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>話(はなし)をこじらせてしまった。
I stepped in meaning to help and ended up tangling the whole thing up instead.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very frequent. It appears in business writing, in argument,
and constantly in conversation. Stiffer written registers have
反対(はんたい)に available as a near-interchangeable alternative.

One thing to be aware of rather than to copy: in casual speech, especially among
younger speakers, 逆(ぎゃく)に has drifted into a general hedge, something like
"actually" or "if anything", often with no genuine opposite in sight -
「逆(ぎゃく)に、それでいいと思(おも)う」. That is a real and widespread usage, but
it is a spoken habit, and in writing or in an exam answer it will read as
imprecise. Use the word where there is an actual reversal.

The noun 逆(ぎゃく) also lives in set phrases worth having:
逆(ぎゃく)に言(い)えば, 話(はなし)が逆(ぎゃく)だ ("you've got that
backwards"), 逆効果(ぎゃくこうか) ("counterproductive").

## Contrast

### 逆(ぎゃく)に vs かえって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 逆(ぎゃく)に flips the **direction**: A did B becomes B did
A. `かえって` says an **intention was defeated**, and the result was the opposite
of what it aimed at.

</div>

<pre>
教(おし)えるつもりが、<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>教(おし)わることになった。
I went in to teach and ended up being taught.
</pre>

<pre>
冷(ひ)やしたら、<b>かえって</b>痛(いた)みがひどくなった。
I put ice on it and the pain only got worse.
</pre>

Where a sentence names both sides of a swap - teach and be taught, cheaper and
dearer - only 逆(ぎゃく)に fits comfortably. Where the point is that your effort
backfired, both are possible and かえって is the more idiomatic. Many sentences
accept either, and the difference is one of emphasis: direction versus defeat.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 逆(ぎゃく)に、山田(やまだ)さんは来(こ)なかった。 → ✅ <b>しかし</b>、山田(やまだ)さんは来(こ)なかった。
  逆(ぎゃく)に is not a general "however". Somebody failing to turn up is not
  the reverse of anything.

- ❌ 私(わたし)の意見(いけん)は逆(ぎゃく)にです。 → ✅ 私(わたし)の意見(いけん)は逆(ぎゃく)です。
  逆(ぎゃく) is a noun, so a sentence that simply asserts "it's the opposite"
  ends in だ・です. The に only appears when the word is modifying a verb.

- ❌ 安(やす)い逆(ぎゃく)に、質(しつ)もいい。 → ✅ 安(やす)い。<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>質(しつ)が心配(しんぱい)になるほどだ。
  It does not join two clauses the way が or のに do. It belongs to the second
  clause, next to the verb, not between them.

## Practice

**1.** 後輩(こうはい)にアドバイスをしたつもりが、____勉強(べんきょう)させられた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>勉強(べんきょう)させられた。

Adviser and advised have swapped places, which is the clearest case for this
word. Note that it goes inside the second clause, immediately before the verb.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural adverb:
節約(せつやく)しようと安(やす)い靴(くつ)を買(か)ったら、すぐに壊(こわ)れて（逆(ぎゃく)に・かえって）高(たか)くついた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>かえって</b>高(たか)くついた。

The point is that an attempt to save money defeated itself, which is かえって's
territory. 逆(ぎゃく)に is not wrong here, but it puts the stress on the
direction of the outcome rather than on the failed intention.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Put the other way round, if we can't finish by Friday there's no point starting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>言(い)えば、金曜日(きんようび)までに終(お)わらないなら始(はじ)める意味(いみ)がない。

"Put the other way round" is the fixed phrase 逆(ぎゃく)に言(い)えば, which
restates a point from the opposite side. It opens the sentence and is followed by
the whole restatement.

</details>

# むしろ

**Meaning** · rather; if anything - of the two descriptions on the table, the second one is the truer

`むしろ` always involves a choice. One candidate has been named or is obvious, and
the speaker sets it aside in favour of another: not cold, if anything pleasantly
cool; not scolding him, better to praise him. It reports a judgement, not an
event.

## Usage

`むしろ` is an adverb and stands immediately before the option the speaker
endorses. The rejected option is normally spelled out in front of it, in one of
these frames:

- <code>AというよりむしろB</code> - 趣味(しゅみ)というより、むしろ仕事(しごと)に近(ちか)い。
- <code>AよりむしろBのほうが～</code> - 夏(なつ)より、むしろ冬(ふゆ)のほうが好(す)きだ。
- <code>AではなくむしろB</code> - 失敗(しっぱい)ではなく、むしろ成功(せいこう)だと言(い)える。

It can also answer a question by rejecting the framing behind it:
「嫌(きら)いなの?」「いや、むしろ好(す)きなくらいだ。」Even there the rejected
alternative is present - the questioner supplied it.

Because it is a judgement, `むしろ` sits comfortably with ～ほうがいい, ～べきだ,
～と思(おも)う and ～くらいだ.

## Examples

<pre>
寒(さむ)いというより、<b>むしろ</b>涼(すず)しくて気持(きも)ちがいい。
It's not so much cold as pleasantly cool.
</pre>

<pre>
失敗(しっぱい)を隠(かく)すより、<b>むしろ</b>早(はや)く報告(ほうこく)したほうがいい。
Better to report a mistake straight away than to hide it.
</pre>

<pre>
あの人(ひと)は厳(きび)しいのではなく、<b>むしろ</b>心配(しんぱい)してくれているのだと思(おも)う。
I don't think she's being harsh - if anything, she's worried about us.
</pre>

<pre>
給料(きゅうりょう)が上(あ)がるより、<b>むしろ</b>休(やす)みが増(ふ)えるほうがありがたいという人(ひと)も多(おお)い。
Plenty of people would rather have more days off than a pay rise.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Mildly bookish, but entirely at home in speech. You will hear it in discussion,
in interviews and in careful conversation, and read it constantly in essays and
opinion pieces. It is not marked for age or gender and has no casual contraction.

Its social value is that it lets you disagree without saying no. Answering
「大変(たいへん)でしょう」 with
「いえ、むしろ楽(たの)しんでいます」 rejects the premise of the question and
sounds gracious doing it. That is why `むしろ` is so common in polite
disagreement: the listener's description is not called wrong, merely improved on.

With くらい or ほど it becomes an understatement device -
むしろ気(き)が楽(らく)になったくらいだ, "if anything it was a relief" - and that
combination is very common in speech.

## Contrast

### むしろ vs 逆(ぎゃく)に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - 逆(ぎゃく)に says the situation came out **flipped**.
`むしろ` says that of two candidates, the speaker **picks** the second.

</div>

<pre>
叱(しか)られると思(おも)ったら、<b>逆(ぎゃく)に</b>ほめられた。
I was braced for a telling-off and got praised instead.
</pre>

<pre>
叱(しか)るより、<b>むしろ</b>ほめたほうが伸(の)びる。
People improve more from praise than from being told off.
</pre>

The first sentence reports what happened; the second offers advice. Where the
sentence records an event that turned out backwards, you want 逆(ぎゃく)に or
かえって. Where it weighs two options and comes down on one side, only `むしろ`
does that.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら、むしろ熱(ねつ)が上(あ)がった。 → ✅ 薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだら、<b>かえって</b>熱(ねつ)が上(あ)がった。
  A result that betrayed the intention behind it is かえって. `むしろ` needs two
  candidate descriptions for the speaker to choose between, and a rising
  temperature is not a description of anything.

- ❌ この映画(えいが)はむしろ面白(おもしろ)かった。 → ✅ この映画(えいが)は期待(きたい)していなかったが、<b>むしろ</b>面白(おもしろ)かったくらいだ。
  With nothing to reject, `むしろ` leaves the sentence hanging. Name the
  alternative - or make it plain from the context - and the word snaps into
  place.

- ❌ 紅茶(こうちゃ)より、むしろコーヒーより好(す)きだ。 → ✅ 紅茶(こうちゃ)より、<b>むしろ</b>コーヒーのほうが好(す)きだ。
  Only the rejected option takes より. What follows `むしろ` is the chosen one, and
  it takes のほうが or nothing at all.

## Practice

**1.** 彼(かれ)の話(はなし)は説明(せつめい)というより、____言(い)い訳(わけ)に聞(き)こえた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

というより、<b>むしろ</b>言(い)い訳(わけ)に聞(き)こえた。

<code>AというよりむしろB</code> is the standard frame: A is named and set aside, and
the word introduces the description the speaker thinks fits better.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural adverb:
早(はや)く着(つ)こうとタクシーに乗(の)ったが、渋滞(じゅうたい)で（むしろ・かえって）遅(おそ)くなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>かえって</b>遅(おそ)くなった。

The taxi was taken in order to arrive sooner and produced the opposite result,
so this is a defeated intention rather than a choice between descriptions.
`むしろ` would need something like "rather than a taxi, the train" to work.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 今回(こんかい)の結果(けっか)は残念(ざんねん)だが、いい経験(けいけん)になった。
(b) 今回(こんかい)の結果(けっか)は残念(ざんねん)というより、<b>むしろ</b>いい経験(けいけん)になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) accepts both labels at once: it was a disappointment *and* it was
useful, held side by side. Sentence (b) rejects the first label in favour of the
second - calling it a disappointment misses the point, and "a good experience" is
the accurate description. `むしろ` replaces a characterisation rather than adding
to it.

</details>

# したがって

**Meaning** · therefore; accordingly - the formal written "so", presenting what follows as a conclusion that anyone would draw

`したがって` belongs to documents. It says: the facts above are established, and
this is what follows from them. The voice behind it is institutional rather than
personal - a report, a notice, a set of findings - which is why it sounds so
strange in the middle of a chat.

## Usage

`したがって` opens a sentence, after a full stop, and is followed by a comma. The
sentence before it supplies established facts; the sentence after it states the
conclusion or the measure that follows.

- Conclusion drawn: 回答(かいとう)数(すう)が少(すく)ない。したがって、一般化(いっぱんか)はできない。
- Measure announced: 台風(たいふう)が接近(せっきん)している。したがって、中止(ちゅうし)とする。
- Polite variant used in announcements and customer-facing text:
  したがいまして、ご利用(りよう)いただけません。

Both spellings appear in print - したがって and 従(したが)って. Official style
guidance prefers kana for the conjunction, keeping the kanji for the quite
different <code>～に従(したが)って</code> ("in accordance with", "as X
progresses"). Following that split is a good habit: the conjunction never takes
に and never attaches to a noun.

What follows `したがって` must be presentable as objective. Personal wishes, sudden
decisions and requests do not belong there.

## Examples

<pre>
台風(たいふう)が接近(せっきん)している。<b>したがって</b>、明日(あした)の試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)とする。
A typhoon is approaching. Tomorrow's match is therefore cancelled.
</pre>

<pre>
部品(ぶひん)の供給(きょうきゅう)が遅(おく)れている。<b>したがって</b>、発売(はつばい)は来月(らいげつ)に延期(えんき)される。
Parts are arriving late; the launch is accordingly being put back to next month.
</pre>

<pre>
この調査(ちょうさ)は回答(かいとう)数(すう)が少(すく)ない。<b>したがって</b>、結果(けっか)をそのまま一般化(いっぱんか)することはできない。
This survey had few respondents, so its results cannot simply be generalised.
</pre>

<pre>
会員(かいいん)資格(しかく)は一年(いちねん)ごとに更新(こうしん)が必要(ひつよう)です。<b>したがいまして</b>、期限(きげん)を過(す)ぎた方(かた)はご利用(りよう)いただけません。
Membership must be renewed annually, and customers whose membership has lapsed are therefore unable to use the service.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is written Japanese. It lives in reports, academic papers, legal and
official notices, and in prepared speech - a presentation, an announcement, a
formal apology. Read aloud in a conversation it sounds as though you are quoting
a document, and among friends it is the kind of thing people say as a joke:
「眠(ねむ)い。したがって、寝(ね)る。」

The everyday equivalents are だから in casual speech and ですから in polite speech.
For written Japanese that is formal but less austere, そのため is the usual
choice, and it is safer whenever the connection is a plain cause and effect
rather than a piece of reasoning.

`したがいまして` is not more formal in the sense of humble; it is simply the
です・ます-register version, and it is standard in service announcements and
business correspondence.

## Contrast

### したがって vs だから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical logic, different speaker. `だから` is a person
telling you why. `したがって` is a document stating what follows.

</div>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。<b>したがって</b>、明日(あした)の準備(じゅんび)は不要(ふよう)である。
The meeting has been cancelled; no preparation is therefore required for tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)、中止(ちゅうし)になったって。<b>だから</b>、明日(あした)の準備(じゅんび)はしなくていいよ。
Apparently the meeting's off - so you don't need to get anything ready for tomorrow.
</pre>

The content is the same; everything else differs. `だから` can carry feeling, can
be followed by an order or a request（だから早(はや)くしろ）, and can be used to
repeat yourself with a sigh（だから、言(い)ったでしょう）. `したがって` can do none
of those things: it has no emotions and it gives no instructions to a person,
only announcements about a situation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 規則(きそく)にしたがって、遅刻(ちこく)は認(みと)められません。（meaning "therefore"） → ✅ 規則(きそく)があります。<b>したがって</b>、遅刻(ちこく)は認(みと)められません。
  With に in front of it the word means "in accordance with the rules", which is
  a different construction entirely. The conjunction stands alone at the head of
  a sentence.

- ❌ 疲(つか)れた。したがって、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)るね。 → ✅ 疲(つか)れた。<b>だから</b>、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)るね。
  A casual sentence about your own tiredness cannot carry a document's
  conjunction. The clash of registers is the whole problem.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がありません。したがって、手伝(てつだ)ってください。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がありませんので、手伝(てつだ)ってください。
  Requests need ので or から. `したがって` states what follows from the facts, and a
  favour you want is not one of those things.

## Practice

**1.** 本製品(ほんせいひん)の生産(せいさん)は終了(しゅうりょう)いたしました。____、修理(しゅうり)のご依頼(いらい)はお受(う)けできません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>したがいまして</b>、修理(しゅうり)のご依頼(いらい)はお受(う)けできません。

The surrounding sentences are polite customer-facing announcements, so the
です・ます form of the conjunction matches them. Plain したがって would also be
grammatical, but it would sit oddly among いたしました and お受(う)けできません.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
明日(あした)は朝(あさ)が早(はや)いよ。（したがって・だから）、もう寝(ね)なさい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>だから</b>、もう寝(ね)なさい。

The second half is an order given to a person, which `したがって` cannot
introduce - and the plain-form, spoken first half rules it out anyway.

</details>

**3.** Find and fix the error: 予算(よさん)が足(た)りない。従(したが)ってに、計画(けいかく)を見直(みなお)す必要(ひつよう)がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 予算(よさん)が足(た)りない。<b>したがって</b>、計画(けいかく)を見直(みなお)す必要(ひつよう)がある。

Delete the に. The learner has crossed the conjunction with
<code>～に従(したが)って</code>, but the conjunction takes no particle at all;
writing it in kana keeps the two apart.

</details>

# そうすると

**Meaning** · if you do that, then…; in that case it follows that… - takes what has just been described as given and works out what results from it

`そうすると` is そう + する + と: "if things go that way, then". It does arithmetic
on the situation. Somebody supplies a fact or an action, and `そうすると`
calculates the consequence out loud.

## Usage

`そうすると` opens a sentence and is followed by a comma. It has two everyday
jobs:

- **Instructions.** You describe an action, then say what it produces:
  ここを押(お)してください。そうすると、電源(でんげん)が入(はい)ります。
- **Inference in conversation.** You take the other person's information as a
  premise and state what must therefore be true:
  そうすると、間(ま)に合(あ)わないことになりますね。

What follows is a **consequence**, not a decision. This is the constraint that
catches learners out: そうすると、私(わたし)が行(い)きます is odd, because going
is something you have chosen, not something that results. Announcing what you
will do about the news needs それなら or じゃあ.

Three relatives are worth keeping straight. `そうすれば` frames the result as
desirable and is used for advice; `そうしたら` (casual そしたら) is the everyday
spoken form and is also used for what actually happened; `そうすると` itself is
neutral about whether the outcome is welcome.

## Examples

<pre>
ここを押(お)してください。<b>そうすると</b>、電源(でんげん)が入(はい)ります。
Press here, and the power comes on.
</pre>

<pre>
三時(さんじ)の電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)るんですね。<b>そうすると</b>、着(つ)くのは六時(ろくじ)ごろですね。
You're taking the three o'clock train? That puts you in at around six, then.
</pre>

<pre>
来年(らいねん)から税金(ぜいきん)が上(あ)がるらしい。<b>そうすると</b>、家計(かけい)はもっと苦(くる)しくなる。
Tax is apparently going up next year, which will squeeze the household budget even harder.
</pre>

<pre>
申込書(もうしこみしょ)を出(だ)してから二週間(にしゅうかん)かかります。<b>そうすると</b>、受(う)け取(と)れるのは月末(げつまつ)になりますね。
It takes two weeks from submitting the form - so you'd have it by the end of the month, then.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It turns up in manuals and instructions, in negotiation, and in
any conversation where two people are working something out together. Because it
does no more than calculate, it is a polite thing to say: you are checking a
consequence rather than pushing a view.

In casual speech そうしたら and そしたら are far more frequent, and そしたら in
particular is the form you will hear from friends. Neither is wrong in the
consequence sense; they simply sit lower on the formality scale, and そしたら
would look out of place in a printed manual.

`そうすると` is also possible for a past sequence -
ボタンを押(お)した。そうすると、音(おと)が鳴(な)った - but for something you
actually watched happen, すると is the idiomatic choice and is what a native
writer reaches for.

## Contrast

### そうすると vs すると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すると` reports what **did** happen next. `そうすると` works
out what **would** happen, or what must be true.

</div>

<pre>
スイッチを入(い)れた。<b>すると</b>、画面(がめん)が真(ま)っ白(しろ)になった。
I flipped the switch, and the screen went completely white.
</pre>

<pre>
スイッチを入(い)れてください。<b>そうすると</b>、画面(がめん)に地図(ちず)が出(で)ます。
Turn the switch on, and a map will appear on the screen.
</pre>

The tense gives it away. `すると` narrates, so its second half is past and its
first half is a completed action. `そうすると` predicts or deduces, so its second
half looks to the future or states a conclusion. Note that both refuse a second
half in which the speaker decides something.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 「会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)です。」「そうすると、帰(かえ)ります。」 → ✅ 「会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)です。」「<b>それなら</b>、帰(かえ)ります。」
  Going home is a decision, and `そうすると` introduces results, not decisions.
  Replying to news with a plan of action calls for それなら.

- ❌ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。そうすると、猫(ねこ)が入(はい)ってきた。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。<b>すると</b>、猫(ねこ)が入(はい)ってきた。
  For something the speaker watched happen, the narrative connector is すると.
  `そうすると` makes it sound like a general rule about windows and cats.

- ❌ ボタンを押(お)すそうすると、音(おと)が鳴(な)ります。 → ✅ ボタンを押(お)すと、音(おと)が鳴(な)ります。
  Inside one sentence the connector is plain と. `そうすると` needs its own
  sentence, with the previous one already finished.

## Practice

**1.** この書類(しょるい)は部長(ぶちょう)の印(いん)が必要(ひつよう)です。____、今日(きょう)中(じゅう)には出(だ)せませんね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>そうすると</b>、今日(きょう)中(じゅう)には出(だ)せませんね。

The speaker takes the requirement as a premise and works out what it means for
the deadline. Deducing a consequence from information just received is exactly
this word's job, and the ね invites the other person to confirm the sum.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
明日(あした)は休(やす)みだそうですよ。（そうすると・それなら）、映画(えいが)でも見(み)に行(い)きませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それなら</b>、映画(えいが)でも見(み)に行(い)きませんか。

The second half is an invitation, and invitations are decisions about what to do
rather than consequences that follow by themselves. `そうすると` would need
something like 「道(みち)が混(こ)みますね」 after it.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) レバーを下(さ)げてください。<b>そうすると</b>、水(みず)が出(で)ます。
(b) レバーを下(さ)げてください。<b>そうすれば</b>、水(みず)が出(で)ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are natural instructions and a manual could print either. Sentence (a) is
purely mechanical: pull the lever and this is what the machine does, with no
comment on whether that is what you wanted. Sentence (b) presents the water as
the goal you were after, so it reads as helpful advice - "do that and you'll get
your water". `そうすれば` carries the sense of a desirable result; `そうすると` is
neutral.

</details>

# それなら

**Meaning** · in that case; then - takes what the other person has just said as given and answers with a suggestion, an offer or a decision

`それなら` is a reply. It picks up the condition somebody has just handed you -
"I've no time", "the shop's shut", "he isn't coming" - and says what should
happen given that. Where そうすると calculates a consequence, `それなら` proposes a
move.

## Usage

`それなら` opens a sentence, after somebody else's remark or after a piece of
news, and is followed by a comma or a pause. Unlike most of the connectors in
this group, it takes the whole range of action forms after it:

- Suggestion: それなら、タクシーで行(い)きましょう。
- Offer: それなら、私(わたし)が持(も)ちますよ。
- Decision: それなら、今日(きょう)はやめておこう。
- Request: それなら、明日(あした)までに送(おく)ってください。

The family of variants is worth having whole. Casual: それだったら, だったら, and
bare なら - 「なら、早(はや)く言(い)ってよ」. Polite: それでしたら, でしたら,
which is what you use with a customer. Neutral spoken shorthand: じゃあ, which is
by far the most frequent of all but is too light for a formal setting.

## Examples

<pre>
時間(じかん)がないんですか。<b>それなら</b>、タクシーで行(い)きましょう。
You're short of time? Let's take a taxi, then.
</pre>

<pre>
かさを忘(わす)れたの?<b>それなら</b>、これを使(つか)って。
You forgot your umbrella? Here, use this one.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は休(やす)みだそうだ。<b>それなら</b>、今夜(こんや)のうちに買(か)い物(もの)をすませておこう。
Apparently tomorrow's a holiday - in that case I'll get the shopping done tonight.
</pre>

<pre>
ご予算(よさん)がまだお決(き)まりでないのですね。<b>それなら</b>、まず見積(みつ)もりをお出(だ)ししましょうか。
So the budget isn't fixed yet. In that case, shall we start by drawing up a quotation for you?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Conversational and cooperative. `それなら` is what you say when you are adjusting
your plan to fit somebody else's situation, so it usually sounds obliging. Its
politeness is set entirely by the ending: それなら、そうしよう among friends,
それなら、そういたしましょう to a client.

Two limits are worth noticing. First, it responds to something **newly** on the
table - a remark, a discovery, a change of circumstances. Used about a fact
everyone has known for weeks it sounds as though you have only just been told.
Second, it presupposes a condition rather than a cause, so it is not a "therefore"
for building an argument; a written argument wants したがって or そのため.

Said sharply, それなら can also turn into a challenge - 「それなら、自分(じぶん)でやれば?」 - the same way "fine, then" does in English. Tone carries all of that;
the word itself is neutral.

## Contrast

### それなら vs そうすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `それなら` says what **we should do** about it. `そうすると`
says what **will follow** from it.

</div>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は明日(あした)お休(やす)みですか。<b>そうすると</b>、決裁(けっさい)は来週(らいしゅう)になりますね。
The manager's off tomorrow? Then approval won't come through until next week.
</pre>

<pre>
部長(ぶちょう)は明日(あした)お休(やす)みですか。<b>それなら</b>、報告(ほうこく)は来週(らいしゅう)にします。
The manager's off tomorrow? In that case I'll report next week instead.
</pre>

Same premise, same week, different second half. The first sentence works out a
fact about the world and nobody in it has decided anything. The second announces
what the speaker is going to do. If you can put "I'll" or "let's" in the English,
`それなら` is the word.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 雲(くも)が厚(あつ)いね。それなら、夕方(ゆうがた)には雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るだろう。 → ✅ 雲(くも)が厚(あつ)いね。<b>そうすると</b>、夕方(ゆうがた)には雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るだろう。
  Predicting the weather is drawing a consequence, not proposing a course of
  action. `それなら` needs somebody to do something about it.

- ❌ 安(やす)いそれなら、買(か)います。 → ✅ 安(やす)い<b>なら</b>、買(か)います。
  Inside a single sentence the conditional is plain なら. The それ version only
  stands at the head of a sentence, standing in for what was said before.

- ❌ 調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)、需要(じゅよう)は少(すく)ないことがわかった。それなら、開発(かいはつ)は中止(ちゅうし)すべきである。 → ✅ …。<b>したがって</b>、開発(かいはつ)は中止(ちゅうし)すべきである。
  In a written report the conclusion is drawn from evidence, not offered in
  reply to a conversational partner. `それなら` makes a formal document sound like
  a chat.

## Practice

**1.** 「この漢字(かんじ)、読(よ)めないんです。」「____、一緒(いっしょ)に調(しら)べてみましょうか。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それなら</b>、一緒(いっしょ)に調(しら)べてみましょうか。

The reply takes the other person's problem as its condition and offers to do
something about it. An offer or invitation in the second half is the clearest
signal that this is the word wanted.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: 会場(かいじょう)が変(か)わったんですか。それなら、遅(おく)れる人(ひと)が多(おお)くなりますね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 会場(かいじょう)が変(か)わったんですか。<b>そうすると</b>、遅(おく)れる人(ひと)が多(おお)くなりますね。

Nobody is deciding anything here; the speaker is predicting what the change will
lead to. Swapping in そうすると - or だとすると - fixes it. Keep それなら for
「地図(ちず)を送(おく)っておきます」 and the like.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You can't eat spicy food? Then let's order something else."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

辛(から)いものが苦手(にがて)なんですか。<b>それなら</b>、別(べつ)のものを頼(たの)みましょう。

The speaker takes the other person's constraint as given and proposes a way
round it, so it is それなら rather than そうすると. Casual speech would do the same
job with だったら or じゃあ.

</details>

# そこで

**Meaning** · and so; that is why - the situation was such-and-such, and this is the step that was taken about it

`そこで` looks back at a problem and reports the move made in response to it. The
weight falls on the **action**: something needed doing, and this is what was done
about it. That is why it belongs to reports, explanations and stories, and why it
sits so awkwardly in front of anything that merely happened by itself.

## Formation

`そこで` attaches to nothing - it opens a sentence. What is worth tabulating is
what may stand on either side of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing; it opens a sentence | ～。そこで、～ | 席(せき)が足(た)りなかった。そこで、椅子(いす)を運(はこ)んだ。 |
| Sentence before | a situation, problem or discovery | 辞書(じしょ)が見(み)つからなかった。 |
| Sentence after | an action deliberately taken, usually past | そこで、新(あたら)しいものを買(か)った。 |
| Sentence after | ❌ an order, request or invitation | ❌そこで、手伝(てつだ)ってください |
| Sentence after | ❌ something that simply happened | ❌そこで、風邪(かぜ)をひいた |
| Spoken lead-in to a favour | そこで、～なんですが | そこで、ご相談(そうだん)があるんですが |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the same three syllables are also plain そこ + で, "there, in that
place": 駅(えき)で会(あ)って、そこで一時間(いちじかん)話(はな)した。
Position tells you which is which. The conjunction stands at the head of a
sentence and is followed by a comma; the locative sits inside the clause and can
be swapped for あそこで.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
パソコンが動(うご)かなくなった。<b>そこで</b>、係(かかり)の人(ひと)を呼(よ)んだ。
The computer stopped working, so I called someone from support.
</pre>

<pre>
道(みち)が混(こ)んでいた。<b>そこで</b>、電車(でんしゃ)に変(か)えることにした。
The roads were jammed, so we switched to the train.
</pre>

<pre>
アンケートでは値段(ねだん)が高(たか)いという声(こえ)が多(おお)かった。<b>そこで</b>、小(ちい)さいサイズも作(つく)ることにした。
The survey was full of complaints about the price, and so we decided to make a smaller size as well.
</pre>

<pre>
同(おな)じ説明(せつめい)を何度(なんど)も求(もと)められた。<b>そこで</b>、よくある質問(しつもん)をまとめて配(くば)ることにした。
We were asked for the same explanation over and over, so we put together a list of common questions and handed it round.
</pre>

<pre>
一人(ひとり)では決(き)められなかった。<b>そこで</b>、部長(ぶちょう)に相談(そうだん)してみたわけです。
I couldn't decide on my own, and that is why I went and asked the manager.
</pre>

<pre>
近所(きんじょ)の店(みせ)が次々(つぎつぎ)に閉(し)まってしまった。<b>そこで</b>、町内会(ちょうないかい)が月(つき)に一度(いちど)市(いち)を開(ひら)くことにしたそうだ。
The local shops closed one after another, so the neighbourhood association apparently started holding a market once a month.
</pre>

<pre>
実(じつ)は来月(らいげつ)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)すことになりまして。<b>そこで</b>、ちょっとお願(ねが)いがあるんですが。
The thing is, I am moving next month - and that is what I wanted to ask you about.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`そこで` leans written and deliberate. It is at home in reports, minutes,
newspaper features and any narrative that explains how a decision came about. In
ordinary conversation people reach for それで far more often, and a spoken
`そこで` carries a faint storytelling formality: the speaker is presenting the
sequence, not just chatting through it.

The one conversational use that is completely ordinary is the pivot to a request.
You lay out your circumstances, then say そこで、ご相談(そうだん)なんですが or
そこで、お願(ねが)いがあるんですが, and the listener knows the real point of the
conversation has arrived. It is a standard business move and worth learning as a
set phrase.

`そこで` has no emotional colour of its own. It does not complain, insist or
apologise; it reports the step. If you want the sentence to push the listener,
you need だから or ですから.

## Contrast

### そこで vs それで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `それで` covers **any** result, including things that merely
happened to you. `そこで` needs the second half to be a deliberate action.

</div>

<pre>
寝坊(ねぼう)した。<b>それで</b>、遅刻(ちこく)した。
I overslept, and so I was late.
</pre>

<pre>
資料(しりょう)が足(た)りなかった。<b>そこで</b>、急(きゅう)きょ印刷(いんさつ)し直(なお)した。
We were short of handouts, so we hurriedly reprinted them.
</pre>

Being late is not something you chose to do about oversleeping, so `そこで` is
impossible in the first sentence. The reverse swap is usually fine - それで works
in the second one too - which makes `それで` the safer word and `そこで` the more
precise one. `それで` is also the everyday spoken form and doubles as a prompt in
conversation, 「それで?」 meaning "and then?". `そこで` never does that.

### そこで vs だから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だから` gives the **reason** for whatever follows, and what
follows may be a judgement, an order or a plan. `そこで` reports **what was done**,
and nothing else.

</div>

<pre>
明日(あした)は早(はや)い。<b>だから</b>、もう寝(ね)なさい。
We have an early start tomorrow, so go to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)は早(はや)い。<b>そこで</b>、今夜(こんや)は早(はや)めに休(やす)むことにした。
We have an early start tomorrow, so I decided to turn in early tonight.
</pre>

The imperative test settles it: anything you can follow with ～なさい、～てください
or ～ましょう belongs to だから. `そこで` only ever introduces a completed or
settled action, and it makes no demands on the listener.

### そこで vs すると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そこで` introduces **what the person did**. `すると`
introduces **what then happened**, outside anyone's control.

</div>

<pre>
返事(へんじ)が来(こ)なかった。<b>そこで</b>、もう一度(いちど)メールを送(おく)った。
There was no reply, so I sent the email again.
</pre>

<pre>
もう一度(いちど)メールを送(おく)った。<b>すると</b>、すぐに返事(へんじ)が来(き)た。
I sent the email again, and a reply came straight back.
</pre>

The two run in sequence, and a piece of narration often uses both in turn: the
problem, the step taken, the result observed. Notice that the second sentence
could not take `そこで` - a reply arriving is not a decision anyone made - and the
first could not take `すると`, because sending the email again is precisely a
decision.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 昨日(きのう)は寒(さむ)かった。そこで、風邪(かぜ)をひいた。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は寒(さむ)かった。<b>それで</b>、風邪(かぜ)をひいた。
  Catching a cold is not a step you took. `そこで` needs a deliberate action in
  the second half.

- ❌ 時間(じかん)がありません。そこで、静(しず)かにしてください。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がありません。<b>ですから</b>、静(しず)かにしてください。
  Requests and orders cannot follow `そこで`. It reports; it does not instruct.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきたそこで、中(なか)に入(はい)った。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた。<b>そこで</b>、中(なか)に入(はい)った。（or 降(ふ)ってきた<b>ので</b>）
  It opens a new sentence and never joins two clauses. Inside one sentence, use
  ので or から.

- ❌ 駅(えき)に着(つ)いた。そこで、友(とも)だちを待(ま)った。（meaning "I waited there"） → ✅ 駅(えき)に着(つ)いて、そこで友(とも)だちを待(ま)った。
  Standing at the head of a sentence with a comma after it, そこで reads as the
  conjunction, and the reader waits for a decision. If you mean "in that place",
  keep it inside the clause.

## Practice

**1.** 会場(かいじょう)の予約(よやく)が取(と)れなかった。____、日程(にってい)を一週間(いっしゅうかん)遅(おく)らせた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>そこで</b>、日程(にってい)を一週間(いっしゅうかん)遅(おく)らせた。

A problem in the first sentence, a deliberate step in the second: that is the
shape this word exists for. Note the past tense - the action has already been
taken.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まってしまった。（そこで・それで）、会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それで</b>、会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れてしまった。

Being late is a consequence that befell the speaker, not a course of action they
chose. `そこで` would only work with something like
「タクシーに乗(の)り換(か)えた」 in the second half.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The old system kept crashing. So we decided to replace it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

古(ふる)いシステムが止(と)まってばかりいた。<b>そこで</b>、入(い)れ替(か)えることにした。

The second sentence is a decision taken in response to the trouble described in
the first, which is `そこで` exactly. <code>～ことにした</code> spells the
deliberateness out and is a very common partner for this connector.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 説明書(せつめいしょ)がわかりにくかった。そこで、作(つく)り直(なお)してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 説明書(せつめいしょ)がわかりにくかった。<b>ですから</b>、作(つく)り直(なお)してください。

The second half is a request, and `そこで` cannot introduce one. To keep そこで,
the sentence has to report what the speaker did instead:
そこで、自分(じぶん)で作(つく)り直(なお)した。

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 問題(もんだい)が見(み)つかった。<b>そこで</b>、調査(ちょうさ)を始(はじ)めた。
(b) 問題(もんだい)が見(み)つかった。<b>すると</b>、次々(つぎつぎ)に同(おな)じ例(れい)が出(で)てきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In (a) the speaker is the agent: a problem appeared and they responded by
launching an investigation. The sentence is about a choice somebody made.

In (b) nobody chose anything. Once the problem came to light, more cases surfaced
by themselves, and the speaker is reporting what was observed. Putting `そこで`
into (b) would suggest the extra cases appeared because someone decided they
should.

</details>

# ただし

**Meaning** · provided that; note however that - attaches a condition or an exception to a statement that has already been made

`ただし` does not take back what was said before it. The main statement stands;
what follows fences it. Admission is free - provided you book. Returns are
accepted - within a week. This is the language of notices, rules, timetables and
contracts, and it is written in a voice that has authority over the reader.

## Formation

`ただし` attaches to nothing: it opens the sentence that carries the proviso.
What is worth tabulating is the shape of what follows it, because the endings are
formulaic and recognising them is most of the work.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing; it opens the proviso sentence | ～。ただし、～ | 入場(にゅうじょう)無料(むりょう)。ただし、予約(よやく)が必要(ひつよう)です。 |
| Written rules | ただし、～こと | ただし、中学生(ちゅうがくせい)以下(いか)は保護者(ほごしゃ)同伴(どうはん)のこと |
| Exceptions to a stated range | ただし、～を除(のぞ)く | 年中(ねんじゅう)無休(むきゅう)。ただし、年末年始(ねんまつねんし)を除(のぞ)く |
| Limits | ただし、～に限(かぎ)る | ただし、お一人(ひとり)様(さま)一点(いってん)に限(かぎ)ります |
| Polite announcements | ただし、～ます・です | ただし、当日(とうじつ)の変更(へんこう)はできません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a proviso clause in a contract or a regulation is itself called a
ただし書(が)き. If a Japanese colleague says
「ただし書(が)きを読(よ)んでください」, they mean the small print that limits
what the main clause promises.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
会場(かいじょう)は自由(じゆう)に使(つか)えます。<b>ただし</b>、飲食(いんしょく)は禁止(きんし)です。
You're free to use the hall - no food or drink, though.
</pre>

<pre>
返品(へんぴん)はお受(う)けします。<b>ただし</b>、一週間(いっしゅうかん)以内(いない)にお願(ねが)いします。
We do accept returns, provided you bring the item back within a week.
</pre>

<pre>
この割引(わりびき)はどなたでもご利用(りよう)になれます。<b>ただし</b>、他(ほか)のクーポンとの併用(へいよう)はできません。
Anyone can use this discount; it cannot be combined with other coupons.
</pre>

<pre>
試験(しけん)中(ちゅう)は辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)ってもよい。<b>ただし</b>、電子(でんし)辞書(じしょ)は不可(ふか)とする。
Dictionaries may be used during the examination. Electronic dictionaries are not permitted.
</pre>

<pre>
駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)は無料(むりょう)でご利用(りよう)いただけます。<b>ただし</b>、二時間(にじかん)を超(こ)える場合(ばあい)は料金(りょうきん)がかかります。
The car park is free of charge, except that a fee applies beyond two hours.
</pre>

<pre>
明日(あした)の集合(しゅうごう)は九時(くじ)です。<b>ただし</b>、雨(あめ)の場合(ばあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりますので、朝(あさ)の連絡(れんらく)を確認(かくにん)してください。
We meet at nine tomorrow - but it's off if it rains, so check the morning message.
</pre>

<pre>
在宅(ざいたく)勤務(きんむ)は週(しゅう)三日(みっか)まで認(みと)められる。<b>ただし</b>、事前(じぜん)に上司(じょうし)の承認(しょうにん)を得(え)ること。
Working from home is permitted up to three days a week, subject to prior approval from your line manager.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and written-leaning. `ただし` belongs to anything that sets terms: signs,
application forms, staff handbooks, product pages, examination instructions. In
speech it appears where somebody is explaining terms out loud -
「ただし、こちらは予約(よやく)が必要(ひつよう)になります」 - and there it sounds
businesslike rather than stiff.

Ordinary conversation uses ただ, the same word minus the し:
「行(い)ってもいいよ。ただ、遅(おそ)くならないでね。」 That pair is worth
memorising as a register ladder - ただし for the notice on the wall, ただ for the
person telling you about it.

The word carries no feeling. It does not object, apologise or regret; it states
the limits of what was just granted. That neutrality is exactly why officials
like it, and why a `ただし` sentence in a personal message can read as chilly.

## Contrast

### ただし vs しかし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しかし` **contradicts** the previous sentence. `ただし`
leaves it standing and **limits its scope**.

</div>

<pre>
このプランは安(やす)い。<b>しかし</b>、人気(にんき)がない。
This plan is cheap. It isn't popular, though.
</pre>

<pre>
このプランは安(やす)い。<b>ただし</b>、三年(さんねん)契約(けいやく)が条件(じょうけん)だ。
This plan is cheap - on a three-year contract.
</pre>

After しかし the reader revises their impression; after ただし the plan is still
cheap, and they now know on what terms. A quick test: if the second sentence
could be printed under an asterisk on an advertisement, it is `ただし`.

### ただし vs なお

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ただし` **restricts** what came before. `なお` **adds**
something further that restricts nothing.

</div>

<pre>
申込(もうしこみ)は三月(さんがつ)末(まつ)までです。<b>ただし</b>、定員(ていいん)になり次第(しだい)締(し)め切(き)ります。
Applications close at the end of March - or earlier if we reach capacity.
</pre>

<pre>
申込(もうしこみ)は三月(さんがつ)末(まつ)までです。<b>なお</b>、詳(くわ)しくはホームページをご覧(らん)ください。
Applications close at the end of March. Full details are on our website.
</pre>

Both open the last paragraph of an announcement, which is why they get mixed up.
Ask whether the new sentence changes what the reader may do. If it does, it is
`ただし`; if it merely tells them something extra, it is `なお`.

### ただし vs もっとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ただし` states a condition that **binds the reader**.
`もっとも` adds the **speaker's own afterthought**, and binds nobody.

</div>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)は貸(か)し出(だ)しできます。<b>ただし</b>、館内(かんない)のみです。
You may borrow this dictionary, but only within the library.
</pre>

<pre>
この辞書(じしょ)はよく使(つか)っています。<b>もっとも</b>、最近(さいきん)はアプリのほうが多(おお)いですが。
I use this dictionary a lot - though these days it's mostly the app, admittedly.
</pre>

`もっとも` softens the speaker's own claim and typically trails off in が or
けれど. `ただし` never trails off: it delivers a rule and stops. Putting もっとも
on a public notice would make the sign sound as though it were musing to itself.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 安(やす)いただし、味(あじ)はよくない。 → ✅ 安(やす)い。<b>ただし</b>、味(あじ)はよくない。
  It opens a new sentence. It cannot be pinned onto the end of a clause the way
  が or けど can.

- ❌ この案(あん)はよくできている。ただし、私(わたし)は反対(はんたい)だ。 → ✅ この案(あん)はよくできている。<b>しかし</b>、私(わたし)は反対(はんたい)だ。
  A personal objection contradicts the first sentence rather than limiting it.
  `ただし` cannot carry disagreement.

- ❌ 今度(こんど)飲(の)みに行(い)こう。ただし、金曜日(きんようび)は無理(むり)だけど。 → ✅ 今度(こんど)飲(の)みに行(い)こう。<b>ただ</b>、金曜日(きんようび)は無理(むり)だけど。
  Between friends, ただ is the natural form. `ただし` turns a drink with a mate
  into a clause in a contract.

- ❌ 入場(にゅうじょう)無料(むりょう)。ただしでも、予約(よやく)が必要(ひつよう)。 → ✅ 入場(にゅうじょう)無料(むりょう)。<b>ただし</b>、予約(よやく)が必要(ひつよう)。
  It takes no particle after it - no でも, no が, no けど. Just a comma.

## Practice

**1.** 当店(とうてん)は年中(ねんじゅう)無休(むきゅう)です。____、一月(いちがつ)一日(ついたち)のみお休(やす)みをいただきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ただし</b>、一月(いちがつ)一日(ついたち)のみお休(やす)みをいただきます。

"Open every day" is granted and then narrowed by a single exception, which is
precisely the job of this word. Note that the shop is still open all year; only
the scope of the claim has been trimmed.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
受付(うけつけ)は五時(ごじ)までです。（ただし・なお）、駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)は建物(たてもの)の裏(うら)にございます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>なお</b>、駐車場(ちゅうしゃじょう)は建物(たてもの)の裏(うら)にございます。

Where the car park is has no bearing on the five o'clock deadline, so nothing is
being restricted. This is supplementary information, and supplementary
information takes なお.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Photography is permitted, provided you do not use a flash."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

写真(しゃしん)撮影(さつえい)は可能(かのう)です。<b>ただし</b>、フラッシュのご使用(しよう)はご遠慮(えんりょ)ください。

"Provided that" attaching a condition to a permission already granted is
textbook `ただし`, and a museum sign would word it much like this.
ご遠慮(えんりょ)ください is the standard polite way notices forbid something.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 参加(さんか)は自由(じゆう)です。参加(さんか)ただし、事前(じぜん)の登録(とうろく)が必要(ひつよう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 参加(さんか)は自由(じゆう)です。<b>ただし</b>、事前(じぜん)の登録(とうろく)が必要(ひつよう)です。

Delete the stray 参加(さんか). The word takes nothing in front of it inside
its own sentence - it comes first, then a comma, then the condition.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 会員(かいいん)なら無料(むりょう)です。<b>ただし</b>、身分証(みぶんしょう)が必要(ひつよう)です。
(b) 会員(かいいん)なら無料(むりょう)です。<b>でも</b>、身分証(みぶんしょう)が必要(ひつよう)です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both sentences tell you the same two facts. In (a) the identification is a
condition of the offer, stated in the register of a notice: this is what free
admission requires. In (b) the speaker sounds as though they are warning a
friend about an inconvenience, and でも hints that the requirement is a nuisance.
`ただし` sets terms; でも grumbles about them.

</details>

# さて

**Meaning** · now then; right - the writer or speaker closes what has just been dealt with and turns to the next thing

`さて` has almost no content of its own. What it does is manage attention: that
part is finished, and here is where we go next. In a letter it separates the
greetings from the business; in a talk it separates one section from the next; on
your own it is the small word you say to yourself before standing up.

## Formation

`さて` attaches to nothing. It stands at the head of a sentence, or entirely
alone, and it never conjugates.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing; it opens a sentence | さて、～ | さて、本題(ほんだい)に入(はい)ります。 |
| Letters, after the opening greetings | さて、このたびは～ | さて、このたびはご入学(にゅうがく)おめでとうございます。 |
| Alone, before starting something | さて。/ さて、～か | さて、そろそろ始(はじ)めようか。 |
| With a question word, wondering | さて、どう～か | さて、どうしたものか。 |
| Narration, moving the scene on | さて、～ | さて、その翌日(よくじつ)のことである。 |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the related はさておき ("setting that aside") is a different, heavier
expression built from the same root: 冗談(じょうだん)はさておき, "joking aside".
`さて` on its own never takes a noun in front of it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>さて</b>、そろそろ帰(かえ)ろうか。
Right, shall we be heading home?
</pre>

<pre>
<b>さて</b>、今日(きょう)の授業(じゅぎょう)はここまでです。
Now then, that's as far as we go in today's lesson.
</pre>

<pre>
自己(じこ)紹介(しょうかい)は以上(いじょう)です。<b>さて</b>、本日(ほんじつ)の議題(ぎだい)に移(うつ)ります。
That concludes the introductions. Let us now turn to today's agenda.
</pre>

<pre>
皆様(みなさま)いかがお過(す)ごしでしょうか。<b>さて</b>、来月(らいげつ)の総会(そうかい)についてご案内(あんない)いたします。
I hope this finds you well. I am writing to give you details of next month's general meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>さて</b>、この問題(もんだい)の答(こた)えは何(なん)でしょうか。
So - what do we think the answer to this one is?
</pre>

<pre>
荷物(にもつ)をすべて運(はこ)び終(お)えた。<b>さて</b>、ここからが本当(ほんとう)の仕事(しごと)である。
All the boxes were in at last. And now the real work began.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)も鍵(かぎ)も見(み)つからない。<b>さて</b>、どうしたものかと玄関(げんかん)で立(た)ち尽(つ)くした。
Neither wallet nor keys anywhere. Well then, I thought, standing helplessly in the hall.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral and composed, with a faint air of being in charge of proceedings. It is
everywhere in writing: letters, business email, essays, narration, and the
written-out version of a speech. In a lesson, a presentation or a meeting it is
the ordinary way to mark a section boundary out loud.

In casual conversation it is less common. The self-directed
「さて、帰(かえ)るか」 is completely natural, but it does carry a slightly settled,
grown-up tone - a tendency that fiction leans on when writing older characters.
Younger casual speech tends to use じゃあ or よし for the same beat.

Two things `さて` is not. It is not a filler like ええと: it always marks a real
boundary, so scattering it through a conversation makes you sound as though you
keep starting again. And it is not a connective that carries meaning between two
events - it says nothing about cause, contrast or sequence.

In business email, the sequence greeting → さて → the actual matter is close to a
formula. Readers expect it, and its absence makes an email feel abrupt.

## Contrast

### さて vs ところで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さて` moves on to the **next thing on the list**. `ところで`
swerves to something **unrelated**, usually a question the speaker has been
holding.

</div>

<pre>
報告(ほうこく)は以上(いじょう)です。<b>さて</b>、来期(らいき)の計画(けいかく)についてですが。
That completes the report. Turning now to next term's plan.
</pre>

<pre>
報告(ほうこく)は以上(いじょう)です。<b>ところで</b>、来週(らいしゅう)の飲(の)み会(かい)は参加(さんか)されますか。
That's the end of the report. By the way, are you coming to the drinks next week?
</pre>

`さて` implies an order that both people can see - an agenda, a structure, a plan
for the conversation. `ところで` implies there is no such order, and that the
speaker is changing the subject on their own authority. In a written report,
`ところで` would look like a lapse of concentration.

### さて vs では・それでは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さて` marks the **boundary**. `では` **triggers the next
action**, and often introduces a request, a start or a farewell.

</div>

<pre>
<b>では</b>、始(はじ)めましょう。
Right, let's begin.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>さて</b>、ここで一度(いちど)まとめておきましょう。
Now then, let's take stock at this point.
</pre>

In speech では and じゃあ do most of this work, and they are the natural choice
when something is about to happen - a meeting starting, a guest leaving. `さて` is
more reflective and much more frequent in writing, where では sounds like somebody
addressing a room.

### さて vs そして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そして` chains events **inside** the story. `さて` steps
outside it and speaks to the reader.

</div>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)んだ。<b>そして</b>、静(しず)かに封筒(ふうとう)に戻(もど)した。
He read the letter, and put it quietly back in its envelope.
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)は手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)んだ。<b>さて</b>、ここで時計(とけい)を三年(さんねん)戻(もど)さなければならない。
He read the letter. Now, at this point we must turn the clock back three years.
</pre>

Swap them and the narration breaks. `そして` promises the next event; `さて`
promises a change of gear, and the reader adjusts accordingly.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 話(はなし)が終(お)わったさて、次(つぎ)に進(すす)みます。 → ✅ 話(はなし)が終(お)わりました。<b>さて</b>、次(つぎ)に進(すす)みます。
  It opens its own sentence and cannot be attached to the end of a clause.

- ❌ さて、昨日(きのう)の試合(しあい)見(み)ましたか。（jumping to an unrelated topic） → ✅ <b>ところで</b>、昨日(きのう)の試合(しあい)見(み)ましたか。
  `さて` promises the next item in a shared order. A sudden unrelated question is
  ところで.

- ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた。さて、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた。<b>そこで</b>、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になった。
  `さて` carries no cause and no consequence. Used between two connected events
  it leaves the link unexplained.

- ❌ さてですが、本題(ほんだい)に入(はい)ります。 → ✅ <b>さて</b>、本題(ほんだい)に入(はい)ります。
  Nothing attaches to it - no ですが, no けれども. If you want a politer boundary,
  use それでは or 早速(さっそく)ですが.

## Practice

**1.** 以上(いじょう)で前半(ぜんはん)の説明(せつめい)を終(お)わります。____、後半(こうはん)の資料(しりょう)をご覧(らん)ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>さて</b>、後半(こうはん)の資料(しりょう)をご覧(らん)ください。

One section has been closed and the next one is being opened, in an order the
audience can already see. That is the boundary this word marks. それでは would
also pass here, and would sound a shade more like an instruction.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
会議(かいぎ)の話(はなし)はこれで終(お)わりです。（さて・ところで）、山田(やまだ)さんの引(ひ)っ越(こ)しはどうなりましたか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ところで</b>、山田(やまだ)さんの引(ひ)っ越(こ)しはどうなりましたか。

Yamada's house move was not on anybody's agenda, so the speaker is stepping away
from the shared plan rather than advancing it. `さて` would promise the next item
of business and then fail to deliver one.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Well then - I suppose I should get started on dinner."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>さて</b>、そろそろ夕飯(ゆうはん)の支度(したく)でもしようか。

This is the self-directed use: nobody is being addressed, and the word marks the
moment of getting up and starting. でも and ようか give it the same half-hearted
"I suppose" as the English.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 以上(いじょう)が調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)である。さてですが、今後(こんご)の課題(かだい)を三点(さんてん)挙(あ)げる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 以上(いじょう)が調査(ちょうさ)の結果(けっか)である。<b>さて</b>、今後(こんご)の課題(かだい)を三点(さんてん)挙(あ)げる。

Delete ですが. The word takes no particle and no polite ending; adding one also
clashes with the plain である style of the surrounding report.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 準備(じゅんび)が全部(ぜんぶ)終(お)わった。<b>さて</b>、出(で)かけよう。
(b) 準備(じゅんび)が全部(ぜんぶ)終(お)わった。<b>そして</b>、出(で)かけた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (a) is spoken from inside the moment: the preparations are done, the
speaker draws a line under them and rouses themselves to leave. The word marks a
change of gear, and the volitional ending shows the decision being made right
then.

Sentence (b) is narration after the fact, simply listing what happened next. No
boundary is being marked and nobody is being addressed, so `さて` would be out of
place there.

</details>

# だが

**Meaning** · but; however - the written, hard-edged "but"

`だが` is the copula だ plus the contrastive particle が, and it behaves like both.
At the head of a sentence it turns the argument round; in the middle of one it
joins a noun or a な-adjective to a contrasting clause. What sets it apart from
its rivals is not meaning but voice: `だが` is the "but" of essays, journalism and
narration, and in ordinary conversation it lands with a thump.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Start of a sentence | ～。だが、～ | 努力(どりょく)はした。だが、結果(けっか)は出(で)なかった。 |
| Noun (mid-sentence) | Noun + だが | 失礼(しつれい)だが、お名前(なまえ)は? |
| な-adjective | ～だが | 不便(ふべん)だが、家賃(やちん)は安(やす)い。 |
| Verb / い-adjective | plain form + が（no だ） | 安(やす)いが／行(い)くが |
| Past | ～だったが | 学生(がくせい)だったが、よく働(はたら)いた。 |
| Polite | ですが／ますが | 恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが、明日(あした)は伺(うかが)えません。 |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the sentence-initial だが and the clause-final が are the same particle.
安(やす)いが、質(しつ)は悪(わる)い and 安(やす)い。だが、質(しつ)は悪(わる)い
say the same thing; splitting the sentence in two puts more weight on the
contrast, which is why writers use it at the start of a paragraph.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
値段(ねだん)は安(やす)い。<b>だが</b>、品質(ひんしつ)は悪(わる)くない。
It is cheap. The quality, however, is not bad at all.
</pre>

<pre>
何度(なんど)も挑戦(ちょうせん)した。<b>だが</b>、一度(いちど)も勝(か)てなかった。
He tried again and again, but never once won.
</pre>

<pre>
失礼(しつれい)<b>だが</b>、どちらさまでしょうか。
Forgive me, but who am I speaking to?
</pre>

<pre>
彼(かれ)の言(い)い分(ぶん)にも一理(いちり)ある。<b>だが</b>、それを認(みと)めるわけにはいかない。
There is something in what he says. Even so, we cannot accept it.
</pre>

<pre>
雨(あめ)は上(あ)がった。<b>だが</b>、風(かぜ)はいっそう強(つよ)くなった。
The rain stopped. The wind, though, blew harder than ever.
</pre>

<pre>
技術(ぎじゅつ)は確実(かくじつ)に進(すす)んでいる。<b>だが</b>、使(つか)う側(がわ)の意識(いしき)が追(お)いついていない。
The technology is advancing steadily. What has not kept pace is the attitude of the people using it.
</pre>

<pre>
準備(じゅんび)に三年(さんねん)をかけた計画(けいかく)だった。<b>だが</b>、中止(ちゅうし)の判断(はんだん)はわずか一日(いちにち)で下(くだ)された。
The plan had taken three years to prepare. The decision to scrap it took a single day.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written Japanese. `だが` belongs to essays, columns, reports, academic prose and
above all to narration, where a paragraph beginning だが tells the reader that the
story is about to turn. It also appears in declamatory speech - a debate, a
speech, a character in a film.

In everyday conversation it is rare, and it sounds either literary or blunt. The
spoken equivalents are でも and けど; polite speech uses ですが or でも; しかし sits
between them, formal but usable aloud. Learners tend to pick `だが` up from
subtitles and novels, where male characters use it heavily - that is a tendency
of the fiction, not a rule about who may say it, but it does mean a `だが` dropped
into a friendly chat gets noticed.

Mid-sentence, the polite ですが is a different matter entirely and is extremely
common in speech, especially as a soft opener:
恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが、失礼(しつれい)ですが、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが.
There the が barely means "but" at all; it is a cushion before a request.

You will also meet the emphatic pair だがしかし, which stacks two contrastives for
effect. It is theatrical, common online and in comedy, and out of place in
serious writing.

## Contrast

### だが vs しかし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are written "but". `しかし` is the **neutral standard**
and can be said aloud; `だが` is **harder and more literary**.

</div>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)は順調(じゅんちょう)に進(すす)んだ。<b>しかし</b>、原因(げんいん)は特定(とくてい)できなかった。
The investigation went smoothly. The cause, however, could not be identified.
</pre>

<pre>
調査(ちょうさ)は順調(じゅんちょう)に進(すす)んだ。<b>だが</b>、真実(しんじつ)は闇(やみ)の中(なか)だった。
The investigation went smoothly. But the truth stayed in the dark.
</pre>

In most written sentences the two are interchangeable, and a report will use
しかし because it is the safe default. `だが` is shorter, heavier and more
dramatic, which makes it the storyteller's choice - and which is also why a
technical document full of だが reads as overwrought.

### だが vs でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, opposite ends of the register scale. `でも` is
**spoken**; `だが` is **written**.

</div>

<pre>
高(たか)いなあ。<b>でも</b>、これしかないんだよね。
It's pricey. Still, it's the only one there is.
</pre>

<pre>
価格(かかく)は高(たか)い。<b>だが</b>、代(か)わりとなる製品(せいひん)は存在(そんざい)しない。
The price is high. There is, however, no alternative product.
</pre>

The content is identical and the sentences are not interchangeable at all.
Putting `だが` into the first one makes a friend sound like a newsreader; putting
でも into the second makes a report sound like a chat. Mixing registers is the
single commonest way learners misuse this word.

### だが vs ですが

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ですが` is what you actually **say to people**. `だが` is
what you **write**.

</div>

<pre>
おっしゃることはわかります。<b>ですが</b>、今回(こんかい)は見送(みおく)らせてください。
I understand your point. Even so, please let us pass this time.
</pre>

<pre>
反対(はんたい)の声(こえ)は強(つよ)かった。<b>だが</b>、計画(けいかく)は変更(へんこう)されなかった。
Opposition was strong. The plan was not changed.
</pre>

Both are contrastive and both are formal, but ですが is polite-register speech and
`だが` is plain-register writing. Said to a client, `だが` sounds curt to the point
of rudeness; written in a report, ですが would look like a transcript of somebody
talking.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いだが、狭(せま)い。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は安(やす)い<b>が</b>、狭(せま)い。
  Verbs and い-adjectives already end in a plain form, so they take bare が. The
  だ belongs only after a noun or a な-adjective.

- ❌ 行(い)くだが、遅(おく)れるかもしれない。 → ✅ 行(い)く<b>が</b>、遅(おく)れるかもしれない。
  Same rule with a verb. <code>行(い)くだ</code> is not a Japanese form at all.

- ❌ ご説明(せつめい)はわかりました。だが、納得(なっとく)できません。（to a superior） → ✅ ご説明(せつめい)はわかりました。<b>ですが</b>、納得(なっとく)できません。
  The plain だが in a polite exchange reads as brusque. Politeness is carried by
  ですが, not by the sentences around it.

- ❌ 眠(ねむ)い。だが、まだ寝(ね)られないんだよね。（chatting with a friend） → ✅ 眠(ねむ)い。<b>でも</b>、まだ寝(ね)られないんだよね。
  The casual んだよね ending and the literary だが pull in opposite directions.
  Keep the register consistent across the whole sentence.

## Practice

**1.** 新(あたら)しい制度(せいど)は評判(ひょうばん)がよかった。____、利用(りよう)する人(ひと)は増(ふ)えなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>だが</b>、利用(りよう)する人(ひと)は増(ふ)えなかった。

The plain, impersonal style of both halves marks this as writing, and the second
sentence overturns the expectation set by the first. しかし would be equally
correct and slightly softer.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
「明日(あした)までにお願(ねが)いできますか。」「ありがたいお話(はなし)です。（だが・ですが）、明日(あした)は難(むずか)しいです。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>ですが</b>、明日(あした)は難(むずか)しいです。

This is polite spoken Japanese, and the contrastive that matches です・ます is
ですが. Dropping the plain `だが` into a です sentence sounds abrupt, and to a
client it would come across as curt.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in written style: "The evidence was overwhelming. But the court did not accept it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

証拠(しょうこ)は圧倒的(あっとうてき)だった。<b>だが</b>、裁判所(さいばんしょ)はそれを認(みと)めなかった。

Plain past forms, an impersonal subject and a sharp reversal at the paragraph
break: this is exactly the environment `だが` was made for. しかし would also be
correct and a little flatter.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 給料(きゅうりょう)は悪(わる)くないだが、残業(ざんぎょう)が多(おお)すぎる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 給料(きゅうりょう)は悪(わる)くない<b>が</b>、残業(ざんぎょう)が多(おお)すぎる。

<code>悪(わる)くない</code> is an い-adjective form, so it connects with bare が.
Split into two sentences it becomes
悪(わる)くない。だが、残業(ざんぎょう)が多(おお)すぎる。

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた。<b>でも</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われた。
(b) 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていた。<b>だが</b>、試合(しあい)は行(おこな)われた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The facts are identical; the voice is not. Sentence (a) sounds like somebody
telling you about their weekend - light, spoken, unremarkable. Sentence (b)
sounds like a written account, and the harder connector gives the contrast a
weight that invites the reader to find it significant. Choosing between them is
a choice about who is speaking and to whom, not about meaning.

</details>

# すると

**Meaning** · thereupon; and then - what was observed to happen immediately after

`すると` is the narrator's connector. Somebody does something, and the next
sentence reports what happened as a result - not what they decided to do next,
but what the world did back. There is nearly always a small element of discovery
in it: the speaker did not know this was coming.

It has a second life in conversation, where it introduces an inference drawn from
what the other person has just said.

## Formation

`すると` attaches to nothing; it opens a sentence. What matters is the shape of
the sentences on either side of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing; it opens a sentence | ～た。すると、～た | 名前(なまえ)を呼(よ)んだ。すると、振(ふ)り向(む)いた。 |
| Sentence before | a completed action or event, usually past | ドアを開(あ)けた。 |
| Sentence after | something observed, outside the speaker's control | すると、冷(つめ)たい風(かぜ)が入(はい)ってきた。 |
| Sentence after | ❌ the speaker's own decision, order or request | ❌すると、帰(かえ)ることにした |
| Dialogue, drawing an inference | すると、～ということですか | すると、全員(ぜんいん)反対(はんたい)だったのですね。 |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the word is そう + する + と, "when things are done that way". That
history explains both uses: the narrative one ("when that was done, this
happened") and the inferential one ("if that is so, then this follows").

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。<b>すると</b>、鳥(とり)の声(こえ)が聞(き)こえてきた。
I opened the window, and birdsong came drifting in.
</pre>

<pre>
ベルを押(お)した。<b>すると</b>、中(なか)から小(ちい)さな女(おんな)の子(こ)が出(で)てきた。
I rang the bell, and a small girl came out.
</pre>

<pre>
薬(くすり)を飲(の)んで横(よこ)になった。<b>すると</b>、いつの間(ま)にか眠(ねむ)ってしまっていた。
I took the medicine and lay down, and before I knew it I had fallen asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
会議(かいぎ)は来週(らいしゅう)に延(の)びたんですか。<b>すると</b>、資料(しりょう)は作(つく)り直(なお)さなくてもいいわけですね。
The meeting's been pushed to next week? So we don't need to redo the handouts after all.
</pre>

<pre>
机(つくえ)の引(ひ)き出(だ)しを開(あ)けてみた。<b>すると</b>、十年(じゅうねん)前(まえ)の手紙(てがみ)が出(で)てきた。
I tried opening the desk drawer, and out came a letter from ten years ago.
</pre>

<pre>
山(やま)の上(うえ)で大(おお)きな声(こえ)を出(だ)してみた。<b>すると</b>、向(む)かいの谷(たに)から同(おな)じ声(こえ)が返(かえ)ってきた。
I shouted from the top of the mountain, and the same shout came back from the valley opposite.
</pre>

<pre>
財布(さいふ)を落(お)としたことに気(き)づいて交番(こうばん)へ走(はし)った。<b>すると</b>、すでに届(とど)けてくれた人(ひと)がいた。
I realised I'd dropped my wallet and ran to the police box - where it turned out somebody had already handed it in.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, but strongly narrative. `すると` is the connector of anecdotes,
folk tales, accident reports and any account of "here is what I did, and here is
what happened". Because of that flavour of discovery, it does not suit a result
that anybody could have predicted: if the second event is the obvious outcome of
the first, そして or それで is the honest choice.

In casual speech the same narrative move is usually made with そしたら or
そうしたら. 「電話(でんわ)したの。そしたら、もう出(で)かけたって」 is what a friend
says; `すると` in that sentence would sound slightly written, as though the story
were being read aloud.

The inferential use - 「すると、つまり間(ま)に合(あ)わないということですね」 -
is entirely at home in discussion and meetings. There it is close to
だとすると or ということは, and it signals that you are following the other person's
reasoning rather than adding information of your own.

## Contrast

### すると vs そうすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すると` reports what **did** happen, in the past. `そうすると`
works out what **would** happen, or what must be true.

</div>

<pre>
レバーを引(ひ)いた。<b>すると</b>、機械(きかい)が止(と)まった。
I pulled the lever, and the machine stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
レバーを引(ひ)いてください。<b>そうすると</b>、機械(きかい)が止(と)まります。
Pull the lever and the machine will stop.
</pre>

The tense tells you which you are looking at. `すると` narrates a completed
sequence; `そうすると` predicts, instructs or deduces. In the inferential use the
two overlap, and 「すると、間(ま)に合(あ)いませんね」 and
「そうすると、間(ま)に合(あ)いませんね」 are both natural.

### すると vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical relation, different packaging. `～と` puts both
halves in **one sentence**; `すると` gives the second half **its own sentence**.

</div>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)ける<b>と</b>、遠(とお)くに海(うみ)が見(み)えた。
When I opened the window, the sea was visible in the distance.
</pre>

<pre>
窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。<b>すると</b>、遠(とお)くに海(うみ)が見(み)えた。
I opened the window. And there in the distance was the sea.
</pre>

`すると` is literally that same と with そうする in front of it, which is why the
two behave alike - both refuse a second half in which the speaker decides or
orders something. The difference is pacing. The one-sentence version moves
briskly; the two-sentence version puts a beat of suspense before the discovery,
which is why storytellers reach for it.

### すると vs そして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そして` simply **adds the next event**. `すると` presents the
next event as **what came of** the first, and as news to the speaker.

</div>

<pre>
手紙(てがみ)を書(か)いた。<b>そして</b>、ポストに入(い)れた。
I wrote the letter, and then posted it.
</pre>

<pre>
手紙(てがみ)を出(だ)した。<b>すると</b>、三日(みっか)後(ご)に返事(へんじ)が届(とど)いた。
I sent the letter, and three days later a reply arrived.
</pre>

Posting the letter is the speaker's own next move, so it takes そして. The reply
arriving is not, and it is the kind of thing the speaker could not have known in
advance - `すると` territory. Where both are grammatical, `すると` adds the sense
that the second event followed *from* the first.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 宿題(しゅくだい)が終(お)わった。すると、寝(ね)た。 → ✅ 宿題(しゅくだい)が終(お)わった。<b>そして</b>、寝(ね)た。
  Going to bed is the speaker's own next action, not something they discovered.
  `すると` needs a second half that happened to them.

- ❌ 寒(さむ)いですね。すると、窓(まど)を閉(し)めてください。 → ✅ 寒(さむ)いですね。<b>ですから</b>、窓(まど)を閉(し)めてください。
  Requests and orders cannot follow it, for the same reason: they are decisions,
  not observations.

- ❌ ボタンを押(お)すすると、音(おと)が鳴(な)ります。 → ✅ ボタンを押(お)す<b>と</b>、音(おと)が鳴(な)ります。
  Inside one sentence the connector is bare と. `すると` needs the first sentence
  to be finished before it.

- ❌ このボタンを押(お)してください。すると、電源(でんげん)が入(はい)ります。 → ✅ このボタンを押(お)してください。<b>そうすると</b>、電源(でんげん)が入(はい)ります。
  Instructions about what will happen are not narration. For a manual, the
  forward-looking そうすると or そうすれば is what you want.

## Practice

**1.** 静(しず)かに台所(だいどころ)をのぞいてみた。____、猫(ねこ)が皿(さら)の上(うえ)で寝(ね)ていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>すると</b>、猫(ねこ)が皿(さら)の上(うえ)で寝(ね)ていた。

The speaker looked, and what they found was a surprise to them. A completed
action followed by an unexpected discovery is the classic shape for this word.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
この欄(らん)に住所(じゅうしょ)をご記入(きにゅう)ください。（すると・そうすると）、次(つぎ)の画面(がめん)に進(すす)めます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>そうすると</b>、次(つぎ)の画面(がめん)に進(すす)めます。

These are instructions about what will happen if the reader does something, so
the sentence looks forward rather than back. `すると` would need a past narrative
in front of it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I called out to him. He turned round, and it was someone else entirely."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

声(こえ)をかけてみた。<b>すると</b>、振(ふ)り返(かえ)ったのはまったく別(べつ)の人(ひと)だった。

Calling out is the speaker's action; who turned round was not up to them, and it
was a surprise. That combination is what `すると` marks, and the past tense on
both halves is essential.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: 駅(えき)に着(つ)いた。すると、友(とも)だちに電話(でんわ)しようと思(おも)う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ 駅(えき)に着(つ)いた。<b>そこで</b>、友(とも)だちに電話(でんわ)することにした。

<code>思(おも)う</code> here states the speaker's own intention, and `すると`
cannot introduce one. Reporting a step you decided to take is そこで; if you
wanted a discovery instead, the second half would have to be something like
すると、友(とも)だちが改札(かいさつ)で待(ま)っていた。

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けると、雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もっていた。
(b) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。<b>すると</b>、雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The two describe the same morning and neither is more correct than the other.
Version (a) packs the action and the discovery into one sentence and reads
smoothly, the way a description usually does. Version (b) closes the first
sentence before the discovery arrives, which gives the snow a small moment of
its own - the pacing of a story being told rather than a scene being described.

</details>
