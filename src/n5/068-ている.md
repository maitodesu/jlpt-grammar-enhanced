# ～ている - is doing; is in a state

**Meaning** · the action is going on now - or its result is still with us

`～ている` is not the English present progressive, and treating it as one is the
single most expensive mistake in beginner Japanese. It says that something
connected to the verb is **true right now**. Whether that something is the action
still running, or the state left behind after the action finished, is decided by
the verb itself - not by `ている`.

## Formation

`いる` is a る-verb, so it conjugates normally after the て-form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb (て) | て-form + いる | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}っている |
| う-verb (で) | で-form + いる | {f|遊|あそ}ぶ → {f|遊|あそ}んでいる |
| る-verb | て-form + いる | {f|食|た}べる → {f|食|た}べている |
| する | している | {f|勉強|べんきょう}する → {f|勉強|べんきょう}している |
| {f|来|く}る | きている | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}ている |
| Polite | ています | {f|食|た}べています |
| Negative | ていない／ていません | {f|食|た}べていません |
| Past | ていた／ていました | {f|食|た}べていました |
| Casual contraction | ている → てる | {f|食|た}べてる |
| Honorific / humble | ていらっしゃる／ております | {f|待|ま}っていらっしゃる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - verbs that take time (<code>{f|食|た}べる</code>,
<code>{f|読|よ}む</code>, <code>{f|走|はし}る</code>) give **ongoing action**. Verbs that
happen in an instant and change something (<code>{f|結婚|けっこん}する</code>,
<code>{f|死|し}ぬ</code>, <code>{f|開|あ}く</code>, <code>{f|知|し}る</code>) give **resulting
state**. Same `ている`, two readings, and the verb decides.

</div>

There is a third, quieter use - **habitual action**
(<code>{f|毎朝|まいあさ}{f|走|はし}っています</code>) - and a fourth for accumulated record
(<code>{f|三回|さんかい}{f|優勝|ゆうしょう}している</code>, "has won three times"). Both are
common; the first is essential at N5.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|今|いま}、ごはんを<b>{f|食|た}べています</b>。
I'm eating right now.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもたちが{f|公園|こうえん}で<b>{f|遊|あそ}んでいる</b>。
The children are playing in the park.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|姉|あね}は<b>{f|結婚|けっこん}しています</b>。
My older sister is married.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|人|ひと}の{f|名前|なまえ}を<b>{f|知|し}っています</b>か。
Do you know that person's name?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんはめがねを<b>かけています</b>。

</pre>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}が<b>{f|開|あ}いています</b>よ。{f|閉|し}めましょうか。
The window's open. Shall I shut it?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|毎朝|まいあさ}{f|六時|ろくじ}に{f|起|お}きて、{f|近|ちか}くの{f|川|かわ}まで<b>{f|走|はし}っています</b>。
Every morning I get up at six and run down to the river nearby.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|庭|にわ}で{f|虫|むし}が<b>{f|死|し}んでいた</b>ので、{f|土|つち}にうめてやった。
There was a dead insect in the garden, so I buried it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|道|みち}が<b>{f|混|こ}んでいる</b>から、{f|着|つ}くのが{f|少|すこ}し{f|遅|おそ}くなりそうです。
The roads are busy, so I'll probably arrive a little late.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**`ている` is where Japanese talks about the present.** The dictionary form does
*not* mean "now": <code>{f|食|た}べる</code> is "will eat" or "eats (habitually)", never
"is eating". If something is happening as you speak, `ている` is not optional.

**`てる` is the normal spoken shape.** <code>{f|何|なに}してるの？</code>,
<code>{f|待|ま}ってるよ</code>, <code>{f|知|し}ってた？</code> - the `い` drops in casual speech,
including in `ていた` → `てた`. It is informal but extremely common; keep the full
form in writing, in `ます` speech and in the exam.

**States you would never think of as verbs.** Japanese uses `ている` for a large
class of descriptions that English handles with adjectives or the simple present:
<code>{f|住|す}んでいます</code> (live), <code>{f|持|も}っています</code> (have),
<code>{f|勤|つと}めています</code> (work for), <code>{f|似|に}ている</code> (resemble),
<code>{f|太|ふと}っている</code> (be heavy-set), <code>{f|曲|ま}がっている</code> (be bent).
Several of these are *only* natural in `ている` form - you cannot describe a
present resemblance with ❌<code>{f|似|に}る</code>.

**Politeness.** Honorific speech gives <code>～ていらっしゃる</code> and
<code>～ておられる</code>; humble speech gives <code>～ております</code>
(<code>{f|承知|しょうち}しております</code>). These are N4 and beyond, but you will hear
`ております` on the phone from day one.

**The negative is where the special cases hide.** <code>{f|食|た}べていません</code> means
"hasn't eaten (yet)" - the action has not started or is not underway. But some
state verbs refuse the negative `ている` altogether, and <code>{f|知|し}る</code> is the famous
one: see below.

## Contrast

### Ongoing action vs resulting state

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask how long the verb takes. If the action itself has
duration, `ている` catches it mid-flow. If the action is over in an instant and
leaves a changed world, `ている` describes the change that is still there.

</div>

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}は{f|今|いま}、{f|手紙|てがみ}を<b>{f|書|か}いています</b>。
My little brother is writing a letter right now. (still writing)
</pre>

<pre>
おじいさんは{f|去年|きょねん}<b>{f|亡|な}くなっています</b>。
My grandfather passed away last year. (and is dead now)
</pre>

The second is not "is dying". <code>{f|死|し}ぬ</code> and
<code>{f|亡|な}くなる</code> take no time at all, so there is no flow for `ている` to catch
 - only the state afterwards. The same logic explains
<code>{f|結婚|けっこん}している</code> (is married, not "is getting married"),
<code>{f|来|き}ている</code> (is here, having come), <code>ドアが{f|閉|し}まっている</code> (the
door is shut) and <code>{f|覚|おぼ}えている</code> (remembers).

<pre>
{f|電気|でんき}が<b>ついています</b>。{f|誰|だれ}かいるのかもしれません。
The light is on. Someone might be in.
</pre>

A useful test: if the English translation is an adjective or "is/has been", you are
looking at a resulting state.

### ～ている vs the dictionary form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` = now. Dictionary form = will, or generally does. The
overlap English speakers expect between "I eat" and "I'm eating" does not exist
here.

</div>

<pre>
{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|新聞|しんぶん}を{f|読|よ}みます。
I read the newspaper every day. (habit - plain present is fine)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}、{f|新聞|しんぶん}を<b>{f|読|よ}んでいます</b>。
I'm reading the newspaper right now.
</pre>

Habitual actions can take either form, with a difference of framing: `ています`
presents the habit as a current, ongoing arrangement of your life - 
<code>{f|銀行|ぎんこう}で{f|働|はたら}いています</code> is how you say what you do for a
living, and <code>{f|銀行|ぎんこう}で{f|働|はたら}きます</code> would sound like a plan for
tomorrow.

### {f|知|し}っている vs {f|知|し}らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - "I know" is <code>{f|知|し}っています</code>. "I don't know" is
<code>{f|知|し}りません</code>. There is no ❌<code>{f|知|し}っていません</code> in ordinary use.

</div>

<pre>
その{f|話|はなし}なら<b>{f|知|し}っています</b>。
Yes, I know about that.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、{f|知|し}りません。
Sorry, I don't know.
</pre>

<code>{f|知|し}る</code> means "to come to know", so <code>{f|知|し}っている</code> is the state of having
come to know - and its opposite is simply never having done so, which is
<code>{f|知|し}らない</code>. Answering a question with ❌<code>{f|知|し}っていません</code> marks you
immediately as a learner; answering with a flat <code>{f|知|し}りません</code> can sound curt, so
soften it: <code>ちょっと{f|分|わ}かりません</code>, <code>すみません、{f|知|し}らないんです</code>.

<code>{f|分|わ}かる</code> behaves differently again: it is already a state, so
<code>{f|分|わ}かります</code>／<code>{f|分|わ}かりません</code> need no `ている` at all.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ その{f|人|ひと}は{f|知|し}っていません。 → ✅ その{f|人|ひと}は{f|知|し}りません。
  The negative of <code>{f|知|し}っている</code> is <code>{f|知|し}らない</code>. This one is worth memorising
  as a pair rather than as a rule.

- ❌ {f|今|いま}、{f|昼|ひる}ごはんを{f|食|た}べます。（intending "I'm eating lunch now"）→ ✅ {f|今|いま}、{f|昼|ひる}ごはんを{f|食|た}べています。
  The plain present is future or habitual. It cannot describe what is happening as
  you speak.

- ❌ {f|兄|あに}は{f|結婚|けっこん}します。（intending "my brother is married"）→ ✅ {f|兄|あに}は{f|結婚|けっこん}しています。
  Without `ている` you have announced a wedding that has not happened yet.

- ❌ {f|東京|とうきょう}に{f|住|す}みます。 → ✅ {f|東京|とうきょう}に{f|住|す}んでいます。
  Living somewhere is a state. The dictionary form would mean you are about to
  move there.

- ❌ {f|駅|えき}で{f|待|ま}ちています。 → ✅ {f|駅|えき}で{f|待|ま}っています。
  <code>{f|待|ま}つ</code> is a `つ` verb, so its て-form is <code>{f|待|ま}って</code>. `ている`
  never attaches to the ます-stem.

## Practice

**1.** {f|母|はは}は{f|台所|だいどころ}で{f|料理|りょうり}を____います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|料理|りょうり}を<b>して</b>います。

<code>する</code> becomes <code>して</code>, and cooking takes time, so this is the ongoing
reading: she is in the middle of it right now.

</details>

**2.** {f|山田|やまだ}さんの{f|電話番号|でんわばんごう}を（{f|知|し}っていません・{f|知|し}りません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|山田|やまだ}さんの{f|電話番号|でんわばんごう}を<b>{f|知|し}りません</b>。

<code>{f|知|し}っている</code> has no negative form of its own - not knowing is expressed by
the plain negative <code>{f|知|し}らない</code>／<code>{f|知|し}りません</code>. The affirmative and the
negative simply do not match in shape.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The door is open."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ドアが<b>{f|開|あ}いています</b>。

<code>{f|開|あ}く</code> is an instantaneous change of state, so `ている` gives the
resulting state - the door is *currently open*, not "is opening". English speakers
often reach for an adjective here; Japanese uses the verb.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: {f|今|いま}、{f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|電話|でんわ}を{f|使|つか}います。あとでかけ{f|直|なお}してください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|今|いま}、{f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|電話|でんわ}を{f|使|つか}っています。あとでかけ{f|直|なお}してください。

The second sentence makes it clear the phone is in use *at this moment*, so the
first needs `ています`. As written, it says Tanaka will use the phone - which gives
the listener no reason to call back later.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けています。
(b) {f|窓|まど}が{f|開|あ}いています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the transitive <code>{f|開|あ}ける</code> with `を`: somebody is in the act of
opening the window, and the sentence is about their action.

(b) uses the intransitive <code>{f|開|あ}く</code> with `が`: the window is open. Nobody
is mentioned, nobody is doing anything, and the sentence simply describes the
state of the room. The particle and the verb pair together - change one and you
must change the other.

</details>
