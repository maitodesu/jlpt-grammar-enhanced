# よ

**Meaning** · tells the listener something they don't yet know

`よ` at the end of a sentence marks the content as **news**. It says: *this is
information you didn't have, and I'm giving it to you.* Used well it is helpful and
warm; used on something the listener already knows, it turns into a lecture.

## Formation

`よ` comes after the finished sentence, and before `ね` if both are present.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite sentence | ～です／～ます + よ | あります → ありますよ |
| Plain sentence (verb / い-adj) | plain + よ | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いよ |
| Noun / な-adjective (plain) | + だ + よ | {f|休|やす}み → {f|休|やす}みだよ |
| Negative | ～ない／～ません + よ | {f|違|ちが}います → {f|違|ちが}いますよ |
| Request | ～てください + よ (pushy) | {f|待|ま}ってください → {f|待|ま}ってくださいよ |
| Combined with ね | よ + ね (never ねよ) | そうだ → そうだよね |
| Feminine variant | Noun／な-adj + よ, no だ | そう → そうよ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `だ` row is where register lives. 「そうだよ」 is neutral to
masculine; 「そうよ」 without the `だ` is marked as women's speech and sounds somewhat
traditional today. In polite speech the question does not arise, because `です` is
always there: 「そうですよ」.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あ、{f|財布|さいふ}が{f|落|お}ちました<b>よ</b>。

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`よ` attaches at any politeness level, and by itself it is neither casual nor formal:
「ありますよ」 is perfectly polite. What `よ` does carry is an attitude, and that is
where it gets dangerous.

Because `よ` claims the listener does not know something, using it on information
they obviously do have sounds condescending - the Japanese equivalent of "just so you
know". 「{f|今日|きょう}は{f|月曜日|げつようび}ですよ」 said to a colleague who has
been at work all morning is not helpful; it is a small correction. The higher the
listener's status, the worse this lands. Telling your boss 「その{f|資料|しりょう}は
{f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}にありますよ」 is fine if they were looking for it, and
faintly impertinent if they were not.

`よ` also sharpens under stress. On a negative or a correction it can sting:
「{f|違|ちが}いますよ」 is a good deal harder than 「{f|違|ちが}います」. On a request
it turns into impatience: 「{f|早|はや}くしてくださいよ」 is a complaint, not a
polite ask. And with the plain imperative - 「{f|早|はや}くしろよ」 - it is
straightforwardly rough male speech.

The friendly uses are just as real. `よ` reassures (「{f|大丈夫|だいじょうぶ}ですよ」),
warns (「{f|危|あぶ}ないよ」), offers (「あちらですよ」) and recommends
(「おいしいですよ」). In all of these you genuinely have information the other person
lacks, which is exactly the condition `よ` wants.

Intonation matters. Rising `よ` is light and helpful; a flat or falling `よ` is firm
and can be read as insistent. A learner unsure of the intonation is safer leaving
`よ` off entirely - a bare `です` never offends.

Finally, `よね`: `よ` and `ね` in that order, never ❌`ねよ`. It offers information
*and* asks for confirmation - "it's this, isn't it?" It is softer than `よ` alone,
because you are leaving the listener room to correct you, and it is one of the most
common endings in real conversation.

## Contrast

### よ vs ね

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よ` gives information the listener lacks. `ね` assumes the
listener already has it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんはもう{f|帰|かえ}りました<b>よ</b>。
Tanaka's already gone home. (you didn't know)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんはもう{f|帰|かえ}りました<b>ね</b>。
Tanaka's gone home, hasn't he. (we both saw him leave)
</pre>

The information is identical; only the assumption about the listener changes. Get it
backwards and the effect is immediate: `よ` on shared knowledge sounds like a
correction, and `ね` on private knowledge leaves the listener unable to answer.

A quick test before you speak: *does this person already know?* If yes, `ね` or
nothing. If no, `よ`.

### よ vs よね

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よ` asserts. `よね` asserts **and** asks you to confirm.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}、「みち」と{f|読|よ}みます<b>よ</b>。
This character is read "michi".
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}、「みち」と{f|読|よ}みます<b>よね</b>。
This character is read "michi", isn't it?
</pre>

The first is confident: you know, they don't, end of story. The second hands the
listener a way out - you are fairly sure, but you would like a second opinion. `よね`
is therefore much safer when talking to someone who might know better than you, and
it is what you should reach for when correcting a superior.

Note that `よね` is not the same as a bare `ね`. `ね` alone would assume the listener
already had the reading in mind; `よね` says you are supplying it and checking at the
same time.

### よ vs saying nothing

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a sentence with no final particle is neutral. Adding `よ` pushes
it at the listener.

</div>

Learners tend to treat `よ` as decoration and sprinkle it everywhere. It is not
neutral, and the plain sentence is very often the better choice.

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|休|やす}みです。
Tomorrow is a day off.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|休|やす}みです<b>よ</b>。
Tomorrow's a day off, you know.
</pre>

The first states a fact - appropriate in a report, an announcement, or an answer to a
direct question. The second addresses the listener personally and implies they were
unaware, which is friendly if true and slightly pointed if not.

In writing, `よ` almost never appears outside dialogue and messages between friends;
it is a spoken-language particle. And when answering a question you were just asked,
the bare form is usually right - the questioner already knows they didn't know.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ そうです**ねよ**。 → ✅ そうです**よね**。
  The order is fixed. `よ` comes first, `ね` is always last.

- ❌ 「{f|今日|きょう}、{f|金曜日|きんようび}ですよ。」 said to someone who plainly knows. → ✅ 「{f|今日|きょう}、{f|金曜日|きんようび}ですね。」
  `よ` claims the listener is missing the information. On shared knowledge it reads
  as a correction.

- ❌ （to your manager）その{f|仕事|しごと}はもう{f|終|お}わりましたよ。 → ✅ もう{f|終|お}わりました。
  Answering a superior's question with `よ` can sound flippant, as though the answer
  should have been obvious. The plain polite form is safer.

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}よ。（male speaker） → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}だよ。
  After a plain noun, `よ` needs `だ` in front of it. Dropping the `だ` is a marked,
  feminine-sounding pattern, not a neutral one.

## Practice

**1.** （your friend is about to leave without their umbrella）あ、{f|傘|かさ}、{f|忘|わす}れています____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|傘|かさ}、{f|忘|わす}れています<b>よ</b>。

They have not noticed, so you are supplying genuinely new information - the
prototypical use of `よ`. Rising intonation keeps it helpful rather than scolding.

</details>

**2.** Choose: you and a colleague both watched it rain all morning.
よく{f|降|ふ}ります（よ・ね）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

よく{f|降|ふ}ります<b>ね</b>。

The rain is shared experience, so there is nothing to inform anyone of. `よ` here
would sound as though you were correcting a colleague who had somehow failed to
notice the weather.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Don't worry - there's still plenty of time."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|心配|しんぱい}しないでください。まだ{f|時間|じかん}があります<b>よ</b>。

The listener believes time is short, so you are correcting their picture with
information they lack - a reassuring `よ`. Without it, 「まだ{f|時間|じかん}があります」
is a flat statement and does much less comforting.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}よね、と{f|先生|せんせい}が{f|言|い}いました。（male speaker, plain style）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}<b>だよね</b>、と{f|先生|せんせい}が{f|言|い}いました。

A plain noun predicate needs `だ` before `よ`. Leaving it out gives the feminine
pattern 「{f|雨|あめ}よね」, which does not match the speaker described.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) こちらのほうが{f|安|やす}いですよ。
(b) こちらのほうが{f|安|やす}いですよね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is you informing the listener: you know this one is cheaper and they do not. Said
by a shop assistant it is helpful; said to someone who has already compared the
prices, it sounds like a correction.

(b) softens the same claim into a check. You still believe this one is cheaper, but
the `ね` invites the listener to confirm or contradict you. That makes it the safer
choice when the other person may know the prices better than you do.

</details>
