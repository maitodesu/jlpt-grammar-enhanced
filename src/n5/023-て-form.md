# て-form

**Meaning** · the connective form - the shape a verb takes when the sentence
isn't finished with it yet

The て-form is not a tense and not a politeness level. It is the form a word
wears when something else is going to attach to it, or when the sentence carries
on past it. On its own it says roughly "…and then", but its real importance is
structural: `～ている`, `～てください`, `～てもいい`, `～てから`, `～てしまう`,
`～てあげる` and dozens more all plug into it. Learn the て-form and a large slice
of the grammar you meet later costs you nothing extra.

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** ({f|五段|ごだん} / godan / Group 1),
**る-verbs** ({f|一段|いちだん} / ichidan / Group 2), and the two irregulars する and
{f|来|く}る (Group 3). This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**. Only う-verbs
have complicated て-forms; the other two classes are trivial.

</div>

## Formation

る-verbs and the irregulars are one-liners. う-verbs change according to their
final syllable, and that table is the thing to memorise.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb, う・つ・る) | → って | {f|買|か}う → {f|買|か}って |
| Verb (う-verb, む・ぶ・ぬ) | → んで | {f|読|よ}む → {f|読|よ}んで |
| Verb (う-verb, く) | → いて | {f|書|か}く → {f|書|か}いて |
| Verb (う-verb, ぐ) | → いで | {f|泳|およ}ぐ → {f|泳|およ}いで |
| Verb (う-verb, す) | → して | {f|話|はな}す → {f|話|はな}して |
| Verb ({f|行|い}く, exception) | → って | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}って |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + て | {f|開|あ}ける → {f|開|あ}けて |
| Verb (する) | して | {f|掃除|そうじ}する → {f|掃除|そうじ}して |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | きて | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}て |
| Verb (negative) | ない → なくて／ないで | {f|行|い}かない → {f|行|い}かなくて／{f|行|い}かないで |
| い-adjective | い → くて | {f|寒|さむ}い → {f|寒|さむ}くて |
| な-adjective | + で | {f|親切|しんせつ} → {f|親切|しんせつ}で |
| Noun | + で | {f|学生|がくせい} → {f|学生|がくせい}で |

The う-verb rows again, one verb at a time, because this is the table you will
come back to:

| Dictionary ending | て-form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う | って | {f|使|つか}う → {f|使|つか}って |
| つ | って | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}って |
| る | って | {f|切|き}る → {f|切|き}って |
| む | んで | {f|休|やす}む → {f|休|やす}んで |
| ぶ | んで | {f|遊|あそ}ぶ → {f|遊|あそ}んで |
| ぬ | んで | {f|死|し}ぬ → {f|死|し}んで |
| く | いて | {f|聞|き}く → {f|聞|き}いて |
| ぐ | いで | {f|脱|ぬ}ぐ → {f|脱|ぬ}いで |
| す | して | {f|出|だ}す → {f|出|だ}して |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the **た-form uses exactly these sound changes**, with た/だ
in place of て/で. {f|読|よ}んで → {f|読|よ}んだ, {f|書|か}いて →
{f|書|か}いた, {f|話|はな}して → {f|話|はな}した, {f|行|い}って →
{f|行|い}った. Learn one table and you have learned both. This is the single
best return on memorisation effort in beginner Japanese.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - {f|行|い}く is the exception every learner trips over. By the く rule
it ought to be ❌{f|行|い}いて; it is {f|行|い}って. Nothing else in ordinary
Japanese behaves this way. Watch out too for う-verbs that *look* like る-verbs:
{f|帰|かえ}る → {f|帰|かえ}って (not ❌{f|帰|かえ}て), {f|走|はし}る →
{f|走|はし}って, {f|入|はい}る → {f|入|はい}って.

</div>

What the て-form is for, in rough order of how soon you will need it:

- **Linking events in sequence** - {f|起|お}きて、{f|顔|かお}を{f|洗|あら}って、{f|出|で}かけます。
- **A continuing action or a state** - `～ている`.
- **Requests** - `～てください`.
- **Permission and prohibition** - `～てもいい`, `～てはいけない`.
- **Cause**, when the second clause is a feeling or a result you did not choose.
- **Manner or means** - {f|歩|ある}いて{f|行|い}く "go on foot".

## Nuance & register

The て-form carries no politeness of its own - politeness is decided by whatever
ends the sentence. {f|食|た}べて{f|行|い}く and {f|食|た}べて{f|行|い}きます
differ only at the tail. That is exactly why it is so useful: a long chain of
て-forms lets you build a complicated sentence and only worry about register at
the very end.

A bare て-form left at the end of a sentence is a **casual request**:
ちょっと{f|待|ま}って, {f|見|み}せて, {f|手伝|てつだ}って. It is `～てください`
with the polite part bitten off, so it is friendly among equals and rude
upwards. Softening it with `ね` - {f|待|ま}ってね - makes it gentler; adding
`よ` makes it more insistent.

Two spoken contractions to expect early, because they are everywhere:

- `～ている` → `～てる`: {f|何|なに}してるの？ , {f|待|ま}ってるよ.
- `～ておく` → `～とく`, `～てしまう` → `～ちゃう`: {f|買|か}っとく,
  {f|食|た}べちゃった.

In formal writing, long chains of て-forms look chatty. Written Japanese often
replaces the linking て with the bare ます-stem instead - {f|朝|あさ}6{f|時|じ}に
{f|起|お}き、{f|新聞|しんぶん}を{f|読|よ}む - which is compact and noticeably
literary. You do not need to produce it at N5, but you will see it.

One real limitation: the て-form does not mark *why* two events are next to each
other. It only says they belong together, and the listener infers sequence,
cause, or simple accompaniment from context. When you need the reason to be
unmistakable, use `から` or `ので` instead.

## Contrast

### ～て vs ～てから

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `～て` says the events belong together; `～てから` says
the first one **finished** before the second started, and puts weight on that
order.

</div>

<pre>
{f|手|て}を<b>{f|洗|あら}って</b>、ご{f|飯|はん}を{f|食|た}べます。
I'll wash my hands and eat.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|手|て}を<b>{f|洗|あら}ってから</b>、ご{f|飯|はん}を{f|食|た}べます。
I'll eat after washing my hands.
</pre>

The difference is emphasis. With plain `て` the two actions are simply a
routine; with `てから` you are insisting on the order - first this, *then* that.
So `てから` is what you want in instructions and in warnings, and it sounds
fussy when the order was never in doubt.

### ～なくて vs ～ないで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～なくて` gives a **reason**; `～ないで` describes **how**
something was done, or asks someone **not** to do it.

</div>

<pre>
バスが<b>{f|来|こ}なくて</b>、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|遅|おく}れました。
The bus didn't come, so I was late for the meeting.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|朝|あさ}ご{f|飯|はん}を<b>{f|食|た}べないで</b>{f|学校|がっこう}に{f|行|い}きました。
I went to school without eating breakfast.
</pre>

Swap them and you get nonsense: ❌バスが{f|来|こ}ないで、{f|会議|かいぎ}に
{f|遅|おく}れました. A useful test - if you could put "so" or "because" in the
English, use `なくて`; if you could put "without", use `ないで`. Requests are
always `ないで`: ここに{f|車|くるま}を{f|止|と}めないでください.

Adjectives and nouns only have the `なくて` option:
{f|安|やす}くなくて, {f|学生|がくせい}じゃなくて.

### ～て (cause) vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～て` can only introduce a cause when the second clause is
something **outside your control**: a feeling, a result, an inability. If the
second clause is a request, an invitation, or a decision, you must use `から` or
`ので`.

</div>

<pre>
ニュースを{f|聞|き}い<b>て</b>、びっくりしました。
I was startled when I heard the news.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今日|きょう}は{f|忙|いそが}しい<b>から</b>、{f|早|はや}く{f|帰|かえ}ってください。
I'm busy today, so please go home early.
</pre>

The second sentence cannot use `て`: ❌{f|忙|いそが}しくて、{f|早|はや}く
{f|帰|かえ}ってください is ungrammatical, because a `て` cause clause refuses to
be followed by a command, a request, an invitation or a volitional. This is the
most common way learners misuse the て-form, and it is invisible in translation - 
English "so" covers both.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|行|い}いて → ✅ <b>{f|行|い}って</b>
  {f|行|い}く is the one exception to the く → いて rule. Everything else with く
  behaves normally: {f|書|か}いて, {f|聞|き}いて, {f|歩|ある}いて.

- ❌ {f|飲|の}んて → ✅ <b>{f|飲|の}んで</b>
  む・ぶ・ぬ take んで with a **voiced** で, never んて. Same for {f|遊|あそ}んで
  and {f|死|し}んで.

- ❌ {f|切|き}て → ✅ <b>{f|切|き}って</b>
  {f|切|き}る is an う-verb even though it ends in る, so it takes って.
  Contrast {f|着|き}る "wear", which really is a る-verb: {f|着|き}て.

- ❌ この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|広|ひろ}いで{f|明|あか}るいです。 → ✅ この{f|部屋|へや}は<b>{f|広|ひろ}くて</b>{f|明|あか}るいです。
  `で` is the connective for nouns and な-adjectives. い-adjectives drop the い
  and take くて.

- ❌ {f|食|た}べるてください。 → ✅ <b>{f|食|た}べて</b>ください。
  `ください` attaches to the て-form, not to the dictionary form. Drop the る
  before adding て.

## Practice

**1.** {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}け____、{f|部屋|へや}の{f|空気|くうき}を{f|入|い}れかえた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|開|あ}<b>けて</b>

{f|開|あ}ける is a る-verb: drop る and add て. Two actions in sequence, so the
first verb goes into the て-form and only the last one carries the tense.

</details>

**2.** {f|時間|じかん}が（なくて・ないで）、{f|昼|ひる}ご{f|飯|はん}が{f|食|た}べられませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|時間|じかん}が<b>なくて</b>、{f|昼|ひる}ご{f|飯|はん}が{f|食|た}べられませんでした。

This is a cause - "because there was no time" - and causes take `なくて`.
`ないで` would describe the manner of doing something, which makes no sense with
an inability in the second clause.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I got up at six, read the newspaper, and left the house at seven."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

6{f|時|じ}に<b>{f|起|お}きて</b>、{f|新聞|しんぶん}を<b>{f|読|よ}んで</b>、7{f|時|じ}に{f|家|いえ}を{f|出|で}ました。

Only the final verb takes tense and politeness; the て-forms in front of it are
neutral. {f|起|お}きる is a る-verb ({f|起|お}きて) and {f|読|よ}む is an う-verb
ending in む, so it takes んで.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}って、{f|傘|かさ}を{f|持|も}って{f|行|い}ってください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}っているから</b>、{f|傘|かさ}を{f|持|も}って{f|行|い}ってください。

A `て` clause used as a cause cannot be followed by a request. Swap it for
`から` or `ので`. The second {f|持|も}って is fine - that one is manner, not
cause.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}んで、{f|寝|ね}ます。
(b) {f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}んでから、{f|寝|ね}ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both describe the same two actions in the same order. (a) simply chains them - 
it is how you would describe your evening routine, with no particular stress on
sequence. (b) foregrounds the order, so it reads as "not before the medicine - 
medicine first, then bed."

That is why (b) is what a pharmacist or a parent would say: `てから` is the
form you reach for when getting the order right actually matters.

</details>
