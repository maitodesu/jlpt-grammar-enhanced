# ～ないでください

**Meaning** · please don't do ~ - the negative request

`～ないでください` is the negative counterpart of `～てください`. Take the ない-form,
add `で`, add `ください`. The `で` is not decoration: `ないで` is the **negative
て-form** of a verb, and it does the same joining work elsewhere in the language - 
{f|傘|かさ}を{f|持|も}たないで{f|出|で}かけた「I went out without an umbrella」.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form + で + ください | {f|吸|す}う → {f|吸|す}わないでください |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + で + ください | {f|捨|す}てる → {f|捨|す}てないでください |
| Verb (する) | しないで + ください | {f|心配|しんぱい}する → {f|心配|しんぱい}しないでください |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | {f|来|こ}ないで + ください | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ないでください |
| Casual | ない-form + で | {f|見|み}ないで／{f|見|み}ないでね |
| More polite | ～ないでいただけませんか | {f|入|はい}らないでいただけませんか |
| Notices and signs | ご{f|遠慮|えんりょ}ください／おやめください | {f|喫煙|きつえん}はご{f|遠慮|えんりょ}ください |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - build the ない-form first, then swap `ない` for `ないで`. The
ない-form is the hard part: {f|吸|す}う → {f|吸|す}<b>わ</b>ない (not ❌{f|吸|す}あない),
する → しない, {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ない (read <b>こ</b>ない, never ❌きない).

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `ないで` on its own is a full form of the verb, not just a piece of this
pattern. It joins clauses in the sense *without doing*
({f|何|なに}も{f|言|い}わないで{f|出|で}て{f|行|い}った), and it feeds other endings:
{f|忘|わす}れないでね, {f|来|こ}ないでほしい, {f|見|み}ないでくれる？

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ここでたばこを<b>{f|吸|す}わないでください</b>。
Please don't smoke here.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ<b>{f|帰|かえ}らないでください</b>。
Please don't leave yet.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>{f|心配|しんぱい}しないでください</b>。
Please don't worry.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|授業|じゅぎょう}{f|中|ちゅう}はスマホを<b>{f|見|み}ないでください</b>。
Please don't look at your phone during class.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|部屋|へや}には<b>{f|入|はい}らないでください</b>。
Please don't go into this room.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|大切|たいせつ}な{f|日|ひ}ですから、{f|明日|あした}は<b>{f|遅|おく}れないでください</b>ね。
Tomorrow's an important day, so please don't be late.
</pre>

<pre>
すみませんが、この{f|話|はなし}はまだ{f|誰|だれ}にも<b>{f|言|い}わないでください</b>。
Sorry, but please don't tell anyone about this yet.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが、{f|館内|かんない}では{f|写真|しゃしん}を<b>{f|撮|と}らないでいただけますか</b>。
I'm very sorry, but could I ask you not to take photographs inside the building?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Everything true of `～てください` is true here: it is polite in form and directive in
function. A negative directive is inherently more confronting than a positive one,
though, because you are telling someone to stop or not to start - so the softeners
matter more.

The usual ones are すみませんが / {f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが at the front, ちょっと
in the middle, and ね at the end. {f|大声|おおごえ}を{f|出|だ}さないでくださいね is
noticeably gentler than the same sentence without it.

Public notices avoid the bare form, because a sign that tells thousands of people
what not to do is at risk of sounding hectoring. Japanese signage prefers
ご{f|遠慮|えんりょ}ください「we ask that you refrain」, おやめください「please stop」,
or a plain noun + {f|禁止|きんし}「prohibited」 on the sternest notices. If you learn
to read those, you have covered most stations and museums.

Casually, `ください` drops off and the ない-form does the whole job:
{f|見|み}ないで！, {f|言|い}わないで. Add ね to soften, よ to press. `～ないでくれる？`
and `～ないでほしい` are common alternatives among friends - the first asks, the
second reports your wish.

One thing `～ないでください` is *not*: a statement of prohibition. It is a request. If
you need to say something is forbidden, `～てはいけません` is the pattern, and it
carries an authority that this one does not.

## Contrast

### ～ないでください vs ～ないでいただけませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative request, then the negative *question*. Upward, and
to strangers, ask rather than tell.

</div>

<pre>
ここに{f|車|くるま}を<b>とめないでください</b>。
Please don't park here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここに{f|車|くるま}を<b>とめないでいただけませんか</b>。
Would you mind not parking here?
</pre>

The second is what a neighbour says to a neighbour; the first is what the sign
says. `～ないでくださいませんか` sits between them, and the fully honorific
ご{f|遠慮|えんりょ}ください is the institutional voice - a shop, a train company, a
museum.

The relationship decides. Telling a stranger not to do something is delicate in any
language, and Japanese solves it by turning the request into a question and letting
the listener seem to choose.

### ～ないで vs ～なくて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - Japanese has **two** negative て-forms. `ないで` means *without
doing* and is the one used in requests; `なくて` means *and so, because … not*.

</div>

<pre>
{f|朝|あさ}ごはんを<b>{f|食|た}べないで</b>{f|学校|がっこう}へ{f|行|い}きました。
I went to school without eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
バスが<b>{f|来|こ}なくて</b>、{f|遅|おく}れました。
The bus didn't come, and so I was late.
</pre>

The first describes *how* the going happened; the second gives the *reason* for
being late. Swap them and the sentences stop making sense.

Two consequences worth memorising. First, requests always use `ないで` - there is no
❌{f|行|い}かなくてください. Second, `なくて` is the form that adjectives and nouns
take when they are negated and joined
({f|高|たか}くなくて{f|安心|あんしん}しました, {f|雨|あめ}じゃなくてよかった), so `ないで`
is verb-only territory.

### ～ないでください vs ～てはいけません

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ないでください` **asks**; `～てはいけません` **forbids**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|教室|きょうしつ}で{f|走|はし}ら<b>ないでください</b>。
Please don't run in the classroom.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|教室|きょうしつ}で{f|走|はし}っ<b>てはいけません</b>。
You must not run in the classroom.
</pre>

The first is a request, however firmly meant; the second states a rule, and the
speaker has to be in a position to state it - a teacher, a parent, a notice. Used
by an equal it sounds like a scolding.

There is a third, gentler option that learners rarely meet early enough:
`～ないようにしてください`「please try not to…」, which frames the same content as
advice. {f|夜|よる}{f|遅|おそ}く{f|電話|でんわ}しないようにしてください is the version
that survives being said to almost anyone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|見|み}ない<b>てください</b> → ✅ {f|見|み}ない<b>でください</b>
  The negative て-form is `ないで`. There is no form ❌`ないて`.

- ❌ {f|行|い}か<b>なくてください</b> → ✅ {f|行|い}か<b>ないでください</b>
  `なくて` gives a reason; it cannot carry a request.

- ❌ <b>するないでください</b> → ✅ <b>しないでください</b>
  する is irregular: its ない-form is しない, so the request is しないでください.

- ❌ たばこを<b>{f|吸|す}うないでください</b> → ✅ たばこを<b>{f|吸|す}わないでください</b>
  `ないで` attaches to the ない-form, not the dictionary form. For う-verbs ending in
  `う`, the ない-form takes `わ`.

- ❌ {f|来|き}ないでください → ✅ {f|来|こ}ないでください
  Same kanji, different reading. The ない-form of {f|来|く}る is read <b>こ</b>ない.

## Practice

**1.** ここは{f|図書館|としょかん}ですから、{f|大|おお}きい{f|声|こえ}で{f|話|はな}____ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|大|おお}きい{f|声|こえ}で{f|話|はな}<b>さないで</b>ください。

<code>{f|話|はな}す</code> → ない-form <code>{f|話|はな}さない</code> → swap `ない` for `ないで`. A
library is exactly the setting where the plain polite request is appropriate.

</details>

**2.** {f|窓|まど}を（{f|開|あ}けなくてください・{f|開|あ}けないでください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|開|あ}けないでください</b>。

Japanese has two negative て-forms and only one of them can carry a request.
`なくて` is for causes and consequences - <code>{f|開|あ}けなくて{f|寒|さむ}かった</code> - 
so it cannot be followed by `ください`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please don't come to the office tomorrow."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|明日|あした}は{f|会社|かいしゃ}に<b>{f|来|こ}ないでください</b>。

The ない-form of <code>{f|来|く}る</code> is <code>{f|来|こ}ない</code> - the kanji stays put and the
reading changes to こ. Writing きない is the classic slip.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|試験|しけん}{f|中|ちゅう}は{f|話|はなし}をするないでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|試験|しけん}{f|中|ちゅう}は{f|話|はなし}を<b>しないでください</b>。

する is one of the two irregular verbs. Its ない-form is しない, not ❌するない, so
the negative request is しないでください.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) ここに{f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|置|お}か<b>ないでください</b>。
(b) ここに{f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|置|お}い<b>てはいけません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a request. It is firm and unambiguous, but it is still the speaker asking,
and a colleague could say it to another colleague without friction.

(b) states a rule and claims the authority to state it. It is what a member of
staff, a teacher or a posted notice would use, and coming from an equal it sounds
like being told off.

</details>
