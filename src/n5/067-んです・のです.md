# んです・のです - explaining, or asking for an explanation

**Meaning** · here's the background - or, as a question, so what's the story?

`んです` does not change what a sentence says. It changes what the sentence is
*for*. Adding it tells the listener that this statement is tied to the situation
you are both in: it explains something, or asks the other person to explain.
`んです` is the spoken form, `のです` the written one, and the `ん` is simply `の`
worn down by speech.

## Formation

`の` is a nominaliser, so everything attaches to it the way it attaches to a noun - 
which is why nouns and な-adjectives need `な` in between.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + んです | {f|遅|おく}れる → {f|遅|おく}れるんです |
| Verb (past) | た-form + んです | {f|遅|おく}れた → {f|遅|おく}れたんです |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + んです | {f|行|い}かない → {f|行|い}かないんです |
| い-adjective | plain form + んです | {f|痛|いた}い → {f|痛|いた}いんです |
| な-adjective | drop だ + **な** + んです | {f|大変|たいへん}だ → {f|大変|たいへん}なんです |
| Noun | noun + **な** + んです | {f|学生|がくせい} → {f|学生|がくせい}なんです |
| Plain style | んです → んだ | {f|痛|いた}いんだ |
| Written / formal | んです → のです | {f|痛|いた}いのです |
| Question | んですか | どうした → どうしたんですか |
| Preface, softener | んですが／んですけど | {f|聞|き}きたいんですが |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `な` after nouns and な-adjectives, nothing after verbs and
い-adjectives. ❌<code>{f|学生|がくせい}だんです</code> is the classic slip;
<code>{f|学生|がくせい}なんです</code> is what you want.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
どうした<b>んです</b>か。
What's the matter?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|頭|あたま}が{f|痛|いた}い<b>んです</b>。
I've got a headache. (that's why I'm asking to go home)
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、{f|道|みち}が{f|分|わ}からない<b>んですが</b>…
Excuse me - I'm a bit lost…
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}はフランス{f|人|じん}<b>なんです</b>。だからワインにくわしいんですよ。
She's French. That's why she knows so much about wine.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}、なんて{f|読|よ}む<b>んです</b>か。
How do you read this kanji?
</pre>

<pre>
「どうして{f|遅|おく}れたんですか。」「{f|事故|じこ}で{f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まった<b>んです</b>。」
"Why were you late?" "The trains were stopped because of an accident."
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}、{f|休|やす}ませていただけませんか。{f|国|くに}から{f|母|はは}が{f|来|く}る<b>んです</b>。
Could I take tomorrow off? My mother is coming over from home.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|実|じつ}は{f|来月|らいげつ}{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>のです</b>。{f|長|なが}い{f|間|あいだ}お{f|世話|せわ}になりました。
The fact is, I'm moving next month. Thank you for everything over the years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**What `んです` adds is *connection*.** <code>{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っています</code> reports
the weather. <code>{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っているんです</code> offers it as the reason for
something - why I'm wet, why the match is off, why I'd rather not go out. Nothing
about the rain has changed; the sentence has been plugged into the conversation.

**That is also its danger.** A learner who ends every sentence with `んです` sounds
as if they are permanently explaining themselves, or making excuses. In neutral
self-introduction and plain description, use plain `です`／`ます`.
<code>{f|東京|とうきょう}に{f|住|す}んでいます</code> is a fact about you;
<code>{f|東京|とうきょう}に{f|住|す}んでいるんです</code> answers a question that was asked, out
loud or in the air.

**`んですか` can accuse.** Because the question demands an explanation, tone decides
everything. <code>{f|何|なに}をしているんですか</code> asked warmly is "oh, what are you up
to?"; asked flatly it is "what do you think you're doing?" Similarly
<code>どうして{f|来|こ}なかったんですか</code> presses for a justification in a way that
<code>{f|来|こ}られませんでしたか</code> does not. When you only want information and not a
defence, drop the `ん`.

**`～んですが` is one of the most useful shapes in the language.** It sets up a
request by explaining the situation first and then trailing off, leaving the
listener to offer:
<code>ちょっとお{f|願|ねが}いがあるんですが…</code>,
<code>すみません、この{f|席|せき}、{f|空|あ}いてるんですけど…</code>. The unfinished ending is
the politeness.

**Register.** `んです` is spoken and polite; `のです` is written and formal, at home
in essays, reports and public announcements, and it sounds stiff in conversation.
Plain style gives `んだ`／`のだ` (<code>{f|忙|いそが}しいんだ</code>) and, very commonly,
bare `の` for questions and soft statements: <code>どうしたの？</code> is neutral for
everyone, while `～のよ` and `～のね` are female-leaning and `～んだよ` male-leaning.
The same `の` turns up as the casual reason connector `んで` (a worn-down `ので`):
<code>ちょっと{f|急|いそ}いでるんで</code>.

## Contrast

### んです vs です

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `です` states. `んです` explains. Ask yourself whether the
sentence is answering a question, spoken or unspoken. If it is not, you probably
do not need `ん`.

</div>

<pre>
かぜを{f|引|ひ}きました。
I've caught a cold. (a report - new information)
</pre>

<pre>
かぜを{f|引|ひ}いた<b>んです</b>。
I've caught a cold. (that's why I look terrible / why I'm not coming)
</pre>

The second sentence needs a context to lean on. Said out of nowhere with nothing
to explain, it makes the listener hunt for the missing connection.

### ～んですか vs ～ですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ですか` asks for a fact. `～んですか` asks for the story behind
something you have just noticed.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}、{f|大阪|おおさか}へ{f|行|い}きますか。
Are you going to Osaka tomorrow? (simple question)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}、{f|大阪|おおさか}へ{f|行|い}く<b>んです</b>か。
Oh, you're going to Osaka tomorrow? (I've seen the ticket / heard something)
</pre>

The `ん` version presupposes evidence. That makes it warm and interested with a
friend, and prickly with a stranger or a subordinate, because you are asking them
to account for something. Learners over-produce `んですか` in exactly the situations
where it stings.

### のです vs んです vs んだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one grammar point, three registers: `のです` on paper, `んです`
in polite speech, `んだ`／`の` among friends.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|一人|ひとり}では{f|解決|かいけつ}できない<b>のです</b>。
This problem cannot be solved alone. (written, formal)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}じゃ{f|無理|むり}な<b>んだ</b>。{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれない？
I can't manage on my own. Could you give me a hand?
</pre>

Note that the plain form of the noun／な-adjective pattern is `なんだ`, not
❌`だんだ` - the `な` survives at every register.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|医者|いしゃ}**だんです**。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|医者|いしゃ}**なんです**。
  Nouns join `の` with `な`. The same goes for な-adjectives:
  ❌<code>{f|静|しず}かだんです</code> → ✅<code>{f|静|しず}かなんです</code>.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}{f|行|い}き**ます**んです。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}{f|行|い}くんです。
  `んです` follows plain forms; the politeness is already in the `です` at the end.

- ❌ はじめまして。{f|田中|たなか}なんです。 → ✅ はじめまして。{f|田中|たなか}です。
  An introduction explains nothing, so there is no `ん`. Using it here sounds like
  you are correcting a misunderstanding.

- ❌ (to a colleague, neutrally) どうして{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|来|こ}なかったんですか。 → ✅ {f|昨日|きのう}の{f|会議|かいぎ}、{f|来|こ}られませんでしたか。
  `んですか` demands an explanation. If you do not want to sound like you are
  taking someone to task, ask the plain question.

## Practice

**1.** どうしたんですか。 -  - {f|財布|さいふ}をなくした____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|財布|さいふ}をなくした<b>んです</b>。

The question asked for the story, so the answer supplies it. `んです` after the
た-form marks this as the explanation the listener was after, rather than a bare
announcement.

</details>

**2.** はじめまして、リンです。{f|中国|ちゅうごく}から{f|来|き}ました。（{f|学生|がくせい}です・{f|学生|がくせい}なんです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|学生|がくせい}<b>です</b>。

A self-introduction is new information, not an explanation of anything, so plain
`です` is right. <code>{f|学生|がくせい}なんです</code> would imply you were clearing up a
misunderstanding - as if someone had just assumed you worked here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Excuse me, I'd like to ask you something…"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません、ちょっと<b>{f|聞|き}きたいんですが</b>…

The `んですが` preface explains your situation and then stops, leaving the listener
room to respond. Finishing the sentence would make it heavier and slightly more
demanding.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: {f|来週|らいしゅう}は{f|休|やす}みます。{f|旅行|りょこう}だんです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|来週|らいしゅう}は{f|休|やす}みます。{f|旅行|りょこう}なんです。

<code>{f|旅行|りょこう}</code> is a noun, so it joins `んです` with `な`, never with `だ`.
The `んです` itself is well chosen here - the second sentence explains the first.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|走|はし}っていますか。
(b) {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|走|はし}っているんですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral question - do you run every day? It could come out of nowhere,
on a questionnaire or in small talk.

(b) is triggered by something: you have seen their running shoes by the door, or
they just mentioned a marathon. It reads as "oh, so you run every day?" - engaged
and curious with a friend, but slightly probing with someone you have just met,
because it asks them to account for what you noticed.

</details>
