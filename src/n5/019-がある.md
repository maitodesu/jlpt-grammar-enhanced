# ～がある

**Meaning** · there is / there are - something inanimate exists, or is available

`ある` is the existence verb for things that do not move under their own will:
objects, places, plants, and abstractions like time, money and plans. The same
verb covers "I have" ({f|時間|じかん}がある) and "there's a scheduled event"
({f|会議|かいぎ}がある) - in Japanese those are all just *something existing*.

## Formation

The thing that exists takes `が`. The place it exists in takes `に`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + が + ある | {f|本|ほん} → {f|本|ほん}がある |
| Noun + place | Place + に + Noun + が + ある | {f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}に{f|本|ほん}がある |
| Polite | ある → あります | {f|本|ほん}があります |
| Negative | ある → **ない** | {f|本|ほん}がない / {f|本|ほん}がありません |
| Past | ある → あった | {f|本|ほん}があった / {f|本|ほん}がありました |
| Negative past | ある → なかった | {f|本|ほん}がなかった / {f|本|ほん}がありませんでした |
| て-form | ある → あって | {f|時間|じかん}があって… |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ある` has one irregularity, and it is the negative. The plain
negative is `ない`, not ❌`あらない`. Everything else conjugates like a normal
う-verb.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}に{f|本|ほん}<b>があります</b>。
There's a book on the desk.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|時間|じかん}<b>がありません</b>。
I don't have time.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|近|ちか}くにコンビニ<b>がある</b>?
Is there a convenience store around here?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}の{f|午後|ごご}、{f|会議|かいぎ}<b>があります</b>。
There's a meeting tomorrow afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
かばんの{f|中|なか}に{f|何|なに}<b>があります</b>か。
What's in the bag?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|去年|きょねん}まで、ここに{f|大|おお}きい{f|木|き}<b>がありました</b>。
Until last year there was a big tree here.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、ちょっとお{f|話|はなし}<b>があるん</b>ですが、{f|今|いま}よろしいですか。
Sorry - there's something I'd like to talk to you about. Do you have a moment?
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|辺|へん}にはスーパー<b>がない</b>ので、{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}のときは{f|駅|えき}まで{f|歩|ある}きます。
There's no supermarket around here, so when I shop I walk all the way to the station.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ある` is register-neutral. `あります` is the ordinary polite form; `ある` is plain.
In formal service or business Japanese it climbs to `ございます`:
{f|何|なに}か{f|質問|しつもん}はございますか - "Are there any questions?" You will hear
that from shop staff and in speeches, and you are not expected to produce it at N5.

Two things learners get blindsided by:

**`が` drops in casual speech.**
{f|時間|じかん}ある?／お{f|金|かね}ない／コンビニあるかな。
This is extremely common - much more common than the full form in
conversation. Keep `が` in writing and in ます-speech.

**`ある` covers far more than physical objects.** Events ({f|試験|しけん}がある),
appointments ({f|約束|やくそく}がある), experiences ({f|行|い}ったことがある),
abilities and resources ({f|自信|じしん}がある). If you find yourself reaching for
"to have" in English, `ある` is usually the right verb.

One historical note: <code>{f|子|こ}どもがある</code> for "to have children" does exist, and you
will meet it in older or formal writing, where it frames children as a fact about
someone's life rather than as people in the room. In modern speech use
<code>{f|子|こ}どもがいる</code>.

## Contrast

### ～がある vs ～がいる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いる` is for things that move under their own will; `ある` is
for everything else. The line is *animacy*, not *life*.

</div>

People and animals take `いる`. Plants - alive, but rooted - take `ある`. So do
cars, buildings, books, and ideas.

<pre>
{f|庭|にわ}に{f|大|おお}きい{f|木|き}<b>があります</b>。
There's a big tree in the garden.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|庭|にわ}に{f|猫|ねこ}が<b>います</b>。
There's a cat in the garden.
</pre>

The animacy test is about how the speaker frames the thing, which is why the same
noun can go either way. A fish swimming in a tank is
{f|魚|さかな}が**いる**; a fish laid out at the shop is
{f|魚|さかな}が**ある**. Ghosts, being willful, take `いる`.

<div class="note">

**Note** - vehicles are the classic grey area. `タクシーがある` means taxis are
*available* here; `タクシーがいる` pictures one sitting there with a driver in it.
Both are heard. When in doubt with a vehicle, `ある` is safe.

</div>

### ～がある vs ～を{f|持|も}っている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ある` says a thing exists and is at your disposal.
<code>{f|持|も}っている</code> says you own it or are physically carrying it.

</div>

Both translate as "have", and English gives you no help choosing.

<pre>
{f|今|いま}、お{f|金|かね}<b>がありません</b>。
I don't have any money right now.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}、お{f|金|かね}を{f|持|も}っていません。
I'm not carrying any money on me right now.
</pre>

The useful hard rule: **<code>{f|持|も}っている</code> needs something you can hold or own.**
Abstract nouns cannot take it, so time, plans, meetings and experience are always
`ある`.

- ✅ {f|時間|じかん}があります　❌ {f|時間|じかん}を{f|持|も}っています
- ✅ {f|会議|かいぎ}があります　❌ {f|会議|かいぎ}を{f|持|も}っています
- ✅ {f|車|くるま}があります／{f|車|くるま}を{f|持|も}っています（both fine - "there's a car I can use" vs "I own a car"）

### ～に～がある vs ～は～にある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - put the **new** information late. If the *thing* is new, lead
with the place. If the *place* is new, lead with the thing and mark it `は`.

</div>

Both sentences below are correct, and they answer different questions.

<pre>
{f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}に{f|本|ほん}<b>があります</b>。
There's a book on the desk.
</pre>

That answers "what's on the desk?" - or describes a scene from scratch. The book
is the news, so it stays on the `が` side.

<pre>
{f|本|ほん}<b>は</b>{f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}にあります。
The book is on the desk.
</pre>

That answers "where's the book?" We both already know which book; the news is the
location, and news comes after `は`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|時間|じかん}が**あらない**。 → ✅ {f|時間|じかん}が**ない**。
  `ある` is the one verb whose plain negative is suppletive. `ない` - and past
  `なかった`, polite `ありません`.

- ❌ {f|部屋|へや}に{f|猫|ねこ}が**あります**。 → ✅ {f|部屋|へや}に{f|猫|ねこ}が**います**。
  Animals move under their own will, so they take `いる`.

- ❌ ペン**を**あります。 → ✅ ペン**が**あります。
  `ある` is intransitive. Nothing is being acted on, so `を` has no job here.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|時間|じかん}を{f|持|も}っていません。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}は{f|時間|じかん}がありません。
  <code>{f|持|も}っている</code> needs a concrete possession. Time is not one.

- ❌ ここ**で**{f|銀行|ぎんこう}があります。 → ✅ ここ**に**{f|銀行|ぎんこう}があります。
  `で` marks where an action happens. Existence is not an action - it takes `に`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|急|いそ}ぎましょう。もう{f|時間|じかん}が____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう{f|時間|じかん}が**ありません**（casual: **ない**）。
"Let's hurry - we're out of time."

The negative of `ある` is suppletive: `ない` in plain form, `ありません` in polite
form. ❌`あらない` and ❌`あらありません` do not exist.

</details>

**2.** {f|公園|こうえん}に{f|古|ふる}い{f|木|き}が（あります・います）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|公園|こうえん}に{f|古|ふる}い{f|木|き}が**あります**。

Trees are alive, but the `ある`／`いる` split is not about life - it's about moving
under your own will. Plants stay put, so they pattern with objects and take `ある`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Is there a post office near the station?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|駅|えき}の{f|近|ちか}くに{f|郵便局|ゆうびんきょく}が**ありますか**。

The location takes `に` (not `で` - nothing is happening there), the thing that
exists takes `が`, and a post office is inanimate, so `ある`. In casual speech you
would drop the particle: {f|駅|えき}の{f|近|ちか}く、{f|郵便局|ゆうびんきょく}ある?

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|来週|らいしゅう}、{f|試験|しけん}を{f|持|も}っていますから、{f|勉強|べんきょう}します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|来週|らいしゅう}、{f|試験|しけん}が**あります**から、{f|勉強|べんきょう}します。
"I have an exam next week, so I'm going to study."

English "have an exam" pulls you toward <code>{f|持|も}つ</code>, but <code>{f|持|も}っている</code>
requires something you can physically hold or legally own. Scheduled events exist
 - they use `ある`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) つくえの{f|上|うえ}に{f|辞書|じしょ}があります。
(b) {f|辞書|じしょ}はつくえの{f|上|うえ}にあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) introduces the dictionary as new information - "there's a dictionary on the
desk." You would say it describing a room, or answering "what's on the desk?"

(b) treats the dictionary as already under discussion (`は` marks it as the topic)
and delivers the *location* as the news - "the dictionary is on the desk." That's
the answer to "where's the dictionary?" Same facts, opposite information flow.

</details>
