# が - subject marker

**Meaning** · marks the subject of a clause, and singles out *which one* it is

`が` names the thing that does the verb, or the thing an adjective is true of. Its
second job is the one learners underestimate: `が` **points**. It presents its noun
as the piece of news in the sentence - the answer to a "which one?" that may never
have been asked out loud.

## Formation

`が` is a particle. It attaches directly to the noun, with no change to the noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + が | {f|犬|いぬ} → {f|犬|いぬ}が{f|好|す}きです |
| Question word | Question word + が (never は) | だれ → だれが{f|来|き}ますか |
| Nominalised clause | Clause + の / こと + が | {f|走|はし}る → {f|走|はし}るのが{f|速|はや}い |
| Subject of a noun-modifying clause | Noun + が + verb + Noun | {f|私|わたし}が{f|作|つく}った → {f|私|わたし}が{f|作|つく}ったケーキ |
| Object of {f|好|す}き・{f|上手|じょうず}・ある・わかる・ほしい | Noun + が + predicate | {f|日本語|にほんご} → {f|日本語|にほんご}がわかります |
| Object of {f|見|み}える・{f|聞|き}こえる・できる | Noun + が + predicate | {f|音|おと} → {f|音|おと}が{f|聞|き}こえる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `が` never combines with `は`, `も` or `を`. When a subject becomes the
topic, `が` is *replaced*: {f|犬|いぬ}が → {f|犬|いぬ}は, {f|犬|いぬ}も. There is no
`がは` or `がも`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}<b>が</b>{f|降|ふ}っています。
It's raining.
</pre>

<pre>
だれ<b>が</b>{f|来|き}ますか。
Who's coming?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}は{f|犬|いぬ}<b>が</b>{f|好|す}きです。
I like dogs.
</pre>

<pre>
あ、バス<b>が</b>{f|来|き}た。
Oh - here comes the bus.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|窓|まど}<b>が</b>{f|大|おお}きくて、{f|明|あか}るいですね。
This room has big windows - it's nice and bright.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|時間|じかん}<b>が</b>ないから、また{f|今度|こんど}{f|話|はな}しましょう。
I don't have time, so let's talk another time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|去年|きょねん}{f|買|か}ったパソコン<b>が</b>{f|急|きゅう}に{f|動|うご}かなくなって、{f|困|こま}っています。
The laptop I bought last year suddenly stopped working, and I'm stuck.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません、あそこに{f|立|た}っている{f|人|ひと}<b>が</b>{f|田中|たなか}さんですか。
Excuse me - is the person standing over there Tanaka-san?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`が` is completely register-neutral. It is identical in plain and polite speech,
in men's and women's speech, and in writing. Nothing about choosing `が` makes you
sound formal or casual.

What *does* vary is whether you say it at all. In relaxed conversation `が` is
routinely dropped when the sentence is short and the subject is obvious:

- {f|雨|あめ}、{f|降|ふ}ってるよ。 - "It's raining."
- {f|時間|じかん}ある? - "Got a minute?"

Dropping `が` is casual, not wrong. Keep it in writing, in ます-form speech, and
any time the sentence is long enough that the listener needs the signpost.

Two things to keep separate. This `が` is a **particle** that follows a noun. There
is a completely different `が` - a **conjunction** meaning "but" - that follows a
whole clause: {f|高|たか}いですが、{f|買|か}います。 Same syllable, unrelated word. If
what precedes `が` is a finished sentence, it is the "but" one.

Finally, `が` marks the subject **of its own clause**, which is why it turns up in
places English speakers do not expect: inside relative clauses, inside <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code>
clauses, inside conditionals. {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ったら、{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。

## Contrast

### が vs は

This is the hardest thing at N5, and the reason is that `は` is not a subject
marker at all. `は` marks a **topic** - "about this thing, here's what I have to
say." `が` marks a **subject**. The two often sit in the same slot in the sentence,
which is why they look like alternatives, but they are answering different needs.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the new information goes **after** `は`, but **before** `が`.

</div>

That one line does most of the work. Look at where the news is:

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さん<b>は</b>{f|京都|きょうと}へ{f|行|い}きました。
Tanaka went to Kyoto.
</pre>

We already know who Tanaka is; the news is *Kyoto*, and it comes after `は`. This
answers "What did Tanaka do?"

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さん<b>が</b>{f|京都|きょうと}へ{f|行|い}きました。
It was Tanaka who went to Kyoto.
</pre>

Here we already know somebody went to Kyoto; the news is *Tanaka*, and it comes
before `が`. This answers "Who went to Kyoto?"

The same logic explains why **question words always take `が`, never `は`**. A
question word *is* the unknown, so it has to sit on the `が` side:

<pre>
だれ<b>が</b>この{f|窓|まど}を{f|割|わ}りましたか。
Who broke this window?
</pre>

And the answer keeps `が`, because the answer is still the news:

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}<b>が</b>{f|割|わ}りました。
My little brother broke it.
</pre>

A second, related use: `が` is how you report something you have just noticed - 
an event presented whole, with no topic yet established. This is why stories begin
with `が` and continue with `は`:

<pre>
むかしむかし、ある{f|村|むら}におじいさん<b>が</b>{f|住|す}んでいました。
Long ago, in a certain village, there lived an old man.
</pre>

<pre>
おじいさん<b>は</b>{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|山|やま}へ{f|木|き}を{f|切|き}りに{f|行|い}きました。
Every day the old man went to the mountain to cut wood.
</pre>

Sentence one introduces him - brand new, so `が`. Sentence two talks *about* him - 
now known, so `は`.

Because they do different jobs, both can appear in one sentence, and the classic
example is worth memorising whole:

<pre>
{f|象|ぞう}<b>は</b>{f|鼻|はな}<b>が</b>{f|長|なが}い。
Elephants have long trunks. (As for elephants - the nose is long.)
</pre>

<code>{f|象|ぞう}</code> is the topic; <code>{f|鼻|はな}</code> is the subject of <code>{f|長|なが}い</code>. Neither particle could do the
other's job here.

One last thing `は` can do that `が` cannot: **contrast**. `は` implicitly sets its
noun against something else.

<pre>
コーヒー<b>は</b>{f|飲|の}みますが、お{f|酒|さけ}<b>は</b>{f|飲|の}みません。
I drink coffee, but I don't drink alcohol.
</pre>

Swap those to `が` and the contrast evaporates.

### が vs は inside a noun-modifying clause

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - inside a clause that modifies a noun, the subject takes `が`.
`は` is not available there.

</div>

`は` scopes over a whole sentence, so it cannot be trapped inside a clause that is
only decorating a noun. This produces one of the most common N5 errors.

<pre>
これは{f|私|わたし}<b>が</b>{f|撮|と}った{f|写真|しゃしん}です。
This is a photo I took.
</pre>

<code>{f|私|わたし}が{f|撮|と}った</code> modifies <code>{f|写真|しゃしん}</code>. Saying
❌<code>{f|私|わたし}は{f|撮|と}った{f|写真|しゃしん}</code> breaks the sentence, because `は`
would try to make <code>{f|私|わたし}</code> the topic of the *outer* sentence.

The same holds inside conditionals and quoted thoughts:

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さん<b>が</b>{f|来|く}ると{f|思|おも}います。
I think Tanaka is coming.
</pre>

<div class="note">

**Note** - in a noun-modifying clause, `が` can be swapped for `の` with no change
in meaning: {f|私|わたし}**の**{f|撮|と}った{f|写真|しゃしん}. This is common in
writing and sounds slightly compact. It only works inside such clauses - never in
a main sentence.

</div>

### が vs を

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a small set of predicates take their "object" with `が`, not
`を`, because grammatically that noun *is* the subject.

</div>

<code>{f|好|す}き</code>, <code>{f|嫌|きら}い</code>, <code>{f|上手|じょうず}</code>, <code>{f|下手|へた}</code>, `ほしい`, `わかる`,
`できる`, `ある`, <code>{f|見|み}える</code>, <code>{f|聞|き}こえる</code> all belong to this set. In English
these look transitive ("I understand Japanese"), so `を` is a strong pull - and it
is wrong.

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}は{f|日本語|にほんご}<b>が</b>わかります。
I understand Japanese.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山|やま}の{f|上|うえ}から{f|海|うみ}<b>が</b>{f|見|み}えます。
You can see the sea from the top of the mountain.
</pre>

Compare <code>{f|見|み}る</code>, an ordinary transitive verb, which does take `を`:
{f|海|うみ}**を**{f|見|み}ます - "I look at the sea." <code>{f|見|み}える</code> is "is visible", so
the sea is what *is* - hence `が`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ だれ**は**{f|来|き}ましたか。 → ✅ だれ**が**{f|来|き}ましたか。
  A question word is the unknown, and unknowns cannot be topics. Question words
  take `が`.

- ❌ これは{f|私|わたし}**は**{f|撮|と}った{f|写真|しゃしん}です。 → ✅ これは{f|私|わたし}**が**{f|撮|と}った{f|写真|しゃしん}です。
  Inside a clause modifying a noun, the subject takes `が`. `は` cannot live there.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|日本語|にほんご}**を**わかります。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}は{f|日本語|にほんご}**が**わかります。
  English transitivity is not a guide. `わかる`, `できる`, <code>{f|好|す}き</code>, `ほしい`,
  <code>{f|見|み}える</code> all take `が`.

- ❌ {f|犬|いぬ}**がは**{f|好|す}きです。 → ✅ {f|犬|いぬ}**は**{f|好|す}きです。
  `が` is replaced by `は` and `も`, never stacked with them.

- ❌ ぼく**は**やります!（volunteering, "I'll do it!"）→ ✅ ぼく**が**やります!
  Volunteering means identifying *who* - you are the news. That is a `が` job.

## Practice

**1.** だれ____このケーキを{f|作|つく}りましたか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

だれ**が**このケーキを{f|作|つく}りましたか。

Question words are always the unknown information in the sentence, and the unknown
sits on the `が` side. `だれは` is never possible.

</details>

**2.** 「{f|山田|やまだ}さんはどんな{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}きますか。」
「{f|山田|やまだ}さん（は・が）ジャズをよく{f|聞|き}きます。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|山田|やまだ}さん**は**ジャズをよく{f|聞|き}きます。

The question already put {f|山田|やまだ}さん on the table, so he is old information - the
topic. The news is *ジャズ*, and news comes after `は`. Using `が` here would mean
"it's *Yamada* who listens to jazz", answering a question nobody asked.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Who made this? - I made it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「だれ**が**これを{f|作|つく}りましたか。」「{f|私|わたし}**が**{f|作|つく}りました。」

Both halves take `が`. The question word is the unknown, and the answer replaces
that unknown - it is still the news, so it keeps `が`. Answering
「{f|私|わたし}は{f|作|つく}りました」 would sound like a contrast: "*I* made it (whatever
the others did)."

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|母|はは}は{f|作|つく}った{f|料理|りょうり}はとてもおいしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|母|はは}**が**{f|作|つく}った{f|料理|りょうり}はとてもおいしいです。
"The food my mother makes is delicious."

<code>{f|母|はは}が{f|作|つく}った</code> is a clause modifying <code>{f|料理|りょうり}</code>, and a subject
inside such a clause must take `が`. The outer sentence keeps its own topic
particle on <code>{f|料理|りょうり}は</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|山田|やまだ}さん**は**{f|来|き}ました。
(b) {f|山田|やまだ}さん**が**{f|来|き}ました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats {f|山田|やまだ}さん as already-known and reports what he did - "Yamada came
(whatever the others did)." It fits a conversation that was already about him, and
with `は` it can carry a hint of contrast.

(b) presents {f|山田|やまだ}さん as the news - "*Yamada* is the one who came", or, said on
noticing him, simply "Yamada's here!" It answers "who came?", and it is what you
would say announcing his arrival to a room that did not know.

</details>
