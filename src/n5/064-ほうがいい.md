# ～ほうがいい - it'd be better to

**Meaning** · you'd better do it - doing this is the better of the two options

`ほう` means "side" or "alternative", so `～ほうがいい` literally weighs one choice
against another: *the doing-it side is better*. That comparison never fully goes
away, and it is what gives the pattern its bite. Saying it means you have judged
the listener's situation and found their current course wanting.

## Formation

For advice, the verb goes in the **た-form**. This is the form you will use ninety
percent of the time, and it is not a past tense.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (advice) | た-form + ほうがいい | {f|休|やす}む → {f|休|やす}んだほうがいい |
| Verb (negative advice) | ない-form + ほうがいい | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}かないほうがいい |
| Verb (general comparison) | dictionary form + ほうがいい | {f|歩|ある}く → {f|歩|ある}くほうがいい |
| い-adjective | plain form + ほうがいい | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いほうがいい |
| な-adjective | な + ほうがいい | {f|静|しず}かだ → {f|静|しず}かなほうがいい |
| Noun | の + ほうがいい | バス → バスのほうがいい |
| Polite | いい → いいです | {f|休|やす}んだほうがいいです |
| Past ("should have") | いい → よかった | {f|休|やす}んだほうがよかった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - affirmative advice takes `た`; negative advice takes `ない`.
There is no ❌`～なかったほうがいい`. The two halves are not symmetrical, and this
is the single most common error with the pattern.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|寝|ね}たほうがいい</b>よ。
You should get to bed early.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今日|きょう}は<b>{f|行|い}かないほうがいい</b>です。
It'd be better not to go today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|薬|くすり}を<b>{f|飲|の}んだほうがいい</b>ですよ。
You ought to take some medicine.
</pre>

<pre>
かさを<b>{f|持|も}っていったほうがいい</b>よ。{f|午後|ごご}から{f|雨|あめ}だって。
Take an umbrella - apparently it's going to rain this afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|店|みせ}は{f|高|たか}いから、こっちで<b>{f|買|か}ったほうがいい</b>と{f|思|おも}う。
That shop's expensive, so I think you're better off buying it here.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|夜|よる}{f|遅|おそ}くにこの{f|道|みち}は<b>{f|通|とお}らないほうがいい</b>です。{f|暗|くら}くて{f|危|あぶ}ないですから。
You'd better not take this road late at night - it's dark and it's dangerous.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}があるなら、{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|休|やす}んで{f|病院|びょういん}に<b>{f|行|い}ったほうがいい</b>と{f|思|おも}いますよ。
If you've got a fever, I really think you should take the day off and see a doctor.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|上司|じょうし}に{f|相談|そうだん}してから<b>{f|決|き}めたほうがいい</b>んじゃないでしょうか。
Wouldn't it be better to decide after you've talked it over with your manager?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ほうがいい` is often taught as a gentle "should", and that is the source of a lot
of accidental rudeness. It is a **recommendation with weight behind it**. The
comparison built into `ほう` means that when you recommend option A, you are
simultaneously saying option B - the one the listener is currently on - is worse.
English "you'd better" carries the same faint edge, and for the same reason.

Some practical consequences:

**Do not aim it upward.**
{f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|早|はや}く{f|帰|かえ}ったほうがいいですよ
is grammatical, polite in form, and still presumptuous, because you are
telling a superior what to do. To a superior, drop the directive framing entirely:
`～てはいかがでしょうか`, <code>～てもよろしいかと{f|思|おも}います</code>, or just describe the
situation and let them decide.

**`よ` is doing real work.** In speech, `～たほうがいいよ` reads as friendly and
concerned; bare `～たほうがいい` can land flat, like a verdict. `～たほうがいいですよ`
is the everyday polite advice form you will actually hear.

**Hedges are normal, not weakness.** <code>～と{f|思|おも}います</code>,
`～んじゃないですか`, `～かもしれません` all take the edge off without changing the
advice. Native speakers use them constantly.

**`～ないほうがいい` is blunter than the affirmative.** Telling someone *not* to do
something presupposes they were about to. Reserve it for cases where you mean it - 
warnings, danger, genuine bad ideas.

Two spelling and shape notes. `ほう` is very often written <code>{f|方|ほう}</code>, and you
should expect <code>{f|方|ほう}がいい</code> in print. And `いい` swaps freely for its politer
relatives: <code>～たほうがよろしいかと{f|思|おも}います</code> is standard business Japanese,
while `～たほうがよかった` shifts the whole thing into regret - "I should have."

## Contrast

### ～たほうがいい vs ～るほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - た-form gives advice about *this* situation. Dictionary form
compares two options in the abstract.

</div>

This is the distinction the pattern is built on, and it is why the "past tense"
form is the normal one for advice.

<pre>
{f|薬|くすり}を<b>{f|飲|の}んだほうがいい</b>です。
You should take some medicine.
</pre>

The た-form presents the action as a completed package - "having taken it" - and
holds that up against the listener's actual, present situation. It points at *you*,
*now*.

<pre>
{f|薬|くすり}を<b>{f|飲|の}むほうがいい</b>です。
Taking medicine is the better approach.
</pre>

The dictionary form floats free of anyone in particular. It reads as a general
policy statement, which is why using it on a sick friend sounds oddly detached, as
though you were reading from a leaflet.

Dictionary form comes into its own when the two options are actually spelled out:

<pre>
バスで{f|行|い}くより、{f|電車|でんしゃ}で<b>{f|行|い}くほうがいい</b>ですよ。
Going by train is better than going by bus.
</pre>

There the sentence really is a comparison, and `より` makes the other side
explicit. That is the same `ほうがいい` you meet as the comparison pattern.

### ～ないほうがいい vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ないほうがいい` = *don't*. `～なくてもいい` = *you don't have
to*. One forbids, the other releases.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は<b>{f|来|こ}ないほうがいい</b>です。
You'd better not come tomorrow. (something is wrong with coming)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は<b>{f|来|こ}なくてもいい</b>です。
You don't have to come tomorrow. (it's optional; come if you like)
</pre>

Mixing these up produces one of the more embarrassing N5 errors, because the two
sentences are almost opposite in social effect. `～なくてもいい` grants permission;
`～ないほうがいい` withdraws it.

### ～ほうがいい vs ～たらどうですか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ほうがいい` says the alternative is worse.
`～たらどうですか` merely puts an idea on the table.

</div>

<pre>
{f|先生|せんせい}に<b>{f|聞|き}いたほうがいい</b>ですよ。
You should ask the teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|先生|せんせい}に{f|聞|き}いたらどうですか。
How about asking the teacher?
</pre>

The second leaves the decision visibly with the listener, which makes it the safer
choice when you are not sure the advice is wanted, or when the person outranks you.
It is not always softer - said sharply, `～たらどうですか` can sound impatient - but
its default is more open.

Further up the scale sit `～なければなりません` ("must") and `～べきです` ("ought
to", moral weight). `ほうがいい` sits between "how about" and "must": stronger than
a suggestion, weaker than an obligation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|行|い}か**なかった**ほうがいいです。 → ✅ {f|行|い}か**ない**ほうがいいです。
  Negative advice uses the plain `ない`-form. The た of `～たほうがいい` has no
  negative counterpart in this pattern.

- ❌ {f|風邪|かぜ}ですか。{f|休|やす}**むほうがいい**ですよ。 → ✅ {f|休|やす}**んだほうがいい**ですよ。
  For advice aimed at the person in front of you, use the た-form. The dictionary
  form turns it into a detached general statement.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、もっと{f|早|はや}く{f|帰|かえ}ったほうがいいですよ。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、そろそろお{f|帰|かえ}りになってはいかがでしょうか。
  The grammar is fine; the direction is not. `ほうがいい` implies the listener is
  currently choosing wrong, which you do not say upward.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|来|こ}ないほうがいいです。（intending "you don't have to come"）→ ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|来|こ}なくてもいいです。
  `～ないほうがいい` tells someone not to do it. Optionality is `～なくてもいい`.

- ❌ この{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}んだほうが**いいでした**。 → ✅ この{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}んだほうが**よかった**です。
  `いい` is an い-adjective. Its past is `よかった`, never ❌`いいでした`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|風邪|かぜ}ですね。{f|今日|きょう}は{f|休|やす}____ほうがいいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|今日|きょう}は{f|休|やす}**んだ**ほうがいいですよ。

Affirmative advice takes the た-form. <code>{f|休|やす}む</code> is a う-verb ending in む, so
む → んだ. The た-form here is not past tense - it is what makes the advice land on
this person, in this situation.

</details>

**2.** {f|危|あぶ}ないですから、あの{f|道|みち}は（{f|通|とお}らない・{f|通|とお}らなかった）ほうがいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの{f|道|みち}は<b>{f|通|とお}らない</b>ほうがいいです。

Affirmative and negative are not symmetrical here. Affirmative advice uses た, but
negative advice uses the plain `ない`-form. ❌<code>{f|通|とお}らなかったほうがいい</code> is
not a form of this pattern at all.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You'd better take an umbrella."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

かさを<b>{f|持|も}っていったほうがいい</b>ですよ。
（also fine: かさを{f|持|も}ったほうがいいですよ。）

た-form because this is advice for the person in front of you. The `ですよ` is worth
adding - `よ` frames it as helpful information rather than a verdict, which is how
this advice is normally delivered in speech.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: {f|部長|ぶちょう}、その{f|資料|しりょう}はもう{f|一度|いちど}{f|確認|かくにん}したほうがいいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The grammar is correct - the problem is the register.

✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、その{f|資料|しりょう}はもう{f|一度|いちど}{f|確認|かくにん}なさってはいかがでしょうか。
（or: …{f|確認|かくにん}していただいたほうがよろしいかと{f|思|おも}います。）

`ほうがいい` carries the implication that the listener's current course is the
worse option, so aiming it at a superior is presumptuous however polite the ending
is. Reframe as a question or a hedged opinion.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もっと{f|野菜|やさい}を{f|食|た}べたほうがいいです。
(b) もっと{f|野菜|やさい}を{f|食|た}べるほうがいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is advice directed at the listener: "you should eat more vegetables." The
た-form ties the recommendation to this particular person's situation, and implies
the speaker has looked at how they currently eat.

(b) is a general comparison: "eating more vegetables is the better option." It
floats free of any individual and reads like a statement of policy - fine in a
health article, oddly impersonal said to a friend over dinner.

</details>
