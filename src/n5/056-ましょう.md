# ～ましょう

**Meaning** · let's - a proposal made on the assumption you already agree

`～ましょう` is the polite volitional. It turns a verb into "let's do it", and its
defining feature is **confidence**: the speaker takes agreement as read. That makes
it the right form for a plan already settled, for a leader moving a group along, and
for a public slogan - and the wrong form for a genuine, open invitation, where
`～ませんか` is what you want.

A second use hides inside the first. With <code>{f|私|わたし}が</code>, `～ましょう` is not "let's"
at all but "I'll do it" - the speaker volunteering.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + ましょう | {f|行|い}きます → {f|行|い}きましょう |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + ましょう | {f|食|た}べます → {f|食|た}べましょう |
| Verb (する) | し + ましょう | します → しましょう |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | き + ましょう | {f|来|き}ます → {f|来|き}ましょう |
| Softened into a question | ましょう + か | {f|行|い}きましょうか |
| Casual equivalent | plain volitional | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}こう／{f|食|た}べる → {f|食|た}べよう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ましょう` replaces `ます`, so it builds off the same stem.
{f|飲|の}み<b>ます</b> → {f|飲|の}み<b>ましょう</b>. It never attaches to the dictionary
form: ❌{f|飲|の}むましょう.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no negative `ましょう`. ❌{f|行|い}きませんしょう does not exist. To
propose *not* doing something, negate the content instead:
{f|今日|きょう}は{f|買|か}うのはやめましょう or {f|今日|きょう}は{f|買|か}わないことにしましょう.

</div>

## Nuance & register

`ましょう` is polite in form but *assertive in function*. You are announcing what
the group is going to do. That is fine when the plan is agreed, when you are the one
running the meeting, or when the proposal is obviously welcome
({f|寒|さむ}いですね、{f|中|なか}に{f|入|はい}りましょう). It is presumptuous when the
other person has not yet agreed to anything, and doubly so upward:
{f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|飲|の}みに{f|行|い}きましょう helps itself to the boss's evening.
{f|飲|の}みに{f|行|い}きませんか or いかがですか leaves the decision where it belongs.

The same assertiveness is why `ましょう` is the voice of public exhortation.
Handwashing posters, road-safety signs and school announcements are full of it:
{f|手|て}を{f|洗|あら}いましょう, {f|気|き}をつけましょう,
{f|時間|じかん}を{f|守|まも}りましょう. Grammatically these are invitations; in
practice they are instructions with a smile.

As an answer, `ましょう` is the standard *yes*. To
{f|一緒|いっしょ}に{f|行|い}きませんか you reply ええ、{f|行|い}きましょう - the
invitation was open, and `ましょう` closes it.

The <code>{f|私|わたし}が</code> use is worth practising: {f|私|わたし}が{f|持|も}ちましょう「I'll
carry that」. It is a shade more take-charge than
{f|持|も}ちましょうか, which asks first, and noticeably more formal than
{f|持|も}ちます. In service Japanese it is often replaced by the humble
お{f|持|も}ちしましょう.

Casually, the whole thing collapses to the plain volitional: {f|行|い}こう,
{f|食|た}べよう, しよう, {f|来|こ}よう. Between friends `ましょう` sounds oddly
ceremonious, like proposing a motion.

## Contrast

### ～ましょう vs ～ませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ましょう` **assumes** you'll agree; `ませんか` **asks** whether
you will. If the other person could reasonably say no, use `ませんか`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|映画|えいが}を<b>{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}きましょう</b>。
Let's go and see a film.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|映画|えいが}を<b>{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}きませんか</b>。
Would you like to go and see a film?
</pre>

The first works when you have already decided to spend the afternoon together and
are choosing what to do. The second works when you are asking someone out - it is a
question, it is negative, and both of those give the listener room.

This is why the pair so often appears in sequence: one person invites with
`ませんか`, the other accepts with `ましょう`. Reversing them is the classic learner
error, and it is the difference between "would you like to?" and "we're going".

### ～ましょう vs ～ましょうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `か` turns a decision into a question. `ましょう` announces;
`ましょうか` checks, or offers to do something *for* the listener.

</div>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>{f|出|で}ましょう</b>。
Let's head off.
</pre>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>{f|出|で}ましょうか</b>。
Shall we head off?
</pre>

Adding `か` costs nothing and softens a great deal, so `ましょうか` is the safer
default whenever you are not certain the other person is ready. It also unlocks a
sense `ましょう` does not have: with the speaker as the doer, it becomes an offer - 
{f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|持|も}ちましょうか「shall I take your bag?」 where
{f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|持|も}ちましょう is you announcing that you will.

### ～ましょう vs the plain volitional

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different politeness. `ましょう` is the ます-level
form; {f|行|い}こう and {f|食|た}べよう are the plain-level ones.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|八|はち}{f|時|じ}に<b>{f|集|あつ}まりましょう</b>。
Let's gather at eight tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
おなかすいた。なんか<b>{f|食|た}べよう</b>。
I'm hungry. Let's get something to eat.
</pre>

The plain forms are built like this: う-verbs change the final `-u` to `-ou`
({f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}こう, {f|飲|の}む → {f|飲|の}もう), る-verbs swap `る` for
`よう` ({f|見|み}る → {f|見|み}よう), and the two irregulars are する → しよう,
{f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}よう.

Register maps straight across: use the plain volitional with friends, family and
anyone you would speak to in plain form, and `ましょう` everywhere you are using
`ます`. Note that the plain form can also take `か` - {f|行|い}こうか is the casual
equivalent of {f|行|い}きましょうか.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|飲|の}む<b>ましょう</b> → ✅ {f|飲|の}み<b>ましょう</b>
  `ましょう` replaces `ます` on the stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ {f|行|い}き<b>ましょうですか</b> → ✅ {f|行|い}き<b>ましょうか</b>
  `ましょう` already contains the copula. Just add `か`.

- ❌ {f|課長|かちょう}、{f|一緒|いっしょ}に{f|帰|かえ}り<b>ましょう</b>。 → ✅ {f|課長|かちょう}、{f|一緒|いっしょ}に{f|帰|かえ}り<b>ませんか</b>。
  Deciding a superior's movements for them. Ask instead of proposing.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}り<b>ましょう</b>。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>でしょう</b>。
  `ましょう` is a proposal, not a prediction. Guessing about the weather is
  `でしょう`.

- ❌ {f|行|い}き<b>ませんしょう</b>。 → ✅ {f|行|い}くのは<b>やめましょう</b>。
  There is no negative `ましょう`; negate the content instead.

## Practice

**1.** つかれましたね。ちょっと{f|休|やす}____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ちょっと{f|休|やす}<b>みましょう</b>。

<code>{f|休|やす}む</code> → ます-stem <code>{f|休|やす}み</code> → + ましょう. Both people are
already tired, so agreement is safe to assume and the assertive form is natural
here.

</details>

**2.** {f|初|はじ}めて{f|話|はな}す{f|人|ひと}に：「{f|今度|こんど}うちに（{f|来|き}ましょう・{f|来|き}ませんか）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|今度|こんど}うちに<b>{f|来|き}ませんか</b>。

You are inviting someone you have only just met, so they must be free to decline.
`ましょう` would announce the visit as settled, which is presumptuous with a
stranger.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It's cold, so let's go into the café."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|寒|さむ}いですから、{f|喫茶店|きっさてん}に<b>{f|入|はい}りましょう</b>。

Here the assumption of agreement is entirely safe - you are both cold - which is
exactly the situation `ましょう` is built for.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|日曜日|にちようび}、テニスをするましょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|日曜日|にちようび}、テニスを<b>しましょうか</b>。

`ましょう` builds on the ます-stem, and the ます-form of する is します, so the stem
is し. ❌するましょう doubles up the verb.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|会議|かいぎ}を{f|始|はじ}め<b>ましょう</b>。
(b) {f|会議|かいぎ}を{f|始|はじ}め<b>ましょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) starts the meeting. The speaker has the floor, everyone is present, and the
sentence is really an announcement dressed as a proposal - the chair's line.

(b) checks first. It reads as "shall we make a start?", which suits someone who is
not chairing, or a chair who can see that two people are still on the phone. The
`か` costs one syllable and buys a lot of room.

</details>
