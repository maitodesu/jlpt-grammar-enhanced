# が - but

**Meaning** · joins two clauses - "…, but …"

This `が` is a **conjunction**, not a particle: it follows a whole finished clause
and links it to the next one. Its core job is contrast, but a great deal of the time
it is doing something softer - simply setting the scene for what you are about to
say.

## Formation

`が` attaches to a complete clause. Whatever politeness that clause ends in stays
put, and `が` goes on after it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite clause | ～ます／～です + が | {f|高|たか}いです → {f|高|たか}いですが |
| Plain clause (verb) | dictionary / ～た / ～ない + が | {f|行|い}った → {f|行|い}ったが |
| Plain clause (い-adj) | ～い + が | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いが |
| Noun / な-adjective | + だ／です first | {f|学生|がくせい} → {f|学生|がくせい}だが／{f|学生|がくせい}ですが |
| Sentence-final | clause + が… (trailing off) | {f|失礼|しつれい}ですが… |
| Casual equivalent | replace が with けど | {f|高|たか}いけど |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun row is the one people get wrong. `が` cannot sit directly on a
noun the way the subject particle can. ❌<code>{f|学生|がくせい}が、…</code> meaning "I'm a student,
but…" is not possible; the clause has to be finished with a copula first - 
<code>{f|学生|がくせい}ですが</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}いです<b>が</b>、あまりおいしくないです。
This place is cheap, but the food isn't great.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|外|そと}は{f|寒|さむ}いです<b>が</b>、{f|部屋|へや}の{f|中|なか}は{f|暖|あたた}かいです。
It's cold outside, but it's warm in the room.
</pre>

<pre>
すみません<b>が</b>、ちょっと{f|静|しず}かにしてください。
Sorry to bother you, but could you keep it down a little?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山田|やまだ}です<b>が</b>、{f|田中|たなか}さんはいらっしゃいますか。
This is Yamada - is Tanaka-san there?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|何度|なんど}も{f|電話|でんわ}しました<b>が</b>、だれも{f|出|で}ませんでした。
I called several times, but nobody picked up.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|行|い}きたい<b>が</b>、{f|今週|こんしゅう}は{f|時間|じかん}がない。
I'd like to go, but I've no time this week.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|映画|えいが}は{f|三時間|さんじかん}もあります<b>が</b>、{f|全然|ぜんぜん}{f|長|なが}く{f|感|かん}じませんでした。
That film is a full three hours long, but it didn't feel long at all.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}の{f|予約|よやく}をお{f|願|ねが}いしたいのです<b>が</b>…。
I'd like to make a booking for tomorrow, if I may…
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`が` is the **formal** member of a family. In descending order of stiffness:
`が` → `けれども` → `けれど` → `けども` → `けど`. All mean the same thing. `が` is at
home in writing, in speeches, in business email and in polite spoken Japanese
attached to `です` / `ます`. Attached to a plain form in casual conversation
(「{f|行|い}きたいが」) it sounds bookish or old-fashioned; a friend would say
「{f|行|い}きたいけど」.

Now the part that changes how you read Japanese. **`が` very often does not mean
"but".** A huge share of its uses are what you might call a preamble: the first
clause exists only to give the listener a place to stand, and there is no contrast
at all.

- 「{f|失礼|しつれい}ですが、{f|日本|にほん}の{f|方|かた}ですか。」 - "Excuse me, are you
  Japanese?" Nothing is being contrasted; `が` is politely clearing its throat.
- 「{f|田中|たなか}ですが、…」 - introducing yourself on the phone before the request.
- 「{f|質問|しつもん}があるんですが、…」 - "I have a question…"

Translating those with "but" produces nonsense. Read `が` as a soft "and so" or a
comma whenever the first clause is not actually being denied by the second.

Related to this is the **trailing** `が`, where the sentence simply stops:
「{f|予約|よやく}したいのですが…」. Leaving the request unsaid is a politeness
strategy - you set up the situation and let the listener supply the rest, which is
less imposing than stating your demand outright. This is extremely common in shops,
offices and phone calls, and a learner who finishes every sentence can sound blunt by
comparison.

One caution about mixing levels. Writing a plain clause before `が` and a polite one
after it (「{f|安|やす}いが、おいしいです」) is normal in written Japanese but odd in
speech. When speaking, keep both halves polite.

## Contrast

### が vs けど

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `が` for writing and polite
speech; `けど` for conversation.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}は{f|読|よ}めます<b>が</b>、{f|書|か}けません。
I can read this character, but I can't write it.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}、{f|読|よ}める<b>けど</b>、{f|書|か}けない。
I can read this one, but I can't write it.
</pre>

`けど` also attaches happily to polite forms - 「{f|読|よ}めますけど」 - which gives
you a middle register: polite, but relaxed. That combination is very common in spoken
Japanese at work. Going the other way, plain form + `が` in speech is the one
combination to avoid unless you are deliberately sounding formal.

### が vs でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` joins two clauses **inside one sentence**. `でも` starts a
**new** sentence.

</div>

<pre>
{f|昨日|きのう}は{f|雨|あめ}でした<b>が</b>、{f|試合|しあい}はありました。
It rained yesterday, but the match went ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昨日|きのう}は{f|雨|あめ}でした。<b>でも</b>、{f|試合|しあい}はありました。
It rained yesterday. But the match went ahead.
</pre>

The meaning is the same; the packaging differs. `でも` puts a full stop in first,
which makes the contrast land harder - it is the spoken equivalent of starting a new
paragraph. ❌<code>{f|雨|あめ}でしたでも</code> is impossible: `でも` cannot glue two clauses
together the way `が` does. In formal writing, `しかし` plays the same sentence-initial
role as `でも`.

### が (but) vs が (subject marker)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what comes **before** the `が`. A bare noun means it is
the subject marker; a finished clause means it is the conjunction.

</div>

The two are unrelated words that happen to share a syllable, and the test is
mechanical.

<pre>
{f|時間|じかん}<b>が</b>ありません。
I don't have time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|時間|じかん}はあります<b>が</b>、お{f|金|かね}がありません。
I have the time, but not the money.
</pre>

In the first, <code>{f|時間|じかん}</code> is just a noun, so `が` is marking it as the subject of
<code>ありません</code>. In the second, <code>{f|時間|じかん}はあります</code> is a whole sentence already, so the
`が` after it can only be the conjunction. Sentences containing both are ordinary and
cause no confusion once you have the test:
「{f|雨|あめ}<b>が</b>{f|降|ふ}っていました<b>が</b>、{f|傘|かさ}はありませんでした。」

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}いですでも、おいしくないです。 → ✅ {f|安|やす}いです**が**、…／{f|安|やす}いです。**でも**、…
  `でも` cannot join clauses. Either use `が` inside one sentence, or break the
  sentence and start the next one with `でも`.

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}が、お{f|金|かね}がありません。 → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}です**が**、お{f|金|かね}がありません。
  The conjunction needs a finished clause in front of it, so a noun must first take
  the copula.

- ❌ Reading 「{f|失礼|しつれい}ですが」 as "it's rude, but". → ✅ "Excuse me, but…"
  Preamble `が` is not adversative. If the second clause does not deny the first,
  do not translate it as "but".

- ❌ {f|友|とも}だちに「{f|安|やす}いが、おいしくないよ」と{f|言|い}いました。 → ✅ 「{f|安|やす}いけど、おいしくないよ」
  Plain form + `が` in casual speech sounds stiff and literary. Between friends it
  is `けど`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|日本語|にほんご}は{f|難|むずか}しいです____、{f|面白|おもしろ}いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|難|むずか}しいです<b>が</b>、{f|面白|おもしろ}いです。

The two clauses genuinely pull against each other, and both are polite, so the polite
conjunction `が` joins them inside a single sentence. In conversation `けど` would do
the same job more casually.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|忙|いそが}しかったです（が・でも）、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|暇|ひま}です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|忙|いそが}しかったです<b>が</b>、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|暇|ひま}です。

`でも` only works at the start of a new sentence. Since the two halves are being kept
in one sentence with a comma, the connector has to be `が`.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "Excuse me, but is this seat taken?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

すみません<b>が</b>、この{f|席|せき}は{f|空|あ}いていますか。

This is preamble `が`, not contrast - the first clause is only softening the
approach. English uses "but" here too, which is a rare case of the two languages
lining up.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|広|ひろ}いですでも、{f|暗|くら}いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|広|ひろ}いです<b>が</b>、{f|暗|くら}いです。

`でも` cannot be attached to the end of a clause. Replace it with `が`, or split the
sentence: 「{f|広|ひろ}いです。でも、{f|暗|くら}いです。」

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|田中|たなか}ですが、{f|山田|やまだ}さんをお{f|願|ねが}いします。
(b) {f|田中|たなか}です。{f|山田|やまだ}さんをお{f|願|ねが}いします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Neither sentence contains any contrast, which is the point of the pair.

(a) uses the preamble `が` to hand the two pieces over as one smooth move: the name
exists only to prepare the request, and the sentence flows into it. This is the
standard shape of a phone call.

(b) states the name and then makes the request as a separate, self-contained
sentence. It is not wrong, but it is more abrupt - two facts placed side by side
rather than one leading into the other.

</details>
