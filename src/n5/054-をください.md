# ～をください

**Meaning** · please give me ~ - asking for an object

`～をください` names a **thing** and asks for it. It is the sentence you use in a
shop, at a ticket window, at a counter, in a restaurant - anywhere the other person
is there to hand you something. `ください` is the imperative of the honorific verb
<code>{f|下|くだ}さる</code> "to give (to me)", which is why a form that is literally a command
still counts as polite.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + を + ください | コーヒー → コーヒーをください |
| Noun + quantity | Noun + を + counter + ください | りんご → りんごを{f|三|みっ}つください |
| Quantity (spoken, `を` dropped) | Noun + counter + ください | コーヒー{f|二|ふた}つください |
| Verb (an action, not a thing) | て-form + ください | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}ってください |
| Politer | ～をお{f|願|ねが}いします | コーヒーをお{f|願|ねが}いします |
| Politer still | ～をいただけますか | メニューをいただけますか |
| Casual | ～ちょうだい | コーヒーちょうだい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the thing takes `を`, never `が`. `ください` is a verb of giving,
so what is given is its direct object - the opposite of `ほしい`, which is an
adjective and takes `が`.

</div>

## Nuance & register

`～をください` is the customer's sentence. Between a customer and staff the balance
of the situation makes it perfectly polite, and it is what Japanese speakers
actually say at a counter - no hedging required.

Turn it around and the picture changes. Because `ください` is an imperative, asking a
**superior** for something with it sounds like an instruction:
{f|部長|ぶちょう}、ペンをください is the sort of thing that gets noticed. Upward you
switch to a question - ペンをお{f|借|か}りできますか, {f|資料|しりょう}をいただけますか
 - or to お{f|願|ねが}いします.

Quantities behave loosely in speech. コーヒーを{f|二|ふた}つください and
コーヒー{f|二|ふた}つください are both normal, and the second is more common at a
counter; what you should not do is mark the number with `を` as well
(❌コーヒーを{f|二|ふた}つをください). Politeness particles cluster at the front and
back instead: すみません、～ください and ～ください<b>ね</b> are both very common.

`ちょうだい` is the casual replacement - それちょうだい - and reads as childish or
warmly familiar depending on the speaker; it is more common from women and from
adults talking to children. In writing, ください survives in fixed instructions
(ご{f|記入|きにゅう}ください, {f|少々|しょうしょう}お{f|待|ま}ちください), which are honorific
forms rather than this pattern.

`ください` is not limited to physical objects. Abstract nouns work fine, and this
is where the pattern turns up outside shops:
{f|時間|じかん}をください, {f|返事|へんじ}をください, もう{f|一度|いちど}チャンスをください.

## Contrast

### ～をください vs ～をお{f|願|ねが}いします

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ください` asks for a **thing you can hand over**;
お{f|願|ねが}いします asks for that *or* for a service, and sounds a touch more
deferential.

</div>

<pre>
{f|生|なま}ビールを<b>ください</b>。
A draught beer, please.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|生|なま}ビールを<b>お{f|願|ねが}いします</b>。
A draught beer, please. (slightly more polite)
</pre>

Where they come apart is scope. お{f|願|ねが}いします covers requests that have no
object at all - {f|会計|かいけい}をお{f|願|ねが}いします "the bill, please",
{f|山田|やまだ}さんをお{f|願|ねが}いします "could I speak to Yamada" - where ください
would be wrong or comic. As a rule, if you are asking someone to *do* something on
your behalf, お{f|願|ねが}いします; if you are asking them to *give* you something,
either works.

### ～をください vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - noun + `を` + ください for an **object**; verb て-form +
ください for an **action**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|地図|ちず}を<b>ください</b>。
Could I have a map.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|地図|ちず}を<b>{f|書|か}いてください</b>。
Please draw me a map.
</pre>

They share the word `ください` and nothing else. English blurs them - "give me a
hand" looks like a noun request but is really an action - so the trap is real:
✅{f|手伝|てつだ}ってください, not ❌{f|手伝|てつだ}いをください.

When you want an object *brought* to you rather than simply handed over, the two
combine: {f|水|みず}を{f|持|も}ってきてください.

### ～をください vs ～がほしいです

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` describes your inner state; `ください` performs the
request. To a shop assistant, only the second one is a sentence that does anything.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|靴|くつ}の{f|黒|くろ}を<b>ください</b>。
I'll take these shoes in black.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新|あたら}しい{f|靴|くつ}が<b>ほしい</b>です。
I want new shoes.
</pre>

The second is a fine thing to say to a friend and an odd thing to say to a
salesperson - you have announced a feeling and left them to guess what to do about
it. Note the particles as well: `ほしい` is an adjective and takes `が`, while
`ください` is a verb and takes `を`. Mixing them up (❌これがください) is the single
most common error with this pattern.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ これ<b>が</b>ください。 → ✅ これ<b>を</b>ください。
  `ください` is a verb of giving; the thing given is its object.

- ❌ （レストランで）お{f|水|みず}が<b>ほしいです</b>。 → ✅ お{f|水|みず}を<b>ください</b>。
  Naming your desire at a server does not place an order.

- ❌ {f|手伝|てつだ}いを<b>ください</b>。 → ✅ <b>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってください</b>。
  Help is an action, not an object. Use the て-form.

- ❌ コーヒーを{f|二|ふた}つ<b>を</b>ください。 → ✅ コーヒーを{f|二|ふた}つ<b>ください</b>。
  One `を` per object. The counter does not take a particle of its own.

- ❌ {f|課長|かちょう}、はんこを<b>ください</b>。 → ✅ {f|課長|かちょう}、はんこを<b>いただけますか</b>。
  `ください` is an imperative. Pointed upward it reads as an instruction.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この{f|本|ほん}____ください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|本|ほん}<b>を</b>ください。

`ください` comes from the verb <code>{f|下|くだ}さる</code>, so the thing you are asking for is
its direct object and takes `を`. The `が` that goes with `ほしい` does not transfer.

</details>

**2.** {f|店|みせ}で：「そのかばんを（ください・ほしいです）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そのかばんを<b>ください</b>。

In a shop you are placing a request, and `ください` is what performs it.
<code>ほしいです</code> only reports how you feel, which leaves the assistant to work out
what you actually want.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Two coffees and one orange juice, please."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

コーヒーを{f|二|ふた}つと、オレンジジュースを{f|一|ひと}つ<b>ください</b>。

Counters go between the noun and `ください`, and only one `を` per item. In speech
the `を` is often dropped altogether: コーヒー{f|二|ふた}つとオレンジジュース{f|一|ひと}つください.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: すみません、ちょっと{f|手伝|てつだ}いをください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ すみません、ちょっと<b>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってください</b>。

What you want is an action, not an object, so it belongs in the て-form pattern.
English "give me a hand" hides a verb inside a noun, and that is where the mistake
comes from.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) タクシーを<b>ください</b>。
(b) タクシーを<b>お{f|願|ねが}いします</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) asks for a taxi as though it were an object to be handed across a counter, and
it sounds odd for that reason - nobody gives you a taxi.

(b) is what a hotel guest actually says. お{f|願|ねが}いします asks the listener to
*arrange* something on your behalf, which covers services, phone calls and errands.
That wider scope is the main practical reason to learn it alongside `ください`.

</details>
