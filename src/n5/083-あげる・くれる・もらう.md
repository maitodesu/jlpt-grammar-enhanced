# あげる・くれる・もらう

**Meaning** · give (away) / give (to me) / receive

English has one verb "give" and lets context sort out the direction. Japanese
does not: it has **two** verbs for giving, and which one you use depends on
whether the thing is moving toward you or away from you. Choosing wrongly is not
a stylistic slip - it makes the sentence unintelligible.

## Formation

Each verb decides who the subject is and which way the object travels. All three
are regular る-verbs (あげます, くれます - but note くださる → くださいます).

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| あげる（away from me） | Aが Bに Nを あげる | {f|私|わたし}は{f|妹|いもうと}に{f|本|ほん}をあげる |
| くれる（toward me） | Aが（{f|私|わたし}に）Nを くれる | {f|友|とも}だちが{f|私|わたし}に{f|本|ほん}をくれる |
| もらう（I receive） | Bが Aに／から Nを もらう | {f|私|わたし}は{f|友|とも}だちに{f|本|ほん}をもらう |
| Polite | あげます／くれます／もらいます | {f|本|ほん}をもらいました |
| I give upward（humble） | さしあげる | {f|先生|せんせい}にさしあげる |
| A superior gives to me（honorific） | くださる → くださいます | {f|先生|せんせい}がくださる |
| I receive from above（humble） | いただく | {f|先生|せんせい}にいただく |
| Giving down the scale | やる | {f|花|はな}に{f|水|みず}をやる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ask where the thing ends up. If it ends up with **me or my
side**, the giving verb is `くれる`. If it ends up with **anyone else**, it is
`あげる`. `もらう` describes the same event from the receiver's chair.

</div>

The particles are worth memorising as a set: the **giver** is `が` (or `は`) with
あげる and くれる, and `に`／`から` with もらう. The **thing** is always `を`.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|妹|いもうと}にプレゼントを<b>あげました</b>。

</pre>

<pre>
{f|友|とも}だちが{f|私|わたし}にチョコレートを<b>くれました</b>。
A friend gave me some chocolate.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}に{f|時計|とけい}を<b>もらいました</b>。
I got a watch from my father.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|田中|たなか}さんに{f|花|はな}を<b>あげました</b>。
Yamada gave Tanaka some flowers.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|毎朝|まいあさ}、{f|犬|いぬ}にごはんを<b>やって</b>から{f|会社|かいしゃ}へ{f|行|い}きます。

</pre>

<pre>
となりの{f|人|ひと}が{f|子|こ}どもたちにおかしを<b>くれました</b>。

</pre>

When I left the company, the people in my department gave me a bouquet.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The in-group is what really matters.** `くれる` is not only for gifts to *me*. It is for gifts to my **side** - what
Japanese calls うち, as opposed to そと, everyone else. My family, and depending on
the conversation my close friends, my colleagues, my company: if the thing lands
with any of them, `くれる` is the verb.

That is why 「{f|先生|せんせい}が{f|弟|おとうと}に{f|本|ほん}をくださいました」 uses
a giving-toward-me verb even though the book went to my brother. My brother is
inside my circle, so the book came toward my side of the line. Say
❌{f|先生|せんせい}が{f|弟|おとうと}に{f|本|ほん}をあげました and you have pushed
your own brother out of your family.

The line moves with the situation. Talking to a customer, everyone at my company
is うち; talking to a colleague, only my own team might be.

**The politeness axis.** Each verb has a version for when a status gap is in play:

- <code>さしあげる</code> - humble, for when *I* give to someone above me.
- <code>くださる</code> - honorific, for when someone above me gives to me or mine.
  The ます-form is irregular: <b>くださいます</b>, not ❌くださります. `ください`, the
  word you already know, is its imperative.
- <code>いただく</code> - humble, for when *I* receive from someone above me.

Of these, `くださる` and `いただく` are safe and extremely common. `さしあげる`
needs care: announcing that you have bestowed something on your superior can sound
self-important, so people often avoid it and say
{f|先生|せんせい}にお{f|渡|わた}ししました or simply
{f|先生|せんせい}に{f|渡|わた}しました instead.

<b>やる</b>**, and how rough it sounds.** `やる` is the downward version of `あげる`, used for plants, animals and - in
older or rougher speech - for children and younger siblings. It is standard for
{f|花|はな}に{f|水|みず}をやる and {f|犬|いぬ}にえさをやる. Used of people it now
sounds blunt and is male-marked; younger speakers say
{f|犬|いぬ}にごはんをあげる without a second thought, and some older speakers find
that overly precious. Either is acceptable; know that both exist.

**Two smaller points.** `もらう` takes `に` or `から` for the source. `から` is preferred when the giver is
an organisation rather than a person:
{f|会社|かいしゃ}から{f|手紙|てがみ}をもらいました.

The plain command `くれ`（{f|水|みず}をくれ）is very rough - men's speech,
brusque. Use ください.

Finally, these three verbs also work as helpers on the て-form - 
{f|買|か}ってあげる, {f|教|おし}えてくれる, {f|作|つく}ってもらう - where the same
direction logic applies to *actions* rather than objects. That construction is
taught at N4, and it is unusable until the direction rule on this page is solid.

## Contrast

### あげる vs くれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "give", and both take the giver as subject. `あげる`
sends the thing away from the speaker; `くれる` brings it toward the speaker's
side.

</div>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}は{f|田中|たなか}さんに{f|傘|かさ}を<b>あげました</b>。
I gave Tanaka an umbrella.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんが{f|私|わたし}に{f|傘|かさ}を<b>くれました</b>。
Tanaka gave me an umbrella.
</pre>

The two sentences describe opposite events, and the only substantive difference is
the verb. Note that `くれる` may **never** have the speaker as giver:
❌{f|私|わたし}が{f|田中|たなか}さんにくれました is impossible in any context.

In `くれる` sentences the {f|私|わたし}に is usually dropped, because the verb has
already said it: {f|田中|たなか}さんが{f|傘|かさ}をくれました is the natural form.

### くれる vs もらう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one event, two camera angles. `くれる` puts the giver in the
subject slot; `もらう` puts the receiver there.

</div>

<pre>
{f|母|はは}が{f|私|わたし}にセーターを<b>くれました</b>。
My mother gave me a jumper.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}は{f|母|はは}にセーターを<b>もらいました</b>。
I got a jumper from my mother.
</pre>

Both are correct and both are common. `くれる` feels slightly warmer - it presents
the other person as acting kindly toward you - while `もらう` is more neutral and
focuses on the fact that you now have the thing. Note that the particle on
{f|母|はは} flips: `が` when she is the subject, `に` when she is the source.

### あげる・くれる・もらう vs さしあげる・くださる・いただく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the plain three are for equals and for people below you. The
polite three encode a status gap, and each one specifies which direction the gap
runs in.

</div>

<pre>
{f|課長|かちょう}が{f|資料|しりょう}を<b>くださいました</b>。
The section chief gave me the documents.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|課長|かちょう}に{f|資料|しりょう}を<b>いただきました</b>。
I received the documents from the section chief.
</pre>

Both are polite and both are safe. What you must not do is use the plain verbs of
a superior's giving in a formal setting - 
{f|課長|かちょう}が{f|資料|しりょう}をくれました is grammatical but sounds
disrespectful in front of the section chief, though it is perfectly normal when
grumbling to a friend.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|友|とも}だちが{f|私|わたし}にプレゼントを<b>あげました</b>。 → ✅ {f|友|とも}だちが{f|私|わたし}にプレゼントを<b>くれました</b>。
  When the thing arrives at the speaker, the verb must be `くれる`. This is the
  single most frequent error learners make, because English uses "gave" for both
  directions.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}が{f|山田|やまだ}さんに{f|本|ほん}を<b>くれました</b>。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}が{f|山田|やまだ}さんに{f|本|ほん}を<b>あげました</b>。
  `くれる` can never have the speaker as the giver. Nothing can move toward you
  when you are the one letting go of it.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|兄|あに}<b>が</b>お{f|金|かね}をもらいました。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}は{f|兄|あに}<b>に</b>お{f|金|かね}をもらいました。
  With `もらう` the source takes `に` or `から`. `が` there would make your brother
  the one doing the receiving.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}が{f|花|はな}を<b>くださります</b>。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}が{f|花|はな}を<b>くださいます</b>。
  <code>くださる</code> is one of a small group of honorific verbs with an irregular
  ます-form - like おっしゃる → おっしゃいます.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}に{f|辞書|じしょ}を<b>あげました</b>。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}に{f|辞書|じしょ}を<b>さしあげました</b>。／{f|先生|せんせい}に{f|辞書|じしょ}をお{f|渡|わた}ししました。
  Using plain `あげる` about a gift to a teacher sounds as though you were doing
  them a favour from above. Even the humble form is often sidestepped in favour of
  a neutral verb.

## Practice

**1.** {f|山田|やまだ}さんが{f|私|わたし}に{f|地図|ちず}を____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|山田|やまだ}さんが{f|私|わたし}に{f|地図|ちず}を<b>くれました</b>。

The map ends up with the speaker, so the verb has to be `くれる`. In real speech
the {f|私|わたし}に would usually be left out, since `くれる` already tells you
where the map went.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|部長|ぶちょう}にお{f|土産|みやげ}を（もらいました・いただきました）。 You are speaking at work, in front of colleagues.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|部長|ぶちょう}にお{f|土産|みやげ}を<b>いただきました</b>。

<code>いただく</code> is the humble verb for receiving from someone above you, and a
department head qualifies. `もらいました` is not wrong grammatically, but in a
workplace setting it sounds too flat about a superior's kindness.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My grandmother gave my son a book."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|祖母|そぼ}が{f|息子|むすこ}に{f|本|ほん}を<b>くれました</b>。

Your son is inside your family, so the book has travelled toward your side and
`くれる` is required - even though the speaker is not the recipient. Using
あげました here would sound as though the boy were somebody else's child.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|私|わたし}は{f|友|とも}だちにおかしをくれました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|私|わたし}は{f|友|とも}だちにおかしを<b>あげました</b>。

The sweets are leaving the speaker, so the verb must be `あげる`. `くれる` with
{f|私|わたし} as the giver is not merely unnatural - it is impossible, because the
verb's whole job is to mark movement toward the speaker.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|父|ちち}が{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}をくれました。
(b) {f|父|ちち}に{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}をもらいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both report the same bicycle changing hands, and both are perfectly natural.

(a) makes the father the subject and presents the act as something he did for the
speaker; there is a faint warmth of gratitude in it. (b) makes the speaker the
subject and simply records the acquisition - closer to "I got a bike from my
dad". The choice is about where you point the camera, not about the facts.

</details>
