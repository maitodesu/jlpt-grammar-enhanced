# ～てください

**Meaning** · please do ~ - a request, and often an instruction

`～てください` attaches to the て-form and asks the listener to perform an action.
It is polite in *form* - `ください` is the imperative of the honorific verb
<code>{f|下|くだ}さる</code> - but it is not a soft or deferential way to ask. Teachers, doctors,
station staff and forms all use it, and that is the flavour it carries: a clear
directive, delivered courteously.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | て-form + ください | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}ってください |
| Verb (る-verb) | て-form + ください | {f|見|み}る → {f|見|み}てください |
| Verb (する) | して + ください | {f|電話|でんわ}する → {f|電話|でんわ}してください |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | {f|来|き}て + ください | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}てください |
| Negative request | ない-form + で + ください | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ないでください |
| Casual | て-form on its own | {f|待|ま}って／{f|待|ま}ってね |
| More polite | ～てくださいませんか／～ていただけませんか | {f|待|ま}っていただけませんか |

To ask for a **thing** rather than an action, `ください` follows a noun with `を`
instead - that pattern has its own page in this book.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ください` attaches to the て-form, never the dictionary form.
Get the て-form right and the rest is automatic:
{f|話|はな}す → {f|話|はな}して, {f|急|いそ}ぐ → {f|急|いそ}いで, {f|読|よ}む → {f|読|よ}んで,
{f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}って.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ちょっと<b>{f|待|ま}ってください</b>。

</pre>

<pre>

Please write your name here.
</pre>

<pre>
もう{f|一度|いちど}<b>{f|言|い}ってください</b>。
Could you say that again, please.
</pre>

<pre>
どうぞ<b>{f|座|すわ}ってください</b>。
Please, have a seat.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|駅|えき}に{f|着|つ}いたら<b>{f|電話|でんわ}してください</b>。
Give me a call when you get to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|寒|さむ}いので、ドアを<b>{f|閉|し}めてください</b>。
It's cold, so please shut the door.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|会議|かいぎ}の{f|資料|しりょう}を、{f|金曜日|きんようび}までにメールで<b>{f|送|おく}ってください</b>。
Please email me the material for next week's meeting by Friday.
</pre>

I'm terribly sorry, but could you possibly come in half an hour early tomorrow?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The single most useful thing to know about `～てください` is that **it is not the
polite form of a request**. It is the *neutral* one. Because it tells rather than
asks, it belongs to situations where the speaker has a right to direct: a teacher
to a class, a doctor to a patient, an announcement to passengers, a form to whoever
is filling it in. Used sideways or upward it can land as bossy - 
{f|部長|ぶちょう}、これをコピーしてください is grammatically impeccable and would
raise eyebrows.

The ladder, roughly from most casual to most deferential:

- <b>{f|待|ま}って</b> - friends, family. て-form alone.
- <b>{f|待|ま}ってくれる？／{f|待|ま}ってくれない？</b> - casual, but genuinely asking.
- <b>{f|待|ま}ってください</b> - the neutral polite directive on this page.
- <b>{f|待|ま}ってくださいませんか</b> - a real question, so noticeably softer.
- <b>{f|待|ま}っていただけませんか／{f|待|ま}っていただけますか</b> - humble; the workplace default upward.
- <b>お{f|待|ま}ちください</b> - honorific, used *to* customers by staff.

`くださる` and `いただく` describe the same event from opposite ends: `くださる` is
"you kindly give", `いただく` is "I humbly receive". Both are polite; the `いただく`
versions are the ones you reach for when you want to sound careful.

Small softeners matter more than learners expect. Front the request with
すみませんが or {f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが, add ちょっと, and finish with ね
({f|気|き}をつけてくださいね) and the same sentence stops sounding like an order.

Note one friendly use that runs against all of this: with どうぞ, `～てください` is an
*offer*, not a command. {f|食|た}べてください at a dinner table means "please, help
yourself".

In casual speech the bare て-form does all this work - {f|見|み}て！, {f|貸|か}して - 
and ちょうだい ({f|貸|か}してちょうだい) is a softer, childish or motherly variant.

## Contrast

### ～てください vs ～ていただけませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` *tells*; `～ていただけませんか` *asks*. Upward, and
for anything that is a favour rather than a duty, ask.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}の{f|読|よ}み{f|方|かた}を<b>{f|教|おし}えてください</b>。
Tell me how to read this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}の{f|読|よ}み{f|方|かた}を<b>{f|教|おし}えていただけませんか</b>。
Would you mind telling me how to read this kanji?
</pre>

The second is longer for a reason. It is a negative question, which leaves the
listener room to say no, and it uses the humble `いただく`, which frames you as the
one receiving a kindness. The pair
`～てくださいませんか` works the same way with `くださる` and is a shade less humble.

A useful test: if a "no" would be perfectly reasonable, use the question form. If
you are describing a step in a procedure - {f|靴|くつ}をぬいでください at the door - 
`～てください` is exactly right and the long version would sound strange.

### ～てください vs the plain て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - drop `ください` and you have the casual request. Drop it with
the wrong person and you have an order.

</div>

<pre>
ちょっと<b>{f|手伝|てつだ}って</b>。
Give me a hand a sec.
</pre>

<pre>
ちょっと<b>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってください</b>。
Could you give me a hand, please.
</pre>

The bare て-form is everywhere in speech between friends, within a family, and from
older to younger. It is not rude in itself - it is simply unmarked for politeness,
which means the relationship has to carry it. Adding ね or よ softens it
({f|急|いそ}いでね); adding よ alone can sharpen it.

`～てくれる？` and `～てくれない？` sit slightly above the bare form: still casual, but
phrased as a question, so they ask rather than tell.

### ～てください vs ～をください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ください` after a **て-form** asks for an action; `ください`
after a **noun + を** asks for a thing.

</div>

<pre>
{f|水|みず}を<b>ください</b>。
Some water, please.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|水|みず}を<b>{f|持|も}ってきてください</b>。
Please bring me some water.
</pre>

Both are natural; they differ in what you are asking for. The first hands over an
object, the second requests the act of fetching. The mistake to avoid is trying to
force a noun into the verb slot - ❌{f|手伝|てつだ}いをしてください is possible but
stilted, and ✅{f|手伝|てつだ}ってください is what a speaker actually says.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|食|た}べる<b>てください</b> → ✅ {f|食|た}<b>べてください</b>
  `ください` attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ {f|待|ま}つ<b>てください</b> → ✅ {f|待|ま}っ<b>てください</b>
  `つ`-verbs make their て-form with a small `っ`: {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}って.

- ❌ {f|行|い}かない<b>てください</b> → ✅ {f|行|い}かない<b>でください</b>
  The negative request uses `ないで`, never `ないて`.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、これを<b>コピーしてください</b>。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、これを<b>コピーしていただけませんか</b>。
  Grammatical, but you are giving an instruction to someone senior. Ask instead.

- ❌ ペンを<b>してください</b>。 → ✅ ペンを<b>ください</b>。
  Asking for an object needs no verb at all.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、この{f|字|じ}が{f|読|よ}めません。ゆっくり{f|話|はな}____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ゆっくり{f|話|はな}<b>してください</b>。

<code>{f|話|はな}す</code> is a `す`-verb, so its て-form is <code>{f|話|はな}して</code>, and `ください`
attaches directly to that. Adding すみませんが at the front, as the question does,
already softens the request.

</details>

**2.** {f|社長|しゃちょう}に：「この{f|書類|しょるい}を（{f|見|み}てください・{f|見|み}ていただけませんか）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|書類|しょるい}を<b>{f|見|み}ていただけませんか</b>。

Looking over your document is a favour, not part of the president's duties, and he
outranks you. The humble question form leaves him room to decline, which is exactly
what politeness upward is made of.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please turn off the light when you leave the room."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|部屋|へや}を{f|出|で}るとき、{f|電気|でんき}を<b>{f|消|け}してください</b>。

<code>{f|消|け}す</code> → <code>{f|消|け}して</code> + ください. This is the instructional use of the
pattern - a house rule, not a personal favour - so plain `～てください` is right.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あぶないですから、そこに{f|立|た}つてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あぶないですから、そこに<b>{f|立|た}たないでください</b>。

Two things are wrong. The て-form of <code>{f|立|た}つ</code> is <code>{f|立|た}って</code>, not
❌<code>{f|立|た}つて</code>; and the sense demands a negative - "it's dangerous, so please
*don't* stand there" - which is built with `ないで`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|開|あ}けてください</b>。
(b) {f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|開|あ}けてくださいませんか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain polite directive. In a classroom, or between people who know each
other well, it is entirely normal - but it assumes compliance and does not invite a
refusal.

(b) is phrased as a negative question, so it genuinely asks. It is longer, more
deferential, and the natural choice when you are imposing on someone, addressing a
stranger, or speaking to someone senior to you.

</details>
