# で - means, instrument, cause

**Meaning** · with, by, in, out of - the tool, vehicle, language, material or cause that
makes the action possible

`で` names **what you used**. A knife, a train, English, wood, a hundred yen, ten
minutes, a typhoon - anything the action was carried out *by means of*, or *because of*.
It is the same particle as the one that marks the place of an action, and it works the
same way: it supplies the circumstance the verb depends on.

## Formation

`で` attaches directly to a noun. Nothing changes shape, and it never follows a verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - tool or instrument | Tool + で + verb | はし → はしで{f|食|た}べる |
| Noun - vehicle | Vehicle + で + motion verb | {f|電車|でんしゃ} → {f|電車|でんしゃ}で{f|行|い}く |
| Noun - language or medium | Language + で + verb | {f|英語|えいご} → {f|英語|えいご}で{f|話|はな}す |
| Noun - material you can still see | Material + で + {f|作|つく}る | {f|木|き} → {f|木|き}で{f|作|つく}る |
| Noun - cause or reason | Cause + で + verb | {f|風邪|かぜ} → {f|風邪|かぜ}で{f|休|やす}む |
| Noun - amount, price or elapsed time | Quantity + で | {f|千円|せんえん} → {f|千円|せんえん}で{f|買|か}える |
| Number of people | Number + で | {f|一人|ひとり} → {f|一人|ひとり}で{f|行|い}く |

<div class="note">

**Note** - "on foot" breaks the pattern. {f|歩|ある}く is a verb, not a tool, so you say
{f|歩|ある}いて{f|行|い}きます, never ❌<code>{f|歩|ある}いてで</code> and never
❌<code>{f|足|あし}で{f|行|い}きます</code>. Learn <code>{f|歩|ある}いて</code> as the one member
of the transport list that is not a noun + で.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
はし<b>で</b>{f|食|た}べます。
I eat with chopsticks.
</pre>

<pre>
バス<b>で</b>{f|会社|かいしゃ}へ{f|行|い}きます。
I go to work by bus.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}は{f|鉛筆|えんぴつ}<b>で</b>{f|書|か}いてください。
Please write this kanji in pencil.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|英語|えいご}<b>で</b>{f|説明|せつめい}してもらえますか。
Could you explain it in English?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|風邪|かぜ}<b>で</b>{f|学校|がっこう}を{f|休|やす}みました。
I stayed off school with a cold.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|椅子|いす}は{f|竹|たけ}<b>で</b>できています。
This chair is made of bamboo.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|大雪|おおゆき}<b>で</b>{f|新幹線|しんかんせん}が{f|止|と}まってしまいました。
The bullet trains have stopped because of the heavy snow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}の{f|資料|しりょう}はメール<b>で</b>{f|送|おく}りますので、{f|後|あと}で{f|確認|かくにん}してください。
I'll send the meeting materials by email, so please check them afterwards.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`で` is register-neutral and is not dropped in casual speech - unlike a destination
particle, it carries information nothing else supplies. はし{f|食|た}べる is not casual
Japanese, it is broken Japanese.

A few things worth knowing about how it behaves in real sentences:

- <b>{f|一人|ひとり}で, {f|二人|ふたり}で, みんなで.</b> This is the standard way to say "alone",
  "the two of us", "all together". It is very frequent and learners reach for `と`
  instead, which does not work with a number.
- **Cause `で` is neutral and slightly detached.** {f|事故|じこ}で{f|遅|おく}れました states
  the cause flatly and is perfect for an apology at work, because it does not argue.
  Compare the reason particle `から`, which sounds like you are making a case.
- **Amounts.** {f|三日|みっか}で{f|終|お}わります - "it'll be done in three days" - and
  {f|五百円|ごひゃくえん}で{f|買|か}いました - "I got it for five hundred yen." Here `で`
  marks the quantity the action was completed *within* or *for*.
- **`で` is often followed by contrastive `は`.** {f|電車|でんしゃ}<b>では</b>{f|行|い}けません
 - "you can't get there by train (whatever else you might manage)."

Because the tool `で` and the place `で` look identical, Japanese avoids stacking two of
them in one clause. If you need both, most speakers restructure the sentence rather than
write two `で` in a row.

## Contrast

### で vs に with vehicles

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|乗|の}る</code> takes `に`; every other verb takes `で`.

</div>

This is the highest-frequency `で` error at N5, because English uses "on" for both.

<pre>
{f|東京駅|とうきょうえき}で{f|新幹線|しんかんせん}<b>に</b>{f|乗|の}ります。
I'll board the shinkansen at Tokyo Station.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新幹線|しんかんせん}<b>で</b>{f|大阪|おおさか}まで{f|行|い}きます。
I'll go to Osaka by shinkansen.
</pre>

<code>{f|乗|の}る</code> is "get on", a movement that lands on a point, and points are `に`.
{f|行|い}く, {f|来|く}る, {f|帰|かえ}る, {f|通|かよ}う all describe travel *using* the vehicle,
so they take `で`. The opposite of <code>{f|乗|の}る</code>, <code>{f|降|お}りる</code>, takes `を`.

### で vs から for material

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can still see what it was made of, use `で`. If the material
was chemically transformed and has disappeared, use `から`.

</div>

<pre>
このかばんは{f|紙|かみ}<b>で</b>{f|作|つく}りました。
I made this bag out of paper.
</pre>

<pre>
ワインはぶどう<b>から</b>{f|作|つく}ります。
Wine is made from grapes.
</pre>

The bag is still visibly paper. The wine is not visibly grapes - the raw material went
in and something else came out. The same split gives {f|木|き}で{f|作|つく}った{f|机|つくえ}
(you can see the wood) but {f|米|こめ}から{f|作|つく}る{f|日本酒|にほんしゅ} (you cannot see the
rice). In practice `で` is far more common; `から` is reserved for genuine
transformations, and native speakers will not object to `で` in borderline cases.

### で as means vs で as place

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the noun decides. Somewhere you could stand is a place; something
you could pick up, speak, or blame is a means.

</div>

Japanese does not experience these as two words, and neither should you after a little
practice - the noun disambiguates before the verb arrives.

<pre>
{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}<b>で</b>{f|来|き}ました。
I came by bicycle.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}{f|置|お}き{f|場|ば}<b>で</b>{f|待|ま}っています。
I'm waiting at the bike park.
</pre>

The tricky residue is nouns that could be either. {f|電話|でんわ} is a means
({f|電話|でんわ}で{f|話|はな}す, "talk on the phone"); {f|会社|かいしゃ} is a place. When a
noun is genuinely ambiguous, Japanese relies on the verb: {f|新聞|しんぶん}で{f|知|し}る is
"find out from the newspaper" (means), because {f|知|し}る is not something you do *at* a
newspaper.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|電車|でんしゃ}<b>で</b>{f|乗|の}ります。 → ✅ {f|電車|でんしゃ}<b>に</b>{f|乗|の}ります。
  <code>{f|乗|の}る</code> is the one transport verb that takes `に`. Everything else in the
  travel vocabulary takes `で`.

- ❌ {f|歩|ある}いて<b>で</b>{f|帰|かえ}りました。 → ✅ {f|歩|ある}いて{f|帰|かえ}りました。
  `で` attaches to nouns only. The て-form already carries the "by doing" sense.

- ❌ この{f|本|ほん}は{f|英語|えいご}<b>を</b>{f|書|か}いてあります。 → ✅ この{f|本|ほん}は{f|英語|えいご}<b>で</b>{f|書|か}いてあります。
  A language used as a medium is a means. `を` would try to make English the thing that
  was written.

- ❌ {f|木|き}<b>から</b>{f|作|つく}った{f|机|つくえ} → ✅ {f|木|き}<b>で</b>{f|作|つく}った{f|机|つくえ}
  The wood is still visible in the desk, so it is `で`. Save `から` for materials that
  have been transformed out of sight.

- ❌ {f|友達|ともだち}と{f|三人|さんにん}<b>と</b>{f|行|い}きました。 → ✅ {f|友達|ともだち}と{f|三人|さんにん}<b>で</b>{f|行|い}きました。
  A number of people is a circumstance, not a companion. Counted groups take `で`.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|手紙|てがみ}はボールペン____{f|書|か}いてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|手紙|てがみ}はボールペン<b>で</b>{f|書|か}いてください。

A pen is the instrument the writing is done with, which is the core use of this
particle. `に` would make no sense - you are not writing *onto* the pen.

</details>

**2.** {f|東京駅|とうきょうえき}で{f|新幹線|しんかんせん}（で・に）{f|乗|の}ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|東京駅|とうきょうえき}で{f|新幹線|しんかんせん}<b>に</b>{f|乗|の}ります。

<code>{f|乗|の}る</code> means "get on", and the vehicle is the point you land on, so it
takes `に`. The first `で` in the sentence is the place the boarding happens - both
particles are doing their own job.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This bridge is made of stone."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|橋|はし}は{f|石|いし}<b>で</b>できています。

You can see the stone in the finished bridge, so the material takes `で`, not `から`.
できている is the standard way to say "is made of"; {f|作|つく}られています is possible but
sounds like a report.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|地震|じしん}に{f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|地震|じしん}<b>で</b>{f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まりました。
"The trains stopped because of the earthquake."

An external cause is marked `で`. `に` cannot mark a cause; it would be trying to make
the earthquake a destination or a recipient.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|一人|ひとり}<b>で</b>{f|旅行|りょこう}しました。
(b) {f|友達|ともだち}と{f|旅行|りょこう}しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the circumstance the trip was made under: alone. `で` here counts the party,
which is why ❌<code>{f|一人|ひとり}と</code> is impossible - there is no companion to name.

(b) names a companion, which is the job of `と`. The two particles are not competing for
one slot: you can say {f|友達|ともだち}と{f|二人|ふたり}で{f|旅行|りょこう}しました and use both
at once, "I travelled with a friend, just the two of us."

</details>
