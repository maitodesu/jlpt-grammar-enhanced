# する

**Meaning** · to do - and the machine that turns nouns into verbs

`する` is the busiest verb in Japanese. On its own it means "do", but its real
job is bigger than that: stick `する` on the back of almost any noun and you have
made a verb. {f|勉強|べんきょう} "study (n.)" → {f|勉強|べんきょう}する "to
study". A very large share of the verbs you will ever meet are built this way.

<div class="note">

**Note** - Japanese has exactly two irregular verbs: `する` and {f|来|く}る. All
other verbs are う-verbs ({f|五段|ごだん} / godan / Group 1) or る-verbs ({f|一段|いちだん} / ichidan /
Group 2). This page uses **う-verb** and **る-verb**. `する` is irregular because
its stem changes vowel from form to form - し, さ, せ - so it cannot be predicted
from the dictionary form.

</div>

## Formation

`する` attaches to nouns. What it attaches to determines whether `を` can appear
between them.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun ({f|漢語|かんご} compound) | Noun + する | {f|掃除|そうじ} → {f|掃除|そうじ}する |
| Noun (with the object marker) | Noun + を + する | {f|掃除|そうじ} → {f|掃除|そうじ}をする |
| Noun (loanword) | Word + する | コピー → コピーする |
| Noun (activity, no する-verb) | Noun + を + する | テニス → テニスをする |
| な-adjective / Noun | ～に + する | {f|静|しず}か → {f|静|しず}かにする |
| い-adjective | ～く + する | {f|短|みじか}い → {f|短|みじか}くする |

The verb itself conjugates like this. Nothing here follows the う-verb or る-verb
rules, which is what "irregular" means - these have to be memorised:

| Form | する | With a noun |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | する | {f|運動|うんどう}する |
| Polite | します | {f|運動|うんどう}します |
| Plain negative | しない | {f|運動|うんどう}しない |
| Polite negative | しません | {f|運動|うんどう}しません |
| Plain past | した | {f|運動|うんどう}した |
| Polite past | しました | {f|運動|うんどう}しました |
| Plain past negative | しなかった | {f|運動|うんどう}しなかった |
| て-form | して | {f|運動|うんどう}して |
| Volitional | しよう／しましょう | {f|運動|うんどう}しよう |
| Potential | できる | {f|運動|うんどう}できる |
| Conditional | したら／すれば | {f|運動|うんどう}したら |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the potential of `する` is **できる**, a completely separate
word. There is no ❌されます, no ❌しられます. And because `できる` is
intransitive, its object takes `が`:
{f|料理|りょうり}<b>が</b>できます, never ❌{f|料理|りょうり}をできます.

</div>

`する` also carries several meanings that have nothing to do with "doing":

- **Sensations**, with `が` - {f|音|おと}がする "there's a sound",
  においがする "there's a smell", {f|味|あじ}がする "it tastes of…".
- **Deciding**, with `に` - わたしはカレーにします "I'll have the curry."
- **Wearing** accessories - ネクタイをする, マスクをする, {f|時計|とけい}をする.
- **Costing** - この{f|傘|かさ}は5000{f|円|えん}しました "this umbrella cost
  5,000 yen."

</pre>

My son is studying economics at a university in Tokyo.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Plain `する` is register-neutral: `します` is polite, `する` is plain, and
nothing about the word itself is stiff or casual. What is worth knowing is that
`する` sits at the bottom of a three-storey politeness building, and the other
two storeys are irregular too:

- <b>{f|致|いた}す</b> - humble. `わたしがいたします` "I'll do it", offering your own
  action deferentially. Extremely common in service and business Japanese:
  {f|説明|せつめい}いたします, ご{f|案内|あんない}いたします.
- **なさる** - honorific, for someone else's action. Note the irregular polite
  form **なさいます** (not ❌なさります). ゴルフをなさいますか。

Because noun + する is so productive, it is the standard way Japanese absorbs
foreign words. Anything that arrives in katakana can become a verb overnight:
チェックする, キャンセルする, ダウンロードする, シェアする. If you know the
noun, you know the verb - this is the single fastest way to expand your active
vocabulary.

One register caution: the {f|漢語|かんご} compounds ({f|確認|かくにん}する,
{f|購入|こうにゅう}する) are a shade more formal than their native equivalents
({f|確|たし}かめる, {f|買|か}う). In an email they sound crisp and professional;
in casual chat with a friend they sound like you are reading from a manual.

`する` also underlies fixed expressions that never translate word for word:
{f|気|き}にする "worry about it", {f|大切|たいせつ}にする "treasure something",
{f|無理|むり}をする "overdo it". Meet these as vocabulary, not as grammar.

## Contrast

### {f|勉強|べんきょう}する vs {f|勉強|べんきょう}をする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are correct and mean the same thing. Insert `を` and
the noun becomes a genuine object, which has two consequences: it can now be
modified, and it now blocks a second `を` in the same clause.

</div>

<pre>
{f|日本語|にほんご}を<b>{f|勉強|べんきょう}します</b>。
I study Japanese.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|文法|ぶんぽう}の<b>{f|勉強|べんきょう}をします</b>。
I do grammar study.
</pre>

The first sentence already has a `を` on {f|日本語|にほんご}, so
❌{f|日本語|にほんご}を{f|勉強|べんきょう}をします is out - one clause, one `を`.
The second sentence needs the `を` version, because {f|文法|ぶんぽう}の has to
attach to a noun; ❌{f|文法|ぶんぽう}の{f|勉強|べんきょう}します does not work.

How to choose: bare `する` when the compound is behaving as a plain verb,
`をする` when you want to describe or measure the activity itself - 
{f|軽|かる}い{f|運動|うんどう}をする, 1{f|時間|じかん}{f|練習|れんしゅう}をする.
Not every noun takes `を` equally happily: activity nouns ({f|洗濯|せんたく}, {f|散歩|さんぽ}, {f|買|か}い{f|物|もの})
accept it readily, while nouns naming a change of state ({f|成功|せいこう}する,
{f|完成|かんせい}する) essentially never appear with it.

### ～にする vs ～になる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `する` means *someone makes it so*; `なる` means *it comes to
be so*. Choosing from a menu is `する`, because you are the one deciding.

</div>

<pre>
テレビの{f|音|おと}を{f|小|ちい}さく<b>して</b>ください。
Please turn the television down.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|外|そと}が{f|急|きゅう}に{f|暗|くら}く<b>なった</b>。
It suddenly got dark outside.
</pre>

The same pair runs through nouns: {f|先生|せんせい}にする "make someone a
teacher / decide on the teacher" against {f|先生|せんせい}になる "become a
teacher". If a person is deliberately causing the change, it is `する`; if the
change simply happens, it is `なる`. Japanese leans on `なる` far more than
English leans on "become", so when in doubt about a change of state, `なる` is
usually the more natural choice.

### する vs やる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `やる` is the casual,
slightly more physical word: it is what friends say to each other.

</div>

<pre>
{f|宿題|しゅくだい}、もう<b>やった</b>？
Have you done your homework yet?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|宿題|しゅくだい}はもう<b>しました</b>。
I have already done my homework.
</pre>

`やる` cannot fuse directly onto a noun the way `する` does. You can say
{f|勉強|べんきょう}する, and you can say {f|勉強|べんきょう}をやる in relaxed
speech, but ❌{f|勉強|べんきょう}やる is not a word. `やる` also means "give (to
an animal, a plant, or someone clearly junior)" - {f|花|はな}に{f|水|みず}をやる
 - and it can sound rough or blunt from the wrong mouth, so it is a poor default
in polite company.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ するます → ✅ <b>します</b>
  `する` is irregular: the ます-stem is `し`, not ❌`すり` or ❌`する`.

- ❌ {f|日本語|にほんご}を{f|勉強|べんきょう}をします。 → ✅ {f|日本語|にほんご}を<b>{f|勉強|べんきょう}します</b>。
  Two `を` in one clause. Once {f|日本語|にほんご} has taken the object marker,
  {f|勉強|べんきょう} must fuse onto `する` directly.

- ❌ テニスを{f|遊|あそ}びます。 → ✅ テニスを<b>します</b>。
  Interference from English "play tennis". {f|遊|あそ}ぶ means to play in the
  sense of having fun; sports and games take `する`.

- ❌ わたしは{f|料理|りょうり}をできます。 → ✅ わたしは{f|料理|りょうり}が<b>できます</b>。
  `できる` is the potential of `する` and it is intransitive, so what you can do
  is marked with `が`.

- ❌ {f|外|そと}で{f|大|おお}きい{f|音|おと}をしました。 → ✅ {f|外|そと}で{f|大|おお}きい{f|音|おと}が<b>しました</b>。
  Sensations - sounds, smells, tastes - take `が` with `する`. Nobody is doing
  anything here; the sound simply occurred.

## Practice

**1.** {f|明日|あした}までにこの{f|書類|しょるい}を{f|確認|かくにん}____おきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|確認|かくにん}<b>して</b>おきます

`おく` attaches to the て-form, and the て-form of `する` is `して` - irregular,
not built from a stem. The whole compound {f|確認|かくにん}する conjugates
exactly as `する` does.

</details>

**2.** {f|来週|らいしゅう}から{f|新|あたら}しい{f|仕事|しごと}を（します・なります）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|来週|らいしゅう}から{f|新|あたら}しい{f|仕事|しごと}を<b>します</b>。

The speaker is the one taking the job, so it is a deliberate action: `する`.
`なる` would need a different frame entirely, such as
{f|来週|らいしゅう}から{f|新|あたら}しい{f|仕事|しごと}になります, which would
mean the job itself is what changes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My daughter practises the piano for an hour every day."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|娘|むすめ}は{f|毎日|まいにち}1{f|時間|じかん}ピアノの<b>{f|練習|れんしゅう}をします</b>。

Both {f|練習|れんしゅう}します and {f|練習|れんしゅう}をします are possible, but
here ピアノの has to modify a noun, so the `を` version is the natural one.
Alternatively: {f|娘|むすめ}は{f|毎日|まいにち}1{f|時間|じかん}ピアノを{f|練習|れんしゅう}します。

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|静|しず}かなしてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|部屋|へや}では<b>{f|静|しず}かにして</b>ください。

To make something take on a quality you use the adverbial form, and for a
な-adjective that is `に`, not `な`. `な` only appears directly before a noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|部屋|へや}を きれいに しました。
(b) {f|部屋|へや}が きれいに なりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says somebody tidied the room - `する` puts an agent behind the change, and
the room is marked with `を` as the thing acted on. (b) simply reports that the
room ended up clean, with `が` marking it as the subject that changed.

Japanese speakers often prefer (b) even when they did the work themselves,
because `なる` sounds less like claiming credit. That preference for describing
outcomes rather than agents runs right through the language.

</details>
