# か - question particle

**Meaning** · turns a statement into a question

Put `か` at the end of a sentence and it becomes a question. Nothing else moves - 
no word order changes, no auxiliary appears. This is the same syllable as the "or"
particle, but at the end of the sentence rather than between two nouns.

## Formation

`か` is the last thing in the sentence, after the predicate is already complete.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ます-form verb | ～ます + か | {f|行|い}きます → {f|行|い}きますか |
| Noun + です | Noun + ですか | {f|学生|がくせい}です → {f|学生|がくせい}ですか |
| い-adjective + です | ～いです + か | {f|高|たか}いです → {f|高|たか}いですか |
| Past / negative | ～ましたか・～ませんか | {f|来|き}ました → {f|来|き}ましたか |
| Plain form | plain + か (blunt or written) | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}くか |
| Question word sentences | question word … + か | どこ → どこですか |
| Inside a sentence | clause + か + わかりません etc. | {f|何時|なんじ}に{f|来|く}るか、わかりません |
| Punctuation | normally 。 - the か already asks | {f|行|い}きますか。 |

<div class="note">

**Note** - after a noun in the **plain** form, the `だ` drops before `か`:
<code>{f|学生|がくせい}か</code>, never ❌<code>{f|学生|がくせい}だか</code>. In the polite form there is no such
problem, because `です` stays: <code>{f|学生|がくせい}ですか</code>.

</div>

## Nuance & register

`か` belongs with polite Japanese. Attached to `です` / `ます` it is simply the normal
way to ask anything, and it carries no attitude at all.

Attached to a **plain** form it is a different animal. 「{f|行|い}くか」 sounds
blunt, brusque, and distinctly masculine - a boss barking at a subordinate, or a
character in a film. Ordinary casual questions do not use `か` at all: you take the
plain form and raise your pitch at the end.

- 「{f|行|い}く?」 - "You going?"
- 「これ、{f|食|た}べる?」 - "You want this?"

That is the single most useful register fact on this page. A learner who says
「{f|食|た}べるか?」 to a friend sounds aggressive; 「{f|食|た}べる?」 is warm and
normal. In writing, plain form + `か` is fine and neutral - it is the everyday shape
of a question in a book or a report, where nobody is being addressed.

Plain `か` also survives in a set of self-directed expressions that everybody uses
regardless of gender: 「そうか」("ah, I see"), 「{f|本当|ほんとう}か」, and the polite
「そうですか」. Watch the intonation on that last one. Rising 「そうですか?」 is a real
question - "is that so?". Falling 「そうですか。」 is not a question at all; it is
"I see", an acknowledgement. Beginners produce the falling version by accident and
puzzle their listener.

Two more things worth knowing. `か` is normally followed by `。`, not `?`, in careful
writing - the particle already marks the question. And a **negative** question,
`～ませんか`, is not really a question about ability or fact: it is an invitation.
「{f|見|み}ませんか」 = "won't you watch it with me?", not "do you not watch it?".

## Contrast

### か vs rising intonation

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - polite speech asks with `か`. Casual speech asks by raising the
pitch and adding nothing.

</div>

<pre>
コーヒーを{f|飲|の}みます<b>か</b>。
Would you like some coffee?
</pre>

<pre>
コーヒー、{f|飲|の}む<b>?</b>
Want some coffee?
</pre>

Both are questions and neither is wrong, but they are for different people. The
first is what you say to a client; the second to a friend. Fusing them - 
❌「コーヒーを{f|飲|の}むか?」 - produces the rough, confrontational register mentioned
above, which is very rarely what a learner intends.

### ～ますか vs ～ませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ますか` asks for information. `～ませんか` offers an invitation.

</div>

<pre>
{f|週末|しゅうまつ}、{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}ます<b>か</b>。
Do you watch films at the weekend?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|週末|しゅうまつ}、{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}ません<b>か</b>。
How about seeing a film at the weekend?
</pre>

The negative shape is doing politeness work, not negation: by phrasing the offer so
that "no" is the easy answer, you leave the listener room to refuse. English does the
same thing with "you wouldn't want to…, would you?" A step further along is
`～ましょうか`, which offers to do something *for* the listener:
「{f|手伝|てつだ}いましょうか」 = "shall I give you a hand?"

### か vs の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `か` is the neutral polite question. Sentence-final `の` is the
casual question that asks for an *explanation*.

</div>

<pre>
どうして{f|来|き}ませんでした<b>か</b>。
Why didn't you come?
</pre>

<pre>
どうして{f|来|こ}なかった<b>の</b>?
How come you didn't come?
</pre>

`の` builds on the explanatory `のだ` / `んです`, so it asks about the reason or the
situation behind something rather than the bare fact. It is soft and personal - used
a great deal with children and between friends, and slightly more by women than by
men in its bare form. Its polite counterpart is `んですか`:
「どうして{f|来|こ}なかったんですか」, which is gentler than a flat `ませんでしたか` and
is often the better choice when a direct `か` question would feel like an
interrogation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あの{f|人|ひと}は{f|学生|がくせい}だか。 → ✅ あの{f|人|ひと}は{f|学生|がくせい}ですか。／{f|学生|がくせい}か。
  In the polite form the copula is `です`; in the plain form the `だ` drops out
  entirely before `か`.

- ❌ 「{f|学生|がくせい}ですか。」「はい、そうですか。」 → ✅ 「はい、そうです。」
  Falling 「そうですか」 means "I see" - it is what the *listener* says. Answering with
  it tells your questioner you have just learned your own occupation.

- ❌ どこか{f|行|い}きますか。（meaning "where are you going?"） → ✅ どこへ{f|行|い}きますか。
  <code>どこか</code> is one word meaning "somewhere". The question word needs its ordinary
  particle instead.

- ❌ {f|友|とも}だちに「{f|食|た}べるか?」と{f|聞|き}きました。 → ✅ 「{f|食|た}べる?」と{f|聞|き}きました。
  Plain form + `か` addressed at a person is rough. Casual questions drop the
  particle and rely on intonation.

## Practice

**1.** すみません、{f|駅|えき}はどちら____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|駅|えき}はどちらです<b>か</b>。

The sentence ends in a noun predicate, so the polite copula `です` comes first and
`か` follows it to make the question. Asking a stranger, the polite form is the only
sensible choice.

</details>

**2.** Choose the more inviting version: {f|一緒|いっしょ}に（{f|帰|かえ}りますか・{f|帰|かえ}りませんか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|一緒|いっしょ}に<b>{f|帰|かえ}りませんか</b>。

The negative question is Japanese's standard invitation. It leaves refusing easy, so
it sounds considerate rather than pushy. <code>{f|帰|かえ}りますか</code> would just be asking
whether the person intends to go home.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "Is this seat free?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|席|せき}は{f|空|あ}いています<b>か</b>。

The predicate is a verb in polite form, so `か` simply attaches to `います`. No word
order changes and no extra word is needed to make it a question.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|友|とも}だちに「{f|明日|あした}{f|来|く}るか?」と{f|聞|き}きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|友|とも}だちに「{f|明日|あした}{f|来|く}る?」と{f|聞|き}きました。

Plain form plus `か` aimed at a listener sounds harsh and confrontational. Between
friends the particle is dropped and the question is carried by rising intonation
alone.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) どうして{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|休|やす}みましたか。
(b) どうして{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|休|やす}んだんですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are polite and both ask why the person missed work. (a) is a bare request for a
fact, and coming from a superior it can feel like being called in for questioning.

(b) uses the explanatory `んです`, which frames the question as "what's the story
behind it?" - it acknowledges there is a situation and invites the person to explain
it. That makes it warmer and more usual in real conversation, especially when the
answer might be personal.

</details>
