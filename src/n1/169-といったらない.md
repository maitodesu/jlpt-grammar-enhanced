# ～といったらない

**Meaning** · there are no words for how ～ it is; unbelievably ～ - an exclamation, usually a complaint

Literally "if you were to try saying it, there is nothing (to say)": the speaker
claims that language runs out. In practice it is the everyday spoken way of
maxing out an adjective, and about four times out of five what it maxes out is an
irritation - heat, noise, hassle, someone's rudeness. It can express admiration
too, most naturally in the past form when recalling something remarkable.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | plain form + といったらない | うるさい → うるさいといったらない |
| な-adjective (stem) | stem + といったらない | {f|不親切|ふしんせつ} → {f|不親切|ふしんせつ}といったらない |
| Noun of quality | noun + といったらない | {f|暑|あつ}さ → {f|暑|あつ}さといったらない |
| Recalling (past) | ～といったらなかった | {f|緊張|きんちょう} → {f|緊張|きんちょう}といったらなかった |
| Casual contraction | ～ったらない | {f|面倒|めんどう}くさい → {f|面倒|めんどう}くさいったらない |
| Emphatic complaint | ～といったらありゃしない／ありはしない | {f|情|なさ}けないといったらありゃしない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun in the third row is nearly always a quality noun: an
adjective turned into a noun with ～さ ({f|暑|あつ}さ, {f|汚|きたな}さ,
{f|美|うつく}しさ), a feeling ({f|緊張|きんちょう}, {f|焦|あせ}り), or a ～ぶり /
～よう "manner of doing" noun ({f|喜|よろこ}びよう, {f|慌|あわ}てよう). A plain
concrete noun does not work: ❌この{f|机|つくえ}といったらない.

</div>

## Nuance & register

This is a **spoken** pattern, and a lively one. It fits conversation, blogs,
essays and dialogue in fiction; it is out of place in a report, a formal letter or
anything read from a podium, where
<code>{f|極|きわ}まりない</code> or a plain
<code>{f|非常|ひじょう}に</code> would take over. Nothing about it is rude, but it
is emphatic, and stacking several in a row makes the speaker sound like a
complainer.

The four shapes are not interchangeable in tone.
<code>といったらない</code> is the neutral base.
<code>～ったらない</code> is its casual squeeze - friends, family, muttering to
yourself - and it usually ends with よ or ね.
<code>といったらありゃしない</code> is louder still, is **only** used for
complaints, and carries a slightly old-fashioned, downtown flavour; it is the
voice of a neighbour grumbling over the fence.
<code>といったらありはしない</code> is the same thing written out properly and
appears mostly in print.

The positive use is real but narrower. It works best in the past, looking back at
something extraordinary, and with a ～さ noun:
<code>{f|美|うつく}しさといったらなかった</code>,
<code>{f|嬉|うれ}しさといったらなかった</code>. In the present tense with a plain
adjective, listeners default to hearing a complaint, so
{f|美味|おい}しいといったらない needs a happy face and a good context to land.

One structural point worth holding on to: this is a **degree exclamation about a
thing**, not a report of the speaker's inner state. It can therefore describe
someone else's behaviour freely - {f|弟|おとうと}の{f|喜|よろこ}びようといったらなかった -
where <code>～{f|限|かぎ}りだ</code> could not.

## Contrast

### ～といったらない vs ～{f|極|きわ}まりない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same job, opposite registers.
<code>といったらない</code> is what you say out loud;
<code>{f|極|きわ}まりない</code> is what you write in a complaint letter about the
same thing.

</div>

<pre>
あの{f|店員|てんいん}の{f|態度|たいど}、{f|失礼|しつれい}<b>ったらない</b>。
That assistant's attitude - unbelievably rude.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|当該|とうがい}{f|職員|しょくいん}の{f|対応|たいおう}は{f|失礼|しつれい}<b>{f|極|きわ}まりない</b>ものであった。
The conduct of the member of staff in question was discourteous in the extreme.
</pre>

The content is the same complaint. The first would be odd in writing addressed to
a company; the second would be comical said to a friend on the pavement outside
the shop.

### ～といったらない vs ～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>といったらない</code> rates **the thing**: it is
off the scale. <code>てたまらない</code> reports **the speaker**: I cannot stand
it.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|部屋|へや}の{f|寒|さむ}さ<b>といったらない</b>。
The cold in this room is something else.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|上着|うわぎ}を{f|忘|わす}れてきて、{f|寒|さむ}く<b>てたまらない</b>。
I left my jacket behind and I am freezing.
</pre>

Because <code>といったらない</code> is a rating, it works on other people's
experiences and on scenery; <code>てたまらない</code> is tied to the person
speaking, so ❌{f|彼|かれ}は{f|寒|さむ}くてたまらない needs らしい or そうだ to be
said at all.

### ～といったらない vs ～といったら (topic)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if a **negative word** follows, it is this pattern. If the
sentence **continues** with what comes to mind, it is the ordinary topic-raising
<code>といったら</code>, "speaking of".

</div>

<pre>
{f|夏|なつ}の{f|京都|きょうと}<b>といったら</b>、やはり{f|祇園祭|ぎおんまつり}だろう。
Speak of Kyoto in summer and it has to be the Gion Festival.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|夏|なつ}の{f|京都|きょうと}の{f|蒸|む}し{f|暑|あつ}さ<b>といったらない</b>。
The humidity of Kyoto in summer has to be experienced to be believed.
</pre>

Same opening five syllables, completely different sentences. The clue is what
lands at the end: ない / ありゃしない closes the exclamation, whereas the topic use
hands over to a comment.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|不便|ふべん}な<b>といったらない</b> → ✅ {f|不便|ふべん}<b>といったらない</b>
  A な-adjective attaches by its bare stem, with no な.

- ❌ この{f|暑|あつ}さといったらない<b>だ</b>。 → ✅ この{f|暑|あつ}さ<b>といったらない</b>。
  The ending is already ない, an い-adjective form. Adding だ is the same error as
  ❌{f|寒|さむ}いだ.

- ❌ うるさい<b>といったらありません</b> → ✅ うるさい<b>といったらありゃしない</b>／<b>といったらありはしない</b>
  This variant is frozen. There is no polite ありません version of it, because the
  whole expression is colloquial.

- ❌ （in a written complaint to a company）{f|対応|たいおう}が{f|遅|おそ}い<b>ったらない</b>。 → ✅ {f|対応|たいおう}の{f|遅|おそ}さは{f|遺憾|いかん}である。
  Grammatical, and the wrong register: ったらない in a formal letter reads as
  though the writer had lost their temper mid-sentence.

- ❌ {f|試験|しけん}に{f|受|う}かって<b>{f|嬉|うれ}しいといったらない</b>けど、{f|次|つぎ}が{f|心配|しんぱい}だ。 → ✅ {f|試験|しけん}に{f|受|う}かって{f|嬉|うれ}しい<b>{f|限|かぎ}りだ</b>けど、{f|次|つぎ}が{f|心配|しんぱい}だ。
  Not impossible, but the present-tense positive use is weak, and a listener may
  hear the first half as sarcasm. For your own happiness,
  <code>{f|限|かぎ}りだ</code> or plain とても{f|嬉|うれ}しい is safer.

## Practice

**1.** {f|満員電車|まんいんでんしゃ}に{f|一時間|いちじかん}{f|立|た}ちっぱなしで、{f|疲|つか}れる____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|疲|つか}れる<b>といったらない</b>（casual: {f|疲|つか}れるったらない）

A complaint about an everyday ordeal is the pattern's core use, and
{f|疲|つか}れる attaches in its plain form just as an い-adjective would. In the
past, {f|疲|つか}れるといったらなかった.

</details>

**2.** Choose: ({f|会議|かいぎ}の{f|報告書|ほうこくしょ}に{f|書|か}く) この{f|事故|じこ}の{f|原因|げんいん}となった{f|管理|かんり}{f|体制|たいせい}は、ずさん（といったらありゃしない・{f|極|きわ}まりない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ずさん<b>{f|極|きわ}まりない</b>。

The setting decides it. といったらありゃしない is spoken grumbling and would
undermine a written report; <code>{f|極|きわ}まりない</code> is the formal written
verdict for exactly this kind of criticism.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You cannot imagine how happy my daughter was when she saw the puppy."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|子犬|こいぬ}を{f|見|み}たときの{f|娘|むすめ}の{f|喜|よろこ}びよう<b>といったらなかった</b>。

Two points make this idiomatic: the ～よう noun {f|喜|よろこ}びよう, "the way she
was delighted", and the past form for recalling a scene. Note that the feeling
belongs to the daughter, which is fine here -
<code>～{f|限|かぎ}りだ</code> could not be used about someone else.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|説明書|せつめいしょ}が{f|分|わ}かりにくいなといったらないです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|説明書|せつめいしょ}が{f|分|わ}かりにくい<b>といったらない</b>。
"The manual is impossibly hard to follow."

Two repairs. The stray な does not belong - {f|分|わ}かりにくい is an
い-adjective and attaches directly. And the tacked-on です clashes with a
thoroughly colloquial pattern; if you need to be polite, say
{f|大変|たいへん}{f|分|わ}かりにくいです instead.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|隣|となり}の{f|部屋|へや}の{f|音|おと}がうるさい<b>といったらない</b>。
(b) {f|隣|となり}の{f|部屋|へや}の{f|音|おと}がうるさく<b>てたまらない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) rates the noise itself: it is off the scale, more than words can cover. The
speaker sounds like someone reporting an outrage, and the sentence would sit
happily in a story told to a friend.

(b) reports the speaker's endurance: I cannot put up with it any longer. The focus
moves from the noise to the person suffering it, and it is the natural line just
before you go and knock on the neighbour's door.

</details>
