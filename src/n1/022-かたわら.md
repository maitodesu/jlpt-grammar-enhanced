# ～かたわら

**Meaning** · alongside one's main work - carrying on a second, smaller activity for years beside the thing one mainly does

<code>かたわら</code> is the noun {f|傍|かたわ}ら, "the side of something". The clause
in front of it names the person's main occupation; the main clause names what they
also do, off to one side of it. Both stretch over a long period. This is the
sentence shape of an author biography, not of an ordinary day.

## Formation

<code>かたわら</code> is a noun, so what precedes it is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + かたわら | {f|働|はたら}く → {f|働|はたら}くかたわら |
| Verb (ている) | ている + かたわら | {f|勤|つと}めている → {f|勤|つと}めているかたわら |
| Noun | ～の + かたわら | {f|研究|けんきゅう} → {f|研究|けんきゅう}のかたわら |
| Written form | {f|傍|かたわ}ら | {f|農業|のうぎょう}を{f|営|いとな}む{f|傍|かたわ}ら |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one person, two long-running activities, and the **main** one
goes in the <code>かたわら</code> clause. If the two clauses have different subjects,
or if the second activity lasted an afternoon, this is the wrong pattern.

</div>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not reach for it.** <code>かたわら</code> belongs to written
Japanese of a particular kind: the author blurb inside a book jacket, a newspaper
profile, an awards citation, an obituary, a company history. It is the standard way
Japanese prose says "and on top of the day job, he also spent thirty years doing
this". Said out loud about your own week it sounds as though you were reading your
own biography aloud. In speech and in ordinary writing, use
<code>{f|働|はたら}きながら</code>, <code>～{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code>, or
<code>～と{f|並行|へいこう}して</code>.

Two constraints follow from what the word is *for*. The activities must be
**durable** - an occupation, a study, a practice - so
❌<code>テレビを{f|見|み}るかたわら</code> is not just stiff, it is wrong. And the
**subject must be one person**, the same in both clauses, because the sentence is a
description of that person's life.

There is a very mild note of admiration in it. The second activity is usually
something creative, civic or scholarly that the person kept up without being paid
for it, and the sentence quietly credits them for the effort. It is not used to say
that someone held down two jobs to make ends meet; that is
<code>{f|掛|か}け{f|持|も}ち</code>.

Separately, {f|傍|かたわ}ら survives as an ordinary noun meaning "the side of",
as in <code>{f|道|みち}の{f|傍|かたわ}らに{f|石碑|せきひ}が{f|立|た}っている</code>.
That use is alive and unremarkable, and reading it in a text tells you nothing about
the grammar point.

## Contrast

### ～かたわら vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` is the everyday word and covers any timescale.
<code>かたわら</code> is written, and only covers **years**.

</div>

<pre>
コーヒーを{f|飲|の}み<b>ながら</b>{f|資料|しりょう}に{f|目|め}を{f|通|とお}した。
I looked over the papers while drinking a coffee.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|工場|こうじょう}で{f|働|はたら}く<b>かたわら</b>、{f|夜間|やかん}の{f|専門|せんもん}{f|学校|がっこう}に{f|通|かよ}った。
He worked in a factory and attended a night-school course alongside it.
</pre>

Swap them and only one direction fails. <code>{f|工場|こうじょう}で{f|働|はたら}きながら{f|夜間|やかん}の{f|学校|がっこう}に{f|通|かよ}った</code>
is perfectly good Japanese and is what a person would actually say. The reverse -
coffee and paperwork joined by <code>かたわら</code> - is not stiff but broken,
because the clause is supposed to name a livelihood. When in doubt, this is the
substitution to make: `ながら` is almost never wrong where <code>かたわら</code> is
right.

### ～かたわら vs ～かたがた・～がてら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>かたわら</code> is a **standing arrangement**;
<code>かたがた</code> and `がてら` are a **single occasion** that serves two purposes.

</div>

<pre>
{f|散歩|さんぽ}<b>がてら</b>、{f|郵便局|ゆうびんきょく}に{f|寄|よ}ってきた。
I walked over to the post office and got a stroll out of it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|翻訳|ほんやく}の{f|仕事|しごと}の<b>かたわら</b>、{f|同人|どうじん}{f|誌|し}を{f|出|だ}し{f|続|つづ}けている。
Alongside her translation work she keeps putting out a small literary magazine.
</pre>

The first sentence covers one afternoon; the second covers a decade. A useful check:
if you could put a date on it, it is `がてら` or <code>かたがた</code>. If you would
have to give a span of years, it is <code>かたわら</code>.

### ～かたわら vs ～{f|一方|いっぽう}で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> is the neutral modern option
and can contrast **anything** with anything, including two subjects or two sides of a
situation. <code>かたわら</code> needs **one person and two activities**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|都市部|としぶ}の{f|人口|じんこう}が{f|増|ふ}える<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|地方|ちほう}では{f|過疎|かそ}が{f|進|すす}んでいる。
While the cities grow, depopulation is advancing in the regions.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|市役所|しやくしょ}に{f|勤|つと}める<b>かたわら</b>、{f|市民|しみん}{f|楽団|がくだん}で{f|指揮|しき}をとっている。
Alongside his job at city hall he conducts a community orchestra.
</pre>

The first cannot take <code>かたわら</code> at all: two different populations are
doing the contrasting, and no one is pursuing anything. The second could take
<code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code>, and in a spoken report it probably would. That
asymmetry is the whole story - <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> is the wider,
safer word.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ テレビを{f|見|み}る<b>かたわら</b>、ご{f|飯|はん}を{f|食|た}べた。 → ✅ テレビを{f|見|み}<b>ながら</b>、ご{f|飯|はん}を{f|食|た}べた。
  Two things inside the same half-hour are `ながら`. <code>かたわら</code> needs
  activities measured in years.

- ❌ {f|仕事|しごと}<b>かたわら</b>{f|絵|え}を{f|習|なら}っている。 → ✅ {f|仕事|しごと}<b>のかたわら</b>{f|絵|え}を{f|習|なら}っている。
  <code>かたわら</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`.

- ❌ {f|働|はたら}い<b>たかたわら</b> → ✅ {f|働|はたら}<b>くかたわら</b>
  The clause takes the dictionary form or `ている`, never the past. The pattern
  describes an ongoing arrangement, and a past-tense clause contradicts that even when
  the whole sentence is about the past.

- ❌ {f|兄|あに}が{f|働|はたら}く<b>かたわら</b>、{f|私|わたし}は{f|大学|だいがく}に{f|通|かよ}った。 → ✅ {f|兄|あに}が{f|働|はたら}いてくれる<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|私|わたし}は{f|大学|だいがく}に{f|通|かよ}った。
  The two clauses must share a subject. With two different people the sentence needs
  <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code>.

## Practice

**1.** {f|彼|かれ}は{f|新聞社|しんぶんしゃ}に{f|勤|つと}める____、{f|長年|ながねん}にわたって{f|児童|じどう}{f|文学|ぶんがく}を{f|書|か}き{f|続|つづ}けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|新聞社|しんぶんしゃ}に{f|勤|つと}める<b>かたわら</b>

A career at a newspaper and a decades-long second calling as a children's writer:
one person, two durable activities, the livelihood in the first clause. This is the
profile-piece sentence <code>かたわら</code> exists for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}き（かたわら・ながら）{f|皿|さら}を{f|洗|あら}った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}き<b>ながら</b>{f|皿|さら}を{f|洗|あら}った。

Washing up to music takes ten minutes. <code>かたわら</code> would claim that
listening to music was the speaker's occupation and dishwashing their life's second
pursuit. Note also that `ながら` attaches to the ます-stem, so
<code>かたわら</code> would additionally need the dictionary form
<code>{f|聞|き}く</code>.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese, as you would say it to a friend: {f|税理士|ぜいりし}として{f|働|はたら}くかたわら、{f|地元|じもと}の{f|少年|しょうねん}チームを{f|指導|しどう}している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|税理士|ぜいりし}として{f|働|はたら}き<b>ながら</b>、{f|地元|じもと}の{f|少年|しょうねん}チームのコーチもしている。

`ながら` carries the same content with none of the biography-blurb formality; adding
`も` keeps the sense that the coaching is the extra thing.
<code>～と{f|並行|へいこう}して</code> would also work and is a shade more formal.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|祖母|そぼ}は{f|畑仕事|はたけしごと}かたわら、{f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|人|ひと}に{f|漬物|つけもの}の{f|作|つく}り{f|方|かた}を{f|教|おし}えていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|祖母|そぼ}は{f|畑仕事|はたけしごと}<b>のかたわら</b>、{f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|人|ひと}に{f|漬物|つけもの}の{f|作|つく}り{f|方|かた}を{f|教|おし}えていた。

The content is right - a lifetime of working the fields, with pickling lessons on
the side - but a noun cannot sit straight against <code>かたわら</code>. Insert
`の`. With a verb instead, <code>{f|畑仕事|はたけしごと}をするかたわら</code> is
also correct.

</details>

**5.** Where would you expect to read each of these, and what does the choice tell you?
(a) {f|銀行|ぎんこう}に{f|勤|つと}める<b>かたわら</b>、{f|俳句|はいく}の{f|同人|どうじん}{f|誌|し}を{f|主宰|しゅさい}していた。
(b) {f|銀行|ぎんこう}で{f|働|はたら}き<b>ながら</b>、{f|俳句|はいく}のサークルをやってたんです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is print: a jacket blurb, an obituary, a prize citation. The writer is looking
back over a whole life and presenting the bank career and the haiku circle as the
two things that defined it. Nobody speaks this sentence.

(b) is the same person telling you about it over a drink. `ながら`, the casual
<code>やってた</code> and the explanatory <code>んです</code> put it in conversation.
The content is identical; only the frame has changed, and that frame is the entire
reason to learn <code>かたわら</code> - so that when you meet it in print you know
you are being handed a summed-up life.

</details>
