# ～ないとも限らない

**Meaning** · you never know; it isn't impossible that ～ - read *とも かぎらない*; the odds are low, but not low enough to ignore

{f|限|かぎ}る is "to be limited to", so <code>とも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> means "things
are not limited to that". Put a **negative** inside it and the two cancel:
<code>{f|降|ふ}らないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> is "it is not necessarily the case
that it won't rain" - in other words, it might. What the double negative adds is the
reason you are mentioning it at all. This is the grammar of taking an umbrella.

## Formation

The clause in front is **negative**, and `と` quotes it. Drop the ない and the meaning
flips over completely.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ない-form + とも{f|限|かぎ}らない | {f|降|ふ}る → {f|降|ふ}らないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + とも{f|限|かぎ}らない | {f|起|お}きる → {f|起|お}きないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | <b>{f|来|こ}ない</b> + とも{f|限|かぎ}らない | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない |
| Verb (past) | ～なかった + とも{f|限|かぎ}らない | {f|見|み}た → {f|見|み}なかったとも{f|限|かぎ}らない |
| い-adjective | ～くない + とも{f|限|かぎ}らない | {f|正|ただ}しい → {f|正|ただ}しくないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない |
| な-adjective | ～ではない + とも{f|限|かぎ}らない | {f|安全|あんぜん} → {f|安全|あんぜん}ではないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない |
| Noun | ～ではない + とも{f|限|かぎ}らない | {f|嘘|うそ} → {f|嘘|うそ}ではないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない |
| Polite | ～とも{f|限|かぎ}りません | {f|漏|も}れない → {f|漏|も}れないとも{f|限|かぎ}りません |
| Common opener | {f|万|まん}が{f|一|いち}／{f|絶対|ぜったい}に | {f|万|まん}が{f|一|いち}、～ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - count the negatives. <code>{f|来|こ}ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code>
= *he might come*. <code>{f|来|く}るとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> = *he might not come*.
Both are grammatical, so nothing will warn you that you have said the opposite of what
you meant.

</div>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

There's always a chance an earthquake knocks the power out, so we keep water in the house.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

The register is **careful adult Japanese**: safety notices, insurance and legal
writing, planning meetings, and the speech of somebody thinking ahead on your behalf.
It is not literary or archaic, and it is perfectly sayable - a parent, a site foreman
or a systems administrator will use it in conversation - but it is measured, and it
sits oddly in light chat.

What it contributes over `かもしれない` is not probability but **relevance**. A
speaker who says <code>{f|停電|ていでん}が{f|起|お}きないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code>
is estimating the chance as small; the reason for raising it is that the consequences
would matter. That is why a precaution nearly always follows in the same breath - ので,
から, and then a torch, a backup, a second set of keys.

Two smaller points. The pattern is at home with unwelcome outcomes, but it is not
restricted to them: <code>まとまらないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> holds out a faint
hope in exactly the same shape, and in negotiation that faint hope is often the
politest thing that can be said. And the `も` in <code>とも</code> is doing work -
it is the "even" that turns <code>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</code>, a flat denial of a
generalisation, into the more tentative "one cannot even say that".

## Contrast

### ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない vs かもしれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かもしれない` says the chance is **real**.
<code>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> says the chance is **small but worth acting
on**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|午後|ごご}から{f|天気|てんき}が{f|崩|くず}れる<b>かもしれない</b>。
The weather may turn this afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山|やま}の{f|天気|てんき}は{f|急|きゅう}に{f|変|か}わら<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>。
You can never be sure the weather in the mountains won't turn on you.
</pre>

The first is a forecast; you might cancel your plans on it. The second is not really
a forecast at all - it is an argument for putting a jacket in the bag. Replacing the
second with `かもしれない` would be perfectly grammatical and would lose the reason
the sentence exists.

### ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない vs とは{f|限|かぎ}らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> **knocks down** a
generalisation somebody is relying on. <code>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code>
**raises** a possibility nobody has considered.

</div>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}が{f|高|たか}ければうまい<b>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>。
Expensive doesn't necessarily mean good.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|時間|じかん}なら、あの{f|店|みせ}が{f|閉|し}まってい<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>。{f|電話|でんわ}してみよう。
At this hour that place may well have shut. Let's ring first.
</pre>

They face opposite ways. The first takes an assumption already in the room and puts
a hole in it; a `が` clause conceding the general rule usually follows. The second
introduces a contingency out of nowhere and then does something about it. Both hang
on {f|限|かぎ}る, and the difference is entirely in whether the negative sits inside
the quotation.

### ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない vs {f|恐|おそ}れがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|恐|おそ}れがある</code> states a risk **as a fact about
the world**. <code>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> states it as something **one
cannot honestly deny**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|大雨|おおあめ}により、{f|土砂|どしゃ}{f|災害|さいがい}の<b>{f|恐|おそ}れがあります</b>。
Heavy rain brings a risk of landslides.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|工事|こうじ}の{f|音|おと}で{f|近所|きんじょ}から{f|苦情|くじょう}が{f|来|こ}<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}りません</b>。
It's not out of the question that the neighbours complain about the noise from the work.
</pre>

An official warning uses <code>{f|恐|おそ}れ</code>: the risk is being asserted, and
somebody's name is on the bulletin. The second sentence asserts nothing - it declines
to promise, which is a much smaller claim and correspondingly easier to make in a
meeting. If you can imagine the sentence on a public notice, use
<code>{f|恐|おそ}れがある</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}が{f|来|く}る<b>とも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>から、{f|席|せき}を{f|空|あ}けておこう。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}が{f|来|こ}<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>から、{f|席|せき}を{f|空|あ}けておこう。
  The inner clause must be negative. Without the ない you have said "he won't
  necessarily come", which is no reason to save him a seat.

- ❌ {f|事故|じこ}<b>とも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>から、{f|保険|ほけん}に{f|入|はい}った。 → ✅ {f|事故|じこ}が{f|起|お}こら<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>から、{f|保険|ほけん}に{f|入|はい}った。
  A bare noun has nothing to negate. Give the pattern a predicate -
  <code>{f|起|お}こらない</code>, <code>ではない</code> - and the double negative can
  do its work.

- ❌ {f|失敗|しっぱい}し<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>ので、{f|安心|あんしん}してください。 → ✅ {f|失敗|しっぱい}し<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>ので、{f|念|ねん}のため{f|確認|かくにん}をお{f|願|ねが}いします。
  The pattern has just raised an alarm, so reassurance cannot follow it. Whatever
  comes after ので has to be a precaution.

- ❌ この{f|店|みせ}のケーキ、おいしく<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らないよ</b>。 → ✅ この{f|店|みせ}のケーキ、おいしいかもしれないよ。
  Far too much machinery for a cake. The pattern implies a contingency somebody ought
  to guard against, and nobody guards against dessert.

## Practice

**1.** {f|台風|たいふう}は{f|来|こ}ないという{f|予報|よほう}だが、{f|進路|しんろ}が{f|変|か}わら____から、{f|準備|じゅんび}はしておこう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|進路|しんろ}が{f|変|か}わら<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>から…

The forecast says no, the speaker is not willing to bet on it, and a precaution
follows in the same sentence - the pattern's standard shape. Note that the ない
attaches to the verb, giving <code>{f|変|か}わらない</code> before `とも`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|日本人|にほんじん}なら{f|誰|だれ}でも{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|書|か}ける（とは{f|限|かぎ}らない・ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|書|か}ける<b>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>。

The sentence is denying a generalisation that the listener is taking for granted,
which is <code>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</code>. The other pattern needs a negative inside
it and would produce <code>{f|書|か}けないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> - "you never
know, they might be able to write it" - a different and much odder claim.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Keep the receipt - you never know when you might need it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

レシートは{f|取|と}っておいたほうがいい。{f|後|あと}で{f|必要|ひつよう}になら<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>から。

"You never know when you might need it" is this pattern almost exactly: an unlikely
contingency offered as the reason for a precaution. Because
<code>{f|必要|ひつよう}</code> is a な-adjective, the negative is built on
<code>になる</code> here, which is tidier than
<code>{f|必要|ひつよう}ではないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}んだら{f|眠|ねむ}くなるとも{f|限|かぎ}らないので、{f|運転|うんてん}は{f|避|さ}けてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}んだら{f|眠|ねむ}く<b>ならないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>ので、{f|運転|うんてん}は{f|避|さ}けてください。
"The medicine may make you drowsy, so please avoid driving."

As written, the sentence says drowsiness is not guaranteed - and then advises against
driving, which makes no sense. The negative has to go **inside**:
<code>ならないとも{f|限|かぎ}らない</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|上司|じょうし}が{f|反対|はんたい}するかもしれません。
(b) {f|上司|じょうし}が{f|反対|はんたい}し<b>ないとも{f|限|かぎ}りません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a straight estimate. The speaker thinks there is a genuine chance of
opposition and is telling you so; you can reasonably ask how likely they think it is.

(b) puts the chance lower and the caution higher. It says: I am not predicting
opposition, but I am not prepared to guarantee its absence, and you should plan on
that basis. In a meeting (b) is the more diplomatic of the two, because it commits
the speaker to no opinion about the boss at all.

</details>
