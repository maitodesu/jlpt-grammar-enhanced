# ～とみるや

**Meaning** · the moment someone judged that the situation was thus, they acted on it - sized it up and moved, usually fast and usually to their own advantage

The {f|見|み}る here is not eyesight; it is the {f|見|み}る of
<code>{f|甘|あま}く{f|見|み}る</code> and <code>{f|様子|ようす}を{f|見|み}る</code> -
to assess, to reckon, to conclude. The や is the same literary particle as in
<code>や{f|否|いな}や</code>. Put together: *saw how things stood, and at once …*.
An act of judgement is built into the pattern, which is what separates it from
every other "the moment that" form at this level.

## Formation

The と in front is the quotative と, so whatever it follows takes its plain
sentence-final shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + とみるや | {f|逃|に}げ{f|切|き}れない → {f|逃|に}げ{f|切|き}れないとみるや |
| い-adjective | plain form + とみるや | {f|分|ぶ}が{f|悪|わる}い → {f|分|ぶ}が{f|悪|わる}いとみるや |
| な-adjective | ～だ + とみるや（or bare） | {f|不利|ふり} → {f|不利|ふり}だとみるや／{f|不利|ふり}とみるや |
| Noun | ～だ + とみるや（or bare） | チャンス → チャンスだとみるや／チャンスとみるや |
| Kanji spelling | ～と{f|見|み}るや | {f|危険|きけん}と{f|見|み}るや |
| Longer variant | ～と{f|見|み}るや{f|否|いな}や | {f|勝|か}てないと{f|見|み}るや{f|否|いな}や |
| Second clause | past tense, an immediate decisive act | {f|話題|わだい}を{f|変|か}えた |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the bare-noun version (<code>{f|不利|ふり}とみるや</code>, with no だ) is the
newspaper shape and is very common in print. Both are correct; the だ version is
marginally the plainer of the two.

</div>

## Nuance & register

<code>とみるや</code> is **written Japanese**. It belongs to news copy, editorials,
sports reporting, history, and fiction with a narrator who is watching people
closely. It is not stiff in the way a legal formula is stiff, but you will not hear
it over dinner; in speech the same idea comes out as
<code>{f|無理|むり}だと{f|分|わ}かると、すぐに</code> or
<code>～のを{f|見|み}て、すぐ</code>.

Two things travel with it almost every time.

**The subject is someone being observed** - a suspect, a company, a rival, a
politician, a child. The narrator is describing behaviour from outside, and the
reader is invited to draw a conclusion about the person's character. Using it about
yourself is possible but unusual, and it makes you sound as though you were
narrating your own biography.

**There is a faint critical edge.** The judgement is quick, self-interested and
often not very admirable: dropping a policy the moment it stops polling well,
changing the subject the moment you are losing. The pattern is not automatically
negative - the shopkeeper example above is simply attentive - but if you want a
neutral report of good timing, <code>～のを{f|見|み}て</code> carries less baggage.

The second clause is a **completed act**, so past tense and no requests,
suggestions or volitional forms. And because the whole point is an assessment, the
first clause has to be something assessable: a state of affairs, a prospect, a
balance of advantage. A bare physical event -
❌<code>ドアが{f|開|あ}いたとみるや</code> - has nothing to judge.

## Contrast

### ～とみるや vs ～や{f|否|いな}や

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>や{f|否|いな}や</code> links **two events**.
<code>とみるや</code> inserts a **person's judgement** between them: they perceived
something, and that perception is why they moved.

</div>

<pre>
{f|開場|かいじょう}のアナウンスが{f|流|なが}れる<b>や{f|否|いな}や</b>、{f|列|れつ}が{f|動|うご}き{f|始|はじ}めた。
No sooner had the opening announcement gone out than the queue began to move.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|係員|かかりいん}は{f|列|れつ}が{f|長|なが}すぎる<b>とみるや</b>、{f|入口|いりぐち}をもう{f|一|ひと}つ{f|開|あ}けた。
Seeing that the queue was too long, the attendant opened a second entrance.
</pre>

The first is pure sequence; nobody in it decides anything. In the second, the
attendant's assessment is the hinge of the sentence, and
<code>や{f|否|いな}や</code> would throw that information away.

### ～とみるや vs ～のを{f|見|み}て

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>のを{f|見|み}て</code> is **literal seeing**, in neutral
everyday Japanese. <code>とみるや</code> is **concluding**, in literary Japanese,
with a note of calculation.

</div>

<pre>
{f|母|はは}は{f|弟|おとうと}が{f|泣|な}いている<b>のを{f|見|み}て</b>、すぐにだき{f|上|あ}げた。
Seeing my little brother crying, my mother picked him straight up.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}は{f|母|はは}が{f|甘|あま}い<b>とみるや</b>、{f|同|おな}じ{f|手|て}を{f|何度|なんど}も{f|使|つか}った。
Once my brother worked out that our mother was a soft touch, he used the same trick over and over.
</pre>

Notice that only the second involves working something out. Notice too that
<code>のを{f|見|み}て</code> requires an object you could photograph, whereas
<code>とみるや</code> takes an invisible judgement - "she is a soft touch" is not
something you see.

### ～とみるや vs ～とみえて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same verb, opposite jobs. <code>とみるや</code> = *the person in
the story judged, and acted*. <code>とみえて</code> = *the narrator infers*:
"apparently, by the look of it".

</div>

<pre>
{f|道|みち}がぬれている。{f|夜中|よなか}に{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った<b>とみえて</b>、{f|草|くさ}にも{f|水|みず}がついている。
The road is wet. It seems to have rained in the night - there's water on the grass too.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|雨|あめ}になる<b>とみるや</b>、{f|洗濯物|せんたくもの}を{f|取|と}り{f|込|こ}んだ。
The moment she judged it was going to rain, she brought the washing in.
</pre>

These are easy to confuse on the page because they differ by two kana. The test is
who is doing the judging: with <code>とみえて</code> it is the writer, and the
sentence is a guess; with <code>とみるや</code> it is the character, and the sentence
is an action.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}の{f|顔|かお}<b>とみるや</b>、{f|逃|に}げ{f|出|だ}した。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}の{f|顔|かお}を{f|見|み}る<b>や</b>、{f|逃|に}げ{f|出|だ}した。
  Two different constructions. <code>とみるや</code> takes a quoted judgement;
  <code>～を{f|見|み}るや</code> is the plain literary "as soon as (he) saw".

- ❌ ドアが{f|開|あ}いた<b>とみるや</b>、{f|中|なか}に{f|入|はい}った。 → ✅ ドアが{f|開|あ}く<b>や{f|否|いな}や</b>、{f|中|なか}に{f|入|はい}った。
  An open door is an event, not a conclusion. The pattern needs something that can
  be assessed.

- ❌ {f|危|あぶ}ない<b>とみるや</b>、すぐに{f|逃|に}げてください。 → ✅ {f|危|あぶ}ないと{f|思|おも}ったら、すぐに{f|逃|に}げてください。
  Instructions are impossible after it. The pattern narrates what somebody already
  did.

- ❌ この{f|絵|え}は{f|高|たか}い<b>とみるや</b>、{f|買|か}う{f|人|ひと}が{f|少|すく}ない。 → ✅ この{f|絵|え}は{f|高|たか}い<b>とみえて</b>、{f|買|か}う{f|人|ひと}が{f|少|すく}ない。
  This is the {f|見|み}えて／{f|見|み}るや mix-up. Here the writer is inferring, so
  the form is <code>とみえて</code>.

## Practice

**1.** {f|警備員|けいびいん}は{f|様子|ようす}がおかしい____、すぐに{f|無線|むせん}で{f|連絡|れんらく}した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|様子|ようす}がおかしい<b>とみるや</b>

The guard assesses the situation and acts on the assessment immediately, and the
second clause is a completed past action. Both halves fit the pattern exactly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|山|やま}の{f|上|うえ}は{f|雲|くも}が{f|厚|あつ}い。{f|天気|てんき}が{f|崩|くず}れる（とみるや・とみえて）、{f|登山者|とざんしゃ}の{f|姿|すがた}が{f|見|み}えない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|天気|てんき}が{f|崩|くず}れる<b>とみえて</b>、{f|登山者|とざんしゃ}の{f|姿|すがた}が{f|見|み}えない。

Here the speaker is guessing from evidence, which is <code>とみえて</code>.
<code>とみるや</code> would need a named person who reached a judgement and then did
something.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in newspaper style: "The moment the company saw that sales were falling, it closed three stores."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|同社|どうしゃ}は{f|売|う}り{f|上|あ}げが{f|落|お}ちている<b>とみるや</b>、{f|三|さん}{f|店舗|てんぽ}を{f|閉鎖|へいさ}した。

An organisation making a fast, hard-headed decision is the classic newspaper use.
{f|同社|どうしゃ} rather than その{f|会社|かいしゃ} keeps the register consistent with
the connective.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|私|わたし}は{f|時間|じかん}がないとみるや、タクシーに{f|乗|の}ろうと{f|思|おも}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|時間|じかん}がないと{f|分|わ}かったら、タクシーに{f|乗|の}ろうと{f|思|おも}います。

Two problems at once: the pattern describes someone else's behaviour as observed
from outside, and it cannot be followed by a volitional or a plan. Neither survives
in a first-person sentence about the future.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|議長|ぎちょう}は{f|反対|はんたい}が{f|多|おお}い<b>とみるや</b>、{f|採決|さいけつ}を{f|見送|みおく}った。
(b) {f|議長|ぎちょう}は{f|反対|はんたい}が{f|多|おお}かったので、{f|採決|さいけつ}を{f|見送|みおく}った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) makes the chair's own reading of the room the reason, and makes it fast: he
took one look at the numbers and dropped the vote. There is a hint that this was a
tactical retreat rather than a principled one, and the sentence is journalistic in
register.

(b) states a cause flatly. The opposition was large, so the vote was postponed. It
says nothing about how quickly he decided, nothing about his motives, and it is
ordinary neutral Japanese.

</details>
