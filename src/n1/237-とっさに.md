# とっさに

**Meaning** · in a split second; on reflex; without stopping to think

`とっさに` is not simply "quickly". It describes an action taken in the instant
something unexpected happens, **before there is time to decide** - swerving, grabbing,
blurting out an excuse. The absence of deliberation is the whole content of the word.

## Usage

An adverb, standing in front of the verb. The kanji spelling
<code>{f|咄嗟|とっさ}に</code> exists but neither character is in everyday use, so kana
is normal.

Three things it needs:

- **A sudden trigger.** Something has to happen to you. A child runs into the road, a
  glass tips over, an interviewer asks a question you had not prepared for. Without
  that jolt, `とっさに` has nothing to be a reaction to.
- **Past tense.** It reports what someone did in a moment that has already passed. A
  plan to act quickly tomorrow is not `とっさに`.
- **An action, or the failure to produce one.** The negative is just as common as the
  positive: <code>とっさに{f|返事|へんじ}ができなかった</code>,
  <code>とっさに{f|言葉|ことば}が{f|出|で}てこなかった</code> - the mind went blank in
  the second available.

Before a noun the `に` becomes `の`: <code>とっさの{f|判断|はんだん}</code> (a
split-second decision), <code>とっさの{f|出来事|できごと}</code>,
<code>とっさのこと</code>. It is a noun plus a particle, never a な-adjective.

Verbs that collocate with it are almost all bodily or verbal reflexes:
{f|手|て}が{f|出|で}る, {f|身|み}をかわす, {f|飛|と}びのく,
{f|受|う}け{f|止|と}める, {f|嘘|うそ}をつく, {f|判断|はんだん}する.

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

Getting under the desk during the earthquake was a split-second reaction, but it turned out to be what saved his life.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It is equally at home in a friend's anecdote, a driver's statement
to the police, a news report and a novel, and it marks nothing about the speaker.

Its social work is worth noticing. Because `とっさに` says there was no time to think,
it functions as a built-in **explanation or excuse**.
とっさに{f|嘘|うそ}をついてしまった admits to a lie while asking the listener to
understand that no better answer was available in the second the speaker had.
Take the adverb out - {f|嘘|うそ}をついてしまった - and the same sentence becomes a
plain confession. Learners often reach for it to soften an account of something they
did badly, and that instinct is correct.

Applied to someone else's action it usually reads as **admiration**:
とっさの{f|判断|はんだん}がよかった praises presence of mind. It very rarely blames,
because it has already granted that no thought was possible.

Two neighbours to keep straight. <code>{f|思|おも}わず</code> is about intention - you
did it without meaning to - and can apply to a slow situation, as in
{f|思|おも}わず{f|笑|わら}ってしまった. <code>{f|反射的|はんしゃてき}に</code> is the
clinical version, "reflexively", and is the natural choice in technical or medical
writing. `とっさに` sits between them, and is the ordinary word.

## Contrast

### とっさに vs すぐに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すぐに` is about **delay**: nothing came between
the cue and the action. `とっさに` is about **deliberation**: there was no time for
any. Only `とっさに` needs the situation to be sudden.

</div>

<pre>
{f|連絡|れんらく}をもらったら、<b>すぐに</b>{f|向|む}かいます。
I'll head over as soon as I hear from you.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|前|まえ}の{f|車|くるま}が{f|急|きゅう}に{f|止|と}まったので、<b>とっさに</b>ハンドルを{f|切|き}った。
The car in front stopped dead, and I yanked the wheel over on instinct.
</pre>

The first sentence is about the future and about an expected cue, so `とっさに` is
impossible there. The second could not take `すぐに` without loss:
すぐにハンドルを{f|切|き}った would suggest the driver saw the problem, decided, and
acted promptly - a sequence, however fast. `とっさに` denies that there was a sequence
at all.

### とっさに vs {f|思|おも}わず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とっさに` = no time. <code>{f|思|おも}わず</code> = no intention.
An action can be both, but the two words point at different things.

</div>

<pre>
{f|懐|なつ}かしい{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|見|み}て、<b>{f|思|おも}わず</b>{f|笑|わら}ってしまった。
Seeing the old photo, I found myself smiling.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|後|うし}ろから{f|声|こえ}をかけられ、<b>とっさに</b>{f|振|ふ}り{f|返|かえ}った。
Someone called out behind me and I spun round on reflex.
</pre>

Nothing sudden happens in the first sentence - the speaker is looking at a photograph
at leisure - so `とっさに` would be wrong; what is missing is intention, not time. In
the second, {f|思|おも}わず would also be acceptable, and the sentence would shift very
slightly towards "I turned round before I could stop myself" rather than "I turned
round in the instant".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}、{f|着|つ}いたら<b>とっさに</b>{f|連絡|れんらく}します。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}、{f|着|つ}いたら<b>すぐに</b>{f|連絡|れんらく}します。
  A promise about tomorrow cannot be a reflex. `とっさに` only reports what already
  happened.

- ❌ {f|宿題|しゅくだい}を<b>とっさに</b>{f|終|お}わらせた。 → ✅ {f|宿題|しゅくだい}を<b>{f|一気|いっき}に</b>{f|終|お}わらせた。
  Homework is a planned task with no sudden trigger, and finishing it takes longer
  than an instant. Two conditions of the word fail at once.

- ❌ <b>とっさな</b>{f|判断|はんだん}で{f|助|たす}かった。 → ✅ <b>とっさの</b>{f|判断|はんだん}で{f|助|たす}かった。
  {f|咄嗟|とっさ} is a noun, so the noun-modifying particle is `の`, not `な`.

- ❌ <b>とっさに</b>{f|三十分|さんじゅっぷん}{f|考|かんが}えてから{f|答|こた}えた。 → ✅ <b>じっくり</b>{f|三十分|さんじゅっぷん}{f|考|かんが}えてから{f|答|こた}えた。
  Thirty minutes of thought is the exact opposite of what the word describes.

## Practice

**1.** {f|棚|たな}から{f|花瓶|かびん}が{f|落|お}ちてきたので、____{f|受|う}け{f|止|と}めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>とっさに</b>{f|受|う}け{f|止|と}めた

The vase falling is the sudden trigger, catching it is a bodily reflex, and the
sentence is in the past. All three conditions are met. Note that
{f|思|おも}わず would also work here, shifting the emphasis from "no time" to "before I
could think about it".

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: {f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|面接|めんせつ}では、{f|質問|しつもん}にとっさに{f|答|こた}えるつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|面接|めんせつ}では、{f|質問|しつもん}に<b>すぐに</b>{f|答|こた}えるつもりです。

You cannot intend to react reflexively - つもり and `とっさに` contradict each other,
and the event is in the future besides. If you want to say that you hope to cope well
with the unexpected, rebuild it around the noun:
とっさの{f|質問|しつもん}にも{f|落|お}ち{f|着|つ}いて{f|答|こた}えたいです。

</details>

**3.** Explain what `とっさに` contributes:
{f|遅|おく}れた{f|理由|りゆう}を{f|聞|き}かれて、<b>とっさに</b>「{f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まっていて」と{f|言|い}ってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Factually it adds only that the excuse came out in the instant the question was
asked. Socially it does much more: it tells the listener that the speaker had not
planned to lie, that the lie was a panic response rather than a scheme, and that the
speaker is not proud of it.

Remove it and the sentence reports a deliberate falsehood. That difference between a
prepared lie and one that escaped in the moment is exactly the work this adverb is
doing, and it is why it appears so often in apologies and confessions.

</details>
