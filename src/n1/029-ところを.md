# ～ところを

**Meaning** · at a moment when you were in the middle of something else - and I am about to impose on it, or something is about to happen to it

<code>ところ</code> is a point in time, and `を` makes that point the **thing acted
upon**. Two very different sentences come out of that one idea: the set greeting that
apologises for interrupting someone (<code>お{f|忙|いそが}しいところを</code>), and the
narrative line where somebody is caught, stopped or rescued at exactly the wrong or
right moment.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | ～い + ところを | お{f|忙|いそが}しい → お{f|忙|いそが}しいところを |
| Noun | ～の + ところを | お{f|休|やす}み → お{f|休|やす}みのところを |
| な-adjective | ～な + ところを | {f|大変|たいへん}な → {f|大変|たいへん}なところをお{f|邪魔|じゃま}しました |
| Verb (ている) | ている + ところを | {f|寝|ね}ている → {f|寝|ね}ているところを |
| Verb (on the point of) | ～ようとした + ところを | {f|逃|に}げようとした → {f|逃|に}げようとしたところを |
| Concessive | ～べき + ところを | {f|伺|うかが}うべき → {f|伺|うかが}うべきところを |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - whatever follows has to be able to take that moment as its
**object**. Thanks, an apology or a request in the greeting use
(<code>ありがとうございます／{f|申|もう}し{f|訳|わけ}ありません／{f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが</code>);
a verb of catching, seeing, stopping or saving in the narrative use, usually in the
passive.

</div>

## Nuance & register

The greeting use is **formal, extremely common, and worth producing**. It is standard at
the top of a business email, at the start of a meeting, in a phone call to a client, and
in the sentence you say when you knock on someone's door. Learn
<code>お{f|忙|いそが}しいところを{f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが</code> and
<code>お{f|休|やす}みのところを{f|申|もう}し{f|訳|わけ}ございません</code> as units; they
will carry a lot of weight. Note that the `を` is often simply dropped -
<code>お{f|忙|いそが}しいところありがとうございます</code> - with no change of meaning
and a slight drop in formality.

What the greeting does is acknowledge a **cost to the other person**. That is why it
does not work downward or sideways among friends: telling a classmate you are sorry to
disturb their busy schedule sounds sarcastic. Between equals, say
<code>{f|今|いま}いい？</code>.

The narrative use is register-neutral and mostly appears in the **past tense**, very
often with a passive main verb. The reason is structural: the ところ is the object, so
the person to whom the moment belongs stays the subject of the sentence -
<code>{f|見|み}られた</code>, <code>{f|捕|つか}まえられた</code>,
<code>{f|助|たす}けられた</code>, <code>{f|呼|よ}び{f|止|と}められた</code>. The events
themselves are nearly always unwelcome - being caught, being seen, being interrupted -
with rescue as the standing exception.

The third use, <code>～べきところを</code> and
<code>Noun のところを</code>, marks a **departure from what was proper or expected**. In
apologies it is a fixed humble move: you name the correct course of action and then
report that things went otherwise. In shops and advertising the same shape does price
comparisons - <code>{f|通常|つうじょう}3,000{f|円|えん}のところを</code> - which is the
one place you will meet it in casual reading.

## Contrast

### ～ところを vs ～ところに・～ところへ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the particle says what role the moment plays.
<code>ところに／へ</code> = something **arrives into** that moment.
<code>ところを</code> = someone **acts on** you in that moment.

</div>

<pre>
{f|出|で}かけようとした<b>ところに</b>、{f|宅配便|たくはいびん}が{f|届|とど}いた。
Just as I was about to leave, a delivery arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|出|で}かけようとした<b>ところを</b>、{f|母|はは}に{f|呼|よ}び{f|止|と}められた。
Just as I was about to leave, my mother called me back.
</pre>

In the first, the parcel is the newcomer and the moment is its destination. In the
second, the moment itself is what the mother acted upon, so it takes `を` and the main
verb is transitive or passive. A quick test: if the main verb is intransitive and
something *turns up*, use `に` or `へ`. If someone *does something to you*, use `を`.

Note also that <code>～たところで</code> is a different pattern altogether, meaning "even
if you do", and shares nothing with these but the word.

### ～ところを vs ～{f|中|なか}

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in the greeting formulas the two are close.
<code>お{f|忙|いそが}しい{f|中|なか}</code> looks at a **stretch** of busy time;
<code>お{f|忙|いそが}しいところを</code> looks at the **point** you are being interrupted
at.

</div>

<pre>
お{f|忙|いそが}しい<b>{f|中|なか}</b>、お{f|集|あつ}まりいただきありがとうございました。
Thank you all for coming despite your busy schedules.
</pre>

<pre>
お{f|忙|いそが}しい<b>ところを</b>{f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが、{f|少|すこ}しだけお{f|時間|じかん}をいただけますか。
I'm sorry to catch you at a busy time, but could you spare me a few minutes?
</pre>

There is a tendency rather than a rule here.
<code>{f|中|なか}</code> is what you hear in a speech to a room of people who all made
the effort to attend; <code>ところを</code> is what you say to one person whose
particular afternoon you are about to eat into. In many sentences either passes, and
choosing the wrong one is not an error a listener would notice.

### ～べきところを vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mark a gap between what should have happened and what did.
`のに` **complains**; <code>べきところを</code> **acknowledges**, and is what you use
about your own failing.

</div>

<pre>
{f|全員|ぜんいん}に{f|確認|かくにん}すべき<b>なのに</b>、どうして{f|連絡|れんらく}しなかったんですか。
You were supposed to check with everyone - why didn't you get in touch?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|全員|ぜんいん}に{f|確認|かくにん}すべき<b>ところを</b>、{f|一部|いちぶ}の{f|担当者|たんとうしゃ}にしか{f|連絡|れんらく}しておりませんでした。
We should have checked with everyone, and in fact only contacted some of those responsible.
</pre>

`のに` puts the speaker outside the failure, looking at it; it is accusing, or at least
disappointed. <code>べきところを</code> puts the speaker inside it and states the lapse
flatly, which is exactly what a written apology needs - no excuses, no emotion, just the
correct standard and the deviation from it. Turn the second sentence into `のに` and you
have someone complaining about themselves, which reads as insincere.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|逃|に}げようとした<b>ところに</b>{f|警官|けいかん}に{f|捕|つか}まえられた。 → ✅ {f|逃|に}げようとした<b>ところを</b>{f|警官|けいかん}に{f|捕|つか}まえられた。
  The moment is what the police officer acted on, so it is the object and takes `を`.
  `に` would need something merely arriving.

- ❌ お{f|忙|いそが}しい<b>のところを</b> → ✅ お{f|忙|いそが}しい<b>ところを</b>
  An い-adjective modifies <code>ところ</code> directly. The `の` belongs only after a
  noun.

- ❌ お{f|休|やす}み<b>ところを</b> → ✅ お{f|休|やす}み<b>のところを</b>
  And the reverse: a noun does need the `の`. Both halves of this pair get written wrong
  about equally often.

- ❌ {f|暇|ひま}な<b>ところを</b>ごめんね、ちょっといい？ → ✅ {f|今|いま}ちょっといい？
  The formula is a piece of deference. Aimed at a friend, and aimed at their *free* time
  at that, it reads as a joke at their expense.

## Practice

**1.** ご{f|多忙|たぼう}の____{f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが、{f|来週|らいしゅう}までにご{f|回答|かいとう}いただけますでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ご{f|多忙|たぼう}の<b>ところを</b>{f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが

<code>{f|多忙|たぼう}</code> is a noun, so it links with `の`. The whole opening is a
standard business-email formula: acknowledge the cost to the reader, then make the
request. <code>ご{f|多忙|たぼう}のところ</code> without the `を` is also correct and very
slightly less formal.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}んでいた（ところに・ところを）、{f|後|うし}ろから{f|肩|かた}を{f|叩|たた}かれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}んでいた<b>ところを</b>、{f|後|うし}ろから{f|肩|かた}を{f|叩|たた}かれた。

Somebody acted on the reader in the middle of their reading, so the moment is the
object: `を`. With `に` the sentence would need something turning up of its own accord,
such as <code>{f|友人|ゆうじん}が{f|訪|たず}ねてきた</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, for an email to a client: "I'm sorry to write to you so late in the evening, but I wanted to report on today's meeting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|夜分|やぶん}{f|遅|おそ}くに{f|失礼|しつれい}いたします。お{f|休|やす}みの<b>ところを</b>{f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが、{f|本日|ほんじつ}の{f|打|う}ち{f|合|あ}わせについてご{f|報告|ほうこく}いたします。

<code>お{f|休|やす}みのところを</code> names the cost to the reader - their evening -
before the request or the message itself, which is the expected order in Japanese
business email. Note that <code>{f|夜分|やぶん}</code> and
<code>{f|本日|ほんじつ}</code> are part of the same register and would look odd next to a
casual ending.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|会議|かいぎ}{f|中|ちゅう}に{f|居|い}{f|眠|ねむ}りしていたところに{f|部長|ぶちょう}に{f|注意|ちゅうい}された。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|会議|かいぎ}{f|中|ちゅう}に{f|居|い}{f|眠|ねむ}りしていた<b>ところを</b>{f|部長|ぶちょう}に{f|注意|ちゅうい}された。

Being told off is something done *to* the sleeping moment, so it takes `を`. This is the
standard shape of the narrative use: an embarrassing activity, the moment marked with
`を`, and a passive main verb that keeps the embarrassed person as the subject.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|本来|ほんらい}なら{f|直接|ちょくせつ}お{f|詫|わ}びに{f|伺|うかが}うべき<b>ところを</b>、{f|書面|しょめん}にて{f|失礼|しつれい}いたします。
(b) {f|本来|ほんらい}なら{f|直接|ちょくせつ}お{f|詫|わ}びに{f|伺|うかが}うべき<b>なのに</b>、{f|書面|しょめん}になってしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a formal written apology. It names the proper course - going in person - and
reports the departure from it without any emotional colouring, then closes with a humble
verb. The reader is left with the impression of someone who knows the standard and is
squarely admitting they have not met it.

(b) leaks feeling. `のに` plus <code>なってしまいました</code> makes it sound as though
circumstances got the better of the writer, and in an apology that reads as
self-justification. The facts are identical; (a) takes responsibility and (b) explains
itself, which is why letters use (a).

</details>
