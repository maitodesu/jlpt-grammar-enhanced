# ～とばかりに

**Meaning** · as if to say ~; with an air that said ~ - the words were never spoken, but the person's manner delivered them

<code>とばかりに</code> is the quotation particle `と` + ばかり + `に`. It puts words
into someone's behaviour: they did not say "now's my chance", but everything about
how they moved said it. This is the language of narration and reportage - novels,
match reports, feature articles. In speech, use
<code>～と{f|言|い}うように</code> or simply describe what happened.

## Formation

What comes before と is a **quotation**: a sentence, a phrase, a command, a single
word. Because it is quoted, no politeness or agreement is required with the rest of
the sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Quoted sentence | plain clause + とばかりに | {f|今|いま}だ → {f|今|いま}だとばかりに |
| Quoted command | imperative + とばかりに | {f|帰|かえ}れ → {f|帰|かえ}れとばかりに |
| Quoted noun + だ | Noun + だ + とばかりに | チャンスだ → チャンスだとばかりに |
| Quoted negative intent | ～まい + とばかりに | {f|逃|のが}すまい → {f|逃|のが}すまいとばかりに |
| Fuller variant | ～と{f|言|い}わんばかりに | {f|帰|かえ}れと{f|言|い}わんばかりに |
| Fixed adverbials | ここぞとばかりに / {f|待|ま}っていましたとばかりに | ここぞとばかりに{f|攻|せ}めた |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>ここぞとばかりに</code> ("seizing the moment", "making the most of
it") has broken free of the pattern and become an ordinary adverb. Adults do say it
in conversation, and you will meet it in blog posts and everyday writing. It is the
one piece of this page you could reasonably produce yourself.

</div>

## Nuance & register

**You will never say this** - with the one exception of
<code>ここぞとばかりに</code>. The pattern belongs to **written description**: a
novelist telling you what a character's body language meant, a journalist
characterising a politician's silence, a match report reading a player's dash.
Spoken Japanese does the same work with
<code>～と{f|言|い}っているような{f|顔|かお}で</code> or by simply reporting the
action.

The pattern always involves a **reading of someone else's behaviour**, which has two
consequences. The subject is normally a third party, and the words in the quotation
are the *writer's* interpretation, not a transcript. If the person actually said the
words, you do not need <code>とばかりに</code> at all - you need と{f|言|い}って.
This is the error to watch for.

There is often a faint edge of criticism or amusement in it. The writer is
attributing an attitude the person did not admit to: opportunism
(<code>ここぞとばかりに</code>), indifference
(<code>{f|関係|かんけい}ないとばかりに</code>), impatience
(<code>{f|帰|かえ}れとばかりに</code>). Neutral behaviour rarely gets described this
way, because there would be nothing to read into it.

<code>と{f|言|い}わんばかりに</code> is the fuller form of the same idea and is
slightly commoner in careful writing. The two are close to interchangeable; if
anything, <code>と{f|言|い}わんばかりに</code> foregrounds that the words were *not*
said, while <code>とばかりに</code> puts more weight on the vigour of the action.

## Contrast

### ～とばかりに vs ～と{f|言|い}わんばかりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nearly the same. <code>と{f|言|い}わんばかりに</code> says
"as if about to say it"; <code>とばかりに</code> says "as if that were the whole
point". The second is punchier and suits sudden action.

</div>

<pre>
まだ{f|足|た}りない<b>と{f|言|い}わんばかりに</b>、{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|資料|しりょう}を{f|見|み}{f|直|なお}した。
She went back over the documents as if to say it still was not enough.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|行|い}くぞ<b>とばかりに</b>、{f|彼|かれ}はかばんをつかんで{f|部屋|へや}を{f|出|で}た。
He grabbed his bag and left the room as though to announce that they were going.
</pre>

Where the sentence describes a **stance held** over some seconds,
<code>と{f|言|い}わんばかりに</code> reads better; where it describes a **single
sharp movement**, <code>とばかりに</code> does. This is a tendency rather than a
rule, and plenty of writers use them interchangeably.

### ～とばかりに vs ～かのように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かのように` compares the whole situation to something imagined.
<code>とばかりに</code> puts specific **words** into someone's manner.

</div>

<pre>
{f|何事|なにごと}もなかった<b>かのように</b>、{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|続|つづ}いた。
The meeting went on as if nothing had happened.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|何|なに}も{f|聞|き}いていない<b>とばかりに</b>、{f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|書類|しょるい}に{f|目|め}を{f|落|お}とした。
The director dropped his eyes to the papers, as if to say he had heard nothing.
</pre>

`かのように` needs no speaker and no attitude - the meeting is not claiming
anything. <code>とばかりに</code> requires a person whose behaviour can be read as a
statement, which is why it always has an animate subject. In modern conversation,
the way to say either of these is
<code>{f|何|なに}も{f|聞|き}こえなかったみたいな{f|顔|かお}で</code>.

### ～とばかりに vs ～んばかりに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とばかりに</code> needs a **quotation and と**.
<code>んばかりに</code> needs a **verb stem**. They are different patterns that
happen to share an ending.

</div>

<pre>
{f|勝|か}った<b>とばかりに</b>、{f|両手|りょうて}を{f|上|あ}げた。
He threw up both arms as though declaring the thing won.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|両手|りょうて}が{f|天井|てんじょう}に{f|届|とど}か<b>んばかりに</b>{f|上|あ}がった。
His arms went up as though they would touch the ceiling.
</pre>

The first reports what the gesture *said*; the second reports what the gesture
*looked like*. The two meet in
<code>と{f|言|い}わんばかりに</code>, which is <code>んばかり</code> attached to
{f|言|い}う and therefore takes a quotation like <code>とばかりに</code>. If you can
hear words in front of it, you need と.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|今|いま}だ<b>ばかりに</b>{f|前|まえ}に{f|出|で}た。 → ✅ {f|今|いま}だ<b>とばかりに</b>{f|前|まえ}に{f|出|で}た。
  The `と` is not optional; without it you have written the N3 pattern
  `ばかりに`, which means "just because", and the sentence stops making sense.

- ❌「{f|帰|かえ}ってください」<b>とばかりに</b>{f|言|い}った。 → ✅「{f|帰|かえ}ってください」と{f|言|い}った。
  If the words were actually spoken, the pattern is wrong by definition.
  <code>とばかりに</code> exists to report what was *not* said.

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>とばかりに</b>{f|空|そら}が{f|暗|くら}くなった。 → ✅ {f|今|いま}にも{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}り{f|出|だ}しそうに{f|空|そら}が{f|暗|くら}くなった。
  Only a person can be read as saying something. The sky has no manner to
  interpret, so the pattern has no subject to work on.

- ❌ {f|逃|のが}す<b>とばかりに</b>{f|追|お}いかけた → ✅ {f|逃|のが}すまい<b>とばかりに</b>{f|追|お}いかけた
  The quotation has to be a complete thought. <code>まい</code> ("I shall not let
  it") supplies one; a bare dictionary form does not.

- ❌（{f|同僚|どうりょう}に）{f|部長|ぶちょう}が{f|早|はや}く{f|帰|かえ}れ<b>とばかりに</b>{f|時計|とけい}を{f|見|み}ていましたよ。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、「{f|早|はや}く{f|帰|かえ}れ」って{f|感|かん}じで{f|時計|とけい}を{f|見|み}てましたよ。
  In conversation, って{f|感|かん}じで does precisely this job, and nobody will
  wonder whether you have been reading too many novels.

## Practice

**1.** {f|試合|しあい}の{f|記事|きじ}: {f|相手|あいて}のミスを{f|見|み}た{f|瞬間|しゅんかん}、____とばかりに{f|一気|いっき}に{f|攻|せ}め{f|込|こ}んだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ここぞ<b>とばかりに</b>（or {f|今|いま}だとばかりに）

Both are fixed and both fit a match report: a mistake by the other side is the
opening, and the team pounces on it. <code>ここぞとばかりに</code> is the phrase to
remember, since it is the only part of this pattern that also lives in ordinary
speech.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|彼|かれ}は{f|天井|てんじょう}に{f|届|とど}か（とばかりに・んばかりに）{f|高|たか}くジャンプした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|届|とど}か<b>んばかりに</b>{f|高|たか}くジャンプした。

{f|届|とど}か is a bare ない-stem, so it takes <code>んばかりに</code>. For
<code>とばかりに</code> you would need something quotable in front of it -
「{f|見|み}ていろ」とばかりに, for instance. The test is whether you can hear words
there.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary spoken Japanese: {f|部長|ぶちょう}は、その{f|話|はなし}はもう{f|終|お}わりだ<b>とばかりに</b>{f|席|せき}を{f|立|た}った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|部長|ぶちょう}、その{f|話|はなし}はもう{f|終|お}わりって{f|感|かん}じで{f|席|せき}を{f|立|た}っちゃったんだよ。

The quotation survives; what changes is how it is attached.
って{f|感|かん}じで is the everyday way of saying "with an air of ~", and the
casual ちゃった matches the register. A slightly more formal spoken version would be
{f|終|お}わりだと{f|言|い}わんばかりの{f|様子|ようす}で.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼女|かのじょ}は「もう{f|知|し}らない」とばかりに{f|大|おお}きな{f|声|こえ}で{f|言|い}った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼女|かのじょ}は「もう{f|知|し}らない」と{f|大|おお}きな{f|声|こえ}で{f|言|い}った。
Or, keeping the pattern: {f|彼女|かのじょ}はもう{f|知|し}らない<b>とばかりに</b>{f|部屋|へや}を{f|出|で}て{f|行|い}った。

The sentence says both that she said the words and that her manner said them for
her. Choose one. <code>とばかりに</code> requires that the words stay unspoken, so
if you keep it, the verb must describe an action rather than an utterance.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}は「もう{f|帰|かえ}る」と{f|言|い}って{f|立|た}ち{f|上|あ}がった。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}はもう{f|帰|かえ}る<b>とばかりに</b>{f|立|た}ち{f|上|あ}がった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a report. He said the words, then he stood up; anyone in the room heard
them, and the sentence commits to nothing beyond that.

(b) says he did **not** speak. The writer is interpreting the way he got to his feet
and telling you what it meant, which means the sentence carries the writer's
judgement as well as the facts - often with a hint that the man was being abrupt.
(b) is also unmistakably written narration; (a) can be said to anybody.

</details>
