# 案の定

**Meaning** · sure enough; just as I was afraid it would be

{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう} reports that a private forecast has come true - and in
almost every sentence a Japanese speaker actually produces, the forecast was a
**bad** one. It is "I knew it", said with a sigh rather than with satisfaction.

## Usage

An adverb. It sits at the head of the clause, or straight after the topic, and the
clause it introduces reports a **finished** outcome, so the verb after it is normally
past tense.

The word is only half of the picture. <code>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</code> is empty
unless the listener already knows what you expected, so it needs an expectation
sitting somewhere in front of it. In practice it arrives in one of three shapes:

- The expectation is spelled out first:
  <code>{f|降|ふ}りそうだと{f|思|おも}っていたら、{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}{f|降|ふ}ってきた。</code>
- Someone ignored a warning:
  <code>やめておけと{f|言|い}ったのに、{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}{f|失敗|しっぱい}した。</code>
- The expectation is obvious to everyone present, so it is left unsaid:
  <code>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}、{f|会場|かいじょう}はがらがらだった。</code>

The kanji spelling is the normal one and far commoner than kana. The `の` in the
middle belongs to the word and never drops, and nothing is ever added after
{f|定|じょう} - no `に`, no `で`, no `だ`.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|嫌|いや}な{f|予感|よかん}がしたが、<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|電車|でんしゃ}は{f|止|と}まっていた。
I had a bad feeling about it, and sure enough the trains had stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|安|やす}い{f|傘|かさ}を{f|買|か}ったら、<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|一日|いちにち}で{f|壊|こわ}れた。
I bought a cheap umbrella and, just as I thought, it broke within a day.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|無理|むり}な{f|日程|にってい}だと{f|何度|なんど}も{f|言|い}ったのに、<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|納期|のうき}に{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わなかった。
I told them over and over that the schedule was impossible, and sure enough we missed the deadline.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|下調|したしら}べもせずに{f|飛|と}び{f|込|こ}みで{f|訪問|ほうもん}した{f|結果|けっか}、<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|担当者|たんとうしゃ}は{f|席|せき}を{f|外|はず}していた。
Having gone in cold with no preparation, we found - predictably enough - that the person in charge was away from her desk.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It is at home in conversation between adults, in a work email, in
a newspaper column and in narration, and it marks nothing about age or gender. It
has a faintly bookish ring next to やっぱり, which is why a spoken complaint often
uses やっぱり and the written-up version of the same complaint uses
{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}.

What is *not* neutral is the polarity. The outcome reported is nearly always
unwelcome: the train stopped, the umbrella broke, the deadline slipped. Native
speakers do occasionally stretch it to a good outcome, but it reads as a joke or as
boasting, and a learner is better off treating the negative bias as fixed. About
your own success it is especially unwise -
{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}{f|合格|ごうかく}した sounds smug in a way
{f|思|おも}った{f|通|とお}り{f|合格|ごうかく}した does not.

The expectation being confirmed is normally the **speaker's own**, or one the speaker
and listener formed together. Reporting that a third party's prediction came true is
the job of <code>{f|予想|よそう}{f|通|どお}り</code>, not of this word.

It is worth keeping apart from てっきり, which a learner meets at the same level and
which does the opposite job: てっきり introduces an assumption that turned out to be
**wrong**. {f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう} is for the forecast that landed, てっきり for the
one that missed.

There is also a mild social edge to it. Aimed at another person's failure -
{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}{f|遅|おく}れてきたね - it says "you did exactly what I
expected of you", which is a reproach. Aimed at the weather, the trains or your own
bad luck it is harmless.

## Contrast

### {f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう} vs やはり・やっぱり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - やはり confirms *any* expectation, good or bad, and does several
other jobs besides. {f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう} does one job only: a bad forecast that
came true.

</div>

<pre>
<b>やっぱり</b>この{f|店|みせ}が{f|一番|いちばん}おいしいね。
This place really is the best after all.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|混|こ}むと{f|思|おも}っていたら、<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|一時間|いちじかん}{f|待|ま}たされた。
I thought it would be busy, and sure enough we were kept waiting an hour.
</pre>

The first sentence cannot take {f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}: nothing has gone wrong.
The second could take やっぱり with no loss of meaning, only a drop in formality -
whenever {f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう} works, やっぱり works too, and not the reverse.
やはり also covers "still, nevertheless" ({f|高|たか}いが、やはり{f|買|か}う) and the
change of mind of やっぱりやめる, neither of which
{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう} can touch.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}に</b>{f|遅|おく}れてきた。 → ✅ <b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|遅|おく}れてきた。
  The word is already adverbial. Adding `に`, `で` or `な` to it is like writing
  "certainlyly".

- ❌ <b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|明日|あした}は{f|混|こ}むだろう。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}{f|混|こ}んでいた。
  It reports a result you can already see. A prediction about tomorrow has not come
  true yet, so there is nothing for it to confirm.

- ❌ {f|宝|たから}くじが<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|当|あ}たった。 → ✅ {f|宝|たから}くじが{f|思|おも}った{f|通|とお}り{f|当|あ}たった。
  Good news breaks the negative bias. Use
  <code>{f|思|おも}った{f|通|とお}り</code> or やっぱり instead.

- ❌ いきなり<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|失敗|しっぱい}しました。 → ✅ {f|危|あぶ}ないと{f|思|おも}っていたのですが、<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|失敗|しっぱい}しました。
  Dropped into a conversation with no expectation behind it, the word has nothing to
  point back at and the listener is left asking "expected by whom?".

## Practice

**1.** {f|準備|じゅんび}が{f|足|た}りないと{f|感|かん}じていたが、____{f|本番|ほんばん}で{f|失敗|しっぱい}してしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|本番|ほんばん}で{f|失敗|しっぱい}してしまった

The first clause supplies the misgiving, the second reports the bad outcome in the
past tense. That is exactly the frame the word needs, and nothing attaches to
{f|定|じょう}.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: {f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}に{f|今度|こんど}の{f|試合|しあい}も{f|負|ま}けるでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}、{f|今度|こんど}の{f|試合|しあい}も{f|負|ま}けてしまった。

Two problems. The `に` after {f|定|じょう} does not belong there, and でしょう makes
this a forecast about a match not yet played. The word can only confirm a result
that has already arrived.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) <b>やっぱり</b>{f|彼|かれ}が{f|選|えら}ばれたね。
(b) <b>{f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう}</b>{f|彼|かれ}が{f|選|えら}ばれたね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is neutral. It says the outcome matched what everyone assumed, and it can be
warm - he deserved it, and we all saw it coming.

(b) sours it. Because {f|案|あん}の{f|定|じょう} carries an expectation of something
going wrong, using it here implies the selection was a foregone conclusion for the
wrong reasons - connections, favouritism, laziness on the committee's part. Said out
loud in front of him, (b) is an insult and (a) is not.

</details>
