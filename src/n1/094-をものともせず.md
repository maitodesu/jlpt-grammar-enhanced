# ～をものともせず

**Meaning** · undaunted by; in defiance of - something that would stop most people simply fails to stop this one, and the sentence admires them for it

<code>ものともしない</code> is "not to treat it as a thing" - not to count it, not
to give it the status of an obstacle. `せず` is the literary negative of `する`
(the same old stem `せ` that gives <code>せざるを{f|得|え}ない</code>), so the whole
phrase means *without making anything of it*. Storm, injury, age, hostile
opinion: whatever is named, the sentence says it did not register.

## Formation

A noun goes in front; a clause is nominalised with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + をものともせず | {f|荒波|あらなみ} → {f|荒波|あらなみ}をものともせず |
| With に | ～をものともせずに | {f|寒|さむ}さをものともせずに |
| Clause | plain form + のをものともせず | {f|足|あし}を{f|痛|いた}めている → {f|足|あし}を{f|痛|いた}めているのをものともせず |
| Modifying a noun | ～をものともしない + Noun | {f|坂|さか}をものともしない{f|足取|あしど}り |
| Ending a sentence | ～をものともしなかった | {f|批判|ひはん}をものともしなかった |
| する irregular stem | **せず**, never ❌しず | ものともせず |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the slot takes **whatever should have stopped them**: weather,
a wound, an illness, a disability, danger, exhaustion, age, or other people's
opposition. The main clause must then say that they went on and, usually, got
somewhere. A sentence that ends in failure contradicts the phrase.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `せず` is the negative of `する` built on the old stem `せ`. ❌`しず` does
not exist. The `に` in <code>ものともせずに</code> is optional and changes nothing.

</div>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; you will almost never need to produce it.** `をものともせず` lives
in **written and broadcast Japanese of a particular, elevated kind**: sports
reporting, obituaries, biographies, award citations, documentary narration, the
paragraph in a company history about the founder. Its `せず` is a literary negative,
and in ordinary conversation the phrase sounds like a caption being read aloud.
Speaking, you would say <code>～に{f|負|ま}けず</code> or simply
「けがをしていたのに{f|頑張|がんば}った」.

The phrase is **praise**, and that is what makes it hard to place. There has to be a
real hardship - a broken foot, a blizzard, eighty-five years, a hostile press - and
there has to be an achievement that the hardship did not prevent. Applied to
something trivial it becomes comic: ❌<code>{f|眠気|ねむけ}をものともせず、テレビを{f|見|み}た</code>
reads as a joke about heroism.

Because it praises, it is **not used of oneself**. 「{f|私|わたし}は{f|困難|こんなん}をものともせず…」
is self-congratulation of a kind Japanese avoids fairly strictly; write
{f|何|なん}とか{f|乗|の}り{f|越|こ}えました instead. The natural subject is somebody
being observed, and the observer is impressed.

## Contrast

### ～をものともせず vs ～にもかかわらず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にもかかわらず` reports that A did not prevent B, and takes no
view. `をものともせず` adds that the subject **did not even flinch**, and hands them
the credit.

</div>

<pre>
{f|大雪|おおゆき}<b>にもかかわらず</b>、{f|列車|れっしゃ}は{f|定刻|ていこく}に{f|到着|とうちゃく}した。
Despite the heavy snow, the train arrived on time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|大雪|おおゆき}<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|運転士|うんてんし}は{f|列車|れっしゃ}を{f|定刻|ていこく}に{f|走|はし}らせた。
Undaunted by the heavy snow, the driver brought the train in on time.
</pre>

`にもかかわらず` is happy with an inanimate subject - the train did it - because it
is only noting a mismatch between two facts. `をものともせず` needs somebody with a
will, since the whole point is that a person refused to be impressed by the
obstacle. This is also the substitution to make whenever you are unsure: it is
neutral, current, and safe in any register.

### ～をものともせず vs ～をよそに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both can take opposition or criticism in the slot, and they
frame it opposite ways. `をものともせず` treats it as an **obstacle overcome** and
approves; `をよそに` treats it as **other people's concern brushed off** and
disapproves.

</div>

<pre>
{f|家族|かぞく}の{f|反対|はんたい}<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|単身|たんしん}で{f|海|うみ}を{f|渡|わた}った。
Undeterred by his family's opposition, he crossed the sea alone.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|家族|かぞく}の{f|反対|はんたい}<b>をよそに</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|単身|たんしん}で{f|海|うみ}を{f|渡|わた}った。
Paying no attention to his family's objections, he crossed the sea alone.
</pre>

The event is identical; the writer's verdict is not. In the first the family were an
obstacle and the man was brave. In the second the family had a point and the man
turned his back on them. Choosing between the two is choosing whose side the
sentence is on, which is precisely why journalists find the pair useful.

### ～をものともせず vs ～に{f|負|ま}けず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same admiration, one written and one sayable.
<code>～に{f|負|ま}けず</code> is ordinary Japanese you can use to a person's face;
`をものともせず` is what the write-up says about them afterwards.

</div>

<pre>
{f|暑|あつ}さに<b>{f|負|ま}けず</b>、{f|毎日|まいにち}よく{f|頑張|がんば}っているね。
You're not letting the heat beat you - you're doing really well.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|記録的|きろくてき}な{f|暑|あつ}さ<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|生徒|せいと}たちは{f|練習|れんしゅう}を{f|続|つづ}けた。
Undeterred by record heat, the students kept up their training.
</pre>

<code>{f|負|ま}けず</code> can be addressed to the person, used of yourself, and put
in a note on a get-well card. `をものともせず` cannot do any of those three. If the
sentence you want has a listener in it, you want <code>{f|負|ま}けず</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|困難|こんなん}をものとも<b>しず</b> → ✅ {f|困難|こんなん}をものとも<b>せず</b>
  `する` uses the old stem `せ` before `ず`. ❌`しず` is not a form of the verb at
  all, and this is the most frequent slip with the pattern.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|病気|びょうき}<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|仕事|しごと}を{f|続|つづ}けました。 → ✅ {f|病気|びょうき}をしましたが、{f|何|なん}とか{f|仕事|しごと}を{f|続|つづ}けました。
  The phrase awards a medal, so awarding it to yourself sounds like boasting. Keep
  it for other people.

- ❌ {f|大雨|おおあめ}<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になった。 → ✅ {f|大雨|おおあめ}のため、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になった。
  The obstacle has to lose. If the rain stopped the match, nothing was made light
  of, and the sentence contradicts itself.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|困難|こんなん}<b>をものともせず</b>。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|困難|こんなん}<b>をものともしなかった</b>。
  `せず` is a connective form and has to lead somewhere. To end a sentence, use
  <code>ものともしなかった</code>, or in the attributive slot
  <code>ものともしない</code>.

## Practice

**1.** {f|強|つよ}い{f|向|む}かい{f|風|かぜ}____、{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|自己|じこ}{f|記録|きろく}を{f|更新|こうしん}した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|強|つよ}い{f|向|む}かい{f|風|かぜ}<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|自己|じこ}{f|記録|きろく}を{f|更新|こうしん}した。

A headwind is a genuine obstacle, the athlete beat it, and the sentence hands her
the credit - all three conditions met. This is the sports-report use the phrase
exists for.

</details>

**2.** Where would you meet this sentence, and how would a friend say it to you?
{f|度重|たびかさ}なる{f|挫折|ざせつ}<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|夢|ゆめ}を{f|追|お}い{f|続|つづ}けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a written profile - a magazine feature, a documentary voice-over, a speech at a
retirement party. It is the register of somebody's life being summed up.

To your face a friend would say
「{f|何度|なんど}{f|失敗|しっぱい}しても{f|諦|あきら}めなかったね」. The admiration is
the same; the ceremony is gone.

</details>

**3.** Choose: {f|住民|じゅうみん}の{f|不安|ふあん}（をものともせず・をよそに）、{f|建設|けんせつ}{f|会社|かいしゃ}は{f|工事|こうじ}を{f|強行|きょうこう}した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|住民|じゅうみん}の{f|不安|ふあん}<b>をよそに</b>、{f|建設|けんせつ}{f|会社|かいしゃ}は{f|工事|こうじ}を{f|強行|きょうこう}した。

{f|強行|きょうこう}した tells you the writer disapproves, and `をよそに` matches that.
`をものともせず` would praise the company for its courage in the face of residents'
fears, which is not what the sentence means.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼|かれ}は{f|寝不足|ねぶそく}をものともしず、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|出|で}た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|寝不足|ねぶそく}<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|出|で}た。

The form is `せず`. Worth adding: a bad night's sleep is thin material for this
phrase, and outside a joke you would more naturally write
「{f|寝不足|ねぶそく}だったが、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|出|で}た」.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|悪天候|あくてんこう}<b>にもかかわらず</b>、{f|捜索|そうさく}は{f|夜通|よどお}し{f|続|つづ}けられた。
(b) {f|悪天候|あくてんこう}<b>をものともせず</b>、{f|隊員|たいいん}たちは{f|夜通|よどお}し{f|捜索|そうさく}を{f|続|つづ}けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) records a fact. The weather was bad and the search continued anyway; the
sentence is passive, has no named agent, and could come from a police statement.

(b) puts people in the frame and salutes them. The weather is presented as something
they refused to count, and the reader is invited to be impressed. It is the
broadcast version of the same night, and it would sound out of place in a neutral
incident report.

</details>
