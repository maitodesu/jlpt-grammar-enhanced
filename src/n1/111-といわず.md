# ～といわず～といわず

**Meaning** · not just A, not just B - the whole of it, with no distinction between the parts

`といわず` comes in pairs, and the pair names two edges of something: two parts of a
body, two ends of a day, two areas of a room. The sentence then reports a state
that ignores the boundary between them. What the pattern really says is **"and
everything in between"** - the two nouns are not a list but a way of covering a
whole surface or a whole stretch of time.

## Formation

Nouns only, and the two should belong to one continuous whole.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (place or part) | N といわず N といわず | {f|壁|かべ}といわず{f|天井|てんじょう}といわず |
| Noun (time) | N といわず N といわず | {f|昼|ひる}といわず{f|夜|よる}といわず |
| Predicate | a state, usually excessive | …{f|泥|どろ}だらけだった |
| Position | before the clause it saturates | {f|腕|うで}といわず{f|足|あし}といわず、{f|傷|きず}がある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - a **single** <code>といわず</code> after a quantity is a different
expression meaning "don't stop at A":
<code>{f|一|ひと}つといわず、{f|二|ふた}つでも{f|三|みっ}つでもどうぞ</code>. The
saturation reading needs the pair.

</div>

## Nuance & register

This belongs to **description**, and description is mostly written. Novels, essays,
travel writing, a vivid report - anywhere a writer is painting a scene. It is not
archaic and a speaker can use it, but it will sound composed rather than casual:
someone complaining about their neighbour's phone calls in this shape is telling a
story, not just grumbling.

What it nearly always describes is **too much of something**, and usually something
unwelcome: dirt, noise, damage, bites, clutter, interruptions. The construction
itself carries a note of "there was no escaping it". That is why a neutral fact
sounds odd in it - 「{f|窓|まど}といわずドアといわず{f|新|あたら}しい」 is strange,
because being new is not a condition that spreads.

The two nouns should be **comparable and part of one whole**. Hands and face; walls
and ceiling; morning and night. Two unrelated things -
❌「{f|犬|いぬ}といわず{f|天気|てんき}といわず」 - produce nothing at all, because
there is no space between them for the pattern to fill.

It shares its shape with the evaluative `といい…といい` and the two are worth
keeping apart deliberately: same slots, same rhythm, and completely different
work.

## Contrast

### ～といわず～といわず vs ～といい～といい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `といい…といい` names two **features** and passes a **verdict**.
`といわず…といわず` names two **points** and reports that a state covered
**everything between them**.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|旅館|りょかん}は{f|部屋|へや}<b>といい</b>{f|風呂|ふろ}<b>といい</b>、{f|手入|てい}れが{f|行|ゆ}き{f|届|とど}いている。
The rooms, the baths - this inn is beautifully kept.
</pre>

<pre>
その{f|空|あ}き{f|家|や}は{f|部屋|へや}<b>といわず</b>{f|廊下|ろうか}<b>といわず</b>、ほこりが{f|積|つ}もっていた。
In that empty house the dust lay thick, in the rooms and along the corridor alike.
</pre>

The first sentence could be answered with "I agree" or "I don't"; it is an opinion.
The second could only be answered with "how awful"; it is a description. A quick
test: if the sentence ends in a judgement word - すばらしい, ひどい,
{f|見事|みごと}だ - you want `といい`.

### ～といわず～といわず vs ～も～も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `も…も` says **both of these**. `といわず…といわず` says **both
of these and everything between**, and adds that the difference between them did
not matter.

</div>

<pre>
{f|手|て}<b>も</b>{f|顔|かお}<b>も</b>{f|真|ま}っ{f|黒|くろ}だった。
His hands and his face were both filthy.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|手|て}<b>といわず</b>{f|顔|かお}<b>といわず</b>{f|真|ま}っ{f|黒|くろ}だった。
He was filthy all over - hands, face, everything.
</pre>

The first counts two things. The second uses two things to stand for the whole
child. This is why `も…も` can take a precise list - 「{f|右手|みぎて}も{f|左手|ひだりて}も」
- and `といわず` cannot: precision is the opposite of what it is for.

### ～といわず～といわず vs ～を{f|問|と}わず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を{f|問|と}わず</code> declares a distinction
**irrelevant**, usually in a rule. `といわず…といわず` reports that a state **ignored
the distinction** in fact.

</div>

<pre>
このイベントは{f|年齢|ねんれい}や{f|経験|けいけん}<b>を{f|問|と}わず</b>ご{f|参加|さんか}いただけます。
Anyone may join this event, regardless of age or experience.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|若|わか}い{f|人|ひと}<b>といわず</b>{f|年配|ねんぱい}の{f|方|かた}<b>といわず</b>、{f|会場|かいじょう}は{f|人|ひと}で{f|埋|う}まっていた。
Young and old alike, the hall was packed.
</pre>

<code>を{f|問|と}わず</code> is administrative language: it sets a policy and belongs
to notices and application forms. `といわず` is descriptive language: it observes
what happened. Neither can take the other's sentence.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|走|はし}る<b>といわず</b>{f|歩|ある}く<b>といわず</b>、{f|足|あし}が{f|痛|いた}い。 → ✅ {f|走|はし}っても{f|歩|ある}いても{f|足|あし}が{f|痛|いた}い。
  Only nouns go in front of `といわず`. For two actions, use `～ても…ても`.

- ❌ {f|昼|ひる}<b>といわず</b>{f|夜|よる}<b>といわず</b>{f|勉強|べんきょう}してください。 → ✅ {f|昼|ひる}<b>といわず</b>{f|夜|よる}<b>といわず</b>{f|勉強|べんきょう}していた。
  The pattern describes a state that came about; it cannot issue an instruction.
  Turned into a request it sounds like a curse.

- ❌ この{f|店|みせ}は{f|味|あじ}<b>といわず</b>{f|接客|せっきゃく}<b>といわず</b>{f|見事|みごと}だ。 → ✅ この{f|店|みせ}は{f|味|あじ}<b>といい</b>{f|接客|せっきゃく}<b>といい</b>{f|見事|みごと}だ。
  Flavour and service are features being appraised, and the sentence ends in a
  verdict. That is `といい`'s job.

- ❌ {f|窓|まど}<b>といわず</b>、{f|部屋|へや}{f|中|じゅう}が{f|汚|よご}れていた。 → ✅ {f|窓|まど}<b>といわず</b>{f|床|ゆか}<b>といわず</b>、{f|部屋|へや}{f|中|じゅう}が{f|汚|よご}れていた。
  A lone `といわず` is read as "not just one window", which is not the meaning
  wanted. The saturation sense needs the pair.

## Practice

**1.** {f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}しの{f|日|ひ}は、{f|玄関|げんかん}____{f|廊下|ろうか}____、{f|段|だん}ボールが{f|山|やま}{f|積|づ}みだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|玄関|げんかん}<b>といわず</b>{f|廊下|ろうか}<b>といわず</b>

Two parts of one flat, and boxes filling the space between them. The predicate
describes an overwhelming state, which is what the pattern wants.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この{f|旅館|りょかん}は{f|食事|しょくじ}（といい・といわず）{f|温泉|おんせん}（といい・といわず）、{f|文句|もんく}のない{f|宿|やど}だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|食事|しょくじ}<b>といい</b>{f|温泉|おんせん}<b>といい</b>

The sentence ends in a verdict on the inn, and meals and baths are features being
appraised rather than places a condition spread across. `といわず` would be
describing something seeping from the dining room into the bath.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "There was sand everywhere in the car, on the seats and in the boot."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|車|くるま}の{f|中|なか}は、シート<b>といわず</b>トランク<b>といわず</b>{f|砂|すな}だらけだった。

English does this with "everywhere … and …"; Japanese names two points and lets the
pattern fill in the rest. だらけ is a natural partner for it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|辺|あた}りは{f|朝|あさ}といわず{f|夜|よる}といわず{f|静|しず}かで、とても{f|住|す}みやすい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|辺|あた}りは{f|朝|あさ}<b>も</b>{f|夜|よる}<b>も</b>{f|静|しず}かで、とても{f|住|す}みやすい。

Quiet is not a condition that spreads and overwhelms. The pattern needs a state
there is too much of; for a pleasant fact that holds at both times, `も…も` is the
natural choice.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}の{f|服|ふく}は{f|袖|そで}も{f|襟|えり}も{f|汚|よご}れていた。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}の{f|服|ふく}は{f|袖|そで}といわず{f|襟|えり}といわず{f|汚|よご}れていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) inspects two named parts and finds both dirty. Someone could reply that the
front was clean.

(b) uses the cuffs and the collar as evidence that the whole garment was filthy.
The claim covers everything in between, and the speaker sounds rather more appalled
than in (a).

</details>
