# ～とあって

**Meaning** · because it is (a special occasion); given a circumstance this out of the ordinary, naturally ~

`とあって` names a cause and then shows what visibly came of it. The cause is never
ordinary - a public holiday, a rare visitor, a first in ten years - and the clause
after it reports the scene that resulted. It is a reporter's expression: you are
describing what happened and telling the reader what produced it.

## Formation

The clause in front is in plain form; nouns and な-adjectives attach bare, with no `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | noun + とあって | {f|連休|れんきゅう} → {f|連休|れんきゅう}とあって |
| Noun (stiff written) | noun + である + とあって | {f|新人|しんじん} → {f|新人|しんじん}であるとあって |
| な-adjective | stem + とあって | {f|有名|ゆうめい} → {f|有名|ゆうめい}とあって |
| い-adjective | plain form + とあって | {f|珍|めずら}しい → {f|珍|めずら}しいとあって |
| Verb | plain form + とあって | {f|出|で}る → {f|出|で}るとあって |
| Verb (past) | ～た + とあって | {f|優勝|ゆうしょう}した → {f|優勝|ゆうしょう}したとあって |
| Conditional relatives | ～とあっては / ～とあれば | {f|頼|たの}み → {f|頼|たの}みとあっては |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the second clause has to be a **statement of fact**, usually in
the past or in the present-progressive of a scene being observed. A request, an
invitation, a command or the speaker's own plan cannot follow `とあって`.

</div>

## Nuance & register

`とあって` belongs to **written and reported Japanese**: newspaper copy, television
news, magazine features, event write-ups. It is not rude or archaic, and an
announcer says it without a second thought, but you will rarely hear it across a
dinner table. Among friends the same thought comes out as `連休だから`.

Two things it always assumes. First, that the circumstance is **worth remarking on**
- something special, unusual or newsworthy. Nobody writes
<code>{f|水曜日|すいようび}とあって</code>. Second, that the result is a **natural,
almost predictable consequence** the reader will accept without argument: a holiday
means crowds, a famous guest means a queue, heavy rain means cancellation. If the
reader has to be talked into the connection, `とあって` is the wrong tool.

Because it is descriptive, the pattern sits badly with the speaker's own small
affairs. <code>{f|頭|あたま}が{f|痛|いた}いとあって</code> reads as though your headache
had been reported in the paper. Third-person subjects, public events and observed
scenes are its natural home; a first-person subject is possible only when you are
narrating yourself from the outside, as in a memoir.

`とあって` is also fixed in tense in a way learners miss: it does not itself carry
tense. <code>{f|優勝|ゆうしょう}したとあって</code> and
<code>{f|優勝|ゆうしょう}するとあって</code> differ, but the `とあって` never becomes
❌<code>とあった</code>. The final verb of the sentence carries the tense.

## Contrast

### ～とあって vs ～ので・～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ので` and `から` join any cause to any result, including your
own plans. `とあって` needs a **notable circumstance** and reports an **observed
outcome**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|連休|れんきゅう}<b>だから</b>、{f|明日|あした}は{f|海|うみ}へ{f|行|い}こう。
It's a long weekend, so let's go to the sea tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|連休|れんきゅう}<b>とあって</b>、{f|海沿|うみぞ}いの{f|道|みち}は{f|昼前|ひるまえ}から{f|渋滞|じゅうたい}していた。
With the long weekend on, the coast road was jammed from before noon.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence collapses: ❌<code>{f|連休|れんきゅう}とあって、{f|海|うみ}へ{f|行|い}こう</code>
puts a proposal where the pattern demands a report. The second sentence works with
`だから` too - it simply loses the flavour of a scene being written up for a reader.

### ～とあって vs ～だけあって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけあって` **praises**: the result lives up to what the cause
promised. `とあって` only explains, and passes no judgement at all.

</div>

<pre>
{f|一流|いちりゅう}のホテル<b>だけあって</b>、サービスが{f|行|い}き{f|届|とど}いている。
It's a first-class hotel, and the service shows it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一流|いちりゅう}のホテルが{f|会場|かいじょう}<b>とあって</b>、{f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}みは{f|定員|ていいん}の{f|三倍|さんばい}に{f|達|たっ}した。
With a first-class hotel as the venue, applications ran to three times the number of places.
</pre>

`だけあって` measures a result against a standard and finds it worthy, so it needs
something praiseworthy on both sides. `とあって` is indifferent: heavy rain, a
scandal and a royal visit are all equally good causes for it.

### ～とあって vs ～とあっては・～とあれば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とあって` reports a fact that **has** produced a result.
`とあっては` and `とあれば` are conditionals: **if** it is a case of ~, then this
follows - and what follows is usually the speaker's own decision.

</div>

<pre>
{f|先生|せんせい}の{f|頼|たの}み<b>とあっては</b>、{f|断|ことわ}るわけにはいかない。
If it's my teacher asking, I can hardly refuse.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|先生|せんせい}が{f|来|こ}られる<b>とあって</b>、{f|部屋|へや}は{f|前日|ぜんじつ}から{f|片付|かたづ}けてあった。
Because the teacher was coming, the room had been tidied since the day before.
</pre>

This is where the ban on volitional endings dissolves: `とあっては` and `とあれば`
exist precisely to introduce what the speaker will therefore do. If your sentence
ends in <code>～しかない</code>, <code>～わけにはいかない</code> or a decision, you want
the conditional form, not bare `とあって`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|熱|ねつ}がある<b>とあって</b>、{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|休|やす}んだ。 → ✅ {f|熱|ねつ}があった<b>ので</b>、{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|休|やす}んだ。
  A private, everyday reason is too small for `とあって`. The pattern wants a
  circumstance a stranger would find worth reporting.

- ❌ {f|連休|れんきゅう}<b>とあって</b>、{f|温泉|おんせん}にでも{f|行|い}きませんか。 → ✅ {f|連休|れんきゅう}<b>だし</b>、{f|温泉|おんせん}にでも{f|行|い}きませんか。
  The second clause must state what happened, not propose what to do.

- ❌ {f|有名|ゆうめい}な{f|店|みせ}だ<b>とあって</b>、{f|行列|ぎょうれつ}ができていた。 → ✅ {f|有名|ゆうめい}な{f|店|みせ}<b>とあって</b>、{f|行列|ぎょうれつ}ができていた。
  Nouns and な-adjectives attach with nothing between them and `とあって`. The stiff
  written alternative is <code>である</code>, never `だ`.

- ❌ {f|開店|かいてん}{f|初日|しょにち}<b>とあった</b>ので、{f|店内|てんない}は{f|混|こ}んでいた。 → ✅ {f|開店|かいてん}{f|初日|しょにち}<b>とあって</b>、{f|店内|てんない}は{f|混|こ}んでいた。
  `とあって` is already a connective. It does not conjugate and does not take another
  reason word after it.

## Practice

**1.** {f|大雪|おおゆき}の{f|予報|よほう}が{f|出|で}た____、スーパーの{f|棚|たな}は{f|夕方|ゆうがた}にはほとんど{f|空|から}になっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|予報|よほう}が{f|出|で}た<b>とあって</b>

A snow warning is a public, newsworthy circumstance, and empty shelves are the scene
it produced - exactly the report shape `とあって` is built for. The verb in front
stays plain past.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|世界|せかい}{f|的|てき}な{f|建築家|けんちくか}が{f|設計|せっけい}した（とあって・だけあって）、{f|建物|たてもの}のどこを{f|見|み}ても{f|美|うつく}しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|設計|せっけい}した<b>だけあって</b>

The sentence is admiring the building, and praise that lives up to a reputation is
`だけあって`. `とあって` would be possible only with a factual outcome instead, such
as crowds of visitors.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It was the first game of the season, and the stadium was full an hour before kick-off."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|今季|こんき}{f|最初|さいしょ}の{f|試合|しあい}<b>とあって</b>、スタジアムは{f|開始|かいし}{f|一時間|いちじかん}{f|前|まえ}には{f|満員|まんいん}になっていた。

The English is a piece of sports reporting, which is `とあって` at its most natural.
The noun attaches directly, and the outcome clause is a plain observed fact.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|社長|しゃちょう}のご{f|指名|しめい}とあって、お{f|受|う}けするしかありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|社長|しゃちょう}のご{f|指名|しめい}<b>とあっては</b>、お{f|受|う}けするしかありません。

The ending is the speaker's own decision, so the conditional `とあっては` is required.
Bare `とあって` can only be followed by a report of what happened.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}り{f|出|だ}した<b>ので</b>、{f|花火大会|はなびたいかい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になった。
(b) {f|台風|たいふう}の{f|接近|せっきん}{f|中|ちゅう}<b>とあって</b>、{f|花火大会|はなびたいかい}は{f|早々|そうそう}に{f|中止|ちゅうし}が{f|決|き}まった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is neutral information. It could be said to a friend, written in a diary, or
posted in a group chat, and it makes no claim that the cause was remarkable.

(b) reads like a news item. The typhoon is presented as a circumstance of public
significance, and the cancellation as the outcome any reader would expect from it.
Said casually to a friend, (b) would sound as though you were narrating your own
evening for television.

</details>
