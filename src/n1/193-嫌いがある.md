# ～嫌いがある

**Meaning** · has an unfortunate tendency to ~ - names a standing fault in a person, an organisation or a piece of work, and names it politely

Read <code>きらいがある</code>. The <code>{f|嫌|きら}い</code> in it is not the everyday
"dislike": it is an older noun meaning a leaning or a bias, and the pattern reports
that leaning as a **defect**. This is the verdict of a book review, an editorial or a
performance appraisal - measured, indirect, and never a compliment.

## Formation

<code>きらい</code> is a noun, so whatever comes in front of it is in noun-modifying
shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain non-past) | ～る + きらいがある | {f|急|いそ}ぐ → {f|結論|けつろん}を{f|急|いそ}ぐきらいがある |
| Verb (negative) | ～ない + きらいがある | {f|聞|き}かない → {f|人|ひと}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}かないきらいがある |
| Verb + すぎる (the commonest shape) | ～すぎる + きらいがある | {f|頼|たよ}りすぎる → {f|機械|きかい}に{f|頼|たよ}りすぎるきらいがある |
| い-adjective | ～い + きらいがある | {f|難|むずか}しい → {f|表現|ひょうげん}が{f|難|むずか}しいきらいがある |
| な-adjective | ～な + きらいがある | {f|感情的|かんじょうてき}な → {f|感情的|かんじょうてき}なきらいがある |
| Noun | ～の + きらいがある | {f|独断|どくだん} → {f|独断|どくだん}のきらいがある |
| Polite | ～きらいがあります | {f|評価|ひょうか}が{f|甘|あま}くなるきらいがあります |
| Softened, as it usually is | やや／{f|少|すこ}し／どうも + … | やや{f|慎重|しんちょう}すぎるきらいがある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - both spellings are current. Newspapers and formal prose often write it in
kana, <code>きらいがある</code>, precisely so that no reader trips over
<code>{f|嫌|きら}い</code> and reads "dislike". The kanji
<code>{f|嫌|きら}いがある</code> is equally correct.

</div>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>
{f|当社|とうしゃ}の{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|結論|けつろん}を{f|先|さき}に{f|延|の}ばす<b>きらいがあります</b>。

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

It is said that the more confident an engineer is, the more they tend to make light of basic checks.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not reach for it.** It lives in written evaluation - book
and film reviews, newspaper editorials, academic criticism, personnel appraisals,
committee reports - and in the spoken register that borrows from them: a lecture, a
formal meeting, a broadcast commentary. In ordinary conversation it sounds like
someone reading out of a report. The everyday ways to say the same thing are
<code>～{f|傾向|けいこう}がある</code>, `～がちだ`, <code>～すぎる</code> and
<code>～ところがある</code>, and a learner should be producing those.

Two hard restrictions come with it.

**The tendency must be a fault.** The pattern carries the criticism by itself, so
the content has to be something the speaker disapproves of. ❌
<code>{f|彼|かれ}は{f|人|ひと}を{f|助|たす}けるきらいがある</code> is not a
compliment gone wrong - it is simply broken, because nothing about helping people
can be read as a defect. Where the English "tends to" is neutral,
<code>きらいがある</code> never is.

**The tendency must be habitual.** It describes a disposition that shows up again
and again, not one occasion. A single overlong meeting last Tuesday cannot have
<code>きらいがあった</code>; it was just long.

Almost every real example is hedged - やや, {f|少|すこ}し, どうも,
{f|一部|いちぶ}に. That is not decoration. The pattern is how Japanese delivers
criticism without pointing: the fault is attributed to a leaning rather than to a
person's will, and the hedge lets the reader decide how hard to take it. Delivered
unhedged about a named individual it is quite cutting, in the way a flat sentence in
a written appraisal is cutting.

## Contrast

### ～きらいがある vs ～{f|傾向|けいこう}がある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|傾向|けいこう}がある</code> is the **neutral** word and
takes good tendencies, bad tendencies and statistics alike.
<code>きらいがある</code> is a **verdict** and only takes faults.

</div>

<pre>
{f|近年|きんねん}、{f|若者|わかもの}の{f|読書|どくしょ}{f|時間|じかん}は{f|増|ふ}える<b>{f|傾向|けいこう}にある</b>。
In recent years young people's reading time has been on an increasing trend.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}の{f|文章|ぶんしょう}は{f|専門|せんもん}{f|用語|ようご}に{f|走|はし}る<b>きらいがある</b>。
His writing has a tendency to lapse into jargon.
</pre>

Swap them and the first sentence becomes an accusation against reading, while the
second loses its point: the writer is not reporting a trend, he is complaining. Note
also that <code>{f|傾向|けいこう}</code> works for populations and numbers -
<code>{f|増|ふ}える{f|傾向|けいこう}にある</code>, <code>{f|傾向|けいこう}が{f|強|つよ}い</code> - where
<code>きらいがある</code> wants a person, an institution, or a piece of work with an
author behind it.

### ～きらいがある vs ～がち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～がち` says the thing **happens often**.
<code>きらいがある</code> says the subject **is inclined that way**, and adds that the
speaker thinks it is a flaw.

</div>

<pre>
{f|最近|さいきん}、{f|朝食|ちょうしょく}を{f|抜|ぬ}き<b>がち</b>だ。
I've been skipping breakfast a lot lately.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの{f|部署|ぶしょ}は{f|前例|ぜんれい}を{f|重|おも}んじすぎる<b>きらいがある</b>。
Our department has a tendency to put too much weight on precedent.
</pre>

`がち` is everyday speech, attaches to a ます-stem or a noun
({f|病気|びょうき}がち, {f|留守|るす}がち, {f|忘|わす}れがち), and describes frequency -
including your own, which is why 「{f|遅|おく}れがちです」 is an ordinary thing to say
about yourself. <code>きらいがある</code> takes a whole clause, describes character
rather than frequency, and used about yourself sounds like a mock self-assessment.

### ～きらいがある vs ～おそれがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal <code>{f|名詞|めいし}＋がある</code> frames, and
they are about different times. <code>きらいがある</code> = a **standing disposition**,
already visible. <code>おそれがある</code> = a **specific bad outcome** that might yet
happen.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|台風|たいふう}は{f|上陸|じょうりく}する<b>おそれがある</b>。
There is a risk that this typhoon will make landfall.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|制度|せいど}は{f|手続|てつづ}きが{f|複雑|ふくざつ}になりすぎる<b>きらいがある</b>。
This system has a tendency to let its procedures get too complicated.
</pre>

Weather, accidents and side effects take <code>おそれがある</code>; they are events,
not character. Anything you could put in a written appraisal takes
<code>きらいがある</code>. The clearest test is the tense you could add: an
<code>おそれ</code> is about the future and can be averted, while a
<code>きらい</code> is already there in the record.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雪|ゆき}になる<b>きらいがある</b>。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雪|ゆき}になる<b>おそれがある</b>。
  A weather forecast is a future event, not an ingrained failing. Nature has no
  disposition to be criticised.

- ❌ {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|人|ひと}に{f|優|やさ}しくする<b>きらいがある</b>。 → ✅ {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|人|ひと}に{f|優|やさ}しくする<b>{f|傾向|けいこう}がある</b>。
  The content has to be a fault. With a virtue inside it, the sentence does not read
  as faint praise - it simply fails.

- ❌ {f|独断|どくだん}<b>きらいがある</b> → ✅ {f|独断|どくだん}<b>のきらいがある</b>
  <code>きらい</code> is a noun. A noun in front of it needs `の`, and a な-adjective
  needs `な`.

- ❌ {f|昨日|きのう}の{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|長|なが}すぎる<b>きらいがあった</b>。 → ✅ {f|昨日|きのう}の{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|長|なが}すぎた。
  One meeting is one event. Use the pattern only where you could add
  <code>いつも</code> or <code>どの{f|会議|かいぎ}も</code>.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|人|ひと}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}かない<b>きらいだ</b>。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|人|ひと}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}かない<b>きらいがある</b>。
  The expression is fixed with <code>がある</code>. Ending in
  <code>きらいだ</code> reads as the ordinary adjective "dislikes", which turns the
  sentence into nonsense.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|辞典|じてん}は{f|語源|ごげん}の{f|説明|せつめい}に{f|紙面|しめん}を{f|使|つか}いすぎる____があり、{f|用例|ようれい}が{f|少|すく}ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|使|つか}いすぎる<b>きらい</b>があり

A book review, a criticised excess, and a hedged verdict - this is the pattern's
native habitat. Note the continuative <code>きらいがあり</code>, which is how it
usually joins to a second clause in written Japanese.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|大雨|おおあめ}が{f|続|つづ}けば、{f|川|かわ}が{f|増水|ぞうすい}する（きらいがある・おそれがある）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|川|かわ}が{f|増水|ぞうすい}する<b>おそれがある</b>。

A river rising is a future event with a risk attached, so it takes
<code>おそれがある</code>. <code>きらいがある</code> would accuse the river of a
character flaw, which is why it reads as unintentionally comic.

</details>

**3.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence, and how would you say it to a friend? {f|彼|かれ}は{f|物事|ものごと}を{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|抱|かか}え{f|込|こ}む<b>きらいがある</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In writing that evaluates someone: a performance review, a reference, a character
sketch in an essay, a newspaper profile. Spoken, it belongs to a formal meeting.

To a friend you would say <code>{f|彼|かれ}って{f|何|なん}でも{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|抱|かか}え{f|込|こ}むところがあるよね</code>
or <code>{f|抱|かか}え{f|込|こ}みがちだよね</code>. Both carry the same observation
without sounding as though you were reading from a file on him.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|新|あたら}しい{f|案|あん}は{f|説明|せつめい}{f|不足|ぶそく}きらいがあるので、{f|資料|しりょう}を{f|足|た}したほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|新|あたら}しい{f|案|あん}は{f|説明|せつめい}{f|不足|ぶそく}<b>のきらいがある</b>ので、{f|資料|しりょう}を{f|足|た}したほうがいい。
"The new proposal tends to be under-explained, so it would be better to add some material."

<code>{f|説明|せつめい}{f|不足|ぶそく}</code> is a noun, and a noun links to the noun
<code>きらい</code> with `の`. Note the reading: the rendaku form is
<code>ぶそく</code>, not ふそく.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|細部|さいぶ}にこだわる<b>{f|傾向|けいこう}がある</b>。
(b) {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|細部|さいぶ}にこだわる<b>きらいがある</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral observation about how Tanaka works. Depending on the job, an
attention to detail may be exactly what the sentence is praising.

(b) has already decided. The same behaviour is being filed as a fault - he gets
stuck on detail - and the register places the judgement in a written assessment
rather than in conversation. If you are not sure you want to criticise him, (b) is
the wrong sentence.

</details>
