# ～てやまない

**Meaning** · never stop feeling it - a wish, a hope or an affection presented as unending. Formal and literary; it is written on the page far more often than it is said out loud.

The やまない is the negative of {f|止|や}む, "to cease", so the shape is literally
"and it does not stop". What stops being a plain statement of fact is the
register: <code>～てやまない</code> is the language of speeches, prefaces,
congratulatory letters and obituaries. It also attaches to a **very small set of
verbs** - wishing, praying, loving, respecting, hoping - and not to actions in
general.

## Formation

The verb goes into the て-form and やまない follows it directly. It is normally
written in kana; the kanji spelling {f|止|や}まない is possible but uncommon.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (emotion, う-verb) | て-form + やまない | {f|願|ねが}う → {f|願|ねが}ってやまない |
| Verb (emotion, する) | して + やまない | {f|尊敬|そんけい}する → {f|尊敬|そんけい}してやまない |
| Polite | ～てやみません | {f|祈|いの}る → {f|祈|いの}ってやみません |
| Past | ～てやまなかった | {f|愛|あい}する → {f|愛|あい}してやまなかった |
| Before a noun | ～てやまない + noun | {f|愛|あい}してやまない{f|故郷|こきょう} |
| Not possible | ordinary action verb | ❌{f|食|た}べてやまない |
| Not possible | negative before it | ❌{f|願|ねが}わなくてやまない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verbs that really occur here can almost be listed:
{f|願|ねが}う, {f|祈|いの}る, {f|愛|あい}する, {f|尊敬|そんけい}する,
{f|期待|きたい}する, {f|支持|しじ}する, {f|求|もと}める. Anything outside that
band sounds like a mistake rather than a stylistic choice.

</div>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

{f|生涯|しょうがい}にわたって{f|師|し}を{f|尊敬|そんけい}し<b>てやまなかった</b>と、{f|弟子|でし}は{f|静|しず}かに{f|語|かた}った。

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this one; do not reach for it.** <code>～てやまない</code> belongs to
written and ceremonial Japanese: the closing line of a formal letter, a speech at
a retirement party, a eulogy, the blurb on a book jacket, a newspaper column. If
you say it to a friend about a film you like, you have quoted a book jacket at
them. The everyday way to say the same thing is
<code>{f|心|こころ}から{f|願|ねが}っています</code>,
<code>ずっと{f|好|す}きです</code> or simply
<code>{f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|大好|だいす}きだ</code> - use one of those and reserve
<code>～てやまない</code> for reading.

Two live contexts are worth knowing. The first is the **fixed closing formula**:
<code>ご{f|健康|けんこう}をお{f|祈|いの}りしております</code> is the ordinary
version, and <code>～を{f|祈|いの}ってやみません</code> is the raised, more
emotional one, used when the speaker wants the sentiment to sound heartfelt rather
than routine. The second is the phrase
<code>{f|愛|あい}してやまない</code> before a noun, which has spread well beyond
formal prose into food writing, travel writing and advertising -
<code>{f|地元|じもと}の{f|人|ひと}が{f|愛|あい}してやまない{f|一杯|いっぱい}</code>.
That single collocation is common in light journalism; the rest of the pattern
is not.

The polite form is <code>～てやみません</code>, and in this pattern the polite
form is if anything **more** frequent than the plain one, because the situations
that call for it are formal ones. There is no casual contraction, because there is
no casual use.

## Contrast

### ～てやまない vs {f|心|こころ}から～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these say the same thing. <code>{f|心|こころ}から</code> +
ordinary verb is what you should actually produce;
<code>～てやまない</code> is the ceremonial edition of it.

</div>

<pre>
ご{f|成功|せいこう}を{f|心|こころ}から<b>{f|願|ねが}っています</b>。
I really hope it goes well for you.
</pre>

<pre>
ご{f|成功|せいこう}を{f|願|ねが}っ<b>てやみません</b>。
It is our sincere and lasting wish that you succeed.
</pre>

The first can be said to a colleague at the door. The second is read from a sheet
of paper at a podium, or printed at the bottom of a letter. Nothing in the meaning
separates them - only the setting, and the setting is the whole point.

### ～てやまない vs ～てならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～てやまない</code> is a feeling that **continues**, and
the speaker is content to have it. <code>～てならない</code> is a feeling that
**overwhelms**, and the speaker cannot push it away.

</div>

<pre>
{f|息子|むすこ}の{f|将来|しょうらい}が{f|心配|しんぱい}<b>でならない</b>。
I can't stop worrying about my son's future.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|息子|むすこ}の{f|幸|しあわ}せを{f|願|ねが}っ<b>てやまない</b>。
I wish for my son's happiness, and always will.
</pre>

<code>ならない</code> takes anxiety, irritation, sadness, suspicion - things that
press on you. <code>やまない</code> takes hopes and affections. Swap them and both
sentences go strange: a wish is not something you struggle to suppress, and worry
is not something you would announce in a congratulatory speech.

### ～てやまない vs ～{f|続|つづ}ける

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～{f|続|つづ}ける</code> is neutral continuation and
works with any verb. <code>～てやまない</code> is not really about duration at
all; it is a way of saying the feeling is deep.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|三十年|さんじゅうねん}{f|同|おな}じ{f|絵|え}を{f|描|か}き<b>{f|続|つづ}けた</b>。
He went on painting the same picture for thirty years.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}が{f|愛|あい}し<b>てやまなかった</b>のは、その{f|一枚|いちまい}の{f|絵|え}だった。
The thing he loved above all else was that one painting.
</pre>

Only <code>{f|続|つづ}ける</code> can carry a stretch of time
({f|三十年|さんじゅうねん}) and only it works on a physical action like
{f|描|か}く. If you want to say someone kept doing something,
<code>～{f|続|つづ}ける</code> is the pattern; <code>～てやまない</code> would be
both ungrammatical and pompous.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|走|はし}っ<b>てやまない</b>。 → ✅ {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|走|はし}り<b>{f|続|つづ}けている</b>。
  Running is an action, not an emotion. The pattern only takes verbs of wishing,
  praying, loving, respecting and hoping.

- ❌ {f|願|ねが}い<b>てやまない</b> → ✅ {f|願|ねが}っ<b>てやまない</b>
  The base is the て-form, not the ます-stem.

- ❌ {f|平和|へいわ}を{f|願|ねが}って{f|止|と}まらない → ✅ {f|平和|へいわ}を{f|願|ねが}っ<b>てやまない</b>
  The verb inside is {f|止|や}む, not {f|止|と}まる. Reading it as とまらない
  produces a different sentence ("cannot stop wishing", which is
  <code>{f|願|ねが}わずにいられない</code>).

- ❌ このケーキ、{f|愛|あい}し<b>てやまない</b>んだよね。 → ✅ このケーキ、{f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|大好|だいす}きなんだよね。
  Attaching a casual <code>んだよね</code> to a ceremonial phrase is the mismatch
  learners produce most. Say the plain thing in conversation.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this sentence? {f|故人|こじん}が{f|愛|あい}し<b>てやまなかった</b>{f|庭|にわ}は、{f|今|いま}も{f|手|て}{f|入|い}れが{f|続|つづ}いている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In writing - a memorial notice, a newspaper feature, a printed programme, or a
line of narration in a novel. The vocabulary
({f|故人|こじん}) and the pattern together mark it as prepared, respectful prose.
Nobody produces this sentence in conversation.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: {f|皆様|みなさま}のご{f|活躍|かつやく}を{f|期待|きたい}し<b>てやみません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|皆様|みなさま}の{f|活躍|かつやく}を{f|心|こころ}から{f|期待|きたい}しています。

<code>{f|心|こころ}から</code> plus a plain polite verb carries the same warmth
without the podium. This is the substitution to reach for whenever you feel
tempted by the pattern.

</details>

**3.** Choose: {f|最近|さいきん}、{f|夜|よる}の{f|音|おと}が{f|気|き}になっ（てやまない・てならない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|気|き}になっ<b>てならない</b>。

The sentence describes something that bothers the speaker and will not go away.
<code>ならない</code> takes that kind of pressing, unwanted feeling;
<code>やまない</code> takes hopes and affections, and would make the noise sound
like something cherished.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|毎晩|まいばん}{f|資料|しりょう}を{f|読|よ}んでやまない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|毎晩|まいばん}{f|資料|しりょう}を{f|読|よ}み<b>{f|続|つづ}けている</b>。
"The department head goes on reading documents every night."

Reading files is an action, so it needs
<code>～{f|続|つづ}ける</code>. The pattern in the question also clashes with
{f|毎晩|まいばん}: <code>～てやまない</code> does not take an adverb of frequency,
because it is not describing repetition.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in register:
(a) ご{f|家族|かぞく}の{f|幸|しあわ}せを{f|祈|いの}っています。
(b) ご{f|家族|かぞく}の{f|幸|しあわ}せを{f|祈|いの}っ<b>てやみません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is ordinary polite Japanese. You could say it at the end of a phone call, write
it in a message, or add it to a card. It sounds warm and unremarkable.

(b) raises the same sentiment to formal written style. It fits the closing line of
a letter of condolence or congratulation, or a speech; delivered in casual
conversation it sounds theatrical, as though the speaker were reading from a
prepared text. The propositional content is identical - only the occasion differs.

</details>
