# もはや

**Meaning** · by now; at this point; (with a negative) no longer

`もはや` marks a **threshold that has been crossed**. Something has changed so far
that the old description has stopped fitting: it is too late, it cannot be undone, or
the thing has simply become something else. English reaches for "by now", "at this
point" or "no longer" depending on which side of the change is in view.

## Usage

An adverb. It stands in front of the predicate, and it works in two frames.

**Negative - "no longer, not any more."** This is the commoner one, and the collocations
are fixed enough to learn as units:
もはや{f|手|て}{f|遅|おく}れだ,
もはや{f|疑|うたが}いの{f|余地|よち}はない,
もはや{f|後戻|あともど}りはできない,
もはや{f|議論|ぎろん}の{f|余地|よち}はない,
もはや<code>～とは{f|言|い}えない</code>. The negative may be a plain ない, a
できない, or a noun of impossibility.

**Affirmative - "at this point it is simply X."** Here `もはや` sits in front of a noun
or in front of a claim about what category the thing now belongs to: もはや{f|別人|べつじん}だ, もはや{f|芸術|げいじゅつ}だ,
もはや{f|趣味|しゅみ}の{f|域|いき}を{f|超|こ}えている,
もはや{f|常識|じょうしき}と{f|言|い}っていい. The claim is always that the thing has
outgrown its previous label.

What `もはや` is *not* is a word for elapsed time. It never means "soon", it never
means "another one", and it does not ask whether a task is finished. Those are all
`もう`, and the two are not interchangeable in either direction.

The kanji spelling <code>{f|最早|もはや}</code> exists and turns up in older prose; kana
is standard now.

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`もはや` belongs to **written and formal Japanese**: editorials, essays, reports,
speeches, novels and news copy. It also carries emphasis and a certain amount of
feeling - resignation in the negative frame, mild astonishment in the affirmative one -
which is why it is the word an editorial reaches for when `もう` would be too flat.

In ordinary conversation the neutral choice is `もう`. もう{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わない
is what a colleague says; もはや{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わない is what the write-up of the
incident says. Using `もはや` in relaxed speech is not an error, but it is audibly
dramatic, and Japanese speakers know it.

That drama is exactly what has made the affirmative frame popular in casual writing
online and among younger speakers: 「これもはやスープじゃなくてソースだよ」. The
overblown register is the joke, and the speaker means it to be heard. It is a real,
current usage - but it depends on the listener recognising the formality being played
with, so it is not a safe first move for a learner.

`もはや` has no politeness of its own and combines happily with ます/です:
もはや{f|説明|せつめい}の{f|必要|ひつよう}はございません. It marks nothing about the
speaker's age or gender.

## Contrast

### もはや vs もう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `もう` is the everyday word and covers four jobs: already, no
longer, soon, and one more. `もはや` covers exactly one of them - **no longer / at
this point** - in a formal register, with a note of finality.

</div>

<pre>
{f|昼|ひる}ご{f|飯|はん}は<b>もう</b>{f|食|た}べましたか。
Have you had lunch already?
</pre>

<pre>
コーヒーを<b>もう</b>{f|一杯|いっぱい}いただけますか。
Could I have one more coffee?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|状況|じょうきょう}はここまで{f|悪化|あっか}しており、<b>もはや</b>{f|楽観|らっかん}はできない。
The situation has deteriorated to this point, and optimism is no longer possible.
</pre>

The first two sentences cannot take `もはや` at all - it has no "already?" and no
"one more". The third could take `もう`, and the sentence would still be correct; what
it would lose is the sense that a line has been crossed and the previous stance has
to be abandoned. That sense is the whole of what `もはや` adds.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ビールを<b>もはや</b>{f|一本|いっぽん}ください。 → ✅ ビールを<b>もう</b>{f|一本|いっぽん}ください。
  `もはや` has no "another, one more" sense. Only `もう` counts.

- ❌ {f|宿題|しゅくだい}は<b>もはや</b>{f|終|お}わりましたか。 → ✅ {f|宿題|しゅくだい}は<b>もう</b>{f|終|お}わりましたか。
  Asking whether something is done is `もう`. `もはや` asserts a state of affairs; it
  does not sit comfortably in a yes-no question about completion.

- ❌ <b>もはや</b>{f|着|つ}きますから、{f|待|ま}っていてください。 → ✅ <b>もうすぐ</b>{f|着|つ}きますから、{f|待|ま}っていてください。
  "Soon" is <code>もうすぐ</code>. `もはや` points backwards at a change already
  complete, never forwards.

- ❌ お{f|皿|さら}、<b>もはや</b>{f|洗|あら}っといたよ。 → ✅ お{f|皿|さら}、<b>もう</b>{f|洗|あら}っといたよ。
  Grammar aside, the register collapses: {f|洗|あら}っといた is relaxed speech and
  `もはや` is leader-column formality.

## Practice

**1.** {f|証拠|しょうこ}がこれだけそろった{f|以上|いじょう}、____{f|言|い}い{f|逃|のが}れはできない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もはや</b>{f|言|い}い{f|逃|のが}れはできない

The first clause states that a point has been reached; the second says the old option
is closed. That threshold-crossed structure is exactly what `もはや` is for, and the
formal {f|以上|いじょう} clause matches its register.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この{f|部屋|へや}、（もう・もはや）{f|一人|ひとり}{f|入|はい}れそうだね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>もう</b>{f|一人|ひとり}{f|入|はい}れそうだね

<code>もう{f|一人|ひとり}</code> means "one more person". `もはや` cannot express
"one more" at all, and the casual ～そうだね ending would clash with it in any case.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}は<b>もう</b>{f|子供|こども}ではない。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}は<b>もはや</b>{f|子供|こども}ではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both mean "he is not a child any more", and both are correct.

(a) is an ordinary observation - he has grown up, so treat him accordingly. A parent
could say it at the dinner table.

(b) is a pronouncement. `もはや` frames the change as decisive and irreversible, and
its written register makes the sentence sound like a line from a novel or the turning
point of an argument: whatever we were assuming about him no longer holds. Said aloud
in a family conversation it would sound theatrical.

</details>
