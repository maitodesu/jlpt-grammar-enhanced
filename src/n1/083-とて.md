# ～とて

**Meaning** · even X; even if - a literary "too / even", used either of a noun ("even I") or after a past verb ("even if one had")

<code>とて</code> is the quotative `と` plus `て`, worn down over centuries into a
single particle. Two jobs survive. After a **noun** it means "X too, X as well,
even X", very often used by a speaker about themselves:
<code>{f|私|わたし}とて</code>. After a **past verb** it means "even if", and the
sentence goes on to say that it would make no difference.

**This is a form you read, not a form you say.** In modern speech the noun use is
`も` or `だって`, and the verb use is <code>ても</code> or
<code>たところで</code>.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | ～とて | {f|私|わたし} → {f|私|わたし}とて |
| Noun (negative main clause) | ～とて…ない | {f|子供|こども} → {f|子供|こども}とて{f|例外|れいがい}ではない |
| Verb | た-form + とて | {f|謝|あやま}る → {f|謝|あやま}ったとて |
| Verb | た-form + とて…ない | {f|急|いそ}ぐ → {f|急|いそ}いだとて{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わない |
| Reason | ～からとて | {f|子供|こども}だからとて |
| Fixed conjunction | さりとて | - → さりとて{f|名案|めいあん}があるわけでもない |
| Modern equivalents | ～も／だって、～ても／～たところで | {f|私|わたし}も、{f|謝|あやま}っても |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>さりとて</code> ("but even so") is the one member of the family
still in ordinary written use. It is a whole conjunction, not a particle, and it
opens a sentence: <code>さりとて、{f|放|ほう}っておくわけにもいかない</code>. If
you learn one thing from this page, learn that one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}<b>とて</b>、{f|同|おな}じ{f|気持|きも}ちだ。
I feel the same way myself.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子供|こども}<b>とて</b>、{f|例外|れいがい}ではない。
Children are no exception either.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}さら{f|謝|あやま}った<b>とて</b>、{f|失|うしな}った{f|信用|しんよう}は{f|戻|もど}らない。
Apologising now would not bring back the trust that was lost.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}<b>とて</b>、{f|好|この}んでこの{f|道|みち}を{f|選|えら}んだわけではあるまい。
He too can hardly have chosen this road because he wanted it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|急|いそ}いだ<b>とて</b>、{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}うものではない。
Hurrying would get us nowhere in time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|勝|か}った<b>とて</b>、{f|失|うしな}ったものが{f|返|かえ}ってくるわけではなかった。
Even in victory, what had been lost did not come back.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|老|お}いた{f|母|はは}<b>とて</b>、{f|息子|むすこ}の{f|嘘|うそ}に{f|気|き}づかぬはずはなかった。
His aged mother, too, could hardly have failed to notice her son's lie.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|都会|とかい}の{f|暮|く}らしには{f|疲|つか}れた。<b>さりとて</b>、{f|故郷|こきょう}に{f|戻|もど}る{f|勇気|ゆうき}もない。
City life has worn him out. And yet he has not the courage to go home either.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Literary, and in speech genuinely old-fashioned.** Where you will meet it: novels
and short stories, especially in narration and in the inner voice of a character;
period drama and historical fiction; essays and columns written in a deliberately
weighty style; the occasional line of a speech reaching for gravity. Where you will
not meet it: conversation, email, business Japanese, anything spoken by a person
under any circumstances you are likely to be in. Saying
「{f|私|わたし}とて{f|忙|いそが}しい」 to a colleague would land somewhere between
quaint and theatrical.

What it adds, when a writer uses it, is a note of **shared condition or quiet
resignation**. <code>{f|私|わたし}とて</code> is not simply
<code>{f|私|わたし}も</code>; it carries "I am no different from you in this, whatever
you may assume", and it very often introduces a hardship the speaker is claiming a
share of. Applied to another person -
<code>{f|彼|かれ}とて</code> - it extends the same sympathy outwards.

The <code>たとて</code> use is bleaker. It nearly always ends in a negative, or in
<code>わけではない</code>, or in a rhetorical question, because its business is
futility: the action would change nothing. That is exactly the meaning modern
Japanese expresses with <code>たところで</code>, and
<code>たところで</code> is what you should write.

Do not confuse this <code>とて</code> with the colloquial contraction of
<code>という</code> and <code>といって</code>. In speech
<code>{f|行|い}くって</code> is `って`, an entirely modern shortening, and it has
nothing to do with the literary particle here beyond a distant shared ancestor.

## Contrast

### ～とて vs ～も／～だって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical function after a noun.
`も` is neutral, `だって` is casual and slightly complaining, and
<code>とて</code> is literary. Say the first two; read the third.

</div>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}<b>だって</b>、{f|好|す}きでやっているわけじゃない。
It's not as if I'm doing this because I enjoy it either, you know.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}<b>とて</b>、{f|望|のぞ}んでこうしているのではない。
Nor am I doing this because I wish to.
</pre>

The two sentences make the same move - claiming a share of a difficulty someone
else assumed was theirs alone - and they belong to different centuries. `だって`
puts it in the voice of a real person mid-argument.
<code>とて</code> puts it in the voice of a narrator, or of a character in a novel
speaking with dignity.

### ～たとて vs ～たところで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same meaning and the same futility.
<code>たところで</code> is current Japanese; <code>たとて</code> is its literary
ancestor.

</div>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}から{f|走|はし}っ<b>たところで</b>、{f|開演|かいえん}には{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わない。
Even if we run now, we won't make the curtain.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}から{f|走|はし}っ<b>たとて</b>、{f|開演|かいえん}には{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}うまい。
Even were we to run now, we should hardly make the curtain.
</pre>

Note how <code>たとて</code> pulls the ending with it: `まい` rather than
<code>ない</code>. The two archaic forms belong together, and a sentence that
starts literary and finishes casual sounds broken. If you find yourself writing
<code>たとて</code>, check the end of your sentence as well.

### ～とて vs ～といえども

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>といえども</code> takes a category that is **expected to
be exempt** and refuses the exemption. <code>とて</code> simply says the subject is
**included**, and is often sympathetic rather than severe.

</div>

<pre>
{f|大臣|だいじん}<b>といえども</b>、{f|法|ほう}を{f|超|こ}えることはできない。
Not even a minister stands above the law.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|大臣|だいじん}<b>とて</b>、{f|一人|ひとり}の{f|人間|にんげん}である。
A minister, too, is only a human being.
</pre>

The first denies a privilege; the second grants a shared frailty. Both are
literary, but their emotional direction is opposite, and swapping them changes an
accusation into an excuse.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|謝|あやま}る<b>とて</b>{f|遅|おそ}い → ✅ {f|謝|あやま}った<b>とて</b>{f|遅|おそ}い
  In the "even if" use the verb goes into the た-form. The `た` here is not past
  tense; it is part of the frame, exactly as in
  <code>{f|謝|あやま}ったところで</code>.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}<b>だとて</b> → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>とて</b>
  A noun attaches bare. There is no `だ` in front of it.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}<b>とて</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}<b>なので</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。
  <code>とて</code> is concessive, never causal. Announcing a cancellation with it
  says the opposite of what you mean, and in a ですます sentence it is also badly
  out of register.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}<b>とて</b>{f|喜|よろこ}んでいるよ。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}<b>も</b>{f|喜|よろこ}んでいるよ。
  Two problems at once: the casual ending clashes with the literary particle, and
  <code>とて</code> leans towards hardship rather than good news.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this line - a group chat, a novel, or a
train announcement?
{f|老|お}いた{f|父|ちち}<b>とて</b>、それを{f|知|し}らぬわけではなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A novel - specifically, narration.

<code>とて</code> plus the classical negative <code>{f|知|し}らぬ</code> plus the
past <code>なかった</code> is the voice of a narrator telling us what a character
privately understood. A train announcement would use plain polite Japanese, and in
a group chat the sentence would read as a joke.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
{f|今|いま}から{f|文句|もんく}を{f|言|い}っ<b>たとて</b>、{f|決|き}まったことは{f|変|か}わらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|今|いま}から{f|文句|もんく}を{f|言|い}っ<b>たところで</b>、{f|決|き}まったことは{f|変|か}わらない。（or {f|言|い}っても）

<code>たところで</code> carries the same futility and is fully current. A plain
<code>{f|言|い}っても</code> also works and is the most neutral option; it just
loses the "and it would achieve nothing" colouring that both of the others have.

</details>

**3.** Choose: {f|社長|しゃちょう}（とて・といえども）、{f|部下|ぶか}に{f|頭|あたま}を{f|下|さ}げねばならぬ{f|時|とき}がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Either is possible, and they say different things. <b>といえども</b> is the sharper
reading: not even the president is above bowing to a subordinate - the privilege of
rank is denied. <b>とて</b> is the gentler one: the president is a person like
anyone else, and has his moments of bowing too. Given <code>ねばならぬ</code>, which
implies an obligation imposed from outside, <b>といえども</b> is the better fit.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the problem: {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|忙|いそが}しかったとて、{f|返事|へんじ}が{f|遅|おく}れてすみません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|忙|いそが}しかった<b>もので</b>、{f|返事|へんじ}が{f|遅|おく}れてすみません。

The sentence is giving a reason, and <code>とて</code> cannot give reasons - it
concedes. The apology also places this firmly in everyday polite Japanese, where a
literary particle has no business. <code>もので</code> or
<code>ので</code> is what the sentence wants.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|私|わたし}<b>も</b>{f|不安|ふあん}です。
(b) {f|私|わたし}<b>とて</b>{f|不安|ふあん}なのだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain, polite statement of fellow feeling: I am worried as well. It could
be said by anyone to anyone.

(b) is the same content delivered in a literary voice, and it carries a note of
protest with it - the speaker is answering an assumption that they are calm, or
unaffected, or somehow above it. The plain <code>のだ</code> ending reinforces
that. In a novel it would read as a moment of a character letting their guard down;
said aloud in an office, it would sound as if the speaker were quoting a book.

</details>
