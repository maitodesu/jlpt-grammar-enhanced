# あえて

**Meaning** · deliberately, even though there is reason not to; to go so far as to; if I must

`あえて` marks a choice made **against resistance**. Something makes the action
difficult, risky, unpopular or simply unnecessary - and the person does it anyway,
knowingly. Turned negative it is just as useful: あえて{f|言|い}わなかった means the
speaker had something to say and chose to keep it back.

## Usage

An adverb, standing before the verb. Both <code>{f|敢|あ}えて</code> and the kana
spelling are normal in print.

The one thing it always needs is **something arguing against the action**. That
counter-reason is usually right there in the sentence - {f|危険|きけん}だが,
{f|嫌|きら}われるのを{f|承知|しょうち}で, {f|分|わ}かっていながら - or it is obvious
from the situation. Without it, the word has nothing to push against and the sentence
reads as though a word is missing.

Four frames account for most of its use.

- **Against an obstacle.** <code>あえて～する</code>:
  あえて{f|反対|はんたい}する, あえて{f|厳|きび}しいことを{f|言|い}う,
  あえて{f|険|けわ}しい{f|道|みち}を{f|選|えら}ぶ.
- **Deliberate restraint.** <code>あえて～ない</code>:
  あえて{f|触|ふ}れない, あえて{f|何|なに}も{f|言|い}わなかった,
  あえて{f|助|たす}けなかった. The action was available and was declined on purpose.
- **Reluctant choice.** <code>あえて{f|言|い}えば</code>,
  <code>あえて{f|言|い}うなら</code>, <code>あえて{f|挙|あ}げるなら</code> - "if I
  really have to name one". This frame is a hedge, and what follows it is a mild
  preference, not a strong claim.
- **Denying the need.** <code>あえて～する{f|必要|ひつよう}はない</code>,
  <code>あえて～するまでもない</code>: there is no call to go out of your way.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|嫌|きら}われるのを{f|承知|しょうち}で、<b>あえて</b>{f|反対|はんたい}した。
Knowing it would make me unpopular, I went ahead and objected.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|理由|りゆう}は{f|分|わ}かっていたが、<b>あえて</b>{f|何|なに}も{f|聞|き}かなかった。
I knew why, but I made a point of not asking.
</pre>

<pre>
どちらか<b>あえて</b>{f|選|えら}ぶなら、{f|静|しず}かなほうの{f|部屋|へや}がいいです。
If I had to pick one, I'd rather have the quieter room.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新人|しんじん}に{f|育|そだ}ってほしいので、{f|手|て}を{f|貸|か}したい{f|気持|きも}ちを{f|抑|おさ}えて<b>あえて</b>{f|最後|さいご}まで{f|任|まか}せることにした。
I want the new staff to grow, so I held back the urge to step in and made a point of leaving it entirely to them.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Neutral to slightly formal. It is standard in written argument - opinion columns,
reports, essays - and equally normal in careful adult speech: a manager explaining a
decision, a reviewer justifying an unpopular verdict. It is not stiff and not
old-fashioned, but it is not chatty either, and you will hear it more in explanation
than in small talk.

The word flatters whatever it modifies. Because it asserts that the actor saw the
cost and accepted it, `あえて` frames a choice as **principled rather than careless**.
That is why あえて{f|言|い}わせていただきますが is such a common preface to criticism:
it announces in advance that the speaker knows the remark is unwelcome and considers
it necessary anyway. Used of your own behaviour it is a small claim to courage or
judgement, so it is worth being sure the claim is warranted.

The negative frame is the one learners most often lack, and it is very common in
Japanese. あえて{f|言|い}わなかった, あえて{f|触|ふ}れなかった and
あえて{f|見|み}ないふりをした all describe silence as an active, considered decision.
Without `あえて`, {f|言|い}わなかった is merely a fact; with it, the silence becomes a
choice the speaker will defend.

One caution about <code>あえて{f|言|い}えば</code>. It signals reluctance - the speaker
is being pressed for an answer and would rather not commit - so it does not sit in
front of a strong opinion. Follow it with a hedged one.

## Contrast

### あえて vs わざわざ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both describe doing something you did not have to do. わざわざ is
about **trouble taken**; `あえて` is about **an objection overridden**. Only わざわざ
appears in thanks.

</div>

<pre>
{f|遠|とお}いところを<b>わざわざ</b>{f|来|き}てくださって、ありがとうございました。
Thank you for coming all this way specially.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|近道|ちかみち}もあったが、{f|景色|けしき}を{f|見|み}たくて<b>あえて</b>{f|遠回|とおまわ}りをした。
There was a shortcut, but I wanted to see the view, so I took the long way round on purpose.
</pre>

Swapping them changes the sentences badly. あえて{f|来|き}てくださって would suggest
the visitor came despite having a reason not to - which is not a thank-you, it is an
accusation. And わざわざ{f|遠回|とおまわ}りをした would put the emphasis on the
nuisance of the detour rather than on the reason for choosing it. わざわざ also has a
sour second life in complaints - わざわざ{f|言|い}わなくてもいいのに, "you didn't have
to say that" - which `あえて` does not share.

### あえて vs わざと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>わざと</code> contrasts with *by accident*. `あえて`
contrasts with *taking the easy option*. わざと often implies the act was underhand;
`あえて` implies it was defensible.

</div>

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}は<b>わざと</b>{f|負|ま}けて、{f|妹|いもうと}を{f|喜|よろこ}ばせた。
My brother lost on purpose to make our little sister happy.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|勝|か}てる{f|相手|あいて}だったが、<b>あえて</b>{f|苦手|にがて}な{f|戦|たたか}い{f|方|かた}を{f|試|ため}した。
I could have beaten him easily, but I deliberately tried a style I'm bad at.
</pre>

わざと answers "did you mean to?"; `あえて` answers "why take the hard road?".
Replace わざと with `あえて` in the first sentence and it starts to sound as though
losing were a principled stand. Replace `あえて` with わざと in the second and the
speaker sounds like they were being difficult on purpose.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>あえて</b>{f|来|き}てくれてありがとう。 → ✅ <b>わざわざ</b>{f|来|き}てくれてありがとう。
  Thanks for someone's trouble is わざわざ. `あえて` would imply the person came
  against their better judgement.

- ❌ <b>あえて</b>コップを{f|割|わ}ってしまった。 → ✅ <b>うっかり</b>コップを{f|割|わ}ってしまった。
  `あえて` describes a reasoned decision. An accident cannot be one - and if it was
  intentional and unjustified, the word is わざと.

- ❌ のどが{f|渇|かわ}いたので<b>あえて</b>{f|水|みず}を{f|飲|の}んだ。 → ✅ のどが{f|渇|かわ}いたので{f|水|みず}を{f|飲|の}んだ。
  Nothing argues against drinking when you are thirsty, so there is no resistance for
  the word to override. Delete it.

- ❌ <b>あえて{f|言|い}えば</b>、この{f|案|あん}こそが{f|唯一|ゆいいつ}の{f|正解|せいかい}だ。 → ✅ <b>あえて{f|言|い}えば</b>、この{f|案|あん}のほうが{f|少|すこ}しましだろうか。
  The <code>あえて{f|言|い}えば</code> frame promises a reluctant, hedged answer.
  Following it with an absolute claim contradicts the hedge you just made.

## Practice

**1.** {f|失敗|しっぱい}させたくなかったが、{f|本人|ほんにん}のために____{f|口|くち}を{f|出|だ}さなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|本人|ほんにん}のために<b>あえて</b>{f|口|くち}を{f|出|だ}さなかった

The first clause supplies the resistance - the speaker wanted to intervene - and the
second reports the deliberate restraint. This <code>あえて～ない</code> shape turns
saying nothing into an active decision made for the other person's sake.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|部長|ぶちょう}が（あえて・わざわざ）{f|空港|くうこう}まで{f|迎|むか}えに{f|来|き}てくださった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>わざわざ</b>{f|空港|くうこう}まで{f|迎|むか}えに{f|来|き}てくださった

The sentence is an expression of gratitude for trouble taken, which is わざわざ.
`あえて` would suggest the department head had grounds for not coming and came
regardless - not a compliment.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼女|かのじょ}の{f|失敗|しっぱい}について{f|何|なに}も{f|言|い}わなかった。
(b) {f|彼女|かのじょ}の{f|失敗|しっぱい}について<b>あえて</b>{f|何|なに}も{f|言|い}わなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a bare report. The speaker said nothing, and we are told nothing about why -
they may not have noticed, or may not have cared.

(b) makes the silence intentional and defends it. The speaker saw the mistake, had
words ready, and decided that saying them would not help. It invites the listener to
read the silence as consideration rather than indifference, and it usually implies
that the speaker would explain the reasoning if asked.

</details>
