# という - all; every

**Meaning** · every single one of them, without exception - said by repeating the noun on both sides of `という`

This is not the quotative `という` the reader already knows. Here the **same noun
appears twice**, and the effect is a sweep of the eye across the whole set:
<code>{f|窓|まど}という{f|窓|まど}</code> means every window there was, all of them,
none left out. Where `すべて` counts, this pattern *looks*.

## Formation

The two nouns must be identical. Nothing may come between them but `という`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (repeated) | Noun + という + Noun | {f|窓|まど} → {f|窓|まど}という{f|窓|まど} |
| As subject | ～という～ + が | {f|店|みせ}という{f|店|みせ}が{f|閉|し}まっていた |
| As object | ～という～ + を | {f|棚|たな}という{f|棚|たな}を{f|探|さが}した |
| With a location particle | ～という～ + に | {f|壁|かべ}という{f|壁|かべ}に{f|貼|は}る |
| Compound noun | Compound + という + compound | {f|引|ひ}き{f|出|だ}し → {f|引|ひ}き{f|出|だ}しという{f|引|ひ}き{f|出|だ}し |
| Modified on the left only | Modifier + ～という～ | {f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|店|みせ}という{f|店|みせ} |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the modifier goes in **front of the first noun only**.
<code>{f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|店|みせ}という{f|店|みせ}</code> is right;
❌<code>{f|店|みせ}という{f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|店|みせ}</code> is not.

</div>

## Nuance & register

This one is **vivid rather than formal**. It reads as naturally in a friend's
account of a bad day as in a news report of a flood, and what it adds in both is
scale: the speaker is not counting but gesturing at the whole set at once. Because
of that it belongs with dramatic content - damage, exhaustive searching, saturation
coverage, obsessive habits - and it falls flat on a neutral fact. Nobody says
<code>{f|机|つくえ}という{f|机|つくえ}に{f|椅子|いす}がある</code>.

There is a light note of exaggeration in it, and speakers know that. Saying
<code>{f|店|みせ}という{f|店|みせ}を{f|回|まわ}った</code> after visiting four shops
is entirely normal Japanese; the pattern reports the effort, not an audit.

Two restrictions matter. The set has to be **plural** - there must be many windows
for <code>{f|窓|まど}という{f|窓|まど}</code> to mean anything, and a unique referent
kills it, which is why ❌<code>{f|母|はは}という{f|母|はは}</code> is nonsense.
And the noun is normally **concrete and countable**. Abstractions do occur, but they
tip into a different, older idiom: <code>{f|人|ひと}という{f|人|ひと}</code> works,
while <code>{f|愛|あい}という{f|愛|あい}</code> would just puzzle a reader.

Do not confuse the shape with the naming `という` that the same characters spell.
The test is mechanical: **if the two nouns are identical, it is this pattern; if
they differ, it is the ordinary quotative.**

## Contrast

### ～という～ vs すべての・あらゆる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すべての` states the total. `あらゆる` covers every *kind*.
`～という～` sweeps the eye over the set and carries feeling with it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|市内|しない}の<b>すべての</b>{f|信号|しんごう}が{f|止|と}まった。
Every traffic light in the city stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|市内|しない}の<b>{f|信号|しんごう}という{f|信号|しんごう}</b>が{f|止|と}まった。
Every last traffic light in the city went dead.
</pre>

The first is what a report says; the second is what somebody who was driving through
it says. `あらゆる` is different again - <code>あらゆる{f|可能性|かのうせい}</code> is
every variety of possibility, and it happily takes abstract nouns, where
`～という～` mostly will not.

### ～という～ vs という (naming or quoting)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical nouns on both sides means "every". Different nouns
means "called" or "the fact that".

</div>

<pre>
{f|富士|ふじ}<b>という</b>{f|山|やま}は{f|日本|にほん}に{f|一|ひと}つしかない。
There is only one mountain called Fuji in Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|周|まわ}りの<b>{f|山|やま}という{f|山|やま}</b>が{f|雪|ゆき}をかぶっていた。
Every mountain around us was capped with snow.
</pre>

Same three characters, two unrelated grammars. In the first, `という` links a name to
a category; in the second it links a noun to itself. There is no ambiguity in
practice, because the repetition is impossible to miss.

### ～という～ vs どの～も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どの～も` says every member qualifies, one by one.
`～という～` says the whole set was hit at once.

</div>

<pre>
<b>どの</b>{f|部屋|へや}<b>も</b>{f|片付|かたづ}いていた。
Every one of the rooms was tidy.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>{f|部屋|へや}という{f|部屋|へや}</b>が{f|散|ち}らかっていた。
Every room in the place was a mess.
</pre>

`どの～も` is neutral and works in any register, including a calm positive statement
like the first sentence above. `～という～` needs something worth exclaiming about,
which is why it gravitates to the second.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|家|いえ}という<b>{f|建物|たてもの}</b>が{f|流|なが}された。 → ✅ <b>{f|家|いえ}という{f|家|いえ}</b>が{f|流|なが}された。
  The two nouns must be the same word. Change one of them and the sentence becomes
  the naming `という`, which means something else entirely.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}の{f|母|はは}という{f|母|はは}が{f|心配|しんぱい}している。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}の{f|母|はは}が{f|心配|しんぱい}している。
  You only have one. The pattern needs a set large enough to sweep across.

- ❌ {f|窓|まど}という{f|窓|まど}が{f|一|ひと}つ{f|割|わ}れた。 → ✅ {f|窓|まど}という{f|窓|まど}が{f|割|わ}れた。
  A singular quantity contradicts the pattern. Anything counting a subset has to go.

- ❌ {f|店|みせ}という{f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|店|みせ}を{f|回|まわ}った。 → ✅ {f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|店|みせ}という{f|店|みせ}を{f|回|まわ}った。
  Modifiers attach to the left-hand noun. Sliding one into the middle breaks the
  repetition that carries the meaning.

## Practice

**1.** {f|地震|じしん}のあと、{f|棚|たな}の{f|皿|さら}____{f|皿|さら}が{f|割|わ}れていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|皿|さら}<b>という</b>{f|皿|さら}が{f|割|わ}れていた。

The noun is repeated on both sides of `という`, and the result is "every single
plate". Note that the modifier <code>{f|棚|たな}の</code> stays in front of the first
{f|皿|さら} and is not repeated.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|会場|かいじょう}の（すべての{f|席|せき}・{f|席|せき}という{f|席|せき}）が{f|埋|う}まり、{f|立|た}っている{f|人|ひと}まで{f|出|で}た。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|席|せき}という{f|席|せき}</b>が{f|埋|う}まり

Both are grammatical, but the sentence goes on to a dramatic consequence - people
standing - so it wants the vivid form. <code>すべての{f|席|せき}</code> would report
the same fact in the flat voice of a venue manager's email.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He tried every dictionary in the library, but the word was in none of them."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}は{f|図書館|としょかん}の<b>{f|辞書|じしょ}という{f|辞書|じしょ}</b>を{f|調|しら}べたが、その{f|語|ご}はどれにも{f|載|の}っていなかった。

Exhaustive searching is the pattern's home ground. <code>{f|載|の}っていない</code>
is the natural verb for a word not appearing in a reference work -
❌<code>{f|書|か}いていない</code> would suggest nobody had written it down.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|通|とお}りの{f|木|き}という{f|桜|さくら}が{f|満開|まんかい}だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|通|とお}りの<b>{f|桜|さくら}という{f|桜|さくら}</b>が{f|満開|まんかい}だった。

The nouns have to match. As written, <code>{f|木|き}という{f|桜|さくら}</code> reads
as "a cherry called Tree", which is not what anyone meant. Pick one word and repeat
it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|机|つくえ}の{f|引|ひ}き{f|出|だ}しを{f|全部|ぜんぶ}{f|開|あ}けた。
(b) {f|机|つくえ}の{f|引|ひ}き{f|出|だ}しという{f|引|ひ}き{f|出|だ}しを{f|開|あ}けた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain report of a completed action: all the drawers, done. It says nothing
about how the speaker felt.

(b) puts you in the room. The repetition drags the action out, and what comes across
is the searching itself - pulling one drawer after another, increasingly desperate.
That is why (b) almost always leads into a result, usually a disappointing one.

</details>
