# ～(よ)うが / ～(よ)うと

**Meaning** · no matter what, no matter how - the main clause is settled in advance and nothing in the first clause can move it

The first half names something that might happen, or a whole range of things that
might happen. The second half then says: it makes no difference. Unlike `ても`,
which merely concedes one awkward case, <code>(よ)うが</code> and
<code>(よ)うと</code> sweep every case off the table at once, which is why they so
often stand next to {f|誰|だれ}が, {f|何|なに}を, どんなに or いくら.

## Formation

Both particles attach to the **volitional** form - the same `う`/`よう` you know
from <code>{f|行|い}こう</code> and <code>{f|食|た}べよう</code>. `が` and `と`
are interchangeable here.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + が／と | {f|言|い}う → {f|言|い}おうが／{f|言|い}おうと |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + が／と | {f|負|ま}ける → {f|負|ま}けようが／{f|負|ま}けようと |
| Verb (する) | しようが／しようと | {f|失敗|しっぱい}する → {f|失敗|しっぱい}しようと |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | {f|来|こ}ようが／{f|来|こ}ようと | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ようと |
| Verb (passive) | ～れようが／られようと | {f|笑|わら}われる → {f|笑|わら}われようと |
| い-adjective | ～かろうが／かろうと | {f|高|たか}い → {f|高|たか}かろうが |
| な-adjective | ～であろうが／であろうと | {f|下手|へた}だ → {f|下手|へた}であろうと |
| Noun | ～であろうが／であろうと | {f|子供|こども} → {f|子供|こども}であろうと |
| Emphatic | ～(よ)うとも | {f|言|い}われようとも |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>{f|高|たか}かろう</code> is the volitional of an い-adjective, and
outside this pattern you will hardly ever meet it. Learn it as part of the shape
rather than as a form you conjugate freely. <code>いい</code> gives
<code>よかろうが</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|誰|だれ}が{f|何|なに}と<b>{f|言|い}おうと</b>、{f|私|わたし}の{f|気持|きも}ちは{f|変|か}わらない。
No matter what anyone says, my feelings will not change.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}ろうが</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|行|おこな}われます。
Rain or no rain, the match goes ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに<b>{f|高|たか}かろうと</b>、この{f|絵|え}を{f|手放|てばな}すつもりはない。
However much it is worth, I have no intention of parting with this painting.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|周|まわ}りに<b>{f|笑|わら}われようが</b>、{f|自分|じぶん}で{f|決|き}めた{f|道|みち}を{f|行|い}く。
Let them laugh - I am taking the road I chose for myself.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|相手|あいて}が{f|社長|しゃちょう}<b>であろうと</b>、{f|間違|まちが}っていることは{f|間違|まちが}っている。
Even if the other party is the company president, what is wrong is wrong.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|結果|けっか}がどう<b>なろうが</b>、{f|挑戦|ちょうせん}したこと{f|自体|じたい}に{f|意味|いみ}がある。
Whatever the outcome turns out to be, there is value in having tried.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|台風|たいふう}が<b>{f|近|ちか}づこうと</b>、{f|工事|こうじ}の{f|期限|きげん}が{f|延|の}びることはないらしい。
Typhoon or no typhoon, the construction deadline apparently is not going to move.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|社内|しゃない}の{f|規則|きそく}である{f|以上|いじょう}、どんな{f|事情|じじょう}が<b>あろうとも</b>{f|例外|れいがい}は{f|認|みと}められません。
As this is a company rule, no exception can be made whatever the circumstances.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The tone is **firm**. A sentence built on <code>(よ)うが</code> or
<code>(よ)うと</code> announces that the speaker has already finished deciding, and
it is at home in a declaration, an editorial, a company notice, a courtroom speech
or a character's moment of resolve in a novel. In conversation it is perfectly
usable, but it raises the temperature: 「{f|何|なん}と{f|言|い}われようと{f|行|い}く」
is a good deal more immovable than 「{f|何|なん}と{f|言|い}われても{f|行|い}く」.

`が` and `と` mean the same thing here. `と` is the lighter and slightly more
frequent of the two in speech; `が` has a faintly more literary, more combative
edge, and turns up more in writing. Adding `も` - <code>(よ)うとも</code> - makes
the sentence more emphatic and more formal again; there is no
❌<code>(よ)うがも</code>.

Two things the pattern likes. First, an **adverb of scale or a question word** in
front: たとえ, どんなに, いくら, {f|誰|だれ}が, {f|何|なに}を, どこで. Second, a main
clause that is a **standing position** - a decision, a rule, an assessment, a
refusal. It can report someone else's steadfastness in the past
(「{f|止|と}められようと{f|彼|かれ}は{f|行|い}った」), but it does not sit well in
front of a hesitant guess, because a guess is exactly the thing the pattern says
has already been settled.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うと vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` concedes **one** inconvenient case and carries on.
<code>(よ)うと</code> sweeps **all** cases aside at once, and is emphatic where
`ても` is neutral.

</div>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っ<b>ても</b>{f|行|い}きます。
I will go even if it rains.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}ろうと</b>{f|行|い}きます。
Rain or no rain, I am going.
</pre>

`ても` is also the far more flexible form. It can carry permission
(<code>{f|入|はい}ってもいいですか</code>), it can be softened, and it fits any
register. <code>(よ)うと</code> can do none of that:
❌<code>{f|入|はい}ろうといいですか</code> is not a sentence. If you only want to
grant one exception politely, `ても` is the form you actually need.

### ～(よ)うが vs ～(よ)うと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical in meaning. Pick one and **keep it** across a pair:
{f|泣|な}こうが{f|騒|さわ}こうが, not a `が` followed by a `と`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|泣|な}こう<b>が</b>{f|騒|さわ}こう<b>が</b>、{f|決定|けってい}は{f|覆|くつがえ}らない。
Cry, shout, do as you like - the decision will not be overturned.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|暑|あつ}かろう<b>と</b>{f|寒|さむ}かろう<b>と</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|毎朝|まいあさ}{f|走|はし}っている。
Hot or cold, he runs every morning.
</pre>

The doubled shape is a favourite of the pattern and reads as "whatever the case
may be". Mixing the particles inside one pair is not exactly ungrammatical, but it
sounds careless, in the way that "neither this nor also that" does in English.

### ～(よ)うと vs ～としても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `としても` **supposes one case** and reasons about it.
<code>(よ)うと</code> **refuses to care** which case it is.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>としても</b>、たいした{f|量|りょう}にはならないだろう。
Even supposing it snows tomorrow, it will not amount to much.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}{f|雪|ゆき}が<b>{f|降|ふ}ろうと</b>、{f|予定|よてい}は{f|変|か}えません。
Even if it snows tomorrow, I am not changing the plan.
</pre>

The first sentence is a piece of reasoning: grant the snow, and here is what
follows about the snow. The second refuses to reason at all - the plan was fixed
before the weather was mentioned. That is why the main clause after
<code>(よ)うと</code> so rarely contains かもしれない or だろう: a hedge undoes the
whole point.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|何|なに}を{f|言|い}う<b>と</b>、{f|気|き}にしない。 → ✅ {f|何|なに}を<b>{f|言|い}おうと</b>、{f|気|き}にしない。
  The particle attaches to the volitional form, not the dictionary form. A plain
  <code>{f|言|い}うと</code> is the ordinary "when/if" conditional and means
  something else entirely.

- ❌ どんなに<b>{f|高|たか}いだろうが</b> → ✅ どんなに<b>{f|高|たか}かろうが</b>
  い-adjectives have their own volitional in `かろう`.
  <code>{f|高|たか}いだろう</code> is a guess about the price, and pasting `が` on
  the end just gives you "it's probably expensive, but".

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>ようと</b>{f|責任|せきにん}はある。 → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>であろうと</b>{f|責任|せきにん}はある。
  Nouns and な-adjectives need <code>である</code> before the volitional ending.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}が<b>{f|来|き}ようと</b> → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}が<b>{f|来|こ}ようと</b>
  {f|来|く}る is irregular: its volitional is <b>こよう</b>, the same stem as
  {f|来|こ}ない.

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}ろうと</b>、{f|中止|ちゅうし}になるかもしれない。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>としても</b>、{f|中止|ちゅうし}にはならないだろう。
  The pattern promises that the main clause is immovable. Following it with a
  guess about that very case contradicts the promise.

## Practice

**1.** {f|周|まわ}りに{f|何|なに}と____、この{f|研究|けんきゅう}は{f|続|つづ}けるつもりだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|何|なに}と<b>{f|言|い}われようと</b>（or {f|言|い}われようが）

The speaker's plan was fixed before anyone opened their mouth, which is the
signature of this pattern. The passive <code>{f|言|い}われる</code> takes the
volitional <code>{f|言|い}われよう</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|試験|しけん}に{f|落|お}ちる（としても・ようと）、もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|受|う}ければいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|試験|しけん}に{f|落|お}ちる<b>としても</b>、もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|受|う}ければいい。

Here the speaker is reasoning about one supposed case - failing - and drawing a
consequence from it. That is `としても`. <code>{f|落|お}ちようと</code> would claim
the outcome is irrelevant, but the sentence is entirely about what to do if that
particular outcome happens.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Whether the customer is a child or an adult, the service we give is the same."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お{f|客様|きゃくさま}が{f|子供|こども}<b>であろうと</b>{f|大人|おとな}<b>であろうと</b>、{f|提供|ていきょう}するサービスは{f|同|おな}じです。

Nouns take <code>であろうと</code>, and the doubled shape is the natural way to
line up two alternatives. Keeping `と` in both halves rather than switching to `が`
is the tidier choice.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: どんなに{f|忙|いそが}しいだろうが、{f|健康|けんこう}{f|診断|しんだん}だけは{f|受|う}けてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ どんなに<b>{f|忙|いそが}しかろうと</b>、{f|健康|けんこう}{f|診断|しんだん}だけは{f|受|う}けてください。

<code>{f|忙|いそが}しい</code> is an い-adjective, so its volitional is
<code>{f|忙|いそが}しかろう</code>. In everyday speech most people would simply say
<code>どんなに{f|忙|いそが}しくても</code>; the かろう form belongs to the firmer,
more written register.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|誰|だれ}に{f|反対|はんたい}され<b>ても</b>、{f|私|わたし}はこの{f|案|あん}を{f|出|だ}します。
(b) {f|誰|だれ}に{f|反対|はんたい}され<b>ようとも</b>、{f|私|わたし}はこの{f|案|あん}を{f|出|だ}します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the ordinary way to say it. The speaker expects opposition and is saying
that it will not stop them. It could be said mildly, over coffee.

(b) is a declaration. The volitional plus `とも` raises the register and the
temperature together: this is the line you deliver standing up in a meeting, or
that a character says in a novel just before walking out. The propositional
content is the same; what changes is that (b) presents the decision as already
beyond discussion.

</details>
