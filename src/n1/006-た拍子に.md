# ～た拍子に

**Meaning** · in the middle of one sudden movement, something else happened by accident - "as I turned round, I knocked it over"

{f|拍子|ひょうし} is "beat, rhythm" (read **ひょうし**, not びょうし). Used as a
connective it names the split second of a physical action and blames it for an
unintended result. Three things are always true: the first clause is a **bodily
movement**, the second is **not what anyone wanted**, and the movement is what
**caused** it.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + {f|拍子|ひょうし}に | {f|転|ころ}ぶ → {f|転|ころ}んだ{f|拍子|ひょうし}に |
| Verb (する) | た-form + {f|拍子|ひょうし}に | くしゃみをする → くしゃみをした{f|拍子|ひょうし}に |
| Set phrase | {f|何|なに}かの + {f|拍子|ひょうし}に | {f|何|なに}かの{f|拍子|ひょうし}に{f|外|はず}れたらしい |
| Second clause | past tense, an unintended result | {f|頭|あたま}をぶつけた／{f|落|お}ちた |
| Not possible | dictionary form + {f|拍子|ひょうし}に | ❌{f|転|ころ}ぶ{f|拍子|ひょうし}に |
| Not possible | a deliberate second clause | ❌{f|開|あ}けた{f|拍子|ひょうし}に{f|中|なか}に{f|入|はい}った |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>{f|何|なに}かの{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</code> is worth learning as a unit. It
means "somehow, at some point, I don't know when" and is the standard way to
explain a small mystery: <code>{f|何|なに}かの{f|拍子|ひょうし}に{f|設定|せってい}が{f|変|か}わったようだ</code>.

</div>

## Nuance & register

This one is **ordinary spoken Japanese**. It is not literary and not stiff; people
use it constantly to explain how a small accident happened, and it fits both
<code>ぶつけた</code> among friends and <code>ぶつけてしまいました</code> in a report to
a supervisor. Of the "the moment that" family it is the most everyday after
<code>とたん</code>.

Its real content is **exculpatory**. Saying <code>{f|振|ふ}り{f|返|かえ}った{f|拍子|ひょうし}に{f|落|お}としました</code>
rather than <code>{f|落|お}としました</code> quietly enters a plea: the movement did
it, not my carelessness. This makes it the natural verb for apologies and insurance
claims, and it is also why it sounds strange attached to something you did on
purpose - you cannot disown an intention.

The first clause has to be a **movement of a body or an object**. Abstract or
purely temporal triggers do not work: ❌<code>{f|連絡|れんらく}が{f|来|き}た{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</code>,
❌<code>{f|春|はる}になった{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</code>. If nothing physically lurched,
this is not the pattern.

Watch the reading. {f|拍子|ひょうし} keeps its plain **ひょうし**; there is no rendaku
here, and the same word is what you hear in {f|三|さん}{f|拍子|びょうし} only because
of the counter in front of it.

## Contrast

### ～た{f|拍子|ひょうし}に vs ～た{f|弾|はず}みに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are **near-identical**, and no dependable meaning
separates them. <code>{f|弾|はず}み</code> is a shade more physical, since the word is
"bounce, momentum"; <code>{f|拍子|ひょうし}</code> is the commoner of the two overall.

</div>

<pre>
ぶつかった<b>{f|弾|はず}みに</b>、{f|手|て}に{f|持|も}っていたカップを{f|落|お}とした。
The collision jolted the cup out of my hand.
</pre>

<pre>
ぶつかった<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>、ポケットのペンが{f|飛|と}び{f|出|だ}した。
The pen shot out of my pocket when we bumped into each other.
</pre>

Either word passes in either sentence. <code>{f|弾|はず}み</code> also appears as
<code>{f|弾|はず}みで</code> and in the fixed phrase
<code>ものの{f|弾|はず}みで</code>, "in the heat of the moment", which
<code>{f|拍子|ひょうし}</code> does not have. Learn them as a pair rather than trying
to keep them apart.

### ～た{f|拍子|ひょうし}に vs ～た{f|瞬間|しゅんかん}（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|瞬間|しゅんかん}</code> reports **when**.
<code>{f|拍子|ひょうし}</code> reports **what caused it**, and the cause is a jolt.

</div>

<pre>
{f|棚|たな}に{f|手|て}を{f|伸|の}ばした<b>{f|瞬間|しゅんかん}</b>、{f|後|うし}ろで{f|物音|ものおと}がした。
The moment I reached up to the shelf, there was a noise behind me.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|棚|たな}に{f|手|て}を{f|伸|の}ばした<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>、{f|隣|となり}の{f|箱|はこ}を{f|落|お}とした。
Reaching up to the shelf, I knocked the box next to it down.
</pre>

The first cannot take {f|拍子|ひょうし}: reaching for a shelf does not produce a
noise behind you. The second can take {f|瞬間|しゅんかん}, but it becomes a bare
statement of timing and stops explaining the accident.

### ～た{f|拍子|ひょうし}に vs ～たとたん（に）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とたん</code> needs only **surprise**, and its first clause
can be anything at all. <code>{f|拍子|ひょうし}</code> needs a **physical action** and
a **causal link**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|外|そと}に{f|出|で}<b>たとたん</b>、{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}り{f|出|だ}した。
The instant I stepped outside, it started to rain.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|傘|かさ}を{f|開|ひら}いた<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>、{f|骨|ほね}が{f|一本|いっぽん}{f|折|お}れた。
One of the ribs snapped as I opened the umbrella.
</pre>

Going outside did not cause the rain, so only <code>とたん</code> works there.
Opening the umbrella is exactly what broke the rib, so
<code>{f|拍子|ひょうし}</code> is the pointed choice in the second - though
<code>とたん</code> would also pass, at the cost of the explanation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|電話|でんわ}が{f|鳴|な}った<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>、{f|驚|おどろ}いた。 → ✅ {f|電話|でんわ}が{f|鳴|な}っ<b>たとたん</b>、{f|驚|おどろ}いた。
  A ringing phone is a sound, not a jolt, and being startled is not an accident
  caused by a movement.

- ❌ ドアを{f|開|あ}けた<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>、{f|部屋|へや}に{f|入|はい}った。 → ✅ ドアを{f|開|あ}けて、{f|部屋|へや}に{f|入|はい}った。
  Walking in is deliberate. The second clause must be something you did not mean to
  do.

- ❌ {f|転|ころ}ぶ<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>{f|膝|ひざ}をすりむいた。 → ✅ {f|転|ころ}んだ<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>{f|膝|ひざ}をすりむいた。
  The た-form is required. There is no dictionary-form version of this pattern.

- ❌ {f|次|つぎ}から{f|立|た}ち{f|上|あ}がった<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>{f|気|き}をつけます。 → ✅ {f|次|つぎ}からは{f|立|た}ち{f|上|あ}がるときに{f|気|き}をつけます。
  It narrates an accident that already happened; it cannot frame a resolution about
  the future.

## Practice

**1.** {f|窓|まど}を{f|閉|し}めようとした____、カーテンレールを{f|引|ひ}っかけてしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|閉|し}めようとした<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>

A physical movement produces an unintended result - the defining shape. Note the
た-form and the てしまった at the end, which is the natural partner for an accident.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|春|はる}になった（{f|拍子|ひょうし}に・とたん）、{f|花粉症|かふんしょう}がひどくなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|春|はる}になった<b>とたん</b>、{f|花粉症|かふんしょう}がひどくなった。

The arrival of spring is not a physical jolt, and hay fever is not knocked over by
it. With no bodily movement to blame, {f|拍子|ひょうし} has nothing to attach to.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He tripped, and as he did he dropped the papers he was carrying."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}はつまずいた<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>、{f|持|も}っていた{f|書類|しょるい}を{f|落|お}としてしまった。

"and as he did" is doing precisely the work of {f|拍子|ひょうし}に - it ties the
mishap to the stumble. てしまった carries the note of regret that English leaves
implicit.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|下|お}ろした{f|拍子|ひょうし}に、ゆっくり{f|休|やす}むことにした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|下|お}ろしてから、ゆっくり{f|休|やす}むことにした。

Deciding to rest is a decision, not an accident, and it is not caused by the
movement of setting the bag down. Any second clause with ことにする or つもりだ rules
the pattern out.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|振|ふ}り{f|向|む}いた<b>{f|拍子|ひょうし}に</b>、{f|花瓶|かびん}を{f|倒|たお}してしまいました。
(b) {f|振|ふ}り{f|向|む}いたとき、{f|花瓶|かびん}を{f|倒|たお}してしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the turn itself as the culprit: the speaker's sleeve or elbow swung
into the vase. It is a mild defence, and it is what you would say while
apologising.

(b) only fixes the timing. The vase went over at the moment of turning, but the
sentence does not say the turning did it - it could have been a second person, or
pure clumsiness.

</details>
