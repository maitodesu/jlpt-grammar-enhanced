# ぞ・ぜ・わ

**Meaning** · three emphatic sentence-final particles - and three of the most heavily register-marked words in the language

`ぞ`, `ぜ` and `わ` all add force or feeling to the end of a plain-form sentence, but
none of them is a neutral option. Each one tells the listener something about who the
speaker is and what kind of scene this is, and the picture a learner picks up from
subtitles is not the picture of how they are actually used.

## Usage

All three are {f|終助詞|しゅうじょし}, sentence-final particles. They attach to the **plain form** of
the predicate and close the sentence.

- After a verb or an い-adjective, directly: {f|行|い}くぞ, やろうぜ,
  {f|寒|さむ}いわ.
- After a noun or a な-adjective, `だ` comes first: {f|本気|ほんき}だぞ,
  {f|最高|さいこう}だぜ, きれいだわ.
- `ぞ` and `ぜ` do not form questions and do not combine with か. They assert.

**ぞ** is a strong assertion, and it points in one of two directions. Aimed at
yourself it is a spur - よし、やるぞ, {f|負|ま}けないぞ - and in that use it is
essentially {f|独|ひと}り{f|言|ごと}, available to any speaker. Aimed at another person it is a warning
or a push delivered from above: {f|危|あぶ}ないぞ,
{f|置|お}いていくぞ, {f|遅|おく}れるぞ. That second use leans strongly male and
strongly downward - to children, to close juniors, to a team you are leading.

**ぜ** is casual, chummy and addressed to a peer. It is the most male-marked of the
three, and it is liveliest in the volitional: {f|行|い}こうぜ, やろうぜ,
{f|飲|の}もうぜ. Outside that, in real conversation, it is much rarer than fiction
would suggest.

**わ** is two different particles wearing one spelling, and **pitch tells them apart**.

- **Rising わ** is the soft, so-called feminine わ of standard Japanese:
  そうだと{f|思|おも}うわ, きれいだわ. It softens an assertion rather than
  strengthening it.
- **Falling わ** is gender-neutral. It came into the standard language from Kansai and
  is now everywhere in casual speech: これは{f|無理|むり}だわ, もう{f|帰|かえ}るわ,
  {f|知|し}らんわ. It marks something the speaker has just realised or decided, more or
  less to themselves.

## Examples

<pre>
よし、{f|今日|きょう}こそ{f|終|お}わらせる<b>ぞ</b>。
Right - today I'm getting this finished.
</pre>

<pre>
そろそろ{f|行|い}こう<b>ぜ</b>、{f|時間|じかん}がないんだから。
Come on, let's get moving - we haven't got time.
</pre>

<pre>
このパスワード、{f|全然|ぜんぜん}{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}せない<b>わ</b>。
I genuinely cannot remember this password.
</pre>

<pre>
「わたくしも{f|同|おな}じことを{f|考|かんが}えておりました<b>わ</b>」と{f|夫人|ふじん}は{f|静|しず}かに{f|言|い}った。
"I had been thinking precisely the same thing," the lady said quietly.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Treat these as things to **recognise in other people's mouths** before you put them in
your own. Nothing here is rude in itself, but each one places the speaker, and placing
yourself wrongly is the risk.

**ぞ and ぜ have no polite form and no upward use.** There is no ですぞ or ますぜ that
you can say to a manager. `ぞ` addressed to a superior is not merely casual, it is
insubordinate, because the particle carries the authority to warn. Between friends of
similar age `ぜ` is warm; from a learner to a teacher it is a small social accident.

You will nevertheless meet ですぞ and ますぞ, and they are worth knowing as what they
are: {f|役割語|やくわりご}, character speech. In fiction, ～ますぞ is the voice of the
elderly professor, the old retainer, the wizard. Real people over seventy do not
generally talk like this. Read it, enjoy it, and do not borrow it.

`ぜ` has a similar problem in reverse. Anime, dubbing, song lyrics and manga are full
of it because it instantly signals "confident young man", and that is exactly why it
is thinner on the ground in real conversation than a learner expects. As a tendency,
men use it and women do not; as a fact about frequency, most men use it far less often
than the fiction they watch.

The two わ are the sharpest trap on this page, because the one learners copy is the one
they should avoid and the one they overlook is the one they could safely use.

Rising わ - the そうだと{f|思|おも}うわ pattern - is strongly associated with women's
speech in fiction, with a genteel Tokyo register, and with older speakers. Real young
women use it much less than the subtitles imply, and ですわ／ますわ is the お{f|嬢様|じょうさま} voice of drama and comedy. A learner who ends sentences this way is not
speaking politely; they are speaking in costume.

Falling わ is the useful one. It is not gendered, it is current, and it is heard from
men and women of all ages: {f|疲|つか}れたわ, これ{f|無理|むり}だわ,
やっぱりそうだわ. It reports a realisation or a decision that the speaker is mostly
announcing to themselves, and it is soft rather than assertive. If you want one of
these three particles in your active vocabulary, this is the one - and the whole
difference from the other わ is the direction of the pitch, so listen for it.

None of the three attaches to keigo, and none of them is used in writing except to
quote speech or to sound deliberately informal.

## Contrast

### ぞ・ぜ・わ vs よ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よ` is the neutral emphatic and says nothing about the speaker.
The other three all say something: `ぞ` claims the standing to warn, `ぜ` claims peer
familiarity, rising `わ` claims a soft feminine register, falling `わ` marks a private
realisation.

</div>

<pre>
{f|電車|でんしゃ}、もう{f|来|き}ます<b>よ</b>。
The train's coming.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|電車|でんしゃ}、もう{f|来|く}る<b>ぞ</b>。
Train's coming - move.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|電車|でんしゃ}、もう{f|来|く}る<b>わ</b>。
Oh, the train's here already.
</pre>

The information is the same in all three. The first is available to anybody talking to
anybody, and it is the safe default; it survives being made polite as
{f|来|き}ますよ. The second pushes the listener into action and would be strange from a
junior member of staff to a client. The third, with falling pitch, is the speaker
noticing something almost to themselves. Where you are unsure, `よ` is always the
correct answer.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|会議|かいぎ}が{f|始|はじ}まります<b>ぞ</b>。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|会議|かいぎ}が{f|始|はじ}まります。
  There is no polite `ぞ`. ～ますぞ exists only as the fiction voice of an elderly
  gentleman, and to a manager it lands as either impertinent or absurd.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}、{f|一緒|いっしょ}に{f|行|い}こう<b>ぜ</b>。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}、{f|一緒|いっしょ}に{f|行|い}きませんか。
  `ぜ` is strictly peer-to-peer and downward. Addressed to a teacher it is not casual
  friendliness, it is a status error.

- ❌ {f|寒|さむ}い<b>だわ</b>。 → ✅ {f|寒|さむ}い<b>わ</b>。
  い-adjectives take the particle directly. The `だ` appears only after a noun or a
  な-adjective: しずかだわ, {f|休|やす}みだわ.

- ❌ （{f|自己|じこ}{f|紹介|しょうかい}で）はじめまして。{f|台湾|たいわん}から{f|来|き}ました<b>わ</b>。 → ✅ はじめまして。{f|台湾|たいわん}から{f|来|き}ました。
  Rising わ on a polite form is the register of a drama heroine, not of an ordinary
  introduction. In です／ます speech, end the sentence and stop.

## Practice

**1.** （{f|現場|げんば}で{f|後輩|こうはい}に）「その{f|箱|はこ}、けっこう{f|重|おも}い____。{f|気|き}をつけて。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

けっこう{f|重|おも}い<b>ぞ</b>

`ぞ` is the particle of a warning issued from a position of seniority, which is exactly
the scene described, and it attaches straight to the い-adjective. `よ` would also be
correct and a shade gentler; `ぜ` would be wrong, because `ぜ` treats the listener as an
equal rather than as someone you are looking out for.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: （{f|会社|かいしゃ}の{f|受付|うけつけ}で）「{f|三時|さんじ}に{f|約束|やくそく}がありますわ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ （{f|会社|かいしゃ}の{f|受付|うけつけ}で）「{f|三時|さんじ}にお{f|約束|やくそく}をいただいております。」

Rising わ on top of a polite sentence is the speech of a wealthy lady in a television
drama, and a reception desk is the wrong stage for it. Business Japanese takes no
sentence-final particle here at all.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう{f|帰|かえ}る<b>わ</b>。（falling pitch）
(b) もう{f|帰|かえ}る<b>ぞ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker announcing their own decision, half to themselves. It is soft, it
is not gendered, and it does not ask the listener to do anything - "right, I'm off
then". Any speaker can use it in casual company.

(b) is aimed outward. It tells the people present that the speaker is leaving and that
they had better move too, and the authority to say that is built into the particle. It
leans male and downward, so it fits a parent to a child or a group leader to the group,
and it would be out of place said to a superior. Swapping (a) for (b) turns a private
decision into an instruction.

</details>
