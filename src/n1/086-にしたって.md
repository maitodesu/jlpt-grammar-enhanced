# ～にしたって

**Meaning** · even X; even in X's case - takes one item or one person's side of things and says the point holds there too

<code>にしたって</code> is the spoken, slightly grumbling relative of
<code>にしても</code>. It picks something out - often the very thing you would
expect to be an exception, or the speaker's own position - and folds it back into
the general case: even them, even that, even from where I sit.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | ～にしたって | {f|子供|こども} → {f|子供|こども}にしたって |
| Question word | ～にしたって | {f|何|なに} → {f|何|なに}にしたって |
| Verb | plain form + にしたって | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}くにしたって |
| い-adjective | plain form + にしたって | {f|忙|いそが}しい → {f|忙|いそが}しいにしたって |
| な-adjective | stem + にしたって | {f|下手|へた}だ → {f|下手|へた}にしたって |
| Doubled | A にしたって B にしたって | {f|味|あじ}にしたって{f|値段|ねだん}にしたって |
| Neutral equivalent | ～にしても | {f|子供|こども}にしても |
| Formal equivalent | ～にしろ／にせよ | {f|子供|こども}にせよ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>たって</code> here is the casual contraction of
<code>ても</code>, the same one in <code>{f|見|み}たっていい</code>. That is the
whole of the difference between <code>にしたって</code> and
<code>にしても</code>: one sound change, and the register that comes with it.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|子供|こども}<b>にしたって</b>、それくらいのことは{f|分|わ}かるよ。
Even a child understands that much.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}<b>にしたって</b>、{f|去年|きょねん}よりずいぶん{f|高|たか}くなっている。
The price, too, has gone up a lot since last year.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|何|なに}<b>にしたって</b>、まずは{f|本人|ほんにん}に{f|聞|き}いてみないと{f|始|はじ}まらない。
Whatever it is, nothing starts until you ask the person themselves.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|行|い}く<b>にしたって</b>、{f|一人|ひとり}では{f|危|あぶ}ないだろう。
Even if you do go, going alone would be dangerous.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}<b>にしたって</b>、わざとやったわけではないと{f|思|おも}う。
I don't think he did it on purpose either.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|店|みせ}は、{f|味|あじ}<b>にしたって</b>{f|値段|ねだん}<b>にしたって</b>、{f|文句|もんく}のつけようがない。
That place - taste, price, you name it - there's nothing to complain about.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|忙|いそが}しい<b>にしたって</b>、{f|一言|ひとこと}{f|連絡|れんらく}するくらいはできたはずだ。

</pre>

<pre>

Even if the new system is going in, I'd like them to hear from the people doing the work first.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Casual spoken Japanese**, and often a little blunt. It belongs to conversation
among friends, family and familiar colleagues, and to writing that imitates speech
- dialogue, blog posts, comment threads. In an email to a client or in a report it
is out of place; there you want <code>にしても</code>, or
<code>にしろ／にせよ</code> if the register is formal.

The attitude it carries is worth learning with it. A large share of real
occurrences are **complaints or defences**:
「{f|忙|いそが}しいにしたって、ひどいよ」 pushes back on someone's excuse, and
「{f|彼|かれ}にしたって{f|好|す}きでやっているわけじゃない」 defends someone from
one. The pattern lets you concede a point and then immediately do something with
the concession, which is exactly what an argument needs.

Two neighbours are easy to mix up with it. <code>としたって</code> supposes a case
- "even supposing" - whereas <code>にしたって</code> points at a real one. And
<code>にしては</code>, which looks almost identical, means something else entirely;
see below, because that confusion is the expensive one.

When the noun in front is a person, the sentence usually means either "even they,
who you would think were exempt" or "from their side of it too". Which one you get
comes from context, and Japanese speakers do not find the ambiguity troubling.

## Contrast

### ～にしたって vs ～にしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different register.
<code>にしても</code> is neutral and can be written; <code>にしたって</code> is
spoken and carries an edge.

</div>

<pre>
{f|急|いそ}ぐ<b>にしても</b>、{f|安全|あんぜん}{f|確認|かくにん}は{f|省|はぶ}けません。
Even if we are in a hurry, the safety check cannot be skipped.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|急|いそ}ぐ<b>にしたって</b>、{f|確認|かくにん}くらいはするでしょ。
Rushing or not, you'd at least check, wouldn't you.
</pre>

Notice that the choice pulls the rest of the sentence with it: the polite
<code>{f|省|はぶ}けません</code> in the first, the casual
<code>でしょ</code> in the second. A sentence that mixes
<code>にしたって</code> with ます form sounds like two people talking at once.

### ～にしたって vs ～にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>にしたって</code> means "**even** X".
<code>にしては</code> means "**for** an X" - measured against what an X would
normally be, this one is surprising.

</div>

<pre>
{f|初|はじ}めて{f|作|つく}った<b>にしては</b>、よくできている。
For a first attempt, it's very well made.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|初|はじ}めて{f|作|つく}った<b>にしたって</b>、{f|説明書|せつめいしょ}くらいは{f|読|よ}むべきだった。
First attempt or not, he should at least have read the instructions.
</pre>

One `は` and one `って` apart, and the sentences do opposite things. The first
lowers the standard and then praises; the second grants an excuse and then refuses
to let it excuse anything. If your English translation is "for a …",
<code>にしては</code> is the form you want, and no amount of context will make
<code>にしたって</code> mean that.

### ～にしたって vs ～だって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are casual "even". `だって` attaches only to nouns and is
the simpler, more frequent word. <code>にしたって</code> also takes verbs and
adjectives, and sounds a shade more argued.

</div>

<pre>
{f|大人|おとな}<b>だって</b>{f|間違|まちが}えることはある。
Grown-ups make mistakes too.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|大人|おとな}<b>にしたって</b>、{f|全部|ぜんぶ}{f|分|わ}かっているわけじゃない。
It's not as if grown-ups understand everything either.
</pre>

With a noun the two are close enough to swap. The difference appears as soon as you
need to concede a whole clause: <code>{f|行|い}くにしたって</code> is fine, and
❌<code>{f|行|い}くだって</code> is not Japanese. `だって` also has a second life as
a sentence-opening "but", which <code>にしたって</code> does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|新人|しんじん}<b>にしては</b>、ミスが{f|多|おお}すぎる。（meaning "even for a newcomer"） → ✅ {f|新人|しんじん}<b>にしたって</b>、ミスが{f|多|おお}すぎる。
  <code>にしては</code> would mean "for a newcomer, he makes a lot of mistakes" -
  which, oddly, is close to praise-shaped criticism. If the point is that being new
  is no excuse, you need <code>にしたって</code>.

- ❌ {f|便利|べんり}<b>だにしたって</b> → ✅ {f|便利|べんり}<b>にしたって</b>
  な-adjectives attach by their stem. The `だ` has nothing to attach to.

- ❌ ご{f|多忙|たぼう}<b>にしたって</b>、ご{f|返信|へんしん}をお{f|待|ま}ちしております。 → ✅ ご{f|多忙|たぼう}<b>とは{f|存|ぞん}じますが</b>、ご{f|返信|へんしん}をお{f|待|ま}ちしております。
  A casual contraction inside honorific Japanese is a serious mismatch, and the
  complaint flavour makes it worse. Business writing needs a different construction
  altogether.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}<b>にしたって</b>{f|行|い}きたいです。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>も</b>{f|行|い}きたいです。
  The pattern concedes ground in an argument; it is not a plain "me too". Used for
  simple agreement it sounds as though you are objecting to something.

## Practice

**1.** {f|疲|つか}れていた____、{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|忘|わす}れるのはよくない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|疲|つか}れていた<b>にしたって</b>

An excuse is granted and then denied any force - the pattern's most typical job.
<code>{f|疲|つか}れていたにしても</code> is the neutral equivalent; the
<code>たって</code> version fits a spoken complaint.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|中学生|ちゅうがくせい}（にしては・にしたって）、ずいぶんしっかりした{f|文章|ぶんしょう}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|中学生|ちゅうがくせい}<b>にしては</b>、ずいぶんしっかりした{f|文章|ぶんしょう}だ。

The sentence is praising the writing by measuring it against what you would expect
of a middle-school student. That "for a …" reading is
<code>にしては</code>. <code>にしたって</code> would set up a complaint, and
nothing in the sentence complains.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if we cancel, we'll have to tell everyone today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|中止|ちゅうし}する<b>にしたって</b>、{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう}にみんなに{f|知|し}らせないといけない。

A clause rather than a noun, so the plain form attaches directly. In a written
notice you would raise this to <code>{f|中止|ちゅうし}するにしても</code>. Note
{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう} - <code>中</code> is じゅう in this compound.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|静|しず}かだにしたって、{f|図書館|としょかん}ほどではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|静|しず}か<b>にしたって</b>、{f|図書館|としょかん}ほどではない。

な-adjectives attach by their stem, with no `だ`. The same rule holds for
<code>にしても</code> and <code>にせよ</code>:
<code>{f|静|しず}かにせよ</code>, never ❌<code>{f|静|しず}かだにせよ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|部長|ぶちょう}<b>にしても</b>、{f|困|こま}っているのだと{f|思|おも}います。
(b) {f|部長|ぶちょう}<b>にしたって</b>、{f|困|こま}ってるんだと{f|思|おも}うよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both defend the department head by pointing out that he is in a difficult position
too, and both are perfectly natural.

(a) is neutral and polite enough to say in a meeting, or to write. (b) is the same
observation between colleagues over lunch: the contraction
<code>{f|困|こま}ってる</code>, the <code>んだ</code> and the final よ all belong
with <code>にしたって</code>, and together they make it sound like one turn in an
ongoing grumble about the situation. Said upward to the department head himself,
(b) would be too familiar.

</details>
