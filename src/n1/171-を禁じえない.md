# ～を禁じ得ない

**Meaning** · cannot suppress; cannot help feeling - a feeling forces its way out despite the writer's restraint

{f|禁|きん}じる is "to forbid" and {f|得|え}ない is "cannot", so the phrase is
literally "I cannot forbid it (in myself)". That image is the whole pattern: a
person who is trying to stay composed, and a feeling that will not be held down.
It takes a **noun** naming that feeling, it belongs to formal written Japanese,
and it reports the writer's own reaction, not a judgement about how bad the thing
is.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (emotion) | noun + を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない | {f|怒|いか}り → {f|怒|いか}りを{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない |
| Noun + の{f|念|ねん} | ～の{f|念|ねん}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない | {f|尊敬|そんけい} → {f|尊敬|そんけい}の{f|念|ねん}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない |
| Past | ～を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}なかった | {f|涙|なみだ}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}なかった |
| Polite | ～を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ません | {f|憤|いきどお}りを{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ません |
| Never a verb | ❌{f|泣|な}くを{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない | ✅{f|涙|なみだ}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない |
| No affirmative | ❌{f|涙|なみだ}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}る | the expression exists only in the negative |

<div class="note">

**Note on the reading and the spelling** - {f|得|え}ない here is the "cannot"
suffix, so it is <b>えない</b>, never うない: きんじえない. The phrase is written
both as {f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない and as <code>{f|禁|きん}じえない</code>, and both
are standard; newspapers tend to use the kana form.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note - the nouns that are actually live.** The slot is not open to any emotion.
What genuinely occurs is {f|怒|いか}り, {f|憤|いきどお}り, {f|涙|なみだ},
{f|驚|おどろ}き, {f|疑問|ぎもん}, {f|失望|しつぼう}, {f|落胆|らくたん},
{f|同情|どうじょう}, {f|違和感|いわかん}, {f|苦笑|くしょう}, {f|感動|かんどう}, and
the ～の{f|念|ねん} phrases ({f|尊敬|そんけい}の{f|念|ねん},
{f|畏敬|いけい}の{f|念|ねん}). Notice the shape of that list: these are feelings a
composed adult would rather not show. Ordinary pleasure does not fit -
❌{f|喜|よろこ}びを{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない is not how the pattern is used.

</div>

<pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; write the plain version.** Its home is print and prepared text:
newspaper editorials, opinion columns, book and film reviews, official statements,
letters of condolence, the closing paragraph of a formal report. Spoken aloud it
belongs to a podium. Said across a table -
<code>この{f|料理|りょうり}には{f|感動|かんどう}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ません</code> -
it is comic. The everyday equivalents are
<code>{f|泣|な}かずにはいられなかった</code>,
<code>どうしても{f|腹|はら}が{f|立|た}ってしまう</code>,
<code>{f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|驚|おどろ}きました</code>.

**It reports a feeling, not a verdict.** This is the distinction English
translations destroy. <code>{f|怒|いか}りを{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</code> says
*anger rose in me and I could not stop it*;
<code>{f|不誠実|ふせいじつ}{f|極|きわ}まりない</code> says *this conduct is as
dishonest as conduct gets*. A columnist often writes both in the same paragraph,
one as the reaction and one as the ruling, and they are not substitutes for each
other.

The subject is the writer, or an unnamed "any decent person" that the writer
invites you to join - which is part of why the pattern is so useful in editorials:
it presents an opinion as an involuntary human reaction rather than as an
argument. In literary narration it can be used of a character whose mind the
narrator can see, but in ordinary writing about a third party you would use
{f|怒|おこ}っていた or {f|涙|なみだ}を{f|流|なが}していた instead.

Finally, the emotion has to be worth the register. Fatigue, hunger and mild
annoyance do not take it, and neither does everyday delight; the pattern implies
something serious enough that a composed adult was trying not to react.

## Contrast

### ～を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない vs ～ずにはいられない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same feeling, different plumbing and different register.
<code>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</code> takes a **noun** and is written formal;
<code>ずにはいられない</code> takes a **verb** and is ordinary Japanese you can
actually say.

</div>

<pre>
{f|遺族|いぞく}の{f|言葉|ことば}に、{f|涙|なみだ}<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}なかった</b>。
The words of the bereaved family brought tears I could not hold back.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|遺族|いぞく}の{f|言葉|ことば}を{f|聞|き}いて、{f|泣|な}か<b>ずにはいられなかった</b>。
Hearing the family speak, I could not help crying.
</pre>

Notice the swap: the noun {f|涙|なみだ} becomes the verb {f|泣|な}く. If you find
yourself wanting ❌{f|泣|な}くを{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない, that is the sign you
should be using <code>ずにはいられない</code> instead.

### ～を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない vs ～{f|極|きわ}まりない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</code> is **emotional**: this
is what rose up in me. <code>{f|極|きわ}まりない</code> is **evaluative**: this is
what the thing is. English "extremely" covers both and tells you nothing.

</div>

<pre>
{f|事故|じこ}の{f|報告書|ほうこくしょ}を{f|読|よ}み、{f|失望|しつぼう}<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</b>。
Reading the accident report, I cannot suppress a sense of dismay.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|点検|てんけん}を{f|怠|おこた}ったまま{f|運行|うんこう}を{f|続|つづ}けたのは{f|無責任|むせきにん}<b>{f|極|きわ}まりない</b>。
To have kept the service running with the inspections neglected was utterly irresponsible.
</pre>

The first sentence is about the writer; the second is about the operator. Only the
second is an accusation, which is why a careful writer chooses between them
deliberately.

### ～を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない vs ～てならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both say a feeling cannot be held down.
<code>てならない</code> attaches to an **adjective or verb** and describes a
feeling that keeps welling up over time; <code>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</code>
takes a **noun** and describes the reaction to one particular thing.

</div>

<pre>
{f|試合|しあい}に{f|出|で}られなかったことが{f|残念|ざんねん}<b>でならない</b>。
I cannot stop feeling sorry that I was not able to play.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会場|かいじょう}の{f|判断|はんだん}には{f|疑問|ぎもん}<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</b>。
I cannot help questioning the decision taken at the venue.
</pre>

<code>てならない</code> is the softer and the more personal of the two, and it can
be said aloud without sounding like a leader column;
<code>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</code> always sounds like one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|泣|な}くこと<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</b> → ✅ {f|涙|なみだ}<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</b>
  The slot wants the noun for the feeling itself, not a nominalised verb.

- ❌ このケーキの{f|美味|おい}しさには{f|感動|かんどう}<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ません</b>。 → ✅ このケーキ、{f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|美味|おい}しくて{f|感動|かんどう}しました。
  The pattern needs a subject weighty enough to justify editorial register.
  Applied to dessert it reads as parody.

- ❌ {f|喜|よろこ}び<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</b> → ✅ {f|嬉|うれ}しい{f|限|かぎ}りです
  The feelings that fit are ones you were trying to hold in. Straightforward
  happiness is not one of them.

- ❌ {f|怒|いか}りを{f|禁|きん}じ<b>{f|得|え}た</b> → ✅ {f|怒|いか}りを{f|禁|きん}じ<b>{f|得|え}ない</b>
  There is no affirmative form; the expression lives only in the negative. Watch
  the reading too - it is きんじえない, and ❌きんじうない is a pure invention.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|驚|おどろ}き<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ませんでした</b>。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|大変|たいへん}{f|驚|おどろ}いていました。
  Outside literary narration the pattern reports the writer's own reaction.
  Attributing it to your boss reads as though you were inside his head.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this sentence? {f|再発|さいはつ}{f|防止|ぼうし}を{f|約束|やくそく}しながら{f|同|おな}じ{f|事故|じこ}を{f|繰|く}り{f|返|かえ}したことに、{f|失望|しつぼう}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a newspaper editorial or an opinion column - possibly in the closing paragraph
of a formal report. The pattern presents the writer's dismay as the natural
reaction of any reasonable reader, which is exactly what editorial writing wants.
It would not be said aloud in a meeting.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: {f|昔|むかし}の{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|見|み}ていたら、{f|涙|なみだ}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}なかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|昔|むかし}の{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|見|み}ていたら、<b>{f|泣|な}かずにはいられなかった</b>。（or {f|涙|なみだ}が{f|出|で}てきてしまった）

The noun {f|涙|なみだ} turns back into the verb {f|泣|な}く, and the register drops
from print to speech. {f|涙|なみだ}が{f|出|で}てきてしまった is what most people
would actually say.

</details>

**3.** Choose: {f|安全|あんぜん}{f|基準|きじゅん}を{f|無視|むし}した{f|工事|こうじ}は{f|危険|きけん}（を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない・{f|極|きわ}まりない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|危険|きけん}<b>{f|極|きわ}まりない</b>。

{f|危険|きけん} is a property of the work, not a feeling in the writer, so it takes
the evaluative pattern. To use <code>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない</code> you would
have to name the reaction instead:
{f|危険|きけん}な{f|工事|こうじ}に{f|不安|ふあん}を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの{f|場面|ばめん}では{f|笑|わら}うを{f|禁|きん}じえませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの{f|場面|ばめん}では<b>{f|笑|わら}わずにはいられませんでした</b>。（written: {f|苦笑|くしょう}<b>を{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}なかった</b>）

A verb cannot fill the slot. Either switch to
<code>ずにはいられない</code>, which takes verbs, or supply the noun for the
reaction - {f|苦笑|くしょう} for a wry laugh you were trying to hold in.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|今回|こんかい}の{f|対応|たいおう}には{f|怒|いか}りを{f|禁|きん}じ{f|得|え}ない。
(b) {f|今回|こんかい}の{f|対応|たいおう}は{f|不誠実|ふせいじつ}{f|極|きわ}まりない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the writer in the frame. It says the handling produced anger that the
writer could not suppress, and it invites the reader to share a human reaction
rather than to accept a claim. Strictly speaking it asserts nothing about the
handling at all.

(b) is a verdict on the handling itself: dishonest to the maximum degree. There is
no writer visible in it, and it can be argued with, whereas (a) cannot. Editorials
often run the two together - the ruling, then the feeling - because each does
something the other cannot.

</details>
