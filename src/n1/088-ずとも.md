# ～ずとも

**Meaning** · even without doing it; even if one does not - the literary ancestor of なくても

`ず` is the classical negative and `とも` the classical concessive, so
<code>ずとも</code> is "even not-doing". The sentence that follows says the thing
happens, or is known, or is fine, anyway - without the action ever being needed.

**This belongs to written Japanese and to set phrases.** Say
<code>なくても</code>. Recognising <code>ずとも</code> matters because it turns up
in maxims, in narration, in formal notices and, oddly enough, in product copy.

## Formation

Take the ない-form, drop the `ない`, and add <code>ずとも</code>. Two verbs are
irregular.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ずとも | {f|言|い}わない → {f|言|い}わずとも |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ずとも | {f|見|み}ない → {f|見|み}ずとも |
| Verb (する) | **せずとも** | {f|説明|せつめい}しない → {f|説明|せつめい}せずとも |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | <b>{f|来|こ}ずとも</b> | {f|来|こ}ない → {f|来|こ}ずとも |
| Permission frame | ～ずともよい／{f|構|かま}わない | {f|来|こ}ずともよい |
| Sufficiency frame | ～ずとも{f|済|す}む | {f|買|か}わずとも{f|済|す}む |
| Modern equivalent | ～なくても | {f|言|い}わなくても |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `ず` itself is not archaic at all. It is alive in
<code>{f|行|い}かずに</code>, <code>{f|知|し}らずに</code>,
<code>{f|言|い}わずにはいられない</code> and
<code>{f|少|すく}なからず</code>, all of which are ordinary modern Japanese. It is
the combination with `とも` that is literary. Do not conclude from this page that
`ず` is a dead form.

</div>

{f|言|い}わ<b>ずとも</b>{f|分|わ}かるだろう。

</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Written, and in conversation genuinely old-fashioned.** Where you will find it:
literary narration and fiction; aphorisms and proverb-like statements; formal
written notices and instructions; and - the one place a modern learner is most
likely to meet it - marketing and interface copy, where
<code>{f|登録|とうろく}せずともご{f|利用|りよう}いただけます</code> sounds compact
and dignified in a way that
<code>{f|登録|とうろく}しなくても</code> does not. Saying
「{f|急|いそ}がずともいいよ」 to a friend would land as a deliberate flourish, the
Japanese equivalent of "thou needst not hurry".

The modern equivalent is <code>なくても</code>, and it is right in essentially
every spoken situation. In writing you also have <code>なくとも</code>, which is a
useful middle rung: formal, current, and not literary.

Two frames account for a good share of real occurrences.
<code>～ずともよい</code> and <code>～ずとも{f|構|かま}わない</code> mean "there is
no need to", and in older texts and period drama they are how permission is
granted from above. <code>～ずとも{f|済|す}む</code> means "one gets by without
having to". Both are worth recognising as units.

Finally, a relative worth knowing:
<code>{f|言|い}わずもがな</code>. It contains the same `ず` and looks as though it
should mean "even without saying", but it has settled into two set meanings -
"needless to say" and "better left unsaid". It is not the same construction, and
you cannot build new phrases on its pattern.

## Contrast

### ～ずとも vs ～なくても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. <code>なくても</code> is the form to
produce; <code>ずとも</code> is the form to recognise.

</div>

<pre>
{f|全部|ぜんぶ}{f|読|よ}ま<b>なくても</b>、{f|内容|ないよう}は{f|分|わ}かりますよ。
You can get the gist without reading all of it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|全|すべ}てを{f|読|よ}ま<b>ずとも</b>、その{f|意|い}は{f|通|つう}じよう。
Even without reading the whole, the meaning will carry.
</pre>

Look at what else changes in the second sentence: {f|全|すべ}て for
{f|全部|ぜんぶ}, <code>{f|意|い}</code> for
<code>{f|内容|ないよう}</code>, the volitional <code>よう</code> for
<code>ますよ</code>. A literary particle drags the whole sentence up with it, and a
<code>ずとも</code> sitting in an otherwise everyday sentence is the tell-tale sign
of a learner who has met the form on a list.

### ～ずとも vs ～ずに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ずに` means simply "**without** doing", and describes how
something was done. <code>ずとも</code> is **concessive**: "even without doing", and
claims the result holds anyway.

</div>

<pre>
{f|朝食|ちょうしょく}を{f|取|と}ら<b>ずに</b>{f|家|いえ}を{f|出|で}た。
He left the house without eating breakfast.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|朝食|ちょうしょく}を{f|取|と}ら<b>ずとも</b>{f|昼|ひる}までは{f|持|も}つ。
Even without breakfast, he can last until midday.
</pre>

This is the more useful of the two contrasts, because `ずに` is fully current and
you will use it often. The test is whether you can insert "even": if you can, it is
<code>ずとも</code> and you should probably be writing
<code>なくても</code> instead.

### ～ずとも vs ～なくとも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three rungs of the same ladder.
<code>なくても</code> everyday, <code>なくとも</code> formal but current,
<code>ずとも</code> literary.

</div>

<pre>
{f|少|すく}な<b>くとも</b>{f|三日|みっか}は{f|必要|ひつよう}です。
It will take at least three days.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|確|たし}かめ<b>なくとも</b>、{f|答|こた}えは{f|見当|けんとう}がついている。
Even without checking, I have a fair idea of the answer.
</pre>

<code>{f|少|すく}なくとも</code> is frozen and extremely common - most learners use
it long before they notice it contains this `とも` - and it is a good anchor for
the middle rung. In a report or a formal email, <code>なくとも</code> gives you the
weight of <code>ずとも</code> without the century.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|説明|せつめい}<b>しずとも</b> → ✅ {f|説明|せつめい}<b>せずとも</b>
  `する` uses the older negative stem `せ`, exactly as in
  <code>せずに</code> and <code>せざるを{f|得|え}ない</code>. This is the most
  frequent error with the form.

- ❌ <b>{f|来|き}ずとも</b> → ✅ <b>{f|来|こ}ずとも</b>
  {f|来|く}る is irregular: the negative stem is こ, as in {f|来|こ}ない.

- ❌ {f|買|か}わ<b>ないずとも</b> → ✅ {f|買|か}わ<b>ずとも</b>
  The base is the ない **stem**. The `ない` itself is replaced, not kept.

- ❌ {f|今日|きょう}は{f|来|こ}ずともいいよ、{f|無理|むり}しないでね。 → ✅ {f|今日|きょう}は{f|来|こ}なくてもいいよ、{f|無理|むり}しないでね。
  A classical concessive inside a friendly text message is a register collision.
  Nothing about the grammar is wrong; the sentence simply is not one a person
  would send.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence - a chat message, a product
page, or a shouted instruction?
{f|工具|こうぐ}を{f|使|つか}わ<b>ずとも</b>、{f|簡単|かんたん}に{f|組|く}み{f|立|た}てられます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A product page, or the instruction leaflet inside the box.

<code>ずとも</code> paired with the polite potential
<code>{f|組|く}み{f|立|た}てられます</code> is exactly the compact, faintly
dignified register that marketing and instruction copy reaches for. In chat it
would be affected, and nobody shouts in the classical negative.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese:
{f|確認|かくにん}せ<b>ずとも</b>、{f|問題|もんだい}はあるまい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|確認|かくにん}し<b>なくても</b>、{f|問題|もんだい}はないだろう。

Two things move together: <code>せずとも</code> becomes
<code>しなくても</code>, and the archaic <code>あるまい</code> becomes
<code>ないだろう</code>. Changing only one of them leaves a sentence with one foot
in each century, which is the usual result when a learner swaps a single word.

</details>

**3.** Choose: {f|傘|かさ}を{f|持|も}た（ずに・ずとも）{f|出|で}かけて、ずぶぬれになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|傘|かさ}を{f|持|も}た<b>ずに</b>{f|出|で}かけて、ずぶぬれになった。

The sentence describes *how* he went out - without an umbrella - and then reports
the consequence. That is `ずに`. <code>ずとも</code> would claim that the lack of
an umbrella made no difference, which the soaking rather contradicts.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|返事|へんじ}をしずとも、{f|意味|いみ}は{f|通|つう}じるはずだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|返事|へんじ}を<b>せずとも</b>、{f|意味|いみ}は{f|通|つう}じるはずだ。

<code>する</code> takes the negative stem <code>せ</code> in this construction, not
<code>し</code>. The same irregularity shows up wherever the classical negative
appears, so <code>せずに</code>, <code>せず</code> and
<code>せざるを{f|得|え}ない</code> all pattern together.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|言|い}わ<b>なくても</b>{f|分|わ}かるよ。
(b) {f|言|い}わ<b>ずとも</b>{f|分|わ}かる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is what a person says. It is warm, casual and addressed to someone in the room:
you don't have to spell it out, I get it.

(b) is a written line, or a line of narration, or a deliberate aphorism. The
classical form and the bare plain ending remove the listener from the sentence
altogether, so it reads as a general observation rather than a reassurance. If you
meet it in a novel it will usually be the narrator's voice; if you meet it in
speech, the speaker is quoting or performing.

</details>
