# さぞ

**Meaning** · you must be ...; it must have been ... - the speaker imagining how
something felt for somebody else

`さぞ` is a word of sympathy. You did not see the thing, you cannot feel it, and
you say so anyway: after a journey like that, you must be worn out. Everything on
this page follows from that one fact.

## Usage

The partner is not optional.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `さぞ` must be completed by a conjecture ending:
<code>だろう／でしょう</code>, <code>～ことだろう／～ことでしょう</code>, or
<code>～にちがいない</code>. A `さぞ` sentence that ends in
<code>です／ます</code> is broken.

</div>

The commonest shape is polite and slightly formulaic:

- <code>さぞお{f|疲|つか}れでしょう</code>
- <code>さぞご{f|心配|しんぱい}だったでしょう</code>
- <code>さぞ{f|驚|おどろ}かれたことでしょう</code>

Two restrictions matter as much as the ending. First, the feeling belongs to
**somebody else** - you know your own feelings, so ❌<code>さぞ{f|私|わたし}は{f|嬉|うれ}しいでしょう</code>
is nonsense. Second, `さぞ` imagines an experience, not a fact: it does not do
weather forecasts or train times, which take <code>きっと</code> or
<code>たぶん</code>.

<code>さぞかし</code> is the emphatic variant and is common;
<code>さぞや</code> is literary and old.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|長|なが}い{f|旅|たび}で、<b>さぞ</b>お{f|疲|つか}れでしょう。
You must be exhausted after such a long journey.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|合格|ごうかく}の{f|知|し}らせに、ご{f|両親|りょうしん}も<b>さぞ</b>{f|喜|よろこ}ばれたことでしょう。
Your parents must have been overjoyed at the news that you passed.
</pre>

<pre>
あんな{f|大|おお}きな{f|地震|じしん}のあとでは、<b>さぞ</b>{f|不安|ふあん}な{f|夜|よる}を{f|過|す}ごされたにちがいありません。
After an earthquake that size, you must have spent some very anxious nights.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|三十年|さんじゅうねん}{f|会|あ}っていなかったのだから、{f|再会|さいかい}の{f|夜|よる}は<b>さぞかし</b>{f|話|はなし}が{f|尽|つ}きなかっただろう。
They hadn't seen each other in thirty years, so I'm sure they talked the whole night through.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Polite, formal-leaning, and close to a set phrase. `さぞ` lives in letters, in
condolences, in hospital corridors and in the careful speech of someone
acknowledging another person's hardship. It is the language of
<code>お{f|疲|つか}れでしょう</code> and <code>おつらかったでしょう</code>, and it
carries warmth without claiming to know.

Between friends it sounds ceremonious. A friend who has just moved house wants
<code>{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}し{f|大変|たいへん}だったでしょ</code> or
<code>つかれたよね</code>, not <code>さぞお{f|疲|つか}れでしょう</code>, which would
sound like a hotel greeting. It is a tendency rather than a rule - people do use it
playfully among friends, precisely because it is so formal.

The default colour is sympathy: fatigue, worry, grief, hardship. It also does
admiring imagination, as in
<code>さぞ{f|立派|りっぱ}なお{f|宅|たく}なのでしょうね</code>, said of a house you
have never seen. What it will not do is malice; there is a sarcastic use
（<code>さぞご{f|満足|まんぞく}でしょうね</code>）and it is unmistakably barbed, so
avoid it unless you mean it.

## Contrast

### さぞ vs きっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>きっと</code> is general-purpose certainty about anything
at all. `さぞ` only imagines another person's feelings or circumstances, and always
leans sympathetic.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は<b>きっと</b>{f|晴|は}れますよ。
I'm sure it will clear up tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|全部|ぜんぶ}{f|準備|じゅんび}したのなら、<b>さぞ</b>{f|大変|たいへん}だったでしょう。
If you got it all ready on your own, it must have been quite something.
</pre>

Swap them and only one direction survives. <code>きっと{f|大変|たいへん}だったでしょう</code>
is perfectly good Japanese, a little plainer. ❌<code>さぞ{f|明日|あした}は{f|晴|は}れるでしょう</code>
is not: the weather has no feelings for the speaker to imagine.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ さぞお{f|疲|つか}れです。 → ✅ さぞお{f|疲|つか}れ<b>でしょう</b>。
  The conjecture ending is obligatory. `さぞ` announces that you are guessing, so a
  flat assertion contradicts it.

- ❌ {f|試験|しけん}に{f|受|う}かって、さぞ{f|嬉|うれ}しいです。 → ✅ {f|試験|しけん}に{f|受|う}かって、<b>とても</b>{f|嬉|うれ}しいです。
  Your own joy is not something you infer. Use a plain intensifier for your own
  feelings.

- ❌ さぞ{f|電車|でんしゃ}は{f|遅|おく}れるでしょう。 → ✅ <b>きっと</b>{f|電車|でんしゃ}は{f|遅|おく}れるでしょう。
  A prediction about a fact takes <code>きっと</code> or <code>たぶん</code>. `さぞ`
  needs a person and a feeling.

## Practice

**1.** {f|夜中|よなか}まで{f|看病|かんびょう}されていたそうですね。____お{f|疲|つか}れになったことでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>さぞ</b>お{f|疲|つか}れになったことでしょう。

Someone else's hardship, a polite honorific verb and a
<code>ことでしょう</code> ending: this is the sentence pattern `さぞ` was made for.
The speaker was not there and is imagining it, which is precisely what the adverb
signals.

</details>

**2.** Find and fix the error: さぞ{f|明日|あした}の{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|長|なが}くなるでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ <b>きっと</b>{f|明日|あした}の{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|長|なが}くなるでしょう。

The conjecture ending is there, but the content is a scheduling prediction, not
somebody's inner state. `さぞ` would only work if the sentence turned to a person -
for instance <code>さぞお{f|疲|つか}れになるでしょう</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, using さぞ:
"You must have been terribly worried while you were waiting for the results."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|結果|けっか}を{f|待|ま}っている{f|間|あいだ}、<b>さぞ</b>ご{f|心配|しんぱい}だったことでしょう。

The English "must have been" maps onto the whole frame, not onto a single word: the
adverb opens the guess and <code>だったことでしょう</code> closes it. The honorific
<code>ご{f|心配|しんぱい}</code> is what makes it sound like genuine sympathy rather
than commentary.

</details>
