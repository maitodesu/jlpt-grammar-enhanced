# ～ならでは

**Meaning** · only this one could produce it - a quality that belongs to this place, this maker or this situation and to nothing else

Historically `ならでは` is `なら` plus a negative `で(は)`: "if it were not this, then
not". Modern Japanese has kept the sense and thrown away the negative ending, and
what remains is a compliment. <code>{f|京都|きょうと}ならではの{f|味|あじ}</code> is a
flavour you would not find anywhere but Kyoto - and the sentence is telling you it is
worth having.

## Formation

The base is a **noun**, and the pattern nearly always continues with `の` into
another noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (modifying) | Noun + ならではの + Noun | {f|京都|きょうと} → {f|京都|きょうと}ならではの{f|味|あじ} |
| Noun (as predicate) | Noun + ならではだ | {f|老舗|しにせ} → この{f|気配|きくば}りは{f|老舗|しにせ}ならではだ |
| Polite predicate | Noun + ならではです | {f|手作|てづく}り → {f|手作|てづく}りならではです |
| Compound noun phrase | Long noun phrase + ならではの | {f|長年|ながねん}{f|続|つづ}く{f|店|みせ}ならではの |
| Literary residue | Noun + ならでは ～ ない | {f|彼|かれ} → {f|彼|かれ}ならでは{f|書|か}けない{f|文章|ぶんしょう} |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `の` is not optional when a noun follows.
❌<code>{f|京都|きょうと}ならでは{f|味|あじ}</code> is one of the two errors learners
make with this pattern; the other is attaching it to a verb, which it does not
accept.

</div>

## Nuance & register

`ならでは` is **evaluative and positive by default**. It is the standard vocabulary
of travel writing, restaurant reviews, product copy, tourism posters and craft
programmes on television, and it is entirely usable in speech - complimenting a
friend's cooking with <code>{f|手作|てづく}りならではですね</code> is natural and
warm. Register-wise it sits comfortably in the middle: not casual slang, not stiff.

The praise is not optional. Because the pattern says "this could come from nowhere
else" *approvingly*, it fights with a negative complement.
❌<code>{f|東京|とうきょう}ならではの{f|渋滞|じゅうたい}</code> is odd for that reason -
traffic jams are not a distinction. Speakers do sometimes use exactly that mismatch
as irony, and you will meet it, but a learner should read the irony rather than
attempt it.

Two shapes to keep apart. As a **modifier** it needs `の`:
<code>～ならではの ＋ noun</code>. As a **predicate** it drops the `の` and takes the
copula: <code>この{f|味|あじ}は{f|母|はは}ならではだ</code>. Learners tend to
generalise one of the two and produce the other wrongly.

Finally, note the neighbour `なり`. <code>{f|私|わたし}なりの{f|考|かんが}え</code>
means "my own way of thinking, such as it is" - modest. <code>ならでは</code> means
"a thing only I could have produced" - a boast. Applying `ならでは` to yourself is
therefore risky, and `なり` is the word you usually want.

## Contrast

### ～ならでは vs ～だけの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけの` states exclusivity as a fact. `ならではの` states it as
a merit.

</div>

<pre>
ここ<b>だけの</b>{f|話|はなし}なんですが、{f|来月|らいげつ}{f|異動|いどう}になります。
This is between us, but I'm being transferred next month.
</pre>

<pre>
ここ<b>ならではの</b>{f|料理|りょうり}をぜひ{f|召|め}し{f|上|あ}がってください。
Do try the dishes you can only get here.
</pre>

`だけの` covers any kind of restriction, including secrets, limits and quantities.
`ならではの` covers one kind only: a distinctive quality worth admiring. Where the
restriction is neutral or unwelcome, `だけの` is the word.

### ～ならでは vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `らしい` means **typical of** and can be a criticism.
`ならでは` means **possible only for**, and is always a compliment.

</div>

<pre>
{f|遅刻|ちこく}するなんて、{f|彼|かれ}<b>らしい</b>ね。
Turning up late - that's so like him.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|丁寧|ていねい}な{f|仕上|しあ}げは、{f|彼|かれ}<b>ならでは</b>だ。
This careful finish is something only he could produce.
</pre>

The first sentence is affectionate at best and pointed at worst; `らしい` merely
matches behaviour to a known character. The second could not be used that way, which
is a good test: if the sentence would work as a mild dig, you need `らしい`.

### ～ならでは vs ～{f|特有|とくゆう}の・～{f|独特|どくとく}の

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `特有の` and `独特の` are descriptive and can take anything,
including unpleasant things. `ならではの` is appreciative and cannot.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|病気|びょうき}<b>{f|特有|とくゆう}の</b>{f|症状|しょうじょう}が{f|現|あらわ}れた。
Symptoms characteristic of this illness appeared.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|土地|とち}<b>ならではの</b>{f|食材|しょくざい}が{f|並|なら}んでいた。
Ingredients you find only in this region were laid out.
</pre>

Swap them and only one direction fails: ❌<code>この{f|病気|びょうき}ならではの{f|症状|しょうじょう}</code>
reads as praising a disease. The other way round,
<code>この{f|土地|とち}{f|特有|とくゆう}の{f|食材|しょくざい}</code> is perfectly fine -
just cooler, the voice of a reference book rather than a menu.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|京都|きょうと}<b>ならでは</b>{f|風情|ふぜい}がある。 → ✅ {f|京都|きょうと}<b>ならではの</b>{f|風情|ふぜい}がある。
  A following noun needs `の`. The `の` is dropped only when `ならでは` ends the
  sentence with a copula.

- ❌ {f|手作|てづく}り<b>する</b>ならではの{f|味|あじ}。 → ✅ {f|手作|てづく}り<b>ならでは</b>の{f|味|あじ}。
  The base is a noun. Verbs do not attach, so nominalise -
  <code>{f|手作|てづく}り</code> rather than <code>{f|手作|てづく}りする</code>.

- ❌ {f|東京|とうきょう}<b>ならではの</b>{f|人|ひと}の{f|多|おお}さにうんざりした。 → ✅ {f|東京|とうきょう}<b>{f|特有|とくゆう}の</b>{f|人|ひと}の{f|多|おお}さにうんざりした。
  The complement has to be something admirable. Pairing `ならでは` with a complaint
  produces a sentence that praises and criticises in the same breath.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}<b>ならではの</b>{f|意見|いけん}を{f|述|の}べさせていただきます。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>なりの</b>{f|意見|いけん}を{f|述|の}べさせていただきます。
  Used about yourself, `ならでは` is self-congratulation, which sits badly with the
  humble ending. `なり` is the modest counterpart and is what the situation calls
  for.

## Practice

**1.** {f|冬|ふゆ}の{f|漁港|ぎょこう}を{f|訪|おとず}れれば、この{f|季節|きせつ}____{f|新鮮|しんせん}な{f|魚|さかな}が{f|味|あじ}わえる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|季節|きせつ}<b>ならではの</b>{f|新鮮|しんせん}な{f|魚|さかな}

A noun follows, so the `の` is required. The sentence is recommending something, which
is the appreciative register `ならでは` is built for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|梅雨|つゆ}の{f|時期|じき}（ならではの・{f|特有|とくゆう}の）{f|湿気|しっけ}で、{f|洗濯物|せんたくもの}が{f|乾|かわ}かない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|梅雨|つゆ}の{f|時期|じき}<b>{f|特有|とくゆう}の</b>{f|湿気|しっけ}

Damp that stops the washing drying is a complaint, and `ならではの` would be
congratulating the rainy season on it. `特有の` is neutral and takes unwelcome
things without difficulty.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The quiet you get in a mountain village at night is something no city can offer."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|夜|よる}の{f|山|やま}の{f|集落|しゅうらく}<b>ならではの</b>{f|静|しず}けさは、{f|都会|とかい}では{f|決|けっ}して{f|味|あじ}わえない。

The English "something no city can offer" is doing exactly what `ならではの` does -
naming a quality as belonging to one place alone. Note that
<code>{f|静|しず}けさ</code>, the noun, is what the pattern modifies.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|音|おと}の{f|良|よ}さは、アナログレコードならでは{f|魅力|みりょく}です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|音|おと}の{f|良|よ}さは、アナログレコード<b>ならではの</b>{f|魅力|みりょく}です。
（or … アナログレコード<b>ならではです</b>）

Either put the `の` in and keep {f|魅力|みりょく} as the head noun, or drop
{f|魅力|みりょく} and let `ならではです` end the sentence. What you cannot do is have a
noun following `ならでは` with nothing joining them.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|子供|こども}<b>らしい</b>{f|発想|はっそう}だ。
(b) {f|子供|こども}<b>ならではの</b>{f|発想|はっそう}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says the idea is what you would expect from a child. Warmly said it is charming;
said flatly about an adult's proposal it is a put-down.

(b) can only be praise. It says the idea is one an adult could not have arrived at,
so the childishness is being treated as an advantage. In a design meeting, (a) risks
offence and (b) is a compliment.

</details>
