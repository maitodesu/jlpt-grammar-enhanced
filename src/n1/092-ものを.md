# ～ものを

**Meaning** · if only; and yet - a better outcome was there for the taking, nobody took it, and the speaker is not letting it go

`ものを` is not really about grammar; it is about a feeling. The sentence names a
door that was standing open - *just apologise*, *just say something*, *just see a
doctor* - and then reports that the person walked past it. What comes with that is
reproach if the person is somebody else, and bitter regret if it is the speaker.
It is one kana away from `ものの`, and it does an entirely different job.

## Formation

<code>もの</code> is a noun, and the `を` behind it is an old adversative particle
that survives almost nowhere else in the modern language. The clause in front is in
noun-modifying shape, and in practice it is nearly always a conditional.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ば-conditional + いい) | ～ばいい + ものを | {f|謝|あやま}ればいい → {f|謝|あやま}ればいいものを |
| Verb (ば-conditional + よかった) | ～ばよかった + ものを | {f|言|い}ってくれればよかったものを |
| Verb (たら-conditional) | ～たらいい + ものを | {f|休|やす}んだらいいものを |
| Verb (plain, past or present) | plain form + ものを | {f|治|なお}った → {f|治|なお}ったものを |
| Sentence-final | …ものを。(the rest left unsaid) | {f|一言|ひとこと}{f|言|い}ってくれればよかったものを。 |
| Noun / adjective | does not occur - use `のに` | - |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the clause before `ものを` must describe **the better outcome
that was available**, and the clause after it (when there is one) reports **what
actually happened instead**. Both halves are statements. A request or an
instruction cannot follow it: ❌<code>{f|時間|じかん}があるものを、{f|手伝|てつだ}ってください</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the second half is very often swallowed. Ending on `ものを` and trailing
off is the pattern's most characteristic move, and it leaves the listener to supply
the disaster: 「{f|早|はや}めに{f|相談|そうだん}してくれればよかったものを…」

</div>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; do not say it.** `ものを` belongs to written and performed
Japanese: novels and the dialogue inside them, period drama, film subtitles, song
lyrics, and the occasional written complaint that wants to sound wounded. It also
survives in the speech of some older speakers, usually while telling somebody off.
Said by a learner in ordinary conversation it does not sound merely formal - it
sounds like a line delivered from a stage, and the listener will hear the costume.
The modern equivalents are `～ばよかったのに` and `～ばいいのに`, and they are what
you should actually produce.

The feeling it carries is specific enough to be worth naming. There is always a
**better path that was open and was not taken**, and the speaker is aggrieved about
it. Pointed at somebody else, that comes out as reproach - *you could have just told
me* - and it is not a gentle reproach; it implies the person's stubbornness or pride
caused the mess. Pointed at oneself or at the dead, it comes out as lament.

Because the emotion is built in, `ものを` cannot be neutral. A newspaper reporting a
policy failure will use `にもかかわらず`; a novelist describing a character's ruin
will use `ものを`. The tell is that the second half is usually something the speaker
minds about - and very often the second half is not said at all, which is more
eloquent than saying it.

## Contrast

### ～ものを vs ～ばよかったのに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same complaint, one archaic and one current.
`～ばよかったのに` is what a modern speaker says; `ものを` is what a modern speaker
reads.

</div>

<pre>
{f|言|い}ってくれれば{f|手伝|てつだ}った<b>のに</b>。
You should have said - I'd have helped.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|言|い}ってくれれば{f|手伝|てつだ}った<b>ものを</b>。
Had you but said the word, I would have helped.
</pre>

The content is identical. The difference is entirely one of register and era: the
first is what you would text a friend, the second is what a character says in a
novel two pages before something goes badly wrong. If you are choosing a form to
produce, choose the first every time.

### ～ものを vs ～ものの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one kana apart, and nothing else in common. `ものの` concedes a
fact and reports a shortfall, with no emotion required. `ものを` reproaches somebody
for a chance they let go.

</div>

<pre>
{f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}んだ<b>ものの</b>、{f|抽選|ちゅうせん}に{f|外|はず}れた。
I applied, but I lost out in the ballot.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}めば{f|当|あ}たった<b>ものを</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|面倒|めんどう}がって{f|出|だ}さなかった。
He would have got a place if he had applied; he couldn't be bothered to send the form.
</pre>

`ものの` takes what did happen and reports the disappointing sequel. `ものを` takes
what did **not** happen and grieves over it, which is why its first clause is a
conditional and `ものの`'s never is. If you can put "although" in front of the
English, you want `ものの`; if you can put "if only", you want `ものを`.

### ～ものを vs ～ばよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～ばよかった` is regret with **no one in the dock**. `ものを`
puts somebody there, and usually it is not the speaker.

</div>

<pre>
もっと{f|早|はや}く{f|家|いえ}を{f|出|で}れ<b>ばよかった</b>。
I should have left home earlier.
</pre>

<pre>
もっと{f|早|はや}く{f|出|で}ればよかった<b>ものを</b>、{f|彼|かれ}はぎりぎりまで{f|動|うご}かなかった。
He could easily have set off earlier; he sat there until the last minute.
</pre>

The first sentence is a person kicking themselves, and it is completely ordinary
Japanese. The second passes judgement on someone's behaviour. That is why `ものを`
addressed straight to the person in front of you is a genuinely sharp thing to say,
even before its old-fashioned flavour is taken into account.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った<b>ものを</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|行|おこな}われた。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った<b>ものの</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|行|おこな}われた。
  Plain concession is `ものの`'s job. `ものを` needs a missed opportunity, and rain
  is not one.

- ❌ {f|時間|じかん}がある<b>ものを</b>、{f|手伝|てつだ}ってください。 → ✅ {f|時間|じかん}があるなら、{f|手伝|てつだ}ってください。
  Nothing that follows `ものを` may be a request or an order. Both halves are
  reports, and the second one is the bad news.

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>ものを</b>、{f|勉強|べんきょう}しない。 → ✅ {f|勉強|べんきょう}すればいい<b>ものを</b>、{f|遊|あそ}んでばかりいる。
  A bare noun cannot sit in front of it. What goes there is a clause describing the
  better course of action.

- ❌ （to a friend, casually）{f|連絡|れんらく}してくれればよかった<b>ものを</b>。 → ✅ {f|連絡|れんらく}してくれればよかった<b>のに</b>。
  Not a grammar error - a register error. Among friends the archaic form lands as
  theatre or sarcasm, and the friend will hear it as either.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence? 「{f|逃|に}げれば{f|助|たす}かった<b>ものを</b>。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In fiction or drama - a line of narration, or a character speaking, very often in a
historical setting. It is the voice of someone looking at a disaster and naming the
escape route that was not used.

You would not hear it in an office, a shop or a conversation between friends; there
the same thought is 「{f|逃|に}げればよかったのに」.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: {f|早|はや}く{f|病院|びょういん}へ{f|行|い}けばよかった<b>ものを</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|放|ほう}っておいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|早|はや}く{f|病院|びょういん}へ{f|行|い}けばよかった<b>のに</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|放|ほう}っておいた。

Swapping `ものを` for `のに` keeps the meaning and the reproach exactly, and drops
the literary costume. This is the substitution to reach for whenever you are
tempted to produce `ものを` yourself.

</details>

**3.** Choose: {f|注意|ちゅうい}はした（ものの・ものを）、{f|誰|だれ}も{f|聞|き}いていなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|注意|ちゅうい}はした<b>ものの</b>、{f|誰|だれ}も{f|聞|き}いていなかった。

The warning was actually given, and the sentence reports the disappointing sequel -
that is `ものの`. `ものを` would require the first clause to be something that never
happened, such as 「{f|注意|ちゅうい}すればよかったものを」.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|安|やす}いものを、{f|品質|ひんしつ}もいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|安|やす}い<b>のに</b>、{f|品質|ひんしつ}もいい。（or {f|安|やす}い<b>うえに</b>、{f|品質|ひんしつ}もいい）

Two things are wrong. `ものを` never carries a **good** outcome - it exists to
lament a bad one - and it does not attach to an い-adjective in this way. As
written, {f|安|やす}いものを would be read as "a cheap one", the ordinary noun
<code>もの</code> with an object marker.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|相談|そうだん}してくれればよかった<b>のに</b>。
(b) {f|相談|そうだん}してくれればよかった<b>ものを</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The propositional content is the same: you did not consult me, and you should have.

(a) is live modern Japanese and can be said warmly - a friend gently telling you off
for struggling alone. Tone of voice decides how hard it lands.

(b) cannot be said warmly. The archaic frame formalises the reproach and puts a
distance between the speaker and the person, so it reads as the summing-up of
someone who has already given up on being listened to. In modern speech it would
also sound staged; you will meet it on the page far more often than in a room.

</details>
