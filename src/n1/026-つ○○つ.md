# ～つ～つ

**Meaning** · back and forth between two opposite actions - alternating, first one then the other, over and over

Two verbs in the ます-stem, each followed by `つ`, describe a pair of actions that keep
trading places: going and coming back, overtaking and being overtaken, giving and being
given. The `つ` is an Old Japanese auxiliary, and the construction stopped being
productive centuries ago. What survives is a short list of fixed expressions.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two opposite verbs | ます-stem + つ + ます-stem + つ | {f|行|い}く・{f|戻|もど}る → {f|行|い}きつ{f|戻|もど}りつ |
| Active + its passive | ます-stem + つ + ～れ + つ | {f|抜|ぬ}く・{f|抜|ぬ}かれる → {f|抜|ぬ}きつ{f|抜|ぬ}かれつ |
| Used adverbially | ～つ～つ + verb | {f|押|お}しつ{f|押|お}されつ{f|進|すす}む |
| Used as a predicate | ～つ～つ + だ／する | {f|持|も}ちつ{f|持|も}たれつだ |
| Modifying a noun | ～つ～つ + の + Noun | {f|抜|ぬ}きつ{f|抜|ぬ}かれつの{f|展開|てんかい} |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in practice you will meet perhaps six of these and no more:
{f|持|も}ちつ{f|持|も}たれつ、{f|行|い}きつ{f|戻|もど}りつ、{f|抜|ぬ}きつ{f|抜|ぬ}かれつ、{f|追|お}いつ{f|追|お}われつ、{f|差|さ}しつ{f|差|さ}されつ、{f|浮|う}きつ{f|沈|しず}みつ、{f|押|お}しつ{f|押|お}されつ.
A related shape with `ん` instead of `つ` on the first verb survives in one idiom,
{f|組|く}んずほぐれつ, "grappling".

</div>

## Nuance & register

**This is an archaic form, and it is not productive.** You cannot build new ones. There
is no ❌<code>{f|食|た}べつ{f|飲|の}みつ</code>, no ❌<code>{f|書|か}きつ{f|消|け}しつ</code>;
inventing a pair does not sound literary, it sounds like a mistake. Learn the handful
that exist, recognise them on the page, and say
<code>～たり～たり</code> when you need the meaning yourself.

Where the survivors actually live:

- **{f|持|も}ちつ{f|持|も}たれつ** is the one genuine exception. It is a live idiom,
  used in ordinary adult speech, and means "we help each other out" - said of business
  partners, neighbours, in-laws. If you use one expression from this page, use this one.
- **{f|抜|ぬ}きつ{f|抜|ぬ}かれつ** and **{f|追|お}いつ{f|追|お}われつ** belong to sports
  commentary and race reporting, where they are close to cliché but entirely current.
- **{f|行|い}きつ{f|戻|もど}りつ**, **{f|浮|う}きつ{f|沈|しず}みつ**,
  **{f|押|お}しつ{f|押|お}されつ** are narrative prose - a novel, a memoir, a piece of
  travel writing. They describe a body moving, and a reader hears them as slightly
  written-up.
- **{f|差|さ}しつ{f|差|さ}されつ** is a set description of a drinking scene, most often
  with sake and most often in writing about the past.

The `つ` here is unrelated to the counter or to the `つ` of
<code>{f|一|ひと}つ</code>. It is the perfective auxiliary of classical Japanese, the
same one behind <code>{f|果|は}てつ</code> in old texts, and the alternating
<code>～つ～つ</code> shape is one of the few places it survives in modern Japanese at
all. That history is worth knowing only because it explains why the pattern is closed:
there is no living rule left to apply.

## Contrast

### ～つ～つ vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～たり～たり</code> is the modern equivalent and is what you
should produce. It attaches to any verbs at all, in any register.

</div>

<pre>
{f|部屋|へや}の{f|中|なか}を<b>{f|行|い}ったり{f|来|き}たり</b>しながら、{f|返事|へんじ}を{f|考|かんが}えた。
I walked back and forth across the room while I thought about what to reply.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|老人|ろうじん}は{f|窓辺|まどべ}を<b>{f|行|い}きつ{f|戻|もど}りつ</b>していた。
The old man moved back and forth by the window.
</pre>

The content is identical; the second sentence has been written rather than said. Note
also that <code>～たり～たり</code> can list more than two actions and does not need them
to be opposites - <code>{f|読|よ}んだり{f|書|か}いたりする</code> is simply "read, write
and so on". <code>～つ～つ</code> is locked to exactly two, and they must be a pair.

If you are ever tempted to reach for <code>～つ～つ</code> in your own Japanese, this is
the substitution to make.

### ～つ～つ vs ～ては～、～ては～

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～ては</code> repeats a **cycle** and often carries frustration
or futility. <code>～つ～つ</code> just reports the alternation, with no attitude.

</div>

<pre>
{f|書|か}い<b>ては</b>{f|消|け}し、{f|書|か}い<b>ては</b>{f|消|け}しして、{f|一晩|ひとばん}かかってしまった。
I wrote and deleted, wrote and deleted, and it took me the whole night.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|波|なみ}にもまれて、{f|木|き}の{f|葉|は}が<b>{f|浮|う}きつ{f|沈|しず}みつ</b>{f|流|なが}れていった。
Tossed by the waves, the leaf floated away, bobbing under and up again.
</pre>

<code>～ては</code> is fully modern, fully productive, and usable in speech; it is the
pattern to use when you want to say that something kept happening and got nowhere.
<code>～つ～つ</code> has no such colour. It is a camera, not a complaint.

### ～つ～つ vs ～つつ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they look alike and mean different things. <code>～つつ</code> after
**one** ます-stem is a formal "while". <code>～つ～つ</code> needs **two** verbs and means
"alternately".

</div>

<pre>
{f|危険|きけん}だと{f|知|し}り<b>つつ</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|扉|とびら}を{f|開|あ}けた。
Knowing it was dangerous, he opened the door all the same.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|両者|りょうしゃ}は{f|終盤|しゅうばん}まで<b>{f|追|お}いつ{f|追|お}われつ</b>の{f|接戦|せっせん}を{f|演|えん}じた。
The two of them fought out a close contest right to the end, each in turn ahead of the other.
</pre>

<code>～つつ</code> is also literary, but it is *productive* - you can attach it to any
verb and be understood, and <code>～つつある</code> ("is in the process of") is
completely standard in news writing. <code>～つ～つ</code> can do neither. Seeing a single
`つ` followed by another `つ` immediately means "while"; seeing `つ` after each of two
different verb stems means alternation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|食|た}べつ{f|飲|の}みつ{f|話|はな}した。 → ✅ {f|食|た}べたり{f|飲|の}んだりしながら{f|話|はな}した。
  The pattern is closed. Coining a new pair does not produce literary Japanese; it
  produces something a reader will stop and puzzle over.

- ❌ {f|持|も}ちつ<b>{f|持|も}ちつ</b>の{f|関係|かんけい} → ✅ {f|持|も}ちつ<b>{f|持|も}たれつ</b>の{f|関係|かんけい}
  The second verb is the **passive**. The idiom means "holding up and being held up", so
  repeating the active form destroys the sense.

- ❌ {f|行|い}くつ{f|戻|もど}るつ → ✅ {f|行|い}きつ{f|戻|もど}りつ
  `つ` follows the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

- ❌ {f|歩|ある}きつ{f|考|かんが}えつ{f|答|こた}えを{f|探|さが}した。 → ✅ {f|歩|ある}き<b>つつ</b>{f|答|こた}えを{f|探|さが}した。
  Walking and thinking are not opposites and do not alternate; they overlap. That is
  <code>～つつ</code>, with the doubled `つ` on a single verb.

## Practice

**1.** Complete the set phrase: {f|困|こま}ったときはお{f|互|たが}いさま、{f|持|も}ちつ______でいきましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|持|も}ちつ<b>{f|持|も}たれつ</b>でいきましょう。

The second half of the idiom is the **passive** {f|持|も}たれ, giving "supporting and
being supported". Paired with
<code>お{f|互|たが}いさま</code> it is the standard way of waving off a debt of gratitude
between people who expect to go on helping each other.

</details>

**2.** Where would you expect to hear or read this, and why? {f|両|りょう}チームは{f|最終|さいしゅう}{f|回|かい}まで<b>{f|追|お}いつ{f|追|お}われつ</b>の{f|熱戦|ねっせん}を{f|繰|く}り{f|広|ひろ}げた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A sports report - a newspaper match summary, a broadcast wrap-up, a club newsletter.
<code>{f|抜|ぬ}きつ{f|抜|ぬ}かれつ</code> and
<code>{f|追|お}いつ{f|追|お}われつ</code> are near-fixed expressions in that genre, and
<code>{f|熱戦|ねっせん}を{f|繰|く}り{f|広|ひろ}げる</code> is from the same stock of
phrases. Nobody describes their weekend five-a-side this way in conversation.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: {f|少年|しょうねん}は{f|門|もん}の{f|前|まえ}を{f|行|い}きつ{f|戻|もど}りつしていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|少年|しょうねん}は{f|門|もん}の{f|前|まえ}を<b>{f|行|い}ったり{f|来|き}たりしていた</b>。

<code>～たり～たり</code> is the everyday equivalent, and
<code>{f|行|い}ったり{f|来|き}たり</code> is its own fixed pair for pacing. Note the
reading: {f|来|き}たり from {f|来|く}る, not こ-. You could also say
<code>{f|門|もん}の{f|前|まえ}をうろうろしていた</code>, which adds the restlessness the
original only implies.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|友達|ともだち}と{f|飲|の}みつ{f|食|た}べつ、{f|楽|たの}しい{f|夜|よる}だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|友達|ともだち}と<b>{f|飲|の}んだり{f|食|た}べたり</b>して、{f|楽|たの}しい{f|夜|よる}だった。

Eating and drinking are not an opposed pair, and in any case the pattern admits no new
members. The intended meaning is a list of things you did, which is
<code>～たり～たり</code>. If you wanted the drinking-party image specifically, the
existing idiom is <code>{f|差|さ}しつ{f|差|さ}されつ</code> - though that would put the
sentence into a novel.

</details>

**5.** Two sentences describe the same scene. What has changed?
(a) {f|舟|ふね}は{f|波|なみ}に<b>{f|浮|う}きつ{f|沈|しず}みつ</b>{f|沖|おき}へ{f|離|はな}れていった。
(b) {f|舟|ふね}は{f|波|なみ}で<b>{f|浮|う}いたり{f|沈|しず}んだり</b>しながら{f|沖|おき}へ{f|離|はな}れていった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Nothing in the facts. (a) is narration: the compressed
<code>{f|浮|う}きつ{f|沈|しず}みつ</code> and the omission of しながら make it read like a
line from a novel, and the reader takes it as the narrator's voice rather than a
speaker's.

(b) is how the same thing is described out loud, or in a diary, or in a news bulletin.
It is longer, looser and completely neutral. A learner should be able to read (a) and
write (b), and that division of labour is what a recognise-only pattern is for.

</details>
