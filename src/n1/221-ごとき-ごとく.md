# ～ごとき / ～ごとく

**Meaning** · like; as if - the classical `～のような / ～のように`. Separately, attached straight to a person, `ごとき` means "someone as insignificant as ~"

<code>{f|如|ごと}し</code> is the classical auxiliary of comparison. Its adverbial
form is <code>ごとく</code> ("like, as"), its noun-modifying form
<code>ごとき</code> ("a ~ like this"), and its sentence-final form
<code>ごとし</code>. That half of the page is literary and you should read it, not
write it. But `ごとき` has a second life as a **dismissive suffix** -
{f|私|わたし}ごとき, お{f|前|まえ}ごとき - and that one is alive in speech.

## Formation

The comparative use needs `の` after a noun; the dismissive use must **not** have
it. That single particle is the whole difference.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (comparison) | Noun + の + ごとく / ごとき / ごとし | {f|矢|や} → {f|矢|や}の{f|如|ごと}し |
| Verb | plain form + (が) + ごとく | {f|流|なが}れる → {f|流|なが}れるが{f|如|ごと}く |
| With かの | ～かの + ごとく / ごとき | {f|知|し}っていた → {f|知|し}っていたかの{f|如|ごと}く |
| Adverbial ("like ~") | ～の{f|如|ごと}く + verb | {f|風|かぜ}の{f|如|ごと}く{f|去|さ}る |
| Noun-modifying | ～の{f|如|ごと}き + noun | {f|氷|こおり}の{f|如|ごと}き{f|冷|つめ}たさ |
| Sentence-final | ～の{f|如|ごと}し | {f|光陰|こういん}{f|矢|や}の{f|如|ごと}し |
| **Dismissive suffix** | Person + ごとき (no の) | {f|私|わたし} → {f|私|わたし}ごとき |
| Set phrase in documents | {f|上記|じょうき}／{f|下記|かき}の{f|如|ごと}く | {f|上記|じょうき}の{f|如|ごと}く |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kanji {f|如|ごと} is optional and both spellings are standard.
Legal documents, older prose and set phrases tend to write it; modern journalism
often uses kana. Learn to recognise {f|如|ごと}く, {f|如|ごと}き and {f|如|ごと}し on sight; the
reading is not one you could guess from the kanji's other uses ({f|如何|いかが},
{f|欠如|けつじょ}).

</div>

## Nuance & register

The two uses have to be separated, because they live in different worlds.

**The comparison (ごとく / ごとき / ごとし) is literary and you should not produce
it.** It belongs to proverbs ({f|光陰|こういん}{f|矢|や}の{f|如|ごと}し), formal
documents ({f|上記|じょうき}の{f|如|ごと}く, {f|下記|かき}の{f|如|ごと}く), speeches,
and novels reaching for grandeur. Said in conversation it is not "very formal", it
is theatrical - somewhere between a sermon and a period drama. The modern forms are
`のように` and `のような`, and they say everything <code>ごとく</code> says.

**The dismissive suffix (ごとき straight after a person) is alive.** It has two
directions:

- **Humble**, about yourself: {f|私|わたし}ごとき, {f|僕|ぼく}ごとき - "someone as
  unimportant as me". This is genuinely used, in formal speech and in polite
  written Japanese, as a way of lowering yourself before saying something.
- **Contemptuous**, about someone else: お{f|前|まえ}ごとき,
  {f|素人|しろうと}ごとき, あいつごとき - "the likes of you". This is rude, and
  deliberately so. It is common in fiction, sport and argument, and you will
  certainly hear it; using it about a real person is picking a fight.

Note that the dismissive use takes **no の** - {f|私|わたし}ごとき, never
❌{f|私|わたし}のごとき - and that the noun in front of it is almost always a person
or a category of person. This is the one part of the page you might reasonably use
yourself, and even then only the humble direction.

A practical reading note: because <code>ごとき</code> also serves the comparison,
context decides. <code>{f|子供|こども}のごとき{f|字|じ}</code> (with の) is
"handwriting like a child's". <code>{f|子供|こども}ごときに</code> (without の) is
"to a mere child". The particle is doing all the work.

## Contrast

### ～の{f|如|ごと}く vs ～のように

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. `のように` is the living form;
<code>の{f|如|ごと}く</code> is what a proverb, a legal document or a novel uses in
its place.

</div>

<pre>
{f|水|みず}が{f|流|なが}れる<b>ように</b>{f|話|はなし}が{f|進|すす}んだ。
The discussion flowed along like water.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|時|とき}は{f|水|みず}の<b>{f|如|ごと}く</b>{f|流|なが}れ、{f|村|むら}の{f|姿|すがた}も{f|変|か}わった。
Time flowed like water, and the village changed with it.
</pre>

Everything the classical form adds is register. Notice that the second sentence has
also picked up {f|姿|すがた} and a literary rhythm - as with the rest of this group,
<code>ごとく</code> is not something you can drop into an ordinary sentence. Also
watch the attachment: after a verb the classical takes が
({f|流|なが}れる<b>が</b>{f|如|ごと}く) where the modern takes nothing at all.

### {f|私|わたし}ごとき vs {f|私|わたし}なんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same move, two registers. `なんか` / `など` is how you
belittle something in speech; <code>ごとき</code> is how you do it in formal
Japanese or in writing.

</div>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}<b>なんか</b>には{f|無理|むり}ですよ。
Someone like me could never manage it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}<b>ごとき</b>にそのような{f|大役|たいやく}が{f|務|つと}まりましょうか。
How could a person as slight as myself carry out so great a duty?
</pre>

Both lower the speaker, and Japanese does this constantly as a politeness move.
`なんか` is neutral and everyday; <code>ごとき</code> is ceremonial, and is what you
would meet in a formal letter of acceptance or a speech of thanks. Turned outward,
both become rude - お{f|前|まえ}なんか and お{f|前|まえ}ごとき are both insults,
the second the colder of the two.

### ～{f|如|ごと}し vs ～かのようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かのようだ` marks the comparison as **counterfactual**: it
looks that way but is not. <code>{f|如|ごと}し</code> is a plain resemblance, with
no such claim - unless you add かの to it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|何|なに}も{f|知|し}らない<b>かのように</b>ふるまった。
He behaved as though he knew nothing.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山|やま}は{f|眠|ねむ}れる{f|巨人|きょじん}の<b>{f|如|ごと}く</b>{f|横|よこ}たわっていた。
The mountain lay there like a sleeping giant.
</pre>

The first tells you he did know. The second makes no claim about giants at all; it
is a straight simile. When a writer does want the counterfactual reading in
classical dress, the form is
<code>かの{f|如|ごと}く</code> - and that combination is common in editorials
describing someone pretending:
<code>{f|何|なに}も{f|起|お}きなかったかの{f|如|ごと}く</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|山|やま}<b>{f|如|ごと}し</b> → ✅ {f|山|やま}の<b>{f|如|ごと}し</b>
  The comparison needs の after a noun. Dropping it turns the phrase into the
  dismissive suffix, which is a different meaning entirely.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}の<b>ごとき</b>{f|者|もの}には{f|無理|むり}です → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>ごとき</b>{f|者|もの}には{f|無理|むり}です
  And the reverse: the dismissive use takes no の. With の, the sentence turns into
  "a person resembling me", which is not what you meant.

- ❌ {f|流|なが}れる<b>のごとく</b> → ✅ {f|流|なが}れる<b>が{f|如|ごと}く</b>／{f|流|なが}れる<b>{f|如|ごと}く</b>
  After a verb the classical linker is が, not の. Modern `ように` takes neither,
  which is where the confusion starts.

- ❌ お{f|前|まえ}<b>ごとき</b>に{f|教|おし}えていただきたいです。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>ごとき</b>にご{f|指導|しどう}いただければ{f|幸|さいわ}いです。
  <code>ごとき</code> aimed at the listener is an insult. Aimed at yourself it is
  humility. Getting the direction wrong is not a grammar error but a serious social
  one.

- ❌（{f|友|とも}だちに）{f|昨日|きのう}のケーキ、{f|雲|くも}の<b>{f|如|ごと}く</b>やわらかかったよ。 → ✅ {f|昨日|きのう}のケーキ、{f|雲|くも}<b>みたいに</b>やわらかかったよ。
  In conversation the simile word is `みたい`. <code>{f|如|ごと}く</code> over a
  slice of cake is a joke whether you meant one or not.

## Practice

**1.** {f|報告書|ほうこくしょ}の{f|書|か}き{f|出|だ}し: {f|調査|ちょうさ}の{f|結果|けっか}は{f|下記|かき}の____{f|報告|ほうこく}いたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|下記|かき}の<b>{f|如|ごと}く</b>

<code>{f|上記|じょうき}の{f|如|ごと}く</code> and
<code>{f|下記|かき}の{f|如|ごと}く</code> are frozen phrases of documentary
Japanese, and they are among the very few places a modern writer still produces
<code>ごとく</code>. The everyday equivalent, {f|下記|かき}のように, is also correct
and slightly less starched.

</details>

**2.** Choose, and say why: {f|新人|しんじん}の{f|私|わたし}（ごとき・のごとき）が{f|司会|しかい}を{f|務|つと}めてよいのでしょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|私|わたし}<b>ごとき</b>が{f|司会|しかい}を{f|務|つと}めてよいのでしょうか。

The dismissive-humble use takes no の. With の the phrase would mean "someone
resembling me", which is not the point - the speaker is lowering *themselves*
before accepting a role, which is a standard and genuinely useful politeness move.

</details>

**3.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|何事|なにごと}もなかったかの<b>{f|如|ごと}く</b>{f|席|せき}に{f|戻|もど}った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|何事|なにごと}もなかった<b>かのように</b>{f|席|せき}に{f|戻|もど}った。

Only one word changes: <code>かの{f|如|ごと}く</code> → `かのように`. The かの
carries the "but it was not so" meaning in both versions, so the classical form is
adding nothing here except a century.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|川|かわ}が{f|流|なが}れるのごとく、{f|時間|じかん}が{f|過|す}ぎていった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|川|かわ}が{f|流|なが}れる<b>が{f|如|ごと}く</b>、{f|時間|じかん}が{f|過|す}ぎていった。
In modern Japanese: {f|川|かわ}の{f|流|なが}れのように、{f|時間|じかん}が{f|過|す}ぎていった。

After a verb, classical <code>{f|如|ごと}し</code> is linked with が, not の. The
の form is only for nouns: {f|川|かわ}の{f|如|ごと}く.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|素人|しろうと}のような{f|意見|いけん}で{f|申|もう}し{f|訳|わけ}ありません。
(b) {f|素人|しろうと}ごときの{f|意見|いけん}で{f|申|もう}し{f|訳|わけ}ありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain, mild apology: the opinion is an amateur's. It is neutral and could
be said by anyone in a meeting.

(b) puts the speaker down harder. <code>ごとき</code> attached to
{f|素人|しろうと} says "a mere amateur, of no account", and coming from the speaker
about themselves it reads as marked humility - appropriate in a formal setting,
faintly overdone among colleagues. Said of *someone else*
({f|素人|しろうと}ごときの{f|意見|いけん}は{f|聞|き}く{f|必要|ひつよう}がない) the
same phrase is contemptuous.

</details>
