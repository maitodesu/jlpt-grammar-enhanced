# ことこの上ない・この上ない

**Meaning** · there is nothing above this - utterly, supremely, in the extreme. Read この{f|上|うえ}ない. Literary and ceremonial; you will meet it in print far more often than in speech.

The phrase says literally "there is nothing on top of this". Two shapes come out of
it. <code>この{f|上|うえ}ない</code> in front of a noun, and its adverb
<code>この{f|上|うえ}なく</code>, are the language of formal greetings and award
speeches: {f|光栄|こうえい}, {f|喜|よろこ}び, {f|幸|しあわ}せ.
<code>～ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない</code> after an adjective is a written predicate,
and it usually complains: {f|面倒|めんどう}, {f|迷惑|めいわく},
{f|危険|きけん}.

## Formation

The predicate form takes an adjective, never a plain verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | plain form + ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない | {f|嬉|うれ}しい → {f|嬉|うれ}しいことこの{f|上|うえ}ない |
| な-adjective | ～な + ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない | {f|面倒|めんどう}だ → {f|面倒|めんどう}なことこの{f|上|うえ}ない |
| Noun (evaluative) | Noun + この{f|上|うえ}ない | {f|失礼|しつれい} → {f|失礼|しつれい}この{f|上|うえ}ない |
| Before a noun | この{f|上|うえ}ない + noun | この{f|上|うえ}ない{f|光栄|こうえい} |
| Adverbially | この{f|上|うえ}なく + adjective | この{f|上|うえ}なく{f|幸|しあわ}せだ |
| Polite | ～この{f|上|うえ}ありません / ございません | {f|光栄|こうえい}この{f|上|うえ}ございません |
| Not possible | plain verb + ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない | ❌{f|走|はし}ることこの{f|上|うえ}ない |
| Not possible | この{f|上|うえ}なく + noun | ❌この{f|上|うえ}なく{f|喜|よろこ}び |

<div class="note">

**Note** - keep the two endings apart. <code>ない</code> is the adjective form and
goes before a noun (この{f|上|うえ}ない{f|喜|よろこ}び);
<code>なく</code> is the adverb and goes before another adjective or a verb
(この{f|上|うえ}なく{f|嬉|うれ}しい). Its nearest relative is
{f|極|きわ}まりない, treated in its own entry; the two overlap heavily in the
complaining use.

</div>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this; use something plainer.** The natural homes of the phrase are a
printed greeting, an acceptance speech, a company announcement, a piece of literary
narration, and - for the ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない half - a written column grumbling
about something. It is not impossible in speech, but only in prepared speech. In
conversation the plain equivalents are <code>{f|最高|さいこう}に{f|嬉|うれ}しい</code>,
<code>{f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|面倒|めんどう}だ</code>, and, if you want a fixed polite
formula, <code>{f|大変|たいへん}{f|光栄|こうえい}です</code>. Say one of those.

The two halves lean in **opposite emotional directions**, and this is the most
useful thing to notice about the phrase. <code>この{f|上|うえ}ない</code> before a
noun overwhelmingly attracts good things - {f|光栄|こうえい},
{f|喜|よろこ}び, {f|名誉|めいよ}, {f|幸|しあわ}せ - and sounds gracious.
<code>～ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない</code> after an adjective attracts bad ones -
{f|面倒|めんどう}, {f|迷惑|めいわく}, {f|危険|きけん}, {f|不便|ふべん} - and
sounds exasperated, often with a dry, half-amused edge, which is why it turns up in
essays and opinion columns. Neither is a hard rule
({f|嬉|うれ}しいことこの{f|上|うえ}ない is perfectly good), but the tendency is
strong enough to rely on when you are reading.

The polite forms are worth recognising because the fixed greetings use them:
<code>{f|光栄|こうえい}この{f|上|うえ}ございません</code> and
<code>この{f|上|うえ}ない{f|光栄|こうえい}に{f|存|ぞん}じます</code> are stock
lines at ceremonies. Treat them as phrases to identify, not as building blocks.

## Contrast

### ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない vs {f|本当|ほんとう}に・{f|最高|さいこう}に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the ordinary intensifiers say the same thing and can be spoken
aloud. Use them; keep this pattern for reading.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|手続|てつづ}き、<b>{f|本当|ほんとう}に</b>{f|面倒|めんどう}だね。
This paperwork is a real pain, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|手続|てつづ}きは{f|面倒|めんどう}な<b>ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない</b>。
This procedure is a nuisance in the extreme.
</pre>

The first is a remark to a colleague at the next desk. The second is a sentence in
a column about bureaucracy. Nothing in the content separates them - only the
occasion, and using the second aloud makes the speaker sound as though they are
reading from an editorial.

### この{f|上|うえ}ない vs これ{f|以上|いじょう}ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>これ{f|以上|いじょう}ない</code> is the everyday cousin.
Same logic ("nothing beyond this"), ordinary register, and it can be spoken.

</div>

<pre>
これ<b>{f|以上|いじょう}ない</b>ほどいい{f|天気|てんき}だった。
The weather could not have been better.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|旅|たび}の{f|最終日|さいしゅうび}は<b>この{f|上|うえ}なく</b>{f|晴|は}れやかだった。
The last day of the journey was as bright as any could be.
</pre>

<code>これ{f|以上|いじょう}ない</code> normally takes ほど or くらい and behaves
like a normal comparative; <code>この{f|上|うえ}ない</code> attaches straight to a
noun and lifts the register. If you are speaking, the first is the safe one.

### ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない vs ～てたまらない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>てたまらない</code> reports a feeling the **speaker**
cannot bear. <code>ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない</code> is a **judgement about a
situation**, delivered from a step back.

</div>

<pre>
{f|待|ま}たされて、{f|腹|はら}が{f|立|た}っ<b>てたまらない</b>。
Being kept waiting, I'm furious.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一時間|いちじかん}も{f|待|ま}たされるとは、{f|不愉快|ふゆかい}な<b>ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない</b>。
To be kept waiting a full hour is disagreeable in the extreme.
</pre>

<code>てたまらない</code> is first-person and immediate, and it is ordinary spoken
Japanese. <code>ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない</code> stands outside the feeling and rates
the situation, which is why it can describe a train station or a set of rules -
things that have no feelings of their own.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|待|ま}つことこの{f|上|うえ}ない。 → ✅ {f|待|ま}ち{f|時間|じかん}が{f|長|なが}いことこの{f|上|うえ}ない。
  The predicate form needs an adjective in front, not a plain verb. Rebuild the
  clause so that an adjective is doing the work.

- ❌ {f|嬉|うれ}しいなことこの{f|上|うえ}ない → ✅ {f|嬉|うれ}しいことこの{f|上|うえ}ない
  い-adjectives join directly. The な belongs only to な-adjectives and nouns
  ({f|面倒|めんどう}なことこの{f|上|うえ}ない).

- ❌ この{f|上|うえ}なく{f|喜|よろこ}びです。 → ✅ この{f|上|うえ}ない{f|喜|よろこ}びです。
  {f|喜|よろこ}び is a noun, so it needs the adjectival <code>ない</code>.
  <code>なく</code> is the adverb and requires an adjective or a verb after it.

- ❌ このラーメン、この{f|上|うえ}なくおいしい! → ✅ このラーメン、めちゃくちゃおいしい!
  A ceremonial phrase in a casual sentence with an exclamation mark is comic. Match
  the register: casual food talk takes casual intensifiers.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to read this, and what does it tell you about the writer's stance? {f|同|おな}じ{f|書類|しょるい}を{f|三度|さんど}も{f|出|だ}させるとは、{f|不合理|ふごうり}な<b>ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In writing - an opinion column, a letter of complaint, an essay, a blog post
written in a slightly formal voice. The stance is exasperated but controlled: the
writer is passing judgement from a distance rather than venting, and the raised
register is part of the effect, giving the complaint a dry edge.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: {f|皆様|みなさま}にお{f|会|あ}いできて、この{f|上|うえ}なく{f|嬉|うれ}しく{f|存|ぞん}じます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|皆様|みなさま}にお{f|会|あ}いできて、{f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|嬉|うれ}しいです。

<code>{f|本当|ほんとう}に</code> or <code>{f|最高|さいこう}に</code> carries the
intensity, and plain です replaces the humble {f|存|ぞん}じます. The result is what
a person actually says on being introduced to a group.

</details>

**3.** Choose the natural form: {f|新入生|しんにゅうせい}の{f|皆|みな}さんを{f|迎|むか}えられることは、（この{f|上|うえ}ない・この{f|上|うえ}なく）{f|喜|よろこ}びです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>この{f|上|うえ}ない</b>{f|喜|よろこ}びです。

{f|喜|よろこ}び is a noun, so it needs the adjectival <code>ない</code> in front of
it. <code>この{f|上|うえ}なく</code> is the adverb and would require an adjective
or a verb - この{f|上|うえ}なく{f|嬉|うれ}しいです would also be correct.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|残業|ざんぎょう}するのは{f|大変|たいへん}ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|残業|ざんぎょう}するのは{f|大変|たいへん}<b>な</b>ことこの{f|上|うえ}ない。
"Working late every day is gruelling in the extreme."

{f|大変|たいへん} is a な-adjective, so it takes な before こと. The sentence is
otherwise well formed - and note that it is written Japanese; a colleague would say
{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|残業|ざんぎょう}は{f|本当|ほんとう}にきつい.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in tone:
(a) {f|危険|きけん}なことこの{f|上|うえ}ない{f|工事|こうじ}{f|現場|げんば}だった。
(b) {f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|危|あぶ}ない{f|工事|こうじ}{f|現場|げんば}だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is written and evaluative. It sounds like an inspection report or a piece of
journalism: the writer is registering a formal judgement about the site, and the
elevated phrasing implies the danger was not merely felt but assessed.

(b) is what a person says. It is immediate, personal and unremarkable in register,
and it would be the natural line from a worker describing the site to a friend. Use
(b) when speaking; expect (a) when reading.

</details>
