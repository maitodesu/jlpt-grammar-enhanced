# ～らしい

**Meaning** · apparently, I gather - and, after a noun, "a proper ～, just as a ～ should be"

`～らしい` does two jobs. As an **evidential** it says the information came from
outside the speaker - heard, read, picked up somewhere - and quietly declines to
vouch for it. As a **suffix on a noun** it says the thing fits its category
admirably: {f|春|はる}らしい{f|天気|てんき} is weather that is exactly what spring
ought to be.

## Formation

`らしい` attaches to plain forms, and nouns and な-adjectives attach **bare** - no
`だ`, no `な`, no `の`. Once attached, it conjugates like an い-adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + らしい | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|く}るらしい |
| Verb (past) | た-form + らしい | {f|来|き}た → {f|来|き}たらしい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + らしい | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ないらしい |
| い-adjective | plain form + らしい | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いらしい |
| な-adjective | drop だ + らしい | {f|大変|たいへん}だ → {f|大変|たいへん}らしい |
| Noun (evidential) | Noun + らしい | {f|病気|びょうき} → {f|病気|びょうき}らしい |
| Noun (typical of) | Noun + らしい + Noun | {f|春|はる} → {f|春|はる}らしい{f|天気|てんき} |
| Polite | らしい + です | {f|来|く}るらしいです |
| Past | らしかった | {f|来|き}たらしかった |
| Adverbial (suffix sense only) | らしく | {f|自分|じぶん}らしく{f|生|い}きる |
| Negative (suffix sense only) | らしくない | {f|君|きみ}らしくない |
| Noun form (suffix sense only) | らしさ | {f|彼|かれ}らしさ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two senses split on the negative. The **suffix** sense has
`らしくない` ("not very ～, unlike them"). The **evidential** sense does not: to say
"apparently he isn't coming", negate the verb, not `らしい` - 
<code>{f|来|こ}ないらしい</code>, never ❌<code>{f|来|く}るらしくない</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|試験|しけん}は{f|来週|らいしゅう}<b>らしい</b>よ。
The exam's apparently next week.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|道|みち}は{f|夜|よる}あぶない<b>らしい</b>です。
I gather this road is dangerous at night.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今日|きょう}は{f|春|はる}<b>らしい</b>{f|天気|てんき}ですね。
Proper spring weather today, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
となりの{f|家|いえ}は{f|犬|いぬ}を{f|飼|か}い{f|始|はじ}めた<b>らしい</b>。
The people next door have apparently got themselves a dog.
</pre>

<pre>
そんなことを{f|言|い}うなんて、{f|君|きみ}<b>らしくない</b>ね。
Saying a thing like that isn't like you at all.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|学生|がくせい}<b>らしく</b>、まじめに{f|勉強|べんきょう}しなさい。
Study properly, the way a student should.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|来年|らいねん}{f|大阪|おおさか}に{f|行|い}く<b>らしい</b>ですが、まだはっきりしません。
Word is the department head is moving to Osaka next year, but nothing's settled yet.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|人|ひと}の{f|話|はなし}では、この{f|辺|へん}に{f|新|あたら}しいスーパーができる<b>らしい</b>です。
According to the neighbours, a new supermarket is going up around here.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`らしい` is at home in ordinary conversation, plain or polite. Its defining flavour
is **distance**: you are relaying something without putting your name to it. That
distance is useful when you genuinely do not know, and damaging when you are
expected to. A doctor saying <code>{f|熱|ねつ}があるらしいです</code> about his own
patient sounds as though he has not checked; a shop assistant saying
<code>{f|明日|あした}{f|届|とど}くらしいです</code> about your order sounds as though
nobody is in charge. In those situations use `そうです` with a named source, or state
the fact plainly.

For the same reason, `らしい` is not the form for formal writing. A report uses
`とのことだ` or hearsay `そうだ`.

The **suffix** sense is a compliment machine. {f|男|おとこ}らしい,
{f|女|おんな}らしい, {f|子|こ}どもらしい, {f|自分|じぶん}らしい,
{f|先生|せんせい}らしい - each says the person or thing lives up to what the noun
implies. Be aware that the gendered pair carries traditional expectations and, in
present-day Japanese, has a somewhat conservative ring for many speakers; it is
still used, but it is not neutral in the way <code>{f|自分|じぶん}らしい</code> is.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>{f|自分|じぶん}らしく{f|生|い}きる</code>, "live as yourself", and the
noun <code>{f|自分|じぶん}らしさ</code>, "one's own character", are set phrases you
will meet constantly in advertising and self-help writing. The `らしさ` form belongs
to the suffix sense only.

</div>

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words.

</div>

### ～らしい vs hearsay ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both pass information on, but `そうだ` forwards it **intact and
sourced**, while `らしい` reports what is **in the air**. If you can say who told
you, `そうだ` is the honest choice.

</div>

<pre>
{f|先生|せんせい}の{f|話|はなし}によると、{f|来月|らいげつ}テストがある<b>そうです</b>。
According to the teacher, there's a test next month.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来月|らいげつ}テストがある<b>らしい</b>よ。
There's a test next month, apparently.
</pre>

There is a formal difference too, and it is decisive in an exam. `らしい` conjugates
and can modify a noun - <code>{f|中止|ちゅうし}になったらしい{f|試合|しあい}</code> - 
while hearsay `そうだ` is frozen at the end of a sentence and can do neither.

### ～らしい vs ～ようだ／～みたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `らしい` points **outward** at a source. `ようだ` and `みたい`
point **inward** at the speaker's own reasoning. Ask "what makes you say that?": if
the answer is "someone said so", it is `らしい`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|林|はやし}さんは{f|来年|らいねん}{f|結婚|けっこん}する<b>らしい</b>。
Hayashi is apparently getting married next year. (that's the word going round)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|林|はやし}さんは{f|来年|らいねん}{f|結婚|けっこん}する<b>ようだ</b>。
It looks as though Hayashi is getting married next year. (the ring, the house-hunting)
</pre>

The two shade into each other in practice, and a speaker choosing between them is
often choosing how much responsibility to take. `ようだ` puts the speaker's judgement
behind the claim; `らしい` sets it down on the table and steps back.

### Noun + らしい vs Noun + っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `らしい` means "admirably like a ～". `っぽい` means "～-ish", and
usually with a shrug or a complaint attached. They are not interchangeable, and
learners reach for the wrong one constantly.

</div>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}ども<b>らしい</b>まっすぐな{f|質問|しつもん}だった。
It was a straightforward question, in the good way children ask them.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}ども<b>っぽい</b>{f|話|はな}し{f|方|かた}はやめたほうがいい。
You should stop talking in that childish way.
</pre>

`っぽい` is casual and productive - {f|安|やす}っぽい, {f|水|みず}っぽい, {f|忘|わす}れっぽい - 
and it very often carries mild disapproval. `らしい` in the same slot is praise.
There is a third option, <code>Noun + のような</code>, which is neither: it merely
notes a resemblance without saying whether the thing is really in the category.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|医者|いしゃ}**だらしい** → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|医者|いしゃ}**らしい**
  Nouns attach bare. The `だ` is required before hearsay `そうだ` and forbidden here
 - a difference worth drilling, because the two patterns sit next to each other.

- ❌ {f|元気|げんき}**ならしい** → ✅ {f|元気|げんき}**らしい**
  な-adjectives also drop everything. `な` belongs in front of `よう`.

- ❌ {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|来|く}る**らしくない**。(meaning "apparently isn't coming") → ✅ {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|来|こ}ない**らしい**。
  The evidential sense has no negative. Negate the clause instead. `らしくない` only
  exists in the "not like a proper ～" sense.

- ❌ {f|子|こ}ども**っぽい**{f|元気|げんき}な{f|声|こえ}がいいですね。(intended as praise) → ✅ {f|子|こ}ども**らしい**{f|元気|げんき}な{f|声|こえ}がいいですね。
  `っぽい` would turn the compliment into a complaint about immaturity.

## Practice

**1.** ニュースで{f|見|み}たんだけど、あの{f|橋|はし}は{f|来年|らいねん}まで{f|使|つか}えない____よ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの{f|橋|はし}は{f|来年|らいねん}まで{f|使|つか}えない<b>らしい</b>よ。

The information came from outside the speaker, which is what `らしい` marks, and it
attaches straight to the plain negative <code>{f|使|つか}えない</code>. Note that the
negation lives on the verb, never on `らしい` itself.

</details>

**2.** {f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}がきれいに{f|片|かた}づいている。{f|彼|かれ}は{f|帰|かえ}った（らしい・ようだ）。Which fits better if nobody told you?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}は{f|帰|かえ}った<b>ようだ</b>。

You are reasoning from a tidy desk that you can see with your own eyes, so the
judgement is yours. `らしい` would imply somebody had mentioned it, which the
situation as described rules out.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "That was a very Japanese answer."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

とても{f|日本人|にほんじん}<b>らしい</b>{f|答|こた}えでしたね。

This is the suffix sense: the answer fits the category admirably. `らしい` attaches
to the bare noun and then modifies <code>{f|答|こた}え</code> like an い-adjective.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}だらしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}**らしい**です。

Nouns take `らしい` bare. The `だ` you are remembering belongs to hearsay `そうだ`:
<code>{f|中止|ちゅうし}だそうです</code> is the correct sentence with that pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼女|かのじょ}は<b>{f|先生|せんせい}らしい</b>。
(b) {f|彼女|かのじょ}は<b>{f|先生|せんせい}のようだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is real: it can mean "apparently she's a
teacher" (evidential, someone told you) or "she is very much the teacher" (suffix,
she has the manner). Context and intonation decide. (b) has only one reading - she
resembles a teacher, and the implication is that she probably is not one. That is
the general rule of thumb: `らしい` puts the person inside the category, `のようだ`
keeps them outside it.

</details>
