# ～てくる

**Meaning** · do it and come back - and, on the time axis, a change that has
reached now

`～てくる` is the mirror image of `～ていく`: **movement towards where the speaker
is**. Three everyday uses fall out of that one idea. Someone does the て-verb
while approaching; or they go off, do it, and return; or - because "towards here"
on the time axis means "up to now" - a change has been building and has just
arrived at the present.

## Formation

`くる` is the irregular motion verb, so watch the readings: {f|来|き}て,
{f|来|こ}ない, {f|来|こ}よう.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (て) | て-form + くる | {f|買|か}う → {f|買|か}ってくる |
| Verb (で) | で-form + くる | {f|呼|よ}ぶ → {f|呼|よ}んでくる |
| Verb (する) | してくる | {f|電話|でんわ}する → {f|電話|でんわ}してくる |
| Polite | てきます | {f|買|か}ってきます |
| Past | てきた／てきました | {f|買|か}ってきました |
| Negative | てこない／てきません | {f|買|か}ってこない |
| Volitional | てこよう | {f|買|か}ってこよう |
| Request | てきて（ください） | {f|買|か}ってきてください |
| Change up to now | い-adj + く + なってくる | {f|暑|あつ}くなってくる |
| Change up to now | Noun／な-adj + に + なってくる | {f|好|す}きになってくる |
| Humble | てまいります | {f|行|い}ってまいります |

<div class="note">

**Note** - as an auxiliary this is normally written in **kana**, which also spares
you the reading trap: {f|買|か}ってくる has no `く` in it to misread, whereas
{f|買|か}って{f|来|こ}ない invites the wrong reading of the kanji.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ちょっとコンビニに<b>{f|行|い}ってきます</b>。
I'm just popping to the convenience store.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}ってきた</b>。
It's started raining.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|牛乳|ぎゅうにゅう}を<b>{f|買|か}ってきて</b>くれない？
Could you pick up some milk while you're out?
</pre>

<pre>

The wind has picked up now that it's evening.
</pre>

<pre>

After three years of study I'm finally starting to be able to read the newspaper.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

As we talked, it gradually became clear what he was getting at.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～てくる` is register-neutral, and `てきます` is its ordinary polite form. The
humble equivalent <code>てまいります</code> belongs to business and announcements - 
<code>{f|行|い}ってまいります</code>, <code>{f|確認|かくにん}してまいります</code> - 
and sounds noticeably formal.

**The round trip is why 「{f|行|い}ってきます」 is the phrase you say leaving the
house.** Literally "I'll go and come back", it promises a return, which is exactly
what the household reply 「{f|行|い}ってらっしゃい」 answers. Any errand can take the
same shape: <code>{f|見|み}てきます</code> ("I'll go and have a look"),
<code>{f|聞|き}いてきます</code> ("I'll go and ask").

**The change reading normally surfaces in the past.** <code>てきた</code> is far
commoner than <code>てくる</code> here, because you notice a change only once it has
arrived: {f|眠|ねむ}くなってきた, {f|見|み}えてきた, {f|慣|な}れてきた. The state it
announces is present even though the verb is past - this catches learners out
regularly.

**With verbs of feeling and perception it means a feeling emerging.**
<code>{f|思|おも}えてくる</code>, <code>{f|気|き}がしてくる</code>,
<code>{f|腹|はら}が{f|立|た}ってくる</code> - the sensation was not there, and now
it is welling up. This is one of the most natural-sounding uses and one learners
almost never reach for.

**There is no tidy contraction** to match `ていく` → `てく`, because `てくる` is
already short. What you will hear instead is `てくる` stacked with other
auxiliaries in casual speech - <code>{f|買|か}ってきちゃった</code>,
<code>{f|買|か}ってきといて</code> - and the request form clipped to
<code>{f|買|か}ってきて</code> with the `ください` simply dropped.

## Contrast

### The three jobs of ～てくる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て-verb tells you which reading you have: a verb of
**motion** gives approach, a verb of **doing** gives a round trip, a verb of
**change or perception** gives the time axis.

</div>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもが{f|部屋|へや}に<b>{f|入|はい}ってきた</b>。
A child came into the room.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|切手|きって}を<b>{f|買|か}ってきます</b>。
I'll go and buy some stamps. (and come back)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|最近|さいきん}、{f|日|ひ}が{f|長|なが}く<b>なってきた</b>。
The days have been getting longer lately.
</pre>

Buying stamps involves no movement towards the speaker at all - the goods come
back with you, and the auxiliary marks the return leg of the errand. That is why
`てくる` on an action verb so often translates as "go and…", which looks backwards
until you notice that the sentence is spoken from the place you will return to.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the round-trip reading needs somewhere to come back to. Said on your
own doorstep, <code>{f|走|はし}ってきます</code> means "I'm off for a run (and
back)". Said watching someone approach, <code>{f|走|はし}ってきます</code> is plain
motion. Nothing in the grammar distinguishes them; the situation does.

</div>

### ～てくる vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` is the state **now**. `てくる` is the **arrival** at
that state - it was not so, and now it is.

</div>

<pre>
{f|雪|ゆき}が<b>{f|降|ふ}ってきた</b>。
It's started to snow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雪|ゆき}が<b>{f|降|ふ}っている</b>。
It's snowing.
</pre>

The second is what you say when somebody asks about the weather: you look out and
describe what is going on. The first is what you say the moment the first flakes
appear - the news is not the snow but the *change*, and you were there for it.

The same split runs through anything gradual:

<pre>
おなかがすいてきた。
I'm starting to get hungry.
</pre>

<pre>
おなかがすいている。
I'm hungry.
</pre>

Because `てくる` reports a change you have witnessed, it does not work for states
that have simply always been true. ❌{f|背|せ}が{f|高|たか}くなってきました said
about an adult's fixed height is odd; said about a growing child it is perfect.

### ～てくる vs ～ていく on the time axis

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - draw an arrow at **now**. `てきた` runs from the past into the
present; `ていく` runs from the present into the future.

</div>

<pre>
{f|去年|きょねん}から{f|会員|かいいん}が<b>{f|増|ふ}えてきました</b>。
Membership has been growing since last year.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}からも{f|会員|かいいん}は<b>{f|増|ふ}えていく</b>でしょう。
Membership will probably go on growing next year as well.
</pre>

The time expressions give it away every time. <code>{f|昔|むかし}から</code>,
<code>{f|最近|さいきん}</code>, <code>これまで</code> and
<code>だんだん…てきた</code> pull `てくる`; <code>これから</code>,
<code>{f|今後|こんご}</code> and a prediction ending pull `ていく`. A sentence with
both is a standard rhetorical move:

<pre>
これまで{f|続|つづ}けてきたことを、これからも{f|続|つづ}けていきたい。
I want to keep on doing what we have been doing up to now.
</pre>

Note that this contrast lives entirely on the time axis. In the *physical* uses,
`てくる` and `ていく` are about direction in space, and no amount of thinking about
tense will help you choose.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|買|か}うてくる → ✅ <b>{f|買|か}ってくる</b>
  `くる` attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

- ❌（leaving the house）{f|行|い}っていきます。 → ✅ <b>{f|行|い}ってきます</b>。
  You intend to return, and the whole point of the phrase is the return, so it has
  to be `てくる`. This is a fixed expression - learn it whole.

- ❌ {f|昔|むかし}から{f|日本人|にほんじん}は{f|魚|さかな}を{f|食|た}べていきました。 → ✅ {f|昔|むかし}から{f|日本人|にほんじん}は{f|魚|さかな}を<b>{f|食|た}べてきました</b>。
  <code>{f|昔|むかし}から</code> runs from the past up to the present, which is
  `てくる` territory. `ていく` would push the eating off into the future.

- ❌ さっき{f|昼|ひる}ごはんを{f|食|た}べていきました。（meaning "I went out and had lunch"）→ ✅ さっき{f|昼|ひる}ごはんを<b>{f|食|た}べてきました</b>。
  You left, ate, and came back to where you are speaking - a round trip, so
  `てくる`. `ていく` would mean you ate and then carried on somewhere else.

## Practice

**1.** {f|急|きゅう}に{f|空|そら}が{f|暗|くら}くなって、{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っ<b>てきた</b>。

The sky darkening and then the rain arriving is a change that has just reached the
present moment, which is exactly the `てくる` reading. Note the past tense:
<code>てきた</code> announces a state that is true right now.

</details>

**2.** {f|田中|たなか}さんに{f|資料|しりょう}を{f|渡|わた}したいので、{f|席|せき}まで（{f|持|も}っていきます・{f|持|も}ってきます）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|席|せき}まで<b>{f|持|も}っていきます</b>。

You are moving the documents away from where you are towards Tanaka's desk, so the
direction is outward and `ていく` is right. `てくる` would be what Tanaka says if
he asked you to fetch them to *his* seat.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll go and ask the teacher, so wait here."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|先生|せんせい}に<b>{f|聞|き}いてきます</b>ので、ここで{f|待|ま}っていてください。

The errand ends with you returning to the person you are speaking to, and that
return is what `てくる` marks. <code>{f|聞|き}きます</code> alone would leave the
listener unsure whether you were coming back at all.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|大学|だいがく}に{f|入|はい}ってから、だんだん{f|数学|すうがく}がおもしろくなっていきました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|大学|だいがく}に{f|入|はい}ってから、だんだん{f|数学|すうがく}がおもしろく<b>なってきました</b>。

The change runs from a point in the past up to the speaker's present, so the arrow
points towards now - `てくる`. `ていく` would only fit if the sentence were looking
ahead: これからもっとおもしろくなっていくでしょう.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|息子|むすこ}は{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|書|か}けるようになってきた。
(b) {f|息子|むすこ}は{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|書|か}けるようになった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) states the achievement as a completed fact: at some point he crossed the line
and now he can write kanji.

(a) presents the same development as a gradual approach to that line, watched from
the present. It implies the process took time, that the speaker has been observing
it, and often that it is not quite finished - he is getting there.

</details>
