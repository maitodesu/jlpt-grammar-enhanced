# ～がする

**Meaning** · a sensation reaches you - a smell, a sound, a taste, a feeling

`する` is not always "do". With a small set of sensation nouns it means something
closer to "there is" or "it comes to me": {f|音|おと}がする, "there's a sound";
においがする, "there's a smell". Nobody is performing anything. The sensation
arrives, and the noun naming it is the subject.

## Formation

Sensation noun + `が` + `する`. There is no conjugating of the noun; only `する`
moves.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sound | {f|音|おと}・{f|声|こえ} + がする | {f|変|へん}な{f|音|おと}がする |
| Smell | におい・{f|香|かお}り + がする | いいにおいがする |
| Taste | {f|味|あじ} + がする | {f|甘|あま}い{f|味|あじ}がする |
| Feeling, hunch | {f|気|き}・{f|感|かん}じ + がする | {f|気|き}がする |
| Bodily sensation | {f|寒気|さむけ}・{f|頭痛|ずつう}・めまい + がする | めまいがする |
| Sense of a presence | {f|気配|けはい} + がする | {f|人|ひと}の{f|気配|けはい}がする |

`する` is irregular and carries the whole conjugation:

| Verb form | Example |
|---|---|
| Polite | {f|音|おと}がします |
| Negative | {f|音|おと}がしない |
| Past | {f|音|おと}がした |
| て-form | {f|音|おと}がして |
| Ongoing | {f|音|おと}がしている |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the sensation is the **subject**, so the particle is `が`.
Never ❌においをする. Nothing is being done to the smell; the smell is what is
there.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the list of nouns that work this way is short and fixed. Sight is not
on it: for something visible you use {f|見|み}える, never ❌{f|山|やま}がする.
Pain is not on it either - it is {f|頭|あたま}が{f|痛|いた}いです or
{f|痛|いた}みがあります, though the noun {f|頭痛|ずつう} does take がする.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
だれかの<b>{f|声|こえ}がする</b>。
I can hear someone's voice.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|台所|だいどころ}からいい<b>においがします</b>。
There's a lovely smell coming from the kitchen.
</pre>

<pre>
このスープは{f|少|すこ}し{f|甘|あま}い<b>{f|味|あじ}がします</b>。
This soup tastes slightly sweet.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|外|そと}で{f|変|へん}な<b>{f|音|おと}がした</b>ので、{f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けてみました。
There was an odd noise outside, so I opened the window to look.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|朝|あさ}から<b>{f|寒気|さむけ}がする</b>ので、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|休|やす}みます。
I've had chills since this morning, so I'm taking the day off work.
</pre>

<pre>
どこかでお{f|会|あ}いしたような<b>{f|気|き}がします</b>。
I have a feeling we've met somewhere before.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|古|ふる}い{f|本|ほん}の<b>においがして</b>、なんだか{f|落|お}ち{f|着|つ}きます。
This room smells of old books, and something about that is calming.
</pre>

<pre>
さっきから{f|上|うえ}の{f|階|かい}で{f|人|ひと}が{f|歩|ある}いている<b>{f|音|おと}がして</b>、なかなか{f|眠|ねむ}れません。
I've been hearing someone walking about upstairs for a while now, and I can't get to sleep.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The defining feature of `がする` is that the sensation is **unbidden and often
unidentified**. {f|音|おと}がする is what you say when something made a noise and
you do not yet know what: you noticed, you did not investigate. That is why it
pairs so naturally with {f|変|へん}な, {f|大|おお}きな and だれかの - words that
admit you have not pinned the source down.

The noun can be modified freely, and this is where most of the expressive work
happens: レモンの{f|味|あじ}がする, {f|焦|こ}げたにおいがする,
{f|子|こ}どもの{f|声|こえ}がする. A `の` phrase in front means "the smell/taste/sound
*of* that thing", which is how you say something tastes *like* something else.

Tense matters more than usual here. `がした` reports one moment - a bang, a whiff.
`がする` in the plain present covers a sensation happening now or as a general
property （このお{f|菓子|かし}はバターのにおいがする）. `がしている` is available for
something clearly ongoing, but Japanese reaches for the plain present far more
readily than English does with "is smelling".

**`気がする` deserves separate attention.** It is one of the most used expressions
in the spoken language: どこかで{f|聞|き}いたことがあるような{f|気|き}がする,
「{f|忘|わす}れ{f|物|もの}をしたような{f|気|き}がする」. It reports a hunch you cannot
justify, and because it commits you to nothing it is very useful for softening - 
{f|少|すこ}し{f|高|たか}いような{f|気|き}がします is a gentler way to say something
is overpriced.

The pattern is register-neutral: identical in casual and polite speech, and
completely standard in writing. The medical uses - 
{f|寒気|さむけ}がする, {f|吐|は}き{f|気|け}がする, めまいがする - are the phrases
you actually need at a clinic, and they are worth memorising as units.

## Contrast

### ～がする vs {f|聞|き}こえる・{f|見|み}える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|聞|き}こえる</code> says **you heard** a particular
thing. `がする` says **there was** a sound. And there is no visual `がする`: for
sight, only <code>{f|見|み}える</code>.

</div>

<pre>
{f|夜中|よなか}に{f|猫|ねこ}の<b>{f|声|こえ}がして</b>、{f|目|め}が{f|覚|さ}めました。
A cat's cry in the night woke me up.
</pre>

Compare {f|猫|ねこ}の{f|声|こえ}が{f|聞|き}こえました, "I could hear a cat." Both are
correct, and the difference is where the sentence puts you. `聞こえる` is about
your ears reaching the sound and implies you made it out; `がする` is about the
sound being there at all, and sits comfortably with not knowing what it was. This
is also why {f|気配|けはい}がする works while ❌{f|気配|けはい}が{f|聞|き}こえる does
not - a presence is not a thing you hear.

### {f|気|き}がする vs と{f|思|おも}う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|気|き}がする</code> is a hunch with no grounds behind
it. <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> is an opinion you would defend.

</div>

<pre>
{f|今日|きょう}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}りそうな<b>{f|気|き}がします</b>。
I've got a feeling it's going to rain today.
</pre>

Say {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ると{f|思|おも}います instead and you sound as though you
checked the forecast. `気がする` explicitly declines to give a reason, which makes
it the safer form when you are half-remembering something or disagreeing gently
with someone senior.

### ～がする vs ～をしている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がする` is a sensation coming **to** you. `をしている`
describes a property a thing **has** - its colour, its shape, its expression.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|石|いし}は{f|変|へん}わった{f|形|かたち}をしています。
This stone has an unusual shape.
</pre>

Same verb, opposite direction, opposite particle. {f|色|いろ}をしている,
{f|形|かたち}をしている, {f|顔|かお}をしている are the three you will actually meet:
{f|青|あお}い{f|目|め}をした{f|猫|ねこ}, {f|悲|かな}しそうな{f|顔|かお}をしていた.
Sensations never take `を`, and appearances never take `が` in this construction.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ いいにおい<b>を</b>します。 → ✅ いいにおい<b>が</b>します。
  The smell is the subject of the sentence, not an object someone acts on.

- ❌ この{f|花|はな}はいいにおいが<b>あります</b>。 → ✅ この{f|花|はな}はいいにおいが<b>します</b>。
  English "has a nice smell" pushes learners towards ある. Japanese treats a
  perceived smell as something that *does* - it `する`.

- ❌ {f|昨日|きのう}から{f|頭痛|ずつう}を<b>しています</b>。 → ✅ {f|昨日|きのう}から{f|頭痛|ずつう}が<b>します</b>。
  Same particle error in a medical setting, where being understood matters most.

- ❌ あそこに{f|大|おお}きい{f|建物|たてもの}が<b>します</b>。 → ✅ あそこに{f|大|おお}きい{f|建物|たてもの}が<b>{f|見|み}えます</b>。
  `がする` covers hearing, smell, taste and bodily feeling - never sight.

## Practice

**1.** {f|台所|だいどころ}からカレーのにおい____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

カレーのにおい<b>がします</b>

Smell is one of the sensations that take this pattern, and the `の` phrase in
front means the smell *of* curry. Polite present is します; in casual speech you
would simply say がする.

</details>

**2.** となりの{f|部屋|へや}から{f|音|おと}が（します・あります）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

となりの{f|部屋|へや}から{f|音|おと}が<b>します</b>。

A sound that reaches you is reported with する, not ある. Using ある would be
treating the noise as an object sitting in the next room rather than something
you are perceiving right now.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This tea tastes a little bitter."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このお{f|茶|ちゃ}は{f|少|すこ}し{f|苦|にが}い<b>{f|味|あじ}がします</b>。

The adjective modifies {f|味|あじ}, and the whole sensation is the subject of
します. You could also say このお{f|茶|ちゃ}は{f|少|すこ}し{f|苦|にが}いです; the
{f|味|あじ}がする version foregrounds the taste as something you are registering.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|朝|あさ}からめまいをしていて、{f|立|た}てません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|朝|あさ}からめまい<b>がして</b>いて、{f|立|た}てません。

めまい belongs to the bodily-sensation group, and every member of that group takes
`が`. The `を` version would suggest you are producing the dizziness on purpose.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|下|した}の{f|階|かい}で{f|音|おと}がします。
(b) {f|下|した}の{f|階|かい}の{f|音|おと}が{f|聞|き}こえます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports that a noise is occurring downstairs. It leaves the source open - you
noticed something, you could not say what, and the sentence is what you would say
while looking up from your book.

(b) puts your ears at the centre: the sound from downstairs reaches you and you
can make it out. It suits describing how thin the walls are, or answering someone
who asked whether you can hear anything. Where (a) is about the event, (b) is
about your perception of it.

</details>
