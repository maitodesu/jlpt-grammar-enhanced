# ～のように・～のような

**Meaning** · like; similar to; such as - comparing something to a **noun**

When the thing you are comparing to is a noun rather than a clause, `よう` needs
`の` to hold on to it. `Noun + のように` goes in front of a verb or adjective;
`Noun + のような` goes in front of another noun. The same pair has a second job that
looks unrelated but is not: introducing an **example** - "fruit such as apples".

## Formation

The `の` is not optional and it is not the possessive `の` you learned at N5 - it is
the connector that lets a noun modify another noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, before a verb or adjective | Noun + のように | {f|雪|ゆき} → {f|雪|ゆき}のように{f|白|しろ}い |
| Noun, before a noun | Noun + のような + Noun | {f|夢|ゆめ} → {f|夢|ゆめ}のような{f|話|はなし} |
| Noun, at the end of a sentence | Noun + のようだ／のようです | {f|夢|ゆめ}のようです |
| Giving an example | Noun や Noun + のような + Noun | りんごやみかんのような{f|果物|くだもの} |
| この・その・あの | drop the の | そのような／そのように |
| Casual equivalent | Noun + みたいな／みたいに | {f|夢|ゆめ}みたいな{f|話|はなし} |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` before `よう`, nothing before `みたい`. And with
`この・その・あの` the `の` is already inside the word, so you write
<code>そのような</code>, never ❌<code>そののような</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|雪|ゆき}<b>のように</b>{f|白|しろ}い。
As white as snow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|人形|にんぎょう}<b>のような</b>{f|顔|かお}ですね。
She has a face like a doll's, doesn't she.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|鳥|とり}<b>のように</b>{f|空|そら}を{f|飛|と}んでみたい。
I'd love to fly through the sky like a bird.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}に{f|仕事|しごと}が{f|山|やま}<b>のように</b>ある。
There's a mountain of work on my desk.
</pre>

<pre>
みかんやバナナ<b>のような</b>{f|果物|くだもの}が{f|好|す}きです。
I like fruit such as mandarins and bananas.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|毎日|まいにち}<b>のように</b>この{f|店|みせ}に{f|通|かよ}っている。
He turns up at this shop practically every day.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昨日|きのう}の{f|痛|いた}みが、うそ<b>のように</b>なくなった。
Yesterday's pain vanished as if it had never been there.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|初|はじ}めて{f|会|あ}ったのに、{f|昔|むかし}からの{f|友|とも}だち<b>のような</b>{f|気|き}がした。
Even though we'd only just met, I felt as if we'd been friends for years.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`のように` and `のような` are neutral: fine in an essay, fine in conversation, not
marked for age or gender. In relaxed speech `みたいに` and `みたいな` are commoner,
and they are what you will hear far more often between friends. Going the other
direction, formal writing has its own set-piece openers:
ご{f|覧|らん}のように - "as you can see", {f|以下|いか}のように - "as below".

Two families of fixed expressions are worth memorising rather than analysing:

- **Exaggerated quantity or manner** - {f|山|やま}のように (heaps of),
  {f|飛|と}ぶように{f|売|う}れる (selling like hot cakes), うそのように (unbelievably).
- **"Practically every…"** - {f|毎日|まいにち}のように,
  {f|毎週|まいしゅう}のように. These do not mean literally every day; they mean often
  enough that it feels like it.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>のように</code> compares; <code>と{f|同|おな}じ</code>, which you met at
N5, equates. {f|兄|あに}のように{f|背|せ}が{f|高|たか}い says you are tall in the way
your brother is tall; {f|兄|あに}と{f|同|おな}じくらい{f|背|せ}が{f|高|たか}い says the
two heights match. If a measurement is involved, you probably want
<code>{f|同|おな}じ</code>.

</div>

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words. `のように` and
`のような` are the noun-comparing corner of the `ようだ` family.

</div>

### ～のように／のような vs ～みたいに／みたいな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning; `よう` needs `の` and `みたい` does not. `よう` is
neutral to formal, `みたい` is casual.

</div>

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}<b>のような</b>{f|技術者|ぎじゅつしゃ}になりたいと{f|思|おも}っています。
I hope to become an engineer like my father.
</pre>

<pre>
おれもお{f|父|とう}さん<b>みたいな</b>エンジニアになりたいな。
I wanna be an engineer like my dad too.
</pre>

Both sentences say the same thing about the same ambition. The first would go in a
university application; the second is a fifteen-year-old talking. Notice that the
`の` disappears the moment you switch to `みたい` - that is the mechanical half of
the difference, and it is where learners slip.

### Noun + のような vs Noun + らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `Xのような` means *resembling X, and probably not X*. `Xらしい`
means *a proper X, behaving as an X should*. They are close to opposites.

</div>

<pre>
{f|男|おとこ}<b>のような</b>{f|人|ひと}が{f|立|た}っていた。
Someone who looked like a man was standing there. (I couldn't tell)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|男|おとこ}<b>らしい</b>{f|人|ひと}だと{f|言|い}われている。
People say he's a very manly sort.
</pre>

The first says the resemblance is all you have; the second says the category fits
so well it is worth remarking on. Use `のような` about someone you are unsure of and
you are being cautious; use it about someone whose gender is obvious and you have
said something strange.

### ～のような "resembling" vs ～のような "such as"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if what precedes `のような` is a **member** of the noun that
follows, it is "such as". If it is outside that category, it is a comparison.

</div>

<pre>
バラ<b>のような</b>{f|花|はな}を{f|買|か}ってきてください。
Please buy some flowers - roses, say. (roses are flowers → "such as")
</pre>

<pre>
{f|火|ひ}<b>のような</b>{f|花|はな}が{f|咲|さ}いていた。
Flowers like flames were in bloom. (fire is not a flower → comparison)
</pre>

Japanese does not mark the difference; the listener works it out from whether the
category contains the example. English does mark it, which is why translating in
either direction here needs a moment's thought.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|雪|ゆき}**ように**{f|白|しろ}い → ✅ {f|雪|ゆき}**のように**{f|白|しろ}い
  A bare noun cannot attach to `よう` directly. Only a clause can.

- ❌ **そののような**{f|話|はなし} → ✅ **そのような**{f|話|はなし}
  `その` already ends in `の`. Adding another is the commonest slip in this whole
  pattern.

- ❌ {f|子|こ}どもの**ように**{f|字|じ} → ✅ {f|子|こ}どもの**ような**{f|字|じ}
  A noun follows, so the form must be `のような`. `のように` only goes in front of
  verbs and adjectives.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|男|おとこ}の**ような**{f|人|ひと}だ。(meaning "he's manly") → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|男|おとこ}**らしい**{f|人|ひと}だ。
  `のような` distances the noun from the category. To praise someone for fitting the
  category, you need `らしい`.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|冬|ふゆ}____さむいですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|冬|ふゆ}<b>のように</b>さむいですね。

<code>{f|冬|ふゆ}</code> is a noun, so it needs `の` before `よう`, and the word that
follows is an adjective, so the adverbial `ように` is required rather than `ような`.

</details>

**2.** {f|彼女|かのじょ}はモデル（のような・みたいな）{f|人|ひと}です。Which fits a job interview?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼女|かのじょ}はモデル<b>のような</b>{f|人|ひと}です。

Both are grammatical and mean the same thing, but `みたいな` is casual and would
sound too loose in an interview. Note also that `みたい` takes no `の` - writing
❌<code>モデルのみたいな</code> mixes the two patterns.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I want to live in a quiet town like Kyoto."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|京都|きょうと}<b>のような</b>{f|静|しず}かな{f|町|まち}に{f|住|す}みたいです。

Here Kyoto genuinely is a quiet town, so this is the "such as" reading: Kyoto is
offered as an example of the category. The pattern goes in front of the whole noun
phrase, adjective included.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あのような{f|人|ひと}のように{f|服|ふく}を{f|着|き}たい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの{f|人|ひと}<b>のような</b>{f|服|ふく}を{f|着|き}たい。

The original stacks the pattern twice. Decide what you are comparing: if it is the
clothes, the noun after the pattern is <code>{f|服|ふく}</code>, so you want
<code>あの{f|人|ひと}のような{f|服|ふく}</code> with a single `のような`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|先生|せんせい}**のような**{f|人|ひと}になりたい。
(b) {f|先生|せんせい}**らしい**{f|人|ひと}になりたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) points at one particular teacher you admire and says you want to resemble that
person. (b) says you want to be the sort of person who is recognisably a teacher - 
to fit the role well. The first is about a model you have in mind; the second is
about living up to a category, and it would only make sense if you are a teacher
already or about to be one.

</details>
