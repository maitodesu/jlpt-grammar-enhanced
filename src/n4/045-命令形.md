# 命令形 - the plain imperative

**Meaning** · a bare order - "do it" - with nothing softening it

The {f|命令形|めいれいけい} is the shortest possible way to tell someone to act.
It has no politeness of any kind built in, which makes it rude between adults and
exactly right in three places: emergencies, signs, and quoting an order that
someone else gave.

## Formation

う-verbs shift their final kana one row; る-verbs take `ろ`; the irregulars must be
learnt.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → え-row kana | {f|書|か}く → {f|書|か}け |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + ろ (spoken) or + よ (written) | {f|食|た}べる → {f|食|た}べろ／{f|食|た}べよ |
| Verb (する) | しろ (spoken) or せよ (written) | {f|注意|ちゅうい}する → {f|注意|ちゅうい}しろ |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | {f|来|こ}い - nothing is added, the whole word changes | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}い |

Every う-verb ending:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → え | {f|買|か}う → {f|買|か}え |
| ～く | く → け | {f|聞|き}く → {f|聞|き}け |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → げ | {f|泳|およ}ぐ → {f|泳|およ}げ |
| ～す | す → せ | {f|出|だ}す → {f|出|だ}せ |
| ～つ | つ → て | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}て |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → ね | {f|死|し}ぬ → {f|死|し}ね |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → べ | {f|呼|よ}ぶ → {f|呼|よ}べ |
| ～む | む → め | {f|飲|の}む → {f|飲|の}め |
| ～る | る → れ | {f|走|はし}る → {f|走|はし}れ |

<div class="note">

**A word about the ぬ row** - {f|死|し}ぬ is used here because it is essentially the
only ぬ-verb in ordinary modern Japanese, so every conjugation table in this book
falls back on it. Be aware that its imperative, <b>{f|死|し}ね</b>, is a serious
insult - roughly "drop dead" - and is genuinely shocking to say. Learn the
conjugation; never use the word.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a handful of verbs break the pattern. `くれる` gives <b>くれ</b>, not
❌くれろ. The four honorific う-verbs take `～い` instead of `～え`:
いらっしゃる → いらっしゃい, なさる → なさい, くださる → ください,
おっしゃる → おっしゃい. Those endings are the reason
<code>ください</code> and <code>なさい</code> look the way they do.

</div>

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for う-verbs the imperative is the potential stem without the
`る`: {f|書|か}<b>け</b>る is "can write", {f|書|か}<b>け</b> is "write!". One syllable
apart, and worlds apart in tone.

</div>

The negative counterpart is dictionary form + `な`: {f|行|い}くな, "don't go".

## Nuance & register

Treat the {f|命令形|めいれいけい} as a form you must **recognise** rather than one
you use. Pointed at a real person it is harsh - it is what you hear in an
argument, from an angry parent, or from a drill instructor. Most Japanese adults go
weeks without saying one to another adult.

Where it does live:

- **Emergencies.** Politeness costs syllables, and syllables cost lives:
  {f|逃|に}げろ, {f|伏|ふ}せろ, {f|離|はな}れろ. Nobody hears these as rude.
- **Signs and instructions.** {f|止|と}まれ, {f|押|お}せ, {f|引|ひ}け. Impersonal
  and addressed to no one in particular. Exam papers use the `よ` variant:
  {f|答|こた}えよ, {f|選|えら}べ, {f|述|の}べよ.
- **Cheering.** がんばれ, {f|勝|か}て, {f|行|い}け - a stadium is exempt.
- **Sport, the military, fiction.** Coaches to players, seniors to juniors in a
  club, and a very large share of what characters shout in manga and anime.
- **Quoting.** Reporting an order almost always keeps the imperative inside the
  quote: 「{f|待|ま}て」と{f|言|い}われた.

**Register colouring.** The plain imperative is strongly male-associated in
conversation. Women use it in emergencies, in anger and in cheering, but a woman
telling a friend {f|早|はや}く{f|食|た}べろ would be marked. The gentler downward
orders - `～なさい` and the bare て-form - are unmarked for either speaker.

`ろ` versus `よ`: `ろ` is spoken, `よ` is written and formal. {f|見|み}ろ in
speech, {f|見|み}よ in a slogan or on a test. For する the split is
しろ／せよ, and せよ has a distinctly stiff, official ring.

Adding a particle does not rescue it. {f|早|はや}くしろ<b>よ</b> is nagging;
{f|来|こ}い<b>よ</b> is a blunt invitation between close male friends. If you want to
be gentle, change the form, not the ending.

## Contrast

### {f|命令形|めいれいけい} vs ～なさい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are orders given downward, but `～なさい` comes with
authority and care, while the {f|命令形|めいれいけい} comes with force. A parent
uses `～なさい`; the same parent uses the {f|命令形|めいれいけい} when they have
run out of patience.

</div>

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|寝|ね}なさい</b>。
Go to bed now. (parent, firm but calm)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|寝|ね}ろ</b>。
Get to bed. (sharp, angry, or male-casual)
</pre>

`～なさい` is also the only one of the two that appears in neutral written
instructions to a *person* - school worksheets, cookery books for children. The
{f|命令形|めいれいけい} in writing is reserved for signage and for the terse `よ`
form on exams.

### {f|命令形|めいれいけい} vs ～てください and the bare ～て

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` is a request and can go upward or sideways. The
bare て-form is a casual request between equals. The
{f|命令形|めいれいけい} is neither - it does not request anything.

</div>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|閉|し}めてください</b>。
Please close the window.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}、<b>{f|閉|し}めて</b>。
Shut the window, would you.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|閉|し}めろ</b>。
Shut that window.
</pre>

The bare て-form is the one to reach for in casual speech. It is friendly, it is
used by everyone, and it is what learners usually *mean* when they produce a
{f|命令形|めいれいけい} by accident.

### {f|命令形|めいれいけい} vs ～な - the negative order

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the positive order changes the verb's ending; the negative
order does not. Dictionary form + `な` is "don't", and it is just as blunt.

</div>

<pre>
そこに<b>{f|入|はい}れ</b>。
Get in there.
</pre>

<pre>
そこに<b>{f|入|はい}るな</b>。
Don't go in there.
</pre>

These two are a matched pair in tone - signs use both
({f|止|と}まれ, {f|入|はい}るな) and so do angry people. Beware the collision with
the *softened* command written the same way: ます-stem + `な` is a gentle order
contracted from `～なさい`. {f|食|た}べる<b>な</b> means "don't eat";
{f|食|た}べ<b>な</b> means "eat up". The difference is a single `る`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|来|く}ろ／{f|来|き}ろ → ✅ <b>{f|来|こ}い</b>
  {f|来|く}る has no `ろ` imperative at all; the whole word is replaced.

- ❌ するろ／しれ → ✅ <b>しろ</b>（written: <b>せよ</b>）
  する is irregular here too. Its imperative is not built from the dictionary
  form.

- ❌ くれろ → ✅ <b>くれ</b>
  `くれる` looks like an ordinary る-verb and is not. Compare
  {f|見|み}せる → {f|見|み}せろ, which is regular.

- ❌ {f|食|た}べれ → ✅ <b>{f|食|た}べろ</b>
  Applying the う-verb rule to a る-verb produces {f|食|た}べれ, which is not a
  word. る-verbs take `ろ`.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}、これを{f|読|よ}め。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}、これを<b>{f|読|よ}んでください</b>。
  The conjugation is fine; the sentence is not. Never aim a
  {f|命令形|めいれいけい} at anyone you owe respect to.

## Practice

**1.** {f|急|いそ}いでいるんだ、{f|早|はや}く{f|歩|ある}____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|早|はや}く{f|歩|ある}<b>け</b>。

<code>{f|歩|ある}く</code> is an う-verb, so the final `く` moves to the え-row and
nothing is added: {f|歩|ある}け. Note how blunt this is - between friends
{f|早|はや}く{f|歩|ある}いて would be normal.

</details>

**2.** {f|試験|しけん}{f|問題|もんだい}: {f|正|ただ}しい{f|答|こた}えを{f|一|ひと}つ（{f|選|えら}べ・{f|選|えら}んでください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|正|ただ}しい{f|答|こた}えを{f|一|ひと}つ<b>{f|選|えら}べ</b>。

Exam papers address no particular person, so the impersonal
{f|命令形|めいれいけい} is standard there. <code>～てください</code> would sound
oddly chatty on a test paper.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a shout: "Stop! Don't touch that!"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>やめろ</b>！それに<b>{f|触|さわ}るな</b>！

The positive order changes the verb ending (やめる → やめろ, a
る-verb) and the negative order adds `な` to the dictionary form
({f|触|さわ}る → {f|触|さわ}るな). Shouted in an emergency, neither is rude.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|部長|ぶちょう}、この{f|書類|しょるい}にサインしろ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、この{f|書類|しょるい}にサイン<b>していただけますか</b>。

`しろ` is the correct imperative of する, so the grammar is not the problem - the
target is. A {f|命令形|めいれいけい} aimed at a superior is about as bad as
Japanese gets. Upwards, a request has to be a request.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|静|しず}かにしろ。
(b) {f|静|しず}かにしなさい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is raw force. It is what someone says when they have lost their temper, or a
line in a film; from a stranger it would start a fight, and in conversation it is
strongly male-coded.

(b) still comes from above - you could not say it to your boss - but it carries the
tone of a person with legitimate authority correcting someone in their care. A
teacher says it to a noisy class without any anger at all. Both are downward; only
(a) is aggressive.

</details>
