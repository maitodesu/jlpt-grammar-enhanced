# と - conditional

**Meaning** · whenever A happens, B follows - automatically, every time

This is not the `と` you already know from N5, which joins nouns ("A and B") or
marks a companion ("with"). This one joins two **clauses** and says the second
follows from the first as a matter of course. Not a plan, not a choice: this is
how the world works.

## Formation

The clause in front of `と` is always **plain non-past**, no matter when the
sentence as a whole happens. There is no past-tense form here at all.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + と | {f|押|お}す → {f|押|お}すと |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + と | {f|押|お}さない → {f|押|お}さないと |
| い-adjective | plain form + と | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いと |
| い-adjective (negative) | くない + と | {f|安|やす}くない → {f|安|やす}くないと |
| な-adjective | stem + **だ** + と | {f|静|しず}か → {f|静|しず}かだと |
| Noun | Noun + **だ** + と | {f|冬|ふゆ} → {f|冬|ふゆ}だと |
| Polite (announcements) | ます-form + と | {f|押|お}します → {f|押|お}しますと |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nothing that expresses the **speaker's will** may stand in the
result clause. No ～ましょう, no ～てください, no ～たい, no ～たほうがいい, no
command, no invitation. `と` reports what *happens*, never what anyone decides
to do.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `だ` in front of `と` is not optional for nouns and な-adjectives.
{f|学生|がくせい}**だ**と is "if you're a student"; bare {f|学生|がくせい}と is
"with a student" - the N5 particle, and a completely different sentence.

</div>

<pre>
ここを<b>{f|押|お}すと</b>、{f|音|おと}が{f|出|で}ます。
Press here and it makes a sound.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|春|はる}に<b>なると</b>、この{f|山|やま}は{f|緑|みどり}になります。
When spring comes, this mountain turns green.
</pre>

<pre>
3に5を<b>{f|足|た}すと</b>、8になります。
Three plus five is eight.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|出|で}ないと</b>、{f|電車|でんしゃ}に{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いません。
If we don't leave soon, we won't make the train.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}は{f|酒|さけ}を<b>{f|飲|の}むと</b>、いつも{f|同|おな}じ{f|話|はなし}を{f|始|はじ}めます。

</pre>

<pre>

Turn right at this corner and there's a little flower shop on your left.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|部屋|へや}に<b>{f|入|はい}ると</b>、{f|誰|だれ}もいませんでした。
When I went into the room, there was nobody there.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}は{f|週末|しゅうまつ}に<b>なると</b>{f|急|きゅう}に{f|込|こ}んで、{f|一時間|いちじかん}ぐらい{f|待|ま}つことになります。
This place suddenly fills up at weekends, and you end up waiting about an hour.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|開|あ}けると</b>、{f|遠|とお}くで{f|祭|まつ}りの{f|太鼓|たいこ}の{f|音|おと}が{f|聞|き}こえた。
When I opened the window, I could hear festival drums somewhere far off.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`と` is register-neutral. It sounds the same in speech and in writing, and the
clause before it stays plain even in the politest sentence:
{f|押|お}すと{f|開|ひら}き**ます** - the politeness lives on the final verb only.
The one polite variant worth recognising is `ますと`, which turns up in
announcements, instruction manuals and careful business speech:
こちらのボタンを{f|押|お}し**ますと**、{f|扉|とびら}が{f|開|ひら}きます。

Where `と` really lives is anywhere a fixed procedure is being described: recipes,
machine instructions, road directions, arithmetic, seasons, and habits. If you can
put "every time" in front of the English and it still makes sense, `と` is
probably right.

Two things learners are blindsided by:

- **`もし` and `と` do not go together.** `もし` announces a hypothesis; `と`
  announces a regularity. もし{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ると… is odd. Use
  たら or ば after `もし`.
- **`～ないと` on its own means "I have to".** The consequence is simply left
  unsaid, and everybody fills it in: もう{f|行|い}かないと。 - "I've got to
  go." これ、{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう}に{f|出|だ}さないと。 - "I have to hand
  this in today." Extremely common in casual speech.

Finally, `と` with a **past-tense main clause** is not a conditional at all. It is
the narrative "upon doing X, …", used for something the speaker then discovered or
witnessed. Novels and travel diaries are full of it, and it is the one place a
`と` sentence describes a single occasion rather than a rule.

## Contrast

### と vs たら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` says *every time, automatically*. `たら` says *on the
occasion that this happens*. Only `たら` can be followed by a request, an
invitation, or a plan.

</div>

This is the difference that gets sentences rejected, because the ban on
will-expressing main clauses after `と` is absolute. Compare:

<pre>
{f|駅|えき}に<b>{f|着|つ}いたら</b>、{f|電話|でんわ}してください。
Call me when you get to the station.
</pre>

❌{f|駅|えき}に{f|着|つ}くと、{f|電話|でんわ}してください is wrong, and not
mildly so - it is asking the listener to do something as an automatic physical
consequence of arriving, which is not a thing you can ask.

<pre>
この{f|自動販売機|じどうはんばいき}は、お{f|金|かね}を<b>{f|入|い}れると</b>ランプがつきます。
On this vending machine, a light comes on when you put money in.
</pre>

That is a property of the machine - invariable, no speaker in it. Switch to
`たら` and it becomes a report of one particular time you fed it a coin.

### と vs ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` states a link the speaker treats as **actual and
invariable**. `ば` puts a condition up as a **premise** and reasons from it.

</div>

Both handle general rules, so they overlap widely, and in many sentences either
is fine. Where they part company is attitude. `と` has no speaker in it; `ば` is
the form you use when you are weighing something up.

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}が<b>{f|安|やす}いと</b>、すぐ{f|売|う}り{f|切|き}れます。
When something is cheap, it sells out straight away.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}が<b>{f|安|やす}ければ</b>、{f|買|か}います。
If it's cheap, I'll buy it.
</pre>

The first is an observation about how this shop behaves. The second is my
decision procedure - and a decision is exactly what `と` cannot host. Two more
consequences of this: `ば` is the form that takes `もし` comfortably, and `ば`
builds the regret pattern ～ばよかった, for which there is no ～とよかった.

### と vs なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なら` picks up a **premise someone has just supplied**,
usually the other person. `と` connects two events with nobody supplying
anything.

</div>

`なら` is a reply; `と` is a description. This shows up most clearly in
conversation, where `なら` can also put its main clause **earlier in time** than
its condition - something `と` can never do.

<pre>
「{f|北海道|ほっかいどう}へ{f|行|い}くんです。」「{f|北海道|ほっかいどう}<b>なら</b>、{f|六月|ろくがつ}がいいですよ。」
"I'm going to Hokkaido." "Hokkaido? June's the time to go."
</pre>

❌{f|北海道|ほっかいどう}へ{f|行|い}くと、{f|六月|ろくがつ}がいいですよ is
impossible: going to Hokkaido does not cause June to be good, and the speaker is
giving advice, not reporting a mechanism.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|駅|えき}に{f|着|つ}くと、{f|電話|でんわ}してください。 → ✅ {f|駅|えき}に{f|着|つ}い**たら**、{f|電話|でんわ}してください。
  The clause after `と` is an automatic result, so no request, command,
  invitation or wish can stand there. This is the single most common `と` error.

- ❌ {f|天気|てんき}がいいと、{f|散歩|さんぽ}しましょう。 → ✅ {f|天気|てんき}が**よかったら**、{f|散歩|さんぽ}しましょう。
  Same rule. `～ましょう` is a proposal - the speaker's will - and `と` has no
  room for it.

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ったと、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る**と**／{f|降|ふ}っ**たら**、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。
  Nothing past ever stands in front of `と`. The tense is carried by the main
  clause.

- ❌ もし{f|時間|じかん}があると、{f|手伝|てつだ}います。 → ✅ もし{f|時間|じかん}が**あれば**／**あったら**、{f|手伝|てつだ}います。
  Two problems at once: `もし` flags a hypothesis, and {f|手伝|てつだ}います is
  an offer. `と` wants neither.

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}と{f|安|やす}くなります。 → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}**だ**と{f|安|やす}くなります。
  Without `だ`, this is the N5 particle: "it gets cheaper with a student."

## Practice

**1.** {f|秋|あき}になる____、この{f|公園|こうえん}の{f|木|き}が{f|赤|あか}くなります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|秋|あき}になる<b>と</b>、この{f|公園|こうえん}の{f|木|き}が{f|赤|あか}くなります。

Every autumn, without fail, with nobody deciding anything - that is the
definition of a `と` sentence. Note the plain non-past form <code>なる</code> in
front of it even though the main clause is polite.

</details>

**2.** {f|家|いえ}に（{f|着|つ}くと・{f|着|つ}いたら）、メールをください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|家|いえ}に<b>{f|着|つ}いたら</b>、メールをください。

The main clause is a request. `と` forbids requests outright because its result
clause reports an automatic consequence, and you cannot ask someone to do
something automatically. `たら` has no such restriction.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When it gets cold, my grandmother's legs hurt."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|寒|さむ}く<b>なると</b>、{f|祖母|そぼ}は{f|足|あし}が{f|痛|いた}くなります。

A recurring, involuntary consequence - nobody's choice, every time. `と` is the
natural form. `たら` would push it towards one specific cold day.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|時間|じかん}があると、{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}きませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|時間|じかん}が<b>あったら</b>（or <b>あれば</b>）、{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}きませんか。

`～ませんか` is an invitation, so the sentence ends with the speaker's will. `と`
cannot carry that. Both `たら` and `ば` work here, because the condition is a
state (`ある`) rather than an action.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けると、{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|降|ふ}っていた。
(b) {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けると、{f|部屋|へや}が{f|寒|さむ}くなる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) has a **past** main clause, so it is not a conditional at all. It narrates one
occasion: "I opened the window, and - snow." The speaker is reporting a
discovery, and this is the standard way to write that in Japanese.

(b) has a **non-past** main clause, so it is the ordinary `と`: a general rule
about this room. Open the window, and it gets cold - every time.

Same `と`, and the tense of the *main* clause is what decides which reading you
get.

</details>
