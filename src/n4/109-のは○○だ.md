# ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences

**Meaning** · singles out one part of a sentence and puts it in the spotlight - "the one who did it was…", "it was last year that…"

Take a sentence, nominalise all of it except the piece you care about, and put
that piece after `は`…`だ`. {f|弟|おとうと}が{f|窓|まど}を{f|割|わ}った becomes
{f|窓|まど}を{f|割|わ}った<b>のは{f|弟|おとうと}です</b> - "the one who broke the
window was my brother." Everything before `のは` is treated as already agreed;
everything after it is the answer.

## Formation

The clause takes the plain form and then `の`, exactly as with the N5 nominaliser.
The focused element follows `は`, with `だ` or `です` closing the sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | dictionary form + のは…だ | {f|作|つく}る → {f|作|つく}るのは{f|母|はは}です |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のは…だ | {f|落|お}とした → {f|落|お}としたのは{f|弟|おとうと}です |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + のは…だ | {f|来|こ}なかった → {f|来|こ}なかったのは{f|田中|たなか}さんです |
| い-adjective | plain form + のは…だ | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いのはこちらです |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + のは…だ | {f|好|す}きだ → {f|好|す}き**な**のは{f|秋|あき}です |
| Focus = a reason | …のは…**からです** | {f|休|やす}んだのは{f|熱|ねつ}があったからです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the subject inside the `の`-clause takes `が`, never `は`. The
sentence already has its `は`, and it belongs to the `のは` frame:
{f|私|わたし}<b>が</b>{f|買|か}ったのはこの{f|本|ほん}です.

</div>

When the focus is a time, a place or a person, the particle that element would
have carried is normally dropped: {f|会|あ}ったのは{f|駅|えき}です. Keeping it - 
{f|駅|えき}ででした - is possible in careful writing but sounds heavy, and
learners are safer without it. A reason is the exception: it keeps `から` and adds
です, as in the last row above.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `の` here is a light noun meaning "the one / the thing / the time".
You can often replace it with the specific noun for extra clarity:
{f|割|わ}った<b>{f|人|ひと}</b>は{f|弟|おとうと}です,
{f|日本|にほん}に{f|来|き}た<b>とき</b>は{f|去年|きょねん}でした. `の` is the
general-purpose version, and it is what you reach for when no particular noun
suggests itself.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この{f|絵|え}を<b>かいたのは{f|妹|いもうと}です</b>。
My little sister is the one who drew this picture.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}が<b>{f|好|す}きなのは{f|秋|あき}です</b>。
The season I like is autumn.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}に<b>{f|来|き}たのは{f|去年|きょねん}の{f|春|はる}です</b>。
It was last spring that I came to Japan.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>{f|悪|わる}いのは{f|私|わたし}です</b>。{f|本当|ほんとう}にすみませんでした。
I'm the one at fault. I really am sorry.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|最初|さいしょ}に<b>{f|気|き}がついたのは{f|田中|たなか}さんでした</b>。
Tanaka was the one who noticed first.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昨日|きのう}<b>{f|休|やす}んだのは{f|熱|ねつ}があったからです</b>。
The reason I was off yesterday is that I had a temperature.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}が{f|毎朝|まいあさ}<b>{f|飲|の}むのはコーヒーではなく{f|紅茶|こうちゃ}です</b>。
What I drink every morning isn't coffee but tea.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}で<b>{f|反対|はんたい}したのは{f|山田|やまだ}さんだけ</b>で、ほかの{f|人|ひと}はみんな{f|賛成|さんせい}しました。
The only person who objected at the meeting was Yamada; everyone else was in favour.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

A cleft splits a sentence into two halves that do different jobs. The `の`-clause
is the part you and your listener are already taking for granted - somebody broke
the window, I came to Japan at some point. The part after `は` is the news, and
because the frame exists only to deliver it, the emphasis is much stronger than
in an ordinary sentence.

That makes clefts the natural answer to a question word. Ask
だれが{f|割|わ}ったんですか and {f|割|わ}ったのは{f|弟|おとうと}です answers it
exactly; ask いつ{f|来|き}ましたか and {f|来|き}たのは{f|先週|せんしゅう}です does
the same. It is equally the form for **correcting** someone, because the frame
lets you accept everything except the one piece you are disputing:
{f|私|わたし}が{f|言|い}ったのはそういう{f|意味|いみ}ではありません。

The pattern is register-neutral. In polite speech the ending is です／でした; in
casual speech it becomes `だ`, or vanishes altogether - 
{f|落|お}としたの、{f|弟|おとうと}? is a perfectly ordinary question between
friends. It is if anything even commoner in writing, where
{f|大切|たいせつ}なのは… and {f|問題|もんだい}なのは… open paragraphs constantly.

One shape worth learning whole: <code>{f|大切|たいせつ}なのは…ことです</code>. When
the focus itself is an action rather than a thing, it has to be nominalised too,
and there the nominaliser must be `こと`, not `の` - see the next section.

## Contrast

### ～のは○○だ vs plain `が` focus

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` presents its noun as the news. The cleft does that *and*
states out loud what everyone is presupposing, which makes it more emphatic and
more exclusive.

</div>

<pre>
{f|昨日|きのう}{f|遅|おく}れたのは<b>{f|私|わたし}です</b>。
The one who was late yesterday was me.
</pre>

Compare {f|昨日|きのう}{f|私|わたし}が{f|遅|おく}れました, which simply reports the
event with the subject highlighted. The cleft takes for granted that somebody was
late - it is the sentence you use when that is already on the table - and it
carries a clear implication that nobody else was. That exclusivity is why clefts
are so useful for owning up, for correcting, and for singling one item out of a
list.

### `の` vs `こと` as the nominaliser

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `の` when the focus is a **person, thing, time or place**.
`こと` when what you are naming is itself an **action or a proposition**, and
always when the nominalised clause sits before `だ`／`です` as the predicate.

</div>

For a cleft that identifies an entity, only `の` works:
{f|落|お}としたのは{f|弟|おとうと}です, never ❌{f|落|お}としたことは{f|弟|おとうと}です.
A person is not an event, so `こと` has nothing to name.

Turn the sentence round, though, and the requirement flips:

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}の{f|趣味|しゅみ}は<b>{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}むことです</b>。
My hobby is reading.
</pre>

Here the nominalised clause is the predicate, and ❌{f|読|よ}むのです is not
available - that string already means something else entirely, the explanatory
`んです`. The same rule governs
{f|大切|たいせつ}なのは{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|続|つづ}けることです.

Two more places where the choice is fixed, both worth memorising. Before a verb
of direct perception - {f|見|み}る, {f|聞|き}く, {f|聞|き}こえる,
{f|見|み}える - only `の` is possible, because you witness an event rather than a
fact: {f|子|こ}どもが{f|泣|な}いているのを{f|聞|き}きました. And a set of idioms
takes `こと` and nothing else: ことができる, たことがある.

### ～のは○○だ vs ～んです

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both contain `の`, and they do opposite things. `んです`
explains the **whole** situation. `のは…だ` isolates **one element** of it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}{f|出張|しゅっちょう}する<b>のは{f|大阪|おおさか}です</b>。
The place I'm travelling to next week is Osaka.
</pre>

Set that beside {f|来週|らいしゅう}{f|大阪|おおさか}に{f|出張|しゅっちょう}するんです,
"the thing is, I'm off to Osaka next week" - an explanation offered as background,
with no single element picked out. If you can point at one word and say "that is
the answer", you want the cleft; if the whole sentence is the answer, you want
`んです`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}<b>は</b>{f|一番|いちばん}{f|好|す}きなのは{f|夏|なつ}です。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>が</b>{f|一番|いちばん}{f|好|す}きなのは{f|夏|なつ}です。
  The subject sits inside the nominalised clause, and a clause like that takes
  `が`. Two `は` in one sentence leaves the first one homeless.

- ❌ {f|静|しず}か<b>だ</b>のは{f|朝|あさ}だけです。 → ✅ {f|静|しず}か<b>な</b>のは{f|朝|あさ}だけです。
  な-adjectives take `な` before a nominalising `の`, the same shape they use
  before `ので` and `のに`.

- ❌ {f|大切|たいせつ}なのは{f|練習|れんしゅう}する<b>のです</b>。 → ✅ {f|大切|たいせつ}なのは{f|練習|れんしゅう}する<b>ことです</b>。
  A nominalised clause standing before `です` must use `こと`. With `の` the
  sentence turns into an explanatory `んです` and stops meaning what you wanted.

- ❌ {f|皿|さら}を{f|割|わ}った<b>ことは</b>{f|猫|ねこ}です。 → ✅ {f|皿|さら}を{f|割|わ}った<b>のは</b>{f|猫|ねこ}です。
  When the focus is a creature, a thing or a time, the nominaliser is `の`.
  `こと` would be naming an event, which cannot equal a cat.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|料理|りょうり}を{f|作|つく}った____{f|父|ちち}です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|作|つく}った<b>のは</b>{f|父|ちち}です

The cook is a person, so the light noun `の` is the one you want, followed by `は`
to mark everything before it as shared background. The past form
{f|作|つく}った goes straight in front of `の`.

</details>

**2.** {f|私|わたし}の{f|夢|ゆめ}は{f|世界|せかい}を{f|旅行|りょこう}する（の・こと）です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|私|わたし}の{f|夢|ゆめ}は{f|世界|せかい}を{f|旅行|りょこう}する<b>こと</b>です。

A nominalised clause immediately in front of `です` has to use `こと`. Writing
するのです would produce the explanatory `んです` pattern, which says something
completely different.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It was in Osaka that we first met."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|初|はじ}めて{f|会|あ}った<b>のは{f|大阪|おおさか}でした</b>。

The meeting is presupposed and the place is the news, which is exactly what a
cleft is for. Note that the `で` you would use in
{f|大阪|おおさか}で{f|会|あ}いました is dropped once the place moves into the focus
slot.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|昨日|きのう}{f|学校|がっこう}を{f|休|やす}んだのは{f|頭|あたま}が{f|痛|いた}かったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|昨日|きのう}{f|学校|がっこう}を{f|休|やす}んだのは{f|頭|あたま}が{f|痛|いた}かった<b>からです</b>。

When the element in focus is a reason rather than a thing, the clause has to be
closed with `から` before `です`. Without it the sentence claims that the act of
staying home *was* a headache.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|弟|おとうと}が{f|皿|さら}を{f|落|お}としました。
(b) {f|皿|さら}を{f|落|お}としたのは{f|弟|おとうと}です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the event as a whole, with the subject marked as the new piece of
information. It works as an answer to "what happened?" and does not assume the
listener knows anything yet.

(b) assumes the broken plate is already common knowledge and supplies the missing
name. It answers "who did it?", it excludes everyone else, and depending on the
situation it can sound like an accusation or like clearing someone's name. Use it
when the event is settled and only the identity is in question.

</details>
