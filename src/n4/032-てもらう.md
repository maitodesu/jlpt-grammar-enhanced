# ～てもらう

**Meaning** · get someone to do something for you; have something done for your benefit

`～てもらう` describes the same favour as `～てくれる`, but from the receiving
chair. The person who benefits becomes the **subject** of the sentence, and the
person who actually does the work is demoted to a `に` phrase. That
re-arrangement is the whole difficulty: English "I had my hair cut" hides the
hairdresser, and Japanese puts them back in with `に`.

## Formation

Attach `もらう` to the て-form. The frame is
<code>Bは Aに ～てもらう</code> - B receives, A does.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + もらう | {f|直|なお}す → {f|直|なお}してもらう |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + もらいます | {f|送|おく}る → {f|送|おく}ってもらいました |
| From someone above you (humble) | て-form + いただく | {f|見|み}る → {f|見|み}ていただきました |
| Potential (used for requests) | て-form + もらえる／いただける | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}てもらえますか |
| Negative potential (polite request) | て-form + もらえませんか | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}ってもらえませんか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the doer takes `に`. Not `が`, which would make them the
receiver, and not `を`, which is reserved for the thing acted on.

</div>

`から` is possible instead of `に` when the favour is information or an object
moving from a source, and it is preferred when the giver is an organisation:
{f|大学|だいがく}<b>から</b>{f|説明|せつめい}してもらいました. With most action verbs,
`に` is the default.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the beneficiary is normally the speaker or the speaker's side, exactly
as with `くれる`. {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|山田|やまだ}さんに{f|手伝|てつだ}ってもらった
is fine when you are narrating about a third party, but it quietly puts you in
Tanaka's camp for the length of the sentence.

</div>

## Examples

</pre>

<pre>
{f|駅員|えきいん}さんに{f|行|い}き{f|方|かた}を<b>{f|教|おし}えてもらいました</b>。
I had the station attendant tell me how to get there.
</pre>

<pre>
パソコンが{f|動|うご}かなくなったので、{f|弟|おとうと}に<b>{f|見|み}てもらった</b>。
My laptop stopped working, so I got my brother to take a look at it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}、{f|美容院|びよういん}で{f|髪|かみ}を<b>{f|切|き}ってもらう</b>{f|予定|よてい}です。
I'm getting my hair cut at the salon next week.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>
{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}しの{f|日|ひ}は{f|後輩|こうはい}に{f|車|くるま}を<b>{f|出|だ}してもらう</b>つもりです。
I'm planning to get a junior colleague to lend me his car on moving day.
</pre>

<pre>

## Nuance & register

**`～てもらう` implies the receiver had a hand in it.** Not always a spoken
request - sometimes just consent, or having gone somewhere for the service - but
the event is not a bolt from the blue. That is why
{f|髪|かみ}を{f|切|き}ってもらった is the normal way to say you had a haircut: you
went to the salon and asked. Compare
{f|急|きゅう}に{f|雨|あめ}がやんでくれた, where nothing was arranged, and only
`くれる` works.

Because the receiver is the subject, `～てもらう` is also the natural frame for
**planning and arranging**: {f|明日|あした}、{f|田中|たなか}さんに{f|来|き}てもらいます
means "I'll have Tanaka come" - the speaker is organising it. Said about a person
present, this can sound managerial, so it belongs downward or sideways, not upward.

`～ていただく` is the humble form and is one of the workhorses of business
Japanese. Whole registers of office speech are built on it:
ご{f|確認|かくにん}いただけますでしょうか, {f|少々|しょうしょう}お{f|待|ま}ちいただきます.
Note the honorific frame <code>お／ご + stem + いただく</code>
（お{f|待|ま}ちいただく, ご{f|記入|きにゅう}いただく）, which is politer still than
<code>{f|待|ま}っていただく</code> and very common on signs and announcements.

The potential forms are where this pattern earns its keep in daily life.
`もらえる` means "can receive the favour", which is exactly what a request is:

- <code>～てもらえる?</code> - casual, to a friend
- <code>～てもらえますか</code> - polite, everyday
- <code>～てもらえませんか</code> - politer; the negative leaves room to refuse
- <code>～ていただけませんか</code> - humble, business standard
- <code>～ていただけないでしょうか</code> - the top of the ladder

Register: `もらう` is neutral in plain and polite speech alike, with no
male/female marking. `いただく` belongs to formal and business situations; using
it with close friends sounds like you are being funny on purpose. Casually
`もらえる?` often contracts in speech to `もらえん?` in western dialects - regional,
not standard.

## Contrast

### ～てもらう vs ～てくれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもらう` says *I got them to*; `～てくれる` says *they
did it for me*. Initiative sits with the subject.

</div>

<pre>
{f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|人|ひと}に{f|留守|るす}の{f|間|あいだ}{f|猫|ねこ}の{f|世話|せわ}を<b>してもらいました</b>。
I got a neighbour to look after the cat while I was away.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|近所|きんじょ}の{f|人|ひと}が{f|留守|るす}の{f|間|あいだ}{f|猫|ねこ}の{f|世話|せわ}を<b>してくれました</b>。
A neighbour looked after the cat for me while I was away.
</pre>

The first says you arranged it. The second says they stepped in - and it carries
more warmth, which is why thanks are almost always phrased with `くれる`:
{f|世話|せわ}をしてくれてありがとう. してもらってありがとう is occasionally heard
in casual speech, but it is not the standard shape: gratitude points at what the
other person did, so the giving verb is the one that belongs there.

There is a hard limit on `もらう`. If the doer acted entirely on their own and the
event was not arranged, `もらう` is unnatural:
{f|知|し}らない{f|人|ひと}が{f|財布|さいふ}を{f|拾|ひろ}ってくれた - a stranger
picked up my dropped wallet - cannot become ❌{f|拾|ひろ}ってもらった unless you
had asked them to.

### ～てもらう vs the causative ～させる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are English "have someone do it". `～てもらう` is
*asking* and the doer is willing; `～させる` is *making* and the speaker has
authority.

</div>

<pre>
{f|息子|むすこ}に{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に<b>{f|行|い}ってもらいました</b>。
I got my son to go and do the shopping (and thanks, son).
</pre>

<pre>
{f|息子|むすこ}に{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に<b>{f|行|い}かせました</b>。
I made my son go and do the shopping.
</pre>

Because `もらう` builds in gratitude, it softens an instruction; because `させる`
asserts power, it is unusable upward and sounds harsh even downward. Combining
both is how you ask for permission politely:
<code>{f|説明|せつめい}させていただきます</code>, "allow me to explain."

### ～てもらう vs ～てほしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てもらう` reports a favour that happened or is arranged;
`～てほしい` states a wish that has not.

</div>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}までに{f|返事|へんじ}を<b>もらいたい</b>んですが。
I'd like to get an answer by next week.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}までに{f|返事|へんじ}を<b>してほしい</b>んですが。
I'd like you to answer by next week.
</pre>

The desiderative <code>～てもらいたい</code> sits between them: it is a wish
expressed through the receiving frame, and it is a shade more indirect - and
therefore softer - than `～てほしい`, because it talks about what you would
receive rather than about what the other person should do.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|友|とも}だち<b>が</b>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってもらいました。 → ✅ {f|友|とも}だち<b>に</b>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってもらいました。
  With `もらう` the doer takes `に`. `が` would make the friend the one receiving
  the favour.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}に{f|推薦状|すいせんじょう}を{f|書|か}いて<b>もらいました</b>。（in a formal report）→ ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}に{f|推薦状|すいせんじょう}を{f|書|か}いて<b>いただきました</b>。
  Receiving a favour from someone above you takes the humble `いただく` in any
  formal setting.

- ❌ {f|助|たす}けてもらって<b>ありがとうございました</b>。 → ✅ {f|助|たす}けて<b>くださって</b>ありがとうございました。
  Thanks point at the *giver's* act, so the giving verb is the one that fits.

- ❌ {f|知|し}らない{f|人|ひと}に{f|道|みち}を{f|教|おし}えてもらった。（when they volunteered）→ ✅ {f|知|し}らない{f|人|ひと}が{f|道|みち}を{f|教|おし}えてくれた。
  Unrequested help is `くれる` territory. `もらう` implies you asked.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}に{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|出|で}てもらいます。（said to the department head）→ ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}に{f|会議|かいぎ}にご{f|出席|しゅっせき}いただけますでしょうか。
  Arranging a superior's actions out loud sounds managerial. Turn it into a
  request with the humble potential.

## Practice

**1.** カメラを{f|持|も}っていなかったので、{f|通|とお}りかかった{f|人|ひと}____{f|撮|と}ってもらいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|通|とお}りかかった{f|人|ひと}<b>に</b>{f|撮|と}ってもらいました。

The doer of the favour is marked with `に` in a `もらう` sentence. The subject slot
belongs to the speaker, who is the one receiving the service.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|荷物|にもつ}が{f|届|とど}かないので、お{f|店|みせ}に{f|電話|でんわ}して{f|調|しら}べて（もらいました・くれました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お{f|店|みせ}に{f|電話|でんわ}して{f|調|しら}べて<b>もらいました</b>。

The speaker rang up and asked, so the favour was arranged, and the shop is marked
with `に` as the doer. `くれました` would need the shop as the `が`-subject and
would suggest they checked without being asked.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Could I have you explain it once more?" (to a client)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

もう{f|一度|いちど}ご{f|説明|せつめい}<b>いただけますでしょうか</b>。／もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|説明|せつめい}して<b>いただけませんか</b>。

Upward, `もらう` becomes `いただく`, and the potential `いただける` in a negative or
でしょうか question is what turns the receiving frame into a polite request. The
お／ご + noun + いただく version is the more formal of the two.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|昨日|きのう}、{f|友|とも}だちが{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|手伝|てつだ}ってもらいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|昨日|きのう}、{f|友|とも}だち<b>に</b>{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|手伝|てつだ}ってもらいました。
（or ✅ {f|昨日|きのう}、{f|友|とも}だちが{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|手伝|てつだ}って<b>くれました</b>。）

The sentence mixes the two frames. Either keep `もらう` and mark the friend with
`に`, or keep `が` on the friend and switch to the giving verb `くれる`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|山田|やまだ}さんに{f|空港|くうこう}まで{f|送|おく}ってもらいました。
(b) {f|山田|やまだ}さんが{f|空港|くうこう}まで{f|送|おく}ってくれました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both describe Yamada driving the speaker to the airport, and both are natural.

(a) puts the speaker in the subject slot and implies the lift was arranged - you
asked, or it had been agreed in advance. (b) puts Yamada in the subject slot and
presents the lift as something he did for you, which foregrounds his kindness. If
you are thanking him, (b) is the frame you want; if you are explaining your travel
plans to someone else, (a) is more informative.

</details>
