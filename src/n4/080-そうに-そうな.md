# ～そうに・～そうな

**Meaning** · looking ～; ～ly, by the look of it - the adverbial and adnominal forms of appearance そうだ

Appearance `そうだ` inflects like a な-adjective, so it can step inside a sentence
instead of ending one. `～そうな` goes in front of a **noun** and describes how that
thing looks. `～そうに` goes in front of a **verb or adjective** and describes the
manner the doer appears to be in. Hearsay `そうだ` has no such forms at all - so a
`そうな` or `そうに` you meet in the wild is always the appearance one.

## Formation

The attachment is the same as for sentence-final appearance `そうだ`: everything
loses its ending first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb, before a noun | ます-stem + そうな | {f|泣|な}きます → {f|泣|な}きそうな{f|顔|かお} |
| Verb, before a verb | ます-stem + そうに | {f|落|お}ちます → {f|落|お}ちそうに{f|見|み}える |
| い-adjective | drop い + そうな／そうに | {f|楽|たの}しい → {f|楽|たの}しそうに |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + そうな／そうに | {f|大変|たいへん}だ → {f|大変|たいへん}そうな{f|仕事|しごと} |
| いい (irregular) | よ + さそうな／さそうに | いい → よさそうな{f|席|せき} |
| ない (irregular) | な + さそうな／さそうに | ない → なさそうに |
| Negative (verb) | ます-stem + そうにない／そうもない | {f|届|とど}きます → {f|届|とど}きそうにない |
| Before a noun | そう + な | そうな{f|人|ひと} |
| Before a verb / adjective | そう + に | そうに{f|笑|わら}う |
| Noun | - | not possible; use `のような`／`みたいな` |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same choice as every な-adjective. Noun follows → `そうな`.
Verb or adjective follows → `そうに`. And `そうにない` is literally `そうに` + `ない`,
which is why the negative of a verb takes this shape rather than ❌`そうくない`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}は<b>ねむそうな</b>{f|顔|かお}をしている。
My little brother has a sleepy look on his face.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもたちが<b>{f|楽|たの}しそうに</b>{f|遊|あそ}んでいる。
The children are playing away happily.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>{f|重|おも}そうな</b>かばんですね。{f|持|も}ちましょうか。
That bag looks heavy. Shall I carry it?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|犬|いぬ}が<b>うれしそうに</b>しっぽをふっている。
The dog is wagging its tail in delight.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}は<b>{f|言|い}いにくそうに</b>{f|話|はな}し{f|始|はじ}めた。
She started speaking as though she found it hard to get the words out.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}にも<b>{f|泣|な}きそうな</b>{f|声|こえ}で{f|電話|でんわ}をかけてきた。
He rang me in a voice that sounded ready to break.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>よさそうな</b>{f|席|せき}が{f|空|あ}いていたので、そこに{f|座|すわ}った。
A decent-looking seat was free, so I sat there.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雪|ゆき}がひどいので、バスは<b>{f|来|き}そうにない</b>ですね。
The snow's bad, so it doesn't look like the bus is coming.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

These forms are register-neutral and belong to speech and writing alike. Politeness
lives elsewhere in the sentence - <code>{f|楽|たの}しそうに{f|話|はな}しています</code>
is polite, <code>{f|楽|たの}しそうに{f|話|はな}している</code> is plain, and `そうに`
itself never changes.

`そうに` before a verb of action is worth dwelling on, because English does not
carve it up the same way. <code>おいしそうに{f|食|た}べる</code> does **not** mean
"eat something that looks delicious" - it means "eat with evident enjoyment". The
`そう` describes the **eater's appearance**, not the food's. The same goes for
<code>{f|眠|ねむ}そうに{f|運転|うんてん}する</code> and
<code>つまらなそうに{f|聞|き}く</code>: in every case you are reading the person.

Three combinations worth learning as units:

- <code>～そうに{f|見|み}える</code> - "looks ～". Slightly more deliberate than plain
  `そうだ`, and useful when you want to contrast appearance with reality:
  {f|元気|げんき}そうに{f|見|み}えるが、{f|本当|ほんとう}は{f|疲|つか}れている。
- <code>～そうにする</code> - "act ～", put on the appearance:
  {f|忙|いそが}しそうにしている。
- <code>{f|今|いま}にも～そうな</code> - "on the very point of ～". `今にも` is the
  standard partner adverb for imminence.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ`, and with it `そうな` and `そうに`, is narrower than any of them: the
**immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis, and has no
adverbial or adnominal form to offer.

</div>

### ～そうな vs ～そうに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうな` describes a **thing**; `そうに` describes an **action**.
Look at what comes after the pattern, not before it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は<b>{f|苦|くる}しそうな</b>{f|顔|かお}をした。
He made a pained face.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は<b>{f|苦|くる}しそうに</b>{f|息|いき}をしていた。
He was breathing with evident difficulty.
</pre>

The first attributes the look to the face; the second attributes it to the way he
was breathing. The difference is small in English and structural in Japanese: put
`そうな` in front of a verb and there is nothing for it to modify.

### ～そうに vs ～ように／みたいに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうに` reads the doer's own appearance. `ように` and `みたいに`
compare the action to **something else**. If a second thing enters the sentence, it
is not `そうに`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|母|はは}は<b>{f|悲|かな}しそうに</b>{f|手紙|てがみ}を{f|読|よ}んでいた。
My mother was reading the letter with a sad look.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|母|はは}は{f|何度|なんど}も{f|読|よ}んだことがある<b>ように</b>{f|手紙|てがみ}を{f|読|よ}んでいた。
My mother read the letter as though she had read it many times before.
</pre>

In the first, all the information is on her face. In the second, the sentence brings
in an imagined alternative situation and measures her against it.

### ～そうにない vs ～ないそうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうにない` is *my* reading of the situation. `ないそうだ` is
*somebody else's* information. They sound alike and they mean entirely different
things.

</div>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんは<b>{f|来|き}そうにない</b>。
It doesn't look like Tanaka is coming. (it's late and there's no sign of him)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんは<b>{f|来|こ}ないそうだ</b>。
Tanaka isn't coming, apparently. (he told somebody, and it got to me)
</pre>

Note the verb shape as well as the ending: `そうにない` sits on the **ます-stem**
<code>{f|来|き}</code>, while hearsay `そうだ` sits on the full negative
<code>{f|来|こ}ない</code>. Two different readings of the same kanji, which makes this
pair worth saying out loud until it sticks.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ねむ**そうだ**{f|顔|かお} → ✅ ねむ**そうな**{f|顔|かお}
  `そうだ` ends a sentence. In front of a noun it takes the `な` form, exactly like
  <code>{f|静|しず}かだ</code> becoming <code>{f|静|しず}かな</code>.

- ❌ {f|楽|たの}し**そうな**{f|遊|あそ}んでいる → ✅ {f|楽|たの}し**そうに**{f|遊|あそ}んでいる
  A verb follows, so the adverbial form is the only option.

- ❌ **いいそうな**{f|店|みせ} → ✅ **よさそうな**{f|店|みせ}
  `いい` takes the irregular `さ` cushion. <code>いいそうな</code> is not a form at
  all - hearsay `そうだ` has no `な`.

- ❌ {f|間|ま}に<b>{f|合|あ}うそうにない</b> → ✅ {f|間|ま}に<b>{f|合|あ}いそうにない</b>
  `そうにない` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form
  before `そう` would be hearsay, which cannot be negated this way.

- ❌ {f|高|たか}い**そうな**{f|店|みせ} → ✅ {f|高|たか}**そうな**{f|店|みせ}
  Drop the `い`. With it, you have tried to build an adnominal form out of the
  hearsay pattern, which does not have one.

## Practice

**1.** {f|山|やま}のような{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|見|み}て、{f|妹|いもうと}は{f|泣|な}き____{f|顔|かお}になった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|妹|いもうと}は{f|泣|な}き<b>そうな</b>{f|顔|かお}になった。

<code>{f|顔|かお}</code> is a noun, so the adnominal `そうな` is required, and it sits
on the ます-stem <code>{f|泣|な}き</code>. With a verb it means the crying is on the
point of starting.

</details>

**2.** {f|天気|てんき}{f|予報|よほう}を{f|見|み}て：「{f|明日|あした}は（{f|降|ふ}りそうにない・{f|降|ふ}らないそうだ）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|明日|あした}は<b>{f|降|ふ}らないそうだ</b>。

The information came from the forecast - somebody else's report - so this is
hearsay `そうだ` on the plain negative. `そうにない` would mean you had looked at the
sky and judged for yourself, with no forecast involved.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The children were eating the cake happily."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|子|こ}どもたちはケーキを<b>うれしそうに</b>{f|食|た}べていた。

`そうに` goes in front of the verb and describes how the children looked, not how
the cake looked. Putting `そうな` here would leave the pattern with no noun to
modify.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|重|おも}いそうな{f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|運|はこ}んでいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|重|おも}**そうな**{f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|運|はこ}んでいた。

The `い` has to go: appearance `そう` attaches to the adjective stem. Keeping it
produces the hearsay pattern, which has no `な` form and so cannot sit in front of
<code>{f|荷物|にもつ}</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}は<b>{f|忙|いそが}しそうに</b>{f|働|はたら}いている。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}は<b>{f|忙|いそが}しそうにしている</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply describes how he looks while working: rushed, absorbed, no judgement
attached. (b) uses <code>そうにする</code>, "to act ～", and so hints that the busy
appearance is being **put on** - he is making a show of being busy. That extra layer
of suspicion is carried entirely by the verb <code>する</code>.

</details>
