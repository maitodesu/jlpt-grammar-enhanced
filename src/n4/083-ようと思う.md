# ～(よ)うと思う

**Meaning** · I think I'll ～; I'm thinking of ～ing - an intention, presented as a
thought rather than a fact

<code>～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}う</code> takes the volitional - the "let's / I'll" form - 
and wraps it in "I think". The result is the most natural way in Japanese to
answer *what are you going to do?* It is softer than `つもり` because it frames
the plan as something going on in your head right now, which leaves the door
open. Note the shape: what you are thinking is <code>{f|行|い}こう</code>, "I'll go";
`と` quotes that thought.

## Formation

The verb before `と` is the **plain volitional**, always. `ましょう` cannot be
quoted here.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|習|なら}う → {f|習|なら}おうと{f|思|おも}う |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|始|はじ}める → {f|始|はじ}めようと{f|思|おも}う |
| Verb (する) | しよう + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|予約|よやく}する → {f|予約|よやく}しようと{f|思|おも}う |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | こよう + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ようと{f|思|おも}う |
| Polite | {f|思|おも}う → {f|思|おも}います | {f|習|なら}おうと{f|思|おも}います |
| Intention already held | ～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}っている | {f|習|なら}おうと{f|思|おも}っています |
| Intention in the past | ～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}った | {f|習|なら}おうと{f|思|おも}った |
| Softer still | ～(よ)うかと{f|思|おも}っている | {f|習|なら}おうかと{f|思|おも}っています |
| Rejecting the idea | ～(よ)うとは{f|思|おも}わない | {f|習|なら}おうとは{f|思|おも}わない |
| As a reason | ～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}って、… | {f|習|なら}おうと{f|思|おも}って、{f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}みました |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - there is no way to say "I'm thinking of **not** doing it" with
a negative volitional in ordinary speech. Use
<code>～のはやめようと{f|思|おも}う</code> ("I'm thinking of giving up ～ing") or
<code>～ないことにしようと{f|思|おも}う</code>. The literary negative volitional
<code>～まいと{f|思|おも}う</code> exists but belongs in novels.

</div>

## Nuance & register

The whole point of this pattern is **softness**. `つもり` states a settled plan;
<code>～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}います</code> reports a thought, and a thought can change
without anyone losing face. That makes it the default answer to a superior, a
customer, or anyone whose reaction you have not heard yet.

The `ています` layer does most of the fine-tuning:

- <code>～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}います</code> - the decision is being taken **as you
  speak**. It fits a reply on the spot: 「じゃあ、そうしようと{f|思|おも}います。」
- <code>～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}っています</code> - you have been holding this intention
  for a while. It sounds more considered and, in practice, closer to a real plan.
  In a job interview or a meeting this is the form you want.

The same `ています` is what lets you talk about **other people**. Exactly as with
<code>{f|思|おも}う</code> generally, a bare
<code>～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}う</code> claims the thought is yours; someone else's
intention needs <code>{f|思|おも}っている</code>, or evidence marking
（<code>{f|思|おも}っているようです</code>,
<code>{f|思|おも}っていると{f|言|い}っていました</code>）.

One structural point that catches people: the person doing the volitional verb
must be the same person doing the thinking. You can say
{f|私|わたし}が{f|手伝|てつだ}おうと{f|思|おも}います ("I'll help"), but you cannot
put someone else in there - ❌<code>{f|彼|かれ}が{f|来|こ}ようと{f|思|おも}います</code>
is impossible. To talk about what you want *others* to do, you need a different
pattern altogether.

Register is otherwise neutral. Plain
<code>{f|行|い}こうと{f|思|おも}う</code> is everyday casual speech; in stiffer
written Japanese you will meet <code>～(よ)うと{f|考|かんが}えております</code> and
<code>～たいと{f|存|ぞん}じます</code>, which do the same softening job in keigo.
Adding `か` - <code>～(よ)うかと{f|思|おも}っています</code> - softens further, to
roughly "I've half a mind to…", and is very useful when you genuinely have not
decided.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}う vs ～つもりだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}う</code> is a decision **being made
or reported now**; `つもり` is a decision **already settled**. Neither one is
English "will".

</div>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}、{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|住|す}もうと{f|思|おも}っています。
I'm thinking of living on my own next year.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}、{f|一人|ひとり}で<b>{f|住|す}むつもりです</b>。
I intend to live on my own next year.
</pre>

The first invites a response - the listener could still say "are you sure?" and
not be contradicting you. The second reports a fixed position, and pushing back
against it means arguing. This is why
<code>～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}います</code> is the safer thing to say when your plan
touches someone else's, and why `つもり` is the right choice when you want to be
believed.

There is also a hard difference: `つもり` can be denied
（<code>{f|行|い}くつもりはありません</code>）and questioned about others
（<code>どうするつもりですか</code>, which sounds like an interrogation). The
<code>{f|思|おも}う</code> pattern has no equivalent edge to it.

### ～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}う vs ～と{f|思|おも}う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **volitional** + <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> = my intention.
**Plain form** + <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> = my opinion or prediction about how
things will go.

</div>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}も<b>{f|行|い}こうと{f|思|おも}います</b>。
I think I'll go too. (I'm deciding to go)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんも<b>{f|行|い}くと{f|思|おも}います</b>。
I think Tanaka will go too. (my guess about him)
</pre>

Get this pair wrong and the sentence does not become ungrammatical - it becomes a
different statement, which is worse. Saying
<code>{f|私|わたし}も{f|行|い}くと{f|思|おも}います</code> about yourself sounds
oddly detached, as though you were predicting your own behaviour from the
outside. It is occasionally said, but it is not how you volunteer.

Because the plain form is for prediction, it is also what you use for events
nobody decides: {f|明日|あした}は{f|晴|は}れると{f|思|おも}います.

### ～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}います vs ～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}っています

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|思|おも}います</code> is the thought arriving now;
<code>{f|思|おも}っています</code> is a thought you have been carrying.

</div>

<pre>
それはいい{f|案|あん}ですね。じゃあ、そう<b>しようと{f|思|おも}います</b>。
That's a good idea. All right, I'll do that.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|前|まえ}から{f|会社|かいしゃ}を<b>{f|辞|や}めようと{f|思|おも}っていました</b>。
I'd been thinking about leaving the company for a while.
</pre>

In the first, the decision is a response to what was just said, so
<code>{f|思|おも}います</code> is right and
<code>{f|思|おも}っています</code> would sound like you had planned it in advance.
In the second, the `ていました` is doing real work: it says the intention predates
this conversation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|行|い}き**ましょう**と{f|思|おも}います。 → ✅ {f|行|い}**こう**と{f|思|おも}います。
  Only the plain volitional can be quoted with `と`. The politeness sits on
  <code>{f|思|おも}います</code>.

- ❌ {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|辞|や}めようと{f|思|おも}います。 → ✅ …{f|辞|や}めようと{f|思|おも}って**います**。
  A bare <code>{f|思|おも}う</code> asserts your own mind. For a third person use
  <code>{f|思|おも}っている</code>, or hedge with
  <code>{f|思|おも}っているようです</code>.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ろうと{f|思|おも}います。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ると{f|思|おも}います。
  Weather has no volition, so it cannot take the volitional. Plain form +
  <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> is prediction.

- ❌ {f|買|か}わないようと{f|思|おも}います。 → ✅ {f|買|か}うのはやめようと{f|思|おも}います。
  There is no everyday negative volitional. Negate the *plan*, not the verb.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}に{f|手伝|てつだ}おうと{f|思|おも}います。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}に{f|手伝|てつだ}ってもらおうと{f|思|おも}います。
  The volitional describes what **you** will do. If someone else does the acting,
  the sentence has to be rebuilt around that.

## Practice

**1.** {f|来年|らいねん}、{f|中国語|ちゅうごくご}を{f|習|なら}____と{f|思|おも}っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|習|なら}<b>おう</b>と{f|思|おも}っています。

<code>{f|習|なら}う</code> is a う-verb ending in `う`, so the volitional is
<code>{f|習|なら}おう</code>. The `ています` says this is an intention you have been
holding, not one formed at this second.

</details>

**2.** {f|面接|めんせつ}で：「{f|卒業|そつぎょう}したら、どうしますか。」
「（{f|働|はたら}くつもりです・{f|働|はたら}こうと{f|思|おも}っています）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|働|はたら}<b>こうと{f|思|おも}っています</b>。

Both are grammatical, but in an interview the softer form is the safer register:
it reports a considered intention without sounding as though your mind is closed.
`つもりです` is not rude, only firmer, and it invites the follow-up "and if that
doesn't work out?"

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm thinking of taking a day off next week."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|来週|らいしゅう}、{f|休|やす}みを<b>{f|取|と}ろうと{f|思|おも}っています</b>。

<code>{f|取|と}る</code> is a う-verb → <code>{f|取|と}ろう</code>. Because this is a
plan you are floating rather than announcing,
<code>{f|思|おも}っています</code> is the natural choice; it is also the standard
way to raise leave with a manager.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|弟|おとうと}は{f|大学|だいがく}で{f|医学|いがく}を{f|勉強|べんきょう}しようと{f|思|おも}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|弟|おとうと}は{f|大学|だいがく}で{f|医学|いがく}を{f|勉強|べんきょう}しようと<b>{f|思|おも}っています</b>。

You cannot state a third person's inner decision with a bare
<code>{f|思|おも}う</code>. <code>{f|思|おも}っている</code> presents it as a state
you have observed. <code>…と{f|言|い}っていました</code> would work too, and marks
where the information came from.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|明日|あした}、{f|銀行|ぎんこう}へ{f|行|い}こうと{f|思|おも}います。
(b) {f|明日|あした}、{f|銀行|ぎんこう}へ{f|行|い}くと{f|思|おも}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is your own intention: the volitional says the going is your decision, and
<code>と{f|思|おも}います</code> presents it gently. This is what you say when
someone asks about your plans.

(b) predicts. With the plain form, <code>と{f|思|おも}います</code> gives an
opinion about what will happen - most naturally about somebody else, and about
yourself it sounds like you are guessing at your own future rather than choosing
it. Learners produce (b) by translating "I think I'll go", which is exactly the
wrong route.

</details>
