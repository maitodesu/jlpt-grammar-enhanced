# ～みたい

**Meaning** · seems like; looks like; is like - the everyday spoken form of ようだ

`～みたい` says exactly what `ようだ` says: the speaker has drawn a conclusion from
evidence, or is comparing one thing to another. What changes is the register - this
is the form you actually hear in conversation - and the attachment, which is
simpler: `みたい` never wants the `の` or the `な` that `よう` insists on.

## Formation

Everything goes in front of `みたい` in its **bare** form. That is the whole rule.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + みたい | {f|降|ふ}る → {f|降|ふ}るみたい |
| Verb (past) | た-form + みたい | {f|来|き}た → {f|来|き}たみたい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + みたい | {f|分|わ}からない → {f|分|わ}からないみたい |
| い-adjective | plain form + みたい | {f|寒|さむ}い → {f|寒|さむ}いみたい |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + みたい | {f|元気|げんき}だ → {f|元気|げんき}みたい |
| Noun | Noun + みたい (no の) | {f|風邪|かぜ} → {f|風邪|かぜ}みたい |
| Polite | みたい + です | {f|降|ふ}るみたいです |
| Past | みたいだった／みたいでした | {f|風邪|かぜ}みたいでした |
| Before a noun | みたい + な | {f|夢|ゆめ}みたいな{f|話|はなし} |
| Before a verb / adjective | みたい + に | {f|雪|ゆき}みたいに{f|白|しろ}い |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `みたい` behaves like a な-adjective *after* it attaches
(みたい**だ**／みたい**な**／みたい**に**), but takes nothing *before* it. Compare
<code>{f|子|こ}どものようだ</code> with <code>{f|子|こ}どもみたいだ</code>: the `の`
belongs to `よう` only.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - this `みたい` is **always written in kana**. <code>{f|見|み}たい</code> with
the kanji is a different word entirely - the ます-stem of <code>{f|見|み}る</code> plus
`たい`, "want to see". <code>{f|見|み}たい{f|映画|えいが}</code> is a film you want to
watch; <code>{f|映画|えいが}みたい</code> is "it's like a film". The two are commonly
explained as related - `みたい` is usually traced back to a contraction of
<code>{f|見|み}たよう</code> - but in modern Japanese they are separate words, and
writing this one with kanji is simply an error.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|外|そと}、{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ってる<b>みたい</b>だよ。
It looks like it's raining out there.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|人|ひと}、{f|先生|せんせい}<b>みたい</b>だね。
That person seems to be a teacher.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}くんは{f|今日|きょう}{f|休|やす}み<b>みたいです</b>。
Tanaka seems to be off today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|家|いえ}のかぎをかけ{f|忘|わす}れた<b>みたい</b>。
I think I forgot to lock up the house.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|辺|へん}は{f|夜|よる}になると{f|静|しず}か<b>みたい</b>だ。
This neighbourhood seems to go quiet once night falls.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}はゲームをしていて、こっちの{f|話|はなし}が{f|聞|き}こえていない<b>みたい</b>だ。
My brother's playing a game and doesn't seem to hear a word I'm saying.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|林|はやし}さんは{f|新|あたら}しい{f|仕事|しごと}が{f|楽|たの}しい<b>みたいです</b>ね。
Hayashi seems to be enjoying the new job.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|何度|なんど}{f|電話|でんわ}してもつながらないから、まだ{f|会議|かいぎ}が{f|終|お}わっていない<b>みたい</b>だ。
I can't get through however many times I call, so the meeting apparently isn't over yet.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`みたい` is **casual**. Between friends, family and close colleagues it is the
default; `ようだ` in the same conversation sounds like reading from a memo. Adding
`です` - みたいです - raises it to a polite-but-relaxed level that is perfectly
normal at work in speech, but it never becomes *formal*. In a written report, a
notice, a news article or a speech, switch to `ようです`.

Sentence-final `みたい` with **nothing after it** is extremely common in speech:
「{f|終|お}わったみたい」「{f|留守|るす}みたい」. The `だ` is optional, and dropping it
softens the sentence; keeping the `だ` makes it flatter and blunter, a tendency
associated more with male speech, though it is not a rule and plenty of speakers of
any gender use both.

`みたい` also does emotional work that `ようだ` is too stiff for. 「うそみたい!」 is
"I can't believe it!", not a claim about lying. 「{f|夢|ゆめ}みたい」 is what you say
when something wonderful happens. These are frozen exclamations; learn them whole.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay
`そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words.

</div>

### ～みたい vs ～ようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. `みたい` for speech, `ようだ` for writing and
formal speech. Attachment: `Noun + みたい` but `Noun + のようだ`; `な-adj + みたい`
but `な-adj + なようだ`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|山田|やまだ}さん、もう{f|帰|かえ}っちゃった<b>みたい</b>。
Looks like Yamada's already gone home.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山田|やまだ}は{f|既|すで}に{f|退社|たいしゃ}した<b>ようです</b>。
It appears that Yamada has already left for the day.
</pre>

Same fact, two worlds. The second is what you would write in a message to a client;
say it to a friend and you sound like an answering machine.

### ～みたい vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are common in casual speech, but they point in opposite
directions. `みたい` says *I* worked it out. `らしい` says *somebody told me* - and
quietly declines responsibility for it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|加藤|かとう}さん、{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>みたい</b>だよ。{f|箱|はこ}がたくさんあった。
Kato seems to be moving - there were boxes everywhere.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|加藤|かとう}さん、{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>らしい</b>よ。{f|課長|かちょう}が{f|言|い}ってた。
Kato's apparently moving - the section chief said so.
</pre>

The second half of each sentence is doing the diagnostic work. `みたい` invites
"what makes you think so?"; `らしい` invites "who told you?".

### ～みたい vs appearance ～そう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そう` reports the **surface**: what the thing looks like right
now, with no reasoning. `みたい` reports a **conclusion**, however small the leap.
And they attach differently: <code>おいしいみたい</code> keeps the `い`;
<code>おいしそう</code> drops it.

</div>

<pre>
このスープ、<b>おいしそう</b>。
This soup looks delicious. (I'm looking at it; I haven't tasted it)
</pre>

<pre>
このスープ、<b>おいしいみたい</b>。
This soup is apparently good. (people are going back for more, so I conclude…)
</pre>

Say <b>おいしそう</b> in front of a bowl you are about to eat. Say
<b>おいしいみたい</b> about a bowl in front of somebody else. Getting this backwards
is the single commonest error in this corner of the grammar.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}**のみたい**です → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}**みたい**です
  The `の` belongs to `よう`. `みたい` attaches to a bare noun.

- ❌ {f|元気|げんき}**なみたい** → ✅ {f|元気|げんき}**みたい**
  な-adjectives drop the `な` in front of `みたい`, unlike in front of `よう`.

- ❌ この{f|映画|えいが}、{f|面白|おもしろ}い{f|見|み}たい。 → ✅ この{f|映画|えいが}、{f|面白|おもしろ}い**みたい**。
  Never write this pattern with kanji. <code>{f|見|み}たい</code> means "want to see"
  and turns the sentence into nonsense.

- ❌ (in a written notice) {f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になった**みたいです**。 → ✅ {f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になった**ようです**。
  Grammatical but out of register. Written announcements do not use `みたい`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|部屋|へや}の{f|電気|でんき}が{f|消|き}えている。だれもいない____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

だれもいない<b>みたい</b>（だ）。

The verb goes in front of `みたい` in plain form with nothing added, and the
final `だ` is optional in speech. The speaker is reasoning from the dark room, which
is what makes this `みたい` rather than `らしい`.

</details>

**2.** となりの{f|部屋|へや}から{f|音|おと}がする。だれか（いるみたい・いるらしい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

だれか<b>いるみたい</b>。

You are hearing the noise yourself, so the evidence is first-hand and the
conclusion is yours. `らしい` would suggest somebody had told you there was a person
next door, which is not the situation the sentence describes.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "This bag seems to be Suzuki's."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このかばん、{f|鈴木|すずき}さん<b>みたい</b>だよ。

A bare noun takes `みたい` with nothing between them. The formal equivalent would be
<code>{f|鈴木|すずき}さんのようです</code>, where the `の` is compulsory.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼|かれ}は{f|料理|りょうり}が{f|上手|じょうず}なみたいですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|料理|りょうり}が{f|上手|じょうず}**みたい**ですね。

<code>{f|上手|じょうず}</code> is a な-adjective, and な-adjectives shed their `な` before
`みたい`. The version with `な` is the one you would use before `よう`:
<code>{f|上手|じょうず}なようですね</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) あの{f|子|こ}、**さむそう**。
(b) あの{f|子|こ}、**さむいみたい**。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a reading of the surface: the child is hunched up and shivering, and you say
so on sight, with no reasoning in between. (b) is a small deduction - perhaps the
child asked for a blanket, or keeps going back indoors - so you are concluding
rather than describing. Both are natural; (a) is what you say the instant you look
over.

</details>
