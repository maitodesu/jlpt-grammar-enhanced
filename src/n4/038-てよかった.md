# ～てよかった

**Meaning** · I'm glad I did that; it's a good thing that happened - relief, looking back

`～てよかった` evaluates something that has **actually happened** and finds it
good. The feeling underneath is relief: it could have gone the other way, and it
didn't. That backward glance is what separates it from a plain "I'm happy", and it
is why the past tense `よかった` is fixed even when the good thing is still going
on.

## Formation

The て-form of the thing that happened, then `よかった` - the past of
<code>いい／よい</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + よかった | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}てよかった |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + よかった | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}かなくてよかった |
| い-adjective | くて + よかった | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}くてよかった |
| な-adjective | で + よかった | {f|元気|げんき} → {f|元気|げんき}でよかった |
| Noun | で + よかった | いい{f|天気|てんき} → いい{f|天気|てんき}でよかった |
| Polite | ～てよかったです | {f|習|なら}う → {f|習|なら}ってよかったです |
| Reported | ～てよかったと{f|思|おも}う | {f|買|か}う → {f|買|か}ってよかったと{f|思|おも}う |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よかった` is always past, even about the present or the
future. {f|明日|あした}{f|休|やす}みでよかった - "I'm glad tomorrow's a day off" - 
is about tomorrow, and `よかった` still cannot become `いい`. What is past is the
*establishing* of the fact, not the fact itself.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `～なくてよかった` and `～ないでよかった` are both heard.
`なくて` is the standard reason form and the safer answer on a test;
`ないで` turns up in speech, especially where a deliberate choice is meant:
{f|行|い}かないでよかった, "good thing I decided not to go."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
<b>{f|来|き}てよかった</b>。
I'm glad I came.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}らなくてよかった</b>。
Good thing it didn't rain.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|映画|えいが}を<b>{f|見|み}てよかった</b>です。
I'm glad I watched this film.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|傘|かさ}を<b>{f|持|も}ってきてよかった</b>。{f|急|きゅう}に{f|降|ふ}りだしたね。
Good thing I brought an umbrella - it's suddenly started pouring.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|事故|じこ}にあったと{f|聞|き}きましたが、けがが<b>なくてよかった</b>ですね。
I heard you were in an accident - I'm so glad you weren't hurt.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|日|ひ}、{f|勇気|ゆうき}を{f|出|だ}して<b>{f|声|こえ}をかけてよかった</b>。
I'm glad I plucked up the courage to say something to her that day.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|学生|がくせい}のうちに{f|日本語|にほんご}を<b>{f|習|なら}っておいてよかった</b>と{f|今|いま}になって{f|思|おも}う。
Now that I'm older I'm glad I learned Japanese while I was still a student.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|大学|だいがく}のときに{f|留学|りゅうがく}<b>してよかった</b>と、{f|働|はたら}き{f|始|はじ}めてから{f|強|つよ}く{f|感|かん}じるようになった。
Since starting work I've come to feel strongly that studying abroad at university was the right call.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The relief is the point.** `～てよかった` implies an alternative that would have
been worse. That is why {f|生|う}まれてよかった sounds strange out of context - 
nothing was at stake - while {f|生|い}きていてよかった is powerful. If you are
simply reporting that something nice occurred, with no sense of a near miss, the
pattern is a poor fit.

**Uses split three ways**, and learners usually only meet the first:

1. **Relief about your own past choice.** {f|早|はや}めに{f|出|で}てよかった.
2. **Relief about someone else's situation.** {f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}ってよかったですね
 - said *to* the person, with `ね`. This is how Japanese expresses "thank
   goodness" on someone else's behalf, and the `ね` is close to obligatory.
3. **Relief that something did *not* happen.** {f|大|おお}きい{f|地震|じしん}じゃなくてよかった.

**Register.** Plain `よかった` is casual and extremely common as a one-word
reaction - a friend tells you their exam went well and you say ああ、よかった.
`よかったです` is polite and fine in ordinary conversation, though in careful or
business speech people often prefer
<code>よかったと{f|思|おも}います</code> or
<code>{f|何|なに}よりです</code>（ご{f|無事|ぶじ}で{f|何|なに}よりです）, because a
bare emotional `よかったです` can sound a little bald upward.

**Emphasis** is done with <code>{f|本当|ほんとう}に</code>, `つくづく`, or by stretching the vowel in
speech: {f|来|き}てよかったあ. The written-out long vowel is casual.

`～てよかった` also lives inside larger structures:
<code>～てよかったと{f|思|おも}う</code> (reporting your own judgement, common in
writing), <code>～てよかったですね</code> (to someone else), and
<code>～てよかったのに</code>, which flips the meaning to "you needn't have" - 
{f|来|こ}なくてよかったのに, "you didn't have to come all this way." The `のに`
does that work, not the `よかった`.

## Contrast

### ～てよかった vs ～ばよかった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てよかった` looks at what you **did** and approves.
`～ばよかった` looks at what you **didn't** and regrets it.

</div>

This is the pair that matters most, because they sit one letter apart in a
learner's head and mean opposite things.

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|予約|よやく}してよかった</b>。
I'm glad I booked early.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|予約|よやく}すればよかった</b>。
I should have booked early.
</pre>

The first is a satisfied look back at a real action; the second is regret about an
action that never happened. `～たらよかった` and `～ておけばよかった` work the same
way. Note the negatives, which double the confusion:
{f|行|い}かなくてよかった is relief that you stayed home,
{f|行|い}かなければよかった is regret that you went.

### ～てよかった vs ～てうれしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `うれしい` reports the emotion; `よかった` delivers a verdict
on the event. `よかった` carries relief, `うれしい` carries joy.

</div>

<pre>
{f|合格|ごうかく}できて<b>うれしい</b>です。
I'm happy I passed.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|合格|ごうかく}できて<b>よかった</b>です。
I'm relieved I passed.
</pre>

Both are natural here, because passing an exam is both a pleasure and a narrow
escape. They come apart at the edges. Pure good news takes `うれしい` - 
プレゼントをもらってうれしい, where nothing bad was ever in prospect. Averted
disaster takes `よかった` - {f|財布|さいふ}が{f|見|み}つかってよかった, where
"うれしい" would understate the panic that preceded it. And only `よかった` works
for a negative event: ❌{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}らなくてうれしい is odd.

### ～てよかった vs ～て{f|助|たす}かった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `よかった` evaluates the situation; <code>{f|助|たす}かった</code> says *you*
personally were rescued from trouble by it. <code>{f|助|たす}かった</code> needs a helper, usually a
person.

</div>

<pre>
{f|道|みち}が{f|空|す}いていて<b>よかった</b>。
Good thing the roads were clear.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくださって<b>{f|助|たす}かりました</b>。
You really saved me by helping out.
</pre>

Because <code>{f|助|たす}かる</code> points at a rescuer, it doubles as thanks and is one of the most
useful phrases in working Japanese - 
{f|代|か}わってくれて{f|助|たす}かった. `よかった` cannot do that job: saying
{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくださってよかったです to the person who helped sounds like you
are grading their performance rather than thanking them.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|来|き}て<b>いいです</b>。（meaning "I'm glad I came"）→ ✅ {f|来|き}て<b>よかったです</b>。
  The pattern is fixed in the past. `いい` here would be read as permission - 
  "you may come."

- ❌ {f|勉強|べんきょう}<b>すればよかった</b>と{f|思|おも}っています。（meaning "I'm glad I studied"）→ ✅ {f|勉強|べんきょう}<b>してよかった</b>と{f|思|おも}っています。
  `～ばよかった` is regret about what you did *not* do. It reverses your meaning
  completely.

- ❌ {f|元気|げんき}<b>くてよかった</b>。 → ✅ {f|元気|げんき}<b>でよかった</b>。
  <code>{f|元気|げんき}</code> is a な-adjective, so it connects with `で`. Only
  い-adjectives take `くて`.

- ❌ {f|遅|おく}れ<b>ないでよかったです</b>ね。（to someone whose train was on time）→ ✅ {f|遅|おく}れ<b>なくてよかったです</b>ね。
  For a fact rather than a decision, `なくて` is the natural reason form. `ないで`
  suggests they chose not to be late.

- ❌ プレゼントをもらって<b>よかった</b>。（just pleased）→ ✅ プレゼントをもらって<b>うれしい</b>。
  Not ungrammatical, but `よかった` implies you had feared not getting one, which
  is not usually what you mean.

## Practice

**1.** {f|台風|たいふう}が{f|来|こ}なく____、{f|旅行|りょこう}に{f|行|い}けました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|台風|たいふう}が{f|来|こ}なく<b>てよかった</b>。

The negative reason form is <code>{f|来|こ}なくて</code>, and `よかった` supplies the
relief: the trip could easily have been cancelled, and wasn't. That averted
alternative is exactly what the pattern is for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|昨日|きのう}、もっと{f|早|はや}く{f|寝|ね}（てよかった・ればよかった）。You were exhausted all day today.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|昨日|きのう}、もっと{f|早|はや}く{f|寝|ね}<b>ればよかった</b>。

You did *not* go to bed early, and you regret it, so the counterfactual
`～ばよかった` is required. <code>{f|寝|ね}てよかった</code> would claim you went to bed early and are
pleased about it - the opposite of the situation described.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm glad I asked you before I bought it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|買|か}う{f|前|まえ}に<b>{f|聞|き}いてよかった</b>。（polite: <b>{f|聞|き}いてよかったです</b>）

The action really happened and turned out well, so it is `～てよかった`, not
`～ばよかった`. The relief is implied: had you not asked, you would have bought the
wrong thing.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: パスポートをなくさなくていいでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ パスポートをなくさ<b>なくてよかった</b>。

Two errors. `よかった` is fixed in the past - the construction has no
❌<code>いいでした</code> form, and `いい` never takes `でした` in any case. The
polite version is <code>なくてよかったです</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|会|あ}えてうれしいです。
(b) {f|会|あ}えてよかったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports emotion: meeting you makes me happy. It is the standard line on being
introduced to someone, and it says nothing about whether the meeting was in doubt.

(b) delivers a verdict with relief behind it: I'm glad the meeting happened. It
fits when there was a real chance it wouldn't - you almost missed each other, the
train was late, you had been trying to arrange it for months. Said at a first
introduction with no such backstory, it can sound as though you had been dreading
the encounter.

</details>
