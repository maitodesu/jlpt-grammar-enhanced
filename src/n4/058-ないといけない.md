# ～ないといけない

**Meaning** · have to; must - the version people actually say out loud

Third conditional, same machine. `～なくては` and `～なければ` build obligation out of
*if you don't … that won't do*; `～ないと` does the same with the `と` conditional.
What makes it worth a page of its own is frequency: in conversation this form
outnumbers the textbook ones, and it has a trick the others do not - it can stop
in the middle and still be a complete sentence.

## Formation

Take the ない-form and add `と`. Then close with `いけない`, `だめだ`, or nothing at
all.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form + といけない | {f|出|だ}す → {f|出|だ}さないといけない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + といけない | {f|調|しら}べる → {f|調|しら}べないといけない |
| Verb (する) | しないといけない | {f|準備|じゅんび}する → {f|準備|じゅんび}しないといけない |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | {f|来|こ}ないといけない | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ないといけない |
| い-adjective | ～くないと + いけない | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}くないといけない |
| な-adjective / Noun | ～でないと + いけない | {f|会員|かいいん} → {f|会員|かいいん}でないといけない |
| Polite | いけません | {f|出|だ}さないといけません |
| Past | いけなかった／いけませんでした | {f|出|だ}さないといけませんでした |
| Blunter casual | といけない → とだめ | {f|出|だ}さないとだめ |
| Ending dropped | ～ないと (＋ ね／な) | {f|出|だ}さないと。／{f|出|だ}さないとね。 |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the verb in front of `と` must be **negative**.
<code>{f|行|い}かないといけない</code> is "I have to go"; <code>{f|行|い}くといけない</code> is a
different sentence altogether - "it would be bad if I went."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
そろそろ<b>{f|帰|かえ}らないといけない</b>。
I have to head home soon.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|銀行|ぎんこう}に<b>{f|行|い}かないといけません</b>。
I have to go to the bank.
</pre>

<pre>
あ、ごみを<b>{f|出|だ}さないと</b>。
Oh - I need to put the rubbish out.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|靴|くつ}が{f|小|ちい}さくなったので、{f|新|あたら}しいのを<b>{f|買|か}わないといけない</b>。
My shoes have got too small, so I have to buy new ones.
</pre>

<pre>
このプールは{f|会員|かいいん}<b>でないといけない</b>んですか。
Do you have to be a member to use this pool?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもが{f|熱|ねつ}を{f|出|だ}したので、{f|早|はや}く{f|迎|むか}えに<b>{f|行|い}かないといけなくなりました</b>。
My child has come down with a fever, so now I have to go and collect them early.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|週末|しゅうまつ}までにこの{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}み{f|終|お}わら<b>ないといけない</b>んです。
The thing is, I have to finish this book by the weekend.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|出張|しゅっちょう}の{f|前|まえ}に、ホテルもチケットも{f|自分|じぶん}で{f|予約|よやく}し<b>ないといけません</b>。
Before next week's business trip I have to book the hotel and the tickets myself.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～ないといけない` sits in the middle of the obligation ladder and leans casual. In
plain form it is what friends, family and colleagues use with each other all day.
In `いけません` it is perfectly usable at work, though it still sounds like speech
rather than writing - a report or a notice would say `なければならない`.

The ending falls off constantly. <code>{f|急|いそ}がないと</code>, <code>{f|買|か}わないとね</code>,
<code>そろそろ{f|寝|ね}ないとな</code> are not fragments; the trailing `と` carries the
whole meaning, and adding `いけない` would sound oddly complete. Learners
consistently under-produce this and it is one of the quickest wins in sounding
natural. `ね` invites agreement, `な` is a masculine-leaning mutter to oneself.

`～ないとだめ` swaps a softer ending for a blunter one. `だめ` is flat and physical - 
"no good" - so <code>{f|出|だ}さないとだめだよ</code> presses harder than
<code>{f|出|だ}さないといけないよ</code>. Said to a child or a close friend it is normal; said
to a colleague it can sting.

Two things to file away. First, `ならない` almost never follows `と`: you will
occasionally hear <code>～ないとならない</code>, but it is uncommon and `いけない` is the
safe choice. Second, in Kansai the whole family is replaced by `～なあかん`
(<code>{f|行|い}かなあかん</code>, <code>せなあかん</code>), which you will hear constantly in the
west and in comedy, and which no textbook prepares you for.

As with every obligation form, aiming it at someone above you is strong. Reporting
your own obligation is always safe; telling your boss
<code>{f|確認|かくにん}しないといけませんよ</code> is not.

## Contrast

### ～ないといけない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same requirement, different room. `ないと` belongs in the
kitchen; `なければならない` belongs on the noticeboard.

</div>

<pre>
{f|申込書|もうしこみしょ}は{f|窓口|まどぐち}に<b>{f|提出|ていしゅつ}しなければならない</b>。
Application forms must be submitted at the counter.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|申込書|もうしこみしょ}、{f|窓口|まどぐち}に<b>{f|出|だ}さないといけないんだって</b>。
Apparently you have to hand the form in at the counter.
</pre>

The information is identical. The first is impersonal and could be printed; the
second is one person relaying it to another, complete with `んだ` and hearsay
`って`. Mixing them up is not a grammar error, it is a costume error - and it is
just as noticeable.

### ～ないと, and stopping there

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a bare `～ないと` at the end of an utterance is a finished
sentence, not a broken one. The unspoken `いけない` is understood.

</div>

<pre>
やばい、{f|電車|でんしゃ}{f|来|く}る。<b>{f|走|はし}らないと</b>。
Uh-oh, the train's coming. We've got to run.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}{f|試験|しけん}だから、{f|今日|きょう}は<b>{f|寝|ね}ないとね</b>。
I've got an exam tomorrow, so I'd better get some sleep.
</pre>

This works because Japanese lets a conditional trail off when the consequence is
obvious, and "that would be bad" is always obvious. The same trick is available to
`なきゃ` and `なくちゃ`, which is why the casual end of this family so often sounds
unfinished to a learner's ear. It is not.

### Obligation ～ないと vs the plain conditional ～ないと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if a consequence follows, `と` is just "if". Obligation only
appears when the consequence is `いけない`/`だめ`, or is left unsaid.

</div>

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|出|で}ないと</b>、{f|電車|でんしゃ}に{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わないよ。
If we don't leave soon we won't make the train.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|出|で}ないと</b>。
We've got to get going.
</pre>

Same five words, two different sentences. In the first, `と` behaves exactly as
it does in any conditional - the second half spells out what goes wrong. In the
second there is no second half, so the listener supplies the standard one and the
whole thing reads as obligation.

This is also why `ないと` cannot be used for a rule stated in the abstract. A
conditional needs a situation; a regulation with no situation attached wants
`なければならない`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|起|お}きるといけない</b>。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|起|お}きないといけない</b>。
  The obligation lives in the negative. <code>{f|起|お}きるといけない</code> means "it would
  be a problem if I got up", which is the opposite of the intended sentence.

- ❌ {f|三人以上|さんにんいじょう}<b>だといけません</b>。（intending "there must be three or more people"）→ ✅ {f|三人以上|さんにんいじょう}<b>でないといけません</b>。
  Nouns negate with `でない` before `と`. `だと` is the affirmative conditional and
  flips the meaning.

- ❌ {f|待|ま}たないといけない<b>でした</b>。 → ✅ {f|待|ま}たないといけません<b>でした</b>。／{f|待|ま}たないといけなかった。
  `いけない` is a negative predicate in its own right; make it past by conjugating it
  (`いけなかった`) or polite-past (`いけませんでした`), not by hanging `でした` on the
  plain form.

- ❌ {f|急|いそ}がないと<b>ならないです</b>。 → ✅ {f|急|いそ}がないと<b>いけません</b>。／{f|急|いそ}が<b>なければなりません</b>。
  `ならない` is at home after `なければ`/`なくては`, not after `と`, and it never takes
  `です`.

- ❌（in a written report）{f|至急|しきゅう}{f|対応|たいおう}し<b>ないといけない</b>。 → ✅ {f|至急|しきゅう}{f|対応|たいおう}し<b>なければならない</b>。
  Not ungrammatical, just spoken. In documents the `と` branch of the family reads
  as if someone forgot they were writing.

## Practice

**1.** {f|明日|あした}は{f|六時|ろくじ}の{f|電車|でんしゃ}に{f|乗|の}る。だから{f|五時|ごじ}に{f|起|お}き____いけない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|五時|ごじ}に{f|起|お}き<b>ないと</b>いけない。

The obligation form needs the negative in front of `と`: <code>{f|起|お}きない</code> +
`と` + `いけない`. The literal logic is "if I don't get up at five, that's no good."

</details>

**2.** （{f|走|はし}らないと・{f|走|はし}ると）{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わないよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|走|はし}らないと</b>{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わないよ。

Here `と` is doing ordinary conditional work, and the two halves have to line up:
not running leads to not making it. <code>{f|走|はし}ると{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わない</code>
would claim that running is what makes you late.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually, as something you would mutter on remembering it: "Ah, I have to answer that email."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あ、メールの{f|返事|へんじ}、<b>{f|書|か}かないと</b>。

The trailing `ないと` with the ending left off is the natural shape for remembering
a task out loud. <code>{f|書|か}かないといけない</code> is fine too, but the fuller form
sounds like reporting the obligation rather than reacting to it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: パスポートを{f|見|み}せるといけませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ パスポートを{f|見|み}せ<b>ないといけません</b>か。

Without the negative, the sentence asks whether showing your passport would cause a
problem. The obligation reading requires <code>{f|見|み}せない</code> before `と`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|部屋|へや}を{f|片付|かたづ}け<b>ないといけないよ</b>。
(b) {f|部屋|へや}を{f|片付|かたづ}け<b>ないとだめだよ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states the requirement and lets `よ` add a nudge. It is the ordinary spoken way
to point something out, and it can be said sympathetically - the speaker may well be
on the listener's side about the chore.

(b) replaces the neutral ending with `だめ`, which is flat and unarguable. It is what
a parent says to a child or one close friend says to another, and it carries
disapproval that (a) does not. To a colleague it would sound like being told off.

</details>
