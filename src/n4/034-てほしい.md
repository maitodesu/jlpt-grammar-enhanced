# ～てほしい

**Meaning** · I want *you* (or someone else) to do something

At N5 you learned two ways to want: `～たい` for an action you will perform
yourself, and `ほしい` for an object. `～てほしい` fills the remaining square - 
an action performed by **somebody else**. The wish is still yours, so the
first-person restriction you learned with `たい` carries over unchanged.

## Formation

Attach `ほしい` to the て-form. `ほしい` is an い-adjective and conjugates like one.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ほしい | {f|手伝|てつだ}う → {f|手伝|てつだ}ってほしい |
| Verb (negative wish) | ない-form + でほしい | {f|言|い}う → {f|言|い}わないでほしい |
| Polite | ～てほしいです | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}てほしいです |
| Past | ほしい → ほしかった | {f|分|わ}かる → {f|分|わ}かってほしかった |
| Negative | ほしい → ほしくない | {f|忘|わす}れる → {f|忘|わす}れてほしくない |
| Softened request | ～てほしいんですが／～てほしいんだけど | {f|見|み}る → {f|見|み}てほしいんですが |
| Third person's wish | ～てほしいと{f|言|い}っている／～てほしがっている | {f|直|なお}す → {f|直|なお}してほしいと{f|言|い}っています |

The person you want to act takes `に`. The wisher is the topic, and as usual is
dropped when it is you:
（{f|私|わたし}は）{f|部長|ぶちょう}<b>に</b>{f|確認|かくにん}してほしい。

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - negatives split. "I want you *not* to do it" is
<code>～ないで<b>ほしい</b></code>; "I *don't* want you to do it" is
<code>～て<b>ほしくない</b></code>. The first is a request, the second a rejection,
and learners routinely swap them.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the subject of `～てほしい` does not have to be a person.
{f|雨|あめ}がやんでほしい and {f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|時間|じかん}どおりに{f|来|き}てほしい
are both natural: you can wish an outcome onto the world. With inanimate subjects
the particle is `が`, not `に`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
ちょっと<b>{f|待|ま}ってほしい</b>。
I want you to wait a moment.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は<b>{f|晴|は}れてほしい</b>な。
I hope it clears up tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
これは{f|誰|だれ}にも<b>{f|言|い}わないでほしい</b>。
I don't want you telling anyone about this.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|息子|むすこ}にはもっと{f|外|そと}で<b>{f|遊|あそ}んでほしい</b>。
I'd like my son to play outside more.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|静|しず}かに<b>してほしい</b>んですが、{f|下|した}の{f|階|かい}の{f|人|ひと}に{f|言|い}いにくくて。
I'd like them to keep it down, but it's hard to say anything to the people downstairs.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|上司|じょうし}に<b>{f|分|わ}かってほしかった</b>ことが、うまく{f|伝|つた}えられなかった。
I couldn't get across the thing I wanted my boss to understand.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}の{f|資料|しりょう}は{f|金曜日|きんようび}までに<b>{f|出|だ}してほしい</b>んですが、{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いますか。
I'd like the meeting materials in by Friday - will you manage it?
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに{f|楽|たの}しい{f|時間|じかん}が<b>{f|終|お}わらないでほしい</b>と{f|心|こころ}から{f|思|おも}った。
I found myself wishing with all my heart that this happy time would never end.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**`～てほしい` is direct.** It states, out loud, what you want another person to
do. Between friends and family, and downward at work, that is ordinary. Aimed
upward it is too bald - you are placing a demand on someone senior - and the
polite ending does not help, because the directness is in the construction, not
in the ending. ❌{f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|確認|かくにん}してほしいです is grammatical
and will make the room uncomfortable.

The standard softeners, from lightest to heaviest:

- <code>～てほしいんだけど</code> / <code>～てほしいんですが</code> - trails off and
  leaves the ask implied. Very common.
- <code>～てほしいなあ</code> - turns the demand into a wistful wish. Friendly.
- <code>～てもらいたい</code> - the receiving frame; a shade more indirect, because
  it talks about what you would receive rather than what they should do.
- <code>～ていただきたい</code> - the humble version, and the normal business form:
  ご{f|確認|かくにん}いただきたいのですが。
- <code>～ていただけませんか</code> - a question rather than a statement, and the
  politest of the set.

The **negative wish** `～ないでほしい` is worth practising, because English "I
want you not to…" is rare and learners avoid the structure.
{f|心配|しんぱい}しないでほしい, {f|無理|むり}をしないでほしい - both are warm,
not scolding.

**Third person.** As with `たい`, you cannot flatly assert someone else's wish in
a main clause: ❌{f|妹|いもうと}は{f|父|ちち}に{f|来|き}てほしいです. Route it
through reported speech or evidence - 
{f|来|き}てほしいと{f|言|い}っています,
{f|来|き}てほしいようです. The form `～てほしがっている` exists but is much less
natural than the simple `ほしがる` for objects; most speakers avoid it.

Register: `～てほしい` is plain, `～てほしいです` polite, and neither is
gender-marked. In writing - opinion essays, editorials - it is extremely common
as a way of stating what society or government ought to do:
{f|国|くに}にはもっと{f|説明|せつめい}してほしい。

## Contrast

### ～てほしい vs ～たい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～たい` is *I* do it; `～てほしい` is *you* do it. Same wish,
different actor.

</div>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}、{f|京都|きょうと}へ<b>{f|行|い}きたい</b>。
I want to go to Kyoto next week.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}、{f|京都|きょうと}へ<b>{f|行|い}ってほしい</b>。
I want you to go to Kyoto next week.
</pre>

Both are い-adjectives and both are restricted to the speaker's own wishes. The
difference is only who does the verb - but English "want" collapses the two, so
the error is common and the resulting sentence is badly wrong rather than merely
odd.

### ～てほしい vs ～てもらいたい・～ていただきたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, rising politeness. `てほしい` states the wish
bare; `てもらいたい` routes it through receiving; `ていただきたい` does that
humbly.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}までに<b>{f|返事|へんじ}してほしい</b>。
I want an answer by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}までに<b>{f|返事|へんじ}していただきたい</b>のですが。
I would like to receive your answer by tomorrow.
</pre>

The second is what you write in an email to a client. Note that `もらいたい` and
`いただきたい` are the desiderative of `もらう`／`いただく` - verbs, so their
subject is you, the receiver, while the other person keeps `に`. The particle
pattern is identical to `てほしい`, which makes them easy to substitute.

### ～てほしい vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` tells someone to do it; `～てほしい` says you
want it done. A wish is not an instruction, and the two are not
interchangeable upward.

</div>

<pre>
ここに{f|名前|なまえ}を<b>{f|書|か}いてください</b>。
Please write your name here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここに{f|名前|なまえ}を<b>{f|書|か}いてほしい</b>んです。
I'd like you to write your name here.
</pre>

`ください` is polite in form but is still an imperative, which is why it is fine
from staff to customer and awkward from junior to superior. `～てほしいんですが`
is softer because it stops short of telling anyone to do anything - it puts the
wish on the table and lets the listener pick it up.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|友|とも}だちに{f|来|き}<b>たいです</b>。 → ✅ {f|友|とも}だちに<b>{f|来|き}てほしいです</b>。
  `たい` can only describe an action *you* perform. When somebody else does the
  verb, the wish takes `～てほしい`.

- ❌ {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|私|わたし}に{f|手伝|てつだ}っ<b>てほしいです</b>。 → ✅ {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|私|わたし}に{f|手伝|てつだ}っ<b>てほしいと{f|言|い}っています</b>。
  A third person's wish cannot be asserted flatly, exactly as with `たい`.

- ❌ {f|心配|しんぱい}し<b>てほしくない</b>。（meaning "please don't worry"）→ ✅ {f|心配|しんぱい}し<b>ないでほしい</b>。
  <code>～てほしくない</code> rejects the wish; <code>～ないでほしい</code> wishes for
  the non-action. Only the second one asks them to stop worrying.

- ❌ {f|社長|しゃちょう}、この{f|書類|しょるい}を{f|見|み}<b>てほしいです</b>。 → ✅ {f|社長|しゃちょう}、この{f|書類|しょるい}を{f|見|み}<b>ていただけませんか</b>。
  Grammatical, and far too direct upward. The humble request form is what the
  situation calls for.

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}<b>に</b>やんでほしい。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}<b>が</b>やんでほしい。
  `に` marks a person you are asking to act. An inanimate subject takes `が`.

## Practice

**1.** うるさいので、もう{f|少|すこ}し{f|静|しず}かに{f|話|はな}し____ほしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|静|しず}かに{f|話|はな}し<b>て</b>ほしいです。

`ほしい` attaches to the て-form when the wish is about somebody else's action.
Note that the polite `です` sits on `ほしい`, because <code>ほしい</code> is an
い-adjective and carries the sentence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|明日|あした}の{f|会議|かいぎ}に（{f|出|で}てほしいです・ご{f|出席|しゅっせき}いただけませんか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|明日|あした}の{f|会議|かいぎ}に<b>ご{f|出席|しゅっせき}いただけませんか</b>。

Stating a wish about a superior's behaviour is too direct, however polite the
ending. The humble potential turns the same content into a request that leaves the
department head free to say no.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I don't want you to worry about me."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|私|わたし}のことは<b>{f|心配|しんぱい}しないでほしい</b>。

The wish is for a *non-action*, so it takes <code>～ないでほしい</code>.
<code>{f|心配|しんぱい}してほしくない</code> would be understood, but it reads as
rejecting the worry rather than asking for it to stop.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|弟|おとうと}は{f|母|はは}に{f|新|あたら}しいゲームを{f|買|か}ってほしいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|弟|おとうと}は{f|母|はは}に{f|新|あたら}しいゲームを{f|買|か}って<b>ほしがっています</b>。
（more natural: ✅ …{f|買|か}って<b>ほしいと{f|言|い}っています</b>。）

`ほしい` is an い-adjective of desire, and Japanese does not let you assert another
person's inner state directly in a plain main clause. Report it, or mark it as
observed.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|明日|あした}までに{f|返事|へんじ}をください。
(b) {f|明日|あした}までに{f|返事|へんじ}してほしいんですが。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an instruction. `ください` is polite in form but imperative in force, so it
suits a situation where you are entitled to ask - a shop to a customer, a teacher
to a class, a manager to a team.

(b) states a need and stops. Because it never tells the listener to act, it leaves
them room to negotiate the deadline, and the trailing `んですが` invites them to
respond. It is what you use sideways or upward, where issuing instructions would
be out of place.

</details>
