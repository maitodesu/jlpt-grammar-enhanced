# ～なくちゃ・～なきゃ

**Meaning** · gotta - the same obligation, squeezed down for speech

These are not new grammar. `なくちゃ` is `なくては` with the syllables worn off, and
`なきゃ` is `なければ` with the same treatment. What they add is register: they are
what obligation sounds like in an actual conversation, and they usually appear with
the `いけない`/`ならない` tail chopped off entirely. Learners are blindsided by
`なきゃ` because no textbook sentence ever contains it and every real one does.

## Formation

Japanese contracts `ては` to `ちゃ` everywhere ({f|見|み}ては → {f|見|み}ちゃ), and
`ければ` to `きゃ`. Apply that to the two obligation conditionals and you get these.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form → なくちゃ／なきゃ | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}かなくちゃ／{f|行|い}かなきゃ |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form → なくちゃ／なきゃ | {f|起|お}きる → {f|起|お}きなくちゃ／{f|起|お}きなきゃ |
| Verb (する) | しなくちゃ／しなきゃ | {f|洗濯|せんたく}する → {f|洗濯|せんたく}しなきゃ |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | {f|来|こ}なくちゃ／{f|来|こ}なきゃ | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}なきゃ |
| い-adjective | ～くなくちゃ／～くなきゃ | {f|新|あたら}しい → {f|新|あたら}しくなきゃ |
| な-adjective / Noun | ～じゃなくちゃ／～じゃなきゃ | {f|無料|むりょう} → {f|無料|むりょう}じゃなきゃ |
| With the tail kept | ＋ いけない／だめ | {f|行|い}かなきゃいけない／{f|行|い}かなきゃだめ |
| With the tail dropped | nothing after it | {f|行|い}かなきゃ。／{f|行|い}かなくちゃ。 |
| Emphatic variant | なくちゃ → なくっちゃ | {f|行|い}かなくっちゃ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two contractions come from different parents and do not
mix. `なくては` → <b>なくちゃ</b>; `なければ` → <b>なきゃ</b>. There is no ❌`なくきゃ` and
no ❌`なけちゃ`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
もう<b>{f|寝|ね}なきゃ</b>。
I've got to go to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
あ、{f|電気|でんき}{f|消|け}さ<b>なきゃ</b>。
Oh - I need to turn the light off.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}{f|早|はや}いから、そろそろ<b>{f|帰|かえ}らなくちゃ</b>。
I've got an early start, so I should be heading home.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|宿題|しゅくだい}、{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう}に<b>{f|終|お}わらせなきゃいけない</b>んだ。
The thing is, I've got to get the homework finished today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|冷蔵庫|れいぞうこ}が{f|空|から}っぽだ。{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に<b>{f|行|い}かなきゃ</b>。
The fridge is empty. I need to go shopping.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|傘|かさ}、{f|持|も}って<b>{f|行|い}かなくちゃだめだよ</b>。{f|午後|ごご}から{f|雨|あめ}だって。
You've got to take an umbrella - apparently it's raining from the afternoon.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}{f|友|とも}だちの{f|結婚式|けっこんしき}だから、{f|新|あたら}しい{f|服|ふく}を<b>{f|買|か}わなきゃ</b>いけないなあ。
My friend's wedding is next week, so I'm going to have to buy something new to wear.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|部屋|へや}がこんなに{f|散|ち}らかっているのに、<b>{f|片付|かたづ}けなくちゃ</b>と{f|思|おも}うだけで{f|何|なに}もしていない。
The room is in this state and all I do is think I ought to tidy it, without ever actually starting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Casual. That is the whole point, and it is also the whole restriction. `なくちゃ`
and `なきゃ` belong in speech among friends, family and close colleagues, and in
writing only where writing imitates speech - messages, chat, blogs, dialogue in
manga and novels. They do not appear in reports, emails to clients, exam answers
or announcements.

They are **not** sloppy or uneducated, and they are not gender-marked: adults of
every age and both sexes use them constantly. What they are is *unguarded*. Saying
<code>{f|帰|かえ}らなきゃ</code> lets the listener see you making a decision, which is
exactly right with a friend and wrong in front of a customer.

`なきゃ` is the more frequent of the two in everyday Tokyo speech. `なくちゃ` is a
touch lighter and rounder, and it turns up often when someone is talking to a
child or being deliberately breezy; a few speakers hear it as slightly cuter than
`なきゃ`, but nobody would call it a different level of politeness. The stretched
`なくっちゃ` adds emphasis - <code>これは{f|見|み}なくっちゃ</code>「this I've *got* to
see」.

The tail is normally dropped. When it is kept, it stays in plain form:
`なきゃいけない`, `なきゃだめ`, and in very casual Tokyo speech `なきゃなんない` (from
`ならない`). Attaching a polite tail is the classic learner error - you cannot
half-contract a sentence, and ❌<code>{f|行|い}かなきゃいけません</code> mixes two
registers inside one predicate. If you need politeness, expand the whole thing back
out to `なければなりません`.

One more thing that catches people out in listening: because the ending is missing,
these forms often end an utterance on a rising or trailing intonation and sound
unfinished. <code>あ、{f|洗濯|せんたく}しなきゃ…</code> is a complete thought. Do not wait
for the rest of it.

## Contrast

### ～なきゃ vs ～なくちゃ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same word twice over. Choose by ear, not by meaning;
`なきゃ` is the more common default.

</div>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>{f|出|で}なきゃ</b>。
I'd better get going.
</pre>

<pre>
そろそろ<b>{f|出|で}なくちゃ</b>。
I'd better get going.
</pre>

There is no difference of meaning, obligation strength or politeness to report, and
a page that invented one would be lying. The only observable tendencies are that
`なきゃ` is heard more often overall, `なくちゃ` sounds slightly softer and is common
in speech directed at children, and only `なくちゃ` has the emphatic stretched form
`なくっちゃ`.

### ～なきゃ vs ～ないと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are casual spoken obligation. `ないと` is neutral and
usable slightly further up the politeness scale; `なきゃ` is more informal and more
self-directed.

</div>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}の{f|資料|しりょう}、{f|作|つく}ら<b>ないと</b>いけませんね。
We'll need to put the meeting materials together, won't we.
</pre>

<pre>
うわ、{f|資料|しりょう}{f|作|つく}ら<b>なきゃ</b>。
Ugh - I've got to make the handouts.
</pre>

`ないと` survives being made polite: <code>{f|行|い}かないといけません</code> is fine at
work. `なきゃ` does not. And `なきゃ` leans towards things the speaker is telling
*themselves*, which is why it so often follows あ or うわ and precedes nothing at
all.

### ～なきゃ as obligation vs ～なきゃ as plain "unless"

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なきゃ` is just contracted `なければ`. If a consequence follows
it, it is an ordinary conditional and has nothing to do with obligation.

</div>

<pre>
{f|自分|じぶん}で<b>{f|見|み}なきゃ</b>わからないよ。
You won't know unless you look for yourself.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|自分|じぶん}で<b>{f|見|み}なきゃ</b>。
I've got to go and look for myself.
</pre>

The first is "if you don't look, you won't understand" - the `いけない` is not
implied and never was. The second has nothing after it, so the listener fills in
the missing "that won't do" and hears an obligation.

The same holds for `なくちゃ`, and it explains a shape you will meet often:
<code>{f|安|やす}くなきゃ{f|買|か}わない</code>「I won't buy it unless it's cheap」,
<code>{f|君|きみ}じゃなきゃだめだ</code>「it has to be you」.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|行|い}か<b>なきゃです</b>。 → ✅ {f|行|い}か<b>なきゃ</b>。／{f|行|い}か<b>なければなりません</b>。
  There is no polite version of a contraction. `です` cannot be bolted onto
  `なきゃ`; if you need politeness, expand the form.

- ❌ {f|行|い}く<b>なきゃ</b> → ✅ {f|行|い}か<b>なきゃ</b>
  The contraction still starts from the ない-form. Build <code>{f|行|い}かない</code>
  first, then squeeze.

- ❌ {f|急|いそ}が<b>なくきゃ</b> → ✅ {f|急|いそ}が<b>なくちゃ</b>／{f|急|いそ}が<b>なきゃ</b>
  The two contractions have different parents and cannot be blended.
  `なくては`→`なくちゃ`, `なければ`→`なきゃ`.

- ❌ {f|会員|かいいん}<b>だなきゃ</b>だめです。 → ✅ {f|会員|かいいん}<b>じゃなきゃ</b>だめだよ。
  Nouns negate to `じゃない`, so the contracted conditional is `じゃなきゃ`. Note the
  ending changed too - `だめです` after `じゃなきゃ` is a register mismatch.

- ❌（in an email to a client）{f|明日|あした}までに{f|送|おく}ら<b>なきゃ</b>いけません。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}までに{f|送|おく}ら<b>なければなりません</b>。
  Contractions do not belong in written business Japanese, however polite the tail
  you attach to them.

## Practice

**1.** やばい、{f|電車|でんしゃ}{f|行|い}っちゃう。{f|走|はし}ら____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|走|はし}ら<b>なきゃ</b>。（also fine: {f|走|はし}ら<b>なくちゃ</b>。）

The situation is urgent, spoken and self-directed, which is the natural home of the
contracted forms with the ending dropped. Anything longer would sound like the
speaker had time to think.

</details>

**2.** {f|部長|ぶちょう}に（{f|報告|ほうこく}しなきゃいけません・{f|報告|ほうこく}しなければなりません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|報告|ほうこく}し<b>なければなりません</b>。

`なきゃ` is a casual contraction and `いけません` is a polite ending; putting them in
one predicate mixes registers inside a single word. Pick one level - fully
contracted for speech among friends, fully expanded when you are being polite.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as something you say to yourself on remembering it: "Oh no, I have to return the library books today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あ、{f|今日|きょう}{f|図書館|としょかん}の{f|本|ほん}{f|返|かえ}さ<b>なきゃ</b>。

Muttered self-obligation drops the `いけない` and drops the particles too. The
interjection at the front is part of the pattern: these forms almost always follow
a moment of realisation.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|映画|えいが}、{f|絶対|ぜったい}{f|見|み}るなきゃ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|映画|えいが}、{f|絶対|ぜったい}{f|見|み}<b>なきゃ</b>。（emphatic: {f|見|み}<b>なくっちゃ</b>。）

`なきゃ` replaces `なければ`, which is built on the ない-form - <code>{f|見|み}ない</code> →
<code>{f|見|み}なければ</code> → <code>{f|見|み}なきゃ</code>. The dictionary form never appears in
front of it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|早|はや}く<b>{f|予約|よやく}しなきゃ</b>{f|席|せき}がなくなるよ。
(b) {f|早|はや}く<b>{f|予約|よやく}しなきゃ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is not an obligation sentence at all. `なきゃ` here is simply contracted
`なければ` working as an ordinary conditional, and the second half states the
consequence: book early, or the seats will be gone.

(b) leaves the consequence out. Because the missing half is understood to be "that
won't do", the whole utterance is heard as the speaker realising they have to act.
The contraction is identical in both; what decides the reading is whether anything
follows it.

</details>
