# でも

**Meaning** · ～ or something; ～ or the like - names one thing as a token example

Attach `でも` to a noun in a suggestion and the noun stops being a demand. You put
one option on the table as a representative of a whole class of acceptable
options, and the listener is left free to counter with something else. It is the
single most useful politeness trick in casual invitations.

## Formation

`でも` follows the noun. It replaces `が` and `を`, and stacks on top of `に`,
`へ` and `と`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + でも | お{f|茶|ちゃ} → お{f|茶|ちゃ}でも |
| Noun taking を | を is **dropped** | ケーキでも{f|食|た}べよう |
| Noun taking が | が is **dropped** | {f|音楽|おんがく}でもあるといいね |
| Noun taking に／へ／と | に／へ／と + でも | {f|海|うみ}にでも{f|行|い}こう |
| Question word | いつ／どこ／だれ／なに + でも | いつでも／だれでも（＝any） |
| Typical predicates | ～でも + どう／いかが／～ませんか／～よう | お{f|茶|ちゃ}でもいかがですか |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this `でも` lives in sentences about **what might happen**:
invitations, suggestions, offers, plans, volitional forms. It cannot report a
completed fact. ❌{f|昨日|きのう}コーヒーでも{f|飲|の}みました is not a sentence you
can rescue with context.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - three different things share these two kana. This page is about the
one that follows a **noun** in a suggestion. There is also the concessive
`～ても／でも` that follows a verb or an adjective
（{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っても, "even if it rains"）, and the sentence-initial
`でも` meaning "but". Same shape, unrelated jobs.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|少|すこ}し{f|休|やす}んで、お{f|茶|ちゃ}<b>でも</b>{f|飲|の}みませんか。
Let's take a break - shall we have some tea or something?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|映画|えいが}<b>でも</b>{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}こうか。
Shall we go and see a film or something?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|週末|しゅうまつ}は{f|公園|こうえん}に<b>でも</b>{f|行|い}きましょうか。
Shall we go to the park or somewhere at the weekend?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|暇|ひま}なら、{f|本|ほん}<b>でも</b>{f|読|よ}んだらどう?
If you're free, why not read a book or something?
</pre>

<pre>
ケーキ<b>でも</b>{f|買|か}って{f|帰|かえ}ろうかな。
Maybe I'll pick up a cake or something on the way home.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|悩|なや}まないで、{f|先生|せんせい}に<b>でも</b>{f|相談|そうだん}してみたらどうですか。
Don't sit with it on your own - why not talk to your teacher or somebody?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|疲|つか}れたでしょう。{f|冷|つめ}たいもの<b>でも</b>{f|飲|の}んで、{f|少|すこ}し{f|休|やす}んでください。
You must be tired. Have something cold to drink and take a rest.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|土曜日|どようび}、{f|時間|じかん}があったら{f|食事|しょくじ}に<b>でも</b>{f|行|い}きませんか。
If you have time next Saturday, would you like to go out for a meal or something?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The whole point of this `でも` is **not committing the listener**. Compare the two
invitations:

- お{f|茶|ちゃ}を{f|飲|の}みませんか - "would you like to drink tea?" The listener
  has to accept tea or refuse the invitation.
- お{f|茶|ちゃ}でも{f|飲|の}みませんか - "shall we have tea or something?" The
  listener can say "sure, but I'd rather have juice" without refusing anything.

That softness makes it standard in casual and semi-polite invitations at every
level of formality: ～でも{f|食|た}べようか between friends,
～でもいかがですか to a guest. It is register-neutral in itself; the politeness is
carried by the ending.

There is one situation where it backfires. If the other person has just told you
exactly what they want, offering it back with `でも` treats their request as
arbitrary. 「コーヒーが{f|飲|の}みたい」「じゃあコーヒーでも{f|買|か}ってくるね」
reads as mildly dismissive - they asked for coffee, not for coffee-or-whatever.
Drop the `でも` and use `を`.

The related **question word + でも** pattern - いつでも, どこでも, だれでも,
{f|何|なん}でも - means "any at all", and it is a different construction with the
same particle: だれでもできる{f|仕事|しごと}です, "it's a job anyone can do".
Worth knowing here because learners meet both in the same week and mix them.

Finally, `でも` is not the only softener of its kind. `とか` does a similar job
（{f|明日|あした}とかどう?）but is more casual and attaches happily to times and
dates; `でも` sits more comfortably with the thing being consumed or done.

## Contrast

### ～でも vs ～を

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` names the thing you mean. `でも` names one thing you would
accept.

</div>

<pre>
{f|昼|ひる}ごはんにそば<b>を</b>{f|食|た}べましょう。
Let's have soba for lunch.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昼|ひる}ごはんにそば<b>でも</b>{f|食|た}べましょう。
Let's have soba or something for lunch.
</pre>

The first has decided; the second is thinking aloud. Note that `を` disappears
entirely when `でも` moves in - ❌そばをでも is impossible, because both are
fighting for the same slot.

### Noun + でも vs question word + でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - noun + `でも` offers an example. Question word + `でも` sweeps
in the whole category: "any at all".

</div>

<pre>
{f|飲|の}み{f|物|もの}<b>でも</b>{f|買|か}ってきます。
I'll go and buy some drinks or something.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|飲|の}み{f|物|もの}なら<b>{f|何|なん}でも</b>いいです。
As for drinks, anything at all is fine.
</pre>

The first suggests one purchase among several possible ones; the second refuses to
narrow anything down. The same contrast runs through the family: どこでも
"anywhere", いつでも "any time", だれでも "anyone".

### ～でも vs ～ても・でも（even if）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - check what comes **before** it. A noun means "or something".
A verb or adjective stem means "even if".

</div>

<pre>
{f|散歩|さんぽ}に<b>でも</b>{f|行|い}きませんか。
Shall we go for a walk or something?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っ<b>ても</b>{f|散歩|さんぽ}に{f|行|い}きます。
I'm going for a walk even if it rains.
</pre>

They are not related in meaning at all, and the second one is not really `でも` - 
it is the て-form plus `も`, which turns into `でも` only after verbs whose
て-form ends in `で`（{f|読|よ}んでも）. And a `でも` at the very front of a
sentence is the conjunction "but": でも、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|忙|いそが}しいです。

## Common mistakes

- ❌ ケーキ<b>を</b>でも{f|食|た}べましょう。 → ✅ ケーキ<b>でも</b>{f|食|た}べましょう。
  `でも` takes the place of `を` and `が`. Only `に`, `へ` and `と` survive
  alongside it.

- ❌ {f|昨日|きのう}コーヒー<b>でも</b>{f|飲|の}みました。 → ✅ {f|昨日|きのう}コーヒー<b>を</b>{f|飲|の}みました。
  You cannot be vague about what you already did. This `でも` only works where the
  action is still open - suggestions, offers, plans.

- ❌ 「{f|紅茶|こうちゃ}がほしいです。」「じゃ、{f|紅茶|こうちゃ}<b>でも</b>{f|入|い}れますね。」 → ✅ 「じゃ、{f|紅茶|こうちゃ}<b>を</b>{f|入|い}れますね。」
  When the other person has named exactly what they want, `でも` says you were not
  really listening.

- ❌ でも、{f|音楽|おんがく}{f|聞|き}きませんか。（meaning "music or something"） → ✅ {f|音楽|おんがく}<b>でも</b>{f|聞|き}きませんか。
  At the head of a sentence `でも` is the conjunction "but". The softening `でも`
  has to sit on the noun.

## Practice

**1.** {f|雨|あめ}でどこにも{f|行|い}けませんね。うちでDVD____{f|見|み}ましょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

うちでDVD<b>でも</b>{f|見|み}ましょうか。

The speaker is proposing a way to spend the afternoon, not insisting on DVDs. With
`でも` the other person can suggest a board game instead without turning anything
down, which is exactly what makes the invitation comfortable.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
「{f|何|なに}が{f|食|た}べたい?」「うーん、ラーメン（でも・が）いいかな。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「うーん、ラーメン<b>でも</b>いいかな。」

You have been asked to choose and you are staying deliberately loose - ramen is a
suggestion, not a decision. Answering ラーメン<b>が</b>いい would be a firm choice,
which is fine too, but it is a different reply: it commits you.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Shall we go to a cafe or somewhere?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

カフェに<b>でも</b>{f|行|い}きませんか。

`に` marks the destination and survives, with `でも` stacking behind it. Note the
word order - ❌でもにカフェ and ❌カフェでもに are both impossible; the case
particle comes first and `でも` follows.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|先週|せんしゅう}の{f|日曜日|にちようび}、{f|友|とも}だちと{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}でも{f|行|い}きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|先週|せんしゅう}の{f|日曜日|にちようび}、{f|友|とも}だちと{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に{f|行|い}きました。

Two problems, one cause. The sentence reports something that already happened, so
the vague `でも` has nothing to be vague about; and with {f|行|い}く the
destination needs `に`. If you want the softened version, move it into the future:
{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}にでも{f|行|い}きませんか.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|山田|やまだ}さんに{f|聞|き}いてみます。
(b) {f|山田|やまだ}さんに<b>でも</b>{f|聞|き}いてみます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says you will ask Yamada, because Yamada is the person who would know.

(b) presents Yamada as one plausible person among several - "I'll ask Yamada or
somebody". It sounds less certain and less planned, which can be a relaxed, modest
way to talk about your own next step. Said in front of Yamada, though, it is a
little unflattering: it implies he was not the obvious choice.

</details>
