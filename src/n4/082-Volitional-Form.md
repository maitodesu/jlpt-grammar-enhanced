# Volitional form - ～(よ)う

**Meaning** · let's do it; I'll do it - the form a verb wears when it carries a
*will*

The volitional is the plain-form counterpart of `ましょう`. It does two jobs that
English keeps apart: proposing something to the people you are with ("let's go"),
and announcing a decision to yourself ("right - I'll go"). It is also the base
that a whole family of N4 patterns is built on, so the table below is worth more
than the page around it: <code>～(よ)うと{f|思|おも}う</code>, <code>～(よ)うとする</code> and
<code>～(よ)うか</code> all need this form and nothing else will do.

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** ({f|五段|ごだん} / godan /
Group 1), **る-verbs** ({f|一段|いちだん} / ichidan / Group 2), and the two
irregulars する and {f|来|く}る. This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**
throughout.

</div>

## Formation

う-verbs swap the final `-u` vowel for `-o` and add `う`; る-verbs drop `る` and
add `よう`. That is the whole rule, and unlike the て-form there are no sound
changes to memorise.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb, く) | ～く → ～こう | {f|書|か}く → {f|書|か}こう |
| Verb (う-verb, ぐ) | ～ぐ → ～ごう | {f|泳|およ}ぐ → {f|泳|およ}ごう |
| Verb (う-verb, す) | ～す → ～そう | {f|話|はな}す → {f|話|はな}そう |
| Verb (う-verb, つ) | ～つ → ～とう | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}とう |
| Verb (う-verb, ぬ) | ～ぬ → ～のう | {f|死|し}ぬ → {f|死|し}のう |
| Verb (う-verb, ぶ) | ～ぶ → ～ぼう | {f|遊|あそ}ぶ → {f|遊|あそ}ぼう |
| Verb (う-verb, む) | ～む → ～もう | {f|飲|の}む → {f|飲|の}もう |
| Verb (う-verb, る) | ～る → ～ろう | {f|帰|かえ}る → {f|帰|かえ}ろう |
| Verb (う-verb, う) | ～う → ～<b>お</b>う | {f|買|か}う → {f|買|か}おう |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る + よう | {f|食|た}べる → {f|食|た}べよう |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る + よう | {f|見|み}る → {f|見|み}よう |
| Verb (する) | する → しよう | {f|散歩|さんぽ}する → {f|散歩|さんぽ}しよう |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}よう | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}よう |
| Polite equivalent | ます-stem + ましょう | {f|行|い}きます → {f|行|い}きましょう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two traps are う-verbs whose dictionary form ends in `う`,
and る-verbs that look like う-verbs. <code>{f|買|か}う</code> gives
<code>{f|買|か}おう</code>, never ❌<code>{f|買|か}うう</code>; <code>{f|帰|かえ}る</code> is a
う-verb, so <code>{f|帰|かえ}ろう</code>, not ❌<code>{f|帰|かえ}よう</code>.

</div>

Things that plug straight into this form:

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Checking a proposal | ～(よ)う + か | {f|座|すわ}ろうか |
| Pressing a proposal | ～(よ)う + よ | {f|座|すわ}ろうよ |
| Stating an intention | ～(よ)う + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|座|すわ}ろうと{f|思|おも}う |
| Attempting / being about to | ～(よ)う + とする | {f|座|すわ}ろうとする |
| Talking to yourself | ～(よ)う + っと（casual） | {f|座|すわ}ろうっと |

<div class="note">

**Note** - verbs that describe things nobody chooses ({f|降|ふ}る, ある, できる,
{f|分|わ}かる) have a volitional form on paper, but it is not used for prediction in
modern spoken Japanese. "It'll probably rain" is <code>{f|降|ふ}るでしょう</code> or
<code>{f|降|ふ}るだろう</code>.

</div>

## Nuance & register

The plain volitional is **casual**. Used as a proposal it belongs with friends,
family, classmates and people below you at work; said to a customer or a
{f|部長|ぶちょう} it is startling. Upward, use `ましょう` - and even `ましょう` can
presume too much, so <code>～ましょうか</code> ("shall I / shall we?") and
<code>～ませんか</code> ("won't you?") are the genuinely safe openers.

Intonation splits the two main uses more reliably than grammar does. Flat or
falling, <code>{f|行|い}こう</code> is a decision you are handing to the group. With
`か` on the end, <code>{f|行|い}こうか</code> hands the decision back to them. Adding
`よ` - <code>{f|行|い}こうよ</code> - pushes, and can sound like coaxing or whining
depending on who is speaking.

Two colloquial tails worth recognising:

- **～(よ)うっと** is a private mutter, not a proposal. Nobody is being invited:
  「あー、もう{f|寝|ね}ようっと。」 It sounds relaxed and slightly childlike, and it
  is common on social media.
- **～(よ)うか{f|迷|まよ}っている** ("I can't decide whether to…") is the standard
  way to say you are torn: {f|買|か}おうか{f|迷|まよ}っています。

In writing, the volitional turns up as the essayist's "let us":
{f|次|つぎ}に、この{f|問題|もんだい}について{f|考|かんが}えよう。 That is a register
away from the friendly spoken use, but it is the same form. The stiff literary
<code>～であろう</code> ("it may well be that…") is something to recognise in print
and never to say out loud.

`ましょう` has one job the plain form cannot do: it is polite. It cannot, however,
be embedded - <code>～ましょうと{f|思|おも}う</code> is not Japanese. Whenever the
volitional is followed by something, it goes in plain.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)う vs ～ましょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different politeness. `ましょう` is the polite
sentence-final form; `～(よ)う` is the plain one, and it is the only one that can
have something attached after it.

</div>

<pre>
みんなで<b>{f|歌|うた}おう</b>。
Let's all sing.
</pre>

<pre>
みんなで<b>{f|歌|うた}いましょう</b>。
Let's all sing. (polite / addressed to a group you are leading)
</pre>

The moment another clause follows, politeness moves to the end of the sentence
and the volitional reverts to plain:

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}から<b>{f|走|はし}ろう</b>と{f|思|おも}っています。
I'm thinking of taking up running from next week.
</pre>

❌<code>{f|走|はし}りましょうと{f|思|おも}っています</code> is impossible. Politeness is
marked once, at the end.

### ～(よ)う vs ～ませんか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～(よ)う` and `ましょう` assume the answer is yes.
`～ませんか` genuinely asks, so it is the polite way to invite someone who is free
to refuse.

</div>

<pre>
{f|昼|ひる}ごはん、いっしょに<b>{f|食|た}べよう</b>。
Let's have lunch together. (we're already doing this)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昼|ひる}ごはん、いっしょに<b>{f|食|た}べませんか</b>。
Would you like to have lunch together? (a real invitation)
</pre>

Inviting someone for the first time, `ませんか` is almost always the better choice;
`ましょう` after they have agreed is what settles the details. A useful ordering
from most to least presumptuous: <code>{f|行|い}こう</code> →
<code>{f|行|い}きましょう</code> → <code>{f|行|い}きましょうか</code> →
<code>{f|行|い}きませんか</code>. Treat that as a tendency, not a rulebook - tone of
voice moves any of them.

### ～(よ)う vs the plain non-past

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English "will" is usually **not** the volitional. A plain
future statement is just the dictionary form or `ます`; the volitional adds the
feeling of a will being exercised at this moment.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}、{f|病院|びょういん}へ<b>{f|行|い}きます</b>。
I'm going to the hospital tomorrow. (a fact about my schedule)
</pre>

<pre>
やっぱり{f|明日|あした}、{f|病院|びょういん}へ<b>{f|行|い}こう</b>。
You know what - I'll go to the hospital tomorrow. (deciding as I speak)
</pre>

This is why the volitional cannot report the weather, someone else's plans, or
anything that happens without a decision. Those all take the plain form, with
`でしょう` or <code>と{f|思|おも}います</code> if you need to hedge.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|手伝|てつだ}**うよう** → ✅ {f|手伝|てつだ}**おう**
  A う-verb ending in `う` becomes `おう`, not `うよう`. The `よう` ending belongs
  to る-verbs only.

- ❌ {f|食|た}べ**ろう** → ✅ {f|食|た}べ**よう**
  <code>{f|食|た}べる</code> is a る-verb: drop `る`, add `よう`. `ろう` would be the
  う-verb pattern, which is why <code>{f|帰|かえ}る</code> - a う-verb despite its
  looks - really is <code>{f|帰|かえ}ろう</code>.

- ❌ {f|来|く}る → きよう → ✅ {f|来|く}る → **こよう**
  The irregular shifts to the `こ` stem, the same one you already use for
  <code>{f|来|こ}ない</code>. Reading the written form <code>{f|来|こ}よう</code> as *kiyou* is a very common
  slip.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|降|ふ}**ろう**。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|降|ふ}る**でしょう**。
  Snow has no intentions. The volitional needs a subject that can choose.

- ❌ {f|課長|かちょう}、コーヒーを{f|飲|の}**もう**。 → ✅ {f|課長|かちょう}、コーヒーでもいかがですか。
  Grammatically fine, socially wrong. Do not propose things to a superior in the
  plain volitional.

## Practice

**1.** {f|荷物|にもつ}が{f|重|おも}いね。タクシーで{f|帰|かえ}____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

タクシーで{f|帰|かえ}<b>ろう</b>。

<code>{f|帰|かえ}る</code> is a う-verb, so the final `る` becomes `ろ` and takes `う`.
The `よう` ending would only be right if this were a る-verb.

</details>

**2.** {f|部長|ぶちょう}に{f|向|む}かって：「そろそろ（{f|出|で}かけよう・{f|出|で}かけましょう）。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そろそろ<b>{f|出|で}かけましょう</b>。

The plain volitional is for friends and juniors. Speaking to a
{f|部長|ぶちょう} you need `ましょう`, and if you want to leave him room to say no,
<code>{f|出|で}かけましょうか</code> is softer still.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "It's cold - shall we go inside?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|寒|さむ}いね。{f|中|なか}に<b>{f|入|はい}ろうか</b>。

<code>{f|入|はい}る</code> is a う-verb → <code>{f|入|はい}ろう</code>, and the `か` turns
a flat proposal into a check with the other person. Without `か` it would be "come
on, inside" rather than "shall we?".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|明日|あした}{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}ましょうと{f|思|おも}っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|明日|あした}{f|映画|えいが}を<b>{f|見|み}よう</b>と{f|思|おも}っています。

`ましょう` cannot be embedded in a larger sentence. When something follows the
volitional it must be plain, and the politeness is carried by
<code>{f|思|おも}っています</code> at the end.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}よう。
(b) {f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}よう**か**。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a decision presented as already taken - you are telling the group what is
happening, in a friendly way. It also works as a resolution said to yourself.

(b) hands the choice back: "shall we watch a film?" The `か` makes it a genuine
question, so the other person can say no without pushing back against you. In a
group where you are not the one who decides, (b) is the safer sentence.

</details>
