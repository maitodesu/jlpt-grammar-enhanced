# ～てくれる

**Meaning** · someone does something for me, or for my side - and I am grateful for it

`～てくれる` is the action counterpart of the `くれる` you met at N5. Someone else
performs the verb, and the good of it arrives at the speaker's うち. Unlike
`～てあげる`, this one is not risky to say: marking a favour that came *to* you is
simply politeness, and leaving it off can sound cold.

## Formation

Attach `くれる` to the て-form. The doer is the subject; the beneficiary - usually
<code>{f|私|わたし}</code> - is normally left unsaid, because the verb has already said it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + くれる | {f|手伝|てつだ}う → {f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれる |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + くれます | {f|送|おく}る → {f|送|おく}ってくれました |
| Verb (negative) | て-form + くれない | {f|返事|へんじ}する → {f|返事|へんじ}してくれない |
| From someone above you (honorific) | て-form + くださる → くださいます | {f|直|なお}す → {f|直|なお}してくださいました |
| Casual request | て-form + くれない？／くれる？ | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}ってくれない? |

The sentence frame is <code>Aが（{f|私|わたし}に）～てくれる</code>. As with plain
`くれる`, the particle on the beneficiary comes from the underlying verb - 
{f|私|わたし}に{f|教|おし}えてくれる but {f|私|わたし}を{f|待|ま}ってくれる.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てくれる` can never have the speaker as the doer. There is
no ❌<code>{f|私|わたし}が{f|田中|たなか}さんに{f|貸|か}してくれた</code>. Nothing can move
toward you while you are the one doing it.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the ます-form of `くださる` is irregular: <b>くださいます</b>, not
❌くださります. And the `ください` you have used since your first week is nothing
more than the imperative of this verb - <code>{f|待|ま}ってください</code> is literally
"give me the favour of waiting".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}が{f|駅|えき}まで<b>{f|送|おく}ってくれました</b>。
My dad drove me to the station.
</pre>

<pre>
だれが{f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|閉|し}めてくれた</b>の?
Who closed the window for me?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|先生|せんせい}が{f|作文|さくぶん}を<b>{f|直|なお}してくださいました</b>。
My teacher kindly corrected my essay.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|同|おな}じ{f|課|か}の{f|人|ひと}が{f|仕事|しごと}を<b>{f|代|か}わってくれて</b>、{f|助|たす}かりました。
Someone in my section covered my shift, which was a real help.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

My wife makes me a packed lunch every morning, so lunch is never a problem.
</pre>

<pre>

When my son had a fever, our neighbour was kind enough to drive us to the hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|忙|いそが}しいのに{f|最後|さいご}まで{f|話|はなし}を<b>{f|聞|き}いてくれて</b>、{f|本当|ほんとう}にありがとう。
Thank you so much for hearing me out to the end even though you were busy.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`～てくれる` is **gratitude built into the grammar**. Japanese has no polite way to
report a favour received without it. If a colleague fixes your spreadsheet and you
tell someone
{f|田中|たなか}さんが{f|直|なお}しました, you have described an event; add
`くれました` and you have acknowledged a kindness. Omitting it where it belongs
sounds ungrateful in a way that has no English equivalent, so learners should err
toward using it.

The single most useful frame built on it is
<code>～てくれて、ありがとう</code> - the standard way to thank someone for an
**action**（{f|来|き}てくれてありがとう）, as opposed to
<code>～をありがとう</code> for a thing. Polite version:
<code>～てくださって、ありがとうございます</code>. Also common:
<code>～てくれて{f|助|たす}かりました</code>, "you saved me."

The **negative** is where the emotion flips. `～てくれない` / `～てくれなかった`
is a complaint, not a neutral report: it says the favour was owed and did not
arrive. {f|誰|だれ}も{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれなかった means "nobody would help me",
with the resentment audible. Compare the bare
{f|誰|だれ}も{f|手伝|てつだ}いませんでした, which is a flat statement of fact.

In the **question** form `～てくれない?` the same negative becomes a soft casual
request - the first rung of the request ladder that runs on up through
`～てくれませんか`, `～てもらえませんか` and `～ていただけませんか`.

Register notes: `くれる` is plain, `くれます` polite, `くださる` honorific.
`くださる` is used for a superior's kindness toward you and is completely standard
in business speech. The plain imperative `くれ`（{f|待|ま}ってくれ）is rough,
male-marked and abrupt - fine among close male friends, never to a stranger. In
fast speech `～てくれる` reduces to `～てくれ` + rising tone or, very casually,
{f|見|み}せてくんない? Do not write that.

One extension worth knowing: `～てくれる` can be used with things and weather by
personification, and it is warm rather than childish - 
{f|天気|てんき}が{f|持|も}ってくれてよかった, "thankfully the weather held out for
us."

## Contrast

### ～てくれる vs ～てもらう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one event, two chairs. `～てくれる` seats the **doer** as
subject; `～てもらう` seats the **beneficiary** as subject.

</div>

<pre>
{f|友|とも}だちが{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}しを<b>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれました</b>。
My friend helped me move.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|友|とも}だちに{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}しを<b>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってもらいました</b>。
I got my friend to help me move.
</pre>

Both are natural and describe the same afternoon. The difference is initiative and
warmth. `くれる` presents the friend as acting of his own accord and reads as
thanks. `もらう` presents the speaker as having arranged it, which is why it is
the form you use when you asked, hired, or requested. Notice the particle flip on
the friend: `が` as subject, `に` as source.

### ～てくれる vs ～てくださる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `くださる` is `くれる` with a status gap. Use it when the
person doing you the favour is above you, or is a customer.

</div>

<pre>
{f|部長|ぶちょう}が{f|空港|くうこう}まで<b>{f|迎|むか}えに{f|来|き}てくださいました</b>。
The department head came to meet me at the airport.
</pre>

Using plain `くれました` about a superior is grammatical but flat, and in front of
that superior or their colleagues it sounds disrespectful. In private grumbling to
a friend it is perfectly normal - the choice tracks the audience as much as the
person.

### ～てくれる vs ～てあげる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `くれる` is safe to say to anyone; `あげる` is not.

</div>

They are mirror images grammatically, but not socially. Thanking someone with
<code>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれてありがとう</code> is warm. Telling that same person
<code>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってあげたよ</code> is smug, because you are the one naming your
own act a favour.

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんが{f|会場|かいじょう}まで{f|地図|ちず}を<b>{f|書|か}いてくれた</b>。
Tanaka drew me a map to the venue.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんに{f|会場|かいじょう}までの{f|地図|ちず}を<b>{f|書|か}いてあげた</b>。
I drew Tanaka a map to the venue.
</pre>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}が{f|友|とも}だちに{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}を{f|貸|か}して<b>くれました</b>。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}が{f|友|とも}だちに{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}を{f|貸|か}して<b>あげました</b>。
  `くれる` cannot take the speaker as the doer. When the favour leaves you, it is
  `あげる`.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}が{f|説明|せつめい}して<b>くださりました</b>。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}が{f|説明|せつめい}して<b>くださいました</b>。
  <code>くださる</code> has an irregular ます-form, like おっしゃる → おっしゃいます.

- ❌ {f|来|き}てくれて<b>ありがとうを{f|言|い}います</b>。 → ✅ {f|来|き}てくれて<b>ありがとう</b>。
  Thanks for an action is expressed with <code>～てくれてありがとう</code> directly;
  there is no need to build a sentence around the word ありがとう.

- ❌ {f|母|はは}が{f|私|わたし}に{f|作|つく}った{f|料理|りょうり}はおいしいです。 → ✅ {f|母|はは}が{f|作|つく}ってくれた{f|料理|りょうり}はおいしいです。
  Not ungrammatical, but a Japanese speaker would hear the missing `くれる` as
  oddly detached about their own mother's cooking.

- ❌ {f|手伝|てつだ}って<b>くれ</b>、お{f|願|ねが}いします。 → ✅ {f|手伝|てつだ}って<b>ください</b>。／{f|手伝|てつだ}って<b>くれない</b>?
  Bare `くれ` is a rough male command and clashes with a polite frame.

## Practice

**1.** {f|友|とも}だちが{f|空港|くうこう}まで{f|迎|むか}えに{f|来|き}て____ので、タクシーに{f|乗|の}らずにすみました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|迎|むか}えに{f|来|き}て<b>くれた</b>ので…

The friend acted and the benefit reached the speaker, so `くれる` is required. It
also does real work in the sentence: it is what tells the listener the trip to the
airport was a kindness rather than a coincidence.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|部長|ぶちょう}が{f|推薦状|すいせんじょう}を{f|書|か}いて（くれました・くださいました）。You are telling a colleague at work.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|部長|ぶちょう}が{f|推薦状|すいせんじょう}を{f|書|か}いて<b>くださいました</b>。

A department head is above you, and you are speaking in a workplace register, so
the honorific `くださる` is the expected choice. `くれました` is not wrong Japanese,
but about a superior at work it sounds careless.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Thank you for waiting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|待|ま}っていてくれて、ありがとう</b>。／ polite: <b>お{f|待|ま}ちくださって、ありがとうございます</b>。

Thanks for an *action* is built on <code>～てくれて</code>, not on a noun. Note the
`ていて`: the person was in the state of waiting, so <code>{f|待|ま}っていて</code> is
more natural than the bare <code>{f|待|ま}って</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|私|わたし}に{f|日本語|にほんご}を{f|教|おし}えてあげました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|私|わたし}に{f|日本語|にほんご}を{f|教|おし}えて<b>くれました</b>。

The teaching arrives at the speaker, so the direction verb must be `くれる`. As
written, the sentence claims Yamada did the speaker a favour *away from* the
speaker, which is impossible.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|誰|だれ}も{f|手伝|てつだ}いませんでした。
(b) {f|誰|だれ}も{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral fact: no help occurred. It could describe a task nobody was
expected to join.

(b) adds the speaker's stake. Because `くれる` frames help as something that should
have come toward them, its negative says the help was owed and withheld - "nobody
would lift a finger for me." It is a complaint, and native listeners hear the
resentment clearly.

</details>
