# ～し

**Meaning** · and what's more - piling up reasons or qualities, with the hint that there are others

`～し` joins clauses the way a person builds a case: one point, another point, and an
unstated "so, you see." It differs from a plain "and" in two ways. The items are
offered as **grounds** for something, and the list is **open** - you are naming a
couple of reasons out of several, not all of them.

## Formation

`し` attaches to the **plain form** of whatever ends the clause, and it can equally
follow a polite form when the sentence around it is polite.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + し | {f|降|ふ}る → {f|降|ふ}るし |
| Verb (past) | plain past + し | {f|降|ふ}った → {f|降|ふ}ったし |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + し | {f|行|い}かない → {f|行|い}かないし |
| Verb (polite) | ます-form + し | {f|降|ふ}ります → {f|降|ふ}りますし |
| い-adjective | plain form + し | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いし |
| い-adjective (polite) | です + し | {f|安|やす}いです → {f|安|やす}いですし |
| な-adjective | だ + し | {f|静|しず}か → {f|静|しず}かだし |
| な-adjective (polite) | です + し | {f|静|しず}かです → {f|静|しず}かですし |
| Noun | だ + し | {f|休|やす}み → {f|休|やす}みだし |
| Noun (polite) | です + し | {f|休|やす}みです → {f|休|やす}みですし |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `し` needs a *finished* clause in front of it. That is why nouns
and な-adjectives must keep their `だ`（❌<code>{f|静|しず}かし</code>）while
い-adjectives must not add one（❌<code>{f|安|やす}いだし</code>）.

</div>

A second habit worth building in from the start: the clause after `し` usually marks
one of its nouns with `も`. <code>{f|雨|あめ}だし、{f|風|かぜ}も{f|強|つよ}い</code>,
<code>{f|安|やす}いし、{f|味|あじ}もいい</code>. That `も` is what makes the sentence feel like
an accumulation of points rather than a sequence of events.

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}い<b>し</b>、おいしい。
This place is cheap, and the food's good.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|休|やす}みだ<b>し</b>、{f|映画|えいが}でも{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}かない?

</pre>

</pre>

"Why are you moving?" "The rent's high, and it's a long way from the office."
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`し` itself is neutral, but it belongs to **spoken** Japanese. It is at home in
conversation, in a chatty email, in a blog post; a formal report would reach for
<code>～{f|上|うえ}に</code> or simply separate sentences instead.

Politeness is decided by the clause, not by `し`. In polite speech you attach it to
the polite form - <code>{f|安|やす}いですし</code>, <code>あります**し**</code> - and mixing
the two inside one sentence sounds careless. Plain-form `し` inside an otherwise
polite sentence is heard sometimes in relaxed workplace speech, but it is not
something to imitate early.

The habit worth copying is **trailing し**: a sentence that ends on `し` and simply
stops.

- 「{f|今日|きょう}はやめておきます。{f|明日|あした}{f|早|はや}いし。」 - "I'll pass
  today. Early start tomorrow and all."
- 「べつにいいよ。もう{f|終|お}わったし。」 - "It's fine. It's over now anyway."

The unfinished shape is doing real work: it hands the conclusion to the listener
instead of stating it, which softens a refusal and lets you avoid saying the awkward
part out loud. The flip side is that a string of trailing `し` about someone else can
sound like grumbling or excuse-making - 「だって{f|忙|いそが}しかったし」 is exactly the
tone of a child explaining why the homework is not done.

One more thing `し` does that `から` cannot: it can list reasons that point in
different directions, letting the speaker weigh them.
<code>{f|給料|きゅうりょう}はいいし、でも{f|残業|ざんぎょう}も{f|多|おお}いし…</code> is a person
thinking out loud.

## Contrast

### ～し vs ～から

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` gives **the** reason and pushes the conclusion at the
listener. `し` gives **a** reason among others and leaves room for more.

</div>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}がある<b>から</b>、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|休|やす}みます。
I've got a fever, so I'm taking today off.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}もある<b>し</b>、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|休|やす}みます。
I've got a bit of a temperature and so on, so I'm taking today off.
</pre>

The `から` version presents one clean cause. The `し` version implies a small pile of
reasons - tiredness, the weather, the fever - of which you have named one. That
vagueness is why `し` sounds softer and less accusatory, and why refusals reach for it.

The two combine, and the combination is extremely common:
<code>{f|安|やす}いし、{f|近|ちか}いし、いい{f|店|みせ}ですから、ぜひ{f|行|い}ってみてください</code>
 - the `し` clauses stack the evidence and the final `から` delivers the verdict.

### ～し vs the て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て-form strings things together in **order**; `し` adds them
up as **evidence**. If the second clause simply happened next, you want て.

</div>

<pre>
{f|朝|あさ}{f|起|お}き<b>て</b>、{f|顔|かお}を{f|洗|あら}って、{f|家|いえ}を{f|出|で}ました。
I got up, washed my face and left the house.
</pre>

Putting `し` in there - ❌<code>{f|朝|あさ}{f|起|お}きたし、{f|顔|かお}を{f|洗|あら}いました</code>
 - makes it sound as though getting up were a reason for washing.

With adjectives the two overlap more, and both are correct:

<pre>
この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|軽|かる}く<b>て</b>、{f|使|つか}いやすいです。
This dictionary is light and easy to use.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|軽|かる}い<b>し</b>、{f|使|つか}いやすいです。
This dictionary is light, and easy to use into the bargain.
</pre>

The て version is a flat description. The `し` version sounds like a recommendation - 
you can hear "so you should buy it" behind it.

### ～し vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both give an incomplete list, but of different things. `し` lists
whole **clauses** as reasons; `たり` lists **activities** as samples of what went on.

</div>

<pre>
{f|昨日|きのう}は{f|洗濯|せんたく}をし<b>たり</b>、{f|部屋|へや}を{f|片|かた}づけ<b>たり</b>しました。
Yesterday I did the washing, tidied my room and so on.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昨日|きのう}は{f|洗濯|せんたく}もした<b>し</b>、{f|部屋|へや}も{f|片|かた}づけた。
Yesterday I did the washing, and I tidied my room as well.
</pre>

The `たり` sentence answers "what did you get up to?" - it paints a picture of the day.
The `し` sentence answers "have you done anything useful?" - it is a defence, item by
item. Note also that `たり` must end in する, while `し` needs nothing after it.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この{f|町|まち}は{f|静|しず}か**し**、{f|空気|くうき}もきれいです。 → ✅ この{f|町|まち}は{f|静|しず}か**だし**、{f|空気|くうき}もきれいです。
  A な-adjective needs its `だ` before `し`. The same goes for nouns: <code>{f|学生|がくせい}だし</code>.

- ❌ この{f|本|ほん}は{f|安|やす}い**だし**、{f|面白|おもしろ}いです。 → ✅ この{f|本|ほん}は{f|安|やす}い**し**、{f|面白|おもしろ}いです。
  An い-adjective is already a complete predicate, so adding `だ` is one word too many.

- ❌ シャワーを{f|浴|あ}びた**し**、{f|寝|ね}ました。 → ✅ シャワーを{f|浴|あ}び**て**、{f|寝|ね}ました。
  `し` does not narrate a sequence. Two events that simply happened one after the
  other take the て-form.

- ❌ {f|近|ちか}いし、{f|安|やす}い**です**し、いい{f|店|みせ}です。 → ✅ {f|近|ちか}い**です**し、{f|安|やす}い**です**し、いい{f|店|みせ}です。
  Keep one politeness level across the whole sentence. Plain `し` followed by polite
  `し` sounds like a slip.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|町|まち}は{f|便利|べんり}____、{f|家賃|やちん}も{f|安|やす}いです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|町|まち}は{f|便利|べんり}<b>だし</b>、{f|家賃|やちん}も{f|安|やす}いです。

<code>{f|便利|べんり}</code> is a な-adjective, so it needs `だ` to finish the clause before `し`
can attach. The polite alternative is <code>{f|便利|べんり}ですし</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}（し・て）、ごはんを{f|食|た}べました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|昨日|きのう}は{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}<b>て</b>、ごはんを{f|食|た}べました。

Nothing here is a reason for anything - the two events simply happened in order, and
that is the て-form's job. `し` would suggest you were listing evidence for some
conclusion you have not stated.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This café is close to the station and it isn't expensive, so let's meet here."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|店|みせ}は{f|駅|えき}から{f|近|ちか}い<b>し</b>、{f|値段|ねだん}も{f|高|たか}くない<b>し</b>、ここで{f|待|ま}ち{f|合|あ}わせましょう。

Two `し` clauses stack the reasons, and the suggestion follows with no connective at
all - once `し` has done the work, `だから` would be redundant.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|親切|しんせつ}し、{f|頭|あたま}もいいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|親切|しんせつ}<b>だし</b>、{f|頭|あたま}もいいです。

<code>{f|親切|しんせつ}</code> is a な-adjective. Without `だ` the clause never finishes, and `し`
has nothing to attach to. Polite version: <code>{f|親切|しんせつ}ですし</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|雨|あめ}だ**から**、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|出|で}かけません。
(b) {f|雨|あめ}だ**し**、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|出|で}かけません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) names the rain as the reason, full stop. It is a clear, slightly firm statement of
cause and effect, and it invites the listener to accept it.

(b) offers the rain as *one* of the things making you stay in - you are tired, there
is nothing on, and besides, it's raining. It sounds more relaxed and less like an
argument, which is exactly why it is the friendlier way to decline an invitation.

</details>
