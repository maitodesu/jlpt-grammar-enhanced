# ～たり～たり

**Meaning** · doing things like A and B - naming a couple of examples out of a larger set

`～たり～たり` lists **samples**. Whatever you name, the listener understands that
other things happened too, and that the order they are given in means nothing. Its
second job is alternation: two states that keep swapping places, one day this, the
next day that.

## Formation

Take the plain past（た-form）of each item, add `り`, and close the whole list with
`する`. The `する` is not decoration - it is the verb of the sentence, and it carries
the tense and the politeness for everything in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + り | {f|読|よ}む → {f|読|よ}んだり |
| Verb (する) | した + り | {f|掃除|そうじ}する → {f|掃除|そうじ}したり |
| Verb (negative) | なかった + り | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}かなかったり |
| い-adjective | かった + り | {f|暑|あつ}い → {f|暑|あつ}かったり |
| な-adjective | だった + り | {f|静|しず}か → {f|静|しず}かだったり |
| Noun | だった + り | {f|雨|あめ} → {f|雨|あめ}だったり |
| Closing the list (verbs) | …たり…たり + する | {f|歩|ある}いたり{f|走|はし}ったりする |
| Closing the list (past, polite) | …たり…たり + しました | {f|歌|うた}ったり{f|踊|おど}ったりしました |
| Closing the list (adjectives / nouns) | …だったり…だったり + だ／です | {f|暑|あつ}かったり{f|寒|さむ}かったりです |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can make the た-form, you can make the たり-form: just add
`り`. The trap is the closing `する`, which learners drop. It is what conjugates:
<code>したり…したりしています</code>, <code>…したりしたい</code>, <code>…したりしないでください</code>.

</div>

## Nuance & register

`～たり` is register-neutral. Everything about the level of the sentence is decided by
the closing verb: <code>{f|見|み}たりします</code> is polite,
<code>{f|見|み}たりする</code> is plain, <code>{f|見|み}たりいたします</code> is humble. The
`たり` parts themselves never change.

Two conversational habits to recognise. First, a **single** `たり` is normal - it
still means "and things like that", and it often softens an accusation:
<code>{f|泣|な}いたりしないで</code>, <code>{f|無理|むり}をしたりしないでね</code>. Second,
`たりして` at the end of a sentence is a hedge, close to English "…or something":
「{f|明日|あした}、{f|急|きゅう}に{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|降|ふ}ったりして」 - "knowing our luck it'll
snow tomorrow." That use is casual and slightly playful; keep it out of a report.

In casual speech you will also hear `とか` doing the same job - 
<code>{f|洗|あら}ったりとかして</code> - which is chattier still, and the closing `する` is
sometimes swallowed altogether: 「{f|掃除|そうじ}したり{f|洗濯|せんたく}したり、いろいろ」.
Understand it, but write the `する`.

The alternation use has one shape worth memorising because it appears everywhere:
**A + たり + A's negative + たり** - <code>{f|降|ふ}ったり{f|降|ふ}らなかったり</code>,
<code>{f|来|き}たり{f|来|こ}なかったり</code> - meaning "sometimes yes, sometimes no". It
carries a faint note of unreliability, so it is not a compliment.

## Contrast

### ～たり～たり vs the て-form

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the て-form reports what happened, **in order and in full**.
`たり` gives an unordered sample and implies there was more.

</div>

<pre>
{f|朝|あさ}、コーヒーを{f|飲|の}ん<b>で</b>、メールをチェックして、{f|家|いえ}を{f|出|で}ました。
This morning I had a coffee, checked my email and left the house.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|朝|あさ}は、コーヒーを{f|飲|の}ん<b>だり</b>メールをチェックし<b>たり</b>しています。
In the mornings I have a coffee, check my email, and so on.
</pre>

The first is a report of one particular morning, in sequence. The second describes
what mornings are generally like, and rearranging the two items would not change it at
all. Because of that, `たり` is wrong when the order matters:
❌<code>{f|歯|は}をみがいたり{f|寝|ね}たりしました</code> for "I brushed my teeth and went to
bed."

### ～たり～たり vs ～や・～とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same "partial list" idea, different word class. `や` and `とか`
list **nouns**; `たり` lists **actions and states**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}にペン<b>や</b>ノートが{f|置|お}いてあります。
There are pens, notebooks and so on lying on the desk.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}で{f|書|か}い<b>たり</b>{f|調|しら}べ<b>たり</b>しています。
I write and look things up at the desk.
</pre>

`とか` straddles the line - it lists nouns like `や`, but in casual speech it also
lists dictionary-form verbs: <code>{f|洗|あら}うとか{f|干|ほ}すとか</code>. That verb use is
markedly more casual than `たり`, and it does not need a closing `する`.

### ～たり～たり vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` is two actions happening **at the same moment**; `たり` is
two actions taking turns, or sampled from a stretch of time.

</div>

<pre>
{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}き<b>ながら</b>{f|走|はし}っています。
I run while listening to music. (both at once)
</pre>

<pre>
{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}い<b>たり</b>{f|走|はし}っ<b>たり</b>しています。
I listen to music, go running, that kind of thing. (at different times)
</pre>

Learners sometimes reach for `たり` when describing multitasking, and it quietly says
the opposite of what they mean. If the two things overlap in time, you want `ながら`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ テレビを{f|見|み}る**たり**、{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}む**たり**します。 → ✅ テレビを{f|見|み}**たり**、{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}ん**だり**します。
  `たり` is built on the た-form, never the dictionary form. Make the past tense first,
  then swap `た` for `たり` - including the voiced `だり`.

- ❌ {f|週末|しゅうまつ}は{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}たり、{f|友|とも}だちに{f|会|あ}ったり。 → ✅ …{f|会|あ}ったり**します**。
  The closing `する` is what carries the tense. Leaving it off is fine in casual
  conversation and wrong in writing or polite speech.

- ❌ {f|部屋|へや}が{f|静|しず}か**たり**、うるさかったりします。 → ✅ {f|部屋|へや}が{f|静|しず}か**だったり**、うるさかったりします。
  な-adjectives and nouns need `だった` before `り`; only verbs and い-adjectives have a
  た-form of their own.

- ❌ {f|起|お}きたり、{f|顔|かお}を{f|洗|あら}ったりして、{f|会社|かいしゃ}に{f|行|い}きます。 → ✅ {f|起|お}き**て**、{f|顔|かお}を{f|洗|あら}っ**て**、{f|会社|かいしゃ}に{f|行|い}きます。
  A fixed morning routine happens in a fixed order, and `たり` explicitly throws the
  order away. Use the て-form.

## Practice

**1.** {f|週末|しゅうまつ}は{f|部屋|へや}を{f|掃除|そうじ}し____、{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に{f|行|い}っ____します。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|掃除|そうじ}し<b>たり</b>、{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に{f|行|い}っ<b>たり</b>します。

Both verbs go into the た-form first（<code>した</code>, <code>{f|行|い}った</code>）and then take
`り`. The final `します` is the verb of the whole sentence and supplies the polite
present tense.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|手|て}を{f|洗|あら}っ（たり・て）から、ごはんを{f|食|た}べましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|手|て}を{f|洗|あら}っ<b>て</b>から、ごはんを{f|食|た}べましょう。

The whole point of the sentence is the order - hands first, then food - and
<code>てから</code> is what expresses that. `たり` would say the two happen in no
particular order, which is not the advice anyone gives about washing your hands.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Lately the weather has been rainy some days and clear on others."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|最近|さいきん}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っ<b>たり</b>{f|晴|は}れ<b>たり</b>しています。

This is the alternation use: two states swapping back and forth rather than a list of
activities. <code>しています</code> presents it as an ongoing situation; <code>します</code>
would sound like a general truth about the climate.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|友|とも}だちと{f|話|はな}するたり、ゲームをしたりしました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|友|とも}だちと{f|話|はな}し<b>たり</b>、ゲームをし<b>たり</b>しました。

<code>{f|話|はな}す</code> is a う-verb, so its た-form is <code>{f|話|はな}した</code> and the
たり-form is <code>{f|話|はな}したり</code>. The dictionary form never appears before `たり`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|洗濯|せんたく}をして、{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に{f|行|い}きました。
(b) {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|洗濯|せんたく}をし**たり**、{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に{f|行|い}っ**たり**しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a report of the day in sequence: the washing first, then the shopping, and by
implication that is what you did.

(b) picks two things out of a fuller day and offers them as examples. The order is not
being claimed, and the listener understands there were other errands you have not
bothered to mention. It is the natural answer to a vague "what did you get up to
yesterday?"

</details>
