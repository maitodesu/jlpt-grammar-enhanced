# それでも

**Meaning** · but still; and yet - everything just said pointed the other way, and it happened anyway

`それでも` opens a sentence by picking up the whole situation you have just described
and refusing to let it decide the outcome. It concedes the point first, then overrules
it. That two-step is why it sounds firmer, and often more thoughtful, than a bare
"but".

## Usage

`それでも` is a conjunction: it stands at the **head** of a sentence, usually followed
by a comma, and it needs a previous sentence to point back at. The `それ` is doing real
work - it means "that whole state of affairs" - so it cannot open a conversation.

<code>{f|寒|さむ}かった。それでも、{f|歩|ある}いて{f|帰|かえ}った。</code>

It also lives inside a clause when the two halves are joined:
{f|失敗|しっぱい}するかもしれない。それでも、やってみる{f|価値|かち}はある。 The second
sentence is where the speaker's real position is; `それでも` marks everything before it
as acknowledged and set aside.

There is one fixed phrase built on it that behaves differently:
<code>それでもいい</code> / <code>それでもかまわない</code> - "even so, that's fine". Here
`それ` + `でも` is the ordinary "even if" construction, and the phrase is the standard
way to accept a condition you have just been warned about.
「{f|時間|じかん}がかかりますよ。」「それでもいいです。」

Two neighbours to keep separate, because they differ by one syllable and mean the
opposite thing: <code>それで</code> and <code>そこで</code> both introduce a **consequence**
("and so"), while `それでも` introduces the **failure** of a consequence.

## Examples

<pre>
この{f|薬|くすり}はとても{f|苦|にが}いです。<b>それでも</b>、{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|飲|の}んでいます。
This medicine is really bitter. Even so, I take it every day.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|何度|なんど}も{f|失敗|しっぱい}した。<b>それでも</b>、あきらめなかった。
He failed over and over. Still, he never gave up.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|外|そと}は{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|降|ふ}っていた。<b>それでも</b>、{f|子|こ}どもたちは{f|公園|こうえん}へ{f|走|はし}っていった。
It was snowing outside. The children ran off to the park regardless.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|給料|きゅうりょう}は{f|安|やす}いし、{f|残業|ざんぎょう}も{f|多|おお}い。<b>それでも</b>、{f|今|いま}の{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|続|つづ}けたいと{f|思|おも}っています。
The pay is poor and there's a lot of overtime. All the same, I want to stay in this job.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`それでも` is register-neutral and equally at home in an essay and in conversation,
though everyday speech often just says `でも`. Choosing the longer word is not merely
emphasis: `でも` brushes the previous statement aside, while `それでも` picks it up,
weighs it, and then decides anyway. That makes it the polite way to disagree with
someone who has a point.

「おっしゃることはよく{f|分|わ}かります。それでも、{f|私|わたし}は{f|反対|はんたい}です。」

Because of that acknowledging feel, `それでも` frequently follows a `し` clause or a
list of drawbacks - the speaker sets out the case against, and then states what they
are going to do. It also sits naturally in front of a resolution or a value judgement:
<code>それでもやってみます</code>, <code>それでも{f|信|しん}じたい</code>.

Three near neighbours, worth keeping straight:

- **でも** - the everyday "but". Lighter, more casual, and does not necessarily
  concede anything.
- **それなのに** - "and yet", with a complaint attached. It says the outcome was
  *unfair*, where `それでも` merely says it was unexpected.
- **それにもかかわらず** - the same idea in formal written Japanese. Stiff in
  conversation; normal in a report.

And one that is not a neighbour at all: **それに** means "what's more", adding a second
point in the *same* direction. Learners reach for `それでも` when they want `それに`
surprisingly often.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}いです。**それでも**、おいしいです。 → ✅ この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}いです。**それに**、おいしいです。
  Cheap and tasty are both good points, so nothing is being overruled. `それでも` needs
  the second sentence to go against the first.

- ❌ {f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まりました。**それでも**、{f|会社|かいしゃ}に{f|遅|おく}れました。 → ✅ {f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まりました。**それで**、{f|会社|かいしゃ}に{f|遅|おく}れました。
  Being late is exactly what you would expect after the trains stop. That is a
  consequence, so it takes `それで`.

- ❌ **それでも**、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|会議|かいぎ}があります。（as the opening line of an email） → ✅ {f|実|じつ}は、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|会議|かいぎ}があります。
  `それ` has to refer to something already said. With nothing in front of it, the
  sentence leaves the reader looking for a missing paragraph.

- ❌ {f|高|たか}かった**それでも**、{f|買|か}いました。 → ✅ {f|高|たか}かったです。**それでも**、{f|買|か}いました。／{f|高|たか}かった**けど**、{f|買|か}いました。
  `それでも` is not a clause connective. It starts a new sentence; joining two clauses
  inside one sentence is `けど` or `が`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|何度|なんど}も{f|注意|ちゅうい}しました。____、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|同|おな}じ{f|失敗|しっぱい}をくり{f|返|かえ}しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それでも</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|同|おな}じ{f|失敗|しっぱい}をくり{f|返|かえ}しています。

Being warned repeatedly ought to stop the mistakes, and it has not. `それでも` picks up
that whole first sentence and reports that it made no difference.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|朝|あさ}から{f|熱|ねつ}がありました。（それでも・それで）、{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|休|やす}みました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|朝|あさ}から{f|熱|ねつ}がありました。<b>それで</b>、{f|会社|かいしゃ}を{f|休|やす}みました。

Taking the day off is the natural result of having a fever, so this is a consequence - 
`それで`. `それでも` would only work if the second sentence went against expectation,
as in <code>それでも、{f|会社|かいしゃ}に{f|行|い}きました</code>.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っていた。**それでも**、{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|待|ま}っていてくれた。
(b) {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っていた。**それなのに**、{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|傘|かさ}を{f|持|も}っていなかった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) acknowledges the rain and then reports something admirable that happened in spite
of it. `それでも` is neutral about blame; here it carries a note of gratitude.

(b) uses `それなのに`, which adds the speaker's dissatisfaction - she *should* have had
an umbrella, and the fact that she did not is being criticised or worried over. Swap
the two connectives and each sentence loses its point.

</details>
