# ～たら

**Meaning** · if / when / after - the all-purpose conditional

`～たら` is the one you reach for when you are not sure which conditional you
want, and you will usually be right. It takes any kind of main clause, it handles
both "if" and "when", and it is the everyday spoken default.

## Formation

Take the **plain past** of anything at all and add `ら`. That is the entire rule,
which is a large part of why `たら` is so easy to use.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain past + ら | {f|飲|の}む → {f|飲|の}んだ → {f|飲|の}んだら |
| Verb (る-verb) | plain past + ら | {f|届|とど}ける → {f|届|とど}けた → {f|届|とど}けたら |
| Verb (negative) | なかった + ら | {f|飲|の}まない → {f|飲|の}まなかったら |
| い-adjective | かった + ら | {f|寒|さむ}い → {f|寒|さむ}かったら |
| い-adjective (negative) | くなかった + ら | {f|寒|さむ}くない → {f|寒|さむ}くなかったら |
| な-adjective | だった + ら | {f|不便|ふべん} → {f|不便|ふべん}だったら |
| Noun | だった + ら | {f|雨|あめ} → {f|雨|あめ}だったら |
| Polite (formal) | ました + ら | {f|届|とど}きます → {f|届|とど}きましたら |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the past form in front of `ら` says nothing about **past
time**. It marks the condition as *completed at the moment the main clause
happens*. The tense of the sentence is carried entirely by the final verb.

</div>

`たら` does three jobs, and it is worth naming them separately:

1. **If** - a genuine condition: {f|雨|あめ}だったら{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。
2. **When / after** - for something certain to happen, where English would not
   say "if": 20{f|歳|さい}になったら…
3. **Discovery** - with a **past** main clause, no conditional meaning survives at
   all. See the last two examples.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>{f|寒|さむ}かったら</b>、エアコンを{f|消|け}してください。
If you're cold, turn the air conditioning off.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}<b>{f|晴|は}れたら</b>、{f|海|うみ}に{f|行|い}こう。
If it's clear tomorrow, let's go to the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|仕事|しごと}が<b>{f|終|お}わったら</b>、{f|飲|の}みに{f|行|い}きませんか。
Shall we go for a drink after work?
</pre>

<pre>
20{f|歳|さい}に<b>なったら</b>、{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|暮|く}らしたいです。
When I turn twenty, I want to live on my own.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

Once next week's meeting materials are ready, show them to the manager and then send them round.
</pre>

If you get lost, don't hesitate - just ask someone nearby.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`たら` is the spoken workhorse. In ordinary conversation it outnumbers `と`, `ば`
and `なら` put together; in formal writing it thins out and
<code>{f|場合|ばあい}</code> and `ば` take over. It is not casual in itself - the
politeness sits on the final verb, and `ましたら` exists for genuinely formal
contexts: {f|荷物|にもつ}が{f|届|とど}きましたら、ご{f|連絡|れんらく}します。

`もし` pairs with `たら` more naturally than with anything else. `もし` is
optional and only reinforces the "this may not happen" reading; it never replaces
the `たら`.

Two constructions built on `たら` that you meet immediately:

- **～たらいい／～たらいいですか** - "it'd be good if…", "what should I…".
- **～たらどうですか／～たら?** - a suggestion aimed at the listener. Both have
  their own pages; note only that the suggestion reading comes from `どう`, not
  from `たら`.

The **discovery** use deserves its own paragraph, because it is the one that
makes learners think they have misread the sentence. With a past-tense main
clause, `たら` means "I did X, and then - as it turned out - Y". There is no
condition anywhere in it. The Y part has to be something outside the speaker's
control: a discovery, a surprise, an event that simply occurred. A *planned* next
action is not allowed there, and needs `～てから` instead.

## Contrast

### ～たら vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `と` needs the link to be automatic and repeatable, and bans
any main clause expressing the speaker's will. `たら` accepts single occasions
and every kind of ending.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}ける<b>と</b>、{f|海|うみ}が{f|見|み}えます。
Open this window and you can see the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}け<b>たら</b>、{f|海|うみ}が{f|見|み}えました。
I opened the window, and there was the sea.
</pre>

The first is a permanent property of the window; the second is one morning. And
where `と` would refuse outright - 
❌{f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けると、{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|撮|と}りましょう - `たら`
is fine: {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けたら、{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|撮|と}りましょう。

Note the shapes too: `と` takes the plain **non-past**, `たら` the plain
**past**. If you find yourself writing a past form in front of `と`, you have
picked the wrong one.

### ～たら vs ～ば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after an **action verb**, only `たら` can be followed by a
request or a command. And only `たら` narrates something that actually happened
once.

</div>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さんが<b>{f|来|き}たら</b>、{f|教|おし}えてください。
Let me know when Tanaka gets here.
</pre>

❌{f|田中|たなか}さんが{f|来|く}れば、{f|教|おし}えてください is wrong. Both
work, though, once the condition is a state rather than an action:
{f|分|わ}からなかったら／{f|分|わ}からなければ、{f|聞|き}いてください are equally
good.

The second gap is the past. A `たら` clause with a past main clause reports a real
sequence; the `ば` equivalent flips into counterfactual regret
({f|聞|き}けばよかった = "I should have asked, and didn't"). If you want to say
what happened, `ば` is not available.

### ～たら vs ～なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a `たら` clause always happens **before** its main clause. A
`なら` clause need not: `なら` takes up a supposition, and the main clause can be
carried out first.

</div>

The clearest illustration is a road-safety slogan you see on posters all over
Japan, which uses both in one breath:

<pre>
<b>{f|飲|の}んだら</b>{f|乗|の}るな、{f|乗|の}る<b>なら</b>{f|飲|の}むな。
If you've drunk, don't drive; if you're going to drive, don't drink.
</pre>

<code>{f|飲|の}んだら</code> = the drinking is finished, and the driving would come after it.
<code>{f|乗|の}るなら</code> = driving is a plan you are entertaining, and the instruction
applies *beforehand*. Swap the two and the slogan collapses.

The same split in a plain sentence:

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}に{f|行|い}っ<b>たら</b>、{f|温泉|おんせん}に{f|入|はい}りたい。
When I get to Japan, I want to go to a hot spring.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}に{f|行|い}く<b>なら</b>、{f|春|はる}がいちばんいいですよ。
If you're going to Japan, spring is the best time.
</pre>

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|行|い}く**たら** → ✅ {f|行|い}っ**たら**
  `たら` is built on the **past** form, never the dictionary form.
  {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}った → {f|行|い}ったら.

- ❌ {f|高|たか}い**たら** → ✅ {f|高|たか}**かったら**
  い-adjectives go through their past form first: {f|高|たか}い →
  {f|高|たか}かった → {f|高|たか}かったら.

- ❌ {f|日本|にほん}に{f|行|い}ったら、ビザを{f|取|と}ります。 → ✅ {f|日本|にほん}に{f|行|い}く**なら**、ビザを{f|取|と}ります。
  A `たら` clause must come first in time, and you get the visa before you fly.
  For a plan you are reacting to, `なら`.

- ❌ {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けたら、{f|部屋|へや}を{f|掃除|そうじ}しました。 → ✅ {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}け**てから**、{f|部屋|へや}を{f|掃除|そうじ}しました。
  With a past main clause, `たら` means the speaker *discovered* something. A
  deliberate next action needs `～てから`.

- ❌ このボタンを{f|押|お}したら、お{f|湯|ゆ}が{f|出|で}ます。 → ✅ このボタンを{f|押|お}す**と**、お{f|湯|ゆ}が{f|出|で}ます。
  Not ungrammatical, but wrong for a manual: `たら` frames it as one occasion,
  while a machine's fixed behaviour calls for `と`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|安|やす}____、もう{f|一|ひと}つ{f|買|か}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|安|やす}<b>かったら</b>、もう{f|一|ひと}つ{f|買|か}います。

An い-adjective forms `たら` through its past: {f|安|やす}い →
{f|安|やす}かった → {f|安|やす}かったら. ❌{f|安|やす}いたら does not exist.

</details>

**2.** {f|大学|だいがく}を（{f|出|で}ると・{f|出|で}たら）、{f|国|くに}に{f|帰|かえ}るつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|大学|だいがく}を<b>{f|出|で}たら</b>、{f|国|くに}に{f|帰|かえ}るつもりです。

`つもり` states the speaker's intention, and `と` cannot host anything that
expresses the speaker's will. `たら` has no such limit - and here it carries the
"when", not "if": graduating is going to happen.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When the rain stops, let's go for a walk."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|雨|あめ}が<b>やんだら</b>、{f|散歩|さんぽ}に{f|行|い}きましょう。

`～ましょう` is a proposal, so `と` is out. The event is expected rather than
doubtful, which is why English says "when" and Japanese still uses `たら` - the
same form covers both.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|来週|らいしゅう}{f|京都|きょうと}に{f|行|い}ったら、ホテルを{f|予約|よやく}しておきます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|来週|らいしゅう}{f|京都|きょうと}に{f|行|い}く<b>なら</b>、ホテルを{f|予約|よやく}しておきます。

You book the hotel *before* leaving, and a `たら` clause is always the earlier
event. `なら` is the one conditional that lets the main clause come first in
time.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}に{f|電話|でんわ}したら、{f|留守|るす}でした。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}に{f|電話|でんわ}したら、{f|教|おし}えてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) has a **past** main clause, so there is no condition in it at all: "I phoned
him, and he was out." The `たら` clause just sets the scene, and what follows is
something the speaker found out.

(b) has a **request** as the main clause, so it is a genuine conditional: "if
(or when) you phone him, let me know." Nothing has happened yet.

The `たら` is identical in both. The tense of the main clause decides whether you
are reading a report or a condition - and this is the single most useful thing to
know about `たら`.

</details>
