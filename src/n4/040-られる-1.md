# ～られる - potential, "can do"

**Meaning** · be able to do it - the ability, or the circumstances, are there

The potential form says that an action is available to the subject. It covers both
halves of English *can*: the skill you have (<code>{f|泳|およ}げる</code>, "I can
swim") and the opportunity the world gives you
(<code>{f|今日|きょう}は{f|休|やす}める</code>, "I can take today off"). Japanese does
not separate the two.

## Formation

Only る-verbs and {f|来|く}る actually use the shape `られる`. う-verbs use a
shorter ending, and する is replaced outright.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → え-row kana, then + る | {f|書|か}く → {f|書|か}ける |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + られる | {f|食|た}べる → {f|食|た}べられる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by できる - no られる at all | {f|運転|うんてん}する → {f|運転|うんてん}できる |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | stem こ, + られる | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}られる |

Every う-verb ending, spelled out. The last row is the one people get wrong:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → え + る | {f|買|か}う → {f|買|か}える |
| ～く | く → け + る | {f|歩|ある}く → {f|歩|ある}ける |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → げ + る | {f|泳|およ}ぐ → {f|泳|およ}げる |
| ～す | す → せ + る | {f|話|はな}す → {f|話|はな}せる |
| ～つ | つ → て + る | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}てる |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → ね + る | {f|死|し}ぬ → {f|死|し}ねる |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → べ + る | {f|遊|あそ}ぶ → {f|遊|あそ}べる |
| ～む | む → め + る | {f|読|よ}む → {f|読|よ}める |
| ～る | る → れ + る | {f|帰|かえ}る → {f|帰|かえ}れる |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a potential verb is always a <b>る-verb</b>, whatever it started
as. So {f|書|か}ける → {f|書|か}けない, {f|書|か}けます, {f|書|か}けて,
{f|書|か}けた; and {f|食|た}べられる → {f|食|た}べられない, {f|食|た}べられます.

</div>

| Further forms | Example |
|---|---|
| Negative | {f|書|か}けない／{f|食|た}べられない／できない／{f|来|こ}られない |
| Polite | {f|書|か}けます／{f|食|た}べられます／できます／{f|来|こ}られます |
| Past | {f|書|か}けた／{f|食|た}べられた／できた／{f|来|こ}られた |
| ら{f|抜|ぬ}き (spoken, nonstandard) | - ／{f|食|た}べれる／ - ／{f|来|こ}れる |

The object of a potential verb normally shifts from `を` to `が`:
{f|漢字|かんじ}<b>を</b>{f|書|か}く → {f|漢字|かんじ}<b>が</b>{f|書|か}ける.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}は{f|泳|およ}<b>げません</b>。
I can't swim.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|辛|から}いものが<b>{f|食|た}べられます</b>か。
Can you eat spicy food?
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|漢字|かんじ}が<b>{f|読|よ}めない</b>。
I can't read this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|予約|よやく}なしでも<b>{f|入|はい}れます</b>か。
Can we get in without a reservation?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}があるので、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|会社|かいしゃ}に<b>{f|来|こ}られません</b>。
I have a fever, so I can't come in to work today.
</pre>

<pre>
チケットが{f|取|と}れたから、{f|来月|らいげつ}のコンサートに<b>{f|行|い}ける</b>よ。
I got tickets, so I can go to the concert next month.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}は{f|現金|げんきん}でしか<b>{f|払|はら}えない</b>ので、カードは{f|使|つか}えません。
This place only takes cash, so you can't use a card.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どものころは{f|牛乳|ぎゅうにゅう}が<b>{f|飲|の}めなかった</b>が、{f|今|いま}は{f|平気|へいき}になった。
I couldn't drink milk as a child, but I'm fine with it now.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The potential form is register-neutral and is the default way to say *can* in
speech. It has no built-in politeness; `～ます` on the end handles that.

**The particle.** `が` is the textbook answer and the safest one:
{f|日本語|にほんご}が{f|話|はな}せます. `を` is also alive and common, and it is
preferred when the object is long, when the clause already contains a `が`, or when
the `を` was never a direct object to begin with - 
{f|道|みち}<b>を</b>{f|歩|ある}ける, {f|家|いえ}<b>を</b>{f|出|で}られる.

<b>ら{f|抜|ぬ}き{f|言葉|ことば}.</b> In everyday speech many speakers drop the `ら` from the る-verb and
{f|来|く}る potentials: {f|食|た}べれる, {f|見|み}れる, {f|起|お}きれる,
{f|来|こ}れる. This is extremely widespread - in some regions it is simply the
normal form - and it has a real advantage: it separates potential from passive,
which the full form cannot do. It is nevertheless still classed as nonstandard.
Do not write it in an exam, an essay or a business email, and note that it never
touches う-verbs: {f|書|か}ける is not ら{f|抜|ぬ}き, it is just the potential.

<b>れ{f|足|た}す{f|言葉|ことば}</b> is the opposite error and is unambiguously wrong: ❌{f|行|い}けれる,
❌{f|読|よ}めれる. An extra `れ` is inserted where nothing belongs.

**Verbs with no potential form.** Some verbs already contain the idea of ability
or spontaneous perception and refuse the potential: `わかる`, `できる`,
<code>{f|見|み}える</code>, <code>{f|聞|き}こえる</code>. `ある` has none either. And
verbs describing something involuntary - <code>{f|疲|つか}れる</code>,
<code>{f|降|ふ}る</code> - take one only in odd, marked contexts.

**Politeness by potential.** Turning a request into a potential question softens it
a great deal: {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けてもらえますか is gentler than
{f|開|あ}けてください, and <code>{f|開|あ}けていただけませんか</code> gentler still.
This is one of the main jobs the form does in adult conversation.

## Contrast

### ～られる - potential vs passive

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - for <b>う-verbs</b> the two forms are different words
({f|読|よ}める vs {f|読|よ}まれる). For <b>る-verbs and {f|来|く}る</b> they are
identical, and only the particles and the context tell you which one you are
looking at.

</div>

<code>{f|食|た}べられる</code> is genuinely two words wearing one coat:

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}は{f|納豆|なっとう}が<b>{f|食|た}べられる</b>。
I can eat nattō. (potential)
</pre>

<pre>
ケーキを{f|弟|おとうと}に<b>{f|食|た}べられた</b>。
My little brother ate my cake (on me). (passive)
</pre>

Three signals separate them. The potential marks its object with `が` and has no
agent; the passive marks its agent with `に`. The potential is about the subject's
own capacity; the passive is about something done to the subject. And the passive
very often turns up in the past, reporting a nuisance.

When the sentence would be genuinely ambiguous, Japanese avoids the form rather
than tolerating it: speakers reach for <code>{f|食|た}べれる</code> in casual speech
or <code>{f|食|た}べることができる</code> in writing, both of which can only mean
"can eat".

### the potential form vs ～ことができる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning; the potential form is spoken Japanese,
`～ことができる` is written and institutional Japanese.

</div>

<pre>
ここから{f|富士山|ふじさん}が<b>{f|見|み}られます</b>。
You can see Mt Fuji from here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここから{f|富士山|ふじさん}を<b>{f|見|み}ることができます</b>。
Mt Fuji can be viewed from here. (a sign, a guidebook)
</pre>

Note the particle again: the potential form pulls the object to `が`, while
`～ことができる` leaves the inner verb intact with its `を`.

### {f|見|み}られる vs {f|見|み}える

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|見|み}られる</code> is *able to see* - you have the
chance, the permission, the ticket. <code>{f|見|み}える</code> is *is visible* - 
the image simply arrives at your eyes.

</div>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}から{f|海|うみ}が<b>{f|見|み}える</b>。
You can see the sea from the window. (it is simply there)
</pre>

<pre>
チケットがあれば、リハーサルも<b>{f|見|み}られる</b>。
If you have a ticket, you can watch the rehearsal too. (access)
</pre>

The same split runs through <code>{f|聞|き}こえる</code> (a sound reaches you) and
<code>{f|聞|き}ける</code> (you get the opportunity to listen). Because
<code>{f|見|み}える</code> and <code>{f|聞|き}こえる</code> already carry the
meaning, they have no potential form of their own:
❌<code>{f|見|み}えられる</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|話|はな}せられる → ✅ {f|話|はな}<b>せる</b>
  `られる` is only for る-verbs and {f|来|く}る. An う-verb changes its own ending
  and stops there.

- ❌ {f|行|い}けれる／{f|読|よ}めれる → ✅ {f|行|い}<b>ける</b>／{f|読|よ}<b>める</b>
  This is れ{f|足|た}す{f|言葉|ことば} - an inserted `れ`. Unlike ら{f|抜|ぬ}き it is not accepted in any
  register.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}{f|来|き}られますか。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}<b>{f|来|こ}られますか</b>。
  The potential stem of {f|来|く}る is こ. `き` is the ます-stem
  ({f|来|き}ます), and it does not belong here.

- ❌ {f|日本語|にほんご}がわかれます。 → ✅ {f|日本語|にほんご}が<b>わかります</b>。
  `わかる` already means "is understandable to me". It has no potential form.

- ❌ レポートを{f|食|た}べれます（in an essay） → ✅ …{f|食|た}<b>べられます</b>
  ら{f|抜|ぬ}き is fine among friends and wrong in writing. In anything marked or
  submitted, keep the `ら`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|田中|たなか}さんはギターが{f|弾|ひ}____ますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ギターが{f|弾|ひ}<b>け</b>ますか。

<code>{f|弾|ひ}く</code> is an う-verb, so the final `く` becomes `け` and `る` is
added: {f|弾|ひ}ける → {f|弾|ひ}けます. The object takes `が` because the verb is
now potential.

</details>

**2.** {f|朝|あさ}{f|早|はや}く（{f|起|お}きられます・{f|起|お}きることができます）か、と{f|友|とも}だちに{f|聞|き}かれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|朝|あさ}{f|早|はや}く<b>{f|起|お}きられます</b>か。

A friend asking a casual question uses the potential form. The
`～ことができる` version is not wrong, but its written, official flavour would sit
strangely in ordinary conversation.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I couldn't sleep at all last night."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|昨夜|ゆうべ}はぜんぜん<b>{f|寝|ね}られなかった</b>。

<code>{f|寝|ね}る</code> is a る-verb, so the potential is
<code>{f|寝|ね}られる</code>, and the plain past negative is
<code>{f|寝|ね}られなかった</code>. In speech you will also hear
<code>{f|寝|ね}れなかった</code> - that is the ら{f|抜|ぬ}き form, fine to say and not to
write.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: バスが{f|来|こ}なくて、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わせられませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|会議|かいぎ}に<b>{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いませんでした</b>／<b>{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}えませんでした</b>

<code>{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}う</code> is an う-verb, so its potential is
<code>{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}える</code> - the `せ` came from applying the causative
pattern by mistake. In practice speakers usually just say
<code>{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いませんでした</code>, since arriving on time is an event
rather than a skill.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|部屋|へや}からは{f|花火|はなび}が{f|見|み}えます。
(b) この{f|部屋|へや}からは{f|花火|はなび}が{f|見|み}られます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports a plain physical fact: point your eyes out of that window and the
fireworks are in your field of view. <code>{f|見|み}える</code> is about the image
arriving, with no effort or permission involved.

(b) frames it as an opportunity - this room is one from which you *get to* watch
the fireworks. It fits a hotel describing what its guests are entitled to, and it
implies that not everyone has that access.

</details>
