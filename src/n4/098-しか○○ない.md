# ～しか～ない

**Meaning** · only ～; nothing but ～ - and it is less than you wanted

`しか` is a limiter that comes with a mood attached. It excludes everything outside
its noun, exactly as `だけ` does, but it also tells you the speaker finds the
remainder thin. The grammar is the strange part: the verb at the end **must** be
negative, even though the sentence is reporting something that does exist.
{f|千円|せんえん}しかありません means "I have a thousand yen" - said by somebody
who wishes they had more.

## Formation

`しか` goes on the noun; the negative goes on the predicate. Anything at all can
sit between them.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + しか + negative | {f|水|みず}しかありません |
| Number / counter | Counter + しか + negative | {f|二|ふた}つしか{f|残|のこ}っていない |
| After が／を／は | those particles are **dropped** | ❌お{f|金|かね}をしか → ✅お{f|金|かね}しか |
| After に／で／と／から | particle + しか | {f|駅|えき}でしか{f|買|か}えない |
| Polite | ～しか + ません／ないです | {f|一人|ひとり}しか{f|来|き}ませんでした |
| With material in between | ～しか … + negative | {f|少|すこ}ししか{f|食|た}べませんでした |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しか` and the negative are one unit with a gap in the middle.
If you write `しか` you have committed yourself to a negative ending, however many
words later it arrives.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the sentence is negative in **form** only. To answer
「お{f|金|かね}、ありますか」 with 「{f|百円|ひゃくえん}しかありません」 is to say
that you *do* have a hundred yen. English speakers hear the negative and conclude
the opposite.

</div>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral: {f|一人|ひとり}しかいない and {f|一人|ひとり}しかいません are the
same sentence at different politeness levels, and nothing about `しか` marks age or
gender. It is extremely common in ordinary speech.

The feeling it carries is **shortfall**. That can be regret
（{f|三時間|さんじかん}しか{f|寝|ね}ていない）, an apology
（{f|少|すこ}ししかご{f|用意|ようい}できませんでした, said while serving a
generous meal - a standard piece of Japanese modesty）, or a warning
（{f|時間|じかん}が{f|少|すこ}ししかありません）. What it cannot be is neutral. If
you have no feelings about the quantity, use `だけ`.

You will hear `だけしか` doubled up - これだけしかない. Strictly it says the same
thing twice, but it is standard colloquial Japanese and intensifies the
disappointment.

One more thing worth flagging early: <code>V(plain) + しかない</code>, as in
{f|行|い}くしかない, is a **different pattern** meaning "there's no choice but to
go". It looks identical and it is not the same construction; the contrast section
separates them.

## Contrast

### ～しか～ない vs ～だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same fact, opposite mood. `だけ` states a limit flatly;
<code>しか～ない</code> states it with regret. And only `しか` forces the verb
negative.

</div>

<pre>
{f|土曜日|どようび}<b>だけ</b>{f|営業|えいぎょう}しています。
We're open on Saturdays.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|土曜日|どようび}<b>しか</b>{f|営業|えいぎょう}してい<b>ません</b>。
We're only open on Saturdays, I'm afraid.
</pre>

A shop advertising its hours writes the first. A shop apologising for them says
the second. Advertisers do sometimes use `しか` on purpose - 
{f|当店|とうてん}でしか{f|買|か}えません, "you can't get it anywhere else" - but
notice what they are doing: they are turning the shopper's inability to buy it
elsewhere into the shortfall, not the shop's own range.

The mechanical difference matters too. `だけ` leaves the verb alone and can take
`が`/`を` on either side; `しか` swallows `が`, `を` and `は` entirely and rewrites
the ending.

### ～しか～ない vs a plain negative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a plain negative denies. <code>しか～ない</code> denies
everything **except** the thing it marks - so it quietly asserts that thing.

</div>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}{f|来|き}<b>ませんでした</b>。
One person didn't come.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}<b>しか</b>{f|来|き}<b>ませんでした</b>。
Only one person came.
</pre>

The first sentence has one absentee and an unknown number of attendees. The second
has one attendee and everybody else absent. The single word `しか` reverses which
side of the count the number is on, and this is the mistake that costs learners
marks in reading comprehension.

### Noun + しかない vs verb + しかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a **noun**, `しかない` means "there is only ～". After a
**plain-form verb**, it means "there is nothing to do but ～".

</div>

<pre>
バスは{f|一日|いちにち}に{f|三本|さんぼん}<b>しかありません</b>。
There are only three buses a day.
</pre>

<pre>
バスがないので、{f|歩|ある}く<b>しかありません</b>。
There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.
</pre>

The second is a decision made under pressure, not a count of anything, and it is
formally an N3 pattern. Keeping them apart is a matter of looking left: a noun or
a counter means quantity, a dictionary-form verb means resignation.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|千円|せんえん}しか<b>あります</b>。 → ✅ {f|千円|せんえん}しか<b>ありません</b>。
  `しか` cannot live with a positive predicate. The negative ending is part of the
  pattern, not a separate decision.

- ❌ お{f|金|かね}<b>を</b>しか{f|持|も}っていません。 → ✅ お{f|金|かね}<b>しか</b>{f|持|も}っていません。
  `を` is dropped in front of `しか`, as are `が` and `は`. Only `に`, `で`, `と`
  and `から` survive, and they come first: {f|駅|えき}でしか.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}<b>は</b>しか{f|知|し}りません。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>しか</b>{f|知|し}りません。
  Same rule, and this one is easy to miss because `は` feels like it belongs to the
  topic rather than to the noun. `しか` takes the slot.

- ❌ {f|二人|ふたり}しか{f|来|こ}なくて、うれしかったです。 → ✅ {f|二人|ふたり}だけ{f|来|き}て、うれしかったです。
  `しか` says the number disappointed you, so it fights with うれしい. When the
  small number is good news, `だけ` is the word.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|町|まち}には{f|病院|びょういん}が{f|一|ひと}つ____あり____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|町|まち}には{f|病院|びょういん}が{f|一|ひと}つ<b>しか</b>あり<b>ません</b>。

`しか` needs the negative ending to complete it. Note that {f|一|ひと}つ, the
counter, is where `しか` attaches - the pattern is very often built on a number,
because that is where shortfall is easiest to feel.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
{f|小|ちい}さいパーティーにしたかったので、{f|友|とも}だちを{f|五人|ごにん}（だけ・しか）{f|呼|よ}び（ました・ませんでした）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|友|とも}だちを{f|五人|ごにん}<b>だけ</b>{f|呼|よ}びました。

The first half says the small guest list was deliberate and wanted, so there is no
shortfall to complain about. `しか` would say five friends was disappointingly few,
which contradicts the reason given - and it would also force the ending negative:
{f|五人|ごにん}しか{f|呼|よ}びませんでした.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've only been to Japan once."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|日本|にほん}へは{f|一度|いちど}<b>しか</b>{f|行|い}ったことが<b>ありません</b>。

`しか` sits on {f|一度|いちど} and the ending goes negative - ありません, not
あります. The English "only once" already contains the regret, which is why `しか`
fits better here than {f|一度|いちど}だけ{f|行|い}ったことがあります, a flatter
statement of fact.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|三十分|さんじゅっぷん}をしか{f|勉強|べんきょう}しました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|三十分|さんじゅっぷん}<b>しか</b>{f|勉強|べんきょう}し<b>ませんでした</b>。

Two fixes. The `を` in front of `しか` is deleted - `しか` occupies that slot
itself. And the ending has to go negative: `しか` is only half a pattern without
it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|土曜日|どようび}だけ{f|休|やす}めます。
(b) {f|土曜日|どようび}しか{f|休|やす}めません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both mean that Saturday is your one free day.

(a) reports it. It could be an answer to "which day are you free?", and it carries
no complaint - you might even be pleased to have the day.

(b) complains about it. The negative ending and `しか` together say one day is not
enough, and the sentence naturally continues with something like
{f|疲|つか}れます. Choosing between them is choosing whether to sound content or
hard done by.

</details>
