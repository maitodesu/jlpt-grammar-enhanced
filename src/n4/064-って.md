# って

**Meaning** · the spoken shortcut - "they said…", "you know…", "the thing called…"

`って` is what `と` (and often <code>という</code>, and sometimes `は`) becomes in
relaxed speech. It quotes, it reports hearsay, it introduces a name, and it puts a
topic on the table with a little more energy than `は` does. One syllable, several
jobs - but they all come from the same place: `って` **presents a chunk of language**
and asks the listener to take it as a unit.

## Formation

Like `と`, `って` follows the **plain form**. Nouns and な-adjectives keep `だ` when
`って` is quoting; they drop it when `って` is marking a topic.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - quoting | plain form + って（+ {f|言|い}う／{f|聞|き}く／{f|思|おも}う） | {f|辞|や}める → {f|辞|や}めるって{f|言|い}ってた |
| Noun / な-adjective - quoting | + だ + って | {f|中止|ちゅうし} → {f|中止|ちゅうし}だって{f|聞|き}いた |
| Sentence-final - hearsay | plain form + って（falling tone） | {f|来|こ}ない → {f|来|こ}ないって |
| Direct quote | 「…」+ って | 「まだだ」 → 「まだだ」って{f|言|い}われた |
| Naming - replaces <code>という</code> | Noun + って + Noun | {f|林|はやし} → {f|林|はやし}さんって{f|人|ひと} |
| Topic - replaces `は` / <code>というのは</code> | Noun + って（no だ） | {f|敬語|けいご} → {f|敬語|けいご}って{f|難|むずか}しい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `って` inherits the `と` rule exactly: the clause in front of it is
**plain**, however polite you are being. ❌<code>{f|晴|は}れますって</code> →
✅<code>{f|晴|は}れるって</code>. The one exception is a direct quote in 「」, where you are
copying someone's actual words.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さん、{f|明日|あした}{f|来|こ}ない<b>って</b>。
Tanaka says he's not coming tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}、{f|中止|ちゅうし}だ<b>って</b>。
Apparently the meeting's off.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|漢字|かんじ}<b>って</b>{f|覚|おぼ}えるのが{f|大変|たいへん}だね。
Kanji really are a slog to memorise, aren't they.
</pre>

<pre>
さっき{f|先生|せんせい}が{f|今日|きょう}の{f|宿題|しゅくだい}はない<b>って</b>{f|言|い}ってたよ。
The teacher said a moment ago that there's no homework today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|林|はやし}さん<b>って</b>{f|人|ひと}から{f|電話|でんわ}がありましたよ。
There was a phone call from someone called Hayashi.
</pre>

<pre>
「もう{f|帰|かえ}りたい」<b>って</b>{f|顔|かお}をしているよ。
You've got an "I want to go home already" look on your face.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|妹|いもうと}に{f|貸|か}した{f|本|ほん}、まだ{f|読|よ}んでない<b>って</b>{f|言|い}うんだけど、もう{f|一|いっ}か{f|月|げつ}{f|経|た}つよ。
My sister says she still hasn't read the book I lent her - and it's been a month.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}{f|留学|りゅうがく}する<b>って</b>{f|本当|ほんとう}？いつ{f|決|き}めたの？
Is it true you're studying abroad next year? When did you decide?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`って` is **spoken casual and nothing else**. It belongs in conversation with friends,
family and people you are on plain-form terms with. It does not belong in an email to
a client, a report, an essay, or a sentence you are otherwise ending in `ます`. The
neutral replacements are `と` for quoting and <code>そうです</code>／
<code>とのことです</code> for hearsay.

Intonation does a lot of work. Falling `って` at the end of a sentence is hearsay - 
{f|雨|あめ}になるって。 Rising `って`? is a request for confirmation, close to "wait,
did you say…?" - {f|明日|あした}{f|休|やす}みだって？ Both are the same particle.

Two extensions you will hear constantly and will not find in a textbook:
`っていうか` at the head of a sentence, which retracts what you just said and
restarts ("or rather…"), and `ってば`, which repeats something impatiently - 
だから{f|違|ちが}うってば。 The second is distinctly childish or intimate; it is not a
neutral emphasis marker.

There is a semi-polite hearsay form, `～んですって`, which keeps a `です` on the end:
となりの{f|店|みせ}、{f|閉|し}めるんですって。 It is polite in form but chatty in feel,
and in modern Tokyo speech it leans female. `～だってです` is not a form at all.

## Contrast

### って vs と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same grammar, different register: `って` is `と` in a T-shirt. But
`って` also covers jobs `と` cannot do, namely naming and topic-marking.

</div>

<pre>
{f|課長|かちょう}は{f|出席|しゅっせき}できない<b>と</b>{f|言|い}っていました。
The section chief said he can't attend.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|課長|かちょう}、{f|出席|しゅっせき}できない<b>って</b>。
The boss says he can't make it.
</pre>

Both report the same fact. The first is what you write in a message to the team; the
second is what you say over your shoulder to the colleague next to you. Where the two
part company is self-introduction and any fixed formula:
{f|田中|たなか}と{f|申|もう}します is the set phrase, and `って` there is simply wrong.

### topic って vs は

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` sets a topic quietly. `って` sets a topic and signals that
you are about to say something **about the category itself**, usually with a reaction
attached.

</div>

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}の{f|夏|なつ}<b>は</b>{f|暑|あつ}いです。
Summers in Japan are hot.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}の{f|夏|なつ}<b>って</b>{f|暑|あつ}いね。
Japanese summers are hot, aren't they.
</pre>

The `って` version reads as a comment brought up fresh, and it pairs naturally with
`ね`, `よ` and evaluative adjectives. It is close to
<code>{f|日本|にほん}の{f|夏|なつ}というのは</code>, which is exactly what it is a
contraction of - and that is why it can also mean "what is…?":
「バイト」<b>って</b>{f|何|なに}？

### hearsay って vs ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical content, opposite registers. `って` is what you say;
<code>そうです</code> is what you write.

</div>

<pre>
{f|山田|やまだ}さん、{f|来月|らいげつ}{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>って</b>。
Yamada's moving house next month, apparently.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|来月|らいげつ}{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>そうです</b>。
I hear that Yamada is moving house next month.
</pre>

One practical difference: `って` easily carries a named source in front of it - 
{f|母|はは}が{f|寒|さむ}くなるって{f|言|い}ってた - while <code>そうだ</code> usually leaves
the source vague or marks it with <code>によると</code>. If you want to say who told
you, `って` is the more natural spoken frame.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|田中|たなか}<b>って{f|申|もう}します</b>。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}は{f|田中|たなか}<b>と{f|申|もう}します</b>。
  Self-introduction is a fixed humble formula. `って` cannot be dropped into a phrase
  that is formal by definition.

- ❌ {f|天気予報|てんきよほう}で{f|明日|あした}は<b>{f|晴|は}れますって</b>{f|言|い}ってた。 → ✅ …<b>{f|晴|は}れるって</b>{f|言|い}ってた。
  An indirect quote takes the plain form, exactly as with `と`. `ます` only survives
  inside 「」.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}<b>だってです</b>。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}<b>だそうです</b>。
  You cannot make `って` polite by bolting `です` on. Switch to
  <code>そうです</code> or <code>とのことです</code>.

- ❌ (in a written report) {f|調査|ちょうさ}の{f|結果|けっか}、{f|問題|もんだい}はなかった<b>って</b>。 → ✅ …{f|問題|もんだい}はなかった<b>とのことです</b>。
  `って` is a spoken form. In writing it reads as though the report were a text
  message.

## Practice

**1.** {f|友達|ともだち}に：「{f|山田|やまだ}さん、{f|今日|きょう}{f|来|こ}られない____。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|山田|やまだ}さん、{f|今日|きょう}{f|来|こ}られない<b>って</b>。

Sentence-final `って` after a plain form reports what you were told, with the source
left implicit. Said with a falling tone it means "apparently", not "did you say?".

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option for an email to a client: {f|納期|のうき}は{f|来週|らいしゅう}（だって・だそうです）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|納期|のうき}は{f|来週|らいしゅう}<b>だそうです</b>。

`って` is casual spoken Japanese and never appears in business writing. The hearsay
meaning is unchanged; only the register is fixed.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually: "Someone called Kimura came looking for you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|木村|きむら}さん<b>って{f|人|ひと}</b>が{f|来|き}たよ。

`って` before a noun is the contraction of <code>という</code> and is the everyday way
of naming someone the listener may not know. <code>{f|木村|きむら}さんという{f|人|ひと}</code>
is the same thing in a neutral register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼|かれ}は{f|新|あたら}しい{f|仕事|しごと}を{f|探|さが}していますって{f|言|い}ってた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|新|あたら}しい{f|仕事|しごと}を<b>{f|探|さが}しているって</b>{f|言|い}ってた。

The quoted clause must be plain. `って` is only a casual `と`, so it obeys the same
restriction: nothing in ます-form immediately in front of it.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|一人暮|ひとりぐ}らしはお{f|金|かね}がかかります。
(b) {f|一人暮|ひとりぐ}らし<b>って</b>お{f|金|かね}がかかるよね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral, polite statement of fact - the kind of line that could open a
paragraph in an article about living costs.

(b) raises "living alone" as a topic the two of you both know something about, and
adds a shared reaction with `よね`. `って` marks the category as newly put on the
table and invites agreement; it is conversation, not exposition.

</details>
