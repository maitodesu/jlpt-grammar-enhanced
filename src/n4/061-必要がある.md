# ～必要がある

**Meaning** · there is a need to do it; it is necessary to do it

Read {f|必要|ひつよう}. <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}</code> is a noun meaning *necessity*, and
`ある` is the ordinary verb of existence - so the pattern literally says *a need to
do X exists*. That is why it feels cooler than the obligation forms: it does not
push anybody, it states a fact about the situation.

## Formation

The verb in front stays in the **dictionary form**. Nouns join with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + {f|必要|ひつよう}がある | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}つ{f|必要|ひつよう}がある |
| Verb (する) | dictionary form + {f|必要|ひつよう}がある | {f|登録|とうろく}する → {f|登録|とうろく}する{f|必要|ひつよう}がある |
| Noun | noun + の + {f|必要|ひつよう}がある | {f|修理|しゅうり} → {f|修理|しゅうり}の{f|必要|ひつよう}がある |
| Negative | {f|必要|ひつよう}はない／{f|必要|ひつよう}がない | {f|待|ま}つ{f|必要|ひつよう}はない |
| Polite | {f|必要|ひつよう}があります／ありません | {f|待|ま}つ{f|必要|ひつよう}があります |
| Past | {f|必要|ひつよう}があった／なかった | {f|待|ま}つ{f|必要|ひつよう}はなかった |
| Question | {f|必要|ひつよう}がありますか | {f|待|ま}つ{f|必要|ひつよう}がありますか |
| Formal negative | {f|必要|ひつよう}はございません | {f|待|ま}つ{f|必要|ひつよう}はございません |
| い-adjective / な-adjective | not available | {f|静|しず}かだ → ❌{f|静|しず}かな{f|必要|ひつよう}がある |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative flips the particle. Affirmative
<code>{f|必要|ひつよう}が<b>ある</b></code>, negative <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}は<b>ない</b></code>. The
`は` is doing what `は` always does with a negative: marking the thing being denied.
<code>{f|必要|ひつよう}がない</code> is possible, but `は` is the everyday choice.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negation goes on `ある`, never on the verb in front. "You don't need
to go" is <code>{f|行|い}く{f|必要|ひつよう}はない</code>, not
❌<code>{f|行|い}かない{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</code>, which would mean something like "there
is a need for you not to go."

</div>

## Examples

<pre>

You need to show your passport.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|急|いそ}ぐ<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}はありません</b>。
There's no need to hurry.
</pre>

<pre>
もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|確認|かくにん}する<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</b>。
We need to check it one more time.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|書類|しょるい}は{f|翻訳|ほんやく}の<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}がありますか</b>。
Does this document need translating?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|心配|しんぱい}する<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}はない</b>よ。{f|全部|ぜんぶ}うまくいっているから。

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

Members do not need to write their address at reception.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>～{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</code> is the **objective** member of the family. It describes
a requirement as a property of the situation, with nobody's authority behind it,
which makes it the default in manuals, instructions, application guidance, business
email and academic writing. If you have ever read a Japanese form, you have read
this pattern.

That neutrality has a practical use: it lets you tell someone what has to happen
without telling *them* what to do. <code>ご{f|確認|かくにん}いただく{f|必要|ひつよう}がございます</code> is still direct, but
<code>{f|三月|さんがつ}までに{f|提出|ていしゅつ}する{f|必要|ひつよう}があります</code> said
about a shared deadline points at the deadline, not at the listener.

The flip side is that it sounds stiff about small personal errands. Nobody says
<code>{f|今日|きょう}は{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}をする{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</code> to a
friend - that is <code>{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}{f|行|い}かないと</code> territory. Using the
formal pattern for a trip to the supermarket sounds like reading from a report.

The negative is the half you will use most, and it is unusually warm for such a
formal pattern. <code>{f|謝|あやま}る{f|必要|ひつよう}はないよ</code>「you don't need to
apologise」 and <code>{f|気|き}にする{f|必要|ひつよう}はありません</code>「there's no need to
worry about it」 are everyday reassurances. In casual speech `が` drops out
altogether: <code>そんなに{f|急|いそ}ぐ{f|必要|ひつよう}ない</code>.

At the formal end, `ある` is replaced by its humble-polite equivalents:
<code>{f|必要|ひつよう}がございます</code> and <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}はございません</code>, which
is what shop staff and official notices use.

## Contrast

### ～{f|必要|ひつよう}がある vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</code> reports that a need exists;
`なければならない` says the alternative is unacceptable. The first describes, the
second compels.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|薬|くすり}は{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|飲|の}む<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}があります</b>。
This medicine needs to be taken every day.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|薬|くすり}は{f|毎日|まいにち}<b>{f|飲|の}まなければなりません</b>。
You must take this medicine every day.
</pre>

Both are correct and a doctor might say either. The first sounds like an
explanation of how the treatment works; the second sounds like an instruction being
issued to you. When the requirement comes from a rule with force behind it - a law,
a contract, a school regulation - `なければならない` is the stronger fit. When it
comes from the nature of the task, <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</code> is.

There is also a grammatical difference worth noticing: `なければならない` attaches to
a *negative* stem, while <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</code> attaches to a plain dictionary
form. That makes the second much easier to build under time pressure.

### ～{f|必要|ひつよう}はない vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくてもいい` grants an exemption; <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}はない</code>
denies that a requirement ever existed.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は<b>{f|来|こ}なくてもいいですよ</b>。
You don't have to come in tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は<b>{f|来|く}る{f|必要|ひつよう}はありません</b>。
There is no need for you to come in tomorrow.
</pre>

The first is a person releasing another person, which is why it needs `よ` or a
smile to sound kind. The second is a statement about the schedule, and because it
does not position the speaker as the one granting permission, it is often the safer
thing to say upward or to a stranger.

In their negative-reassurance use they come very close:
<code>{f|心配|しんぱい}しなくてもいい</code> and
<code>{f|心配|しんぱい}する{f|必要|ひつよう}はない</code> are near-synonyms, the second
slightly firmer, as if arguing the worry away rather than waving it off.

### ～{f|必要|ひつよう}がある vs ～が{f|必要|ひつよう}だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>する{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</code> needs an **action**;
<code>が{f|必要|ひつよう}だ</code> needs a **thing**.

</div>

<pre>
ここで{f|靴|くつ}を<b>{f|脱|ぬ}ぐ{f|必要|ひつよう}があります</b>。
You need to take your shoes off here.
</pre>

<pre>
ここでは{f|上|うわ}ばきが<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}です</b>。
You need indoor shoes here.
</pre>

The two are often interchangeable in translation and never in grammar. A verb
before <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}</code> requires the dictionary form and the `が…ある` frame; a
noun before it takes `が` and the plain な-adjective predicate
<code>{f|必要|ひつよう}だ／です</code>. A noun can also be forced into the `ある` frame with
`の` - <code>{f|修理|しゅうり}の{f|必要|ひつよう}がある</code> - which sounds distinctly more
formal than <code>{f|修理|しゅうり}が{f|必要|ひつよう}だ</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|行|い}き<b>ます</b>{f|必要|ひつよう}があります。 → ✅ {f|行|い}く{f|必要|ひつよう}があります。
  The verb in front of <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}</code> is plain and non-past. `ます` cannot appear
  inside the sentence; politeness goes on `あります` at the end.

- ❌ {f|行|い}か<b>ない</b>{f|必要|ひつよう}があります。 → ✅ {f|行|い}く{f|必要|ひつよう}は<b>ありません</b>。
  To say there is no need, negate `ある`, not the verb. Negating the verb creates a
  need *not* to go, which is a different and much rarer idea.

- ❌ ビザ{f|必要|ひつよう}があります。 → ✅ ビザ<b>の</b>{f|必要|ひつよう}があります。／ビザ<b>が</b>{f|必要|ひつよう}です。
  A noun cannot sit straight against <code>{f|必要|ひつよう}</code>. Either link it with `の` or
  switch to the <code>が{f|必要|ひつよう}だ</code> pattern.

- ❌ {f|部屋|へや}が{f|静|しず}か<b>な</b>{f|必要|ひつよう}があります。 → ✅ {f|部屋|へや}は{f|静|しず}か<b>でなければなりません</b>。
  Adjectives do not take this pattern at all. Requirements about qualities go
  through the obligation forms.

- ❌（to a friend）そろそろ{f|帰|かえ}る{f|必要|ひつよう}がある。 → ✅ そろそろ{f|帰|かえ}らないと。
  Not wrong, just wrongly dressed. For your own small obligations in conversation,
  the `ないと` / `なきゃ` end of the family is what people actually use.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|申込書|もうしこみしょ}には、はんこを{f|押|お}す____ありますか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

はんこを{f|押|お}す<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}が</b>ありますか。

The verb stays in the dictionary form, and the question is about whether a
requirement exists - exactly what this pattern is for. On a form, it is more
natural than asking <code>{f|押|お}さなくてもいいですか</code>.

</details>

**2.** {f|課長|かちょう}に（{f|報告|ほうこく}する{f|必要|ひつよう}があります・{f|報告|ほうこく}しなきゃ）と{f|会議|かいぎ}で{f|言|い}った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|報告|ほうこく}する<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}があります</b>。

A meeting is a formal setting and the sentence is stating what the situation
requires, not muttering a personal chore. `なきゃ` is a casual contraction and would
sound out of place in front of colleagues.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, as a line of written guidance: "Applicants need to submit two photographs."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|申込者|もうしこみしゃ}は{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|二枚|にまい}{f|提出|ていしゅつ}する<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}があります</b>。

Written guidance wants the impersonal pattern: it states what the process requires
without addressing anyone. <code>{f|提出|ていしゅつ}しなければならない</code> would also fit
a rulebook, but it sounds more like a command than an instruction sheet.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|明日|あした}は{f|来|こ}ない{f|必要|ひつよう}がありません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|来|く}る<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}はありません</b>。

Two things went wrong. The verb must be affirmative and in the dictionary form, and
the negation belongs on `ある`. Note also the particle flip: negatives of this
pattern normally take `は`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|返事|へんじ}を{f|書|か}く<b>{f|必要|ひつよう}はありません</b>。
(b) {f|返事|へんじ}を<b>{f|書|か}かなくてもいいです</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states that the process does not call for a reply. The speaker is describing
how things work, which makes it neutral and slightly formal - the wording of a
notice or of someone explaining a procedure.

(b) tells the listener they are released from writing one. It implies the speaker
is in a position to decide, so it sounds more personal and, depending on who is
speaking to whom, either kind or presumptuous.

</details>
