# ～てやる

**Meaning** · do something for someone below you - and, in a second life, *I'll show them*: a declaration of defiant intent

`～てやる` is the bottom rung of the `さしあげる／あげる／やる` ladder you met at
N5, now carrying an action instead of an object. It points **downward**: to
animals, plants, small children, your own younger family. Alongside that, it has a
completely separate use with no beneficiary at all, where it means "just watch me
do it" - and that second use is far more common in real speech than textbooks let
on.

## Formation

Attach `やる` to the て-form. `やる` is a regular う-verb: やります, やった,
やらない, やろう.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (favour, downward) | て-form + やる | {f|買|か}う → {f|買|か}ってやる |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + やります | {f|読|よ}む → {f|読|よ}んでやります |
| Verb (offer / resolve) | て-form + やろう | {f|開|あ}ける → {f|開|あ}けてやろう |
| Verb (resolve, plain) | て-form + やる（no `に` phrase） | {f|勝|か}つ → {f|勝|か}ってやる |
| Contracted, very casual | て-form + たる | {f|買|か}ってやる → {f|買|か}ったる |

In the favour sense the particles come from the underlying verb, exactly as with
`～てあげる`: {f|弟|おとうと}<b>に</b>{f|説明|せつめい}してやる,
{f|犬|いぬ}<b>を</b>{f|散歩|さんぽ}に{f|連|つ}れて{f|行|い}ってやる.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the sentence has a `に` phrase naming who benefits, it is
the favour `～てやる`. If it has no beneficiary and the verb is about the speaker's
own achievement, it is the resolve `～てやる`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|犬|いぬ}を{f|散歩|さんぽ}に<b>{f|連|つ}れて{f|行|い}ってやった</b>。
I took the dog for a walk.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|猫|ねこ}が{f|外|そと}に{f|出|で}たがっているから、{f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|開|あ}けてやろう</b>。
The cat wants to go out, so I'll open the window for her.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}が{f|泣|な}いていたので、アイスを<b>{f|買|か}ってやった</b>。
My little brother was crying, so I bought him an ice cream.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもたちが{f|寝|ね}る{f|前|まえ}に、{f|毎晩|まいばん}{f|絵本|えほん}を<b>{f|読|よ}んでやります</b>。

</pre>

<pre>

This time I'm definitely going to win.
</pre>

If my son gets into university, I'm thinking I'll buy him whatever bike he wants.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**The favour sense is rough, and it is age- and gender-marked.** `やる` for people
was once the ordinary form; it now sounds blunt, and it is noticeably more common
in men's speech and in older speakers. A father saying
{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|見|み}てやる about his own child is completely normal and
even affectionate. The same sentence to a colleague's child would be presumptuous.

Toward animals and plants it is entirely neutral - 
{f|花|はな}に{f|水|みず}をやる is the standard phrase, though younger speakers
increasingly say あげる there too, and some older speakers find *that* precious.
Both are heard; neither will get you into trouble.

Where you must not use it: **anyone senior, anyone outside your circle, and
anyone you are being polite to.** `～てやります` exists but does not fix the
problem, because the rudeness lives in `やる`, not in the politeness ending. There
is no upward version - the ladder goes やる → あげる → さしあげる, and the top rung
has its own difficulties (see `～てあげる`, which is presumptuous for a different
reason).

**The resolve sense** is the one you will hear in dramas, sports and complaints.
It attaches to a verb about the speaker's own action, has no beneficiary, and
means roughly "I'm going to do it, and let that be noted." The emotional colour
comes from the same source as the favour sense: you are casting your own act as
something imposed on the world.

- {f|絶対|ぜったい}に{f|合格|ごうかく}してやる。 - I *will* pass.
- こんな{f|仕事|しごと}、{f|辞|や}めてやる。 - I quit. (defiant)
- {f|文句|もんく}を{f|言|い}ってやった。 - I gave them a piece of my mind.

That last type shades into threat: {f|殴|なぐ}ってやる is "I'll hit you", not a
kindness. Tone and context do all the disambiguating, and it is almost always
obvious.

Casually - and especially in Kansai speech - `～てやる` contracts to `～たる`:
{f|買|か}ってやる → {f|買|か}ったる, {f|貸|か}してやる → {f|貸|か}したる. You will
hear it constantly in comedy and in the west of the country; do not write it, and
do not use it in an exam.

<div class="note">

**Note** - `～てやる` is also how you tell someone to do something for a third
party who is below them: {f|犬|いぬ}にえさを<b>やってください</b>,
{f|弟|おとうと}に{f|教|おし}えて<b>やって</b>ください. The polite frame is on the
outside and the downward frame on the inside, and this is normal, not
contradictory.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～てやる vs ～てあげる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same direction, different altitude. `～てあげる` is for
equals and outsiders; `～てやる` is for those clearly below you, and for animals.

</div>

<pre>
{f|娘|むすめ}の{f|髪|かみ}を<b>{f|結|むす}んでやった</b>。
I tied my daughter's hair for her.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|同僚|どうりょう}の{f|仕事|しごと}を<b>{f|引|ひ}き{f|受|う}けてあげた</b>。
I took on a colleague's work for them.
</pre>

Swap those and both sentences go wrong in opposite ways: `あげる` about your own
small child sounds oddly formal, as if she were a guest; `やる` about a colleague
sounds contemptuous. When you are not sure of the altitude, `あげる` is the safer
error - but remember that neither should be said *to* the beneficiary's face.

### ～てやる (resolve) vs ～てみせる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both declare determination. `～てやる` aims the declaration at
the world; `～てみせる` aims it at an audience: *I'll show you*.

</div>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}は{f|一位|いちい}を<b>{f|取|と}ってやる</b>。
Next year I'm taking first place.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}は{f|一位|いちい}を<b>{f|取|と}ってみせる</b>。
Next year I'll show you I can take first place.
</pre>

`～てみせる` promises a demonstration to someone who doubts you; `～てやる` is
closer to spite or grit and does not need a doubter. They combine freely - 
{f|必|かなら}ず{f|勝|か}ってみせてやる is heavy-handed but grammatical.

### ～てやる vs ～てさしあげる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - these are the two ends of one vertical scale, and in modern
speech **both ends are avoided**. The safe middle is `～てあげる`, or no giving
verb at all.

</div>

<pre>
{f|部長|ぶちょう}の{f|奥|おく}さまを{f|駅|えき}まで<b>お{f|送|おく}りしました</b>。
I saw the department head's wife to the station.
</pre>

`さしあげる` is grammatically the humble upward form, but announcing your own
kindness to a superior is presumptuous no matter how humble the verb, so speakers
use the お + stem + します frame instead. At the other end, `やる` is fine within
the family and rude outside it. The lesson is the same in both directions: the
higher the stakes, the less Japanese wants you to name the favour.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|先輩|せんぱい}のコピーを{f|取|と}って<b>やりました</b>。 → ✅ {f|先輩|せんぱい}のコピーを{f|取|と}って<b>おきました</b>。
  `やる` points downward. Used about a senior it is close to insulting, and the
  polite ending does not soften it.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|荷物|にもつ}を{f|持|も}って<b>やりましょうか</b>。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、お{f|荷物|にもつ}を<b>お{f|持|も}ちしましょうか</b>。
  Upward offers use the humble お + ます-stem + します frame, with no giving verb.

- ❌ {f|弟|おとうと}を{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|見|み}てやった。 → ✅ {f|弟|おとうと}の{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|見|み}てやった。
  `やる` does not assign particles. The verb acts on the homework, so the owner
  takes `の` and only one `を` survives.

- ❌ {f|絶対|ぜったい}に{f|合格|ごうかく}して<b>やります</b>。（in a formal speech）→ ✅ {f|絶対|ぜったい}に{f|合格|ごうかく}<b>したい</b>と{f|思|おも}っています。
  The resolve sense is emotional and confrontational. It does not belong in
  formal or public register even with `ます` on the end.

- ❌ {f|母|はは}が{f|私|わたし}にセーターを{f|編|あ}んで<b>やりました</b>。 → ✅ {f|母|はは}が{f|私|わたし}にセーターを{f|編|あ}んで<b>くれました</b>。
  `やる` sends the favour away from the speaker, so it can never land on the
  speaker. Toward you it is always `くれる`, however junior the doer.

## Practice

**1.** {f|息子|むすこ}が{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}に{f|乗|の}れないので、{f|後|うし}ろを{f|持|も}っ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|後|うし}ろを{f|持|も}っ<b>てやった</b>。（or {f|持|も}っ<b>てあげた</b>）

The beneficiary is the speaker's own young child, which is `やる` territory:
downward, inside the family, and affectionate rather than rude. `あげた` is also
perfectly acceptable and is what many younger parents say.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|課長|かちょう}に{f|資料|しりょう}を{f|届|とど}けて（やりました・{f|差|さ}し{f|上|あ}げました・きました）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|課長|かちょう}に{f|資料|しりょう}を{f|届|とど}けて<b>きました</b>。

`やる` is rude upward, and `さしあげる`, though humble in form, announces your own
kindness to a superior. The natural sentence drops the giving verb altogether - 
here <code>{f|届|とど}けてきました</code>, "I went and delivered it."

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll never give up. I'm going to finish this."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|絶対|ぜったい}にあきらめない。<b>やり{f|遂|と}げてやる</b>。（or <b>{f|最後|さいご}までやってやる</b>）

This is the resolve `～てやる`: no beneficiary, the speaker's own action, and a
defiant edge. In polite speech you would soften it to
<code>やり{f|遂|と}げたいと{f|思|おも}います</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|田中|たなか}さんが{f|忙|いそが}しそうだったので、{f|仕事|しごと}を{f|手伝|てつだ}ってやりました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|田中|たなか}さんが{f|忙|いそが}しそうだったので、{f|仕事|しごと}を{f|手伝|てつだ}い<b>ました</b>。

Tanaka is a peer, not someone below you, so `やる` is out of place and sounds
scornful. <code>{f|手伝|てつだ}ってあげました</code> is possible when narrating to a third party, but the
plainest version, with no giving verb, is what you would actually say.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|必|かなら}ず{f|終|お}わらせます。
(b) {f|必|かなら}ず{f|終|お}わらせてやる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain polite commitment: the work will be finished. It is what you say to
a manager, and it carries no emotion beyond reliability.

(b) is the resolve `～てやる`. It adds defiance - against a deadline, a doubter, or
the situation itself - and it is plain, emphatic and slightly aggressive. You mutter
it to yourself or say it among friends; saying it to your boss would sound like you
were picking a fight.

</details>
