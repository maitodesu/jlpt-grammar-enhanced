# ～なさい

**Meaning** · a firm instruction given downward - "do it", said with authority
rather than anger

`～なさい` is the imperative of `なさる`, the honorific verb for "do". The
politeness in its ancestry has drained away, and what is left is a command that is
softer in tone than the bare {f|命令形|めいれいけい} but strictly one-directional:
parent to child, teacher to student, exam paper to candidate. You cannot aim it
upwards, and pointing it at an adult equal is condescending.

## Formation

Attach `なさい` to the <b>ます-stem</b>. If you can build {f|書|か}きます, you can build
{f|書|か}きなさい.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem (final う-row kana → い-row kana) + なさい | {f|書|か}く → {f|書|か}きなさい |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + なさい | {f|食|た}べる → {f|食|た}べなさい |
| Verb (する) | し + なさい | {f|勉強|べんきょう}する → {f|勉強|べんきょう}しなさい |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | stem き, + なさい | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}なさい |

Every う-verb ending:

| Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ～う | う → い | {f|買|か}う → {f|買|か}いなさい |
| ～く | く → き | {f|聞|き}く → {f|聞|き}きなさい |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → ぎ | {f|急|いそ}ぐ → {f|急|いそ}ぎなさい |
| ～す | す → し | {f|出|だ}す → {f|出|だ}しなさい |
| ～つ | つ → ち | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}ちなさい |
| ～ぬ | ぬ → に | {f|死|し}ぬ → {f|死|し}になさい |
| ～ぶ | ぶ → び | {f|呼|よ}ぶ → {f|呼|よ}びなさい |
| ～む | む → み | {f|読|よ}む → {f|読|よ}みなさい |
| ～る | る → り | {f|座|すわ}る → {f|座|すわ}りなさい |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - three different stems of {f|来|く}る turn up in this chapter and
they are easy to swap by accident. <b>{f|来|き}なさい</b> (ます-stem),
<b>{f|来|こ}い</b> (imperative), <b>{f|来|こ}られる／{f|来|こ}させる</b> (potential,
passive, causative). Only `なさい` uses き.

</div>

| Related forms | Example |
|---|---|
| Contracted, casual | ます-stem + な - {f|食|た}べな, {f|行|い}きな |
| Negative | none. Use ～ないようにしなさい or ～てはいけません |
| Fossilised in fixed phrases | お{f|休|やす}みなさい, お{f|帰|かえ}りなさい, ごめんなさい |

## Nuance & register

The politeness of `～なさい` is a **historical** fact, not a usable one. Because it
descends from the honorific `なさる`, it sounds educated and controlled rather than
crude - but its social direction is fixed downward. A teacher, a parent, an older
sibling or a coach can use it. To a colleague it is patronising; to a customer or a
superior it is unthinkable.

Three settings where it is completely unmarked:

- **Adults to children.** This is its home. {f|早|はや}く{f|起|お}きなさい,
  ちゃんと{f|挨拶|あいさつ}しなさい.
- **Written instructions to a reader.** Textbook exercises and exam papers use it
  constantly: {f|正|ただ}しいものを{f|選|えら}びなさい,
  {f|理由|りゆう}を{f|書|か}きなさい. Here it addresses nobody in particular and
  carries no attitude.
- **Set expressions.** お{f|休|やす}みなさい and お{f|帰|かえ}りなさい are frozen
  `～なさい` forms that stopped being commands centuries ago. Nobody hears them as
  orders.

<b>The contracted `～な`.</b> Dropping `さい` gives a much warmer, more casual order:
{f|食|た}べな, {f|行|い}きな, {f|見|み}な, もう{f|帰|かえ}りな. It is friendly - 
closer to English "go on, eat up" - and is common from older to younger and among
close friends. It leans masculine in some regions but is by no means exclusively
so. Never use it in writing.

**There is no negative.** ❌{f|食|た}べないなさい does not exist. To forbid
something at the same level of authority you switch construction:
{f|食|た}べてはいけません, {f|食|た}べないようにしなさい, or - much blunter - the
prohibitive {f|食|た}べるな.

<b>お～なさい</b> softens it a little further: お{f|入|はい}りなさい,
お{f|食|た}べなさい. This sounds distinctly old-fashioned and grandmotherly in
modern Japanese, and you will meet it more in fiction than in life.

## Contrast

### ～なさい vs {f|命令形|めいれいけい}

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same direction, different heat. `～なさい` is the order of
someone with legitimate authority; the {f|命令形|めいれいけい} is the order of
someone applying force.

</div>

<pre>
{f|自分|じぶん}の{f|部屋|へや}を<b>{f|片付|かたづ}けなさい</b>。
Tidy your room.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|自分|じぶん}の{f|部屋|へや}を<b>{f|片付|かたづ}けろ</b>。
Tidy your damn room.
</pre>

A parent says the first every week and the second when they have snapped. There
is also a difference in who says them: the {f|命令形|めいれいけい} is strongly
male-coded in conversation, while `～なさい` is used freely by everyone.

Note too that only `～なさい` is at home in print. A worksheet says
{f|計算|けいさん}しなさい; a road sign says {f|止|と}まれ. Neither would trade
places.

### ～なさい vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `～てください` **asks**, and can point in any direction.
`～なさい` **tells**, and only points down.

</div>

<pre>
すみません、こちらに<b>{f|座|すわ}ってください</b>。
Please have a seat over here.
</pre>

<pre>
そこに<b>{f|座|すわ}りなさい</b>。
Sit down there. (teacher to pupil)
</pre>

Learners often assume that because `～なさい` looks longer and comes from an
honorific verb, it must be politer than `～てください`. It is not. `～てください` is
the neutral all-purpose form; `～なさい` is what you use precisely when you are
*not* asking. Said to a guest, {f|座|すわ}りなさい would be startling - 
お{f|座|すわ}りください is the polite version.

### {f|食|た}べな vs {f|食|た}べるな

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same `な` after two different stems means opposite things.
<b>ます-stem + な</b> = do it. <b>Dictionary form + な</b> = don't do it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|冷|さ}めないうちに<b>{f|食|た}べな</b>。
Go on, eat it while it's hot.
</pre>

<pre>
まだ<b>{f|食|た}べるな</b>。
Don't eat it yet.
</pre>

The only visible difference is the `る`. The first is `～なさい` with its tail
bitten off and is friendly; the second is the prohibitive and is as blunt as the
{f|命令形|めいれいけい}. Intonation helps in speech - the prohibitive `な` is
clipped and stressed - but the stem is the reliable signal.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|来|こ}なさい → ✅ <b>{f|来|き}なさい</b>
  `なさい` attaches to the ます-stem, and the ます-stem of {f|来|く}る is
  {f|来|き}. The `こ` stem belongs to {f|来|こ}い, {f|来|こ}られる and
  {f|来|こ}させる.

- ❌ {f|食|た}べるなさい → ✅ <b>{f|食|た}べなさい</b>
  Never attach `なさい` to the dictionary form. {f|食|た}べます → {f|食|た}べ +
  なさい.

- ❌ するなさい → ✅ <b>しなさい</b>
  The ます-stem of する is し.

- ❌ {f|廊下|ろうか}を{f|走|はし}らないなさい。 → ✅ {f|廊下|ろうか}を{f|走|はし}っ<b>てはいけません</b>／{f|走|はし}ら<b>ないようにしなさい</b>。
  `～なさい` has no negative form. You have to change construction.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、ここに{f|名前|なまえ}を{f|書|か}きなさい。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、ここにお{f|名前|なまえ}を<b>お{f|書|か}きください</b>。
  Correctly formed and socially impossible. `～なさい` only ever points downward.

## Practice

**1.** {f|時間|じかん}がありませんよ。{f|早|はや}く{f|準備|じゅんび}____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|早|はや}く{f|準備|じゅんび}<b>しなさい</b>。

<code>{f|準備|じゅんび}する</code> is a する-verb, and the ます-stem of する is `し`,
so the form is {f|準備|じゅんび}しなさい. ❌{f|準備|じゅんび}するなさい applies
`なさい` to the dictionary form, which never happens.

</details>

**2.** （{f|先生|せんせい}が{f|学生|がくせい}に）この{f|問題|もんだい}をもう{f|一度|いちど}（{f|考|かんが}えなさい・{f|考|かんが}えてください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|問題|もんだい}をもう{f|一度|いちど}<b>{f|考|かんが}えなさい</b>。

Teacher to student is exactly the downward relationship `～なさい` is for.
<code>{f|考|かんが}えてください</code> is not wrong, but it frames the instruction
as a request between equals, which is a different classroom.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Get up. You'll be late for school."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|起|お}きなさい</b>。{f|学校|がっこう}に{f|遅|おく}れますよ。

<code>{f|起|お}きる</code> is a る-verb, so drop `る` and add `なさい`. This is the
classic parent-to-child use, and `なさい` is warmer here than
{f|起|お}きろ without being a request.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|危|あぶ}ないから、{f|道|みち}で{f|遊|あそ}ばないなさい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|危|あぶ}ないから、{f|道|みち}で<b>{f|遊|あそ}んではいけません</b>（or {f|遊|あそ}ば<b>ないようにしなさい</b>）。

`～なさい` has no negative. To forbid something you either use
<code>～てはいけません</code>, or keep `なさい` on a positive verb by routing the
negative through <code>～ないようにする</code>. The bluntest option is the
prohibitive {f|遊|あそ}ぶな.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう{f|帰|かえ}りなさい。
(b) もう{f|帰|かえ}りな。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an instruction from someone in charge - a parent sending a child home, a
teacher clearing the classroom. It is calm, but it is not negotiable, and the full
`なさい` keeps a certain formality.

(b) is the same order with the ending clipped, and clipping it makes it kind. It
sounds like an older friend saying "go on, head home" - casual, a bit warm, and
completely unusable in writing. Note that neither can be said upwards: even the
friendly (b) assumes you are the senior one.

</details>
