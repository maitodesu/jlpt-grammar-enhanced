# ～と思う

**Meaning** · I think (that) … - <code>{f|思|おも}う</code> is "think", and `と` is the string that ties a whole sentence into a parcel for it to carry

`と` is a **quotation marker**. It takes an entire sentence, wraps it up, and hands
it to a verb of thinking or saying. <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> is therefore not
"think about" - it reports a complete thought, word for word, as your opinion. The
parcel is always tied in **plain form**, no matter how polite the sentence around
it is.

## Formation

`と` follows the plain form of the quoted clause. Nouns and な-adjectives keep
their `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|く}ると{f|思|おも}う |
| Verb (past) | た-form + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|終|お}わった → {f|終|お}わったと{f|思|おも}う |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わない → {f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わないと{f|思|おも}う |
| い-adjective | plain form + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いと{f|思|おも}う |
| な-adjective | + だ + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|無理|むり} → {f|無理|むり}だと{f|思|おも}う |
| Noun | + だ + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|冗談|じょうだん} → {f|冗談|じょうだん}だと{f|思|おも}う |
| Volitional - "I'm thinking of…" | よう-form + と{f|思|おも}う | {f|休|やす}もう → {f|休|やす}もうと{f|思|おも}う |
| Somebody else's opinion | plain form + と{f|思|おも}っている | {f|正|ただ}しい → {f|正|ただ}しいと{f|思|おも}っている |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - nothing polite goes in front of `と`.
❌<code>{f|行|い}きますと{f|思|おも}います</code> → ✅<code>{f|行|い}くと{f|思|おも}います</code>. The
politeness of the whole sentence is carried by the **final** verb and nowhere else.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `だ` that disappears before `でしょう` stays before `と`. It is
<code>{f|大丈夫|だいじょうぶ}でしょう</code> but <code>{f|大丈夫|だいじょうぶ}だと{f|思|おも}います</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
あの{f|店|みせ}はもう{f|閉|し}まっている<b>と{f|思|おも}う</b>。
I think that shop has already closed.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|傘|かさ}は{f|山田|やまだ}さんのだ<b>と{f|思|おも}います</b>。
I think this umbrella is Yamada's.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|試験|しけん}はそんなに{f|難|むずか}しくない<b>と{f|思|おも}う</b>よ。
I don't think the test will be all that hard.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今年|ことし}の{f|冬|ふゆ}は{f|去年|きょねん}より{f|暖|あたた}かい<b>と{f|思|おも}いませんか</b>。
Don't you think this winter is milder than last year's?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来月|らいげつ}から{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}で{f|通|かよ}おう<b>と{f|思|おも}っています</b>。
I'm thinking of cycling to work from next month.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|妹|いもうと}は{f|自分|じぶん}の{f|料理|りょうり}が{f|世界|せかい}で{f|一番|いちばん}おいしい<b>と{f|思|おも}っている</b>。
My sister is convinced her own cooking is the best in the world.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|三時|さんじ}に{f|終|お}わる<b>と{f|思|おも}います</b>が、{f|念|ねん}のため{f|確認|かくにん}しておきます。
I think the meeting finishes at three, but I'll check to be safe.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|正直|しょうじき}に{f|言|い}うと、あの{f|計画|けいかく}はうまくいかない<b>と{f|思|おも}います</b>。
To be honest, I don't think that plan is going to work.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>{f|思|おも}う</code> is the plain form and <code>{f|思|おも}います</code> the polite one;
everything before `と` is untouched by that choice. This is the single most useful
thing to know about the pattern, and the single most common place learners slip.

Beyond grammar, <code>と{f|思|おも}います</code> is a **softener**. A bare assertion - 
その{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|無理|むり}です - commits you to a fact. Adding
<code>と{f|思|おも}います</code> reframes it as one person's reading of the situation, which
is why Japanese workplace speech is full of it. Overusing it is a real thing too:
answering a question about your own department with
<code>{f|多分|たぶん}そうだと{f|思|おも}います</code> can sound as if you have not bothered to
find out.

In casual speech you will hear `って` in place of `と` - 
{f|無理|むり}だって{f|思|おも}った - and the whole clause often ends in
<code>んだけど</code> to leave the door open:
{f|明日|あした}でもいいと{f|思|おも}うんだけど。 At the other end, formal writing prefers
the passive <code>と{f|思|おも}われます</code> or <code>と{f|考|かんが}えられます</code>, which
removes the "I" altogether.

One limit worth stating plainly: the plain-form rule is about **indirect** quotation.
If you put actual quotation marks round someone's words you are reproducing what
came out of their mouth, so anything can appear inside:
「{f|明日|あした}は{f|休|やす}みます」と{f|言|い}っていました。 That is a direct quote, and
it is not an exception to the rule - it is a different operation.

## Contrast

### ～と{f|思|おも}う vs ～と{f|思|おも}っている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|思|おも}う</code> is a thought formed **now**, at the moment of
speaking. <code>{f|思|おも}っている</code> is a view somebody **holds** - and it is the only
option for a flat statement about a third person.

</div>

<pre>
その{f|色|いろ}のほうが{f|似合|にあ}う<b>と{f|思|おも}う</b>。
I think that colour suits you better.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}は{f|私|わたし}をまだ{f|子|こ}どもだ<b>と{f|思|おも}っている</b>。
My father still thinks of me as a child.
</pre>

You cannot see into someone else's head, so a bare
❌<code>{f|父|ちち}は…と{f|思|おも}う</code> claims access you do not have. The `ている` turns
it into an observation about a state you can infer from outside. About yourself both
work: <code>{f|私|わたし}もそう{f|思|おも}っています</code> just adds that you have thought so
for a while.

### ～と{f|思|おも}う vs ～でしょう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> labels the claim as **mine**. `でしょう`
estimates how **likely** the claim is. One is about the speaker, the other about the
world.

</div>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}は{f|野菜|やさい}の{f|値段|ねだん}が{f|上|あ}がる<b>と{f|思|おも}います</b>。
I think vegetable prices will go up next year.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}は{f|野菜|やさい}の{f|値段|ねだん}が{f|上|あ}がる<b>でしょう</b>。
Vegetable prices will probably go up next year.
</pre>

The second is what a news presenter says; the first is what you say at dinner. Because
<code>と{f|思|おも}います</code> keeps the responsibility on you, it is usually the safer
choice when you might be wrong, and the wrong choice when you are supposed to be the
authority.

### ～ないと{f|思|おも}う vs ～と{f|思|おも}わない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - English negates "think"; Japanese negates the **thought**. "I don't
think it will rain" is <code>{f|降|ふ}らないと{f|思|おも}う</code>, not
<code>{f|降|ふ}ると{f|思|おも}わない</code>.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}はその{f|話|はなし}を{f|知|し}らない<b>と{f|思|おも}います</b>。
I don't think she knows about it.
</pre>

<code>と{f|思|おも}わない</code> is not ungrammatical - it is simply much stronger. It
rejects the proposition rather than doubting it, so it lands as disagreement:

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}はそうは<b>{f|思|おも}いません</b>。
I don't see it that way.
</pre>

Note the `は` on `そう`, which is typical: negating <code>{f|思|おも}う</code> almost always
comes with contrastive `は`, and that is part of why it feels like pushing back.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}りますと{f|思|おも}います</b>。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}ると{f|思|おも}います</b>。
  Nothing in ます-form may sit in front of `と`. Politeness belongs to the last verb.

- ❌ この{f|辞書|じしょ}は<b>{f|便利|べんり}と{f|思|おも}う</b>。 → ✅ この{f|辞書|じしょ}は<b>{f|便利|べんり}だと{f|思|おも}う</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns keep `だ` before `と`. Only い-adjectives and verbs attach bare.

- ❌ {f|弟|おとうと}はその{f|映画|えいが}がつまらないと<b>{f|思|おも}います</b>。 → ✅ {f|弟|おとうと}はその{f|映画|えいが}がつまらないと<b>{f|思|おも}っています</b>。
  A plain statement about what someone else thinks needs `ている` - or a hedge such as
  <code>{f|思|おも}っているようです</code>.

- ❌ {f|時間|じかん}が<b>あると{f|思|おも}いません</b>。 → ✅ {f|時間|じかん}が<b>ないと{f|思|おも}います</b>。
  Put the negative inside the quoted thought. The outer negative is reserved for
  disagreeing.

## Practice

**1.** {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|来週|らいしゅう}{f|出張|しゅっちょう}____{f|思|おも}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|来週|らいしゅう}{f|出張|しゅっちょう}<b>だと</b>{f|思|おも}います。

<code>{f|出張|しゅっちょう}</code> is a noun, and a noun predicate keeps its `だ` in front of
the quotative `と`. Dropping it is only correct before `でしょう`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|姉|あね}は{f|自分|じぶん}が{f|正|ただ}しい（と{f|思|おも}います・と{f|思|おも}っています）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|姉|あね}は{f|自分|じぶん}が{f|正|ただ}しい<b>と{f|思|おも}っています</b>。

The subject is a third person, so a bare <code>と{f|思|おも}います</code> would claim direct
knowledge of her mind. `ている` presents the belief as something you observe her
holding.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I don't think he will come today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}は{f|今日|きょう}<b>{f|来|こ}ないと{f|思|おも}います</b>。

The negative goes inside the quoted thought, not on
<code>{f|思|おも}う</code>. <code>{f|来|く}ると{f|思|おも}いません</code> would read as flatly
rejecting the idea rather than doubting it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの{f|人|ひと}は{f|親切|しんせつ}ですと{f|思|おも}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの{f|人|ひと}は<b>{f|親切|しんせつ}だと{f|思|おも}います</b>。

Two things go wrong at once: `です` cannot appear before `と`, and the な-adjective
still needs its plain copula `だ`. The polite ending survives on
<code>{f|思|おも}います</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|案|あん}は{f|失敗|しっぱい}すると{f|思|おも}います。
(b) この{f|案|あん}は{f|失敗|しっぱい}するでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) marks the prediction as your own reading and keeps the responsibility with you - 
appropriate in a meeting where you are offering an opinion someone may argue with.

(b) states it as a likely outcome, the way a forecast or an analysis does. It sounds
more detached and more authoritative, which is fine from an expert and presumptuous
from a newcomer.

</details>
