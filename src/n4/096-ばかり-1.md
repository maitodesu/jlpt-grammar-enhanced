# ばかり - only; nothing but

**Meaning** · nothing but ～; ～ and nothing else - usually with the feeling that
there is too much of it

`ばかり` draws the same line `だけ` does, then adds an opinion. It says one thing
fills the whole picture and, nine times out of ten, that this is too much of it.
It is also not a mathematician: someone who
{f|肉|にく}ばかり{f|食|た}べている probably eats a salad occasionally. `ばかり` is
about **proportion and attitude**, not arithmetic.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ばかり | マンガ → マンガばかり |
| Verb (habitual excess) | て-form + ばかりいる | {f|遊|あそ}ぶ → {f|遊|あそ}んでばかりいる |
| Verb (plain form) | V(plain) + ばかりだ | {f|文句|もんく}を{f|言|い}うばかりだ |
| Modifying a noun | ～ばかり + の + Noun | {f|男|おとこ}の{f|人|ひと}ばかりのクラス |
| After が／を | が and を are **dropped** | ❌{f|水|みず}をばかり → ✅{f|水|みず}ばかり |
| After に／で／と | particle is **kept** | {f|外|そと}でばかり{f|食|た}べる |
| Casual | ばっかり／ばっか | マンガばっかり |
| Quantity (approximately) | Number + ばかり | {f|三日|みっか}ばかり（written, dated） |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if you can add "…and I'm getting tired of it" to the English,
`ばかり` is the right word. If you are just stating a boundary, you want `だけ`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}はゲーム<b>ばかり</b>しています。
My little brother does nothing but play games.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|甘|あま}いもの<b>ばかり</b>{f|食|た}べてはいけませんよ。
You mustn't live on nothing but sweets.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|文句|もんく}<b>ばかり</b>{f|言|い}わないで、{f|手伝|てつだ}ってよ。
Stop complaining all the time and give me a hand.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|息子|むすこ}は{f|寝|ね}て<b>ばかり</b>いて、{f|勉強|べんきょう}しようとしません。
My son does nothing but sleep - he won't even try to study.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|最近|さいきん}、{f|雨|あめ}の{f|日|ひ}<b>ばかり</b>で{f|洗濯物|せんたくもの}が{f|乾|かわ}きません。
Lately it's been rain, rain, rain, and the washing won't dry.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|人|ひと}は{f|自分|じぶん}の{f|話|はなし}<b>ばかり</b>して、{f|人|ひと}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}きません。
He only ever talks about himself and never listens to anyone else.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}では{f|同|おな}じ{f|話|はなし}<b>ばかり</b>{f|繰|く}り{f|返|かえ}していて、{f|時間|じかん}だけが{f|過|す}ぎていきました。
In the meeting they went over the same ground again and again, and the time just drained away.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|去年|きょねん}はいいこと<b>ばかり</b>で、{f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|幸|しあわ}せな{f|一年|いちねん}でした。
Last year was nothing but good news - a genuinely happy year.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ばかり` itself is register-neutral: {f|遊|あそ}んでばかりいます and
{f|遊|あそ}んでばかりいる differ only in politeness of ending. The contraction
`ばっかり`（and the clipped `ばっか`）is casual and adds emphasis and feeling - 
ゲームばっかりして! is what a parent shouts, not what a report says.

The attitude is the heart of the word. In the great majority of sentences it is
**criticism**, either of somebody else（{f|文句|もんく}ばかり）or, turned inward,
an apology（ご{f|迷惑|めいわく}ばかりかけてすみません, "I do nothing but cause you
trouble"）. That inward use is genuinely humble and very common in polite speech.

But the underlying idea is **excess**, not badness, and excess is occasionally
welcome. いいことばかりでした and
{f|好|す}きな{f|人|ひと}ばかり{f|集|あつ}まったパーティー are both positive. What
you cannot do is use `ばかり` neutrally: it always comments.

Two smaller points:

- The counting `ばかり` - {f|一週間|いっしゅうかん}ばかり{f|休|やす}みます, "I'll
  take about a week off" - is formal and slightly old-fashioned. Recognise it;
  in speech use `ぐらい`.
- <code>～たばかり</code> is a **different pattern** meaning "just did", and it is
  built on the past form: {f|着|つ}いたばかりです, "I've only just arrived". The
  contrast section below separates them.

## Contrast

### ばかり vs だけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけ` reports a boundary and says nothing about how it feels.
`ばかり` says the boundary is *too tight*, and does not claim to be exact.

</div>

<pre>
{f|昼|ひる}ごはんはパン<b>だけ</b>でした。
Lunch was just bread.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昼|ひる}ごはんはパン<b>ばかり</b>です。
It's bread for lunch every single time.
</pre>

The first is a fact about one lunch: bread, nothing else, no comment. The second
complains about a pattern across many lunches - and would still be a fair thing to
say on a day when you also had an apple. That inexactness is why `ばかり` and a
precise number sit badly together: {f|三人|さんにん}だけ{f|来|き}ました is a count,
{f|三人|さんにん}ばかり{f|来|き}ました means "about three people came" and belongs
to the other, formal `ばかり`.

<pre>
テレビを{f|見|み}ている{f|人|ひと}<b>ばかり</b>で、{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれる{f|人|ひと}がいません。
Everyone's just sitting watching television and nobody will help.
</pre>

Swap `だけ` in there and the annoyance evaporates.

### ばかり vs ～しか～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ばかり` complains that there is **too much of one thing**.
<code>しか～ない</code> complains that there is **not enough of everything else**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|冷蔵庫|れいぞうこ}にビール<b>ばかり</b>{f|入|はい}っています。
The fridge is full of nothing but beer.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|冷蔵庫|れいぞうこ}にビール<b>しか</b>{f|入|はい}っていません。
There's nothing in the fridge but beer.
</pre>

Both describe the same fridge and both are unhappy, but they are unhappy about
different things. The first is disgusted by the quantity of beer; the second is
disappointed that there is no food. Note the grammar too: `ばかり` takes a positive
verb, `しか` demands a negative one.

### Noun + ばかり vs ～たばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at what is in front. A **noun** or a **て-form** gives
you "nothing but". A **past-tense verb** gives you "just did".

</div>

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}に{f|来|き}た<b>ばかり</b>で、まだ{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|読|よ}めません。
I've only just come to Japan, so I still can't read kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}へ{f|来|き}てから、コンビニのごはん<b>ばかり</b>{f|食|た}べています。
Since coming to Japan I've eaten nothing but convenience store food.
</pre>

The first is about time - the arrival was recent. The second is about proportion - 
the diet is monotonous. Learners produce
❌{f|来|き}るばかりです for "I just arrived"; the pattern needs the plain past
{f|来|き}た.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|水|みず}<b>を</b>ばかり{f|飲|の}んでいます。 → ✅ {f|水|みず}<b>ばかり</b>{f|飲|の}んでいます。
  `が` and `を` disappear in front of `ばかり`. If you want the object marked, it
  goes behind instead: {f|水|みず}ばかりを{f|飲|の}む.

- ❌ {f|遊|あそ}ぶばかりいます。 → ✅ {f|遊|あそ}<b>んでばかり</b>います。
  With `いる`, the verb has to be in て-form. The plain form works only in the
  <code>V(plain) + ばかりだ</code> frame, which has no `いる`.

- ❌ きのうは{f|一人|ひとり}<b>ばかり</b>{f|来|き}ました。（meaning "only one person came"） → ✅ きのうは{f|一人|ひとり}<b>しか</b>{f|来|き}ませんでした。
  `ばかり` cannot mean "a mere N". With a number it either means "approximately"
  or nothing at all; the "disappointingly few" job belongs to
  <code>しか～ない</code>.

- ❌ {f|野菜|やさい}<b>ばかり</b>{f|食|た}べて、{f|健康|けんこう}にいいですね。 → ✅ {f|野菜|やさい}をたくさん{f|食|た}べていて、{f|健康|けんこう}にいいですね。
  `ばかり` on somebody's habits reads as a complaint, so pairing it with praise
  sounds sarcastic. Use it when you mean the criticism.

## Practice

**1.** {f|姉|あね}はスマホ____{f|見|み}ていて、{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}いてくれません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|姉|あね}はスマホ<b>ばかり</b>{f|見|み}ていて、{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}いてくれません。

The second half - she won't listen - tells you the speaker is annoyed, and that
annoyance is exactly what `ばかり` carries. `だけ` would report the same behaviour
with no complaint attached, which would clash with くれません.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural option:
このクラスには{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}が{f|二人|ふたり}（だけ・ばかり）います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

このクラスには{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}が{f|二人|ふたり}<b>だけ</b>います。

A precise count needs `だけ`. Putting `ばかり` after a number switches on its other
meaning - "about two" - and sounds like a formal announcement estimating a crowd.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He does nothing but grumble, and he never actually does anything."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}は{f|文句|もんく}を{f|言|い}う<b>ばかり</b>で、{f|何|なに}もしません。

The <code>V(plain) + ばかりで</code> frame links the excess to its consequence and
is very common in criticism. {f|文句|もんく}ばかり{f|言|い}っていて、
{f|何|なに}もしません says the same thing with the noun taking `ばかり` instead - 
both are natural.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|父|ちち}はお{f|酒|さけ}をばかり{f|飲|の}むばかりいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|父|ちち}はお{f|酒|さけ}<b>ばかり</b>{f|飲|の}んでいます。

Two errors. First, `を` cannot stand in front of `ばかり` - it is simply dropped.
Second, `ばかり` should appear once, not twice; and if you do use the
<code>～てばかりいる</code> frame, the verb must be in て-form:
お{f|酒|さけ}を{f|飲|の}んでばかりいます.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|休|やす}みの{f|日|ひ}は{f|家|いえ}にいる<b>だけ</b>です。
(b) {f|休|やす}みの{f|日|ひ}は{f|家|いえ}にいて<b>ばかり</b>います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states what your days off consist of and leaves the judgement to the listener.
It can even sound content - "I just stay in, that's all."

(b) hands down a verdict on yourself: you stay in far too much and you know it.
`ばかり` supplies the excess and the disapproval, which is why (b) fits naturally
before a phrase like <code>{f|運動|うんどう}しないといけませんね</code> and (a) does
not.

</details>
