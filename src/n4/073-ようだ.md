# ～ようだ

**Meaning** · it seems that; it appears that - a conclusion the speaker has reached themselves

`～ようだ` reports an **inference**. The speaker has some evidence - something seen,
heard, felt, or worked out - and offers a careful conclusion drawn from it. The
judgement belongs to the speaker, which is exactly what separates it from
`らしい`, where the judgement belongs to somebody else.

## Formation

`よう` is a noun meaning "appearance, manner", so everything attaches to it the way
things attach to any noun you learned to modify at N5: verbs and い-adjectives in
plain form, な-adjectives with `な`, nouns with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ようだ | {f|降|ふ}る → {f|降|ふ}るようだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ようだ | {f|降|ふ}った → {f|降|ふ}ったようだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ようだ | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ないようだ |
| Verb (ている) | ている + ようだ | {f|寝|ね}ている → {f|寝|ね}ているようだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + ようだ | {f|忙|いそが}しい → {f|忙|いそが}しいようだ |
| な-adjective | ～な + ようだ | {f|大変|たいへん}だ → {f|大変|たいへん}なようだ |
| Noun | Noun + の + ようだ | {f|事故|じこ} → {f|事故|じこ}のようだ |
| Polite | ようだ → ようです | {f|降|ふ}るようです |
| Past | ようだ → ようだった／ようでした | {f|降|ふ}ったようでした |
| Before a verb | ようだ → ように | {f|降|ふ}るように{f|見|み}える |
| Before a noun | ようだ → ような | {f|夢|ゆめ}のような{f|話|はなし} |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` inflects exactly like a な-adjective: よう**だ**, よう**な**,
よう**に**, よう**で**, よう**でした**. And nothing in front of it ever keeps its `だ`:
❌<code>{f|事故|じこ}だようだ</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|外|そと}は{f|寒|さむ}い<b>ようです</b>。
It seems to be cold outside.
</pre>

<pre>
だれか<b>{f|来|き}たようだ</b>。
Looks like somebody's arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|中村|なかむら}さん<b>のようです</b>。
This dictionary appears to be Nakamura's.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|六月|ろくがつ}なのに、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|夏|なつ}<b>のようだ</b>。
It's only June, but today feels like summer.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}がある<b>ようなので</b>、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|早|はや}く{f|帰|かえ}ります。
I seem to have a temperature, so I'm going home early today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|道|みち}がぬれているから、{f|夜|よる}のうちに{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}ったようだ</b>。
The road is wet, so it must have rained during the night.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}の{f|話|はな}し{f|方|かた}を{f|聞|き}くと、この{f|町|まち}に{f|長|なが}く{f|住|す}んでいる<b>ようですね</b>。
From the way he talks, it sounds like he's lived in this town a long time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|課長|かちょう}は{f|今|いま}{f|会議|かいぎ}{f|中|ちゅう}の<b>ようですので</b>、あとでもう{f|一度|いちど}おかけします。
The section chief seems to be in a meeting just now, so I'll call again later.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ようです` is the neutral polite form and is safe everywhere - with strangers, with
customers, in a report. Plain `ようだ` is the written form and the one you meet in
newspapers and essays; spoken out loud between friends it sounds a little
buttoned-up, and casual speech reaches for `みたい` instead. Nothing about `ようだ`
is male- or female-marked.

The adverb `どうやら` is its natural partner and makes the hedge audible:
どうやら{f|道|みち}を{f|間違|まちが}えたようだ。 A speaker who wants to sound less
sure adds `かもしれない`; one who wants to sound sure drops the hedge entirely.

`ようだ` has a second job that has nothing to do with guessing: it is also the
**politeness** device you use to say an awkward thing without saying it flatly.
{f|私|わたし}にはできないようです is softer than {f|私|わたし}にはできません,
because it presents your refusal as an observation rather than a decision. In
business Japanese そのようです ("that does appear to be the case") is the standard
way to confirm something you were not personally present for.

Finally, `ようだ` is fine about **your own body**, because the evidence there is
still indirect: {f|風邪|かぜ}をひいたようだ - you feel bad, you have not seen a
doctor. It is not fine about facts you simply know.

## Contrast

<div class="rule">

**The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - 
what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what
you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance
`そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing, before
reasoning starts. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis altogether - it is a plain relay
of somebody's words.

</div>

### ～ようだ vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` is *my* conclusion from *my* evidence. `らしい` is
somebody else's information, which I am passing on and not vouching for.

</div>

<pre>
{f|山本|やまもと}さんは{f|会社|かいしゃ}をやめた<b>ようだ</b>。
It looks like Yamamoto has quit - his desk is cleared and nobody's seen him.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山本|やまもと}さんは{f|会社|かいしゃ}をやめた<b>らしい</b>。
Apparently Yamamoto has quit - that's what people are saying.
</pre>

Because `ようだ` commits you to the reasoning, it is the wrong choice when you name
an outside source out loud. A sentence that opens with <code>ニュースによると</code>
wants `そうだ` or `らしい` to finish it.

### ～ようだ vs ～みたい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register, different attachment.
`ようだ` is written and formal; `みたい` is spoken and casual. Nouns take `の` before
`ようだ` and **nothing** before `みたい`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|今日|きょう}の{f|彼|かれ}は{f|子|こ}ども<b>のようだ</b>。
He's behaving like a child today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今日|きょう}の{f|彼|かれ}、{f|子|こ}ども<b>みたいだ</b>ね。
He's acting like such a kid today, isn't he.
</pre>

The second is what you actually say to a friend. Putting `みたい` into a written
report, or `ようだ` into a chat about a coworker, is not an error of grammar - it is
an error of tone, and it is heard as one.

### ～ようだ vs the two ～そうだ

The word `そうだ` covers **two unrelated grammars**, and they attach differently.
Line all three up on one adjective and the difference is impossible to miss.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain form + `そうだ` = "I hear that". Adjective **stem** +
`そうだ` = "looks". Plain form + `ようだ` = "seems, judging by the evidence".

</div>

<pre>
このケーキは{f|甘|あま}い<b>そうだ</b>。
I hear this cake is sweet. (someone told me; I haven't tried it)
</pre>

<pre>
このケーキは<b>{f|甘|あま}そうだ</b>。
This cake looks sweet. (I'm looking straight at it)
</pre>

<pre>
このケーキは{f|甘|あま}い<b>ようだ</b>。
This cake seems to be sweet. (everyone is leaving half of it, so I conclude…)
</pre>

Note the shape: <code>{f|甘|あま}い</code> keeps its `い` before hearsay `そうだ` and before
`ようだ`, and **loses** it before appearance `そうだ`. That single `い` is the whole
difference between reporting a rumour and describing a photograph.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|病気|びょうき}**だ**ようです → ✅ {f|病気|びょうき}**の**ようです
  A noun connects to `よう` with `の`, never with `だ`. The same rule you learned
  for noun-modifying clauses at N5 applies here.

- ❌ {f|静|しず}か**ようだ** → ✅ {f|静|しず}か**なようだ**
  A な-adjective keeps its `な` in front of `よう`, because `よう` is a noun.

- ❌ {f|降|ふ}るよう**だです** → ✅ {f|降|ふ}る**ようです**
  `ようだ` and `ようです` are one form at two politeness levels. You never stack them.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}はきのう{f|京都|きょうと}へ{f|行|い}った**ようです**。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}はきのう{f|京都|きょうと}へ{f|行|い}き**ました**。
  You were there. `ようだ` needs indirect evidence; using it about your own known
  actions sounds as though you have lost your memory.

## Practice

**1.** {f|受付|うけつけ}にだれもいない。{f|今日|きょう}は{f|休|やす}み____ようだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|今日|きょう}は{f|休|やす}み**の**ようだ。

<code>{f|休|やす}み</code> is a noun, and nouns attach to `よう` with `の`. Only な-adjectives
take `な`; verbs and い-adjectives attach in plain form with nothing added.

</details>

**2.** ニュースによると、{f|来週|らいしゅう}は{f|寒|さむ}く（なるようだ・なるそうだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|来週|らいしゅう}は{f|寒|さむ}く<b>なるそうだ</b>。

The sentence names its source out loud, so it is a relay, not a deduction. Once you
have said <code>ニュースによると</code>, the evidence is other people's, and hearsay
`そうだ` (or `らしい`) is what finishes the sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It seems the train has stopped."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|電車|でんしゃ}が<b>{f|止|と}まっているようです</b>。

Use `ている` for the resulting state - the train stopped and is still stopped - then
attach `ようです` to that plain form. Nothing goes between the verb and `よう`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼|かれ}は{f|元気|げんき}ようですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|元気|げんき}**な**ようですね。

<code>{f|元気|げんき}</code> is a な-adjective, so it needs `な` to modify the noun `よう`.
Dropping it is the commonest slip learners make with this pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼女|かのじょ}はもう{f|帰|かえ}った**ようだ**。
(b) {f|彼女|かのじょ}はもう{f|帰|かえ}った**らしい**。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In (a) the speaker worked it out: her coat is gone, the light is off, so the
conclusion is theirs and they would defend it. In (b) the speaker heard it from
somebody - a colleague mentioned it - and is repeating the information at a slight
distance, without taking responsibility for whether it is true.

</details>
