# ～がる

**Meaning** · to show signs of feeling something - how you report another person's inner state

Japanese does not let you simply announce what somebody else feels. `～がる`
solves that: it takes an adjective of emotion or sensation and turns it into a
**verb** meaning "behaves as though X". You are no longer claiming to know what is
in their head; you are describing what you can see them doing.

## Formation

`がる` attaches to the adjective stem and produces an ordinary う-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | drop い + がる | {f|寒|さむ}い → {f|寒|さむ}がる |
| な-adjective | stem + がる | {f|嫌|いや}だ → {f|嫌|いや}がる |
| ほしい | ほし + がる | ほしい → ほしがる |
| ～たい | たい → たがる | {f|行|い}きたい → {f|行|い}きたがる |

Once formed, it conjugates as a う-verb like <code>{f|作|つく}る</code>:

| Verb form | Example |
|---|---|
| Polite | {f|怖|こわ}がります |
| Negative | {f|怖|こわ}がらない |
| Past | {f|怖|こわ}がった |
| て-form | {f|怖|こわ}がって |
| Progressive / current state | {f|怖|こわ}がっている |
| Person noun | {f|怖|こわ}がり、{f|寒|さむ}がり、{f|恥|は}ずかしがり{f|屋|や} |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がる` makes a transitive verb, so what the feeling is aimed at
takes `を`, not the `が` the original adjective wanted:
おもちゃ<b>が</b>ほしい → おもちゃ<b>を</b>ほしがる.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `がる` only attaches to adjectives describing **how someone feels**:
{f|寒|さむ}い, {f|暑|あつ}い, {f|痛|いた}い, {f|怖|こわ}い, うれしい,
{f|悲|かな}しい, さびしい, {f|恥|は}ずかしい, うらやましい, ほしい,
{f|嫌|いや}だ, {f|残念|ざんねん}だ. It cannot attach to an adjective describing a
thing from outside: there is no ❌{f|高|たか}がる or ❌{f|赤|あか}がる.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもが<b>{f|寒|さむ}がって</b>います。
The children are feeling the cold.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|妹|いもうと}は{f|犬|いぬ}を<b>{f|怖|こわ}がります</b>。
My little sister is afraid of dogs.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}は{f|新|あたら}しい{f|時計|とけい}を<b>ほしがって</b>いる。
My father's had his eye on a new watch.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|息子|むすこ}は{f|注射|ちゅうしゃ}のあとで{f|腕|うで}を<b>{f|痛|いた}がって</b>いました。
My son was making a fuss about his arm after the injection.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|猫|ねこ}はおふろを<b>{f|嫌|いや}がる</b>ので、{f|洗|あら}うのが{f|大変|たいへん}です。
Cats hate baths, so washing them is hard work.
</pre>

<pre>
みんなが<b>{f|不思議|ふしぎ}がって</b>いたので、{f|理由|りゆう}を{f|説明|せつめい}しました。
Everyone seemed puzzled, so I explained the reason.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|住|す}んでいるので、ときどき<b>さびしがり</b>ます。
Yamada lives on his own, so he gets lonely from time to time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|先輩|せんぱい}は{f|留学|りゅうがく}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}いて、ずいぶん<b>うらやましがって</b>いました。
My senior sounded thoroughly envious when he heard about the exchange programme.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The heart of `がる` is **evidence**. <code>{f|痛|いた}がっている</code> does not mean
"is in pain"; it means the person is behaving in a way that shows pain - wincing,
holding the spot, saying it hurts. That is why the pattern belongs to third
persons: you can see other people's behaviour, but you feel your own sensations
directly.

**Bare `がる` versus `がっている`.** The plain form describes a habit or a general
truth: {f|子|こ}どもは{f|注射|ちゅうしゃ}を{f|怖|こわ}がる is "children are afraid
of injections", a statement about children at large. For what someone is doing
right now, use `がっている`.

**First person.** You would not normally say
❌{f|私|わたし}は{f|今|いま}{f|寒|さむ}がっています - for your own present feeling,
the plain adjective {f|寒|さむ}いです is what you want. But `がる` is fine about
yourself when you step outside your own experience: describing a habit
（{f|私|わたし}は{f|昔|むかし}から{f|暗|くら}いところを{f|怖|こわ}がります）or looking
back on your younger self.

**Tone.** Because it reports behaviour, `がる` can slide into "making a fuss
about", and with some verbs it tends to sound faintly critical or amused. That
tendency, plus the fact that it is a bald description of someone's inner life,
makes it a poor fit for talking about a superior. Prefer a hedge:
{f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|残念|ざんねん}そうでした, or
{f|残念|ざんねん}だとおっしゃっていました.

**The `がり` nouns.** Drop the verb ending and you get a noun for the kind of
person: {f|寒|さむ}がり (someone who feels the cold), {f|暑|あつ}がり,
{f|怖|こわ}がり, and with `屋` attached, さびしがり{f|屋|や} and
{f|恥|は}ずかしがり{f|屋|や}. These are everyday self-descriptions - 
{f|私|わたし}、{f|寒|さむ}がりなんです is a perfectly normal thing to say in
January.

`がる` on `たい` gives `たがる`, which is common enough to get its own treatment;
everything above about evidence, third persons and politeness applies there too.

## Contrast

### ～がる vs ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` is a guess from **how something looks** at a glance.
`がる` reports **what someone is actually doing**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|上着|うわぎ}をぬいで、ずっと<b>{f|暑|あつ}がって</b>います。
He's taken his jacket off and has been hot the whole time.
</pre>

Compare {f|彼|かれ}は{f|暑|あつ}そうです, "he looks hot" - one look, one inference,
no claim about behaviour over time. Two further differences follow from this.
`そうだ` works on things as well as people
（このケーキ、おいしそう）, while `がる` needs someone with feelings; and `そうだ`
is a much softer thing to say about a superior, which is why it is the polite way
out.

### ～がる vs the plain adjective

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - in a plain main-clause statement, an emotion adjective about a
third person needs `がる` or a hedge. About yourself, use the bare adjective.

</div>

<pre>
{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}のリーさんは{f|国|くに}の{f|料理|りょうり}が{f|食|た}べられなくて、<b>さびしがって</b>います。
Lee, the exchange student, can't get food from home and is feeling homesick.
</pre>

A flat ❌リーさんはさびしいです claims direct access to Lee's feelings. The escapes
are `がる`, or one of the reporting frames you already have:
さびしいそうです, さびしいと{f|言|い}っています, さびしいようです. Inside a
subordinate clause the restriction lifts entirely - 
さびしい{f|人|ひと}は{f|連絡|れんらく}してください is fine.

### ほしがる vs ほしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` is an い-adjective and takes `が`; `ほしがる` is a verb
and takes `を`. The particle change is the giveaway that the word class changed.

</div>

<pre>
{f|娘|むすめ}は{f|自分|じぶん}のパソコンを<b>ほしがって</b>います。
My daughter wants a computer of her own.
</pre>

Set that against {f|私|わたし}は{f|自分|じぶん}のパソコンがほしいです for your own
want. The same asymmetry you met with `たい` at N5 is at work: the adjective is
for the inside view, the `がる` verb for the outside one. And as with `たい`,
`ほしがっている` is the normal shape for a want someone has right now, while bare
ほしがる suits a habit.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|寒|さむ}<b>いがる</b> → ✅ {f|寒|さむ}<b>がる</b>
  The final `い` is replaced, exactly as it is for `さ` and `すぎる`.

- ❌ {f|弟|おとうと}はおもちゃ<b>が</b>ほしがっています。 → ✅ {f|弟|おとうと}はおもちゃ<b>を</b>ほしがっています。
  `がる` builds a transitive verb, and transitive verbs take `を`. Leaving `が` in
  place is the single commonest slip with this pattern.

- ❌ この{f|店|みせ}は<b>{f|高|たか}がる</b>。 → ✅ この{f|店|みせ}は{f|高|たか}いです。
  `がる` needs an adjective about someone's feelings. {f|高|たか}い describes a
  price, not an emotion, so there is nothing for `がる` to report.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|今|いま}{f|寒|さむ}<b>がって</b>います。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}は{f|今|いま}{f|寒|さむ}<b>いです</b>。
  You do not need evidence for your own present sensation. Reserve `がる` for
  other people, or for yourself viewed as a type of person.

## Practice

**1.** {f|子|こ}どもがアイスクリームを____います。（ほしい）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

アイスクリームを<b>ほしがって</b>います

Drop the `い` of ほしい and add `がる`, then put it in the て-form for a want that
is happening now. Notice that the particle on アイスクリーム is `を`, because
ほしがる is a verb.

</details>

**2.** {f|弟|おとうと}は{f|新|あたら}しい{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}を（ほしいです・ほしがっています）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|弟|おとうと}は{f|新|あたら}しい{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}を<b>ほしがっています</b>。

Your brother is a third person, so a flat ほしいです would be claiming to feel his
desire yourself. The `を` already in the sentence is another clue that the verb
form is the one being asked for.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My daughter is afraid of dark places."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|娘|むすめ}は{f|暗|くら}いところを<b>{f|怖|こわ}がります</b>。

This is a standing trait rather than something happening at this moment, so the
plain form is right; {f|怖|こわ}がっています would describe her right now. The
thing feared takes `を`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|友|とも}だちは{f|新|あたら}しいゲームがほしいがっています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|友|とも}だちは{f|新|あたら}しいゲーム<b>をほしがって</b>います。

Two faults. The `い` of ほしい has to go before `がる` attaches, and the particle
must move from `が` to `を` because the result is a transitive verb.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|寒|さむ}そうです。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|寒|さむ}がっています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is your inference from appearance in the moment - he is thinly dressed, his
shoulders are hunched, so he looks cold. You are guessing, and you are being
tentative about it.

(b) says he is behaving in a way that displays being cold: rubbing his hands,
saying it is freezing, edging towards the heater. It is a report of observable
conduct rather than a guess, and depending on tone it can carry a hint that he is
making rather a lot of it.

</details>
