# 要するに

**Meaning** · the point is; in short; when you boil it down - compresses a long
account into the one thing that actually matters

<code>{f|要|よう}</code> is the noun "gist, essential point", and
<code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> announces that the speaker is about to supply it.
Unlike a neutral restatement, it carries a small claim: *I have decided what the
important part was.*

## Usage

<code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> is an adverb and opens the clause that carries the
summary. Nothing attaches to it and it never changes form.

Two habits are worth building. First, what comes **before** it. The pattern almost
always follows something long - an explanation, a list of conditions, somebody's
rambling account - either in the same sentence with `が` or `けど`, or as a
preceding sentence:

- {f|説明|せつめい}は{f|三十分|さんじゅっぷん}{f|続|つづ}いたが、{f|要|よう}するに…
- {f|条件|じょうけん}が{f|五|いつ}つ{f|書|か}いてある。{f|要|よう}するに…

Second, what comes **after**. Because the clause is a verdict rather than a fresh
report, it lands very often on <code>ということだ</code>, `のだ`, `わけだ` or
`んです`: <code>{f|要|よう}するに{f|人手|ひとで}が{f|足|た}りないということです</code>.

There is a shorter, more colloquial cousin, <code>{f|要|よう}は</code>. Same job,
one syllable less ceremony, and very common in speech:
<code>{f|要|よう}は、やる{f|気|き}があるかどうかだよ</code>.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>、お{f|金|かね}の{f|問題|もんだい}だ。
It comes down to money.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>、{f|君|きみ}は{f|行|い}きたくないんだね。
So the point is you don't want to go.
</pre>

<pre>
いろいろ{f|理由|りゆう}を{f|並|なら}べていたが、<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>{f|準備|じゅんび}が{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わなかったのだろう。
He gave all sorts of reasons, but the truth of it is he simply wasn't ready in time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|条件|じょうけん}が{f|三|みっ}つ{f|並|なら}んでいるが、<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>{f|経験者|けいけんしゃ}{f|以外|いがい}は{f|応募|おうぼ}するなということだ。
Three conditions are listed, but the message is: don't apply unless you have experience.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in form - it is at home in a meeting, an essay and a conversation
alike - but not neutral in *attitude*. Every use of <code>{f|要|よう}するに</code>
implies that what preceded it was longer than it needed to be, and that the speaker
has done the work of cutting it down. That is fine when the words being cut down
are your own, and it is what makes the pattern so useful for wrapping up your own
explanation: <code>{f|要|よう}するに、{f|私|わたし}の{f|準備|じゅんび}{f|不足|ぶそく}でした</code>.

Pointed at somebody else it can bite. Summarising a client's or a manager's
explanation with <code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> tells them, politely but audibly,
that they went on too long. It is a tendency rather than a rule - tone, relationship
and a `ね` on the end all soften it - but the safe default when the words are not
yours is `つまり`, or a question shape:
<code>{f|要|よう}するに、こういうことでしょうか</code>, which hands the judgement
back to them.

The word also has a genuinely combative use. In an argument,
<code>{f|要|よう}するに、{f|反対|はんたい}なんですね</code> strips away everything
diplomatic the other person said and names what they were avoiding saying. Speakers
use it that way on purpose. Recognise it when it is aimed at you.

<code>{f|要|よう}は</code> is the casual variant and carries the same edge in a
lighter package; it is common among friends and colleagues and slightly too blunt
for a formal presentation.

## Contrast

### {f|要|よう}するに vs つまり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `つまり` re-expresses; <code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> **discards**.
`つまり` keeps all the content in different words; <code>{f|要|よう}するに</code>
throws most of it away and keeps the core.

</div>

<pre>
{f|会場|かいじょう}は{f|変更|へんこう}になりました。<b>つまり</b>、{f|南口|みなみぐち}に{f|集合|しゅうごう}です。
The venue has changed - that is, we're meeting at the south exit.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|注意|ちゅうい}が{f|十|じゅっ}か{f|条|じょう}も{f|書|か}いてあるが、<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>{f|静|しず}かにしろということだ。
There are ten separate warnings, but what it all amounts to is: be quiet.
</pre>

Delete the second half of the `つまり` sentence and you lose nothing. Delete the
second half of the other one and you lose the whole point, because the ten warnings
never said "be quiet" - the speaker decided that was the gist. That decision is what
<code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> adds, and it is also what makes it risky.

### {f|要|よう}するに vs すなわち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すなわち` is an equals sign between two expressions.
<code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> is an arrow from a lot of material to a little.

</div>

<pre>
{f|締切|しめきり}は{f|年度末|ねんどまつ}、<b>すなわち</b>{f|三月|さんがつ}{f|末日|まつじつ}である。
The deadline is the end of the fiscal year, that is, the last day of March.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|報告書|ほうこくしょ}は{f|五十|ごじゅう}ページあるが、<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>{f|計画|けいかく}は{f|失敗|しっぱい}だったのである。
The report runs to fifty pages, but in essence the plan failed.
</pre>

`すなわち` could not replace the second one: fifty pages and "the plan failed" are
not the same expression, and no honest equals sign joins them. Conversely
<code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> could not replace the first, because there is nothing
to boil down - a date is already as short as it gets.

Register differs too. `すなわち` is written-only; <code>{f|要|よう}するに</code>
works in speech and on the page.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}のお{f|話|はなし}は、<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>{f|残業|ざんぎょう}するなということですね。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}のお{f|話|はなし}は、<b>つまり</b>{f|残業|ざんぎょう}するなということですね。
  Grammatically fine, socially loaded: it tells your manager he was long-winded.
  `つまり` restates without passing that judgement.

- ❌ <b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>、{f|明日|あした}は{f|九時|くじ}{f|集合|しゅうごう}です。（as the first thing said）→ ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|九時|くじ}{f|集合|しゅうごう}です。
  There has to be something to summarise. Opening with
  <code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> leaves the listener hunting for the long version
  they apparently missed.

- ❌ {f|要|よう}する<b>に</b>は、{f|準備|じゅんび}が{f|大切|たいせつ}だ。 → ✅ <b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>、{f|準備|じゅんび}が{f|大切|たいせつ}だ。
  It is a frozen adverb. No particle follows it, and it never conjugates - there is
  no ❌{f|要|よう}した or ❌{f|要|よう}しますに.

## Practice

**1.** {f|彼|かれ}の{f|説明|せつめい}は{f|長|なが}かったが、____{f|自分|じぶん}のせいではないと{f|言|い}いたいのだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>{f|自分|じぶん}のせいではないと{f|言|い}いたいのだ

A long explanation is being cut down to the one thing behind it, and the speaker -
not the person who spoke - is deciding what that thing was. The <code>のだ</code>
ending is the usual landing place for such a verdict.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 「{f|前向|まえむ}きに{f|検討|けんとう}します」というのは、（{f|要|よう}するに・すなわち）{f|断|ことわ}られたということだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（…というのは、）<b>{f|要|よう}するに</b>{f|断|ことわ}られたということだ。

The two expressions are not equivalents - one is a polite formula, the other is
what the speaker judges it really meant - so `すなわち` is out. Reading through
diplomatic language to the blunt core is exactly the job
<code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> does.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|先生|せんせい}のお{f|話|はなし}は、つまり{f|復習|ふくしゅう}が{f|大事|だいじ}だということですね。
(b) {f|先生|せんせい}のお{f|話|はなし}は、{f|要|よう}するに{f|復習|ふくしゅう}が{f|大事|だいじ}だということですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply plays the teacher's point back to check that you followed it. It makes
no comment on how the point was delivered, so it is safe with anyone.

(b) adds an unspoken "…and you could have said so in one line." Aimed at a teacher
or a superior that is a small rudeness, even though nothing in the sentence is
grammatically different. Save <code>{f|要|よう}するに</code> for summarising your
own words, or for a situation where you mean the edge.

</details>
