# ～たまえ

**Meaning** · an old-fashioned command spoken downward - "do this" from someone who assumes he outranks you

<code>たまえ</code> is the imperative of <code>{f|給|たま}う</code>, an honorific verb
meaning "to bestow", so a thousand years ago it was a *softened* order. It is not
soft now. In modern Japanese it survives as the voice of the professor, the
commanding officer and the old company president - overwhelmingly in fiction. Learn
to read it; say <code>～てください</code> instead.

## Formation

<code>たまえ</code> attaches to the ます-stem, exactly where <code>なさい</code> goes.
It does not conjugate: there is no past, no negative and no polite form.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb / る-verb) | ます-stem + たまえ | {f|座|すわ}る → {f|座|すわ}りたまえ |
| する-verb | し + たまえ | {f|説明|せつめい}する → {f|説明|せつめい}したまえ |
| {f|来|く}る | き + たまえ | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}たまえ |
| Negative | no negative form exists | {f|騒|さわ}ぐ → {f|騒|さわ}ぐな（not ❌{f|騒|さわ}ぎたまえない） |
| Spelling | usually kana, sometimes {f|給|たま}え | {f|読|よ}む → {f|読|よ}みたまえ |
| Same verb as an honorific auxiliary | ます-stem + たまう（literary） | {f|与|あた}える → {f|与|あた}えたまう |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the two regular classes ({f|五段|ごだん} / {f|一段|いちだん}, Group 1 /
Group 2). {f|来|く}る is irregular, and its stem here is **き**, the same stem as
{f|来|き}ます - not こ, which belongs to {f|来|こ}ない and {f|来|こ}られる.

</div>

## Examples

These are the kinds of line the form actually occurs in: dialogue by a superior in
a novel, a manga or a dubbed film.

<pre>
まあ、{f|座|すわ}り<b>たまえ</b>。
Well now - sit down.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|君|きみ}、ちょっと{f|来|き}<b>たまえ</b>。
You there - come here a moment.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あす}までに{f|報告書|ほうこくしょ}を{f|出|だ}し<b>たまえ</b>。
Have the report on my desk by tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|心配|しんぱい}するな。あとは{f|私|わたし}に{f|任|まか}せ<b>たまえ</b>。
Don't worry. Leave the rest to me.
</pre>

<pre>
「{f|失礼|しつれい}します。」「うむ、{f|入|はい}り<b>たまえ</b>。」
"Excuse me." "Mm. Come in."
</pre>

<pre>
その{f|辺|へん}でやめておき<b>たまえ</b>。{f|君|きみ}のためを{f|思|おも}って{f|言|い}っているのだ。
That's enough of that. I'm saying it for your own good.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|諸君|しょくん}、この{f|事件|じけん}が{f|何|なに}を{f|意味|いみ}するのか、よく{f|考|かんが}え<b>たまえ</b>。
Gentlemen, think hard about what this incident means.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|若|わか}いうちに、できるだけ{f|多|おお}くの{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}み<b>たまえ</b>。
While you are young, read as many books as you possibly can.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Do not produce this form.** It is not a register that is available to a modern
learner, and there is no situation in ordinary life where it is the right choice.
Where you will meet it is on the page and the screen: pre-war and mid-century
novels, detective fiction, manga and anime, and above all the dubbing and
subtitling of foreign films, where <code>たまえ</code> is the standard way to make an
English-speaking colonel or headmaster sound like an English-speaking colonel or
headmaster. Between friends it appears occasionally as a joke - someone doing the
voice on purpose.

Three things mark it. It is strongly **male**. It is **old-fashioned**: even the
older men who might once have used it are mostly gone from workplaces. And it is
**downward** - it announces that the speaker considers the listener his junior.
That last point is why it cannot be rescued by tone. A student saying
{f|座|すわ}りたまえ to a classmate is not being quaint; they are being rude, or
funny, and it will be read as one of the two.

Older reference books sometimes describe <code>たまえ</code> as a "polite command".
That was true in the Meiji era, when it was the mild alternative to a bare
imperative, and it is why the form is filed as N2 grammar at all. It has not been
true in living speech for a long time.

Use instead: <code>～てください</code> for anyone at all in normal life,
<code>～なさい</code> when an adult is instructing a child or a teacher a class, and
the plain imperative <code>{f|座|すわ}れ</code> when bluntness is genuinely wanted -
a coach, an emergency, a shout.

One relative worth recognising: the same verb survives as an honorific auxiliary
<code>～たまう</code> in literary and religious prose -
<code>{f|神|かみ}が{f|与|あた}えたまう{f|恵|めぐ}み</code>. That is a different beast
again, respectful rather than commanding, and equally not for production.

## Contrast

### ～たまえ vs ～てください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～てください</code> is what you say. <code>～たまえ</code>
is what you read. Whenever you are tempted by the second, use the first.

</div>

<pre>
{f|詳|くわ}しいことは、あとで{f|説明|せつめい}し<b>たまえ</b>。
Explain the details to me later. （fiction - a superior speaking）
</pre>

<pre>
{f|詳|くわ}しいことは、あとで{f|説明|せつめい}し<b>てください</b>。
Please explain the details later. （anyone, any workplace）
</pre>

The propositional content is identical. What changes is everything social: the
first says the speaker is senior, male and probably in a book; the second says
nothing about the speaker at all, which is what you want from a request form.

### ～たまえ vs ～なさい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are commands from above and both take the ます-stem, but
<code>なさい</code> is alive and <code>たまえ</code> is not.

</div>

<pre>
{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|終|お}わらせてから{f|遊|あそ}び<b>なさい</b>。
Finish your homework before you go and play.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|終|お}わったら{f|報告|ほうこく}し<b>たまえ</b>。
Report to me when you've finished.
</pre>

<code>なさい</code> has a defined modern home: parent to child, teacher to pupil,
and printed instructions on exam papers. It sounds caring or strict depending on
delivery, and adults do not say it to each other. <code>たまえ</code> has no
equivalent live home - only the fictional one - and where <code>なさい</code> leans
maternal and instructional, <code>たまえ</code> leans masculine and hierarchical.

### ～たまえ vs the plain imperative

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|座|すわ}れ</code> is blunt. <code>{f|座|すわ}りたまえ</code>
is not politer to a modern ear - it is haughtier.

</div>

<pre>
おい、そこに<b>{f|座|すわ}れ</b>。
Oi - sit down there.
</pre>

<pre>
そこに{f|座|すわ}り<b>たまえ</b>。
Take a seat there. （said by someone who is about to interrogate you）
</pre>

The plain imperative is rough but socially readable: a coach, a parent losing
patience, a warning shout. <code>たまえ</code> adds a layer of assumed rank on top,
which is why it feels theatrical rather than merely rude. Neither belongs in a
learner's active speech; of the two, the plain imperative is at least a form you
will need to understand every day.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|座|すわ}る<b>たまえ</b> → ✅ {f|座|すわ}り<b>たまえ</b>
  It attaches to the ます-stem, in the same slot as <code>なさい</code>, not to the
  dictionary form.

- ❌ {f|来|こ}<b>たまえ</b> → ✅ {f|来|き}<b>たまえ</b>
  {f|来|く}る takes こ only in {f|来|こ}ない, {f|来|こ}よう and {f|来|こ}られる. The
  ます-stem is き.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}、こちらに{f|座|すわ}り<b>たまえ</b>。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}、こちらにお{f|掛|か}け<b>ください</b>。
  The form points downward. Aimed at anyone senior it is not merely impolite, it is
  faintly absurd.

- ❌ {f|静|しず}かにし<b>たまえない</b> → ✅ {f|静|しず}かにし<b>ないでくれ</b>（or {f|騒|さわ}ぐな）
  <code>たまえ</code> is a frozen imperative. It has no negative, no past and no
  ます-form.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this line, and who is speaking?
{f|君|きみ}、この{f|数字|すうじ}をもう{f|一度|いちど}{f|調|しら}べ{f|直|なお}し<b>たまえ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In fiction - a novel, a manga, or a dubbed film - spoken by an older man to a
junior: a professor to a student, a department head to a new employee, an officer
to a subordinate. The combination of {f|君|きみ} as the address term and
<code>たまえ</code> as the command is close to a signature for that character type.
In a real modern office you would hear
もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|調|しら}べ{f|直|なお}してもらえますか.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: まず{f|私|わたし}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}き<b>たまえ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

まず{f|私|わたし}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}い<b>てください</b>。

<code>～てください</code> is the default replacement in almost every case. If the
speaker is an adult addressing a child, {f|聞|き}き<b>なさい</b> also works; if the
situation is urgent and informal, {f|聞|き}い<b>てくれ</b>.

</details>

**3.** Find and fix the errors: {f|明日|あす}の{f|朝|あさ}、{f|九時|くじ}に{f|来|こ}たまえない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|明日|あす}の{f|朝|あさ}、{f|九時|くじ}に{f|来|き}<b>たまえ</b>。

Two faults. The stem of {f|来|く}る before <code>たまえ</code> is き, not こ. And
<code>たまえ</code> cannot be negated - to forbid something you would need a
different form altogether, such as {f|来|く}るな.

</details>

**4.** You are a new employee and want your section chief to look over a document.
Which is possible: ご{f|確認|かくにん}し<b>たまえ</b> / {f|確認|かくにん}し<b>てください</b> /
ご{f|確認|かくにん}いただけますか - and why?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ご{f|確認|かくにん}いただけますか is the right choice; {f|確認|かくにん}してください is
grammatical but a little bare when aimed upward. ご{f|確認|かくにん}したまえ is
impossible twice over: <code>たまえ</code> is a downward command, and it does not
combine with the honorific ご frame - the two are pulling in opposite directions.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|待|ま}て。
(b) {f|待|ま}ち<b>たまえ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the plain imperative: sharp, urgent, and socially neutral in the sense that
anybody might shout it - a policeman, a friend stopping you from stepping into the
road. You will hear it in real life.

(b) is the same instruction issued by someone claiming authority over you, in an
old-fashioned masculine voice. It is unhurried where (a) is urgent, and it belongs
to a character rather than to a person. A learner needs to understand (b) and
should never say it.

</details>
