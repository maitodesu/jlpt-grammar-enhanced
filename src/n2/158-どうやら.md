# どうやら

**Meaning** · it looks as though; apparently; by all appearances - flags at the front of the sentence that what follows is the speaker's reading of the signs, not a fact

`どうやら` is an early warning. Dropped in near the head of the sentence, it tells the
listener that the speaker has been piecing evidence together and has arrived, tentatively,
somewhere. The conclusion itself is delivered by the ending - `らしい`, `ようだ`, `みたいだ`
- and `どうやら` is what colours it: *the picture is becoming clear, and I do not much like
where it points.*

## Usage

`どうやら` is an adverb, so it conjugates for nothing and attaches to nothing. Two things
govern where it goes and what it needs.

**It wants a hedged ending.** Almost every natural sentence with `どうやら` finishes in
`ようだ`, `らしい`, `みたいだ`, `そうだ`, `のだろう` or `かもしれない`. The adverb sets up a
guess; something has to complete it. A flat assertion after `どうやら` leaves the listener
waiting - the exception is the second meaning below.

**It goes early.** Sentence-initial is the default, and it can also follow the topic:
<code>{f|彼|かれ}はどうやら{f|気|き}づいていないらしい</code>. Late in the sentence it
sounds displaced.

The second meaning is unrelated and worth knowing: **"somehow, just barely"**, followed by
a plain past. <code>どうやら{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}った</code> is "we scraped in". The
reduplicated <code>どうやらこうやら</code> is the emphatic version, and it is always about
managing something with difficulty. Nothing tentative about it - the outcome is known, and
the speaker is exhaling.

## Examples

<pre>
<b>どうやら</b>{f|道|みち}を{f|間違|まちが}えたようだ。
Looks as though we've taken a wrong turn.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>どうやら</b>{f|彼|かれ}は{f|何|なに}も{f|聞|き}いていないらしい。
Apparently nobody has told him anything.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}が{f|下|さ}がらないので、<b>どうやら</b>{f|明日|あした}の{f|遠足|えんそく}は{f|無理|むり}みたいだ。
His temperature isn't coming down, so it's looking like tomorrow's outing is off.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|長|なが}い{f|話|はな}し{f|合|あ}いのすえ、<b>どうやら</b>{f|全員|ぜんいん}が{f|納得|なっとく}してくれたようです。
After a long discussion, it seems everyone has come round.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|大雨|おおあめ}で{f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まったが、<b>どうやら</b>{f|会議|かいぎ}には{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}った。
The trains stopped in the downpour, but I just about made the meeting.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. `どうやら` is at home in conversation, in news copy and in narration
alike, and it carries no marking for age or gender. What it does carry is a faint
reluctance: the speaker did not want this conclusion, but the evidence keeps arriving.
That is why so many `どうやら` sentences are about rain, delays, illness and things going
wrong.

In polite speech it is a gentle way to hand over bad news, because it puts the blame on
the evidence rather than on the speaker:
<code>どうやら{f|今週中|こんしゅうじゅう}の{f|納品|のうひん}は{f|難|むずか}しいようです</code>
softens a missed deadline into something both parties are merely observing. That makes it
genuinely useful at work.

It also has a nice self-deprecating use, where the speaker discovers something about
themselves: <code>どうやら{f|私|わたし}は{f|向|む}いていないらしい</code> - "it appears I'm
not cut out for this."

## Contrast

### どうやら vs どうも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どうやら` reports a **conclusion taking shape**. `どうも` reports a
**feeling that will not go away**, and it is the one that pairs with negatives.

</div>

<pre>
<b>どうやら</b>{f|財布|さいふ}を{f|落|お}としたらしい。
It appears I've dropped my wallet.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>どうも</b>この{f|説明|せつめい}が{f|分|わ}からない。
Somehow I just can't make sense of this explanation.
</pre>

They overlap in the middle - <code>どうも{f|変|へん}だ</code> and
<code>どうやら{f|変|へん}らしい</code> are both natural - but the edges are clean. `どうも`
is the one that goes with a plain negative to mean "I can't quite …", and the one that
lives in fixed greetings (<code>どうもありがとう</code>, <code>どうもすみません</code>, and
`どうも` alone as an all-purpose hello). `どうやら` does none of that. Conversely `どうやら`
is the one that means "barely", which `どうも` cannot do.

### どうやら vs たぶん・おそらく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たぶん` estimates a **probability**. `どうやら` reads the **evidence
in front of it**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}は<b>たぶん</b>{f|雪|ゆき}になるでしょう。
It'll probably snow next week.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|外|そと}が{f|白|しろ}い。<b>どうやら</b>{f|夜中|よなか}に{f|降|ふ}ったらしい。
Everything outside is white. Looks as though it came down in the night.
</pre>

`たぶん` needs nothing to look at; you can say it about next year. `どうやら` needs
something observable - a symptom, a silence, a wet street - and so it usually talks about
now or the very recent past. `おそらく` is `たぶん` in a suit: same job, formal register,
common in writing and speeches.

### どうやら vs らしい・ようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they are not rivals. `らしい` and `ようだ` are the **ending**;
`どうやら` is the **flag at the start** that announces one is coming.

</div>

<pre>
{f|店|みせ}が{f|閉|し}まっている。<b>どうやら</b>{f|定休日|ていきゅうび}のようだ。
The shop's shut. Seems it's their regular closing day.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|店|みせ}が{f|閉|し}まっている。{f|定休日|ていきゅうび}のようだ。
The shop's shut. Must be their regular closing day.
</pre>

Both sentences are correct, and the second is complete without the adverb. Adding
`どうやら` does two things: it warns the listener a guess is coming, and it adds the sense
that the speaker has been working the problem for a moment rather than reacting instantly.
Dropping the ending instead is what does not work - the adverb alone cannot carry the
guess.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>どうやら</b>{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る。 → ✅ <b>どうやら</b>{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}りそうだ。
  The adverb sets up a guess and then needs something to finish it. Left with a flat
  assertion, the sentence sounds cut off.

- ❌ <b>どうやら</b>ありがとうございました。 → ✅ <b>どうも</b>ありがとうございました。
  The fixed greetings belong to `どうも` and only to `どうも`. `どうやら` never appears in a
  set phrase of thanks or apology, and the substitution is a very common early slip.

- ❌ <b>どうやら</b>この{f|味|あじ}が{f|好|す}きになれない。 → ✅ <b>どうも</b>この{f|味|あじ}が{f|好|す}きになれない。
  "Somehow I just can't …" with a plain negative is `どうも` territory. `どうやら` needs a
  guess-marking ending, and there is none here.

- ❌ <b>どうやら</b>{f|彼|かれ}が{f|犯人|はんにん}に{f|違|ちが}いない。 → ✅ <b>どうやら</b>{f|彼|かれ}が{f|犯人|はんにん}らしい。
  <code>に{f|違|ちが}いない</code> claims near-certainty, and `どうやら` announces
  tentativeness. Putting them in one sentence has the speaker hedging and insisting at the
  same time.

## Practice

**1.** {f|窓|まど}の{f|外|そと}が{f|静|しず}かだ。____{f|雨|あめ}はやんだらしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>どうやら</b>{f|雨|あめ}はやんだらしい。

The silence outside is the evidence, and `らしい` at the end is the guess it produces.
`どうやら` is the flag that ties the two together - exactly the shape this adverb wants.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|何度|なんど}{f|読|よ}んでも、（どうやら・どうも）この{f|文|ぶん}の{f|意味|いみ}が{f|分|わ}からない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

（どうも）この{f|文|ぶん}の{f|意味|いみ}が{f|分|わ}からない。

With a plain negative meaning "I somehow can't", only `どうも` works. `どうやら` would need
a hedged ending such as `らしい` or `ようだ`, and here the speaker is not guessing at all -
they are describing their own stubborn incomprehension.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) <b>どうやら</b>{f|試験|しけん}に{f|受|う}かったようだ。
(b) <b>どうやら</b>{f|試験|しけん}に{f|受|う}かった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the guessing `どうやら`: the result is not official yet, but the signs - a friend's
message, a number on a list - point that way, and `ようだ` keeps it tentative.

(b) is the other `どうやら` entirely, the one that means "just barely". The result is known
and confirmed; what the speaker is conveying is relief that it was a near thing. The plain
past ending is what forces this second reading.

</details>
