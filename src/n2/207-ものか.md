# ～ものか

**Meaning** · like hell I will; as if that were true - a question in shape, a flat and angry refusal in force

<code>{f|負|ま}けるものか</code> looks like "is it a thing that I lose?" and means "I
am **not** going to lose." The form is positive and the meaning is negative, because
it is a rhetorical question thrown at the listener or at yourself. What comes with
it is emotion: defiance, contempt, or a refusal to believe a word of it. Recognise
it on the page; in your own speech say <code>{f|絶対|ぜったい}に～ない</code> or
<code>～わけがない</code>.

## Formation

The clause in front is in noun-modifying shape, because <code>もの</code> is a noun.
The verb is almost always in the **plain non-past**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ものか | {f|負|ま}ける → {f|負|ま}けるものか |
| Verb (potential) | potential + ものか | {f|信|しん}じられる → {f|信|しん}じられるものか |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものか | {f|怖|こわ}い → {f|怖|こわ}いものか |
| な-adjective | ～な + ものか | {f|平気|へいき}だ → {f|平気|へいき}なものか |
| Noun | ～な + ものか | {f|本物|ほんもの}だ → {f|本物|ほんもの}なものか |
| Casual contraction | もんか | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}くもんか |
| Rough emphatic | もんか + よ | {f|泣|な}く → {f|泣|な}くもんかよ |
| Polite in shape | ものですか・もんですか | {f|許|ゆる}す → {f|許|ゆる}すものですか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - do not put `ない` in front of it to get a negative meaning. The pattern is
already negative. <code>{f|行|い}くものか</code> means "I'm not going";
<code>{f|行|い}けないものか</code> is a completely different sentence, a wistful "isn't
there some way I could go?" - see the third contrast below.

</div>

## Examples

These are the kinds of line the pattern really occurs in: fiction dialogue, interior
monologue, narration.

<pre>
{f|負|ま}ける<b>ものか</b>。まだ{f|終|お}わっていない。
I will not lose. It isn't over yet.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなやつに{f|頼|たの}む<b>もんか</b>。
Like hell I'd ask him for help.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|二度|にど}とこの{f|店|みせ}に{f|来|く}る<b>もんか</b>。
I am never setting foot in this shop again.
</pre>

<pre>
そんな{f|話|はなし}、{f|信|しん}じられる<b>ものか</b>。
As if anyone could believe a story like that.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|平気|へいき}な<b>ものか</b>。{f|手|て}が{f|震|ふる}えているじゃないか。
Fine, is he? His hands are shaking.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|人|ひと}が{f|本当|ほんとう}のことを{f|言|い}う<b>ものですか</b>。
That man, tell the truth? Hardly.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|何|なに}があっても、この{f|子|こ}だけは{f|渡|わた}す<b>ものか</b>と{f|思|おも}った。
Whatever happened, I told myself, this child I will not hand over.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなところで{f|終|お}われる<b>ものか</b>。やり{f|残|のこ}したことが{f|多|おお}すぎる。
I can't be finished here - there is far too much I haven't done.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**Recognise this pattern; do not reach for it.** It is not a neutral way of saying
no. Every occurrence carries heat, and the heat is the point: refusal
(<code>{f|謝|あやま}るものか</code>, "I'll never apologise"), contempt
(<code>{f|頼|たの}むもんか</code>), or open disbelief
(<code>{f|信|しん}じられるものか</code>). You will meet it constantly in novels, manga
and anime, in sports narration, in song lyrics, and in the internal voice of a
character bracing themselves - and you will also hear it in real arguments between
people who know each other well enough to shout.

What makes it a poor choice for a learner is that there is no mild setting. The
casual `もんか` leans male and young and reads as petulant when the speaker is not
genuinely angry - it is the sound of a child refusing to give in. The apparently
polite `ものですか` and `もんですか` are worse, not better: the politeness is the
frame, and what sits inside it is a sneer. <code>いらっしゃるものですか</code> aimed
at a colleague is far more cutting than a plain refusal, and it leans female in the
stereotype. Say it by accident and you have picked a fight in keigo.

Note also that the target is often not the listener at all - a great many examples
are addressed to the speaker's own resolve, which is why narration is full of them.
That does not make the form usable in conversation; it means the sentences you
remember from fiction are mostly monologue.

Say instead: <code>{f|絶対|ぜったい}に～ません</code> or
<code>{f|二度|にど}と～ません</code> when you are refusing;
<code>～わけがありません</code> or <code>～はずがありません</code> when you disbelieve
something; and <code>それはちょっと…</code> or
<code>{f|遠慮|えんりょ}しておきます</code> when you simply want to say no politely.

## Contrast

### ～ものか vs {f|絶対|ぜったい}に～ない・{f|二度|にど}と～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this is the modern equivalent for the **refusal** sense. Same
content, no shouting.

</div>

<pre>
あんな{f|会社|かいしゃ}で{f|働|はたら}く<b>ものか</b>。
Like hell I'd work for a company like that.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|会社|かいしゃ}で{f|働|はたら}く<b>つもりは{f|絶対|ぜったい}にありません</b>。
I have absolutely no intention of working for that company.
</pre>

The second is the sentence you can say to a careers adviser. It is just as firm -
<code>{f|絶対|ぜったい}に</code> is doing the work the rhetorical question was doing -
but it states a decision rather than performing an outburst. In casual speech among
friends, <code>{f|二度|にど}と{f|行|い}かない</code> covers the "never again" flavour.

### ～ものか vs ～わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - this is the modern equivalent for the **disbelief** sense:
"there is no way that is so."

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}がそんなことを{f|言|い}う<b>ものか</b>。
As if he would say a thing like that.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}がそんなことを{f|言|い}う<b>わけがありません</b>。
There's no way he would say a thing like that.
</pre>

<code>わけがない</code> is fully usable in any register, spoken or written, and it is
what you want in a meeting, in an argument you intend to win on the facts, and in
anything you publish. It denies the proposition; `ものか` denies it *and* looks down
on whoever raised it, which is why the two are not interchangeable even though the
English translation often is.

### ～ものか vs ～ないものか・どうしたものか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same string with `ない` in front, or with a question word,
is not a refusal at all. It is a wish, or a deliberation.

</div>

<pre>
{f|何|なん}とかならない<b>ものか</b>。
Isn't there something that can be done about this?
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|件|けん}、どうした<b>ものか</b>。
What on earth should we do about this business?
</pre>

<code>～ないものか</code> is a sigh of longing - the speaker wants a way out and
cannot see one - and it is genuinely usable in adult speech and writing, including
in a workplace. <code>どうしたものか</code> is the sound of someone thinking a problem
over, and it too is unmarked. Neither is hostile. The difference is entirely in what
precedes `ものか`, so read that part before you decide what tone you are looking at.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あれが{f|本物|ほんもの}<b>だものか</b>。 → ✅ あれが{f|本物|ほんもの}<b>なものか</b>。
  <code>もの</code> is a noun, so a noun predicate links with `な`, never with `だ`.

- ❌ もう{f|行|い}か<b>ないものか</b>。（intending "I'm never going again"） → ✅ もう{f|行|い}く<b>ものか</b>。
  The pattern is negative already. Adding `ない` turns an angry refusal into a
  wistful "isn't there some way not to go?"

- ❌ 「{f|飲|の}みに{f|行|い}きませんか。」「{f|行|い}く<b>もんですか</b>。」 → ✅ 「すみません、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|遠慮|えんりょ}しておきます。」
  The `です` does not make it polite. Answering an invitation this way tells your
  colleague you find the suggestion contemptible.

- ❌ {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|行|い}った<b>ものか</b>。 → ✅ {f|昨日|きのう}は{f|行|い}かなかった。
  The rhetorical refusal takes the plain non-past. A past-tense verb in front of
  `ものか` belongs to the deliberating pattern instead - as in
  <code>どう{f|答|こた}えたものか</code>, "how should one answer this?" - so it will
  not be read as a denial.

## Practice

**1.** What does the speaker mean by
「あいつに{f|謝|あやま}る<b>もんか</b>。」? Choose:
(a) I suppose I ought to apologise. (b) I will never apologise to him.
(c) Should I apologise to him?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b)** I will never apologise to him.

The positive verb plus `もんか` is a rhetorical question with the force of a flat
refusal, and the speaker is angry. (c) would need
<code>{f|謝|あやま}ったものか</code> or <code>{f|謝|あやま}るべきだろうか</code> - the
deliberating use, which takes a different shape.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in neutral modern Japanese, keeping the meaning:
そんな{f|噂|うわさ}、{f|本当|ほんとう}な<b>ものか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そんな{f|噂|うわさ}が{f|本当|ほんとう}である<b>はずがありません</b>。（or {f|本当|ほんとう}である<b>わけがありません</b>）

This is the disbelief sense, so the modern equivalents are
<code>はずがない</code> and <code>わけがない</code>. Both can be said out loud in any
company; the original cannot.

</details>

**3.** Where would you expect to meet this line, and who is speaking?
ここで{f|倒|たお}れる<b>ものか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In fiction or narration - a character's interior monologue at a low point in a
novel, a manga or a sports drama, or a voice-over. It is addressed to the speaker's
own resolve rather than to another person. A real athlete interviewed after a match
would say something like
<code>{f|絶対|ぜったい}にあきらめたくなかったです</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼女|かのじょ}の{f|話|はなし}が{f|嘘|うそ}だものか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼女|かのじょ}の{f|話|はなし}が{f|嘘|うそ}<b>なものか</b>。

A noun attaches to <code>もの</code> with `な`. Note that the corrected sentence
means "her story is certainly **not** a lie" - the positive <code>{f|嘘|うそ}な</code>
is denied by `ものか`, which is the opposite of what an English reader expects at
first glance.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance, and say which one you could use at work:
(a) {f|何|なん}とかなる<b>ものか</b>。
(b) {f|何|なん}とかならない<b>ものか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the rhetorical refusal: "as if this is going to sort itself out." It is
bitter, and it is aimed at whoever suggested that things would be fine. Keep it for
reading.

(b) is the wish: "isn't there something we can do?" The speaker wants a solution and
is appealing, not attacking. This one is perfectly usable in a workplace - said to
yourself or to a colleague over a problem - and in writing it appears in editorials
and opinion pieces.

The single `ない` reverses the entire social meaning of the sentence, which is the
main reason this pattern is worth being able to read accurately.

</details>
