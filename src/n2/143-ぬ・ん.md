# ～ぬ・～ん

**Meaning** · the classical negative - the older ancestor of `ない`, still alive in set phrases, signage, fiction and casual speech

Before `ない` took over, Japanese negated verbs with `ぬ`. The old form never fully
died: it survives frozen inside words（{f|思|おも}わぬ, {f|見知|みし}らぬ）, inside
proverbs, inside formal set phrases, and - contracted to `ん` - inside perfectly
ordinary spoken Japanese（{f|知|し}らん, {f|分|わ}からん）. You will meet it on a sign
or in a novel long before anyone says it to you.

**What is denied: exactly what `ない` denies.** There is no difference in meaning at
all. `ぬ` is `ない` in older clothes, and everything on this page is about *where the
clothes are appropriate* - which is a question of register, not of logic. It is worth
learning because a learner who does not recognise
<code>{f|見|み}て{f|見|み}ぬふり</code> will simply lose the sentence.

## Formation

`ぬ` attaches to the same base as `ない` - the {f|未然形|みぜんけい}, the "a-row" stem - with two
irregular verbs to memorise.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | ない-base + ぬ | {f|知|し}らない → {f|知|し}らぬ |
| る-verb | ない-base + ぬ | {f|見|み}ない → {f|見|み}ぬ |
| する | せ + ぬ | しない → せぬ |
| {f|来|く}る | {f|来|こ} + ぬ | {f|来|こ}ない → {f|来|こ}ぬ |
| Colloquial contraction | ない-base + ん | {f|分|わ}からない → {f|分|わ}からん |
| する (colloquial) | せ + ん | しない → せん |
| Obligation | ない-base + ねば | {f|行|い}かない → {f|行|い}かねばならない |
| Before a noun | ～ぬ + Noun | {f|思|おも}わぬ → {f|思|おも}わぬ{f|結果|けっか} |
| Literary adnominal | ～ざる + Noun | {f|知|し}らざる → {f|知|し}らざる{f|世界|せかい} |
| Fossil inside ません | ませ + ぬ | - → {f|行|い}きません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - build the `ない` form first, then swap `ない` for `ぬ` or `ん`.
The only two that will catch you out are する → <b>せ</b>ぬ・<b>せ</b>ん and
{f|来|く}る → <b>{f|来|こ}</b>ぬ・<b>{f|来|こ}</b>ん. Note that {f|来|こ} here is the same
base as in {f|来|こ}ない, so the reading is こ, not き.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `ぬ` does not attach to adjectives or to nouns. ❌{f|高|たか}いぬ,
❌{f|学生|がくせい}ぬ. Only verbs take it. And the polite `ません` you learned at N5 is
literally ませ + ぬ - the classical negative has been hiding in your first textbook
all along.

</div>

## Nuance & register

Treat `ぬ` and `ん` as two different problems, because their registers have almost
nothing in common.

**`ぬ` is literary.** In modern Japanese it belongs to proverbs, set phrases, period
drama, narration in fiction, song lyrics, old-fashioned signage and a small number of
formal fixed expressions（{f|変|か}わらぬ, {f|絶|た}えぬ, {f|尽|つ}きぬ）. Saying
「{f|分|わ}かりませぬ」 in a shop would land as a joke or as costume drama. Recognise
it, enjoy it in reading, and use `ない` when you speak. The exception is the frozen
adnominals - {f|思|おも}わぬ{f|事故|じこ}, {f|見知|みし}らぬ{f|人|ひと},
{f|少|すく}なからぬ{f|影響|えいきょう} - which are ordinary modern vocabulary and quite
safe to produce.

**`ん` is alive.** {f|知|し}らん, {f|分|わ}からん, できん, {f|行|い}かん and すまん are
everyday casual speech. The form is strongly associated with western Japan - it is
the default negative across the Kansai region and much of the west - and in eastern
casual speech it tends to sound blunt and to lean masculine, though
{f|知|し}らん and {f|分|わ}からん in particular are used very widely regardless of
speaker. Those are tendencies rather than rules. What is a rule is that `ん` is
**casual**: it does not belong in ます-form speech, in a workplace email, or in
anything you write for marks.

**`ねば`** is the conditional shape of `ぬ` (its {f|仮定形|かていけい}), and
<code>ねばならない</code> is stiff, written, and slightly declamatory - editorials,
speeches, resolutions. In ordinary Japanese you would say
<code>なければならない</code> or <code>ないといけない</code>.

One last practical point. `ぬ` sometimes appears where a modern speaker would use
`ない` purely for **rhythm** - a five- or seven-syllable line in a slogan, a title, a
headline. If a sentence has no other archaic feature and the `ぬ` is doing nothing but
saving a syllable, that is usually what is going on.

## Contrast

### ～ぬ vs ～ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, opposite register. `ない` is the modern
default; `ぬ` marks the sentence as literary, formulaic or deliberately old.

</div>

<pre>
そんな{f|話|はなし}は{f|聞|き}いてい<b>ない</b>。
I haven't heard anything about that.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|過|す}ぎ{f|去|さ}った{f|日|ひ}は{f|二度|にど}と{f|戻|もど}ら<b>ぬ</b>。
The days that have gone will never return.
</pre>

The second is not more formal in the ます sense - it is *literary*, which is a
different axis. A business email is formal and would still use `ない`. Reserve `ぬ`
for writing that is meant to sound like writing.

### ～ん as a negative vs ～ん as `の`

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - look at the syllable in front. `ん` after the **a-row base**
（{f|行|い}か-, {f|知|し}ら-, {f|分|わ}から-）is the negative. `ん` after a **plain
form**（{f|行|い}く-, {f|来|き}た-）is the contraction of `の`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|行|い}か<b>ん</b>。
I'm not going tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|行|い}く<b>ん</b>だ。
The thing is, I'm going tomorrow.
</pre>

One syllable apart and directly contradictory - this is the single most expensive
confusion on this page. The `の` version is what you met at N5 in
<code>んです</code>, and it always sits after a full plain form. The negative version
sits on the stem, and it can end the sentence bare.

Note also 「{f|行|い}かんの?」, where **both** appear: the negative `ん` plus the
question `の`. "You're not going?"

### ～ぬ vs ～ず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same auxiliary in two shapes. `ぬ` **modifies a noun or ends
a sentence**; `ず` **connects to what comes next**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|何|なに}も{f|言|い}わ<b>ず</b>に{f|部屋|へや}を{f|出|で}て{f|行|い}った。
He left the room without saying anything.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|何|なに}も{f|言|い}わ<b>ぬ</b>まま、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|去|さ}った。
Saying nothing, he departed.
</pre>

You already know `ず` and `ずに` from N3, and the two forms are the same word: `ず`
is the connective shape, `ぬ` the one that attaches to a noun or closes a clause. The
same family gives you `ざる` for literary noun-modification
（{f|許|ゆる}されざる{f|行為|こうい}）and `ねば` for the conditional.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|何|なに}も<b>しぬ</b>。 → ✅ {f|何|なに}も<b>せぬ</b>。
  する has the irregular base せ-. And the wrong form collides with a real and very
  different verb: {f|死|し}ぬ, "to die".

- ❌ {f|誰|だれ}も<b>{f|来|き}ぬ</b>{f|夜|よる}。 → ✅ {f|誰|だれ}も<b>{f|来|こ}ぬ</b>{f|夜|よる}。
  {f|来|く}る takes the こ- base here, the same one as in {f|来|こ}ない. The き- base
  belongs to {f|来|き}ます and {f|来|き}っこない.

- ❌ この{f|店|みせ}は<b>{f|安|やす}くぬ</b>。 → ✅ この{f|店|みせ}は<b>{f|安|やす}くない</b>。
  `ぬ` attaches to verbs only. Adjectives and nouns have no classical negative of
  this shape available to them in modern Japanese.

- ❌（{f|会議|かいぎ}で）その{f|件|けん}は<b>{f|存|ぞん}じませぬ</b>。 → ✅ その{f|件|けん}は<b>{f|存|ぞん}じません</b>。
  `ません` already contains the `ぬ`. Adding another one produces a form that reads as
  costume drama.

## Practice

**1.** しない → ____（{f|古|ふる}い{f|形|かたち}で）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

しない → <b>せぬ</b>（{f|話|はな}し{f|言葉|ことば}では <b>せん</b>）

する is irregular: its negative base is せ-, not し-. Writing しぬ would produce
{f|死|し}ぬ, an entirely unrelated verb, which is why this one is worth drilling.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|田中|たなか}さんは（{f|来|こ}ん・{f|来|く}るん）よ。（meaning "Tanaka isn't coming"）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|田中|たなか}さんは<b>{f|来|こ}ん</b>よ。

The negative `ん` sits on the stem {f|来|こ}-, the same base as {f|来|こ}ない.
<code>{f|来|く}るんよ</code> uses the other `ん` - the contraction of `の` - and means
"he *is* coming", which is the opposite.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, using the classical negative: "an unexpected visitor"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|思|おも}わぬ</b>{f|来客|らいきゃく}

<code>{f|思|おも}わぬ</code> is one of the frozen adnominals that survive in ordinary
modern Japanese, alongside {f|見知|みし}らぬ and {f|変|か}わらぬ. These are safe to
produce; a freshly invented ～ぬ form is not.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|係員|かかりいん}{f|以外|いがい}は{f|中|なか}に{f|入|はい}るぬこと。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|係員|かかりいん}{f|以外|いがい}は{f|中|なか}に<b>{f|入|はい}らぬこと</b>。（modern: {f|入|はい}らないこと）

`ぬ` goes on the ない-base, not on the dictionary form:
{f|入|はい}らない → {f|入|はい}らぬ. On a real sign today you would far more likely see
{f|入|はい}らないでください or {f|立入禁止|たちいりきんし}.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|明日|あした}までに{f|決|き}め<b>ねば</b>ならない。
(b) {f|明日|あした}までに{f|決|き}め<b>なければ</b>ならない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

They mean exactly the same thing. (a) uses the classical conditional of `ぬ` and
sounds weightier and more written - the register of an editorial, a formal report or
a speech, and slightly declamatory in ordinary conversation.

(b) is the everyday modern equivalent and is what you would say out loud. In casual
speech both give way to <code>{f|決|き}めないと</code>, which is what most people
actually use.

</details>
