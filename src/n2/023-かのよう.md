# ～かのようだ・～かのように

**Meaning** · as if; just as though - compares a real situation to something that is
plainly not the case

The `か` in the middle is the question particle, and it is doing real work:
<code>かのようだ</code> says "it is like *the question of whether* this were so".
That extra step is why the pattern always feels counterfactual. Plain
<code>ようだ</code> can mean "it seems", leaving the door open; <code>かのようだ</code>
shuts it. The thing compared to is not true, and both speaker and listener know it.

## Formation

<code>か</code> follows a plain-form clause, so nouns and な-adjectives need
<code>である</code> to become one.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + かのようだ | {f|見|み}た → {f|見|み}たかのようだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + かのようだ | {f|知|し}らない → {f|知|し}らないかのようだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + かのようだ | {f|苦|くる}しい → {f|苦|くる}しいかのようだ |
| な-adjective | ～である + かのようだ | {f|当然|とうぜん}だ → {f|当然|とうぜん}であるかのようだ |
| Noun | ～である + かのようだ | {f|夢|ゆめ} → {f|夢|ゆめ}であるかのようだ |
| Modifying a verb | ～かのように + verb | {f|忘|わす}れた → {f|忘|わす}れたかのように{f|笑|わら}う |
| Modifying a noun | ～かのような + Noun | {f|春|はる}が{f|来|き}た → {f|春|はる}が{f|来|き}たかのような{f|暖|あたた}かさ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `である` before <code>かのよう</code> is not optional in careful
writing. ❌<code>{f|夢|ゆめ}だかのように</code> is wrong, and bare
<code>{f|夢|ゆめ}かのように</code>, though heard, is not what an editor would let
through. Write <code>{f|夢|ゆめ}であるかのように</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the literary variant is <code>かのごとく</code> / <code>かのごとき</code>,
which belongs to speeches, headlines and deliberately archaic prose. Recognise it;
it is not a form to produce at N2.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|夢|ゆめ}を{f|見|み}ている<b>かのようだ</b>。
It's as though I were dreaming.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|何|なに}も{f|知|し}らない<b>かのように</b>{f|笑|わら}っていた。
He smiled as if he knew nothing about it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|部屋|へや}は{f|時間|じかん}が{f|止|と}まった<b>かのように</b>{f|静|しず}かだった。
The room was as quiet as if time had stopped in it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|春|はる}が{f|来|き}た<b>かのような</b>{f|暖|あたた}かさで、{f|上着|うわぎ}がいらなかった。
It was as warm as if spring had arrived, and I didn't need a jacket.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|昨日|きのう}のことなど{f|忘|わす}れた<b>かのように</b>{f|話|はな}しかけてきた。
She started chatting to me as though yesterday had never happened.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|少年|しょうねん}は{f|大人|おとな}である<b>かのような</b>{f|話|はな}し{f|方|かた}をする。
The boy talks as though he were a grown man.
</pre>

<pre>
その{f|記事|きじ}は、{f|事故|じこ}の{f|原因|げんいん}がすでに{f|分|わ}かっている<b>かのように</b>{f|書|か}かれていた。
The article was written as if the cause of the accident were already established.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|外|そと}は{f|真夜中|まよなか}である<b>かのように</b>{f|暗|くら}く、{f|時計|とけい}を{f|見|み}ると{f|正午|しょうご}だった。
Outside it was as dark as midnight, and when I looked at the clock it was noon.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a written pattern. It lives in novels, essays, newspaper features and
formal speeches, and it is the reason a description feels literary rather than
chatty. In ordinary conversation people say <code>みたいに</code>, or
<code>ように</code>, and a learner who deploys
<code>かのように</code> over dinner will sound as though they are narrating
themselves.

What it adds over the <code>ように</code> you already have is not meaning but
*commitment*. <code>ようだ</code> is famously double-jobbed: it does likeness and it
does conjecture, and a sentence like
<code>{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ったようだ</code> means "it seems to have rained", not
"as if it had rained". <code>かのようだ</code> can only do likeness, so it is
unambiguous - and that unambiguity is why writers reach for it in the middle of a
description where a stray "seems" would be confusing.

Because the comparison is understood to be false, the pattern often carries a
critical edge when aimed at a person's behaviour.
<code>まるで{f|自分|じぶん}が{f|決|き}めたかのように{f|話|はな}す</code> is not
neutral: it says he is claiming credit that is not his. Aimed at weather, silence,
light or landscape it is purely descriptive.

`まるで` is its natural partner and appears in front of it constantly. The two are
independent - each works alone - but together they are the standard shape:
<code>まるで～かのように</code>.

## Contrast

### ～かのようだ vs ～ようだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ようだ</code> does two jobs, likeness **and** conjecture.
<code>かのようだ</code> does only likeness, and marks the likeness as untrue.

</div>

<pre>
{f|誰|だれ}か{f|来|き}た<b>ようだ</b>。
Somebody seems to have arrived.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|誰|だれ}か{f|来|き}た<b>かのように</b>、{f|犬|いぬ}が{f|急|きゅう}に{f|吠|ほ}え{f|出|だ}した。
The dog suddenly started barking as though someone had arrived.
</pre>

In the first, somebody probably did arrive. In the second, nobody did. Swapping the
patterns reverses the meaning of the sentence, which makes this the one distinction
on the page you cannot afford to blur.

The corollary is that <code>かのようだ</code> can never be used for a guess. If you
are inferring from evidence - wet ground, a light left on, a closed shutter -
<code>ようだ</code>, `らしい` or <code>みたいだ</code> is the pattern, and
<code>かのようだ</code> would be nonsense.

### ～かのようだ vs ～みたいだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same comparison, opposite ends of the register scale.
<code>みたいだ</code> is casual speech; <code>かのようだ</code> is written prose.

</div>

<pre>
{f|外|そと}、{f|夜|よる}<b>みたい</b>に{f|暗|くら}いね。
It's dark as night out there.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|空|そら}は{f|夜|よる}である<b>かのように</b>{f|暗|くら}かった。
The sky was as dark as though it were night.
</pre>

The content is the same; only the clothes differ. Notice how the grammar follows the
register: <code>みたい</code> attaches straight to a noun, while
<code>かのよう</code> demands <code>である</code>, and the sentence around it moves
into plain written style to match. Mixing them -
❌<code>{f|夜|よる}かのようだったよ</code> - is exactly the kind of register clash
that marks a sentence as learner Japanese.

### ～かのように vs ～ふりをする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ふりをする</code> says the person is **pretending on
purpose**. <code>かのように</code> is the **observer's simile** and claims nothing
about intention.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|寝|ね}た<b>ふりをして</b>いた。
He was pretending to be asleep.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|眠|ねむ}っている<b>かのように</b>{f|動|うご}かなかった。
He lay as still as if he were asleep.
</pre>

The first accuses him of an act. The second only describes how he looked - he may
genuinely have been unconscious, or dead, or simply very still. This is worth
holding onto, because English "as if he were asleep" and "pretending to be asleep"
are much closer in feel than the Japanese pair, and choosing
<code>ふりをする</code> where you meant a simile puts an accusation in the sentence
you did not intend.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|本物|ほんもの}<b>だかのように</b>{f|光|ひか}っていた。 → ✅ {f|本物|ほんもの}<b>であるかのように</b>{f|光|ひか}っていた。
  `か` follows a plain-form clause. A noun becomes one with <code>である</code>,
  never with `だ`.

- ❌ {f|財布|さいふ}を{f|落|お}とした<b>かのようだ</b>。（meaning "I seem to have dropped my wallet"）→ ✅ {f|財布|さいふ}を{f|落|お}とした<b>ようだ</b>。
  <code>かのようだ</code> cannot express a guess. Used here it would mean the wallet
  was not dropped and something merely resembles it having been.

- ❌ まるで{f|子|こ}どもだ。 → ✅ まるで{f|子|こ}ども<b>のようだ</b>。（or {f|子|こ}どもであるかのようだ）
  `まるで` announces a comparison and must be answered by a likeness marker. Left
  bare, the sentence asserts the impossible thing instead of comparing to it.

- ❌ {f|昨日|きのう}のことを{f|忘|わす}れた<b>かのように</b>してください。 → ✅ {f|昨日|きのう}のことを{f|忘|わす}れた<b>ことにして</b>ください。
  You cannot ask somebody to perform a simile. Requests about pretending need
  <code>ふりをする</code> or <code>ことにする</code>.

## Practice

**1.** {f|会場|かいじょう}は{f|誰|だれ}もいない____{f|静|しず}まり{f|返|かえ}っていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|誰|だれ}もいない<b>かのように</b>{f|静|しず}まり{f|返|かえ}っていた

The hall was full of people - that is what makes it a comparison with something
untrue, and therefore a job for <code>かのように</code> rather than
<code>ようだ</code>. The `ない` form attaches directly, with no
<code>である</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|窓|まど}が{f|濡|ぬ}れている。{f|夜中|よなか}に{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った（ようだ・かのようだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|夜中|よなか}に{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った<b>ようだ</b>。

The wet window is evidence, and the speaker is inferring what actually happened.
That is conjecture, which <code>かのようだ</code> cannot express - it would claim
that no rain fell and something merely resembles rain having fallen.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He answered as though the question had been put to somebody else."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}は{f|質問|しつもん}が{f|別|べつ}の{f|人|ひと}に{f|向|む}けられた<b>かのように</b>{f|答|こた}えた。

The question was in fact put to him, so the comparison is counterfactual and
<code>かのように</code> is the right marker. A past-tense plain clause attaches with
no extra material.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼女|かのじょ}の{f|部屋|へや}は{f|美術館|びじゅつかん}だかのような{f|静|しず}けさだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼女|かのじょ}の{f|部屋|へや}は{f|美術館|びじゅつかん}<b>であるかのような</b>{f|静|しず}けさだった。

A noun cannot be followed by `だ` in front of `か`. The written-style copula
<code>である</code> is what turns <code>{f|美術館|びじゅつかん}</code> into a clause
the question particle can attach to - and it also matches the literary register the
rest of the sentence is in.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|何|なに}も{f|聞|き}いていない<b>ふりをした</b>。
(b) {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|何|なに}も{f|聞|き}いていない<b>かのように</b>{f|書類|しょるい}をめくっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) states that he deliberately put on an act. It is a claim about his intentions,
and if you are wrong about them the sentence is simply false.

(b) describes only what the observer saw: the page-turning had the quality of
someone who had heard nothing. He may have been pretending, or he may genuinely have
missed it. The speaker paints the scene and leaves the interpretation to the reader,
which is exactly why the pattern belongs to written narrative.

</details>
