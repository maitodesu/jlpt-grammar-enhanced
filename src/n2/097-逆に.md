# 逆に

**Meaning** · conversely; the other way round - the situation comes out flipped, roles and all

{f|逆|ぎゃく} is the noun "the reverse", and {f|逆|ぎゃく}に is its adverb: *in
reverse*. Where かえって says an effort backfired, {f|逆|ぎゃく}に says the arrow
turned around - the scolder got scolded, the cheap shop turned out to be the
expensive one.

## Usage

{f|逆|ぎゃく}に is an adverb and stands inside the clause it modifies, usually
right before the verb. It does three jobs:

- **A literal swap.** The two sides trade places:
  {f|注意|ちゅうい}したら、{f|逆|ぎゃく}に{f|怒|おこ}られた。
- **The other side of an argument.** Fixed phrases:
  {f|逆|ぎゃく}に{f|言|い}えば…, {f|逆|ぎゃく}に{f|考|かんが}えれば…, meaning "put the
  other way round…".
- **Turning a question back.** {f|逆|ぎゃく}に{f|聞|き}きますが… - "let me ask you
  the same thing".

The related forms are worth knowing because they are ordinary noun grammar:
{f|逆|ぎゃく}だ ("it's the other way round"), {f|逆|ぎゃく}の{f|意見|いけん} ("the
opposite view"), {f|逆|ぎゃく}になる ("to be reversed"). Only the に form is the
adverb.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|注意|ちゅうい}したら、<b>{f|逆|ぎゃく}に</b>{f|怒|おこ}られた。
I told him off and got told off myself instead.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|駅前|えきまえ}のほうが{f|高|たか}いと{f|思|おも}っていたが、<b>{f|逆|ぎゃく}に</b>{f|安|やす}かった。
I'd assumed the shops by the station were pricier, but it was the other way round.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>{f|逆|ぎゃく}に</b>{f|言|い}えば、{f|条件|じょうけん}さえ{f|合|あ}えばいつでもお{f|引|ひ}き{f|受|う}けできます。
Turning that around: as long as the terms suit, we can take it on any time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|助|たす}けるつもりで{f|口|くち}を{f|出|だ}したのに、<b>{f|逆|ぎゃく}に</b>{f|話|はなし}をこじらせてしまった。
I stepped in meaning to help and ended up tangling the whole thing up instead.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very frequent. It appears in business writing, in argument,
and constantly in conversation. Stiffer written registers have
{f|反対|はんたい}に available as a near-interchangeable alternative.

One thing to be aware of rather than to copy: in casual speech, especially among
younger speakers, {f|逆|ぎゃく}に has drifted into a general hedge, something like
"actually" or "if anything", often with no genuine opposite in sight -
「{f|逆|ぎゃく}に、それでいいと{f|思|おも}う」. That is a real and widespread usage, but
it is a spoken habit, and in writing or in an exam answer it will read as
imprecise. Use the word where there is an actual reversal.

The noun {f|逆|ぎゃく} also lives in set phrases worth having:
{f|逆|ぎゃく}に{f|言|い}えば, {f|話|はなし}が{f|逆|ぎゃく}だ ("you've got that
backwards"), {f|逆効果|ぎゃくこうか} ("counterproductive").

## Contrast

### {f|逆|ぎゃく}に vs かえって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - {f|逆|ぎゃく}に flips the **direction**: A did B becomes B did
A. `かえって` says an **intention was defeated**, and the result was the opposite
of what it aimed at.

</div>

<pre>
{f|教|おし}えるつもりが、<b>{f|逆|ぎゃく}に</b>{f|教|おし}わることになった。
I went in to teach and ended up being taught.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|冷|ひ}やしたら、<b>かえって</b>{f|痛|いた}みがひどくなった。
I put ice on it and the pain only got worse.
</pre>

Where a sentence names both sides of a swap - teach and be taught, cheaper and
dearer - only {f|逆|ぎゃく}に fits comfortably. Where the point is that your effort
backfired, both are possible and かえって is the more idiomatic. Many sentences
accept either, and the difference is one of emphasis: direction versus defeat.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|逆|ぎゃく}に、{f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|来|こ}なかった。 → ✅ <b>しかし</b>、{f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|来|こ}なかった。
  {f|逆|ぎゃく}に is not a general "however". Somebody failing to turn up is not
  the reverse of anything.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}の{f|意見|いけん}は{f|逆|ぎゃく}にです。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}の{f|意見|いけん}は{f|逆|ぎゃく}です。
  {f|逆|ぎゃく} is a noun, so a sentence that simply asserts "it's the opposite"
  ends in だ・です. The に only appears when the word is modifying a verb.

- ❌ {f|安|やす}い{f|逆|ぎゃく}に、{f|質|しつ}もいい。 → ✅ {f|安|やす}い。<b>{f|逆|ぎゃく}に</b>{f|質|しつ}が{f|心配|しんぱい}になるほどだ。
  It does not join two clauses the way が or のに do. It belongs to the second
  clause, next to the verb, not between them.

## Practice

**1.** {f|後輩|こうはい}にアドバイスをしたつもりが、____{f|勉強|べんきょう}させられた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|逆|ぎゃく}に</b>{f|勉強|べんきょう}させられた。

Adviser and advised have swapped places, which is the clearest case for this
word. Note that it goes inside the second clause, immediately before the verb.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural adverb:
{f|節約|せつやく}しようと{f|安|やす}い{f|靴|くつ}を{f|買|か}ったら、すぐに{f|壊|こわ}れて（{f|逆|ぎゃく}に・かえって）{f|高|たか}くついた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>かえって</b>{f|高|たか}くついた。

The point is that an attempt to save money defeated itself, which is かえって's
territory. {f|逆|ぎゃく}に is not wrong here, but it puts the stress on the
direction of the outcome rather than on the failed intention.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Put the other way round, if we can't finish by Friday there's no point starting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|逆|ぎゃく}に</b>{f|言|い}えば、{f|金曜日|きんようび}までに{f|終|お}わらないなら{f|始|はじ}める{f|意味|いみ}がない。

"Put the other way round" is the fixed phrase {f|逆|ぎゃく}に{f|言|い}えば, which
restates a point from the opposite side. It opens the sentence and is followed by
the whole restatement.

</details>
