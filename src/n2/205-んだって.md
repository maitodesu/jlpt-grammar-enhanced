# ～んだって

**Meaning** · apparently…, I hear… - passing on something you were told, in casual conversation

`んだって` is <code>のだ</code> plus the quoting `って`. The `って` is what makes it
hearsay; the `ん` is what makes it *news* - it frames the information as the story
behind something, which is why it is the shape gossip and updates naturally take.
With a rising tail it turns round and asks: <code>{f|辞|や}めるんだって？</code>, "is
it true you're quitting?"

## Formation

Everything in front of `んだって` is in plain form, exactly as it would be before
`んだ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + んだって | {f|辞|や}める → {f|辞|や}めるんだって |
| Verb (past) | plain past + んだって | {f|受|う}かった → {f|受|う}かったんだって |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + んだって | {f|来|こ}ない → {f|来|こ}ないんだって |
| い-adjective | plain form + んだって | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いんだって |
| な-adjective | ～な + んだって | {f|苦手|にがて}だ → {f|苦手|にがて}なんだって |
| Noun | ～な + んだって | {f|双子|ふたご}だ → {f|双子|ふたご}なんだって |
| Polite-flavoured | ～んですって | {f|中止|ちゅうし}だ → {f|中止|ちゅうし}なんですって |
| Asking for confirmation | ～んだって？（rising） | {f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す → {f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}すんだって？ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the `な` on nouns and な-adjectives is not optional and not a `だ`. It is
the same `な` that appears in <code>{f|双子|ふたご}なんです</code>. Speakers often add
`さ` on the end - <code>{f|来|く}るんだってさ</code> - which makes the relay sound
more offhand.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}、{f|雨|あめ}な<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently it's going to rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|田中|たなか}さん、{f|来月|らいげつ}{f|結婚|けっこん}する<b>んだって</b>。
I hear Tanaka's getting married next month.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|店|みせ}、{f|先週|せんしゅう}{f|閉|し}まっちゃった<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently that shop closed down last week.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|山田|やまだ}くん、{f|試験|しけん}に{f|受|う}かった<b>んだって</b>？おめでとう。
Yamada - I hear you passed the exam? Congratulations.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|課長|かちょう}、{f|来週|らいしゅう}から{f|大阪|おおさか}に{f|転勤|てんきん}な<b>んですって</b>。
They say the section chief is transferring to Osaka from next week.
</pre>

<pre>
うちの{f|近|ちか}くに{f|大|おお}きな{f|公園|こうえん}ができる<b>んだって</b>。{f|楽|たの}しみだね。
I hear a big park's going in near us. Something to look forward to.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新|あたら}しくできたパン{f|屋|や}、{f|朝|あさ}{f|早|はや}く{f|行|い}かないと{f|売|う}り{f|切|き}れちゃう<b>んだってさ</b>。
Apparently that new bakery sells out unless you get there early.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|妹|いもうと}が{f|言|い}ってたんだけど、あのドラマ、{f|最終回|さいしゅうかい}がとんでもなかった<b>んだって</b>。
My sister was telling me the last episode of that drama was something else.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a conversation word. It belongs to speech and to the writing that imitates
speech - messages, chat, dialogue in fiction - and it is out of place in an essay,
a report or anything addressed to a customer. In those, the same content goes into
`そうだ`, `とのことだ` or <code>と{f|伺|うかが}っております</code>.

The source is usually left vague. `んだって` says "this reached me", not "X told
me", and that vagueness is part of its texture: it is the form news travels in. If
you do want to name the source you put it in front as a separate clause -
<code>{f|母|はは}が{f|言|い}ってたんだけど、…んだって</code>.

`んですって` is the polite-flavoured version, and in adult speech it leans female;
men relaying the same news more often say `んだってさ` or `んだとさ`. Those are
tendencies, not rules, and neither form is formal - `んですって` is polite in shape
but still chatty, so it does not fix the register problem in a business setting.

The rising <code>んだって？</code> is worth handling carefully. It means "I've heard
this about you - true?", and while it is friendly between people who know each
other, aimed at someone you barely know it can sound like you have been discussing
them. <code>ご{f|結婚|けっこん}されるとお{f|聞|き}きしました</code> is the safe version.

One structural limit: you cannot hearsay yourself. Facts you know first-hand -
your own plans, your own feelings - take plain `んだ`, not `んだって`.

## Contrast

### ～んだって vs ～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same information, different room. `そうだ` is the neutral
reporting form; `んだって` is its casual spoken twin.

</div>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}、{f|駅|えき}の{f|前|まえ}にホテルが{f|建|た}つ<b>そうです</b>。
I gather a hotel is going up in front of the station next year.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}、{f|駅|えき}の{f|前|まえ}にホテルが{f|建|た}つ<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently they're putting a hotel up by the station next year.
</pre>

The content is identical. `そうです` can be written down, said to a client, and
used in a news summary; `んだって` cannot. Note too that hearsay `そうだ` attaches to
the plain form with nothing in between - ❌<code>{f|建|た}つなそうです</code> - while
`んだって` inserts `な` after nouns and な-adjectives.

### ～んだって vs ～らしい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `んだって` relays **words somebody said**. `らしい` draws a
conclusion, and needs no speaker behind it at all.

</div>

<pre>
{f|隣|となり}の{f|人|ひと}、{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently the neighbour is moving out. （someone told me so）
</pre>

<pre>
{f|隣|となり}の{f|人|ひと}、{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>らしい</b>。
It looks like the neighbour is moving out. （boxes in the hallway, a van booked）
</pre>

`らしい` covers both reported speech and inference from evidence, so it is the
wider word; `んだって` is specifically a relay. That is why `らしい` can sound
non-committal - it may be your own guess - while `んだって` puts the responsibility
on whoever told you.

### ～んだって vs bare ～って

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `って` can quote **anything** somebody said, including orders
and questions. `んだって` only reports a **statement of fact**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|早|はや}く{f|来|こ}い<b>って</b>。
He says get over here, quick.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}な<b>んだって</b>。
Apparently the meeting is off.
</pre>

You cannot rewrite the first with `んだって`; there is no ❌<code>{f|来|こ}いんだって</code>,
because `のだ` needs a proposition, not a command. Where both are possible - 
<code>{f|中止|ちゅうし}だって</code> versus <code>{f|中止|ちゅうし}なんだって</code> -
the bare `って` is a flat relay of the message, while the `ん` version presents it as
the explanation for what is going on around you, which is why it is the natural
choice when you are breaking news to someone.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あの{f|人|ひと}、{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}<b>だんだって</b>。 → ✅ あの{f|人|ひと}、{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}<b>なんだって</b>。
  Nouns link with `な`. The `だ` of <code>{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}だ</code> cannot sit
  in front of `ん`.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}、{f|来月|らいげつ}{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>んだって</b>。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}、{f|来月|らいげつ}{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}す<b>んだ</b>。
  Your own plans are not hearsay. Reporting yourself this way sounds as though you
  learned of your move from a third party.

- ❌ {f|来|き}ます<b>んだって</b>。 → ✅ {f|来|く}る<b>んだって</b>。
  `ん` takes the plain form in front of it, never ます.

- ❌ （in a written report）{f|市|し}の{f|人口|じんこう}が{f|減|へ}っている<b>んだって</b>。 → ✅ {f|市|し}の{f|人口|じんこう}が{f|減|へ}っている<b>とのことだ</b>。
  The information is fine; the register is not. Written hearsay is `そうだ`,
  `とのことだ` or <code>ということだ</code>.

## Practice

**1.** Fill the blank: {f|山田|やまだ}さん、{f|来週|らいしゅう}から{f|東京|とうきょう}の{f|本社|ほんしゃ}____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|本社|ほんしゃ}<b>なんだって</b>。

A noun predicate takes `な` before `ん`. If you wanted the same news in a written
message to a colleague you would switch to
{f|本社|ほんしゃ}{f|勤務|きんむ}になるそうです.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|部屋|へや}の{f|電気|でんき}がついているから、{f|誰|だれ}か{f|中|なか}にいる（んだって・らしい）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|誰|だれ}か{f|中|なか}にいる<b>らしい</b>。

You are reasoning from the light, not repeating what anyone said, and only `らしい`
covers inference. `んだって` would require somebody to have told you there was
someone in the room.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casual: "I hear the new teacher is really strict."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|今度|こんど}の{f|先生|せんせい}、すごく{f|厳|きび}しい<b>んだって</b>。

An い-adjective attaches straight to `ん` with no `な`. Between friends this is the
natural form; in a staffroom you would say
{f|厳|きび}しいそうです.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|友|とも}だちが{f|言|い}ってたけど、あの{f|映画|えいが}、とても{f|有名|ゆうめい}だんですって。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|友|とも}だちが{f|言|い}ってたけど、あの{f|映画|えいが}、とても{f|有名|ゆうめい}<b>なんですって</b>。

<code>{f|有名|ゆうめい}</code> is a な-adjective, so it links with `な`, not `だ`. The
named source in front is perfectly normal - `んですって` does not object to you
saying who told you.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|来週|らいしゅう}のテスト、{f|中止|ちゅうし}だ<b>って</b>。
(b) {f|来週|らいしゅう}のテスト、{f|中止|ちゅうし}な<b>んだって</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a flat relay of a message: "word is, the test is cancelled." It reports the
words and nothing more, and it is what you would say if you were simply passing on
an announcement you half heard.

(b) frames the cancellation as the news of the moment - the thing that explains why
everyone is talking, or why you no longer need to revise. It is the form you use
when you are telling someone something you expect them to react to. The difference
is small, and in most conversations either would pass without comment.

</details>
