# すると

**Meaning** · thereupon; and then - what was observed to happen immediately after

`すると` is the narrator's connector. Somebody does something, and the next
sentence reports what happened as a result - not what they decided to do next,
but what the world did back. There is nearly always a small element of discovery
in it: the speaker did not know this was coming.

It has a second life in conversation, where it introduces an inference drawn from
what the other person has just said.

## Formation

`すると` attaches to nothing; it opens a sentence. What matters is the shape of
the sentences on either side of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing; it opens a sentence | ～た。すると、～た | {f|名前|なまえ}を{f|呼|よ}んだ。すると、{f|振|ふ}り{f|向|む}いた。 |
| Sentence before | a completed action or event, usually past | ドアを{f|開|あ}けた。 |
| Sentence after | something observed, outside the speaker's control | すると、{f|冷|つめ}たい{f|風|かぜ}が{f|入|はい}ってきた。 |
| Sentence after | ❌ the speaker's own decision, order or request | ❌すると、{f|帰|かえ}ることにした |
| Dialogue, drawing an inference | すると、～ということですか | すると、{f|全員|ぜんいん}{f|反対|はんたい}だったのですね。 |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the word is そう + する + と, "when things are done that way". That
history explains both uses: the narrative one ("when that was done, this
happened") and the inferential one ("if that is so, then this follows").

</div>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, but strongly narrative. `すると` is the connector of anecdotes,
folk tales, accident reports and any account of "here is what I did, and here is
what happened". Because of that flavour of discovery, it does not suit a result
that anybody could have predicted: if the second event is the obvious outcome of
the first, そして or それで is the honest choice.

In casual speech the same narrative move is usually made with そしたら or
そうしたら. 「{f|電話|でんわ}したの。そしたら、もう{f|出|で}かけたって」 is what a friend
says; `すると` in that sentence would sound slightly written, as though the story
were being read aloud.

The inferential use - 「すると、つまり{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わないということですね」 -
is entirely at home in discussion and meetings. There it is close to
だとすると or ということは, and it signals that you are following the other person's
reasoning rather than adding information of your own.

## Contrast

### すると vs そうすると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すると` reports what **did** happen, in the past. `そうすると`
works out what **would** happen, or what must be true.

</div>

<pre>
レバーを{f|引|ひ}いた。<b>すると</b>、{f|機械|きかい}が{f|止|と}まった。
I pulled the lever, and the machine stopped.
</pre>

<pre>
レバーを{f|引|ひ}いてください。<b>そうすると</b>、{f|機械|きかい}が{f|止|と}まります。
Pull the lever and the machine will stop.
</pre>

The tense tells you which you are looking at. `すると` narrates a completed
sequence; `そうすると` predicts, instructs or deduces. In the inferential use the
two overlap, and 「すると、{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いませんね」 and
「そうすると、{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いませんね」 are both natural.

### すると vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical relation, different packaging. `～と` puts both
halves in **one sentence**; `すると` gives the second half **its own sentence**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}ける<b>と</b>、{f|遠|とお}くに{f|海|うみ}が{f|見|み}えた。
When I opened the window, the sea was visible in the distance.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けた。<b>すると</b>、{f|遠|とお}くに{f|海|うみ}が{f|見|み}えた。
I opened the window. And there in the distance was the sea.
</pre>

`すると` is literally that same と with そうする in front of it, which is why the
two behave alike - both refuse a second half in which the speaker decides or
orders something. The difference is pacing. The one-sentence version moves
briskly; the two-sentence version puts a beat of suspense before the discovery,
which is why storytellers reach for it.

### すると vs そして

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そして` simply **adds the next event**. `すると` presents the
next event as **what came of** the first, and as news to the speaker.

</div>

<pre>
{f|手紙|てがみ}を{f|書|か}いた。<b>そして</b>、ポストに{f|入|い}れた。
I wrote the letter, and then posted it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|手紙|てがみ}を{f|出|だ}した。<b>すると</b>、{f|三日|みっか}{f|後|ご}に{f|返事|へんじ}が{f|届|とど}いた。
I sent the letter, and three days later a reply arrived.
</pre>

Posting the letter is the speaker's own next move, so it takes そして. The reply
arriving is not, and it is the kind of thing the speaker could not have known in
advance - `すると` territory. Where both are grammatical, `すると` adds the sense
that the second event followed *from* the first.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|宿題|しゅくだい}が{f|終|お}わった。すると、{f|寝|ね}た。 → ✅ {f|宿題|しゅくだい}が{f|終|お}わった。<b>そして</b>、{f|寝|ね}た。
  Going to bed is the speaker's own next action, not something they discovered.
  `すると` needs a second half that happened to them.

- ❌ {f|寒|さむ}いですね。すると、{f|窓|まど}を{f|閉|し}めてください。 → ✅ {f|寒|さむ}いですね。<b>ですから</b>、{f|窓|まど}を{f|閉|し}めてください。
  Requests and orders cannot follow it, for the same reason: they are decisions,
  not observations.

- ❌ ボタンを{f|押|お}すすると、{f|音|おと}が{f|鳴|な}ります。 → ✅ ボタンを{f|押|お}す<b>と</b>、{f|音|おと}が{f|鳴|な}ります。
  Inside one sentence the connector is bare と. `すると` needs the first sentence
  to be finished before it.

- ❌ このボタンを{f|押|お}してください。すると、{f|電源|でんげん}が{f|入|はい}ります。 → ✅ このボタンを{f|押|お}してください。<b>そうすると</b>、{f|電源|でんげん}が{f|入|はい}ります。
  Instructions about what will happen are not narration. For a manual, the
  forward-looking そうすると or そうすれば is what you want.

## Practice

**1.** {f|静|しず}かに{f|台所|だいどころ}をのぞいてみた。____、{f|猫|ねこ}が{f|皿|さら}の{f|上|うえ}で{f|寝|ね}ていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>すると</b>、{f|猫|ねこ}が{f|皿|さら}の{f|上|うえ}で{f|寝|ね}ていた。

The speaker looked, and what they found was a surprise to them. A completed
action followed by an unexpected discovery is the classic shape for this word.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
この{f|欄|らん}に{f|住所|じゅうしょ}をご{f|記入|きにゅう}ください。（すると・そうすると）、{f|次|つぎ}の{f|画面|がめん}に{f|進|すす}めます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>そうすると</b>、{f|次|つぎ}の{f|画面|がめん}に{f|進|すす}めます。

These are instructions about what will happen if the reader does something, so
the sentence looks forward rather than back. `すると` would need a past narrative
in front of it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I called out to him. He turned round, and it was someone else entirely."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|声|こえ}をかけてみた。<b>すると</b>、{f|振|ふ}り{f|返|かえ}ったのはまったく{f|別|べつ}の{f|人|ひと}だった。

Calling out is the speaker's action; who turned round was not up to them, and it
was a surprise. That combination is what `すると` marks, and the past tense on
both halves is essential.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|駅|えき}に{f|着|つ}いた。すると、{f|友|とも}だちに{f|電話|でんわ}しようと{f|思|おも}う。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|駅|えき}に{f|着|つ}いた。<b>そこで</b>、{f|友|とも}だちに{f|電話|でんわ}することにした。

<code>{f|思|おも}う</code> here states the speaker's own intention, and `すると`
cannot introduce one. Reporting a step you decided to take is そこで; if you
wanted a discovery instead, the second half would have to be something like
すると、{f|友|とも}だちが{f|改札|かいさつ}で{f|待|ま}っていた。

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けると、{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|積|つ}もっていた。
(b) {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けた。<b>すると</b>、{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|積|つ}もっていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The two describe the same morning and neither is more correct than the other.
Version (a) packs the action and the discovery into one sentence and reads
smoothly, the way a description usually does. Version (b) closes the first
sentence before the discovery arrives, which gives the snow a small moment of
its own - the pacing of a story being told rather than a scene being described.

</details>
