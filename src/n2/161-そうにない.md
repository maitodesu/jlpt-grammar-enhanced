# ～そうにない

**Meaning** · shows no sign of ～ing; there's no prospect of it - the speaker has looked at the situation and can see nothing that points to the thing happening

`～そうにない` is the negative of the *looks like it will* `そうだ`, and it reports
**prospects, not probabilities**. Nothing is being calculated. The speaker is describing
what the queue, the sky, the pile of work or the other person's face actually looks like,
and what it looks like is: not happening. On the confidence ladder it sits low down and on
the negative side - weaker than <code>わけがない</code>, which rules the thing out
altogether, but far more definite than a shrugged <code>ないだろう</code>.

## Formation

A **verb** pattern. It attaches to the ます-stem, exactly where the positive `そうだ`
attaches.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + そうにない | {f|進|すす}みます → {f|進|すす}みそうにない |
| する-verb | ～し + そうにない | {f|回復|かいふく}する → {f|回復|かいふく}しそうにない |
| {f|来|く}る | {f|来|き} + そうにない | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|き}そうにない |
| Potential verb (very common) | potential ます-stem + そうにない | {f|行|い}けます → {f|行|い}けそうにない |
| Polite | ます-stem + そうにありません | {f|届|とど}きます → {f|届|とど}きそうにありません |
| Modifying a noun | … + そうにない + Noun | {f|直|なお}る → {f|直|なお}りそうにない{f|時計|とけい} |
| Emphatic variants | ます-stem + そうもない / そうにもない | {f|勝|か}てます → {f|勝|か}てそうもない |
| い-adjective (no direct form) | ～く + なさそうだ | {f|甘|あま}い → {f|甘|あま}くなさそうだ |

</pre>

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}はまだ{f|起|お}き<b>そうにない</b>。
There's no sign of my little brother getting up yet.
</pre>

{f|話|はなし}が{f|長|なが}くて、{f|昼|ひる}ごはんは{f|食|た}べられ<b>そうにない</b>。

</pre>

<pre>
こんな{f|字|じ}では、{f|誰|だれ}にも{f|読|よ}んでもらえ<b>そうにありません</b>。

</pre>

<pre>
{f|荷物|にもつ}が{f|多|おお}すぎて、{f|一度|いちど}では{f|運|はこ}び{f|切|き}れ<b>そうにない</b>。

</pre>

<pre>
{f|上司|じょうし}の{f|機嫌|きげん}が{f|悪|わる}くて、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|相談|そうだん}を{f|切|き}り{f|出|だ}せ<b>そうにありません</b>。
The boss is in a foul mood, so it doesn't look as if I can raise it with him today.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and equally at home in speech and writing. The polite
`そうにありません` is standard business Japanese for delivering an inconvenience: it puts
the blame on circumstances rather than on the speaker, which is why the potential form
plus this ending - <code>{f|伺|うかが}えそうにありません</code>,
<code>{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わせられそうにありません</code> - is the workhorse of polite bad
news.

Because it reads **signs**, it belongs to the near future and to situations already under
way. It also carries a faint note of the speaker having waited: a queue that
<code>{f|進|すす}みそうにない</code> is one you have been standing in. That is what makes
<code>{f|気配|けはい}がない</code> and
<code>{f|見込|みこ}みがない</code> feel like relatives - all three describe an absence of
signs rather than a judgement about the world.

The noun-modifying use is worth practising because it is compact and very common:
<code>{f|終|お}わりそうにない{f|仕事|しごと}</code>, "work that shows no sign of ending".
English needs a relative clause for this; Japanese just puts it in front of the noun.

<div class="note">

**Note** - `そうにない`, `そうもない` and `そうにもない` are the same pattern in three
shapes. `そうにない` is the plainest; the ones with `も` carry a "not even" colouring and
feel a shade more emphatic. In most sentences speakers use them interchangeably, so treat
the difference as a tendency rather than a rule.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～そうにない vs ～そうもない・～そうにもない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - all three mean the same thing. `も` adds emphasis, not information.

</div>

<pre>
バスは30{f|分|ぷん}{f|待|ま}っても{f|来|き}<b>そうにない</b>。
Half an hour of waiting and there's still no sign of a bus.
</pre>

<pre>
バスは30{f|分|ぷん}{f|待|ま}っても{f|来|き}<b>そうにもない</b>。
Half an hour of waiting and not the slightest sign of a bus.
</pre>

The second is the one you say through your teeth. Since the difference is only emphasis,
the practical advice is to produce `そうにない` and recognise the other two - and to notice
that a sentence with `も` usually has something else in it doing emotional work as well
(いくら, どんなに, まったく, an exasperated ending).

### ～そうにない vs ～っこない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうにない` reports **what it looks like**. `っこない` declares the
thing **flatly impossible**, in casual speech, with feeling.

</div>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}から{f|出|で}ても、{f|開演|かいえん}には{f|着|つ}け<b>そうにない</b>。
Even leaving now, it doesn't look as though I'll get there for the curtain.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}から{f|出|で}たって、{f|着|つ}け<b>っこない</b>よ。
Leave now? No way you'd make it.
</pre>

`っこない` also takes the ます-stem, so the two are easy to swap by accident. The register
is not the same at all: `っこない` is blunt, casual and slightly rough, fine among friends
and wrong in front of a client. It is also absolute, where `そうにない` leaves the door
open a crack - and that crack is often exactly what politeness needs.

### ～そうにない vs ～ようがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうにない` says there is **no sign** of it happening.
<code>ようがない</code> says there is **no way to do it** - the means do not exist.

</div>

<pre>
{f|連絡|れんらく}が{f|取|と}れ<b>そうにない</b>。
It doesn't look as though I'll be able to reach them.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|電話番号|でんわばんごう}も{f|住所|じゅうしょ}も{f|知|し}らないので、{f|連絡|れんらく}の<b>しようがない</b>。
I have neither their number nor their address, so there is simply no way to contact them.
</pre>

The first is a forecast from circumstances: the line is busy, nobody is picking up, but
you might get through later. The second is structural - the method itself is missing, and
waiting will not help. Learners often use `そうにない` where the real problem is a missing
method, which sounds oddly optimistic.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|来|く}る<b>そうにない</b>。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|来|き}<b>そうにない</b>。
  It attaches to the ます-stem, and the ます-stem of {f|来|く}る is {f|来|き}. Writing the
  dictionary form here also risks being read as the hearsay `そうだ`.

- ❌ この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|今日中|きょうじゅう}に{f|解決|かいけつ}できる<b>そうにない</b>。 → ✅ この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|今日中|きょうじゅう}に{f|解決|かいけつ}でき<b>そうにない</b>。
  Potential verbs follow the same rule as any other verb: strip to the stem.
  <code>できる</code> → <code>でき</code>.

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ら<b>なさそうにない</b>。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}り<b>そうにない</b>。
  The negation is already inside the ending. Negating the verb as well flips the meaning
  back to "it looks like it will rain".

- ❌ このスープは{f|熱|あつ}<b>そうにない</b>。 → ✅ このスープは{f|熱|あつ}<b>くなさそうだ</b>。
  Adjectives have no direct form here. Negate the adjective first, then add そう - which
  inserts the さ.

## Practice

**1.** {f|窓|まど}の{f|外|そと}を{f|見|み}ると{f|工事|こうじ}がまだ{f|続|つづ}いている。この{f|音|おと}では{f|集中|しゅうちゅう}でき____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|音|おと}では{f|集中|しゅうちゅう}でき<b>そうにない</b>（or できそうにありません）。

The noise is visible, audible evidence, and the speaker is reading it: there is no prospect
of concentrating. The potential <code>できる</code> drops to the stem
<code>でき</code> before the ending.

</details>

**2.** Choose, thinking about who you are speaking to - a client on the phone:
{f|申|もう}し{f|訳|わけ}ありません、{f|本日中|ほんじつちゅう}のご{f|返答|へんとう}は（できっこありません・できそうにありません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|本日中|ほんじつちゅう}のご{f|返答|へんとう}は<b>できそうにありません</b>。

`っこない` is casual and blunt, and putting it in polite clothes does not rescue it - to a
client it sounds like a flat refusal thrown across a table. `そうにありません` presents the
same fact as a reading of the circumstances, which is what an apology needs.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It doesn't look as though this cold is going to clear up before the weekend."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|風邪|かぜ}は{f|週末|しゅうまつ}までには{f|治|なお}り<b>そうにない</b>。

A cold you are currently suffering is exactly the kind of thing whose signs you can read,
which is why English "doesn't look as though" maps so neatly onto this pattern.
{f|治|なお}る is an う-verb, so its stem is {f|治|なお}り.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|今年|ことし}の{f|夏|なつ}は{f|暑|あつ}そうにないですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|今年|ことし}の{f|夏|なつ}は<b>{f|暑|あつ}くなさそう</b>ですね。

{f|暑|あつ}い is an い-adjective and cannot take `そうにない` directly. Negate it first -
<code>{f|暑|あつ}くない</code> - and then add そう, which inserts さ to give
<code>{f|暑|あつ}くなさそうだ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}に{f|勝|か}て<b>そうにない</b>。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}に{f|勝|か}て<b>っこない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a reading of the evidence in front of you - the score so far, his form, your own
tired legs. It is discouraged rather than final, and it would be a perfectly ordinary
thing to say to a coach.

(b) shuts the question. It is casual, emphatic and slightly rough, closer to "there's no
chance whatsoever", and it carries an emotional charge (resignation, or contempt) that (a)
does not. Said about a teammate rather than yourself, (b) is an insult.

</details>
