# さすが

**Meaning** · as one would expect (and it lives up to it); even ～, admittedly

`さすが` measures reality against a reputation and finds that the two match. You
knew this person was good, this shop was famous, this material was expensive - and
now the evidence is in front of you. Most of the time it is praise.

## Usage

Three distinct moves live under one word. They are worth learning separately.

**1. さすが(は)＋noun** - "that's ～ for you". A verdict on a person or thing whose
reputation has just been confirmed.

- さすが{f|先生|せんせい}ですね。
- さすがはプロの{f|仕事|しごと}だ。

The `は` is optional and adds a touch of formality; both are common.

**2. さすが～だけあって / だけのことはある** - the same praise with its evidence
attached. The `だけあって` clause states the qualification, the main clause states
the result that lives up to it.

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>さすが～だけあって</code> = "being ～, as you'd expect,
…". The reason comes first and must be something **impressive in itself**:
training, experience, cost, pedigree.

</div>

- さすが{f|三年|さんねん}{f|習|なら}っただけあって、{f|発音|はつおん}がきれいだ。
- {f|高|たか}かっただけのことはある。

**3. さすがに** - a different move. Here the speaker concedes a **limit**: even
granted everything, this far and no further. It heads a clause admitting that
something was too much, or that even an exceptional person could not manage it.

- さすがに{f|疲|つか}れた。
- さすがにそれは{f|無理|むり}だ。

Related to this is <code>さすがの～も</code>, "even ～, of all people":
さすがの{f|彼|かれ}も{f|黙|だま}ってしまった.

The kanji spelling {f|流石|さすが} is ateji and appears mostly in casual writing;
kana is the safe default.

</pre>

## Nuance & register

Conversational and warm. `さすが` is what you say out loud when someone has just
done something well, and it appears constantly in speech, in reviews and in
advertising copy. In formal writing it is rarer, because it is an expression of
personal admiration.

Two cautions worth knowing.

First, it is **evaluation**. Praising a superior by ranking them -
さすが{f|部長|ぶちょう}ですね - is extremely common, but some listeners feel that
grading the boss is presumptuous, and overdone it sounds like flattery. Tendency,
not rule: judge the relationship.

Second, the admiration has to be **expected**. `さすが` says "just as I thought",
so it does not fit a surprise. If the result was genuinely unforeseen, the natural
words are <code>{f|意外|いがい}と</code> or a plain <code>すごい</code>.

`さすがに` is more neutral in colour than bare `さすが` and belongs to ordinary
speech and writing alike. Note that its "even so" sense can be faintly negative -
さすがに{f|言|い}いすぎだ, "that really is going too far" - which surprises learners
who have only met the praising `さすが`.

## Contrast

### さすが vs やはり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "as expected", but `さすが` **admires** and
`やはり` merely **confirms**. Only `やはり` can report an expected bad outcome.

</div>

<pre>
<b>さすが</b>{f|一流|いちりゅう}のホテルだ。サービスが{f|違|ちが}う。
This is a first-class hotel all right - the service is another thing entirely.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>やはり</b>{f|彼|かれ}は{f|来|こ}なかった。
He didn't come, just as I thought.
</pre>

Reverse them and the second sentence becomes strange: there is nothing admirable
about someone failing to turn up, so `さすが` has nothing to praise. Conversely
`やはり` in the first sentence flattens it - the hotel is merely as predicted, and
the compliment evaporates.

The casual spoken form of `やはり` is `やっぱり`, and it is far more frequent in
conversation than the full form.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ <b>さすが</b>{f|彼|かれ}はまた{f|遅刻|ちこく}した。 → ✅ <b>やはり</b>{f|彼|かれ}はまた{f|遅刻|ちこく}した。
  `さすが` only greets a good outcome. Using it for a predictable failure reads as
  sarcasm, whether or not you meant it.

- ❌ さすが{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|練習|れんしゅう}した<b>から</b>、うまくなった。 → ✅ さすが{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|練習|れんしゅう}した<b>だけあって</b>、うまくなった。
  Plain `から` states a cause; the frame that carries the "lives up to it" meaning
  is <code>だけあって</code> or <code>だけのことはある</code>.

- ❌ さすが{f|疲|つか}れた。 → ✅ <b>さすがに</b>{f|疲|つか}れた。
  The "even so, admittedly" sense requires the `に`. Without it the sentence looks
  like praise and lands nowhere.

## Practice

**1.** {f|三十年|さんじゅうねん}{f|寿司|すし}を{f|握|にぎ}ってきた____、{f|包丁|ほうちょう}の{f|使|つか}い{f|方|かた}が{f|美|うつく}しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

さすが{f|三十年|さんじゅうねん}{f|寿司|すし}を{f|握|にぎ}ってきた<b>だけあって</b>、{f|包丁|ほうちょう}の{f|使|つか}い{f|方|かた}が{f|美|うつく}しい。

The first clause gives an impressive qualification and the second gives the result
that matches it. <code>だけのことはある</code> would also work if it were moved to
the end: {f|美|うつく}しい。さすが{f|三十年|さんじゅうねん}やってきただけのことはある。

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|山|やま}の{f|上|うえ}は{f|八月|はちがつ}でも（さすが / さすがに）{f|涼|すず}しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|山|やま}の{f|上|うえ}は{f|八月|はちがつ}でも<b>さすがに</b>{f|涼|すず}しい。

<code>でも</code> sets up a concession - even in August - and the clause that
answers it takes `さすがに`. Bare `さすが` would have to be followed by a noun it is
praising, and there is none here.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) さすが{f|林|はやし}さんですね。
(b) やっぱり{f|林|はやし}さんですね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a compliment: Hayashi has just done something well, and the speaker is
saying that this is exactly the standard Hayashi is known for.

(b) only confirms a prediction. Depending on what Hayashi just did, it can be
admiring, resigned or quietly critical - the same words serve for "of course you
came through" and "of course you forgot again".

</details>
