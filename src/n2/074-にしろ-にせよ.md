# ～にしろ / ～にせよ

**Meaning** · even if; whatever it may be; granted that - the concession is made formally, and made in order to object

`にしろ` and `にせよ` are the same word twice over: both are the imperative of
<code>～にする</code>, `しろ` being the everyday imperative and `せよ` the written one.
The pattern takes a reason, an excuse or one possibility out of several, grants it,
and then says the important thing anyway. *Whatever your reasons were, that was not
acceptable.*

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
these two sit at *minds a good deal* and *distinctly formal*. That combination is
their whole point: they let you push back hard while sounding measured.

## Formation

Both attach to a plain form. Nouns and な-adjective stems attach **bare** - no `だ`,
no `な` - or with `である` in more formal writing.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + にしろ／にせよ | {f|断|ことわ}る → {f|断|ことわ}るにせよ |
| Verb (past) | plain past + にしろ／にせよ | {f|落|お}ちた → {f|落|お}ちたにしろ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + にしろ／にせよ | {f|参加|さんか}しない → {f|参加|さんか}しないにしろ |
| い-adjective | plain form + にしろ／にせよ | {f|忙|いそが}しい → {f|忙|いそが}しいにせよ |
| な-adjective | stem（+ である）+ にしろ／にせよ | {f|不便|ふべん} → {f|不便|ふべん}であるにせよ |
| Noun | Noun（+ である）+ にしろ／にせよ | {f|冗談|じょうだん} → {f|冗談|じょうだん}にせよ |
| With a question word | どんな／{f|誰|だれ}が … + にしろ | - → {f|誰|だれ}がやるにしろ |
| Fixed opener | いずれにしろ／いずれにせよ | - → いずれにせよ、{f|時間|じかん}がない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `だ` in front. ✅<code>{f|冗談|じょうだん}にせよ</code>,
❌<code>{f|冗談|じょうだん}だにせよ</code>. If you want the copula visible, the written
option is `である`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the two are interchangeable in meaning. `にせよ` is the more literary of the
pair and dominates in editorials, contracts and formal speech; `にしろ` is a step down
and turns up in careful conversation. Neither is casual, and `にしても` is the ordinary
spoken member of the family.

</div>

## Nuance & register

Neither form is casual. `にせよ` in particular reads as written Japanese, and it is the
one you meet in newspaper columns, official statements and the formal parts of
business correspondence. `にしろ` is a little softer and can be spoken, but it still
belongs to careful speech - a meeting, a complaint made politely, a teacher addressing
a class. Among friends, `にしても` or plain `ても` is what people actually say.

The rhetorical shape is worth naming, because it is the reason this pattern exists.
You concede first so that your objection cannot be brushed aside as ignorance of the
other side. <code>{f|忙|いそが}しかったにせよ</code> tells your listener that yes, you
know they were busy - and precisely because you have granted it, the criticism that
follows is harder to answer. Used well, this is one of the most effective moves in
polite Japanese argument. Used carelessly, it is unmistakably a telling-off.

Question words are its natural companions: <code>どんな</code>,
<code>{f|誰|だれ}が</code>, <code>いつ</code>, <code>どちらを</code>. With one of these
in front, the meaning shifts from "even if" to "whatever / whoever", and the sentence
becomes a statement about all the cases at once.

<code>いずれにせよ</code> and <code>いずれにしろ</code> are frozen and extremely
common - "either way", "in any case". They close off a list of options rather than
conceding a single one, and they are usable in speech where the bare pattern would
sound stiff.

## Contrast

### にしろ・にせよ vs にしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one family, three registers. `にしても` is neutral speech,
`にしろ` careful speech, `にせよ` writing. The formality rises and so does the force.

</div>

<pre>
{f|疲|つか}れていた<b>にしても</b>、あの{f|言|い}い{f|方|かた}はないよ。
Tired or not, that's no way to speak to someone.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|疲|つか}れていた<b>にせよ</b>、あのような{f|発言|はつげん}は{f|適切|てきせつ}ではなかった。
Even allowing for tiredness, remarks of that kind were not appropriate.
</pre>

Same objection, two different rooms. The first is a friend across a table; the second
belongs in minutes of a meeting. Choosing `にせよ` for the first sentence would make a
small grumble sound like a formal reprimand - which, in the wrong company, is exactly
the mistake to avoid.

### にしろ・にせよ vs ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` concedes **one condition**. `にしろ` concedes **one case out
of several**, and quietly implies the others.

</div>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っ<b>ても</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|行|おこな}われます。
The match will be held even if it rains.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>にせよ</b>、{f|試合|しあい}の{f|準備|じゅんび}は{f|進|すす}めておきます。
Rain or no rain, we'll press on with the preparations.
</pre>

The second sentence has the unspoken alternative built in: it rains, or it does not,
and either way the preparations continue. That is why `にしろ` and `にせよ` pair so
easily into the A-or-B construction, and why `ても` does not.

### いずれにせよ vs それにしても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いずれにせよ` **closes** the alternatives; `それにしても` **reopens**
the subject to comment on it.

</div>

<pre>
<b>いずれにせよ</b>、{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう}に{f|決|き}めなければならない。
Either way, it has to be decided today.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>それにしても</b>、こんなに{f|時間|じかん}がかかるとは{f|思|おも}わなかった。
All the same, I never expected it to take this long.
</pre>

The first sweeps the options aside and gets on with it. The second accepts everything
that has been said and then adds a remark of surprise or complaint on top. Learners
mix them up because both translate as "even so"; the test is whether options are being
dismissed or a comment is being added.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>だにせよ</b> → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>にせよ</b>（or {f|学生|がくせい}であるにせよ）
  Nouns attach bare. The `だ` has no place in front of `にしろ` or `にせよ`.

- ❌ {f|新人|しんじん}<b>にせよ</b>、よくやっている。（intending "for a newcomer, he's doing well"） → ✅ {f|新人|しんじん}<b>にしては</b>、よくやっている。
  `にしては` measures against a standard and is usually complimentary. `にせよ` concedes
  and then objects, so the two lead in opposite directions.

- ❌（in a text message to a friend）{f|眠|ねむ}かった<b>にせよ</b>、{f|寝|ね}ちゃだめでしょ。 → ✅ {f|眠|ねむ}かった<b>にしても</b>、{f|寝|ね}ちゃだめでしょ。
  The rest of the sentence is casual, and `にせよ` is not. Match the register at both
  ends.

- ❌ {f|不便|ふべん}<b>なにしろ</b> → ✅ {f|不便|ふべん}<b>にしろ</b>（or {f|不便|ふべん}であるにしろ）
  There is no `な` here either. Unlike <code>くせに</code> or <code>ものの</code>, this
  pattern does not treat what precedes it as a noun modifier.

## Practice

**1.** どちらを{f|選|えら}ぶ____、{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう}に{f|返事|へんじ}をしてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

どちらを{f|選|えら}ぶ<b>にしろ</b>（or <b>にせよ</b>）、{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう}に{f|返事|へんじ}をしてください。

A question word in front turns the pattern into "whichever": both options are
conceded, and the requirement holds for both. A plain verb attaches with no change.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|大学生|だいがくせい}（にせよ・にしては）、そのくらいの{f|常識|じょうしき}は{f|持|も}っているべきだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|大学生|だいがくせい}<b>にせよ</b>、そのくらいの{f|常識|じょうしき}は{f|持|も}っているべきだ。

The sentence concedes a status and then makes a demand of it. `にしては` would say
"for a university student, he has surprisingly much common sense", which is a
judgement against a standard, not a concession.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, formally: "Even if the decision was made in a hurry, the residents should have been consulted."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|急|いそ}いで{f|決|き}めた<b>にせよ</b>、{f|住民|じゅうみん}の{f|意見|いけん}を{f|聞|き}くべきだった。

The concession comes first so that the criticism cannot be answered with "we had no
time". `にせよ` keeps the whole sentence at the register of a written complaint.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|冗談|じょうだん}だにしろ、{f|彼女|かのじょ}が{f|傷|きず}つくようなことは{f|言|い}うべきではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|冗談|じょうだん}<b>にしろ</b>、{f|彼女|かのじょ}が{f|傷|きず}つくようなことは{f|言|い}うべきではない。

A noun attaches to `にしろ` directly. If you want a copula there for a formal written
effect, it must be `である`: <code>{f|冗談|じょうだん}であるにしろ</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|理由|りゆう}があった<b>にせよ</b>、{f|無断|むだん}{f|欠勤|けっきん}は{f|問題|もんだい}だ。
(b) {f|理由|りゆう}があった<b>としても</b>、{f|無断|むだん}{f|欠勤|けっきん}は{f|問題|もんだい}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) grants that there *were* reasons - they are treated as real, one case among the
possible ones - and then rules that they do not settle the matter. It reads as a
formal judgement, the sort of line that appears in a written warning.

(b) treats the reasons as a supposition that has not been established: *even assuming
there were reasons*. That is cooler and slightly more sceptical. If you want to
acknowledge the person's side before disagreeing, (a) is the more generous choice.

</details>
