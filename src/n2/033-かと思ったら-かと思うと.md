# ～かと思ったら / ～かと思うと

**Meaning** · just when it looked as though X, Y - and Y is the opposite of what X promised

The literal shape is "no sooner had I thought X". The speaker is watching, forms a
quick impression, and is immediately proved wrong. The gap between the two events
is tiny, but the real content of the pattern is the **reversal**: what happens next
undercuts what the first half led you to expect.

## Formation

The clause in front normally ends in the **た-form**. The two endings
<code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code> and <code>かと{f|思|おも}ったら</code> are
interchangeable in this meaning; <code>かと{f|思|おも}えば</code> is a third variant.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + かと{f|思|おも}うと | {f|止|や}む → {f|止|や}んだかと{f|思|おも}うと |
| Verb (た-form) | た-form + かと{f|思|おも}ったら | {f|着|つ}く → {f|着|つ}いたかと{f|思|おも}ったら |
| Verb (ている) | ているかと{f|思|おも}えば | {f|泣|な}いている → {f|泣|な}いているかと{f|思|おも}えば |
| Noun / question word | Noun + かと{f|思|おも}ったら | {f|何|なに} → {f|何|なに}かと{f|思|おも}ったら |
| ❌ Dictionary form | ❌{f|止|や}むかと{f|思|おも}うと | ✅{f|止|や}んだかと{f|思|おも}うと |
| ❌ Request or volitional | ❌…かと{f|思|おも}ったら、{f|休|やす}みましょう | ✅…たら、{f|休|やす}みましょう |
| ❌ The speaker's own deliberate act | ❌{f|私|わたし}は{f|座|すわ}ったかと{f|思|おも}うと、{f|食|た}べ{f|始|はじ}めた | ✅{f|座|すわ}るとすぐ{f|食|た}べ{f|始|はじ}めた |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the first verb is **past**, the main clause is a **past report**
or a **habitual present**, and the speaker is an **observer**. You cannot be
surprised by your own decisions, which is why your own volitional acts do not fit
here.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - with a noun or question word in front,
<code>かと{f|思|おも}ったら</code> keeps its literal sense of a mistaken guess:
<code>{f|誰|だれ}かと{f|思|おも}ったら{f|田中|たなか}さんだった</code>, "I wondered who
it was - turned out to be Tanaka." Same words, and the timing sense grows straight
out of it.

</div>

## Nuance & register

Both endings are ordinary in speech and in writing.
<code>かと{f|思|おも}ったら</code> is the more conversational of the two;
<code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code> leans a little more written and turns up in
narration and essays. <code>かと{f|思|おも}えば</code> is the one you want when you
are listing two habits that contradict each other, and it commonly pairs with
<code>{f|今度|こんど}は</code> or <code>{f|次|つぎ}の{f|瞬間|しゅんかん}には</code>.

The feeling is exasperation or bemusement more often than delight. Rain that stops
and restarts, a child who calms down and erupts again, a colleague who settles in
and resigns - the pattern is drawn to things that will not stay put. Used of a
person it is close to a complaint, and it is worth knowing that before you aim it
at your boss.

There is one shape that is not a complaint at all. With a question word in front -
<code>{f|何|なに}かと{f|思|おも}ったら</code>,
<code>{f|誰|だれ}かと{f|思|おも}ったら</code>,
<code>どこかと{f|思|おも}ったら</code> - it simply reports that a guess turned out
wrong, often with relief. That use is completely neutral and extremely common in
conversation.

Since the sentence reports what the speaker saw, it does not take `ください`,
`ましょう`, `つもり` or a prediction about tomorrow. A habitual present is fine
({f|泣|な}いたかと{f|思|おも}うと{f|笑|わら}う), because a habit is still something
observed.

## Contrast

### ～かと{f|思|おも}うと vs ～たとたん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たとたん</code> is surprise at **how fast**.
<code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code> is surprise at **what**: the second event contradicts
the first.

</div>

<code>たとたん</code> is the N3 pattern this one grew up next to, and the two share
the た-form, the past main clause and the ban on requests. The divergence is the
relationship between the halves.

<pre>
{f|席|せき}に{f|座|すわ}っ<b>たとたん</b>、{f|名前|なまえ}を{f|呼|よ}ばれた。
The instant I sat down, my name was called.
</pre>

Sitting and being called have nothing to do with each other; only the timing is
remarkable.

<pre>
{f|席|せき}に{f|座|すわ}っ<b>たかと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、{f|彼|かれ}はまた{f|立|た}ち{f|上|あ}がった。
He had barely sat down before he was on his feet again.
</pre>

Here the two halves are in tension - he sat, so you expected him to stay sitting.
Take that tension away and <code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code> loses its point.
<code>たとたん</code> also allows the speaker to be the one who acted, as in the
first sentence; <code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code> generally does not.

### ～かと{f|思|おも}うと vs ～か～ないかのうちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>か～ないかのうちに</code> measures the **interval** and
finds it too small to measure. <code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code> measures the
speaker's **expectation** and finds it wrong.

</div>

<pre>
ドアが<b>{f|開|あ}くか{f|開|あ}かないかのうちに</b>、{f|犬|いぬ}が{f|飛|と}び{f|出|だ}してきた。
The dog was out before the door had properly opened.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|犬|いぬ}は{f|外|そと}に{f|出|で}た<b>かと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、すぐに{f|戻|もど}ってきた。
No sooner was the dog outside than it came straight back in.
</pre>

The first sentence would be strange if the gap were a whole second; the second one
survives a longer gap perfectly well, because what makes it worth saying is the
about-turn. That is also why only <code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code> can describe a
repeating habit.

### ～かと{f|思|おも}うと vs ～と

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `と` reports a **regular** sequence with no comment.
<code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code> adds that the sequence is unreasonably quick or
contrary to expectation.

</div>

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}は{f|家|いえ}に{f|帰|かえ}る<b>と</b>、すぐテレビをつける。
My father turns the television on as soon as he gets home.
</pre>

Flat description of a routine; nobody is being judged.

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}は{f|家|いえ}に{f|帰|かえ}った<b>かと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、もうソファで{f|寝|ね}ている。
My father is barely through the door before he's asleep on the sofa.
</pre>

Same routine, now with an eyebrow raised. Note the form difference too: `と` takes
the dictionary form, this pattern takes `た`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|止|や}む<b>かと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、また{f|降|ふ}り{f|出|だ}した。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|止|や}んだ<b>かと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、また{f|降|ふ}り{f|出|だ}した。
  The impression has already formed, so the verb in front is `た`.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|家|いえ}に{f|着|つ}いた<b>かと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、シャワーを{f|浴|あ}びた。 → ✅ {f|家|いえ}に{f|着|つ}くとすぐシャワーを{f|浴|あ}びた。
  You cannot be startled by your own plan. The pattern needs an observer and
  something observed.

- ❌ {f|晴|は}れた<b>かと{f|思|おも}ったら</b>、{f|出|で}かけましょう。 → ✅ {f|晴|は}れ<b>たら</b>、{f|出|で}かけましょう。
  Invitations, requests and intentions are all shut out; this pattern only reports.

- ❌ {f|止|や}んだ<b>かと{f|思|おも}った</b>、また{f|降|ふ}り{f|出|だ}した。 → ✅ {f|止|や}んだ<b>かと{f|思|おも}ったら</b>、また{f|降|ふ}り{f|出|だ}した。
  Without `ら` (or `と`) the first half is a finished sentence and the two clauses
  no longer join.

## Practice

**1.** {f|部屋|へや}が{f|静|しず}かになった____{f|思|おも}うと、{f|今度|こんど}は{f|上|うえ}の{f|階|かい}から{f|音|おと}がし{f|始|はじ}めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|静|しず}かになった<b>かと</b>{f|思|おも}うと

The full connector is <code>かと{f|思|おも}うと</code>, and the `か` is essential - it
is what turns the clause into a fleeting impression rather than a fact.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|会場|かいじょう}に{f|着|つ}いた（かと{f|思|おも}ったら・{f|次第|しだい}）、すぐに{f|帰|かえ}ってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|会場|かいじょう}に{f|着|つ}いた<b>かと{f|思|おも}ったら</b>、すぐに{f|帰|かえ}ってしまった。

The sentence reports a past event with a note of disbelief.
<code>{f|次第|しだい}</code> only ever points forward into an intended action, and it
would need the ます-stem <code>{f|着|つ}き</code> besides.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The cat had barely settled on my lap before it jumped down again."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|猫|ねこ}は{f|膝|ひざ}の{f|上|うえ}に{f|落|お}ち{f|着|つ}いた<b>かと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、また{f|飛|と}び{f|下|お}りた。

"Barely ... before" plus an about-turn by something the speaker is watching is
exactly this pattern's territory, and the first verb takes `た`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|息子|むすこ}は{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を{f|始|はじ}めるかと{f|思|おも}うと、ゲームを{f|始|はじ}めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|息子|むすこ}は{f|宿題|しゅくだい}を<b>{f|始|はじ}めたかと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、ゲームを{f|始|はじ}めた。

The verb in front must be past. The speaker saw him start the homework and formed
an impression, and the game console is what proved that impression wrong.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|部屋|へや}に{f|入|はい}っ<b>たとたん</b>、{f|電話|でんわ}をかけ{f|始|はじ}めた。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|部屋|へや}に{f|入|はい}った<b>かと{f|思|おも}うと</b>、{f|電話|でんわ}をかけ{f|始|はじ}めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the speed and nothing more: the phone call followed the entrance with
no gap, and the speaker found that abrupt.

(b) adds a judgement. Coming into a room sets up an expectation - a greeting, a
pause, sitting down - and the call cuts across it. (b) sounds faintly critical in a
way (a) does not.

</details>
