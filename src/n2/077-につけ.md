# ～につけ

**Meaning** · every time; whenever - and, doubled, "in good times and bad" - a stimulus that always brings the same feeling with it

`につけ` has two shapes and one idea. Attached to a verb of seeing, hearing or
thinking, it says that whenever the speaker meets a certain thing, a feeling rises
unbidden. Doubled around a pair of opposites - good and bad, rain and wind - it says
the same feeling comes in either circumstance.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`につけ` is *not minding at all* and *literary*. Nothing is being conceded against
anybody. What it shares with its neighbours is the shape: two cases named, one
constant result.

## Formation

The verb goes in the **dictionary form**, and it comes from a short list - seeing,
hearing, reading, remembering, thinking. `て` may be added with no change of meaning.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb of perception | dictionary form + につけ（て） | {f|見|み}る → {f|見|み}るにつけ |
| Verb of hearing | dictionary form + につけ（て） | {f|聞|き}く → {f|聞|き}くにつけ |
| Verb of thought | dictionary form + につけ（て） | {f|考|かんが}える → {f|考|かんが}えるにつけ |
| Paired い-adjectives | Adj + につけ + Adj + につけ | いい・{f|悪|わる}い → いいにつけ{f|悪|わる}いにつけ |
| Paired nouns | N + につけ + N + につけ | {f|雨|あめ}・{f|風|かぜ} → {f|雨|あめ}につけ{f|風|かぜ}につけ |
| Emphatic | … + につけても | {f|思|おも}う → {f|思|おも}うにつけても |
| Fixed adverb | {f|何|なに}かにつけ（て） | - → {f|何|なに}かにつけ{f|口|くち}を{f|出|だ}す |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the main clause must be something that **happens to** the speaker,
not something they decide to do. Feelings, memories and involuntary thoughts belong
there. ❌<code>{f|桜|さくら}を{f|見|み}るにつけ{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|撮|と}る</code> - 
taking a photograph is a choice, so this needs `たびに`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is a pattern to recognise fluently and produce sparingly. Outside the
handful of perception verbs and the standard antonym pairs, it barely occurs; modern
speech uses `たびに` for "every time" and simply repeats the two cases for "either
way". Its liveliest survivor is <code>{f|何|なに}かにつけ</code>.

</div>

## Nuance & register

`につけ` is written and slightly old-fashioned. It suits essays, memoirs, letters,
novels and speeches, and it carries a reflective, faintly nostalgic colour that
`たびに` does not have. Said in ordinary conversation it sounds like a line from a
book - which is sometimes exactly the effect a speaker wants at a memorial or in a
farewell address.

The main clause is nearly always an emotion or an involuntary thought, and it very
often ends in a form that underlines the lack of choice:
<code>～てならない</code>, <code>～ずにはいられない</code>,
<code>～ものだ</code>, <code>{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}す</code>,
<code>{f|感|かん}じる</code>. If your main clause is a plan or an instruction, this is
the wrong pattern.

The paired form draws on a small stock of conventional opposites - いい／{f|悪|わる}い,
うれしい／{f|悲|かな}しい, {f|雨|あめ}／{f|風|かぜ}, {f|見|み}る／{f|聞|き}く. Inventing new pairs is possible but tends to
sound laboured; the construction reads best when the pair is one a reader already
knows.

<code>{f|何|なに}かにつけ</code> is the one part of this entry that is genuinely common
in speech. It means "at every turn", "on any excuse", and it usually introduces
something mildly unwelcome - interference, complaints, comparisons. Its close relative
<code>{f|何|なに}かと</code> is neutral by comparison.

## Contrast

### につけ vs たびに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たびに` is the neutral "every time" and takes **any** verb and
**any** main clause. `につけ` needs a **perception verb** and a **feeling** in reply.

</div>

<pre>
{f|京都|きょうと}へ{f|行|い}く<b>たびに</b>、この{f|店|みせ}に{f|寄|よ}っている。
I stop in at this shop every time I go to Kyoto.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|京都|きょうと}の{f|町|まち}を{f|歩|ある}く<b>につけ</b>、{f|時間|じかん}の{f|流|なが}れの{f|遅|おそ}さを{f|感|かん}じる。
Walking through Kyoto, I am struck each time by how slowly time moves there.
</pre>

Stopping at a shop is a decision, so it can only take `たびに`. Being struck by
something is not, and that is where `につけ` is at home. Note too that `たびに` accepts
nouns - <code>{f|試験|しけん}のたびに</code> - while `につけ` in its single form does
not.

### につけ…につけ vs にしろ…にしろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the `につけ` pair is about **circumstances that produce a feeling**.
The `にしろ` pair is about **options in a decision**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|勝|か}つ<b>につけ</b>{f|負|ま}ける<b>につけ</b>、{f|監督|かんとく}の{f|言葉|ことば}を{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}す。
Win or lose, I find myself remembering what the coach said.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|勝|か}つ<b>にしろ</b>{f|負|ま}ける<b>にしろ</b>、{f|最後|さいご}まで{f|全力|ぜんりょく}で{f|戦|たたか}う。
Whether we win or lose, we fight flat out to the end.
</pre>

The first sentence reports what happens inside the speaker in both cases; the second
states a policy that both cases fall under. Where a rule, a requirement or a decision
is involved, `につけ` is the wrong frame.

### {f|何|なに}かにつけ vs {f|何|なに}かと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|何|なに}かにつけ</code> means "at every opportunity" and
usually introduces something **unwelcome**. <code>{f|何|なに}かと</code> means "in
various ways" and is **neutral**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|課長|かちょう}は{f|何|なに}か<b>につけ</b>{f|昔|むかし}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|持|も}ち{f|出|だ}す。
The section chief drags up the old days at every opportunity.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}しの{f|前|まえ}は{f|何|なに}かと{f|忙|いそが}しい。
There's all sorts to do before a move.
</pre>

The first is a complaint; the second is not. Learners who have only met one of the two
tend to use it for both, and putting <code>{f|何|なに}かにつけ</code> into the second
sentence would turn a neutral remark into a grumble about moving house.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|桜|さくら}を{f|見|み}る<b>につけ</b>、{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|撮|と}る。 → ✅ {f|桜|さくら}を{f|見|み}る<b>たびに</b>、{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|撮|と}る。
  The main clause of a `につけ` sentence is a feeling that arises, not an action the
  speaker chooses.

- ❌ {f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|見|み}た<b>につけ</b> → ✅ {f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|見|み}る<b>につけ</b>
  The dictionary form only. `につけ` describes a recurring situation, so a past tense
  in front of it is not possible.

- ❌ ごはんを{f|食|た}べる<b>につけ</b>、{f|国|くに}のことを{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}す。 → ✅ ふるさとの{f|味|あじ}を{f|口|くち}にする<b>につけ</b>、{f|国|くに}のことを{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}す。
  The verb has to be one of perception or reflection. Eating on its own is not; tasting
  something and being reminded by it is.

- ❌ いい<b>につけ</b>{f|悪|わる}く<b>につけ</b> → ✅ いい<b>につけ</b>{f|悪|わる}い<b>につけ</b>
  Both halves keep the plain form. The `く` form belongs to `ても` and `とも`, not here.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|絵|え}を{f|見|み}る____、{f|亡|な}くなった{f|祖父|そふ}を{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}す。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|絵|え}を{f|見|み}る<b>につけ</b>、{f|亡|な}くなった{f|祖父|そふ}を{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}す。

A verb of seeing in the dictionary form, and a main clause that is an involuntary
memory. That combination is the core use of the pattern.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|駅|えき}を{f|通|とお}る（につけ・たびに）、{f|新|あたら}しいパン{f|屋|や}に{f|寄|よ}ることにしている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|駅|えき}を{f|通|とお}る<b>たびに</b>、{f|新|あたら}しいパン{f|屋|や}に{f|寄|よ}ることにしている。

<code>{f|寄|よ}ることにしている</code> is a decision the speaker has made, and `につけ`
cannot introduce a deliberate action. `たびに` is neutral and accepts it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "In good times and bad, this town has looked after its river."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

いい<b>につけ</b>{f|悪|わる}い<b>につけ</b>、この{f|町|まち}は{f|川|かわ}を{f|守|まも}ってきた。

"In good times and bad" is the standard antonym pair
<code>いいにつけ{f|悪|わる}いにつけ</code>, kept in the plain form on both sides. The
literary frame suits a sentence about a town's history.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|息子|むすこ}の{f|成績|せいせき}を{f|見|み}るにつけ、{f|塾|じゅく}に{f|通|かよ}わせようと{f|決|き}めた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|息子|むすこ}の{f|成績|せいせき}を{f|見|み}る<b>につけ</b>、{f|不安|ふあん}になる。
（or {f|成績|せいせき}を{f|見|み}て、{f|塾|じゅく}に{f|通|かよ}わせようと{f|決|き}めた）

The original main clause is a decision, and `につけ` requires a feeling. Either change
the main clause to an emotion, or drop the pattern and use a plain て-form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|義母|ぎぼ}は{f|何|なに}か<b>につけ</b>{f|口|くち}を{f|出|だ}してくる。
(b) {f|義母|ぎぼ}は{f|何|なに}かと{f|口|くち}を{f|出|だ}してくる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says she seizes on any excuse to interfere. <code>{f|何|なに}かにつけ</code> implies
a pattern the speaker has noticed and resents, and the sentence is plainly a
complaint.

(b) is milder. <code>{f|何|なに}かと</code> means "in one way or another" and is
neutral in itself, so the disapproval in (b) comes only from
<code>{f|口|くち}を{f|出|だ}す</code> and could be softened away entirely by a different
verb - <code>{f|何|なに}かと{f|助|たす}けてくれる</code> is warm praise.

</details>
