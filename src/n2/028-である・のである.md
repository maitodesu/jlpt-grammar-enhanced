# である・のである

**Meaning** · the written-style copula, and its explanatory form - what `だ` becomes
when Japanese is written to be read rather than heard

`である` is not a politer `だ`, and it is not a stiffer `です`. It is the copula of a
text that has no speaker: newspaper editorials, academic papers, laws, reports,
encyclopaedia entries, essays. `のである` is the same move applied to the
explanatory `んです` you learned at N5. No textbook syllabus insists on this page,
and every N1 reading passage is written in it.

## Formation

`である` is historically <code>で</code> plus <code>ある</code>, which is why it
conjugates like the verb <code>ある</code> and can do things `だ` cannot.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + である | {f|事実|じじつ}だ → {f|事実|じじつ}である |
| な-adjective | stem + である | {f|明|あき}らかだ → {f|明|あき}らかである |
| い-adjective | no copula at all | {f|高|たか}い → {f|高|たか}い（❌{f|高|たか}いである）|
| Past | ～であった | {f|学生|がくせい}だった → {f|学生|がくせい}であった |
| Negative | ～ではない／ではなかった | {f|簡単|かんたん}だ → {f|簡単|かんたん}ではない |
| Conjecture | ～であろう | {f|事実|じじつ}だろう → {f|事実|じじつ}であろう |
| Joining clauses | ～であり、… | {f|教師|きょうし}だ → {f|教師|きょうし}であり、{f|作家|さっか}でもある |
| Before a noun | ～である + Noun | {f|必要|ひつよう}だ → {f|必要|ひつよう}である{f|理由|りゆう} |
| Explanatory | plain clause + のである | {f|変|か}わった → {f|変|か}わったのである |
| Explanatory after noun / な-adj | ～な + のである | {f|問題|もんだい}だ → {f|問題|もんだい}なのである |
| Explanatory, past | ～のであった | {f|知|し}らなかった → {f|知|し}らなかったのであった |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - written Japanese comes in two styles, and you pick one and stay
in it for the whole document. <code>だ・である{f|体|たい}</code> is the plain,
impersonal style of reports and essays; <code>です・ます{f|体|たい}</code> is the
polite style of letters, email and anything addressed to a reader as a person.
Mixing them inside one text is the single most visible flaw in learner writing.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - `である` cannot follow an い-adjective. The い-adjective already contains
its own predicate: <code>{f|正|ただ}しい。</code> is a complete written sentence, and
❌<code>{f|正|ただ}しいである</code> is not Japanese. The same goes for verbs.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
これは{f|重要|じゅうよう}な{f|問題|もんだい}<b>である</b>。
This is an important problem.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|調査|ちょうさ}の{f|結果|けっか}は{f|明|あき}らか<b>である</b>。
The findings of the survey are clear.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|人口|じんこう}は{f|減|へ}り{f|続|つづ}けている。これは{f|統計|とうけい}が{f|示|しめ}す{f|事実|じじつ}<b>である</b>。
The population is continuing to fall. This is a fact the statistics bear out.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|当時|とうじ}、この{f|地域|ちいき}は{f|漁業|ぎょぎょう}の{f|町|まち}<b>であった</b>。
At that time this district was a fishing town.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|三時間|さんじかん}に{f|及|およ}んだ。それだけ{f|意見|いけん}が{f|分|わ}かれていた<b>のである</b>。
The meeting ran to three hours. That is how divided opinion was.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|一度|いちど}も{f|振|ふ}り{f|返|かえ}らなかった。{f|戻|もど}る{f|気|き}はなかった<b>のである</b>。
He did not look back once. He had no intention of returning.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|技術|ぎじゅつ}が{f|進|すす}めば{f|幸|しあわ}せになるとは{f|限|かぎ}らない。{f|問題|もんだい}はその{f|使|つか}い{f|方|かた}な<b>のである</b>。
Advancing technology does not necessarily bring happiness. The question is how it is used.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|本|ほん}{f|研究|けんきゅう}の{f|目的|もくてき}は、{f|両者|りょうしゃ}の{f|関係|かんけい}を{f|明|あき}らかにすること<b>である</b>。
The aim of this study is to clarify the relationship between the two.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Start with what `である` does to a text's *voice*. `だ` sounds like a person
asserting something - it has the flavour of a spoken judgement, and in conversation
it can even sound blunt. `です` sounds like a person addressing another person.
`である` sounds like neither, because it removes the speaker altogether. What is left
is the proposition, standing on its own. That impersonality is exactly what a
newspaper leader, a court judgment, a laboratory report and an academic paper all
want: the claim is presented as belonging to the evidence rather than to the author.

It follows that `である` is **not polite**. It is not impolite either - it sits
outside the politeness system. Writing <code>{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}である</code>
in an email to a colleague is not rude in the way `だ` would be; it is *strange*, in
the way that answering a friend's text in the register of a company annual report is
strange. Speak it aloud to somebody and the effect is comic.

Two shapes you should be able to recognise. <code>であり</code> is the connecting
form and is how written Japanese strings predicates together without a full stop:
<code>{f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|研究者|けんきゅうしゃ}であり、{f|教育者|きょういくしゃ}でもある</code>.
<code>であろう</code> is the written `だろう`, and in academic prose it is the
standard way to hedge a claim:
<code>{f|影響|えいきょう}は{f|一時的|いちじてき}であろう</code>. There is also
<code>であります</code>, which is `である` plus politeness; it survives in speeches,
in the language of the Diet and the old military, and in some western dialects, and
it sounds formal and slightly dated. Do not confuse it with
<code>でございます</code>, the maximally polite copula of shop and hotel staff -
that one is alive and everywhere.

Now `のである`. Everything you know about `んです` transfers: it presents a sentence
as an explanation, a background, or the point the writer has been driving at. The
spoken chain runs `のです` → `んです` → `んだ`; the written chain runs `のである` →
`のだ`. In an essay, `のである` is the sound of the author stepping forward for one
sentence to say "and *this* is what it comes to". Used well, it is the punctuation
of an argument. Used every third sentence, it becomes insistent and even preachy -
Japanese style guides warn about this specifically, and it is the commonest fault in
non-native academic writing. Write the sentence plainly first; add the `の` only if
the sentence is genuinely explaining something.

The practical advice for a learner is simple. **Recognise `である` everywhere** - you
cannot read a Japanese newspaper, textbook or novel without it. **Produce it only in
essays, reports and academic writing**, and when you do, keep the whole document in
that style. Never speak it, and never put it in a message to a person.

## Contrast

### である vs だ・です

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `です` addresses a **reader**. `だ` is a **speaker's** assertion.
`である` has no speaker at all.

</div>

<pre>
{f|水質|すいしつ}の{f|改善|かいぜん}が{f|必要|ひつよう}<b>です</b>。
Improving water quality is necessary.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|水質|すいしつ}の{f|改善|かいぜん}が{f|必要|ひつよう}<b>である</b>。
Improvement of water quality is necessary.
</pre>

Nothing about the second is politer - if anything it is less accommodating, because
it has stopped talking to you. It is what you would write in the conclusion of a
report; the first is what you would write in the covering email that attaches it.

The choice also drags the rest of the sentence with it. A `である` text prefers
`しかし` to `でも`, <code>{f|行|おこな}う</code> to `やる`, `ない` to `ません`, and it
does not use `ね` or `よ` at all. Register is a whole outfit, not one garment.

### のである vs んです・のだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical grammar, identical job, different clothes. `のである`
is the written twin of the `んです` you have had since N5.

</div>

<pre>
{f|昨日|きのう}は{f|休|やす}みました。{f|熱|ねつ}があった<b>んです</b>。
I was off yesterday. I had a temperature.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|計画|けいかく}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}された。{f|予算|よさん}が{f|尽|つ}きた<b>のである</b>。
The plan was abandoned. The budget had run out.
</pre>

That is the honest summary: this half of the page adds no new meaning to what you
already know, only a new register. What is worth learning is the shape after a noun
or a な-adjective, where `の` needs a `な` in front of it -
<code>{f|問題|もんだい}なのである</code>, <code>{f|明|あき}らかなのである</code> -
exactly as in <code>{f|問題|もんだい}なんです</code>.

Note also the intermediate form `のだ`, which is `のである` with the copula
shortened. It is common in essays and columns and slightly less formal than
`のである`; both belong to the same written style.

### である vs のである

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `である` states. `のである` states **and claims that this
explains something**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|原因|げんいん}は{f|管理|かんり}の{f|不備|ふび}<b>である</b>。
The cause is inadequate supervision.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|事故|じこ}は{f|偶然|ぐうぜん}ではない。{f|原因|げんいん}は{f|管理|かんり}の{f|不備|ふび}な<b>のである</b>。
The accident was no accident. The cause was inadequate supervision.
</pre>

The first sentence could open a paragraph; the second could only close one, because
the `の` points back at something already on the table and says "here is what was
behind it." Ask yourself whether the sentence answers a question the previous
sentences raised. If it does not, delete the `の` - and most of the time it does not.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|結果|けっか}は{f|明|あき}らかである。{f|今後|こんご}の{f|課題|かだい}を{f|考|かんが}えたいと{f|思|おも}います。 → ✅ …{f|明|あき}らかである。{f|今後|こんご}の{f|課題|かだい}を{f|考|かんが}えたい。
  One style per document. Sliding into です・ます in the middle of a
  だ・である essay is the most visible flaw in learner writing, and graders look for
  it.

- ❌ この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|新|あたら}し<b>いである</b>。 → ✅ この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|新|あたら}し<b>い</b>。
  An い-adjective is already a predicate. `である` follows nouns and な-adjective
  stems only.

- ❌ {f|静|しず}か<b>だである</b>。 → ✅ {f|静|しず}か<b>である</b>。
  `である` replaces `だ`; it does not stack on top of it.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}の{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}である。（in an email to a colleague）→ ✅ {f|明日|あした}の{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。
  Email addresses a person, so it needs a copula that acknowledges one. `である`
  reads as though a notice had been machine-generated.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}<b>だのである</b>。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}<b>なのである</b>。
  Before `の`, a noun or な-adjective takes `な` - the same rule as
  <code>{f|留学生|りゅうがくせい}なんです</code>.

## Practice

**1.** {f|本論文|ほんろんぶん}の{f|目的|もくてき}は、{f|両国|りょうこく}の{f|制度|せいど}を{f|比較|ひかく}すること____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|比較|ひかく}すること<b>である</b>。

<code>{f|本論文|ほんろんぶん}</code> announces an academic paper, and academic papers
are written in だ・である style throughout. <code>こと</code> is a noun, so the
copula attaches directly.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|新|あたら}しい{f|制度|せいど}は{f|複雑|ふくざつ}（だである・である・ですである）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|新|あたら}しい{f|制度|せいど}は{f|複雑|ふくざつ}<b>である</b>。

<code>{f|複雑|ふくざつ}</code> is a な-adjective, and `である` attaches to the bare
stem. The other two options try to stack copulas: `である` is itself the copula and
replaces `だ` or `です` rather than following one.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in written essay style: "The problem is not the technology. It is how we choose to use it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|技術|ぎじゅつ}そのものではない。それをどう{f|使|つか}うかな<b>のである</b>。

The first sentence states, and the second explains what the first was pointing at -
which is precisely when `の` earns its place. Note the `な` before it, required
because what precedes is a nominalised phrase functioning as a noun.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|以上|いじょう}のことから、この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|有効|ゆうこう}だと{f|言|い}えるのであります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|以上|いじょう}のことから、この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|有効|ゆうこう}だと{f|言|い}える<b>のである</b>。

<code>のであります</code> is `のである` with politeness added, and it belongs to
speeches rather than to written argument - in a paper it sounds like a politician at
a podium. In a report or essay the plain <code>のである</code> is the form.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|制度|せいど}は{f|三十年|さんじゅうねん}{f|前|まえ}に{f|作|つく}られた。
(b) この{f|制度|せいど}は{f|三十年|さんじゅうねん}{f|前|まえ}に{f|作|つく}られた<b>のである</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply reports the date. It could sit anywhere in a paragraph, including at the
start.

(b) offers the date as the explanation of something the reader has just been shown -
why the rules fit badly, why the wording is odd, why nobody remembers the reasoning.
It looks backwards, and dropped into a paragraph with nothing behind it, it leaves
the reader hunting for the point it was supposed to be explaining. That is the test
for every `のである` you write.

</details>
