# ～ようでは

**Meaning** · if things are in that state, there is no hope for it

This is `ようだ` - "it appears that, it looks like" - wearing the same `では` as the
rest of this family. The speaker takes an observed state of affairs, treats it as a
symptom, and delivers a verdict: *if that is what we are seeing, this is not going to
work.* The verdict is always unfavourable.

## Formation

`よう` is a noun, so the clause in front of it takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ようでは | {f|続|つづ}く → {f|続|つづ}くようでは |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ようでは | {f|解|と}けない → {f|解|と}けないようでは |
| い-adjective | plain form + ようでは | {f|高|たか}い → {f|高|たか}いようでは |
| な-adjective | ～な + ようでは | {f|不安定|ふあんてい} → {f|不安定|ふあんてい}なようでは |
| Noun | ～の + ようでは | この{f|調子|ちょうし} → この{f|調子|ちょうし}のようでは |
| Casual contraction | ようでは → ようじゃ | {f|読|よ}めないようじゃ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with a bare noun, most speakers drop `よう` and say
<code>この{f|調子|ちょうし}では</code>. The `のようでは` row is grammatical but far less
common than the verb rows, which are where this pattern really lives.

</div>

<pre>

If you can't even solve a question this easy, passing is going to be hard.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|読|よ}めない<b>ようじゃ</b>、この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|続|つづ}かないよ。
If you can't read the kanji, you won't last in this job.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|朝|あさ}{f|自分|じぶん}で{f|起|お}きられない<b>ようでは</b>、{f|社会人|しゃかいじん}としてやっていけない。
If you can't get yourself up in the morning, you won't manage in the working world.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|部長|ぶちょう}まで{f|反対|はんたい}している<b>ようでは</b>、この{f|案|あん}は{f|通|とお}らないだろう。
If even the department head is against it, this proposal isn't going through.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|練習|れんしゅう}を{f|休|やす}みがちな<b>ようでは</b>、レギュラーにはなれませんよ。
If you're inclined to skip practice, you're not going to make the first team.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}がこれほど{f|高|たか}い<b>ようでは</b>、{f|買|か}ってくれるお{f|客|きゃく}さんはいない。
At a price this steep, there won't be a customer willing to buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|初日|しょにち}から{f|遅刻|ちこく}してくる<b>ようでは</b>、{f|先|さき}が{f|思|おも}いやられる。

</pre>

<pre>

If you still don't follow after hearing the same explanation three times, we can't leave you to it alone.

## Nuance & register

`ようでは` is a judgement, and judgements come from above. The natural speakers of this
pattern are teachers, coaches, supervisors and critics; aimed upward at a boss or a
customer it is close to insulting, because you are grading their performance out loud.
Between friends it works when the tone is teasing. About yourself it is possible and
rather rueful - <code>この{f|程度|ていど}で{f|疲|つか}れているようでは、まだまだだ</code> -
and that self-directed use is one of the safest ways for a learner to deploy it.

The consequence half is a small set of set expressions, and knowing them is half of
knowing the pattern: <code>{f|無理|むり}だ</code>, <code>できない</code>,
<code>{f|難|むずか}しい</code>, <code>{f|話|はなし}にならない</code>,
<code>{f|先|さき}が{f|思|おも}いやられる</code>, <code>おしまいだ</code>. All of them say
the same thing in different tempers.

In speech `ようでは` contracts to `ようじゃ`, which is very common and slightly rougher:
<code>そんなことも{f|知|し}らないようじゃ{f|困|こま}るな</code>. In editorial writing the
uncontracted form is standard and carries no personal sting - there the failing state
belongs to a policy or an industry rather than to the listener.

## Contrast

### ～ようでは vs ～ようなら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ようなら` is a neutral "if it turns out that" and can be followed
by a request or an instruction. `ようでは` can only be followed by a bad verdict.

</div>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}が{f|下|さ}がらない<b>ようなら</b>、もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|病院|びょういん}へ{f|行|い}ってください。
If the fever doesn't come down, please go back to the hospital.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}んでも{f|熱|ねつ}が{f|下|さ}がらない<b>ようでは</b>、ただの{f|風邪|かぜ}ではないかもしれない。
If the fever won't come down even with medicine, this may not be an ordinary cold.
</pre>

Both start from the same observed state. `ようなら` then tells you what to do about it;
`ようでは` refuses to give advice and instead announces the conclusion. Swap them and
the first sentence loses its instruction, the second gains one it cannot carry.

### ～ようでは vs ～ては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ては` blames a **repeated act**. `ようでは` reads a **state** as
evidence about someone's ability or a plan's prospects.

</div>

<pre>
{f|大事|だいじ}な{f|書類|しょるい}をなくし<b>ては</b>、{f|信用|しんよう}を{f|失|うしな}う。
Lose an important document and you forfeit people's trust.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|大事|だいじ}な{f|書類|しょるい}をなくす<b>ようでは</b>、この{f|部署|ぶしょ}は{f|任|まか}せられない。
If documents like this get lost, we can't hand this section over to you.
</pre>

The first says losing the paper causes the damage. The second treats the loss as a
diagnostic - it reveals what sort of worker you are - and pronounces on your future.
That diagnostic flavour is what `よう` adds, and it is why the sentence feels colder.

### ～ようでは vs ～のでは

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のでは` takes the situation as an established fact. `ようでは`
frames it as an appearance the speaker has inferred from what is in front of them.

</div>

<pre>
{f|毎月|まいつき}これだけ{f|赤字|あかじ}が{f|出|で}る<b>のでは</b>、{f|店|みせ}を{f|閉|し}めるしかない。
Running this much of a loss every month, there's nothing for it but to close the shop.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|毎月|まいつき}これだけ{f|赤字|あかじ}が{f|出|で}ている<b>ようでは</b>、{f|店|みせ}は{f|長|なが}くもたないだろう。
If the shop is losing this much every month, it isn't going to last long.
</pre>

The accounts are on the table in the first; in the second the speaker is reading the
signs. `のでは` also contracts, to `んじゃ` - <code>これだけ{f|高|たか}いんじゃ{f|買|か}えない</code> - 
which is one of the most frequent shapes in casual complaint, and worth recognising
alongside `ようじゃ`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>ようでは</b>{f|無理|むり}だ。 → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>のようでは</b>{f|無理|むり}だ。
  <code>よう</code> is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の` - the same link
  <code>{f|学生|がくせい}のようだ</code> uses.

- ❌ {f|不安|ふあん}<b>ようでは</b>{f|続|つづ}けられない。 → ✅ {f|不安|ふあん}<b>なようでは</b>{f|続|つづ}けられない。
  な-adjectives keep their `な` before <code>よう</code>.

- ❌ よく{f|勉強|べんきょう}する<b>ようでは</b>、きっと{f|合格|ごうかく}できる。 → ✅ よく{f|勉強|べんきょう}す<b>れば</b>、きっと{f|合格|ごうかく}できる。
  The verdict has to be unfavourable. A good outcome needs an ordinary conditional.

- ❌ {f|痛|いた}みが{f|続|つづ}く<b>ようでは</b>、{f|医者|いしゃ}に{f|相談|そうだん}してください。 → ✅ {f|痛|いた}みが{f|続|つづ}く<b>ようなら</b>、{f|医者|いしゃ}に{f|相談|そうだん}してください。
  An instruction to the listener needs `ようなら`. `ようでは` ends the sentence in
  judgement, not in advice.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|程度|ていど}のミスに{f|気|き}づかない____、{f|検査|けんさ}の{f|意味|いみ}がない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|気|き}づかない<b>ようでは</b>（casual {f|気|き}づかないようじゃ）

The missed mistake is being read as a symptom of how the inspection is being run, and
the conclusion is a flat negative verdict - both hallmarks of `ようでは`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|明日|あした}までに{f|返事|へんじ}が{f|来|こ}ない（ようでは・ようなら）、こちらから{f|電話|でんわ}してみます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|返事|へんじ}が{f|来|こ}ない<b>ようなら</b>、こちらから{f|電話|でんわ}してみます。

The second half is a plan of action, not a verdict. `ようでは` cannot be followed by
what the speaker intends to do about it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If you get this nervous at practice, you'll be no use on the day."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|練習|れんしゅう}でこんなに{f|緊張|きんちょう}している<b>ようでは</b>、{f|本番|ほんばん}では{f|使|つか}いものにならない。

The present nervousness is evidence about the performance to come, which is exactly
what `ようでは` is for, and the harsh closing phrase suits its register.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|部品|ぶひん}が{f|足|た}りないようでは、すぐに{f|注文|ちゅうもん}しておきましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|部品|ぶひん}が{f|足|た}りない<b>ようなら</b>、すぐに{f|注文|ちゅうもん}しておきましょう。

The sentence ends in a proposal, so it needs `ようなら`. Keeping `ようでは` would demand
something like <code>{f|作業|さぎょう}が{f|進|すす}まない</code> instead.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|新人|しんじん}が{f|三日|みっか}で{f|辞|や}める<b>ようでは</b>、この{f|職場|しょくば}に{f|問題|もんだい}がある。
(b) {f|新人|しんじん}が{f|三日|みっか}で{f|辞|や}め<b>ては</b>、{f|人|ひと}が{f|育|そだ}たない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats the quick resignations as a symptom and diagnoses the workplace from them:
the sentence is about what the fact reveals.

(b) treats the resignations as the cause of a practical consequence - nobody stays long
enough to be trained. It complains about the effect rather than reading the evidence.

</details>
