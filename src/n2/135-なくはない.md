# ～なくはない

**Meaning** · it is not that I don't ～; I can, up to a point - a double negative that concedes the bare minimum

Two negatives cancel, but they do not cancel cleanly. <code>{f|飲|の}めなくはない</code>
is not "I can drink"; it is "it is not the case that I cannot drink", which in
practice means *a little, if I have to, and do not make a thing of it*. The pattern
exists so that you can avoid saying no without saying a proper yes.

**What is denied: a total negative that somebody has assumed.** Compare the two N3
neighbours. <code>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> denies that a rule always holds;
<code>わけではない</code> denies an inference drawn from what was said. `なくはない`
denies **zero** - it answers the assumption "so you don't X at all, then" with "well,
not *none*". Almost always a が or けど clause follows, spelling out the reservation
that made the speaker hedge in the first place.

## Formation

The `ない` of an ordinary negative becomes `なく`, and `はない` is added on top.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ～ない → ～なくはない | {f|飲|の}まない → {f|飲|の}まなくはない |
| Verb (potential) | ～ない → ～なくはない | {f|行|い}けない → {f|行|い}けなくはない |
| い-adjective | ～くない → ～くなくはない | おいしくない → おいしくなくはない |
| な-adjective | ～で + なくはない | {f|便利|べんり}でない → {f|便利|べんり}でなくはない |
| Noun | Noun + で + なくはない | {f|問題|もんだい}でない → {f|問題|もんだい}でなくはない |
| ある | ない → なくはない | {f|不安|ふあん}がない → {f|不安|ふあん}がなくはない |
| Polite | ～なくはありません／なくはないです | {f|分|わ}かる → {f|分|わ}からなくはありません |
| Softer variants | ～なくもない／～ないでもない | {f|言|い}えない → {f|言|い}えなくもない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with な-adjectives and nouns the double negative gets clumsy fast
（{f|静|しず}かでなくはない）, and Japanese speakers usually switch to
<code>{f|静|しず}かでないわけではない</code> or simply rephrase. Verbs and potential
forms are where this pattern really lives.

</div>

## Nuance & register

`なくはない` is hedging equipment, and everything about how it feels follows from
that. It is polite, it is non-committal, and it buys the speaker room. In business
Japanese it is the standard way to say "possible, but I would rather not", and a
listener who knows the language hears the reluctance immediately - so it is not a
way of hiding your reservations, only of stating them courteously.

Register is broad. <code>なくはないです</code> and
<code>なくはありません</code> are fine in formal speech; the plain form is fine among
friends. What it is *not* is enthusiastic. Answering an invitation with
<code>{f|行|い}けなくはない</code> is technically a yes and socially a shrug; if you
actually want to go, say so.

The variants shade the feeling. `なくもない` swaps the contrastive `は` for `も` and
comes out vaguer and more musing - the speaker is thinking aloud rather than
answering an accusation. <code>ないでもない</code> is the most literary of the three
and turns up more in writing than in speech. All three are grudging.

There is a real intonation cue here too: the sentence usually trails off, and the
listener is expected to supply the "but". Delivering
<code>{f|分|わ}からなくはない</code> and then stopping is a complete social act -
partial agreement, plus an invitation to keep talking.

## Contrast

### ～なくはない vs ～ないことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - very close in meaning. <code>ないことはない</code> nominalises
the whole idea and sounds a little more deliberate; `なくはない` is tighter and
attaches straight to the negative.

</div>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}でもでき<b>ないことはない</b>。
It is not as though it can't be done single-handed.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}でもでき<b>なくはない</b>。
I could just about do it on my own.
</pre>

Honest answer: in most sentences either is fine and native speakers will accept
both. The tendency - and it is a tendency, not a rule - is that
<code>ないことはない</code> concedes that the *event is possible at all*, while
`なくはない` concedes a *small degree or frequency*, which is why `なくはない` sits so
naturally on habits: <code>{f|食|た}べなくはない</code>, "I do eat it, sometimes".

### ～なくはない vs ～なくもない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `は` answers a challenge; `も` muses. Same grudging concession,
different posture.

</div>

<pre>
「{f|全然|ぜんぜん}{f|興味|きょうみ}ないんでしょ?」「{f|興味|きょうみ}が<b>なくはない</b>よ。」
"You're not interested at all, are you." "It's not that I'm not interested."
</pre>

<pre>
そう{f|言|い}われてみると、{f|彼|かれ}の{f|案|あん}のほうがいいような{f|気|き}がし<b>なくもない</b>。
Now that you put it like that, I half feel his plan might be the better one.
</pre>

The `は` version pushes back against something the other person just said - the
contrastive `は` is doing exactly what it always does. The `も` version has no
opponent; the speaker is turning an idea over.

### ～なくはない vs ～わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくはない` denies **zero**. <code>わけではない</code> denies
**a conclusion**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|使|つか}わ<b>なくはない</b>が、{f|週|しゅう}に{f|二回|にかい}{f|程度|ていど}です。
It is not that I never use it daily - but realistically twice a week.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|使|つか}っている<b>わけではない</b>。
It is not as though I use it every day.
</pre>

These face in opposite directions. The first starts from "you never use it" and
concedes upward; the second starts from "you use it constantly" and trims downward.
Choosing the wrong one leaves your listener with the opposite impression of how much
you use the thing.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 「{f|行|い}きませんか。」「はい、<b>{f|行|い}けなくはないです</b>。」 → ✅ 「はい、ぜひ<b>{f|行|い}きたいです</b>。」
  As an acceptance this is chilly. The double negative always implies a reservation,
  so it reads as "I suppose I could", not as a keen yes.

- ❌ この{f|部屋|へや}は<b>{f|広|ひろ}いなくはない</b>。 → ✅ この{f|部屋|へや}は<b>{f|広|ひろ}くなくはない</b>。
  An い-adjective has to reach its ～くない shape first; only then does the second
  negative go on.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は<b>{f|学生|がくせい}なくはない</b>。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}は<b>{f|学生|がくせい}でなくはない</b>。（more naturally {f|学生|がくせい}でないわけではない）
  Nouns need `で` before the negative. In practice this shape is clumsy enough that
  most speakers rephrase.

- ❌ <b>{f|全|まった}く{f|興味|きょうみ}がなくはない</b>。 → ✅ <b>{f|興味|きょうみ}がなくはない</b>。
  {f|全|まった}く insists on zero and `なくはない` refuses zero, so the two cancel each
  other out. If you want a partner adverb, {f|少|すこ}しは or それなりに fits.

## Practice

**1.** 「この{f|仕事|しごと}、{f|今日中|きょうじゅう}にできますか。」「できな____が、{f|残業|ざんぎょう}になります。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「でき<b>なくはありません</b>が、{f|残業|ざんぎょう}になります。」

The が clause names the price, which is the classic environment for this pattern:
possible in principle, unattractive in practice. <code>なくはないです</code> is
equally polite and slightly softer.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|甘|あま}いものは（{f|好|す}きではない・{f|好|す}きでなくはない）が、{f|毎日|まいにち}は{f|食|た}べない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|甘|あま}いものは<b>{f|好|す}きでなくはない</b>が、{f|毎日|まいにち}は{f|食|た}べない。

The が clause limits the frequency rather than denying the liking, so a flat
{f|好|す}きではない would contradict it. That said, this is exactly the case where a
speaker would more likely say
<code>{f|嫌|きら}いではない</code> - shorter and less tangled.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I can read it without a dictionary, more or less, but it takes a very long time."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|辞書|じしょ}なしでも{f|読|よ}め<b>なくはない</b>が、ものすごく{f|時間|じかん}がかかる。

"More or less" plus a "but" clause is the shape this pattern was built for. The
potential form {f|読|よ}める takes the double negative directly:
{f|読|よ}めない → {f|読|よ}めなくはない.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼|かれ}の{f|気持|きも}ちは{f|少|すこ}しも{f|分|わ}からなくはない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼|かれ}の{f|気持|きも}ちは<b>{f|分|わ}からなくはない</b>。（or {f|少|すこ}しは{f|分|わ}かる）

{f|少|すこ}しも demands a full negative underneath it, and `なくはない` is the
opposite - a concession that there is *some*. The two adverbial pressures point
against each other, so one of them has to go.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|手伝|てつだ}え<b>なくはない</b>。
(b) {f|手伝|てつだ}う<b>わけではない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a reluctant offer. Starting from the assumption that the speaker cannot help,
it concedes that help is possible - and the listener is meant to hear the unspoken
"but not gladly, and not for long".

(b) is not an offer at all. It cancels an idea the listener had formed, probably
from something the speaker said: no, helping is not what this amounts to. (a) moves
towards yes; (b) pushes an interpretation away.

</details>
