# ～はもとより

**Meaning** · not to mention; needless to say; let alone - the first item goes
without saying, and the second one is the news

<code>もとより</code> means "from the outset, as a matter of course". The pattern uses
that to rank two items: what stands in front of it is treated as **already obvious**,
and the sentence spends its energy on what follows. It is the formal written cousin
of <code>はもちろん</code>.

## Formation

It attaches to a noun. A clause has to be nominalised first, which is one reason the
pattern is far commoner with nouns than with anything else.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + はもとより | {f|英語|えいご} → {f|英語|えいご}はもとより |
| Noun + particle | Noun + で／に + はもとより | {f|国内|こくない}ではもとより |
| Verb (nominalised) | plain form + の／こと + はもとより | {f|読|よ}める → {f|読|よ}めるのはもとより |
| い-adjective (nominalised) | ～こと + はもとより | {f|安|やす}いことはもとより |
| な-adjective (nominalised) | ～なこと + はもとより | {f|静|しず}かなことはもとより |
| Second item | Noun + も／まで／さえ + predicate | …{f|中国語|ちゅうごくご}<b>も</b>{f|話|はな}せる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the は is part of the pattern and does not change. A noun that would
normally take を or が loses that particle here, exactly as it does before an ordinary
topic は: ❌<code>{f|漢字|かんじ}をもとより</code> → ✅<code>{f|漢字|かんじ}はもとより</code>.

</div>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

Formal and written. Company statements, tourist brochures, prospectuses, newspaper
features and prepared speeches are where it lives. In ordinary conversation the same
sentence takes <code>はもちろん</code>, which means the same thing and costs nothing
in formality.

The pattern makes a claim, and it is worth being deliberate about it: **the first
item is uncontroversial.** Saying <code>{f|英語|えいご}はもとより</code> asserts that
this person speaking English is not news. If the listener would in fact be surprised
by it, the sentence misfires - it sounds as if you are pretending everyone already
knew. When neither item is obvious, <code>のみならず</code> or
<code>だけでなく</code> is the honest choice.

In a negative sentence the same ranking comes out as English "let alone". The mountain
hut example above works because both facts are absences, and さえ marks the second as
the more striking one. This is a common shape in writing about hardship or shortage.

The second item is normally marked with も, まで or さえ - a plain が or を there leaves
the sentence sounding truncated. Note also that this pattern describes; it does not
introduce a request. A notice asking you to bring two documents wants
<code>に{f|加|くわ}えて</code>, not this.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>もとより</code> without は is a separate adverb meaning "from the
start, all along": <code>{f|危険|きけん}はもとより{f|承知|しょうち}の{f|上|うえ}だった</code>,
"I knew the risk from the outset". Same word, different job; the connector always
follows a noun with は.

</div>

## Contrast

### はもとより vs はもちろん

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different room. <code>はもちろん</code> is
everyday; <code>はもとより</code> is written and formal.

</div>

<pre>
あの{f|店|みせ}は{f|昼|ひる}<b>はもちろん</b>、{f|夜|よる}も{f|混|こ}んでるよ。
That place is packed in the evening as well as at lunchtime, obviously.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|当|とう}{f|施設|しせつ}は{f|平日|へいじつ}<b>はもとより</b>、{f|休日|きゅうじつ}も{f|多|おお}くの{f|方|かた}にご{f|利用|りよう}いただいております。
The facility is used by large numbers of people at weekends as well as, naturally, on weekdays.
</pre>

There is no difference in what the two sentences claim. The difference is who is
speaking: a friend, and an organisation. <code>はもちろん</code> is also the one that
can be softened, doubled or thrown into the middle of a sentence in speech;
<code>はもとより</code> is fixed.

### はもとより vs のみならず

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are formal, but <code>はもとより</code> **ranks** the two
items, while <code>のみならず</code> merely **adds** the second one.

</div>

<pre>
{f|市|し}の{f|補助|ほじょ}は{f|高齢者|こうれいしゃ}<b>はもとより</b>、{f|子育|こそだ}て{f|世帯|せたい}にも{f|広|ひろ}がった。
The city's support has been extended to households with children, as well as to the elderly of course.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|市|し}の{f|補助|ほじょ}は{f|高齢者|こうれいしゃ}<b>のみならず</b>、{f|子育|こそだ}て{f|世帯|せたい}にも{f|広|ひろ}がった。
The city's support has been extended not only to the elderly but to households with children.
</pre>

The first assumes the reader already takes support for the elderly for granted; the
second makes no assumption at all and simply reports both. If you are not certain
your reader shares the assumption, <code>のみならず</code> is safer - a wrong
"obviously" is a small rudeness.

### はもとより vs もとより

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with は it is a **connector** between two items. Without は it is
an **adverb** meaning "from the beginning".

</div>

<pre>
{f|反対|はんたい}される<b>ことはもとより</b>、{f|批判|ひはん}まで{f|覚悟|かくご}していた。
He was prepared for opposition as a matter of course, and for criticism as well.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|反対|はんたい}されることは<b>もとより</b>{f|覚悟|かくご}していた。
He had been prepared for opposition from the start.
</pre>

They look almost identical on the page. The test is whether a second item follows:
the connector always has one, marked with も, まで or さえ. The adverb has none, and
modifies the verb directly.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|漢字|かんじ}<b>をもとより</b>、{f|敬語|けいご}も{f|難|むずか}しい。 → ✅ {f|漢字|かんじ}<b>はもとより</b>、{f|敬語|けいご}も{f|難|むずか}しい。
  The は is part of the pattern, and it displaces を or が the way a topic marker
  always does.

- ❌ {f|野菜|やさい}はもとより、{f|果物|くだもの}が{f|値上|ねあ}がりしている。 → ✅ {f|野菜|やさい}はもとより、{f|果物|くだもの}<b>も</b>{f|値上|ねあ}がりしている。
  Without も the second half reads as a replacement rather than an addition, and the
  sentence loses its point.

- ❌ この{f|映画|えいが}、{f|音楽|おんがく}はもとより{f|映像|えいぞう}もすごいんだよ。 → ✅ この{f|映画|えいが}、{f|音楽|おんがく}<b>はもちろん</b>{f|映像|えいぞう}もすごいんだよ。
  A written connector in a sentence that ends in んだよ. Nothing is ungrammatical;
  it simply does not belong in that room.

- ❌ {f|会員|かいいん}{f|証|しょう}はもとより、{f|保険証|ほけんしょう}もお{f|持|も}ちください。 → ✅ {f|会員|かいいん}{f|証|しょう}<b>に{f|加|くわ}えて</b>、{f|保険証|ほけんしょう}もお{f|持|も}ちください。
  This pattern comments on how things are; it does not issue instructions. A notice
  telling you what to bring takes the neutral additive.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|地方|ちほう}は{f|冬|ふゆ}の{f|寒|さむ}さ____、{f|夏|なつ}の{f|暑|あつ}さも{f|厳|きび}しい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|冬|ふゆ}の{f|寒|さむ}さ<b>はもとより</b>、{f|夏|なつ}の{f|暑|あつ}さも{f|厳|きび}しい。

Cold winters in a northern region are exactly the sort of thing a reader takes for
granted, which is what licenses the pattern; the hot summers are the information.
Note the も carrying the second half.

</details>

**2.** Choose: 「ここのラーメン、スープ（はもとより・はもちろん）{f|麺|めん}もうまいよ。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

スープ<b>はもちろん</b>{f|麺|めん}もうまいよ。

The sentence is a recommendation to a friend, ending in よ. <code>はもとより</code>
would be like putting a tuxedo on a bowl of noodles. The two mean the same thing;
only the register differs.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese (formal written style): "This hall is used by local residents, needless to say, and by visitors from other prefectures as well."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|会館|かいかん}は{f|地元|じもと}の{f|住民|じゅうみん}<b>はもとより</b>、{f|県外|けんがい}からの{f|来訪者|らいほうしゃ}にも{f|利用|りよう}されている。

"Needless to say" is the giveaway: the first group is presented as obvious. The
second item takes にも, and the passive
<code>{f|利用|りよう}されている</code> keeps the register consistent.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|新製品|しんせいひん}は{f|若者|わかもの}はもとより、{f|高齢者|こうれいしゃ}に{f|人気|にんき}がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|新製品|しんせいひん}は{f|若者|わかもの}はもとより、{f|高齢者|こうれいしゃ}<b>にも</b>{f|人気|にんき}がある。

The second item needs も. As written, the sentence says the product is popular with
older people *instead*, which contradicts the "of course" it just applied to younger
ones.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|大学|だいがく}は{f|研究|けんきゅう}{f|環境|かんきょう}はもとより、{f|寮|りょう}の{f|設備|せつび}も{f|充実|じゅうじつ}している。
(b) この{f|大学|だいがく}は{f|研究|けんきゅう}{f|環境|かんきょう}のみならず、{f|寮|りょう}の{f|設備|せつび}も{f|充実|じゅうじつ}している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats good research facilities as the baseline any university of this standing
would have, and offers the halls of residence as the extra. It sounds like a
prospectus written by someone confident of the institution's reputation.

(b) makes no such assumption. It simply reports two strengths, and would be the safer
sentence in a piece of neutral description or a comparison between several
universities.

</details>
