# 気がする・気になる

**Meaning** · {f|気|き}がする = I have a feeling that; {f|気|き}になる = it is on my mind, it bothers me

{f|気|き} is the mind's weather - mood, attention, inclination. Japanese builds a
whole family of expressions on it, and two of them do work that English handles
with quite different verbs. <code>{f|気|き}がする</code> reports a hunch you have
not reasoned your way to. <code>{f|気|き}になる</code> reports something that has
snagged your attention and will not let go.

## Usage

<code>{f|気|き}がする</code> follows a **plain-form clause** and turns it into a
hunch: {f|明日|あした}は{f|晴|は}れる{f|気|き}がする, "I've a feeling it'll clear
up". With a noun or な-adjective the link is `な` -
{f|無理|むり}な{f|気|き}がする - and the softened
<code>ような{f|気|き}がする</code> is available for any of them when you want to
sound less certain still. It conjugates as an ordinary verb:
{f|気|き}がした, {f|気|き}がします, {f|気|き}がしない.

<code>{f|気|き}になる</code> is intransitive, so whatever is bothering you takes
**が**: {f|結果|けっか}が{f|気|き}になる, あの{f|一言|ひとこと}が{f|気|き}になる.
A clause becomes a noun first, with `の` or `か`:
{f|遅|おそ}いのが{f|気|き}になる, {f|来|く}るかどうかが{f|気|き}になる. The feeling
covers everything from mild curiosity to real anxiety, and nothing about the word
chooses between them - the context does.

Its transitive partner <code>{f|気|き}にする</code> is the same emotion with a
person deciding to have it, and it takes **を**:
{f|人|ひと}の{f|目|め}を{f|気|き}にする. That is why the standard reassurance is
<code>{f|気|き}にしないで</code> - "don't let it get to you" - and not
❌<code>{f|気|き}にならないで</code>, which would be asking someone's attention to
behave itself.

<div class="note">

**Note** - a third member, <code>{f|気|き}に{f|入|い}る</code>, looks like the
others and means something else entirely: to like, to take to. What you like is
marked with `が` or `を` - この{f|色|いろ}が{f|気|き}に{f|入|い}った,
この{f|色|いろ}を{f|気|き}に{f|入|い}っている - and the negative
<code>{f|気|き}に{f|入|い}らない</code> means "I don't care for it".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>{f|気|き}がする</b>。
I have a feeling it's going to rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|誰|だれ}かに{f|見|み}られている<b>{f|気|き}がした</b>。
I had the feeling somebody was watching me.
</pre>

<pre>
さっきの{f|彼|かれ}の{f|一言|ひとこと}が<b>{f|気|き}になって</b>、{f|眠|ねむ}れない。
That one remark of his is preying on my mind, and I can't sleep.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|周|まわ}りの{f|目|め}を<b>{f|気|き}にしすぎる</b>と、{f|自分|じぶん}のやりたいことができなくなる。
If you worry too much about how others see you, you end up unable to do what you want.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

All three expressions are **register-neutral** and completely ordinary. They work
in plain and polite speech, in writing, and in every age group; nothing about them
is casual or formal.

<code>{f|気|き}がする</code> is a hedge, and Japanese speakers use it constantly to
avoid sounding too sure. Compared with <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> it claims less:
it says the impression arrived on its own rather than that the speaker worked it
out. That makes it useful for softening opinions, and it is why
<code>そんな{f|気|き}がします</code> is such a common answer to a question you do
not want to answer flatly.

<code>{f|気|き}になる</code> spans a wide emotional range.
あの{f|人|ひと}が{f|気|き}になる can mean "I'm worried about her" or "I'm rather
taken with her",
and only the situation decides - which is a small trap, since the romantic reading
is very much alive in ordinary conversation. The noun
<code>{f|気|き}になること</code>, "things I'm wondering about", is a standard
heading in a meeting or a checklist.

<code>{f|気|き}にしないで</code> and its polite
<code>{f|気|き}にしないでください</code> are among the most useful set phrases in
the language: they cover "never mind", "don't worry about it" and "no offence
taken". The blunter <code>{f|気|き}にするな</code> is the plain command form and
belongs between close friends or from a senior to a junior.

## Contrast

### {f|気|き}になる vs {f|気|き}にする

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|気|き}になる</code> is something happening **to
you**, and takes が. <code>{f|気|き}にする</code> is something **you do**, and
takes を.

</div>

<pre>
{f|試験|しけん}の{f|結果|けっか}<b>が{f|気|き}になって</b>、{f|何|なに}も{f|手|て}につかない。
I'm so anxious about the exam results that I can't settle to anything.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|小|ちい}さなミスをいつまでも<b>{f|気|き}にして</b>いても、{f|仕方|しかた}がない。
There's no point dwelling on a small mistake for ever.
</pre>

The first describes a state the speaker did not choose; the second describes a
habit they could drop. This is exactly why advice and reassurance use
<code>{f|気|き}にする</code>: you can be told to stop doing something, but not to
stop having something happen to you.

### {f|気|き}がする vs と{f|思|おも}う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> is a **judgement** you would
defend. <code>{f|気|き}がする</code> is an **impression** that simply arrived.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|案|あん}のほうがいい<b>と{f|思|おも}います</b>。
I think this proposal is the better one.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|案|あん}のほうがいい<b>{f|気|き}がします</b>。
I have a feeling this proposal is the better one.
</pre>

In a meeting the difference is real. The first invites a discussion of your
reasons; the second signals that you have none yet and are going on instinct.
Because it claims so little, <code>{f|気|き}がする</code> is also the safer way to
disagree with someone senior.

### {f|気|き}になる vs {f|気|き}に{f|入|い}る

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|気|き}になる</code> means it **occupies** your mind.
<code>{f|気|き}に{f|入|い}る</code> means it **pleases** you.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|時計|とけい}のデザインが<b>{f|気|き}に{f|入|い}った</b>ので、{f|買|か}うことにした。
I liked the design of this watch, so I decided to buy it.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|時計|とけい}の{f|値段|ねだん}が<b>{f|気|き}になって</b>、まだ{f|買|か}えずにいる。
The price of this watch is nagging at me, and I still haven't been able to buy it.
</pre>

The shapes are close enough that learners swap them, and the result is a
compliment turning into a complaint. Note also the tense habits:
<code>{f|気|き}に{f|入|い}った</code> in the past is the normal way to say "I like
it", where English keeps the present.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|結果|けっか}<b>を</b>{f|気|き}になる。 → ✅ {f|結果|けっか}<b>が</b>{f|気|き}になる。
  <code>なる</code> is intransitive, so what bothers you is the subject.
  <code>を</code> belongs with <code>{f|気|き}にする</code>.

- ❌ {f|気|き}に<b>ならないで</b>ください。 → ✅ {f|気|き}に<b>しないで</b>ください。
  You can ask a person to stop dwelling on something; you cannot ask their
  attention to stop being caught.

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>のを</b>{f|気|き}がする。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>{f|気|き}がする</b>。
  The clause attaches directly in its plain form. There is no particle between it
  and <code>{f|気|き}がする</code>.

- ❌ {f|大丈夫|だいじょうぶ}<b>だ{f|気|き}がする</b>。 → ✅ {f|大丈夫|だいじょうぶ}<b>な{f|気|き}がする</b>。
  な-adjectives and nouns link with `な` (or with
  <code>ような</code>), never with `だ`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|会議|かいぎ}で{f|言|い}い{f|過|す}ぎたかもしれない。{f|部長|ぶちょう}の{f|反応|はんのう}____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|部長|ぶちょう}の{f|反応|はんのう}<b>が{f|気|き}になる</b>

The manager's reaction is what has caught the speaker's attention, and it did so
without being invited - so the intransitive <code>{f|気|き}になる</code> with `が`
is the fit. <code>{f|気|き}にする</code> would suggest the speaker is choosing to
brood.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|失敗|しっぱい}は{f|誰|だれ}にでもあることだから、そんなに（{f|気|き}にならないで・{f|気|き}にしないで）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そんなに<b>{f|気|き}にしないで</b>。

You are asking the listener to stop dwelling on it, which is an action they can
take, so the transitive <code>{f|気|き}にする</code> is required.
<code>{f|気|き}にならないで</code> would be instructing their attention to behave,
which is not something anyone can promise.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I have a feeling I've met him somewhere before, but I can't remember where."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}とはどこかで{f|会|あ}ったことがある<b>{f|気|き}がする</b>が、どこだったか{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}せない。

"I have a feeling" is <code>{f|気|き}がする</code>, and the clause in front of it
stays in its plain form with no particle. Using
<code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> here would overstate the case - the speaker has an
impression, not a belief they could argue for.

</details>
