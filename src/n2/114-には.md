# ～には

**Meaning** · in order to do this - here is what it takes

`には` sets up a goal and then measures the world against it. The main clause never
says what you are going to do; it says what is **needed**, what **works**, or how the
situation **rates** for that purpose. The `は` is doing its usual contrastive job: for
*this* aim, as opposed to any other, the following is true.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + には | {f|働|はたら}く → {f|働|はたら}くには |
| Verb (compound) | dictionary form + には | {f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}む → {f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}むには |
| Noun (purpose) | Noun + には | {f|合格|ごうかく} → {f|合格|ごうかく}には |
| Noun (person as yardstick) | Noun + には | {f|子供|こども} → {f|子供|こども}には |
| Main clause | requirement, means or rating | ～が{f|必要|ひつよう}だ / ～ばいい / ～すぎる / ～しかない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - only the plain dictionary form goes in front of `には`. Not
<code>{f|働|はたら}きますには</code>, not <code>{f|働|はたら}いたには</code>. Politeness is
carried entirely by the ending of the main clause.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}で{f|働|はたら}く<b>には</b>、ビザが{f|必要|ひつよう}です。
To work in Japan you need a visa.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|服|ふく}は{f|子供|こども}<b>には</b>{f|大|おお}きすぎる。
These clothes are too big for a child.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|駅|えき}まで{f|行|い}く<b>には</b>、どのバスに{f|乗|の}ればいいですか。
Which bus should I take to get to the station?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|健康|けんこう}を{f|保|たも}つ<b>には</b>、{f|規則|きそく}{f|正|ただ}しい{f|生活|せいかつ}が{f|一番|いちばん}だ。
For keeping healthy, nothing beats a regular routine.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|持|も}ち{f|歩|ある}く<b>には</b>{f|重|おも}すぎますね。
This dictionary is too heavy to carry around.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|住|す}む<b>には</b>、この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|広|ひろ}すぎるかもしれない。
For living alone, this flat may be rather too big.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|外国語|がいこくご}を{f|身|み}につける<b>には</b>、{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|少|すこ}しずつでも{f|続|つづ}けるしかありません。
The only way to get a foreign language into your bones is to keep at it a little every day.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会員|かいいん}{f|割引|わりびき}を{f|受|う}ける<b>には</b>、{f|事前|じぜん}にアプリで{f|登録|とうろく}しておく{f|必要|ひつよう}があります。
To get the member's discount you have to register on the app in advance.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`には` is the natural voice of instructions. Guidebooks, recipes, help pages, station
signs and advice columns are built out of it, because its whole shape is *goal, then
requirement*. It is register-neutral and takes whatever politeness the main clause
carries, so it is equally at home in <code>ビザが{f|必要|ひつよう}です</code> and
<code>ビザがいる</code>.

The evaluation use - <code>{f|子供|こども}には{f|大|おお}きすぎる</code>,
<code>{f|私|わたし}には{f|難|むずか}しい</code> - is worth noticing as the same pattern from
a different angle. There the noun is the yardstick rather than the goal, and the `は`
still contrasts: too big *for a child*, whatever it may be for an adult. Learners find
this softening useful, because <code>{f|私|わたし}には{f|分|わ}かりません</code> claims only
that *you* cannot follow it, where <code>{f|分|わ}かりません</code> can sound like a verdict
on the explanation.

Two things `には` cannot do. It cannot be followed by a statement of what you intend to
do - that is `ために` - and it does not attach comfortably to negatives; "in order not
to" is <code>～ないように</code> or <code>～ないために</code>, not <code>～ないには</code>.

## Contrast

### ～には vs ～ために

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the main clause says **what you do**, use `ために`. If it says
**what is required or how things rate**, use `には`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|資格|しかく}を{f|取|と}る<b>ために</b>、{f|毎晩|まいばん}{f|二時間|にじかん}{f|勉強|べんきょう}している。
I study two hours every evening in order to get the qualification.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|資格|しかく}を{f|取|と}る<b>には</b>、{f|実務|じつむ}{f|経験|けいけん}が{f|三年|さんねん}{f|以上|いじょう}{f|要|い}る。
To get the qualification you need three years or more of practical experience.
</pre>

The first reports the speaker's own effort; the second states a condition of the world
that holds for anybody. This is also why job adverts and application forms use `には`
almost exclusively - they are describing requirements, not intentions.

### ～には vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` frames the task as something you spend resources on -
time, tools, effort. `には` frames it as a goal you are trying to reach.

</div>

<pre>
このソフトは{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|整理|せいり}する<b>のに</b>とても{f|便利|べんり}だ。
This software is very handy for organising photos.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|整理|せいり}する<b>には</b>、まずフォルダを{f|作|つく}りましょう。
To organise your photos, start by making folders.
</pre>

`のに` pairs with <code>{f|便利|べんり}だ</code>, <code>{f|使|つか}う</code>,
<code>{f|時間|じかん}がかかる</code>, <code>{f|役|やく}に{f|立|た}つ</code> - all of which
measure a cost or a usefulness. `には` pairs with requirements and methods. Where either
fits, as in 「{f|駅|えき}まで{f|歩|ある}くには/のに{f|二十分|にじゅっぷん}かかる」, `のに` is the
smoother choice.

### ～には vs ～なら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なら` supposes the listener might do it. `には` takes the goal as
given and gets straight to the requirements.

</div>

<pre>
{f|京都|きょうと}へ{f|行|い}く<b>なら</b>、{f|秋|あき}がおすすめですよ。
If you're going to Kyoto, I'd recommend autumn.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|紅葉|こうよう}の{f|時期|じき}に{f|泊|と}まる<b>には</b>、{f|半年|はんとし}{f|前|まえ}から{f|予約|よやく}が{f|要|い}ります。
To get a room during the autumn leaves you need to book six months ahead.
</pre>

`なら` responds to something the other person has just raised and can be followed by a
suggestion. `には` does not care whether anyone is planning the trip; it states what the
goal costs. In a guidebook you will see `には`, in a conversation `なら`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|働|はたら}きます<b>には</b>、ビザが{f|要|い}ります。 → ✅ {f|働|はたら}く<b>には</b>、ビザが{f|要|い}ります。
  The verb in front of `には` is always in plain dictionary form, however polite the
  rest of the sentence is.

- ❌ {f|風邪|かぜ}をひかない<b>には</b>、うがいをしましょう。 → ✅ {f|風邪|かぜ}をひかない<b>ように</b>、うがいをしましょう。
  "In order not to" is not `には` territory. Use <code>～ないように</code>, or
  <code>～ないために</code> in writing.

- ❌ {f|大学|だいがく}に{f|入|はい}る<b>には</b>、{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|塾|じゅく}に{f|通|かよ}おう。 → ✅ {f|大学|だいがく}に{f|入|はい}る<b>には</b>、{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|塾|じゅく}に{f|通|かよ}うのが{f|近道|ちかみち}だ。
  A `には` clause cannot be answered with a resolution. Turn the second half into a
  statement about what is needed or what works.

- ❌ {f|試験|しけん}に{f|合格|ごうかく}した<b>には</b>、{f|努力|どりょく}が{f|必要|ひつよう}だった。 → ✅ {f|試験|しけん}に{f|合格|ごうかく}する<b>には</b>、{f|努力|どりょく}が{f|必要|ひつよう}だった。
  A past form in front of `には` is not this pattern. The goal is stated in the
  dictionary form even when the main clause is in the past.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|機械|きかい}を{f|動|うご}かす____、まず{f|電源|でんげん}を{f|入|い}れてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|動|うご}かす<b>には</b>

The sentence names a goal and then gives the first step towards it, which is the
instruction-manual shape `には` was made for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|海外|かいがい}で{f|生活|せいかつ}する（には・ために）、{f|語学|ごがく}{f|学校|がっこう}に{f|通|かよ}っています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|海外|かいがい}で{f|生活|せいかつ}する<b>ために</b>、{f|語学|ごがく}{f|学校|がっこう}に{f|通|かよ}っています。

The main clause reports what the speaker is doing about the goal, not what the goal
requires, so `ために` is correct. `には` would need an ending like
<code>{f|語学力|ごがくりょく}が{f|欠|か}かせない</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This report is a bit long to read in one sitting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|報告書|ほうこくしょ}は{f|一度|いちど}で{f|読|よ}む<b>には</b>{f|少|すこ}し{f|長|なが}い。

Here `には` supplies the yardstick - reading it in one go - and the main clause rates
the report against it. <code>～すぎる</code> would work equally well in place of
<code>{f|少|すこ}し{f|長|なが}い</code>.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|太|ふと}らないには、{f|夜|よる}{f|遅|おそ}く{f|食|た}べないほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|太|ふと}らない<b>ように</b>、{f|夜|よる}{f|遅|おそ}く{f|食|た}べないほうがいい。

`には` does not sit on a negative verb. A goal phrased as something you want to avoid
takes <code>～ないように</code>, or you can flip it to
<code>{f|体重|たいじゅう}を{f|保|たも}つには</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|私|わたし}<b>に</b>{f|難|むずか}しいです。
(b) この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|私|わたし}<b>には</b>{f|難|むずか}しいです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is not natural Japanese - a bare `に` cannot mark the person a judgement is
relative to, so the sentence reads as an unfinished thought.

(b) works because `は` adds the contrast the meaning needs: hard *for me*, whatever it
may be for someone else. That contrastive shading is the reason this pattern is `には`
and not `に`.

</details>
