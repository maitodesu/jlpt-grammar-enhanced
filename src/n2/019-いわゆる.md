# いわゆる

**Meaning** · so-called; what people call - hands the following noun over as a
label that is already in circulation

`いわゆる` says: this is the word other people use for it. It does not claim the
word is a good one, and - unlike English "so-called" - it does not usually claim it
is a bad one either. Most of the time it is simply a way of quoting the public
vocabulary.

## Usage

`いわゆる` is a {f|連体詞|れんたいし} - a word whose only job is to sit in front of a noun. It
never conjugates, never takes `な` or `の`, and cannot modify a verb or an
adjective. The shape is always the same:

- いわゆる + noun → いわゆる{f|方言|ほうげん}
- いわゆる + noun phrase → いわゆる{f|地方|ちほう}{f|創生|そうせい}の{f|政策|せいさく}
- いわゆる + a quoted term → いわゆる「{f|終活|しゅうかつ}」

Because it is quoting somebody else's word, the noun after it is very often in
quotation marks, in katakana, or a piece of jargon the writer expects some readers
not to know. In casual speech the same move is made with
<code>いわゆる～ってやつ</code>:
<code>これがいわゆる{f|時差|じさ}ぼけってやつか</code>.

It descends from <code>{f|言|い}う</code> plus an old passive ending - literally
"which is said" - and is occasionally written {f|所謂|いわゆる}, though modern
practice is kana.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は<b>いわゆる</b>{f|天才|てんさい}だ。
He is what you'd call a genius.
</pre>

<pre>
これが<b>いわゆる</b>{f|五月病|ごがつびょう}です。
This is the thing people call "May sickness".
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|辺|あた}りは<b>いわゆる</b>{f|下町|したまち}で、{f|昔|むかし}ながらの{f|商店街|しょうてんがい}が{f|残|のこ}っている。
This area is what's known as shitamachi - the old shopping streets are still here.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>いわゆる</b>「{f|働|はたら}き{f|方|かた}{f|改革|かいかく}」が{f|始|はじ}まってから、{f|残業|ざんぎょう}は{f|本当|ほんとう}に{f|減|へ}ったのだろうか。
Since the so-called "work-style reform" began, has overtime really gone down?
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It is equally at home in a newspaper, a lecture and a
conversation, and it does not change form for politeness.

The trap for English speakers is the one the translation creates. English
"so-called" is nearly always sceptical - "his so-called friends" means they are not
really friends. `いわゆる` is only *mildly* distancing, and in most sentences it is
not distancing at all: it is a courtesy to the listener, marking a term as one that
circulates publicly so that nobody has to pretend they invented it. When a Japanese
writer wants the sceptical reading, they get it from the surrounding sentence, from
quotation marks, or from a following <code>とやら</code> or `らしい` - not from
`いわゆる` on its own.

The one condition the word does impose is that the label must already exist. You
cannot use `いわゆる` to introduce a name you have just made up, and you cannot use
it for a term you are about to define for the first time. If your reader could not
in principle have met the word before, `いわゆる` is the wrong signal.

There is a slightly bookish flavour to it in speech - a hair more considered than
「なんていうか」 - but nothing that would sound out of place among friends.

## Contrast

### いわゆる vs いわば

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いわゆる` quotes a label **other people** already use.
`いわば` introduces a comparison **you** are making up on the spot: "so to speak;
as it were."

</div>

<pre>
{f|祖父|そふ}はこの{f|村|むら}の<b>いわゆる</b>{f|世話役|せわやく}だった。
My grandfather was what the village called its fixer.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|祖父|そふ}はこの{f|村|むら}の、<b>いわば</b>{f|生|い}きた{f|図書館|としょかん}だった。
My grandfather was, so to speak, the village's living library.
</pre>

Nobody in the village ever called him a library; that is the speaker's metaphor,
which is why `いわば` is the only option in the second sentence. The two words look
and sound close enough to be swapped by accident, and the swap is always audible:
`いわゆる` in front of a fresh metaphor sounds as if you are attributing your own
invention to the public, and `いわば` in front of an established term sounds as if
you had never heard it before.

Grammatically they differ too. `いわゆる` must be followed by a noun. `いわば` is an
adverb and can head a whole clause: <code>いわば、これは{f|練習|れんしゅう}なのだ</code>.

### いわゆる vs {f|呼|よ}ばれる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `いわゆる` assumes the label is known and moves on.
<code>～と{f|呼|よ}ばれる</code> stops to **state** that the label exists, and is
what you use when introducing a term for the first time.

</div>

<pre>
{f|京都|きょうと}には<b>いわゆる</b>{f|町家|まちや}が{f|多|おお}く{f|残|のこ}っている。
Kyoto still has many of what are called machiya townhouses.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|京都|きょうと}の{f|古|ふる}い{f|木造|もくぞう}{f|住宅|じゅうたく}は{f|町家|まちや}と<b>{f|呼|よ}ばれる</b>。
Kyoto's old wooden houses are called machiya.
</pre>

The second sentence teaches the word; the first sentence uses it. That is why a
textbook or an encyclopaedia entry introduces a term with
<code>{f|呼|よ}ばれる</code> and only afterwards allows itself `いわゆる`. Using
`いわゆる` for a word the reader has certainly never met is a small failure of
manners: it implies they should have known.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ いわゆる<b>な</b>{f|天才|てんさい} → ✅ <b>いわゆる</b>{f|天才|てんさい}
  `いわゆる` is a {f|連体詞|れんたいし} and is already in noun-modifying shape. It takes neither
  `な` nor `の`.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}の{f|話|はなし}は<b>いわゆる</b>{f|面白|おもしろ}い。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}の{f|話|はなし}は<b>いわば</b>{f|落語|らくご}だ。
  Something has to follow `いわゆる`, and that something must be a noun. It cannot
  modify an adjective or a verb at all.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}が<b>いわゆる</b>「ゆる{f|勉|べん}」と{f|呼|よ}んでいる{f|方法|ほうほう} → ✅ {f|私|わたし}が「ゆる{f|勉|べん}」と{f|呼|よ}んでいる{f|方法|ほうほう}
  The sentence itself admits the name is the speaker's own coinage. `いわゆる`
  claims the opposite - that the term is already out there - so the two halves
  contradict each other.

## Practice

**1.** {f|最近|さいきん}よく{f|聞|き}く____「{f|副業|ふくぎょう}」を{f|始|はじ}める{f|人|ひと}が{f|増|ふ}えている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|最近|さいきん}よく{f|聞|き}く<b>いわゆる</b>「{f|副業|ふくぎょう}」を{f|始|はじ}める{f|人|ひと}が{f|増|ふ}えている。

<code>{f|最近|さいきん}よく{f|聞|き}く</code> already tells us the term is in
circulation, which is precisely the condition `いわゆる` needs. Note that it sits
directly in front of the noun, with the quotation marks doing the extra work of
flagging the word as borrowed.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この{f|建物|たてもの}は{f|町|まち}の（いわゆる・いわば）{f|顔|かお}のような{f|存在|そんざい}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|建物|たてもの}は{f|町|まち}の<b>いわば</b>{f|顔|かお}のような{f|存在|そんざい}だ。

Nobody actually calls the building "the town's face" - the speaker is inventing the
image, and <code>のような</code> confirms it is a comparison rather than a name.
`いわゆる` would falsely present the metaphor as a term everyone uses.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼女|かのじょ}は<b>いわゆる</b>{f|優等生|ゆうとうせい}だ。
(b) {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|優等生|ゆうとうせい}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) is a plain description: she gets good marks and behaves well.

(a) holds the word at arm's length. It reports the label society attaches to her
without the speaker fully signing up to it, so depending on the sentence around it
you may hear anything from neutral quoting - "she's the model-student type" - to a
faint reservation about whether the label means much. The distancing is mild, and
much weaker than English "so-called": to say clearly that she is *not* really an
{f|優等生|ゆうとうせい} you would need to add something like
<code>{f|表向|おもてむ}きは</code> or a following `らしい`.

</details>
