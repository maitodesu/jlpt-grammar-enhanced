# 以上 - now that

**Meaning** · now that; given that; so long as X is the case - read <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code>. Once the premise holds, the conclusion is not optional

This <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> has nothing to do with counting. It follows a
whole clause and says: take X as established, and Y follows from it as a matter of
logic. What comes after is a duty, a judgement, a resolution or a piece of firm
advice - never a bare report of events. It is the reasoning voice of contracts,
editorials and people explaining why a rule applies to them too.

## Formation

The clause in front is in plain, noun-modifying form. Nouns and な-adjectives
require である; だ and な are both wrong here.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + {f|以上|いじょう}（は） | やる → やる{f|以上|いじょう} |
| Verb (past) | た-form + {f|以上|いじょう} | {f|引|ひ}き{f|受|う}けた → {f|引|ひ}き{f|受|う}けた{f|以上|いじょう} |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + {f|以上|いじょう} | {f|謝|あやま}らない → {f|謝|あやま}らない{f|以上|いじょう} |
| い-adjective | plain form + {f|以上|いじょう} | {f|若|わか}い → {f|若|わか}い{f|以上|いじょう} |
| な-adjective | ～である + {f|以上|いじょう} | {f|必要|ひつよう}な → {f|必要|ひつよう}である{f|以上|いじょう} |
| Noun | ～である + {f|以上|いじょう} | {f|社員|しゃいん} → {f|社員|しゃいん}である{f|以上|いじょう} |
| With topic marker | … + {f|以上|いじょう}は | {f|出|で}る → {f|出|で}る{f|以上|いじょう}は |

<div class="note">

**Note** - adding は changes almost nothing; it puts a fraction more weight on the
premise, the way は always does. Both
<code>やる{f|以上|いじょう}</code> and <code>やる{f|以上|いじょう}は</code> are
completely ordinary.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
やる<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>は、きちんとやりたい。
If I'm doing it at all, I want to do it properly.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|約束|やくそく}した<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、{f|必|かなら}ず{f|守|まも}ります。
I gave my word, so I will keep it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|社員|しゃいん}である<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、{f|会社|かいしゃ}の{f|規則|きそく}には{f|従|したが}う{f|必要|ひつよう}があります。
So long as you are an employee, you have to follow the company's rules.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|証拠|しょうこ}がない<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、{f|彼|かれ}を{f|疑|うたが}うことはできない。
In the absence of evidence, we cannot treat him as a suspect.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|海外|かいがい}で{f|暮|く}らす<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、その{f|国|くに}の{f|習慣|しゅうかん}を{f|尊重|そんちょう}すべきだ。
If you're going to live abroad, you should respect the customs of the country.
</pre>

<pre>

Since it is written into the contract, "I didn't know" will not wash afterwards.
</pre>

Given that the facility is run on public money, there is a duty to explain matters to residents.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> sounds *reasoned*. Where `からには` sounds like a
person steeling themselves, <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> sounds like an argument
being made - which is why it is so common in written Japanese, in company
communication and in the parts of conversation where someone is explaining why a
principle applies. It is not stiff in the way <code>{f|上|うえ}は</code> is; you
can say it at work without sounding theatrical.

Two things follow from that reasoned quality. First, it handles **standing
states** comfortably: <code>{f|社員|しゃいん}である{f|以上|いじょう}</code>,
<code>{f|日本|にほん}にいる{f|以上|いじょう}</code>, "for as long as this remains
true". Second, the premise is often something the speaker cannot change - a
contract, a legal status, a promise already given - and the sentence acknowledges
that it binds them. That gives it a faint air of resignation when the speaker is
talking about themselves, and a distinctly firm one when they are talking about
somebody else.

Handle that second case with some care. A sentence of the shape
<code>～である{f|以上|いじょう}、～べきだ</code> aimed at a listener is a strong
move: it tells them their own
situation obliges them. In Japanese, as in English, "as long as you're living
under my roof" is an argument that wins the point and costs something. The polite
ending softens the form but not the content.

## Contrast

### ～{f|以上|いじょう} vs ～からには

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "now that". `からには` is the speaker's resolve;
<code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> is a principle being applied.

</div>

<pre>
{f|部長|ぶちょう}を{f|任|まか}された<b>からには</b>、チームを{f|変|か}えてみせます。
Now they've made me department head, I'm going to change this team, you'll see.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|部長|ぶちょう}である<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、{f|部下|ぶか}の{f|失敗|しっぱい}にも{f|責任|せきにん}がある。
As department head, you bear responsibility for your subordinates' mistakes too.
</pre>

Notice what each takes in front of it. `からには` is happiest with an action the
speaker has taken or will take. <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> takes that too, but
it also takes a plain standing state with である, and that is where `からには` starts
to sound odd - a state is not something you can resolve to do.

### ～{f|以上|いじょう} vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` and `ので` explain *why something happened*.
<code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> explains *why something must be so*.

</div>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っていた<b>ので</b>、{f|遠足|えんそく}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になりました。
The outing was cancelled because it was raining.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|台風|たいふう}が{f|近|ちか}づいている<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、{f|中止|ちゅうし}にするしかない。
With a typhoon coming in, cancelling is the only option.
</pre>

The first sentence reports a completed event and its cause; nothing is being
argued. The second reasons forward from a fact to the decision it forces. Swap the
endings and both sentences go wrong - <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> cannot lead
into a plain past-tense report, and `ので` drains the second sentence of its
"therefore".

### ～{f|以上|いじょう} vs ～{f|限|かぎ}り（は）

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both can be "as long as".
<code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> says the fact *obliges* you.
<code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> marks the *duration or extent* over which something
holds.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|会社|かいしゃ}にいる<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、ルールには{f|従|したが}うべきだ。
As long as you work here, you ought to follow the rules.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}が{f|知|し}っている<b>{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、そんな{f|話|はなし}は{f|出|で}ていない。
As far as I know, no such proposal has come up.
</pre>

<code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> draws a boundary around a period, a scope or a body of
knowledge, and the second half stays inside it. <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> does
not measure anything; it converts a fact into an obligation. Where they genuinely
overlap - <code>{f|生|い}きている{f|以上|いじょう}</code> versus
<code>{f|生|い}きている{f|限|かぎ}り</code> - the first stresses the duty that
comes with being alive, the second the stretch of time.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}だ<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b> → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}である<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>
  <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> is a noun-like element, and a noun links to it with
  である. Plain だ cannot modify it.

- ❌ {f|会員|かいいん}な<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b> → ✅ {f|会員|かいいん}である<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>
  Do not carry the な over from <code>わけだ</code>, where
  <code>{f|会員|かいいん}なわけだ</code> is correct. These two nouns behave
  differently, and である is the only safe link here.

- ❌ {f|遅|おく}れた<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いませんでした。 → ✅ {f|遅|おく}れた<b>ので</b>、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いませんでした。
  The second half is a plain report of what happened. There is no obligation or
  judgement for the premise to force, so this is `ので` territory.

- ❌ {f|参加|さんか}する<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>は、たぶん{f|楽|たの}しいでしょう。 → ✅ {f|参加|さんか}する<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>は、{f|全力|ぜんりょく}で{f|楽|たの}しみたい。
  A vague guess is not something the premise compels. What follows
  <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> has to be a conclusion you are prepared to stand
  on.

## Practice

**1.** プロの{f|選手|せんしゅ}である____、{f|体調|たいちょう}{f|管理|かんり}も{f|仕事|しごと}のうちだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

プロの{f|選手|せんしゅ}である<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>

Being a professional is a standing state, and である is the required link. The
second half states the duty that the state creates - exactly the shape this
pattern is built for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まっていた（{f|以上|いじょう}・ので）、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|遅|おく}れてしまいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|止|と}まっていた<b>ので</b>、{f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|遅|おく}れてしまいました。

This is a past event explained by its cause - an apology, not an argument.
<code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> needs a conclusion with force behind it, and
"I ended up late" has none.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "As long as you have borrowed the money, you have to pay it back."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お{f|金|かね}を{f|借|か}りた<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、{f|返|かえ}さなければならない。

The た-form marks the borrowing as an established fact, and
<code>なければならない</code> supplies the obligation that fact creates. With は -
<code>{f|借|か}りた{f|以上|いじょう}は</code> - the premise is pressed a little
harder.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|外国人|がいこくじん}な{f|以上|いじょう}、ビザの{f|規則|きそく}をよく{f|調|しら}べておくべきだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|外国人|がいこくじん}である<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、ビザの{f|規則|きそく}をよく{f|調|しら}べておくべきだ。

Nouns take である before <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code>, not な. The rest of the
sentence is well formed: <code>べきだ</code> is a natural second half.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|日本|にほん}で{f|働|はたら}く<b>{f|以上|いじょう}</b>、{f|敬語|けいご}は{f|避|さ}けて{f|通|とお}れない。
(b) {f|日本|にほん}で{f|働|はたら}く<b>からには</b>、{f|敬語|けいご}をものにしたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an argument about how things are. Working in Japan simply entails dealing
with honorific language, whoever you are - the sentence would sit comfortably in an
article.

(b) is a personal undertaking. The speaker has decided to work in Japan and is
committing themselves to master the language that goes with it. Swap the endings
and both sentences feel slightly off-key: a resolution wants `からには`, a general
principle wants <code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code>.

</details>
