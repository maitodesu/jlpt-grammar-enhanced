# ～のも当然だ

**Meaning** · it's only natural that…; no wonder - given what has just been established, this outcome could not really have gone any other way

The kanji word here is {f|当然|とうぜん}, "natural, a matter of course". Where `わけだ` at N3 worked a conclusion out,
<code>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</code> **passes sentence on** it: the speaker has the
premise in hand and rules that the result was inevitable. The `の` packages a whole
clause into a noun, so the pattern can take a long, argued lead-up - which is exactly
what it usually has. Socially it is a judgement, and it cuts both ways: it can be
sympathy ("of course you're upset") or condemnation ("well, what did they expect?").

## Formation

`の` nominalises the clause; <code>{f|当然|とうぜん}</code> is a な-adjective-shaped
noun, so `だ` / `です` follows it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ | {f|怒|おこ}る → {f|怒|おこ}るのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ | {f|落|お}ちた → {f|落|お}ちたのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ | {f|信|しん}じない → {f|信|しん}じないのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ |
| い-adjective | plain form + のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ | {f|寒|さむ}い → {f|寒|さむ}いのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ |
| な-adjective | ～な + のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ | {f|不安|ふあん}だ → {f|不安|ふあん}なのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ |
| Noun | ～な + のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ | {f|赤字|あかじ} → {f|赤字|あかじ}なのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ |
| Polite | …のも{f|当然|とうぜん}です | {f|驚|おどろ}く → {f|驚|おどろ}くのも{f|当然|とうぜん}です |
| With は | …のは{f|当然|とうぜん}だ | {f|反対|はんたい}する → {f|反対|はんたい}するのは{f|当然|とうぜん}だ |
| Modifying a noun | {f|当然|とうぜん}の + Noun | {f|結果|けっか} → {f|当然|とうぜん}の{f|結果|けっか} |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the premise normally arrives in the same sentence, in a
<code>～のだから</code> or <code>～んだから</code> clause. Without it the verdict has
nothing to rest on and sounds abrupt.

</div>

## Nuance & register

<code>{f|当然|とうぜん}</code> is an everyday word and the pattern is neither formal
nor casual - it turns up in conversation, in newspaper commentary and in the middle
of an argument alike. What varies is how hard it lands. The verdict is absolute:
`当然` says the outcome *had* to follow, and a listener who disagrees with the premise
will hear it as high-handed.

That gives the pattern two very different social lives. Aimed at a person's feelings
it is kind - <code>{f|不安|ふあん}になるのも{f|当然|とうぜん}ですよ</code> tells someone
their reaction is normal, and the ですよ softens it further. Aimed at an organisation
or at somebody's conduct it is severe, and it is a favourite of editorials for exactly
that reason.

The bare word `当然` used as a reply ("obviously") is blunt, and used about a favour
someone has done you it is worse - it implies you were owed. Keep
<code>{f|当然|とうぜん}です</code> for endorsing an outcome, not for accepting a
kindness.

<div class="note">

**Note** - `も` and `は` both appear here. <code>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</code> is
slightly the softer, adding the outcome to a picture the listener can already see;
<code>のは{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</code> asserts it more squarely, and is what you use when
you are arguing the point rather than observing it. The difference is small, and both
are common.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ vs ～のももっともだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|当然|とうぜん}</code> = it could not have been otherwise.
<code>もっとも</code> = you can hardly blame them. The first ranges over any outcome;
the second judges a **person's reaction** and takes their side.

</div>

<pre>
{f|一晩中|ひとばんじゅう}{f|停電|ていでん}していたのだから、{f|冷蔵庫|れいぞうこ}の{f|中身|なかみ}がだめになった<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>。
The power was out all night, so of course everything in the fridge was ruined.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|何度|なんど}も{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|破|やぶ}られたのだから、{f|彼|かれ}が{f|腹|はら}を{f|立|た}てる<b>のももっともだ</b>。
He's had promises broken again and again, so you can hardly blame him for being angry.
</pre>

A ruined fridge has no feelings and no case to make, so `もっともだ` is not available
there. Where the subject *is* a human reaction, both work, and the choice is one of
stance: `当然だ` rules, `もっともだ` sympathises.

### ～のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ vs ～て{f|当然|とうぜん}だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same verdict on two different chassis.
<code>て{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</code> hangs straight off the predicate and lands
compactly; <code>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</code> nominalises first and can carry a
longer, heavier clause.

</div>

<pre>
これだけ{f|準備|じゅんび}したのだから、{f|合格|ごうかく}し<b>て{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>。
After preparation like that, passing is the least you'd expect.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|試験|しけん}の{f|前|まえ}の{f|晩|ばん}に{f|徹夜|てつや}でゲームをしていたのだから、{f|落|お}ちた<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>。
He was up all night gaming before the exam, so it's no wonder he failed.
</pre>

The second clause in the `のも` version has its own past tense and its own subject,
which the て-form version would strain to carry. Where the clause is short, either
works and the difference is one of weight.

### ～のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ vs ～わけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけだ` reports that the pieces **add up**.
<code>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</code> adds that they could not have added up any other
way.

</div>

<pre>
{f|時計|とけい}が{f|五分|ごふん}{f|進|すす}んでいたのか。どうりで{f|早|はや}く{f|着|つ}いた<b>わけだ</b>。
The clock was five minutes fast? That explains why I got here early.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|整備|せいび}を{f|一度|いちど}もしていなかったのだから、{f|途中|とちゅう}で{f|止|と}まった<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>。
It had never once been serviced, so of course it broke down on the way.
</pre>

`わけだ` can be pure arithmetic and carries no attitude. `のも当然だ` always carries
one: there is a judgement in it that somebody should have seen this coming.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|怒|おこ}る{f|当然|とうぜん}だ。 → ✅ {f|怒|おこ}る<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>。
  <code>{f|当然|とうぜん}</code> is a noun-like word, so a verb clause has to be
  nominalised with `の` before it can be the subject.

- ❌ {f|不安|ふあん}<b>だのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b> → ✅ {f|不安|ふあん}<b>なのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>
  な-adjectives and nouns take `な` before `の`. <code>{f|赤字|あかじ}なのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</code>
  works the same way.

- ❌ {f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれるのは{f|当然|とうぜん}です。（thanking someone） → ✅ {f|手伝|てつだ}ってくださって{f|助|たす}かりました。
  Applied to a favour, the verdict says you were entitled to it. Use it on outcomes,
  not on other people's kindness.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}るのも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}りそうだ。
  The pattern rules on something already the case or already decided. A forecast has
  nothing established behind it, so there is no verdict to give.

## Practice

**1.** {f|三日|みっか}も{f|水|みず}をやらなかったのだから、{f|枯|か}れてしまった____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|枯|か}れてしまった<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>

The <code>のだから</code> clause supplies the premise, and the sentence closes by
ruling the outcome inevitable. Note that the past tense sits inside the nominalised
clause, not on <code>{f|当然|とうぜん}</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|残業|ざんぎょう}が{f|続|つづ}いているのだから、{f|彼女|かのじょ}が{f|疲|つか}れた{f|顔|かお}をしている（のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ・のももっともだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|疲|つか}れた{f|顔|かお}をしている<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>

Looking tired is a physical outcome, not a judgement or a complaint she is making, so
`もっともだ` has nothing to endorse. `もっともだ` would fit if the clause were
「{f|文句|もんく}を{f|言|い}う」 or 「{f|辞|や}めたくなる」.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "They cut the budget in half, so it's only natural that the work is behind schedule."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|予算|よさん}を{f|半分|はんぶん}に{f|減|へ}らしたのだから、{f|作業|さぎょう}が{f|遅|おく}れている<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>。

The <code>のだから</code> clause carries the premise, which the pattern needs, and
"only natural" is exactly <code>{f|当然|とうぜん}</code>. Note the verdict here is
critical, not sympathetic - that is normal for this pattern.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|初|はじ}めての{f|発表|はっぴょう}だから、{f|緊張|きんちょう}だのも{f|当然|とうぜん}です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|初|はじ}めての{f|発表|はっぴょう}だから、{f|緊張|きんちょう}する<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}です</b>。

<code>{f|緊張|きんちょう}</code> is a noun that needs する to become a clause; as a
bare noun it would have to be <code>{f|緊張|きんちょう}なのも</code>, which is not what
the sentence means. Either way, `だ` cannot stand before `の`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|社員|しゃいん}が{f|辞|や}めていく<b>のも{f|当然|とうぜん}だ</b>。
(b) {f|社員|しゃいん}が{f|辞|や}めていく<b>のももっともだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a verdict on the company. Given whatever has just been described, people
leaving was the only possible result, and the sentence carries an implied "they
brought it on themselves". It is cool and a little severe.

(b) sides with the employees. It treats leaving as a reasonable response to their
situation and says the speaker would have done the same. Where (a) judges the
organisation, (b) defends the people - which is why (b) so often continues with
「が、…」 and a qualification.

</details>
