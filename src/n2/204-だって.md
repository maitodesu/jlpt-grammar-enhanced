# だって

**Meaning** · but…! because…! - the word you push back with when you are defending yourself against what the other person just said

`だって` opens a reply. Someone has told you off, told you to do something, or
asked why you did not, and `だって` announces that you are about to give your side.
It is not neutral: built into it is the refusal to simply accept what was said.

## Usage

`だって` stands at the **head of a whole clause**, usually at the very start of your
turn in a conversation, and it needs something to push against. It cannot join two
clauses inside one sentence - that is the job of `から` and `ので`.

The typical shape is a two-part exchange:

- **A** asks or complains.
- **B** answers with `だって` + the reason, very often finished off with `んだもん`,
  `んだから` or `じゃない`.

That final `もん` is what makes the combination sound so much like a child; without
it the same sentence lands as an ordinary, if unrepentant, explanation. `だって`
itself has no polite form. The politeness of the sentence lives in its ending -
<code>だって、{f|忙|いそが}しかったんです</code> is possible, though the word still colours the
whole reply as an excuse.

Two other `だって` exist and are unrelated to this one. There is the particle
`だって` meaning "even, too" (<code>{f|子供|こども}だってわかる</code>, "even a child
knows that"), which follows a noun rather than opening a clause; and there is the
hearsay `だって` at the *end* of a sentence
(<code>{f|来|こ}ないんだって</code>, "apparently he isn't coming"). Position tells
you which you have: front of the clause, this one; after a noun, "even"; at the end,
"I hear".

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`だって` is spoken, casual, and never written except in dialogue. It does not appear
in a report, a business email or a formal apology.

Its core flavour is self-defence, and self-defence sounds childish when the other
person has a point. The stereotype attached to it is a sulking child or a
girlfriend in a drama, and the pairing <code>だって…んだもん</code> is the full
version of that voice. Adults do use it - constantly, with friends, with family,
and often with a knowing smile that acknowledges the excuse is an excuse - so the
word is not off-limits to grown-ups. It leans young, and in the stereotype somewhat
feminine, but those are tendencies rather than rules; a man protesting to a friend
that <code>だって、{f|時間|じかん}がなかったんだよ</code> is entirely ordinary.

Where it genuinely goes wrong is upward and outward: to a boss, a client, a
teacher or a police officer. There, any sentence beginning `だって` turns an apology
into an argument, however polite the ending is. The adult repair is to put the
reason before the apology and drop the protest word:
<code>{f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まっておりまして、{f|遅|おく}れました。{f|申|もう}し{f|訳|わけ}ございません</code>.
In writing, <code>～ものですから</code> and <code>～ため</code> carry the same content
with none of the attitude.

Compare `でも`, the other way to start a reply with "but". `でも` simply contradicts
- it can be followed by anything, including another opinion. `だって` promises a
**reason**, and specifically a reason why you are not at fault. If nothing follows
that could serve as a justification, `だって` sounds unfinished.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|時間|じかん}がなかった<b>だって</b>{f|行|い}けませんでした。 → ✅ {f|時間|じかん}がなかった<b>から</b>{f|行|い}けませんでした。
  `だって` cannot sit between two clauses. It opens a clause; it does not link one
  to another.

- ❌ 「{f|報告|ほうこく}が{f|遅|おく}れていますね。」「<b>だって</b>、{f|忙|いそが}しかったんです。」（to your manager） → ✅ 「{f|申|もう}し{f|訳|わけ}ありません。{f|急|きゅう}な{f|対応|たいおう}が{f|続|つづ}いておりまして…」
  The polite ending does not rescue it. Opening with `だって` tells your manager you
  are contesting the criticism.

- ❌ <b>だってです</b>、{f|知|し}らなかったんです。 → ✅ <b>だって</b>、{f|知|し}らなかったんです。
  `だって` never inflects. It has no ですform, no past, no negative.

- ❌ <b>だって</b>{f|私|わたし}は{f|新人|しんじん}です。（meaning "even I am a beginner"） → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>だって</b>{f|新人|しんじん}です。
  The "even" `だって` follows the noun it applies to. Moving it to the front of the
  sentence changes it into the protest word.

## Practice

**1.** Fill the blank: 「どうして{f|電話|でんわ}してくれなかったの。」「____、{f|番号|ばんごう}を{f|知|し}らなかったんだもん。」

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「<b>だって</b>、{f|番号|ばんごう}を{f|知|し}らなかったんだもん。」

The reply is defending itself against a complaint and ends in `んだもん`, which is
the partner `だって` almost always turns up with. `でも` would also fit the slot
grammatically but would sound like the start of a different point rather than an
excuse.

</details>

**2.** Translate into Japanese, casual: "Don't blame me - you're the one who said we didn't need a reservation."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|私|わたし}のせいにしないでよ。<b>だって</b>、{f|予約|よやく}はいらないって{f|言|い}ったのは{f|君|きみ}じゃない。

`だって` introduces the justification for refusing the blame, and the
<code>～じゃない</code> ending pushes the fact back at the listener. In this shape
`だって` is doing exactly the job it was made for.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance, and say which one you could use with a
client:
(a) <b>だって</b>、{f|資料|しりょう}が{f|届|とど}かなかったんです。
(b) {f|資料|しりょう}が{f|届|とど}かなかった<b>ものですから</b>、{f|準備|じゅんび}が{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) and (b) give the same reason, but (a) leads with a protest: it answers an
accusation and declines to take the blame, and however polite the `んです` ending
is, the client hears an argument.

(b) presents the same fact as background to an apology and never contradicts
anyone. Only (b) is usable with a client, and it is also the form you would write.
Keep (a) for friends and family.

</details>
