# ～というわけではない

**Meaning** · it's not that ～; that's not to say ～ - takes back an interpretation while leaving the facts alone

You already know <code>わけではない</code> from N3: a partial denial that removes an
inference without denying what produced it. `というわけではない` is that same tool with
`という` bolted on, and the `という` does two concrete jobs. It **quotes** - so the
thing being denied is presented as a *claim*, something somebody might say - and
because it quotes, a bare noun can attach to it directly, with no `な` in sight.

**What is denied: the wording of the conclusion, not the state of affairs.** That
extra step of distance is why this is the shape people reach for when correcting a
misunderstanding of what they just said. <code>{f|嫌|きら}いなわけではない</code> talks
about the speaker's feelings; <code>{f|嫌|きら}いというわけではない</code> talks about
whether "dislikes it" is the right description.

## Formation

`という` quotes the clause, so nouns and な-adjectives need no linking `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + というわけではない | {f|反対|はんたい}している → {f|反対|はんたい}しているというわけではない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + というわけではない | {f|知|し}らない → {f|知|し}らないというわけではない |
| い-adjective | plain form + というわけではない | {f|高|たか}い → {f|高|たか}いというわけではない |
| な-adjective | ～（だ）+ というわけではない | {f|不満|ふまん} → {f|不満|ふまん}だというわけではない |
| Noun | Noun + というわけではない | {f|専門家|せんもんか} → {f|専門家|せんもんか}というわけではない |
| Polite | … + というわけではありません | {f|急|いそ}いでいる → {f|急|いそ}いでいるというわけではありません |
| Casual speech | … + ってわけじゃない | {f|嫌|いや}だ → {f|嫌|いや}だってわけじゃない |
| Mid-sentence | … + というわけではなく | {f|値段|ねだん} → {f|値段|ねだん}が{f|理由|りゆう}というわけではなく |
| Usual partner adverbs | {f|必|かなら}ずしも／{f|全|まった}く／いつも／{f|全部|ぜんぶ} | - → {f|必|かなら}ずしもそうというわけではない |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if what you are denying is something **somebody said or might
say**, use `という`. If you are denying a **plain state of affairs**, the bare
<code>わけではない</code> is enough - and with a noun it would then need `な`.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>
{f|全|まった}く{f|知|し}らない<b>というわけではありません</b>。
It's not as though I know nothing about it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|専門家|せんもんか}<b>というわけではない</b>が、{f|少|すこ}しは{f|詳|くわ}しい。

</pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

## Nuance & register

This is the polite way to say "you've slightly misunderstood me", and it is
everywhere in careful conversation. Because it denies a *description* rather than a
fact, it lets you correct someone without accusing them of being wrong about the
world - a genuinely useful social move.

Register runs the full range. <code>というわけではありません</code> is standard
business Japanese; <code>ってわけじゃない</code> is casual speech and very common
among friends; plain <code>というわけではない</code> is what you write in an essay.
The `という` itself is register-neutral and adds a shade of formality relative to the
bare form, mostly because it is longer.

The partner adverbs matter. {f|必|かなら}ずしも is the most frequent, and the pair
<code>{f|必|かなら}ずしも～というわけではない</code> is close to a fixed expression in
written argument. {f|全|まった}く, いつも, {f|全部|ぜんぶ} and {f|誰|だれ}でも work the
same way: they set up a total claim so that this pattern can trim it back to
something defensible. Note what that combination does to
{f|全|まった}く - normally an absolute, here it is *inside* the denial, so
<code>{f|全|まった}く{f|知|し}らないというわけではない</code> means "I do know
something", not "I know nothing".

A small point of politeness. Because the pattern hands the other person a correction,
it is often prefaced with a softener - いえ, あの, or a
<code>すみません、{f|言|い}い{f|方|かた}が{f|悪|わる}かったのですが</code>. Delivered
bare it can sound as if you are picking them up on their words.

## Contrast

### ～というわけではない vs ～わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `という` denies **a description someone has offered**. The bare
form denies **a state of affairs**. With a noun, the difference is also visible:
`という` attaches directly, the bare form needs `な`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}が{f|責任者|せきにんしゃ}<b>というわけではない</b>。
He isn't exactly what you'd call the person in charge.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}が{f|責任者|せきにんしゃ}な<b>わけではない</b>。
It isn't the case that he's the person in charge.
</pre>

The first quibbles with the label: he does some of what a person in charge does, but
the word overstates it. The second denies the arrangement itself. In practice
Japanese speakers use the `という` version far more often with nouns, because the
label is usually what is at issue.

### ～というわけではない vs ～とは{f|限|かぎ}らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `というわけではない` withdraws **this interpretation, here**.
<code>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> removes **the word "always" from a general rule**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|静|しず}かな{f|人|ひと}が{f|冷|つめ}たい<b>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>。
Quiet people aren't necessarily cold.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|静|しず}かにしていたからといって、{f|怒|おこ}っていた<b>というわけではありません</b>。
Just because I was quiet doesn't mean I was angry.
</pre>

The first is about people in general and would sit comfortably in an essay. The
second is about the speaker, on one occasion, defending against a reading of their
own behaviour. If the subject is *me, just now*, the pattern is nearly always
`というわけではない`.

### ～というわけではない vs ～わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one letter apart in the middle, opposite in strength.
<code>ではない</code> is a **partial** denial; <code>がない</code> is a **total** one.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}が{f|嘘|うそ}をついている<b>というわけではない</b>。
It's not that he's lying.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}が{f|嘘|うそ}をつく<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way he'd lie.
</pre>

The first leaves room - maybe he is mistaken, maybe he is only telling part of it.
The second slams the door and vouches for him completely. Learners swap these
constantly, and the social cost is real: reaching for
<code>わけがない</code> when you meant a gentle correction commits you to a defence
you may not want to make.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>なというわけではない</b>。 → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>というわけではない</b>。
  `という` already does the linking. The `な` belongs to the bare form
  （{f|学生|がくせい}なわけではない）and cannot appear alongside it.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}が{f|犯人|はんにん}だ<b>というわけではない</b>。（intending "there's no way he did it"） → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}が{f|犯人|はんにん}の<b>わけがない</b>。
  This pattern only ever softens. For a flat denial of possibility you need
  <code>わけがない</code> or <code>はずがない</code>.

- ❌（レポートに）データが{f|足|た}りない<b>ってわけじゃない</b>。 → ✅ データが{f|足|た}りない<b>というわけではない</b>。
  ってわけじゃない is spoken contraction. Restore the full form in anything written.

- ❌ {f|必|かなら}ずしも{f|安全|あんぜん}だ<b>というわけがない</b>。 → ✅ {f|必|かなら}ずしも{f|安全|あんぜん}だ<b>というわけではない</b>。
  {f|必|かなら}ずしも means "not necessarily" and therefore needs a partial denial to
  land on. Paired with the total <code>わけがない</code> the two halves fight each
  other.

## Practice

**1.** お{f|酒|さけ}が{f|飲|の}めない____。{f|弱|よわ}いだけです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

お{f|酒|さけ}が{f|飲|の}めない<b>というわけではありません</b>。{f|弱|よわ}いだけです。

The second sentence corrects a description rather than a fact - the speaker does
drink, just not much - which is exactly what this pattern is for. Note that the thing
denied is itself a negative（{f|飲|の}めない）, and that is perfectly normal here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|彼女|かのじょ}が{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|忘|わす}れる（というわけではない・わけがない）。あんなに{f|几帳面|きちょうめん}な{f|人|ひと}なんだから。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼女|かのじょ}が{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|忘|わす}れる<b>わけがない</b>。

The second sentence gives grounds for complete confidence, so the denial has to be
the total one. <code>というわけではない</code> would concede that she might well have
forgotten and merely quibble with how it was put.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm not saying the plan is bad - I'd just like more time."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|計画|けいかく}が{f|悪|わる}い<b>というわけではありません</b>。もう{f|少|すこ}し{f|時間|じかん}がほしいだけです。

"I'm not saying ～" maps almost exactly onto `というわけではない`, because `という` is
what quotes the thing you are declining to have said. This is the standard opening
for a polite objection in a meeting.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|誰|だれ}でもできる{f|簡単|かんたん}なというわけではない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|誰|だれ}でもできる{f|簡単|かんたん}な{f|仕事|しごと}<b>というわけではない</b>。

The stray `な` was left hanging with no noun after it. Either supply the noun the
`な` was modifying, as above, or drop it and write
<code>{f|簡単|かんたん}だというわけではない</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|勉強|べんきょう}している<b>というわけではない</b>。
(b) {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|勉強|べんきょう}している<b>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is about the speaker and corrects an impression: somebody has concluded that they
study daily, and this takes that conclusion back while conceding that they do study.
It is personal and defensive.

(b) is about somebody else, or about people in general, and refuses a guarantee: you
cannot assume they study every day. It is the sentence you would say about a student
whose habits you do not know, not about yourself.

</details>
