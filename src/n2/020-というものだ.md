# ～というものだ

**Meaning** · that is what you call ～; that is simply ～ - names a situation with
the word the speaker thinks it deserves

<code>{f|物|もの}</code> here is the abstract "thing", and
<code>という{f|物|もの}だ</code> literally says "it is a thing called ～". In use it
is a verdict. The speaker takes a situation, puts a noun on it -
<code>{f|無理|むり}</code>, <code>{f|失礼|しつれい}</code>,
<code>{f|友情|ゆうじょう}</code> - and presents that label as obvious to any
reasonable person.

## Formation

The slot in front of <code>というものだ</code> is a **noun** or a な-adjective stem:
the word that names the verdict.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というものだ | {f|常識|じょうしき} → {f|常識|じょうしき}というものだ |
| な-adjective | stem + というものだ | {f|失礼|しつれい} → {f|失礼|しつれい}というものだ |
| Polite | … + というものです | {f|無理|むり} → {f|無理|むり}というものです |
| Softened | … + というものだろう／ではないか | {f|贅沢|ぜいたく} → {f|贅沢|ぜいたく}というものだろう |
| Generic topic | Noun + というものは + general statement | {f|時間|じかん} → {f|時間|じかん}というものは |
| Denying a generalisation | plain clause + というものではない | {f|安|やす}ければいい → {f|安|やす}ければいいというものではない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negative is not a mirror image. <code>というものだ</code> takes a
noun and asserts a label; <code>というものではない</code> takes a whole clause and
denies a blanket rule. They are best learned as two patterns that happen to share a
shape.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - a potential verb occasionally appears in the slot instead of a noun:
<code>これで{f|安心|あんしん}して{f|眠|ねむ}れるというものだ</code>, "now I can
finally sleep easy." It is a real construction but a narrow and rather literary
one. Learn the noun pattern; recognise this one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
それは{f|無理|むり}<b>というものだ</b>。
That is simply asking too much.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|時間|じかん}<b>というもの</b>は、{f|待|ま}ってくれない。
Time, that thing, waits for no one.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|人|ひと}の{f|日記|にっき}を{f|読|よ}むなんて、{f|失礼|しつれい}<b>というものです</b>。
Reading somebody's diary is plain bad manners.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一日|いちにち}で{f|百|ひゃく}{f|語|ご}{f|覚|おぼ}えろというのは、{f|無茶|むちゃ}<b>というものだ</b>。
Telling someone to learn a hundred words in a day is just unreasonable.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|困|こま}っているときに{f|黙|だま}って{f|手|て}を{f|貸|か}してくれる、それが{f|友情|ゆうじょう}<b>というものだろう</b>。
Quietly lending a hand when you're stuck - surely that is what friendship is.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新人|しんじん}に{f|全部|ぜんぶ}{f|任|まか}せておいて{f|文句|もんく}を{f|言|い}うのは、{f|身勝手|みがって}<b>というものではないか</b>。
Dumping the whole thing on the new hire and then complaining is rather selfish, isn't it?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|努力|どりょく}すれば{f|必|かなら}ず{f|報|むく}われる<b>というものでもない</b>。
It isn't as though hard work is always rewarded.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|給料|きゅうりょう}さえ{f|高|たか}ければいい{f|仕事|しごと}だ<b>というものではない</b>。
A job isn't a good job just because the pay is high.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The positive form is a pronouncement, and it sounds like one. It presents the
speaker's judgement as something any sensible person would share, which makes it
useful for stating a principle and slightly grand for stating a preference. You will
hear it from older speakers, in essays and columns, in speeches, and in the mouths
of characters in fiction who are about to say something wise. Among friends it is
mildly theatrical - which is why it is also common as a joke.

Because it claims universality, aiming it at a specific person's behaviour is a
telling-off: <code>それは{f|甘|あま}えというものだ</code> is not a description, it
is a rebuke. Soften it to <code>というものだろう</code> or
<code>というものではないか</code> and it becomes an invitation to agree rather than
a ruling. That is the shape to use if you are not the senior person in the
conversation.

<code>Nというものは</code> is a different animal and much safer. With `は` instead
of `だ`, it introduces a noun as a general category and says something true of the
category as a whole: <code>{f|習慣|しゅうかん}というものは{f|急|きゅう}には{f|変|か}わらない</code>.
This is the reflective, essayistic register - it slows the sentence down and signals
"a general truth follows".

The negative <code>というものではない</code> is the most usable of the three and the
least marked. It is the standard way to push back on an oversimplification in a
meeting without contradicting anyone flatly, and it fits polite speech comfortably:
<code>{f|人数|にんずう}を{f|増|ふ}やせば{f|解決|かいけつ}するというものではありません</code>.

## Contrast

### ～というものだ vs ～ものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ものだ</code> describes **how the world tends to be**.
<code>というものだ</code> puts a **name** on a particular case in front of you.

</div>

<pre>
{f|年|とし}を{f|取|と}ると{f|昔|むかし}のことを{f|思|おも}い{f|出|だ}す<b>ものだ</b>。
People do tend to dwell on the past as they get older.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昔話|むかしばなし}ばかりして{f|人|ひと}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}かないのは、{f|独|ひと}りよがり<b>というものだ</b>。
Talking only about the old days and never listening is what I'd call self-absorbed.
</pre>

<code>ものだ</code> attaches to a verb or adjective clause and generalises about
behaviour; <code>というものだ</code> attaches to a noun and labels. If you can put
the sentence in the form "X is (an instance of) Y", where Y is a noun,
<code>というものだ</code> is available. If the sentence has no such Y, it is not.

### ～というものだ vs ～というものではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the negative does **not** deny the positive. It denies a
*blanket rule* that somebody else has implied.

</div>

<pre>
{f|練習|れんしゅう}すれば{f|勝|か}てる<b>というものではない</b>。
It isn't simply a matter of practising and winning.
</pre>

The sentence does not say practice is useless. It says the "practise and you win"
formula is too crude. That is why the pattern needs a whole conditional-ish clause
in front of it - the thing being denied is a rule, not a label - and why
<code>でもない</code> is such a common variant: it adds "and that isn't the whole
story either".

Note also that this is where the pattern earns its keep in real conversation.
<code>それは{f|違|ちが}います</code> contradicts a person;
<code>そういうものではありません</code> contradicts an idea.

### ～というものだ vs ～わけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>わけだ</code> draws a **logical** conclusion from a
premise. <code>というものだ</code> passes a **value judgement** on a situation.

</div>

<pre>
{f|十年|じゅうねん}{f|住|す}んでいたのだから、{f|方言|ほうげん}が{f|出|で}る<b>わけだ</b>。
He lived there ten years, so naturally the dialect slips out.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|十年|じゅうねん}{f|世話|せわ}になった{f|人|ひと}に{f|挨拶|あいさつ}もしないのは、{f|恩|おん}{f|知|し}らず<b>というものだ</b>。
Not even saying goodbye to someone who looked after you for ten years is sheer ingratitude.
</pre>

Both look backwards at a stated fact, and both end the sentence, which is why they
get confused. But <code>わけだ</code> could be replaced by "that follows", and
<code>というものだ</code> by "that deserves to be called". Only the second is an
opinion, and only the second can be argued with.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|時間|じかん}<b>というものだ</b>、{f|大切|たいせつ}だ。 → ✅ {f|時間|じかん}<b>というものは</b>{f|大切|たいせつ}だ。
  With `だ` the phrase ends the sentence. To introduce a category and then say
  something about it, you need the topic particle `は`.

- ❌ {f|失礼|しつれい}<b>だというものだ</b>。 → ✅ {f|失礼|しつれい}<b>というものだ</b>。
  The noun or な-adjective stem attaches to <code>という</code> bare here. A `だ`
  before it belongs to the ordinary quoting <code>という</code>, not to this
  pattern.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}<b>というものだ</b>。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}だ<b>そうだ</b>。
  Reporting a fact is not judging one. <code>というものだ</code> needs a noun that
  evaluates - <code>{f|無理|むり}</code>, <code>{f|当然|とうぜん}</code>,
  <code>{f|贅沢|ぜいたく}</code> - not a neutral one.

- ❌ {f|安|やす}い<b>というものだ</b>から、{f|買|か}わない。 → ✅ {f|安|やす}ければいい<b>というものではない</b>から、{f|買|か}わない。
  "Cheap" on its own is not a verdict anyone would pronounce. What the speaker
  actually wants to reject is the rule "cheap is good", and rejecting a rule is the
  negative pattern's job.

## Practice

**1.** {f|約束|やくそく}の{f|時間|じかん}に{f|三十分|さんじゅっぷん}も{f|遅|おく}れて{f|謝|あやま}りもしないとは、____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|失礼|しつれい}<b>というものだ</b>（or {f|失礼|しつれい}というものではないか）

The sentence sets up a behaviour and then wants a noun that names it. The plain
<code>だ</code> version is a flat rebuke; the
<code>ではないか</code> version invites the listener to agree, which is gentler and
usually the better choice unless you outrank them.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|値段|ねだん}が{f|高|たか}ければ{f|品質|ひんしつ}がいい（というものだ・というものではない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|値段|ねだん}が{f|高|たか}ければ{f|品質|ひんしつ}がいい<b>というものではない</b>。

What precedes the blank is a conditional rule, not a noun, and only the negative
pattern takes a clause. The meaning is also the natural one: the speaker is
rejecting an oversimplification, not endorsing it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Sending a one-line message after three years of silence is, frankly, rude."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|三年|さんねん}{f|連絡|れんらく}もしなかったのに{f|一行|いちぎょう}のメッセージだけとは、{f|失礼|しつれい}<b>というものだ</b>。

The English "frankly" is doing exactly what
<code>というものだ</code> does in Japanese: presenting the judgement as one any
reasonable person would reach. <code>とは</code> in front is a natural partner,
since it flags the described behaviour as something to be marvelled at.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|習慣|しゅうかん}というものだ、{f|一日|いちにち}では{f|変|か}わらない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|習慣|しゅうかん}<b>というものは</b>、{f|一日|いちにち}では{f|変|か}わらない。

The sentence wants to introduce "habits" as a general category and then state
something true of them, so the noun phrase has to be topicalised with `は`. With
<code>だ</code> the sentence has already finished before the interesting part
arrives.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|人|ひと}に{f|頼|たよ}りすぎるのはよくない。
(b) {f|人|ひと}に{f|頼|たよ}りすぎるのは{f|甘|あま}え<b>というものだ</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an ordinary opinion, offered as the speaker's own and easy to disagree with.

(b) names the behaviour - <code>{f|甘|あま}え</code>, leaning on others - and
presents that name as the obvious one. It is heavier, more moralising, and lands as
a judgement on whoever is doing it rather than as a general remark. Said to
someone's face it is a telling-off; softened to
<code>{f|甘|あま}えというものではないか</code> it becomes a point offered for
agreement.

</details>
