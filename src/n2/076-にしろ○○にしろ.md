# ～にしろ～にしろ

**Meaning** · whether A or B - both possibilities are named and granted, and the sentence states what is true of both, in a formal frame

This is the paired form of `にしろ`, and its written twin `にせよ…にせよ`. It sets out
two options - the two ends of a decision, two kinds of thing, an action and its
opposite - concedes each, and then delivers the requirement, the consequence or the
judgement that neither option escapes.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
this is the *even-handed* one at the *formal* end. It does not take sides and it does
not complain; it lays down what holds in every case. That is why it is the shape rules
and policies are written in.

## Formation

Both halves take a plain form or a bare noun, and both halves must be built alike.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb + its opposite | V + にしろ + V + にしろ | {f|辞|や}める・{f|残|のこ}る → {f|辞|や}めるにしろ{f|残|のこ}るにしろ |
| Verb + its negative | V + にしろ + V ない + にしろ | やる → やるにしろやらないにしろ |
| Two nouns | N + にしろ + N + にしろ | {f|紙|かみ}・データ → {f|紙|かみ}にしろデータにしろ |
| い-adjectives | Adj + にしろ（×2） | {f|多|おお}い・{f|少|すく}ない → {f|多|おお}いにしろ{f|少|すく}ないにしろ |
| な-adjectives | stem + にしろ（×2） | {f|好|す}き・{f|嫌|きら}い → {f|好|す}きにしろ{f|嫌|きら}いにしろ |
| Written register | … + にせよ … + にせよ | {f|認|みと}める・{f|退|しりぞ}ける → {f|認|みと}めるにせよ{f|退|しりぞ}けるにせよ |
| Formal copula | N + であるにしろ（×2） | {f|事実|じじつ} → {f|事実|じじつ}であるにしろ… |
| Past tense in both halves | V た + にしろ（×2） | {f|勝|か}った・{f|負|ま}けた → {f|勝|か}ったにしろ{f|負|ま}けたにしろ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `しろ` and `せよ` are both imperatives of <code>する</code>, and they
do not mix inside one sentence. ✅ にしろ…にしろ, ✅ にせよ…にせよ,
❌ にしろ…にせよ.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the frame is not limited to two. Three or four items in a row are perfectly
normal in written Japanese, and the effect is to sweep the whole field:
<code>{f|春|はる}にせよ{f|夏|なつ}にせよ{f|秋|あき}にせよ{f|冬|ふゆ}にせよ</code>.

</div>

</pre>

<pre>
{f|東京|とうきょう}<b>にしろ</b>{f|大阪|おおさか}<b>にしろ</b>、{f|家賃|やちん}は{f|安|やす}くない。

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にしろ…にしろ` is careful Japanese; `にせよ…にせよ` is written Japanese. Neither is
casual. In relaxed conversation the same job is done by `にしても…にしても`, or simply by
`～ても～なくても`, and a learner who reaches for `にせよ` in a chat message will sound
like a memo.

Where it belongs is anywhere a general rule is being stated: internal guidelines,
notices, editorials, academic prose, and the summing-up part of a formal discussion.
Because it commits to nothing, it is also the polite way to give counsel about a
decision that is not yours to make - you concede both of the other person's options
and then say the one thing you actually want them to hear.

The imperative origin is audible. `しろ` and `せよ` are commands, and the pattern
inherits a faint tone of *take it as you like*. That is what stops the sentence from
sounding indecisive: it is not "I don't know which"; it is "settle that however you
wish - here is what does not change."

Ending the main clause with <code>こと</code>, <code>べきだ</code>,
<code>必要がある</code> or a passive is very common, because the whole construction is
built for general statements rather than personal reactions.

## Contrast

### にしろ…にしろ vs にせよ…にせよ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same pattern, two imperatives. `しろ` is the spoken imperative and
`せよ` the written one, and the pair inherits exactly that difference.

</div>

<pre>
{f|受|う}ける<b>にしろ</b>{f|受|う}けない<b>にしろ</b>、{f|今週中|こんしゅうちゅう}に{f|返事|へんじ}をしよう。
Whether I take it or not, I should reply this week.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|受理|じゅり}する<b>にせよ</b>{f|却下|きゃっか}する<b>にせよ</b>、{f|理由|りゆう}を{f|明記|めいき}すること。
Whether the application is accepted or rejected, the reasons are to be stated.
</pre>

The first is a person thinking aloud. The second is a line from a procedures document,
and the <code>こと</code> ending confirms it. Mixing the two halves - `にしろ` then
`にせよ` - is a straightforward error.

### The paired form vs a single にしろ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **one** `にしろ` concedes and then **objects**. **Two** concede
symmetrically and then state what holds either way.

</div>

<pre>
{f|急|いそ}いでいた<b>にしろ</b>、あの{f|運転|うんてん}は{f|危|あぶ}なすぎる。
Even if he was in a hurry, that was far too dangerous a piece of driving.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|急|いそ}いでいる<b>にしろ</b>いない<b>にしろ</b>、{f|制限|せいげん}{f|速度|そくど}は{f|守|まも}らなければならない。
In a hurry or not, the speed limit has to be observed.
</pre>

The first has a target and is a criticism of one person. The second has no target at
all and is a rule. If your sentence is aimed at somebody, you want the single form.

### にしろ…にしろ vs も…ば…も

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にしろ…にしろ` says **whichever one it is**. `も…ば…も` says **both
of them are so**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}<b>にしろ</b>{f|品質|ひんしつ}<b>にしろ</b>、{f|他社|たしゃ}には{f|勝|か}てない。
On price or on quality, we can't beat the competition.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}<b>も</b>{f|高|たか}けれ<b>ば</b>{f|品質|ひんしつ}<b>も</b>{f|悪|わる}い。
It's expensive and the quality is poor into the bargain.
</pre>

Both frames pair two nouns, and learners reach for the wrong one because both come out
in English with "and" or "or". The `にしろ` frame is about alternatives - pick either,
the verdict is the same. The `も…ば…も` frame is additive - it piles the second fact on
top of the first.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|事実|じじつ}<b>だにしろ</b>うそ<b>だにしろ</b> → ✅ {f|事実|じじつ}<b>にしろ</b>うそ<b>にしろ</b>
  Nouns attach bare. In formal writing `である` is available; `だ` is not.

- ❌ {f|行|い}く<b>にしろ</b>{f|行|い}かない<b>にせよ</b> → ✅ {f|行|い}く<b>にしろ</b>{f|行|い}かない<b>にしろ</b>
  Pick one imperative and use it in both halves.

- ❌（to a friend, over coffee）コーヒー<b>にせよ</b>{f|紅茶|こうちゃ}<b>にせよ</b>、{f|熱|あつ}いのがいいよね。 → ✅ コーヒー<b>にしても</b>{f|紅茶|こうちゃ}<b>にしても</b>、{f|熱|あつ}いのがいいよね。
  Not wrong, but comically formal for the subject. `にしても` is the conversational
  member of the family.

- ❌ {f|勝|か}つ<b>にしろ</b>{f|負|ま}けた<b>にしろ</b> → ✅ {f|勝|か}つ<b>にしろ</b>{f|負|ま}ける<b>にしろ</b>
  Keep the tense the same on both sides. A mixed pair reads as two unrelated clauses.

## Practice

**1.** {f|賛成|さんせい}する____{f|反対|はんたい}する____、{f|理由|りゆう}を{f|述|の}べてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|賛成|さんせい}する<b>にしろ</b>{f|反対|はんたい}する<b>にしろ</b>、{f|理由|りゆう}を{f|述|の}べてください。

Two opposite verbs, both in the plain form, both followed by the same `にしろ`. The
request holds under either option, which is exactly what the paired frame is for.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|社内|しゃない}{f|規定|きてい}に「{f|出張|しゅっちょう}する____{f|在宅|ざいたく}で{f|行|おこな}う____、{f|事前|じぜん}に{f|申請|しんせい}すること」とある。(にしろ・にせよ)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|出張|しゅっちょう}する<b>にせよ</b>{f|在宅|ざいたく}で{f|行|おこな}う<b>にせよ</b>、{f|事前|じぜん}に{f|申請|しんせい}すること。

Internal regulations are written Japanese, and the <code>こと</code> ending confirms
it. `にせよ` is the written imperative and matches the register end to end.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Whether it is a mistake or deliberate, the same rule applies."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|間違|まちが}い<b>にせよ</b>{f|故意|こい}<b>にせよ</b>、{f|同|おな}じ{f|規則|きそく}が{f|適用|てきよう}される。

Two nouns attaching bare, and a main clause in the passive - the shape a written rule
naturally takes. <code>{f|故意|こい}</code>, "deliberate intent", is a noun here, not an
adjective.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|留学|りゅうがく}する<b>にしろ</b>、{f|準備|じゅんび}に{f|一年|いちねん}はかかるだろう。（intended meaning: "whether you study abroad or not, it will take a year to prepare"）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|留学|りゅうがく}する<b>にしろ</b>しない<b>にしろ</b>、{f|準備|じゅんび}に{f|一年|いちねん}はかかるだろう。

A single `にしろ` concedes one case and objects to it; the intended meaning covers both
cases, so the frame has to be doubled. Adding the negative half is what makes it
exhaustive.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|遅|おそ}い<b>にしろ</b>{f|早|はや}い<b>にしろ</b>、いつかは{f|分|わ}かることだ。
(b) {f|遅|おそ}かれ{f|早|はや}かれ、いつかは{f|分|わ}かることだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the ordinary paired concessive: it names the two possibilities as genuine
options and says the outcome is unaffected by which one holds. It is formal but
transparent, and you could substitute any other pair of opposites into the frame.

(b) is a fossilised idiom - "sooner or later" - built from an older concessive form.
It cannot be extended or altered, and it reads as a set phrase rather than as
grammar. Where (a) sounds like a considered statement, (b) sounds proverbial.

</details>
