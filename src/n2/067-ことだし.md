# ～ことだし

**Meaning** · what with X (among other things); since X is the case - one reason of several, offered as grounds for a decision or a suggestion

`ことだし` names a circumstance and then proposes something on the strength of it.
Two features define it. The reason is presented as **one among several**, not the
whole story - which is why も so often appears inside the clause. And the second
half is a **decision, invitation or suggestion**, never a report of what happened.
It is the sentence you use to talk a group gently into leaving early.

## Formation

The clause takes plain, noun-modifying form, because <code>こと</code> is a noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ことだし | やんだ → やんだことだし |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことだし | {f|来|こ}ない → {f|来|こ}ないことだし |
| い-adjective | plain form + ことだし | {f|寒|さむ}い → {f|寒|さむ}いことだし |
| な-adjective | ～な + ことだし | {f|元気|げんき}な → {f|元気|げんき}なことだし |
| Noun | ～である + ことだし | {f|休|やす}み → {f|休|やす}みであることだし (uncommon) |
| Typical shape | ～も … + ことだし | {f|遅|おそ}い → {f|時間|じかん}も{f|遅|おそ}いことだし |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the noun row is stiff and rarely worth using. In speech people simply
say <code>{f|休|やす}みだし</code>. Verbs and adjectives carry almost every real
example of this pattern.

</div>

<pre>

The rain's stopped, among other things - let's get going.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|時間|じかん}も{f|遅|おそ}い<b>ことだし</b>、{f|今日|きょう}はこの{f|辺|へん}で{f|終|お}わりにしましょう。
It's getting late, so let's call it a day here.
</pre>

<pre>

Since we've all managed to get together, shall we take a photo?
</pre>

</pre>

<pre>

I'm back on my feet now, so I'll be returning to work from next week.
</pre>

</pre>

The manager knows about it, so I don't see any problem with going ahead on this plan.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ことだし` is spoken Japanese, and it is polite in a particular way: it makes a
proposal look like a natural consequence of facts everybody can see, rather than
something the speaker wants. That is genuinely useful. Suggesting the meeting end
with <code>{f|時間|じかん}も{f|遅|おそ}いことだし</code> is much easier to accept -
and much easier to refuse - than announcing that you would like to go home.

The も is doing real work. It signals "this reason, and others I'm not listing",
which is why the pattern feels considerate rather than pushy. It also suits
speakers who do not want to be seen deciding on their own: a chairperson, a host, a
parent proposing something to the family. There is a faintly mature, settled tone
to it; you hear it more from adults arranging things than from students making
plans.

The same softness has a shadow side, and it is worth naming. Because `ことだし`
implies unstated further reasons, it can be used to dress up a decision that was
already made and give it the appearance of following from circumstances. Piled up -
<code>{f|時間|じかん}も{f|遅|おそ}いことだし、{f|雨|あめ}も{f|降|ふ}っていることだし</code>
- it starts to sound like someone assembling justifications, and a
listener may reasonably suspect the conclusion came first. One `ことだし` per
suggestion is plenty.

Register is plain-to-polite; both
<code>{f|行|い}こう</code> and <code>{f|行|い}きましょう</code> follow it happily.
It is not used in formal writing, where <code>ため</code> or
<code>{f|以上|いじょう}</code> would appear instead.

## Contrast

### ～ことだし vs ～から・～ので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `から` and `ので` give *the* reason. `ことだし` gives *a* reason,
one of several, and only ever leads into a decision or a proposal.

</div>

<pre>
{f|寒|さむ}い<b>ので</b>、エアコンをつけました。
I put the air conditioning on because it was cold.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|寒|さむ}くなってきた<b>ことだし</b>、そろそろこたつを{f|出|だ}そうか。
It's turning cold - shall we get the kotatsu out?
</pre>

The first reports what the speaker did and why; nothing is being proposed. The
second invites agreement. Put a plain past-tense report after `ことだし` and the
sentence stalls, because the pattern is built to point forward at a choice.

### ～ことだし vs ～し

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `し` lists reasons casually and can stack.
`ことだし` picks one representative reason and presents it a little more
deliberately.

</div>

<pre>
{f|眠|ねむ}い<b>し</b>、{f|明日|あした}も{f|早|はや}いし、もう{f|寝|ね}るよ。
I'm sleepy, and I've an early start tomorrow, so I'm off to bed.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}も{f|早|はや}い<b>ことだし</b>、そろそろ{f|失礼|しつれい}いたします。
I've an early start tomorrow, so I'll take my leave.
</pre>

They mean much the same thing. `し` is the everyday, slightly breezy version and
is what you say to a friend; `ことだし` is a shade more composed and fits the moment
when you are excusing yourself from someone else's party. Note that `ことだし` can
be built on `し` - it is literally <code>こと</code> plus `だ` plus `し` - which is
why the two feel related.

### ～ことだし vs ～もので

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - mirror images. `ことだし` must be followed by a proposal or a
decision; `もので` must **not** be.

</div>

<pre>
{f|荷物|にもつ}も{f|届|とど}いた<b>ことだし</b>、{f|作業|さぎょう}を{f|始|はじ}めましょう。
The delivery's arrived, so let's get started.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|荷物|にもつ}が{f|届|とど}かなかった<b>もので</b>、{f|作業|さぎょう}が{f|始|はじ}められませんでした。
The delivery didn't arrive, so we weren't able to start.
</pre>

Both are socially smooth reason clauses, and both are spoken rather than written,
but they face opposite ways: `ことだし` opens a door, `もので` apologises for one
that stayed shut. Swapping them produces sentences that are not merely odd but
ungrammatical.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った<b>ことだし</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になりました。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った<b>ので</b>、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になりました。
  The second half reports what happened. `ことだし` needs a decision the speaker is
  making now.

- ❌ {f|忙|いそが}しかった<b>ことだし</b>、{f|返事|へんじ}が{f|遅|おく}れてすみません。 → ✅ {f|忙|いそが}しかった<b>もので</b>、{f|返事|へんじ}が{f|遅|おく}れてすみません。
  Apologising for something you failed to do is `もので` territory. `ことだし` does
  not excuse; it proposes.

- ❌ {f|元気|げんき}<b>ことだし</b>、{f|散歩|さんぽ}に{f|行|い}きましょう。 → ✅ {f|元気|げんき}<b>なことだし</b>、{f|散歩|さんぽ}に{f|行|い}きましょう。
  <code>こと</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective needs its な.

- ❌ {f|台風|たいふう}も{f|来|く}る<b>ことだし</b>、{f|明日|あした}は{f|休|やす}みになるでしょう。 → ✅ {f|台風|たいふう}も{f|来|く}る<b>ことだし</b>、{f|明日|あした}は{f|休|やす}みにしましょう。
  A guess about what will happen is not a decision. Turn it into one and the
  sentence works.

## Practice

**1.** みんな{f|集|あつ}まった____、そろそろ{f|始|はじ}めましょうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

みんな{f|集|あつ}まった<b>ことだし</b>

Everyone being present is one of the reasons to begin, and the second half is a
suggestion - the two halves `ことだし` requires. Adding も
(<code>みんなも{f|集|あつ}まったことだし</code>) makes the "among other things"
feeling explicit.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|寝坊|ねぼう}した（ことだし・もので）、{f|朝|あさ}ご{f|飯|はん}を{f|食|た}べる{f|時間|じかん}がありませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|寝坊|ねぼう}した<b>もので</b>、{f|朝|あさ}ご{f|飯|はん}を{f|食|た}べる{f|時間|じかん}がありませんでした。

This is an excuse for something that already went wrong, and the main clause is a
plain report. `ことだし` would need a proposal after it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The kids are asleep, so why don't we watch a film?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|子|こ}どもたちも{f|寝|ね}た<b>ことだし</b>、{f|映画|えいが}でも{f|見|み}ようか。

The children being asleep is the visible reason, も hints that there are others,
and <code>でも</code> plus the volitional keeps the suggestion light. This is
`ことだし` in its most typical setting.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|準備|じゅんび}も{f|終|お}わったことだし、{f|昨日|きのう}は{f|早|はや}く{f|帰|かえ}りました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|準備|じゅんび}も{f|終|お}わった<b>ことだし</b>、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|早|はや}く{f|帰|かえ}りましょう。

`ことだし` points forward to a decision, so a past-tense report of yesterday
cannot follow it. Either change the second half to a proposal, as here, or switch
the connective to <code>ので</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|暗|くら}くなってきた<b>ことだし</b>、そろそろ{f|帰|かえ}りましょう。
(b) {f|暗|くら}くなってきた<b>から</b>、そろそろ{f|帰|かえ}りましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the fading light as one of several good reasons and lets the
suggestion arise from the situation rather than from the speaker. It is the softer,
more considerate version, and easy for the others to agree with.

(b) states the reason directly and is perfectly polite, but the speaker is more
visibly the one deciding. Between friends there is nothing wrong with it; when you
are the guest, or when you would rather not look like you are hurrying everyone
along, (a) does the job more gracefully.

</details>
