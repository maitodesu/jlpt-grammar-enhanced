# ～に限る

**Meaning** · nothing beats ～; ～ is the best thing there is - and, in notices, "restricted to ～"

<code>{f|限|かぎ}る</code> means "to limit". <code>～に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> therefore says
*the good options here narrow down to exactly one*. In everyday speech that comes out
as a verdict - "for a cold, sleep, full stop." In signs and rules the same word stays
literal: the eligible people narrow down to exactly one group.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + に{f|限|かぎ}る | {f|温泉|おんせん} → {f|温泉|おんせん}に{f|限|かぎ}る |
| Verb | dictionary form + に{f|限|かぎ}る | {f|寝|ね}る → {f|寝|ね}るに{f|限|かぎ}る |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + に{f|限|かぎ}る | {f|無理|むり}をしない → {f|無理|むり}をしないに{f|限|かぎ}る |
| Polite | … + に{f|限|かぎ}ります | {f|休|やす}む → {f|休|やす}むに{f|限|かぎ}ります |
| Rule / notice sense | Noun + に{f|限|かぎ}る・に{f|限|かぎ}り | {f|会員|かいいん} → {f|会員|かいいん}に{f|限|かぎ}ります |

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>～に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> does not attach to adjectives. For
<code>{f|安|やす}い</code> or <code>{f|静|しず}かだ</code> you want
<code>～のが{f|一番|いちばん}だ</code> or <code>～に{f|越|こ}したことはない</code> instead.

</div>

## Nuance & register

The "nothing beats" use is warm, opinionated and thoroughly conversational. It is the
shape of a food recommendation, a piece of folk wisdom, an advertising slogan. Because
it announces a verdict rather than a fact, it belongs to **the speaker**: you are saying
*I have decided this is the best*. That is why it sits so comfortably with
<code>やっぱり</code> and with a final <code>ね</code> or <code>よ</code>, and why it is
odd for someone else's taste. To report a colleague's preference, say
<code>{f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|温泉|おんせん}が{f|一番|いちばん}{f|好|す}きらしい</code>, not
<code>{f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|温泉|おんせん}に{f|限|かぎ}る</code>.

<code>に{f|限|かぎ}ります</code> is the polite form and is perfectly natural in
conversation. It does not make the sentence formal; it just makes it polite.

The literal "restricted to" use lives in a different world - application forms, museum
signage, ticket terms - and there the shortened <code>に{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is the usual
shape: <code>{f|本日|ほんじつ}に{f|限|かぎ}り{f|半額|はんがく}</code>. Read out of
context, <code>{f|学生|がくせい}に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> is genuinely ambiguous between
"students only" and "nothing beats being a student". Register and surroundings settle it:
posted on a wall it is a rule, said over a beer it is a verdict.

There is no casual contraction, and nothing about the pattern is age- or gender-marked.

## Contrast

### に{f|限|かぎ}る vs に{f|越|こ}したことはない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> picks one option and dismisses the rest.
<code>に{f|越|こ}したことはない</code> says the option is *preferable, other things being
equal*, and leaves the alternatives standing.

</div>

<pre>
{f|暑|あつ}い{f|日|ひ}はやっぱり{f|冷|ひ}えたビール<b>に{f|限|かぎ}る</b>。
On a hot day it really has to be a cold beer.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|給料|きゅうりょう}は{f|高|たか}い<b>に{f|越|こ}したことはない</b>が、{f|休|やす}みも{f|大事|だいじ}だ。
A high salary is nice to have, but time off matters too.
</pre>

The second sentence expects a <code>が</code>. That is the giveaway:
<code>に{f|越|こ}したことはない</code> is a hedged preference and often comes with a
qualification attached, while <code>に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> is a closed verdict that would
sound strange if you immediately undercut it. Note also that
<code>に{f|越|こ}したことはない</code> attaches happily to
<code>{f|高|たか}い</code>; <code>に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> cannot.

### に{f|限|かぎ}る vs ～ほうがいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ほうがいい</code> is advice aimed at **a person in a
situation**. <code>に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> is a general verdict the speaker is issuing about
**how the world works**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}があるなら、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|病院|びょういん}へ<b>{f|行|い}ったほうがいい</b>。
If you've got a temperature you should go to the doctor today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}が{f|出|で}たら、{f|早|はや}めに{f|病院|びょういん}へ{f|行|い}く<b>に{f|限|かぎ}る</b>。
When you run a fever, the thing to do is get to a doctor early.
</pre>

The first is about you, now. The second is a maxim; it would fit a magazine column with
no listener in front of it. This is also why
<code>{f|行|い}ったほうがいい</code> can sound pushy and
<code>{f|行|い}くに{f|限|かぎ}る</code> cannot - the second is not telling anyone what to do.

### に{f|限|かぎ}る vs だけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だけ</code> **describes** how many there are.
<code>に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> **ranks or rules**: it either crowns a best option or lays
down a restriction.

</div>

<pre>
{f|入場|にゅうじょう}できるのは{f|小学生|しょうがくせい}<b>だけです</b>。
The only ones who can go in are primary-school children.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|入場|にゅうじょう}は{f|小学生|しょうがくせい}<b>に{f|限|かぎ}ります</b>。
Admission is restricted to primary-school children.
</pre>

Both are true of the same doorway, but the first reports a fact and the second is the
rule itself being stated - which is why the second is what goes on the sign and the
first is what you say to a friend who asks. And <code>だけ</code> never has the "nothing
beats" reading: <code>{f|夏|なつ}はビールだけだ</code> means you drink nothing else all
summer, which is a different and sadder claim.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|夏|なつ}はビール<b>が{f|限|かぎ}る</b>。 → ✅ {f|夏|なつ}はビール<b>に{f|限|かぎ}る</b>。
  The particle is fixed at <code>に</code>. Nothing else goes in that slot.

- ❌ {f|疲|つか}れたときは<b>{f|寝|ね}たに{f|限|かぎ}る</b>。 → ✅ {f|疲|つか}れたときは<b>{f|寝|ね}るに{f|限|かぎ}る</b>。
  The verb stays in the dictionary form. The verdict is timeless, so there is nothing
  for a past tense to do.

- ❌ この{f|店|みせ}は<b>{f|安|やす}いに{f|限|かぎ}る</b>。 → ✅ この{f|店|みせ}は<b>{f|安|やす}いのが{f|一番|いちばん}だ</b>。
  Adjectives do not attach. Nouns and verbs only.

- ❌ {f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|旅行|りょこう}なら{f|北海道|ほっかいどう}<b>に{f|限|かぎ}る</b>そうだ。 → ✅ {f|山田|やまだ}さんは{f|旅行|りょこう}なら{f|北海道|ほっかいどう}が{f|一番|いちばん}だと{f|言|い}っていた。
  The verdict is the speaker's own. Attributing it to a third party reads oddly, as
  though you were speaking on their behalf.

## Practice

**1.** {f|二日酔|ふつかよ}いの{f|朝|あさ}は、とにかく{f|水|みず}を____{f|限|かぎ}る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

とにかく{f|水|みず}を<b>{f|飲|の}むに</b>{f|限|かぎ}る。

A verb attaches in its dictionary form, followed by <code>に</code>. The sentence is a
piece of general wisdom about hangovers, which is exactly the job this pattern does.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|荷物|にもつ}は{f|軽|かる}い（に{f|限|かぎ}る・に{f|越|こ}したことはない）が、{f|必要|ひつよう}なものまで{f|置|お}いていくのは{f|困|こま}る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|荷物|にもつ}は{f|軽|かる}い<b>に{f|越|こ}したことはない</b>が…

Two reasons. <code>{f|軽|かる}い</code> is an い-adjective, and
<code>に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> does not take adjectives at all. And the
<code>が</code> clause immediately qualifies the preference, which is
<code>に{f|越|こ}したことはない</code> behaviour, not the closed verdict of
<code>に{f|限|かぎ}る</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When you're this tired, nothing beats a hot bath."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

こんなに{f|疲|つか}れているときは、{f|熱|あつ}いお{f|風呂|ふろ}<b>に{f|限|かぎ}る</b>。

A noun attaches directly. <code>{f|熱|あつ}い</code> here modifies
<code>お{f|風呂|ふろ}</code>, so the thing sitting in front of
<code>に{f|限|かぎ}る</code> is still a noun and the pattern is happy.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}みは{f|一人|ひとり}{f|一回|いっかい}が{f|限|かぎ}ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}みは{f|一人|ひとり}{f|一回|いっかい}<b>に{f|限|かぎ}ります</b>。

The restriction sense takes <code>に</code> just as the verdict sense does. This is the
notice use, so the shortened form also works in running text:
<code>{f|申|もう}し{f|込|こ}みは{f|一人|ひとり}{f|一回|いっかい}に{f|限|かぎ}り{f|受|う}け{f|付|つ}けます</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|部屋|へや}を{f|使|つか}えるのは{f|社員|しゃいん}だけです。
(b) この{f|部屋|へや}の{f|使用|しよう}は{f|社員|しゃいん}に{f|限|かぎ}ります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports the situation to whoever asked - the same information you would give a
visitor who wondered whether they could sit down. It is descriptive and neutral.

(b) states the rule. It is the wording of a notice on the door or a line in the office
handbook, and it carries the authority of whoever wrote it. Saying (b) casually to a
friend would sound as if you were quoting regulations at them.

</details>
