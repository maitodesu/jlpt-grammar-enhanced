# ～反面

**Meaning** · on the other hand; while - one thing is shown to have two opposite faces, usually a merit and the drawback that comes with it

<code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is literally "the opposite face". The sentence names a
quality of something and then names the quality on its other side, and the two belong
to the *same* thing: the same job, the same city, the same person. It is the sentence
you write when you are being fair about something.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
<code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is the one that *minds nothing at all*, and sits at the *neutral-to-written* end.
It is not conceding to an opponent; it is describing something honestly from both
sides.

## Formation

<code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + {f|反面|はんめん} | {f|減|へ}る → {f|減|へ}る{f|反面|はんめん} |
| Verb (past) | plain past + {f|反面|はんめん} | {f|簡単|かんたん}になった → {f|簡単|かんたん}になった{f|反面|はんめん} |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + {f|反面|はんめん} | {f|売|う}れない → {f|売|う}れない{f|反面|はんめん} |
| い-adjective | plain form + {f|反面|はんめん} | {f|軽|かる}い → {f|軽|かる}い{f|反面|はんめん} |
| な-adjective | な（or である）+ {f|反面|はんめん} | {f|便利|べんり} → {f|便利|べんり}な{f|反面|はんめん} |
| Noun | である + {f|反面|はんめん} | {f|自由|じゆう} → {f|自由|じゆう}である{f|反面|はんめん} |
| With で | … + {f|反面|はんめん}で | {f|強|つよ}い → {f|強|つよ}い{f|反面|はんめん}で |
| Written variant | {f|半面|はんめん} | same word, same meaning, different kanji |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - **one topic, two faces.** Both clauses must describe the same thing,
and the two descriptions must genuinely pull against each other - typically one good
and one bad.
❌<code>{f|兄|あに}は{f|背|せ}が{f|高|たか}い{f|反面|はんめん}、{f|弟|おとうと}は{f|低|ひく}い</code> compares two people,
and needs
<code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>{f|半面|はんめん}</code> with the "half" kanji is the same word and is
equally correct; newspapers use both. Nothing in the meaning changes, so pick one and
be consistent inside a document.

</div>

## Nuance & register

<code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is neutral-to-formal and thoroughly at home in writing: reviews, reports,
comparisons, essay paragraphs weighing pros and cons. It is perfectly usable in polite
speech - explaining a product to a customer, answering an interview question about
your own character - and it is only in very casual conversation that it sounds a
little written, where people say <code>けど</code> instead.

It carries no complaint. That is what separates it from most of this group: nobody's
expectations have been defeated, and nobody is being criticised. The two halves are
simply both true. Because of that even-handedness it is a useful softener - naming a
drawback after a merit sounds honest rather than negative, and naming a merit after a
drawback sounds generous.

The order matters. Whatever comes **second** is what the sentence leaves you with, so
<code>{f|安|やす}い{f|反面|はんめん}、{f|質|しつ}が{f|悪|わる}い</code> is a warning and
<code>{f|質|しつ}が{f|悪|わる}い{f|反面|はんめん}、{f|安|やす}い</code> is a
recommendation. Writers use that deliberately.

One limit to respect: the two faces have to belong to one thing. If your second clause
introduces a new subject, <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> will read as a mistake, and
<code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> is the pattern you want.

## Contrast

### {f|反面|はんめん} vs {f|一方|いっぽう}で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> needs **one topic with two sides**. <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code>
can also set **two different things** beside each other.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|薬|くすり}はよく{f|効|き}く<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|眠|ねむ}くなりやすい。
This medicine works well, but it tends to make you drowsy.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|都心|としん}の{f|人口|じんこう}は{f|増|ふ}えている。<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|地方|ちほう}では{f|減少|げんしょう}が{f|続|つづ}く。
The population of the city centre is rising. Meanwhile, the regions continue to lose people.
</pre>

The second sentence has two separate subjects and no shared thing to have two faces of,
so <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is impossible there. Where the topic *is* shared, both work, and <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code>
sounds tighter because it insists on the connection.

### {f|反面|はんめん} vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> reports two coexisting facts **without surprise**. `のに`
says the second fact **should not have happened**.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}い<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|待|ま}ち{f|時間|じかん}が{f|長|なが}い。
This place is cheap, but the wait is long.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}い<b>のに</b>、いつもすいている。
This place is cheap, and yet it's always empty.
</pre>

In the first, cheapness and queueing are two sides of the same bargain and nothing is
odd. In the second, the emptiness contradicts what cheapness leads you to expect, and
the speaker is puzzled. If nobody is surprised, <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is the honest choice.

### {f|反面|はんめん} vs に{f|反|はん}して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the kanji is shared and the jobs are not. <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> = **the other side
of the same thing**. <code>に{f|反|はん}して</code> = **contrary to an expectation, a
rule or a prediction**.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|車|くるま}は{f|力|ちから}が{f|強|つよ}い<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|燃費|ねんぴ}が{f|悪|わる}い。
This car is powerful, but it's thirsty.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|予想|よそう}に<b>{f|反|はん}して</b>、{f|燃費|ねんぴ}はかなりよかった。
Contrary to expectations, the fuel economy was rather good.
</pre>

<code>に{f|反|はん}して</code> takes a noun that names something to be defied -
expectation, prediction, the rules, someone's wishes. <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> takes a whole clause and
names no adversary at all. Learners who go by the kanji alone regularly swap them.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|便利|べんり}<b>だ{f|反面|はんめん}</b> → ✅ {f|便利|べんり}<b>な{f|反面|はんめん}</b>（or {f|便利|べんり}である{f|反面|はんめん}）
  <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective links with `な`.

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b> → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>である{f|反面|はんめん}</b>
  A bare noun cannot modify another noun. `である` supplies the link.

- ❌ {f|姉|あね}は{f|静|しず}かな<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|妹|いもうと}はよくしゃべる。 → ✅ {f|姉|あね}は{f|静|しず}かだ。<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|妹|いもうと}はよくしゃべる。
  Two different people, so there is no single thing with two faces.

- ❌ {f|今日|きょう}は{f|寒|さむ}い<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|風|かぜ}も{f|強|つよ}い。 → ✅ {f|今日|きょう}は{f|寒|さむ}い<b>{f|上|うえ}に</b>、{f|風|かぜ}も{f|強|つよ}い。
  Cold and windy are not opposites; they pile up on the same side. <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> needs the
  second clause to pull the other way.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|説明|せつめい}がくわしい____、{f|重|おも}くて{f|持|も}ち{f|運|はこ}びにくい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|説明|せつめい}がくわしい<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|重|おも}くて{f|持|も}ち{f|運|はこ}びにくい。

One dictionary, two opposing qualities - detail on one face, weight on the other. An
い-adjective attaches with no change.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|去年|きょねん}の{f|売上|うりあげ}は{f|好調|こうちょう}だった。（{f|反面|はんめん}・{f|一方|いっぽう}で）、{f|人件費|じんけんひ}も{f|大|おお}きく{f|増|ふ}えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|人件費|じんけんひ}も{f|大|おお}きく{f|増|ふ}えた。

The sentence has already ended, and <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> cannot open a new one - it needs a clause in
front of it inside the same sentence. Rewritten as one sentence,
<code>{f|売上|うりあげ}は{f|好調|こうちょう}だった{f|反面|はんめん}…</code> would be fine.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This town is quiet, but there is nowhere to shop after eight."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|町|まち}は{f|静|しず}かな<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|夜|よる}{f|八時|はちじ}を{f|過|す}ぎると{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}をする{f|店|みせ}がない。

Quiet and inconvenient are the two faces of the same small town, which is exactly the
relationship <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> expresses. <code>{f|静|しず}か</code> is a な-adjective, so it links
with `な`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|安全|あんぜん}だ{f|反面|はんめん}、{f|時間|じかん}がかかる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|安全|あんぜん}<b>な{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|時間|じかん}がかかる。

<code>{f|安全|あんぜん}</code> is a な-adjective and
<code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is a noun, so the link is `な`. In more formal writing
<code>{f|安全|あんぜん}である{f|反面|はんめん}</code> is also available.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|機能|きのう}は{f|便利|べんり}な<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|電池|でんち}を{f|消耗|しょうもう}する。
(b) この{f|機能|きのう}は{f|便利|べんり}な<b>のに</b>、あまり{f|使|つか}われていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an even-handed product description. Convenience and battery drain are the two
faces of one feature, and the speaker is neither surprised nor annoyed - they are
telling you what the trade-off is.

(b) expresses mild puzzlement or regret. The feature is good, so people ought to be
using it, and they are not. `のに` always carries that defeated expectation, which is
why it would be out of place in (a): nothing about a convenient feature makes battery
drain surprising.

</details>
