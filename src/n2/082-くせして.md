# ～くせして

**Meaning** · even though; and yet - the same jab as `くせに`, delivered more roughly

`くせして` is `くせに` with the `に` swapped for `して`, and the swap does exactly what
you would expect: it drags the phrase further into spoken Japanese and sharpens the
sneer. The grammar is identical - a gap between what somebody is and how they behave,
with the speaker holding it against them - but the tone is blunter, more personal and
markedly less printable.

On this batch's two axes - how much the speaker minds, and how formal the frame is - 
`くせして` occupies the far corner: *minds the most*, *least formal of all*. Put it
beside `とも` from earlier in this batch and you have the two extremes of the group in
one glance.

## Formation

Identical to `くせに`. <code>くせ</code> is the noun "habit, trait", so what precedes it
is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + くせして | {f|知|し}っている → {f|知|し}っているくせして |
| Verb (past) | plain past + くせして | {f|遅刻|ちこく}した → {f|遅刻|ちこく}したくせして |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + くせして | {f|泳|およ}げない → {f|泳|およ}げないくせして |
| い-adjective | plain form + くせして | {f|若|わか}い → {f|若|わか}いくせして |
| な-adjective | な + くせして | {f|下手|へた} → {f|下手|へた}なくせして |
| Noun | の + くせして | {f|新人|しんじん} → {f|新人|しんじん}のくせして |
| Sentence-final jab | …くせして。 | - → {f|分|わ}かってるくせして。 |
| Contracted in speech | くせして → くせしてさ | - → {f|知|し}らないくせしてさ |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two restrictions from `くせに` carry over unchanged. The clause
must be about a **person**, and **both halves must be about the same person**.
❌<code>{f|私|わたし}が{f|手伝|てつだ}ったくせして、{f|彼|かれ}はお{f|礼|れい}も{f|言|い}わない</code>
splits its attention across two people and needs `のに`.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>くせ</code> is a noun, which is why a noun in front of it takes `の`
and a な-adjective takes `な`. If you can build the `くせに` version, you can build this
one; nothing about the attachment changes.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|知|し}ってる<b>くせして</b>、とぼけないでよ。
You know perfectly well - don't play innocent.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもの<b>くせして</b>、{f|生意気|なまいき}なことを{f|言|い}うな。
You're a child - don't talk back like that.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|自分|じぶん}は{f|片付|かたづ}けない<b>くせして</b>、{f|人|ひと}にばかりうるさい。
He never tidies up himself, and yet he's forever on at everyone else.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|泳|およ}げない<b>くせして</b>、{f|深|ふか}いところまで{f|行|い}こうとする。
He can't even swim and he still tries to go out of his depth.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|本当|ほんとう}は{f|行|い}きたい<b>くせして</b>、{f|興味|きょうみ}ないふりをしている。
She's dying to go and she's putting on this act of not being interested.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新人|しんじん}の<b>くせして</b>、あいさつもろくにしない。
He's brand new here and he can't even manage a greeting.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|自分|じぶん}が{f|遅刻|ちこく}した<b>くせして</b>、{f|人|ひと}の{f|時間|じかん}にはうるさいんだから。

</pre>

<pre>

He'd argued so hard against it, and once it started he enjoyed it more than anybody.
</pre>

He's never cooked a thing in his life and yet he's got opinions about the seasoning.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`くせして` is casual spoken Japanese and belongs nowhere else. It does not appear in
essays, reports, business e-mail or polite conversation, and it does not take
ます-endings comfortably - a sentence that begins
<code>{f|新人|しんじん}のくせして</code> and ends
<code>…していません</code> pulls in two directions at once. In writing it turns up
almost exclusively as dialogue.

It is ruder than `くせに`, which is itself not polite. The difference is a matter of
degree rather than of kind: `くせに` covers everything from affectionate teasing to
real contempt, whereas `くせして` leans towards the harsh end and is heard as
name-calling more readily. Among close friends and within a family that harshness can
read as banter - <code>{f|下手|へた}なくせしてよく{f|言|い}うよ</code> between two
people who like each other is a joke. Said upwards at work, or about somebody who is
present and is not a friend, it is an insult.

There is a tendency for `くせして` to sound a shade more casual and more everyday-spoken
than `くせに`, and it turns up often in the speech of children and in television
dialogue. That is a tendency, not a rule; plenty of adults use it in ordinary
grumbling.

The sentence-final use is worth having.
<code>{f|分|わ}かってるくせして。</code> - trailing off, with nothing after it - is a
complete accusation, and it is one of the commonest ways the pattern actually occurs.

Because the whole point is disapproval, `くせして` cannot introduce praise. If you want
to say somebody is doing well for what they are, you need `にしては`, which is a
compliment; `くせして` would take the same facts and turn them into an insult.

## Contrast

### くせして vs くせに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same grammar, same restrictions. `くせに` is the standard form;
`くせして` is rougher, more spoken, and harder to soften.

</div>

<pre>
{f|新人|しんじん}の<b>くせに</b>、なかなか{f|度胸|どきょう}があるな。
For a newcomer, he's got some nerve about him.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新人|しんじん}の<b>くせして</b>、{f|口|くち}ばかり{f|達者|たっしゃ}だ。
He's the new boy and all he's got is a big mouth.
</pre>

The first can be said with a grin and half-admiration. The second resists that
reading: `くせして` presses the speaker's contempt into the sentence, so it needs a
warm delivery and a close relationship to come out as teasing. If you are unsure of
the relationship, `くせに` is the safer of the two - and `のに` is safer still.

### くせして vs のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` is about a **situation**; `くせして` is about a **person**,
and it is always a judgement on them.

</div>

<pre>
{f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}んだ<b>のに</b>、{f|熱|ねつ}が{f|下|さ}がらない。
I took the medicine and the fever still hasn't gone down.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}まない<b>くせして</b>、{f|治|なお}らないって{f|文句|もんく}ばかり{f|言|い}う。
He won't take the medicine and then complains that he isn't getting better.
</pre>

The first sentence has no target. Fevers cannot be blamed, so `くせして` is impossible
there. The second is aimed squarely at a person, and both halves are about that same
person - the two conditions this pattern always imposes.

### くせして vs にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both measure somebody against what they are. `にしては` is usually
**generous**; `くせして` is always **hostile**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|初|はじ}めて<b>にしては</b>、うまく{f|焼|や}けているよ。
For a first attempt, that's baked rather well.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|料理|りょうり}が{f|得意|とくい}な<b>くせして</b>、{f|人|ひと}には{f|作|つく}ってくれない。
She's a good cook and she still won't make anything for anybody.
</pre>

`にしては` allows the outcome to exceed the standard and is a compliment more often
than not. `くせして` insists the outcome falls short of what the person's position
demands, or that it contradicts it. Note that only `にしては` is usable with a
non-person: <code>{f|値段|ねだん}のわりに</code> and
<code>{f|値段|ねだん}にしては</code> are both fine, and
❌<code>{f|値段|ねだん}のくせして</code> is not standard.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>だくせして</b> → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}<b>のくせして</b>
  <code>くせ</code> is a noun, so a noun links to it with `の`. `だ` cannot modify a
  noun.

- ❌ {f|上手|じょうず}<b>くせして</b> → ✅ {f|上手|じょうず}<b>なくせして</b>
  な-adjectives keep their `な`. The link is the same as before any other noun.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}が{f|決|き}めた<b>くせして</b>、{f|私|わたし}が{f|叱|しか}られた。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}が{f|決|き}めた<b>のに</b>、{f|私|わたし}が{f|叱|しか}られた。
  Two different people. `くせして` judges one person across both clauses and cannot
  split its attention.

- ❌ {f|新人|しんじん}の<b>くせして</b>、よく{f|頑張|がんば}っています。 → ✅ {f|新人|しんじん}<b>にしては</b>、よく{f|頑張|がんば}っています。
  Praise and `くせして` do not mix, and neither do `くせして` and ます-form. If you mean
  it kindly, `にしては` is the pattern.

## Practice

**1.** {f|自分|じぶん}では{f|何|なに}もしない____、{f|人|ひと}の{f|失敗|しっぱい}にはうるさい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|自分|じぶん}では{f|何|なに}もしない<b>くせして</b>、{f|人|ひと}の{f|失敗|しっぱい}にはうるさい。

Both halves are about the same person, and the sentence is a criticism of that person,
which is exactly what `くせして` is for. The ない-form attaches with no change.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|課長|かちょう}にメールを{f|送|おく}るとき、「ご{f|多忙|たぼう}____、ご{f|対応|たいおう}ありがとうございました」と{f|書|か}く。(のくせして・にもかかわらず)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

「ご{f|多忙|たぼう}<b>にもかかわらず</b>、ご{f|対応|たいおう}ありがとうございました」

An e-mail to a superior is formal written Japanese, and `くせして` is a spoken insult.
`にもかかわらず` is the polite way to acknowledge that someone went out of their way.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casually and with an edge: "You're the one who forgot, and you're blaming me?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|忘|わす}れたのは{f|自分|じぶん}の<b>くせして</b>、{f|人|ひと}のせいにするの?

Both clauses are about the listener, and the speaker is plainly annoyed - the
conditions `くせして` needs. Note that this is genuinely sharp; say it only where you
would say the English.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}いくせして、{f|味|あじ}がいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|店|みせ}は{f|安|やす}い<b>のに</b>、{f|味|あじ}がいい。

Two problems. `くせして` wants a person as its subject, and it cannot be followed by
something complimentary. `のに` is neutral about both, and here it carries the pleasant
surprise the sentence intends.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|新人|しんじん}の<b>くせに</b>、なかなかやるじゃないか。
(b) {f|新人|しんじん}の<b>くせして</b>、なかなかやるじゃないか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is teasing praise. `くせに` is wide enough to be said with a grin, and the
<code>じゃないか</code> ending makes it warm - the speaker is impressed and pretending
not to be.

(b) says the same words with a rougher edge. Because `くせして` leans harder on the
contempt, the compliment has to fight the frame, and the sentence only reads as
friendly if the speaker's tone and the relationship carry it. Between people who are
not close, (b) risks sounding like a put-down dressed up as praise.

</details>
