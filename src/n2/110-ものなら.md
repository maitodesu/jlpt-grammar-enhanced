# ～ものなら

**Meaning** · if it were actually possible - which the speaker doubts

`ものなら` sits on a **potential** verb and turns it into a wish the speaker has already
half given up on: *if I could, I would.* The same shape, said to someone else, becomes
a dare - *go on then, do it if you can.* Either way the pattern quietly asserts that
the thing is out of reach.

## Formation

The verb in front of `ものなら` must be in potential form. That is the whole trick.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う-verb | potential ～える + ものなら | {f|戻|もど}る → {f|戻|もど}れるものなら |
| る-verb | potential ～られる + ものなら | やめる → やめられるものなら |
| する | できる + ものなら | {f|旅行|りょこう}する → {f|旅行|りょこう}できるものなら |
| {f|来|く}る | {f|来|こ}られる + ものなら | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}られるものなら |
| Casual contraction | ものなら → もんなら | {f|勝|か}てるもんなら |
| Dare, with the verb repeated | potential + ものなら + imperative | {f|止|と}められるものなら{f|止|と}めてみろ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the labels used throughout this book for what other
courses call {f|五段|ごだん}/Group 1 and {f|一段|いちだん}/Group 2. The second half of a `ものなら` sentence is
almost always <code>～たい</code>, <code>～たかった</code>, <code>～のに</code>, or an
imperative. A flat statement of fact does not fit.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
できる<b>ものなら</b>、もう{f|一度|いちど}やり{f|直|なお}したい。
If I could, I'd like to start over.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|戻|もど}れる<b>ものなら</b>、{f|高校時代|こうこうじだい}に{f|戻|もど}りたいなあ。
If it were possible to go back, I'd go back to my high school days.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|止|と}められる<b>ものなら</b>{f|止|と}めてみろ。
Stop me if you can.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|代|か}われる<b>ものなら</b>、{f|私|わたし}が{f|代|か}わってあげたかった。
If I could have taken her place, I would have.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|勝|か}てる<b>もんなら</b>{f|勝|か}ってみろよ。{f|口|くち}だけじゃないか。
Beat us then, if you can. You're all talk.
</pre>

<pre>
もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|会|あ}える<b>ものなら</b>、{f|祖母|そぼ}にちゃんとお{f|礼|れい}を{f|言|い}いたかった。
If I could have seen her once more, I'd have wanted to thank my grandmother properly.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|忙|いそが}しさから{f|逃|に}げられる<b>ものなら</b>、{f|明日|あした}にでも{f|田舎|いなか}に{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}したい。
If I could escape this workload, I'd move out to the countryside tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一日|いちにち}でも{f|早|はや}く{f|完成|かんせい}させられる<b>ものなら</b>そうしたいのですが、{f|人手|ひとで}がまったく{f|足|た}りません。
I'd finish it a day earlier if I possibly could, but we're badly short of hands.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The wishing use is emotional, and it is the reason this pattern turns up in songs,
letters and eulogies. It carries longing rather than planning: the speaker has already
concluded that the thing is impossible, and the sentence is a sigh. That is why
<code>もう{f|一度|いちど}</code>, <code>{f|今|いま}すぐ</code> and past-tense
<code>～たかった</code> are such frequent companions. Said about something that is in
fact perfectly easy, `ものなら` sounds theatrical.

The dare is a different animal. <code>{f|止|と}められるものなら{f|止|と}めてみろ</code> is
confrontational, and the `～てみろ` ending is blunt - it leans male and it leans angry.
Softened to <code>{f|止|と}められるものなら{f|止|と}めてみてください</code> it becomes a
sardonic challenge instead of a shouted one, which in an office is arguably worse. A
learner should be able to recognise this use without needing to produce it.

`もんなら` is the casual contraction and belongs to speech only. Note also that
<code>もの</code> here has drifted a long way from "thing": you cannot swap in
<code>こと</code>, and <code>できることなら</code>, which does exist, is a softer,
more matter-of-fact "if possible" with none of the resignation.

## Contrast

### ～ものなら vs ～（よ）うものなら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ものなら` takes the **potential** form and means "if I could".
`(よ)うものなら` takes the **volitional** and means "if I ever dared to".

</div>

<pre>
{f|文句|もんく}が{f|言|い}える<b>ものなら</b>、はっきり{f|言|い}ってやりたい。
If I could complain, I'd give them a piece of my mind.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|文句|もんく}を{f|言|い}お<b>うものなら</b>、あとが{f|大変|たいへん}なことになる。
If I so much as complained, there'd be hell to pay afterwards.
</pre>

Same verb, same `ものなら`, opposite sentences. The first wishes for an ability the
speaker lacks; the second imagines choosing to act and flinches at the fallout. Check
the form in front: <code>{f|言|い}える</code> is potential, <code>{f|言|い}おう</code>
is volitional.

### ～ものなら vs ～られたら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a plain potential + `たら` is a live hypothetical. `ものなら`
signals the speaker does **not** expect it to happen.

</div>

<pre>
{f|来週|らいしゅう}{f|休|やす}みが{f|取|と}れ<b>たら</b>、{f|温泉|おんせん}に{f|行|い}こうと{f|思|おも}っている。
If I can get time off next week, I'm thinking of going to a hot spring.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|休|やす}みが{f|取|と}れる<b>ものなら</b>、{f|一週間|いっしゅうかん}ぐらい{f|何|なに}もせずに{f|過|す}ごしたい。
If I could actually get time off, I'd like to spend a week doing nothing at all.
</pre>

The first speaker may well be booking a room. The second is daydreaming and knows it.
This is also why `ものなら` will not take a following plan - 「～ものなら、{f|行|い}きます」 -
since you cannot schedule something you have written off.

### ～ものなら vs ～ば…のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ば…のに` complains about how reality **is**. `ものなら` reaches
for something the speaker knows reality will not supply.

</div>

<pre>
もっと{f|時間|じかん}があ<b>れば</b>、ゆっくり{f|準備|じゅんび}できる<b>のに</b>。
If only there were more time, I could prepare properly.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|時間|じかん}を{f|止|と}められる<b>ものなら</b>、{f|止|と}めてしまいたい。
If I could stop time, I'd stop it dead.
</pre>

`ば…のに` points at a specific obstacle - the schedule - with a note of grievance.
`ものなら` does not blame anything; it names an impossibility and lets the wish stand.
The more fantastical the wish, the more `ものなら` is the only option.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|行|い}く<b>ものなら</b>、{f|今|いま}すぐ{f|行|い}きたい。 → ✅ {f|行|い}ける<b>ものなら</b>、{f|今|いま}すぐ{f|行|い}きたい。
  The verb has to be in potential form. A dictionary form in front of `ものなら`
  produces the volitional pattern's meaning by accident, or simply nothing.

- ❌ {f|来|く}る<b>ものなら</b>{f|来|き}てほしい。 → ✅ {f|来|こ}られる<b>ものなら</b>{f|来|き}てほしい。
  <code>{f|来|く}る</code> is irregular: the potential is <code>{f|来|こ}られる</code>,
  built on the こ- stem, while the て-form is <code>{f|来|き}て</code>.

- ❌ できる<b>ものなら</b>、{f|明日|あした}{f|手伝|てつだ}います。 → ✅ できる<b>ものなら</b>、{f|明日|あした}{f|手伝|てつだ}いたいのですが。
  A firm commitment contradicts the pattern. `ものなら` needs a wish, a regret or a
  challenge at the end.

- ❌ {f|勝|か}てる<b>ものなら</b>{f|勝|か}ちます。 → ✅ {f|勝|か}てる<b>ものなら</b>{f|勝|か}ってみろ。
  In the dare use the same verb comes back as an imperative or `～てみろ`. Without
  that shape the sentence loses the challenge entirely.

## Practice

**1.** {f|忘|わす}れられる____、あの{f|日|ひ}のことは{f|全部|ぜんぶ}{f|忘|わす}れてしまいたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|忘|わす}れられる<b>ものなら</b>

The verb is already in potential form and the sentence ends in <code>～たい</code>, so
`ものなら` fits exactly: the speaker wants to forget and knows perfectly well that they
cannot.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|社長|しゃちょう}に{f|意見|いけん}を（{f|言|い}えるものなら・{f|言|い}おうものなら）、その{f|場|ば}で{f|怒鳴|どな}られるだろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|意見|いけん}を<b>{f|言|い}おうものなら</b>、その{f|場|ば}で{f|怒鳴|どな}られるだろう。

The consequence is a disaster that follows from *choosing* to speak, so the volitional
`(よ)うものなら` is needed. The potential version would have to end in a wish instead.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If I could swap places with him, I'd take the exam myself."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}と{f|代|か}われる<b>ものなら</b>、{f|私|わたし}が{f|試験|しけん}を{f|受|う}けたい。

<code>{f|代|か}わる</code> becomes the potential <code>{f|代|か}われる</code>, and the
closing <code>～たい</code> supplies the wish that `ものなら` requires.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|逃|に}げるものなら{f|逃|に}げてみろ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|逃|に}げ<b>られる</b>ものなら{f|逃|に}げてみろ。

The dare use still needs the potential form in the first half - the challenge is
precisely about whether the other person *can*. The imperative half stays as it is.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|留学|りゅうがく}でき<b>たら</b>、アメリカに{f|行|い}きたい。
(b) {f|留学|りゅうがく}できる<b>ものなら</b>、アメリカに{f|行|い}きたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an ordinary hypothetical: study abroad is on the table, and the speaker is
saying where they would choose to go. It could be part of a real plan.

(b) adds resignation. The speaker doubts the chance will ever come - money, family,
work stand in the way - so the sentence reads as a wistful "if only I could".

</details>
