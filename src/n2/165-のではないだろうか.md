# ～のではないだろうか

**Meaning** · isn't it the case that ～?; I would suggest that ～ - the writer states a view and dresses it as a question so the reader is invited rather than told

Take it apart and it is four familiar pieces: `の` nominalises the clause, `ではない`
negates it, `だろう` guesses, `か` questions. Put together they do something none of them
does alone. **The speaker is not in doubt.** A negative question in Japanese, as in
English, is a way of asserting politely - "isn't this a bit expensive?" means you think it
is - and this pattern is the standard machinery for advancing a claim while leaving the
other person room to disagree. On the confidence ladder it sits close to
<code>と{f|思|おも}う</code>, well above `かもしれない`, and it is nowhere near as tentative
as it looks.

## Formation

`の` is a nominaliser, so the clause in front of it is in noun-modifying shape. Nouns and
な-adjectives need `な` before the `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のではないだろうか | {f|違|ちが}う → {f|違|ちが}うのではないだろうか |
| Verb (past) | plain past + のではないだろうか | {f|間違|まちが}えた → {f|間違|まちが}えたのではないだろうか |
| い-adjective | plain form + のではないだろうか | {f|早|はや}い → {f|早|はや}いのではないだろうか |
| な-adjective | ～な + のではないだろうか | {f|無理|むり}だ → {f|無理|むり}なのではないだろうか |
| Noun | ～な + のではないだろうか | {f|誤解|ごかい} → {f|誤解|ごかい}なのではないだろうか |
| Polite (speech, business) | … + のではないでしょうか | {f|高|たか}い → {f|高|たか}いのではないでしょうか |
| Casual speech | … + んじゃない / んじゃないかな | {f|高|たか}い → {f|高|たか}いんじゃない |
| Literary | … + のではあるまいか | {f|高|たか}い → {f|高|たか}いのではあるまいか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - in speech the `の` contracts to `ん` almost without exception:
<code>のではないでしょうか</code> becomes
<code>んじゃないでしょうか</code>, and in relaxed conversation
<code>んじゃない?</code>. Written out in full, the pattern belongs to the page.

</div>

## Nuance & register

This is **the academic hedge**, and it is worth being clear-eyed about what it does. A
writer who ends a paragraph with <code>のではないだろうか</code> has just made a claim.
The negative question does not soften the claim's content at all; it softens the writer's
posture. The reader is left with a formal opportunity to disagree, and the argument moves
on as though the point were made. Japanese academic prose, editorials and thoughtful
essays run on this, and reading them requires taking the hedged sentences as the author's
actual position.

The family is a register ladder, and every rung says the same thing:

- <code>んじゃない?</code> - casual speech, friends
- <code>んじゃないですか</code> - polite conversation
- <code>のではないでしょうか</code> - business, presentations, careful speech
- <code>のではないだろうか</code> - writing: essays, papers, columns
- <code>のではあるまいか</code> - literary, and not for production

**What a learner should actually use in speech is
<code>のではないでしょうか</code>, or its contracted
<code>んじゃないでしょうか</code>.** It is one of the most valuable sentence endings in
business Japanese, because it lets you disagree with someone senior without contradicting
them: <code>{f|少|すこ}し{f|早|はや}すぎるのではないでしょうか</code> raises the objection
and simultaneously hands them the decision. Saying
<code>{f|早|はや}すぎると{f|思|おも}います</code> in the same meeting is not rude, but it
puts your judgement against theirs.

Two cautions. First, **overuse**. A paper in which every sentence hedges this way reads as
evasive, and Japanese style guides say so; use it at the points where you are actually
asking the reader to come with you. Second, **intonation**. Spoken with a rising tone it
can be heard as a genuine question; falling, it is the assertion described above. If you
really want to know something, ask directly.

## Contrast

### ～のではないだろうか vs ～と{f|思|おも}う

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both express the speaker's view.
<code>と{f|思|おも}う</code> puts the speaker's name on it;
<code>のではないだろうか</code> leaves the view on the table and steps back.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|案|あん}には{f|無理|むり}がある<b>と{f|思|おも}います</b>。
I think there's something unworkable about this proposal.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|案|あん}には{f|無理|むり}がある<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。
Isn't there something unworkable about this proposal?
</pre>

The content is identical. What differs is who is exposed. The first is a position you can
be argued with about; the second is an observation the room can take up or let drop.
Japanese meetings lean heavily on the second, and learners who use only the first can come
across as more combative than they intend - a tendency, not a rule, but a reliable one.

### ～のではないだろうか vs ～だろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だろう</code> is a **guess** the speaker owns.
<code>のではないだろうか</code> is a **judgement** the speaker offers.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}になる<b>だろう</b>。
It'll probably rain tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|判断|はんだん}を{f|急|いそ}ぎすぎた<b>のではないだろうか</b>。
Was the decision not made too hastily?
</pre>

Weather takes `だろう`, because there is nothing to argue about and nobody to persuade.
The second sentence is an argument: it invites the reader to look again at something that
has already happened and to reach the speaker's conclusion. That is why
<code>だろう</code> is at home in forecasts and
<code>のではないだろうか</code> is at home in criticism.

### ～のではないだろうか vs ～のではないか・んじゃない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same pattern at three volumes. With `だろう` it muses; without
`だろう` it presses; contracted to `んじゃない` it is casual speech.

</div>

<pre>
それは{f|話|はなし}が{f|違|ちが}う<b>のではないか</b>。
Surely that is not what was agreed.
</pre>

<pre>
それ、{f|話|はなし}が{f|違|ちが}う<b>んじゃない</b>?
Hang on - that's not what we said, is it?
</pre>

<code>のではないか</code> without `だろう` is noticeably firmer, and in speech it can sound
like a challenge; it is the shape a superior uses when questioning a subordinate's work,
and it turns up in written argument where the author wants an edge. Inserting `だろう` puts
the writer back into the position of thinking aloud, which is usually the safer place to
stand.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この{f|条件|じょうけん}では{f|無理|むり}<b>だのではないだろうか</b>。 → ✅ この{f|条件|じょうけん}では{f|無理|むり}<b>なのではないだろうか</b>。
  `の` is a nominaliser, so what precedes it must be in noun-modifying shape. な-adjectives
  and nouns take `な`, never `だ`.

- ❌ ねえ、この{f|店|みせ}、{f|高|たか}い<b>のではないだろうか</b>。 → ✅ ねえ、この{f|店|みせ}、{f|高|たか}い<b>んじゃない</b>?
  Nothing is wrong with the grammar; it is the wrong rung of the ladder. Written-register
  Japanese aimed at a friend in a shop sounds like a lecture.

- ❌ {f|今|いま}、3{f|時|じ}なの<b>ではないでしょうか</b>。（with a clock on the wall） → ✅ {f|今|いま}、3{f|時|じ}です。
  The pattern is for judgements that could be disputed. Hedging a fact anyone can check
  makes the speaker sound oddly unsure of the room.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|会|かい}には{f|出|で}ない<b>のではないだろうか</b>。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}は{f|来週|らいしゅう}の{f|会|かい}には{f|出|で}ない<b>つもりです</b>。
  Your own intentions are not something you reason your way to. Guess about other people's
  situations, not about your own decisions.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|数字|すうじ}の{f|急|きゅう}な{f|変化|へんか}には、{f|何|なに}か{f|理由|りゆう}がある____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|何|なに}か{f|理由|りゆう}がある<b>のではないだろうか</b>（or のではないでしょうか）。

The writer has spotted something in the data and is proposing an explanation while leaving
the reader room to weigh it - the pattern's core job. The verb ある attaches in plain form
with no `な`.

</details>

**2.** Choose, speaking to a section head in a meeting: この{f|計画|けいかく}は{f|予算|よさん}が{f|足|た}りない（と{f|思|おも}います・のではないでしょうか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|予算|よさん}が{f|足|た}りない<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。

Both are polite Japanese, but the second raises the concern without setting your judgement
against your section head's. It is the standard way to disagree upward, and it leaves the
decision visibly with them.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in written register: "Is the real problem not that nobody checked the figures?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|本当|ほんとう}の{f|問題|もんだい}は、{f|誰|だれ}も{f|数字|すうじ}を{f|確|たし}かめなかったこと<b>なのではないだろうか</b>。

The predicate here is the noun phrase <code>{f|確|たし}かめなかったこと</code>, so it needs
`な` before the `の`. English "is it not that …" is this pattern almost exactly.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|説明|せつめい}は{f|少|すこ}し{f|不十分|ふじゅうぶん}だのではないだろうか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|説明|せつめい}は{f|少|すこ}し{f|不十分|ふじゅうぶん}<b>なのではないだろうか</b>。

{f|不十分|ふじゅうぶん} is a な-adjective, and the nominaliser `の` requires the
noun-modifying form. The `だ` cannot modify a noun, and `の` is functioning as one here.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|効果|こうか}がない<b>かもしれません</b>。
(b) この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|効果|こうか}がない<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) genuinely leaves the question open. The speaker is flagging a possibility they have not
made their mind up about, and would be unsurprised to be shown that the method works
perfectly well.

(b) is a view, politely packaged. The speaker thinks the method does not work and is saying
so in a shape that lets the listener push back without anyone losing face. Read as a
neutral "maybe", (b) is routinely underestimated by learners - and by anyone who takes the
question mark at face value.

</details>
