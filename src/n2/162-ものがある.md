# ～ものがある

**Meaning** · there is something ～ about it - the speaker names a quality they can feel in something but could not fully spell out

The `もの` here is deliberately vague: *something*. The pattern picks out a quality the
speaker has genuinely registered - moving, remarkable, unbearable, faintly sad - and
locates it **in the thing itself** rather than in the speaker's mood. That is what makes
it a judgement rather than a description. It is not a guess about facts, so it does not
compete with `かもしれない` and its relatives; it is the sentence you reach for once you
have stopped guessing and started evaluating.

## Formation

`もの` is a noun, so what precedes it is in noun-modifying shape. The frame is very often
<code>Noun + には … ものがある</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ものがある | {f|心|こころ}を{f|打|う}つ → {f|心|こころ}を{f|打|う}つものがある |
| Verb (passive of feeling) | ～させられる + ものがある | {f|考|かんが}えさせられる → {f|考|かんが}えさせられるものがある |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ものがある | {f|捨|す}てられない → {f|捨|す}てられないものがある |
| い-adjective | plain form + ものがある | {f|寂|さび}しい → {f|寂|さび}しいものがある |
| な-adjective | ～な + ものがある | {f|異常|いじょう}だ → {f|異常|いじょう}なものがある |
| Polite | … + ものがあります | {f|厳|きび}しい → {f|厳|きび}しいものがあります |
| Past impression | … + ものがあった | {f|光|ひか}る → {f|光|ひか}るものがあった |
| Noun | not available | ❌ {f|才能|さいのう}ものがある |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern needs a predicate that **evaluates**. Verbs of effect
（{f|心|こころ}を{f|打|う}つ, {f|人|ひと}を{f|引|ひ}きつける,
{f|考|かんが}えさせられる, {f|目|め}を{f|見|み}{f|張|は}る）and adjectives of impression
（{f|厳|きび}しい, {f|寂|さび}しい, {f|見事|みごと}な）are its natural partners. A plain
physical fact - <code>{f|狭|せま}い</code>, <code>{f|重|おも}い</code> - produces a
sentence that means nothing.

</div>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>
この{f|絵|え}には{f|人|ひと}を{f|引|ひ}きつける<b>ものがある</b>。
There is something about this painting that draws you in.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}の{f|努力|どりょく}には{f|目|め}を{f|見|み}{f|張|は}る<b>ものがある</b>。
The effort she has put in is genuinely remarkable.
</pre>

<pre>
その{f|一言|ひとこと}には{f|考|かんが}えさせられる<b>ものがありました</b>。

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

Written and considered rather than chatty. It belongs to reviews, essays, commentary,
speeches, sports reporting and the kind of conversation where somebody is summing
something up. Nobody says it about lunch. The polite `ものがあります` is common in
presentations and formal remarks, and the past `ものがあった` is how a writer reports an
impression that has stayed with them.

The pattern is usually - not always - **appreciative**. Its most frequent partners are
compliments: <code>{f|目|め}を{f|見|み}{f|張|は}るものがある</code> for astonishing
progress, <code>{f|光|ひか}るものがある</code> for raw talent,
<code>{f|心|こころ}を{f|打|う}つものがある</code> for something moving. It also handles
weight and difficulty well - <code>{f|厳|きび}しいものがある</code>,
<code>{f|耐|た}えがたいものがある</code> - where it lends a measured, adult tone to a
complaint.

What it will not do is report your own condition. The quality has to be **in the object**,
observed from outside; <code>{f|今日|きょう}は{f|疲|つか}れたものがある</code> is not
Japanese. Nor does it take a bare noun: the slot in front of `もの` is a modifying clause,
so a noun has to be turned into something like
<code>{f|天才|てんさい}{f|的|てき}な</code> first.

There is a real vagueness in the meaning, and it is deliberate. Choosing this pattern
signals that the speaker is pointing at something they can sense but not itemise - which
is precisely why it sounds thoughtful, and why over-using it sounds evasive.

## Contrast

### ～ものがある vs ～ものだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ものだ` states **how things are in general**.
`ものがある` picks out a quality **in one particular thing**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|時間|じかん}というのは{f|早|はや}く{f|過|す}ぎる<b>ものだ</b>。
Time does go by quickly, doesn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|久|ひさ}しぶりに{f|母校|ぼこう}を{f|訪|たず}ねると、なつかしい<b>ものがある</b>。
Visiting my old school after so long, there is something quite nostalgic about it.
</pre>

`ものだ` generalises: it says this is the nature of time, or of people, or of youth.
`ものがある` refuses to generalise - it is anchored to this school, this painting, this
performance. If you could put <code>{f|人間|にんげん}は</code> or
<code>というのは</code> at the front of the sentence, you want `ものだ`.

### ～ものがある vs ～ところがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ところがある</code> names a **definable trait**, usually of a
person. `ものがある` names an **indefinable quality**, usually of a work, a scene or a
performance.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}には{f|人|ひと}の{f|話|はなし}を{f|聞|き}かない<b>ところがある</b>。
He has a habit of not listening to people.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}の{f|文章|ぶんしょう}には{f|人|ひと}を{f|引|ひ}き{f|込|こ}む<b>ものがある</b>。
There is something about his writing that pulls you in.
</pre>

<code>ところ</code> is a place, so it identifies a spot in someone's character you could
point at - and because you can point at it, the pattern is often mildly critical.
`もの` is the opposite: the whole appeal of the sentence is that the speaker cannot point
at it. Describing a person's flaw with `ものがある` sounds grandly vague; describing an
artwork's magic with <code>ところがある</code> makes it sound like a checklist item.

### ～ものがある vs the literal もの

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - if the sentence could be answered with **"which one?"**, `もの` is an
ordinary noun meaning *thing*. The pattern cannot be pointed at.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}には{f|安|やす}い<b>ものがある</b>。
This shop has some cheap items.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}の{f|品|しな}ぞろえには{f|見事|みごと}な<b>ものがある</b>。
There is something quite admirable about this shop's range.
</pre>

The first sentence has real objects in it; you could pick one up. The second has nothing
you could pick up, only a quality. In practice the evaluative predicate does most of the
disambiguating - <code>{f|安|やす}い</code> describes goods,
<code>{f|見事|みごと}な</code> describes an impression - and so does the
<code>には</code> frame, which sets up an *aspect of* rather than a *contents of*.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|親切|しんせつ}<b>ものがある</b>。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}には{f|親切|しんせつ}な<b>ところがある</b>。
  Two repairs. A な-adjective needs its `な`, and a nameable trait in a person belongs to
  <code>ところがある</code>.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}の{f|部屋|へや}は{f|狭|せま}い<b>ものがある</b>。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}の{f|部屋|へや}は{f|狭|せま}い。
  A measurable physical fact has no hidden quality to point at. The pattern needs a
  predicate that evaluates.

- ❌ {f|今日|きょう}は{f|疲|つか}れた<b>ものがある</b>。 → ✅ {f|今日|きょう}はかなり{f|疲|つか}れた。
  Your own tiredness is a state, not a quality perceived in something outside you.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}の{f|才能|さいのう}には{f|天才|てんさい}<b>ものがある</b>。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}の{f|才能|さいのう}には{f|天才|てんさい}{f|的|てき}な<b>ものがある</b>。
  A bare noun cannot sit in front of `もの`. Make it a modifier first.

## Practice

**1.** {f|彼|かれ}のスピーチには、{f|聞|き}く{f|人|ひと}を{f|動|うご}かす____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|聞|き}く{f|人|ひと}を{f|動|うご}かす<b>ものがある</b>（or ものがあります）。

<code>{f|動|うご}かす</code> is a verb of effect, exactly the kind this pattern wants, and
the <code>には</code> frame sets up "there is something about his speech that …". The verb
attaches in plain form.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|田中|たなか}さんには{f|時々|ときどき}{f|冷|つめ}たい（ものがある・ところがある）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|時々|ときどき}{f|冷|つめ}たい<b>ところがある</b>。

A recurring streak in a person's character is a nameable trait, and
<code>ところ</code> is the pattern for that. `ものがある` would suggest some ineffable
quality of coldness radiating from him, which is far grander than the sentence intends.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "There is something about the ending of that film that stays with you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

あの{f|映画|えいが}の{f|終|お}わり{f|方|かた}には、{f|心|こころ}に{f|残|のこ}る<b>ものがある</b>。

English "there is something about X that …" maps almost word for word onto the
<code>Xには … ものがある</code> frame, and <code>{f|心|こころ}に{f|残|のこ}る</code> supplies
the evaluative verb.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|工場|こうじょう}の{f|技術|ぎじゅつ}は{f|見事|みごと}ものがあります。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|工場|こうじょう}の{f|技術|ぎじゅつ}<b>には{f|見事|みごと}なものがあります</b>。

{f|見事|みごと} is a な-adjective and must keep its `な` before the noun `もの`. Changing
<code>は</code> to <code>には</code> is not strictly required, but it is the frame the
pattern normally appears in and it makes the sentence read naturally.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|作品|さくひん}は{f|素晴|すば}らしいです。
(b) この{f|作品|さくひん}には{f|素晴|すば}らしい<b>ものがあります</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a straight verdict on the whole work: it is excellent, and the speaker is willing
to say so plainly.

(b) is narrower and more careful. It says that somewhere in the work there is something of
real quality - which leaves open that the rest may be uneven, and which suggests the
speaker has looked closely enough to find it. In a review, (b) is the sentence you write
about a promising newcomer and (a) the one you write about a masterpiece.

</details>
