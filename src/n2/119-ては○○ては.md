# ～ては～ては

**Meaning** · doing A, then B, then A again, then B again - a cycle that keeps turning

The same `ては` as the rest of this family, but doubled. Instead of setting a condition,
the repetition paints a picture: two actions alternating, over and over, until the
shape of the sentence *is* the repetition being described. Waves that come in and go
out. A writer who writes and deletes and writes and deletes.

## Formation

Two verbs form a pair, and the pair is stated twice.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb pair | V1て + は + V2 (ます-stem), repeated | {f|書|か}く・{f|消|け}す → {f|書|か}いては{f|消|け}し、{f|書|か}いては{f|消|け}し |
| Verb (て-form in で) | ～で + は | {f|読|よ}む → {f|読|よ}んでは{f|置|お}き |
| Closing the cycle | the last verb takes a finite form | ～ては～した / ～ては～している |
| As a noun phrase | ～ては～の + noun | ～ては～の{f|繰|く}り{f|返|かえ}し / ～ては～の{f|毎日|まいにち} |
| Single descriptive pair | Aては B | {f|波|なみ}が{f|寄|よ}せては{f|返|かえ}す |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb in the middle of the cycle appears in its ます-stem -
<code>{f|消|け}し</code>, <code>{f|寝|ね}</code>, <code>{f|置|お}き</code> - not its
dictionary form. That connective form is what lets one clause run into the next and
gives the pattern its rhythm.

</div>

## Nuance & register

This is a describing pattern rather than an arguing one. It belongs to narrative -
novels, essays, reminiscence, nature writing - and it works by rhythm. Read one aloud
and you can hear why the middle verb is in the ます-stem: the clauses run into each
other without ever coming to a stop, which is the point.

The feeling it carries is usually one of two. Either the cycle is futile or wearying -
learning and forgetting, writing and deleting, eating and sleeping - or it is calm and
natural, as with waves and weather. It very rarely sounds cheerful. Applied to the
speaker's own life, as in <code>{f|食|た}べては{f|寝|ね}の{f|毎日|まいにち}</code>, the tone is
self-deprecating.

In conversation people reach for `～たり～たり` far more often, and this pattern in
speech sounds a little literary - which is sometimes exactly the effect wanted. The
noun-phrase version, <code>～ては～の{f|繰|く}り{f|返|かえ}し</code> and
<code>～ては～の{f|毎日|まいにち}</code>, is the shape that does turn up in ordinary talk,
and it is the most useful one for a learner to be able to produce.

One structural point: the two verbs should form a genuine pair, usually opposites or a
natural sequence - write and erase, open and close, stand and sit, come and go. Two
unrelated actions do not make a cycle, and the sentence will simply read as broken.

## Contrast

### ～ては～ては vs ～たり～たり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `たり～たり` lists a **sample** of things done, in no particular
order. `ては～ては` describes a **cycle** in a fixed order, repeating.

</div>

<pre>
{f|休日|きゅうじつ}は{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}ん<b>だり</b>{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}<b>たり</b>しています。
On my days off I read, watch films, that sort of thing.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|同|おな}じページを<b>{f|読|よ}んでは{f|戻|もど}り、{f|読|よ}んでは{f|戻|もど}り</b>して、なかなか{f|先|さき}へ{f|進|すす}まない。
I read the same page, go back, read it, go back - I'm getting nowhere.
</pre>

Swap them and the sentences change shape entirely. The `たり` version says nothing about
order or repetition; the `ては` version is *about* order and repetition and would be
pointless without them.

### ～ては～ては vs the conditional ～ては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one `ては` followed by a bad outcome is the conditional. Two `ては`
with a matching pair of verbs is the repetition pattern.

</div>

<pre>
{f|途中|とちゅう}で{f|投|な}げ{f|出|だ}し<b>ては</b>、{f|何|なに}も{f|身|み}につかない。
If you keep abandoning things halfway, nothing will ever stick.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新|あたら}しい{f|趣味|しゅみ}を<b>{f|始|はじ}めては{f|飽|あ}き、{f|始|はじ}めては{f|飽|あ}き</b>で、{f|何|なに}も{f|続|つづ}かない。
Taking up a new hobby, getting bored, taking up another, getting bored - nothing lasts.
</pre>

The first delivers a verdict; the second describes a habit. They are close in feeling,
because both are usually a little disapproving, but only the first one is a conditional.
The test is whether a second `ては` clause is coming.

### ～ては～ては vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ながら` means the two actions run **at the same time**.
`ては～ては` means they take **turns**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}き<b>ながら</b>{f|料理|りょうり}をするのが{f|好|す}きです。
I like to cook with music on.
</pre>

<pre>
レシピを<b>{f|見|み}ては{f|手|て}を{f|止|と}め、{f|見|み}ては{f|手|て}を{f|止|と}め</b>、{f|料理|りょうり}に{f|一時間|いちじかん}もかかってしまった。
Checking the recipe, stopping, checking again, stopping again - the cooking took a whole hour.
</pre>

With `ながら` the hands never leave the pan. With `ては～ては` they do, repeatedly, and
that interruption is the whole content of the sentence. If the two actions could
genuinely be done together, this pattern is the wrong choice.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|書|か}く<b>ては</b>{f|消|け}す。 → ✅ {f|書|か}い<b>ては</b>{f|消|け}し。
  The first verb must be in て-form. A dictionary form in front of `は` is not a form
  Japanese has.

- ❌ {f|覚|おぼ}えては{f|忘|わす}れた。（meaning "over and over"） → ✅ {f|覚|おぼ}えては{f|忘|わす}れ、{f|覚|おぼ}えては{f|忘|わす}れた。
  One turn of the wheel is not a cycle. The pair has to be stated twice for the
  repetition to be there.

- ❌ {f|朝|あさ}ご{f|飯|はん}を{f|食|た}べ<b>ては</b>{f|出|で}かけた。 → ✅ {f|朝|あさ}ご{f|飯|はん}を{f|食|た}べ<b>てから</b>{f|出|で}かけた。
  A single sequence of two events takes `てから`. `ては` would claim the speaker ate and
  left again and again.

- ❌ {f|押|お}しては{f|引|ひ}<b>く</b>、{f|押|お}しては{f|引|ひ}<b>く</b>した。 → ✅ {f|押|お}しては{f|引|ひ}<b>き</b>、{f|押|お}しては{f|引|ひ}<b>いた</b>。
  The verb in the middle of the cycle goes into the ます-stem, and only the final one
  takes a finished form.

## Practice

**1.** {f|母|はは}はスーツケースに{f|服|ふく}を{f|詰|つ}め____{f|出|だ}し、{f|詰|つ}めては{f|出|だ}しして、なかなか{f|決|き}められないようだった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|詰|つ}め<b>ては</b>{f|出|だ}し

The て-form plus `は`, with the second verb in its ます-stem, sets up the alternation
that the repeated phrase then completes.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|昨日|きのう}の{f|夜|よる}は、{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}（たり・ては）{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}い（たり・ては）していた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}<b>たり</b>{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}い<b>たり</b>していた。

Watching a film and listening to music are two examples of how the evening was spent,
not a cycle turning over and over, so `たり～たり` is what the sentence needs.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He kept standing up and sitting down again, unable to calm himself."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}は<b>{f|立|た}っては{f|座|すわ}り、{f|立|た}っては{f|座|すわ}り</b>して、どうしても{f|落|お}ち{f|着|つ}けないようだった。

Standing and sitting are natural opposites, which is what makes them a usable pair, and
stating the pair twice is what conveys "kept".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|手紙|てがみ}を{f|書|か}いては{f|破|やぶ}る、{f|書|か}いては{f|破|やぶ}るした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|手紙|てがみ}を{f|書|か}いては<b>{f|破|やぶ}り</b>、{f|書|か}いては{f|破|やぶ}<b>った</b>。

The middle verb takes the ます-stem <code>{f|破|やぶ}り</code>, and the cycle is closed by
putting the last verb into a finished form. <code>{f|破|やぶ}るした</code> is not a
possible ending.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}け<b>たり</b>{f|閉|し}め<b>たり</b>している。
(b) {f|窓|まど}を<b>{f|開|あ}けては{f|閉|し}め、{f|開|あ}けては{f|閉|し}め</b>している。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) reports two things the person does with the window, loosely and without order. It
could describe someone adjusting the room temperature over an afternoon.

(b) makes it a fixed, restless cycle - open, close, open, close - and invites you to
read something into it: indecision, agitation, or an inability to settle. The pattern
supplies that reading, not the verbs.

</details>
