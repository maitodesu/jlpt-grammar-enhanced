# ～せいか

**Meaning** · perhaps because of ～; maybe it's because ～

`せいか` offers a cause without committing to it. The speaker has noticed
something - a headache, a quiet street, a change in someone's mood - and is
guessing at the reason out loud.

## Usage

`せいか` is `せい` ("fault, cause") plus the question particle `か`, and that `か`
is the whole point: it turns a stated cause into a suggested one. It attaches to
the clause or noun that names the cause, and the result follows.

- Verb, plain form: {f|歩|ある}きすぎた<b>せいか</b>、{f|足|あし}が{f|痛|いた}い
- い-adjective, plain form: {f|暑|あつ}い<b>せいか</b>、{f|客|きゃく}が{f|少|すく}ない
- な-adjective + `な`: {f|静|しず}かな<b>せいか</b>、よく{f|眠|ねむ}れた
- Noun + `の`: {f|年|とし}<b>のせいか</b>、{f|字|じ}が{f|読|よ}みにくくなった

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せい` assigns **blame**. The result after `せいか` is normally
something unwelcome, or at least something the speaker did not plan.

</div>

That bias is inherited from `せいで`, "because of (and it was bad)". `せいか`
softens it in two ways at once - the cause is only a guess, and the guess is
hedged - so it is the mildest member of the family and can be used for results
that are merely surprising rather than harmful. It is still wrong for good news:
{f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}んだせいか{f|元気|げんき}になった sounds as though
recovering were a nuisance. That sentence wants おかげか.

One fixed phrase to learn whole: <code>{f|気|き}のせい</code>, "it's just your
imagination", and its hedged form <code>{f|気|き}のせいか</code>, "maybe I'm
imagining it" - 「{f|気|き}のせいか、{f|部屋|へや}が{f|寒|さむ}い」.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|寝不足|ねぶそく}の<b>せいか</b>、{f|頭|あたま}が{f|痛|いた}い。
Maybe it's the lack of sleep, but I have a headache.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}の<b>せいか</b>、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|客|きゃく}が{f|少|すく}ない。
Perhaps because of the rain, there aren't many customers today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|緊張|きんちょう}していた<b>せいか</b>、{f|名前|なまえ}を{f|書|か}くのを{f|忘|わす}れてしまった。
I must have been nervous - I forgot to write my name on it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}してから{f|通勤|つうきん}が{f|長|なが}くなった<b>せいか</b>、{f|最近|さいきん}{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}む{f|時間|じかん}が{f|増|ふ}えた。
Maybe it's the longer commute since I moved, but lately I've had more time for reading.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very common in ordinary speech. It also appears in writing,
though a report that wants to hedge a cause more formally will use
<code>～ためか</code> or <code>～からか</code>, which carry no blame.

The hedge is genuine, not merely polite. A speaker who says
{f|年|とし}のせいか really is unsure whether age is the reason; if they were sure
they would say {f|年|とし}のせいで. So `せいか` is a good way to raise a possible
cause without arguing for it - and, when the cause involves someone else, a good
way to avoid accusing them. 「{f|昨日|きのう}の{f|話|はなし}のせいか、{f|元気|げんき}がないみたいだ」floats a reason for someone's low mood without
pinning it on anyone.

Because `せい` means fault, using it about a person to their face is pointed.
あなたのせいで is an accusation; あなたのせいか is a slightly softened accusation,
not a neutral remark. If no blame is intended, restructure the sentence rather
than reaching for `か` to fix it.

## Contrast

### ～せいか vs ～せいで

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `せいで` **states** the cause and blames it. `せいか` **guesses**
at the cause and only half-blames it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|台風|たいふう}の<b>せいで</b>{f|試合|しあい}が{f|中止|ちゅうし}になった。
The game was cancelled because of the typhoon.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|台風|たいふう}が{f|近|ちか}づいている<b>せいか</b>、{f|頭|あたま}が{f|重|おも}い。
Maybe it's the typhoon coming, but my head feels heavy.
</pre>

The cancellation has an established cause, so `せいで` is right and `せいか` would
sound as though the organisers were unsure why they cancelled. The headache does
not, so `せいか` is right and `せいで` would claim a piece of medical knowledge the
speaker does not have.

### ～せいか vs ～おかげか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same structure, opposite sign. `せい` for a cause you resent,
`おかげ` for one you are grateful to.

</div>

<pre>
よく{f|寝|ね}た<b>おかげか</b>、{f|今日|きょう}は{f|調子|ちょうし}がいい。
Maybe it's because I slept well, but I feel good today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|寝|ね}すぎた<b>せいか</b>、{f|一日|いちにち}ぼんやりしている。
Maybe I overslept - I've been fuzzy all day.
</pre>

Both are hedged, and both are polite. Choosing the wrong one is not a grammar
error but a tone error: `せいか` before good news reads as ingratitude, and
`おかげか` before bad news reads as sarcasm - which is, in fact, exactly how it is
used when someone means it that way.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}せいか、{f|道|みち}が{f|空|す}いている。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}<b>の</b>せいか、{f|道|みち}が{f|空|す}いている。
  After a noun the `の` is obligatory. `せい` is itself a noun and has to be
  linked to the one in front of it.

- ❌ {f|便利|べんり}のせいか、よく{f|使|つか}っている。 → ✅ {f|便利|べんり}<b>な</b>せいか、よく{f|使|つか}っている。
  A な-adjective takes `な`, not `の`. Only true nouns take `の`.

- ❌ いい{f|先生|せんせい}に{f|習|なら}った<b>せいか</b>、{f|上手|じょうず}になった。 → ✅ いい{f|先生|せんせい}に{f|習|なら}った<b>おかげか</b>、{f|上手|じょうず}になった。
  Grammatical, and insulting. `せい` blames; improving under a good teacher calls
  for `おかげ`.

- ❌ {f|寝不足|ねぶそく}のせいか、{f|頭|あたま}が{f|痛|いた}いです<b>か</b>。 → ✅ {f|寝不足|ねぶそく}のせいか、{f|頭|あたま}が{f|痛|いた}いです。
  The `か` in `せいか` is already the whole hedge; the sentence is a statement, not
  a question, and does not take a second `か` at the end.

## Practice

**1.** {f|新|あたら}しい{f|靴|くつ}____、{f|足|あし}が{f|痛|いた}くなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|新|あたら}しい{f|靴|くつ}<b>のせいか</b>、{f|足|あし}が{f|痛|いた}くなった。

A noun precedes, so `の` is needed before `せい`. The result is unwelcome and the
speaker has not verified the cause, which is exactly the situation `せいか` was
made for.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural word: {f|毎日|まいにち}{f|歩|ある}いている（せいか・おかげか）、{f|体重|たいじゅう}が{f|減|へ}った。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|歩|ある}いている<b>おかげか</b>、{f|体重|たいじゅう}が{f|減|へ}った。

Losing weight through daily walking is a welcome result, and `せい` would frame
the speaker's own healthy habit as something to complain about.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|暑|あつ}さのせいで{f|食欲|しょくよく}がない。
(b) {f|暑|あつ}さのせいか{f|食欲|しょくよく}がない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) treats the heat as the established reason and says so flatly; the speaker is
explaining, and could go on to say what they intend to do about it. (b) is
thinking aloud - the appetite is gone, the heat is the best guess, and the speaker
leaves room for it to be something else. In conversation (b) invites the listener
to agree or offer another idea, while (a) closes the question.

</details>
