# ～の中を

**Meaning** · through it - moving across a space or a condition that surrounds you

<code>{f|中|なか}を</code> takes rain, a crowd, darkness, applause, somebody's packed
schedule, and treats it as ground to be crossed. The `を` is the same one as in
{f|道|みち}を{f|歩|ある}く: it marks the stretch a movement passes through, which is
why the verb that follows must be a verb of **motion**.

## Usage

The shape is **Noun + の + {f|中|なか} + を + a verb of movement**:
{f|歩|ある}く, {f|走|はし}る, {f|進|すす}む, {f|通|とお}る, {f|帰|かえ}る,
{f|出|で}かける, {f|泳|およ}ぐ, {f|来|く}る. Once you see the pattern as "traverse",
everything about it follows. You cannot sit, read or wait
<code>{f|中|なか}を</code>, because those verbs go nowhere - they take
<code>{f|中|なか}で</code> instead.

The noun in front does not have to be a physical space. Conditions work just as
well - {f|雨|あめ}の{f|中|なか}を, {f|混乱|こんらん}の{f|中|なか}を,
{f|拍手|はくしゅ}の{f|中|なか}を - and this is where the pattern earns its keep,
because it quietly adds "despite" to the sentence. Crossing weather or noise or
disorder implies that it took something to do it.

An い-adjective can stand in for the noun with no `の`:
<code>お{f|忙|いそが}しい{f|中|なか}を</code>. That phrase, and
<code>{f|雨|あめ}の{f|中|なか}を</code>, head two of the most common set expressions
in polite Japanese - thanking someone for making the journey.

## Examples

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}の<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>{f|走|はし}って{f|帰|かえ}った。
I ran home through the rain.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|人|ひと}ごみの<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>ゆっくり{f|進|すす}んだ。
I made my way slowly through the crowd.
</pre>

<pre>
お{f|忙|いそが}しい<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>お{f|越|こ}しいただき、ありがとうございます。
Thank you for coming when you are so busy.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|激|はげ}しい{f|雪|ゆき}の<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>、{f|選手|せんしゅ}たちは{f|一列|いちれつ}になってゴールを{f|目指|めざ}した。
Through the driving snow, the runners strung out in a line and pressed on for the finish.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The construction itself is neutral, but it has a faintly literary weight when used
in narration. A novel or a news report will say
<code>{f|嵐|あらし}の{f|中|なか}を{f|船|ふね}は{f|進|すす}んだ</code>; in casual speech
you would more often hear <code>{f|雨|あめ}なのに{f|走|はし}って{f|帰|かえ}った</code>.
The exception is the two set phrases, which are completely ordinary and which every
learner should be able to produce on demand:
<code>お{f|忙|いそが}しい{f|中|なか}をありがとうございます</code> and
<code>{f|雨|あめ}の{f|中|なか}をわざわざすみません</code>.

In those polite formulas the `を` is often dropped -
<code>お{f|忙|いそが}しい{f|中|なか}{f|恐|おそ}れ{f|入|い}りますが</code> is at least
as common - and nothing is lost. Elsewhere the `を` is doing real grammatical work
and cannot go.

The "despite" flavour is a tendency rather than a rule, but a strong one. Because
you only mention the surrounding condition when it was worth mentioning,
<code>{f|中|なか}を</code> almost always implies difficulty, inconvenience or drama.
Saying <code>いい{f|天気|てんき}の{f|中|なか}を{f|歩|ある}いた</code> is not
ungrammatical, but it sounds slightly odd - fine weather is not something you have
to get through.

## Contrast

### ～の{f|中|なか}を vs ～の{f|中|なか}で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` means you **cross** it. `で` means you **stay in** it and do
something there.

</div>

<pre>
{f|森|もり}の<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>{f|歩|ある}いた。
I walked through the woods.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|森|もり}の<b>{f|中|なか}で</b>{f|弁当|べんとう}を{f|食|た}べた。
I ate my lunch in the woods.
</pre>

The test is the verb. If it takes you from one place to another, `を`; if it happens
on the spot, `で`. Note that a single verb can go either way -
<code>{f|中|なか}を{f|走|はし}る</code> is running through, while
<code>{f|中|なか}で{f|走|はし}る</code> is running about inside.

### ～の{f|中|なか}を vs ～の{f|中|なか}に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `を` is a path **across**. `に` is a destination **into**, or a
location where something exists.

</div>

<pre>
{f|建物|たてもの}の<b>{f|中|なか}に</b>{f|入|はい}った。
I went into the building.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|建物|たてもの}の<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>{f|案内|あんない}してもらった。
I was shown around inside the building.
</pre>

With `に` the movement stops at the boundary; that is the whole event. With `を` the
inside is the ground being covered. English "in" and "into" hide this distinction,
which is why learners reach for `に` and produce ❌{f|森|もり}の{f|中|なか}に{f|歩|ある}いた.

### ～の{f|中|なか}を vs {f|中|じゅう}

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>じゅう</code> means "**all through**" a period or a place,
and is a suffix on the noun itself. <code>の{f|中|なか}を</code> means "**across**"
something, and needs a verb of motion after it.

</div>

<code>{f|中|なか}</code> read <code>じゅう</code> is the N3 pattern this one is
closest to, and it is closest only because the two share a character. The reading is
different, the attachment is different, and so is the meaning.

<pre>
{f|一日中|いちにちじゅう}{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っていた。
It rained all day.
</pre>

<pre>
その{f|雨|あめ}の<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|傘|かさ}も{f|差|さ}さずに{f|歩|ある}いていった。
He walked off through that rain without even putting up an umbrella.
</pre>

Watch the readings when you meet {f|中|なか} in the wild:
<code>じゅう</code> after a time or place word means "throughout",
<code>ちゅう</code> in {f|工事中|こうじちゅう} means "in progress", and
<code>なか</code> with `の` in front and `を` after is this pattern.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}の<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}んだ。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}の<b>{f|中|なか}で</b>{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}んだ。
  Reading goes nowhere, so there is no path for `を` to mark. A stationary action
  takes `で`.

- ❌ {f|森|もり}の<b>{f|中|なか}に</b>{f|一時間|いちじかん}{f|歩|ある}いた。 → ✅ {f|森|もり}の<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>{f|一時間|いちじかん}{f|歩|ある}いた。
  `に` would make the woods a destination. Walking *through* them is `を`.

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>{f|来|き}てくれた。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}<b>の{f|中|なか}を</b>{f|来|き}てくれた。
  A noun needs `の` before {f|中|なか}. Only an い-adjective attaches bare
  (お{f|忙|いそが}しい{f|中|なか}を).

- ❌ {f|一日中|いちにちじゅう}を{f|歩|ある}いた。 → ✅ {f|一日中|いちにちじゅう}{f|歩|ある}いた。
  <code>じゅう</code> is already an adverb and takes no particle. It is a different
  word from the <code>なか</code> of this pattern.

## Practice

**1.** {f|台風|たいふう}の____、{f|父|ちち}は{f|自転車|じてんしゃ}で{f|会社|かいしゃ}へ{f|向|む}かった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|台風|たいふう}の<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>

<code>{f|向|む}かう</code> is movement, and the typhoon is the condition being
crossed. The sentence also carries the usual implication - he went despite it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|図書館|としょかん}の（{f|中|なか}を・{f|中|なか}で）{f|静|しず}かに{f|勉強|べんきょう}した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|図書館|としょかん}の<b>{f|中|なか}で</b>{f|静|しず}かに{f|勉強|べんきょう}した。

Studying happens on the spot; nobody is crossing the library. A stationary activity
takes `で`, and `を` would need a verb of motion.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|人|ひと}ごみの<b>{f|中|なか}で</b>{f|友|とも}だちを{f|探|さが}した。
(b) {f|人|ひと}ごみの<b>{f|中|なか}を</b>{f|友|とも}だちを{f|探|さが}して{f|歩|ある}いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) puts the speaker standing in the crowd, looking around. The crowd is where the
searching took place, and nothing says they moved.

(b) makes the crowd something being pushed through: the speaker is on the move,
covering ground, and the sentence conveys the effort of it. The `を` needs the verb
{f|歩|ある}く to lean on, which is why (b) has to spell the walking out.

</details>
