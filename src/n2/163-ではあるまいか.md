# ～ではあるまいか

**Meaning** · might it not be that ～?; is it not perhaps ～? - a literary way of asserting something while wearing the face of a question

`まい` is the classical negative of `だろう`: where `だろう` guesses that something *is*,
`まい` guesses that something *is not*. So <code>ではあるまい</code> means "surely it is
not", and adding `か` turns that into "surely it is not …, is it?" - which, by the logic of
rhetorical questions, is how a writer says **I believe it is**. In force it is identical to
<code>のではないだろうか</code>. In register it is a century older.

## Formation

The `ある` in the middle is the `ある` of `である`, so the pattern behaves like a formal
copula. Verbs and い-adjectives need `の` to nominalise the clause first.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ではあるまいか | {f|偶然|ぐうぜん} → {f|偶然|ぐうぜん}ではあるまいか |
| な-adjective | drop だ + ではあるまいか | {f|乱暴|らんぼう}だ → {f|乱暴|らんぼう}ではあるまいか |
| Verb | plain form + のではあるまいか | {f|忘|わす}れている → {f|忘|わす}れているのではあるまいか |
| い-adjective | plain form + のではあるまいか | {f|危|あぶ}ない → {f|危|あぶ}ないのではあるまいか |
| Statement, no question | … + ではあるまい | {f|簡単|かんたん} → {f|簡単|かんたん}ではあるまい |
| Modern equivalent (write this) | … + のではないだろうか | {f|偶然|ぐうぜん} → {f|偶然|ぐうぜん}なのではないだろうか |
| Spoken equivalent (say this) | … + のではないでしょうか | {f|偶然|ぐうぜん} → {f|偶然|ぐうぜん}なのではないでしょうか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `まい` does attach to verbs directly（<code>{f|行|い}くまい</code>,
<code>するまい</code>）, and the rules for which stem it takes are messy. In this pattern
you are spared them: `まい` follows `ある` and nothing else, so
<code>ではあるまいか</code> can be learnt as one fixed block.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
それは{f|偶然|ぐうぜん}<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Might that not have been a coincidence?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|我々|われわれ}は{f|大切|たいせつ}なことを{f|忘|わす}れているの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Are we not forgetting something important?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|数字|すうじ}だけを{f|見|み}て{f|判断|はんだん}するのは、いささか{f|乱暴|らんぼう}<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
To judge on the figures alone is surely a little crude.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|静|しず}けさこそ、この{f|町|まち}の{f|本当|ほんとう}の{f|魅力|みりょく}なの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
It is this quietness, surely, that is the real charm of the town.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|効率|こうりつ}ばかりを{f|求|もと}めるあまり、{f|大事|だいじ}なものを{f|失|うしな}いつつあるの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
In chasing efficiency above all, are we not in the process of losing something that matters?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|技術|ぎじゅつ}の{f|進歩|しんぽ}を{f|急|いそ}ぎすぎるのは、かえって{f|危|あぶ}ないの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Might it not in fact be dangerous to hurry technological progress so?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|少年|しょうねん}はあの{f|日|ひ}、{f|何|なに}もかも{f|分|わ}かっていたの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Had the boy not, on that day, understood everything?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|人|ひと}は{f|皆|みな}、どこかで{f|誰|だれ}かに{f|支|ささ}えられて{f|生|い}きているの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Every one of us, surely, is being held up by somebody somewhere.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

**This is written, literary Japanese, and a learner should not produce it in
conversation.** Its homes are newspaper editorials, essays, criticism, the narration of
novels and the more elevated kind of speech - a memorial address, a retirement lecture. In
an ordinary exchange it lands like Victorian English: not wrong, but audibly costumed.

**What to say instead**, in order of how much weight you want:
<code>んじゃないですか</code> in relaxed speech,
<code>のではないでしょうか</code> in polite speech and business,
<code>のではないだろうか</code> in ordinary writing. All three do exactly the job that
<code>ではあるまいか</code> does; the only thing you lose is the antique varnish.

The varnish is not decorative, though - it does work. `まい` is grave, and a writer
choosing it is signalling that the question is a serious one, often a moral one. This is
why the pattern clusters around sentences beginning
<code>{f|我々|われわれ}は</code> and <code>{f|人|ひと}は</code>: it is the grammar of an
essayist asking the reader to reconsider something.

Note also that the question is **rhetorical**. Nobody answers it. A speaker who says
<code>ではあるまいか</code> has already decided, and is inviting agreement rather than
information - which is exactly the trick the whole family of negative-question hedges is
built on.

Two smaller points. The `か`-less form <code>ではあるまい</code> is a flat literary
negative guess: "it will surely not be". And in older prose you will meet
<code>あるまいか</code> written after であろう-style copulas and inside long sentences; if
you can spot the `まい`, you can read the rest.

## Contrast

### ～ではあるまいか vs ～のではないだろうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different century. Read
<code>ではあるまいか</code>; write and say
<code>のではないだろうか</code>.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|方法|ほうほう}には{f|無理|むり}があるの<b>ではないだろうか</b>。
Isn't there something unworkable about this approach?
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|方法|ほうほう}には{f|無理|むり}があるの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Is there not something unworkable about this approach?
</pre>

The English translations show the difference fairly: "isn't there" against "is there not".
Both hedge, both mean the writer thinks so, and the second sounds like a leading article.
In a university essay the first is safe everywhere; the second will read as a deliberate
stylistic choice, and had better be one.

### ～まい as a negative guess vs ～まい as a refusal

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - about **someone else**, `まい` means *surely will not*. About
**oneself**, it means *I will not* - a resolution.

</div>

<pre>
こんな{f|時間|じかん}に{f|来|く}る{f|客|きゃく}もある<b>まい</b>。
No customer is going to turn up at this hour.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|二度|にど}とあの{f|店|みせ}には{f|行|い}く<b>まい</b>と{f|心|こころ}に{f|決|き}めた。
I resolved never to set foot in that shop again.
</pre>

The first is <code>ないだろう</code>; the second is a literary
<code>ないつもりだ</code>. Both are worth recognising, because `まい` survives in modern
Japanese mostly inside fixed shapes - <code>{f|負|ま}けるまい</code>,
<code>{f|人|ひと}には{f|言|い}うまい</code> - where a character is steeling themselves.

### ～ではあるまいか vs ～だろうか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>だろうか</code> genuinely **wonders**.
<code>ではあるまいか</code> has already **decided** and is asking you to agree.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|本当|ほんとう}のことを{f|話|はな}している<b>だろうか</b>。
Is he telling the truth, I wonder?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|何|なに}かを{f|隠|かく}しているの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
Is he not hiding something?
</pre>

The first leaves the writer genuinely uncertain, and the paragraph after it can go either
way. The second is a claim: what follows will be the evidence for it. This is the single
most useful thing to know about the whole negative-question family - the negative shape is
not doubt, it is politeness wrapped around a position.

## Common mistakes

- ❌「この{f|店|みせ}、{f|高|たか}いの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。」（to a friend in a shop） → ✅「この{f|店|みせ}、{f|高|たか}い<b>んじゃない</b>?」
  Grammatical and absurd. The literary register cannot be switched on for a small everyday
  remark without sounding like a parody.

- ❌ この{f|判断|はんだん}は{f|早|はや}すぎる<b>ではあるまいか</b>。 → ✅ この{f|判断|はんだん}は{f|早|はや}すぎるの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
  A verb or い-adjective clause has to be nominalised with `の` first. Only nouns and
  な-adjectives connect directly.

- ❌ {f|危険|きけん}だ<b>ではあるまいか</b>。 → ✅ {f|危険|きけん}<b>ではあるまいか</b>。
  The `だ` is already inside <code>ではある</code>. Leaving the original `だ` in place
  stacks two copulas.

- ❌「{f|無理|むり}ではあるまいか。」「はい、{f|無理|むり}です。」 → ✅「{f|無理|むり}ではあるまいか。」（no answer expected）
  It is a rhetorical question. Treating it as a request for information misreads the
  sentence: the writer has already concluded, and is bringing the reader along.

## Practice

**1.** Where would you expect to meet this sentence?
{f|我々|われわれ}は{f|便利|べんり}さと{f|引|ひ}き{f|換|か}えに、{f|大切|たいせつ}な{f|時間|じかん}を{f|手放|てばな}しているの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In a newspaper editorial, an essay or a formal address - written Japanese with a moral
argument in it. The giveaways are <code>{f|我々|われわれ}</code> and the literary `まい`.
Nobody says this to a colleague at lunch; in speech it would be
<code>{f|手放|てばな}しているのではないでしょうか</code>.

</details>

**2.** Rewrite in ordinary modern Japanese: {f|説明|せつめい}が{f|足|た}りなかったのではあるまいか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|説明|せつめい}が{f|足|た}りなかったの<b>ではないだろうか</b>。（polite: のではないでしょうか）

<code>ではあるまいか</code> maps one-to-one onto
<code>のではないだろうか</code>. Nothing else in the sentence changes, which is the
clearest possible demonstration that the difference between them is register alone.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese in a literary register: "Might it not be that we have been looking in the wrong place all along?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|我々|われわれ}は{f|初|はじ}めから{f|違|ちが}う{f|場所|ばしょ}を{f|探|さが}していたの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。

The verb clause takes `の` before <code>ではあるまいか</code>, and
<code>{f|我々|われわれ}</code> matches the register the pattern brings with it. English
"might it not be that" is the closest natural equivalent.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|計画|けいかく}は{f|危険|きけん}だではあるまいか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|計画|けいかく}は{f|危険|きけん}<b>ではあるまいか</b>。

<code>ではある</code> already contains the copula, so the `だ` of
<code>{f|危険|きけん}だ</code> has to go. The same trimming applies to nouns:
<code>{f|偶然|ぐうぜん}ではあるまいか</code>, never
<code>{f|偶然|ぐうぜん}だではあるまいか</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}の{f|説|せつ}は{f|正|ただ}しい<b>だろうか</b>。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}の{f|説|せつ}は{f|正|ただ}しいの<b>ではあるまいか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an open question. The writer does not know whether the theory holds, and the next
paragraph could argue either side.

(b) is a defence of the theory dressed as a question. The writer thinks it is right, and
is asking the reader to entertain that view; the paragraph after it will supply reasons.
The literary `まい` also raises the temperature, so (b) reads as the turn in an argument
rather than a passing thought.

</details>
