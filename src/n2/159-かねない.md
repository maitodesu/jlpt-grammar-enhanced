# ～かねない

**Meaning** · could well ～; is liable to ～; I wouldn't put it past ～ - always about an outcome the speaker does not want

`かねない` is the negative of `かねる`, the verb-ending that means *cannot bring oneself
to*. Read it literally and it says "it is not the case that this could not happen" - which
is a roundabout way of saying **don't be surprised if it does**. That double negative is
the whole flavour of the pattern: it is not a neutral estimate of odds but a warning, and
it only ever points at bad news.

## Formation

A **verb** pattern, attaching to the ます-stem. Adjectives and nouns cannot take it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + かねない | なる → なりかねない |
| する-verb | ～し + かねない | {f|失敗|しっぱい}する → {f|失敗|しっぱい}しかねない |
| Compound outcome | ます-stem + かねない | {f|招|まね}く → {f|招|まね}きかねない |
| Polite | ます-stem + かねません | {f|遅|おく}れる → {f|遅|おく}れかねません |
| Modifying a noun | ます-stem + かねない + Noun | {f|起|お}こる → {f|起|お}こりかねない{f|事態|じたい} |
| Positive counterpart (opposite meaning) | ます-stem + かねる | {f|決|き}める → {f|決|き}めかねる |

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no negative version. To say something is *unlikely*, you want
`そうにない` or <code>ないだろう</code>; `かねない` has the negation built into it already,
and <code>かねなくない</code> is not Japanese.

</div>

<pre>
{f|寝|ね}{f|不足|ぶそく}が{f|続|つづ}くと、{f|体|からだ}を{f|壊|こわ}し<b>かねない</b>。
Keep sleeping this badly and you could easily make yourself ill.
</pre>

<pre>

Leave it as it is and it could well turn into a serious accident.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|冗談|じょうだん}のつもりでも、{f|相手|あいて}を{f|傷|きず}つけ<b>かねない</b>。
Even if you mean it as a joke, you could easily hurt the other person.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|人|ひと}なら、{f|平気|へいき}でうそをつき<b>かねない</b>。
I wouldn't put it past him to lie without blinking.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|値上|ねあ}げが{f|続|つづ}けば、{f|客|きゃく}が{f|離|はな}れていき<b>かねません</b>。

</pre>

<pre>

Send it without checking and personal data could end up leaking outside.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`かねない` is a shade formal but nowhere near stiff. It is everywhere in news writing,
editorials, safety notices and business talk, and it is also perfectly ordinary in speech
when one person is warning another. The polite `かねません` is the standard form for
raising a risk in a meeting without accusing anybody.

The speaker's attitude is built in. Saying <code>なりかねない</code> is not a forecast; it
is an argument that something ought to be prevented, which is why the pattern so often
follows a conditional - <code>そのままにしておくと</code>, <code>～すれば</code>,
<code>～たら</code> - and why the natural response to it is to change the plan.

With a **person** as the subject it turns into an accusation.
<code>あの{f|人|ひと}ならやりかねない</code> means "he's quite capable of it", and it is not
a compliment. Used about someone present, it is a serious thing to say. Speakers do use it
about themselves, half-ruefully - <code>{f|私|わたし}も{f|忘|わす}れかねない</code> - but
that is a confession, not a neutral statement.

One structural point worth holding on to: because the pattern is a **double negative**, it
lands heavier than `かもしれない` while being grammatically shorter. Learners often reach
for it as a fancier "maybe" and end up sounding alarmed about nothing.

## Contrast

### ～かねない vs ～かもしれない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `かもしれない` weighs a possibility of **any kind**. `かねない` warns
about a **bad** one, and implies something should be done.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|雪|ゆき}になる<b>かもしれない</b>。
It might turn to snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|坂|さか}は{f|凍|こお}ると、すべって{f|転|ころ}び<b>かねない</b>。
If this slope ices over, someone could easily slip and fall.
</pre>

Swap them and you can hear the difference. The first sentence with `かねない` would be odd
unless snow were a genuine hazard; the second with `かもしれない` becomes a shrug, which is
the wrong thing to say about a hazard. Note also that `かもしれない` attaches to plain
forms, including adjectives and nouns, while `かねない` only ever follows a verb stem.

### ～かねない vs ～{f|恐|おそ}れがある

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|恐|おそ}れがある</code> is **institutional**: an official
statement that a risk exists. `かねない` is the **speaker's** judgement, and it can be
aimed at a person.

</div>

<pre>
{f|夜|よる}にかけて{f|川|かわ}の{f|水|みず}が{f|増|ふ}える<b>{f|恐|おそ}れがあります</b>。
There is a risk of the river rising through the night.
</pre>

<pre>
このままでは{f|締|し}め{f|切|き}りに{f|遅|おく}れ<b>かねません</b>。
At this rate we could well miss the deadline.
</pre>

The first is what a broadcaster says; it reports a risk without anyone owning it, and it
attaches to a plain form rather than a stem. The second is a colleague telling you the
truth. And where a person is the subject, only `かねない` is available:
<code>{f|彼|かれ}なら{f|断|ことわ}りかねない</code> works, while the
<code>{f|恐|おそ}れ</code> version would sound like a police bulletin.

### ～かねない vs ～かねる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the positive form means the negative thing. `かねる` = *cannot*;
`かねない` = *could well*.

</div>

<pre>
その{f|件|けん}については、お{f|答|こた}え<b>しかねます</b>。
I'm afraid I am not in a position to answer that.
</pre>

<pre>
はっきり{f|断|ことわ}らないと、{f|相手|あいて}は{f|了承|りょうしょう}したと{f|思|おも}い<b>かねない</b>。
If you don't refuse clearly, the other side could well take it as agreement.
</pre>

`かねる` is the polite refusal machine of business Japanese -
<code>いたしかねます</code>, <code>わかりかねます</code>, <code>{f|決|き}めかねる</code> -
and it says the speaker cannot or will not do the thing. Attach `ない` to it and the
meaning flips to its opposite. This pair catches almost everyone once, and the way out is
to remember which one is polite: refusals are `かねます`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|今度|こんど}の{f|試合|しあい}は{f|勝|か}ち<b>かねない</b>。 → ✅ {f|今度|こんど}の{f|試合|しあい}は{f|勝|か}つ<b>かもしれない</b>。
  Winning is good news, and `かねない` only takes bad news. The sentence as written sounds
  as if victory would be a disaster.

- ❌ {f|大|おお}きな{f|問題|もんだい}になる<b>かねない</b>。 → ✅ {f|大|おお}きな{f|問題|もんだい}になり<b>かねない</b>。
  It attaches to the ます-stem, never to the dictionary form.

- ❌ この{f|方法|ほうほう}では{f|失敗|しっぱい}し<b>かねます</b>。（meaning "we could well fail"） → ✅ この{f|方法|ほうほう}では{f|失敗|しっぱい}し<b>かねません</b>。
  `かねます` says the speaker is unable to fail, which is nonsense here. The warning form
  is the negative one.

- ❌ その{f|薬|くすり}は{f|危険|きけん}<b>かねない</b>。 → ✅ その{f|薬|くすり}は{f|危険|きけん}な{f|状態|じょうたい}を{f|招|まね}き<b>かねない</b>。
  Verbs only. To get an adjective idea into the pattern, turn it into something that
  happens.

## Practice

**1.** {f|説明|せつめい}を{f|省|はぶ}くと、{f|後|あと}でトラブルになり____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|後|あと}でトラブルになり<b>かねない</b>（or かねません）。

Trouble is an unwanted outcome and the sentence is a warning against cutting the
explanation short, which is exactly what this pattern is for. Note the ます-stem
<code>なり</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この{f|辺|あた}りは{f|夜|よる}になると{f|暗|くら}いので、{f|事故|じこ}が（{f|起|お}こりかねません・{f|起|お}こるかもしれません）から{f|気|き}をつけてください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|事故|じこ}が<b>{f|起|お}こりかねません</b>から{f|気|き}をつけてください。

Both are grammatical, but the sentence ends in a warning, and `かねない` is the form that
carries one. `かもしれません` would merely note the possibility and then oddly ask the
listener to take care about it.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Saying that in front of the client could well damage our relationship with them."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そんなことをお{f|客様|きゃくさま}の{f|前|まえ}で{f|言|い}うと、{f|関係|かんけい}を{f|悪|わる}くし<b>かねません</b>。

The conditional plus `かねません` is the standard shape for "doing X could well cause bad
Y". <code>{f|悪|わる}くする</code> supplies a verb for the pattern to attach to, since the
adjective on its own cannot take it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|無理|むり}な{f|計画|けいかく}を{f|立|た}てると、みんなが{f|疲|つか}れてしまうかねる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|無理|むり}な{f|計画|けいかく}を{f|立|た}てると、みんなが{f|疲|つか}れてしまい<b>かねない</b>。

Two repairs. The ending must be the negative `かねない` - `かねる` would claim that
everyone is unable to get tired - and it attaches to the ます-stem, so
<code>しまう</code> becomes <code>しまい</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|忘|わす}れる<b>かもしれない</b>。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|忘|わす}れ<b>かねない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral possibility. Anyone can forget; the speaker is simply allowing for it,
and might follow up with a friendly reminder.

(b) is about his character. It says forgetting is the sort of thing he does, and the
speaker disapproves - close to English "he's perfectly capable of forgetting". Said where
he can hear it, (b) is an insult that (a) is not.

</details>
