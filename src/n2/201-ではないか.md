# ～ではないか

**Meaning** · come on, let's do it - a rallying call; and, on a plain clause, "but look, it *is* so" - a point pushed in front of the listener

`ではないか` is a negative question that is not asking anything. On a volitional it
turns "shall we?" into a call to arms: <code>{f|始|はじ}めようではないか</code>, "let
us begin". On an ordinary clause it holds a fact up to the listener's face: "you
*did* promise." Both uses are aimed at a person, and both expect agreement rather
than an answer.

## Formation

`ではないか` attaches to the plain form. Nothing is added in front of it - in
particular, no `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (volitional) - the appeal | volitional + ではないか | {f|戦|たたか}う → {f|戦|たたか}おうではないか |
| Verb (plain) - pressing a point | plain form + ではないか | {f|約束|やくそく}した → {f|約束|やくそく}したではないか |
| い-adjective | plain form + ではないか | {f|危|あぶ}ない → {f|危|あぶ}ないではないか |
| な-adjective | stem + ではないか | {f|立派|りっぱ}だ → {f|立派|りっぱ}ではないか |
| Noun | noun + ではないか | {f|嘘|うそ}だ → {f|嘘|うそ}ではないか |
| Casual | では → じゃ | {f|行|い}こうじゃないか・{f|言|い}ったじゃないか |
| Polite | ではありませんか・じゃないですか | {f|進|すす}めようではありませんか |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the volitional row is the "let's" meaning; every other row is the
"isn't it / look at that" meaning. One form, two jobs, and the form of the verb in
front of it tells you which.

</div>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

The full <code>ではないか</code> is platform Japanese. It belongs to speeches, editorials,
pep talks and fiction, and in ordinary conversation it sounds declamatory - a
tendency that also makes it lean male and older when it does turn up in speech. A
manager can open a meeting with <code>{f|考|かんが}えようではありませんか</code>; a
friend suggesting lunch cannot.

Everyday speech uses the contraction. <code>じゃないか</code> is the casual form and
leans male; <code>じゃない</code> with a rising tail is the version everyone uses,
regardless of gender; <code>じゃないですか</code> is its polite counterpart. The
meaning does not shift as you move down that ladder - only how theatrical you
sound.

Two warnings about the "pressing a point" use. First, it is delivered downward or
sideways: aimed at a superior, <code>{f|言|い}ったじゃないですか</code> reads as an
accusation, and <code>{f|申|もう}し{f|上|あ}げましたよね</code> or
<code>～のではないでしょうか</code> is what you want. Second,
<code>じゃないですか</code> has developed a filler use in modern conversation -
<code>{f|私|わたし}、{f|辛|から}いものが{f|苦手|にがて}じゃないですか</code>, "as you
know, I can't take spicy food" - said about things the listener could not possibly
know. Many speakers find it grating. Recognise it; do not build a habit of it.

The volitional appeal is also warm rather than bossy: it includes the speaker.
That is why it works for a coach or a chairman and reads oddly from someone who is
not part of the effort.

## Contrast

### ～ではないか vs ～ましょう・～ようか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～ましょう</code> and <code>～ようか</code> *invite*, and
leave room for no. <code>～ではないか</code> *rallies*, and assumes yes.

</div>

<pre>
そろそろ{f|出|で}かけ<b>ましょうか</b>。
Shall we head out soon?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|最後|さいご}まであきらめずに{f|走|はし}り{f|続|つづ}けよう<b>ではないか</b>。
Let us keep running to the finish, and not give up.
</pre>

The first checks with the listener. The second announces a shared resolve and
carries the room with it - which is why it fits a captain before a match and not a
colleague at the lift.

### ～ではないか vs ～でしょう・～だろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>でしょう</code> asks the listener to *agree*.
<code>ではないか</code> tells the listener they already know, and points.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}、おいしい<b>でしょう</b>。
This place is good, isn't it.
</pre>

<pre>
ここは{f|禁煙|きんえん}だと{f|書|か}いてある<b>ではないか</b>。
It says right there that this is a no-smoking area.
</pre>

<code>でしょう</code> is companionable; it hands the judgement over. <code>ではないか</code>
is not asking - the listener has overlooked something obvious, and the sentence
says so. That edge is exactly why it so often carries reproach.

### ～ではないか vs ～のではないか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - a bare clause + <code>ではないか</code> points at a fact in
front of you. <code>の・ん</code> + <code>ではないか</code> floats a possibility you
are not sure of.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|計算|けいさん}、{f|間違|まちが}っている<b>ではないか</b>。
This calculation is wrong - look at it.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|計算|けいさん}、{f|間違|まちが}っている<b>のではないか</b>。
I wonder whether this calculation might be wrong.
</pre>

One `の` turns a rebuke into a hedge. The second sentence is the polite one in an
office, and <code>{f|間違|まちが}っているのではないでしょうか</code> is politer still.
Note also that the conjecture use does **not** take a volitional in front of it:
there is no ❌<code>{f|行|い}こうのではないか</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ あれは{f|嘘|うそ}<b>だではないか</b>。 → ✅ あれは{f|嘘|うそ}<b>ではないか</b>。
  Nouns and な-adjectives attach bare. The `だ` is already inside <code>では</code>.

- ❌ みんなで{f|行|い}き<b>ましょうではないか</b>。 → ✅ みんなで{f|行|い}こう<b>ではありませんか</b>。
  Keep one register. Plain volitional pairs with <code>ではないか</code>, and the
  polite version puts the politeness in <code>ありません</code>, not in the verb.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|一緒|いっしょ}に{f|行|い}こう<b>ではないか</b>。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、{f|一緒|いっしょ}に{f|行|い}き<b>ませんか</b>。
  The rallying use is spoken downward, to a group you are leading. Pointed at your
  own boss it sounds as though you have taken charge of him.

- ❌ 「{f|寒|さむ}いではないか。」「はい、{f|寒|さむ}くないです。」 → ✅ 「{f|寒|さむ}いではないか。」「{f|本当|ほんとう}ですね、{f|窓|まど}を{f|閉|し}めます。」
  It looks like a yes/no question and is not one. Answering the literal negative
  misses that the speaker is asserting, not asking.

## Practice

**1.** {f|来年|らいねん}は{f|新|あたら}しい{f|工場|こうじょう}を{f|建|た}てよう____。（rallying a meeting, plain style）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|建|た}てよう<b>ではないか</b>。

The volitional plus <code>ではないか</code> is the standard shape for a leader
putting a shared goal to a room. In polite style the same sentence ends
<code>{f|建|た}てようではありませんか</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この{f|数字|すうじ}、どこかで{f|見|み}た{f|覚|おぼ}えがある。{f|去年|きょねん}の{f|資料|しりょう}と{f|同|おな}じ（ではないか・のではないか）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|同|おな}じ<b>ではないか</b>。

You are looking straight at the two documents, so this is the "point at the fact"
use. <code>のではないか</code> would mean you merely suspect a match and have not
checked - a reasonable thing to say, but a different sentence.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, casual and male-leaning: "Hey, you left the light on, didn't you."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

おい、{f|電気|でんき}をつけっぱなしにした<b>じゃないか</b>。

The reproach use on a plain past verb. The contracted <code>じゃないか</code> is the
form this actually takes in speech; the full <code>ではないか</code> here would sound
like a line from a novel.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|先生|せんせい}、もっと{f|早|はや}く{f|教|おし}えてくれたじゃないですか。ひどいですよ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|先生|せんせい}、もっと{f|早|はや}く{f|教|おし}えてくださればよかったのに。

Two problems. <code>くれた</code> is too plain for a teacher, and more importantly
<code>じゃないですか</code> aimed upward turns a complaint into an accusation. A
regretful <code>～ばよかったのに</code> keeps the same content without the finger
pointing.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|時間|じかん}が{f|足|た}りない<b>じゃないですか</b>。
(b) {f|時間|じかん}が{f|足|た}りない<b>のではないでしょうか</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) asserts a shortfall the listener should already have seen, and pushes it at
them. Between friends it is ordinary; in a meeting it lands as blame, and it can
also be heard as the conversational filler "we're short on time, as you know".

(b) offers the same observation as a possibility for the group to consider. It is
the safe form in any professional setting: the speaker takes responsibility for
raising it without accusing anyone of missing it.

</details>
