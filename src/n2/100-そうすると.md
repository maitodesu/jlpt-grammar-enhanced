# そうすると

**Meaning** · if you do that, then…; in that case it follows that… - takes what has just been described as given and works out what results from it

`そうすると` is そう + する + と: "if things go that way, then". It does arithmetic
on the situation. Somebody supplies a fact or an action, and `そうすると`
calculates the consequence out loud.

## Usage

`そうすると` opens a sentence and is followed by a comma. It has two everyday
jobs:

- **Instructions.** You describe an action, then say what it produces:
  ここを{f|押|お}してください。そうすると、{f|電源|でんげん}が{f|入|はい}ります。
- **Inference in conversation.** You take the other person's information as a
  premise and state what must therefore be true:
  そうすると、{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わないことになりますね。

What follows is a **consequence**, not a decision. This is the constraint that
catches learners out: そうすると、{f|私|わたし}が{f|行|い}きます is odd, because going
is something you have chosen, not something that results. Announcing what you
will do about the news needs それなら or じゃあ.

Three relatives are worth keeping straight. `そうすれば` frames the result as
desirable and is used for advice; `そうしたら` (casual そしたら) is the everyday
spoken form and is also used for what actually happened; `そうすると` itself is
neutral about whether the outcome is welcome.

## Examples

<pre>
ここを{f|押|お}してください。<b>そうすると</b>、{f|電源|でんげん}が{f|入|はい}ります。
Press here, and the power comes on.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|三時|さんじ}の{f|電車|でんしゃ}に{f|乗|の}るんですね。<b>そうすると</b>、{f|着|つ}くのは{f|六時|ろくじ}ごろですね。
You're taking the three o'clock train? That puts you in at around six, then.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|来年|らいねん}から{f|税金|ぜいきん}が{f|上|あ}がるらしい。<b>そうすると</b>、{f|家計|かけい}はもっと{f|苦|くる}しくなる。
Tax is apparently going up next year, which will squeeze the household budget even harder.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|申込書|もうしこみしょ}を{f|出|だ}してから{f|二週間|にしゅうかん}かかります。<b>そうすると</b>、{f|受|う}け{f|取|と}れるのは{f|月末|げつまつ}になりますね。
It takes two weeks from submitting the form - so you'd have it by the end of the month, then.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. It turns up in manuals and instructions, in negotiation, and in
any conversation where two people are working something out together. Because it
does no more than calculate, it is a polite thing to say: you are checking a
consequence rather than pushing a view.

In casual speech そうしたら and そしたら are far more frequent, and そしたら in
particular is the form you will hear from friends. Neither is wrong in the
consequence sense; they simply sit lower on the formality scale, and そしたら
would look out of place in a printed manual.

`そうすると` is also possible for a past sequence -
ボタンを{f|押|お}した。そうすると、{f|音|おと}が{f|鳴|な}った - but for something you
actually watched happen, すると is the idiomatic choice and is what a native
writer reaches for.

## Contrast

### そうすると vs すると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `すると` reports what **did** happen next. `そうすると` works
out what **would** happen, or what must be true.

</div>

<pre>
スイッチを{f|入|い}れた。<b>すると</b>、{f|画面|がめん}が{f|真|ま}っ{f|白|しろ}になった。
I flipped the switch, and the screen went completely white.
</pre>

<pre>
スイッチを{f|入|い}れてください。<b>そうすると</b>、{f|画面|がめん}に{f|地図|ちず}が{f|出|で}ます。
Turn the switch on, and a map will appear on the screen.
</pre>

The tense gives it away. `すると` narrates, so its second half is past and its
first half is a completed action. `そうすると` predicts or deduces, so its second
half looks to the future or states a conclusion. Note that both refuse a second
half in which the speaker decides something.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ 「{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。」「そうすると、{f|帰|かえ}ります。」 → ✅ 「{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}です。」「<b>それなら</b>、{f|帰|かえ}ります。」
  Going home is a decision, and `そうすると` introduces results, not decisions.
  Replying to news with a plan of action calls for それなら.

- ❌ {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けた。そうすると、{f|猫|ねこ}が{f|入|はい}ってきた。 → ✅ {f|窓|まど}を{f|開|あ}けた。<b>すると</b>、{f|猫|ねこ}が{f|入|はい}ってきた。
  For something the speaker watched happen, the narrative connector is すると.
  `そうすると` makes it sound like a general rule about windows and cats.

- ❌ ボタンを{f|押|お}すそうすると、{f|音|おと}が{f|鳴|な}ります。 → ✅ ボタンを{f|押|お}すと、{f|音|おと}が{f|鳴|な}ります。
  Inside one sentence the connector is plain と. `そうすると` needs its own
  sentence, with the previous one already finished.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|書類|しょるい}は{f|部長|ぶちょう}の{f|印|いん}が{f|必要|ひつよう}です。____、{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう}には{f|出|だ}せませんね。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>そうすると</b>、{f|今日|きょう}{f|中|じゅう}には{f|出|だ}せませんね。

The speaker takes the requirement as a premise and works out what it means for
the deadline. Deducing a consequence from information just received is exactly
this word's job, and the ね invites the other person to confirm the sum.

</details>

**2.** Choose the natural connector:
{f|明日|あした}は{f|休|やす}みだそうですよ。（そうすると・それなら）、{f|映画|えいが}でも{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}きませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>それなら</b>、{f|映画|えいが}でも{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}きませんか。

The second half is an invitation, and invitations are decisions about what to do
rather than consequences that follow by themselves. `そうすると` would need
something like 「{f|道|みち}が{f|混|こ}みますね」 after it.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) レバーを{f|下|さ}げてください。<b>そうすると</b>、{f|水|みず}が{f|出|で}ます。
(b) レバーを{f|下|さ}げてください。<b>そうすれば</b>、{f|水|みず}が{f|出|で}ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are natural instructions and a manual could print either. Sentence (a) is
purely mechanical: pull the lever and this is what the machine does, with no
comment on whether that is what you wanted. Sentence (b) presents the water as
the goal you were after, so it reads as helpful advice - "do that and you'll get
your water". `そうすれば` carries the sense of a desirable result; `そうすると` is
neutral.

</details>
