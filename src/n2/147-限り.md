# ～限り

**Meaning** · as long as ～ holds; within the bounds of ～ - and, in the negative, "unless"

<code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is the noun form of "to limit". It draws a boundary around the
main clause and says: *inside this line, what follows is true; outside it, all bets are
off*. The negative <code>～ない{f|限|かぎ}り</code> draws the same line from the other
side and comes out as English "unless".

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain non-past + {f|限|かぎ}り | {f|生|い}きている → {f|生|い}きている{f|限|かぎ}り |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + {f|限|かぎ}り | {f|謝|あやま}らない → {f|謝|あやま}らない{f|限|かぎ}り |
| Verb (past, "as far as I…") | た-form + {f|限|かぎ}り(では) | {f|調|しら}べた → {f|調|しら}べた{f|限|かぎ}りでは |
| い-adjective (negative) | ～くない + {f|限|かぎ}り | ひどくない → ひどくない{f|限|かぎ}り |
| な-adjective | ～な + {f|限|かぎ}り | {f|可能|かのう}な → {f|可能|かのう}な{f|限|かぎ}り |
| Noun | Noun + の／である + {f|限|かぎ}り | {f|学生|がくせい} → {f|学生|がくせい}である{f|限|かぎ}り |
| Noun (negative) | Noun + でない{f|限|かぎ}り | {f|特別|とくべつ} → {f|特別|とくべつ}でない{f|限|かぎ}り |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verb before <code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is non-past even when the
sentence is about the future: <code>{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}らない{f|限|かぎ}り、{f|明日|あした}やります</code>.
A past tense there switches the meaning to "as far as I checked / heard".

</div>

<pre>

Unless it rains, the match will go ahead.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|体|からだ}が{f|動|うご}く<b>{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、この{f|仕事|しごと}を{f|続|つづ}けたい。
As long as my body holds out, I want to keep doing this job.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>
{f|可能|かのう}な<b>{f|限|かぎ}り</b>{f|早|はや}くお{f|返事|へんじ}いたします。

</pre>

<pre>
{f|締|し}め{f|切|き}りを{f|守|まも}る<b>{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|進|すす}め{f|方|かた}は{f|皆|みな}さんに{f|任|まか}せます。

</pre>

</pre>

From what I saw yesterday, the work wasn't even half done.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

<code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is neutral in politeness and lives comfortably in speech and
writing alike, though the density of it in official Japanese is high: contracts, notices
and terms of service are full of <code>～ない{f|限|かぎ}り</code>, because that is how
you write a condition that has no exceptions.

Three shapes are worth knowing as units.
<code>{f|可能|かのう}な{f|限|かぎ}り</code> ("as far as possible") is a fixed adverbial
phrase and appears constantly in business Japanese, a step more formal than
<code>できるだけ</code>. <code>{f|見|み}た{f|限|かぎ}りでは</code>,
<code>{f|聞|き}いた{f|限|かぎ}りでは</code>, <code>{f|調|しら}べた{f|限|かぎ}りでは</code>
hedge a statement by naming the limits of your evidence - useful and modest, and worth
having ready before you report something you are not certain of. And
<code>{f|私|わたし}の{f|知|し}る{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is a slightly bookish variant of
<code>{f|知|し}っている{f|限|かぎ}り</code>, common in writing.

The "as long as" use carries a faint sense of *dependency*: the main clause is not just
co-occurring with the condition, it is **propped up by it**. That is why
<code>{f|生|い}きている{f|限|かぎ}り</code> and
<code>{f|体|からだ}が{f|動|うご}く{f|限|かぎ}り</code> sound determined and a little
emotional - the speaker is naming the one thing the promise rests on.

There is no casual contraction and no gender or age marking.

## Contrast

### ～{f|限|かぎ}り vs ～{f|間|あいだ}(は)

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|間|あいだ}</code> is a **stretch of time**.
<code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is a **condition** that happens to last a while.

</div>

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}にいる<b>{f|間|あいだ}</b>に、{f|東北|とうほく}を{f|旅行|りょこう}したい。
While I'm in Japan I want to travel around Tohoku.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|日本|にほん}にいる<b>{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、この{f|保険|ほけん}に{f|入|はい}る{f|義務|ぎむ}がある。
So long as you are in Japan, you are obliged to join this insurance scheme.
</pre>

The first measures a window. The second states what depends on your being here - leave
the country and the obligation lapses. Swap them and the first sentence would sound as
though your travel plans were somehow conditional on your presence, which is trivially
true and therefore odd to say.

### ～ない{f|限|かぎ}り vs ～なければ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>なければ</code> is an ordinary "if not".
<code>ない{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is "unless", and it presents the condition as **the only
thing that would change the outcome**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|薬|くすり}を{f|飲|の}ま<b>なければ</b>{f|熱|ねつ}は{f|下|さ}がらないよ。
If you don't take the medicine your temperature won't come down.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|体質|たいしつ}が{f|変|か}わら<b>ない{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|同|おな}じ{f|症状|しょうじょう}は{f|何度|なんど}でも{f|出|で}る。
Unless your constitution itself changes, the same symptoms will keep coming back.
</pre>

The second is stronger. It shuts the door: nothing short of that condition will make any
difference. That is why it is the workhorse of rules and warnings, and why using it for
a small everyday if-not can sound heavier than you intended.

### ～た{f|限|かぎ}りでは vs ～によると

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both hedge, but they hedge different things.
<code>によると</code> names **someone else's report**;
<code>た{f|限|かぎ}りでは</code> names **the limits of your own checking**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|天気予報|てんきよほう}<b>によると</b>、{f|週末|しゅうまつ}は{f|荒|あ}れるそうだ。
According to the forecast, the weekend is going to be rough.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}から{f|外|そと}を{f|見|み}た<b>{f|限|かぎ}りでは</b>、{f|雪|ゆき}はもう{f|止|や}んでいた。
As far as I could tell looking out of the window, the snow had already stopped.
</pre>

<code>によると</code> passes responsibility to the source and pairs with
<code>そうだ</code>. <code>た{f|限|かぎ}りでは</code> keeps responsibility with you but
fences off how much you actually saw - which is why it is the polite way to answer a
question you cannot fully answer.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}らなかった{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|出発|しゅっぱつ}します。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}{f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}らない{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|出発|しゅっぱつ}します。
  A condition about the future stays non-past. The past form would turn the phrase into
  "as far as I saw it not raining", which is not what you mean.

- ❌ <b>{f|元気|げんき}だ{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|働|はたら}きたい。 → ✅ <b>{f|元気|げんき}な{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|働|はたら}きたい。
  <code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is a noun, so a な-adjective links with <code>な</code>,
  never with <code>だ</code>.

- ❌ <b>{f|学生|がくせい}{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|割引|わりびき}が{f|受|う}けられる。 → ✅ <b>{f|学生|がくせい}である{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|割引|わりびき}が{f|受|う}けられる。
  A bare noun cannot sit directly in front of it. Use <code>である</code> or
  <code>の</code>.

- ❌ {f|時間|じかん}がある<b>{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|少|すこ}し{f|休|やす}みませんか。 → ✅ {f|時間|じかん}がある<b>うちに</b>、{f|少|すこ}し{f|休|やす}みませんか。
  Here you mean "while there's still time", a window - that is
  <code>うちに</code>. <code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> would make the resting somehow
  contingent on time existing.

## Practice

**1.** {f|地図|ちず}を{f|見|み}た____では、{f|駅|えき}からそんなに{f|遠|とお}くないはずだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|地図|ちず}を{f|見|み}た<b>{f|限|かぎ}り</b>では、{f|駅|えき}からそんなに{f|遠|とお}くないはずだ。

The past form plus <code>では</code> is the "as far as I checked" shape. It fences the
claim off to what the map showed, which is exactly why the sentence can end with the
cautious <code>はずだ</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|日本|にほん}にいる（{f|間|あいだ}・{f|限|かぎ}り）、できるだけ{f|温泉|おんせん}を{f|回|まわ}りたい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|日本|にほん}にいる<b>{f|間|あいだ}</b>、できるだけ{f|温泉|おんせん}を{f|回|まわ}りたい。

This is a time window - a stay with a beginning and an end - not a condition the wish
depends on. <code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> would imply the desire itself is propped up by
being in Japan, which is a strange thing to say about a holiday plan.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Unless something unexpected happens, we'll finish by Friday."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|予想外|よそうがい}のことが{f|起|お}こら<b>ない{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|金曜日|きんようび}までに{f|終|お}わります。

"Unless" is the negative form plus <code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code>, and the verb stays
non-past even though the sentence looks forward. The phrasing promises that nothing else
would derail the schedule.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|健康|けんこう}だ{f|限|かぎ}り、{f|旅行|りょこう}を{f|続|つづ}けるつもりです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|健康|けんこう}<b>である{f|限|かぎ}り</b>、{f|旅行|りょこう}を{f|続|つづ}けるつもりです。（or {f|健康|けんこう}な{f|限|かぎ}り）

<code>{f|限|かぎ}り</code> is a noun and needs a noun-modifying shape in front of it.
<code>{f|健康|けんこう}だ</code> is a finished predicate and cannot modify anything, so
it becomes <code>な</code> or <code>である</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|私|わたし}が{f|知|し}っている{f|限|かぎ}り、{f|彼|かれ}は{f|来|き}ません。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|来|こ}ないと{f|思|おも}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) fences the statement off to your own information. You are saying he is not coming
*so far as your knowledge extends*, and quietly leaving room for someone better informed
to correct you. It is the careful thing to say in a meeting.

(b) is a straightforward opinion. It commits you to a judgement rather than to a body of
evidence, so if he walks in five minutes later you were simply wrong - whereas with (a)
you were merely under-informed.

</details>
