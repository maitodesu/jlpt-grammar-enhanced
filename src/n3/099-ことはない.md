# ～ことはない

**Meaning** · there's no need to; it never happens - two readings of one shape, sorted out by who the subject is

With **you** as the subject, `～ことはない` is reassurance: the effort or the worry
is unnecessary, so stop. With **somebody or something else** as the subject, it is
a statement about the world: that does not happen. The words are identical; the
subject decides.

## Formation

The verb is in the **dictionary form** in both senses.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - "no need to" | dictionary form + ことはない | {f|心配|しんぱい}する → {f|心配|しんぱい}することはない |
| Verb - "never happens" | dictionary form + ことはない | {f|遅|おく}れる → {f|遅|おく}れることはない |
| Verb (negative) - "it's not that ～ can't" | ない-form + ことはない | {f|飲|の}めない → {f|飲|の}めないことはない |
| Polite | ～ことはありません | {f|心配|しんぱい}することはありません |
| Casual, は dropped | ～ことない | {f|心配|しんぱい}することない |
| Very formal | ～ことはございません | {f|心配|しんぱい}することはございません |

<div class="note">

**Note** - with {f|決|けっ}して, めったに or ほとんど in front of it, only the "never
happens" reading survives:
{f|決|けっ}して{f|遅|おく}れることはない. With よ, だいじょうぶ or a comforting tone,
only the "no need to" reading survives. Japanese almost never leaves the two
genuinely ambiguous.

</div>

</pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

Nobody is going to laugh at you if it goes wrong, so give it a try without worrying.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The "no need to" sense is **consolation**, and it is one of the kindest patterns
in the language. Its natural home is comforting someone who is upset, anxious, or
apologising too much: <code>{f|泣|な}くことないよ</code>,
<code>{f|謝|あやま}ることはありません</code>. In speech the `は` regularly drops - 
<code>{f|気|き}にすることないよ</code> - and that shortened version is what you
will actually hear between friends.

The same words can turn cool. Said flatly about someone else's plan,
<code>わざわざ{f|行|い}くことはない</code> means "there's no point going", and the
warmth disappears. Tone and context carry that difference entirely; the grammar
does not mark it.

The "never happens" sense is a statement of reliability. It is stronger than
`あまり～ない` and softer than an absolute: it claims a pattern, not a law of
physics. It reads well in descriptions, product blurbs and character sketches, and
it takes `ほとんど` comfortably - 
{f|故障|こしょう}することはほとんどありません.

Two register cautions. Turning down help from someone senior with
<code>ことはありません</code> is grammatical but a little blunt; the set humble
phrase is <code>～には{f|及|およ}びません</code>. And `ことはない` **never** means
"must not" - reading <code>{f|遅|おく}れることはない</code> as a warning about
lateness reverses the sentence.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the negative row in the table is a separate, very common idiom.
<code>～ないことはない</code> is a double negative meaning "it's not that I
can't": {f|辛|から}い{f|物|もの}が{f|食|た}べられないことはないんですが、あまり
{f|得意|とくい}ではありません. It is a hedge, and it usually signals a polite
reluctance rather than genuine ability.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～ことはない vs ～なくてもいい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なくてもいい` **permits** you to skip it. `ことはない` says the
action would be **pointless or unwarranted**, and normally comes with a reason.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|来|こ}<b>なくてもいいですよ</b>。
You don't have to come in tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}があるのに{f|来|く}る<b>ことはありませんよ</b>。
There's no call for you to come in with a fever.
</pre>

The first grants permission - the speaker has the authority to excuse you, and is
doing so. The second argues that coming would be unreasonable, and it carries
concern for the listener. That is why `ことはない` fits consolation and
`なくてもいい` fits a manager approving a day off. Note also that `なくてもいい`
answers a question; `ことはない` heads off an intention.

### ～ことはない vs ～ことがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ことがない</code> with the **た-form** is experience: you
have never done it. <code>ことはない</code> with the **dictionary form** is about
need or frequency.

</div>

<pre>
{f|北海道|ほっかいどう}へ{f|行|い}った<b>ことがありません</b>。
I've never been to Hokkaido.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|北海道|ほっかいどう}では、{f|夏|なつ}に{f|冷房|れいぼう}を{f|使|つか}う<b>ことはありません</b>。
In Hokkaido nobody uses air conditioning in summer.
</pre>

The first looks back over your life; the second describes a standing state of
affairs. The `は`/`が` swap is not decorative - `が` presents the experience as a
fact you possess, `は` sets the situation against expectation ("as for that
happening: it doesn't"). If you meet <code>{f|行|い}ったことはない</code> with `は`
and the た-form, it is still experience, now with a contrastive edge: "been there,
no - heard of it, yes."

### ～ことはない vs ～はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことはない` reports that something **does not happen**, from
observation. `はずがない` says it **could not possibly** be so, from reasoning.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}が{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|忘|わす}れる<b>ことはない</b>。
She never forgets an arrangement.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}が{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|忘|わす}れる<b>はずがない</b>。
There's no way she's forgotten the arrangement.
</pre>

The first is a general character reference and could be said about her at any
time. The second is about **this** occasion and is an inference - you are ruling
out a possibility that has just been raised. So `はずがない` answers "do you think
she forgot?", while `ことはない` answers "what's she like?"

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|遅|おく}れる**ことはない**。（intending "you mustn't be late"） → ✅ {f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|遅|おく}れ<b>てはいけない</b>。
  `ことはない` never prohibits. As written, the sentence praises someone's
  punctuality.

- ❌ {f|泣|な}いた**ことはない**よ、{f|大丈夫|だいじょうぶ}。 → ✅ {f|泣|な}く<b>ことはない</b>よ、{f|大丈夫|だいじょうぶ}。
  Consolation takes the dictionary form. With the た-form the sentence flips to
  experience - "I have never cried" - which is not what a crying friend needs to
  hear.

- ❌ この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|難|むずか}しい**ことはない**。 → ✅ この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|難|むずか}し<b>くはない</b>。
  The pattern takes verbs. An い-adjective negates on its own stem.

- ❌（to a senior colleague）{f|部長|ぶちょう}、わざわざお{f|越|こ}しいただく**ことはありません**。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、お{f|越|こ}しいただくには<b>{f|及|およ}びません</b>。
  Declining a superior's offer with `ことはありません` sounds like you are telling
  them not to bother. <code>～には{f|及|およ}びません</code> is the set humble
  phrase for exactly this.

## Practice

**1.** そんなに{f|落|お}ち{f|込|こ}む____よ。{f|次|つぎ}のチャンスは{f|必|かなら}ずあるから。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

そんなに{f|落|お}ち{f|込|こ}む<b>ことはない</b>よ。

The second sentence gives the reason - there will be another chance - which marks
this as consolation rather than a statement about frequency. In speech
<code>{f|落|お}ち{f|込|こ}むことないよ</code>, with the `は` dropped, is if anything
more natural.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|明日|あした}の{f|会|かい}は{f|自由|じゆう}{f|参加|さんか}ですから、{f|無理|むり}に（{f|来|こ}なくてもいいです・{f|来|く}ることはありません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|無理|むり}に<b>{f|来|こ}なくてもいいです</b>。

The organiser is granting permission to skip an optional event, and permission is
`なくてもいい`. <code>{f|来|く}ることはありません</code> would argue that attending
is pointless, which is an odd thing for the host to say about their own gathering.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "There's no need to apologise - it wasn't your fault."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|謝|あやま}る<b>ことはありません</b>。あなたのせいではないんですから。

Consolation with a reason attached is the core use of this pattern. Between
friends, <code>{f|謝|あやま}ることないよ</code> says the same thing more warmly.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|一度|いちど}も{f|遅刻|ちこく}するはずがない{f|人|ひと}なので、{f|今日|きょう}も{f|時間|じかん}どおりに{f|来|く}るでしょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|一度|いちど}も{f|遅刻|ちこく}<b>することはない</b>{f|人|ひと}なので、{f|今日|きょう}も{f|時間|じかん}どおりに{f|来|く}るでしょう。

The clause is describing what the person is *like* - an observed pattern over
time, which is `ことはない`. `はずがない` is an inference about one specific
occasion and cannot sit inside a general character description with
<code>{f|一度|いちど}も</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|人|ひと}の{f|悪口|わるぐち}を{f|言|い}うことはない。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}は{f|人|ひと}の{f|悪口|わるぐち}を{f|言|い}ったことがない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) describes his character as it stands: speaking ill of people is simply not
something he does. It is about a tendency, and it makes a claim about the future
as well as the past.

(b) reports his record: in your experience, no such occasion has occurred. It is a
statement about history, and it leaves open the possibility that today is
different. (a) would be your answer to "what kind of person is he?"; (b) to "has
he ever said anything about me?"

</details>
