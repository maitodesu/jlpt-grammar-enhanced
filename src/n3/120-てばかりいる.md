# ～てばかりいる

**Meaning** · do nothing but ～; be forever ～ing

`～てばかりいる` says a person spends their time on **one activity to the exclusion
of everything else** - and that the speaker thinks they should be doing something
different. It is a complaint dressed as a description of a schedule.

## Formation

`ばかり` slots between the て-form and `いる`. The whole thing is an ordinary
る-verb, so it conjugates exactly like `いる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ばかり + いる | {f|遊|あそ}ぶ → {f|遊|あそ}んでばかりいる |
| Polite | て-form + ばかり + います | {f|寝|ね}てばかりいます |
| Past | て-form + ばかり + いた | {f|泣|な}いてばかりいた |
| Joining a clause | て-form + ばかり + いて / いても | {f|見|み}てばかりいても |
| Without いる | て-form + ばかり + だ | {f|謝|あやま}ってばかりだ |
| Noun + する verb | Noun + ばかり + している | {f|心配|しんぱい}ばかりしている |
| Casual contraction | て-form + ばっかり(いる) | {f|遊|あそ}んでばっかり |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `～てばかりいる` and `～てばかりだ` are close, and both are common. The
version with `いる` presents the behaviour as going on right now or over a stretch of
time; the version with `だ` is flatter, closer to a verdict on the person. Do not
mix them into ❌<code>～てばかりする</code>, which is not a form.

</div>

</pre>

The manager did nothing but object throughout the meeting, yet didn't offer a single idea of his own.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **almost always critical**. Even when the activity is one you would
normally praise, `てばかりいる` turns it into an imbalance:
{f|勉強|べんきょう}してばかりいる is not "such a hard worker", it is "he never comes
out of his room" - worry, not admiration. If you want the approving version, use a
plain progressive: よく{f|勉強|べんきょう}しています.

Aimed at yourself it becomes self-reproach, and this is extremely common:
{f|謝|あやま}ってばかりですみません, {f|迷惑|めいわく}をかけてばかりで. In that use
it functions almost as an apology formula.

The casual contraction is `ばっかり`, and in fast speech it shortens further to
`ばっか`: {f|遊|あそ}んでばっかりいないで{f|手伝|てつだ}ってよ. That is friends and
family register; keep `ばかり` for anything else.

One genuine ambiguity to be aware of. `ばかり` has a second life after the **た-form**
meaning "just did" - {f|着|つ}いたばかりです, "I've only just arrived". The two are
different patterns with nothing in common but the word, and the て-form versus
た-form is what tells them apart. Confusing them produces sentences that are
grammatical and mean the wrong thing, which is the worst kind of error.

Register is otherwise neutral: `てばかりいる` is at home in speech, in fiction and in
a newspaper column about people who spend too long online.

## Contrast

### ～てばかりいる vs Noun + ばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after a **noun**, `ばかり` restricts *what* you do it to. After a
**て-form**, `ばかり` restricts *what you do* - the whole activity.

</div>

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}は{f|肉|にく}<b>ばかり</b>{f|食|た}べて、{f|野菜|やさい}を{f|残|のこ}す。
My brother eats nothing but meat and leaves the vegetables.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|弟|おとうと}は{f|朝|あさ}から<b>{f|食|た}べてばかりいる</b>。
My brother has done nothing but eat since this morning.
</pre>

In the first, eating is fine; the problem is the menu. In the second, eating itself
is the problem - he should be doing something else with his day. The noun version is
also neutral enough to be used approvingly（{f|野菜|やさい}ばかり{f|食|た}べている
can be a compliment）, which the て-form version essentially never is.

### ～てばかりいる vs ～ている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ている` reports what is happening. `てばかりいる` reports the
same thing plus "and nothing else, and I object".

</div>

<pre>
{f|娘|むすめ}は{f|部屋|へや}でゲームを<b>している</b>。
My daughter is playing a game in her room.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|娘|むすめ}は{f|部屋|へや}でゲームを<b>してばかりいる</b>。
My daughter does nothing but play games in her room.
</pre>

`ている` covers this moment or a neutral habit. `てばかりいる` needs a **stretch of
repeated behaviour**, so it cannot describe a single instance: pointing at someone
right now and saying {f|見|み}てばかりいます about one glance is wrong the way "he's
forever looking" would be wrong about one look.

### ～てばかりいる vs ～がち

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `がち` says it happens **often**, leaving room for other things.
`てばかりいる` says it is **all** that happens.

</div>

<pre>
{f|忙|いそが}しい{f|時期|じき}は{f|外食|がいしょく}が{f|多|おお}くなり<b>がち</b>だ。
When things get busy I tend to eat out more.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}{f|暮|ぐ}らしを{f|始|はじ}めてから、{f|外|そと}で<b>{f|食|た}べてばかりいる</b>。
Since I started living alone I've done nothing but eat out.
</pre>

Both disapprove, but they measure different things - frequency against exclusivity.
They also attach differently: `がち` takes the ます-stem and builds a な-adjective,
while `てばかりいる` takes the て-form and stays a verb. That means only
`てばかりいる` can carry tense and aspect of its own:
{f|食|た}べてばかりいた, {f|食|た}べてばかりいるだろう.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|遊|あそ}ぶ<b>ばかりいる</b> → ✅ {f|遊|あそ}ん<b>でばかりいる</b>
  `ばかり` in this pattern follows the **て-form**, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ {f|食|た}べてばかり<b>する</b> → ✅ {f|食|た}べてばかり<b>いる</b>
  The verb at the end is `いる`. <code>している</code> belongs to the noun pattern
  <code>{f|心配|しんぱい}ばかりしている</code>.

- ❌ お{f|昼|ひる}を{f|食|た}べ<b>てばかりいます</b>。（meaning "I've just eaten lunch"） → ✅ お{f|昼|ひる}を{f|食|た}べ<b>たばかりです</b>。
  "Just did" is `た-form + ばかり`. The て-form version says you have been eating
  all day.

- ❌ {f|娘|むすめ}は{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|練習|れんしゅう}してばかりいて、えらいですね。 → ✅ {f|娘|むすめ}さんは{f|毎日|まいにち}よく{f|練習|れんしゅう}していて、えらいですね。
  The pattern carries disapproval, so it cannot be used to praise someone.

- ❌ {f|今|いま}、{f|彼|かれ}はこちらを{f|見|み}てばかりいます。（about one moment） → ✅ {f|今|いま}、{f|彼|かれ}はこちらを{f|見|み}ています。
  `てばかりいる` needs behaviour repeated over time; it cannot report a single
  instant.

## Practice

**1.** {f|試験|しけん}が{f|近|ちか}いのに、{f|兄|あに}はテレビを{f|見|み}____いる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|兄|あに}はテレビを{f|見|み}<b>てばかり</b>いる。

<code>{f|見|み}る</code> goes into its て-form <code>{f|見|み}て</code>, then
`ばかり`, then `いる`. The <code>のに</code> in the first half tells you a complaint
is coming, which is exactly the tone this pattern supplies.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|山田|やまだ}さんは（{f|甘|あま}い{f|物|もの}ばかり{f|食|た}べている・{f|食|た}べてばかりいる）ので、{f|歯|は}が{f|心配|しんぱい}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|山田|やまだ}さんは<b>{f|甘|あま}い{f|物|もの}ばかり{f|食|た}べている</b>ので、{f|歯|は}が{f|心配|しんぱい}だ。

The worry is about *what* he eats, not about him eating instead of doing other
things - so `ばかり` goes after the noun.
<code>{f|食|た}べてばかりいる</code> would make the eating itself the problem, which
has nothing to do with his teeth.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You can't just keep apologising - please explain what happened."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|謝|あやま}ってばかりいないで</b>、{f|何|なに}があったのか{f|説明|せつめい}してください。

<code>いる</code> takes its ordinary negative て-form <code>いないで</code>, which is
how you tell someone to stop doing only that one thing. This
<code>～てばかりいないで</code> frame is very common in real speech.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|昨日|きのう}{f|東京|とうきょう}に{f|着|つ}いてばかりなので、まだ{f|何|なに}も{f|見|み}ていません。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|昨日|きのう}{f|東京|とうきょう}に{f|着|つ}い<b>たばかり</b>なので、まだ{f|何|なに}も{f|見|み}ていません。

The meaning wanted is "I only arrived yesterday", which is `た-form + ばかり`. The
て-form version would say the speaker has been arriving over and over - the same
word, an entirely different pattern.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|最近|さいきん}よく{f|本|ほん}を{f|読|よ}んでいる。
(b) {f|彼女|かのじょ}は{f|最近|さいきん}{f|本|ほん}を<b>{f|読|よ}んでばかりいる</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a neutral report of a habit, and in most contexts it is mildly positive - she
has got into reading lately.

(b) says reading has crowded everything else out. Even though reading is a
respectable activity, the pattern makes it sound like an escape or a neglect of
something: she is not going out, not studying, not talking to anyone. The speaker is
worried or exasperated, and that comes entirely from `てばかりいる`.

</details>
