# ～(よ)うとする - be about to

**Meaning** · on the very point of happening - the instant before the event tips over

Same three syllables as the "try to" pattern, but a different job. Here nobody is
trying anything: <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> freezes the frame just before a
change completes. The winter that has not quite ended, the doors that have not
quite shut. Because no will is involved, the subject can be a season, a train or
a storm.

## Formation

This sense almost always wears `ている` or `ていた`, or else feeds into `とき` or
`ところ`. The bare non-past <code>～(よ)うとする</code> does **not** carry the
"about to" reading.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + としている | {f|終|お}わる → {f|終|お}わろうとしている |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + としている | {f|消|き}える → {f|消|き}えようとしている |
| Verb (する) | しよう + としている | {f|発車|はっしゃ}する → {f|発車|はっしゃ}しようとしている |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | こよう + としている | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ようとしている |
| Was on the point of | ～(よ)うとしていた | {f|消|き}えようとしていた |
| Just as it did | ～(よ)うとしたとき | {f|閉|し}まろうとしたとき |
| Right at that moment | ～(よ)うとしたところ(に・へ・を) | {f|出|で}ようとしたところに |
| Polite | している → しています | {f|終|お}わろうとしています |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the verbs that turn up here are overwhelmingly **intransitive verbs of
change**: {f|始|はじ}まる, {f|終|お}わる, {f|沈|しず}む, {f|暮|く}れる,
{f|消|き}える, {f|散|ち}る, {f|倒|たお}れる, {f|変|か}わる. If your verb is one of
those, the "be about to" reading is the only one available.

</div>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

The reliable test for which sense you are in is **the subject**. If it could not
possibly decide anything - a season, a candle, a train, a century - you are in the
"about to" sense, and no amount of context will make it mean "tried to". With a
person, the form is genuinely ambiguous, and this is harmless: both readings point
at the same instant, and the sentence is normally interrupted by something anyway.

The register is **narrative**. This pattern belongs to news copy, novels, essays
and the sort of speech that is consciously describing a scene. In ordinary
conversation people reach for もうすぐ, ところ or `～そう`:
<code>もうすぐ{f|着|つ}くよ</code>, not
<code>{f|着|つ}こうとしているよ</code>. Saying
<code>ご{f|飯|はん}が{f|炊|た}けようとしています</code> to a housemate sounds like
you are narrating a documentary about your rice cooker.

<code>{f|今|いま}にも</code> pairs with it naturally and raises the tension a
notch: <code>{f|今|いま}にも{f|倒|たお}れようとしている</code>. So does
<code>まさに</code> in written Japanese.

One boundary worth marking: this is about a **moment**, not about the near future.
Next week's roadworks are not
<code>{f|始|はじ}まろうとしています</code>; they simply
<code>{f|始|はじ}まります</code>. If you can attach a date to it, you have left the
pattern's territory.

Finally, the `ところ` frame is the most conversational corner of this sense.
<code>～(よ)うとしたところに</code> plus an interruption - 
a phone call, a visitor, rain - is a small, very common storytelling move, and it
survives in casual speech where the plain `ている` form would sound stiff.

## Contrast

### ～(よ)うとしている vs ～ところだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ところだ` needs a subject with a schedule; it is the neutral
"I'm just about to". <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> is a camera angle, and it works
on things that never planned anything.

</div>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}から{f|出|で}かける<b>ところです</b>。
I'm just heading out now.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雪|ゆき}が{f|屋根|やね}から<b>{f|落|お}ちようとしている</b>。
The snow is on the point of sliding off the roof.
</pre>

Swap them and both go wrong. Snow has no `ところ` of its own to be standing at,
and narrating your own departure with
<code>{f|出|で}かけようとしています</code> makes you sound like a character in a
film rather than a person telling a friend where you are.

### ～(よ)うとしている vs ～かけている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> means it has **not started**.
`～かけている` means it **started and stopped partway**.

</div>

<pre>
ろうそくの{f|火|ひ}が<b>{f|消|き}えようとしている</b>。
The candle is about to go out.
</pre>

<pre>
ろうそくの{f|火|ひ}が<b>{f|消|き}えかけている</b>。
The candle is guttering out.
</pre>

The first candle is still burning steadily; you can see that it has almost no wax
left. The second is already halfway gone - the flame is failing as you watch. The
same split explains
<code>{f|読|よ}みかけの{f|本|ほん}</code> (a book you are partway through) against
<code>{f|読|よ}もうとしている{f|本|ほん}</code> (one you have not opened).

### ～(よ)うとしている vs {f|今|いま}にも～そうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "any second now", but `～そうだ` is a **guess read
off the surface** of something, while <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> reports the
event as genuinely underway.

</div>

<pre>
{f|棚|たな}の{f|箱|はこ}が<b>{f|落|お}ちそうです</b>。
That box on the shelf looks like it's going to fall.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|棚|たな}の{f|箱|はこ}が<b>{f|落|お}ちようとしています</b>。
The box on the shelf is on the point of falling.
</pre>

The first is your inference from how the box is sitting; it may sit there all
year. The second commits: the box is going. In warnings and in speech, `そうだ` is
almost always what you want - it is the one that leaves you room to be wrong.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|月|つき}が{f|出|で}**ようとする**。 → ✅ {f|月|つき}が{f|出|で}<b>ようとしている</b>。
  The "about to" reading lives in `ている`. Without it, the plain non-past reads as
  a general statement about attempting, which the moon cannot do.

- ❌ バスが{f|来|き}**ようとしている**。 → ✅ バスが{f|来|こ}<b>ようとしている</b>。
  The volitional of <code>{f|来|く}る</code> is こよう, not きよう. The ます-stem
  reading き belongs to <code>{f|来|き}ます</code> and nowhere near this pattern.

- ❌ {f|来週|らいしゅう}、{f|駅前|えきまえ}の{f|工事|こうじ}が{f|始|はじ}**まろうとしています**。 → ✅ {f|来週|らいしゅう}、{f|駅前|えきまえ}の{f|工事|こうじ}が{f|始|はじ}<b>まります</b>。
  A dated future event is just the plain non-past. This pattern is for the second
  before something happens, not for next week.

- ❌ もうすぐ{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}**ようとしています**。 → ✅ もうすぐ{f|映画|えいが}を{f|見|み}<b>るところです</b>。
  About yourself, with a plan, `ところだ` is the natural form.
  <code>～(よ)うとしている</code> here narrates you from outside, which is why it
  sounds oddly grand.

- ❌ {f|会議|かいぎ}が{f|終|お}わろうと**しましたとき** → ✅ {f|会議|かいぎ}が{f|終|お}わろうと<b>したとき</b>
  A clause in front of `とき` takes the plain form. Politeness goes on the end of
  the sentence, not inside it.

## Practice

**1.** {f|太陽|たいよう}が{f|山|やま}の{f|向|む}こうに{f|沈|しず}____としていた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|太陽|たいよう}が{f|山|やま}の{f|向|む}こうに{f|沈|しず}<b>もう</b>としていた。

<code>{f|沈|しず}む</code> is a う-verb ending in む, so the volitional is
<code>{f|沈|しず}もう</code>. The sun has no will, which confirms this is the "on
the point of" sense rather than an attempt.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|屋根|やね}の{f|雪|ゆき}が（{f|落|お}ちそうです・{f|落|お}ちようとしています）から、{f|下|した}を{f|通|とお}らないでください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|屋根|やね}の{f|雪|ゆき}が<b>{f|落|お}ちそうです</b>から…

A warning is an inference - you are reading the risk off the roof, not announcing
that the snow is already moving. `～そうだ` is the form for that.
<code>{f|落|お}ちようとしています</code> would claim the fall has begun, which
would make "don't walk under it" oddly late advice.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Just as I was about to leave the house, it started to rain."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|家|いえ}を<b>{f|出|で}ようとしたとき</b>、{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}り{f|出|だ}しました。

<code>～(よ)うとしたとき</code> is the standard frame for "just as I was about to,
something cut in". <code>{f|出|で}ようとしたところに</code> works equally well and
sounds a shade more conversational.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|一年|いちねん}が{f|終|お}わろうとしていますので、{f|来年|らいねん}の{f|計画|けいかく}を{f|立|た}てましょう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|一年|いちねん}が{f|終|お}わろうと<b>している</b>ので、{f|来年|らいねん}の{f|計画|けいかく}を{f|立|た}てましょう。

The sentence is fine in meaning; the error is placement of politeness. A clause in
front of `ので` normally takes the plain form, with ます kept for the sentence
ending. <code>{f|終|お}わろうとしていますので</code> is not ungrammatical, but it
belongs to very formal announcements, not to a suggestion among colleagues.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|花|はな}が{f|散|ち}ろうとしている。
(b) {f|花|はな}が{f|散|ち}りかけている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) says none of the petals have come down yet, but the moment is here - the tree
is at its last full day. It is a narrator's sentence, the kind that opens a
paragraph.

(b) says the falling has already begun and is partway through: petals on the
ground, gaps in the branches. `かける` is about an interrupted or incomplete
process, so it reports a state you can photograph, while (a) reports a threshold
about to be crossed.

</details>
