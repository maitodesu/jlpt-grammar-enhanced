# ～か何か - or something

**Meaning** · names one example and deliberately leaves the door open - "X, or something like it"

<code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> is `か` ("or") plus <code>{f|何|なに}か</code> ("something"),
and it means exactly what the parts say: this, or whatever else would do. You use it when
you do not know
（<code>{f|風邪|かぜ}か{f|何|なに}かだろう</code>）, when it does not matter
（<code>お{f|茶|ちゃ}か{f|何|なに}か{f|買|か}ってくる</code>）, or when being precise would
sound demanding. It has a small family - <code>か{f|誰|だれ}か</code> for people and
<code>かどこか</code> for places - built exactly the same way.

## Formation

The whole phrase behaves as a single noun, so any particle attaches after it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - thing | Noun + か{f|何|なに}か | コーヒー → コーヒーか{f|何|なに}か |
| Noun - person | Noun + か{f|誰|だれ}か | {f|田中|たなか}さん → {f|田中|たなか}さんか{f|誰|だれ}か |
| Noun - place | Noun + かどこか | {f|京都|きょうと} → {f|京都|きょうと}かどこか |
| Modifying a noun | ～か{f|何|なに}かの + Noun | {f|病気|びょうき}か{f|何|なに}かの{f|理由|りゆう} |
| With が／を | particle usually **dropped** in speech | ペンか{f|何|なに}か（を）{f|貸|か}して |
| With other particles | particle follows the phrase | {f|山田|やまだ}さんか{f|誰|だれ}か<b>に</b>{f|聞|き}く |
| As a predicate | ～か{f|何|なに}かだ／でしょう | {f|風邪|かぜ}か{f|何|なに}かでしょう |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the question word has to match the category. Things take
<code>{f|何|なに}か</code>, people take <code>{f|誰|だれ}か</code>, places take
`どこか`. ❌<code>{f|田中|たなか}さんか{f|何|なに}か</code> is not vague - it is rude,
because it files a person under "things".

</div>

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

<pre>
お{f|礼|れい}にお{f|菓子|かし}<b>か{f|何|なに}か</b>{f|送|おく}ろうと{f|思|おも}っています。

</pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is neutral in politeness and extremely common in speech. What it adds is
**vagueness on purpose**, and vagueness is a social tool. Asking
<code>ペンか{f|何|なに}か{f|貸|か}してもらえますか</code> is softer than asking for a pen,
because you have told the listener that anything will do and left them a way to help
without going to trouble. The same applies to gifts, invitations and suggestions:
<code>お{f|茶|ちゃ}か{f|何|なに}かどうですか</code> commits nobody to anything.

The other main use is a guess you cannot verify - 
<code>{f|工事|こうじ}か{f|何|なに}かでしょう</code>, <code>{f|病気|びょうき}か{f|何|なに}かだったらしい</code>.
Here the phrase protects you from asserting something you do not know, which is why it
appears so often with `でしょう`, `かな`, `らしい` and `みたい`.

Two register limits. In formal writing, `など` does this job instead:
<code>{f|身分証明書|みぶんしょうめいしょ}などをご{f|用意|ようい}ください</code>, never
<code>か{f|何|なに}か</code>. And pointing it at a person is risky. The correct member of
the family for people is <code>か{f|誰|だれ}か</code>, and even that should be used for
someone not present - saying it about the person in front of you sounds like they are
interchangeable.

## Contrast

### ～か{f|何|なに}か vs でも

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `でも` is for **offers and suggestions**: "how about X or something?"
<code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> is for **reference**: naming a thing loosely because the exact
identity is unknown or unimportant.

</div>

<pre>
お{f|茶|ちゃ}<b>でも</b>{f|飲|の}みに{f|行|い}きませんか。
Shall we go for a cup of tea or something?
</pre>

<pre>
のどが{f|渇|かわ}いたので、お{f|茶|ちゃ}<b>か{f|何|なに}か</b>{f|買|か}ってきます。
I'm thirsty, so I'll go and buy some tea or something.
</pre>

The first is an invitation, and `でも` is what makes it casual enough to refuse. The
second is a plan of your own, where there is nothing to be polite about - and `でも` there
would sound as though you were inviting the tea. As a rough guide: if the sentence ends in
「～ませんか」 or 「～ましょう」, use `でも`.

### ～か{f|何|なに}か vs とか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `とか` gives **two or more** examples of a set.
<code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> gives **one**, and leaves the rest blank.

</div>

<pre>
{f|果物|くだもの}<b>とか</b>お{f|菓子|かし}<b>とか</b>を{f|持|も}っていこう。
Let's take fruit or sweets or something like that.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|果物|くだもの}<b>か{f|何|なに}か</b>を{f|持|も}っていこう。
Let's take fruit or something.
</pre>

`とか` sketches the shape of the category by listing; <code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> points
at one member and shrugs. Both are casual, and both are avoided in formal writing.

### ～か{f|何|なに}か vs {f|何|なに}か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|何|なに}か</code> alone means "something" with **no anchor**.
<code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> needs a noun in front of it to be an alternative *to*.

</div>

<pre>
{f|冷蔵庫|れいぞうこ}に<b>{f|何|なに}か</b>{f|残|のこ}っていますか。
Is there anything left in the fridge?
</pre>

<pre>
{f|冷蔵庫|れいぞうこ}に{f|卵|たまご}<b>か{f|何|なに}か</b>{f|残|のこ}っていますか。
Are there eggs or something left in the fridge?
</pre>

The first asks about the contents in general. The second has a specific thing in mind and
is willing to accept a substitute - which, incidentally, makes it the more useful sentence
when you are about to cook.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|何|なに}かコーヒー{f|飲|の}む? → ✅ コーヒー<b>か{f|何|なに}か</b>{f|飲|の}む?
  The example noun comes **first** and <code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> follows it. Putting
  <code>{f|何|なに}か</code> in front produces "something coffee", which is not a phrase.

- ❌ {f|田中|たなか}さんか{f|何|なに}かに{f|聞|き}いてみます。 → ✅ {f|田中|たなか}さん<b>か{f|誰|だれ}か</b>に{f|聞|き}いてみます。
  People take <code>か{f|誰|だれ}か</code>. Using
  <code>{f|何|なに}か</code> for a person is not merely wrong; it is insulting.

- ❌ {f|果物|くだもの}や{f|何|なに}か{f|買|か}ってきて。 → ✅ {f|果物|くだもの}<b>か</b>{f|何|なに}か{f|買|か}ってきて。
  The particle is `か` ("or"), not `や`. `や` lists things that are all actually present.

- ❌ {f|申込書|もうしこみしょ}か{f|何|なに}かをご{f|提出|ていしゅつ}ください。 → ✅ {f|申込書|もうしこみしょ}<b>など</b>をご{f|提出|ていしゅつ}ください。
  In formal or written instructions, `など` is the vague-example word.
  <code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> sounds too offhand when you are telling someone what to
  submit.

## Practice

**1.** かぜをひいたみたいだから、{f|薬|くすり}____{f|飲|の}んで{f|早|はや}く{f|寝|ね}ます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|薬|くすり}<b>か{f|何|なに}か</b>{f|飲|の}んで{f|早|はや}く{f|寝|ね}ます。

You are naming one obvious option and leaving room for whatever is actually in the cupboard.
Note that `を` is comfortably dropped here, as it usually is in speech.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|時間|じかん}があったら、{f|映画|えいが}（か{f|何|なに}か・でも）{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}きませんか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|映画|えいが}<b>でも</b>{f|見|み}に{f|行|い}きませんか。

The sentence is an invitation ending in 「～ませんか」, and `でも` is the particle that makes
an invitation easy to decline. <code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> would sound as though you were
listing options rather than asking.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Could you ask Suzuki or somebody where the meeting room is?"

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|鈴木|すずき}さん<b>か{f|誰|だれ}か</b>に{f|会議室|かいぎしつ}がどこか{f|聞|き}いてもらえますか。

A person takes <code>か{f|誰|だれ}か</code>, and the particle `に` attaches after the whole
phrase, not inside it.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}に{f|何|なに}かはさみ{f|置|お}いていませんでしたか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}にはさみ<b>か{f|何|なに}か</b>{f|置|お}いていませんでしたか。

The word order was reversed. The concrete example goes first and
<code>か{f|何|なに}か</code> trails it; <code>{f|何|なに}か</code> on its own cannot sit in
front of another noun.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|何|なに}か{f|持|も}っていったほうがいいですか。
(b) {f|会議|かいぎ}に{f|資料|しりょう}<b>か{f|何|なに}か</b>{f|持|も}っていったほうがいいですか。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is an open question: is there anything at all I should bring? The asker has no idea
what would be appropriate.

(b) shows that the asker has already guessed - handouts, probably - and is checking while
leaving room to be corrected. It sounds better prepared, and it gives the listener an
easier question to answer, which is why it is the more natural thing to say to a colleague.

</details>
