# ～わけがない

**Meaning** · there's no way ～; it's impossible that ～ - denies that any path of reasoning could lead to the thing being true

Keep the literal sense in view: <code>わけ</code> is the {f|訳|わけ}, the reason or
logic by which something could come about, and `がない` says there is none. So
`わけがない` does not say "unlikely" or "I doubt it". It says the door is shut. This
is the strongest denial in the わけ family, and it is the one learners over-use.

**What is negated: the possibility itself.** That single fact separates it from
`わけではない`, which leaves the possibility standing and denies only an inference.

## Formation

<code>わけ</code> is a noun, so the clause in front takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + わけがない | {f|勝|か}てる → {f|勝|か}てるわけがない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけがない | {f|気|き}づかない → {f|気|き}づかないわけがない |
| い-adjective | plain form + わけがない | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いわけがない |
| な-adjective | ～な + わけがない | {f|簡単|かんたん}な → {f|簡単|かんたん}なわけがない |
| Noun | ～な / ～という + わけがない | {f|本物|ほんもの} → {f|本物|ほんもの}なわけがない |
| Slightly softer / more written | … + わけはない | {f|忘|わす}れる → {f|忘|わす}れるわけはない |
| Polite | … + わけがありません | {f|届|とど}く → {f|届|とど}くわけがありません |
| Casual speech | … + わけない | できる → できるわけないよ |

## Examples

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもにこの{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|読|よ}める<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way a child can read this kanji.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|人|ひと}がうそをつく<b>わけがありません</b>。
He would never lie - it's simply not possible.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|練習|れんしゅう}もしないで、{f|試合|しあい}に{f|勝|か}てる<b>わけがない</b>よ。
You don't practise, so of course you can't win the match.
</pre>

<pre>
こんなに{f|甘|あま}いのに、カロリーが{f|低|ひく}い<b>わけがない</b>。
As sweet as this is, there is no chance it's low in calories.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一度|いちど}{f|会|あ}っただけの{f|人|ひと}の{f|名前|なまえ}を{f|覚|おぼ}えている<b>わけがない</b>じゃないか。
Come on, nobody remembers the name of someone they met once.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|説明書|せつめいしょ}も{f|読|よ}まずに{f|組|く}み{f|立|た}てて、うまくいく<b>わけがない</b>でしょう。
Putting it together without even reading the instructions - that was never going to work.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|夜中|よなか}まで{f|残業|ざんぎょう}が{f|続|つづ}いているのだから、{f|体|からだ}に{f|影響|えいきょう}が{f|出|で}ない<b>わけがない</b>。
With overtime running until the middle of the night every day, it cannot possibly be leaving him unaffected.
</pre>

<pre>
あれほど{f|楽|たの}しみにしていたのだから、{f|中止|ちゅうし}と{f|聞|き}いて{f|娘|むすめ}ががっかりしない<b>わけがありません</b>。
She'd been looking forward to it so much that there is no way our daughter isn't crushed to hear it's cancelled.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a forceful, emotionally coloured pattern. It does not weigh evidence the way
`はずがない` does; it announces that the speaker regards the idea as out of the
question. That makes it excellent for arguing, for reassuring - 
<code>{f|忘|わす}れるわけがないよ</code> - and for sarcasm, and risky when the thing you are ruling out is
what the person in front of you just said. Contradicting a superior with
<code>そんなわけがありません</code> is blunt to the point of rudeness; softer options are
<code>そうとは{f|思|おも}えません</code> or <code>はずがありません</code>.

Casual speech drops `が`: <code>できるわけない</code>, <code>{f|知|し}ってるわけないじゃん</code>.
That short form is very common among friends and carries a shrug with it. Going the
other way, `わけはない` - with `は` instead of `が` - reads a shade calmer and more
written, because the `は` gives it a whiff of contrast rather than flat denial.

Two neighbours to keep separate. <code>わけにはいかない</code> looks similar and means
something else entirely - "I can't, for reasons of duty or circumstance" - and
`わけではない` is the partial denial treated on its own page. Confusing `わけがない`
with `わけではない` is the single most common error in this corner of the grammar, so
the first contrast below is worth reading twice.

## Contrast

### わけがない vs わけではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけがない` denies **the thing itself**: it cannot be true.
`わけではない` denies **an inference**: the thing may well be partly true, but it
does not follow the way you assumed.

</div>

<pre>
{f|大好|だいす}きな{f|先輩|せんぱい}に{f|会|あ}えるのに、{f|嬉|うれ}しくない<b>わけがない</b>。
I get to see the senior I adore - there's no way I'm not delighted.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会|あ}えて{f|嬉|うれ}しいが、{f|緊張|きんちょう}していない<b>わけではない</b>。
I'm glad to see her, though that isn't to say I'm not nervous.
</pre>

Try the substitution test. Replace `わけではない` with "it's not that…" and the
sentence should still work; replace `わけがない` with "there's no way…" and *that*
should work. If both readings feel possible, look at what the speaker is doing: are
they rejecting a claim outright, or trimming an over-reading?

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}が{f|犯人|はんにん}である<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way he's the culprit.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}を{f|疑|うたが}っている<b>わけではない</b>。
It's not that I suspect him.
</pre>

The first defends him absolutely. The second only corrects an impression you may
have given - you are not accusing him, but you have not said he is innocent either.
That gap between the two is exactly the gap between the patterns.

### わけがない vs はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `はずがない` argues **from a known fact**: given X, Y cannot
follow. `わけがない` states the speaker's **conviction** that Y is impossible, with
or without an argument.

</div>

<pre>
{f|鍵|かぎ}は{f|私|わたし}が{f|持|も}っていたのだから、{f|誰|だれ}かが{f|入|はい}れた<b>はずがない</b>。
I had the key on me, so nobody can have got in.
</pre>

<pre>
あんなに{f|優|やさ}しい{f|人|ひと}が、そんなひどいことを{f|言|い}う<b>わけがない</b>。
Someone that kind would never say something so cruel.
</pre>

The first is a piece of reasoning you could write out step by step. The second is
conviction about a person's character, and it would be odd to demand the speaker's
evidence. In many sentences either pattern is acceptable and the difference is
temperature: `はずがない` cool, `わけがない` warm. Note also that `はずがない` more
comfortably admits it was wrong - <code>はずがないと{f|思|おも}っていたのに</code> - while
`わけがない` sounds strange doing so.

### わけがない vs わけにはいかない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけがない` is about what is **possible**. `わけにはいかない` is
about what is **permissible**: you could do it, but circumstances or obligation stop
you.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|大事|だいじ}な{f|試験|しけん}だから、{f|休|やす}む<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
There's an important exam tomorrow, so I can't very well take the day off.
</pre>

<pre>
あの{f|忙|いそが}しさで、ゆっくり{f|休|やす}める<b>わけがない</b>。
As busy as that, there's no chance of getting any real rest.
</pre>

In the first the speaker is perfectly able to stay home; what rules it out is what
is at stake. In the second nothing is stopping him except reality. Because the two
forms sit one syllable apart, they get swapped constantly - check whether you mean
"cannot happen" or "mustn't happen".

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この{f|仕事|しごと}が{f|簡単|かんたん}<b>だわけがない</b>。 → ✅ この{f|仕事|しごと}が{f|簡単|かんたん}<b>なわけがない</b>。
  A な-adjective takes `な` before <code>わけ</code>. The same goes for nouns, which
  take `な` or `という`.

- ❌ {f|嫌|きら}いな<b>わけがない</b>が、{f|毎日|まいにち}は{f|食|た}べたくない。 → ✅ {f|嫌|きら}いな<b>わけではない</b>が、{f|毎日|まいにち}は{f|食|た}べたくない。
  The speaker means "it's not that I dislike it" - a partial denial. `わけがない`
  would claim disliking it is impossible, which clashes with the second half.

- ❌ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>わけがない</b>から、{f|傘|かさ}はいらないだろう。 → ✅ {f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}り<b>そうもない</b>から、{f|傘|かさ}はいらないだろう。
  Tomorrow's weather is uncertain, not impossible. `わけがない` is far too absolute
  for a forecast.

- ❌ {f|車|くるま}で{f|来|き}たので、お{f|酒|さけ}を{f|飲|の}む<b>わけがない</b>。 → ✅ {f|車|くるま}で{f|来|き}たので、お{f|酒|さけ}を{f|飲|の}む<b>わけにはいかない</b>。
  You are able to drink; you must not. Impossibility and prohibition are different
  patterns.

## Practice

**1.** {f|徹夜|てつや}で{f|作|つく}った{f|資料|しりょう}だ。{f|間違|まちが}いが{f|一|ひと}つもない____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|間違|まちが}いが{f|一|ひと}つもない<b>わけがない</b>。

Something written overnight cannot plausibly be flawless, and the speaker rules the
idea out rather than merely doubting it - the categorical denial `わけがない`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|甘|あま}いものが（{f|嫌|きら}いなわけがない・{f|嫌|きら}いなわけではない）が、{f|健康|けんこう}のために{f|控|ひか}えている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|甘|あま}いものが<b>{f|嫌|きら}いなわけではない</b>が、{f|健康|けんこう}のために{f|控|ひか}えている。

The second half admits the speaker avoids sweets, so the first half is correcting a
likely misreading - "not that I dislike them". `わけがない` would declare disliking
them impossible and leave the `が` clause hanging.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He only started last month - there's no way he knows the whole system."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|先月|せんげつ}{f|入|はい}ったばかりの{f|人|ひと}が、システム{f|全体|ぜんたい}を{f|分|わ}かっている<b>わけがない</b>。

The point is impossibility, not doubt, so `わけがない` (or, arguing more coolly from
the same fact, <code>はずがありません</code>).

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|大切|たいせつ}な{f|会議|かいぎ}だから、{f|遅|おく}れるわけがない。（intended: "I mustn't be late"）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|大切|たいせつ}な{f|会議|かいぎ}だから、{f|遅|おく}れる<b>わけにはいかない</b>。

As written, the sentence claims that being late is impossible. What the speaker
means is that it is not allowed to happen, and that is `わけにはいかない`.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}の{f|気持|きも}ちが{f|分|わ}からない<b>わけがない</b>。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}の{f|気持|きも}ちが{f|分|わ}からない<b>わけではない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) insists that failing to understand him is impossible - you know him too well, or
the situation is too obvious. It is emphatic, and often reproachful: "of course you
understand how he feels."

(b) concedes the opposite way round. You do, to some extent, understand him; the
sentence heads off the assumption that you are unsympathetic, and it almost always
continues with が or けど into whatever the objection really is.

</details>
