# ～たて

**Meaning** · freshly ～ed; only just ～ed

`～たて` says a thing came out of some process **moments ago and is still in that
condition**. Bread out of the oven, rice out of the cooker, paint still wet, a
graduate still blinking at the world. The freshness is presented as a quality of the
thing, and it is a quality that will fade.

## Formation

`たて` attaches to the **ます-stem** and produces a **noun**.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (ます-stem) | ます-stem + たて | {f|焼|や}きます → {f|焼|や}きたて |
| する-verb | Noun + し + たて | {f|卒業|そつぎょう}します → {f|卒業|そつぎょう}したて |
| Before a noun | ～たて + の + Noun | {f|焼|や}きたてのパン |
| Predicate | ～たて + だ / です | ご{f|飯|はん}は{f|炊|た}きたてです |
| Joining a clause | ～たて + で | {f|習|なら}いたてで、まだ{f|下手|へた}だ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - this is the most **lexically restricted** suffix in the group. Two small
families use it and nothing else does. Things coming out of a **process**:
{f|焼|や}きたて・{f|揚|あ}げたて・{f|炊|た}きたて・{f|入|い}れたて・{f|塗|ぬ}りたて・できたて・とれたて・{f|生|う}まれたて.
People who have just **entered a status**: {f|習|なら}いたて・{f|覚|おぼ}えたて・{f|卒業|そつぎょう}したて・{f|入社|にゅうしゃ}したて・なりたて.
Outside those, it simply is not said: ❌<code>{f|買|か}いたて</code>,
❌<code>{f|読|よ}みたて</code>, ❌<code>{f|見|み}たて</code>. (The existing words
{f|見立|みた}て, {f|仕立|した}て and {f|組|く}み{f|立|た}て are separate vocabulary,
not this suffix.)

</div>

## Nuance & register

`たて` is a **warm, appetising** word, and it is the only suffix in this group that
is reliably positive. Shops and menus lean on it - 
{f|焼|や}きたてパン, できたてのお{f|弁当|べんとう} - because it promises the best
version of the thing. The two combinations {f|焼|や}きたて and できたて are so
entrenched that they function as ordinary nouns.

The freshness has a **short shelf life** built in, and this is what makes it
different from "new". Bread is {f|焼|や}きたて for an hour, not a week. A person is
{f|卒業|そつぎょう}したて for a few months. If the interval you mean is long, the
word is wrong.

Applied to people, the tone is affectionate but a little indulgent:
{f|新入社員|しんにゅうしゃいん}なりたて and {f|習|なら}いたて both carry "and it
shows". It is not an insult, but it is not a compliment on competence either.

There is no register restriction - {f|塗|ぬ}りたて appears on public signs,
{f|揚|あ}げたて on menus, {f|覚|おぼ}えたて in casual conversation. Some
combinations take the kanji {f|立|た}て in writing（{f|焼|や}き{f|立|た}て）, but kana
is far more usual.

## Contrast

### ～たて vs ～たばかり

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>たばかり</code> works with **any verb** and measures time
since an event. `たて` works with a **short fixed list** and describes the thing as
being in a fresh condition.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|車|くるま}は{f|先週|せんしゅう}{f|買|か}っ<b>たばかり</b>だ。
I only bought this car last week.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|朝|あさ}{f|六時|ろくじ}の{f|店|みせ}には、<b>{f|焼|や}きたて</b>のパンが{f|並|なら}んでいた。
At six in the morning the shop had freshly baked bread lined up.
</pre>

Where both are available, they say slightly different things.
{f|焼|や}いたばかりのパン tells you when the baking happened;
{f|焼|や}きたてのパン tells you what the bread is like. The second is what you want
on a sign, and the first is what you want in a sentence about your morning.

The safe rule for production: if the combination is not one of the fixed set, use
<code>たばかり</code>. ❌<code>{f|買|か}いたての{f|車|くるま}</code> is not Japanese;
<code>{f|買|か}ったばかりの{f|車|くるま}</code> is.

### ～たて vs {f|新|あたら}しい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|新|あたら}しい</code> means not old. `たて` means
straight out of the process that made it, with the heat still in it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|新|あたら}しいパンを{f|二|ふた}つください。
Two of the fresh loaves, please.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|昼|ひる}すぎに{f|行|い}くと、もう<b>{f|焼|や}きたて</b>は{f|残|のこ}っていない。
If you go after midday, there's nothing left that's just out of the oven.
</pre>

<code>{f|新|あたら}しい</code> covers anything baked today, and it would still be true
tomorrow morning relative to last week's loaf. `たて` is a claim about minutes. This
is also why <code>{f|新|あたら}しい</code> can describe things that were never made - 
{f|新|あたら}しい{f|山|やま}の{f|道|みち} - while `たて` needs a process and an agent.

### ～たて vs ～かけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two ends of one action. `かけ` = begun and **abandoned
partway**; `たて` = **just completed**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|飲|の}み<b>かけ</b>のお{f|茶|ちゃ}が、まだテーブルに{f|残|のこ}っていた。
There was a half-drunk cup of tea still on the table.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|入|い}れ<b>たて</b>のお{f|茶|ちゃ}を、{f|来客|らいきゃく}に{f|出|だ}した。
I served the visitors tea I'd just made.
</pre>

Structurally they are twins: both sit on the ます-stem, both produce a noun, both
take `の` before a following noun. The difference is entirely one of where in the
action they point - and, as a result, of mood. A {f|飲|の}みかけ cup is a small
domestic mess; an {f|入|い}れたて cup is hospitality.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|焼|や}く<b>たて</b>のパン → ✅ {f|焼|や}き<b>たて</b>のパン
  `たて` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.

- ❌ {f|焼|や}きたて<b>な</b>パン → ✅ {f|焼|や}きたて<b>の</b>パン
  The result is a noun and links with `の`.

- ❌ {f|買|か}い<b>たて</b>のパソコン → ✅ {f|買|か}っ<b>たばかり</b>のパソコン
  Buying is not a process that leaves a thing freshly made, and
  <code>{f|買|か}いたて</code> is not used. The productive pattern is
  <code>たばかり</code>.

- ❌ {f|去年|きょねん}{f|卒業|そつぎょう}したてなので、まだ{f|仕事|しごと}に{f|慣|な}れていません。 → ✅ {f|先月|せんげつ}{f|卒業|そつぎょう}したてなので、まだ{f|仕事|しごと}に{f|慣|な}れていません。
  The freshness must be genuinely recent. A year is too long for `たて`; use
  <code>{f|去年|きょねん}{f|卒業|そつぎょう}したばかり</code> if you really mean a
  year ago.

- ❌ この{f|服|ふく}は{f|洗|あら}いたてで{f|古|ふる}いです。 → ✅ この{f|服|ふく}は{f|洗|あら}いたてでいいにおいがします。
  `たて` is a positive word about a fresh state. Pairing it with a negative
  assessment of the same thing pulls the sentence in two directions.

## Practice

**1.** {f|駅前|えきまえ}のパン{f|屋|や}では、{f|夕方|ゆうがた}に____のパンが{f|出|で}る。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|夕方|ゆうがた}に<b>{f|焼|や}きたて</b>のパンが{f|出|で}る。

<code>{f|焼|や}く</code> gives the stem <code>{f|焼|や}き</code>, and
<code>{f|焼|や}きたて</code> is the most standard member of this small family. Note
the `の` linking it to <code>パン</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|山田|やまだ}さんは（{f|入社|にゅうしゃ}したて・{f|入社|にゅうしゃ}しかけ）なので、まだ{f|社内|しゃない}のことがよく{f|分|わ}かっていない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|山田|やまだ}さんは<b>{f|入社|にゅうしゃ}したて</b>なので…

He has joined - the process is complete and he is fresh in the role, which is `たて`.
<code>{f|入社|にゅうしゃ}しかけ</code> would mean he started joining and stopped
partway, which is not a situation that exists.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Careful - the bench is freshly painted."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|気|き}をつけて。ベンチはペンキ<b>{f|塗|ぬ}りたて</b>ですよ。

<code>ペンキ{f|塗|ぬ}りたて</code> is the set phrase you will actually see on signs
in Japan. The result is a noun, so です attaches to it directly with no `の` and no
`な`.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|去年|きょねん}{f|買|か}いたてのカメラが、もう{f|動|うご}かなくなった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|去年|きょねん}{f|買|か}ったばかりのカメラが、もう{f|動|うご}かなくなった。

Two things are wrong. <code>{f|買|か}う</code> is not one of the verbs that takes
`たて`, and a year ago is far too long for the freshness `たて` claims.
<code>たばかり</code> fixes both - it is productive and it can stretch to a year when
the speaker feels it was recent.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) できたてのカレーを{f|出|だ}してくれた。
(b) {f|作|つく}ったばかりのカレーを{f|出|だ}してくれた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) presents the curry as being in its best possible state - hot, just off the
stove, the way a restaurant would advertise it. The focus is on what the food is
like now, and the sentence carries a small note of pleasure.

(b) simply reports when it was made: not long before it was served. It is the more
neutral, factual phrasing, and it would fit equally well in a sentence explaining
why there was none left over.

</details>
