# ～そうもない

**Meaning** · there's no sign of it happening; very unlikely to ～ - the negative of the "looks like it will" そうだ, and considerably stronger than a plain "probably not"

`～そうもない` says that you have looked at the situation and can see **no prospect
at all** of the thing happening. It is not a calculation and not a rumour: it is
what the evidence in front of you looks like. Because the `も` is a "not even"
particle, the pattern lands harder than <code>～ないだろう</code> - the rain is not
merely unlikely to stop, it shows no sign whatsoever of stopping.

## Formation

This is a **verb** pattern. It attaches to the ます-stem, exactly like the
"looks like" そうだ it negates.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + そうもない | {f|止|や}みます → {f|止|や}みそうもない |
| Verb, common variant | ます-stem + そうにない | {f|終|お}わります → {f|終|お}わりそうにない |
| Verb, emphatic variant | ます-stem + そうにもない | {f|勝|か}てます → {f|勝|か}てそうにもない |
| Potential verb (very common) | potential ます-stem + そうもない | {f|着|つ}けます → {f|着|つ}けそうもない |
| Polite | … + そうもありません / そうにありません | {f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}います → {f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}いそうもありません |
| い-adjective (use なさそう instead) | ～く + なさそうだ | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}くなさそうだ |
| な-adjective / noun (use なさそう instead) | ～では + なさそうだ | {f|簡単|かんたん} → {f|簡単|かんたん}ではなさそうだ |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the plain "looks like it will" そうだ has an irregular pair worth keeping
straight: いい → よさそう, ない → なさそう. Those `さ` insertions are what produce the
adjective negatives in the last two rows.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}は{f|当分|とうぶん}{f|止|や}み<b>そうもない</b>。
There's no sign of the rain stopping any time soon.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|今日中|きょうじゅう}に{f|終|お}わり<b>そうにない</b>。
This job isn't going to get finished today by the look of it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|席|せき}が{f|空|あ}き<b>そうもない</b>から、{f|別|べつ}の{f|店|みせ}にしよう。
No sign of a table coming free - let's try somewhere else.
</pre>

<pre>
どんなに{f|考|かんが}えても、いい{f|案|あん}が{f|出|で}てき<b>そうにもない</b>。

</pre>

<pre>

The traffic is dreadful - I don't think I can possibly get there for the time we agreed.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

The parts still haven't arrived, so there's no realistic chance of making next week's launch.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral. You will hear it from a site foreman, a schoolchild and a news
reporter alike, and the polite forms `そうもありません` / `そうにありません` are
ordinary business Japanese for delivering bad news gently - the fault lies with the
situation, not with you.

The three shapes are near-identical in meaning. `そうにない` is the plainest,
`そうもない` adds the "not even" flavour of `も`, and `そうにもない` piles both on and
is the most emphatic. Treat this as a tendency rather than a rule: in most sentences
the three are freely interchangeable, and speakers vary.

Two things follow from `そうもない` being the negative of *looks-like* そうだ. First,
it is about **observable prospects**, so it wants a subject whose behaviour you can
watch: rain, a queue, a deadline, a person's progress. It sits badly on abstract
impossibility, which belongs to `わけがない`. Second, it does not mean "I hear that
… not". The hearsay そうだ negates inside the clause instead:
<code>{f|来|こ}ないそうだ</code> - "I hear they aren't coming."

The pattern loves potential verbs, and that combination is the everyday way to
decline gracefully: <code>{f|今週|こんしゅう}は{f|行|い}けそうもありません</code>
softens "I can't come" into "it doesn't look as though I'll manage it."

## Contrast

### ～そうもない vs ～なさそうだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` negates the *appearance of it happening*;
`なさそうだ` negates the *verb* and then reports how that looks. The first says "no
sign of it"; the second says "looks like not".

</div>

<pre>
バスは{f|来|き}<b>そうもない</b>。
There's no sign of a bus coming at all.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|時間|じかん}だと、バスは{f|来|こ}<b>なさそうだ</b>ね。
At this hour it looks like no bus is coming.
</pre>

Both are natural, and the difference is one of weight. The first is what you say
after twenty minutes at the stop, with impatience; the second is a calmer reading of
the timetable and the empty road. With **adjectives** you have no choice - only the
`なさそう` route exists, which is why
❌<code>{f|高|たか}そうもない</code> should be
<code>{f|高|たか}くなさそうだ</code>.

### ～そうもない vs ～ないだろう

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` is grounded in what you can see right now.
`ないだろう` is a guess that needs no evidence at all.

</div>

<pre>
{f|明日|あした}は{f|雪|ゆき}は{f|降|ふ}ら<b>ないだろう</b>。
It probably won't snow tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|空|そら}が{f|明|あか}るくなってきた。もう{f|降|ふ}り<b>そうもない</b>。
The sky's brightening. It doesn't look like it's going to come down any more.
</pre>

Because `そうもない` reports signs, it usually talks about the near future or a
situation already under way. A guess about next year takes `ないだろう`; a guess
about the next twenty minutes, with the sky in front of you, takes `そうもない`.

### ～そうもない vs わけがない・はずがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` says there is **no sign** of it. `わけがない` and
`はずがない` say it is **impossible**. Unlikely is not the same as ruled out.

</div>

<pre>
{f|今|いま}から{f|始|はじ}めても、{f|今日中|きょうじゅう}には{f|終|お}わり<b>そうもない</b>。
Even if we start now, it doesn't look like finishing today.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}でこの{f|量|りょう}を{f|運|はこ}べる<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way one person can shift this much.
</pre>

The first leaves the door open - a miracle, extra hands, a shorter route. The second
slams it. Learners reach for `わけがない` when they only mean the first, and it comes
out sounding much more forceful than they intended.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}る<b>そうもない</b>。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}り<b>そうもない</b>。
  It attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form plus
  そうだ is the *hearsay* pattern, which is a different thing entirely.

- ❌ この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|簡単|かんたん}<b>そうもない</b>。 → ✅ この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|簡単|かんたん}<b>ではなさそうだ</b>。
  Adjectives do not take `そうもない`. Negate the adjective first, then add そう.

- ❌ {f|今日中|きょうじゅう}に{f|終|お}わら<b>なそうもない</b>。 → ✅ {f|今日中|きょうじゅう}に{f|終|お}わり<b>そうもない</b>。
  The negative is already inside `そうもない`. Negating the verb as well leaves you
  saying it looks like it will finish.

- ❌ {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|来|く}る<b>そうもない</b>（meaning "I hear he isn't coming"）。 → ✅ {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|来|こ}ない<b>そうだ</b>。
  For reported information, put the negative in the clause and keep plain `そうだ`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|風|かぜ}がどんどん{f|強|つよ}くなっている。この{f|様子|ようす}では{f|船|ふね}は{f|出|で}____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|船|ふね}は{f|出|で}<b>そうもない</b>（or {f|出|で}そうにない）。

The wind is visible evidence, and the speaker reads it as leaving no prospect of the
boat sailing. <code>{f|出|で}る</code> is a る-verb, so its ます-stem is <code>{f|出|で}</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|古|ふる}いけれど、（{f|高|たか}そうもない・{f|高|たか}くなさそうだ）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|古|ふる}いけれど、<b>{f|高|たか}くなさそうだ</b>。

<code>{f|高|たか}い</code> is an adjective, and `そうもない` only attaches to verb stems.
The adjective route is to negate first - <code>{f|高|たか}くない</code> - and then add
そう, which inserts さ: <code>{f|高|たか}くなさそう</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm afraid I won't be able to make it to Saturday's meeting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|申|もう}し{f|訳|わけ}ありませんが、{f|土曜日|どようび}の{f|会議|かいぎ}には{f|出席|しゅっせき}でき<b>そうもありません</b>。

The potential form plus `そうもありません` is the standard polite way to decline: it
presents the refusal as a reading of the circumstances rather than as a flat "no".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|今日|きょう}{f|来|く}るそうもないと{f|聞|き}きました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}は{f|今日|きょう}{f|来|こ}<b>ないそうです</b>。

Two things went wrong. `そうもない` needs the ます-stem
（<code>{f|来|き}そうもない</code>）, and more importantly the sentence is reporting
what you were told, which is the hearsay そうだ with the negative inside the clause.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼|かれ}が{f|試合|しあい}に{f|勝|か}て<b>そうもない</b>。
(b) {f|彼|かれ}が{f|試合|しあい}に{f|勝|か}てる<b>わけがない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a reading of what you can see - the score, his condition, the opponent. It
says the prospects look bad, and it leaves room for a surprise.

(b) declares the thing impossible on principle, and it carries a note of contempt or
exasperation that (a) does not have. Said in front of him, (a) is a discouraged
observation and (b) is an insult.

</details>
