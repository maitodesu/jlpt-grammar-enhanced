# ～向き - suited to

**Meaning** · suitable for; a good fit for - it happens to suit that person, group or purpose, whether or not anyone planned it that way（むき）

This is the same <code>{f|向|む}き</code> that gives you
<code>{f|南|みなみ}{f|向|む}きの{f|部屋|へや}</code>, but with the direction sense worn
away. Here it says: **this thing and that audience match**. A book that suits
beginners, a dish that suits summer, a job that suits people who like talking.

The key word is *happens to*. <code>{f|向|む}き</code> is the speaker's assessment of
a fit. It makes no claim that anybody designed the thing for that audience - and
that is exactly what separates it from <code>{f|向|む}け</code>, which does.

## Formation

Attaches to a bare noun: a person, a group, a season, an activity, a purpose.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person or group) | Noun + {f|向|む}きだ | {f|初心者|しょしんしゃ} → この{f|本|ほん}は{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}{f|向|む}きだ |
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + {f|向|む}きの + Noun | {f|家族|かぞく} → {f|家族|かぞく}{f|向|む}きのコース |
| Noun (season or purpose) | Noun + {f|向|む}きだ | {f|夏|なつ} → {f|夏|なつ}{f|向|む}きの{f|服|ふく} |
| Negative | Noun + {f|向|む}きではない | {f|子|こ}ども → {f|子|こ}ども{f|向|む}きではない |
| Unsuited (one word) | {f|不向|ふむ}きだ | {f|私|わたし} → {f|私|わたし}には{f|不向|ふむ}きだ |
| Fixed noun pair | {f|向|む}き{f|不向|ふむ}き | {f|仕事|しごと} → {f|仕事|しごと}には{f|向|む}き{f|不向|ふむ}きがある |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - before a noun it is <code>{f|向|む}きの</code>, not
<code>{f|向|む}きな</code>. The `な` form belongs only to
<code>{f|前|まえ}{f|向|む}き</code> and <code>{f|後|うし}ろ{f|向|む}き</code> in their
figurative "positive / negative" sense, which have hardened into adjectives:
<code>{f|前|まえ}{f|向|む}きな{f|意見|いけん}</code>.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
この{f|本|ほん}は{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>です。
This book suits beginners.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|辛|から}い{f|料理|りょうり}は{f|子|こ}ども<b>{f|向|む}き</b>ではない。
Spicy food isn't right for children.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|服|ふく}は{f|軽|かる}くて{f|涼|すず}しいので、{f|夏|なつ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>だ。
These clothes are light and cool, so they're good for summer.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|静|しず}かで{f|席|せき}も{f|広|ひろ}いから、{f|勉強|べんきょう}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>の{f|店|みせ}だと{f|思|おも}う。
It's quiet and the seats are roomy, so I'd call it a good café for studying.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|山|やま}は{f|道|みち}がよく{f|整|ととの}っていて、{f|家族|かぞく}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>のコースが{f|多|おお}い。
The paths on this mountain are well maintained, and many of the routes suit families.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|説明|せつめい}が{f|細|こま}かすぎて、{f|入門書|にゅうもんしょ}としては{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>とは{f|言|い}えない。
The explanations are too fine-grained; as an introductory book it can't really be called beginner-friendly.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|営業|えいぎょう}は{f|人|ひと}と{f|話|はな}すのが{f|好|す}きな{f|人|ひと}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>の{f|仕事|しごと}で、{f|私|わたし}には{f|不向|ふむ}きだと{f|思|おも}う。
Sales suits people who enjoy talking to others; I don't think it's for me.
</pre>

<pre>
このアプリは{f|文字|もじ}が{f|大|おお}きくて{f|操作|そうさ}も{f|少|すく}ないので、スマホに{f|慣|な}れていない{f|人|ひと}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>だ。
This app has big text and few controls, so it suits people who aren't used to smartphones.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral and fully at home in speech. You can say
<code>これ、{f|子|こ}ども{f|向|む}きだね</code> to a friend in a shop without sounding
formal, and the same phrase can appear in a product review.

What the word carries is a **judgement**, and judgements belong to somebody. When
you say <code>{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}{f|向|む}きだ</code> you are giving your reading of
how well the thing fits - you are not reporting a fact about the publisher's
intentions. This is why <code>{f|向|む}き</code> works so naturally in reviews,
recommendations and warnings, and why it so often appears with hedges:
<code>{f|向|む}きかもしれない</code>, <code>{f|向|む}きだと{f|思|おも}う</code>,
<code>{f|向|む}きとは{f|言|い}えない</code>.

The negative <code>{f|不向|ふむ}き</code> is a single word, not
<code>{f|向|む}き</code> plus a prefix you can apply freely, and it is used above all
of **people and jobs**: <code>{f|彼|かれ}は{f|接客|せっきゃく}には{f|不向|ふむ}きだ</code>.
It is blunter than <code>{f|向|む}きではない</code> and can sting, so in careful
speech people soften it: <code>あまり{f|向|む}いていないかもしれませんね</code>.

<code>{f|向|む}き{f|不向|ふむ}き</code> as a pair is a small fixed noun meaning
"aptitude" - literally the suits and the does-not-suits.
<code>{f|人|ひと}には{f|向|む}き{f|不向|ふむ}きがある</code> is a stock consoling phrase:
"people are cut out for different things."

## Contrast

### ～{f|向|む}き vs ～{f|向|む}け

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|向|む}き</code> = it **happens to suit** them.
<code>{f|向|む}け</code> = it was **made for** them. Fit versus intention.

</div>

This is the pair the whole page exists for, and the difference is real even though
many sentences accept both.

<pre>
この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|字|じ}が{f|大|おお}きいので{f|高齢者|こうれいしゃ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>だ。
This dictionary has large print, so it works well for older readers.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|辞書|じしょ}は{f|高齢者|こうれいしゃ}<b>{f|向|む}け</b>に{f|作|つく}られている。
This dictionary was made for older readers.
</pre>

The first is a reader's verdict: whatever the publisher had in mind, the big print
suits them. The second is a statement about the design brief. Consequently
<code>{f|向|む}け</code> is what appears in business and marketing language - 
<code>{f|海外|かいがい}{f|向|む}け</code>, <code>{f|法人|ほうじん}{f|向|む}け</code> - while
<code>{f|向|む}き</code> is what appears in reviews and advice.

One useful consequence: <code>{f|向|む}け</code> takes `に` before a verb naturally
(<code>{f|向|む}けに{f|作|つく}る</code>, <code>{f|向|む}けに{f|開|ひら}く</code>) because
intention modifies an action. <code>{f|向|む}き</code> mostly stays a predicate or a
noun modifier, which is why ❌<code>{f|子|こ}ども{f|向|む}きに{f|作|つく}る</code> sounds
wrong to a native ear.

### ～{f|向|む}き vs ～{f|用|よう}

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|用|よう}</code> states a **designated use**: this item
is *for* that and not for anything else. <code>{f|向|む}き</code> only rates a fit.

</div>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}ども<b>{f|用|よう}</b>のいすはあちらにございます。
The children's chairs are over there.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|映画|えいが}は{f|子|こ}ども<b>{f|向|む}き</b>だと{f|思|おも}う。
I think this film is one for children.
</pre>

<code>{f|子|こ}ども{f|用|よう}</code> is a category label - the chair is a child's
chair, and an adult using it is misusing it. <code>{f|子|こ}ども{f|向|む}き</code> is a
recommendation; an adult can watch the film perfectly well, it just fits children
better. Labels on shelves and menus overwhelmingly use <code>{f|用|よう}</code>:
<code>{f|業務用|ぎょうむよう}</code>, <code>{f|洗濯用|せんたくよう}</code>,
<code>{f|家庭用|かていよう}</code> - it is the suffix of designated purpose.

### ～{f|向|む}きだ vs ～に{f|向|む}いている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the two say the same thing from opposite ends.
<code>A は B {f|向|む}きだ</code>: the **thing** is described, B is the audience.
<code>B は A に{f|向|む}いている</code>: the **person** is described, A is the activity.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|細|こま}かい{f|作業|さぎょう}が{f|好|す}きな{f|人|ひと}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>だ。
This job suits people who like detailed work.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|妹|いもうと}は{f|細|こま}かい{f|作業|さぎょう}に<b>{f|向|む}いている</b>。
My younger sister is well suited to detailed work.
</pre>

Getting these crossed is the commonest error with the pattern. Remember that
<code>{f|向|む}いている</code> is a verb and needs `に`, while
<code>{f|向|む}き</code> is a suffix and needs nothing at all. Also note that with a
named individual, <code>に{f|向|む}いている</code> is far more natural:
<code>{f|妹|いもうと}{f|向|む}きだ</code> would be odd, because
<code>{f|向|む}き</code> prefers a **type** of person rather than a particular one.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|子|こ}ども{f|向|む}きに{f|作|つく}られた{f|番組|ばんぐみ}です。 → ✅ {f|子|こ}ども<b>{f|向|む}け</b>に{f|作|つく}られた{f|番組|ばんぐみ}です。
  Being *made for* an audience is intention, and intention is
  <code>{f|向|む}け</code>. <code>{f|向|む}き</code> would only rate the result.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}はこの{f|仕事|しごと}{f|向|む}きです。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}はこの{f|仕事|しごと}に<b>{f|向|む}いています</b>。
  The two ends are reversed. <code>{f|向|む}き</code> attaches to the **audience**,
  so the correct form with this word order would be
  <code>この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|彼|かれ}のような{f|人|ひと}{f|向|む}きです</code>.

- ❌ {f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}{f|向|む}きな{f|教材|きょうざい}を{f|探|さが}しています。 → ✅ {f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}<b>{f|向|む}きの</b>{f|教材|きょうざい}を{f|探|さが}しています。
  <code>{f|向|む}き</code> is a noun and links to the next noun with `の`. Only
  <code>{f|前|まえ}{f|向|む}き</code> and <code>{f|後|うし}ろ{f|向|む}き</code> take `な`.

- ❌ このコースは{f|体力|たいりょく}に{f|自信|じしん}がない{f|人|ひと}に{f|向|む}きです。 → ✅ …{f|自信|じしん}がない{f|人|ひと}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>です。
  No particle stands between the audience and <code>{f|向|む}き</code>; they form a
  compound noun. The `に` belongs to the verb version,
  <code>{f|人|ひと}に{f|向|む}いています</code>.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|旅館|りょかん}は{f|部屋|へや}が{f|広|ひろ}くて{f|静|しず}かなので、{f|小|ちい}さい{f|子|こ}どもがいる{f|家族|かぞく}____です。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|家族|かぞく}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>です。

The sentence gives reasons and then delivers a verdict on the fit - big rooms, quiet,
therefore good for families. That is <code>{f|向|む}き</code>.
<code>{f|家族|かぞく}{f|向|む}け</code> would claim the inn was designed with families
in mind, which the sentence does not say.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この{f|教科書|きょうかしょ}は{f|外国人|がいこくじん}（{f|向|む}き・{f|向|む}け）に{f|書|か}かれています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|外国人|がいこくじん}<b>{f|向|む}け</b>に{f|書|か}かれています。

<code>{f|書|か}かれている</code> is an act of creation, so the sentence is about the
author's intention. Intention takes <code>{f|向|む}け</code>, and
<code>{f|向|む}け</code> is the form that combines with `に` before a verb.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This mountain has steep paths, so it isn't really one for beginners."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|山|やま}は{f|道|みち}が{f|急|きゅう}なので、あまり{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>ではありません。

A judgement about fit, hedged with <code>あまり</code> - the classic environment for
<code>{f|向|む}き</code>. <code>{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}には{f|向|む}いていません</code>
would also be natural and puts the emphasis slightly more on the beginners.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|静|しず}かな{f|性格|せいかく}なので、{f|接客|せっきゃく}の{f|仕事|しごと}{f|向|む}きではないかもしれない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|田中|たなか}さんは{f|静|しず}かな{f|性格|せいかく}なので、{f|接客|せっきゃく}の{f|仕事|しごと}には<b>{f|向|む}いていない</b>かもしれない。

The sentence is describing a **person**, so the verb form is what you want, with
`に` marking the activity. Written with <code>{f|向|む}き</code>, the job would have
to be the thing described and Tanaka the audience:
<code>{f|接客|せっきゃく}の{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|田中|たなか}さんのような{f|人|ひと}{f|向|む}きではない</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) この{f|雑誌|ざっし}は{f|若|わか}い{f|女性|じょせい}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>だ。
(b) この{f|雑誌|ざっし}は{f|若|わか}い{f|女性|じょせい}<b>{f|向|む}け</b>だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is the speaker's assessment: whatever the magazine was meant to be, its
contents suit young women. It is the sort of thing you say while flicking through
it in a shop, and it leaves open the possibility that other people read it too.

(b) is a statement about the publisher: this magazine is **aimed at** young women - 
that is its market. It is the sentence an editor or a bookshop clerk would use, and
it says nothing about whether it actually suits them. Note also that (b) can be
extended with `に` and a verb - <code>{f|若|わか}い{f|女性|じょせい}{f|向|む}けに{f|作|つく}られた</code> - 
where (a) cannot.

</details>
