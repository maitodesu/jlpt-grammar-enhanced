# をこめて

**Meaning** · with X poured into it - putting a feeling, a wish or physical force into what you make or do

Written ～を{f|込|こ}めて when kanji is used. The verb <code>{f|込|こ}める</code> means to
pack something into a container, and that is the picture: you are packing a
feeling into an object or an action. The English gloss "filled with" is a trap - 
this pattern is never about ordinary physical fullness.

## Formation

The noun in front comes from a small, closed set: feelings, wishes, and force.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (feeling) | Noun + をこめて + verb | {f|心|こころ} → {f|心|こころ}をこめて{f|作|つく}る |
| Noun (wish, prayer) | Noun + をこめて + verb | {f|願|ねが}い → {f|願|ねが}いをこめて{f|贈|おく}る |
| Noun (physical force) | Noun + をこめて + verb | {f|力|ちから} → {f|力|ちから}をこめて{f|押|お}す |
| Longer feeling phrase | Noun + の{f|気持|きも}ち + をこめて | {f|感謝|かんしゃ} → {f|感謝|かんしゃ}の{f|気持|きも}ちをこめて |
| Before a noun | Noun + をこめた + Noun | {f|愛情|あいじょう} → {f|愛情|あいじょう}をこめた{f|料理|りょうり} |
| Passive | Noun + がこめられている | {f|思|おも}い → {f|思|おも}いがこめられている |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the nouns that work here are few enough to learn as a list:
{f|心|こころ}, {f|愛|あい}, {f|愛情|あいじょう}, {f|感謝|かんしゃ}, {f|思|おも}い,
{f|願|ねが}い, {f|祈|いの}り, {f|気持|きも}ち, {f|力|ちから}. If your noun is not an
emotion, a wish or force, the pattern is almost certainly the wrong one.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|心|こころ}<b>をこめて</b>ケーキを{f|焼|や}きました。
I baked the cake with all my heart.
</pre>

<pre>

I pushed the heavy door with all my strength.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

<pre>

There is a powerful feeling packed into this piece of music.
</pre>

<pre>

We all made an album, full of our thanks, for the graduating seniors.
</pre>

<pre>

Willing them to win, we kept up our shouting from the stands right to the end.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The pattern is **warm and slightly literary**. It belongs to gift-giving, cards,
speeches, product labelling and song lyrics, and it always implies that the
speaker took care. Said about your own work it can sound either sincere or a
little self-congratulatory, which is why advertising loves it: 「{f|職人|しょくにん}が{f|心|こころ}をこめて{f|一|ひと}つずつ{f|作|つく}りました」 is close to a cliché on Japanese
packaging.

Two of the collocations behave differently from the rest.
<code>{f|力|ちから}をこめて</code> is plain and physical with no emotional colour at all - 
gripping, pushing, swinging. And <code>～がこめられている</code>, the passive, is the form
you use to talk about meaning already inside something: a name, a song, a
tradition. 「この{f|名前|なまえ}には{f|親|おや}の{f|願|ねが}いがこめられている。」

In casual speech people mostly do not use it. To say a friend's cooking was made
with care you would more likely say 「{f|丁寧|ていねい}に{f|作|つく}ってあるね」. Where it
does turn up in conversation is in fixed farewells and dedications - 
「{f|感謝|かんしゃ}をこめて」 written at the bottom of a card, or announced before a
performance.

Note finally that the verb after it has to be an act of **making, giving or
doing**. There is nothing to pack a feeling into if the predicate is a state:
❌「{f|心|こころ}をこめてうれしいです」 has no container.

## Contrast

### をこめて vs ～でいっぱい・～だらけ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the English "filled with" covers three unrelated Japanese
patterns. `をこめて` is a **feeling put into an act**. Plain physical fullness is
`でいっぱい`. Unpleasant covering is `だらけ`.

</div>

<pre>
バケツは{f|水|みず}<b>でいっぱい</b>だった。
The bucket was full of water.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|子|こ}どもの{f|服|ふく}は{f|泥|どろ}<b>だらけ</b>になっていた。
The child's clothes were covered in mud.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|心|こころ}<b>をこめて</b>{f|手紙|てがみ}を{f|書|か}いた。
I wrote the letter with everything I had.
</pre>

❌<code>{f|水|みず}をこめたバケツ</code> is not a stylistic problem, it is meaningless - 
water is not a feeling, and a bucket is not something you make. `でいっぱい` also
handles abstract fullness where no act is involved:
<code>{f|不安|ふあん}でいっぱいだ</code>, "I'm full of worry."

### をこめて vs をもって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `をもって` is formal "with / by means of". It states the
manner or instrument of an official act. `をこめて` says an emotion went **into**
what you produced.

</div>

<pre>
{f|会社|かいしゃ}として、{f|誠意|せいい}<b>をもって</b>{f|対応|たいおう}してまいります。
As a company, we will respond to this in good faith.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|感謝|かんしゃ}<b>をこめて</b>、この{f|歌|うた}を{f|皆様|みなさま}に{f|贈|おく}ります。
With our thanks, we dedicate this song to you all.
</pre>

`をもって` is business and legal register - <code>{f|書面|しょめん}をもって</code>,
<code>{f|本日|ほんじつ}をもって</code> - and it never carries warmth. Swap the two and the
first sentence becomes oddly emotional while the second turns bureaucratic.

### {f|力|ちから}をこめて vs {f|力|ちから}を{f|入|い}れる

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|力|ちから}をこめる</code> is **physical force at a moment**.
<code>～に{f|力|ちから}を{f|入|い}れる</code> is **effort and resources put into an area over
time**.

</div>

<pre>
バットに{f|力|ちから}<b>をこめて</b>、{f|思|おも}い{f|切|き}り{f|振|ふ}った。
I put everything into the bat and swung as hard as I could.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|市|し}は{f|去年|きょねん}から{f|子育|こそだ}て{f|支援|しえん}に{f|力|ちから}<b>を{f|入|い}れて</b>いる。
Since last year the city has been putting real effort into childcare support.
</pre>

The second is a policy, not a muscle. Note the particle too: `をこめる` takes the
force as its object, while <code>{f|力|ちから}を{f|入|い}れる</code> marks the field with `に`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ コップに{f|水|みず}<b>をこめて</b>ください。 → ✅ コップに{f|水|みず}<b>を{f|入|い}れて</b>ください。
  Only feelings, wishes and force can be packed in. For putting an actual
  substance somewhere, the verb is <code>{f|入|い}れる</code>.

- ❌ {f|心|こころ}<b>にこめて</b>{f|歌|うた}います。 → ✅ {f|心|こころ}<b>をこめて</b>{f|歌|うた}います。
  The particle is `を`. The feeling is what gets packed, not the place it goes.

- ❌ {f|感謝|かんしゃ}<b>をこめて</b>{f|手紙|てがみ}をもらった。 → ✅ {f|感謝|かんしゃ}<b>をこめた</b>{f|手紙|てがみ}をもらった。
  In front of a noun the pattern takes `た`. As written, the sentence says *you*
  poured the gratitude in while receiving the letter.

- ❌ {f|心|こころ}<b>をこめて</b>この{f|町|まち}が{f|好|す}きです。 → ✅ {f|心|こころ}から この{f|町|まち}が{f|好|す}きです。
  A feeling needs something to be packed into - an act of making, giving or
  doing. For "from the bottom of my heart" plus a state, the phrase is
  <code>{f|心|こころ}から</code>.

## Practice

**1.** {f|退職|たいしょく}する{f|部長|ぶちょう}に、{f|感謝|かんしゃ}の{f|気持|きも}ち____{f|花束|はなたば}を{f|渡|わた}した。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|感謝|かんしゃ}の{f|気持|きも}ち<b>をこめて</b>{f|花束|はなたば}を{f|渡|わた}した。

Handing over flowers is an act you can pack a feeling into, and
<code>{f|感謝|かんしゃ}の{f|気持|きも}ち</code> is one of the pattern's standard nouns. The
longer <code>の{f|気持|きも}ち</code> version is slightly warmer than a bare
<code>{f|感謝|かんしゃ}をこめて</code>.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|新|あたら}しい{f|制度|せいど}については、{f|書面|しょめん}（をこめて・をもって）お{f|知|し}らせいたします。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|書面|しょめん}<b>をもって</b>お{f|知|し}らせいたします。

A document is an instrument, not an emotion, and the sentence is formal business
Japanese. <code>をもって</code> is the pattern for the manner or means of an official
act; `をこめて` would be nonsense here.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "She sings every song as though she means every word of it."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼女|かのじょ}はどの{f|曲|きょく}も{f|心|こころ}<b>をこめて</b>{f|歌|うた}う。

<code>{f|心|こころ}をこめて</code> is the standard way to say that an action is performed
with genuine feeling rather than mechanically. Singing is an act, so it gives
the feeling somewhere to go.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|人形|にんぎょう}は{f|作|つく}った{f|人|ひと}の{f|思|おも}いをこめている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|人形|にんぎょう}<b>には</b>{f|作|つく}った{f|人|ひと}の{f|思|おも}い<b>がこめられている</b>。

The doll is not the one doing the packing. When you want to describe the feeling
already sitting inside an object, use the passive
<code>がこめられている</code> and mark the object with `には`. The active form would need
the maker as its subject: 「{f|作|つく}った{f|人|ひと}が{f|思|おも}いをこめた{f|人形|にんぎょう}」.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|力|ちから}<b>をこめて</b>ロープを{f|引|ひ}いた。
(b) {f|心|こころ}<b>をこめて</b>ロープを{f|引|ひ}いた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a description of muscles: the rope was pulled hard, and nothing at all is
being said about the puller's feelings. This is the one collocation in the
family that is purely physical.

(b) is strange as a plain report and would only work in something like a
festival or a ceremony, where pulling the rope is a meaningful act - hauling a
shrine float, ringing a temple bell. The pattern always asks "what is this
feeling being packed into?", and an ordinary tug of a rope has no room for it.

</details>
