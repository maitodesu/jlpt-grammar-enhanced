# ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand

**Meaning** · sets two true sides of the same situation next to each other, without cancelling either

<code>{f|一方|いっぽう}</code> is "one side". The pattern says: here is one side, and here
is the other, and both are the case. That is what separates it from `でも` and `しかし`,
which put a stronger fact on top of a weaker one. <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code>
weighs; it does not overrule. It is also how you say that one person or one thing is doing
two things at once.

## Formation

The clause in front is plain form. Only nouns and な-adjectives need help joining.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + {f|一方|いっぽう}（で） | {f|働|はたら}く → {f|働|はたら}く{f|一方|いっぽう}で |
| い-adjective | plain form + {f|一方|いっぽう}で | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}い{f|一方|いっぽう}で |
| な-adjective | ～な／～である + {f|一方|いっぽう}で | {f|便利|べんり} → {f|便利|べんり}な{f|一方|いっぽう}で |
| Noun | Noun + である + {f|一方|いっぽう}で | {f|教師|きょうし} → {f|教師|きょうし}である{f|一方|いっぽう}で |
| Past tense | V(た) + {f|一方|いっぽう}で | {f|増|ふ}えた → {f|増|ふ}えた{f|一方|いっぽう}で |
| Starting a new sentence | {f|一方|いっぽう}、／その{f|一方|いっぽう}で、 | {f|一方|いっぽう}、{f|地方|ちほう}では… |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - do not confuse <code>～する{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> with
<code>～する{f|一方|いっぽう}だ</code>. The first joins two sides; the second means "keeps
getting more so, and only that". <code>{f|物価|ぶっか}が{f|上|あ}がる{f|一方|いっぽう}だ</code>
means prices just keep climbing, and it needs no second clause at all.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|兄|あに}は{f|働|はたら}く<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|大学|だいがく}にも{f|通|かよ}っています。
My brother works and goes to university at the same time.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|町|まち}は{f|静|しず}かでいい<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}が{f|不便|ふべん}です。
This town is nicely quiet, but on the other side of that, shopping is inconvenient.
</pre>

<pre>
スマホは{f|便利|べんり}な<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|時間|じかん}をうばうものでもある。
Smartphones are convenient, and by the same token they eat your time.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|都会|とかい}の{f|人口|じんこう}は{f|増|ふ}え{f|続|つづ}けている。<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}</b>、{f|地方|ちほう}では{f|人|ひと}が{f|減|へ}っている。
City populations keep growing. In the regions, meanwhile, people are leaving.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|父|ちち}は{f|厳|きび}しい<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|困|こま}ったときには{f|必|かなら}ず{f|助|たす}けてくれました。
My father was strict, and at the same time he never failed to help when I was in trouble.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|給料|きゅうりょう}がいい<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|休|やす}みがほとんど{f|取|と}れないそうです。
The pay in that job is good, but on the other hand you can hardly take any time off.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新|あたら}しい{f|制度|せいど}は{f|若|わか}い{f|社員|しゃいん}に{f|喜|よろこ}ばれている<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|長|なが}く{f|勤|つと}めてきた{f|人|ひと}たちからは{f|不満|ふまん}の{f|声|こえ}も{f|出|で}ています。
The new system has gone down well with younger staff, while long-serving employees have voiced complaints.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|祖母|そぼ}は{f|畑|はたけ}の{f|仕事|しごと}を{f|続|つづ}ける<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|週|しゅう}に{f|一度|いちど}は{f|町|まち}の{f|教室|きょうしつ}で{f|習字|しゅうじ}を{f|習|なら}っています。
My grandmother keeps working her field, and on top of that goes into town once a week for a calligraphy class.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is a **written** pattern first. It belongs to newspaper articles, reports, essays and
prepared presentations, and it is the standard way to present a balanced picture: an
advantage and its cost, a national trend and its regional exception, a policy and the
grumbling it caused. In casual conversation people join the same two ideas with `けど`, `し`
or 「でも」, and reaching for <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> over a drink sounds like
you are reading from notes.

The tone is **even-handed**, and this is what learners most often miss. Because the
construction gives both clauses equal footing, the writer comes across as fair rather than
critical, which is why it appears so often in business writing where a flat complaint would
be impolitic. If you want the second clause to win, use `が` or `しかし`.

The two clauses do not have to be opposites. A perfectly ordinary use is simple
simultaneity - one subject doing two things
（<code>{f|働|はたら}く{f|一方|いっぽう}で{f|大学|だいがく}にも{f|通|かよ}う</code>） - 
where English reaches for "while also". When the clauses *are* opposites, the `で` is often
dropped in more formal writing:
<code>{f|増|ふ}える{f|一方|いっぽう}、{f|質|しつ}は{f|落|お}ちている</code>. Sentence-initial <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}、</code> and
<code>その{f|一方|いっぽう}で、</code> do the same job across a full stop and are extremely
common in news writing.

## Contrast

### {f|一方|いっぽう}で vs {f|反面|はんめん}

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> needs the two sides to be **opposite in
evaluation**: a merit and its matching drawback, in the same subject.
<code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> is wider - the two clauses may simply be parallel, and may
even have different subjects.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|素材|そざい}は{f|軽|かる}い<b>{f|反面|はんめん}</b>、{f|傷|きず}がつきやすい。
This material is light, but by the same token it scratches easily.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|姉|あね}は{f|銀行|ぎんこう}で{f|働|はたら}く<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|週末|しゅうまつ}はボランティアもしています。
My sister works at a bank, and on top of that volunteers at weekends.
</pre>

Put <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> in the second sentence and it goes wrong, because
volunteering is not the downside of banking. Anywhere
<code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> works, <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> also works; the
reverse is not true.

### {f|一方|いっぽう}で vs が・しかし

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `が` and `しかし` make the second clause the point.
<code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> leaves both clauses standing. If you are building to a
conclusion, you want the adversative; if you are describing a situation, you want
<code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code>.

</div>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}は{f|安|やす}い<b>が</b>、{f|品質|ひんしつ}が{f|心配|しんぱい}だ。
It's cheap, but I'm worried about the quality.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}が{f|安|やす}い<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|品質|ひんしつ}にはばらつきがあるようだ。
While the price is low, the quality seems to vary.
</pre>

The first is heading towards a decision not to buy. The second is a report, and the reader
is left to weigh it. In a recommendation or a complaint, `が` is what you want; in an
analysis, it can sound like you have prejudged.

### ～{f|一方|いっぽう}で vs ～{f|一方|いっぽう}だ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - with `で` it needs a second clause and means "on the other side of
that". With `だ` it ends the sentence and means "keeps going that way, more and more".

</div>

<pre>
{f|外国人|がいこくじん}の{f|観光客|かんこうきゃく}は{f|増|ふ}える<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}だ</b>。
The number of foreign tourists just keeps rising.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|観光客|かんこうきゃく}が{f|増|ふ}える<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|地元|じもと}の{f|生活|せいかつ}は{f|不便|ふべん}になっている。
As visitor numbers rise, life for locals is getting less convenient.
</pre>

<code>～{f|一方|いっぽう}だ</code> takes only a non-past verb of change - 
{f|増|ふ}える, {f|減|へ}る, {f|悪|わる}くなる - and always carries a sense that the trend
is one-way and usually unwelcome.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|簡単|かんたん}だ{f|一方|いっぽう}で、{f|時間|じかん}がかかる。 → ✅ この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|簡単|かんたん}な{f|一方|いっぽう}で、{f|時間|じかん}がかかる。
  な-adjectives take `な` (or the formal `である`), never plain `だ`.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|医者|いしゃ}{f|一方|いっぽう}で、{f|小説|しょうせつ}も{f|書|か}いている。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}は{f|医者|いしゃ}である{f|一方|いっぽう}で、{f|小説|しょうせつ}も{f|書|か}いている。
  A noun cannot attach bare. It needs `である` - and note that this makes the sentence
  distinctly written in tone.

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った{f|一方|いっぽう}で、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になった。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ったので、{f|試合|しあい}は{f|中止|ちゅうし}になった。
  Cause and effect is not two sides of anything.
  <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> joins clauses that are independently true, not a reason
  and its result.

- ❌ {f|仕事|しごと}が{f|増|ふ}える{f|一方|いっぽう}で。 → ✅ {f|仕事|しごと}が{f|増|ふ}える{f|一方|いっぽう}だ。
  If you mean "the work just keeps piling up" and have no second clause, you want
  <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}だ</code>. With `で` the sentence is left hanging.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|学校|がっこう}は{f|授業料|じゅぎょうりょう}が{f|安|やす}い____、{f|設備|せつび}が{f|古|ふる}いという{f|問題|もんだい}もある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|安|やす}い<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、…

Two facts about the same school, both true, neither cancelling the other - the balanced
frame. <code>{f|反面|はんめん}</code> would also fit here, since cheap fees and old
facilities really are two sides of one coin.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|事故|じこ}の{f|件数|けんすう}は{f|減|へ}る（{f|一方|いっぽう}だ・{f|一方|いっぽう}で）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|事故|じこ}の{f|件数|けんすう}は{f|減|へ}る<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}だ</b>。

The sentence ends there, so it has to be the `だ` form: accident numbers keep falling. The
`で` form is a connective and demands a second clause.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This job pays well; on the other hand, you can never plan your weekends."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|収入|しゅうにゅう}がいい<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|週末|しゅうまつ}の{f|予定|よてい}が{f|立|た}てられません。

The two clauses are the up side and the down side of one job, given equal weight, which is
what <code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code> is for. Using `が` would tip the sentence towards a
complaint.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: この{f|地域|ちいき}は{f|自然|しぜん}が{f|豊|ゆた}かだ{f|一方|いっぽう}で、{f|交通|こうつう}が{f|不便|ふべん}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ この{f|地域|ちいき}は{f|自然|しぜん}が{f|豊|ゆた}かな{f|一方|いっぽう}で、{f|交通|こうつう}が{f|不便|ふべん}だ。

<code>{f|豊|ゆた}か</code> is a な-adjective, so it joins with `な`. The `だ` form only
appears before <code>という</code>-type quoting, not before
<code>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|外国語|がいこくご}の{f|授業|じゅぎょう}を{f|増|ふ}やしたが、{f|効果|こうか}は{f|出|で}ていない。
(b) {f|外国語|がいこくご}の{f|授業|じゅぎょう}を{f|増|ふ}やした<b>{f|一方|いっぽう}で</b>、{f|効果|こうか}はまだ{f|見|み}えてこない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses `が`, so the second clause is the punchline: the extra lessons did not work. It
reads as a criticism, and the reader expects a proposal to follow.

(b) presents both facts as findings and stops short of a verdict. It is the sentence you
write in a report you have to show to the people who added the lessons - even-handed,
and noticeably more diplomatic.

</details>
