# ～だらけ - covered all over

**Meaning** · with X all over it; caked in X

`～だらけ` attaches to a noun and says that the stuff named is **all over the
surface** of something, in an amount nobody wanted. Mud on a coat, dust on a shelf,
scratches on a table. The word is a complaint before it is a description.

## Formation

`だらけ` attaches to a **bare noun** - no particle in front of it - and the result
is itself a **noun**. It predicates with だ/です, modifies a following noun with
`の`, and pairs constantly with なる.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だらけ | {f|泥|どろ} → {f|泥|どろ}だらけ |
| Predicate | ～だらけ + だ / です | {f|机|つくえ}がほこりだらけだ |
| Before a noun | ～だらけ + の + Noun | {f|泥|どろ}だらけの{f|靴|くつ} |
| Becoming that way | ～だらけ + に + なる | {f|砂|すな}だらけになる |
| Negation | ～だらけ + では ない | {f|傷|きず}だらけではない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - `だらけ` never attaches to a verb or an adjective, and never takes `な`
before a noun. It is also **not** freely productive in the way it looks: the noun
has to name something you would rather were not there. {f|泥|どろ}・{f|砂|すな}・{f|血|ち}・ほこり・{f|傷|きず}・{f|毛|け}・しわ
are all natural; ❌<code>{f|花|はな}だらけ</code> and ❌<code>{f|光|ひかり}だらけ</code>
are not, unless you are being pointedly sarcastic.

</div>

## Examples

The kids' clothes ended up covered in mud.
</pre>

The top of the shelf is thick with dust.
</pre>

The room where the work had just finished was covered in white dust, right up to the window frames.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|祖父|そふ}の{f|手|て}は<b>{f|傷|きず}だらけ</b>で、{f|長|なが}く{f|畑|はたけ}{f|仕事|しごと}をしてきたことがすぐに{f|分|わ}かった。
My grandfather's hands were covered in scars - you could tell at once he'd worked the fields for years.
</pre>

<pre>

He must have run through the rain - my brother was standing in the entryway with his shoes and bag still caked in mud.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

`だらけ` is **evaluative**. Even when the fact reported is neutral, the word supplies
the reaction: dismay, disgust, or a weary "look at the state of this". A parent says
{f|泥|どろ}だらけ about a child's trousers with the sigh already in it.

It is also **hyperbolic**. {f|傷|きず}だらけ does not mean every square centimetre is
scarred; it means enough of them that the scars are what you notice. Nobody counts.

Register is ordinary spoken and written Japanese. It is not slangy, but it is blunt,
so in a formal report or a polite complaint to a company you would soften it - 
{f|汚|よご}れがひどい{f|状態|じょうたい}でした rather than
{f|泥|どろ}だらけでした. Between friends and inside a family it is completely
standard.

Note the shape of the sentence: what is covered is the **subject**, and what covers
it goes in front of だらけ with nothing between.
{f|机|つくえ}<b>が</b>ほこりだらけだ - the desk is the topic of the complaint, the
dust is the incriminating substance. Learners often reverse this and produce
❌<code>ほこりが{f|机|つくえ}だらけだ</code>, which says the dust is covered in desks.

## Contrast

### ～だらけ vs ～まみれ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `まみれ` is for a **liquid or a fine powder smeared over a
surface**. `だらけ` is for **many separate things scattered all over**, and it also
reaches abstract nouns, which `まみれ` cannot.

</div>

Where the substance is genuinely a coating, both work, and `まみれ` is the more
vivid, slightly more literary of the two:

<pre>
{f|工場|こうじょう}から{f|出|で}てきた{f|作業員|さぎょういん}は、{f|顔|かお}まで<b>{f|油|あぶら}まみれ</b>だった。
The worker who came out of the factory was covered in oil right up to his face.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|台所|だいどころ}の{f|床|ゆか}が<b>{f|油|あぶら}だらけ</b>で、{f|滑|すべ}りそうだった。
The kitchen floor was greasy all over and felt like it would be slippery.
</pre>

The line shows itself at the edges. Sweat forms a film, so
{f|汗|あせ}まみれ is normal and ❌<code>{f|汗|あせ}だらけ</code> is odd. Scars are
countable marks, so {f|傷|きず}だらけ is normal and ❌<code>{f|傷|きず}まみれ</code>
is odd. Dust, mud and blood sit in the middle and take either - with
{f|血|ち}まみれ sounding like a crime scene and {f|血|ち}だらけ sounding like a
kitchen accident.

`まみれ` extends metaphorically to only a very small set of fixed expressions
（{f|借金|しゃっきん}まみれ, {f|罪|つみ}にまみれる）, always with the image of being
smeared. It does not take scattered abstract defects:
❌<code>{f|間違|まちが}いまみれ</code>, ❌<code>{f|穴|あな}まみれ</code>.

### ～だらけ vs ～っぽい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だらけ` says the stuff is **physically there, all over it**.
`っぽい` says the thing merely **has the quality** of the stuff.

</div>

The clearest pair is dust, because both are everyday complaints about a room:

<pre>
{f|使|つか}っていない{f|部屋|へや}は<b>ほこりだらけ</b>で、まず{f|掃除|そうじ}から{f|始|はじ}めた。
The unused room was thick with dust, so we started with the cleaning.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|窓|まど}を{f|閉|し}めきっていたので、{f|部屋|へや}が<b>ほこりっぽくて</b>{f|息苦|いきぐる}しい。
We'd kept the windows shut, so the room feels dusty and stuffy.
</pre>

The first is about what is lying on the surfaces; you could photograph it. The
second is about how the air feels; you could not. Grammatically they are unrelated
too - `だらけ` builds a noun, `っぽい` an い-adjective - so the endings never look
alike: ほこりだらけだった against ほこりっぽかった.

### ～だらけ vs ～がついている

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>～がついている</code> reports that some of the stuff is on
there. `だらけ` reports that it is *everywhere* on there. The difference is amount,
and with it, tone.

</div>

<pre>
シャツに{f|醤油|しょうゆ}が<b>ついている</b>よ。
You've got soy sauce on your shirt.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|試合|しあい}のあと、ユニフォームは<b>{f|泥|どろ}だらけ</b>だった。
After the match the uniform was caked in mud.
</pre>

The first is a helpful heads-up about one spot. The second is a verdict on the whole
garment. This is why <code>ついている</code> is the polite way to tell a stranger
about a stain and `だらけ` is not: pointing at someone's jacket and saying
{f|泥|どろ}だらけですよ makes it sound as though they had rolled in a field.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|泥|どろ}<b>が</b>だらけ → ✅ {f|泥|どろ}だらけ
  Nothing goes between the noun and `だらけ` - no `が`, no `で`, no `の`.

- ❌ {f|泥|どろ}だらけ<b>な</b>{f|靴|くつ} → ✅ {f|泥|どろ}だらけ<b>の</b>{f|靴|くつ}
  The result is a noun, not a な-adjective, so it links with `の`.

- ❌ {f|部屋|へや}が{f|汚|よご}れている<b>だらけ</b>だ。 → ✅ {f|部屋|へや}がごみだらけだ。
  `だらけ` attaches only to nouns. A verb or an adjective in front of it is not
  possible.

- ❌ {f|汗|あせ}<b>だらけ</b>になって{f|走|はし}っていた → ✅ {f|汗|あせ}<b>まみれ</b>になって{f|走|はし}っていた
  Sweat is a film over the skin, not a scatter of separate marks, so it takes
  `まみれ`. <code>{f|汗|あせ}びっしょり</code> is the everyday alternative.

- ❌ {f|庭|にわ}が{f|花|はな}<b>だらけ</b>できれいだ。 → ✅ {f|庭|にわ}が{f|花|はな}でいっぱいできれいだ。
  `だらけ` cannot be used approvingly. Saying it about flowers turns a pretty garden
  into an infestation.

## Practice

**1.** {f|自転車|じてんしゃ}で{f|転|ころ}んで、ズボンが{f|砂|すな}____になってしまった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ズボンが{f|砂|すな}<b>だらけ</b>になってしまった。

Sand is a scatter of separate grains all over the surface, so `だらけ`, and it
attaches straight to the bare noun. <code>～だらけになる</code> is the standard way
to say something *ended up* in that state.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|試合|しあい}が{f|終|お}わったとき、{f|選手|せんしゅ}たちは（{f|汗|あせ}だらけ・{f|汗|あせ}まみれ）だった。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|選手|せんしゅ}たちは<b>{f|汗|あせ}まみれ</b>だった。

Sweat is a liquid coating the skin, and liquids take `まみれ`. `だらけ` wants
countable things or a layer of loose material, which is why
<code>{f|泥|どろ}だらけ</code> is fine but <code>{f|汗|あせ}だらけ</code> sounds
wrong to a native ear.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Don't come into the house with muddy shoes."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|泥|どろ}<b>だらけ</b>の{f|靴|くつ}で{f|家|いえ}に{f|入|はい}らないで。

Before a noun, `だらけ` links with `の`, giving
<code>{f|泥|どろ}だらけの{f|靴|くつ}</code>. The English adjective "muddy" has no
single-word Japanese equivalent here - the noun-plus-suffix construction does the
whole job.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|棚|たな}の{f|奥|おく}から、ほこりだらけな{f|箱|はこ}が{f|出|で}てきた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|棚|たな}の{f|奥|おく}から、ほこりだらけ<b>の</b>{f|箱|はこ}が{f|出|で}てきた。

`だらけ` produces a noun, and a noun modifying another noun links with `の`. The `な`
you may be thinking of belongs before ので and のに
（<code>ほこりだらけなので</code>）, which is the same `な` any noun takes there.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|台所|だいどころ}の{f|窓|まど}に{f|油|あぶら}がついている。
(b) {f|台所|だいどころ}の{f|窓|まど}が{f|油|あぶら}<b>だらけ</b>だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) simply notes that there is grease on the window - perhaps one patch, perhaps a
thin film. It is a neutral observation and could be the first step in offering to
clean it.

(b) says the grease is all over the glass and adds the speaker's reaction: this has
been neglected, and it is unpleasant. Said in someone else's kitchen it would land
as criticism, which (a) would not.

</details>
