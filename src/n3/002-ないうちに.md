# ～ないうちに

**Meaning** · before something happens - said from inside the time when it still hasn't

English says "before it rains." Japanese takes the opposite angle and says "while it
is not-yet raining": <code>{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}らないうちに</code>. There is no
natural English shape for that, which is why the pattern feels strange for a long time
and then suddenly feels obvious. The thing in the ない clause is nearly always
something you would rather avoid, and the main clause is what you do to get ahead of
it.

## Formation

Take the plain negative and put <code>うちに</code> straight after it. The negative
does the work; nothing else changes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ない-form + うちに | {f|降|ふ}る → {f|降|ふ}らないうちに |
| Verb (る-verb) | ない-form + うちに | {f|冷|さ}める → {f|冷|さ}めないうちに |
| Verb (する) | しないうちに | {f|変化|へんか}する → {f|変化|へんか}しないうちに |
| Verb ({f|来|く}る) | {f|来|こ}ないうちに | {f|来|く}る → {f|来|こ}ないうちに |
| い-adjective (via なる) | ～く + ならないうちに | {f|暗|くら}い → {f|暗|くら}くならないうちに |
| な-adjective / Noun (via なる) | ～に + ならないうちに | {f|病気|びょうき} → {f|病気|びょうき}にならないうちに |

<div class="note">

**Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the labels used throughout this book; you will also
see them as {f|五段|ごだん}/Group 1 and {f|一段|いちだん}/Group 2. The irregular one to watch is
<code>{f|来|く}る</code>, whose negative stem is <b>こ</b>: {f|来|こ}ないうちに, never
❌くないうちに.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - adjectives almost never attach directly. ❌<code>{f|寒|さむ}くないうちに</code>
is not how this is said; route it through ならない - 
<code>{f|寒|さむ}くならないうちに</code>. The negative has to name an **event that has
not started**, and adjectives name states, so なる supplies the missing event.

</div>

<pre>

</pre>

<b>{f|忘|わす}れないうちに</b>メモしておきます。

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

</pre>

Children grow before you notice, and the clothes we bought last year already don't fit.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and common in both speech and writing. What is not free is the shape
of the main clause: it is almost always something the speaker **chooses to do in
response** - よう, ておく, てしまう, たほうがいい, てください, なさい, ないと. That is the
whole reason the pattern exists.

The ない clause is normally a change for the worse: rain, cold food, darkness, crowds,
your boss returning. This is a strong tendency rather than a hard rule, but if the
event is welcome the sentence starts to sound odd, and you probably wanted
<code>{f|前|まえ}に</code>.

Three expressions have hardened into set phrases, and they behave like adverbs rather
than like clauses:

- <code>{f|知|し}らないうちに</code> - without one's knowledge
- <code>{f|気|き}づかないうちに</code> - without noticing
- <code>{f|分|わ}からないうちに</code> - before one has worked out what is going on

In these the main clause is not an action you took but a change you discovered
afterwards, usually with ていた or てしまう:
{f|知|し}らないうちに{f|時間|じかん}が{f|過|す}ぎていた.

In casual speech the whole thing survives intact - this is not a pattern that gets
contracted. What does happen is that the main clause loses its particles:
「{f|雨|あめ}{f|降|ふ}らないうちに{f|帰|かえ}ろ」.

## Contrast

### ～ないうちに vs ～{f|前|まえ}に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|前|まえ}に</code> puts two events in order and stops there.
<code>ないうちに</code> says the later event is a **problem you are outrunning**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|出|で}かける<b>{f|前|まえ}に</b>{f|窓|まど}を{f|閉|し}めた。
I shut the windows before going out.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が{f|入|はい}って<b>こないうちに</b>{f|窓|まど}を{f|閉|し}めた。
I shut the windows before the rain could get in.
</pre>

The first is a checklist item. The second has a threat in it. Notice too that
<code>{f|前|まえ}に</code> takes a **dictionary form** and <code>ないうちに</code> takes
a **negative**, even though the English translation is "before" both times - this is
the single most reliable source of errors with the pattern.

One more practical difference: <code>{f|前|まえ}に</code> is happy with a scheduled,
neutral event ({f|会議|かいぎ}が{f|始|はじ}まる{f|前|まえ}に), while
<code>ないうちに</code> wants something that might land on you at any moment. You would
not normally say ❌<code>{f|会議|かいぎ}が{f|始|はじ}まらないうちに</code> for a meeting
that starts at ten sharp - unless you mean you are racing it.

### ～ないうちに vs the affirmative ～うちに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the same situation can be framed from either side. Affirmative
<code>うちに</code> names the **good state you still have**; <code>ないうちに</code>
names the **bad state that hasn't arrived**.

</div>

<pre>
<b>{f|明|あか}るいうちに</b>{f|洗濯物|せんたくもの}を{f|干|ほ}してしまおう。
Let's get the washing hung out while it's still light.
</pre>

<pre>
<b>{f|暗|くら}くならないうちに</b>{f|洗濯物|せんたくもの}を{f|干|ほ}してしまおう。
Let's get the washing hung out before it gets dark.
</pre>

These are interchangeable here, and either is natural. The choice matters when only one
side of the pair has a name. There is a comfortable word for the good state in
{f|明|あか}るい, so both work; but for "before the shop closes" there is no adjective
for "still open in the sense that matters", so you reach for
<code>{f|店|みせ}が{f|閉|し}まらないうちに</code>. Conversely, {f|若|わか}いうちに has no
useful negative twin - ❌{f|年|とし}を{f|取|と}らないうちに is understandable but
distinctly odd.

### ～ないうちに vs ～ない{f|間|あいだ}に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ない{f|間|あいだ}に</code> is a **stretch of non-happening**
with a start and an end. <code>ないうちに</code> is the run-up **before the thing
starts**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}が{f|見|み}ていない<b>{f|間|あいだ}に</b>、{f|猫|ねこ}が{f|魚|さかな}を{f|食|た}べてしまった。
The cat ate the fish while I wasn't looking.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|猫|ねこ}が{f|起|お}き<b>ないうちに</b>、{f|魚|さかな}をしまっておこう。
Let's put the fish away before the cat wakes up.
</pre>

In the first, "not looking" is a period that has already begun and already ended - the
cat operated inside it. In the second, "waking up" has not happened at all yet, and the
sentence is about getting in first. A quick test: if you can put ずっと in front of the
negative clause, it is <code>ない{f|間|あいだ}に</code>.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}るないうちに</b>{f|帰|かえ}ります。 → ✅ {f|雨|あめ}が<b>{f|降|ふ}らないうちに</b>{f|帰|かえ}ります。
  <code>うちに</code> attaches to the finished ない-form, not to the dictionary form
  plus ない. Build {f|降|ふ}らない first, then add うちに.

- ❌ <b>{f|暗|くら}いうちに</b>{f|帰|かえ}りましょう。（meaning "before it gets dark"） → ✅ <b>{f|暗|くら}くならないうちに</b>{f|帰|かえ}りましょう。
  <code>{f|暗|くら}いうちに</code> is perfectly grammatical, but it means "while it is
  still dark" - the pre-dawn start, not the evening deadline. The two are opposites.

- ❌ <b>{f|忘|わす}れないうちで</b>{f|書|か}いておきます。 → ✅ <b>{f|忘|わす}れないうちに</b>{f|書|か}いておきます。
  The particle is fixed at `に`. <code>うちで</code> is not a variant of this pattern.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}<b>は</b>{f|来|こ}ないうちに{f|片付|かたづ}けよう。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}<b>が</b>{f|来|こ}ないうちに{f|片付|かたづ}けよう。
  The ない clause is embedded, so its subject takes `が`. And note the stem:
  <code>{f|来|く}る</code> goes to {f|来|こ}ない, not ❌くない.

## Practice

**1.** ご{f|飯|はん}が____うちに{f|食|た}べてください。（{f|冷|さ}める）

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ご{f|飯|はん}が<b>{f|冷|さ}めない</b>うちに{f|食|た}べてください。

English wants "before it gets cold", but Japanese frames the same moment as "while it
has not yet gone cold", so the verb goes into the plain negative. Using the affirmative
{f|冷|さ}めるうちに would be ungrammatical here - there is no state of "being in the
middle of cooling" to sit inside.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|台風|たいふう}が（{f|来|く}る{f|前|まえ}に・{f|来|こ}ないうちに）{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}を{f|済|す}ませておこう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both are grammatical, but <b>{f|来|こ}ないうちに</b> is the better fit.

<code>{f|来|く}る{f|前|まえ}に</code> would simply order the two events, as though the
typhoon were an appointment. <code>{f|来|こ}ないうちに</code> treats it as a threat you
are outrunning, which is what ておこう and {f|済|す}ませて are already telling us. Note the
irregular negative stem: {f|来|こ}ない.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The line will get long, so let's buy the tickets before people find out."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|列|れつ}が{f|長|なが}くなるから、みんなに<b>{f|知|し}られないうちに</b>チケットを{f|買|か}っておこう。

The negative passive {f|知|し}られない is what carries "before people find out" - the
event you are getting ahead of is other people learning about it. ておこう in the main
clause is typical: this pattern nearly always ends in something volitional.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|子|こ}どもが{f|起|お}きるないうちに、しずかに{f|出|で}かけた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|子|こ}どもが<b>{f|起|お}きないうちに</b>、しずかに{f|出|で}かけた。

{f|起|お}きる is a る-verb, so its negative is {f|起|お}きない - drop る, add ない.
❌{f|起|お}きるない stacks the dictionary form and ない together, which never happens in
Japanese.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|留守|るす}の<b>{f|間|あいだ}に</b>、だれかが{f|部屋|へや}に{f|入|はい}ったようだ。
(b) {f|帰|かえ}って<b>こないうちに</b>、{f|部屋|へや}を{f|片付|かたづ}けておこう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) looks backwards at a window that has closed. The absence was a real stretch of
time, and the sentence places an event somewhere inside it - a neutral report of when
something happened.

(b) looks forwards from inside the window. Nobody has come back yet, and the speaker is
using the remaining time to get something done first. The clue is the pairing of a
negative clause with a volitional ending: that combination is almost always
<code>ないうちに</code>, not <code>{f|間|あいだ}に</code>.

</details>
