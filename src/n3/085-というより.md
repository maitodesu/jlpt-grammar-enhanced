# ～というより - rather than

**Meaning** · rejects one description as not quite right and puts a better one in its place - "it's not so much X as Y"

<code>というより</code> compares **two ways of describing the same thing**, not two things.
The first label is usually the one already in the air - the word the other person used, or
the polite word you started to reach for - and the second is what you actually mean. The
first is never called wrong, only inexact, which is why the pattern is so useful for
saying blunt things gently.

## Formation

Everything attaches in its plain form, and nouns and な-adjectives normally drop `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というより | {f|趣味|しゅみ} → {f|趣味|しゅみ}というより |
| な-adjective | な-adj + というより（no `な`） | {f|静|しず}か → {f|静|しず}かというより |
| い-adjective | plain form + というより | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いというより |
| Verb | plain form + というより | {f|走|はし}る → {f|走|はし}っているというより |
| Emphatic | というよりも／というよりむしろ | {f|趣味|しゅみ}というよりむしろ |
| Casual | っていうより | {f|趣味|しゅみ}っていうより |
| Common closing | …に{f|近|ちか}い／…のほうが{f|近|ちか}い | {f|仕事|しごと}に{f|近|ちか}い |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - the **better** label goes second. English puts it first ("more a hobby
than a job"), Japanese puts it last
（<code>{f|仕事|しごと}というより{f|趣味|しゅみ}だ</code>）. Reversing the order reverses
the meaning, and this is the single commonest error with the pattern.

</div>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself, but the *move* it makes is a politeness device. Naming the
gentler word first and correcting it downwards lets you land a hard judgement without
having stated it flatly: <code>まずいというより、{f|味|あじ}が{f|薄|うす}い</code> is far
kinder than starting with 「まずい」. The same works upwards - 
<code>{f|上手|じょうず}というより、もうプロだ</code> - where the pattern reads as genuine
admiration rather than flattery, because you appear to be searching for the accurate word.

`むしろ` is its natural companion and adds "if anything":
<code>{f|嫌|きら}いというより、むしろ{f|好|す}きなほうです</code>. `も` on the end
(<code>というよりも</code>) simply adds weight. In casual speech the whole thing contracts
to <code>っていうより</code>, and in fast conversation you will hear it trailing off
entirely - 「{f|怒|おこ}ってるっていうより…ね」 - where the listener is expected to supply
the better word.

Because the pattern is essentially a self-correction, it also works as a hedge on your own
speech. Starting a sentence over with <code>というより</code> mid-turn signals that you
are refining your point, not contradicting yourself, and it buys you a moment to find the
word you want.

## Contrast

### というより vs より

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `より` compares **two things** on one scale.
<code>というより</code> compares **two labels** for one thing. If you can insert
「のほうが」 after the second item, you want plain `より`.

</div>

<pre>
{f|去年|きょねん}<b>より</b>{f|今年|ことし}のほうが{f|雨|あめ}が{f|多|おお}い。
There's more rain this year than last.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|今年|ことし}は{f|雨|あめ}が{f|多|おお}い<b>というより</b>、ずっと{f|降|ふ}り{f|続|つづ}いている{f|感|かん}じだ。
It's not that there's a lot of rain this year - it feels like it simply hasn't stopped.
</pre>

In the first sentence there are two years and one quantity of rain. In the second there is
one year and two ways of putting it. That is the whole distinction, and the grammar follows
from it: `より` takes a noun, <code>というより</code> takes a full description.

### というより vs ではなく

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ではなく` says the first label is **wrong**.
<code>というより</code> says it is **understandable but inexact**.

</div>

<pre>
これは{f|誤解|ごかい}<b>ではなく</b>、はっきりとした{f|間違|まちが}いです。
This isn't a misunderstanding - it's an outright error.
</pre>

<pre>
これは{f|誤解|ごかい}<b>というより</b>、{f|説明|せつめい}の{f|仕方|しかた}が{f|悪|わる}かったのだと{f|思|おも}います。
I'd call this less a misunderstanding than a failure to explain properly.
</pre>

`ではなく` corrects; <code>というより</code> negotiates. In a disagreement the second is
much easier to say to someone senior, because it concedes that their word was a reasonable
first attempt.

### というより vs どころか

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `どころか` overshoots on purpose: not X - in fact something far beyond
X, often in the opposite direction. <code>というより</code> stays calm and simply
re-labels.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|泳|およ}げない<b>どころか</b>、{f|水|みず}に{f|顔|かお}をつけることもできない。
Far from being unable to swim, he can't even put his face in the water.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼|かれ}は{f|泳|およ}げない<b>というより</b>、{f|水|みず}が{f|怖|こわ}いのだろう。
It's not that he can't swim - it's more that he's afraid of the water.
</pre>

`どころか` is emphatic and often a little dramatic; <code>というより</code> is analytical.
Choosing the wrong one is not ungrammatical, but it misreports how strongly you feel.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ これはおやつというより{f|食事|しょくじ}だ。（meaning "it's more a snack than a meal"） → ✅ これは{f|食事|しょくじ}というよりおやつだ。
  The accurate label goes **second**. Word order alone decides which description you are
  endorsing, so a reversed pair says the opposite of what you meant.

- ❌ {f|静|しず}か<b>な</b>というより → ✅ {f|静|しず}かというより
  な-adjectives lose their `な` here, and they do not take `だ` either. Only the bare stem
  goes in front of <code>という</code>.

- ❌ {f|兄|あに}というより{f|弟|おとうと}のほうが{f|背|せ}が{f|高|たか}い。 → ✅ {f|兄|あに}より{f|弟|おとうと}のほうが{f|背|せ}が{f|高|たか}い。
  Two different people on one scale is a plain `より` comparison.
  <code>というより</code> would mean you were choosing between calling the same person an
  older or a younger brother.

- ❌ {f|嫌|きら}いというより。 → ✅ {f|嫌|きら}いというより、{f|苦手|にがて}なんです。
  The pattern needs both halves. Stopping after the rejected label leaves the listener
  with the word you have just told them not to use. (In casual speech people do trail off
  like this - but only when the better word is obvious from context.)

## Practice

**1.** {f|彼|かれ}の{f|部屋|へや}は{f|汚|きたな}い____、{f|物|もの}が{f|多|おお}すぎて{f|片付|かたづ}けられないだけだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|汚|きたな}い<b>というより</b>、…

The sentence sets aside "dirty" as an unfair word and offers a fairer account of the same
room. Note the order: the label being rejected comes first, the accurate one second.

</details>

**2.** Choose: この{f|問題|もんだい}は{f|難|むずか}しい（より・というより）、{f|説明|せつめい}が{f|分|わ}かりにくいのだと{f|思|おも}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|難|むずか}しい<b>というより</b>、{f|説明|せつめい}が{f|分|わ}かりにくいのだと{f|思|おも}います。

There is only one problem here and two competing descriptions of it, so you need
<code>というより</code>. Plain `より` would require two items being ranked against each
other.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He isn't kind so much as he simply can't say no."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|彼|かれ}は{f|優|やさ}しい<b>というより</b>、{f|断|ことわ}れないだけなんです。

The gentle label goes first and is set aside; the sharper, more accurate one follows. The
<code>だけ</code> at the end does the work of English "simply".

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: あの{f|店|みせ}は{f|喫茶店|きっさてん}というより{f|静|しず}かなというより{f|図書館|としょかん}のようだ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ あの{f|店|みせ}は{f|喫茶店|きっさてん}というより{f|図書館|としょかん}のようだ。

Two problems: な-adjectives take no `な` before <code>という</code>, and a single sentence
carries one <code>というより</code>. The re-labelling happens once - first label, then the
better one.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|今回|こんかい}の{f|案|あん}は{f|新|あたら}しい<b>というより</b>、{f|昔|むかし}のやり{f|方|かた}に{f|近|ちか}い。
(b) {f|今回|こんかい}の{f|案|あん}は{f|新|あたら}しい<b>ではなく</b>、{f|昔|むかし}のやり{f|方|かた}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) allows that someone could reasonably have called the plan new, then quietly moves the
description somewhere more accurate. It is the version you can say in a meeting without
embarrassing the person who wrote the plan.

(b) - leaving aside that it should be
<code>{f|新|あたら}しいのではなく</code> with an い-adjective - flatly denies the first
description. It is clearer and colder, and it is what you use when the point of the
sentence is that someone was wrong.

</details>
