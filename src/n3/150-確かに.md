# 確かに

**Meaning** · certainly; admittedly; you're right - vouching for something, or
granting a point before you argue with it

{f|確|たし}かに has two lives. It can mean "definitely, without doubt", said of
something you are sure of. Far more often at this level it means "granted" - you
concede what the other person said, and then the `が` or `けど` arrives.

## Usage

{f|確|たし}かに is an adverb: it goes in front of the predicate, or at the head of
the sentence, and never changes shape. Three uses:

- **Conceding** - the big one. {f|確|たし}かに～が／けど、～. The first half agrees,
  the second half pushes back.
  {f|確|たし}かに{f|値段|ねだん}は{f|高|たか}いが、{f|質|しつ}はいい。
- **Vouching** - "for certain", of an event you witnessed or a fact you can stand
  behind.
  {f|昨日|きのう}、{f|確|たし}かに{f|渡|わた}しました。
- **Agreeing, on its own** - 「{f|確|たし}かに。」 is a complete turn in
  conversation, meaning "true" or "fair point". This is very common in modern
  casual speech.

The single most useful thing to remember is the concessive frame. Whenever an
essay or a discussion needs "it's true that…, but…", {f|確|たし}かに at the front
and `が` in the middle is the standard shape, and a listener hears the `が` coming
the moment you say the first word.

Kanji {f|確|たし}かに and kana たしかに are both normal; the kanji is the default in
print.

</pre>

<pre>
<b>{f|確|たし}かに</b>{f|寒|さむ}いけど、{f|雪|ゆき}は{f|降|ふ}らないよ。

</pre>

His explanation was certainly easy to follow, but it left out the crucial question of cost.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and equally at home in an academic paper and in a text message.
The concessive frame {f|確|たし}かに～が is a staple of written argument; the
one-word 「{f|確|たし}かに。」 reply is casual but not slangy, and works in the office
as well as with friends.

The vouching use has a slightly formal flavour, because it is what you say when
responsibility is at stake: {f|確|たし}かに{f|承|うけたまわ}りました, "I have
definitely taken note of that", is standard business phrasing. In everyday talk
about the future, きっと is the natural word instead.

Be aware of what conceding does to the shape of a conversation. Opening with
{f|確|たし}かに sets up an expectation that a `が` is coming; if you concede and
then stop, the listener will wait for the rest of the sentence. That is often
exactly the effect you want when disagreeing politely - the agreement takes the
sting out of what follows - but it does mean {f|確|たし}かに is rarely the whole
of your position.

## Contrast

### {f|確|たし}かに vs {f|確|たし}か

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one `に` reverses the meaning. {f|確|たし}かに is confidence;
{f|確|たし}か is a memory you are not sure of.

</div>

<pre>
<b>{f|確|たし}か</b>{f|会議|かいぎ}は{f|三時|さんじ}からだったと{f|思|おも}います。
The meeting was at three, if I remember rightly.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}は<b>{f|確|たし}かに</b>{f|三時|さんじ}から{f|始|はじ}まりました。
The meeting definitely started at three.
</pre>

{f|確|たし}か is an adverb of *hedged recollection* and normally comes with
と{f|思|おも}う, はず or だったよね. {f|確|たし}かに asserts. Mixing them up tells
your listener the opposite of what you meant, which is why this pair is worth
drilling.

### {f|確|たし}かに vs きっと

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - {f|確|たし}かに is about what is already established. きっと is
a confident guess about what is not.

</div>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}は<b>きっと</b>{f|来|き}ます。
She'll definitely come.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|彼女|かのじょ}は<b>{f|確|たし}かに</b>{f|来|き}ました。
She did come - I can vouch for it.
</pre>

きっと looks forward and is a prediction, however confident; {f|確|たし}かに looks
back at evidence. Saying ❌{f|確|たし}かに{f|来|き}ます about a future event sounds
like you are promising on someone else's behalf.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|確|たし}かに{f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}でしょう。 → ✅ <b>きっと</b>{f|明日|あした}は{f|雨|あめ}でしょう。
  A forecast is a guess, not something you can vouch for. {f|確|たし}かに needs
  evidence already in hand.

- ❌ {f|確|たし}かに{f|田中|たなか}さんの{f|傘|かさ}だったと{f|思|おも}います。 → ✅ <b>{f|確|たし}か</b>{f|田中|たなか}さんの{f|傘|かさ}だったと{f|思|おも}います。
  と{f|思|おも}います signals that you are not sure, so the hedging
  {f|確|たし}か is the form that fits. With `に` the sentence contradicts itself.

- ❌ {f|確|たし}かにです。 → ✅ <b>{f|確|たし}かに</b>そうですね。
  {f|確|たし}かに is an adverb and cannot take `です` directly. To agree politely,
  give it something to modify.

## Practice

**1.** ____{f|不便|ふべん}な{f|場所|ばしょ}だが、{f|家賃|やちん}が{f|安|やす}いのは{f|魅力|みりょく}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|確|たし}かに</b>{f|不便|ふべん}な{f|場所|ばしょ}だが、{f|家賃|やちん}が{f|安|やす}いのは{f|魅力|みりょく}だ。

The `が` in the middle is the giveaway: the writer grants the drawback first and
then argues past it, which is the standard concessive frame this word builds.

</details>

**2.** Choose the right form: （{f|確|たし}か・{f|確|たし}かに）{f|去年|きょねん}も{f|同|おな}じ{f|問題|もんだい}が{f|出|で}たはずです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

<b>{f|確|たし}か</b>{f|去年|きょねん}も{f|同|おな}じ{f|問題|もんだい}が{f|出|で}たはずです。

`はず` marks this as inference from a hazy memory rather than a fact the speaker
can guarantee, so the hedging form without `に` is the one that fits.

</details>

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|確|たし}かにこの{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|速|はや}い。
(b) この{f|方法|ほうほう}は{f|速|はや}い。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sentence (b) is a flat statement of the speaker's own view. Sentence (a) sounds
like a reply: it accepts a point somebody else has made, and it leaves the door
open for a "but" - about cost, or safety, or whatever the speaker is really
driving at. Ending there without the "but" makes the agreement sound reluctant.

</details>
