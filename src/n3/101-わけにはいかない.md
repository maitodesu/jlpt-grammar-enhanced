# ～わけにはいかない

**Meaning** · I can't very well do that - it is perfectly possible, and I still can't

This is not a pattern about ability and not a pattern about rules. Everything
`わけにはいかない` describes is physically available to you: you could take the
day off, you could tell the secret, you could go home first. What stops you is a
promise, a position, a debt of gratitude, or your own sense of what is decent. It
is obligation as you *feel* it, rather than obligation as somebody imposes it.

## Formation

`いく` here is written in kana. It is the verb of "things not going", not of going
somewhere, and it never becomes いける.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + わけにはいかない | {f|休|やす}む → {f|休|やす}むわけにはいかない |
| Verb (negative) - "I have to" | ない-form + わけにはいかない | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}かないわけにはいかない |
| Verb (する) | ～するわけにはいかない | {f|無視|むし}する → {f|無視|むし}するわけにはいかない |
| Polite | ～わけにはいきません | {f|休|やす}むわけにはいきません |
| Past | ～わけにはいかなかった | {f|休|やす}むわけにはいかなかった |
| Casual, は dropped | ～わけにいかない | {f|休|やす}むわけにいかない |
| Added to a list | ～わけにもいかない | {f|断|ことわ}るわけにもいかない |
| Humble / very formal | ～わけにはまいりません | {f|休|やす}むわけにはまいりません |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - ❌<code>わけにはいけない</code> is the single most common error
with this pattern. The verb is <code>いく</code> in its negative
<code>いかない</code>; there is no potential form here.

</div>

## Nuance & register

Ask, of any sentence you are tempted to build with this: **could I physically do
it?** If the answer is no, the pattern is wrong. A person who cannot swim does not
say <code>{f|泳|およ}ぐわけにはいかない</code>; they say
<code>{f|泳|およ}げません</code>. The pattern's whole content is the gap between
*could* and *can't bring myself to*.

For the same reason the subject is nearly always **the speaker**. You are
reporting the inside of your own head - the obligation you feel, the awkwardness
you would not survive. Said about somebody else it presumes to know their
conscience, which is why <code>{f|君|きみ}は{f|帰|かえ}るわけにはいかない</code>
sounds less like an observation than like pressure. Describing a third person's
position is possible when you have grounds:
<code>{f|社長|しゃちょう}としては{f|認|みと}めるわけにはいかないだろう</code> - note
the <code>だろう</code> doing the hedging.

The `も` variant is worth having. <code>～わけにもいかない</code> adds this
impossibility to one already mentioned, and it is how the pattern usually appears
when you are boxed in from both sides:
{f|断|ことわ}るわけにもいかず、{f|引|ひ}き{f|受|う}けてしまった.

Register is neutral in itself. The polite <code>わけにはいきません</code> is
completely ordinary at work; <code>わけにはまいりません</code> is humble and belongs
to formal apologies and customer-facing speech. In casual conversation the `は`
often drops - <code>{f|寝|ね}るわけにいかないんだよ</code>.

<div class="note">

**Note** - the double negative <code>～ないわけにはいかない</code> means "I have to",
and the reluctance is part of the meaning. It is the sentence of someone who has
looked for a way out and not found one. Do not use it for things you are glad to
do: ❌<code>{f|好|す}きな{f|人|ひと}に{f|会|あ}わないわけにはいかない</code> is a
strange thing to say about a date.

</div>

## Contrast

### ～わけにはいかない vs ～てはいけない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てはいけない` is a **prohibition from outside**: a rule, a
parent, a sign. `わけにはいかない` is **you stopping yourself**, for reasons you
would have to explain.

</div>

<pre>
{f|館内|かんない}では{f|飲食|いんしょく}し<b>てはいけません</b>。
Eating and drinking are not allowed inside.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|会議|かいぎ}の{f|途中|とちゅう}なので、{f|席|せき}を{f|立|た}つ<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
We're in the middle of a meeting, so I can hardly get up and leave.
</pre>

Nothing forbids leaving a meeting; you simply cannot do it and remain the person
you want to be at that table. Put `てはいけない` there and you invent a rule that
does not exist. Conversely, `わけにはいかない` on a museum notice would be
peculiar - the building has no feelings to protect.

A quick test: if you can name **who** forbids it, use `てはいけない`.

### ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` is **neutral necessity**; it says nothing
about how you feel. <code>～ないわけにはいかない</code> says you would rather not,
and cannot see a way out.

</div>

<pre>
{f|申込書|もうしこみしょ}には{f|印鑑|いんかん}を{f|押|お}さ<b>なければなりません</b>。
You have to put your seal on the application form.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|部長|ぶちょう}に{f|直接|ちょくせつ}{f|頼|たの}まれたので、{f|引|ひ}き{f|受|う}け<b>ないわけにはいかなかった</b>。
The manager asked me personally, so I couldn't very well refuse.
</pre>

The first is a form's requirement and carries no emotion at all - it would be
identical on a website's help page. The second is a small story about pressure:
you did not want the job, you looked for an exit, and the way it was asked closed
it. Swap them and the form sentence becomes oddly resentful, while the second
becomes a flat report that misses the whole point.

This is also why <code>ないわけにはいかない</code> almost always has a **reason
clause** in front of it. Without one, the reluctance has nothing to attach to.

### ～わけにはいかない vs ～ことができない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ことができない` is genuine **inability**: the skill, the
permission or the physics is missing. `わけにはいかない` is **unwillingness on
principle**, with the ability intact.

</div>

<pre>
{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|読|よ}めないので、この{f|書類|しょるい}に{f|署名|しょめい}する<b>ことができません</b>。
I can't read the kanji, so I'm unable to sign this document.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|中身|なかみ}を{f|確認|かくにん}していないので、この{f|書類|しょるい}に{f|署名|しょめい}する<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
I haven't checked what's in it, so I'm not prepared to sign this document.
</pre>

The first speaker cannot sign. The second could sign in a second, and is refusing
on principle. That distinction is often the difference between an apology and a
stand, and choosing the wrong one misrepresents you badly - the first sentence
sounds helpless where you meant to sound careful.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|今日|きょう}は{f|帰|かえ}る**わけにはいけません**。 → ✅ {f|今日|きょう}は{f|帰|かえ}る<b>わけにはいきません</b>。
  The verb is <code>いく</code>, negated as <code>いかない／いきません</code>. There
  is no potential form in this pattern.

- ❌ {f|泳|およ}げないので、{f|向|む}こう{f|岸|ぎし}まで{f|行|い}く**わけにはいかない**。 → ✅ {f|泳|およ}げないので、{f|向|む}こう{f|岸|ぎし}まで{f|行|い}く<b>ことができない</b>。
  Real inability is `ことができない`. `わけにはいかない` requires that you *could*
  do the thing.

- ❌（on a sign）ここに{f|車|くるま}を{f|止|と}める**わけにはいきません**。 → ✅ ここに{f|車|くるま}を{f|止|と}め<b>てはいけません</b>。
  A notice issues a prohibition. `わけにはいかない` reports a private conflict, and
  car parks do not have those.

- ❌ {f|君|きみ}は{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|破|やぶ}る**わけにはいかないよ**。 → ✅ {f|君|きみ}は{f|約束|やくそく}を{f|破|やぶ}っ<b>てはいけないよ</b>。
  Aimed at someone else, the pattern claims to know what their conscience will
  allow. If you mean to tell them not to, say so directly.

- ❌ {f|大好|だいす}きなケーキだから、{f|食|た}べ**ないわけにはいかない**。 → ✅ {f|大好|だいす}きなケーキだから、{f|食|た}べ<b>ずにはいられない</b>。
  <code>ないわけにはいかない</code> carries reluctance. For something you cannot
  resist, you want the "can't help it" pattern instead.

## Practice

**1.** {f|明日|あした}は{f|大事|だいじ}な{f|試験|しけん}だから、{f|今夜|こんや}{f|遊|あそ}びに{f|行|い}く____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|今夜|こんや}{f|遊|あそ}びに{f|行|い}く<b>わけにはいかない</b>。

Nothing physically prevents you going out; the exam does. That gap between "could"
and "can't bring myself to" is exactly what the pattern expresses, and the reason
clause in front supplies the pressure.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|足|あし}をけがしているので、{f|今日|きょう}の{f|練習|れんしゅう}に{f|参加|さんか}する（ことができません・わけにはいきません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|参加|さんか}する<b>ことができません</b>。

An injury is real inability, so `ことができない` is the honest form.
<code>わけにはいきません</code> would imply your leg is fine and you are declining
on principle, which would puzzle the coach.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "They lent me the money without asking any questions, so I can't not pay it back."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|何|なに}も{f|聞|き}かずにお{f|金|かね}を{f|貸|か}してくれたのだから、{f|返|かえ}さ<b>ないわけにはいかない</b>。

The double negative is the point: paying it back is not something you chose, it is
something the kindness has made unavoidable. <code>{f|返|かえ}さなければならない</code>
would state the obligation flatly and lose the sense of being morally cornered.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|部長|ぶちょう}に{f|誘|さそ}われたので、{f|飲|の}み{f|会|かい}に{f|行|い}かないわけにはいけませんでした。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}に{f|誘|さそ}われたので、{f|飲|の}み{f|会|かい}に{f|行|い}か<b>ないわけにはいきませんでした</b>。

The only fault is <code>いけません</code> for <code>いきません</code>. The rest is
a model use of the double negative: an invitation from a superior is precisely the
kind of pressure that removes your options without anyone ordering anything.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|明日|あした}は{f|早|はや}く{f|起|お}きなければなりません。
(b) {f|明日|あした}は{f|早|はや}く{f|起|お}き<b>ないわけにはいきません</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is a plain statement of necessity. There is an early train or an early
meeting, and the speaker reports the fact without any attitude to it. You could
say it cheerfully.

(b) adds a person who does not want to. It implies you have already thought about
staying in bed, considered whether anyone would mind, and concluded that you
cannot get away with it. The obligation in (a) comes from the timetable; in (b) it
comes from your own sense of what you owe someone.

</details>
