# ～しかない

**Meaning** · there's nothing for it but to… - the circumstances have closed every other door

`しか` means "nothing other than", and it always drags a negative along with it.
Put it after a verb and the sentence says: of all the things one might do here,
exactly one remains. Nobody ordered it and nobody judged it - the situation simply
ran out of options.

## Formation

The verb stays in the dictionary form. `しか` here does the same work it does after
a noun, which you already know from <code>{f|千円|せんえん}しかない</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + しかない | {f|待|ま}つ → {f|待|ま}つしかない |
| Verb (する) | ～するしかない | {f|我慢|がまん}する → {f|我慢|がまん}するしかない |
| Noun | Noun + しかない | {f|千円|せんえん} → {f|千円|せんえん}しかない |
| Past | ～しかなかった | {f|待|ま}つしかなかった |
| Polite | ～しかありません | {f|待|ま}つしかありません |
| Hedged guess | ～しかなさそうだ・～しかないだろう | {f|待|ま}つしかなさそうだ |
| Formal written equivalent | ～ほかない・～よりほかない | {f|待|ま}つよりほかない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - there is no positive form. ❌<code>{f|待|ま}つしかある</code> does not
exist; `しか` is grammatically bound to `ない`. The negative is doing the work of
"other than", not of "not".

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
バスがないから、{f|歩|ある}く<b>しかない</b>。
There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.
</pre>

<pre>
こうなったら、やる<b>しかありません</b>。
Now that it's come to this, we just have to do it.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|終電|しゅうでん}を{f|逃|のが}したので、タクシーで{f|帰|かえ}る<b>しかなかった</b>。
I missed the last train, so there was nothing for it but to take a taxi home.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}がやむまで、ここで{f|待|ま}つ<b>しかないですね</b>。
Looks like we've no choice but to wait here until the rain stops.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|部品|ぶひん}がもう{f|作|つく}られていないので、{f|新|あたら}しいのを{f|買|か}う<b>しかありません</b>。
The part isn't made any more, so there's nothing for it but to buy a new one.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|誰|だれ}も{f|手伝|てつだ}ってくれないなら、{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|全部|ぜんぶ}やる<b>しかない</b>。
If nobody's going to help, I'll just have to do the whole thing on my own.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|説明書|せつめいしょ}を{f|何度|なんど}{f|読|よ}んでも{f|分|わ}からないので、メーカーに{f|電話|でんわ}して{f|聞|き}く<b>しかなさそうです</b>。
I've read the manual over and over and still don't understand it, so I'll probably just have to ring the maker.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|店|みせ}の{f|売上|うりあげ}がここまで{f|落|お}ちてしまった{f|以上|いじょう}、{f|営業|えいぎょう}{f|時間|じかん}を{f|短|みじか}くする<b>しかないでしょう</b>。
With takings down this far, we're going to have to cut the opening hours.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|試合|しあい}に{f|勝|か}つには、{f|残|のこ}り{f|五分|ごふん}で{f|二点|にてん}{f|取|と}る<b>しかない</b>。
To win the match, we have to score twice in the last five minutes - there's no other way.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

The feeling is **resignation**, but not always gloom. The same sentence can be a
sigh or a squaring of the shoulders, depending on delivery:
<code>やるしかない</code> is one of the most common things a Japanese speaker says
before starting something difficult, and it is closer to "right, let's get on with
it" than to despair. What is constant is that the alternatives have been
considered and found gone.

That is the pattern's boundary. `しかない` reports what **circumstances** have left
you. It does not report a rule, an order, or a moral duty. A deadline set by your
boss is <code>{f|出|だ}さなければならない</code>; a duty you feel is
<code>{f|行|い}かないわけにはいかない</code>; a situation in which the only
remaining option is to hand it in unfinished is
<code>{f|出|だ}すしかない</code>.

Register is neutral, and the polite form is simply
<code>しかありません</code>. Two siblings sit above it in formality:
<code>～ほかない</code> and <code>～よりほかない</code> are written, slightly
literary equivalents, and <code>～ざるを{f|得|え}ない</code> is formal and adds a
sense of being compelled against your will - an inspection you must accept, a
resignation you must tender. In conversation, `しかない` covers almost everything
those two do.

One small caution. Applied to somebody else's situation, `しかない` can sound
dismissive - <code>あきらめるしかないですよ</code> tells a person their problem is
over. Softening it to <code>しかないかもしれませんね</code> leaves them their
options.

## Contrast

### ～しかない vs ～なければならない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` comes from **outside**: a rule, an order, a
deadline. `しかない` comes from the **situation**: the other options have
disappeared.

</div>

<pre>
{f|外国人|がいこくじん}{f|登録|とうろく}の{f|書類|しょるい}は{f|本人|ほんにん}が{f|出|だ}さ<b>なければなりません</b>。
The registration paperwork has to be submitted by the person themselves.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|郵便|ゆうびん}が{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わないので、{f|自分|じぶん}で{f|持|も}っていく<b>しかありません</b>。
The post won't get there in time, so I'll have to take it in myself.
</pre>

The first would still be true if the post were quick, the office were near and you
had all week; a regulation does not care. The second exists only because of a
timing problem, and it would evaporate if the problem did. Ask yourself: **who
says so?** If the answer is a person or a rulebook, use `なければならない`.

### ～しかない vs ～ざるを{f|得|え}ない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both mean "have no choice", but `しかない` is about **options
running out**, while <code>～ざるを{f|得|え}ない</code> is about being **compelled
against your own wishes**, usually by logic, duty or authority. It is also
distinctly formal.

</div>

<pre>
{f|終|お}わらないので、{f|明日|あした}{f|続|つづ}きをやる<b>しかない</b>。
It isn't going to be finished, so I'll just carry on tomorrow.
</pre>

<pre>
これだけの{f|証拠|しょうこ}がそろえば、{f|事実|じじつ}だと<b>{f|認|みと}めざるを{f|得|え}ない</b>。
With this much evidence, one is forced to accept it as fact.
</pre>

<code>ざるを{f|得|え}ない</code> belongs to reports, editorials and formal speech,
and it carries a note of reluctance that `しかない` does not require. Saying it
about tomorrow's leftover work would sound as though the work had defeated you in
some tragic way. Conversely, `しかない` in the evidence sentence would make the
conclusion sound like a practical inconvenience rather than an intellectual
surrender.

### ～しかない vs ～だけだ

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `だけだ` says **that is all that's left to do**, neutrally, and
often happily. `しかない` says **there is nothing else you could do**, with the
other options mourned.

</div>

<pre>
{f|準備|じゅんび}は{f|終|お}わった。あとは{f|本番|ほんばん}を{f|待|ま}つ<b>だけだ</b>。
The preparation's done. All that's left is to wait for the day.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|準備|じゅんび}が{f|間|ま}に{f|合|あ}わなかった。このまま{f|本番|ほんばん}に{f|出|で}る<b>しかない</b>。
We didn't get everything ready. We'll just have to go on as we are.
</pre>

`だけだ` looks forward from a job well done; `しかない` looks back at options that
closed. The two are not interchangeable even where both are grammatical - telling
a nervous performer <code>{f|楽|たの}しむだけだよ</code> is encouragement, while
<code>{f|楽|たの}しむしかないよ</code> implies the performance itself is a write-off.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ バスがないから{f|歩|ある}か**ないしかない**。 → ✅ バスがないから{f|歩|ある}く<b>しかない</b>。
  `しかない` attaches to the **dictionary form**. The negative is already inside
  `ない` at the end; putting another one on the verb reverses the sentence.

- ❌ さいふには{f|百円|ひゃくえん}**しかあります**。 → ✅ さいふには{f|百円|ひゃくえん}<b>しかありません</b>。
  `しか` can never end in a positive. It is one half of a pair, and `ない` is the
  other half.

- ❌ パスポートの{f|申請|しんせい}は{f|本人|ほんにん}が{f|行|い}く**しかありません**。（reporting the rule） → ✅ パスポートの{f|申請|しんせい}は{f|本人|ほんにん}が{f|行|い}か<b>なければなりません</b>。
  A published requirement is an obligation from outside, not a situation that ran
  out of options.

- ❌ このお{f|菓子|かし}はここに**しか{f|売|う}っています**。 → ✅ このお{f|菓子|かし}はここで<b>しか{f|売|う}っていません</b>。
  Two things: the sentence needs its negative, and `しか` follows the particle it
  attaches to - <code>ここで</code>, since the shop is the location of selling.

## Practice

**1.** {f|雪|ゆき}で{f|電車|でんしゃ}が{f|止|と}まってしまった。{f|今日|きょう}は{f|駅前|えきまえ}のホテルに{f|泊|と}まる____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|駅前|えきまえ}のホテルに{f|泊|と}まる<b>しかない</b>。

The trains have stopped, so getting home is off the table - the situation has
closed the other doors, which is exactly `しかない`. Note the dictionary form
<code>{f|泊|と}まる</code> in front of it.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|会社|かいしゃ}の{f|規則|きそく}で、{f|出張|しゅっちょう}の{f|報告書|ほうこくしょ}は{f|三日|みっか}{f|以内|いない}に{f|出|だ}（さなければなりません・すしかありません）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|三日|みっか}{f|以内|いない}に{f|出|だ}<b>さなければなりません</b>。

<code>{f|会社|かいしゃ}の{f|規則|きそく}で</code> names the source of the
obligation, and a rule imposed from outside is `なければならない`.
<code>{f|出|だ}すしかありません</code> would suggest that circumstances happened to
leave you with no other way of dealing with the report.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The shop was closed, so we had no choice but to eat at the station."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|店|みせ}が{f|閉|し}まっていたので、{f|駅|えき}で{f|食|た}べる<b>しかありませんでした</b>。

The past polite form is <code>しかありませんでした</code>; plain past is
<code>しかなかった</code>. The closed shop is a circumstance, not a rule, which is
what makes `しかない` right here.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|時間|じかん}がないので、タクシーを{f|使|つか}わないしかなかったです。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|時間|じかん}がないので、タクシーを<b>{f|使|つか}うしかなかったです</b>。

The verb before `しかない` is positive and in the dictionary form. As written, the
sentence tries to negate twice and ends up saying something like "there was no
option of not using a taxi", which is not what the speaker means. Polite:
<code>{f|使|つか}うしかありませんでした</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) あとは{f|結果|けっか}を{f|待|ま}つだけだ。
(b) あとは{f|結果|けっか}を{f|待|ま}つしかない。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is calm and slightly satisfied. Everything that could be done has been done,
and waiting is the natural next step - it fits someone who has just posted a good
application.

(b) admits helplessness. It says there is nothing you *can* do, which implies you
would act if you could, and often that you are not confident about the outcome.
Same waiting, two different states of mind: (a) is finished, (b) is stuck.

</details>
