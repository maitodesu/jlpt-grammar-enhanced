# ～向き - facing

**Meaning** · facing such-and-such a way - the direction a thing is turned toward（むき）

<code>{f|向|む}き</code> is the noun form of <code>{f|向|む}く</code>, "to face". Stuck
onto a direction word it gives you the orientation of something that is standing
still: a south-facing room, an arrow pointing down, an envelope placed face up.

There is no movement in it. A south-facing window is not going anywhere; it is
simply turned that way. That single fact separates this pattern from
<code>に{f|向|む}かって</code>, and it is where learners slip.

The same word has a second, unrelated-looking life meaning "suited to", as in
<code>{f|子|こ}ども{f|向|む}きの{f|本|ほん}</code>. That sense is treated separately;
this page is only about direction.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direction word | {f|南|みなみ}・{f|北|きた}・{f|東|ひがし}・{f|西|にし} + {f|向|む}き | {f|南|みなみ} → {f|南|みなみ}{f|向|む}きの{f|部屋|へや} |
| Up / down / sideways | {f|上|うわ}・{f|下|した}・{f|横|よこ} + {f|向|む}き | {f|下|した} → {f|下|した}{f|向|む}きの{f|矢印|やじるし} |
| Front / back (also figurative) | {f|前|まえ}・{f|後|うし}ろ + {f|向|む}き | {f|前|まえ} → {f|前|まえ}{f|向|む}きに{f|考|かんが}える |
| As a bare noun | Noun + の + {f|向|む}き | いす → いすの{f|向|む}きを{f|変|か}える |
| Adverbially, before a verb | ～{f|向|む}きに + verb | {f|上|うわ}{f|向|む}き → {f|上|うわ}{f|向|む}きに{f|置|お}く |
| As a predicate | ～{f|向|む}きだ／である | {f|東|ひがし} → {f|玄関|げんかん}は{f|東|ひがし}{f|向|む}きだ |

<div class="note">

**Note on readings** - two of these are worth memorising as whole words rather
than as sums. <code>{f|上|うわ}{f|向|む}き</code> is standardly うわむき, not うえむき.
And <code>{f|風|かざ}{f|向|む}き</code> is かざむき: <code>{f|風|かぜ}</code> takes its
bound form かざ inside compounds, as it does in
<code>{f|風車|かざぐるま}</code> and <code>{f|風上|かざかみ}</code>. In both words the
<code>{f|向|む}き</code> half is regular. <code>{f|下|した}{f|向|む}き</code> is regular
throughout.

</div>

</pre>

{f|封筒|ふうとう}は{f|表|おもて}を{f|上|うわ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>にして{f|箱|はこ}に{f|入|い}れてください。

When you rent a flat, it's worth checking which way the windows face, not just the rent and the floor space.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

This is an ordinary, register-neutral noun. Nothing about it is formal or casual;
it turns up in estate agents' listings, in assembly instructions, in weather
reports and in conversation, all in the same shape.

Two of its habitats are worth knowing about, because they are where you will
actually meet it.

**Property.** <code>{f|南|みなみ}{f|向|む}き</code> is a selling point in Japan, and
listings advertise it the way an English listing advertises a garden. The reason is
practical - sun for the laundry and warmth in winter - and the vocabulary is fixed:
<code>{f|南|みなみ}{f|向|む}きバルコニー</code>,
<code>{f|東|ひがし}{f|向|む}きの{f|窓|まど}</code>.

**Attitude.** <code>{f|前|まえ}{f|向|む}き</code> and
<code>{f|後|うし}ろ{f|向|む}き</code> have drifted from literal facing to mean
"positive / constructive" and "negative / defeatist". These are extremely common in
work Japanese: <code>{f|前|まえ}{f|向|む}きに{f|検討|けんとう}します</code> is what a
company says when it wants to sound willing.

<div class="note">

**Note** - <code>{f|前|まえ}{f|向|む}きに{f|検討|けんとう}します</code> is a famous soft
refusal. Literally "we will consider it positively", it very often means the matter
will quietly go nowhere. Take it as encouragement, not as a promise.

</div>

The bare noun <code>{f|向|む}き</code> also appears in a fixed phrase you will hear
in shops and offices: <code>{f|向|む}きを{f|変|か}える</code>, "turn it round". And
<code>{f|風|かざ}{f|向|む}き</code> has its own figurative use - 
<code>{f|風|かざ}{f|向|む}きが{f|変|か}わった</code>, "the wind has changed", said of
a situation turning in someone's favour.

## Contrast

### ～{f|向|む}き vs ～に{f|向|む}かって

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|向|む}き</code> is a **static orientation**;
<code>に{f|向|む}かって</code> is **movement or action toward** something. If anything
is travelling, you need <code>に{f|向|む}かって</code>.

</div>

<pre>
その{f|像|ぞう}は{f|海|うみ}のほうを{f|向|む}いて{f|立|た}っている。
The statue stands facing the sea.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|船|ふね}は{f|島|しま}に<b>{f|向|む}かって</b>{f|進|すす}んでいる。
The ship is heading toward the island.
</pre>

The statue is not going to the sea; it is merely turned that way, and
<code>{f|海|うみ}{f|向|む}き</code> would describe it. The ship is under way, so
<code>に{f|向|む}かって</code> is the only option: ❌<code>{f|島|しま}{f|向|む}きに{f|進|すす}む</code>
does not work. Note also the grammatical shape - <code>{f|向|む}き</code> attaches
directly to the noun with no particle, while <code>{f|向|む}かって</code> demands `に`.

### {f|向|む}き vs {f|方向|ほうこう}

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|向|む}き</code> is **which way a thing is turned**;
<code>{f|方向|ほうこう}</code> is **a direction out in the world**, the way you go.
A chair has a <code>{f|向|む}き</code>; a road has a <code>{f|方向|ほうこう}</code>.

</div>

<pre>
テーブルの<b>{f|向|む}き</b>を{f|少|すこ}し{f|右|みぎ}に{f|回|まわ}した。
I turned the table a little to the right.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|駅|えき}はこの<b>{f|方向|ほうこう}</b>でいいですか。
Is the station this way?
</pre>

Swapping them produces sentences that sound off rather than wrong. ❌<code>テーブルの{f|方向|ほうこう}を{f|変|か}える</code>
suggests moving the table somewhere else, not rotating it; ❌<code>{f|駅|えき}はこの{f|向|む}きですか</code>
sounds as though the station is facing you. The pair
<code>{f|向|む}きを{f|変|か}える</code> (rotate) versus
<code>{f|方向|ほうこう}を{f|変|か}える</code> (change course) is the cleanest way to
hold them apart.

### ～{f|向|む}き vs ～{f|向|む}け

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - only <code>{f|向|む}き</code> does direction.
<code>{f|向|む}け</code> means "made for, aimed at an audience" and never means
"facing".

</div>

<pre>
{f|北|きた}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>の{f|窓|まど}からは{f|一日中|いちにちじゅう}{f|日|ひ}が{f|入|はい}らない。
A north-facing window gets no sun all day.
</pre>

<pre>
これは{f|海外|かいがい}<b>{f|向|む}け</b>に{f|作|つく}られたモデルです。
This is a model built for overseas markets.
</pre>

❌<code>{f|北|きた}{f|向|む}けの{f|窓|まど}</code> is not Japanese. The two suffixes do
overlap in the *other* sense of <code>{f|向|む}き</code> - "suitable for" - where
<code>{f|子|こ}ども{f|向|む}き</code> and <code>{f|子|こ}ども{f|向|む}け</code> are both
possible and differ subtly. In the direction sense there is no overlap at all, so
here at least the choice is easy.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|南|みなみ}に{f|向|む}きです。 → ✅ この{f|部屋|へや}は{f|南|みなみ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>です。
  The direction word joins directly to <code>{f|向|む}き</code> as a compound noun.
  There is no particle in between. With `に` you would need the verb:
  <code>{f|南|みなみ}に{f|向|む}いています</code>.

- ❌ {f|北|きた}{f|向|む}けのベランダは{f|洗濯物|せんたくもの}が{f|乾|かわ}きにくい。 → ✅ {f|北|きた}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>のベランダは{f|洗濯物|せんたくもの}が{f|乾|かわ}きにくい。
  <code>{f|向|む}け</code> means "intended for" and cannot express facing. Only
  <code>{f|向|む}き</code> takes a compass point.

- ❌ {f|子|こ}どもたちは{f|公園|こうえん}{f|向|む}きに{f|走|はし}っていった。 → ✅ {f|子|こ}どもたちは{f|公園|こうえん}に<b>{f|向|む}かって</b>{f|走|はし}っていった。
  Running is movement, and <code>{f|向|む}き</code> has no movement in it. Anything
  that travels takes <code>に{f|向|む}かって</code>.

- ❌ カメラの{f|方向|ほうこう}を{f|上|うえ}にしてください。 → ✅ カメラの<b>{f|向|む}き</b>を{f|上|うわ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>にしてください。
  Tilting the camera changes how it is turned, not where it is going.
  <code>{f|方向|ほうこう}</code> would suggest carrying it somewhere.

## Practice

**1.** この{f|建物|たてもの}の{f|入口|いりぐち}は{f|西|にし}____なので、{f|夕方|ゆうがた}はまぶしい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|入口|いりぐち}は{f|西|にし}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>なので、{f|夕方|ゆうがた}はまぶしい。

A compass point plus <code>{f|向|む}き</code> gives the orientation of a fixed
thing. No particle comes between the two, and
<code>{f|西|にし}{f|向|む}け</code> would be meaningless here.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|台風|たいふう}は{f|北|きた}（{f|向|む}きに・に{f|向|む}かって）{f|進|すす}んでいます。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|台風|たいふう}は{f|北|きた}<b>に{f|向|む}かって</b>{f|進|すす}んでいます。

A typhoon is moving, and <code>{f|向|む}き</code> describes only static facing.
Weather forecasts say <code>{f|北|きた}に{f|向|む}かって</code> or
<code>{f|北|きた}へ{f|進|すす}む</code>; ❌<code>{f|北|きた}{f|向|む}きに{f|進|すす}む</code>
would try to give a storm a fixed orientation.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Please lay the photographs face up on the desk."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|写真|しゃしん}は{f|上|うわ}<b>{f|向|む}き</b>にして{f|机|つくえ}の{f|上|うえ}に{f|置|お}いてください。

<code>{f|上|うわ}{f|向|む}き</code> - note the うわ reading - plus
<code>にする</code> gives "put it into a face-up orientation". A common alternative
is <code>{f|表|おもて}を{f|上|うえ}にして</code>, which says the same thing by naming
the front rather than the direction.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|寝|ね}るときは{f|下|した}{f|向|む}きより{f|上|うえ}{f|向|む}きのほうが{f|体|からだ}にいいと{f|言|い}われている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|寝|ね}るときは{f|下|した}{f|向|む}きより<b>{f|上|うわ}{f|向|む}き</b>のほうが{f|体|からだ}にいいと{f|言|い}われている。

The grammar is fine; the **reading** is not. The standard reading of
<code>{f|上|うわ}{f|向|む}き</code> is うわむき, even though
<code>{f|下|した}{f|向|む}き</code> is the regular したむき. This is a compound whose
first element keeps the older うわ form, as in
<code>{f|上着|うわぎ}</code> and <code>{f|上手|うわて}</code>.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) いすを{f|窓|まど}のほうに<b>{f|向|む}けた</b>。
(b) いすの<b>{f|向|む}き</b>を{f|変|か}えた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) uses the transitive verb <code>{f|向|む}ける</code> and names the destination of
the turning: the chair now faces the window specifically. The window is the point of
the sentence.

(b) uses the noun and says only that the orientation is now different from what it
was. Where it ended up is left open. In practice (b) is what you say while doing it
and (a) is what you say when the direction matters - for instance because you wanted
to look outside.

</details>
