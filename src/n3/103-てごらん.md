# ～てごらん

**Meaning** · go on, try it; have a look - a warm, gentle command, always aimed downward

`ごらん` comes from the honorific verb for "look", but do not let that mislead you:
in `～てごらん` the respect has been worn away completely. What is left is an
**encouraging imperative** that an adult uses to a child, a teacher to a student, a
senior to a junior. It is kind. It is also unmistakably from above.

## Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ごらん | {f|食|た}べる → {f|食|た}べてごらん |
| Verb (する) | ～してごらん | {f|想像|そうぞう}する → {f|想像|そうぞう}してごらん |
| Fuller, more instructional | ～てごらんなさい | {f|食|た}べてごらんなさい |
| Softened with a particle | ～てごらんよ・～てごらんね | {f|食|た}べてごらんよ |
| As a gentle invitation | ～てごらん? | {f|読|よ}んでごらん? |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - there is **no negative**. ❌<code>{f|見|み}ないでごらん</code>
does not exist. If you want someone not to do something, you are outside this
pattern altogether.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - the kanji is ご{f|覧|らん}, and you will see
<code>{f|見|み}てご{f|覧|らん}</code> in print. In the `～てごらん` pattern kana is
much commoner, and it helps keep this use visually separate from the honorific
<code>ご{f|覧|らん}ください</code>.

</div>

## Nuance & register

The one thing to fix in your head is **direction**. `～てごらん` flows downhill:
parent to child, teacher to pupil, coach to player, an older friend to a much
younger one. Between adults of equal standing it sounds patronising, and pointed
at anyone senior it is close to rude - no amount of politeness elsewhere in the
sentence rescues it. This surprises learners, because the word inside it is an
honorific one; the honorific is directed at the *looking*, historically, not at
the listener.

Within its range, though, it is genuinely affectionate. Compared with the plain
imperative <code>{f|食|た}べろ</code> or even with
<code>{f|食|た}べなさい</code>, `～てごらん` invites rather than orders. It very
often comes with a reason or a reassurance attached - 
<code>おいしいよ</code>, <code>{f|大丈夫|だいじょうぶ}だから</code> - and that is
part of its character: it is the grammar of coaxing.

<code>～てごらんなさい</code> is the fuller form. It is a little more formal and a
little more instructional, which is why it turns up in children's books, teaching
materials and the speech of teachers; <code>～てごらん</code> is the clipped,
homelier version you hear at a kitchen table. Neither is polite in the ます sense - 
adding です or ます to them is not possible.

You will also meet it addressed to nobody in particular, in lyrics, adverts and
narration: <code>{f|空|そら}を{f|見|み}てごらん</code>. There it reads as gentle,
slightly poetic, and the downward angle becomes warmth rather than authority.

## Contrast

### ～てごらん vs ～てみて

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `てみて` is neutral and works in **any** direction.
`てごらん` adds warmth and a difference in standing, and only works **downward**.

</div>

<pre>
これ、おいしいので{f|食|た}べ<b>てみてください</b>。
Do try this, it's delicious.
</pre>

<pre>
これ、おいしいから{f|食|た}べ<b>てごらん</b>。
Go on, try this - it's delicious.
</pre>

To a guest, a colleague or a customer, the first is the only safe option. The
second is what you would say to your nephew. Note that they are not doing the same
grammatical work either: <code>てみる</code> means "do it and find out", so
<code>{f|食|た}べてみて</code> is literally "eat it and see", while
<code>{f|食|た}べてごらん</code> is closer to "let's see you eat it".

### ～てごらん vs ～てみなさい

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - both are downward. `てみなさい` is an **instruction** and can
be stern; `てごらん` is an **invitation** and is warm.

</div>

<pre>
もう{f|一度|いちど}、{f|最初|さいしょ}から{f|読|よ}ん<b>でみなさい</b>。
Read it again from the beginning.
</pre>

<pre>
もう{f|一度|いちど}、{f|最初|さいしょ}から{f|読|よ}ん<b>でごらん</b>。
Have another go at it from the start.
</pre>

A teacher who has said the same thing three times uses the first; a teacher
sitting beside a struggling child uses the second. `なさい` carries the authority
of an order that expects compliance, which is why parents reach for it when
patience is running out. `ごらん` expects willingness rather than obedience.

### ～てごらん vs ご{f|覧|らん}ください

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - same word, opposite directions.
<code>ご{f|覧|らん}ください</code> and <code>ご{f|覧|らん}になる</code> are
**honorific**: they raise the listener. `～てごらん` does not.

</div>

<pre>
{f|資料|しりょう}の{f|三|さん}ページ{f|目|め}を<b>ご{f|覧|らん}ください</b>。
Please look at page three of the handout.
</pre>

<pre>
ここ、{f|見|み}<b>てごらん</b>。{f|名前|なまえ}が{f|書|か}いてあるよ。
Look here - your name's written on it.
</pre>

The first is what you say to an audience, a client or your manager; the second is
what you say to a seven-year-old. Because they look so similar, learners produce
the fatal hybrid ❌<code>{f|見|み}てごらんください</code>, which staples an
honorific ending onto a downward command. It is not a politer `てごらん`; it is a
sentence that cannot be repaired without changing pattern.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}、この{f|写真|しゃしん}を{f|見|み}**てごらんください**。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}、この{f|写真|しゃしん}を<b>ご{f|覧|らん}ください</b>。
  `てごらん` cannot be made polite by adding ください. To ask a superior to look,
  use the honorific verb on its own.

- ❌ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、こちらの{f|席|せき}に{f|座|すわ}っ**てごらん**。 → ✅ {f|部長|ぶちょう}、こちらの{f|席|せき}に<b>お{f|座|すわ}りください</b>。
  The pattern is downward only. Aimed at a superior it reads as though you were
  addressing a child.

- ❌ その{f|箱|はこ}は{f|開|あ}けない**でごらん**。 → ✅ その{f|箱|はこ}は{f|開|あ}け<b>ないでね</b>。
  There is no negative form. Use an ordinary negative request instead.

- ❌ もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|言|い}う**ごらん**。 → ✅ もう{f|一度|いちど}{f|言|い}っ<b>てごらん</b>。
  `ごらん` attaches to the て-form, like `てみる` and `てください`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|自転車|じてんしゃ}、こわくないから{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|乗|の}っ____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|乗|の}っ<b>てごらん</b>。

The reassurance <code>こわくないから</code> in front of it is the giveaway: this is
an adult coaxing a child onto a bike, which is the pattern's home ground. The
fuller <code>{f|乗|の}ってごらんなさい</code> would sound more like an instructor
than a parent.

</details>

**2.** Choose: （お{f|客様|きゃくさま}に）こちらの{f|新製品|しんせいひん}を{f|試|ため}し（てごらん・てみてください）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|試|ため}し<b>てみてください</b>。

A customer must never be spoken to downward, and `てごらん` is downward by
definition. <code>てみてください</code> is neutral and polite; in a shop you would
often go further still with
<code>お{f|試|ため}しください</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese, said by a parent to a small child: "Look - the moon's come out."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|見|み}<b>てごらん</b>。お{f|月|つき}さまが{f|出|で}てきたよ。

`てごらん` fits the speaker and the listener exactly, and the final `よ` matches
its warm, coaxing tone. <code>{f|見|み}なさい</code> would turn the same sentence
into an instruction.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|社長|しゃちょう}、こちらのグラフを{f|見|み}てごらんください。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|社長|しゃちょう}、こちらのグラフを<b>ご{f|覧|らん}ください</b>。

<code>てごらんください</code> combines a downward command with an honorific
ending, and the two cannot coexist. The honorific verb
<code>ご{f|覧|らん}になる</code> supplies the polite request on its own.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) もう{f|少|すこ}し{f|待|ま}ってみて。
(b) もう{f|少|すこ}し{f|待|ま}ってごらん。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) is neutral. You are asking someone - anyone, of any standing - to wait and see
what happens. Between adults it is the ordinary way to say it.

(b) adds a difference in position and a note of encouragement. The speaker knows
something the listener does not, and is reassuring them that waiting will be
worthwhile. Said to a child or a junior colleague it is warm; said to your boss it
would sound as though you were patting them on the head.

</details>
