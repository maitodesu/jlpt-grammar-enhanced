# ～わけではない

**Meaning** · it's not that ～; that doesn't mean ～ - cancels a conclusion somebody has drawn, without denying the facts behind it

`わけではない` is a **partial** denial. Something has been said, or is about to be
assumed, and the natural inference from it is wrong - so you reach in and take away
the inference, leaving everything else standing. English does the same job with
"it's not that…", "that's not to say…" and "not necessarily".

**What is negated: the inference, not the possibility.** `わけがない` slams the door
on the whole idea; `わけではない` only says the idea does not follow from what you
have just heard. Learners swap the two constantly, and the results are much ruder
than intended.

## Formation

<code>わけ</code> is a noun, so the clause in front of it takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + わけではない | {f|嫌|きら}っている → {f|嫌|きら}っているわけではない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけではない | {f|払|はら}えない → {f|払|はら}えないわけではない |
| い-adjective | plain form + わけではない | {f|安|やす}い → {f|安|やす}いわけではない |
| な-adjective | ～な + わけではない | {f|嫌|きら}いな → {f|嫌|きら}いなわけではない |
| Noun | ～な / ～という + わけではない | {f|専門家|せんもんか} → {f|専門家|せんもんか}というわけではない |
| Polite | … + わけではありません | {f|反対|はんたい}している → {f|反対|はんたい}しているわけではありません |
| Casual speech | … + わけじゃない | {f|怒|おこ}っている → {f|怒|おこ}っているわけじゃない |
| Softened further | … + というわけではない | {f|嫌|きら}いだ → {f|嫌|きら}いというわけではない |

<div class="note">

**Note** - the pattern is at its most natural with a partner phrase in front of it:
{f|必|かなら}ずしも ("not necessarily"), {f|全|まった}く ("not entirely"), いつも,
{f|全部|ぜんぶ}, or a からといって clause ("just because…"). Those words all set up the
over-general idea that `わけではない` then trims back.

</div>

## Examples

<pre>
{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|走|はし}っているが、{f|大会|たいかい}に{f|出|で}る<b>わけではない</b>。
I run every day, but that doesn't mean I'm going to enter a race.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|反対|はんたい}している<b>わけではありません</b>が、もう{f|少|すこ}し{f|考|かんが}えたいです。
It's not that I'm against it - I'd just like a bit more time to think.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|返事|へんじ}が{f|遅|おそ}くなっただけで、{f|怒|おこ}っている<b>わけじゃない</b>よ。
I was just slow replying; it's not that I'm cross with you.
</pre>

<pre>
お{f|金|かね}がない<b>わけではない</b>が、これは{f|高|たか}すぎる。
It's not that I can't afford it - this is simply too expensive.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|日本語|にほんご}が{f|全|まった}く{f|話|はな}せない<b>わけではない</b>けれど、{f|会議|かいぎ}についていくのは{f|難|むずか}しい。
It isn't that I can't speak Japanese at all, but keeping up in meetings is hard.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|暮|く}らしているからといって、{f|寂|さび}しい<b>わけではない</b>。
Living alone doesn't automatically mean being lonely.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|方法|ほうほう}がいつでもうまくいく<b>というわけではない</b>ので、{f|状況|じょうきょう}を{f|見|み}て{f|判断|はんだん}してください。
This approach doesn't always work, so use your judgment as the situation requires.
</pre>

It's true that I said I want to quit, but that doesn't mean I'm walking out tomorrow.
</pre>

## Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and indispensable in polite conversation. Its social function is
**face**: it lets you disagree with an interpretation while accepting the facts, so
nobody has to be wrong. In a meeting,
<code>{f|予算|よさん}が{f|足|た}りないというわけではありません</code> concedes that money
has been discussed and merely corrects what the listener made of it.

Casual speech contracts it to `わけじゃない`, which is one of the most frequent
phrases in everyday Japanese - 「べつに{f|嫌|きら}いなわけじゃないけど」. The `べつに`
in front is almost a fixed companion, and adding `けど` at the end leaves room for
whatever the real point turns out to be.

Because the pattern *corrects an inference*, it needs the inference to exist. Said
out of the blue, with nothing for it to push back against, it sounds like an answer
to a question nobody asked. Give it a premise: a からといって clause, a previous
remark, or a visible situation.

Two combinations are worth memorising whole. <code>{f|必|かなら}ずしも～わけではない</code>
is the standard "not necessarily", and it is common in written argument.
<code>～ないわけではない</code> is a double negative meaning "it isn't that I can't" - 
grudging agreement, one step short of saying yes.

## Contrast

### わけではない vs わけがない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `わけではない` denies **the inference**; `わけがない` denies **the
possibility**. One trims, the other refuses.

</div>

<pre>
{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|苦手|にがて}な<b>わけではない</b>。ただ{f|書|か}く{f|練習|れんしゅう}が{f|足|た}りないだけだ。
It isn't that I'm bad at kanji - I just haven't practised writing them enough.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|習|なら}ってもいない{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|書|か}ける<b>わけがない</b>。
There's no way anyone can write kanji they've never been taught.
</pre>

The first accepts that the speaker's kanji are weak and rejects only the explanation
offered for it. The second rules the whole proposition out. Swap them and you get
nonsense: ❌<code>{f|漢字|かんじ}が{f|苦手|にがて}なわけがない</code> would insist that being
bad at kanji is impossible.

### わけではない vs ではない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - plain `ではない` denies the **fact**. `わけではない` leaves the
fact alone and denies **what you would read into it**.

</div>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}のコーヒーはおいし<b>くない</b>。
The coffee at this place isn't good.
</pre>

<pre>
この{f|店|みせ}のコーヒーがおいしい<b>わけではない</b>が、{f|静|しず}かなのでよく{f|来|く}る。
It's not as if the coffee here is any good, but it's quiet, so I come often.
</pre>

The first is a verdict on the coffee. The second is a verdict on your reasoning: you
assumed the coffee was why the speaker keeps coming, and they are correcting that,
not reviewing the drink. This is why `わけではない` so often has a `が` or `けど`
clause attached - the real point is usually in the second half.

### わけではない vs とは{f|限|かぎ}らない

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>とは{f|限|かぎ}らない</code> attacks a **generalisation**:
it isn't always so. `わけではない` attacks an **inference about the case in front of
you**.

</div>

<pre>
{f|高|たか}い{f|店|みせ}の{f|料理|りょうり}がおいしいとは<b>{f|限|かぎ}らない</b>。
Expensive restaurants aren't always good.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|値段|ねだん}で{f|選|えら}んだ<b>わけではない</b>。{f|味|あじ}が{f|気|き}に{f|入|い}ったから{f|選|えら}んだのだ。
I didn't choose it on price - I chose it because I liked the taste.
</pre>

The first would sit comfortably in an essay about restaurants in general. The second
is about one decision, one speaker, one misreading being corrected. With
{f|必|かなら}ずしも in front, the two come very close together, and in sentences about
general rules they are often interchangeable.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|嫌|きら}い<b>だわけではない</b>。 → ✅ {f|嫌|きら}い<b>なわけではない</b>。（or {f|嫌|きら}いというわけではない）
  な-adjectives and nouns link to <code>わけ</code> with `な` or `という`, never `だ`.

- ❌ この{f|問題|もんだい}が{f|解|と}ける<b>わけではない</b>。（intended: "there's no way I can solve this"） → ✅ この{f|問題|もんだい}が{f|解|と}ける<b>わけがない</b>。
  For flat impossibility you want `わけがない`. `わけではない` only says that being
  able to solve it does not follow from whatever was just said.

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}は{f|医者|いしゃ}な<b>わけではありません</b>。（simply stating you are not a doctor） → ✅ {f|私|わたし}は{f|医者|いしゃ}<b>ではありません</b>。
  A plain fact takes a plain negative. `わけではない` is for cancelling an inference,
  and using it here implies somebody had just concluded that you were one.

- ❌ {f|寒|さむ}くない<b>わけではない</b>から、{f|上着|うわぎ}はいらない。 → ✅ {f|寒|さむ}くない<b>わけではない</b>から、{f|上着|うわぎ}を{f|持|も}っていこう。
  <code>～ないわけではない</code> is a double negative: "it isn't that it's not cold",
  i.e. it *is* somewhat cold. Make sure the second half agrees with the arithmetic.

## Practice

**1.** ダイエット{f|中|ちゅう}だと{f|言|い}っても、{f|何|なに}も{f|食|た}べない____。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|何|なに}も{f|食|た}べない<b>わけではない</b>。

Saying you are on a diet invites the conclusion that you eat nothing, and the
sentence heads that conclusion off while leaving the diet itself intact - the
textbook use of `わけではない`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|彼|かれ}はまだ{f|新人|しんじん}だから、{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|全部|ぜんぶ}{f|任|まか}せられる（わけがない・わけではない）。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|一人|ひとり}で{f|全部|ぜんぶ}{f|任|まか}せられる<b>わけがない</b>。

He is brand new, and the speaker is ruling the idea out entirely. `わけではない`
would say only that being new does not *mean* he can be trusted with everything,
which is a much milder claim than the からだ clause sets up.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Just because a book is famous doesn't mean it's interesting."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|有名|ゆうめい}な{f|本|ほん}だからといって、{f|面白|おもしろ}い<b>わけではない</b>。

からといって sets up the inference - fame implies quality - and `わけではない` removes
it. The famousness of the book is not being denied at all.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: お{f|茶|ちゃ}が{f|好|す}きだわけではないが、{f|毎朝|まいあさ}{f|飲|の}んでいる。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ お{f|茶|ちゃ}が{f|好|す}き<b>なわけではない</b>が、{f|毎朝|まいあさ}{f|飲|の}んでいる。

<code>{f|好|す}きだ</code> is a な-adjective, so it becomes <code>{f|好|す}きな</code>
before the noun <code>わけ</code>. The sense is right: drinking it daily does not
prove fondness.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|彼女|かのじょ}のことを{f|信|しん}じていない<b>わけがない</b>。
(b) {f|彼女|かのじょ}のことを{f|信|しん}じていない<b>わけではない</b>。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) declares that distrusting her is impossible - of course the speaker believes
her, and the sentence is emphatic, almost indignant.

(b) is a careful concession: the speaker does trust her, up to a point, and is
correcting the impression that they do not. It nearly always continues with が or
けど into the reservation that prompted the sentence in the first place. The
grammatical distance between the two is one particle; the social distance is
enormous.

</details>
