# ～にとって

**Meaning** · for; from the standpoint of - the sentence is judged from inside the position of the person or group named

`にとって` hands the microphone to somebody else. It says: seen from where **they**
stand, here is how things look. What follows is almost always an **evaluation** - 
important, hard, welcome, painful, worthless - because that is the only kind of
statement a standpoint can change.

It cannot mean "about", and it cannot mark the target of an action. Those two facts
do most of the work of keeping it apart from its neighbours.

## Formation

Attaches to a bare noun naming somebody capable of having a stake in the matter: a
person, a group, a company, a country, sometimes a living thing.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (person or group) | Noun + にとって | {f|私|わたし} → {f|私|わたし}にとって{f|大切|たいせつ}だ |
| Noun, modifying a noun | Noun + にとっての + Noun | {f|学生|がくせい} → {f|学生|がくせい}にとっての{f|理想|りそう} |
| Noun, as topic | Noun + にとっては | {f|彼|かれ} → {f|彼|かれ}にとっては{f|簡単|かんたん}だ |
| Noun, as addition | Noun + にとっても | {f|会社|かいしゃ} → {f|会社|かいしゃ}にとっても{f|損|そん}だ |
| Noun, business keigo | Noun + にとりまして | {f|弊社|へいしゃ} → {f|弊社|へいしゃ}にとりましては |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - after `にとって` you need an **evaluation**, not an action.
<code>{f|大切|たいせつ}だ・{f|必要|ひつよう}だ・{f|難|むずか}しい・ありがたい・{f|問題|もんだい}だ・{f|忘|わす}れられない</code>
all work. A plain report of what somebody did does not:
❌<code>{f|私|わたし}にとって{f|毎日|まいにち}{f|走|はし}っています</code>.

</div>

</pre>

<pre>

This book is too hard for a child.
</pre>

<pre>

It's a bit pricey as far as I'm concerned, but I think it's worth buying.
</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にとって` is close to register-neutral. It is at home in an essay, a news article,
a job interview and a conversation with a friend. Nothing about it is stiff, and
unlike <code>に{f|関|かん}して</code> or `において` it has not been pushed out of speech
by a plainer alternative.

What it does carry is a mild rhetorical weight. Because it explicitly frames a
judgement as belonging to somebody, it invites the reader to notice that other
people might see it differently. <code>{f|私|わたし}にとっては{f|高|たか}い</code> is
politer than <code>{f|高|たか}い</code> for exactly that reason: it presents the
verdict as personal rather than absolute. Sales staff and diplomats lean on this.

In casual speech, `には` covers most of the same ground with fewer syllables - 
<code>{f|僕|ぼく}にはちょっと{f|無理|むり}かな</code> - and younger speakers also reach
for <code>{f|個人的|こじんてき}には</code>, "personally speaking". `にとって` is not
unnatural in conversation, but a whole conversation full of it would sound like an
essay read aloud.

<code>にとりまして</code> is the business-keigo form, common in apologies and in
speeches: <code>{f|弊社|へいしゃ}にとりまして{f|大変|たいへん}ありがたいお{f|話|はなし}です</code>.
Recognise it; you will rarely need to produce it.

One restriction worth stating plainly: the noun has to be something that can
**have interests**. People, groups, companies, countries, and living things all
qualify - <code>{f|植物|しょくぶつ}にとって{f|水|みず}は{f|欠|か}かせない</code> is
perfectly normal. A pure abstraction or an inanimate object usually does not:
❌<code>この{f|机|つくえ}にとって</code> is odd, because a desk has no point of view to
speak from.

## Contrast

### ～にとって vs ～には

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - they overlap heavily, and `には` is the shorter, more spoken
option. `にとって` is unambiguous; `には` is a stack of two particles and can also
mean "to" or "in", so context has to sort it out.

</div>

<pre>
{f|私|わたし}<b>には</b>この{f|説明|せつめい}がよく{f|分|わ}からなかった。
I didn't really follow this explanation.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}<b>にとって</b>、この{f|説明|せつめい}は{f|不親切|ふしんせつ}だ。
For a beginner, this explanation is unhelpful.
</pre>

Both frame a judgement from somebody's position. The difference shows up when the
sentence could be read another way. <code>{f|山田|やまだ}さんには{f|話|はな}した</code>
means "I told Yamada" - `に` marking the recipient, `は` marking contrast - and has
nothing to do with standpoints. <code>{f|山田|やまだ}さんにとって</code> can only ever
mean "from Yamada's point of view". In writing, where you cannot lean on
intonation, that certainty is worth the extra syllables.

### ～にとって vs ～に{f|対|たい}して

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にとって` is the **viewer**; <code>に{f|対|たい}して</code> is the
**target**. Nothing is being aimed at the noun in a `にとって` sentence.

</div>

<pre>
{f|新人|しんじん}<b>にとって</b>、{f|最初|さいしょ}の{f|一年|いちねん}は{f|覚|おぼ}えることばかりだ。
For a new employee, the first year is nothing but things to learn.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|新人|しんじん}<b>に{f|対|たい}して</b>、もう{f|少|すこ}し{f|丁寧|ていねい}に{f|説明|せつめい}したほうがいい。
You'd do better to explain things to a new employee a bit more carefully.
</pre>

The first sentence describes the world as the newcomer experiences it. The second
describes what somebody else should do **to** them. A quick test: if you can add
"…and they may be the only one who sees it that way", it is `にとって`. If you can
add "…and they are on the receiving end", it is <code>に{f|対|たい}して</code>.

### ～にとって vs ～として

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `にとって` = "**for** X, from X's side of the fence".
`として` = "**as** X, in the role of X". `にとって` looks out from the noun;
`として` puts the subject *into* the noun.

</div>

Both come out as English "as" or "for" often enough to blur, and they can even sit
in the same slot with different meanings.

<pre>
{f|親|おや}<b>にとって</b>、{f|子|こ}どもの{f|進路|しんろ}は{f|口|くち}を{f|出|だ}しにくい{f|問題|もんだい}だ。
For a parent, a child's career choice is a hard thing to weigh in on.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|親|おや}<b>として</b>、{f|一度|いちど}は{f|本音|ほんね}を{f|伝|つた}えておきたい。
As a parent, I'd like to say what I really think at least once.
</pre>

In the first, parents are the group whose viewpoint we are borrowing; the speaker
need not be one. In the second, the speaker **is** a parent and is acting in that
capacity. Notice also what follows each: `にとって` is followed by an evaluation,
`として` by an action.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|私|わたし}にとって{f|毎朝|まいあさ}コーヒーを{f|飲|の}みます。 → ✅ {f|私|わたし}<b>は</b>{f|毎朝|まいあさ}コーヒーを{f|飲|の}みます。
  `にとって` frames a judgement, so it needs an evaluative predicate. A plain report
  of what you do takes an ordinary topic `は`.

- ❌ この{f|計画|けいかく}にとって{f|意見|いけん}を{f|聞|き}かせてください。 → ✅ この{f|計画|けいかく}<b>について</b>{f|意見|いけん}を{f|聞|き}かせてください。
  `にとって` cannot mean "about". A plan is not a viewpoint-holder; it is a topic.

- ❌ {f|先生|せんせい}にとって{f|失礼|しつれい}なことを{f|言|い}ってしまった。 → ✅ {f|先生|せんせい}に<b>{f|対|たい}して</b>{f|失礼|しつれい}なことを{f|言|い}ってしまった。
  Rudeness is aimed **at** someone. That is <code>に{f|対|たい}して</code>;
  `にとって` would put the teacher in the judge's seat instead of the victim's.

- ❌ {f|彼|かれ}にとって{f|感謝|かんしゃ}しています。 → ✅ {f|彼|かれ}<b>に</b>{f|感謝|かんしゃ}しています。
  Gratitude has a recipient, not a standpoint. Plain `に` is what
  <code>{f|感謝|かんしゃ}する</code> takes.

## Practice

**1.** {f|受験生|じゅけんせい}____、{f|冬|ふゆ}は{f|一年|いちねん}で{f|一番|いちばん}つらい{f|季節|きせつ}だろう。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|受験生|じゅけんせい}<b>にとって</b>、{f|冬|ふゆ}は{f|一年|いちねん}で{f|一番|いちばん}つらい{f|季節|きせつ}だろう。

The sentence rates winter **from the exam candidates' position** - for everyone
else winter is just winter. <code>つらい</code> is an evaluation, which is exactly
what `にとって` requires.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|外国|がいこく}のお{f|客様|きゃくさま}（にとって・に{f|対|たい}して）は、{f|英語|えいご}のメニューを{f|用意|ようい}しています。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|外国|がいこく}のお{f|客様|きゃくさま}<b>に{f|対|たい}して</b>は、{f|英語|えいご}のメニューを{f|用意|ようい}しています。

The restaurant is doing something **for** - that is, aimed at - those customers, so
they are a target, not a viewpoint. `にとって` would need a judgement to follow, such
as <code>{f|外国|がいこく}のお{f|客様|きゃくさま}にとって{f|注文|ちゅうもん}は{f|難|むずか}しい</code>.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "For me, that failure ended up being a good experience."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|私|わたし}<b>にとって</b>、あの{f|失敗|しっぱい}はいい{f|経験|けいけん}になりました。

The whole point of the sentence is that the verdict is personal - someone else
might well call the same failure a disaster. That is precisely the work `にとって`
does. <code>{f|私|わたし}には</code> would also be natural in speech.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|会社|かいしゃ}にとって、{f|社員|しゃいん}にとって{f|新|あたら}しい{f|制度|せいど}を{f|説明|せつめい}する{f|義務|ぎむ}がある。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|会社|かいしゃ}には、{f|社員|しゃいん}<b>に{f|対|たい}して</b>{f|新|あたら}しい{f|制度|せいど}を{f|説明|せつめい}する{f|義務|ぎむ}がある。

The second `にとって` is wrong: the employees are who the explanation is delivered
**to**, so they need <code>に{f|対|たい}して</code>. The first is better replaced by
plain <code>には</code>, since <code>{f|義務|ぎむ}がある</code> states a fact about the
company rather than judging something from its viewpoint.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|田中|たなか}さん<b>にとって</b>この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|簡単|かんたん}だ。
(b) この{f|仕事|しごと}は{f|簡単|かんたん}だ。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(b) states the job's difficulty as a plain fact about the job - anyone would find
it easy.

(a) attaches the verdict to one person. It says the job is easy **as Tanaka
experiences it**, and it quietly implies that this is not everybody's experience:
someone else might struggle. That built-in "…though not necessarily for others" is
the whole reason the pattern exists, and it is why `にとって` is useful for
softening an opinion you do not want to present as objective truth.

</details>
