# ～にしても

**Meaning** · even if; even granting that - the speaker accepts the other side's point and objects anyway

`にしても` picks up a reason, an excuse or a possibility that is already on the table,
grants it, and then says it is not enough. Almost every `にしても` sentence has an
argument going on inside it: *fine, suppose that is true - even so, this still does
not follow.*

On this batch's axis of how much the speaker minds, `にしても` is the reasonable
complainer. `のに` blurts out its dissatisfaction; `くせに` sneers; `にしても` concedes
first and criticises second, which is why it is the concession you hear in meetings.

## Formation

`にしても` follows a **plain form** - a whole clause, with its own tense - and nouns
and な-adjectives attach bare, with no `だ` and no `な`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + にしても | {f|断|ことわ}る → {f|断|ことわ}るにしても |
| Verb (past) | plain past + にしても | {f|忘|わす}れた → {f|忘|わす}れたにしても |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + にしても | {f|行|い}かない → {f|行|い}かないにしても |
| い-adjective | plain form + にしても | {f|忙|いそが}しい → {f|忙|いそが}しいにしても |
| な-adjective | stem (+ である) + にしても | {f|不便|ふべん} → {f|不便|ふべん}（である）にしても |
| Noun | Noun (+ である) + にしても | {f|冗談|じょうだん} → {f|冗談|じょうだん}にしても |
| Two alternatives | A にしても B にしても | {f|買|か}う・{f|借|か}りる → {f|買|か}うにしても{f|借|か}りるにしても |
| Fixed conjunction | それにしても + remark | - → それにしても{f|暑|あつ}いね |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - no `だ` and no `な` before `にしても`:
✅<code>{f|学生|がくせい}にしても</code>, ❌<code>{f|学生|がくせい}だにしても</code>. If you
want the copula visible, the formal option is `である`:
<code>{f|学生|がくせい}であるにしても</code>.

</div>

<div class="note">

**Note** - with a な-adjective the bare stem can be ambiguous, because
<code>{f|静|しず}かにしても</code> also reads as "even if you make it quiet"
(<code>{f|静|しず}かにする</code> + `ても`). In speech people dodge this by saying
<code>{f|静|しず}かだとしても</code> instead. In writing, `である` does the same job.

</div>

<pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`にしても` is the vocabulary of **argument conducted politely**. It concedes in order
to earn the right to object, which makes it a staple of meetings, negotiations, letters
of complaint and mild parental scolding. Because the concession comes first, the
objection that follows lands as reasonable rather than hostile - this is a genuinely
useful piece of adult Japanese.

The main clause is where the speaker's feeling lives, and it is usually one of:

- a criticism - <code>～すぎる</code>, <code>ひどい</code>, <code>おかしい</code>;
- a minimum demand - <code>ぐらいは～てほしい</code>, <code>～べきだ</code>;
- a limit on a plan - <code>それでも～は{f|変|か}えない</code>.

A flat, neutral report after `にしても` sounds unfinished, because the listener is
waiting for the objection the concession promised.

Its family, roughly by register:

- `としても` - the most clearly hypothetical: "even supposing." Neutral.
- `にしても` - concedes something already raised, real or supposed. Neutral.
- `にしろ` - the same, a shade curt; common in spoken instructions.
- `にせよ` - formal and written; documents and speeches.

`それにしても` is worth learning as a single word. It re-opens a topic to comment on
its degree: 「{f|遅|おそ}いね。」「うん、それにしても{f|遅|おそ}すぎるよ。」 It is not
conceding anything specific - it just says "granting everything we have said, still."

## Contrast

### ～にしても vs ～ても

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ても` attaches to a **te-form** and states a bare concession.
`にしても` attaches to a **whole plain clause**, so it can carry past tense and nouns,
and it concedes a *claim someone has actually made*.

</div>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}っ<b>ても</b>、{f|畑|はたけ}の{f|水|みず}やりは{f|必要|ひつよう}です。
Even if it rains, the field still needs watering.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}った<b>にしても</b>、これだけしか{f|降|ふ}らなければ{f|同|おな}じことです。
Granted it rained - but this little makes no difference.
</pre>

Notice what the second one does that the first cannot: it keeps the past tense
(<code>{f|降|ふ}った</code>) and it answers somebody. `ても` cannot take tense at all,
and it does not imply a conversation.

### ～にしても vs ～にしては

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - one syllable, two different sentences. `にしても` = "even granting
X, Y is still not acceptable." `にしては` = "measured against X, Y is surprising."
`にしても` complains; `にしては` evaluates.

</div>

<pre>
{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}<b>にしては</b>、ずいぶん{f|上手|じょうず}に{f|削|けず}れていますね。
For a beginner, that's remarkably well planed.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|初心者|しょしんしゃ}<b>にしても</b>、{f|安全|あんぜん}な{f|持|も}ち{f|方|かた}は{f|習|なら}っておくべきだ。
Beginner or not, he ought to have learned how to hold it safely.
</pre>

The first is a compliment: the standard "beginner" predicts little, and the result
beat it. The second is a reproach: being a beginner is offered as an excuse, accepted,
and ruled insufficient.

### ～にしても vs ～のに

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `のに` reports a **fact** that disappointed the speaker.
`にしても` concedes a **premise** - which may be a fact or merely a supposition - 
and then argues from it.

</div>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}があった<b>のに</b>、{f|最後|さいご}まで{f|司会|しかい}を{f|続|つづ}けた。
She had a temperature and still chaired the whole thing.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|熱|ねつ}があった<b>にしても</b>、{f|一言|ひとこと}{f|言|い}ってくれればよかったのに。
Even with a temperature, she could have said something.
</pre>

The first admires a fact. The second takes the same fact as an excuse and pushes
back on it. If you find yourself simply reporting something surprising with no
argument attached, `のに` is the word.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|学生|がくせい}だにしても → ✅ {f|学生|がくせい}にしても
  Nouns and な-adjectives attach bare. `だ` never appears before `にしても`; the formal
  alternative is `である`.

- ❌ {f|忙|いそが}しいにしても、{f|妹|いもうと}は{f|手伝|てつだ}いに{f|来|き}てくれた → ✅ {f|忙|いそが}しい<b>のに</b>、{f|妹|いもうと}は{f|手伝|てつだ}いに{f|来|き}てくれた
  `にしても` sets up an objection. When you are grateful rather than dissatisfied,
  the fact-reporting `のに` is what you want.

- ❌ この{f|字|じ}は{f|子|こ}どもが{f|書|か}いたにしても、きれいだ → ✅ この{f|字|じ}は{f|子|こ}どもが{f|書|か}いた<b>にしては</b>、きれいだ
  You are measuring the handwriting against the standard "written by a child", so
  the particle is `は`, not `も`.

- ❌ {f|行|い}くにしても、{f|行|い}かないにも、{f|返事|へんじ}をください → ✅ {f|行|い}くにしても、{f|行|い}かないにしても、{f|返事|へんじ}をください
  In the paired "whether A or B" frame, both halves take the full `にしても`.

## Practice

**1.** {f|冗談|じょうだん}____、{f|人|ひと}の{f|見|み}た{f|目|め}のことは{f|言|い}わないほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|冗談|じょうだん}<b>にしても</b>、{f|人|ひと}の{f|見|み}た{f|目|め}のことは{f|言|い}わないほうがいい。

A noun attaches bare, with no `だ`. The sentence grants the excuse "it was only a
joke" and rules it insufficient, which is the core job of `にしても`.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|中学生|ちゅうがくせい}（にしても・にしては）、{f|作文|さくぶん}がずいぶん{f|大人|おとな}っぽい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|中学生|ちゅうがくせい}<b>にしては</b>、{f|作文|さくぶん}がずいぶん{f|大人|おとな}っぽい。

The sentence is an assessment: judged against what you expect from a junior-high
student, the essay is surprisingly grown-up. `にしても` would turn it into a
complaint - "even for a junior-high student, this is not good enough."

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if we do postpone it, we should tell the guests today."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|延期|えんき}する<b>にしても</b>、お{f|客|きゃく}さんには{f|今日|きょう}{f|知|し}らせるべきです。

`にしても` takes the plain form <code>{f|延期|えんき}する</code> directly. The pattern
fits because the sentence grants a possibility that is under discussion and then
states a requirement that holds regardless.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|失敗|しっぱい}しただにしても、{f|報告|ほうこく}は{f|早|はや}いほうがいい。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|失敗|しっぱい}した<b>にしても</b>、{f|報告|ほうこく}は{f|早|はや}いほうがいい。

<code>{f|失敗|しっぱい}した</code> is already a complete plain past form, so nothing
goes between it and `にしても`. The stray `だ` comes from treating
<code>{f|失敗|しっぱい}</code> as a bare noun; here it is a verb.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|新人|しんじん}にしても、この{f|資料|しりょう}は{f|雑|ざつ}すぎる。
(b) {f|新人|しんじん}にしては、この{f|資料|しりょう}はよくできている。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) grants that the person is new - the excuse is heard and accepted - and then
rejects it: newness does not stretch far enough to cover work this sloppy. The
speaker is dissatisfied and is saying so in a controlled way.

(b) uses newness as a yardstick instead of an excuse. Expectations for a new hire
were low, the work cleared them, and the speaker is impressed. Same noun, opposite
attitude, decided entirely by `も` versus `は`.

</details>
