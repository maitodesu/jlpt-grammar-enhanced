# ～ついでに

**Meaning** · while you're doing X anyway, do Y as well - Y gets a free ride

`ついでに` is the grammar of efficiency. X is the real reason you are going, doing
or moving; Y is a smaller job you slip in because the opportunity is already
there. If Y is the reason and X is the excuse, you have the two the wrong way
round.

## Formation

`ついで` is a noun - "an occasion that happens to arise" - so a clause modifies it
in the plain form, and a noun attaches with `の`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary) - not yet done | dictionary + ついでに | {f|出|で}かける → {f|出|で}かけるついでに |
| Verb (た-form) - already under way | た-form + ついでに | {f|行|い}く → {f|行|い}ったついでに |
| Action noun | Noun + の + ついでに | {f|散歩|さんぽ} → {f|散歩|さんぽ}のついでに |
| On its own, at the head of a sentence | ついでに、… | ついでに、これもお{f|願|ねが}い |
| As a plain noun | ついでが+ある／ついでで | ついでがあったら{f|寄|よ}ります |
| ❌ の after a verb | ❌{f|行|い}くのついでに | ✅{f|行|い}くついでに |
| ❌ Y bigger than X | ❌{f|切手|きって}を{f|買|か}うついでに{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}した | ✅{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}すついでに{f|切手|きって}を{f|買|か}った |

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - `ついでに` needs a **main errand** and a **minor extra**, in
that order, and they must share one outing, one trip or one stretch of activity.
The dictionary form means the errand is still ahead of you; the た-form means you
are already out.

</div>

<pre>

While I was out shopping I stopped by the post office too.
</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

<pre>
{f|散歩|さんぽ}の<b>ついでに</b>、{f|公園|こうえん}のごみを{f|拾|ひろ}うようにしている。

</pre>

<pre>

</pre>

## Nuance & register

`ついでに` is register-neutral and enormously common in everyday speech, but it
carries a social charge that learners should feel. Attached to a **request**, it
shrinks the favour: コンビニに{f|行|い}くついでに means "this costs you nothing
extra", and that is why it makes an imposition easy to accept. Attached to your
**own** offer, it does the same work in reverse - 
「ついでですから、{f|気|き}にしないでください」 is the standard way to wave off
thanks for a lift or a delivery.

The flip side is that calling something a `ついで` **demotes** it. Say
{f|結婚式|けっこんしき}のついでに and you have announced that the wedding was a
convenient excuse. Around anything the other person takes seriously - a funeral,
a hospital visit, a formal invitation - reach for a neutral phrase such as
<code>{f|帰|かえ}りに</code> or <code>{f|行|い}った{f|時|とき}に</code> instead. Japanese
speakers also avoid `ついでに` when talking about something they did *for* a
superior, since it implies the superior's business was the side job.

The choice between dictionary and た-form is aspect, not politeness. The
dictionary form plans: {f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に{f|行|い}くついでに{f|寄|よ}ろう. The
た-form reports from inside the trip:
{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}に{f|行|い}ったついでに{f|寄|よ}った. Both are common, and native
speakers slide between them without ceremony.

## Contrast

### ～ついでに vs ～ながら

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>ながら</code> means the two actions happen **at the same
time**, both by the same person. `ついでに` means one **occasion** covers two
separate jobs; they can be minutes apart.

</div>

<pre>
{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}きながら{f|皿|さら}を{f|洗|あら}っています。
I'm doing the washing-up while listening to music.
</pre>

You genuinely do both at once, so this cannot be `ついでに`. Now something you
cannot do simultaneously:

<pre>
ゴミを{f|出|だ}しに{f|行|い}く<b>ついでに</b>、ポストに{f|手紙|てがみ}を{f|入|い}れてきた。
I put the letter in the postbox while I was out taking the rubbish down.
</pre>

Note also which clause is the main one. With <code>ながら</code> the main action is the
**second** clause; with `ついでに` the main errand is the **first**. Swap them and
you change what the sentence is about.

### ～ついでに vs ～{f|途中|とちゅう}で

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|途中|とちゅう}で</code> is about **position on a route**:
somewhere between start and finish. `ついでに` is about **taking advantage of an
occasion**, and the extra job does not have to lie on the way.

</div>

<pre>
{f|帰|かえ}る{f|途中|とちゅう}で{f|本屋|ほんや}に{f|寄|よ}りました。
I dropped into a bookshop on the way home.
</pre>

<pre>
{f|実家|じっか}に{f|帰|かえ}る<b>ついでに</b>、{f|祖母|そぼ}の{f|家|いえ}にも{f|顔|かお}を{f|出|だ}した。
Since I was going back to my parents' anyway, I looked in on my grandmother too.
</pre>

The grandmother's house may be a detour of an hour; `ついでに` does not care, as
long as the one trip made it worth doing. <code>{f|途中|とちゅう}で</code> would be false
if she lived in the opposite direction. Conversely,
{f|帰|かえ}る{f|途中|とちゅう}で{f|雨|あめ}が{f|降|ふ}ってきた is fine and `ついでに` is
impossible there, because rain is not an errand you slip in.

### ～ついでに vs ～{f|際|さい}に

<div class="rule">

**Rule of thumb** - <code>{f|際|さい}に</code> is a formal "on the occasion of" and says
nothing about efficiency. `ついでに` always implies the second job was tacked on
to save a trip - which is why it is out of place in a notice or a polite request
to a customer.

</div>

<pre>
ご{f|来店|らいてん}の<b>{f|際|さい}に</b>、こちらのカードをお{f|見|み}せください。
Please show this card when you visit the shop.
</pre>

Replacing that with ご{f|来店|らいてん}のついでに would tell customers their visit
is a side errand - polite in form, insulting in content. Among friends the same
information is perfectly friendly:

<pre>
{f|店|みせ}に{f|行|い}く<b>ついでに</b>、このカード{f|見|み}せてきてよ。
Show them this card while you're at the shop.
</pre>

So the split is two-way: register (formal versus everyday) and meaning (occasion
versus opportunity taken).

## Common mistakes

- ❌ {f|散歩|さんぽ}するのついでに、パンを{f|買|か}った。 → ✅ {f|散歩|さんぽ}するついでに、パンを{f|買|か}った。
  A verb modifies `ついで` directly, with no `の`. The `の` appears only after a
  noun: {f|散歩|さんぽ}のついでに.

- ❌ {f|切手|きって}を{f|買|か}うついでに、{f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}しの{f|手続|てつづ}きをした。 → ✅ {f|引|ひ}っ{f|越|こ}しの{f|手続|てつづ}きのついでに、{f|切手|きって}を{f|買|か}った。
  The bigger errand has to come first. As written, the sentence claims a house
  move was a minor extra attached to buying stamps.

- ❌ お{f|見舞|みま}いのついでに、{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}をしてきました。 → ✅ お{f|見舞|みま}いの{f|帰|かえ}りに、{f|買|か}い{f|物|もの}をしてきました。
  Grammatically fine, socially cold: it says visiting the patient was the
  convenient excuse. Use <code>{f|帰|かえ}りに</code> when the first event matters to
  someone.

- ❌ {f|勉強|べんきょう}するついでに{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}いています。 → ✅ {f|勉強|べんきょう}しながら{f|音楽|おんがく}を{f|聞|き}いています。
  Two activities that overlap continuously need <code>ながら</code>. `ついでに` wants
  two distinct jobs sharing one occasion.

## Practice

**1.** {f|銀行|ぎんこう}に{f|行|い}く____、この{f|手紙|てがみ}も{f|出|だ}してきてくれない?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|銀行|ぎんこう}に{f|行|い}く<b>ついでに</b>、この{f|手紙|てがみ}も{f|出|だ}してきてくれない?

The bank trip is already planned and posting a letter is the small extra, so
`ついでに` frames the request as almost no trouble at all. The verb takes the
dictionary form because the errand has not started yet.

</details>

**2.** Choose: {f|新聞|しんぶん}を{f|読|よ}み（ながら・ついでに）コーヒーを{f|飲|の}んでいた。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|新聞|しんぶん}を{f|読|よ}み<b>ながら</b>コーヒーを{f|飲|の}んでいた。

Reading and drinking happen at the same moment, which is what <code>ながら</code> is
for. `ついでに` would need two separable jobs and would also require the plain
form, not the stem.

</details>

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Since I was at the library anyway, I returned the book my sister had borrowed."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

{f|図書館|としょかん}に{f|行|い}った<b>ついでに</b>、{f|妹|いもうと}が{f|借|か}りていた{f|本|ほん}も{f|返|かえ}してきた。

"Since I was there anyway" is the English signature of `ついでに`. The た-form fits
because the speaker is reporting from a trip that already happened.

</details>

**4.** Find and fix the error: {f|結婚式|けっこんしき}のついでに、{f|京都|きょうと}を{f|案内|あんない}してもらいました。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

✅ {f|結婚式|けっこんしき}で{f|京都|きょうと}に{f|行|い}ったついでに、{f|町|まち}を{f|案内|あんない}してもらいました。

The original makes the wedding itself the throwaway errand, which is rude to
whoever was getting married. Anchoring `ついでに` to the *trip* rather than the
ceremony keeps the efficiency meaning without demoting the event.

</details>

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance:
(a) {f|東京|とうきょう}に{f|行|い}く<b>ついでに</b>、{f|叔父|おじ}に{f|会|あ}います。
(b) {f|東京|とうきょう}に{f|行|い}った{f|時|とき}に、{f|叔父|おじ}に{f|会|あ}います。

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(a) makes clear that something else is taking the speaker to Tokyo and the uncle
is being fitted in around it. Said to the uncle himself, it would be a little
tactless.

(b) simply locates the meeting in time - when I'm in Tokyo, I'll see him - and
says nothing about which was the reason for the trip. It is the safer sentence in
almost any company.

</details>
