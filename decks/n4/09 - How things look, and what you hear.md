# ～ようだ

**Meaning** · it seems that; it appears that - a conclusion the speaker has reached themselves

`～ようだ` reports an **inference**. The speaker has some evidence - something seen, heard, felt, or worked out - and offers a careful conclusion drawn from it. The judgement belongs to the speaker, which is exactly what separates it from `らしい`, where the judgement belongs to somebody else.

---

## ～ようだ · Formation

`よう` is a noun meaning "appearance, manner", so everything attaches to it the way things attach to any noun you learned to modify at N5: verbs and い-adjectives in plain form, な-adjectives with `な`, nouns with `の`.

---

## ～ようだ · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ようだ | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るようだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ようだ | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>った → <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったようだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ようだ | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないようだ |
| Verb (ている) | ている + ようだ | <ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ている → <ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ているようだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + ようだ | <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい → <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいようだ |
| な-adjective | ～な + ようだ | <ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>なようだ |
| Noun | Noun + の + ようだ | <ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>のようだ |
| Polite | ようだ → ようです | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るようです |
| Past | ようだ → ようだった／ようでした | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったようでした |

---

## ～ようだ · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Before a verb | ようだ → ように | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える |
| Before a noun | ようだ → ような | <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>のような<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |

> **Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` inflects exactly like a な-adjective: よう**だ**, よう**な**, よう**に**, よう**で**, よう**でした**. And nothing in front of it ever keeps its `だ`: ❌<code><ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>だようだ</code>.

---

## ～ようだ · Examples

<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い**ようです**。<br>*It seems to be cold outside.*

だれか**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たようだ**。<br>*Looks like somebody's arrived.*

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中村<rt>なかむら</rt></ruby>さん**のようです**。<br>*This dictionary appears to be Nakamura's.*

<ruby>六月<rt>ろくがつ</rt></ruby>なのに、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>**のようだ**。<br>*It's only June, but today feels like summer.*

---

## ～ようだ · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>がある**ようなので**、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります。<br>*I seem to have a temperature, so I'm going home early today.*

<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>がぬれているから、<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>のうちに<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったようだ**。<br>*The road is wet, so it must have rained during the night.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>くと、この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>く<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>んでいる**ようですね**。<br>*From the way he talks, it sounds like he's lived in this town a long time.*

<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby><ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby><ruby>中<rt>ちゅう</rt></ruby>の**ようですので**、あとでもう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>おかけします。<br>*The section chief seems to be in a meeting just now, so I'll call again later.*

---

## ～ようだ · Nuance & register

`ようです` is the neutral polite form and is safe everywhere - with strangers, with customers, in a report. Plain `ようだ` is the written form and the one you meet in newspapers and essays; spoken out loud between friends it sounds a little buttoned-up, and casual speech reaches for `みたい` instead. Nothing about `ようだ` is male- or female-marked.

The adverb `どうやら` is its natural partner and makes the hedge audible: どうやら<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>間違<rt>まちが</rt></ruby>えたようだ。 A speaker who wants to sound less sure adds `かもしれない`; one who wants to sound sure drops the hedge entirely.

---

## ～ようだ · Nuance & register (cont.)

`ようだ` has a second job that has nothing to do with guessing: it is also the **politeness** device you use to say an awkward thing without saying it flatly. <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>にはできないようです is softer than <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>にはできません, because it presents your refusal as an observation rather than a decision. In business Japanese そのようです ("that does appear to be the case") is the standard way to confirm something you were not personally present for.

Finally, `ようだ` is fine about **your own body**, because the evidence there is still indirect: <ruby>風邪<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>をひいたようだ - you feel bad, you have not seen a doctor. It is not fine about facts you simply know.

---

## ～ようだ · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing, before reasoning starts. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis altogether - it is a plain relay of somebody's words.

---

## ～ようだ · ～ようだ vs ～らしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` is *my* conclusion from *my* evidence. `らしい` is somebody else's information, which I am passing on and not vouching for.

<ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>をやめた**ようだ**。<br>*It looks like Yamamoto has quit - his desk is cleared and nobody's seen him.*

<ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>をやめた**らしい**。<br>*Apparently Yamamoto has quit - that's what people are saying.*

Because `ようだ` commits you to the reasoning, it is the wrong choice when you name an outside source out loud. A sentence that opens with `ニュースによると` wants `そうだ` or `らしい` to finish it.

---

## ～ようだ · ～ようだ vs ～みたい

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register, different attachment. `ようだ` is written and formal; `みたい` is spoken and casual. Nouns take `の` before `ようだ` and **nothing** before `みたい`.

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**のようだ**。<br>*He's behaving like a child today.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**みたいだ**ね。<br>*He's acting like such a kid today, isn't he.*

The second is what you actually say to a friend. Putting `みたい` into a written report, or `ようだ` into a chat about a coworker, is not an error of grammar - it is an error of tone, and it is heard as one.

---

## ～ようだ · ～ようだ vs the two ～そうだ

The word `そうだ` covers **two unrelated grammars**, and they attach differently. Line all three up on one adjective and the difference is impossible to miss.

> **Rule of thumb** - plain form + `そうだ` = "I hear that". Adjective **stem** + `そうだ` = "looks". Plain form + `ようだ` = "seems, judging by the evidence".

このケーキは<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>い**そうだ**。<br>*I hear this cake is sweet. (someone told me; I haven't tried it)*

このケーキは**<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>そうだ**。<br>*This cake looks sweet. (I'm looking straight at it)*

---

## ～ようだ · ～ようだ vs the two ～そうだ (cont.)

このケーキは<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>い**ようだ**。<br>*This cake seems to be sweet. (everyone is leaving half of it, so I conclude…)*

Note the shape: <code><ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>い</code> keeps its `い` before hearsay `そうだ` and before `ようだ`, and **loses** it before appearance `そうだ`. That single `い` is the whole difference between reporting a rumour and describing a photograph.

---

## ～ようだ · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>病気<rt>びょうき</rt></ruby>**だ**ようです → ✅ <ruby>病気<rt>びょうき</rt></ruby>**の**ようです<br>*A noun connects to `よう` with `の`, never with `だ`. The same rule you learned for noun-modifying clauses at N5 applies here.*

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**ようだ** → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**なようだ**<br>*A な-adjective keeps its `な` in front of `よう`, because `よう` is a noun.*

- ❌ <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るよう**だです** → ✅ <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る**ようです**<br>*`ようだ` and `ようです` are one form at two politeness levels. You never stack them.*

---

## ～ようだ · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はきのう<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**ようです**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はきのう<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き**ました**。<br>*You were there. `ようだ` needs indirect evidence; using it about your own known actions sounds as though you have lost your memory.*

---

## ～ようだ · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>にだれもいない。<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み____ようだ。

{reveal}
<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み**の**ようだ。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み</code> is a noun, and nouns attach to `よう` with `の`. Only な-adjectives take `な`; verbs and い-adjectives attach in plain form with nothing added.

---

## ～ようだ · Practice 2

**2.** ニュースによると、<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>く（なるようだ・なるそうだ）。

{reveal}
<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>く**なるそうだ**。

{reveal}
The sentence names its source out loud, so it is a relay, not a deduction. Once you have said `ニュースによると`, the evidence is other people's, and hearsay `そうだ` (or `らしい`) is what finishes the sentence.

---

## ～ようだ · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "It seems the train has stopped."

{reveal}
<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まっているようです**。

{reveal}
Use `ている` for the resulting state - the train stopped and is still stopped - then attach `ようです` to that plain form. Nothing goes between the verb and `よう`.

---

## ～ようだ · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>ようですね。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**な**ようですね。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby></code> is a な-adjective, so it needs `な` to modify the noun `よう`. Dropping it is the commonest slip learners make with this pattern.

---

## ～ようだ · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>った**ようだ**。 (b) <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>った**らしい**。

{reveal}
In (a) the speaker worked it out: her coat is gone, the light is off, so the conclusion is theirs and they would defend it. In (b) the speaker heard it from somebody - a colleague mentioned it - and is repeating the information at a slight distance, without taking responsibility for whether it is true.

# ～ように・～ような

**Meaning** · as; like; in the way that - the adverbial and adnominal faces of ようだ

`ようだ` is a な-adjective, so it has the two forms every な-adjective has. `～ように` goes in front of a **verb or adjective** and says the action happens *in that manner*. `～ような` goes in front of a **noun** and says the thing is *of that kind*. This page is about attaching them to a clause; attaching them to a bare noun takes `の` first, and that is the sibling pattern `のように／のような`.

---

## ～ように・～ような · Formation

Attachment is identical to `ようだ` - only the ending changes according to what comes next.

---

## ～ように・～ような · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ように／ような | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったように |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ように／ような | <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない → <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らないような |
| Verb (ている) | ている + ように／ような | <ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>いている → <ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>いているように |
| い-adjective | plain form + ように／ような | <ruby>冷<rt>つめ</rt></ruby>たい → <ruby>冷<rt>つめ</rt></ruby>たいような |
| な-adjective | ～な + ように／ような | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きだ → <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きなように |
| Noun | Noun + の + ように／ような | <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>のような |
| Before a verb / adjective | ように | ように<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える |
| Before a noun | ような | ような<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |
| End of sentence | ようだ／ようです | <ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>いているようです |

---

## ～ように・～ような · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - look at the **next** word, not the previous one. Noun next → `ような`. Anything else next → `ように`. It is the same choice you already make between <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かな<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby></code> and <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>す</code>.

---

## ～ように・～ような · Examples

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いた**ように**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでください。<br>*Please read it the way I wrote it.*

<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っていた**ような**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>ではなかった。<br>*It wasn't the sort of shop I'd been picturing.*

<ruby>雲<rt>くも</rt></ruby>が<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>いている**ように**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える。<br>*The clouds look as though they're moving.*

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**ように**、この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>には<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>二<rt>ふた</rt></ruby>つある。<br>*As the teacher said, this kanji has two readings.*

---

## ～ように・～ような · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもでも<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かる**ような**<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>をしてください。<br>*Please give an explanation that even a child could follow.*

<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>た**ような**きれいな<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>だった。<br>*It was a beautiful stretch of sea, just like the one in the photo.*

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんがやっている**ように**やれば、きっとうまくいきますよ。<br>*If you do it the way Yamada does, I'm sure it'll go fine.*

<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った**ように**は<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>まなかったが、なんとか<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わらせた。<br>*It didn't go the way I'd expected, but I got it finished in time somehow.*

---

## ～ように・～ような · Nuance & register

`ように` and `ような` are register-neutral and belong equally to speech and writing. Their casual twins are `みたいに` and `みたいな`, which say the same thing in a looser voice; in a report or a presentation you want the `よう` forms.

Several fixed phrases are worth learning whole, because they open sentences in written and semi-formal Japanese:

- ご<ruby>存<rt>ぞん</rt></ruby>じのように - "as you know"
- <ruby>次<rt>つぎ</rt></ruby>のように - "as follows"
- <ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>述<rt>の</rt></ruby>べたように - "as stated above"

---

## ～ように・～ような · Nuance & register (cont.)

> **Note** - `ように` has a second, unrelated job: **purpose**. After a verb that is not under your direct control - a potential form, a negative, or an intransitive - `ように` means "so that": <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れないようにメモしておく, <ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えるように<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>る. That `ように` is not making a comparison at all, and it is the root of the separate patterns `ようにする`, `ようになる` and <code>ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う</code>. Tell them apart by asking whether the clause describes *how* something is done (comparison) or *why* (purpose).

The purpose sense also gives Japanese its wish formula, left hanging with no main clause: <ruby>合格<rt>ごうかく</rt></ruby>できますように。 - "May I pass." You see it written on <ruby>絵馬<rt>えま</rt></ruby> at shrines and hear it muttered before exams.

---

## ～ように・～ような · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis - it is a plain relay of somebody's words. `ように` and `ような` inherit their position from `ようだ`.

---

## ～ように・～ような · ～ように vs ～ような

> **Rule of thumb** - `ような` is an adjective and must be followed by a noun. `ように` is an adverb and must be followed by a verb or an adjective. Nothing else distinguishes them.

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>る**ような**カレーが<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたい。<br>*I want to eat curry like the kind my mother makes.*

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>る**ように**カレーを<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>ってみた。<br>*I tried making curry the way my mother makes it.*

The first compares two curries; the second compares two methods. Swap the endings and both sentences break - `ような` with a verb after it and `ように` with a noun after it are simply ungrammatical, not just odd.

---

## ～ように・～ような · ～ように vs ～みたいに

> **Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different level. `ように` is neutral to formal; `みたいに` is casual and slightly more emphatic. And the attachment to a noun differs: `Noun + のように` but `Noun + みたいに`.

<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてある**ように**<ruby>組<rt>く</rt></ruby>み<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>ててください。<br>*Please assemble it as described in the instructions.*

さっき<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**みたいに**やってみてよ。<br>*Try doing it like I said a minute ago.*

The first belongs in a manual; the second belongs in a kitchen. Say the first to a friend and you sound like a manual.

---

## ～ように・～ような · comparison ～ように vs purpose ～ように

> **Rule of thumb** - if you can replace it with "in the same way as", it is comparison. If you can replace it with "so that", it is purpose. Purpose `ように` almost always follows a potential verb, a negative, or an intransitive one.

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る**ように**<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ってみたが、<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby><ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>くならなかった。<br>*I tried running the way he runs, but I didn't get any faster at all.*

<ruby>後<rt>うし</rt></ruby>ろの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえる**ように**、<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>した。<br>*I spoke loudly so that the people at the back could hear too.*

Same three syllables, two different grammars. In the first, `ように` answers *how*; in the second, it answers *what for*.

---

## ～ように・～ような · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**ような**やってください → ✅ <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**ように**やってください<br>*`ような` may only be followed by a noun. In front of a verb it has to be `ように`.*

- ❌ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**ような**<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**のような**<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby><br>*A bare noun needs `の` before `よう`. Only clauses attach directly.*

- ❌ <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**ように**やっていいよ → ✅ <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**なように**やっていいよ<br>*な-adjectives keep their `な` in front of `よう`, because `よう` is a noun.*

- ❌ <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>の**ようだ**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>の**ような**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby><br>*`ようだ` ends a sentence. To put it in front of a noun you need the `な` form, the same way <code><ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>だ</code> becomes <code><ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>な</code>.*

{reveal}

{reveal}

# ～のように・～のような

**Meaning** · like; similar to; such as - comparing something to a **noun**

When the thing you are comparing to is a noun rather than a clause, `よう` needs `の` to hold on to it. `Noun + のように` goes in front of a verb or adjective; `Noun + のような` goes in front of another noun. The same pair has a second job that looks unrelated but is not: introducing an **example** - "fruit such as apples".

---

## ～のように・～のような · Formation

The `の` is not optional and it is not the possessive `の` you learned at N5 - it is the connector that lets a noun modify another noun.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, before a verb or adjective | Noun + のように | <ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby> → <ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>のように<ruby>白<rt>しろ</rt></ruby>い |
| Noun, before a noun | Noun + のような + Noun | <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>のような<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |
| Noun, at the end of a sentence | Noun + のようだ／のようです | <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>のようです |
| Giving an example | Noun や Noun + のような + Noun | りんごやみかんのような<ruby>果物<rt>くだもの</rt></ruby> |
| この・その・あの | drop the の | そのような／そのように |
| Casual equivalent | Noun + みたいな／みたいに | <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>みたいな<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |

---

## ～のように・～のような · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `の` before `よう`, nothing before `みたい`. And with `この・その・あの` the `の` is already inside the word, so you write `そのような`, never ❌`そののような`.

---

## ～のように・～のような · Examples

<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>**のように**<ruby>白<rt>しろ</rt></ruby>い。<br>*As white as snow.*

<ruby>人形<rt>にんぎょう</rt></ruby>**のような**<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>ですね。<br>*She has a face like a doll's, doesn't she.*

<ruby>鳥<rt>とり</rt></ruby>**のように**<ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飛<rt>と</rt></ruby>んでみたい。<br>*I'd love to fly through the sky like a bird.*

<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>**のように**ある。<br>*There's a mountain of work on my desk.*

---

## ～のように・～のような · Examples (cont.)

みかんやバナナ**のような**<ruby>果物<rt>くだもの</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*I like fruit such as mandarins and bananas.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>**のように**この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>通<rt>かよ</rt></ruby>っている。<br>*He turns up at this shop practically every day.*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>みが、うそ**のように**なくなった。<br>*Yesterday's pain vanished as if it had never been there.*

<ruby>初<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めて<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ったのに、<ruby>昔<rt>むかし</rt></ruby>からの<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**のような**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がした。<br>*Even though we'd only just met, I felt as if we'd been friends for years.*

---

## ～のように・～のような · Nuance & register

`のように` and `のような` are neutral: fine in an essay, fine in conversation, not marked for age or gender. In relaxed speech `みたいに` and `みたいな` are commoner, and they are what you will hear far more often between friends. Going the other direction, formal writing has its own set-piece openers: ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>のように - "as you can see", <ruby>以下<rt>いか</rt></ruby>のように - "as below".

Two families of fixed expressions are worth memorising rather than analysing:

---

## ～のように・～のような · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **Exaggerated quantity or manner** - <ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>のように (heaps of),<br>*<ruby>飛<rt>と</rt></ruby>ぶように<ruby>売<rt>う</rt></ruby>れる (selling like hot cakes), うそのように (unbelievably).*
- **"Practically every…"** - <ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>のように,<br>*<ruby>毎週<rt>まいしゅう</rt></ruby>のように. These do not mean literally every day; they mean often enough that it feels like it.*

> **Note** - `のように` compares; <code>と<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ</code>, which you met at N5, equates. <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>のように<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い says you are tall in the way your brother is tall; <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>と<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じくらい<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い says the two heights match. If a measurement is involved, you probably want <code><ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ</code>.

---

## ～のように・～のような · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words. `のように` and `のような` are the noun-comparing corner of the `ようだ` family.

---

## ～のように・～のような · ～のように／のような vs ～みたいに／みたいな

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning; `よう` needs `の` and `みたい` does not. `よう` is neutral to formal, `みたい` is casual.

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>**のような**<ruby>技術者<rt>ぎじゅつしゃ</rt></ruby>になりたいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*I hope to become an engineer like my father.*

おれもお<ruby>父<rt>とう</rt></ruby>さん**みたいな**エンジニアになりたいな。<br>*I wanna be an engineer like my dad too.*

Both sentences say the same thing about the same ambition. The first would go in a university application; the second is a fifteen-year-old talking. Notice that the `の` disappears the moment you switch to `みたい` - that is the mechanical half of the difference, and it is where learners slip.

---

## ～のように・～のような · Noun + のような vs Noun + らしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `Xのような` means *resembling X, and probably not X*. `Xらしい` means *a proper X, behaving as an X should*. They are close to opposites.

<ruby>男<rt>おとこ</rt></ruby>**のような**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>っていた。<br>*Someone who looked like a man was standing there. (I couldn't tell)*

<ruby>男<rt>おとこ</rt></ruby>**らしい**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>だと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われている。<br>*People say he's a very manly sort.*

The first says the resemblance is all you have; the second says the category fits so well it is worth remarking on. Use `のような` about someone you are unsure of and you are being cautious; use it about someone whose gender is obvious and you have said something strange.

---

## ～のような "resembling" vs ～のような "such as"

> **Rule of thumb** - if what precedes `のような` is a **member** of the noun that follows, it is "such as". If it is outside that category, it is a comparison.

バラ**のような**<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってきてください。<br>*Please buy some flowers - roses, say. (roses are flowers → "such as")*

<ruby>火<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>**のような**<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>が<ruby>咲<rt>さ</rt></ruby>いていた。<br>*Flowers like flames were in bloom. (fire is not a flower → comparison)*

Japanese does not mark the difference; the listener works it out from whether the category contains the example. English does mark it, which is why translating in either direction here needs a moment's thought.

---

## ～のように・～のような · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>**ように**<ruby>白<rt>しろ</rt></ruby>い → ✅ <ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>**のように**<ruby>白<rt>しろ</rt></ruby>い<br>*A bare noun cannot attach to `よう` directly. Only a clause can.*

- ❌ **そののような**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> → ✅ **そのような**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby><br>*`その` already ends in `の`. Adding another is the commonest slip in this whole pattern.*

- ❌ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもの**ように**<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもの**ような**<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby><br>*A noun follows, so the form must be `のような`. `のように` only goes in front of verbs and adjectives.*

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>男<rt>おとこ</rt></ruby>の**ような**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>だ。(meaning "he's manly") → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>男<rt>おとこ</rt></ruby>**らしい**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>だ。<br>*`のような` distances the noun from the category. To praise someone for fitting the category, you need `らしい`.*

# ～みたい

**Meaning** · seems like; looks like; is like - the everyday spoken form of ようだ

`～みたい` says exactly what `ようだ` says: the speaker has drawn a conclusion from evidence, or is comparing one thing to another. What changes is the register - this is the form you actually hear in conversation - and the attachment, which is simpler: `みたい` never wants the `の` or the `な` that `よう` insists on.

---

## ～みたい · Formation

Everything goes in front of `みたい` in its **bare** form. That is the whole rule.

---

## ～みたい · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + みたい | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るみたい |
| Verb (past) | た-form + みたい | <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たみたい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + みたい | <ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からない → <ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からないみたい |
| い-adjective | plain form + みたい | <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いみたい |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + みたい | <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>みたい |
| Noun | Noun + みたい (no の) | <ruby>風邪<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>風邪<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>みたい |
| Polite | みたい + です | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るみたいです |
| Past | みたいだった／みたいでした | <ruby>風邪<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>みたいでした |
| Before a noun | みたい + な | <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>みたいな<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |

---

## ～みたい · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Before a verb / adjective | みたい + に | <ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>みたいに<ruby>白<rt>しろ</rt></ruby>い |

> **Rule of thumb** - `みたい` behaves like a な-adjective *after* it attaches (みたい**だ**／みたい**な**／みたい**に**), but takes nothing *before* it. Compare <code><ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものようだ</code> with <code><ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもみたいだ</code>: the `の` belongs to `よう` only.

---

## ～みたい · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - this `みたい` is **always written in kana**. <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たい</code> with the kanji is a different word entirely - the ます-stem of <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る</code> plus `たい`, "want to see". <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たい<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby></code> is a film you want to watch; <code><ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>みたい</code> is "it's like a film". The two are commonly explained as related - `みたい` is usually traced back to a contraction of <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たよう</code> - but in modern Japanese they are separate words, and writing this one with kanji is simply an error.

---

## ～みたい · Examples

<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>、<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ってる**みたい**だよ。<br>*It looks like it's raining out there.*

あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>、<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>**みたい**だね。<br>*That person seems to be a teacher.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>くんは<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み**みたいです**。<br>*Tanaka seems to be off today.*

<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>のかぎをかけ<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れた**みたい**。<br>*I think I forgot to lock up the house.*

---

## ～みたい · Examples (cont.)

この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>になると<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**みたい**だ。<br>*This neighbourhood seems to go quiet once night falls.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>はゲームをしていて、こっちの<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえていない**みたい**だ。<br>*My brother's playing a game and doesn't seem to hear a word I'm saying.*

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しい**みたいです**ね。<br>*Hayashi seems to be enjoying the new job.*

<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby><ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>してもつながらないから、まだ<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わっていない**みたい**だ。<br>*I can't get through however many times I call, so the meeting apparently isn't over yet.*

---

## ～みたい · Nuance & register

`みたい` is **casual**. Between friends, family and close colleagues it is the default; `ようだ` in the same conversation sounds like reading from a memo. Adding `です` - みたいです - raises it to a polite-but-relaxed level that is perfectly normal at work in speech, but it never becomes *formal*. In a written report, a notice, a news article or a speech, switch to `ようです`.

Sentence-final `みたい` with **nothing after it** is extremely common in speech: 「<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わったみたい」「<ruby>留守<rt>るす</rt></ruby>みたい」. The `だ` is optional, and dropping it softens the sentence; keeping the `だ` makes it flatter and blunter, a tendency associated more with male speech, though it is not a rule and plenty of speakers of any gender use both.

---

## ～みたい · Nuance & register (cont.)

`みたい` also does emotional work that `ようだ` is too stiff for. 「うそみたい!」 is "I can't believe it!", not a claim about lying. 「<ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>みたい」 is what you say when something wonderful happens. These are frozen exclamations; learn them whole.

---

## ～みたい · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words.

---

## ～みたい · ～みたい vs ～ようだ

> **Rule of thumb** - identical meaning. `みたい` for speech, `ようだ` for writing and formal speech. Attachment: `Noun + みたい` but `Noun + のようだ`; `な-adj + みたい` but `な-adj + なようだ`.

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さん、もう<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>っちゃった**みたい**。<br>*Looks like Yamada's already gone home.*

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>既<rt>すで</rt></ruby>に<ruby>退社<rt>たいしゃ</rt></ruby>した**ようです**。<br>*It appears that Yamada has already left for the day.*

Same fact, two worlds. The second is what you would write in a message to a client; say it to a friend and you sound like an answering machine.

---

## ～みたい · ～みたい vs ～らしい

> **Rule of thumb** - both are common in casual speech, but they point in opposite directions. `みたい` says *I* worked it out. `らしい` says *somebody told me* - and quietly declines responsibility for it.

<ruby>加藤<rt>かとう</rt></ruby>さん、<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>す**みたい**だよ。<ruby>箱<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>がたくさんあった。<br>*Kato seems to be moving - there were boxes everywhere.*

<ruby>加藤<rt>かとう</rt></ruby>さん、<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>す**らしい**よ。<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってた。<br>*Kato's apparently moving - the section chief said so.*

The second half of each sentence is doing the diagnostic work. `みたい` invites "what makes you think so?"; `らしい` invites "who told you?".

---

## ～みたい · ～みたい vs appearance ～そう

> **Rule of thumb** - `そう` reports the **surface**: what the thing looks like right now, with no reasoning. `みたい` reports a **conclusion**, however small the leap. And they attach differently: `おいしいみたい` keeps the `い`; `おいしそう` drops it.

このスープ、**おいしそう**。<br>*This soup looks delicious. (I'm looking at it; I haven't tasted it)*

このスープ、**おいしいみたい**。<br>*This soup is apparently good. (people are going back for more, so I conclude…)*

Say **おいしそう** in front of a bowl you are about to eat. Say **おいしいみたい** about a bowl in front of somebody else. Getting this backwards is the single commonest error in this corner of the grammar.

---

## ～みたい · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**のみたい**です → ✅ <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**みたい**です<br>*The `の` belongs to `よう`. `みたい` attaches to a bare noun.*

- ❌ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**なみたい** → ✅ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**みたい**<br>*な-adjectives drop the `な` in front of `みたい`, unlike in front of `よう`.*

- ❌ この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>、<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>い<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たい。 → ✅ この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>、<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>い**みたい**。<br>*Never write this pattern with kanji. <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たい</code> means "want to see" and turns the sentence into nonsense.*

- ❌ (in a written notice) <ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になった**みたいです**。 → ✅ <ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になった**ようです**。<br>*Grammatical but out of register. Written announcements do not use `みたい`.*

---

# ～みたいに・～みたいな

**Meaning** · like; similar to - the adverbial and adnominal forms of みたい

`みたい` inflects like a な-adjective, so it has the same two faces as `ようだ`. `～みたいに` goes in front of a **verb or adjective**: the action happens in that manner. `～みたいな` goes in front of a **noun**: the thing is of that kind. These are the casual counterparts of `のように` and `のような`, and the whole difference in form is that no `の` appears anywhere.

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun, before a verb or adjective | Noun + みたいに | <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby> → <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>みたいにあたたかい |
| Noun, before a noun | Noun + みたいな + Noun | <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>みたいな<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |
| Verb clause | plain form + みたいに／みたいな | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったみたいに |
| い-adjective | plain form + みたいに／みたいな | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いみたいな |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + みたいに／みたいな | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだ → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かみたいに |
| Before a verb / adjective | みたい + に | みたいに<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>う |
| Before a noun | みたい + な | みたいな<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby> |
| Formal equivalent | のように／のような (with の) | <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>のような<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - choose by what comes **next**. Noun next → `みたいな`. Anything else → `みたいに`. And never insert `の` or `な` before `みたい`, however strongly the `よう` patterns have trained you to.

> **Note** - the casual counterparts of `このような・そのような・あのような` are not ❌`これみたいな` and friends but the ordinary demonstratives `こんな`, `そんな`, `あんな`, and for the adverbial, `こんなに`, `そんなに`, `あんなに`. そんな<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いたことがない is what a speaker actually says.

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · Examples

<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>**みたいに**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いてね。<br>*Walk quietly, like a cat.*

お<ruby>父<rt>とう</rt></ruby>さん**みたいな**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>と<ruby>結婚<rt>けっこん</rt></ruby>したい。<br>*I want to marry someone like my dad.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>**みたいに**あたたかいね。<br>*It's as warm as spring today, isn't it.*

<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>**みたいに**うすいコーヒーだった。<br>*The coffee was as weak as water.*

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · Examples (cont.)

さっき<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>した**みたいに**、ボタンを<ruby>二回<rt>にかい</rt></ruby><ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*Press the button twice, the way I explained a moment ago.*

<ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ている**みたいな**<ruby>一日<rt>いちにち</rt></ruby>だった。<br>*It was the kind of day that felt like a dream.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**みたいに**<ruby>大声<rt>おおごえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>っていた。<br>*He was laughing at the top of his voice like a child.*

<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>していれば、<ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>**みたいに**<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げるようになるよ。<br>*If you practise every day, you'll end up swimming as fast as my brother.*

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · Nuance & register

These are casual forms and they carry that everywhere they go. In a school essay, a company email or a formal presentation, use `のように` and `のような`. In speech among friends, `みたいに` and `みたいな` are so much the default that the `よう` forms sound self-consciously careful.

`みたいな` has a further, very modern life as a **hedge** in young people's speech, tacked on after a quoted thought or a rough description: 「もうやめようかな、 みたいな」 - "sort of, like, 'maybe I should quit'". This use is heard constantly among speakers in their teens and twenties, is disliked by some older listeners, and should stay out of anything you write. It is worth recognising, not worth copying.

`みたいに` combines freely with a negative to mean "not like…": <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>みたいに<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>にはできない - "I can't do it as well as other people can." The `は` there is the same contrastive `は` you met at N5.

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words.

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · ～みたいに vs ～みたいな

> **Rule of thumb** - `みたいな` must be followed by a noun; `みたいに` must be followed by a verb or an adjective. That is the only difference, and it is absolute.

<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>**みたいな**<ruby>波<rt>なみ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た。<br>*A wave like a mountain came in.*

<ruby>波<rt>なみ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>**みたいに**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くなった。<br>*The waves rose as high as mountains.*

The first describes the wave as an object; the second describes how it rose. Put `みたいな` in front of a verb and the sentence has no predicate to hang on.

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · ～みたいに／みたいな vs ～のように／のような

> **Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, opposite ends of the register scale. And the form differs by exactly one syllable: `Noun + のような` versus `Noun + みたいな`.

<ruby>氷<rt>こおり</rt></ruby>**みたいに**<ruby>冷<rt>つめ</rt></ruby>たい<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>だね。<br>*Your hands are freezing - like ice.*

<ruby>氷<rt>こおり</rt></ruby>**のように**<ruby>冷<rt>つめ</rt></ruby>たい<ruby>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>吹<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>いていた。<br>*A wind as cold as ice was blowing.*

The first is something you say holding somebody's hand; the second is a line from a novel. Neither is more correct - but a written description in `みたいに` reads as sloppy, and a remark to a friend in `のように` reads as arch.

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · ～みたいに vs ～そうに

> **Rule of thumb** - `みたいに` compares the action to **something else**. `そうに` describes how the doer **appears while doing it**, with no comparison at all.

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は**うれしそうに**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>していた。<br>*My sister was talking happily. (she looked happy)*

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**みたいに**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>していた。<br>*My sister was talking like a child. (she sounded childish)*

Learners mix these because both translate with "-ly" or "like". Ask yourself whether a second thing is being brought into the sentence. If yes, `みたいに`. If you are just reading somebody's face, `そうに`.

---

## ～みたいに・～みたいな · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**のみたいな**<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**みたいな**<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby><br>*The `の` belongs to `よう` alone. Attaching both is the classic crossover error.*

- ❌ <ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>**みたいな**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く → ✅ <ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>**みたいに**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く<br>*A verb follows, so the adverbial form is required.*

- ❌ **そんなみたいな**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> → ✅ **そんな**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby><br>*`こんな・そんな・あんな` already *are* the casual "like this/that" words. They never combine with `みたいな`.*

- ❌ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**なみたいに**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える → ✅ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**みたいに**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える<br>*な-adjectives shed their `な` in front of `みたい`. Only `よう` keeps it.*

# ～そうだ - hearsay

**Meaning** · I hear that; they say that - passing on information you got from somewhere else

This `～そうだ` is a **relay**. Somebody told you, you read it, you saw it on the news, and now you are handing the same content on unchanged. You are not judging it and not adding to it.

> **Warning** - there are **two** grammars spelled `そうだ`, and they are unrelated. This one attaches to the **plain form** and means "I hear that". The other attaches to the **stem** and means "looks like". <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いそうだ</code> = "I hear it's expensive". <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうだ</code> = "it looks expensive". One `い` is the entire difference.

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Formation

Attach `そうだ` to the plain form of anything - and unlike `ようだ`, nouns and な-adjectives **keep their だ**.

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + そうだ | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るそうだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + そうだ | <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たそうだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + そうだ | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないそうだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + そうだ | <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いそうだ |
| い-adjective (past) | かった-form + そうだ | <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>かった → <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>かったそうだ |
| な-adjective | ～だ + そうだ | <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>だそうだ |
| Noun | Noun + だ + そうだ | <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby> → <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だそうだ |
| Polite | そうだ → そうです | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るそうです |
| Naming the source | Source + によると／の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では | <ruby>新聞<rt>しんぶん</rt></ruby>によると… |

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - hearsay `そうだ` **never conjugates**. There is no ❌`そうだった`, no ❌`そうな`, no ❌`そうに`, no ❌`そうじゃない`. Tense and negation go on the clause *in front* of it: <code><ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったそうです</code>, not ❌<code><ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るそうでした</code>. This is the sharpest formal difference from the other `そうだ`, which conjugates freely.

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Examples

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**そうです**。<br>*I hear Yamada isn't coming.*

あの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>のラーメンは<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**そうだ**。<br>*They say the ramen at that place is expensive.*

<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby><ruby>予報<rt>よほう</rt></ruby>によると、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>だ**そうです**。<br>*According to the forecast, tomorrow will be snowy.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>はとても<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>だ**そうだ**。<br>*The new station is supposed to be very convenient.*

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では、おばあさんは<ruby>若<rt>わか</rt></ruby>いころ<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>んでいた**そうだ**。<br>*According to my mother, my grandmother lived in Tokyo when she was young.*

<ruby>先輩<rt>せんぱい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>のとき<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>アルバイトをしていた**そうです**。<br>*Apparently my senior worked a part-time job every single day at university.*

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Nuance & register

`そうです` is neutral polite and turns up everywhere: news reports, workplace updates, ordinary conversation. Plain `そうだ` is the written form; spoken between friends it sounds bookish, and casual Japanese reaches instead for the quotative `って` - 「<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないって」 - or for `らしい`.

Two structural facts follow from the fact that this is a pure relay:

- **It cannot be questioned.** ❌<code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るそうですか</code> is not how you ask.<br>*If you want to check what somebody heard, you say <code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るそうですね</code> (seeking agreement) or <code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るんですか</code>.*
- **It cannot be hedged.** You do not write<br>*❌<code>たぶん<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るそうです</code>. The hedging belongs to whoever said it first, not to you.*

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Nuance & register (cont.)

Naming the source is optional but very common, and it is what makes the sentence sound responsible rather than gossipy. `～によると` is the neutral formula; <code>～の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では</code> is slightly softer and more personal; <code>～の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>によると</code> is the fullest and most formal of the three.

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis - it does not weigh evidence at all, it forwards a message.

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · hearsay ～そうだ vs appearance ～そうだ

This is the distinction the whole page exists for.

> **Rule of thumb** - **plain form** + `そうだ` = "I hear". **Stem** + `そうだ` = "looks". Nouns can take the first and never the second.

このかばんは<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**そうだ**。<br>*I hear this bag is expensive. (someone told me the price)*

このかばんは**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうだ**。<br>*This bag looks expensive. (I'm looking at it; I have no idea of the price)*

Three further tests separate them when the form alone is ambiguous:

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · hearsay ～そうだ vs appearance ～そうだ (cont.)

1. **Conjugation.** Appearance `そうだ` becomes <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうな</code>, <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうに</code>, <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうだった</code>. Hearsay `そうだ` does none of that. 2. **Nouns.** <code><ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だそうだ</code> ("I hear he's a student") is fine; ❌<code><ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>そうだ</code> does not exist, because appearance `そうだ` cannot attach to a noun at all. 3. **Source phrases.** `～によると` can only introduce the hearsay one.

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · ～そうだ vs ～らしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` forwards a message with its source intact. `らしい` says "this is going around" - the source is vaguer, and a little of the speaker's own judgement leaks in.

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>によると、その<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になった**そうです**。<br>*According to the department head, the plan has been cancelled.*

その<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>、<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になった**らしい**ですよ。<br>*That plan's apparently been cancelled.*

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · ～そうだ vs ～らしい (cont.)

The first is a report you would stand behind because you can name who said it. The second is what you say when you are not quite sure where you picked it up. There is also a formal difference: `らしい` conjugates like an い-adjective and can modify a noun - <code><ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になったらしい<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby></code> - while `そうだ` can only end a sentence.

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · ～そうだ vs ～と言っていた

> **Rule of thumb** - <code>と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていた</code> quotes a **person** saying a **thing**. `そうだ` reports the **content** and leaves the speech act behind.

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ると**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていました**。<br>*Tanaka said he'd be leaving early today.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**そうです**。<br>*Tanaka is apparently leaving early today.*

Use the first when the fact that he *said it* matters - you are reporting a conversation. Use the second when only the information matters. In a workplace, the first is the safer choice if somebody might later ask who told you.

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**そうです**。 → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**だそうです**。<br>*Nouns keep their `だ` before hearsay `そうだ`. Dropping it produces the other `そうだ`, which cannot take a noun anyway - so the sentence is simply broken.*

- ❌ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**なそうです** → ✅ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**だそうです**<br>*な-adjectives also keep `だ`. The `な` form belongs in front of `よう`, not `そう`.*

- ❌ きのうは<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い**そうでした**。 → ✅ きのうは<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>かった**そうです**。<br>*`そうだ` itself never carries tense. Put the past on the adjective in front of it.*

---

## ～そうだ - hearsay · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>辛<rt>から</rt></ruby>そうです**。(meaning "I hear it's spicy") → ✅ この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>辛<rt>から</rt></ruby>いそうです**。<br>*Without the `い` you have said it *looks* spicy, which is a claim about the photo rather than the taste.*

# ～そうだ - appearance

**Meaning** · looks like; seems about to - an immediate impression taken off the surface

This `～そうだ` reports what something **looks like right now**. There is no reasoning in it and no information from anybody else: you are describing a surface you can see. With an adjective it means "looks ～"; with a verb it means "looks about to ～".

> **Warning** - there are **two** grammars spelled `そうだ`, and this is the other one. Hearsay `そうだ` attaches to the **plain form**: <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いそうだ</code> = "I hear it's expensive". Appearance `そうだ` attaches to the **stem**: <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうだ</code> = "it looks expensive". They are different sentences and they are not interchangeable.

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · Formation

Everything loses its ending before `そう`. Verbs give up `ます`, い-adjectives give up `い`, な-adjectives give up `な`. **Nouns cannot take this pattern at all.**

---

> **Rule of thumb** - one-syllable adjectives need a cushion. `いい` and `ない` are too short to survive losing their ending, so they insert `さ`: **よさそう**, **なさそう**. ❌`いそう` and ❌`なそう` are not words.

## ～そうだ - appearance · Examples

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りそうだ**。<br>*It looks like it could rain any moment.*

## ～そうだ - appearance · Examples (cont.)

この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しそう**だが、やってみよう。<br>*This problem looks hard, but let's have a go at it.*

このアプリ、**よさそう**だから<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってみよう。<br>*This app looks decent - let's give it a try.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**なさそう**なので、また<ruby>今度<rt>こんど</rt></ruby>にしましょう。<br>*It doesn't look like there'll be time today, so let's do it another time.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby><ruby>中<rt>じゅう</rt></ruby>には<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わり**そうにない**から、<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>きは<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>にします。<br>*There's no sign of this being finished today, so I'll carry on tomorrow.*

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · Nuance & register

`そうだ` is register-neutral and enormously common in speech. As a one-word reaction to food it is almost obligatory - 「おいしそう!」 said over somebody's plate is a compliment, not a comment, and its absence is noticed.

Two restrictions are worth knowing, because breaking either sounds distinctly odd:

- **Not for properties you can simply see.** Colour, shape and<br>*`きれい` are already right in front of you, so `そう` has nothing to add: you say `きれいですね`, not ❌`きれいそうですね`. `そう` is for a property you are *inferring* from the surface - tenderness, expense, difficulty.*
- **Not about yourself.** ❌<code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はねむそうです</code> is strange,<br>*because you do not need to read your own face. Say <code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はねむいです</code>. `そう` about other people's inner states - `うれしそう`, <code><ruby>寂<rt>さび</rt></ruby>しそう</code> - is exactly right, because their feelings are only available to you through appearance.*

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · Nuance & register (cont.)

> **Note** - `かわいい` has no `そう` form, because `かわいそう` is already a separate word meaning "poor thing, pitiable". To say something looks cute, use `かわいらしい` or <code>かわいく<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える</code>.

The negative deserves a paragraph of its own. For **adjectives**, the negative goes inside: <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くなさそう</code> ("doesn't look cheap"). For **verbs**, the standard form is `ます-stem + そうにない` or `そうもない`: <code><ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いそうにない</code>. You will also hear <code><ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らなさそう</code> in casual speech, formed on the ない-form instead; it is widespread but not what a textbook or a careful writer will use.

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing, before reasoning starts. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of a message.

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · appearance ～そうだ vs hearsay ～そうだ

> **Rule of thumb** - count the `い`. <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い</code> **そうだ** is a rumour about the price. <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby></code>**そうだ** is a look at the object.

この<ruby>時計<rt>とけい</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうだ**。<br>*This watch looks expensive.*

この<ruby>時計<rt>とけい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**そうだ**。<br>*I hear this watch is expensive.*

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · appearance ～そうだ vs hearsay ～そうだ (cont.)

Everything else follows from that. The appearance form **conjugates** - you can say <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうな<ruby>時計<rt>とけい</rt></ruby></code> and <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうだった</code> - whereas the hearsay form is frozen: no ❌<code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いそうな</code>, no ❌<code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いそうだった</code>. And a noun can precede the hearsay one (<code><ruby>本物<rt>ほんもの</rt></ruby>だそうだ</code>) but never the appearance one.

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · appearance ～そうだ vs ～ようだ／～みたい

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` describes the **surface**. `ようだ` and `みたい` report a **conclusion** you have reasoned your way to. `そうだ` is what you say at a glance; `ようだ` is what you say after thinking.

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しそう**だ。<br>*She looks busy. (she's rushing around with her arms full)*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい**ようだ**。<br>*She seems to be busy. (she hasn't answered my messages for three days)*

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · appearance ～そうだ vs ～ようだ／～みたい (cont.)

The first is a photograph, the second is an argument. Notice also that `そうだ` cannot look at things that have no surface: you can say <code><ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しそう</code> about a person in front of you, but for someone you have not seen, only `ようだ` and `みたい` work.

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · ～そうにない／そうもない vs ～なさそう

> **Rule of thumb** - verbs take `そうにない`; adjectives take `なさそう`. Mixing them is the commonest error in the negative.

この<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>は**やみそうにない**。<br>*There's no sign of this rain letting up.*

この<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>丈夫<rt>じょうぶ</rt></ruby>じゃなさそう**だ。<br>*This umbrella doesn't look sturdy.*

`そうもない` is a slightly stronger, more emphatic variant of `そうにない` - "not the faintest sign of it" - and `そうにもない` combines both for extra weight. All three are natural; `そうにない` is the neutral one to default to.

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>**そうです**。 → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>**のようです**。<br>*Appearance `そうだ` cannot attach to a noun. Use `ようだ` or `みたい` instead.*

- ❌ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は**いいそう**だ。(meaning "looks good") → ✅ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は**よさそう**だ。<br>*`いい` inserts `さ`. Note that `いいそうだ` is not meaningless - it is the *hearsay* form, "I hear it's good".*

- ❌ お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>が**ないそう**だ。(meaning "looks like there's no money") → ✅ お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>が**なさそう**だ。<br>*Same irregularity: `ない` also takes the `さ` cushion.*

- ❌ この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>は**おいしいそう**だ。(meaning "looks delicious") → ✅ この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>は**おいしそう**だ。<br>*Keeping the `い` turns it into hearsay. The `い` must go.*

---

## ～そうだ - appearance · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は**うれしそうです**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は**うれしいです**。<br>*You do not infer your own feelings from your own appearance. `そう` about the speaker's inner state sounds as if you were watching yourself on video.*

# ～そうに・～そうな

**Meaning** · looking ～; ～ly, by the look of it - the adverbial and adnominal forms of appearance そうだ

Appearance `そうだ` inflects like a な-adjective, so it can step inside a sentence instead of ending one. `～そうな` goes in front of a **noun** and describes how that thing looks. `～そうに` goes in front of a **verb or adjective** and describes the manner the doer appears to be in. Hearsay `そうだ` has no such forms at all - so a `そうな` or `そうに` you meet in the wild is always the appearance one.

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Formation

The attachment is the same as for sentence-final appearance `そうだ`: everything loses its ending first.

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb, before a noun | ます-stem + そうな | <ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>きます → <ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>きそうな<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby> |
| Verb, before a verb | ます-stem + そうに | <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちます → <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちそうに<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える |
| い-adjective | drop い + そうな／そうに | <ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しい → <ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しそうに |
| な-adjective | drop だ／な + そうな／そうに | <ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>そうな<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby> |
| いい (irregular) | よ + さそうな／さそうに | いい → よさそうな<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby> |
| ない (irregular) | な + さそうな／さそうに | ない → なさそうに |

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | - | not possible; use `のような`／`みたいな` |

> **Rule of thumb** - the same choice as every な-adjective. Noun follows → `そうな`. Verb or adjective follows → `そうに`. And `そうにない` is literally `そうに` + `ない`, which is why the negative of a verb takes this shape rather than ❌`そうくない`.

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Examples

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は**ねむそうな**<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>をしている。<br>*My little brother has a sleepy look on his face.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもたちが**<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しそうに**<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでいる。<br>*The children are playing away happily.*

**<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>そうな**かばんですね。<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ちましょうか。<br>*That bag looks heavy. Shall I carry it?*

<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>が**うれしそうに**しっぽをふっている。<br>*The dog is wagging its tail in delight.*

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくそうに**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めた。<br>*She started speaking as though she found it hard to get the words out.*

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>にも**<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>きそうな**<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>をかけてきた。<br>*He rang me in a voice that sounded ready to break.*

**よさそうな**<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>空<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いていたので、そこに<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>った。<br>*A decent-looking seat was free, so I sat there.*

<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>がひどいので、バスは**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>そうにない**ですね。<br>*The snow's bad, so it doesn't look like the bus is coming.*

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Nuance & register

These forms are register-neutral and belong to speech and writing alike. Politeness lives elsewhere in the sentence - <code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しそうに<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>しています</code> is polite, <code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しそうに<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>している</code> is plain, and `そうに` itself never changes.

`そうに` before a verb of action is worth dwelling on, because English does not carve it up the same way. <code>おいしそうに<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる</code> does **not** mean "eat something that looks delicious" - it means "eat with evident enjoyment". The `そう` describes the **eater's appearance**, not the food's. The same goes for <code><ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>そうに<ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>する</code> and <code>つまらなそうに<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く</code>: in every case you are reading the person.

Three combinations worth learning as units:

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Nuance & register (cont.)

- <code>～そうに<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える</code> - "looks ～". Slightly more deliberate than plain<br>*`そうだ`, and useful when you want to contrast appearance with reality: <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>そうに<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えるが、<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れている。*
- `～そうにする` - "act ～", put on the appearance:<br>*<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しそうにしている。*
- <code><ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>にも～そうな</code> - "on the very point of ～". `今にも` is the<br>*standard partner adverb for imminence.*

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ`, and with it `そうな` and `そうに`, is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis, and has no adverbial or adnominal form to offer.

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · ～そうな vs ～そうに

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうな` describes a **thing**; `そうに` describes an **action**. Look at what comes after the pattern, not before it.

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>苦<rt>くる</rt></ruby>しそうな**<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>をした。<br>*He made a pained face.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>苦<rt>くる</rt></ruby>しそうに**<ruby>息<rt>いき</rt></ruby>をしていた。<br>*He was breathing with evident difficulty.*

The first attributes the look to the face; the second attributes it to the way he was breathing. The difference is small in English and structural in Japanese: put `そうな` in front of a verb and there is nothing for it to modify.

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · ～そうに vs ～ように／みたいに

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうに` reads the doer's own appearance. `ように` and `みたいに` compare the action to **something else**. If a second thing enters the sentence, it is not `そうに`.

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>悲<rt>かな</rt></ruby>しそうに**<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいた。<br>*My mother was reading the letter with a sad look.*

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby>も<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んだことがある**ように**<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいた。<br>*My mother read the letter as though she had read it many times before.*

In the first, all the information is on her face. In the second, the sentence brings in an imagined alternative situation and measures her against it.

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · ～そうにない vs ～ないそうだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうにない` is *my* reading of the situation. `ないそうだ` is *somebody else's* information. They sound alike and they mean entirely different things.

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>そうにない**。<br>*It doesn't look like Tanaka is coming. (it's late and there's no sign of him)*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないそうだ**。<br>*Tanaka isn't coming, apparently. (he told somebody, and it got to me)*

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · ～そうにない vs ～ないそうだ (cont.)

Note the verb shape as well as the ending: `そうにない` sits on the **ます-stem** <code><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby></code>, while hearsay `そうだ` sits on the full negative <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない</code>. Two different readings of the same kanji, which makes this pair worth saying out loud until it sticks.

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Common mistakes

- ❌ ねむ**そうだ**<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby> → ✅ ねむ**そうな**<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby><br>*`そうだ` ends a sentence. In front of a noun it takes the `な` form, exactly like <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだ</code> becoming <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かな</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>し**そうな**<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでいる → ✅ <ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>し**そうに**<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでいる<br>*A verb follows, so the adverbial form is the only option.*

- ❌ **いいそうな**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby> → ✅ **よさそうな**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby><br>*`いい` takes the irregular `さ` cushion. `いいそうな` is not a form at all - hearsay `そうだ` has no `な`.*

---

## ～そうに・～そうな · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うそうにない** → ✅ <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いそうにない**<br>*`そうにない` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form before `そう` would be hearsay, which cannot be negated this way.*

- ❌ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**そうな**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>**そうな**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby><br>*Drop the `い`. With it, you have tried to build an adnominal form out of the hearsay pattern, which does not have one.*

# ～らしい

**Meaning** · apparently, I gather - and, after a noun, "a proper ～, just as a ～ should be"

`～らしい` does two jobs. As an **evidential** it says the information came from outside the speaker - heard, read, picked up somewhere - and quietly declines to vouch for it. As a **suffix on a noun** it says the thing fits its category admirably: <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>らしい<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby> is weather that is exactly what spring ought to be.

---

## ～らしい · Formation

`らしい` attaches to plain forms, and nouns and な-adjectives attach **bare** - no `だ`, no `な`, no `の`. Once attached, it conjugates like an い-adjective.

---

## ～らしい · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + らしい | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るらしい |
| Verb (past) | た-form + らしい | <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たらしい |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + らしい | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないらしい |
| い-adjective | plain form + らしい | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いらしい |
| な-adjective | drop だ + らしい | <ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>らしい |
| Noun (evidential) | Noun + らしい | <ruby>病気<rt>びょうき</rt></ruby> → <ruby>病気<rt>びょうき</rt></ruby>らしい |
| Noun (typical of) | Noun + らしい + Noun | <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby> → <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>らしい<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby> |
| Polite | らしい + です | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るらしいです |
| Past | らしかった | <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たらしかった |

---

## ～らしい · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Adverbial (suffix sense only) | らしく | <ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>らしく<ruby>生<rt>い</rt></ruby>きる |
| Negative (suffix sense only) | らしくない | <ruby>君<rt>きみ</rt></ruby>らしくない |
| Noun form (suffix sense only) | らしさ | <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>らしさ |

> **Rule of thumb** - the two senses split on the negative. The **suffix** sense has `らしくない` ("not very ～, unlike them"). The **evidential** sense does not: to say "apparently he isn't coming", negate the verb, not `らしい` - <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないらしい</code>, never ❌<code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るらしくない</code>.

---

## ～らしい · Examples

<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>**らしい**よ。<br>*The exam's apparently next week.*

この<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>あぶない**らしい**です。<br>*I gather this road is dangerous at night.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>**らしい**<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby>ですね。<br>*Proper spring weather today, isn't it.*

となりの<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飼<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めた**らしい**。<br>*The people next door have apparently got themselves a dog.*

---

## ～らしい · Examples (cont.)

そんなことを<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うなんて、<ruby>君<rt>きみ</rt></ruby>**らしくない**ね。<br>*Saying a thing like that isn't like you at all.*

<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**らしく**、まじめに<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しなさい。<br>*Study properly, the way a student should.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby><ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**らしい**ですが、まだはっきりしません。<br>*Word is the department head is moving to Osaka next year, but nothing's settled yet.*

<ruby>近所<rt>きんじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では、この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいスーパーができる**らしい**です。<br>*According to the neighbours, a new supermarket is going up around here.*

---

## ～らしい · Nuance & register

`らしい` is at home in ordinary conversation, plain or polite. Its defining flavour is **distance**: you are relaying something without putting your name to it. That distance is useful when you genuinely do not know, and damaging when you are expected to. A doctor saying <code><ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるらしいです</code> about his own patient sounds as though he has not checked; a shop assistant saying <code><ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>くらしいです</code> about your order sounds as though nobody is in charge. In those situations use `そうです` with a named source, or state the fact plainly.

For the same reason, `らしい` is not the form for formal writing. A report uses `とのことだ` or hearsay `そうだ`.

---

## ～らしい · Nuance & register (cont.)

The **suffix** sense is a compliment machine. <ruby>男<rt>おとこ</rt></ruby>らしい, <ruby>女<rt>おんな</rt></ruby>らしい, <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもらしい, <ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>らしい, <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>らしい - each says the person or thing lives up to what the noun implies. Be aware that the gendered pair carries traditional expectations and, in present-day Japanese, has a somewhat conservative ring for many speakers; it is still used, but it is not neutral in the way <code><ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>らしい</code> is.

> **Note** - <code><ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>らしく<ruby>生<rt>い</rt></ruby>きる</code>, "live as yourself", and the noun <code><ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>らしさ</code>, "one's own character", are set phrases you will meet constantly in advertising and self-help writing. The `らしさ` form belongs to the suffix sense only.

---

## ～らしい · Contrast

> **The four evidentials, on one axis.** `らしい` leans on **outside** evidence - what you heard, read or were told. `ようだ` leans on **your own** evidence - what you saw, felt and reasoned from. `みたい` is `ようだ` in casual clothes. Appearance `そうだ` is narrower than any of them: the **immediate look** of a thing. Hearsay `そうだ` is off the axis - a plain relay of somebody's words.

---

## ～らしい · ～らしい vs hearsay ～そうだ

> **Rule of thumb** - both pass information on, but `そうだ` forwards it **intact and sourced**, while `らしい` reports what is **in the air**. If you can say who told you, `そうだ` is the honest choice.

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>によると、<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>テストがある**そうです**。<br>*According to the teacher, there's a test next month.*

<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>テストがある**らしい**よ。<br>*There's a test next month, apparently.*

---

## ～らしい · ～らしい vs hearsay ～そうだ (cont.)

There is a formal difference too, and it is decisive in an exam. `らしい` conjugates and can modify a noun - <code><ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になったらしい<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby></code> - while hearsay `そうだ` is frozen at the end of a sentence and can do neither.

---

## ～らしい · ～らしい vs ～ようだ／～みたい

> **Rule of thumb** - `らしい` points **outward** at a source. `ようだ` and `みたい` point **inward** at the speaker's own reasoning. Ask "what makes you say that?": if the answer is "someone said so", it is `らしい`.

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby><ruby>結婚<rt>けっこん</rt></ruby>する**らしい**。<br>*Hayashi is apparently getting married next year. (that's the word going round)*

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby><ruby>結婚<rt>けっこん</rt></ruby>する**ようだ**。<br>*It looks as though Hayashi is getting married next year. (the ring, the house-hunting)*

The two shade into each other in practice, and a speaker choosing between them is often choosing how much responsibility to take. `ようだ` puts the speaker's judgement behind the claim; `らしい` sets it down on the table and steps back.

---

## ～らしい · Noun + らしい vs Noun + っぽい

> **Rule of thumb** - `らしい` means "admirably like a ～". `っぽい` means "～-ish", and usually with a shrug or a complaint attached. They are not interchangeable, and learners reach for the wrong one constantly.

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**らしい**まっすぐな<ruby>質問<rt>しつもん</rt></ruby>だった。<br>*It was a straightforward question, in the good way children ask them.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**っぽい**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>はやめたほうがいい。<br>*You should stop talking in that childish way.*

---

## ～らしい · Noun + らしい vs Noun + っぽい (cont.)

`っぽい` is casual and productive - <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>っぽい, <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>っぽい, <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れっぽい - and it very often carries mild disapproval. `らしい` in the same slot is praise. There is a third option, `Noun + のような`, which is neither: it merely notes a resemblance without saying whether the thing is really in the category.

---

## ～らしい · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**だらしい** → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**らしい**<br>*Nouns attach bare. The `だ` is required before hearsay `そうだ` and forbidden here*

- a difference worth drilling, because the two patterns sit next to each other.

- ❌ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**ならしい** → ✅ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**らしい**<br>*な-adjectives also drop everything. `な` belongs in front of `よう`.*

- ❌ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る**らしくない**。(meaning "apparently isn't coming") → ✅ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**らしい**。<br>*The evidential sense has no negative. Negate the clause instead. `らしくない` only exists in the "not like a proper ～" sense.*

---

## ～らしい · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**っぽい**<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>な<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>がいいですね。(intended as praise) → ✅ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**らしい**<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>な<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>がいいですね。<br>*`っぽい` would turn the compliment into a complaint about immaturity.*

## ～らしい · Practice 1

**1.** ニュースで<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たんだけど、あの<ruby>橋<rt>はし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えない____よ。

{reveal}
あの<ruby>橋<rt>はし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えない**らしい**よ。

{reveal}
The information came from outside the speaker, which is what `らしい` marks, and it attaches straight to the plain negative <code><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えない</code>. Note that the negation lives on the verb, never on `らしい` itself.

## ～らしい · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>がきれいに<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づいている。<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>った（らしい・ようだ）。Which fits better if nobody told you?

{reveal}
<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>った**ようだ**。

{reveal}
You are reasoning from a tidy desk that you can see with your own eyes, so the judgement is yours. `らしい` would imply somebody had mentioned it, which the situation as described rules out.

## ～らしい · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "That was a very Japanese answer."

{reveal}
とても<ruby>日本人<rt>にほんじん</rt></ruby>**らしい**<ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>えでしたね。

{reveal}
This is the suffix sense: the answer fits the category admirably. `らしい` attaches to the bare noun and then modifies <code><ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>え</code> like an い-adjective.

## ～らしい · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>だらしいです。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>**らしい**です。

{reveal}
Nouns take `らしい` bare. The `だ` you are remembering belongs to hearsay `そうだ`: <code><ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>だそうです</code> is the correct sentence with that pattern.

## ～らしい · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>らしい**。 (b) <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>のようだ**。

{reveal}
(a) is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is real: it can mean "apparently she's a teacher" (evidential, someone told you) or "she is very much the teacher" (suffix, she has the manner). Context and intonation decide. (b) has only one reading - she resembles a teacher, and the implication is that she probably is not one. That is the general rule of thumb: `らしい` puts the person inside the category, `のようだ` keeps them outside it.
