# ～ことができる

**Meaning** · can; be able to - the long, formal way of saying that something is possible

`～ことができる` turns a verb into a noun-like chunk with `こと` and then says that that chunk "exists as a possibility". It means exactly what the short potential form means, but it is longer, flatter and more official - the shape you meet on signs, in manuals, in contracts and in written explanations.

---

## ～ことができる · Formation

Attach `ことができる` to the **dictionary form**. Nothing about the verb changes, which is what makes this pattern the safe fallback while the potential form is still settling in.

---

## ～ことができる · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + ことができる | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ → <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐことができる |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + ことができる | <ruby>調<rt>しら</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>調<rt>しら</rt></ruby>べることができる |
| Verb (する) | する + ことができる | <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>することができる |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る + ことができる | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ることができる |
| Noun (an activity) | Noun + ができる - no こと | テニス → テニスができる |
| Negative | ことができない | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐことができない |
| Past | ことができた | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐことができた |
| Polite | ことができます／ことができません／ことができました | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐことができます |

---

## ～ことができる · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - `できる` is an ordinary る-verb, so everything after `こと` conjugates normally: ことができて, ことができれば, ことができるように. The `こと` itself never changes and is never replaced by `の`.

---

## ～ことができる · Examples

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はピアノを**<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>くことができます**。<br>*I can play the piano.*

ここで<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>ることができません**。<br>*You can't take photographs here.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>中国語<rt>ちゅうごくご</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すことができる**。<br>*Tanaka can speak Chinese.*

このカードで<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>にも**<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>ることができます**。<br>*You can use this card on the trains as well.*

---

## ～ことができる · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものころは<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>ることができなかった**。<br>*As a child I couldn't ride a bicycle.*

<ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>三日<rt>みっか</rt></ruby><ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>まで**<ruby>変更<rt>へんこう</rt></ruby>することができます**。<br>*Reservations may be changed up to three days in advance.*

インターネットさえあれば、<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>からでも<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>参加<rt>さんか</rt></ruby>することができます**。<br>*As long as you have internet, you can join the meeting from home too.*

<ruby>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>強<rt>つよ</rt></ruby>い<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>屋上<rt>おくじょう</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がることができません**のでご<ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>ください。<br>*On windy days the roof terrace cannot be accessed, so please be aware.*

---

## ～ことができる · Nuance & register

`～ことができる` is **not** casual. It is grammatically fine at any politeness level, but its flavour is written and institutional. A shop puts <code>ご<ruby>利用<rt>りよう</rt></ruby>いただくことができます</code> on a sign; a friend asking whether you can come tonight says <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られる?</code>, never ❌<code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ることができる?</code>. Using it in chatty speech is the single clearest way to sound like a textbook.

Three places where it is genuinely the better choice:

---

## ～ことができる · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **Rules and notices.** <ruby>館内<rt>かんない</rt></ruby>では<ruby>飲食<rt>いんしょく</rt></ruby>することができません.<br>*The length reads as officialdom, which is the point.*
- **When the potential form would be ambiguous.** For る-verbs,<br>*<code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられる</code> could be "can eat" or "gets eaten"; <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べることができる</code> can only be "can eat".*
- **In careful writing**, where the ら<ruby>抜<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>き potential<br>*(<code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べれる</code>) is unacceptable and the full <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられる</code> feels heavy.*

---

## ～ことができる · Nuance & register (cont.)

With する-verbs the pattern is often padded out for no reason. <code><ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>することができます</code> and <code><ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>できます</code> mean the same thing, and the short one is what people say. Reserve the long version for the register that wants it.

Finally, note that `できる` here is a stative verb, so it does not take `～ている` for a present ability: ❌<code><ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐことができています</code>. Plain <code><ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐことができます</code> already covers the present.

---

## ～ことができる · ～ことができる vs the potential form

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. The potential form is what you **say**; `～ことができる` is what the notice on the wall **prints**.

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>くことが**できます**。<br>*I can write kanji. (measured, explanatory)*

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けます**。<br>*I can write kanji. (ordinary spoken Japanese)*

---

## ～ことができる · ～ことができる vs the potential form (cont.)

Watch the particle. `～ことができる` keeps the original `を`, because the object still belongs to the inner verb. The potential form pulls the object into `が`. Both <code><ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>ける</code> and <code><ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>ける</code> exist, but ❌<code><ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>くことができる</code> does not - the `が` has nothing to attach to.

---

## ～ことができる · ～ことができる vs Noun + ができる

> **Rule of thumb** - a **verb** needs `こと` in front of `が`. A **noun** takes `が` directly and must not have `こと`.

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>はスキー**ができます**。<br>*My little brother can ski.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>はスキーを**することができます**。<br>*My little brother is able to ski. (stiffer, but grammatical)*

Nouns of activity - スキー, テニス, <ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>, <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>, <ruby>通訳<rt>つうやく</rt></ruby> - go straight into `が` + `できる`. Adding `こと` to a noun (❌スキーのことができる) is meaningless.

---

## ～ことができる · ～ることができる vs ～たことがある

> **Rule of thumb** - the tense before `こと` decides everything. **Dictionary form** + ことができる = *can*. **た-form** + ことがある = *have ever*.

These two look almost identical on the page and mean completely different things.

<ruby>富士山<rt>ふじさん</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>ることができます**。<br>*You can climb Mt Fuji. (it is possible)*

<ruby>富士山<rt>ふじさん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>った**ことがあります**。<br>*I have climbed Mt Fuji. (experience)*

The verb at the end differs too - `できる` for ability, `ある` for experience. Mixing them (❌<ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>ったことができます) produces a sentence that means nothing.

---

## ～ことができる · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのができます。 → ✅ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ**ことができます**。<br>*`の` nominalises fine before <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> and <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code>, but this pattern is fixed with `こと`.*

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられることができる → ✅ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ることができる**／<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**られる**<br>*Pick one. Stacking the potential form inside `ことができる` says "can" twice.*

- ❌ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>をことができます。 → ✅ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>**ができます**。<br>*A bare noun takes `が` + `できる` with no `こと` and no `を`.*

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ることができないでした。 → ✅ <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ること**ができませんでした**。<br>*`できる` is a verb, not an adjective; the past negative is `できませんでした`.*

---

## ～ことができる · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むことができます。 → ✅ <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むことができます。<br>*Inside the `こと` chunk the verb is still ordinary and transitive, so its object keeps `を`.*

---

## ～ことができる · Practice 1

**1.** すみません、ここでタバコを<ruby>吸<rt>す</rt></ruby>う____ができますか。

{reveal}
タバコを<ruby>吸<rt>す</rt></ruby>う**こと**ができますか。

{reveal}
A verb has to be nominalised by `こと` before `が できる` can take it. The dictionary form <code><ruby>吸<rt>す</rt></ruby>う</code> is what `こと` attaches to, and the object keeps its `を`.

---

## ～ことができる · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>はフランス<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby>（ができます・のことができます）。

{reveal}
<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>はフランス<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby>**ができます**。

{reveal}
<code>フランス<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby></code> is a noun, so it goes straight into `が` + `できる`. `こと` is only needed to turn a *verb* into something `できる` can accept.

---

## ～ことができる · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese, in the register of a museum notice: "Photography is not permitted inside the hall."

{reveal}
<ruby>館内<rt>かんない</rt></ruby>では<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>る**ことができません**。

{reveal}
This is exactly the register `～ことができません` was made for - impersonal, written, institutional. The spoken equivalent <code><ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>、<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>れないよ</code> would be badly out of place on a sign.

---

## ～ことができる · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったことができます。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**ことがあります**。

{reveal}
The た-form before `こと` signals *experience*, and experience pairs with `ある`, not `できる`. If the intended meaning were "I can go to Hokkaido", it would be <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くことができます</code> - dictionary form, and `できる`.

---

## ～ことができる · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>うことができます。 (b) この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えます。

{reveal}
Both say the room is available. (a) is the announcement version - a receptionist explaining the rules, a sign on the door, a written policy. Its length and the retained `を` make it sound like an official statement of what is permitted.

{reveal}
(b) is what a colleague says to you in the corridor. The potential form <code><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>える</code> with `が` is compact and unremarkable, and in ordinary conversation (a) would sound oddly ceremonious for the same piece of information.

# ～られる - potential, "can do"

**Meaning** · be able to do it - the ability, or the circumstances, are there

The potential form says that an action is available to the subject. It covers both halves of English *can*: the skill you have (<code><ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げる</code>, "I can swim") and the opportunity the world gives you (<code><ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>める</code>, "I can take today off"). Japanese does not separate the two.

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Formation

Only る-verbs and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る actually use the shape `られる`. う-verbs use a shorter ending, and する is replaced outright.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → え-row kana, then + る | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>ける |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + られる | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by できる - no られる at all | <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>できる |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | stem こ, + られる | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られる |

Every う-verb ending, spelled out. The last row is the one people get wrong:

| ～く | く → け + る | <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>ける |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → げ + る | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ → <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げる |
| ～す | す → せ + る | <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せる |
| ～つ | つ → て + る | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>てる |

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - a potential verb is always a **る-verb**, whatever it started as. So <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>ける → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けない, <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けます, <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けて, <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けた; and <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられない, <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられます.

| Further forms | Example |
|---|---|
| Negative | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けない／<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられない／できない／<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られない |
| Polite | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けます／<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられます／できます／<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られます |
| Past | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けた／<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられた／できた／<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られた |
| ら<ruby>抜<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>き (spoken, nonstandard) | - ／<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べれる／ - ／<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>れる |

The object of a potential verb normally shifts from `を` to `が`: <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>ける.

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Examples

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>**げません**。<br>*I can't swim.*

<ruby>辛<rt>から</rt></ruby>いものが**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられます**か。<br>*Can you eat spicy food?*

この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めない**。<br>*I can't read this kanji.*

<ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>なしでも**<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>れます**か。<br>*Can we get in without a reservation?*

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるので、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られません**。<br>*I have a fever, so I can't come in to work today.*

チケットが<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>れたから、<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>のコンサートに**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ける**よ。<br>*I got tickets, so I can go to the concert next month.*

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>現金<rt>げんきん</rt></ruby>でしか**<ruby>払<rt>はら</rt></ruby>えない**ので、カードは<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えません。<br>*This place only takes cash, so you can't use a card.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものころは<ruby>牛乳<rt>ぎゅうにゅう</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>めなかった**が、<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>は<ruby>平気<rt>へいき</rt></ruby>になった。<br>*I couldn't drink milk as a child, but I'm fine with it now.*

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Nuance & register

The potential form is register-neutral and is the default way to say *can* in speech. It has no built-in politeness; `～ます` on the end handles that.

**The particle.** `が` is the textbook answer and the safest one: <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せます. `を` is also alive and common, and it is preferred when the object is long, when the clause already contains a `が`, or when the `を` was never a direct object to begin with - <ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>ける, <ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>られる.

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Nuance & register (cont.)

**ら<ruby>抜<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>き<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>.** In everyday speech many speakers drop the `ら` from the る-verb and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る potentials: <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べれる, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>れる, <ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きれる, <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>れる. This is extremely widespread - in some regions it is simply the normal form - and it has a real advantage: it separates potential from passive, which the full form cannot do. It is nevertheless still classed as nonstandard. Do not write it in an exam, an essay or a business email, and note that it never touches う-verbs: <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>ける is not ら<ruby>抜<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>き, it is just the potential.

**れ<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>す<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>** is the opposite error and is unambiguously wrong: ❌<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けれる, ❌<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めれる. An extra `れ` is inserted where nothing belongs.

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Verbs with no potential form.** Some verbs already contain the idea of ability or spontaneous perception and refuse the potential: `わかる`, `できる`, <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える</code>, <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえる</code>. `ある` has none either. And verbs describing something involuntary - <code><ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れる</code>, <code><ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る</code> - take one only in odd, marked contexts.

**Politeness by potential.** Turning a request into a potential question softens it a great deal: <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてもらえますか is gentler than <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてください, and <code><ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けていただけませんか</code> gentler still. This is one of the main jobs the form does in adult conversation.

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · ～られる - potential vs passive

> **Rule of thumb** - for **う-verbs** the two forms are different words (<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>める vs <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まれる). For **る-verbs and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る** they are identical, and only the particles and the context tell you which one you are looking at.

<code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられる</code> is genuinely two words wearing one coat:

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>納豆<rt>なっとう</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられる**。<br>*I can eat nattō. (potential)*

ケーキを<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられた**。<br>*My little brother ate my cake (on me). (passive)*

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · ～られる - potential vs passive (cont.)

Three signals separate them. The potential marks its object with `が` and has no agent; the passive marks its agent with `に`. The potential is about the subject's own capacity; the passive is about something done to the subject. And the passive very often turns up in the past, reporting a nuisance.

When the sentence would be genuinely ambiguous, Japanese avoids the form rather than tolerating it: speakers reach for <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べれる</code> in casual speech or <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べることができる</code> in writing, both of which can only mean "can eat".

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · the potential form vs ～ことができる

> **Rule of thumb** - identical meaning; the potential form is spoken Japanese, `～ことができる` is written and institutional Japanese.

ここから<ruby>富士山<rt>ふじさん</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>られます**。<br>*You can see Mt Fuji from here.*

ここから<ruby>富士山<rt>ふじさん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ることができます**。<br>*Mt Fuji can be viewed from here. (a sign, a guidebook)*

Note the particle again: the potential form pulls the object to `が`, while `～ことができる` leaves the inner verb intact with its `を`.

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · 見られる vs 見える

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>られる</code> is *able to see* - you have the chance, the permission, the ticket. <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える</code> is *is visible* - the image simply arrives at your eyes.

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>から<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える**。<br>*You can see the sea from the window. (it is simply there)*

チケットがあれば、リハーサルも**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>られる**。<br>*If you have a ticket, you can watch the rehearsal too. (access)*

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · 見られる vs 見える (cont.)

The same split runs through <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえる</code> (a sound reaches you) and <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>ける</code> (you get the opportunity to listen). Because <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える</code> and <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえる</code> already carry the meaning, they have no potential form of their own: ❌<code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えられる</code>.

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せられる → ✅ <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>**せる**<br>*`られる` is only for る-verbs and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る. An う-verb changes its own ending and stops there.*

- ❌ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けれる／<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めれる → ✅ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>**ける**／<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>**める**<br>*This is れ<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>す<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby> - an inserted `れ`. Unlike ら<ruby>抜<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>き it is not accepted in any register.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>られますか。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られますか**。<br>*The potential stem of <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る is こ. `き` is the ます-stem (<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます), and it does not belong here.*

- ❌ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>がわかれます。 → ✅ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>が**わかります**。<br>*`わかる` already means "is understandable to me". It has no potential form.*

---

## ～られる - potential, "can do" · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ レポートを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べれます（in an essay） → ✅ …<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>**べられます**<br>*ら<ruby>抜<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>き is fine among friends and wrong in writing. In anything marked or submitted, keep the `ら`.*

# ～られる - passive, "it was done to me"

**Meaning** · something is done to the subject - often, in Japanese, something the subject would rather have avoided

The passive rebuilds a sentence around the person or thing **on the receiving end**. English does this to move an object into the subject slot. Japanese does that too, but it also does something English cannot: it can build a passive on an *intransitive* verb, to say that an event happened and you were stuck with the consequences.

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Formation

The passive is built on the あ-stem - the same stem the ない-form uses - plus `れる`. る-verbs and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る instead take `られる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → あ-row kana, + れる | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かれる |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + られる | <ruby>褒<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>める → <ruby>褒<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>められる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by される | <ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>される |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | stem こ, + られる | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られる |

Every う-verb ending. The `～う` row is the trap - it goes to `わ`, not `あ`:

---

| Ending | Change | Example |

| ～く | く → か + れる | <ruby>叩<rt>たた</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>叩<rt>たた</rt></ruby>かれる |
| ～ぐ | ぐ → が + れる | <ruby>騒<rt>さわ</rt></ruby>ぐ → <ruby>騒<rt>さわ</rt></ruby>がれる |

| ～ぬ | ぬ → な + れる | <ruby>死<rt>し</rt></ruby>ぬ → <ruby>死<rt>し</rt></ruby>なれる |

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - a passive verb is always a **る-verb**: <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われる → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われない, <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われます, <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われて, <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた.

Three sentence shapes use it:

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plain passive | A は B に + passive | <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>褒<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>められた |

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Examples

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>叱<rt>しか</rt></ruby>られた**。<br>*I got told off by my mother.*

<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>噛<rt>か</rt></ruby>まれました**。<br>*I was bitten on the hand by a dog.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>月曜日<rt>げつようび</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>おこな</rt></ruby>われます**。<br>*The meeting will be held next Monday.*

<ruby>満員<rt>まんいん</rt></ruby><ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で<ruby>足<rt>あし</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>踏<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>まれた**。<br>*Someone stepped on my foot in the packed train.*

---

<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る<ruby>途中<rt>とちゅう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>られて**、<ruby>服<rt>ふく</rt></ruby>がびしょびしょになった。<br>*I got caught in the rain on the way home and my clothes were soaked.*

この<ruby>寺<rt>てら</rt></ruby>は<ruby>四百<rt>よんひゃく</rt></ruby><ruby>年<rt>ねん</rt></ruby><ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>建<rt>た</rt></ruby>てられた**そうだ。<br>*Apparently this temple was built four hundred years ago.*

<ruby>夜中<rt>よなか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>ちゃんに**<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>かれて**、ほとんど<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>れなかった。<br>*The baby cried all night on me and I barely slept.*

<ruby>発表<rt>はっぴょう</rt></ruby>のあと<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>たちにいろいろ**<ruby>質問<rt>しつもん</rt></ruby>されて**、<ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>えるのに<ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>った。<br>*After the talk the students fired all sorts of questions at me and I struggled to answer.*

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Nuance & register

The passive itself is register-neutral; politeness comes from `～ます` as usual. What is *not* neutral is how often you reach for it. English uses the passive freely to avoid naming an agent; Japanese usually solves that problem by simply leaving the subject out of an active sentence. Translating every English passive into a Japanese one produces stiff, faintly self-pitying prose.

**Marking the agent.** `に` is the default. Two alternatives matter:

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **によって** for creation, discovery, invention and other one-off acts, especially<br>*with an inanimate subject: この<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby>はピカソによって<ruby>描<rt>か</rt></ruby>かれた. Saying ピカソに<ruby>描<rt>か</rt></ruby>かれた makes the painting sound put-upon.*
- **から** with verbs of giving, telling and teaching, where the thing flows out of<br>*the agent: <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>から<ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>された.*

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Nuance & register (cont.)

**The affected subject.** A Japanese passive normally wants a *person* as its subject, because the form is fundamentally about who is affected. Inanimate subjects are fine when the sentence is a neutral report - <ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>行<rt>おこな</rt></ruby>われる, <ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>開<rt>ひら</rt></ruby>かれる, この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>はよく<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まれている - and that use is at home in newspapers and formal writing.

**られる as honorific.** The same form doubles as a light honorific: <ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>られました means "the president has already gone home", not that anyone went home on him. It is weaker than <code>お<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りになる</code> and very common in offices precisely because it is easy. Context disambiguates: an honorific `られる` has no `に`-marked agent and its subject is someone you are looking up to.

---

## ～られる - passive vs potential

> **Rule of thumb** - う-verbs keep them apart (<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まれる vs <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>める). る-verbs and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る do not - <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられる, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>られる and <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られる are one shape doing two jobs.

<ruby>夜中<rt>よなか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られて**、<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>られなかった。<br>*A friend turned up in the middle of the night and I couldn't sleep.*

That sentence contains both: <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られて</code> is passive (a friend did it *to* me, and it was a nuisance), <code><ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>られなかった</code> is potential (I lacked the ability). Nothing in the endings tells you apart - the particles do. A passive has an agent in `に`; a potential has an object in `が` and no agent.

---

## ～られる - passive vs potential (cont.)

Because the collision is real, speakers avoid it. In casual speech the ら<ruby>抜<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>き potential (<code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べれる</code>, <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>れる</code>, <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>れる</code>) is unambiguous, which is one honest reason it keeps spreading despite being nonstandard. In writing, `～ことができる` does the same job.

---

## the plain passive vs the suffering passive

> **Rule of thumb** - if the active sentence had an object, the passive is the ordinary kind. If it had **no object at all**, you are looking at the suffering passive, and its whole job is to add a victim who was not in the original sentence.

Start from an intransitive verb with nobody affected:

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>った**。<br>*It rained.*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>られた**。<br>*I got rained on. (and it ruined things for me)*

---

## the plain passive vs the suffering passive (cont.)

The second sentence has a silent <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は in front. Nothing was done *to* the rain; the passive exists purely to say that the rain's falling landed on me. The same machinery gives <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもに<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>かれる, <ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>たれる, <ruby>客<rt>きゃく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られる, and - for one of the heaviest sentences in the language - <ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>死<rt>し</rt></ruby>なれる, "my father died on me".

A closely related form takes a transitive verb but puts the *owner*, not the object, in the subject slot:

<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>盗<rt>ぬす</rt></ruby>まれた**。<br>*Someone stole my bicycle.*

---

## the plain passive vs the suffering passive (cont.)

Note that `を` survives: <ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby> is still the object of the stealing. The subject is me, because I am the one out a bicycle. Saying <ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>盗<rt>ぬす</rt></ruby>まれた is grammatical but sounds like a police report about a bicycle, not a complaint about my morning.

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · ～られる vs ～てもらう

> **Rule of thumb** - same event, opposite feelings. `～てもらう` says you are grateful; the passive typically says you are not.

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>宿題<rt>しゅくだい</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらった**。<br>*A friend helped me with my homework. (thank you)*

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>日記<rt>にっき</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まれた**。<br>*A friend read my diary. (thanks for nothing)*

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · ～られる vs ～てもらう (cont.)

Both take the other person in `に` and both make me the subject, so the only thing carrying the attitude is the choice of ending. Using the passive for something kind - ❌<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えられた for "my teacher taught me" - reads as a complaint; <code><ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えていただきました</code> is what you want.

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>あれる → ✅ <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>**われる**<br>*Verbs ending in `～う` go to `わ`, never `あ`. Same rule as the ない-form: <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わない.*

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>られた。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>られた。<br>*In a suffering passive the cause is the agent, and agents take `に`. The `が` slot belongs to the person who suffered.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>褒<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>められた。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>褒<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>められた。<br>*The praiser is the agent, marked `に`. `を` here would try to make the teacher the thing being praised.*

---

## ～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>小説<rt>しょうせつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby>な<ruby>作家<rt>さっか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かれた。 → ✅ …<ruby>作家<rt>さっか</rt></ruby>**によって**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かれた。<br>*With acts of creation and an inanimate subject, `によって` is the natural agent marker; plain `に` makes the novel sound like a victim.*

- ❌ <ruby>夜中<rt>よなか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>られた。 → ✅ …<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られた**。<br>*The passive stem of <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る is こ. `き` is the ます-stem and has no business in a passive.*

# ～させる - causative

**Meaning** · make someone do something, or let someone do something

One form, two meanings that feel like opposites in English. `～させる` says that the subject **caused** the action - and Japanese leaves it to the particles and the situation to say whether that causing was an order or a permission. Learning to read that difference is most of the work on this page.

---

## ～させる - causative · Formation

The causative is built on the あ-stem - the same stem as the ない-form and the passive - plus `せる`. る-verbs and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る take `させる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → あ-row kana, + せる | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かせる |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + させる | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べさせる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by させる | <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>させる |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | stem こ, + させる | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>させる |

Every う-verb ending. `～う` goes to `わ`, exactly as in the passive:

## ～させる - causative · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - passive `れる`, causative `せる`. The stem is identical; one consonant carries the whole difference. <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ま**れる** = it was read on me. <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ま**せる** = I made someone read it.

A causative verb is a る-verb: <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かせる → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かせない, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かせます, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かせて, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かせた.

**Particles.** This is where the meaning lives:

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>ばせて**くれた。<br>*My father let me choose whichever university I wanted.*

<ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>したので、<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ませる**ことにした。<br>*My daughter had a fever, so I decided to keep her home from school.*

あの<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>部下<rt>ぶか</rt></ruby>を<ruby>毎晩<rt>まいばん</rt></ruby><ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>くまで**<ruby>残業<rt>ざんぎょう</rt></ruby>させている**。<br>*That manager has his staff working overtime until late every night.*

<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>もしないで<ruby>三日<rt>みっか</rt></ruby>も<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>らないなんて、<ruby>家族<rt>かぞく</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>させる**にもほどがある。<br>*Not coming home for three days without a word - there's a limit to how much worry you can put your family through.*

---

## ～させる - causative · Nuance & register

The form is register-neutral, but the **social geometry** is not. A causative sentence says the subject has enough authority over the causee to bring the action about. That means it is fundamentally a downward or lateral form: a parent over a child, a teacher over a student, a manager over staff. Using it with a superior as the causee is not merely impolite, it is close to nonsense - ❌<ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>らせました claims you gave the president orders. What you want is <code><ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>っていただきました</code>.

The one place a causative points **upward** is when you turn it on yourself. `～させていただく` frames your own action as something the listener graciously permits, and it is the backbone of business humility: <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>させていただきます, お<ruby>先<rt>さき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>失礼<rt>しつれい</rt></ruby>させていただきます.

---

## ～させる - causative · Nuance & register (cont.)

**The short causative.** う-verbs have a contracted causative in `～す`: <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かす, <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ます, <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たす, やらす. It is casual, common in speech and especially associated with Kansai. It conjugates as an う-verb (<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かした, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かして). Keep `～せる` in writing and in polite speech.

**Emotion causatives** are so common they are worth learning as vocabulary: <ruby>喜<rt>よろこ</rt></ruby>ばせる, <ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>らせる, <ruby>驚<rt>おどろ</rt></ruby>かせる, <ruby>安心<rt>あんしん</rt></ruby>させる, がっかりさせる. They take the person in `を`, and they are the normal way to say "make someone feel X" - there is no separate construction.

## ～させる - causative · させる - "make" vs "let"

> **Rule of thumb** - nothing in the verb distinguishes them. Look at three things: the particle on the causee, whether the causee wanted it, and whether a `～てあげる`／`～てくれる` is attached.

With an **intransitive** verb the particle is a live choice, and it carries weight:

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>塾<rt>じゅく</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>通<rt>かよ</rt></ruby>わせている**。<br>*I have my son attending cram school. (my decision)*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きなだけ**<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>ばせている**。<br>*I let my son play as much as he likes. (his wish, I allow it)*

---

## ～させる - causative · させる - "make" vs "let" (cont.)

`を` presents the causee as something the subject moves around; `に` presents them as a person with their own will who is being given room. With a **transitive** verb the object has already claimed `を`, so the causee is stuck with `に` and the particle stops helping - <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>わせた is "made" or "let" depending entirely on whether <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby> wanted to.

The clearest signal in real Japanese is the benefactive tail. A causative with `～てくれる`, `～てあげる` or `～てやる` is always *let*, because those verbs mean the action was a favour:

<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>新人<rt>しんじん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>させてくれた**。<br>*The section chief let the new hire sit in on the meeting.*

---

## ～させる - causative · させる - "make" vs "let" (cont.)

Strip the `くれた` and <ruby>新人<rt>しんじん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>させた tilts straight back towards "made him attend".

---

## ～させる - causative · ～させる vs ～てもらう

> **Rule of thumb** - who is holding the power? `～させる` puts it with the subject; `～てもらう` puts it with the other person, whose cooperation you receive.

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>めさせた**。<br>*The manager had Yamada gather the materials.*

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>めてもらった**。<br>*I got Yamada to gather the materials (and I'm grateful).*

Both English translations can be "had someone do it", which is why the pair is confusing. The Japanese is not ambiguous at all: `させる` says you had the standing to require it; `てもらう` says they did you a favour. Since you almost never have standing over a customer or a senior, `～ていただく` is the only safe choice upwards.

---

## ～させる - causative · ～させる vs a lexical transitive verb

> **Rule of thumb** - if the language already owns a transitive verb for the idea, use it. A causative built on the intransitive twin sounds laboured, and sometimes means something different.

Japanese has hundreds of intransitive/transitive pairs, and the transitive member already contains "cause":

<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>七<rt>しち</rt></ruby><ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもを**<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>こす**。<br>*I wake the children at seven every morning.*

---

## ～させる - causative · ～させる vs a lexical transitive verb (cont.)

<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きさせる exists, but it means something narrower and stranger - forcing someone to get themselves up. The same holds for <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せる versus <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>させる: <ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せる is "show someone a photo", while <ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>させる is "make them look at it". Other pairs to reach for first: <ruby>着<rt>き</rt></ruby>せる (dress someone), <ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>める (stop something), <ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れる (put in), <ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>める (decide).

---

## ～させる - causative · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>あせる → ✅ <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>**わせる**<br>*Verbs in `～う` take `わ`, matching <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わない and <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>われる.*

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べらせる → ✅ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**させる**<br>*る-verbs add the whole of `させる`. Only う-verbs get the bare `せる`.*

- ❌ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**に**<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>かせた。 → ✅ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**を**<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>かせた。<br>*Nobody permits a child to cry. Verbs of emotion take the causee in `を`, because the "letting" reading is unavailable.*

- ❌ <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>作文<rt>さくぶん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かせる → ✅ <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>作文<rt>さくぶん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かせる<br>*One `を` per clause. The object has it, so the causee falls to `に`.*

# ～させられる - causative-passive

**Meaning** · to be made to do something you did not want to do

This is the causative with the passive stacked on top of it, and the meaning is exactly that sum: somebody made me do it, and I am the one it happened to. Almost every sentence built with `～させられる` carries an audible *and I hated it*.

---

## ～させられる - causative-passive · Formation

Build it in two steps, in this order. Get the causative first; the passive is then mechanical.

1. Make the **causative**: <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ませる. 2. Treat that as a る-verb and make it **passive**: drop `る`, add `られる` → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ませられる.

For う-verbs, step 2 then contracts: `～せられ` collapses to `～され`.

---

## ～させられる - causative-passive · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | あ-stem + せられる, normally contracted to あ-stem + される | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ませられる → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>まされる |
| Verb (う-verb ending in ～す) | あ-stem + せられる - **never contracts** | <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>させられる |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + させられる | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べさせられる |
| Verb (する) | replaced by させられる | <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>させられる |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | stem こ, + させられる | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>させられる |

## ～させられる - causative-passive · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the `～す` row is the one that catches people. Contracting it would give ❌<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>さされる, with two `さ` in a row, and Japanese refuses. Verbs in `～す` keep the full <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>させられる, <ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>させられる, <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>させられる.

る-verbs, する and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る never contract either: <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べさせられる, させられる, <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>させられる are the only forms.

The sentence shape is the passive's, with the person who gave the order in `に`:

<code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>に <ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べさせられた。</code>

## ～させられる - causative-passive · Examples

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものころ、<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>に<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べさせられた**。<br>*As a child I was made to eat my vegetables by my mother.*

<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かされる**。<br>*We get made to write kanji every day.*

<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み<ruby>会<rt>かい</rt></ruby>でカラオケを**<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>わされる**のが<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>だ。<br>*I hate being made to sing karaoke at drinking parties.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>意見<rt>いけん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わされて**、<ruby>頭<rt>あたま</rt></ruby>が<ruby>真<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>白<rt>しろ</rt></ruby>になった。<br>*I was suddenly put on the spot for an opinion in the meeting and my mind went blank.*

<ruby>戦争<rt>せんそう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>扱<rt>あつか</rt></ruby>ったあの<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>には、いろいろ**<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えさせられた**。<br>*That film about the war really gave me a lot to think about.*

---

## ～させられる - causative-passive · Nuance & register

The default colour of `～させられる` is **grievance**. Even without any explicit complaint attached, the form implies the speaker had no say. That makes it the natural way to describe unpaid overtime, childhood piano lessons, drinking-party rituals and school rules, and an odd way to describe anything you enjoyed. If the action was welcome, use `～させてもらう` or `～させていただく` instead: <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>をやらせてもらっている.

**Contracted or full?** For う-verbs, the contracted `～される` is what people actually say: <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たされる, <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>まされる, <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わされる. The full `～せられる` is not wrong, but it sounds heavy and slightly literary, and outside the `～す` verbs you will rarely hear it in conversation.

---

## ～させられる - causative-passive · Nuance & register (cont.)

**The one warm use.** A handful of verbs of thought and feeling take the causative-passive to mean "it moved me", with no unwillingness at all - <ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えさせられる, <ruby>驚<rt>おどろ</rt></ruby>かされる, <ruby>感動<rt>かんどう</rt></ruby>させられる. Here the cause is usually a thing rather than a person, and the `に` marks that thing: その<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>に<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えさせられた. This is common in reviews and essays.

**Register.** The form is neutral and works at any politeness level - <ruby>残業<rt>ざんぎょう</rt></ruby>させられました is perfectly polite. What it is not is *neutral in content*: describing your boss's instructions as `させられました` in front of that boss is a small act of protest, and will be heard as one.

---

## ～させられる - causative-passive · ～させられる vs ～させる

> **Rule of thumb** - same event seen from opposite ends. `～させる` puts the person with the power in the subject slot; `～させられる` puts the person who obeyed there.

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>教室<rt>きょうしつ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けさせた**。<br>*The teacher made me tidy the classroom.*

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>教室<rt>きょうしつ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けさせられた**。<br>*I was made to tidy the classroom by the teacher.*

The facts are identical; the sympathy is not. The second sentence is the one you say to a friend afterwards. Note the particle swap: the teacher moves from subject to `に`-marked agent, exactly as in an ordinary passive.

---

## ～させられる vs the plain passive ～られる

> **Rule of thumb** - in the plain passive somebody did the action **to** you; in the causative-passive **you** did the action, under orders.

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>作文<rt>さくぶん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まれた**。<br>*The teacher read my essay. (the teacher's eyes, my writing)*

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>作文<rt>さくぶん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まされた**。<br>*The teacher made me read my essay out. (my mouth, under duress)*

The two differ by a single `さ`, and they attribute the action to different people. Check who physically performs the verb: if it is you, the sentence needs the extra causative layer.

---

## ～させられる - causative-passive · ～させられる vs ～なければならない

> **Rule of thumb** - `～させられる` needs a **person** applying pressure. `～なければならない` is obligation with no named source - rules, circumstance, common sense.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までにこの<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>**さなければならない**。<br>*I have to submit this document by tomorrow.*

<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までにこの<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>させられる**。<br>*The section chief is making me submit this document by tomorrow.*

The first is a statement about a deadline; the second is a complaint about a man. Because `～させられる` points at a person, it is far more loaded - using it where plain obligation was meant sounds like you are picking a fight.

---

## ～させられる - causative-passive · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>さされる → ✅ <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>**させられる**<br>*Verbs ending in `～す` do not contract. The same goes for <ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>させられる and <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>させられる.*

- ❌ <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まさせられる → ✅ <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>**ませられる**／<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>**まされる**<br>*This is さ<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れ<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby> - an extra `さ` in an う-verb. Either the full form or the contraction, never a hybrid of the two.*

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べさされる → ✅ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**させられる**<br>*る-verbs never contract. Only う-verbs can lose the `せら`.*

- ❌ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べさせられた。 → ✅ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べさせられた。<br>*The person giving the order is an agent, and agents in a passive take `に`.*

## ～させられる - causative-passive · Common mistakes (cont.)

# ～させてください

**Meaning** · please let me do it - asking permission by asking the other person to cause it

Literally this says "please cause me to do X". You are not asking whether you may; you are asking the listener to grant it, which puts the decision squarely on their side of the table. That framing is why `～させてください` is so much more deferential than a flat "is it okay if I…".

---

## ～させてください · Nuance & register

`～させてください` is polite, but it is not humble in itself; it is a direct request with `ください` on the end. In business the humble branch of the ladder does most of the work:

---

## ～させてください · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **～させていただきます** announces what you are about to do while crediting the<br>*other side with permitting it: <ruby>担当<rt>たんとう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>が <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>させていただきます. It is everywhere in Japanese business and broadcasting, and it is also the form that gets criticised for overuse - used where nobody is granting anything (<ruby>開店<rt>かいてん</rt></ruby>させていただきます on a shop notice), it sounds hollow.*
- **～させていただけませんか** is a genuine question and the safest way to ask a<br>*superior for something inconvenient.*
- **～させていただきたいのですが** trails off deliberately, leaving the listener to<br>*offer. Very common, very soft.*

---

## ～させてください · Nuance & register (cont.)

Several fixed phrases live here and are worth memorising whole: お<ruby>先<rt>さき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>失礼<rt>しつれい</rt></ruby>させていただきます (leaving before your colleagues), <ruby>遠慮<rt>えんりょ</rt></ruby>させていただきます (a polite refusal - literally "let me hold back"), <ruby>検討<rt>けんとう</rt></ruby>させていただきます (we'll consider it, which in practice often means no).

In casual speech the bare て-form is the everyday version: ちょっと<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>させて, <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わせて. Among friends `～させてよ` and `～させてくれ` also occur, the latter distinctly masculine and blunt.

One structural point: because the *speaker* is the one performing the action, `～させてください` almost always has an unstated <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に. Adding it explicitly (<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>にやらせてください) is a way of pushing yourself forward - volunteering, or claiming the job over someone else.

---

## ～させてください · ～させてください vs ～てください

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てください` asks **you** to do it. `～させてください` asks you to let **me** do it. The `させ` flips who acts.

This is the highest-frequency error at N4, because English "please help" hides the distinction entirely.

<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>っ**てください**。<br>*Please help me. (you do the helping)*

<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>わ**せてください**。<br>*Please let me help. (I do the helping)*

---

## ～させてください · ～させてください vs ～てください (cont.)

Both are polite, and they are nearly opposite offers. Before you say either one, check whose hands are going to move.

---

## ～させてください · ～させてください vs ～てもいいですか

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てもいいですか` asks whether a rule allows it. `～させてください` asks a person to allow it. The second is warmer and puts you lower.

この<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>っ**てもいいですか**。<br>*Is it okay if I sit here? (checking, neutral)*

その<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>担当<rt>たんとう</rt></ruby>させてください**。<br>*Please put me on that job. (asking to be granted it)*

---

## ～させてください · ～させてください vs ～てもいいですか (cont.)

Use `～てもいいですか` for small, impersonal permissions - a seat, a window, a photograph. Use `～させてください` when the other person genuinely holds the authority, and when you want to sound like you are asking a favour rather than checking the rules. Asking a manager ❌<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んでもいいですか is not rude exactly, but <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ませていただけませんか is what a careful employee says.

---

## ～させてください · ～させてください vs ～させていただきます

> **Rule of thumb** - `～させてください` **asks**. `～させていただきます` **announces**, having already assumed the permission.

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に、<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>配<rt>くば</rt></ruby>らせてください**。<br>*Please let me hand out the materials before the meeting.*

それでは、<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>配<rt>くば</rt></ruby>らせていただきます**。<br>*Right then - allow me to hand out the materials.*

The first is still open; the second is what you say as you start moving. Because `いただきます` is a statement, using it for something the listener has not agreed to can come across as presumptuous - that is the real risk with this very useful phrase.

---

## ～させてください · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってください（meaning "let me help"） → ✅ <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>**わせてください**<br>*Without `させ` you are asking the other person to do the work.*

- ❌ <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わさせてください → ✅ <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>**わせてください**<br>*This is さ<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れ<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>. う-verbs take a bare `せる`, so the causative て-form is <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わせて - no extra `さ`.*

- ❌ <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>らさせていただきます → ✅ <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>**らせていただきます**<br>*Same error, in the phrase where it is heard most often. <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る is an う-verb.*

- ❌ <ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えらせてください → ✅ <ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>え**させてください**<br>*<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>える is a る-verb, so it takes the full `させる`, not `せる`.*

---

## ～させてください · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>させてください → ✅ **<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>させてください**<br>*The causative stem of <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る is こ.*

# 命令形 - the plain imperative

**Meaning** · a bare order - "do it" - with nothing softening it

The <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> is the shortest possible way to tell someone to act. It has no politeness of any kind built in, which makes it rude between adults and exactly right in three places: emergencies, signs, and quoting an order that someone else gave.

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Formation

う-verbs shift their final kana one row; る-verbs take `ろ`; the irregulars must be learnt.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final う-row kana → え-row kana | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>け |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + ろ (spoken) or + よ (written) | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べろ／<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べよ |
| Verb (する) | しろ (spoken) or せよ (written) | <ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>しろ |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>い - nothing is added, the whole word changes | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>い |

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Formation (cont.)

> **A word about the ぬ row** - <ruby>死<rt>し</rt></ruby>ぬ is used here because it is essentially the only ぬ-verb in ordinary modern Japanese, so every conjugation table in this book falls back on it. Be aware that its imperative, **<ruby>死<rt>し</rt></ruby>ね**, is a serious insult - roughly "drop dead" - and is genuinely shocking to say. Learn the conjugation; never use the word.

> **Note** - a handful of verbs break the pattern. `くれる` gives **くれ**, not ❌くれろ. The four honorific う-verbs take `～い` instead of `～え`: いらっしゃる → いらっしゃい, なさる → なさい, くださる → ください, おっしゃる → おっしゃい. Those endings are the reason `ください` and `なさい` look the way they do.

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - for う-verbs the imperative is the potential stem without the `る`: <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>**け**る is "can write", <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>**け** is "write!". One syllable apart, and worlds apart in tone.

The negative counterpart is dictionary form + `な`: <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くな, "don't go".

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るぞ、<ruby>白<rt>しろ</rt></ruby>い<ruby>線<rt>せん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>内側<rt>うちがわ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>下<rt>さ</rt></ruby>がれ**。<br>*Train coming - get back behind the white line.*

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Nuance & register

Treat the <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> as a form you must **recognise** rather than one you use. Pointed at a real person it is harsh - it is what you hear in an argument, from an angry parent, or from a drill instructor. Most Japanese adults go weeks without saying one to another adult.

Where it does live:

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **Emergencies.** Politeness costs syllables, and syllables cost lives:<br>*<ruby>逃<rt>に</rt></ruby>げろ, <ruby>伏<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>せろ, <ruby>離<rt>はな</rt></ruby>れろ. Nobody hears these as rude.*
- **Signs and instructions.** <ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まれ, <ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>せ, <ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>け. Impersonal<br>*and addressed to no one in particular. Exam papers use the `よ` variant: <ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>えよ, <ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>べ, <ruby>述<rt>の</rt></ruby>べよ.*
- **Cheering.** がんばれ, <ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>て, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>け - a stadium is exempt.
- **Sport, the military, fiction.** Coaches to players, seniors to juniors in a<br>*club, and a very large share of what characters shout in manga and anime.*
- **Quoting.** Reporting an order almost always keeps the imperative inside the<br>*quote: 「<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>て」と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた.*

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Register colouring.** The plain imperative is strongly male-associated in conversation. Women use it in emergencies, in anger and in cheering, but a woman telling a friend <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べろ would be marked. The gentler downward orders - `～なさい` and the bare て-form - are unmarked for either speaker.

`ろ` versus `よ`: `ろ` is spoken, `よ` is written and formal. <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ろ in speech, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>よ in a slogan or on a test. For する the split is しろ／せよ, and せよ has a distinctly stiff, official ring.

Adding a particle does not rescue it. <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>くしろ**よ** is nagging; <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>い**よ** is a blunt invitation between close male friends. If you want to be gentle, change the form, not the ending.

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · 命令形 vs ～なさい

> **Rule of thumb** - both are orders given downward, but `～なさい` comes with authority and care, while the <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> comes with force. A parent uses `～なさい`; the same parent uses the <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> when they have run out of patience.

<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>なさい**。<br>*Go to bed now. (parent, firm but calm)*

<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ろ**。<br>*Get to bed. (sharp, angry, or male-casual)*

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · 命令形 vs ～なさい (cont.)

`～なさい` is also the only one of the two that appears in neutral written instructions to a *person* - school worksheets, cookery books for children. The <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> in writing is reserved for signage and for the terse `よ` form on exams.

---

## 命令形 vs ～てください and the bare ～て

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てください` is a request and can go upward or sideways. The bare て-form is a casual request between equals. The <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> is neither - it does not request anything.

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めてください**。<br>*Please close the window.*

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>、**<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めて**。<br>*Shut the window, would you.*

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めろ**。<br>*Shut that window.*

---

## 命令形 vs ～てください and the bare ～て (cont.)

The bare て-form is the one to reach for in casual speech. It is friendly, it is used by everyone, and it is what learners usually *mean* when they produce a <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> by accident.

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · 命令形 vs ～な - the negative order

> **Rule of thumb** - the positive order changes the verb's ending; the negative order does not. Dictionary form + `な` is "don't", and it is just as blunt.

そこに**<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>れ**。<br>*Get in there.*

そこに**<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>るな**。<br>*Don't go in there.*

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · 命令形 vs ～な - the negative order (cont.)

These two are a matched pair in tone - signs use both (<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まれ, <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>るな) and so do angry people. Beware the collision with the *softened* command written the same way: ます-stem + `な` is a gentle order contracted from `～なさい`. <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる**な** means "don't eat"; <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**な** means "eat up". The difference is a single `る`.

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ろ／<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ろ → ✅ **<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>い**<br>*<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る has no `ろ` imperative at all; the whole word is replaced.*

- ❌ するろ／しれ → ✅ **しろ**（written: **せよ**）<br>*する is irregular here too. Its imperative is not built from the dictionary form.*

- ❌ くれろ → ✅ **くれ**<br>*`くれる` looks like an ordinary る-verb and is not. Compare <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せる → <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せろ, which is regular.*

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べれ → ✅ **<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べろ**<br>*Applying the う-verb rule to a る-verb produces <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べれ, which is not a word. る-verbs take `ろ`.*

---

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>、これを<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>め。 → ✅ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>、これを**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでください**。<br>*The conjugation is fine; the sentence is not. Never aim a <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> at anyone you owe respect to.*

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>にサイン**していただけますか**。

{reveal}
`しろ` is the correct imperative of する, so the grammar is not the problem - the target is. A <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> aimed at a superior is about as bad as Japanese gets. Upwards, a request has to be a request.

## 命令形 - the plain imperative · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにしろ。 (b) <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにしなさい。

{reveal}
(a) is raw force. It is what someone says when they have lost their temper, or a line in a film; from a stranger it would start a fight, and in conversation it is strongly male-coded.

{reveal}
(b) still comes from above - you could not say it to your boss - but it carries the tone of a person with legitimate authority correcting someone in their care. A teacher says it to a noisy class without any anger at all. Both are downward; only (a) is aggressive.

# ～なさい

**Meaning** · a firm instruction given downward - "do it", said with authority rather than anger

`～なさい` is the imperative of `なさる`, the honorific verb for "do". The politeness in its ancestry has drained away, and what is left is a command that is softer in tone than the bare <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> but strictly one-directional: parent to child, teacher to student, exam paper to candidate. You cannot aim it upwards, and pointing it at an adult equal is condescending.

---

## ～なさい · Formation

Attach `なさい` to the **ます-stem**. If you can build <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きます, you can build <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きなさい.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem (final う-row kana → い-row kana) + なさい | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きなさい |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + なさい | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べなさい |
| Verb (する) | し + なさい | <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しなさい |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | stem き, + なさい | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>なさい |

Every う-verb ending:

## ～なさい · Nuance & register

The politeness of `～なさい` is a **historical** fact, not a usable one. Because it descends from the honorific `なさる`, it sounds educated and controlled rather than crude - but its social direction is fixed downward. A teacher, a parent, an older sibling or a coach can use it. To a colleague it is patronising; to a customer or a superior it is unthinkable.

Three settings where it is completely unmarked:

---

## ～なさい · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **Adults to children.** This is its home. <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きなさい,<br>*ちゃんと<ruby>挨拶<rt>あいさつ</rt></ruby>しなさい.*
- **Written instructions to a reader.** Textbook exercises and exam papers use it<br>*constantly: <ruby>正<rt>ただ</rt></ruby>しいものを<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>びなさい, <ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きなさい. Here it addresses nobody in particular and carries no attitude.*
- **Set expressions.** お<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みなさい and お<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りなさい are frozen<br>*`～なさい` forms that stopped being commands centuries ago. Nobody hears them as orders.*

---

## ～なさい · Nuance & register (cont.)

**The contracted `～な`.** Dropping `さい` gives a much warmer, more casual order: <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べな, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きな, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>な, もう<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りな. It is friendly - closer to English "go on, eat up" - and is common from older to younger and among close friends. It leans masculine in some regions but is by no means exclusively so. Never use it in writing.

**There is no negative.** ❌<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べないなさい does not exist. To forbid something at the same level of authority you switch construction: <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてはいけません, <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べないようにしなさい, or - much blunter - the prohibitive <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるな.

---

## ～なさい · Nuance & register (cont.)

**お～なさい** softens it a little further: お<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>りなさい, お<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べなさい. This sounds distinctly old-fashioned and grandmotherly in modern Japanese, and you will meet it more in fiction than in life.

---

## ～なさい · ～なさい vs 命令形

> **Rule of thumb** - same direction, different heat. `～なさい` is the order of someone with legitimate authority; the <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> is the order of someone applying force.

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けなさい**。<br>*Tidy your room.*

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けろ**。<br>*Tidy your damn room.*

---

## ～なさい · ～なさい vs 命令形 (cont.)

A parent says the first every week and the second when they have snapped. There is also a difference in who says them: the <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby> is strongly male-coded in conversation, while `～なさい` is used freely by everyone.

Note too that only `～なさい` is at home in print. A worksheet says <ruby>計算<rt>けいさん</rt></ruby>しなさい; a road sign says <ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まれ. Neither would trade places.

---

## ～なさい · ～なさい vs ～てください

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てください` **asks**, and can point in any direction. `～なさい` **tells**, and only points down.

すみません、こちらに**<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>ってください**。<br>*Please have a seat over here.*

そこに**<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>りなさい**。<br>*Sit down there. (teacher to pupil)*

---

## ～なさい · ～なさい vs ～てください (cont.)

Learners often assume that because `～なさい` looks longer and comes from an honorific verb, it must be politer than `～てください`. It is not. `～てください` is the neutral all-purpose form; `～なさい` is what you use precisely when you are *not* asking. Said to a guest, <ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>りなさい would be startling - お<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>りください is the polite version.

---

## ～なさい · 食べな vs 食べるな

> **Rule of thumb** - the same `な` after two different stems means opposite things. **ます-stem + な** = do it. **Dictionary form + な** = don't do it.

<ruby>冷<rt>さ</rt></ruby>めないうちに**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べな**。<br>*Go on, eat it while it's hot.*

まだ**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるな**。<br>*Don't eat it yet.*

---

## ～なさい · 食べな vs 食べるな (cont.)

The only visible difference is the `る`. The first is `～なさい` with its tail bitten off and is friendly; the second is the prohibitive and is as blunt as the <ruby>命令形<rt>めいれいけい</rt></ruby>. Intonation helps in speech - the prohibitive `な` is clipped and stressed - but the stem is the reliable signal.

---

## ～なさい · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なさい → ✅ **<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>なさい**<br>*`なさい` attaches to the ます-stem, and the ます-stem of <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る is <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>. The `こ` stem belongs to <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>い, <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られる and <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>させる.*

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるなさい → ✅ **<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べなさい**<br>*Never attach `なさい` to the dictionary form. <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ + なさい.*

- ❌ するなさい → ✅ **しなさい**<br>*The ます-stem of する is し.*

- ❌ <ruby>廊下<rt>ろうか</rt></ruby>を<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>らないなさい。 → ✅ <ruby>廊下<rt>ろうか</rt></ruby>を<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っ**てはいけません**／<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ら**ないようにしなさい**。<br>*`～なさい` has no negative form. You have to change construction.*

---

## ～なさい · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、ここに<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きなさい。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、ここにお<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を**お<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きください**。<br>*Correctly formed and socially impossible. `～なさい` only ever points downward.*

# ～ように言う

**Meaning** · tell someone to do something - reporting an instruction without quoting it

<code>～ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う</code> passes on the **content** of an order or request. The original words are gone: you cannot tell from the report whether the speaker barked <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>れ or murmured お<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りいただけますか. That flattening is the whole point of the pattern, and it is why learners meet it most often in the passive - <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた, "I was told to".

---

## ～ように言う · Formation

`ように` takes the **plain form** of the verb, positive or negative. Nothing changes about the verb itself, which is why the four verb classes all behave the same way here.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + ように + <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + ように + <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う | <ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>ける → <ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けるように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う |
| Verb (する) | dictionary form + ように + <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う | <ruby>準備<rt>じゅんび</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>準備<rt>じゅんび</rt></ruby>するように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | dictionary form + ように + <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う |
| Negative order | ない-form + ように + <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う | <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる → <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れないように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う |
| な-adjective / noun | ～にする + ように | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにするように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う |
| Asking a favour | ～てくれる／～てもらう + ように | <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれるように<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>む |

## ～ように言う · Formation (cont.)

The verb at the end is not restricted to <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う</code>:

| Verb | Sense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う | tell | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う |
| <ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>える | pass on a message | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>える |
| <ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>む | ask a favour | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>む |

## ～ように言う · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the person receiving the instruction is marked with `に`, the same as the listener of any speech act: <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けるように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った. In the passive that person becomes the subject and the speaker moves into `に`: <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた.

---

## ～ように言う · Examples

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かに**するように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**。<br>*The teacher told the students to be quiet.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に、<ruby>金曜日<rt>きんようび</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>報告書<rt>ほうこくしょ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>すように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われている**。<br>*The manager has told me to hand in the report by Friday.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けるように**<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby>も**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**のに、まだそのままだ。<br>*I've told my little brother to tidy his room over and over, and it's still exactly as it was.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんに、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わったことをみんなに**<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えるように<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>んだ**。<br>*I asked Tanaka to let everyone know that the meeting time had changed.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもたちには、<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>について**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないようにと<ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>して**あります。<br>*We've warned the children not to go off with strangers.*

---

## ～ように言う · Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral. It appears in family conversation and in business email alike, and any politeness sits on the final verb - <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いました, <ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えていただけますか, お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いしました.

**The lost politeness.** Because `ように` reports content rather than words, the report is silent about the tone of the original. If that matters, you say so outside the pattern: <ruby>強<rt>つよ</rt></ruby>い<ruby>口調<rt>くちょう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた, <ruby>丁寧<rt>ていねい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>まれた. Alternatively, quote directly with `と`.

**ようにと.** Adding `と` - <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るように**と**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた - is very common and slightly more formal or written. It changes nothing semantically; think of it as the quotative `と` reasserting itself.

---

## ～ように言う · Nuance & register (cont.)

**The passive is the everyday form.** In real speech you will hear <code>～ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた</code> far more than the active. Reporting what you were told to do is simply a more frequent thing to say than reporting what you told someone else.

**Business softening.** Upward, <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う</code> is too blunt. Use <code>ようにお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします</code> or <code>ようにお<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えください</code> - <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>折<rt>お</rt></ruby>り<ruby>返<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>をくださるように お<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えください is the sort of sentence this pattern was made for.

---

## ～ように言う · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Dangling ように.** A written instruction sometimes stops after `ように`, with the verb left off entirely: <ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>にお<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>しくださいますように. This is formal and a bit old-fashioned, but you will see it on notices.

---

## ～ように言う · ～ように言う vs ～と言う

> **Rule of thumb** - `と` quotes the **words**; `ように` reports the **instruction**. If you can put quotation marks round it, use `と`.

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は「<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ろ」**と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**。<br>*My father said, "Get to bed."*

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>る**ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**。<br>*My father told me to go to bed early.*

---

## ～ように言う · ～ように言う vs ～と言う (cont.)

The first preserves the imperative and with it the temper. The second could have been said in any tone at all. Note what happens to the verb: inside a `と` quote you keep whatever form was actually uttered (<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ろ, <ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>なさい, <ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>てください); with `ように` the verb goes into the plain dictionary form no matter what.

`と` can also report a request in indirect speech - <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った and 「<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>なさい」と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った are both natural - but ❌「<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>なさい」ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った mixes the two and is simply ungrammatical.

---

## ～ように言う · ～ように頼む vs ～てくださいと頼む

> **Rule of thumb** - same message, different packaging. `～てくださいと` reproduces the polite words that were used; `～ように` summarises what was asked for.

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>空港<rt>くうこう</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>っ**てくれるように<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>んだ**。<br>*I asked my friend to drive me to the airport.*

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに「<ruby>空港<rt>くうこう</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>ってください」**と<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>んだ**。<br>*I asked my friend, "Please take me to the airport."*

---

## ～ように言う · ～ように頼む vs ～てくださいと頼む (cont.)

When the request is a favour rather than an order, the `ように` version usually keeps a benefactive verb - `～てくれるように`, `～てもらえるように` - because without it the sentence sounds like an instruction rather than a request. Compare bare <ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>んだ, which reads oddly high-handed for something a friend is doing for you.

---

## ～ように言う · ～ように言う vs ～ようにする・～ようになる

> **Rule of thumb** - same `ように`, three unrelated jobs. <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う</code> reports an instruction, `する` describes an effort you make, `なる` describes a change that happened.

<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く**ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた**。<br>*The doctor told me to walk every day.*

<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く**ようにしている**。<br>*I make a point of walking every day.*

<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く**ようになった**。<br>*I've got into the habit of walking every day.*

---

## ～ように言う · ～ように言う vs ～ようにする・～ようになる (cont.)

Learners meet all three within a few weeks of each other and fold them together. Keep the final verb in view: it, not `ように`, is carrying the meaning.

---

## ～ように言う · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>は「<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りなさい」ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った。 → ✅ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**。<br>*`ように` never follows a quotation. Either quote with `と` or summarise with `ように` - not both.*

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → ✅ **<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う**<br>*`ように` takes the plain form, not the て-form.*

- ❌ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れないで**ように**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → ✅ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れ**ないように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う**<br>*The negative slot wants the plain ない-form. `～ないで` belongs with `～ないでください`, a different pattern.*

- ❌ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>発表<rt>はっぴょう</rt></ruby>するように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った。 → ✅ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**に**…<br>*The person told is the addressee of a speech act, so `に`.*

---

## ～ように言う · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**ように**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**にするように**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った<br>*`ように` needs a verb in front of it. A な-adjective has to be turned into one with `～にする`.*

## ～ように言う · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に、ここで<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>____ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた。

{reveal}
ここで<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>**つ**ように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた。

{reveal}
`ように` takes the plain dictionary form, so <code><ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ</code> goes in unchanged. The receptionist is the source of the instruction and takes `に` in the passive.

## ～ように言う · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは「<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てください」（と・ように）<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いました。

{reveal}
「<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てください」**と**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いました。

{reveal}
Once the words are inside quotation marks, only the quotative `と` can follow. The `ように` version would drop the quotes and the politeness: <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いました.

## ～ように言う · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The doctor told me not to eat anything before the examination."

{reveal}
<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>に、<ruby>検査<rt>けんさ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>も**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べないように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた**。

{reveal}
A negative instruction goes in as the plain ない-form, <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べない</code>, directly before `ように`. The passive <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた is the natural way to report an instruction you received.

## ～ように言う · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>をコピーしてように<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>まれました。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を**コピーするように**<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>まれました。

{reveal}
The て-form cannot sit in front of `ように`; the slot takes the plain form, here `コピーする`. If you wanted to keep the て-form you would have to quote: 「コピーしてください」と<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>まれました.

## ～ように言う · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>日記<rt>にっき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>くように<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った。 (b) <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>は「<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>日記<rt>にっき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きなさい」と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った。

{reveal}
(a) reports only the substance of the instruction. It tells you what the students now have to do and says nothing about how the teacher put it - she may have asked politely or issued an order.

{reveal}
(b) reproduces the teacher's actual words, and those words carry information: the `なさい` marks her as speaking downward with authority, calmly rather than angrily. Direct quotation is how Japanese keeps that texture, since `ように` deliberately throws it away.
