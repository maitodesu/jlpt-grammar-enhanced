# ～てあげる

**Meaning** · do something *for* someone - the action itself is the gift, and it travels away from the speaker

At N5 you learned that `あげる` hands an **object** to someone outside your circle. `～てあげる` hands over an **action** instead. The direction rule is identical; what is new is that naming an act as a favour puts it on the record, and that can be generous or it can be smug, depending on who you say it to.

---

## ～てあげる · Formation

Attach `あげる` to the て-form. `あげる` then conjugates as an ordinary る-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + あげる | <ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>ってあげる |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + あげます | <ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してあげました |
| Verb (offering) | て-form + あげよう／あげましょうか | <ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>ぶ → <ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>んであげましょうか |
| To someone above you (humble) | て-form + さしあげる | <ruby>案内<rt>あんない</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>案内<rt>あんない</rt></ruby>してさしあげる |
| To someone below you / an animal | て-form + やる | <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってやる |

The particle on the person who benefits is **not** decided by `あげる`. It is decided by the verb underneath, exactly as it would be without `あげる` attached:

---

## ～てあげる · Formation (cont.)

| Underlying verb frame | Particle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb already takes <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に | に | <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**に**<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてあげる |
| Verb already takes <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を | を | <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**を**<ruby>迎<rt>むか</rt></ruby>えに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってあげる |
| Verb acts on the person's possession | の | <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってあげる |

> **Rule of thumb** - the benefit must land **outside** the speaker. There is no ❌<code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてあげる</code>. If the favour comes toward you or your family, the verb you need is <code><ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくれる</code>.

---

## ～てあげる · Examples

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んであげた**。<br>*I read my little sister a book.*

<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>そうだったので、**<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってあげました**。<br>*The bags looked heavy, so I carried them for her.*

お<ruby>母<rt>かあ</rt></ruby>さんに**<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>してあげて**。<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>してるよ。<br>*Give your mum a call. She's worried.*

<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からないと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うので、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで**<ruby>案内<rt>あんない</rt></ruby>してあげた**。<br>*He said he didn't know the way, so I walked him to the station.*

---

## ～てあげる · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>してあげたら**、とても<ruby>喜<rt>よろこ</rt></ruby>んだ。<br>*When I fixed my son's bike for him, he was delighted.*

<ruby>留学生<rt>りゅうがくせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてあげる**<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>をしました。<br>*I promised to teach the exchange student how to read kanji.*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ってきたから、<ruby>隣<rt>となり</rt></ruby>のおばあさんの<ruby>洗濯物<rt>せんたくもの</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>り<ruby>込<rt>こ</rt></ruby>んであげました**。<br>*It started raining, so I brought in the laundry for the old lady next door.*

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えさせたほうがいいので、<ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>えは**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてあげません**でした。<br>*It's better to make him think for himself, so I didn't give him the answer.*

---

## ～てあげる · Nuance & register

**`～てあげる` announces the favour.** That is its entire function, and it is why the pattern needs handling. <code><ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ちましょうか</code> is a plain offer to carry a bag. <code><ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってあげましょうか</code> adds "…as a kindness to you", and the person hearing it now has a debt on the table they did not ask for. Between friends this is warm. Aimed at a stranger, a customer or anyone senior, it lands as condescending.

The safe habit: **do not say `～てあげる` to the person receiving the favour.** Say it *about* the favour afterwards, or to a third party.

---

## ～てあげる · Nuance & register (cont.)

- To the person: <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>いましょうか。／お<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>いしましょうか。
- About it later: <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんの<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>しを<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってあげた。

`さしあげる` does **not** rescue the situation. It is grammatically the humble form, but announcing to a superior that you have bestowed something on them is worse, not better - the humility is in the word and the presumption is in the construction. Real Japanese reaches for the honorific-humble frame instead: お<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ちします, ご<ruby>案内<rt>あんない</rt></ruby>します, お<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>しします. Keep `さしあげる` for narrating in the third person （<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>にお<ruby>土産<rt>みやげ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってさしあげた）and even there it is slightly stiff.

---

## ～てあげる · Nuance & register (cont.)

`～てやる` is the downward member of the family - for animals, plants, small children, one's own younger siblings - and it is blunter and more male-marked.

There is a second, sharper `～てあげる`: said with a flat tone about something the listener does not want, it turns sarcastic or threatening. <ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってあげようか - "shall I *do you the favour* of telling you the truth?" The mechanism is the same: you are insisting that this is a kindness when both of you know it is not.

In casual speech the contraction is `～たげる`: <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってあげる → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったげる, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ておいてあげる → <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>といたげる. Common in the Kansai area and in relaxed speech generally; never write it.

---

## ～てあげる · ～てあげる vs ～てくれる

> **Rule of thumb** - same event, opposite direction. `～てあげる` when the favour leaves your side; `～てくれる` when it arrives at your side.

The subject of both is the person **doing** the action. What changes is where the benefit ends up relative to the うち／そと line you learned at N5.

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんの<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってあげた**。<br>*I helped Yamada with his work.*

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれた**。<br>*Yamada helped me with my work.*

---

## ～てあげる · ～てあげる vs ～てくれる (cont.)

The line is your family, not just your body. If a teacher explains something to your daughter, that favour has come toward your side, so it is <ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>してくださいました - never ❌してあげました, which would push your own daughter into the そと group.

---

## ～てあげる · ～てあげる vs the plain verb

> **Rule of thumb** - the plain verb reports *what happened*. `～てあげる` reports *who it was for*. If the beneficiary is not the point, leave it off.

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づけました**。<br>*I tidied my brother's room yesterday.*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づけてあげました**。<br>*I tidied my brother's room for him yesterday.*

Both are correct. The second one is the version you say when you want credit, or when you are complaining that you had to. This is not a subtlety learners can skip: overusing `～てあげる` is one of the clearest markers of a foreign speaker, because English "for him" is neutral and Japanese `～てあげる` is not.

---

## ～てあげる · ～てあげる vs ～させてあげる

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てあげる` means you *did* it for them. `～させてあげる` means you *let them* do it, as a kindness.

English "let him" and "did it for him" are far apart; in Japanese they are one suffix apart, and learners reach for the wrong one.

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもに<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>描<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてあげた**。<br>*I drew a picture for my child.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもに<ruby>自由<rt>じゆう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>描<rt>か</rt></ruby>かせてあげた**。<br>*I let my child draw whatever they liked.*

---

## ～てあげる · ～てあげる vs ～させてあげる (cont.)

The causative `～させてあげる` is extremely common with permission （<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ませてあげる, <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>ばせてあげる）and carries the same warmth - and the same risk of sounding lofty about your own generosity.

---

## ～てあげる · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて**あげました**。 → ✅ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて**くれました**。<br>*A favour arriving at the speaker can never be `～てあげる`. This is the N5 あげる／くれる rule, unchanged.*

- ❌ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>をコピーして**さしあげましょうか**。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>をコピー**しましょうか**。／お<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ちしましょうか。<br>*Offering a superior a *labelled* favour is presumptuous even in the humble form. Drop the giving verb and use the plain offer or the お～します frame.*

- ❌ <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**に**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってあげました。 → ✅ <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**を**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってあげました。<br>*`あげる` does not assign the particle. <code><ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>う</code> takes <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を, so the person keeps `を`.*

---

## ～てあげる · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>し**あげます**。 → ✅ <ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>し**てあげます**。<br>*`あげる` attaches to the て-form, not the ます-stem.*

- ❌ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>にセーターを<ruby>編<rt>あ</rt></ruby>んで**あげました**。（telling an outsider）→ ✅ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>にセーターを<ruby>編<rt>あ</rt></ruby>んで**くれました**。<br>*Both are inside your family, so from your chair the favour landed on your side. `あげる` here would sound as though neither of them were related to you.*

---

## ～てあげる · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>かったので、<ruby>後輩<rt>こうはい</rt></ruby>のかばんを<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っ____。

{reveal}
<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っ**てあげました**。

{reveal}
The favour goes from the speaker outward to a junior colleague, so `～てあげる` is the right direction. Note the `の` on <code><ruby>後輩<rt>こうはい</rt></ruby></code>: the verb acts on the bag, and the owner of the bag is marked with `の`.

## ～てあげる · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいやり<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えて（あげました・くださいました）。

{reveal}
<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいやり<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくださいました**。

{reveal}
The section chief is the one acting and the benefit reaches the speaker, so the verb must be the toward-me one, in its honorific form. `あげました` would reverse the direction and put the speaker above the boss.

## ～てあげる · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'll look after the dog for you while you're away."

{reveal}
<ruby>留守<rt>るす</rt></ruby>の<ruby>間<rt>あいだ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>世話<rt>せわ</rt></ruby>を**してあげるよ**。

{reveal}
Said to a close friend this is warm and normal. To an acquaintance you would drop the giving verb - <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>世話<rt>せわ</rt></ruby>をしておきますよ - because announcing the favour to its recipient is what makes `～てあげる` sound self-satisfied.

## ～てあげる · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>、<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>そうですね。<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってあげましょうか。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>、<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>そうですね。お<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ち**しましょうか**。

{reveal}
The grammar is fine and the effect is rude: you are informing your teacher that you are about to be generous to them. Swapping in `さしあげましょうか` does not help. The natural offer upward is the humble お + ます-stem + します frame.

---

## ～てあげる · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けました。 (b) <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてあげました。

{reveal}
(a) is a neutral report of an action: the window got opened, and nothing is said about who wanted it open.

{reveal}
(b) frames the same action as a service performed for somebody - you opened it *for* them, because they were hot or could not reach. It implies a specific beneficiary who is not you, and depending on tone it can carry either kindness or a hint of "and you're welcome". If the beneficiary is the person you are speaking to, (a) is almost always the better choice.

# ～てくれる

**Meaning** · someone does something for me, or for my side - and I am grateful for it

`～てくれる` is the action counterpart of the `くれる` you met at N5. Someone else performs the verb, and the good of it arrives at the speaker's うち. Unlike `～てあげる`, this one is not risky to say: marking a favour that came *to* you is simply politeness, and leaving it off can sound cold.

---

## ～てくれる · Formation

Attach `くれる` to the て-form. The doer is the subject; the beneficiary - usually <code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby></code> - is normally left unsaid, because the verb has already said it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + くれる | <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれる |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + くれます | <ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>ってくれました |
| Verb (negative) | て-form + くれない | <ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>してくれない |
| From someone above you (honorific) | て-form + くださる → くださいます | <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>してくださいました |
| Casual request | て-form + くれない？／くれる？ | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれない? |

---

## ～てくれる · Formation (cont.)

The sentence frame is <code>Aが（<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に）～てくれる</code>. As with plain `くれる`, the particle on the beneficiary comes from the underlying verb - <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくれる but <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれる.

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てくれる` can never have the speaker as the doer. There is no ❌<code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してくれた</code>. Nothing can move toward you while you are the one doing it.

> **Note** - the ます-form of `くださる` is irregular: **くださいます**, not ❌くださります. And the `ください` you have used since your first week is nothing more than the imperative of this verb - <code><ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってください</code> is literally "give me the favour of waiting".

---

## ～てくれる · Examples

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで**<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>ってくれました**。<br>*My dad drove me to the station.*

だれが<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めてくれた**の?<br>*Who closed the window for me?*

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>作文<rt>さくぶん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>してくださいました**。<br>*My teacher kindly corrected my essay.*

<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ<ruby>課<rt>か</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>代<rt>か</rt></ruby>わってくれて**、<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かりました。<br>*Someone in my section covered my shift, which was a real help.*

---

## ～てくれる · Examples (cont.)

だれも**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくれなかった**から、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わったのを<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らなかった。<br>*Nobody told me, so I didn't know the meeting time had changed.*

<ruby>妻<rt>つま</rt></ruby>が<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby>お<ruby>弁当<rt>べんとう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>ってくれる**ので、<ruby>昼<rt>ひる</rt></ruby>ごはんに<ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>りません。<br>*My wife makes me a packed lunch every morning, so lunch is never a problem.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>したとき、<ruby>隣<rt>となり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>んでくださいました**。<br>*When my son had a fever, our neighbour was kind enough to drive us to the hospital.*

<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいのに<ruby>最後<rt>さいご</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いてくれて**、<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>にありがとう。<br>*Thank you so much for hearing me out to the end even though you were busy.*

---

## ～てくれる · Nuance & register

`～てくれる` is **gratitude built into the grammar**. Japanese has no polite way to report a favour received without it. If a colleague fixes your spreadsheet and you tell someone <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>しました, you have described an event; add `くれました` and you have acknowledged a kindness. Omitting it where it belongs sounds ungrateful in a way that has no English equivalent, so learners should err toward using it.

The single most useful frame built on it is `～てくれて、ありがとう` - the standard way to thank someone for an **action**（<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てくれてありがとう）, as opposed to `～をありがとう` for a thing. Polite version: `～てくださって、ありがとうございます`. Also common: <code>～てくれて<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かりました</code>, "you saved me."

---

## ～てくれる · Nuance & register (cont.)

The **negative** is where the emotion flips. `～てくれない` / `～てくれなかった` is a complaint, not a neutral report: it says the favour was owed and did not arrive. <ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれなかった means "nobody would help me", with the resentment audible. Compare the bare <ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>いませんでした, which is a flat statement of fact.

In the **question** form `～てくれない?` the same negative becomes a soft casual request - the first rung of the request ladder that runs on up through `～てくれませんか`, `～てもらえませんか` and `～ていただけませんか`.

---

## ～てくれる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register notes: `くれる` is plain, `くれます` polite, `くださる` honorific. `くださる` is used for a superior's kindness toward you and is completely standard in business speech. The plain imperative `くれ`（<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれ）is rough, male-marked and abrupt - fine among close male friends, never to a stranger. In fast speech `～てくれる` reduces to `～てくれ` + rising tone or, very casually, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せてくんない? Do not write that.

One extension worth knowing: `～てくれる` can be used with things and weather by personification, and it is warm rather than childish - <ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってくれてよかった, "thankfully the weather held out for us."

---

## ～てくれる · ～てくれる vs ～てもらう

> **Rule of thumb** - one event, two chairs. `～てくれる` seats the **doer** as subject; `～てもらう` seats the **beneficiary** as subject.

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちが<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>しを**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれました**。<br>*My friend helped me move.*

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>しを**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらいました**。<br>*I got my friend to help me move.*

Both are natural and describe the same afternoon. The difference is initiative and warmth. `くれる` presents the friend as acting of his own accord and reads as thanks. `もらう` presents the speaker as having arranged it, which is why it is the form you use when you asked, hired, or requested. Notice the particle flip on the friend: `が` as subject, `に` as source.

---

## ～てくれる · ～てくれる vs ～てくださる

> **Rule of thumb** - `くださる` is `くれる` with a status gap. Use it when the person doing you the favour is above you, or is a customer.

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>空港<rt>くうこう</rt></ruby>まで**<ruby>迎<rt>むか</rt></ruby>えに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てくださいました**。<br>*The department head came to meet me at the airport.*

Using plain `くれました` about a superior is grammatical but flat, and in front of that superior or their colleagues it sounds disrespectful. In private grumbling to a friend it is perfectly normal - the choice tracks the audience as much as the person.

---

## ～てくれる · ～てくれる vs ～てあげる

> **Rule of thumb** - `くれる` is safe to say to anyone; `あげる` is not.

They are mirror images grammatically, but not socially. Thanking someone with <code><ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれてありがとう</code> is warm. Telling that same person <code><ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってあげたよ</code> is smug, because you are the one naming your own act a favour.

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>会場<rt>かいじょう</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてくれた**。<br>*Tanaka drew me a map to the venue.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>会場<rt>かいじょう</rt></ruby>までの<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてあげた**。<br>*I drew Tanaka a map to the venue.*

---

## ～てくれる · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>して**くれました**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>して**あげました**。<br>*`くれる` cannot take the speaker as the doer. When the favour leaves you, it is `あげる`.*

- ❌ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>して**くださりました**。 → ✅ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>して**くださいました**。<br>*`くださる` has an irregular ます-form, like おっしゃる → おっしゃいます.*

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てくれて**ありがとうを<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>います**。 → ✅ <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てくれて**ありがとう**。<br>*Thanks for an action is expressed with `～てくれてありがとう` directly; there is no need to build a sentence around the word ありがとう.*

---

## ～てくれる · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>った<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>はおいしいです。 → ✅ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>ってくれた<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>はおいしいです。<br>*Not ungrammatical, but a Japanese speaker would hear the missing `くれる` as oddly detached about their own mother's cooking.*

- ❌ <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>って**くれ**、お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします。 → ✅ <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>って**ください**。／<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>って**くれない**?<br>*Bare `くれ` is a rough male command and clashes with a polite frame.*

# ～てもらう

**Meaning** · get someone to do something for you; have something done for your benefit

`～てもらう` describes the same favour as `～てくれる`, but from the receiving chair. The person who benefits becomes the **subject** of the sentence, and the person who actually does the work is demoted to a `に` phrase. That re-arrangement is the whole difficulty: English "I had my hair cut" hides the hairdresser, and Japanese puts them back in with `に`.

---

## ～てもらう · Formation

Attach `もらう` to the て-form. The frame is `Bは Aに ～てもらう` - B receives, A does.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + もらう | <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>してもらう |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + もらいます | <ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>ってもらいました |
| From someone above you (humble) | て-form + いただく | <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ていただきました |
| Potential (used for requests) | て-form + もらえる／いただける | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てもらえますか |
| Negative potential (polite request) | て-form + もらえませんか | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってもらえませんか |

> **Rule of thumb** - the doer takes `に`. Not `が`, which would make them the receiver, and not `を`, which is reserved for the thing acted on.

---

## ～てもらう · Formation (cont.)

`から` is possible instead of `に` when the favour is information or an object moving from a source, and it is preferred when the giver is an organisation: <ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>**から**<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>してもらいました. With most action verbs, `に` is the default.

> **Note** - the beneficiary is normally the speaker or the speaker's side, exactly as with `くれる`. <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらった is fine when you are narrating about a third party, but it quietly puts you in Tanaka's camp for the length of the sentence.

---

## ～てもらう · Examples

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>ってもらった**。<br>*I got a friend to take a photo of me.*

<ruby>駅員<rt>えきいん</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてもらいました**。<br>*I had the station attendant tell me how to get there.*

パソコンが<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かなくなったので、<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てもらった**。<br>*My laptop stopped working, so I got my brother to take a look at it.*

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>美容院<rt>びよういん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>髪<rt>かみ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>ってもらう**<ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>です。<br>*I'm getting my hair cut at the salon next week.*

---

## ～てもらう · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>にサインを**していただきました**。<br>*I had the department head sign the documents.*

<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>しの<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>後輩<rt>こうはい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>してもらう**つもりです。<br>*I'm planning to get a junior colleague to lend me his car on moving day.*

<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からなかったので、<ruby>窓口<rt>まどぐち</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>にゆっくり**<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>していただきました**。<br>*I didn't know how to fill it in, so I had the clerk at the counter explain it slowly.*

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>められないなら、<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>専門家<rt>せんもんか</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>ってもらった**ほうがいいですよ。<br>*If you can't decide on your own, you'd do better to get a specialist to talk it through with you.*

---

## ～てもらう · Nuance & register

**`～てもらう` implies the receiver had a hand in it.** Not always a spoken request - sometimes just consent, or having gone somewhere for the service - but the event is not a bolt from the blue. That is why <ruby>髪<rt>かみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>ってもらった is the normal way to say you had a haircut: you went to the salon and asked. Compare <ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>がやんでくれた, where nothing was arranged, and only `くれる` works.

Because the receiver is the subject, `～てもらう` is also the natural frame for **planning and arranging**: <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てもらいます means "I'll have Tanaka come" - the speaker is organising it. Said about a person present, this can sound managerial, so it belongs downward or sideways, not upward.

---

## ～てもらう · Nuance & register (cont.)

`～ていただく` is the humble form and is one of the workhorses of business Japanese. Whole registers of office speech are built on it: ご<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>いただけますでしょうか, <ruby>少々<rt>しょうしょう</rt></ruby>お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちいただきます. Note the honorific frame `お／ご + stem + いただく` （お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちいただく, ご<ruby>記入<rt>きにゅう</rt></ruby>いただく）, which is politer still than <code><ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていただく</code> and very common on signs and announcements.

The potential forms are where this pattern earns its keep in daily life. `もらえる` means "can receive the favour", which is exactly what a request is:

---

## ～てもらう · Nuance & register (cont.)

- `～てもらえる?` - casual, to a friend
- `～てもらえますか` - polite, everyday
- `～てもらえませんか` - politer; the negative leaves room to refuse
- `～ていただけませんか` - humble, business standard
- `～ていただけないでしょうか` - the top of the ladder

Register: `もらう` is neutral in plain and polite speech alike, with no male/female marking. `いただく` belongs to formal and business situations; using it with close friends sounds like you are being funny on purpose. Casually `もらえる?` often contracts in speech to `もらえん?` in western dialects - regional, not standard.

---

## ～てもらう · ～てもらう vs ～てくれる

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てもらう` says *I got them to*; `～てくれる` says *they did it for me*. Initiative sits with the subject.

<ruby>近所<rt>きんじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>留守<rt>るす</rt></ruby>の<ruby>間<rt>あいだ</rt></ruby><ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>世話<rt>せわ</rt></ruby>を**してもらいました**。<br>*I got a neighbour to look after the cat while I was away.*

<ruby>近所<rt>きんじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>留守<rt>るす</rt></ruby>の<ruby>間<rt>あいだ</rt></ruby><ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>世話<rt>せわ</rt></ruby>を**してくれました**。<br>*A neighbour looked after the cat for me while I was away.*

---

## ～てもらう · ～てもらう vs ～てくれる (cont.)

The first says you arranged it. The second says they stepped in - and it carries more warmth, which is why thanks are almost always phrased with `くれる`: <ruby>世話<rt>せわ</rt></ruby>をしてくれてありがとう. してもらってありがとう is occasionally heard in casual speech, but it is not the standard shape: gratitude points at what the other person did, so the giving verb is the one that belongs there.

There is a hard limit on `もらう`. If the doer acted entirely on their own and the event was not arranged, `もらう` is unnatural: <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>財布<rt>さいふ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>拾<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>ってくれた - a stranger picked up my dropped wallet - cannot become ❌<ruby>拾<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>ってもらった unless you had asked them to.

---

## ～てもらう · ～てもらう vs the causative ～させる

> **Rule of thumb** - both are English "have someone do it". `～てもらう` is *asking* and the doer is willing; `～させる` is *making* and the speaker has authority.

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってもらいました**。<br>*I got my son to go and do the shopping (and thanks, son).*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かせました**。<br>*I made my son go and do the shopping.*

---

## ～てもらう · ～てもらう vs the causative ～させる (cont.)

Because `もらう` builds in gratitude, it softens an instruction; because `させる` asserts power, it is unusable upward and sounds harsh even downward. Combining both is how you ask for permission politely: <code><ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>させていただきます</code>, "allow me to explain."

---

## ～てもらう · ～てもらう vs ～てほしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てもらう` reports a favour that happened or is arranged; `～てほしい` states a wish that has not.

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>を**もらいたい**んですが。<br>*I'd like to get an answer by next week.*

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>を**してほしい**んですが。<br>*I'd like you to answer by next week.*

The desiderative `～てもらいたい` sits between them: it is a wish expressed through the receiving frame, and it is a shade more indirect - and therefore softer - than `～てほしい`, because it talks about what you would receive rather than about what the other person should do.

---

## ～てもらう · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**が**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらいました。 → ✅ <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**に**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらいました。<br>*With `もらう` the doer takes `に`. `が` would make the friend the one receiving the favour.*

- ❌ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>推薦状<rt>すいせんじょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて**もらいました**。（in a formal report）→ ✅ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>推薦状<rt>すいせんじょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて**いただきました**。<br>*Receiving a favour from someone above you takes the humble `いただく` in any formal setting.*

- ❌ <ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>けてもらって**ありがとうございました**。 → ✅ <ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>けて**くださって**ありがとうございました。<br>*Thanks point at the *giver's* act, so the giving verb is the one that fits.*

---

## ～てもらう · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてもらった。（when they volunteered）→ ✅ <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくれた。<br>*Unrequested help is `くれる` territory. `もらう` implies you asked.*

- ❌ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>てもらいます。（said to the department head）→ ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>にご<ruby>出席<rt>しゅっせき</rt></ruby>いただけますでしょうか。<br>*Arranging a superior's actions out loud sounds managerial. Turn it into a request with the humble potential.*

---

## ～てもらう · Practice 1

**1.** カメラを<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていなかったので、<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>りかかった<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>____<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>ってもらいました。

{reveal}
<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>りかかった<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>ってもらいました。

{reveal}
The doer of the favour is marked with `に` in a `もらう` sentence. The subject slot belongs to the speaker, who is the one receiving the service.

# ～てやる

**Meaning** · do something for someone below you - and, in a second life, *I'll show them*: a declaration of defiant intent

`～てやる` is the bottom rung of the `さしあげる／あげる／やる` ladder you met at N5, now carrying an action instead of an object. It points **downward**: to animals, plants, small children, your own younger family. Alongside that, it has a completely separate use with no beneficiary at all, where it means "just watch me do it" - and that second use is far more common in real speech than textbooks let on.

---

## ～てやる · Formation

Attach `やる` to the て-form. `やる` is a regular う-verb: やります, やった, やらない, やろう.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (favour, downward) | て-form + やる | <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってやる |
| Verb (polite) | て-form + やります | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでやります |
| Verb (offer / resolve) | て-form + やろう | <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ける → <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてやろう |
| Verb (resolve, plain) | て-form + やる（no `に` phrase） | <ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってやる |
| Contracted, very casual | て-form + たる | <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってやる → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったる |

In the favour sense the particles come from the underlying verb, exactly as with `～てあげる`: <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>してやる, <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>連<rt>つ</rt></ruby>れて<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってやる.

---

## ～てやる · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - if the sentence has a `に` phrase naming who benefits, it is the favour `～てやる`. If it has no beneficiary and the verb is about the speaker's own achievement, it is the resolve `～てやる`.

---

## ～てやる · Examples

<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>連<rt>つ</rt></ruby>れて<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってやった**。<br>*I took the dog for a walk.*

<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たがっているから、<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてやろう**。<br>*The cat wants to go out, so I'll open the window for her.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>いていたので、アイスを**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってやった**。<br>*My little brother was crying, so I bought him an ice cream.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもたちが<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>る<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に、<ruby>毎晩<rt>まいばん</rt></ruby><ruby>絵本<rt>えほん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでやります**。<br>*Every night I read the children a picture book before bed.*

---

## ～てやる · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>今度<rt>こんど</rt></ruby>こそ<ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってやる**。<br>*This time I'm definitely going to win.*

<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ていろよ、いつか**<ruby>見返<rt>みかえ</rt></ruby>してやる**。<br>*Just you wait - one day I'll show them all.*

こんな<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>、いつか**やめてやる**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いながら<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>いている。<br>*I go to work thinking that one day I'm going to walk out of this place.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>かったら、<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってやろう**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*If my son gets into university, I'm thinking I'll buy him whatever bike he wants.*

---

## ～てやる · Nuance & register

**The favour sense is rough, and it is age- and gender-marked.** `やる` for people was once the ordinary form; it now sounds blunt, and it is noticeably more common in men's speech and in older speakers. A father saying <ruby>宿題<rt>しゅくだい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てやる about his own child is completely normal and even affectionate. The same sentence to a colleague's child would be presumptuous.

Toward animals and plants it is entirely neutral - <ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>に<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>をやる is the standard phrase, though younger speakers increasingly say あげる there too, and some older speakers find *that* precious. Both are heard; neither will get you into trouble.

---

## ～てやる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Where you must not use it: **anyone senior, anyone outside your circle, and anyone you are being polite to.** `～てやります` exists but does not fix the problem, because the rudeness lives in `やる`, not in the politeness ending. There is no upward version - the ladder goes やる → あげる → さしあげる, and the top rung has its own difficulties (see `～てあげる`, which is presumptuous for a different reason).

**The resolve sense** is the one you will hear in dramas, sports and complaints. It attaches to a verb about the speaker's own action, has no beneficiary, and means roughly "I'm going to do it, and let that be noted." The emotional colour comes from the same source as the favour sense: you are casting your own act as something imposed on the world.

---

## ～てやる · Nuance & register (cont.)

- <ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合格<rt>ごうかく</rt></ruby>してやる。 - I *will* pass.
- こんな<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>、<ruby>辞<rt>や</rt></ruby>めてやる。 - I quit. (defiant)
- <ruby>文句<rt>もんく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってやった。 - I gave them a piece of my mind.

That last type shades into threat: <ruby>殴<rt>なぐ</rt></ruby>ってやる is "I'll hit you", not a kindness. Tone and context do all the disambiguating, and it is almost always obvious.

---

## ～てやる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Casually - and especially in Kansai speech - `～てやる` contracts to `～たる`: <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってやる → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったる, <ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してやる → <ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>したる. You will hear it constantly in comedy and in the west of the country; do not write it, and do not use it in an exam.

> **Note** - `～てやる` is also how you tell someone to do something for a third party who is below them: <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>にえさを**やってください**, <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えて**やって**ください. The polite frame is on the outside and the downward frame on the inside, and this is normal, not contradictory.

---

## ～てやる · ～てやる vs ～てあげる

> **Rule of thumb** - same direction, different altitude. `～てあげる` is for equals and outsiders; `～てやる` is for those clearly below you, and for animals.

<ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>髪<rt>かみ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>結<rt>むす</rt></ruby>んでやった**。<br>*I tied my daughter's hair for her.*

<ruby>同僚<rt>どうりょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>き<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>けてあげた**。<br>*I took on a colleague's work for them.*

Swap those and both sentences go wrong in opposite ways: `あげる` about your own small child sounds oddly formal, as if she were a guest; `やる` about a colleague sounds contemptuous. When you are not sure of the altitude, `あげる` is the safer error - but remember that neither should be said *to* the beneficiary's face.

---

## ～てやる · ～てやる (resolve) vs ～てみせる

> **Rule of thumb** - both declare determination. `～てやる` aims the declaration at the world; `～てみせる` aims it at an audience: *I'll show you*.

<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一位<rt>いちい</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>ってやる**。<br>*Next year I'm taking first place.*

<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一位<rt>いちい</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>ってみせる**。<br>*Next year I'll show you I can take first place.*

---

## ～てやる · ～てやる (resolve) vs ～てみせる (cont.)

`～てみせる` promises a demonstration to someone who doubts you; `～てやる` is closer to spite or grit and does not need a doubter. They combine freely - <ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ず<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってみせてやる is heavy-handed but grammatical.

---

## ～てやる · ～てやる vs ～てさしあげる

> **Rule of thumb** - these are the two ends of one vertical scale, and in modern speech **both ends are avoided**. The safe middle is `～てあげる`, or no giving verb at all.

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>奥<rt>おく</rt></ruby>さまを<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで**お<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>りしました**。<br>*I saw the department head's wife to the station.*

`さしあげる` is grammatically the humble upward form, but announcing your own kindness to a superior is presumptuous no matter how humble the verb, so speakers use the お + stem + します frame instead. At the other end, `やる` is fine within the family and rude outside it. The lesson is the same in both directions: the higher the stakes, the less Japanese wants you to name the favour.

---

## ～てやる · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>先輩<rt>せんぱい</rt></ruby>のコピーを<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>って**やりました**。 → ✅ <ruby>先輩<rt>せんぱい</rt></ruby>のコピーを<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>って**おきました**。<br>*`やる` points downward. Used about a senior it is close to insulting, and the polite ending does not soften it.*

- ❌ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>って**やりましょうか**。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、お<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を**お<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ちしましょうか**。<br>*Upward offers use the humble お + ます-stem + します frame, with no giving verb.*

- ❌ <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>宿題<rt>しゅくだい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てやった。 → ✅ <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>宿題<rt>しゅくだい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てやった。<br>*`やる` does not assign particles. The verb acts on the homework, so the owner takes `の` and only one `を` survives.*

---

## ～てやる · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合格<rt>ごうかく</rt></ruby>して**やります**。（in a formal speech）→ ✅ <ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合格<rt>ごうかく</rt></ruby>**したい**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*The resolve sense is emotional and confrontational. It does not belong in formal or public register even with `ます` on the end.*

- ❌ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>にセーターを<ruby>編<rt>あ</rt></ruby>んで**やりました**。 → ✅ <ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>にセーターを<ruby>編<rt>あ</rt></ruby>んで**くれました**。<br>*`やる` sends the favour away from the speaker, so it can never land on the speaker. Toward you it is always `くれる`, however junior the doer.*

# ～てほしい

**Meaning** · I want *you* (or someone else) to do something

At N5 you learned two ways to want: `～たい` for an action you will perform yourself, and `ほしい` for an object. `～てほしい` fills the remaining square - an action performed by **somebody else**. The wish is still yours, so the first-person restriction you learned with `たい` carries over unchanged.

---

## ～てほしい · Formation

Attach `ほしい` to the て-form. `ほしい` is an い-adjective and conjugates like one.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ほしい | <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってほしい |
| Verb (negative wish) | ない-form + でほしい | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わないでほしい |
| Polite | ～てほしいです | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てほしいです |
| Past | ほしい → ほしかった | <ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かる → <ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かってほしかった |
| Negative | ほしい → ほしくない | <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れる → <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れてほしくない |
| Softened request | ～てほしいんですが／～てほしいんだけど | <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てほしいんですが |
| Third person's wish | ～てほしいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っている／～てほしがっている | <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>してほしいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っています |

---

## ～てほしい · Formation (cont.)

The person you want to act takes `に`. The wisher is the topic, and as usual is dropped when it is you: （<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は）<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>してほしい。

> **Rule of thumb** - negatives split. "I want you *not* to do it" is `～ないで**ほしい**`; "I *don't* want you to do it" is `～て**ほしくない**`. The first is a request, the second a rejection, and learners routinely swap them.

> **Note** - the subject of `～てほしい` does not have to be a person. <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>がやんでほしい and <ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>どおりに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てほしい are both natural: you can wish an outcome onto the world. With inanimate subjects the particle is `が`, not `に`.

---

## ～てほしい · Examples

ちょっと**<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってほしい**。<br>*I want you to wait a moment.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れてほしい**な。<br>*I hope it clears up tomorrow.*

これは<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>にも**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わないでほしい**。<br>*I don't want you telling anyone about this.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>にはもっと<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでほしい**。<br>*I'd like my son to play outside more.*

---

## ～てほしい · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かに**してほしい**んですが、<ruby>下<rt>した</rt></ruby>の<ruby>階<rt>かい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくくて。<br>*I'd like them to keep it down, but it's hard to say anything to the people downstairs.*

<ruby>上司<rt>じょうし</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かってほしかった**ことが、うまく<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えられなかった。<br>*I couldn't get across the thing I wanted my boss to understand.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>金曜日<rt>きんようび</rt></ruby>までに**<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>してほしい**んですが、<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いますか。<br>*I'd like the meeting materials in by Friday - will you manage it?*

こんなに<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わらないでほしい**と<ruby>心<rt>こころ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った。<br>*I found myself wishing with all my heart that this happy time would never end.*

---

## ～てほしい · Nuance & register

**`～てほしい` is direct.** It states, out loud, what you want another person to do. Between friends and family, and downward at work, that is ordinary. Aimed upward it is too bald - you are placing a demand on someone senior - and the polite ending does not help, because the directness is in the construction, not in the ending. ❌<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>してほしいです is grammatical and will make the room uncomfortable.

The standard softeners, from lightest to heaviest:

---

## ～てほしい · Nuance & register (cont.)

- `～てほしいんだけど` / `～てほしいんですが` - trails off and<br>*leaves the ask implied. Very common.*
- `～てほしいなあ` - turns the demand into a wistful wish. Friendly.
- `～てもらいたい` - the receiving frame; a shade more indirect, because<br>*it talks about what you would receive rather than what they should do.*
- `～ていただきたい` - the humble version, and the normal business form:<br>*ご<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>いただきたいのですが。*
- `～ていただけませんか` - a question rather than a statement, and the<br>*politest of the set.*

The **negative wish** `～ないでほしい` is worth practising, because English "I want you not to…" is rare and learners avoid the structure. <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>しないでほしい, <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>をしないでほしい - both are warm, not scolding.

---

## ～てほしい · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Third person.** As with `たい`, you cannot flatly assert someone else's wish in a main clause: ❌<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てほしいです. Route it through reported speech or evidence - <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てほしいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っています, <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てほしいようです. The form `～てほしがっている` exists but is much less natural than the simple `ほしがる` for objects; most speakers avoid it.

Register: `～てほしい` is plain, `～てほしいです` polite, and neither is gender-marked. In writing - opinion essays, editorials - it is extremely common as a way of stating what society or government ought to do: <ruby>国<rt>くに</rt></ruby>にはもっと<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>してほしい。

---

## ～てほしい · ～てほしい vs ～たい

> **Rule of thumb** - `～たい` is *I* do it; `～てほしい` is *you* do it. Same wish, different actor.

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>へ**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい**。<br>*I want to go to Kyoto next week.*

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>へ**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってほしい**。<br>*I want you to go to Kyoto next week.*

Both are い-adjectives and both are restricted to the speaker's own wishes. The difference is only who does the verb - but English "want" collapses the two, so the error is common and the resulting sentence is badly wrong rather than merely odd.

---

## ～てほしい · ～てほしい vs ～てもらいたい・～ていただきたい

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning, rising politeness. `てほしい` states the wish bare; `てもらいたい` routes it through receiving; `ていただきたい` does that humbly.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までに**<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>してほしい**。<br>*I want an answer by tomorrow.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までに**<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>していただきたい**のですが。<br>*I would like to receive your answer by tomorrow.*

The second is what you write in an email to a client. Note that `もらいたい` and `いただきたい` are the desiderative of `もらう`／`いただく` - verbs, so their subject is you, the receiver, while the other person keeps `に`. The particle pattern is identical to `てほしい`, which makes them easy to substitute.

---

## ～てほしい · ～てほしい vs ～てください

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てください` tells someone to do it; `～てほしい` says you want it done. A wish is not an instruction, and the two are not interchangeable upward.

ここに<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてください**。<br>*Please write your name here.*

ここに<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてほしい**んです。<br>*I'd like you to write your name here.*

`ください` is polite in form but is still an imperative, which is why it is fine from staff to customer and awkward from junior to superior. `～てほしいんですが` is softer because it stops short of telling anyone to do anything - it puts the wish on the table and lets the listener pick it up.

---

## ～てほしい · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>**たいです**。 → ✅ <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てほしいです**。<br>*`たい` can only describe an action *you* perform. When somebody else does the verb, the wish takes `～てほしい`.*

- ❌ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>っ**てほしいです**。 → ✅ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>っ**てほしいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っています**。<br>*A third person's wish cannot be asserted flatly, exactly as with `たい`.*

- ❌ <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>し**てほしくない**。（meaning "please don't worry"）→ ✅ <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>し**ないでほしい**。<br>*`～てほしくない` rejects the wish; `～ないでほしい` wishes for the non-action. Only the second one asks them to stop worrying.*

---

## ～てほしい · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**てほしいです**。 → ✅ <ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**ていただけませんか**。<br>*Grammatical, and far too direct upward. The humble request form is what the situation calls for.*

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>**に**やんでほしい。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>**が**やんでほしい。<br>*`に` marks a person you are asking to act. An inanimate subject takes `が`.*

---

# ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか

**Meaning** · could you do that for me? - the everyday polite request, built on the giving-and-receiving verbs

A Japanese request is not an imperative with "please" stuck on it. It is a question about a **favour**: either "will you give me this action?" （`～てくれませんか`）or "could I receive this action?"（`～てもらえませんか`）. Once you see that, the whole ladder from `～てくれない?` up to `～ていただけないでしょうか` is one structure with the politeness dial turned.

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Formation

Both families take the て-form. `くれる` is used as it stands; `もらう` goes into the **potential**, `もらえる`, because you are asking whether receiving the favour is possible.

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (giving frame) | て-form + くれる? | <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ける → <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてくれる? |
| Verb (giving frame, negative) | て-form + くれない? | <ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してくれない? |
| Verb (giving frame, polite) | て-form + くれませんか | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれませんか |
| Verb (receiving frame) | て-form + もらえる? | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てもらえる? |
| Verb (receiving frame, polite) | て-form + もらえますか | <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらえますか |
| Verb (receiving frame, politest) | て-form + もらえませんか | <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てもらえませんか |
| Negative request | ない-form + でくれませんか／でもらえませんか | <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>らないでもらえませんか |

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - two dials, and they turn independently. **Dial 1: frame.** `くれ-` asks the listener to give; `もらえ-` asks whether you may receive. Receiving is more indirect, so `もらえ-` is politer than `くれ-` at every matching rung. **Dial 2: negative.** A negative question - `くれない?`, `くれませんか`, `もらえませんか` - is politer than the affirmative, because it builds in room to say no.

Put the two dials together and you get the ladder learners need memorised as a single sequence:

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Formation (cont.)

| Rung | Form | Who you say it to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>って | close friends, family; blunt |
| 2 | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれる? | friends |
| 3 | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれない? | friends; softer than rung 2 |
| 4 | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってもらえる? | friends; softer still |
| 5 | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれませんか | polite, but the listener is the giver - slightly direct |
| 6 | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってもらえませんか | polite, standard with strangers and colleagues |
| 7 | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていただけませんか | humble; superiors, customers, business |
| 8 | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていただけないでしょうか | the top rung; a real imposition |

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Examples

その<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>ってくれない**?<br>*Could you pass me that book?*

ペンを**<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してもらえませんか**。<br>*Could you lend me a pen?*

もう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>してくれませんか**。<br>*Could you speak up a bit?*

すみません、<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてもらえませんか**。<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>くて。<br>*Excuse me, could you open the window? It's stifling.*

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>いてくれませんか**。あとで<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づけます。<br>*Could you just leave it on the desk? I'll sort it out later myself.*

あさっての<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>代<rt>か</rt></ruby>わりに**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>てもらえない**かな。<br>*I don't suppose you could go to the meeting the day after tomorrow instead of me?*

<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>用事<rt>ようじ</rt></ruby>ができたので、<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>えてもらえませんか**。<br>*Something's come up, so could we move our appointment to next week?*

<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ないんですが、<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby><ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>い<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>洗濯機<rt>せんたくき</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わないでもらえませんか**。<br>*I'm sorry to ask, but could you not run the washing machine late at night?*

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Nuance & register

**Why negatives are politer.** `～てもらえますか` asks whether the favour is possible; `～てもらえませんか` asks whether it is *not* possible. The second one presents refusal as the expected answer, so agreeing is a generosity rather than a duty. Japanese politeness runs on exactly this move, and it is why the longest forms are the politest: every extra layer is another step away from a bare demand.

**The `くれ-`／`もらえ-` split is small but real.** Both are polite; `くれませんか` puts the listener in the subject slot and so points at them a little more directly, while `もらえませんか` talks about what you would receive. With strangers and in service situations, `もらえませんか` is the safer default. Inside a team, `くれませんか` is perfectly normal and sounds slightly warmer, because `くれる` carries the same gratitude it always does.

**Softening prefaces** matter as much as the ending. Real requests almost never start cold:

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Nuance & register (cont.)

- すみませんが… - the general-purpose opener
- <ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>いんだけど… - casual, "sorry to ask"
- <ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありませんが… - formal
- お<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいところ<ruby>恐<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>れ<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>りますが… - business, for a real imposition
- ちょっとお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いがあるんですが… - announces that a request is coming

Trailing off is also standard: 「<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらえませんか…」 with the sentence left hanging is softer than finishing it crisply.

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Register warnings.** The plain imperative `くれ`（<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれ）is rough and male-marked; do not use it. `～てくれる?` and `～てもらえる?` with rising intonation are casual and completely normal among friends of any gender. The affirmative `～てくれますか` is fine but a touch businesslike - the tone of a form or an announcement.

> **Note** - do not stack. ❌<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってくれてもらえませんか is a beginner error produced by trying to be extra polite. One giving verb per request; climb the ladder instead of doubling up.

---

## ～てくれませんか vs ～てもらえませんか

> **Rule of thumb** - same politeness band, different camera. `くれませんか` asks *you* to give; `もらえませんか` asks whether *I* may receive. The receiving frame is the more distant, and therefore slightly politer.

この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくれませんか**。<br>*Would you tell me how to read this kanji?*

この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてもらえませんか**。<br>*Could I get you to tell me how to read this kanji?*

Both are perfectly usable to a classmate or a colleague. The gap only becomes important as the social distance grows: to a stranger on the street, choose the `もらえ-` version, and to your section chief, climb one more rung to `いただけませんか`.

---

## Affirmative vs negative: ～てもらえますか vs ～てもらえませんか

> **Rule of thumb** - the negative question is the politer one. Adding `ません` does not make it a refusal; it makes it an offer of one.

すみません、この<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>まで**<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>んでもらえますか**。<br>*Excuse me, can you carry this luggage upstairs?*

すみません、この<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>まで**<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>んでもらえませんか**。<br>*Excuse me, would you mind carrying this luggage upstairs?*

The first is brisk and appropriate when the favour is part of the person's job. The second treats the favour as optional, which is what you want when you are asking for something extra, or from someone who owes you nothing. English does the same thing with "can you" versus "would you mind".

---

## ～てもらえませんか vs ～てください

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てください` is an instruction dressed politely. The `もらえ-`／`くれ-` forms are questions, and questions can be declined.

こちらに**<ruby>並<rt>なら</rt></ruby>んでください**。<br>*Please queue here.*

すみません、こちらに**<ruby>並<rt>なら</rt></ruby>んでもらえませんか**。<br>*Sorry - would you mind queueing over here?*

The first is what a member of staff says to the public: they have the standing to direct people. The second is what one customer says to another, because no such standing exists. Learners overuse `～てください` for exactly this reason - it is the first request form taught, and it is the one that carries authority.

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>って**もらいませんか**。 → ✅ <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>って**もらえませんか**。<br>*The request form uses the **potential** `もらえる`. `もらいませんか` would be an invitation - "shall we not receive?" - and does not work as a request.*

- ❌ <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>って**くれられませんか**。 → ✅ <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>って**くれませんか**。<br>*`くれる` is not put into the potential in this construction. If you want the potential, switch to the receiving frame: <code><ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってもらえませんか</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>て**くれませんか**。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>て**いただけませんか**。<br>*Rungs 5 and 6 are polite but not humble. A superior needs the `いただく` rung.*

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かに**してくれませんでしたか**。 → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かに**してくれませんか**。<br>*The request is about now, not the past. The negative here is a politeness device, not a tense.*

- ❌ ここでたばこを**<ruby>吸<rt>す</rt></ruby>ってもらえませんか**。（meaning "please don't smoke"）→ ✅ ここでたばこを**<ruby>吸<rt>す</rt></ruby>わないでもらえませんか**。<br>*To ask for a non-action, negate the **verb**, not the request ending.*

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Practice 1

**1.** すみません、この<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めないので、<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んで____か。

{reveal}
<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んで**もらえません**か。（or <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んで**くれません**か）

{reveal}
The potential of `もらう` plus a negative question is the standard polite request. To a stranger the `もらえ-` frame is the safer of the two, because it asks about your own receiving rather than pointing at what the listener should do.

---

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>、こちらにサインを（してくれませんか・していただけませんか）。

{reveal}
<ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>、こちらにサインを**していただけませんか**。

{reveal}
`くれませんか` is polite but not humble, and a company president sits well above the rung it occupies. `いただく` is the humble verb for receiving from above, and its potential-negative question is the normal business request.

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Sorry to bother you, but could you take a photo of us?"

{reveal}
すみません、<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>ってもらえませんか**。

{reveal}
The opener `すみません` is not optional politeness padding - a bare request to a stranger sounds abrupt without it. `もらえませんか` is the right rung for someone you have never met but are not obliged to honour.

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Practice 4

{reveal}
✅ あの、うるさいので、<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>めて**もらえませんか**。

{reveal}
`もらいませんか` is the plain negative of `もらう` and reads as a strange invitation. Requests need the potential form `もらえる`, which asks whether the favour is available to you.

## ～てもらえませんか・～てくれませんか · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれる? (b) <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらえませんか。

{reveal}
(a) is rung 2 on the ladder: plain, affirmative, giving frame. It is what you say to a friend or a sibling, and it assumes a relationship where small favours pass back and forth without ceremony.

{reveal}
(b) is rung 6: polite, negative, receiving frame. Every one of those three choices adds distance - `ます` politeness, a negative that invites refusal, and a frame that talks about your receiving rather than their giving. It is the version for a colleague you do not know well, or for a favour large enough that you want to make saying no easy.

# ～ていただけませんか

**Meaning** · could you please do this for me? - the humble, business-standard request

`～ていただけませんか` is the top of the request ladder that starts at `～てくれない?`. Every piece of it is doing work: `いただく` is the humble verb for receiving, `いただける` is its potential ("may I receive"), and `ませんか` is a negative question that hands the listener an easy way out. Stack all three and you have the sentence that runs Japanese offices.

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Formation

Attach `いただく` to the て-form, put it in the potential, and ask the question in the negative.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + いただけませんか | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いていただけませんか |
| Verb (lighter) | て-form + いただけますか | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ていただけますか |
| Verb (heaviest) | て-form + いただけないでしょうか | <ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>える → <ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>えていただけないでしょうか |
| Verb (negative request) | ない-form + でいただけませんか | <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>らないでいただけませんか |
| Verb (statement form) | て-form + いただきたいのですが | <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>していただきたいのですが |
| Honorific-humble frame | お + ます-stem + いただけませんか | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちいただけませんか |
| する-verbs in that frame | ご + noun + いただけませんか | <ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>する → ご<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>いただけませんか |

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the length *is* the politeness. Each of the four rungs below is longer than the one before, and each is politer: `いただけますか` → `いただけませんか` → `いただけないでしょうか` → <code>いただけますと<ruby>幸<rt>さいわ</rt></ruby>いです</code>.

> **Note** - the person you are asking takes `に`, exactly as with `もらう`, and is almost always dropped because it is obvious: （<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に）<ruby>推薦状<rt>すいせんじょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いていただけませんか。

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Examples

もう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていただけませんか**。<br>*Could you say that once more, please?*

ここに<ruby>住所<rt>じゅうしょ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いていただけますか**。<br>*Could you write your address here?*

すみませんが、<ruby>少々<rt>しょうしょう</rt></ruby>**お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちいただけますか**。<br>*I'm sorry - could you wait just a moment?*

<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>までに**<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>っていただけないでしょうか**。<br>*Would it be possible for you to send the materials by tomorrow morning?*

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありませんが、<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>にちを**<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>えていただけませんか**。<br>*I do apologise, but could we change the date?*

<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がひどいので、<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby><ruby>八時<rt>はちじ</rt></ruby><ruby>以降<rt>いこう</rt></ruby>は**やめていただけませんか**。<br>*The construction noise is terrible, so could you stop after eight in the evening?*

お<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいところ<ruby>恐<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>れ<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>りますが、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>していただけないでしょうか**。<br>*I'm sorry to trouble you when you're busy, but might I ask you to look over this document?*

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>打<rt>う</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わせですが、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>に**していただけると<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かります**。<br>*About next week's meeting - it would be a great help if you could make it three o'clock.*

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Nuance & register

This is **business Japanese's default request**, and it is worth over-learning. Emails, phone calls, service counters, and any conversation with a customer or a superior run on it. Used with a friend it sounds like you are joking or being cold.

The pieces are separable, and knowing which piece you are turning lets you fine-tune:

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **`いただける` vs `いただけます`** - plain vs polite potential. In a request you<br>*almost always want the polite one.*
- **negative vs affirmative** - `いただけませんか` is politer than<br>*`いただけますか` because refusal is written into the question.*
- **`でしょうか` instead of `か`** - adds hesitancy. `いただけないでしょうか` is<br>*the form for a genuine imposition: a deadline change, a favour outside someone's job.*
- **statement instead of question** - `していただきたいのですが` trails<br>*off and leaves the request unspoken. Softer again, and very common in speech.*
- **conditional endings** - <code>いただけると<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かります</code> ("it would<br>*help"), <code>いただけますと<ruby>幸<rt>さいわ</rt></ruby>いです</code> ("I would be grateful"), <code>いただければと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code>. These are email register; the last two are stiff enough that you would rarely say them out loud.*

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Nuance & register (cont.)

**The お／ご frame is politer than the plain て-form version.** お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちいただけませんか outranks <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていただけませんか, and ご<ruby>記入<rt>きにゅう</rt></ruby>いただけませんか outranks <ruby>記入<rt>きにゅう</rt></ruby>していただけませんか. Use `お` with native-Japanese verbs （お<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>き, お<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い）and `ご` with Sino-Japanese nouns （ご<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>, ご<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>）. There are exceptions - お<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>, お<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby> - that simply have to be learned.

**Two things to keep apart.** `～ていただけませんか` asks the *other* person to act. `～させていただけませんか` asks for permission for *you* to act: <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>らせていただけませんか, "would you allow me to leave early?" The middle syllables are almost identical and the meanings are opposite.

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Nuance & register (cont.)

Spoken register also compresses. In quick service speech you will hear <ruby>少々<rt>しょうしょう</rt></ruby>お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちいただけますでしょうか - technically doubled politeness （`ます` plus `でしょう`）that prescriptivists dislike and everyone uses.

---

## ～ていただけませんか · ～ていただけませんか vs ～てもらえませんか

> **Rule of thumb** - identical structure, one step apart on the humility scale. `もらう` is neutral; `いただく` is humble and marks the other person as above you.

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん、この<ruby>数字<rt>すうじ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かめてもらえませんか**。<br>*Tanaka, could you check these figures?*

<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>数字<rt>すうじ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かめていただけませんか**。<br>*Sir, could you check these figures?*

Nothing changes but the verb, and the social reading changes completely. Using `もらえませんか` upward is not rude in the way a bare imperative is - it is more like calling your manager by their first name in a formal meeting: nothing is broken, but you have misjudged the room.

---

## ～ていただけませんか vs ～ていただけないでしょうか

> **Rule of thumb** - `でしょうか` adds doubt, and doubt reads as deference. Save it for requests you would feel awkward making.

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>らせて**いただけませんか**。<br>*Could we push the meeting back a little?*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>らせて**いただけないでしょうか**。<br>*Might it be at all possible to push the meeting back a little?*

Both are correct and both are polite. The second sounds more apologetic and is what you use when you are asking someone to rearrange their day. Overusing it for small things - asking for a pen - sounds theatrical.

---

## ～ていただけませんか · ～ていただけませんか vs お～ください

> **Rule of thumb** - `お～ください` is honorific but still an **instruction**; `～ていただけませんか` is a **question**. Instructions suit signs and staff; questions suit asking a favour.

こちらで**お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちください**。<br>*Please wait here.*

こちらで**お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちいただけませんか**。<br>*Would you mind waiting here?*

The first is what a receptionist says - perfectly polite, and it presumes compliance because directing visitors is her job. The second asks. Learners default to `ください` because it is the first request form they meet, and end up issuing gentle orders to their boss.

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>って**いただきませんか**。 → ✅ <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>って**いただけませんか**。<br>*The request needs the **potential** `いただける`. `いただきませんか` reads as a strange invitation, not a request.*

- ❌ <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて**いただけましたか**。（as a request）→ ✅ <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて**いただけませんか**。<br>*The past tense turns it into a question about whether it has already been done. A request stays in the non-past.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んで**いただけませんか**。（meaning "may I take tomorrow off?"）→ ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ま**せていただけませんか**。<br>*`～ていただく` asks the *other* person to act. Asking permission for your own action needs the causative `～させていただく`.*

---

## ～ていただけませんか · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ ここで<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>っていただけませんか**。（meaning "please don't take photos"）→ ✅ ここで<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>らないでいただけませんか**。<br>*To ask someone to refrain, negate the verb. Negating only the ending still asks them to do it.*

- ❌ お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ち**していただけませんか**。 → ✅ お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ち**いただけませんか**。<br>*`お + stem + する` is the humble form for *your own* actions. When the other person is the one waiting, the frame is `お + stem + いただく`, with no `する`.*

# ～てすみません

**Meaning** · I'm sorry for doing (or not doing) that - an apology attached to the specific act

Bare `すみません` apologises for nothing in particular. `～てすみません` names the offence: the て-form supplies the cause, `すみません` supplies the apology, and the two are joined by the ordinary "reason" use of the て-form you already know from <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れて<ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>りました. What makes it worth its own page is that Japanese reaches for this construction in places where English would say *thank you*.

---

## ～てすみません · Formation

The て-form of whatever caused the trouble, then the apology word.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + すみません | <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる → <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れてすみません |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + すみません | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けなくてすみません |
| Verb (past act) | て-form + すみませんでした | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んですみませんでした |
| い-adjective | くて + すみません | <ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>くてすみません |
| な-adjective | で + すみません | <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby> → <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>ですみません |
| Noun | で + すみません | この<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>汚<rt>きたな</rt></ruby>い<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>ですみません |
| Casual | て + ごめん／ごめんね | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たせる → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たせてごめん |
| Formal | て + <ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません | <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる → <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れて<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません |

---

## ～てすみません · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `すみません` for a thing that is still going on, and `すみませんでした` for a thing that is finished. Arriving late, you say <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れてすみません as you come in; the next day you say <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れてすみませんでした.

> **Note** - `すみません` is the standard written and careful-speech form. `すいません` is the same word as it is actually pronounced in casual speech. Say it, do not write it.

## ～てすみません · Examples

**<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れてすみません**。<br>*Sorry I'm late.*

うるさく**してすみません**。<br>*Sorry for the noise.*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んですみませんでした**。<br>*I'm sorry for taking the day off at short notice yesterday.*

---

## ～てすみません · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby>も**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いてすみません**が、もう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>住所<rt>じゅうしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてください。<br>*Sorry to keep asking, but could you give me the address once more?*

わざわざ<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>て**いただいてすみません**。<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>くまで<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>りますよ。<br>*It's very good of you to have come all this way. Let me give you a lift part of the way back.*

<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>っていただいたのに、お<ruby>礼<rt>れい</rt></ruby>も**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わなくてすみませんでした**。<br>*I'm sorry I never even thanked you after you helped me out.*

つまらないことで<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>らせてしまってすみません**でした。<br>*I'm sorry to have taken up your time with something so trivial.*

## ～てすみません · Nuance & register

**The apology ladder.** Four rungs, and they are not interchangeable:

- `～てごめん／ごめんね` - casual, friends and family. Warm.
- `～てすみません` - the everyday polite apology. Works almost everywhere.
- <code>～て<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません</code> - formal; business, customers,<br>*real mistakes.*
- <code>～て<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ございません</code> - the most formal; service<br>*industry and written apologies.*

`すみません` is the safe default. `ごめんなさい` sits between rungs one and two: it is politer than `ごめん` but has a slightly childlike, personal quality, and it is odd in a business setting. Note also that `ごめん` cannot take the past-tense trick - there is no ❌ごめんでした.

---

## ～てすみません · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Japanese apologises where English thanks.** This is the point that catches learners hardest. When someone has gone out of their way for you, Japanese often acknowledges the *trouble you caused them* rather than the *good they did you*:

- <ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていただいてすみません。
- こんなに<ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>くまで<ruby>付<rt>つ</rt></ruby>き<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わせてすみません。

Both would be "thank you" in English. `ありがとうございます` is also correct in these situations and is increasingly common among younger speakers; the `すみません` version sounds more considerate and slightly more formal. Using both together - すみません、ありがとうございます - is entirely normal.

---

## ～てすみません · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Negatives.** For an apology about something you *failed* to do, the form is `～なくてすみません`, built on the plain negative plus て: <ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>くなってすみません, <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>えなくてすみません. `～ないですみません` is not used.

**Common set phrases** worth learning whole, because their internal grammar is opaque until you have seen it:

- お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たせしてすみません - sorry to have kept you waiting (causative + humble)
- ご<ruby>迷惑<rt>めいわく</rt></ruby>をおかけしてすみません - sorry for the trouble I've caused
- <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってすみません - sorry to ask something unreasonable

---

## ～てすみません · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register: `すみません` itself is neutral, used by all speakers. `すまない` is its plain form and is male-marked and gruff. In Kansai speech `すんません` and `かんにん` turn up; both are regional and casual.

---

## ～てすみません · ～てすみません vs ～てありがとう

> **Rule of thumb** - `ありがとう` looks at what the other person gave you; `すみません` looks at what it cost them. Both can describe the same kindness.

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>って**くれてありがとう**。<br>*Thanks for driving me to the station.*

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>って**いただいてすみません**。<br>*I'm sorry to have made you drive me to the station.*

---

## ～てすみません · ～てすみません vs ～てありがとう (cont.)

Both are natural and both are grateful. The first is warmer and more direct - the normal choice between friends. The second acknowledges the imposition, which reads as more considerate and fits an unequal relationship or a genuinely large favour. Note the pairing that native speakers keep: `くれて` with thanks, because thanks point at the giver's act, and `いただいて` with an apology, because the apology points at your receiving.

---

## ～てすみません · ～てすみません vs ～て申し訳ありません

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません</code> is for real damage. If nobody was actually harmed, `すみません` is enough, and the heavier form sounds melodramatic.

ペンをお<ruby>借<rt>か</rt></ruby>りします。**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わせてもらってすみません**。<br>*Let me borrow your pen - sorry to use your things.*

こちらの<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby><ruby>不足<rt>ぶそく</rt></ruby>で**ご<ruby>迷惑<rt>めいわく</rt></ruby>をおかけして<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません**でした。<br>*I sincerely apologise for the trouble caused by our failure to check properly.*

---

## ～てすみません · ～てすみません vs ～て申し訳ありません (cont.)

In business the two also split by role: `すみません` is what you say about your own small slips, while <code><ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません</code> is what you say on behalf of your company. A customer-facing apology almost never uses `すみません`.

---

## ～てすみません · ～てすみません vs ～てしまってすみません

> **Rule of thumb** - inserting `しまって` says the act was unintended or regrettable. It makes the apology sound more sincere, and it is very common.

<ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>な<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>汚<rt>よご</rt></ruby>して**すみません**。<br>*Sorry for getting your important book dirty.*

<ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>な<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>汚<rt>よご</rt></ruby>して**しまってすみません**。<br>*I'm so sorry - I've gone and got your important book dirty.*

---

## ～てすみません · ～てすみません vs ～てしまってすみません (cont.)

`～てしまう` adds the sense of "and now it's done, and I wish it weren't". Because apologies are exactly the situation where that feeling belongs, the combination is almost formulaic: <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れてしまってすみません, <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れてしまって<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません.

---

## ～てすみません · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**ですみません**。 → ✅ <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られ**なくてすみません**。<br>*A negative reason takes `なくて`, not `ないで`. `ないで` marks manner, not cause.*

- ❌ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる**すみません**。 → ✅ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れ**てすみません**。<br>*The apology attaches to the て-form. The dictionary form cannot sit in front of `すみません`.*

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は**ごめんでした**。 → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は**ごめんね**。／<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は**すみませんでした**。<br>*`ごめん` has no past form. If you need the past, move up the ladder to `すみませんでした`.*

- ❌ お<ruby>客<rt>きゃく</rt></ruby>さま、<ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たせして**すみません**。 → ✅ お<ruby>客<rt>きゃく</rt></ruby>さま、<ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>お<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たせして**<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ございません**。<br>*To a customer, `すみません` is too light. Service register uses <code><ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ございません</code>.*

---

## ～てすみません · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていただいて**すみましたか**。 → ✅ <ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていただいて**すみません**。<br>*`すみません` is a fixed apology expression here. Do not try to conjugate it like the verb <code><ruby>済<rt>す</rt></ruby>む</code> it came from.*

---

# ～てよかった

**Meaning** · I'm glad I did that; it's a good thing that happened - relief, looking back

`～てよかった` evaluates something that has **actually happened** and finds it good. The feeling underneath is relief: it could have gone the other way, and it didn't. That backward glance is what separates it from a plain "I'm happy", and it is why the past tense `よかった` is fixed even when the good thing is still going on.

---

## ～てよかった · Formation

The て-form of the thing that happened, then `よかった` - the past of `いい／よい`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + よかった | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てよかった |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + よかった | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かなくてよかった |
| い-adjective | くて + よかった | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くてよかった |
| な-adjective | で + よかった | <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby> → <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>でよかった |
| Noun | で + よかった | いい<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby> → いい<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby>でよかった |
| Polite | ～てよかったです | <ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>ってよかったです |
| Reported | ～てよかったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う | <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってよかったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う |

---

## ～てよかった · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `よかった` is always past, even about the present or the future. <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みでよかった - "I'm glad tomorrow's a day off" - is about tomorrow, and `よかった` still cannot become `いい`. What is past is the *establishing* of the fact, not the fact itself.

> **Note** - `～なくてよかった` and `～ないでよかった` are both heard. `なくて` is the standard reason form and the safer answer on a test; `ないで` turns up in speech, especially where a deliberate choice is meant: <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないでよかった, "good thing I decided not to go."

---

## ～てよかった · Examples

**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てよかった**。<br>*I'm glad I came.*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らなくてよかった**。<br>*Good thing it didn't rain.*

この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てよかった**です。<br>*I'm glad I watched this film.*

<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってきてよかった**。<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りだしたね。<br>*Good thing I brought an umbrella - it's suddenly started pouring.*

---

## ～てよかった · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>にあったと<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きましたが、けがが**なくてよかった**ですね。<br>*I heard you were in an accident - I'm so glad you weren't hurt.*

あの<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>勇気<rt>ゆうき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>して**<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>をかけてよかった**。<br>*I'm glad I plucked up the courage to say something to her that day.*

<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>のうちに<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>っておいてよかった**と<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>になって<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う。<br>*Now that I'm older I'm glad I learned Japanese while I was still a student.*

<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>のときに<ruby>留学<rt>りゅうがく</rt></ruby>**してよかった**と、<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>き<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めてから<ruby>強<rt>つよ</rt></ruby>く<ruby>感<rt>かん</rt></ruby>じるようになった。<br>*Since starting work I've come to feel strongly that studying abroad at university was the right call.*

---

## ～てよかった · Nuance & register

**The relief is the point.** `～てよかった` implies an alternative that would have been worse. That is why <ruby>生<rt>う</rt></ruby>まれてよかった sounds strange out of context - nothing was at stake - while <ruby>生<rt>い</rt></ruby>きていてよかった is powerful. If you are simply reporting that something nice occurred, with no sense of a near miss, the pattern is a poor fit.

**Uses split three ways**, and learners usually only meet the first:

---

## ～てよかった · Nuance & register (cont.)

1. **Relief about your own past choice.** <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めに<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>てよかった. 2. **Relief about someone else's situation.** <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ってよかったですね - said *to* the person, with `ね`. This is how Japanese expresses "thank goodness" on someone else's behalf, and the `ね` is close to obligatory. 3. **Relief that something did *not* happen.** <ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>地震<rt>じしん</rt></ruby>じゃなくてよかった.

**Register.** Plain `よかった` is casual and extremely common as a one-word reaction - a friend tells you their exam went well and you say ああ、よかった. `よかったです` is polite and fine in ordinary conversation, though in careful or business speech people often prefer <code>よかったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code> or <code><ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>よりです</code>（ご<ruby>無事<rt>ぶじ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>よりです）, because a bare emotional `よかったです` can sound a little bald upward.

---

## ～てよかった · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Emphasis** is done with <code><ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>に</code>, `つくづく`, or by stretching the vowel in speech: <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てよかったあ. The written-out long vowel is casual.

`～てよかった` also lives inside larger structures: <code>～てよかったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う</code> (reporting your own judgement, common in writing), `～てよかったですね` (to someone else), and `～てよかったのに`, which flips the meaning to "you needn't have" - <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なくてよかったのに, "you didn't have to come all this way." The `のに` does that work, not the `よかった`.

---

## ～てよかった · ～てよかった vs ～ばよかった

> **Rule of thumb** - `～てよかった` looks at what you **did** and approves. `～ばよかった` looks at what you **didn't** and regrets it.

This is the pair that matters most, because they sit one letter apart in a learner's head and mean opposite things.

<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>してよかった**。<br>*I'm glad I booked early.*

<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>すればよかった**。<br>*I should have booked early.*

---

## ～てよかった · ～てよかった vs ～ばよかった (cont.)

The first is a satisfied look back at a real action; the second is regret about an action that never happened. `～たらよかった` and `～ておけばよかった` work the same way. Note the negatives, which double the confusion: <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かなくてよかった is relief that you stayed home, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かなければよかった is regret that you went.

---

## ～てよかった · ～てよかった vs ～てうれしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `うれしい` reports the emotion; `よかった` delivers a verdict on the event. `よかった` carries relief, `うれしい` carries joy.

<ruby>合格<rt>ごうかく</rt></ruby>できて**うれしい**です。<br>*I'm happy I passed.*

<ruby>合格<rt>ごうかく</rt></ruby>できて**よかった**です。<br>*I'm relieved I passed.*

---

## ～てよかった · ～てよかった vs ～てうれしい (cont.)

Both are natural here, because passing an exam is both a pleasure and a narrow escape. They come apart at the edges. Pure good news takes `うれしい` - プレゼントをもらってうれしい, where nothing bad was ever in prospect. Averted disaster takes `よかった` - <ruby>財布<rt>さいふ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>つかってよかった, where "うれしい" would understate the panic that preceded it. And only `よかった` works for a negative event: ❌<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らなくてうれしい is odd.

---

## ～てよかった · ～てよかった vs ～て助かった

> **Rule of thumb** - `よかった` evaluates the situation; <code><ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かった</code> says *you* personally were rescued from trouble by it. <code><ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かった</code> needs a helper, usually a person.

<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>空<rt>す</rt></ruby>いていて**よかった**。<br>*Good thing the roads were clear.*

<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくださって**<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かりました**。<br>*You really saved me by helping out.*

---

## ～てよかった · ～てよかった vs ～て助かった (cont.)

Because <code><ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かる</code> points at a rescuer, it doubles as thanks and is one of the most useful phrases in working Japanese - <ruby>代<rt>か</rt></ruby>わってくれて<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かった. `よかった` cannot do that job: saying <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくださってよかったです to the person who helped sounds like you are grading their performance rather than thanking them.

---

## ～てよかった · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>て**いいです**。（meaning "I'm glad I came"）→ ✅ <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>て**よかったです**。<br>*The pattern is fixed in the past. `いい` here would be read as permission - "you may come."*

- ❌ <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>**すればよかった**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。（meaning "I'm glad I studied"）→ ✅ <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>**してよかった**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*`～ばよかった` is regret about what you did *not* do. It reverses your meaning completely.*

- ❌ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**くてよかった**。 → ✅ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**でよかった**。<br>*<code><ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby></code> is a な-adjective, so it connects with `で`. Only い-adjectives take `くて`.*

---

## ～てよかった · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れ**ないでよかったです**ね。（to someone whose train was on time）→ ✅ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れ**なくてよかったです**ね。<br>*For a fact rather than a decision, `なくて` is the natural reason form. `ないで` suggests they chose not to be late.*

- ❌ プレゼントをもらって**よかった**。（just pleased）→ ✅ プレゼントをもらって**うれしい**。<br>*Not ungrammatical, but `よかった` implies you had feared not getting one, which is not usually what you mean.*

---

{reveal}
<ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なく**てよかった**。

The negative reason form is <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なくて</code>, and `よかった` supplies the relief: the trip could easily have been cancelled, and wasn't. That averted alternative is exactly what the pattern is for.

## ～てよかった · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>えてうれしいです。 (b) <ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>えてよかったです。

{reveal}
(a) reports emotion: meeting you makes me happy. It is the standard line on being introduced to someone, and it says nothing about whether the meeting was in doubt.

{reveal}
(b) delivers a verdict with relief behind it: I'm glad the meeting happened. It fits when there was a real chance it wouldn't - you almost missed each other, the train was late, you had been trying to arrange it for months. Said at a first introduction with no such backstory, it can sound as though you had been dreading the encounter.
