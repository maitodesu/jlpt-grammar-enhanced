# より

**Meaning** · than - it marks the side of a comparison that comes off worse

`より` is a postposition: it goes **after** the noun that loses. Nothing else in the sentence changes, because Japanese adjectives have no comparative ending - <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い covers both "cheap" and "cheaper", and `より` is what tells you which one is meant. It also has a second, unrelated life as a stiff written stand-in for `から`, "from".

---

## より · Formation

`より` attaches to whatever names the standard you are measuring against. That standard does not have to be another object: it can be a moment in time, an expectation, or a number.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + より | <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby> → <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>より<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い |
| Verb (plain form) | V(plain) + より | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つより<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>い |
| An expectation | <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った／<ruby>予想<rt>よそう</rt></ruby>した + より | <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったより<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い |
| A habitual standard | いつも／<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>／<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby> + より | いつもより<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きた |
| A number | Number + より + <ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>い／<ruby>少<rt>すく</rt></ruby>ない | <ruby>百人<rt>ひゃくにん</rt></ruby>より<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>い |
| With emphasis | ～よりも | <ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>よりも<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い |
| Written "from" (= から) | Time / place + より | <ruby>九時<rt>くじ</rt></ruby>より<ruby>開始<rt>かいし</rt></ruby>します |

---

## より · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `より` compares exactly **two** things. The moment there are three or more, Japanese switches to the superlative frame with <code><ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で</code> and `いちばん`; the two constructions never combine in one clause.

The winner half of the comparison is optional. <code><ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>より<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いです</code> is a complete sentence - the thing being measured is simply the topic, stated or understood.

---

## より · Examples

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>はあの<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*This dictionary is heavier than that one.*

<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った**より**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>少<rt>すく</rt></ruby>なかったです。<br>*There were fewer people than I expected.*

<ruby>今年<rt>ことし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>冬<rt>ふゆ</rt></ruby>はいつも**より**<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*There's more snow than usual this winter.*

<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>する**より**、メールを<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>る<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>楽<rt>らく</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Sending an email is easier than phoning.*

---

## より · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>五<rt>ご</rt></ruby>センチも<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くなりました。<br>*My little brother has grown a whole five centimetres since last year.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>ホールで<ruby>行<rt>おこな</rt></ruby>います。<br>*The meeting will be held from three o'clock in the main hall.*

この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>の<ruby>冬<rt>ふゆ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われています。<br>*They say the winters in this town are colder than in Hokkaido.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいアパートは<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>から<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>いですが、<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>のところ**より**<ruby>家賃<rt>やちん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くて<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かです。<br>*The new flat is far from the station, but the rent is lower than my old place and it's quiet.*

---

## より · Nuance & register

The comparative `より` is register-neutral. It is the same word in <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いです and <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い, in conversation and in a report, and nothing about it is marked for age or gender.

`よりも` is the emphatic form - "than that, *definitely*". It is common in both speech and writing and costs nothing to use: <ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>よりも<ruby>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>強<rt>つよ</rt></ruby>い. There is also a casual `よりか` heard in relaxed speech; recognise it, but keep it out of writing and out of anything you are graded on.

Two expressions are worth learning whole, because native speakers reach for them constantly:

---

## より · Nuance & register (cont.)

- <code><ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったより</code> - "more/less … than I thought". Always the **past**<br>*form <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った, even when you are talking about right now.*
- <code><ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>より</code> - "above all else".<br>*お<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>そうで<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>よりです is a set greeting meaning "I'm glad above all to see you well".*

The "from" `より` is a different animal. It is written, formal and slightly old-fashioned in feel: timetables, invitations, event notices, price tags （<ruby>千円<rt>せんえん</rt></ruby>より, "from ¥1,000"）, and the sign-off of a letter （<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>より, "from Tanaka"）. Nobody says it to a friend. If you are speaking, use `から`.

---

## より · より vs ～の方が

> **Rule of thumb** - these are not rivals; they are the two ends of one comparison. `より` goes on the loser, <code>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が</code> on the winner. Either half can stand alone, but `より` can never mark the winner.

<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>地下鉄<rt>ちかてつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*The subway is faster than a bicycle.*

Drop either half and the sentence still works, because context supplies the rest:

<ruby>地下鉄<rt>ちかてつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*The subway is faster.*

---

## より · より vs ～の方が (cont.)

<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*It's faster than a bicycle.*

What you cannot do is put `より` on the thing that wins. ❌<code><ruby>地下鉄<rt>ちかてつ</rt></ruby>より<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いのは<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>です</code> reverses the meaning: it now says the bicycle is the faster one.

---

## より · より（＝から）vs から

> **Rule of thumb** - where they overlap, `より` is the formal written form and `から` the spoken one. But `から` covers ground `より` never touches.

<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>午前<rt>ごぜん</rt></ruby><ruby>十時<rt>じゅうじ</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まります。<br>*Reception opens at 10 a.m. (printed on a notice)*

<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>十時<rt>じゅうじ</rt></ruby>**から**だって。<br>*Apparently reception starts at ten. (said to a friend)*

`から` also means "because" and marks the material something is made of; <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いから<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります cannot become ❌<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いより<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります. The overlap is only the starting-point sense.

---

## より · より vs もっと

> **Rule of thumb** - `より` names what you are comparing against; `もっと` refuses to.

もっと<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*Please speak louder.*

さっき**より**<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*Please speak louder than you were a moment ago.*

`もっと` is a bare "more" - one step further up the scale from wherever we are now. `より` demands a stated benchmark. That is why the two do not stack: with `より` already in the sentence, `もっと` is redundant.

---

## より · Common mistakes

- ❌ より<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby><ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いです。 → ✅ <ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*English puts "than" in front of its noun; Japanese puts `より` behind it. Word order is the whole grammar here.*

- ❌ <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う**より**<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しかったです。 → ✅ <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った**より**<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しかったです。<br>*The fixed expression takes the plain past <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った. The thought came first, then reality contradicted it.*

- ❌ <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>より<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>がもっと<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>より<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*`より` already supplies "more". Adding `もっと` says it twice.*

- ❌ <ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>より<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>こうよ。 → ✅ <ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>こうよ。<br>*The "from" `より` belongs on printed notices. In casual speech it sounds like you are reading an announcement aloud.*

---

## より · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>____<ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がりました。

{reveal}
<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がりました。

{reveal}
The standard being measured against is last year, and it is the side that comes off lower, so it takes `より`. Note that the benchmark here is a point in time, not another object - that is completely normal for `より`.

---

## より · Practice 2

**2.** Choose the natural particle, said to a friend over lunch: コンサートは<ruby>七時<rt>しちじ</rt></ruby>（より・から）<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まるよ。

{reveal}
コンサートは<ruby>七時<rt>しちじ</rt></ruby>**から**<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まるよ。

{reveal}
Both words can mark a starting time, but the `より` version belongs to a printed programme. Saying it to a friend, with the casual ending `よ`, mixes two registers that do not go together.

---

## より · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This bag is lighter than it looks."

{reveal}
このかばんは<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>た<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>いです。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>た<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby></code> ("appearance") is the benchmark, so it takes `より`, and <ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>い stays in its ordinary form - there is no separate comparative to conjugate into. <code><ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったより<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>いです</code> is also fine and shifts the benchmark from how it looks to what you had assumed.

---

## より · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: ぼくは<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>よりもっと<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いです。

{reveal}
✅ ぼくは<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いです。

{reveal}
Delete `もっと`. The comparison is already carried by `より`; `もっと` is a free-standing "more" for when no benchmark is named, so putting both in one clause doubles the same idea. If you want extra force, use `よりも` instead.

---

## より · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) もっと<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かな<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>がいいです。 (b) この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>より<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かな<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>がいいです。

{reveal}
(a) asks for a quieter room without saying quieter than what - it works anywhere, including before you have seen a single room, and it is the softer request.

{reveal}
(b) pins the comparison to the room you are standing in. It is a complaint with a measurement attached, and the desk clerk now knows exactly which room failed. The benchmark is what `より` adds.

# ～の中で

**Meaning** · in; among - it fences off the set you are choosing or ranking within

<code>～の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で</code> names the field. Everything that follows it - a superlative, a second place, an exception, a question word - is understood to apply *inside that fence and nowhere else*. Literally "in the middle of ～", but the set does not have to be a physical container: friends, seasons, films you have watched all work.

---

## ～の中で · Formation

The pattern is a noun (<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>) plus the particle `で`, so anything that can modify a noun can sit in front of it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a set) | Noun + の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で | クラス → クラスの<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で |
| A spelled-out set | AとBとCの<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で | <ruby>米<rt>こめ</rt></ruby>とパンと<ruby>麺<rt>めん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で |
| Demonstrative | この／その／あの + <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で（no の） | この<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で |
| Noun-modifying clause | Clause + Noun + の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んだ<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で |
| Topicalised | ～の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>では | スポーツの<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>では |
| Place or time span | Place／span + で（**no** <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>） | <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>で／<ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>で |

---

## ～の中で · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - <code>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で</code> is only half a sentence. It has to be followed by something that picks a member out: `いちばん`, <code><ruby>二番目<rt>にばんめ</rt></ruby>に</code>, `だけ`, <code><ruby>特<rt>とく</rt></ruby>に</code>, or a question word like `どれ` / `だれ` / `どこ`.

> **Note** - a demonstrative already contains the "this set" idea, so it takes <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> directly: この<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で, never ❌このの<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で.

---

## ～の中で · Examples

この<ruby>四<rt>よっ</rt></ruby>つ**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で**、どれがいいですか。<br>*Which of these four would you like?*

<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で**、トマトだけ<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Tomatoes are the one vegetable I can't handle.*

<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>都市<rt>とし</rt></ruby>**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>二番目<rt>にばんめ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*Of Japan's cities, Kyoto is my second favourite.*

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>だけが<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*She's the only one of my friends who knows the truth.*

---

## ～の中で · Examples (cont.)

スポーツ**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>では**、<ruby>特<rt>とく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>水泳<rt>すいえい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Among sports, swimming is what I'm especially good at.*

<ruby>世界<rt>せかい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>国<rt>くに</rt></ruby>**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってみたいのはアイスランドです。<br>*Of all the countries in the world, the one I'd like to visit once is Iceland.*

<ruby>今学期<rt>こんがっき</rt></ruby><ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>った<ruby>文法<rt>ぶんぽう</rt></ruby>**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で**、いちばん<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しかったのは<ruby>敬語<rt>けいご</rt></ruby>でした。<br>*Of the grammar we studied this term, the hardest was the honorific language.*

<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じチームのメンバー**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>いちばん<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るのは<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Out of everyone on the team, the one who gets in earliest is the department head.*

---

## ～の中で · Nuance & register

Register-neutral and equally at home in speech and writing. <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> is often written in kanji and often in kana; neither spelling changes the reading or the formality.

Two things do vary. First, with a **group of members** the <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> is frequently dropped in speech: クラスでいちばん<ruby>足<rt>あし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>い says the same thing as クラスの<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>でいちばん<ruby>足<rt>あし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>い, a little more briskly. Keeping <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> makes the boundary of the set explicit, which is why it survives in careful speech and in writing.

---

## ～の中で · Nuance & register (cont.)

Second, with a **place or a time span** the <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> is not merely optional - it is out of place. <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>で, <ruby>世界<rt>せかい</rt></ruby>で, <ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>で, クラスで are locations and stretches of time, not bags of items, so there is no "inside the set" to point at. <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で is not ungrammatical, but it reads as though you meant physically within Japan's borders, and most speakers would just say <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>で.

Adding `は` - <code>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>では</code> - topicalises the field and quietly implies a contrast: "*within this set* it's true; elsewhere, who knows." <ruby>和食<rt>わしょく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>ではおすしがいちばん<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです leaves room for some non-Japanese dish to be the overall winner.

---

## ～の中で · ～の中で vs bare で

> **Rule of thumb** - a set of members takes <code>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で</code>; a place or a period takes plain `で`.

<ruby>家族<rt>かぞく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>がいちばんよく<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>います。<br>*My father laughs the most of anyone in the family.*

この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>で<ruby>一番<rt>いちばん</rt></ruby><ruby>古<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>いお<ruby>寺<rt>てら</rt></ruby>はどこですか。<br>*Which is the oldest temple in this town?*

A family is a collection of individuals you can line up and compare - the fence is real, so <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> earns its place. A town is where the comparing happens, not what is being compared, and inserting <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> there points at geography instead of at a set.

---

## ～の中で · ～の中で vs ～の中から

> **Rule of thumb** - `で` compares within the set; `から` takes something out of it.

この<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で、いちばん<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>いのはどれですか。<br>*Which of these is the lightest?*

この<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>から、<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きなものを<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>んでください。<br>*Please choose one thing you like from these.*

Verbs of choosing and extracting - <ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>ぶ, <ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>り<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>す, <ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>める - pull the item out, so they want `から`. Adjectival judgements leave everything where it is, so they want `で`. Using <code><ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>ぶ</code> is not impossible, but it shifts the meaning to "do your choosing inside this room".

---

## ～の中で · ～の中で vs より

> **Rule of thumb** - three or more members: <code>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で</code> plus a superlative. Exactly two: `より`.

この<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby><ruby>種類<rt>しゅるい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で、これがいちばん<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*Of these three varieties, this one is the sweetest.*

こちらのほうがあちら**より**<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*This one is sweeter than that one.*

The mistake to avoid is running a two-item set through the superlative frame. <ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>と<ruby>緑茶<rt>りょくちゃ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>でどちらがいいですか is overbuilt - with two candidates on the table, drop the fence and just ask <ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>と<ruby>緑茶<rt>りょくちゃ</rt></ruby>と、どちらがいいですか.

---

## ～の中で · Common mistakes

- ❌ この**の**<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で → ✅ この<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で<br>*この, その, あの already attach to the following noun. Doubling the `の` is the single most frequent slip on this pattern.*

- ❌ <ruby>果物<rt>くだもの</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>**に**みかんがいちばん<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ <ruby>果物<rt>くだもの</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>**で**みかんがいちばん<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*`に` would say the tangerines are physically located inside the fruit. `で` is the particle that turns a set into an arena for comparison.*

- ❌ <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>と<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>がいちばん<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>と<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>と、<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>のほうが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*Two candidates cannot have a "number one". A superlative needs a field of at least three.*

---

## ～の中で · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>世界<rt>せかい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>でこの<ruby>湖<rt>みずうみ</rt></ruby>がいちばん<ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>いです。 → ✅ <ruby>世界<rt>せかい</rt></ruby>でこの<ruby>湖<rt>みずうみ</rt></ruby>がいちばん<ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*<ruby>世界<rt>せかい</rt></ruby>, <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>, <ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby> and the like are places. Attaching <ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby> to them sounds like you are drawing a line on a map.*

---

## ～の中で · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>____<ruby>二月<rt>にがつ</rt></ruby>がいちばん<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いです。

{reveal}
<ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>**で**<ruby>二月<rt>にがつ</rt></ruby>がいちばん<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いです。

{reveal}
A year is a stretch of time, not a bag of members, so it takes plain `で`. <ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で is heard, but it makes the year sound like a container you are rummaging in; the bare `で` is what a native speaker says.

---

## ～の中で · Practice 2

**2.** Choose the natural option: <ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby>つのプラン（の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で・の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>から）<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>んでください。

{reveal}
<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby>つのプラン**の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>から**<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>んでください。

{reveal}
<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>ぶ physically removes one item from the group, and the source of a movement is `から`. Use <code>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で</code> when nothing is being taken out - when you are only judging which member wins.

{reveal}

{reveal}
<code><ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>た</code> is a clause modifying <ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>, and the whole noun phrase then takes <code>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で</code>. Note that <ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby> is plural here without any marking - Japanese leaves number to context, and the fence does the work of "of the films".

{reveal}

# AとBとどちらが

**Meaning** · which is more ～, A or B? - the question form of a two-way comparison

You already know how to *state* a comparison. This is how to *ask* for one. Put the two candidates side by side with `と`, then let `どちら` stand in for the answer. The reply comes back with <code>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が</code>, which is why the two patterns are really one conversation split in half.

---

## AとBとどちらが · Formation

The frame is fixed: **A と B と、どちらが + adjective + か**. The second `と` is usually there and usually followed by a comma when written.

---

## AとBとどちらが · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two nouns | AとBと、どちらが | <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>と<ruby>秋<rt>あき</rt></ruby>と、どちらが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか |
| Two verbs | Vるのと Vるのと、どちらが | <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>くのと<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>るのと、どちらが |
| Two い-adjective options | ～いのと～いのと | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いのと<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>いのと |
| With <ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby> spelled out | どちらの<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が | どちらの<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>いですか |
| Casual | どっち（の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>）が | どっちが<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>い? |
| The answer | Bの<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が + adjective + です | <ruby>秋<rt>あき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです |
| Answering "both" | どちらも + affirmative | どちらも<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです |
| Answering "neither" | どちらも + negative | どちらも<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きではありません |

---

## AとBとどちらが · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - with exactly **two** candidates the question word is `どちら`, whatever they are. Not `どれ` for things, not `だれ` for people, not `どこ` for places. Two means `どちら`.

> **Note** - `どちら` takes `が`, never `は`. It is the unknown in the sentence, and unknowns sit on the `が` side. The same applies to the answer: <code><ruby>秋<rt>あき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が</code>.

---

## AとBとどちらが · Examples

コーヒーと<ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>と、**どちらが**いいですか。<br>*Which would you like, coffee or tea?*

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>と<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>と、**どちらが**<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいですか。<br>*Which is busier for you, mornings or evenings?*

<ruby>新幹線<rt>しんかんせん</rt></ruby>と<ruby>飛行機<rt>ひこうき</rt></ruby>と、**どちらが**<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いですか。<br>*Which is cheaper, the bullet train or flying?*

この<ruby>二<rt>ふた</rt></ruby>つ、**どっちが**<ruby>似合<rt>にあ</rt></ruby>うと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う?<br>*Which of these two do you think suits me better?*

---

## AとBとどちらが · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>るのと<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うのと、**どちらが**<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いでしょうか。<br>*Which would be quicker, making it myself or buying it in a shop?*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんと<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんと、**どちらが**<ruby>先<rt>さき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>きましたか。<br>*Which of them arrived first, Tanaka or Yamada?*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>水曜日<rt>すいようび</rt></ruby>と<ruby>木曜日<rt>もくようび</rt></ruby>と、**どちらが**ご<ruby>都合<rt>つごう</rt></ruby>がよろしいですか。<br>*For the meeting, which suits you better - Wednesday or Thursday?*

<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>すなら、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>くと<ruby>公園<rt>こうえん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>くと、**どちらが**いいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いますか。<br>*If you were moving, which do you think would be better - near the station or near the park?*

---

## AとBとどちらが · Nuance & register

`どちら` is the neutral-to-polite form and works everywhere: with strangers, with customers, in writing. `どっち` is its casual contraction and is extremely common in relaxed speech among friends and family. There is nothing rude about `どっち` between equals, but it lands badly in a shop or an office.

Adding <code>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby></code> - どちらの<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が - is slightly more deliberate and a shade more formal. It is never wrong, and it is what you hear when someone is choosing their words carefully.

`どちら` has a second job worth knowing: it is the **polite word for "where" and "which one"** in general. どちらからいらっしゃいましたか means "where have you come from", and どちらさまですか is a formal "who are you?". So when you hear `どちら`, check whether two options were actually put on the table.

---

## AとBとどちらが · Nuance & register (cont.)

Politeness at the top end: いかがですか or よろしいですか replace いいですか, and でしょうか softens the question further. To a customer, a shop assistant is more likely to say どちらになさいますか than どちらがいいですか.

One thing English speakers forget: this is **not** a yes/no question. Answering はい to どちらがいいですか leaves the other person waiting.

---

## AとBとどちらが · どちらが vs どれが

> **Rule of thumb** - two candidates: `どちら`. Three or more: `どれ`.

<ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>と<ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>と、**どちらが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。<br>*Which do you prefer, red or blue?*

この<ruby>五<rt>いつ</rt></ruby>つの<ruby>色<rt>いろ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で、**どれが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。<br>*Of these five colours, which do you like?*

The split is about how many options exist, not about what kind of thing they are. This is also why the two patterns come with different frames: `どれ` normally needs a stated field with <code>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>で</code>, while `どちら` carries its own field in the AとBと part.

---

## AとBとどちらが · どちらが vs どっちが

> **Rule of thumb** - same grammar, different clothes. `どっち` is the casual form; `どちら` is the one to default to when you are unsure.

ねえ、**どっちが**おいしそう?<br>*Hey, which one looks better?*

お<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>は、**どちらが**よろしいでしょうか。<br>*Which would you prefer to drink?*

Notice that the register is carried by the *whole* sentence, not just the question word. どっち sits with plain forms and a rising intonation; どちら sits with です・ます, よろしい and でしょうか. Mixing them - ❌どっちがよろしいでしょうか - sounds like two people talking at once.

---

## AとBとどちらが · どちらが vs どちらか・どちらも

> **Rule of thumb** - `どちらが` asks which one. `どちらか` means "one or the other, I don't mind which". `どちらも` means "both" with an affirmative verb and "neither" with a negative one.

<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>は、**どちらが**<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>いですか。<br>*Which umbrella is lighter?*

<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>は**どちらか**<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていってください。<br>*Take one umbrella or the other with you.*

<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>は**どちらも**<ruby>古<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*Both umbrellas are old.*

---

## AとBとどちらが · どちらが vs どちらか・どちらも (cont.)

`か` and `も` are doing exactly what they do after `だれ` and `なに`: `か` makes the identity vague, `も` sweeps in everything. The negative flip on `どちらも` is automatic - どちらも<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しくないです is "neither is new".

---

## AとBとどちらが · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>と<ruby>緑茶<rt>りょくちゃ</rt></ruby>と、どれが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。 → ✅ <ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>と<ruby>緑茶<rt>りょくちゃ</rt></ruby>と、**どちらが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。<br>*Only two things were named. `どれ` needs a field of three or more.*

- ❌ <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>と<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>と、だれが<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いですか。 → ✅ <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>と<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>と、**どちらが**<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いですか。<br>*Even for people, two options means `どちら`. `だれ` opens the field to everybody.*

- ❌ どちら**は**<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いですか。 → ✅ どちら**が**<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いですか。<br>*A question word is the unknown, and it cannot be a topic. `どちらが`, always.*

- ❌ 「どちらが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。」「はい、コーヒーです。」 → ✅ 「コーヒーの<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。」<br>*This is a choice question, not a yes/no one. The answer names a winner, normally with <code>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が</code>.*

---

## AとBとどちらが · Practice 1

**1.** バスと<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>と、____が<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>ですか。

{reveal}
バスと<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>と、**どちら**が<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>ですか。

{reveal}
Two candidates have been put on the table, so the question word is `どちら`, and it takes `が` because it stands for the unknown. In casual speech the same sentence becomes バスと<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>、どっちが<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>?

## AとBとどちらが · Practice 2

**2.** Choose the natural option, spoken to a client: お<ruby>支払<rt>しはら</rt></ruby>いは<ruby>現金<rt>げんきん</rt></ruby>とカードと、（どっち・どちら）がよろしいですか。

{reveal}

{reveal}
The rest of the sentence is fully polite - お<ruby>支払<rt>しはら</rt></ruby>い, よろしい - so the casual `どっち` would clash with it. Register has to be consistent across the whole sentence, not just at the end.

## AとBとどちらが · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Which is harder, reading or listening?"

{reveal}

{reveal}
Verbs cannot be joined by `と` directly, so each one is nominalised with `の` first. <code><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むこと</code> and <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>くこと</code> also work and sound slightly more written.

## AとBとどちらが · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>と<ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>と、どちらは<ruby>人口<rt>じんこう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>いですか。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>と<ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>と、**どちらが**<ruby>人口<rt>じんこう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>いですか。

{reveal}
`どちらは` is impossible: `は` presents old, settled information, and a question word is by definition the thing nobody has settled yet. Two `が` in one sentence looks odd to an English speaker but is entirely normal here - the first marks the unknown, the second marks what is numerous.

## AとBとどちらが · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>和食<rt>わしょく</rt></ruby>と<ruby>洋食<rt>ようしょく</rt></ruby>と、どちらが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。 (b) <ruby>和食<rt>わしょく</rt></ruby>と<ruby>洋食<rt>ようしょく</rt></ruby>と、どちらも<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。

{reveal}
(a) assumes you have a preference and asks which way it falls. It cannot be answered with はい - the reply names a winner, normally <ruby>和食<rt>わしょく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです.

{reveal}
(b) sweeps both options in at once and asks whether you like them equally. It **is** a yes/no question, and はい、どちらも<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです is a complete answer. `が` singles one out; `も` refuses to.

# など

**Meaning** · such as; things like - it declares a list unfinished

`など` is a full stop that refuses to be one. You name one or two items, add `など`, and the listener understands that more of the same kind exist but are not worth spelling out. Its second use grows straight out of the first: if you present something as a mere example, you are also saying it is nothing special - which is how `など` becomes modest about yourself and dismissive about everything else.

---

## など · Formation

`など` follows the **last** item of the list. The case particle for the whole phrase then follows `など`.

---

## など · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + など | ペン → ペンなど |
| A や-list | AやB + など | パンや<ruby>卵<rt>たまご</rt></ruby>など |
| A single example | Noun + など | お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>など |
| Before a case particle | ～など + が／を／に／で | <ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>などを<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う |
| Quoted words | ～などと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う／<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う | <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>だなどと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う |
| Dismissive / humble | Noun + など + deprecating predicate | お<ruby>礼<rt>れい</rt></ruby>など<ruby>要<rt>い</rt></ruby>りません |
| Casual equivalent | なんか | ケーキなんか |
| Emphatic equivalent | なんて | <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>なんて |

---

## など · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `や` *joins* items; `など` *closes* the list. They are not alternatives, and the two together - AやBなど - is the most common shape of all.

> **Note** - `など` attaches to nouns. To leave a list of **actions** open, use `～とか～とか` or the `～たり～たり` frame instead; ❌<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>るなど<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ is not a sentence. In writing you will meet ～などする （<ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>するなどして）, but that is a formal construction, not everyday speech.

---

## など · Examples

かばんの<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>財布<rt>さいふ</rt></ruby>や<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>**など**が<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*There's a wallet, keys and so on in the bag.*

<ruby>昼<rt>ひる</rt></ruby>ごはんはおにぎり**など**でいいです。<br>*Rice balls or something will do for lunch.*

この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>にはコンビニや<ruby>郵便局<rt>ゆうびんきょく</rt></ruby>**など**があって<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>です。<br>*There's a convenience store, a post office and the like around here, so it's handy.*

お<ruby>礼<rt>れい</rt></ruby>**など**<ruby>要<rt>い</rt></ruby>りませんよ。<br>*There's really no need to thank me.*

---

## など · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**など**にはとても<ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Someone like me could never manage that.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>では<ruby>予算<rt>よさん</rt></ruby>や<ruby>日程<rt>にってい</rt></ruby>**など**について<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*At the meeting we discussed the budget, the schedule and so forth.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は「もう<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>けられない」**など**と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていました。<br>*He was saying things like "I can't keep this up any more".*

<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くときは、パスポートや<ruby>保険証<rt>ほけんしょう</rt></ruby>**など**を<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れないようにしてください。<br>*When you travel, try not to forget your passport, insurance card and the like.*

---

## など · Nuance & register

In its plain listing sense `など` is neutral, and if it leans anywhere it leans **written and careful**. It is the form you want in a report, an announcement or a polite explanation to a customer.

Its two informal twins are worth keeping straight:

- **なんか** is the everyday spoken form. Same meaning, no formality.<br>*<ruby>漫画<rt>まんが</rt></ruby>なんか<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まないよ. Very frequent, completely ordinary in conversation, out of place in writing.*
- **なんて** adds feeling - surprise, contempt, or dismissal.<br>*<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>なんて<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>だ is boastful; <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>なんて is self-deprecating. It is not a neutral option.*

---

## など · Nuance & register (cont.)

The **softening** use is one you will hear constantly and rarely see taught: offering something as a mere example makes the offer less pushy. コーヒーなどいかがですか is warmer and less presumptuous than コーヒーはいかがですか, because it pretends not to have decided anything for you.

The dismissive use cuts both ways, and the direction depends on **who** the noun refers to. Pointed at yourself it is humble and perfectly polite: <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>などが<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>げることではありませんが. Pointed at someone else it belittles them, so ❌<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんなどに<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いてみます, said in front of Tanaka, is an insult you did not intend.

---

## など · など vs や

> **Rule of thumb** - `や` sits **between** two nouns and links them. `など` sits **after** the last noun and stops the list without finishing it.

<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>にノート**や**はさみがあります。<br>*There's a notebook, scissors and so on on the desk.*

<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>にノートやはさみ**など**があります。<br>*There's a notebook, scissors and that sort of thing on the desk.*

`や` already implies the list is partial, so `など` is not strictly required - it just makes the implication explicit and a little more polite. What you cannot do is swap their positions: ❌ノートなどはさみ joins nothing.

---

## など · など vs なんか・なんて

> **Rule of thumb** - one meaning, three registers: `など` written and neutral, `なんか` casual and spoken, `なんて` emotional.

<ruby>報告書<rt>ほうこくしょ</rt></ruby>には<ruby>人数<rt>にんずう</rt></ruby>や<ruby>費用<rt>ひよう</rt></ruby>**など**を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*In the report we write the number of people, the cost and so on.*

そんなこと、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**なんか**に<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>かないでよ。<br>*Don't ask me about that, of all people.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**なんて**、あぶないよ。<br>*Going alone - that's dangerous.*

---

## など · など vs なんか・なんて (cont.)

Note the third one: `なんて` will attach to a whole verb phrase, which `など` resists in speech. `なんて` also tends to run straight into the predicate without a case particle behind it.

---

## など · など vs とか

> **Rule of thumb** - `とか` is repeated after **each** item and is casual; `など` appears **once** at the end and is the neutral or written choice.

<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>**とか**<ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>**とか**しています。<br>*At weekends I go shopping, take walks, that sort of thing.*

<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>や<ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>**など**をしています。<br>*At weekends I do things like shopping and going for walks.*

Both say the same thing about the world. The difference is who is speaking: the first is a friend over coffee, the second could go in a self-introduction. `とか` also attaches happily to verbs and whole clauses, where `など` has to be helped along by a noun.

---

## など · Common mistakes

- ❌ パン**など**<ruby>卵<rt>たまご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。 → ✅ パン**や**<ruby>卵<rt>たまご</rt></ruby>**など**を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*`など` is not a conjunction. It cannot stand between two nouns; it can only close the list from behind.*

- ❌ <ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>**を**など<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。 → ✅ <ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>**など**を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。<br>*The case particle goes **outside** `など`, because `など` belongs to the noun phrase and `を` belongs to the sentence.*

- ❌ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>**など**にご<ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>します。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>にご<ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>します。<br>*Attaching `など` to a person you are showing respect to reduces them to a random example. Keep the dismissive `など` for yourself.*

---

## など · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>には<ruby>住所<rt>じゅうしょ</rt></ruby>や<ruby>電話番号<rt>でんわばんごう</rt></ruby>**なんか**が<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてあります。 → ✅ ～<ruby>電話番号<rt>でんわばんごう</rt></ruby>**など**が<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてあります。<br>*Not ungrammatical, but `なんか` is spoken register and jars against a written document. Match the word to the medium.*

# ～とか～とか

**Meaning** · things like A and B - a casual, deliberately incomplete list

`とか` tags each item as *one example among others*. Two or three go on the record, the rest stay implied, and the whole thing sounds relaxed rather than official. Unlike the tidier `や`, it will happily list verbs, adjectives and whole complaints, which is why spoken Japanese leans on it so heavily.

---

## ～とか～とか · Formation

`とか` follows **each** item, including - usually - the last one.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Nとか Nとか | すし → すしとかラーメンとか |
| Verb (plain form) | Vるとか Vるとか | <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>くとか<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>るとか |
| Verb (past) | Vたとか Vたとか | <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れたとか<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたとか |
| い-adjective | ～いとか | <ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>いとか<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いとか |
| な-adjective / noun | ～だとか | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだとか<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>だとか |
| A single item | Nとか（one only） | <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>とか、どう? |
| Before a case particle | ～とか + が／を／に | パンとかを<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う（often dropped） |

---

## ～とか～とか · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the final `とか` is optional but normal. Leaving it on is what makes the list feel open; cutting it off after the last item makes the sentence sound as though you had planned it.

> **Note** - with な-adjectives and nouns the standard written form inserts `だ`: <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだとか. In fast speech you will hear the bare <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かとか as well; treat `だとか` as the safe form.

---

## ～とか～とか · Examples

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>はパン**とか**ヨーグルト**とか**を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます。<br>*For breakfast I have bread, yoghurt, that sort of thing.*

<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>はジャズ**とか**クラシック**とか**が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*Music-wise I like jazz, classical and so on.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>**とか**、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>ある?<br>*Are you free tomorrow or something?*

<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みの<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>ける**とか**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**とか**しています。<br>*On my days off I tidy the flat, go shopping, things like that.*

---

## ～とか～とか · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい**とか**<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れた**とか**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>って、<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>まりに<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ません。<br>*He says he's busy, he's tired and so on, and never turns up to the get-togethers.*

<ruby>沖縄<rt>おきなわ</rt></ruby>**とか**<ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>**とか**、<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>いところへ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってみたいなあ。<br>*Okinawa, Hokkaido - I'd love to go somewhere far away like that.*

アルバイトを<ruby>探<rt>さが</rt></ruby>すなら、カフェ**とか**<ruby>本屋<rt>ほんや</rt></ruby>**とか**がいいんじゃない?<br>*If you're looking for part-time work, wouldn't a cafe or a bookshop or somewhere be good?*

<ruby>面接<rt>めんせつ</rt></ruby>では<ruby>志望<rt>しぼう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>**とか**<ruby>将来<rt>しょうらい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>**とか**を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>かれました。<br>*In the interview they asked me about my reasons for applying, my future plans, that kind of thing.*

---

## ～とか～とか · Nuance & register

`とか` is **casual and spoken**. It is not slang and no one will think less of you for it in conversation, but it does not belong in a report, a formal email or an exam essay - the neutral equivalents there are `や` and `など`.

Piled on too thickly it makes a speaker sound vague and non-committal, which is exactly why it is useful when you *want* to be non-committal:

---

## ～とか～とか · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **Softening a proposal.** <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>火曜日<rt>かようび</rt></ruby>とか、どうですか<br>*floats a date instead of demanding one. This is one of the most common modern uses and it involves no listing at all.*
- **Distancing yourself from what you are reporting.**<br>*<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になるとか<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました - "I heard something about it being cancelled." You pass on the information without vouching for it.*
- **Quoting a complaint you find tiresome.** The<br>*<code>～とか～とか<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>って</code> frame in the fifth example above carries an audible eye-roll.*

---

## ～とか～とか · Nuance & register (cont.)

`とかいう` deserves its own line: <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>とかいう<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました means "some person called Tanaka, or something like that, came". It signals that you barely registered the name - polite in a self-effacing way when the name is yours to remember, faintly rude when it is a person the listener cares about.

Written forms of the same word exist（`だとか`, `～とか～とかいう`）, but if you are choosing between registers, the rule is simple: speech takes `とか`, paper takes `や` and `など`.

---

## ～とか～とか · とか vs や

> **Rule of thumb** - both leave the list open. `や` goes **between** nouns only and is register-neutral; `とか` goes **after each** item, is casual, and takes verbs and adjectives as well.

<ruby>棚<rt>たな</rt></ruby>に<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>**や**<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>が<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>いてあります。<br>*There are dictionaries, maps and so on on the shelf.*

<ruby>棚<rt>たな</rt></ruby>に<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>**とか**<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>**とか**<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>いてあるよ。<br>*There are dictionaries and maps and stuff on the shelf.*

The first could be a museum label; the second could not. And where `や` simply stops working, `とか` carries on:

---

## ～とか～とか · とか vs や (cont.)

<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ**とか**<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る**とか**、<ruby>体<rt>からだ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かすことが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*I like moving my body - swimming, running, that sort of thing.*

❌<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐや<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る is impossible: `や` joins nouns and nothing else.

---

## ～とか～とか · とか vs ～たり～たり

> **Rule of thumb** - `～たり～たり` lists **actions** and has to be closed with `する`. `とか` just lists, and needs no closing verb.

<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>は<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>い**たり**、<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ん**だり**します。<br>*In the evening I listen to music, read, and so on.*

<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>は<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く**とか**、<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**とか**かな。<br>*Evenings are, like, listening to music or reading, I suppose.*

`～たり` is neutral in register and grammatically tighter - the `する` at the end is not optional. `とか` is looser in both senses. Note also that `とか` can list nouns, which `～たり` cannot.

---

## ～とか～とか · とか vs か

> **Rule of thumb** - `か` offers a genuine either/or. `とか` offers examples and asks you to choose nothing.

コーヒー**か**<ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってきてください。<br>*Please bring coffee or tea.*

コーヒー**とか**<ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>**とか**を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってきてください。<br>*Please bring coffee, tea, something along those lines.*

With `か` the listener picks one of exactly two. With `とか` the two named drinks are only illustrations, and green tea would satisfy the request perfectly well. This is also why a question offering a real choice cannot use `とか`.

---

## ～とか～とか · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐや<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>るが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ**とか**<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る**とか**が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*`や` links nouns and only nouns. When the items are actions, `とか` is the listing particle that works.*

- ❌ この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>には<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>とか<ruby>住所<rt>じゅうしょ</rt></ruby>とかを<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてください。 → ✅ ～<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>や<ruby>住所<rt>じゅうしょ</rt></ruby>などを<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてください。<br>*Grammatically fine, socially wrong. A written instruction takes `や` and `など`; `とか` makes an official form sound like a chat.*

- ❌ <ruby>肉<rt>にく</rt></ruby>とか<ruby>魚<rt>さかな</rt></ruby>とか、どちらが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。 → ✅ <ruby>肉<rt>にく</rt></ruby>と<ruby>魚<rt>さかな</rt></ruby>と、どちらが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。<br>*`どちら` asks for a choice between two fixed options, but `とか` says the two named items are merely examples. The two ideas cancel each other out.*

---

## ～とか～とか · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ とかコーヒーとか<ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい。 → ✅ コーヒー**とか**<ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>**とか**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい。<br>*`とか` is a postposition: it follows the item it tags. It never leads.*

---

# でも

**Meaning** · ～ or something; ～ or the like - names one thing as a token example

Attach `でも` to a noun in a suggestion and the noun stops being a demand. You put one option on the table as a representative of a whole class of acceptable options, and the listener is left free to counter with something else. It is the single most useful politeness trick in casual invitations.

---

## でも · Formation

`でも` follows the noun. It replaces `が` and `を`, and stacks on top of `に`, `へ` and `と`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + でも | お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby> → お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>でも |
| Noun taking を | を is **dropped** | ケーキでも<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べよう |
| Noun taking が | が is **dropped** | <ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>でもあるといいね |
| Noun taking に／へ／と | に／へ／と + でも | <ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>にでも<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>こう |
| Question word | いつ／どこ／だれ／なに + でも | いつでも／だれでも（＝any） |
| Typical predicates | ～でも + どう／いかが／～ませんか／～よう | お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>でもいかがですか |

---

## でも · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - this `でも` lives in sentences about **what might happen**: invitations, suggestions, offers, plans, volitional forms. It cannot report a completed fact. ❌<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>コーヒーでも<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みました is not a sentence you can rescue with context.

> **Note** - three different things share these two kana. This page is about the one that follows a **noun** in a suggestion. There is also the concessive `～ても／でも` that follows a verb or an adjective （<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っても, "even if it rains"）, and the sentence-initial `でも` meaning "but". Same shape, unrelated jobs.

---

## でも · Examples

<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んで、お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みませんか。<br>*Let's take a break - shall we have some tea or something?*

<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>こうか。<br>*Shall we go and see a film or something?*

<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>公園<rt>こうえん</rt></ruby>に**でも**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きましょうか。<br>*Shall we go to the park or somewhere at the weekend?*

<ruby>暇<rt>ひま</rt></ruby>なら、<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んだらどう?<br>*If you're free, why not read a book or something?*

---

## でも · Examples (cont.)

ケーキ**でも**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>って<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ろうかな。<br>*Maybe I'll pick up a cake or something on the way home.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>悩<rt>なや</rt></ruby>まないで、<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に**でも**<ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>してみたらどうですか。<br>*Don't sit with it on your own - why not talk to your teacher or somebody?*

<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れたでしょう。<ruby>冷<rt>つめ</rt></ruby>たいもの**でも**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んで、<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んでください。<br>*You must be tired. Have something cold to drink and take a rest.*

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>土曜日<rt>どようび</rt></ruby>、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>があったら<ruby>食事<rt>しょくじ</rt></ruby>に**でも**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きませんか。<br>*If you have time next Saturday, would you like to go out for a meal or something?*

---

## でも · Nuance & register

The whole point of this `でも` is **not committing the listener**. Compare the two invitations:

- お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みませんか - "would you like to drink tea?" The listener<br>*has to accept tea or refuse the invitation.*
- お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>でも<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みませんか - "shall we have tea or something?" The<br>*listener can say "sure, but I'd rather have juice" without refusing anything.*

That softness makes it standard in casual and semi-polite invitations at every level of formality: ～でも<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べようか between friends, ～でもいかがですか to a guest. It is register-neutral in itself; the politeness is carried by the ending.

---

## でも · Nuance & register (cont.)

There is one situation where it backfires. If the other person has just told you exactly what they want, offering it back with `でも` treats their request as arbitrary. 「コーヒーが<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい」「じゃあコーヒーでも<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってくるね」 reads as mildly dismissive - they asked for coffee, not for coffee-or-whatever. Drop the `でも` and use `を`.

The related **question word + でも** pattern - いつでも, どこでも, だれでも, <ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>でも - means "any at all", and it is a different construction with the same particle: だれでもできる<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>です, "it's a job anyone can do". Worth knowing here because learners meet both in the same week and mix them.

---

## でも · Nuance & register (cont.)

Finally, `でも` is not the only softener of its kind. `とか` does a similar job （<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>とかどう?）but is more casual and attaches happily to times and dates; `でも` sits more comfortably with the thing being consumed or done.

---

## でも · ～でも vs ～を

> **Rule of thumb** - `を` names the thing you mean. `でも` names one thing you would accept.

<ruby>昼<rt>ひる</rt></ruby>ごはんにそば**を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べましょう。<br>*Let's have soba for lunch.*

<ruby>昼<rt>ひる</rt></ruby>ごはんにそば**でも**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べましょう。<br>*Let's have soba or something for lunch.*

The first has decided; the second is thinking aloud. Note that `を` disappears entirely when `でも` moves in - ❌そばをでも is impossible, because both are fighting for the same slot.

---

## でも · Noun + でも vs question word + でも

> **Rule of thumb** - noun + `でも` offers an example. Question word + `でも` sweeps in the whole category: "any at all".

<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってきます。<br>*I'll go and buy some drinks or something.*

<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>なら**<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>でも**いいです。<br>*As for drinks, anything at all is fine.*

The first suggests one purchase among several possible ones; the second refuses to narrow anything down. The same contrast runs through the family: どこでも "anywhere", いつでも "any time", だれでも "anyone".

---

## でも · ～でも vs ～ても・でも（even if）

> **Rule of thumb** - check what comes **before** it. A noun means "or something". A verb or adjective stem means "even if".

<ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>に**でも**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きませんか。<br>*Shall we go for a walk or something?*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っ**ても**<ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*I'm going for a walk even if it rains.*

---

## でも · ～でも vs ～ても・でも（even if） (cont.)

They are not related in meaning at all, and the second one is not really `でも` - it is the て-form plus `も`, which turns into `でも` only after verbs whose て-form ends in `で`（<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでも）. And a `でも` at the very front of a sentence is the conjunction "but": でも、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいです。

---

## でも · Common mistakes

- ❌ ケーキ**を**でも<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べましょう。 → ✅ ケーキ**でも**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べましょう。<br>*`でも` takes the place of `を` and `が`. Only `に`, `へ` and `と` survive alongside it.*

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>コーヒー**でも**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みました。 → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>コーヒー**を**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みました。<br>*You cannot be vague about what you already did. This `でも` only works where the action is still open - suggestions, offers, plans.*

- ❌ 「<ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>がほしいです。」「じゃ、<ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れますね。」 → ✅ 「じゃ、<ruby>紅茶<rt>こうちゃ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れますね。」<br>*When the other person has named exactly what they want, `でも` says you were not really listening.*

---

## でも · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ でも、<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きませんか。（meaning "music or something"） → ✅ <ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きませんか。<br>*At the head of a sentence `でも` is the conjunction "but". The softening `でも` has to sit on the noun.*

# ばかり - only; nothing but

**Meaning** · nothing but ～; ～ and nothing else - usually with the feeling that there is too much of it

`ばかり` draws the same line `だけ` does, then adds an opinion. It says one thing fills the whole picture and, nine times out of ten, that this is too much of it. It is also not a mathematician: someone who <ruby>肉<rt>にく</rt></ruby>ばかり<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べている probably eats a salad occasionally. `ばかり` is about **proportion and attitude**, not arithmetic.

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + ばかり | マンガ → マンガばかり |
| Verb (habitual excess) | て-form + ばかりいる | <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>ぶ → <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでばかりいる |
| Verb (plain form) | V(plain) + ばかりだ | <ruby>文句<rt>もんく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うばかりだ |
| Modifying a noun | ～ばかり + の + Noun | <ruby>男<rt>おとこ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>ばかりのクラス |
| After が／を | が and を are **dropped** | ❌<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>をばかり → ✅<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>ばかり |
| After に／で／と | particle is **kept** | <ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>でばかり<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる |
| Casual | ばっかり／ばっか | マンガばっかり |
| Quantity (approximately) | Number + ばかり | <ruby>三日<rt>みっか</rt></ruby>ばかり（written, dated） |

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - if you can add "…and I'm getting tired of it" to the English, `ばかり` is the right word. If you are just stating a boundary, you want `だけ`.

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Examples

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>はゲーム**ばかり**しています。<br>*My little brother does nothing but play games.*

<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いもの**ばかり**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてはいけませんよ。<br>*You mustn't live on nothing but sweets.*

<ruby>文句<rt>もんく</rt></ruby>**ばかり**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わないで、<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってよ。<br>*Stop complaining all the time and give me a hand.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>て**ばかり**いて、<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しようとしません。<br>*My son does nothing but sleep - he won't even try to study.*

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>最近<rt>さいきん</rt></ruby>、<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>**ばかり**で<ruby>洗濯物<rt>せんたくもの</rt></ruby>が<ruby>乾<rt>かわ</rt></ruby>きません。<br>*Lately it's been rain, rain, rain, and the washing won't dry.*

あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>**ばかり**して、<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きません。<br>*He only ever talks about himself and never listens to anyone else.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>では<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>**ばかり**<ruby>繰<rt>く</rt></ruby>り<ruby>返<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>していて、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>だけが<ruby>過<rt>す</rt></ruby>ぎていきました。<br>*In the meeting they went over the same ground again and again, and the time just drained away.*

<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>はいいこと**ばかり**で、<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>幸<rt>しあわ</rt></ruby>せな<ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>でした。<br>*Last year was nothing but good news - a genuinely happy year.*

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Nuance & register

`ばかり` itself is register-neutral: <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでばかりいます and <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでばかりいる differ only in politeness of ending. The contraction `ばっかり`（and the clipped `ばっか`）is casual and adds emphasis and feeling - ゲームばっかりして! is what a parent shouts, not what a report says.

The attitude is the heart of the word. In the great majority of sentences it is **criticism**, either of somebody else（<ruby>文句<rt>もんく</rt></ruby>ばかり）or, turned inward, an apology（ご<ruby>迷惑<rt>めいわく</rt></ruby>ばかりかけてすみません, "I do nothing but cause you trouble"）. That inward use is genuinely humble and very common in polite speech.

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Nuance & register (cont.)

But the underlying idea is **excess**, not badness, and excess is occasionally welcome. いいことばかりでした and <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>ばかり<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>まったパーティー are both positive. What you cannot do is use `ばかり` neutrally: it always comments.

Two smaller points:

- The counting `ばかり` - <ruby>一週間<rt>いっしゅうかん</rt></ruby>ばかり<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みます, "I'll<br>*take about a week off" - is formal and slightly old-fashioned. Recognise it; in speech use `ぐらい`.*
- `～たばかり` is a **different pattern** meaning "just did", and it is<br>*built on the past form: <ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>いたばかりです, "I've only just arrived". The contrast section below separates them.*

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · ばかり vs だけ

> **Rule of thumb** - `だけ` reports a boundary and says nothing about how it feels. `ばかり` says the boundary is *too tight*, and does not claim to be exact.

<ruby>昼<rt>ひる</rt></ruby>ごはんはパン**だけ**でした。<br>*Lunch was just bread.*

<ruby>昼<rt>ひる</rt></ruby>ごはんはパン**ばかり**です。<br>*It's bread for lunch every single time.*

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · ばかり vs だけ (cont.)

The first is a fact about one lunch: bread, nothing else, no comment. The second complains about a pattern across many lunches - and would still be a fair thing to say on a day when you also had an apple. That inexactness is why `ばかり` and a precise number sit badly together: <ruby>三人<rt>さんにん</rt></ruby>だけ<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました is a count, <ruby>三人<rt>さんにん</rt></ruby>ばかり<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました means "about three people came" and belongs to the other, formal `ばかり`.

テレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ている<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>**ばかり**で、<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれる<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>がいません。<br>*Everyone's just sitting watching television and nobody will help.*

Swap `だけ` in there and the annoyance evaporates.

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · ばかり vs ～しか～ない

> **Rule of thumb** - `ばかり` complains that there is **too much of one thing**. `しか～ない` complains that there is **not enough of everything else**.

<ruby>冷蔵庫<rt>れいぞうこ</rt></ruby>にビール**ばかり**<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*The fridge is full of nothing but beer.*

<ruby>冷蔵庫<rt>れいぞうこ</rt></ruby>にビール**しか**<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>っていません。<br>*There's nothing in the fridge but beer.*

Both describe the same fridge and both are unhappy, but they are unhappy about different things. The first is disgusted by the quantity of beer; the second is disappointed that there is no food. Note the grammar too: `ばかり` takes a positive verb, `しか` demands a negative one.

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Noun + ばかり vs ～たばかり

> **Rule of thumb** - look at what is in front. A **noun** or a **て-form** gives you "nothing but". A **past-tense verb** gives you "just did".

<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た**ばかり**で、まだ<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めません。<br>*I've only just come to Japan, so I still can't read kanji.*

<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てから、コンビニのごはん**ばかり**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べています。<br>*Since coming to Japan I've eaten nothing but convenience store food.*

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Noun + ばかり vs ～たばかり (cont.)

The first is about time - the arrival was recent. The second is about proportion - the diet is monotonous. Learners produce ❌<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>るばかりです for "I just arrived"; the pattern needs the plain past <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た.

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>**を**ばかり<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んでいます。 → ✅ <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>**ばかり**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んでいます。<br>*`が` and `を` disappear in front of `ばかり`. If you want the object marked, it goes behind instead: <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>ばかりを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む.*

- ❌ <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>ぶばかりいます。 → ✅ <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>**んでばかり**います。<br>*With `いる`, the verb has to be in て-form. The plain form works only in the `V(plain) + ばかりだ` frame, which has no `いる`.*

- ❌ きのうは<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>**ばかり**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました。（meaning "only one person came"） → ✅ きのうは<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ませんでした。<br>*`ばかり` cannot mean "a mere N". With a number it either means "approximately" or nothing at all; the "disappointingly few" job belongs to `しか～ない`.*

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>**ばかり**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べて、<ruby>健康<rt>けんこう</rt></ruby>にいいですね。 → ✅ <ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>をたくさん<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べていて、<ruby>健康<rt>けんこう</rt></ruby>にいいですね。<br>*`ばかり` on somebody's habits reads as a complaint, so pairing it with praise sounds sarcastic. Use it when you mean the criticism.*

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>はスマホ____<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ていて、<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いてくれません。

{reveal}
<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>はスマホ**ばかり**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ていて、<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いてくれません。

{reveal}
The second half - she won't listen - tells you the speaker is annoyed, and that annoyance is exactly what `ばかり` carries. `だけ` would report the same behaviour with no complaint attached, which would clash with くれません.

---

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Practice 2

**2.** Choose the natural option: このクラスには<ruby>留学生<rt>りゅうがくせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby>（だけ・ばかり）います。

このクラスには<ruby>留学生<rt>りゅうがくせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby>**だけ**います。

A precise count needs `だけ`. Putting `ばかり` after a number switches on its other meaning - "about two" - and sounds like a formal announcement estimating a crowd.

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "He does nothing but grumble, and he never actually does anything."

{reveal}

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>はお<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>をばかり<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>むばかりいます。

{reveal}

{reveal}

## ばかり - only; nothing but · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みの<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>にいる**だけ**です。 (b) <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みの<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>にいて**ばかり**います。

{reveal}

# ～だけで

**Meaning** · just by doing ～; with only ～ - that small amount is all it takes

`だけ` draws the line; the `で` behind it says "and that line is where the cause sits". So `～だけで` is not simply "only" - it links a minimal amount of effort, time, money or evidence to a result that follows from it. Sometimes the tone is admiring ("that's all it takes!") and sometimes dismissive ("that's all it is").

---

## ～だけで · Formation

`だけ` attaches as it always does, and `で` follows it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | Vる + だけで | <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>るだけで |
| Verb (past) | Vた + だけで | <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた → <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いただけで |
| Noun | Noun + だけで | <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby> → <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>だけで |
| Number / counter | Counter + だけで | <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>だけで |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけで | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いだけで |
| な-adjective | stem + な + だけで | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだ → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かなだけで |
| Common endings | ～だけで + いい／<ruby>十分<rt>じゅうぶん</rt></ruby>／<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かる | <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>るだけでいいです |
| Negative judgement | ～だけで**は** + negative | それだけでは<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りません |

---

## ～だけで · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - if the sentence goes on to say what **results** from the small amount, you want `だけで`. If it simply reports the amount and stops, plain `だけ` is enough.

---

## ～だけで · Examples

<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る**だけで**<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>になります。<br>*Just seeing your face cheers me up.*

ボタンを<ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>す**だけで**お<ruby>湯<rt>ゆ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ます。<br>*Hot water comes out just by pressing the button.*

<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>える**だけで**<ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>くなります。<br>*It frightens me just to think about it.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>**だけで**<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>ぶのは<ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>ですよ。<br>*There's no way you can carry all of it with just one person.*

---

## ～だけで · Examples (cont.)

スマホ**だけで**<ruby>切符<rt>きっぷ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えます。<br>*You can buy a ticket with nothing but your phone.*

<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた**だけで**、<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>が<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きたかすぐに<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりました。<br>*Just from hearing about it, I knew straight away what had happened.*

この<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>がいい**だけで**、やりがいがありません。<br>*This job has nothing going for it but the pay - there's no satisfaction in it.*

<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>十分<rt>じゅっぷん</rt></ruby><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く**だけで**、<ruby>体<rt>からだ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>調子<rt>ちょうし</rt></ruby>がずいぶんよくなりました。<br>*Just walking for ten minutes every morning has made me feel a great deal better.*

---

## ～だけで · Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself; it takes its politeness from the ending. Two very different tones live inside the same three kana, and which one you hear depends on what the result is:

- **"That's all it takes."** The result is good and the effort is tiny.<br>*Instruction manuals, advertising and recipes are built out of this: <ruby>混<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ぜるだけでできます. In speech it is warm and encouraging.*
- **"That's all it is."** Here `だけで` follows an adjective and the<br>*second half of the sentence takes something away: <ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいだけで、<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いにくい. This is faint praise turning into a complaint, and it is quite cutting.*

---

## ～だけで · Nuance & register (cont.)

The frame `～だけでいい` - "that's all you need to do" - is worth memorising whole. It is how permission and reassurance are given in Japanese, and it is much softer than an imperative: ここにサインするだけでいいです is far friendlier than ここにサインしてください.

Adding `は` - `だけでは` - flips the sentence to "that alone is not enough", and it almost always precedes a negative: <ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>だけでは<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>になりません. The `は` is the ordinary contrastive `は`, and leaving it out sounds unfinished.

---

## ～だけで · ～だけで vs ～だけ

> **Rule of thumb** - plain `だけ` reports an amount. `だけで` makes that amount the **cause** of what comes next.

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>**だけ**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みました。<br>*Yesterday I drank only water.*

<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>**だけで**<ruby>三日間<rt>みっかかん</rt></ruby><ruby>生<rt>い</rt></ruby>きられますか。<br>*Can you survive for three days on water alone?*

---

## ～だけで · ～だけで vs ～だけ (cont.)

In the first, water is what got drunk. In the second, water is the *means* by which surviving happens. Slipping a `で` into the first sentence - ❌<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>だけで<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みました - would say you drank something using only water, which is nonsense.

---

## ～だけで · ～だけで vs ～だけでは

> **Rule of thumb** - `だけで` says the small amount **is** enough. `だけでは` says it **is not**.

この<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**だけで**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かります。<br>*You can understand it just by reading this explanation.*

この<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**だけでは**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりません。<br>*Just reading this explanation isn't enough to understand it.*

The `は` is contrastive: it sets "reading the explanation" against everything else you would also have to do. Because of that, the predicate after `だけでは` is nearly always negative - <ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りない, できない, いけない, <ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からない.

---

## ～だけで · ～だけで vs ～ば・～と

> **Rule of thumb** - a conditional says *if* X, then Y. `だけで` says X **and nothing more** is required for Y.

<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>め**ば**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かります。<br>*If you read the manual you'll understand how to use it.*

<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**だけで**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かります。<br>*Just reading the manual is enough to understand how to use it.*

The conditional states a route to the result; `だけで` also insists the route is short. That extra claim is what makes it the natural choice for anything selling ease - and what makes it sound sarcastic if the task is in fact difficult.

---

## ～だけで · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>て**だけで**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かります。 → ✅ <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る**だけで**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かります。<br>*`だけ` attaches to the plain form or the past form, never to the て-form. Both <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>るだけで and <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ただけで are fine; the past one presents the looking as already done.*

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**だ**だけで、<ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**な**だけで、<ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>です。<br>*な-adjectives take `な` before `だけ`, exactly as they do before a noun. `だ` only appears at the end of a sentence.*

- ❌ これだけで<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りません。 → ✅ これだけ**では**<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りません。<br>*When the point is that the amount is **insufficient**, the contrastive `は` is required. Without it the sentence sounds like it was cut off halfway.*

---

## ～だけで · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ サラダ**だけで**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べました。（meaning "I only ate salad"） → ✅ サラダ**だけ**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べました。<br>*The `で` adds "by means of", which the sentence does not want. Reserve `だけで` for when a result follows.*

---

# ～しか～ない

**Meaning** · only ～; nothing but ～ - and it is less than you wanted

`しか` is a limiter that comes with a mood attached. It excludes everything outside its noun, exactly as `だけ` does, but it also tells you the speaker finds the remainder thin. The grammar is the strange part: the verb at the end **must** be negative, even though the sentence is reporting something that does exist. <ruby>千円<rt>せんえん</rt></ruby>しかありません means "I have a thousand yen" - said by somebody who wishes they had more.

---

## ～しか～ない · Formation

`しか` goes on the noun; the negative goes on the predicate. Anything at all can sit between them.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + しか + negative | <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>しかありません |
| Number / counter | Counter + しか + negative | <ruby>二<rt>ふた</rt></ruby>つしか<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>っていない |
| After が／を／は | those particles are **dropped** | ❌お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>をしか → ✅お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>しか |
| After に／で／と／から | particle + しか | <ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>でしか<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えない |
| Polite | ～しか + ません／ないです | <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>しか<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ませんでした |
| With material in between | ～しか … + negative | <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>ししか<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べませんでした |

---

## ～しか～ない · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `しか` and the negative are one unit with a gap in the middle. If you write `しか` you have committed yourself to a negative ending, however many words later it arrives.

> **Note** - the sentence is negative in **form** only. To answer 「お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>、ありますか」 with 「<ruby>百円<rt>ひゃくえん</rt></ruby>しかありません」 is to say that you *do* have a hundred yen. English speakers hear the negative and conclude the opposite.

---

## ～しか～ない · Examples

<ruby>冷蔵庫<rt>れいぞうこ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>卵<rt>たまご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ**しか**<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>ってい**ません**。<br>*There's only one egg left in the fridge.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んでい**ません**。<br>*All I've had to drink today is water.*

その<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんに**しか**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>してい**ません**。<br>*Tanaka is the only person I've told about it.*

この<ruby>雑誌<rt>ざっし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>書店<rt>しょてん</rt></ruby>で**しか**<ruby>売<rt>う</rt></ruby>ってい**ません**。<br>*This magazine is only sold in the big bookshops.*

---

## ～しか～ない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>会場<rt>かいじょう</rt></ruby>には<ruby>十人<rt>じゅうにん</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>**ませんでした**。<br>*Only ten people turned up at the venue.*

<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>り<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>五分<rt>ごふん</rt></ruby>**しか****ありません**。<br>*There are only five minutes left.*

<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby><ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったコートは<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>かったのに、まだ<ruby>二回<rt>にかい</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>着<rt>き</rt></ruby>てい**ません**。<br>*The coat I bought last year was expensive, and I've still only worn it twice.*

<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わらなくて、<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>三時間<rt>さんじかん</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>られ**ませんでした**。<br>*I couldn't finish work, so I only got three hours' sleep last night.*

---

## ～しか～ない · Nuance & register

Register-neutral: <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>しかいない and <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>しかいません are the same sentence at different politeness levels, and nothing about `しか` marks age or gender. It is extremely common in ordinary speech.

The feeling it carries is **shortfall**. That can be regret （<ruby>三時間<rt>さんじかん</rt></ruby>しか<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ていない）, an apology （<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>ししかご<ruby>用意<rt>ようい</rt></ruby>できませんでした, said while serving a generous meal - a standard piece of Japanese modesty）, or a warning （<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>ししかありません）. What it cannot be is neutral. If you have no feelings about the quantity, use `だけ`.

You will hear `だけしか` doubled up - これだけしかない. Strictly it says the same thing twice, but it is standard colloquial Japanese and intensifies the disappointment.

---

## ～しか～ない · Nuance & register (cont.)

One more thing worth flagging early: `V(plain) + しかない`, as in <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くしかない, is a **different pattern** meaning "there's no choice but to go". It looks identical and it is not the same construction; the contrast section separates them.

---

## ～しか～ない · ～しか～ない vs ～だけ

> **Rule of thumb** - same fact, opposite mood. `だけ` states a limit flatly; `しか～ない` states it with regret. And only `しか` forces the verb negative.

<ruby>土曜日<rt>どようび</rt></ruby>**だけ**<ruby>営業<rt>えいぎょう</rt></ruby>しています。<br>*We're open on Saturdays.*

<ruby>土曜日<rt>どようび</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>営業<rt>えいぎょう</rt></ruby>してい**ません**。<br>*We're only open on Saturdays, I'm afraid.*

---

## ～しか～ない · ～しか～ない vs ～だけ (cont.)

A shop advertising its hours writes the first. A shop apologising for them says the second. Advertisers do sometimes use `しか` on purpose - <ruby>当店<rt>とうてん</rt></ruby>でしか<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えません, "you can't get it anywhere else" - but notice what they are doing: they are turning the shopper's inability to buy it elsewhere into the shortfall, not the shop's own range.

The mechanical difference matters too. `だけ` leaves the verb alone and can take `が`/`を` on either side; `しか` swallows `が`, `を` and `は` entirely and rewrites the ending.

---

## ～しか～ない · ～しか～ない vs a plain negative

> **Rule of thumb** - a plain negative denies. `しか～ない` denies everything **except** the thing it marks - so it quietly asserts that thing.

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>**ませんでした**。<br>*One person didn't come.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>**ませんでした**。<br>*Only one person came.*

The first sentence has one absentee and an unknown number of attendees. The second has one attendee and everybody else absent. The single word `しか` reverses which side of the count the number is on, and this is the mistake that costs learners marks in reading comprehension.

---

## ～しか～ない · Noun + しかない vs verb + しかない

> **Rule of thumb** - after a **noun**, `しかない` means "there is only ～". After a **plain-form verb**, it means "there is nothing to do but ～".

バスは<ruby>一日<rt>いちにち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>三本<rt>さんぼん</rt></ruby>**しかありません**。<br>*There are only three buses a day.*

バスがないので、<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く**しかありません**。<br>*There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.*

The second is a decision made under pressure, not a count of anything, and it is formally an N3 pattern. Keeping them apart is a matter of looking left: a noun or a counter means quantity, a dictionary-form verb means resignation.

---

## ～しか～ない · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>千円<rt>せんえん</rt></ruby>しか**あります**。 → ✅ <ruby>千円<rt>せんえん</rt></ruby>しか**ありません**。<br>*`しか` cannot live with a positive predicate. The negative ending is part of the pattern, not a separate decision.*

- ❌ お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>**を**しか<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていません。 → ✅ お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていません。<br>*`を` is dropped in front of `しか`, as are `が` and `は`. Only `に`, `で`, `と` and `から` survive, and they come first: <ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>でしか.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**は**しか<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りません。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**しか**<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りません。<br>*Same rule, and this one is easy to miss because `は` feels like it belongs to the topic rather than to the noun. `しか` takes the slot.*

---

## ～しか～ない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby>しか<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なくて、うれしかったです。 → ✅ <ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby>だけ<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>て、うれしかったです。<br>*`しか` says the number disappointed you, so it fights with うれしい. When the small number is good news, `だけ` is the word.*

---

## ～しか～ない · Practice 1

**1.** この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>には<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ____あり____。

{reveal}
この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>には<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ**しか**あり**ません**。

{reveal}
`しか` needs the negative ending to complete it. Note that <ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ, the counter, is where `しか` attaches - the pattern is very often built on a number, because that is where shortfall is easiest to feel.

## ～しか～ない · Practice 2

{reveal}

{reveal}

{reveal}
`しか` sits on <ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby> and the ending goes negative - ありません, not あります. The English "only once" already contains the regret, which is why `しか` fits better here than <ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>だけ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったことがあります, a flatter statement of fact.

{reveal}

# あまり～ない

**Meaning** · not very; not much; not particularly

`あまり` sits in front of a negative and softens it. It converts a flat denial into a measured one: not "I don't like it" but "I'm not all that keen". Because Japanese conversation avoids blunt refusals, this is one of the most-used adverbs in the language - and one learners under-use, because English rarely needs it.

---

## あまり～ない · Formation

`あまり` is an adverb, but it comes with a condition attached, which is why it is treated as a pattern: the predicate at the end of its clause **must be negative**. The two halves belong together the way `しか～ない` does.

---

## あまり～ない · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | あまり + ～くない | <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い → あまり<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くない |
| な-adjective | あまり + ～じゃない | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだ → あまり<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かじゃない |
| Noun predicate | あまり + ～じゃない | あまり<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>じゃない |
| Verb | あまり + ない-form | <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る → あまり<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ない |
| Polite | あまり + ～ません／～ないです | あまり<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません |
| Past | あまり + ～なかった／～ませんでした | あまり<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>くなかった |
| Casual | あんまり／あんま + negative | あんまりおいしくない |

---

## あまり～ない · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `あまり` goes in front of whatever it is measuring, and the negative comes at the end of that same clause. Anything may sit between them: あまり<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ません.

> **Note** - `あまり` with a **positive** predicate is a different, more literary construction meaning "excessively": あまりの<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>さに<ruby>驚<rt>おどろ</rt></ruby>いた, "I was startled by how bitterly cold it was". At N4 the safe rule is the one above - `あまり` takes a negative.

---

## あまり～ない · Examples

この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>辛<rt>から</rt></ruby>く**ありません**。<br>*This dish isn't very spicy.*

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は**あまり**テレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**ません**。<br>*I don't watch much television.*

きのうの<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>れ**ませんでした**。<br>*I didn't sleep very well last night.*

その<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>は**あまり**おもしろく**なかった**ですね。<br>*That film wasn't all that interesting, was it.*

---

## あまり～ない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>は**あんまり**<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えられ**ない**んです。<br>*I'm not very good at getting kanji to stick, to be honest.*

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>から<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>いので、この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>込<rt>こ</rt></ruby>み**ません**。<br>*It's a long way from the station, so this place doesn't get very crowded.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>はいいのですが、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**あまり**<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>れ**ません**。<br>*The new job pays well, but I don't get much time to myself.*

<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いものは**あまり**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きでは**ない**ので、<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>だけいただきます。<br>*I'm not that fond of sweet things, so I'll just have a drink.*

---

## あまり～ない · Nuance & register

`あまり` itself is register-neutral. What varies is the contraction:

- **あまり** - the standard form. Safe everywhere, including writing.
- **あんまり** - very common in ordinary speech, slightly warmer and more<br>*emphatic. Fine in conversation, out of place in a report or an exam essay.*
- **あんま** - clipped and quite casual. Recognise it; think twice before using it.

The reason to reach for `あまり` is almost always **politeness**. Japanese treats a bare negative judgement as blunt, so the softened version is the default in company:

---

## あまり～ない · Nuance & register (cont.)

- <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いです - "I dislike it." Strong, and can sound aggressive about<br>*someone's cooking.*
- <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きではありません - "I don't like it." Still flat.
- あまり<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きではありません - "I'm not particularly keen." This is the one<br>*people actually say.*

The same softening lets `あまり` function as a **polite refusal**. Asked whether you can come on Saturday, <ruby>土曜日<rt>どようび</rt></ruby>はあまり… - trailing off, with no negative at all - is understood as no. The listener supplies the rest. Learning to hear this saves a lot of confusion.

---

## あまり～ない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Do not confuse the degree with zero. あまり<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません means you drink a little; <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません means you do not drink; ぜんぜん<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません means never, not a drop.

---

## あまり～ない · あまり～ない vs ぜんぜん～ない

> **Rule of thumb** - `あまり` means a small amount. `ぜんぜん` means none at all.

<ruby>肉<rt>にく</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ません**。<br>*I don't eat much meat.*

<ruby>肉<rt>にく</rt></ruby>は**ぜんぜん**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ません**。<br>*I don't eat meat at all.*

The first speaker will accept a slice of ham; the second will not. Because `ぜんぜん` is absolute, it is also more forceful, and using it about someone's work or cooking can sound harsh where `あまり` would be diplomatic. If you are unsure how strong to be, `あまり` is the safer word.

---

## あまり～ない · あまり～ない vs a plain negative

> **Rule of thumb** - the plain negative states a fact. `あまり` states the same fact politely, and leaves the door slightly open.

この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>のことは<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>り**ません**。<br>*I don't know this area.*

この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>のことは**あまり**<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>り**ません**。<br>*I don't know this area very well.*

The second is both more modest and more accurate - you know a little. In conversation the softened version also invites the other person to keep talking, where the flat one closes the topic. This is why Japanese speakers add `あまり` to statements that are, strictly speaking, absolute.

---

## あまり～ない · あまり～ない vs そんなに～ない

> **Rule of thumb** - `あまり` measures against a general expectation. `そんなに` measures against something **the other person just said or did**.

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>く**ない**ですよ。<br>*This place isn't very expensive.*

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は**そんなに**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>く**ない**ですよ。<br>*It isn't that expensive here - not as much as you're imagining.*

`そんなに` points backwards at a standard someone else raised: they winced at the menu, or said they had heard the place was pricey. `あまり` needs no such antecedent and works as an opening remark. In many contexts either is fine, and `そんなに` simply sounds more conversational.

---

## あまり～ない · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>じゃありません。<br>*`あまり` cannot sit with a positive predicate. Without the negative the sentence has no meaning to land on.*

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>はあまり<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>かったです。（meaning "it was very hot"） → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>はとても<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>かったです。<br>*`あまり` is not a synonym for とても. It looks like an intensifier and behaves like the opposite of one.*

- ❌ この<ruby>報告書<rt>ほうこくしょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>数字<rt>すうじ</rt></ruby>は**あんまり**<ruby>正<rt>ただ</rt></ruby>しくありません。 → ✅ ～は**あまり**<ruby>正<rt>ただ</rt></ruby>しくありません。<br>*Grammatically fine, but `あんまり` is spoken register and clashes with a written document.*

---

## あまり～ない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ 「<ruby>納豆<rt>なっとう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか。」「ぜんぜん<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃありません。」（meaning "not all that keen"） → ✅ 「あまり<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃありません。」<br>*`ぜんぜん` is a total rejection and can sting. If you only mean "not especially", `あまり` is the word - and the polite one.*

---

## あまり～ない · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>今朝<rt>けさ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しくて、____<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ____。

{reveal}
`あまり` and the negative ending are one unit: you cannot supply one without the other. The meaning is that you ate a little, not that you skipped breakfast - for that you would need ぜんぜん<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べませんでした.

## あまり～ない · Practice 2

**2.** Choose the natural option: 「<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいカメラ、<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってみましたか。」「いいえ、まだ（あまり・とても）<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>っていません。」

The reply ends in a negative, and only `あまり` can pair with one. `とても` needs a positive predicate - とても<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすいです - so it has nothing to attach to here.

---

## あまり～ない · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese, politely: "I'm not very good at singing."

{reveal}

---

## あまり～ない · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かあまりじゃないです。

{reveal}
✅ この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かじゃないです。

{reveal}
`あまり` is an adverb and has to come **before** the word it modifies, not after it. Word order is what tells the listener how much of the sentence `あまり` is measuring.

---

## あまり～ない · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) その<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りません。 (b) その<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りません。

{reveal}

{reveal}
(b) admits partial knowledge and hedges it. You have heard something, but not enough to be useful - and by declining to sound certain, you invite the other person to fill you in. In a workplace, (b) is also the safer answer, because it does not commit you to knowing nothing.

# ぜんぜん

**Meaning** · (not) at all; not in the slightest

`ぜんぜん` is the zero end of the scale. Where `あまり` says "not much", `ぜんぜん` says "none, nothing, no part of it". It normally pairs with a negative, and in casual modern speech it has picked up a second, affirmative life that textbooks tend not to mention and learners hear on their first day in Japan.

---

## ぜんぜん · Usage

`ぜんぜん` is an adverb. It sits in front of the predicate it is emptying out, and anything may stand between it and the end of the sentence. In kanji it is <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>; both spellings are ordinary.

There are three patterns to recognise:

---

## ぜんぜん · Usage (cont.)

- **With a negative** - the core use.<br>*<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby><ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりません, <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby><ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>くなかった, <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃない.*
- **With a positive verb or adjective that already means "not"** -<br>*<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby><ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>います ("completely wrong"), <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>だめです ("no good at all"). These are positive in form and negative in sense, and they are fully standard.*
- **With an ordinary positive, in casual speech** - <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby><ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>,<br>*<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>いいよ, meaning "totally fine, no problem at all". Extremely common in conversation; see the register note below before you write it down.*

It also works as a one-word answer: 「<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れた?」「ぜんぜん。」 - "Tired?" "Not a bit."

---

## ぜんぜん · Examples

その<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は**ぜんぜん**<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りませんでした。<br>*I had absolutely no idea about that.*

この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>が**ぜんぜん**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりません。<br>*I don't understand what this question means at all.*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>と**ぜんぜん**<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>うじゃないですか。<br>*That's completely different from what you told us yesterday.*

「<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たせてごめん。」「ううん、**ぜんぜん**<ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>だよ。」<br>*"Sorry to keep you waiting." "No, no, it's totally fine."*

---

## ぜんぜん · Nuance & register

In its negative use `ぜんぜん` is register-neutral and works in speech and writing alike. What it is not is **soft**. It is an absolute, and pointed at another person's work it can be blunt: <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>おもしろくなかったです about a friend's film recommendation is a flat verdict. When you want to be diplomatic, `あまり` is the word.

---

## ぜんぜん · Nuance & register (cont.)

The affirmative use - <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby><ruby>平気<rt>へいき</rt></ruby>, <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>いける - needs a clear-eyed description, because you will meet it constantly and be told it is wrong. It is casual spoken Japanese, especially common among younger speakers, and in conversation nobody blinks. Style guides and many older speakers treat it as incorrect, and it does not belong in an essay, a business email or a JLPT answer. Interestingly the affirmative pattern is not new: it appears in Meiji-era literature, and the "negative only" rule solidified later - so what is often called a modern corruption is closer to a revival. Use it in speech if you like; keep it off paper.

Two more points of feel:

---

## ぜんぜん · Nuance & register (cont.)

- The intensity ladder runs <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby> → あまり → ちょっと. Only the first<br>*is a true zero.*
- Because it is absolute, `ぜんぜん` cannot be qualified. There is no "almost not<br>*at all"; if the amount is small but real, you have moved to `あまり`.*

---

## ぜんぜん · Common mistakes

- ❌ その<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>はぜんぜん<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しかったです。（meaning "not fun at all"） → ✅ その<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>はぜんぜん<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しく**なかった**です。<br>*With an ordinary adjective the predicate has to go negative. Leaving it positive produces the casual "totally fun", which is the opposite of what you meant.*

- ❌ ぜんぜん<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かります。 → ✅ <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かります。／あまり<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりません。<br>*`ぜんぜん` is zero, so it cannot be attached to a small quantity. Choose one or the other.*

- ❌ この<ruby>作品<rt>さくひん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>すばらしいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。（in an essay） → ✅ この<ruby>作品<rt>さくひん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>非常<rt>ひじょう</rt></ruby>にすばらしいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*The affirmative `ぜんぜん` is spoken register. In writing it reads as an error even to readers who use it themselves every day.*

---

## ぜんぜん · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>ありましたから、ゆっくりできました。 → ✅ <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がたくさんありましたから、ゆっくりできました。<br>*<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>ある is not the affirmative pattern; the casual use attaches to evaluations like <ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby> and いい, not to plain statements of quantity.*

---

## ぜんぜん · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>三年<rt>さんねん</rt></ruby><ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>いましたが、まだ____<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せ____。

{reveal}
<ruby>三年<rt>さんねん</rt></ruby><ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>いましたが、まだ**ぜんぜん**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せ**ません**。

{reveal}
`ぜんぜん` demands the negative ending, and the contrast with <ruby>三年<rt>さんねん</rt></ruby><ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>いました is what makes it the right adverb here - three years of study and *nothing* to show for it. あまり<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せません would mean you can manage a little, which is a different complaint.

---

## ぜんぜん · Practice 2

**2.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>のテストはぜんぜん<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しいでした。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>のテストはぜんぜん<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しく**ありませんでした**。

{reveal}
Two things. い-adjectives never take でした - the past goes on the adjective itself. And `ぜんぜん` needs the negative, so the correct past negative is <ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しくありませんでした（or <ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しくなかったです）.

---

## ぜんぜん · Practice 3

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>辛<rt>から</rt></ruby>くないです。 (b) この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>は**ぜんぜん**<ruby>辛<rt>から</rt></ruby>くないです。

{reveal}
(a) says there is a little heat in it, but not much. Someone who cannot take spice at all should still be careful.

{reveal}
(b) says there is no heat whatsoever - it is safe for anyone. `ぜんぜん` is an absolute zero, `あまり` a small but real amount, and choosing the wrong one is a genuinely useful thing to get right at a dinner table.

# そんなに

**Meaning** · that much; so much; to that extent

`そんなに` measures against something **the other person has just supplied** - their words, their reaction, the amount they poured, the price they winced at. It is not a free-standing "very". Point it at a standard nobody has raised and it sounds as though you are answering a question that was never asked.

---

## そんなに · Usage

`そんなに` is an adverb built from `そんな` ("that kind of") plus `に`. It goes directly in front of the adjective, adverb or verb whose degree it is measuring.

Most of the time it appears **with a negative**, playing down an amount the other person has overestimated:

- そんなに<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くないですよ - "it isn't that expensive."
- そんなに<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>くありません - "it isn't that far."
- そんなに<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>はかかりません - "it won't take that long."

---

## そんなに · Usage (cont.)

With a **positive** predicate it usually carries surprise or a mild protest: そんなに<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたの? ("you ate that much?"), そんなに<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>がないで ("don't rush like that").

It belongs to a set of four. `こんなに` measures against something in the speaker's own hands, `そんなに` against the listener's, `あんなに` against something remembered or far from both, and `どんなに` asks "how much?".

The crucial spelling point: **そんな** goes in front of a noun （そんな<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>, そんなこと）and **そんなに** in front of an adjective or verb （そんなに<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい, そんなに<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>う）.

---

## そんなに · Examples

**そんなに**<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>がなくてもいいですよ。<br>*There's no need to rush like that.*

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>は**そんなに**<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>くありません。<br>*This dictionary isn't as heavy as all that.*

**そんなに**<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>さないでください。<ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>ちゃんが<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きてしまいます。<br>*Please don't talk that loudly - you'll wake the baby.*

「<ruby>三時間<rt>さんじかん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>並<rt>なら</rt></ruby>んだんだ。」「えっ、**そんなに**<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ったの?」<br>*"I queued for three whole hours." "What - you waited that long?"*

---

## そんなに · Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very frequent in conversation. In formal writing the same job is done by `それほど`, which is the stiffer equivalent: それほど<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しくはありません in a manual, そんなに<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しくないよ to a friend.

The reason it turns up so often is that it is a **polite way to disagree**. Rather than contradicting someone flatly, you scale their estimate down: 「<ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>でしょう」「いえ、そんなに<ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>じゃありません」. The trailing 「そんなに…」 on its own - with the rest left unsaid - is a very soft way of saying "not really".

---

## そんなに · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two feelings to keep apart. With a negative, `そんなに` is reassuring: it tells the listener to relax. With a positive it is often the opposite - surprise （そんなにかかったの?）or restraint （そんなに<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>らないで, "don't be so angry"）, and in that second use it can sound like a mild telling-off.

Compared with `あまり`, `そんなに` is more conversational and more tied to context. `あまり` measures against the world in general and can open a conversation; `そんなに` needs something to point back at.

---

## そんなに · Common mistakes

- ❌ そんなに<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>信用<rt>しんよう</rt></ruby>できません。 → ✅ **そんな**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>信用<rt>しんよう</rt></ruby>できません。<br>*Before a **noun** the form is `そんな`. `そんなに` is the adverb and needs an adjective or a verb to modify.*

- ❌ そんな<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くないですよ。 → ✅ **そんなに**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くないですよ。<br>*The mirror image of the mistake above. You will hear the reduced そんな<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い in fast casual speech, but `そんなに` is the standard form and the only safe one in writing or on a test.*

- ❌ （opening a conversation with no context）そんなに<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しくないです。 → ✅ あまり<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しくないです。<br>*`そんなに` answers an estimate someone else has made. With nothing to point back at, it leaves the listener hunting for what "that much" refers to.*

---

## そんなに · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ （holding up your own enormous suitcase）そんなに<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いんです。 → ✅ **こんなに**<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いんです。<br>*The thing is in your hands, so it belongs to the speaker's territory: `こんなに`. `そんなに` would put it on the listener's side of the conversation.*

---

## そんなに · Practice 1

**1.** 「<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>くと<ruby>一時間<rt>いちじかん</rt></ruby>ぐらいかかりますか。」「いいえ、____かかりません。<ruby>十五分<rt>じゅうごふん</rt></ruby>ぐらいです。」

{reveal}
「いいえ、**そんなに**かかりません。<ruby>十五分<rt>じゅうごふん</rt></ruby>ぐらいです。」

{reveal}
The other person has just put an hour on the table, and `そんなに` scales that specific estimate down without calling them wrong. This is the pattern's home ground: a polite correction that points back at what was just said.

## そんなに · Practice 3

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) **こんなに**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るとは<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いませんでした。 (b) **あんなに**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るとは<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いませんでした。

{reveal}
