# こと - nominaliser

**Meaning** · turns a verb, an adjective or a whole clause into a noun, so the sentence can talk *about* it

Japanese has no infinitive and no "-ing" noun. When you need an action to behave like a thing - to be liked, to be important, to be decided at a meeting - you put `こと` on the end of the clause. What comes out is an ordinary noun and takes `が`, `を`, `は` or `です` like any other.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Formation

`こと` attaches to the **plain form**. Only な-adjectives and nouns change shape first, and they change in the way they always do before a noun.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | dictionary form + こと | <ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>める → <ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>めること |
| Verb (past) | た-form + こと | <ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まった → <ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まったこと |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + こと | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないこと |
| い-adjective | plain form + こと | <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい → <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいこと |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + こと | <ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>なこと |
| Noun | Noun + **である** + こと | <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>であること |
| A whole clause | clause (plain) + こと | <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>が<ruby>結婚<rt>けっこん</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>が<ruby>結婚<rt>けっこん</rt></ruby>すること |
| As the predicate | ～ことだ／～ことです | <ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ることです |
| Fixed frames | ことができる／たことがある／ことにする | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐことができる |

---

## こと - nominaliser · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - never ❌<code><ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だこと</code>. A noun needs `である` in front of `こと`, and in everyday Japanese most speakers avoid the problem altogether by using <code><ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だということ</code>, which is the clause-packaging pattern treated separately at this level.

> **Note** - `こと` is also an ordinary noun meaning "thing, matter", and it keeps that job here: <code><ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りたい</code> ("I want to know about Japan"), <code><ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんのこと</code> ("the Tanaka business", or simply Tanaka as a topic). The nominaliser is the same word doing the same thing to a clause instead of a noun.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Examples

<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby>をかく**こと**が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*I like drawing.*

<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>守<rt>まも</rt></ruby>る**こと**は<ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Keeping to the time you agreed is important.*

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>古<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>い<ruby>切手<rt>きって</rt></ruby>を<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>める**こと**です。<br>*My hobby is collecting old stamps.*

<ruby>外国<rt>がいこく</rt></ruby>で<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>く**こと**を<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えています。<br>*I'm thinking about working abroad.*

---

## こと - nominaliser · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>結婚<rt>けっこん</rt></ruby>する**こと**は、まだ<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>していません。<br>*I haven't told anyone yet that my brother is getting married next month.*

<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>で<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>をかける**こと**が、まだ<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*Making phone calls in Japanese still frightens me a little.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>で<ruby>一番<rt>いちばん</rt></ruby><ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>なのは、<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>五時<rt>ごじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きる**こと**です。<br>*The hardest part of the new job is getting up at five in the morning.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>出張<rt>しゅっちょう</rt></ruby>する**こと**は、<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まりました。<br>*It was settled at yesterday's meeting that the manager is going away on business next week.*

---

## こと - nominaliser · Nuance & register

The nominaliser itself carries no politeness. What it carries is a slight lean towards the **written and the abstract**, which is the one thing that separates it from its rival `の`. `こと` names a fact, a rule, a plan, a proposition - something you can think about, decide, promise or announce. `の` names an event you are watching, hearing or living through. Where both are grammatical, `の` is the more spoken of the two, and a page of conversation will contain many more of them.

`こと` never contracts. `の` shrinks to `ん` before `です`, and the packaging form `ということ` shrinks to `ってこと` in casual speech, but plain `こと` stays as it is in every register.

<code><ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>は…ことです</code> deserves a line of its own because it is the first thing you will need it for. Self-introductions, job interviews and profile forms are full of it, and there `の` is not an option at all - the reason is in the Contrast section.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Nuance & register (cont.)

One more `こと` exists and it looks identical on the page. A verb plus a bare `こと` and a full stop, with **nothing after it**, is not a noun phrase at all: it is a written instruction, the `こと` of classroom notices and exam papers, and it is treated as its own point at N3. The test is mechanical: if a particle or `です` follows the `こと`, you are looking at the nominaliser on this page. If the sentence stops dead, you are looking at a rule.

---

## こと - nominaliser · こと vs の

> **Rule of thumb** - `の` for what you see, hear and take part in; `こと` for facts, plans and propositions. Where either would work, `の` is the spoken choice - but three situations take the decision out of your hands.

**Verbs of direct perception require `の`.** <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く, <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえる, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える, <ruby>感<rt>かん</rt></ruby>じる are watching an event happen, and an event is not a fact.

<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>で<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>した**の**に<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がつかなかった。<br>*I didn't notice that it had started raining outside.*

---

## こと - nominaliser · こと vs の (cont.)

**A nominalised clause standing in front of `だ`／`です` requires `こと`.** <code><ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ることです</code> is right; ❌<code><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>るのです</code> is not available, because that string already means something else - the explanatory `んです`.

**A set of fixed frames requires `こと`.** `ことができる`, `たことがある`, `ことにする`, `ことになる` are frozen expressions and admit no substitution: ❌<code><ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのができる</code>.

Everywhere else both are grammatical. There is one pair worth memorising because the two versions genuinely mean different things:

---

## こと - nominaliser · こと vs の (cont.)

<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>する**の**を<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れた。<br>*I forgot to ring.*

<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>した**こと**を<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れた。<br>*I forgot that I had rung.*

The first is an action that never happened; the second is a fact that slipped your mind. That is the difference between the two words in miniature.

---

## こと - nominaliser · ことができる vs the potential form

> **Rule of thumb** - the same meaning at two lengths. The potential form (<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せる) is the default in speech; `ことができる` is longer, flatter and more official, and it is what notices and manuals use.

ここで<ruby>切符<rt>きっぷ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う**ことができます**。<br>*Tickets may be purchased here.*

ここで<ruby>切符<rt>きっぷ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えます**。<br>*You can buy tickets here.*

Both are correct and mean the same thing. The `こと` in `ことができる` is frozen in place - it cannot become `の`, and it cannot be dropped - which is a good reminder that `こと` is not always a free choice.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Nominalising こと vs the instruction こと

> **Rule of thumb** - look at what comes **after** the `こと`. A particle or `です` means it is a noun. A full stop and nothing else means it is a rule.

<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>った<ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>う**こと**が、<ruby>今週<rt>こんしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>係<rt>かかり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Washing the dishes we've used is this week's duty.*

<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>った<ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>は<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>う**こと**。<br>*Wash your own dishes.*

The first sentence talks about the washing-up as a thing; the second orders it. The second has no politeness level and no addressee - it is the voice of a notice on a kitchen wall - and that impersonal instruction `こと` is a separate grammar point you meet at N3. Nothing may follow it, so the moment you see `が`, `を`, `は` or `です` after the `こと`, you are back on this page.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**だこと**を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らなかった。 → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**だということ**を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らなかった。<br>*A plain noun cannot sit in front of `こと` with `だ` on it. Everyday Japanese uses `だということ`; formal writing uses `であること`.*

- ❌ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**こと**が<ruby>一番<rt>いちばん</rt></ruby><ruby>大事<rt>だいじ</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**なこと**が<ruby>一番<rt>いちばん</rt></ruby><ruby>大事<rt>だいじ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*な-adjectives keep their `な` in front of `こと`, exactly as they do in front of any other noun.*

- ❌ <ruby>隣<rt>となり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>がピアノを<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>いている**こと**が<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえます。 → ✅ …<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>いている**の**が<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえます。<br>*You are hearing an event, not a fact. Verbs of direct perception take `の` and nothing else.*

---

## こと - nominaliser · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもに<ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>える**のです**。 → ✅ …<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>える**ことです**。<br>*In front of `です`, only `こと` works. With `の` the sentence turns into the explanatory `んです` and stops saying what you meant.*

- ❌ <ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きること<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きること**が**<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Once the clause has become a noun it needs a particle like any other noun. The nominaliser does not replace `が` or `を`; it makes room for one.*

---

## こと - nominaliser · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>は、いつか<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>つ____です。

{reveal}
<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>つ**こと**です。

{reveal}
The nominalised clause is standing directly in front of `です` as the predicate, and that slot only accepts `こと`. <code><ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>つのです</code> would be read as the explanatory `んです` instead.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもたちが<ruby>公園<rt>こうえん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>り<ruby>回<rt>まわ</rt></ruby>っている（の・こと）が<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>から<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えます。

{reveal}
…<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>り<ruby>回<rt>まわ</rt></ruby>っている**の**が<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>から<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えます。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える</code> is direct perception: the running is happening in front of your eyes, so the nominaliser has to be `の`. `こと` would turn it into a fact you had been told about, which is not something a window can show you.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I promised my mother that I would come home by nine."

{reveal}
<ruby>九時<rt>くじ</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**こと**を<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>と<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>しました。

{reveal}
A promise is made about a proposition, not about an event you are watching, so `こと` is the natural choice. <code><ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ると<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>に<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>した</code> with the quotative `と` is also perfectly good Japanese.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>するのが<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>習慣<rt>しゅうかん</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>健康<rt>けんこう</rt></ruby>にいいのは<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>けるのです。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>するのが<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>習慣<rt>しゅうかん</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>健康<rt>けんこう</rt></ruby>にいいのは<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>ける**ことです**。

{reveal}
The first `の` is fine - it is followed by `が`, and either nominaliser would do there. The second one is in front of `です`, where only `こと` is possible.

---

## こと - nominaliser · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference: (a) <ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>をかける**の**を<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れました。 (b) <ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>をかけた**こと**を<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れました。

{reveal}
(a) says the locking never happened. `の` names the act itself, and <code><ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れる</code> reports that you failed to carry it out - the door is unlocked.

{reveal}
(b) says the locking did happen, and the memory of it is what went missing. `こと` names the *fact* that you locked up, which is why the past form `かけた` is possible here and impossible in (a). The door is locked; you are just about to go back and check it.

# ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun

**Meaning** · takes a whole sentence and hands it on as a single noun - "the fact that ～", "that ～"

`こと` on its own nominalises a verb phrase. `ということ` nominalises a **finished statement**: the quotative `という` wraps the clause up with its own ending intact, `だ` and all, and `こと` then turns the parcel into a noun the rest of the sentence can take as its subject or object.

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Formation

`という` attaches to a **plain-form clause** exactly as it does after <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う and <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う. Nouns and な-adjectives keep their `だ`, which is the whole reason the pattern exists.

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ということ | <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないということ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ということ | <ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まった → <ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まったということ |
| い-adjective | plain form + ということ | <ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>いということ |
| な-adjective | ～**だ** + ということ | <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>だということ |
| Noun | Noun + **だ** + ということ | <ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>だということ |
| Embedded question | ～か + ということ | どこで<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うか → どこで<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うかということ |
| Taking a particle | ～ということ + が／を／は | ～ということを<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>る |
| Casual speech | ってこと | <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>だってこと |

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the `だ` is what tells the two nominalisers apart. Plain `こと` cannot follow `だ` (❌<code><ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>だこと</code>), so a clause ending in a noun or a な-adjective needs `ということ`.

> **Note** - the same `という` sits inside a family of expressions built the same way: <code>という<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby></code>, <code>という<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby></code>, <code>という<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby></code>. `こと` is simply the most general noun you can put there, which is why this one turns up far more often than the others.

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Examples

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**ということ**を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っていましたか。<br>*Did you know that Yamada isn't coming?*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>だ**ということ**を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I heard that the meeting has been called off.*

<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>い**ということ**は、<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っていました。<br>*I already knew that summers in Japan are hot.*

<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>しても<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>ける**ということ**が<ruby>大事<rt>だいじ</rt></ruby>だと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*I think what matters is carrying on even when you fail.*

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>している**ということ**が、だんだん<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かってきました。<br>*It gradually became clear that he was telling the truth.*

どこで<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わせるか**ということ**を、まだ<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>めていません。<br>*We still haven't settled the question of where to meet.*

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>若<rt>わか</rt></ruby>いころ<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>んでいた**ということ**は、この<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>るまで<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りませんでした。<br>*I had no idea my father lived in Tokyo when he was young until I saw this photograph.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に、<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>までにお<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>がほしい**ということ**を<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えておいてください。<br>*Please pass on to the manager that we would like an answer by next week.*

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Nuance & register

`ということ` is register-neutral but noticeably **heavy**. It is four syllables of scaffolding, and Japanese drops it whenever the sentence can stand without it: <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないと<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました</code> says the same thing as <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないということを<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました</code> in half the space. Reach for the full form when the clause is long, when it is doing something a bare `と` cannot do (taking `が`, taking `は`, sitting in front of `です`), or when you want the content held up for inspection rather than slipped past.

In casual speech it contracts to `ってこと`, and this is extremely common: <code><ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだってこと、<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いてる?</code> The contraction is conversational only - it would look wrong in a report - but it is what you will actually hear, and learners who only know the long form miss it entirely.

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Nuance & register (cont.)

Because the pattern begins with a quotative, it leans towards content that came from **somewhere**: something said, written, decided, noticed or worked out. That is not a rule - <code><ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>いということ</code> above is nobody's utterance - but it explains why the pattern feels natural after <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く, <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>える, <ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>する and awkward after a verb of direct perception.

One warning about the shape. Put `だ`／`です` on the end of this string and let the sentence stop there, and you have a different pattern altogether: the hearsay `ということだ`, "I hear that ～", which is a separate point at N3. On this page the `ということ` is always followed by something - a particle, or a predicate that takes it as an argument. The next section sets the two side by side.

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · ということ vs こと

> **Rule of thumb** - `こと` nominalises an **action**. `ということ` nominalises a **statement**. If the clause ends in a noun or a な-adjective, only `ということ` is available, because plain `こと` cannot follow `だ`.

<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る**こと**は<ruby>体<rt>からだ</rt></ruby>にいいです。<br>*Running every day is good for you.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っている**ということ**は、<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りませんでした。<br>*Nobody knew that he ran every day.*

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · ということ vs こと (cont.)

The first names an activity; the second names a piece of information about somebody. Where the clause is a complete statement with a subject in it, the longer form is the natural one. Note the difference this makes with a noun in front: <code><ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました</code> is "I heard about the accident" - a topic - while <code><ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>だったということを<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました</code> is "I heard that it was an accident", a proposition.

---

## ということ vs ということだ

> **Rule of thumb** - look at what comes **after** it. A particle means it is a noun and the sentence is still going. `だ`／`です` closing the sentence means it is a report: "I hear that ～".

<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わる**ということ**を、<ruby>係<rt>かかり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>しました。<br>*I checked with the person in charge that the work finishes next month.*

<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わる**ということです**。<br>*The work finishes next month, I gather.*

---

## ということ vs ということだ (cont.)

Same eleven characters, two different grammars. In the first the `ということ` is the object of <ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>する - it is a noun, and it could equally take `が` or `は`. In the second nothing follows, `です` closes the sentence, and the whole frame has become a sentence-final marker that puts the information at arm's length; that hearsay use is treated at N3. The one place the test needs care is a cleft frame - <code><ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>なのは…ということです</code> - where the sentence does end on です but the `のは` in front makes it plainly the nominaliser again.

---

## ということ vs the bare quotative と

> **Rule of thumb** - `と` feeds a clause straight into <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う, <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う, <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く. `ということ` makes it a noun first, so it can take a particle or be the subject.

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない**と**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていました。<br>*Hayashi said he wasn't going.*

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない**ということ**は、みんなに<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えました。<br>*I've told everyone that Hayashi isn't going.*

---

## ということ vs the bare quotative と (cont.)

With <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う and <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う, `と` is shorter and better, and a learner who writes `ということ` every time sounds laboured. But `と` cannot be marked with `は` or `が`, and it cannot be the subject of an adjective, so the moment the clause needs to move around the sentence, the full nominaliser is the only option.

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Common mistakes

- ❌（meaning "I heard that it was cancelled"）<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。 → ✅ <ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>だ**ということ**を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*<code><ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>のこと</code> is a topic - "about the cancellation". To report the statement itself you need the clause packaged with `という`.*

- ❌ この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**ということ**を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りませんでした。 → ✅ この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**だということ**を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りませんでした。<br>*A な-adjective keeps its `だ` in front of `という`. This is the opposite of what happens before plain `こと`, where the same word takes `な`.*

- ❌ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**だということ**が<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**ということ**が<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>です。<br>*い-adjectives never take `だ`. Only nouns and な-adjectives do.*

---

## ～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**ということだ**を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らなかった。 → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**ということ**を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らなかった。<br>*The `だ` belongs to the sentence-final hearsay pattern. Inside a sentence, where a particle follows, the noun ends at `こと`.*

- ❌ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>いている**ということ**を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ました。 → ✅ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>いている**の**を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*You cannot watch a proposition. Verbs of direct perception take `の`, never `こと` and never `ということ`.*

# ～やすい

**Meaning** · easy to do - or, with some verbs, liable to happen

`～やすい` attaches to a verb and produces an い-adjective. What that adjective describes is the **thing**, not the person: the pen is easy to write with, the white shirt is quick to get dirty. You already met this machinery at N5 with `～すぎる` - same ます-stem, same い-adjective coming out the other end.

---

## ～やすい · Formation

Cut the verb back to its ます-stem and add `やすい`. The result conjugates exactly like <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い</code>.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + やすい | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすい |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + やすい | <ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>える → <ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えやすい |
| Verb (する) | し + やすい | <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>しやすい |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby> + やすい | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>やすい |

> **Note** - う-verb / る-verb are the labels used throughout this book; you will also meet them as <ruby>五段<rt>ごだん</rt></ruby>/Group 1 and <ruby>一段<rt>いちだん</rt></ruby>/Group 2 elsewhere.

---

## ～やすい · Formation (cont.)

Once `やすい` is on, everything an い-adjective does is available:

| い-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | + です | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすいです |
| Negative | やすい → やすくない | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすくない |
| Past | やすい → やすかった | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすかった |
| て-form | やすい → やすくて | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすくて |
| Before a noun | やすい + Noun | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすいアプリ |
| Adverbial | やすい → やすく | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすくなる |
| Noun form | やすい → やすさ | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすさ |

---

## ～やすい · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the thing that is easy takes `は` or `が`, never `を`. Adding `やすい` turns the verb into an adjective, and adjectives do not take objects: この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすい, not ❌この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすい.

---

## ～やすい · Examples

このペンは**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きやすい**。<br>*This pen is easy to write with.*

ひらがなは**<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えやすい**です。<br>*Hiragana is easy to learn.*

<ruby>白<rt>しろ</rt></ruby>いシャツは**<ruby>汚<rt>よご</rt></ruby>れやすい**。<br>*White shirts get dirty easily.*

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりやすかった**です。<br>*The teacher's explanation was easy to follow.*

---

## ～やすい · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れているときは**<ruby>間違<rt>まちが</rt></ruby>えやすい**から、<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>をつけてください。<br>*You slip up easily when you're tired, so please be careful.*

この<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さくて、あまり**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすくない**。<br>*The print in this manual is small, so it isn't very easy to read.*

この<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>くて**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きやすい**ので、<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>のときはいつもこれをはいています。<br>*These shoes are light and easy to walk in, so I always wear them when I travel.*

<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>は<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わりやすい**ですから、かさを<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>って<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけたほうがいいですよ。<br>*The weather turns quickly in spring, so you'd do well to take an umbrella out with you.*

---

## ～やすい · Nuance & register

The two meanings are not two words. Which one a listener hears is decided by the verb underneath.

- A verb someone **chooses** to do - <code><ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く</code>, <code><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>う</code>,<br>*<code><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く</code>, <code><ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む</code> - gives **ease**. The thing has a property that makes the action comfortable.*
- A verb that **just happens** - <code><ruby>汚<rt>よご</rt></ruby>れる</code>, <code><ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>れる</code>,<br>*<code><ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れる</code>, <code><ruby>太<rt>ふと</rt></ruby>る</code>, <code><ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わる</code> - gives **propensity**. Nobody is finding anything easy; the event simply occurs readily.*

The propensity reading is often, but not always, a complaint. <code><ruby>壊<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>れやすい</code> is a defect; <code><ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えやすい</code> is a compliment. Do not assume `やすい` is the positive one and `にくい` the negative one - each takes its colour from the verb.

---

## ～やすい · Nuance & register (cont.)

`～やすい` is register-neutral. It is equally at home in casual speech （このアプリ、<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすいね）, in polite conversation, and in written product copy, where <code><ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ちやすい</code>・<code><ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>いやすい</code>・<code><ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちやすい</code> are everyday advertising vocabulary.

Two derived forms are worth having now. `やすくなる` means "become easier" or "start happening more readily": <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えてから、<ruby>新聞<rt>しんぶん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすくなりました。 And `やすさ` is its noun - the `さ` nominaliser applied to the whole compound: この<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>は<ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>のしやすさで<ruby>人気<rt>にんき</rt></ruby>があります。

---

## ～やすい · ～やすい vs ～にくい

> **Rule of thumb** - they are opposites in meaning but not perfect mirrors in use. Negating a `やすい` word by swapping in `にくい` is safe when the verb is a happening; it can shift the sense when the verb is a choice.

この<ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>れやすい**から、<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>をつけて。<br>*These plates break easily, so be careful.*

この<ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>は<ruby>丈夫<rt>じょうぶ</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>れにくい**です。<br>*These plates are sturdy and don't break easily.*

---

## ～やすい · ～やすい vs ～にくい (cont.)

That pair is a clean mirror. Now compare <code><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>やすい</code>, an everyday word meaning "easy to get to", with <code><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>にくい</code>, which a listener will usually take socially - awkwardness about turning up - rather than as a statement about distance. The lesson is to learn the useful compounds as words, not to generate every possible one mechanically.

---

## ～やすい · ～やすい vs 簡単だ

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>だ</code> judges the **task**; `～やすい` credits a **thing** with making the task go smoothly.

<ruby>図<rt>ず</rt></ruby>があるので、この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えやすい**です。<br>*There's a diagram, so this question is easy to think through.*

Compare この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>です, which says the question is not difficult. The `やすい` sentence says nothing about its difficulty - it says the diagram makes the thinking go smoothly. That gap matters when you write a review: このアプリは<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>です sounds like the app barely does anything, while <code><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすい</code> praises how it was built.

---

## ～やすい · ～やすい vs the potential form

> **Rule of thumb** - the potential form asks *can you at all?*; `～やすい` asks *how smoothly?*

この<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さいですが、**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすい**です。<br>*The characters are small, but they're easy to read.*

<code><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めます</code> is a yes-or-no ability. `やすい` is a comfort rating, and a sentence can carry both at once: <ruby>眼鏡<rt>めがね</rt></ruby>をかければ<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めますが、<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすくはありません。 "I can read it with glasses on, but it isn't comfortable reading."

---

## ～やすい · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>う**やすい** → ✅ <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>**いやすい**<br>*`やすい` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>います → <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い + やすい.*

- ❌ <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすい**でした** → ✅ <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやす**かったです**<br>*The compound is an い-adjective, so the past tense is built on its `い`, and `です` simply rides on top without changing.*

- ❌ この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすいです。 → ✅ この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすいです。<br>*Once `やすい` is attached there is no verb left to take an object, so the former object becomes the topic or the subject.*

- ❌ うちは**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>やすい**です。 → ✅ うちは**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>やすい**です。<br>*<code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る</code> contributes its ます-stem here, the same <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby> you see in <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます - not the ない-form stem <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>.*

{reveal}

## ～やすい · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This town is quiet and easy to live in."

{reveal}

<code><ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>む</code> gives the stem <ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>み, and a な-adjective links onward with `で`. <code><ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>みやすい</code> is the ordinary way to praise a place as liveable.

---

## ～やすい · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>きやすいじゃないです。

{reveal}
✅ この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>**は<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>きやすくない**です。

{reveal}
There are two faults. The object particle `を` cannot survive, because the compound is an adjective and adjectives take no object; and `じゃない` is the negative for nouns and な-adjectives, while `やすい` needs `やすくない`.

---

## ～やすい · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この<ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>は<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>りやすい。 (b) この<ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>は<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>れやすい。

{reveal}
The difference lives in the verb, not in `やすい`. (a) uses transitive <code><ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>る</code>, so somebody is deliberately breaking the plate and finding it easy - the sort of remark you would make while smashing old crockery on purpose.

{reveal}
(b) uses intransitive <code><ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>れる</code>, so nothing is deliberate: the plate is fragile and breaks of its own accord. This is the sentence you actually want when warning someone to handle it carefully.

---

# ～にくい

**Meaning** · hard to do - the action meets resistance, physical or social

`～にくい` is built the same way as `～やすい`, from the ます-stem, and it comes out as an い-adjective describing the thing. But it is not simply "not easy". `にくい` says there is something in the way: the meat is tough, the handle is the wrong shape, the request is embarrassing to make.

---

## ～にくい · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + にくい | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくい |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + にくい | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べにくい |
| Verb (する) | し + にくい | <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>しにくい |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby> + にくい | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>にくい |

The compound then behaves as a plain い-adjective:

---

## ～にくい · Formation (cont.)

| い-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | + です | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくいです |
| Negative | にくい → にくくない | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくくない |
| Past | にくい → にくかった | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくかった |
| て-form | にくい → にくくて | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくくて |
| Before a noun | にくい + Noun | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくい<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |
| Adverbial | にくい → にくく | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくくなる |
| Noun form | にくい → にくさ | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくさ |

---

## ～にくい · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - `にくい` is occasionally written <ruby>難<rt>にく</rt></ruby>い, but kana is the normal spelling for the suffix. Keep it apart from the unrelated adjective <ruby>憎<rt>にく</rt></ruby>い, "hateful", which is a full word in its own right and never attaches to a stem.

---

## ～にくい · Examples

この<ruby>肉<rt>にく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>固<rt>かた</rt></ruby>くて**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べにくい**。<br>*This meat is tough and hard to eat.*

その<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きにくい**です。<br>*That kanji is awkward to write.*

<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>暗<rt>くら</rt></ruby>くて、<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みにくい**。<br>*The room is dark and the writing is hard to read.*

すみません、ちょっと**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくい**ことなんですが…<br>*Sorry - this is a little awkward to bring up, but…*

---

## ～にくい · Examples (cont.)

この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>くて、**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりにくかった**です。<br>*The write-up for this question was long, and it was hard to follow.*

この<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>強<rt>つよ</rt></ruby>くて、**<ruby>壊<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>れにくい**そうです。<br>*Apparently this umbrella stands up to wind and doesn't break easily.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいスマホは<ruby>画面<rt>がめん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きすぎて、<ruby>片手<rt>かたて</rt></ruby>では**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いにくい**です。<br>*The new phone's screen is too big, so it's awkward to use one-handed.*

<ruby>先輩<rt>せんぱい</rt></ruby>には<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し**<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>みにくかった**ので、<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じクラスの<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってもらいました。<br>*It felt a bit awkward to ask my senior, so I got a classmate to help me instead.*

---

## ～にくい · Nuance & register

`にくい` covers three kinds of resistance, and it is worth naming them because learners tend to notice only the first.

1. **Physical** - the shape, size or state of a thing fights the action: <code><ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>りにくい</code>, <code><ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ちにくい</code>, <code><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きにくい</code>. 2. **Social or emotional** - nothing physical is stopping you; it is simply uncomfortable: <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくい</code>, <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きにくい</code>, <code><ruby>断<rt>ことわ</rt></ruby>りにくい</code>. This is one of the most useful things `にくい` does, and it has no neat English equivalent. 3. **Resistance to a spontaneous event** - with a verb that happens by itself, `にくい` means the thing holds out against it: <code><ruby>汚<rt>よご</rt></ruby>れにくい</code>, <code><ruby>冷<rt>さ</rt></ruby>めにくい</code>, <code><ruby>太<rt>ふと</rt></ruby>りにくい</code>. This sense is a selling point, and packaging copy is full of it.

---

## ～にくい · Nuance & register (cont.)

The social sense earns its keep as a softener. Opening a difficult request with <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくいんですが or お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いしにくいのですが tells the listener you know you are imposing, and it is standard in polite adult speech at any level of formality.

`にくい` itself is register-neutral: no politeness, no gender or age marking. In casual speech people often reach for `づらい` instead (see below), but `にくい` is never wrong and is the safer choice in writing.

Two derived forms carry over from `やすい`. `にくくなる` - "become harder": <ruby>年<rt>とし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>ると、<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>が<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えにくくなります。 And the noun in `さ`: この<ruby>道具<rt>どうぐ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いにくさが<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>です。

---

## ～にくい · ～にくい vs ～づらい

> **Rule of thumb** - `づらい` is about **the speaker's own discomfort** while doing something on purpose. `にくい` is about the situation, and it can also describe a thing resisting an event nobody is doing.

`づらい` is <code><ruby>辛<rt>つら</rt></ruby>い</code>, "painful", in suffix form, and that origin still shows: it reports how the action feels to the person performing it.

<ruby>足<rt>あし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>くて、<ruby>階段<rt>かいだん</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>上<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>りづらい**です。<br>*My leg hurts, so the stairs are hard going.*

このドアは<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>くて**<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けにくい**です。<br>*This door is heavy and hard to open.*

---

## ～にくい · ～にくい vs ～づらい (cont.)

Both would be understood either way round, and for volitional actions （<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いにくい／<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いづらい） the two are close to interchangeable, with `づらい` a shade more subjective and more colloquial. The clear split is at sense 3 above: for a thing resisting a spontaneous event, `にくい` is the settled choice. <code><ruby>壊<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>れづらい</code> is increasingly heard in casual speech, but many speakers still prefer <code><ruby>壊<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>れにくい</code>, and it is what you will see in print.

---

## ～にくい · ～にくい vs ～がたい

> **Rule of thumb** - `がたい` means *almost impossible to bring oneself to*, it is formal and written, and it attaches to a short fixed list of verbs.

その<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>信<rt>しん</rt></ruby>じがたい**。<br>*That story is hard to credit.*

Compare <code><ruby>信<rt>しん</rt></ruby>じにくい</code>, which is the everyday spoken version and sounds milder. `がたい` lives on a handful of verbs of thought and feeling - <ruby>信<rt>しん</rt></ruby>じる, <ruby>想像<rt>そうぞう</rt></ruby>する, <ruby>理解<rt>りかい</rt></ruby>する, <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れる - and never on a physical action: ❌<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>りがたい is not Japanese, while <code><ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>りにくい</code> is ordinary. Treat `がたい` as vocabulary to recognise rather than a productive pattern.

---

## ～にくい · ～にくい vs the negative potential

> **Rule of thumb** - `にくい` says the action is possible but unpleasant. The negative potential says it cannot be done at all.

この<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さくて**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みにくい**ですが、<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めます。<br>*This print is small and hard on the eyes, but I can read it.*

Swap in <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めません and the sentence contradicts itself. This distinction is worth practising because English uses "can't read this" loosely for both. If a magnifying glass would solve it, you want `にくい`.

---

## ～にくい · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる**にくい** → ✅ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>**べにくい**<br>*Like `やすい` and `すぎる`, `にくい` attaches to the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.*

- ❌ この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>し**にくい**です。 → ✅ この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みにくい**です。<br>*`にくい` attaches to verbs only. There is no way to hang it on an adjective; if you want "hard", the adjective <ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しい already says it.*

- ❌ <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いにくい**でした** → ✅ <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いにく**かったです**<br>*The whole compound is an い-adjective, so the past tense is formed on its `い`.*

---

## ～にくい · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きにくいです。 → ✅ この<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きにくいです。<br>*There is no verb left to take an object once `にくい` is attached, so the thing being described becomes the topic.*

---

## ～にくい · Practice 1

**1.** このコップは<ruby>口<rt>くち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さいので、<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>____です。

{reveal}
<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>**いにくい**です

{reveal}
The stem is <ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>い, from <ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>います. A narrow opening physically gets in the way of washing, which is exactly the resistance `にくい` reports.

---

## ～にくい · Practice 2

**2.** この<ruby>服<rt>ふく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>色<rt>いろ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>濃<rt>こ</rt></ruby>いので、<ruby>汚<rt>よご</rt></ruby>れが（<ruby>目立<rt>めだ</rt></ruby>ちやすい・<ruby>目立<rt>めだ</rt></ruby>ちにくい）です。

{reveal}
<ruby>汚<rt>よご</rt></ruby>れが**<ruby>目立<rt>めだ</rt></ruby>ちにくい**です。

{reveal}
Dark colours hide marks, so the stains resist standing out. This is the third sense: a thing holding out against something that happens by itself, which is the one advertising copy uses constantly.

---

## ～にくい · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "That's a hard thing to ask my boss."

{reveal}
それは<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>には**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きにくい**ことです。

{reveal}
Nothing physical prevents the question; the obstacle is social, and that is precisely what `にくい` conveys. <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きづらい</code> would also work here and sounds slightly more personal.

## ～にくい · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: この<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>くて<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>ぶにくいでした。

{reveal}
✅ この<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>くて**<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>びにくかった**です。

{reveal}
Two faults in one short sentence: `にくい` needs the ます-stem <ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>び, not the dictionary form, and the past of an い-adjective is `にくかった`, never `にくいでした`.

---

## ～にくい · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この<ruby>質問<rt>しつもん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>えにくいです。 (b) この<ruby>質問<rt>しつもん</rt></ruby>には<ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>えられません。

{reveal}
(a) says an answer is available but uncomfortable to give - you know the answer and would rather not say it, or the question is delicate. It leaves the door open and often precedes an answer anyway.

{reveal}
(b) shuts the door: you cannot answer, whether from ignorance or from a rule that forbids it. Choosing (b) when you mean (a) sounds far more final than you probably intend, which is why (a) is the diplomatic option.

# ～さ

**Meaning** · turns an adjective into a noun - the height, the weight, the difficulty of it

At N5 you learned to nominalise a whole clause with `の`. `さ` does something narrower and more mechanical: it takes a single adjective and gives you the noun for the **dimension it measures**. <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い</code> is "high"; <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>さ</code> is "height" - a scale that short things have too.

---

## ～さ · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | drop い + さ | <ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>さ |
| な-adjective | stem (no な, no だ) + さ | <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>さ |
| いい／よい | よ + さ (irregular) | いい → よさ |
| ない | な + さ | ない → なさ |
| ～やすい／～にくい | drop い + さ | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすい → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みやすさ |
| ～たい | たい → たさ | <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たい → <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たさ |

---

## ～さ · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - whatever you would put in front of `くない` (for い-adjectives) or in front of `です` (for な-adjectives) is what `さ` attaches to. If you can still see an `い` or a `な`, you have cut too little.

A `さ`-noun is an ordinary noun from that moment on. It takes `が`, `を`, `の`, `に` like any other: <ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>さ**を**<ruby>測<rt>はか</rt></ruby>る、<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>さ**に**<ruby>驚<rt>おどろ</rt></ruby>く、 <ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>さ**が**<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りない。

> **Note** - `さ` is productive: given an adjective, you can generally build its `さ`-noun and be understood. The exceptions are な-adjectives that are already nouns in their own right - <ruby>健康<rt>けんこう</rt></ruby>, <ruby>自由<rt>じゆう</rt></ruby>, <ruby>安心<rt>あんしん</rt></ruby> - where the bare word normally does the job and the `さ`-form sounds laboured.

---

## ～さ · Examples

この<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>さ**は<ruby>三千<rt>さんぜん</rt></ruby>メートルです。<br>*This mountain is three thousand metres high.*

テーブルの**<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>さ**を<ruby>測<rt>はか</rt></ruby>ってください。<br>*Please measure the length of the table.*

この<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>さ**はどのくらいですか。<br>*How much does this parcel weigh?*

<ruby>今年<rt>ことし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>さ**は<ruby>特別<rt>とくべつ</rt></ruby>でした。<br>*The heat this summer was something else.*

---

## ～さ · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>の**やさしさ**に<ruby>感謝<rt>かんしゃ</rt></ruby>しています。<br>*I'm grateful for my teacher's kindness.*

この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>の**よさ**は、<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>親切<rt>しんせつ</rt></ruby>なところです。<br>*What's good about this town is that the people are kind.*

<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>してみて、<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しさ**がやっと<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりました。<br>*Now that I've tried studying Japanese, I finally understand how hard kanji are.*

この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>さ**が<ruby>十分<rt>じゅうぶん</rt></ruby>ですが、<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>が<ruby>少<rt>すく</rt></ruby>なくて<ruby>暗<rt>くら</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*This room is big enough, but it has few windows and it's dark.*

---

## ～さ · Nuance & register

The single most useful thing to understand about `さ` is that it is **neutral**. <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>さ</code> does not mean "tallness" in the sense of being tall; it names the scale, and a low thing has one too. That is why この<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>さは<ruby>七十<rt>ななじゅう</rt></ruby>センチです is perfectly ordinary Japanese about an unremarkable desk. The same goes for <code><ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>さ</code>, <code><ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>さ</code>, <code><ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>さ</code>, <code><ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>さ</code>, <code><ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>さ</code> - these are the words you use to ask for a measurement.

With adjectives that judge rather than measure - <code><ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しさ</code>, <code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しさ</code>, <code><ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>さ</code>, やさしさ - the noun names *how much* or *the quality of being so*, and it tends to turn up after verbs of realising and feeling: <ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>さに<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づく、<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しさを<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>る。

---

## ～さ · Nuance & register (cont.)

`さ` is register-neutral, and it is if anything commoner in writing than in speech: product specifications, recipes, weather reports and essays all lean on it. Nothing about it sounds stiff in conversation either.

> **Note** - do not confuse this suffix with the casual sentence-final particle `さ`, as in そんなの<ruby>関係<rt>かんけい</rt></ruby>ないさ. That one attaches to a finished sentence, is markedly casual, and leans male in most speech. Two unrelated words that happen to share a syllable.

---

## ～さ · ～さ vs ～み

> **Rule of thumb** - `さ` is productive and measures. `み` is **not productive**: it belongs to a fixed set of established words, and it names something felt or concrete rather than a quantity.

This is the pair learners most want to treat as a free choice, and it is not one. You may build a `さ`-noun from any adjective; you may only *use* the `み`-nouns that already exist.

この<ruby>石<rt>いし</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>さ**は<ruby>五<rt>ご</rt></ruby>キロです。<br>*This stone weighs five kilos.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>には**<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>み**があります。<br>*The manager's words carry weight.*

---

## ～さ · ～さ vs ～み (cont.)

The established pairs are worth learning as vocabulary: <code><ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>さ</code> is a depth in metres while <code><ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>み</code> is a deep spot or a richness of character; <code><ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>さ</code> is a degree of sweetness while <code><ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>み</code> is the taste itself; <code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しさ</code> is how enjoyable something is while <code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しみ</code> has become a plain noun meaning something you look forward to. Some adjectives have only the `み` form in common use - the noun for hurting is <code><ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>み</code>, not <code><ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>さ</code>, unless you specifically mean the degree of the pain.

Anything outside that set is not available: ❌<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しみ, ❌<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しみ, ❌<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>み. When in doubt, use `さ`.

---

## ～さ · ～さ vs こと／の

> **Rule of thumb** - `さ` nominalises **one adjective** and names its degree. `こと` and `の` nominalise **a whole clause** and name the fact.

<ruby>川<rt>かわ</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>さ**に<ruby>驚<rt>おどろ</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I was taken aback by how deep the river was.*

Compare <ruby>川<rt>かわ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>いことに<ruby>驚<rt>おどろ</rt></ruby>きました, "I was surprised at the fact that the river was deep." The `さ` version takes the depth for granted and points at its degree; the `こと` version presents the depth as news. The `さ` one is also the only option once you attach a number: <ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>さは<ruby>二<rt>に</rt></ruby>メートルです, never ❌<ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>いことは<ruby>二<rt>に</rt></ruby>メートルです.

---

## ～さ · ～さ vs こと／の (cont.)

Because `さ` only takes adjectives, a verb has no `さ`-form at all: for "swimming", you still need <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐこと or <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐの.

---

## ～さ · ～さ vs leaving the adjective alone

> **Rule of thumb** - use `さ` only when the sentence needs a **noun** in that slot. If the adjective can simply be the predicate, leave it as an adjective.

Learners who have just met `さ` tend to sprinkle it everywhere, and the result is a sentence that says the same thing twice:

この<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**です。<br>*This mountain is high.*

---

## ～さ · ～さ vs leaving the adjective alone (cont.)

❌この<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>の<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>さは<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いです is not a stronger version of that sentence; it is a broken one, roughly "the height of this mountain is high." Use `さ` when a noun is genuinely required - as the object of <code><ruby>測<rt>はか</rt></ruby>る</code>, after `に`, before `です` with a number, or as the topic of a comparison: この<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>の<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>さは<ruby>富士山<rt>ふじさん</rt></ruby>と<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じくらいです。

---

## ～さ · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>**いさ** → ✅ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>**さ**<br>*The final `い` of an い-adjective is replaced, not kept.*

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**なさ**／<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**ださ** → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**さ**<br>*な-adjectives contribute their bare stem. Neither `な` nor `だ` survives.*

- ❌ この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>の**いさ** → ✅ この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>の**よさ**<br>*`いい` is irregular everywhere, and `さ` is no exception - it builds on the older stem `よ`, exactly as `よくない` and `よかった` do.*

- ❌ <ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>し**み**を<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>しました。（meaning "I told them how fun the trip was"）→ ✅ <ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>し**さ**を<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>しました。<br>*<code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しみ</code> is a separate word meaning something you are looking forward to, so the sentence with `み` says you talked about your anticipation.*

---

## ～さ · Practice 1

**1.** このプールの<ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>____は<ruby>二<rt>に</rt></ruby>メートルです。

{reveal}
<ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>**さ**は<ruby>二<rt>に</rt></ruby>メートルです

{reveal}
A number of metres needs a noun in front of `です`, and that noun is the measurement word. Drop the `い` of <ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>い and add `さ`.

## ～さ · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>が（<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しさ・<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しみ）です。

{reveal}

{reveal}
This is the fixed lexical `み` word, meaning something you look forward to, and it is the only natural choice here. <code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しさ</code> would name how enjoyable the trip was, which you cannot yet know about next week.

## ～さ · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I was surprised at the size of this city."

{reveal}

{reveal}

## ～さ · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: この<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いさを<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてください。

{reveal}

{reveal}
The `い` of <ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い is replaced by `さ`, not followed by it. A useful check: the finished noun should end in exactly one `さ` and contain no `い` from the adjective ending.

## ～さ · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この<ruby>石<rt>いし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>さ (b) この<ruby>石<rt>いし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>み

{reveal}
(a) is the measurable weight, the thing a scale reports in kilos. It is neutral and works for a pebble as well as a boulder.

{reveal}
(b) is the felt heaviness - the sense of substance or gravity the stone gives you when you pick it up, and by extension the impressiveness of something. It is one of the established `み` words, and it is a judgement rather than a measurement. You cannot generate new `み` nouns this way; where no fixed `み` word exists, `さ` is what you use.

# ～方 - way of doing

**Meaning** · the way of doing something - how to do it, or the manner in which someone does it

Read `かた`. Attach it to a verb's ます-stem and you get a noun: <code><ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>り<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code> "how to make it", <code><ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code> "the way someone speaks". This is the single most productive noun-building suffix at N4, and it turns any verb you already know into something you can ask about, teach, learn and criticise.

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + <ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby> | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby> |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + <ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby> | <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ける → <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>け<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby> |
| Verb (する) | the noun is <ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby> | する → <ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby> |
| Noun + する | Noun + の + <ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby> | <ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby> |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby> + <ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby> | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby><ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>（rare） |

> **Rule of thumb** - the object particle `を` becomes `の`. <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>. Nothing in the phrase is a verb any more, so nothing can take an object.

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Formation (cont.)

A destination `に`／`へ` usually survives, with `の` added after it: <ruby>空港<rt>くうこう</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>空港<rt>くうこう</rt></ruby>への<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>. Some fixed pairs simply drop the particle instead - バスに<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>る is normally バスの<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>り<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby> - so learn the common ones as phrases.

The finished word is an ordinary noun and behaves like one: <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からない、<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>り<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>う、<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**で**<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かる。

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - `方` is only one of the things a ます-stem can do. The bare stem is itself a noun-maker, and a good deal of everyday vocabulary is built that way: <ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>き, <ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まる → <ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まり, <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>り, <ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>える → <ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>え, <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み. Not every verb yields a usable one, so treat these as words to collect rather than forms to generate - but when you meet a noun that looks like a ます-stem, it usually is one.

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Examples

この<ruby>機械<rt>きかい</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてください。<br>*Please show me how to use this machine.*

この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**が<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりません。<br>*I don't know how to read this kanji.*

カレーの**<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>り<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**は<ruby>意外<rt>いがい</rt></ruby>と<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Curry is easier to make than you'd think.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**はとてもていねいです。<br>*The way she speaks is very polite.*

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>空港<rt>くうこう</rt></ruby>への**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**をスマホで<ruby>調<rt>しら</rt></ruby>べた。<br>*I looked up how to get to the airport on my phone.*

おはしの**<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**は<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものときに<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*I learned how to hold chopsticks when I was a child.*

パソコンの<ruby>調子<rt>ちょうし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>いので、<ruby>修理<rt>しゅうり</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby>**をネットで<ruby>探<rt>さが</rt></ruby>しています。<br>*My computer is playing up, so I'm hunting online for how to repair it.*

<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じことを<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えても、**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**によって<ruby>相手<rt>あいて</rt></ruby>の<ruby>気持<rt>きも</rt></ruby>ちは<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わります。<br>*Even when you convey the same thing, how you put it changes how the other person feels.*

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Nuance & register

`～方` splits into two closely related senses, and it is worth knowing which one you are producing.

- **Method** - <code><ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>り<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code>,<br>*<code><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code>. These answer "how do I…?" and are what you ask a shop assistant or a recipe.*
- **Manner** - <code><ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code>,<br>*<code><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></code>. These describe someone's personal style and almost always come with an evaluation attached: <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>がきれいですね。*

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Nuance & register (cont.)

Because the manner sense evaluates a person, it can carry criticism. <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>かった - "I put that badly" - is a standard apology, and あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>はちょっと… is a soft way of saying you dislike how someone talks. Nothing about the suffix itself is rude; the judgement comes from the adjective you pair it with.

The suffix is register-neutral and equally normal in speech and writing. <code><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えていただけますか</code> is a polite request; <code><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby><ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かる?</code> is what you say to a friend. Only the surrounding grammar changes.

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Nuance & register (cont.)

One high-frequency idiom: <code><ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby>がない</code> - "it can't be helped", "there's nothing for it". In casual speech this contracts to <code><ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby>ない</code> or, very commonly, しょうがない. Learn it as a set phrase; the `方` inside it is the same word, but the meaning of the whole is fixed.

---

## ～方 - way of doing · ～方 vs 方法

> **Rule of thumb** - `～方` is built out of a specific verb and is everyday. <code><ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby></code> is a free-standing noun meaning "method", and it is more abstract and more formal.

この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>解<rt>と</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてください。<br>*Please show me how to solve this problem.*

Compare この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>解決<rt>かいけつ</rt></ruby>する<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えましょう - "let's think of a way to resolve this issue." The `方` version is about the concrete steps of one action; <code><ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby></code> takes a whole clause in front of it and suits reports, meetings and instructions. In a manual you will often see both: <ruby>支払<rt>しはら</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby> for the section heading and <ruby>払<rt>はら</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby> in the shop.

---

## ～方 - way of doing · ～方 vs どうやって

> **Rule of thumb** - どうやって **asks** the question. `～方` packages the answer as a noun you can then do things to.

このお<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>の**いれ<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**は<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しいです。<br>*The way you brew this tea is a little tricky.*

You could ask このお<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>はどうやっていれますか and get the same information, but only the noun can be the subject of <ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しい, the object of <ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>う, or the thing you <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れた. That is the whole point of nominalising: どうやって is a question word and cannot sit in a noun slot.

---

## ～方 - way of doing · 方 vs 方

> **Rule of thumb** - after a ます-stem, `方` is always **かた**. The `ほう` reading belongs to the comparison word <code>～の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が</code>, which follows a noun or a plain-form clause, not a stem.

バスの**<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>り<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**が<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からなくて、<ruby>駅員<rt>えきいん</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I didn't know how to ride the bus, so I asked a station attendant.*

Set that against <ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いです, "the train is faster", where `方` means "side" and is read ほう. There is a third word here too: `方` read かた as a free noun is the polite word for a person - あの<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>はどなたですか. Same character, three jobs; the stem test sorts out the suffix instantly.

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>** → ✅ <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>**み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**<br>*`方` attaches to the ます-stem, like `やすい` and `すぎる`. The dictionary form before `方` produces the *ほう* comparison word instead, which means something else entirely.*

- ❌ <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby><br>*The compound is a noun, so its former object is attached with `の`.*

- ❌ <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>**し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>** → ✅ <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>**の<ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby>**<br>*A noun + する verb uses the lexicalised noun <ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby>, linked with `の`.*

---

## ～方 - way of doing · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>の<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み**<ruby>方<rt>ほう</rt></ruby>**を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてください。 → ✅ …<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み**<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>**…<br>*A written mistake becomes a spoken one the moment you read it aloud. On a ます-stem the reading is かた, always.*

---

# ～がる

**Meaning** · to show signs of feeling something - how you report another person's inner state

Japanese does not let you simply announce what somebody else feels. `～がる` solves that: it takes an adjective of emotion or sensation and turns it into a **verb** meaning "behaves as though X". You are no longer claiming to know what is in their head; you are describing what you can see them doing.

---

## ～がる · Formation

`がる` attaches to the adjective stem and produces an ordinary う-verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| い-adjective | drop い + がる | <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>がる |
| な-adjective | stem + がる | <ruby>嫌<rt>いや</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>嫌<rt>いや</rt></ruby>がる |
| ほしい | ほし + がる | ほしい → ほしがる |
| ～たい | たい → たがる | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがる |

Once formed, it conjugates as a う-verb like <code><ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>る</code>:

---

## ～がる · Formation (cont.)

| Verb form | Example |
|---|---|
| Polite | <ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がります |
| Negative | <ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がらない |
| Past | <ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がった |
| て-form | <ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がって |
| Progressive / current state | <ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がっている |
| Person noun | <ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がり、<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>がり、<ruby>恥<rt>は</rt></ruby>ずかしがり<ruby>屋<rt>や</rt></ruby> |

> **Rule of thumb** - `がる` makes a transitive verb, so what the feeling is aimed at takes `を`, not the `が` the original adjective wanted: おもちゃ**が**ほしい → おもちゃ**を**ほしがる.

---

## ～がる · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - `がる` only attaches to adjectives describing **how someone feels**: <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い, <ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>い, <ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>い, <ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>い, うれしい, <ruby>悲<rt>かな</rt></ruby>しい, さびしい, <ruby>恥<rt>は</rt></ruby>ずかしい, うらやましい, ほしい, <ruby>嫌<rt>いや</rt></ruby>だ, <ruby>残念<rt>ざんねん</rt></ruby>だ. It cannot attach to an adjective describing a thing from outside: there is no ❌<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>がる or ❌<ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>がる.

---

## ～がる · Examples

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが**<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>がって**います。<br>*The children are feeling the cold.*

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がります**。<br>*My little sister is afraid of dogs.*

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>時計<rt>とけい</rt></ruby>を**ほしがって**いる。<br>*My father's had his eye on a new watch.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>注射<rt>ちゅうしゃ</rt></ruby>のあとで<ruby>腕<rt>うで</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>がって**いました。<br>*My son was making a fuss about his arm after the injection.*

---

## ～がる · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>はおふろを**<ruby>嫌<rt>いや</rt></ruby>がる**ので、<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>うのが<ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Cats hate baths, so washing them is hard work.*

みんなが**<ruby>不思議<rt>ふしぎ</rt></ruby>がって**いたので、<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>しました。<br>*Everyone seemed puzzled, so I explained the reason.*

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>んでいるので、ときどき**さびしがり**ます。<br>*Yamada lives on his own, so he gets lonely from time to time.*

<ruby>先輩<rt>せんぱい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>留学<rt>りゅうがく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いて、ずいぶん**うらやましがって**いました。<br>*My senior sounded thoroughly envious when he heard about the exchange programme.*

---

## ～がる · Nuance & register

The heart of `がる` is **evidence**. <code><ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>がっている</code> does not mean "is in pain"; it means the person is behaving in a way that shows pain - wincing, holding the spot, saying it hurts. That is why the pattern belongs to third persons: you can see other people's behaviour, but you feel your own sensations directly.

**Bare `がる` versus `がっている`.** The plain form describes a habit or a general truth: <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもは<ruby>注射<rt>ちゅうしゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がる is "children are afraid of injections", a statement about children at large. For what someone is doing right now, use `がっている`.

---

## ～がる · Nuance & register (cont.)

**First person.** You would not normally say ❌<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby><ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>がっています - for your own present feeling, the plain adjective <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いです is what you want. But `がる` is fine about yourself when you step outside your own experience: describing a habit （<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>昔<rt>むかし</rt></ruby>から<ruby>暗<rt>くら</rt></ruby>いところを<ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がります）or looking back on your younger self.

**Tone.** Because it reports behaviour, `がる` can slide into "making a fuss about", and with some verbs it tends to sound faintly critical or amused. That tendency, plus the fact that it is a bald description of someone's inner life, makes it a poor fit for talking about a superior. Prefer a hedge: <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>残念<rt>ざんねん</rt></ruby>そうでした, or <ruby>残念<rt>ざんねん</rt></ruby>だとおっしゃっていました.

---

## ～がる · Nuance & register (cont.)

**The `がり` nouns.** Drop the verb ending and you get a noun for the kind of person: <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>がり (someone who feels the cold), <ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>がり, <ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>がり, and with `屋` attached, さびしがり<ruby>屋<rt>や</rt></ruby> and <ruby>恥<rt>は</rt></ruby>ずかしがり<ruby>屋<rt>や</rt></ruby>. These are everyday self-descriptions - <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>、<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>がりなんです is a perfectly normal thing to say in January.

`がる` on `たい` gives `たがる`, which is common enough to get its own treatment; everything above about evidence, third persons and politeness applies there too.

---

## ～がる · ～がる vs ～そうだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうだ` is a guess from **how something looks** at a glance. `がる` reports **what someone is actually doing**.

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>上着<rt>うわぎ</rt></ruby>をぬいで、ずっと**<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>がって**います。<br>*He's taken his jacket off and has been hot the whole time.*

Compare <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>そうです, "he looks hot" - one look, one inference, no claim about behaviour over time. Two further differences follow from this. `そうだ` works on things as well as people （このケーキ、おいしそう）, while `がる` needs someone with feelings; and `そうだ` is a much softer thing to say about a superior, which is why it is the polite way out.

---

## ～がる · ～がる vs the plain adjective

> **Rule of thumb** - in a plain main-clause statement, an emotion adjective about a third person needs `がる` or a hedge. About yourself, use the bare adjective.

<ruby>留学生<rt>りゅうがくせい</rt></ruby>のリーさんは<ruby>国<rt>くに</rt></ruby>の<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>が<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられなくて、**さびしがって**います。<br>*Lee, the exchange student, can't get food from home and is feeling homesick.*

A flat ❌リーさんはさびしいです claims direct access to Lee's feelings. The escapes are `がる`, or one of the reporting frames you already have: さびしいそうです, さびしいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っています, さびしいようです. Inside a subordinate clause the restriction lifts entirely - さびしい<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>してください is fine.

---

## ～がる · ほしがる vs ほしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` is an い-adjective and takes `が`; `ほしがる` is a verb and takes `を`. The particle change is the giveaway that the word class changed.

<ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>のパソコンを**ほしがって**います。<br>*My daughter wants a computer of her own.*

Set that against <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>のパソコンがほしいです for your own want. The same asymmetry you met with `たい` at N5 is at work: the adjective is for the inside view, the `がる` verb for the outside one. And as with `たい`, `ほしがっている` is the normal shape for a want someone has right now, while bare ほしがる suits a habit.

---

## ～がる · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>**いがる** → ✅ <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>**がる**<br>*The final `い` is replaced, exactly as it is for `さ` and `すぎる`.*

- ❌ <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>はおもちゃ**が**ほしがっています。 → ✅ <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>はおもちゃ**を**ほしがっています。<br>*`がる` builds a transitive verb, and transitive verbs take `を`. Leaving `が` in place is the single commonest slip with this pattern.*

- ❌ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>がる**。 → ✅ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*`がる` needs an adjective about someone's feelings. <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い describes a price, not an emotion, so there is nothing for `がる` to report.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby><ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>**がって**います。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby><ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>**いです**。<br>*You do not need evidence for your own present sensation. Reserve `がる` for other people, or for yourself viewed as a type of person.*

# ～たがる

**Meaning** · someone else wants to do something - as read off their behaviour

`～たがる` is `がる` applied to `たい`. At N5 you learned that plain `～たい` is only for your own wishes and for asking about the listener's; `～たがる` is the form that lets you talk about everybody else. It is a verb, not an adjective, and it reports a want the way an observer sees it: from the outside, in what the person does.

---

## ～たがる · Formation

Take the ます-stem, add `たがる`. Equivalently: build the `たい` form and swap `たい` for `たがる`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + たがる | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたがる |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + たがる | <ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かける → <ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけたがる |
| Verb (する) | し + たがる | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>したがる |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby> + たがる | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たがる |

The result is a plain う-verb and conjugates like one:

---

## ～たがる · Formation (cont.)

| Verb form | Example |
|---|---|
| Polite | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがります |
| Negative | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがらない |
| Past | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがった |
| て-form | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがって |
| Current state | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがっている |
| Past state | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがっていた |
| Person noun | <ruby>目立<rt>めだ</rt></ruby>ちたがり<ruby>屋<rt>や</rt></ruby> |

---

## ～たがる · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the object takes `を`, never the `が` that `たい` permits. <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい, but <ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたがっている. `たい` is an adjective; `たがる` is a verb, and verbs take `を`.

---

## ～たがる · Examples

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>びたがって**います。<br>*My little brother wants to play outside.*

<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>りたがって**いる。<br>*The cat wants to get into the room.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたがりません**。<br>*My son won't eat his vegetables.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもはなんでも**<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りたがる**ものです。<br>*Children want to know about everything - that's just how they are.*

---

## ～たがる · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>のことをあまり**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>したがらない**。<br>*He isn't very forthcoming about himself.*

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちがその<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たがって**いたので、<ruby>一緒<rt>いっしょ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*A friend of mine had been wanting to see that film, so we went to see it together.*

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがらない**ので、<ruby>家族<rt>かぞく</rt></ruby>はみんな<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>しています。<br>*My father won't go to the doctor, so the whole family is worried.*

クラスのみんなが<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>へ**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがって**いたので、<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>しました。<br>*Everyone in the class had been wanting to go to Kyoto, so I planned a weekend trip.*

---

## ～たがる · Nuance & register

**The negative is the workhorse.** `～たがらない` is at least as common as the positive, and it does a job English handles with "won't": <ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたがらない is not a neutral report of an absent desire - it says the person is visibly resisting. Doctors, parents and teachers use it constantly.

**Bare form versus `～たがっている`.** As with `がる`, the plain form states a habit or a general truth - <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はいつも<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいことを<ruby>試<rt>ため</rt></ruby>したがる - while `たがっている` reports what someone wants at this moment. If you are describing what is going on in front of you, `ている` is almost always the right choice, and leaving it off is a common learner slip.

---

## ～たがる · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Tone.** Because it describes someone from outside, `たがる` can carry a faint edge - "keeps wanting to", "is always trying to". That edge is what powers <code><ruby>目立<rt>めだ</rt></ruby>ちたがり<ruby>屋<rt>や</rt></ruby></code>, "attention seeker", and it is why the pattern is a bad idea about anyone you are being polite to. Do not say ❌<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りたがっています. Say instead <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りたいとおっしゃっていました, or お<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りになりたいようです.

**Yourself.** `たがる` about your own present wish is wrong - you do not need to observe yourself. It does become available when you describe your own character from a distance: <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>昔<rt>むかし</rt></ruby>から<ruby>人前<rt>ひとまえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>いたがらない<ruby>性格<rt>せいかく</rt></ruby>です。

---

## ～たがる · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Register.** The pattern itself is neutral: no politeness marking, no gender or age association, equally at home in speech and writing. What is marked is *who* you point it at.

---

## ～たがる · ～たがる vs ～たい

> **Rule of thumb** - `たい` for me and for you; `たがる` for everyone else. Three things change at once: the person, the particle, and the word class.

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>はピアノを**<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>いたがって**います。<br>*My little sister wants to take piano lessons.*

Say <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はピアノが<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>いたいです about yourself and every piece of the sentence differs: `が` is allowed, and the polite form is `です` on an adjective rather than `ます` on a verb. Mixing the two produces ❌<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>いたがりたいです or ❌<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>いたがるです, both of which come from forgetting that `たがる` is a verb.

---

## ～たがる · ～たがる vs ～たい (cont.)

The restriction is on **plain main-clause statements** only. Inside a modifying clause, plain `たい` about a third person is fine: <ruby>参加<rt>さんか</rt></ruby>したい<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てください。

---

## ～たがる · ～たがっている vs ～たがる

> **Rule of thumb** - `たがっている` for a want someone has **now**; bare `たがる` for a **habit** or a general truth about a group.

<ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>ちゃんが**だっこされたがって**います。<br>*The baby wants to be picked up.*

Compare <ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>ちゃんは<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>になるとだっこされたがる, "babies want to be held once evening comes" - a pattern of behaviour rather than a report on this baby right now. The generic use is where you will most often meet the bare form, frequently rounded off with `ものだ`.

---

## ～たがる · ～たがる vs ～たいそうです・～たいと言っています

> **Rule of thumb** - `たがる` says you **saw** it. The reporting frames say you **heard** it, and they are the polite route upward.

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たがっていません。<br>*Tanaka doesn't want to sit in on the meeting.*

That sentence claims to read Tanaka from his conduct, which is fine among peers and rude about a superior. The alternatives shift the source of the information: <ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たくないそうです ("I hear he doesn't"), <ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たくないと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っています ("he says he doesn't"), <ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たくないようです ("he seems not to"). All three are softer, and the last two are what you use in a workplace when the person outranks you.

---

## ～たがる · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**たがる** → ✅ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>**きたがる**<br>*`たがる` builds on the ます-stem, exactly like `たい` does.*

- ❌ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもがジュース**が**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたがっています。 → ✅ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもがジュース**を**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたがっています。<br>*The `が` option belongs to `たい`. Once the word is a verb, the object must be marked with `を`.*

- ❌ <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがっ**たです**。 → ✅ <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがっ**ていました**。<br>*Learners carry `たい`'s adjective endings across. `たがる` is a う-verb: the past polite is <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがりました, and for a past state, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたがっていました.*

- ❌ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたがっています。 → ✅ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたいとおっしゃっていました。<br>*Grammatical, but it inspects a teacher's motives out loud. Report what they said instead.*

**1.** <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>外国<rt>がいこく</rt></ruby>で<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>____います。

{reveal}

# ～がする

**Meaning** · a sensation reaches you - a smell, a sound, a taste, a feeling

`する` is not always "do". With a small set of sensation nouns it means something closer to "there is" or "it comes to me": <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がする, "there's a sound"; においがする, "there's a smell". Nobody is performing anything. The sensation arrives, and the noun naming it is the subject.

---

## ～がする · Formation

Sensation noun + `が` + `する`. There is no conjugating of the noun; only `する` moves.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sound | <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>・<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby> + がする | <ruby>変<rt>へん</rt></ruby>な<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がする |
| Smell | におい・<ruby>香<rt>かお</rt></ruby>り + がする | いいにおいがする |
| Taste | <ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby> + がする | <ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>い<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>がする |
| Feeling, hunch | <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>・<ruby>感<rt>かん</rt></ruby>じ + がする | <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする |
| Bodily sensation | <ruby>寒気<rt>さむけ</rt></ruby>・<ruby>頭痛<rt>ずつう</rt></ruby>・めまい + がする | めまいがする |
| Sense of a presence | <ruby>気配<rt>けはい</rt></ruby> + がする | <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>気配<rt>けはい</rt></ruby>がする |

`する` is irregular and carries the whole conjugation:

---

## ～がする · Formation (cont.)

| Verb form | Example |
|---|---|
| Polite | <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がします |
| Negative | <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がしない |
| Past | <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がした |
| て-form | <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がして |
| Ongoing | <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がしている |

> **Rule of thumb** - the sensation is the **subject**, so the particle is `が`. Never ❌においをする. Nothing is being done to the smell; the smell is what is there.

---

## ～がする · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the list of nouns that work this way is short and fixed. Sight is not on it: for something visible you use <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える, never ❌<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>がする. Pain is not on it either - it is <ruby>頭<rt>あたま</rt></ruby>が<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>いです or <ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>みがあります, though the noun <ruby>頭痛<rt>ずつう</rt></ruby> does take がする.

---

## ～がする · Examples

だれかの**<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>がする**。<br>*I can hear someone's voice.*

<ruby>台所<rt>だいどころ</rt></ruby>からいい**においがします**。<br>*There's a lovely smell coming from the kitchen.*

このスープは<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>い**<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>がします**。<br>*This soup tastes slightly sweet.*

<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>で<ruby>変<rt>へん</rt></ruby>な**<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がした**ので、<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてみました。<br>*There was an odd noise outside, so I opened the window to look.*

---

## ～がする · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>から**<ruby>寒気<rt>さむけ</rt></ruby>がする**ので、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みます。<br>*I've had chills since this morning, so I'm taking the day off work.*

どこかでお<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いしたような**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします**。<br>*I have a feeling we've met somewhere before.*

この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>古<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>い<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>の**においがして**、なんだか<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*This room smells of old books, and something about that is calming.*

さっきから<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>階<rt>かい</rt></ruby>で<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いている**<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がして**、なかなか<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>れません。<br>*I've been hearing someone walking about upstairs for a while now, and I can't get to sleep.*

---

## ～がする · Nuance & register

The defining feature of `がする` is that the sensation is **unbidden and often unidentified**. <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がする is what you say when something made a noise and you do not yet know what: you noticed, you did not investigate. That is why it pairs so naturally with <ruby>変<rt>へん</rt></ruby>な, <ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きな and だれかの - words that admit you have not pinned the source down.

The noun can be modified freely, and this is where most of the expressive work happens: レモンの<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>がする, <ruby>焦<rt>こ</rt></ruby>げたにおいがする, <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもの<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>がする. A `の` phrase in front means "the smell/taste/sound *of* that thing", which is how you say something tastes *like* something else.

---

## ～がする · Nuance & register (cont.)

Tense matters more than usual here. `がした` reports one moment - a bang, a whiff. `がする` in the plain present covers a sensation happening now or as a general property （このお<ruby>菓子<rt>かし</rt></ruby>はバターのにおいがする）. `がしている` is available for something clearly ongoing, but Japanese reaches for the plain present far more readily than English does with "is smelling".

**`気がする` deserves separate attention.** It is one of the most used expressions in the spoken language: どこかで<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いたことがあるような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする, 「<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れ<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>をしたような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする」. It reports a hunch you cannot justify, and because it commits you to nothing it is very useful for softening - <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします is a gentler way to say something is overpriced.

---

## ～がする · Nuance & register (cont.)

The pattern is register-neutral: identical in casual and polite speech, and completely standard in writing. The medical uses - <ruby>寒気<rt>さむけ</rt></ruby>がする, <ruby>吐<rt>は</rt></ruby>き<ruby>気<rt>け</rt></ruby>がする, めまいがする - are the phrases you actually need at a clinic, and they are worth memorising as units.

---

## ～がする · ～がする vs 聞こえる・見える

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえる</code> says **you heard** a particular thing. `がする` says **there was** a sound. And there is no visual `がする`: for sight, only <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える</code>.

<ruby>夜中<rt>よなか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>がして**、<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>が<ruby>覚<rt>さ</rt></ruby>めました。<br>*A cat's cry in the night woke me up.*

---

## ～がする · ～がする vs 聞こえる・見える (cont.)

Compare <ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえました, "I could hear a cat." Both are correct, and the difference is where the sentence puts you. `聞こえる` is about your ears reaching the sound and implies you made it out; `がする` is about the sound being there at all, and sits comfortably with not knowing what it was. This is also why <ruby>気配<rt>けはい</rt></ruby>がする works while ❌<ruby>気配<rt>けはい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえる does not - a presence is not a thing you hear.

---

## ～がする · 気がする vs と思う

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする</code> is a hunch with no grounds behind it. <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う</code> is an opinion you would defend.

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りそうな**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします**。<br>*I've got a feeling it's going to rain today.*

Say <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ると<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います instead and you sound as though you checked the forecast. `気がする` explicitly declines to give a reason, which makes it the safer form when you are half-remembering something or disagreeing gently with someone senior.

---

## ～がする · ～がする vs ～をしている

> **Rule of thumb** - `がする` is a sensation coming **to** you. `をしている` describes a property a thing **has** - its colour, its shape, its expression.

この<ruby>石<rt>いし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>変<rt>へん</rt></ruby>わった<ruby>形<rt>かたち</rt></ruby>をしています。<br>*This stone has an unusual shape.*

Same verb, opposite direction, opposite particle. <ruby>色<rt>いろ</rt></ruby>をしている, <ruby>形<rt>かたち</rt></ruby>をしている, <ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>をしている are the three you will actually meet: <ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>い<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>をした<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>, <ruby>悲<rt>かな</rt></ruby>しそうな<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>をしていた. Sensations never take `を`, and appearances never take `が` in this construction.

---

## ～がする · Common mistakes

- ❌ いいにおい**を**します。 → ✅ いいにおい**が**します。<br>*The smell is the subject of the sentence, not an object someone acts on.*

- ❌ この<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>はいいにおいが**あります**。 → ✅ この<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>はいいにおいが**します**。<br>*English "has a nice smell" pushes learners towards ある. Japanese treats a perceived smell as something that *does* - it `する`.*

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>から<ruby>頭痛<rt>ずつう</rt></ruby>を**しています**。 → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>から<ruby>頭痛<rt>ずつう</rt></ruby>が**します**。<br>*Same particle error in a medical setting, where being understood matters most.*

---

## ～がする · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ あそこに<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>建物<rt>たてもの</rt></ruby>が**します**。 → ✅ あそこに<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>建物<rt>たてもの</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えます**。<br>*`がする` covers hearing, smell, taste and bodily feeling - never sight.*

# ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences

**Meaning** · singles out one part of a sentence and puts it in the spotlight - "the one who did it was…", "it was last year that…"

Take a sentence, nominalise all of it except the piece you care about, and put that piece after `は`…`だ`. <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>った becomes <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>った**のは<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>です** - "the one who broke the window was my brother." Everything before `のは` is treated as already agreed; everything after it is the answer.

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Formation

The clause takes the plain form and then `の`, exactly as with the N5 nominaliser. The focused element follows `は`, with `だ` or `です` closing the sentence.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | dictionary form + のは…だ | <ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>るのは<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>です |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のは…だ | <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>とした → <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたのは<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>です |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + のは…だ | <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかった → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかったのは<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんです |
| い-adjective | plain form + のは…だ | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いのはこちらです |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + のは…だ | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きだ → <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**な**のは<ruby>秋<rt>あき</rt></ruby>です |
| Focus = a reason | …のは…**からです** | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んだのは<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があったからです |

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the subject inside the `の`-clause takes `が`, never `は`. The sentence already has its `は`, and it belongs to the `のは` frame: <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったのはこの<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>です.

When the focus is a time, a place or a person, the particle that element would have carried is normally dropped: <ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ったのは<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>です. Keeping it - <ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>ででした - is possible in careful writing but sounds heavy, and learners are safer without it. A reason is the exception: it keeps `から` and adds です, as in the last row above.

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the `の` here is a light noun meaning "the one / the thing / the time". You can often replace it with the specific noun for extra clarity: <ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>った**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>**は<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>です, <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た**とき**は<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>でした. `の` is the general-purpose version, and it is what you reach for when no particular noun suggests itself.

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Examples

この<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby>を**かいたのは<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>です**。<br>*My little sister is the one who drew this picture.*

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きなのは<ruby>秋<rt>あき</rt></ruby>です**。<br>*The season I like is autumn.*

<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たのは<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>です**。<br>*It was last spring that I came to Japan.*

**<ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>いのは<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>です**。<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>にすみませんでした。<br>*I'm the one at fault. I really am sorry.*

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Nuance & register

A cleft splits a sentence into two halves that do different jobs. The `の`-clause is the part you and your listener are already taking for granted - somebody broke the window, I came to Japan at some point. The part after `は` is the news, and because the frame exists only to deliver it, the emphasis is much stronger than in an ordinary sentence.

That makes clefts the natural answer to a question word. Ask だれが<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>ったんですか and <ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>ったのは<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>です answers it exactly; ask いつ<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ましたか and <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たのは<ruby>先週<rt>せんしゅう</rt></ruby>です does the same. It is equally the form for **correcting** someone, because the frame lets you accept everything except the one piece you are disputing: <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったのはそういう<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>ではありません。

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Nuance & register (cont.)

The pattern is register-neutral. In polite speech the ending is です／でした; in casual speech it becomes `だ`, or vanishes altogether - <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたの、<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>? is a perfectly ordinary question between friends. It is if anything even commoner in writing, where <ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>なのは… and <ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>なのは… open paragraphs constantly.

One shape worth learning whole: <code><ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>なのは…ことです</code>. When the focus itself is an action rather than a thing, it has to be nominalised too, and there the nominaliser must be `こと`, not `の` - see the next section.

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · ～のは○○だ vs plain `が` focus

> **Rule of thumb** - `が` presents its noun as the news. The cleft does that *and* states out loud what everyone is presupposing, which makes it more emphatic and more exclusive.

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れたのは**<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>です**。<br>*The one who was late yesterday was me.*

Compare <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れました, which simply reports the event with the subject highlighted. The cleft takes for granted that somebody was late - it is the sentence you use when that is already on the table - and it carries a clear implication that nobody else was. That exclusivity is why clefts are so useful for owning up, for correcting, and for singling one item out of a list.

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · `の` vs `こと` as the nominaliser

> **Rule of thumb** - `の` when the focus is a **person, thing, time or place**. `こと` when what you are naming is itself an **action or a proposition**, and always when the nominalised clause sits before `だ`／`です` as the predicate.

For a cleft that identifies an entity, only `の` works: <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたのは<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>です, never ❌<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたことは<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>です. A person is not an event, so `こと` has nothing to name.

Turn the sentence round, though, and the requirement flips:

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むことです**。<br>*My hobby is reading.*

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · `の` vs `こと` as the nominaliser (cont.)

Here the nominalised clause is the predicate, and ❌<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むのです is not available - that string already means something else entirely, the explanatory `んです`. The same rule governs <ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>なのは<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>けることです.

Two more places where the choice is fixed, both worth memorising. Before a verb of direct perception - <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く, <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえる, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える - only `の` is possible, because you witness an event rather than a fact: <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>いているのを<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました. And a set of idioms takes `こと` and nothing else: ことができる, たことがある.

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · ～のは○○だ vs ～んです

> **Rule of thumb** - both contain `の`, and they do opposite things. `んです` explains the **whole** situation. `のは…だ` isolates **one element** of it.

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>出張<rt>しゅっちょう</rt></ruby>する**のは<ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>です**。<br>*The place I'm travelling to next week is Osaka.*

Set that beside <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出張<rt>しゅっちょう</rt></ruby>するんです, "the thing is, I'm off to Osaka next week" - an explanation offered as background, with no single element picked out. If you can point at one word and say "that is the answer", you want the cleft; if the whole sentence is the answer, you want `んです`.

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>一番<rt>いちばん</rt></ruby><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きなのは<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>一番<rt>いちばん</rt></ruby><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きなのは<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*The subject sits inside the nominalised clause, and a clause like that takes `が`. Two `は` in one sentence leaves the first one homeless.*

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**だ**のは<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>だけです。 → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**な**のは<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>だけです。<br>*な-adjectives take `な` before a nominalising `の`, the same shape they use before `ので` and `のに`.*

- ❌ <ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>なのは<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>する**のです**。 → ✅ <ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>なのは<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>する**ことです**。<br>*A nominalised clause standing before `です` must use `こと`. With `の` the sentence turns into an explanatory `んです` and stops meaning what you wanted.*

---

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>った**ことは**<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>った**のは**<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*When the focus is a creature, a thing or a time, the nominaliser is `の`. `こと` would be naming an event, which cannot equal a cat.*

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Practice 1

**1.** この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>った____<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>った**のは**<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>です

{reveal}
The cook is a person, so the light noun `の` is the one you want, followed by `は` to mark everything before it as shared background. The past form <ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>った goes straight in front of `の`.

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んだのは<ruby>頭<rt>あたま</rt></ruby>が<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>かったです。

## ～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としました。 (b) <ruby>皿<rt>さら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたのは<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
(a) reports the event as a whole, with the subject marked as the new piece of information. It works as an answer to "what happened?" and does not assume the listener knows anything yet.

{reveal}
(b) assumes the broken plate is already common knowledge and supplies the missing name. It answers "who did it?", it excludes everyone else, and depending on the situation it can sound like an accusation or like clearing someone's name. Use it when the event is settled and only the identity is in question.
