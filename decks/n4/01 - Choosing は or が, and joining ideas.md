# は vs が

**Meaning** · which of the two to reach for once the "new information" test has done its work

At N5 the rule was: the news comes **after** `は` and **before** `が`. That is still the first thing to check, and it settles most sentences. This page is about what the rule does not cover - `は` used to set one thing against another, `が` used to say "this one and no other", and what happens to both particles once a clause is buried inside a larger sentence.

---

## は vs が · Formation

Neither particle changes shape, and neither changes the noun. What you are choosing between is a **job**, so the table sorts by job rather than by word class.

---

## は vs が · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Known noun → topic | Noun + は | <ruby>祖母<rt>そぼ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>祖母<rt>そぼ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>です |
| Two nouns set against each other → contrast | A は …、B は … | <ruby>平日<rt>へいじつ</rt></ruby>・<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>平日<rt>へいじつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>き、<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む |
| Non-subject noun, contrasted | Particle + は | <ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで → <ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>までは<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きます |
| Quantity word, contrasted | Quantity + は + negative | <ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えられません |
| New noun in a reported event | Noun + が | <ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>がありました |
| The one and only → exhaustive が | Noun + が + predicate | <ruby>山口<rt>やまぐち</rt></ruby>さん → <ruby>山口<rt>やまぐち</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>担当<rt>たんとう</rt></ruby>です |
| Subject inside a noun-modifying clause | Noun + が（or の） | <ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby> → <ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>建<rt>た</rt></ruby>てた<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby> |
| Subject inside a と・ば・たら・とき clause | Noun + が | <ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby> → <ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったら… |

---

## は vs が · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast reaching into a けど・から clause | Noun + は | <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いけど… |

> **Note** - a sentence can carry only one **topic**, but it can carry several `は`. The first is normally the topic; every later one is contrastive. <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>刺身<rt>さしみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べません。 - "Me, I don't eat sashimi (whatever else I might eat)."

---

## は vs が · Examples

<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めますが、<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けません。<br>*I can read kanji, but I can't write it.*

<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ちます。<br>*I'll take the bags.*

さっき<ruby>変<rt>へん</rt></ruby>な<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>**が**したけど、<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだね。<br>*There was an odd noise a minute ago, but it's quiet now.*

<ruby>司会<rt>しかい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>森<rt>もり</rt></ruby>さん**が**、<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さん**が**<ruby>担当<rt>たんとう</rt></ruby>します。<br>*Mori will run the meeting and Hayashi will look after reception.*

---

## は vs が · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>昔<rt>むかし</rt></ruby><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>っていたカメラ**は**、<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>でもちゃんと<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*The camera my father used years ago still works perfectly.*

<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がったので、<ruby>今年<rt>ことし</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>をやめました。<br>*Vegetable prices went up, so we've given up on a trip this year.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わったこと、<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に**は**<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えましたが、<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんに**は**まだ<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えていません。<br>*I've told the manager the meeting time changed, but I haven't told Yamada yet.*

<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**しますので、<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>のコピー**は**<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんにお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いできますか。<br>*I'll do the explaining, so could I leave copying the handouts to you, Tanaka?*

---

## は vs が · Nuance & register

Both particles are register-neutral: nothing about choosing one makes you sound formal or casual. What they do carry is **attitude**, and that is where politeness gets involved.

`は` drags a comparison in behind it whether you want one or not, so an unnecessary `は` can sound pointed. Volunteering with <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**は**やります in a room where a job is being handed out reads as "*I*, at any rate, will do it - never mind the rest of you"; the cooperative version is <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**やります, which simply fills the empty slot of "who". The same edge is useful on purpose: 「<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>はちょっと…」 turns an invitation down gently precisely because `は` implies "another day, gladly."

---

## は vs が · Nuance & register (cont.)

`が` in its selecting sense is the particle of taking responsibility, and business Japanese leans on it: <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>いたします, こちら**が**<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>でございます. Learners who default to `は` in those slots sound oddly detached from what they are saying.

In casual speech both particles are routinely dropped - 「あれ、<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>、どこ?」 - and the contrast `は` would have carried is done by stress instead. Note that a **contrastive** `は` survives that dropping far more often than a topic `は`: 「<ruby>肉<rt>にく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べない」 keeps its particle because the particle is the whole point of the sentence.

---

## は vs が · Nuance & register (cont.)

Writing gives `は` one job that speech uses less: definitions. <code><ruby>敬語<rt>けいご</rt></ruby>とは、<ruby>相手<rt>あいて</rt></ruby>を<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>める<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>である</code> - "*keigo* is language that elevates the other party." That `とは` frame opens dictionary entries and academic articles, and it is always `は`, never `が`.

---

## は vs が · Topic は vs contrastive は

> **Rule of thumb** - if you can tack "…whatever about the others" onto the end and the sentence still means what you meant, the `は` is contrastive. A topic `は` sits at the head of the sentence; a contrastive `は` can sit anywhere, including on top of another particle, and it takes the stress in speech.

The topic use simply announces what you are talking about:

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>で<ruby>建築<rt>けんちく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しています。<br>*My younger brother is studying architecture at university.*

The contrastive use is a different move. It lifts its noun out of a field of alternatives and pointedly says nothing about the rest:

---

## は vs が · Topic は vs contrastive は (cont.)

<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>**は**コーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません。<br>*I don't drink coffee at night. (daytime is another matter)*

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで**は**<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>りましたが、<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いませんでした。<br>*I ran as far as the station, at least - but I didn't make it.*

The most useful case is a **quantity word plus a negative**. `は` there produces partial negation, and leaving it out flips the meaning:

<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えられませんでした。<br>*I couldn't memorise all of it. (I did learn some)*

---

## は vs が · Topic は vs contrastive は (cont.)

Without the `は`, <ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えられませんでした is normally taken as "I couldn't learn any of it." The same trick works on <code><ruby>全員<rt>ぜんいん</rt></ruby>は</code>, `いつもは` and <code><ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>は</code>.

---

## は vs が · Neutral-description が vs exhaustive が

> **Rule of thumb** - `が` either **reports** a whole event as news, or **selects** one member out of a set. The selecting kind answers a "which one?" and cannot be swapped for `は`.

Reporting: nothing is being singled out. You are presenting a situation you have just noticed, or announcing one the listener has not heard.

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいパン<ruby>屋<rt>や</rt></ruby>**が**できました。<br>*A new bakery has opened in front of the station.*

Selecting: everyone already knows the predicate applies to somebody, and `が` names who. This is why identifying yourself takes `が`:

---

## は vs が · Neutral-description が vs exhaustive が (cont.)

<ruby>本日<rt>ほんじつ</rt></ruby><ruby>案内<rt>あんない</rt></ruby>いたします、<ruby>木村<rt>きむら</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>担当<rt>たんとう</rt></ruby>でございます。<br>*I'm Kimura, and I'll be looking after you today.*

The selecting `が` is what makes **exhaustive listing** possible - handing out roles one by one, with the understanding that between them the list is complete:

<ruby>土曜日<rt>どようび</rt></ruby>は<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**、<ruby>日曜日<rt>にちようび</rt></ruby>は<ruby>妻<rt>つま</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ます。<br>*I'll be at the shop on Saturday and my wife on Sunday.*

One practical consequence: with an adjective and an already-known subject, `が` forces the selecting reading and sounds strange as a plain description. この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>明<rt>あか</rt></ruby>るいです means "*this* is the bright one"; for "this room is bright" you want <code>この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>明<rt>あか</rt></ruby>るいです</code>.

---

## は vs が · Where は cannot go, and what it means when it can

> **Rule of thumb** - `は` scopes over a whole sentence, so it cannot sit inside a clause that is only a *part* of one. Inside noun-modifying clauses, and inside と・ば・たら・なら・とき clauses, the subject takes `が` - or `の` in a noun-modifying clause.

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>ってくれた<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を、<ruby>近所<rt>きんじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>けました。<br>*I shared the vegetables my mother sent me out among the neighbours.*

<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まったら、タクシーで<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります。<br>*If the trains stop, I'll go home by taxi.*

❌<code><ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まったら</code> is not a stylistic slip - it breaks, because the topic would be trying to scope over a sentence whose main clause is about you, not about trains.

---

## は vs が · Where は cannot go, and what it means when it can (cont.)

The exception is worth knowing, because it looks like a counterexample. Clauses joined with けど・が・から・ので are not *embedded*; they are full clauses stuck together. `は` can appear inside those - and when it does, it is always the contrastive `は`, never a second topic:

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>**は**かかりますが、<ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ず<ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>します。<br>*It will take time, granted - but I will definitely fix it.*

That is exactly the "…whatever about the rest" reading: time, yes; difficulty, cost and outcome, not commented on. Test any `は` you meet inside a subordinate clause against that, and it will almost always turn out to be contrast.

---

## は vs が · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>暗<rt>くら</rt></ruby>くなったら、<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>をつけてください。 → ✅ <ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>暗<rt>くら</rt></ruby>くなったら、<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>をつけてください。<br>*A たら clause is embedded inside the sentence, and a topic cannot live there. Its subject takes `が`.*

- ❌ 「どちらが<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんですか。」「<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>です。」 → ✅ 「<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>です。」<br>*<code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>です</code> is a fine self-introduction, but as an answer to "which of you is Tanaka?" it dodges the question. You were asked to select, and selecting is `が`.*

- ❌ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かです。（meaning "this room is quiet"） → ✅ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かです。<br>*With a known subject and an adjective, `が` is heard as "this is the quiet *one*". For a plain description, use `は`.*

---

## は vs が · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りました。 → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りました。<br>*The second `は` forces a contrast - "snow, at least, fell" - which is not what you meant. A reported event takes `が`.*

## は vs が · Practice 1

**1.** 「どうしたんですか。」「エアコン____<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かないんです。」

{reveal}
エアコン**が**<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かないんです。

{reveal}
You are reporting a problem the listener does not know about yet, so the air conditioner is brand-new information - the reporting `が`. `は` would imply the two of you were already discussing that particular machine.

---

## は vs が · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>（は・が）ないので、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>（は・が）ここまでにしましょう。

{reveal}
<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>**が**ないので、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>**は**ここまでにしましょう。

{reveal}
The first slot sits inside a `ので` clause and simply states a fact about time, so it takes `が`. The second sets today against other days - we stop here today, and next time we will go further - which is contrastive `は`.

---

## は vs が · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I've read the first half, but I haven't read the second half yet."

{reveal}
<ruby>前半<rt>ぜんはん</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みましたが、<ruby>後半<rt>こうはん</rt></ruby>**は**まだ<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいません。

{reveal}
Both halves are grammatical objects and would normally take `を`, but the sentence exists in order to set them against each other, so `を` is replaced by contrastive `は` on both sides.

---

## は vs が · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったら、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったら、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
The first `は` is inside a たら clause and has to become `が`. The second one is in the main clause and is correct as it stands - the match is the topic of the sentence.

---

## は vs が · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>山口<rt>やまぐち</rt></ruby>さん**は**<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>です。 (b) <ruby>山口<rt>やまぐち</rt></ruby>さん**が**<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
(a) takes <ruby>山口<rt>やまぐち</rt></ruby>さん as already under discussion and tells you something about him: his job is manager. It is the answer to "what does he do?"

{reveal}
(b) starts from the other end. Everyone knows there is a manager; the sentence identifies which person it is - "<ruby>山口<rt>やまぐち</rt></ruby>さん is the one" - picking him out of the group. It answers "which of them is the manager?", and it is what you say while nodding across the room.

# ～し

**Meaning** · and what's more - piling up reasons or qualities, with the hint that there are others

`～し` joins clauses the way a person builds a case: one point, another point, and an unstated "so, you see." It differs from a plain "and" in two ways. The items are offered as **grounds** for something, and the list is **open** - you are naming a couple of reasons out of several, not all of them.

---

## ～し · Formation

`し` attaches to the **plain form** of whatever ends the clause, and it can equally follow a polite form when the sentence around it is polite.

---

## ～し · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + し | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るし |
| Verb (past) | plain past + し | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>った → <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったし |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + し | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないし |
| Verb (polite) | ます-form + し | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ります → <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りますし |
| い-adjective | plain form + し | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いし |
| い-adjective (polite) | です + し | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いです → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いですし |
| な-adjective | だ + し | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだし |
| な-adjective (polite) | です + し | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かです → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かですし |
| Noun | だ + し | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだし |

---

## ～し · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (polite) | です + し | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みです → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みですし |

> **Rule of thumb** - `し` needs a *finished* clause in front of it. That is why nouns and な-adjectives must keep their `だ`（❌<code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かし</code>）while い-adjectives must not add one（❌<code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いだし</code>）.

A second habit worth building in from the start: the clause after `し` usually marks one of its nouns with `も`. <code><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>だし、<ruby>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>強<rt>つよ</rt></ruby>い</code>, <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いし、<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>もいい</code>. That `も` is what makes the sentence feel like an accumulation of points rather than a sequence of events.

---

## ～し · Examples

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**し**、おいしい。<br>*This place is cheap, and the food's good.*

<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れた**し**、そろそろ<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ろう。<br>*I'm tired - let's head home soon.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだ**し**、<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>でも<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない?<br>*Tomorrow's a day off - how about going to see a film or something?*

この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>い**し**、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>い**し**、ここに<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>めました。<br>*The room's big and it's near the station too, so I've settled on this one.*

---

## ～し · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>守<rt>まも</rt></ruby>らない**し**、<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>もくれない**し**、もう<ruby>信<rt>しん</rt></ruby>じられない。<br>*He doesn't keep his promises, he never gets in touch - I just can't trust him any more.*

まだ<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>もあります**し**、もう<ruby>一軒<rt>いっけん</rt></ruby><ruby>寄<rt>よ</rt></ruby>っていきませんか。<br>*We've still got time, so shall we stop off at one more place?*

<ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>くない**し**、<ruby>人間関係<rt>にんげんかんけい</rt></ruby>もいい**し**、<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>の<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>辞<rt>や</rt></ruby>める<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>がありません。<br>*The pay isn't bad and I get on with everyone, so I've no reason to leave my current company.*

「どうして<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>すんですか。」「<ruby>家賃<rt>やちん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いです**し**、<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>からも<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>いので。」<br>*"Why are you moving?" "The rent's high, and it's a long way from the office."*

---

## ～し · Nuance & register

`し` itself is neutral, but it belongs to **spoken** Japanese. It is at home in conversation, in a chatty email, in a blog post; a formal report would reach for <code>～<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>に</code> or simply separate sentences instead.

Politeness is decided by the clause, not by `し`. In polite speech you attach it to the polite form - <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いですし</code>, `あります**し**` - and mixing the two inside one sentence sounds careless. Plain-form `し` inside an otherwise polite sentence is heard sometimes in relaxed workplace speech, but it is not something to imitate early.

The habit worth copying is **trailing し**: a sentence that ends on `し` and simply stops.

---

## ～し · Nuance & register (cont.)

- 「<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>はやめておきます。<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いし。」 - "I'll pass<br>*today. Early start tomorrow and all."*
- 「べつにいいよ。もう<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わったし。」 - "It's fine. It's over now anyway."

The unfinished shape is doing real work: it hands the conclusion to the listener instead of stating it, which softens a refusal and lets you avoid saying the awkward part out loud. The flip side is that a string of trailing `し` about someone else can sound like grumbling or excuse-making - 「だって<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しかったし」 is exactly the tone of a child explaining why the homework is not done.

One more thing `し` does that `から` cannot: it can list reasons that point in different directions, letting the speaker weigh them. <code><ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>はいいし、でも<ruby>残業<rt>ざんぎょう</rt></ruby>も<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>いし…</code> is a person thinking out loud.

---

## ～し · ～し vs ～から

> **Rule of thumb** - `から` gives **the** reason and pushes the conclusion at the listener. `し` gives **a** reason among others and leaves room for more.

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>がある**から**、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みます。<br>*I've got a fever, so I'm taking today off.*

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>もある**し**、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みます。<br>*I've got a bit of a temperature and so on, so I'm taking today off.*

The `から` version presents one clean cause. The `し` version implies a small pile of reasons - tiredness, the weather, the fever - of which you have named one. That vagueness is why `し` sounds softer and less accusatory, and why refusals reach for it.

---

## ～し · ～し vs ～から (cont.)

The two combine, and the combination is extremely common: <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いし、<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>いし、いい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>ですから、ぜひ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってみてください</code> - the `し` clauses stack the evidence and the final `から` delivers the verdict.

---

## ～し · ～し vs the て-form

> **Rule of thumb** - the て-form strings things together in **order**; `し` adds them up as **evidence**. If the second clause simply happened next, you want て.

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>き**て**、<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>って、<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*I got up, washed my face and left the house.*

Putting `し` in there - ❌<code><ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きたし、<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>いました</code> - makes it sound as though getting up were a reason for washing.

With adjectives the two overlap more, and both are correct:

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>く**て**、<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすいです。<br>*This dictionary is light and easy to use.*

---

## ～し · ～し vs the て-form (cont.)

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>い**し**、<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いやすいです。<br>*This dictionary is light, and easy to use into the bargain.*

The て version is a flat description. The `し` version sounds like a recommendation - you can hear "so you should buy it" behind it.

---

## ～し · ～し vs ～たり～たり

> **Rule of thumb** - both give an incomplete list, but of different things. `し` lists whole **clauses** as reasons; `たり` lists **activities** as samples of what went on.

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>洗濯<rt>せんたく</rt></ruby>をし**たり**、<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づけ**たり**しました。<br>*Yesterday I did the washing, tidied my room and so on.*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>洗濯<rt>せんたく</rt></ruby>もした**し**、<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>も<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づけた。<br>*Yesterday I did the washing, and I tidied my room as well.*

The `たり` sentence answers "what did you get up to?" - it paints a picture of the day. The `し` sentence answers "have you done anything useful?" - it is a defence, item by item. Note also that `たり` must end in する, while `し` needs nothing after it.

---

## ～し · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**し**、<ruby>空気<rt>くうき</rt></ruby>もきれいです。 → ✅ この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**だし**、<ruby>空気<rt>くうき</rt></ruby>もきれいです。<br>*A な-adjective needs its `だ` before `し`. The same goes for nouns: <code><ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だし</code>.*

- ❌ この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**だし**、<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>いです。 → ✅ この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**し**、<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*An い-adjective is already a complete predicate, so adding `だ` is one word too many.*

- ❌ シャワーを<ruby>浴<rt>あ</rt></ruby>びた**し**、<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ました。 → ✅ シャワーを<ruby>浴<rt>あ</rt></ruby>び**て**、<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*`し` does not narrate a sequence. Two events that simply happened one after the other take the て-form.*

- ❌ <ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>いし、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**です**し、いい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>い**です**し、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**です**し、いい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Keep one politeness level across the whole sentence. Plain `し` followed by polite `し` sounds like a slip.*

---

## ～し · Practice 1

**1.** この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>____、<ruby>家賃<rt>やちん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いです。

{reveal}
この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>**だし**、<ruby>家賃<rt>やちん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いです。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby></code> is a な-adjective, so it needs `だ` to finish the clause before `し` can attach. The polite alternative is <code><ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>ですし</code>.

---

## ～し · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>（し・て）、ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べました。

{reveal}
<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**て**、ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べました。

{reveal}
Nothing here is a reason for anything - the two events simply happened in order, and that is the て-form's job. `し` would suggest you were listing evidence for some conclusion you have not stated.

## ～し · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "This café is close to the station and it isn't expensive, so let's meet here."

{reveal}
この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>から<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>い**し**、<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くない**し**、ここで<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わせましょう。

{reveal}
Two `し` clauses stack the reasons, and the suggestion follows with no connective at all - once `し` has done the work, `だから` would be redundant.

## ～し · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>親切<rt>しんせつ</rt></ruby>し、<ruby>頭<rt>あたま</rt></ruby>もいいです。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>親切<rt>しんせつ</rt></ruby>**だし**、<ruby>頭<rt>あたま</rt></ruby>もいいです。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>親切<rt>しんせつ</rt></ruby></code> is a な-adjective. Without `だ` the clause never finishes, and `し` has nothing to attach to. Polite version: <code><ruby>親切<rt>しんせつ</rt></ruby>ですし</code>.

## ～し · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>だ**から**、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけません。 (b) <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>だ**し**、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけません。

{reveal}
(a) names the rain as the reason, full stop. It is a clear, slightly firm statement of cause and effect, and it invites the listener to accept it.

{reveal}
(b) offers the rain as *one* of the things making you stay in - you are tired, there is nothing on, and besides, it's raining. It sounds more relaxed and less like an argument, which is exactly why it is the friendlier way to decline an invitation.

# ～たり～たり

**Meaning** · doing things like A and B - naming a couple of examples out of a larger set

`～たり～たり` lists **samples**. Whatever you name, the listener understands that other things happened too, and that the order they are given in means nothing. Its second job is alternation: two states that keep swapping places, one day this, the next day that.

---

## ～たり～たり · Formation

Take the plain past（た-form）of each item, add `り`, and close the whole list with `する`. The `する` is not decoration - it is the verb of the sentence, and it carries the tense and the politeness for everything in front of it.

---

## ～たり～たり · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | た-form + り | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んだり |
| Verb (する) | した + り | <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>したり |
| Verb (negative) | なかった + り | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かなかったり |
| い-adjective | かった + り | <ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>かったり |
| な-adjective | だった + り | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだったり |
| Noun | だった + り | <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>だったり |
| Closing the list (verbs) | …たり…たり + する | <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いたり<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ったりする |
| Closing the list (past, polite) | …たり…たり + しました | <ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>ったり<ruby>踊<rt>おど</rt></ruby>ったりしました |
| Closing the list (adjectives / nouns) | …だったり…だったり + だ／です | <ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>かったり<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>かったりです |

---

## ～たり～たり · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - if you can make the た-form, you can make the たり-form: just add `り`. The trap is the closing `する`, which learners drop. It is what conjugates: `したり…したりしています`, `…したりしたい`, `…したりしないでください`.

---

## ～たり～たり · Examples

<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>は<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>い**たり**、ギターを<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>い**たり**します。<br>*In the evenings I listen to music, play the guitar, that sort of thing.*

<ruby>教室<rt>きょうしつ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ん**だり**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**たり**しないでください。<br>*Please don't eat or drink in the classroom.*

<ruby>最近<rt>さいきん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>かっ**たり**<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>かっ**たり**で、<ruby>体調<rt>たいちょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>崩<rt>くず</rt></ruby>しやすい。<br>*Lately it's hot one day and cold the next, so it's easy to get run down.*

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>はさっきから<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>の<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>を<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>っ**たり**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>**たり**している。<br>*Dad's been pacing up and down the room for a while now.*

---

## ～たり～たり · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>場所<rt>ばしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>本社<rt>ほんしゃ</rt></ruby>だっ**たり**<ruby>支店<rt>してん</rt></ruby>だっ**たり**で、よく<ruby>間違<rt>まちが</rt></ruby>えます。<br>*The meeting is sometimes at head office, sometimes at the branch, so I often get it wrong.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**たり**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べなかっ**たり**で、<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>です。<br>*My son eats his vegetables some days and not others, which worries me.*

<ruby>日曜日<rt>にちようび</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>くの<ruby>公園<rt>こうえん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>い**たり**、カフェで<ruby>新聞<rt>しんぶん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ん**だり**して、ゆっくり<ruby>過<rt>す</rt></ruby>ごしています。<br>*On Sundays I walk in the park nearby, read the paper in a café, and generally take it slow.*

<ruby>一人暮<rt>ひとりぐ</rt></ruby>らしを<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めてから、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>っ**たり**<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づけ**たり**するのが<ruby>当<rt>あ</rt></ruby>たり<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>になりました。<br>*Since I started living alone, cooking for myself and tidying up have just become normal.*

---

## ～たり～たり · Nuance & register

`～たり` is register-neutral. Everything about the level of the sentence is decided by the closing verb: <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たりします</code> is polite, <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たりする</code> is plain, <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たりいたします</code> is humble. The `たり` parts themselves never change.

Two conversational habits to recognise. First, a **single** `たり` is normal - it still means "and things like that", and it often softens an accusation: <code><ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>いたりしないで</code>, <code><ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>をしたりしないでね</code>. Second, `たりして` at the end of a sentence is a hedge, close to English "…or something": 「<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったりして」 - "knowing our luck it'll snow tomorrow." That use is casual and slightly playful; keep it out of a report.

---

## ～たり～たり · Nuance & register (cont.)

In casual speech you will also hear `とか` doing the same job - <code><ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>ったりとかして</code> - which is chattier still, and the closing `する` is sometimes swallowed altogether: 「<ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>したり<ruby>洗濯<rt>せんたく</rt></ruby>したり、いろいろ」. Understand it, but write the `する`.

The alternation use has one shape worth memorising because it appears everywhere: **A + たり + A's negative + たり** - <code><ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったり<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らなかったり</code>, <code><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たり<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかったり</code> - meaning "sometimes yes, sometimes no". It carries a faint note of unreliability, so it is not a compliment.

---

## ～たり～たり · ～たり～たり vs the て-form

> **Rule of thumb** - the て-form reports what happened, **in order and in full**. `たり` gives an unordered sample and implies there was more.

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>、コーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ん**で**、メールをチェックして、<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*This morning I had a coffee, checked my email and left the house.*

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>は、コーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ん**だり**メールをチェックし**たり**しています。<br>*In the mornings I have a coffee, check my email, and so on.*

---

## ～たり～たり · ～たり～たり vs the て-form (cont.)

The first is a report of one particular morning, in sequence. The second describes what mornings are generally like, and rearranging the two items would not change it at all. Because of that, `たり` is wrong when the order matters: ❌<code><ruby>歯<rt>は</rt></ruby>をみがいたり<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たりしました</code> for "I brushed my teeth and went to bed."

---

## ～たり～たり · ～たり～たり vs ～や・～とか

> **Rule of thumb** - same "partial list" idea, different word class. `や` and `とか` list **nouns**; `たり` lists **actions and states**.

<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>にペン**や**ノートが<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>いてあります。<br>*There are pens, notebooks and so on lying on the desk.*

<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>い**たり**<ruby>調<rt>しら</rt></ruby>べ**たり**しています。<br>*I write and look things up at the desk.*

---

## ～たり～たり · ～たり～たり vs ～や・～とか (cont.)

`とか` straddles the line - it lists nouns like `や`, but in casual speech it also lists dictionary-form verbs: <code><ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>うとか<ruby>干<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>すとか</code>. That verb use is markedly more casual than `たり`, and it does not need a closing `する`.

---

## ～たり～たり · ～たり～たり vs ～ながら

> **Rule of thumb** - `ながら` is two actions happening **at the same moment**; `たり` is two actions taking turns, or sampled from a stretch of time.

<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>き**ながら**<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*I run while listening to music. (both at once)*

<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>い**たり**<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っ**たり**しています。<br>*I listen to music, go running, that kind of thing. (at different times)*

Learners sometimes reach for `たり` when describing multitasking, and it quietly says the opposite of what they mean. If the two things overlap in time, you want `ながら`.

---

## ～たり～たり · Common mistakes

- ❌ テレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る**たり**、<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**たり**します。 → ✅ テレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**たり**、<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ん**だり**します。<br>*`たり` is built on the た-form, never the dictionary form. Make the past tense first, then swap `た` for `たり` - including the voiced `だり`.*

- ❌ <ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たり、<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちに<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ったり。 → ✅ …<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ったり**します**。<br>*The closing `する` is what carries the tense. Leaving it off is fine in casual conversation and wrong in writing or polite speech.*

- ❌ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**たり**、うるさかったりします。 → ✅ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**だったり**、うるさかったりします。<br>*な-adjectives and nouns need `だった` before `り`; only verbs and い-adjectives have a た-form of their own.*

---

## ～たり～たり · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きたり、<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>ったりして、<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。 → ✅ <ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>き**て**、<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>っ**て**、<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*A fixed morning routine happens in a fixed order, and `たり` explicitly throws the order away. Use the て-form.*

---

## ～たり～たり · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>し____、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>っ____します。

{reveal}
<ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>し**たり**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>っ**たり**します。

{reveal}
Both verbs go into the た-form first（`した`, <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った</code>）and then take `り`. The final `します` is the verb of the whole sentence and supplies the polite present tense.

---

## ～たり～たり · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>っ（たり・て）から、ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べましょう。

{reveal}
<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>っ**て**から、ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べましょう。

{reveal}
The whole point of the sentence is the order - hands first, then food - and `てから` is what expresses that. `たり` would say the two happen in no particular order, which is not the advice anyone gives about washing your hands.

{reveal}
This is the alternation use: two states swapping back and forth rather than a list of activities. `しています` presents it as an ongoing situation; `します` would sound like a general truth about the climate.

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちと<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>するたり、ゲームをしたりしました。

# ～ながら

**Meaning** · while doing something else - two actions by one person at the same time

`～ながら` puts two activities on top of each other. The clause with `ながら` is the **background**; the main clause at the end is what the sentence is really about. Both actions must belong to the **same person**, and both must be things that last long enough to overlap.

---

## ～ながら · Formation

`ながら` attaches to the ます-stem and never changes shape itself. All the tense and politeness live in the final verb.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + ながら | <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きます → <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きながら |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + ながら | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べながら |
| Verb (する) | し + ながら | <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>しながら |
| Verb (ている, concessive) | てい + ながら | <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っている → <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っていながら |
| い-adjective (concessive) | plain form + ながら（も） | <ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>いながらも |
| な-adjective (concessive) | stem + ながら（も） | <ruby>残念<rt>ざんねん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>残念<rt>ざんねん</rt></ruby>ながら |
| Noun (concessive) | Noun + ながら | <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども → <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもながら |

---

## ～ながら · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `ながら` takes the same stem as `ます`, so <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べながら</code>, never ❌<code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるながら</code>. The verb before it also has to be one that *takes time*: you can walk while talking, but not ❌<code><ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>きながら</code> ("while arriving").

> **Note** - the last four rows are a **different** `ながら`, meaning "although". It attaches to `ている` forms, い-adjectives, な-adjective stems and bare nouns - shapes the "at the same time" `ながら` never takes - so the form itself usually tells you which one you are looking at. See the third Contrast section.

---

## ～ながら · Examples

コーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み**ながら**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>しましょう。<br>*Let's talk over a coffee.*

<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>き**ながら**スマホを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ないでください。<br>*Please don't look at your phone while you're walking.*

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>は<ruby>鼻歌<rt>はなうた</rt></ruby>を<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>い**ながら**<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>をしている。<br>*My mother is doing the washing-up, humming to herself.*

アルバイトをし**ながら**<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>通<rt>かよ</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*I'm working part-time while going to university.*

---

## ～ながら · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>残念<rt>ざんねん</rt></ruby>**ながら**、<ruby>今回<rt>こんかい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になりました。<br>*Regrettably, this time the plan has been called off.*

<ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>いと<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>ってい**ながら**、<ruby>嘘<rt>うそ</rt></ruby>をついてしまいました。<br>*I lied, even though I knew it was wrong.*

<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**ながら**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いていたのに、<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じところに<ruby>戻<rt>もど</rt></ruby>ってきてしまった。<br>*I was walking along looking at the map, and still ended up back in the same place.*

<ruby>先輩<rt>せんぱい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>をメモし**ながら**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いていたので、あとでゆっくり<ruby>見直<rt>みなお</rt></ruby>すことができました。<br>*I took notes while listening to my senior colleague's explanation, so I could go back over it properly later.*

---

## ～ながら · Nuance & register

`ながら` is neutral and belongs equally to speech and writing. What learners get wrong is not the register but the **weight**: whichever verb ends the sentence is the one being reported. テレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**ながら**ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる is a sentence about eating; ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ながら**テレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る is a sentence about watching television. Both are correct, and they answer different questions.

The pattern also stretches over long spans of time, not just a single moment. <ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>きながら<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>する describes years of your life, and this use is completely standard in a self-introduction or a job interview.

---

## ～ながら · Nuance & register (cont.)

Modern Japanese has turned `ながら` into a noun prefix for doing something *while* distracted: <code>ながら<ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby></code> (using your phone at the wheel), <code>ながら<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ</code>, `ながらスマホ`. These carry disapproval - they are the words on the warning poster.

The "although" `ながら` is a different animal in register: it is **written and formal**, and in conversation it survives mainly in fixed expressions - <code><ruby>残念<rt>ざんねん</rt></ruby>ながら</code> (regrettably), `しかしながら` (however), `いつもながら` (as always), <code><ruby>失礼<rt>しつれい</rt></ruby>ながら</code> (if I may be so bold). Adding `も` - <code><ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>いながらも</code> - makes the concession explicit and sounds more literary still.

---

## ～ながら · ～ながら vs ～ている間に

> **Rule of thumb** - `ながら` needs **one** person doing both things. If the two clauses have different subjects, you need <code><ruby>間<rt>あいだ</rt></ruby>に</code>.

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>昼寝<rt>ひるね</rt></ruby>をしている**<ruby>間<rt>あいだ</rt></ruby>に**、<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づけます。<br>*I'll get my work done while the children are having their nap.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもの<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>き**ながら**、<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>をしていました。<br>*I was working while listening to my child talk.*

---

## ～ながら · ～ながら vs ～ている間に (cont.)

In the first, the sleeper and the worker are different people, so `ながら` is impossible. In the second, one person is doing both. There is a second difference worth noticing: <code><ruby>間<rt>あいだ</rt></ruby>に</code> means "at some point during", so the main action fits inside the other one, while `ながら` means the two run side by side for the whole time.

---

## ～ながら · ～ながら vs the て-form

> **Rule of thumb** - て puts one action *after* another, or gives the **means**; `ながら` puts them on top of each other.

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>い**て**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*I'll walk to the station. (walking is *how* I go)*

<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>き**ながら**<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えましょう。<br>*Let's think it over as we walk.*

---

## ～ながら · ～ながら vs the て-form (cont.)

The て version answers "how did you get there?"; the `ながら` version answers "what were you doing at the same time?" Swapping them changes the sentence rather than just its style: <code><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いて<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>える</code> would suggest you walked and then thought.

---

## ～ながら · Concessive ～ながら（も）vs ～のに

> **Rule of thumb** - both mean "although". `のに` is spoken and carries surprise or complaint; `ながらも` is written, restrained, and often faintly admiring.

<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>**ながらも**、<ruby>品<rt>しな</rt></ruby>ぞろえは<ruby>立派<rt>りっぱ</rt></ruby>だ。<br>*Small though the shop is, the range it stocks is impressive.*

<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>な**のに**、<ruby>品<rt>しな</rt></ruby>ぞろえがすごいね。<br>*The shop's tiny, but look at everything they've got!*

---

## ～ながら · Concessive ～ながら（も）vs ～のに (cont.)

Two practical limits on the concessive `ながら`. It attaches only to a short list of things - a ます-stem, a `ている` form, an い-adjective, a な-adjective stem or a plain noun - so there is no ❌<code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったながら</code>. And with verbs it is almost always a `ている` form, meaning "knowing full well that…": <code><ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っていながら</code>, <code><ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かっていながら</code>, <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ていながら</code>.

---

## ～ながら · Common mistakes

- ❌ ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる**ながら**テレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ます。 → ✅ ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ながら**テレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ます。<br>*`ながら` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.*

- ❌ <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>がピアノを<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>き**ながら**、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しました。 → ✅ <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>がピアノを<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>いている**<ruby>間<rt>あいだ</rt></ruby>に**、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しました。<br>*Two different people, so `ながら` cannot join them. The sentence as written claims your sister was studying.*

- ❌ <ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>をつけ**ながら**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけました。 → ✅ <ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>をつけた**まま**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけました。<br>*`つける` is instantaneous - the light goes on and that's that. For "leaving something in a state", the word is `まま`.*

---

## ～ながら · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>き**ながらでした**、<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>しました。 → ✅ <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>き**ながら**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>しました。<br>*`ながら` never takes tense or politeness. Both live on the final verb.*

---

# ～ても

**Meaning** · even if; even though - the second half happens anyway

`～ても` sets up a condition and then knocks it down. Whatever is in the `ても` clause would normally be expected to change the outcome, and the sentence says it does not. Depending on context it covers "even if it happens" (a hypothesis) and "even when it happens" (a repeated fact).

---

## ～ても · Formation

The mechanics are simple: build the て-form, add `も`. The only thing to watch is that adjectives and nouns have their own て-forms.

---

## ～ても · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + も | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>っても |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + も | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かなくても |
| い-adjective | くて + も | <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くても |
| い-adjective (negative) | くなくて + も | <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くない → <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くなくても |
| な-adjective | で + も | <ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>でも |
| Noun | で + も | <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>でも |
| Noun / な-adjective (negative) | じゃなくて + も | <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby> → <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>じゃなくても |
| Question word | question word + ても | どんなに → どんなに<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っても |

---

## ～ても · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - no tense ever appears before `ても`. Past, future and habit are all decided by the final verb: ❌<code><ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったても</code>, ✅<code><ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っても</code> for "even if it rains" *and* for "even though it rained".

> **Note** - you already know this pattern from N5 without knowing it. `～てもいい` ("you may") is literally "even if you do, it is fine", and `～なくてもいい` ("you don't have to") is "even if you don't, it is fine". Same construction, with an adjective supplied for the second half.

---

## ～ても · Examples

<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>く**ても**、<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*Even when it's cold, I run every morning.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っ**ても**、<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>ります。<br>*Even if it rains tomorrow, we're climbing the mountain.*

<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>が**なくても**<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いますよ。<br>*You'll make it even without rushing.*

<ruby>日曜日<rt>にちようび</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いている<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>探<rt>さが</rt></ruby>しています。<br>*I'm looking for a shop that's open even on Sundays.*

---

## ～ても · Examples (cont.)

いくら<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>し**ても**、<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かってくれません。<br>*No matter how much I explain, he just won't understand.*

<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ても**おいしく<ruby>感<rt>かん</rt></ruby>じないので、<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むことにしました。<br>*Nothing tastes good to me at the moment, so I've decided to take a short break.*

どんなに<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>し**ても**、<ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>には<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>も<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>てませんでした。<br>*However much I practised, I never once beat my older brother.*

<ruby>留学<rt>りゅうがく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>費用<rt>ひよう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>く**ても**、<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>は<ruby>海外<rt>かいがい</rt></ruby>で<ruby>生活<rt>せいかつ</rt></ruby>してみたいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*Even if studying abroad is expensive, I'd like to live overseas at least once.*

---

## ～ても · Nuance & register

`ても` is register-neutral; the politeness of the sentence is set entirely by its final verb. It is as much at home in a contract as in a conversation.

The casual spoken contraction is `～たって`, and it is everywhere: <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったって</code>, <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たって</code>, <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くたって</code>, <code><ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもだって</code>. Verbs build it on the plain past - take <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った</code> and add `って`; い-adjectives use `くたって` (<code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くたって</code>), and nouns and な-adjectives use `だって`. It is friendly, slightly emphatic, and out of place in writing or with a superior. 「そんなこと、<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったって<ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>いよ」 - "It's too late to say that now."

`ても` pairs up with a small family of adverbs that make the concession explicit, and using one makes you sound noticeably more fluent:

---

## ～ても · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **たとえ～ても** - "even supposing", for hypotheticals: たとえ<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>しても。
- **どんなに～ても／いくら～ても** - "no matter how much".
- **<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>を／だれが／どこへ～ても** - "no matter what/who/where".

One caution about the noun form. <code><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>でも</code> can be this pattern ("even in the rain"), but `でも` is *also* an independent word meaning "…or something" （お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>でも<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みませんか）and the sentence-initial "but" （でも、<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いですよ）. Three unrelated things that look identical; the context always sorts them out, but expect the double-take.

---

## ～ても · ～ても vs ～たら・～ば

> **Rule of thumb** - `たら` and `ば` say the result **depends** on the condition; `ても` says the result comes anyway. Same slot in the sentence, opposite claims.

<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くなっ**たら**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。<br>*If it gets cheaper, I'll buy it.*

<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くなっ**ても**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いません。<br>*Even if it gets cheaper, I won't buy it.*

This is the pair to burn into memory, because English "if" hides the difference. A useful test: if you could insert "in that case" before the main clause, you want `たら`; if you could insert "all the same", you want `ても`.

---

## ～ても · ～ても vs ～のに

> **Rule of thumb** - `のに` reports something that **actually happened** and adds frustration or surprise. `ても` is neutral, and it covers hypotheses and repeated situations that `のに` cannot.

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っ**ても**、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>行<rt>おこな</rt></ruby>われます。<br>*The match will go ahead even if it rains.*

Swapping in `のに` there is impossible - nothing has rained yet, and `のに` cannot talk about a hypothesis. Where both are available, the difference is attitude:

<ruby>三回<rt>さんかい</rt></ruby>メールを<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>っ**ても**、<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ません。<br>*I've sent three emails and I still get no reply.*

---

## ～ても · ～ても vs ～のに (cont.)

<ruby>三回<rt>さんかい</rt></ruby>メールを<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>った**のに**、<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ません。<br>*I sent three emails and there's *still* no reply.*

The `ても` version states the situation. The `のに` version complains about it - you can hear the speaker's irritation, and it would be undiplomatic in front of the person responsible.

---

## ～ても · ～ても vs ～けど・～が

> **Rule of thumb** - `けど` joins **two facts**; `ても` names a condition that fails to have its usual effect. Only `ても` can be hypothetical.

この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>いです**が**、<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>あたりがいいです。<br>*This room is small, but it gets good light.*

<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>く**ても**、<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>あたりがよければ<ruby>満足<rt>まんぞく</rt></ruby>です。<br>*As long as it gets good light, I don't mind a small room.*

The `が` sentence describes one particular room you have both seen. The `ても` sentence describes a policy that would hold for any room at all. Note also that `ても` attaches to a bare stem while `けど・が` follow a complete clause with its own tense.

---

## ～ても · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**ても**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。 → ✅ <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>く**ても**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。<br>*An い-adjective has to become `くて` first. The `い` never survives in front of `も`.*

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>**ても**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけます。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけます。<br>*Nouns and な-adjectives take `で`, not `て` - the same shape they use for the て-form everywhere else.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**ても**いいですか。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>っ**ても**いいですか。<br>*`ても` is built on the て-form, never the dictionary form.*

---

## ～ても · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>**も**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っても、<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>りません。 → ✅ <ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っても、<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>りません。<br>*The "no matter what" meaning comes from `ても` itself, so the question word keeps its ordinary particle. <code><ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>も</code> plus a negative is a different pattern entirely.*

---

## ～ても · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>く____、いらないものは<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いません。

{reveal}
<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>く**ても**、いらないものは<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いません。

{reveal}
Cheapness would normally be a reason to buy, and the sentence overrules it - that is exactly the `ても` move. <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い</code> becomes <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くて</code> before `も`.

---

## ～ても · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れ（ても・たら）、<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きましょう。

{reveal}
<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れ**たら**、<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きましょう。

{reveal}
The trip to the sea *depends* on the weather, so this is a plain condition. <code><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れても</code> would mean you are going regardless - and then mentioning the sunshine would make no sense.

## ～ても · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "Even if you're busy, please eat breakfast."

{reveal}
<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しく**ても**、<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ごはんは<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてください。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい</code> is an い-adjective, so it goes to <code><ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しくて</code> before `も`. The `は` on <code><ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ごはん</code> is optional but natural - it sets breakfast against everything else you might skip.

## ～ても · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しいても、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>でやってみます。

{reveal}
✅ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>し**くても**、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>でやってみます。

{reveal}
い-adjectives form `ても` from their くて-form. A quick check: if you can say <code><ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しくて</code>, you can say <code><ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しくても</code>.

## ～ても · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ん**でも**、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>下<rt>さ</rt></ruby>がりません。 (b) <ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んだ**のに**、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>下<rt>さ</rt></ruby>がりませんでした。

{reveal}
(a) describes an ongoing situation flatly: taking the medicine does not bring the fever down, and it may have happened several times. It is what you would say to a doctor when reporting symptoms.

{reveal}
(b) points at one occasion that is over and adds an emotional charge - you took the medicine, so the fever *should* have gone down, and you are frustrated or puzzled that it did not. `のに` always carries that "but it didn't work out" feeling; `ても` does not.

# ～のに - although

**Meaning** · although; even though - and the speaker is not happy about it

`～のに` joins two facts that ought not to fit together, and it always carries the speaker's reaction to the mismatch: surprise, disappointment, or a flat complaint. That feeling is not optional. It is the difference between `のに` and the neutral "but" you already know.

---

## ～のに - although · Formation

`のに` follows a **plain form** - a complete clause, with its own tense. Only nouns and な-adjectives need a linking `な`.

---

## ～のに - although · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のに | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つのに |
| Verb (past) | plain past + のに | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>った → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ったのに |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + のに | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たない → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たないのに |
| い-adjective | plain form + のに | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いのに |
| い-adjective (past) | かった + のに | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>かった → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>かったのに |
| な-adjective | な + のに | <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby> → <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>なのに |
| な-adjective (past) | だった + のに | <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>だった → <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>だったのに |
| Noun | な + のに | <ruby>日曜日<rt>にちようび</rt></ruby> → <ruby>日曜日<rt>にちようび</rt></ruby>なのに |
| Noun (past) | だった + のに | <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもだった → <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもだったのに |

---

## ～のに - although · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - present-tense nouns and な-adjectives take `な`, never `だ`: ✅<code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かなのに</code>, ❌<code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだのに</code>. In the **past** the `だった` comes back, because `だった` is already a finished form: <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだったのに</code>.

---

## ～のに - although · Examples

<ruby>日曜日<rt>にちようび</rt></ruby>な**のに**、<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*It's Sunday, and I still have to go in to work.*

たくさん<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>た**のに**、まだ<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>い。<br>*I slept plenty and I'm still sleepy.*

<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>した**のに**、<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかった。<br>*He promised, and then he didn't come.*

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>から<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>い**のに**、いつも<ruby>混<rt>こ</rt></ruby>んでいる。<br>*That shop is miles from the station, yet it's always packed.*

---

## ～のに - although · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>な**のに**、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>真冬<rt>まふゆ</rt></ruby>のように<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い。<br>*It's spring, and yet today feels like the middle of winter.*

<ruby>一生懸命<rt>いっしょうけんめい</rt></ruby><ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>した**のに**、テストの<ruby>点<rt>てん</rt></ruby>はあまりよくなかった。<br>*I studied as hard as I could, and my test score still wasn't much good.*

せっかく<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>った**のに**、<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてくれなくて<ruby>悲<rt>かな</rt></ruby>しかったです。<br>*I went to the trouble of making it and nobody would eat any, which was upsetting.*

「<ruby>体調<rt>たいちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>かったんですか。もう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってくれればよかった**のに**。」<br>*"You weren't feeling well? You really should have said something sooner."*

---

## ～のに - although · Nuance & register

`のに` is neither polite nor casual in itself - it attaches to plain forms, and the politeness of the sentence is decided at the end. What it *is* is **emotionally loaded**, and that is where it gets you into trouble.

Because `のに` always says "this should not have turned out this way", pointing it at someone's behaviour is a reproach. お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いした**のに**、まだ <ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わっていないんですか is an accusation, however politely the sentence ends. In front of a customer or a superior, use `が` or `けど` and let the facts speak.

The **sentence-final** `のに` is very common in speech and drops the second clause entirely, leaving the complaint hanging:

---

## ～のに - although · Nuance & register (cont.)

- 「<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てくれればよかったのに。」 - "You should have come."
- 「もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>えばいいのに。」 - "I wish he'd say so sooner."
- 「<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってきたのに…」 - "But I brought an umbrella…"

This is the tone of mild reproach or wistfulness - warm between friends, sharp from a stranger. It combines especially often with `せっかく` ("after going to all that trouble"), which supplies the disappointment in advance.

`のに` attaches to plain forms. You will occasionally meet `～ますのに` in older or very formal writing, but it sounds dated, and it is not a form to produce.

---

## ～のに - although · ～のに vs ～が・～けど

> **Rule of thumb** - `が` and `けど` just say "but". `のに` says "but" *and* adds "which I find surprising, unfair or annoying".

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>古<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>いです**が**、まだ<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えます。<br>*This dictionary is old, but it's still usable.*

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>古<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>い**のに**、まだ<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えます。<br>*This dictionary is ancient and it still works - remarkable.*

There is also a hard grammatical limit. `のに` reports what **happened**, so its main clause cannot be a request, an invitation, a command or a statement of intention. All of these need `が` or `けど`:

---

## ～のに - although · ～のに vs ～が・～けど (cont.)

- ❌<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れたのに、もう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>頑張<rt>がんば</rt></ruby>ろう。 → ✅<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れたけど、もう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>頑張<rt>がんば</rt></ruby>ろう。
- ❌<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っているのに、<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してください。 → ✅<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っているので、<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してください。

If the end of your sentence is something you are asking for or deciding, `のに` is the wrong connective.

---

## ～のに - although · ～のに vs ～ても

> **Rule of thumb** - `のに` needs a **real** event; `ても` can be a hypothesis. And `のに` complains, while `ても` merely states.

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っ**ても**、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけます。<br>*I'm going out tomorrow even if it rains.*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っていた**のに**、<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>たずに<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけました。<br>*It was raining and I went out without an umbrella anyway.*

---

## ～のに - although · ～のに vs ～ても (cont.)

There is no ❌<code><ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るのに、<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけます</code> for "even if it rains" - nothing has happened yet, so there is nothing for `のに` to object to. The same reason explains why `ても` and not `のに` follows question words: いくら<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っても、 どんなに<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くても.

---

## ～のに - although · ～のに "although" vs ～のに "in order to"

> **Rule of thumb** - check the form in front. "Although" follows any complete plain clause - past, negative, な. "In order to" follows a **dictionary-form verb only**, and the sentence ends in a judgement about difficulty, cost or usefulness.

この<ruby>道具<rt>どうぐ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>な**のに**、あまり<ruby>売<rt>う</rt></ruby>れていない。<br>*This tool is handy, and yet it doesn't sell well. (although)*

この<ruby>道具<rt>どうぐ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>る**のに**<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>です。<br>*This tool is handy for cutting vegetables. (in order to)*

Two more tests, if the form does not settle it. The purpose `のに` can be replaced by `ために` with almost no change; the concessive one cannot. And the purpose `のに` never takes a comma before it, while the concessive one usually does.

---

## ～のに - although · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**だのに**、<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みました。 → ✅ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**なのに**、<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みました。<br>*な-adjectives link with `な` in the present tense. The same applies to nouns: ❌<code><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>のに</code> → ✅<code><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>なのに</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>混<rt>こ</rt></ruby>んでいた**のに**、<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れてしまいました。 → ✅ <ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>混<rt>こ</rt></ruby>んでいた**ので**、<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れてしまいました。<br>*Heavy traffic and arriving late fit together perfectly, so there is no mismatch for `のに` to react to. It needs two halves that clash.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る**のに**、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>をします。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っ**ても**、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>をします。<br>*`のに` cannot describe a hypothetical. For "even if", use `ても`.*

---

## ～のに - although · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新入社員<rt>しんにゅうしゃいん</rt></ruby>な**のに**、よく<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>きますね。（said to his manager as praise） → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新入社員<rt>しんにゅうしゃいん</rt></ruby>です**が**、よく<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>きますね。<br>*Grammatical, but `のに` frames the situation as odd or unsatisfactory, which is not how you compliment somebody's new recruit.*

---

# ～のに - in order to

**Meaning** · for doing something - what a thing is good for, and what it costs you

This `のに` is not the "although" one at all. It is the nominaliser `の` plus the particle `に`: the verb is turned into a noun ("the doing of it") and `に` points at it as a target. The sentence then judges something against that target - this tool is handy **for** it, that job took two hours **to** do.

---

## ～のに - in order to · Formation

Only the **dictionary form** goes in front of it, and the sentence has to end in an evaluation: a cost, a length of time, or a judgement of usefulness.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | dictionary form + のに | <ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>るのに |
| Verb (る-verb) | dictionary form + のに | <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ける → <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けるのに |
| Verb (する) | する + のに | <ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>するのに |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る + のに | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るのに |
| With contrast marking | のに + は | <ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>るのには |

The predicates that finish these sentences come from a short list, and it is worth learning them as a set:

> **Rule of thumb** - no past tense, no negative, no `な` ever appears before this `のに`. ---

## ～のに - in order to · Examples

---

## ～のに - in order to · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>片<rt>かた</rt></ruby>づける**のに**、<ruby>一日中<rt>いちにちじゅう</rt></ruby>かかってしまった。<br>*Tidying the room ended up taking me the whole day.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>を<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>える**のに**、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はカードを<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>って<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>るようにしています。<br>*For learning new words, I make cards and try to look at them every day.*

---

## ～のに - in order to · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and unusually even-handed about where it appears: it is the ordinary way to say what a thing is for, so it turns up in conversation, in instruction manuals, in product descriptions and in recipes alike.

Two shapes to have ready. The `のに` phrase can be **fronted**, with a comma, when the purpose is what the sentence is about: <ruby>大人数<rt>おおにんずう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>まるのに、 あの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>はちょうどいいですよ。 And because `の` is a genuine nominaliser, ordinary particles can stack on it - `のには` for contrast, `のにも` for "for that as well": <ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>るのには<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が かかりますが、<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるのは<ruby>一瞬<rt>いっしゅん</rt></ruby>です。

Watch what the sentence is actually judging, because that decides between `のに` and `のが`. If the thing being described is a **tool, an amount of time or a cost**, you want `のに`. If it is the **action itself** that is difficult, tiring or fun, you want `のが`:

---

## ～のに - in order to · Nuance & register (cont.)

- この<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>作業<rt>さぎょう</rt></ruby>する**のに**ちょうどいい。 - the desk is being judged.
- <ruby>作業<rt>さぎょう</rt></ruby>する**のが**<ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>だった。 - the work is being judged.

Learners produce ❌<code><ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えるのに<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しい</code> constantly. Say <code><ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えるのが<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しい</code>, or keep `のに` and change the ending to <code><ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がかかる</code>.

---

## ～のに - in order to · ～のに vs ～ために

> **Rule of thumb** - `ために` is followed by what you **do**; `のに` is followed by an **assessment**. If the second half is an action you deliberately take, it is `ために`.

<ruby>健康<rt>けんこう</rt></ruby>のために、<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>ジュースを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んでいます。<br>*I drink vegetable juice every morning for my health.*

<ruby>健康<rt>けんこう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>保<rt>たも</rt></ruby>つ**のに**、<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>ジュースはとてもいいそうです。<br>*Apparently vegetable juice is very good for keeping healthy.*

---

## ～のに - in order to · ～のに vs ～ために (cont.)

The first sentence reports a decision you made and carried out. The second rates the juice against a purpose. That is why ❌<code><ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>するのに<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます</code> fails: going to Japan is a deliberate act, not a verdict - it needs `ために`.

There is also a subject rule that catches people out. `ために` normally requires the same person to have the purpose and do the action; `のに` has no such restriction, because nobody has to be doing anything at all - <code>この<ruby>道具<rt>どうぐ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けるのに<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>われます</code> is fine.

---

## ～のに - in order to · ～のに vs ～ように

> **Rule of thumb** - `ように` follows a verb you **cannot simply decide to do**: a potential form, a negative, or a change of state. `のに` follows a plain action.

<ruby>後<rt>うし</rt></ruby>ろの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える**ように**、<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きく<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I wrote the letters big so that the people at the back could see too.*

<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>くから<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**のに**いいですね。<br>*Big letters are good for reading from a distance.*

---

## ～のに - in order to · ～のに vs ～ように (cont.)

`ように` states a desired outcome and is followed by the action taken to bring it about; `のに` states a purpose and is followed by a judgement. A quick test: <code><ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れないように</code> ("so as not to forget") could never become ❌<code><ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れないのに</code> in that sense, because `のに` takes no negatives.

---

## ～のに - in order to · ～のに "in order to" vs ～のに "although"

> **Rule of thumb** - look at the verb form. Dictionary form and an evaluation at the end means "in order to". Anything else - past, negative, `な`, an い-adjective - means "although".

この<ruby>袋<rt>ふくろ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>ぶ**のに**<ruby>丈夫<rt>じょうぶ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*This bag is sturdy enough for carrying books. (in order to)*

この<ruby>袋<rt>ふくろ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>丈夫<rt>じょうぶ</rt></ruby>な**のに**、<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*This bag is sturdy and yet light. (although)*

The swap test settles the rest. Try `ために` in the gap: if the sentence survives, it is the purpose one. Try `けど` instead: if *that* survives, it is the concessive one. No sentence takes both, because the two `のに` want different verb forms in front of them in the first place.

---

## ～のに - in order to · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>した**のに**<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ この<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>する**のに**<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Only the dictionary form goes in front of the purpose `のに`. The past tense turns it into the "although" one, and the sentence stops making sense.*

- ❌ <ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>る**のに**、<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しています。 → ✅ <ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>る**ために**、<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しています。<br>*Studying every day is something you have decided to do, not an assessment of how hard university entrance is. Deliberate action takes `ために`.*

---

## ～のに - in order to · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>える**のに**<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しいです。 → ✅ この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>える**のが**<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しいです。<br>*<code><ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しい</code> describes the *act* of memorising, so it needs the nominaliser `の` as its subject. - ❌ <ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**に**<ruby>三時間<rt>さんじかん</rt></ruby>かかります。 → ✅ <ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**のに**<ruby>三時間<rt>さんじかん</rt></ruby>かかります。<br>*A verb cannot take `に` directly. # それでも

**Meaning** · but still; and yet - everything just said pointed the other way, and it happened anyway

`それでも` opens a sentence by picking up the whole situation you have just described and refusing to let it decide the outcome. It concedes the point first, then overrules it. That two-step is why it sounds firmer, and often more thoughtful, than a bare "but".

---

## それでも · Usage

`それでも` is a conjunction: it stands at the **head** of a sentence, usually followed by a comma, and it needs a previous sentence to point back at. The `それ` is doing real work - it means "that whole state of affairs" - so it cannot open a conversation.

<code><ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>かった。それでも、<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いて<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>った。</code>

It also lives inside a clause when the two halves are joined: <ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>するかもしれない。それでも、やってみる<ruby>価値<rt>かち</rt></ruby>はある。 The second sentence is where the speaker's real position is; `それでも` marks everything before it as acknowledged and set aside.

---

## それでも · Usage (cont.)

There is one fixed phrase built on it that behaves differently: `それでもいい` / `それでもかまわない` - "even so, that's fine". Here `それ` + `でも` is the ordinary "even if" construction, and the phrase is the standard way to accept a condition you have just been warned about. 「<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がかかりますよ。」「それでもいいです。」

Two neighbours to keep separate, because they differ by one syllable and mean the opposite thing: `それで` and `そこで` both introduce a **consequence** ("and so"), while `それでも` introduces the **failure** of a consequence.

---

## それでも · Examples

この<ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>はとても<ruby>苦<rt>にが</rt></ruby>いです。**それでも**、<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んでいます。<br>*This medicine is really bitter. Even so, I take it every day.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby>も<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>した。**それでも**、あきらめなかった。<br>*He failed over and over. Still, he never gave up.*

<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っていた。**それでも**、<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもたちは<ruby>公園<rt>こうえん</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っていった。<br>*It was snowing outside. The children ran off to the park regardless.*

## それでも · Nuance & register

`それでも` is register-neutral and equally at home in an essay and in conversation, though everyday speech often just says `でも`. Choosing the longer word is not merely emphasis: `でも` brushes the previous statement aside, while `それでも` picks it up, weighs it, and then decides anyway. That makes it the polite way to disagree with someone who has a point.

「おっしゃることはよく<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かります。それでも、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>反対<rt>はんたい</rt></ruby>です。」

Because of that acknowledging feel, `それでも` frequently follows a `し` clause or a list of drawbacks - the speaker sets out the case against, and then states what they are going to do. It also sits naturally in front of a resolution or a value judgement: `それでもやってみます`, <code>それでも<ruby>信<rt>しん</rt></ruby>じたい</code>.

Three near neighbours, worth keeping straight:

---

## それでも · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **でも** - the everyday "but". Lighter, more casual, and does not necessarily<br>*concede anything.*
- **それなのに** - "and yet", with a complaint attached. It says the outcome was<br>**unfair*, where `それでも` merely says it was unexpected.*
- **それにもかかわらず** - the same idea in formal written Japanese. Stiff in<br>*conversation; normal in a report.*

And one that is not a neighbour at all: **それに** means "what's more", adding a second point in the *same* direction. Learners reach for `それでも` when they want `それに` surprisingly often.

---

## それでも · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いです。**それでも**、おいしいです。 → ✅ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いです。**それに**、おいしいです。<br>*Cheap and tasty are both good points, so nothing is being overruled. `それでも` needs the second sentence to go against the first.*

- ❌ <ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まりました。**それでも**、<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れました。 → ✅ <ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まりました。**それで**、<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れました。<br>*Being late is exactly what you would expect after the trains stop. That is a consequence, so it takes `それで`.*

---

## それでも · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ **それでも**、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>があります。（as the opening line of an email） → ✅ <ruby>実<rt>じつ</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>があります。<br>*`それ` has to refer to something already said. With nothing in front of it, the sentence leaves the reader looking for a missing paragraph.*

- ❌ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>かった**それでも**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。 → ✅ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>かったです。**それでも**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。／<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>かった**けど**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*`それでも` is not a clause connective. It starts a new sentence; joining two clauses inside one sentence is `けど` or `が`.*

## それでも · Practice 3

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っていた。**それでも**、<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていてくれた。 (b) <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っていた。**それなのに**、<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていなかった。

{reveal}
(a) acknowledges the rain and then reports something admirable that happened in spite of it. `それでも` is neutral about blame; here it carries a note of gratitude.

{reveal}
(b) uses `それなのに`, which adds the speaker's dissatisfaction - she *should* have had an umbrella, and the fact that she did not is being criticised or worried over. Swap the two connectives and each sentence loses its point.
