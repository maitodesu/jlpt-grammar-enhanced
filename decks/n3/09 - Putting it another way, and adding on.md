# という - called; named

**Meaning** · attaches a name to the thing it names - "a Y called X"

<code><ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さんという<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby></code> is "a person called Yamamoto"; <code>「そら」という<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby></code> is "a shop called Sora". The name goes first, the ordinary noun goes last, and `という` - literally "which one says" - is the joint between them. The same joint fastens a whole clause to a noun: <code><ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になったという<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby></code>, "word that it has been called off."

---

## という - called; named · Formation

`という` sits between two nouns, or between a plain-form clause and a noun. Nothing in front of it changes shape, and a clause ending in a noun or a な-adjective keeps its `だ`.

---

## という - called; named · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Name + common noun | Name + という + Noun | <ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さん → <ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さんという<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby> |
| Title, quoted | 「…」+ という + Noun | 「そら」 → 「そら」という<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby> |
| Asking a name | <ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>という + Noun | <ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>という<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>ですか |
| Clause + noun | plain form + という + Noun | <ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まる → <ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まるという<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> |
| Clause ending in a noun | Noun + **だ** + という + Noun | <ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>だという<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らせ |
| Clause ending in な-adj | ～**だ** + という + Noun | <ruby>危険<rt>きけん</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>危険<rt>きけん</rt></ruby>だという<ruby>情報<rt>じょうほう</rt></ruby> |
| Casual speech | っていう／って | <ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さんって<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby> |
| Formal equivalent | ～と<ruby>呼<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ばれる | <ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>と<ruby>呼<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ばれる |

---

## という - called; named · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the **name comes first**. ❌<code><ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>という<ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さん</code> reverses the two and says something nonsensical. Whatever `という` points forward to is the general word; whatever sits behind it is the label.

> **Note** - `という` is for names the listener may not know. Once a name is common ground it drops out: you say <code><ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きました</code>, not <code><ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>という<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きました</code>, unless you are genuinely introducing the place to someone who has never heard of it.

---

## という - called; named · Examples

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん**という**<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>から<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>がありました。<br>*There was a call from someone called Tanaka.*

「そら」**という**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っていますか。<br>*Do you know a shop called Sora?*

それは<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>**という**<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>ですか。<br>*What is that flower called?*

この<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>くに「みどり」**という**<ruby>喫茶店<rt>きっさてん</rt></ruby>があります。<br>*There's a coffee shop called Midori near here.*

---

## という - called; named · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>から<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まる**という**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I've heard talk that the building work starts next week.*

<ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>辞<rt>や</rt></ruby>める**という**<ruby>噂<rt>うわさ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>社内<rt>しゃない</rt></ruby>に<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>がっています。<br>*A rumour that the president is stepping down is going round the company.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>から、<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>運動会<rt>うんどうかい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>行<rt>おこな</rt></ruby>う**という**お<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らせが<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*A notice came from my son's school saying that sports day will be held next month.*

「<ruby>星<rt>ほし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>」**という**<ruby>小説<rt>しょうせつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>になると<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いて、<ruby>久<rt>ひさ</rt></ruby>しぶりに<ruby>本屋<rt>ほんや</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I heard that a novel called "The Night the Stars Fell" is being made into a film, so I went to a bookshop for the first time in ages.*

---

## という - called; named · Nuance & register

`という` is register-neutral and enormously common. It is not decoration: leaving it out where it is needed produces a noun phrase that will not parse, and putting it in where it is not needed makes you sound as though you doubt the listener has heard of Kyoto.

**In speech it shrinks.** `という` becomes `っていう`, and then simply `って`: <code><ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんって<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>辞<rt>や</rt></ruby>めるって<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby></code>. You will also see it written `ってゆう` in casual messages, which reflects how the reduced form is actually pronounced. All of these are conversational only - in a report or an email to a client, write `という` in full. Note too that the bare `って` is doing several jobs at once in speech (quotation, topic-marking, hearsay), so context rather than form tells you which one you are hearing.

---

## という - called; named · Nuance & register (cont.)

With a **person's name**, the phrase carries a small social signal: it presents the person as unknown to the speaker as well as to the listener. <code><ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんという<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>がいらっしゃいました</code> is what a receptionist says about a stranger. Said about a colleague you see daily, it would sound as though you were disowning them.

For **your own** name the pattern is not used. Introducing yourself, you say <code><ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>と<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>します</code> - the `という` frame describes a name from the outside, and you are on the inside of yours.

---

## という - called; named · Nuance & register (cont.)

One shape to keep separate. When the **same noun appears on both sides** of `という` - <code><ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>という<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>という<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby></code> - it is not naming anything. That is a different pattern, treated at N1, meaning "every single one of them". The test is mechanical: two different nouns means "called"; the same noun twice means "every".

---

## という - called; named · X という Y vs X の Y

> **Rule of thumb** - `という` says the two nouns are the **same thing**, one naming the other. `の` says they are **different things** in some relationship.

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん**という**<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てもらいました。<br>*I was seen by a doctor called Tanaka.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん**の**<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てもらいました。<br>*I was seen by Tanaka's doctor.*

---

## という - called; named · X という Y vs X の Y (cont.)

In the first, Tanaka *is* the doctor. In the second, Tanaka is somebody else and the doctor belongs to him. The same split explains <code>「<ruby>坊<rt>ぼっ</rt></ruby>ちゃん」という<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby></code> (a book whose title is Botchan) against <code><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby></code> (a book belonging to, or written by, the teacher).

Japanese does have an appositive `の` - <code><ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちの<ruby>木村<rt>きむら</rt></ruby>さん</code>, "my friend Kimura" - but notice the order there: the general noun comes **first** and the name second. With `という` the name always leads. If you find yourself wanting `の` between a name and the word it names, in that order, you want `という`.

---

## Clause + という + noun vs a plain noun-modifying clause

> **Rule of thumb** - if the noun is a **container for words or information** - <ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>, <ruby>噂<rt>うわさ</rt></ruby>, <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らせ, <ruby>情報<rt>じょうほう</rt></ruby>, <ruby>意見<rt>いけん</rt></ruby>, <ruby>点<rt>てん</rt></ruby> - the clause needs `という`. If the clause simply **describes** the noun, attach it directly.

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>入院<rt>にゅういん</rt></ruby>した**という**<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らせが<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>いた。<br>*Word came that Hayashi had gone into hospital.*

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>入院<rt>にゅういん</rt></ruby>した<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*The hospital Hayashi went into is in front of the station.*

---

## Clause + という + noun vs a plain noun-modifying clause (cont.)

The first noun holds the *content* of the clause - the news is that he was admitted - and `という` is the join. The second noun is a place the clause simply describes, so the clause attaches with nothing in between. Slipping `という` in there is not ungrammatical, but it changes the sentence: <code><ruby>入院<rt>にゅういん</rt></ruby>したという<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby></code> is "the hospital he is *said* to have gone into", with the information marked as second-hand. That reportive flavour is a side effect of the quotative, and it is worth knowing about before you add a `という` you did not need.

---

## という - called; named · Naming という vs the repeated-noun という

> **Rule of thumb** - count the nouns. Two **different** nouns is the naming pattern on this page. The **same** noun on both sides is the N1 pattern meaning "every single one".

<ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>のあと、<ruby>港<rt>みなと</rt></ruby>に「あおば」**という**<ruby>船<rt>ふね</rt></ruby>だけが<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>っていた。<br>*After the typhoon, the only boat left in the harbour was one called Aoba.*

<ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>のあと、<ruby>港<rt>みなと</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>船<rt>ふね</rt></ruby>という<ruby>船<rt>ふね</rt></ruby>**が<ruby>傷<rt>きず</rt></ruby>ついていた。<br>*After the typhoon, every last boat in the harbour was damaged.*

Same three characters, two unrelated grammars, and no real ambiguity once you have seen the repetition. The repeated form is vivid and slightly exaggerated, and it needs a set big enough to sweep across; the naming form is neutral and works with one item.

---

## という - called; named · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>という<ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さんから<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>がありました。 → ✅ <ruby>山本<rt>やまもと</rt></ruby>さん**という**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>から<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>がありました。<br>*The name goes in front, the general noun behind. Reversing them is the single commonest error with this pattern, and it comes straight from the English word order "a person called Yamamoto".*

- ❌ <ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**だという**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた。 → ✅ <ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**という**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた。<br>*い-adjectives never take `だ`. The `だ` in the Formation table belongs to clauses that end in a noun or a な-adjective.*

- ❌ それは<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>という<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>ですか。 → ✅ それは**どういう**<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>ですか。<br>*Asking what something *means* is <code>どういう<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby></code>, not <code><ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>という</code>. Keep <code><ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>という</code> for asking a name: <code><ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>という<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>ですか</code>.*

---

## という - called; named · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌（in a written report）「<ruby>中村<rt>なかむら</rt></ruby>」って<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました。 → ✅ <ruby>中村<rt>なかむら</rt></ruby>さん**という**<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られました。<br>*`って` is the spoken reduction of `という` and looks careless on paper. The full form is the one that belongs in writing.*

# ～というのは - means; is

**Meaning** · picks a word up and announces that its definition is coming - "X means…", "X is…"

<code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う</code> is doing its literal work here: `というのは` is "the one that gets *called* X". Everything in front of it is a **label**; everything after it is what the label stands for. This is the frame you use when you know the listener does not know the word - and it is how dictionaries, glossaries and patient colleagues all sound.

---

## ～というのは - means; is · Formation

`というのは` attaches to whatever you are holding up as a *term*: usually a noun, sometimes a quoted phrase, sometimes a whole clause when the thing being defined is an action.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というのは | <ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby>というのは |
| Quoted word or phrase | 「…」+ というのは | 「<ruby>遠慮<rt>えんりょ</rt></ruby>」 → 「<ruby>遠慮<rt>えんりょ</rt></ruby>」というのは |
| Clause - defining an action | plain form + というのは | <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>頼<rt>たよ</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>頼<rt>たよ</rt></ruby>るというのは |
| Usual closing | …ことだ／…という<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>だ | …<ruby>預<rt>あず</rt></ruby>けるお<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>のことです |
| Casual contraction | っていうのは／ってのは | <ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby>ってのは |
| Written, compact | Noun + とは | <ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby>とは |

---

## ～というのは - means; is · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the noun in front takes no `だ`: ❌<code><ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby>だというのは</code>. And the sentence normally lands on `…ことです` or <code>…という<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>です</code>, because what you are describing is a *word*, not an object.

---

## ～というのは - means; is · Examples

「<ruby>大家<rt>おおや</rt></ruby>」**というのは**、アパートの<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>主<rt>ぬし</rt></ruby>のことです。<br>*"Ōya" means the owner of the building you rent from.*

「<ruby>寝坊<rt>ねぼう</rt></ruby>」**というのは**、<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きられないことです。<br>*"Nebō" means failing to get up in the morning.*

<ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby>**というのは**、<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>借<rt>か</rt></ruby>りるときに<ruby>大家<rt>おおや</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>預<rt>あず</rt></ruby>けるお<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>です。<br>*A shikikin is the money you leave with the landlord when you rent a room.*

<ruby>自由<rt>じゆう</rt></ruby>**というのは**、<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>をしてもいいという<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>ではない。<br>*Freedom does not mean that anything goes.*

---

## ～というのは - means; is · Examples (cont.)

「<ruby>花見<rt>はなみ</rt></ruby>」**というのは**、<ruby>桜<rt>さくら</rt></ruby>の<ruby>下<rt>した</rt></ruby>で<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んだり<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたりする<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>の<ruby>行事<rt>ぎょうじ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Hanami is the spring custom of eating and drinking under the cherry trees.*

<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>で「<ruby>検討<rt>けんとう</rt></ruby>します」**というのは**、やんわり<ruby>断<rt>ことわ</rt></ruby>る<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>だと<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えたほうがいいでしょう。<br>*At a Japanese company, "we'll look into it" is best understood as a gentle refusal.*

<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>育<rt>そだ</rt></ruby>てる**というのは**、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>一緒<rt>いっしょ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わっていくことなのだと、<ruby>最近<rt>さいきん</rt></ruby><ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>うようになりました。<br>*I've come to feel lately that raising someone means changing alongside them.*

---

## ～というのは - means; is · Nuance & register

The pattern itself is register-neutral, but the *act* is not: you are explaining a word, so you are momentarily the one who knows. Used on a word the listener obviously knows, it sounds like a lecture. Used on a word you have just introduced yourself, it is simply helpful, and it is exactly what a Japanese speaker will produce if you ask 「<ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby>って<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>ですか」.

In speech the whole thing collapses. `っていうのは` is the everyday form, `ってのは` is looser still and leans male in casual conversation, and plain `って` - the N4 workhorse - does the same job with less ceremony. In writing you go the other way: `とは` is the compact form that opens dictionary headwords and legal definitions.

---

## ～というのは - means; is · Nuance & register (cont.)

One structural habit worth copying: the definition normally ends on `…ことです`, <code>…という<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>です</code> or `…ということです`. Because the subject of the sentence is a *word* rather than a thing, closing on a bare noun can feel like you stopped halfway. <code><ruby>大家<rt>おおや</rt></ruby>というのはアパートの<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>主<rt>ぬし</rt></ruby>のことです</code> is the polished version of a sentence many learners end at <code><ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>主<rt>ぬし</rt></ruby>です</code>.

---

## ～というのは - means; is · というのは vs とは

> **Rule of thumb** - same job, different medium. `というのは` is what you *say*; `とは` is what you *write*. `とは` is shorter, drier, and expects a full definition after it.

「<ruby>時効<rt>じこう</rt></ruby>」**とは**、<ruby>一定<rt>いってい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>過<rt>す</rt></ruby>ぎて<ruby>権利<rt>けんり</rt></ruby>がなくなることをいう。<br>*"Jikō" denotes the lapsing of a right after a fixed period of time.*

「<ruby>時効<rt>じこう</rt></ruby>」**というのは**、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>経<rt>た</rt></ruby>つともう<ruby>訴<rt>うった</rt></ruby>えられなくなる、ということですね。<br>*So "jikō" means that after enough time passes you can't be sued any more, right?*

---

## ～というのは - means; is · というのは vs とは (cont.)

The first belongs in a textbook; the second belongs in a conversation about one. `とは` also turns up in speech with a raised eyebrow - <code>プロとは<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>えない</code>, "you'd never call that professional" - but that is a different `とは` and does not define anything.

---

## ～というのは - means; is · というのは vs ということは

> **Rule of thumb** - `というのは` explains a **word**. `ということは` draws a conclusion from a **fact**. If English wants "which means…", you need `ということは`.

「<ruby>定休日<rt>ていきゅうび</rt></ruby>」**というのは**、<ruby>毎週<rt>まいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まっている<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みの<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>のことです。<br>*"Teikyūbi" means the fixed day each week when a shop is closed.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>定休日<rt>ていきゅうび</rt></ruby>だ。**ということは**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby><ruby>済<rt>す</rt></ruby>ませないといけない。<br>*Tomorrow's their closing day. Which means the shopping has to be done today.*

The giveaway is what sits in front. A bare label takes `というのは`; a finished statement of fact takes `ということは`.

---

## ～というのは - means; is · というのは vs のは

> **Rule of thumb** - `のは` takes an **act** and comments on it. `というのは` takes the **idea or the name** of that act and explains it. If you could put quotation marks round the front half, you want `というのは`.

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きる**のは**<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>です。<br>*I'm no good at getting up early.*

「<ruby>朝活<rt>あさかつ</rt></ruby>」**というのは**、<ruby>出勤<rt>しゅっきん</rt></ruby><ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>や<ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>うことです。<br>*"Asakatsu" means using the time before work for study or exercise.*

The first is about your actual mornings. The second never touches anyone's morning - it is about a term. Swapping them produces sentences that are grammatical and beside the point.

---

## ～というのは - means; is · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby>**だ**というのは、<ruby>預<rt>あず</rt></ruby>けるお<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>のことです。 → ✅ <ruby>敷金<rt>しききん</rt></ruby>というのは、<ruby>預<rt>あず</rt></ruby>けるお<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>のことです。<br>*No `だ` on the noun being defined. The `だ` version is the quoting `という` ("saying that it's a deposit"), which is not what you mean.*

- ❌ 「<ruby>花見<rt>はなみ</rt></ruby>」というのは、とても<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しいです。 → ✅ 「<ruby>花見<rt>はなみ</rt></ruby>」は、とても<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しいです。<br>*`というのは` promises a definition, not an opinion. If you are saying how you feel about the thing itself, plain `は` is what you want.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>というのは<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>です。<br>*You are not a vocabulary item. `というのは` frames its noun as a term to be explained, so using it on yourself sounds like you are defining the word "I".*

## ～というのは - means; is · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>残業<rt>ざんぎょう</rt></ruby>ということは、<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>められた<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>後<rt>あと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>くことです。 → ✅ <ruby>残業<rt>ざんぎょう</rt></ruby>というのは、…<br>*A single word takes `というのは`. `ということは` needs a whole fact in front of it and produces an inference, not a definition.*

# というのは - because

**Meaning** · opens the sentence that gives the reason for what you have just said - "the reason being that…"

You make a statement. You can hear the listener's silent "why?". `というのは` starts a new sentence and answers it. Unlike `から` or `ので`, which live inside the sentence they explain, this one stands at the head of its own - which is why it can carry a long, unhurried reason that would sag if you tried to bolt it on.

---

## というのは - because · Formation

This `というのは` is a **sentence-opening connective**. It follows a full stop, takes a comma after it, and the clause it introduces almost always closes on `からだ`, `ためだ` or `のだ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| After a finished sentence | …。というのは、…からだ。 | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けない。というのは、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるからだ。 |
| Polite style | …。というのは、…からです。 | …<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるからです。 |
| With のだ instead of から | …。というのは、…のです。 | …<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たのです。 |
| Near-identical variant | というのも、…からだ | というのも、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるからだ |
| Casual speech | っていうのは、…んだ | っていうのは、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるんだ |
| Formal writing | なぜなら（ば）、…からである | - |

---

## というのは - because · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - if you delete the reason clause, what is left must still be a complete, sensible sentence. `というのは` is an afterthought by design; it cannot be the grammatical backbone of anything.

---

## というのは - because · Examples

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります。**というのは**、<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るからです。<br>*I'm going home early today - my mother is coming, you see.*

その<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>断<rt>ことわ</rt></ruby>った。**というのは**、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りなかったからだ。<br>*I turned that job down. The reason is that there wasn't enough time.*

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>まりには<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けません。**というのは**、その<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>出張<rt>しゅっちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>っているからです。<br>*I can't make next week's get-together - I've got a business trip that day.*

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>をずっと<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>っています。**というのは**、<ruby>例文<rt>れいぶん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>くて<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりやすいからです。<br>*I've been using this dictionary for years, because it has plenty of example sentences and is easy to follow.*

---

## というのは - because · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>はあまり<ruby>信<rt>しん</rt></ruby>じないほうがいい。**というのは**、<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じことで<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>らせているからだ。<br>*You'd do better not to take what he says at face value; he's landed people in trouble with the same story before.*

<ruby>今年<rt>ことし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>をやめることにした。**というのは**、<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby><ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>のローンがまだ<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>っているからだ。<br>*We've decided against a holiday this summer - the loan on the car we bought last year still isn't paid off.*

<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありませんが、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>欠席<rt>けっせき</rt></ruby>させてください。**というのは**、<ruby>工場<rt>こうじょう</rt></ruby>のほうで<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きてしまったのです。<br>*I'm sorry, but please let me miss tomorrow's meeting - a problem has suddenly come up at the plant.*

---

## というのは - because · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はこの<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>離<rt>はな</rt></ruby>れられない。**というのは**、ここには<ruby>祖父<rt>そふ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>してくれた<ruby>畑<rt>はたけ</rt></ruby>があるからだ。<br>*I can't leave this town. The reason is that my grandfather's fields are here.*

---

## というのは - because · Nuance & register

This is a **considered** connective. Splitting the reason off into its own sentence buys you a beat of pause, so it suits explanations you have thought about: declining an invitation, justifying a decision, walking a reader through an argument. It is at home in essays, reports, formal e-mail and prepared speech, and it appears in careful conversation too - but in fast, casual talk people reach for `だって`, `から` or a bare `のだ` instead.

`というのも` is the same connective with `も` and is, if anything, slightly more common in writing. Treat them as interchangeable at this level; `も` adds a faint sense of "and there is this on top of it", which is why it often follows a reason already given.

---

## というのは - because · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two small register facts. First, the polite version is fully polite - the ending carries the politeness (`…からです`), while `というのは` itself is fixed and never becomes `といいますのは` in ordinary speech. Second, dropping the `からだ` at the end and simply stating the reason flat sounds abrupt in writing, though it is normal in speech: 「<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けない。というのは、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があってね。」

---

## というのは - because · というのは vs なぜなら

> **Rule of thumb** - same slot, different formality. `なぜなら` is written, structured and a little insistent; `というのは` is the version you can say out loud without sounding like a textbook.

この<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby>はすすめられない。**なぜなら**、コストが<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>すぎるからである。<br>*This approach cannot be recommended, because the cost is too high.*

この<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby>はあまりすすめません。**というのは**、コストが<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>すぎるからです。<br>*I wouldn't really recommend this approach - the cost is too high.*

`なぜなら` all but demands `からだ／からである` at the end; leaving it off feels unfinished. `というのは` is looser about it. Note too that `なぜなら` announces a reason before you have heard any of it, which is what makes it feel like an argument being built.

---

## というのは - because · というのは vs だって

> **Rule of thumb** - `だって` is the same move at the bottom of the register ladder, and it carries a defensive tone: you are answering a criticism, not filling in background.

<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べちゃった。**だって**、おいしかったんだもん。<br>*I ate the lot. Well - it was good!*

<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてしまいました。**というのは**、<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>すと<ruby>失礼<rt>しつれい</rt></ruby>になると<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったからです。<br>*I finished everything, because I thought leaving food would be rude.*

`だって` is casual and reads as childish or petulant from an adult in a formal setting. `というのは` is neutral. If you are explaining yourself to a superior, the choice is not stylistic.

---

## The "because" というのは vs the "means" というのは

> **Rule of thumb** - look at what sits **in front**. A finished sentence and a full stop → "because". A bare noun or quoted word → "means". The endings confirm it: `…からだ` versus `…ことだ`.

<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていったほうがいい。**というのは**、<ruby>午後<rt>ごご</rt></ruby>から<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>になるからだ。<br>*You'd better take an umbrella - it's going to rain from the afternoon.*

「<ruby>夕立<rt>ゆうだち</rt></ruby>」**というのは**、<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>午後<rt>ごご</rt></ruby>に<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>のことだ。<br>*"Yūdachi" means the rain that falls suddenly on a summer afternoon.*

Nothing about the phrase itself distinguishes them; position and closing do all the work. In speech, a clear pause before `というのは` also marks the "because" one.

---

## というのは - because · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるからというのは、<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けません。 → ✅ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けません。というのは、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるからです。<br>*The order is fixed: statement first, then `というのは`, then the reason. Putting the reason in front leaves the connective with nothing to attach back to.*

- ❌ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けません。というのは、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があります。 → ✅ …<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるからです。<br>*In writing, the reason clause needs `からだ／からです`, `ためだ` or `のです` to close it. A bare statement leaves the sentence sounding cut off.*

---

## というのは - because · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っているというのは、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っているので、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Here the reason comes first and the result second - that is `ので`/`から` territory. `というのは` can only look backwards.*

- ❌ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れてすみません。というのは、<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まったからです。（to a customer, in person）→ ✅ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れて<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません。<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まってしまいまして…<br>*Not ungrammatical, but announcing "the reason is" while apologising sounds like you are building a defence. Apologies usually run the reason in with `て` and trail off.*

# ～というのは - the thing about X

**Meaning** · lifts a noun up and holds it there while you say what it is really like

This is `というのは` used not to define a word but to **single a topic out**. `は` would just say "as for X"; `というのは` says "X - now, the thing about X is…", and it commits you to a verdict. The noun in front can be a whole class （<ruby>親<rt>おや</rt></ruby>, <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>, <ruby>人生<rt>じんせい</rt></ruby>） or one particular thing you want to pick out of the room, and the sentence that follows is almost always an opinion, a generalisation, or something you have discovered the hard way.

---

## ～というのは - the thing about X · Formation

The shape is identical to the defining `というのは`. What tells them apart is what comes after: a definition, or a judgement.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (a class) | Noun + というのは | <ruby>親<rt>おや</rt></ruby> → <ruby>親<rt>おや</rt></ruby>というのは |
| Noun phrase (one specific thing) | Noun + というのは | この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>のカレー → この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>のカレーというのは |
| Clause - an activity | plain form + というのは | <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つというのは |
| Typical closing | …ものだ／…ものです | …ありがたいものだ |
| Casual contraction | って／っていうのは | <ruby>親<rt>おや</rt></ruby>って |
| Heavier, more emphatic | Noun + というものは | <ruby>親<rt>おや</rt></ruby>というものは |

---

> **Note** - `ものだ` at the end is not compulsory, but it belongs to the same gesture. It marks the sentence as a truth about how things generally are, which is exactly what this `というのは` sets up.

## ～というのは - the thing about X · Nuance & register

This use has a **reflective** feel. It sets the noun down, steps back, and pronounces - which is why it turns up in essays, in advice, in the mouths of older speakers, and in anyone's voice at the end of a long day. Said about something trivial it sounds pompous: <code>このボールペンというのは<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い</code> is a strange sentence, because nobody needs a verdict on a pen.

In conversation the contraction `って` does the same work and does it constantly: 「<ruby>親<rt>おや</rt></ruby>ってありがたいよね」, 「<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>って<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>いよね」. That form is casual but not rude, and it is the single most common way this idea gets expressed out loud. `っていうのは` sits in between - spoken, but with a beat of deliberation.

---

## ～というのは - the thing about X · Nuance & register (cont.)

Because the pattern promises a generalisation, it pairs naturally with `ものだ`, `ものではない`, `らしい` and `のだろう`, and it likes adverbs of the "as a rule" family - `なかなか`, `どうしても`, `つい`, `やはり`. If your sentence has none of that flavour and you only want to say what one thing is like right now, plain `は` is the better particle.

---

## ～というのは - the thing about X · というのは vs は

> **Rule of thumb** - `は` reports on **this** thing. `というのは` raises the noun to "X, as a kind of thing" and warns the listener that a verdict is coming.

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>厳<rt>きび</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>でした。<br>*My father was a strict man.*

<ruby>父親<rt>ちちおや</rt></ruby>**というのは**、<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもの<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>では<ruby>弱<rt>よわ</rt></ruby>いところを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せないものだ。<br>*Fathers, as a rule, don't let their children see them weak.*

The first is a fact about one man. The second is a claim about fathers in general, and the speaker is prepared to defend it. Note that you cannot swap them freely: the second sentence with plain `は` would sound like a report on some specific father, and the first with `というのは` would sound like you were about to define the word "father".

---

## ～というのは - the thing about X · というのは vs って

> **Rule of thumb** - identical meaning, different register. `って` is the spoken default; `というのは` is what you write, and what you say when you want the weight.

<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>し**って**、<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったよりお<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>がかかるね。<br>*Moving house costs more than you'd think, doesn't it.*

<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>し**というのは**、<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っている<ruby>以上<rt>いじょう</rt></ruby>にお<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>も<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>もかかるものです。<br>*Moving house takes far more money and time than people imagine.*

The second would sound stiff between friends and exactly right in an article or a piece of advice to a junior colleague. The first would look sloppy in writing. Nothing else separates them.

---

## ～というのは - the thing about X · というのは vs というものは

> **Rule of thumb** - `というものは` is the same gesture with more weight behind it. It sounds like the opening of a proverb, and it is nearly always followed by a sweeping claim.

<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>**というのは**、<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>にでもあることです。<br>*Failure is something that happens to anybody.*

<ruby>時<rt>とき</rt></ruby>**というものは**、<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってはくれない。<br>*Time, in the end, waits for no one.*

Use `というものは` sparingly. In ordinary conversation it can sound theatrical - the linguistic equivalent of standing up before you speak - whereas `というのは` passes unnoticed.

---

## ～というのは - the thing about X · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>というのは<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>いです。 → ✅ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*A plain description of one thing takes `は`. `というのは` promises either a definition or a verdict, and "this room is big" is neither.*

# ～というと - speaking of; when you say…

**Meaning** · takes a word that has just been said and uses it as a springboard - either for what it brings to mind, or for a "wait, which one do you mean?"

`というと` is `と`, the conditional: "**if** we say X, then…". That is why it has two faces. Point it forward and you get automatic association - <ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>というと、お<ruby>寺<rt>てら</rt></ruby>. Point it back at the person who spoke and you get a request for clarification - <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんというと、<ruby>営業<rt>えいぎょう</rt></ruby>の? Both start from the same place: a word has landed, and you are working out what follows from it.

---

## ～というと - speaking of; when you say… · Formation

The phrase attaches to whatever word you are picking up. It is a connective, so a comma usually follows.

| Clause | plain form + というと | <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くというと |
| Standing alone, as a question | というと？ | - |

| Close relatives | といえば／といったら | <ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>といえば |

## ～というと - speaking of; when you say… · Nuance & register

`というと` is **reactive**. Something has to be on the table already - a word the other person used, a place name that has just come up, a topic you yourself introduced one sentence earlier. That is what separates it from simply starting to talk about something.

Register is neutral. `といいますと` is the polite form and is standard in customer-facing speech: 「<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>といいますと、<ruby>何日<rt>なんにち</rt></ruby>ごろでしょうか。」 In casual speech it becomes `っていうと` or, with the vowel stretched, `ってゆうと`, which is very common and mildly sloppy in writing.

---

## ～というと - speaking of; when you say… · Nuance & register (cont.)

The clarification use deserves separate practice, because it is the politest way to say "I'm not sure I follow." A bare `というと？` puts the burden back on the speaker without accusing them of being unclear, and in a meeting it is far softer than 「どういう<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>ですか」. The full form `…というと、…ということですか` is the careful version.

One thing `というと` does not do is give advice. It draws out what a word implies; it does not tell the listener what to do about it. That job belongs to `なら`.

---

## ～というと - speaking of; when you say… · というと vs といえば

> **Rule of thumb** - heavily overlapping. As a tendency: `といえば` freely *raises* a topic by association, including one nobody asked about; `というと` leans on a word that has just been said, and is the one that turns into a question.

<ruby>温泉<rt>おんせん</rt></ruby>**といえば**、<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>草津<rt>くさつ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くんです。<br>*Speaking of hot springs - I'm off to Kusatsu next month.*

<ruby>温泉<rt>おんせん</rt></ruby>**というと**、やはり<ruby>冬<rt>ふゆ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたくなりますね。<br>*Hot springs - you do want to go in winter, don't you.*

The first changes the subject to the speaker's own news; that is `といえば` territory, and `そういえば` ("that reminds me") is its most common form. The second stays on the general association. In the association use they are close to interchangeable, and nobody will misunderstand you either way.

---

## ～というと - speaking of; when you say… · というと vs といったら

> **Rule of thumb** - `といったら` is the emotional one. It sets the noun up so you can exclaim about it: "when it comes to X - honestly!"

<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>暑<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>さ**といったら**、<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>にもなれない。<br>*The heat of a Kyoto summer - you don't even feel like stepping outside.*

<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>**というと**、<ruby>祇園祭<rt>ぎおんまつり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>す<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>いでしょう。<br>*Summer in Kyoto brings the Gion Festival to mind for most people.*

`といったら` almost always ends in something extreme - an exclamation, a negative you cannot fix, a comparison nobody could beat. Used for a flat, neutral association it sounds like you started an exclamation and forgot to finish it.

---

## ～というと - speaking of; when you say… · というと vs なら

> **Rule of thumb** - both pick up what the other person said. `というと` asks what it means or what it evokes; `なら` accepts it as a premise and gives advice.

「<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>こうと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。」「<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>**なら**、<ruby>秋<rt>あき</rt></ruby>がおすすめですよ。」<br>*"I'm thinking of going to Kyoto." "If it's Kyoto, I'd recommend autumn."*

「<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>こうと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。」「<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>**というと**、お<ruby>寺<rt>てら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>回<rt>まわ</rt></ruby>るんですか。」<br>*"I'm thinking of going to Kyoto." "Kyoto - so you'll be doing the temples?"*

Swap them and both replies go wrong: `なら` cannot ask what a word meant, and `というと` cannot recommend a season.

---

## ～というと - speaking of; when you say… · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>になるというと、<ruby>花見<rt>はなみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。 → ✅ <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>になると、<ruby>花見<rt>はなみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*For "when spring comes", plain `と` is the conditional you want. `というと` needs a *word* or a *statement* in front of it, not an event.*

- ❌ <ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>だというと、お<ruby>寺<rt>てら</rt></ruby>ですね。 → ✅ <ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>というと、お<ruby>寺<rt>てら</rt></ruby>ですね。<br>*Nouns attach directly. The `だ` version quotes a proposition - "if we say that it is Kyoto" - which is not what you mean.*

- ❌ 「<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がなくて<ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>っています。」「というと、<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>いましょうか。」 → ✅ 「それなら、<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>いましょうか。」<br>*`というと` opens a question about meaning, so following it with an offer leaves the listener waiting for the rest of the question.*

---

## ～というと - speaking of; when you say… · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>寿司<rt>すし</rt></ruby>というと、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ <ruby>寿司<rt>すし</rt></ruby>といえば、この<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>おいしい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>つけました。／<ruby>寿司<rt>すし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*What follows `というと` should be what the word calls up *in general*, or a question about it - not a bare personal fact. For "as for sushi, I like it", plain `は` is enough.*

{reveal}

# ～というより - rather than

**Meaning** · rejects one description as not quite right and puts a better one in its place - "it's not so much X as Y"

`というより` compares **two ways of describing the same thing**, not two things. The first label is usually the one already in the air - the word the other person used, or the polite word you started to reach for - and the second is what you actually mean. The first is never called wrong, only inexact, which is why the pattern is so useful for saying blunt things gently.

---

## ～というより - rather than · Formation

Everything attaches in its plain form, and nouns and な-adjectives normally drop `だ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + というより | <ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>というより |
| な-adjective | な-adj + というより（no `な`） | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かというより |
| い-adjective | plain form + というより | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いというより |
| Verb | plain form + というより | <ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っているというより |
| Emphatic | というよりも／というよりむしろ | <ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>というよりむしろ |
| Casual | っていうより | <ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>っていうより |
| Common closing | …に<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>い／…のほうが<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>い | <ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>い |

---

## ～というより - rather than · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the **better** label goes second. English puts it first ("more a hobby than a job"), Japanese puts it last （<code><ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>というより<ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>だ</code>）. Reversing the order reverses the meaning, and this is the single commonest error with the pattern.

---

## ～というより - rather than · Examples

これは<ruby>食事<rt>しょくじ</rt></ruby>**というより**おやつだね。<br>*This is more of a snack than a meal.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>涼<rt>すず</rt></ruby>しい**というより**、もう<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いくらいだ。<br>*Today isn't so much cool as downright cold.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**というより**、<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>と<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すのが<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>なんだと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う。<br>*He's not quiet so much as bad at talking to people, I think.*

あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>**というより**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえました。<br>*What he said sounded less like an explanation than an excuse.*

---

## ～というより - rather than · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>向<rt>む</rt></ruby>こうから<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っている**というより**<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>いで<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いているように<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えた。<br>*The person coming towards us looked less like they were running than walking in a hurry.*

この<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**というより**、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>すぎて<ruby>逆<rt>ぎゃく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>になる。<br>*This price isn't cheap so much as suspiciously cheap.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っているのは、やせたい**というより**、<ruby>一日<rt>いちにち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>気持<rt>きも</rt></ruby>ちよく<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めたいからだそうです。<br>*She runs every morning not so much to lose weight as to get the day off to a good start.*

<ruby>今回<rt>こんかい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>変更<rt>へんこう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>改善<rt>かいぜん</rt></ruby>**というより**、<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>のやり<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>に<ruby>戻<rt>もど</rt></ruby>しただけだという<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>社内<rt>しゃない</rt></ruby>にはあります。<br>*Some inside the company say this change is less an improvement than simply a return to the old way.*

---

## ～というより - rather than · Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself, but the *move* it makes is a politeness device. Naming the gentler word first and correcting it downwards lets you land a hard judgement without having stated it flatly: <code>まずいというより、<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>薄<rt>うす</rt></ruby>い</code> is far kinder than starting with 「まずい」. The same works upwards - <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>というより、もうプロだ</code> - where the pattern reads as genuine admiration rather than flattery, because you appear to be searching for the accurate word.

---

## ～というより - rather than · Nuance & register (cont.)

`むしろ` is its natural companion and adds "if anything": <code><ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いというより、むしろ<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きなほうです</code>. `も` on the end (`というよりも`) simply adds weight. In casual speech the whole thing contracts to `っていうより`, and in fast conversation you will hear it trailing off entirely - 「<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>ってるっていうより…ね」 - where the listener is expected to supply the better word.

Because the pattern is essentially a self-correction, it also works as a hedge on your own speech. Starting a sentence over with `というより` mid-turn signals that you are refining your point, not contradicting yourself, and it buys you a moment to find the word you want.

---

## ～というより - rather than · というより vs より

> **Rule of thumb** - `より` compares **two things** on one scale. `というより` compares **two labels** for one thing. If you can insert 「のほうが」 after the second item, you want plain `より`.

<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>**より**<ruby>今年<rt>ことし</rt></ruby>のほうが<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>い。<br>*There's more rain this year than last.*

<ruby>今年<rt>ことし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>い**というより**、ずっと<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>いている<ruby>感<rt>かん</rt></ruby>じだ。<br>*It's not that there's a lot of rain this year - it feels like it simply hasn't stopped.*

In the first sentence there are two years and one quantity of rain. In the second there is one year and two ways of putting it. That is the whole distinction, and the grammar follows from it: `より` takes a noun, `というより` takes a full description.

---

## ～というより - rather than · というより vs ではなく

> **Rule of thumb** - `ではなく` says the first label is **wrong**. `というより` says it is **understandable but inexact**.

これは<ruby>誤解<rt>ごかい</rt></ruby>**ではなく**、はっきりとした<ruby>間違<rt>まちが</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*This isn't a misunderstanding - it's an outright error.*

これは<ruby>誤解<rt>ごかい</rt></ruby>**というより**、<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>仕方<rt>しかた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>かったのだと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*I'd call this less a misunderstanding than a failure to explain properly.*

`ではなく` corrects; `というより` negotiates. In a disagreement the second is much easier to say to someone senior, because it concedes that their word was a reasonable first attempt.

---

## ～というより - rather than · というより vs どころか

> **Rule of thumb** - `どころか` overshoots on purpose: not X - in fact something far beyond X, often in the opposite direction. `というより` stays calm and simply re-labels.

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げない**どころか**、<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>に<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>をつけることもできない。<br>*Far from being unable to swim, he can't even put his face in the water.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げない**というより**、<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>が<ruby>怖<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>いのだろう。<br>*It's not that he can't swim - it's more that he's afraid of the water.*

`どころか` is emphatic and often a little dramatic; `というより` is analytical. Choosing the wrong one is not ungrammatical, but it misreports how strongly you feel.

---

## ～というより - rather than · Common mistakes

- ❌ これはおやつというより<ruby>食事<rt>しょくじ</rt></ruby>だ。（meaning "it's more a snack than a meal"） → ✅ これは<ruby>食事<rt>しょくじ</rt></ruby>というよりおやつだ。<br>*The accurate label goes **second**. Word order alone decides which description you are endorsing, so a reversed pair says the opposite of what you meant.*

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**な**というより → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かというより<br>*な-adjectives lose their `な` here, and they do not take `だ` either. Only the bare stem goes in front of `という`.*

- ❌ <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>というより<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>のほうが<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い。 → ✅ <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>より<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>のほうが<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い。<br>*Two different people on one scale is a plain `より` comparison. `というより` would mean you were choosing between calling the same person an older or a younger brother.*

---

## ～というより - rather than · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いというより。 → ✅ <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いというより、<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>なんです。<br>*The pattern needs both halves. Stopping after the rejected label leaves the listener with the word you have just told them not to use. (In casual speech people do trail off like this - but only when the better word is obvious from context.)*

# ～とおり - in the way; the same as

**Meaning** · exactly as some model specifies - as told, as taught, as planned, as expected

The noun behind it is <code><ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby></code>'s cousin <code><ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>り</code>, "a route", and the metaphor still shows: something has laid down a path - an instruction, a schedule, a prediction - and the action follows it without stepping off. What `とおり` insists on is **exactness**. If there is room for interpretation, this is the wrong word.

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · Formation

Two things decide the shape: whether a verb or a noun comes first, and - for nouns - whether you use `の`. The `どおり` form with rendaku is not optional.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain non-past) | V(る) + とおり（に） | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うとおりに |
| Verb (plain past) | V(た) + とおり（に） | <ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>ったとおりに |
| Noun, with の | Noun + の + とおり（に） | <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby> → <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>のとおりに |
| Noun, bare | Noun + どおり（に） - rendaku | <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby> → <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>どおりに |
| Modifying a noun | ～とおりの + Noun | <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったとおりの<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby> |
| Demonstratives | このとおり／そのとおり／あのとおり | そのとおりです |
| Set phrases | ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>のとおり、おっしゃるとおり、<ruby>文字<rt>もじ</rt></ruby>どおり | - |

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `の` + とおり, or bare noun + **どおり**. Never `の` + どおり, and never a bare noun + とおり. <code><ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>のとおり</code> and <code><ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>どおり</code> are both correct; ❌<code><ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>のどおり</code> and ❌<code><ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>とおり</code> are not.

> **Note** - the `に` is dropped when the phrase is followed by `だ／です` or stands as a comment: <code><ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったとおりだ</code>, `そのとおりです`. Keep the `に` when it modifies a verb: <code><ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったとおりに<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>んだ</code>. Written in kanji the bare-noun form is <code><ruby>予定通<rt>よていどお</rt></ruby>り</code> - same reading, and the rendaku is still there.

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · Examples

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**とおりに**やってみます。<br>*I'll try doing it exactly as the teacher said.*

<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>**どおり**に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*The train arrived right on schedule.*

<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>の**とおりに**<ruby>組<rt>く</rt></ruby>み<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>てたのに、なぜか<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>きません。<br>*I put it together exactly as the manual said, and for some reason it doesn't work.*

<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った**とおり**、あの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>も<ruby>混<rt>こ</rt></ruby>んでいた。<br>*Just as I thought, that place was packed again today.*

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · Examples (cont.)

「<ruby>準備<rt>じゅんび</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めにということですね。」「その**とおり**です。」<br>*"So you're saying we should prepare early." "Exactly."*

<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>**どおり**<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>んでいますので、<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>には<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わる<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>こみです。<br>*The work is going to plan, so we expect it to finish next month.*

ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>の**とおり**、<ruby>会場<rt>かいじょう</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>満席<rt>まんせき</rt></ruby>ですので、<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>になってしまいます。<br>*As you can see, the hall is already full, so you'd have to stand.*

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>に<ruby>教<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>わった**とおりに**<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>ったつもりでしたが、<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>はまったく<ruby>別物<rt>べつもの</rt></ruby>になってしまいました。<br>*I thought I'd made it just the way my mother taught me, but it came out tasting like something else entirely.*

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral and equally at home in a manual, a news report and a kitchen. What varies is the fixed phrases built on it, and several of them are worth learning as units.

`そのとおりです` is strong agreement - "precisely so" - and is common in business Japanese, where a bare 「はい」 can sound thin. Its polite version, `おっしゃるとおりです`, agrees with a superior and is one of the most useful phrases in an office; `おっしゃるとおりですが…` is how you begin to disagree politely. <code>ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>のとおり</code> ("as you can see") belongs to presentations, and <code><ruby>文字<rt>もじ</rt></ruby>どおり</code> means "literally".

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · Nuance & register (cont.)

The bare-noun `どおり` forms behave almost like single words - <code><ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>どおり</code>, <code><ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>どおり</code>, <code><ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>どおり</code>, <code><ruby>期待<rt>きたい</rt></ruby>どおり</code>, <code><ruby>注文<rt>ちゅうもん</rt></ruby>どおり</code> - and they carry a faint official tone, the sound of things going as they were supposed to. The verb forms with `とおりに` are more personal: someone told you, taught you or predicted, and you matched it.

One tense point that trips people up: both <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うとおりに</code> and <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったとおりに</code> are correct, and they are not quite the same. The non-past form is habitual or still ongoing - "as he says", the way he always tells you. The past form points at one particular occasion - "as he said (that time)".

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · とおりに vs ように

> **Rule of thumb** - `とおりに` means "matching the model exactly". `ように` means "in a manner resembling", and it also does purpose ("so that"), which `とおりに` cannot do at all.

<ruby>見本<rt>みほん</rt></ruby>の**とおりに**<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてください。<br>*Please write it exactly like the sample.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもにも<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>める**ように**、<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きく<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてください。<br>*Please write it large, so that children can read it too.*

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · とおりに vs ように (cont.)

The second sentence has no model to copy - it states a goal. Note also that `ように` can take a resemblance with no instruction behind it （<code><ruby>鳥<rt>とり</rt></ruby>のように<ruby>飛<rt>と</rt></ruby>ぶ</code>）, where `とおりに` would be nonsense: a bird has issued no instructions.

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · とおりに vs まま

> **Rule of thumb** - `とおりに` is about **following** something. `まま` is about **not changing** something. One copies a model; the other leaves a state alone.

<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>った**とおりに**<ruby>包丁<rt>ほうちょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えば、<ruby>指<rt>ゆび</rt></ruby>を<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>ることはありません。<br>*If you use the knife the way you were taught, you won't cut your finger.*

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けた**まま**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけてしまいました。<br>*I went out and left the window open.*

The two look similar after a past-tense verb, which is where the confusion starts. Ask what the previous action is doing: supplying a pattern to imitate (`とおりに`), or supplying a condition that persists (`まま`)?

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · とおりに vs にしたがって

> **Rule of thumb** - `にしたがって` is the formal "in accordance with", used for rules, orders and procedures. It also has a second life meaning "as X progresses, Y", which `とおりに` never has.

<ruby>係員<rt>かかりいん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>指示<rt>しじ</rt></ruby>**にしたがって**<ruby>移動<rt>いどう</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*Please move in accordance with the staff's instructions.*

<ruby>係員<rt>かかりいん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**とおりに**<ruby>階段<rt>かいだん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>下<rt>お</rt></ruby>りました。<br>*We went down the stairs exactly as the attendant told us.*

`にしたがって` sounds like a public announcement; `とおりに` sounds like a person recounting what they did. For the "as X increases" meaning - <code><ruby>年<rt>とし</rt></ruby>をとるにしたがって</code> - only `したがって` works.

---

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>のどおり<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>んでいます。 → ✅ <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>どおり／<ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>のとおり<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>んでいます。<br>*Pick one: `の` plus `とおり`, or a bare noun plus `どおり`. The two cannot be combined, and this is the single most common error with the pattern.*

- ❌ <ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>とおりに<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きました。 → ✅ <ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>どおりに<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*A noun attaching directly triggers rendaku. `とおり` only stays unvoiced after `の` or after a verb.*

- ❌ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったのとおりに → ✅ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったとおりに<br>*No `の` after a verb. The `の` in <code><ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>のとおり</code> belongs to the noun, not to the pattern.*

## ～とおり - in the way; the same as · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>鳥<rt>とり</rt></ruby>のとおりに<ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飛<rt>と</rt></ruby>んでみたい。 → ✅ <ruby>鳥<rt>とり</rt></ruby>のように<ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飛<rt>と</rt></ruby>んでみたい。<br>*`とおりに` needs an instruction, plan or prediction to follow. For plain resemblance you want `ように`.*

# ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand

**Meaning** · sets two true sides of the same situation next to each other, without cancelling either

<code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby></code> is "one side". The pattern says: here is one side, and here is the other, and both are the case. That is what separates it from `でも` and `しかし`, which put a stronger fact on top of a weaker one. <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code> weighs; it does not overrule. It is also how you say that one person or one thing is doing two things at once.

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Formation

The clause in front is plain form. Only nouns and な-adjectives need help joining.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + <ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>（で） | <ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>く<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で |
| い-adjective | plain form + <ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で |
| な-adjective | ～な／～である + <ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で | <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby> → <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>な<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で |
| Noun | Noun + である + <ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で | <ruby>教師<rt>きょうし</rt></ruby> → <ruby>教師<rt>きょうし</rt></ruby>である<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で |
| Past tense | V(た) + <ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で | <ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>えた → <ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>えた<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で |
| Starting a new sentence | <ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>、／その<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で、 | <ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>地方<rt>ちほう</rt></ruby>では… |

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - do not confuse <code>～する<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code> with <code>～する<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>だ</code>. The first joins two sides; the second means "keeps getting more so, and only that". <code><ruby>物価<rt>ぶっか</rt></ruby>が<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がる<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>だ</code> means prices just keep climbing, and it needs no second clause at all.

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Examples

<ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>は<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>通<rt>かよ</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*My brother works and goes to university at the same time.*

この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かでいい**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>が<ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>です。<br>*This town is nicely quiet, but on the other side of that, shopping is inconvenient.*

スマホは<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>な**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>をうばうものでもある。<br>*Smartphones are convenient, and by the same token they eat your time.*

<ruby>都会<rt>とかい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人口<rt>じんこう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>え<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>けている。**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>**、<ruby>地方<rt>ちほう</rt></ruby>では<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>減<rt>へ</rt></ruby>っている。<br>*City populations keep growing. In the regions, meanwhile, people are leaving.*

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>厳<rt>きび</rt></ruby>しい**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>ったときには<ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ず<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>けてくれました。<br>*My father was strict, and at the same time he never failed to help when I was in trouble.*

この<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>がいい**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みがほとんど<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>れないそうです。<br>*The pay in that job is good, but on the other hand you can hardly take any time off.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>制度<rt>せいど</rt></ruby>は<ruby>若<rt>わか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>社員<rt>しゃいん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>喜<rt>よろこ</rt></ruby>ばれている**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>く<ruby>勤<rt>つと</rt></ruby>めてきた<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>たちからは<ruby>不満<rt>ふまん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ています。<br>*The new system has gone down well with younger staff, while long-serving employees have voiced complaints.*

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>祖母<rt>そぼ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>畑<rt>はたけ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>ける**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>週<rt>しゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>は<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>の<ruby>教室<rt>きょうしつ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>習字<rt>しゅうじ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*My grandmother keeps working her field, and on top of that goes into town once a week for a calligraphy class.*

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Nuance & register

This is a **written** pattern first. It belongs to newspaper articles, reports, essays and prepared presentations, and it is the standard way to present a balanced picture: an advantage and its cost, a national trend and its regional exception, a policy and the grumbling it caused. In casual conversation people join the same two ideas with `けど`, `し` or 「でも」, and reaching for <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code> over a drink sounds like you are reading from notes.

The tone is **even-handed**, and this is what learners most often miss. Because the construction gives both clauses equal footing, the writer comes across as fair rather than critical, which is why it appears so often in business writing where a flat complaint would be impolitic. If you want the second clause to win, use `が` or `しかし`.

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Nuance & register (cont.)

The two clauses do not have to be opposites. A perfectly ordinary use is simple simultaneity - one subject doing two things （<code><ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>く<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>通<rt>かよ</rt></ruby>う</code>） - where English reaches for "while also". When the clauses *are* opposites, the `で` is often dropped in more formal writing: <code><ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>える<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>質<rt>しつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちている</code>. Sentence-initial <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>、</code> and <code>その<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で、</code> do the same job across a full stop and are extremely common in news writing.

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · 一方で vs 反面

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>反面<rt>はんめん</rt></ruby></code> needs the two sides to be **opposite in evaluation**: a merit and its matching drawback, in the same subject. <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code> is wider - the two clauses may simply be parallel, and may even have different subjects.

この<ruby>素材<rt>そざい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>い**<ruby>反面<rt>はんめん</rt></ruby>**、<ruby>傷<rt>きず</rt></ruby>がつきやすい。<br>*This material is light, but by the same token it scratches easily.*

<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>は<ruby>銀行<rt>ぎんこう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>はボランティアもしています。<br>*My sister works at a bank, and on top of that volunteers at weekends.*

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · 一方で vs 反面 (cont.)

Put <code><ruby>反面<rt>はんめん</rt></ruby></code> in the second sentence and it goes wrong, because volunteering is not the downside of banking. Anywhere <code><ruby>反面<rt>はんめん</rt></ruby></code> works, <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code> also works; the reverse is not true.

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · 一方で vs が・しかし

> **Rule of thumb** - `が` and `しかし` make the second clause the point. <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code> leaves both clauses standing. If you are building to a conclusion, you want the adversative; if you are describing a situation, you want <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code>.

<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**が**、<ruby>品質<rt>ひんしつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>だ。<br>*It's cheap, but I'm worried about the quality.*

<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>品質<rt>ひんしつ</rt></ruby>にはばらつきがあるようだ。<br>*While the price is low, the quality seems to vary.*

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · 一方で vs が・しかし (cont.)

The first is heading towards a decision not to buy. The second is a report, and the reader is left to weigh it. In a recommendation or a complaint, `が` is what you want; in an analysis, it can sound like you have prejudged.

---

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · ～一方で vs ～一方だ

> **Rule of thumb** - with `で` it needs a second clause and means "on the other side of that". With `だ` it ends the sentence and means "keeps going that way, more and more".

<ruby>外国人<rt>がいこくじん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>観光客<rt>かんこうきゃく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>える**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>だ**。<br>*The number of foreign tourists just keeps rising.*

<ruby>観光客<rt>かんこうきゃく</rt></ruby>が<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>える**<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で**、<ruby>地元<rt>じもと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>生活<rt>せいかつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>になっている。<br>*As visitor numbers rise, life for locals is getting less convenient.*

<code>～<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>だ</code> takes only a non-past verb of change - <ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>える, <ruby>減<rt>へ</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>くなる - and always carries a sense that the trend is one-way and usually unwelcome.

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>だ<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がかかる。 → ✅ この<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>な<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がかかる。<br>*な-adjectives take `な` (or the formal `である`), never plain `だ`.*

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>小説<rt>しょうせつ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いている。 → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>である<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>小説<rt>しょうせつ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いている。<br>*A noun cannot attach bare. It needs `である` - and note that this makes the sentence distinctly written in tone.*

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>った<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になった。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったので、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になった。<br>*Cause and effect is not two sides of anything. <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code> joins clauses that are independently true, not a reason and its result.*

## ～一方で - on one hand; on the other hand · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>える<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で。 → ✅ <ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>える<ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>だ。<br>*If you mean "the work just keeps piling up" and have no second clause, you want <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>だ</code>. With `で` the sentence is left hanging.*

# ～だけでなく - not only… but also

**Meaning** · the first item is true, and it is not the whole story - something further comes with it

`だけ` draws a boundary; `でなく` says the boundary is in the wrong place. Together they set up a second item that the listener was not counting on. This is the everyday, neutral member of the "not only… but also" family - the one you can use in a meeting, an essay or a conversation without anyone noticing the choice.

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Formation

The clause or noun in front takes its plain form. The **second half almost always carries `も`**, and leaving it out is the commonest thing that makes these sentences sound unfinished.

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だけでなく | <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>だけでなく |
| Noun + が／を | particle is **dropped** | ❌<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>をだけでなく → ✅<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>だけでなく |
| Noun + に／で／と | particle is **kept** | <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもに → <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもにだけでなく |
| い-adjective | plain form + だけでなく | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いだけでなく |
| な-adjective | ～な + だけでなく | <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby> → <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>なだけでなく |
| Verb | plain form + だけでなく | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むだけでなく |
| Second clause | …も／…まで／…さえ | …<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>もいい |
| Casual | だけじゃなく（て） | <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>だけじゃなくて |
| Formal writing | のみならず | <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>のみならず |

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - both halves must point the **same way**. A good point followed by another good point, or a complaint followed by a worse one. If the second half reverses the first, you want `が` or <code><ruby>一方<rt>いっぽう</rt></ruby>で</code>, not `だけでなく`.

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Examples

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**だけでなく**、<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>もいいです。<br>*This place isn't just cheap - the food is good too.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>**だけでなく**、フランス<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby>も<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せます。<br>*She speaks not only English but French as well.*

この<ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>は<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>くなる**だけでなく**、<ruby>食欲<rt>しょくよく</rt></ruby>も<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちるそうです。<br>*This medicine doesn't just make you drowsy - apparently it kills your appetite too.*

<ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まった**だけでなく**、<ruby>道路<rt>どうろ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>れなくなった。<br>*The typhoon didn't just stop the trains; the roads became impassable as well.*

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Examples (cont.)

この<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>経験<rt>けいけん</rt></ruby>**だけでなく**、<ruby>体力<rt>たいりょく</rt></ruby>も<ruby>必要<rt>ひつよう</rt></ruby>です。<br>*This job takes stamina as well as experience.*

<ruby>祖父<rt>そふ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>畑<rt>はたけ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>近所<rt>きんじょ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>配<rt>くば</rt></ruby>る**だけでなく**、<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>って<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>けています。<br>*My grandfather doesn't just hand out vegetables from his field - he cooks meals and delivers those too.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいアプリは<ruby>操作<rt>そうさ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>な**だけでなく**、<ruby>外国語<rt>がいこくご</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>対応<rt>たいおう</rt></ruby>しているので、<ruby>旅行先<rt>りょこうさき</rt></ruby>でも<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えます。<br>*The new app isn't just easy to operate - it handles foreign languages too, so you can use it while travelling.*

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>今度<rt>こんど</rt></ruby>の<ruby>大会<rt>たいかい</rt></ruby>には<ruby>市内<rt>しない</rt></ruby>の<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>**だけでなく**、<ruby>隣<rt>となり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>県<rt>けん</rt></ruby>からも<ruby>参加<rt>さんか</rt></ruby>するチームがあるそうです。<br>*I hear teams are coming to this tournament from the neighbouring prefecture as well as from schools in the city.*

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Nuance & register

This is the **neutral default** of its family. It is equally natural in ます-form speech, in plain-form conversation, in a report and in a school essay, and nothing about it marks you as formal or casual. The casual contraction `だけじゃなく` - often `だけじゃなくて` - is what you actually hear in relaxed talk, and it is the only register move the pattern makes. At the other end, `のみならず` is the written formal equivalent and belongs in documents rather than speech.

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Nuance & register (cont.)

The `も` in the second half is doing real work: it marks the second item as an **addition** to the first, which is what stops the sentence sounding like a correction. Compare <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いだけでなく、<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>もいい</code> with <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いだけでなく、<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>がいい</code> - the second is not wrong, but it reads as if the taste were a replacement for the cheapness rather than a bonus on top. When the second item goes further than you might expect, `まで` or `さえ` replaces `も` and adds a note of surprise.

One use worth naming: `だけでなく` is a tactful way to widen a demand. Saying <code><ruby>今回<rt>こんかい</rt></ruby>だけでなく、<ruby>次回<rt>じかい</rt></ruby>からもお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします</code> adds a request without repeating it, because the first half is already agreed.

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · だけでなく vs ばかりでなく

> **Rule of thumb** - the same structure, one shade apart in register. `だけでなく` is neutral and the more frequent in speech; `ばかりでなく` leans written and puts a little more weight on the second item.

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>詳<rt>くわ</rt></ruby>しい**だけでなく**、<ruby>例文<rt>れいぶん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>い。<br>*This dictionary isn't just detailed in its explanations - it has plenty of examples too.*

この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つの<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>**ばかりでなく**、<ruby>業界<rt>ぎょうかい</rt></ruby><ruby>全体<rt>ぜんたい</rt></ruby>にかかわっている。<br>*This issue concerns not just one company but the industry as a whole.*

You could swap them in either sentence and be understood. The difference is the room they sit in: the first belongs to a conversation about a dictionary, the second to a newspaper.

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · だけでなく vs うえに

> **Rule of thumb** - `だけでなく` corrects an assumption: "you thought it was just X - it's Y as well." <code><ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>に</code> simply piles on: "X, and on top of that Y." Only `だけでなく` implies somebody was underestimating.

この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>い**<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>に**、<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>当<rt>あ</rt></ruby>たらない。<br>*This room is cramped, and on top of that it gets no sun.*

この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>い**だけでなく**、<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>当<rt>あ</rt></ruby>たらないんです。<br>*It isn't just that the room is small - it gets no sun either.*

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · だけでなく vs うえに (cont.)

The first is a straight list of grievances. The second answers someone who thought the size was the only problem. Note also that <code><ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>に</code> is almost always used within one topic and one evaluation direction, while `だけでなく` can span two separate subjects.

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · だけでなく vs はもちろん

> **Rule of thumb** - `はもちろん` presents the first item as **too obvious to argue about** and moves straight to the second. `だけでなく` makes no such claim; it treats both items as news.

この<ruby>温泉<rt>おんせん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>地元<rt>じもと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>**はもちろん**、<ruby>外国<rt>がいこく</rt></ruby>からの<ruby>客<rt>きゃく</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>人気<rt>にんき</rt></ruby>があります。<br>*This hot spring is popular with locals, needless to say, and with visitors from abroad too.*

この<ruby>温泉<rt>おんせん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>地元<rt>じもと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>**だけでなく**、<ruby>外国<rt>がいこく</rt></ruby>からの<ruby>客<rt>きゃく</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>人気<rt>にんき</rt></ruby>があります。<br>*This hot spring is popular not only with locals but with visitors from abroad.*

Nearly interchangeable. The first flatters the reader slightly - of course you knew about the locals - and is common in advertising for that reason. The second is plainer and safer when the first item may genuinely be new information.

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>だけでなく、フランス<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せます。 → ✅ <ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>だけでなく、フランス<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby>**も**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せます。<br>*The second half needs `も` (or `まで`, `さえ`). Without it, the addition reads as a substitution and the sentence feels cut short.*

- ❌ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>だけでなく、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いです。 → ✅ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>**な**だけでなく、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くもあります。<br>*な-adjectives keep their `な` in front of `だけ`. The `だ` form never appears here.*

- ❌ <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>をだけでなく、<ruby>文法<rt>ぶんぽう</rt></ruby>も<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>した。 → ✅ <ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>だけでなく、<ruby>文法<rt>ぶんぽう</rt></ruby>も<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>した。<br>*`が` and `を` drop before `だけ`. `に`, `で` and `と` stay: <code><ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちにだけでなく</code>.*

---

## ～だけでなく - not only… but also · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いだけでなく、まずいです。 → ✅ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いですが、まずいです。<br>*The two halves have to run in the same direction. A good point followed by a bad one is a contrast, and needs `が` or `けれど`.*

# ～ばかりでなく - not only… but also; as well as

**Meaning** · one item is not the limit of it - and what follows often goes further than the listener expected

Structurally this is the twin of `だけでなく`: first item, then a second one added with `も`, `まで` or `さえ`. What `ばかり` brings is the attitude it always brings. It says the first item was being treated as the whole picture, and it very often introduces a second item that is heavier, worse or more surprising than the first. It also leans towards the written page.

## ～ばかりでなく - not only… but also; as well as · Nuance & register

`ばかりでなく` is the **written-leaning** member of the family. It is at home in newspapers, reports, formal letters and public announcements, and it is perfectly usable in careful speech - but in relaxed conversation most speakers say `だけでなく` or `だけじゃなくて`. Nothing about it is stiff enough to sound wrong out loud; it simply sounds considered.

The attitude carried over from plain `ばかり` is worth noticing. The first item is presented as something that was being taken for the whole, and the second often lands harder - which is why the pattern is so common in accounts of things going wrong: <code><ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れたばかりでなく、<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>もなかった</code>. When the second item is genuinely startling, `まで` or `さえ` replaces `も` and the sentence tips into indignation or admiration.

---

## ～ばかりでなく - not only… but also; as well as · Nuance & register (cont.)

`ばかりか` is the same idea intensified, and it is common in writing. Two practical differences: it is more emphatic, and it cannot be followed by a request, suggestion or command - a restriction `ばかりでなく` does not share. That makes `ばかりでなく` the safer choice whenever your sentence is heading towards 「～てください」 or 「～べきだ」.

---

## ばかりでなく vs だけでなく

> **Rule of thumb** - same grammar, different room. `だけでなく` is neutral and dominates speech; `ばかりでなく` is a shade more formal and puts more weight on what follows.

この<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>授業料<rt>じゅぎょうりょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**だけでなく**、<ruby>寮<rt>りょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>費用<rt>ひよう</rt></ruby>もかかります。<br>*This university isn't just expensive in tuition - there are dormitory costs as well.*

<ruby>環境<rt>かんきょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つの<ruby>国<rt>くに</rt></ruby>**ばかりでなく**、<ruby>世界<rt>せかい</rt></ruby><ruby>全体<rt>ぜんたい</rt></ruby>で<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えるべきだ。<br>*Environmental problems must be considered not by one country alone but by the world as a whole.*

Put `ばかりでなく` in a chat about dormitory fees and it sounds like you are quoting a brochure. Put `だけでなく` in the second sentence and nothing breaks - it simply reads a little lighter.

---

## ばかりでなく vs ばかりか

> **Rule of thumb** - `ばかりか` is stronger and reports only. `ばかりでなく` is milder and can lead into a request or a recommendation.

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくれた**ばかりか**、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>ってくれた。<br>*He not only told me the way - he walked me to the station.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**ばかりでなく**、<ruby>大人<rt>おとな</rt></ruby>も<ruby>一緒<rt>いっしょ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>参加<rt>さんか</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*Please join in together - adults as well as children.*

Reverse them and the second sentence goes wrong: `ばかりか` cannot carry an invitation. The first sentence would survive the swap, just with less punch.

---

## ばかりでなく vs plain ばかり

> **Rule of thumb** - plain `ばかり` says "**nothing but** X, and that's too much". `ばかりでなく` says "**not only** X - Y as well". Same word, opposite work: one narrows, the other widens.

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>漫画<rt>まんが</rt></ruby>**ばかり**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいる。<br>*My brother reads nothing but comics.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>漫画<rt>まんが</rt></ruby>**ばかりでなく**、<ruby>小説<rt>しょうせつ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むようになった。<br>*My brother has started reading novels as well as comics.*

The giveaway is `でなく`, which negates the boundary. Learners who have met `ばかり` first often read the second sentence as a complaint; it is the opposite - a small piece of good news.

## ～ばかりでなく - not only… but also; as well as · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>はきれいばかりでなく、<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだ。 → ✅ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>はきれい**な**ばかりでなく、<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**でもある**。<br>*な-adjectives keep `な` in front of `ばかり`, and the second half needs something (`も`, `でもある`) marking it as an addition.*

# ～か何か - or something

**Meaning** · names one example and deliberately leaves the door open - "X, or something like it"

<code>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> is `か` ("or") plus <code><ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> ("something"), and it means exactly what the parts say: this, or whatever else would do. You use it when you do not know （<code><ruby>風邪<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>かだろう</code>）, when it does not matter （<code>お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってくる</code>）, or when being precise would sound demanding. It has a small family - <code>か<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>か</code> for people and `かどこか` for places - built exactly the same way.

---

## ～か何か - or something · Formation

The whole phrase behaves as a single noun, so any particle attaches after it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun - thing | Noun + か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か | コーヒー → コーヒーか<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か |
| Noun - person | Noun + か<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>か | <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん → <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんか<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>か |
| Noun - place | Noun + かどこか | <ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby> → <ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>かどこか |
| Modifying a noun | ～か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>かの + Noun | <ruby>病気<rt>びょうき</rt></ruby>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>かの<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby> |

## ～か何か - or something · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the question word has to match the category. Things take <code><ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code>, people take <code><ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>か</code>, places take `どこか`. ❌<code><ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんか<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> is not vague - it is rude, because it files a person under "things".

## ～か何か - or something · Examples (cont.)

さっきから<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>で<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がするけど、<ruby>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>**か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か**かな。<br>*There's been a noise outside for a while - the wind or something, maybe.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くのが<ruby>不安<rt>ふあん</rt></ruby>なら、<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さん**か<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>か**に<ruby>一緒<rt>いっしょ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てもらったらどうですか。<br>*If you're nervous about going alone, why not ask Yamada or somebody to come with you?*

---

## ～か何か - or something · Nuance & register

The pattern is neutral in politeness and extremely common in speech. What it adds is **vagueness on purpose**, and vagueness is a social tool. Asking <code>ペンか<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してもらえますか</code> is softer than asking for a pen, because you have told the listener that anything will do and left them a way to help without going to trouble. The same applies to gifts, invitations and suggestions: <code>お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>かどうですか</code> commits nobody to anything.

The other main use is a guess you cannot verify - <code><ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>かでしょう</code>, <code><ruby>病気<rt>びょうき</rt></ruby>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>かだったらしい</code>. Here the phrase protects you from asserting something you do not know, which is why it appears so often with `でしょう`, `かな`, `らしい` and `みたい`.

---

## ～か何か - or something · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two register limits. In formal writing, `など` does this job instead: <code><ruby>身分証明書<rt>みぶんしょうめいしょ</rt></ruby>などをご<ruby>用意<rt>ようい</rt></ruby>ください</code>, never <code>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code>. And pointing it at a person is risky. The correct member of the family for people is <code>か<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>か</code>, and even that should be used for someone not present - saying it about the person in front of you sounds like they are interchangeable.

---

## ～か何か - or something · ～か何か vs でも

> **Rule of thumb** - `でも` is for **offers and suggestions**: "how about X or something?" <code>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> is for **reference**: naming a thing loosely because the exact identity is unknown or unimportant.

お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>**でも**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きませんか。<br>*Shall we go for a cup of tea or something?*

のどが<ruby>渇<rt>かわ</rt></ruby>いたので、お<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>**か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ってきます。<br>*I'm thirsty, so I'll go and buy some tea or something.*

---

## ～か何か - or something · ～か何か vs でも (cont.)

The first is an invitation, and `でも` is what makes it casual enough to refuse. The second is a plan of your own, where there is nothing to be polite about - and `でも` there would sound as though you were inviting the tea. As a rough guide: if the sentence ends in 「～ませんか」 or 「～ましょう」, use `でも`.

---

## ～か何か - or something · ～か何か vs とか

> **Rule of thumb** - `とか` gives **two or more** examples of a set. <code>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> gives **one**, and leaves the rest blank.

<ruby>果物<rt>くだもの</rt></ruby>**とか**お<ruby>菓子<rt>かし</rt></ruby>**とか**を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていこう。<br>*Let's take fruit or sweets or something like that.*

<ruby>果物<rt>くだもの</rt></ruby>**か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か**を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていこう。<br>*Let's take fruit or something.*

`とか` sketches the shape of the category by listing; <code>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> points at one member and shrugs. Both are casual, and both are avoided in formal writing.

---

## ～か何か - or something · ～か何か vs 何か

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> alone means "something" with **no anchor**. <code>か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> needs a noun in front of it to be an alternative *to*.

<ruby>冷蔵庫<rt>れいぞうこ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か**<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>っていますか。<br>*Is there anything left in the fridge?*

<ruby>冷蔵庫<rt>れいぞうこ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>卵<rt>たまご</rt></ruby>**か<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か**<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>っていますか。<br>*Are there eggs or something left in the fridge?*

The first asks about the contents in general. The second has a specific thing in mind and is willing to accept a substitute - which, incidentally, makes it the more useful sentence when you are about to cook.

# なんか / なんて - things like; something

**Meaning** · throws a word out lightly - as an example, as something beneath discussion, or as something you can hardly believe

Both come from <code><ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby></code>. `なんか` is the worn-down form of <code><ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か</code> and of `など`; `なんて` is the worn-down form of `などと` and of `という`. What survives in both is a **dismissive shrug** - the speaker is holding the word at arm's length. Aim that shrug at yourself and it becomes modesty; aim it at your listener and it becomes an insult. Aim it at a fact and it becomes disbelief.

---

---

## なんか / なんて - things like; something · Nuance & register

Both words are **casual and spoken**. In writing they become `など` and `などと`, and a report that says <code><ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>なんか</code> reads as though it slipped out. Neither is rude in itself; what matters is where the shrug lands.

Pointed at **yourself**, `なんか` is the standard humble move: <code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>なんかでよければ</code>, <code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>なんかまだまだです</code>. This is polite, common, and expected when you are accepting praise you do not want to accept out loud. Pointed at **your listener**, the same word is an attack - <code>お<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>なんて</code> is the language of an argument, and learners should treat `あなたなんて` as unusable. Pointed at a **thing**, it downgrades it, which is fine for your own possessions and risky for someone else's.

---

## なんか / なんて - things like; something · Nuance & register (cont.)

`なんて` is the stronger of the two, and it has one job `なんか` cannot do: marking a whole clause as astonishing. <code><ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>ったなんてすごい</code> works; <code><ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>ったなんかすごい</code> does not. That use is not always negative - surprise cuts both ways, and admiration is as common as disapproval. `なんて` also appears as an exclamation before an adjective （<code>なんて<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>だろう</code>）, where it is the spoken cousin of literary `なんと`.

Finally, the filler `なんか` - "like", "sort of" - is everywhere in casual speech, especially among younger speakers. One or two soften a sentence; a string of them reads as vague, and it is one of the first habits teachers try to break in presentations.

---

## なんか / なんて - things like; something · なんか vs など

> **Rule of thumb** - identical grammar, different register. `など` is the written and formal form; `なんか` is what it sounds like in relaxed speech.

<ruby>回答<rt>かいとう</rt></ruby>では、<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>や<ruby>品質<rt>ひんしつ</rt></ruby>**など**に<ruby>対<rt>たい</rt></ruby>する<ruby>不満<rt>ふまん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>く<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>られた。<br>*In the responses, complaints about price and quality among other things were prominent.*

あの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>**なんか**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>にしない<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>だよ。<br>*That shop is for people who don't worry about little things like price.*

Note that `など` is neutral in attitude - it simply means "and the like" - while `なんか` usually keeps the downgrading tone even when it is only listing.

---

## なんか / なんて - things like; something · なんか vs なんて

> **Rule of thumb** - `なんか` follows a **noun** and downplays it. `なんて` is stronger, and it is the only one that can take a **whole clause** and mark it as unbelievable.

テレビ**なんか**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ていないで、<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>なさい。<br>*Never mind the television - get to bed.*

こんな<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>までテレビを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ている**なんて**、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きられないよ。<br>*Watching television at this hour - you'll never get up tomorrow.*

After a noun the two overlap and `なんて` is simply harsher: <code>テレビなんて<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ない</code> rejects television more firmly than <code>テレビなんか<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ない</code>. After a clause, only `なんて` is possible.

---

## なんか / なんて - things like; something · なんか・なんて vs とか

> **Rule of thumb** - `とか` lists examples neutrally. `なんか` and `なんて` name one thing **and comment on it**. If you have no attitude to express, `とか` is the safer word.

<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>洗濯<rt>せんたく</rt></ruby>**とか**<ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>**とか**で<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わってしまう。<br>*Weekends get eaten up by laundry, cleaning and so on.*

<ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>**なんか**<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>でいいよ。<br>*The cleaning can wait until tomorrow.*

The first sentence is a flat report of how the weekend goes. The second dismisses cleaning as not worth doing now - and if you said it to the person who has just started cleaning, it would sting.

# ～っけ - what was… again?

**Meaning** · asks for something you once knew and have mislaid - "what was it again?", "did I…?"

`っけ` is a sentence-final particle for **retrieval**, not enquiry. Using it says two things at once: I do not have this information right now, and I ought to. That admission is why it sounds friendly rather than demanding - you are not asking the listener to inform you, you are asking them to jog your memory. It also explains where it cannot go: you cannot `っけ` something you were never told.

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Formation

`っけ` wants a `だ` or a past tense in front of it. That is the whole difficulty, and it is where nearly every mistake comes from.

---

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Noun + だっけ／だったっけ | <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby> → <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>だっけ |
| な-adjective | ～だっけ／～だったっけ | <ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>だったっけ |
| い-adjective | ～かったっけ／～いんだっけ | <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>かったっけ |
| Verb, past | V(た) + っけ | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>った → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったっけ |
| Verb, non-past | V(る) + **んだっけ** | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るんだっけ |
| Negative | ～ないんだっけ／～なかったっけ | <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかったっけ |
| Polite | ～でしたっけ／～ましたっけ | <ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>でしたっけ |
| With a question word | どこ・<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>・<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby> + だっけ | どこだっけ |

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - a plain non-past verb does not take `っけ` directly. Insert `んだ`: ❌<code><ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るっけ</code> → ✅<code><ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るんだっけ</code>. Past-tense verbs need nothing: <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったっけ</code> is fine on its own.

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Examples (cont.)

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Nuance & register

`っけ` is **casual**. In plain form it belongs among friends, family and colleagues you are comfortable with, and it carries a slight thinking-out-loud quality - it often arrives with 「あれ」, 「えーと」 or a tilt of the head. It is used by speakers of every age and gender and is not marked either way.

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Nuance & register (cont.)

The polite forms `でしたっけ` and `ましたっけ` are the useful ones for work, and they do something clever: they ask for information while implying you were already given it. 「<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は3<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>からでしたっけ」 is softer than 「<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>からですか」 because it credits the listener with having told you. That said, the particle stays casual underneath the polite ending, so it is for colleagues, not for a first meeting with a client - and asking a senior person their name with <code>お<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>でしたっけ</code> is memorably bad, because it advertises that you were told and forgot.

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Nuance & register (cont.)

There is a second, quieter use: with a past-tense verb and no question intended, `っけ` becomes **reminiscence**. <code>よく<ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いたっけ</code> is not asking anything; it is "we used to walk here, didn't we", said half to yourself. Falling intonation marks it. This use is warm and slightly wistful and turns up constantly in song lyrics and in older speakers' reminiscing.

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · っけ vs か

> **Rule of thumb** - `か` asks for information. `っけ` asks for information **back**. Use `っけ` only when you had it once.

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>からです**か**。<br>*What time does the meeting start?*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>からでした**っけ**。<br>*What time was the meeting again?*

The first is a plain question and works with a stranger, at an information desk, about anything at all. The second only makes sense if the meeting time has already been announced - otherwise the listener is left wondering when they told you.

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · っけ vs かな

> **Rule of thumb** - `かな` wonders about something **unsettled**; `っけ` reaches for something **already settled** but forgotten.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れる**かな**。<br>*I wonder if it'll be fine tomorrow.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだった**っけ**。<br>*Tomorrow was a day off - was it?*

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · っけ vs かな (cont.)

Tomorrow's weather is not a fact anyone possesses yet, so `っけ` is impossible there. Tomorrow's holiday is a fact on a calendar, and you have simply lost it. The two do combine - <code>どこに<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>いたっけかな</code> - when you are searching your own memory aloud.

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · っけ vs ね

> **Rule of thumb** - `ね` checks something you **believe**; `っけ` admits you have **lost** it. `ね` invites agreement, `っけ` asks to be told.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は10<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>からです**ね**。<br>*It's from ten tomorrow, isn't it.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は10<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>からでした**っけ**。<br>*Was it ten tomorrow?*

The `ね` version is close to a confirmation of something you are fairly sure of, and the listener need only say 「はい」. The `っけ` version concedes that you might be wrong, which is more modest - and, if you have already asked once, more honest.

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みっけ? → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み**だっけ**?<br>*A noun needs `だ` (or `だった`) before `っけ`. The bare noun form does not exist in standard speech.*

- ❌ <ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>るっけ? → ✅ <ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>る**んだっけ**?<br>*A plain non-past verb takes `んだ` first. With the past tense you can attach `っけ` directly: <code><ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たっけ</code>.*

- ❌ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いっけ? → ✅ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>**かったっけ**?／<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>**いんだっけ**?<br>*い-adjectives behave like verbs here: past tense, or `んだ` inserted.*

---

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ （<ruby>初対面<rt>しょたいめん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に）お<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>でしたっけ。 → ✅ お<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>をうかがってもよろしいでしょうか。<br>*`っけ` claims you were told before. Used on someone you have just met it is merely confusing; used on someone who *did* tell you, it announces that you forgot.*

## ～っけ - what was… again? · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みました**ね**。 (b) <ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んだ**っけ**。

{reveal}
(a) states something you remember clearly and invites the listener to share the memory. It is polite, and the listener is expected to agree.

{reveal}
(b) is hazier and more casual. Depending on the intonation it is either a genuine question - was it this place last year? - or, with a falling tone, a piece of fond reminiscence said half to yourself. Either way it admits that the memory has gone soft, which is something `ね` never does.
