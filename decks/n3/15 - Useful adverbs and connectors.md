# だけど

**Meaning** · but; however - the casual sentence-opening "but"

`だけど` opens a **new sentence** that pushes back on the one just said. It is the everyday spoken "however": friends use it constantly, and it is one of the few words you should deliberately leave outside a job interview.

---

## だけど · Usage

`だけど` stands at the head of a sentence, with a comma after it in writing, and the objection follows. It does not conjugate, take particles or attach to anything - it is a fixed one-word opener.

- 「<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>こうよ。」「だけど、<ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るらしいよ。」

Historically it is the copula `だ` plus the clause-final `けど` you met at N5, and that history explains the one place learners reliably go wrong. After a **noun or な-adjective** the same string turns up *inside* a sentence, because the `だ` belongs to the first clause: <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だけど、もう<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>いている. After a **verb or い-adjective** there is no `だ` available, so it is plain `けど`: <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いけど<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いにくい - never ❌<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いだけど.

---

## だけど · Usage (cont.)

Sentence-initially, then, `だけど` is always available. Mid-sentence it is only available after a noun or な-adjective, and there it is really two words wearing one coat.

One more very common shape: `～んだけど`, which prefaces a request or a piece of news - ちょっと<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きたいことがあるんだけど… - where the trailing `だけど` softens the approach rather than contradicting anything.

---

## だけど · Examples

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い。**だけど**、<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>は<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らないと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う。<br>*It's cold today. But I don't think it'll snow.*

「もう<ruby>一杯<rt>いっぱい</rt></ruby>どう?」「**だけど**、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いんだよね。」<br>*"Another drink?" "But I've got an early start tomorrow."*

<ruby>三年<rt>さんねん</rt></ruby><ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>った。**だけど**、まだ<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すのはこわい。<br>*I studied it for three years. But phone calls still terrify me.*

<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>いし、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>からも<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>い。**だけど**、この<ruby>家賃<rt>やちん</rt></ruby>なら<ruby>文句<rt>もんく</rt></ruby>は<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>えない。<br>*The flat is cramped and far from the station. But at this rent I can't complain.*

---

## だけど · Nuance & register

Casual. `だけど` belongs to conversation with friends and family, to messages, and to relaxed writing such as blog posts. In a business email, an essay or anything said to a customer, use `ですが` or `しかし` instead. Roughly, the ladder runs `しかし` (formal, written) - `ですが` (polite) - `でも` (neutral casual) - `だけど` (casual) - `けど` (very casual, often trailing off).

Against `でも`, `だけど` tends to be the firmer of the two. `でも` can register a mild reservation; `だけど` picks up the previous statement and argues with it, so opening several sentences in a row with it comes across as combative.

`だけれど` and `だけれども` are the unshortened forms: a shade more careful and slightly bookish, and they lean older in speech. `だけどさ`, `だけどね` do the opposite, softening the pushback with a final particle. None of these are marked for gender.

---

## だけど · Nuance & register (cont.)

Note also that `だけど` is not a subordinating conjunction. It cannot join two clauses into one sentence the way `が` or `けど` do; it needs a full stop in front of it, or at least the pause that a full stop would represent.

---

## だけど · だけど vs でも

> **Rule of thumb** - same slot, same register, different force. `でも` raises a qualification; `だけど` contradicts.

「これ、おいしいね。」「**でも**、ちょっと<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>すぎない?」<br>*"This is good." "Though - isn't it a bit too sweet?"*

「これ、おいしいね。」「**だけど**、<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>た?」<br>*"This is good." "But did you see the price?"*

Both are natural; the second sounds more like the start of an argument. When you are not sure which you want, `でも` is the safer default.

---

## だけど · だけど vs けど

> **Rule of thumb** - `だけど` begins a sentence; `けど` ends a clause. They are not interchangeable positions.

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>はある**けど**、お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>がない。<br>*I have the time, but not the money.*

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>はある。**だけど**、お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>がない。<br>*I have the time. But I don't have the money.*

---

## だけど · だけど vs けど (cont.)

The content is identical; the second version breaks it into two beats and lands harder. Sentence-initial `けど` alone does exist in very casual speech - 「けど、<ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>でしょ」 - but `だけど` is the standard choice there.

---

## だけど · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がないだけど、<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。 → ✅ <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がない**けど**、<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*A verb or い-adjective cannot be followed by `だ`. Only nouns and な-adjectives can carry the `だ` that makes `だけど`.*

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かですだけど → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かです**けど**<br>*`です` is already the copula; adding `だ` on top of it doubles up. In polite speech the clause-final connector is `ですけど` or `ですが`.*

- ❌（<ruby>取引先<rt>とりひきさき</rt></ruby>へのメールで）だけど、<ruby>納期<rt>のうき</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>になります。 → ✅ **しかし**、<ruby>納期<rt>のうき</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>になります。<br>*`だけど` in business writing reads as though you forgot who you were talking to. `しかし` or `ですが` carries the same meaning at the right register.*

---

## だけど · Practice 1

**1.** 「<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>のパーティー、<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る?」「<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい。____、バイトなんだ。」

{reveal}
「<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい。**だけど**、バイトなんだ。」

{reveal}
The speaker has finished one sentence and starts another that contradicts it, so the connector has to be the sentence-initial kind. `でも` would also fit here and would sound a little softer.

---

## だけど · Practice 2

**2.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いだけど、けっきょく<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うことにした。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**けど**、けっきょく<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うことにした。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い</code> is an い-adjective, and い-adjectives are never followed by `だ`. Splitting it into two sentences - <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い。だけど、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うことにした。 - works too.

---

## だけど · Practice 3

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) **だけど**、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はそうは<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>わない。 (b) **ですが**、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はそうは<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いません。

{reveal}
The grammar is the same and the meaning is the same: both reject what was just said. The difference is entirely register. Sentence (a) is what you say to a friend over coffee, while (b) is what you say in a meeting or write in an essay. Using (a) in a formal discussion would sound abrupt and a little rude, and using (b) with a close friend would sound stiff and distant.

# ところで

**Meaning** · by the way - putting the current topic down and picking up a new one

`ところで` is a signal, not a link. It tells the listener that what follows has nothing to do with what came before, and that the speaker is doing it on purpose. Nine times out of ten, what follows is a question the speaker has been sitting on.

---

## ところで · Usage

`ところで` goes at the **head of a sentence**, followed by a comma or a pause. It does not attach to a word, does not conjugate, and never appears in the middle of a clause.

- ところで、<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>はどうなりましたか。
- ところで、あの<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>、<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えてる?

---

## ところで · Usage (cont.)

Two things it is *not*. It is not "however" - it contradicts nothing, so ❌ところで、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>反対<rt>はんたい</rt></ruby>です is wrong where `しかし` was wanted. And it is not the `ところ` family of grammar points: the `～たところで` of <ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>いだところで<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない ("even if you hurry…") and the `～ているところ` of <ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べているところです ("in the middle of eating") are separate constructions that merely look alike. The `ところで` of this page always begins a sentence and never follows a verb.

Because the whole point of the word is to close one subject and open another, it usually appears once in a conversation, at a natural seam - after a topic has run its course, not on top of someone else who is still talking.

---

## ところで · Examples

**ところで**、<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>?<br>*By the way, how's Yamada doing?*

**ところで**、<ruby>昼<rt>ひる</rt></ruby>ごはんはもう<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べましたか。<br>*By the way, have you had lunch yet?*

<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>以上<rt>いじょう</rt></ruby>です。**ところで**、<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>からでしたか。<br>*That's everything in the handouts. By the way, what time was next week's meeting again?*

<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>、ありがとう。すごくよく<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>れてるね。**ところで**、あのカメラっていくらぐらいしたの?<br>*Thanks for the photos - they came out really well. By the way, roughly how much was that camera?*

---

## ところで · Nuance & register

`ところで` is register-neutral in itself: it works in ます-form speech and in plain speech, and it appears in writing wherever a writer changes tack. What is not neutral is the act it performs. Announcing a change of subject is a small claim on the floor, so it lands most comfortably when you have the standing to make it - among equals, or from whoever is hosting the conversation.

Speaking to a customer or someone well above you, the same move is usually padded: <ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わりますが、… or それはそうと、… or simply <ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つお<ruby>伺<rt>うかが</rt></ruby>いしてもよろしいでしょうか. `ところで` itself is not rude - it is used in business meetings constantly - but it is brisk, and dropped straight after someone has said something emotionally weighty it can sound as though you were not listening.

There is no casual contraction of it, and it is not marked for age or gender.

---

## ところで · ところで vs ちなみに

> **Rule of thumb** - `ところで` drops the current topic. `ちなみに` stays on it and adds a footnote.

このカフェ、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くておいしいよ。**ちなみに**、<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>は<ruby>十時<rt>じゅうじ</rt></ruby>までやってる。<br>*This café is cheap and good. Incidentally, it's open until ten at night.*

このカフェ、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くておいしいよ。**ところで**、<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>しの<ruby>準備<rt>じゅんび</rt></ruby>は<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>んでる?<br>*This café is cheap and good. Anyway - how's the packing for your move coming along?*

Swapping them breaks both sentences: the opening hours are plainly still about the café, and the packing plainly is not. A quick test - if the new sentence would also work with そういえば in front of it, `ところで` is the right choice.

---

## ところで · Common mistakes

- ❌ ところで、この<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>には<ruby>賛成<rt>さんせい</rt></ruby>できません。 → ✅ **しかし**、この<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>には<ruby>賛成<rt>さんせい</rt></ruby>できません。<br>*`ところで` changes the subject; it does not disagree. Learners reach for it because it looks like a conjunction, but it carries no adversative meaning at all.*

- ❌ <ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たところで、<ruby>晩<rt>ばん</rt></ruby>ごはんは<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>にする?（meaning "by the way"）→ ✅ <ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>、<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>かったね。**ところで**、<ruby>晩<rt>ばん</rt></ruby>ごはんは<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>にする?<br>*Placed after a verb, the same syllables become the pattern meaning "even if", and the sentence turns into "even if I watch the film…". Keep it at the front.*

- ❌ ところでですが、… → ✅ ところで、…<br>*`ところで` already contains everything it needs, and `ですが` does not politen it. If you want a politer version, replace the whole word with <ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わりますが.*

---

## ところで · Practice 1

**1.** ____、<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんの<ruby>連絡先<rt>れんらくさき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っていますか。

{reveal}
**ところで**、<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんの<ruby>連絡先<rt>れんらくさき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っていますか。

{reveal}
A question with no connection to the previous topic needs a marker warning the listener to switch tracks, and that is exactly the job this word does.

---

## ところで · Practice 2

**2.** Choose the natural connector: <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>会場<rt>かいじょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>市民<rt>しみん</rt></ruby>ホールです。（ところで・ちなみに）、<ruby>駐車場<rt>ちゅうしゃじょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>有料<rt>ゆうりょう</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
**ちなみに**、<ruby>駐車場<rt>ちゅうしゃじょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>有料<rt>ゆうりょう</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
The parking charge is a further detail about the same venue, so the topic has not changed at all. `ところで` would promise the listener something unrelated and then fail to deliver it.

---

## ところで · Practice 3

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) ところで、<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、そろそろ<ruby>失礼<rt>しつれい</rt></ruby>してもよろしいでしょうか。 (b) <ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わりますが、<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、そろそろ<ruby>失礼<rt>しつれい</rt></ruby>してもよろしいでしょうか。

{reveal}
Both change the subject and both are grammatical. Sentence (a) is brisk: the speaker announces the switch in one word and moves on, which is fine between colleagues but a little bald when addressing a superior. Sentence (b) spends a few extra syllables acknowledging that a switch is being made, and that small detour is what makes it sound considerate.

# つまり

**Meaning** · in other words; which means - restating something more plainly, or spelling out what it amounts to

`つまり` announces that the same content is coming round again in a clearer shape. It does two jobs with one word: rewording something complicated, and stating the consequence nobody has said out loud yet.

---

## つまり · Usage

`つまり` is an adverb and sits at the front of the clause it introduces. Nothing attaches to it, and it never changes form.

The habit worth building is what comes at the **end** of that clause. A `つまり` sentence very often closes with `ということだ`, `わけだ` or `のだ`, because you are labelling a conclusion rather than reporting a fresh fact:

- つまり、<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られないということですね。
- <ruby>三年<rt>さんねん</rt></ruby><ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>んでいた。つまり、<ruby>地元<rt>じもと</rt></ruby>のようなものだ。

The two jobs, one line each:

---

## つまり · Usage (cont.)

- **Rewording** - the second half says the same thing in easier words.<br>*<ruby>検討<rt>けんとう</rt></ruby>します。つまり、<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>は<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>められないということです。*
- **Consequence** - the second half is what the first one comes to.<br>*<ruby>終電<rt>しゅうでん</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>た。つまり、<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いて<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>るしかない。*

As a standalone check on your understanding, `つまり` with a rising question is extremely common: 「つまり、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みってこと?」

---

## つまり · Examples

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>の<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>、**つまり**おばです。<br>*My father's older sister - my aunt, in other words.*

<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>がない。**つまり**、<ruby>断<rt>ことわ</rt></ruby>られたということだ。<br>*There's been no reply. Which means I've been turned down.*

<ruby>材料費<rt>ざいりょうひ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>送料<rt>そうりょう</rt></ruby>も<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がっている。**つまり**、<ruby>値上<rt>ねあ</rt></ruby>げは<ruby>避<rt>さ</rt></ruby>けられないわけです。<br>*Materials and shipping have both gone up. In other words, a price rise can't be avoided.*

「<ruby>前向<rt>まえむ</rt></ruby>きに<ruby>検討<rt>けんとう</rt></ruby>します」って<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われたんだけど、**つまり**<ruby>断<rt>ことわ</rt></ruby>られたってこと?<br>*They said they'd "consider it positively" - so does that mean I've been refused?*

---

## つまり · Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very frequent. It is at home in a lecture, a contract summary, a news broadcast and a conversation between friends, with no change of form.

In speech it has a second life as a filler. Some speakers open every third sentence with つまり、つまりですね… while they assemble a thought, and the word then carries no summarising meaning at all. Worth recognising, not worth imitating.

One genuine risk: aiming `つまり` at what *somebody else* just said can sound like a correction - "so what you actually meant was…". A softener fixes it: つまり、こういうことでしょうか. Aimed at your own words it is completely safe, and in a presentation it is a useful signpost that the summary has arrived.

---

## つまり · Nuance & register (cont.)

`つまり` is not a cause-and-effect connector. `だから` and したがって say "therefore, here is a new fact"; `つまり` says "the same fact, re-expressed". When the second sentence contains information the first one did not, you want `だから`.

---

## つまり · つまり vs 要するに

> **Rule of thumb** - `つまり` re-expresses; <code><ruby>要<rt>よう</rt></ruby>するに</code> compresses. The second one claims the right to decide what the point was.

<ruby>会場<rt>かいじょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わりました。**つまり**、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>南口<rt>みなみぐち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>集合<rt>しゅうごう</rt></ruby>です。<br>*The venue has changed - that is, we meet at the south exit of the station.*

<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>かったが、**<ruby>要<rt>よう</rt></ruby>するに**<ruby>予算<rt>よさん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りないのだ。<br>*The explanation went on a while, but the gist is that there isn't enough budget.*

<code><ruby>要<rt>よう</rt></ruby>するに</code> implies the original was long-winded, so pointing it at a superior's remarks is risky. `つまり` carries no such judgement.

---

## つまり · つまり vs すなわち

> **Rule of thumb** - same job, different clothes. すなわち is the written, formal register: it puts an equals sign between two terms.

<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>首都<rt>しゅと</rt></ruby>、**すなわち**<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>本社<rt>ほんしゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>いている。<br>*Our head office is in the capital of Japan, namely Tokyo.*

In conversation すなわち sounds like a legal document being read aloud. Reach for `つまり` when you are speaking, and keep すなわち for formal writing, where it is usually left in kana.

---

## つまり · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っていた。つまり、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になった。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っていた。**だから**、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になった。<br>*The cancellation is a new event, not a restatement of the rain. `つまり` needs the second sentence to be the *same* content in another form.*

- ❌ つまり<ruby>三人<rt>さんにん</rt></ruby><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました。 → ✅ <ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>三人<rt>さんにん</rt></ruby><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*`つまり` cannot introduce a plain new fact out of nowhere. Something has to have been said already for it to restate.*

- ❌ <ruby>要<rt>よう</rt></ruby>するに、<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>のおっしゃりたいことは…（said to your own teacher）→ ✅ **つまり**、<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>のおっしゃりたいことは…<br>*Summarising a superior's words with <code><ruby>要<rt>よう</rt></ruby>するに</code> hints that they rambled. `つまり` is the neutral choice whenever the words being restated are not your own.*

---

# たとえば

**Meaning** · for example; say - pulling one concrete case out of a general statement

`たとえば` warns the listener that what follows is an illustration, not a new claim. The example is offered as one of several possibilities, which is why it so often arrives with `とか` or `など` trailing behind it.

---

## たとえば · Usage

`たとえば` normally stands at the head of the sentence, but it can also sit directly in front of the noun phrase it is introducing:

- <ruby>果物<rt>くだもの</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。たとえば、みかんとかぶどうとか。
- <ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>くの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>、たとえばコンビニでも<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えます。

Three shapes carry most of its use:

---

## たとえば · Usage (cont.)

- **たとえば～とか／など** - a list left deliberately open.<br>*たとえば<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>のアプリなど、<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>えるものがいいです。*
- **たとえば～のような／みたいな + noun** - the example modifies a noun.<br>*たとえば<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>のような<ruby>古<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>い<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>*
- **たとえば～なら** - set up a hypothetical case and then say what happens in it.<br>*たとえば<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>なら、<ruby>中<rt>なか</rt></ruby>でやりましょう。*

In kanji it is <ruby>例<rt>たと</rt></ruby>えば, and both spellings are completely normal: printed prose and official documents tend to use the kanji, casual writing the kana. The reading and the meaning are identical either way.

---

## たとえば · Usage (cont.)

Do not confuse it with `たとえ`, which is a different word entirely - see the contrast below.

---

## たとえば · Examples

<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いものが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。**たとえば**、チョコレートやアイスです。<br>*I like sweet things - chocolate and ice cream, for example.*

**たとえば**、この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つあります。<br>*This kanji, for instance, has three different readings.*

<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みの<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>にできること、**たとえば**<ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>とか<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>とかを<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>やしたい。<br>*I want more things I can do on a day off - walks, cooking, that sort of thing.*

**たとえば**<ruby>地震<rt>じしん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きたら、まず<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>下<rt>した</rt></ruby>に<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>ってください。<br>*Say an earthquake happens - the first thing to do is get under a desk.*

---

## たとえば · Nuance & register

Register-neutral. Nothing about `たとえば` marks formality, age or gender, and it appears unchanged in academic writing and in chat with friends.

Two conversational uses are worth memorising whole. 「たとえばの<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>だけど」 prefaces a hypothetical you are not committing to - "just supposing, though". And 「たとえば?」 said alone is a request for one: "such as?" That one-word question is enormously useful and very common.

In writing, a `たとえば` sentence is usually short and ends with `など`, `といった` or a plain noun phrase, and the paragraph then returns to the general point. If you find yourself giving three sentences of example, consider <ruby>具体<rt>ぐたい</rt></ruby><ruby>的<rt>てき</rt></ruby>には ("specifically") instead, which promises a fuller account.

---

## たとえば · Nuance & register (cont.)

`たとえば` presents one member of a set. When you want to say that your example is the *representative* one - the first of many, the flagship - Japanese has `をはじめ` for that, and it is not interchangeable.

---

## たとえば · たとえば vs たとえ○○ても

> **Rule of thumb** - `たとえば` introduces an example. `たとえ` introduces a concession, and it always has a `ても` waiting for it later in the sentence.

**たとえば**<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったら、どうしますか。<br>*If it rains tomorrow, say, what will you do?*

**たとえ**<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>っ**ても**、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>はやります。<br>*Even if it rains tomorrow, the match is going ahead.*

The first sentence asks about one possible case among many; the second brushes the case aside. The `ば` is the whole difference in writing, and in speech the giveaway is the `ても` at the far end: if you hear one, the word at the front was `たとえ`.

---

## たとえば · Common mistakes

- ❌ たとえ<ruby>日本料理<rt>にほんりょうり</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ **たとえば**<ruby>日本料理<rt>にほんりょうり</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*Without the `ば` the word means "even if", and the sentence is left dangling with nothing for it to concede to.*

- ❌ たとえば、<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人口<rt>じんこう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>約<rt>やく</rt></ruby><ruby>一億<rt>いちおく</rt></ruby><ruby>二千万人<rt>にせんまんにん</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人口<rt>じんこう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>約<rt>やく</rt></ruby><ruby>一億<rt>いちおく</rt></ruby><ruby>二千万人<rt>にせんまんにん</rt></ruby>です。<br>*A single hard fact is not an example of anything. `たとえば` needs a general statement in front of it for the example to illustrate.*

- ❌ <ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>、たとえばにんじんをはじめ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。 → ✅ <ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>、**たとえば**にんじんなどを<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*`たとえば` pairs with `など`, `とか` and `といった`. `をはじめ` is a different pattern meaning "starting with", and the two cannot be stacked.*

---

## たとえば · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby><ruby>不足<rt>ぶそく</rt></ruby>なので、____<ruby>階段<rt>かいだん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>うようにしています。

{reveal}
**たとえば**<ruby>階段<rt>かいだん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>うようにしています。

{reveal}
The stairs are one concrete instance of the general problem stated first, which is what this word is for. Note that the example still needs the general statement in front of it to lean on.

{reveal}

{reveal}
The `ても` later in the sentence is the tell: this is a concession, "even if I fail", not an illustration. `たとえば` would leave the `ても` with no partner.

## たとえば · Practice 3

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かな<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>、たとえば<ruby>奈良<rt>なら</rt></ruby>のような<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>みたい。 (b) <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かな<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>、<ruby>奈良<rt>なら</rt></ruby>に<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>みたい。

Sentence (b) names Nara as the actual destination and describes it as quiet along the way. The example frame is what keeps the choice open.

# 確かに

**Meaning** · certainly; admittedly; you're right - vouching for something, or granting a point before you argue with it

<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに has two lives. It can mean "definitely, without doubt", said of something you are sure of. Far more often at this level it means "granted" - you concede what the other person said, and then the `が` or `けど` arrives.

---

## 確かに · Usage

<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに is an adverb: it goes in front of the predicate, or at the head of the sentence, and never changes shape. Three uses:

- **Conceding** - the big one. <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに～が／けど、～. The first half agrees,<br>*the second half pushes back. <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いが、<ruby>質<rt>しつ</rt></ruby>はいい。*
- **Vouching** - "for certain", of an event you witnessed or a fact you can stand<br>*behind. <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>渡<rt>わた</rt></ruby>しました。*
- **Agreeing, on its own** - 「<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに。」 is a complete turn in<br>*conversation, meaning "true" or "fair point". This is very common in modern casual speech.*

---

## 確かに · Usage (cont.)

The single most useful thing to remember is the concessive frame. Whenever an essay or a discussion needs "it's true that…, but…", <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに at the front and `が` in the middle is the standard shape, and a listener hears the `が` coming the moment you say the first word.

Kanji <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに and kana たしかに are both normal; the kanji is the default in print.

---

## 確かに · Examples

「この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いね。」「**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに**。」<br>*"This place is cheap." "It is, isn't it."*

**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに**<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いけど、<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>は<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らないよ。<br>*It's cold, admittedly, but it isn't going to snow.*

<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに**お<ruby>預<rt>あず</rt></ruby>かりしました。<br>*I have received the documents - they are safely with me.*

**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに**<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりやすかったが、<ruby>肝心<rt>かんじん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>費用<rt>ひよう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>抜<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>けていた。<br>*His explanation was certainly easy to follow, but it left out the crucial question of cost.*

---

## 確かに · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and equally at home in an academic paper and in a text message. The concessive frame <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに～が is a staple of written argument; the one-word 「<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに。」 reply is casual but not slangy, and works in the office as well as with friends.

The vouching use has a slightly formal flavour, because it is what you say when responsibility is at stake: <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>承<rt>うけたまわ</rt></ruby>りました, "I have definitely taken note of that", is standard business phrasing. In everyday talk about the future, きっと is the natural word instead.

---

## 確かに · Nuance & register (cont.)

Be aware of what conceding does to the shape of a conversation. Opening with <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに sets up an expectation that a `が` is coming; if you concede and then stop, the listener will wait for the rest of the sentence. That is often exactly the effect you want when disagreeing politely - the agreement takes the sting out of what follows - but it does mean <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに is rarely the whole of your position.

---

## 確かに · 確かに vs 確か

> **Rule of thumb** - one `に` reverses the meaning. <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに is confidence; <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>か is a memory you are not sure of.

**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>か**<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>からだったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*The meeting was at three, if I remember rightly.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに**<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まりました。<br>*The meeting definitely started at three.*

---

## 確かに · 確かに vs 確か (cont.)

<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>か is an adverb of *hedged recollection* and normally comes with と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う, はず or だったよね. <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに asserts. Mixing them up tells your listener the opposite of what you meant, which is why this pair is worth drilling.

---

## 確かに · 確かに vs きっと

> **Rule of thumb** - <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに is about what is already established. きっと is a confident guess about what is not.

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は**きっと**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます。<br>*She'll definitely come.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*She did come - I can vouch for it.*

きっと looks forward and is a prediction, however confident; <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに looks back at evidence. Saying ❌<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます about a future event sounds like you are promising on someone else's behalf.

---

## 確かに · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>でしょう。 → ✅ **きっと**<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>でしょう。<br>*A forecast is a guess, not something you can vouch for. <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに needs evidence already in hand.*

- ❌ <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんの<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>だったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。 → ✅ **<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>か**<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんの<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>だったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います signals that you are not sure, so the hedging <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>か is the form that fits. With `に` the sentence contradicts itself.*

- ❌ <ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かにです。 → ✅ **<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに**そうですね。<br>*<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かに is an adverb and cannot take `です` directly. To agree politely, give it something to modify.*

---

The `が` in the middle is the giveaway: the writer grants the drawback first and then argues past it, which is the standard concessive frame this word builds.

Ending there without the "but" makes the agreement sound reluctant.

# おそらく

**Meaning** · in all likelihood; probably - a considered estimate, stated carefully

`おそらく` is the measured, slightly formal cousin of たぶん. It says the speaker has weighed what they know and arrived at a probable answer. The weighing is the point: `おそらく` sounds like a judgement, not a shrug.

---

## おそらく · Usage

`おそらく` is an adverb and stands at the head of the sentence or immediately before the predicate. It never conjugates and takes no particles.

What matters is the **end of the sentence**. `おそらく` almost always pairs with a marker of conjecture:

- **おそらく～だろう／でしょう** - the default.<br>*おそらく<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>には<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>くでしょう。*
- **おそらく～と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う／と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>われる** - reasoning out loud, or in<br>*writing. おそらく<ruby>原因<rt>げんいん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>配線<rt>はいせん</rt></ruby>だと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>われます。*
- **おそらく～はずだ／に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない** - when the estimate is close to a<br>*conclusion. おそらく<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>いているはずだ。*

---

## おそらく · Usage (cont.)

Ending a plain assertion with nothing at all - ❌おそらく<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る - sounds unfinished to a native ear. The hedge at the end is not decoration; it is what `おそらく` is agreeing with.

In kanji it is <ruby>恐<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>らく. The kanji spelling is common in print; the kana is common in ordinary writing. It has nothing to do with fear despite the character.

---

## おそらく · Examples

**おそらく**<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っているでしょう。<br>*He probably already knows.*

この<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>なら、**おそらく**<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>空<rt>す</rt></ruby>いているだろう。<br>*At this hour the roads are most likely clear.*

<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>がないところを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ると、**おそらく**<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいのだろう。<br>*Judging by the lack of a reply, he's probably swamped.*

<ruby>今回<rt>こんかい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>不具合<rt>ふぐあい</rt></ruby>は、**おそらく**<ruby>先月<rt>せんげつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>更新<rt>こうしん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>原因<rt>げんいん</rt></ruby>だと<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えられます。<br>*This fault is in all likelihood caused by last month's update.*

---

## おそらく · Nuance & register

`おそらく` leans formal. It is the natural choice in a report, a news article, a presentation or a careful explanation to a client, and it sounds a little stiff between friends deciding where to eat, where たぶん is what people actually say.

It also leans toward **inference about circumstances** rather than announcements about your own plans. たぶん<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます ("I'll probably go") is ordinary; おそらく<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます sounds oddly detached, as though you were forecasting the behaviour of a stranger who happens to be you. Reserve `おそらく` for things outside your control.

On the confidence scale, `おそらく` sits high - above もしかしたら, roughly level with or a shade above たぶん - but it is still a guess. If you know, say so plainly; hedging a fact with `おそらく` reads as evasive, and in business that impression is hard to shake off.

---

## おそらく · おそらく vs たぶん

> **Rule of thumb** - same probability, different room. `たぶん` is spoken and casual; `おそらく` is written and considered.

**たぶん**<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>うよ。<br>*We'll probably make it, I reckon.*

**おそらく**<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うものと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>われます。<br>*We are likely to arrive in time.*

`たぶん` can be tossed off with no evidence at all; `おそらく` implies you looked at something first. In casual conversation `おそらく` is not wrong, merely bookish.

---

## おそらく · おそらく vs きっと

> **Rule of thumb** - `おそらく` estimates. `きっと` believes.

**きっと**<ruby>喜<rt>よろこ</rt></ruby>んでくれるよ。<br>*She's going to love it.*

**おそらく**<ruby>喜<rt>よろこ</rt></ruby>んでくれるだろう。<br>*She will probably be pleased.*

`きっと` carries the speaker's feeling - hope, conviction, encouragement - and is what you say when reassuring someone. `おそらく` carries none of that warmth, which makes it excellent for analysis and poor for comfort.

---

## おそらく · Common mistakes

- ❌ おそらく<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ります。 → ✅ おそらく<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る**でしょう**。<br>*A flat ます-ending states a fact, which contradicts the word at the front. `おそらく` needs `でしょう`, `だろう` or と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います to lean on.*

- ❌ おそらく<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>りました。 → ✅ **たぶん**<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>ったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*You do not estimate your own past actions from the outside. `おそらく` is for inference about things you did not do.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>のパーティー、おそらく<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くね。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>のパーティー、**たぶん**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くね。<br>*Announcing your own plans to a friend calls for the casual word. `おそらく` with the final particle `ね` mixes two registers in one short sentence.*

---

The speaker reasons from visible evidence - the lights are off - to a probable conclusion, and the `だろう` at the end is the partner this adverb expects.

# もしかしたら

**Meaning** · maybe; there's a chance that - raising a possibility the speaker has just thought of

`もしかしたら` opens the door to something that has not been considered yet, often something slightly unexpected or unwelcome. It is a low-confidence word: the speaker is not predicting, only pointing at a possibility.

---

## もしかしたら · Usage

`もしかしたら` sits at the head of the sentence. Like every adverb of conjecture, its real lesson is the **partner at the other end of the sentence**:

- **もしかしたら～かもしれない／かも** - the default pairing, by a wide margin.<br>*もしかしたら<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みかもしれない。*
- **もしかしたら～のではないか** - the same idea in careful or written style.<br>*もしかしたら<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か<ruby>誤解<rt>ごかい</rt></ruby>があるのではないでしょうか。*
- **もしかしたら～かな／かしら** - thinking aloud.<br>*もしかしたら<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>迷<rt>まよ</rt></ruby>ったのかな。*

---

## もしかしたら · Usage (cont.)

Leaving the sentence to end flatly - ❌もしかしたら<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ります - is the single most common error. The adverb only sets up the possibility; something at the end has to keep it open.

Three variants mean essentially the same thing and are interchangeable in most sentences: もしかすると (a shade more formal), ひょっとしたら and ひょっとすると (more colloquial, slightly more surprised). `もしかして` looks like a fourth but behaves differently - see the contrast below.

---

## もしかしたら · Examples

**もしかしたら**<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>かもしれない。<br>*There's a chance it'll snow tomorrow.*

**もしかしたら**、<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れてきたかも。<br>*I might have left my keys at the office.*

<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>もないので、**もしかしたら**<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>にちを<ruby>間違<rt>まちが</rt></ruby>えているのではないでしょうか。<br>*There's been no word at all, so I wonder if she hasn't got the date wrong.*

**もしかしたら**<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>、<ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>転勤<rt>てんきん</rt></ruby>になるかもしれないと<ruby>上司<rt>じょうし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>われた。<br>*My boss told me there's a chance I'll be transferred to Osaka next year.*

---

## もしかしたら · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and usable in ます-form or plain form without changing shape. In careful business speech it usually comes with the fuller ending のではないでしょうか rather than `かも`, which is where the register is actually carried.

The word has a distinctly tentative colour. Because it presents an idea as one that has only just occurred to the speaker, it is a soft way to raise something uncomfortable: もしかしたら<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>勘違<rt>かんちが</rt></ruby>いかもしれませんが… lets you question someone's claim while taking the blame for the doubt yourself. That politeness trick is worth stealing.

---

## もしかしたら · Nuance & register (cont.)

Contracted `もしかしたら～かも` is very common in speech and messages, and the whole sentence is often left trailing: 「もしかしたら<ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>かも…」 The trailing-off is part of the effect - you have floated the bad news without stating it.

On the confidence ladder, `もしかしたら` sits at the bottom: below たぶん and well below おそらく. If you are more than about half sure, it undersells you.

---

## もしかしたら · もしかしたら vs もしかして

> **Rule of thumb** - `もしかしたら` floats a possibility to the world and ends in `かもしれない`. `もしかして` aims a guess at the listener and ends in a question.

**もしかして**、<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんですか。<br>*Excuse me - are you Tanaka, by any chance?*

**もしかしたら**<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんも<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るかもしれません。<br>*There's a chance Tanaka will come too.*

---

## もしかしたら · もしかしたら vs もしかして (cont.)

`もしかして` is what you say when you suspect something about the person in front of you - that they are the one you were told to meet, that they are angry, that they have not eaten. It expects an answer. Using `もしかしたら` there （❌もしかしたら<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんですか）sounds like you are guessing at a stranger rather than asking one.

---

## もしかしたら · Common mistakes

- ❌ もしかしたら<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます。 → ✅ もしかしたら<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**かもしれません**。<br>*A bare ます-ending asserts, which cancels the doubt the adverb just raised. The possibility has to stay open at the end of the sentence.*

- ❌ もしかしたら<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>がありますか。 → ✅ **もしかして**<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>がありますか。<br>*A direct question to the listener takes `もしかして`. `もしかしたら` is for possibilities you are laying out, not for questions you are putting.*

- ❌ もしかしたら<ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby><ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる。 → ✅ **きっと**<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる。<br>*You cannot hedge and insist in the same breath. If you are sure, drop the adverb of possibility entirely.*

---

## もしかしたら · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>がついているから、____<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>か<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>っている____。

{reveal}
Both blanks belong to one frame. The adverb opens the possibility and `かもしれない` keeps it open; filling only one of them leaves the sentence lopsided.

---

## もしかしたら · Practice 2

---

## もしかしたら · Practice 3

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) もしかしたら<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わないかもしれません。 (b) たぶん<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わないと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。

# まるで

**Meaning** · just like; as if - flagging a comparison that everyone knows is not literally true

`まるで` is the word that turns a plain statement into a simile. It tells the listener in advance: what I am about to say is an exaggeration, take it as a picture rather than a fact.

---

## まるで · Usage

`まるで` is an adverb and normally opens the clause. The lesson of this page is the ending, because `まるで` cannot stand alone - it must be answered by a likeness-marker:

- **まるで～ようだ／ような／ように** - the standard, and the one to learn first.<br>*まるで<ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>のようだ。*
- **まるで～みたいだ／みたいな／みたいに** - the same thing in casual speech.<br>*まるで<ruby>子供<rt>こども</rt></ruby>みたいに<ruby>喜<rt>よろこ</rt></ruby>んでいた。*
- **まるで～かのようだ** - literary and a shade more emphatic; common in writing.<br>*まるで<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>もなかったかのように<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>けた。*

---

## まるで · Usage (cont.)

A sentence like ❌まるで<ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>だ is broken. Whatever comes after `まるで` has to be marked as a comparison; without that, you have asserted the impossible thing instead of comparing to it.

There is a second, quite different use. With a **negative** predicate, `まるで` means "not at all": まるで<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>にならない, まるで<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えていない. Here it is an intensifier of the negation, and no `ようだ` appears. This use is emphatic and a little literary; ぜんぜん and まったく are the everyday equivalents.

---

## まるで · Examples

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は**まるで**<ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>のように<ruby>暖<rt>あたた</rt></ruby>かい。<br>*It's as warm as spring today.*

その<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>は**まるで**ぬいぐるみみたいだった。<br>*That dog looked just like a stuffed toy.*

<ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>ぶりに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たのに、**まるで**<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>のことのように<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えている。<br>*I hadn't been back in a year, yet I remember it as though it were yesterday.*

<ruby>質問<rt>しつもん</rt></ruby>には<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つも<ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>えず、**まるで**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえていないかのように<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>の<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ていた。<br>*He answered not one question and stared out of the window as if he could not hear us.*

---

## まるで · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and common in speech, novels and journalism alike. The choice of ending carries the register instead: `みたい` is casual, `ようだ` is neutral, `かのようだ` is written and rather literary.

`まるで` is an exaggeration marker, so the listener understands the comparison to be false. That makes it a good tool for complaint and for praise, and a bad one for description. If the resemblance is real - a child who genuinely looks like her mother - the natural word is そっくり: <ruby>母親<rt>ははおや</rt></ruby>にそっくりだ. Saying まるで<ruby>母親<rt>ははおや</rt></ruby>のようだ suggests the likeness is uncanny rather than simply present.

Also note that `まるで` is optional. <ruby>夢<rt>ゆめ</rt></ruby>のようだ is a complete and natural sentence; adding `まるで` in front of it only turns the volume up. Overusing it in writing quickly sounds breathless.

---

## まるで · Nuance & register (cont.)

The negative use is a different register again. まるで<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からない is emphatic, slightly old-fashioned in flavour, and more common in writing and in grumbling than in neutral conversation.

---

## まるで · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はまるで<ruby>王様<rt>おうさま</rt></ruby>だ。 → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はまるで<ruby>王様<rt>おうさま</rt></ruby>**のようだ**。<br>*Without a likeness-marker the sentence claims he *is* a king. `まるで` sets up a comparison and needs `ようだ`, `みたいだ` or `かのようだ` to complete it.*

- ❌ <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>はまるで<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>のようです。（meaning they really do look alike）→ ✅ <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>は<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>に**そっくり**です。<br>*`まるで` says the likeness is a figure of speech. For an actual family resemblance it undercuts what you meant.*

- ❌ まるで<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めます。 → ✅ まるで<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>め**ません**。<br>*In its intensifier use, `まるで` attaches only to a negative. There is no affirmative version of that sense.*

---

## まるで · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>にぬれて、____ぬれた<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>____<ruby>姿<rt>すがた</rt></ruby>だった。

{reveal}
<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>にぬれて、**まるで**ぬれた<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>**のような**<ruby>姿<rt>すがた</rt></ruby>だった。

{reveal}
The two blanks are one frame: the adverb opens the comparison and `のような` attaches it to the noun. Because a noun follows, `のように` would be wrong here.

---

## まるで · Practice 2

**2.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>はまるでホテルだ。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>はまるでホテル**のようだ**。

{reveal}
As written, the sentence says her room *is* a hotel. Adding `のようだ` - or `みたいだ` in casual speech - turns it back into the comparison the speaker intended.

---

## まるで · Practice 3

**3.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>はまるで<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からなかった。 (b) <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>はあまり<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からなかった。

# なるべく

**Meaning** · as far as possible; if you can - a preference stated without making it an order

`なるべく` softens whatever follows it into an aim rather than a requirement. Put it in front of a request and the request becomes a wish; put it in front of your own plan and the plan becomes an intention you will try to keep.

---

## なるべく · Usage

`なるべく` is an adverb. It goes immediately in front of the part it qualifies - usually a verb, often an adverb of degree such as <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く or <ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>く - and it does not change form.

Three places it lives:

---

## なるべく · Usage (cont.)

- **Requests.** なるべく<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めにお<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>をください。 The deadline is<br>*still a deadline, but you have signalled that you understand life happens.*
- **Negative requests.** なるべく<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>てないでください。 Very common<br>*on notices, where an outright prohibition would be too strong.*
- **Resolutions and habits.** なるべく<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>くようにしています。 Here it pairs<br>*naturally with `ようにする`, which is itself a "make an effort to" pattern.*

---

## なるべく · Usage (cont.)

`なるべく` belongs with things that are still open - requests, intentions, advice. It sits badly on a finished fact: ❌なるべく<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました reports what you did, and there is nothing left to try for. Use できるだけ<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>いで <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました, or simply <ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>いで<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました.

In kanji it is <ruby>成<rt>な</rt></ruby>るべく, but the kana spelling is overwhelmingly the norm and the kanji looks archaic.

---

## なるべく · Examples

**なるべく**<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります。<br>*I'll be home as early as I can.*

<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>は**なるべく**コーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>まないようにしている。<br>*I try not to drink coffee at night if I can help it.*

<ruby>会場<rt>かいじょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>いので、**なるべく**<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>少<rt>すく</rt></ruby>なめにお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします。<br>*The venue is small, so please keep your luggage to a minimum if you can.*

<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぎませんが、**なるべく**<ruby>今週中<rt>こんしゅうちゅう</rt></ruby>にいただけると<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かります。<br>*There's no rush on the reply, but it would help if I could have it this week if at all possible.*

---

## なるべく · Nuance & register

`なるべく` is neutral to slightly informal, and works fine inside ます-form speech - なるべく<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>くお<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>りします is perfectly good business Japanese. It is common in notices and instructions, where its softness is the whole point.

The softness cuts both ways. Attach `なるべく` to something that genuinely must happen and you have accidentally made it optional: a hard deadline announced as なるべく<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までに will be read as "tomorrow would be nice". When the requirement is firm, drop the adverb, or use <ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ず.

---

## なるべく · Nuance & register (cont.)

Received rather than given, `なるべく` is a small courtesy: the speaker is telling you they know what they are asking may be inconvenient. Answering 「はい、なるべく<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く」 in return is a natural way to accept without promising a time.

---

## なるべく · なるべく vs できるだけ

> **Rule of thumb** - the same request, measured differently. `なるべく` asks for willingness; できるだけ asks for the maximum that is actually possible.

**なるべく**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにしてください。<br>*Please keep it down if you can.*

**できるだけ**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにしてください。<br>*Please be as quiet as you possibly can.*

---

## なるべく · なるべく vs できるだけ (cont.)

Both are polite and both are common; できるだけ is the firmer of the two because it names the limit of your ability and asks you to go to it. It is also the one that suits objective, written contexts - できるだけ<ruby>正確<rt>せいかく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>記入<rt>きにゅう</rt></ruby>して ください on a form. Stronger still, and distinctly formal, is できる<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>り. Where a difference is felt, the ordering runs `なるべく` - できるだけ - できる<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>り, but in most everyday sentences the first two are interchangeable.

---

# めったに

**Meaning** · hardly ever; rarely - and only ever with a negative verb

`めったに` says that something happens so seldom it is almost worth remarking on. The frequency it describes is very low - lower than あまり, close to ほとんど - and the word is grammatically incomplete without a negative to attach to.

---

## めったに · Usage

`めったに` is an adverb and goes in front of the predicate. The rule that matters:

> **Rule of thumb** - `めったに` **requires a negative predicate**. めったに～ない, めったに～ません. There is no affirmative version.

❌めったに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く and ❌めったに<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ます are not "rarely go" - they are not Japanese at all. The negative carries the frequency; `めったに` only tells you how far down the scale it goes.

- <ruby>外食<rt>がいしょく</rt></ruby>はめったにしません。
- <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はめったに<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>らない。
- <ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>はめったに<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りませんでした。 - past tense works normally.

---

## めったに · Usage (cont.)

One fixed expression is worth learning as a unit: `めったにない` used before a noun means "rare, exceptional" - めったにないチャンス, "a chance you don't get twice". The same negative rule is still doing the work; it has simply become a noun-modifier.

In kanji it is <ruby>滅多<rt>めった</rt></ruby>に, an ateji spelling that appears in print, but the kana is more usual.

---

## めったに · Examples

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は**めったに**<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ません。<br>*My father hardly ever watches films.*

この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>では**めったに**<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らない。<br>*It almost never snows around here.*

<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しくて、<ruby>平日<rt>へいじつ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>家族<rt>かぞく</rt></ruby>そろって<ruby>夕食<rt>ゆうしょく</rt></ruby>をとることは**めったに**ない。<br>*We're so busy that the whole family is hardly ever at dinner together on a weekday.*

あの<ruby>寡黙<rt>かもく</rt></ruby>な<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>が**めったに**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せない<ruby>笑顔<rt>えがお</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せた。<br>*Our famously silent department head produced a smile of a kind he almost never shows.*

---

## めったに · Nuance & register

Register-neutral: it appears in ます-form and plain form, in speech and in writing, with no change. It is not marked for age or gender.

What it does carry is a faint note of *comment*. Saying めったに<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない tends to imply that the rarity is notable - surprising, disappointing, or precious. When you simply want to report a low frequency with no colour at all, あまり～ない is flatter and safer.

Because the frequency is so low, `めったに` and a stated number sit badly together. <ruby>月<rt>つき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>一回<rt>いっかい</rt></ruby>めったに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない is contradictory: if you can count them, they are not "hardly ever". Use たまに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます for a countable occasional habit.

---

## めったに · Nuance & register (cont.)

Note finally that `めったに` describes frequency, not degree. It cannot modify an adjective: ❌めったに<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くない is wrong for "not very expensive"; that is あまり<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くない.

---

## めったに · めったに vs あまり○○ない

> **Rule of thumb** - both need a negative, but `あまり` measures **degree or moderate frequency**, and `めったに` measures **very low frequency only**.

お<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません。<br>*I don't drink much.*

お<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>は**めったに**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません。<br>*I almost never drink.*

---

## めったに · めったに vs あまり○○ない (cont.)

The first speaker probably has a beer now and then; the second is close to teetotal. And because `あまり` can also scale degree, it works with adjectives - あまり<ruby>辛<rt>から</rt></ruby>くない - where `めったに` cannot. The everyday positive counterpart of both is たまに, "occasionally", which takes an ordinary affirmative verb: たまに<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みます.

---

## めったに · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はめったに<ruby>外食<rt>がいしょく</rt></ruby>します。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はめったに<ruby>外食<rt>がいしょく</rt></ruby>し**ません**。<br>*The adverb cannot appear with an affirmative predicate. If you mean "I eat out occasionally", the word you want is たまに.*

- ❌ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>はめったに<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くない。 → ✅ この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くない。<br>*`めったに` counts occasions, not degrees, so it cannot modify an adjective.*

- ❌ <ruby>年<rt>ねん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>二回<rt>にかい</rt></ruby>ぐらいめったに<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない。 → ✅ <ruby>年<rt>ねん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>二回<rt>にかい</rt></ruby>ぐらいしか<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない。<br>*Naming a frequency and then calling it "hardly ever" pulls in two directions. # 決して～ない

**Meaning** · never; not under any circumstances - a categorical denial or an absolute commitment

<ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して does not make a sentence negative; the verb does that. What <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して adds is *finality*. It closes the door on exceptions, which is why it belongs to promises, warnings, rules and firm denials rather than to ordinary reporting.

---

## 決して～ない · Formation

## 決して～ない · Nuance & register

<ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して is emphatic and leans formal. It is the natural word in written rules, safety notices, apologies, contracts and public statements, and it is what you reach for in speech when you want to be heard as making a commitment rather than a remark. Among friends, the everyday word for the same force is <ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に.

Because it is absolute, it is also a favourite of denial. A politician saying <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>してそのような<ruby>事実<rt>じじつ</rt></ruby>はございません is not saying "probably not" - and the very strength of the word is why it can sound defensive when overused.

---

## 決して～ない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two smaller points. The kana spelling けっして is common in casual writing, and the kanji <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して in print; the reading is the same. And in formal writing you will meet <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して～まい, an old-fashioned negative-volitional pairing meaning "I shall never": <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れまい. Recognise it; you do not need to produce it.

---

## 決して～ない · 決して vs 絶対に

> **Rule of thumb** - <ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に works with both affirmatives and negatives. <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して works only with negatives, and sounds more formal doing it.

<ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>ちます**。<br>*We are absolutely going to win.*

**<ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して**あきらめ**ません**。<br>*I will never give up.*

---

## 決して～ない · 決して vs 絶対に (cont.)

Note that ❌<ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>ちます is impossible while <ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>負<rt>ま</rt></ruby>けません is perfectly natural - so <ruby>絶対<rt>ぜったい</rt></ruby>に is the more flexible word, and the one you will hear far more often in conversation. In writing, <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して is the more dignified of the two.

---

## 決して～ない · 決して vs 全然

> **Rule of thumb** - <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby> measures **degree**: not at all, right now. <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して makes a claim about **every occasion**: not ever, no exceptions.

Putting <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby> in the second sentence would say your lies are unconvincing rather than absent. And <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して in the first would turn a statement about today's homework into a lifelong vow of incomprehension.

---

## 決して～ない · 決して vs めったに

> **Rule of thumb** - both take a negative, but めったに leaves room for exceptions and <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して does not.

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は**めったに**<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>ら**ない**。<br>*My father hardly ever gets angry.*

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して**<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>ら**ない**。<br>*My father never gets angry - not once.*

めったに is a frequency report; <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して is a claim about principle or character. If you can imagine a counter-example, you want めったに.

## 決して～ない · Common mistakes

# べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない

**Meaning** · not really; not particularly - denying that there is anything special to report

`べつに` does not deny the fact so much as deny that the fact matters. It says there is nothing worth singling out here: no strong feeling, no problem, no reason worth going into. That is what makes it so useful for deflecting a question - and so easy to sound sulky with.

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · Formation

`べつに` is an adverb placed before the predicate, and the predicate is negative. Nothing attaches to it and it never changes shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | べつに + ない-form | <ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>る → べつに<ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>らない |
| Verb (polite) | べつに + ません | <ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぐ → べつに<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぎません |
| い-adjective | べつに + くない | <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい → べつに<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しくない |
| な-adjective・Noun | べつに + じゃない／ではない | <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い → べつに<ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いじゃない |
| Permission | べつに + なくてもいい | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → べつに<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なくてもいい |
| Standalone | べつに。 | 「どうしたの?」「べつに。」 |

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - like <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して and めったに, `べつに` needs a negative predicate. The standalone reply 「べつに。」 is not an exception: the negative is simply left unsaid, and both speakers hear it.

> **Note** - there is a second, unrelated <ruby>別<rt>べつ</rt></ruby>に meaning "separately", and it takes an ordinary affirmative verb: <ruby>別<rt>べつ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>包<rt>つつ</rt></ruby>んでいただけますか, "could you wrap them separately?" Same characters, different word. Context separates them without any effort: if the predicate is negative, it is the "not particularly" one.

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · Examples

「<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>ってる?」「**べつに**<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>って**ない**よ。」<br>*"Are you angry?" "Not particularly, no."*

**べつに**<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぎ**ません**から、ゆっくりどうぞ。<br>*There's no rush at all - please take your time.*

「どうしたの?」「……**べつに**。」<br>*"What's wrong?" "…Nothing."*

**べつに**<ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い**じゃない**けど、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>では<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わないかな。<br>*It's not that I dislike it - I just wouldn't buy one myself.*

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · Examples (cont.)

**べつに**<ruby>深<rt>ふか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>は**ありません**。ただ<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>になっただけです。<br>*There's no deeper meaning to it. I was just curious.*

<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れた<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いただけで、**べつに**<ruby>責<rt>せ</rt></ruby>めるつもりは**ありません**。<br>*I only asked why you were late; I'm not blaming you or anything.*

「<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>おうか?」「ありがとう。でも**べつに**<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>でも<ruby>平気<rt>へいき</rt></ruby>だから、<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んでて。」<br>*"Want a hand?" "Thanks, but I'm honestly fine on my own - go and rest."*

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · Nuance & register

`べつに` is casual to neutral. It is entirely normal inside ます-form speech - べつに<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>ありません is ordinary office Japanese - but the flavour is conversational, and in a formal document <ruby>特<rt>とく</rt></ruby>に is the better word.

Everything depends on delivery. Said warmly and followed by the rest of the sentence, `べつに` reassures: べつに<ruby>迷惑<rt>めいわく</rt></ruby>じゃありませんよ. Said flat, alone, and clipped, 「べつに。」 is the classic teenage non-answer - "nothing", meaning "something, and I'm not telling you". Learners sometimes pick up the standalone form from television and use it with a teacher or a manager, where it lands as rude. Keep the rest of the sentence, and the risk disappears.

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · Nuance & register (cont.)

The word also does real politeness work. Because it denies that anything special is going on, it lowers the temperature: telling someone べつに<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>っていませんよ is gentler than a bare <ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>っていません, which can sound like a protest.

Both spellings, <ruby>別<rt>べつ</rt></ruby>に and べつに, are common. The kana version looks slightly softer and is usual in messages.

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · べつに vs 特に

> **Rule of thumb** - <ruby>特<rt>とく</rt></ruby>に～ない is a neutral "nothing in particular". `べつに` adds a shrug, and sometimes a hint of defensiveness.

<ruby>質問<rt>しつもん</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>特<rt>とく</rt></ruby>に**ありません。<br>*I have no questions in particular.*

<ruby>質問<rt>しつもん</rt></ruby>は**べつに**ないけど。<br>*Nah, I haven't got any questions.*

In a meeting, on a form, or replying to a customer, <ruby>特<rt>とく</rt></ruby>に is the safe choice. `べつに` in the same slot is not wrong, but it sounds like you could not be bothered to think about it.

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · べつに vs あまり

> **Rule of thumb** - あまり～ない scales the **degree**: "not very". `べつに` denies that the thing is **notable at all**.

この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>は**あまり**<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>く**なかった**。<br>*That film wasn't very good.*

この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>は**べつに**<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>く**なかった**。<br>*That film was nothing special.*

The first is a measured judgement; the second dismisses the film, and often implies the speaker is contradicting someone who praised it.

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · べつに vs 全然

> **Rule of thumb** - <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby> is emphatic: not at all, none whatsoever. `べつに` is deliberately unemphatic.

**<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>にして**ない**よ。<br>*I don't mind in the slightest.*

**べつに**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>にして**ない**よ。<br>*I don't especially mind.*

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · べつに vs 全然 (cont.)

Reassuring a friend who has apologised, <ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby> is warmer, because it sounds like you mean it. `べつに` can come out cooler than you intended - which is exactly why it works when you *want* to sound unbothered.

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · Common mistakes

- ❌ べつに<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ べつに<ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い**じゃない**です。<br>*The predicate must be negative. Learners who translate "not really" one word at a time often leave the verb affirmative and reverse the meaning.*

- ❌（<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に）「<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>かありましたか。」「べつに。」 → ✅ 「いえ、**<ruby>特<rt>とく</rt></ruby>に**ありません。」<br>*The one-word reply is fine among friends and abrupt to anyone above you. Give the full sentence, or switch to <ruby>特<rt>とく</rt></ruby>に.*

- ❌ べつに<ruby>包<rt>つつ</rt></ruby>まないでください。（meaning "please wrap them separately"）→ ✅ **<ruby>別<rt>べつ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>包<rt>つつ</rt></ruby>んでください。<br>*With an affirmative verb the word means "separately", so a negative here says the opposite of what you wanted.*

---

## べつに～ない・べつに～じゃない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ べつに<ruby>三人<rt>さんにん</rt></ruby>しか<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかった。 → ✅ <ruby>三人<rt>さんにん</rt></ruby>しか<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかった。<br>*`べつに` denies that anything is noteworthy, so it clashes with `しか`, which points out that the number is small.*

# ふと - a thought that arrives on its own

**Meaning** · suddenly; out of nowhere - used of a thought, a memory or a feeling that surfaces without being summoned

`ふと` is the word for the moment when something occurs to you. Nobody made it happen, you were not trying to remember it, and there is no cause worth naming - it simply came.

---

## ふと - a thought that arrives on its own · Usage

`ふと` is an adverb and stands directly in front of the verb. It does not conjugate and takes no particles.

The lesson of the page is which verbs it goes with. In this sense `ふと` selects **verbs of the mind**:

- <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う - ふと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>ったんだけど…
- <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>す - ふと<ruby>昔<rt>むかし</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>した。
- <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づく - ふと<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づくと、<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>暗<rt>くら</rt></ruby>くなっていた。
- <ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>える, <ruby>不安<rt>ふあん</rt></ruby>になる, <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>になる, <ruby>頭<rt>あたま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>浮<rt>う</rt></ruby>かぶ

---

## ふと - a thought that arrives on its own · Usage (cont.)

Two shapes are near-fixed and worth learning whole:

- <code>ふと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>うことがある</code> - "the thought sometimes strikes me".
- <code>ふと<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づくと～ていた</code> - "before I knew it, …". The `と` here is the<br>*N4 conditional, and the second half is almost always in a past or `ていた` form.*

`ふと` describes a mental event happening to you, so it is almost always narrated in the **past**. ❌ふと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います, as a statement about the present, does not work; a thought you are having on purpose is not a `ふと` thought.

---

## ふと - a thought that arrives on its own · Usage (cont.)

It cannot be used of external events. Rain, noises and visitors do not arrive `ふと` - that is <ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に or <ruby>突然<rt>とつぜん</rt></ruby>.

---

## ふと - a thought that arrives on its own · Examples

**ふと**<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>の<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>した。<br>*My mother's voice suddenly came back to me.*

**ふと**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づくと、<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>終点<rt>しゅうてん</rt></ruby>だった。<br>*Before I knew it, the train had reached the last stop.*

<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>をしているとき、**ふと**<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>のことが<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>になった。<br>*While I was doing the washing-up, a worry about next year came over me out of nowhere.*

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>のために<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>いているのだろうと、**ふと**<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えてしまうことがある。<br>*Now and then the thought creeps up on me: what exactly am I working for?*

---

## ふと - a thought that arrives on its own · Nuance & register

`ふと` is neutral in politeness - it fits ます-form and plain form equally - but it is not evenly distributed. It belongs to reflective narration: novels, essays, diary entries, and the kind of conversation where somebody is telling you what went through their head. You will hear it far less in brisk, practical talk.

Because of that, `ふと` colours a sentence. It signals that the speaker was not in control of the thought, which makes it useful for softening: ふと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>った んだけど、… is a gentle way to introduce a suggestion, since you are presenting it as something that merely occurred to you rather than a position you have taken.

The word is written in kana. It has no kanji in ordinary use, no contraction, and no politeness variants.

---

## ふと - a thought that arrives on its own · ふと vs 急に・突然

> **Rule of thumb** - <ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に and <ruby>突然<rt>とつぜん</rt></ruby> describe an event changing suddenly. `ふと` describes something surfacing in your own mind.

**<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>した。<br>*It suddenly started to rain.*

**ふと**、<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってきていないことに<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づいた。<br>*It struck me that I hadn't brought an umbrella.*

---

## ふと - a thought that arrives on its own · ふと vs 急に・突然 (cont.)

The rain is out there in the world, so it takes <ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に; the realisation is inside the speaker's head, so it takes `ふと`. Note also the difference in force: <ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に is abrupt and often unwelcome, while `ふと` is quiet - a thought drifting in, not a door slamming.

---

## ふと - a thought that arrives on its own · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby>ふと<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>します。 → ✅ <ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>します。<br>*Something that happens every morning is a habit, not a thought arriving unbidden. `ふと` needs the occasion to be one-off and unplanned.*

# ふと - without meaning to

**Meaning** · unintentionally; by chance - of an action done with no purpose behind it, and of what you happen to notice as a result

This is the same word as the `ふと` of thoughts arriving unbidden, pointed outward. Here it modifies something you **did** - glancing up, stopping, saying a thing - and its job is to strip the action of any intention.

## ふと - without meaning to · Usage

- <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る・<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby><ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>げる - ふと<ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby><ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>げた。

## ふと - without meaning to · Usage (cont.)

- ふとしたことから<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>り<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>った。 - "we met through some small chance"
- ふとした<ruby>瞬間<rt>しゅんかん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>す

Two limits are worth stating plainly. First, the action has to be **small and momentary**: you can ふと glance at a shop window, but not ふと buy the shop. ❌ふと<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>辞<rt>や</rt></ruby>めた is wrong, because quitting a job takes decisions. Second, `ふと` implies no carelessness and no damage. It is neutral - for an unintended action with a bad outcome, Japanese uses うっかり.

Like the other sense, `ふと` is almost always narrated in the past.

## ふと - without meaning to · Nuance & register

Register-neutral in politeness, but stylistically marked: `ふと` belongs to narration. It is common in novels, essays and anecdotes, and rarer in brisk practical speech, where people simply say なんとなく ("for no particular reason") or たまたま ("as it happened").

The word is quiet. It gives an action a drifting, unhurried feel, which is why writers use it to open a scene - ふと<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>の<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ると… - and why it sits oddly in a hurried context.

Note that `ふと` says nothing about the consequences. In the fourth example above the remark caused trouble, but the trouble is carried by the rest of the sentence, not by `ふと`. If you want the listener to hear that you were careless and are sorry, you need うっかり.

`ふと` is written in kana, has no contraction, and is not marked for age or gender.

---

## ふと - without meaning to · ふと vs うっかり

> **Rule of thumb** - both mean "without intending to", but うっかり adds carelessness and a bad result. `ふと` is neutral and blames nobody.

**うっかり**<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れてしまった。<br>*I carelessly left my umbrella on the train.*

**ふと**<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>の<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>をやった。<br>*I let my eyes drift to the window.*

---

## ふと - without meaning to · ふと vs うっかり (cont.)

うっかり goes with mistakes - forgetting, mixing things up, pressing the wrong button - and typically appears with `てしまう`, which is itself the marker of regret. `ふと` goes with harmless small actions and does not take `てしまう` for that reason. Swapping them is immediately audible: ❌うっかり<ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby><ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>げた makes looking at the sky sound like an error.

---

- ❌ ふとパスワードを<ruby>間違<rt>まちが</rt></ruby>えた。 → ✅ **うっかり**パスワードを<ruby>間違<rt>まちが</rt></ruby>えた。<br>*A mistake with a consequence calls for the careless-action adverb. `ふと` says only that there was no intention, which is not what you want to convey when apologising.*

- ❌ ふと<ruby>三時間<rt>さんじかん</rt></ruby><ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>した。 → ✅ **なんとなく**<ruby>三時間<rt>さんじかん</rt></ruby><ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>してしまった。<br>*`ふと` covers an instant, not a stretch of time. It marks the moment an action begins, so a three-hour activity is outside its range.*
