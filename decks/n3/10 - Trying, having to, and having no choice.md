# ～(よ)うとする - try to

**Meaning** · make a move to do something - the attempt itself, with the result left hanging

The volitional form carries the will; `とする` turns that will into a physical move. `～(よ)うとする` photographs the effort, not the outcome - and Japanese only bothers to photograph the effort when the outcome was not the obvious one. That is why so many of these sentences end in `が` or `けれど`.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Formation

The volitional is frozen. `する` at the end is an ordinary verb and carries all the tense and politeness for the whole phrase.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + とする | <ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>そうとする |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + とする | <ruby>逃<rt>に</rt></ruby>げる → <ruby>逃<rt>に</rt></ruby>げようとする |
| Verb (する) | しよう + とする | <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>しようとする |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | こよう + とする | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ようとする |
| Attempt already made | ～(よ)うとした | <ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>そうとした |
| Attempt that ran into something | ～(よ)うとしたが／けれど | <ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>そうとしたが、<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>かった |
| Attempt plus its consequence | ～(よ)うとして + clause | <ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>そうとして、<ruby>腰<rt>こし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>めた |
| Polite | する → します | <ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>そうとしました |
| Even if you try | ～(よ)うとしても | <ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>そうとしても |

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - う-verb and る-verb are the same categories as <ruby>五段<rt>ごだん</rt></ruby> (Group 1) and <ruby>一段<rt>いちだん</rt></ruby> (Group 2). The only stem to memorise here is <code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る</code>, whose volitional is こよう - <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ようとした</code>, never くよう.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Examples

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>を**はこうとした**。<br>*My little sister tried to put her shoes on by herself.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>か**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>おうとして**、やめた。<br>*He started to say something, then stopped.*

<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>いドアを**<ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>そうとした**が、びくともしなかった。<br>*I tried to push the heavy door, but it didn't budge an inch.*

<ruby>逃<rt>に</rt></ruby>げた<ruby>鳥<rt>とり</rt></ruby>を**つかまえようとして**、<ruby>木<rt>き</rt></ruby>に<ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>りました。<br>*I climbed the tree trying to catch the bird that had got out.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>れたコップを<ruby>素手<rt>すで</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>拾<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>おうとして**、<ruby>指<rt>ゆび</rt></ruby>を<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>ってしまいました。<br>*I went to pick up the broken glass with my bare hands and cut my finger.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>飛<rt>と</rt></ruby>び<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>そうとした**ので、あわてて<ruby>腕<rt>うで</rt></ruby>をつかんだ。<br>*The child made a dash for the road, so I grabbed his arm.*

<ruby>上司<rt>じょうし</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>しようとした**のに、<ruby>一日中<rt>いちにちじゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>外<rt>はず</rt></ruby>していて<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せなかった。<br>*I meant to talk it over with my boss, but he was away from his desk all day and I never got the chance.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>停電<rt>ていでん</rt></ruby>のとき、<ruby>懐中電灯<rt>かいちゅうでんとう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>探<rt>さが</rt></ruby>そうとしました**が、<ruby>真<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>暗<rt>くら</rt></ruby>でどこにあるのか<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かりませんでした。<br>*When the power went out I tried to find a torch, but it was pitch dark and I had no idea where it was.*

どんなに**<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>そうとしても**、その<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>だけが<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>てこないんです。<br>*However hard I try to remember, that one person's name just won't come to me.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Nuance & register

The single most useful fact: **in the past tense, the attempt normally failed.** If you tried and got there, Japanese reports the arrival - <code><ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めました</code> - and says nothing about trying. So <code><ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めようとしました</code> tells your listener the window is still open. English "I tried to" is often modest understatement for success; this pattern is not.

The `て`-form is where the pattern earns its keep at N3. `～(よ)うとして` plus a following clause says *the attempt is what caused what came next*: <ruby>拾<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>おうとして<ruby>指<rt>ゆび</rt></ruby>を<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>った - the cutting happened in the course of the reaching. This is the natural way to narrate a small accident, and it is far more common in speech than the textbook `～(よ)うとしたが`.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Nuance & register (cont.)

The subject has to have a will. That is what separates this sense from the "about to" sense, where a typhoon or a sunset can be the subject. Here, only a creature that can decide something gets to try.

For **your own standing effort** the plain non-past is wrong: ❌<code><ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>こうとしています</code>. A policy you are keeping up is `～ようにしている`. The plain non-past `～(よ)うとする` is comfortable in general statements about people - <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>楽<rt>らく</rt></ruby>な<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>ぼうとする - where it describes a tendency rather than one person's morning.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral; `する` takes whatever politeness the sentence needs. In very casual speech the whole thing is often replaced by `～(し)かけた` or by <code><ruby>一応<rt>いちおう</rt></ruby>やってみた</code>, so if you hear <code><ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けようとしたんだけど</code> in conversation, note that it already sounds a shade more deliberate than the alternatives.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · ～(よ)うとする vs ～ようと思う

> **Rule of thumb** - <code>～ようと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う</code> happens in your head; `～(よ)うとする` happens with your hands. The first is a decision, the second is a movement.

<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>、<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>おうと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています**。<br>*I'm thinking of buying a car next year.*

<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>おうとした**ら、<ruby>貯金<rt>ちょきん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りませんでした。<br>*I went to buy a car and found I didn't have enough saved.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · ～(よ)うとする vs ～ようと思う (cont.)

The first sentence is compatible with never going near a dealership. The second one puts you in front of the price tag. This is also why <code>～ようと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う</code> is the polite way to announce a plan and `～(よ)うとする` is not: telling your boss <code><ruby>辞<rt>や</rt></ruby>めようとしています</code> sounds like you are halfway out of the building.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · ～(よ)うとする vs ～てみる

> **Rule of thumb** - both are "try", but they doubt different things. `～(よ)うとする` doubts whether the action can happen at all. `～てみる` takes the action for granted and doubts what it will be *like*.

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいアプリを**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってみました**が、<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>のほうが<ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby>でした。<br>*I gave the new app a go, but the old one was handier.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいアプリを**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>おうとしました**が、<ruby>登録<rt>とうろく</rt></ruby>できませんでした。<br>*I tried to use the new app, but I couldn't even register.*

In the first the app ran. In the second it never opened. Because they are about different things, they stack quite happily: <code><ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってみようとした</code> - "I made an attempt at giving it a go."

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · ～(よ)うとする vs ～ようにする

> **Rule of thumb** - `～(よ)うとする` is one moment of trying. `～ようにする` is a rule you have set yourself and are keeping to.

エレベーターを**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わないようにしています**。<br>*I make a point of not using the lift.*

<ruby>階段<rt>かいだん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>上<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>ろうとしました**が、<ruby>足<rt>あし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>くて<ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>でした。<br>*I tried to go up the stairs, but my leg hurt too much.*

Anything with <code><ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>最近<rt>さいきん</rt></ruby></code> or なるべく in it wants `～ようにする`. Anything with <code>その<ruby>時<rt>とき</rt></ruby></code> in it wants `～(よ)うとする`.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>探<rt>さが</rt></ruby>す**とした** → ✅ <ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>探<rt>さが</rt></ruby>**そうとした**<br>*`とする` here attaches to the **volitional**, not the dictionary form. Dictionary form + とする exists, but it is a separate written pattern meaning "supposing that", and a learner who mixes them will be misread.*

- ❌ <ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>び**ましょうとしました** → ✅ <ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>**ぼうとしました**<br>*Only the final `する` gets the polite ending. The volitional inside stays plain - ましょう never appears in front of `と`.*

- ❌ その<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**ようとしました**。とても<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>かったです。 → ✅ その<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**てみました**。<br>*You watched it and formed an opinion, so the attempt succeeded. A successful "try it and see" is `てみる`.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby><ruby>十一時<rt>じゅういちじ</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>**ようとしています** → ✅ <ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby><ruby>十一時<rt>じゅういちじ</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>**るようにしています**<br>*A bedtime you are trying to keep is a standing policy, not a single lunge at the duvet.*

- ❌ <ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>島<rt>しま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>づ**こうとしました** → ✅ <ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>島<rt>しま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>づ**こうとしていました**<br>*A typhoon cannot attempt anything. With a subject that has no will, only the "on the verge of" reading is available, and that reading needs `ている`.*

---

{reveal}

{reveal}

{reveal}
<code><ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>から</code> and <code><ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ず</code> make this a rule you have been keeping for a year - a standing policy, which is `～ようにする`. `～(よ)うとしている` would describe you at one particular breakfast, fork in hand.

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I tried to open the jar, but the lid wouldn't come off."

{reveal}

{reveal}
The lid stayed on, so the action never completed - that is exactly the job of `～(よ)うとした`. Using <code><ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてみました</code> would say the jar opened and you looked inside.

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>つとして、テーブルに<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>をついた。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>**とうとして**、テーブルに<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>をついた。 "He put a hand on the table as he went to stand up."

{reveal}
<code><ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>つ</code> is a う-verb ending in つ, so the volitional is <code><ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>とう</code>. The `て`-form then links the attempt to what it caused - the hand on the table happened in the course of getting up.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - try to · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてみました。 (b) <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けようとしました。

{reveal}
(a) says the window opened. `てみる` is "do it and see what happens", so the interesting part is what followed - a breeze, a noise from the street, the smell of rain. The action itself was never in doubt.

{reveal}
(b) says the window did not open, or at least that something interrupted you. The interesting part is the obstacle, and a listener will wait for the `が` clause that explains it. Said with no follow-up, (b) sounds unfinished.

# ～(よ)うとする - be about to

**Meaning** · on the very point of happening - the instant before the event tips over

Same three syllables as the "try to" pattern, but a different job. Here nobody is trying anything: `～(よ)うとしている` freezes the frame just before a change completes. The winter that has not quite ended, the doors that have not quite shut. Because no will is involved, the subject can be a season, a train or a storm.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Formation

This sense almost always wears `ている` or `ていた`, or else feeds into `とき` or `ところ`. The bare non-past `～(よ)うとする` does **not** carry the "about to" reading.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + としている | <ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わる → <ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わろうとしている |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + としている | <ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>える → <ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>えようとしている |
| Verb (する) | しよう + としている | <ruby>発車<rt>はっしゃ</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>発車<rt>はっしゃ</rt></ruby>しようとしている |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | こよう + としている | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ようとしている |
| Was on the point of | ～(よ)うとしていた | <ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>えようとしていた |
| Just as it did | ～(よ)うとしたとき | <ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>まろうとしたとき |
| Right at that moment | ～(よ)うとしたところ(に・へ・を) | <ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ようとしたところに |
| Polite | している → しています | <ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わろうとしています |

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the verbs that turn up here are overwhelmingly **intransitive verbs of change**: <ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まる, <ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わる, <ruby>沈<rt>しず</rt></ruby>む, <ruby>暮<rt>く</rt></ruby>れる, <ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>える, <ruby>散<rt>ち</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>倒<rt>たお</rt></ruby>れる, <ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わる. If your verb is one of those, the "be about to" reading is the only one available.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Examples

<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>い<ruby>冬<rt>ふゆ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わろうとしている**。<br>*The long winter is drawing to a close.*

<ruby>船<rt>ふね</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ようとしています**。<br>*The ship is about to leave.*

エレベーターのドアが**<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>まろうとした**とき、<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かが<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>呼<rt>よ</rt></ruby>び<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>めた。<br>*Just as the lift doors were closing, someone called out to stop them.*

お<ruby>風呂<rt>ふろ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>ろうとしたところに**、<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>がかかってきました。<br>*The phone rang just as I was about to get in the bath.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>会場<rt>かいじょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>えようとしていた**ので、<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>いで<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>をまとめました。<br>*The lights in the hall were on the point of going out, so I gathered my things in a hurry.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>時代<rt>じだい</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まろうとしている**と、その<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>新聞<rt>しんぶん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いていた。<br>*That day's paper wrote that a new age was about to begin.*

<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>りに**<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちようとしていた**ところを、<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>階<rt>かい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>物音<rt>ものおと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>こされた。<br>*I was just drifting off when a noise from upstairs woke me.*

ホームで<ruby>最終<rt>さいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>発車<rt>はっしゃ</rt></ruby>しようとしている**のを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>て、<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>るのをあきらめました。<br>*Seeing the last train on the point of pulling out, I gave up on running for it.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>辞<rt>や</rt></ruby>めようとしていた**ちょうどその<ruby>時<rt>とき</rt></ruby>、<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>から<ruby>異動<rt>いどう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た。<br>*Just when I was on the point of quitting, my manager raised the idea of a transfer.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Nuance & register

The reliable test for which sense you are in is **the subject**. If it could not possibly decide anything - a season, a candle, a train, a century - you are in the "about to" sense, and no amount of context will make it mean "tried to". With a person, the form is genuinely ambiguous, and this is harmless: both readings point at the same instant, and the sentence is normally interrupted by something anyway.

The register is **narrative**. This pattern belongs to news copy, novels, essays and the sort of speech that is consciously describing a scene. In ordinary conversation people reach for もうすぐ, ところ or `～そう`: <code>もうすぐ<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>くよ</code>, not <code><ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>こうとしているよ</code>. Saying <code>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>炊<rt>た</rt></ruby>けようとしています</code> to a housemate sounds like you are narrating a documentary about your rice cooker.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Nuance & register (cont.)

<code><ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>にも</code> pairs with it naturally and raises the tension a notch: <code><ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>倒<rt>たお</rt></ruby>れようとしている</code>. So does `まさに` in written Japanese.

One boundary worth marking: this is about a **moment**, not about the near future. Next week's roadworks are not <code><ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まろうとしています</code>; they simply <code><ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まります</code>. If you can attach a date to it, you have left the pattern's territory.

Finally, the `ところ` frame is the most conversational corner of this sense. `～(よ)うとしたところに` plus an interruption - a phone call, a visitor, rain - is a small, very common storytelling move, and it survives in casual speech where the plain `ている` form would sound stiff.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · ～(よ)うとしている vs ～ところだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `ところだ` needs a subject with a schedule; it is the neutral "I'm just about to". `～(よ)うとしている` is a camera angle, and it works on things that never planned anything.

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>から<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かける**ところです**。<br>*I'm just heading out now.*

<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>屋根<rt>やね</rt></ruby>から**<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちようとしている**。<br>*The snow is on the point of sliding off the roof.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · ～(よ)うとしている vs ～ところだ (cont.)

Swap them and both go wrong. Snow has no `ところ` of its own to be standing at, and narrating your own departure with <code><ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけようとしています</code> makes you sound like a character in a film rather than a person telling a friend where you are.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · ～(よ)うとしている vs ～かけている

> **Rule of thumb** - `～(よ)うとしている` means it has **not started**. `～かけている` means it **started and stopped partway**.

ろうそくの<ruby>火<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>えようとしている**。<br>*The candle is about to go out.*

ろうそくの<ruby>火<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>えかけている**。<br>*The candle is guttering out.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · ～(よ)うとしている vs ～かけている (cont.)

The first candle is still burning steadily; you can see that it has almost no wax left. The second is already halfway gone - the flame is failing as you watch. The same split explains <code><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みかけの<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby></code> (a book you are partway through) against <code><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>もうとしている<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby></code> (one you have not opened).

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · ～(よ)うとしている vs 今にも～そうだ

> **Rule of thumb** - both mean "any second now", but `～そうだ` is a **guess read off the surface** of something, while `～(よ)うとしている` reports the event as genuinely underway.

<ruby>棚<rt>たな</rt></ruby>の<ruby>箱<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちそうです**。<br>*That box on the shelf looks like it's going to fall.*

<ruby>棚<rt>たな</rt></ruby>の<ruby>箱<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちようとしています**。<br>*The box on the shelf is on the point of falling.*

The first is your inference from how the box is sitting; it may sit there all year. The second commits: the box is going. In warnings and in speech, `そうだ` is almost always what you want - it is the one that leaves you room to be wrong.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>月<rt>つき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>**ようとする**。 → ✅ <ruby>月<rt>つき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>**ようとしている**。<br>*The "about to" reading lives in `ている`. Without it, the plain non-past reads as a general statement about attempting, which the moon cannot do.*

- ❌ バスが<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>**ようとしている**。 → ✅ バスが<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>**ようとしている**。<br>*The volitional of <code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る</code> is こよう, not きよう. The ます-stem reading き belongs to <code><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます</code> and nowhere near this pattern.*

- ❌ <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>駅前<rt>えきまえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>**まろうとしています**。 → ✅ <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>駅前<rt>えきまえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>**まります**。<br>*A dated future event is just the plain non-past. This pattern is for the second before something happens, not for next week.*

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ もうすぐ<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**ようとしています**。 → ✅ もうすぐ<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**るところです**。<br>*About yourself, with a plan, `ところだ` is the natural form. `～(よ)うとしている` here narrates you from outside, which is why it sounds oddly grand.*

- ❌ <ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わろうと**しましたとき** → ✅ <ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わろうと**したとき**<br>*A clause in front of `とき` takes the plain form. Politeness goes on the end of the sentence, not inside it.*

---

{reveal}
<ruby>太陽<rt>たいよう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>の<ruby>向<rt>む</rt></ruby>こうに<ruby>沈<rt>しず</rt></ruby>**もう**としていた。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>沈<rt>しず</rt></ruby>む</code> is a う-verb ending in む, so the volitional is <code><ruby>沈<rt>しず</rt></ruby>もう</code>. The sun has no will, which confirms this is the "on the point of" sense rather than an attempt.

---

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>屋根<rt>やね</rt></ruby>の<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が（<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちそうです・<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちようとしています）から、<ruby>下<rt>した</rt></ruby>を<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>らないでください。

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Practice 3

{reveal}
`～(よ)うとしたとき` is the standard frame for "just as I was about to, something cut in". <code><ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ようとしたところに</code> works equally well and sounds a shade more conversational.

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わろうとしていますので、<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>てましょう。

✅ <ruby>一年<rt>いちねん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わろうと**している**ので、<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>てましょう。

{reveal}

## ～(よ)うとする - be about to · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>が<ruby>散<rt>ち</rt></ruby>ろうとしている。 (b) <ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>が<ruby>散<rt>ち</rt></ruby>りかけている。

{reveal}

{reveal}

# ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない

**Meaning** · won't even try - the person is unwilling, and the speaker is not pleased about it

Negating the attempt does something the plain negative cannot: it puts the other person's **will** in the frame. <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べない</code> reports that no eating is happening. <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べようとしない</code> says they have decided against it, and you can hear the speaker's patience running out.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | volitional + としない | <ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>こうとしない |
| Verb (る-verb) | volitional + としない | <ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>める → <ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めようとしない |
| Verb (する) | しよう + としない | <ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>しようとしない |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | こよう + としない | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ようとしない |
| Past | ～(よ)うとしなかった | <ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めようとしなかった |
| Polite | ～(よ)うとしません | <ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めようとしません |
| Emphatic (contrastive) | ～(よ)うとはしない | <ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めようとはしない |
| Strongest - "didn't even" | ～(よ)うともしない | <ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めようともしない |

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the negative goes on `する` at the end, never on the verb inside. ❌<code><ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めまいとする</code> is not a variant of this pattern; the shape is always positive volitional + `としない`.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Examples

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>から**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ようとしない**。<br>*My son won't come out of his room.*

<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>とうとしなかった**。<br>*Nobody would get up from their seat.*

その<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>づこうとしない**。<br>*That dog won't go anywhere near strangers.*

いくら<ruby>謝<rt>あやま</rt></ruby>っても、<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>げようとはしなかった**。<br>*However much I apologised, she would not so much as look up.*

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>同僚<rt>どうりょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>のミスを**<ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めようとしません**。<br>*My colleague refuses to admit his own mistakes.*

<ruby>祖母<rt>そぼ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>足<rt>あし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>いのに、<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>へ**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>こうとしない**んです。<br>*My grandmother's leg hurts, and still she won't go to the hospital.*

<ruby>市役所<rt>しやくしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>住民<rt>じゅうみん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>質問<rt>しつもん</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>答<rt>こた</rt></ruby>えようともしなかった**。<br>*The city office would not even attempt to answer the residents' questions.*

<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いは<ruby>三時間<rt>さんじかん</rt></ruby><ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>きましたが、どちらの<ruby>側<rt>がわ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>一歩<rt>いっぽ</rt></ruby>も**ゆずろうとしませんでした**。<br>*The talks ran for three hours, but neither side would give an inch.*

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいやり<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>をどれだけ<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>しても、ベテランの<ruby>職人<rt>しょくにん</rt></ruby>たちは<ruby>耳<rt>みみ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>そうとしなかった**。<br>*No matter how thoroughly the new method was explained, the veteran craftsmen wouldn't hear of it.*

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Nuance & register

This pattern is a **complaint**, and that is not a side effect - it is the reason the pattern exists. Using it means asserting that you know what is going on inside someone else's head. That makes it comfortable about children, animals, machines and institutions, awkward about equals, and genuinely risky about people you owe deference to. <code><ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>めようとしません</code> is not a neutral status report; it is an accusation of obstruction, and it will be heard as one.

Unlike the "try to" sense, the **non-past is completely natural here**, because it describes a standing state of refusal rather than one moment of effort. <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べようとする</code> about a habit is odd; <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べようとしない</code> about a habit is the normal thing to say.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Nuance & register (cont.)

The three degrees are worth learning as a set:

- `～(よ)うとしない` - plain report of unwillingness.
- `～(よ)うとはしない` - the `は` sets this action against everything<br>*else the person did do: *that* one thing, they would not do. More pointed, and commoner in writing.*
- `～(よ)うともしない` - "would not so much as". The most dismissive of<br>*the three, and often paired with どれだけ, いくら or <ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>も.*

Applied to objects, the pattern is a deliberate personification and reads as exasperated rather than mistaken: <code><ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>が<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>こうとしない</code> casts the car as sulking. That is a stylistic choice, not the default - a bus that is merely late is <code>なかなか<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ません</code>.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Nuance & register (cont.)

About yourself, the present tense is strange - you would simply say `やりたくない`. The past is fine in reflective narration: <code>あのころの<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>も<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こうとしなかった</code>, looking back at your younger self as if at someone else.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない vs ～たがらない

> **Rule of thumb** - `～たがらない` says they **don't want to**. `～(よ)うとしない` says they **won't do it**, whatever they may want.

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>られ**たがりません**。<br>*My little brother doesn't like having his photo taken.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>とうとしません**。<br>*My little brother won't stand in front of the camera.*

---

## ～(よ)うとしない vs ～たがらない (cont.)

`たがる` reports a desire you have inferred from the outside; the sentence is about his feelings. `～(よ)うとしない` reports behaviour, and it works even where desire is irrelevant - someone may badly want to apologise and still <code><ruby>謝<rt>あやま</rt></ruby>ろうとしない</code>. That gap is the useful part: it is how you describe pride, fear or stubbornness overriding what a person actually wants.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない vs ～てくれない

> **Rule of thumb** - `～(よ)うとしない` criticises the other person's attitude. `～てくれない` complains that **you** did not get what you needed.

<ruby>店員<rt>てんいん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>し**てくれなかった**。<br>*The assistant didn't explain it to me.*

<ruby>店員<rt>てんいん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>し**ようとしなかった**。<br>*The assistant wouldn't even try to explain.*

---

## ～(よ)うとしない vs ～てくれない (cont.)

The first is about the loss to you; the assistant might have been busy or new. The second is about the assistant's unwillingness. Stacking them makes the strongest version of the complaint: <code><ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>しようとしてくれなかった</code> - they would not make the effort *for me*.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない vs ～(よ)うとはしない vs ～(よ)うともしない

> **Rule of thumb** - bare `と` states it, `とは` contrasts it against what they did do, `とも` says they did not even get as far as starting.

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>そうとしなかった**。<br>*He wouldn't tell me why.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>はしたが、<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>そうとはしなかった**。<br>*He made excuses, but the actual reason he would not give.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>そうともしなかった**。<br>*He didn't even attempt to tell me why.*

---

## ～(よ)うとしない vs ～(よ)うとはしない vs ～(よ)うともしない (cont.)

The middle sentence needs the first clause to earn its `は`; without something to contrast against, `とはしない` can sound as though a comparison has gone missing. The third needs no setup at all - `も` is doing the work of "not even" by itself.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>履<rt>は</rt></ruby>く**としない** → ✅ <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>履<rt>は</rt></ruby>**こうとしない**<br>*The dictionary form never appears here. It is volitional + `としない`, so <code><ruby>履<rt>は</rt></ruby>く</code> becomes <code><ruby>履<rt>は</rt></ruby>こう</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ようとしません**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ごはんを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ません**。<br>*About your own present habit, the pattern is odd - it makes you sound like a bystander disapproving of yourself. Use the plain negative, or <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたくないんです</code> if you want the reason in.*

- ❌ バスがなかなか<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>**ようとしません**。 → ✅ バスがなかなか<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>**ません**。<br>*A late bus is not refusing anything. Reserve the pattern for subjects with a will, or for a personification you actually mean.*

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌（to your manager）<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>はメールに<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>を**しようとしませんね**。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、メールの<ruby>件<rt>けん</rt></ruby>、まだお<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>をいただいていないのですが。<br>*Said to the person themselves, this accuses them of deliberate obstruction. When the subject is someone you must be polite to, drop the pattern entirely and report the fact.*

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>ちゃんが<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>____としないので、<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一晩中<rt>ひとばんじゅう</rt></ruby>だっこしていた。

{reveal}
<ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>ちゃんが<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>**ろう**としないので…

{reveal}
<code><ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>る</code> is a う-verb, so the volitional is <code><ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>ろう</code>. The pattern fits a baby perfectly: it is exactly the sentence of a parent who has decided the child is doing this on purpose.

---

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>人前<rt>ひとまえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>い（たがりません・ようとしません）。カラオケでもずっとすみに<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>っています。

{reveal}
Both are possible, and they say different things. **<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>いたがりません** reports that he has no desire to sing, which fits someone quietly sitting in the corner. **<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>おうとしません** would add that people have been pushing him and he is holding out. Given that the second sentence only describes him sitting there, **<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>いたがりません** is the more natural fit.

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I offered several times, but he wouldn't take the money."

{reveal}
<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby>もすすめましたが、<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はお<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>け<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>ろうとしませんでした**。

{reveal}
The point of the sentence is that he could have taken it and chose not to - that is `～(よ)うとしない` rather than a plain <code><ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>け<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>りませんでした</code>, which would merely say no money changed hands.

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>がやもうとしないので、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になりました。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が**やまない**ので、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になりました。

{reveal}
Rain has no will, so it cannot refuse to stop. The plain negative is what you want. `やもうとしない` is possible only as a conscious personification in literary writing - in a report of a cancelled match it just reads as an error.

## ～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>はこちらを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>なかった。 (b) <ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>はこちらを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ようともしなかった。

{reveal}

{reveal}

# ～べき

**Meaning** · ought to; is the right thing to do - measured against a principle, not against convenience

`べき` passes a **judgement**. It says the action is correct - morally, socially, professionally - and it does so with the weight of a standard behind it. That is what separates it from `ほうがいい`, which merely says the action would work out better for you.

---

## ～べき · Formation

`べき` is a survival from classical Japanese, which is why it attaches to the dictionary form and why `する` has two shapes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + べきだ | <ruby>守<rt>まも</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>守<rt>まも</rt></ruby>るべきだ |
| Verb (する) | するべき / すべき | <ruby>報告<rt>ほうこく</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>報告<rt>ほうこく</rt></ruby>するべきだ・<ruby>報告<rt>ほうこく</rt></ruby>すべきだ |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るべき | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るべきだ |
| Before a noun | ～べき + noun | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むべき<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby> |
| Regret about the past | ～べきだった | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきだった |
| Polite | ～べきです・べきでした | <ruby>守<rt>まも</rt></ruby>るべきです |
| Noun / な-adjective (written) | ～である + べき | <ruby>公平<rt>こうへい</rt></ruby> → <ruby>公平<rt>こうへい</rt></ruby>であるべきだ |

---

## ～べき · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - `するべき` and `すべき` mean the same thing. `すべき` is the older form and is preferred in writing, editorials and formal speech; `するべき` is easier on the ear in conversation. Neither is wrong. In noun-modifying position the `だ` disappears - <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきこと</code>, not ❌<code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきだこと</code>.

---

## ～べき · Examples

もっと<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるべきだ**。<br>*You ought to eat more vegetables.*

<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>守<rt>まも</rt></ruby>るべきです**。<br>*Promises should be kept.*

こういうことは、<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めに<ruby>上司<rt>じょうし</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>報告<rt>ほうこく</rt></ruby>すべきだ**。<br>*Something like this ought to be reported to your manager early.*

あの<ruby>時<rt>とき</rt></ruby>、はっきり「できません」と**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきだった**。<br>*I should have said "I can't" clearly at the time.*

---

## ～べき · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>のうちに**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むべき**<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を、<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>紹介<rt>しょうかい</rt></ruby>してくださいました。<br>*My teacher recommended some books one ought to read while still a student.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に、<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>しておくべきでした**。<br>*I should have looked through the handouts before the meeting.*

<ruby>災害<rt>さいがい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>備<rt>そな</rt></ruby>えて、<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>と<ruby>食料<rt>しょくりょう</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>用意<rt>ようい</rt></ruby>しておくべきだ**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*I think we ought to keep water and food ready in case of a disaster.*

<ruby>本来<rt>ほんらい</rt></ruby>この<ruby>制度<rt>せいど</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>一番<rt>いちばん</rt></ruby><ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>っている<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>けるべきもの**です。<br>*This system is supposed, by its very nature, to help the people in the greatest difficulty.*

---

## ～べき · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>したときにどうするかを、チーム<ruby>全員<rt>ぜんいん</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>めておくべきだった**と<ruby>後悔<rt>こうかい</rt></ruby>しています。<br>*I regret that we didn't agree as a team, in advance, what to do if it went wrong.*

---

## ～べき · Nuance & register

`べき` is heavy. It appeals to something outside the speaker - a principle, a duty, the way things are done - and that is why it turns up constantly in newspaper editorials, essays, court judgements and meeting minutes, and why it can land badly in a kitchen conversation. Told <code>もっと<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるべきだ</code>, a friend hears a small sermon.

Aimed at a listener, therefore, `べき` needs softening, and Japanese has a standard kit for it:

- <code>～べきだと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code> - makes it your opinion rather than the<br>*law.*
- `～べきではないでしょうか` - a rhetorical question, the politest form<br>*of the same claim, very common in meetings.*
- `～べきかもしれません` - hedged almost to invisibility.

---

## ～べき · Nuance & register (cont.)

Bare `～べきだ` pointed at someone senior to you is close to insubordinate. Between friends it is fine if you are half-joking, and it is completely fine when the subject is *you* - <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くべきでした - or an abstraction: <ruby>政治<rt>せいじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>公平<rt>こうへい</rt></ruby>であるべきだ.

The past form is the one learners under-use. `～べきだった` is the everyday way to express regret about a choice you made: <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うべきだった, <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いておくべきだった. It is neither formal nor preachy in that use, because the person being judged is yourself.

---

## ～べき · Nuance & register (cont.)

> **Note** - you will meet the same word as `～べく` ("in order to") and `～べからず` ("do not") on signs and in stiff writing: <ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>るべからず. Both are frozen classical leftovers; recognise them, but write `～ために` and `～ないでください` yourself.

---

## ～べき · ～べきだ vs ～なければならない

> **Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` is an obligation **imposed** on you: a rule, a deadline, a law. `べきだ` is a judgement you are **making**: this is the right thing.

<ruby>外国人<rt>がいこくじん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>在留<rt>ざいりゅう</rt></ruby>カードを<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>か**なければなりません**。<br>*Foreign residents are required to carry their residence card.*

<ruby>留学生<rt>りゅうがくせい</rt></ruby>にもっと<ruby>情報<rt>じょうほう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>す**べきです**。<br>*More information ought to be made available to international students.*

The first is the law; you have no view about it, you are reporting it. The second is an argument, and someone could disagree with you. Swap them and the first sentence becomes an opinion about immigration policy, while the second sounds as if a regulation exists that you have not cited.

---

## ～べき · ～べきだ vs ～なければならない (cont.)

This also explains a common error. If the requirement is already binding, `べき` is the weaker and stranger choice: a visa application that *must* include a photo takes <code><ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れなければなりません</code>.

---

## ～べき · ～べきだ vs ～ほうがいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ほうがいい` compares outcomes for **this person, this situation**. `べきだ` measures the action against a **standard** and is indifferent to whether it suits you.

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるなら、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んだ**ほうがいいですよ**。<br>*If you've got a fever, you'd be better off taking today off.*

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるのに<ruby>出勤<rt>しゅっきん</rt></ruby>させる<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>のほうが、<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えを<ruby>改<rt>あらた</rt></ruby>める**べきです**。<br>*It's the company making people come in with a fever that ought to change its thinking.*

`ほうがいい` is advice and is heard as kindness. `べき` is a verdict and is heard as criticism - which is exactly why the second sentence uses it. For everyday suggestions to a friend, `ほうがいい` is almost always the safer form.

---

## ～べき · ～べきだった vs ～ばよかった

> **Rule of thumb** - both look back at a wrong choice. `ばよかった` is **wishing**; `べきだった` is **judging yourself**.

もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>れ**ばよかった**。<br>*I wish I'd left earlier.*

もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>る**べきだった**。<br>*I should have left earlier.*

---

## ～べき · ～べきだった vs ～ばよかった (cont.)

The first is a sigh; it fits missing a nice sunset. The second accepts fault and fits missing a flight you were responsible for. `ばよかった` can also be used about things nobody controlled - <code><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れればよかったのに</code> - where `べきだった` is impossible, because weather cannot be blamed.

---

## ～べき · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>教師<rt>きょうし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>公平<rt>こうへい</rt></ruby>**べきだ**。 → ✅ <ruby>教師<rt>きょうし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>公平<rt>こうへい</rt></ruby>**であるべきだ**。<br>*`べき` needs a verb in front of it. Nouns and な-adjectives get there through `である`.*

- ❌ <ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>**しるべき** → ✅ <ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>**するべき**・<ruby>注意<rt>ちゅうい</rt></ruby>**すべき**<br>*`する` gives two legitimate forms and neither is しる. Pick `すべき` for writing, `するべき` for speech.*

- ❌ ビザの<ruby>申請<rt>しんせい</rt></ruby>には<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れる**べきです**。 → ✅ ビザの<ruby>申請<rt>しんせい</rt></ruby>には<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れ**なければなりません**。<br>*A published requirement is not your judgement. Reporting a rule takes `なければならない`.*

---

## ～べき · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌（to your manager）<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、もっと<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む**べきです**。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しお<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みになったほうがよろしいのではないでしょうか。<br>*Bare `べきだ` aimed upwards is a reprimand. Move to `ほうがいい`, or at the very least to `～べきではないでしょうか`.*

- ❌ <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべき**だ**ことがあります。 → ✅ <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う**べき**ことがあります。<br>*In front of a noun, `べき` stands alone. The `だ` only appears at the end of a sentence.*

---

## ～べき · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>いと<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かっているなら、すぐに<ruby>謝<rt>あやま</rt></ruby>____だ。

{reveal}
すぐに<ruby>謝<rt>あやま</rt></ruby>**るべき**だ。

{reveal}
`べき` attaches to the dictionary form, so <code><ruby>謝<rt>あやま</rt></ruby>る</code> stays whole. The sentence is a moral judgement - apologising is the right thing - which is precisely `べき` territory rather than `ほうがいい`.

---

## ～べき · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>受験票<rt>じゅけんひょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってこ（なければなりません・るべきです）。

{reveal}
<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってこ**なければなりません**。

{reveal}
The exam board requires the admission slip; this is a rule being reported, not an opinion you are advancing. <code><ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってくるべきです</code> would suggest you personally think it would be a good idea, which understates a hard requirement.

## ～べき · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I should have asked her before I decided."

{reveal}
<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>める<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に、<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>くべきでした**。

`～べきだった／べきでした` is the standard way to say "I should have", with the fault accepted. <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>けばよかった</code> is also possible but softer - closer to "I wish I'd asked" than to "that was my mistake".

## ～べき · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>については、<ruby>全員<rt>ぜんいん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うべきだことだと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。

✅ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>については、<ruby>全員<rt>ぜんいん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>う**べき**ことだと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。

{reveal}

## ～べき · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>は<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>たほうがいいよ。 (b) この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>は<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>るべきだよ。

{reveal}

{reveal}

Between close friends this comes across as enthusiastic overstatement, and that is often exactly the joke; said flatly to someone you do not know well, it sounds like being told off.

# ～べきではない

**Meaning** · ought not to; it is wrong to do this - a verdict, not a rule posted on a wall

The negative of `べき` keeps everything that made `べき` heavy. It appeals to a principle and finds the action falls short of it. Nobody is forbidding you - `べきではない` has no authority behind it, only judgement - which is why it feels at once softer than a prohibition and harder to argue with.

---

## ～べきではない · Formation

The negative goes on `べき`, never on the verb inside it.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + べきではない | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきではない |
| Verb (する) | するべきではない / すべきではない | <ruby>優先<rt>ゆうせん</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>優先<rt>ゆうせん</rt></ruby>すべきではない |
| Casual | ～べきじゃない | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきじゃない |
| Polite | ～べきではありません | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきではありません |
| Regret about the past | ～べきではなかった | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきではなかった |
| Polite past | ～べきではありませんでした | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきではありませんでした |
| Noun / な-adjective (written) | ～である + べきではない | あいまい → あいまいであるべきではない |

---

## ～べきではない · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - ❌<code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わないべきだ</code> does not exist. Japanese negates the judgement, not the action: <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきではない</code>. The same is true of the past - <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきではなかった</code>, never ❌<code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わないべきだった</code>.

---

## ～べきではない · Examples

<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>日記<rt>にっき</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むべきではない**。<br>*You shouldn't read other people's diaries.*

そんなことを**<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきじゃない**よ。<br>*You shouldn't say things like that.*

<ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>するなら、お<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>むべきではありません**。<br>*If you're driving, you shouldn't be drinking.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもの<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>夫婦<rt>ふうふ</rt></ruby>げんかを**するべきではなかった**。<br>*We shouldn't have had that argument in front of the children.*

---

## ～べきではない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>意見<rt>いけん</rt></ruby>だけで<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>めるべきではない**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*I don't think everything should be decided on one person's opinion alone.*

あんな<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>時計<rt>とけい</rt></ruby>を、<ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>る<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うべきではありませんでした**。<br>*I shouldn't have bought a watch that expensive before payday.*

<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>社員<rt>しゃいん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>健康<rt>けんこう</rt></ruby>より<ruby>利益<rt>りえき</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>優先<rt>ゆうせん</rt></ruby>すべきではない**。<br>*A company should not put profit ahead of its employees' health.*

<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らないことを、<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っているふりをして**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すべきではありません**。<br>*You shouldn't talk about things you don't know as if you did.*

---

## ～べきではない · Examples (cont.)

うわさだけで<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>判断<rt>はんだん</rt></ruby>すべきではない**というのが、<ruby>祖父<rt>そふ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>口<rt>くち</rt></ruby>ぐせでした。<br>*"Never judge someone on rumour alone" was my grandfather's favourite saying.*

---

## ～べきではない · Nuance & register

The force of `べきではない` is **moral**, not institutional. It is what an editorial says about a government, what a parent says about lying, what you say to yourself at three in the morning. There is no penalty attached and no authority cited; the sentence simply asserts that the action is wrong, and invites you to agree.

Because of that, aimed straight at a listener it preaches. Between close friends <code><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきじゃないよ</code> is normal and even warm - the casual contraction takes most of the weight off - but `べきではありません` to someone you barely know reads as a dressing-down. Softening works the same way as with the positive: put <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code> on it, or move to `ないほうがいい`.

---

## ～べきではない · Nuance & register (cont.)

The past form is the everyday one. `～べきではなかった` is how you own a mistake, and it carries no preachiness at all when the subject is yourself: <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>うべきではなかった, <ruby>信<rt>しん</rt></ruby>じるべきではなかった.

> **Note** - one trap that catches almost everybody. `～べきではないでしょうか` and `～べきではないか` are **rhetorical questions**, and they argue *for* the action, not against it. <code>もっと<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うべきではないでしょうか</code> means "shouldn't we talk about it more?" - that is, we should. If you want to argue against something politely, you need <code>～べきではないと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code>.

---

## ～べきではない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is otherwise straightforward: `すべきではない` for writing and formal speech, `するべきではない` and `べきじゃない` for conversation. The word is at home in essays, opinion columns and meetings, and slightly stiff in small talk.

---

## ～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～てはいけない

> **Rule of thumb** - `てはいけない` is a **prohibition**: someone with authority says no, and there are consequences. `べきではない` is an **opinion about right and wrong**, with nobody enforcing it.

<ruby>館内<rt>かんない</rt></ruby>では<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>っ**てはいけません**。<br>*Photography is not permitted inside the building.*

<ruby>相手<rt>あいて</rt></ruby>の<ruby>許可<rt>きょか</rt></ruby>なく<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>る**べきではありません**。<br>*You shouldn't photograph someone without their permission.*

The first is the museum's rule; you would find it on a sign, and breaking it gets you stopped. The second is an argument about how people should behave, and it holds in places where no such sign exists. Put `べきではない` on a notice and it reads oddly - as if the building were expressing a hope rather than setting a rule.

---

## ～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～てはいけない (cont.)

`てはいけない` also works for immediate, physical danger, where `べきではない` is far too abstract: <code><ruby>熱<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>いから<ruby>触<rt>さわ</rt></ruby>ってはいけません</code>.

---

## ～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～ないほうがいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ないほうがいい` weighs **outcomes for this person**. `べきではない` weighs the action against a **principle** and does not care whether it suits you.

<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby><ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>くにコーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ま**ないほうがいいですよ**。<br>*You're better off not drinking coffee late at night.*

<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>う**べきではありません**。<br>*You should not laugh at other people's mistakes.*

---

## ～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～ないほうがいい (cont.)

Swap them and both go wrong in an instructive way. <code>コーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>むべきではない</code> turns caffeine into a moral failing. <code><ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わないほうがいい</code> turns cruelty into a tactical error - as though the problem were that you might get caught. For advice to a friend, `ないほうがいい` is nearly always the right register; save `べきではない` for when you actually mean to make a judgement.

---

## ～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～べきではないでしょうか

> **Rule of thumb** - adding `でしょうか` flips the meaning. The statement opposes the action; the question **recommends** it.

この<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby><ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>める**べきではない**。<br>*We shouldn't decide this proposal now.*

この<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby><ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>める**べきではないでしょうか**。<br>*Shouldn't we decide this proposal now?*

---

## ～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～べきではないでしょうか (cont.)

The second sentence is arguing for a decision today. Japanese does this with negative questions generally - `いいんじゃない?` is approval, not doubt - but with `べき` the reversal catches learners because the words look so much like a refusal. In a meeting, hearing <code><ruby>見直<rt>みなお</rt></ruby>すべきではないでしょうか</code> means someone wants a review, and nodding along while believing the opposite is an expensive mistake.

---

## ～べきではない · Common mistakes

- ❌ そのことは<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わ**ないべきだ**。 → ✅ そのことは<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う**べきではない**。<br>*The negation attaches to `べき`, not to the verb. There is no ないべき form.*

- ❌（on a notice）ここでたばこを<ruby>吸<rt>す</rt></ruby>う**べきではありません**。 → ✅ ここでたばこを<ruby>吸<rt>す</rt></ruby>っ**てはいけません**。<br>*A posted rule is a prohibition. `べきではない` on a sign sounds like the building is sharing its personal views.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る**べきではない**。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ら**ないでほしい**。<br>*`べき` judges a chosen action. Weather chooses nothing, so there is nothing to judge - what you mean is a wish.*

---

## ～べきではない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌「この<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>はやめるべきではないでしょうか」（intending "we shouldn't cancel this plan"） → ✅ この<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>はやめる**べきではないと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います**。<br>*The `でしょうか` version argues *for* cancelling. To argue against something politely, keep the statement and soften it with <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>、そんな<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を**するべきではありません**。 → ✅ <ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>え**ていただけないでしょうか**。<br>*Aimed upwards, `べきではない` is a reprimand. Reframe it as a request rather than a verdict.*

---

## ～べきではない · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>から<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた<ruby>秘密<rt>ひみつ</rt></ruby>を、<ruby>勝手<rt>かって</rt></ruby>に<ruby>他人<rt>たにん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>____。

{reveal}

{reveal}
The sentence is a judgement about how one ought to behave, so it wants `べきではない` rather than `ないほうがいい`, which would only say that letting the secret out tends to backfire. <code><ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すべきではない</code> and <code><ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すべきではありません</code> differ only in politeness.

## ～べきではない · Practice 2

## ～べきではない · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つべきではない。 (b) この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つべきではないでしょうか。

{reveal}

# ～こと - written rules and instructions

**Meaning** · you are to do this - the sentence-ending `こと` of notices, house rules and exam papers

A verb followed by a bare `こと` and a full stop is not a noun phrase that has lost its verb. It is a complete sentence, and it issues an instruction. Nobody in particular is being addressed: the rule simply exists, and anyone it applies to is expected to follow it.

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Formation

Nothing follows the `こと`. No `だ`, no `です`, no `。ます`. The sentence stops.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + こと | <ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>すこと |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + こと | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わないこと |
| Verb (する) | ～すること | <ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>すること |
| Verb (with ておく) | ～ておくこと | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでおく → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでおくこと |
| Terse memo style | Noun + のこと | <ruby>至急<rt>しきゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby> → <ruby>至急<rt>しきゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>のこと |

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - if you can put `です` after it, it is not this pattern. <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにすること</code> is a rule; <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにすることです</code> is the *advice* pattern `～ことだ`, which is a different thing said to a different person.

> **Note** - only verbs work here. There is no ❌<code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かなこと</code> instruction; an adjective has to be turned into an action first - <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにすること</code>.

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Examples

<ruby>教室<rt>きょうしつ</rt></ruby>では<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かに**すること**。<br>*Be quiet in the classroom.*

<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>った<ruby>道具<rt>どうぐ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>元<rt>もと</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>戻<rt>もど</rt></ruby>すこと**。<br>*Return tools to where you found them.*

<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる<ruby>場合<rt>ばあい</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ず<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>**すること**。<br>*If you are going to be late, be sure to get in touch.*

<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby><ruby>中<rt>ちゅう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わないこと**。<br>*Dictionaries are not to be used during the examination.*

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>提出<rt>ていしゅつ</rt></ruby>する<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>に、<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてあるか<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かめる**こと**。<br>*Check that your name is written on it before handing it in.*

<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>められた<ruby>場所<rt>ばしょ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>め、<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>を**かけること**。<br>*Park bicycles in the designated area and lock them.*

アレルギーのある<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>込<rt>こ</rt></ruby>みの<ruby>際<rt>さい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ず<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>る**こと**。<br>*Anyone with an allergy must declare it when applying.*

<ruby>見学<rt>けんがく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>希望<rt>きぼう</rt></ruby>する<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>前日<rt>ぜんじつ</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いておく**こと**。<br>*Students wishing to attend the site visit are to sign up at reception by the day before.*

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>作業<rt>さぎょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わったら、<ruby>火<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>えているかどうかを<ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>する**こと**。<br>*When work is finished, two people are to confirm that the fire is out.*

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Nuance & register

This is a **written** form, and specifically the written form of an institution talking to whoever is covered by its rules. You meet it on classroom walls, in staff handbooks, on the front page of exam papers, in club regulations and in the list of conditions on an application form. It is often set out as a bulleted list, one `こと` per line.

It has no politeness level, because it has no addressee. That is worth pausing on: `てください` is polite because there is a person you are being polite *to*, whereas `こと` speaks past everyone to the rule itself. The effect is impersonal and slightly cold, which in a notice is exactly right.

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Nuance & register (cont.)

Said to an actual human being, it is another matter. A manager who writes <code><ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>すこと</code> in an email to one named person sounds curt and rather military. It is not ungrammatical - some workplaces use it deliberately for terse internal memos - but a learner should assume it will be heard as brusque, and write <code><ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>してください</code> instead.

Spoken, it survives in one narrow place: a teacher or leader reading a list of rules aloud, essentially quoting the notice. Outside that, it is not conversational Japanese.

> **Note** - the same word ends several other patterns that look identical on the page. `～ことだ` gives advice, `～ことがある` reports occasional events, `～ことにする` announces a decision. The instruction `こと` is the one with **nothing after it**.

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · ～こと vs ～てください

> **Rule of thumb** - `てください` asks **a person**; `こと` states **a rule**. If you could name who you are talking to, use `てください`.

すみません、この<ruby>紙<rt>かみ</rt></ruby>にお<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>い**てください**。<br>*Excuse me - please write your name on this form.*

<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>用紙<rt>ようし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>け<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>り、<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く**こと**。<br>*Collect a form at reception and write your name on it.*

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · ～こと vs ～てください (cont.)

The first is a receptionist speaking to the person in front of her. The second is what the form itself says. Note also that `てください` can be softened (<code><ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いていただけますか</code>) and `こと` cannot - there is no polite version, because there is nobody to be polite to.

---

## ～こと vs ～ようにしてください

> **Rule of thumb** - `こと` is a requirement. `～ようにしてください` asks people to **make an effort** in that direction, and leaves room for exceptions.

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>十分<rt>じゅっぷん</rt></ruby><ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>まる**こと**。<br>*Assemble by ten minutes before the meeting.*

なるべく<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めに<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>まる**ようにしてください**。<br>*Please try to gather a little early if you can.*

---

## ～こと vs ～ようにしてください (cont.)

The first admits no exceptions and reads as policy; the second is a request from one person to a group and expects some slippage. A written instruction in a customer-facing context - a hotel, a hospital - will usually choose `ようにしてください` or the still politer <code>お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いいたします</code>, because `こと` addresses people as rule subjects rather than as guests.

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · ～こと vs ～なければならない

> **Rule of thumb** - `こと` **issues** the requirement. `なければならない` **reports** that a requirement exists.

<ruby>参加者<rt>さんかしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>保険<rt>ほけん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>る**こと**。<br>*Participants are to take out insurance.*

<ruby>参加者<rt>さんかしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>保険<rt>ほけん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>ら**なければなりません**。<br>*Participants have to take out insurance.*

Both appear in regulations, and they are close enough that many documents use them interchangeably. The difference in feel: the first is the rule speaking in its own voice, and belongs in the list of conditions itself; the second is someone explaining the rule, and belongs in the covering letter or in speech. Only the second can be said aloud comfortably to an applicant on the phone.

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>教室<rt>きょうしつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>るときは<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>消<rt>け</rt></ruby>す**ことです**。 → ✅ <ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>消<rt>け</rt></ruby>す**こと**。<br>*Adding `です` turns a notice into personal advice. On a wall, the bare form is what you want.*

- ❌ <ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わ**なくこと** → ✅ <ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>わ**ないこと**<br>*The negative is the plain ない-form. `なく` belongs to `なくて` and `なくても`, not here.*

- ❌（email to one colleague）<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さん、<ruby>水曜日<rt>すいようび</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>をする**こと**。 → ✅ <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さん、<ruby>水曜日<rt>すいようび</rt></ruby>までにお<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>をお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします。<br>*Aimed at a named person, `こと` reads as an order barked down a corridor. Use a request form.*

---

## ～こと - written rules and instructions · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は**きれいなこと**。 → ✅ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>はきれいに**しておくこと**。<br>*Only verbs take the instruction `こと`. Turn the adjective into an action first.*

With `です` attached, the sentence becomes advice - "the thing to do is switch it off" - which is a reasonable sentence but not a rule. A notice ends bare at `こと`.

# ～ことだ

**Meaning** · the thing to do is… - advice offered as the obvious answer to a problem

`～ことだ` hands over a remedy. It does not weigh options or hedge; it names the one action that solves the problem, in the tone of somebody who has seen this situation before. That confidence is the whole flavour of the pattern, and it is also the reason it has to be used carefully.

---

## ～ことだ · Formation

Only verbs. The sentence almost always follows a clause that sets up the problem - a `たら`, a `なら`, a `ければ`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + ことだ | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むことだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ことだ | <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>をする → <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>をしないことだ |
| Verb (する) | ～することだ | <ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>することだ |
| Polite | ～ことです | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むことです |
| Softened | ～ことですね・～ことでしょうね | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むことですね |
| Typical frame | ～たい／～たら + ～ことだ | <ruby>治<rt>なお</rt></ruby>したいなら、<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むことだ |

---

## ～ことだ · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the `だ`/`です` is what makes this advice. Strip it and you get the notice pattern <code><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むこと。</code>, which is a rule on a wall rather than a suggestion to a friend.

---

## ～ことだ · Examples

<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぐなら、タクシーに<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>る**ことだ**。<br>*If you're in a hurry, take a taxi.*

<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>になりたいなら、<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>ける**ことだ**。<br>*If you want to get good, keep at it a little every day.*

<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>なら、<ruby>先<rt>さき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>して<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かめる**ことです**。<br>*If you're worried, ring ahead and check.*

---

<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>がなかなか<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えられないなら、<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>を<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かして<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby>も<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてみる**ことです**。<br>*If kanji won't stick, write them out by hand over and over.*

<ruby>面接<rt>めんせつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>不安<rt>ふあん</rt></ruby>なら、<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>して<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>してみる**ことです**。<br>*If you're nervous about the interview, run through it out loud the day before.*

---

## ～ことだ · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>嫌<rt>いや</rt></ruby>なことがあった<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>に<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えないで<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>てしまう**ことだ**。<br>*On a day when something horrible has happened, don't force yourself to think it through - just go to bed early.*

---

## ～ことだ · Nuance & register

The speaker of a `ことだ` sentence has taken a position: *I know the answer to this.* That is fine coming from a coach, a teacher, a doctor, a parent, or a friend who has been through the same thing, and it is the standard voice of advice columns and self-help writing. It is much less comfortable between strangers, and it is not appropriate aimed upwards - telling your section chief <code><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むことです</code> puts you in the role of the person who knows better.

`ことです` is polite in form, but politeness of form does not remove the stance. If you want to advise a senior colleague, leave this pattern alone and use <code>お<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みになってはいかがでしょうか</code> or <code>ほうがよろしいかと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code>.

---

## ～ことだ · Nuance & register (cont.)

The advice is **general**, not a request about one occasion. It answers "what should I do about X?" rather than "will you do X for me?" So <code><ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けることだ</code> is not a way to ask someone to open a window; it is what you say when the room's stuffiness is a recurring problem.

You cannot use it about **yourself**. ❌<code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ることです</code> is not a plan; for that you want `つもりだ` or `ようにしている`.

---

## ～ことだ · Nuance & register (cont.)

> **Note** - a different `ことだ` turns up in exclamations: <code>なんと<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かなことだろう</code>, <code><ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>ったことだ</code>. That one takes adjectives and な-adjectives, sits at the end of a written or old-fashioned sentence, and expresses feeling rather than advice. Context separates them easily - advice always has an action verb in front.

---

## ～ことだ · ～ことだ vs ～ほうがいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ほうがいい` **compares**: this option beats that one. `ことだ` **prescribes**: this is what solves it, and no comparison is offered.

<ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んだ**ほうがいいですよ**。<br>*You'd be better off taking something for it.*

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>下<rt>さ</rt></ruby>がらないなら、<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**ことです**。<br>*If the fever isn't coming down, go to a doctor.*

`ほうがいい` leaves the decision with the listener and is the gentler everyday choice - it is what you say to a friend at their desk. `ことだ` closes the question. That is why it fits the second sentence, where the situation has already gone past the point of weighing things up.

---

## ～ことだ · ～ことだ vs ～べきだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `ことだ` is **practical**: here is the route to the goal *you* said you wanted. `べきだ` is **moral**: here is what is right, whether you want it or not.

<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>かりたいなら、<ruby>過去<rt>かこ</rt></ruby><ruby>問<rt>もん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>解<rt>と</rt></ruby>く**ことだ**。<br>*If you want to pass the exam, work through past papers.*

<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>した<ruby>以上<rt>いじょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>最後<rt>さいご</rt></ruby>までやる**べきだ**。<br>*Having promised, you ought to see it through.*

The first is conditional on your goal: no goal, no advice, and you are free to drop the exam. The second holds regardless of what you want - that is what makes it a judgement. In practice `ことだ` follows `～たいなら` and `べきだ` follows `～のだから`.

---

## ～ことだ · ～ことだ vs ～ものだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `ことだ` advises **this person about this problem**. `ものだ` states a **general truth or social norm** that applies to everyone.

<ruby>先輩<rt>せんぱい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ったら、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>から<ruby>挨拶<rt>あいさつ</rt></ruby>する**ものだ**。<br>*When you meet someone senior, you greet them first - that's how it's done.*

<ruby>職場<rt>しょくば</rt></ruby>になじみたいなら、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>から<ruby>挨拶<rt>あいさつ</rt></ruby>する**ことだ**。<br>*If you want to settle in at work, be the one who says hello first.*

The same action, two different arguments. `ものだ` appeals to custom and would be just as true if you did not care about fitting in; `ことだ` is a piece of tactical advice tied to the goal in the first clause. `ものだ` therefore sounds old-fashioned and moralising in a way `ことだ` does not - and can be dropped into a scolding, which `ことだ` cannot.

---

## ～ことだ · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きる**ことです**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きる**つもりです**。<br>*`ことだ` is advice you give someone else. It cannot announce your own plan.*

- ❌ すみません、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>をコピーする**ことです**。 → ✅ すみません、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>をコピーし**ていただけますか**。<br>*A request about one occasion is not advice. `ことだ` prescribes a general solution, not a favour.*

- ❌ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>な**ことです**。 → ✅ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>す**ことです**。<br>*Advice `ことだ` takes a verb. An adjective has to become an action first.*

With it, the wall appears to be offering friendly guidance.*

# ～ことはない

**Meaning** · there's no need to; it never happens - two readings of one shape, sorted out by who the subject is

With **you** as the subject, `～ことはない` is reassurance: the effort or the worry is unnecessary, so stop. With **somebody or something else** as the subject, it is a statement about the world: that does not happen. The words are identical; the subject decides.

---

## ～ことはない · Formation

The verb is in the **dictionary form** in both senses.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - "no need to" | dictionary form + ことはない | <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>することはない |
| Verb - "never happens" | dictionary form + ことはない | <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる → <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れることはない |
| Verb (negative) - "it's not that ～ can't" | ない-form + ことはない | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>めない → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>めないことはない |
| Polite | ～ことはありません | <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>することはありません |
| Casual, は dropped | ～ことない | <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>することない |
| Very formal | ～ことはございません | <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>することはございません |

---

## ～ことはない · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - with <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して, めったに or ほとんど in front of it, only the "never happens" reading survives: <ruby>決<rt>けっ</rt></ruby>して<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れることはない. With よ, だいじょうぶ or a comforting tone, only the "no need to" reading survives. Japanese almost never leaves the two genuinely ambiguous.

---

## ～ことはない · Examples

<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>する**ことはない**よ。<br>*There's no need to worry.*

そんなに<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぐ**ことはありません**。<br>*There's no need to rush like that.*

まだ<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>があるから、あわてて<ruby>準備<rt>じゅんび</rt></ruby>する**ことはない**。<br>*There's still time, so there's no need to go rushing at the preparations.*

<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>したくらいで、あきらめる**ことはない**よ。<br>*One failure is no reason to give up.*

---

## ～ことはない · Examples (cont.)

わざわざ<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>迎<rt>むか</rt></ruby>えに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ていただく**ことはありません**。<br>*There's really no need for you to come all the way to the station to meet me.*

あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる**ことはない**。<br>*He is never late for an appointment.*

この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>冬<rt>ふゆ</rt></ruby>でも<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>積<rt>つ</rt></ruby>もる**ことはありません**。<br>*Round here the snow never settles, even in winter.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>厳<rt>きび</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>ですが、<ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>もなく<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>る**ことはありません**。<br>*Our manager is strict, but he never gets angry without a reason.*

---

## ～ことはない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>しても<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かに<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>われる**ことはない**ので、<ruby>安心<rt>あんしん</rt></ruby>して<ruby>挑戦<rt>ちょうせん</rt></ruby>してみてください。<br>*Nobody is going to laugh at you if it goes wrong, so give it a try without worrying.*

---

## ～ことはない · Nuance & register

The "no need to" sense is **consolation**, and it is one of the kindest patterns in the language. Its natural home is comforting someone who is upset, anxious, or apologising too much: <code><ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>くことないよ</code>, <code><ruby>謝<rt>あやま</rt></ruby>ることはありません</code>. In speech the `は` regularly drops - <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>にすることないよ</code> - and that shortened version is what you will actually hear between friends.

The same words can turn cool. Said flatly about someone else's plan, <code>わざわざ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くことはない</code> means "there's no point going", and the warmth disappears. Tone and context carry that difference entirely; the grammar does not mark it.

---

## ～ことはない · Nuance & register (cont.)

The "never happens" sense is a statement of reliability. It is stronger than `あまり～ない` and softer than an absolute: it claims a pattern, not a law of physics. It reads well in descriptions, product blurbs and character sketches, and it takes `ほとんど` comfortably - <ruby>故障<rt>こしょう</rt></ruby>することはほとんどありません.

Two register cautions. Turning down help from someone senior with `ことはありません` is grammatical but a little blunt; the set humble phrase is <code>～には<ruby>及<rt>およ</rt></ruby>びません</code>. And `ことはない` **never** means "must not" - reading <code><ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れることはない</code> as a warning about lateness reverses the sentence.

---

## ～ことはない · Nuance & register (cont.)

> **Note** - the negative row in the table is a separate, very common idiom. `～ないことはない` is a double negative meaning "it's not that I can't": <ruby>辛<rt>から</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>が<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べられないことはないんですが、あまり <ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby>ではありません. It is a hedge, and it usually signals a polite reluctance rather than genuine ability.

---

## ～ことはない · ～ことはない vs ～なくてもいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `なくてもいい` **permits** you to skip it. `ことはない` says the action would be **pointless or unwarranted**, and normally comes with a reason.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>**なくてもいいですよ**。<br>*You don't have to come in tomorrow.*

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるのに<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る**ことはありませんよ**。<br>*There's no call for you to come in with a fever.*

The first grants permission - the speaker has the authority to excuse you, and is doing so. The second argues that coming would be unreasonable, and it carries concern for the listener. That is why `ことはない` fits consolation and `なくてもいい` fits a manager approving a day off. Note also that `なくてもいい` answers a question; `ことはない` heads off an intention.

---

## ～ことはない · ～ことはない vs ～ことがない

> **Rule of thumb** - `ことがない` with the **た-form** is experience: you have never done it. `ことはない` with the **dictionary form** is about need or frequency.

<ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**ことがありません**。<br>*I've never been to Hokkaido.*

<ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>では、<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>冷房<rt>れいぼう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>う**ことはありません**。<br>*In Hokkaido nobody uses air conditioning in summer.*

---

## ～ことはない · ～ことはない vs ～ことがない (cont.)

The first looks back over your life; the second describes a standing state of affairs. The `は`/`が` swap is not decorative - `が` presents the experience as a fact you possess, `は` sets the situation against expectation ("as for that happening: it doesn't"). If you meet <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったことはない</code> with `は` and the た-form, it is still experience, now with a contrastive edge: "been there, no - heard of it, yes."

---

## ～ことはない · ～ことはない vs ～はずがない

> **Rule of thumb** - `ことはない` reports that something **does not happen**, from observation. `はずがない` says it **could not possibly** be so, from reasoning.

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れる**ことはない**。<br>*She never forgets an arrangement.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れる**はずがない**。<br>*There's no way she's forgotten the arrangement.*

The first is a general character reference and could be said about her at any time. The second is about **this** occasion and is an inference - you are ruling out a possibility that has just been raised. So `はずがない` answers "do you think she forgot?", while `ことはない` answers "what's she like?"

---

## ～ことはない · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる**ことはない**。（intending "you mustn't be late"） → ✅ <ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れ**てはいけない**。<br>*`ことはない` never prohibits. As written, the sentence praises someone's punctuality.*

- ❌ <ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>いた**ことはない**よ、<ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>。 → ✅ <ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>く**ことはない**よ、<ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>。<br>*Consolation takes the dictionary form. With the た-form the sentence flips to experience - "I have never cried" - which is not what a crying friend needs to hear.*

- ❌ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しい**ことはない**。 → ✅ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>し**くはない**。<br>*The pattern takes verbs. An い-adjective negates on its own stem.*

---

## ～ことはない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌（to a senior colleague）<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、わざわざお<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>しいただく**ことはありません**。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、お<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>しいただくには**<ruby>及<rt>およ</rt></ruby>びません**。<br>*Declining a superior's offer with `ことはありません` sounds like you are telling them not to bother. <code>～には<ruby>及<rt>およ</rt></ruby>びません</code> is the set humble phrase for exactly this.*

---

## ～ことはない · Practice 1

# ～しかない

**Meaning** · there's nothing for it but to… - the circumstances have closed every other door

`しか` means "nothing other than", and it always drags a negative along with it. Put it after a verb and the sentence says: of all the things one might do here, exactly one remains. Nobody ordered it and nobody judged it - the situation simply ran out of options.

---

## ～しかない · Formation

The verb stays in the dictionary form. `しか` here does the same work it does after a noun, which you already know from <code><ruby>千円<rt>せんえん</rt></ruby>しかない</code>.

---

## ～しかない · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + しかない | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つしかない |
| Verb (する) | ～するしかない | <ruby>我慢<rt>がまん</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>我慢<rt>がまん</rt></ruby>するしかない |
| Noun | Noun + しかない | <ruby>千円<rt>せんえん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>千円<rt>せんえん</rt></ruby>しかない |
| Past | ～しかなかった | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つしかなかった |
| Polite | ～しかありません | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つしかありません |
| Hedged guess | ～しかなさそうだ・～しかないだろう | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つしかなさそうだ |
| Formal written equivalent | ～ほかない・～よりほかない | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つよりほかない |

---

## ～しかない · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - there is no positive form. ❌<code><ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つしかある</code> does not exist; `しか` is grammatically bound to `ない`. The negative is doing the work of "other than", not of "not".

---

## ～しかない · Examples

バスがないから、<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く**しかない**。<br>*There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.*

こうなったら、やる**しかありません**。<br>*Now that it's come to this, we just have to do it.*

<ruby>終電<rt>しゅうでん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>逃<rt>のが</rt></ruby>したので、タクシーで<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**しかなかった**。<br>*I missed the last train, so there was nothing for it but to take a taxi home.*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>がやむまで、ここで<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ**しかないですね**。<br>*Looks like we've no choice but to wait here until the rain stops.*

---

## ～しかない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>部品<rt>ぶひん</rt></ruby>がもう<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>られていないので、<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいのを<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う**しかありません**。<br>*The part isn't made any more, so there's nothing for it but to buy a new one.*

<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれないなら、<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>やる**しかない**。<br>*If nobody's going to help, I'll just have to do the whole thing on my own.*

<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでも<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からないので、メーカーに<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>して<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く**しかなさそうです**。<br>*I've read the manual over and over and still don't understand it, so I'll probably just have to ring the maker.*

<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>売上<rt>うりあげ</rt></ruby>がここまで<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ちてしまった<ruby>以上<rt>いじょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>営業<rt>えいぎょう</rt></ruby><ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>短<rt>みじか</rt></ruby>くする**しかないでしょう**。<br>*With takings down this far, we're going to have to cut the opening hours.*

---

## ～しかない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>つには、<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>り<ruby>五分<rt>ごふん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>二点<rt>にてん</rt></ruby><ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>る**しかない**。<br>*To win the match, we have to score twice in the last five minutes - there's no other way.*

---

## ～しかない · Nuance & register

The feeling is **resignation**, but not always gloom. The same sentence can be a sigh or a squaring of the shoulders, depending on delivery: `やるしかない` is one of the most common things a Japanese speaker says before starting something difficult, and it is closer to "right, let's get on with it" than to despair. What is constant is that the alternatives have been considered and found gone.

That is the pattern's boundary. `しかない` reports what **circumstances** have left you. It does not report a rule, an order, or a moral duty. A deadline set by your boss is <code><ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>さなければならない</code>; a duty you feel is <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないわけにはいかない</code>; a situation in which the only remaining option is to hand it in unfinished is <code><ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>すしかない</code>.

---

## ～しかない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral, and the polite form is simply `しかありません`. Two siblings sit above it in formality: `～ほかない` and `～よりほかない` are written, slightly literary equivalents, and <code>～ざるを<ruby>得<rt>え</rt></ruby>ない</code> is formal and adds a sense of being compelled against your will - an inspection you must accept, a resignation you must tender. In conversation, `しかない` covers almost everything those two do.

One small caution. Applied to somebody else's situation, `しかない` can sound dismissive - `あきらめるしかないですよ` tells a person their problem is over. Softening it to `しかないかもしれませんね` leaves them their options.

---

## ～しかない · ～しかない vs ～なければならない

> **Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` comes from **outside**: a rule, an order, a deadline. `しかない` comes from the **situation**: the other options have disappeared.

<ruby>外国人<rt>がいこくじん</rt></ruby><ruby>登録<rt>とうろく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>本人<rt>ほんにん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>さ**なければなりません**。<br>*The registration paperwork has to be submitted by the person themselves.*

<ruby>郵便<rt>ゆうびん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わないので、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていく**しかありません**。<br>*The post won't get there in time, so I'll have to take it in myself.*

The first would still be true if the post were quick, the office were near and you had all week; a regulation does not care. The second exists only because of a timing problem, and it would evaporate if the problem did. Ask yourself: **who says so?** If the answer is a person or a rulebook, use `なければならない`.

---

## ～しかない · ～しかない vs ～ざるを得ない

> **Rule of thumb** - both mean "have no choice", but `しかない` is about **options running out**, while <code>～ざるを<ruby>得<rt>え</rt></ruby>ない</code> is about being **compelled against your own wishes**, usually by logic, duty or authority. It is also distinctly formal.

<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わらないので、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>きをやる**しかない**。<br>*It isn't going to be finished, so I'll just carry on tomorrow.*

これだけの<ruby>証拠<rt>しょうこ</rt></ruby>がそろえば、<ruby>事実<rt>じじつ</rt></ruby>だと**<ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めざるを<ruby>得<rt>え</rt></ruby>ない**。<br>*With this much evidence, one is forced to accept it as fact.*

---

## ～しかない · ～しかない vs ～ざるを得ない (cont.)

<code>ざるを<ruby>得<rt>え</rt></ruby>ない</code> belongs to reports, editorials and formal speech, and it carries a note of reluctance that `しかない` does not require. Saying it about tomorrow's leftover work would sound as though the work had defeated you in some tragic way. Conversely, `しかない` in the evidence sentence would make the conclusion sound like a practical inconvenience rather than an intellectual surrender.

---

## ～しかない · ～しかない vs ～だけだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `だけだ` says **that is all that's left to do**, neutrally, and often happily. `しかない` says **there is nothing else you could do**, with the other options mourned.

<ruby>準備<rt>じゅんび</rt></ruby>は<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わった。あとは<ruby>本番<rt>ほんばん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ**だけだ**。<br>*The preparation's done. All that's left is to wait for the day.*

<ruby>準備<rt>じゅんび</rt></ruby>が<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わなかった。このまま<ruby>本番<rt>ほんばん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>る**しかない**。<br>*We didn't get everything ready. We'll just have to go on as we are.*

---

## ～しかない · ～しかない vs ～だけだ (cont.)

`だけだ` looks forward from a job well done; `しかない` looks back at options that closed. The two are not interchangeable even where both are grammatical - telling a nervous performer <code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しむだけだよ</code> is encouragement, while <code><ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しむしかないよ</code> implies the performance itself is a write-off.

---

## ～しかない · Common mistakes

- ❌ バスがないから<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>か**ないしかない**。 → ✅ バスがないから<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>く**しかない**。<br>*`しかない` attaches to the **dictionary form**. The negative is already inside `ない` at the end; putting another one on the verb reverses the sentence.*

- ❌ さいふには<ruby>百円<rt>ひゃくえん</rt></ruby>**しかあります**。 → ✅ さいふには<ruby>百円<rt>ひゃくえん</rt></ruby>**しかありません**。<br>*`しか` can never end in a positive. It is one half of a pair, and `ない` is the other half.*

- ❌ パスポートの<ruby>申請<rt>しんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>本人<rt>ほんにん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**しかありません**。（reporting the rule） → ✅ パスポートの<ruby>申請<rt>しんせい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>本人<rt>ほんにん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>か**なければなりません**。<br>*A published requirement is an obligation from outside, not a situation that ran out of options.*

---

## ～しかない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ このお<ruby>菓子<rt>かし</rt></ruby>はここに**しか<ruby>売<rt>う</rt></ruby>っています**。 → ✅ このお<ruby>菓子<rt>かし</rt></ruby>はここで**しか<ruby>売<rt>う</rt></ruby>っていません**。<br>*Two things: the sentence needs its negative, and `しか` follows the particle it attaches to - `ここで`, since the shop is the location of selling.*

---

{reveal}
<ruby>駅前<rt>えきまえ</rt></ruby>のホテルに<ruby>泊<rt>と</rt></ruby>まる**しかない**。

{reveal}
The trains have stopped, so getting home is off the table - the situation has closed the other doors, which is exactly `しかない`. Note the dictionary form <code><ruby>泊<rt>と</rt></ruby>まる</code> in front of it.

---

## ～しかない · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>規則<rt>きそく</rt></ruby>で、<ruby>出張<rt>しゅっちょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>報告書<rt>ほうこくしょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>三日<rt>みっか</rt></ruby><ruby>以内<rt>いない</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>（さなければなりません・すしかありません）。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>規則<rt>きそく</rt></ruby>で</code> names the source of the obligation, and a rule imposed from outside is `なければならない`. <code><ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>すしかありません</code> would suggest that circumstances happened to leave you with no other way of dealing with the report.

## ～しかない · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "The shop was closed, so we had no choice but to eat at the station."

{reveal}
<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>まっていたので、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>で<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる**しかありませんでした**。

{reveal}
The past polite form is `しかありませんでした`; plain past is `しかなかった`. The closed shop is a circumstance, not a rule, which is what makes `しかない` right here.

## ～しかない · Practice 4

{reveal}

# ～わけにはいかない

**Meaning** · I can't very well do that - it is perfectly possible, and I still can't

This is not a pattern about ability and not a pattern about rules. Everything `わけにはいかない` describes is physically available to you: you could take the day off, you could tell the secret, you could go home first. What stops you is a promise, a position, a debt of gratitude, or your own sense of what is decent. It is obligation as you *feel* it, rather than obligation as somebody imposes it.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Formation

`いく` here is written in kana. It is the verb of "things not going", not of going somewhere, and it never becomes いける.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | dictionary form + わけにはいかない | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むわけにはいかない |
| Verb (negative) - "I have to" | ない-form + わけにはいかない | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないわけにはいかない |
| Verb (する) | ～するわけにはいかない | <ruby>無視<rt>むし</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>無視<rt>むし</rt></ruby>するわけにはいかない |
| Polite | ～わけにはいきません | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むわけにはいきません |
| Past | ～わけにはいかなかった | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むわけにはいかなかった |
| Casual, は dropped | ～わけにいかない | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むわけにいかない |
| Added to a list | ～わけにもいかない | <ruby>断<rt>ことわ</rt></ruby>るわけにもいかない |
| Humble / very formal | ～わけにはまいりません | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むわけにはまいりません |

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - ❌`わけにはいけない` is the single most common error with this pattern. The verb is `いく` in its negative `いかない`; there is no potential form here.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Examples

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む**わけにはいかない**。<br>*I can't very well take today off.*

これは<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>す**わけにはいきません**。<br>*This isn't something I can tell anyone.*

<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>で<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たので、お<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む**わけにはいかない**。<br>*I drove here, so I can hardly have a drink.*

<ruby>図書館<rt>としょかん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>だから、<ruby>線<rt>せん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>く**わけにはいかない**。<br>*It's a library book, so I can't go underlining it.*

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>後輩<rt>こうはい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>って<ruby>働<rt>はたら</rt></ruby>いているのに、<ruby>先<rt>さき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**わけにはいきません**。<br>*My juniors are still here working - I can't just leave ahead of them.*

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>から<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>した<ruby>以上<rt>いじょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>さらやめる**わけにはいかない**。<br>*I was the one who proposed it, so backing out now is out of the question.*

<ruby>大事<rt>だいじ</rt></ruby>な<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>預<rt>あず</rt></ruby>かっているので、この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>空<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ける**わけにはいきません**。<br>*I'm looking after some important documents, so I can't leave this room unattended.*

わざわざ<ruby>招待<rt>しょうたい</rt></ruby>してくれたのだから、<ruby>出席<rt>しゅっせき</rt></ruby>し**ないわけにはいかない**。<br>*They went to the trouble of inviting me, so I can't really not go.*

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>はありますが、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>打<rt>う</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わせにだけは<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>**ないわけにはいきません**。<br>*I do have a fever, but today's meeting is the one I can't get out of.*

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Nuance & register

Ask, of any sentence you are tempted to build with this: **could I physically do it?** If the answer is no, the pattern is wrong. A person who cannot swim does not say <code><ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐわけにはいかない</code>; they say <code><ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げません</code>. The pattern's whole content is the gap between *could* and *can't bring myself to*.

For the same reason the subject is nearly always **the speaker**. You are reporting the inside of your own head - the obligation you feel, the awkwardness you would not survive. Said about somebody else it presumes to know their conscience, which is why <code><ruby>君<rt>きみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>るわけにはいかない</code> sounds less like an observation than like pressure. Describing a third person's position is possible when you have grounds: <code><ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>としては<ruby>認<rt>みと</rt></ruby>めるわけにはいかないだろう</code> - note the `だろう` doing the hedging.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Nuance & register (cont.)

The `も` variant is worth having. `～わけにもいかない` adds this impossibility to one already mentioned, and it is how the pattern usually appears when you are boxed in from both sides: <ruby>断<rt>ことわ</rt></ruby>るわけにもいかず、<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>き<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>けてしまった.

Register is neutral in itself. The polite `わけにはいきません` is completely ordinary at work; `わけにはまいりません` is humble and belongs to formal apologies and customer-facing speech. In casual conversation the `は` often drops - <code><ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>るわけにいかないんだよ</code>.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Nuance & register (cont.)

> **Note** - the double negative `～ないわけにはいかない` means "I have to", and the reluctance is part of the meaning. It is the sentence of someone who has looked for a way out and not found one. Do not use it for things you are glad to do: ❌<code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わないわけにはいかない</code> is a strange thing to say about a date.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · ～わけにはいかない vs ～てはいけない

> **Rule of thumb** - `てはいけない` is a **prohibition from outside**: a rule, a parent, a sign. `わけにはいかない` is **you stopping yourself**, for reasons you would have to explain.

<ruby>館内<rt>かんない</rt></ruby>では<ruby>飲食<rt>いんしょく</rt></ruby>し**てはいけません**。<br>*Eating and drinking are not allowed inside.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>途中<rt>とちゅう</rt></ruby>なので、<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>つ**わけにはいきません**。<br>*We're in the middle of a meeting, so I can hardly get up and leave.*

Nothing forbids leaving a meeting; you simply cannot do it and remain the person you want to be at that table. Put `てはいけない` there and you invent a rule that does not exist. Conversely, `わけにはいかない` on a museum notice would be peculiar - the building has no feelings to protect.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · ～わけにはいかない vs ～てはいけない (cont.)

A quick test: if you can name **who** forbids it, use `てはいけない`.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～なければならない

> **Rule of thumb** - `なければならない` is **neutral necessity**; it says nothing about how you feel. `～ないわけにはいかない` says you would rather not, and cannot see a way out.

<ruby>申込書<rt>もうしこみしょ</rt></ruby>には<ruby>印鑑<rt>いんかん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>さ**なければなりません**。<br>*You have to put your seal on the application form.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>直接<rt>ちょくせつ</rt></ruby><ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>まれたので、<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>き<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>け**ないわけにはいかなかった**。<br>*The manager asked me personally, so I couldn't very well refuse.*

The first is a form's requirement and carries no emotion at all - it would be identical on a website's help page. The second is a small story about pressure: you did not want the job, you looked for an exit, and the way it was asked closed it. Swap them and the form sentence becomes oddly resentful, while the second becomes a flat report that misses the whole point.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～なければならない (cont.)

This is also why `ないわけにはいかない` almost always has a **reason clause** in front of it. Without one, the reluctance has nothing to attach to.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · ～わけにはいかない vs ～ことができない

> **Rule of thumb** - `ことができない` is genuine **inability**: the skill, the permission or the physics is missing. `わけにはいかない` is **unwillingness on principle**, with the ability intact.

<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めないので、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>署名<rt>しょめい</rt></ruby>する**ことができません**。<br>*I can't read the kanji, so I'm unable to sign this document.*

<ruby>中身<rt>なかみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>していないので、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>署名<rt>しょめい</rt></ruby>する**わけにはいきません**。<br>*I haven't checked what's in it, so I'm not prepared to sign this document.*

The first speaker cannot sign. The second could sign in a second, and is refusing on principle. That distinction is often the difference between an apology and a stand, and choosing the wrong one misrepresents you badly - the first sentence sounds helpless where you meant to sound careful.

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**わけにはいけません**。 → ✅ <ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**わけにはいきません**。<br>*The verb is `いく`, negated as `いかない／いきません`. There is no potential form in this pattern.*

- ❌ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げないので、<ruby>向<rt>む</rt></ruby>こう<ruby>岸<rt>ぎし</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**わけにはいかない**。 → ✅ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げないので、<ruby>向<rt>む</rt></ruby>こう<ruby>岸<rt>ぎし</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**ことができない**。<br>*Real inability is `ことができない`. `わけにはいかない` requires that you *could* do the thing.*

---

## ～わけにはいかない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>君<rt>きみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>破<rt>やぶ</rt></ruby>る**わけにはいかないよ**。 → ✅ <ruby>君<rt>きみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>破<rt>やぶ</rt></ruby>っ**てはいけないよ**。<br>*Aimed at someone else, the pattern claims to know what their conscience will allow. If you mean to tell them not to, say so directly.*

- ❌ <ruby>大好<rt>だいす</rt></ruby>きなケーキだから、<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ないわけにはいかない**。 → ✅ <ruby>大好<rt>だいす</rt></ruby>きなケーキだから、<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ずにはいられない**。<br>*`ないわけにはいかない` carries reluctance. For something you cannot resist, you want the "can't help it" pattern instead.*

# ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません

**Meaning** · it's fine either way; I don't mind - permission given by disclaiming any objection

`かまう` means "to mind, to be bothered". So `～てもかまわない` does not say the action is *good*; it says the speaker has **no objection** to it. That small difference in angle is why the pattern is at home in written rules and business Japanese, and why it can sound a shade cooler than a warm "go ahead".

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Formation

`かまわない` sits on top of the same `～ても` you already know. Everything before it takes the ordinary `ても` shapes.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + もかまわない | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってもかまわない |
| Verb (negative) | なくて + もかまわない | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なくてもかまわない |
| い-adjective | ～くて + もかまわない | <ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>くてもかまわない |
| な-adjective | ～で + もかまわない | <ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>でもかまわない |
| Noun | Noun + でもかまわない | <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby> → <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>でもかまわない |
| Polite | ～てもかまいません | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってもかまいません |
| Asking permission | ～てもかまいませんか | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってもかまいませんか |
| Past | ～てもかまわなかった | <ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってもかまわなかった |

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the kanji is <ruby>構<rt>かま</rt></ruby>う, but this pattern is written in kana far more often than not. Both `かまいません` and <code><ruby>構<rt>かま</rt></ruby>いません</code> are correct; kana is the safer default.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Examples

ここに<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>っ**てもかまいませんか**。<br>*Do you mind if I sit here?*

<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>では<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>っ**てもかまいません**。<br>*You may use a dictionary in the exam.*

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がなければ、<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>やらなく**てもかまいません**。<br>*If you're short of time, you don't have to do all of it.*

<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>く**てもかまわない**ので、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>いところがいいです。<br>*I don't mind a small flat - I'd rather have somewhere near the station.*

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Examples (cont.)

お<ruby>支払<rt>しはら</rt></ruby>いは<ruby>現金<rt>げんきん</rt></ruby>でもカードでも**かまいません**。<br>*You're welcome to pay by cash or by card.*

<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>き<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しくらい<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>っ**てもかまいません**から、まず<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>してみてください。<br>*It doesn't matter if the format isn't quite right - just hand something in first.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>集合<rt>しゅうごう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>八時<rt>はちじ</rt></ruby>でも<ruby>九時<rt>くじ</rt></ruby>でも**かまわない**と<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていました。<br>*The manager said eight or nine would both be fine for tomorrow's start.*

<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>っ**てもかまいませんか**。<ruby>後<rt>あと</rt></ruby>でブログに<ruby>載<rt>の</rt></ruby>せたいのですが。<br>*Would you mind if I took a photo? I'd like to put it on my blog later.*

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぎではありませんので、お<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>になっ**てもかまいません**。<br>*There's no rush, so it's perfectly all right if you reply next week.*

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Nuance & register

`てもいい` and `てもかまわない` are close, but they are not saying quite the same thing. `てもいい` calls the action **good** - a green light. `てもかまわない` says the speaker **is not bothered** - the light was never red in the first place. Out of that comes the pattern's characteristic flatness: it is generous without being warm.

That flatness is an advantage in **writing and business**, which is where you meet it most: exam instructions, application forms, hotel policies, emails from suppliers. <code>キャンセルは<ruby>前日<rt>ぜんじつ</rt></ruby>までにご<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>いただければかまいません</code> sounds like an institution, and is meant to.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Nuance & register (cont.)

It is also, spoken with the wrong intonation, the sentence of someone who is politely annoyed. <code><ruby>別<rt>べつ</rt></ruby>にかまいませんけど</code> ("I don't *mind*, but…") is a recognisable way of not being fine at all. Learners often reach for `かまいません` when they mean to be reassuring and land somewhere colder than intended; if warmth is the goal, `もちろんいいですよ` or <code><ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>ですよ</code> does the job better.

As a **question**, `～てもかまいませんか` is politer and more formal than `～てもいいですか`, and slightly less formal than `～てもよろしいでしょうか`. All three are usable at work; the middle one is the safe everyday choice with someone you do not know well.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Nuance & register (cont.)

One direction to avoid: **granting** permission to someone senior. Telling your manager <code><ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>ってもかまいませんよ</code> puts you in charge of the chairs. Answering their question with a bare `かまいません` is normal and fine - it is the unprompted granting that presumes.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · ～てもかまわない vs ～てもいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `てもいい` says the action is **all right**. `てもかまわない` says the speaker **doesn't mind**. The first is permission; the second is the absence of objection.

そのペン、<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>っ**てもいいよ**。<br>*Sure, use that pen.*

どのペンを<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>っ**てもかまいません**。<br>*It doesn't matter which pen you use.*

The first is a friend handing something over. The second is an invigilator stating that pen choice is not regulated. Notice how naturally `かまわない` takes どの, どちら, いつ and でも - it is the form of choice for saying "any of these is acceptable", because indifference is exactly what it expresses.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · ～てもかまわない vs ～てもいい (cont.)

In casual conversation between friends, `てもいい` and simply `いいよ` are far commoner. `かまわないよ` is not wrong there, but it sounds a little grown-up and measured.

---

## ～てもかまいませんか vs ～てもよろしいでしょうか

> **Rule of thumb** - same request, three rungs of politeness: `～てもいいですか` ＜ `～てもかまいませんか` ＜ `～てもよろしいでしょうか`.

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>け**てもいいですか**。<br>*Can I open the window?*

<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>け**てもかまいませんか**。<br>*Would you mind if I opened the window a little?*

<ruby>恐<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>れ<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>りますが、<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>け**てもよろしいでしょうか**。<br>*Excuse me - would it be all right if I opened the window?*

---

## ～てもかまいませんか vs ～てもよろしいでしょうか (cont.)

Treat these as a ladder rather than as synonyms. The first is what you say to a friend or a shop assistant, the second to a colleague or a stranger, the third to a customer or in a formal setting. Going too far up the ladder is a mild error too: `よろしいでしょうか` to a close friend sounds like a joke.

---

## ～てもかまわない vs ～ても大丈夫

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different worlds. <code>ても<ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby></code> is the spoken, everyday version; `てもかまわない` is the written and formal one.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れ**ても<ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>ですか**。<br>*Is it OK if I'm a bit late tomorrow?*

<ruby>提出<rt>ていしゅつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>になっ**てもかまいません**。<br>*It is acceptable for submission to be tomorrow.*

---

## ～てもかまわない vs ～ても大丈夫 (cont.)

<code><ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby></code> has spread enormously in modern spoken Japanese and now covers most of what `かまわない` used to do in conversation. The reverse swap is what sounds off: an official notice reading <code><ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>です</code> feels chatty, and in a contract it would be unusable.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**でもかまいません**。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>っ**てもかまいません**。<br>*Verbs come in on the て-form. `でもかまわない` is for nouns and な-adjectives: <code><ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>でもかまいません</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い**でもかまいません**。 → ✅ <ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>く**てもかまいません**。<br>*An い-adjective becomes `～くても` first. The `でも` route is not open to it.*

- ❌ <ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まなく**でもかまいません**。 → ✅ <ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まなく**てもかまいません**。<br>*The negative is `～なくても`. `なくで` does not exist.*

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌（to your manager, unprompted）<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、こちらの<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>っ**てもかまいませんよ**。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、こちらの<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>に**どうぞ**。<br>*Handing out permission upwards presumes authority you do not have. Offer, don't permit.*

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Practice 1

**1.** コピーの<ruby>紙<rt>かみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>裏<rt>うら</rt></ruby>が<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってあるものでも____。

{reveal}
<ruby>裏<rt>うら</rt></ruby>が<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>ってあるものでも**かまいません**。

{reveal}
The thing being permitted is a **noun** - paper of a certain kind - so it takes `でもかまいません` rather than the て-form. The sentence says the speaker has no objection either way, which is precisely this pattern rather than `いいです`.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: （<ruby>友達<rt>ともだち</rt></ruby>に）このおかし、<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ（てもいい?・てもかまいませんか?）

{reveal}
<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**てもいい?**

{reveal}
To a friend, `てもいい?` is the natural register. `てもかまいませんか` is a step up in formality and would sound as though you had suddenly started addressing them as a client - which is occasionally a joke, but not the default.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "You don't have to write your name if you'd rather not."

{reveal}

{reveal}
The negative permission is `～なくてもかまいません`. On a survey form this is exactly the register you want - it disclaims any requirement without sounding as if the form is doing you a favour.

---

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かくてもかまいませんから、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いほうがいいです。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かでもかまいません**から、<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いほうがいいです。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か</code> is a な-adjective, so it takes `でも`, not `～くても`; ❌<code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かくて</code> is not a form. (Note the sentence is odd in meaning too - nobody objects to quiet - so <code><ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>くてもかまいません</code> would make a better sentence.)

## ～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>でもいいですよ。 (b) <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>でもかまいませんよ。

{reveal}
(a) offers tomorrow as a positively acceptable option. It is warm and helpful, the sort of thing you say to put someone at ease about a deadline.

{reveal}
(b) says you would not be bothered by tomorrow. The content is the same, but the tone is a step cooler and more businesslike - it disclaims an objection rather than extending an offer. In an email to a client that neutrality is an advantage; to a friend who is apologising for being late, (a) is the kinder sentence.

# ～てごらん

**Meaning** · go on, try it; have a look - a warm, gentle command, always aimed downward

`ごらん` comes from the honorific verb for "look", but do not let that mislead you: in `～てごらん` the respect has been worn away completely. What is left is an **encouraging imperative** that an adult uses to a child, a teacher to a student, a senior to a junior. It is kind. It is also unmistakably from above.

---

## ～てごらん · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | て-form + ごらん | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてごらん |
| Verb (する) | ～してごらん | <ruby>想像<rt>そうぞう</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>想像<rt>そうぞう</rt></ruby>してごらん |
| Fuller, more instructional | ～てごらんなさい | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてごらんなさい |
| Softened with a particle | ～てごらんよ・～てごらんね | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてごらんよ |
| As a gentle invitation | ～てごらん? | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでごらん? |

> **Rule of thumb** - there is **no negative**. ❌<code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ないでごらん</code> does not exist. If you want someone not to do something, you are outside this pattern altogether.

---

## ～てごらん · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the kanji is ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>, and you will see <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby></code> in print. In the `～てごらん` pattern kana is much commoner, and it helps keep this use visually separate from the honorific <code>ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>ください</code>.

---

## ～てごらん · Examples

これ、<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**てごらん**。おいしいよ。<br>*Here, try this. It's good.*

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>の<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**てごらん**。<br>*Have a look outside the window.*

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>え**てごらん**。きっと<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かるから。<br>*Have a think about it yourself. You'll get there.*

こわがらないで、<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>っ**てごらんなさい**。<br>*Don't be frightened - get on and try it once.*

---

## ～てごらん · Examples (cont.)

お<ruby>母<rt>かあ</rt></ruby>さんに<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>い**てごらん**。<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っているかもしれないよ。<br>*Go and ask your mum. She might know.*

その<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>、もう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>い**てごらん**。<ruby>今度<rt>こんど</rt></ruby>はゆっくりね。<br>*Write that kanji once more. Slowly this time.*

<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>してもいいから、まず<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>でやっ**てごらん**。<br>*It doesn't matter if you get it wrong - have a go on your own first.*

<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにして、<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>を<ruby>閉<rt>と</rt></ruby>じて<ruby>鳥<rt>とり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>い**てごらん**。<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby><ruby>種類<rt>しゅるい</rt></ruby>いるか<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かるかな。<br>*Be quiet, close your eyes and listen to the birds. See if you can tell how many kinds there are.*

---

## ～てごらん · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しそうに<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えますが、<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>のとおりにやっ**てごらんなさい**。<ruby>十分<rt>じゅっぷん</rt></ruby>でできますよ。<br>*It looks difficult, but try following the instructions - it takes ten minutes.*

---

## ～てごらん · Nuance & register

The one thing to fix in your head is **direction**. `～てごらん` flows downhill: parent to child, teacher to pupil, coach to player, an older friend to a much younger one. Between adults of equal standing it sounds patronising, and pointed at anyone senior it is close to rude - no amount of politeness elsewhere in the sentence rescues it. This surprises learners, because the word inside it is an honorific one; the honorific is directed at the *looking*, historically, not at the listener.

Within its range, though, it is genuinely affectionate. Compared with the plain imperative <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べろ</code> or even with <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べなさい</code>, `～てごらん` invites rather than orders. It very often comes with a reason or a reassurance attached - `おいしいよ`, <code><ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>だから</code> - and that is part of its character: it is the grammar of coaxing.

---

## ～てごらん · Nuance & register (cont.)

`～てごらんなさい` is the fuller form. It is a little more formal and a little more instructional, which is why it turns up in children's books, teaching materials and the speech of teachers; `～てごらん` is the clipped, homelier version you hear at a kitchen table. Neither is polite in the ます sense - adding です or ます to them is not possible.

You will also meet it addressed to nobody in particular, in lyrics, adverts and narration: <code><ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てごらん</code>. There it reads as gentle, slightly poetic, and the downward angle becomes warmth rather than authority.

---

## ～てごらん · ～てごらん vs ～てみて

> **Rule of thumb** - `てみて` is neutral and works in **any** direction. `てごらん` adds warmth and a difference in standing, and only works **downward**.

これ、おいしいので<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**てみてください**。<br>*Do try this, it's delicious.*

これ、おいしいから<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**てごらん**。<br>*Go on, try this - it's delicious.*

---

## ～てごらん · ～てごらん vs ～てみて (cont.)

To a guest, a colleague or a customer, the first is the only safe option. The second is what you would say to your nephew. Note that they are not doing the same grammatical work either: `てみる` means "do it and find out", so <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてみて</code> is literally "eat it and see", while <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べてごらん</code> is closer to "let's see you eat it".

---

## ～てごらん · ～てごらん vs ～てみなさい

> **Rule of thumb** - both are downward. `てみなさい` is an **instruction** and can be stern; `てごらん` is an **invitation** and is warm.

もう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>、<ruby>最初<rt>さいしょ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ん**でみなさい**。<br>*Read it again from the beginning.*

もう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>、<ruby>最初<rt>さいしょ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>ん**でごらん**。<br>*Have another go at it from the start.*

A teacher who has said the same thing three times uses the first; a teacher sitting beside a struggling child uses the second. `なさい` carries the authority of an order that expects compliance, which is why parents reach for it when patience is running out. `ごらん` expects willingness rather than obedience.

---

## ～てごらん · ～てごらん vs ご覧ください

> **Rule of thumb** - same word, opposite directions. <code>ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>ください</code> and <code>ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>になる</code> are **honorific**: they raise the listener. `～てごらん` does not.

<ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby>ページ<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>を**ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>ください**。<br>*Please look at page three of the handout.*

ここ、<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**てごらん**。<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いてあるよ。<br>*Look here - your name's written on it.*

---

## ～てごらん · ～てごらん vs ご覧ください (cont.)

The first is what you say to an audience, a client or your manager; the second is what you say to a seven-year-old. Because they look so similar, learners produce the fatal hybrid ❌<code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てごらんください</code>, which staples an honorific ending onto a downward command. It is not a politer `てごらん`; it is a sentence that cannot be repaired without changing pattern.

---

## ～てごらん · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**てごらんください**。 → ✅ <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を**ご<ruby>覧<rt>らん</rt></ruby>ください**。<br>*`てごらん` cannot be made polite by adding ください. To ask a superior to look, use the honorific verb on its own.*

- ❌ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、こちらの<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>っ**てごらん**。 → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、こちらの<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>に**お<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>りください**。<br>*The pattern is downward only. Aimed at a superior it reads as though you were addressing a child.*

- ❌ その<ruby>箱<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けない**でごらん**。 → ✅ その<ruby>箱<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>け**ないでね**。<br>*There is no negative form. Use an ordinary negative request instead.*

- ❌ もう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う**ごらん**。 → ✅ もう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っ**てごらん**。<br>*`ごらん` attaches to the て-form, like `てみる` and `てください`.*

---

{reveal}

## ～てごらん · Practice 5

# ～ずに

**Meaning** · without doing - the written, slightly formal twin of `～ないで`

`ずに` and `ないで` mean the same thing. What separates them is texture: `ずに` is tighter, older and more literary, and it belongs to writing, to careful speech and to a large family of set expressions. Learn the two irregular stems - `せずに` and <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ずに</code> - and the rest is straightforward.

---

## ～ずに · Formation

Take the ない-form, drop the ない, add `ずに`. Two verbs break the rule.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ずに | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わない → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わずに |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ずに | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べない → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べずに |
| Verb (する) | **せずに** | <ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>せずに |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | **<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ずに** | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ずに |
| Joining clauses (literary) | ～ず、 | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わず、<ruby>去<rt>さ</rt></ruby>っていった |
| Common set forms | ～ずにすむ・～ずにおく・～ずにいる | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かずにすんだ |

---

## ～ずに · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - ❌`しずに` is the commonest error on this page. `する` does not use its ordinary stem here; the form is `せずに`, from an older negative. Likewise <code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る</code> gives <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ずに</code>, never ❌`きずに`.

> **Note** - `ある` and adjectives have no `ずに` form. "Because there wasn't any" is `なくて`, and that is a different pattern with a different job.

---

## ～ずに · Examples

<ruby>昨夜<rt>さくや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一睡<rt>いっすい</rt></ruby>もせ**ずに**<ruby>原稿<rt>げんこう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いていました。<br>*I was up writing the manuscript last night without a wink of sleep.*

---

## ～ずに · Examples (cont.)

---

## ～ずに · Nuance & register

`ずに` is a survival from classical Japanese, and it still carries a faint tightness that `ないで` does not. In writing - essays, news, instructions, business email - it is the default. In conversation, `ないで` is the default, and `ずに` sounds a shade formal or deliberate: <code><ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ずに<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていた</code> is perfectly sayable, just slightly more composed than <code><ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ないで<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていた</code>.

Certain combinations, however, are `ずに` only or nearly so, and they are worth learning whole:

- `～ずにすむ` - "get away without having to":<br>*<ruby>払<rt>はら</rt></ruby>わずにすんだ.*
- `～ずにはいられない` - "can't help doing".
- `～ずじまい` - "ended up never doing it":<br>*<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>えずじまいだった.*

---

## ～ずに · Nuance & register (cont.)

The bare `ず` without `に` is a further step into literary Japanese. It links clauses rather than modifying a verb - <code><ruby>振<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り<ruby>返<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>らず、<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>き<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>けた</code> - and it belongs to novels, speeches and song lyrics. Recognise it; write `ずに`.

The one thing `ずに` cannot do is make a **request**. `ないで` has a second life as a negative command - <code><ruby>触<rt>さわ</rt></ruby>らないでください</code> - and `ずに` has no share in it. ❌<code><ruby>触<rt>さわ</rt></ruby>らずにください</code> is not a formal version of anything.

---

## ～ずに · ～ずに vs ～ないで

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register: `ずに` written and formal, `ないで` spoken and neutral. But only `ないで` can form a **request**.

<ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ま**ないで**<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>てしまった。<br>*I fell asleep without taking my medicine.*

<ruby>所定<rt>しょてい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>欄<rt>らん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>記入<rt>きにゅう</rt></ruby>せ**ずに**<ruby>提出<rt>ていしゅつ</rt></ruby>しないでください。<br>*Please do not submit the form without filling in the designated fields.*

---

## ～ずに · ～ずに vs ～ないで (cont.)

The second sentence shows both at once, and the division of labour is exact: `ずに` does the "without", `ないで` does the "please don't". Swapping them produces ❌<code><ruby>記入<rt>きにゅう</rt></ruby>しないで<ruby>提出<rt>ていしゅつ</rt></ruby>せずにください</code>, which is not repairable.

Note also that the choice is partly one of **rhythm**: a formal written sentence that has already used `ないで` for its request will reach for `ずに` for the "without", simply to avoid saying ない twice.

---

## ～ずに · ～ずに vs ～なくて

> **Rule of thumb** - `ずに` says **without doing it**, describing how the main action happened. `なくて` says **because it didn't happen**, giving a cause.

<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>**ずに**<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けました。<br>*I made it to the station without looking at a map.*

<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>め**なくて**、<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>けませんでした。<br>*I couldn't read the map, so I never got to the station.*

---

## ～ずに · ～ずに vs ～なくて (cont.)

The first describes your method; the second explains a failure. A quick test: if you could insert "because" into the English, you want `なくて`. `なくて` also takes adjectives and `ある` (<code>お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>がなくて</code>), which `ずに` cannot touch at all.

---

## ～ずに · ～ずに vs ～ないまま

> **Rule of thumb** - `ずに` modifies **the action**: you did B without doing A. `ないまま` describes **a state left unchanged**: A never got done, and things stayed that way.

<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>消<rt>け</rt></ruby>さ**ずに**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけてしまった。<br>*I went out without turning the light off.*

<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>え**ないまま**、<ruby>三日<rt>みっか</rt></ruby><ruby>留守<rt>るす</rt></ruby>にしていた。<br>*The light stayed on the whole three days I was away.*

---

## ～ずに · ～ずに vs ～ないまま (cont.)

`ずに` is tied to a single moment - the moment of leaving. `ないまま` stretches over a period and emphasises that nothing changed during it. Where both are possible, `ないまま` adds a sense of something being left unresolved: <code><ruby>理由<rt>りゆう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>かないまま<ruby>別<rt>わか</rt></ruby>れた</code> lingers in a way <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>かずに<ruby>別<rt>わか</rt></ruby>れた</code> does not.

---

## ～ずに · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>**しずに**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったら、<ruby>満席<rt>まんせき</rt></ruby>でした。 → ✅ <ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>**せずに**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったら、<ruby>満席<rt>まんせき</rt></ruby>でした。<br>*`する` becomes `せずに`. This is irregular and there is no way to derive it from the ordinary stem - memorise it.*

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ずに**、<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けた。 → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ずに**、<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けた。<br>*<code><ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る</code> uses こ- here, the same stem as <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない</code>. き- belongs to <code><ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>廊下<rt>ろうか</rt></ruby>を<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ら**ずにください**。 → ✅ <ruby>廊下<rt>ろうか</rt></ruby>を<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ら**ないでください**。<br>*`ずに` never forms a request. There is no formal-sounding upgrade of `ないでください` along these lines.*

- ❌ <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**なずに**、<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>られなかった。 → ✅ <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**なくて**、<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>られなかった。<br>*`ある` has no `ずに` form, and in any case this sentence is giving a reason, which is `なくて` territory.*

---

{reveal}
<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ごはんも<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ずに**<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>向<rt>む</rt></ruby>かった。

The sentence describes *how* you set off - without eating - so it wants `ずに`, not `なくて`. <code><ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べないで</code> would be equally correct and simply sound more conversational.

**2.** Choose: レポートは<ruby>下書<rt>したが</rt></ruby>きを（せずに・しなくて）いきなり<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>き<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めました。

{reveal}
<ruby>下書<rt>したが</rt></ruby>きを**せずに**いきなり<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>き<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めました。

{reveal}

## ～ずに · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>たずに<ruby>出発<rt>しゅっぱつ</rt></ruby>した。 (b) <ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なくて<ruby>出発<rt>しゅっぱつ</rt></ruby>できなかった。

{reveal}

{reveal}

# ～ずにはいられない

**Meaning** · can't help doing it; can't stop myself - an urge that overrides you

This is the only "no choice" pattern in which the pressure comes from **inside**. Nobody has forbidden anything, no rule applies, and the circumstances have not closed off your options - you simply cannot keep yourself from doing it. Laughing, crying, saying the thing you should not say: `ずにはいられない` is the grammar of being overruled by your own feelings.

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Formation

Built on the `ずに` you already know, plus the potential of `いる`: literally "cannot be, without doing it".

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ～ない stem + ずにはいられない | <ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わずにはいられない |
| Verb (る-verb) | ～ない stem + ずにはいられない | <ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>える → <ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えずにはいられない |
| Verb (する) | **せずにはいられない** | <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>せずにはいられない |
| Colloquial variant | ～ないではいられない | <ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わないではいられない |
| Shorter variant | ～ずにいられない | <ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わずにいられない |
| Polite | ～ずにはいられません | <ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わずにはいられません |
| Past | ～ずにはいられなかった | <ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わずにはいられなかった |

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `する` gives `せずには`, never ❌`しずには`, exactly as it does in plain `ずに`. Since half the verbs that fit this pattern are する-verbs - <ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>する, <ruby>同情<rt>どうじょう</rt></ruby>する, <ruby>応援<rt>おうえん</rt></ruby>する - this is the form to drill.

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Examples

この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ると、<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>か**ずにはいられない**。<br>*I can't watch this film without crying.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>のことを<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>せ**ずにはいられません**。<br>*I can't help worrying about my son.*

あの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>ると、パンを<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わ**ずにはいられない**。<br>*I can't walk past that shop without buying bread.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>し<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>がおかしくて、<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わ**ずにはいられなかった**。<br>*The way she told it was so funny I couldn't keep a straight face.*

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Examples (cont.)

こんなひどい<ruby>記事<rt>きじ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むと、<ruby>一言<rt>ひとこと</rt></ruby><ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わ**ずにはいられません**。<br>*When I read an article this bad, I can't let it pass without saying something.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>いているのを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>て、<ruby>声<rt>こえ</rt></ruby>をかけ**ずにはいられなかった**。<br>*Seeing a child crying on his own, I couldn't not go over and speak to him.*

<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>最後<rt>さいご</rt></ruby>の<ruby>五分間<rt>ごふんかん</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がって<ruby>応援<rt>おうえん</rt></ruby>せ**ずにはいられませんでした**。<br>*For the last five minutes of the match I simply had to get up and cheer.*

<ruby>災害<rt>さいがい</rt></ruby>のニュースを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ていると、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>ができるのかを<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>え**ずにはいられない**。<br>*Watching the news about the disaster, I can't stop asking myself what I could do.*

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>祖母<rt>そぼ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>返<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>すたびに、あの<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>台所<rt>だいどころ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>さ**ずにはいられません**。<br>*Every time I reread my grandmother's letters, I find myself back in that kitchen, that summer.*

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Nuance & register

The compulsion is **emotional**, and the verbs that appear here reflect that: to laugh, to cry, to worry, to remember, to sympathise, to say something, to cheer. Physical needs and practical necessities do not belong - <code><ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わずにはいられなかった</code> is odd if it merely started raining, and natural if you saw an umbrella so beautiful you had no defence against it.

Because it reports an inner state, the subject is normally **you**, or a narrator speaking of themselves. About a third person you need a hedge, exactly as with `たい`: <code><ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わずにはいられなかったようだ</code>. Written flat about someone else, it claims access to a mind you cannot see into.

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Nuance & register (cont.)

The register leans **written and expressive**: essays, reviews, letters, blog posts, novels. Spoken, it is entirely usable but a touch dramatic - in casual conversation people say <code>つい<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>っちゃった</code> instead. That is worth knowing, because a learner who uses `ずにはいられない` about a mildly amusing thing sounds as though they are narrating their own memoir.

> **Note** - two neighbours, distinguished mainly by register. `～ないではいられない` means the same and is a little more conversational. `～ずにいられない`, without the `は`, is a shortened form that turns up in speech and in lyrics. The `は` adds a touch of emphasis; none of the three differs in meaning.

---

## ～ずにはいられない · ～ずにはいられない vs ～てしまう

> **Rule of thumb** - `てしまう` reports that the action **happened despite you**. `ずにはいられない` reports the **pressure to do it**, and usually says something about you as a person.

おかしくて<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>っ**てしまいました**。<br>*It was so funny I ended up laughing.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>るたびに<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>わ**ずにはいられません**。<br>*I can't look at his face without laughing.*

`てしまう` is about one occasion and often carries regret. `ずにはいられない` states a standing susceptibility - every time, without fail - and carries no regret at all, only recognition. That is why the second sentence takes `たびに` comfortably and the first does not.

---

## ～ずにはいられない · ～ずにはいられない vs ～ないわけにはいかない

> **Rule of thumb** - both come out as "I have to" in English, and they are opposites underneath. `ずにはいられない` is a **want** you cannot suppress; `ないわけにはいかない` is a **duty** you cannot escape.

<ruby>友達<rt>ともだち</rt></ruby>の<ruby>作品<rt>さくひん</rt></ruby>なので、<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>か**ないわけにはいかない**。<br>*It's my friend's work, so I can hardly not go and see it.*

<ruby>評判<rt>ひょうばん</rt></ruby>がよすぎて、<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>か**ずにはいられない**。<br>*Everyone's raving about it - I can't stay away.*

---

## ～ずにはいられない · ～ずにはいられない vs ～ないわけにはいかない (cont.)

In the first you would rather stay home; friendship has removed the exit. In the second nothing would keep you away. Choosing wrongly says something unfortunate about your feelings: telling a friend <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないわけにはいかなくて</code> admits you went out of obligation.

---

## ～ずにはいられない · ～ずにはいられない vs ～しかない

> **Rule of thumb** - `しかない` is about **the world** running out of options. `ずにはいられない` is about **you** running out of self-control.

<ruby>終電<rt>しゅうでん</rt></ruby>がないので、<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いて<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**しかない**。<br>*There's no last train, so I'll have to walk home.*

あんまり<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>がきれいだったので、<ruby>遠回<rt>とおまわ</rt></ruby>りせ**ずにはいられなかった**。<br>*The night was so lovely I couldn't help taking the long way round.*

Both sentences end with the speaker walking, and they explain the walking in opposite ways. If the sentence would still be true of someone with a completely different personality, you want `しかない`; if it depends on how *this* person feels, you want `ずにはいられない`.

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>結果<rt>けっか</rt></ruby>が<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>になって、<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>**しずにはいられない**。 → ✅ <ruby>結果<rt>けっか</rt></ruby>が<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>になって、<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>**せずにはいられない**。<br>*`する` takes the irregular `せずに`. There is no しずに anywhere in Japanese.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までにレポートを<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>さ**ずにはいられない**。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までにレポートを<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>さ**なければならない**。<br>*A deadline is an obligation, not an urge. This pattern reports a feeling you cannot suppress, and nobody is emotionally overwhelmed by a report.*

- ❌ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れそうだったので、タクシーに<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>ら**ずにはいられなかった**。 → ✅ <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れそうだったので、タクシーに<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>る**しかなかった**。<br>*Circumstances closing off the alternatives is `しかない`. `ずにはいられない` would suggest you have an emotional weakness for taxis.*

---

## ～ずにはいられない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>感動<rt>かんどう</rt></ruby>**せずにはいられません**。 → ✅ <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんも<ruby>感動<rt>かんどう</rt></ruby>**せずにはいられなかったようです**。<br>*About someone else, the claim needs hedging - `ようだ`, `だろう`, `らしい` - because you cannot see inside their head.*

---

{reveal}

## ～ずにはいられない · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>まれたので、<ruby>残業<rt>ざんぎょう</rt></ruby>せずにはいられませんでした。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>頼<rt>たの</rt></ruby>まれたので、**<ruby>残業<rt>ざんぎょう</rt></ruby>しないわけにはいきませんでした**。

{reveal}
Being asked by your manager creates a duty, and duty you cannot escape is `ないわけにはいかない`. As written, the sentence says you were unable to restrain your appetite for overtime.

## ～ずにはいられない · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) その<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んで、<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>いてしまった。 (b) その<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むと、<ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>かずにはいられない。

{reveal}
(a) reports one occasion. You read the letter, and the tears arrived without your consent; `てしまう` adds a faint sense of "I didn't mean to", which can shade into embarrassment.

{reveal}
(b) reports a rule about yourself. Note the non-past <code><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むと</code>: this happens every time, and the speaker is stating a known feature of their own reaction. (a) is an event, (b) is a disposition.
