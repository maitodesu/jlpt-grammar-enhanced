# ～としたら / ～とすれば

**Meaning** · supposing that A is true, then B - a premise granted for the sake of working out what follows

The `と` here is the quotative `と`, and `する` is "take it as". `～としたら` literally means *if we take it that…*. It does not report a condition on its way to happening; it **grants a proposition** and asks the listener to see what falls out of it. That is why the main clause is nearly always a judgement - a deduction, an estimate, a question, a "then there's nothing for it but…" - rather than a plan or an instruction.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Formation

Everything goes into its plain form and takes `と`. Nouns and な-adjectives need `だ` in front - the opposite of `なら`, which forbids it.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain non-past + としたら／とすれば | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くとしたら |
| Verb (past) | た-form + としたら | <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>とした → <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたとしたら |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + としたら | <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない → <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わないとしたら |
| い-adjective | plain form + としたら（no だ） | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いとしたら |
| な-adjective | stem + **だ** + としたら | <ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby> → <ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>だとしたら |
| Noun | Noun + **だ** + としたら | <ruby>嘘<rt>うそ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>嘘<rt>うそ</rt></ruby>だとしたら |
| Explanatory | plain form + のだとしたら | <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っている → <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っているのだとしたら |
| Variants | とすると／となると | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るとすると |
| Polite (in a clause) | としましたら／といたしますと | <ruby>事実<rt>じじつ</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>事実<rt>じじつ</rt></ruby>だとしましたら |

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the adverbs もし, <ruby>仮<rt>かり</rt></ruby>に and <ruby>万一<rt>まんいち</rt></ruby> sit naturally in front of this pattern and announce the supposition early: <ruby>仮<rt>かり</rt></ruby>に<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったとしたら… They are optional, but they make a long sentence much easier to follow.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Examples

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>から<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**としたら**、<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>きますか。<br>*If we were to set off now, what time would we get there?*

これが<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>だ**とすれば**、<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>です。<br>*If this is true, then it's a serious problem.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>やる**としたら**、<ruby>三日<rt>みっか</rt></ruby>はかかるだろう。<br>*If I had to do the whole thing alone, it would take a good three days.*

<ruby>仮<rt>かり</rt></ruby>に<ruby>宝<rt>たから</rt></ruby>くじが<ruby>当<rt>あ</rt></ruby>たった**としたら**、<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>に<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>いますか。<br>*Supposing you won the lottery, what would you spend it on?*

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**とすれば**、<ruby>司会<rt>しかい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>がやるしかない。<br>*If he isn't coming, then I have no choice but to run the thing myself.*

この<ruby>数字<rt>すうじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>正<rt>ただ</rt></ruby>しい**とすると**、<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>より<ruby>売<rt>う</rt></ruby>り<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>げが<ruby>減<rt>へ</rt></ruby>っていることになる。<br>*If these figures are right, it means sales are down on last year.*

<ruby>留学<rt>りゅうがく</rt></ruby>する**としたら**、やっぱり<ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>通<rt>つう</rt></ruby>じる<ruby>国<rt>くに</rt></ruby>がいいなあ。<br>*If I were going to study abroad, I'd still want somewhere English gets you by.*

<ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>新幹線<rt>しんかんせん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まっている**としたら**、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>延期<rt>えんき</rt></ruby>するほかないでしょう。<br>*If the bullet trains are down because of the typhoon, there'll be nothing for it but to postpone tomorrow's meeting.*

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>の<ruby>条件<rt>じょうけん</rt></ruby>のまま<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>き<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>ける**とすれば**、<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby><ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>やしていただく<ruby>必要<rt>ひつよう</rt></ruby>があります。<br>*If we're to take this on under the current terms, we'd need two more people on it.*

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Nuance & register

The family is register-neutral in the sense that no member is rude or childish, but the three are not evenly distributed. `としたら` is the everyday spoken member and the one that handles frankly unreal suppositions - <ruby>生<rt>う</rt></ruby>まれ<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わったとしたら, <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>だとしたら. `とすれば` leans towards written argument, reports and analysis, where it introduces an inference the writer is prepared to defend. `とすると` tends to mark a consequence the speaker has just this second worked out, and often carries a small "ah - then…" in it.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Nuance & register (cont.)

Because the clause is subordinate, it stays plain even in the politest sentence: <ruby>事実<rt>じじつ</rt></ruby>だとすれば、<ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby><ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ございません。 If you want the politeness to reach into the clause itself - service-counter Japanese, a careful reply to a superior - the forms are `としましたら` and, at the top of the scale, `といたしますと`. These are common at a bank or a travel desk and sound stiff anywhere else.

One restriction is worth internalising. The main clause is where the **speaker's judgement** goes: だろう, はずだ, ことになる, しかない, ～たい, or a question. A bare statement of scheduled fact does not belong there, and neither does a request. If what you mean is "let me know once you get home", the sentence wants `たら`, not `としたら` - you are not supposing anything, you are waiting for something.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Nuance & register (cont.)

Finally, `としたら` freely takes a **past** clause in front without referring to past time: <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたとしたら、どこだろう。 The `た` marks the supposed situation as already complete at the moment you are reasoning about it, exactly as it does with `たら`.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · ～としたら vs ～たら

> **Rule of thumb** - `たら` supposes an **event** that could actually turn up; `としたら` supposes a **proposition** so that you can reason from it. If you could replace the clause with "assuming that…", you want `としたら`.

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>いたら**<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>してね。<br>*Give me a call when you get to the station.*

That one cannot take `としたら`: arriving is not a hypothesis, it is the plan. Swap in a genuine supposition and the pattern comes back:

<ruby>終電<rt>しゅうでん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>り<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れた**としたら**、<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>はどこで<ruby>泊<rt>と</rt></ruby>まるつもりだろう。<br>*Supposing she did miss the last train, where is she planning to stay?*

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · ～としたら vs ～たら (cont.)

The same split explains why `としたら` sounds odd with an instruction after it. You cannot act on a premise that is only being entertained.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · ～としたら vs ～なら

> **Rule of thumb** - `なら` picks up something the other person has just put on the table and answers it, usually with advice or an offer. `としたら` may suppose something nobody has claimed, and answers itself with a conclusion.

<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くなら**、<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たほうがいいよ。<br>*If you're going to Kyoto, you'd do better to leave early.*

<ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くとしたら**、<ruby>日帰<rt>ひがえ</rt></ruby>りでは<ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>だろうね。<br>*If one were to go to Kyoto, a day trip probably wouldn't work.*

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · ～としたら vs ～なら (cont.)

The first is aimed at a person with a plan; the second weighs an idea. Note also the form: `なら` refuses `だ` (<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>なら) while `としたら` requires it (<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>だとしたら). They look like a pair and conjugate as opposites.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · ～としたら vs ～とすれば vs ～とすると

> **Rule of thumb** - all three grant a premise. `としたら` is the freest and most spoken; `とすれば` is the reasoning one and dominates writing; `とすると` presents the consequence as something that simply falls out, and often marks a realisation.

もし<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>だ**としたら**、まずこの<ruby>制度<rt>せいど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>えますね。<br>*If I were the manager, the first thing I'd change is this system.*

この<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>正<rt>ただ</rt></ruby>しい**とすれば**、<ruby>原因<rt>げんいん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つしか<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えられない。<br>*If this account is correct, only one cause is conceivable.*

<ruby>木村<rt>きむら</rt></ruby>さんがもう<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>た**とすると**、さっき<ruby>廊下<rt>ろうか</rt></ruby>ですれ<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>ったのかな。<br>*If Kimura's already left, then maybe that was him I passed in the corridor.*

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · ～としたら vs ～とすれば vs ～とすると (cont.)

> **Note** - `となると` is a fourth cousin and is *not* interchangeable. It takes a situation that has genuinely arisen and asks what now becomes of things: <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>出張<rt>しゅっちょう</rt></ruby>となると、<ruby>準備<rt>じゅんび</rt></ruby>が<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない。 Where `としたら` supposes, `となると` concedes.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>としたら、<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>**だ**としたら、<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Nouns and な-adjectives take `だ` before `と`. `なら` is the one that drops it, and learners routinely apply the wrong half of the pair.*

- ❌ <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**だ**とすれば<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。 → ✅ <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いとすれば<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。<br>*い-adjectives are already complete predicates. No `だ`.*

- ❌ <ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>いたとしたら、<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>してください。 → ✅ <ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>いたら、<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*Arriving home is expected, not supposed, and the main clause is a request. Both point away from `としたら` and towards plain `たら`.*

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>になるとしたら、<ruby>桜<rt>さくら</rt></ruby>が<ruby>咲<rt>さ</rt></ruby>きます。 → ✅ <ruby>春<rt>はる</rt></ruby>になると、<ruby>桜<rt>さくら</rt></ruby>が<ruby>咲<rt>さ</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*A regularity that always holds is `と`. Nothing is being supposed.*

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>仮<rt>かり</rt></ruby>に<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>大雪<rt>おおゆき</rt></ruby>____、<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>はどうなりますか。

{reveal}
<ruby>大雪<rt>おおゆき</rt></ruby>**だとしたら**（or **だとすれば**）

{reveal}
The clause ends in a noun, so `だ` is obligatory before `と`. The main clause is a question about consequences, which is exactly the slot this pattern was built for.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>い（たら・たとしたら）、すぐ<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてみてください。

{reveal}
<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>い**たら**、すぐ<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてみてください。

{reveal}
The parcel is on its way - this is an expected event, not a hypothesis - and the main clause is a request. `としたら` refuses both of those.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "If this report is accurate, we'll have to change the plan."

{reveal}
この<ruby>報告<rt>ほうこく</rt></ruby>が<ruby>正<rt>ただ</rt></ruby>しい**とすれば**、<ruby>計画<rt>けいかく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>えなければなりません。

{reveal}
`とすれば` is the reasoning member of the family and fits a written or workplace inference. The main clause states what necessarily follows, which is the natural partner for this pattern.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>犯人<rt>はんにん</rt></ruby>としたら、どうして<ruby>逃<rt>に</rt></ruby>げなかったんだろう。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>犯人<rt>はんにん</rt></ruby>**だとしたら**、どうして<ruby>逃<rt>に</rt></ruby>げなかったんだろう。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>犯人<rt>はんにん</rt></ruby></code> is a noun, so `だ` has to appear. Everything else about the sentence is right: a supposition followed by a puzzled question is a textbook use.

---

## ～としたら / ～とすれば · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>留学<rt>りゅうがく</rt></ruby>する**なら**、アメリカがいいですよ。 (b) <ruby>留学<rt>りゅうがく</rt></ruby>する**としたら**、アメリカがいいですね。

{reveal}
(a) responds to a plan the listener has already announced. You have heard that they intend to study abroad, and you are advising them where to go - hence the listener-directed `よ`.

{reveal}
(b) entertains the idea without attributing it to anyone. The speaker is weighing a hypothetical, quite possibly about themselves, and `ね` invites the listener to weigh it too. Nobody has necessarily decided anything.

# ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば

**Meaning** · if only A, then B - A named as the single condition that is enough on its own

`さえ` picks out **one** thing and the `ば`-clause says that this one thing is all it takes. Everything else is waved away: it may be missing, it may be against you, it does not matter. The main clause then reports that the result is assured - or, in the past, that it *would have been*, which is why this pattern is also how Japanese sighs over a small thing that was not done.

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Formation

Two halves have to arrive together: the particle `さえ` on the crucial element, and a `ば`-form on the verb or adjective that governs it. Which helper you need depends on what the crucial element is.

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (subject or object) | Noun + さえ + ば-form（が・を drop） | お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>がある → お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>さえあれば |
| Noun (other particle) | Particle + さえ + ば-form | <ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>にさえ<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>けば |
| Verb (action) | ます-stem + さえすれば | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みさえすれば |
| Verb (ongoing state) | て-form + さえいれば | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってさえいれば |
| Verb (negative) | ます-stem + さえしなければ | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>いさえしなければ |
| Noun + する verb | Noun + さえすれば | <ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>さえすれば |
| い-adjective | く + さえあれば | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くさえあれば |
| な-adjective | で + さえあれば | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かでさえあれば |
| Noun (predicate) | で + さえあれば | <ruby>本物<rt>ほんもの</rt></ruby> → <ruby>本物<rt>ほんもの</rt></ruby>でさえあれば |

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `さえ` **replaces** `が` and `を`, and **follows** every other particle. ❌<code>お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>がさえ</code>, ❌`これをさえ`; but <code><ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>にさえ</code>, `ここでさえ`, <code><ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちからさえ</code> are all fine.

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Examples

お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>**さえあれば**、<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>ないんだけどな。<br>*If only we had the money, there'd be no problem.*

これ**さえ<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えれば**、テストは<ruby>大丈夫<rt>だいじょうぶ</rt></ruby>ですよ。<br>*Learn just this and you'll be fine on the test.*

<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby>**さえよければ**、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>出発<rt>しゅっぱつ</rt></ruby>できます。<br>*As long as the weather holds, we can set off tomorrow morning.*

<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**でさえあれば**、<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>くてもかまいません。<br>*So long as it's quiet, I don't mind the room being small.*

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み**さえすれば**、だれでも<ruby>組<rt>く</rt></ruby>み<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>てられますよ。<br>*Anyone can put it together, as long as they actually read the instructions.*

<ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>ん**でさえいれば**、<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>はもっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>下<rt>さ</rt></ruby>がっていたはずです。<br>*If you'd just kept taking the medicine, the fever would have come down sooner.*

あなたが<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**でさえいてくれれば**、ほかには<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>も<ruby>望<rt>のぞ</rt></ruby>みません。<br>*As long as you stay well, I don't ask for anything else.*

<ruby>締<rt>し</rt></ruby>め<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>り**さえ<ruby>守<rt>まも</rt></ruby>ってくれれば**、やり<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>はそちらに<ruby>任<rt>まか</rt></ruby>せます。<br>*Provided you hit the deadline, I'll leave the method entirely to you.*

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Examples (cont.)

あの<ruby>時<rt>とき</rt></ruby>、<ruby>一言<rt>ひとこと</rt></ruby><ruby>謝<rt>あやま</rt></ruby>り**さえすれば**、こんな<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>ごとにはならなかったのに。<br>*If I had just said one word of apology back then, none of this would have blown up.*

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral and extremely common in speech, but it always carries an attitude, and the attitude changes with the tense of the main clause.

With a **non-past** main clause it is reassuring or businesslike: here is the one hurdle, clear it and the rest takes care of itself. This is the tone of instructions, of a manager delegating, of somebody talking a nervous friend down - <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>ち<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>きさえすれば、できるよ。

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Nuance & register (cont.)

With a **past** main clause, and especially with `のに` or `んだけど` hanging off the end, it turns wistful or accusing. The clause names a trivially small thing that was not done, and the main clause names the disaster that followed. Said about yourself it is regret; said about the person in front of you it is a reproach, and a fairly sharp one - <ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>さえしてくれれば is not a neutral remark. Soften it, or say it about the situation rather than the person.

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two spoken habits worth knowing. First, the `ます`-stem construction (<code><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みさえすれば</code>) sounds slightly emphatic and bookish; in casual speech people more often hang `さえ` on a noun and leave the verb alone (<code>それさえ<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めば</code>). Second, `すら` is a near-synonym of `さえ` in the "even" sense but is confined to writing and almost never appears in this minimum-condition frame - do not swap them.

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · ～さえ○○ば vs plain ～ば

> **Rule of thumb** - plain `ば` says *this is a condition*. `さえ…ば` says *this is the only condition*. Removing `さえ` costs you the whole point of the sentence.

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**あれば**、<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>います。<br>*If I have time, I'll help.*

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>**さえあれば**、<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>えるんですが。<br>*It's purely a question of time - if I had it, I could help.*

The second sentence claims that willingness, skill and permission are all already in place, so that time is the last thing standing in the way. The first claims nothing of the sort. Notice too that `が` disappears once `さえ` arrives.

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · ～さえ○○ば vs ～だけで

> **Rule of thumb** - `だけ` counts; `さえ` selects. `だけで` says the amount is small; `さえ…ば` says this particular thing is the one that matters, however large it is.

<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く**だけで**<ruby>参加<rt>さんか</rt></ruby>できます。<br>*You can take part just by writing your name.*

<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>**さえ<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>けば**、あとはこちらでやっておきます。<br>*Once you've put your name down, we'll take care of the rest.*

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · ～さえ○○ば vs ～だけで (cont.)

`だけで` is about how little effort is involved and is a natural thing to put in an advertisement. `さえ…ば` is about clearing the one remaining obstacle, and it implies a list of other things that are already settled. That is also why `さえ…ば` can take a heavy condition - <ruby>一億円<rt>いちおくえん</rt></ruby>さえあれば - where `だけで` would sound absurd.

---

## ～さえ○○ば vs さえ meaning "even"

> **Rule of thumb** - same particle, opposite ends of the scale. Without a `ば` after it, `さえ` picks an **extreme** case ("even X"). With a `ば` after it, it picks the **minimum** sufficient case ("if only X").

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**さえ**<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っている<ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby>です。<br>*It's a word even children know.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ども**さえ<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにしていれば**、いい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>なんですけどね。<br>*It's a lovely restaurant, as long as the children keep quiet.*

The first ranks children at the bottom of a scale of knowledge and says the word reaches even there. The second says the children are the sole problem. The `ば` is what flips the reading, so leaving it off does not weaken the sentence - it changes it into a different one.

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Common mistakes

- ❌ お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>**が**さえあれば → ✅ お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>さえあれば<br>*`さえ` takes the place of `が` and `を`. It stacks only on `に`, `で`, `から`, `と` and the like.*

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るさえすれば → ✅ <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>さえすれば<br>*`さえする` attaches to the `ます`-stem, never the dictionary form. For a `する` verb built on a noun, put `さえ` after the noun instead: <ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>さえすれば.*

- ❌ <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いさえあれば<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。 → ✅ <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くさえあれば<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います。<br>*An い-adjective goes into its `く` form before `さえある`, exactly as it does before `ない`.*

- ❌ <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>さえある、<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わります。 → ✅ <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>さえあれば、<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わります。<br>*The `ば` is not decoration. Without it the sentence says "even time exists", which is not what anybody means.*

---

## ～さえ○○ば / ～さえすれば / ～さえいれば · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>さえ<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>せば、<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってください。 → ✅ この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>したら、<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってください。<br>*A sufficient-condition clause is a claim about what will follow, not a step in a set of instructions. Like plain `ば` with an action verb, it does not take a request or a command after it.*

# ～ばいい

**Meaning** · that's all you have to do; you should just ~; it's fine if ~

`ば` names a condition and `いい` says that meeting it is **enough**. Put together they give advice not by ordering someone about but by pointing at the one action that will settle the matter: *do that, and we're done*. The same shape asks the question in reverse - `どうすればいいですか`, "what would be enough here?" - which is the single most useful sentence in the book when you are lost.

---

## ～ばいい · Formation

The first half is the ordinary `ば`-form. Nouns and な-adjectives use `なら`, since `なら` is the copula's `ば`-form.

---

<ruby>文句<rt>もんく</rt></ruby>があるなら、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で**やればいい**じゃないか。<br>*If you've got complaints, do it yourself then.*

<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぎませんので、<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>までに**<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>めていただければいい**です。<br>*There's no rush on the reply - it's fine if you decide by next week.*

コピーで<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>け<ruby>付<rt>つ</rt></ruby>けますから、<ruby>原本<rt>げんぽん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>後<rt>あと</rt></ruby>から**<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>っていただければいい**んですよ。<br>*We'll accept a copy, so you can just send the original on afterwards.*

---

## ～ばいい · Nuance & register

`ばいい` is short, and short can be cold. Said flatly to somebody who has asked for help, <ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>調<rt>しら</rt></ruby>べればいい is not friendly advice - it means "look it up yourself, then." The pattern's structure is partly to blame: naming the sufficient action implies the listener could have worked it out. Softeners matter more here than with most patterns.

---

## ～ばいい · Nuance & register (cont.)

The usual repairs, roughly from casual to careful: `ばいいよ` (warm, to a friend), `ばいいんじゃない?` (a suggestion offered rather than handed down), `ばいいですよ`, <code>ばいいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code>, and - with a superior or a customer - dropping the pattern entirely for <code>～ていただければと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code>. Telling your section head 「メールで<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>ればいいです」 is technically an answer to their question and still lands badly.

There is a deliberately dismissive use, and you should recognise it even if you never say it: <ruby>勝手<rt>かって</rt></ruby>にすればいい, <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りたければ<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ればいい. Attached to a `～たければ` clause and delivered flatly, `ばいい` means "suit yourself" and is a way of ending a conversation badly.

---

## ～ばいい · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two everyday frames run in the opposite direction and are entirely safe. `ばいいんです` is the reassuring "all you have to do is…" of a helpful colleague. And `どうすればいいですか` is the standard polite request for instructions - neutral, businesslike, usable with anyone.

Finally, note what the past tense does. `ばいい` has **no** ❌`ばいいでした`. Its past is `ばよかった`, and that form means regret, not advice.

---

## ～ばいい · ～ばいい vs ～たらいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ばいい` presents the action as *sufficient*: that, and nothing more, will do it. `たらいい` presents it as a *suggestion*: how about doing that? The first is closer to a solution, the second to a proposal.

きっぷは<ruby>券売機<rt>けんばいき</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えばいい**んです。<br>*You buy your ticket at the machine - that's all there is to it.*

<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れてるなら、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たらいい**よ。<br>*If you're tired, why not get an early night today.*

---

## ～ばいい · ～ばいい vs ～たらいい (cont.)

Swapping them is not ungrammatical, but the flavour shifts: <ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ればいい sounds like you are closing the topic, while <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったらいい sounds oddly tentative about a fixed procedure. The same split shows in the two questions: `どうすればいいですか` asks for the correct procedure, while `どうしたらいいですか` more often comes from somebody genuinely at a loss. That is a tendency rather than a rule, and either is acceptable in either situation.

---

## ～ばいい · ～ばいい vs ～たほうがいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `たほうがいい` **compares**: this course is better than the other one, and there is an implication that you might be about to choose wrongly. `ばいい` does not compare; it simply supplies an answer.

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があるなら、<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったほうがいい**よ。<br>*If you've got a fever you'd better go to a clinic.*

<ruby>保険証<rt>ほけんしょう</rt></ruby>がなければ、<ruby>後<rt>あと</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>せばいい**そうです。<br>*Apparently if you don't have your insurance card you can just hand it in later.*

The first weighs going against not going and comes down on one side, which is why it can sound like mild pressure. The second answers a procedural worry and applies no pressure at all. Use `たほうがいい` when there is a real risk in the alternative.

---

## ～ばいい · ～ばいい vs ～てもいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `てもいい` grants **permission**: doing it would not be a problem. `ばいい` states **sufficiency**: doing it is the thing that solves this.

この<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>、<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>っ**てもいい**ですか。<br>*Is it all right if I sit here?*

<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>空<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いていなければ、<ruby>後<rt>うし</rt></ruby>ろに**<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>てばいい**ですよ。<br>*If there are no seats free, you can just stand at the back.*

---

## ～ばいい · ～ばいい vs ～てもいい (cont.)

English "you can" covers both, which is exactly why learners mix them up. Ask yourself whether you are being *allowed* to do something or *told what will work*. Answering 「はい、<ruby>座<rt>すわ</rt></ruby>ればいいです」 to a request for permission sounds brusque, almost like "well, sit down then."

---

## ～ばいい · Common mistakes

- ❌ ここに<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>くばいいですか。 → ✅ ここに<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>**けば**いいですか。<br>*`いい` follows the `ば`-form, not the dictionary form. The `ば` is part of the verb, not a separate particle.*

- ❌ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだばいいです。 → ✅ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**なら**いいです。<br>*Nouns and な-adjectives have no `ば`-form of their own; they borrow the copula's, which is `なら`.*

- ❌ もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ればいいでした。 → ✅ もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>れば**よかった**です。<br>*`いい` cannot be put into the past behind `ば`. The past of this pattern is `ばよかった`, and it means "I should have", not "it was fine to".*

---

## ～ばいい · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ (<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>に) 「この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>はメールで<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>ればいいです。」 → ✅ 「この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>はメールでお<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>りいただければと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。」<br>*Grammatical, but telling a superior what is sufficient sounds like issuing them instructions. Move to a `ていただければ` frame.*

# ～たらいい / ～といい - should

**Meaning** · you should ~; why not ~ - advice given by describing a good outcome rather than issuing an order

Read the words literally and they say *if you did it, that would be good*. That is the whole trick. Instead of telling somebody what to do, you sketch a small hypothetical world in which they have already done it, and pronounce that world good. The listener is left to draw the obvious conclusion, which is far easier to accept than an instruction.

This page covers the advice reading only. The identical string also means "I hope" and "it would be nice if"; what tells them apart is **who acts** and **whether they can**.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - should · Formation

The advice reading is essentially a **verb** pattern, and the verb has to be something the listener could actually decide to do.

## ～たらいい / ～といい - should · Nuance & register

Because the pattern hides advice inside a conditional, it is gentler than `～なさい` or the plain imperative, and slightly gentler than `～たほうがいい`. It is the register of a helpful colleague, a friendly teacher, a shop assistant who has decided to be kind to you.

`たらいい` and `といい` are close, with a small difference of angle. `たらいい` responds to *this* person's situation - the conditional takes their circumstances as read. `といい` states something closer to a general good practice, which is why it turns up so often in teacherly and written advice: <ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>はときどき<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>くといい。 Both are entirely ordinary in speech.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - should · Nuance & register (cont.)

The pattern is still advice, and advice flows downhill in Japanese. To a friend or a junior, <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んだらいいよ is warm. To your section head the same words are presumptuous, and you climb the ladder of hedges instead: <code>たらいいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code> → `たらいいんじゃないでしょうか` → dropping the pattern altogether for `～てはいかがでしょうか`.

`たらいいでしょう` deserves a warning. It is grammatical, but it lands as distant and lecturing - a teacher addressing a hall rather than a person addressing a person. In conversation prefer `たらいいですよ`.

Two things do **not** happen. `いい` here is an い-adjective, so it never takes `だ` (❌`たらいいだ`). And the pattern has no past form of its own: putting `いい` into the past gives you `たらよかった`, which is regret, not advice.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - should · ～たらいい vs ～ばいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ばいい` names a **sufficient** action: do that, and the matter is settled. `たらいい` makes a **suggestion**: how about doing that? The first closes a question, the second opens one.

きっぷは<ruby>自動<rt>じどう</rt></ruby><ruby>券売機<rt>けんばいき</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えばいい**んです。<br>*You buy the ticket from the machine - that's all there is to it.*

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>があるなら、<ruby>窓口<rt>まどぐち</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いてみたらいい**ですよ。<br>*If you've got time, you might try asking at the counter.*

Neither swap is ungrammatical, but the tone moves. `ばいい` on a suggestion can sound like you are shutting the conversation down; `たらいい` on a fixed procedure sounds oddly unsure of a thing everybody knows.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - should · ～たらいい vs ～たほうがいい

> **Rule of thumb** - `たほうがいい` weighs this course against the alternative and warns you off the other one. `たらいい` does not weigh anything; it simply offers an idea.

<ruby>夜中<rt>よなか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>であの<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>らないほうがいい**よ。<br>*You'd better not take that road alone at night.*

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がかかってもいいなら、あの<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>ったらいい**よ。<br>*If you don't mind it taking longer, take that road.*

The first implies a risk; the second implies a choice. That is also why `たほうがいい` has a natural negative and `たらいい` does not: warning somebody off is exactly the job `たほうがいい` was built for.

---

## The advice reading vs the "I hope" reading

> **Rule of thumb** - if the listener could decide to do it, the sentence is advice. If nobody in the room controls it, the sentence is a hope. Same words, and the test is about the world, not the grammar.

<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めに**<ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>したらいい**ですよ。<br>*You'd do well to book early.*

<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>空<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いているといい**ですね。<br>*I hope there are seats free.*

---

## The advice reading vs the "I hope" reading (cont.)

Booking is up to the listener, so the first is advice. Whether seats are free is up to nobody, so the second is a hope - and note that hopes take `ですね`, which invites the listener to share the feeling, while advice takes `ですよ`, which hands them something they did not have. The final particle is often the fastest clue.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - should · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むたらいいですよ。 → ✅ <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>**んだら**いいですよ。<br>*`たら` attaches to the plain **past**. The dictionary form goes with `と`, not with `たら`.*

- ❌ <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たらいいだ。 → ✅ <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たらいい。<br>*`いい` is an い-adjective and is already a complete predicate. No `だ` - in the polite form it becomes `いいです`.*

- ❌ <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>しないといいですよ。（meaning "you shouldn't push yourself"） → ✅ <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>しない**ほうが**いいですよ。<br>*A negative clause in front of `といい` is read as a wish, so your advice turns into "I hope you don't", which is not what you meant.*

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - should · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ (<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に)「この<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>めたらいいです。」 → ✅ 「この<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>めてはいかがでしょうか。」<br>*The grammar is fine; the direction is not. Advice aimed upwards needs a different frame, not just a `です` on the end.*

# ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope

**Meaning** · I hope ~ ; here's hoping ~ - a wish about an outcome nobody in the conversation controls

Same words as the advice pattern, different job. The conditional still sketches a world and calls it good; what changes is that neither speaker nor listener can bring that world about. The weather, an exam result, somebody's recovery, whether the train is on time - once the outcome is out of human hands, "you should" is impossible and the only reading left is **hope**.

This is the everyday Japanese for "I hope so", and the fixed phrase `～といいですね` is worth learning as a single unit.

Unlike the advice reading, the hope reading takes adjectives and nouns as happily as verbs, because a hope can be about a *state*.

## ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope · Nuance & register

The pattern is neutral and usable at every level of politeness, but **whose** hope it is changes the ending, and getting that wrong is the most audible mistake learners make here.

For a hope about the **listener's** situation, the ending is `ですね` (or plain `ね`). `ね` asks the listener to share a feeling, and since the feeling is about them, that works: <ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>てるといいですね。 This is the standard way of wishing somebody luck, and it is far more common than 「がんばってください」 in careful adult speech.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope · Nuance & register (cont.)

For a hope of **your own**, the natural ending trails off: `～といいんですが`, `～といいのですが`, `～といいんですけど`. The unfinished `が`/`けど` does the work of English "…though" - it stops the sentence from sounding like a demand. <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うといいんですが is modest; <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うといいですね about your own train sounds like you are asking a stranger to agree with your wishes.

`なあ` is the private version. It is spoken to yourself or to somebody very close, it draws the vowel out, and it is one of the most natural-sounding things a learner can say: <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだといいなあ。 In writing it appears mostly in dialogue and diaries.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two small points of substance. First, the negative `ないといい` is fully idiomatic for hoping something **doesn't** happen - <ruby>渋滞<rt>じゅうたい</rt></ruby>していないといいですね - and is the normal way to say it. Second, when the outcome is already settled but unknown to you, put the clause into `ている`/`ていた`: <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れずに<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていってくれて いるといいんですが. The hope is not that it will happen, but that it already did.

---

## ～といい vs ～たらいい in the hope reading

> **Rule of thumb** - both are used and neither is wrong. `と` presents the hoped-for thing as simply happening, and is the default in the set phrases. `たら` frames it a little more as a condition being met, so it leans slightly further from expectation.

<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>、**<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しいといい**ですね。<br>*I hope next month's trip is fun.*

<ruby>宝<rt>たから</rt></ruby>くじ、**<ruby>当<rt>あ</rt></ruby>たったらいい**なあ。<br>*Wouldn't it be something if the lottery came in.*

---

## ～といい vs ～たらいい in the hope reading (cont.)

The trip is a real event with a decent chance of being fun; `と` fits. Winning the lottery is remote, and `たら` sits more comfortably with remoteness. Do not turn this into a rule - <code><ruby>当<rt>あ</rt></ruby>たるといいなあ</code> is perfectly natural too. What is close to fixed is the polite formula: 「～といいですね」 is heard far more often than 「～たらいいですね」.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope · ～といいですね vs ～といいんですが

> **Rule of thumb** - `ですね` for somebody else's hope, `んですが` for your own. `ね` asks the listener to agree, and you cannot ask people to agree that your wishes should come true.

<ruby>面接<rt>めんせつ</rt></ruby>、**うまくいくといい**ですね。<br>*I hope your interview goes well.*

<ruby>面接<rt>めんせつ</rt></ruby>、**うまくいくといい**んですが。<br>*I hope my interview goes well - we'll see.*

The second is what you say about yourself, and the trailing `が` is not laziness; it is the politeness. Ending flatly on 「うまくいくといいです」 about your own affairs sounds blunt in a way that is hard to explain and easy to hear.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope · ～といい vs ～てほしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `てほしい` aims at a **person** and asks them to act. `といい` aims at the **world** and asks nothing of anybody.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>を**もらえるといい**んですが。<br>*I hope I can get an answer by tomorrow.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>を**もらいたい**んですが。<br>*I'd like an answer by tomorrow, please.*

The first leaves the other party free; the second is a request wearing a soft coat. Said to the person who owes you the answer, `といい` is a hint and `てほしい`／`たい` is a push. Choosing the wrong one either fails to get you your answer or annoys the person who has to give it.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、いい<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby>といいですね。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、いい<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby>**だ**といいですね。<br>*A noun needs `だ` before this `と`. The same goes for な-adjectives: <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>だといいですね.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>無事<rt>ぶじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>くといいですね。 → ✅ …<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>くといい**んですが**。<br>*`ですね` invites agreement, which only works when the hope is about the listener. About your own luggage it sounds like you are recruiting them into your worry.*

- ❌ <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくれるといいです。（meaning "I want you to help"） → ✅ <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>って**ほしい**です。<br>*Said to the person who could help, a hope is not a request. Use `てほしい` when you actually want them to act.*

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - I hope · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らないといいだね。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らないといいね。<br>*`いい` is an い-adjective and never takes `だ` in front of `ね`.*

# ～たらいい / ～といい - it would be nice if

**Meaning** · it would be nice if ~; I wish ~ were the case - a wish aimed at something that is not so and probably won't be

The third reading of the same words. Advice supposes the listener will act; hope supposes the world might oblige. This one supposes **neither**. The clause describes a state that is not this one - a bigger flat, a shorter commute, a father who listens - and the speaker looks at it and sighs.

Nothing in the conjugation marks the difference. What marks it is `のに` (and its spoken relatives `なあ`, `んだけど`), which quietly supplies the second half of the thought: *…but it isn't*.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - it would be nice if · Formation

The wish reading takes anything that can be a state, and normally carries `のに` or `なあ` on the end. Without one of those, the sentence usually slides back into the plain "I hope" reading.

## ～たらいい / ～といい - it would be nice if · Nuance & register

This is the emotional member of the family, and it sounds like it. `のに` on its own is already a small complaint; `のになあ` drawn out is a sigh; `んだけど` trailing away is the resigned version that adults use in conversation. All of them are casual, and none of them belongs in a report.

The polite equivalent is `～たらいいのですが`, which sounds measured rather than wistful, and is what you would write in an email. Note that `～のに` does not politely become `～のにです`; if you need politeness, the sentence has to be rebuilt.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - it would be nice if · Nuance & register (cont.)

Direction matters more here than in the other two readings. Aimed at yourself or at the world, `のに` is harmless. Aimed at a **person present**, it becomes a reproach: <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってくれたらいいのに is not a wish, it is a complaint that they didn't. That can be affectionate between friends and is close to insubordinate said upwards at work. If a superior did something inconvenient, do not reach for this pattern.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - it would be nice if · Nuance & register (cont.)

One consequence of the "…but it isn't" is worth spelling out: using `のに` about something that could still easily happen sounds defeatist, and occasionally rude. Saying 「<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>のピクニック、<ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れたらいいのに」 to the person who organised it tells them you have written the day off. The live-hope form 「<ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れるといいですね」 is what you actually want.

Finally, note that the wish reading loves the **potential** form - <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せたら, <ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>めたら, <ruby>飛<rt>と</rt></ruby>べたら. That is not an accident: the things we wish for are usually things we cannot do, and the potential is where that shows up in the grammar.

---

## ～たらいいのに vs ～といいですね

> **Rule of thumb** - the difference is **expectation**, not form. `といいですね` treats the outcome as still open; `たらいいのに` treats it as closed and disappointing.

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>、**<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>てるといい**ですね。<br>*I hope we can win next week's match.*

うちのチームにもう<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>いい<ruby>選手<rt>せんしゅ</rt></ruby>が**いたらいい**のに。<br>*If only our team had one more decent player.*

The match has not been played, so hope is available. The squad is what it is, so only a wish is. Learners who use `のに` for every wish end up sounding pessimistic about perfectly ordinary plans.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - it would be nice if · ～たらいいのに vs ～たい

> **Rule of thumb** - `たい` is for what **you** could go and do. `たらいいのに` is for what you cannot arrange: a state of the world, somebody else's behaviour, an ability you do not have.

<ruby>沖縄<rt>おきなわ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい**なあ。<br>*I want to go to Okinawa.*

<ruby>沖縄<rt>おきなわ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けたらいい**のになあ。<br>*I wish you could get to Okinawa by train.*

The first is a desire you could act on tomorrow with a plane ticket. The second wishes the map were different. Using `たらいいのに` where `たい` belongs makes you sound oddly helpless about your own life; using `たい` where the world is the obstacle simply doesn't parse.

---

## ～たらいいのに vs ～ばよかった

> **Rule of thumb** - `たらいいのに` wishes about **now**: the state is wrong at this moment. `ばよかった` regrets the **past**: a decision went the wrong way and cannot be retaken.

この<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>、もっと<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>**かったらいい**のに。<br>*I wish this dictionary were lighter.*

<ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>いほうを**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えばよかった**。<br>*I should have bought the lighter one.*

The dictionary's weight is a present fact you are stuck with; the purchase is a past act you are second-guessing. If you can name the moment you would go back and change, you want `ばよかった`.

---

## ～たらいい / ～といい - it would be nice if · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>いだったらいいのに。 → ✅ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>**かったら**いいのに。<br>*An い-adjective forms its own past - `ひろかった` - and never borrows `だった`.*

- ❌ あたたかいスープが<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたらいいなあ。 → ✅ あたたかいスープが<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>**べたい**なあ。<br>*Eating soup is something you can go and do, so the desire form is `たい`. The wish pattern is for what you cannot arrange.*

- ❌ (<ruby>運動会<rt>うんどうかい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前日<rt>ぜんじつ</rt></ruby>に)「<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れたらいいのに。」 → ✅ 「<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れるといいですね。」<br>*`のに` announces that you expect it not to happen. For a genuinely open outcome, use the live-hope form.*

- ❌ (<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に)「もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくれたらいいのに。」 → ✅ 「もう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>くお<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らせいただけると<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かります。」<br>*Pointed at a person, `のに` is a complaint about what they did. # ～ば○○のに

**Meaning** · if only A were so, B would be - and A is not, which is exactly the problem

Two pieces do the work. The `ば`-clause names a condition the speaker asserts is **not** met; the main clause names what would follow if it were. `のに` then sits at the end and marks the gap between those two worlds as a grievance.

This is the same `のに` you met at N4 meaning "although" - a marker of a contrast that ought not to be there. Here the contrast is between the reality you are stuck with and the one you can see perfectly well from where you are standing.

---

## ～ば○○のに · Formation

The front half is an ordinary `ば`-form. The back half is a plain clause plus `のに`, and **its tense is what tells you whether the wish is about now or about the past**.

---

## ～ば○○のに · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ば-form + …のに | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>れば…のに |
| Verb (negative) | なければ + …のに | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わない → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わなければ…のに |
| い-adjective | ければ + …のに | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ければ…のに |
| いい | irregular | いい → よければ…のに |
| な-adjective | stem + なら + …のに | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かなら…のに |
| Noun | Noun + なら + …のに | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みなら…のに |
| Main clause - present wish | plain non-past + のに | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ければ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うのに |
| Main clause - past wish | plain past + のに | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ければ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったのに |
| `たら` version | たら + …のに | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってくれたら…のに |

---

## ～ば○○のに · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Truncated | ～ばいいのに／～ばよかったのに | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ればいいのに |

> **Rule of thumb** - <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ければ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う**のに** = it isn't cheap, so I'm not buying. <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>ければ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った**のに** = it wasn't cheap, so I didn't. Only the main verb moves; the `ば`-clause looks identical in both.

> **Note** - at N4 you learned that a `ば`-clause with an action verb cannot be followed by a request, a command or a statement of intention. That restriction never bites here, because the main clause of a counterfactual is a statement about an imaginary world, not something anyone is being asked to do. This is one of the places where `ば` is at its most natural.

---

## ～ば○○のに · Examples

もう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>**ければ**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う**のに**。<br>*If it were a bit cheaper I'd buy it.*

<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**あれば**<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>える**のに**。<br>*I'd help if I had the time.*

<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby>が**よければ**、ここから<ruby>富士山<rt>ふじさん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>えた**のに**。<br>*If the weather had been better, you'd have been able to see Mt Fuji from here.*

<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>が**あれば**、こんなに<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>苦労<rt>くろう</rt></ruby>しない**のに**。<br>*If we had a car we wouldn't be struggling with the luggage like this.*

---

## ～ば○○のに · Examples (cont.)

もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>病院<rt>びょういん</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていれば**、こんなに<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>く<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>まずにすんだ**のに**。<br>*If I'd gone to the doctor sooner, I wouldn't have had to take so much time off.*

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>**れば**<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>った**のに**、<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はのんびり<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いていた。<br>*He'd have made it if he'd run to the station, but he just strolled along.*

<ruby>一言<rt>ひとこと</rt></ruby><ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>してくれ**れば**、<ruby>力<rt>ちから</rt></ruby>になれた**のに**と<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>でも<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う。<br>*I still think that if she'd said one word to me, I could have helped.*

この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>にもう<ruby>一本<rt>いっぽん</rt></ruby><ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>って**いれば**、<ruby>若<rt>わか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>もこんなに<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ていかなかった**のに**。<br>*If this town had one more rail line, the young people wouldn't have left in these numbers.*

---

## ～ば○○のに · Nuance & register

`のに` is not neutral. It is the audible sound of dissatisfaction, and the sentence is usually delivered with a fall on the last syllable and nothing after it. That is also why the main clause is so often left out entirely - 「もう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってくれれば…」 - with the trailing silence doing what `のに` would have done. Both versions are casual.

---

## ～ば○○のに · Nuance & register (cont.)

Who the complaint lands on is the thing to watch. When the unmet condition is about the speaker or about the world, the sentence is simply regret, and nobody is hurt by it. When the condition is about **the person you are talking to** - 「<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>して くれれば<ruby>迎<rt>むか</rt></ruby>えに<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったのに」 - it is a reproach, however gently you say it. Between friends and family that is normal and often affectionate. Upwards at work it is close to unusable; rebuild the thought as 「<ruby>次回<rt>じかい</rt></ruby>はご<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>いただければ<ruby>幸<rt>さいわ</rt></ruby>いです」 and let the complaint go.

---

## ～ば○○のに · Nuance & register (cont.)

There is no polite form of `のに` itself. If you need the register lifted, the sentence becomes `～のですが` - <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>があればお<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>いできるのですが - which is measured and slightly formal, and loses most of the sting.

`ば` and `たら` are both usable in this frame and neither is wrong. `ば` is the tighter, more reasoned one and dominates when the sentence sounds like an argument; `たら` is a shade more conversational. `なら` is the odd one out: it can appear (<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>ならこんな<ruby>決定<rt>けってい</rt></ruby>はしないのに) but only where the condition is genuinely a premise about identity or circumstance, not an event.

---

## ～ば○○のに · ～ば○○のに vs ～ばよかった

> **Rule of thumb** - `ばよかった` names the **action** you wish had been taken and stops there. `ば…のに` names the **consequence** you were denied. The first is self-directed regret; the second easily points at somebody else.

もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ればよかった**。<br>*I should have left earlier.*

もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>れば**、<ruby>渋滞<rt>じゅうたい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>巻<rt>ま</rt></ruby>き<ruby>込<rt>こ</rt></ruby>まれなかった**のに**。<br>*If we'd left earlier we wouldn't have got caught in the traffic.*

---

## ～ば○○のに · ～ば○○のに vs ～ばよかった (cont.)

The first is a verdict on a decision. The second spells out what the decision cost. Note that only the second can comfortably take a second person as its subject - turn `ばよかった` on somebody else and you have to add `のに` anyway to make it land: <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってくれればよかったのに.

---

## ～ば○○のに · ～ば○○のに vs ～ばいいのに

> **Rule of thumb** - `ばいいのに` is about **now**: I wish this were the case, and it still could be. `ば…のに` with a past main clause is about a chance that has closed.

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんも<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>**ればいいのに**。<br>*I wish Yamada would come too.*

<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんも<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>**れば**、みんなで<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>れた**のに**。<br>*If Yamada had come too, we could have had a photo with everyone in it.*

The first is aimed at a party that has not started; the second at one that is over. In speech the difference is carried entirely by the tense of the main verb, so it is worth practising the pair aloud until the switch is automatic.

---

## ～ば○○のに · ～ば○○のに vs plain ～ば

> **Rule of thumb** - without `のに`, the condition is left open and might yet be met. With `のに`, the speaker has asserted that it is not.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>**れれば**、<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*If it's fine tomorrow, we'll go to the sea.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>**れれば**<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ける**のに**。<br>*We could go to the sea if only it were going to be fine tomorrow.*

The first is a plan with a condition attached. The second has already read the forecast. That is why `のに` cannot be added to a sentence about something you are genuinely waiting to find out - it gives away an answer you do not have.

---

## ～ば○○のに · Common mistakes

- ❌ もっと<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>**いければ**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うのに。 → ✅ もっと<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>**ければ**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うのに。<br>*The `い` of an い-adjective is replaced, not kept. `やすい` → `やすければ`.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>が<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み**だなら**、<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くのに。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>が<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み**なら**、<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くのに。<br>*`なら` is itself the copula's `ば`-form, so no `だ` goes in front of it. Compare `としたら`, which requires one.*

- ❌ (<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わった<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>について) もっと<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>すれば<ruby>合格<rt>ごうかく</rt></ruby>する**のに**。 → ✅ …<ruby>合格<rt>ごうかく</rt></ruby>**した**のに。<br>*The main clause carries the time. A non-past main verb says the chance is still open, which contradicts an exam you have already sat.*

---

- ❌ (<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>に)「<ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってくださればもっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わったのに。」 → ✅ 「<ruby>次回<rt>じかい</rt></ruby>はお<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>を<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>していただけると<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かります。」<br>*Aimed upwards, `のに` is a complaint about a superior's conduct. # ～ばよかった

**Meaning** · I should have ~; I wish I had ~ - looking back at a choice and wanting it back

`ば` names an action; `よかった` is `いい` in the past, passing judgement on the world in which that action was taken. Put together they say: *doing it would have been the good outcome* - and since you are saying so afterwards, you didn't.

This is the most useful single thing an intermediate learner can say about their own past. English needs four different constructions - "I should have", "I wish I'd", "I ought to have", "why didn't I" - and Japanese does all of them with one ending. It is worth over-practising.

---

## ～ばよかった · Formation

Any `ば`-form plus `よかった`. The `よかった` is frozen: it is always past, and it never becomes ❌`ばいいでした`.

---

| Verb (する) | すれば + よかった | <ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>すればよかった |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | irregular | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ればよかった |

## ～ばよかった · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | ばよかったです | <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>けばよかったです |

> **Rule of thumb** - the negative is not a softener, it is a reversal. <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>えばよかった = I wish I *had* bought it. <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わなければよかった = I wish I *hadn't*. Learners reach for the negative when they mean the positive more often than for any other pattern in this group.

## ～ばよかった · Examples

もっと<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ればよかった**。<br>*I should have gone to bed earlier.*

こんなに<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るなら、<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってくればよかった**。<br>*If it was going to rain like this, I should have brought an umbrella.*

<ruby>人気<rt>にんき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>だから、<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>しておけばよかった**ですね。<br>*It's a popular place - we should have booked the day before.*

---

## ～ばよかった · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かめておけばよかった**。おかげで３０<ruby>分<rt>ぷん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>迷<rt>まよ</rt></ruby>ってしまった。<br>*I should have checked the map. As it is I spent half an hour lost.*

こんなに<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がかかると<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>かっていたら、<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>のうちに**<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めればよかった**。<br>*If I'd known it would take this long, I'd have started first thing.*

## ～ばよかった · Examples (cont.)

あのとき<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>を**やめなければよかった**のかな、と<ruby>時々<rt>ときどき</rt></ruby><ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えることがあります。<br>*Now and then I wonder whether I should have stayed at that company after all.*

---

## ～ばよかった · Nuance & register

Bare `ばよかった` is casual, self-directed and extremely common. With `なあ` on the end it is close to a sigh - <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>っておけばよかったなあ - and that is how it most often turns up in speech, half muttered, with nobody expected to answer.

The polite `ばよかったです` is perfectly usable and you will hear it, though in careful or written Japanese <code>～ばよかったと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code> sounds more measured and less like an outburst. In an apology at work the natural frame is a humble verb: <ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めにご<ruby>報告<rt>ほうこく</rt></ruby>すればよかったです、<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ありません。

---

## ～ばよかった · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two combinations carry most of the traffic. `ておけばよかった` - "should have done it in advance" - is the workhorse, because most regret is about preparation you skipped. And `なければよかった` is the regret of having done something, which learners chronically avoid because the double negative feels backwards in English.

Direction is what changes the temperature. Pointed at yourself, `ばよかった` is harmless and even endearing. Pointed at somebody else it needs `のに`, and then it becomes a gentle "why on earth didn't you": <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってくれればよかったのに。 That is milder than it looks - it often functions as consolation rather than blame - but it is still a comment on their decision, so aim it downwards or sideways, not upwards.

---

## ～ばよかった · Nuance & register (cont.)

Finally, a limit worth respecting. `ばよかった` is about a **choice**, so its most natural subject is somebody who could have chosen otherwise. For things nobody controlled - the weather, an illness, a train - a bare `ばよかった` sounds incomplete; add `のに` and let it be a wish instead: <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れればよかったのに。

`たらよかった` exists and is heard, particularly in casual speech and in western Japan. `ばよかった` is the standard set form and the one to produce; treat `たらよかった` as something to recognise.

---

## ～ばよかった · ～ばよかった vs ～てよかった

> **Rule of thumb** - `てよかった` looks at what **did** happen and is glad. `ばよかった` looks at what **didn't** and wishes it had. One is relief, the other is regret, and the only visible difference is the conditional.

<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めに<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>てよかった**。<br>*I'm glad I left the house early.*

<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めに<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ればよかった**。<br>*I should have left the house early.*

Both end in `よかった`, and beginners hear them as the same sentence. The `て`-form reports something real; the `ば`-form floats something imaginary. If you are ever unsure which one you have just said, check whether the thing actually happened.

---

## ～ばよかった · ～ばよかった vs ～ばよかったのに

> **Rule of thumb** - without `のに`, the regret is yours. With `のに`, it is aimed at somebody else and means "you should have".

<ruby>会<rt>かい</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ればよかった**。<br>*I should have come to the gathering.*

<ruby>会<rt>かい</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>ればよかったのに**。<br>*You should have come to the gathering - it's a shame you didn't.*

The second is what you say to a friend who stayed home, and it usually reads as warmth rather than criticism: *we'd have liked you there*. Drop the `のに` while talking about somebody else and the sentence sounds like you are regretting **their** existence at your own event, which is not the effect anyone wants.

---

## ～ばよかった · ～ばよかった vs ～べきだった / ～たほうがよかった

> **Rule of thumb** - `ばよかった` is felt: it is a wish. `べきだった` is judged: it is an obligation you failed. `たほうがよかった` is weighed: of two options, the other one was better.

<ruby>親<rt>おや</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>すればよかった**。<br>*I wish I'd talked to my parents about it.*

<ruby>親<rt>おや</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>相談<rt>そうだん</rt></ruby>すべきだった**。<br>*I ought to have consulted my parents.*

<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>より、バスで**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったほうがよかった**ですね。<br>*It would have been better to go by bus than by train.*

---

## ～ばよかった · ～ばよかった vs ～べきだった / ～たほうがよかった (cont.)

`べきだった` is stiffer, more written, and carries a moral edge - it is the form of a public apology and of self-reproach. `たほうがよかった` is the coolest of the three; it compares two courses without much feeling and is what you use when reviewing a decision with colleagues. `ばよかった` is the one that sounds like a person.

---

## ～ばよかった · Common mistakes

- ❌ もっと<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるばよかった。 → ✅ もっと<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>**べれば**よかった。<br>*`よかった` follows the `ば`-form. The dictionary form never appears here.*

- ❌ <ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってこなければよかった。（meaning "I should have brought an umbrella"） → ✅ <ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>って**くれば**よかった。<br>*The negative reverses the regret. As written, the sentence says you wish you had left the umbrella at home - which, standing in the rain, is the opposite of your point.*

- ❌ もっと<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>すればいいでした。 → ✅ もっと<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>すれば**よかった**です。<br>*`いい` cannot be put into the past behind `ば` while staying `いい`. The past of `いい` is `よかった`, and it takes the whole meaning with it.*

---

## ～ばよかった · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ (<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>に)「<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>先<rt>さき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってくれればよかったのに。」 → ✅ 「<ruby>次<rt>つぎ</rt></ruby>からは<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めに<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>するようにいたします。」<br>*`ばよかったのに` is a judgement on the other person's choice. Aimed at a superior that is insubordinate however politely you say it; convert it into what you will do next time.*

---

## ～ばよかった · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>のチケットが<ruby>売<rt>う</rt></ruby>り<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>れだった。ネットで<ruby>先<rt>さき</rt></ruby>に____。

{reveal}

{reveal}

## ～ばよかった · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: <ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってきたから、<ruby>濡<rt>ぬ</rt></ruby>れずにすんだ。（<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってくればよかった・<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってきてよかった）。

{reveal}

{reveal}
The umbrella *was* brought, so the speaker is relieved, not regretful. <code><ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>ってくればよかった</code> would contradict the first half of the sentence by claiming the umbrella stayed at home.

{reveal}

# ～つもりだった

**Meaning** · I had meant to (but didn't); I thought I had (but I hadn't) - an intention or a belief that reality declined to confirm

`つもり` is what is in your head. Put it in the past and you announce that what was in your head and what happened in the world came apart. Nothing in the words says the plan failed - but the past tense sets up a "but" so reliably that a Japanese listener hears it before you finish the sentence.

The pattern has **two** readings, and which one you get depends entirely on the tense of the verb in front:

---

## ～つもりだった · Overview

- <code><ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>すつもりだった</code> - I meant to send it. (I didn't.)
- <code><ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>したつもりだった</code> - I thought I had sent it. (I hadn't.)

---

## ～つもりだった · Formation

`つもり` is a noun, so what precedes it takes the shape a noun-modifying clause takes: な-adjectives get `な`, nouns get `の`.

---

## ～つもりだった · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb - intention | plain non-past + つもりだった | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くつもりだった |
| Verb - intention (negative) | ない-form + つもりだった | <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わない → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わないつもりだった |
| Verb - mistaken belief | plain past + つもりだった | かけた → かけたつもりだった |
| Verb - belief about a state | ている + つもりだった | やっている → やっているつもりだった |
| い-adjective | plain form + つもりだった | <ruby>優<rt>やさ</rt></ruby>しい → <ruby>優<rt>やさ</rt></ruby>しいつもりだった |
| な-adjective | stem + **な** + つもりだった | <ruby>親切<rt>しんせつ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>親切<rt>しんせつ</rt></ruby>なつもりだった |
| Noun | Noun + **の** + つもりだった | <ruby>冗談<rt>じょうだん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>冗談<rt>じょうだん</rt></ruby>のつもりだった |
| Polite | つもりでした | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くつもりでした |
| With the "but" spelled out | つもりだったのに／つもりだったが | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くつもりだったのに |

---

## ～つもりだった · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Denying an intention | ～つもりはなかった | <ruby>傷<rt>きず</rt></ruby>つける → <ruby>傷<rt>きず</rt></ruby>つけるつもりはなかった |

> **Rule of thumb** - `つもり` needs a **will** behind it. Only something that can decide things can have one. A train, a parcel or the weather cannot: for those the pattern is `はずだった`.

---

## ～つもりだった · Examples

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby><ruby>銀行<rt>ぎんこう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**つもりだった**んですが、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がありませんでした。<br>*I'd meant to go to the bank today, but I didn't have time.*

<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>をかけた**つもりだった**のに、ドアが<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いていた。<br>*I could have sworn I locked up, but the door was open.*

あれは<ruby>冗談<rt>じょうだん</rt></ruby>の**つもりだった**んです。<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>にすみませんでした。<br>*It was meant as a joke. I really am sorry.*

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>では<ruby>丁寧<rt>ていねい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>した**つもりだった**が、まったく<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>わっていなかった。<br>*I thought I'd explained it carefully, but none of it had got through.*

---

## ～つもりだった · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たら<ruby>地元<rt>じもと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**つもりだった**けれど、<ruby>結局<rt>けっきょく</rt></ruby><ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>った。<br>*I'd intended to go back home after university, but in the end I stayed in Tokyo.*

お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>は<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby><ruby>返<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>した**つもりでした**が、まだ<ruby>三千円<rt>さんぜんえん</rt></ruby><ruby>残<rt>のこ</rt></ruby>っていたようです。<br>*I was under the impression I'd paid it all back, but apparently three thousand yen is still outstanding.*

<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>もしない**つもりだった**のに、<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がついたら<ruby>一日中<rt>いちにちじゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けをしていた。<br>*I'd planned to do nothing all weekend, and then found I'd spent the whole day tidying.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>にはお<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えした**つもりでした**が、<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>勘違<rt>かんちが</rt></ruby>いだったようです。<ruby>申<rt>もう</rt></ruby>し<ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>ございません。<br>*I was sure I'd passed it on to the manager, but it seems I was mistaken. My apologies.*

---

## ～つもりだった · Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral - `つもりでした` sits comfortably in a business email - but it is socially loaded, because most of the time you are using it to explain yourself.

The intention reading is the everyday softener of excuses. The full apology shape is `～つもりだったんですが` with a trailing clause: <ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>するつもりだったんです が、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>びいてしまって。 The unfinished ending is deliberate. It lets the listener supply the conclusion, which is more polite than announcing it.

---

## ～つもりだった · Nuance & register (cont.)

The mistaken-belief reading (`～たつもりだった`) is the Japanese for "I could have sworn I…". It is honest and unglamorous, and it is what you say when you are the one who dropped something. Its slightly sharper cousin is `～ているつもりだった` - "I thought I was doing that" - which comes up when somebody tells you your effort has not shown: <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>をつけているつもりだったんですが。

There is one construction here you should learn as a unit: `～つもりはなかった`, "I didn't mean to." <ruby>傷<rt>きず</rt></ruby>つけるつもりはありませんでした is the standard way to apologise for an accidental hurt. Note the `は`: it is contrastive, and it does the work of "whatever else I may have done, that was not my intention." The casual version is `つもりじゃなかった`.

---

## ～つもりだった · Nuance & register (cont.)

> **Note** - do not confuse this with `～たつもりで`, a separate idiom meaning "as if you had": <ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったつもりで<ruby>貯金<rt>ちょきん</rt></ruby>する - "save the money as though you'd taken the trip." The `で` form is a way of pretending on purpose; the `だった` form is finding out you were wrong.

---

## ～つもりだった · ～つもりだった vs ～はずだった

> **Rule of thumb** - `つもり` is what **you decided**. `はず` is what the **facts led you to expect**. If somebody asked どうして？ and your answer would be "because I'd made up my mind to", it is `つもり`; if it would be "because that's what the schedule said", it is `はず`.

<ruby>七時<rt>しちじ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>る**つもりだった**んだけど、<ruby>寝坊<rt>ねぼう</rt></ruby>した。<br>*I'd meant to catch the seven o'clock train, but I overslept.*

<ruby>七時<rt>しちじ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>八時<rt>はちじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>く**はずだった**のに、まだ<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かない。<br>*The seven o'clock was supposed to arrive at eight, and it still hasn't moved.*

Only a person can hold an intention, which is why the second sentence cannot take `つもり`. Trains have timetables, not plans.

---

## ～つもりだった · ～るつもりだった vs ～たつもりだった

> **Rule of thumb** - non-past in front means the action was never performed. Past in front means you believe it was performed, and you were wrong.

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めるつもりだった**。<br>*I meant to close the window.*

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めたつもりだった**。<br>*I thought I'd closed the window.*

In the first, the window was never touched. In the second, you did something to it and it wasn't enough. That is a real difference in what you are confessing to, and getting it backwards in an apology gives entirely the wrong impression of how careful you were.

---

## ～つもりだった · ～つもりだった vs ～(よ)うと思っていた

> **Rule of thumb** - `つもりだった` is a **decision** you were carrying around. <code>～(よ)うと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っていた</code> is a **thought** you were entertaining. The second is vaguer, and therefore softer.

<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>す**つもりだった**んですが、<ruby>事情<rt>じじょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わりました。<br>*I'd been planning to move next month, but circumstances changed.*

<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>そうと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っていたんですが、まだ<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>めていません。<br>*I'd been thinking of moving next month, but I haven't decided yet.*

---

## ～つもりだった · ～つもりだった vs ～(よ)うと思っていた (cont.)

Because `つもり` reports something settled, it can sound firm when you are asked about your plans by somebody with authority over them. If you want to leave yourself room, <code><ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っていた</code> is the more diplomatic choice.

---

## ～つもりだった · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きますつもりでした。 → ✅ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>**く**つもりでした。<br>*`つもり` is a noun and takes a plain noun-modifying clause. The politeness goes on the end of the sentence, never inside it.*

- ❌ <ruby>冗談<rt>じょうだん</rt></ruby>つもりだったんです。 → ✅ <ruby>冗談<rt>じょうだん</rt></ruby>**の**つもりだったんです。<br>*A noun in front of `つもり` needs `の`; a な-adjective needs `な` (<ruby>親切<rt>しんせつ</rt></ruby>なつもり).*

- ❌ <ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>くつもりだった。 → ✅ <ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>く**はずだった**。<br>*`つもり` requires somebody with a will. Timetables, deliveries and the weather take `はずだった`.*

---

## ～つもりだった · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>らせるつもりじゃなかったです。 → ✅ <ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>らせるつもり**はありませんでした**。<br>*The meaning is right but the registers clash: casual `じゃなかった` under a polite `です`. In an apology, use the full form.*

---

# ～はずだった

**Meaning** · it was supposed to ~, and it didn't - a well-grounded expectation that the world declined to meet

At N4, `はずだ` was an expectation you could justify if asked: a timetable, a promise, a rule, a fact somebody told you. `はずだった` keeps the grounds and loses the outcome. It says *this is what should have followed from what I knew* - and the fact that you are saying it in the past tense tells the listener that it did not.

Where `つもりだった` reports a plan of your own that came to nothing, `はずだった` reports the world failing to behave as the evidence promised. Nobody's will is involved, which is why it works for parcels, trains and weather forecasts.

---

## ～はずだった · Formation

`はず` is a noun, so the clause in front takes noun-modifying shapes: `な` for な-adjectives, `の` for nouns.

---

## ～はずだった · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain non-past + はずだった | <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>くはずだった |
| Verb (past) | た-form + はずだった | <ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れた → <ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>れたはずだった |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + はずだった | <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないはずだった |
| い-adjective | plain form + はずだった | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いはずだった |
| な-adjective | stem + **な** + はずだった | <ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>なはずだった |
| Noun | Noun + **の** + はずだった | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みのはずだった |
| Polite | はずでした | <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>くはずでした |
| With the "but" spelled out | はずだったのに／はずだったが | <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>くはずだったのに |
| Set phrase | こんなはずではなかった | |

---

## ～はずだった · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - say `はずだった` and you have as good as said the rest. If the expectation *was* met, you do not use this pattern at all - you report the fact, or say <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>どおり. Using `はずだった` about something that worked out puzzles the listener, who is still waiting for the "but".

---

## ～はずだった · Examples

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わる**はずだった**。<br>*The meeting was supposed to finish at three.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みの**はずだった**のに、<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>呼<rt>よ</rt></ruby>び<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>された。<br>*Today was supposed to be my day off, and then I got called in out of nowhere.*

<ruby>財布<rt>さいふ</rt></ruby>にお<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>が<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>っている**はずだった**んですが、<ruby>空<rt>から</rt></ruby>っぽでした。<br>*There was supposed to be money in my wallet, but it was empty.*

<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>な<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>の**はずでした**が、けっきょく<ruby>三日<rt>みっか</rt></ruby>もかかりました。<br>*It was meant to be a simple job, and it ended up taking three days.*

---

## ～はずだった · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいパソコンは<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>く**はずでした**が、まだ<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>いておりません。<br>*The new laptop was due to arrive yesterday, but it still hasn't come.*

## ～はずだった · Nuance & register

`はずだった` is register-neutral and travels everywhere, which makes it unusually useful at work. The polite `はずでした` plus a trailing `が` is the standard way of raising a problem without accusing anybody: <ruby>本日<rt>ほんじつ</rt></ruby><ruby>午前中<rt>ごぜんちゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>く はずでしたが、まだ<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>できておりません。 It is firm - you are pointing out that a commitment was not kept - while attributing the failure to the situation rather than to the person you are speaking to. That combination is hard to achieve in English and easy here.

---

## ～はずだった · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two flavours are worth separating. With a **non-past** clause in front, the pattern is about a scheduled or predicted event that failed to occur. With a **past** clause (<code><ruby>渡<rt>わた</rt></ruby>したはずだった</code>) it is about your own confident memory being wrong - "I was sure I had." That second one shades into `～たつもりだった`, and the difference is where the confidence came from: `つもり` from your intention, `はず` from evidence you thought you had.

The negative deserves its own line. `こんなはずではなかった` - "this isn't how it was supposed to go" - is a fixed phrase, slightly dramatic, and extremely common in novels, films and genuine bad days. Its casual form is `こんなはずじゃなかった`.

---

## ～はずだった · Nuance & register (cont.)

One honest qualification. In a narrative, `はずだった` can simply report what was expected at a point in the story, and the next sentence may confirm it. But in conversation, standing alone, it is heard as a defeated expectation almost without exception - so treat the failure reading as the default and do not rely on the neutral one.

---

## ～はずだった · ～はずだった vs ～つもりだった

> **Rule of thumb** - `つもり` is what somebody **decided**; `はず` is what the **evidence implied**. Only a being with a will can have a `つもり`; anything at all can have a `はず`.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みを<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>る**つもりだった**んですが、<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>りました。<br>*I'd been meaning to take tomorrow off, but work came up.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みの**はずだった**んですが、<ruby>出勤<rt>しゅっきん</rt></ruby>になりました。<br>*Tomorrow was supposed to be a company holiday, but now we're working.*

---

## ～はずだった · ～はずだった vs ～つもりだった (cont.)

The first is about your own decision; the second about the calendar. Note what happens when you get it backwards: ❌<code><ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れるつもりだった</code> attributes an intention to a train, which is not merely unusual but funny.

---

## ～はずだった · ～はずだった vs ～予定だった

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>だった</code> reports a **schedule** and says nothing about whether it held. `はずだった` reports an **expectation** and implies it did not.

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出張<rt>しゅっちょう</rt></ruby>する**<ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>でした**が、<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になりました。<br>*I was scheduled to go to Osaka next week, but it's been called off.*

<ruby>飛行機<rt>ひこうき</rt></ruby>は<ruby>十時<rt>じゅうじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>く**はずでした**。<br>*The plane was supposed to land at ten.*

---

## ～はずだった · ～はずだった vs ～予定だった (cont.)

<code><ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>だった</code> can be followed by "…and that's what happened", which is why it is the neutral word for an itinerary. `はずでした` said on its own already tells the listener the plane is late. If the flight landed on time, you say <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>どおり<ruby>十時<rt>じゅうじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>きました and the `はず` never appears.

---

## ～はずだった · ～はずだった vs ～ことになっていた

> **Rule of thumb** - `ことになっていた` reports an **arrangement** somebody made - a rule, an agreement, a rota. `はずだった` reports your **inference** from whatever grounds you had, which may include such an arrangement.

<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>担当<rt>たんとう</rt></ruby>する**ことになっていた**のに、<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてくれなかった。<br>*I was down to cover reception, and nobody told me.*

<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>には<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かいる**はずだった**のに、<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>空<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いていた。<br>*There was supposed to be somebody on reception, but the desk was empty.*

The first points at a decision that was taken; the second at a conclusion you drew. `ことになっていた` is the better choice whenever you can name who agreed to what, and it is the safer one in a workplace because it appeals to a shared arrangement rather than to your own reasoning.

---

## ～はずだった · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>はずだったのに。 → ✅ <ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>**な**はずだったのに。<br>*`はず` is a noun, so a な-adjective needs `な` and a noun needs `の` (<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みのはず). Compare `かもしれない`, which takes both bare.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>七時<rt>しちじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きるはずだった。（meaning "I meant to get up at seven"） → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>七時<rt>しちじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きる**つもりだった**。<br>*Your own resolve is `つもり`. `はず` would need external grounds - an alarm somebody else set, a schedule you were given.*

- ❌ <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>どおり<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>くはずでした。（meaning "and we did"） → ✅ <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby>どおり<ruby>三時<rt>さんじ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>きました**。<br>*`はずでした` announces that the expectation was not met. If it was, simply report what happened.*

---

## ～はずだった · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ こんなはずじゃなかったです。 → ✅ こんなはず**ではありませんでした**。<br>*The casual contraction under a polite `です` clashes. In careful speech, expand it - or keep the whole sentence casual.*

{reveal}
The expectation came from what kind of train it is, not from anyone's intention, so `はず` is the right noun. `のに` then supplies the contradiction that the second half spells out.

## ～はずだった · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>の<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めた**はずだった**。 (b) この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>の<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めた**つもりだった**。

{reveal}
(a) rests on evidence: the speaker remembers the click, or knows it is on their checklist every evening. The surprise is that the facts they were relying on have let them down.

{reveal}
(b) rests on intention: the speaker meant to lock it and believed they had followed through. It admits more personal responsibility, which is why (b) is the better sentence in an apology and (a) the better one when you are genuinely baffled.
