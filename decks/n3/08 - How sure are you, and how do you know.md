# ～によると / ～によれば

**Meaning** · according to ～ - names where a piece of information came from, when the information is not your own

`によると` attaches to a **source**: a person, a broadcast, a survey, a rumour, a map. It says "this is not mine - here is who or what it came from." Because of that, it is only half of the pattern. The other half is the ending, which has to agree that the information is second-hand: そうだ, ということだ, とのことだ, らしい, or, in newspaper style, という.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Formation

`によると` follows a noun. It never attaches to a verb or an adjective directly - a clause has to be turned into a noun-like source first.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (source) | Noun + によると | <ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby><ruby>予報<rt>よほう</rt></ruby> → <ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby><ruby>予報<rt>よほう</rt></ruby>によると |
| Noun (source), written / formal | Noun + によれば | <ruby>調査<rt>ちょうさ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>調査<rt>ちょうさ</rt></ruby>によれば |
| Noun (source), announcements and broadcasting | Noun + によりますと | <ruby>気象庁<rt>きしょうちょう</rt></ruby> → <ruby>気象庁<rt>きしょうちょう</rt></ruby>によりますと |
| Clause repackaged as a source | Clause + ところによると | <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いたところによると |
| A person's account, casual | Person + の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では | <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby> → <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では |
| Ending the frame requires | … + そうだ / ということだ / とのことだ / らしい / という | <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るそうです |

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the source has to be **outside the speaker**. ❌<code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>によると</code> is not Japanese; for your own view, say <code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えでは</code> or <code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>に<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わせれば</code>. The one exception people do say is <code><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>記憶<rt>きおく</rt></ruby>によると</code> - "as far as I remember" - where the source is your memory rather than you.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Examples

<ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby><ruby>予報<rt>よほう</rt></ruby>**によると**、<ruby>午後<rt>ごご</rt></ruby>から<ruby>風<rt>かぜ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>強<rt>つよ</rt></ruby>くなるそうです。<br>*According to the forecast, the wind picks up from the afternoon.*

<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>**によると**、あの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>水曜日<rt>すいようび</rt></ruby>が<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだそうだ。<br>*My sister says that shop is closed on Wednesdays.*

この<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>**によると**、<ruby>橋<rt>はし</rt></ruby>はもう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>先<rt>さき</rt></ruby>にあるはずだ。<br>*Going by this map, the bridge should be a little further on.*

うわさ**によれば**、<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>から<ruby>制服<rt>せいふく</rt></ruby>が<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わるらしい。<br>*Rumour has it the uniforms are changing from next year.*

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>放送<rt>ほうそう</rt></ruby>**によると**、<ruby>事故<rt>じこ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>影響<rt>えいきょう</rt></ruby>で<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>が20<ruby>分<rt>ぷん</rt></ruby>ほど<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れているとのことです。<br>*According to the station announcement, the trains are running about twenty minutes late because of an accident.*

<ruby>昨夜<rt>ゆうべ</rt></ruby><ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>いたメール**によりますと**、<ruby>面接<rt>めんせつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>場所<rt>ばしょ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わったということです。<br>*According to the email that came last night, the interview venue has changed.*

<ruby>市<rt>し</rt></ruby>が<ruby>行<rt>おこな</rt></ruby>った<ruby>調査<rt>ちょうさ</rt></ruby>**によれば**、この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>む<ruby>外国人<rt>がいこくじん</rt></ruby>は10<ruby>年<rt>ねん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>倍<rt>ばい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>えたという。<br>*According to a survey carried out by the city, the number of foreign residents in this town has doubled in ten years.*

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>近所<rt>きんじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>から<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>**によると**、<ruby>向<rt>む</rt></ruby>かいの<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>すそうですよ。<br>*From what I heard from the neighbours, the family across the road are moving out next month.*

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Nuance & register

The three shapes differ only in formality, and the ordering is easy to hold on to. `によると` is the everyday one and is at home in conversation, email and writing alike. `によれば` - literally the ば-conditional of the same verb `よる` - leans written and formal; newspapers, reports and academic prose use it constantly, and it sounds a touch stiff in chat. `によりますと` is the polite-verb version and belongs to announcements: news readers, station staff and company briefings use it, and it would sound theatrical over dinner.

Below all three sits the casual option, <code><ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では</code>: <code><ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんの<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では、<ruby>今週<rt>こんしゅう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られないって。</code> That is what you actually say to a friend.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Nuance & register (cont.)

The ending is not decoration. Because `によると` announces that the information is somebody else's, closing with <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code> pulls the sentence in two directions at once - the frame disclaims ownership and the ending claims it. Keep the ending on the hearsay side. The one systematic exception is that a source you are *reading off* rather than being told - a map, a timetable, a set of figures - can take an inference ending instead: <code>この<ruby>表<rt>ひょう</rt></ruby>によると、<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>より<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いですね</code>.

One limit worth stating. A source is something that carries a **statement**: a person, a document, a broadcast, a survey. Physical evidence you interpret yourself is not a source. For the sound of rain on the window or the look of the sky, you want <code>この<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>からすると</code> or plain `ようだ`, not `によると`.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · によると vs によれば vs によりますと

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning, three registers: `によると` neutral, `によれば` written and formal, `によりますと` for announcements. If in doubt in speech, use `によると`.

となりのクラスの<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>**によると**、テストは<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>延<rt>の</rt></ruby>びたらしいよ。<br>*A kid in the next class says the test has been pushed back to next week.*

<ruby>政府<rt>せいふ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>発表<rt>はっぴょう</rt></ruby>**によれば**、<ruby>制度<rt>せいど</rt></ruby>の<ruby>見直<rt>みなお</rt></ruby>しは<ruby>来年度<rt>らいねんど</rt></ruby><ruby>以降<rt>いこう</rt></ruby>になるという。<br>*According to the government announcement, the review of the system will not happen until next fiscal year or later.*

ただいま<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>った<ruby>情報<rt>じょうほう</rt></ruby>**によりますと**、<ruby>高速<rt>こうそく</rt></ruby><ruby>道路<rt>どうろ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>通行止<rt>つうこうど</rt></ruby>めは<ruby>解除<rt>かいじょ</rt></ruby>されたもようです。<br>*According to information just in, the motorway closure has been lifted.*

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · によると vs によって

> **Rule of thumb** - both come from the verb `よる`, and they do unrelated jobs. `によると` frames **where information came from**; `によって` marks a **means, an agent, a cause, or a thing that varies**.

<ruby>専門家<rt>せんもんか</rt></ruby>**によると**、この<ruby>建物<rt>たてもの</rt></ruby>は<ruby>地震<rt>じしん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>強<rt>つよ</rt></ruby>いそうだ。<br>*According to the experts, this building stands up well to earthquakes.*

この<ruby>建物<rt>たてもの</rt></ruby>は<ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby>な<ruby>建築家<rt>けんちくか</rt></ruby>**によって**<ruby>設計<rt>せっけい</rt></ruby>された。<br>*This building was designed by a famous architect.*

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · によると vs によって (cont.)

The second sentence is a passive with an agent; swapping in `によると` there would mean "according to the architect", which is not what it says. The other `によって` you will meet is the "it varies" one - <code><ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>によって<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>え<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>う</code> - and again the two are not interchangeable in either direction.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · によると vs ～と言っていた

> **Rule of thumb** - `によると` **frames** the information and lets the ending mark it as hearsay. <code>～と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていた</code> **quotes** the person and is closer to their actual words.

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>**によると**、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は30<ruby>分<rt>ぷん</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>まったとのことです。<br>*According to the manager, the meeting has been moved half an hour earlier.*

<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>が「<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>を30<ruby>分<rt>ぷん</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めよう」**と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていました**。<br>*The manager said, "let's move the meeting half an hour earlier."*

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · によると vs ～と言っていた (cont.)

The quoting version keeps the manager's own wording, including his volitional form, so it reports an utterance. The `によると` version reports a *fact he is the source of*, which is what you want when you pass information on to a third person. In casual speech the quoting version contracts to `って`: <code><ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>めるって<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ってたよ。</code>

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>によると、この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>は<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>いです。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えでは、この<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>は<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>いです。<br>*`によると` points *away* from the speaker. You cannot cite yourself as a source of news you are inventing on the spot.*

- ❌ <ruby>天気<rt>てんき</rt></ruby><ruby>予報<rt>よほう</rt></ruby>によると、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ると<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。 → ✅ …<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るそうです。<br>*The frame says "not my information"; <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code> says "my judgment". Finish with そうです, ということです, とのことです or らしいです.*

- ❌ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんによると<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。 → ✅ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんから<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*`によると` frames a whole reported sentence. It is not a particle for marking who you heard something from - that is `から`.*

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ この<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>によると、<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>のようだ。 → ✅ この<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>からすると、<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>のようだ。<br>*A sound is evidence you are interpreting, not a source that told you something.*

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Practice 1

**1.** ニュース____、<ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>夜中<rt>よなか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>関東<rt>かんとう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>づくそうです。

{reveal}
ニュース**によると**、<ruby>台風<rt>たいふう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>夜中<rt>よなか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>関東<rt>かんとう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>づくそうです。

{reveal}
The news is the source of information, and the sentence already ends in そうです, so the frame that agrees with it is `によると` (or the more formal `によれば`).

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Practice 2

**2.** Choose: この<ruby>論文<rt>ろんぶん</rt></ruby>（によると・によって）、<ruby>睡眠<rt>すいみん</rt></ruby><ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>記憶<rt>きおく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きく<ruby>影響<rt>えいきょう</rt></ruby>するという。

{reveal}
この<ruby>論文<rt>ろんぶん</rt></ruby>**によると**、<ruby>睡眠<rt>すいみん</rt></ruby><ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>記憶<rt>きおく</rt></ruby>に<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きく<ruby>影響<rt>えいきょう</rt></ruby>するという。

{reveal}
The paper is where the claim comes from, so it is a source: `によると`. `によって` would make the paper the *agent* of something, which fits a passive like <code><ruby>論文<rt>ろんぶん</rt></ruby>によって<ruby>明<rt>あき</rt></ruby>らかにされた</code>, not a reported claim.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "According to my grandfather, there used to be a river here."

{reveal}
<ruby>祖父<rt>そふ</rt></ruby>**によると**、<ruby>昔<rt>むかし</rt></ruby>ここに<ruby>川<rt>かわ</rt></ruby>があったそうです。

{reveal}
The person is the source, so `によると` (casually, <code><ruby>祖父<rt>そふ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では</code>), and because you are passing on what he told you, the sentence closes with そうです rather than a bare でした.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんによると、<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>係長<rt>かかりちょう</rt></ruby>はとても<ruby>厳<rt>きび</rt></ruby>しいだと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さん**によると**、<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>係長<rt>かかりちょう</rt></ruby>はとても<ruby>厳<rt>きび</rt></ruby>しいそうです。

{reveal}
Two problems. <code><ruby>厳<rt>きび</rt></ruby>しいだ</code> is not a possible form - い-adjectives never take `だ`. And the ending has to keep the information on Yamada's side of the fence, so そうです replaces <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います</code>.

---

## ～によると / ～によれば · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>中村<rt>なかむら</rt></ruby>さん**によると**、<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わるそうです。 (b) <ruby>中村<rt>なかむら</rt></ruby>さんは「<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わります」**と<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っていました**。

{reveal}
(a) presents Nakamura as the source of a piece of information you are now handing on as fact-you-heard. The listener's attention goes to the content - the work finishes next week - and Nakamura is the guarantee behind it.

{reveal}
(b) reports the act of speaking, close to his actual words. It is what you say when the point is that *he said it* - useful if you want to leave room for "well, that is what he told me" and step back from the claim yourself.

# ～ということだ / ～とのことだ

**Meaning** · I hear that ～; word is that ～ - relays information you received from somewhere else, with the wording kept at arm's length

Both endings hand a piece of information on. `ということだ` is the general relay: whatever you were told, read or heard, repackaged as a report. `とのことだ` is the message-carrier's version - it fits a phone call, an email or a note you are passing to someone else, and it is the standard shape in business Japanese.

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Formation

Both attach to a **plain-form clause**. Nothing changes shape except the noun and な-adjective slots.

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + ということだ | <ruby>戻<rt>もど</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>戻<rt>もど</rt></ruby>るということだ |
| Verb (past) | た-form + ということだ | <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>いた → <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>いたということです |
| い-adjective | plain form + ということだ | <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい → <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しいということだ |
| な-adjective | ～だ + ということだ | <ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>だということだ |
| Noun | Noun + だ + ということだ | <ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby> → <ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>だということです |
| Noun, with とのことだ | Noun + とのことだ | <ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby> → <ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>とのことです |
| Polite past | … + ということでした / とのことでした | <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる → <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れるとのことでした |
| Casual speech | … + ってことだ | <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>だってことだ |

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - before `とのことだ`, the `だ` after a noun is normally left out: <code><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みとのことです</code> rather than <code><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだとのことです</code>. Before `ということだ` the `だ` is usually there. This is a tendency in how the two are written, not a hard rule of grammar.

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Examples

<ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>戻<rt>もど</rt></ruby>る**ということです**。<br>*I'm told the president is back next week.*

<ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>は5<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>まで**とのことです**。<br>*Reception is open until five, apparently.*

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>のお<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>では、<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>範囲<rt>はんい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わらない**ということだ**。<br>*According to the teacher, the scope of the exam isn't changing.*

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんから<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>があって、<ruby>渋滞<rt>じゅうたい</rt></ruby>で<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れる**とのことでした**。<br>*Hayashi got in touch - he says he'll be a little late because of the traffic.*

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>歓迎会<rt>かんげいかい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>延期<rt>えんき</rt></ruby>になった**ということなので**、みんなに<ruby>伝<rt>つた</rt></ruby>えておきます。<br>*The welcome party has been put off until next month, I gather, so I'll let everyone know.*

お<ruby>客様<rt>きゃくさま</rt></ruby>は<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>午前中<rt>ごぜんちゅう</rt></ruby>にもう<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>をくださる**とのことです**。<br>*The customer says she'll ring again tomorrow morning.*

<ruby>担当者<rt>たんとうしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>したところ、<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>金曜日<rt>きんようび</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>せば<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>う**とのことでした**。<br>*I checked with the person in charge, and she said we'll be in time if we submit the papers by Friday.*

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>工事<rt>こうじ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がうるさいという<ruby>苦情<rt>くじょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>何件<rt>なんけん</rt></ruby>か<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ている**ということです**ので、<ruby>作業<rt>さぎょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見直<rt>みなお</rt></ruby>しましょう。<br>*Word is there have been several complaints about the noise from the building work, so let's reconsider the working hours.*

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Nuance & register

Both are more formal than the everyday hearsay `そうだ`. `ということだ` is the ending a news bulletin closes on and a report uses to attribute a fact; in speech it sounds careful and slightly detached, which is exactly why it is useful when you do not want to be held responsible for the content. `とのことだ` is narrower and more human: somebody told you, and you are passing it along. In an office you will hear <code><ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>本日<rt>ほんじつ</rt></ruby><ruby>直帰<rt>ちょっき</rt></ruby>とのことです</code> many times a week.

In plain form, `ということだ` and `とのことだ` both read as written or announcement Japanese. Casual speech contracts the first to `ってことだ` - 「あの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>、 <ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだってことだよ」 - and often drops the frame altogether in favour of a bare `って`: 「<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだって」.

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Nuance & register (cont.)

`ということだ` has a **second job** that shares the same form: drawing a conclusion, "which means", "so what it amounts to is". It usually arrives with つまり or <ruby>要<rt>よう</rt></ruby>するに and restates something already said rather than reporting news.

<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>がないということは、<ruby>興味<rt>きょうみ</rt></ruby>がない**ということだ**。<br>*No reply means they aren't interested.*

Context tells the two apart without much effort: a named source, or a fresh fact, points to hearsay; a premise sitting right in front of it points to the conclusion reading.

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · ということだ vs そうだ

> **Rule of thumb** - same information, different formality - and one grammatical difference: hearsay `そうだ` does not conjugate, while `ということだ` does.

Hearsay `そうだ` has exactly one shape. There is no ❌`そうでした`, no ❌`そうだった` and no ❌`そうではない` in the hearsay sense; those forms all belong to the *looks-like* `そうだ`. So when you need a past or a negative on the reporting frame itself, `ということだ` is the pattern that has one.

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>問<rt>と</rt></ruby>い<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わせたときは、<ruby>在庫<rt>ざいこ</rt></ruby>がまだある**ということでした**。<br>*When I enquired yesterday, they said there was still stock.*

<ruby>在庫<rt>ざいこ</rt></ruby>がまだある**そうです**。<br>*Apparently there's still stock.*

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · ということだ vs そうだ (cont.)

The second is what you say to a colleague in passing. The first pins the report to a moment in time, and only `ということだ` can do it.

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · とのことだ vs ということだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `とのことだ` relays a **message from an identifiable person**, usually one you received directly. `ということだ` covers that too, but also general report, rumour and conclusion.

<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>の<ruby>上<rt>うえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>旅館<rt>りょかん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby><ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めるらしい、**ということだ**。<br>*Word going round is that the inn up the mountain is closing next year.*

<ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>変更<rt>へんこう</rt></ruby>はメールでお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いしたい**とのことです**。<br>*They'd like changes to bookings to be made by email, they said.*

Swapping them is rarely *ungrammatical*, but `とのことだ` on an anonymous rumour sounds oddly official, and `ということだ` on a message you took two minutes ago sounds needlessly distant.

---

## ということだ ("I hear") vs ということだ ("which means")

> **Rule of thumb** - if it introduces **new** information from a source, it is hearsay. If it restates something **already** on the table, it is a conclusion.

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>支店<rt>してん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>駅前<rt>えきまえ</rt></ruby>にできる**ということです**。<br>*I hear the new branch is going up in front of the station.*

<ruby>駅前<rt>えきまえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>土地<rt>とち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったのなら、そこに<ruby>支店<rt>してん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>る**ということですね**。<br>*If they've bought the land by the station, that means they're going to build a branch there.*

The second one has its premise attached with `なら`, and it adds no facts - it just spells out what follows. That is the conclusion use, and it is the one that turns up in `ということは` at the head of a sentence.

---

## ～ということだ / ～とのことだ · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>だそうでした。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>だということでした。<br>*Hearsay `そうだ` has no past form. When the reporting itself happened in the past, use `ということでした` or `とのことでした`.*

- ❌ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>いだということです。 → ✅ <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が<ruby>狭<rt>せま</rt></ruby>いということです。<br>*い-adjectives never take `だ`. Only nouns and な-adjectives do, and only before `ということだ`.*

- ❌ <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むそうということです。 → ✅ <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むということです。<br>*One hearsay marker per sentence. Stacking `そうだ` and `ということだ` reports the same second-hand status twice.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はこの<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>賛成<rt>さんせい</rt></ruby>だということです。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はこの<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>賛成<rt>さんせい</rt></ruby>です。<br>*The frame exists to put distance between you and the content. Your own position needs no distance.*

# ～に違いない

**Meaning** · it must be ～; I'm sure ～ - read as *ちがいない*, the speaker's near-certainty about something they have worked out rather than been told

<code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> is the top of the guessing range. You have not been told, and you cannot check; but the evidence in front of you leaves you in no real doubt. The conviction is the speaker's own, which is why the pattern belongs to inference and not to fact - you never use it about something you simply know.

---

## ～に違いない · Formation

Attaches to a **plain form**, with the usual noun and な-adjective trimming: the `だ` disappears.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない | <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>とした → <ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としたに<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない | <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない → <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らないに<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない |
| い-adjective | plain form + に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない | <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いに<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない |
| な-adjective | drop だ + に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない | <ruby>不安<rt>ふあん</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>不安<rt>ふあん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない |
| Noun | Noun + に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない | <ruby>本物<rt>ほんもの</rt></ruby> → <ruby>本物<rt>ほんもの</rt></ruby>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない |
| Polite | … + に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いありません | <ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れている → <ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れているに<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いありません |

## ～に違いない · Examples

この<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたのは<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*My sister must be the one who wrote this.*

<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>消<rt>き</rt></ruby>えているから、もう<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>た**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*The lights are off, so they must have gone to bed.*

こんなに<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>並<rt>なら</rt></ruby>んでいるのだから、この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>はおいしい**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*With a queue like that, the food here must be good.*

<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>がまだ<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない。きっと<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いありません**。<br>*Still no reply. They must be snowed under.*

---

## ～に違いない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>が<ruby>割<rt>わ</rt></ruby>れているところを<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ると、<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かが<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>った**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*Judging by the broken window, someone must have got in.*

<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>いたはずの<ruby>封筒<rt>ふうとう</rt></ruby>がない。<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かが<ruby>片付<rt>かたづ</rt></ruby>けてしまった**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*The envelope I'm sure I left on the desk is gone. Somebody must have tidied it away.*

あれだけ<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>重<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>ねてきたのだから、<ruby>本人<rt>ほんにん</rt></ruby>もこの<ruby>結果<rt>けっか</rt></ruby>には<ruby>納得<rt>なっとく</rt></ruby>していない**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*After all the practice he put in, he can't possibly be satisfied with this result himself.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>も<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わずに<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ったのは、よほど<ruby>嫌<rt>いや</rt></ruby>なことがあった**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いありません**。<br>*For her to leave without a word, something must have upset her badly.*

---

## ～に違いない · Nuance & register

Everything from `かもしれない` up to a flat assertion is a ladder, and it pays to have the whole thing in view rather than learning each rung separately.

| Pattern | English | What it rests on |
|---|---|---|
| かもしれない | might, could | one possibility among several |
| だろう / でしょう | probably | a general guess, no working shown |
| はずだ | should be, is supposed to be | a fact or rule you can point to |
| に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない | must be, I'm certain | evidence plus the speaker's conviction |
| に<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まっている | obviously, of course | conviction alone, stated bluntly |

---

## ～に違いない · Nuance & register (cont.)

<code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> is a strong, slightly dramatic ending. It is completely at home in writing - novels lean on it for a character's inner deduction, and essays use it to press a point - and in speech it sounds emphatic and a little declamatory. It is not rude; it just takes up space. In relaxed conversation people more often reach for きっと with a plain ending, or for <code>に<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まってる</code>, or simply `はずだ`.

<code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いありません</code> is the polite form and is unremarkable in a careful spoken report. <code>に<ruby>相違<rt>そうい</rt></ruby>ない</code> is the same pattern in legal and formal-document register and is worth recognising rather than using.

---

## ～に違いない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two things it will not do. It does not stack with other hedges - ❌<code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いないでしょう</code> and ❌<code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いないかもしれない</code> cancel the certainty they just claimed - and it does not apply to your own plans and feelings, which are not things you deduce. Say <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます, not ❌<code><ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くに<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code>.

---

## ～に違いない · に違いない vs はずだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `はずだ` reasons from something you can point to and survives being wrong; <code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> states your conviction and does not.

`はずだ` has an escape hatch built in. It says "given what I know, this follows", which is why `はずだったのに` - "it was supposed to be, and yet" - is such a common sentence. <code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> has no such form; you cannot walk back a certainty you just declared.

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は3<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まる**はず**です。<br>*The meeting should start at three.*

---

## ～に違いない · に違いない vs はずだ (cont.)

あの<ruby>様子<rt>ようす</rt></ruby>では、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>はまだ<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わっていない**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*Going by the look of things, the meeting can't have finished yet.*

The first reads off a schedule; you did not deduce anything, so <code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> there would sound oddly overwrought. The second has no schedule to read - only evidence and the speaker's reading of it, which is exactly <code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> territory.

---

## ～に違いない · に違いない vs かもしれない・だろう

> **Rule of thumb** - same guess, three strengths: `かもしれない` leaves the other possibilities open, `だろう` picks one without much working, <code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> closes the question.

あの<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雷<rt>かみなり</rt></ruby>**かもしれない**。<br>*That noise might be thunder.*

あの<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雷<rt>かみなり</rt></ruby>**だろう**。<br>*That noise is probably thunder.*

あの<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雷<rt>かみなり</rt></ruby>**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*That noise must be thunder.*

---

## ～に違いない · に違いない vs かもしれない・だろう (cont.)

Notice what changes with the evidence. The first is fine when you barely heard it; the third needs the sky to have flashed a second earlier. Using the top rung on thin evidence is the commonest way learners sound strange with this pattern - it is not wrong, it is simply overclaiming.

---

## ～に違いない · に違いない vs に決まっている

> **Rule of thumb** - <code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> is a considered conclusion; <code>に<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まっている</code> is a blunt "obviously", often impatient, and much more casual.

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>から<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っても<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない**に<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まっている**よ。<br>*Of course we won't make it, even if we run.*

この<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たのだから、<ruby>渋滞<rt>じゅうたい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>巻<rt>ま</rt></ruby>き<ruby>込<rt>こ</rt></ruby>まれた**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*Leaving at that hour, they must have got caught in traffic.*

<code>に<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>まっている</code> treats the conclusion as too obvious to argue with, which can come across as dismissive of the person you are talking to. <code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code> shows its working and stays civil.

---

## ～に違いない · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**だに<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。 → ✅ この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。<br>*The `だ` of a な-adjective or a noun drops before <code>に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない</code>, exactly as it does before `らしい`.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*Your own plans are not something you deduce. The pattern is for conclusions about the world outside your head.*

- ❌ <ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>は9<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>からだ**に<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>いない**ので、<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちましょう。 → ✅ <ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>は9<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>から**のはず**なので、<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちましょう。<br>*Opening times come from a notice, not from deduction. Facts you can look up take `はずだ`.*

## ～に違いない · Common mistakes (cont.)

# ～そうもない

**Meaning** · there's no sign of it happening; very unlikely to ～ - the negative of the "looks like it will" そうだ, and considerably stronger than a plain "probably not"

`～そうもない` says that you have looked at the situation and can see **no prospect at all** of the thing happening. It is not a calculation and not a rumour: it is what the evidence in front of you looks like. Because the `も` is a "not even" particle, the pattern lands harder than `～ないだろう` - the rain is not merely unlikely to stop, it shows no sign whatsoever of stopping.

---

## ～そうもない · Formation

This is a **verb** pattern. It attaches to the ます-stem, exactly like the "looks like" そうだ it negates.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ます-stem + そうもない | <ruby>止<rt>や</rt></ruby>みます → <ruby>止<rt>や</rt></ruby>みそうもない |
| Verb, common variant | ます-stem + そうにない | <ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わります → <ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わりそうにない |
| Verb, emphatic variant | ます-stem + そうにもない | <ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>てます → <ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>てそうにもない |
| Potential verb (very common) | potential ます-stem + そうもない | <ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>けます → <ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>けそうもない |
| Polite | … + そうもありません / そうにありません | <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>います → <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いそうもありません |
| い-adjective (use なさそう instead) | ～く + なさそうだ | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くなさそうだ |
| な-adjective / noun (use なさそう instead) | ～では + なさそうだ | <ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby> → <ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>ではなさそうだ |

---

## ～そうもない · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the plain "looks like it will" そうだ has an irregular pair worth keeping straight: いい → よさそう, ない → なさそう. Those `さ` insertions are what produce the adjective negatives in the last two rows.

---

## ～そうもない · Examples

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>当分<rt>とうぶん</rt></ruby><ruby>止<rt>や</rt></ruby>み**そうもない**。<br>*There's no sign of the rain stopping any time soon.*

この<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今日中<rt>きょうじゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わり**そうにない**。<br>*This job isn't going to get finished today by the look of it.*

<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>空<rt>あ</rt></ruby>き**そうもない**から、<ruby>別<rt>べつ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>にしよう。<br>*No sign of a table coming free - let's try somewhere else.*

どんなに<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えても、いい<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>てき**そうにもない**。<br>*However hard I think about it, no good idea looks likely to come out.*

---

## ～そうもない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>渋滞<rt>じゅうたい</rt></ruby>がひどくて、<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>には<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>け**そうもありません**。<br>*The traffic is dreadful - I don't think I can possibly get there for the time we agreed.*

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>下<rt>さ</rt></ruby>がりましたが、<ruby>咳<rt>せき</rt></ruby>のほうはしばらく<ruby>治<rt>なお</rt></ruby>り**そうにありません**。<br>*The fever has come down, but the cough shows no sign of clearing for a while.*

<ruby>隣<rt>となり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>の<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>し<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いは、まだ<ruby>当分<rt>とうぶん</rt></ruby><ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わり**そうにない**ですね。<br>*The discussion in the next room doesn't look like ending for some time yet, does it.*

<ruby>部品<rt>ぶひん</rt></ruby>がまだ<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>いていないので、<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>発売<rt>はつばい</rt></ruby>には<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>い**そうもありません**。<br>*The parts still haven't arrived, so there's no realistic chance of making next week's launch.*

---

## ～そうもない · Nuance & register

Register-neutral. You will hear it from a site foreman, a schoolchild and a news reporter alike, and the polite forms `そうもありません` / `そうにありません` are ordinary business Japanese for delivering bad news gently - the fault lies with the situation, not with you.

The three shapes are near-identical in meaning. `そうにない` is the plainest, `そうもない` adds the "not even" flavour of `も`, and `そうにもない` piles both on and is the most emphatic. Treat this as a tendency rather than a rule: in most sentences the three are freely interchangeable, and speakers vary.

---

## ～そうもない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two things follow from `そうもない` being the negative of *looks-like* そうだ. First, it is about **observable prospects**, so it wants a subject whose behaviour you can watch: rain, a queue, a deadline, a person's progress. It sits badly on abstract impossibility, which belongs to `わけがない`. Second, it does not mean "I hear that … not". The hearsay そうだ negates inside the clause instead: <code><ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないそうだ</code> - "I hear they aren't coming."

The pattern loves potential verbs, and that combination is the everyday way to decline gracefully: <code><ruby>今週<rt>こんしゅう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>けそうもありません</code> softens "I can't come" into "it doesn't look as though I'll manage it."

---

## ～そうもない · ～そうもない vs ～なさそうだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` negates the *appearance of it happening*; `なさそうだ` negates the *verb* and then reports how that looks. The first says "no sign of it"; the second says "looks like not".

バスは<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>**そうもない**。<br>*There's no sign of a bus coming at all.*

この<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>だと、バスは<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>**なさそうだ**ね。<br>*At this hour it looks like no bus is coming.*

---

## ～そうもない · ～そうもない vs ～なさそうだ (cont.)

Both are natural, and the difference is one of weight. The first is what you say after twenty minutes at the stop, with impatience; the second is a calmer reading of the timetable and the empty road. With **adjectives** you have no choice - only the `なさそう` route exists, which is why ❌<code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>そうもない</code> should be <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くなさそうだ</code>.

---

## ～そうもない · ～そうもない vs ～ないだろう

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` is grounded in what you can see right now. `ないだろう` is a guess that needs no evidence at all.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>は<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ら**ないだろう**。<br>*It probably won't snow tomorrow.*

<ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>が<ruby>明<rt>あか</rt></ruby>るくなってきた。もう<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り**そうもない**。<br>*The sky's brightening. It doesn't look like it's going to come down any more.*

Because `そうもない` reports signs, it usually talks about the near future or a situation already under way. A guess about next year takes `ないだろう`; a guess about the next twenty minutes, with the sky in front of you, takes `そうもない`.

---

## ～そうもない · ～そうもない vs わけがない・はずがない

> **Rule of thumb** - `そうもない` says there is **no sign** of it. `わけがない` and `はずがない` say it is **impossible**. Unlikely is not the same as ruled out.

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>から<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>めても、<ruby>今日中<rt>きょうじゅう</rt></ruby>には<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わり**そうもない**。<br>*Even if we start now, it doesn't look like finishing today.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>でこの<ruby>量<rt>りょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>べる**わけがない**。<br>*There's no way one person can shift this much.*

The first leaves the door open - a miracle, extra hands, a shorter route. The second slams it. Learners reach for `わけがない` when they only mean the first, and it comes out sounding much more forceful than they intended.

---

## ～そうもない · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る**そうもない**。 → ✅ <ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り**そうもない**。<br>*It attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form plus そうだ is the *hearsay* pattern, which is a different thing entirely.*

- ❌ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>**そうもない**。 → ✅ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>**ではなさそうだ**。<br>*Adjectives do not take `そうもない`. Negate the adjective first, then add そう.*

- ❌ <ruby>今日中<rt>きょうじゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わら**なそうもない**。 → ✅ <ruby>今日中<rt>きょうじゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わり**そうもない**。<br>*The negative is already inside `そうもない`. Negating the verb as well leaves you saying it looks like it will finish.*

---

## ～そうもない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る**そうもない**（meaning "I hear he isn't coming"）。 → ✅ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**そうだ**。<br>*For reported information, put the negative in the clause and keep plain `そうだ`.*

# ～ような気がする

**Meaning** · I have a feeling that ～ - a hunch, offered without the evidence that would make it a claim

Read as *ような き がする*. The pattern reports an impression that has formed in you without your being able to show your working. It is the softest thing in the whole certainty range: weaker than <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う</code>, weaker than `ようだ`, and much weaker than `はずだ`. Japanese uses it constantly, both to hedge a real opinion and to describe a genuine sensation you cannot account for.

---

## ～ような気がする · Formation

`ような` is the noun-modifying form of `ようだ`, and <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby></code> is the noun it modifies. So the clause attaches exactly the way it does before any noun.

---

## ～ような気がする · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and one of the highest-frequency hedges in ordinary conversation. <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする</code> in plain form is casual, <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします</code> fits any polite situation, and nothing about it is stiff or literary. In writing, the close relative <code>～ように<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う</code> does the same job with a more composed tone.

Two flavours share the one form, and context separates them. The first is a real **sensation**: something smells faintly of smoke, a room feels colder, you half heard your name. The second is **politeness** - you have an opinion, and you present it as a mere feeling so that disagreeing with you costs nothing. In a meeting, <code>この<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぎすぎているような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします</code> is a carefully wrapped objection, and everyone hears it as one.

---

## ～ような気がする · Nuance & register (cont.)

The variant <code>ような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>もする</code> is worth having. Replacing `が` with `も` turns it into "part of me feels", and it is how you sit on the fence out loud: <code><ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>もするし、<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>もする</code>.

Do not confuse the phrase with its neighbours. <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>になる</code> means something is on your mind or bothering you; <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>に<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>る</code> means you like it. Only <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする</code> means "I have the impression", and the particle is `が`, never `を`.

---

## ～ような気がする · ような気がする vs と思う

> **Rule of thumb** - <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う</code> is an opinion you could defend if pressed. <code>ような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする</code> is an impression you could not.

この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>には<ruby>塩<rt>しお</rt></ruby>が<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りない**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います**。<br>*I think this dish needs more salt.*

この<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>には<ruby>塩<rt>しお</rt></ruby>が<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りない**ような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします**。<br>*This dish feels like it could do with more salt.*

---

## ～ような気がする · ような気がする vs と思う (cont.)

The first invites a reply - agreement, or "no, it's fine as it is". The second barely puts the claim on the table, and that is often the point. Note the practical consequence: if a superior asks for your judgment, answering only with <code>ような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします</code> can read as evasive rather than modest.

---

## ～ような気がする · ような気がする vs ようだ・みたいだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `ようだ` is a conclusion **drawn from evidence**; <code>ような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする</code> is a feeling that arrives **without** it.

<ruby>玄関<rt>げんかん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>がある。<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>か<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ている**ようだ**。<br>*There are shoes in the entrance. It seems someone's here.*

<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かいる**ような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする**。<br>*I have a feeling there's someone in the house.*

---

## ～ような気がする · ような気がする vs ようだ・みたいだ (cont.)

The first points at the shoes; anybody could check the reasoning. The second points at nothing at all, which is exactly why it is the sentence that belongs in a ghost story. Because they sit next to each other, the two combine happily: <code>いるような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がしただけかもしれない</code>.

---

## ～ような気がする · ような気がする vs 気がする

> **Rule of thumb** - the bare <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする</code> is a shade more direct; adding `よう` puts one more layer of "sort of" between you and the claim.

<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする**。<br>*I think I've heard this story before.*

<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>にも<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた**ような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする**。<br>*I have some vague feeling I've heard this story before.*

Both are natural, and in casual speech the short form is if anything the commoner of the two. Choose the long one when you want the extra padding - an awkward opinion, a memory you distrust, a criticism you would rather not sharpen.

---

For "I have the impression", the verb is <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れ<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>をしたような**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>をします**。 → ✅ <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れ<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>をしたような**<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします**。<br>*The particle is `が`. Nothing is being done to the feeling; the feeling simply occurs.*

- ❌ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**だような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする**。 → ✅ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**な<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする**。<br>*Before a noun, な-adjectives take `な`, never `だ` - and with this pattern the short form is the one people actually say.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>30<ruby>歳<rt>さい</rt></ruby>になる**ような<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がします**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>30<ruby>歳<rt>さい</rt></ruby>になります。<br>*A fact you know about yourself is not a hunch. Hedging it sounds as though you are unsure how old you are.*

# ～ふりをする

**Meaning** · to pretend to ～; to act as if - deliberately putting on an appearance you know to be false

The noun `ふり` (sometimes written <ruby>振<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り) is the *show* you put on, and する is doing it. Two things are built into the pattern: the appearance is produced on purpose, and the person producing it knows it is not true. That is what separates it from every "it seems" pattern in the book - those report how things look, while this one reports somebody staging how things look.

---

## ～ふりをする · Formation

`ふり` is a noun, so whatever comes in front of it takes the shape a noun modifier takes.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (present) | plain form + ふりをする | <ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>泣<rt>な</rt></ruby>くふりをする |
| Verb (past state) | た-form + ふりをする | <ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>た → <ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たふりをする |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + ふりをする | <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない → <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らないふりをする |
| Verb (progressive) | ている + ふりをする | <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いている → <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いているふりをする |
| い-adjective | plain form + ふりをする | <ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>いふりをする |
| な-adjective | ～な + ふりをする | <ruby>平気<rt>へいき</rt></ruby>な → <ruby>平気<rt>へいき</rt></ruby>なふりをする |
| Noun | ～の + ふりをする | <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby> → <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>のふりをする |
| Continuing the sentence | ふりをして + verb | <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づかない → <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づかないふりをして<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>る |

---

## ～ふりをする · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the tense inside matters. <code><ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たふり</code> is the normal way to say "pretending to be asleep", because what you are faking is the *state* of having fallen asleep. <code><ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>るふり</code> would be miming the act of dropping off - possible, but rarely what people mean.

---

## ～ふりをする · Examples

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>た**ふりをして**いる。<br>*My little brother is pretending to be asleep.*

<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない**ふりをしないで**ください。<br>*Please don't pretend you don't know.*

<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>で<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ったが、<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づかない**ふりをして**<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>り<ruby>過<rt>す</rt></ruby>ぎた。<br>*I ran into my teacher in the street, but I walked past pretending not to notice.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>平気<rt>へいき</rt></ruby>な**ふりをして**いたが、<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>が<ruby>震<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>えていた。<br>*She was acting as though nothing was wrong, but her hands were shaking.*

---

## ～ふりをする · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもたちは<ruby>警察官<rt>けいさつかん</rt></ruby>の**ふりをして**<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでいる。<br>*The children are playing at being police officers.*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby><ruby>中<rt>ちゅう</rt></ruby>はどうしても<ruby>眠<rt>ねむ</rt></ruby>くて、<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いている**ふりをする**だけでやっとだった。<br>*I was so sleepy in the meeting that it was all I could do to look as if I was listening.*

<ruby>上司<rt>じょうし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>冗談<rt>じょうだん</rt></ruby>に、みんな<ruby>面白<rt>おもしろ</rt></ruby>い**ふりをして**<ruby>笑<rt>わら</rt></ruby>っていた。<br>*Everyone laughed at the boss's joke, pretending to find it funny.*

<ruby>困<rt>こま</rt></ruby>っている<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>かけても<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>て<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ぬ**ふりをする**ような<ruby>大人<rt>おとな</rt></ruby>にはなりたくない。<br>*I don't want to grow into the sort of adult who looks the other way when someone is in trouble.*

---

## ～ふりをする · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and very common in ordinary speech. In writing you will meet both `ふり` and <code><ruby>振<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り</code>; kana is the safer default in a sentence, since the kanji is also used for unrelated words.

The pattern carries a faint moral charge, because pretending is a small deception. How heavy that charge is depends entirely on the content: <code><ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たふり</code> to avoid a chore is comic, while <code><ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らないふり</code> about something you were told is an accusation. Said about yourself it is often self-deprecating; said about someone else it is rarely a compliment.

---

## ～ふりをする · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two shortenings are worth recognising. In casual speech `を` drops - <code><ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえないふりした</code> - and the fixed expression <code><ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らんぷり</code> ("playing dumb", "blanking someone") turns up constantly in conversation with no `する` in sight: 「あいつ、<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らんぷりしてたよ」.

The idiom <code><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>て<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ぬふりをする</code> - literally "see and do the not- seeing show" - means turning a blind eye, and it is worth learning whole; the `ぬ` is an old negative you will otherwise not meet at this level.

One limitation. Because the pattern needs an actor, an inanimate subject cannot use it. Clouds do not pretend to be a face; for that you want `ようだ` or `みたいだ`.

---

## ～ふりをする · ふりをする vs まねをする

> **Rule of thumb** - `ふりをする` is pretending to **be** something you are not. `まねをする` is **copying** somebody's behaviour, with no claim about who you are.

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>父親<rt>ちちおや</rt></ruby>の**まねをして**、<ruby>新聞<rt>しんぶん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>げて<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいる。<br>*My son copies his father, spreading out the newspaper and reading it.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>大人<rt>おとな</rt></ruby>の**ふりをして**、<ruby>映画館<rt>えいがかん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>ろうとした。<br>*My son tried to get into the cinema pretending to be an adult.*

Copying can be admiring, playful or educational - `まねをする` is how you learn a craft. Pretending is aimed at somebody's belief: the point of the second sentence is that the ticket seller was meant to be fooled.

---

## ～ふりをする · ふりをする vs ように見える

> **Rule of thumb** - `ふりをする` describes what the **actor is doing**; <code>ように<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える</code> describes what the **observer sees**. Only the first claims intent.

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>な**ふりをしている**。<br>*He's putting on a brave face.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>そうに**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>える**。<br>*He looks well.*

The first says you have seen through him; the second says nothing about whether the appearance is genuine. Getting these backwards is how learners accidentally accuse people of lying, so keep the intent inside `ふり` firmly in mind.

---

## ～ふりをする · ふりをする vs ～つもりだ

> **Rule of thumb** - with `ふり`, **you know it is false and want others to believe it**. With the "acting as if" `つもり`, **you have convinced yourself**.

<ruby>祖父<rt>そふ</rt></ruby>はまだ<ruby>若<rt>わか</rt></ruby>い**つもり**で、<ruby>重<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>ぼうとする。<br>*My grandfather still thinks of himself as young and tries to carry heavy things.*

<ruby>祖父<rt>そふ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>若<rt>わか</rt></ruby>い**ふりをして**、<ruby>階段<rt>かいだん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>駆<rt>か</rt></ruby>け<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がってみせた。<br>*My grandfather put on a show of youth and ran up the stairs.*

The `つもり` version is about his self-image; nobody is being deceived except possibly himself. The `ふり` version has an audience. Note that `つもり` here is not the "intend to" `つもり` you learned at N5 - same word, and context does the sorting.

---

## ～ふりをする · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**ふりをする**。 → ✅ <ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**のふりをする**。<br>*`ふり` is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs `の`, exactly as in <code><ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby></code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**だふりをする**。 → ✅ <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>**なふりをする**。<br>*な-adjectives take `な` before a noun. The `だ` form never modifies anything.*

- ❌ <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい**ふりがする**。 → ✅ <ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい**ふりをする**。<br>*The particle is `を` - you *do* a pretence. <code><ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>がする</code> has `が` because a feeling happens to you; a performance does not.*

- ❌ <ruby>授業<rt>じゅぎょう</rt></ruby><ruby>中<rt>ちゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>る**ふりをした**。（meaning "I pretended to be asleep"） → ✅ <ruby>授業<rt>じゅぎょう</rt></ruby><ruby>中<rt>ちゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>た**ふりをした**。<br>*What you fake is the state of being asleep, and that state is the た-form.*

# ～わけだ

**Meaning** · so that's why; it follows that; that means - presents the sentence as the conclusion that falls out of something already known

`わけ` is the noun <ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>, "reason, logic, the way a thing works out". `わけだ` therefore never stands alone: it always looks back at a premise - something just said, just discovered, or plainly visible - and says "given that, this is how it adds up." Three flavours share the one form: *no wonder* (a realisation), *it follows that* (a calculation), and *in other words* (a restatement).

---

## ～わけだ · Formation

`わけ` is a noun, so the clause in front of it is in noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + わけだ | <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っている → <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っているわけだ |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけだ | <ruby>通<rt>つう</rt></ruby>じない → <ruby>通<rt>つう</rt></ruby>じないわけだ |
| い-adjective | plain form + わけだ | <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いわけだ |
| な-adjective | ～な + わけだ | <ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>な → <ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>なわけだ |
| Noun | ～な / ～という + わけだ | <ruby>留学生<rt>りゅうがくせい</rt></ruby> → <ruby>留学生<rt>りゅうがくせい</rt></ruby>なわけだ |
| Polite | … + わけです | <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いわけです |
| Restating what was just said | ～という + わけだ | <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られない → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>られないというわけだ |
| Connecting two sentences | というわけで | - → というわけで、<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>します |

---

## ～わけだ · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the realisation use is almost always announced in advance by どうりで ("no wonder") or だから. Hearing どうりで, a Japanese listener is already expecting `わけだ` at the end of the sentence.

---

## ～わけだ · Examples

<ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>が<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いている。どうりで<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>い**わけだ**。<br>*The window's open. No wonder it's cold.*

<ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>のご<ruby>出身<rt>しゅっしん</rt></ruby>ですか。<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>に<ruby>慣<rt>な</rt></ruby>れている**わけですね**。<br>*You're from Hokkaido? That explains why snow doesn't bother you.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は10<ruby>年<rt>ねん</rt></ruby><ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>に<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>んでいたのだから、<ruby>関西弁<rt>かんさいべん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せる**わけだ**。<br>*He lived in Osaka for ten years, so of course he can speak Kansai dialect.*

バスが<ruby>一時間<rt>いちじかん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>一本<rt>いっぽん</rt></ruby>しかないのだから、<ruby>不便<rt>ふべん</rt></ruby>な**わけだ**。<br>*There's only one bus an hour - no wonder the place is inconvenient.*

---

## ～わけだ · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>3<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby><ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>すれば、<ruby>一週間<rt>いっしゅうかん</rt></ruby>で21<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>になる**わけです**。<br>*If you practise three hours a day, that works out at twenty-one hours a week.*

つまり、<ruby>今回<rt>こんかい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>には<ruby>参加<rt>さんか</rt></ruby>できない**というわけですね**。<br>*So what you're saying is that you can't join this trip.*

<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夏<rt>なつ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>工場<rt>こうじょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>まったのだから、この<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人口<rt>じんこう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>減<rt>へ</rt></ruby>っている**わけだ**。<br>*The factory shut last summer, so it stands to reason the town's population is falling.*

<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby>5<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きて<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っているそうです。あんなに<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>れる**わけですね**。<br>*Apparently he's up at five every morning to run. No wonder he's that fast.*

---

## ～わけだ · Nuance & register

`わけだ` is register-neutral in itself; `わけです` fits any polite setting and turns up constantly in teaching, briefings and explanations, because its whole job is showing how a conclusion follows. In casual speech it contracts to `わけ` with the `だ` dropped - 「あ、それで<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かなわけか」 - and `わけか` is the shape a realisation takes when you are talking to yourself.

The realisation use is warm and companionable: you have just understood something, and saying so out loud gives the other person credit for explaining it. The restating use - `つまり…というわけですね` - is the standard way to confirm you have followed a complicated explanation, and in business it is a genuinely useful move. Handle it with a little care, though. Restating something the listener already knows perfectly well can sound as if you are explaining them to themselves, and delivered flatly it comes across as condescending. That is a tendency, not a rule; tone and context decide.

---

## ～わけだ · Nuance & register (cont.)

`というわけで` deserves separate mention. As a sentence opener it means "and so", wrapping up a line of reasoning and moving to what follows from it: <code>というわけで、<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>はここまでにします</code>. It is extremely common in spoken explanation and in blog-style writing.

One thing `わけだ` cannot do: announce your own decision. A decision is not something that follows from evidence, so <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くことにしました</code> or <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くつもりです</code> is what you want there.

---

## ～わけだ · わけだ vs のだ・んです

> **Rule of thumb** - `んです` supplies the **background** to a situation. `わけだ` draws the **conclusion** from a background already established.

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みました。<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があった**んです**。<br>*I was off yesterday. I had a temperature.*

<ruby>熱<rt>ねつ</rt></ruby>があったのなら、<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んだ**わけだ**。<br>*If you had a temperature, then naturally you took the day off.*

The two point in opposite directions. `んです` runs from the situation back to its explanation; `わけだ` runs from the explanation forward to the situation. That is why `わけだ` is so often preceded by だから, どうりで or a `のだから` clause - the premise has to already be on the table.

---

## ～わけだ · わけだ vs はずだ

> **Rule of thumb** - `はずだ` is an expectation about something you have **not confirmed**. `わけだ` accounts for something you **already know to be so**.

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>中国語<rt>ちゅうごくご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せる**はずです**。<br>*Tanaka should be able to speak Chinese.*

<ruby>上海<rt>シャンハイ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>育<rt>そだ</rt></ruby>ったのだから、<ruby>中国語<rt>ちゅうごくご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せる**わけです**。<br>*He grew up in Shanghai, so of course he speaks Chinese.*

In the first, you have not heard him speak it; you are predicting. In the second you have just heard him, and the sentence explains why. This is why `はずだったのに` exists and there is no natural equivalent for `わけだ` - you cannot be disappointed by a conclusion about a fact you have already observed.

---

## ～わけだ · わけだ vs はずだ (cont.)

The overlap that confuses people is that both can translate as "so he must speak Chinese". Ask which way the sentence is facing: forward to an unverified prediction (`はずだ`), or backward to explain a known fact (`わけだ`).

---

## ～わけだ · わけだ vs ということだ

> **Rule of thumb** - in the "that means" sense the two overlap; `わけだ` stresses that the conclusion **follows logically**, `ということだ` simply **restates** the content.

<ruby>全員<rt>ぜんいん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>反対<rt>はんたい</rt></ruby>したのなら、この<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>らない**わけだ**。<br>*If everyone opposed it, then the proposal isn't going through.*

<ruby>全員<rt>ぜんいん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>反対<rt>はんたい</rt></ruby>した。この<ruby>案<rt>あん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>らない**ということだ**。<br>*Everyone opposed it. That means the proposal isn't going through.*

The difference is thin here, and in many sentences either will pass. Where they part company is that `ということだ` also carries the hearsay meaning - "I hear that" - and `わけだ` never does. If a source is named, only `ということだ` is possible.

---

## ～わけだ · Common mistakes

- ❌ あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**だわけだ**。 → ✅ あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**なわけだ**。（or <ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>というわけだ）<br>*A noun links to `わけ` with `な` or `という`, never with `だ`.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る**わけだ**。（said out of nowhere） → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>るでしょう。<br>*`わけだ` needs a premise behind it. With nothing to conclude *from*, the sentence leaves the listener waiting for the missing first half.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby><ruby>留学<rt>りゅうがく</rt></ruby>する**わけです**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby><ruby>留学<rt>りゅうがく</rt></ruby>する**つもりです**。<br>*Your own plans are decisions, not conclusions. `わけだ` would suggest your studying abroad was somehow deduced from circumstances.*

---

## ～わけだ · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ どうりで<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>込<rt>こ</rt></ruby>んでいる**はずだ**。 → ✅ どうりで<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>込<rt>こ</rt></ruby>んでいる**わけだ**。<br>*どうりで introduces an explanation for something you are looking at right now, and that pairs with `わけだ`. `はずだ` would turn a fact into a prediction.*

# ～わけがない

**Meaning** · there's no way ～; it's impossible that ～ - denies that any path of reasoning could lead to the thing being true

Keep the literal sense in view: `わけ` is the <ruby>訳<rt>わけ</rt></ruby>, the reason or logic by which something could come about, and `がない` says there is none. So `わけがない` does not say "unlikely" or "I doubt it". It says the door is shut. This is the strongest denial in the わけ family, and it is the one learners over-use.

**What is negated: the possibility itself.** That single fact separates it from `わけではない`, which leaves the possibility standing and denies only an inference.

---

## ～わけがない · Formation

`わけ` is a noun, so the clause in front takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + わけがない | <ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>てる → <ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>てるわけがない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけがない | <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づかない → <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>づかないわけがない |
| い-adjective | plain form + わけがない | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いわけがない |
| な-adjective | ～な + わけがない | <ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>な → <ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>なわけがない |
| Noun | ～な / ～という + わけがない | <ruby>本物<rt>ほんもの</rt></ruby> → <ruby>本物<rt>ほんもの</rt></ruby>なわけがない |
| Slightly softer / more written | … + わけはない | <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れる → <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れるわけはない |
| Polite | … + わけがありません | <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>くわけがありません |
| Casual speech | … + わけない | できる → できるわけないよ |

---

## ～わけがない · Examples

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもにこの<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>める**わけがない**。<br>*There's no way a child can read this kanji.*

あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>がうそをつく**わけがありません**。<br>*He would never lie - it's simply not possible.*

<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>もしないで、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>勝<rt>か</rt></ruby>てる**わけがない**よ。<br>*You don't practise, so of course you can't win the match.*

こんなに<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いのに、カロリーが<ruby>低<rt>ひく</rt></ruby>い**わけがない**。<br>*As sweet as this is, there is no chance it's low in calories.*

---

## ～わけがない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby><ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>っただけの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えている**わけがない**じゃないか。<br>*Come on, nobody remembers the name of someone they met once.*

<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>も<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まずに<ruby>組<rt>く</rt></ruby>み<ruby>立<rt>た</rt></ruby>てて、うまくいく**わけがない**でしょう。<br>*Putting it together without even reading the instructions - that was never going to work.*

<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>夜中<rt>よなか</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>残業<rt>ざんぎょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>いているのだから、<ruby>体<rt>からだ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>影響<rt>えいきょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ない**わけがない**。<br>*With overtime running until the middle of the night every day, it cannot possibly be leaving him unaffected.*

---

## ～わけがない · Examples (cont.)

あれほど<ruby>楽<rt>たの</rt></ruby>しみにしていたのだから、<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>と<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いて<ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>ががっかりしない**わけがありません**。<br>*She'd been looking forward to it so much that there is no way our daughter isn't crushed to hear it's cancelled.*

---

## ～わけがない · Nuance & register

This is a forceful, emotionally coloured pattern. It does not weigh evidence the way `はずがない` does; it announces that the speaker regards the idea as out of the question. That makes it excellent for arguing, for reassuring - <code><ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れるわけがないよ</code> - and for sarcasm, and risky when the thing you are ruling out is what the person in front of you just said. Contradicting a superior with `そんなわけがありません` is blunt to the point of rudeness; softer options are <code>そうとは<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>えません</code> or `はずがありません`.

---

## ～わけがない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Casual speech drops `が`: `できるわけない`, <code><ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>ってるわけないじゃん</code>. That short form is very common among friends and carries a shrug with it. Going the other way, `わけはない` - with `は` instead of `が` - reads a shade calmer and more written, because the `は` gives it a whiff of contrast rather than flat denial.

Two neighbours to keep separate. `わけにはいかない` looks similar and means something else entirely - "I can't, for reasons of duty or circumstance" - and `わけではない` is the partial denial treated on its own page. Confusing `わけがない` with `わけではない` is the single most common error in this corner of the grammar, so the first contrast below is worth reading twice.

---

## ～わけがない · わけがない vs わけではない

> **Rule of thumb** - `わけがない` denies **the thing itself**: it cannot be true. `わけではない` denies **an inference**: the thing may well be partly true, but it does not follow the way you assumed.

<ruby>大好<rt>だいす</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>先輩<rt>せんぱい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>えるのに、<ruby>嬉<rt>うれ</rt></ruby>しくない**わけがない**。<br>*I get to see the senior I adore - there's no way I'm not delighted.*

<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>えて<ruby>嬉<rt>うれ</rt></ruby>しいが、<ruby>緊張<rt>きんちょう</rt></ruby>していない**わけではない**。<br>*I'm glad to see her, though that isn't to say I'm not nervous.*

Try the substitution test. Replace `わけではない` with "it's not that…" and the sentence should still work; replace `わけがない` with "there's no way…" and *that* should work. If both readings feel possible, look at what the speaker is doing: are they rejecting a claim outright, or trimming an over-reading?

---

## ～わけがない · わけがない vs わけではない (cont.)

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>犯人<rt>はんにん</rt></ruby>である**わけがない**。<br>*There's no way he's the culprit.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>疑<rt>うたが</rt></ruby>っている**わけではない**。<br>*It's not that I suspect him.*

The first defends him absolutely. The second only corrects an impression you may have given - you are not accusing him, but you have not said he is innocent either. That gap between the two is exactly the gap between the patterns.

---

## ～わけがない · わけがない vs はずがない

> **Rule of thumb** - `はずがない` argues **from a known fact**: given X, Y cannot follow. `わけがない` states the speaker's **conviction** that Y is impossible, with or without an argument.

<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていたのだから、<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かが<ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>れた**はずがない**。<br>*I had the key on me, so nobody can have got in.*

あんなに<ruby>優<rt>やさ</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が、そんなひどいことを<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う**わけがない**。<br>*Someone that kind would never say something so cruel.*

---

## ～わけがない · わけがない vs はずがない (cont.)

The first is a piece of reasoning you could write out step by step. The second is conviction about a person's character, and it would be odd to demand the speaker's evidence. In many sentences either pattern is acceptable and the difference is temperature: `はずがない` cool, `わけがない` warm. Note also that `はずがない` more comfortably admits it was wrong - <code>はずがないと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っていたのに</code> - while `わけがない` sounds strange doing so.

---

## ～わけがない · わけがない vs わけにはいかない

> **Rule of thumb** - `わけがない` is about what is **possible**. `わけにはいかない` is about what is **permissible**: you could do it, but circumstances or obligation stop you.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>大事<rt>だいじ</rt></ruby>な<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>だから、<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む**わけにはいかない**。<br>*There's an important exam tomorrow, so I can't very well take the day off.*

あの<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しさで、ゆっくり<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>める**わけがない**。<br>*As busy as that, there's no chance of getting any real rest.*

In the first the speaker is perfectly able to stay home; what rules it out is what is at stake. In the second nothing is stopping him except reality. Because the two forms sit one syllable apart, they get swapped constantly - check whether you mean "cannot happen" or "mustn't happen".

---

## ～わけがない · Common mistakes

- ❌ この<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>**だわけがない**。 → ✅ この<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>簡単<rt>かんたん</rt></ruby>**なわけがない**。<br>*A な-adjective takes `な` before `わけ`. The same goes for nouns, which take `な` or `という`.*

- ❌ <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いな**わけがない**が、<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたくない。 → ✅ <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いな**わけではない**が、<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたくない。<br>*The speaker means "it's not that I dislike it" - a partial denial. `わけがない` would claim disliking it is impossible, which clashes with the second half.*

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>る**わけがない**から、<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>はいらないだろう。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>り**そうもない**から、<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>はいらないだろう。<br>*Tomorrow's weather is uncertain, not impossible. `わけがない` is far too absolute for a forecast.*

---

## ～わけがない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>で<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たので、お<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む**わけがない**。 → ✅ <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>で<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たので、お<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む**わけにはいかない**。<br>*You are able to drink; you must not. Impossibility and prohibition are different patterns.*

# ～わけではない

**Meaning** · it's not that ～; that doesn't mean ～ - cancels a conclusion somebody has drawn, without denying the facts behind it

`わけではない` is a **partial** denial. Something has been said, or is about to be assumed, and the natural inference from it is wrong - so you reach in and take away the inference, leaving everything else standing. English does the same job with "it's not that…", "that's not to say…" and "not necessarily".

**What is negated: the inference, not the possibility.** `わけがない` slams the door on the whole idea; `わけではない` only says the idea does not follow from what you have just heard. Learners swap the two constantly, and the results are much ruder than intended.

---

## ～わけではない · Formation

`わけ` is a noun, so the clause in front of it takes noun-modifying shape.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + わけではない | <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>っている → <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>っているわけではない |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + わけではない | <ruby>払<rt>はら</rt></ruby>えない → <ruby>払<rt>はら</rt></ruby>えないわけではない |
| い-adjective | plain form + わけではない | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いわけではない |
| な-adjective | ～な + わけではない | <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いな → <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いなわけではない |
| Noun | ～な / ～という + わけではない | <ruby>専門家<rt>せんもんか</rt></ruby> → <ruby>専門家<rt>せんもんか</rt></ruby>というわけではない |
| Polite | … + わけではありません | <ruby>反対<rt>はんたい</rt></ruby>している → <ruby>反対<rt>はんたい</rt></ruby>しているわけではありません |
| Casual speech | … + わけじゃない | <ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>っている → <ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>っているわけじゃない |
| Softened further | … + というわけではない | <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いだ → <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いというわけではない |

---

## ～わけではない · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - the pattern is at its most natural with a partner phrase in front of it: <ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ずしも ("not necessarily"), <ruby>全<rt>まった</rt></ruby>く ("not entirely"), いつも, <ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>, or a からといって clause ("just because…"). Those words all set up the over-general idea that `わけではない` then trims back.

---

## ～わけではない · Examples

<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っているが、<ruby>大会<rt>たいかい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>る**わけではない**。<br>*I run every day, but that doesn't mean I'm going to enter a race.*

<ruby>反対<rt>はんたい</rt></ruby>している**わけではありません**が、もう<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>考<rt>かんが</rt></ruby>えたいです。<br>*It's not that I'm against it - I'd just like a bit more time to think.*

<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>くなっただけで、<ruby>怒<rt>おこ</rt></ruby>っている**わけじゃない**よ。<br>*I was just slow replying; it's not that I'm cross with you.*

お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>がない**わけではない**が、これは<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>すぎる。<br>*It's not that I can't afford it - this is simply too expensive.*

---

## ～わけではない · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>全<rt>まった</rt></ruby>く<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せない**わけではない**けれど、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>についていくのは<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しい。<br>*It isn't that I can't speak Japanese at all, but keeping up in meetings is hard.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>暮<rt>く</rt></ruby>らしているからといって、<ruby>寂<rt>さび</rt></ruby>しい**わけではない**。<br>*Living alone doesn't automatically mean being lonely.*

この<ruby>方法<rt>ほうほう</rt></ruby>がいつでもうまくいく**というわけではない**ので、<ruby>状況<rt>じょうきょう</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>て<ruby>判断<rt>はんだん</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*This approach doesn't always work, so use your judgment as the situation requires.*

<ruby>辞<rt>や</rt></ruby>めたいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったのは<ruby>本当<rt>ほんとう</rt></ruby>ですが、<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>すぐ<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ていく**わけではありません**。<br>*It's true that I said I want to quit, but that doesn't mean I'm walking out tomorrow.*

---

## ～わけではない · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and indispensable in polite conversation. Its social function is **face**: it lets you disagree with an interpretation while accepting the facts, so nobody has to be wrong. In a meeting, <code><ruby>予算<rt>よさん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りないというわけではありません</code> concedes that money has been discussed and merely corrects what the listener made of it.

Casual speech contracts it to `わけじゃない`, which is one of the most frequent phrases in everyday Japanese - 「べつに<ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いなわけじゃないけど」. The `べつに` in front is almost a fixed companion, and adding `けど` at the end leaves room for whatever the real point turns out to be.

---

## ～わけではない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Because the pattern *corrects an inference*, it needs the inference to exist. Said out of the blue, with nothing for it to push back against, it sounds like an answer to a question nobody asked. Give it a premise: a からといって clause, a previous remark, or a visible situation.

Two combinations are worth memorising whole. <code><ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ずしも～わけではない</code> is the standard "not necessarily", and it is common in written argument. `～ないわけではない` is a double negative meaning "it isn't that I can't" - grudging agreement, one step short of saying yes.

---

## ～わけではない · わけではない vs わけがない

> **Rule of thumb** - `わけではない` denies **the inference**; `わけがない` denies **the possibility**. One trims, the other refuses.

<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>な**わけではない**。ただ<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>りないだけだ。<br>*It isn't that I'm bad at kanji - I just haven't practised writing them enough.*

<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>ってもいない<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>ける**わけがない**。<br>*There's no way anyone can write kanji they've never been taught.*

---

## ～わけではない · わけではない vs わけがない (cont.)

The first accepts that the speaker's kanji are weak and rejects only the explanation offered for it. The second rules the whole proposition out. Swap them and you get nonsense: ❌<code><ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>なわけがない</code> would insist that being bad at kanji is impossible.

---

## ～わけではない · わけではない vs ではない

> **Rule of thumb** - plain `ではない` denies the **fact**. `わけではない` leaves the fact alone and denies **what you would read into it**.

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>のコーヒーはおいし**くない**。<br>*The coffee at this place isn't good.*

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>のコーヒーがおいしい**わけではない**が、<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かなのでよく<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る。<br>*It's not as if the coffee here is any good, but it's quiet, so I come often.*

The first is a verdict on the coffee. The second is a verdict on your reasoning: you assumed the coffee was why the speaker keeps coming, and they are correcting that, not reviewing the drink. This is why `わけではない` so often has a `が` or `けど` clause attached - the real point is usually in the second half.

---

## ～わけではない · わけではない vs とは限らない

> **Rule of thumb** - <code>とは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない</code> attacks a **generalisation**: it isn't always so. `わけではない` attacks an **inference about the case in front of you**.

<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>がおいしいとは**<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない**。<br>*Expensive restaurants aren't always good.*

<ruby>値段<rt>ねだん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>んだ**わけではない**。<ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>に<ruby>入<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったから<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>んだのだ。<br>*I didn't choose it on price - I chose it because I liked the taste.*

---

## ～わけではない · わけではない vs とは限らない (cont.)

The first would sit comfortably in an essay about restaurants in general. The second is about one decision, one speaker, one misreading being corrected. With <ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ずしも in front, the two come very close together, and in sentences about general rules they are often interchangeable.

---

## ～わけではない · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い**だわけではない**。 → ✅ <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い**なわけではない**。（or <ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いというわけではない）<br>*な-adjectives and nouns link to `わけ` with `な` or `という`, never `だ`.*

- ❌ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>解<rt>と</rt></ruby>ける**わけではない**。（intended: "there's no way I can solve this"） → ✅ この<ruby>問題<rt>もんだい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>解<rt>と</rt></ruby>ける**わけがない**。<br>*For flat impossibility you want `わけがない`. `わけではない` only says that being able to solve it does not follow from whatever was just said.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>な**わけではありません**。（simply stating you are not a doctor） → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>医者<rt>いしゃ</rt></ruby>**ではありません**。<br>*A plain fact takes a plain negative. `わけではない` is for cancelling an inference, and using it here implies somebody had just concluded that you were one.*

---

## ～わけではない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>くない**わけではない**から、<ruby>上着<rt>うわぎ</rt></ruby>はいらない。 → ✅ <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>くない**わけではない**から、<ruby>上着<rt>うわぎ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていこう。<br>*`～ないわけではない` is a double negative: "it isn't that it's not cold", i.e. it *is* somewhat cold. Make sure the second half agrees with the arithmetic.*

---

# ～とは限らない

**Meaning** · not necessarily ～; ～ isn't always the case - denies that a general rule holds every time

Read as *とは かぎらない*. The verb <ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>る means "to be limited to", so <code>とは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない</code> literally says "things are not limited to that outcome". You use it when somebody - the listener, common sense, or the speaker's own earlier sentence - is treating something as a rule, and you want to point out the exceptions without denying the rule's usual force.

---

## ～とは限らない · Formation

It attaches to a whole **plain-form clause**, because what it denies is a whole proposition, not a word.

---

## ～とは限らない · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, with a slight lean towards careful speech and writing. It is the standard way to introduce a caveat in an argument, so it fills newspaper columns, textbooks and advice of every kind, and it is equally at home in conversation when you want to warn someone off an assumption.

Socially it is gentle. Because it denies only universality, not the claim itself, it lets you push back without contradicting anyone flatly: "true as a rule, not true every time." <code><ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ずしもそうとは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>りません</code> is a stock polite disagreement in a meeting, and it is much softer than `そんなわけがありません`.

---

## ～とは限らない · Nuance & register (cont.)

The variant <code>～ないとも<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない</code> deserves attention, because it is a double negative and learners routinely read it backwards. Literally "it is not limited to not happening", it means "it might well happen - you never know", and it almost always introduces a **risk** worth preparing for: <code><ruby>地震<rt>じしん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きないとも<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない</code>. If a sentence with `とも限らない` seems to be arguing for complacency, you have the polarity the wrong way round.

One boundary: the pattern needs something general to push against - a rule, a tendency, a "just because" clause, an *everyone* or an *always*. Applied to a plain one-off prediction it sounds oddly stiff, and `かもしれません` or <code>とは<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>えません</code> is what a speaker would actually use.

## ～とは限らない · とは限らない vs わけではない

> **Rule of thumb** - <code>とは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない</code> denies that something is **always** so. `わけではない` denies that a conclusion **follows in this case**.

<ruby>人気<rt>にんき</rt></ruby>のある<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>がいつも<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**とは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない**。<br>*Popular shops aren't always cheap.*

その<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い**わけではない**。ただ<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>いから<ruby>通<rt>かよ</rt></ruby>っているだけだ。<br>*It's not that the shop is cheap - I go there simply because it's close.*

---

## ～とは限らない · とは限らない vs わけではない (cont.)

The first is about shops in general and could open a paragraph of consumer advice. The second is about one shop and one listener's mistaken assumption. Where a sentence is about a general rule, the two patterns often both fit, and <code><ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ずしも</code> can appear with either.

---

## ～とは限らない · とは限らない vs ～ないとも限らない

> **Rule of thumb** - <code>Xとは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない</code> = "X is not always so". <code>Xないとも<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない</code> = "it's not impossible that X happens" - a warning.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>晴<rt>は</rt></ruby>れる**とは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない**。<br>*It won't necessarily be fine tomorrow.*

<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らない**とも<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない**から、<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っていきなさい。<br>*It's not out of the question that it'll suddenly rain, so take an umbrella.*

The first removes a guarantee. The second raises a possibility you had dismissed, and it points forward to a precaution. Note the different attitudes: `とは限らない` dampens optimism, `とも限らない` disturbs complacency.

---

## ～とは限らない · とは限らない vs かもしれない

> **Rule of thumb** - `かもしれない` floats **one possibility**. <code>とは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない</code> corrects **someone's generalisation**.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になる**かもしれない**。<br>*Tomorrow's match might be called off.*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>ったら<ruby>必<rt>かなら</rt></ruby>ず<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になる**とは<ruby>限<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>らない**。<br>*Rain doesn't automatically mean the match is off.*

The first adds a new thought to the conversation. The second takes an existing belief - rain means cancellation - and puts a limit on it. If nobody in the conversation holds the belief, `とは限らない` has nothing to work on.

# ～ないことはない

**Meaning** · it's not that ～ can't be done; it isn't impossible - a double negative that concedes a small, reluctant yes

Two negatives make a weak positive. <code><ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>めないことはない</code> does not mean "I can drink"; it means "it isn't the case that I can't drink" - which in practice is a hedged, half-hearted "I can, up to a point". The pattern exists precisely so that you can agree without committing, and a `が` or `けど` clause spelling out the reservation almost always follows.

---

## ～ないことはない · Formation

The clause inside is in the **negative**, and `ことはない` negates it again.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | ない-form + ことはない | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないことはない |
| Potential verb (most common) | potential ない-form + ことはない | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>めない → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>めないことはない |
| い-adjective | ～くない + ことはない | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くない → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くないことはない |
| な-adjective | ～ではない + ことはない | <ruby>不安<rt>ふあん</rt></ruby>ではない → <ruby>不安<rt>ふあん</rt></ruby>ではないことはない |
| Noun | ～ではない + ことはない | <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>ではない → <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>ではないことはない |
| Softer, commoner in speech | ～ない + こともない | <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない → <ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わないこともない |
| Polite | … + ないことはありません | できない → できないことはありません |
| Usual continuation | … + が / けど + reservation | - → できないことはないが、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がかかる |

## ～ないことはない · Examples

お<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>め**ないことはない**。<br>*It's not that I can't drink.*

<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>め**ないことはない**が、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がかかる。<br>*I can read kanji well enough, though it takes me a while.*

<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>か**ないこともない**けど、できれば<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かについてきてほしい。<br>*I could go on my own, I suppose, but I'd rather someone came with me.*

<ruby>予算<rt>よさん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>やせば<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>完成<rt>かんせい</rt></ruby>でき**ないこともありません**が、<ruby>品質<rt>ひんしつ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>です。<br>*With a bigger budget it wouldn't be impossible to finish by next month, but I'd worry about the quality.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>い<ruby>分<rt>ぶん</rt></ruby>も<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>から**ないことはない**が、あのやり<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>には<ruby>賛成<rt>さんせい</rt></ruby>できない。<br>*I can see his side of it, up to a point, but I can't agree with the way he went about it.*

## ～ないことはない · Nuance & register

Register-neutral, and heavily used in polite negotiation, because it is the shape of a qualified yes. When a Japanese colleague answers a request with `できないことはありませんが…`, the important information is in whatever comes after `が`; the first half has only agreed that the thing is theoretically possible. Hearing it as an enthusiastic acceptance is a classic misreading.

`ないこともない` - with `も` instead of `は` - is softer again and is the version you hear most in conversation. Both forms keep company with hedging words: <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し, なんとか, どうにか and a trailing けど all cluster around them.

---

## ～ないことはない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Tone matters. Said about your own abilities the pattern is modest, which is usually welcome. Said about someone else's work - <code><ruby>悪<rt>わる</rt></ruby>くないことはない</code> - it can come out grudging or faintly damning, so it is worth being aware that you are praising with the handbrake on. Used constantly, it starts to sound evasive; a speaker who never gives a straight yes is noticed.

Finally, note that the pattern says nothing about *willingness* on its own. It reports that the door is not closed. Whether you actually intend to walk through it is the job of the sentence that follows.

---

## ～ないことはない · ～ないことはない vs ～ことはない

> **Rule of thumb** - with the negative inside, it means "it's not impossible". Without it, `～ことはない` means **"there's no need to"** - a completely different pattern that happens to look similar.

そんなに<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぐ**ことはない**よ。<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>はまだある。<br>*There's no need to rush. We've still got time.*

<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>が**ないことはない**が、<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>るほどではない。<br>*We do need to get a move on, though not to the point of running.*

The first tells someone to relax; the second grudgingly admits some hurry is called for. The only visible difference is the negative on the inner verb, so read these two slowly - mixing them up reverses your advice.

---

## ～ないことはない · ～ないことはない vs ～できる

> **Rule of thumb** - the potential form states an ability plainly. The double negative concedes it **with reservations attached**.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>せ**ます**。<br>*I can have it fixed by tomorrow.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>直<rt>なお</rt></ruby>せ**ないことはありません**が、ほかの<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まります。<br>*I could have it fixed by tomorrow, but everything else would have to stop.*

The first is a promise; the second is an opening position in a negotiation. If you mean the first and say the second, you will be heard as reluctant - and if you mean the second and say the first, you have just agreed to something you did not intend.

---

## ～ないことはない · ～ないことはない vs ～ないわけではない

> **Rule of thumb** - they overlap a great deal. The tendency: `ないことはない` concedes a **possibility or ability**; `ないわけではない` corrects an **impression** the listener has formed.

<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>れ**ないことはない**が、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったほうが<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>い。<br>*I could make one myself, but buying one is quicker.*

<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>が<ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い**なわけではない**。ただ<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がないだけだ。<br>*It's not that I dislike cooking - I just haven't the time.*

The first weighs whether the thing can be done; the second repairs a wrong reading of the speaker's feelings. In many sentences either would pass, and a native speaker would not always be able to say why they chose one. Where the sentence is about capability, `ないことはない` is the safer choice.

## ～ないことはない · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる**ないことはない**。 → ✅ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**ないことはない**。<br>*The inner clause has to be a proper ない-form. Attaching `ない` to the dictionary form is not a possible shape in Japanese.*

- ❌ この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**ないことはない**。 → ✅ この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**ではないことはない**。<br>*な-adjectives and nouns negate with `ではない` (casually `じゃない`) before the pattern.*

- ❌ 「<ruby>一緒<rt>いっしょ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きませんか。」「<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>け**ないことはありません**!」 → ✅ 「ぜひ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたいです。」<br>*As a reply to an invitation you are pleased to accept, the double negative sounds as if you are being dragged along. Save it for when you really do have a reservation.*

## ～ないことはない · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ そんなに<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>し**ないことはない**よ。（intended: "there's no need to worry"） → ✅ そんなに<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>する**ことはない**よ。<br>*"No need to" takes the dictionary form plus `ことはない`, with no inner negative.*

# ～のでしょうか

**Meaning** · I wonder if ～; might I ask ～ - a question wrapped in two layers of softening

Take it apart and it explains itself. The `の` is the explanatory `の` you met in `んです`: it asks about the *circumstances behind* something rather than the bare fact. The `でしょう` is the tentative ending: it presents the question as a musing rather than a demand. Put together, `のでしょうか` is the politest ordinary way to ask about something, and it is what fills enquiry emails, service counters and the rhetorical questions in speeches.

---

## ～のでしょうか · Formation

The clause takes the same shape it takes before `んです`.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | plain form + のでしょうか | <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>くのでしょうか |
| Verb (past) | た-form + のでしょうか | <ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まった → <ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まったのでしょうか |
| い-adjective | plain form + のでしょうか | <ruby>正<rt>ただ</rt></ruby>しい → <ruby>正<rt>ただ</rt></ruby>しいのでしょうか |
| な-adjective | ～な + のでしょうか | <ruby>必要<rt>ひつよう</rt></ruby>な → <ruby>必要<rt>ひつよう</rt></ruby>なのでしょうか |
| Noun | ～な + のでしょうか | <ruby>無料<rt>むりょう</rt></ruby> → <ruby>無料<rt>むりょう</rt></ruby>なのでしょうか |
| Spoken contraction | … + んでしょうか | <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>くんでしょうか |
| Plain, written musing | … + のだろうか | <ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>続<rt>つづ</rt></ruby>くのだろうか |
| Casual, to yourself | … + のかな / のかしら | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>るのかな |

---

## ～のでしょうか · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - with no `の`, plain `でしょうか` attaches straight to a noun or a ます-form and asks a simple tentative question: <code>お<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>でしょうか</code>. Adding `の` changes what you are asking about - see the second contrast below.

---

## ～のでしょうか · Examples

この<ruby>席<rt>せき</rt></ruby>、<ruby>空<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いている**のでしょうか**。<br>*Is this seat free, I wonder?*

どうして<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>はこんなに<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>多<rt>おお</rt></ruby>い**のでしょうか**。<br>*Why are there so many people here today?*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>説明会<rt>せつめいかい</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わる**のでしょうか**。<br>*What time does tomorrow's briefing finish, may I ask?*

すみません、この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>はこちらの<ruby>窓口<rt>まどぐち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>出<rt>だ</rt></ruby>せばいい**のでしょうか**。<br>*Excuse me - is this the window I should hand these papers in at?*

---

## ～のでしょうか · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>はどうしてあんなに<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>引<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>っ<ruby>越<rt>こ</rt></ruby>した**のでしょうか**。<br>*Why on earth did he move away so suddenly?*

お<ruby>申込<rt>もうしこ</rt></ruby>みは<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>受<rt>う</rt></ruby>け<ruby>付<rt>つ</rt></ruby>けていただける**のでしょうか**。<br>*Would you still be accepting applications up to next week?*

なぜ<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>同<rt>おな</rt></ruby>じ<ruby>失敗<rt>しっぱい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>何度<rt>なんど</rt></ruby>も<ruby>繰<rt>く</rt></ruby>り<ruby>返<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>してしまう**のでしょうか**。<br>*Why is it that people repeat the same mistakes over and over?*

<ruby>先週<rt>せんしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>注文<rt>ちゅうもん</rt></ruby>した<ruby>品物<rt>しなもの</rt></ruby>がまだ<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>かないのですが、いつごろ<ruby>発送<rt>はっそう</rt></ruby>していただける**のでしょうか**。<br>*The item I ordered last week still hasn't arrived - could I ask roughly when it will be dispatched?*

---

## ～のでしょうか · Nuance & register

`のでしょうか` is polite and slightly formal. It fits enquiry emails, counters, customer calls, and any moment when you would rather not sound as though you were interrogating someone. `んでしょうか` is the same thing in spoken shape: natural in conversation, a little too casual for a written enquiry to a company you do not know. `のだろうか` is the plain form and reads as thinking aloud - it is very common in essays and in a narrator's inner voice, and rare as a real question to a person.

`のかな` is the casual, mostly self-directed version - 「<ruby>間<rt>ま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>合<rt>あ</rt></ruby>うのかな」 - and can be aimed gently at a friend. `のかしら` does the same job with a softer ending that leans feminine in most people's speech; that is a tendency, not a rule, and it also sounds a touch old-fashioned to younger speakers.

---

## ～のでしょうか · Nuance & register (cont.)

The rhetorical use is worth noticing. In a presentation or an essay, `なぜ～のでしょうか` asks a question nobody is expected to answer, and the speaker answers it themselves in the next sentence. It is one of the standard ways to open a paragraph in Japanese exposition.

Two cautions. First, the softness is real but it is not infinite politeness: for a **request**, you still need a request form - `いただけませんか`, <code>お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いできますでしょうか</code> - because a question about circumstances is not an ask. Second, business Japanese is fond of stacking `ますでしょうか` (as in `いただけますでしょうか`). You will hear it constantly; some style guides call it redundant. It is safe to recognise it and to use the simpler `いただけないでしょうか` yourself.

---

## ～のでしょうか · のでしょうか vs ですか・ますか

> **Rule of thumb** - `ますか` asks for a **fact**. `のでしょうか` asks about the **situation behind** the fact, and takes the edge off the asking.

この<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まり**ますか**。<br>*Does this train stop at Tokyo?*

この<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>まらない**のでしょうか**。<br>*Am I right that this train doesn't stop at Tokyo?*

---

## ～のでしょうか · のでしょうか vs ですか・ますか (cont.)

The first is a clean request for information and is perfectly polite. The second implies you have noticed something - the train shot past the platform, the board says something odd - and are asking about *that*. Note the practical effect: with a stranger, `のでしょうか` sounds a little more deferential, but a long string of them can come across as roundabout. For a simple factual question, `ますか` is not rude.

---

## ～のでしょうか · のでしょうか vs でしょうか

> **Rule of thumb** - no `の`: you are asking **which/whether**. With `の`: you are asking **what lies behind it**. Why-questions almost always need the `の`.

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>から**でしょうか**。<br>*What time is the meeting, please?*

<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>変<rt>か</rt></ruby>わった**のでしょうか**。<br>*Has the meeting time been changed, then?*

---

## ～のでしょうか · のでしょうか vs でしょうか (cont.)

The first asks for a scheduled fact. The second asks about a change you suspect has happened and want explained. This is also why ❌<code>どうして<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかったでしょうか</code> sounds broken while <code>どうして<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかったのでしょうか</code> is fine: a "why" question is by definition about circumstances, and circumstances are what `の` marks.

---

## ～のでしょうか · のでしょうか vs のかな・のだろうか

> **Rule of thumb** - same question, three registers: `のでしょうか` polite and addressed to someone, `のかな` casual and half to yourself, `のだろうか` plain and written.

この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>、まだやっている**のかな**。<br>*Is this place still open, I wonder.*

こちらのお<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何時<rt>なんじ</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>営業<rt>えいぎょう</rt></ruby>している**のでしょうか**。<br>*Until what time is your shop open, may I ask?*

<ruby>人間<rt>にんげん</rt></ruby>はなぜ<ruby>物語<rt>ものがたり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>必要<rt>ひつよう</rt></ruby>とする**のだろうか**。<br>*Why is it that human beings need stories?*

---

## ～のでしょうか · のでしょうか vs のかな・のだろうか (cont.)

The first would be muttered to a friend outside a dark window; the second said to the person behind the counter; the third printed at the head of a chapter. Using the casual one at the counter is not offensive, only childish-sounding; using the polite one to yourself is merely odd.

---

## ～のでしょうか · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>は3<ruby>階<rt>がい</rt></ruby>**のでしょうか**。 → ✅ <ruby>受付<rt>うけつけ</rt></ruby>は3<ruby>階<rt>がい</rt></ruby>**なのでしょうか**。（or 3<ruby>階<rt>がい</rt></ruby>でしょうか）<br>*A noun needs `な` before `のでしょうか`, exactly as it needs `な` before `んです`.*

- ❌ どうして<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んだ**でしょうか**。 → ✅ どうして<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んだ**のでしょうか**。<br>*A why-question asks about circumstances, and that is precisely the job of `の`. Leaving it out makes the sentence sound unfinished.*

- ❌ この<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>んでくださる**のでしょうか**。（intended as a request） → ✅ この<ruby>荷物<rt>にもつ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>運<rt>はこ</rt></ruby>んで**いただけませんか**。<br>*Softening a question does not turn it into a request. As written, you are asking whether the person happens to be about to carry it.*

---

## ～のでしょうか · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>件<rt>けん</rt></ruby>はどうする**んでしょうか**。（in a formal email） → ✅ <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、この<ruby>件<rt>けん</rt></ruby>はどのように<ruby>進<rt>すす</rt></ruby>めればよろしい**でしょうか**。<br>*`んでしょうか` is the spoken contraction and reads as casual on the page; in writing, use `のでしょうか` or a plainly deferential request form.*

---
