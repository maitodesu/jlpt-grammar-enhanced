# の - of; possessive

**Meaning** · links two nouns, with the first one describing or owning the second

`の` is the glue between nouns. <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby> is "my book", <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>の<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby> is "a Japanese-language book", <ruby>木<rt>き</rt></ruby>の<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby> is "a wooden desk" - ownership is only the most obvious of a much wider set of relationships.

---

## の - of; possessive · Formation

The rule is one-directional and never varies: **the modifier comes first, the head noun comes last.** Whatever `の` points at, the thing being described is always on the right.

---

## の - of; possessive · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun + Noun | N1 の N2 | <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby> + かばん → <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>のかばん |
| Chained | N1 の N2 の N3 | <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちの<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>のかぎ |
| Apposition ("N1, who is N2") | N1 の N2 | <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちのミラーさん |
| Material or origin | N1 の N2 | <ruby>木<rt>き</rt></ruby>の<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>／イタリアのくつ |
| Standing in for the noun | N1 の (nothing after) | この<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>の**です** |
| After another particle | N + particle + の + N | <ruby>友人<rt>ゆうじん</rt></ruby>**から**の<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby> |
| ❌ な-adjective | takes `な`, not `の` | ❌きれいの<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby> → ✅きれい**な**<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby> |
| ❌ い-adjective | attaches bare | ❌<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きいの<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby> → ✅<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby> |

---

## の - of; possessive · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `の` joins a **noun** to a noun. If the word in front of it is an adjective, `の` is wrong: い-adjectives attach directly, な-adjectives take `な`.

---

## の - of; possessive · Examples

これは<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>です。<br>*This is my bicycle.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん**の**<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>にあります。<br>*Mr Tanaka's company is in Tokyo.*

この<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>**の**です。<br>*This umbrella is my sister's.*

イタリア<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby><ruby>冊<rt>さつ</rt></ruby><ruby>借<rt>か</rt></ruby>りました。<br>*I borrowed three Italian-language books.*

---

## の - of; possessive · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**の**ミラーさんが<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>びに<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます。<br>*My friend Miller is coming to visit next week.*

<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>くにおいしいラーメン<ruby>屋<rt>や</rt></ruby>があります。<br>*There's a good ramen place near the office.*

これは<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>焼<rt>や</rt></ruby>いたパンで、あちらは<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>**の**です。<br>*This is bread my mother baked; that one over there is from the shop.*

<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby><ruby>時代<rt>じだい</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>友人<rt>ゆうじん</rt></ruby>から**の**<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby>が、<ruby>久<rt>ひさ</rt></ruby>しぶりに<ruby>届<rt>とど</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*A letter arrived from a university friend for the first time in ages.*

---

## の - of; possessive · Nuance & register

`の` is completely register-neutral - the single most common word in Japanese text, and invisible in the way that English "of" and "'s" are invisible.

Three things worth knowing beyond the basic rule:

**`の` is vague on purpose.** <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんの<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby> can mean the book Yamada owns, the book Yamada wrote, the book Yamada is holding, or the book about Yamada. Japanese does not distinguish these, and context does all the work. Do not go looking for a more precise particle; there isn't one.

---

## の - of; possessive · Nuance & register (cont.)

**`の` can stand in for a noun already understood.** これは<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>のです - "this is mine." The noun has been deleted, not the `の`. This is why <ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>いの ("the red one") looks similar but is a different `の`: there, `の` is standing in for a whole noun after an adjective rather than marking a possessor.

**Chains get heavy fast.** <ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>近<rt>ちか</rt></ruby>くの<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby> is grammatical and no Japanese speaker would say it. Two `の` in a row is ordinary; three starts to strain; four means you should rebuild the sentence.

---

## の - of; possessive · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Contracted `ん` in speech.** In fast casual speech `の` sometimes reduces: <ruby>俺<rt>おれ</rt></ruby>んち ("my place", from <ruby>俺<rt>おれ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>家<rt>うち</rt></ruby>), <ruby>君<rt>きみ</rt></ruby>んとこ. These are fixed, informal, and mostly limited to places and homes; do not generalise the pattern.

---

## の - of; possessive · の vs な

> **Rule of thumb** - `の` after a **noun**, `な` after a **な-adjective**. The words look alike in the dictionary, which is the whole problem.

Many な-adjectives - きれい, <ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby>, <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>, <ruby>便利<rt>べんり</rt></ruby> - feel like nouns to a learner, and some of them genuinely can be nouns in other contexts. When they are describing the following noun, they take `な`.

<ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby>**な**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I went to a famous shop.*

<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I went to a shop in Tokyo.*

---

## の - of; possessive · の vs な (cont.)

<code><ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby></code> is a noun, so it takes `の`; <code><ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby></code> is a な-adjective, so it takes `な`. ❌<ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby> and ❌<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>な<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby> are both wrong, and the second one is instantly noticeable to a native ear.

The awkward middle case is <code><ruby>病気<rt>びょうき</rt></ruby></code> and <code><ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby></code>, which go both ways depending on what you mean: <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby>な<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby> = "a lively child"; <ruby>病気<rt>びょうき</rt></ruby>の<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby> = "a child who is ill" (the noun "illness" describing them).

---

## の - of; possessive · の (linking nouns) vs の (the nominaliser)

> **Rule of thumb** - if the word in front of `の` is a **noun**, it is this `の`. If it is a **verb or a clause**, it is the nominaliser - a different job entirely.

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>です。<br>*My hobby is photography. (noun + の - possession)*

<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>をとる**の**が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*I like taking photographs. (verb + の - the verb has been made into a noun)*

The two never compete for the same slot, so once you can see whether the word in front is a noun or a verb, the ambiguity disappears. Both are extremely common and you will use them in the same conversation.

---

## の - of; possessive · AのB vs BのA

> **Rule of thumb** - the **head noun goes last**, always. English puts it first ("a map *of* Tokyo") and second ("Tokyo*'s* map"), which is exactly why learners get the order backwards.

<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*I bought a map of Tokyo.*

<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby><br>*the Tokyo that belongs to the map - not a thing anyone would say*

---

## の - of; possessive · AのB vs BのA (cont.)

The test that always works: **what am I actually talking about?** In "a map of Tokyo" you are talking about a map, so <ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby> goes at the end. In "the teacher's car" you are talking about a car, so <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby> goes at the end: <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>.

---

## の - of; possessive · Common mistakes

- ❌ きれい**の**<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>をもらいました。 → ✅ きれい**な**<ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>をもらいました。<br>*きれい is a な-adjective despite ending in い. It takes `な`, never `の`.*

- ❌ <ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい**の**<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>んでいます。 → ✅ <ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>住<rt>す</rt></ruby>んでいます。<br>*い-adjectives attach straight to the noun. Nothing goes in between.*

- ❌ <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>です。<br>*The owner comes first and the thing owned comes last. Reversing them reverses the meaning, or produces nonsense.*

---

## の - of; possessive · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。 → ✅ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*Two nouns in a row need `の` to link them. (Some pairs have fused into single compound words over time, but you cannot invent new ones.)*

- ❌ これは<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>のかばん**の**です。 → ✅ これは<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>のかばんです。<br>*`の` stands in for a missing noun. When the noun is right there, a second `の` has nothing to do.*

---

## の - of; possessive · Practice 1

**1.** これは<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん____<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>ですか。

{reveal}
これは<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん**の**<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>ですか。

{reveal}
Two nouns side by side always need `の` between them. Note that the answer could also drop the second noun entirely - これは<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん**の**ですか - because `の` can stand in for a noun both speakers already have in mind.

---

## の - of; possessive · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か（の・な）<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>をお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします。

{reveal}
<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**な**<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>をお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か</code> is a な-adjective, so it links to the following noun with `な`. `の` is for nouns only, and the difference is audible - ❌<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かの<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby> marks a speaker as a beginner faster than almost any other error.

---

## の - of; possessive · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "That's my mother's friend's dog."

{reveal}
あれは<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だち**の**<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
Chained `の` runs in the same order as the English possessives - mother, then friend, then dog - with the thing you are actually talking about at the very end. Two `の` in a row like this is perfectly ordinary.

---

## の - of; possessive · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いました。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby>**な**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いました。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby></code> is a な-adjective. The `の` here comes from thinking of it as a noun meaning "fame", which it is not in this sentence - it is describing the person, so it needs `な`.

---

## の - of; possessive · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。 (b) <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>の<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。

{reveal}
(a) is a normal sentence: <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby> describes <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>, so this is "I bought a Japanese car." The head noun - the thing that was bought - sits at the end.

{reveal}
(b) reverses the roles, making <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby> the description and <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby> the thing bought: "I bought the Japan that belongs to a car." It is not ambiguous or poetic, just broken. The lesson is that in Japanese the order carries all the meaning; there is no second particle to fall back on.

# の - nominaliser

**Meaning** · turns a verb or a whole clause into a noun, so it can be a subject or an object

Japanese has no infinitive and no "-ing" noun. To say "swimming is fun" or "I forgot to lock the door", you take the clause you want and stick `の` on the end of it. What comes out behaves like an ordinary noun and can take `が`, `を` or `は`.

---

## の - nominaliser · Formation

`の` attaches to the **plain form** of whatever is in front of it. Nouns and な-adjectives need `な` first - the same shape as `ので` and `のに`.

---

## の - nominaliser · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (plain) | dictionary form + の | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ → <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐの |
| Verb (past) | た-form + の | <ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>った → <ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>ったの |
| Verb (negative) | ない-form + の | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かないの |
| い-adjective | plain form + の | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>いの |
| な-adjective | ～**な** + の | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かだ → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**な**の |
| Noun | Noun + **な** + の | <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だ → <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**な**の |
| A whole clause | clause + の | <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかったの |
| Before です／だ | の → **ん** | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くのです → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**ん**です |

---

## の - nominaliser · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - never ❌`だの`. A noun or な-adjective takes `な` before the nominalising `の`, exactly as it does before `ので`.

Once nominalised, the phrase takes a case particle like any noun: <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐの**が**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです、<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>めるの**を**<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れた、<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>うの**は**やめた。

---

## の - nominaliser · Examples

<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ**の**が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*I like swimming.*

<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>える**の**は<ruby>難<rt>むずか</rt></ruby>しい。<br>*Memorising kanji is hard.*

<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>を<ruby>消<rt>け</rt></ruby>す**の**を<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れました。<br>*I forgot to turn off the light.*

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもが<ruby>公園<rt>こうえん</rt></ruby>で<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んでいる**の**を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*I saw children playing in the park.*

---

## の - nominaliser · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きる**の**は<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>です。<br>*I'm no good at getting up early.*

<ruby>隣<rt>となり</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>で<ruby>誰<rt>だれ</rt></ruby>かが<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>っている**の**が<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>こえた。<br>*I could hear someone singing in the next room.*

<ruby>彼女<rt>かのじょ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>をやめた**の**を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っていますか。<br>*Did you know she'd quit the company?*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>通勤<rt>つうきん</rt></ruby>する**の**は、<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っていたより<ruby>大変<rt>たいへん</rt></ruby>でした。<br>*Commuting by bike on rainy days turned out to be harder than I'd expected.*

---

## の - nominaliser · Nuance & register

The nominaliser `の` is register-neutral, but it leans **spoken and concrete**. Its rival `こと` leans written and abstract, and the two are treated in the Contrast section below.

The thing worth real attention here is the `ん` contraction, because it is everywhere and it changes the feel of a sentence completely.

**`の` becomes `ん` before `です` and `だ`.** <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くのです → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くんです. The `のです` form is the written or careful-speech version; `んです` is what you actually hear.

**`んです` is not decoration.** It frames the sentence as an *explanation* - the speaker is accounting for something, or asking the listener to account for something.

---

## の - nominaliser · Nuance & register (cont.)

- <ruby>頭<rt>あたま</rt></ruby>が<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>い**です**。 - "I have a headache." A fact, offered flat.
- <ruby>頭<rt>あたま</rt></ruby>が<ruby>痛<rt>いた</rt></ruby>い**んです**。 - "The thing is, I have a headache." Now it<br>*explains why you are leaving early.*
- どうした**んですか**。 - "What's the matter?" Asking for an explanation. The bare<br>*❌どうしましたか is possible but noticeably colder.*

A learner who never uses `んです` sounds like they are reading a list; one who uses it in every sentence sounds like they are constantly justifying themselves. It belongs where there is a *reason* in the air.

---

## の - nominaliser · Nuance & register (cont.)

The plain-form version is `んだ`: <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>なんだ。 Note the `な` survives the contraction - noun + `な` + `んだ`.

Finally, a sentence-final `の` with rising intonation is a soft question, and in Tokyo speech it is marked as feminine or as child-directed: どうしたの? <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べないの? Men use it too, but usually with a following `か` or in a gentler register.

---

## の - nominaliser · の vs こと

> **Rule of thumb** - `の` for things you directly see, hear and experience; `こと` for abstractions, facts and reported information. Where either works, `の` is the spoken choice.

Three cases where the choice is **not** free:

**Verbs of direct perception require `の`.** <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る、<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く、<ruby>感<rt>かん</rt></ruby>じる and friends are watching an event happen, not contemplating a fact.

<ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っている**の**を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*I saw the cat running. (I watched it happen - の)*

---

## の - nominaliser · の vs こと (cont.)

**Fixed expressions require `こと`.** `ことができる`, `ことがある`, `ことにする`, `ことになる` are set phrases and admit no substitution: ❌<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのができる.

**`X は Y です` equations require `こと`.** <ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むことです。 With `の` this sounds like the hobby *is* the act happening in front of you.

Everywhere else - <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐの／ことが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです - both are correct, and `の` is what you will hear.

---

## の - nominaliser · の (nominaliser) vs の ("the one")

> **Rule of thumb** - after a **verb or clause**, `の` makes an action into a noun. After an **adjective**, `の` usually stands in for a concrete thing: "the ~ one".

<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る**の**が<ruby>速<rt>はや</rt></ruby>いですね。<br>*You're fast at running. (の = the act of running)*

<ruby>赤<rt>あか</rt></ruby>い**の**をください。<br>*I'll take the red one. (の = the object itself)*

---

## の - nominaliser · の (nominaliser) vs の ("the one") (cont.)

In practice the difference is decided by what makes sense, and a clause can go either way: <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>ったの can mean "the one I bought yesterday" or "the fact that I bought it yesterday". Context settles it, and native speakers do not experience this as ambiguous.

---

## の - nominaliser · ～のです／んです vs the plain statement

> **Rule of thumb** - `んです` says "here is the explanation". A plain `です` sentence just states the fact.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き**ます**。<br>*I'm going to Tokyo tomorrow.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>へ<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**んです**。<br>*I'm going to Tokyo tomorrow, you see. (…which is why I can't come / why I'm packing)*

---

## の - nominaliser · ～のです／んです vs the plain statement (cont.)

Get this wrong in either direction and you sound odd. Answering <ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>をしていますか with a bare fact is fine; answering どうして<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なかったんですか without `んです` in your own reply sounds evasive, because the question explicitly asked for a reason.

---

## の - nominaliser · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>す**のができます**。 → ✅ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>す**ことができます**。<br>*`ことができる` is a fixed expression. `の` cannot be substituted into it, however natural the rest of the sentence sounds.*

- ❌ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**の**が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か**なの**が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*な-adjectives take `な` before the nominaliser, exactly as they do before `ので` and `のに`.*

- ❌ <ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>鳴<rt>な</rt></ruby>いている**こと**を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。 → ✅ <ruby>猫<rt>ねこ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>鳴<rt>な</rt></ruby>いている**の**を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*With `こと` this becomes "I heard *that* the cat was crying" - secondhand news. Direct perception needs `の`.*

- ❌ <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**の**んです。 → ✅ <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>**な**んです。<br>*A noun keeps its `な` when `の` contracts to `ん`. ❌<code><ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>のんです</code> and ❌<code><ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>だんです</code> are both wrong.*

---

## の - nominaliser · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>める**の**<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れた。 → ✅ <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>める**のを**<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れた。<br>*Once you have made a noun, it needs a particle like any other noun. (`を` does drop in fast speech, but learn the full form first.)*

## の - nominaliser · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る____は<ruby>気持<rt>きも</rt></ruby>ちがいいです。

{reveal}
<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る**の**は<ruby>気持<rt>きも</rt></ruby>ちがいいです。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る</code> is a verb, and the sentence needs a subject for <code><ruby>気持<rt>きも</rt></ruby>ちがいい</code> - so the verb has to become a noun first. `こと` would also be grammatical here; `の` is the more spoken choice.

---

## の - nominaliser · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はギターを<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>く（の・こと）ができます。

{reveal}
<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はギターを<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>く**こと**ができます。

{reveal}
`ことができる` is a set phrase for ability, and its `こと` is frozen in place. This is one of the small number of contexts where `の` and `こと` are not interchangeable at all.

---

## の - nominaliser · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I saw him get on the train."

{reveal}
<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>る**の**を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ました。

{reveal}
Direct perception - you watched it with your own eyes - so this must be `の`, not `こと`. Note that the subject inside the nominalised clause takes `が`, never `は`.

---

## の - nominaliser · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>だのです。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>**な**のです。 (spoken: <ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>**なんです**)

{reveal}
A noun takes `な` before the nominaliser, never `だ`. The same `な` is what survives into the contracted `んです`, which is why you hear <code><ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>なんです</code> and never ❌<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>だんです.

## の - nominaliser · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ません。 (b) <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないんです。

{reveal}
(a) simply reports that Tanaka is not coming. It is the right sentence if someone asked you for the guest list.

{reveal}
(b) presents the same fact as the explanation for something - perhaps why there is a spare chair, or why the meeting is being moved. `んです` always points at a surrounding situation the fact is accounting for, and using it with no such situation in view makes the listener hunt for one.

# Noun-modifying clauses

**Meaning** · put a whole clause in front of a noun to describe it - no relative pronoun, no word order change

English says "the book **that** I bought yesterday", moving the noun to the front and inserting a relative pronoun. Japanese says <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby> - clause first, noun last, nothing inserted. This one structure is the key to every sentence above N5.

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · Formation

The clause goes **in front of** the noun, in the **plain form**. That is the whole rule.

---

| Verb (negative) | ない-form + Noun | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>まない<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby> |

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ❌ Topic は inside | never | ❌<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby> |

> **Rule of thumb** - three things Japanese does *not* do: it does not use a relative pronoun, it does not move the noun, and it does not use polite forms inside the clause. Everything you would express with "who / which / that" is expressed by position alone.

> **Note** - the clause can be any length at all, and it keeps its own particles inside. <ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby><ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>で<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さい<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>わん - every word before <ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>わん is describing it. When reading, look for the noun at the end first, then work backwards.

## Noun-modifying clauses · Examples

これは**<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>が<ruby>作<rt>つく</rt></ruby>った**ケーキです。<br>*This is a cake my mother made.*

**<ruby>眼鏡<rt>めがね</rt></ruby>をかけている**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>中村<rt>なかむら</rt></ruby>さんです。<br>*The person wearing glasses is Nakamura.*

**<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>た**<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>はとてもおもしろかったです。<br>*The film I saw yesterday was really good.*

**<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby><ruby>泊<rt>と</rt></ruby>まった**ホテルは<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>から<ruby>遠<rt>とお</rt></ruby>かった。<br>*The hotel we stayed at last year was a long way from the station.*

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · Examples (cont.)

**<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>の<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>った**<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>新聞<rt>しんぶん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>載<rt>の</rt></ruby>りました。<br>*A photograph my father took appeared in the newspaper.*

**<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>にこの<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>をおすすめします。<br>*I'd recommend this shop to anyone who likes sweet things.*

**<ruby>家族<rt>かぞく</rt></ruby>みんなで<ruby>旅行<rt>りょこう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>い<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>を<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>でもよく<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えています。<br>*I still remember clearly the trip we all took as a family.*

**<ruby>駅前<rt>えきまえ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しくできた**<ruby>図書館<rt>としょかん</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby><ruby>九時<rt>くじ</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いています。<br>*The library that's just opened by the station stays open until nine at night.*

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · Nuance & register

The construction itself carries no register - it is simply how Japanese describes things, and it appears in every sentence type at every politeness level. What *is* register-marked is the fact that the clause is always plain: <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>をお<ruby>送<rt>おく</rt></ruby>りします is entirely polite, because politeness lives on the final verb of the sentence and nowhere else.

Three habits that separate fluent-sounding Japanese from translated English:

**Long modifiers are normal.** English resists front-loading and reaches for "which…" clauses instead. Japanese does not: a modifier of fifteen syllables sitting in front of a two-syllable noun is unremarkable in a newspaper. Learn to read to the end of the phrase before deciding what it means.

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · Nuance & register (cont.)

**`が` inside the clause often becomes `の`.** <ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>が<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>った<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby> and <ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>の<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>った<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby> mean the same thing. The `の` version sounds slightly more compact and slightly more written; it is very common in headlines and titles. This substitution works **only** inside a modifying clause - never in a main sentence.

**Ambiguity is tolerated.** <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby> is "the person I like" in most contexts, but it can also be read as "the person who likes me". Japanese does not disambiguate this, and speakers rely on context rather than rebuilding the sentence.

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · が vs の inside the clause

> **Rule of thumb** - inside a noun-modifying clause, the subject may be marked `が` **or** `の`, with no change of meaning. Outside such a clause, only `が` works.

**<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちが<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してくれた**<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいます。<br>*I'm reading a book a friend lent me.*

**<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちの<ruby>貸<rt>か</rt></ruby>してくれた**<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいます。<br>*I'm reading a book a friend lent me. (same meaning, slightly tighter)*

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · が vs の inside the clause (cont.)

Two guard rails. The `の` substitution fails as soon as the clause contains another `の`, because the string becomes unreadable. And it never applies to a main clause: ❌<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちの<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました is simply wrong, where <ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちが<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ました is right.

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · clause + Noun vs clause + の

> **Rule of thumb** - a clause in front of a **noun** describes that noun. A clause in front of **`の`** turns the clause itself into a noun.

The two look identical until you notice what is at the end.

**<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っている**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*I saw a person who runs every morning.*

<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っている**の**を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*I saw them running every morning.*

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · clause + Noun vs clause + の (cont.)

In the first, the thing seen is a **person**. In the second, the thing seen is the **event** of running. Learners often produce the second when they meant the first, because `の` feels like a safe filler - it is not; it changes what the sentence is about.

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · modifying clause vs ～という + Noun

> **Rule of thumb** - if the noun could fill a slot inside the clause, modify it directly. If the clause is the *content* of the noun, you need `という`.

**<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いた**<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みました。<br>*I read a letter Tanaka wrote. (the letter is what he wrote - direct modification)*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**という**<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I heard that Tanaka isn't coming. (the news *is* the clause - という)*

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · modifying clause vs ～という + Noun (cont.)

The test: can you put the noun back into the clause? <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いた works, so <ruby>手紙<rt>てがみ</rt></ruby> modifies directly. ❌<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>を<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない does not work, so <ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby> needs `という`. Content nouns like <ruby>話<rt>はなし</rt></ruby>、ニュース、<ruby>約束<rt>やくそく</rt></ruby> and <ruby>予定<rt>よてい</rt></ruby> behave this way; you will meet the rule properly at N4.

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い**ました**<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>はこれです。 → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>っ**た**<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>はこれです。<br>*The clause is always plain. Politeness goes on the last verb of the sentence, which here is `です`.*

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>た**の**<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>はよかった。 → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>た<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>はよかった。<br>*Nothing goes between the clause and the noun. The `の` is imported from <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>の<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>, where it links two *nouns* - a verb needs no linker.*

- ❌ <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っている、<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんです。 → ✅ <ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>っている<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんです。<br>*The description goes **before** the noun. Japanese has no way to hang a clause off the back of a noun.*

---

## Noun-modifying clauses · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**は**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>をなくしました。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>をなくしました。<br>*`は` marks the topic of a whole sentence and cannot appear inside a modifying clause. Inside, the subject takes `が` (or `の`).*

- ❌ <ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby><ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いました。 → ✅ <ruby>有名<rt>ゆうめい</rt></ruby>**な**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*な-adjectives keep their `な` in front of the noun, just as they do outside a clause.*

---

# Counters

**Meaning** · you cannot count a Japanese noun with a bare number - every noun needs a counter word

English says "three apples". Japanese says りんごを<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つ - number plus a counter chosen to fit the *kind* of thing being counted. The counters themselves are easy; the readings are not, because the number and the counter fuse and change sound. Those changes are the whole content of this page.

---

## Counters · Formation

The counter attaches directly to the number, and the resulting phrase usually sits **after** the noun and its particle, behaving like an adverb: りんご**を**<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。

---

## Counters · Formation (cont.)

Learn the pattern once and most counters fall out of it.

## Counters · Nuance & register

Counters are not optional and not stylistic - they are part of the grammar, and a bare number in front of a noun is simply ungrammatical.

**The ～つ series is your safety net.** It is native Japanese vocabulary, it runs only from one to ten, and it counts almost any concrete object whose proper counter you have forgotten. Nobody will misunderstand りんごを<ruby>四<rt>よっ</rt></ruby>つ. Above ten, ～つ stops existing and you fall back on <ruby>個<rt>こ</rt></ruby> or the specific counter.

---

## Counters · Nuance & register (cont.)

**People are the one place you cannot improvise.** ❌いちにん and ❌ににん do not exist. <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby> and <ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby> are irregular native forms, and from three on the counter switches to the Sino-Japanese にん. This is worth over- learning: <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>で ("alone") and <ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby>で ("the two of us") are everyday words.

**Register.** In shops and restaurants you will hear the deferential <code><ruby>名<rt>めい</rt></ruby><ruby>様<rt>さま</rt></ruby></code> for people - <ruby>二名様<rt>にめいさま</rt></ruby> - and <code>お<ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby><ruby>様<rt>さま</rt></ruby></code>. You are not expected to produce these at N5, only to recognise them. In casual speech the counter can drop when context is obvious: コーヒー、<ruby>二<rt>ふた</rt></ruby>つ。

---

## Counters · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Where the phrase sits.** The neutral position is after the noun-plus-particle, as an adverb. Putting it in front with `の` (<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つのりんご) is also correct but feels more written and slightly more emphatic - it is what you use when the quantity is the point.

---

## Counters · ～つ vs ～個

> **Rule of thumb** - both count generic objects. ～つ is native, softer, and stops at ten; <code><ruby>個<rt>こ</rt></ruby></code> is Sino-Japanese, has no upper limit, and leans towards small, discrete, roundish things.

たまごを**<ruby>六<rt>むっ</rt></ruby>つ**<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>います。<br>*I'll use six eggs.*

たまごを**<ruby>十二個<rt>じゅうにこ</rt></ruby>**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*I bought twelve eggs.*

The second sentence cannot use ～つ, because the series has no word for twelve. Below ten the two are largely interchangeable for objects like eggs, apples and boxes, with ～つ sounding a shade more everyday.

---

## Counters · ～つ vs ～個 (cont.)

<code><ruby>個<rt>こ</rt></ruby></code> is also what a shop assistant will say, because it works for any quantity and does not need a decision about shape.

---

## Counters · ～回 vs ～階

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>回<rt>かい</rt></ruby></code> counts **times**; <code><ruby>階<rt>かい</rt></ruby></code> counts **floors**. They sound almost identical, and the one place they diverge is at three: <ruby>三回<rt>さんかい</rt></ruby> but <ruby>三階<rt>さんがい</rt></ruby>.

エレベーターで**<ruby>三階<rt>さんがい</rt></ruby>**まで<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がってください。<br>*Please take the lift up to the third floor.*

このボタンを**<ruby>三回<rt>さんかい</rt></ruby>**<ruby>押<rt>お</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*Please press this button three times.*

Both <ruby>何回<rt>なんかい</rt></ruby> ("how many times") and <ruby>何階<rt>なんがい</rt></ruby> ("which floor") exist, and the <ruby>階<rt>かい</rt></ruby> one is voiced. Getting <ruby>三階<rt>さんがい</rt></ruby> right is a small thing that immediately marks a careful learner.

---

## Counters · りんごを三つ vs 三つのりんご

> **Rule of thumb** - the counter phrase normally goes **after** the noun and its particle. Moving it in front with `の` is grammatical but shifts the emphasis onto the quantity.

テーブルにりんごを**<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つ**<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I put three apples on the table.*

テーブルに**<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つの**りんごを<ruby>置<rt>お</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I put three apples on the table. (with the "three" foregrounded)*

The first is what people say. The second reads like a description in a catalogue or a maths problem, and a learner who defaults to it - because it matches English word order - will sound stilted without ever making an outright error.

---

## Counters · Common mistakes

- ❌ りんごを<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby><ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。 → ✅ りんごを<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*A bare number cannot count a noun. You must choose a counter, and ～つ is the safe default for objects up to ten.*

- ❌ いちにん、ににん → ✅ <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby>、<ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby><br>*The first two people are native readings and do not follow the pattern. From <ruby>三人<rt>さんにん</rt></ruby> onwards the Sino-Japanese にん takes over.*

- ❌ よんにん → ✅ よにん<br>*<code><ruby>四人<rt>よにん</rt></ruby></code> drops the ん. (よんにん is widely heard, but よにん is what you should say and what the JLPT expects.)*

- ❌ さんほん、いちほん → ✅ さんぼん、いっぽん<br>*<code><ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby></code> voices to ぼん after <ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby> and geminates to ぽん after 1, 6, 8 and 10. The same shift hits <ruby>杯<rt>はい</rt></ruby>、<ruby>匹<rt>ひき</rt></ruby> and <ruby>分<rt>ふん</rt></ruby>.*

---

## Counters · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>二<rt>に</rt></ruby>つ、<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby>つ → ✅ <ruby>二<rt>ふた</rt></ruby>つ、<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つ<br>*The ～つ series takes **native** numerals (ひと、ふた、みっ…), never the Sino-Japanese ones. Mixing the two systems is the most audible counter error there is.*

# ずつ

**Meaning** · ~ each; ~ at a time - the same amount, distributed or repeated

`ずつ` takes a quantity and spreads it evenly. Either across people (<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つずつ = one each) or across time (<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ = a little at a time). Both readings come from the same idea: the same portion, again and again.

---

## ずつ · Formation

`ずつ` attaches to the **end of a quantity expression** - a number with its counter, or a word of amount. It never attaches to a bare noun.

---

## ずつ · Formation (cont.)

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Number + counter | Number+Counter + ずつ | <ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つずつ |
| Number + counter (people) | <ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby> + ずつ | <ruby>二人<rt>ふたり</rt></ruby>ずつ |
| Bare number | Number + ずつ | <ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby>ずつ |
| Amount word | <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し + ずつ | <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ |
| Casual amount word | ちょっと + ずつ | ちょっとずつ |
| With an object | Noun を Number+Counter ずつ | あめを<ruby>二<rt>ふた</rt></ruby>つずつ |
| Per unit | ～に + Number+Counter ずつ | <ruby>一日<rt>いちにち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby>ページずつ |
| ❌ Bare noun | not possible | ❌りんごずつ |

---

## ずつ · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - count first, `ずつ` second. The quantity always comes in front: <ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つずつ, never ❌ずつ<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ. And there must *be* a quantity - `ずつ` cannot hang off a noun on its own.

> **Note** - `ずつ` is written in hiragana. You will occasionally meet the old spelling `づつ` in older books and on shop signs; it is the same word and it is not the modern standard.

---

## ずつ · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>一日<rt>いちにち</rt></ruby>に<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby>ページ**ずつ**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>めば、<ruby>一<rt>いっ</rt></ruby>か<ruby>月<rt>げつ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わります。<br>*If you read three pages a day, you'll finish it in a month.*

<ruby>貯金<rt>ちょきん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>毎月<rt>まいつき</rt></ruby><ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し**ずつ**<ruby>増<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>えている。<br>*My savings are growing a little at a time.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>にも、<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し**ずつ**<ruby>慣<rt>な</rt></ruby>れてきました。<br>*I'm gradually getting used to the new job too.*

## ずつ · Nuance & register

`ずつ` is register-neutral and unremarkable - it appears in classroom instructions, in recipes, in business email and in conversation without changing shape.

Its two senses are worth separating in your head, because they behave differently:

**Distributive - "each".** <ruby>一人<rt>ひとり</rt></ruby><ruby>二<rt>に</rt></ruby><ruby>枚<rt>まい</rt></ruby>ずつ. There is a set of recipients and everyone gets the same amount. This is the sense you need when handing things out, forming groups, or dividing anything up. Japanese uses `ずつ` here where English often uses nothing at all: "take one" is ambiguous in English, <ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つずつとって is not.

---

## ずつ · Nuance & register (cont.)

**Incremental - "at a time".** <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ. There is no set of recipients; the repetition is over time. This is by far the most common use of the word, and <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ is close to being a fixed adverb meaning "gradually".

The casual variant is `ちょっとずつ`, which is warmer and softer than <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ and belongs to speech only. You will also hear the emphatic <ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つ ("one by one, individually"), which is not the same thing: that stresses handling each item separately rather than distributing equally.

One small pitfall: `ずつ` needs the sharing to be *even*. If two people get different amounts, `ずつ` is wrong and you should just name the amounts.

---

## ずつ · 一つ vs 一つずつ

> **Rule of thumb** - without `ずつ`, the number is the total. With `ずつ`, the number is the share **per person** or **per occasion**.

クッキーを**<ruby>二<rt>ふた</rt></ruby>つ**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*I bought two cookies. (two in total)*

クッキーを**<ruby>二<rt>ふた</rt></ruby>つずつ**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*I bought two cookies each. (two per person, so more than two altogether)*

The difference is not decoration - it changes the arithmetic. Leaving `ずつ` out of a sentence about handing things round is one of the errors most likely to produce an actual misunderstanding.

---

## ずつ · 少しずつ vs だんだん

> **Rule of thumb** - <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ describes the **size of the steps**; だんだん describes the **direction of the change**. They overlap in English as "gradually".

スープに<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し**ずつ**<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>してください。<br>*Add the water to the soup a little at a time.*

だんだん<ruby>暖<rt>あたた</rt></ruby>かくなってきました。<br>*It's been getting warmer and warmer.*

You cannot swap them here: ❌だんだん<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>足<rt>た</rt></ruby>してください gives no instruction about portions at all.

---

## ずつ · 少しずつ vs だんだん (cont.)

<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ implies a series of small increments you could in principle count; だんだん implies a smooth trend and carries no notion of portions. They combine happily - <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつだんだん<ruby>良<rt>よ</rt></ruby>くなる is redundant, but <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>える and だんだん<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>える differ: the first is about the batches, the second about the trajectory.

---

## ずつ · ずつ vs それぞれ

> **Rule of thumb** - `ずつ` needs a **quantity** and says everyone gets the same amount. `それぞれ` needs no quantity and says only that each person has their own - possibly different - one.

<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>一冊<rt>いっさつ</rt></ruby>**ずつ**<ruby>渡<rt>わた</rt></ruby>しました。<br>*I handed each student one book. (same number for everybody)*

<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>はそれぞれ<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>う<ruby>意見<rt>いけん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>持<rt>も</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*The students each have different opinions.*

Because `ずつ` insists on equality, ❌それぞれ<ruby>違<rt>ちが</rt></ruby>う<ruby>数<rt>かず</rt></ruby>ずつ is self-contradictory. And because it needs a quantity, `ずつ` cannot replace `それぞれ` in the second sentence at all.

---

## ずつ · Common mistakes

- ❌ ずつ<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つとってください。 → ✅ <ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つずつとってください。<br>*`ずつ` follows the quantity; it never leads. Word order here is fixed.*

- ❌ りんごずつ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。 → ✅ りんごを<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つずつ<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました。<br>*`ずつ` attaches to an amount, not to a noun. Without a number and counter in front of it there is nothing for it to distribute.*

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>にケーキを<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つずつください。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>にケーキを<ruby>一<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>つください。<br>*With a single recipient there is nothing to distribute across, so `ずつ` has no work to do and sounds strange.*

- ❌ <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつずつ<ruby>良<rt>よ</rt></ruby>くなる → ✅ <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつ<ruby>良<rt>よ</rt></ruby>くなる<br>*One `ずつ` per quantity. Doubling it for emphasis is not a thing; if you want emphasis, use <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しずつではあるが or simply repeat the verb.*

---

## ずつ · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>三<rt>さん</rt></ruby><ruby>枚<rt>まい</rt></ruby>、<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>二<rt>に</rt></ruby><ruby>枚<rt>まい</rt></ruby>ずつもらいました。 → ✅ …<ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>二<rt>に</rt></ruby><ruby>枚<rt>まい</rt></ruby>もらいました。<br>*`ずつ` promises that the shares are equal. If they are not, drop it and state the amounts.*

## ずつ · Practice 1

**1.** クラスを<ruby>四人<rt>よにん</rt></ruby>____のグループに<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>けます。

{reveal}
クラスを<ruby>四人<rt>よにん</rt></ruby>**ずつ**のグループに<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>けます。

{reveal}
The point is that every group has the same size, which is exactly what `ずつ` encodes. Without it the sentence would just describe one group of four rather than a whole class divided evenly.

## ずつ · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>に<ruby>紙<rt>かみ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>一枚<rt>いちまい</rt></ruby>（ずつ・それぞれ）<ruby>渡<rt>わた</rt></ruby>してください。

## ずつ · Practice 4

## ずつ · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもたちにあめを<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つあげました。 (b) <ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どもたちにあめを<ruby>三<rt>みっ</rt></ruby>つずつあげました。

{reveal}
(a) means three sweets were given to the children as a group - they will have to work out among themselves who gets what, and if there are four children someone is going without.

{reveal}
(b) means every child got three, so the total depends on how many children there were. The single word `ずつ` is doing all of that work, and English needs a whole phrase ("three each") to match it. When you are dividing anything up, leaving `ずつ` out changes the facts.
