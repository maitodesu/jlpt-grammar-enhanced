# ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした

**Meaning** · the polite verb endings - non-past and past, affirmative and negative

These four are one system, not four separate words. `ます` is a **politeness** marker that happens to carry tense as well: it tells your listener you are speaking to them carefully. Every Japanese verb has a plain form and a `ます` form, and the choice between them is a choice about the relationship, not about the meaning.

> **Note** - Japanese verbs fall into three classes, and every textbook names them differently. **う-verbs** are also called <ruby>五段<rt>ごだん</rt></ruby> (godan) verbs, or Group 1; **る-verbs** are also called <ruby>一段<rt>いちだん</rt></ruby> (ichidan) verbs, or Group 2; する and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る are the two irregulars, sometimes called Group 3. This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb** throughout.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Formation

All four endings attach to the same place - the **ます-stem**. Get the stem right and the rest is just picking one of four endings.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final -u → -i, + ます | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>きます |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + ます | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます |
| Verb (する) | し + ます | する → します |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | き + ます | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます |

Then the four endings, which never change shape:

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Formation (cont.)

| Tense and polarity | Ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Non-past, affirmative | ます | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みます |
| Non-past, negative | ません | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません |
| Past, affirmative | ました | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みました |
| Past, negative | ませんでした | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みませんでした |

For う-verbs, the final vowel moves from the う row to the い row. Every ending behaves the same way:

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Formation (cont.)

| Dictionary ending | ます-stem ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う | い | <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>います |
| つ | ち | <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>つ → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ちます |
| る | り | <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります |
| む | み | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みます |
| ぶ | び | <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>ぶ → <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>びます |
| ぬ | に | <ruby>死<rt>し</rt></ruby>ぬ → <ruby>死<rt>し</rt></ruby>にます |
| く | き | <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>きます |
| ぐ | ぎ | <ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぐ → <ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぎます |
| す | し | <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>す → <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>します |

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - a verb ending in る is only a る-verb if the vowel before the る is **i** or **e**. But plenty of verbs that pass that test are still う-verbs: <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>要<rt>い</rt></ruby>る. They give <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります, <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>ります, <ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ります, <ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>ります, <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>ります, <ruby>要<rt>い</rt></ruby>ります - never ❌<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ます. <ruby>着<rt>き</rt></ruby>る "wear" is a genuine る-verb (<ruby>着<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます); <ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>る "cut" is not (<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>ります). Same sound, different class.

The ます-stem is not only for `ます`. It is the hub the rest of the language hangs off: <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み**たい**, <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み**ながら**, <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み**ましょう**, <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み**やすい**, <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み**に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**. Learning to find it once pays off many times.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Examples

<ruby>毎朝<rt>まいあさ</rt></ruby>6<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby><ruby>半<rt>はん</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きます**。<br>*I get up at half past six every morning.*

わたしはお<ruby>酒<rt>さけ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みません**。<br>*I don't drink alcohol.*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>一日中<rt>いちにちじゅう</rt></ruby><ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りました**。<br>*It rained all day yesterday.*

すみません、この<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>が**わかりません**。<br>*Sorry - I don't know how to read this kanji.*

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>金曜日<rt>きんようび</rt></ruby>、<ruby>大阪<rt>おおさか</rt></ruby>から<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます**。<br>*The department manager is coming up from Osaka next Friday.*

<ruby>先週<rt>せんしゅう</rt></ruby>のテストは、あまりよく**できませんでした**。<br>*I didn't do very well on last week's test.*

<ruby>遅<rt>おそ</rt></ruby>くまで<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>をしていたので、<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>晩<rt>ばん</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べませんでした**。<br>*I was working late, so I didn't have any dinner yesterday.*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>は9<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>始<rt>はじ</rt></ruby>まります**ので、8<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby><ruby>半<rt>はん</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>てください。<br>*Tomorrow's meeting starts at nine, so please be at the office by eight thirty.*

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Nuance & register

`ます` is <ruby>丁寧語<rt>ていねいご</rt></ruby> - the polite, or "distal", style. It is the default with anyone you are not close to: shop staff, colleagues, teachers, strangers, and anyone you have just met. The plain forms (<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>む, <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んだ, <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>まない) are the "close" style, used with family, friends, and people clearly junior to you.

Three things learners get wrong about that split:

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **`ます` is not honorific.** It says nothing about respecting the *subject* of<br>*the sentence. Honorific and humble language (<ruby>召<rt>め</rt></ruby>し<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がります, いたします) is a separate layer stacked on top.*
- **Plain is not sloppy.** Newspapers, essays, academic writing and most novels<br>*are written in plain form throughout. `ます` in an essay looks like a letter to the reader.*
- **Plain form is grammatically required inside sentences.** Before と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>う,<br>*つもり, とき, ので, から and inside a clause modifying a noun, the verb goes plain regardless of how polite the sentence ends up: <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います, not ❌<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きますと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います. `ます` lives at the **end** of the sentence.*

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Nuance & register (cont.)

The non-past `ます` covers habits and the future alike - <ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ります "I run every day" and <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>ります "I'll run tomorrow" - but never an action happening at this moment; that needs `～ています`.

Within a single conversation you normally hold one register. Dropping one plain sentence into polite speech is a real and natural move - it reads as thinking aloud, or as a flash of warmth - but a whole reply in plain form to a stranger is rude. Going the other way, switching from plain back to `ます` with a friend signals distance, and Japanese speakers use it deliberately when they are annoyed.

In speech, `ます` is very often followed by softeners: `～ますね`, `～ますよ`, `～ますけど`. Bare `～ます` sentence after sentence sounds like a public announcement.

---

## ～ます vs the dictionary form

> **Rule of thumb** - the ます-form is about **who you are talking to**. The plain form is what the **grammar around the verb** demands. They answer different questions, so they are not really alternatives.

<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>、<ruby>国<rt>くに</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります**。<br>*I'm going home to my country next year.*

<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>、<ruby>国<rt>くに</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*I think I'll go home to my country next year.*

The second sentence is exactly as polite as the first - the politeness is carried by <ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います at the end. The embedded verb has to be plain.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · ～ません vs ～ないです

> **Rule of thumb** - the meaning is identical. `～ません` is the tidier, more formal negative; `～ないです` is the plain negative with a polite `です` bolted on, and belongs to relaxed speech.

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**ありません**。<br>*I don't have time today. (careful, businesslike)*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>はちょっと<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**ないです**。<br>*I haven't really got time today. (softer, conversational)*

The same split runs through the past: `～ませんでした` is the formal past negative, `～なかったです` the colloquial one. In writing, an email to a client, or any situation with a script, use the `ません` column. With a colleague you get on with, `ないです` is friendlier.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · ～ませんか vs ～ましょう

> **Rule of thumb** - `～ませんか` **asks** whether you'd like to; `～ましょう` **assumes** you would. Both are built on the same ます-stem.

<ruby>一緒<rt>いっしょ</rt></ruby>にお<ruby>昼<rt>ひる</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べませんか**。<br>*Would you like to have lunch together?*

じゃ、そろそろ**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きましょう**。<br>*Right, let's get going then.*

`～ませんか` leaves the other person free to say no, so it is the polite way to open an invitation. `～ましょう` is for when the plan is already agreed, or when you are the one leading - a tour guide, a teacher, the person organising the evening. Using `～ましょう` to make the first invitation to someone senior is pushy.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるます → ✅ **<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます**<br>*`ます` attaches to the ます-stem, never to the dictionary form. Drop the る first.*

- ❌ <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みませんかった → ✅ **<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みませんでした**<br>*`ません` has no past form of its own. You put the past tense on the copula behind it: ません + でした.*

- ❌ <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ます → ✅ **<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ります**<br>*<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る ends in る but is an う-verb, so the stem is <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>り, not <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>.*

- ❌ この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いますか。 → ✅ この<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いですか**。<br>*`ます` is for verbs only. Adjectives and nouns take `です`.*

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きますと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。 → ✅ <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。<br>*Inside a clause the verb is plain. `ます` only appears at the very end of the sentence.*

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby><ruby>電車<rt>でんしゃ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>____。

{reveal}
<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>**きます**

{reveal}
<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く is an う-verb, so the final -ku becomes -ki before `ます`: <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き + ます. The non-past covers habits, which is what <ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby> calls for.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が（<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りません・<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りませんでした）。

{reveal}
<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りませんでした**。

{reveal}
<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby> fixes the sentence in the past, so the negative has to be past too. Japanese marks tense on the verb even when the time word already says it - you cannot leave the ending non-past the way English can.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I didn't watch television last night."

{reveal}
<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>はテレビを**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ませんでした**。

{reveal}
<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る is a る-verb, so the stem is just <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>: drop る, add ませんでした. The plain-form answer <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>なかった is equally correct if you are talking to a friend.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>今朝<rt>けさ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるませんでした。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>今朝<rt>けさ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べませんでした**。

{reveal}
The dictionary form and the polite ending cannot both be there. <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる is a る-verb: remove る to get the stem <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ, then attach ませんでした.

---

## ～ます・～ません・～ました・～ませんでした · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がありますか。 (b) <ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>、<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>ある？

{reveal}
They ask exactly the same question; only the relationship differs. (a) is the polite style - what you say to a colleague, a teacher, or anyone you are not close to. (b) is plain form with the question mark carried by rising intonation alone, which is what you use with a friend or a family member.

{reveal}
Say (b) to your manager and it sounds startlingly familiar; say (a) to a close friend and it sounds like you are about to deliver bad news.

# する

**Meaning** · to do - and the machine that turns nouns into verbs

`する` is the busiest verb in Japanese. On its own it means "do", but its real job is bigger than that: stick `する` on the back of almost any noun and you have made a verb. <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby> "study (n.)" → <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>する "to study". A very large share of the verbs you will ever meet are built this way.

> **Note** - Japanese has exactly two irregular verbs: `する` and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る. All other verbs are う-verbs (<ruby>五段<rt>ごだん</rt></ruby> / godan / Group 1) or る-verbs (<ruby>一段<rt>いちだん</rt></ruby> / ichidan / Group 2). This page uses **う-verb** and **る-verb**. `する` is irregular because its stem changes vowel from form to form - し, さ, せ - so it cannot be predicted from the dictionary form.

---

## する · Formation

`する` attaches to nouns. What it attaches to determines whether `を` can appear between them.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (<ruby>漢語<rt>かんご</rt></ruby> compound) | Noun + する | <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>する |
| Noun (with the object marker) | Noun + を + する | <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>をする |
| Noun (loanword) | Word + する | コピー → コピーする |
| Noun (activity, no する-verb) | Noun + を + する | テニス → テニスをする |
| な-adjective / Noun | ～に + する | <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か → <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにする |
| い-adjective | ～く + する | <ruby>短<rt>みじか</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>短<rt>みじか</rt></ruby>くする |

---

## する · Formation (cont.)

The verb itself conjugates like this. Nothing here follows the う-verb or る-verb rules, which is what "irregular" means - these have to be memorised:

---

## する · Formation (cont.)

| Form | する | With a noun |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | する | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>する |
| Polite | します | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>します |
| Plain negative | しない | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>しない |
| Polite negative | しません | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>しません |
| Plain past | した | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>した |
| Polite past | しました | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>しました |
| Plain past negative | しなかった | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>しなかった |
| て-form | して | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>して |
| Volitional | しよう／しましょう | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>しよう |

---

## する · Formation (cont.)

| Form | する | With a noun |
|---|---|---|
| Potential | できる | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>できる |
| Conditional | したら／すれば | <ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>したら |

> **Rule of thumb** - the potential of `する` is **できる**, a completely separate word. There is no ❌されます, no ❌しられます. And because `できる` is intransitive, its object takes `が`: <ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>**が**できます, never ❌<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>をできます.

`する` also carries several meanings that have nothing to do with "doing":

---

## する · Formation (cont.)

- **Sensations**, with `が` - <ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>がする "there's a sound",<br>*においがする "there's a smell", <ruby>味<rt>あじ</rt></ruby>がする "it tastes of…".*
- **Deciding**, with `に` - わたしはカレーにします "I'll have the curry."
- **Wearing** accessories - ネクタイをする, マスクをする, <ruby>時計<rt>とけい</rt></ruby>をする.
- **Costing** - この<ruby>傘<rt>かさ</rt></ruby>は5000<ruby>円<rt>えん</rt></ruby>しました "this umbrella cost<br>*5,000 yen."*

---

## する · Examples

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>、<ruby>宿題<rt>しゅくだい</rt></ruby>を**しています**。<br>*I'm doing my homework right now.*

わたしはコーヒーに**します**。<br>*I'll have the coffee.*

<ruby>毎晩<rt>まいばん</rt></ruby>30<ruby>分<rt>ぷん</rt></ruby>ぐらい**<ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>します**。<br>*I exercise for about thirty minutes every evening.*

<ruby>土曜日<rt>どようび</rt></ruby>に<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>掃除<rt>そうじ</rt></ruby>をしました**。<br>*I cleaned my room on Saturday.*

---

## する · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>台所<rt>だいどころ</rt></ruby>のほうから<ruby>変<rt>へん</rt></ruby>な<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>が**しました**。<br>*There was a strange noise from the direction of the kitchen.*

あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>はいつも<ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby>いネクタイを**している**。<br>*That guy always wears a blue tie.*

<ruby>来月<rt>らいげつ</rt></ruby><ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>くので、きのうホテルを**<ruby>予約<rt>よやく</rt></ruby>しました**。<br>*I'm going to Kyoto next month, so I booked a hotel yesterday.*

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>東京<rt>とうきょう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>で<ruby>経済<rt>けいざい</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>をしています**。<br>*My son is studying economics at a university in Tokyo.*

---

## する · Nuance & register

Plain `する` is register-neutral: `します` is polite, `する` is plain, and nothing about the word itself is stiff or casual. What is worth knowing is that `する` sits at the bottom of a three-storey politeness building, and the other two storeys are irregular too:

- **<ruby>致<rt>いた</rt></ruby>す** - humble. `わたしがいたします` "I'll do it", offering your own<br>*action deferentially. Extremely common in service and business Japanese: <ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>いたします, ご<ruby>案内<rt>あんない</rt></ruby>いたします.*
- **なさる** - honorific, for someone else's action. Note the irregular polite<br>*form **なさいます** (not ❌なさります). ゴルフをなさいますか。*

---

## する · Nuance & register (cont.)

Because noun + する is so productive, it is the standard way Japanese absorbs foreign words. Anything that arrives in katakana can become a verb overnight: チェックする, キャンセルする, ダウンロードする, シェアする. If you know the noun, you know the verb - this is the single fastest way to expand your active vocabulary.

One register caution: the <ruby>漢語<rt>かんご</rt></ruby> compounds (<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>する, <ruby>購入<rt>こうにゅう</rt></ruby>する) are a shade more formal than their native equivalents (<ruby>確<rt>たし</rt></ruby>かめる, <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う). In an email they sound crisp and professional; in casual chat with a friend they sound like you are reading from a manual.

---

## する · Nuance & register (cont.)

`する` also underlies fixed expressions that never translate word for word: <ruby>気<rt>き</rt></ruby>にする "worry about it", <ruby>大切<rt>たいせつ</rt></ruby>にする "treasure something", <ruby>無理<rt>むり</rt></ruby>をする "overdo it". Meet these as vocabulary, not as grammar.

---

## する · 勉強する vs 勉強をする

> **Rule of thumb** - both are correct and mean the same thing. Insert `を` and the noun becomes a genuine object, which has two consequences: it can now be modified, and it now blocks a second `を` in the same clause.

<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>します**。<br>*I study Japanese.*

<ruby>文法<rt>ぶんぽう</rt></ruby>の**<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>をします**。<br>*I do grammar study.*

---

## する · 勉強する vs 勉強をする (cont.)

The first sentence already has a `を` on <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>, so ❌<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>をします is out - one clause, one `を`. The second sentence needs the `を` version, because <ruby>文法<rt>ぶんぽう</rt></ruby>の has to attach to a noun; ❌<ruby>文法<rt>ぶんぽう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>します does not work.

How to choose: bare `する` when the compound is behaving as a plain verb, `をする` when you want to describe or measure the activity itself - <ruby>軽<rt>かる</rt></ruby>い<ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>をする, 1<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby><ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>をする. Not every noun takes `を` equally happily: activity nouns (<ruby>洗濯<rt>せんたく</rt></ruby>, <ruby>散歩<rt>さんぽ</rt></ruby>, <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>い<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby>) accept it readily, while nouns naming a change of state (<ruby>成功<rt>せいこう</rt></ruby>する, <ruby>完成<rt>かんせい</rt></ruby>する) essentially never appear with it.

---

## する · ～にする vs ～になる

> **Rule of thumb** - `する` means *someone makes it so*; `なる` means *it comes to be so*. Choosing from a menu is `する`, because you are the one deciding.

テレビの<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さく**して**ください。<br>*Please turn the television down.*

<ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>急<rt>きゅう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>暗<rt>くら</rt></ruby>く**なった**。<br>*It suddenly got dark outside.*

---

## する · ～にする vs ～になる (cont.)

The same pair runs through nouns: <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>にする "make someone a teacher / decide on the teacher" against <ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>になる "become a teacher". If a person is deliberately causing the change, it is `する`; if the change simply happens, it is `なる`. Japanese leans on `なる` far more than English leans on "become", so when in doubt about a change of state, `なる` is usually the more natural choice.

---

## する · する vs やる

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning, different register. `やる` is the casual, slightly more physical word: it is what friends say to each other.

<ruby>宿題<rt>しゅくだい</rt></ruby>、もう**やった**？<br>*Have you done your homework yet?*

<ruby>宿題<rt>しゅくだい</rt></ruby>はもう**しました**。<br>*I have already done my homework.*

---

## する · する vs やる (cont.)

`やる` cannot fuse directly onto a noun the way `する` does. You can say <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>する, and you can say <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>をやる in relaxed speech, but ❌<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>やる is not a word. `やる` also means "give (to an animal, a plant, or someone clearly junior)" - <ruby>花<rt>はな</rt></ruby>に<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>をやる - and it can sound rough or blunt from the wrong mouth, so it is a poor default in polite company.

---

## する · Common mistakes

- ❌ するます → ✅ **します**<br>*`する` is irregular: the ます-stem is `し`, not ❌`すり` or ❌`する`.*

- ❌ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>をします。 → ✅ <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>します**。<br>*Two `を` in one clause. Once <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby> has taken the object marker, <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby> must fuse onto `する` directly.*

- ❌ テニスを<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>びます。 → ✅ テニスを**します**。<br>*Interference from English "play tennis". <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>ぶ means to play in the sense of having fun; sports and games take `する`.*

- ❌ わたしは<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>をできます。 → ✅ わたしは<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>が**できます**。<br>*`できる` is the potential of `する` and it is intransitive, so what you can do is marked with `が`.*

---

## する · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>で<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>をしました。 → ✅ <ruby>外<rt>そと</rt></ruby>で<ruby>大<rt>おお</rt></ruby>きい<ruby>音<rt>おと</rt></ruby>が**しました**。<br>*Sensations - sounds, smells, tastes - take `が` with `する`. Nobody is doing anything here; the sound simply occurred.*

---

## する · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>までにこの<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>を<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>____おきます。

{reveal}
<ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>**して**おきます

{reveal}
`おく` attaches to the て-form, and the て-form of `する` is `して` - irregular, not built from a stem. The whole compound <ruby>確認<rt>かくにん</rt></ruby>する conjugates exactly as `する` does.

---

## する · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>から<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を（します・なります）。

{reveal}
<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>から<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を**します**。

{reveal}
The speaker is the one taking the job, so it is a deliberate action: `する`. `なる` would need a different frame entirely, such as <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>から<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>になります, which would mean the job itself is what changes.

---

## する · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "My daughter practises the piano for an hour every day."

{reveal}
<ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>1<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>ピアノの**<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>をします**。

{reveal}
Both <ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>します and <ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>をします are possible, but here ピアノの has to modify a noun, so the `を` version is the natural one. Alternatively: <ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>毎日<rt>まいにち</rt></ruby>1<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>ピアノを<ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>します。

---

## する · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かなしてください。

{reveal}
✅ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>では**<ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにして**ください。

{reveal}
To make something take on a quality you use the adverbial form, and for a な-adjective that is `に`, not `な`. `な` only appears directly before a noun.

---

## する · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を きれいに しました。 (b) <ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が きれいに なりました。

{reveal}
(a) says somebody tidied the room - `する` puts an agent behind the change, and the room is marked with `を` as the thing acted on. (b) simply reports that the room ended up clean, with `が` marking it as the subject that changed.

{reveal}
Japanese speakers often prefer (b) even when they did the work themselves, because `なる` sounds less like claiming credit. That preference for describing outcomes rather than agents runs right through the language.

# て-form

**Meaning** · the connective form - the shape a verb takes when the sentence isn't finished with it yet

The て-form is not a tense and not a politeness level. It is the form a word wears when something else is going to attach to it, or when the sentence carries on past it. On its own it says roughly "…and then", but its real importance is structural: `～ている`, `～てください`, `～てもいい`, `～てから`, `～てしまう`, `～てあげる` and dozens more all plug into it. Learn the て-form and a large slice of the grammar you meet later costs you nothing extra.

> **Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** (<ruby>五段<rt>ごだん</rt></ruby> / godan / Group 1), **る-verbs** (<ruby>一段<rt>いちだん</rt></ruby> / ichidan / Group 2), and the two irregulars する and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る (Group 3). This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**. Only う-verbs have complicated て-forms; the other two classes are trivial.

---

## て-form · Formation

る-verbs and the irregulars are one-liners. う-verbs change according to their final syllable, and that table is the thing to memorise.

---

| Verb (う-verb, く) | → いて | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて |

## て-form · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - the **た-form uses exactly these sound changes**, with た/だ in place of て/で. <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んで → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んだ, <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いた, <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>して → <ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>した, <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>って → <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った. Learn one table and you have learned both. This is the single best return on memorisation effort in beginner Japanese.

> **Note** - <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く is the exception every learner trips over. By the く rule it ought to be ❌<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>いて; it is <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>って. Nothing else in ordinary Japanese behaves this way. Watch out too for う-verbs that *look* like る-verbs: <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>って (not ❌<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>て), <ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>って, <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>入<rt>はい</rt></ruby>って.

What the て-form is for, in rough order of how soon you will need it:

---

## て-form · Formation (cont.)

- **Linking events in sequence** - <ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きて、<ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby>を<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>って、<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけます。
- **A continuing action or a state** - `～ている`.
- **Requests** - `～てください`.
- **Permission and prohibition** - `～てもいい`, `～てはいけない`.
- **Cause**, when the second clause is a feeling or a result you did not choose.
- **Manner or means** - <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いて<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く "go on foot".

---

## て-form · Examples

ちょっと**<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>って**。<br>*Hang on a second.*

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べて**、<ruby>会社<rt>かいしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます。<br>*I have breakfast and then go to work.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>シャワーを**<ruby>浴<rt>あ</rt></ruby>びて**います。<br>*My brother is in the shower right now.*

<ruby>駅<rt>えき</rt></ruby>まで**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いて**10<ruby>分<rt>ぷん</rt></ruby>ぐらいかかります。<br>*It's about a ten-minute walk to the station.*

---

## て-form · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>電気<rt>でんき</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>消<rt>け</rt></ruby>して**、<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>を<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>ました。<br>*I turned the light off and left the room.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>くて**、<ruby>髪<rt>かみ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>長<rt>なが</rt></ruby>い<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>です。<br>*Tanaka is the tall one with long hair.*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>って**、<ruby>試合<rt>しあい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>中止<rt>ちゅうし</rt></ruby>になりました。<br>*It rained from the morning yesterday and the match was called off.*

この<ruby>辺<rt>へん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>になると<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>全部<rt>ぜんぶ</rt></ruby>**<ruby>閉<rt>し</rt></ruby>まって**、<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>も<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いていません。<br>*Around here everything shuts at night and there's nobody about.*

---

## て-form · Nuance & register

The て-form carries no politeness of its own - politeness is decided by whatever ends the sentence. <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べて<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く and <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べて<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きます differ only at the tail. That is exactly why it is so useful: a long chain of て-forms lets you build a complicated sentence and only worry about register at the very end.

A bare て-form left at the end of a sentence is a **casual request**: ちょっと<ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>って, <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>せて, <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>って. It is `～てください` with the polite part bitten off, so it is friendly among equals and rude upwards. Softening it with `ね` - <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってね - makes it gentler; adding `よ` makes it more insistent.

Two spoken contractions to expect early, because they are everywhere:

---

## て-form · Nuance & register (cont.)

- `～ている` → `～てる`: <ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>してるの？ , <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってるよ.
- `～ておく` → `～とく`, `～てしまう` → `～ちゃう`: <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>っとく,<br>*<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べちゃった.*

In formal writing, long chains of て-forms look chatty. Written Japanese often replaces the linking て with the bare ます-stem instead - <ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>6<ruby>時<rt>じ</rt></ruby>に <ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>き、<ruby>新聞<rt>しんぶん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む - which is compact and noticeably literary. You do not need to produce it at N5, but you will see it.

One real limitation: the て-form does not mark *why* two events are next to each other. It only says they belong together, and the listener infers sequence, cause, or simple accompaniment from context. When you need the reason to be unmistakable, use `から` or `ので` instead.

---

## て-form · ～て vs ～てから

> **Rule of thumb** - plain `～て` says the events belong together; `～てから` says the first one **finished** before the second started, and puts weight on that order.

<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>って**、ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます。<br>*I'll wash my hands and eat.*

<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>洗<rt>あら</rt></ruby>ってから**、ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます。<br>*I'll eat after washing my hands.*

The difference is emphasis. With plain `て` the two actions are simply a routine; with `てから` you are insisting on the order - first this, *then* that. So `てから` is what you want in instructions and in warnings, and it sounds fussy when the order was never in doubt.

---

## て-form · ～なくて vs ～ないで

> **Rule of thumb** - `～なくて` gives a **reason**; `～ないで` describes **how** something was done, or asks someone **not** to do it.

バスが**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>なくて**、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れました。<br>*The bus didn't come, so I was late for the meeting.*

<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べないで**<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きました。<br>*I went to school without eating breakfast.*

---

## て-form · ～なくて vs ～ないで (cont.)

Swap them and you get nonsense: ❌バスが<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ないで、<ruby>会議<rt>かいぎ</rt></ruby>に <ruby>遅<rt>おく</rt></ruby>れました. A useful test - if you could put "so" or "because" in the English, use `なくて`; if you could put "without", use `ないで`. Requests are always `ないで`: ここに<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>を<ruby>止<rt>と</rt></ruby>めないでください.

Adjectives and nouns only have the `なくて` option: <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くなくて, <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>じゃなくて.

---

## て-form · ～て (cause) vs ～から

> **Rule of thumb** - `～て` can only introduce a cause when the second clause is something **outside your control**: a feeling, a result, an inability. If the second clause is a request, an invitation, or a decision, you must use `から` or `ので`.

ニュースを<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>い**て**、びっくりしました。<br>*I was startled when I heard the news.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しい**から**、<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ってください。<br>*I'm busy today, so please go home early.*

---

## て-form · ～て (cause) vs ～から (cont.)

The second sentence cannot use `て`: ❌<ruby>忙<rt>いそが</rt></ruby>しくて、<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>ってください is ungrammatical, because a `て` cause clause refuses to be followed by a command, a request, an invitation or a volitional. This is the most common way learners misuse the て-form, and it is invisible in translation - English "so" covers both.

---

## て-form · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>いて → ✅ **<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>って**<br>*<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く is the one exception to the く → いて rule. Everything else with く behaves normally: <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて, <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いて, <ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いて.*

- ❌ <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んて → ✅ **<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んで**<br>*む・ぶ・ぬ take んで with a **voiced** で, never んて. Same for <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んで and <ruby>死<rt>し</rt></ruby>んで.*

- ❌ <ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>て → ✅ **<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>って**<br>*<ruby>切<rt>き</rt></ruby>る is an う-verb even though it ends in る, so it takes って. Contrast <ruby>着<rt>き</rt></ruby>る "wear", which really is a る-verb: <ruby>着<rt>き</rt></ruby>て.*

- ❌ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>いで<ruby>明<rt>あか</rt></ruby>るいです。 → ✅ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>くて**<ruby>明<rt>あか</rt></ruby>るいです。<br>*`で` is the connective for nouns and な-adjectives. い-adjectives drop the い and take くて.*

---

## て-form · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるてください。 → ✅ **<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べて**ください。<br>*`ください` attaches to the て-form, not to the dictionary form. Drop the る before adding て.*

{reveal}

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んで、<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ます。 (b) <ruby>薬<rt>くすり</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んでから、<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>ます。

# た-form / plain past

**Meaning** · the plain past - "did", "was" - and the base that a whole family of patterns attaches to

The た-form is the plain-style counterpart of `～ました`. It says an event happened, or that something is now finished. It is also structural: `～たら`, `～たり`, `～たことがある`, `～たあとで` and `～たほうがいい` are all built on it, and none of them will make sense until this form is automatic.

> **Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** (<ruby>五段<rt>ごだん</rt></ruby> / godan / Group 1), **る-verbs** (<ruby>一段<rt>いちだん</rt></ruby> / ichidan / Group 2), and the irregulars する and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る (Group 3). This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**.

---

## た-form / plain past · Formation

> **Rule of thumb** - **the た-form is the て-form with た instead of て.** Every sound change is identical; only the last letter differs, and で becomes だ. <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んで → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んだ. <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いて → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>いた. If you know one, you already know the other.

---

## た-form / plain past · Formation (cont.)

Patterns that need the た-form to exist at all:

- `～たら` - if / when. <ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>いたら<ruby>電話<rt>でんわ</rt></ruby>して。
- `～たり～たりする` - doing this and that. <ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たり<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きたり。
- `～たことがある` - have ever done. <ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>ったことがある。
- `～たあとで` - after. <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたあとで。
- `～たほうがいい` - had better. <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>んだほうがいい。

## た-form / plain past · Examples

もう**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べた**？<br>*Have you eaten yet?*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>た**。<br>*I went to bed early yesterday.*

あ、<ruby>鍵<rt>かぎ</rt></ruby>、ここに**あった**。<br>*Oh - the key was here after all.*

---

## た-form / plain past · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものころ、この<ruby>川<rt>かわ</rt></ruby>でよく**<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>いだ**。<br>*When I was a child I often swam in this river.*

これは<ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby><ruby>京都<rt>きょうと</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>った**<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>です。<br>*This is a photo I took in Kyoto last year.*

<ruby>大学<rt>だいがく</rt></ruby>のとき<ruby>習<rt>なら</rt></ruby>った<ruby>中国語<rt>ちゅうごくご</rt></ruby>を、すっかり**<ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れた**。<br>*I've completely forgotten the Chinese I learned at university.*

<ruby>空港<rt>くうこう</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>いた**ら、すぐ<ruby>連絡<rt>れんらく</rt></ruby>しますので、<ruby>心配<rt>しんぱい</rt></ruby>しないでください。<br>*I'll get in touch as soon as I land, so please don't worry.*

---

## た-form / plain past · Nuance & register

The た-form is plain style. Ending a sentence with it to a stranger or a superior is too familiar - <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べた to your boss should be <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べました. Inside a sentence, though, plain is compulsory regardless of politeness: the verb before とき, ので, から, あとで, and before any noun it modifies, goes into the た-form even when the sentence ends politely. <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいます is correct; ❌<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby> is not.

The た-form does more than past tense. Three uses that surprise learners:

---

## た-form / plain past · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **Completion, not past time.** バスが<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た! - the bus is arriving right<br>*now; you are reporting that the awaited event has landed. Likewise あ、あった "there it is", said while still looking at the thing.*
- **A sudden recollection.** <ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>、<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みだったね - "oh, tomorrow's<br>*a day off, isn't it." Nothing is past; the `た` marks the fact newly clicking into place.*
- **A sharp command in casual speech.** <ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>った、<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>った! -<br>*"go on, off you go!", typical of a shopkeeper closing up. Rough, and worth recognising rather than using.*

---

## た-form / plain past · Nuance & register (cont.)

In writing, plain past is the default: novels, newspapers and reports are written in `た`, not `ました`. So the た-form is not "informal Japanese", it is *plain-style* Japanese - a different axis from politeness.

In casual speech the た-form very often turns up inside the contraction `～ちゃった` (from `～てしまった`), and `～じゃった` after ん-forms: <ruby>忘<rt>わす</rt></ruby>れちゃった "oh no, I forgot", <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>んじゃった "I went and drank it." Learners hear these long before they are taught them.

---

## た-form / plain past · ～た vs ～ました

> **Rule of thumb** - same tense, different register. `ました` is polite style; `た` is plain style, and is also the only option inside a sentence.

<ruby>先週<rt>せんしゅう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いました**。<br>*I bought a new bicycle last week.*

<ruby>先週<rt>せんしゅう</rt></ruby>**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>った**<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>が、もう<ruby>壊<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>れました。<br>*The bicycle I bought last week has already broken.*

In the second sentence the politeness sits on <ruby>壊<rt>こわ</rt></ruby>れました at the end. The relative clause modifying <ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby> has to be plain - this is a hard grammatical rule, not a stylistic preference.

---

## た-form / plain past · ～た vs ～ていた

> **Rule of thumb** - `～た` reports an event as a single point; `～ていた` looks inside it and describes it as ongoing, repeated, or a state that held for a while.

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んだ**。<br>*I read a book yesterday.*

<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>の<ruby>夜<rt>よる</rt></ruby>はずっと<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでいた**。<br>*I was reading all last night.*

---

## た-form / plain past · ～た vs ～ていた (cont.)

The trap is verbs of change. <ruby>結婚<rt>けっこん</rt></ruby>した means the wedding happened; <ruby>結婚<rt>けっこん</rt></ruby>している means the person is married *now*. <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>る behaves the same way: "I know" is <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>っています, never a plain <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>ります, while the negative flips back - <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りません "I don't know", <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りませんでした "I didn't know."

---

## た-form / plain past · ～た vs ～たことがある

> **Rule of thumb** - `～た` reports a **particular occasion**; `～たことがある` reports that the thing is somewhere in your **experience**, with no occasion attached.

2019<ruby>年<rt>ねん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>へ**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**。<br>*I went to Hokkaido in 2019.*

<ruby>北海道<rt>ほっかいどう</rt></ruby>へ**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったことがある**。<br>*I've been to Hokkaido before.*

---

## た-form / plain past · ～た vs ～たことがある (cont.)

Because `たことがある` is about experience rather than occasion, it clashes with a specific time word: ❌<ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>ったことがあります. It also implies the experience is unusual enough to be worth mentioning, so it sounds odd about everyday things - <ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたことが あります is grammatical and absurd.

---

## た-form / plain past · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>いでした → ✅ **<ruby>寒<rt>さむ</rt></ruby>かったです**<br>*い-adjectives carry their own past tense. The い becomes かった, and `です` is added afterwards for politeness without changing anything.*

- ❌ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>いた → ✅ **<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>った**<br>*<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>く breaks the く → いた rule. Compare <ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>いた, <ruby>着<rt>つ</rt></ruby>いた, which are regular.*

- ❌ <ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んた → ✅ **<ruby>遊<rt>あそ</rt></ruby>んだ**<br>*む・ぶ・ぬ verbs take the voiced んだ. So do ぐ verbs: <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>いだ, never ❌<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>いた.*

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるた → ✅ **<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べた**<br>*For a る-verb you replace る with た; you never keep both.*

---

## た-form / plain past · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ます。 → ✅ <ruby>昨日<rt>きのう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ました**。<br>*A past time word does not conjugate the verb for you. Japanese marks tense on the verb every time.*

---

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>冬<rt>ふゆ</rt></ruby>はあまり<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りませんだった。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>去年<rt>きょねん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>冬<rt>ふゆ</rt></ruby>はあまり<ruby>雪<rt>ゆき</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>りませんでした**。

{reveal}
`ません` takes `でした`, not ❌`んだった`. The plain-form equivalent is <ruby>降<rt>ふ</rt></ruby>らなかった - note that the plain past negative is built on `ない`, which inflects like an い-adjective.

## た-form / plain past · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>財布<rt>さいふ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>とした。 (b) <ruby>財布<rt>さいふ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>落<rt>お</rt></ruby>としてしまった。

{reveal}
(a) is a neutral report: the wallet got dropped, and that is the fact. (b) adds `～てしまう`, which colours the same event with regret or with the sense that it is now irreversible - closer to "I've gone and dropped my wallet."

{reveal}
Both are plain past. The tense is identical; what changes is how the speaker feels about it, which is a job Japanese hands to an auxiliary rather than to intonation.

# ない-form

**Meaning** · the plain negative - "doesn't", "won't", "isn't" - and the base a whole set of later patterns attaches to

The ない-form is the plain-style negative, the partner of `～ません`. It is also the foundation of `～ないでください`, `～なくてもいい`, `～なければならない`, `～ないほうがいい` and `～ないで`. Get the stem wrong here and every one of those patterns comes out wrong, which is why it repays care.

> **Note** - verbs come in three classes: **う-verbs** (<ruby>五段<rt>ごだん</rt></ruby> / godan / Group 1), **る-verbs** (<ruby>一段<rt>いちだん</rt></ruby> / ichidan / Group 2), and the irregulars する and <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る (Group 3). This page says **う-verb** and **る-verb**.

---

## ない-form · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | final -u → -a, + ない | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>く → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かない |
| Verb (う-verb ending in う) | う → **わ**, + ない | <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わない |
| Verb (る-verb) | drop る, + ない | <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ない |
| Verb (する) | しない | <ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>練習<rt>れんしゅう</rt></ruby>しない |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | こない | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない |
| Verb (ある, irregular) | ない | ある → ない |
| い-adjective | い → くない | <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い → <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>くない |
| な-adjective | + じゃない／ではない | <ruby>暇<rt>ひま</rt></ruby> → <ruby>暇<rt>ひま</rt></ruby>じゃない |
| Noun | + じゃない／ではない | <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby> → <ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>じゃない |

---

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Past negative (all of the above) | ない → なかった | <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かない → <ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>かなかった |

## ない-form · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - verbs ending in う take **わない**, not ❌あない. <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わない, <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わない, <ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない, <ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>わない. The reason is historical - these verbs used to end in a -wu sound, and the w only survives before あ - but for learning purposes it is simply the rule.

> **Rule of thumb** - **ある has no ない-form**. Its negative is the word `ない` itself. There is no ❌あらない. Polite: ありません. <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がない "there's no time", <ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>がなかった "there was no time". This one irregularity catches almost everybody once.

---

## ない-form · Formation (cont.)

> **Note** - <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る is read three different ways depending on the form: <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る, <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ます／<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>た／<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>て, and <ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない. The kanji does not change, so the negative is read **こない**, never ❌きない.

Once `ない` is attached, the whole thing behaves as an **い-adjective**, and inflects like one:

|---|---|---|

| て-form (cause) | ない → なくて | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かなくて |

| Before a noun | ない + Noun | <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby> |

わたしは<ruby>肉<rt>にく</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べない**。<br>*I don't eat meat.*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>授業<rt>じゅぎょう</rt></ruby>が**ない**。<br>*There's no class today.*

<ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さん、まだ**<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**ね。<br>*Tanaka still isn't here, is he.*

---

## ない-form · Examples (cont.)

ここでは<ruby>写真<rt>しゃしん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>撮<rt>と</rt></ruby>らないで**ください。<br>*Please don't take photographs here.*

<ruby>試験<rt>しけん</rt></ruby>が<ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>なのに、<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>全然<rt>ぜんぜん</rt></ruby>**<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>しない**。<br>*The exam is next week and my brother isn't studying at all.*

<ruby>給料<rt>きゅうりょう</rt></ruby>が<ruby>何年<rt>なんねん</rt></ruby>も**<ruby>上<rt>あ</rt></ruby>がらない**ので、<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>を<ruby>探<rt>さが</rt></ruby>している。<br>*My pay hasn't gone up in years, so I'm looking for a new job.*

## ない-form · Nuance & register

`ない` is plain style, so as a sentence ending it belongs with friends and family. To a customer or a superior you want `～ません`. The middle option, `～ないです`, is polite but relaxed, and is what colleagues say to each other all day.

Bare `ない` at the end of a question - <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない？ - is a casual invitation, not a plain question: "want to come?" It is the plain-style twin of `～ませんか`, and it is extremely common. <ruby>今晩<rt>こんばん</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby> <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べない？

The negative also softens requests, exactly as it does in English. Compare <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてくれますか with <ruby>窓<rt>まど</rt></ruby>を<ruby>開<rt>あ</rt></ruby>けてくれませんか - the negative version is more polite, because a negative question makes it easier to refuse.

---

## ない-form · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two things to keep separate. `ない` is the plain **negative** of a verb; the adjective `ない` meaning "there isn't" is a different word that happens to look the same. And in speech, ん is a very common contraction of ない in the Kansai area and in men's rough speech (<ruby>分<rt>わ</rt></ruby>からん, <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らん) - recognise it, but stick to `ない` yourself until your speech is firmly anchored somewhere.

Written formal Japanese prefers `ず` and `ぬ` in fixed phrases - <ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らずに, やむを<ruby>得<rt>え</rt></ruby>ず - and uses `ではない` where speech uses `じゃない`. `じゃない` is a contraction of `ではない`, and it is casual: never write it in a report.

---

## ない-form · ～ない vs ～ません vs ～ないです

> **Rule of thumb** - one meaning, three registers. `ない` plain, `ないです` polite-casual, `ません` polite-formal.

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>かない**。<br>*I'm not going to school tomorrow. (to a friend)*

<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>は<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きません**。<br>*I will not be going to school tomorrow. (to a teacher)*

---

## ない-form · ～ない vs ～ません vs ～ないです (cont.)

Note the different bases: `ません` is built on the **ます-stem** (<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き), while `ない` is built on the **あ-row stem** (<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>か). They are not two endings on the same stem, which is why you cannot convert one to the other by ear.

---

## ない-form · The verb ない vs the adjective ない

> **Rule of thumb** - three different negatives all end in `ない`, and each is formed from a different base: verbs take あ-row + ない, い-adjectives swap い for くない, and nouns and な-adjectives take じゃない.

このカメラは**<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かない**。<br>*This camera doesn't work.*

このカメラは**<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しくない**。<br>*This camera isn't new.*

このカメラは**<ruby>防水<rt>ぼうすい</rt></ruby>じゃない**。<br>*This camera isn't waterproof.*

---

## ない-form · The verb ない vs the adjective ない (cont.)

All three then inflect identically, because `ない` is an い-adjective: <ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かなかった, <ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しくなかった, <ruby>防水<rt>ぼうすい</rt></ruby>じゃなかった. That shared tail is why ❌<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>かないでした and ❌<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しくないでした are wrong - an い-adjective never takes `でした`.

---

## ない-form · ある → ない vs いる → いない

> **Rule of thumb** - both mean "there isn't", but they split the world the same way their positives do: `ある`/`ない` for things, `いる`/`いない` for people and animals.

<ruby>冷蔵庫<rt>れいぞうこ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>も**ない**。<br>*There's nothing in the fridge.*

<ruby>教室<rt>きょうしつ</rt></ruby>にはだれも**いない**。<br>*There's nobody in the classroom.*

`いる` is a perfectly regular る-verb, so its negative is formed the ordinary way: drop る, add ない. `ある` is the odd one out - it simply becomes `ない`. The polite forms are ありません and いません respectively, and both keep the thing-versus-creature split.

---

## ない-form · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>あない → ✅ **<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>わない**<br>*う-verbs ending in う insert わ, not あ. Same for <ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>わない and <ruby>会<rt>あ</rt></ruby>わない.*

- ❌ お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>があらない → ✅ お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>が**ない**<br>*`ある` has no regular negative. Its negative is the word `ない`, and the polite form is ありません.*

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるない → ✅ **<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べない**<br>*For a る-verb you replace る with ない. Keeping both is the commonest beginner slip of all.*

- ❌ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>いない → ✅ この<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>広<rt>ひろ</rt></ruby>くない**<br>*い-adjectives do not take the verb negative. Swap the final い for くない.*

---

## ない-form · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>ない → ✅ <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → **<ruby>来<rt>こ</rt></ruby>ない**<br>*The kanji stays the same but the reading changes to こ in the negative. Say it aloud when you practise, or the mistake will be invisible on paper.*

## ない-form · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べないで<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>かけた。 (b) <ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>ご<ruby>飯<rt>はん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べなくて、おなかがすいた。

{reveal}
