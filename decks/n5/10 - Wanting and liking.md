# N5 - Wanting and liking

- ～たい
- ほしい
- ～のが好き
- ～のが上手
- ～のが下手

---

# ～たい

**Meaning** · want to do something - the speaker's own desire

`～たい` attaches to a verb and reports a wish felt from the inside. That "from the inside" is not decoration: it is a grammatical restriction. Plain `～たい` is for **your own** desires, and for asking about the **listener's**. You cannot use it to state flatly what a third person wants.

---

## ～たい · Formation

Attach `たい` to the ます-stem, then conjugate it exactly like an い-adjective.

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (う-verb) | ます-stem + たい | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みます → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい |
| Verb (る-verb) | ます-stem + たい | <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたい |
| Verb (する) | し + たい | する → したい／<ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>したい |
| Verb (<ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る) | き + たい | <ruby>来<rt>く</rt></ruby>る → <ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>たい |

Once `たい` is attached, <code><ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい</code> behaves like <code><ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い</code> in every respect:

---

## ～たい · Formation (cont.)

| い-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | + です | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたいです |
| Negative | たい → たくない | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたくない |
| Past | たい → たかった | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたかった |
| Negative past | たい → たくなかった | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたくなかった |
| て-form | たい → たくて | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたくて |
| Adverbial | たい → たく | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたくなる |
| Before a noun | たい + Noun | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたいもの |
| Third person | ます-stem + たがる | <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたがる／<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたがっている |

---

## ～たい · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `たい` is an い-adjective, not a verb. So it is <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたくない</code>, never ❌<code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたいじゃない</code>, and <code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたかった</code>, never ❌<code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたいでした</code>.

---

## ～たい · Examples

<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい**。<br>*I want some water.*

<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>へ**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい**です。<br>*I want to go to Japan.*

<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたい**ですか。<br>*What do you want to eat?*

<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>はどこにも**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたくない**。<br>*I don't want to go anywhere today.*

---

## ～たい · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものとき、パイロットに**なりたかった**。<br>*When I was a kid, I wanted to be a pilot.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいゲームを**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたがっている**。<br>*My little brother wants to buy the new game.*

<ruby>疲<rt>つか</rt></ruby>れたので<ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>寝<rt>ね</rt></ruby>たい**んですが、まだ<ruby>仕事<rt>しごと</rt></ruby>が<ruby>終<rt>お</rt></ruby>わりません。<br>*I'm exhausted and I want an early night, but I still haven't finished work.*

<ruby>来年<rt>らいねん</rt></ruby>は<ruby>沖縄<rt>おきなわ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>って、<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぎたい**と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。<br>*Next year I'm hoping to go to Okinawa and swim in the sea.*

---

## ～たい · Nuance & register

`～たい` itself is neutral; `～たいです` is polite in *form*. The trouble is that politeness of form does not fix bluntness of content. Stating a raw want at someone senior - <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りたいです - is grammatical and still lands badly, because you are pushing your preference at a superior. Softeners exist for exactly this:

---

## ～たい · Nuance & register (cont.)

- **～たいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います** - "I'm thinking I'd like to…". The standard business<br>*hedge. <ruby>来週<rt>らいしゅう</rt></ruby>お<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みを<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>りたいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>います。*
- **～たいんですが** - sets up a request and leaves the ask unsaid.<br>*すみません、これを<ruby>返<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>したいんですが…*
- **～たいのですが／～たく<ruby>存<rt>ぞん</rt></ruby>じます** - written and formal registers.

The same asymmetry runs the other way. Asking a superior <ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>が<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたいですか is a bit bald; <ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>になさいますか or <ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>がよろしいですか is what you actually say. Among friends and to subordinates, plain `～たい?` is perfectly normal.

---

## ～たい · Nuance & register (cont.)

In casual speech the negative `～たくない` is the everyday form; `～たくありません` is correct but stiff, and mostly turns up in writing or careful speech.

`～たがる` is a real verb (a う-verb: `たがらない`, `たがります`, `たがって`) and it carries a slight sense of *watching someone from outside* - you are describing observable behaviour, not reading a mind. That distance can shade into criticism, so `たがる` about a superior is rude: ❌<ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>帰<rt>かえ</rt></ruby>りたがっています.

---

## ～たい · ～たい vs ～たがる

> **Rule of thumb** - `～たい` for me and for you; `～たがる` for him, her and them.

A flat statement about a third person's desire in plain `～たい` sounds wrong to native ears, because you are claiming direct access to someone else's inner state.

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>はケーキを**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたがっています**。<br>*My little sister wants cake.*

---

## ～たい · ～たい vs ～たがる (cont.)

Note the particle: `たがる` is an ordinary verb, so its object takes `を`, never the `が` that `たい` allows. Note the `～ている` too. For a desire someone is feeling *right now*, `たがっている` is the normal form; bare `たがる` reads as a habit - <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>はいつも<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいものを<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたがる - "my brother always wants to buy the newest thing."

`たがる` is not the only way out. These are often more natural, and politer:

- <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>はケーキが<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたいそうです。 (I hear that…)
- <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>はケーキが<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っています。 (she says…)
- <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>はケーキが<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたいようです／みたいです。 (it seems…)

---

## ～たい · ～たい vs ～たがる (cont.)

> **Note** - the third-person ban only applies to a **plain main-clause statement**. Inside a subordinate clause, plain `たい` about someone else is fine: <ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>手<rt>て</rt></ruby>を<ruby>挙<rt>あ</rt></ruby>げてください。 「Anyone who wants to go to Japan, raise your hand.」 It is also fine under <code>と<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っている</code>: <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたいと<ruby>思<rt>おも</rt></ruby>っています。

---

## ～たい · が vs を with ～たい

> **Rule of thumb** - both are correct. `が` spotlights the *thing* you crave; `を` keeps the focus on the *action*. When in doubt at N5, `が` is the safer answer.

Because `たい` turns the verb into an adjective-like word, the old object can take `が` - and that option is genuinely alive in modern Japanese.

ビール**が**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい。<br>*I want a beer. (it's beer specifically that I'm after)*

ビール**を**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい。<br>*I want to drink a beer. (the drinking is what I'm picturing)*

Three places where `を` is the only option:

---

## ～たい · が vs を with ～たい (cont.)

1. **The `を` is not a direct object.** Path and departure `を` never becomes `が`: <ruby>空<rt>そら</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>飛<rt>と</rt></ruby>びたい, <ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>出<rt>で</rt></ruby>たい. 2. **The clause already has a `が`.** Two `が` in one clause collide: ❌<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>がビールが<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい. 3. **The object is far from the verb.** Once material intervenes, `が` degrades: ビール**を**<ruby>冷<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>やして、あとでゆっくり<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい.

`を` is also the safer default in writing and in longer sentences; `が` is at its most natural in short, felt statements - the ones you say when you actually want the thing.

---

## ～たい · ～たい vs ほしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` wants a **noun**; `～たい` wants an **action**.

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいパソコン**がほしい**です。<br>*I want a new computer.*

<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいパソコンが**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたい**です。<br>*I want to buy a new computer.*

`ほしい` is also an い-adjective, and it carries the same first-person restriction as `たい` - third person needs `ほしがっている`. Its verb-taking cousin, `～てほしい`, means you want *someone else* to do something: <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってほしい - "I want you to help."

---

## ～たい · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたいです。 → ✅ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き**たがっています**。<br>*A flat statement about a third person's desire needs `たがる`, or a hedge like `たいそうです`／<code>たいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っています</code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい**じゃない**／<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたい**でした** → ✅ <ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き**たくない**／<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き**たかった**<br>*`たい` is an い-adjective. It never takes `じゃない` or `でした`.*

- ❌ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べる**たい** → ✅ <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ**たい**<br>*`たい` attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べます → <ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ + たい.*

- ❌ コーヒーが**ほしいたい**。 → ✅ コーヒーが**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい**。／コーヒーが**ほしい**。<br>*`ほしい` and `たい` do the same job for different word classes. Never stack them.*

---

## ～たい · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>が<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたいですか。 → ✅ <ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>がよろしいですか。<br>*Grammatical, but too direct upward. Asking a superior about their wants calls for `よろしいですか` or `なさいますか`.*

---

## ～たい · Practice 1

**1.** のどがかわきました。<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>が<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>____です。

{reveal}
<ruby>水<rt>みず</rt></ruby>が<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>**みたい**です。

{reveal}
`たい` attaches to the ます-stem: <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みます → <ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>み + たい. The polite form is just `です` on the end, because <code><ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい</code> is an い-adjective.

---

## ～たい · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>を（<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたいです・<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたがっています）。

{reveal}
<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>靴<rt>くつ</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>買<rt>か</rt></ruby>いたがっています**。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby></code> is a third person, so plain `たい` in a main clause is out - you cannot assert someone else's inner state directly. `たがっている` describes the desire as something you can observe from outside.

---

## ～たい · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I didn't want to go to school yesterday."

{reveal}
きのうは<ruby>学校<rt>がっこう</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたくなかった**（です）。

{reveal}
Past negative of an い-adjective: たい → たく → たくない → たくなかった. There is no ❌<code><ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたいじゃなかった</code>. Adding `です` makes it polite without changing the stem.

---

## ～たい · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>きたいじゃないです。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>今日<rt>きょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>に<ruby>行<rt>い</rt></ruby>き**たくない**です。

{reveal}
`じゃない` is the negative for nouns and な-adjectives. `～たい` is an い-adjective, so the negative is built by swapping the final `い` for `くない`.

---

## ～たい · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) ラーメンが<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたい。 (b) ラーメンを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたい。

{reveal}
Both are correct and a speaker would accept either. (a) puts the spotlight on *ramen* as the object of the craving - "it's ramen I want", the thing itself. (b) keeps the sentence built around the action of eating, so it reads a touch more neutral and descriptive, and it's what you'd reach for in a longer or written sentence.

{reveal}
The choice narrows once the sentence grows: with material between the object and the verb, or with a `が`-marked subject already present, only `を` works.

# ほしい

**Meaning** · want a thing - the speaker's own desire for an object

`ほしい` (kanji <ruby>欲<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>しい, but usually written in kana at this level) is an **い-adjective**, not a verb. It does not take an object with `を`; the thing you want is marked with `が`. And like `～たい`, it reports a want felt from the inside, so plain `ほしい` covers **your own** wants and questions about the **listener's** - never a flat statement about what a third person wants.

---

## ほしい · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (the thing wanted) | Noun + が + ほしい | <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby> → <ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>がほしい |
| Verb (an action you want *from someone*) | て-form + ほしい | <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>う → <ruby>手伝<rt>てつだ</rt></ruby>ってほしい |
| Verb (an action you want *to do*) | not possible - use `～たい` | <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>みたい |
| Third person's want | Noun + を + ほしがる | <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby> → <ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>をほしがっている |

Once `ほしい` is in place it conjugates exactly like <ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>い or <ruby>安<rt>やす</rt></ruby>い:

---

## ほしい · Formation (cont.)

| い-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Polite | + です | ほしいです |
| Negative | ほしい → ほしくない | ほしくない |
| Polite negative | + です／ありません | ほしくないです／ほしくありません |
| Past | ほしい → ほしかった | ほしかった |
| Negative past | ほしい → ほしくなかった | ほしくなかった |
| て-form | ほしい → ほしくて | ほしくて |
| Adverbial | ほしい → ほしく | ほしくなる |
| Before a noun | ほしい + Noun | ほしいもの |

---

## ほしい · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` is an い-adjective. So it is `ほしくない`, never ❌`ほしいじゃない`, and `ほしかった`, never ❌`ほしいでした`. And it never conjugates like a verb: there is no ❌`ほしみます`.

---

## ほしい · Examples

お<ruby>金<rt>かね</rt></ruby>が**ほしい**。<br>*I want money.*

もっと<ruby>時間<rt>じかん</rt></ruby>が**ほしい**です。<br>*I want more time.*

<ruby>誕生日<rt>たんじょうび</rt></ruby>に<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>が**ほしい**ですか。<br>*What do you want for your birthday?*

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>は<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>も**ほしくない**。<br>*I don't want anything right now.*

---

## ほしい · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものころ、<ruby>自分<rt>じぶん</rt></ruby>の<ruby>部屋<rt>へや</rt></ruby>が**ほしかった**。<br>*When I was little I wanted a room of my own.*

<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>犬<rt>いぬ</rt></ruby>を**ほしがっています**。<br>*My little brother has wanted a dog for ages.*

<ruby>返事<rt>へんじ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>急<rt>いそ</rt></ruby>ぎませんが、<ruby>一度<rt>いちど</rt></ruby>ゆっくり<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>す<ruby>機会<rt>きかい</rt></ruby>が**ほしい**です。<br>*There's no rush on an answer, but I'd like a chance to talk it through properly.*

この<ruby>書類<rt>しょるい</rt></ruby>、<ruby>明日<rt>あした</rt></ruby>の<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby>までに<ruby>目<rt>め</rt></ruby>を<ruby>通<rt>とお</rt></ruby>して**ほしい**んですが、お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いできますか。<br>*I'd like you to look these documents over by tomorrow morning - could I ask you to?*

---

## ほしい · Nuance & register

`ほしいです` is polite in *form* only. The content is still a bare statement of appetite, and pointing it at someone above you lands like a child asking for a present: <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいパソコンがほしいです is grammatical and socially awkward. Upward, you reframe the want as a request or a hedge:

- **～がほしいのですが／～がほしいんですが** - trails off and lets the listener<br>*offer. <ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>のコピーがほしいんですが…*
- **～をいただけますか／お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いできますか** - asks instead of wanting.
- **～があると<ruby>助<rt>たす</rt></ruby>かります** - "it would help if I had…", the standard<br>*workplace softener.*

---

## ほしい · Nuance & register (cont.)

The same asymmetry runs upward in questions. <ruby>社長<rt>しゃちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>何<rt>なに</rt></ruby>がほしいですか is far too direct; offering with いかがですか is what you actually say.

In a shop or a café, `ほしい` is simply the wrong tool. You do not say これがほしいです to a waiter - you say これをください or これをお<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします. `ほしい` describes your inner state; `ください` performs the request.

---

## ほしい · Nuance & register (cont.)

Casually, `ほしい` is very common and takes all the usual endings: ほしいなあ (wistful), ほしい！, ほしいんだけど…. Men and women use it alike. In writing the kanji <ruby>欲<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>しい appears. What `ほしい` does **not** have is a humble register of its own - there is no polite conjugation that makes it suitable upward, which is why formal Japanese switches word entirely, to <code><ruby>頂<rt>いただ</rt></ruby>きたい</code> or <code>お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いしたい</code>.

`ほしがる` is an ordinary う-verb (<ruby>欲<rt>ほ</rt></ruby>しがらない, ほしがります, ほしがって). It describes desire as something you can *see* in someone - the reaching, the asking, the looking. That outside-observer flavour makes it faintly belittling, so it is not used about superiors: ❌<ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>をほしがっています.

---

## ほしい · ほしい vs ～たい

> **Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` wants a **noun**; `～たい` wants an **action**. They divide the work; they never stack.

アイスクリーム**がほしい**。<br>*I want ice cream.*

アイスクリームが**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたい**。<br>*I want to eat ice cream.*

---

## ほしい · ほしい vs ～たい (cont.)

The two are otherwise twins: both are い-adjectives, both mark their target with `が`, both are barred from flat third-person statements, and both have a `～がる` form for other people. What `ほしい` cannot do is attach to a verb - ❌<ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>むがほしい is not a sentence. If the thing you want is an action you perform yourself, `～たい` is the only route.

---

## ほしい · ほしい vs ～てほしい

> **Rule of thumb** - `Noun + がほしい` wants a **thing**; `Verb-て + ほしい` wants **someone else to do something**. The person you want it from takes `に`.

<ruby>説明書<rt>せつめいしょ</rt></ruby>が**ほしい**。<br>*I want the instruction manual.*

<ruby>使<rt>つか</rt></ruby>い<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えてほしい**。<br>*I want you to show me how to use it.*

<ruby>母<rt>はは</rt></ruby>に<ruby>来<rt>き</rt></ruby>て**ほしくない**。<br>*I don't want my mother to come.*

---

## ほしい · ほしい vs ～てほしい (cont.)

`～てほしい` is a wish, not a command, so it is gentler than a direct request - but it is also more personal, and to a superior it presumes. The polite ladder runs <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>ってほしい → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていただきたい → <ruby>待<rt>ま</rt></ruby>っていただけないでしょうか. The negative is `～ないでほしい`: <ruby>勝手<rt>かって</rt></ruby>に<ruby>決<rt>き</rt></ruby>めないでほしい.

---

## ほしい · ほしい vs ほしがる

> **Rule of thumb** - `ほしい` for me and you; `ほしがる` for him, her and them. The particle flips too: `が` with `ほしい`, `を` with `ほしがる`.

<ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>はずっとピアノを**ほしがっています**。<br>*My daughter has been wanting a piano for a long time.*

Note the `～ている`. A desire someone is feeling *now* is `ほしがっている`; bare `ほしがる` reads as a habit - あの<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>友<rt>とも</rt></ruby>だちのものを<ruby>何<rt>なん</rt></ruby>でもほしがる.

`ほしがる` is not the only escape. These are often smoother, and safer upward:

---

## ほしい · ほしい vs ほしがる (cont.)

- <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>がほしいそうです。 (I hear that…)
- <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>がほしいと<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>っています。 (she says…)
- <ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>がほしいようです。 (it seems…)

> **Note** - the ban is on **plain main-clause statements** only. Inside a modifying clause, plain `ほしい` about other people is perfectly normal: <ruby>資料<rt>しりょう</rt></ruby>がほしい<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は、<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>机<rt>つくえ</rt></ruby>から<ruby>取<rt>と</rt></ruby>ってください。 「Anyone who wants a copy, take one from the desk at the front.」

---

## ほしい · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>**を**ほしいです。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>**が**ほしいです。<br>*`ほしい` is an adjective, so it has no direct object. `を` appears only with the verb `ほしがる`.*

- ❌ <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>がほしいです。 → ✅ <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しい<ruby>車<rt>くるま</rt></ruby>を**ほしがっています**。<br>*A flat statement about a third person's want needs `ほしがっている`, or a hedge like `ほしいそうです`.*

- ❌ **ほしいじゃない**／**ほしいでした** → ✅ **ほしくない**／**ほしかった**<br>*い-adjective endings, not な-adjective ones.*

- ❌ <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>む**がほしい**。 → ✅ <ruby>休<rt>やす</rt></ruby>み**たい**。<br>*`ほしい` cannot take a verb. Wanting to perform an action is `～たい` territory.*

---

## ほしい · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ （カフェで）これ**がほしいです**。 → ✅ これを**ください**。<br>*Naming your inner state at a server is odd. Ordering is done with `ください` or <code>お<ruby>願<rt>ねが</rt></ruby>いします</code>.*

---

## ほしい · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいカメラ____ほしいです。

{reveal}
<ruby>新<rt>あたら</rt></ruby>しいカメラ**が**ほしいです。

{reveal}
`ほしい` is an い-adjective, and what it wants is marked with `が`, not `を`. The English "want a camera" has an object, but the Japanese does not.

---

## ほしい · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>はバイクを（ほしいです・ほしがっています）。

{reveal}
<ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby>はバイクを**ほしがっています**。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>兄<rt>あに</rt></ruby></code> is a third person, so a plain `ほしい` statement would be claiming direct access to someone else's inner state. `ほしがっている` reports the desire as something you can observe - and note that it takes `を`, which is why the sentence was written with `を` to begin with.

---

## ほしい · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "When I was a student, I wanted a motorbike."

{reveal}
<ruby>学生<rt>がくせい</rt></ruby>のとき、バイク**がほしかった**（です）。

{reveal}
The past of an い-adjective: ほしい → ほしかった. There is no ❌`ほしいでした`; adding `です` after `ほしかった` is what makes it polite.

## ほしい · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>をほしいんですが、いい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りませんか。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>辞書<rt>じしょ</rt></ruby>**が**ほしいんですが、いい<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>りませんか。

{reveal}
The `を` is the interference: English treats "want" as a transitive verb, Japanese treats `ほしい` as an adjective whose target takes `が`.

## ほしい · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにして**ほしい**。 (b) <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにして**ください**。

{reveal}
(b) is a direct request aimed at the listener - it tells them to do something, and in the wrong context it can sound like an instruction. (a) reports the speaker's wish instead of issuing the request, so it is indirect, and among friends it comes across as softer, almost a complaint said out loud.

{reveal}
Indirectness is not the same as politeness upward, though. To a superior neither is ideal: <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かにしていただけませんか is the form that actually works.

# ～のが好き

**Meaning** · like doing something - a standing preference, not a present urge

<code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> is a **な-adjective** meaning "pleasing, to my taste", and what pleases you is marked with `が`. English makes "like" a verb with an object; Japanese makes it an adjective with a subject. To like an *action*, you first turn the verb into a noun with the nominaliser `の`, then feed it to <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code>.

---

## ～のが好き · Formation

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb (dictionary form) | Dictionary + のが + <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ → <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き |
| Verb (alternative nominaliser) | Dictionary + ことが + <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き | <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む → <ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むことが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き |
| Noun | Noun + が + <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き | <ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby> → <ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き |
| Verb (する-noun) | Noun + が + <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き | <ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>する → <ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>が<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き |

<code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> then behaves like <ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>か or <ruby>元気<rt>げんき</rt></ruby> - a な-adjective in every respect:

---

## ～のが好き · Formation (cont.)

| な-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plain | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きだ | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きだ |
| Polite | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです |
| Negative | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃない | <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃない |
| Polite negative | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃないです／ではありません | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃありません |
| Past | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きだった／<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きでした | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きでした |
| Negative past | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃなかった | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃなかったです |
| Before a noun | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな + Noun | <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きな<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby> |
| Intensified | <ruby>大好<rt>だいす</rt></ruby>き | <ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>うのが<ruby>大好<rt>だいす</rt></ruby>き |

---

## ～のが好き · Formation (cont.)

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> is an adjective, so there is no ❌<code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きます</code>, no ❌<code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きません</code> and no ❌<code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きくない</code>. And the verb needs `の` to become a noun first: ❌<code><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> → ✅<code><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>むのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code>.

---

## ～のが好き · Examples

<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>うのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**です。<br>*I like singing.*

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>走<rt>はし</rt></ruby>るのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**じゃない。<br>*I don't like running.*

どんな<ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>聞<rt>き</rt></ruby>くのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**ですか。<br>*What kind of music do you like listening to?*

<ruby>父<rt>ちち</rt></ruby>は<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きるのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**です。<br>*My father likes getting up early.*

---

## ～のが好き · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>子<rt>こ</rt></ruby>どものころは<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby>を**かくのが<ruby>大好<rt>だいす</rt></ruby>き**でした。<br>*As a child I absolutely loved drawing.*

<ruby>雨<rt>あめ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>日<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>映画<rt>えいが</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>るのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**です。<br>*I like watching films at home on a rainy day.*

<ruby>妻<rt>つま</rt></ruby>は<ruby>週末<rt>しゅうまつ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>市場<rt>いちば</rt></ruby>まで<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>いて<ruby>野菜<rt>やさい</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>選<rt>えら</rt></ruby>ぶのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**です。<br>*My wife likes walking to the market at the weekend and picking out the vegetables.*

<ruby>地図<rt>ちず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>ないで<ruby>知<rt>し</rt></ruby>らない<ruby>町<rt>まち</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>歩<rt>ある</rt></ruby>き<ruby>回<rt>まわ</rt></ruby>るのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**なんです。<br>*The thing is, I love wandering around a town I don't know without looking at a map.*

---

## ～のが好き · Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral. <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです</code> is the everyday polite form, <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きだ</code> the plain one, and in casual speech the copula usually vanishes altogether - <code><ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いものが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code>, with a rising tone for a question. Upward, お<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか is the polite version: <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、コーヒーはお<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きですか.

One important difference from `～たい`: <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> may be used flatly about a third person. A preference is a settled, observable trait, not a private impulse, so <ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>は<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>に<ruby>登<rt>のぼ</rt></ruby>るのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです needs no `がる` and no hedge. This is one of the few places where describing someone else is *easier* than describing yourself.

---

## ～のが好き · Nuance & register (cont.)

Be careful with people as the object. <code><ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです</code> is a confession of romantic feeling, not "I get on with Tanaka". If you mean the latter, say <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは、いい<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>ですね or <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんと<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです - attaching the liking to an activity defuses it.

Finally, negation carries weight. Japanese hedges dislike far more than English does: あまり<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃないです is the polite way to decline food, music or a suggestion, and it is what a learner should reach for by default.

---

## ～のが好き · ～のが好き vs ～ことが好き

> **Rule of thumb** - `の` for concrete, lived, experienced activity; `こと` for the activity in the abstract. With <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> both are usually fine, and `の` is the more conversational of the two.

<ruby>海<rt>うみ</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**です。<br>*I like swimming in the sea.*

<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えることが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**です。<br>*I like teaching people.*

The first is the feel of the water; the second is teaching as a category of thing. Push the difference and you can hear it, but for a plain statement of taste nobody will correct you either way.

---

## ～のが好き · ～のが好き vs ～ことが好き (cont.)

Where the choice stops being free is when the nominalised clause is not the object of an adjective but a noun in its own right - a `は…です` sentence, for instance. There only `こと` works:

<ruby>趣味<rt>しゅみ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>古<rt>ふる</rt></ruby>い<ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>めること**です。<br>*My hobby is collecting old books.*

❌<code><ruby>集<rt>あつ</rt></ruby>めるのです</code> would be read as the explanatory `のです`, which means something else entirely.

---

## ～のが好き · ～のが好き vs ～たい

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> is what you like **in general**; `～たい` is what you want **now**. Liking something does not mean you want it this minute.

コーヒーを**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>むのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**です。<br>*I like drinking coffee. (a habit, a taste)*

<ruby>今<rt>いま</rt></ruby>、コーヒーが**<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みたい**です。<br>*I want a coffee right now. (an urge)*

---

## ～のが好き · ～のが好き vs ～たい (cont.)

The two also behave differently across persons. `～たい` cannot be stated flatly about someone else, but <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> can - which is why a description of another person's tastes almost always comes out in <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code>: <ruby>夫<rt>おっと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>するのが<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです.

---

## ～のが好き · 好き vs 嫌い

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い</code> is much stronger than English "don't like". The everyday negative is あまり<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃない.

<ruby>運動<rt>うんどう</rt></ruby>するのは**あまり<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃない**です。<br>*I'm not that keen on exercising.*

<ruby>虫<rt>むし</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い**です。<br>*I hate insects.*

---

## ～のが好き · 好き vs 嫌い (cont.)

Two traps sit here. First, <code><ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>い</code> ends in `い` but is a **な-adjective**: <code><ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いな<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>いじゃない</code>, never ❌<code><ruby>嫌<rt>きら</rt></ruby>くない</code>. Second, notice the `は` in the first sentence. Swapping the `が` for `は` when you negate is standard, and it softens further - you are marking this one thing as the exception rather than making a sweeping declaration.

---

## ～のが好き · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**が**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ <ruby>本<rt>ほん</rt></ruby>を<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>む**のが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*A verb cannot be the subject of an adjective on its own. `の` nominalises it.*

- ❌ <ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>**を**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。 → ✅ <ruby>音楽<rt>おんがく</rt></ruby>**が**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。<br>*Interference from English. <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> is not a verb and has no direct object.*

- ❌ ピアノを**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きます**。 → ✅ ピアノが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです**。<br>*<code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> takes adjective endings. A verb <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>く</code> does exist, but in everyday Japanese it survives mainly in the passive (<code><ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>かれる</code>).*

- ❌ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きくない**です。 → ✅ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きじゃない**です。<br>*な-adjective negation, despite the final `き` looking harmless.*

---

## ～のが好き · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**の**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き**な**<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べ<ruby>物<rt>もの</rt></ruby><br>*Before a noun, a な-adjective takes `な`, not `の`.*

---

## ～のが好き · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>る____<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。

{reveal}
<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>に<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>る**のが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです。

{reveal}
The verb <code><ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>る</code> has to become a noun before it can be the subject of <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code>, and that is what `の` does. The particle after it is `が`, never `を`.

---

## ～のが好き · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いものを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるのが（<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです・<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べたいです）。

{reveal}
<ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>甘<rt>あま</rt></ruby>いものを<ruby>食<rt>た</rt></ruby>べるのが**<ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>きです**。

{reveal}
Two reasons. The sentence describes a standing taste rather than a present urge, and it is about a third person, where a flat `～たい` is not allowed anyway. The second option would also leave the nominalised clause stranded.

Two problems in one short sentence. The object of <code><ruby>好<rt>す</rt></ruby>き</code> takes `が`, not `を`; and before a noun a な-adjective uses `な`, not `の`.

## ～のが好き · Practice 5

# ～のが上手

**Meaning** · to be good at something - skill you can see in the result

<code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is a **な-adjective** describing an ability that has visibly been trained. What someone is good at is marked with `が`, and to say they are good at an *action* you nominalise the verb with `の` first. The important thing to learn alongside the grammar is who you may say it about: <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is a judgement handed down, so it is used about other people, and almost never about yourself.

---

---

## ～のが上手 · Examples (cont.)

<ruby>息子<rt>むすこ</rt></ruby>はまだ<ruby>五<rt>ご</rt></ruby>さいですが、<ruby>自転車<rt>じてんしゃ</rt></ruby>に**<ruby>乗<rt>の</rt></ruby>るのが<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*My son is only five, but he's good at riding a bicycle.*

<ruby>娘<rt>むすめ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>小<rt>ちい</rt></ruby>さいころから<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>に**かきました。<br>*My daughter drew well from the time she was small.*

<ruby>祖母<rt>そぼ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>昔<rt>むかし</rt></ruby>から<ruby>着物<rt>きもの</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>着<rt>き</rt></ruby>るのが<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**で、<ruby>近所<rt>きんじょ</rt></ruby>の<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>にもよく<ruby>教<rt>おし</rt></ruby>えています。<br>*My grandmother has always been good at putting on a kimono, and she often teaches the neighbours too.*

---

## ～のが上手 · Nuance & register

<code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is a **verdict**. You are standing outside someone's performance and rating it, and that is exactly why the word behaves the way it does socially.

First, you do not normally use it about yourself. ❌<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です is grammatical and sounds boastful; the natural equivalents are <ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby>です, <ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せます, or the modest classic まだまだです.

---

## ～のが上手 · Nuance & register (cont.)

Second - and this catches learners going the other way - praising a **superior** with <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is also awkward, because rating someone above you is not your place. <ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>、<ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>がお<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>ですね is softened by the honorific `お`, and is heard, but さすがですね or <ruby>勉強<rt>べんきょう</rt></ruby>になります is safer. Between equals and downward it is completely normal.

Third, when someone says <ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>がお<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>ですね to you, the expected reply is not ありがとうございます but a deflection: いいえ、まだまだです or そんなことないです. Accepting the compliment flatly is not wrong, just noticeably un-Japanese.

---

## ～のが上手 · Nuance & register (cont.)

<code>お<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>ですね</code> said with the wrong timing means "you flatterer" - お<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>を<ruby>言<rt>い</rt></ruby>って is a fixed phrase for exactly that. Context carries it, but be aware the word has that second life.

---

## ～のが上手 · ～のが上手 vs ～のが得意

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> rates someone **else**; <code><ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby></code> is how you talk about **yourself**. <code><ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby></code> also carries confidence, not just skill.

<ruby>林<rt>はやし</rt></ruby>さんはギターを**<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>くのが<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*Hayashi is good at playing the guitar.*

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>で**<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すのが<ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*I'm good at speaking in front of people.*

---

## ～のが上手 · ～のが上手 vs ～のが得意 (cont.)

The difference is where the judgement comes from. <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is an outside assessment of quality, so turning it on yourself is self-congratulation. <code><ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby></code> describes your own sense of what you are strong at and can be asserted without immodesty - it is what you write on a job application.

Note that <code><ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby></code> is not a full escape either: said too readily it still sounds confident, so many speakers reach for <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>しできます or a plain potential form instead.

<code><ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby></code> about a third person is fine too, but it then reports *their* confidence: <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>数学<rt>すうがく</rt></ruby>が<ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby>です suggests he would say so himself, whereas <ruby>弟<rt>おとうと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>数学<rt>すうがく</rt></ruby>が<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です is your opinion of him - and for school subjects it sounds odd, because <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> wants a performed skill.

---

## ～のが上手 · 上手 vs できる

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is *how well*; できる is *whether at all*.

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>はスペイン<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby>が**できます**。<br>*My little sister can speak Spanish.*

<ruby>妹<rt>いもうと</rt></ruby>はスペイン<ruby>語<rt>ご</rt></ruby>を<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>すのが**<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*My little sister speaks Spanish well.*

The first says the ability exists; the second grades it. This is why できる and the potential form are the safe way to describe **your own** abilities - <ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>げます reports a fact, while <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐのが<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です awards yourself a prize.

---

## ～のが上手 · 上手 vs できる (cont.)

They also differ in what they accept. できる takes nouns and する-nouns (<ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby>ができる) and, with `ことが`, verbs. <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> takes nouns and `のが` clauses, and refuses anything that is not a trainable skill: ❌<ruby>背<rt>せ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>高<rt>たか</rt></ruby>いのが<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です is nonsense.

---

## ～のが上手 · 上手 vs うまい

> **Rule of thumb** - same meaning, lower register. うまい is casual, slightly male-coded, and ambiguous with "tasty".

あいつ、<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>**うまい**な。<br>*Wow, he's a good singer.*

<ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby>はテニスが**お<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**ですね。<br>*You're good at tennis, aren't you, sensei.*

---

## ～のが上手 · 上手 vs うまい (cont.)

うまい is an い-adjective (<ruby>上手<rt>うま</rt></ruby>い in kanji, though usually kana), so it takes うまくない, うまかった - a different paradigm from <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> entirely. It belongs to friends, sport and the internet, never to a business email.

Its other meaning is "delicious", which is where the ambiguity bites: この<ruby>店<rt>みせ</rt></ruby>、うまい in a restaurant is about the food, not the chef's technique. And the negative <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby> has no equivalent slot here - the casual opposite of うまい is へた or へたくそ, which is blunter still.

---

## ～のが上手 · Common mistakes

- ❌ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です**。 → ✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>日本語<rt>にほんご</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>得意<rt>とくい</rt></ruby>です**。／<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>話<rt>はな</rt></ruby>せます。<br>*Grammatical but boastful. <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is a verdict on someone else.*

- ❌ ギター**を**<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ ギター**が**<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です。<br>*<code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is an adjective and has no direct object.*

- ❌ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ**が**<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>泳<rt>およ</rt></ruby>ぐ**のが**<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>です。<br>*A verb needs the nominaliser `の` before `が`.*

- ❌ <ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**に**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**な**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby><br>*`に` modifies verbs, `な` modifies nouns.*

## ～のが上手 · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**します**。 → ✅ <ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>**です**。／<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>に**します**。<br>*<code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code> is not a verb; it needs the copula, or `に` if it is describing how something is done.*

# ～のが下手

**Meaning** · to be bad at something - a lack of skill, plainly stated

<code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> is a **な-adjective**, the mirror image of <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code>. What you are bad at is marked with `が`, and an *action* has to be nominalised with `の` first. Because it is a blunt verdict, it is comfortable about yourself - where it reads as modesty - and uncomfortable about anyone else.

---

| Attaches to | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|

| Person + thing | Person + は + Thing + が + <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby> | <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby>が<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です |
| Adverbially (modifying a verb) | <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>に + Verb | <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>に<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>く |

| な-adjective form | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Plain | <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>だ | <ruby>踊<rt>おど</rt></ruby>るのが<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>だ |
| Polite | <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です | <ruby>踊<rt>おど</rt></ruby>るのが<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です |

## ～のが下手 · Nuance & register

Pointed at yourself, <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> is safe and even likeable. Japanese conversation runs on modesty, and <ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>が<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>なんです is a normal, friendly thing to say about your own cooking. There is no need to soften it.

Pointed at anyone else it is a judgement, and a harsh one. Telling a colleague <ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>が<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>ですね is close to an insult in English terms. The two standard repairs are あまり<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>じゃない (a hedged negative) and <ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby> (a weakness rather than a failing) - and about a superior you simply do not go there at all.

---

## ～のが下手 · Nuance & register (cont.)

<code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>くそ</code> exists and is much cruder; it is what one friend shouts at another over a video game, not something to try on strangers. Note also that unlike <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code>, <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> has no polite お-form in ordinary use - there is nothing to be honorific about.

> **Note** - <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>に</code> before a verb has drifted away from "badly". It means *carelessly, without thinking, rashly*: <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>に<ruby>動<rt>うご</rt></ruby>くと<ruby>危<rt>あぶ</rt></ruby>ないです「Move carelessly and you'll get hurt」. The literal "does it badly" sense is usually carried by <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>な<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby> or あまり<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>じゃない instead.

---

## ～のが下手 · 下手 vs 苦手

> **Rule of thumb** - <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> is about **skill**; <code><ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby></code> is about how you **feel** about it. <code><ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby></code> covers things you have no skill in at all - food, insects, particular people.

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>を**<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>うのが<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*I'm a bad singer.*

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はカラオケが**<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*I'm not keen on karaoke.*

---

## ～のが下手 · 下手 vs 苦手 (cont.)

The first is a claim about your voice. The second says karaoke makes you uncomfortable - perhaps because you cannot sing, perhaps because you hate being looked at. That extra emotional layer is why <code><ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby></code> reaches places <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> cannot: なっとうが<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>です and あの<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>は<ruby>少<rt>すこ</rt></ruby>し<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>です are both natural, while ❌なっとうが<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です is nonsense - you do not perform natto.

<code><ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby></code> is also the kinder word about other people, because "he finds it hard" is gentler than "he is bad at it": <ruby>山田<rt>やまだ</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>数字<rt>すうじ</rt></ruby>が<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>みたいです.

---

## ～のが下手 · 下手 vs あまり上手じゃない

> **Rule of thumb** - about **yourself**, <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code>. About **other people**, the hedged negative あまり<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>じゃない.

<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**ですが、やってみます。<br>*I'm bad at this, but I'll give it a go.*

<ruby>彼<rt>かれ</rt></ruby>は<ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>が**あまり<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>じゃない**です。<br>*He isn't a very good cook.*

---

## ～のが下手 · 下手 vs あまり上手じゃない (cont.)

The hedge does two things at once. あまり plus a negative is the standard Japanese way of saying "not very", and phrasing the criticism as a *shortfall of skill* rather than *presence of badness* takes the sting out. Add みたいです or ようです and it softens again, because you are now reporting an impression rather than passing sentence.

The asymmetry is the same one that governs <code><ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby></code>, run in reverse: you praise other people and criticise yourself, never the other way round.

---

## ～のが下手 · Noun が下手 vs Verb のが下手

> **Rule of thumb** - if a single noun names the skill, use it bare. Reach for `のが` when the skill only exists as an action.

<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>はピアノが**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*My older sister is bad at the piano.*

<ruby>姉<rt>あね</rt></ruby>はピアノを**<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>前<rt>まえ</rt></ruby>で<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>くのが<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*My older sister is bad at playing the piano in front of an audience.*

---

## ～のが下手 · Noun が下手 vs Verb のが下手 (cont.)

Nouns that already name an activity - ピアノ, テニス, <ruby>料理<rt>りょうり</rt></ruby>, <ruby>運転<rt>うんてん</rt></ruby> - go straight in with `が`. Adding <code>を<ruby>弾<rt>ひ</rt></ruby>くのが</code> is not wrong, it just spells the activity out, which is what you want as soon as the description gets more specific than the noun can carry.

And some skills have no noun at all. There is no single word for "remembering people's names" or "saying no", so the verb clause is the only route: <ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>の<ruby>名前<rt>なまえ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>覚<rt>おぼ</rt></ruby>えるのが<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です. What you cannot do is drop the `の` and hand the raw verb to the adjective.

---

## ～のが下手 · Common mistakes

- ❌ なっとうが**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**です。 → ✅ なっとうが**<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>**です。<br>*Food is not a skill. <code><ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby></code> is the word for things you would rather avoid.*

- ❌ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**ですね。 → ✅ <ruby>田中<rt>たなか</rt></ruby>さんは<ruby>英語<rt>えいご</rt></ruby>が**あまり<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>じゃない**ですね。<br>*Direct <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> about another person is rude. Hedge it, or use <code><ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby></code>.*

- ❌ <ruby>踊<rt>おど</rt></ruby>る**が**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です。 → ✅ <ruby>踊<rt>おど</rt></ruby>る**のが**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です。<br>*The verb has to be nominalised by `の` before it can take `が`.*

- ❌ <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**くない**です。 → ✅ <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**じゃない**です。<br>*<code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> is a な-adjective. The final `た` is part of the reading, not an adjective ending.*

---

## ～のが下手 · Common mistakes (cont.)

- ❌ <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**の**<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby> → ✅ <ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**な**<ruby>絵<rt>え</rt></ruby><br>*な-adjectives take `な` before a noun.*

## ～のが下手 · Practice 1

**1.** <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby>に<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>する____<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
<ruby>道<rt>みち</rt></ruby>を<ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>する**のが**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>する</code> is a verb, so `の` turns it into something `が` can mark. You could also say <code><ruby>説明<rt>せつめい</rt></ruby>が<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です</code>, using the noun on its own.

---

## ～のが下手 · Practice 2

**2.** <ruby>課長<rt>かちょう</rt></ruby>に：「<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>はゴルフが（<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>です・あまり<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>じゃないです）ね。」

{reveal}
<ruby>部長<rt>ぶちょう</rt></ruby>はゴルフがあまり**<ruby>上手<rt>じょうず</rt></ruby>じゃないです**ね。

{reveal}
Even this is risky about a superior, but of the two it is the survivable one. A flat <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> about someone above you is a straight insult; the hedged negative reads as an observation rather than a verdict.

---

## ～のが下手 · Practice 3

**3.** Translate into Japanese: "I'm bad at getting up early."

{reveal}
<ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>朝<rt>あさ</rt></ruby><ruby>早<rt>はや</rt></ruby>く**<ruby>起<rt>お</rt></ruby>きるのが<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**です。

{reveal}
About yourself, <code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> is the modest, natural choice. Many speakers would also say <code><ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>です</code> here, which adds "and I dislike it" to the meaning.

## ～のが下手 · Practice 4

**4.** Find and fix the error: <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>が<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>くなかったです。

{reveal}
✅ <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>歌<rt>うた</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>じゃなかった**です。

{reveal}
<code><ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby></code> only looks like an い-adjective. It is a な-adjective, so the negative past is `じゃなかった`, exactly as with <code><ruby>静<rt>しず</rt></ruby>かじゃなかった</code>.

## ～のが下手 · Practice 5

**5.** Explain the difference in nuance: (a) <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>書<rt>か</rt></ruby>くのが**<ruby>下手<rt>へた</rt></ruby>**です。 (b) <ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>は<ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby>が**<ruby>苦手<rt>にがて</rt></ruby>**です。

{reveal}
(a) is a statement about the output: your kanji come out badly formed. It says nothing about how you feel while writing them.

{reveal}
(b) is about your relationship with kanji - they are your weak point, you avoid them, tests involving them make you nervous. It may or may not imply that your handwriting is poor, and it is the sentence a learner is usually reaching for when they want to say "kanji are my nemesis".
